Topology-Aware Reasoning over Incomplete Knowledge Graph with Graph-Based Soft Prompting
Shuai Wang, Xixi Wang, Yinan Yu

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have shown remarkable capabilities across various tasks but remain prone to hallucinations in knowledge-intensive scenarios. Knowledge Base Question Answering (KBQA) mitigates this by grounding generation in Knowledge Graphs (KGs). However, most multi-hop KBQA methods rely on explicit edge traversal, making them fragile to KG incompleteness. In this paper, we proposed a novel graph-based soft prompting framework that shifts the reasoning paradigm from node-level path traversal to subgraph-level reasoning. Specifically, we employ a Graph Neural Network (GNN) to encode extracted structural subgraphs into soft prompts, enabling LLM to reason over richer structural context and identify relevant entities beyond immediate graph neighbors, thereby reducing sensitivity to missing edges. Furthermore, we introduce a two-stage paradigm that reduces computational cost while preserving good performance: a lightweight LLM first leverages the soft prompts to identify question-relevant entities and relations, followed by a more powerful LLM for evidence-aware answer generation. Experiments on four multi-hop KBQA benchmarks show that our approach achieves state-of-the-art performance on three of them, demonstrating its effectiveness. 1 1 1 Code is available at the repository: https://github.com/Wangshuaiia/GraSP .



1
Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated remarkable capabilities across a wide range of applications. However, despite these advances, LLMs often suffer from hallucinations and unstable accuracy when handling knowledge-intensive tasks
13
. To address these limitations, Knowledge Graphs (KGs) have gained increasing attention as a reliable source of structured and explicit knowledge
20
. By integrating the complementary strengths of LLMs and KGs, Knowledge Base Question Answering (KBQA) has emerged as an important research direction. KBQA frameworks improve answer reliability by explicitly retrieving and reasoning over relevant entities and relations on KGs, enabling more accurate and evidence-grounded responses
22
.

Figure 1:
An example of multi-hop reasoning over an incomplete knowledge graph. Existing methods (middle) solely rely on explicit edges and fail to perform multi-hop reasoning under graph incompleteness (e.g. missing edges). Our method (bottom) overcomes this limitation by exploiting implicit relations in the graph structure.

Addressing complex questions often requires multi-hop reasoning, as the answer nodes are typically distant from the starting topic nodes in the Knowledge Graph. To bridge this gap, representative methods start from topic entities and progressively expand along KG edges under the guidance of LLMs
23
;
38
. Building on this paradigm, subsequent works further make some improvements by enhancing search efficiency via Monte Carlo Tree Search
18
or by strengthening reasoning coherence through explicit memory of explored paths
12
;
5
.

However, such entity-to-entity traversal paradigm suffers from an inherent limitation: they are strictly confined to explicit KG edges. In practice, knowledge graphs are often incomplete, making the reasoning process highly sensitive to missing relations, which can easily disrupt multi-hop inference.
As illustrated in the Figure
1
, answering “
What language do the people in the area where the newspaper Knews is circulated speak?
” requires identifying the newspaper’s circulation region and then inferring the predominant language spoken in that region. The absence of a direct edge between
Knews
and
Cyprus
blocks further forward expansion, causing the reasoning chain to break.

To mitigate these bottlenecks,
27
attempt to enrich the context by extracting subgraphs and converting them into textual descriptions via LLMs. While this approach increases information redundancy to hedge against missing edges, such graph-to-text transformation inherently obscures the underlying structural topology, hindering the model’s ability to discern and exploit implicit correlations between seemingly disconnected entities. Furthermore, this verbalization process is highly susceptible to semantic noise, as the generated narratives often incorporate extraneous or irrelevant information that can distract the LLM from the core reasoning task.

Our key insight is that even in the absence of explicit edges, the surrounding subgraph structure often provides sufficient evidence to bridge reasoning gaps. The topological structure of KGs often contains essential information
24
. For example, as illustrated in the Figure
1
, although
Knews
and
Cyprus
are not directly connected, their local subgraph exposes convergent associative paths, such as (Knews, owned by, SPP Media Group)
\rightarrow
(SPP Media Group, operates in, Cyprus). Such structural patterns offer strong structural cues for inferring the missing relation
circulated in
between
Knews
and
Cyprus
.
Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) are well suited to exploit this information by capturing complex entity dependencies through message-passing mechanisms
30
.
Unlike previous methods that make isolated, step-by-step decisions, using GNNs allows candidate entities to be evaluated within their local structural contexts, enabling the LLM to reason beyond immediate neighbors and reach entities without direct connections.

In this paper, we proposed a graph-based soft prompting (
GraSP
) framework for multi-hop KBQA. GraSP first employs a GNN to encode the retrieved subgraph, capturing its rich structural dependencies as dense entity representations. We then used a transformation layer to map these representations into soft prompts, which provides the LLM with global structural context. Inspired by the two-stage paradigm of
4
, we first used a small LLM to identifies question-relevant entities in the subgraph with the help of the soft prompts. Then we further injected the selected entities together with their associated relations into another larger LLM to generate accurate and high-quality answers. This two-stage approach, using a lightweight LLM to generate a draft and then a larger LLM to refine and verify, improves overall performance while reducing computational cost.
Our contributions are summarized as follows:

•

We propose a novel subgraph-level reasoning paradigm that encodes structural subgraphs into graph-based soft prompts using a GNN, enabling more robust reasoning under knowledge graph incompleteness.

•

We propose a two-stage reasoning paradigm in which a lightweight model performs soft-prompt-based reasoning, and a powerful LLM generates evidence-aware answers, reducing computational cost while enabling seamless integration with off-the-shelf LLMs.

•

Our framework achieves state-of-the-art performance on three out of four multi-hop KBQA benchmarks, validating its effectiveness in complex knowledge-intensive tasks.

2
Related Work

2.1
Knowledge Graph Reasoning

Multi-hop knowledge base question answering (KGQA) requires reasoning over multiple relations to find answers.
Recent work increasingly treats LLMs as reasoning agents that explore knowledge graphs. Methods such as Think-on-Graph
23
perform structured traversal over entities and relations, while Plan-on-Graph
5
and Debate-on-Graph
19
introduce step-wise verification or role-based collaboration to reduce spurious reasoning. Other approaches enhance exploration via auxiliary mechanisms, including memory augmentation
12
and iterative query decomposition
29
;
11
.
Learning-based methods further guide multi-hop reasoning, including transition modeling
38
and reinforcement learning–based decision processes
36
. To improve efficiency, LightPROF
1
compresses reasoning graphs into compact embeddings. However, these approaches still rely on explicit KG edges at inference time. LMP
27
alleviates this by linearizing subgraphs into text, but with a loss of graph structure.

2.2
Incomplete Knowledge Graphs

Incomplete knowledge graphs are traditionally addressed by knowledge graph completion (KGC), which employs embedding-based, structure-aware, or large pretrained models to predict missing links
3
;
39
;
21
;
28
. Some works further explores inductive and foundation-model-based KGC to improve transferability
6
;
21
;
10
. Despite methodological advances, these methods are primarily designed to explicitly complete the KG before downstream use.

There are also some work focuses on reasoning over incomplete KGs. To mitigate missing knowledge at inference time, Generate-on-Graph
32
employs LLMs to generate implicit facts, but such generation may suffer from hallucination. Alternatively,
8
estimate the likelihood of missing relations in the retrieved subgraph, but this approach still relies on explicit edge prediction and makes limited use of the global graph structure.
In contrast, our work perform multi-hop KGQA directly over incomplete graphs, without requiring prior graph completion.

2.3
Soft Prompt Reasoning

Soft prompting enables parameter-efficient adaptation of frozen language models by optimizing continuous prompt vectors
15
;
14
;
16
. Recently, this paradigm has been extended to latent reasoning
33
;
9
;
37
as well as structured reasoning, like graph-based reasoning
17
;
26
;
34
. However, existing methods typically require access to model internals, which restricts their applicability to open-source LLMs. In contrast, our proposed two-stage paradigm applies soft prompting to a lightweight open-source model and then translates the learned representations into natural language, enabling seamless integration with any off-the-shelf closed-source LLMs.

3
Task Definition

We consider the problem of Knowledge Base Question Answering (KBQA) over a knowledge graph

G\subseteq\mathcal{E}\times\mathcal{R}\times\mathcal{E}
,
where
\mathcal{E}
and
\mathcal{R}
denote the sets of entities and relations, respectively.
Each fact in the graph is represented as a triple
(e,r,e^{\prime})
, indicating that a relation
r
holds between entities
e
and
e^{\prime}
.
Given a natural language question
q
and a topic entity
e_{t}\in\mathcal{E}
identified from the question,
the goal is to determine the corresponding answer entity
e_{a}\in\mathcal{E}
by reasoning over
G
. This process typically involves traversing the graph starting from
e_{t}
.
However, due to the sparsity and incompleteness of real-world knowledge graphs,
the answer entity often cannot be reached via direct one-hop relations.
Consequently, effective KBQA systems must support multi-hop reasoning,
where
e_{a}
is connected to
e_{t}
through a sequence of intermediate entities and relations.

to

and subsequently treats
Cyprus
as the topic entity to derive the final answer.

4
Method

Our proposed method is illustrated in Figure
2
. It consists of three components: (1)
Subgraph Extraction
, (2)
Graph-based Soft Prompting
, and (3)
Two-Stage Reasoning and Answering
. Given a complex query, we first extract a subgraph centered on the topic entities and encode its structure using a GNN with message passing. The encoded entity representations are then used to construct soft prompts. Finally, we decouple reasoning and answering via a two-stage LLM framework, reducing computational cost while enabling flexible integration with off-the-shelf LLMs.

4.1
Subgraph Extraction

We begin subgraph extraction based on a specific entity, which is the topic entity when reasoning starts.
We aim to retrieve its
l
-hop neighborhood of the entity; however, directly expanding all neighbors is infeasible due to the large number of irrelevant entities. To address this, we adopt a relevance-based retrieval strategy.

Specifically, we encode the question
q
and each candidate entity
e
using a BERT-based encoder and compute their cosine similarity:

s(q,e)=\cos(\mathbf{q},\mathbf{e}),

(1)

where
\mathbf{q}
and
\mathbf{e}
denote the vector representations of the question and the entity, respectively. Entities are ranked according to
s(q,e)
, and the top-
k_{1}
entities are selected.

To enable multi-hop expansion, we iteratively repeat this process: for each selected entity, we retrieve its neighboring entities and again select the top-
k_{2}
entities based on similarity to the question. By iterating this procedure up to
l
hops, we obtain a question-relevant
l
-hop subgraph. The hop number
l
is treated as a hyperparameter. The specific SPARQL queries used for neighbor retrieval are provided in the Appendix
C
.

4.2
Graph-based Soft Prompting

To encode the extracted subgraph, we employ an
l
-layer graph neural network. Since cosine similarity alone is insufficient to model relational dependencies, we adopt a graph attention network to focus on entities that are more relevant to the question.

At each layer, for a target entity
e_{i}
at
(l+1)
-th layer and its neighbor
e_{j}
at
l
-th layer, let
\mathbf{h}^{(l+1)}_{i}
and
\mathbf{h}^{(l)}_{j}
denote their representations, respectively. The node update is computed as:

\mathbf{h}^{(l+1)}_{i}=\sigma\Bigl(\sum_{j}a_{ij}\mathbf{W}^{(l)}\mathbf{h}^{(l)}_{j}+\mathbf{b}^{(l)}\Bigr),

(2)

where
\mathbf{W}^{(l)}
and
\mathbf{b}^{(l)}
are learnable parameters,
\sigma
represents an activation function, and
a_{ij}
is the attention weight between entities
e_{i}
and
e_{j}
.

The attention weight
a_{ij}
is computed as:

a_{ij}=\text{softmax}\left(f\big(\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{q}\,\|\,\mathbf{h}_{i}),\;\mathbf{W}(\mathbf{h}_{j}\,\|\,\mathbf{r}_{ij})\big)\right),

(3)

where
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a scoring function,
\mathbf{r}_{ij}
denotes the relation embedding between
e_{i}
and
e_{j}
, and
\|
represents vector concatenation.

The GNN layer computes the message passing over the entire graph structure as:

\mathbf{H}^{(l+1)}=\sigma\Bigl(\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\hat{\mathbf{A}}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{H}^{(l)}\mathbf{W}^{(l)}+\mathbf{b}^{(l)}\Bigr),

(4)

Where
\mathbf{H}
is the feature matrix of entity representations,

\mathbf{D}
is the degree matrix which its diagonal element represent how many entities connected to each entity, and

\hat{\mathbf{A}}=[a_{ij}]
denotes the adjacency matrix.
After
l
layers, we obtain contextualized embeddings for all entities in the subgraph.

We then transform the embeddings of all entities in the subgraph into a soft prompt.
To bridge the modality gap between graph structures and natural language, we introduce a lightweight feed-forward network (FFN) to map these representations:

\mathbf{\hat{H}}=\text{FFN}(\mathbf{H}),

(5)

where
\mathbf{\hat{H}}
represents the aggregated graph-level soft prompt matrix.

We then concatenate the instruction context
I_{\text{instr}}
, the question
Q
, the list of candidate entities
E
, and the soft prompt
\mathbf{\hat{H}}
as input to an LLM.

\mathbf{x}_{\text{LLM}}=\text{concat}[\mathcal{I}_{\text{instr}},\mathcal{Q},\mathcal{E},\mathbf{\hat{H}}],

(6)

All textual information together with the graph-based soft prompt constitutes the final prompt, guiding the LLM to select entities relevant to the question.

4.3
Two-Stage Reasoning and Answering

To reduce computational cost while maintaining strong performance, we borrowed the conception of draft-and-refine framework
4
and utilize a two-stage answering framework.

First, the generated soft prompt is fed into a lightweight LLM with fewer parameters, which is used to extract the entity (or entities) most relevant for answering the question:

\mathcal{\hat{E}}=\text{LLM}_{\text{select}}(\mathbf{x}_{\text{LLM}})

(7)

To efficiently leverage powerful closed-source models, we design the output of the first stage in natural language, allowing it to serve as explicit evidence that can be jointly consumed with the question by any powerful off-the-shelf LLM for answer generation. Beyond directly producing an answer, the LLM is first prompted to assess whether the currently available information is sufficient to answer the question. If so, it generates the final answer; otherwise, it selects an intermediate entity as the next reasoning target and repeats the subgraph extraction and reasoning process. Formally, this decision process is defined as:

\text{LLM}_{\text{ans}}(q,\mathcal{\hat{E}},\mathcal{\hat{R}})=\begin{cases}\text{answer},&\text{if sufficient;}\\
\text{entity},&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(8)

Here,
\mathcal{\hat{R}}
denotes the set of relations corresponding to the entity set
\mathcal{\hat{E}}
.

4.4
Optimization

Overall, the trainable components of our GraSP framework include the GNN and the FFN in Section
4.2
.

For the LLM used in entity selection (denoted as
\text{LLM}_{\text{select}}
in Section 5.3), we allow two training strategies: either fine-tuning the model or keeping it frozen. Since the primary objective of this component is to identify question-relevant entities, we optimize it using a cross-entropy loss:

\mathcal{L}=-\log P(y\mid\mathbf{I}_{\text{instr}},\mathbf{Q},\mathbf{E},\mathbf{\hat{H}}),

(9)

where
y
denotes the ground-truth target entity. Gradients are backpropagated through the graph-based soft prompt
\mathbf{\hat{H}}
to update the parameters of both the GNN and the FFN.

In contrast, for
\text{LLM}_{\text{ans}}
in the answer generation stage, once sufficient information has been retrieved, it answers the question does not rely on task-specific modeling assumptions. Therefore, the LLM used for answer generation can be any off-the-shelf LLM and does not require fine-tuning.

5.2
Comparative Methods

We compared our approach with the following state-of-the-art methods:

•

ToG

23
: A step-by-step reasoning framework based on LLMs. We re-implement this method using GPT-5.2.

•

KBQA-o1

18
: Enhances search efficiency on knowledge graphs by training a Monte Carlo Tree Search strategy, which requires model fine-tuning. We re-implement this based on LLaMA3.3-70B.

•

KG-Agent

•

iQUEST

LMP

27

5.3
Main Results

The main experimental results of our method are reported in Table
1
. As shown, our approach achieves the best performance on three multi-hop datasets. On the generalization dataset, our results are also comparable to the strongest baseline. Among the compared methods, ToG exhibits the poorest performance, primarily because it relies solely on node-level semantic information at each reasoning step. In contrast, KBQA-o1, iQUEST, and KG-Agent show consistent improvements, indicating that incorporating sub-questions or memory mechanisms can effectively enhance reasoning performance. Notably, KG-Agent extracts entities from both the training and test sets in advance, significantly reducing the search space for the LLM. This substantially lowers the difficulty of evaluating generalization on GrailQA, leading to relatively strong results.

We also observe that LMP, which also uses subgraphs to enhance reasoning, achieves the best performance among the baselines, as it can utilize richer contextual information at each reasoning step. However, this approach incurs substantial computational overhead due to repeated subgraph extraction and transformation. Also, the transformation process is less effective because introduce noises, and overly large subgraphs can exceed the LLM’s effective context window, negatively affecting performance. In contrast, our method adopts a soft-prompt-based design and employs a GNN to efficiently compute graph representations in parallel, resulting in a more effective and scalable utilization of graph structure.

5.4
Ablation on Graph-based Soft Prompt

To investigate in detail the effect of the Graph-based Soft prompt, we designed several variants for comparison. (1)
Without Soft Prompt
, we removed the soft prompting module and allowed the LLM to directly select question-relevant entities. (2)
Triplet-based Representation
, where the retrieved subgraph was linearized into a list of knowledge triplets, which were then provided to the LLM as input, discarding the explicit graph structure. (3)
Text-based Soft Prompt
, in which all triplets were encoded into semantic vectors using BERT, and the resulting embeddings were used as soft prompts instead of graph-structured representations. These variants enable us to isolate and analyze the contribution of graph-structured soft prompts to overall model performance.

The experimental results are reported in Table
2
. Overall, our GraSP achieves the best performance across all settings. Removing graph information entirely (
Without Soft Prompt
) leads to a substantial performance drop, highlighting the importance of structured knowledge for multi-hop reasoning.
Triplet-based Representation
yields noticeable improvements over the no-graph baseline, indicating that incorporating graph-derived information benefits reasoning. Furthermore, compared to
Triplet-based Representation
,
Text-based Soft Prompt
consistently performs better, demonstrating that soft prompts effectively enhance the reasoning capability of LLMs. Notably,
Graph-based Soft Prompt
further outperforms
Text-based Soft Prompt
, confirming that GNNs can more effectively encode and exploit structural information. These trends are highly consistent across experiments with two different LLMs, providing strong evidence for the effectiveness and robustness of the proposed GraSP.

5.5
Performance under Incomplete KGs

To evaluate the robustness of our method under incomplete KGs, we conducted experiments on the most complex dataset CWQ by randomly removing a portion of edges connected to the topic entities, simulating varying degrees of KG incompleteness. The removal ratio ranges from 5% to 25% to assess the impact of increasing incompleteness. We compared our approach with three representative baselines, ToG, KBQA-o1 and LMP.
Notably, LMP addresses KG incompleteness by converting subgraphs into redundant natural language representations, which partially alleviates the impact of missing edges.
The results are shown in Figure
3
.

As the proportion of removed edges increases, all methods exhibit performance degradation compared to using the complete KG, with more severe drops under higher levels of incompleteness, which is expected. However, across all incompleteness settings, our method consistently shows a slower degradation trend than the baselines. In particular, the overall performance drop of our approach is only 10.8 points, significantly smaller than that of ToG (18.2), KBQA-o1 (17.2) and LMP (14.9).
While both GraSP and LMP are more effective at mitigating the impact of KG incompleteness, ToG and KBQA-o1 suffer substantially larger performance degradation, as they rely heavily on explicit KG edge traversal for reasoning. In contrast, our method performs reasoning over a retrieved subgraph augmented with graph-based soft prompts, which effectively mitigates the impact of missing edges. These results demonstrate that GraSP is more robust to KG incompleteness.

Effectiveness of Two-Stage Reasoning

Firstly, we evaluate the impact of the compact model
\text{LLM}_{\text{select}}
in the first stage. Specifically, we experimented with LLMs of different scales, including Qwen3-0.6B, Qwen3-8B and GPT-20B, and further examined the effect of fine-tuning. We fix
\text{LLM}_{\text{answer}}
as GPT-5.2 across all experiments. The results are summarized in Table

Overall, larger
LLM

models achieve better performance after fine-tuning. Notably, once fine-tuned, even an 8B model can achieve strong performance comparable to GPT-20B, suggesting that a tuned
LLM
select
collaborates more effectively with the GNN to identify relevant entities. Meanwhile, we observe that, benefiting from our paradigm, the performance remains acceptable even without fine-tuning; under this setting, the 0.6B model still delivers competitive results.

We further extended our evaluation to a broader range of LLMs, with results reported in Table

(Appendix

).The results consistently show that our method remains stable even when using very small size LLMs.
This is because LLMs are more effective at verification and refinement, making the framework more tolerant to noise introduced in the selection stage.

To examine the impact of the answer LLM used in the second stage, we experimented with seven different LLMs. The results are reported in Table

(Appendix

5.7
Impact of Subgraph Structure

As described in Section
4.1
, subgraph extraction starts from a topic entity and collects its
l
-hop neighbors to construct an induced subgraph. Increasing the hop number introduces more multi-hop neighbors, resulting in a structurally more complex subgraph. To examine the impact of the hop number on subgraph construction, we evaluate our method using 1-hop, 2-hop, and 3-hop subgraphs, with the results reported in Table
4
.

The results show that 2-hop subgraphs achieve the best overall performance. With 1-hop extraction, missing edges cannot be effectively compensated, making it difficult to infer potential connections between entities; besides, the resulting subgraph is often too simple to fully exploit the proposed method. In contrast, 2-hop subgraphs can partially recover missing relations through intermediate entities, as the example in Figure
1
. However, increasing the hop number to 3 introduces excessive entities and edges, which complicate information passing and introduces substantial noise, leading to little benefit. Therefore, we adopt 2-hop subgraphs as they strike a favorable balance between structural expressiveness and noise, achieving effective encoding of graph-based soft prompts.

Conclusion

In this paper, we proposed GraSP, a novel subgraph-level reasoning framework that encodes subgraphs into soft prompts using GNNs. By reducing reliance on explicit knowledge graph edges, GraSP effectively mitigates the impact of missing relations and alleviates issues caused by incomplete knowledge graphs. We further introduced a two-stage reasoning paradigm, where a lightweight LLM identifies question-relevant entities guided by soft prompts, followed by a more powerful LLM for evidence-aware answer generation. This modular design allows GraSP to be seamlessly integrated with off-the-shelf LLMs. Experiments on four benchmarks show state-of-the-art performance on three datasets, while extensive analyses further validate the effectiveness of our approach.

Limitations

While our approach demonstrates promising effectiveness, it still has several limitations. First, our method relies on the structural information of the underlying knowledge graph. When the knowledge graph is severely incomplete, contains sparse information, or exhibits overly simple structures, the extracted subgraphs may provide limited useful evidence, which in turn constrains the effectiveness of our approach. Second, our framework employs soft prompts, which are continuous representations without explicit semantic grounding. As a result, the reasoning process becomes less interpretable, making it difficult to precisely understand how specific structural signals influence the LLM’s decision-making.

Appendix A
Implementation Details

In our main experiments, we use GPT-5.2 as the answer LLM and GPT-OSS-20B as the selection LLM. Specifically, we apply LoRA to the query and value projection matrices of each self-attention layer, with the LoRA rank set to
r=8
and the scaling factor set to
\alpha=16
. A dropout rate of 0.1 is applied to the LoRA layers. During fine-tuning, only the LoRA parameters are updated while all original model parameters remain frozen.

During subgraph construction, we extract 2-hop subgraphs, and accordingly employ a two-layer attention-based GNN for subgraph encoding. We adopt
bert-base-uncased
as the text encoder, which has a hidden size of 768, while the hidden dimension of the GNN is set to 128. Since GPT-OSS-20B has a hidden size of 2880, the feed-forward mapping layer that projects GNN representations into soft prompts has a dimension of 128 × 2880. All models are trained on 8 NVIDIA A100 GPUs (80GB each). In addition, we conduct extensive comparisons using LLMs of different scales, including Qwen3-0.6B, Qwen3-1.7B, Qwen3-4B, Qwen3-8B, Qwen-30B-A3B, LLaMA-3.3-7B, and GPT-4o.

For constructing ground-truth labels for question-related entity selection (i.e.,
y
in Function
9
), we treat the answer entity as the most relevant entity for 1-hop questions. For multi-hop questions, all entities along the shortest path between the topic entity and the answer entity are considered relevant.
