Prevalence and factors influencing work-related musculoskeletal disorders among sonographers in Gamo and Wolaita zones, southern Ethiopia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Melkamu W/amlak Tafesse, Yosef Haile Gebremariam, Samuel Alemu Bamboro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Work-related musculoskeletal disorders are the major public health problems globally. Despite the vital role sonography service providers play in diagnostic healthcare delivery, there is a paucity of studies addressing the magnitude and determinants of work-related musculoskeletal disorders among Sonography service providers in Ethiopia. Therefore, this study sought to determine the prevalence and factors associated with work-related musculoskeletal disorders among sonography service providers in the Gamo and Wolaita Zones of Southern Ethiopia. An institution-based, cross-sectional study was conducted using simple random sampling in public and private hospitals in the Gamo and Wolaita Zones, southern Ethiopia, from September to November 2024. A total of 313 participants working at public and private health facilities in the study area were included. Data were collected using a pretested, structured self-administered questionnaire. The analysis was done using SPSS version 27. Bivariate and Multivariable logistic regression analyses were employed to determine the association. The P-value of < 0.05, with its corresponding 95% confidence interval, was used to compute the strength of association. Three hundred thirteen sonography service providers participated in this study, giving a response rate of 98.74%. The prevalence of work-related musculoskeletal disorders among sonography service providers was 79.6% (95% CI [74.48–83.99%]). Regarding the parts of the body involved, the shoulder (57.03%), neck (24.09%), elbow/wrist/hand (16.06%), and lower back (2.8%) were the most common. Lack of height adjustable ultrasound screen (AOR = 2.945, 95%CI [1.33–6.53]), alternating posture with periodic breaks (AOR = 0.22, 95%CI [0.093–0.55]), having no work fatigue (AOR = 0 .088 95%CI [0.022–0.35]) and having no break times during the work day (AOR = 4.06, 95%CI [1.27–12.90]) were significantly associated with work related-musculoskeletal disorders. A high prevalence of work-related musculoskeletal disorders was observed. Having no work fatigue, static posture, lack of adjustable equipment, and insufficient work breaks were significantly associated with work-related musculoskeletal disorders, suggesting a potential role of workplace ergonomics and work practices. Interventions focusing on ergonomic improvements, structured work-rest schedules, and promotion of proper body mechanics may help reduce the burden of work-related musculoskeletal disorders.

Introduction

The term musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) refers to injuries or disorders of the nerves, ligaments, muscles, joints, tendons, and supporting structures of the upper and lower limbs, neck, and spine, caused by sudden or cumulative exposures to physical exertion. Sometimes they can also be referred to as repetitive strain injury, cumulative trauma disorder, and repetitive motion injury. When the above conditions occur due to work conditions, it is referred to as Work-Related Musculoskeletal Disorder (WRMSD)1.

According to the World Health Organization (WHO) report, MSDs include all forms of ill-health, ranging from light, transitory disorders to irreversible, disabling injuries. These disorders are widespread and cause increasing occupational health problems in the workplace worldwide. They usually occur when a muscle, tendon, nerve, or joint is stressed and traumatized on a repeated basis for days, months, or years, and those body tissues eventually become damaged2.

Health care professionals most often report having pain in the low back, neck, shoulder, hand, wrist, elbow, and upper back3. Sonography service providers encounter a variety of work-related challenges due to the nature of ultrasound examinations. These procedures involve repetitive movements, such as twisting the body, stretching to operate equipment while scanning, and applying pressure, especially in cases of obese patients. Additionally, sonographers often need to maintain prolonged and awkward body positions to achieve the necessary angles for scanning various body parts. This can include leaning across the patient’s body without adequate support, leading to strain on the shoulders and elbows4.

Addressing the risk of injuries among sonographers necessitates a multifaceted approach, encompassing ergonomic adjustments, equipment upgrades, and consistent application of ergonomic practices. The success of these measures is closely tied to the willingness of users to adopt them. A comprehensive strategy is therefore essential to enhance the safety and well-being of sonography professionals4.

The World Health Organization estimates that approximately 50% to 70% of workers experience work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WMSDs). Globally, around 317 million individuals are affected each year, and an estimated 6300 deaths occur daily due to these conditions5. With prevalence in Africa ranging from 15 to 93.6%, studies reveal that low back pain (LBP) is the most frequently reported MSD among health care professionals5. Even though LBP is often underestimated as a minor illness, especially in Sub-Saharan Africa, the Global Burden of Disease (GBD 2015) reveals that LBP is the leading cause of disability and incurs high costs6.

MSDs are a significant concern for public health in affluent nations such as the USA, Saudi Arabia, Canada, and the United Kingdom, and contribute substantially to worker incapacity, absenteeism, lost productivity, increased healthcare costs, disability, and workers’ compensation expenses7–10.

The Australian Sonographers Association has disclosed alarming statistics indicating that 80–95% of sonographers encounter work-related pain. Moreover, a staggering 90% of them endure this pain for more than half of their career, while one in five sonographers sustains a career-ending work-related injury11. Despite a steady increase in the proportion of sonographers experiencing pain during scans, MSDs remain persistently underreported, despite their significant negative impact on occupational health and related morbidity.

Studies conducted in various regions worldwide, spanning from the Far East, Europe, and Africa, have revealed a prevalence range of WRMSDs among sonographers from 75 to 98%. Regarding the body parts, the shoulder, wrist, neck, and back were identified as the most commonly affected areas among the service providers3,8,12–14. The workplace, physical activity, age, gender, working hours, work experience, health status, mental stress, patient load, working postures, psychological fatigue, and access to ergonomic equipment such as height-adjustable tables and chairs are among the factors associated with WRMSDs12,15,16.

In Ethiopia, numerous studies on WRMSDs among healthcare professionals have been undertaken. However, the majority of these studies focused on specific professional groups, such as nurses, dentists, and other healthcare practitioners17–21.

Given the nature of ultrasound scanning, even if Sonography service providers operate in the proper positioning, this can lead to significant stress on the body, particularly the upper limbs and back4,22. Thus, a study should be conducted to determine the issue among sonographers in the Ethiopian context.

To the best of the researcher’s knowledge, there are limited studies regarding WRMSD among Sonography service providers. Therefore, this study sought to investigate the prevalence of WRMSD among Sonography service providers and its associated factors in the Gamo and Wolaita zones of southern Ethiopia.

Methods

Study area and period

The study was conducted in the Gamo and Wolaita zones from September to November 2024. The Gamo zone is located 505 km southwest of Addis Ababa, the capital city of Ethiopia. It hosted eight public hospitals (one referral hospital, one general, and five primary hospitals), one private primary hospital, 56 health centres, and 299 health posts. Wolaita Zone is located 327 km south of the country’s capital, Addis Ababa. There are 14 hospitals in the zone: 9 public and 5 private hospitals; 74 health centres; and 355 health posts. According to the data from the respective Zonal Health Departments, there are 4845 and 3200 permanently employed healthcare professionals across hospitals. Overall, there are 413 sonography service providers in the hospitals in both zones, with 94 in the Gamo zone and 319 in the Wolaita zone. This group comprises radiologists (MD), radiology technologists (MRT), IESOs, surgeons, clinical midwives, obstetricians (MD), and gynaecologists(MD)23.

Study design period and population

A facility-based cross-sectional study was employed from September to November 2024. All sonography service providers working in health facilities of Gamo and Wolaita Zones, southern Ethiopia, were the source population. The Sonography service providers at the selected health facilities were the study population.

The study included all licensed medical radiology technologists, licensed medical doctors (Radiologists and Obstetricians), Residents (R2, R3, and R4), general practitioners with professional ultrasound skills, clinical midwives, surgeons, and IESO working at ANC follow-up. Participants with less than twelve months of employment were excluded.

Sample size determination and sampling procedure

The sample size was determined using a single population proportion formula, by taking prevalence P = 75%, which was obtained from a study conducted in Sindh Province, Pakistan3. The assumptions considered were 5% margin of error and 95% confidence level. The calculated sample size was 288, and by adding a 10% non-response rate, the final sample size used for this study is 317.

A simple random technique with a proportional allocation method was applied in this study. Initially, all the 22 hospitals, which means 8 hospitals from Gamo Zone and 14 hospitals from Wolaita Zone, were included. Then, after a lottery method was applied to select 5 hospitals from Gamo zone hospitals and 10 hospitals from Wolaita Zone hospitals. The total eligible population was 413 (94 eligible participants were from all 5 hospitals in Gamo Zone, and 319 eligible participants were from all 10 hospitals in Wolaita Zone). The total calculated sample size was 317, which was allocated proportionally for each hospital depending on the available eligible participants. The list of sonography service providers in each selected hospital was obtained from the respective Zonal Health Bureaus. Finally, a simple random method was applied to select the allocated number of participants from each hospital to fulfil the total sample size of 317 (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Proportional allocation of sample size to selected hospitals based on the number of sonography service providers.

Data collection instrument and procedure

The questionnaire was adapted from the literature and uploaded to Kobo Collect, an open-source software used for field data collection3,12,24,25. The questionnaire has four sections. The first section included demographic questions about age, sex, height, and weight. The second section included magnitude-related questions, with emphasis on symptoms of musculoskeletal disorders experienced by the respondent. The third section involved ergonomic/working condition-related factors12, and the final section included psychosocial and behavioural factors12,24.

Before the actual data collection, a pretest was conducted at five hospitals to check the consistency and understandability of the tools. Data collectors received two days of intensive training on the study’s objectives, the content of the tools, and proper data collection procedures. Data were collected by five trained healthcare providers from the respective sites.

Data quality control

Daily supervision was conducted by the principal investigator (MW) and other supervisors to ensure the data quality. Based on pre-test feedback, necessary adjustments were made to the tools to improve clarity and consistency. The investigator checked the collected data for completeness and consistency. The reliability of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha.

Data processing and analysis

The collected data were exported to SPSS version 27 for statistical analysis. The data was cleaned, coded, and processed for analysis. Descriptive statistics were computed, and the data were described using tables, figures, and charts, while the continuous variables were described using means and standard deviations.

The outcome variable is work-related MSDs, and was assessed by a single “Yes” or “No” question. Those who responded “Yes” to this question were considered to have WRMSDs according to this study. The internal consistency of the questionnaire was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and demonstrated acceptable reliability (α = 0.78).

A bivariable and multivariable binary logistic regression model was used to identify the association between independent and dependent variables. The Hosmer and Lemeshow test was conducted to verify the model’s fitness, and the data fitted the model well with a P-value of 0.76. A bivariable logistic regression was employed to identify candidate independent variables for multivariable logistic regression. Independent variables with a P-value < 0.25 were candidates for the multivariable regression analysis to account for potential confounding effects. To assess the strength of association, crude and adjusted odds ratios with their corresponding 95% confidence intervals were estimated. Statistical significance was determined in the multivariable analysis using a 95% confidence level and a P-value < 0.05.

From the study participants, 249 (79.6%) (95% CI [74.48; 83.99]) of the respondents had a history of WRMSDs in the last 12 months (Fig. 2). Among those who reported they had WRMSDs, 113 (45.38%) had visited a health facility for treatment in the last 12 months. Factors associated with WRMSDs among sonography service providers

Based on the bivariate analysis, age of the participant, work experience, working in an uncomfortable posture, not taking measures to prevent WRMSDs, maintaining the same posture for an extended period of time, lack of an ultrasound machine with a height-adjustable screen, workplace comfort, and feeling of fatigue at the workplace were significantly associated with WRMSDs.

In multivariate analysis, the unavailability of a height-adjustable ultrasound screen, maintaining the same posture for an extended period without taking breaks, work fatigue, and having no break times during the working day were significantly and positively associated with WRMSDs.

The odds of having WRMSDs were 2.945 times higher among sonography service providers who work with an ultrasound machine without a height-adjustable screen compared to their counterparts (AOR = 2.945, 95% CI [1.33–6.53]). The odds of experiencing WRMDs among sonography service providers who practice without a work break were 4 times higher compared to their counterparts (AOR = 4.06, 95% CI [1.27–12.90]).

Moreover, sonography service providers who didn’t experience work fatigue were 91.2% less likely to have WRMSDs than those who did (AOR = 0.088, 95% CI [0.022–0.35]).

Discussions

The study determined the prevalence of WRMSDs and their determinants among Sonography service providers at hospitals in Gamo and Wolaita Zones.

The prevalence of WRMSDs among sonography service providers was 79.6%. This study finding was in line with a systematic review and meta-analysis conducted in Iran (75.8%)26, Makkah Province, Saudi Arabia (81%)27, Riyadh, Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (84%)28, and Pakistan (75. 17%)3. The similarity could be attributable to the correspondence in the study methodology, population, and data collection techniques.

The prevalence in the current study was higher than the results of studies conducted in Australia (40%), Saudi Arabia (65.3%)9, Saudi Arabia: a multi-centre cross-sectional study (74%)10, and Polish sonography service providers (65%)29. However, it was lower than the study findings conducted in the USA (90%)13, Australia(97%)8, Canada (91%)30, Guangdong province, China (95.1%)7, China (results from a national web-based survey) (99.3%)31, Nigeria32 and Central China (98.3%)12. The possible explanation for the discrepancies could be significant variations in the equipment used (e.g., different types of ultrasound machines, transducers), patient volume, examination types, and work schedules. Another possible explanation could be the difference in socioeconomic status, which could reduce the working time per day and improve the working environment.

WRMSDs among the study participants most commonly affected the shoulder (57.05%), followed by the neck (24.09%), elbow/wrist/hand (16.06%), and lower back (2.8%). This finding corroborates the findings from a study conducted in secondary care hospitals in the city of Al-Ahsa, Saudi Arabia9. However, it contrasts with findings from a study in Saudi Arabia, which reported lower back pain as the most prevalent10.

The finding revealed that the absence of a sonography machine with a height-adjustable screen is positively associated with WRMSDs among the professionals. The likelihood of experiencing a WRMSD was threefold higher among professionals who work with an ultrasound machine without a height-adjustable screen, compared to their counterparts. In addition to manipulating the transducer on the patient, sonography service providers must keep their eyes on the screen. To maintain ideal viewing, sonography service providers are forced to adopt unnatural positions, such as neck flexion, shoulder abduction, or back twisting, if the screen is not at the proper height. These postures eventually cause repetitive lower back, shoulder, and neck injuries.

In addition, having no work break during working hours was found to be significantly associated with WRMSDs. The likelihood of experiencing WRMSDs among those who don’t take breaks was 4 times higher. This could be explained by the fact that sonography requires prolonged durations of stable posture. To view inside organs, the transducer must be held and maintained in a specific position, which puts continuous strain on the muscles, tendons, ligaments, and joints. Without breaks, these tissues will sustain continuous strains, become irritated, and eventually be harmed. Muscle exhaustion is also a result of working for extended periods of time without breaks. Fatigued muscles are more prone to injury because they are less stable, less effective, and more susceptible to pressure and abuse26.

Sonography professionals who don’t experience work fatigue during operation have a lower risk of having WRMSDs when compared with their counterparts. Fatigue impairs hand–eye coordination and fine motor skills, increasing the risk of falls, slips, and awkward movements, which can result in injuries like rotator cuff injuries, carpal tunnel syndrome, and tenosynovitis; it can create complacency, which makes sonography service providers more likely to take shortcuts or disregard ergonomic guidelines, increasing their exposure to risk factors and the likelihood of developing WRMSD.

Moreover, it reduces muscle strength and endurance, making it harder for them to perform repetitive movements without strain, handle heavy ultrasound transducers, and maintain proper posture, thereby increasing the risk of musculoskeletal injuries. This finding is supported by the study conducted in the province of China7.

Furthermore, not periodically changing sitting posture during prolonged periods was associated with a higher likelihood of WRMSDs among sonography service providers. Maintaining a static posture requires continuous contraction of muscles. Muscle fatigue, reduced blood supply, and the accumulation of metabolic waste products are the results of this extended isometric contraction. These factors increase the risk of muscular strains, tendinitis, and other musculoskeletal conditions. Moreover, prolonged muscle contraction can obstruct blood flow, deprive the muscles of oxygen, and cause lactic acid to accumulate. This results in pain, stiffness, and a reduction in performance. Weariness and injuries will also get worse as a result of restricted blood flow, which makes it more difficult for the muscles to obtain nutrients and eliminate waste33.

The current study has several limitations. First, the scarcity of directly comparable studies limited the ability to contextualize the findings, particularly in interpreting the associations. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes establishing causal relationships. Third, the study was limited to assessing the prevalence of work-related musculoskeletal disorders (WRMSDs) in the Wolaita and Gamo Zones, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Finally, clustering at the hospital level was not accounted for in the analysis, which may have affected the precision of the estimates.

Conclusions

This study found a high prevalence of WRMSDs. The lack of work fatigue, maintaining the same posture for extended periods, the absence of height-adjustable ultrasound screens, and insufficient work breaks were significantly associated with WRMSDs. These findings suggest that workplace ergonomics and work practices may play an important role.

Hospital administrators, sonography service providers, occupational health professionals, policymakers, and researchers should collaboratively reduce WRMSDs by ensuring ergonomic workstations and standards, enforcing work-rest schedules and micro-breaks, promoting proper body mechanics and varied postures, monitoring and funding occupational health interventions, and advancing evidence through robust analytical studies in the Ethiopian context.
