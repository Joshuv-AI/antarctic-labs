The effects of anodal transcranial direct current stimulation on psychological factors in athletes: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Mahdi Mollazadeh, Sara Ramezanpour
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTranscranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is a safe, non-invasive neuromodulation method that delivers a low-intensity, constant direct current through electrodes positioned on the scalp. Therefore, the purpose of this systematic review is to examine the effects of tDCS on psychological factors in athletes.MethodsPrimary sources were sourced from four databases: SCOPUS, PubMed, CENTRAL, and Web of Science. The search period spanned from the inception of these databases up to February 1, 2026. Hedges’ g effect size, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were computed using either random or fixed-effect models for the outcomes.ResultsAfter the screening process, a total of 14 studies comprising 376 participants were included in the systematic review. The results of the meta-analysis showed that tDCS improved attentional performance (1.18 [95% CI: 0.59 to 1.78], p = 0.001), cognitive function (1.12 [95% CI: 0.81 to 1.42], p = 0.001), and reduces mental fatigue (-1.35 [95% CI: -0.84 to -1.85], p = 0.001), reaction time (-1.35 [95% CI: -1.04 to -1.66], p = 0.001).ConclusionThe present meta-analysis demonstrates that the tDCS notably enhances attention, cognitive skills, and reduces mental fatigue, and reaction times among athletes. This research offers a detailed examination of how tDCS influences psychological aspects specifically in athletes, highlighting its potential as a valuable method for enhancing psychological performance in sports.Trial registrationCRD420261304015Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05000-8.Keywords: Transcranial direct current stimulation, Psychological performance, Athletes

Introduction

Athletes invest years of dedicated training to reach peak performance and excel under the pressure of competition [1]. Success in elite sports depends not only on superior physical attributes such as muscular endurance, explosive power, coordination, and rapid decision-making [2], but also on emotional regulation, mental clarity, and cognitive resilience under stress [3]. Increasing evidence indicates that the central nervous system is a critical determinant of athletic performance, often representing the decisive limiting factor beyond physical capacity alone [4].

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is a safe, non-invasive neuromodulation technique that delivers a low-intensity constant current (typically 1–2 mA) via scalp electrodes to modulate neuronal excitability by altering resting membrane potentials [5, 6]. Anodal tDCS increases cortical excitability, while cathodal tDCS decreases it, with effects that can persist beyond the stimulation period depending on stimulation parameters [7]. Beyond local excitability changes, tDCS can also induce broader alterations in brain functional connectivity and promote neuroplasticity [8]. Consequently, tDCS has been widely investigated in neuroscience and clinical settings [9, 10] and has gained growing interest in sports science.

A substantial body of research has examined the effects of tDCS applied over regions such as the primary motor cortex (M1), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), cerebellum, and premotor cortex on athletic performance. These studies have reported improvements in muscular strength [11, 12], endurance capacity [13], dynamic balance [14], and reaction time [15]. However, previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have predominantly focused on the ergogenic effects of tDCS on physical performance outcomes. For instance, Alix-Fages, Romero-Arenas [16], Machado, Jansen [17], Shyamali Kaushalya, Romero-Arenas [18], and Chinzara, Buckingham and Harris [19] consistently reported small-to-moderate effects on strength, endurance, and visuomotor skills, with the strongest benefits often observed in whole-body endurance tasks. While Yu, Zhang [20] extended the scope by including both physical and psychological outcomes in high-level athletes, their analysis was limited to single-session tDCS.

Despite these advances, several important gaps remain in the current literature. Most existing studies and reviews have heavily emphasized physical manifestations of performance while paying limited attention to psychological factors such as attentional control, mental fatigue, cognitive resilience, and perceived exertion variables that are crucial for sustained competitive success. Moreover, the vast majority of investigations have focused on acute, single-session tDCS protocols, leaving the potential benefits and neuroplastic adaptations induced by chronic or multi-session tDCS largely unexplored. In addition, few studies have systematically synthesized the effects of tDCS specifically on psychological domains in athletic populations.

The present systematic review and meta-analysis address these limitations by focusing specifically on the effects of tDCS on psychological outcomes in athletes. Unlike previous reviews that primarily examined physical performance, this study provides a comprehensive synthesis of tDCS effects on key psychological domains, including attentional performance, overall cognitive function, mental fatigue, and reaction time. By incorporating data from both single- and multi-session interventions, it offers novel insights into both acute and more sustained neuromodulatory effects. This focus on psychological factors is particularly important because mental attributes often become the primary performance-limiting factors at the elite level, where physical differences between athletes are minimal. By filling this gap, the current study contributes to a more balanced understanding of tDCS as a neuromodulatory tool in sports and provides a stronger foundation for its potential application in optimizing the mental aspects of athletic performance. Therefore, the objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to investigate the effects of tDCS on various psychological domains in athletes.

Method

This systematic review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [21], as detailed in the accompanying supplementary materials (Supplementary Files 1 and 2). The study protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO international database under registration number CRD420261304015.

Search strategy

This systematic review conducted a comprehensive literature search across four primary electronic databases: PubMed, Scopus, CENTRAL (Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials), and Web of Science. The search encompassed all records available from each database’s inception through February 1, 2026, with no limitations imposed on publication dates. Keyword selection was initially guided by Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms and subsequently refined to optimize sensitivity and capture all pertinent studies. To enhance retrieval, Google Scholar was additionally queried to identify articles indexed across diverse sources. After identifying eligible studies, the reference lists of included publications were manually screened (hand-searching) to detect any additional relevant citations that may have been missed during electronic searches. The databases were queried using structured combinations of keywords organized into the following thematic groups: (1) “tDCS” OR “transcranial Direct Current Stimulation”; AND (2) “psychological performance” OR “cognitive performance” OR “cognitive function” OR “cognitive fatigue” OR “executive function” OR “decision making” OR “perceived fatigue”; AND (3) athletes OR “sport player” OR player OR sport OR “recreational player” OR “recreational sport”. The three conceptual keyword groups were combined using the Boolean AND operator to ensure that retrieved records addressed all three required elements simultaneously (i.e., tDCS intervention, cognitive/psychological outcomes, and an athletic/sporting population). Within each individual group, the OR operator was applied to incorporate synonymous or closely related terms, thereby increasing the sensitivity of the search and capturing the broadest possible range of relevant terminology.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were evaluated using the PICOS framework. The inclusion criteria were defined as follows: (1) Population: Professional, semi-professional or recreational athletes (the study included professional, recreational, and semi-professional athletes who met specific criteria for competitive level, training status, and experience); (2) Intervention: tDCS; (3) Comparator: Comparison of tDCS with either a control group or sham training (Due to the inability to combine crossover studies with parallel designs, crossover randomized controlled trials were excluded from this analysis, and it is recommended that future research examine crossover studies separately); (4) Outcomes: Reaction time, mental fatigue, attention, working memory, anxiety and cognitive function; (5) Study Designs: Randomized controlled trials; (6) Peer-reviewed articles published in English and Persian. The literature search and initial screening were conducted independently by two researchers (M.M. and M.R.GH.). Each researcher independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records against the predefined eligibility criteria. Any disagreements regarding study inclusion or exclusion were resolved through discussion until a consensus was reached. Studies that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded, with the most common reasons for exclusion being recorded (e.g., not an athlete, n = 1).

Outcome measures

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, “psychological factors” were operationally defined as cognitive and affective variables that reflect mental processes, cognitive performance, and subjective psychological states relevant to athletic performance. Specifically, the following variables were classified as psychological factors: attentional performance (including selective attention and sustained attention), overall cognitive function, reaction time, mental fatigue (perceived mental exhaustion and cognitive fatigue), working memory, and anxiety. These domains were selected because they represent key mental determinants of performance under competitive pressure and are distinct from purely physiological or physical performance outcomes (e.g., muscular strength, endurance capacity, or power output).

Outcome data for these psychological factors were extracted directly from the original studies based on the validated measurement tools reported by the authors (e.g., psychometric scales, computerized cognitive tasks, or self-report inventories). When a study reported multiple related measures within the same domain (e.g., different attention tasks), the most representative or primary outcome was selected according to the study’s stated focus. Classification of outcomes into the respective psychological domains was performed independently by two reviewers, with disagreements resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by two researchers (M.M. and M.R.GH.). From each included study, the following information was systematically collected: the first author’s name, study design, participant characteristics (including sample size, age range or mean age ± standard deviation, and sex distribution), outcome measures assessed, methods and units of measurement, as well as detailed characteristics of any training programs or interventions applied (see Table 1 for a comprehensive summary).

Quality of evidence

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently appraised using the Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro) scale. This instrument yields a total score ranging from 0 to 11, based on the presence or absence of 11 specified criteria that evaluate aspects of statistical reporting as well as key elements of internal validity (such as randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, baseline comparability, and adequacy of follow-up). Studies were subsequently classified according to the following thresholds: scores of 7–11 indicated high methodological quality, scores of 5–6 were considered fair, and scores of ≤ 4 were rated as poor [22]. Statistical analyses

Heterogeneity among the included studies was quantified using the I² statistic, which estimates the proportion of total variability attributable to between-study differences rather than chance. Interpretation followed these approximate thresholds: 0%–30% suggesting negligible or no important heterogeneity, 30%–50% indicating low heterogeneity, 50%–75% reflecting moderate heterogeneity, and 75%–100% representing high (or considerable) heterogeneity. To account for potential between-study heterogeneity and within-study sampling error, both fixed-effect and random-effects models were employed in the meta-analyses. A random-effects model was specifically selected when I² exceeded 50% (indicating at least moderate heterogeneity) or when sampling error was deemed substantial [23]. Effect sizes were expressed as Hedges’ g effect size with accompanying 95% confidence intervals (CIs), calculated under either the fixed-effect or random-effects model as appropriate. Statistical significance was defined at p ≤ 0.05. Assessment of potential publication bias involved visual inspection of funnel plots for asymmetry, supplemented by Egger’s regression test as a formal statistical measure (with a p-value threshold of < 0.1 indicating suggestive evidence of bias). When publication bias was suspected or detected, the trim-and-fill method was applied to impute potentially missing studies and derive adjusted effect estimates [24]. All statistical analyses were conducted using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis software, version 2.0 (Biostat Inc., Englewood, New Jersey, USA).

Results

A total of 795 potentially relevant records were initially retrieved from the four electronic databases. An additional 5 studies were identified through backward citation searching (ancestry method) of the reference lists from included articles, bringing the total number of records to 800. After removing 429 duplicates, 371 unique titles and abstracts underwent screening against the predefined eligibility criteria. Of these, 352 were excluded based on title and abstract review, leaving 19 studies for full-text assessment. Following detailed evaluation of the full texts, 5 studies were excluded (the excluded studies lacked sufficient data, did not employ a parallel design (instead utilizing a crossover design), and failed to provide clarity in the reporting of results) because they did not satisfy the inclusion criteria. Ultimately, 14 studies, encompassing a total of 376 participants, were deemed eligible and included in the systematic review (Fig. 1).

Quality of evidence

According to the PEDro scale assessment, the methodological quality of the 14 included studies investigating the effects of tDCS on psychological and cognitive outcomes in athletes was generally strong. Specifically, 13 studies (93%) achieved high methodological quality scores, ranging from 7 to 11, while the remaining 1 study (7%) received a fair quality score in the range of 5 to 6. No studies were rated as poor (≤ 4). Overall, the body of evidence included in this systematic review and meta-analysis exhibited satisfactory methodological rigor as evaluated by the PEDro criteria, supporting the reliability of the findings for quantitative synthesis (detailed individual scores and criterion ratings are provided in Table 2).

Quality of the evidence using the GRADE system

The quality of evidence was assessed utilizing the methodology established by the GRADE system [25]. This framework identifies five factors that can adversely affect the quality of evidence derived from a trial: study design, risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, and imprecision. Each of these factors was evaluated and classified as follows: not serious (no point reduction), serious (1-point reduction), or very serious (2-point reduction), with scores assigned based on the risk of bias in the study. An evaluation of the quality of studies using the GRADE system indicates that all assessed outcomes are of high to moderate quality. Consequently, the conclusions drawn from the evidence in this study can be made with a high degree of confidence. (refer to Table 3).

Publication characteristics

The 14 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) included in this systematic review adopted a consistent two-arm parallel design to evaluate the effects of tDCS predominantly dual-site anodal montages versus sham stimulation on cognitive and psychological performance in athletic populations. Sample sizes ranged from small (8–9 participants) to moderate (up to 44 participants), resulting in a total pooled sample of 376 individuals across all studies. Participants were primarily young adults (mean ages typically between 19 and 27 years), with the majority being male, although several trials included mixed-sex or female-only samples. The athletic cohorts encompassed elite and professional athletes, collegiate competitors, and experienced or amateur players from various sports, most frequently soccer, followed by basketball, boxing, swimming, and power-based disciplines.

Intervention characteristics showed relative uniformity in session duration, with most protocols delivering 20 min of stimulation (one exception used 15 min), typically at conventional intensities of 1–2 mA. The number of sessions varied considerably, ranging from a single acute application to repeated administrations (up to 10 sessions), with the majority involving 2 to 6 sessions over several days or weeks. This variation in dosing likely reflects differing objectives, such as investigating immediate neuromodulatory effects versus cumulative adaptations. Sham conditions were standardized across trials, involving identical electrode placement and initial current ramp-up/down without sustained active stimulation, thereby supporting participant blinding.

Outcome measures centered on domains relevant to athletic performance under cognitive demand, including cognitive function (often quantified as task completion time in seconds), reaction time (in seconds), attention (rating scales), mental fatigue (rating scales), working memory (rating scales), and anxiety (rating scales). Many trials assessed multiple endpoints simultaneously. Overall, the included evidence derives from methodologically sound RCTs (predominantly rated high or fair on the PEDro scale), features a clear emphasis on dual-site tDCS targeting cortical regions implicated in perceptual-cognitive processing, and focuses on enhancing domain-specific cognitive and psychological capacities critical to sport performance across both team and individual disciplines.

Meta-analysis

The meta-analysis evaluated the effects of transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) across multiple domains. For attentional performance, data from three intervention arms showed that tDCS produced a statistically significant enhancement (standardized mean difference: 1.18; 95% confidence interval: 0.59 to 1.78; p = 0.001). However, due to the small number of studies (k = 3), the heterogeneity estimate is unstable. Although the I² value was 0.00% (p = 0.67), this should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of homogeneity (see Fig. 2). For overall cognitive function, eight intervention arms were analyzed. tDCS was associated with a significant improvement (standardized mean difference: 1.12; 95% CI: 0.81 to 1.42; p = 0.001), accompanied by low heterogeneity (I² = 11.88%, p = 0.33), supporting the reliability of this finding (see Fig. 2). Regarding mental fatigue, three intervention arms indicated that tDCS led to a significant reduction in perceived fatigue (standardized mean difference: -1.35; 95% CI: -0.84 to -1.85; p = 0.001). Although the I² value fell within the low-to-moderate range (28.48%, p = 0.24), the small number of studies (k = 3) renders this estimate unstable. Consequently, any conclusions regarding the consistency of effects across studies should be drawn with caution (see Fig. 2). Finally, for reaction time, nine intervention arms demonstrated that tDCS significantly shortened response latencies (standardized mean difference: -1.35; 95% CI: -1.04 to -1.66; p = 0.001). Heterogeneity was moderate (I² = 39.90%, p = 0.10), indicating some methodological variability across studies. This level of heterogeneity should not be characterized as “no heterogeneity” or “low heterogeneity” (see Fig. 2). Visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s test did not indicate significant publication bias (p = 0.72).

Forest plot of the tDCS versus control group analysis on attentional performance, cognitive function, mental fatigue and reaction time

The findings from the meta-analysis provide compelling evidence for the efficacy of tDCS in enhancing cognitive functions across multiple domains. The statistically significant improvements observed in attentional performance, overall cognitive function, mental fatigue, and reaction time suggest that tDCS may modulate neural excitability and synaptic plasticity, thereby facilitating cognitive processes. The Hedges’ g effect size indicates meaningful effects, with values above 1.0 reflecting substantial enhancements in performance. The lack of heterogeneity in attentional performance and overall cognitive function suggests that the effects of tDCS are consistent across different studies and populations, reinforcing the reliability of these interventions. Furthermore, the significant reduction in perceived mental fatigue indicates that tDCS might alleviate cognitive load and enhance mental resilience. The findings related to reaction time highlight the potential of tDCS to improve processing speed, which is crucial for tasks requiring quick decision-making. Overall, these results underscore the therapeutic potential of tDCS in cognitive enhancement, providing a basis for further exploration in clinical and applied settings.

Discussion

The current systematic review and meta-analysis sought to assess the impact of tDCS on attentional performance, cognitive abilities, mental fatigue, and reaction times in athletes. The results revealed that tDCS led to significant enhancements in these areas. Notably, this is the comprehensive meta-analysis to investigate the influence of tDCS on these cognitive aspects in athletes. Previous systematic reviews and meta-analyses have demonstrated the potential of anodal tDCS (a-tDCS) to improve physical performance in healthy adults, reporting small-to-moderate gains in endurance and strength, particularly in whole-body activities such as cycling. A-tDCS applied to the primary motor cortex was found to significantly enhance cycling endurance, although its effects on strength were minimal. Overall, tDCS exhibited a modest ergogenic effect, with greater improvements observed in muscular strength and visuomotor skills compared to endurance, particularly among elite athletes [16–20]. The key distinction of the present review lies in its focus on cognitive function, whereas earlier reviews concentrated on physical performance outcomes. Additionally, the studies utilized standardized measurement tools to evaluate outcomes, allowing for clear meta-analyses for each result. Egger’s test was also employed to examine publication bias, revealing no evidence of bias in the research findings. Notably, an interesting aspect of this study was that the meta-analyses conducted on the specified variables showed no heterogeneity among the studies, indicating a consistent and homogeneous effect across the research.

Heterogeneity and mechanistic considerations

Although the meta-analysis revealed consistently large effects of tDCS across attentional performance, overall cognitive function, mental fatigue, and reaction time, with mostly low to moderate and non-significant statistical heterogeneity, several methodological and biological factors likely contributed to the observed variability between studies. Key sources of heterogeneity included differences in stimulation parameters (electrode montage, current intensity, duration, and timing online versus offline), athlete characteristics (competitive level, sport type, and baseline cortical excitability), and task features (complexity and cognitive demand of the outcome measures). For instance, studies targeting the DLPFC tended to show stronger effects on attention and mental fatigue compared to those stimulating the M1, while reaction time improvements were more pronounced with M1 montages. Variations in single-session versus multi-session protocols further influenced effect sizes, as repeated stimulation may promote greater neuroplastic changes.

From a mechanistic perspective, these differences can be partly explained by the distinct functional roles of the targeted regions. Anodal tDCS over the DLPFC primarily enhances top-down attentional control, conflict resolution, and cognitive resilience by increasing excitability in executive networks, which helps mitigate mental fatigue and sustain performance under pressure. In contrast, stimulation of M1 appears to facilitate corticospinal output and sensorimotor integration, leading to faster reaction times and reduced perceived effort through improved motor planning and connectivity with the supplementary motor area (SMA) and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC). Bilateral motor cortex stimulation may additionally induce widespread functional connectivity changes involving prefrontal areas, providing a synergistic effect on both cognitive and fatigue-related outcomes. The relatively low overall heterogeneity observed in the present meta-analysis suggests that, despite protocol variations, tDCS exerts robust neuromodulatory effects on psychological domains in athletes. Nevertheless, the exploratory nature of the field underscores the need for more standardized protocols to minimize variability and identify individual responders.

The results from three intervention arms demonstrated positive effects of tDCS on attentional performance [26–28]. However, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear. Milham, Banich [29] identified the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) as the primary area responsible for implementing top-down attentional control. Furthermore, Krompinger and Simons [30] noted that the DLPFC resolves conflicts arising during the processing of incongruent stimuli in the attentional task, indicating that attentional performance is closely linked to DLPFC activation. Additionally, previous research has shown that bilateral stimulation of the motor cortex leads to extensive changes in functional connectivity, particularly with the prefrontal cortex and both primary and secondary motor cortices [31]. Anatomically, the M1 is adjacent to the SMA. Activation of M1 may influence the SMA, which is involved in movement planning and decision-making regarding action initiation [32]. Prior studies have also indicated that the SMA may collaborate with the dACC to address cognitive interference produced by the conflict conditions of the attentional task [33]. The dACC transmits signals related to cognitive interference to the DLPFC, which plays a role in resolving such interference [34]. Thus, the enhancement in attentional performance observed in this study may result from tDCS increasing activation in the DLPFC, in addition to stimulating both sides of the motor cortex. Further research is necessary to elucidate the effects of tDCS on attentional performance.

The results from eight intervention arms demonstrated positive effects of tDCS on cognitive function [28, 35–42]. In trained athletes, where baseline neural networks for motor and cognitive processing are highly refined through extensive expertise, tDCS especially anodal stimulation targeting the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) proves capable of enhancing key executive functions essential to sports performance. These include improvements in attention, decision-making under pressure, working memory, perceptual-motor integration, and reduced perceived effort or fatigue-related cognitive decline during demanding scenarios [28]. Such enhancements are particularly evident in cognitively loaded aspects of athletic tasks, including rapid strategy formulation, visuomotor coordination, and maintaining focus amid competition or fatigue, thereby offering potential competitive advantages in dynamic, decision-heavy sports [39]. Mechanistically, tDCS applies a low-intensity direct current (typically 1.6–2.0 mA for 16–20 min in optimal protocols) that modulates cortical excitability by polarizing neuronal membranes in the targeted region [38]. Anodal stimulation increases neuronal firing rates and promotes long-term potentiation (LTP)-like synaptic strengthening, reinforcing existing connections within prefrontal and motor networks. In athletes, this leads to optimized neural recruitment, heightened corticospinal, and reduced inhibition at subcortical levels, including partial disinhibition of propriospinal interneurons that regulate lower-limb motor commands [37]. For cognitive domains, dlPFC-targeted tDCS facilitates better integration with basal ganglia circuits involved in planning complex sequences and effort regulation, mitigating fatigue-induced decrements in executive control without necessarily eliciting global spinal arousal or maximal force gains in isolated tasks [42]. These results underscore the value of tailored montages such as those over dlPFC for executive enhancements or combined M1/dlPFC approaches for integrated cognitive-motor benefits and highlight the need for continued refinement of parameters to maximize ergogenic outcomes in sport contexts.

The results from three intervention arms demonstrated positive effects of tDCS on mental fatigue [26, 43, 44]. Neuroimaging and neurophysiological data suggest that tDCS applied over the primary motor cortex (M1) significantly affects the perception of mental fatigue [44]. Since early central mental fatigue acts as a psychological barrier that prevents athletes from utilizing their full physiological potential, tDCS could be an effective complementary strategy to postpone the onset of undesirable early central mental fatigue [26]. While tDCS targeting M1 and/or the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) has shown effectiveness in reducing mental fatigue across various neurological and immune disorders, the specific mechanisms behind tDCS-induced performance enhancements may differ depending on the type of sport [43]. Further research is necessary to elucidate how tDCS influences mental fatigue and performance across various activities.

The results from nine intervention arms demonstrated positive effects of tDCS on reaction time [26–28, 35, 37, 38, 41, 44, 45] An increase in corticospinal excitability and stimulation may significantly contribute to enhanced reaction speed. Research indicates tDCS over the primary motor cortex (M1) can boost corticospinal output [45]. It can be inferred that stimulating the motor cortex enhances this area and increases corticospinal output during sports-related tasks. M1 stimulation likely improves reaction time by recruiting more motor units [41]. The administration of NMDA receptor antagonists, such as dextromethorphan, has been shown to diminish the effects of tDCS. Furthermore, studies suggest that tDCS not only improves reaction time but may also increase punching force [38]. Anodal tDCS applied to the cortex can enhance muscular power by engaging additional motor units, likely due to acute corticospinal responses [26]. Additionally, research has demonstrated that tDCS can improve motor reflexes, which may further benefit athletes. While tDCS likely accounts for a significant portion of the improvement in reaction time, the enhancement of selective attention, which is associated with higher cortical functions, suggests that the tDCS technique may play a crucial role in improving this cognitive ability [35].

The present findings of large effect sizes in attentional performance, overall cognitive function, mental fatigue reduction, and reaction time align closely with previous meta-analyses demonstrating the cognitive benefits of tDCS, particularly when targeting prefrontal and motor areas. However, the magnitude of effects observed here appears somewhat larger than those reported in earlier studies involving healthy populations or different stimulation protocols. This discrepancy may reflect differences in the specific participant characteristics or task demands in the included trials. These results extend the existing literature by providing further support for tDCS as a promising neuromodulatory tool while highlighting the need for more mechanistic investigations to clarify how tDCS influences interconnected neural networks underlying attention and fatigue.

Practical and clinical implications

Although the present meta-analysis demonstrates large and consistent positive effects of tDCS on key cognitive domains in athletes, no definitive evidence-based optimal tDCS protocol can yet be recommended for routine athletic application. The included studies exhibited considerable heterogeneity in stimulation parameters (e.g., electrode montage, current intensity ranging from 1 to 2 mA, duration of 10–30 min, and single versus repeated sessions), target sites (primarily DLPFC or M1), and timing relative to testing. While preliminary patterns suggest that anodal tDCS at 1.6–2.0 mA for 16–20 min may offer a promising range for cognitive enhancement, particularly when targeting the DLPFC for attentional control and mental fatigue or M1 for reaction time, these observations remain exploratory. From a practical standpoint, tDCS could serve as a non-invasive adjunct to traditional training to acutely enhance attentional focus, reduce mental fatigue, and improve reaction speed during high-pressure competition or cognitively demanding training sessions. However, translation into real-world sports settings requires caution due to the predominance of single-session laboratory protocols, small sample sizes, and limited data on long-term safety, cumulative effects, and individual responsiveness. Larger, well-controlled trials with standardized protocols, proper blinding, and athlete-specific outcome measures are essential before tDCS can be confidently integrated into athletic preparation programs. Until such evidence accumulates, its use should remain within research contexts or under strict supervision by qualified professionals.

Limitations and future scope

This meta-analysis has several methodological limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Due to imposed sanctions, access to the Embase and SPORTDiscus databases was not feasible for this study. This limitation may have affected the comprehensiveness of the literature review and the scope of available research on the topic. The included studies lacked allocation concealment, raising the risk of selection bias and potentially affecting the internal validity of the results. Additionally, the small number of intervention arms for certain outcomes (e.g., only three arms for attentional performance and mental fatigue) and overall limited sample sizes reduced statistical power and may limit the generalizability of the findings to broader athletic populations. Considerable variability in tDCS parameters including electrode montage, stimulation intensity, duration, timing (online vs. offline), and single versus repeated sessions introduced potential heterogeneity in cortical modulation effects, making it difficult to identify optimal protocols. Furthermore, the predominance of acute, single-session designs precludes conclusions about long-term neuroplastic changes or sustained cognitive benefits during prolonged training or competitive periods. Although no significant publication bias was detected, challenges in effective blinding due to perceptible tingling sensations and reliance on task-specific cognitive measures remain notable concerns.

Despite these limitations, the present study contributes to the existing literature by demonstrating consistently large effects of tDCS on attentional performance, overall cognitive function, reaction time, and mental fatigue reduction in athletes’ effects that appear stronger than those typically reported in general populations. These findings highlight tDCS as a promising non-invasive tool for cognitive enhancement and fatigue management in sports. Future research should prioritize larger multicenter randomized controlled trials with rigorous methodological standards, including proper allocation concealment and improved blinding techniques. Longitudinal designs are particularly needed to evaluate the cumulative and chronic effects of tDCS. Standardized stimulation protocols, stratification by athlete expertise level and baseline cortical excitability, and exploration of individual response predictors will be essential to advance personalized application and better understand the underlying neurophysiological mechanisms.

Conclusion

The systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrated that tDCS significantly improves attentional performance, cognitive abilities, mental fatigue, and reaction times in athletes. This study provides a comprehensive examination of the effects of tDCS on cognitive factors within the athletic population, emphasizing its potential as an effective tool for enhancing cognitive performance in sports.
