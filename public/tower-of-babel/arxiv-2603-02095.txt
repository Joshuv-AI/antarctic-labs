On the Rate of Convergence of GD in Non-linear Neural Networks: An Adversarial Robustness Perspective
Guy Smorodinsky, Sveta Gimpleson, Itay Safran

Abstract
We study the convergence dynamics of Gradient Descent (GD) in a minimal binary classification setting, consisting of a two-neuron ReLU network and two training instances. We prove that even under these strong simplifying assumptions, while GD successfully converges to an optimal robustness margin, effectively maximizing the distance between the decision boundary and the training points, this convergence occurs at a prohibitively slow rate, scaling strictly as Θ ⁡ ( 1 / ln ⁡ ( t ) ) \Theta(1/\ln(t)) . To the best of our knowledge, this establishes the first explicit lower bound on the convergence rate of the robustness margin in a non-linear model. Through empirical simulations, we further demonstrate that this inherent failure mode is pervasive, exhibiting the exact same tight convergence rate across multiple natural network initializations. Our theoretical guarantees are derived via a rigorous analysis of the GD trajectories across the distinct activation patterns of the model. Specifically, we develop tight control over the system’s dynamics to bound the trajectory of the decision boundary, overcoming the primary technical challenge introduced by the non-linear nature of the architecture.

Introduction

The implicit bias of optimization algorithms has become a central pillar of deep learning theory in the effort to better understand the generalization capabilities of neural networks. It is now well-understood that continuing optimization long after achieving perfect classification can significantly improve standard generalization performance. Furthermore, research has increasingly shown that this over-training can also enhance a model’s adversarial robustness—its resilience against imperceptible perturbations designed to induce misclassification
[
1
,
19
]
. Gradient descent (GD) remains the fundamental algorithm for training these models; however, its theoretical analysis is often confounded by the non-differentiability of ReLU activations and the complexities of discrete step-size tuning. To bypass these technical hurdles, many researchers employ gradient flow (GF) as a continuous-time surrogate. While GF yields more amenable analyses, it often abstracts away the discrete dynamics of actual training. Thus, determining the precise convergence rates of its discrete-time counterpart, GD, remains a critical and non-trivial challenge.

Unlike its discrete counterpart, the implicit bias of GF is significantly better characterized. In the context of homogeneous networks, GF has been shown to maximize the classification margin in the sense of the Karush-Kuhn-Tucker (KKT) conditions
[
10
,
8
]
. Apart from improving generalization
[
11

17
,
3
]

Building on this intuition, recent work has explicitly guaranteed that gradient flow can inherently drive certain networks toward adversarially robust solutions
[
13
]
.

However, it remains an open question whether GD inherently exhibits the same bias. Even when explicit robustness guarantees are established for GF, and in specific regimes where GD and GF are known to align
[
2
]
, GF fundamentally abstracts away computational constraints. Consequently, it remains unclear whether practical algorithms like GD can converge to these robust optima within a tractable number of iterations. Moreover, the introduction of adaptive methods such as Adam complicates this landscape, as their distinct implicit biases may render robustness guarantees derived from GF analysis inapplicable. Despite ample evidence that the convergence rate for linear models is of order

1.1
Related work

Implicit Bias Towards Robust Solutions

The seminal works of
10
and
8
provide a strong characterization of the implicit bias of GF in homogeneous neural networks. Specifically,
10
link the implicit bias in non-linear networks to margin maximization. They prove that for ReLU networks, GF converges in direction to a KKT point of the maximum margin problem (see Subsection
2.2
for a formal definition), a geometric property that inherently corresponds to pushing the network’s decision boundary farther away from the training instances.

Building on this,
8
utilize Kurdyka-Łojasiewicz inequalities to analyze deep homogeneous networks, demonstrating that the network’s parameters and internal features directionally align to maximize this margin, thereby guaranteeing that the margin distribution asymptotically converges. While these topological approaches suggest an implicit regularization toward improved adversarial robustness, they do not yield explicit finite-time convergence guarantees.

Recently,
13
formalized this connection between implicit bias and adversarial robustness by proving that for shallow networks trained on clustered data, the use of polynomial ReLU (pReLU) activations allows gradient flow to implicitly find solutions with certified
\mathcal{O}(1)
adversarial robustness. However, because this guarantee pertains exclusively to the continuous-time dynamics of GF, it inherently disregards computational constraints.

Despite the optimistic guarantees discussed above, the connection between margin maximization in the KKT sense and adversarial robustness is not absolute. For instance,
4
and
12
demonstrate that there exist settings where the implicit bias of GF leads to KKT points that are provably suboptimal in terms of their robustness margin. While these works highlight failure modes in the asymptotic direction of GF, our work exposes an orthogonal computational vulnerability: even in settings where the implicit bias perfectly aligns with the optimal robust decision boundary, the finite-time dynamics of GD prevent convergence in tractable time.

Slow Margin Convergence

As noted in the introduction, there is ample work studying the rate of convergence to an optimal margin
[
18
,
7
,
10
,
16
,
15
,
9
,
6
,
20
]
. While these papers are foundational for the modern understanding of implicit bias, their core quantitative results regarding the convergence rate of the weight vector to the max-margin direction are predominantly derived for linear classifiers. Moreover, these analyses typically bound the growth rate of the weights, which in linear models inherently dictates a slow, logarithmic convergence to the optimal margin. In contrast, our setting is significantly more technically involved. In non-linear models, a logarithmic growth bound on the weights does not trivially preclude fast convergence of the robustness margin, as the non-linearities and bias terms could theoretically allow the decision boundary to rapidly shift to the optimal location. Consequently, existing analyses based solely on parameter growth are inadequate for establishing convergence lower bounds in our setting.

Acceleration Algorithms to Improve Convergence Rate

15
,
6
, and
20
propose alternatives to standard GD designed to accelerate convergence to the optimal margin in the linear setting. Specifically,
15
and
6
prove that NGD, a variant of GD with a step size scaled by the training loss, converges at a faster rate of
\mathcal{O}(1/t)
.
20
further improve this rate to
\mathcal{O}(\exp(-t))
by introducing a novel algorithm, PRGD, that progressively rescales the network parameters to artificially bypass the logarithmic norm growth bottleneck of standard GD. Despite these vastly improved convergence rates, it is not obvious whether such algorithms necessarily enjoy the same implicit bias benefits as GF or unaccelerated GD. Moreover, because these methods scale the step size by a significant quantity, they are highly susceptible to “overshooting” in non-linear loss landscapes. In such scenarios, an excessively large step size can prevent convergence entirely or severely impede optimization by permanently deactivating ReLU neurons.

The Optimization Dynamics of GD

Having characterized the implicit bias of GF, we now analyze the robustness properties of its discrete-time counterpart, GD. To do so, we must first establish the underlying optimization dynamics. Throughout this section, we assume that

5
Exponentially Slow Convergence to Robust Solutions

In this section, we leverage the analytical machinery developed in Section
4
to derive the convergence rate of GD toward a robust solution.

5.1
Computational Intractability of Margin Minimization with GD

We first demonstrate that once both neurons specialize and their activation thresholds are sufficiently close to the instances for which they are inactive, there exists a unique intersection point between the network output and the
x
-axis. This point, denoted by
x^{\star}(t)
at iteration
t
, determines the robustness margin of our model and is defined by:

x^{\star}(t)=\frac{b_{2}^{(t)}-b_{1}^{(t)}}{w_{1}^{(t)}-w_{2}^{(t)}}.

(14)

According to Equation (
13
), the numerator in our expression for
x^{\star}(t)
converges to an initialization-dependent constant. Given that neuron specialization implies
w_{2}^{(t)}<0<w_{1}^{(t)}
, the denominator remains strictly positive. By further characterizing the growth rate of the weights, we demonstrate that their magnitudes are
\Theta(\ln(t))
. Consequently, unless
w_{1}^{(0)}-b_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}+b_{2}^{(0)}=0
(a condition that occurs only on an initialization set of Lebesgue measure zero), the convergence rate of
x^{\star}(t)
to the origin is
\Omega(1/\ln(t))
. More formally, we establish the following main result.

Theorem 5.1
.

Consider the optimization of the objective in Equation (
2
) using GD with a fixed step size
\eta\in(0,0.5]
. Then, for almost all initializations
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}
satisfying
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})<0.5
, we have.

\gamma^{*}-\gamma\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}\right)=\Theta\left(\frac{1}{\ln(t)}\right).

The proof of the above theorem, deferred to Appendix
E.1
, leverages the machinery developed in the previous section to provide a comprehensive characterization of the optimization dynamics within the small-loss regime. Specifically, we establish the eventual convergence of GD while demonstrating that the asymptotic limit governing the decision boundary remains permanently biased away from the optimal robustness margin.

The above result establishes that even under extremely mild assumptions, GD on homogeneous neural networks cannot efficiently optimize the robustness margin. Crucially, this logarithmic rate bottleneck is inherent to the optimization dynamics: the probability of encountering such slow convergence is not merely positive, but equals
1
under any continuous distribution over the initial parameters. This implies that reaching a sufficiently small desired robustness threshold
\varepsilon>0
requires a number of iterations that scales exponentially with
1/\varepsilon
.

5.2
Non-asymptotic Analysis

While the preceding theorem establishes a strong negative result, its asymptotic nature leaves open a practical loophole: it could be that achieving a margin arbitrarily close to the optimum requires prohibitive computational resources, but reaching a practical near-optimal margin is relatively easy. To rule out this possibility, we provide a non-asymptotic analysis of the model’s robustness margin under conditions commonly satisfied by standard initialization schemes
[
5
]
. Concretely, our analysis reveals that under these assumptions, the convergence toward the equilibrium limit in Equation (
13
) is strictly monotone. Because He initialization typically initializes biases to zero, the model trivially begins with an optimal margin. However, any initial imbalance in the weights triggers the dynamics discussed in the previous section, actively driving the margin away from the optimum at a linear rate.

Finally, once the system reaches a step-size equilibrium, the margin begins to gradually improve toward the optimum, but it does so at a prohibitively slow rate.

Formally, we establish the following non-asymptotic lower bound on the robustness margin, demonstrating its prohibitively slow recovery after the initial degradation phase.

Theorem 5.2
.

Consider optimizing the objective in Equation (
2
) using GD with step size
\eta\in(0,0.3]
. Suppose that
w_{1}^{(0)}\in(2.7,3)
,
w_{2}^{(0)}\in(-0.5,0)

b_{1}^{(0)}=b_{2}^{(0)}=0

t\geq 7/\eta

x^{\star}(t)\geq\frac{1}{25+5\ln(1+4\eta t)}.

The proof of the above theorem, deferred to Appendix
E.2
, relies on a careful analysis of the decision boundary dynamics under the specified initialization conditions. Notably, the primary technical challenge arises because merely bounding the individual growth rates of the weights and biases is insufficient for establishing tight control over the decision boundary. Instead, we explicitly track the trajectory of the parameters to exploit the zero-initialization of the biases alongside the initial discrepancy between the weights. This allows us to prove that during the early iterations of the algorithm, the second bias term “escapes” the first, permanently establishing a lower bound on their difference that drives the
\Omega(1/\ln(t))

As a direct consequence of Theorem

Corollary 5.3

Consequently, our model cannot efficiently return a robust predictor with arbitrarily high probability under standard gradient descent. The initialization exhibits a constant probability of trapping the dynamics in a logarithmically slow regime; thus, unlike our previous asymptotic analysis in Subsection

6
Experiments: The Convergence Rate to a Stable System

In this section, we complement our theoretical findings with empirical studies. We investigate the number of iterations required to reach a near-equilibrium state, and we demonstrate that the convergence rates obtained in practice are even more pessimistic than what our theory predicts.

Since our analysis is asymptotic in nature, it does not explicitly capture the transient rate at which the system converges to the equilibrium governed by the updates in Equations (
9
–
12
). Moreover, while Equation (
13
) dictates the limiting behavior of the numerator in the decision boundary expression Equation (
14
), the speed of this convergence is not obvious. In particular, it is possible that while the denominator grows slowly, it could still be much larger than the numerator during early training, potentially keeping the distance from the optimal margin small for a significant duration.

We now turn to study this rate empirically to demonstrate that the convergence to equilibrium is sufficiently rapid, ensuring that the overall margin convergence is primarily governed by the slow growth rate of the denominator. To this end, we conducted
10,000
independent trials of GD with a fixed step size
\eta=0.05
to optimize the objective in Equation (
2
). We employed standard He initialization
[
5
]
, setting
b_{1}^{(0)}=b_{2}^{(0)}=0
and drawing weights from a normal distribution,
w_{1}^{(0)},w_{2}^{(0)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,2)
.

Optimization continued unless a trial failed to achieve a training error below
0.5
within
10,000
iterations. For successful trials, we terminated the process once the equilibrium condition was met:
|w_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}|\leq 0.001
. This simulation reveals that a necessary condition for correctly classifying both data instances under this initialization is that the weights are initialized with appropriate signs:
w_{2}^{(0)}<0<w_{1}^{(0)}
. Consequently, only about a quarter of the trials (specifically precisely those where the initialization fell into the correct quadrant) were able to train beyond the initial
10,000

In all successful trials, GD eventually reached the equilibrium point where
|w_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}|\leq 0.001
, confirming that the system always stabilizes. While most runs converged to this threshold within a few hundreds of thousands of iterations, exceptional cases existed where a significant initial discrepancy in weight magnitudes led to a much slower convergence rate. However, because these are precisely the cases where the bias difference is largest, the convergence to an optimal margin remains significantly slower than in more balanced, stable cases where the difference is more modest.

13
) implies

\lim_{t\to\infty}b_{2}^{(t)}-b_{1}^{(t)}=\frac{1}{2}w_{1}^{(0)}+\frac{1}{2}w_{2}^{(0)}.

Conditioning on each weight having the correct sign for specialization at initialization (
w_{1}^{(0)}>0
and
w_{2}^{(0)}<0
), we let
W_{1}=w_{1}^{(0)}
and
W_{2}=-w_{2}^{(0)}
. In this regime,
W_{1},W_{2}
are independent half-normally distributed random variables with parameter
\sigma^{2}=2
. The asymptotic numerator in Equation (
14
) is thus distributed as
B\sim\frac{1}{2}|W_{1}-W_{2}|
. It is not too difficult to compute the PDF and CDF of the random variable
B
, that are given by

f_{B}(x)\coloneqq 2\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2}\right)\erfc\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)

(15)

and

F_{B}(x)\coloneqq 1-\erfc\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{2}}\right)^{2},

respectively, where
\text{erfc}(x)\coloneqq\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_{x}^{\infty}e^{-t^{2}}\,dt
denotes the complementary error function.

A visualization of this PDF against the empirical values from our simulation is provided in Figure
3
. According to the derived CDF, we have
\Pr[B>1]\approx 0.10069
, indicating that more than 10% of all successful GD runs converge to an equilibrium where the numerator is at least
1
, which is also consistent with our empirical findings. In such instances, achieving a margin that is suboptimal by only
\varepsilon=0.01
would require a denominator magnitude of approximately
100
, which, given the
\Theta(\ln(t))
growth rate, necessitates a prohibitively large number of iterations.

Auxiliary Lemmas

The following lemma bounds the updates of GD once a neuron has specialized over a specific instance.

Lemma B.1
.

Consider the objective in Equation (
2
), and suppose that
w_{1}^{(t)}>0
and
\beta_{1}^{(t)}\in(-1,1)
, and that the GD update at iteration
t
is
w_{1}^{(t+1)}=w_{1}^{(t)}+x
and
b_{1}^{(t+1)}=b_{1}^{(t)}+x
, for some
x>0
. Then,
w_{1}^{(t+1)}>0
and
\beta_{1}^{(t+1)}\in(-1,\beta_{1}^{(t)})
.

Likewise, suppose that
w_{2}^{(t)}<0
and
\beta_{2}^{(t)}\in(-1,1)
, and that the GD update at iteration
t
is
w_{2}^{(t+1)}=w_{2}^{(t)}-x
and
b_{1}^{(t+1)}=b_{1}^{(t)}+x
, for some
x>0
. Then,
w_{2}^{(t+1)}<0
and
\beta_{2}^{(t+1)}\in(\beta_{2}^{(t)},1)
.

Proof.

The invariance of the signs of
w_{1}^{(t)}
and
w_{2}^{(t)}
follows immediately from the monotonicity of their update rules. We now turn to show the monotonicity of the breakpoints towards
\pm 1
. Starting with
\beta_{1}^{(t)}
, let
w>0
and
b\in(-w,w)
, which implies
-\frac{b}{w}\in(-1,1)
. Define

f(x)\coloneqq-\frac{b+x}{w+x}.

To compute the derivative, we rewrite

f(x)=-\frac{b-w+x+w}{w+x}=\frac{w-b}{w+x}-1.

Differentiating with respect to
x
yields

f^{\prime}(x)=\frac{b-w}{(w+x)^{2}}.

Since
b\in(-w,w)
and
w>0
, the numerator is negative, and therefore
f^{\prime}(x)<0
for all
x
, namely
f
is decreasing. This also implies that
f
is lower bounded by its limit when
x\to\infty
. We compute
\lim_{x\to\infty}f(x)=-1
, and we have that

\beta_{1}^{(t+1)}=-\frac{b_{1}^{(t+1)}}{w_{1}^{(t+1)}}=-\frac{b_{1}^{(t)}+x}{w_{1}^{(t)}+x}\in\left(-1,\beta_{1}^{(t)}\right),

where in the second equality uses the update rules for the weights. Likewise, a similar analysis shows that
\beta_{2}^{(t+1)}\in\left(\beta_{2}^{(t)},1\right)
, concluding the proof of the lemma.
∎

The following lemma allows us to exclude many configurations of the architecture in Equation (

.

Consider the objective in Equation (

), and suppose that either one of the following conditions hold for
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(w_{1},b_{1},w_{2},b_{2})
:

•

Neuron 1 is right-active (

) and

w_{2}<0
) and
\beta_{2}\in(-\infty,-1]

•

Neuron 1 is left-active and

\beta_{2}\in[-1,\infty)

•

w_{1}\leq 0
and
w_{2}\geq 0

•

w_{1}=0
and
b_{1}\leq 0
, or
w_{2}=0
and
b_{2}\leq 0
.

Then, we have that
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq 0.5

•

First consider all cases where

•

Consider all remaining cases in which either
w_{1}=0
or
w_{2}=0
.

or
b_{2}\leq 0

), at least one instance is misclassified and

.

•

Consider the remaining cases in which
w_{1}<0
and
w_{2}<0
.

–

Suppose that

. Since both neurons are left-active, we have that

. Since
\beta_{1}\leq 1
and

, we have that
\Phi(\boldsymbol{\theta};x)\leq 0
for all

, which misclassifies
x_{2}
, and therefore
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq 0.5
.

–

If
\beta_{1}>1
and
\beta_{2}\leq-1
, then
\Phi(\boldsymbol{\theta};x_{i})>0
for all
i\in\{1,2\}
, which misclassifies
x_{1}=-1
, and therefore
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq 0.5
.

•

Likewise, consider the remaining cases in which
w_{1}>0
and
w_{2}>0
.

–

Suppose that
\beta_{2}\geq-1
. Since both neurons are right-active, we have that
\Phi(\boldsymbol{\theta};x)=0
for all sufficiently small
x
. Since
\beta_{2}\geq-1

, we have that
\Phi(\boldsymbol{\theta};x)\geq 0
for all
x\geq-1
, which misclassifies
x_{1}
, and therefore
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq 0.5
.

–

If
\beta_{2}<-1
and
\beta_{1}\geq 1
, then
\Phi(\boldsymbol{\theta};x_{i})<0
for all
i\in\{1,2\}
, which misclassifies
x_{2}=1
, and therefore
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\geq 0.5
.

To conclude the derivation so far, we have excluded all cases in which
w_{1}\leq 0
or
w_{2}\geq 0
, except for 6 (
w_{1},w_{2}<0
,
\beta_{1}>1
,
\beta_{2}>-1

w_{1},w_{2}>0
,
\beta_{2}<-1
,
\beta_{1}<1

. In all remaining cases, since the breakpoints

Moving on to the cases where

and
w_{2}<0

•

If
\beta_{1}\geq 1
, then
\Phi(\boldsymbol{\theta};x)\leq 0

The following technical lemma allows us to bound the growth rate of recursions that are repeatedly encountered in our analysis.

Lemma B.3
.

Suppose that
a_{t}
is a sequence such that
a_{t+1}=a_{t}+C_{1}\exp(-C_{2}a_{t})
for some
C_{1}\neq 0
and
C_{2}>0
. Then, if
C_{1}>0
, we have

C_{2}^{-1}\ln\left(\exp(C_{2}a_{0})+tC_{2}C_{1}\right)\leq a_{t}\leq C_{2}^{-1}\ln\left(\exp(C_{2}a_{0})+tC_{2}C_{1}\exp\left(C_{2}C_{1}\exp(-C_{2}a_{0})\right)\right),

and if
C_{1}<0
, we have

-C_{2}^{-1}\ln\left(\exp(C_{2}a_{0})-tC_{2}C_{1}\exp\left(-C_{2}C_{1}\exp(-C_{2}a_{0})\right)\right)\leq a_{t}\leq-C_{2}^{-1}\ln\left(\exp(C_{2}a_{0})-tC_{2}C_{1}\right).

Proof.

First, assume that
C_{1}>0
, and denote
\alpha_{t}\coloneqq\exp(C_{2}a_{t})
. By the recurrence relation, we have

\displaystyle\alpha_{t+1}

\displaystyle=\exp(C_{2}a_{t+1})=\exp\left(C_{2}a_{t}+C_{2}C_{1}\exp(-C_{2}a_{t})\right)

\displaystyle=\alpha_{t}\exp\left(C_{2}C_{1}\exp(-C_{2}a_{t})\right)=\alpha_{t}\exp\left(\frac{C_{2}C_{1}}{\alpha_{t}}\right).

By a standard Taylor expansion argument, we have for all
x\in\mathbb{R}
that
1+x\leq\exp(x)\leq 1+x\exp(x)
. Using these inequalities and the above displayed equation, we arrive at

\alpha_{t}+C_{2}C_{1}\leq\alpha_{t+1}\leq\alpha_{t}+C_{2}C_{1}\exp\left(\frac{C_{2}C_{1}}{\alpha_{t}}\right).

Since
a_{t}
is increasing due to
C_{1}\exp(-C_{2}a_{t})>0
, we have
\alpha_{0}\leq\alpha_{t}
for all
t\geq 0
, and therefore

C_{2}C_{1}\leq\alpha_{t+1}-\alpha_{t}\leq C_{2}C_{1}\exp\left(\frac{C_{2}C_{1}}{\alpha_{0}}\right).

Summing the above for all
t
, the middle expression telescopes and we obtain

tC_{2}C_{1}\leq\alpha_{t}-\alpha_{0}\leq tC_{2}C_{1}\exp\left(\frac{C_{2}C_{1}}{\alpha_{0}}\right).

Adding
\alpha_{0}
and taking the logarithm yields

C_{2}^{-1}\ln\left(\alpha_{0}+tC_{2}C_{1}\right)\leq a_{t}\leq C_{2}^{-1}\ln\left(\alpha_{0}+tC_{2}C_{1}\exp\left(\frac{C_{2}C_{1}}{\alpha_{0}}\right)\right),

and the claim follows by substituting
\alpha_{0}
with its definition.

For the case
C_{1}<0
, consider the sequence
a^{\prime}_{t}=-a_{t}
. Then we have

a^{\prime}_{t+1}=a^{\prime}_{t}-C_{1}\exp(-C_{2}a_{t})=a^{\prime}_{t}+|C_{1}|\exp(-C_{2}a_{t}),

and the lemma follows by applying the same bounds, substituting
a^{\prime}_{t}
with
a_{t}
and
|C_{1}|
with
-C_{1}
, and rearranging.
∎

Appendix C
Proof of Theorem
3.1

Proof.

The proof of the theorem relies on a careful case-by-case analysis, where we will exhaustively go over all possible activations patterns of the neurons and examine them for possible solutions. To do this more cleanly, we will derive new equations that are implied by computing the subgradients and substituting them in Equation (
4
). To this end, we begin with computing the partial derivatives.

We denote the subgradient of the
j^{\textrm{th}}
neuron on the
i^{\textrm{th}}
instance by

g_{i,j}\coloneqq\begin{cases}0,&\text{if }w_{j}x_{i}+b_{j}<0,\\
[0,1],&\text{if }w_{j}x_{i}+b_{j}=0,\\
1,&\text{if }w_{j}x_{i}+b_{j}>0.\end{cases}

For
j\in\{1,2\}
, the partial derivatives are given by

\displaystyle\frac{\partial}{\partial w_{j}}\Phi(\boldsymbol{\theta};x)=v_{j}xg_{i,j},\qquad\frac{\partial}{\partial b_{j}}\Phi(\boldsymbol{\theta};x)=v_{j}g_{i,j}.

Combining these with Equation (
4
), we obtain for all
j

\displaystyle w_{j}\;

\displaystyle=\;v_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}y_{i}x_{i}g_{i,j},

(16)

\displaystyle b_{j}\;

\displaystyle=\;v_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}y_{i}g_{i,j}.

(17)

Since the above equations are implied whenever a certain point
\boldsymbol{\theta}
satisfies the KKT conditions, satisfying them is a necessary condition for satisying KKT. The converse, however, fails to hold as readily seen by examining the trivial all-zero solution. This solution clearly satisfies Equations (
16
,
17
), but fails to satisfy Equation (
5
) since the network computes the zero function under this assignment of weights. In light of this, our strategy will be to exhaustively find all feasible solutions to Equations (
16
,
17
), and subsequently verify their correctness in Equations (
4
–
7
).

Since we have two data instances, each neuron’s breakpoint can reside in five different intervals with distinct activation pattern and subgradient behavior:
(-\infty,-1),-1,(-1,1),1,(1,\infty)
. Moreover, each neuron can be right-active (
w_{j}>0
), left-active (
w_{j}<0
), or constant with no breakpoint (
w=0
). This amounts to 11 distinct patterns for each neuron, and 121 cases to analyze in total. However, many cases can be excluded immediately by considering the training loss attained on the sample. To this end, we invoke Lemma
B.2
, which guarantees that when its conditions hold then the KKT conditions cannot be satisfied. This follows immediately from Equation (
5
), which implies that the training loss is strictly smaller than
0.5
.

In the remainder of the proof, we turn to examine a much smaller number of cases, that are more complex and require a more careful analysis. This part will also include the only feasible solution for our system.

•

We begin with ruling out all
6
cases in which either
w_{1}=0
or
w_{2}=0
. The only such remaining cases are when
b_{1}>0
and
b_{2}>0
. Let
j\in\{1,2\}
be the index satisfying
w_{j}=0
. Then by Equation (
16
), we have
0=v_{j}(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})
. Multiplying this by
v_{j}
yields
\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}=0
. Equation (
6
) then implies that
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=0
, which in turn implies that
\boldsymbol{\theta}=0
by Equation (
4
), but then
\Phi(\boldsymbol{\theta},\cdot)
is the zero function, violating Equation (
5
).

•

We are now left with
6+9=15
cases. Suppose that
w_{1}<0
,
w_{2}<0
and
\beta_{1}>1
. We have 3 remaining cases under this assumption, determined by the value of
\beta_{2}
. However, it suffices to consider only the first neuron to show that these cases are infeasible, as we show next.

Since
w_{1}<0
and active on both neurons, we get

\displaystyle w_{1}\;

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}y_{i}x_{i}g_{i,j}=\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2},

\displaystyle b_{1}\;

\displaystyle=\;\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}y_{i}g_{i,j}=-\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}.

The assumption in this case requires strict activity on both points, and in particular

w_{1}x_{1}+b_{1}>0.

Substituting the expressions for
w_{1},b_{1}
in the above displayed equation yields

w_{1}x_{1}+b_{1}=-(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})+(-\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})=-2\lambda_{1}>0,

(18)

which implies
\lambda_{1}<0
, contradicting Equation (
6
).
Therefore these cases are infeasible.

•

Suppose that
w_{1}>0
and
w_{2}>0
. We have 3 remaining cases under this assumption, determined by the value of
\beta_{1}
. However, it suffices to consider only the second neuron to show that these cases are infeasible, as we show next.

Since
w_{2}>0
and active on both neurons we get

\displaystyle w_{2}\;=\;-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}y_{i}x_{i}g_{i,j}=-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2},

\displaystyle b_{2}\;=\;-\sum_{i=1}^{n}\lambda_{i}y_{i}g_{i,j}=\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}.

The assumption in this case requires strict activity on both points, and in particular

w_{2}x_{2}+b_{2}>0.

Substituting the expressions for
w_{2},b_{2}
in the above displayed equation yields

w_{2}x_{2}+b_{2}=-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}+\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}=-2\lambda_{2}>0,

(19)

which implies
\lambda_{2}<0
, contradicting Equation (
6
).
Therefore these cases are infeasible.

•

Suppose that
w_{1}>0
and
w_{2}<0
. We have 9 remaining cases under this assumption.

–

Suppose that
\beta_{1}<-1
; namely, the first neuron is strictly active on both instances. Despite the opposite orientation of the first neuron, the analysis in this case is identical to the one done in a previous case (see the contradiction derived in Equation (
18
)), and it is thus skipped.

–

Likewise, the analysis in the case
\beta_{2}>1
is identical to the one done in a previous case (see the contradiction derived in Equation (
19
)), and therefore it is also skipped.

–

There are only 4 cases remaining, which include the case with the only feasible solution. We will now rule out two cases.

Suppose that
\beta_{1}\in[-1,1)
and
\beta_{2}\in(-1,1)
. Focusing on the second neuron, we have by our assumption that
|-\frac{b_{2}}{w_{2}}|<1
. Since
w_{2}
is left-active, it must hold that
w_{2}x_{1}+b_{2}>0
and
w_{2}x_{2}+b_{2}<0
, implying that
g_{1,2}=1
and
g_{2,2}=0
. Substituting these subgradients in Equations (
16
,
17
) yields

w_{2}=-\lambda_{1}g_{1,2}-\lambda_{2}g_{2,2}=-\lambda_{1},\qquad b_{2}=\lambda_{1}g_{1,2}-\lambda_{2}g_{2,2}=\lambda_{1}.

In particular, it must hold that
b_{2}=-w_{2}
and therefore
\beta_{2}=1\notin(-1,1)
, contradicting our assumption in this case, hence this activation configuration is infeasible.

–

Suppose that
\beta_{1}\in(-1,1)
and
\beta_{2}=1
. Focusing on the first neuron, we have by our assumption that
|-\frac{b_{1}}{w_{1}}|<1
. Since
w_{1}
is right-active, it must hold that
w_{1}x_{1}+b_{1}<0
and
w_{1}x_{2}+b_{1}>0
, implying that
g_{1,1}=0
and
g_{2,1}=1
. Substituting these subgradients in Equations (
16
,
17
) yields

w_{1}=\lambda_{1}g_{1,1}+\lambda_{2}g_{2,1}=\lambda_{2},\qquad b_{1}=-\lambda_{1}g_{1,1}+\lambda_{2}g_{2,1}=\lambda_{2}.

In particular, it must hold that
b_{1}=w_{1}
and therefore
\beta_{1}=-1\notin(-1,1)
, contradicting our assumption in this case, hence this activation configuration is infeasible.

–

We are now left with the final case, which contains the only feasible solution. Suppose that
\beta_{1}=-1
and
\beta_{2}=1
. In this case, our assumptions imply strong constraints on the weights. In particular,
\beta_{1}=-1
implies
b_{1}=w_{1}
; and
\beta_{2}=1
implies
b_{2}=-w_{2}
. Moreover, since the first neuron is active on
x_{2}
and the second is active on
x_{1}
, we have
g_{1,2}=g_{2,1}=1
; and since both neurons have a subgradient on the remaining instance we have that
g_{1,1}
and
g_{2,2}
can take any value in
[0,1]
.

Applying the above to Equations (
16
,
17
) yields

\displaystyle w_{1}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}g_{1,1}+\lambda_{2}g_{2,1}=\lambda_{1}g_{1,1}+\lambda_{2},

(20)

\displaystyle w_{2}

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{1}g_{1,2}-\lambda_{2}g_{2,2}=-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}g_{2,2},

(21)

\displaystyle b_{1}

\displaystyle=-\lambda_{1}g_{1,1}+\lambda_{2}g_{2,1}=-\lambda_{1}g_{1,1}+\lambda_{2},

(22)

\displaystyle b_{2}

\displaystyle=\lambda_{1}g_{1,2}-\lambda_{2}g_{2,2}=\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}g_{2,2}.

(23)

Plugging the above into the four activation constraints yields

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=w_{1}x_{1}+b_{1}=-w_{1}+b_{1}=-(\lambda_{1}g_{1,1}+\lambda_{2})+(-\lambda_{1}g_{1,1}+\lambda_{2})=-2\lambda_{1}g_{1,1},

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle<w_{1}x_{2}+b_{1}=w_{1}+b_{1}=(\lambda_{1}g_{1,1}+\lambda_{2})+(-\lambda_{1}g_{1,1}+\lambda_{2})=2\lambda_{2},

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle<w_{2}x_{1}+b_{2}=-w_{2}+b_{2}=-(-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}g_{2,2})+(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}g_{2,2})=2\lambda_{1},

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=w_{2}x_{2}+b_{2}=w_{2}+b_{2}=(-\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}g_{2,2})+(\lambda_{1}-\lambda_{2}g_{2,2})=-2\lambda_{2}g_{2,2}.

In particular, the middle equations imply
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2}>0
, which together with the first and last equations forces
g_{1,1}=g_{2,2}=0
. Next, by the middle equations and Equation (
7
), it must hold that
1=w_{1}x_{2}+b_{1}=2\lambda_{2}
and
1=w_{2}x_{1}+b_{2}=2\lambda_{1}
, implying
\lambda_{1}=\lambda_{2}=0.5
. Finally, plugging the lambdas and the subgradients in Equations (
20
–
23
), we obtain the only feasible solution
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(w_{1},b_{1},w_{2},b_{2})=(0.5,0.5,-0.5,0.5)
. Following our derivation, it is straightforward to plug this solution in Equations (
4
–
7
) and verify that all constraints are indeed satisfied.

Since Equations (
4
–
7
) assume a normalized margin, we have that the set of all feasible KKT points is given precisely by

\left\{\left(\alpha,\alpha,-\alpha,\alpha\right):\alpha>0\right\}.

The following proposition establishes that once each neuron ‘specializes’ on its target training instance, the training dynamics become invariant: the activation pattern and the signs of the weights of the neurons remain fixed.

Proposition D.3

Under Assumption
D.1
or Assumption
D.2

\eta>0

\big\{b_{1}^{(t)}\big\}_{t=0}^{\infty}

3.

\big\{w_{2}^{(t)}\big\}_{t=0}^{\infty}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})}{\partial w_{1}^{(t)}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})}{\partial b_{1}^{(t)}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})}{\partial w_{2}^{(t)}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}{(t)})}{\partial b_{2}^{(t)}}

\displaystyle w_{1}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=w_{1}^{(t+1)}+w_{2}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=w_{1}^{(t)}+\eta\exp(-2w_{1}^{(t)}+v_{1}^{(0)})+w_{2}^{(t)}-\eta\exp(2w_{2}^{(t)}-u_{2}^{(0)})

b_{1}^{(t+1)}-b_{1}^{(t)}=E_{t}=w_{1}^{(t+1)}-w_{1}^{(t)}.

Summing over the above from
0
to
t-1
and rearranging yields

b_{1}^{(t)}=w_{1}^{(t)}-w_{1}^{(0)}+b_{1}^{(0)}.

By Lemma
D.4
, we have
w_{1}^{(t)}=\Omega(\ln(t))
, where the Omega notation hides constants that depend only on the initialization and the fixed step size
\eta
. This, along with the above displayed equation, implies that
b_{1}^{(t)}=\Omega(\ln(t))
. Namely, after a constant number of iterations
t_{1}
, we have
b_{1}^{(t_{1})}>0
, and therefore
\beta_{1}^{(t_{1})}<0
.

Likewise, by Equations (
26
,
27
), we have

b_{2}^{(t+1)}-b_{2}^{(t)}=\frac{1}{2}\eta\exp\left(w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}\right)=-w_{2}^{(t+1)}+w_{2}^{(t)}.

Summing over the above from
0
to
t-1
and rearranging yields

b_{2}^{(t)}=-w_{2}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(0)}+b_{2}^{(0)}.

By Lemma
D.5
, we have
-w_{2}^{(t)}=\Omega(\ln(t))
, where the Omega notation hides constants that depend only on the initialization and the fixed step size
\eta
. This, along with the above displayed equation, implies that
b_{2}^{(t)}=\Omega(\ln(t))
. Namely, after a constant number of iterations
t_{2}
, we have
b_{2}^{(t_{2})}>0
, and therefore
\beta_{2}^{(t_{2})}>0
.

Taking
T=\max\{t_{1},t_{2}\}
, the proposition follows after at most
T
iterations.
∎

D.2
Only One Neuron Has Specialized

In this section, we analyze all the cases where one neuron has specialized on its instance, while the other neuron is active on both. We limit ourselves to cases where
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})<0.5
, since these are the cases of interest in our analysis, in which a perfect classification error is obtained.

D.2.1
Right pointing neuron active on both, left pointing neuron specialized

Assumption D.9
.

Given the training set
(x_{1},y_{1})=(-1,-1)
,
(x_{2},y_{2})=(1,1)
, consider the network
\Phi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)};x)=\left[w_{1}^{(0)}x+b_{1}^{(0)}\right]_{+}-\left[w_{2}^{(0)}x+b_{2}^{(0)}\right]_{+}
. We assume that at initialization we have
w_{1}^{(0)}>0
,
w_{2}^{(0)}<0
,
\beta_{1}^{(0)}<-1
and
\beta_{2}^{(0)}\in(-1,1)
, and that
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})<0.5
for all
t\geq 0
.

Proposition D.10
.

Under Assumption
D.9
, consider the optimization dynamics of GD on the exponential loss with a fixed step size
\eta>0
. Then, there exists a constant
T\geq 0
that depends only on
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}
and
\eta
, such that after at most
T
iterations, the network satisfies Assumption
D.1
or Assumption
D.2
.

Proof.

For a network that satisfies Assumption
D.9
, the outputs of the network on the training points are

\displaystyle\Phi(\boldsymbol{\theta},x_{1})=\left[-w_{1}+b_{1}\right]_{+}-\left[-w_{2}+b_{2}\right]_{+}=-w_{1}+b_{1}+w_{2}-b_{2},

\displaystyle\Phi(\boldsymbol{\theta};x_{2})=\left[w_{1}+b_{1}\right]_{+}-\left[w_{2}+b_{2}\right]_{+}=w_{1}+b_{1},

and the training loss is therefore

\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-w_{1}+b_{1}+w_{2}-b_{2}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-w_{1}-b_{1}\right).

(36)

With the above, we can compute the partial derivatives to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial w_{1}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\exp(-w_{1}+b_{1}+w_{2}-b_{2})-\frac{1}{2}\exp(-w_{1}-b_{1}),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial b_{1}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\exp(-w_{1}+b_{1}+w_{2}-b_{2})-\frac{1}{2}\exp(-w_{1}-b_{1}),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial w_{2}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\exp(-w_{1}+b_{1}+w_{2}-b_{2}),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial b_{2}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\exp(-w_{1}+b_{1}+w_{2}-b_{2}).

Thus, the GD updates are given by

\displaystyle w_{1}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=w_{1}^{(t)}+\frac{1}{2}\eta\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\eta\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}-b_{1}^{(t)}\right),

(37)

\displaystyle b_{1}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=b_{1}^{(t)}-\frac{1}{2}\eta\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\eta\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}-b_{1}^{(t)}\right),

(38)

\displaystyle w_{2}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=w_{2}^{(t)}-\frac{1}{2}\eta\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}\right),

(39)

\displaystyle b_{2}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=b_{2}^{(t)}+\frac{1}{2}\eta\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}\right).

(40)

By the above update rules and Lemma
B.1
, we have that the signs of the weights are invariant, as well as the breakpoint
\beta_{2}^{(t)}
. Since
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})<0.5
for all
t\geq 0
, the first neuron cannot turn inactive on
x_{2}
, and we thus deduce that we must remain under the conditions of Assumption
D.9
, or transition to Assumption
D.1
or Assumption
D.2
. We will now show that we cannot remain in Assumption
D.9
for more than a constant number of iterations.

From Equations (
37
,
38
), we get

v_{1}^{(t+1)}=w_{1}^{(t+1)}-b_{1}^{(t+1)}=w_{1}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}+\eta\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}\right)=v_{1}^{(t)}+\eta\exp\left(v_{2}^{(t)}-v_{1}^{(t)}\right),

(41)

and from Equations (
39
,
40
) we obtain

v_{2}^{(t+1)}=w_{2}^{(t+1)}-b_{2}^{(t+1)}=w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}-\eta\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}\right)=v_{2}^{(t)}-\eta\exp\left(v_{2}^{(t)}-v_{1}^{(t)}\right).

(42)

Using the shorthand
S\coloneqq v_{1}^{(0)}+v_{2}^{(0)}
, we observe that

v_{1}^{(t+1)}+v_{2}^{(t+1)}=v_{1}^{(t)}+\eta\exp\left(v_{2}^{(t)}-v_{1}^{(t)}\right)+v_{2}^{(t)}-\eta\exp\left(v_{2}^{(t)}-v_{1}^{(t)}\right)=v_{1}^{(t)}+v_{2}^{(t)}.

By induction, we get that
v_{1}^{(t)}+v_{2}^{(t)}=S
for all
t
. Substituting
v_{2}^{(t)}=S-v_{1}^{(t)}
into the update rule for
v_{1}^{(t)}
in Equation (
41
), we have

v_{1}^{(t+1)}=v_{1}^{(t)}+\eta\exp\left(S-2v_{1}^{(t)}\right)=v_{1}^{(t)}+\eta\exp(S)\exp\left(-2v_{1}^{(t)}\right).

Applying Lemma
B.3
to the above recursion, we have that
v_{1}^{(t)}
increases at a logarithmic rate. In particular, there exists an initialization-dependent constant
T
such that
v_{1}^{(T)}>0
, implying
w_{1}^{(T)}>b_{1}^{(T)}
. Since
w_{1}^{(T)}>0
, we divide by
w_{1}^{(T)}
and multiply by
-1
to obtain

\beta_{1}^{(T)}>-1,

implying that the first neuron is no longer active on
x_{2}
.
∎

Proposition D.18
.

Under Assumption
D.17
, consider the optimization dynamics of GD on the exponential loss. Assume that for any step size
\eta>0
, there exists
T>0
that depends only on
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}
and
\eta
, such that after
T
iterations of GD, we have that at least one neuron turns inactive on a single instance, and .

Proof.

We begin with evaluating
\Phi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)};x)
at the data points
x_{1}=-1
and at
x_{2}=1
. Since both neurons are active on both instances, we have

\displaystyle\Phi\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)};x_{1}\right)=\left[-w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}\right]_{+}-\left[-w_{2}^{(t)}+b_{2}^{(t)}\right]_{+}=-w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)},

\displaystyle\Phi\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)};x_{2}\right)=\left[w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}\right]_{+}-\left[w_{2}^{(t)}+b_{2}^{(t)}\right]_{+}=w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}-w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}.

The loss at iteration
t
is thus given by

\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}-b_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}+b_{2}^{(t)}\right).

(49)

Using the shorthands
A_{t}\coloneqq\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}\right)
and
B_{t}\coloneqq\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}-b_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}+b_{2}^{(t)}\right)
, the partial derivatives can be written compactly as

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})}{\partial w_{1}^{(t)}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}A_{t}-\frac{1}{2}B_{t},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})}{\partial b_{1}^{(t)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}A_{t}-\frac{1}{2}B_{t},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})}{\partial w_{2}^{(t)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}A_{t}+\frac{1}{2}B_{t},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}{(t)})}{\partial b_{2}^{(t)}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}A_{t}+\frac{1}{2}B_{t}.

Using the above to compute the gradient step update for each parameter, we get

\displaystyle w_{1}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=w_{1}^{(t)}+\frac{1}{2}\eta A_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\eta B_{t},

(50)

\displaystyle b_{1}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=b_{1}^{(t)}-\frac{1}{2}\eta A_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\eta B_{t},

(51)

\displaystyle w_{2}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=w_{2}^{(t)}-\frac{1}{2}\eta A_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\eta B_{t},

\displaystyle b_{2}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=b_{2}^{(t)}+\frac{1}{2}\eta A_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\eta B_{t}.

We now bound the difference
v_{1}^{(t)}\coloneqq w_{1}^{(t)}-b_{1}^{(t)}
, by subtracting Equation (
51
) from Equation (
50
) to obtain

v_{1}^{(t+1)}=v_{1}^{(t)}+\eta A_{t}.

(52)

Additionally, summing the gradient updates of the weights and the biases separately, we get

\displaystyle w_{1}^{(t+1)}+w_{2}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=w_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)},

\displaystyle b_{1}^{(t+1)}+b_{2}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=b_{1}^{(t)}+b_{2}^{(t)}.

Recursively applying the above, we arrive at

\displaystyle w_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)},

\displaystyle b_{1}^{(t)}+b_{2}^{(t)}

\displaystyle=b_{1}^{(0)}+b_{2}^{(0)}.

By adding the right-hand-side and subtracting the left-hand-side of the above, we can simplify
A_{t}
as follows

\displaystyle A_{t}

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}\right)=\exp\left(-2w_{1}^{(t)}+2b_{1}^{(t)}+w_{1}^{(0)}-b_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}-b_{2}^{(0)}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(v_{1}^{(0)}+v_{2}^{(0)}\right)\exp\left(-2v_{1}^{(t)}\right).

Plugging the above in Equation (
52
), we obtain the recursion

v_{1}^{(t+1)}=v_{1}^{(t)}+\eta\exp\left(v_{1}^{(0)}+v_{2}^{(0)}\right)\exp\left(-2v_{1}^{(t)}\right).

(53)

By our assumption that both neurons are active on both instances, we have that
b_{1}^{(0)}>0
(since otherwise at least one of
w_{1}^{(0)}x_{1}+b_{1}^{(0)}
and
w_{1}^{(0)}x_{2}+b_{1}^{(0)}
is negative). Moreover, we have that
|w_{1}^{(0)}|<b_{1}^{(0)}
(since otherwise the breakpoint of the first neuron which is
-b_{1}^{(0)}/w_{1}^{(0)}
will be in
[-1,1]
). We therefore have that
v_{1}^{(0)}<0
, and by Equation (
53
) we get that it is monotonically increasing.

Using Lemma
B.3
, we have that
v_{1}^{(t)}-v_{1}^{(0)}\geq C\ln(t)
for all
t\geq 1
and some
C>0
that depends only on
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}
and
\eta
. In particular, for some
T
large enough such that
C\ln(T)\geq v_{1}^{(0)}
, it must hold that
v_{1}^{(T)}\geq 0
, implying
|w_{1}^{(T)}|\geq b_{1}^{(T)}
. Namely, it holds that
-b_{1}^{(T)}/w_{1}^{(T)}\in[-1,1]
, and that the first neuron has turned inactive on one of the instances, concluding the proof of the proposition.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

The assumption

–

Starting from the case where each neuron is active on one instance, we compute

at

\displaystyle=\left[-w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}\right]_{+}-\left[-w_{2}^{(t)}+b_{2}^{(t)}\right]_{+}=w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)},

\displaystyle\Phi(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)},x_{2})

With the above, we compute the partial derivatives as follows

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})}{\partial b_{1}^{(t)}}

Now suppose that the first neuron is active on both instances, and the second neuron is only active on

With the above, we can compute the partial derivatives to obtain

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial w_{2}}

Plugging the above in Equation (

Suppose that the first neuron is active on

\displaystyle\Phi(\boldsymbol{\theta},x_{1})

\displaystyle=\left[-w_{1}+b_{1}\right]_{+}-\left[-w_{2}+b_{2}\right]_{+}=w_{2}-b_{2},

With the above, we can compute the partial derivatives to obtain

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\exp(w_{2}+b_{2}-w_{1}-b_{1}),

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial b_{1}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial w_{2}}

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})}{\partial b_{2}}

\ell\left(-\Phi\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t+1)};x_{1}\right)\right)=\exp\left(w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}-\eta\exp\left(w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}\right)\right),

Suppose that both neurons are active on both instances. Then the outputs of the network on the training points are given by

\displaystyle\Phi\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)};x_{1}\right)=\left[-w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}\right]_{+}-\left[-w_{2}^{(t)}+b_{2}^{(t)}\right]_{+}=-w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)},

\displaystyle\Phi\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)};x_{2}\right)=\left[w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}\right]_{+}-\left[w_{2}^{(t)}+b_{2}^{(t)}\right]_{+}=w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}-w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}.

\mathcal{L}\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}\right)+\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}-b_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}+b_{2}^{(t)}\right).

(57)

Using the shorthands
A_{t}\coloneqq\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}\right)

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})}{\partial w_{1}^{(t)}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}A_{t}-\frac{1}{2}B_{t},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})}{\partial b_{1}^{(t)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}A_{t}-\frac{1}{2}B_{t},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})}{\partial w_{2}^{(t)}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}A_{t}+\frac{1}{2}B_{t},

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}{(t)})}{\partial b_{2}^{(t)}}

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}A_{t}+\frac{1}{2}B_{t}.

Using the above to compute the gradient step update for each parameter, we get

\displaystyle w_{1}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=w_{1}^{(t)}+\frac{1}{2}\eta A_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\eta B_{t},

\displaystyle b_{1}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=b_{1}^{(t)}-\frac{1}{2}\eta A_{t}+\frac{1}{2}\eta B_{t},

\displaystyle w_{2}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=w_{2}^{(t)}-\frac{1}{2}\eta A_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\eta B_{t},

\displaystyle b_{2}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=b_{2}^{(t)}+\frac{1}{2}\eta A_{t}-\frac{1}{2}\eta B_{t}.

Substituting the above in Equation (
57

\displaystyle\ell\left(\Phi\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t+1)};x_{1}\right),y_{1}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t+1)}+b_{1}^{(t+1)}+w_{2}^{(t+1)}-b_{2}^{(t+1)}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}+b_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}-2\eta A_{t}\right)

\displaystyle=\exp\left(-2\eta A_{t}\right)\cdot\ell\left(\Phi\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)};x_{1}\right),y_{1}\right),

Likewise, we can verify that

\ell\left(\Phi\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t+1)};x_{2}\right),y_{2}\right)=\exp\left(-2\eta B_{t}\right)\cdot\ell\left(\Phi\left(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)};x_{2}\right),y_{2}\right),

and therefore
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t+1)})<\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})<0.5
.

Fix any
\eta\in(0,0.5]
, and define the GD map
G:\mathbb{R}^{4}\to\mathbb{R}^{4}
by
G(\boldsymbol{\theta})\coloneqq\boldsymbol{\theta}-\eta\nabla\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
. We will show that if
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})<0.5
then this is an analytic open map by analyzing its Jacobian,
J_{G}
. We have

J_{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=I_{4}-\eta\nabla^{2}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}),

where
I_{4}
is the four-dimensional identity matrix. For ease of notation, let
\theta_{1}\coloneqq w_{1}
,
\theta_{2}\coloneqq b_{1}
,
\theta_{3}\coloneqq w_{2}
and
\theta_{4}\coloneqq b_{2}
. We now bound the entries of the Hessian as follows: For any activation pattern and any
i\in\{1,2\}
,
\Phi(\boldsymbol{\theta};x_{i})
computes an affine transformation of the form
\sum_{j=1}^{4}\xi_{i,j}\theta_{j}
for some
\xi_{i,j}\in\{-1,0,1\}
,
j\in[4]
. From this, we obtain that
G
is analytic (it is the difference between the identity map and a composition between an affine transformation and an exponent) wherever it is differentiable, and we also have that for all
j_{1},j_{2}\in[4]
,

\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{j_{1}}\partial\theta_{j_{2}}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})=\frac{1}{2}\xi_{1,j_{1}}\xi_{1,j_{2}}\exp\left(\Phi\left(\boldsymbol{\theta};x_{1}\right)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\xi_{2,j_{1}}\xi_{2,j_{2}}\exp\left(-\Phi\left(\boldsymbol{\theta};x_{2}\right)\right).

Bounding the above in absolute value yields

\left|\frac{\partial^{2}}{\partial\theta_{j_{1}}\partial\theta_{j_{2}}}\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})\right|\leq\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(\Phi\left(\boldsymbol{\theta};x_{1}\right)\right)+\frac{1}{2}\exp\left(-\Phi\left(\boldsymbol{\theta};x_{2}\right)\right)=\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta})<0.5.

Thus, for
\eta\in(0,0.5]
we have that
J_{G}(\boldsymbol{\theta})
is strictly diagonally dominant, and hence non-singular. This implies that
G
is an open map on any domain where it is differentiable.

We now prove Items (
1
–
5
) by induction. First, we have that
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})<0.5
holds by our assumption, and that if Item
5
holds for
t
then it also holds for
t+1
by the previous case analysis.

By removing the measure zero set where Items (
1
–
4
) are violated, we have that the induction base holds for
t=0
. For the induction step, assume that these items hold for
t
. Since
\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})<0.5
, we have that
G
is an analytic open map, and its composition with itself
t+1
times,
G^{t+1}\coloneqq G\circ\ldots\circ G
, is also an analytic open map. Thus, the maps

•

\beta_{1,\pm}^{t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})\coloneqq G_{1}^{t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})\pm G_{2}^{t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})
,

•

\beta_{2,\pm}^{t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})\coloneqq G_{3}^{t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})\pm G_{4}^{t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})
,

•

S^{t+1}\coloneqq G_{1}^{t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})-G_{2}^{t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})+G_{3}^{t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})+G_{4}^{t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})
,

•

G_{1}^{t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})
and
G_{3}^{t+1}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})
,

where
G^{k+1}_{j}
returns the
j^{\textrm{th}}
coordinate of
G^{t+1}
, are all analytic. Moreover, none of these can be the zero map, since
G^{t+1}
is an open map, and thus any open set is mapped by
G^{t+1}
to an open set
U
. Let
\boldsymbol{\theta}=(w_{1},b_{1},w_{2},b_{2})\in U
be arbitrary, we can choose
\varepsilon>0
small enough such that
(w_{1}+\varepsilon,b_{1},w_{2},b_{2})\in U
, and thus either one of
w_{1}-b_{1}+w_{2}+b_{2}\neq 0
or
w_{1}+\varepsilon-b_{1}+w_{2}+b_{2}\neq 0
hold, and thus
S^{t+1}
cannot collapse an open set to zero, and it is not the zero map. A similar argument proves that the remaining maps are not the zero map either, and therefore it holds that the zero-set (the preimage of zero) of these maps has measure zero
[

]
. By removing these measure-zero sets from the set of valid initializations for
\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}
, we have that Items (
1
–
4
) hold. Finally, by taking an infinite yet countable union over the measure-zero sets removed at each iteration of GD, we are removing a set of measure zero from the set
\{\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)}:\mathcal{L}(\boldsymbol{\theta}^{(0)})<0.5\}
, which concludes the proof of the proposition.
∎

D.5
we get

w_{1}^{(t)}-w_{2}^{(t)}\leq\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\exp\left(2w_{1}^{(0)}\right)+2\eta C_{1}\exp\left(v_{1}^{(0)}\right)\cdot t\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\exp\left(-2w_{2}^{(0)}\right)+2\eta\exp\left(-u_{2}^{(0)}\right)C_{2}\cdot t\right)

where
C_{1}=\exp\left(\eta\exp\left(v_{1}^{(0)}\right)\exp\left(-2w_{1}^{(0)}\right)\right)
and
C_{2}=\exp\left(\eta\exp\left(-u_{2}^{(0)}\right)\exp\left(2w_{2}^{(0)}\right)\right)
.
Since
b_{1}^{(0)}=b_{2}^{(0)}=0
we can simplify the constant to get

w_{1}^{(t)}-w_{2}^{(t)}\leq\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\exp\left(2w_{1}^{(0)}\right)+2\eta C_{1}\exp\left(w_{1}^{(0)}\right)\cdot t\right)+\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\exp\left(-2w_{2}^{(0)}\right)+2\eta\exp\left(-w_{2}^{(0)}\right)C_{2}\cdot t\right)

where
C_{1}=\exp\left(\eta\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(0)}\right)\right)
and
C_{2}=\exp\left(\eta\exp\left(w_{2}^{(0)}\right)\right)
.
Merging the
\ln
functions we get

w_{1}^{(t)}-w_{2}^{(t)}\leq\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\left[\exp\left(2w_{1}^{(0)}\right)+2\eta C_{1}\exp\left(w_{1}^{(0)}\right)\cdot t\right]\left[\exp\left(-2w_{2}^{(0)}\right)+2\eta\exp\left(-w_{2}^{(0)}\right)C_{2}\cdot t\right]\right)

Factor the leading exponential in each term:

\displaystyle\exp\left(2w_{1}^{(0)}\right)+2\eta C_{1}\exp\left(w_{1}^{(0)}\right)\cdot t=\exp\left(2w_{1}^{(0)}\right)\left(1+2\eta C_{1}\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(0)}\right)\cdot t\right)

\displaystyle\exp\left(-2w_{2}^{(0)}\right)+2\eta\exp\left(-w_{2}^{(0)}\right)C_{2}\cdot t=\exp\left(-2w_{2}^{(0)}\right)\left(1+2\eta\exp\left(w_{2}^{(0)}\right)C_{2}\cdot t\right)

Plugging it in the bound we get

w_{1}^{(t)}-w_{2}^{(t)}\leq\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\left[\exp\left(2w_{1}^{(0)}-2w_{2}^{(0)}\right)\right]\left[1+2\eta C_{1}\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(0)}\right)\cdot t\right]\left[1+2\eta\exp\left(w_{2}^{(0)}\right)C_{2}\cdot t\right]\right).

Once again by using logarithm rules we get

w_{1}^{(t)}-w_{2}^{(t)}\leq w_{1}^{(0)}-w_{2}^{(0)}+\frac{1}{2}\ln\left(\left[1+2\eta C_{1}\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(0)}\right)\cdot t\right]\left[1+2\eta\exp\left(w_{2}^{(0)}\right)C_{2}\cdot t\right]\right).

Using the inequality
(1+A)(1+B)\leq(1+\max\{A,B\})^{2}
for all
A,B>0
we get

w_{1}^{(t)}-w_{2}^{(t)}\leq w_{1}^{(0)}-w_{2}^{(0)}+\ln\left(1+\eta At\right).

where
A=\max\{2C_{1}\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(0)}\right),2C_{2}\exp\left(w_{2}^{(0)}\right)\}
.
Using the assumptions that
w_{1}^{(0)}\in(0,3)
and
w_{2}^{(0)}\in(-2,0)
we get
w_{1}^{(0)}-w_{2}^{(0)}<5
and
A<2\exp\left(\eta\right)
and thus

w_{1}^{(t)}-w_{2}^{(t)}\leq 5+\ln\left(1+2\exp\left(\eta\right)\eta t\right).

∎

The following lemma shows that under a zero biases assumption and a tamed step size, the convergence rate of the system to a balanced GD step for both neurons is monotone.

Lemma E.3
.

Under Assumption
D.1
, suppose that we optimize the objective in Equation (
2
) using GD with step size
\eta\in(0,0.5]
. Further assume that
b_{1}^{(0)}=b_{2}^{(0)}
. Then,

\lim_{t\to\infty}b_{2}^{(t)}-b_{1}^{(t)}=\lim_{t\to\infty}w_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}=\frac{v_{1}^{(0)}+u_{2}^{(0)}}{2}.

where the convergence is monotone.

Proof.

First, by Lemma
D.7
we have that the limits hold, and therefore it is only left to prove monotonicity. Since
w_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{1}^{(t)}+b_{2}^{(t)}=v_{1}^{(0)}+u_{2}^{(0)}
for all
t\geq 0
by Equations (
24
–
27
), it suffices to prove monotonicity for just the sum of the weights.

For the sake of brevity, we define
S_{t}\coloneqq w_{1}^{(t)}+w_{2}^{(t)}
. Compute

\displaystyle S_{t+1}

\displaystyle=w_{1}^{(t+1)}+w_{2}^{(t+1)}

\displaystyle=w_{1}^{(t)}+\eta\exp(-2w_{1}^{(t)}+v_{1}^{(0)})+w_{2}^{(t)}-\eta\exp(2w_{2}^{(t)}-u_{2}^{(0)})

\displaystyle=S_{t}+\eta\left[\exp(-2w_{1}^{(t)}+v_{1}^{(0)})-\exp(2w_{2}^{(t)}-u_{2}^{(0)})\right]

Define
D_{t}\coloneqq w_{1}^{(t)}-w_{2}^{(t)}>0
. We can rewrite
w_{1}^{(t)}=\frac{1}{2}(S_{t}+D_{t}),\penalty\ w_{2}^{(t)}=\frac{1}{2}(S_{t}-D_{t})
, and thus we can also rewrite
S_{t+1}-S_{t}
using
v_{1}^{(0)},u_{2}^{(0)},S_{t}
and
D_{t}
as follows

\displaystyle S_{t+1}-S_{t}

\displaystyle=\eta\left[\exp(-2w_{1}^{(t)}+v_{1}^{(0)})-\exp(2w_{2}^{(t)}-u_{2}^{(0)})\right]

\displaystyle=\eta\left[\exp(-S_{t}-D_{t}+v_{1}^{(0)})-\exp(S_{t}-D_{t}-u_{2}^{(0)})\right]

\displaystyle=\eta\exp(-D_{t})\left[\exp(v_{1}^{(0)}-S_{t})-\exp(S_{t}-u_{2}^{(0)})\right]

\displaystyle=\eta\exp(-D_{t})g(S_{t})

where
g(s)\coloneqq\exp(v_{1}^{(0)}-s)-\exp(s-u_{2}^{(0)})
. We notice that if
S_{t}=S^{\star}=\frac{v_{1}^{(0)}+u_{2}^{(0)}}{2}
, then
S_{t+1}=S_{t}
, implying that this is a fixed point. Since
\eta\exp(-D_{t})>0
, the only term that affects the sign of
S_{t+1}-S_{t}
is
g(S_{t})
. If
S_{t}<S^{\star}
then
g(S_{t})<0
which implies that
S_{t+1}>S_{t}
; and if
S_{t}>S^{\star}
, then
g(S_{t})>0
, which implies that
S_{t+1}<S_{t}
. Assume without loss of generality that
S_{t}<S^{\star}
(the other direction is symmetric). We have that
g(s)
is decreasing since

g^{\prime}(s)=-\exp(v_{1}^{(0)}-s)-\exp(s-u_{2}^{(0)})<0.

Denote
L\coloneqq\sup_{s\in[S_{0},S^{\star}]}|g^{\prime}(s)|
. We will now compute
L
. We have
g^{\prime\prime}(s)=\exp(v_{1}^{(0)}-s)-\exp(s-u_{2}^{(0)})
. Equating this to zero to find the extrema points, we get that
S^{\star}
is an extremum point of
g^{\prime}(s)
. Since

g^{(3)}(s)=-\exp(v_{1}^{(0)}-s)-\exp(s-u_{2}^{(0)})<0,

then
S^{\star}
is a maximum point of
g^{\prime}(s)
. But since
g^{\prime}(s)<0
, then
S^{\star}
is a minimum point of
|g^{\prime}(s)|
. This implies that the maximum point in the interval must be attained at the boundary, thus

L=\max\{\exp(v_{1}^{(0)}-S_{0})+\exp(S_{0}-u_{2}^{(0)}),\exp(v_{1}^{(0)}-S^{\star})+\exp(S^{\star}-u_{2}^{(0)})\}.

Since
g(S)
is
L
-Lipschitz, we get

\displaystyle|g(S_{t})-g(S^{\star})|\leq L|S_{t}-S^{\star}|.

But
g(S)
is also decreasing, and
g(S^{\star})=0
and
g(S_{t})\geq g(S^{\star})=0
, so we get that

\displaystyle g(S_{t})

\displaystyle=g(S_{t})-g(S^{\star})\leq L(S^{\star}-S_{t}),

and thus by the above equation we compute

S_{t+1}-S_{t}=\eta\exp(-D_{t})g(S_{t})\leq\eta L(S^{\star}-S_{t})\exp(-D_{t}).

Rearranging yields

S_{t+1}<\eta L\exp(-D_{t})S^{\star}+(1-\eta L\exp(-D_{t}))S_{t},

and further simplifying, we have

S_{t+1}-S^{\star}<(1-\exp(-D_{t})\eta L)(S_{t}-S^{\star}).

Combining Assumption
D.1
and Proposition
D.3
, we have that
w_{1}^{(t)}>0
and increases, and that
w_{2}^{(t)}<0
and decreases, hence
\exp(-D_{t})
decreases, and we can upper bound it by
\exp(-D_{0})=\exp(w_{2}^{(0)}-w_{1}^{(0)})
.
Plugging this upper bound in the above displayed equation, we get

S_{t+1}-S^{\star}<(1-\eta L\exp(w_{2}^{(0)}-w_{1}^{(0)}))(S_{t}-S^{\star}).

(58)

Next, we upper bound
L
as follows. The assumption that
b_{1}^{(0)}=b_{2}^{(0)}=0
yields

\exp\left(v_{1}^{(0)}-S^{\star}\right)+\exp(S^{\star}-u_{2}^{(0)})=2\exp\left(\frac{w_{2}^{(0)}-w_{1}^{(0)}}{2}\right)\leq\exp\left(w_{2}^{(0)}-w_{1}^{(0)}\right).

Similarly, we have

\exp\left(v_{1}^{(0)}-S_{0}\right)+\exp\left(S_{0}-u_{2}^{(0)}\right)=\exp\left(-w_{2}^{(0)}\right)+\exp\left(w_{1}^{(0)}\right).

Thus, when the above is the maximum in
L
, we have

\frac{\exp(w_{1}^{(0)}-w_{2}^{(0)})}{L}=\left(\exp\left(w_{2}^{(0)}\right)+\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(0)}\right)\right)^{-1}\geq\frac{1}{2},

and if the maximum in
L
is attained by the second argument, we have

\frac{\exp(w_{1}^{(0)}-w_{2}^{(0)})}{L}\geq 1.

Therefore, in both cases, we have by our assumption that
\eta\leq 0.5\leq\frac{\exp(w_{1}^{(0)}-w_{2}^{(0)})}{L}
, implying that
1-\eta L\exp(w_{2}^{(0)}-w_{1}^{(0)})\geq 0
, and thus by Equation (
58
) and by using the assumption that
S_{t}<S^{\star}
, we conclude that

S_{t+1}\in(S_{t},S^{\star}].

∎

The following proposition shows that under a zero biases assumption, the difference between the biases throughout optimization increases at a linear rate to a constant that depends on the sum of the weights.

Proposition E.4
.

Under Assumption
D.1
, suppose that we optimize the objective in Equation (
2
) using GD with step size
\eta\in(0,0.3]
. Suppose that
w_{2}^{(0)}<0<w_{1}^{(0)}
,
w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}>1
and
b_{1}^{(0)}=b_{2}^{(0)}=0
. Then,

b_{2}^{(t)}-b_{1}^{(t)}\geq\frac{w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{11}-\frac{1}{10}

for all
t\geq t_{0}
, where

t_{0}\coloneqq\left\lfloor\frac{w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2\eta\exp\left(\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}\right)}\right\rfloor.

Consider
\frac{1}{2}\eta\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}-b_{1}^{(t)}\right)
. By the monotonicity of the weights in Proposition
D.3
,

\frac{1}{2}\eta\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(t)}-b_{1}^{(t)}\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\eta\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(0)}-b_{1}^{(0)}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\eta\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(0)}\right)

for all
t
, so

b_{1}^{(t)}\leq b_{1}^{(0)}+t\cdot\frac{1}{2}\eta\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(0)}\right)=t\cdot\frac{1}{2}\eta\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(0)}\right).

(59)

On the other hand, as long as
w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}\geq\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}
, then the growth rate of
b_{2}^{(t)}
can be lower bounded as follows

b_{2}^{(t)}=b_{2}^{(0)}+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i=0}^{t-1}\eta\exp\left(w_{2}^{(i)}-b_{2}^{(i)}\right)\geq t\cdot\frac{1}{2}\eta\exp\left(\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}\right),

(60)

and we also have by subtracting Equation (
27
) from Equation (
26
) that

w_{2}^{(t+1)}-b_{2}^{(t+1)}=w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}-\eta\exp\left(w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}\right)\leq w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}-\eta\exp\left(\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}\right),

which recursively implies that

w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}\leq w_{2}^{(0)}-b_{2}^{(0)}-t\eta\exp\left(\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}\right)=w_{2}^{(0)}-t\eta\exp\left(\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}\right).

By the above,
w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}
decreases at a linear rate if it is lower bounded, which entails that a necessary condition for
w_{2}^{(t)}-b_{2}^{(t)}\geq\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}
to hold is

\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}\leq w_{2}^{(0)}-t\eta\exp\left(\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}\right).

Solving for
t
, we have

t\leq\frac{w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2\eta\exp\left(\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}\right)}.

(61)

The assumptions
w_{2}^{(0)}<0<w_{1}^{(0)}
and
w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}>1
imply
-w_{1}^{(0)}<w_{2}^{(0)}
, yielding that at the first iteration we have

w_{2}^{(0)}-b_{2}^{(0)}=w_{2}^{(0)}\geq\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2},

thus Equation (
61
) holds for at least one iteration. Moreover, since our assumptions also entail

\frac{w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2\eta\exp\left(\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}\right)}>\frac{1}{2\eta\exp\left(\frac{-1+0}{2}\right)}>\frac{0.3}{\eta}\geq 1,

hence we can take the floor function of the right-hand side in Equation (
61
),

\frac{w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2\eta\exp\left(\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}\right)}-1\leq t_{0}\coloneqq\left\lfloor\frac{w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2\eta\exp\left(\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}\right)}\right\rfloor\leq\frac{w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2\eta\exp\left(\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}\right)},

(62)

and multiply by it since it is strictly positive.
Subtracting Equation (
59
) from Equation (
60
) yields

b_{2}^{(t)}-b_{1}^{(t)}\geq t\cdot\frac{1}{2}\eta\exp\left(\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}\right)-t\cdot\frac{1}{2}\eta\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(0)}\right).

Substituting
t
with
t_{0}
, we have

b_{2}^{(t_{0})}-b_{1}^{(t_{0})}\geq\frac{1}{2}\eta t_{0}\exp\left(\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\eta t_{0}\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(0)}\right).

(63)

Lower bounding the first summand in the right-hand side of the above using Equation (
62
), we have

\frac{1}{2}\eta t_{0}\exp\left(\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}\right)\geq\frac{w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{4}-\frac{1}{2}\eta\exp\left(\frac{-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}\right)\geq\frac{w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{4}-0.1,

(64)

where in the last inequality, we used
\eta\leq 0.3
and
-w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}<1

We now lower bound the second summand in Equation (
63

-\frac{1}{2}\eta t_{0}\exp\left(-w_{1}^{(0)}\right)\geq-\frac{w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{4}\exp\left(-\frac{w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{2}\right)\geq-\frac{w_{1}^{(0)}+w_{2}^{(0)}}{4}\exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\right).

Plugging the above and Equation (

Finally, we use the monotonicity of

E.3
