ICG-based NIR fluorescence imaging can help to detect residual giant cell tumor of bone after intralesional curettage: Results of two-year follow-up
Authors: Han Wang, Yi Yang, Huayi Qu, Siyi Huang, Ruifeng Wang, Xu Liu, Wei Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimIdentification of clinical occult tumor residuals is crucial during giant cell tumor (GCT) intralesional curettage. This study investigated the capacity of indocyanine green (ICG) fluorescence imaging to detect tumor residual during intralesional curettage of GCT.MethodsClinical data of 20 patients with GCT who received ICG near-infrared (NIR) fluorescence-assisted intralesional curettage with a minimal two-year follow-up in our institute were reviewed. Tumors were initially curetted under white light with naked eyes. Then, wounds were scanned to detect fluorescence residuals. Biopsies were conducted on the sites with fluorescence positivity or negativity. The fluorescence intensity (FI) of biopsied samples were determined using standardized process, and samples were sent for pathological examination.ResultsAll the tumors were successfully stained (100%). One patient (5%) experienced a local recurrence. The overall accuracy of ICG fluorescence in detecting tumor residuals (true positivity and true negativity) was 68%. The false positive rate and false negative rate were 38% and 21%, respectively. Fluorescence-positive samples showed a significantly higher rate of pathological positivity than negative samples (P = 0.001). The meanFI and maxFI of samples with pathological positivity were significantly higher than those with pathological negativity (P value was 0.001 and 0.002, respectively). The AUCROC values for meanFI and maxFI predicting pathological results were 0.713 and 0.719, respectively.ConclusionICG-based NIR fluorescence imaging can detect residual GCT after intralesional curettage, and can be used as a supplement to extended curettage to reduce recurrence. The FI is positively related to pathological results. However, false signals may occur. Efforts to increase accuracy and confirm its clinical benefits in preventing recurrence are still needed.Keywords: Indocyanine green, Giant cell tumor, Intralesional curettage, Quantitative analysis, Tumor residuals

Introduction

Giant cell tumor (GCT) is a kind of primary bone tumor with high local aggressiveness that usually affect young adults, accounting for 20% of all benign bone tumors [1]. To preserve limb function as much as possible, intralesional curettage is the first treatment for GCT [2]. However, it is usually associated with a higher recurrence rate [3].

Identification and location of clinical occult tumor residuals is crucial to reduce recurrence during intralesional curettage. However, effective methods are lacking. Application of frozen sections is limited due to the need for long-term decalcification of the bone tissue. Currently, surgeons primarily rely on conventional visual and tactile information to identify tumor residuals, which can be subjective and unreliable [4]. As such, new methods to identify tumor residuals intraoperatively are urgently needed.

Indocyanine green (ICG)-based near-infrared (NIR) fluorescence imaging has been widely applied in the resection of many tumor [5], [6], [7]. The mechanism underlying ICG-tumor labeling is thought to include an enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect. Prior studies, including ours, have shown that ICG has a high stain rate for bone and soft tissue tumors, with potential for detecting residual tumors, especially during piecemeal resection [8], [9]. However, the utility of this technique in GCT, which is treated by intralesional curettage, is unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to explore:

(1)clinical results of ICG-based NIR assisted intralesional curettage for GCT;

clinical results of ICG-based NIR assisted intralesional curettage for GCT;

(2)the accuracy of ICG fluorescence imaging detecting tumor residual;

the accuracy of ICG fluorescence imaging detecting tumor residual;

(3) whether fluorescence intensity (FI) is associated with results of pathology examination of residual tumor.

Methods

Study design and ethics

This retrospective single-institute study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and was approved by the Ethics Committee of our institute (2023PHB441). All patients provided informed consents.

Patients

The clinical data of twenty patients with GCT who received ICG NIR fluorescence-assisted intralesional curettage in our institute from August 2022 to December 2023 were reviewed. All diagnoses were confirmed by preoperative biopsy. The mean age of enrolled patients was 37 ± 12y (mean ± SD), with an average body weight of 73 ± 13 kg (mean ± SD). The median follow-up duration was 32 m (range:24-40 m). Very short therapy of preoperative denosumab (4 doses or less) were applied in some patients to reduce blood supply or preserve the articular surface and at the surgeons' discretion. No patient received more than 4 courses of denosumab. General information of patients is summarized in Table 1. The baseline of biopsied samples is summarized in Table 2.

Drug administration

ICG (Ruida Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Dandong, China) was intravenously administrated at a dose of 2 mg/kg on the night before surgery. The drug was dissolved into 250 mL of normal saline and administered using a light-proof infusion apparatus. The time course of administration was 60 min. The median time interval between the end of drug administration and start of operation was 11 h (Q1, Q3: 9 h,13 h; range:7-22 h). All patients were confirmed to have normal liver function and no history of allergy to iodine before administration.

Surgical procedure with intraoperative fluorescence guidance

Extended intralesional curettage was routinely conducted by senior surgeons in our center. The surgical approach was determined according to lesion anatomy and surgeons' preferences. A window was first created on the cortex, after which the tumor was directly exposed. Subsequently, the tumor surface was scanned using the H3800 system (Digital Precision Medicine Co., Ltd., Beijing, China, Supplementary 1) to confirm that the tumor could be successfully stained (Fig. 1. A —C). Gross tumor was then curetted under white light with the naked eye. After tumor removal, the cavity wall was smoothed down using a high-speed burr, with electrocautery performed as inactivation therapy. This was only applied for tumors located in the extremities, not for tumors located in the sacrum, to protect the presacral soft tissue and sacral nerves.

Subsequently, the surgeon inspected the wounds and confirmed that all tumors had been satisfactorily removed under white light with the naked eye. Subsequently, the wounds were scanned to detect fluorescence residuals with the fluorescence-white fusion mode of H3800 system. Intraoperative fluorescence residuals were confirmed by two independent surgeons. A third surgeon was consulted in case of disagreement. Biopsies were conducted on the sites classified as fluorescence positive and negative after careful selection (Fig. 1.D). Under the guidance of fluorescence imaging, extended curettage and electrocauterization were applied on sites which showed as fluorescence positive until the fluorescence completely disappeared or the curettage area can no longer be further expanded (for example, it will damage the joint).

Selection of biopsy sites

To avoid unnecessary damage to the normal joint and bone/soft tissue, all biopsies were confirmed to not damage the load-bearing ability of the bone, or the stability of the joint. For the selection of fluorescence positive sites, the biopsied sites must not be located in the subchondral bone, or crucial ligaments. For fluorescence negative sites, the biopsied sites must be cancellous bone or soft tissue located 5 -10 mm from the sites of fluorescence positivity. The number of biopsies with fluorescence negativity was based on the number of biopsies with fluorescence positivity. For 0–2 and 3–5 pieces of biopsied tissue with fluorescence positivity, one and two pieces of tissue with fluorescence negativity were biopsied, respectively. The minimum diameter of samples was controlled to be greater than 2 mm. Biopsy was performed using a curette or rongeur, according to intraoperative convenience.

Standardized fluorescence imaging of biopsied samples

A standardized process to obtain standard fluorescence image of biopsied samples was established to avoid influence of the environmental light and the angle and distance between camera and samples. A laparoscopic surgery simulator (Lap Game, Mandaodaxin Science, CO., Ltd., Hangzhou, China) was used to establish a black box by blocking all holes with opaque tapes (Supplementary 2.A). The camera and light source of the H3800 system were confirmed to be firmly fixed to this box (Supplementary 2.B). A test bottle capable of filling the vision of the H3800 system with 10 mL of standard ICG solution (concentration, 10 μg/mL with 10% fetal bovine serum, No.086–15, Wisent Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Quebec, Canada) was initially imaged in this system (Supplementary 2.C-D). The fluorescence intensity (FI) of the test bottle showed an attenuated trend from the center to periphery (Supplementary 2.E-F). Image J software (version 1.48; U.S. National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) was used to identify the area with 90% FI with central area defined as 100% FI (Supplementary 2.G-H). The former area, approximately half the central area of the whole field-of-view of the H3800 system, was marked by two quarter lines (Supplementary 2.I). The excitation light intensity was set to the maximum value during all fluorescence imaging.

Quantitative fluorescence analysis of biopsied samples

Standard fluorescence images of all biopsied samples were obtained in the operating room using the above-mentioned black box within 20 min of sample collection to avoid fluorescence quenching. Samples were oriented ensure the side most suspected to contain tumor residuals pointed upward. Samples were placed within the marked two quarter lines of the H3800 system. Images under black and white fluorescence mode and fluorescence-white fusion mode were captured. The outline of sample was selected as the region of interest (ROI) with Image J software on fluorescence-white fused images, and this ROI was then used to register the sample area on black and white fluorescence images (Supplementary 3). The meanFI and maxFI were determined in the black and white fluorescence images using Image J.

Pathological examination of biopsied samples

After standardized fluorescence imaging, all biopsied samples were fixed in the 10% formalin and sent to the Pathology Department of our institute for pathological examination according to clinical routine. The diagnosis of pathological positivity or negativity were made by senior pathologists. Representative pathological images are shown in Fig. 2. Pathologists were blinded to all information of intraoperative fluorescence imaging and FI analysis.

Postoperative treatment and follow-up

All patients were followed up according to routine protocol, which included X-ray, CT, and MRI every six months after surgery. One patient with a lesion located in the femoral head were administrated with six doses of denosumab (120 mg per 4 weeks) postoperatively. Other patients did not receive denosumab therapy postoperatively.

Statistical analysis

Based on the results of Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and Shapiro-Wilk test, meanFI and maxFI did not follow normal distribution. Thus, the Mann-Whitney test was used to detect differences in meanFI and maxFI between different groups. The overall accuracy was defined as the ratio of total number of samples with true positivity and true negativity to the total number of all samples. The Youden index was calculated using the following formula: sensitivity+specificity-1. The method of Clopper and Pearson was used to calculate the 95% confidence intervals of single proportion [10]. Chi-square test was used to detect of differences in four-fold table data. The area under the curve of the receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve was calculated to determine the predictive ability of meanFI and maxFI. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 19.0(IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). The level of significance was 0.05. All P values were obtained from two-sided tests.

Results

Tumor recurrence and complications

Among the 20 enrolled patients, one patient (5%) experienced a local recurrence, which occurred 30 months postoperatively. This patient received wide resection of tumor and endoprosthesis reconstruction. One patient underwent reoperation due to bone resorption at 14 months postoperatively. No ICG-related complications were observed. All patients with extremity tumors preserved satisfactory joint function, whereas patients with sacral tumors may develop neurological dysfunction depending on the intraoperative status of sacral nerve injury.

We reviewed the preoperative imaging and intraoperative fluorescence imaging of this patient. The preoperative CT scan showed a small area of bone destruction located in the posterior cortex of femur. This site showed a residual fluorescence in the intraoperative fluorescence imaging after curettage and electrocauterization, while no tumor residue was visible to the naked eye. The CT scan at the time of recurrence suggested that this lesion likely contributed to the recurrence (Fig. 3).

Efficacy of ICG fluorescence in detecting tumor residuals

All the tumors were successfully stained. Overall, all patients had fluorescence-positive samples, and 80% (16/20) of patients had pathologically-positive samples. A total of 73 samples were harvested from 20 patients, of which 34 (47%) and 39 (53%) were pathologically-positive and negative, respectively. No difference in baseline characteristics between the two groups of samples was detected (Table 1). The level of agreement between the two surgeons on intraoperative fluorescence of samples was 81% (59/73); 80% for fluorescence positivity (36/45) and 82% for fluorescence negativity (23/28). The number of biopsies taken from each patient was 3.6 ± 1.8 (mean ± SD, range: 2–7). Samples with fluorescence positivity showed higher rate of pathological positivity than those with fluorescence negativity (62% vs 22%, P = 0.001, Table 2,). The overall accuracy of ICG fluorescence in detecting tumor residuals (true positivity and true negativity) was 68% (Table 3). The false positive rate and false negative rate were 38% and 21%, respectively.

Diagnostic value of FI in detecting tumor residuals

The meanFI and maxFI of biopsied samples with intraoperative fluorescence positivity were significantly higher than those with negativity (Fig. 4 A-B). Similarly, the meanFI and maxFI of biopsied samples with pathological positivity were significantly higher than those with negativity (Fig. 4.C-D). The AUCROC of the meanFI and maxFI for predicting pathological outcome was 0.713 and 0.719, respectively (Fig. 4.E).

The cutoff value with the highest Youden index of the meanFI and maxFI obtained from ROC, were 3.59 and 16, respectively. These two cutoff values were then used to calculate 2 × 2 tables (Table 3). The Youden index of meanFI and maxFI in predicting pathology were 0.31 and 0.45, respectively. No significant effect of preoperative denosumab was found on the intraoperative fluorescence, maxFI, and meanFI analysis.

Discussion

An effective method to detect small tumor residuals during intralesional curettage of GCT is currently lacking. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report the long-term follow-up results of NIR technique application in treatments of GCT. We found that ICG-based NIR fluorescence imaging can detect tumor residuals during intralesional curettage of GCT and may reduce recurrence with 2-year follow-up results. Quantitative analysis of FI and ROC further confirmed the predictive value of ICG fluorescence, indicating that the FI was positively related to pathological results.

This work has some limitations. Firstly, the control group is lacked and sample size is small. However, by comparing with the recurrence rate of traditional intralesional curettage reported in the previous literature including ours, a preliminary conclusion can still be drawn. Secondly, a median follow-up of 32 months may fail to capture very late recurrence cases. Furthermore, the extension of the tumor within soft tissues around the bone was not properly evaluated, which may be an important cause of the recurrent case.

NIR fluorescence-guided surgery is a promising method, providing surgeons with another observational dimension in addition to conventional visual and tactile information [4], [11]. This technique has been used in many types of tumors [5], [12], [13], [14]. ICG is the most commonly used fluorescent contrast at present due to its good safety and cheap price [15]. The difference of distribution in tumor and normal tissue following intravenous administration is the basis of ICG fluorescence-guided surgery [11], [15]. However, the accumulation of ICG in tumor tissue primarily relies on the EPR effect, which is based on histologically-increased capillary permeability and lymphatic reflux obstruction. This is a non-tumor specific passive process, and therefore may generate false signals.

The application of NIR fluorescence in surgery is also associated with some technical issues. The original fluorescence signal captured by the camera is a black and white image which is vague and not suitable for surgical practice [16], [17]. Images must be fused with a white light image to observe anatomy. The process of fusion is complicated and remains a problem in optics [18], [19], requiring precise registration and proper enhancement of weak fluorescence signals. The results of enhancement are dynamic and relevant to the mean intensity of all pixels in the field-of-view to obtain the most comfortable image to the human eyes. Improper fusion may generate imaging error, instability and strobing. Therefore, to avoid the influence of fusion processing, the diagnostic value of this technique was not only confirmed by intraoperative fluorescence but also by analysis of FI with original image in this study.

The recurrence rate of this study (5%) was lower than the recurrence rate of in previous literatures including ours (16% -25%) [20], [21], [22], indicating that this technique may reduce recurrence after intralesional curettage of GCT. Brookes et al. also reported low recurrence (0/3) of GCT using this technique despite some minor differences in technical details [23]. Their findings are consistent with this study, strongly suggesting that this technique can serve as an adjuvant method to reduce the local recurrence rate.

As mentioned above, false signals may occur. The possible reason for false positivity may be the over enhancement of weak signals and passive accumulation of ICG in tumor-free area. While false negativity may be caused by the presence of tumor residuals too small to be detected by this technique. The former reason explains the high sensitivity but relative low specificity of intraoperative fluorescence. While the latter may explain the high specificity but relative low sensitivity of FI as the optimal cutoff value may be elevated by some high FI of tumor-free samples. This also explains the overlapping MaxFI in pathologically positive and negative samples.

Given the small sample size, the impact of denosumab on FI still requires further confirmation, despite the balanced baseline of samples and negative results of subgroup analysis. Although in the authors' experience, the intraoperative imaging was hardly influenced by denosumab due to the enhancement of fluorescence by imaging processing [8], a confounding effect is still possible because denosumab can reduce the tumor blood supply and thus theoretically reducing ICG retention.

Based on our results, the clinical practice of this technique is raised. For safe sites which carry no risk of damage to load-bearing and joint stability, radical extended curettage and further inactivation are recommended on all fluorescent residuals. For unsafe sites that may influence load-bearing function and joint stability, we recommend making thoughtful decisions by taking both fluorescence imaging and conventional tactile and visual information into account. It should be noted that fluorescence is only a supplementary method due to the non-tumor specific labeling mechanism of ICG. Conventional information should always be considered during the entire decision-making process. It should also be noted that over-resection of unnecessary sites and insufficient resection of small residuals may still occur, even if combining all information.

The standardization of fluorescence interpretation is important. Kulkarni et al. reported a 38% discrepancy between ICG fluorescence imaging and clinical judgment in emergency surgery [24]. Although under non-emergency condition, we still observed a disagreement of 19%between two surgeons in interpreting the fluorescence signals, suggesting that human factors may influence the interpretation of fluorescence imaging. Therefore, we tried to quantitatively measure the FI to standardize this technique and eliminate the influence of human factors. Although methods in the literature differ [25], [26], [27], [28], [29], standardization of imaging conditions and analysis of imaging pixels with software is universal. FI analysis can eliminate observational bias from surgeon's judgment of intraoperative fluorescence, reflecting the objective relationship between fluorescence signals and pathological results [28]. Similar findings to ours, showing a positive relation of FI and pathology has been reported in gastric cancer [26], [28] and colorectal cancer [29]. The accuracy in GCT seems worse than lung and GI tumors [14], [26], [28], [29] but superior to that in ovarian tumor [6], which may be related to the differences in the metabolism of tumor and peritumor tissue.

In conclusion, ICG-based NIR fluorescence imaging can help detect residual GCT after intralesional curettage, and be used as supplement to extended curettage to reduce recurrence. The FI is positively related to pathological results. However, false signals may occur. Efforts to increase accuracy, such as through intraoperative rapid analysis of FI, artificial intelligence-assisted image interpretation and processing [30], and confirmation of clinical benefits of recurrence with larger multicenter studies are still needed.
