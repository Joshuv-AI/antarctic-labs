The genome sequence of Jasione montana L., 1753 (Asterales: Campanulaceae)
Authors: Maarten J. M. Christenhusz, Ilia J. Leitch, Michael F. Fay, Royal Botanic Gardens Kew Genome Acquisition Lab, Plant Genome Sizing Collective, Darwin Tree of Life Barcoding collective, Wellcome Sanger Institute Tree of Life Management, Samples and Laboratory team, Wellcome Sanger Institute Scientific Operations: Sequencing Operations, Wellcome Sanger Institute Tree of Life Core Informatics team, Tree of Life Core Informatics collective, Darwin Tree of Life Consortium, Richard Michelmore, Kuntal Dey, Panagiotis Madesis, D Blaine Marchant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We present a genome assembly of Jasione montana (sheep’s-bit; Streptophyta; Magnoliopsida; Asterales; Campanulaceae). The assembly consists of two haplotypes with total lengths of 1 282.03 megabases and 1 294.56 megabases. Most of haplotype 1 (98.9%) is scaffolded into 6 chromosomal pseudomolecules. Haplotype 2 was assembled to scaffold level. The mitochondrial sequence has a length of 321.12 kilobases and the plastid genome assembly has a length of 200.38 kilobases. This assembly was generated as part of the Darwin Tree of Life project, which produces reference genomes for eukaryotic species found in Britain and Ireland.

Background

As part of the Darwin Tree of Life Project, a collaborative effort to sequence all named eukaryotic species in the Atlantic Archipelago of Britain and Ireland, we sequenced the genome of sheep’s-bit, Jasione montana L. Here we present a chromosomally complete genome sequence based on a wild specimen from Trefor, Gwynedd, Wales, UK.

Jasione montana is an annual or biennial herb although in Britain and Ireland, it is normally a partially winter-green biennial. The plant itself is pubescent with oblong to oblanceolate leaves and its inflorescence comprises a congested terminal head of tubular, actinomorphic flowers with a blue corolla ( Stace, 2010). When bruised, all plant parts have a strong, unpleasant smell ( Grieve, 1994).

The native range of J. montana is Europe and north-west Africa. It also occurs as an introduction in the Azores and the eastern USA. It includes three subspecies: one (subsp. montana) is widespread, whereas subsp. cornuta (Ball) Greuter & Burdet is endemic to Morocco, and subsp. paivae Horjales & Rubido is endemic to Spain ( POWO, 2026). In Britain, the stronghold of subsp. montana is now in coastal western Britain, having disappeared from many central and eastern sites during the 20th century. In Ireland, it is predominantly coastal ( BSBI, 2026).

In coastal sites, it is a plant of sea cliffs, maritime grasslands and stabilised sand dunes on acidic, shallow, well-drained soils, whereas inland sites include dry heathland, stone walls, hedge banks and railway cuttings. Open sites and recently burnt ground are also frequently colonised. Overall, it is predominantly a lowland species, occurring from sea level to nearly 1 000 m in Co. Kerry, Ireland ( BSBI, 2026).

The flower heads superficially resemble those found in species of scabious ( Scabiosa L. and Succisa Haller; Caprifoliaceae), and an alternative common name for J. montana is sheep’s-bit scabious (e.g. North Wales Wildlife Trust, 2026). However, the flowers can be distinguished by their fused stamens (in contrast to free in Scabiosa and Succisa).

The sporophytic chromosome count has been reported as 2 n = 12 (e.g. Henniges et al., 2022). Most of the assembly presented here is scaffolded into 6 chromosomal pseudomolecules, consistent with the reported chromosome count. This study provides a genomic resource for comparative phylogenomics and classification in Campanulaceae.

Methods

Sample acquisition, flow cytometry and DNA barcoding

A specimen of Jasione montana (specimen ID KDTOL10350, ToLID daJasMont1; Figure 1) was used for genome sequencing. It was collected from Trefor, Gwynedd, Wales, UK (latitude 52.9938, longitude −4.4403) on 2021-08-12. The specimen was collected and identified by Maarten Christenhusz (Herbarium voucher: M. Christenhusz 9269 (K)).

Photographs showing the habitat and habit of the Jasione montana (daJasMont1) specimen from which samples were taken for genome sequencing.

The genome size was estimated by flow cytometry following the ‘one-step’ method outlined in Pellicer et al. (2021) and using propidium iodide as the fluorochrome. The General Purpose Buffer (GPB) supplemented with 3% PVP was used for isolation of nuclei ( Loureiro et al., 2007), and the internal calibration standard was Petroselinum crispum ‘Champion Moss Curled’ with an assumed 1C-value of 2 200 Mb ( Obermayer et al., 2002).

The initial identification was verified by an additional DNA barcoding process according to the framework developed by Twyford et al. (2024). Part of the plant specimen was preserved in silica gel desiccant ( Chase & Hills, 1991). DNA extracted from the dried plant was amplified by PCR for standard barcode markers, with the amplicons sequenced and compared to public sequence databases including GenBank and the Barcode of Life Database (BOLD) ( Ratnasingham & Hebert, 2007). Following whole genome sequence generation, the relevant DNA barcode region was also used alongside the initial barcoding data for sample tracking at the WSI ( Twyford et al., 2024). The standard operating procedures for Darwin Tree of Life barcoding are available on protocols.io.

Nucleic acid extraction

Protocols for high molecular weight (HMW) DNA extraction developed at the Wellcome Sanger Institute (WSI) Tree of Life Core Laboratory are available on protocols.io ( Howard et al., 2025). The daJasMont1 sample was weighed and triaged to determine the appropriate extraction protocol. For HMW DNA extraction, 52 mg of flower tissue was used. Tissue from the flower was homogenised by cryogenic bead beating. Sheared DNA was purified by automated SPRI (solid-phase reversible immobilisation), using AMPure PB beads (Pacific Biosciences) and the Thermo Fisher KingFisher™ Apex to eliminate shorter fragments and concentrate the DNA. The concentration of the sheared and purified DNA was assessed using a Nanodrop spectrophotometer and Qubit Fluorometer using the Qubit dsDNA High Sensitivity Assay kit. Fragment size distribution was evaluated by running the sample on the FemtoPulse system. For this sample, the final post-shearing DNA had a Qubit concentration of 20.8 ng/μL and a yield of 2 808.00 ng. The 260/280 spectrophotometric ratio was 1.79, and the 260/230 ratio was 1.48. The Genomic Quality Number (GQN) was 5.4.

PacBio HiFi library preparation and sequencing

Library preparation and sequencing were performed at the WSI Scientific Operations core. Libraries were prepared using the SMRTbell Prep Kit 3.0 (Pacific Biosciences) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The kit includes reagents for end repair/A-tailing, adapter ligation, post-ligation SMRTbell bead clean-up, and nuclease treatment. Size selection and clean-up were performed using diluted AMPure PB beads (Pacific Biosciences). DNA concentration was quantified using a Qubit Fluorometer v4.0 (ThermoFisher Scientific) and the Qubit 1X dsDNA HS assay kit. Final library fragment size was assessed with the Agilent Femto Pulse Automated Pulsed Field CE Instrument (Agilent Technologies) using the gDNA 55 kb BAC analysis kit.

The sample was sequenced on a Revio instrument (Pacific Biosciences). The prepared library was normalised to 2 nM, and 15 μL was used for making complexes. Primers were annealed and polymerases bound to generate circularised complexes, following the manufacturer’s instructions. Complexes were purified using 1.2X SMRTbell beads, then diluted to the Revio loading concentration (200–300 pM) and spiked with a Revio sequencing internal control. The sample was sequenced on a Revio 25 M SMRT cell. The SMRT Link software (Pacific Biosciences), a web-based workflow manager, was used to configure and monitor the run and to carry out primary and secondary data analysis.

Hi-C

Sample preparation and crosslinking

Hi-C data were generated from the leaf tissue of daJasMont1 using the Arima-HiC v2 kit (Arima Genomics). Tissue was finely ground using the Covaris cryoPREP Dry Pulverizer (Covaris), and then subjected to nuclei isolation. Nuclei were isolated using a modified protocol based on the Qiagen QProteome Cell Compartment Kit (Qiagen), in which only the Lysis and CE2 buffers were used, with QIAshredder spin columns. After isolation, nuclei were fixed using formaldehyde to a final concentration of 2% to crosslink the DNA. The crosslinked DNA was then digested and biotinylated according to the manufacturer’s instructions. A clean-up step was performed with SPRIselect beads before library preparation. DNA concentration was quantified using the Qubit Fluorometer v4.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and the Qubit HS Assay Kit, following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Hi-C library preparation and sequencing

Biotinylated DNA constructs were fragmented using a Covaris E220 sonicator and size selected to 400–600 bp using SPRISelect beads. DNA was enriched with Arima-HiC v2 kit Enrichment beads. End repair, A-tailing, and adapter ligation were carried out with the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs), following a modified protocol where library preparation occurs while DNA remains bound to the Enrichment beads. Library amplification was performed using KAPA HiFi HotStart mix and a custom Unique Dual Index (UDI) barcode set (Integrated DNA Technologies). Depending on sample concentration and biotinylation percentage determined at the crosslinking stage, libraries were amplified with 10–16 PCR cycles. Post-PCR clean-up was performed with SPRISelect beads. Libraries were quantified using the AccuClear Ultra High Sensitivity dsDNA Standards Assay Kit (Biotium) and a FLUOstar Omega plate reader (BMG Labtech).

Prior to sequencing, libraries were normalised to 10 ng/μL. Normalised libraries were quantified again to create equimolar and/or weighted 2.8 nM pools. Pool concentrations were checked using the Agilent 4200 TapeStation (Agilent) with High Sensitivity D500 reagents before sequencing. Sequencing was performed using paired-end 150 bp reads on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000.

Genome assembly

Prior to assembly of the PacBio HiFi reads, a database of k-mer counts ( k = 31) was generated from the filtered reads using FastK. GenomeScope2 ( Ranallo-Benavidez et al., 2020) was used to analyse the k-mer frequency distributions, providing estimates of genome size, heterozygosity, and repeat content.

The HiFi reads were assembled using Hifiasm in Hi-C phasing mode ( Cheng et al., 2021, 2022), producing two haplotypes. Hi-C reads ( Rao et al., 2014) were mapped to the primary contigs using bwa-mem2 ( Vasimuddin et al., 2019). Contigs were further scaffolded with Hi-C data in YaHS ( Zhou et al., 2023), using the --break option for handling potential misassemblies. The scaffolded assemblies were evaluated using Gfastats ( Formenti et al., 2022), BUSCO ( Manni et al., 2021) and MerquryFK ( Rhie et al., 2020). The organelle genomes were assembled using OATK ( Zhou et al., 2025).

Assembly curation

The assembly was decontaminated using the Assembly Screen for Cobionts and Contaminants ( ASCC) pipeline. TreeVal was used to generate the flat files and maps for use in curation. Manual curation was conducted primarily in PretextView and HiGlass ( Kerpedjiev et al., 2018). Scaffolds were visually inspected and corrected as described by Howe et al. (2021). Manual corrections included 20 breaks, 31 joins, and removal of 21 haplotypic duplications. This reduced the scaffold count by 6.4%, reduced the scaffold N50 by 47.7%, and reduced the total assembly length by 0.6%. The curation process is described at https://gitlab.com/wtsi-grit/rapid-curation . PretextSnapshot was used to generate a Hi-C contact map of the final assembly.

Assembly quality assessment

The MerquryFK tool ( Rhie et al., 2020) was run in a Singularity container ( Kurtzer et al., 2017) to evaluate k-mer completeness and assembly quality for both haplotypes using the k-mer database ( k = 31) computed prior to genome assembly. The analysis outputs included assembly QV scores and completeness statistics.

The genome was analysed using the BlobToolKit pipeline, a Nextflow implementation of the earlier Snakemake version ( Challis et al., 2020). The pipeline aligns PacBio reads using minimap2 ( Li, 2018) and SAMtools ( Danecek et al., 2021) to generate coverage tracks. It runs BUSCO ( Manni et al., 2021) using lineages identified by querying NCBI datasets ( O’Leary et al., 2024). For the three domain-level lineages, BUSCO genes are aligned to the UniProt Reference Proteomes database ( Bateman et al., 2023) using DIAMOND blastp ( Buchfink et al., 2021). The genome is divided into chunks based on the density of BUSCO genes from the closest taxonomic lineage, and each chunk is aligned to the UniProt Reference Proteomes database with DIAMOND blastx. Sequences without hits are chunked using seqtk and aligned to the NT database with blastn ( Altschul et al., 1990). The BlobToolKit suite consolidates all outputs into a blobdir for visualisation. The BlobToolKit pipeline was developed using nf-core tooling ( Ewels et al., 2020) and MultiQC ( Ewels et al., 2016), with containerisation through Docker ( Merkel, 2014) and Singularity ( Kurtzer et al., 2017).

Genome sequence report

Sequence data

The genome of a specimen of Jasione montana was sequenced using Pacific Biosciences single-molecule HiFi long reads, generating 43.11 Gb (gigabases) from 3.57 million reads, which were used to assemble the genome. GenomeScope2.0 analysis estimated the haploid genome size at 1 359.34 Mb, with a heterozygosity of 2.09% and repeat content of 63.35% ( Figure 2). Using flow cytometry, the genome size (1C-value) of the sample was estimated to be 1.81 pg, equivalent to 1 770.00 Mb. These estimates guided expectations for the assembly. Based on the estimated genome size, the sequencing data provided approximately 30× coverage. Hi-C sequencing produced 330.69 Gb from 1 095.01 million reads, which were used to scaffold the assembly. This data note provides useful documentation on the sequencing and assembly of Jasione montana as part of the Darwin Tree of Life Project. As the genomes of thousands of species are assembled, it is important to have the associated metadata available as well as assemblies that are as accurate and complete as the data allow.

The metadata provided in the introduction is good; it would be useful to have a little more taxonomic context, particularly relative to other assembled genomes in the family and species diversity of this genus.

The materials and methods are straightforward and standard. It is unclear whether, fresh, frozen, or dried material was used for making the PacBio and HiC libraries. It is not stated the size of fragments that the DNA was sheared to for HiFi sequencing and the size distribution of the libraries. The mean read length of the HiFi reads is also useful because this greatly impacts the ease of assembly. The version of Hifiasm used should be stated.

This genome is of high quality and exceeds the QC metrics of the Earth BioGenome project. The amount of polishing seems to have been minimal. Downloading the assembly and HiFi reads reveals that there are reads that span several of the gaps and therefore a little polishing could improve the assembly further.

Importantly, there is a problem with the telomeres. Canonical telomeric repeats are absent on one or both end of chromosome 4, 5, 6. One of the telomeric repeats on the ends of chromosome 1 (OZ060879) has to be incorrect because there is a similar repeat on both ends, whereas these repeats should be complementary.

They claim that it is “chromosomally complete”. It would be more accurate to claim “chromosomally near-complete” until there is a gapless T2T assembly. There is no mention of the NORs. Hifiasm typically collapses these arrays into a small number of repeats representing only a fraction of the NORs. Also, how many non-organellar contigs are not in the chromosomal scaffolds? There are usually some; these need to be mentioned and documented.

No attempt has been made at annotation, which would considerably increase the value of the assembly. There are tools such as Liftoff and Helixer than can provide useful draft annotations without a lot of effort.

In summary, this is a good data report that like the assembly could be improved with a little more polishing.

We confirm that we have read this submission and believe that we have an appropriate level of expertise to confirm that it is of an acceptable scientific standard, however we have significant reservations, as outlined above.

This Data Note presents a high-quality chromosome-level genome assembly of Jasione montana generated as part of the Darwin Tree of Life Project. The manuscript is well structured, follows the standard format adopted for Tree of Life genome reports, and provides comprehensive information regarding specimen collection, sequencing, assembly, quality assessment, and data availability. The genome assembly represents a valuable genomic resource for future comparative genomic, phylogenetic, and evolutionary studies within Campanulaceae and related taxa.

Some suggestions below

The Background section could briefly expand on the broader biological significance of Jasione montana, particularly its ecological importance or relevance within Campanulaceae, to further highlight the value of the genomic resource beyond its inclusion in the Darwin Tree of Life initiative.

The discrepancy between the flow cytometry genome size estimate (1,770 Mb) and the assembled genome size (1,282 Mb) is reported but only briefly discussed. Although such differences are common in highly repetitive plant genomes, one or two additional sentences commenting on possible causes (e.g. repeat-rich regions, collapsed repeats or heterozygosity) would improve interpretation for readers.

The BUSCO analysis reports approximately 9.2% duplicated genes. While this does not diminish the quality of the assembly, a brief comment explaining whether this likely reflects biological heterozygosity, residual haplotypic duplication or lineage-specific gene duplication would provide additional context.
