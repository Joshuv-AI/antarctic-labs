Comparing the i31-SLNB and the MIA Nomogram for Sentinel Lymph Node Biopsy Positivity Prediction in Cutaneous Melanoma: A Prospective Cohort Analysis
Authors: Rohit Sharma, Rajan P Kulkarni, Richard Essner, Andrew Ward, Andrew H Lewis, Shireen Guide, J Michael Guenther
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionSentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) for cutaneous melanoma (CM) provides essential prognostic information and is a component of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging; however, up to 88% of SLNBs performed are negative. This means most patients who undergo the procedure do not benefit but are exposed to additional costs and surgery-associated morbidities. Improved tools are needed to identify patients at low risk of sentinel lymph node (SLN) positivity for whom the procedure is unnecessary and can be avoided. Here, we compared the sentinel lymph node positivity predictive accuracy of the integrated 31-gene expression profile (GEP) for sentinel lymph node biopsy (i31-SLNB) and the Melanoma Institute Australia (MIA) nomogram in patients with CM enrolled in the prospective DECIDE study who underwent SLNB.MethodsData from patients with T1–T4 tumors and i31-SLNB test results from a prospective, multicenter study were evaluated. Area under the curve (AUC) was used to compare the discriminative performance of the i31-SLNB and MIA nomogram in predicting SLN positivity. Observed SLN positivity rates in patients with conflicting risk predictions were examined.ResultsThe analysis included 912 patients, of whom 430 were SLN-assessed. The i31-SLNB predicted < 5% risk of SLN positivity more often than the MIA nomogram and observed positivity was below 5% for the i31-SLNB (2.6%, negative predictive value [NPV] = 97.4%, 95% CI 94.2–100.0%) but not the MIA nomogram (5.8%, NPV = 94.2%, 95% CI 88.1–98.7%). The AUC for the i31-SLNB was 0.74, significantly higher than that of the MIA nomogram (0.61; p = 0.001), demonstrating better discriminative performance. At 5% and 10% risk thresholds, when the tools conflicted in their risk predictions, the i31-SLNB was accurate, with observed SLN positivity rates of 1.9% and 2.8% below each threshold and 11.5% and 25.8% above each threshold.ConclusionsThe i31-SLNB more accurately stratifies patients under consideration for SLNB into groups based on risk of SLN positivity than the clinicopathologic-only MIA nomogram.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13555-026-01819-6.Keywords: Cutaneous melanoma, Sentinel lymph node biopsy, 31-gene expression profile

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Up to 88% of sentinel lymph node biopsies (SLNB) for cutaneous melanoma (CM) are negative, while 15% of patients experience surgery-associated morbidities. Therefore, tools to predict sentinel lymph node (SLN) positivity and decrease unnecessary SLNBs are needed.Here, we compare the accuracy of a clinicopathologic-based nomogram, the Melanoma Institute Australia (MIA) and the integrated 31-gene expression profile for SLNB (i31-SLNB), which combines the 31-GEP score with clinicopathologic factors.What was learned from the study?The i31-SLNB more accurately identified patients at high and low risk of SLN positivity than the MIA nomogram.Using i31-SLNB to aid SLNB decision-making can help low-risk patients avoid unnecessary SLNB, decreasing associated morbidities.

Introduction

Sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB) for cutaneous melanoma (CM) is a primary component of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system [1]. A positive SLNB is prognostic for poor outcomes, including tumor recurrence and death [2]. Up to 88% of SLNBs are negative, meaning that the majority of patients might be able to avoid this unnecessary procedure and associated costs. Moreover, of those undergoing the procedure, 15% experience surgery-associated morbidities, including infection, seroma, hematoma, and lymphedema [3–5], and up to 16% of patients with a negative SLNB still experience tumor recurrence [6].

The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines for SLNB are predicated on an estimation of the likelihood of a positive sentinel lymph node (SLN); the primary determinants are Breslow thickness and whether the tumor is ulcerated [7]. The NCCN recommends that patients avoid SLNB if the risk of a positive SLN is < 5%, discuss and consider SLNB if the risk is 5–10%, and discuss and offer SLNB if the risk is > 10%. Therefore, any tool developed to identify patients who could potentially avoid SLNB should identify a group with a < 5% risk (NPV > 95%), aligning with nationally accepted thresholds for safely avoiding SLNB. Further, patients who avoid SLNB on the basis of any tools’ prediction should also have low recurrence rates, verifying that the tool truly identifies a patient population who can safely avoid SLNB.

Multiple groups have developed clinicopathologic-only nomograms attempting to help identify patients unlikely to have a positive SLN who could consider avoiding the SLNB procedure. Current NCCN guidelines suggest that SLNB decisions may be informed by these nomograms. Memorial Sloan Kettering Cancer Center (MSKCC) and the Melanoma Institute Australia (MIA) have both developed nomograms [8, 9], with the MIA nomogram demonstrating better performance than the MSKCC nomogram [10]. Neither nomogram, however, has been validated in a prospective study. In addition, multiple studies have called into question the clinical use of these nomograms, showing that, at the relevant SLNB risk threshold (5%), neither nomogram adds value over performing SLNB for all patients [11–13].

In the initial development of the MIA nomogram, the area under the curve (AUC) was 0.75, indicating good discrimination [8]. However, subsequent studies across relevant risk threshold ranges have called into question the clinical utility of the MIA nomogram [10, 14], and one study reported that, in patients predicted to have < 5% risk by the MIA nomogram, the observed positivity rate was 13.7% (NPV = 86.3%) [15]. Although NCCN guidelines reference the MIA nomogram [7], it has not been validated in any prospective cohorts, has not been shown to have clinical utility in helping patients avoid unnecessary SLNB procedures, and is not supported by evidence that patients who avoid SLNB on the basis of the MIA prediction have low recurrence rates. An additional limitation of the MIA nomogram is that it requires the melanoma histologic subtype, which is unavailable in approximately half of patients in the USA [16], thereby minimizing its clinical utility.

The integrated 31-gene expression profile (GEP) for SLNB (i31-SLNB) combines the 31-GEP risk score based on tumor biology with clinical and pathologic factors (Breslow thickness, ulceration, mitotic rate, and age) to provide a personalized prediction of a patient’s percent risk of SLN positivity [17]. The i31-SLNB has been retrospectively and prospectively validated, demonstrating a tight linear relationship between predicted and observed SLN positivity (y = 0.999x −0.005; where x is predicted and y is observed SLN positivity) [17–19]. Decision curve analysis showed a net benefit of using the i31-SLNB for patients with T1b–T2 tumors, and it has been demonstrated that clinicians use the i31-SLNB to aid decision-making about SLNB, thereby performing fewer unnecessary procedures [18–21]. Critically, patients who avoid SLNB on the basis of the 31-GEP results have no recurrences at a median of 2 years of follow-up, indicating that patients with a low-risk result can safely avoid SLNB [19].

The current analysis aims to compare the accuracy of the i31-SLNB and the MIA nomogram in a prospective cohort of patients with CM being considered for SLNB.

Methods

Study Design

This study compared the performance of the i31-SLNB and the MIA nomogram in predicting SLN positivity among patients enrolled in the prospective, multicenter DECIDE (DecisionDx-Melanoma Impact on Sentinel Lymph Node Biopsy Decisions and Clinical Outcomes) study [22, 23]. Patients with CM who were being considered for SLNB and who were using the 31-GEP as part of SLNB decision-making were enrolled at 30 sites in the USA (Fig. 1). The central institutional review board approval was obtained from the WCG-IRB (reference number IRB00000533, approval number 20191435). This study was performed in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration of 1964 and its later amendments. Patients provided written informed consent before enrollment, and all patients enrolled underwent 31-GEP testing. Available approval numbers are provided in the supplemental Table S1.

31-GEP/i31-SLNB Testing

The 31-GEP yields a continuous probability score that stratifies risk of disease progression. The i31-SLNB uses a neural network that combines the 31-GEP continuous score with clinicopathologic variables (i.e., Breslow thickness, ulceration, mitotic rate, and age) to individualize SLN-positivity risk assessment for each patient [17]. The i31-SLNB became clinically available in 2021; thus, most patients received an i31-SLNB risk prediction in addition to the 31-GEP result on their initial clinical report (n = 846). To reflect real-world SLNB decision-making, including consideration of i31-SLNB results alongside other clinicopathologic and patient-specific factors, test results were reviewed during shared SLNB decision-making and may have influenced patient or provider decisions. To evaluate i31-SLNB performance in the additional 66 re-consented patients who underwent 31-GEP testing prior to i31-SLNB becoming clinically available, i31-SLNB risk predictions were calculated retroactively for all 912 patients analyzed in this study. After the initial development and internal validation, this study serves as an external validation cohort for both the i31-SLNB and MIA nomogram.

MIA Nomogram

The MIA nomogram for SLN positivity prediction has been described previously [8]. One of the required MIA nomogram inputs is valid histologic tumor subtype; however, because subtype information is not required in the College of American Pathologist melanoma protocol for synoptic reporting [24, 25] nor required for melanoma staging [1], subtype information is not specified in approximately half of patients with CM [26, 27]. Epidemiological outcome data for melanoma not otherwise specified indicate that these melanomas behave comparably to superficial spreading melanoma [28], which is also the most common subtype [29]. This suggests that other infrequent and more distinct subtypes may be more likely to receive a medical annotation, and that unspecified subtypes are often superficial spreading melanoma. Thus, cases in our study with a missing tumor subtype (not otherwise specified) were entered as superficial spreading to the MIA nomogram to calculate risk predictions for analysis, an approach which has been previously reported [30]. An additional subset sensitivity analysis was performed using only patients whose clinical records contained a supported subtype for input to the MIA nomogram (without imputation). One spitzoid melanoma was excluded from this study because it is not a supported input subtype for the MIA nomogram. All analyses comparing the i31-SLNB and MIA nomograms were conducted on data from the same tissue samples to directly compare the two tools.

Area under the Curve (AUC)

The area under the (receiver operating characteristic) curve was used to compare the discriminative performance of the i31-SLNB and MIA nomogram in terms of sensitivity and specificity. The AUC for the two tools was compared using DeLong’s test, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Software and Tools

Analyses were performed in R version 4.5.0 using the pROC package v1.18.5 to generate and compare AUCs.

Results

The study included 912 patients with T1–T4 tumors. Table 1 presents patient demographics and clinical characteristics. The i31-SLNB predicted 474/912 (52.0%) patients to be at low (< 5%) risk of SLN positivity, while the MIA nomogram predicted 289/912 (31.7%) patients to be at a low (< 5%) risk of SLN positivity. Patients who received SLNB had tumors with a median Breslow thickness of 1.0 mm compared with 0.7 mm for tumors of those who did not receive SLNB. Ulceration was more frequent (14.4% versus 4.2%) in those who received SLNB. Of the 430 patients who received SLNB, 44 were confirmed positive (10.2%).

In the subset of 430 patients who underwent SLNB, the i31-SLNB classified 114 (26.5%) as low risk (< 5% predicted risk), with an observed SLN positivity rate of 2.6% (NPV = 97.4%, 95% CI 94.2–100.0%), below the NCCN risk threshold for considering SLNB (< 5% positivity). However, the MIA nomogram classified only 69 (16.0%) as low risk (< 5% predicted risk), with an observed positivity rate of 5.8% (NPV = 94.2%, 95% CI 88.1–98.7%), thereby failing to meet the NCCN threshold for avoiding an SLNB (Table 2). Patients predicted to have an SLN positivity rate > 10% by the i31-SLNB (n = 131, 30.5%) were observed to have a 21.4% positivity rate, an 8.2-fold increase over the rate in patients predicted < 5%. For patients predicted to have > 10% SLN-positivity risk by the MIA nomogram (n = 152, 35.4%), a 13.8% positivity rate was observed, a 2.4-fold increase over the rate in patients predicted < 5%.

To compare the overall accuracy of each predictive model, AUC was calculated for each tool. The MIA nomogram had an AUC of 0.61, while the i31-SLNB had a significantly higher AUC of 0.74 (p = 0.001; difference = 0.13, 95% CI 0.05–0.20, DeLong’s test; Table 2), indicating better discriminative performance of the i31-SLNB in terms of sensitivity and specificity.

Of the 912 patients in this study, 297 (33%) were reported to have “unspecified” tumor subtype, which is not valid for direct input to the MIA nomogram tool (Table 1). For the analyses described above, “unspecified” subtypes were converted to “superficial spreading,” the most common subtype and most likely not to receive a pathological notation. When the i31-SLNB and the MIA nomogram were again compared using data from only the SLN-assessed patients with tumors having MIA nomogram-supported subtypes (n = 266), the i31-SLNB maintained a significantly higher AUC than the MIA nomogram (0.73 versus 0.58, p = 0.003, difference = 0.15, 95% CI 0.05–0.25).

We next analyzed observed SLN positivity in patients with conflicting risk predictions between the i31-SLNB and MIA nomogram (Table 3). Seventy-one patients were predicted to be low risk (< 5% predicted positivity) by the i31-SLNB but high risk (≥ 5% predicted positivity) by the MIA nomogram. Among an additional 26 patients, the inverse was true, bringing the total to 97 patients (23% of all patients whose SLNs were assessed) with conflicting predictions at the 5% risk threshold. Among these patients, those with < 5% predicted risk by the i31-SLNB and ≥ 5% by the MIA nomogram had observed SLN positivity rates of 2.8%, whereas in those predicted < 5% risk by MIA and ≥ 5% by the i31-SLNB, the positivity rate was 11.5% (Table 3). Fifty-two patients had a ≤ 10% predicted risk by i31-SLNB but a > 10% risk by the MIA nomogram; the inverse was true for 31 additional patients who had a > 10% i31-SLNB predicted risk but ≤ 10% predicted risk by the MIA nomogram. Thus, at the 10% threshold, risk predictions conflicted for 83 patients (19% of all patients whose SLNs were assessed). Among these patients, those with a ≤ 10% predicted risk by i31-SLNB and > 10% risk by the MIA nomogram had a 1.9% positivity rate versus a 25.8% positivity rate in those with ≤ 10% predicted risk by the MIA nomogram and > 10% predicted risk by the i31-SLNB (Table 3). These results demonstrate that for patients with conflicting i31-SLNB and MIA risk predictions, using the i31-SLNB results in more accurate risk-aligned decision-making.

Discussion

In the current study of two tools that predict SLN positivity in patients with CM, the i31-SLNB, which integrates the 31-GEP with clinicopathologic factors, was more accurate than the clinicopathologic-only MIA nomogram for predicting SLN positivity. Patients predicted to have < 5% risk of SLN positivity by the i31-SLNB had an observed SLN positivity rate of 2.6% (NPV: 97.4%), well below the 5% threshold set forth in clinical guidelines to forgo the procedure. In contrast, the MIA nomogram failed to meet this benchmark, limiting its utility as a clinical tool for guiding decisions to forgo SLNB. The accuracy of the i31-SLNB in identifying patients with < 5% SLN positivity in this unique prospective cohort is consistent with that reported in prior validation studies, with SLN positivity prevalence ranging from 6.4% to 20.5% [17, 18, 20, 22]. These data further distinguish the i31-SLNB from an alternative clinicopathologic-integrated gene expression test, the CP-GEP, for which an initial AUC of 0.82 was reported in an internal validation cohort [31], followed by an AUC of 0.66 in external validation [32]. More recently, a sponsored prospective multicenter study in patients with T1–T3 tumors failed to meet its primary endpoint of < 5% SLN positivity. It reported a 7.1% positivity rate (NPV: 92.9%) among patients with CP-GEP “low-risk” results, raising concerns about both the clinical validity and utility of CP-GEP as a tool to guide decisions to forgo SLNB (Fig. 2) [33]. The CP-GEP “low-risk” result also failed to identify patients with < 5% SLN positivity in the subset aged 65 years and older (SLN positivity: 6.6%) and in those with T1b tumors (SLN positivity: 5.2%) [33]. For patients already considering SLNB on the basis of the ≥ 5% SLN-positivity risk associated with their staging, the use of an additional predictive test or tool is only helpful in a decision to safely forgo the procedure if it can reliably identify patients with risk below the standard 5% threshold.

The integrated 31-gene expression profile for sentinel lymph node biopsy (i31-SLNB) identifies more patients at low risk of sentinel lymph node (SLN) positivity with better accuracy than the Melanoma Institute Australia (MIA) nomogram. Patients considered low risk (< 5% predicted risk of positivity) by the i31-SLNB had a low positivity rate (2.6%) versus those considered low risk by the MIA nomogram (5.8%). Recent results from the MERLIN_001 study reporting performance of another predictive tool for SLN positivity, the clinicopathologic-gene expression profile (CP-GEP), are also shown for reference (7.1% positivity rate in CP-GEP “low risk”). National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) guidelines recommend patients with < 5% SLN-positivity risk avoid sentinel lymph node biopsy (SLNB)

The i31-SLNB was designed to provide a precise, individualized prediction of a patient’s risk of having a positive SLNB. In contrast to nomograms, the i31-SLNB has been prospectively validated [18]. Further studies have shown that when clinicians and patients use the test to help guide SLNB-related decisions, patients who did not undergo SLNB were unharmed, as there were no recurrences at a median of 2 years of follow-up [19]. The current analysis further supports the accuracy of the i31-SLNB and demonstrates that adding the 31-GEP to clinical and pathological factors improves accuracy beyond these factors alone, thereby identifying additional patients who may not need to undergo SLNB.

Using AUC to assess predictive accuracy, the i31-SLNB demonstrated significantly better discriminative performance in predicting SLN positivity than the MIA nomogram. In this first prospective evaluation of the MIA nomogram, its AUC was lower than in previous retrospective studies, including those assessed in a recent meta-analysis by Ma et al. [34]. Many of the prior validation cohorts included a majority of patients with thicker tumors (median Breslow thickness ≥ 1.4 mm) [8, 11, 35, 36], for whom the risk of a positive SLNB would have been > 10% based on staging alone (≥ T2a). In contrast, our unique prospective cohort enrolled patients actively considering SLNB, which resulted in an inclusion of many patients who ultimately did not undergo SLNB, but also in an SLN-assessed subset that more precisely represents those patients whose decision would have been equivocal (the 5–10% “discuss and consider” category in NCCN guidelines) based on staging alone (i.e. those with T1b tumors, T1a tumors with high-risk features), essentially those patients who need these predictive tools the most. Consistent with clinical guidelines and other studies, our study indicates that in patients with an MIA-predicted risk < 10%, the nomogram underestimates SLN positivity and offers little to no net benefit over performing an SLNB for every patient [7, 10–12, 15].

It is also important to note that, while AUC represents predictive accuracy across the entire range of prediction thresholds, in practice, these tools should perform at the 5% risk threshold specifically to ensure safety if they are to be considered clinically useful for guiding a decision to forgo an SLNB. Not only did the i31-SLNB accurately identify the patients at low risk (< 5%) of SLN positivity; it also identified 185 more patients overall at < 5% risk (i31-SLNB < 5%: 474/912, 52.0% versus MIA < 5%: 289/912, 31.7%), demonstrating utility for de-escalating care. The combination of better predictive accuracy and higher reduction rate support an expectation that use of the i31-SLNB to guide SLNB decisions would better align with patient preference to avoid unnecessary surgical procedures and improve quality of life. Further, health economic analyses have shown that, even if one applies the Medicare rate to 31-GEP testing, there is a 30% cost savings with 31-GEP guided care compared with AJCC-guided care [37].

In our study, the predicted SLN-positivity risk for 19–23% of patients whose SLNs were assessed was conflicting between the i31-SLNB and the MIA nomogram—a discrepancy that highlights the clinical implications of using each tool to guide SLNB decision-making. When i31-SLNB predicted low risk (< 5%) and MIA predicted high risk, the observed positivity was only 2.8%. Conversely, when i31-SLNB predicted high risk (≥ 5%), and MIA predicted low risk, the observed positivity was 11.5%. This demonstrates the added clinical utility of the GEP in resolving predictive ambiguity and guiding more appropriate patient selection for SLNB. For these conflicting cases, use of the clinicopathologic-only MIA nomogram with a 5% threshold to select patients to undergo SLNB would have led to performing 71 (16.5% of total SLNBs) biopsies, which the i31-SLNB could have accurately identified to have < 5% positivity risk. For conflicting cases at the 10% threshold (i31-SLNB ≤ 10%, MIA nomogram > 10%), 52 biopsies (12% of total SLNBs) yielding only one positive SLNB would have been recommended by the MIA nomogram, whereas the i31-SLNB accurately identified these patients as lower risk. These results demonstrate the added value of combining the 31-GEP with clinicopathologic factors to predict SLN status and the stronger discriminative performance of the i31-SLNB predictive algorithm.

The strength of this study is the direct comparison of a clinical test currently available in the USA versus a nomogram in a large, prospective, real-world cohort of patients with cutaneous melanoma from 30 US sites. Limitations of the study include missing tumor subtypes in many patients, which limited the analysis of the MIA nomogram. To address this concern, the data were analyzed assuming superficial spreading when the subtype was missing, and a subset sensitivity analysis was performed for only patients with known tumor subtypes. Both analyses resulted in similar performance of the MIA nomogram, indicating that assuming superficial spreading as a tumor subtype did not meaningfully alter the results. Further, the real-world study design brought i31-SLNB test results into the SLNB decision-making conversation, which may have influenced management choices. Individuals with very low-risk results may have been more likely to forgo the procedure, resulting in an SLN-assessed cohort with higher-risk tumors and test results, and higher SLN positivity rates. Although this may be considered a limitation in one sense, this design reflects how the information provided with the i31-SLNB is integrated into real-world practice alongside other relevant clinicopathologic and patient-specific factors.

Conclusions

The i31-SLNB demonstrated accuracy in identifying patients with CM at < 5% risk of SLN positivity from a clinically relevant cohort who may consider forgoing SLNB. Furthermore, this study reports higher accuracy for the i31-SLNB than for the clinicopathologic-only MIA nomogram. Consideration of i31-SLNB test results during SLNB decision-making can help reduce unnecessary SLNBs and the associated complications of the procedure, and redirect resources to those likely to benefit from SLNB. Notably, our study demonstrates that SLN-positivity risk in thin tumors is underestimated by the clinicopathologic-only MIA nomogram but is more accurately estimated through the i31-SLNB, which integrates the 31-GEP.
