 ABSTRACT

Intentional nuclear forensics is aimed at deliberately incorporating taggants within nuclear fuel and/or
fuel cladding to create unique and persistent signatures that enable the determination of material
provenance. This work reports on the characterization of secondary chemical phases formed from the
incorporation of rare earth element (REE) fluorescent taggants into Al-6061-T6 welds intended to mimic
those found on fuel cladding. Various secondary phases were present in all taggant systems, including
some unknown phases. The results highlight the complexity of incorporating taggants and the continued
need for further research within the area of cladding taggants.

• Fluorescent REE oxide taggants were easily detected via standard characterization methods.
• Taggants were segregated on either side of the weld and at the top.
• Formation of secondary chemical phases was evident for all REE oxide taggants; only minor phases
 of the parent REE oxides remained.
• Identified secondary chemical phases were primarily REE aluminates.

 1. INTRODUCTION

Whereas traditional nuclear forensics is aimed at determining the origin, history, and intended use of
unknown nuclear material outside regulatory control, intentional nuclear forensics is aimed at deliberately
incorporating unique and persistent signatures during fuel fabrication to enable the determination of
material provenance. Additional information regarding the broader intentional forensics project can be
found in other works [1, 2]. Although this concept is most commonly associated with adding taggants to
final nuclear fuel forms, some work has investigated the incorporation of taggants into fuel cladding.
Ideally, a taggant can encode data pertaining to the provenance (e.g., manufacturer, date or time,
constituents) and is robust enough to survive in-pile exposure and/or further destruction. Additionally, a
taggant should not change the behavior or performance of the fuel or cladding in any deleterious way.
Photoluminescent tagging is an attractive solution to many of these challenges because it is undetectable
under normal lighting conditions, is visible to the naked eye under specific lighting conditions, does not
require a significant quantity of material to be photoluminescent, is detectable with simple commercial
detection tools, and is versatile and robust in application.

Previous studies have shown that the photoluminescence of many rare earth element (REE) oxides can
survive the welding process, although it typically undergoes some degree of change via the formation of
secondary chemical phases. In many cases, the color emitted after welding differs from the original color
[3], which can complicate detection. These changes may affect the persistence of or method for detecting
a taggant; thus, the formation (or lack thereof) of secondary chemical phases throughout the welding
process must be well understood.

This work aimed to further elucidate secondary phases produced by incorporating loose taggant powders
into aluminum welds. General disbursement of taggants was analyzed using x-ray fluorescence
spectrometry (XRF), and chemical composition was investigated using Raman spectroscopy and x-ray
diffraction (XRD). Overall, the incorporation of taggant oxides resulted in the formation of various
known and unknown phases. The formation of REE aluminate phases was evident in all taggant systems;
small remnants of the initial base taggants were retained in only three of the six systems.

 2. MATERIALS AND METHODS

2.1 TAGGANT INCORPORATION

Taggant preparation and welding was performed at Los Alamos National Laboratory. Incorporated
taggants consisted of yttrium oxide (Y2O3), cerium oxide (CeO2), cerium-doped yttrium aluminum garnet
(YAG) (Ce:YAG), praseodymium oxide (Pr6O11), dysprosium oxide (Dy2O3), and erbium oxide (Er2O3).
Before being used, all taggants were dried overnight at 150°C in air to remove any moisture. Dried
powders were then passed through a 50 mesh (297 μm) sieve to remove any large clumps.

Welding was carried out using 6061-T6 aluminum alloy as the substrate. Taggants were incorporated into
the welds using a hole-drilling method in which 1.27 mm deep linear holes were drilled into the substrate
surface, spaced 6.35 mm apart. Machined substrates were degreased using a pressure spray of ethanol and
then dried with compressed air. The degreasing process was carried out at least three times per substrate
to ensure the complete removal of residual cutting oils. Oxide taggants were then packed into the
machined holes. Welding was carried out using a 6 kW, 200 μm diameter ytterbium fiber laser with a
1,070 nm wavelength. The welding head was set to a 27° leading angle to prevent back reflection into the
laser optics. Laser power was set to 3.9 kW; travel speed was set to 25.4 mm/s; and the focal condition
was set to 20 mm (negative defocus). A shield gas of 100% argon was supplied coaxially at 30 ft3/h.

2.2 XRF

XRF elemental maps of all welded samples was collected at Oak Ridge National Laboratory on an
ATLAS Apex benchtop spectrometer (IXRF Systems). Analyzed sample areas were varied from sample
to sample to capture the full width of each sample weld. Incident x-rays were produced using the built-in
Rh anode tube, a running voltage of 50 kV, and a 1 mA current. All samples were collected using a spot
size of 5 μm and 10 ms dwell time.

2.3 RAMAN SPECTROSCOPY

Raman spectra for all samples were collected at Oak Ridge National Laboratory using a Renishaw inVia
µ-Raman spectrometer with a 785 nm excitation wavelength. Spectra of powder lanthanide oxide material
were acquired using extended scans (100–3,200 cm−1) with four 10 s accumulation per scan. Three
spectra were collected and averaged for each sample. Spectral mapping of doped aluminum welds was
carried out using static scans; each scan collected 480 spectra across a 280 × 435 μm area. Spectral ranges
were chosen based on the previously collected powder spectra. Each spectrum was collected using twelve
10 s accumulations. The resulting spectral maps were analyzed using the Empty Modeling package within
the WiRE software (Renishaw). Empty modeling analysis separates multispectral datasets into individual,
identifiable chemical spectra—that is, empty modeling components (EMCs), without any initial reference
spectra. An extended scan of each EMC was collected using four 10 s accumulations; then, the spectral
background was subtracted using asymmetric least squares smoothing in the OriginPro software suite.

2.4 XRD

XRD patterns of the welds was carried out at Oak Ridge National Laboratory using a Rigaku Miniflex
diffractometer with a Cu-Kα source and a D/teX Ultra2 1D silicon-strip detector. Patterns were collected
from 10° to 90° 2θ using a step size of 0.01° and a scan speed of 0.2°/min. The collected patterns were
processed using the OriginPro software suite. Processing consisted of background subtraction using
minimum local values, five-point adjacent averaging, and normalization to total counts.

 3. RESULTS

3.1 XRF

XRF spectroscopy was conducted on all samples to elucidate trends pertaining to the dispersion of
taggants within the welds in relation to other elements comprising the base Al 6061 alloy. XRF mapping
of the tagged welds indicates that the majority of the incorporated taggants did not have high mobility
within the welds themselves (Figure 1). This limited mobility is particularly evident for Y2O3, CeO2, and
Ce:YAG taggants, which experienced a sort of “snowplowing” effect. This effect is characterized by the
apparent bisecting of the taggants away from the center of the weld. The result of such separation is two
parallel lines composed of taggant material with little to no taggant in the central path of the welds.
Higher mobility is evident within welds tagged with Pr6O11 and Dy2O3, in which the snowplow effect is
not as apparent. Taggant incorporation across the weld width is evident for Dy2O3; however, taggant
continuity along the path of the weld was not achieved. The Er2O3 taggant shows the highest mobility; Er
is evident across both the width and length of the weld. The unique character of the cesium map obtained
from the Ce:YAG sample is notable. Based on the low concentration of Ce within the material and the
relatively close position of the Ce Lα line to the Ti Kα line, the map is evidently not an accurate
representation of the disbursement of Ce within the weld.

XRF mapping of the two major alloying components of Al-6061-T6, Mg and Si, reveals differences in
element disbursement within the welded areas. Magnesium appears to remain primarily within the welds
themselves, as is evident for the samples tagged with Y2O3 and Pr6O11. However, silicon appears to be

separated from the bulk of the welds because it is evident primarily on the sides of the welded areas,
along the edges. In the Y2O3 and Ce:YAG samples, Si appears to be present within the areas of high
taggant concentrations. This result is most likely due to the slight overlap of Y Lα lines with Si Kα lines

3.2 RAMAN SPECTROSCOPY

3.2.1 Y2O3

Raman spectra of yttria taggant powder show a single region of vibrational peaks and a region of
photoluminescent peaks at higher wavenumbers. Raman vibrational modes are evident in the 100–
850 cm−1 range, dominated by the characteristic Fg vibrational peak of the cubic Y2O3 structure at
384 cm−1 [4]. At higher energies, additional peaks are evident between 1,100 and 2,300 cm−1. These peaks
are assigned to the emission of minor Nd3+ impurities [5, 6]. Two EMCs were extracted from the mapping
analysis of Y2O3-doped welds. EMC1 is composed of a wide area of broad convoluted intensity from
1,000 to 2,200 cm−1 (Figure 2E), whereas EMC2 is composed of narrow peaks in the range of 1,200–
2,200 cm−1 (Figure 2F). EMC1 appears within the same wavenumber region as EMC2 and may be a
related fluorescent phase or remnants of the aforementioned Nd3+ contamination. EMC1 appears to occur
primarily within darker raised globules on the surface of the aluminum substrate (Figure 2B). The EMC2
phase is less common and appears to occur within the center of barnacle-like craters on the surface
(Figure 2C). This barnacle morphology was described in a previous report [3] as taggant that has floated
to the surface of the weld. Comparisons of EMC2 with known Raman spectra indicate similarities with
the spectra of YAG (Y3Al5O12) [6]. These similarities would explain the previously reported difference in
photoluminescent color between the taggant and the welded material [3].

3.2.2 CeO2

Like the spectra of the Y2O3 taggant powder, the spectra of CeO2 contain a region of low-energy
vibrational modes from 100 to 800 cm−1 and a region of higher-energy peaks from 1,000 to 2,600 cm−1
(Figure 3F). However, in contrast to the results for the Y2O3 taggant, no specific contamination could be
assigned to these higher-energy fluorescent peaks for CeO2. Mapping of the CeO2-tagged weld shows
four individual EMCs, the most components for any of the examined welds (Figure 3G and H).
Interestingly, EMC3 aligns well with the Raman spectrum of pure CeO2 taggant (Figure 3I). EMC3 has a
narrow high-intensity peak centered at 468 cm−1 that is assigned to the F2g vibrational band of CeO2 [7].
This suggests that certain taggants can persist in their pure form without full dissolution within the Al
weld substrate. A similar peak is evident in EMC4, centered at about 413 cm−1; this peak may be assigned
to the combined Eg + A1g stretching modes of Ce2O3 as calculated by Schilling et al. (Figure 3J) [8].
Additional work is required to be certain of this phase assignment. Sharp low-energy peaks are noticeably
absent from EMC1 and EMC2 (Figure 3G and H). Rather, the bulk of spectral intensity for both
components is in the range from 850 to 2,000 cm−1. Multiple studies on the Ce–Al–O tertiary system have
been conducted, however, there is a noticeable absence of higher-energy Raman spectroscopy studies in
the literature within the current area of interest (850–2,000 cm−1). Studies on the deposition of CeO2(111)
thin films onto Al(111) and the inverse system by Skála et al. show that Al atoms diffuse immediately
into ceria, which leads to partial reduction of Ce4+ to Ce3+ [9]. Thus, a mixed-valent cerium aluminate
phase (Ce3+xCe4+1−xAlyOz) may start to form [10]. Similar to what was mentioned in the previous section
regarding the Y2O3 system, the formation of a cerium aluminate phase would also explain the previously
reported change in fluorescence color from red (taggant powder) to green (weld) [3].

3.2.3   Ce:YAG

The pure Ce:YAG powder taggant has several vibrational modes from 100 to 2,300 cm−1 (Figure 4C).
Tagging of aluminum welds using Ce:YAG shows a single EMC that is isolated to the welding edge
(Figure 4B). EMC1 has a series of low-intensity peaks in the 100–2,200 cm−1 range and a series of sharp,
high-intensity peaks from 2,200 to 3,000 cm−1 (Figure 4D). The low-intensity peaks are within the same
range as the fluorescent peaks in the pure taggant powder; however, their positions do not align. This
result may be due to the addition of extra Al3+ ions into the Ce:YAG lattice when the taggant was
incorporated into the weld. The presence of the intense fluorescent peaks at higher energies suggests the
formation of a separate fluorescent phase from either Ce:YAG or YAG.

3.2.4   Pr6O11

Raman spectra of pure Pr6O11 taggant powder show prominent low-energy peaks at 291, 357, and
465 cm−1 corresponding to the reported A1g, E2g and E1g vibrational modes of Pr(OH)3 (Figure 5C) [11].
These results agree with previous XRD analysis of the powders, which indicated the presence of the
hydroxide phase [3]. Additionally, a peak at 435 cm−1 aligns with the F2g peak of PrO2, which was also
observed within the diffraction data [12]. Spectral mapping of the examined weld does not show any
distinguishable peaks beyond 520 cm−1. A single EMC evident within the sampled area follows along the
edge of the weld, which appears beige in Figure 5B. The mapped spectra have peaks centered at 158, 218,
and 502 cm−1 (Figure 5D), matching the peaks reported for PrAlO3 [13]. The most prominent peak,
which is centered at 414 cm−1, can be assigned to Pr2O3; this assignment is further confirmed by the
presence of peaks centered at 105 and 188 cm−1[14].

3.2.5   Dy2O3

Spectra of pure Dy2O3 taggant powder show low-intensity peaks from 100 to 1,000 cm−1 and a single,
narrow high-intensity peak centered at 373 cm−1 (Figure 6C). Spectral mapping of the tagged weld shows
a single EMC that follows the inner edge of the weld (Figure 6B). Raman analysis of the spectral
component reveals peaks only at higher energies within the 700–1,800 cm−1 range, beyond the range for
the pure taggant power (Figure 6D). A narrow high-intensity peak is centered at 1,251 cm−1, and two
broad lower-intensity peaks are evident at 996 and 1,515 cm−1. Two sets of convoluted peaks are evident;
the first pair is centered at 787 and 831 cm−1, and the second pair is at 1,666 and 1,722 cm−1. The
positions of these peaks are notable because they align exactly with the EMC4 peaks for the CeO2 sample
weld (Section 2.2). The identical nature of these shared peaks for the two lanthanide-tagged welds
indicates that the peaks may correspond to lanthanide aluminate phases (LnxAlyOz) with similar
compositions in the two lanthanide-tagged welds. Interestingly, EMC4 in the CeO2 weld contains the
characteristic F2g peak of Ce2O3, but in this sample the Fg peak of Dy2O3 at 373 cm−1 is absent [15]. This
discrepancy may indicate that the spectral component of the Dy2O3 weld was composed completely of the
aluminate phase whereas that of EMC4 of the CeO2 weld may have been composed of variations of Ce3O4
and a secondary aluminate phase.

3.2.6   Er2O3

Raman spectra of the pure taggant powder shows a series of low-intensity peaks from 100 to 1,000 cm−1
(Figure 7C). No peaks are evident until higher energies—a large grouping of overlapping higher-intensity
peaks is present from 2,300 to 3,100 cm−1. None of the peaks are assigned to vibrational modes; rather,
they are a result of fluorescence [5, 16]. A study by Johnson et al. reported that using a 785 nm excitation
wavelength results in fluorescence that dominates the emission spectrum [17]. Therefore, although Raman
analysis of welds tagged with Er2O3 can be performed, it is important to note that the resulting spectra do
not consist purely of Raman vibrational modes. Spectral mapping of the welds shows a single prominent
EMC that covers the raised dark gray areas of the weld (Figure 7B). The fluorescent peaks within EMC1
do not align with the peaks in the pure taggant powder (Figure 7D). Because luminescence is still present
in the weld, we posit that the taggant may convert to an unknown erbium aluminate compound like the
lanthanide aluminate phases mentioned for previous REE taggant systems.

3.3 XRD

XRD patterns of all samples are dominated by diffraction peaks from the base Al 6061 alloy; however,
additional diffraction peaks are evident when the intensity scale is expanded (Figure 8). Exact
assignments of metal phases are difficult because of the nature of sample preparation in this study and
possible mixed stoichiometries. Thus, not all peaks are assigned, and the assignments presented are the
highest-confidence assignments based on the previously discussed results.

 11

 Figure 8. XRD patterns for Al 6061 welds tagged with Y2O3 (A), CeO2 (B), Ce:YAG (C), Pr6O11 (D), Dy2O3
                                            (E), and Er2O3 (F).

The XRD patterns support observations made based on the Raman mapping of the welds that all taggant
systems appear to form one or multiple REE aluminate phases. The Y2O3-tagged weld shows the
formation of Al2Y4O9, a monoclinic variation of YAG (Figure 8A). This result agrees with the YAG-like
fluorescent peaks within EMC2 of the Y2O3-tagged weld (Section 3.2.1). In addition, the presence of
Y2O3 indicates that the base taggant endures the welding process without full dissolution into the welding
substrate.

The CeO2 taggant system shows the formation of CeAlO3 and a minor presence of the base taggant, CeO2
(Figure 8B). The presence of Ce2O3—suggested by EMC4 within the CeO2 mapping data (Section
3.2.2)—is not evident. However, because it was present as only a minor phase within the Raman mapping
of the weld, it is possible that the concentration of the phase is too low to be detected by XRD.

As with the Y2O3 taggant system, the Ce:YAG taggant system shows the formation of Al2Y4O9 (Figure
8C). YAG itself (Y3Al5O12) is not evident; its absence suggests the preferential formation of the yttrium
aluminum monoclinic (YAM) phase within the preparation environment.

The XRD pattern of the Pr6O11 taggant system agrees with the reported pattern for PrAlO3 (Figure 8D).
This result aligns with the Raman peaks from the mapping data (Section 3.2.4). Although Pr2O3 was
evident in the Raman data, is not evident within the XRD pattern. As discussed previously for Ce2O3, if
Pr2O3 is a minor phase within the weld, it may not be detectable using XRD.

Just as the Pr6O11-tagged weld results in the formation of PrAlO3, the Dy2O3-tagged Al weld results in the
formation of AlDyO3; however, the diffraction peak intensities are not as strong as those in the Pr6O11
system (Figure 8E). The lower peak intensities suggest that the AlDyO3 phase is not as prevalent as
PrAlO3 phase. Because additional peaks within the weld’s pattern are unassigned, additional
nonstoichiometric AlxDyyOz or AlaDyb phases may be present.

The Er2O3 tagged weld shows the presence of numerous erbium aluminate phases (Figure 8F), which
explains the higher mobility of erbium throughout the weld compared to the other taggants, as was
evident in the XRF maps (Section Error! Reference source not found.). Assigned erbium aluminate
phases include ErAlO3, Er3Al5O12 and Er4Al2O9. The base taggant, Er2O3, is also present, suggesting that
although the taggant appears to readily dissociate within the aluminum melt, some pure taggant is
retained.

 4. CONCLUSIONS

Based on the foregoing results obtained using various characterization methods, it is apparent that,
although tagging of Al 6061 welds using REE oxides does produce readily detectable fluorescent tags
within the welds, the dissolution of the oxide taggants results in a plethora of known and unknown
chemical species. All tagged systems appear to show preferential dissolution of the base oxide taggant
within the aluminum melt and the formation of one or more REE aluminate phases (i.e., MxAlyOz, M =
REE). Although the base oxide taggants appear to persist within the welds in some systems (e.g., Y2O3,
CeO2, and Er2O3), they are only present as minor phases. It is possible that the use of the REE aluminates
themselves as taggants instead of the oxides could result in the formation of fewer secondary phases and
provide fluorescent taggants with more definitive phase composition in the final welds produced.

The taggants were segregated on either side of the welds (snowplow effect) and were most likely
concentrated at the top of the welds. This suggests that these REE fluorescent taggants would be suitable
for bulk analyses but that measurements for taggant recovery on smaller subsamples would not be
advisable without further research to confirm the presence of the minor-phase parent taggant oxides
throughout the welds.
