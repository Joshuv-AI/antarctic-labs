Chemokines in Alzheimer's Disease: Early Defence, Late Damage and the Impact of Sex and Infection
Authors: Blanca I Aldana, Kristine Freude
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChemokines constitute a versatile signalling network maintaining homeostasis and glia–neuron communication in the healthy brain but become progressively dysregulated during aging and Alzheimer's disease (AD). This review examines how chemokine systems transition from tightly regulated homeostatic signals to drivers of chronic neuroinflammation in AD. We describe the major chemokine families (CC, CXC, CX3C) and their dominant central nervous system (CNS) receptors (CCR2, CXCR3, CX3CR1), which activate canonical inflammatory pathways including NF‐κB, JAK/STAT and PI3K‐AKT. In AD, chemokine dysregulation occurs in a coordinated manner across multiple functional modules, including recruitment‐associated (CCL2, CXCL1), interferon‐inducible (CXCL10), loss‐of‐restraint (CX3CL1) and vascular‐associated chemokines. These alterations shift the network from regulated immune communication to self‐sustaining inflammatory circuits perpetuating chronic neuroinflammation. These networks reprogram microglia and astrocytes into disease‐associated phenotypes, amplify peripheral immune cell infiltration and destabilise synaptic function. Biological sex profoundly influences neuroinflammatory trajectories, with females exhibiting enhanced microglial senescence and interferon signalling, while males show accelerated complement activation. Viral pathogens, particularly neurotropic viruses (HSV‐1, HHV‐6, VZV) and SARS‐CoV‐2, actively reprogram chemokine networks, linking infection to amyloid‐β accumulation, tau pathology and neurodegeneration. Therapeutically, chemokine axes represent precision targets requiring stage‐matched, sex‐stratified interventions rather than broad anti‐inflammatory approaches. Understanding chemokine network dynamics offers mechanistic insights into AD pathogenesis and could provide pointers for therapeutic strategies.Keywords: chemokine networks, glial reprogramming, sex differences, viral pathogens

Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) represents the most prevalent neurodegenerative disorder worldwide, characterised by progressive cognitive decline, accumulation of amyloid‐β (Aβ) plaques, neurofibrillary tau tangles and chronic neuroinflammation [1]. While amyloid and tau pathologies have dominated therapeutic development for decades, mounting evidence indicates that neuroinflammation is not merely a consequence of protein aggregation but an active driver of disease progression [2]. Central to this inflammatory landscape are chemokines. These small, secreted proteins have traditionally been associated with immune cell trafficking. However, recent research highlights their role as master regulators of glial activation states, neuron–glia communication and central nervous system (CNS) homeostasis [3].

Chemokines and their cognate G‐protein–coupled receptors form an integrated signalling network that, in the healthy brain, maintains microglial surveillance territories, calibrates synaptic pruning and coordinates tissue repair responses. This system operates through precise spatial and temporal control, generating localised gradients that guide cellular positioning and specify activation thresholds. During normal aging, subtle dysregulation of chemokine signalling contributes to the chronic, low‐grade inflammatory state termed neuroinflammaging, characterised by progressive microglial priming, astrocyte reactivity and elevated cytokine production [4]. In AD, this dysregulation intensifies dramatically: chemokine networks undergo systems‐level rewiring wherein homeostatic signals are converted into persistent inflammatory drivers, gradients become distorted and cell‐type specificity diminishes [5]. Recent advances in single‐cell transcriptomics, spatial profiling and mechanistic modelling have revealed that AD‐associated chemokine dysregulation is not random but reflects coordinated modular reprogramming [6, 7, 8]. Simultaneously, loss of homeostatic neuron‐derived chemokines removes inhibitory control over glial activation [9, 10, 11], while vascular‐associated chemokines compromise blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity and amplify leukocyte entry [11, 12, 13, 14]. Together, these modules create self‐reinforcing inflammatory circuits that determine immune‐cell positioning, activation thresholds, vascular permeability and tissue vulnerability (Sections 2 and 3). Importantly, chemokine network behaviour is profoundly influenced by biological sex and environmental factors (Sections 4 and 5). These sex‐specific patterns arise from complex interactions between sex hormones, sex chromosomes and epigenetic programming, and they likely contribute to the disproportionate AD burden observed in women [15, 16, 17]. Additionally, viral pathogens actively reprogram chemokine regulatory architecture, shifting signalling from homeostatic surveillance toward persistent pro‐inflammatory activation [18, 19, 20]. These infections induce chemokine receptor dysregulation, drive BBB disruption, promote peripheral immune cell infiltration and directly trigger amyloid and tau pathology through multiple converging mechanisms including mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammasome activation and excitotoxicity.

This review synthesises current understanding of chemokine systems in healthy brain aging and AD pathogenesis, with emphasis on: (1) the molecular architecture of chemokine families and their dominant CNS receptors; (2) how chemokines function as phenotype signals and metabolic modulators that specify glial activation states; (3) the systems‐level rewiring of chemokine networks in AD and the resulting modular dysregulation; (4) cell‐autonomous mechanisms through which specific chemokine–receptor axes reprogram microglial and astrocytic behaviour; (5) sex‐specific differences in neuroinflammatory trajectories and chemokine dynamics; (6) the role of viral pathogens in reshaping chemokine signalling and accelerating neurodegeneration; and (7) therapeutic implications, including the conceptual shift from viewing chemokines as generic inflammatory biomarkers to recognising them as precision therapeutic targets requiring stage‐matched, sex‐stratified and context‐dependent interventions. Moreover, this review provides context for understanding how chemokine dysregulation may be involved in AD pathogenesis and highlights potential strategies that could be explored for future therapeutic development. Rather than advocating for broad anti‐inflammatory approaches, we argue that effective chemokine‐targeted therapies must reshape glial set‐points, dampen maladaptive amplification loops and restrict pathologic immune recruitment while accounting for disease stage, biological sex and secondary inflammatory triggers. This precision‐medicine perspective positions chemokine networks as critical control nodes whose modulation could fundamentally alter disease trajectories in AD.

Chemokine Systems in the Healthy and Aging Brain

Chemokines form a versatile signalling network that supports homeostasis, immune surveillance and glia–neuron communication in the healthy brain [21, 22]. In aging, progressive dysregulation of this system contributes to chronic neuroinflammation and altered microglial and astrocytic phenotypes [23]. Functioning as modulators of cellular responses and activation status (state‐control signals), chemokines integrate environmental cues, metabolic status, cellular stress and immune activation to regulate glial states across lifespan.

Chemokine Families and Dominant CNS Receptors

Chemokines are small, secreted proteins (typically 8–14 kDa) characterised by conserved cysteine residues that regulate diverse biological functions far beyond immune cell recruitment. The chemokine superfamily comprises four subfamilies: CC, CXC, CX3C and XC based on the spacing of N‐terminal cysteine residues (as previously reviewed [5, 24, 25, 26]).

CC chemokines contain adjacent cysteines and are frequently associated with myeloid cell positioning and recruitment of monocytes, lymphocytes and eosinophils [27]. Members such as CCL2, CCL3 and CCL5 are central to microglial activation and neuroinflammatory responses [28, 29]. CXC chemokines, with a single amino acid separating their first two cysteines, contribute to constitutive neuromodulatory axes and interferon‐inducible inflammatory programs [14, 30]. This family plays major roles in CNS aging and cellular senescence [31, 32]. CX3C chemokines are represented solely by fractalkine (CX3CL1), which exists in both membrane‐anchored and soluble forms, functioning as both an adhesion molecule and chemoattractant via CX3CR1 [10, 33]. XC chemokines (e.g., XCL1) are structurally simplified but remain part of the broader chemotactic repertoire.

The CC, CXC and CX3C chemokine families are predominant in the CNS, forming an integrated system that encodes context‐specific instructions for synaptic maintenance, glial surveillance and tissue repair [3, 24]. CC chemokines signal through CCR2 and CCR5 to orchestrate monocyte and lymphocyte trafficking while modulating microglial activation states [28, 34]. CXC chemokines, notably CXCL1, CXCL8 and CXCL10, engage CXCR2 and CXCR3 to regulate leukocyte recruitment, glial activation and neuroinflammatory amplification [11, 12, 13, 14]. CX3CL1 signals exclusively through CX3CR1, establishing a dedicated neuron–microglia communication channel that continuously calibrates microglial activation thresholds and synaptic surveillance [10, 35, 36, 37, 38].

Among CNS‐expressed G‐protein–coupled chemokine receptors (GPCRs), three exhibit dominant functional roles. CCR2, the primary receptor for CCL2, is essential for monocyte recruitment and inflammatory microglial polarisation [28]. CXCR3, expressed throughout the CNS and peripheral nervous system, couples inflammatory signals to MAPK pathway activation and participates in diverse neurological processes [39]. CX3CR1 is highly enriched on microglia and CNS‐associated macrophages, governing microglial homeostasis, synaptic pruning and cell‐survival decisions [10, 35, 36, 37, 38]. Together, these receptors enable microglia and astrocytes to decode chemokine gradients as precise instructions that determine activation thresholds, motility patterns, phagocytic activity and neuron–glia interactions [40].

Downstream Pathways: NF‐κB, JAK/STAT and PI3K‐AKT

Chemokine receptor engagement triggers a characteristic set of intracellular cascades that underlie their role as control nodes in CNS homeostasis. NF‐κB activation via GPCR‐dependent IκB kinase pathways induces transcription of pro‐inflammatory cytokines, chemokines, complement components and adhesion molecules in microglia and astrocytes, thereby amplifying and propagating inflammatory states [41, 42]. JAK/STAT signalling, often initiated via autocrine and paracrine cytokines (e.g., IL‐6) released in response to chemokine stimulation, promotes astrogliosis, cell survival and expression of genes such as GFAP and CXCL10, integrating chemokine input into long‐lasting transcriptional programs [11, 43].

PI3K‐AKT signalling downstream of chemokine receptors regulates cytoskeletal dynamics, chemotaxis, survival and metabolic reprogramming in glia and infiltrating myeloid cells. In macrophages and microglia, CXCL10/CXCR3‐dependent recruitment is accompanied by JAK/STAT3 and PI3K‐AKT activation that enhances motility and cytokine release, reinforcing inflammatory circuits and coupling positional information to effector function [44, 45]. Through convergence on NF‐κB, JAK/STAT and PI3K‐AKT modules, chemokines exert multilayered control over glial and immune states, functioning as integrated state‐control systems that are finely tuned in health but progressively dysregulated with aging [46, 47, 48, 49].

Chemokines as Phenotype Signals and Metabolic Modulators

At the systems level, chemokines function as phenotype signals that specify and stabilise discrete activation states of microglia and astrocytes rather than simply attracting cells along gradients. Neuronally produced CX3CL1 tonically signals to microglia CX3CR1 to maintain a homeostatic, surveillant phenotype with restrained production of TNF, IL‐1β and IL‐6, and preserved synaptic pruning capacity [10, 38, 50]. Disruption of CX3CL1/CX3CR1 signalling shifts microglia toward pro‐inflammatory, synapse‐stripping states, demonstrating that this axis encodes a set‐point between protective surveillance and neurotoxicity [12, 51, 52]. CCL2–CCR2 signalling controls recruitment and differentiation of peripheral Ly6C+ inflammatory monocytes and impacts resident glial activation [53, 54]. Elevated CCL2 destabilises the BBB, enhances monocyte and T‐cell entry into the CNS, and induces local inflammatory microglia phenotypes, acting as a system‐level switch between quiescent and inflamed tissue states in both acute and chronic contexts [55, 56]. Similarly, CXCL10–CXCR3 signalling, prominently induced in astrocytes and microglia, promotes recruitment and activation of T cells and myeloid cells and amplifies inflammatory loops that reinforce reactive astrocyte and microglial phenotypes [57, 58, 59]. Coordinated induction of CXCL10 in astrocytes via NF‐κB and JAK2/STAT3 signalling drives T‐cell infiltration and sustains neuroinflammation [60]. In parallel, microglia exposed to CXCL10 or related CXC chemokines increase cytokine production and chemotactic responses, creating positive feedback loops wherein chemokines both report and reprogram glial network activation states [61].

Chemokine signalling interfaces tightly with metabolic pathways, linking environmental cues to cellular bioenergetics and effector functions. In microglia, engagement of chemokine GPCRs activates PI3K‐AKT and downstream mTOR and glycolytic regulators, promoting a shift toward aerobic glycolysis typical of pro‐inflammatory, biosynthetic states while supporting survival and motility. In astrocytes, chemokine and cytokine receptor activation drives JAK/STAT and NF‐κB signalling, which reshapes the expression of metabolic enzymes and transporters, thereby modulating glutamate–glutamine cycling, antioxidant capacity and lactate shuttling to neurons [62, 63].

In glia, these same pathways are co‐opted to couple inflammatory tone to metabolic state: chronic, low‐grade activation of NF‐κB and JAK/STAT in the aging brain establishes a persistently reprogrammed, primed phenotype with heightened responsiveness to chemokine input [64, 65]. In this configuration, chemokines function as metabolic switches that lock microglia and astrocytes into specific metabolic–phenotypic configurations, constraining how they subsequently respond to amyloid, tau or systemic inflammatory challenges [2, 66].

In summary, during normal aging, gradual shifts in chemokine expression and receptor signalling contribute to a primed but largely adaptive glial state that maintains tissue homeostasis. In contrast, sustained or excessive dysregulation of these pathways can drive chronic neuroinflammatory signalling, altered glial phenotypes and disrupted neuron–glia interactions. Such changes are increasingly recognised as contributing factors in the transition from physiological aging to neurodegenerative disease states, including AD.

How AD Rewires the Chemokine Network

Systems‐Level Network Dysregulation: From Homeostatic Signalling to Chronic Inflammation

AD fundamentally reshapes the chemokine signalling landscape, converting a tightly organised and spatially restricted communication network into a diffuse state of chronic activation. In AD, this regulatory architecture deteriorates, chemokine gradients become distorted, cell‐type specificity diminishes and signalling shifts from stimulus‐dependent to persistent activity. Consequently, the defining alteration is not simply quantitative upregulation of chemokines but a systems‐level rewiring in which signals that normally maintain homeostasis instead drive sustained inflammation.

Recruitment‐associated chemokines such as CCL2, CCL3, CCL4, CXCL1 and CXCL5 establish gradients that guide peripheral leukocyte infiltration and redistribution of resident microglia. In AD, persistent expression converts these transient cues into stable positional instructions, resulting in prolonged microglial clustering around plaques, accumulation of infiltrating immune cells and enhanced astrocyte–microglia proximity. These lesion‐centred niches increase local cytokine concentrations, lower activation thresholds and produce self‐reinforcing microenvironments as recruited cells themselves secrete additional chemokines [67, 68, 69]. Additionally, CCR7 is relevant to meningeal immune surveillance and lymphatic function. Age‐related reductions in CCR7 expression in meningeal T cells have been linked to impaired immune trafficking and altered meningeal immunity, which may further exacerbate neuroinflammation and cognitive decline [70].

Recent transcriptomic, genetic and biochemical studies support the inclusion of CXCL1, CCL5 and CXCL16 in AD‐associated chemokine networks [6, 71]. Elevated levels of CXCL1 are found in patients with AD, and it is associated with increased tau abnormalities, including hyperphosphorylation and cleavage of the protein. CXCL1 can activate neuronal receptors (such as CXCR2), triggering pathways that promote these pathological changes and potentially contribute to disease progression by disrupting normal tau function and distribution [71]. CCL5, a ligand for CCR1, CCR3 and CCR5, promotes the recruitment and activation of T cells, monocytes and microglia, potentially driving the transition from localised glial responses to broader neuroimmune activation [72]. CXCL16, signalling through CXCR6, is increasingly recognised as a link between glial activation and adaptive immune‐cell recruitment. These chemokines are consistently upregulated in both human AD brains and mouse models and correlate with β‐amyloid and tau pathology, suggesting an active role in disease progression rather than a bystander effect. CXCL16 is also elevated in the blood of patients with mild cognitive impairment and AD, highlighting its potential as a noninvasive biomarker, although common coding variants in chemokine genes do not appear to directly increase AD risk [6]. Elevated CXCL16 may further promote the recruitment or retention of CXCR6‐expressing CD8+ T cells in the brain and cerebrospinal fluid (see Section 3.2.1.4).

Interferon‐inducible chemokines, exemplified by CXCL10, mark a shift toward innate immune alarm programs. Elevated CXCL10 in AD is observed both centrally and peripherally, correlating with neuronal aging phenotypes, cognitive decline and microglial activation, particularly in hippocampal and prefrontal regions [73]. CXCL10–CXCR3 signalling initiates pathways linked to cellular senescence, reduced autophagy and amyloid‐related dysfunction [74], while promoting CD8+ T cell recruitment and persistence, sustaining a pro‐inflammatory environment [75]. Epigenetic mechanisms, including reduced DNA methylation at CXCL10 promoter CpG sites, may further amplify expression with aging [73] and its interferon‐inducible nature suggests a repurposing of antiviral defence programs in response to endogenous stimuli such as amyloid‐β [76]. These signals interact synergistically with recruitment chemokines to reinforce inflammatory loops.

Loss‐of‐restraint modules, comprising homeostatic neuron‐derived chemokines such as CX3CL1 and CXCL12, reduce inhibitory control over microglia and astrocytes. CX3CL1 interacts with microglial CX3CR1 to maintain surveillance states [11, 77]. Disruption of this pathway in AD amplifies inflammatory responses and contributes to neurodegeneration. CX3CR1 deficiency in mouse models increases pro‐inflammatory microglial activation, IL‐1β expression, spatial learning deficits and altered stress responses [78, 79]. Furthermore, reduced CX3CL1 signalling contributes to tau pathology by promoting tau hyperphosphorylation, impairing tau clearance and altering tau handling in the brain; notably, tau itself can interact with CX3CR1, further disturbing this regulatory pathway [5, 80, 81]. Overall, dysfunction of the CX3CL1–CX3CR1 axis removes a critical anti‐inflammatory ‘brake,’ thereby facilitating neuroinflammation‐driven tau pathology and disease progression. Similarly, reduced neuronal CXCR4 expression impairs learning and memory, while CXCL12–CXCR4 activation in glia promotes TNF‐α release and inflammatory cascades [82, 83], highlighting how loss of homeostatic chemokines amplifies other inflammatory modules.

Vascular‐associated chemokines, including CXCL1, CXCL5 and CCL2, link chemokine signalling to endothelial activation and BBB permeability, facilitating leukocyte entry and perivascular inflammation [7, 84]. This vascular module further expands the network beyond the CNS, integrating peripheral immune cues.

Furthermore, AD chemokine signalling is stage dependent. During early pathology, transient elevations of CX3CL1, CXCL12 and CXCL10 represent adaptive attempts to restore homeostasis. Microglia are drawn to emerging plaques, their phagocytic activity is enhanced and astrocytes provide metabolic support to stabilise stressed neurons, while inhibitory chemokine signals remain active to prevent excessive inflammation. As pathology progresses, inhibitory control declines while recruitment and interferon‐like modules strengthen. Once thresholds are crossed, feedback loops become self‐sustaining: spatial clustering, interferon‐like transcription and vascular permeability increase, allowing peripheral immune components to reinforce central inflammation. In late stages, recruitment‐associated chemokines dominate, maintaining glycolytic, pro‐inflammatory glial phenotypes, suppressing repair and stabilising chronic activation loops.

Cell‐Autonomous Reprogramming: Chemokine–Receptor Axes as Functional State Switches

While chemokines drive network‐level reorganisation, individual chemokine–receptor axes function as molecular switches governing cell‐autonomous state transitions, spatial positioning and functional outputs in microglia and astrocytes. Biological outcomes are determined by cellular source, spatial distribution and disease stage [85, 86, 87, 88].

Microglial Reprogramming

CX3CL1–CX3CR1 Calibrates Microglial Plaque Engagement and Aβ Handling

Neuron‐derived chemokine CX3CL1 and its microglial receptor CX3CR1 form a well‐characterised neuron–microglia communication axis in AD. High‐resolution imaging in amyloid mouse models reveals that microglia form process barriers around plaques, restricting outward plaque expansion and preventing the formation of protofibrillar Aβ42 hotspots associated with neuritic dystrophy. Mechanistically, microglia preferentially ingest protofibrillar Aβ but are ineffective at phagocytosing fibrillar Aβ. CX3CR1 deletion increases microglial phagocytic capacity and plaque‐adjacent proliferation, resulting in greater Aβ uptake into phagolysosomes and reduced plaque burden [85, 86]. However, this axis exhibits context‐sensitive duality. While CX3CR1 deficiency promotes amyloid‐β clearance via heightened microglial activation, it may paradoxically intensify amyloid‐associated neuronal pathology and cognitive decline through sustained IL‐1β upregulation, which contributes to neurotoxicity and tau hyperphosphorylation [89], reflecting CX3CR1's dual role in maintaining homeostatic restraint while shaping pathological responses [86, 90]. Interventions boosting neuron‐to‐microglia signalling, such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, increase neuronal CX3CL1 expression, enhance microglial phagocytosis and improve cognition in 5xFAD mice [91]. Pharmacological interventions targeting this axis, including the natural compounds baicalein and morroniside, shift microglial inflammatory states and improve AD‐like outcomes [92, 93].

CXCL10–CXCR3 Promotes Pathological Microglial States

In APP/PS1 mice, the CXCL10 receptor CXCR3 promotes plaque formation and behavioural deficits, while CXCR3 deficiency reduces plaque burden and increases microglial Aβ uptake. Pharmacologic CXCR3 antagonism enhances microglial phagocytosis while dampening inflammatory output in vitro [94]. This axis bridges glial activation and immune‐driven neurotoxicity in human‐relevant systems. In a 3D human neuroimmune model, CXCL10–CXCR3 promotes CD8+ T‐cell infiltration, microglial activation and neurodegeneration‐like injury [75].

CCL2–CCR2 Drives Recruitment That Can Amplify or Resolve Pathology

CCR2 signalling enables mononuclear phagocyte recruitment to pathology sites. In mouse models of AD pathology (Tg2576 and APP/PS1 mice), CCR2 deficiency impairs microglial accumulation, leading to earlier Aβ accumulation, early cognitive impairment and premature mortality, supporting the idea that CCR2‐linked trafficking balances toxic assemblies and effective containment [95, 96]. However, persistent CCL2–CCR2 elevation reprograms Aβ handling in a maladaptive direction. CCL2 overexpression in Tg2576 accelerates memory and synaptic dysfunction and promotes microglia‐associated Aβ oligomer dynamics consistent with amplification rather than detoxification. Long‐term CNS expression of a dominant‐negative CCL2 mutant (7ND) suppresses gliosis, reduces Aβ and improves learning in APP/PS1, indicating that dampening CCL2 signalling can shift the system toward a less pathological glial state [97, 98].

Microglia–T Cell Crosstalk

Microglia actively participate in chemokine‐mediated T cell recruitment. In APP/PS1 mice and human AD brain, microglia are a major source of CXCL16, which supports the recruitment of CXCR6+ CD8+ T cells in an amyloid‐dependent manner [99, 100]. These CXCR6+ CD8+ T cells localise near plaque‐associated microglia and may limit Aβ deposition and cognitive decline, suggesting that microglia–T cell interactions are not uniformly detrimental but can also contribute to local immune containment. Consistent with this axis, CSF immune profiling in aging and cognitive impairment revealed clonal CD8+ T effector memory cells with increased CXCR6 expression, together with elevated CXCL16, supporting a role for CXCL16–CXCR6 signalling in CNS‐associated T cell trafficking and persistence [101].

CD8+ T cells can, in turn, reshape microglial states through CCL5–CCR5 signalling, modulating microglial transitions and amyloid handling [72]. In tauopathy models, microglia‐mediated T cell infiltration has been linked to neurodegeneration, indicating that adaptive immune engagement may shift from protective plaque‐associated containment to pathogenic inflammatory amplification as disease progresses [102]. Suppressing CD8+ T cell–microglia interactions (via intranasal cobrotoxin) improved cognition in 5xFAD mice while downregulating CXCL9, CXCL10, CXCL16 and CCL5, and reducing microglial activation [103]. Microglial chemokine signalling responds rapidly to therapeutic perturbation. Acute anti‐Aβ antibody exposure reprograms microglial states and alters chemokine communication within days [88], while danger signals such as extracellular miR‐29a‐5p activate TLR7/8 signalling, driving microglial inflammatory mediator release [104].

In summary, chemokine–receptor axes act as functional state switches that dynamically reprogram microglia in AD. The CX3CL1–CX3CR1 pathway fine‐tunes plaque engagement and Aβ handling, balancing homeostatic restraint with phagocytic activation in a stage‐dependent manner, whereas CXCL10–CXCR3 drives inflammatory, neurotoxic phenotypes and promotes immune‐cell infiltration. The CCL2–CCR2 axis regulates myeloid recruitment that may support early containment but, when persistently activated, amplifies pathology. Together with emerging CXCL16–CXCR6 and CCL5–CCR5 microglia–T cell circuits, these pathways illustrate that microglial chemokine signalling is highly context‐dependent, capable of shifting from protective to pathogenic programs as the disease progresses.

Astrocyte Reprogramming

CXCL10 is Prominent in Astrocyte Reactivity and Immune–CNS Coupling

CXCL10 emerges as a key mediator of astrocyte reactivity and immune–CNS communication. In AD patients, CXCL10 is markedly elevated in plaque‐associated astrocytes. Neuronal expression of its receptor CXCR3, together with evidence that CXCR3 activation induces ERK signalling in neurons in vitro, supports a role for astrocyte‐to‐neuron chemokine signalling in plaque‐adjacent neuronal stress responses [105]. Subsequent mechanistic work in hippocampal Aβ‐stimulated astrocytes indicates CXCL10 is part of an Aβ‐driven astrocyte secretome that can enhance astrocyte migration, interpreted as either protective containment (barrier‐like) or detrimental scar‐like remodelling [106]. Moreover, CXCL10–CXCR3 signalling promotes CD8+ T‐cell infiltration and is coupled to increased microglial activation and neuronal loss in the 3D human neuroimmune axis model [75]. Together, these data position CXCL10 as a central chemokine node that not only marks astrocyte reactivity but also reshapes glial–neuronal interactions and facilitates immune–CNS coupling that can amplify neurodegeneration.

CCL2 Recruits Astrocytes and Can Position Them for Aβ Handling

Astrocytes increase their expression of CCL2 and migrate toward Aβ plaques. They can bind and degrade Aβ1–42 in vitro, suggesting a protective clearance role [107]. However, sustained CCL2 overexpression, particularly when astrocyte‐driven, leads to increased phagocytic activation yet higher Aβ deposition, worsened synaptic physiology and accelerated cognitive impairment [97, 108]. This paradox suggests that CCL2 recruits myeloid cells without guaranteeing effective clearance, especially if recruited cells adopt inflammatory phenotypes or shift lipid‐handling mechanisms that stabilise amyloid deposition. Posttranslational processing adds further complexity. The enzyme isoglutaminyl cyclase (isoQC) catalyses pyroglutamate modification of both N‐truncated Aβ and CCL2, enhancing aggregation and strengthening CCL2‐driven neuroinflammation. IsoQC and CCL2 are upregulated in Tg2576 mice and human AD tissue, including plaque‐associated reactive astrocytes [109]. This metabolic‐chemokine link operates through dual catalysis of pGlu‐Aβ and pGlu‐CCL2, promoting inflammatory responses and impairing cognition. Chemokine function is thus regulated transcriptionally and through ligand modification, shaping receptor engagement and gradient stability in plaque environments. CCR2/CCL2 should therefore be discussed as a two‐edged axis, necessary for myeloid trafficking, but capable of amplifying pathology depending on chronicity, cellular source and downstream polarisation.

Astrocyte Chemokine Release is Receptor‐Driven

Astrocyte chemokine programs can be modulated upstream. In adult human cortical astrocytes, Aβ engagement of the calcium‐sensing receptor (CaSR) drives release of CCL5 and CCL8. Pharmacologic CaSR antagonism (calcilytic NPS 2143) suppresses this chemokine output [110]. Similarly, astrocytic chemokine‐like receptor 1 (CMKLR1) increases in APP/PS1 mice, and chemerin–CMKLR1 signalling promotes astrocyte recruitment to plaques via STING‐associated signalling, linking migration to innate immune pathways [111]. Systemic inflammation primes glia for exacerbated chemokine programs. In APP/PS1 mice, systemic inflammation amplifies neuroinflammatory responses, with IL‐1β driving heightened astrocyte chemokine responses and hippocampal dysfunction [87]. Region‐ and model‐dependent profiling shows chemokine networks differ across compartments and transgenic models, indicating that chemokine reprogramming is anatomically and temporally structured [112].

Together, these findings indicate that AD pathology does not arise from isolated chemokine changes but from coordinated, modular and temporally stratified network dysregulation. Early adaptive chemokine signalling gradually transitions into maladaptive recruitment and interferon‐like activation. Moreover, this leads to loss of neuronal restraint where neurons fail to provide inhibitory chemokine signals, normally keeping microglia and astrocytes in check, allowing these glial cells to become overactive and amplify inflammation. This is accompanied by the activation of specific chemokines (e.g., CCL2, CXCL1, CXCL5) that act on endothelial cells lining blood vessels, increases BBB permeability that promotes leukocyte adhesion and infiltration into the brain in combination with, creating self‐sustaining inflammatory circuits. This integrated view (Figure 1) is supported by multiple mechanistic observations. CCL2 overexpression activates microglia and promotes tau pathology [113, 114]; CCR2 signalling recruits monocytes to facilitate β‐amyloid clearance [96]; CXCR2 promotes amyloid production, tau hyperphosphorylation as well as leukocyte migration [115]. CCL20‐induced IL‐1β, TNF‐α and IL‐6 production drives microglial activation and supports neurorepair [116].

Influence of Biological Sex on Neuroinflammatory Dynamics

Biological sex profoundly shapes neuroinflammatory trajectories during aging, producing distinct patterns of microglial activation, cytokine signalling and astrocyte reactivity. In aging females, microglia exhibit enhanced senescence, enrichment of disease‐associated microglial (DAM) phenotypes, amplified type I interferon signalling and heightened astrocyte gliosis compared to age‐matched males. In contrast, males display accelerated complement system activation, distinct inflammatory gene expression profiles and greater interindividual variability in neuroinflammatory responses. These sex‐specific differences are driven by complex interactions between sex hormones, sex chromosomes and age‐related alterations in neuroimmune function [15]. Understanding these mechanisms is critical for developing sex‐tailored therapeutic strategies for age‐related cognitive decline and neurodegenerative diseases.

As described in Section 2, the aging brain undergoes significant immune remodelling. Under physiological conditions, these changes include modest shifts in glial activation states and inflammatory signalling that support tissue surveillance and maintenance of CNS homeostasis. In some contexts, however, this remodelling progresses into a chronic, low‐grade inflammatory state termed neuroinflammaging, which develops with advanced age independent of overt infection or acute injury [4]. Neuroinflammaging is characterised by progressive microglial and astrocyte activation, elevated pro‐inflammatory cytokine production and dysregulation of chemokine and immune signalling pathways [117, 118]. When persistent or amplified, these alterations may contribute to cognitive decline and increase susceptibility to neurodegenerative processes, particularly AD [17, 117].

Epidemiologically, women bear a disproportionate burden of AD, representing approximately two‐thirds of cases [15, 16]. While this was initially attributed to greater longevity, accumulating evidence suggests fundamental sex differences in how male and female brains age and respond to pathological insults [119, 120]. Transcriptomic, single‐cell and longitudinal animal studies reveal that these differences manifest in distinct trajectories of microglial activation, cytokine production and glial reactivity across the lifespan [17].

A major driver of female‐biased neuroinflammation is the loss of circulating oestrogens with aging. Declining estradiol is associated with increased inflammatory gene expression in the cortex and hippocampus [121]. In ovariectomised mice, estradiol supplementation attenuates IL‐1β and IL‐6 expression but does not uniformly suppress chemokines such as CCL2, CCL3 and CXCL1, indicating a selective modulatory role. Beyond hormones, sex chromosomes and sex‐specific epigenetic programming shape microglial transcriptional and chromatin states, generating divergent chemokine profiles and influencing immune responses throughout life [117, 122, 123]. Age‐related changes in peripheral–central immune coupling, including CCR7 expression on meningeal T cells, further modulate lymphatic egress, glymphatic clearance and Aβ pathology in a sex‐dependent manner [70, 122].

These mechanisms collectively produce sexually dimorphic chemokine patterns in the aging brain. In cortical regions, females show higher baseline levels or more pronounced age‐related upregulation of CCL3, CCL4, CCL17, CCL22 and CXCL1 [120, 124]. In the hippocampus, males exhibit elevated extracellular levels or distinct temporal dynamics of CCL3, CCL20 and IL‐1α [125]. The CX3CL1–CX3CR1 neuron–microglia axis is upregulated in aged females, promoting T cell recruitment [126] and complement‐related chemokines (C1q‐associated milieu) also show stronger age‐related increases in females [120, 123]. CCL11 (eotaxin‐1) rises with age and is linked to cognitive decline and AD risk, although sex‐specific differences remain incompletely defined [127]. Overall, these findings demonstrate that females exhibit heightened cortical and complement‐associated chemokine responses, whereas males show distinct hippocampal chemokine dynamics (Figure 2).

The decline in circulating oestrogens with aging may serve as a primary driver of these female‐specific inflammatory changes. In males, aging is marked by accelerated complement system activation, distinct inflammatory gene expression profiles and greater interindividual variability in neuroinflammatory responses. Hippocampal regions display elevated or temporally distinct expression of chemokines CCL3, CCL20 and IL‐1α. Males show heightened susceptibility to acute inflammatory challenges that disrupt cognitive function. Central to both sexes is the phenomenon of neuroinflammaging—a chronic, low‐grade inflammatory state characterised by progressive microglial and astrocyte activation, elevated pro‐inflammatory cytokine production and dysregulation of immune signalling pathways. These sex‐specific differences arise from complex interactions between sex hormones, sex chromosome effects and age‐related alterations in neuroimmune function, collectively influencing differential susceptibility to cognitive decline and neurodegenerative diseases. Such sexually dimorphic patterns likely interact with broader age‐related immune changes, including peripheral immune trafficking, glymphatic clearance and Aβ handling, to influence differential susceptibility to neurodegeneration and AD. Notably, females may be more prone to chronic microglial activation and chemokine‐driven inflammation, while males may be more susceptible to acute inflammatory challenges that disrupt cognition. Chemokines such as CCL2, CCL3, CCL11, CX3CL1 and complement‐linked signals lie at this intersection, yet rigorous sex‐stratified human studies of chemokine dynamics in aging and dementia remain a critical unmet need. Integrating sex as a biological variable is therefore essential for understanding neuroinflammaging and for the development of targeted interventions.

Sex‐dependent effects in AD may be especially relevant for tau pathology. Several lines of evidence suggest that sex differences in tau accumulation, spreading and tau‐associated neurodegeneration may be more robust or more closely related to cognitive decline than Aβ pathological trajectories [128, 129]. Females may exhibit greater vulnerability to tau pathology in the presence of amyloid, potentially reflecting interactions among hormonal changes, APOE genotype, microglial priming and inflammatory signalling [130]. Female‐specific gene associations with tau neuropathology are concentrated in microglia and enriched in immune and inflammatory stress‐response pathways [131]. Sex alone, independent of age and APOE genotype, modulates the amyloid‐to‐phosphorylated tau axis, with women exhibiting stronger amyloid‐dependent p‐tau secretion driven at least in part by elevated TREM2‐related microglial activation [132]. Consistently, in amyloid‐positive individuals, women exhibit stronger associations between TREM2 and CSF tau and neurofilament light chain than men, alongside sex‐specific remodelling of circulating CCL2/MCP‐1 concentrations [133]. Chemokine networks may contribute to this vulnerability by shaping these sex‐dependent glial activation states, leukocyte trafficking and cytokine environments that regulate tau‐associated kinase activity. Female AD astrocytes selectively upregulate CCL2, and CCL2 genetic variation is associated with faster tau accumulation relative to amyloid deposition [134], while in female APOE4 tauopathy mice CXCL10, CCL5 and CCL3 are co‐elevated as part of a senescence‐associated microglial secretory phenotype linked to neurodegeneration [135]. At the adaptive immune interface, peripheral immune cell activation peaks earlier in MCI‐stage females than in males, indicating that leukocyte trafficking dynamics are themselves sexually dimorphic in AD [136]. Thus, such chemokine axes should be considered not only in relation to immune recruitment but also as potential modifiers of sex‐specific tau trajectories.

Viral Pathogens and Chemokine Signalling in AD: Mechanisms of Dysregulation

Viral and Systemic Inflammation Actively Reprogram Neuroimmune Signalling

Viral infections and systemic inflammatory events are increasingly recognised not as passive background stressors but as active, mechanistically relevant modulators of neuroimmune signalling in AD. Rather than simply adding inflammatory noise, these insults reprogram chemokine regulatory architecture, particularly along axes such as CCL2/CCR2, CXCL10/CXCR3 and CCR3/CCR5, shifting signalling from tightly controlled homeostatic surveillance toward persistent pro‐inflammatory activation states. This transition alters cellular trafficking logic within the brain: dysregulated chemokine gradients promote peripheral leukocyte recruitment, prime microglia and astrocytes into disease‐associated reactive phenotypes and destabilise neuron–glia communication networks. The downstream consequence is a feed‐forward inflammatory cascade characterised by amplified cytokine production, synaptic destabilisation, metabolic stress and progressive neuronal injury that contributes directly to cognitive decline. Mechanistically, viral or systemic immune triggers enhance interferon‐inducible chemokines such as CXCL10, increase CCR2 ligands such as CCL2 and activate complement‐associated signalling pathways, thereby establishing self‐reinforcing inflammatory circuits that couple peripheral immune activation to central neurodegenerative processes [76, 123].

Evidence Linking Viral Pathogens to AD Biology

Multiple independent lines of investigation converge on viral pathogens, especially neurotropic herpesviruses, as biologically plausible contributors to AD pathogenesis [18, 19, 20]. Evidence spanning epidemiological cohorts, molecular profiling, animal models and cell culture systems supports a long‐standing hypothesis that herpes simplex virus‐1 (HSV‐1) participates mechanistically in disease development. Observations include correlations between viral reactivation and incident AD, localisation of viral DNA within AD lesions, selective vulnerability of hippocampal and cortical regions that mirrors AD neurodegeneration and infection‐induced molecular changes such as amyloid‐β dysregulation, tau phosphorylation, synaptic dysfunction, mitochondrial stress and neuroinflammation. Antiviral treatment reduces amyloid and tau pathology in infected experimental models, strengthening causal plausibility.

Complementing HSV‐1 data, large cohort‐scale molecular analyses reveal enrichment of HHV‐6A and HHV‐7 in AD brains, linked to regulators of amyloid production, while experimental studies demonstrate that these viruses can induce amyloid expression, tau modification and microglial activation, potentially through miR‐155 suppression and disruption of autophagy–stress response pathways [19, 137, 138]. Additional animal work shows that diverse viral infections accelerate tau pathology and disease progression, collectively supporting a model in which specific neurotropic viruses function as mechanistic contributors rather than incidental correlates of AD‐related neurodegeneration.

Viral Remodelling of Chemokine Receptor Landscapes in Neural Cells

A central mechanism linking infection to AD biology involves pathogen‐driven remodelling of chemokine receptor expression within the brain. HSV‐1 and other pathogens increase expression of CCR3, CCR5 and CXCR3 on microglia, astrocytes and neurons, particularly in regions vulnerable to AD pathology [139]. Such receptor upregulation sensitises neural cells to inflammatory ligands and enhances responsiveness to circulating immune signals. Comparable processes occur in other neurotropic infections; HIV exploits CCR3, CCR5 and CXCR4 on neural cells, and receptor activation directly contributes to calcium dysregulation and apoptosis [140].

HSV‐1 can also directly perturb neuronal physiology. Viral binding increases Ca2+ influx and impairs autophagy. This leads to failure of Aβ42 containing autophagosomes to fuse with lysosomes, leading to intracellular Aβ42 accumulation and neuronal stress [141]. Viral synergy may amplify pathology, as severe varicella zoster virus (VZV) infection can reactivate latent HSV‐1 in the brain, dramatically increasing amyloid‐β and phosphorylated tau, producing AD‐like neuropathology [142]. SARS‐CoV‐2 provides a complementary systemic mechanism: ACE2 expression is elevated in AD brains [143], potentially strengthening spike‐protein binding, increasing viral entry and promoting Aβ42 plaque accumulation and tau neurofibrillary tangles that accelerate neurodegeneration [144, 145].

Chemokine‐Mediated Leukocyte Recruitment Across the BBB

Chemokine‐dependent leukocyte trafficking across the BBB represents a major interface between infection and neurodegeneration. Infection‐induced cytokines such as IL‐1β and TNF‐α upregulate endothelial adhesion molecules and chemokines including CCL2, increasing BBB permeability and leukocyte migration into the CNS [146, 147]. In HSV‐1–infected AD‐model mice, infiltrating T cells and macrophages secrete CCL2, CCL5 and MCP‐1, reinforcing inflammatory recruitment loops [146]. Similar mechanisms are established in HIV encephalitis, where microglia and astrocytes release β‐chemokines such as MCP‐1, MIP‐1α/β and RANTES that actively drive monocyte migration across the BBB [148]. Once leukocyte entry begins, infiltrating immune cells themselves become chemokine sources, amplifying inflammatory density.

T‐Cell Chemotaxis and the CXCL10–CXCR3 Amplification Loop

Elevated CXCL10 expression together with BBB compromise promotes infiltration of IFN‐γ–producing T cells in AD brains. These T cells further activate microglia and stimulate additional chemokine release, accelerating amyloid production and neurodegeneration [87, 139]. Chronic CXCL10/CXCR3 signalling alone can induce neural senescence and cognitive impairment in mice [74], demonstrating that chemokine‐guided T‐cell recruitment is not merely correlative but mechanistically pathogenic.

Astrocyte Priming and Secondary‐Hit Amplification

Astrocytes function as major inflammatory amplifiers once primed by pathology. In APP/PS1 AD‐model mice, amyloid plaques render astrocytes hypersensitive so that secondary inflammatory stimuli such as IL‐1β or systemic lipopolysaccharide trigger exaggerated production of CCL2, CXCL1, CXCL10 and IL‐6 at both endothelial and parenchymal levels [87]. This primed state intensifies chemokine gradients, amplifies microglial activation, and destabilises neural networks, helping explain why systemic infections often precipitate acute cognitive decline in vulnerable individuals.

Epidemiological and Clinical Evidence for Viral Contributions

Population‐level evidence supports mechanistic findings. Neurotropic herpesviruses have long been implicated in dementia risk, and a natural‐experiment study shows that zoster vaccination may prevent or delay dementia onset, suggesting a causal relationship between viral exposure and neurodegeneration [149]. A meta‐analysis reports increased AD risk after infections with human herpesviruses, cytomegalovirus, hepatitis C virus and SARS‐CoV‐2 (OR ≈ 1.2–1.9) [140]. Biobank analyses similarly associate viral encephalitis and other infections with later AD or neurodegenerative disease. Seropositivity or reactivation of HSV‐1 and related viruses repeatedly correlates with increased AD risk, particularly in APOE ε4 carriers [150]. However, most human evidence remains observational, and causality and timing of exposure remain unresolved.

Viral Mechanisms Driving Core Alzheimer's Pathology

Amyloid‐β as an Antimicrobial Effector

Amyloid‐β (Aβ), traditionally viewed solely as a pathological peptide, is now widely recognised to possess innate immune properties consistent with antimicrobial peptides [151]. Experimental work demonstrates that Aβ can bind microbial surface structures, promote agglutination and assemble into fibrillar aggregates that physically entrap invading pathogens [152]. This rapid sequestration mechanism resembles classical innate immune defence strategies such as neutrophil extracellular traps or complement‐mediated opsonisation. Within this framework, Aβ deposition may initially represent a protective host response to infection rather than a primary pathogenic event. The antimicrobial protection hypothesis proposes that repeated or chronic microbial exposure, particularly from latent or reactivating neurotropic pathogens, appears to drive sustained Aβ production, eventually overwhelming clearance systems and resulting in plaque accumulation [153].

Importantly, this model integrates infection biology with classical amyloid cascade concepts by suggesting that Aβ overproduction is not intrinsically abnormal but becomes pathological when immune activation is persistent, dysregulated or occurs in aging brains with impaired proteostasis. Chronic viral latency, systemic inflammation or repeated reactivation events could therefore transform an adaptive antimicrobial response into a maladaptive neurodegenerative process [154]. This paradigm also explains why Aβ deposition often colocalises with microbial signatures in postmortem tissue and why inflammatory signalling frequently precedes detectable plaque formation.

Induction of Amyloid and Tau Pathology

A substantial body of experimental evidence indicates that diverse pathogens can directly induce molecular hallmarks of AD, including Aβ accumulation, tau hyperphosphorylation, synaptic injury and neuronal death. Microbial species implicated in these processes span multiple biological classes and include neurotropic viruses such as HSV‐1 and HHV‐6, intracellular bacteria such as Chlamydia pneumoniae and spirochetes, highlighting that convergent host responses rather than pathogen identity alone may be central drivers of pathology. Across in vitro neuronal cultures, organoid systems and animal models, infection consistently triggers amyloidogenic processing of APP, increases extracellular plaque‐like deposits and activates kinase pathways that modify tau into aggregation‐prone forms [155].

Among these agents, HSV‐1 has emerged as one of the most mechanistically characterised. Infection of human neural models alters MAPT splicing, shifting isoform expression toward 4R‐tau variants and promoting tau oligomerisation, processes closely linked to neurofibrillary tangle formation [155]. In vivo, HSV‐1 infection of AD‐model mice accelerates amyloid deposition, induces gliosis and worsens cognitive decline, effects mediated in part through activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. Pharmacologic or genetic inhibition of NLRP3 signalling attenuates amyloid pathology and improves behavioural outcomes, demonstrating a causal inflammatory pathway linking viral infection to AD‐like neurodegeneration [146].

Mechanistically, infection‐induced tau pathology is thought to arise from kinase activation (e.g., GSK‐3β, CDK5, MAPKs), oxidative stress and calcium dysregulation. These are processes that can be initiated by viral entry, replication or host immune signalling. These pathways converge on cytoskeletal destabilisation, impaired axonal transport and synaptic dysfunction. Since amyloid and tau pathologies can mutually reinforce one another, infection‐triggered perturbations in either pathway may initiate a feed‐forward cascade that accelerates neurodegenerative progression [156].

Neuroinflammation and Cellular Stress Pathways

Persistent or latent viral infections can impose long‐lasting immunometabolism stress on the CNS, driving a self‐sustaining neuroinflammatory state that closely parallels core pathological processes in AD. Chronic viral antigen exposure maintains microglia and astrocytes in a primed or partially activated state, lowering their activation threshold and promoting exaggerated responses to secondary stimuli. This persistent glial activation elevates pro‐inflammatory cytokines, chemokines and reactive oxygen species (ROS), creating a toxic microenvironment that enhances amyloid‐β deposition, promotes tau hyperphosphorylation and accelerates synaptic dysfunction and neuronal loss [157]. Importantly, these processes are not purely inflammatory but reflect widespread disruption of intracellular homeostatic systems. Many neurotropic viruses interfere with autophagy pathways, impair mitochondrial quality control and activate inflammasome complexes, thereby shifting cells toward stress‐response phenotypes that favour protein aggregation and neurodegeneration [158].

Among these mechanisms, mitochondrial dysfunction represents a central hub linking infection to neurodegenerative signalling. Viral interference with mitochondrial dynamics disrupts ATP production, increases oxidative stress and alters apoptotic signalling thresholds. HSV‐1 infection, for example, directly damages mitochondria, inducing mitochondrial DNA injury, excessive ROS generation, dysregulated calcium handling and failure of neuronal bioenergetic systems [159]. Since neurons rely heavily on mitochondrial function for synaptic transmission and plasticity, such defects can rapidly translate into impaired network activity and heightened vulnerability to amyloid‐ and tau‐mediated toxicity.

Viruses also perturb neurotransmission systems that regulate neuronal excitability and survival. HSV‐1 and HIV‐1 have both been shown to disrupt glutamatergic signalling and nitric‐oxide–related pathways, producing excitotoxic conditions that favour synaptic damage and trigger kinase cascades leading to tau phosphorylation [160]. Excess glutamate and nitric oxide can synergistically enhance oxidative stress, destabilise cytoskeletal architecture and impair axonal transport, thereby linking viral infection directly to canonical molecular features of Alzheimer‐type neurodegeneration. Collectively, these findings indicate that persistent viral exposure does not merely provoke transient inflammation but instead reprograms cellular metabolism, stress signalling and immune responses in ways that reinforce protein aggregation, neuronal dysfunction and progressive cognitive decline.

BBB Disruption and Routes of Neuroinvasion

Viral infections can compromise BBB integrity through multiple converging mechanisms, thereby facilitating the entry of viral particles, peripheral cytokines and immune mediators into the CNS. Systemic or neurotropic infections induce endothelial activation characterised by upregulation of adhesion molecules, disruption of tight junction proteins (e.g., claudins and occludins), oxidative stress and inflammatory signalling, all of which weaken barrier selectivity and increase paracellular permeability. This inflammatory endothelial state not only permits direct viral neuroinvasion but also allows circulating cytokines and chemokines to penetrate brain tissue, where they can activate resident microglia and astrocytes and amplify neuroinflammatory cascades. Several routes of entry have been proposed. Hematogenous spread through a compromised vasculature represents a major pathway, particularly during systemic viremia or cytokine storm conditions. In addition, certain neurotropic viruses may bypass vascular barriers entirely via neuronal transport mechanisms. The olfactory–nasal pathway, for example, provides a direct anatomical conduit from peripheral tissues to the brain and has been implicated as a potential entry route for HSV‐1, enabling viral particles to access limbic and cortical structures that are highly vulnerable in AD [161].

Beyond free viral particles, extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as an additional and increasingly recognised transport mechanism linking peripheral infection to central pathology. Virus‐infected cells can release EVs containing viral RNA, proteins, host inflammatory mediators and regulatory microRNAs. EVs can cross the BBB and fuse with recipient neural cells; they function as protected carriers that disseminate pathogenic signals without requiring intact viral particles. Through this mechanism, peripheral infections may remotely influence CNS physiology by delivering inflammatory or neurotoxic cargo directly to neurons, astrocytes and microglia, thereby initiating or amplifying local immune responses and cellular stress pathways even in the absence of productive infection within brain tissue [162].

Integrated Viral Contributions to AD: Multipathogen, Mechanistic and Translational Perspectives

Accumulating evidence indicates that infection‐associated risk in AD is best understood not through a single‐pathogen lens but within a multipathogen systems framework in which diverse microbes converge on shared neurodegenerative pathways. Biomarker, serological and transcriptomic studies show that many individuals with AD harbour signatures of multiple latent or persistent infections, suggesting that co‐infection may act synergistically rather than additively. In such contexts, sequential or simultaneous pathogen exposure can prime innate immune cells, particularly microglia and astrocytes, lowering their activation threshold and amplifying inflammatory responses to subsequent stimuli. This priming effect may intensify chemokine production, oxidative stress and complement activation, thereby accelerating amyloid aggregation and synaptic injury. Some models further propose that polymicrobial communities or biofilm‐like assemblies may enhance immune evasion and prolong inflammatory signalling within neural tissue [163]. Supporting this integrative view, systems‐bioinformatics analyses demonstrate that phylogenetically distinct viruses, including herpesviruses, hepatitis viruses, influenza A and SARS‐CoV‐2, nonetheless converge on a restricted set of host pathways central to AD pathobiology, including amyloidogenic processing, mitochondrial dysfunction, autophagy impairment, oxidative injury and neuronal death [164]. Such convergence implies that the host response architecture, rather than pathogen identity alone, determines whether infection contributes to neurodegenerative risk.

Within this broader infectious landscape, herpesviruses emerge as leading mechanistic candidates because their biological properties closely parallel key features of late‐onset AD. Members of this family, including HSV‐1, HHV‐6, cytomegalovirus and Epstein–Barr virus establish lifelong latency punctuated by periodic reactivation, often triggered by stress, immunosenescence, or systemic inflammation. This cyclical reactivation pattern mirrors the chronic, progressive trajectory of AD and provides repeated opportunities for inflammatory and metabolic disruption in neural tissue. Genomic analyses further strengthen this association, revealing elevated HHV‐6A and HHV‐7 DNA abundance in AD brains and transcriptional correlations between viral load and host genes regulating amyloid production and immune signalling [165]. Clinical observations also suggest therapeutic relevance: antiviral treatment of HSV‐1–positive individuals has been associated with substantial reductions (approximately 70% in one cohort) in subsequent AD risk [166]. Although causality remains under investigation, such findings have led to conceptual frameworks proposing that sporadic AD may, in at least a subset of patients, represent a partially virus‐driven disorder in which neurotropic viruses function as initiating triggers or disease accelerators rather than sole causes.

Recent clinical and epidemiological studies have provided preliminary support for this concept, although the evidence remains mixed. The phase II VALAD trial (NCT03282916), which evaluated high‐dose valacyclovir in HSV‐seropositive patients with early AD, failed to demonstrate a slowing of cognitive decline or changes in AD biomarkers, suggesting that antiviral treatment initiated after symptom onset may be insufficient to modify disease progression. In contrast, the phase IIa ART‐AD study of lamivudine (3TC), aimed at suppressing endogenous retrotransposon activity, demonstrated favourable safety and biomarker changes consistent with reduced retrotransposon activation, although the small sample size and absence of a control group precluded conclusions regarding clinical efficacy. Furthermore, observational studies have reported reduced dementia incidence among recipients of herpes zoster vaccines and users of the antiparasitic combination atovaquone/proguanil, lending indirect support to viral and protozoal hypotheses of AD pathogenesis. However, these associations are susceptible to residual confounding and healthy‐user bias and therefore cannot establish causality.

Mechanistically, infection‐associated neurodegeneration can be interpreted through two complementary models: direct and indirect pathways of viral influence. In the direct model, pathogens access the CNS, infect neurons or glial cells and initiate pathological cascades, including amyloid‐β overproduction, tau hyperphosphorylation, mitochondrial dysfunction and inflammasome activation. Repeated cycles of viral reactivation may produce cumulative structural and functional damage, gradually lowering neuronal resilience and promoting network failure. In contrast, the indirect model posits that peripheral infections need not invade the brain to influence its biology. Instead, systemic immune activation generates circulating cytokines, chemokines and danger signals capable of crossing or modulating the BBB, thereby inducing central inflammation, glial activation and synaptic dysregulation. These pathways are not mutually exclusive and likely interact dynamically. Peripheral infections may prime the brain for exaggerated responses to later insults, while central infections may sensitise systemic immunity, creating bidirectional feedback loops that sustain pathology.

Viral infections are increasingly recognised as active contributors to AD pathogenesis, rather than incidental stressors. HSV‐1, VZV and SARS‐CoV‐2 exemplify viruses capable of reshaping neuroimmune and neurodegenerative processes. These pathogens reprogram chemokine networks (CCL2/CCR2, CXCL10/CXCR3, CCR3/CCR5), promote microglial and astrocyte activation and disrupt neuron–glia signalling, leading to chronic inflammation, synaptic dysfunction and neuronal injury. HSV‐1 establishes lifelong latency, reactivates periodically and directly induces amyloid‐β accumulation, tau phosphorylation, mitochondrial dysfunction and excitotoxicity. VZV can reactivate latent HSV‐1 and amplify AD‐like pathology, with zoster vaccination showing potential protective effects against dementia. SARS‐CoV‐2, while primarily acute, can increase BBB permeability, trigger systemic cytokine surges and exacerbate amyloid and tau pathology, particularly in individuals with elevated ACE2 expression. Together, these viruses converge on shared AD‐related pathways, including chemokine dysregulation, neuroinflammation, amyloidogenesis and neuronal injury, supporting a model in which infection‐driven immune and cellular dysfunction accelerates or modulates disease progression. This perspective highlights HSV‐1, VZV and SARS‐CoV‐2 as key viral modulators of Alzheimer's pathology and potential targets for preventive or therapeutic strategies (Figure 3).

Therapeutic Implications: Chemokine Axes as Levers, Not Just Biomarkers

Chemokines exert complex pleiotropic effects in the CNS. Rather than generic anti‐inflammatory strategies, chemokine interventions should be designed as reprogramming therapies that (i) reshape glial set‐points, (ii) dampen maladaptive amplification and (iii) restrict pathologic immune recruitment while accounting for sex as a biological variable and secondary inflammatory triggers.

Recognition of infection‐linked mechanisms has important therapeutic and translational implications. If pathogens contribute to AD initiation or progression, antimicrobial strategies—including antivirals, vaccines, antiparasitic agents and immunomodulators—may have preventive or disease‐modifying potential. However, clinical and epidemiological evidence remains mixed.

The phase II VALAD trial (NCT03282916), $$$$published in JAMA [167], tested high‐dose valacyclovir in 120 HSV‐seropositive patients with early AD but found no cognitive or biomarker benefit, suggesting limited efficacy when treatment is initiated after symptom onset.

Beyond classical antiviral approaches, lamivudine (3TC) extends this framework through a different mechanism. Although traditionally an antiviral nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitor, it is here used to target endogenous retrotransposon activity rather than viral replication. The rationale is that retrotransposon activation may drive innate immune signalling and neuroinflammation in AD. Lamivudine is therefore hypothesised to reduce LINE‐1 activity and downstream cGAS–STING–mediated inflammation.

Consistent with this, the phase IIa ART‐AD study reported safety and biomarker changes suggesting reduced retrotransposon activity, but the study was small, open‐label and not powered for clinical outcomes [168].

Observational studies further suggest reduced dementia incidence after herpes zoster vaccination [169] and in users of atovaquone/proguanil [170], supporting possible infectious contributions. However, these findings are subject to confounding and do not establish causality.

Despite increasing evidence linking microbial exposure and chronic immune activation to AD, major mechanistic and translational uncertainties remain. Causal inference is limited by the ubiquity of latent infections in the general population and the difficulty of distinguishing lifelong exposure from disease‐associated reactivation. The relative contribution of specific pathogens, the modifying effects of host genetics such as APOE ε4, and the critical windows of vulnerability remain unresolved. Likewise, it is unclear which patient subgroups, if any, derive meaningful benefit from pathogen‐targeted interventions.

Most clinical studies to date are constrained by small sample sizes, short follow‐up and late‐stage intervention after neurodegenerative cascades are established. This design limits sensitivity to disease‐modifying effects and leaves open the possibility that infectious or immune‐driven processes act earlier in the disease trajectory. Addressing these gaps will require longitudinal, deeply phenotyped cohorts, mechanistic studies linking infection, immunity and neurodegeneration, and biomarker‐informed stratification strategies. Definitive evaluation will ultimately depend on adequately powered randomised trials with early, precision‐targeted intervention paradigms.

The AD chemokine landscape contains amplification pathways and coordination pathways, a distinction that clarifies what success should look like in preclinical and clinical studies. Amplification pathways push microglia toward pathological states or connect glial reactivity to immune‐cell entry. CXCR3 promotes plaque formation and behavioural deficits in APP/PS1 mice; genetic deletion reduces amyloid burden and increases microglial Aβ uptake, while pharmacologic antagonism increases phagocytosis and reduces inflammatory output in vitro [94]. These findings support framing CXCL10–CXCR3 as an axis that can be inhibited to reduce neuroimmune amplification while restoring uptake‐competent microglial states. Coordination pathways tune microglial set‐points for plaque engagement and synaptic interaction. Microglia can form plaque‐adjacent barriers that restrict neurotoxic protofibrillar Aβ hotspots and neuritic damage, and CX3CR1 signalling tunes this selective Aβ handling and microglial engagement with plaques. However, CX3CR1 loss can also worsen neuronal pathology (including tau hyperphosphorylation), synaptic loss and cognitive decline, underscoring that CX3CL1–CX3CR1 is strongly stage‐ and context‐dependent [85, 86, 89, 171]. Therapeutically, CX3CL1–CX3CR1 should not be treated as a simple block vs. boost target but rather as a pathway requiring stage‐matched modulation, potentially with sex‐stratified effect expectations [172].

Secondary inflammatory triggers expose vulnerabilities not obvious under baseline pathology. In APP/PS1 mice, acute systemic inflammation exacerbates neuroinflammation; IL‐1β drives amplified responses in primed astrocytes, resulting in inflammatory surge accompanied by hippocampal network dysfunction and acute cognitive impairment [87]. Similarly, systemic LPS challenge amplifies cortical pro‐inflammatory transcripts including IL‐1β and glial activation markers in 5xFAD mice [173]. This supports a model wherein AD pathology establishes a sensitised baseline, and systemic inflammatory events push chemokine‐linked glial programs into higher gain states. This has direct therapeutic implications: if systemic inflammation is a common real‐world modifier of symptoms and progression, chemokine‐targeting strategies should be evaluated not only for baseline pathological effects but also for capacity to blunt triggered exacerbations. Infection/inflammation paradigms are mechanistically aligned with how chemokine circuits likely influence disease trajectories in patients.

An Experimental Roadmap: From Transgenic Approaches to Sex‐Stratified Translation

First, establish baseline chemokine circuit behaviour across transgenic models spanning distinct pathological contexts. Amyloid‐predominant models (APP/PS1, 5xFAD) define core plaque‐associated chemokine axes and microglial state transitions [85, 94], while mixed pathology models (3xTg) and vascular amyloid models (Tg‐SwDI) capture chemokine programs dominating when tau pathology or vascular pathology [174]. Studies showing rapid microglial chemokine rewiring after acute anti‐Aβ exposure underscore that immunotherapy itself modifies chemokine networks; preclinical evaluation should explicitly include immunotherapy‐relevant paradigms when proposing chemokines as targets or biomarkers [88].

Second, incorporate infection or systemic inflammation as a required validation axis. LPS or IL‐1β‐driven exacerbation models test whether chemokine intervention reduces the amplitude and duration of flare responses, closer to the clinical scenario of symptom worsening during infection or inflammatory stress [173]. This step assesses whether sex differences amplify under inflammatory challenge, an outcome suggested by sex‐divergent responses under metabolic stress and pharmacologic protection [172].

Third, implement explicit sex‐dimorphism translation. Preclinical studies revealing sex‐specific protection (EP2 antagonism) and sex‐specific inflammatory/chemokine remodelling indicate that chemokine and prostaglandin‐linked neuroinflammatory circuits can be differentially wired across sexes. Translationally, this argues for early‐phase human studies designed to detect sex‐dependent pharmacodynamics through sex‐stratified analyses of chemokine biomarkers and immune signatures (CSF/plasma CXCL10, CCL2/CCL5‐related profiles, immune‐cell chemokine receptor patterns), with prespecified evaluation of whether pathway modulation produces parallel shifts in microglial/astrocyte activation markers and cognitive endpoints (Figure 4).

Chemokines as Early Pharmacodynamic Biomarkers in Therapy Development

Because chemokine circuits shift quickly in response to perturbation, they are well‐positioned as early indicators of target engagement and mechanistic reporters of glial state transitions. Acute anti‐Aβ exposure reprograms microglial state composition and chemokine signalling within days, showing that chemokine networks capture immediate immune remodelling during therapy [88]. In sex‐focused development pipelines, early pharmacodynamic biomarker panels should be evaluated separately in females and males, ideally under baseline and trigger conditions, to ensure the biomarker genuinely reports the intended circuit rather than a sex‐ or trigger‐specific confound.

The therapeutic dimension of chemokine biology in AD is best understood as precision reprogramming of glial circuits. The dominant question is not whether this reduces inflammation, but whether it reshapes the chemokine network in the appropriate direction, within the proper tissue context and for the correct patient subgroup. The most actionable path forward is a development strategy that is explicitly sex‐aware, validated under systemic inflammatory triggers and benchmarked across diverse transgenic models capturing amyloid, tau and vascular contributions.

Conclusion

AD emerges not solely from amyloid and tau accumulation, but from a progressive failure of immune regulation in which chemokine networks become chronically reprogrammed. In early stages, chemokines coordinate protective glial responses, facilitating plaque containment, metabolic support and immune surveillance. Over time, however, persistent activation, loss of neuronal restraint signals, vascular compromise and pathogen‐induced remodelling shift the system toward self‐sustaining inflammatory amplification.

Biological sex and infectious exposures critically shape this trajectory. Female‐biased interferon and complement signalling, together with pathogen‐driven chemokine receptor remodelling, may lower the threshold for chronic neuroinflammation and accelerate disease progression in genetically vulnerable individuals. Viral pathogens act not merely as coincidental stressors but as active modulators of chemokine circuits, linking peripheral immune events to central neurodegeneration.

Therapeutically, these insights argue against broad immunosuppression. Instead, effective disease modification will require stage‐matched, sex‐aware and context‐dependent modulation of specific chemokine axes. Interventions should aim to recalibrate glial set‐points, dampen maladaptive amplification loops and prevent pathologic immune recruitment, especially during systemic inflammatory or infectious triggers.

Understanding how chemokines rewire microglia and astrocytes at the intersection of aging, sex and infection provides a unifying framework for AD pathogenesis. Precision targeting of these immune control nodes may offer a path toward truly disease‐modifying therapies in AD.
