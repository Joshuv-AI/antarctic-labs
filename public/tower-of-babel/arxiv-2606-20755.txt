Abstract
Visual navigation in unknown environments remains a core challenge in mobile robotics, especially for resource-constrained platforms. Most existing approaches rely on loosely coupled modular pipelines and strong assumptions on perception quality or environmental structure, often resorting to multi-modal sensor suites that increase system complexity and deployment cost. Vision-only navigation offers a lightweight alternative, but its performance degrades severely under motion blur, low texture, and illumination changes, largely because they neglect the tight coupling between commanded motion and perception. While perception-aware methods partially address this issue, they typically optimize individual modules and fail to propagate uncertainty consistently across the navigation stack. In this paper, we present UNSEEN, a unified uncertainty- and perception-aware navigation framework that explicitly couples localization, mapping, and planning using only a front-mounted camera. UNSEEN estimates sparse maps and robot poses with associated uncertainties at 6 Hz, and leverages them to plan trajectories that jointly optimize task progress and estimation accuracy in receding-horizon. Simulations and extensive real-world experiments in unknown environments demonstrate the robustness of the proposed approach, with UNSEEN-SLAM reducing absolute translational error by 9.8% and UNSEEN-PLAN improving estimation accuracy by up to 45% compared to state-of-the-art methods, while achieving a 100% task success rate.

I
Introduction

Autonomous navigation in unknown environments remains a fundamental challenge in robotics for resource-constrained
Unmanned Aerial Vehicles (UAVs)
. In the absence of prior knowledge, perception, mapping, and planning are tightly coupled, such that even minor estimation errors can rapidly compromise safety and mission success.

Perception-aware navigation methods partially address this coupling, but typically optimize isolated components of the autonomy stack. To maintain tractability, many approaches rely on strong assumptions, including prior maps
[
1
,
2
]
, near-perfect localization
[
3
,
1
,
4
]
, or noise-agnostic models that neglect estimation uncertainty
[
5
,
6
]
. Other solutions mitigate drift through multimodal sensing
[
7
,
8
]
, at the cost of increased hardware complexity and computational overhead, limiting their applicability to lightweight aerial platforms.

Vision-only navigation offers a compelling alternative due to its low power consumption and minimal sensor footprint
[
9
,
10
]
. However, most visual pipelines remain brittle under motion blur, low texture, or lighting variations. As a result, visual navigation is often integrated with other sensors and relies on dense environment representations, promoting decoupled estimation and planning pipelines that partially or entirely neglect the probabilistic nature of state estimation.

In this work, we present UNSEEN, a vision-only framework for navigation in unknown environments that explicitly relies on sparse map representations and consistent end-to-end uncertainty propagation. The framework is built around a lightweight Visual-
Simultaneous Localization and Mapping (SLAM)
module, UNSEEN-SLAM, which estimates sparse, uncertain maps and poses that are structured and managed within UNSEEN-MAP. Leveraging this sparse representation, UNSEEN-PLAN and UNSEEN-OPT generate safe motion plans in free and known space, explicitly improving future estimation quality.

UNSEEN executing a trajectory in an unknown environment containing regions of varying texture and perceptual difficulty.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows:

1.

A novel V-
SLAM
extracting state covariances from a pose graph and propagating them both up- and downstream to concurrently improve estimation conditioning and safety in mapping and planning (Sec.
IV
).

2.

A novel mapping framework for probabilistic sparse maps employing truncated Gaussian distributions, enabling occupancy updates and uncertainty-aware planning (Sec.
IV
).

3.

An uncertainty-aware receding horizon planning algorithm for sparse visual navigation in unknown environments that ensures safety and estimation robustness, combining global exploration planning for exploration with local smoothing (Sec.
V
).

4.

A novel constrained
Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE)
problem on
\mathrm{SE(3)}
for trajectory optimization maximizing the effectiveness of visual navigation under uncertainty with a novel visibility model (Sec.
VI
).

5.

A tightly-integrated and unified autonomy stack that ensures vision-only safe and reliable navigation for resource-constrained platforms.

To the best of our knowledge, UNSEEN is the first framework to achieve online, uncertainty-aware, perception-aware planning and trajectory optimization in unknown environments using only a front facing camera for sparse estimation.

II
Related Work

Literature on autonomous visual navigation in unknown environments is vast, although it can be categorized based on the single component of the pipeline each contribution mostly focus on: Localization and Mapping, Planning and Trajectory Optimization. Hence, we report the main contributions in each category, providing a summary of the most relevant works compared to UNSEEN in Table
I
.

II-A

SLAM and Map representations for Navigation

Modern autonomous navigation pipelines rely on visual state estimation
to infer both robot motion and a representation of the surrounding
environment.
V-
SLAM
systems jointly estimate camera poses and scene structure
from image measurements leveraging probabilistic inference.
In general, V-
SLAM
backends can be broadly
categorized into filtering- and smoothing-based.

II-A
1
Sparse Visual and Visual-Inertial SLAM

Filtering-based methods estimate the state recursively through Bayesian
updates, which requires to maintain state covariances over time to perform predictions and corrections.
MonoSLAM
[
11
]
, ROVIO
[
12
]
, OpenVINS
[
13
]
and other proposed approaches
[
14
]
provide principled uncertainty propagation, but scale
poorly with map size, limiting long-term or
large-scale deployments.

In contrast, smoothing-based methods propose factor-graph optimization over a selected set of keyframes and landmarks in a nonlinear least-squares framework.
For example, Kimera
[
15
]
, OKVIS2-X
[
16
]
and VINS-Mono
[
17
]
employ smoothing backends such as g2o
[
18
]
, iSAM2
[
19
]
or GTSAM
[
20
]
to perform fixed-lag smoothing or full-batch optimization.
These methods leverage sliding-windows and factor graphs to either marginalize old poses and landmarks to limit computational complexity, or efficiently exploit sparsity during inference. Due to the re-linearization of past poses, these methods achieve superior accuracy and scalability compared to filtering approaches
[
16
,
15
]
.
As such, most of these systems operate on sparse feature maps but strictly rely on correcting IMU measurements for accurate localization rather than effective environment representation.
For this reason, marginalized states are discarded after optimization.
As a result, the optimized map is rarely refined beyond its immediate
use for pose estimation.
ORB-SLAM
[
21
]
and its successors
[
22
,
23
]
partially mitigate this limitation by
maintaining a global map atlas and reoptimizing landmarks during bundle
adjustment, but uncertainty information is still discarded after optimization.

II-A
2
Dense V-
SLAM

Although less common in navigation, dense methods have been developed in parallel to sparse ones.
Instead of optimizing poses and landmarks, dense and semi-dense V-
SLAM
methods optimize dense maps, thus delivering richer environment representations.
DTAM
[
24
]
and other approaches
[
25
]
estimate camera motion by minimizing photometric
error over full images, enabling denser reconstructions at the
cost of robustness and computational load.
The introduction of RGB-D sensing made
real-time dense reconstruction possible with volumetric and surfel-based dense
mapping systems such as KinectFusion
[
26
]
and ElasticFusion
[
27
]
.
However, most of these systems alternate between tracking within a static map and
mapping with fixed poses, which degrades trajectory accuracy at scale
and prevents their use for navigation.
Consequently, current navigation methods often decouple state
estimation and mapping, ensuring accurate pose estimates from feature-based V-
SLAM
and using them to update dense maps using point clouds from RGB-D, stereo cameras or lidars
[
28
,
29
,
30
]
.

II-A
3
Uncertainty Handling in V-
SLAM

Both sparse and dense V-
SLAM
systems share limitations in
the handling of uncertainty.
Most smoothing-based formulations assume uncertainty to flow
unidirectionally from sensor noise into the backend optimization, after which
state covariances are not computed or retained.
Reoptimizing landmarks without retaining their prior confidence ignores
the evolution of estimation uncertainty and prevents its exploitation
by downstream modules such as mapping or planning.

Recent learning-based approaches have attempted to infer uncertainty
models jointly with state estimation.
DROID-SLAM
[
31
]
introduced a differentiable bundle adjustment layer to learn
feature confidence, but it relies on simplified diagonal covariance models
and focuses on relative uncertainty rather than metric consistency.

Qiu
et al.

[
32
]
proposed MAC-VO, which propagates learned 2D uncertainties into 3D covariances using
empirical projection models and first-order corrections, capturing complex image-level uncertainty but only
approximating its evolution through bundle adjustment.
Overall, uncertainty is either ignored, heuristically approximated, or
not preserved in a form suitable for downstream decision-making.

II-A
4
Mapping for Navigation

For navigation, sensor data is typically converted into an
occupancy representation to enable collision checking and path
planning.
Octomap
[
29
]
enables compact storage and multi-resolution
queries, making it a de facto standard for mobile robot
navigation.
However, it assumes deterministic and accurate pose estimates, not allowing for reoptimization of map sections from older poses.
Additionally, it only updates voxels intersected by sensor rays, neglecting the spatial
uncertainty induced by state estimation.

In practice, however, V-
SLAM
delivers probabilistic 3D landmark distributions.
When subject to drift, noisy measurements and degraded visual conditions, the uncertainty spreads spatially becoming relevant to neighboring voxels.
Duong
et al.

[
33
]
proposed a kernel-based representation to model free and
occupied space continuously as distributions while accounting for sensor noise, but at the expense of scalability and
compatibility with standard planning frameworks.
Pairet
et al.

[
34
]
proposed a method that incorporates collision risk
into planning to account for the probabilistic nature of estimated obstacle positions, but it relies on externally provided maps and ignores state
estimation uncertainty.

Modern navigation systems integrate occupancy maps with volumetric representations such as

Truncated Signed Distance Fields (TSDFs)

[
35
,
28
]
or
hierarchical
octrees
[
29
,
36
,
37
,
38
]
. These are combined with search-based
[
39
]
,
sampling-based
[
40
]
, or optimization-based
[
41
,
42
]
planners.

While robust, these pipelines often execute estimation, mapping, and
planning sequentially, introducing latency and limiting reactivity by locking each process based on the outcome of the previous.
Hierarchical mapping approaches such as WAVEMAP
[
38
]
improve efficiency by
adapting map resolution to planning needs, but still assume deterministic state
estimates and do not incorporate estimation uncertainty.

Accounting for estimation uncertainty requires to factor estimated maps and poses and tightly couple estimation with occupancy mapping.
As stated above, while sparse V-
SLAM
presents a valid option for navigation tasks. However, most of the aforementioned marginalize landmarks, neglecting maps.
Attempts to merge sparse V-
SLAM
maps into useful environment representations have been made.
Chen and Liu
et al.

[
43
]
proposed a method to convert sparse point clouds into navigable space, focusing on local free-space extraction and convex decomposition, not considering uncertainty and therefore without producing an environment representation suitable for planning purposes.

In summary, existing approaches either provide accurate state
estimation without reusable uncertainty, or scalable mapping without
accounting for estimation uncertainty.
Methods that partially address these limitations lack representations
that are both probabilistic and efficient for real-time navigation.
This gap motivates representations that preserve V-
SLAM
uncertainty and
bridge sparse estimation with occupancy-based planning, enabling
uncertainty- and perception-aware navigation in unknown environments.

Planning in Unknown Environments

Autonomous planning in unknown environments has gained traction
recently, as it spans different goal functionalities: rapid exploration completion

44
,

,

,

,

,
increased reconstruction
accuracy
[

,

, and reliable
localization

,

.

II-B
1
Rapid exploration

these methods aim to extend the boundary between known and
unknown space quickly and efficiently. In
Next Best View (NBV)
planning,
candidate viewpoints are sampled and scored to maximize information
gain, while different cost and constraint formulations exist. For example,
Dharmadhikari
et al.

[
46
]
included

UAV
dynamics to achieve feasible and faster exploration.
Bircher
et al.

[
44
]
used
NBV
for receding
horizon autonomous exploration. These approaches often result in
greedy exploration paradigms, leading to dead-ends and failures in
complex scenarios.

An alternative paradigm is frontier-based exploration, which targets the boundary between known and unknown space.
Cieslewski
et al.

[
45
]
selected frontiers that minimize change in velocity to maintain high-speed flight, improving exploration efficiency.
However, the method becomes failure-prone at high speed due to poor estimation performance.
Zhou
et al.

[
3
]
proposed FUEL, an efficient hierarchical framework to perform rapid quadrotor exploration.
Thanks to a frontier information structure, the system performs three hierarchical steps: global tours, optimal local viewpoints and minimum-time trajectories. Even though it delivers faster trajectories compared to other methods, it assumes perfect state estimation.
Zhang
et al.

[
5
]
proposed FALCON, a framework leveraging coverage path guidance for
UAV
s with limited
Field of View (FoV)
sensors.
The method stores the environment topology and generates a global coverage path.
It successfully explores environments using solely on-board state estimation.
However, the system requires a multimodal sensor stream and does not account for the accuracy of SLAM nor drift, thus degrading the mapping quality over time.

II-B
2
Accurate reconstruction

this type of planning introduces alternative information
gain formulations and is often integrated with sampling-based
methods
[
53
,
50
,
54
]
. Palazzolo
et al.

[
55
]

proposed a greedy method selecting views to improve 3D uncertainty of
reconstructed landmarks, but did not address localization.
Schmid
et al.

[
53
]
first proposed a planning
algorithm to extend a single trajectory tree to perform exploration
and 3D reconstruction. The method was extended
in
[
50
]
, leveraging a learned distribution to
sample new views in partially-observed maps. Most frameworks are not
favorable for performing tasks in unknown environments. They require
heuristic-dependent fine tuning to balance exploration and
exploitation. Moreover, they only account for mapping uncertainty,
which often favors novel views which might hinder localization.

II-B
3
Reliable localization

Papachristos
et al.

[
56
]
minimized the
localization uncertainty while exploring the environment. The method
balances exploration and exploitation with belief space planning. It
employs multimodal state estimation
[
12
]
and
accounts for uncertainty. The approach was demonstrated in a
visually-degraded cave environment
in
[
57
]
. While the method performed well in
challenging scenarios, it is computationally heavy and requires a
tailored sensor suite. Moreover, it delivers simple piecewise
linear paths.
Zhang
et al.

[
58
]
proposed a kinodynamic RRT* to perform a perception-aware receding-horizon approach that generates a collection of possible trajectories and evaluates them in terms of landmark concentration, collision probability, and distance to the goal.

Bartolomei
et al.

[
59
]
proposed a
kinodynamic A
∗
to introduce semantic segmentation. The planned
paths explore reliable features and discard clouds, water and grass. The
trajectory is smoothed based on a compound cost formulation leveraging
B-splines optimization. It delivers trajectories balancing smoothness
and co-visibility of as many landmarks as possible. The method
requires fine-tuning for the cost and assumes perfect knowledge of
both robot pose and map. Moreover, the approach is tailored for

UAVs
and does not leverage the full 3D state space. Similarly,
Chen
et al.

[
8
]
introduced a perception-aware
trajectory optimization method for
UAVs
. The system plans in

\mathbb{R}^{3}
and optimizes a B-spline to select yaws steering the camera
towards higher number of landmarks. The method leverages ray-casting
and combines a voxel grid and an
Euclidean Signed Distance Function (ESDF)

[
28
]
.
However, it is not suitable for unknown environments, as it assumes a
known sparse map and perfect localization, using estimation merely as
an evaluation tool.

Kondo
et al.

[
6
]
devised an uncertainty-aware
planning system to handle dynamic obstacles. The method implements a
multi-layer planning strategy introducing backup and contingency
trajectories. It tracks dynamic obstacles through a vision-based

Extended Kalman Filter (EKF)
, while concurrently estimating an accurate map using
Lidar-Inertial Odometry. The system adds safety factoring dynamic
obstacle uncertainties leveraging the tracking covariances, achieves
high success rates and delivers high speed trajectories. However, to
do so, it relies on the use of Lidar, IMU and RGB-D cameras
simultaneously. The produced trajectories are not perception-aware,
as the planning does not account for the quality of visual features
along the path.

In this work, we focus on a holistic system factoring estimation and planning simultaneously.
We focus on
Exploratory Point-to-Point
motion, assuming the robot traverses an unknown environment from its current position to a prescribed goal.
As such, it explores solely necessary sections of the map to successfully reach the goal.
We propose a novel planning framework to include uncertainty and perception-awareness to deliver safe trajectories even in degraded visual conditions, relying solely on a front-mounted camera.

II-C

Perception-aware Trajectory Optimization

Trajectory generation delivers optimal trajectories while satisfying constraints, which can be addressed in two ways.
Hard-constrained methods deliver guarantees on solutions, but hinder computation time and feasibility, while soft-constrained methods introduce cost terms to penalize constraint violations, but cannot guarantee they will never be violated.

A number of methods use smoothing to satisfy constraints while optimizing for perception quality.
Murali
et al.

[
60
]
introduced an optimization method to maximize co-visibility of large numbers of features for
UAVs
.
The same formulation is used by Bartolomei
et al.

[
59
]
and enforce soft-constraints.
The trajectories are feasible but the process does not scale effectively with map size.
Moreover, these approaches do not account for exploration and assume complete knowledge of the map a priori.

Similarly, Spasojevic
et al.

[
61
]
proposed a minimum time parametrization preserving features in the camera
FoV
.
Most methods perform optimization in
\mathbb{R}^{3}
or
\mathbb{R}^{4}
, not leveraging the full range of motion of the cameras.
Specht
et al.

[
7
]
proposed a data-driven perception-aware cost formulation for space robots in
\mathrm{SE(3)}
.
The method, however, is computationally inefficient and is not suitable for real-time execution.
Chen
et al.

[
8
]
proposed a two step approach optimizing position and yaw, achieving high-speed perception-aware flight thanks to a novel co-visibility cost formulation, but it does not scale with large maps, which are also required to be known a priori.
Tordesillas
et al.

[
1
]
delivered plans that account for the camera
FoV
to avoid obstacles, neglecting the actual perception-quality and assuming perfect localization.

In this work, we introduce a novel perception-aware trajectory optimization method on
\mathrm{SE(3)}
.
We leverage the Lie Group and Algebra to efficiently express and handle hard-constraints and uncertainties.
The method is formulated as a
MLE
based on probabilistic visibility of landmarks.
UNSEEN-OPT handles exploration, occlusions and large maps thanks to ray-casting queries and local submaps.
Additionally, we steer the camera towards low-uncertainty landmarks rather than just maximizing their number as other methods do.

III-B

System Overview

To solve the
Exploratory Point-to-Point
motion (
3
) we introduce a perception- and uncertainty-aware method where the information about uncertainty flows end-to-end. In particular, UNSEEN-SLAM receives RGB-D images and computes uncertainties on its estimates, which are concurrently propagated downstream to UNSEEN-MAP and reinjected as regularization terms in the backend optimization. The estimated map
\mathcal{G}
is fed through UNSEEN-MAP, which updates voxel occupancies accounting for the spatial uncertainty of each estimated landmark. UNSEEN-MAP delivers an efficient memory representation of the map, while allowing to enforce obstacle avoidance and visibility queries.
UNSEEN-PLAN leverages these information to produce perception-aware paths promoting localization accuracy, while delivering free-space representation and local visible submaps, accounting for occlusions and
FoV
constraints. Finally, the path is smoothed to avoid venturing in unknown space and promoting visibility of landmarks with low uncertainty. The resulting trajectory is fed in receding-horizon to a low level controller, which will promote state estimation quality by steering the camera towards areas which were confidently estimated before.

IV
UNSEEN-SLAM & Occupancy Mapping

Let
\mathcal{K}_{k}=\{\mathbf{T}_{i}\}_{1}^{N}
be the set of all
N
keyframes and
\mathcal{G}_{k}=\{\ell_{j}\}_{1}^{M}
the
global map of
M
landmarks at time
k
; the state estimates of all keyframes and
landmarks is given by:

\mathcal{X}_{k}=\left[\mathcal{K}_{k},\mathcal{G}_{k}\right].

(4)

In common Visual SLAM methods, such as
[
23
]
, the
Maximum a Posteriori (MAP)
estimate is
obtained by solving the bundle adjustment over all measurements:

\mathcal{X}_{k}^{*}=\arg\max p(\mathcal{X}_{k}|\mathcal{C}_{k})=\arg\min\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}_{k}}\sum_{l\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\|r_{\mathcal{C}}{{}_{i,l}}\|_{\Sigma_{\mathcal{C}}}^{2},

(5)

where
r_{\mathcal{C}}{{}_{i,l}}
is the residual of the reprojection error of
\ell_{l}
seen from
\mathbf{T}_{i}
, weighted on measurement covariance
\Sigma_{\mathcal{C}}
.
Then, a visibility graph is built, and old keyframes, i.e., the ones
viewing the active landmarks, are inserted in the optimization as
fixed.

In contrast, in this work, we re-optimize old poses, leveraging covariance information on both poses and landmarks.
We introduce Tikhonov Regularization
[
64
]
residuals on latest estimate of keyframes
\{\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{K}_{k}}
and landmarks
\{\hat{\ell}_{i}\}_{i\in\mathcal{G}_{k}}
weighted by their marginal covariances
\Sigma_{\mathbf{T}},\Sigma_{\ell}
:

\displaystyle r_{\mathbf{T}_{i}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{i}\boxminus\mathbf{T}_{i}

(6a)

\displaystyle r_{\ell_{i}}

\displaystyle=\hat{\ell}_{i}-\ell_{i}.

(6b)

With slight abuse of notation, we refer to
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{G}
as their set of indices, following the same convention as
[
65
]
. The resulting
MAP
problem becomes:

\displaystyle\mathcal{X}_{k}^{*}=\arg\min_{\mathcal{X}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}_{k}}\sum_{\ell\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\|r_{\mathcal{C}}{{}_{i,l}}\|_{\Sigma_{\mathcal{C}}}^{2}

\displaystyle+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{K}_{k}}\|r_{\mathbf{T}}{{}_{i}}\|_{\Sigma_{\mathbf{T}}{{}_{i}}}^{2}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{C}_{k}}\|r_{\ell}{{}_{i}}\|_{\Sigma_{\ell}{{}_{i}}}^{2}\;,

(7)

which is iteratively solved using the Levenberg-Marquardt method. The solution of (
7
) is related to the following normal equation:

\mathbf{J}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{r}}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\delta\mathcal{X}=\mathbf{J}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{r}}^{-1}\mathbf{r}_{0}\;,

(8)

where
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{r}}^{-1}
is the stacked measurement covariance;
\delta\mathcal{X}
is the perturbation around the linearization point
\mathcal{X}_{0}
;
\mathbf{r}_{0}
is the vector of stacked residuals, also computed in
\mathcal{X}_{0}
:

\mathbf{r}_{0}=\begin{bmatrix}r_{\mathcal{C}}{{}_{0,0}}(\mathcal{X}_{0})\\
\vdots\\
r_{\mathcal{C}}{{}_{N,M}}(\mathcal{X}_{0})\\
r_{\mathbf{T}}{{}_{0}}(\mathcal{X}_{0})\\
\vdots\\
r_{\mathbf{T}}{{}_{N}}(\mathcal{X}_{0})\\
r_{\ell}{{}_{0}}(\mathcal{X}_{0})\\
\vdots\\
r_{\ell}{{}_{N}}(\mathcal{X}_{0})\end{bmatrix}\;,

(9)

and
\mathbf{J}
is its Jacobian respect to the state
\mathcal{X}
.

IV-A

Covariance Computation

Since computational efficiency becomes a critical issue
for any SLAM problem on medium-sized areas, we start by identifying on
the left-hand side of (
8
) the Information
Matrix

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{X}}=\mathbf{J}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathbf{r}}^{-1}\mathbf{J}\;.

(10)

To solve our estimation problem with uncertainty, we need to
efficiently compute the covariance

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{X}}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{X}}^{-1}
. To this end, we
exploit the known structure of the bundle adjustment
problem
[
66
]
:

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{X}}=\begin{bmatrix}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{K}\mathcal{K}}&\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{K}\mathcal{G}}\\
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{K}\mathcal{G}}^{\mathsf{T}}&\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{G}}\end{bmatrix}.

(11)

During the local bundle adjustment, backend optimization libraries
such as
g2o

[
18
]
and
GTSAM
[
20
]
compute the Schur complement
\mathbf{S}
to perform back-substitution and update poses with fewer computation. This is statistically equivalent to computing the conditional covariance of
\mathcal{K}
given
\mathcal{G}
:

\mathbf{S}=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{K}\mathcal{K}}-\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{K}\mathcal{G}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{G}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{K}\mathcal{G}}^{\mathsf{T}}.

(12)

Our method optimizes landmarks and updates the underlying global
map
\mathcal{G}
, which is divided in active and inactive maps

\mathcal{M}_{\textup{A}}
,
\mathcal{M}_{\textup{I}}
.
In particular, because the
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{G}}
block is block-diagonal and symmetric, its inverse can be computed as follows:

\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{G}}^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}\Lambda_{\ell_{0},\ell_{0}}^{-1}&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\Lambda_{\ell_{1},\ell_{1}}^{-1}&\cdots&\mathbf{0}\\
\vdots&\vdots&\ddots&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{0}&\cdots&\Lambda_{\ell_{M},\ell_{M}}^{-1}\end{bmatrix}.

(13)

Inverting this block of size
3M\times 3M
goes from complexity

\mathcal{O}(M^{3})
to a linear complexity
\mathcal{O}(M)
after
leveraging its structure. This operation is embarassingly parallelisable
[
67
]
, with
a substantial reduction of the computation time.

Once
\mathbf{S}
is available, it is possible to compute the
marginal covariances of the state
\mathcal{X}_{k}
. Once more, we study
the structure of the resulting covariance matrix, noting we are only
interested in the diagonal blocks:

\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{X}}=\Biggl[\begin{array}[]{ccc}\pagecolor{green!30}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{K}\mathcal{K}}&\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{K}\mathcal{G}}\\
\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{K}\mathcal{G}}^{\mathsf{T}}&\pagecolor{green!30}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{G}}\end{array}\Biggr]

(14)

Leveraging Schur Decomposition, we reduce unnecessary and expensive
operations and compute the selected blocks
[
66
]
:

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{K}\mathcal{K}}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{S}^{\dagger}

(15a)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{G}}

\displaystyle=\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{G}}^{-1}+\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{G}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{K}\mathcal{G}}^{\mathsf{T}}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{\mathcal{K}\mathcal{K}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{K}\mathcal{G}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\mathcal{G}\mathcal{G}}^{-1}

(15b)

where the superscript
†
represents the Moore-Penrose
Pseudo-Inverse. The computation of the Pseudo-inverse is

\mathcal{O}(N^{3})
, while the Schur decomposition is

\mathcal{O}(NM^{2})
.

The covariance extracted expresses the accumulated uncertainty,
combining the effects of the measurement uncertainty and of the
feature extraction and matching. In particular, textured areas with
unreliable features will lead to highly uncertain landmark positions,
introducing a covariance increase in the direction of the
surface. This effect will be leveraged to produce occupancy grids for
navigation using solely the optimized landmarks from UNSEEN-SLAM.

Covariance Extraction Implementation

The covariance extraction described in Sec.
IV-A

is triggered at each local bundle adjustment, but it takes approximately
10
times the time of a single local bundle
adjustment. For this reason, we perform it asynchronously, as depicted in Fig.
3
.

After the feature extraction, the
Tracking
thread estimates poses based on matched features. When new keyframes are selected for insertion, the

thread optimizes the active map
\mathcal{M}_{\textup{A}}
and associated keyframes
\mathcal{K}_{k}

Once triggered, the

IV-C

UNSEEN Occupancy Mapping

At runtime, the UNSEEN-SLAM front-end tracks the active map

\mathcal{M}_{\textup{A}}\subset\mathcal{G}
, which is treated as an
incoming measurement in the form of a point cloud and fed to the UNSEEN-MAP
representation of the environment. We adopt
Octomap
[
29
]
as the basic structure and introduce
an approach to treat uncertainties and handle local and visible submaps

\mathcal{M},\mathcal{M}_{\textup{V}}
. In particular, we integrate spatial
uncertainty within the probabilistic nature of Octomap, thus
accounting for the spatial extent of measurement uncertainties.

When a new landmark with associated covariance is obtained, its
covariance ellipsoid is computed based on a user-specified threshold
of confidence
\chi_{\textup{Th}}^{2}
. All cells falling inside the covariance ellipsoid
are involved in the probabilistic update. The update is computed by
integrating the PDF over the volume occupied by each voxel. The
resulting integral yields a probability of occupancy which is factored
through the Octomap Bayesian update.

In this work, we propose a new formulation to update the hit
probability for each voxel
n
. A simplified toy example in 2D using
quadtrees is shown in Fig.
4
.

Fig. 4:
Quadtree toy example of occupancy mapping.
Left:
a new measurement with associated covariance is obtained.
Middle:
a bounding box at the desired resolution of the tree is created.
Right:
each cell is filled with the integral of the Gaussian distribution of the sample and added to the map as log odds.

For each landmark
\ell_{j}\in\mathcal{M}
, we update all voxels contained in the ellipsoid defined by the following condition:

(\ell_{j}-x)^{\mathsf{T}}\Sigma_{\ell}{{}_{j}}(\ell_{j}-x)\leq\chi_{\textup{Th}}^{2},

(16)

hence, the likelihood of the voxel containing the estimated landmark
is:

p(\ell\in V_{n})=\int_{V_{n}}\mathcal{N}(\ell_{j},\Sigma_{\ell}{{}_{j}})\,\mathrm{d}x.

(17)

We then define the voxel occupancy probability conditioned on the
landmark as:

p(n|\ell_{j})=\,p_{\max}-p_{\textup{th}}(1-p(\ell\in V_{n})).

(18)

This value is then fed to the Octomap log-odds formulation as the

hit probability
. This approach weighs the
occupancy based on the uncertainty of the observation. Therefore, areas with
high confidence are more likely to be considered occupied, while
uncertain sections contribute weakly to the occupied space. This
approach is then effectively coupled with Euclidean clustering, to
remove artifacts and outliers produced by UNSEEN-SLAM.

V-A

Global Planning

To provide optimal plans at each iteration, we use the current
knowledge of the entire environment expressed as free and occupied
space within the UNSEEN Occupancy Map
(Sec.
IV-C
). During
Global
Planning
, we generate a path to the goal ignoring potential
obstacles in the unknown space, i.e., assuming

\Omega\subseteq\mathcal{F}
.

From RRT*, we generate a tree

\mathcal{T}
and obtain the optimal path
\Pi_{\textup{G}}
. During this step, the robot
is guided toward exploring new areas. Each sampled state
\mathbf{T}_{\text{new}}

is scored based on an approximated visibility metric:

\zeta_{i}=\frac{V_{o}}{V_{\textup{FOV}}},

(19)

where
\zeta_{i}
is the cost of node
\nu_{i}\in\mathcal{T}
,
V_{\textup{FOV}}
is the volume of visible voxels in the

FoV
, and
V_{o}
is the occupied volume of voxels in the
local submap
\mathcal{M}
. This metric encourages plans where the
camera is pointing towards occupied regions, which are more likely to contain
textured areas and reliable landmarks for localization.

V-B

FoV
Truncation and Visibility Checks

To prevent the robot from entering unknown areas before they are
explored, we verify the global path
\Pi_{\textup{G}}
from start to
goal. For this purpose, we impose constraints on the tree nodes

\nu
and edges
\mathcal{E}
to ensure obstacle avoidance, dynamic
feasibility, and compliance with
FoV
distance limits. These
constraints remove the overly optimistic assumption that unknown space lies
within free space (
\Omega\subseteq\mathcal{F}
), which would produce unsafe or unfeasible paths.

Perception and uncertainty are handled through a

FoV
truncation
step applied after the global plan is
generated. Specifically, the plan is truncated at the boundary between
known and unknown space. Each remaining state is then validated by
checking the visibility of occupied voxels; that is, its
FoV

V_{\textup{FOV}}
must include at least some occupied voxels.

This truncation and constraint validation yields a new local path

\Pi_{\textup{L}}\subseteq\Pi_{\textup{G}}
that is fully contained in
known free space, never extends beyond the currently visible region, and
always orients the camera toward textured areas. As a result,

\Pi_{\textup{L}}
is conservative and safe to execute: the robot will not
enter unknown space without exploring it first, and it will not lose
feature tracks due to insufficient texture.

V-C

Convex Decomposition and Smoothing

After verifying safety, the discrete path
\Pi_{\textup{L}}
is converted into a smooth, dynamically feasible trajectory on
\mathrm{SE(3)}
. This requires extracting local environment information, constructing convex free-space regions, and performing trajectory smoothing under geometric and dynamic constraints.

For each edge
\mathcal{E}
, UNSEEN-MAP provides a local submap
\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{E}}
and its visible subset
\mathcal{M}_{\textup{V}}^{\mathcal{E}}
. Their unions

\mathcal{M}=\bigcup_{\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{M}^{\mathcal{E}},\qquad\mathcal{M}_{\textup{V}}=\bigcup_{\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{M}_{\textup{V}}^{\mathcal{E}}

(20)

identify the relevant obstacles and occlusions along the path. These become seeds to perform convex decomposition, delivering free-space polytopes
\mathcal{P}^{\mathcal{E}}\subseteq\hat{\mathcal{F}}
, and defining the global free-space representation as

\mathcal{P}=\bigcup_{\mathcal{E}}\mathcal{P}^{\mathcal{E}}.

(21)

These convex sets constrain the trajectory optimization described in Sec.
VI
. Path segments are time-parameterized based on length and dynamic limits, and initialized with a constant nominal velocity
v
.

V-C
1
Free-Space Extraction and Convex Decomposition

The map provides access to the estimated free space

\hat{\mathcal{F}}=\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\mathcal{G},

(22)

and to occupied voxels
\hat{\mathcal{O}}
. Feasibility of each tree edge is verified by ray-casting: an edge is accepted if the straight segment between its endpoints intersects neither occupied nor unknown voxels.

During smoothing, we again query the occupancy grid to gather obstacle voxels near the path. Exploiting voxel convexity, we extract their vertices and apply the convex decomposition method from
[
69
]
to generate conservative convex polytopes enclosing free space. These polytopes serve as convex constraints in the optimizer.

V-C
2
Efficient Map Queries and Visibility

Each occupied voxel stores the landmarks contributing to its occupancy, enabling efficient obstacle queries. For each node
\nu\in\Pi_{\textup{L}}
, a bounded map query retrieves local obstacles, which are inflated to account for the robot’s size.

Visibility is computed using ray-casting, producing the local visible submaps
\mathcal{M}_{\textup{V}}^{\mathcal{E}}
. In contrast to prior works
[
61
,
59
,
2
,
8
]
, this captures occlusions and sensor
FoV
limitations, improving smoothing accuracy while maintaining low computational cost.

V-C
3
Smoothing

Finally, the edges of the local path
\mathcal{E}\in\Pi_{\textup{L}}
are interpolated with B-splines for smoothing. Once an initial and final time is appointed for an edge, based on its length and nominal velocity
v
, the segment is optimized to deliver a continuous trajectory in
\mathrm{SE(3)}
. Both segments end conditions become the starting boundary conditions for the next. The smoothing is iteratively performed over all edges in the path until a piecewise smooth and continuous trajectory
\hat{\Theta}^{*}
is obtained, as detailed in Sec.
VI
.

VI
Probabilistic Trajectory Optimization

We define trajectory optimization as a
MLE
problem, where we aim to maximize the likelihood of maintaining low-uncertainty landmarks in the field of view.
Leveraging the visible submap
\mathcal{M}_{\textup{V}}
, we can query the subset of landmarks that can be seen along an edge
\mathcal{E}
.
We model the visibility of a landmark by defining the camera direction
d\in\mathbb{S}^{2}
and the landmark’s direction as its projected position on the sphere:

\pi(\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{t},\ell_{i})=\frac{\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{t}\ell_{i}}{\|\ell_{i}\|}.

(23)

the landmark’s covariance is also projected, obtaining:

\varsigma_{\ell_{i}}=\frac{1}{\|\ell_{i}\|}\mathbf{R}\Sigma_{\ell}{{}_{i}}\mathbf{R}^{\mathsf{T}}.

(24)

We can thus formulate the likelihood of observing a landmark
\ell{{}_{i}}
in the visible map
\mathcal{M}_{\textup{V}}
from pose
\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{t}
as a Fisher-Bingham distribution, equivalent to a multivariate Gaussian distribution wrapped on the unit sphere
\mathbb{S}^{2}

[
70
]
:

p(d|\mathbf{T}_{t},\ell_{i})\propto(\pi(\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{t},\ell_{i})-d)^{\mathsf{T}}\varsigma_{\ell_{i}}^{-1}(\pi(\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{t},\ell_{i})-d).

(25)

Given (
25
) we compute the trajectory by maximizing the likelihood of
observing the landmarks in the visible map
\mathcal{M}_{\textup{V}}
:

\hat{\Theta}^{*}=\arg\max_{\hat{\Theta}}\prod_{t=1}^{N}\prod_{i=1}^{M}p(d|\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{t},\ell_{i}).

(26)

Introducing the log-likelihood of (
25
):

\displaystyle\log\bigl(p(d|\mathbf{T}_{t},\ell_{i})\bigr)

\displaystyle=\log(e^{-\frac{1}{2}\|\pi(\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{t},\ell_{i})-d\|_{\varsigma_{\ell_{i}}}^{2}})

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{2}\|\pi(\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{t},\ell_{i})-d\|_{\varsigma_{\ell_{i}}}^{2},

(27)

we finally obtain the optimal trajectory by minimizing (
27
):

\displaystyle\hat{\Theta}^{*}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\hat{\Theta}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{f}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{1}{2}\|(\pi(\hat{\mathbf{T}}_{t},\ell_{i})-d)\|_{\varsigma_{\ell_{i}}}^{2}=

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\hat{\Theta}}\Gamma(\hat{\Theta},\mathcal{G}).

(28)

VI-A

B-Spline optimization on Special Euclidean Group

The infinite-dimensional optimization problem
in (
28
) is solved introducing a smooth
parametrization of the trajectory of the poses:

\hat{\mathbf{T}}(t)=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}(t)&\mathbf{t}(t)\\
\mathbf{0}&1\end{bmatrix},

(29)

using a B-spline representation of the trajectory with the curve

\Gamma:t\in[0,1]\rightarrow\mathrm{SE(3)}
. To this end,
we first leverage the topological structure of the Lie group

\mathrm{SE(3)}
as the outer product of
\mathbb{R}^{3}\times SO(3)
, thus
opening to the optimization over two separate curves

(\mathbf{R}(t),\mathbf{t}(t))
.

As in
[
7
]
, we express the rotational motion
using a composition of an initial orientation
\mathbf{R}_{0}
and an
angle-axis vector
\xi(t)\in\mathbb{RP}^{3}
, where

[\xi^{\wedge}]\in\mathfrak{so}(3)
is mapped through

SO(3)
exponential map
\exp:\mathfrak{so}(3)\rightarrow SO(3)
.
Consequently, the following a curve:

\mathbf{R}(t)=\mathbf{R}_{0}\exp([\xi(t)]^{\wedge}),

(30)

defines the rotational trajectories of robot’s body frame.
Therefore, we can express translation and
rotation separately as:

\displaystyle\mathbf{t}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\prescript{\mathbf{t}}{}{\mathrm{K}}}B_{i}(t)\prescript{\mathbf{t}}{}{\mathbf{c}}_{i},

\displaystyle\xi(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\prescript{\xi}{}{\mathrm{K}}}B_{i}(t)\prescript{\xi}{}{\mathbf{c}}_{i},

(31)

where
B_{i}(t)
are the B-spline basis functions, while

\prescript{\mathbf{t}}{}{\mathbf{c}}_{i}
and
\prescript{\xi}{}{\mathbf{c}}_{i}
are the control
points of the translational and rotational B-spline, respectively.

VI-B

Constraints

To produce feasible trajectories, avoid collisions, and promote
perceptual improvement of the motion, we introduce motion, obstacle and platform
constraints while also keeping the problem solvable in real time.

VI-B
1
Motion Constraints

To obtain a dynamically feasible trajectory, we enforce conservative bounds on the
robot control effort. Additionally, we actively reduce
motion blur and distortion effects by limiting acceleration and
velocities, for both the linear and angular quantities. Moreover,
in view of the convex hull
[
71
]
property of the
B-splines, imposing constraints on the control points
guarantees their satisfaction on the infinite-dimensional continuous
curve.

For the translational trajectory, we define the
velocity and the accelerations as:

\displaystyle v(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\prescript{\mathbf{t}}{}{\mathrm{K}}-1}B_{i}(t)\>\prescript{\mathbf{t}}{}{\mathbf{c}}_{i}^{\prime},

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\prescript{\xi}{}{\mathrm{K}}-1}B_{i}(t)\>\prescript{\xi}{}{\mathbf{c}}_{i}^{\prime},

(32a)

\displaystyle a(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\prescript{\mathbf{t}}{}{\mathrm{K}}-2}B_{i}(t)\>\prescript{\mathbf{t}}{}{\mathbf{c}}_{i}^{\prime\prime},

\displaystyle\xi^{\prime\prime}(t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{\prescript{\xi}{}{\mathrm{K}}-2}B_{i}(t)\>\prescript{\xi}{}{\mathbf{c}}_{i}^{\prime\prime}.

(32b)

For the angular velocity and acceleration, we apply a
small-perturbation approach by mapping the derivatives on the Lie
algebra to dynamic quantities using the left Jacobian

J_{\mathit{l}}(\xi)=J_{\mathit{l}}
and its derivative

J_{\mathit{l}}(\xi,\xi^{\prime})=J_{\mathit{l}}^{\prime}
, thus yielding:

\displaystyle\omega

\displaystyle=J_{\mathit{l}}\xi^{\prime},

(33a)

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=J_{\mathit{l}}^{\prime}\xi^{\prime}+J_{\mathit{l}}\xi^{\prime\prime}.

(33b)

In order to constrain the orientations, we prescribe limits on the
angular components of
\xi
, obtaining a conservative upper
bound vector
\bar{\xi}
. This way, we constrain the
trajectory to be enclosed within a Geodesic Ball around
\mathbf{R}_{0}

as in
[
72
]
:

\displaystyle B(\mathbf{R}_{0},\|\bar{\xi}\|)

\displaystyle=\Bigl\{\mathbf{R}\in\mathrm{SO(3)}\>|

\displaystyle\|\log(\mathbf{R}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}_{0})^{\vee}{}\|\leq\|\bar{\xi}\|\Bigl\}.

(34)

Now, we can effectively solve for all higher derivatives limits by
defining the following nonlinear system:

\begin{bmatrix}\bar{J}_{\mathit{l}}(\bar{\xi})&0\\
\bar{J}_{\mathit{l}}^{\prime}(\bar{\xi},\bar{\xi}^{\prime})&\bar{J}_{\mathit{l}}(\bar{\xi})\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\xi}^{\prime}\\
\bar{\xi}^{\prime\prime}\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}\bar{\omega}\\
\bar{\alpha}\end{bmatrix}.

(35)

Despite its nonlinearity, (
35
) is lower triangular,
therefore the analytical solution is given by:

\displaystyle\bar{J}_{\mathit{l}}

\displaystyle=J_{\mathit{l}}(\bar{\xi})

(36a)

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\bar{J}_{\mathit{l}}^{-1}\bar{\omega}

(36b)

\displaystyle\bar{\xi}^{\prime\prime}

\displaystyle=\bar{J}_{\mathit{l}}^{-1}(\bar{J}_{\mathit{l}}^{\prime}\bar{\xi}^{\prime}-\bar{\alpha}).

(36c)

The main outcome of the proposed approach is that all the needed
quantities can be computed at start time, while the user-defined
constraints can be conservatively applied on the
\mathrm{SE(3)}
trajectory
as follows:

\displaystyle\|\prescript{\mathbf{t}}{}{\mathbf{c}}_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\bar{\mathbf{t}},

\displaystyle\quad\|\prescript{\xi}{}{\mathbf{c}}_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\bar{\xi},

(37a)

\displaystyle\|\prescript{\mathbf{t}}{}{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime}_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\bar{\mathbf{v}},

\displaystyle\quad\|\prescript{\xi}{}{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime}_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\bar{\xi^{\prime}},

(37b)

\displaystyle\|\prescript{\mathbf{t}}{}{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime\prime}_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\bar{\mathbf{a}},

\displaystyle\quad\|\prescript{\xi}{}{\mathbf{c}}^{\prime\prime}_{i}\|_{\infty}\leq\bar{\xi^{\prime\prime}},

(37c)

where (
37
) can be summarized as the following matrix inequality:

\mathbf{A}_{\textup{M}}\boldsymbol{\tau}\leq\mathbf{b}_{\textup{M}}.

(38)

To summarize, this approach introduces some level of conservativeness
and an efficient formulation of constraints as linear
inequalities. The method allows for efficient constraints on both
translational and rotational components of motions and leverages the
properties of B-splines to guarantee constraint satisfaction. In a
similar way, we can clamp the B-splines to fix boundary conditions on
each edge of the path
\mathcal{E}
.

\mathcal{E}

\mathcal{M}_{\textup{V}}
and a free-space convex polytope, resulting in a
selection of non-occluded nearby landmarks and a navigable portion of
free space
\mathcal{F}
, as detailed in Sec.
V
. We
constrain each portion of
\hat{\Theta}^{\star}
to lie in the free-space polytope,
ensuring satisfaction of (

).
Particularly, we leverage the convex hull property of B-Splines to
conservatively guarantee safety against collisions:

\mathbf{A}_{\mathcal{F}}\prescript{\mathbf{t}}{}{\mathbf{c}}_{i}\leq\mathbf{b}_{\mathcal{F}}\text{ for }i\in\prescript{\mathbf{t}}{}{\mathrm{K}}

(39)

where
\mathbf{A}_{\mathcal{F}}
and
\mathbf{b}_{\mathcal{F}}

are the matrix representing the half-spaces generating the polytope.

We generate trajectories for a
UAV
introducing a differential
flatness constraint. Our formulation allows for factoring the
specific system dynamics directly in the optimizer formulation, thus
maintaining a level of abstraction and generality. Since we can define the acceleration along

\hat{\Theta}^{\star}
as
in (
32
), the specific thrust vector for
our UAV is defined as:

\mathbf{f}=a-g\mathrm{e}_{2}^{W}

(40)

where

is the gravitational acceleration. For the drone to follow
the trajectory
\mathbf{t}

\mathbf{R}\mathrm{e}_{2}^{B}=\frac{a-g\mathrm{e}_{2}^{W}}{\|a-g\mathrm{e}_{2}^{W}\|}=f.

(41)

By defining an arbitrary orientation
\mathbf{R}_{\textup{align}}

satisfying the condition (
41
) and by
constructing a fictitious frame with
\mathrm{e}_{0}^{B}
in the
forward direction, the constraint satisfaction is ensured iff

\mathbf{R}
differs from
\mathbf{R}_{\textup{align}}
by a rotation of

\psi
around
\mathrm{e}_{2}^{B}
, i.e.

\mathbf{R}_{\psi}=\exp\left(\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
\psi\end{bmatrix}^{\wedge}\right),

(42)

leading to

\mathbf{R}_{\textup{align}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}=\mathbf{R}_{\psi}.

(43)

The constraint is then mapped onto the Lie Algebra as:

\xi=\log(\big[\mathbf{R}_{\textup{align}}^{\mathsf{T}}\mathbf{R}\big])=\begin{bmatrix}0\\
0\\
\psi\end{bmatrix}^{\wedge}.

(44)

Hence, due to the homography between the Lie Algebra and
\mathbb{R}^{3}
, we
factor the constraint as a nonlinear equality:

h(\boldsymbol{\tau})=0,

(45)

which is a function of the control points. Solving the constrained
optimization problem, we can effectively deliver dynamically feasible
trajectories satisfying multiple linear
constraints (
39
) and one nonlinear
equality constraint (

Gradients

The proposed B-Spline formulation benefits from efficient gradient
computation with respect to its coefficients as defined
in (
31
). Expressing the optimization
variables as

\boldsymbol{\tau}=\left[\prescript{\xi}{}{\mathbf{c}}_{0},\ldots,\prescript{\xi}{}{\mathbf{c}}_{\prescript{\xi}{}{\mathrm{K}}},\prescript{\mathbf{t}}{}{\mathbf{c}}_{0},\ldots,\prescript{\mathbf{t}}{}{\mathbf{c}}_{\prescript{\mathbf{t}}{}{\mathrm{K}}}\right]
, we
can efficiently split the gradient computation over a cost function

\Gamma
as:

\frac{\partial\Gamma}{\partial\boldsymbol{\tau}}=\frac{\partial\Gamma}{\partial\hat{\Theta}}\frac{\partial\hat{\Theta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\tau}}.

(46)

We exploit our formulation of the pose:

\frac{\partial\hat{\Theta}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\tau}}=\frac{\partial\hat{\Theta}}{\partial[\mathbf{R},\mathbf{t}]}\frac{\partial[\mathbf{R},\mathbf{t}]}{\partial\boldsymbol{\tau}},

(47)

and the B-spline linearity with respect to the control points to further
rewrite:

\frac{\partial[\mathbf{R},\mathbf{t}]}{\partial\boldsymbol{\tau}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_{0}\frac{\partial\exp([\xi]^{\wedge})}{\partial\xi}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}\frac{\partial[\xi,\mathbf{t}]}{\partial\boldsymbol{\tau}},

(48)

and finally introducing the gradient of the B-spline with respect to its control points
\mathbf{B}

\frac{\partial[\mathbf{R},\mathbf{t}]}{\partial\boldsymbol{\tau}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{R}_{0}J_{\mathit{l}}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\mathbf{I}\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}\prescript{\xi}{}{\mathbf{B}}&\mathbf{0}\\
\mathbf{0}&\prescript{\mathbf{t}}{}{\mathbf{B}}\end{bmatrix}.

(49)

VI-E

Validity of B-Spline Representation on
SO(3)

In this work, we parameterize the rotational trajectory using a
B-spline in the Lie algebra
\mathfrak{so}(3)
, mapped to
\mathrm{SO(3)}

through the exponential map. This representation provides smooth
interpolation and enables direct enforcement of motion constraints in
a vector space. While cumulative B-splines on Lie
groups
[
73
]
offer a fully group-consistent
formulation based on recursive compositions and
Baker-Campbell-Hausdorff (BCH)

[
74
]
corrections, our setting does not require such
machinery. The trajectory optimization explicitly constrains angular
velocity and acceleration, ensuring that consecutive control points
increments remain well within the injectivity radius of the
exponential map. As a result, rotations stay in a locally Euclidean
neighborhood where the logarithm is unique and linear interpolation in

\mathfrak{so}(3)
remains accurate.

These bounded increments eliminate the need to involve an incremental structure, allowing us to rely on properties of convex neighborhoods and local diffeomorphisms without additional complexity.
Moreover, this approach significantly reduces computational overhead, making it suitable for real-time applications while maintaining sufficient accuracy for the constrained maneuvers considered in this work.

VIII
Conclusions

In this work, we introduced UNSEEN, a navigation pipeline that
tightly couples Localization, Mapping, and Planning to produce an
end-to-end, uncertainty-aware navigation system solely relying on a
front-facing RGB-D camera and sparse maps.

We first developed
UNSEEN-SLAM, an uncertainty-driven SLAM module that provides both
poses and landmark estimates together with their covariances. The
extracted uncertainty is directly incorporated into planning through
UNSEEN-MAP, enabling consistent obstacle avoidance, occlusion
handling, and visibility queries. These elements, combined with a
global path from UNSEEN-PLAN, feed into a receding-horizon strategy
on
\mathrm{SE(3)}
using perception-aware B-spline trajectory optimization
UNSEEN-OPT. The resulting formulation introduces a
perception-aware cost that encourages trajectories maximizing
visibility of informative regions of the environment as it is
explored.

Each component was evaluated independently. UNSEEN-SLAM outperforms
standard ORB-SLAM3 in navigation tasks, delivering improvements in

Absolute Translation Error (ATE)
up to 9% and in
Relative Translation Error (RTE)
up to 2%. UNSEEN-PLAN and
UNSEEN-OPT effectively work in simulation even with large maps
with up to 40’000 points when other state-of-the-art methods fail.
The proposed method computes feasible,
constraint-satisfying motions and leads to notably better state
estimation when compared to
PAG
and state-of-the-art
PAW
methods. Finally, UNSEEN was deployed in 10 increasingly
challenging real-world unknown scenarios with limited reliable
textured areas. It delivered 100% success in reaching the goal
without collisions leveraging noisy measurements. The method
demonstrated robust performance to satisfy prescribed motion
constraints, thus providing safe navigation to perform
Exploratory Point-to-Point

mapping.

Future work will include an extension of the method to challenging
outdoor scenarios, thus considering environmental issues increasing
the measurement uncertainties, the inclusion of a wider class of
robotic platforms and the extension of the method to distributed
navigation and SLAM problems.
