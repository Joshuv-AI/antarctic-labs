Multifunctional biohybrid nanoplatform interferes electron transport chain amplifying mitochondrial metabolism-mediated anti-tumor therapy
Authors: Tingting Meng, Ting Gao, Na Yu, Jueshuo Guo, Wenbao Zuo, Jing Chen, Jianhong Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMitochondria play a crucial role in cancer occurrence and progression, and the electron transport chain (ETC) is the core junction of mitochondrial energy metabolism. Interference ETC can induce mitochondrial dysfunction, but the efficacy is limited based on a single ETC complex. In this study, a multifunctional MOF@TK nanoplatform with intrinsic activity was innovatively synthesized by Zinc (Zn2+) and Fenofibric acid (FFa), which was modified through tumor cell membranes transfected with PD-1 (CMP-MOF@TK) to efficaciously induce mitochondrial dysfunction with ETC inactivation and amplified anti-tumor response. Significantly, the CMP-MOF@TK nano-biohybrid platform was effectively taken up following mitochondriotropic movement, and ROS-responsive decomposition releases Zn2+ and FFa. More importantly, the activities of ETC complex I and IV were synchronously inhibited through FFa and Zn2+, blocking electron transport and promoting the generation of ROS associated with increased oxidative stress and mitochondrial depolarisation, which systematacially induces mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction, down-regulating the production of ATP. In addition, the mitochondrial damage-mediated ICD of tumor cells promoted the increased HMGB1 and CRT in the cytomatrix, and tumor cell membrane transfection of PD-1 effectively down-regulated T cell-mediated immune tolerance, effectively activating anti-tumor immune response. In a word, this work offers a potent strategy for developing anti-tumor platforms with multiple functionalities, which provides translational promise for mitochondrial metabolism interference based on ETC dysfunction to strengthen the anti-tumor effect.Keywords: Melanoma, Mitochondria, Electron transport chain (ETC), Immunogenic cell death, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Melanoma is a malignant tumor caused by the uncontrolled division of melanocytes in the neuroectoderm. Patients usually have limited treatment options, poor prognosis and low survival rate, accounting for 80% of skin cancer deaths [1,2]. Given that tumorigenesis, progression and metastasis are intricately linked to intracellular organelles and related molecule, modulation of intracellular organelle and related molecules synthesis has emerged as a non-pharmacological anti-tumor strategy with enhanced controllability, safety and efficacy [3]. Mitochondria, as the power source of cells, participate biosynthesis, signal transduction, bioenergy and stress perception, and play an important role in regulating biological processes such as cell differentiation, proliferation and apoptosis [4,5]. In recent years, mitochondria have become a promising therapeutic target because their metabolism plays a critical role in how cancer cells respond to or evade apoptotic signals [6,7]. Recent related studies have shown that down-regulating oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) can inhibit mitochondrial respiration [8], resulting in higher expression of reactive oxygen species (ROS) [9], decreasing mitochondrial membrane potential, reducing synthesis of adenosine triphosphate (ATP), etc., thereby inducing mitochondrial dysfunction and promoting apoptosis of tumor cells [10,11]. Because the functions of ATP production, aerobic respiration and redox homeostasis are involved in all aspects of tumorigenesis and progression, it is proposed that disruption of the entire mitochondrion, rather than inhibition of a single pathway, may provide a more comprehensive strategy [3] (see Scheme 1).

The mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC), an important component of OXPHOS, is necessary for the metabolism of tumor cells [12]. ETC is composed of four polysubunit complexes, CI and CII transfer high-energy electrons from NADH and flavin adenine dinucleotide (FADH2) generated by the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA) to ubiquinone, then to CIII, and finally to oxygen through CIV, ultimately utilizing fuel to produce adenosine triphosphate (ATP) [7,13,14]. Inhibiting ETC complexes I, III and IV can reduce mitochondrial NAD+ regeneration that are available for other reactions [7], leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, which play a vital role in amplifying anti-tumor effects [[15], [16], [17]]. Studies have shown that fenofibric acid (FFa) can inhibit ETC complex I, leading to electron leakage and reacting with O2 to produce more ROS [18,19]. However, the hydrophilic carboxyl groups in FFa limit their accumulation in mitochondria [19]. The ingenious construction of appropriate nanoplatforms can effectively solve the lower bioavailability and anti-tumor effect of therapeutics.

In recent years, nanoscale metal-organic frameworks (nMOFs) assembled through metal ions or clusters and organic ligands showed a considerable potential to be employed as multifunctional and biocompatible carriers, exhibiting intrinsic activity derived from their components with superior biocompatibility [6,20]. FFa can be used as a desired organic ligand to prepare nMOFs, but down-regulating a single ETC complex function is generally limited to effectively induce mitochondrial dysfunction-mediated anti-tumor efficacy due to the highly adaptive tolerance of the tumor [12,21]. Metal ions can also inhibit tumor energy metabolism by interfering with mitochondrial functions (such as ETC, ROS generation, and metabolic enzyme activity), thus becoming a potential anti-tumor strategy. Related researches have demonstrated that metal zinc (Zn2+) can inhibit ETC complex IV, block mitochondrial aerobic respiration, enhance ·O2− and H2O2, generation and achieve rapid accumulation of endogenous ROS [22,23]. In addition, actively enriching therapeutics at the action sites is an important prerequisite for exerting therapeutic effects, which can be achieved through constructing stimulus-responsive nanoplatforms, such as functionalized nMOFs, based on the special microenvironment of tumors [[24], [25], [26]]. Whereas, developing nMOFs that can effectively balance good biocompatibility and multifunctionality to present meaningful efficacy remains challenging.

An increase in mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mito-ROS) along with a decline in ATP synthesis can efficiently induce damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) release, thereby initiating mitochondria damage-mediated ICD, which is beneficial to inducing DC maturation [4,18]. However, CD8+ T cell infiltration is insufficient, and the CD8+ T cells that infiltrate tumors frequently exhibit dysfunction in an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, necessitating strategies to improve their infiltration and restore their function in tumors [27]. Programmed death receptor 1 (PD-1) and its ligand (PD-L1) are important immune checkpoint pathway molecules. The primary function of PD-L1 on the cell membrane is to inhibit the antitumor activity of activated T cells. Therefore, blocking the cell membrane PD-L1/PD-1 axis is an ideal target for tumor immunotherapy and has excellent potential [28].

In this study, MOF@TK nanoplatform with intrinsic activity was firstly constructed by Zn2+ and FFa, which was modified through tumor cell membranes transfected with PD-1 (CMP-MOF@TK) to disrupt intracellular mitochondria and amplify antitumor response significantly. After being taken up by tumor cells, CMP-MOF@TK with a positive charge actively accumulated into mitochondria, and ROS-responsive decomposition releases Zn2+ and FFa, reducing the long-term retention toxicity of conventional MOFs. Following, FFa was able to directly suppress the function of mitochondrial complex I, and Zn2+ impeded the mitochondrial complex IV activity, inhibiting electron transport and aerobic respiration, promoting the generation of ROS, inducing mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction, and down-regulating the production of ATP, which synergistically exerts the intrinsic anti-tumor activity. In addition, tumor cell membrane transfection of PD-1 could effectively down-regulate T cell-mediated immune tolerance through immune checkpoint blockade and significantly enhance the anti-tumor effect resulting from mitochondrial-damage mediated ICD. This study characterized the constructed CMP-MOF@TK delivery system, and evaluated its intracellular distribution and TME-responsive dissociation characteristics. The mechanisms and anti-tumor effects were systematically evaluated in both in vitro and in vivo studies, which provides a new perspective for construction of functional preparations that enhance anti-tumor effect based on mitochondrial bioenergetics.

Materials and methods

Materials

The chemical reagents necessary for synthesis were bought from commercial suppliers. The lentiviral vectors came from Haixing Biosciences, and Mem-PER™ Plus Membrane Protein Extraction Kit sourced from Thermo Fisher Scientific. 3,3'-(Propane-2,2-diylbis (sulfanediyl)) dipropionic acid (TK, compound 1) was purchased from Shanghai Apeptide Co.,Ltd. Streptavidin-FITC, Lyso-Tracker Red and Mito-Tracker Red CMXRos purchased from Beyotime. Hochest 33342 was obtained from solarbio. Mitochondrial respiratory complex I and IV activity Assay Kit was purchased from Solarbio. The JC-1 probe from Solarbio, DCFH-DA and Calcein AM/PI Kit from Beyotime, CRT and HMGB1 from Abways were purchased for experiment. The flow antibodies (APC anti-mouse CD279 (PD-1), PE anti-mouse CD274 (B7-H1, PD-L1), PE-CD11c, APC-CD80, FITC-CD86, PE-CD3, FITC-CD4, APC-anti-mouse-CD8a, PerCP/Cyanine5.5-IFN-γ, APC-CD25, PE-Foxp3) from BioLegend.

Cells and animals

The B16-F10 melanoma cell lines were purchased from were sourced from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The C57BL/6J mice, aged between 6 and 8 weeks, were sourced from Ningxia Medical University. All experiments involving animals received approval from the ethics committee at the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University (KYLL-2024-0721).

Preparation method of ROS-responsive MOF (MOF@TK)

Dissolve FFa in 40 mL of ethanol containing 4 mL triethylamine, add compound 1 after it is completely dissolved in 10 mL of ethanol, and stir at 300 rpm at 25°C for 30 min to gain solution A, Zn(CH3COO)2 2H2O was added to 10 mL of pure water. After complete dissolution, solution B was obtained. Slowly add solution B to solution An under magnetic stirring. First, stir at 300 rpm for 30 min at room temperature, then continuously stir at 70°C for 3h, and centrifuge at 10000 rpm for 10 min to collect the product.

Characterization of MOF@TK

The particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) of MOF, MOF@TK were measured through the Malvern particle size analyzer (ZS90, Malvern). TEM mapping images and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to analyze element distribution. To visualize the morphology, transmission electron microscopy (TEM, HITACHIH-7560B, Hitachi) was conducted. The UV-vis spectrophotometer (Shimadzu UV-1800, Japan) was utilized to evaluate the ultraviolet-visible absorption spectra, and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT-IR, Bruker Vertex 70 spectrometer, Germany) was used to examine the infrared spectra.

The ROS-responsive release of Zn2+ in the MOF@TK

The MOF@TK powder was dispersed in phosphate-buffered solution (PBS, pH 5.5) without or with 1.0 mM H2O2, and incubated at 37°C with 200 rpm. The above buffers were centrifuged at the given time (12,000 rpm; 30 min), the Zn2+ concentration in the supernatant from different groups was detected using ICP-MS (710-ES). The change in morphology was observed using TEM (HITACHIH-7560B, Hitachi).

Extraction of tumor cell membranes

The B16-F10-PD-1 cells were established by using lentiviral transfection to introduce the murine PD-1 gene fragment, thereby enhancing PD-1 expression on the B16-F10 cell membrane. A cell density of 1×10^5 per well was established for pre-incubation overnight. At 48 h following transfection, puromycin (3.5 μg/mL) was introduced into the culture medium to discard cells with lower purity, followed by replating to enhance and secure stable cells. The PD-1 expression has been characterized qualitatively using Confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Zeiss LSM900). The PD-1+ cells labeled with APC anti-mouse CD279 (PD-1) were measured using a flow cytometer (FCM, FACS Celesta).

The B16-F10 and B16-F10-PD-1 cells were harvested, resuspended in PBS, and centrifuged at 300g for 5min. Resuspend the cells in cell cleaning solution, centrifuged at 300g for 5min and discard the supernatant. Add permeation buffer to the cell precipitate, briefly vortex to obtain a homogeneous cell suspension, and incubate under continuous mixing conditions at 4°C for 10min. Centrifuged the permeated cells at 16000g for 15min. Added solubilizing buffer to the precipitate and resuspend it by aspirating up and down with a pipette tip. Incubate at 4°C for 30 min under continuous mixing conditions. Centrifuge repeatedly to collect the supernatant from B16-F10 cells (CM) or B16-F10-PD-1 cells (CMP) to a new centrifuge tube. Aliquot and store at −80°C for future use.

The preparation of CM-MOF@TK and CMP-MOF@TK

The tumor cell membranes were introduced into the preparations in a proportion of 1:10, and ultrasound was performed for 2 min with an interval of 2 min while in an ice bath. The cycle was repeated three times. The mixture was then centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 10 min and resuspended to prepare CM-MOF@TK and CMP-MOF@TK.

Characterization of CM-MOF@TK and CMP-MOF@TK

The particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) of MOF, MOF@TK, CM-MOF and CM-MOF@TK were measured through the Malvern particle size analyzer (ZS90, Malvern). To visualize, transmission electron microscopy (TEM, HITACHIH-7560B, Hitachi) was performed to observe the morphology. The encapsulation efficiency of the cancer cell membranes was assessed through SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis experiment.

Cell cytotoxicity

The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was employed to determine the toxicity of the MOF, MOF@TK, CMP-MOF, CM-MOF@TK, CMP-MOF@TK to B16F10 cells.

Evaluation of tumor cell uptake, lysosomal escape and mitochondrial distribution

To evaluate the distribution of CMP-MOF@TK, B16-F10 cells were inoculated in a confocal dish, and then FITC-labeled CMP-MOF@TK was added and incubated for 2, 4 and 6h, respectively, with PBS treated cells as the control. The nuclei and lysosomes were stained with Hoechst (blue) and Lyso-Tracker Red (red) for 2, 4 and 6h, respectively. For mitochondrial distribution, cells were cultured on confocal dish and further incubated with nano-preparations (FITC) for 4, 6h. Afterward, the cells underwent three PBS washes, were stained with MitoTracker (red), fixed in a 4% paraformaldehyde solution, and then stained with Hoechst (blue) for 10 min. Fluorescence images were obtained using CLSM.

Evaluation of mitochondrial respiratory complex inhibitory effect

B16-F10 cells were cultured in 100 mm dishes until they reached about 5 × 106 cells per dish. Next, free FFa, free Zn2+ and their equimolar combinations, and different preparations were added and incubated for 24 h. After incubation, the cells were collected, and complex I activity was evaluated using the mitochondrial respiratory Complex I activity Assay Kit. Then, the cells were collected using the same method to measure complex IV activity with the mitochondrial respiratory Complex IV activity Assay Kit.

Detection of intracellular ATP levels

B16-F10 cells were cultured in 100 mm dishes until they reached about 5 × 106 cells per dish. Then add CMP-MOF@TK and incubate for 24 h. Collect the cells and use an ATP (trace) assay kit to measure ATP levels within the cells.

Determination of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in the mitochondria of tumor cells

B16-F10 cells were seeded in 6-well plates and cultured for 12 h. Then, CMP-MOF@TK was added and incubated for 24 h. DCFH-DA staining was performed and inverted fluorescence microscopy was used to observe how the preparation promoted ROS production.

Mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) analysis

B16-F10 cells, at a concentration of 5×104 per well, were inoculated in confocal dishes. Following a 24 h administration period, the cells were exposed to JC-1 probes. Take images with CLSM.

Cellular oxygen consumption rate (OCR) assay

The black transparent cell culture plate was set with blank wells, control wells and sample wells, then the cell concentration of approximately 5×10^5 cells/mL was inoculated into the control wells and sample wells. After treating with different preparations for 24h culture, 100 μL of the working solution was added to each well and incubated at 37°C for 30 min. The 80 μL Reagent II was immediately added to each well, which was measured by using the kinetic mode of FlexStation 3 Multi-Mode Microplate Reader.

Evaluation of tumor cell apoptosis induced by CMP-MOF@TK

The cells were introduced into 6-well plates and treated with CMP-MOF@TK for 24 h. Following this, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were rinsed with PBS. Add an appropriate volume of Calcein AM/PI detection working solution and incubate at 37°C in the dark for 30 min. After the incubation was completed, the staining effect was observed under a fluorescence microscope. B16-F10 cells, at a concentration of 1 × 106, were placed in 6-well plates and left to culture overnight. The cells were digested with trypsin without EDTA, centrifuged and washed once with PBS, and then suspended in a binding buffer containing 3 μL of Annexin V-FITC and PI. The cells were incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min and analyzed using a flow cytometer.

Evaluation of ICD effect of CMP-MOF@TK

In 12-well plates, B16-F10 cells were cultured and treated with MOF@TK and CMP-MOF@TK for 24 h. Then the cells were stained with anti-HMGB1 and anti-CRT antibodies, respectively, and analyzed by immunofluorescence. Following the above method, the influence of CMP-MOF@TK on ICD markers, including HMGB1 and CRT, was investigated over time and dose response. The B16-F10 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and treated with different preparations. The culture supernatant was then collected, and extracellular ATP levels were determined using an ATP (trace) assay kit.

In vitro DCs maturation and PD-1/PD-L1 blockade

For the maturation of dendritic cells (DCs) in vitro, bone marrow-derived dendritic cells (BMDCs) were extracted from mice and placed in 12-well plates. The B16-F10 cells were first incubated with the different preparations for 12 h and co-cultured with BMDCs for 24 h. The cells were harvested, and matured DCs were labeled with flow cytometric antibodies, including PE-CD11c, APC-CD80, and FITC-CD86, and analyzed by FCM. For PD-1/PD-L1 blockade analysis, the B16-F10 cells were seeded into 12-well plates and treated with MOF@TK and CMP-MOF@TK. Then the cells were collected by trypsin and labeled with PE anti-mouse CD274 (B7-H1, PD-L1) antibody, which was analyzed through FCM.

In vivo targeted detection

When the tumor size reached approximately 200 mm3, after fasting overnight, free Cy5 or Cy5-CMP-MOF@TK was injected via the tail vein at pre-designed time intervals. Mice were anesthetized at the predetermined time point and underwent whole-body fluorescence imaging using an in vivo imaging system. The Zn levels in the tumor and main organs were assessed using ICP-MS.

Evaluation of anti-tumor effect and safety in vivo

Establish a mouse model of melanoma, tail vein injection (1) PBS group (2) MOF group (3) MOF@TK group (4) CMP-MOF group (5) CM-MOF@TK group (6) CMP-MOF@TK group (n = 5, once every other day), a total of 5 doses were administered. The body weight, tumor size and survival rate of the mice were measured every two days. Safety evaluation of nano-preparations: The hearts, livers, spleens, lungs and kidneys of mice were collected respectively for H&E staining, and the Zn contents in the major organs were measured using ICP-MS. Hemolysis test Hematological indicators (whole blood, blood collection tubes containing EDTA) and biochemical (serum) data: Liver function: Alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase; Renal function: creatinine, urea. After the experiment, the mice were sacrificed to obtain tumor tissues. The apoptosis of tumor cells mediated by the preparation was analyzed through H&E staining (fixation), TUNEL and Ki67 staining.

Induce mitochondrial dysfunction in vivo

The levels of ROS in tumor tissues of each group were detected by immunohistochemical method, employing 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein (DCFH) as a probe to measure total ROS. The alleviating effect of Zn2+ on tumor hypoxia: Tumors were collected for HIF-1α immunofluorescence staining. Immunogenic cell death investigation: The contents of the classic ICD markers HMGB-1 (release) and CRT (surface exposure) in tumor tissues were investigated by immunofluorescence staining.

Transcriptomic analysis

After the treatment process was completed, tumor tissues from different groups are collected for RNA sequencing analysis. Bioinformatics analysis of the RNA-seq data was performed using R software. The DESeq2 package was used to obtain differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with the following criteria: padj ≤ 0.05 |log2(FoldChange)|≥1.0. We conducted a hierarchical clustering analysis on the FPKM values of the genes using the mainstream method, and performed normalization on the rows (Z-score) to draw heatmap. GO (Gene Ontology) functional enrichment and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment was performed using clusterProfiler software.

Detection of in vivo immune activation effect

The expression levels of DCs cells (CD80+CD86+), CD4+T cells (CD3+CD4a+) and CD8+T cells (CD3+CD8a+) in the tumor were detected by flow cytometry (supplemented by the detection of the expression levels of immunosuppressive cells). After the anti-tumor experiment was completed, tumor tissues from different groups were collected and sectioned. They were incubated with digestive buffer (RPMI1640 containing 2 mg/mL collagenase IV) at 37°C for 3 h. Then the obtained cell suspension was filtered through a 70 μm cell filter screen and washed with RPMI 1640. Finally, the cells were incubated with flow antibodies (PE-CD11c, APC-CD80, FITC-CD86, PE-CD3, FITC-CD4, APC-CD8a, PerCP/Cyanine5.5- IFN-γ, APC-CD25, PE-Foxp3), and then detected by flow cytometry.

Statistical analysis

The study presented experimental data as mean ± standard deviation. Two-group comparisons were conducted using a T-test, whereas One-way ANOVA with Tukey's test was applied for multiple group comparisons (GraphPad Prism 9.5). The significant differences between the groups were presented as follows: ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of CMP-MOF@TK nanoplatform

The MOF and MOF@TK containing ROS-responsive linker were constructed through a one-pot assembly method (Fig. 1A). The hydrodynamic size of the MOF was 268.00 ± 25.83 nm, which was 427.67 ± 12.13 nm for MOF@TK as ascertained by dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis (Fig. 1B–Table S1). The Zeta potential of MOF and MOF@TK shown in Fig. 1C were 30.65 ± 0.72 mV and 38.75 ± 0.29 mV, respectively. In addition, tumor cell mitochondria have a higher transmembrane potential, which is 3 to 5 times higher than the plasma membrane potential, which favors the accumulation of positively charged molecules within the mitochondria [29]. Further, TEM images showed that the resulting MOF and MOF@TK had a typical structure, and MOF@TK had a more regular morphology similar to a cuboid compared with MOF (Fig. 1D) [30]. TEM mapping and SEM images, and XPS data confirmed the presence of the characteristic S element, demonstrating the successful preparation of MOF@TK by showing the elemental composition and distribution (Fig. 1E–F, Fig. S1–S2). [31]. The UV-visible spectrum was carried to present the successful preparation of MOF and MOF@TK (Fig. S3). The FT-IR spectrum showed absorptions at 1420 and 1305 cm−1, attributed to the out-of-plane bending vibration of C−H, and a peak at 1725 cm−1 was associated with the C=O stretching vibration in both MOF and MOF@TK (Fig. S4) [22]. The Zn release measured by ICP-MS and TEM images showed that the resulting MOF@TK could be decomposed responsively under pH 5.5 with 1.0 mM H2O2, which demonstrates that MOF@TK could be decomposed under the ROS environment inside the mitochondria through breaking its thioketal bonds (Fig. S5) [32].

To enhance its ability to target tumors and prolong its circulation time in vivo, tumor cell membranes were extracted to encapsulate MOF@TK [33]. The TEM demonstrated that the cancer membrane successfully encapsulated MOF and MOF@TK by ultrasound to prepare CM-MOF and CM-MOF@TK (Fig. 1H). The results of SDS-PAGE images (Fig. S6A) confirmed that the protein bands of the MOF and MOF@TK encapsulated by the cancer membrane were basically the same as those of the purified tumor cell membrane (CM), indicating that the synthesized biomimetic nanoplatforms preserved almost all the proteins on the tumor cell membrane [34]. The hydrodynamic sizes of the CM-MOF and CM-MOF@TK were 426.97 ± 0.87 nm and 359.90 ± 18.84 nm, respectively, and the Zeta potential underwent a transition from positive to negative, aiding in extending the duration of blood circulation (Fig. 1H–I, Table S2) [22].

The B16-F10 cells transfected with PD-1 were prepared according to the method described in previous studies to construct CMP-MOF@TK nanoplatform through ultrasound [35]. The PD-1 expression level was characterized qualitatively and quantitatively through CLSM and FCM. The CLSM results showed that the zsGreen-tagged PD-1 protein (green) colocalized with the DiR fluorescence-labeled cell membrane (green) (Fig. 1J). And quantitative analysis by FCM presented that approximately 97.37% of PD-1+ cells, indicating successful overexpression of PD-1 protein on cell membranes (Fig. 1K, Fig. S6B). The hydrodynamic sizes of the CMP-MOF@TK nanoplatform were 330.17 ± 15.99 nm with uniform distribution, and the Zeta potential underwent a transition from positive to negative (Fig. 1L–M, Table S2). The colloidal stability and serum stability of CMP-MOF@TK were investigated by measuring particle size every day, the results demonstrated that CMP-MOF@TK had excellent stability for 7 days (Fig. S7) [36,37].

Evaluation of cell uptake, lysosomal escape and mitochondriotropic movement of CMP-MOF@TK

Firstly, the cell cytotoxicity of CMP-MOF@TK was evaluated through the CCK-8 method. The results (Fig. S8) showed that CMP-MOF@TK had no apparent cytotoxicity on the B16F10 cells with 10 μg/mL, and with concentration increasing to 15 μg/mL, CM-MOF@TK and CMP-MOF@TK all presented cytotoxicity compared to MOF and MOF@TK. The process of efficient internalization and accumulation to specific organelles are essential for therapeutics accumulation, which is necessary for effective tumour inhibition [38]. The localization of nanomedicine in cellular mitochondria plays a crucial role in triggering selective cell death caused by reactive oxygen species in cancer cells [39]. The CLSM was used to evaluate the cell uptake, lysosomal escape, and mitochondriotropic movement of CMP-MOF@TK (Fig. 2A). The analytic results revealed that green fluorescence intensity from CMP-MOF@TK increased over time, which was higher than the green fluorescence intensity from MOF@TK (Fig. 2B), suggesting that the enhanced cell uptake of CMP-MOF@TK was time-dependent due to homing targeting ability of membrane protein [22,30]. In addition, co-localization of lysosomes with CMP-MOF@TK was performed to the ability to escape from lysosomes. Unsurprisingly, overlapping red (lysosomes) and green (CMP-MOF@TK) fluorescence signals were observed after 2h and gradually diminished over time [22,40], which demonstrated that CMP-MOF@TK could effectively escape from lysosomes mediated by the proton sponge effect (Fig. 2C, Fig. S9A). To further investigate the intracellular mitochondriotropic movement of MOF@TK with positive charge, the co-localization of CMP-MOF@TK with mitochondria in B16F10 tumor cells was observed doing with the MitoTracker Red probe [38,41]. When B16F10 cells were treated with CMP-MOF@TK, they exhibited increased yellow fluorescence due to the merging of red (mitochondria) and green (CMP-MOF@TK) signals. In contrast, B16F10 cells treated with MOF@TK alone, which lacked the cancer membrane coating, showed significantly less yellow fluorescence compared to those treated with CMP-MOF@TK (Fig. 2D, Fig. S9B). The results demonstrated that CMP-MOF@TK could effectively be taken up and movement toward mitochondria driven by the higher transmembrane electric potential, laying the foundation for the subsequent anti-tumor effects.

Evaluation of CMP-MOF@TK-triggered mitochondria dysfunction

Mitochondria are responsible not only for energy production but also for maintaining redox metastasis [38]. In mitochondria, electrons are passed to transmembrane protein complexes known as the ETC, which generates a proton gradient that powers ATP production [42]. Fig. 3A schematically illustrates the mitochondrial respiratory complex inhibitory effect and dysfunction induced through CMP-MOF@TK. First, the experimental result found that MOF@TK coated with cancer membrane could effectively inhibit respiratory complex I and IV at the same concentration compared to other groups, which reflected the specific mitochondrial injury (Fig. 3B–C, Fig. S10) [19,43]. The intracellular ROS levels elevated by CM-MOF@TK were measured using the 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) probe. The results presented that CM-MOF@TK and CMP-MOF@TK could effectively promote the production of ROS compared to the other groups, increasing oxidative stress in the mitochondria (Fig. 3D).

The ETC generates a proton gradient to maintain ΔΨ and drive OXPHOS [44], and since mitochondria are very sensitive to ROS, the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP) was assessed using the JC-1 fluorescent dye [22,41]. The B16F10 cells treated with CM-MOF@TK and CMP-MOF@TK presented evident green fluorescence compared to the control and other groups, suggesting that the mitochondrial membrane potential was largely depolarized due to inhibition of ETC complexes I and IV (Fig. 3E) [38]. The reason is that JC-1 forms aggregates that emit red fluorescence in healthy mitochondria, but when the Δψm decreases, JC-1 disperses and emits green fluorescence [41]. In addition, the mitochondria ultimately utilized fuel from ETC in the form of ATP, so the ATP level in the cells was measured to demonstrate the ETC dysfunction. The result showed that CM-MOF@TK and CMP-MOF@TK notably inhibit the ATP production compared to the other groups(Fig. 3F). In addition, the mitochondrial energy metabolism was further evaluated by measuring oxygen consumption rate (OCR) using fluorescence staining [45]. The results showed that CMP-MOF@TK treatment effectively inhibited oxygen consumption compared to the control groups (Fig. S11).

Changes in mitochondrial morphology and notable swelling are key indicators of advancing mitochondrial dysfunction [46]. Consequently, regulating the overall morphology and function of mitochondria may represent a potential strategy for inhibiting tumors [3]. The Biological Transmission Electron Microscopy (Bio-TEM) was then utilized to visualize the morphological changes of mitochondria [3]. Compared to the control and MOF@TK groups, treatment of B16F10 cells with CMP-MOF@TK caused noticeable damage to mitochondrial structures (Fig. 3G) [31,41]. Overall, the above experimental results indicated that the ETC function in the mitochondria was significantly disturbed by CM-MOF@TK and CMP-MOF@TK, which were much more potent than MOF and MOF@TK due to the effectively cellular internalization [3].

In vitro anticancer performance of CMP-MOF@TK

To visually assess the therapeutic effect, live cells were marked with calcein (green fluorescence) and dead cells were stained with propidium iodide (red fluorescence). After Calcein-AM/PI double staining, living cells showed green fluorescence (Calcein-AM metabolites), while dead cell nuclei were marked red by PI. In the CMP-MOF@TK and CMP-MOF@TK treatment groups, the proportion of red fluorescent cells increased significantly compared to the other groups (Fig. 4A). Then, the therapeutic effects were further tested using the Annexin V-FITC/PI with flow cytometry. The results demonstrated that the CMP-MOF@TK and CMP-MOF@TK significantly promoted apoptosis ratios of B16F10 cells (Fig. 4B, Fig. S12) [30]. To further confirm the ICD effects resulted from increased mitochondrial oxidative stress caused by CMP-MOF@TK, we examined the characteristic markers of ICD, including the release of HMGB-1, the expression of CRT and extracellular ATP, in B16F10 tumor cells after CMP-MOF@TK treatment. As shown in Fig. 4C–D, relative to the control and MOF@TK groups, the CMP-MOF@TK group significantly induced the release of HMGB-1 from the nucleus into the extracellular milieu, accompanied by an elevated level of cell surface CRT exposure. In addition, the influence of CMP-MOF@TK on ICD markers, including HMGB1 and CRT, with time-course and dose-response were measured, which demonstrates that release of HMGB-1 and the expression of CRT present dose and time dependent, supporting causality between mitochondrial dysfunction and ICD markers (HMGB1, CRT) (Fig. S13). Furthermore, CM-MOF@TK and CMP-MOF@TK groups could effectively promote ATP secretion in the cytoplasm, supporting induced ICD effects (Fig. S14) [47]. Fig. 4E demonstrated that the BMDC maturation rates in the CM-MOF@TK and CMP-MOF@TK groups were greater than those in the other groups, suggesting that CMP-MOF@TK could significantly promote DC maturation based on the ICD effect (Fig. 4F, Fig. S15). Further, the FCM results, which quantitatively analyzed PD-L1 expression in tumor cells, showed that CMP-MOF@TK specifically bound to tumor cells compared to the CM-MOF@TK groups (Fig. S16). The effective inhibition of the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway could enhance the efficacy of T cell-mediated immunotherapy, as further verified in in vivo studies [35,48]. Indeed, these results confirm the therapeutic efficacy of the engineered biohybrid CMP-MOF@TK, emphasizing its ability to induce the ICD and blockade PD-1/PD-L1 combination, and activing immune effects effectively.

In vivo biodistribution and therapeutic performance of CMP-MOF@TK

B16F10 tumor-bearing mice were randomly and blindly separated into two groups (n = 3), and tail intravenous injection with Cy5 and Cy5 labeled CMP-MOF@TK. B16 tumor-bearing mice were imaged at various time intervals (0, 2, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h) using an in vivo imaging system (IVIS) [3]. The fluorescence signal from mice injected with Cy5-labeled CMP-MOF@TK through the tail vein was tracked with the IVIS instrument, demonstrating rapid accumulation and prolonged retention of CMP-MOF@TK at the tumor location [32]. Moreover, at 24h and 48h postinjection, the CMP-MOF@TK groups still showed a stronger fluorescence signal in the tumor site (Fig. 5A, Fig. S17). Furthermore, ICP-MS was used to detect Zn levels in the main organs and tumor tissues [22,43,49]. The results presented that the CMP-MOF@TK could effectively deliver Zn to the tumor compared to the control groups, and there was no significant difference in Zn contents in the major organs between the control groups and CMP-MOF@TK groups (Fig. S18), indicating significant accumulation in the tumor tissues.

To further assess the therapeutic efficacy of CMP-MOF@TK in vivo, the subcutaneous tumor models were successfully established with B16F10 cells. Mice were inoculated with B16F10 cells for 8 days and randomly divided into four groups when tumor volumes reached approximately 100-200 mm3. Mice in each group were injected with different reagents via the tail vein every 2 days for 10 days: 1) PBS group, 2) MOF group, 3) MOF@TK group, 4) CMP-MOF group, 5) CM-MOF@TK group, 6) CMP-MOF@TK groups (Fig. 5B). The continuous therapeutic effectiveness in terms of tumor size and body weight was recorded every 2 days [29]. As shown, the CMP-MOF group, CM-MOF@TK group and CMP-MOF@TK all exhibited significant tumor growth inhibition compared to the control, MOF and MOF@TK groups, and the CMP-MOF@TK group showed the more optimal tumor growth inhibition effect (Fig. 5C–E, Fig. S19–S20).

At the experimental endpoint of anti-tumor treatment, organs and serum of mice were collected for in vivo safety evaluation. H&E staining was conducted on heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys, revealing negligible damage in all organs [3]. No significant changes were observed in body weights, and serum biochemistry, and histological examination of major organs in mice indicated good health (Fig. 5D, Figs. S21–S22), demonstrating the excellent biosafety of CMP-MOF@TK [19]. Further, the Zn contents in major organs were measured through ICP-MS, the results showed that the constructed nanoplatform does not cause off-target toxicity (Fig. S23).

Meanwhile, histological evaluation of treated tumor tissue sections was performed by H&E, TUNEL and Ki-67 staining, the CM-MOF@TK combined with the PD-1 groups showed the strongest histological damage and apoptotic signal, along with a marked reduction in cell proliferation and an increase in apoptotic cells (Fig. 5F–H). In summary, these results indicated that CMP-MOF@TK could meaningfully distribute into mouse tumor regions, inhibit tumor growth, and present stronger apoptotic signal, but the holistic anti-tumor response was limited due to metabolic plasticity and the immunosuppressive nature of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [43,50].

In vivo oxidative stress and ICD effect of CMP-MOF@TK

Detection of ROS levels in tumor tissues was performed using DCFH-DA as a probe, which was consistent with H&E and TUNEL staining results (Fig. 6A). Notably, the CM-MOF@TK and CMP-MOF@TK treatment groups had the highest ROS levels in tumor tissues, which were significantly stronger than the other treatment groups, favoring the effect of ICD treatment [47]. HIF-1α (hypoxia-inducible factor-1α) is a key transcription factor stably expressed by cells under hypoxic conditions, and its immunofluorescence staining results can reflect the stress state of cells under hypoxia or specific drug treatments. Compared to the other groups, the CMP-MOF@TK group showed a significant decrease in the HIF-1α (Fig. 6B), demonstrating that CMP-MOF@TK group could effectively alleviate hypoxia in tumor cells. The CMP-MOF@TK effectively inhibited the ETC complex IV activity, which exert an crucial role in mitochondrial aerobic respiration. Moreover, the CMP-MOF@TK could effectively inhibited the ETC complex I activity synchronously, synergistically inducing mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction, which could economize endogenous O2 and alleviate tumor hypoxia [22,30]. Immunofluorescence staining was used to determine the content of classical ICD markers HMGB-1 and CRT in tumor tissues. The findings indicated that the CM-MOF@TK and CMP-MOF@TK groups exhibited an amplified ICD effect, with a significant increase in the release of HMGB-1 and an increase in the expression of CRT compared with the other groups (Fig. 6C–D) [47]. Relevant research has demonstrated that the ICD of tumor cells can promote dendritic cell (DC) maturation and T cell infiltration, thereby reversing the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (ITME) [51].

Bulk RNA sequencing was performed on treated tumor tissues to further dissect the molecular mechanism of action of CMP-MOF@TK anti-tumor effect, aiming to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and signaling pathways potentially involved in the treatment process [49,51]. In comparison to cells treated with PBS, 549 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified following treatment, with 144 genes showing increased expression and 405 genes showing decreased expression in tumors treated with CMP-MOF@TK (Fig. 6E). A heat map was used to show the transcription levels of target genes in the tumor after treatment with either PBS or CMP-MOF@TK (Fig. 6F). Differential genes were categorized by integrating the Rich Factor, p-value, GO functional enrichment analysis and KEGG were illustrated in Fig. G-H. The results found that the treatment with CMP-MOF@TK affected tumor tissue function, including cytokine receptor binding associated with downstream immune-related signaling pathways, membrane protein complex, cell death, apoptosis process, response to oxidative stress, immune response and immune system process (Fig. 6G, Fig. S24). Furthermore, the KEGG analysis was performed to understand potential pathways involved in anti-tumor effect of CMP-MOF@TK. The results demonstrate that metabolism and immune-related pathways were enriched in the CMP-MOF@TK groups compared with the control groups (Fig. 6H). In general, blocking mitochondrial respiratory caused by inhibition of ETC complex function promote ROS production accompanied increased oxidative stress, inducing ICD effect and activating immune response, therefor amplifying anti-tumor effect.

Detection of in vivo immune activation effect with CMP-MOF@TK

Dendritic cells (DCs) play a crucial role in immune stimulation as powerful antigen-presenting cells (APCs) that efficiently take up and present antigens, activate T cells, and initiate and maintain immune responses [52]. Fig. 7A presents the schematic diagram of the activation of anti-tumor immune response based on the mitochondria-mediated ICD of tumor cells and immune checkpoint blockade with tumor cell membrane transfection of PD-1. The flow cytometric analysis showed that CM-MOF@TK and CMP-MOF@TK treatments significantly induced DC maturation with an increased proportion of co-stimulatory molecules (CD80+ and CD86+) compared to PBS, MOF, MOF@TK, and CMP-MOF groups (Fig. 7B, Fig. S25A) [19,52]. DC activation was closely related to T cell priming and recruitment, and the immune response of T cells along with their infiltration into the tumor site is crucial for boosting the anti-tumor immune response. Therefore, the responses of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells within the tumors were assessed. [19,52]. Both flow cytometry and immunohistochemistry analyses demonstrated that CMP-MOF@TK treatment led to a significant influx of CD4+ and CD8+ T cells into tumors, leading to markedly increased levels of IFN-γ (Fig. 7C–G, Fig. S25B–D). The CM-MOF@TK successfully triggered CD8+ T cell-dependent immunity, which were further strengthened by PD-L1 blockade leading to the suppression of the primary tumor [19]. In addition, compared to the control group, the CM-MOF@TK and CMP-MOF@TK groups could reduce the proportion of immunosuppressive Treg cells indicated with Foxp3 (Fig. S26). The above results suggest that CM-MOF@TK effectively induced tumor immunogenic death and significantly enhanced the proliferation and differentiation of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), which was important for tumor growth inhibition [52].

Conclusion

In conclusion, a multifunctional nanoplatform MOF@TK with intrinsic activity was constructed by Zn2+ and FFa, which was modified through tumor cell membranes transfected with PD-1 (CMP-MOF@TK) to induce mitochondrial dysfunction with ETC inactivation and amplified anti-tumor response. The results presented that CMP-MOF@TK was effectively taken up following mitochondriotropic movement, and ROS-responsive decomposition releases Zn2+ and FFa. Significantly, ETC complex I and IV activities were synchronously inhibited through FFa and Zn2+, blocking electron transport and promoting ROS generation. The elevated oxidative stress led to mitochondrial depolarization, which systematically induces mitochondrial metabolic dysfunction, down-regulating the production of ATP. In addition, the immunostimulatory activity was effectively activated through the mitochondria-mediated ICD of tumor cells that promoted the increased HMGB1 and CRT in the cytomatrix, and tumor cell membrane transfection of PD-1 down-regulated T cell-mediated immune tolerance. In conclusion, this work provides translational promise for realizing precise and efficient anti-tumor bioenergetic therapy based on mitochondrial ETC dysfunction-mediated metabolic disorder. In subsequent studies, the sample size of in vivo experiments will be expanded to explore the anti-tumor effect of the CMP-MOF@TK delivery system, which needs to be combined with other therapeutic methods to advance precision anti-tumor therapy.
