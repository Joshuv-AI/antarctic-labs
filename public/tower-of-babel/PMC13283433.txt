Clostridial necrotizing infection with multiorgan failure in an unhoused patient following a presumed spider bite
Authors: Linh N-D Vo, Lee D Lam, Shotaro Naganawa, Ahmadreza Ghasemiesfe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpider bites are commonly encountered in medicine and, when recognized early, can often be effectively managed with supportive care or antivenom. However, in rare cases, particularly in individuals with poor living conditions, chronic comorbidities, or immunocompromised states, arachnid envenomation can act as a portal for rare but life-threatening complications. We introduce the case of a 59-year-old male who presented with a presumed spider bite and later developed necrotizing soft tissue infection complicated by compartment syndrome, acute mesenteric ischemia, septic shock, and multiorgan failure, including pulmonary edema, acute kidney injury, and hepatic dysfunction. This case highlights the importance of early recognition and multidisciplinary intervention in medically and socially complex patients, and underscores how structural vulnerability can both increase exposure risk and amplify clinical severity.

Introduction

Spider bites are a common cause of minor skin and soft tissue complaints, often resolving with conservative management or short courses of antibiotics. In the United States, envenomation by species such as the brown recluse (Loxosceles reclusa) or black widow (Latrodectus mactans) can occasionally cause more severe symptoms, ranging from localized necrosis to systemic effects like hemolysis or neurotoxicity [1]. However, life-threatening complications such as necrotizing infections, mesenteric ischemia, and multiorgan failure are exceedingly rare, but can be seen in patients with medical risk factors and unfavorable social determinants of health.

We present a complex case where the patient developed clostridial necrotizing soft tissue infection, mesenteric ischemia, and multisystem organ failure following a presumed spider bite, illustrating the devastating consequences of an otherwise minor exposure when compounded by social vulnerability.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old unhoused White male presented to the emergency department in May with a three-day history of rapidly progressive pain and swelling in the left upper extremity following a presumed spider bite sustained while sleeping outdoors in a densely vegetated encampment. He reported noticing two puncture wounds at the site, followed by intense 9/10 pain and swelling. He denied systemic symptoms such as fever, chills, nausea, vomiting, or local paresthesia.

His past medical history is significant for type II diabetes mellitus, autoimmune skin conditions (lichen planus and porphyria cutanea tarda), suspected mixed cryoglobulinemia, and hepatitis C virus (genotype 2) with progression to cirrhosis, successfully treated with sofosbuvir/velpatasvir and confirmed eradicated at the time of this visit as evidenced by undetectable HCV RNA on two separate occasions. He also has a long-standing history of injection and inhaled methamphetamine use, tobacco use (22 pack-years), and daily alcohol use. Notably, he had recently been hospitalized for incision and drainage of a right upper extremity abscess due to an insect bite.

Physical examination showed a 1 cm ulcerative lesion to the volar wrist, erythematous left forearm with circumferential edema, cool hands, and a lesion with purulent exudate (Fig. 1). Radial pulse was present with normal capillary refill; concern for early compartment syndrome and necrotizing fasciitis prompted immediate imaging and surgical consultation. CT imaging revealed diffuse circumferential subcutaneous edema of the left upper extremity with subcutaneous stranding consistent with cellulitis, without organized abscess formation. A small focus of gas was identified adjacent to the draining wound, without evidence of gas tracking along tissue planes (Fig. 2).

On Day 1 of his hospital course, the patient was started on piperacillin-tazobactam and linezolid and underwent emergent surgical decompression, including a fasciotomy, debridement and irrigation, and carpal tunnel release of the left hand. Intraoperative findings noted extensive woody, scarred subcutaneous tissue with weeping edema and no overt muscle necrosis. The forearm fascia was left open and managed with wound Vacuum-Assisted Closure therapy. Cultures of the tissue taken during the surgery grew Clostridium perfringens. Blood cultures drawn on admission showed no growth, confirming the absence of bacteremia.

Despite early intervention, the patient’s clinical status deteriorated rapidly. On Day 3, he developed severe abdominal pain with metabolic derangements including a high anion gap metabolic acidosis (pH 7.18, bicarbonate 11 mmol/l), elevated lactate > 13 mmol/l, and hypoglycemia—raising concern for acute hypoxic hepatitis (ischemic hepatitis). Labs revealed disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), with markedly elevated INR (2.6), aPTT (60.8 sec), and thrombin time (30.7 sec), along with low fibrinogen (172 mg/dl). Concurrently, elevated creatine kinase (>4299 U/l) and AST/ALT suggested rhabdomyolysis and hepatocellular injury. CT chest demonstrated patchy bilateral ground-glass opacities and imaging findings consistent with early non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema (Fig. 3), likely representing an acute lung injury attributable to systemic inflammation and tissue necrosis from clostridial sepsis. Echocardiography showed normal cardiac function with a preserved LVEF (55%) and no pericardial effusion. Further CT imaging revealed signs of mesenteric ischemia, so the patient was taken emergently for exploratory laparotomy.

CT of the chest demonstrated patchy bilateral ground-glass opacities (circles) and interlobular septal thickening, consistent with early non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema.

Surgical pathology revealed widespread small and large bowel ischemia with transmural hemorrhage and congestion, necessitating multiple sequential surgeries over the following weeks. These included small bowel and right colon resection (Day 4), completion colectomy and enterorrhaphy (Day 6), cholecystectomy and end ileostomy creation (Day 10), and repeat small bowel resection (Day 20). Peritoneal cultures obtained on Day 20 confirmed opportunistic fungal peritonitis with Candida parapsilosis and Candida glabrata, which he was treated with micafungin and fluconazole for three weeks.

The patient's hospital course extended over four months, from May to September. His renal function ultimately recovered, with creatinine stabilizing at 1.10 mg/dl, and he was successfully transitioned off renal replacement therapy. However, he developed short gut syndrome because of extensive bowel resection, with only 90 cm of remaining small intestine, necessitating total parenteral nutrition (TPN) and high ileostomy output management. Additional complications included hepatic and splenic infarcts, ascites requiring therapeutic paracentesis, bilateral pleural effusions requiring thoracentesis, and dry gangrene of the toes. He ultimately opted for a self-directed discharge. His post-discharge course was complicated by recurrent hospitalizations for severe hyponatremia and ongoing difficulties maintaining TPN adherence, ultimately reflecting the significant barriers to sustained recovery imposed by his social circumstances.

Discussion

This case illustrates a rare but devastating progression from simple presumed spider envenomation to clostridial necrotizing soft tissue infection, mesenteric ischemia, and multisystem organ failure. The patient’s predisposing factors, compounded by fragmented access to medical care, likely delayed early intervention and heightened susceptibility to various systemic sequelae.

The clinical syndrome observed in this patient is best understood as a cascade of distinct but interrelated processes. First, the presumed spider bite likely disrupted the skin barrier, creating a portal of entry in a host with severely impaired wound healing and immune function. Second, C. perfringens infection, whether inoculated at the bite site or introduced through concurrent skin breach, drove the primary infectious process, producing exotoxins responsible for local tissue destruction, systemic inflammatory response, and DIC. Notably, the absence of bacteremia on admission blood cultures suggests the systemic organ injury was driven primarily by clostridial exotoxin absorption rather than hematogenous dissemination, a mechanistically distinct pathway. Third, the resulting septic shock precipitated the downstream complications of mesenteric ischemia, acute lung injury, and multiorgan failure through well-established mechanisms of hypoperfusion and systemic inflammation.

Distinguishing between these layers is critical. Envenomation potentially served as the pivotal initiating event in this cascade, but without barrier disruption, clostridial inoculation and the ensuing systemic complications may not have occurred. In this context, the spider bite, while not directly responsible for systemic toxicity, may have been the singular event that rendered this patient's profound underlying vulnerability catastrophically expressed.

While most spider bites are self-limiting or require short-term intervention, severe outcomes in vulnerable populations have been described, typically presenting as localized dermo-necrosis or mild systemic symptoms. For example, one case detailed a malnourished woman with poly-substance use disorder who developed necrotic lesions after a brown recluse bite but recovered quickly with wound care and antibiotics [2]. Another described a soldier who developed transient myocarditis following black widow envenomation and fully recovered within five days [3]. Even among at-risk populations, progression to irreversible multiorgan dysfunction is extremely rare, unlike the prolonged hospitalization required in our patient’s case.

Systemic illness and multiorgan failure following spider bites are extremely uncommon. Prior reports of similar severity, typically involving arthropods like scorpions and ticks, have been implicated in cases of acute respiratory distress syndrome [4, 5]. Two other comparable cases involved patients who developed mesenteric ischemia following massive wasp and lancehead snakebite envenomation [6, 7]. To our knowledge, based on a review of available published literature, spider envenomation complicated by imaging-confirmed acute lung injury has not been previously described, though we acknowledge that the limitations of any literature search preclude definitive claims of novelty. Similarly, while mesenteric ischemia has been reported following envenomation by other arthropods and reptiles, we were unable to identify a prior published case attributing this complication specifically to spider envenomation.

The patient was unable to identify the responsible species, which is a common limitation in arachnid envenomation cases. Given the geographic location of Northern California, the western black widow (Latrodectus hesperus) is the most clinically plausible candidate, as it is endemic to this region. While L. reclusa (brown recluse) is frequently cited in envenomation literature as causing more severe systemic manifestations, its established range does not extend to Northern California, and its involvement in this case is considered unlikely [8].

This case illustrates how structural vulnerability operates at every stage of illness: housing instability placed this patient in environments of increased arthropod exposure, while substance use disorder, cirrhosis, and fragmented healthcare access likely delayed presentation and impaired recovery. His recurrent arthropod-related infections underscore another critical point: without addressing the underlying social determinants, patients return to the same conditions that precipitated their illness in the first place. For this reason, this case serves as a critical reminder that early recognition, multidisciplinary management, and preventative education about spider bites may be essential, particularly in medically and socially complex populations.
