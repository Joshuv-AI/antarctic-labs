Prevalence and factors associated with loneliness among older adults in Africa: systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Chilot Kassa Mekonnen, Moges Tadesse Abebe, Hailemichael Kindie Abate, Worku Chekol Tassew, Tesfahun Zemene Tafere
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLoneliness is increasingly becoming a serious public health problem, particularly among older adults. Older adults are highly influenced by underlying factors such as unemployment/retirement, age-related diseases, and loss of relatives/friends; as a result, they are prone to loneliness. Despite several studies being conducted in developed countries and a few in African countries, there has been no documented systematic review evidence of this problem in the African region.ObjectiveTo determine the pooled prevalence and associated factors of loneliness among older adults in Africa.MethodOriginal studies on the prevalence of loneliness among older adults in Africa were retrieved from well-known international databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library, as well as search engines like Google and Google Scholar. A random-effects model was applied to estimate the combined prevalence of loneliness among older adults in Africa. Statistical analysis was performed using STATA version 17 software.ResultsThis systematic review and meta-analysis included 18 cross-sectional observational studies that provided data on the prevalence of loneliness among older adults. The overall pooled prevalence of loneliness in this population was 37.86 [95% CI (30.11, 45.62), I2 = 100%, p < 0.01]. The odds of experiencing loneliness among married older adults were 59% less likely to feel lonely [AOR = 0.41, 95% CI (0.24, 0.68)] compared to unmarried, alcohol intake, which was associated with higher odds of loneliness [AOR = 1.87, 95% CI (1.09, 4.11)] compared to did not take alcohol, and the presence of depressive symptoms had a substantially increased odds of loneliness [AOR = 3.13, 95% CI (1.03, 10.0)] compared to without depressive symptoms.ConclusionIn this systematic review well over one-third of older adults suffered from loneliness which highlights that loneliness is a significant concern among older adults in Africa. Marital status, alcohol-drinking habits, and depressive symptoms were found significant predictors of loneliness among older adults in Africa.

Introduction

Loneliness is increasingly becoming a serious public health problem, particularly among older adults [1]. The geriatric population in Africa is rapidly growing on account of lifestyle changes and improved health access that play a pivotal role [2]. Loneliness has been established as a risk factor for adverse mental and physical health outcomes among older adults [3]. The global population aged 60 years or over numbered 962 million in 2017 and is expected to double again by 2050, when it is projected to reach nearly 2.1 billion [4].

This segment of the population passes through different physical, mental, and psycho-social problems due to ageing. Of these problems, loneliness takes a lion’s share and is becoming a growing public health issue [4, 5]. Its burden is growing, particularly among older adults, owing to various internal and external factors related to ageing [6]. It is defined as a subjective feeling which encompasses unpleasantness, sadness, emptiness, distress, suffering, isolation, lack of meaning, unwanted feelings, non-belongingness, and lack of companionship [7, 8]. There is a significant mismatch between actual and desired (or ideal) social connections and not merely social isolation. In other words, one might feel lonely despite enjoying a large social network and a high number of social connections [9–11]. Social isolation is an objective description of a lack of social connections. Although social isolation may lead to loneliness, these terms are not interchangeable [10, 11]. Loneliness is widely recognized as a public health issue with a prevalence ranging from 10.5 to 34% in the older global population [12, 13].

Loneliness may develop in individuals of all ages, but it is a crucial issue in older adults and has become a serious public health concern [14]. Multiple factors contribute to the increased loneliness among older adults. In fact, due to changes in their life cycle stages, such as retirement or age-related losses, declining health status, and decreased physical activity, older adults experience loneliness and social isolation [15]. The prevalence of loneliness in older adults (aged 60 years and older) across European countries has shown: Ukraine (34.0%), Russia (24.4%), Hungary (21.1%), and Poland (20.1%) [16]. A study conducted in the UK showed that over one million older people (aged greater than 60 years) say they always or often feel lonely, and half (49%) of all people aged 75 and over live alone [17]. A study in the United States showed that approximately 17− 57% of people feel lonely, especially older adults suffering from anxiety, depression, and dementia [18]. A study conducted in Southern African countries shows that approximately 10% of older adults report being lonely [19].

Evidence shows that loneliness in older adults is a major risk factor for broad-based morbidities, both psychological and physical, including depression, physical health, anxiety, sleep disturbances, unhealthy behavior (smoking, excessive use of alcohol, and substance abuse), cognitive impairment, and increased obesity and diabetes challenges, as well as the risk of death in later life [15, 16, 18]. In addition, chronic illnesses such as Alzheimer’s disease, vascular resistance, and high blood pressure, as well as chronic diseases such as metabolic disorders, cardiovascular disorders, hypertension, lung disease, eating disorders, obesity, and premature mortality are associated with loneliness [17, 20, 21].

Several studies show that loneliness is influenced by a wide range of factors, such as sociodemographic, and psychosocial, with gender, age, marital status, living arrangement, education level, family income, health-related behaviors, health status, and so on, and having little contact with significant friends or low-quality friendship ties; worsening physical health (e.g., increased chronic illness and impaired mobility); and lacking socio-economic resources (e.g., limited education and low income) [11, 16, 20]. In addition, a study conducted in Ireland showed that loneliness is influenced by individual characteristics of the social environment, such as having no children, reality/place of residence, length of residence, and older age [22].

Older adults are highly influenced by underlying factors such as unemployment/retirement, age-related diseases, and loss of relatives/friends; as a result, they are prone to loneliness. The results of this study will help health administrators and policymakers formulate interventions to provide need-based healthcare service plans and budget allocation. It would also help service providers to understand the factors that are associated with loneliness and provide hints for intervention. Although numerous studies have been conducted among older adults in developed countries and a few in African countries, there has been no documented systematic review and meta-analysis evidence of this problem in the African region. Therefore, this study aimed to generate a pooled prevalence of loneliness and its associated factors among the older adult population in Africa.

Inclusion and exclusion

Participants (population) of the review

The review incorporated primary articles from Africa given the population is older adults aged ≥ 60 years with the diagnosis or outcome of loneliness.

Condition of the review

In this review study, the prevalence of loneliness among the geriatric population in African countries was considered as the condition. Regarding this particular review, loneliness was defined as a depressed mood and loss of interest or pleasure in daily activities for long periods. It is defined as a subjective feeling which encompasses unpleasantness, sadness, emptiness, distress, suffering, and isolation, lack of meaning, unwanted feelings, non-belongingness, and lack of companionship [5, 6].

Context

African countries For the sake of this particular review, the African countries were defined as any one of the countries occupying the African continent [23].

The outcome of interest

This review aimed to determine the pooled prevalence of loneliness among the geriatric population in Africa. The targeted articles were observational cross-sectional studies that correctly reported the outcome of interest loneliness among the geriatric population.

Materials and methods

Registration and report of the study protocol

This systematic review and meta-analysis have utilized the guidelines developed by the JBI for Systematic Reviews [24] and the report is written consistent with the revised 2020 PRISMA guidelines [25] (S1 File). This systematic review and meta-analysis title and its protocol have been registered in the PROSPERO online database (with registration number CRD42025645286).

Searching strategies

The known and international databases (PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochran Library) and search engines (google and Google Scholar) were used to locate research articles on geriatrics loneliness in Africa. The string for searching was developed using “AND” and “OR” Boolean operators with the keywords extracted from the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) database. The search strategy was based on the research question of this review and utilized the CoCoPop (Co = Condition, Co = Context, Pop = Population) model. The article locating strategy was through “Loneliness prevalence” OR “Older Adults” OR “geriatrics loneliness” “geriatrics population loneliness” OR “Elderly Loneliness” “Elderly loneliness epidemiology” OR “Later age loneliness” OR “Elderly Social Isolation” OR “geriatrics social isolation” AND *Africa OR Africa. This search strategy primarily aimed to trace all reviewed (published) and unpublished primary studies. The list of all retrieved primary articles and systematic review and meta-analysis references were also screened or cross-referenced to get extra studies. The sources of information range from electronic databases to direct contact with the principal investigator if the need arises. The first search through Pub Med, Cochran Library, Scopus, Web of Science, Google, and Google Scholar was done in November 2024. The final search for updating was conducted from December 15/2024 to January 20/2025. The publication date was used as a filter mechanism in which articles published from January 2014 to January 2025 were included in the current systematic Review and Meta-analysis study to generate the most recent evidence for the scientific community.

Eligibility criteria

In this review, the primary articles were eligible if and only if (1) they reported loneliness among older adults (2) aged ≥ 60 years old (3) those articles reported geriatrics loneliness (4) observational studies (both analytical and descriptive cross-sectional) (5) articles published in English and only from Africa were included, and (6) published from 2014 January to January 2025 were included in this particular systematic review and meta-analysis. Nonetheless, after a thorough examination of the titles and abstracts using eligibility criteria, irrelevant studies such as conference reports, qualitative papers, retracted articles and published in languages other than English were excluded.

The study selection and outcome

After comprehensive searching, all located citations were selected and exported to Endnote citation manager software version X7. Following this, irrelevant and duplicated articles were removed. Then three independent researchers (HKA, MTA, and CKM) screened each particular article for its title, abstract, and full text by far and cross-checked it against the inclusion criteria. The other research team (WCT and TZT) checked the screened articles with full text for details by already defined criteria to take it to the final review process. Any sort of disagreement between the research team while including and excluding articles on predefined criteria of this particular review was resolved by a thorough discussion of the team. The exclusion of the articles was presented with countable reasons which could be consistent with the pre-defined criteria. The result of searching further screening and inclusion process of articles in this review was done in agreement with the PRISMA guidelines for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis 2020.

Quality appraisal of included studies

The methodological quality of the included studies was critically appraised by three independent reviewers (HKA, MTA, and CKM) by using the standardized JBI critical appraisal tool for the studies reporting prevalence [24]. This critical appraisal tool consists of 9 items designed to oversee the study population, sample size adequacy of the study, study subject and setting, reliability of condition or problem measurement, the appropriateness of the statistical test used to analyze the data, and adequacy of the response rate of each selected article for the review process (S1 Table). Each item has an answer of “No,” “Yes,” or “unclear” to rate its risk of bias (ROB). After the critical appraisal, the reviewers decided to include or exclude screened articles based on the overall quality of the appraisal score in which those articles with the lowest score out of 9 were considered as poor quality or high risk of bias. The article was excluded when the score was below average that is 4.5 of the three independent reviewers. In this regard, there had to be more than 3 “no” or “unclear” quality categories for the article to be excluded from the review. This critical appraisal threshold was supported by a previously published systematic review and meta-analysis study [26]. Any sort of disagreement between the involved reviewers was solved through the discussion of the reviewers. Furthermore, if the disagreement unfolds, the fourth reviewer was indicated to oversee the source of the doubt and reach to consensus.

Data extraction

Data were independently extracted by five authors using a standardized data extraction format that was developed according to the 2014 JBI Reviewers’ Manual [27]. The tool includes authors, region, study year, study design, sample size, the prevalence of loneliness among older adults, a tool used to measure the outcome, response rate, and risk of bias assessment score included in the extraction. The data were extracted by three (HKA, MTA, and CKM) independent reviewers and any inconsistent data were cross-checked (S2 File). The disagreement between the reviewers was solved by a thorough discussion.

Data synthesis and analysis

The outcome of included primary studies was narratively presented and expanded with supplementary materials in text, tables, and figures where necessary. All necessary and relevant information from every article was extracted through a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet, and exported to STATA Version 17 for further analysis. The random-effects model was employed to estimate the prevalence of loneliness among older-adult population due to the presence of heterogeneity. Heterogeneity was identified by using the standard chi-square and I-square statistical tests. The variation between different study characteristics such as the country where the primary article was conducted, outcome ascertainment tool, and year of publication were investigated through subgroup analyses. This subgroup analysis could demonstrate the sources of heterogeneity and let the researcher for another remedy such as the use of meta-regression to treat the heterogeneity. Moreover, the publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of the funnel plot, Begg–Mazumdar Rank correlation tests and Egger’s test to see the funnel plot’s asymmetry. The influence of individual articles on the overall pooled effect size of loneliness was assessed by using sensitivity analysis. The forest plot with 95% CI was used to present the overall pooled prevalence as well as the subgroup analysis of pooled effect size about loneliness among the older-adult population in Africa. Moreover, the Bivariate and Multivariate meta-regression analysis was conducted to identify the potential covariates that brought about the variations.

Result

The article selection and outcome

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, a total of 1180 articles related to the prevalence of loneliness among older adults in Africa were identified through electronic databases and search engines. Of these, 985 articles were removed due to irrelevance or duplication. An additional 415 articles were excluded because they were deemed ineligible either due to differences in study design or title (357) or for other reasons (58), as determined by automated screening tools. After this initial screening, 41 articles remained eligible for further assessment. Among these, 19 papers were excluded because they were conducted outside Africa or focused on populations younger than 60 years. Subsequently, 21 articles were sought for full-text retrieval, but one could not be accessed.

A total of 20 research articles were then assessed for eligibility in the review process. However, two studies were excluded due to ambiguity in measuring loneliness or the absence of the outcome of interest. Ultimately, 18 original research articles were included in the final systematic review and meta-analysis (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Eighteen original research articles were included in the final systematic review and meta-analysis

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, a total of 21,944 participants were included, with response rates ranging from 70 to 100%. All studies were observational cross-sectional designs published between 2014 and 2025. The included articles were conducted in six African countries, with the majority of studies originating from Nigeria and Egypt. Moreover, the lowest prevalence of loneliness was 9.90% in South Africa [19], followed by 17.7% in Ghana [30]. There are a total of 18 articles assessed for methodological quality using a 9-point score tool developed by JBI for observational prevalence studies. The outcome of the quality appraisal ranged from moderate to high methodological quality in which three studies scored six [28, 29, 42], six studies [19, 31, 34, 36, 40, 41] scored seven, four studies [43–46] scored eight, and the other four [30, 33, 35, 39] scored nine (S1 Table).

A total of 18 primary articles were appraised and retrieved to pool the prevalence of loneliness among the older adult population in Africa. These articles were from ten African countries with Nigeria and Egypt the majority. The overall pooled prevalence of loneliness among older adults was 37.86 [95% CI (30.11, 45.62), I2 = 100%, p < 0.01]. Heterogeneity assessment

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, the analysis output using a random-effects model showed high variability across the primary articles included in the study (I2 = 100%, p < 0.001). This variability is inevitable in meta-analysis studies resulting from quality differences of the included studies, methodological differences, sample size, inclusion and exclusion, and the difference in measuring tools to ascertain the outcome of interest. Therefore, we have conducted the meta-regression analysis by using publication year, sample size, and standard error as covariates to figure out the potential source of heterogeneity among included studies.

In this regard, the meta-regression analysis revealed that there was a significant correlation found between the outcome of interest (loneliness) and the included covariates by far (p = 0.001 for publication year and p = 0.042 for sample size). Hence, there was no statistically significant association and possible existence of variability as shown (Table 3). This again implies that the source of high variability (heterogeneity) could be due to chance or the other variables not investigated in this particular review (Table 4). Subgroup analysis

In this review, the subgroup analysis was employed by using the country and the year of publication in the primary reviewed articles. In the subgroup analysis by country, the lowest pooled prevalence of loneliness using the random-effects model was 25.95% [95% CI (− 5.51, 57.41), I2 = 100.0%, p < 0.001] in South Africa followed by 35.03% [95% CI (23.68, 46.38), I2 = 100.0%, p < 0.001] in Nigeria. While, the highest prevalence of loneliness was 51.90% [95% CI (8.58, 95.22), I2 = 100.0%, p < 0.001] in Uganda. Moreover, the pooled prevalence of loneliness based on the publication year ≥ 2021 was 39.20% [95% CI (29.66, 48.74), I2 = 100.0%, p < 0.001] (Table 3).

Publication bias assessment

In this particular systematic review and meta-analysis, study publication bias was assessed by using visual inspection of a funnel plot which referred to asymmetry, almost all the studies laid to the right of the line (Fig. 3). However, it was not statistically significant as shown in Egger’s test (p = 0.149) which depicted that the outputs were not influenced by publication bias for one or the other reasons. Sensitivity analysis

We have conducted a sensitivity analysis to identify whether there is evidence of the influencing effect of one study on the other. The output of leave-one-out sensitivity analysis through the random-effects model revealed that there was no individual study that influenced the overall pooled estimate of loneliness among the geriatric population in Africa in this particular review. The included studies’ certainty assessment evidence

In this systematic review and meta-analysis study, all the included primary articles were cross-sectional and we reached a low certainty of evidence conclusion we extended our assessment of the certainty of the evidence through five down-grading domains and three up-grading elements of the GRADE certainty assessment criteria [49]. In this regard, evidence is liable to inconsistencies because a few studies were eligible for this particular study. This was revealed by the existence of heterogeneity in the estimate of the outcome of interest across different regions of the continent of Africa. However, we found no publication bias and risk of bias as most included studies followed robust and scientific methods. The absence of publication bias was revealed by the Egger’s test and funnel plots. The absence of outcome measurement imprecision was revealed by the well-enough sample size of all included studies and the narrow confidence interval of the pooled effect size. Besides, no included studies indirectly measured the outcome of interest in the prevalence of loneliness among the geriatric population, but direct measurement was employed from all the included studies. The confounding was controlled by seeing all the potential factors that probably disturb the measurement of the estimate of the outcome variable and the estimate of outcome of interest in all included studies was well enough. Overall, the prevalence of loneliness among the geriatric population in Africa is highly liable to change given more primary articles included; therefore, it entails further studies to produce robust evidence that authors could rely on for decision-making capability and purpose (S4 Table).

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis study, the overall pooled prevalence of loneliness among older adults was 37.86% [95% CI (30.11, 45.62), I2 = 100%, p < 0.01]. The finding of this study was consistent with the studies conducted in India [50] and Belgium [51] and that reported 44.0% and 31.6% of older adults experienced loneliness, respectively. The current systematic review and meta-analysis result was higher than the study conducted in Canada [46] in which 28.6% of the elderly suffered from loneliness.

The current study finding was also higher than the studies conducted in Australia [52] and the UK [53] which reported 24.2% and 28.5% of older adults experienced loneliness, respectively. The discrepancy might be due to socio-demographic, socio-economic, and socio-cultural variation between the sample population of the current study and the study in Australia.

Similarly, it was higher than the study reported the prevalence of loneliness ranged from 25.3% to 32.4% across Latin American countries [54]. The possible reason for the variation might be the socio-demographic difference between the African population and Latin America and India. Moreover, it was higher than the study conducted in Germany [55] which reported 20.8% of older adults suffering from chronic loneliness. The discrepancy might be due to the time gap and population difference in which the study in Germany focused on chronic loneliness, but the current included all cases of loneliness irrespective of onset. On the other hand, the current finding was lower than the study conducted in New Zealand [56] that reported 35–61% of older adults suffered from moderate to severe loneliness.

In this systematic review and meta-analysis study, we have found variables such as sex of the participants, marital status, habit of alcohol intake, social capital, functional impairment, and having depressive symptoms reported as predictors of loneliness among the elderly population in our review of primary articles.

Regarding the determinants of loneliness, we found marital status, alcohol intake, and having depressive symptoms as significantly associated with loneliness among older adults in an African population. Married individuals have 59% lower odds of experiencing loneliness compared to their unmarried counterparts. This might be because emotional, social, and practical support within a marital relationship often creates an environment that naturally mitigates loneliness. For instance, marriage usually gives a constant source of companionship and emotional support. Provided that the marriage is positive, the spouses could share whatever feelings, experiences, and difficulties with each other, hence mitigating feelings of loneliness. That said marriage boosts confidence and creates a buffering positive energy against stress and loneliness. Furthermore, married individuals tend to have larger and more stable social networks. Couples often maintain connections with friends, family, and shared communities, which can expand their opportunities for social interaction. This was supported by a study conducted in the United States of America that stated that marriage has a pivotal role in mitigating feelings of isolation and loneliness [57].

On the other hand, the odds of having loneliness were 1.87 times increased among the elderly population who had a habit of alcohol intake compared to its counterpart. Increased odds of loneliness among older adults who consume alcohol compared to non-drinkers may be attributed to several interconnected factors such as considering alcohol as a coping mechanism, social isolation due to alcohol consumption, physical and mental decline, and lack of healthy coping strategies [58]. For example, many elderly individuals may turn to alcohol as a way to cope with feelings of loneliness, stress, or sadness. However, while alcohol may temporarily numb negative emotions, it often exacerbates feelings of isolation in the long run. Research supported that alcohol consumption and loneliness correlated directly or indirectly in the elderly population [59, 60].

However, there are 3.13 times the odds of increased loneliness among the elderly with depressive symptoms compared to depression-symptom-free individuals. This might be because depression can cause individuals to perceive social interactions negatively, leading to feelings of rejection, unworthiness, or pessimism about relationships. Research supports that depression is one of the reinforcing factors for loneliness by boosting social withdrawal [61, 62]. For instance, depressive symptoms often cause fatigue, lack of motivation, and a tendency to isolate oneself.

Strengths and limitations of the study

The output of this systematic review and meta-analysis provides an up-to-date body of knowledge about the prevalence and severity of loneliness among this overlooked segment population in Africa. It produced aggregated and rigorous evidence to think about and devise strategies to tackle loneliness and its psychosocial impact. Furthermore, this study has its strength in generating pooled data in Africa which could help those in need of such evidence to devise strategies to tackle depression. The findings of this study have had an important clinical implication in providing pooled evidence of loneliness for those who are interested in the area, particularly for those focusing on the mental health and well-being of the geriatric population.

However, it has also important limitations such as it only includes articles published in English, and the primary studies were all observational cross-sectional studies. In addition, this meta-analysis collected data only from six African countries hence there might be an underrepresentation of certain subgroups such as older adults with disabilities, and minority ethnic groups. Findings may not represent all African regions, especially those with scarce research. There was potential publication bias; there was high heterogeneity across countries. The cross-sectional nature of many studies may limit causal inferences about determinants. Therefore; further global-based studies to assess other factors related to loneliness and determinant factors by incorporating qualitative studies.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis revealed that well over one-third of older adults in Africa suffered from loneliness. Marital status, alcohol drinking habits, and depressive symptoms were found significant predictors of loneliness among older adults in Africa. Moreover, there has to be further research to figure out the potential factors perhaps using both qualitative and quantitative research approaches.
