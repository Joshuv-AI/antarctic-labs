SoK: From Silicon to Netlist and Beyond – Two Decades of Hardware Reverse Engineering Research
Zehra Karadağ, Simon Klix, René Walendy, Felix Hahn, Kolja Dorschel, Julian Speith, Christof Paar, and Steffen Becker, [0.8em] Ruhr University Bochum

Abstract
As hardware serves as the root of trust in modern computing systems, Hardware Reverse Engineering (HRE) is foundational for security assurance. In practice, HRE enables critical security applications, including design verification, supply-chain assurance, and vulnerability discovery. Over the past two decades, academic research on Integrated Circuit (IC), Field-Programmable Gate Array (FPGA), and netlist reverse engineering has steadily grown. However, knowledge remains fragmented across domains and communities, which complicates assessing the state of the art and hampers identifying shared research challenges. In this paper, we present a systematization of knowledge based on an in-depth analysis of 187 peer-reviewed publications. Using this corpus, we characterize technical methods across the HRE workflow and identify technical and organizational challenges that impede research progress. We analyze all 30 artifacts from our corpus using established artifact evaluation practices. Key results could be reproduced for only seven publications (4%). Based on our findings, we derive stakeholder-centric recommendations for academia, industry, and government to enable more coordinated and reproducible HRE research. These recommendations target three cross-cutting opportunities: (i) improving reproducibility and reuse via artifact-centric practices, (ii) enabling rigorous comparability through standardized benchmarks and evaluation metrics, and (iii) improving legal clarity for public HRE research.

1
Introduction

Modern computing systems increasingly rely on a silicon root of trust, particularly in high-assurance and safety-critical domains
[
32
]
.
However, hardware manufacturing is characterized by globally distributed and complex supply chains, which introduce opportunities for the insertion of malicious functionality such as hardware Trojans
[
2
]
, counterfeiting
[
64
]
, and intellectual property theft
[
89
]
.
Recognition of these supply chain vulnerabilities catalyzed research into
HRE
(
HRE
)—the process of reconstructing higher levels of abstraction from fabricated hardware or intermediate design representations
[
27
]
—as an essential capability for establishing and verifying trust in the hardware supply chain
[
178
]
.
In practice,
HRE
supports critical security tasks including vulnerability discovery
[
133
]
, design verification through hardware Trojan detection
[
140
]
, and counterfeit avoidance
[
5
,
12
]
.
As
HRE
can also be used offensively to extract design secrets
[
88
]
or facilitate hardware-level attacks
[
80
,
90
,
93
]
, public
HRE
research is equally essential for assessing attacker capabilities and evaluating protections.

Annual publication count of the peer-reviewed
HRE
papers in our corpus, grouped by primary research topic.

After two decades of steadily growing research (see
Figure 1
),
HRE
evolved into an established yet fragmented field, divided into
IC
(
IC
),
FPGA
(
FPGA
), and netlist reverse engineering, with researchers from hardware design, hardware manufacturing, failure analysis, and hardware security.
As a result, there is no holistic perspective, and research often proceeds in parallel across communities.
Earlier surveys
[
6
,
12
,
142
,
56
]
cover specific sub-disciplines—such as image processing and machine learning
[
12
]
, algorithmic methods
[
6
]
, or
HRE
countermeasures
[
142
]
—but do not capture the field as a whole.
The prevailing fragmentation across topics and communities limits the ability to systematically assess existing techniques and motivates our first research question:

RQ1:
What technical approaches exist for
HRE
?

To answer RQ1, we conducted an extensive literature review and compiled a systematization of techniques for
IC
,
FPGA
, and netlist reverse engineering across 187 peer-reviewed publications.
In the process, we observed recurring issues that appear to hinder progress across the field, motivating our second research question:

RQ2:
What are the technical and organizational challenges hindering the advancement of
HRE
?

In addressing RQ2, we analyzed these challenges and observed substantial overlap across
HRE
subdomains.
In particular, published techniques are only sporadically compared, evaluation metrics and benchmarks vary across publications, and prior results are rarely used as direct foundations for subsequent work.
In search of a root cause, we define our final research question, which focuses on the availability and quality of artifacts such as implementations and benchmarks:

RQ3:
To what extent do
HRE
publications provide artifacts that are available, functional, and reproducible?

In response to RQ3, we find that the scarcity of high-quality artifacts appears to contribute to limited reproducibility and, consequently, comparability across publications.
Across 187 papers, 31 publications (
17\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
) claim to provide artifacts, corresponding to 30 distinct artifacts in total.
However, only 24 were fully accessible, and one was partially accessible.
Of the 20 artifacts with exercisable tools, only seven allowed us to reproduce at least some of the key results reported in their corresponding publications.
Based on these insights, we identify three structural opportunities for rigorous, cumulative, and scalable
HRE
research that cut across technical, organizational, and legal dimensions.
Correspondingly, we propose concrete stakeholder-centric actions for academia, industry, and government that target (i) promoting reproducibility and reusability, (ii) community-driven efforts for comparability, and (iii) strengthening the legal framework surrounding public
HRE
research.
We argue that acting systematically and jointly on these opportunities is a necessary step toward evolving
HRE
into a more coordinated research discipline.

2
Methodology

To provide a holistic overview of peer-reviewed
HRE
research, we consider the complete end-to-end process, spanning from gate-level netlist recovery from
IC
or
FPGA
to subsequent netlist reverse engineering.
As illustrated in
Figure 2
, we followed a structured four-stage analysis pipeline, guided by the methodology of Wolfswinkel
et al.

[
192
]
.
Starting with database searches (Step A), we filter and organize the discovered papers (Step B).
The retained papers are then used as a seed set to iteratively expand the corpus through citation mining, with newly identified papers subjected to the same filtering process (Step B).
We then analyze the resulting papers in detail (Step C) and examine the available artifacts according to the common artifact badge categories
Availability
,
Functionality
, and
Reproducibility
(Step D).

Figure 2:
Four-stage pipeline for systematizing knowledge in
HRE
: literature search, paper selection and domain categorization, distillation of techniques and open challenges, and artifact evaluation.

2.1
Analysis Pipeline

Step A: Literature Search.

We identified 571 papers through database searches of DBLP, Semantic Scholar, and Google Scholar using a search query (see
Appendix A
) that was designed based on domain expertise and highly cited
HRE
publications
[
56
,
199
,
12
]
.
To ensure comprehensive coverage of
HRE
literature, we performed backward and forward citation mining using Crossref and Semantic Scholar.
Backward mining identified works cited within our corpus, while forward mining identified publications that referenced papers in the corpus.
A paper was included if it appeared at least three times in backward or forward mining, balancing coverage and manageability by filtering weakly connected works.
This step added 402 publications over four iterations, bringing the total count to 973 papers.
The final iteration in December 2025 added only one paper, indicating saturation.

Step B: Select & Organize Relevant Papers.

In each iteration, we filtered papers for relevance in two stages.
First, to determine whether a paper contributed to
HRE
, one author screened each title and abstract and proposed an initial decision, which was then discussed and finalized with two additional authors.
After the final iteration, this process had resulted in 344 publications for subsequent categorization.
Second, two authors reviewed each paper and labeled it according to the topics it covers, following the codebook in
Table 2
.
For each paper, exactly one primary code and any number of secondary codes were assigned.
Additionally, each paper received at least one type label from the codebook in
Table 3
based on its primary contribution.
Disagreements were resolved with a third author to reach a final decision.

In both stages, papers were excluded if they were not in English, not peer-reviewed, behind paywalls that prevented access despite standard institutional subscriptions, or written as technical reports, dissertations, or tutorials.
Papers were also excluded if they did not primarily contribute to
HRE
research.
Examples for such exclusions include research on pedagogical aspects, hardware design, Trojans, obfuscation, or side-channel analysis.
Ultimately, this process yielded a final corpus of 187 papers, divided into three categories based on their primary topic: 87 focused on
IC
, 23 on
FPGA
, and 77 on netlist reverse engineering.

Step C: Distill Techniques & Challenges.

To answer RQ1 and RQ2, three teams of two researchers—one team per major topic (i. e.,
IC
,
FPGA
, and netlist reverse engineering)—analyzed all publications.
Every paper was read by one researcher, who highlighted text according to a labeling scheme developed by their team.
These labels are tailored to each major topic and focus on techniques, challenges, and evaluation procedures.
This categorization provided the empirical basis for distilling techniques and identifying open challenges.

Step D: Artifact Evaluation.

We categorized all
HRE
artifacts as tools, images, or other resources (e. g., scripts or
RTL
(
RTL
) code).
We then evaluated all artifacts with respect to
Availability
,
Functionality
, and
Reproducibility
.
For availability, we distinguish between permanent repositories (e. g., Zenodo), non-permanent hosting (e. g., GitHub or personal websites), availability upon request, and unavailable artifacts (no longer hosted or no response to requests).
Functionality comprises
completeness
(verifying that all claimed components are provided),
documentation quality
, and
exercisability
(successful execution), with exercisability assessed only for artifacts classified as tools.
Reproducibility was assessed by whether we could reproduce key results reported in the paper, using a binary pass/fail criterion.
The evaluation was conducted retrospectively, without artifact author interaction.
Two undergraduate research assistants with prior
HRE
experience performed the hands-on evaluation and documented their findings for each artifact using a structured template.
Senior researchers supervised the process through regular review and discussion meetings.
On average, each artifact required approximately six hours of evaluation effort.

2.2
Limitations

Our literature search may have excluded relevant work in other languages.
Additionally, our exclusion criteria may have removed papers containing relevant insights at the boundaries of our defined scope.
These methodological trade-offs were necessary to maintain feasibility, and we argue their impact was mitigated through iterative backward and forward citation mining until saturation.
Furthermore, parts of the screening and in-depth analysis relied on individual assessments, and—as with any interpretive review—alternative coding or inclusion decisions could lead to slightly different classifications.
To reduce such effects, all selection, labeling, and analysis decisions were systematically reviewed through multi-author discussions and consensus procedures.

3.1
From
IC
to Netlist

Modern digital
IC
consist of large-scale Boolean logic circuits, realized by a few hundred thousand to many billions
[
65
]
of nanoscopic transistors on the polysilicon layer.
Stacked on top, multiple layers of metal routing connect those transistors to form a circuit.
Extracting the netlist entails recovering transistors and interconnection layers alike, generally in a destructive manner, relying extensively on advanced imaging and computer vision techniques.

3.1.1
Techniques for
IC
Reverse Engineering (RQ1)

The literature shows that recovering a netlist from an
IC
is generally described as a process divided into five stages:
I1
sample preparation,
I2
imaging,
I3
stitching and stacking,
I4
semantic segmentation, and
I5
netlist extraction.

I1
Sample Preparation.

An
IC
is protected by an encapsulation, also known as a package.
The package ensures physical durability
[
11
]
and provides connections, e. g., to a circuit board
[
63
]
.
This package must be removed to access the die before the delayering process can reveal the circuit.

Depackaging.

Methods for depackaging depend on the package material.
Packages are typically made of plastic, metal, or ceramic
[
114
,
11
]
.
Plastic packages constitute the majority and are primarily removed using acid
[
113
,
149
,
87
]
.
The literature provides information on the ratios of acid mixtures and processing temperatures required for effective removal
[
114
,
80
]
.
Some packages allow for the top cover to be removed with simple mechanical means instead
[
112
]
.
Advanced packages may encapsulate multiple dies under a single metal lid, in which case the metal can be removed by milling
[
135
]
.
Decapsulation of ceramic packages has not been discussed in our corpus of
HRE
literature.

Cross-Section.

A first step in analyzing a die is often sacrificing one sample for cross-sectioning by cutting across all of its layers.
Examining the cross-section allows identifying the materials and thickness of each layer
[
99
,
103
,
135
]
,
which then can guide the choice of delayering methods.

Delayering.

Layer-removal methodologies can be classified into several categories.

Wet etching
removes material through chemical reactions in liquid solutions
[
103
,
112
,
87
]
.

Mechanical etching
relies on direct mechanical contact with a solid tool or abrasive
[
133
,
80
,
103
,
149
,
134
,
102
]
.
It may be combined with chemistry, as in chemical-mechanical polishing
[
135
]
.

Dry etching
comprises a variety of techniques in the gas phase or vacuum using ions, plasma, or reactive gases
[
103
,
87
]
.
For instance, in
ion milling
, a beam of energetic ions is accelerated toward the sample, physically sputtering atoms from its surface
[
147
,
148
,
113
]
.
Ion milling can be chemically assisted
[
177
]
; however, material removal remains ion-driven.
It is not to be confused with
reactive ion etching
, which also uses ion bombardment but removes material primarily chemically
[
138
,
85
]
.

During the delayering process, it is important to keep the surface planar
[
80
,
36
,
9
,
150
]
, and to know when to stop to avoid damage to subsequent layers, a process called endpoint detection.
The endpoint can be detected using manual
SEM
(
SEM
) inspection
[
103
]
, or other methods such as ultraviolet photon spectroscopy emission
[
43
]
, or optical microscopy
[
149
,
102
,
134
]
.

I2
Imaging.

Imaging
IC
is usually done with an
SEM
.
Proper use requires balancing parameters to obtain reliable results in reasonable time, and improper settings can produce low-quality images.
Optimal settings vary depending on the instrument, even when imaging the same layer of the same
IC

[
107
]
.
Settings and detector choice also depend on the type of layer to be imaged.
While detectors can be combined
[
101
]
, they are typically used individually.
For metal layers, backscattered electron detectors are preferred, as they provide the necessary contrast to distinguish metal from surrounding insulation
[
148
,
87
]
.
For imaging transistor gates, however, secondary electron detectors are used to reveal fine surface topography
[
11
,
10
]
.

I3
Stitching & Stacking.

Since the field of view of an
SEM
is limited, it cannot capture an entire layer at once.
Consequently, each layer is scanned in a two-dimensional grid, with every image depicting only a small tile of the layer.
To reconstruct transitions between images, tiles need to be
stitched
together.
To facilitate this stitching, images are taken with an overlap.
Although some modern imaging systems offer automated stitching, researchers often use custom algorithms
[
173
]
or adapt tools from other domains
[
133
,
80
]
.
To this end, adjacent tiles are first positioned locally, i. e., relative to each other, leveraging overlap using techniques such as phase correlation
[
98
,
153
]
, normalized cross-correlation
[
153
]
, or feature extraction
[
153
,
103
]
.
Because local stitching can be imperfect, global stitching optimizes the mosaic by considering multiple neighboring tiles, either sequentially
[
17
]
or simultaneously
[
102
,
153
]
.
To recover signal routing across layers and reveal the
IC
’s 3D structure, the stitched layers must be
stacked
.
To this end, anchor points are defined between adjacent layers, and the layer image is warped accordingly
[
143
]
.
Notably, we found no use of deep learning for stitching, though it has been applied to stack alignment
[
98
,
153
]
and to detect stitching errors
[
98
]
.

I4
Semantic Segmentation.

Wires, vias, transistor gates, and active regions are identified through semantic segmentation, with optional pre- and post-processing.

Pre-Processing.

IC
images can be pre-processed to facilitate subsequent recognition methods, although these approaches do not always improve results
[
108
]
.
This step typically has two objectives, which are often achieved using classical computer vision methods:
First, noise is suppressed through averaging of multiple images
[
103
]
, or spatial filters incorporating surrounding pixels
[
117
,
108
]
.
Second, adjusting brightness and contrast corrects for visual inconsistencies between tiles caused by acquisition variations
[
171
,
108
]
.
Deep learning has also been applied for noise reduction
[
195
]
, but requires retraining for each new dataset
[
62
]
.

Segmentation.

To eventually reconstruct a gate-level netlist, all visible circuit elements on each layer must be identified.
Historically, this identification was performed manually by crawling over printed images
[
173
]
, and later using digital annotation software
[
144
,
80
]
.
However, with the rapid increase in transistor counts, manual analysis has become impractical, and thus automated methods have emerged.
For metal layers, researchers first relied on classical computer vision methods that group pixels based on intensity thresholds
[
11
,
117
,
118
]
and texture
[
189
]
, or detect boundaries through brightness gradients
[
101
]
.
More sophisticated techniques take design rules, such as track thickness or spacing, into account
[
101
]
.

An established classical approach for detecting standard cells on the polysilicon and active layer is template matching.
Here, image regions are compared against a library of visual reference patterns
[
35
,
144
]
.
If details about the manufacturing process are unknown, a standard cell library might first need to be extracted from the images, either manually
[
133
]
or automatically through finding common patterns
[
209
,
104
]
.
Other research adopts techniques used for metal track segmentation to automatically detect standard cells by individually identifying transistors and their interconnections
[
74
,
143
]
.

Recently, the focus has gradually shifted to machine-learning approaches
[
70
]
.
The plethora of such research utilizes supervised learning
[
103
,
97
,
26
,
197
,
104
,
176
]
on all
IC
layers; however, these models require massive amounts of labeled training data.
Manually labeling real
IC
images is time-consuming
[
23
,
144
]
.
To bridge this gap, standard data augmentation—such as random rotations and intensity shifts—is used to artificially expand limited datasets
[
203
,
167
]
.
Beyond simple augmentation, researchers have investigated generating synthetic images from layout masks, i. e.,
GDSII
(
GDSII
) files.
These provide a ground truth and enable training at scale
[
190
,
113
,
194
,
94
]
.
Despite promising results with synthetic data
[
26
]
, domain transfer of a trained model from one
IC
to another is limited, as
IC
vary significantly in appearance.
Consequently, we observe a push toward unsupervised learning techniques
[
147
]
or automatic pseudo-label generation
[
24
]
.
Recent publications propose using transfer learning.
To this end, Meta’s SAM has been fine-tuned on
IC
images
[
132
]
.
Another technique relies on
CNN
pretrained on the generic ImageNet dataset to extract general visual features from
IC
images without fine-tuning
[
171
]
.
Another interesting approach, in cases when no ground truth is available, is to transform other images with existing ground truth to resemble the unlabeled images.
A model trained on these images can then be applied to the unlabeled data
[
168
,
26
]
.

Post-Processing.
Post-processing can be applied to identify or reduce segmentation errors.
This prevents misclassifications from propagating into semantic violations in the extracted netlist, such as unintended open or short circuits.
Approaches from the literature correct segmentations based on neighboring pixel values, also resulting in smoother boundaries and less noise
[
26
,
203
,
46
]
.
Other post-processing procedures check for design rule violations related to, e. g., via size
[
117
]
, wire width
[
102
]
, or spacing
[
117
]
.
Another approach detects segmentation errors using
CNN

[
207
]
.

I5
Netlist Extraction.

After all images are stitched, stacked, and segmented, a gate-level netlist can be extracted, abstracting the physical layout into a network of Boolean logic gates and sequential elements.
The connection between logic gates can be reconstructed by tracing vias and wires
[
133
,
103
,
74
]
.
The extraction mechanism for logic gates is dependent on the employed segmentation technique.
Where standard cells have been identified using template matching, the connection of each cell to the metal layers above can be immediately identified
[
103
]
.
If transistor-level segmentation was used, the logic function realized by the transistors in each standard cell must first be identified
[
74
,
47
,
143
]
.
To this end, subgraph matching is commonly used
[
74
,
141
]
.

Challenges in

,

Major Research—Industry Gap in Technology Node.

Particularly for advanced processes, such as FinFET nodes introduced around 2012

, academic

.

Error Propagation.

The success of extracting a netlist from an

Contamination such as dust particles can obscure critical circuit features

During imaging itself, poor parameter choice, e. g., for acceleration voltage and dwell time, can deteriorate image quality

Finally, most semantic segmentation algorithms assume error-free inputs, which is impossible to achieve in practice

Many stages in

For instance, delayering must stop at a precise depth to avoid obscuring features or destroying the sample

Similarly,

However, reliance on human intuition inherently introduces inter-operator variability

This puts the field in a quandary where manual intervention remains both irreplaceable and undesirable.
To mitigate this issue, researchers have explored tools for individual sub-steps, such as automated layer-thickness estimation

However, fully automating the

Advanced equipment can improve both throughput and quality

.

.
Beyond equipment, access to specialized facilities, e. g., cleanrooms, is needed for advanced technology nodes.
Consequently, reverse engineering state-of-the-art

.
The difficulty to obtain

However, the effects of using synthetic

Even for ideal images, recognition methods struggle to generalize across technology nodes and

.

When designing recognition algorithms, validation depends on accurate ground-truth labels and appropriate metrics, raising two concerns.
First, manual image labeling is an arduous and costly task

3.2
From
FPGA
to Netlist

FPGA
are reconfigurable hardware devices that can be programmed to implement almost any digital circuit.
To achieve this, a bitstream file in a proprietary format is loaded into the
FPGA
’s configuration memory, which is typically implemented using
SRAM
(
SRAM
).
Because this memory is volatile, the
FPGA
must be re-programmed on every power-up.
Hence, the bitstream must be stored in an external non-volatile memory.
To recover a gate-level netlist from an
FPGA
bitstream, one must understand its proprietary file format, recover the bitstream, potentially decrypt it, and finally convert it into a netlist
[
179
]
.

3.2.1
Techniques for
FPGA
Reverse Engineering (RQ1)

Based on our literature review, we split
FPGA
reverse engineering into four major steps:
F1
Bitstream Format Reverse Engineering,
F2
Bitstream Extraction,
F3
Bitstream Decryption, and finally
F4
Bitstream Conversion.
The majority of our analysis is grounded in the 23
FPGA
papers included in our final corpus.
However, because the number of
FPGA
reverse engineering publications is relatively small, we also extracted relevant information from work that was initially excluded—e. g., concerning bitstream protections and bitstream manipulation.

F1
Bitstream Format Reverse Engineering.

Bitstreams follow a standard binary file structure, comprising metadata, control commands, cryptographic material, checksums, and the configuration data that defines the functionality of the programmable
FPGA
fabric.
To reverse-engineer a bitstream’s high-level structure, prior work has mostly relied on public documentation from
FPGA
vendors
[
210
,
202
,
130
]
.
Undocumented structure information can be reverse-engineered through manual analysis using
EDA
(
EDA
) tool features such as debug bitstreams
[
81
]
, or by reverse-engineering vendor software
[
164
,
126
]
.

The configuration data encodes both the functionality of logic elements, aka.
PLP
, such as
LUT
,
FF
, or hard
IP
(
IP
) blocks like DSPs, BRAMs, and PCIe, as well as the routing between them via
PIP

[
44
]
.
Vendors typically do not disclose the mapping from configuration bits in the bitstream to the corresponding physical components in the
FPGA
fabric
[
163
]
.
To reveal this proprietary relationship, researchers often construct hardware designs using vendor-provided
EDA
tools, generate corresponding bitstreams, and then systematically modify the positions and functions of individual programmable elements.
By comparing bitstream differences before and after each change, they identify which bits configure specific logic or routing elements
[
206
,
8
,
86
]
.
While this methodology remains consistent across studies, nuanced variations were introduced to improve accuracy and performance.

Table 1
provides an overview of the different approaches.

Initially, a correlation-based approach was proposed, in which designs are synthesized, and the behavior of individual bits across multiple bitstreams is correlated with the resources used by each design
[
8
]
.
This approach was later improved by generating bitstreams that contain isolated logic or routing elements, enabling a direct one-to-one mapping between each configurable element and the bits that control it
[
29
]
.
To reduce computational effort, some studies have leveraged the regular structure of many
FPGA
architectures rather than explicitly mapping every configuration bit
[
206
,
8
]
.
Because similar elements often share a consistent configuration pattern in the bitstream, reverse engineering such patterns once can suffice to extrapolate them to other instances across the device
[
202
]
.
The format reverse engineering process may be further sped up by reducing the number of generated bitstreams—either by partitioning bitstreams to extract information about multiple elements simultaneously
[
205
]
, or by combining information from different bitstreams
[
29
]
.

F2
Bitstream Extraction.

Most papers treat bitstream extraction as a technical challenge and either tap the memory buses connecting the non-volatile memory to the
FPGA

[
202
,
129
]
or dump the memory
[
56
]
.
Some research mentions niche methods in which the bitstream is loaded by a co-processor, allowing for extraction via software analysis
[
88
]
or reading back the bitstream directly from the
FPGA
via (hidden) debug mechanisms
[
156
,
210
]
.
Overall, bitstream extraction receives little attention in the academic literature.

F3
Bitstream Decryption.

Most modern
FPGA
provide encryption and authentication to protect bitstreams.
However, prior work has shown that these bitstream protections can often be broken or circumvented by (i) side channel attacks based on leaked information through power or electromagnetic radiation
[
126
,
164
,
68
]
, (ii) optical attacks leaking encryption keys
[
165
,
106
]
, or (iii) attacks exploiting bugs in the implementation of security mechanisms
[
51
,
50
,
49
]
.
We deem bitstream protection mechanisms out of scope for our study and focus on the reverse engineering of unencrypted bitstreams, as most works on
FPGA
reverse engineering do.

F4
Bitstream Conversion.

In the literature, the process of converting a bitstream into a gate-level netlist comprises two steps: (i) the recovery of logic elements and (ii) the reconstruction of routing.
The behavior of logic elements can be inferred from the corresponding configuration bits in the reversed bit mapping.
This includes reconstructing Boolean functions from truth tables, either through Boolean minimization
[
202
,
77
]
or machine learning
[
130
]
.

Interconnections of logic elements are implemented using sequences of routing segments, which can be controlled via the bitstream at physical junctions using programmable switches.
To reconstruct such connections, the individual segments must be merged into coherent nets, e. g., through a
BFS
(
BFS
)
[
206
]
.
Some elements have ambiguous configurations.
For example, distinguishing between an unused component and a component in default configuration may be impossible
[
44
]
.
In such cases, surrounding elements must be taken into consideration
[
206
]
.

A related branch of research has successfully inferred high-level information about the design, such as the presence of specific
IP
cores or functional blocks, by analyzing the bitstream directly without performing netlist recovery.
Initial work identifies
IP
cores based on
LUT
contents
[
210
]
, while more recent research uses machine-learning techniques
[
109
]
, or operates on bitstreams transformed into images
[
21
]
.

3.2.2
Challenges in
FPGA
Reverse Engineering (RQ2)

Based on our literature review, we identify five open challenges in
FPGA
reverse engineering.

(Verified) Completeness.

Modern
FPGA
are highly complex and include device-specific features beyond the classical configurable elements, e. g., hard
IP
blocks such as DSPs and PCIe interfaces.
A major technical challenge is achieving and verifying the completeness of the recovered bitstream format in a black-box setting.
To solve this challenge, additional information, such as default values applied at power-up and a comprehensive understanding of the configuration process itself, is required
[
187
]
.
Although some studies claim perfect recovery of the entire bitstream format, they validate their work only on minimal benchmarks (see
Table 1
).

Reliance on Deprecated Tooling and Narrow Focus.

Many existing approaches rely on the ability to control each configurable element in isolation
[
206
,
28
,
205
]
.
While older
EDA
tools such as AMD
1
1

1

We use AMD to refer to devices formerly branded as Xilinx, consistent with their current ownership under AMD.
ISE, which has been deprecated since 2013, often allowed for such fine-grained control, it is no longer feasible in modern tools such as AMD Vivado
[
205
]
.
Although some progress was made adapting legacy reverse-engineering workflows to more recent toolchains, the aforementioned issues have not been overcome, especially for routing
[
30
]
.
Consequently, many proposed methods cannot be applied to current-generation devices, including the speed-ups proposed by Zhang
et al.

[
205
]
.
Existing research on bitstream reverse engineering exhibits a disproportionate focus on AMD
FPGA
, see
Table 1
.
The resulting over-representation of a single vendor could stem from the lack of public low-level documentation from other vendors.
The absence of other manufacturers and the lack of generalization leave a significant gap in research.

Scattered Information.

FPGA
bitstream documentation is used not only for reverse engineering but also for design applications.
Therefore, relevant knowledge is scattered across research communities, which hampers a comprehensive understanding.
Knowledge is distributed among hardware reverse engineers, open-source hardware designers
[
175
,
40
,
54
,
33
,
172
]
, specialized groups focusing on bitstream manipulation
[
39
,
115
,
78
,
79
]
, and researchers investigating
FPGA
bitstream security
[
164
,
126
,
49
]
.

Limitations in Methodology and Validation.

Overall, research on
FPGA
reverse engineering is of mixed quality.
Often, works omit crucial details, rendering independent reproduction or use of their techniques challenging, even for domain experts, further worsened by the fact that only two publications release reference implementations.
Evaluations often validate only a subset of the stated claims, limiting confidence in the reported results and obscuring the rigor of the underlying methods.
Moreover, some publications offer limited novelty by revisiting problems already solved in prior work.
These issues impede cumulative progress and may explain the lack of
FPGA
reverse engineering publications at top-tier venues.
This scarcity may also be supported by vendors’ efforts to protect their
IP
through licensing restrictions that try to legally prohibit bitstream analysis.
Collectively, these limitations may explain the lack of publicly available, peer-reviewed academic tools capable of recovering arbitrary netlists from
FPGA
bitstreams.
For now, open-source initiatives such as Project X-Ray
[
54
]
and IceStorm
[
33
]
, while not achieving
100\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
completeness, represent the only available and usable solutions to this end.

3.3
Netlist Reverse Engineering

Both
I5
Netlist Extraction and
F3
Bitstream Conversion yield a gate-level netlist consisting of combinational gates (e. g., AND, OR, and XOR gates), sequential logic elements (e. g.,
FF
and latches), and their interconnections.
Such a netlist is often described as a
“sea of gates”

[
3
]
, reflecting the scale and structural complexity of the extracted design.
Netlist reverse engineering aims to interpret this sea of gates by recovering higher-level information and reintroducing abstractions removed during design and manufacturing.
Its goals range from identifying regions of interest (e. g., for locating fault-injection targets)
[
93
]
to fully understanding an implementation, including its algorithms
[
88
]
, e. g., to verify the absence of backdoors in third-party designs
[
122
]
.

3.3.1
Techniques for Netlist Reverse Engineering (RQ1)

Netlist reverse engineering can be broadly organized into four steps:
N1
Partitioning,
N2
Module Identification,
N3
Algorithmic Recovery, and finally
N4
Sensemaking.

N1
Partitioning.

Commonly, the netlist is partitioned by assigning gates to groups that correspond to functional cores, structures such as registers and arithmetic subcircuits, or just categories such as datapath and control path
[
161
]
.

Cores.
Several works suggest that connectivity is denser within a module than across module boundaries.
To this end, Cakir
et al.
cluster gates using eigenvalue decompositions of the adjacency matrix
[
18
,
19
]
, Weber
et al.
apply graph clustering algorithms to gate-level netlists
[
182
]
, and Couch
et al.
use an approximation of the NCut criterion
[
34
]
.
Werner
et al.
employ graph clustering, refined with structural properties, and combined with visual netlist graph inspection
[
184
]
.

Another cue for separating cores is physical proximity: due to routing and timing constraints, related cells are often placed in close proximity.
Such placement information can be used, for example, as input to a
GNN
(
GNN
) that partitions the netlist into clusters
[
72
]
.

Word-Level Structures.
Partitioning can also focus on smaller structures, grouping gates of the same word-level register or (arithmetic) operation.
Physical proximity helps cluster single-bit elements into words
[
131
]
.
Many methods assume that each bit of a datapath word undergoes the same computation, so their surrounding logic should be structurally similar.
Similarity can be measured by comparing fan-in cuts
[
122
,
166
]
, computing graph edit distances over local neighborhoods
[
131
]
, or using BERT-style models
[
204
]
.
To better separate words with similar fan-in structure, Meade
et al.
incorporate additional structural features and apply
PCA
(
PCA
) to embed bits of the same word nearby in a high-dimensional space
[
122
]
.

A complementary heuristic groups gates by shared control signals: since those steered by the same control typically belong to the same word
[
27
,
3
,
166
,
57
]
.
Albartus
et al.
extend this approach by potentially incorporating expected register sizes and examining shared predecessor and successor groups, based on the insight that all bits of a word-level register typically connect to the same surrounding registers and belong to the same register stage
[
3
]
.
Subsequent work refines this by using known word groupings from other analyses
[
88
]
, and leveraging
FPGA
primitives that already expose word-level interfaces
[
119
]
.
The shared successor and predecessor assumption can also be used to propagate known word boundaries, e. g., by symbolically evaluating the combinational logic around an already identified word
[
95
,
162
,
161
]
.

Control Logic.
Many approaches partition circuits into datapath and control path by identifying
FSM
(
FSM
)
FF
that belong to the control path.
Here, the datapath-similarity assumption is inverted:
FF
whose fan-in structure is dissimilar to word-level datapath logic are likely control logic.
This can be done by structural comparison of fan-in cuts
[
120
,
14
]
or by comparing the same cuts at a functional level
[
60
]
.
Another heuristic assumes control logic connects to many parts of the circuit.
Chowdury
et al.
use this assumption and calculate graph centrality metrics as inputs to a
GNN
that classifies
FF
as control or datapath
[
31
]
.
A specialized approach targeting block ciphers analyzes the circuit under induced
FF
faults, assuming faults in control logic produce different effects than those in datapath registers
[
125
]
.

N2
Module Identification.

Next, functionality is assigned either to modules obtained from partitioning or by using an interplay of partitioning and module identification.

Datapath.
Most commonly, datapath functionality is identified by exploiting structural similarities between similar high-level modules within the implemented circuit.
This enables the creation of libraries comprising known templates, which are then matched against unknown (parts of) netlists.
The central challenge is thus measuring similarity between candidate subcircuits and reference implementations.

Early work explored subgraph isomorphism on subcircuits or whole designs
[
27
,
13
]
, but its computational cost motivates alternative comparisons.
These include handcrafted subgraph embeddings combined with classical machine learning
[
7
]
, graph-similarity measures
[
57
]
and embeddings
[
34
]
, and hyper-dimensional graph encoding
[
61
]
.
Other approaches compute node embeddings from local neighborhoods and sample them for machine-learning-based classification
[
55
]
or match nodes individually against a pattern library
[
7
,
155
,
37
]
.
Some techniques use
GNN
to learn node embeddings and predict the operation a node is part of, trained on labeled pattern libraries
[
4
,
16
,
139
,
208
,
67
,
181
]
.
Beyond structural matching, extracted cuts can also be compared functionally to find semantically equivalent matches, independent of their structural implementation
[
151
,
95
,
162
,
161
]
.

Not all methods rely on generic matching.
Several approaches search for domain-specific characteristics, such as
FF
structures for
LFSR
or counters
[
162
,
161
,
180
]
, or carry chains hinting at arithmetic operations
[
88
,
128
]
.
On architectures without dedicated carry primitives, half adders can be reconstructed from XOR patterns
[
160
,
183
]
.
Related techniques reintroduce (half-)adder primitives to ease equivalence checking, using functional bitslice analysis
[
110
]
, rewriting via e-graphs
[
198
]
, or
GNN

[
193
]
.
Similarly, Leander
et al.
exploit the structure of block ciphers to detect cryptographic implementations
[
93
]
.

Another issue is reconstructing the order of subcircuit
IO
(
IO
) signals and mapping them to operands or templates.
For arithmetic subcircuits, this
IO
mapping can be deduced from structural or functional patterns.
A common cue is that the least significant input bits affect many output bits, whereas the most significant bits influence fewer outputs
[
69
,
67
,
181
,
88
,
59
]
.
Other approaches evaluate the circuit on all-zero or one-hot/cold inputs
[
45
,
27
,
151
]
.
Some techniques even use such behavioral signatures to perform module identification and
IO
mapping simultaneously
[
96
,
158
]
.

Given a functional hypothesis for an unknown subcircuit, formal verification is commonly used to confirm equivalence.
Depending on the setting, this includes
SAT
(
SAT
) and
SMT
(

3.3.2
Challenges in Netlist Reverse Engineering (RQ2)

Based on our literature review, we identify four key challenges in current netlist reverse engineering research.

Focus on Early Stages.

Existing work often targets early-stage tasks, particularly partitioning and module identification
[
6
]
.
These stages decompose a netlist into (identified) word-level modules and possibly even larger functional blocks.
However, many important applications, such as vulnerability discovery, require insights beyond the word level.
A holistic design understanding is only achieved through algorithmic recovery and higher-level sensemaking.
In turn, these processes require interpreting datapath and control logic in conjunction, often by introducing additional abstractions.
These later stages remain largely unexplored, with a few exceptions suggesting that dynamic analysis is a promising research direction
[
88
,
146
]
.
We argue that efforts to improve its scalability, together with developing complementary tools, could advance netlist reverse engineering.

Hurdles for Automation.

Manual netlist reverse engineering is highly time-consuming, making automation essential for practical and economic viability
[
57
,
93
]
.
While many recent works propose algorithms for individual subproblems, end-to-end automation remains elusive.
Isolated solutions are often difficult to combine: they may be non-interoperable, rely on incompatible assumptions (e. g., specific architectures or design layouts), or require idealized input from preceding steps.
Further, many approaches depend on hand-tuned parameters or thresholds, fundamentally limiting full automation
[
6
,
131
,
31
,
122
,
14
,
83
]
.
Effective integration also requires standardized interfaces and data formats for exchanging intermediate results, and ideally a common framework.
While such frameworks exist
[
179
,
157
]
, adoption is limited.
Even with a unified toolchain, human experts must interpret and validate intermediate results
[
27
,
161
,
88
]
, highlighting the need for tools that efficiently integrate analyst expertise and for further study of how analysts perform these tasks
[
56
,
185
]
.

Overly Idealized Assumptions.

Many proposed algorithms rely on assumptions that rarely hold in practice.
Almost all assume error-free netlists as input
[
27
,
184
,
7
,
71
,
57
,
142
]
, which is unrealistic given the error-prone netlist recovery process for
IC
.
Some module identification approaches further assume perfect partitioning into (yet unidentified) modules
[
161
]
or a known
IO
mapping from library candidates to such unidentified modules
[
66
,
27
,
59
,
82
]
, both of which are challenging and error-prone tasks on their own.
Other algorithms are evaluated either on unoptimized netlists—e. g., without logic merging across module boundaries
[
4
]
—or on task-specific netlists that only include arithmetic components, sidestepping cases that break their assumptions and can trigger false detections or excessive runtimes
[
69
,
105
]
.
Several control-logic identification methods assume that
FSM
form a small, separable
SCC
, yet real designs often collapse control and datapath into a single large
SCC
due to feedback loops
[
88
]
.
Together, these assumptions make it difficult to assess algorithm effectiveness under realistic conditions.

Barriers to Cumulative Progress.

Several core challenges in netlist reverse engineering—some noted as early as 1999
[
27
]
—remain central in recent work
[
88
]
, and many solution strategies show substantial overlap.
While individual techniques have evolved, overall progress appears limited.

During our research, we rarely observed techniques building upon one another, or even just quantitatively comparing themselves to previous work.
Hence, we assume that the observed stagnation in netlist reverse engineering progress is largely driven by poor reusability and comparability of existing work.
Moreover, we found that methodological descriptions are often insufficient to reproduce results or validate claims, preventing cumulative and incremental research and forcing authors to repeatedly re-address known problems.
The field also lacks widely adopted tools
[
88
]
and standardized benchmarks
[
121
,
88
]
, making fair comparison across approaches difficult and obscuring genuine progress.

Several factors contribute to this situation.
Legal constraints, such as export regulations, licensing agreements, or funding agency restrictions, may prevent artifact release.
Restrictive publication page limits further impede detailed methodological reporting: 50 of 77 papers span 8 pages or fewer.
Even with better descriptions, shared tools, and public benchmarks, the absence of agreed-upon ground truth definitions and evaluation metrics remains a fundamental challenge, given the diversity of reverse engineering goals
[
6
,
122
,
69
]
.

Evaluation of
HRE
Artifacts (RQ3)

Building on the challenges identified in
Section 3

HRE
publications.
Out of the 187 papers, 31 (
17\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
) report providing publicly available artifacts.
As two papers reference the same artifact, this corresponds to 30 unique artifacts.
Despite a recent increase in artifact publication—
23\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
for papers published between 2021 and 2025 compared to
9\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
between 1986 and 2020—the overall integration of artifacts into
HRE
research remains limited.
We summarize our findings in
Table 4
.
Detailed per-artifact evaluation notes, including documented evaluation steps, encountered issues, and mitigation attempts, are available as part of our open-science materials.

Availability.

Of the 30 artifacts identified in our corpus, 24 were fully available, one was partially available, and five were inaccessible.
Permanent storage was used infrequently, appearing only 6 times; however, its first use in 2023 may indicate a shift toward more durable artifact hosting.
This trend is encouraging, as three artifacts hosted on non-permanent websites are no longer accessible.
While 23 artifacts were stored using a single method, either permanent storage, non-permanent storage, or access upon request, two artifacts were distributed across multiple storage types.
One of these relied on non-permanent hosting and on-request access, but no response was received when access was requested.

Functionality.

Across the 25 available artifacts, quality ranged from no usable guidance (2 artifacts with no or non-applicable instructions) to major documentation gaps (4 artifacts, e. g., with hard-coded absolute paths, no execution instructions, or a missing dependency list).
The remaining 19 artifacts described a workable
“path to first success,”
with only minor friction such as adding a missing package, a build flag, or adjusting a configuration or file path.
In terms of completeness, all artifacts were fully delivered, except for one, where part of the artifact had to be requested, but was not provided.
Regarding exercisability, our evaluation of the 20 available tools showed that all were executable, but only 6 could be executed without issues.
For 14 artifacts, execution typically required adjustments to the setup—often due to incomplete or unclear documentation—resulting in a trial-and-error approach.
All encountered issues, along with the corresponding resolution strategies, are documented for each artifact individually in our open science materials.
Given our best-effort evaluation without author support, these results are a good approximation of the artifacts’ real-world usability.

Reproducibility.

Of 18 papers with available artifacts that include concrete, testable results and provide the corresponding executable tools, we could reproduce some key results of seven papers.
The remaining attempts failed primarily due to missing evaluation materials (e. g., required binaries, netlists, bitstreams,
IC
images, or labeled datasets) or because the tools necessary for reproducing the results were not provided (e. g., for computing the error rate between segmented images and labeled data, or for calculating evaluation scores).
While we acknowledge that not all papers in our corpus contain verifiable claims, we consider the number of 7 reproducible papers (
4\text{\,}\mathrm{\%}
) out of 187 to be exceptionally low.

5
Opportunities for Scalable
HRE
Research

Collectively, the findings reveal substantial overlap in obstacles across
IC
,
FPGA
, and netlist reverse engineering.
Mitigating three systemic, cross-cutting challenges would significantly reduce the difficulty of addressing the localized technical problems identified in RQ2:
(i) limited reproducibility and reusability due to the scarcity of shared artifacts and usable tooling,
(ii) limited comparability due to the absence of standardized benchmarks, ground truth, and evaluation metrics, and
(iii) limited legal clarity and clearance, which constrain data sharing and collaboration.
Together, they present clear opportunities for enabling more rigorous, cumulative, and scalable
HRE
research.
We structure our recommendations around these three opportunities and outline stakeholder-specific actions to address each challenge.

Opportunity I: Value Reproducibility and Reusability.

Many contributions remain difficult to reproduce and build upon: code is unpublished or fragile, and pipelines are bespoke.
Consequently, technical approaches are often incomplete and localized, limiting generalizability.
We attribute this to structural factors, including missing academic incentives, limited awareness, insufficient industry cooperation, and legal constraints on publication.
This situation is particularly harmful in
HRE
, where intermediate results naturally feed later pipeline stages.

HRE
lacks broadly adopted, end-to-end community frameworks into which new methods can be integrated, comparable to the rich open-source ecosystem in
SRE
(
SRE
) with tools like Ghidra.
Early promising efforts, such as the Degate
IC
reverse engineering suite, have largely stagnated
[
41
]
.
Meanwhile,
FPGA
-focused tools like Project X-Ray
[
54
]
remain limited to a narrow device family.
For netlist reverse engineering, HAL
[
58
]
represents a notable exception and has gained some traction
[
159
]
, yet it still lacks widespread adoption.

Venues and Academic Incentives.

Improving reproducibility and reuse requires aligning community incentives with artifact-centric research practices.
While USENIX Security has demonstrated effective mechanisms through mandatory artifact evaluation, artifact publication and long-term archival should become the default for all
HRE
-relevant venues, with explicit exceptions.
The community should also explicitly reward engineering work through tool and infrastructure tracks and through mechanisms promoting long-term maintenance, e. g., recognition for impactful and living artifacts
[
1
]
.
These priorities should extend beyond venues and be reflected in Ph.D. program design, hiring, and funding decisions, treating tools and curated datasets as first-class research outputs.

Individual Researchers.

Within such incentive-aligned environments, researchers should select venues whose page limits do not impede reporting methodological details sufficient for replication.
When publishing artifacts in—and beyond—
HRE
, researchers should package pipelines in containers or virtual machines and pin dependency versions to ensure long-term reproducibility.
Methods should be implemented as modular components with clear interfaces and adapters to shared frameworks wherever possible, enabling reuse beyond a single paper.
Furthermore, all inputs and parameters used to generate published results should be clearly documented.

Industry and Funders.

Industry can accelerate reproducibility by contributing engineering time to extend and maintain open-source
HRE
tooling.
Funders of academic research projects should consider supporting multi-year maintenance grants for open
HRE
infrastructure that focus on community-available tooling rather than purely on algorithmic novelty.

Opportunity II: Ensure Rigorous Comparability.

Even when authors publish functional and exercisable algorithms, progress is often hard to judge: papers evaluate on incomparable targets, rely on private datasets, and report metrics that do not translate across methods.
A major reason for this is the lack of community-wide benchmarks, which prevents consistent, meaningful comparison.
To serve as dependable, shared evaluation targets, future benchmarks should (i) be legally cleared, (ii) provide explicit ground truth where possible, and (iii) use agreed-upon metrics tied to practical utility.
Examples include:
IC
images paired with
GDSII
and netlists;
FPGA
bitstreams paired with original netlists; and netlists paired with gate-level labels, modular structure,
RTL
(where feasible), and design specifications.
Because hardware manufacturing and the threats addressed by defensive
HRE
evolve rapidly, benchmarks must be treated as living infrastructure with versioning and long-term curation.

Venues and Community Norms.

Venues should recognize benchmark creation and maintenance as an integral part of research impact.
Especially for widely cited claims, replication and re-evaluation on contemporary technologies should be encouraged.
Inspiration for organizing such efforts may be drawn from fields such as machine learning, where sustained, community-driven benchmark projects have become central to progress
[
73
,
20
]
.
For benchmark creation in
HRE
, initiatives such as ICCAD’s annual CAD Contest
[
75
]
, which has included netlist reverse engineering problems in past editions, could serve as a valuable starting point.
Existing efforts like TrustHUB
[
174
]
provide centralized dataset hosting, but broader community engagement and sustained curation appear limited
[
91
]
, highlighting the need for clear governance, reliable ground truth, and ongoing maintenance.

Individual Researchers.

Researchers should provide evaluation scripts and metric implementations and, where benchmarks exist, evaluate their tools and techniques on those shared targets using multiple metrics.
To support reproducibility and reuse, publications should explicitly state assumptions, describe limitations, and highlight potential failure scenarios.

Industry.

Creating and maintaining realistic benchmarks—especially for
IC
and netlist reverse engineering—often exceeds what academic groups can achieve.
Industry is uniquely positioned to supply legally cleared reference designs with verified ground truth and support long-term curation, including updates, governance, and documentation.
Such challenge datasets, reflecting modern constraints, can accelerate research into supply chain security and defensive
HRE
.

Opportunity III: Provide Legal Clearance and Clarity.

Legal constraints and uncertainties are a structural inhibitor of
HRE
: they limit dataset and tool distribution, and lead to target and workflow descriptions that are insufficient for replication.
Constraints arise from licensing terms, non-disclosure agreements, and export-control regulations.
For instance, open-source
IC
reverse engineering frameworks may be classified as dual-use technologies under
EU
(
EU
) export regulations, requiring explicit export approval
[
53
,
52
]
.

IP
concerns introduce further uncertainty about which results may be published.
We argue that such restrictions are increasingly misaligned with contemporary hardware security needs.
For instance, this misalignment manifests in workforce development: unlike
SRE
,
HRE
lacks a broad talent pipeline due to the high barrier to entry
[
178
]
.
Public research and education are therefore among the few viable entry points for training practitioners, necessitating appropriate legal protection.

Venues and Institutions.

To reduce legal uncertainty for individual researchers, venues and institutions should provide clear guidance on legally constrained artifacts, including recommended sanitization techniques and minimum disclosure requirements.
Well-resourced institutions could also support researchers by offering legal consultation, handling approvals, or negotiating exemptions where possible, thereby enabling broader participation in
HRE
research.

Industry.

Industry can reduce friction without compromising
IP
by offering research-friendly licensing terms for benchmark use and publication.

Government and Regulators.

Governments should reassess legal barriers and export restrictions that disproportionately impede
HRE
, support broad access to publicly funded research outputs, and fund public-private mechanisms for legally sound sharing and long-term infrastructure maintenance.
While both
HRE
and
SRE
involve dual-use technology, the
SRE
ecosystem demonstrates that government-backed open-source tools can flourish (e. g., Ghidra).
A key goal is to avoid concentrating
HRE
capability within a small set of privileged actors, which would reduce transparency and weaken independent verification.

6
Conclusion

This work systematizes over two decades of peer-reviewed
HRE
(
HRE
) research, consolidating fragmented techniques and terminology across 187 publications spanning
IC
,
FPGA
, and netlist reverse engineering.
We provide a shared reference for the community, offering an overview of current
HRE
practices that enable critical security applications, including design verification, supply-chain assurance, and vulnerability discovery.
Our analysis also reveals a concerning pattern: despite substantial technical advances, progress often remains difficult to reproduce or build upon.
With only 4% of studies providing reproducible artifacts, much of the field continues to rely on effectively opaque implementations rather than shared progress.

To address persisting technical and organizational challenges in the field, we suggest coordinated action: academic venues and programs should reward artifact creation and maintenance, individual researchers should publish reusable and well-documented methods, industry should provide legally cleared reference designs and maintain standardized benchmarks, and regulators should reassess legal barriers that impede open research.
By tackling both technical and systemic challenges,
HRE
can evolve into a rigorous, reproducible, and scalable discipline that strengthens hardware trust, verification, and supply-chain security.

Ethical Considerations

Our work constitutes a systematization and critical reflection on existing publicly available research.
It does not propose new reverse-engineering techniques, disclose novel vulnerabilities, or lower the barrier to conducting attacks beyond what is already described in the surveyed literature.
The study involves no human participants and does not collect, process, or analyze personal data.
Throughout this work, we respect the rights of authors and publishers of the analyzed publications and comply with applicable copyright and licensing requirements.
Given the descriptive and reflective nature of our contribution, we believe that this work raises no material ethical concerns that would preclude its publication.

Nonetheless, we acknowledge the dual
-
use nature of
HRE
(
HRE
) itself.
It can support legitimate goals such as vulnerability discovery, design verification, supply-chain assurance, and patent infringement analysis, but may also be abused to extract proprietary design information or facilitate hardware-level attacks.
In the following, we provide a stakeholder analysis of
HRE
, discussing each stakeholder’s interests in
HRE
and how they may be affected.

Chip Designers
could use
HRE
to verify that their
IC
designs and schematics are correctly implemented and were not maliciously modified during manufacturing.
Furthermore, they can use
HRE
to detect
IP
infringements by other vendors or to perform competitor analysis.
At the same time, chip designers may be negatively affected if adversaries use
HRE
to extract their proprietary designs to conduct
IP
theft or to insert hardware Trojans during manufacturing.

Manufacturers
such as foundries and their fabs can use
HRE
to verify third-party
IP
blocks or to debug complex manufacturing issues.
However, manufacturers may also abuse
HRE
to extract
IP
from customers or competitors, or to introduce malicious modifications during fabrication.
Conversely, manufacturers may themselves be harmed if their proprietary manufacturing process or
IP
are reverse engineered by others.

System integrators
, who purchase chips to incorporate them into larger systems, may rely on
HRE
to verify the trustworthiness of third-party components, particularly in safety- or security-critical applications.
This can help identify counterfeit, vulnerable, or trojanized hardware.

Government agencies
, including intelligence, military, and law enforcement organizations, may apply
HRE
in both offensive and defensive contexts: to extract data or exploit hardware vulnerabilities, assess adversarial systems, verify the integrity of critical components, or evaluate proprietary technology in the context of national security.
Because of the prohibitive cost of some
HRE
steps, particularly those related to
IC
sample preparation and imaging, state-level actors likely possess the most advanced capabilities in practice.
Increased open research in
HRE
and public availability of tools may reduce the exclusivity of such capabilities.
Given that agencies often seek to keep such capabilities secret to maintain a strategic advantage, they may attempt to limit or counteract open research and public dissemination of
HRE
tools.

Academic researchers
engaged in
HRE
aim to understand attacker capabilities, uncover vulnerabilities, and develop verification and assurance techniques.
Open publication of such research can strengthen security by enabling independent scrutiny and reproducibility.
However, academic researchers may face legal, contractual, or political pressures that constrain disclosure, limit access to hardware, or discourage publication due to national security or
IP
infringement concerns.

Regulators and certification bodies
, such as standardization organizations and lawmakers, have a strong interest in
HRE
to assess the safety, security, and compliance of hardware used in critical infrastructure and government domains.
In practice,
HRE
supports independent certification and helps enforce trust and accountability across global supply chains.

The general public
—as end users of hardware and beneficiaries of critical digital infrastructure—is indirectly but significantly affected by
HRE
.
Undisclosed vulnerabilities, counterfeit components, or implanted hardware Trojans can undermine safety, privacy, and trust.
At the same time, responsible and transparent
HRE
research can improve long-term security and resilience by enabling earlier detection of risks.

Overall, ethical
HRE
research requires balancing openness with legitimate state and commercial interests, recognizing its dual-use nature, and considering how publication would affect commercial actors, governments, and the public.
We believe that carefully conducted public
HRE
research is essential for improving hardware security, provided that ethical risks are explicitly acknowledged and appropriately mitigated.
