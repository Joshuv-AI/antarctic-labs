Driven by climate change, sudden swings between wet and dry create “hydrologic whiplash”
Authors: Amy McDermott
Published: 2026

On Christmas Day 2022, most of California was in serious drought. Reservoirs were critically low, and crops failed as water bills rose. But when it finally started raining on December 26, the region quickly faced the opposite problem. Atmospheric rivers rolled in and delivered a deluge. These streams of water vapor ride the wind and dump rain and snow when they bump up against the mountains. Over just three weeks, nine strong atmospheric rivers flowed into the coast—essentially an entire season’s worth—causing flooding and other damage that led to 22 fatalities and an estimated $5 billion to $7 billion in economic losses (1, 2).

“That’s an iconic example of going from super dry to super wet in a short period,” says meteorologist Marty Ralph at the University of California, San Diego (UCSD), who has studied the incident.

Historically, researchers have studied natural hazards like droughts or floods as separate events, says John Hammond, a research hydrologist with the US Geological Survey in Baltimore, Maryland. But increasingly, weather extremes are happening back-to-back, potentially amplifying their collective impacts. Hammond and others have argued that scientists should widen their focus, studying not only periods of acute wet or dry conditions, but also the sudden swings between them, a phenomenon he calls “hydrologic whiplash.”

Most urgently, these swings complicate water management. Storage basins could overflow or dry out—plunging downstream communities into floods or shortages—if reservoir operators don’t plan for whiplash events. Today’s drought and flood forecasting models don’t anticipate whiplashes well, Hammond says, because they’re trained on historical data, when such rapid fluctuations were rarer. So understanding these transitions is vital to help water managers foresee flips from very wet or very dry in quick succession.

Hydrologic whiplash is a relatively new term; other labels for rapid swings in metrics like temperature, rainfall, and streamflow include “weather whiplash,” “climate whiplash,” and “hydrological volatility.” While not exactly synonymous, these terms all point to similar patterns: sudden swings from one set of extreme conditions to another, along with the dire consequences they can have. Stream flow data, as far back as the 1990s, showed occasional dramatic shifts from drought to flood, or vice versa. Now, whiplash events are happening much more often than they were 30 years ago, and they’re expected to accelerate in the future.

Water management works something like a checking account: Water inflows are positive, and water outflows are negative. “You try to keep a balance,” Hammond explains. “But if you have these unexpected events happening over and over, you might keep dipping into that checking until you have nothing left.”

A New Vocabulary

The increasing use of “whiplash” and similar terms is revealing in itself, notes Daniel Swain, a prominent climate scientist at the California Institute for Water Resources at the University of California. “It’s a paradigm shift,” he says, noting that researchers are paying more attention to the transitions between climate extremes.

In 2025, Swain wrote a widely cited review (4), asking if atmospheric data show a rise in sudden, dramatic swings between wet and dry, a phenomenon he termed “hydroclimate volatility.” To qualify as a dramatic swing, a region’s precipitation and potential evaporation had to exceed a once-in-10-years deviation from the local baseline. Quick swings could happen over weeks to months, sometimes taking up to a year. Swain’s answer—based on 50 years of global hydroclimate data and dozens of runs of a climate model—was a resounding yes. Globally, whiplash events were about 7.6% more common in 2015 than in 1975, at the start of the study period. And on a sub-seasonal basis, whiplashes occurring within a three-month timeframe were about 31% more common than in 1975.

Swain’s next question: “Is this something we should have expected from first principles physics?” The answer, again, was yes. A key equation in physical chemistry explains it: the Clausius–Clapeyron relation, which describes that air’s ability to hold water vapor rises exponentially with temperature (see https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2304077120). It shows that there’s an approximately 4% increase in air’s capacity to hold water per degree Fahrenheit of warming. “Essentially, the warmer the air, the thirstier the air,” Swain says. This turns a warming atmosphere into a bigger sponge.

Now, because of greenhouse gas emissions, virtually every place on Earth is heating up, meaning that the air holds more water. In humid periods, when the air is loaded with water, that means more rain. And in dry periods, when warm air isn’t saturated at 100% humidity, it’s thirstier to suck water out of the environment, which can lead to drier dry periods and more drought. “So anywhere with a little water to evaporate, it will evaporate faster,” Swain says, including out of soils, plants, and reservoirs. That can lead to extreme dry periods, causing drought and wildfire.

“Climate change is doing this because virtually every corner of the Earth is warming,” Swain says. That whiplash is expected to be more frequent, and more severe, in places that warm faster.

The Gathering Storm

Hundreds of stream gauges sticking out of lakes and rivers tell a similar story. Based on daily and even hourly water level measurements, whiplashes are “measurable and widespread across the US,” says Hesham El-Askary, an environmental physicist at Chapman University in Southern California. He coauthored a study last year (5) quantifying the number of whiplashes between 1981 and 2023 detected across 469 western US stream gauges.

Key gauges include those in the Colorado River, the Columbia River, and streams around the Great Basin and California River Basin. El-Askary found that 44% of monitoring sites had experienced at least one whiplash of dry-to-wet within two consecutive years during that period. Another 23% experienced a whiplash of wet-to-dry. The study offers a baseline to compare against future anomalous stream flows, to help anticipate seasonal water availability. “This is the challenge,” El-Askary says. “How on Earth can water managers manage? How will they be prepared?”

Thomas Piechota, a water resources engineer at Chapman, collaborated with El-Askary on two other papers in 2024 and 2025 outlining the challenges that these extremes pose for water management (6, 7). One major issue, Piechota says, is that reservoirs can go from almost dry to overflowing within a single year during these whiplash events.

Four decades ago, whiplashes only happened every third or fourth year in California. They’re happening more frequently now; about 50% of the years that Piechota and El-Askary studied were whiplash years. Their work also suggests that the trend will continue, with 60% of future years seeing some kind of extreme drought or flood by 2061.

Not all studies concur. In a recent preprint, which has not yet been peer reviewed, a research team from Princeton and Nanjing University saw declines in whiplashes when they studied daily mean discharge data from more than 2,000 streamflow stations across the contiguous United States between 1951 and 2024 (8). However, that may be due to a buffering effect, caused by groundwater and soil moisture, Hammond says. If groundwater tables are low in some areas, heavy rains would need to top them up before researchers saw any aboveground rise in creek or stream water levels. And on the other hand, if soil moisture and groundwater levels have been high, he says, you wouldn’t see a rapid transition from wet to dry. “If what’s happening in the atmosphere keeps on happening,” Hammond says, “then we might see more [whiplash] within streamflow.”

Thinking Clearly

While researchers try to better understand and forecast whiplash events, reservoir managers are already facing difficult choices. “Basically, these reservoirs have to figure out supply and demand,” says Darren Ficklin, a hydrologist at Indiana University in Bloomington. During the heaviest storms, they’ll need to release some water to avoid overflowing and flooding. But they can’t release too much, or they’ll risk water shortages if a drought follows the storm (9). “They’re faced with a tough task,” he says. Improving weather forecasts would help to decide when to release water, he adds. Ralph at UCSD is doing just that. He founded and directs the Center for Western Weather and Water Extremes (CW3E), where researchers work to improve extreme weather forecasting. One area of interest is atmospheric rivers, which cause roughly 90% (10) of flooding damage on the West Coast—just as they did during those California storms in 2022 and 2023 after prolonged drought.

Predicting the path of atmospheric rivers, and where exactly they’ll stall over the coast, has been a historical blind spot (see https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.1607656113). So when Ralph established the center 12 years ago, he began a program making dozens of flights over the Pacific Ocean every winter, to measure the size and direction of nascent atmospheric rivers, as they form out past Hawaii. Hurricane hunter aircraft, which normally monitor tropical Atlantic storms, collect data about the size and trajectory of coalescing Pacific atmospheric rivers. The data inform forecast models to predict when and where atmospheric rivers will hit the West Coast and how much precipitation they’re carrying.

Those improved forecasts fit into a broader strategy called Forecast Informed Reservoir Operations (FIRO). Researchers, engineers, and reservoir operators are developing FIRO to help them decide when to keep or release water from dams, based on the forecast for the next few days. Most reservoirs today aren’t that agile, Ralph says. But as forecasts of extreme precipitation and flooding from atmospheric rivers have improved, some dams in California are using FIRO methods.

During the 2022–23 deluge in California, these improved forecasts may have saved lives. Flood-prone communities, for instance along the Russian River, still saw some minor flooding, but it wasn’t compounded into a disaster by the dam overflowing upstream. The state government of California also readied and positioned emergency workers based on the forecasts.

Beyond Water

So far, the whiplash conversation has centered on water management. Hammond and others are now exploring whether that definition should be expanded to capture impacts to ecosystems. One such impact occurred in the Washington, DC, area in 2018, which experienced an unusually wet winter, followed by a dry spring. “There were reports from the northern side of DC,” Hammond says, “of all these oaks dying around a similar time.” Hundreds of majestic old trees and branches collapsed, ostensibly because sudden swings between wet and dry can abruptly shift tree growth (11–13).

Lauren Lowman, a hydrologist at Wake Forest University in North Carolina, is studying quick shifts in vegetation that are associated with weather whiplash. A typical example: when plants turn green during an early wet spring but then face hot or dry spells and drop their new leaves. She wants to know how often that’s happening now and if temperature or precipitation extremes are more impactful.

Based on unpublished results from global satellite data, Lowman recently found that changes in vegetation density more closely correlate with changes in temperature than in precipitation. She presented the work at the American Geophysical Union annual conference last December (14). In the same talk, she also identified global hotspots of rapid vegetation impacts in South America and Southeast Asia, where climate warming is also accelerating. Losses in canopy cover would make local environments hotter and drier, in a feedback loop that forest and climate models should account for, she says. “The changes are not uniform globally,” Lowman notes. “We see savannas moving towards grassland in some regions, and towards forests in other regions.”

Ecological work on climate whiplash is in its infancy, and researchers will need a lot more data if they hope to inform environmental management practices. Richard Turcotte, a forest health group leader with the US Department of Agriculture’s Forest Service in Morgantown, West Virginia, is not yet convinced that climate whiplash killed the trees around Washington, DC. He responded to reports of dead, fallen trees in the winter of 2018, especially along riverbanks. “We felt comfortable it was flooding, and that’s where it ended for us,” he says. Whiplash hasn’t been on his radar and certainly isn’t something that the Forest Service invokes to explain die-offs today. They haven’t codified it as a damage-causing agent.

If more research suggests that whiplashes do affect forest health, then analyzing soil moisture probes and long-term changes in vegetation could be used to track impacts, Turcotte says. He’d also like to investigate if these swings affect invasive plants, whose seeds can spread through flooding.

For now, though, the immediate impacts of hydrologic whiplash are already clear, Hammond says. And as the climate heats up, the combined effects of drought and flood are likely to cause broader damage: “Whether that’s destruction to property due to flooding, followed up by poor returns from crops in the same county,” Hammond says, “it puts more of an economic stress on the same area, especially if you have those things happen every few years.”
