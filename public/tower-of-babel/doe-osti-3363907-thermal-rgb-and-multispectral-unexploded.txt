Executive Summary
This document provides a description of a data collection campaign of UXO set. The dataset captures a controlled UAV imaging
campaign designed to support detection of inert unexploded ordnance (UXO) across varied environmental conditions. Data were
collected during three campaigns in Norris and Georgetown, Tennessee, using RGB, thermal, and limited multispectral sensors
mounted on Skydio X10 and Wingtra platforms. In total, the dataset contains 23,004 images, approximately 76.2 GB of data,
collected across summer and winter conditions, every hour during sunlight, and multiple surface contexts, including tall grass, short
grass, and gravel.

The collection was designed to capture thermal and visual variability relevant to UXO detection in agricultural land. Review of the
imagery showed that ordnance was most detectable during periods of changing solar input, especially approximately 10-60 minutes
after sunrise, approximately 20-60 minutes after sunset, and 2-3 min after cloud cover interrupted prolonged solar heating. These
conditions increased thermal contrast because many ordnance items retained or released heat differently than the surrounding
vegetation and ground surface.

This dataset provides a useful resource for developing and evaluating airborne UXO detection methods under realistic field
conditions. All ordnance used in the study was inert, and thermal behavior may differ from that of live ordnance. In addition,
variation in ordnance type, composition, and placement introduced differences in thermal response that should be considered when
interpreting results.

Background
As of May 1, 2026 Landmine contamination affects 58 countries and many hundreds of thousands of km2 of land. For example, the
National Mine Action Program Demining Ukraine reporting that up to 144,000 km² of territory are potentially contaminated and
require survey and clearance (National Mine Action Program “Demining Ukraine,” n.d.) alone. This contamination includes mines
and other explosive remnants of war and continues to constrain civilian access, agricultural use, infrastructure recovery, and broader
socioeconomic activity (International Campaign to Ban Landmines–Cluster Munition Coalition [ICBL-CMC], 2024; Mine Action
Review, 2024). Current response activities rely on established mine-action approaches including non-technical survey, technical
survey, clearance, and explosive ordnance disposal, consistent with international mine-action terminology and operational practice
(United Nations Mine Action Service [UNMAS], 2024; Geneva International Centre for Humanitarian Demining [GICHD], 2023). In this
context, UAV-based sensing, including UAV-mounted thermal imaging, may provide a useful supplementary capability by supporting
faster, safer detection and mapping of suspect hazards prior to ground intervention (Smiljanic, 2022).

Experimental Design and Data Collection
Data were collected during three separate campaigns: two in Norris, Tennessee, and one in Georgetown, Tennessee. Each Norris
campaign was conducted over two days, while the Georgetown campaign was completed in a single day. The timing of the Norris
campaigns was planned to capture variability in temperature and weather conditions, specifically representing summer and winter
environments. The Georgetown campaign was ancillary and was conducted to introduce additional variability into a training dataset.

During the Norris campaigns, ~31 items of ordnance were placed during the first campaign and 21 during the second. Most inert
ordnance was positioned in taller vegetation and marked with red survey flags, with placement guided by Explosive Ordnance
Disposal (EOD) specialists from the U.S. Army’s 52nd EOD Group at Fort Campbell, Kentucky. This strategic placement was intended
to emulate how UXO might be encountered in real-world environments. All training ordnance placed in taller grass was marked with
red survey flags. Additional inert ordnance was also placed in areas of shorter grass. Due to material availability, hourly ordnance
captures were collected only during the first data collection campaign. Inert ordnance was placed in the mid-afternoon, followed by
test flights and equipment checks.

For each campaign, flights were then conducted on day 1, from ~3 PM every hour until sunset. Followed by hourly flights from
sunrise to ~3 PM the following day. Flights were programmed at ~33 m to obtain a resolution of ~2.6 cm/pixel. Furthermore, to aid
in the creation of orthomosaics, flights were programmed to have between 80-85 front and side overlap. This flight schedule was
designed to capture a full diurnal cycle, from sunrise to sunset, within the scheduling constraints of the 52nd EOD Group. Although
the schedule was not ideal, similar weather and temperature conditions across both days of each campaign allowed us to capture
thermal variability between the ordnance and the ground surface.

The Georgetown campaign was conducted over the period of ~5 hours with only 5 inert pieces. However, three flights were
conducted each hour, and the inert ordnance was systematically repositioned from the south field to the gravel road, and then to
the north field. This design was intended to maximize variability despite the limited number of materials available and to capture a
broad range of conditions between 10:00 AM and 2:20 PM.

RGB, and thermal imagery was captured during each flight for each campaign. Multispectral imagery was captured over the course
of three flights only during the September Norris collection campaign.

This data collection campaign was undergone in two sperate fields field near East Tennessee. Two collections were conducted in
Norris, TN and one in Georgetown. Either Norris or Georgetown was chosen on specific days based on availability of UXO and
proximity to material suppliers. The Norris collection site consisted of a freshly cut (~1 km2) and unkept field grass (~5 km2). Unkept
portions ranged in height from ~1-1.5 meters while the manicured portions were ~3-5 cm. The grasses present in the area consisted
of Verbesina occidentalis (i.e., Yellow Cornbeard), Asprera spica-veni (i.e., Loose Silky-bent), and Bromus diandrus (i.e., Broncho
grass). These grasses displayed typical bimodal phenological behavior based on whether the data were collected in warmer months
versus winter conditions. Vegetation was similar at both collection sites.

The collection site in Georgetown consisted of a 1.5 km2 area of similar vegetation species and bisected in the approximate center by
a gravel road. Grasses at this site range from ~0.5-1 m in height.

The collection in Georgetown took place on Dec 9. In Norris, Tennessee, on Thursday, September 18, 2025, sunrise was at 7:21 AM
and sunset was at 7:38 PM. The weather was mostly clear and dry, with temperatures ranging from about 59°F to 85°F. On Friday,
September 19, 2025, sunrise was at 7:22 AM and sunset was at 7:37 PM. The weather was mostly clear, becoming somewhat
cloudier later in the day, with temperatures ranging from about 59°F to 87°F. On Tuesday, January 20, 2026, sunrise was at 7:44 AM

Wednesday, January 21, 2026, sunrise was at 7:44 AM and sunset was at 5:51 PM. The weather became increasingly cloudy, with
light rain in the evening, and temperatures ranged from about 18°F to 58°F.In Georgetown, Tennessee, on Tuesday, December 9,
2025, sunrise was at 7:36 AM and sunset was at 5:27 PM. The weather was clear and cold, with temperatures ranging from about

Aircraft and Sensor Payloads

Flights were conducted with two aircraft, a Skydio X10 (https://www.skydio.com/x10) equipped with thermal and RGB sensors for
simultaneous image captures and a Wingtra (https://wingtra.com/ ) equipped with a MicaSense Red-Edge
(https://wingtra.com/mapping-drone-wingtraone/drone-sensors/micasense-rededge-p/ )multispectral sensor.

The Skydio X10 sensor package integrates high-resolution RGB imaging with radiometric thermal sensing payload. Its visible-
spectrum cameras include a 50.3 MP wide camera, a 64 MP narrow camera, and a 48 MP telephoto camera, while the thermal
sensor is a FLIR Boson+ uncooled VOx microbolometer (640 × 512) with thermal sensitivity below 30 mK NEDT. In combination, these
sensors support co-registered collection of high-detail visual imagery and temperature-resolved thermal data.

A MicaSense multispectral camera is designed to acquire narrowband reflectance data for quantitative remote-sensing analyses,
typically capturing the blue, green, red, red-edge, and near-infrared portions of the spectrum. These sensors are intended for
radiometrically calibrated image collection and are commonly used to derive vegetation indices, spectral composites, and other
biophysical products.

Inert Ordnance

Inert UXO were donated on loan for experimental use by several sources: private collectors donated several US made landmines,
grenades, and artillery pieces, plus a modern German Panzerfaust. The bulk of the materials were brought to our flight location by
two members of the US Army, 52nd EOD Group out of Ft. Campbell, Kentucky, which included 21 Soviet/RU type landmines, artillery,
guided rockets, submunitions, and missiles. The combined inert collection consisted in anti-personnel mines, anti-tank mines, guided
rockets, cluster munitions, hand grenades, claymores, mortar shells, and tank artillery round ejecta.

Description of Data
This dataset consists of a combined total of 23,004 images (RGB, Thermal, and Multispectral), totaling approximately 76.2 GB

The folder names follow a structured, timestamped convention in which each name begins with a session or collection identifier and
ends with a filesystem-safe ISO-style datetime stamp. In general, the format is [session label]__[timestamp], where the
label encodes the dataset, mission, run, or acquisition context, and the timestamp records the date and time of capture or export in
a sortable form. The prefixes often use short descriptors such as EOD, numbered run labels like with contextual tags such as
Flight and Sunset, which distinguish unique collection periods or operating conditions. The timestamp portion used a modified
ISO 8601 format with year, month, day, time, fractional seconds, and UTC offset, written with hyphens instead of colons to remain
filename-safe while still preserving chronological order. Overall, the convention was designed to make folders both human-readable
and easy to sort programmatically by session identity and acquisition time. Each Skydio frame captured one RGB image, and two
thermal images (i.e., .jpg, and .r.jpg).

Observations and Caveats
Observations

Over the course of these collection campaigns and manual inspection of imagery, several operational practices became apparent
and were validated. Inspection of thermal imagery revealed that ordnance was most easily detectable when environmental
conditions allowed thermal equilibrium to occur, followed by the addition or removal of direct sunlight. These environmental
changes occurred during three types of events: (1) approximately 30-60 minutes after sunrise, (2) approximately 30-60 minutes after
sunset, and (3) after prolonged periods of sunshine followed by cloud cover that blocked solar input. Event 3 produced the most
rapid change because most of the ordnance tested had much higher thermal inertia than the surrounding vegetation. As expected,
tall grasses dissipated heat more quickly than very short grass or bare ground. This phenomenon was similar in both winter and
summer ambient temperatures, although temperature changes occurred more rapidly under summer conditions. Exact temperature
changes for each ordnance type were not recorded because of the quantity and variety of ordnance, differences in ordnance
placement, and the limited number of data collectors (see Figs. 5A and 5B).

Limitations, Disclaimers, and Data Usage

Several factors should be understood by teams using these data. First, all ordnance used in this work was inspected by EOD
specialists and verified to be inert. Although these conditions were necessary at this stage of the research, removal of explosive
materials from the ordnance almost certainly altered object density and, therefore, produced thermal behavior different from that
of live ordnance. Second, the ordnance used in this data collection varied widely in type. As expected, differences in ordnance
composition (i.e., steel, aluminum, Bakelite, rubber, plastic, etc.) resulted in different thermal behavior under these conditions.
