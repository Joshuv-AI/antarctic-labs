Cuff-first technique is associated with a lower 6-month type II endoleak rate after endovascular aneurysm repair for infrarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm
Authors: Suguru Tatsuki, Ryuji Koshima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveType II endoleak (T2EL) remains the most common endoleak after endovascular aneurysm repair. We evaluated whether the cuff-first technique (CFT), in which an aortic cuff is deployed at the branch-vessel level before main-body deployment, is associated with lower early T2EL rates.MethodsWe performed a single-center retrospective comparative study of infrarenal endovascular aneurysm repair (October 2020-December 2024). Patients without contrast-enhanced computed tomography at 6 months and other predefined exclusions were excluded. The primary endpoint was T2EL on 6-month computed tomography (early and delayed phases). Secondary endpoints included aneurysm sac regression ≥5 mm at 6 months, reintervention for T2EL, operative time, contrast volume, and radiation exposure (air kerma). Freedom from reintervention was analyzed by Kaplan-Meier methods.ResultsWe analyzed 100 patients (cuff-first, n = 33; noncuff-first, n = 67). Inferior mesenteric artery patency (93.9% vs 74.6%; P = .028) and diameter (2.4 ± 1.0 vs 1.8 ± 1.3 mm; P = .039) were higher in the cuff-first group, whereas lumbar artery counts were similar (5.5 ± 1.8 vs 5.4 ± 1.8; P = .827). At 6 months, T2EL was less frequent with the CFT (9.1% vs 31.3%; P = .014), and aneurysm sac regression ≥5 mm was more frequent (24.2% vs 7.5%; P = .027). Operative time, contrast volume, and radiation exposure did not differ significantly. Reintervention for T2EL occurred in one cuff-first patient and seven noncuff-first patients; freedom from reintervention at 36 months was 94.7% vs 85.5% (log-rank P = .251).ConclusionsThe CFT was associated with lower 6-month T2EL rates and higher early sac regression without increasing procedural burden. Larger studies with longer follow-up are warranted.Keywords: Abdominal aortic aneurysm, Cuff-first technique, Endovascular aneurysm repair, Preemptive procedure, Type II endoleak

Type II endoleak (T2EL) remains the most common endoleak after endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) and continues to be a clinical concern because persistent sac perfusion may contribute to sac enlargement and the need for secondary interventions.1, 2, 3, 4 Although many T2ELs resolve without treatment, the uncertainty of sac behavior and heterogeneity in management strategies create ongoing controversy in routine practice.

Several preventive approaches have been explored to reduce T2EL at the time of index EVAR, including embolization of the inferior mesenteric artery (IMA) and lumbar arteries, as well as adjunctive sac-directed techniques.5 However, these strategies may increase procedural complexity and are not uniformly adopted, and the reported effectiveness has varied across studies and practice settings.6, 7, 8 In particular, prophylactic embolization can be technically challenging when lumbar arteries are multiple or small, and IMA catheterization is not always feasible in case of unsuitable anatomy.9,10

The cuff-first technique (CFT) is a simple intraoperative modification in which an aortic cuff is deployed at the level of major branch vessels before main-body deployment.11,12 By placing graft material near the ostia of the IMA and lumbar arteries early in the procedure, CFT may reduce early retrograde inflow into the aneurysm sac without adding substantial procedural burden. However, evidence supporting its clinical utility remains limited. Therefore, we aimed to compare 6-month T2EL rates and perioperative procedural metrics between CFT and non-CFT EVAR in a single-center retrospective cohort.

The patients provided written consent for publication of clinical details and images.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a single-center retrospective comparative cohort study to evaluate the association between the CFT and T2EL after EVAR for infrarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA). The study period was from October 2020 to December 2024. The study protocol was approved by the institutional review board (Approval No. C26-2-01). The requirement for informed consent was waived because of the retrospective design.

Patient selection

We screened consecutive patients who underwent EVAR for infrarenal AAA during the study period. We excluded patients with saccular aneurysms, ruptured aneurysms, impending rupture, dissection-related aneurysms, emergency procedures, and those without contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) follow-up at 6 months. The final analytic cohort was divided into two groups based on intraoperative strategy: CFT and non-CFT (Fig 1).

Study flow diagram. Consecutive patients who underwent endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) for infrarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) between October 2020 and December 2024 were screened. Exclusion criteria were saccular aneurysm, ruptured/impending rupture, dissection-related aneurysm, emergency procedure, and lack of contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) at 6 months. The final cohort was grouped by intraoperative strategy [cuff-first technique (CFT) vs non-CFT].

In general, repair was considered for men with aneurysm diameter ≥55 mm and for women with aneurysm diameter ≥50 mm. Repair was also considered for patients with rapid aneurysm growth (>5 mm within 6 months) or other patient-specific clinical considerations, even if the diameter had not reached the standard threshold.

Procedural details, devices, and adjunctive embolization

EVAR was performed according to standard institutional practice. Multiple commercially available bifurcated endovascular aortic stent graft systems were used for main-body EVAR, including the GORE EXCLUDER AAA Endoprosthesis (W. L. Gore & Associates, Inc), the Zenith Alpha Abdominal Endovascular Graft (Cook Medical), the Endurant II/IIs Stent Graft System (Medtronic Vascular), the AFX Endovascular AAA System (Endologix, LLC), and the Aorfix AAA Flexible Stent Graft System (Lombard Medical Limited) (Supplementary Table I, online only). The decision to perform IMA embolization was based on surgeon preference. Aortic cuff selection was based primarily on anatomic fit, available cuff diameter and length, and device availability. When feasible, manufacturer matching and component compatibility were considered; however, cuff and main-body components were not always from the same manufacturer because sizing and anatomic requirements were prioritized.

Definition of the CFT and procedural intent

In the CFT group, an aortic cuff was deployed before main-body deployment. The cuff diameter and length were selected based on preoperative CT scans to match the aortic diameter and length at the target branches level to achieve sealing at that level. The cuff was positioned under fluoroscopy at the height of the target branches (eg, IMA and/or lumbar arteries) (Supplementary Video, online only). Because cuff migration was a potential concern, the procedure proceeded promptly to main-body deployment after cuff placement. The primary technical intent of CFT at our institution is to reduce early retrograde sac inflow originating from lumbar arteries and from IMAs that are difficult to catheterize for embolization. Because prophylactic IMA embolization and CFT were applied at surgeon discretion, they were sometimes used in combination. To address this practice pattern, we reported IMA embolization rates by group and planned an additional stratified analysis restricted to patients who underwent IMA embolization.

As this was a retrospective study, formal prospective selection criteria for CFT were not predefined. In practice, operators assessed anatomic suitability on preoperative CT at the intended target branch level rather than solely by the maximum aneurysm diameter. The target level was determined by the location of the IMA and/or lumbar artery ostia intended to be covered or approximated by the cuff. Cuff sizing was based on the diameter of the available flow lumen at this target level, measured along the inner boundary of mural thrombus when mural thrombus was present, rather than on the outer aneurysm diameter. Therefore, a large maximum aneurysm diameter did not necessarily preclude CFT when the target branch level had an adequate thrombus-lined lumen for stable cuff positioning. Conversely, CFT was avoided when the lumen at the target branch level substantially exceeded the available cuff diameter, or when stable positioning was judged unlikely because of migration risk.

Imaging protocol and outcome definitions

All patients underwent contrast-enhanced CT at 6 months after EVAR, and T2EL was assessed using both early and delayed phases. T2EL was defined as persistent contrast opacification of the aneurysm sac supplied by branch vessels on follow-up imaging (Fig 2). In our surveillance protocol, contrast-enhanced CT is performed at 6 months, 1 and 2 years, and annually through 5 years after repair when feasible.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the presence of T2EL on contrast-enhanced CT at 6 months. Secondary endpoints were (1) aneurysm sac regression of ≥5 mm at 6 months, (2) reintervention for T2EL, (3) operative time (skin-to-skin), (4) contrast volume, and (5) radiation exposure measured by the fluoroscopy system as air kerma (mGy).

Reintervention criteria for T2EL

Reintervention for T2EL was considered when aneurysm sac expansion of ≥5 mm was observed compared with the preoperative diameter, in the setting of acceptable procedural tolerance. When feasible, transarterial retrograde coil embolization was performed. If transarterial embolization was not feasible, open surgical treatment was selected.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the Student t-test. Categorical variables were summarized as counts (percentages) and compared using the χ2 test or Fisher exact test, as appropriate. Freedom from reintervention for T2EL was estimated using Kaplan-Meier methods and compared between groups with the log-rank test. A two-sided P value <.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using EZR (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University), a graphical user interface for R.

Results

Study cohort

A total of 100 patients met the inclusion criteria and were analyzed: 33 patients underwent EVAR with the CFT (CFT group) and 67 underwent EVAR without CFT (non-CFT group).

Baseline and anatomic characteristics

Baseline demographics were similar between groups (Table I). Age (76 ± 8 vs 78 ± 9 years; P = .286), male sex (84.8% vs 71.6%; P = .213), and preoperative aneurysm diameter (49.6 ± 5.8 vs 52.2 ± 8.3 mm; P = .113) did not differ significantly. IMA embolization was performed in 69.7% of the CFT group and 52.2% of the non-CFT group (P = .132). IMA patency (93.9% vs 74.6%; P = .028) and diameter (2.4 ± 1.0 vs 1.8 ± 1.3 mm; P = .039) were higher in the CFT group. Lumbar artery counts were similar (5.5 ± 1.8 vs 5.4 ± 1.8; P = .827), and the maximum lumbar artery diameter tended to be larger (2.0 ± 0.6 vs 1.8 ± 0.6 mm; P = .066).

Secondary endpoints

Aneurysm sac regression of ≥5 mm at 6 months was more frequent in the CFT group than the non-CFT group (8 [24.2%] vs 5 [7.5%]; P = .027). Operative time (skin-to-skin) was similar between groups (147.2 ± 56.9 vs 149.4 ± 57.3 minutes; P = .859). Contrast volume did not differ significantly (210.5 ± 56.9 vs 199.4 ± 75.9 mL; P = .458). Radiation exposure measured as air kerma was also comparable (1384.3 ± 808.3 vs 1354.4 ± 914.8 mGy; P = .875) (Table III).

Reintervention for T2EL

During a mean follow-up of 22.8 months, reintervention for T2EL was performed in one patient in the CFT group and seven patients in the non-CFT group. At 36 months, freedom from reintervention for T2EL was 94.7% in the CFT group and 85.5% in the non-CFT group, without a statistically significant difference (log-rank P = .251) (Fig 3). In a sensitivity analysis restricted to patients treated with the GORE EXCLUDER platform, 6-month T2EL was numerically lower in the CFT group (14.3% vs 40.6%; P = .066). In the same restricted cohort, aneurysm sac regression ≥5 mm at 6 months occurred in 4 of 21 patients with CFT and 3 of 32 non-CFT patients (19.0% vs 9.4%; P = .415) (Supplementary Table II, online only).

Kaplan-Meier estimates of freedom from reintervention for type II endoleak (T2EL). Freedom from reintervention for T2EL was compared between the cuff-first technique (CFT) and non-CFT groups using the log-rank test.

Subgroup analyses by IMA embolization

In stratified analyses, the direction of the association between CFT and lower 6-month T2EL rates was consistent. Among patients who underwent IMA embolization, T2EL occurred in 3 of 23 patients (13.0%) in the CFT group and in 11 of 35 patients (31.4%) in the non-CFT group (P = .13) (Supplementary Table III, online only). Among patients without IMA embolization, T2EL occurred in zero of 10 patients (0.0%) in the CFT group and in 10 of 32 patients (31.2%) in the non-CFT group (P = .084) (Supplementary Table IV, online only). Procedural metrics, including operative time, contrast volume, and radiation exposure, were similar within each stratum (Supplementary Tables III and IV, online only).

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective comparative study of infrarenal EVAR, CFT was associated with a lower rate of T2EL at 6 months without increasing operative time, contrast volume, or radiation exposure, and was also associated with a higher rate of aneurysm sac regression of ≥5 mm at 6 months. This finding is clinically relevant because prophylactic strategies such as branch embolization or sac-directed approaches may increase procedural complexity and are not uniformly adopted across centers. An international survey also suggests substantial variability in T2EL management and the role of preemptive embolization in real-world practice.6 Our results support the possibility that a simple sequence modification during EVAR may reduce early T2EL detection while preserving procedural efficiency.

A notable feature of our cohort is that the CFT group had a higher prevalence of patent IMA and a larger IMA diameter, whereas the number and the diameter of lumbar arteries were similar between groups. These anatomic factors are often considered to increase the potential for retrograde sac inflow. Despite this, T2EL at 6 months was lower in the CFT group (9.1% vs 31.3%). This pattern is consistent with the hypothesis that deploying a cuff at the branch level before main-body deployment may promote early thrombus formation around branch ostia and reduce early retrograde filling of the aneurysm sac. However, this interpretation remains hypothesis-generating given the observational design. One potential mechanism is promotion of intrasac thrombosis by introducing foreign material within the aneurysm environment, consistent with prior coil-based preventive approaches.13,14

The applicability of CFT should be interpreted according to target-level lumen anatomy rather than maximum aneurysm diameter alone. In the CFT group, the mean lumen diameter at the cuff deployment level was 25.3 ± 5.0 mm, the mean cuff diameter was 29.6 ± 3.9 mm, and the mean cuff-to-lumen diameter ratio was 1.2 ± 0.2. These findings suggest that CFT may be feasible when the target branch level has a thrombus-lined lumen suitable for stable cuff positioning, but may be unsuitable when the target-level lumen substantially exceeds the available cuff diameter.

Because CFT and IMA embolization were sometimes applied in combination, we performed stratified analyses by IMA embolization status. The association between CFT and lower 6-month T2EL rates remained directionally consistent in both strata. In the IMA embolization subgroup, T2EL was numerically lower with CFT (13.0% vs 31.4%), although the difference was not statistically significant, likely reflecting limited sample size. In the subgroup without IMA embolization, no T2EL was observed in the CFT group (0/10) compared with 31.2% in the non-CFT group (P = .084). These findings support but do not prove that CFT may reduce early retrograde sac filling beyond the effect of IMA embolization alone.

Our findings should be interpreted in the context of the broader literature on preventive interventions for T2EL. Intraoperative branch embolization during EVAR has been investigated as a preventive strategy, but results vary by patient selection, anatomic risk, and technical details.10,15 Other approaches, including staged or adjunctive endovascular strategies for high-risk anatomy, have also been reported.8

Compared with these approaches, CFT may offer a low-burden alternative that does not require additional catheterization of branch vessels, which may explain the comparable operative time, contrast use, and radiation exposure observed in our cohort. Because device platform selection may confound the association between CFT and T2EL, we performed a platform-restricted sensitivity analysis. A platform-restricted sensitivity analysis (GORE EXCLUDER only) showed a consistent direction of association for 6-month T2EL, although statistical significance was not reached due to limited sample size (Supplementary Table II, online only).

We did not observe a statistically significant difference in freedom from reintervention for T2EL at 36 months, although fewer reinterventions occurred in the CFT group (1 vs 7). This discrepancy between early imaging findings and later clinical events may reflect limited statistical power due to a small number of reintervention events, as well as heterogeneity in thresholds and techniques for treating T2EL. For example, multiple endovascular treatment strategies have been described with variable technical success and follow-up reporting.16 In our cohort, reintervention was considered for sac expansion of ≥5 mm relative to the preoperative diameter, and the treatment approach was selected based on feasibility and procedural tolerance, which may differ from other institutions.

Cost is an important consideration because the CFT requires an additional device. Although device pricing varies by institution and contract and was not evaluated in this study, any incremental cost would need to be justified by clinically meaningful reductions in endoleak-related surveillance, reintervention, or other downstream resource use. Formal cost-effectiveness analyses are warranted.

This study has limitations. First, the retrospective nonrandomized design introduces the possibility of selection bias and unmeasured confounding. The choice to perform CFT and IMA embolization depended on surgeon preference. Second, although we evaluated IMA patency/diameter and the number of lumbar arteries, we did not quantify lumbar artery diameter or other anatomic variables that may influence T2EL. Third, imaging assessment was based on contrast-enhanced CT at 6 months using early and delayed phases, and detection sensitivity may differ from other surveillance protocols. Long-term imaging assessment of T2EL was available only for a subset of patients and was not performed uniformly at the same time points in the entire cohort. Therefore, we did not calculate freedom from T2EL at 36 months for the full cohort and instead reported reintervention-free survival as the clinically meaningful long-term outcome. Finally, these results reflect a single-center experience and may not be generalizable.

Conclusions

In this single-center retrospective cohort, the CFT was associated with a lower rate of T2EL at 6 months without increasing operative time, contrast volume, or radiation exposure. Further studies with longer follow-up and detailed anatomic assessment are warranted to clarify its impact on late outcomes and reintervention.
