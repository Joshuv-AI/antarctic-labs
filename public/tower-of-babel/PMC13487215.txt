Fibrillin-1 alleviates high glucose-induced fibroblast apoptosis and inflammation via autophagy activation
Authors: Ya Wang, Sihong Xie, Xiaoyu Weng, Cheng Xu, Shujuan Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEffective management of fibroblast inflammation and apoptosis caused by high glucose is pivotal for mitigating diabetic foot ulcers (DFUs). Recent studies have indicated the potential involvement of Fibrillin-1 (FBN1) in DFU healing through its impact on fibroblast proliferation, migration, and apoptosis. However, detailed cellular mechanism remained uncleared.MethodsThree primary fibroblast cultures were established conventionally. The impact of FBN1 was studied through knockdown and overexpression. FBN1 levels were gauged via reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR for mRNA) and Western blot (for protein). Immunofluorescence localized FBN1. Proliferation was assessed via MTT assays, and EDU staining tracked cell viability. TUNEL detected apoptosis. Inflammation was measured by ELISA. Electron microscopy visualized cellular structure changes linked to autophagy, corroborated by Western blot markers.ResultsFibroblasts exposed to high glucose exhibited dysregulated FBN1 expression and disrupted autophagy. FBN1 overexpression promoted proliferation and suppressed inflammation and apoptosis under high glucose conditions. Additionally, FBN1 was found to restore autophagy inhibited by high glucose. In contrast, FBN1 silencing further suppressed autophagy, thereby aggravating inflammation and apoptosis.ConclusionDysregulation of FBN1 in fibroblasts induced by high glucose hampers autophagy, ultimately leading to apoptosis and inflammation. The upregulation of FBN1 counters these processes, potentially enhancing the healing of DFUs.Subject terms: Cell biology, Diabetes

Introduction

Diabetic foot ulcer (DFU), a prevailing complication of diabetes, is characterized by a complex interplay of pathophysiological factors that significantly amplify its clinical implications [1, 2]. As the global prevalence of diabetes continues to surge, DFU emerges as a compelling healthcare challenge with profound socio-economic issues [3]. This condition is distinguished by its multifactorial etiology, encompassing hyperglycemia-induced microvascular and macrovascular dysfunction, neuropathy, impaired immune response, and delayed wound healing [4, 5]. Consequently, the resulting injuries often display a chronic and recalcitrant nature, frequently leading to infection, gangrene, and, in severe cases, lower limb amputations [6]. In clinical practice, DFUs demand a multidisciplinary approach involving endocrinologists, podiatrists, wound care specialists, vascular surgeons, and infectious disease experts [7]. The existing therapeutic strategies encompass wound debridement, offloading, infection control, revascularization, and addressing systemic factors such as glycemic control. However, achieving successful DFU healing remains a formidable challenge, primarily due to the sophisticated interactions of these factors. Moreover, DFUs are frequently associated with a substantial psychological and financial burden on patients and healthcare systems alike [8]. Thus, comprehending the underlying mechanisms driving DFU pathogenesis and identifying innovative therapeutic avenues is imperative to mitigate the escalating DFU burden.

The association between fibroblast inflammation, apoptosis, and the pathogenesis of DFUs constitutes a essential area of research, offering profound insights into the mechanisms underlying DFU development and potential therapy. The huge clinical impact of DFUs, characterized by their chronicity, delayed healing, and propensity for infection [9], emphasizing the urgency to comprehend how cellular processes and wound pathology interact. Emerging research has spotlighted the pivotal role of fibroblasts in the complex of DFUs. Hyperglycemia, a hallmark of diabetes, engenders a microenvironment conducive to chronic inflammation [10]. As critical orchestrators of wound healing, fibroblasts exhibit increased inflammatory responses under the influence of elevated glucose [11]. This chronic inflammation disrupts the delicate balance between the extracellular matrix remodeling and tissue regeneration processes, impairing wound healing. Furthermore, the dysregulation of fibroblast apoptosis has emerged as a crucial aspect of DFU pathogenesis. Evidence from recent studies [12] supports the notion that fibroblast apoptosis is elevated in DFUs, perpetuating a state of impaired healing. This is exemplified by the findings of a significant correlation between dysregulated fibroblast apoptosis and the progression of DFUs [13]. These findings collectively emphasize the intricate relationship between fibroblast behavior, inflammation, apoptosis, and the development of DFUs.

Fibrillin-1 (FBN1), a glycoprotein acknowledged primarily as a fundamental component of extracellular microfibrils, has emerged as a subject of heightened interest in wound healing, specifically within the realm of DFUs. Current research has begun to unveil FBN1’s multifaceted involvement in critical cellular processes that hold sway over effective wound repair [14, 15]. Remarkably, studies focus on the pertinence of FBN1 by illuminating the profound impact of its suppression on fibroblast dynamics—manifesting as a curtailment of both proliferation and migration, alongside an elevation in apoptosis rates [16]. This spectrum of effects positions FBN1 as an putative contributor to the intricate orchestration of DFU wound healing, yet the detailed mechanistic underpinnings that govern its regulatory effect remain unveiled. Long non-coding RNA H19 is an extensively studied lncRNA, known to play diverse roles in various biological processes, including cancer progression and wound healing [17]. It is an imprinted gene located on chromosome 11p15.5, closely associated with the IGF2 gene. H19 has been reported to regulate gene expression through its interaction with microRNAs, such as miR-29b and miR-675, functioning as a competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) or precursor to these miRNAs. H19 has also been shown to promote cell proliferation, migration, and survival in different tissues, notably in DFUs and cancers such as gliomas [17]. Further investigations have revealed that the interaction between H19 and FBN1 is important for wound healing, as H19 can inhibit miR-29b, which directly regulates the structural protein FBN1 in extracellular microfibrils. Moreover, H19 acts as a sponge for miR-29b, blocking its suppression of FBN1 expression, thereby increasing FBN1 levels and promoting fibroblast proliferation, migration, and extracellular matrix production, all of which are essential for tissue repair and wound healing [18, 19]. Despite these knowledge, there is still a lack of confirmatory research regarding the correlation between FBN1 and wound healing and how to target it for improving DFU treatment.

Autophagy, a tightly correlated cellular process that mediates the recycling and renewal of cellular components, has emerged as a noteworthy contender in the context of DFUs. Previous studies have confirmed a unique relationship between autophagy and the complex pathological environment of DFU [20]. Specifically, studies have uncovered diminished levels of autophagy within Achilles tendon tissues of DFU-afflicted rats when contrasted with their normoglycemic counterparts [21]. The decrease in autophagic activity affects the various aspects of DFUs and suggests that enhancing autophagy can improve wound healing. Interestingly, the influence of FBN1 on autophagic processes adds complexity. Its modulation is uncharted and requires exploration [22]. To this end, investigating the regulatory dynamics encompassing FBN1 and its potential regulatory influence over autophagy is an area ripe for comprehensive inquiry.

In brief, this study delves into the complex interactions occurring in DFUs, with a focus on the impact of altered FBN1 expression in fibroblasts when exposed to high glucose (HG) levels. This disruption can lead to issues with autophagy, making fibroblasts more susceptible to inflammation and apoptosis, ultimately worsening DFU progression. However, targeting upregulated FBN1 may provide a way to address these processes and offer potential benefits.

Methods and materials

Sample collection and ethical statements

Granulation tissues from the DFU patients were collected to construct fibroblast cell lines. These patients without chronic hypertension, and with a history of type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM) for five to seven years, presented with an open wound that had been present for more than 30 days. Prior to sample collection, informed consent was obtained from the donors adhering to the ethical guidelines outlined in the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki. This study was approved by the Ethic Committee of our institution.

Isolation and Culture of Primary Fibroblasts

DFU granulation tissue was processed for culture following a previously described method [23]. Briefly, a 6 mm diameter biopsy cylinder was taken from a well-granulated area of the wound. The tissue fragments were dissected into small pieces and evenly distributed in a 60 mm tissue culture plate. After incubating for 1 h at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 and 95% air atmosphere without culture media, to ensure adhesion to the plastic surface, 3 mL of HG Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) culture medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 10082147), 2 mM glutamine, and 100 U/mL penicillin/100 µg/mL streptomycin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 15140122) were added to the plate. To minimize the risk of contamination, the HG solution was prepared by dissolving glucose in sterilized solvent and filtering the solution through a 0.22 μm filter under aseptic conditions in a sterile laminar flow hood. The culture medium was replaced every three days until fibroblasts sprouted from the tissue fragments and reached approximately 50% confluency. The cells were then trypsinized and subcultured at a ratio of 7500 cells/cm2, following which total of 3 identical primary fibroblasts were retrieved, named as Fibroblast-1, Fibroblast-2 and Fibroblast-3.

Plasmid transfection

The fibroblast cells were cultured in a 6-well plate at an initial density of 4 × 105 cells per well. Once cells reached 80%–90% confluence, plasmids containing different constructs, including the Control plasmid, ov-NC (negative control), ov-FBN1 (overexpression of FBN1), sh-NC (negative control for RNA interference), and sh-FBN1 (RNA interference targeting FBN1), were introduced into the cells. For transfection, the Lipofectamine 3000 Transfection Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA; Cat. No. L3000008) was used following the manufacturer’s instructions. Plasmids were mixed with the transfection reagent in Opti-MEM Reduced Serum Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 31985070) and allowed to form complexes. The complexes were then added to the cells, and transfection was carried out for the specified period. During post-transfection, the cells were allowed to recover in a complete growth medium. The medium was replaced with fresh medium containing antibiotics as needed to maintain cell health. Transfection efficiency and effects were evaluated at appropriate time points post-transfection. This involved assessing changes in gene expression through methods such as reverse transcription-quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) and Western blot analysis.

To modulate autophagy, rapamycin (RAP; 100 nM) was applied for 24 h following transfection where indicated. Cells in the HG+sh-FBN1 + RAP group received RAP under high glucose conditions. Downstream assays (RT-qPCR, Western blot, immunofluorescence, TEM, MTT, ELISA, TUNEL) were performed as described above.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cultured fibroblast cells using the RNeasy Plus Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Cat. No. 74136) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were lysed and homogenized, and RNA was purified using silica membrane-based columns. The quantity and quality of RNA were assessed using a spectrophotometer (NanoDrop, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and gel electrophoresis. Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from the isolated RNA using the SuperScript™ IV First-Strand Synthesis System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 18091050) as per the provided instructions. Reverse transcription was performed using a thermal cycler (PCR machine) under optimized conditions. RT-qPCR was performed to analyze the expression levels of target genes. Primers specific for the genes of interest, including FBN1, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), IL-6, and the reference gene glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH), were designed using Primer Premier and obtained from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT). RT-qPCR was carried out using the PowerUp™ SYBR™ Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. A25742) and a real-time PCR system (QuantStudio™ 3 Real-Time PCR System, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Cycling conditions included an initial denaturation step, followed by 40 amplification cycles.

Western Blot

Cultured fibroblast cells were harvested and lysed using RIPA lysis buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 89900) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 78440). Protein concentration was determined using the Pierce™ BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 23225). Equal amounts of protein (typically 20-30 μg) were separated by -PAGE using precast gels. Separated proteins were then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, Cat. No. IPVH00010) using a semi-dry transfer system. The PVDF membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat dry milk (Bio-Rad, Cat. No. 170-6404) in Tris-buffered saline with Tween 20 (TBST) for 1 h at room temperature. Membranes were then incubated with primary antibody as anti-FBN1 (1:1000) (Abcam, Cat#: ab53076), anti-microtubule-associated protein 1 A/1B light chain 3B (LC3B) (1:500) (Abcam, Cat#: ab51520) and anti- p62 (1:1000) (Abcam, Cat#: ab56416) overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies, such as Anti-Rabbit IgG HRP-linked antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. No. 7074) or Anti-Mouse IgG HRP-linked antibody (Cell Signaling Technology, Cat. No. 7076), for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. No. 32106) and detected using a chemiluminescence imaging system.

Analysis of FBN1 expression in DFU granulation tissue

FBN1 expression was directly assessed in granulation tissue samples. Tissues were collected from the DFUs patients and non-diabetic individuals undergoing elective foot procedures who served as controls. One portion of each sample was snap-frozen for RNA and protein extraction, and the remainder was fixed for histological examination. Total RNA and protein were isolated using the same protocols as for fibroblast cultures. FBN1 expression was quantified by RT-qPCR (normalized to GAPDH) and Western blot, as described above.

Immunofluorescence (IF) detection

Fibroblast cells were grown on glass slides and briefly rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The cells were then fixed in 4% formaldehyde for 10 min at room temperature. Following fixation, the cells were washed three times with PBS, each wash lasting 5 min. The cells were then permeabilized with 0.1% to 0.25% Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. After permeabilization, the cells were washed three times with PBS, each wash lasting 5 min. The cells were then incubated in PBST (PBS + 0.1% Tween 20) containing 1% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 22.52 mg/mL glycine for 30 min to block non-specific antibody binding. For FBN1 detection, the FBN1 antibody (Abcam, catalog number: ab53076) was used at a dilution of 1:1500. For LC3BII/I detection, the LC3B antibody (Abcam, catalog number: ab51520) was used at a dilution of 1:2000. The cells were incubated in the diluted primary antibodies in 1% BSA in PBST for one hour at room temperature or overnight at 4 °C. Following incubation, the solution was discarded, and the cells were washed three times with PBS, each wash lasting 5 min. Finally, the cells were incubated in the secondary antibodies diluted in 1% BSA for one hour at room temperature in the dark. After incubation, the solution was discarded, and the cells were washed three times with PBS, each wash lasting 5 min. The slides were then for subsequent IF microscopy examination.

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM)

TEM was employed to examine fibroblast cells, focusing particularly on their autophagosome. In the initial step, the cells were fixed using a 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution (Sigma-Aldrich, Product No. G5882) in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate buffer (Sigma-Aldrich, Product No. C0250) for 2 h at room temperature. Subsequently, a post-fixation process was conducted by treating the cells with 1% osmium tetroxide (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Product No. 19150) within the same buffer for 1 h. To proceed, cellular dehydration was performed using ethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, Product No. E7023), followed by embedding the cells in epoxy resin (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Product No. 14120). The creation of ultrathin sections (70–90 nm) was achieved using an ultramicrotome (Leica Microsystems, Model UC7), onto which copper grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Product No. 01814-F) were used to collect the sections. Enhancing the contrast of the samples involved staining with uranyl acetate (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Product No. 22400) and lead citrate (Electron Microscopy Sciences, Product No. 17800). For observation, a TEM (JEOL, Model JEM-1400Plus) operating at 80 kV was employed. Images were captured utilizing a digital camera (Olympus, Product No. Veleta) to facilitate further analysis.

Methylthiazolyldiphenyl-Tetrazolium Bromide (MTT) Assay

The fibroblast cells were seeded at a density of 5000 cells per well in 96-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. Experimental treatments were performed, and after the treatment period, the culture medium was replaced with PBS. MTT solution (0.5 mg/ml, Sigma-Aldrich, Catalog Number: M5655) was added to each well, and the cells were incubated at 37 °C for 3 h. Following incubation, the formazan crystals were solubilized using dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO). Absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader. Cell viability was calculated as the percentage of absorbance in treated cells compared to control cells.

5-Ethynyl-2’-Deoxyuridine (EDU) Staining

Cell proliferation was assessed using the EDU incorporation assay. The fibroblast cells were seeded at a density of 10,000 cells per well in 24-well plates and allowed to adhere overnight. The culture medium was aspirated after experimental treatments, and cells were washed with PBS. EDU labeling solution (10 μM, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog Number: C10339) was prepared in serum-free medium and added to the cells, which were then incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. After labeling, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100, and treated with Click-iT reaction cocktail according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Nuclei were counterstained using DAPI. Fluorescence microscopy was employed to visualize EDU-positive cells, indicative of active DNA synthesis. The percentage of EDU-positive cells was determined, providing insights into cell proliferation rates.

Terminal Deoxynucleotidyl Transferase dUTP Nick End Labeling (TUNEL) Assay for Apoptosis Detection

Apoptosis assessment was conducted using the TUNEL assay. The fibroblast cells were cultured on coverslips and exposed to various experimental conditions. After the treatment, cellular fixation was undertaken using 4% paraformaldehyde, followed by permeabilization with 0.1% Triton X-100. The TUNEL assay was performed employing the TUNEL reaction mixture (Roche Diagnostics, Catalog Number: 11684795910), inclusive of terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase enzyme and fluorescein-dUTP, with incubation at 37 °C for a duration of 1 h. To facilitate comprehensive analysis, nuclei were subsequently counterstained using DAPI. Visualization of the prepared samples under fluorescence microscopy enabled the clear identification of TUNEL-positive cells, a hallmark of apoptosis-associated DNA fragmentation. By quantifying the percentage of TUNEL-positive cells, invaluable insights were gleaned regarding the apoptotic activity and the dynamic cellular response elicited by the applied experimental conditions.

Enzyme-linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

The quantification of inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, was performed using ELISA techniques. The fibroblast cells were cultured under distinct experimental conditions to induce potential inflammatory responses. After the experimental treatments, cell culture supernatants were collected and subjected to ELISA analysis. ELISA kits specific for TNF-α (BioLegend, Catalog Number: 430907), IL-1β (R&D Systems, Catalog Number: DY201), and IL-6 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Catalog Number: 88-7066) were employed as per the manufacturer’s protocols. Briefly, the collected supernatants were incubated with respective capture antibodies immobilized on microplate wells, followed by detection antibody conjugation and subsequent colorimetric detection. The absorbance was measured at a wavelength of 450 nm, with reference correction at 570 nm. Cytokine concentrations were extrapolated from standard curves generated using known concentrations of respective recombinant cytokines.

Animal model of DFU and FBN1 overexpression

A diabetic foot ulcer (DFU) mouse model was established in C57BL/6 J mice (6–8 weeks old, n = 5 per group) by streptozotocin (STZ) induction of diabetes followed by creation of full-thickness excisional wounds on the dorsal surface of the footpad. The mice were randomly assigned to Control, Model+ov-NC, and Model+ov-FBN1 groups. FBN1 overexpression was achieved via tail-vein injection of adeno-associated virus carrying the FBN1 coding sequence (AAV-ov-FBN1). All animal procedures were conducted under protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Wound tissues were harvested on days 0, 3, 6, and 9 post-injury for subsequent analyses. Wound healing was monitored by digital photography and quantified as the percentage of wound closure. Histopathological evaluation was conducted by hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining. FBN1 expression was measured by RT-qPCR and Western blot. Inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6) were detected by ELISA, while apoptosis was assessed by TUNEL staining. Autophagy markers (LC3B-I/II and p62) were analyzed by Western blot.

Statistical analysis

The experimental procedures were replicated across three independent trials for each cell line, and the outcomes are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) derived from the three discrete experiments. Statistical computations were executed employing GraphPad Prism 5 software from La Jolla, California, U.S.A. Student’s t-test was engaged to assess distinctions between groups, while comparisons among multiple groups were conducted through one-way analysis of variance, succeeded by Dunnett’s test. Significance was established at p < 0.05, indicating statistically significant differences.

No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size. The group size of five animals per group was based on commonly adopted practice in similar DFU mouse model studies and was deemed sufficient to observe reproducible effects, while minimizing animal use in accordance with ethical considerations. No blinding was used for animal studies.

Results

Downregulation of FBN1 in DFU granulation tissue

We first examined FBN1 expression directly in granulation tissue samples collected from patients with DFUs (n = 10) and from non-diabetic individuals undergoing elective foot procedures. RT-qPCR analysis revealed a significant reduction of FBN1 mRNA in DFU granulation tissue compared with control samples (Fig. 1A). Consistently, Western blot demonstrated lower FBN1 protein expression in DFU tissues (Fig. 1B), confirming FBN1 downregulation in vivo in DFU patient tissues. Furthermore, compare to control group (primary fibroblasts of non-diabetic), mRNA and protein levels of FBN1 were downregulated in primary fibroblasts derived from DFU patients’ wound tissues (Fig. 1C, D).

FBN1 expression imbalance and autophagic perturbation in HG-induced fibroblasts

To address the fundamental hypothesis that HG levels play a significant role in modulating cellular behavior and autophagic processes, our investigation focused on primary fibroblasts derived from DFU patients’ wound tissues. Given the potential implications of extracellular matrix protein FBN1 in wound healing, we postulated that HG conditions might disrupt its expression within fibroblasts. The experimental design included categorizing the study groups into control and HG groups, allowing us to systematically evaluate the impact of HG exposure on FBN1 expression and autophagic activity. RT-qPCR results (Fig. 1E) exhibited a significant decrease in FBN1 mRNA levels in all three fibroblast lines, which was further corroborated by Western blot (Fig. 1F). IF staining showed a substantial reduction in FBN1 signal intensity under HG conditions (Fig. 1G).

Next, we investigated the autophagic activity within the HG-treated fibroblasts. Autophagy is a fundamental cellular process involved in degrading and recycling damaged or surplus cellular components. To assess autophagy, we examined two key markers: LC3BII/I and p62. LC3BII/I is a marker of autophagosome formation and is considered a reliable indicator of autophagic activity [24, 25]. In our analysis, Western blot (Fig. 1H) demonstrated a suppression of the LC3BII/I ratio under HG conditions, which suggests compromised autophagosome formation within the three fibroblast lines exposed to HG. Furthermore, p62, another autophagy-related protein, serves as a selective substrate for autophagic degradation. An increase in p62 levels often signifies impairment in the autophagic flux [26]. Strikingly, our findings showed a substantial increase of p62 levels by approximately 2.5-fold in the HG group compared to the control group, which strongly suggests disrupted autophagic degradation in response to HG. These findings provide the significant impact of HG on FBN1 expression and autophagic processes in fibroblasts, highlighting potential mechanisms contributing to altered wound healing responses in DFUs.

The role of FBN1 in HG-induced fibroblasts involves promoting proliferation and inhibiting inflammation and apoptosis

To further investigate the effects of FBN1 in primary fibroblasts exposed to HG, we examined its influence on proliferation, inflammation, and apoptosis. Fibroblasts were divided into four groups: Control, HG, HG+ov-NC, and HG+ov-FBN1. As an initial step, we analyzed FBN1 expression by RT-qPCR (Fig. 2A). HG treatment significantly reduced FBN1 expression by approximately 70% compared with control fibroblasts. Infection with ov-FBN1 did not induce supraphysiological expression but effectively restored FBN1 levels suppressed by HG to values comparable with those observed in control fibroblasts.

Compellingly, transfection with ov-FBN1 mitigated the downregulation of FBN1 induced by HG, reinstating it to near-baseline levels. This restoration was robustly observed, as FBN1 levels decreased by approximately 70% in the HG group, but ov-FBN1 transfection led to chiefly recovery. Further investigations were directed at assessing cell viability and proliferation. Employing MTT assays (Fig. 2B) and EDU staining (Fig. 2C), we observed that HG treatment substantially suppressed primary fibroblast vitality and proliferation. Remarkably, the overexpression of FBN1 alleviated the inhibitory effects of HG on cellular viability. This indicated that the presence of FBN1 counteracted the adverse impact of HG on cell proliferation. We extended our scrutiny to inflammation, evaluating the secretion of vital inflammatory cytokines, including TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6. As presented through qPCR and ELISA analyses (Fig. 2D, E), the experimental findings demonstrated a significant augmentation of these inflammatory factors in the HG group and HG+ov-NC group, with levels elevated up to more than threefold. Strikingly, overexpression of FBN1 greatly restored the inflammatory cytokine secretion to baseline levels. This observation underscores FBN1’s potential in facilitating the pro-inflammatory response elicited by HG conditions. Lastly, we explored the role of FBN1 in influencing apoptosis. TUNEL assays (Fig. 2F) revealed a twofold increase in cell apoptosis in response to HG treatment. Overexpression of FBN1 counteracted this effect, bringing cell apoptosis back to baseline levels. These results illuminate the multifunctional role of FBN1 in HG-induced fibroblasts, where it promotes cellular proliferation and exerts anti-inflammatory and anti-apoptotic effects.

FBN1 overexpression promotes wound healing in DFU mice

To validate our in vitro findings, we examined the effect of FBN1 overexpression in a DFU mouse model. Compared with normal tissue, wound tissues from DFU mice showed significantly reduced FBN1 expression. Overexpression of FBN1 restored FBN1 levels in wound tissue (Fig. 3A, B). HE staining demonstrated that FBN1 overexpression accelerated re-epithelialization and reduced necrotic tissue formation, particularly on day 6 to day 9 (Fig. 3C, D). ELISA analysis revealed that FBN1 overexpression markedly decreased TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 secretion in wound tissues (Fig. 3E). TUNEL staining confirmed that apoptosis in wound-edge tissue was alleviated by FBN1 overexpression (Fig. 3F). These data revealed that FBN1 overexpression alleviated wound injury in DFU mice via suppression of inflammation and apoptosis.

Restoration of HG-inhibited autophagy by FBN1 in fibroblasts

Next, we evaluated whether FBN1 modulates autophagy in DFU mouse model. Western blotting showed that DFU mouse decreased the LC3B-II/I ratio and increased p62 accumulation, whereas FBN1 overexpression reversed these changes (Fig. 4A). Extending the analysis across experimental groups, HG markedly reduced LC3B-II and increased p62 compared with control group, while FBN1 overexpression under HG-exposed restored LC3B-II and lowered p62; rapamycin (RAP, an autophagy activator) treatment in the ov-FBN1 background further activated the autophagy profile (Fig. 4B). Immunofluorescence confirmed these biochemical findings: LC3B puncta were reduced by HG, increased by ov-FBN1, and restored to levels comparable to or exceeding control in the HG+ov-FBN1 + RAP group (Fig. 4C). Transmission electron microscopy demonstrated fewer autophagosomes under HG and HG+ov-NC, partial recovery with ov-FBN1, and clear restoration with HG+ ov-FBN1 + RAP (Fig. 4D). Together, these data indicate that FBN1 counters HG-induced suppression of autophagy and that pharmacologic activation of autophagy further activated phenotype caused by FBN1 overexpression, supporting an autophagy-dependent mechanism.

Exacerbation of HG-induced fibroblast inflammation by FBN1 silencing through autophagy inhibition

Continuing our investigation, we explored the connection between FBN1, autophagy, and inflammation in fibroblasts subjected to HG stimulation. The experimental set up encompassed the stimulation of fibroblasts-1 with HG and included distinct groupings: Control, HG+sh-NC, HG+sh-FBN1, and HG+sh-FBN1 + RAP. In fibroblasts-1, sh-FBN1 exerted an additional inhibitory effect on HG-induced FBN1 downregulation (Fig. 5A, B), and RAP treatment did not impact FBN1 expression. Next, the fluorescence intensity trends indicated that silencing FBN1 enhanced the inhibitory effect of HG on LC3B signal intensity, while RAP treatment effectively reversed the inhibitory effect of sh-FBN1 on LC3B signal intensity, underscoring the role of RAP in counteracting the suppression exerted by sh-FBN1 on LC3B levels (Fig. 5C). Importantly, results from MTT assay demonstrated that deprivation FBN1 intensified inhibition of HG on the cell proliferation of fibroblasts-1, while this phenomenon was reversed by RAP administration (Fig. 5D). In the context of inflammation, FBN1 silencing exacerbated HG-induced cytokine secretion, with RAP treatment effectively counteracting the pro-inflammatory effect of sh-FBN1 (Fig. 5E, F). Moreover, FBN1 silencing was linked to enhanced cell apoptosis in response to HG, a phenomenon mitigated by the activation of autophagy through RAP treatment (Fig. 5G). Concomitantly, these findings highlight the intricate orchestration of FBN1, autophagy, and inflammation in HG-induced fibroblast responses. The modulation of autophagy activation and its impact on inflammation and cell apoptosis further emphasize the intricate regulatory role of FBN1 in the cellular context under HG conditions.

Discussion

DFUs stand as a critical complication of diabetes mellitus, posing significant challenges to both patients and healthcare systems worldwide. These ulcers, often triggered by a combination of factors including neuropathy, peripheral vascular disease, and impaired wound healing, can lead to severe infections, gangrene, and even lower limb amputations if not managed promptly and effectively [6, 27]. The global prevalence of DFUs has escalated in recent years, paralleling the surge in diabetes cases. This mounting burden not only impairs the quality of life for affected individuals but also exerts a considerable economic toll on healthcare resources [28]. The complex interaction of various factors, such as HG levels, reduced circulation, and compromised immune response, exhibiting the nature of DFUs [29]. Considering the gravity of the situation, research efforts have been directed toward understanding the mechanisms underlying DFU development and progression. The current research findings reveal that FBN1 is crucial in HG-induced fibroblasts by promoting proliferation, suppressing inflammation and apoptosis, and restoring suppressed autophagy. By modulating these cellular processes, FBN1 emerges as a potential target for interventions to mitigate the adverse effects of DFUs and improve patient outcomes.

From different levels of experimental results, we have dogged into the vital role of FBN1 in the context of HG-induced fibroblasts. In particularly, we investigated the impact of FBN1 on various cellular processes and how these effects align with or diverge from existing literature. Our study revealed a significant disruption in FBN1 expression and autophagic operations within fibroblasts subjected to HG conditions. This observation is consistent with prior studies suggesting FBN1 participates in cellular signaling and maintenance [30, 31]. As highlighted in Fig. 1, the perturbation of FBN1 expression and its consequences on autophagy highlight its potential contribution to DFU pathogenesis. Moreover, our results unveiled the dual role of FBN1 in HG-induced fibroblasts: promoting proliferation while concurrently inhibiting inflammation and apoptosis, as shown in Fig. 2. This impact echoes previous research indicating FBN1’s capacity to modulate various cellular responses [32, 33]. Importantly, Fig. 3 visually captures this restorative phenomenon, offering a tangible representation of FBN1’s role in autophagy regulation and introducing a novel insight by showcasing the ability of FBN1 to restore autophagy, inhibited by HG, in fibroblasts. This observation adds depth to our understanding adaptive capabilities of FBN1, suggesting its involvement in countering metabolic stress-induced cellular dysregulation. These data persist with previous literature and prompt questions that warrant further investigation.

Autophagy is an essential cellular process responsible for recycling damaged or unnecessary components to maintain cellular homeostasis [34]. It plays a pivotal role in adapting cells to stressors such as nutrient deprivation, thereby influencing various cellular behaviors, including proliferation, inflammation, and apoptosis [35]. Our findings align with the existing literature, particularly regarding the influence of FBN1 on autophagy modulation. The dynamic interactions we observed between FBN1, autophagy, and cellular behaviors highlight the complexity of FBN1 regulatory role. Our findings suggest that FBN1 suppressing autophagy could potentially contribute to the observed effects on fibroblast proliferation and inflammatory responses (Fig. 4). Studies in various cellular contexts have provided evidence that inhibiting autophagy can lead to enhanced cell proliferation while concurrently promoting inflammatory pathways. For instance, research has demonstrated that autophagy suppression can facilitate tumor cell proliferation, contributing to tumor growth and progression [36, 37]. Additionally, in specific immune cell populations, autophagy inhibition has been linked to promoting inflammatory responses, exacerbating autoimmune conditions [38, 39]. These observations exert the possible relationship between autophagy, cell fate decisions, and inflammation in diverse physiological and pathological scenarios.

The interplay among autophagy, fibroblast inflammation, cytokine secretion, and apoptosis represents a central aspect of our findings. Autophagy is essential for maintaining cellular homeostasis by degrading damaged organelles and protein aggregates, and accumulating evidence indicates that it profoundly influences inflammatory and apoptotic processes across diverse physiological and pathological contexts [20, 35]. In our study, suppression of autophagy caused by FBN1 silencing in fibroblasts led to enhanced secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6) and increased apoptosis (Fig. 4). This is consistent with prior studies showing that impaired autophagy promotes the accumulation of dysfunctional cellular components, elevates reactive oxygen species, and activates inflammasome pathways, thereby amplifying inflammatory mediator release [40]. Furthermore, defective autophagy has been linked to the initiation of apoptotic cascades, contributing to cell death [41]. These processes are particularly relevant in chronic conditions such as diabetic complications, which are characterized by persistent inflammation and dysregulated apoptosis [42]. Within fibroblasts, autophagy serves a dual regulatory role, whereby the activation of autophagy can suppress cytokine expression by targeting pro-IL-1β for degradation and attenuating NF-κB signaling [38, 39], and conversely, when autophagy is affected, cytokine secretion is enhanced, reinforcing pro-inflammatory signaling loops [38–40]. Similarly, the relationship between autophagy and apoptosis is context-dependent: autophagy can promote survival by mitigating cellular stress and removing pro-apoptotic stimuli, but excessive or dysregulated autophagy may also facilitate apoptosis through the depletion of essential cellular constituents [43]. Together, these findings support the concept that autophagy functions as a key checkpoint in fibroblast responses, balancing inflammation, cytokine secretion, and apoptosis under hyperglycemic conditions.

Overall, based our results, FBN1 showed significant potential for clinical applications, particularly in the treatment of HG-induced complications such as DFUs. FBN1 was demonstrated to alleviate fibroblast inflammation and apoptosis, which are key contributors to delayed wound healing in DFUs, by restoring autophagic activity that is typically suppressed in hyperglycemic environments. Additionally, FBN1 promoted fibroblast proliferation while reducing the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby supporting tissue repair and regeneration. These findings suggest that therapies aimed at enhancing FBN1 expression or mimicking its effects could be beneficial in accelerating wound healing in diabetic patients. Moreover, FBN1’s role in modulating autophagy and apoptosis highlights its potential to mitigate the cellular dysfunctions underlying diabetic complications, suggesting FBN1 as a promising therapeutic target for improving clinical outcomes in wound healing and other diabetic pathologies.

In conclusion, this investigation culminates in a holistic comprehension of the involvements of FBN1 within HG-induced fibroblast responses. It elucidates the participation of FBN1 in shaping autophagic retorts, modulating inflammatory dynamics, and regulating apoptotic pathways. The ramifications of these revelations transcend the scope of DFU etiology, permeating the broader spectrum of cellular equilibriums challenged by metabolic stresses. The significance of this study resonates in its discerning dissection of FBN1’s abilities within the context of cellular circuitry. By unmasking the complex intertwinements among FBN1, autophagy, inflammation, and apoptosis, this research augments our cognizance of the molecular mechanism that govern fibroblast responses, holding ramifications not merely for diabetic complications but also broader clinical paradigms entwined with perturbed autophagy dynamics. Yet, inherent limitations should be taken into accounting. The current investigation predominantly stems from an in vitro framework, with inherent complexities of in vivo systems awaiting exploration. Additionally, delineating the precise molecular mechanisms underpinning FBN1 modulatory effects on autophagy, inflammation, and apoptosis remains an uncharted territory. The employment of advanced molecular techniques and model systems holds promise in unraveling these mechanisms.
