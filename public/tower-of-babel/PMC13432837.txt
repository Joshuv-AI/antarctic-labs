Respiratory syncytial virus disease burden in adults aged ≥50 years old: A retrospective national surveillance database analysis in Mexico (2015–2023)
Authors: Eduardo López Ortíz, José Alberto Díaz-Quiñonez, Juan Francisco Román Pedroza, Salma Pacheco Montes, Adriana Guzmán-Holst
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDespite widespread circulation among older adults and those with comorbidities, the burden of respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) in this population remains under-recognized. This study characterized the epidemiology of RSV among adults aged ≥50 years in Mexico. We retrospectively analyzed data from the National Epidemiological Surveillance System (SiNaVE) in Mexico, including laboratory-confirmed RSV cases between 01/01/2015 and 31/12/2023. Descriptive analyses characterized RSV occurrence, age, symptoms, comorbidities, and temporal patterns. Temporal and spatial autocorrelation analyses assessed RSV circulation. Spatial regression models examined associations between structural health determinants and the geographic distribution of RSV cases. We identified 7,958 RSV cases of all ages, of which 9.4% occurred in adults aged ≥50 years. Among these, 60.5% were female patients, and 53.8% had more than one comorbidity, the most common being hypertension and diabetes. Fatality rate was 12.6% among adults aged ≥50 years. RSV positivity was higher among the older age groups and fluctuated over the years, peaking in 2019 and declining in 2020. RSV seasonality was observed, with the highest frequency in October, and broader peaks than those of influenza. Spatial analysis revealed heterogeneous RSV activity across Mexico, with Ciudad de Mexico and Mexico accounting for ~36% of cases. Socioeconomic disadvantage was associated with fewer reported RSV cases. RSV represents a substantial and likely under-recognized burden among Mexican adults. The seasonality and spatial heterogeneity highlight the need for active, timely, and adult-focused RSV surveillance to improve awareness, equitable detection, and targeted prevention strategies, including vaccination.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a leading viral cause of acute respiratory infection worldwide.1 Although historically associated with severe disease in infants, RSV is now recognized as a major pathogen in adults.1 In adults, RSV typically manifests as an upper respiratory tract infection with symptoms like rhinorrhea, headache, fatigue, and fever.1,2 Among older adults or those with comorbidities, such as immunodeficiency or chronic cardiopulmonary conditions, RSV infection more often progresses to the lower respiratory tract.3 This progression can lead to pneumonia or other severe outcomes requiring hospitalization.4–6 Mortality rates are disproportionately higher among older adults and have shown an upward trend in recent years, in contrast to the stable rates seen in younger populations.7

Despite widespread circulation among older adults, RSV remains under-recognized in many countries. In Latin America, epidemiological data on the burden of RSV in adults are limited, although RSV is an established cause of illness contributing to hospitalizations in older adults and those with comorbidities.8–16 In Mexico specifically, the population aged ≥50 years is growing rapidly. According to projections from the National Population Council (CONAPO), adults aged 50 years and older are expected to exceed 25% by 2050.17 This demographic group carries a disproportionate burden of chronic comorbidities – including hypertension, diabetes, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease – that are well-established risk factors for severe RSV outcomes. Demographic shifts toward an aging population and persistent socioeconomic challenges, including high levels of informal employment and unequal access to health services,18,19 may influence susceptibility to and outcomes of respiratory infections such as RSV in densely populated settings.

Within this regional context, Mexico illustrates an imbalance in adult respiratory virus surveillance. While influenza and severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) surveillance systems are well established nationwide,20 comparable routine monitoring for RSV is lacking. In clinical practice, adults presenting with influenza-like illness (ILI) may undergo nasopharyngeal testing for RSV using reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) or immunofluorescence assays. However, testing is not systematic and is primarily driven by influenza-focused surveillance frameworks. Consequently, epidemiological analyses specifically addressing RSV infection in adults in Mexico remain limited and fragmented. Most existing studies on RSV burden in adults have been conducted in high-income countries, and population-based data from Latin America are scarce. Few studies have characterized disease burden, seasonality, or clinical outcomes in Mexico,8,10,11,21 their findings are based on smaller sample size and used older data (2009–2015) or modeling projections. Limited awareness, underdiagnosis, and insufficient surveillance likely contribute to these evidence gaps, thereby hindering effective patient management and prevention strategies.16 In this context, improved understanding of the adult RSV burden is critical, particularly as modeling studies project that this burden could be substantially reduced through RSV vaccination among adults aged ≥60 years in Latin America.21 This assertion is supported by recent meta-analyses demonstrating the real-world effectiveness of RSV vaccines.22,23

This study aimed to describe RSV epidemiology in adults using data from a retrospective surveillance database collected between 2015 and 2023 across all ages in Mexico. The present analysis focuses on participants aged ≥50 years, as this age group represents the population at highest risk for severe RSV outcomes. The data were analyzed according to age, comorbidity, time period, region, and structural health determinants, including marginalization, poverty, access to healthcare, and education.

Materials & methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective observational database study was conducted in Mexico using the National Epidemiological Surveillance System (SiNaVE), specifically the Special Surveillance System for Viral Respiratory Disease (SISVER). The dataset included 2,570,469 laboratory-confirmed cases identified via PCR or other molecular diagnostic techniques within a multistep diagnostic workflow between 01 January 2015 and 31 December 2023. The investigators had full access to the study database and were responsible for data validation, quality control, and analysis in accordance with the study protocol.

Data were extracted from the national surveillance system for all ages meeting the case definition for suspected viral respiratory disease (ILI, acute respiratory infection [ARI], or severe acute respiratory infection [SARI]) across inpatient and outpatient settings in both public and private sectors (Figure 1). The SISVER system operates under a mixed surveillance strategy combining sentinel sampling with universal testing according to clinical and epidemiological context. Approximately 10% of outpatient ILI cases at sentinel units are systematically selected for laboratory testing, while all hospitalized ARI/SARI cases and epidemiologically linked cases are tested universally. Samples were processed using an algorithm designed to detect influenza and SARS-CoV-2. Specimens testing negative for these pathogens were subsequently assessed for other respiratory viruses circulating nationally, including RSV, adenovirus, rhinovirus, and parainfluenza viruses. Case notification in SISVER is syndromic and is based on the operational definition of respiratory infection, while laboratory testing provides pathogen-specific identification in a subset of cases according to the surveillance workflow. For the detection of non-influenza, non-SARS-CoV-2 respiratory viruses, multiplex testing is performed in selected severe acute respiratory infection cases, particularly among hospitalized patients or fatal cases that are negative for COVID-19 and influenza. Although the SISVER platform can record coinfections when identified, coinfections could not be systematically assessed in the dataset used for this study and were therefore not included in the present analysis. For the present analysis, the study population was restricted a priori to adults aged ≥50 years with laboratory-confirmed RSV infection, reflecting the higher clinical relevance of RSV disease burden in this age group and its alignment with current public health and prevention strategies. The study included all eligible laboratory‑confirmed RSV cases meeting inclusion criteria in SISVER during 2015–2023. Outcomes

The primary outcome was the number of RSV cases identified from the SISVER database from 01 January 2015 to 31 December 2023. Case counts were aggregated at the municipal level and used as the basis for descriptive, temporal, and spatial analyses. The database recorded molecular pathogen identification without International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10) coding, as it was not intended for diagnostic purposes. These data were subsequently aggregated for the analysis.

Confirmed respiratory virus cases were classified into six mutually exclusive analytical groups based on the SISVER result variable: (1) “RSV,” comprising all RSV-A, RSV-B, and unsubtyped RSV detections; (2) “Influenza,” comprising influenza A (H1N1pdm09, H3N2, and unsubtyped) and influenza B detections; (3) “SARS-CoV-2,” comprising all variants and lineages; (4) “Parainfluenza,” comprising types 1 through 4; (5) “Rhinovirus/Adenovirus,” comprising enterovirus/rhinovirus combined detections (as recorded by SISVER) and adenovirus – grouped together due to their shared year-round circulation pattern and the surveillance system’s combined reporting of rhinovirus and enterovirus; and (6) “Others,” comprising human metapneumovirus, endemic seasonal coronaviruses (CoV-229E, CoV-OC43, CoV-NL63, CoV-HKU1), and bocavirus, which were grouped due to insufficient individual case volumes for independent analysis. Demographic characteristics (age, grouped into 10-year intervals), clinical symptoms, comorbidities (binary), and severity category (ambulatory, severe, and fatal) were measured for descriptive and comparative analyses. Severity was classified using the SISVER outcome variable, which contains 14 administrative and clinical categories. These were collapsed into three mutually exclusive groups for analysis: (1) ambulatory, comprising all non-severe discharge categories; (2) severe, comprising severe and referred cases; and (3) fatal. Importantly, the SISVER system assigns these categories in a mutually exclusive manner, such that fatal cases are not simultaneously classifiable as severe. The severe category therefore captures non-fatal severe cases only, and the reported fatality rate represents the proportion of fatal cases among all identified cases regardless of severity classification.

Structural determinants of health were derived from the multidimensional poverty indices produced by the National Council for the Evaluation of Social Development Policy (CONEVAL).24 Within this framework, access to healthcare is defined based on formal affiliation or entitlement to a health institution rather than on actual healthcare utilization or effective access. These indices, derived from the National Household Income and Expenditure Survey (ENIGH), provide standardized measures of socioeconomic and structural deprivation across municipalities.25 The indicators included in the analyses were the multidimensional marginalization index, a composite measure of social exclusion capturing deficits in education, housing, income, and public services; poverty percentage, reflecting the proportion of the municipal population living below the national multidimensional poverty threshold as defined by CONEVAL; lack of access to healthcare, defined as the proportion of the population without formal affiliation or entitlement to a health institution; and educational lag, reflecting the proportion of the population aged 15 years or older without completed basic education. All structural indicators were standardized (z-scores) before inclusion in the models to ensure comparability across variables measured on different scales and to facilitate interpretation.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive analyses summarized RSV case rates, age distribution, symptoms, comorbidities, and temporal patterns. To compare clinical characteristics and comorbidity profiles between RSV and other respiratory viruses, a series of mixed-effects logistic regression models were fitted using the glmer function from the lme4 package in R. Each model assessed a single binary outcome (e.g., fever, dyspnea, intensive care unit [ICU] admission) as a function of virus type as a fixed effect, with age group included as a random effect to account for variability in clinical presentation across age strata. Influenza was used as the reference category in all models. Results are reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).

For the time-series analyses, monthly case counts were aggregated for each virus and converted into time-series objects (frequency = 12) that were decomposed using the classical multiplicative method to extract trend, seasonal, and random fluctuations. Correlation matrixes and cross-correlation functions were used to compare seasonal patterns across viruses and evaluate lead – lag relationships relative to RSV.

Spatial analyses began with Moran’s I to quantify spatial autocorrelation in the distribution of RSV cases at the state and municipal levels.26 Local Moran’s I statistics identified areas of significant local clustering (“hot spots” and “cold spots”) and spatial outliers, with significance assessed through permutation testing. To assess the association between structural determinants of health (independent variables) and the geographic distribution of RSV cases (dependent variables), two spatial regression models were used: the spatial lag model (SLM) and the spatial error model (SEM).27,28 The SLM incorporated spatial dependence in the outcome with the measurement of the spatial autoregressive Rho (ρ) parameter. The ρ parameter quantified the magnitude of spatial dependence in the outcome. A significant positive value indicates that municipalities with high RSV case counts tend to be located near others with similarly elevated counts. The SEM accounted for spatial correlation in the residuals, with the Lambda (λ) parameter capturing unmeasured spatially structured influences. The λ parameter represents the strength of spatial dependence in the error term. A significant λ indicates that unmeasured spatial processes influence the outcome. Both spatial models incorporated the standardized structural health determinants as predictors and were fitted using maximum likelihood estimation.29–31 Coefficients represent the change in municipal RSV case counts associated with a one standard deviation increase in each indicator. Results are reported descriptively as associations and should not be interpreted as causal estimates.

Additional secondary analyses assessed the potential influence of surveillance coverage on reported RSV case counts. Annual counts of unique reporting facilities registered in the Mexican health facility registry (Clave Única de Establecimientos de Salud or CLUES) were estimated for each virus type. This evaluation changes in reporting infrastructure and potential biases in observed temporal trends.

Databases were cleaned in compliance with data protection regulations, including consistency checks, duplicate removal, exclusion of variables with >50% missing data, and exclusion of RSV cases without laboratory confirmation or with missing key information (age or date of diagnosis). Missing data were reported, and imputation was performed only for time-series (seasonality) analyses when required.

Ethical considerations

This study used aggregated, de-identified, anonymized data from routinely collected national epidemiological surveillance systems in Mexico. The Dirección General de Epidemiología provided access to the datasets under a formal data-use and confidentiality agreement. Data handling and analysis complied with Mexican regulations on data protection and transparency, including the General Law on the Protection of Personal Data Held by Obligated Subjects and related transparency laws. As the study involved secondary analysis of anonymized surveillance data and did not include direct interaction with human participants, review and approval by an institutional ethics or bioethics committee were not required.

Software and tools

All analyses were conducted in R (version 4.3.2; packages: tidyverse, lubridate, ggplot2, viridis for data wrangling and visualization; stats, forecast for time-series analysis; broom, lme4, nnet, brms, broom.mixed for regression modeling; sf, spdep, tmap for spatial analysis and choropleth mapping).32

Results

Descriptive characteristics of RSV cases

Between 01 January 2015 and 31 December 2023, 7,958 RSV cases across all ages were identified among the 2,570,469 cases recorded in the SISVER database in Mexico. For context, other respiratory viruses identified in the database during the same period were SARS-CoV2 (2,480,142 cases), influenza (73,709 cases), Rhinovirus/Adenovirus (4,760 cases), Parainfluenza (1,387 cases), and other respiratory viruses (2,513 cases).

Among the 7,958 RSV cases of all ages identified, the majority originated from inpatient facilities (80.8%) and 18.3% from outpatient settings, and 0.9% from diagnostic support units. 746 (9.4%) occurred in adults aged ≥50 years. Among patients aged ≥50 years, 60.5% were female, and 401 (53.8%) had more than one comorbidity. The most common comorbidities in patients aged ≥50 years were hypertension (38.5%), diabetes (28.4%), and obesity (13.0%) (Figure 2(A)). Reported symptoms were cough (81.6%), headache (61.9%), fever (51.9%), rhinorrhea (51.7%), dyspnea (47.5%), odynophagia (46.5%), and chest pain (32.6%) (Figure 2(B)). In total, 1.5% of patients were admitted to an intensive care unit, and 3.2% required intubation, with similar magnitudes observed across age groups. The fatality rate was 12.6% and tended to increase with age, ranging from 5.3% (50–59 years) to 22.2% (90–99 years) (Supplementary Figure 1). Mixed-effects logistic regression models comparing RSV to influenza (reference category) revealed a clinical profile that, while distinct in some features, suggests comparable severity burden. RSV cases were significantly less likely to present with fever (OR 0.33, 95% CI 0.32–0.33, p < .001) and showed modestly lower odds of cough (OR 0.83, 95% CI 0.82–0.85, p < .001) compared to influenza. However, RSV cases were substantially more likely to present with dyspnea (OR 2.37, 95% CI 2.30–2.43, p < .001), suggesting a tendency toward lower respiratory tract involvement. RSV cases showed no significant difference in ICU admission rates compared to influenza (OR 0.98, 95% CI 0.89–1.08, p = .710). Taken together, these findings indicate that while RSV may present with a symptom profile that overlaps substantially with other respiratory viruses – making clinical differentiation unreliable without laboratory confirmation – its severity burden is comparable to that of influenza. This underscores the need for specific diagnostic capacity and targeted prevention strategies, particularly in adults aged ≥50 years.

Temporal trends and seasonal patterns

The relative proportion of RSV among confirmed respiratory infections fluctuated over time, peaking in 2019 across all age subgroups ≥50 years (Figure 3(A)). Following the onset of the Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, RSV was minimally detected in 2020 but has increased progressively from 2022 onward. RSV positivity was proportional to age, ranging from 1.0% among adults aged 50–59 years to 9.3% among adults aged 90–99 years in 2019 (Figure 3(A)). Based on monthly RSV positivity, viral circulation exhibited a pronounced seasonal pattern, with a marked increase beginning in July and peaking in October across most studied years (Figure 3(B)). Seasonal decomposition yielded a concordant pattern, with RSV activity increasing in late summer, reaching its highest seasonal component in October (1.9), and then declining steadily through the winter months (0.9 in January, Supplementary Figure 2). In contrast, influenza demonstrated a sharper, well-defined winter peak, typically between December and February, followed by a rapid decline through late spring and summer. Other respiratory viruses exhibited heterogeneous seasonal profiles. SARS-CoV-2 likewise showed weak seasonality, characterized by irregular and non-recurrent seasonal components rather than a consistent annual seasonal signal. Collectively, these observations underscore distinct seasonal behaviors and temporal shifts in dominance among respiratory viruses, with implications for clinical management and public health planning.

Spatial distribution of RSV cases

The states with the highest proportions of RSV cases nationwide were Ciudad de Mexico (21.4%), Mexico (14.9%), and Aguascalientes (12.0%). Together, these three states accounted for nearly half of all reported cases, indicating a concentration of RSV diagnoses in central and more densely populated regions. The choropleth map (Figure 4) illustrates the geographic distribution of RSV cases across Mexican states, showing regions with higher case concentrations and illustrating spatial disparities in case reporting and viral circulation. The global spatial autocorrelation analysis at the state level, using Moran’s I, indicated a positive but non-significant spatial association in the distribution of RSV cases across Mexican states (Moran’s I = 0.1339, p = .064). This finding suggests a weak tendency for neighboring states to exhibit similar RSV case counts. However, the evidence was insufficient to support a statistically significant clustering pattern at this spatial level. Recognizing that state level aggregation may mask local spatial patterns, we extended the analysis to the municipal level for a more granular assessment through local spatial autocorrelation methods. At the municipal level, the global Moran’s I revealed a highly significant spatial autocorrelation (Moran’s I = 0.2107, p < .001), indicating a clear clustering of municipalities with similar RSV case counts across the country.

At the state level, local spatial autocorrelation analysis identified two states with statistically significant patterns. Mexico City exhibited a clustering of high case counts (p = .0285), indicating a localized concentration of high RSV case counts within the municipality and its neighboring areas. In contrast, Morelos was identified as a low-value spatial outlier (p = .0047), reflecting a significantly lower number of cases than expected relative to surrounding municipalities. This finding indicates that the distribution of RSV cases is not random but follows a distinct spatial pattern.

Associations between structural health determinants and RSV case counts

Spatial regression models assessed the associations between municipal RSV case counts and structural socioeconomic indicators, including marginalization, poverty, healthcare access, and educational lag (Figure 5), while accounting for spatial dependence. Multidimensional marginalization index

Spatial autocorrelation analysis revealed that areas with lower marginalization indices reported higher numbers of RSV cases (Figure 5). In the standardized models, the SLM estimated a regression coefficient of 7.55 (95% CI: 4.93 to 10.17, p < .001), whereas the SEM produced a slightly higher coefficient of 8.41 (95% CI: 5.58 to 11.23, p < .001). These findings indicate that municipalities with lower levels of social exclusion experienced a disproportionate burden of RSV infections. Moreover, the spatial dependence parameters (ρ in SLM: 0.17, 95% CI: 0.09 to 0.26; λ in SEM: 0.18, 95% CI: 0.09 to 0.26) show that RSV cases cluster geographically, with adjacent municipalities exhibiting similar transmission dynamics.

Poverty

Poverty was negatively associated with RSV case counts, with coefficients of −2.68 (95% CI: −3.40 to −1.87, p < .001) in SLM and −3.65 (95% CI: −4.60 to −2.62, p < .001) in SEM (Figure 5). This finding shows that municipalities with higher poverty levels reported fewer RSV cases. Spatial dependence parameters were positive (ρ in SLM: 0.31, 95% CI: 0.25 to 0.37; λ in SEM: 0.31, 95% CI: 0.26 to 0.37), indicating strong spatial autocorrelation, with adjacent municipalities exhibiting similar RSV dynamics. This pattern suggests that RSV case reporting is influenced by geographic clustering.

Lack of access to healthcare

No significant association was observed between lack of healthcare access and RSV case counts (Figure 5). The coefficients were −0.07 (95% CI: −0.80 to 0.73, p = .85) in SLM and −0.20 (95% CI: −1.10 to 0.71, p = .65) in SEM. However, spatial dependence remains evident (ρ in SLM: 0.34, 95% CI: 0.28 to 0.40; λ in SEM: 0.34, 95% CI: 0.28 to 0.40), suggesting that RSV cases continue to exhibit strong geographic clustering regardless of healthcare access levels.

Educational lag

Educational lag is defined as a delay or slow progress in educational attainment within a population (Figure 5). We found that higher educational lag significantly correlated with fewer reported RSV cases. The SLM estimated a coefficient of −4.05 (95% CI: −5.00 to −3.08, p < .001), while the SEM yielded a coefficient of −3.21 (95% CI: −4.00 to −2.40, p < .001). Spatial dependence was strong in both models (ρ in SLM: 0.31, 95% CI: 0.25 to 0.36; λ in SEM: 0.31, 95% CI: 0.25 to 0.37), indicating clustering of RSV cases across municipalities.

Assessment of health surveillance coverage

To explore how surveillance efforts may influence reported respiratory virus case counts, we analyzed the number of CLUES that reported cases each year by virus type. In 2019, the pre-pandemic peak in RSV cases was observed (91 facilities reporting 415 cases), followed by a dramatic decline in 2020, despite the number of reporting units remaining similar to or slightly higher than in previous years (86 facilities reporting 130 cases). A resurgence in RSV cases coincided with increases in the number of reporting facilities in 2021 (283 facilities reporting 1,840 cases) and 2022 (420 facilities reporting 2,323 cases) (Supplementary Figure 3).

Discussion

Key results and interpretation

This retrospective observational database study provides novel epidemiological evidence on the burden of RSV among adults aged ≥50 years in Mexico, addressing a critical gap in national surveillance data.20 The nature of the surveillance system has important implications for interpreting our results. First, RSV case counts in SISVER are predominantly derived from hospitalized patients, meaning that our dataset is enriched for severe disease and likely underrepresents mild or moderate outpatient RSV illness. This is consistent with and partially explains the relatively high fatality rate observed in our cohort, which should be interpreted as an in-hospital or severity-enriched estimate rather than a population-level case fatality rate. Second, the differential surveillance intensity across virus types – particularly the more robust outpatient sentinel network for influenza compared to RSV – means that direct comparisons of absolute case counts between viruses should be made cautiously. Accordingly, our analysis of the SISVER surveillance data showed that influenza accounted for the majority of laboratory-confirmed respiratory virus cases during the pre-pandemic years (2015–2019). With the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020, SARS-CoV-2 rapidly became the dominant pathogen, reflecting both an epidemiological shift and the reallocation of diagnostic capacity toward pandemic control.33–36 Our findings showed that the proportion of RSV cases among confirmed respiratory infections fluctuated over time, peaking in 2019 (before the COVID-19 pandemic) across all age subgroups ≥50 years.

Among the RSV cases identified in our study, 9.4% occurred in adults aged ≥50 years, and approximately half of them had more than one comorbidity, most commonly diabetes and hypertension. This underscores the substantial burden of RSV in vulnerable populations affected by comorbidities that may exacerbate disease severity.37,38 RSV-related mortality was concentrated among older adults, as previously reported in Mexico, where RSV has been associated with one of the highest hospitalization rates among respiratory viruses.8,9,12

Our temporal analysis showed that RSV activity was markedly reduced during periods of intense SARS-CoV-2 circulation and rebounded substantially after the pandemic. This trend may reflect relaxed mitigation measures, increased population susceptibility after limited pandemic exposure, or altered immune responses following prior SARS-CoV-2 infection.39,40 These findings are consistent with the increase in CLUES reported in 2019, followed by a marked decline in 2020 and a resurgence after 2021. This pattern likely reflects an epidemiological shift in which the transmission niche previously occupied by seasonal respiratory pathogens was temporarily displaced by SARS-CoV-2. The subsequent resurgence in RSV cases coincided with declines in SARS-CoV-2 circulation after 2021.

SARS-CoV-2 circulation appeared to be driven by pandemic waves, public health measures, and variant emergence rather than by seasonal factors. In contrast, influenza and RSV both exhibited winter seasonality; although influenza activity tended to peak earlier in the winter months, while RSV circulation often extended later into the season and increased as influenza activity declined.

The RSV seasonality observed in our study in Mexico is consistent with patterns reported in tropical and subtropical regions, where RSV epidemics often precede winter and show greater heterogeneity in timing than in temperate climates.41,42 In this context, accounting for interactions among co-circulating respiratory viruses may improve preparedness and resource planning, highlighting the need to include RSV alongside influenza in seasonal strategies.

We also showed that RSV activity exhibited spatial heterogeneity across Mexico. Ciudad de Mexico and Mexico, two densely populated urban municipalities, accounted for approximately 36% of patients with RSV. This unequal distribution likely reflects differences in surveillance and reporting capacity beyond population size, whereas lower incidence in peripheral areas such as Morelos may indicate under-detection rather than absence of disease. To explore the underlying drivers of these spatial patterns, we incorporated structural health determinants at the municipal level into two spatial models. Both models revealed an inverse association between socioeconomic vulnerability indicators – including marginalization, poverty, and educational lag – and reported RSV case counts at the municipal level, whereby municipalities with worse socioeconomic conditions reported fewer cases. This pattern contrasts with prior reports of higher respiratory virus transmission in rural and marginalized settings, and warrants careful interpretation.43 This ecological association is derived from municipal-level data, and the study design cannot establish causality or identify the mechanisms driving this pattern. At least three non-mutually exclusive explanations are plausible. First, differential surveillance and diagnostic capacity, whereby marginalized municipalities have fewer sentinel reporting units and less diagnostic infrastructure, can reduce case ascertainment – a pattern supported by our surveillance coverage analysis. Second, wealthier municipalities in Mexico tend to be more urban and densely populated, which independently increases both transmission probability and healthcare-seeking behavior. Third, differential healthcare-seeking patterns related to socioeconomic factors can influence whether symptomatic individuals access formal surveillance-linked facilities. Regardless of the underlying mechanism, the consistent spatial clustering and the inverse association with structural vulnerability indicators suggest that reported RSV burden in Mexico is unlikely to reflect true disease distribution equitably across the territory. These findings underscore the need for active, equity-oriented surveillance strategies that extend beyond sentinel units in urban centers.

Of note, the non-significant association between RSV case counts and lack of access to healthcare should be interpreted in the context of how access is operationalized within the multidimensional poverty framework developed by CONEVAL. In this framework, access to health services is defined primarily by formal affiliation or entitlement to medical care. As acknowledged in CONEVAL’s methodological guidelines, this indicator does not capture other critical dimensions of access, including geographic proximity to health facilities, timeliness of care, quality and effectiveness of services received, or local diagnostic capacity. These conceptual and methodological limitations may partly explain the absence of a statistically significant association between this indicator and reported RSV case counts.

Limitations

A key limitation of this study is its reliance on epidemiological surveillance notifications rather than clinical records, which may reduce data granularity and lead to misclassification of disease severity. Consequently, some observations may not fully meet expectations for clinically curated datasets, particularly for individual variables such as comorbidities or symptom duration. As described in the methods, the SISVER system assigns outcome categories in a mutually exclusive manner, such that fatal cases are not simultaneously classifiable as severe; the severity and fatality analyses should be interpreted in this context. Also, relying solely on case-based surveillance can lead to significant underreporting as observed with COVID-19 in Indonesia.44 Furthermore, the surveillance workflow does not systematically assess viral coinfections as only selected severe acute respiratory infection cases negative for SARS-CoV-2 and influenza were tested using a multiplex panel. This limitation has specific implications for our findings. Reported fatality rates may not reflect RSV-attributable mortality exclusively, as coinfecting pathogens in fatal cases cannot be identified or excluded. Severity comparisons between RSV and influenza, including ICU admission rates and dyspnea odds ratios, may be influenced by differential coinfection prevalence between virus groups, which cannot be assessed in our data. Furthermore, RSV cases presenting as coinfections attributed to other pathogens would not appear in our case counts, potentially contributing to underestimation of true RSV burden. These considerations reinforce the need for surveillance systems capable of multiplex pathogen detection, particularly as RSV vaccination programs for older adults are introduced and accurate burden estimation becomes essential for program evaluation. Finally, we must acknowledge that sociodemographic and clinical characterizations were conducted using individual-level data, whereas structural determinants were derived from municipal level estimates. As a result, these analyses operate at different spatial scales, and the interpretation of the spatial regression models is ecological rather than causal. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted with caution to avoid ecological fallacy. Nevertheless, the plausibility of the observed associations and their consistency with known structural conditions in the country support the robustness and overall coherence of the study’s findings.

Conclusion

Overall, our findings align with previous epidemiological evidence from Latin America, indicating a substantial RSV burden among older adults and those with comorbidities.8–16 In Mexico, RSV was estimated to cause 8,651 hospitalizations and 3,806 deaths in 2019, with the majority occurring in adults aged ≥65 years.12 Among the limited number of studies conducted over a comparable period, reported age-related patterns are broadly consistent with our observations. However, differences in surveillance design, case definition, and study populations limit direct comparisons of estimates across studies.8–16,43,45

Our seasonality and spatial analyses have revealed a predictable pattern of RSV resurgence, underscoring the importance of timely, virus-specific surveillance to improve disease awareness among both patients and healthcare professionals. Strengthened surveillance is crucial to improve case reporting, support preparedness efforts, and inform the implementation of adult vaccination strategies that could alleviate the burden caused by RSV in older adults. In addition, disparities in RSV detection, such as fewer cases identified in marginalized or high-poverty populations, highlight potential gaps in RSV surveillance for these vulnerable populations. These findings emphasize the critical role of robust adult RSV surveillance in reducing disease impact and supporting equitable prevention strategies in Mexico. Finally, the use of surveillance data also emphasized the key limitations of surveillance programs, demonstrating the need for future researches on the epidemiology and burden of RSV among the adult population.
