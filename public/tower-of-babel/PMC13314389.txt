Comparison of Lignan Contents and Antioxidant Activities in Five Perilla (Perilla frutescens) Korean Cultivars
Authors: Yoonjeong Kim, Jihwan Kim, Min Young Kim, Jung-In Kim, Younghwa Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe analyzed five Korean perilla (Perilla frutescens) cultivars for their lignan contents and antioxidant activities. Lignans were measured using liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry; six compounds were detected: secoisolariciresinol, matairesinol, syringaresinol, lariciresinol, medioresinol, and pinoresinol. The total lignan content differed among cultivars, with Anyu having the highest level at 88.639 µg/100 g; among them, syringaresinol was predominant, whereas several others were not detected. The antioxidant activity also varied among cultivars; Dayu and Gayeon had higher polyphenols and flavonoids than the others. Dayu and Neulbora showed the highest 2,2'-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) diammonium salt radical-scavenging activity, whereas Neulbora exhibited the strongest 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl radical-scavenging activity. Correlation analysis indicated that antioxidant activity was not robustly associated with lignan content and was only weakly related to total phenolic and flavonoid contents. These results suggest that the antioxidant capacity of perilla seeds may be influenced by multiple components rather than by lignans or phenolic compounds alone.Keywords: antioxidants, correlation of data, lignans, perilla, seeds

INTRODUCTION

Perilla frutescens has been cultivated in East Asia for centuries; it is an important oilseed crop in Korea. Traditional interest in perilla seeds has focused on their lipid composition, particularly the high α-linolenic acid content, in relation to nutritional and cardiovascular health (Simopoulos, 2002; Asif, 2011). In addition to lipids, perilla seeds contain several non-lipids, including phenolic acids and flavonoids. Rosmarinic acid is consistently the predominant phenolic, while caffeic acid, ferulic acid, and various flavonoids are in smaller amounts (Ma et al., 2024). Studies have mainly examined these compounds for their antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities, and others for potential neuroprotective effects (Kwak and Ju, 2015; Hashimoto et al., 2021; Lee and Shin, 2024). Other reported bioeffects, such as on allergic responses or cancer-related processes, are largely based on experimental models and are generally discussed in relation to oxidative stress (Makino et al., 2003; Kim et al., 2021).

Antioxidant capacity is a common indicator of the functional properties of plant-derived foods (Prior et al., 2005). Perilla has long been consumed as part of the traditional diet, and its seeds contain a variety of bioactive compounds. Previous studies have largely focused on its unsaturated fatty acid composition and associated bioeffects (Asif, 2011). In addition, phenolics, including rosmarinic and caffeic acids, contribute to antioxidant activity in perilla seeds (Lee and Cho, 2021). Comparatively, less attention has been given to the overall antioxidant capacity of perilla, which reflects the combined effects of multiple constituents rather than the activity of a single compound. Additionally, the phytochemical composition of perilla varies depending on the cultivar and growth-related factors, including cultivation conditions and harvest stage (Lee et al., 2013; Ha et al., 2015). The selection of specific cultivars is nutritionally and functionally meaningful, as differences in genetic background induced pronounced variations in the accumulation of bioactives, ultimately influencing their health-promoting potential. In Korea, perilla cultivars include commercially bred varieties and local landraces, which differ in agronomic traits such as yield, seed composition, and adaptability, thereby contributing to the food system and breeding programs. These cultivar-specific differences may also result in distinct antioxidant profiles, including lignans and phenolics, which are crucial determinants of functional quality. Therefore, comparing antioxidant capacities among cultivars is relevant from a preventive nutrition perspective, as it provides a basis for identifying varieties with enhanced health benefits and supports their informed selection for dietary use and crop improvement. Lignans represent a class of phenolics commonly found in oilseeds and have been associated with various bioactivities (Gerstenmeyer et al., 2013). Lignans are dimers of two phenylpropanoid units and encompass a range of compounds with diverse substitution patterns and stereochemical configurations (Durazzo et al., 2018). Due to their structural variations, lignans encompass a broad range of compounds, among which secoisolariciresinol (Seco), matairesinol (Mat), lariciresinol (Lar), pinoresinol (Pin), medioresinol (Med), and syringaresinol (Syr) are considered the most frequently reported lignans in plant-based foods (Rodríguez-García et al., 2019). These compounds are widely distributed among oilseeds, such as flaxseed and sesame, as well as cereals and vegetables, although their composition and relative abundance vary depending on species (Berenshtein et al., 2024). However, compared with other phenolic constituents, lignans in perilla seeds have been relatively investigated less, particularly regarding their variation among cultivars and their relationship with antioxidant activity. Studies on perilla have largely focused on fatty acid composition or the profiles of common phenolics, whereas quantitative information on individual lignans across cultivars remains limited. Although lignans are recognized bioactive compounds, their contribution to the antioxidant activity of perilla seeds has not been clearly established. The six lignans selected in this study－Seco, Mat, Lar, Pin, Syr, and Med－are the major plant lignans commonly quantified in foods and metabolomics; they represent crucial intermediates and derivatives of the lignan biosynthetic pathway. Therefore, their simultaneous analysis enables a comprehensive yet targeted evaluation of the lignan composition of perilla seeds. Although the lipid composition and functional attributes of perilla have been studied extensively, relatively few investigations have compared antioxidant activity and lignan profiles among perilla cultivars. Therefore, this study was conducted to characterize the lignan content of seeds from five commercially available and local landrace cultivars of P. frutescens cultivated in South Korea and evaluate its relationship with antioxidant activity alongside other major bioactive compounds, including total phenolics and flavonoids.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials and chemicals

Seeds from five perilla cultivars, Anyu, Dayu, Gayeon, Namcheon, and Neulbora, cultivated in the Miryang region and harvested in November 2024, were provided by the Rural Development Administration (National Institute of Crop and Food Science). Syr and Med were purchased from ChemFaces. Seco, Mat, Lar, and Pin were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Acetonitrile was purchased from Merck, and methanol was bought from Honeywell Burdick & Jackson. Gallic acid, Folin-ciocalteu reagent, 2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH), 2,2’-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) diammonium salt (ABTS), and ethanol were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. All chemicals and solvents were of analytical grade.

Liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) quantitative analysis of six lignans in perilla seeds

The extraction protocol of six lignans was adapted from Kim et al. (2024a). Briefly, a 0.1 g sample of ground perilla seeds was mixed with 1 mL of 84% methanol and extracted using a WUC-D10H ultrasonic water bath (Daihan Scientific) at 40°C for 1 h. The extract was then centrifuged at 25,160 g, and the supernatant was passed through a 0.22 µm nylon membrane filter before analysis. Lignans were subsequently analyzed by LC-MS/MS using an LCMS-8050 high-performance triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Shimadzu) operated in negative ion mode with multiple reaction monitoring (Table 1).

Determination of the total polyphenol contents (TPCs)

The TPCs was measured according to the protocol of Kim et al. (2024a). The A750 was read using a spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific Ltd.). Gallic acid was used for calibration, and TPC was expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents per g of sample.

Determination of the total flavonoid contents (TFCs)

The TFCs was determined according to the method of Hamdy et al. (2009). The A510 was assessed. Catechin served as the standard, and the contents were expressed as mg catechin equivalents (CE) per g of sample.

Determination of free radical-scavenging activity

The ABTS scavenging activity was assessed following the method of Re et al. (1999). A735 was measured using a spectrophotometer. The DPPH radical-scavenging activity was determined according to the procedure originally described by Blois (1958). After the reaction, the A520 was ascertained. Antioxidant activity was expressed as mg CE per g of sample.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean±standard deviation from at least three independent determinations. Statistical analysis was conducted using one-way analysis of variance in SAS software (version 9.0, SAS Institute). Statistical significance of the differences among the means was evaluated using Duncan’s multiple range test at P<0.05. Pearson correlation analysis was performed using SPSS (version 24.0, IBM Corp.) to assess the associations between the total lignan content and antioxidant activity.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Lignan contents in five perilla cultivars

The lignan contents of five perilla cultivars were determined (Table 2). The chromatograms of the analyzed lignan standards are presented in Fig. 1. Lar, Pin, and Syr were detected in all cultivars, whereas Seco, Mat, and Med were not identified. The total lignan content differed significantly among cultivars, ranging from 30.910 to 88.639 µg/100 g. Anyu exhibited the highest total lignan content, followed by Namcheon and Neulbora, and the least by Gayeon. Overall, the lignan content of perilla seeds was relatively low compared with oilseeds known to contain substantially elevated lignan levels, such as sesame and flaxseed. Flaxseed (Linum usitatissimum L.) is recognized as a rich dietary source of lignans, primarily as Seco diglucoside (Rodríguez-García et al., 2019). The total lignan content of flaxseeds ranged from several 100 mg to >1 g per 100 g, which is considerably greater than that of other oilseeds (Rothwell et al., 2013). As an oilseed crop, sesame (Sesamum indicum L.) has been extensively investigated, and its lignan composition is well documented. Enhanced levels of sesamin and sesamolin have been reported, with total lignan contents typically ranging from several 100 mg per 100 g of seed (Kim et al., 2024b). In this study, the lignan levels detected in perilla seeds were substantially lower, remaining at µg/100 g. Studies on perilla seeds have primarily focused on phenolic acids and flavonoids as contributors to antioxidant activity (Dossou et al., 2023), and information regarding lignans remains limited. Although lignans were detected in all cultivars, their concentrations were low, and cultivar-dependent variations were observed. Compared with sesame or flaxseed, perilla contains markedly lower amounts of lignans. However, these compounds may still contribute to the overall antioxidant profile of the seeds.

Phenolic contents and radical-scavenging activities

Perilla seed TPCs and TFCs differed among the five cultivars (Table 3). The TPC ranged from 2.765 to 8.702 mg GAE/g. The highest level was observed in Dayu (8.702 mg GAE/g), followed by Gayeon (8.209 mg GAE/g), whereas Neulbora exhibited the lowest content. The TFC showed a similar trend, with Gayeon and Dayu containing 7.447 and 6.870 mg CE/g, respectively, higher among all cultivars. Antioxidant activity was evaluated using ABTS and DPPH scavenging assays (Table 4). In the ABTS assay, Dayu (2.649 mg CE/g) and Neulbora (2.601 mg CE/g) demonstrated relatively robust free radical-scavenging activity compared with the other cultivars. In the DPPH assay, Neulbora exhibited the highest activity (3.005 mg CE/g), followed by Dayu (2.525 mg CE/g). Notably, Neulbora showed elevated radical-scavenging activity despite its comparatively low TPCs and TFCs. This finding suggests that the antioxidant activity of perilla seeds is not based solely on the TPC. Several phenolic constituents have been identified in perilla seeds and may contribute uniquely to antioxidant behavior. Rosmarinic acid is a major phenolic acid in seeds and leaves and has been associated with radical-scavenging and metal-chelating activities (Deguchi and Ito, 2020b). Caffeic acid, ferulic acid, and p-coumaric acid have also been detected in perilla seeds (Deguchi and Ito, 2020a). Flavonoids such as luteolin, apigenin, and their glycoside derivatives have been reported in perilla and related species, primarily in studies investigating antioxidant-dependent responses (Dossou et al., 2023). These compounds are considered a part of the phenolic profile of the samples analyzed in this study.

Correlation among total lignans, polyphenols, and antioxidant activities

Pearson correlation analysis examined the associations between bioactive compounds and antioxidant parameters in perilla seeds (Table 5). A robust positive correlation was observed between TPC and TFC (r=0.975, P<0.01), indicating that flavonoids constituted a substantial proportion of the TPCs among the cultivars examined. ABTS and DPPH scavenging activities were also positively correlated (r=0.942, P<0.05). In contrast, total lignan content was negatively correlated with ABTS and DPPH scavenging activities (r=−0.683 and −0.621, respectively). The free radical-scavenging activity of lignans varies with their chemical structure and is often lower than that of other phenolics, such as flavonoids (Eklund et al., 2005; Polat Kose and Gulcin, 2021). However, this difference alone does not explain the negative correlations observed in the present study, suggesting that additional factors, such as variations in the compositional profiles among cultivars, may also be involved. The correlations between TPC or TFC and radical-scavenging activities were weak and statistically insignificant, with correlation coefficients ranging from −0.022 to −0.350. These results suggest that the antioxidant capacity of perilla seeds may not be strongly associated with total lignans, polyphenols, or flavonoids alone and may reflect the contribution of additional components with antioxidant activities. In the present study, the free radical-scavenging activity of certain cultivars, including Neulbora, was high despite relatively low polyphenol and flavonoid contents, indicating that antioxidant activity cannot be fully explained by phenolic contents. The lignan levels were low, which may limit their contribution to the overall antioxidant capacity relative to other components. Nevertheless, an analysis of lignans remains relevant, as these compounds are structurally distinct from other phenolics and their occurrence in perilla seeds has been characterized only to a limited extent. The present results suggested that variations in lignan contents among cultivars were not clearly associated with differences in radical-scavenging activity, suggesting that lignans may not be a major determinant of antioxidant capacity under the conditions examined. Overall, antioxidant activity in perilla seeds appears to reflect the combined effects of compounds belonging to multiple classes under the conditions examined in this study.

In this study, lignan content and antioxidant activity were evaluated in five perilla cultivars. Anyu exhibited the highest total lignan content, with Syr detected at a notably greater level, whereas other lignans were not detected. Gayeon and Dayu showed the maximum TPCs and TFCs. Dayu and Neulbora demonstrated the strongest ABTS radical-scavenging activity, while Neulbora exhibited the highest DPPH scavenging activity. Correlation analysis indicated that such scavenging activity was more closely associated with TPC than with lignan content. Within the observed range, lignan content showed negative correlations with ABTS and DPPH radical-scavenging activities.
