Phase Transition and Censorship Principle for Holographic Casimir Effect
Zhen-Rui Huang, Rong-Xin Miao

Abstract
This paper explores the holographic Casimir effect associated with parallel spherical defects. The gravity dual is dominated by the AdS soliton with connected EOW branes at small widths, transitioning to AdS space with disconnected EOW branes as the width increases. Consequently, the holographic Casimir effect undergoes a first-order phase transition and vanishes in the disconnected phase. This new behavior highlights a significant difference from free theories, holographic parallel plane defects, and hyperbolic defects. Additionally, we confirm that the free theories adhere to the holographic bound for the Casimir effect in the case of parallel spherical defects. Interestingly, we observe that cosmic censorship provides a holographic interpretation of the attractive nature of the Casimir force when identical boundary conditions are imposed on both parallel surfaces. The repulsive Casimir force is associated with the bulk spacetime containing a naked singularity, which is generally considered forbidden. Additionally, we argue that topological censorship offers a natural explanation for why the vacuum of parallel defects is dual to the AdS soliton.

We are interested in the holographic Casimir effect for two parallel defects (PD), including plane, hyperbolic, and spherical defects. The metric is given by

\displaystyle ds_{\text{PD}}^{2}=dx^{2}+R^{2}\Big(d\theta^{2}+\frac{\sin(\sqrt{k}\theta)^{2}}{k}d\Omega_{d-2}^{2}\Big),\ \ \ \ 0\leq x\leq L.

Here,

By examining the symmetry of the geometry, the energy conservation condition
\nabla_{i}\langle T^{ij}\rangle=0

\displaystyle\langle T^{i}_{\ j}\rangle=\frac{\kappa_{1}}{L^{d}}\text{diag}\Big(-(d-1),1,\dots,1\Big)+\Big(\text{anomalous terms}\sim\frac{A}{R^{d}}\Big),

\displaystyle\lim_{R\to\infty}\kappa_{1}|_{k\in\{-1,1\}}=\kappa_{1}|_{k=0}.

Remarkably, the Casimir effect vanishes in the disconnected phase. It is noteworthy that free theories do not exhibit such a phase transition or this vanishing at finite width, highlighting key differences in the Casimir effect between free and strongly coupled theories.

Furthermore, we observe novel relationships between the censorship principle and the holographic Casimir effect. We find that cosmic censorship prevents the divergent Casimir effect and provides a holographic explanation for the attractive Casimir force. The repulsive Casimir force is dual to a spacetime with a naked singularity, which should be ruled out. Additionally, topological censorship offers a natural resolution to the discrepancy concerning the AdS soliton: while the bulk exhibits periodicity, the AdS boundary does not.

The paper is organized as follows: In Section 2, we examine the gravity dual of parallel spherical defects and derive the holographic lower bound for the corresponding Casimir effect. Section 3 discusses the holographic phase transition associated with parallel spherical defects. As the width

In Section 4, we examine the constraints on the Casimir effect imposed by the cosmic and topological censorships. Section 5 compares the Casimir effects in free theories and holographic theories. Notably, we confirm that the AdS/BCFT with minimal brane tension produces a smaller ratio of

3
Holographic phase transition

In this section, we investigate the holographic phase transition of the Casimir effect concerning parallel spherical defects. In the previous section, we observed a puzzling feature of the AdS soliton solution with
c_{1}>0
: the boundary width
L
cannot exceed a maximum value,
L_{\max}
. This is illustrated in Fig.

The dominant saddle corresponds to the one with the lower free energy or, equivalently, a smaller renormalized Euclidean action.

We propose that the second saddle is represented by AdS space with disconnected end-of-the-world (EOW) branes, which we refer to as the disconnected phase. Conversely, we call the AdS soliton with connected EOW branes the connected phase. In the disconnected phase, we have
c_{1}=0
, whereas in the connected phase,
c_{1}>0
.
The case of
c_{1}<0
is unphysical and will be discussed further in Section 4.
As we increase the boundary width
L
, a first-order phase transition occurs, resulting in a discontinuous pressure. Notably, the pressure vanishes in the disconnected phase, indicating that there is no Casimir effect for sufficiently large widths. This behavior is quite distinct from that observed in free theories and is a unique characteristic of the Casimir effect in strongly coupled field theories with a gravity dual.

Disconnected phase

\displaystyle I_{\mathrm{ren}}

\displaystyle=-\int_{N}\!\mathrm{d}^{4}X\sqrt{G}\,(R_{N}-2\Lambda)-2\int_{M}\!\mathrm{d}^{3}x\sqrt{g}\Bigl(K-2-\frac{1}{2}R_{M}\Bigr)

4
Censorship principle

In this section, we discover that various censorship principles impose novel constraints on the holographic Casimir effect.
They prohibit the singular Casimir effect and clarify why the Casimir force is attractive when the same boundary conditions are applied to both parallel surfaces. Additionally, it provides insight into why the AdS soliton is a reasonable gravitational dual for parallel defects, despite its puzzling properties. Specifically, while the soliton exhibits periodicity in the bulk, it lacks periodicity at the AdS boundary.
For simplicity, we will use holographic parallel spherical defects as an example in the following discussion. The conclusions drawn here also apply to parallel plane and hyperbolic defects.

4.1
Cosmic censorship

Let us first explain why the vacuum of a parallel spherical defect is dual to an AdS soliton. Based on an analysis of symmetries, the Weyl anomaly, and energy conservation, we find that the vacuum expectation values of stress tensors take the form (
2
) for parallel defects in the Introduction. With the CFT stress tensors (
2
), we can reconstruct the asymptotically AdS spacetime in the Fefferman–Graham gauge. For further details, see
deHaro:2000vlm
. It turns out that the asymptotic metric near the AdS boundary is equivalent to that of the AdS soliton (
10
). This equivalence is most easily observed for odd dimensions
d
, where the bulk Weyl anomaly vanishes. For simplicity, we focus on
d=3
below, but the conclusions apply to general dimensions.

The discussions above indicate that the vacuum of parallel spherical defects is dual to the AdS-soliton (
10
) with the metric function

\displaystyle h(z)=1+z^{2}-c_{1}z^{3},

(50)

where we focus on
R=1
and
d=3
. Here,
c_{1}
is an arbitrary constant related to the Casimir amplitude, denoted as
\kappa_{1}=c_{1}L^{3}
. In Section 3, we propose that the metric with
c_{1}>0
serves as the dominant saddle for small widths
L\leq L_{\text{crit}}
, while the metric with
c_{1}=0
is the dominant saddle for large widths
L\geq L_{\text{crit}}
. A natural question arises: why do we not consider the case where
c_{1}<0
? In fact, the metric with
c_{1}<0
results in the smallest free energy among the three cases. Therefore, this option appears to be the most favored gravity dual for parallel spherical defects.

Let us examine the renormalized Euclidean action for the case where
c_{1}<0
and
d=3
. For simplicity, we will focus on the scenario involving tensionless EOW branes, meaning
\rho=0
. The analysis for cases with tension does not alter the conclusion.
From (
3.2
) with
T=2\tanh(\rho)=0
, we derive

\displaystyle I_{\text{ren}}(c_{1}<0)=4\pi c_{1}L,

(51)

which is unbounded from below as
c_{1}\to-\infty
. Therefore, this represents the dominant saddle point across all widths. However, it leads to a singular Casimir effect, specifically

\kappa_{1}=c_{1}L^{3}\to-\infty
, which is physically unacceptable.

Fortunately, such a pathological solution is prohibited by the principle of cosmic censorship
Penrose:1969pc
;
Penrose123
. This principle asserts that nature does not favor naked singularities; all singularities should be hidden behind a horizon. The solution with
c_{1}<0
exhibits a naked singularity as
z\to\infty
. To illustrate this, we consider the Kretschmann scalar for
d=3
:

\displaystyle R_{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}R^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}=24+12c_{1}^{2}z^{6},

(52)

which diverges as
z\to\infty
. Moreover, for
c_{1}<0
, the metric function

\displaystyle h(z)=1+z^{2}-c_{1}z^{3}>0

(53)

has no zeros, indicating that the singularity is naked rather than concealed behind the horizon (where
h(z_{h})=0
). Consequently, we must exclude this case from the set of physical solutions.

The reflection positivity of field theories requires an attractive Casimir force if we impose the same boundary conditions on both parallel surfaces
Bachas:2006ti
;
Diatlyk:2024qpr
. As a byproduct, we obtain a holographic interpretation of this phenomenon. Recall that the holographic Casimir pressure is given by
T_{nn}=-(d-1)c_{1}
. The requirement of cosmic censorship dictates that
c_{1}\geq 0
, thereby resulting in an attractive force between the two parallel defects. It is important to note that although the solution with
c_{1}=0
has no horizon, its Kretschmann scalar (
52
) is finite, yielding no naked singularities. Now, let’s clarify what it means to impose the same boundary conditions on the two boundaries in AdS/BCFT. The tension
T
can be interpreted as the gravitational dual of the boundary conditions since it influences the boundary central charges in the same way as the boundary condition itself. It means that the EOW branes terminating at both boundaries must have the same tension. In the connected phase, where
c_{1}>0
, this condition is automatically satisfied because only one EOW brane is present. In contrast, for the disconnected phase with
c_{1}=0
, we need to ensure that the two disconnected EOW branes also possess the same tension.

In summary, cosmic censorship prevents the divergent Casimir effect and offers a holographic explanation for the attractive Casimir force within the context of AdS/BCFT.

4.2
Topological censorship

Let us discuss an intriguing aspect of the gravity dual of parallel defects. For smaller widths, the dual geometry is represented by a portion of the AdS soliton, as illustrated in Fig.
1
. To eliminate the conical singularity in the bulk, the coordinate
x
must have a period
\beta
(
13
). However, in the context of the gravity dual for parallel defects,
x
is defined within the range
0\leq x\leq L
instead of exhibiting periodicity
x\simeq x+\beta
at the AdS boundary. This discrepancy is indeed puzzling.

As illustrated in Fig.
1
, for negative brane tension
T\leq 0
, the gravity dual is represented by the region between the EOW brane (shown as the blue curve) and the AdS boundary (denoted by the green line). In this scenario, the horizon (depicted as the black line) is hidden behind the EOW brane and is not part of the gravity dual. Therefore, the conical singularity at the horizon is irrelevant. Conversely, for positive brane tension
T\geq 0
, the gravity dual encompasses the complement of the region with negative brane tension. Here, the horizon is included in the gravity dual, which raises questions about the aforementioned discrepancy.

Interestingly, this issue can be resolved by the topological censorship
Witten:1999xp
;
Galloway:1999br
, which asserts that disconnected regions of CFTs cannot correspond to a connected region in the bulk if the null energy condition holds. Assuming there is no periodicity in the bulk, and the two dotted vertical lines shown in Fig.
1
are not identified, the gravity dual with
T>0
presents a counterexample to the principle of topological censorship: the two disconnected boundary regions (red lines) correspond to a connected bulk region. However, if there is a periodicity in the bulk and the two dotted vertical lines are identified, this creates connections between the two boundary regions (red lines), aligning the geometry with the topological censorship principle. In this scenario, a connected region of CFTs is dual to a connected region in the bulk.

Next, we will discuss the disconnected phase at large widths, where a portion of AdS space with two disconnected EOW branes gives the gravity dual. See Fig.
6
(a). Considering the complement of this geometry, the existence of the disconnected EOW branes leads to two disconnected CFT regions that are dual to two disconnected bulk regions, which complies with the principle of topological censorship.

In summary, we propose that topological censorship offers a natural resolution to the discrepancy regarding the AdS soliton: the bulk exhibits periodicity, while the AdS boundary does not.

6
Conclusions and Discussions

This paper investigates the holographic Casimir effect for parallel spherical defects. At small widths, the gravity dual is dominated by the AdS soliton with connected EOW branes. In contrast, at larger widths, the dominant phase is the AdS space with disconnected EOW branes. Notably, the Casimir effect exhibits a first-order phase transition
as the width increases and disappears in the disconnected phase. This behavior differs significantly from that observed in free theories, underscoring a key distinction between free and strongly coupled theories. It is important to highlight that this holographic phase transition is unique to parallel spherical defects and does not occur in parallel plane or hyperbolic defects. It is one of the key findings of this paper. Additionally, we confirm that free theories satisfy the holographic bound of the Casimir effect, further supporting the proposal that the AdS/BCFT with minimal brane tension sets the lower bound of the Casimir effect
Miao:2024gcq
;
Miao:2025utb
.

Interestingly, we find that cosmic censorship provides a holographic explanation for why the Casimir force is always attractive when the same boundary conditions are applied to both parallel surfaces. The repulsive Casimir force is associated with a bulk spacetime containing a naked singularity, which should be ruled out. Additionally, we discover that topological censorship offers a natural resolution to the discrepancy regarding the AdS soliton: the bulk exhibits periodicity, whereas the AdS boundary does not. These are the main physical findings of this paper.

So far, studies of the holographic Casimir effect have mainly focused on symmetric configurations, such as parallel plane, hyperbolic and spherical defects. It is intriguing to explore cases with lower symmetry. Surprisingly, the gravity dual of the relatively symmetric case of a ball remains unknown. The metric for the ball is given by:

\displaystyle ds^{2}=-dt^{2}+dr^{2}+r^{2}d\Omega^{2},\ \ \ r\leq R,

(60)

where the asymmetry arises from the time component. According to
Miao:2017aba
, the Casimir effect takes a universal form near the boundary

\displaystyle\langle T_{ij}\rangle\sim C_{D}\frac{\bar{k}_{ij}}{(R-r)^{3}}+...

(61)

Here,
\bar{k}_{ij}
represents the traceless components of the extrinsic curvatures, which are nonzero because of the asymmetry in the time component. In contrast, for parallel defects, we have
\bar{k}_{ij}=0
. The non-trivial Casimir effect (
61
) suggests that the ball cannot be dual to a simple geometry, such as AdS space or an AdS soliton. It is a primary reason why the gravitational dual of a ball has yet to be identified. We intend to address this interesting problem in the future. Another motivation for studying this problem is that there remains debate over whether the Casimir force acting on a spherical shell is attractive or repulsive
Milton:2004ya
;
Boyer:1968uf
;
Bowers:1998nr
;
Kenneth:2006vr
. Holographic studies can provide insights to clarify this controversy.

It is also interesting to study additional aspects of the holographic phase transition, particularly its temperature dependence. The investigation of holographic entanglement entropy and its various generalizations in AdS/BCFT is also a compelling area of research.

Appendix A
Casimir effect of free scalar

This appendix explores the Casimir effect for parallel spherical defects in a conformally coupled free scalar field, utilizing the heat-kernel method
Vassilevich:2003xt
. The metric of the manifold
M=[0,L]\times S^{d-1}
is given by

\displaystyle ds_{M}^{2}=dx^{2}+R^{2}d\Omega_{d-1}^{2}=dx^{2}+R^{2}h_{ab}d\Omega^{a}d\Omega^{b},\qquad 0\leq x\leq L,

(62)

where
R
is the radius of the sphere, we set
R=1
for simplicity, and
L
is the width of the interval.
The Euclidean action of the conformally coupled free scalar field is given by:

\displaystyle I=\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}d^{d}X\sqrt{g_{M}}\left(\nabla_{i}\phi\nabla^{i}\phi+\xi R_{M}\phi^{2}\right)+\int_{\partial M}d^{d-1}\Omega\sqrt{\sigma}\,\xi K_{M}\phi^{2},

(63)

where
\xi=\frac{d-2}{4(d-1)}
is the conformal coupling constant,
R_{M}=(d-1)(d-2)
is the Ricci scalar, and
K_{M}=0
represents the extrinsic curvature. There are two types of conformally invariant boundary conditions:

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\text{Dirichlet BC}:\phi|_{\partial M}=0,\\
&\text{Robin BC}:\ \ (\nabla_{n}+2\xi K_{M})\phi|_{\partial M}=0,\end{split}

(64)

where
n
denotes the normal direction.

The heat kernel satisfies the equation of motion (EOM)

\displaystyle\partial_{s}K(s;X,X^{\prime})-\Big(\nabla^{j}\nabla_{j}-\xi R_{M}\Big)K(s;X,X^{\prime})=0,

(65)

and the boundary condition (BC) (
64
) along with

\displaystyle\lim_{s\rightarrow 0}K(s;X,X^{\prime})=\frac{\delta^{(d)}(X-X^{\prime})}{\sqrt{g_{M}}}.

(66)

By solving the above EOM and BCs, we obtain the heat kernel expressed in terms of eigenfunctions:

\displaystyle K(s;X,X^{\prime})=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{g_{\ell}^{(0)}}Y_{\ell m}(\Omega)Y_{\ell m}(\Omega^{\prime})^{*}\begin{cases}\displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{\lambda s}\frac{2}{L}\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x^{\prime}}{L}\right),\\[6.00006pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1}{L}e^{\lambda_{0}s}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{\lambda s}\frac{2}{L}\cos\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\cos\!\left(\frac{n\pi x^{\prime}}{L}\right),\end{cases}

(67)

where
g_{\ell}^{(0)}
is the degeneracy of the harmonics for each
\ell
. The upper entry corresponds to DBC and the lower entry corresponds to RBC. The eigenvalue
\lambda
is given by:

\displaystyle\lambda=-\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{L^{2}}+\lambda_{0},\qquad\lambda_{0}=-\left(\ell+\frac{d-2}{2}\right)^{2}.

(68)

It is convenient to rewrite the heat kernel by employing the Poisson summation formula and the properties of spherical harmonics. For Dirichlet BC:

\displaystyle K(s;X,X^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\Bigg[\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2}{L}e^{-\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{L^{2}}s}\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x^{\prime}}{L}\right)\Bigg]\Bigg[\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{g_{\ell}^{(0)}}e^{\lambda_{0}s}Y_{\ell m}(\Omega)Y_{\ell m}(\Omega^{\prime})^{*}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\Bigg[\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi s}}\left(e^{-\frac{(x-x^{\prime}+2nL)^{2}}{4s}}-e^{-\frac{(x+x^{\prime}+2nL)^{2}}{4s}}\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad\times\Bigg[\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{2\pi^{d/2}}\frac{2\ell+d-2}{d-2}e^{-\left(\ell+\frac{d-2}{2}\right)^{2}s}C_{\ell}^{(d/2-1)}(\cos\gamma)\Bigg],

(69)

where
C_{\ell}^{(d/2-1)}(\cos\gamma)
is the Gegenbauer polynomial, and

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \cos\gamma&=\cos\theta_{1}\cos\theta_{1}^{\prime}+\sin\theta_{1}\sin\theta_{1}^{\prime}\\
&\quad\times\Big[\cos\theta_{2}\cos\theta_{2}^{\prime}+\sin\theta_{2}\sin\theta_{2}^{\prime}(\cdots+\sin\theta_{d-2}\sin\theta_{d-2}^{\prime}\cos(\theta_{d-1}-\theta_{d-1}^{\prime})\cdots)\Big].\end{aligned}

(70)

Similarly, for Robin BC:

\displaystyle K(s;X,X^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\Bigg[\frac{1}{L}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{2}{L}e^{-\frac{n^{2}\pi^{2}}{L^{2}}s}\cos\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right)\cos\!\left(\frac{n\pi x^{\prime}}{L}\right)\Bigg]\Bigg[\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{g_{\ell}^{(0)}}e^{\lambda_{0}s}Y_{\ell m}(\Omega)Y_{\ell m}(\Omega^{\prime})^{*}\Bigg]

\displaystyle=\Bigg[\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi s}}\left(e^{-\frac{(x-x^{\prime}+2nL)^{2}}{4s}}+e^{-\frac{(x+x^{\prime}+2nL)^{2}}{4s}}\right)\Bigg]

\displaystyle\quad\times\Bigg[\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{2\pi^{d/2}}\frac{2\ell+d-2}{d-2}e^{-\left(\ell+\frac{d-2}{2}\right)^{2}s}C_{\ell}^{(d/2-1)}(\cos\gamma)\Bigg].

(71)

The free-space heat kernel is given by

\displaystyle K_{0}(s;X,X^{\prime})=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi s}}\,e^{-\frac{(x-x^{\prime})^{2}}{4s}}\Bigg[\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{2\pi^{d/2}}\frac{2\ell+d-2}{d-2}e^{\lambda_{0}s}C_{\ell}^{(d/2-1)}(\cos\gamma)\Bigg].

(72)

From the heat kernels (
A
),(
A
), and (
72
), we can derive the renormalized Green function

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \hat{G}(X,X^{\prime})&=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,\Big(K(s;X,X^{\prime})-K_{0}(s;X,X^{\prime})\Big)\\
&=k_{1}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}C_{\ell}^{(d/2-1)}(\cos\gamma)\Bigg[\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}\Bigl(e^{-k_{2}|x-x^{\prime}+2nL|}\mp e^{-k_{2}|x+x^{\prime}+2nL|}\Bigr)-e^{-k_{2}|x-x^{\prime}|}\Bigg],\end{aligned}

(73)

where the minus sign corresponds to DBC, and the plus sign corresponds to RBC, and

\displaystyle k_{1}=\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{2(d-2)\pi^{d/2}},\qquad k_{2}=\ell+\frac{d-2}{2}.

(74)

The renormalized stress tensor is obtained using the following formula:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle T_{ij}\rangle&=\lim\limits_{X\to X^{\prime}}\Biggl[(1-2\xi)\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j^{\prime}}-2\xi\nabla_{i}\nabla_{j}+\Bigl(2\xi-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)g_{Mij}\nabla_{l}\nabla^{l^{\prime}}\\
&\quad+\xi\Bigl(R_{Mij}+\frac{4\xi-1}{2}R_{M}g_{Mij}\Bigr)\Biggr]\hat{G}(X,X^{\prime}).\end{aligned}

(75)

The result is

\displaystyle\langle T_{xx}\rangle=-\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{4\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(d-1)}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+d-2)^{2}\frac{\Gamma(\ell+d-2)}{\Gamma(\ell+1)}\frac{1}{e^{L(2\ell+d-2)}-1}.

(76)

Comparing the renormalized stress tensor with the expression
\langle T_{xx}\rangle=-(d-1)\kappa_{1}/L^{d}
, we derive the Casimir amplitude:

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}=\frac{\Gamma(d/2)}{4\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(d)}L^{d}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2\ell+d-2)^{2}\frac{\Gamma(\ell+d-2)}{\Gamma(\ell+1)}\frac{1}{e^{L(2\ell+d-2)}-1},

(77)

which is independent of the choice of boundary conditions. Numerical evaluations of this expression for various spacetime dimensions
d=3
and
d=4
are presented
in Fig.
11
.
For a large separation
L
, the Casimir amplitude

gradually approaches zero. In contrast, for a small separation
L
,
\kappa_{1}
approaches the value corresponding to plane defects, as expected. Interestingly, for
d=3

\kappa_{1}
initially increases and then decreases with increasing
L

The summation (

) can be evaluated analytically in the limit

\kappa_{1}=\frac{1}{32\pi}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}(2L)\bigl[(2\ell+1)L\bigr]^{2}\frac{1}{e^{(2\ell+1)L}-1}.

(78)

For small

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}=\frac{\zeta(3)}{16\pi}+\frac{L^{2}}{192\pi}+\mathcal{O}(L^{4}),

(79)

where the leading term
\frac{\zeta(3)}{16\pi}\approx 0.02391

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}

\displaystyle\approx\frac{\Gamma(d/2)\,\zeta(d)}{2^{d}\pi^{d/2}}+\mathcal{O}(L^{2}),\qquad L\ll 1,

which reproduces the result for parallel plates

Appendix B
Casimir effect of Maxwell theory

In this appendix, we calculate the Casimir effect of the Maxwell field on the Euclidean product manifold
M=[0,L]\times S^{3}
. We employ two methods: the heat-kernel method and the mode summation method, both of which yield the same Casimir pressure
T_{nn}
. We restrict attention to four dimensions, since the Maxwell action is conformally invariant only in four dimensions.

Let us start with the Euclidean Maxwell action in the Feynman gauge

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4}\int_{M}d^{4}X\sqrt{g_{M}}\,F_{ij}F^{ij}+\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}d^{4}X\sqrt{g_{M}}\,(\nabla_{i}A^{i})^{2},

(81)

where
F_{ij}=\nabla_{i}A_{j}-\nabla_{j}A_{i}
. After integrating by parts, the quadratic operator
\mathcal{D}
can be expressed as:

\displaystyle I

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\int_{M}d^{4}X\sqrt{g_{M}}\,A_{i}\mathcal{D}^{ij}A_{j},\qquad\mathcal{D}^{ij}=-g_{M}^{ij}\nabla^{2}+R_{M}^{ij}.

(82)

It leads to the following decompositions:

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}A)_{x}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}^{2}A_{x}-D^{a}D_{a}A_{x},

(83)

\displaystyle(\mathcal{D}A)_{a}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}^{2}A_{a}-D^{b}D_{b}A_{a}+2A_{a},

(84)

where
D_{a}
represents the covariant derivative on the unit
S^{3}
.
The boundary term of the action variation is given by:

\displaystyle\delta I\big|_{\partial M}=\int_{\partial M}d^{3}\Omega\sqrt{\sigma}\,\left(n_{i}F^{ij}+n^{j}\nabla_{k}A^{k}\right)\delta A_{j}.

(85)

Two types of boundary conditions define a well-defined action principle, i.e.,
\delta I\big|_{\partial M}=0
. The first type is the relative, or perfectly conducting, boundary condition, which is given by:

\displaystyle A_{a}\big|_{\partial M}=0,\qquad\partial_{x}A_{x}\big|_{\partial M}=0.

(86)

The second type is the absolute boundary condition, represented as:

\displaystyle A_{x}\big|_{\partial M}=0,\qquad\partial_{x}A_{a}\big|_{\partial M}=0.

(87)

Let’s briefly review the scalar and vector harmonics on
S^{3}

Lindblom:2017maa
, which will be useful for our upcoming calculations. The component
A_{x}
has no
S^{3}
index, so it behaves as a scalar on
S^{3}
. In contrast,
A_{a}
has one tangential index and must be expanded using vector harmonics.

The normalized scalar harmonics
Y_{\ell m}
are defined by
Lindblom:2017maa

\displaystyle-D^{a}D_{a}Y_{\ell m}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\ell(\ell+2)Y_{\ell m}(\Omega),\qquad\ell=0,1,2,\ldots,\qquad m=1,2,\ldots,(\ell+1)^{2},

(88)

which obeys the following addition theorems:

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}}\sum_{m=1}^{(\ell+1)^{2}}Y_{\ell m}(\Omega)Y_{\ell m}^{*}(\Omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{(\ell+1)^{2}}{2\pi^{2}},

(89)

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}}\sum_{m=1}^{(\ell+1)^{2}}h^{ab^{\prime}}D_{a}Y_{\ell m}(\Omega)D_{b^{\prime}}Y_{\ell m}^{*}(\Omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell(\ell+2)(\ell+1)^{2}}{2\pi^{2}}.

(90)

A vector field on
S^{3}
can be decomposed into two parts: a longitudinal part and a transverse, divergence-free part. The normalized longitudinal vector harmonics are defined as follows:

\displaystyle Y^{L}_{a,\ell m}(\Omega)

\displaystyle=\frac{D_{a}Y_{\ell m}(\Omega)}{\sqrt{\ell(\ell+2)}},\qquad\ell=1,2,\ldots,\qquad m=1,2,\ldots,(\ell+1)^{2},

(91)

which obeys the equation of motion (EOM)

\displaystyle\left(-D^{b}D_{b}+2\right)Y^{L}_{a,\ell m}

\displaystyle=\ell(\ell+2)Y^{L}_{a,\ell m},

(92)

and the following trace identity

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}}\sum_{m=1}^{(\ell+1)^{2}}h^{ab^{\prime}}Y^{L}_{a,\ell m}(\Omega)Y^{L*}_{b^{\prime},\ell m}(\Omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{(\ell+1)^{2}}{2\pi^{2}}.

(93)

The transverse harmonics
Y^{T,\sigma}_{a,\ell m}
are defined by

\displaystyle D^{a}Y^{T,\sigma}_{a,\ell m}=0,\quad\ell=1,2,\ldots,

\displaystyle\quad\sigma=\pm,\quad m=1,2,\ldots,\ell(\ell+2),

(94)

\displaystyle\left(-D^{b}D_{b}+2\right)Y^{T,\sigma}_{a,\ell m}

\displaystyle=(\ell+1)^{2}Y^{T,\sigma}_{a,\ell m}.

(95)

The corresponding trace identity is

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{\Omega\to\Omega^{\prime}}\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\sum_{m=1}^{\ell(\ell+2)}h^{ab^{\prime}}Y^{T,\sigma}_{a,\ell m}(\Omega)Y^{T,\sigma*}_{b^{\prime},\ell m}(\Omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\ell(\ell+2)}{2\pi^{2}}.

(96)

B.1
Heat-kernel method

The vector heat kernel satisfies the EOM

\displaystyle\left(\partial_{s}+\mathcal{D}\right)_{i}{}^{k}K_{kj^{\prime}}(s;X,X^{\prime})=0,

(97)

along with the boundary conditions (BC) (
86
,
87
), as well as the initial condition:

\displaystyle\lim\limits_{s\to 0}K_{ij^{\prime}}(s;X,X^{\prime})=g_{Mij^{\prime}}\frac{\delta^{(4)}(X-X^{\prime})}{\sqrt{g_{M}}}.

(98)

On the manifold
M=[0,L]\times S^{3}
, the heat kernel can be factored into one-dimensional interval heat kernels and scalar or vector harmonic sums on
S^{3}
.

Let
\hat{K}_{N}
and
\hat{K}_{D}
denote the renormalized one-dimensional heat kernels on the interval
[0,L]
with Neumann and Dirichlet BCs. After subtracting the free-space term on
\mathbb{R}\times S^{3}
, these kernels are given by:

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{N}(s;x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi s}}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{Z}\\
n\neq 0\end{subarray}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-x^{\prime}+2nL)^{2}}{4s}\right)+\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x+x^{\prime}+2nL)^{2}}{4s}\right)\right],

(99)

\displaystyle\hat{K}_{D}(s;x,x^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi s}}\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{Z}\\
n\neq 0\end{subarray}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x-x^{\prime}+2nL)^{2}}{4s}\right)-\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}\exp\left(-\frac{(x+x^{\prime}+2nL)^{2}}{4s}\right)\right].

(100)

By utilizing
\hat{K}_{N}
and
\hat{K}_{D}
, we can construct the components of the renormalized heat kernel as follows:

\displaystyle K_{xx^{\prime}}(s;X,X^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{(\ell+1)^{2}}e^{-\ell(\ell+2)s}Y_{\ell m}(\Omega)Y_{\ell m}^{*}(\Omega^{\prime})\begin{cases}\hat{K}_{N}(s;x,x^{\prime})\\
\hat{K}_{D}(s;x,x^{\prime})\end{cases},

(101)

\displaystyle K_{ab^{\prime}}(s;X,X^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\begin{cases}\hat{K}_{D}(s;x,x^{\prime})\\
\hat{K}_{N}(s;x,x^{\prime})\end{cases}\times\Bigg[\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{m=1}^{(\ell+1)^{2}}e^{-\ell(\ell+2)s}Y^{L}_{a,\ell m}(\Omega)Y^{L*}_{b^{\prime},\ell m}(\Omega^{\prime})

\displaystyle\quad+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\sum_{m=1}^{\ell(\ell+2)}e^{-(\ell+1)^{2}s}Y^{T,\sigma}_{a,\ell m}(\Omega)Y^{T,\sigma*}_{b^{\prime},\ell m}(\Omega^{\prime})\Bigg],

(102)

\displaystyle K_{xa^{\prime}}(s;X,X^{\prime})

\displaystyle=K_{ax^{\prime}}(s;X,X^{\prime})=0.

(103)

In each brace, the upper entry corresponds to relative boundary conditions,
whereas the lower entry corresponds to absolute boundary conditions. From the heat kernel presented above, we can derive the renormalized Green function as follows:

\displaystyle\hat{G}_{ij^{\prime}}(X,X^{\prime})

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}ds\,K_{ij^{\prime}}(s;X,X^{\prime}).

(104)

The explicit point-splitting calculation below shows that the contribution from
K_{xx^{\prime}}
and the longitudinal part of
K_{ab^{\prime}}
cancel in the final result. Only the two transverse polarizations contribute to the Casimir pressure

\displaystyle T_{xx}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}h^{ab}F_{xa}F_{xb}-\frac{1}{4}h^{ac}h^{bd}F_{ab}F_{cd}.

(105)

To evaluate the expectation value, we separate the two gauge fields into
X
and
X^{\prime}
, replace
\langle A_{i}(X)A_{j^{\prime}}(X^{\prime})\rangle
with the renormalized Green function, apply the necessary derivatives, and finally take the coincidence limit
Christensen:1976vb
. In our notation, we arrive at:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} \langle T_{xx}\rangle&=\lim\limits_{X\to X^{\prime}}\Bigg\{\frac{1}{2}h^{ab^{\prime}}\left(\partial_{x}\partial_{x^{\prime}}\hat{G}_{ab^{\prime}}+D_{a}D_{b^{\prime}}\hat{G}_{xx^{\prime}}-\partial_{x}D_{b^{\prime}}\hat{G}_{ax^{\prime}}-D_{a}\partial_{x^{\prime}}\hat{G}_{xb^{\prime}}\right)\\
&\hskip 28.45274pt-\frac{1}{4}h^{ac^{\prime}}h^{bd^{\prime}}\left(D_{a}D_{c^{\prime}}\hat{G}_{bd^{\prime}}-D_{a}D_{d^{\prime}}\hat{G}_{bc^{\prime}}-D_{b}D_{c^{\prime}}\hat{G}_{ad^{\prime}}+D_{b}D_{d^{\prime}}\hat{G}_{ac^{\prime}}\right)\Bigg\}.\end{aligned}

(106)

Since
\hat{G}_{xa^{\prime}}=\hat{G}_{ax^{\prime}}=0
, the two mixed terms vanish. It is convenient to denote the remaining six terms by

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} I_{1}&=\lim\limits_{X\to X^{\prime}}\frac{1}{2}h^{ab^{\prime}}\partial_{x}\partial_{x^{\prime}}\hat{G}_{ab^{\prime}},&I_{2}&=\lim\limits_{X\to X^{\prime}}\frac{1}{2}h^{ab^{\prime}}D_{a}D_{b^{\prime}}\hat{G}_{xx^{\prime}},\\
I_{3}&=-\lim\limits_{X\to X^{\prime}}\frac{1}{4}h^{ac^{\prime}}h^{bd^{\prime}}D_{a}D_{c^{\prime}}\hat{G}_{bd^{\prime}},&I_{4}&=-\lim\limits_{X\to X^{\prime}}\frac{1}{4}h^{ac^{\prime}}h^{bd^{\prime}}D_{b}D_{d^{\prime}}\hat{G}_{ac^{\prime}},\\
I_{5}&=\lim\limits_{X\to X^{\prime}}\frac{1}{4}h^{ac^{\prime}}h^{bd^{\prime}}D_{a}D_{d^{\prime}}\hat{G}_{bc^{\prime}},&I_{6}&=\lim\limits_{X\to X^{\prime}}\frac{1}{4}h^{ac^{\prime}}h^{bd^{\prime}}D_{b}D_{c^{\prime}}\hat{G}_{ad^{\prime}}.\end{aligned}

(107)

After inserting the Green’s function and applying the coincident identities of the harmonics, the contribution from
K_{xx^{\prime}}
cancels the longitudinal contribution from
K_{ab^{\prime}}
. The remaining transverse contributions from
I_{1}+I_{2}
yield:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} I_{1}+I_{2}&=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell(\ell+1)(\ell+2)\left[\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{Z}\\
n\neq 0\end{subarray}}e^{-2(\ell+1)L|n|}\pm\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-2(\ell+1)|x+nL|}\right].\end{aligned}

(108)

Here and below, the upper sign corresponds to relative boundary conditions,
whereas the lower sign corresponds to absolute boundary conditions. Similarly, the transverse parts of
I_{3}+I_{4}+I_{5}+I_{6}
produce:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} I_{3}+I_{4}+I_{5}+I_{6}&=-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell(\ell+1)(\ell+2)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{Z}\\
n\neq 0\end{subarray}}e^{-2(\ell+1)L|n|}\\
&\quad\pm\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell(\ell+1)(\ell+2)\sum_{n\in\mathbb{Z}}e^{-2(\ell+1)|x+nL|}.\end{aligned}

(109)

The
x
-dependent terms cancel out between Eqs. (
108
) and (
109
). Therefore, the normal pressure is independent of
x
in the interior. Using the identity:

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathbb{Z}\\
n\neq 0\end{subarray}}e^{-2(\ell+1)L|n|}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{e^{2(\ell+1)L}-1},

(110)

we obtain

\displaystyle\langle T_{xx}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{\ell(\ell+1)(\ell+2)}{e^{2(\ell+1)L}-1},

(111)

which is independent of the choice of boundary conditions.
For
L\ll 1
, the Euler–Maclaurin expansion gives

\displaystyle\langle T_{xx}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{240L^{4}}+\frac{1}{24L^{2}}-\frac{1}{4\pi^{2}L}+\frac{11}{240\pi^{2}}+\mathcal{O}(L).

(112)

The leading term agrees with the standard result for flat plates, as expected in the local flat limit where
L\ll R
.

B.2
Mode summation method

We now present an independent derivation based on the physical-mode summation. On
S^{3}
, the physical polarizations are represented by the transverse and divergence-free vector harmonics
Y^{T,\sigma}_{a,\ell m}
. The normal scalar component and the longitudinal vector component do not represent independent propagating polarizations, consistent with the previously identified cancellation.

For a physical transverse mode, the gauge potential can be expanded as

\displaystyle A_{a}(x,\Omega)

\displaystyle=f_{n}(x)Y^{T,\sigma}_{a,\ell m}(\Omega),\qquad A_{x}=0.

(113)

The BCs (
86
,
87
) yield

\displaystyle\text{relative BC}:\ \ f_{n}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\sin\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right),\qquad n=1,2,\ldots,

(114)

\displaystyle\text{absolute BC}:\ f_{n}(x)=\sqrt{\frac{2}{L}}\cos\left(\frac{n\pi x}{L}\right),\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.

(115)

Acting in this mode with the vector operator and utilizing (
95
), we obtain the following eigenvalue equation

\displaystyle\mathcal{D}_{a}{}^{b}\left[f_{n}(x)Y^{T,\sigma}_{b,\ell m}(\Omega)\right]

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n\ell}f_{n}(x)Y^{T,\sigma}_{a,\ell m}(\Omega).

(116)

The corresponding eigenvalue is given by

\displaystyle\lambda_{n\ell}

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^{2}+(\ell+1)^{2},\qquad n=1,2,\ldots,\qquad\ell=1,2,\ldots.

(117)

For absolute BC, there is also a zero mode
f_{0}(x)=1/\sqrt{L}
. It gives eigenvalue

\lambda_{0\ell}=(\ell+1)^{2}
, which is independent of
L
. Hence it contributes only an
L
-independent term to the effective action and we omit this zero mode in the following
L
-dependent Casimir part.
For each value of
\ell
, the total degeneracy of the physical transverse sector is calculated as follows:

\displaystyle g_{\ell}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma=\pm}\sum_{m=1}^{\ell(\ell+2)}1=2\ell(\ell+2).

(118)

For a real bosonic Gaussian mode with eigenvalue
\lambda
, the functional integral yields
Z_{\lambda}\propto\lambda^{-1/2}
. Consequently, the Euclidean effective action can be expressed as

\displaystyle W=-\log Z=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\lambda}\log\lambda+\text{constant}.

(119)

Applying this formula to the physical transverse spectrum gives

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} W&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}g_{\ell}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\log\left[\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^{2}+(\ell+1)^{2}\right]\\
&=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell(\ell+2)\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\log\left[\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^{2}+(\ell+1)^{2}\right].\end{aligned}

(120)

To perform the sum over
n
, we utilize the following formula:

\displaystyle\sinh z

\displaystyle=z\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{z^{2}}{n^{2}\pi^{2}}\right),\qquad\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\log\left[\left(\frac{n\pi}{L}\right)^{2}\right]=\log(2L).

(121)

From this, we obtain:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} W&=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell(\ell+2)\log\left[1-e^{-2(\ell+1)L}\right]\\
&\quad+\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell(\ell+1)(\ell+2)L-\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell(\ell+2)\log(\ell+1).\end{aligned}

(122)

The third term is independent of
L
. The second term is proportional to
L
and represents the contribution from free space
\mathbb{R}\times S^{3}
. In fact, we have:

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} W_{\mathbb{R}\times S^{3}}&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}g_{\ell}L\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{dk}{2\pi}\log\left[k^{2}+(\ell+1)^{2}\right]\\
&=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell(\ell+1)(\ell+2)L+\text{constant}.\end{aligned}

(123)

After subtracting this free-space contribution and disregarding
L
-independent terms, the interaction part of the Casimir effective action is given by:

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{Cas}}(L)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\ell(\ell+2)\log\left[1-e^{-2(\ell+1)L}\right].

(124)

The normal pressure is obtained by differentiating with respect to separation:

\displaystyle\langle T_{xx}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{1}{\operatorname{Vol}(S^{3})}\frac{\partial W_{\mathrm{Cas}}}{\partial L}=-\frac{1}{\pi^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{\ell(\ell+1)(\ell+2)}{e^{2(\ell+1)L}-1}.

(125)

This result is consistent with the point-splitting result presented in (
111
). By utilizing

\displaystyle\langle T_{xx}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{3\kappa_{1}(L)}{L^{4}},

(126)

we finally obtain the Casimir amplitude for the Maxwell field in four dimensions

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}(L)=\frac{L^{4}}{3\pi^{2}}\sum_{\ell=1}^{\infty}\frac{\ell(\ell+1)(\ell+2)}{e^{2(\ell+1)L}-1}.

(127)

Appendix C
Casimir effect of free Dirac field

In this appendix, we calculate the interaction part of the Casimir effective action for a free massless Dirac field defined on the Euclidean product manifold
M=[0,L]\times S^{d-1}
. We impose the bag boundary conditions and apply the mode-sum method, as discussed in Appendix B.

Let us quickly establish our notation for the gamma matrices. Denote the gamma matrices in a local orthonormal frame as
\Gamma^{\hat{A}}
, where
\hat{x}
represents the direction along the interval and
\hat{a}=1,\ldots,d-1
represents the tangent directions on
S^{d-1}
. The curved gamma matrices are given by
Alcubierre:2025wgj
:

\displaystyle\gamma^{x}

\displaystyle=\Gamma^{\hat{x}},\qquad\gamma^{a}=e_{\hat{b}}{}^{a}\Gamma^{\hat{b}},\qquad\{\Gamma^{\hat{A}},\Gamma^{\hat{B}}\}=2\delta^{\hat{A}\hat{B}}\mathbf{1}_{N_{d}},\qquad N_{d}=2^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}.

(128)

For even dimensions
d=2m
, let
\widetilde{\Gamma}^{\hat{a}}
represent a set of gamma matrices for the Clifford algebra tangent to
S^{d-1}
, acting on
2^{m-1}
components. We choose:

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\hat{x}}

\displaystyle=\sigma_{3}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{2^{m-1}},\qquad\Gamma^{\hat{a}}=\sigma_{1}\otimes\widetilde{\Gamma}^{\hat{a}},\qquad\{\widetilde{\Gamma}^{\hat{a}},\widetilde{\Gamma}^{\hat{b}}\}=2\delta^{\hat{a}\hat{b}}\mathbf{1}_{2^{m-1}}.

(129)

For odd dimensions
d=2m+1
, the dimension of the sphere
d-1=2m
is even. In this case, let
\widetilde{\Gamma}^{\hat{a}}
denote the gamma matrices on
S^{2m}
and define the corresponding chirality matrix as:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\Gamma}_{*}

\displaystyle=i^{m}\widetilde{\Gamma}^{\hat{1}}\widetilde{\Gamma}^{\hat{2}}\cdots\widetilde{\Gamma}^{\widehat{2m}},\qquad\widetilde{\Gamma}_{*}^{2}=1,\qquad\{\widetilde{\Gamma}_{*},\widetilde{\Gamma}^{\hat{a}}\}=0.

(130)

We choose:

\displaystyle\Gamma^{\hat{x}}

\displaystyle=\widetilde{\Gamma}_{*},\qquad\Gamma^{\hat{a}}=\widetilde{\Gamma}^{\hat{a}}.

(131)

In both cases,
\Gamma^{\hat{x}}
is Hermitian, obeys

(\Gamma^{\hat{x}})^{2}=1
. Thus its eigenvalues are
\pm 1
.
Moreover,
\{\Gamma^{\hat{x}},\Gamma^{\hat{a}}\}=0

implies that the two eigenspaces have equal dimensions. We can therefore
diagonalize
\Gamma^{\hat{x}}
as
\Gamma^{\hat{x}}=\sigma_{3}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{N_{d}/2}.

We use the Hermitian Dirac spectral operator given by

\displaystyle D_{d}

\displaystyle=-i\gamma^{i}\nabla_{i}=-i\Gamma^{\hat{x}}\partial_{x}+D_{S^{d-1}},\qquad D_{S^{d-1}}=-i\Gamma^{\hat{a}}e_{\hat{a}}{}^{a}\nabla_{a}^{S^{d-1}}.

(132)

The product geometry has no mixed spin connection between the interval and the sphere. Therefore, we have
\{\Gamma^{\hat{x}},D_{S^{d-1}}\}=0
and
[\partial_{x},D_{S^{d-1}}]=0
, which results in

\displaystyle D_{d}^{2}

\displaystyle=-\partial_{x}^{2}+D_{S^{d-1}}^{2}.

(133)

The spectral problem therefore factorizes into an interval problem and the intrinsic Dirac spectrum on
S^{d-1}
.

We impose the bag boundary conditions
Chodos:1974je
as follows:

\displaystyle\Pi_{-}\psi|_{\partial M}=0,\qquad\bar{\psi}\Pi_{-}|_{\partial M}=0,\qquad\Pi_{-}=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{1}_{N_{d}}-n_{x}\sigma_{1}\otimes\mathbf{1}_{N_{d}/2})

(134)

so that the boundary term
\bar{\psi}\Gamma_{n}\delta\psi
in the variation vanishes on the boundary. These boundary conditions then determine the spectrum. The eigenvalue equation for the Dirac field on the unit sphere is given by
Camporesi:1995fb

\displaystyle D_{S^{d-1}}\eta_{\ell m}^{s}

\displaystyle=s\lambda_{\ell}\eta_{\ell m}^{s},\qquad s=\pm 1,\qquad\lambda_{\ell}=\ell+\frac{d-1}{2},\qquad\ell=0,1,2,\ldots.

(135)

For each sign branch, the degeneracy is

\displaystyle g_{\ell}

\displaystyle=2^{\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor}\frac{\Gamma(\ell+d-1)}{\Gamma(d-1)\Gamma(\ell+1)}.

(136)

Using the spinor eigenmodes on
S^{d-1}
, we expand the two components of
the
d
-dimensional spinor as

\displaystyle u(x,\Omega)=f(x)\eta^{s}_{\ell m}(\Omega),\qquad v(x,\Omega)=g(x)\eta^{s}_{\ell m}(\Omega).

(137)

The angular dependence is therefore diagonalized by

\eta^{s}_{\ell m}
, and the remaining eigenvalue problem reduces to a
one-dimensional problem for
f(x)
and
g(x)
.
Substituting the BC (
134
) into the interval part of the Dirac eigenvalue equation gives the half-integer momenta

\displaystyle k_{n}

\displaystyle=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\frac{\pi}{L},\qquad n=0,1,2,\ldots.

(138)

Therefore, the eigenvalues of
D_{d}^{2}
are

\displaystyle\Lambda_{n\ell}^{2}

\displaystyle=\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\frac{\pi^{2}}{L^{2}}+\left(\ell+\frac{d-1}{2}\right)^{2}.

(139)

The total multiplicity entering the determinant of
D_{d}^{2}
is

\displaystyle G_{\ell}

\displaystyle=2g_{\ell}\frac{N_{d}}{N_{d-1}}=2^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor+1}\frac{\Gamma(\ell+d-1)}{\Gamma(d-1)\Gamma(\ell+1)}.

(140)

Here, the factor
2
combines the two signs
s=\pm 1
after squaring, while
N_{d}/N_{d-1}
accounts for the embedding of intrinsic sphere spinors into the full
d
-dimensional Dirac spinor.

The effective action of Dirac fields can be calculated as follows:

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=-\log Z=-\frac{1}{2}\log\det D_{d}^{2}=-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}G_{\ell}\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\log\Lambda_{n\ell}^{2}.

(141)

By using

\displaystyle\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\left[\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\frac{\pi^{2}}{L^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=2,\qquad\cosh z=\prod_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac{4z^{2}}{(2n+1)^{2}\pi^{2}}\right),

(142)

we derive the effective action

\displaystyle W

\displaystyle=-2^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\ell+d-1)}{\Gamma(d-1)\Gamma(\ell+1)}\left[\lambda_{\ell}L+\log\left(1+e^{-2\lambda_{\ell}L}\right)\right].

(143)

The term proportional to
L
represents the local contribution from the reference geometry
\mathbb{R}\times S^{d-1}
. After subtracting this term, the interaction part of the Casimir effective action becomes

\displaystyle W_{\mathrm{Cas}}(L)

\displaystyle=-2^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\ell+d-1)}{\Gamma(d-1)\Gamma(\ell+1)}\log\left[1+e^{-2L(\ell+\frac{d-1}{2})}\right].

(144)

Dividing by
\operatorname{Vol}(S^{d-1})=\frac{2\pi^{d/2}}{\Gamma(d/2)}
gives us the averaged effective action density

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{Cas}}(L)

\displaystyle=-\frac{2^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\Gamma(d/2)}{2\pi^{d/2}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\ell+d-1)}{\Gamma(d-1)\Gamma(\ell+1)}\log\left[1+e^{-2L(\ell+\frac{d-1}{2})}\right].

(145)

The normal pressure can be calculated as

\displaystyle\langle T_{xx}\rangle

\displaystyle=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{W}_{\mathrm{Cas}}}{\partial L}=-\frac{2^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\Gamma(d/2)}{\pi^{d/2}}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\ell+d-1)}{\Gamma(d-1)\Gamma(\ell+1)}\frac{\ell+\frac{d-1}{2}}{e^{2L(\ell+\frac{d-1}{2})}+1}.

(146)

The pressure is negative, indicating an attractive Casimir force. Recall that
\langle T_{xx}\rangle=-(d-1)\kappa_{1}/L^{d}
, we finally obtain the Casimir amplitude

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}(L)

\displaystyle=\frac{2^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor}\Gamma(d/2)L^{d}}{\pi^{d/2}\Gamma(d)}\sum_{\ell=0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma(\ell+d-1)}{\Gamma(\ell+1)}\frac{\ell+\frac{d-1}{2}}{e^{2L(\ell+\frac{d-1}{2})}+1}.

(147)

To end this appendix, let us examine the behavior of the Casimir amplitude for small values of
L
. When
L\ll 1
, the primary contribution arises from large values of
\lambda_{\ell}=\ell+(d-1)/2
. Using the leading asymptotic behavior for large
\lambda_{\ell}
:

\displaystyle\frac{\Gamma(\ell+d-1)}{\Gamma(d-1)\Gamma(\ell+1)}

\displaystyle\sim\frac{\lambda_{\ell}^{d-2}}{\Gamma(d-1)},

(148)

and replacing the sum by an integral, we obtain

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}(L)

\displaystyle\sim\frac{2^{\lfloor d/2\rfloor-d}(1-2^{1-d})\Gamma(d/2)\zeta(d)}{\pi^{d/2}}.

(149)

This is precisely the flat parallel-plate limit for a massless Dirac field with bag BC
DePaola:1999im
. For
d=3
, the Euler-Maclaurin expansion yields

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}=\frac{3\zeta(3)}{32\pi}-\frac{L^{4}}{480\pi}+\cdots

(150)

For
d=4
, we find

\displaystyle\kappa_{1}=\frac{7\pi^{2}}{2880}-\frac{L^{2}}{288}-\frac{17L^{4}}{2880\pi^{2}}+\cdots

(151)

Note that we have set the radius
R=1
. When considering the radius, we substitute
L
with
L/R
in the equations above.
