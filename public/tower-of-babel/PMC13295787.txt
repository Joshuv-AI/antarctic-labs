[68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET/CT in newly diagnosed giant cell arteritis: an inflammation-specific, treatment-responsive molecular imaging biomarker
Authors: Simon M Petzinna, Jim Küppers, Benedikt Schemmer, Raul N Jamin, Sophie-Marie Kirch, Claus-Jürgen Bauer, Reza Gheitasi, Anna L Kernder, Daniel Ginzburg, Niklas T Baerlecken, Gustav B Jung, Maike S Adamson, Markus Essler, Valentin S Schäfer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAssessing disease activity in giant cell arteritis (GCA) remains challenging, as existing clinical, laboratory and imaging biomarkers lack specificity. This proof-of-concept study explored the diagnostic potential of vascular adhesion protein-1 (VAP-1) targeting [68Ga]Ga-(1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7,10-tetraacetic acid) DOTA-sialic acid-binding immunoglobulin-like lectin-9 (Siglec-9) positron emission tomography-CT (PET/CT) as an inflammation-specific molecular imaging biomarker in newly diagnosed and relapsing GCA.MethodsPatients with newly diagnosed GCA underwent [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET/CT and vascular ultrasound. Tracer uptake (SUVmean/SUVmax) was quantified in aortic, supra-aortic and extravascular (shoulder/hip) regions and compared with relapsing patients. Levels of soluble VAP-1 (sVAP-1) and matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)2/MMP3/MMP9 were measured in patients and controls as potential biomarkers related to vascular inflammation.ResultsEight patients with newly diagnosed GCA were compared with eight relapsing patients, alongside eight controls. Imaging revealed a positive association between ultrasound-assessed subclavian artery intima-media thickness and tracer uptake (r=0.67, p=0.035). Prednisolone exposure was inversely associated with vascular uptake across various vascular regions, with SUVmean showing a stronger overall negative association than SUVmax (SUVmean r=–0.51, p=0.043; SUVmax r=–0.62, p=0.010). Levels of sVAP-1 were reduced in newly diagnosed GCA compared with relapsing patients (p=0.0145) and controls (p=0.0446), and inversely associated with MMP2 (r=–0.56, p=0.037) and MMP9 (r=–0.47, p=0.089). Notably, MMP2 and MMP3 were negatively associated with vascular uptake (eg, aortic arch: r=–0.59, p=0.027).ConclusionsThis first-in-human study demonstrates that [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET/CT detects vascular inflammation in GCA and shows attenuation with prednisolone exposure. Reduced sVAP-1 levels and inverse associations between MMP2/MMP3 and tracer uptake support a functional VAP-1/MMP axis underlying the imaging signal. Our findings further position [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET/CT as a promising potential molecular imaging biomarker in GCA.Keywords: Vasculitis, Giant Cell Arteritis, Inflammation, Polymyalgia Rheumatica, Biomarkers

Introduction

Giant cell arteritis (GCA) is characterised by vascular inflammation leading to neoangiogenesis, remodelling and potential occlusion of medium-sized and large-sized vessels.1 A large-vessel phenotype typically involves the aorta and its major branches, including the axillary, subclavian and carotid arteries, whereas a cranial phenotype predominantly affects the temporal, facial and occipital arteries, with anterior ischaemic optic neuropathy representing a severe complication.2 3 Up to 67.5% of patients exhibit a combined cranial and large-vessel involvement.4

Diagnostic and monitoring strategies depend on the vascular territories affected, with imaging modalities playing a central role.5 6 Vascular ultrasound is particularly valuable in detecting increased intima-media thickness (IMT) in cranial, axillary, subclavian and carotid arteries at diagnosis.7 Longitudinal, quantitative ultrasound assessments may also support evaluation of disease activity and relapse risk.8 However, ultrasound is limited in visualising the aorta, and often additional imaging modalities are required.7

MRI and whole-body [18F]fluorodeoxyglucose ([18F]FDG) positron emission tomography-CT (PET/CT) are the most established modalities, with PET/CT visualising inflammation indirectly through [18F]FDG uptake in hypermetabolic cells.9 However, the lack of specificity for immune-mediated inflammation poses major challenges.10 Concordant findings have been reported for 18F-labelled fibroblast activation protein inhibitor ([18F]F-FAPI) PET/CT, where tracer uptake may also persist in remission.11 Similarly, both vascular ultrasound and MRI show limited sensitivity in detecting therapeutic response in large-vessel disease, complicating reliable activity assessment.12–14

As a novel approach, the radiotracer [68Ga]Ga-(1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7,10-tetraacetic acid)DOTA-Sialic acid-binding immunoglobulin-like lectin-9 (Siglec-9) has recently been introduced for PET/CT imaging of vascular inflammation in relapsing GCA.15–17 Siglec-9 is a ligand of vascular adhesion protein-1 (VAP-1), a type II transmembrane sialoglycoprotein encoded by the AOC3 gene.18 It exists in both a membrane-bound and a soluble form (sVAP-1), with the latter generated by matrix metalloproteinase (MMP)-mediated cleavage from its membrane-bound counterpart.18 19 Within the vasculature, VAP-1 is predominantly expressed on vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) and endothelial cells.20 21 In homeostatic conditions, it localises to caveolae in VSMCs and intracellular vesicles in endothelial cells.18 On inflammatory stimulation, including tumour necrosis factor-α, interferon-γ, lipopolysaccharide and interleukin-1β, VAP-1 is translocated to the endothelial surface,22 23 where it mediates leucocyte adhesion via Siglec-9 on neutrophils and monocytes24 and Siglec-10 on B cells, monocytes and eosinophils.25 The VAP-1/Siglec interaction contributes to both the enzyme-independent adhesion and transmigration of immune cells into inflamed tissues and the enzyme-dependent oxidative deamination of primary amines.26 The latter induces cytotoxic endothelial injury and amplifies inflammation through upregulation of adhesion molecules (intercellular adhesion molecule-1, mucosal addressin cell adhesion molecule-1, E-selectin, P-selectin), secretion of C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 8, nuclear factor kappa B activation and MMP expression, thereby facilitating leucocyte recruitment and migration.27–30

While we have recently demonstrated that patients with relapsing GCA exhibit increased [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 uptake on PET/CT, capitalising on its theoretically higher specificity for vascular inflammation, the exact role of VAP-1 in GCA pathophysiology and its implications for Siglec-9-based imaging remain insufficiently understood. In particular, the impact of treatment on VAP-1 expression and tracer uptake has not been elucidated yet. This study is the first to comprehensively investigate the role of VAP-1 in newly diagnosed GCA, to evaluate the diagnostic potential of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET/CT for detection of inflammation and to explore potential treatment effects on imaging readouts.

Methods

Patient characteristics and clinical assessment

Patients with newly diagnosed GCA were recruited at the Department of Rheumatology and the Department of Ophthalmology University Hospital Bonn (Germany), between 1 October 2023 and 30 April 2025. The diagnosis was established by board-certified rheumatologists with extensive experience in the management of vasculitis, each with >10 years of clinical experience and regular involvement in the care of patients with GCA. Diagnosis was based on the overall clinical assessment, including clinical presentation, laboratory findings and available imaging results, and was made independently of the [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET/CT findings.

Patients were required to fulfil the American College of Rheumatology/European Alliance of Associations for Rheumatology classification criteria for GCA.31 Patients with a history of another rheumatological disease prior to enrolment were excluded. For comparative analysis, patients with relapsing GCA were also included. This cohort has been described in detail before.15 Relapse was defined as the reoccurrence of clinical symptoms compatible with active GCA accompanied by laboratory evidence of inflammation and/or imaging findings suggestive of vascular inflammation. All diagnoses were confirmed by the same board-certified rheumatologists. Both cohorts (newly diagnosed and relapsing GCA) were analysed in parallel to enable direct group comparisons. Healthy control subjects were recruited from the same departments. Eligibility required an age >50 years and absence of systemic autoimmune or inflammatory disease, or relevant cardiovascular disease based on medical history and clinical assessment. Controls did not undergo PET/CT imaging (with the exception of one individual who underwent PET/CT imaging) and were required to have no current or prior treatment with glucocorticoids or disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs).

Comprehensive demographic, disease and treatment data were collected for all patients, including GCA-related symptoms and PMR-like manifestations affecting the shoulder and/or pelvic girdle. PMR symptoms were defined as bilateral shoulder and/or pelvic girdle pain and stiffness. Routine laboratory parameters, including C reactive protein (CRP) and complete blood count, were also recorded.

Glucocorticoid exposure was quantified as prior prednisolone exposure (PPE), defined as the cumulative prednisolone dose administered during the 7 days preceding PET imaging. In addition, the duration of current prednisolone treatment prior to imaging was recorded.

Ultrasound assessment

All patients underwent a comprehensive ultrasound examination of the superficial temporal arteries and their frontal and parietal branches, as well as the facial, axillary, subclavian and carotid arteries as described before.32 33 All ultrasound examinations were performed by board-certified rheumatologists proficient in ultrasound imaging in GCA (>1000 examinations performed, >100 examinations per year), or by a medical student under supervision of one of the aforementioned certified rheumatologists. Examinations were conducted on a GE LOGIQ e10 (2021 GE HealthCare Technologies, Chicago, Illinois, USA). A 18–24 MHz hockey stick probe, standardised to a B-mode frequency of 18 MHz and an image depth of 8 mm, was used for temporal and facial arteries. The focus point was set at 2.5 mm beneath the skin surface. For axillary, subclavian and carotid arteries, a 6–15 MHz probe was employed. Standardised parameters included a B-mode frequency of 15 MHz, an image depth of 2.2 cm and a focus point set 1.5 cm beneath the skin surface. Notably, parameters such as B-mode frequency, image depth and focus point position were adjusted according to individual patient requirements to ensure optimal image quality.

Extent of IMT was measured in B-mode at the point of greatest thickness, defined from the distal edge of the medial-adventitial complex to the luminal end of the intimal layer. The highest IMT values per vessel were recorded and compared with established cut-off values and Outcome Measures in Rheumatology (OMERACT) ultrasound definitions for GCA.34 35 Vessel wall abnormalities were further quantified using the OMERACT Giant Cell Arteritis Ultrasonography Score (OGUS), calculated as previously described.36

In patients reporting shoulder pain suggestive of PMR, additional musculoskeletal ultrasound of the shoulder region was performed. The examination focused on the long head of the biceps tendon sheath and the glenohumeral joint to assess typical inflammatory changes associated with PMR.

[68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET/CT—synthesis

Gallium-68 was obtained from a germanium-68/gallium-68 generator (EZAG, Berlin, Germany). Synthesis was performed using an automated cassette module (GAIA; Elysia-Raytest, Straubenhardt, Germany). Standard fluidic and reagent kit for gallium-68 radiolabeling of peptides (ABX advanced biochemical compounds GmbH, Radeberg, Germany) were employed. TraceSelect water and ethanol Pharmacopoeia Europaea (Ph. Eur.) were purchased from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany).

DOTA-Siglec-9 was obtained from ABX and diluted with TraceSelect water to achieve a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. For the synthesis, the module was equipped with 150±5.00 µg DOTA-Siglec-9, 500 µL eluent, 2.49 mL ammonium acetate buffer (pH 4.5) and 200 µL ethanol. After radiolabeling at 65±5°C for 15 min, the reaction mixture was purified through C-18 solid phase extraction and the final product was obtained in isotonic sodium chloride solution.

For quality control, an aliquot of about 50 µL was taken from the final formulation before measurement of radioactivity. All chemicals were pure or analytical grade and used as received, unless otherwise specified.

Radioactivity of the final product was measured with a dose calibrator (ISOMED 2010, MED Nuklear-Medizintechnik Dresden GmbH, Dresden, Germany). Radiochemical purity was determined using glass microfibre chromatography paper impregnated with silica gel (instant thin-layer chromatography silica gel (iTLC-SG) strips, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, USA) and analysed with a single trace radioTLC scanner (PET-miniGita, Elysia-Raytest) and evaluation software (Gina Star TLC, Elysia-Raytest). iTLC strips were developed in 0.1 M citric buffer (pH 4; Merck) and 1 M ammonium acetate/methanol (1:1) (v:v). Furthermore, radio high-performance liquid chromatography (radioHPLC) was used to determine the radiochemical purity and identification of the product species. RadioHPLC was performed using Agilent 1260 Infinity II reverse-phase HPLC system (Agilent Technologies) equipped with Gabi γ-HPLC flow detector (Elysia-Raytest) and a PC interface running Gina Star (Elysia-Raytest). A Nucleodur 100-3 C18 ec 125/4 column (Macherey-Nagel, Düren, Germany) was applied. The gradient used mobile phase A (deionised water+0.1% Trifluoracetat) and mobile phase B (acetonitrile+0.1% TFA) at a flow rate of 0.7 mL/min starting with 100% A/0% B to 0% A/100% B within 20 min. pH was measured using pH-indicator strips MColorpHast 2.0−9.0 (Merck). The approximate half-life of gallium-68 was determined using a dose calibrator (ISOMED 2010, MED Nuklear-Medizintechnik Dresden GmbH). The nuclide specific energy of gallium-68 as well as germanium-68 breakthrough (48 hours postlabelling) were measured using a multichannel analyser for γ-spectroscopy (Elysia-Raytest). Clear appearance was checked visually. Filter integrity was tested with GAIA (Elysia-Raytest).

Non-decay-corrected activity yield (AY) as well as decay-corrected radiochemical yield were calculated based on the activity trapped on the SCX, activity trapped on C-18 and remaining activity on C-18 after final formulation as measured by the module. Volume activity and apparent molar activity were calculated based on activity of the final product.

Radiosynthesis of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 with 1.38±0.27 GBq gallium-68 revealed a decay-corrected radiochemical yield of 86.0±6.09% (69.1±5.28% AY). A volume activity of 110±28.3 MBq/mL and an apparent molar activity of 17.2±4.24 MBq/nmol were obtained. Radiochemical purity was >98%.

[68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET/CT—imaging

The patients underwent [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET-CT scans with an injected dose of 146.0±39.3 MBq [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9. A low-dose CT for attenuation correction and a consecutive whole-body PET scan were performed 67.3±14.3 min postinjection.

A digital photon-counting PET-CT scanner (Vereos, Philips, Amsterdam, the Netherlands) was used with transaxial and axial fields of view of 67.6 and 16.4 cm.37 The transaxial and axial fields of view were 67.6 and 16.4 cm, respectively.37 Emission data were acquired for 60 s per bed. Images were reconstructed using the ordered subset expectation maximization (OSEM) algorithm. Both the time-of-flight algorithm and point spread function correction were applied. The reconstructed images had a matrix size of 256×256 mm and a voxel size of 2.0×2.0×2.0 mm.

To compensate for noise and random effects, a single thick coronal maximum intensity projection with a thickness of 240 mm was created for each patient. Vascular tracer uptake was quantified using SUVmax within circular regions of interest (ROIs) placed along predefined segments in correlation with the low-dose CT. The following vascular and periarticular regions were assessed: the aortic arch, descending thoracic aorta, abdominal aorta, bilateral subclavian, axillary and brachial arteries, as well as bilateral shoulder and hip regions. SUVmax was calculated as SUVmax=maximum tracer uptake in ROI/(injected activity/patient weight) analogue to the newest Quantitative Imaging Biomarker Alliance (QIBA) profile (V.1.14) for [18F]FDG-PET/CT.38 This allowed for the exclusion of low uptake from areas of adjacent uninvolved structures. However, the ROI had to be manually selected with care to include only structures of interest, which was especially important when structures like the bladder or kidneys with high unspecific uptake were adjacent to the vessels. Furthermore, SUVmean was calculated as the mean standardised uptake value, defined as (tissue activity concentration/injected dose)×body weight.

To minimise bias, all image evaluations, including ROI placement and SUVmax determination, were performed by an experienced reader who was blinded to all clinical data, laboratory results and treatment information of the patients.

Laboratory assessment

Circulating sVAP-1 concentrations were measured in patients with GCA and controls using an ELISA according to the manufacturer’s instructions (dilution 1:25). A quantikine immunoassay control set 754 (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA) was used for validation. MMP3 levels were determined following the AESKU group protocol (Wendelsheim, Germany), while MMP2 and MMP9 were quantified using DuoSet ELISA kits (Bio-Techne, Minneapolis, Minnesota, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocols.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and visualisation were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software) and its built-in statistical tools. Comparisons between patients with relapsing GCA and healthy controls were conducted using the independent samples t-test for continuous variables. Associations between PPE and PET signal intensity were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation analyses. Correlations between sVAP-1 levels, MMP and PET tracer uptake (SUVmean/SUVmax across vascular regions) were likewise assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. Continuous variables with non-normal distribution were summarised as median (IQR) and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. A p value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

A total of eight patients with newly diagnosed GCA (four females, four males; mean age 73.5±10.8 years) were included and compared with eight patients with relapsing disease (five females, three males; mean age 72.4±10.9 years; p=0.85), as well as eight healthy controls (five females, three males; mean age 70.8±8.1 years). The diagnosis established at baseline remained unchanged during follow-up in all included patients. Prednisolone exposure within the preceding 7 days (PPE) was significantly higher in newly diagnosed patients (532.5±365.5 mg; mean daily dose at imaging 166.3±116.6 mg) compared with relapsing patients (36.8±48.9 mg; mean daily dose at imaging 5.3±7.0 mg; p=0.0019). The median duration of current prednisolone treatment prior to imaging was 3.0 (2.0–4.0) in newly diagnosed patients compared with 60.0 (0.0–229.5) days in relapsing patients (p=0.530). Intravenous prednisolone pulse therapy had been administered in five newly diagnosed patients for a median of 3 (2–4) days and no patients with relapsing disease. None of the patients received conventional, biologic or targeted synthetic DMARDs at the time of imaging. No prednisolone use was reported in the control group.

At presentation, patients exhibited a heterogeneous spectrum of clinical manifestations. Cranial symptoms were observed in both groups, including headache (2/8 relapsing vs 5/8 newly diagnosed), temporal pain (1/8 vs 3/8) and jaw claudication (2/8 vs 4/8). Visual symptoms were reported in 1/8 relapsing and 3/8 newly diagnosed patients. Constitutional symptoms, including weight loss (1/8 vs 4/8) and night sweats (1/8 vs 2/8), were also reported. Concomitant PMR symptoms were present in six patients overall, including two patients with newly diagnosed GCA (2/8) and four patients with relapsing disease (4/8). Ultrasound assessment

At inclusion, IMT values exceeded predefined cut-off thresholds in multiple vascular territories in both GCA cohorts. The OGUS was significantly higher in newly diagnosed patients (1.45±0.56) compared with those with relapsing disease (0.92±0.16; p=0.021). No significant differences in IMT of large vessels were observed between the two groups. Thus, in the axillary arteries, mean IMT values were 0.98±0.18 mm (right) and 0.88±0.30 mm (left) in newly diagnosed GCA, compared with 1.25±0.31 mm (right) and 1.07±0.28 mm (left) at relapse (right: p=0.072; left: p=0.260). In the subclavian arteries, wall thickness measured 1.04±0.15 mm (right) and 0.97±0.11 mm (left) in newly diagnosed patients, vs 1.20±0.31 mm (right) and 1.13±0.25 mm (left) at relapse (right: p=0.445; left: p=0.336). Similarly, no significant differences were observed in the common carotid arteries, with mean IMT values of 1.32±0.54 mm (right) and 1.11±0.04 mm (left) in patients with newly diagnosed disease compared with 1.14±0.17 mm (right) and 1.11±0.25 mm (left) in relapsing GCA (right: p=0.79; left: p=1.00).

In the temporal artery segments, IMT values were significantly in newly diagnosed disease. Mean IMT values in the common temporal arteries were 0.54±0.13 mm (right) and 0.55±0.15 mm (left) in newly diagnosed GCA, compared with 0.29±0.12 mm and 0.35±0.08 mm in relapsing disease (p<0.050). In the frontal branches, IMT measured 0.47±0.21 mm (right) and 0.55±0.15 mm (left) in newly diagnosed patients vs 0.24±0.09 mm and 0.29±0.10 mm in relapsing patients (p<0.050). Likewise, in the parietal branches, mean IMT values were 0.44±0.15 mm (right) and 0.43±0.09 mm (left) in newly diagnosed GCA compared with 0.22±0.05 mm and 0.21±0.06 mm in relapsing disease (p<0.050).

In patients reporting shoulder pain suggestive of PMR, additional musculoskeletal ultrasound of the shoulder region was performed. In all examined cases, ultrasound demonstrated inflammatory changes consistent with PMR, including effusions surrounding the long head of the biceps tendon and/or within the glenohumeral joint.

[68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET/CT

The administration of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 was well tolerated in all patients. In newly diagnosed GCA, PET/CT revealed localised, patient-specific increases in SUVmean and SUVmax across several vascular territories (figures 1 and 2), with the descending thoracal aorta displaying the highest uptake (SUVmax 4.25±0.81). Among supra-aortic branches, subclavian and axillary arteries demonstrated increased uptake (SUVmax 1.82–2.01), whereas brachial arteries showed the lowest uptake (SUVmax <1.5). Extravascular tracer accumulation was also observed, particularly in patients with concomitant symptoms (n=2). These patients exhibited higher SUVmax values in the right hip (mean 3.61±0.33 vs 1.90±0.74, p=0.022) and showed a trend towards increased uptake in the left hip and shoulders, which narrowly missed statistical significance (p=0.097–0.143).

When comparing newly diagnosed patients with relapsing GCA, in most areas investigated, tracer uptake was lower at diagnosis. In the aortic arch (SUVmax 2.78±0.89 vs 3.61±0.47; p=0.042), descending thoracal aorta (SUVmax 3.21±1.12 vs 4.25±0.81; p=0.053, SUVmean 2.03±0.79 vs 2.79±0.41; p=0.002) and abdominal aorta (2.72±0.54 vs 3.39±0.47; p=0.018), uptake was significantly lower in newly diagnosed patients. Supra-aortic branches followed a similar pattern, although without statistical significance (p>0.15). Brachial artery values remained uniformly low in both groups (table 2).

Clinical and laboratory assessment

At inclusion, CRP levels were elevated in patients with newly diagnosed GCA (73.1±88.1 mg/L), without significant differences compared with relapsing patients (44.3±43.5 mg/L; p=0.42). Circulating sVAP-1 levels were significantly reduced in newly diagnosed GCA (502.7±202.1 pg/mL) compared with relapsing patients (819.9±208.4 pg/mL; p=0.0145) and healthy controls (726.1±170.7 pg/mL; p=0.0446). In contrast, MMP2 concentrations were significantly increased in newly diagnosed patients (134.9±47.6 ng/mL) relative to relapsing patients (59.5±13.8 ng/mL; p=0.001) and controls (73.7±49.6 ng/mL; p=0.0386). Discussion

This study is pioneering to examine the role of VAP-1 in newly diagnosed and relapsing GCA, to assess the diagnostic utility of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET/CT for evaluating disease activity and to explore the influence of prednisolone exposure on tracer uptake and VAP-1-related pathways.

Previous research has established the safety, tolerability and efficacy of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET/CT in animals and humans.24 Its clinical utility has been demonstrated in the detection of inflammation in bacterial infections, pulmonary inflammation in a porcine acute respiratory distress syndrome model39 and synovitis in rabbits.22 Initial human applications have yielded promising results for conditions like rheumatoid arthritis,40 myocarditis41 and idiopathic inflammatory myopathies42 and we recently highlighted the method’s ability to detect vascular inflammation during GCA relapses, with increased tracer uptake in regions such as the aorta and subclavian arteries.15–17

In our current study, we provide further evidence that [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET/CT can specifically visualise vascular inflammation in active GCA. Importantly, in newly diagnosed patients, we observed distinct focal tracer uptake that paralleled vascular ultrasound findings of IMT thickening in corresponding arterial segments. Together with results from relapsing disease, these findings suggest that the targeted VAP-1 is locally upregulated on endothelial cells and VSMCs, and that [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET/CT reliably captures this process, thereby enabling in vivo imaging of inflammation in GCA.

Although our imaging results support VAP-1 involvement in GCA pathogenesis, the underlying mechanisms remain speculative. Evidence from other autoimmune diseases with vascular inflammation provides potential parallels: in granulomatosis with polyangiitis, for example, VAP-1 is strongly expressed in renal endothelium during active disease, contributing to endothelial barrier dysfunction.43 44 Likewise, in neuronal blood-brain barrier models, VAP-1 expression has been linked to endothelial activation, pro-inflammatory cytokine release and angiogenic signalling.45 Cells expressing human VAP-1 overproduce cytokines implicated in GCA pathogenesis, including IL-6 and IL-8, as well as adhesion molecules (ICAM, vascular cell adhesion molecule) and trophic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor and nerve growth factor. Importantly, IL-6 signalling appears to be enhanced in VAP-1-positive endothelial cells, while IL-6 receptor blockade reverses VAP-1-induced permeability changes.46 However, in our study, none of the patients had received tocilizumab at the time of imaging. Future studies will therefore need to clarify how IL-6 receptor inhibition modulates [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 tracer uptake.

In contrast, most of our patients were exposed to prednisolone, a drug known to rapidly suppress vascular inflammation. Prednisolone exposure was markedly higher in newly diagnosed patients, which paralleled the observation of lower tracer uptake compared with relapsing disease, despite vascular ultrasound depicting more pronounced large-vessel IMT. Consistently, tracer uptake across multiple vascular territories showed a strong negative association with prednisolone exposure. These findings provide compelling evidence that glucocorticoids rapidly downregulate VAP-1 expression, thereby reducing [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 binding in vivo.

Interestingly, SUVmean values demonstrated a stronger negative association with prednisolone exposure than SUVmax. This discrepancy may indicate that SUVmean more accurately reflects the global reduction of inflammatory activity across the vessel wall, whereas SUVmax is more influenced by focal ‘hot spots’ of peak inflammation that may require longer or higher cumulative glucocorticoid exposure to resolve. Thus, SUVmean may serve as a more sensitive biomarker for treatment response, while SUVmax might preferentially reflect residual focal inflammatory activity. Importantly, this treatment sensitivity contrasts with [18F]FDG-PET/CT, where persistent vascular uptake is frequently observed despite clinical remission, often reflecting vascular remodelling rather than active inflammation. Comparable findings have been reported for [18F]F-FAPI-PET/CT, where uptake may also persist in remission,11 reinforcing the notion that not all tracer signals directly reflect ongoing immune activity. In our study, the consistent downregulation of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 uptake under glucocorticoids underscores its potential as a more inflammation-specific and treatment-responsive imaging biomarker in GCA.

In addition to vascular uptake, we observed extravascular tracer accumulation in patients reporting PMR-like symptoms (which were mirrored by ultrasound findings) affecting the shoulder and/or pelvic girdle (n=6; newly diagnosed n=2, relapsing n=4). In this small subgroup, higher SUVmax values were noted in the hips and a trend towards increased uptake in the shoulders compared with patients without such symptoms. While, given the limited sample size, these observations should be interpreted cautiously, our findings suggest that [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET/CT may also capture periarticular and musculoskeletal inflammatory activity in PMR. This observation aligns with previous reports demonstrating that muscular inflammation in idiopathic inflammatory myopathies can be visualised with this tracer.42 If confirmed in larger cohorts, this feature may have potential clinical implications for the evaluation of patients with suspected PMR, in whom concomitant GCA occurs in up to 40% of cases.6 Future studies systematically evaluating [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET/CT in PMR and other musculoskeletal conditions are warranted. Multimodal imaging approaches incorporating structural imaging modalities and standardised assessment of PMR-related symptoms may further clarify its potential diagnostic value.

While membrane-bound VAP-1 serves as a transmembrane glycoprotein within the vascular wall, sVAP-1 arises from the proteolytic cleavage of its membrane-bound form by MMPs, releasing it into circulation.18 This allows VAP-1 to influence leucocyte migration in both transmembrane and soluble forms, with sVAP-1 contributing significantly to the circulating monoamine oxidase activity in human blood.17 Increased sVAP-1 expression has been observed in various chronic inflammatory conditions, including type 1 diabetes,45 chronic liver diseases,47 48 psoriasis,49 synovitis,22 active relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis50 and systemic lupus erythematosus.51 It is also linked to cardiovascular disorders such as diabetes mellitus complications,52 congestive heart failure,53 multiple cerebral infarctions54 and atherosclerosis,55 where sVAP-1 levels correlate with IMT and carotid plaque presence.56 In contrast, healthy individuals typically exhibit stable, lower sVAP-1 levels modulating the adhesive activity of its membrane-bound counterpart and enhancing leucocyte adhesion.

In our study, circulating sVAP-1 levels were significantly reduced in newly diagnosed GCA compared with both relapsing patients and healthy controls. Correlation analyses suggested a negative association between sVAP-1 concentrations and recent prednisolone exposure, supporting the concept that immunosuppressive treatment rapidly modulates the VAP-1 axis. Notably, sVAP-1 levels did not correlate with CRP, highlighting the potential of sVAP-1 as a marker of distinct inflammatory pathways rather than general systemic inflammation, although confirmation in larger cohorts is warranted. Given that MMPs cleave membrane-bound VAP-1 to generate its soluble form, we further examined MMP expression. Newly diagnosed GCA was characterised by a marked increase in MMP2 and MMP9, both significantly elevated compared with relapsing disease and controls. Levels of MMP3 were also higher, reaching statistical significance versus controls but not relapse. These findings confirm prior evidence assigning MMPs a key role in initiating vascular inflammation in GCA and indicate a dynamic interplay between the MMP system and the VAP-1 axis. Supporting this, higher sVAP-1 levels correlated with lower MMP2 and MMP9. Importantly, MMP2 concentrations showed strong negative associations with [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 vascular uptake across multiple vascular territories, with MMP3 displaying a similar but weaker pattern. These correlations suggest that high MMP activity may reduce tracer binding by limiting membrane-bound VAP-1 availability, thereby directly influencing the imaging readout. Thus, our findings provide first mechanistic insights into the regulation of the VAP-1/Siglec-9 axis in newly diagnosed GCA. On the one hand, MMPs contribute to the initiation of vascular inflammation; on the other hand, altered MMP-mediated cleavage may lower circulating sVAP-1 and attenuate [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 uptake. These observations underscore a complex, treatment-sensitive regulatory interplay between MMP activity and VAP-1 expression that warrants further investigation in larger, longitudinal cohorts.

In summary, this study provides novel insights into the potential role and underlying pathophysiology of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET/CT in newly diagnosed GCA. Our findings suggest that tracer uptake reflects vascular inflammation and declines with increasing glucocorticoid exposure, indicating a potential role for in vivo monitoring of therapy-induced resolution of inflammation. This may represent a crucial advantage over established imaging modalities, which often struggle to reliably distinguish active disease from vascular remodelling in GCA. Beyond the vessel wall, [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 also highlighted periarticular sites typical for PMR, suggesting added diagnostic value in patients with overlapping GCA/PMR phenotypes. At the serological biomarker level, reduced circulating sVAP-1, elevated MMP2/MMP9 in newly diagnosed disease and inverse associations between MMPs and vascular uptake support an MMP-dependent regulation of membrane VAP-1 that directly influences the imaging results. Collectively, our findings position [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET/CT as a promising, inflammation-specific tool for disease activity assessment and treatment monitoring in GCA.

However, some limitations must be acknowledged. First, the study was conducted in a relatively small, single-centre cohort, which may limit generalisability. While our findings support an association between tracer uptake and inflammatory activity, the current data do not allow a definitive distinction between inflammatory processes and potential contributions from vascular remodelling, particularly in patients with longer disease duration. Second, heterogeneous glucocorticoid exposure at inclusion introduces potential bias regarding treatment effects. Third, although we observed consistent associations between MMPs, sVAP-1 and tracer uptake, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference.

In addition, VAP-1 has been linked to several cardiometabolic conditions. Healthy controls were screened through medical history and clinical assessment to exclude known cardiovascular disease. However, no systematic cardiological evaluation was performed. Therefore, cardiovascular preconditions, for example, the presence of subclinical atherosclerotic changes cannot be entirely excluded and may potentially influence circulating sVAP-1 levels. The inclusion of an atherosclerotic comparator cohort for both imaging and laboratory assessment would be highly valuable to further explore the specificity of VAP-1-targeted imaging and should be considered in future studies.

Finally, healthy controls did not undergo PET/CT imaging, restricting direct comparisons of vascular uptake to patient groups only.

Future prospective longitudinal studies including different disease states such as diagnosis, remission and relapse will be required to further define the role of [68Ga]Ga-DOTA-Siglec-9 PET/CT as a biomarker in GCA.
