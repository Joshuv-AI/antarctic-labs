Pancreatic organoids and organoid-on-a-chip platforms: from disease modeling to precision therapy
Authors: Mengran Lin, Wenzhuang Ma, Teng Ma, Yanjie He, Huan Shi, Nan Ge, Jintao Guo, Ying Xie, Siyu Sun, Fan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPancreatic diseases, including diabetes, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, pancreatitis, and cystic fibrosis, impose a substantial clinical burden and are challenging to model using conventional experimental systems. Pancreatic organoids provide physiologically relevant three-dimensional models that recapitulate the key structural and functional features of the human pancreas. This review summarizes recent advances in pancreatic organoid and organoid-on-a-chip technologies, focusing on their applications in pancreatic disease modeling, therapeutic screening, and potential relevance to personalized treatment.Main textThis review discusses the construction of pancreatic organoids and pancreatic organoid-on-a-chip platforms, including key culture parameters and practical strategies for model establishment. The applications of pancreatic organoids in modeling pancreatic cancer, diabetes, pancreatitis, and cystic fibrosis are reviewed. In addition, the role of patient-derived organoids in therapeutic screening and their potential integration into personalized treatment workflows are evaluated. The enabling role of artificial intelligence in pancreatic organoid research is also discussed, followed by an overview of the current challenges and future perspectives.ConclusionPancreatic organoid-based platforms are promising tools for pancreatic disease modeling and therapeutic evaluation. Addressing the current limitations of cellular complexity, vascularization, reproducibility, and scalability is essential for expanding their research and clinical utility.

Background

Animal and classical cell line models are frequently used in biomedical research. However, notable differences exist between humans and animals regarding susceptibility to infectious diseases, developmental timescales, and cell-type diversification. Consequently, complete reliance on animal models may limit our understanding of its etiology and pathophysiology [1]. In vitro two-dimensional (2D) cell cultures lack multicellular interactions and native tissue architecture, making it difficult to evaluate the effects of cytokines and chemokines on tissue structure, cellular metabolism, gene expression, and drug resistance [2]. Remarkable advances have been made in stem cell research. For example, human embryonic stem cells (hESCs) have been successfully directed to differentiate into insulin-secreting cell types in vitro, offering a potential source for cell-based therapy for diabetes [3, 4]. The discovery of induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) has further advanced regenerative medicine by expanding the available sources for cell replacement and facilitating the development of in vitro disease models [5].

Organoids are three-dimensional (3D) cell aggregates cultured in vitro and are derived from stem cells or tissue-specific cells. Under the regulation of cytokines, small-molecule inhibitors/activators, specialized culture media, and other supplements, they can self-assemble and differentiate into functional 3D structures with characteristic cellular compositions and organizations. For example, Huang et al. [6] generated acinar-like organoids that exhibited gene expression profiles and structural characteristics consistent with human fetal pancreatic exocrine acinar cells and secreted immature zymogen granules. In a study involving 11 types of gastrointestinal tumors, Hogenson et al. [7] reported an 88% concordance in mutational profiles between patient-derived organoids (PDOs) and original donor tumors. Moreover, compared with conventional cancer cell lines, pancreatic tumor organoids show substantially elevated expression of multiple cancer-initiating stemness-related genes [8]. Collectively, these findings indicate that organoids can faithfully retain the genetic markers of their tissue of origin, recapitulate the key structural and functional features of the corresponding organs, and reproduce relevant mutational spectra. Thus, organoid technology represents a powerful tool for modeling human physiology and diseases with high fidelity.

Pancreatic disorders, ranging from highly lethal cancers to chronic conditions such as diabetes, represent a major healthcare challenge. Addressing this requires novel models that faithfully emulate human physiological complexity and the development of advanced therapeutic strategies. Organ-on-a-chip systems are microfluidic devices with miniaturized structures and microchannels that help guide the spatial organization of cells and enable tunable regulation of culture conditions by modulating fluidic and chemical parameters [9]. Although organoids have become valuable tools in disease research, they still face limitations in replicating the dynamic and spatially regulated microenvironments found in vivo. Integrating organoids with organ-on-a-chip technology to form organoid-on-a-chip platforms may offer an effective strategy to overcome these constraints. Figure 1 chronologically outlines the key milestones in the development of pancreatic organoids and organoid-on-a-chip technologies. Emerging therapeutic strategies have shown promising translational potential. For instance, Zimislecel, an allogeneic stem cell-derived islet cell therapy, has entered phase 1/2 clinical trials [10]. Simultaneously, artificial intelligence (AI) is emerging as an enabling technology in pancreatic organoid research, with potential applications in high-throughput image analysis and drug-response prediction. These advances may improve the efficiency, reproducibility, and predictive value of pancreatic organoid-based models for studying pancreatic diseases.

Although research on pancreatic organoids has been increasing, existing reviews have mostly focused on specific diseases or single applications, and systematic overviews of the applications of pancreatic organoids and organoid-on-a-chip platforms in pancreatic diseases remain relatively limited. A recent systematic review [11] evaluated the translational potential of PDOs in precision medicine across multiple dimensions, including predictive validity, biomarker strength, pharmacogenetics, and clinical trial alignment, providing important guidance for optimizing the pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) PDO platform. In contrast, the present review differs from and complements previous work by adopting a broader perspective on technological development and disease modeling. Specifically, we discuss the construction strategies of pancreatic organoids and pancreatic organoid-on-a-chip platforms, including key parameters for model establishment; summarize the applications of these platforms in multiple pancreatic diseases, including pancreatic cancer, diabetes, pancreatitis, and cystic fibrosis, with a focus on disease modeling and drug screening; and discuss the current limitations and future directions in clinical translation. This review highlights the supportive role of AI in pancreatic organoid research. Overall, this review aims to provide a comprehensive perspective on pancreatic organoid-based platforms, ranging from disease modeling to precision medicine applications.

Construction of pancreatic organoids

Directed differentiation of pancreatic organoids

The stepwise differentiation of pancreatic cell lineages is governed by a highly conserved, complex network of transcription factors and signaling pathways. Figure 2 summarizes the key transcription factors and pathways involved in pancreatic differentiation. A comprehensive understanding of this network provides an indispensable blueprint for directing the differentiation of pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) into pancreatic organoids, as successful organoid-construction strategies often rely on precise targeting of these key regulators to enhance the efficiency and purity of specific cell fates. However, it is important to recognize that the current knowledge of pancreatic development is largely derived from mouse models. Although the regulatory network is highly conserved, murine pancreatic development cannot fully recapitulate human pancreatic development. For instance, although humans and mice share many transcription factors, such as PDX1, the target gene networks and epigenetic regulation of PDX1 exhibit species-specific differences between mice and humans [12].

Cell source

Pancreatic organoids are primarily constructed using ESCs, iPSCs, or tissue cells (Fig. 3). Human PSCs (hPSCs), including ESCs and iPSCs, are the most commonly used. hESCs can form islet-like 3D clusters expressing β-cell markers (PDX1, C-peptide, insulin, and MAFA) with glucose-responsive insulin secretion in vivo and in vitro [13]. Huang et al. [6] subsequently constructed acinus- and duct-like organoids representing neonatal states using established ESC differentiation protocols. IPSCs are reprogrammed somatic cells that can develop into most cell types. ESCs and iPSCs can generate organoids containing multiple germ-layer components, better mimicking embryonic development and epithelial-mesenchymal interactions. However, hPSC-derived organoids require longer culture times and more complex procedures. Furthermore, PSC-derived organoids that fail to eliminate partially differentiated stem cells during transplantation have an increased risk of teratoma formation [14]. While iPSCs offer ethical and noninvasive advantages over ESCs [15], they carry tumorigenic risks from residual reprogramming factors [16] and exhibit lower genomic stability and higher transcriptional noise [17]. In summary, hPSC-derived organoids, owing to their self-renewal capacity and multilineage differentiation potential, are suitable for constructing disease models associated with genetic mutations and can also serve as expandable, directionally differentiated cell sources for cell replacement therapy.

Cell sources for pancreatic organoid generation. Stem cell-derived pancreatic organoids are mainly generated from embryonic stem cells (ESCs) or induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) through directed differentiation. These cells typically pass through a pancreatic progenitor stage and then undergo further differentiation and maturation in a three-dimensional culture environment. Tissue cell-derived pancreatic organoids can be established from digested primary pancreatic epithelial cells, ductal cells, or tumor cells. Supported by a three-dimensional matrix and defined culture factors, these cells can self-organize into organoids while maintaining the corresponding lineage or disease-specific features

As undifferentiated precursors in mature tissues, adult stem cells (ASCs) generate only epithelial organoids that lack mesenchymal components, resulting in simpler structures than hPSC-derived counterparts. Although the existence of ASCs in the adult pancreas has long been debated, it is now widely accepted that cell populations with progenitor characteristics are present in the adult pancreas. For instance, a recent single-cell RNA sequencing study identified a population of putative PROCR⁺ precursor cells within the mouse pancreatic islets. These cells are capable of clonal expansion and can generate all endocrine lineages under homeostatic conditions in vivo [18]. Importantly, organoids derived from these cells can reverse hyperglycemia upon transplantation into diabetic mouse models, providing direct functional evidence of their potential. Furthermore, research has demonstrated that LGR5, which is not expressed under physiological conditions, becomes activated in mouse pancreatic ductal cells following injury (e.g., partial duct ligation) or under specific in vitro culture conditions (e.g., RSPO1 stimulation). These LGR5⁺ cells can form long-term expandable organoids and differentiate into both ductal and endocrine cell types after transplantation, highlighting their potential as injury-induced bipotent progenitor cells [19, 20]. In summary, the adult pancreatic progenitor cells identified to date are predominantly lineage-restricted, typically remain quiescent under normal conditions, and require specific triggers for activation, such as injury or particular culture environments. Most of these findings were derived from mouse models; evidence supporting the existence of multipotent adult pancreatic stem cells capable of stably generating all three major epithelial lineages—acinar, ductal, and endocrine—remains insufficient.

Tissue-derived pancreatic organoids better maintain primary tissue characteristics with a lower teratoma risk, albeit with reduced heterogeneity. Representative models include ductal cell-derived organoids [19, 21, 22] and patient tumor-derived organoids [23–25].

Of note, pancreatic cells exhibit considerable plasticity. β-cells may undergo dedifferentiation in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) [26]. In addition, under injury and inflammatory conditions, pancreatic ductal cells may display plasticity [27], whereas acinar cells may undergo acinar-to-ductal metaplasia [28]. Therefore, careful attention should be paid to the influence of phenotypic stability and lineage conversion on the interpretation of experimental results when designing and using pancreatic organoid models.

Methods for constructing pancreatic organoids

Stem cell-derived pancreatic organoid generation involves three key steps: (1) endodermal differentiation through developmental pathway modulation, (2) pancreatic progenitor induction via two-dimensional (2D) culture, and (3) 3D maturation using stage-specific bioactive factors. Differentiation of each organoid is induced using a range of bioactive nutrients at designated differentiation stages to emulate the regulation of signaling pathways implicated in pancreatic growth and development in vivo, including the transforming growth factor (TGF)-β, WNT, SHH, Ras homologue gene family A (RhoA)–Rho-associated protein kinase (ROCK), retinoic acid (RA), bone morphogenetic protein (BMP), and Notch pathways (Table 1).

3D culture methods can be broadly classified into scaffold-free aggregation-based, scaffold-supported, and air-liquid interface cultures. Scaffold-free aggregation-based cultures mainly rely on cell self-aggregation to form 3D structures, including hanging-drop culture, low-attachment plate or microwell-based aggregation culture, and stirred-suspension culture. These methods are suitable for generating spherical structures and are commonly used to construct islet-like organoids, pseudoislets, spheroid cell aggregates, and PDO models. In the hanging-drop method, droplets of cell suspension are placed on a culture plate, which is then inverted. This method promotes organoid formation via gravity-mediated cell aggregation, and organoid size can be controlled by adjusting droplet parameters and cell density [49]. It has been advantageous for generating multicellular pancreatic organoids [50]. Low-attachment plate aggregation culture reduces cell adhesion to the culture surface, promoting the self-aggregation of pancreatic cells into spherical 3D structures. Compared with conventional low-attachment cultures, microwell structures can partially restrict the space available for cell aggregation, improving the size uniformity of cell clusters [51]. Stirred-suspension cultures rely on spinner or rotating flasks to provide constant agitation and evenly distribute nutrients and oxygen. However, the resultant shear forces may compromise cell physiology and limit size control [52–54]. For these aggregation-dependent, scaffold-free systems, organoid size and nutrient diffusion are key factors that affect model quality. An increase in the number of seeded cells is usually associated with an increase in the volume of 3D cell aggregates; however, excessively large aggregates increase the diffusion distance for oxygen and nutrients, reducing cell viability in the central region. In addition, size heterogeneity among organoids complicates functional assays and data interpretation because organoids of different sizes contain different numbers of cells and differ in diffusion conditions and metabolic states [55]. Overall, balancing cell aggregation, size control, and nutrient diffusion is essential for improving the reproducibility and functional consistency of the model.

Scaffold-based construction methods provide an extracellular matrix (ECM) that mimics cell–ECM interactions in the natural niche of pancreatic tissue. These methods are more suitable for constructing pancreatic epithelial organoids that require polarity establishment or long-term expansion, such as ductal cell-derived organoids, hPSC-derived duct-like or acinar-like organoids, and conventional PDAC PDOs. Compared with scaffold-free aggregation systems, scaffolds not only provide 3D spatial support for cells but may also partially buffer the adverse effects of shear stress during large-scale cultures. In scaffold-based systems, ECM secreted by cells can be distributed within the scaffold framework throughout the 3D porous structure, forming a relatively stable support network. In contrast, in suspended cell aggregates, ECM proteins are often concentrated mainly at the periphery of the cell cluster, forming a capsule-like structure, whereas the internal cells may still lack sufficient ECM support. Therefore, scaffolds are not only physical carriers for cell culture but also influence ECM distribution and structural stability within the organoids [56]. Although Matrigel has an undefined composition and batch-to-batch variability, it remains the most commonly used natural scaffold [47, 57, 58]. Notably, even under the same culture medium conditions, different ECM materials may induce markedly different pancreatic organoid morphologies [59]. This indicates that the ECM type can shape the spatial architecture and tissue-like morphology of the organoids. Besides Matrigel, alternative materials such as decellularized pancreatic ECM (dpECM) also show considerable potential [60, 61]. Synthetic scaffolds can overcome some limitations of natural matrices by allowing precise control over the material composition, pore size, and mechanical properties, while also promoting organoid maturation. For example, compared with Matrigel, Amikagel enhances the expression of pancreatic markers NKX6.1 and PDX1 [62].

Air-liquid interface culture exposes the top layer of cultured cells to air and the basal layer to a liquid medium. This method enhances apical–basal polarity and promotes tumor tissue maturation through hyperoxia [4, 63], although it carries an increased risk of contamination [64, 65].

Persisting challenges include the difficulty in replicating the precise concentration gradients of developmental signaling molecules and growth factors. Emerging technologies, such as microfluidics for creating defined concentration gradients and 3D bioprinting for specifying the spatial location of cells and factors, offer promising solutions for enhancing organoid accuracy and reproducibility [66]. In summary, an effective platform for constructing organoids should precisely manipulate the organoid composition, size, and functional maturity.

Construction of a pancreatic organoid-on-a-chip

Organoid-on-chip platforms integrate microfluidic perfusion with traditional organoid cultures to better mimic in vivo conditions. These systems typically comprise three core components: (1) polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS)-based culture chambers housing multiple organoids, (2) interconnected microchannels for nutrient/organoid communication, and (3) perfusion pumps that generate physiological flow (Fig. 4). Porous membranes often separate chambers from channels to minimize shear stress [67–69]. The organoid-on-a-chip system requires precise regulation of the flow rate and shear forces through computational modeling to maintain physiological conditions. Perfusion pumps provide controlled fluid dynamics, whereas supplementary components enable microenvironment modulation using mechanical, biochemical, electromagnetic, or optical methods. Flow-related shear stress

requires optimization of channel geometry and flow rate

One key challenge in constructing organoid-on-a-chip systems is controlling the flow rate and shear stress. Flow rate affects not only the delivery of nutrients and stimulatory factors but also the retention of local paracrine signals and the mechanical stress cells experience. Low-flow perfusion has been used to mimic interstitial fluid-like flow in islet-on-a-chip systems. For example, Jun et al. [55] showed that slower interstitial-like flow reduces shear-related damage and helps maintain islet function. In parallel, physiological interstitial flow in tissues has been reported to be approximately 0.1–2 μm/s, although islet-specific interstitial flow parameters have not yet been directly measured. Therefore, fluidic conditions should not be defined by a fixed physiological flow rate but should be optimized according to the specific model, experimental purpose, and organoid type.

High-throughput formats and sensor integration can further enhance data acquisition in organoid-on-chip platforms. In high-throughput designs, transparent-bottom 384-well plates or microwell arrays are suitable for single cells or small cell aggregates and can increase drug-screening throughput [58]. The integration of sensors and electrophysiology-based microfluidic chips enables real-time, noninvasive monitoring, which is a major advantage of organoid-on-a-chip systems over conventional endpoint assays [77, 78]. In addition, automation and miniaturization are important directions for future development. Schuster et al. [75] developed a multiplexer device controlled by solenoid valves and custom software, enabling programmed fluid delivery, supporting automated drug administration, and continuous analysis.

Gene editing of organoids

Clustered regulatory interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-associated nuclease 9 (CRISPR-Cas9) is a gene-editing tool that comprises nuclease Cas9, CRISPR RNA (crRNA), and trans-activating crRNA (tracrRNA). The combination of tracrRNA and crRNA forms a single guide RNA (sgRNA). The precise gene editing achieved by Cas9 depends on the recognition of the protospacer-adjacent motif (PAM) by the sgRNA [85]. This technology has proven invaluable for creating disease-specific organoid models, including monogenic diabetes models through targeted islet organoid mutations [86] and PDAC models via CDKN2A deletions in ductal organoids [57]. CRISPR-Cas9 has also been used to generate genetically modified liver, gastrointestinal, and lung organoids [87–89]. In addition, numerous diseases have been attributed to point mutations. Base editors (BEs) and prime editors (PEs) enable precise single-nucleotide changes without double-strand breaks, minimizing indel mutations [90].

Whole-genome sequencing and single-cell RNA-seq analyses have revealed that both Cas9 and adenine BE can induce off-target structural variants in mouse embryos [91]. Furthermore, the editing efficiency of existing systems requires further improvement. To address these challenges, this field has rapidly advanced in recent years, and to enhance specificity, researchers have developed multiple high-fidelity Cas9 variants, such as Sniper-Cas9, eSpCas9 (1.1), SpCas9-HF1, HypaCas9, HiFi Cas9, evoCas9, Sniper2L, and EnAsCas12f [92–99]. These variants successfully reduce off-target effects while maintaining the editing activity. Zhou et al. [100] developed a continuous directed evolution system in mammalian cells and used it to evolve the deaminase domains of BE3, ABEmax, and ABE8e. The resulting variants exhibit narrower editing windows and significantly reduced off-target activity. Furthermore, a recently developed Rep-X-assisted ciPE system achieved editing efficiencies of 2.7–55.4%. Notably, this system demonstrated 96.7% editing purity at the disease-relevant BRCA1 locus, highlighting its potential for precise disease modeling and gene therapy [101].

The applicability of gene-editing tools continues to grow. For instance, mitochondrial-targeted Cas9 enables direct editing of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), providing a crucial technological approach for studying diseases associated with mtDNA mutations [102].

Therefore, owing to the rapid development of gene-editing tools, researchers will be equipped with a more diverse toolkit for constructing genetically modified pancreatic organoids in the future. Given the broad prospects of organoids in disease modeling and regenerative medicine, the selection of gene-editing tools should involve a systematic evaluation of editing specificity, efficiency, and safety.

Applications of organoids and organoid-on-a-chip platforms in pancreatic disease modeling

Diabetes mellitus modeling

Diabetes mellitus, characterized by hyperglycemia resulting from insulin deficiency and/or insulin resistance, affects 529 million individuals worldwide [103, 104]. hPSC-derived β-like cells and islet organoid systems have emerged as valuable platforms for diabetes modeling, as they recapitulate key aspects of islet architecture, intercellular crosstalk, and gene regulation (Fig. 5).

Islet organoids for modeling diabetes-associated pathophysiology

In type 1 diabetes (T1DM)-related disease modeling, stem cell-derived β-like cells and islet-like organoids are commonly used to model pro-inflammatory cytokine-mediated β-cell injury. Millman et al. differentiated iPSCs derived from patients with T1DM into functional SC-β cells and treated them with IL-1β/TNF-α/IFN-γ, which led to a reduction in β-cell markers such as C-peptide+/NKX6-1+. This suggests that the model can recapitulate inflammation-induced β-cell phenotypic changes. However, T1DM-derived cells did not exhibit greater cytokine sensitivity than non-diabetic cells, indicating that the patient-specific disease modeling significance of this system should be interpreted with caution [52]. Another study generated islet-like organoids from healthy donor-derived iPSCs and exposed them to short-term IFN-γ + IL-1β or IFN-α stimulation. Cytokine exposure induces β-cell phenotypic loss, ER stress, apoptosis, and upregulation of inflammatory chemokines and human leukocyte antigen (HLA) class I molecules, suggesting that this model can mimic β-cell inflammatory injury in T1DM [105]. Overall, these models reflect T1DM-associated cytokine-mediated β-cell damage. However, because they largely rely on short-term exogenous cytokine stimulation and lack immune cell co-culture, they more closely resemble acute inflammatory injury models rather than complete autoimmune T1DM models.

To model T2DM-associated glucolipotoxic stress, Mir-Coll et al. [106] exposed pseudoislets to prolonged high glucose and free fatty acid (FFA) conditions. This model reproduces key features of β-cell dysfunction, including elevated basal insulin secretion, impaired glucose-stimulated insulin secretion (GSIS), reduced intracellular insulin content, and decreased chronic insulin secretion under combined metabolic stress. Notably, these defects were not accompanied by marked changes in cell viability or apoptosis; therefore, this model is more suitable for investigating β-cell functional decompensation during T2DM progression rather than end-stage β-cell loss.

Islet organoids for genotype–phenotype studies

Typically, researchers can use patient-derived iPSCs carrying diabetes-associated variants or genetically engineered hPSCs harboring specific mutations and differentiate them into islet-like organoids. Isogenic controls can be generated by correcting the mutation or introducing the same mutation into healthy hPSCs. By comparing these paired models, researchers can more precisely determine how specific risk genes or mutations affect pancreatic endocrine differentiation, β-cell maturation, insulin secretion, gene regulatory networks, and cellular stress responses (Table 3).

Organoid-on-a-chip platforms for modeling metabolic crosstalk

Tao et al. [80] developed a hiPSC-derived liver-islet multi-organoid chip, where liver and islet organoids were cultured in separate chambers connected by parallel microchannels. Pump-driven recirculating perfusion enabled the dynamic exchange of culture medium and metabolites between the two organoid compartments. Compared with monoculture, this system enhanced tissue-specific functions and maturation-associated phenotypes in both organoid types and recapitulated insulin-stimulated hepatic glucose utilization and GSIS in islet organoids. Under 25 mM high-glucose stimulation, both organoid types show impaired mitochondrial function and reduced glucose transporter expression. To mimic metabolic dysregulation associated with obesity, T2DM, and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), another study used a pump-free, dual-recirculating organoid-on-a-chip platform wherein hPSC-derived islet and liver organoids were maintained in two gravity-driven recirculating loops separated by a permeable membrane, allowing bidirectional exchange between the two compartments. Upon FFA and fructose stimulation, liver organoids developed steatosis and insulin resistance, whereas islet organoids secreted pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-22, IL-8, and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 [81]. Together, these systems effectively recapitulate the key aspects of the liver-islet axis and mimic T2DM manifestations under adverse dietary conditions. However, incorporating additional metabolic tissues, such as islets, liver, muscle, and adipose tissue, remains challenging for comprehensive glucose homeostasis modeling.

Overall, although islet organoids and organoid-on-a-chip models can recapitulate diabetes-related β-cell inflammatory injury, metabolic stress, and inter-organ crosstalk, limited long-term culture stability remains a common bottleneck for current diabetes organoid and organoid-on-a-chip models. Even in relatively long-term organoid-on-a-chip co-culture systems, the culture duration is limited to approximately 2–4 weeks [67–69]. Consequently, current models largely rely on short-term stimulation to induce acute β-cell injury, making it difficult to fully recapitulate the chronic and dynamic progression of diabetes.

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma modeling

PDAC is a highly lethal malignancy with a 5-year survival rate of only 11% [120]. Despite diagnostic advances, most cases present with metastasis and exhibit resistance to treatment [121]. These challenges underscore the need for physiologically relevant models to study PDAC progression, identify early biomarkers, and overcome therapeutic resistance.

Pancreatic organoids for modeling PDAC initiation and early progression

Organoid models are well-suited for dissecting how genetic mutations shape the early tumorigenic trajectory of PDAC. These models are established in two ways: first, PDAC-associated genetic alterations are introduced at the hESC/iPSC or pancreatic progenitor stage using CRISPR/Cas9 or inducible transgenes, followed by differentiation into acinus-like or duct-like organoids; second, patient-derived iPSCs carrying mutations are directly differentiated into organoids to model tumor initiation in a genetically predisposed background. Huang et al. [6] established hPSC-derived acinus-like and duct-like organoids and found that KRASG12D induced ADM-like changes and early pancreatic cancer-like lesions more efficiently in acinus-like organoids than in duct-like organoids. Conversely, GNASR201C preferentially induces cystic growth and mucin-associated changes in duct-like organoids. This lineage-dependent difference suggests that PDAC initiation is not solely determined by driver mutations but also depends on cellular origin and plasticity.

Breunig et al. [57] further optimized hPSC differentiation toward the ductal lineage and established relatively homogeneous pancreatic duct-like organoids (PDLOs) with polarized architecture and ductal function. This more clearly defined ductal model reduces confounding effects arising from differences in cell composition and differentiation state. Notably, KRASG12D produces distinct phenotypes across different ductal organoid systems. The duct-like organoids generated by Huang et al. were closer to a neonatal exocrine state, in which KRASG12D mainly induced progenitor-like changes in the cells. In contrast, the PDLOs generated by Breunig et al. showed more homogeneous ductal epithelial features and functional ductal phenotypes; upon KRASG12D stimulation, they exhibited lumen filling, DNA damage, senescence-like responses, and partial epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), with EMT-related changes showing KRASG12D dose dependence. Furthermore, Breunig et al. found that in the context of CDKN2A loss, KRASG12D drove PDLOs to form dedifferentiated, invasive PDAC-like lesions upon transplantation [6, 57]. Unlike models with complete CDKN2A loss, Merkle et al. generated PDLOs from patient-derived CDKN2A-mutated iPSCs and induced KRASG12D expression in a hereditary pancreatic cancer-predisposing background. This model similarly showed polarity disruption, DNA damage, and EMT-like changes and formed PDAC-like tumors after orthotopic transplantation [122]. Taken together, these studies show that KRAS-driven ductal tumorigenesis is not a fixed process but is shaped by organoid maturity, ductal cell state, KRAS signaling strength, and CDKN2A functional status.

Pancreatic organoids for modeling PDAC invasion, metastasis, and tumor heterogeneity

Unlike mutation-driven models of PDAC initiation, organoid models of tumor progression typically use PDAC cells or patient-derived tumor organoids, with a greater focus on ECM-dependent invasion, morphogenesis, and lumen formation. Randriamanantsoa et al. [59] showed that ECM composition has a decisive impact on PDAC organoid morphology: the same murine PDAC cells mainly formed spherical or cystic structures in Matrigel, whereas they developed branched tubular organoids in floating collagen I, suggesting that a collagen I-rich matrix is more permissive for invasion and complex morphogenesis. Building on this concept, Jeong et al. established a collagen I-based invasion model using freshly derived human PDAC organoids and classified their invasive behavior as either collective or mesenchymal. Mesenchymal organoids exhibited more invasive protrusions and a higher inverse circularity score. Notably, these invasion patterns were not significantly associated with common driver mutations, including KRAS, CDKN2A, TP53, and SMAD4, but appeared to reflect differences in transcriptional programs. Further analysis showed that mesenchymal invasion was enriched for EMT/basal-like and fast-progressor programs, whereas collective invasion was closer to a classical-like state, echoing the findings of Randriamanantsoa et al. in murine PDAC organoids [59, 123].

Branched organoid models can also be used to dissect intratumoral and intertumoral heterogeneity in PDAC. Branched organoids grown in collagen gels can recapitulate the diverse morphological and phenotypic spectra observed in murine and human PDAC. Distinct organoid phenotypes correspond to specific transcriptional programs and tumor cell states, particularly those associated with EMT/EMP plasticity. After orthotopic transplantation, different phenotypes exhibit distinct metastatic capacities [124]. These findings indicate that this model not only recapitulates branching morphogenesis in PDAC but also links morphological heterogeneity with transcriptional states, metastatic potential, and other progression-related features.

Overall, these models show that key phenotypes during PDAC progression are highly dependent on ECM composition and EMT/EMP plasticity. Compared with initiation-focused models, branched and invasive PDAC organoids are better suited for studying tumor morphogenesis, invasion patterns, phenotypic heterogeneity, and metastatic potential.

Pancreatic organoids for modeling the PDAC tumor microenvironment (TME)

The PDAC TME, which contains abundant ECM, tumor endothelial cells (TECs), cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), and diverse immune cells, plays a critical role in tumor progression and therapeutic resistance [125]. Conventional epithelial organoids lack key TME components and are therefore limited in their ability to model TME-dependent tumor behaviors. Therefore, constructing PDAC organoid models that integrate stromal, vascular, ECM, and immune components is essential for establishing more physiologically relevant disease models.

CAFs promote PDAC progression through distinct subtypes (apCAFs, iCAFs, and myCAFs) that drive proliferation, metastasis, and chemoresistance [126–128]. In PDAC PDO/CAF co-culture models, CAFs promoted organoid proliferation and increased the expression of EMT-related genes in PDAC organoid cells. Conversely, co-culture induces a pro-inflammatory phenotype in CAFs, suggesting that this model recapitulates CAF-mediated tumor–stroma interactions [129]. To restore CAF heterogeneity in the human PDAC microenvironment, hiPSC-derived mesenchymal cells (MCs) can be used as a CAF source for iCAFs, myCAFs, and apCAFs in organoids [47]. Furthermore, 3D co-culture models of PDAC organoids and pancreatic stellate cells (PaSCs) have been used to dissect CAF subtype differentiation and identify an interferon-response CAF subtype, indicating that organoid co-culture systems can also be used to study CAF plasticity [130].

Vascular components can also be incorporated into PDAC organoid models to study how vascular niches regulate the tumor cell state. In an assembloid model composed of PDAC organoid-derived cancer cells, human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs), and patient-matched peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), JAG1–Notch/WNT-related signaling between ECs and PDAC cells was associated with the reacquisition of a CIC-like state by ductal-like cancer cells, suggesting that the vascular niche may contribute to PDAC cell plasticity and cell-state heterogeneity [131].

Besides cellular components, ECM properties and physicochemical cues influence PDAC organoid phenotypes. Common basement membrane extract-derived matrices, including Matrigel, Cultrex, and UltiMatrix, differ in their effects on organoid growth, with Matrigel promoting PDAC organoid proliferation more effectively than Cultrex or UltiMatrix [132]. Engineered gelatin matrices with tunable stiffness further show that mechanical cues can regulate stemness, as stiffer matrices enhance stem-like properties in PDOs [133]. Furthermore, an acidic TME (pH 6.5) promotes aggressive growth and therapy resistance in wild-type p53 organoids through sustained upregulation of CDA and RRM2, even after returning to normal pH [134].

Immune cells are also important components of the PDAC TME. PDAC is typically characterized by an immunosuppressive microenvironment with limited CD8 + T-cell infiltration and effector function, and an enrichment of immunosuppressive populations, such as MDSCs, TAMs, and Tregs. These immune cells do not act in isolation but interact with tumor cells, CAFs, ECM, and vascular components to form a complex regulatory network that collectively promotes immune evasion and therapeutic resistance [135]. Therefore, incorporating immune cells into PDAC organoid models is important for modeling tumor–immune interactions. Early organotypic co-culture models have integrated patient-derived PDAC organoids with CAFs and T cells within a single 3D system. These models showed tumor-dependent T-cell migration toward organoids, suggesting their utility in studying tumor–stroma–immune interactions [136]. Further organoid–immune cell co-culture models have been used to reconstruct myeloid cell-mediated immunosuppression and immune escape in PDAC. PD-L1-positive PDAC organoids were co-cultured with autologous cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) and polymorphonuclear myeloid-derived suppressor cells (PMN-MDSCs). In the absence of PMN-MDSCs, PD-1 blockade promotes CTL proliferation and induces tumor organoid death [137]. In a PDAC organoid model comprising macrophages, ECs, and MCs, macrophages shifted from a pro-inflammatory state to a pro-angiogenic phenotype, promoting endothelial network formation and PDAC cell survival. This finding suggests that multicellular organoid platforms can not only model interactions between tumor cells and immune cells but also help investigate how immune cells promote PDAC progression through vascular and stromal remodeling [138]. However, most current models incorporate only selected immune cell populations and, therefore, cannot fully recapitulate the complex immune microenvironment of PDAC, leaving a gap toward more physiologically relevant immune microenvironment organoid models.

Overall, multicellular PDAC organoid models have greater physiological relevance than conventional PDOs, as they incorporate stromal, vascular, immune, and ECM-related components. However, these models are limited in their ability to recapitulate the spatial heterogeneity of the PDAC TME. Spatial multi-omics studies have shown that PDAC tumors are not composed of a homogeneous mixture of stromal and immune components but contain distinct tumor microenvironmental subregions (subTMEs) that can coexist within the same tumor. Among them, reactive subTMEs are enriched in activated fibroblasts, vascular structures, and immune infiltration, whereas deserted subTMEs are characterized by ECM deposition [139]. In addition, cancer cells and CAFs in the tumor center and invasive front exhibit distinct transcriptional states, further highlighting the spatial organization of malignant and stromal phenotypes [140]. Therefore, the key challenge in PDAC TME modeling is not merely adding missing cell types but reconstructing microenvironments with spatial organization and functional compartmentalization.

Future models should more precisely control the spatial positioning of tumor cells and microenvironmental components while recapitulating local gradients in microenvironmental signals. In addition, model construction should, where possible, match patient-specific TME heterogeneity and incorporate autologous or isogenic cellular components. These advances may enable PDAC organoid platforms to more faithfully capture individual TMEs and enhance their utility in studying TME interactions and personalized therapeutic testing.

Organoid-on-a-chip platforms for modeling the PDAC microenvironment

Compared with conventional organoid cultures, organoid-on-a-chip platforms can integrate organoids with stromal, vascular, and immune components in predefined spatial compartments. With advantages such as spatial control and dynamic perfusion, these platforms are better suited for reconstructing complex cellular interactions within the PDAC TME. First, they can be used to model tumor–stromal interactions and matrix-remodeling processes. In a vascularized InVADE organoid-chip system, PDOs were co-cultured with primary human fibroblasts and endothelial cells to generate tumor tissues containing perfusable vessel-like structures. This model showed that fibroblasts promoted organoid growth, whereas organoids induced fibroblast activation toward a myofibroblast-like phenotype. Fibroblast–organoid crosstalk further enhanced collagen deposition, matrix contraction, and tissue stiffness [141]. Another dual-chamber PDO tumor chip co-cultured PDO with PaSCs and macrophages. This model showed that stromal cells enhanced cancer cell growth and invasive phenotypes, whereas PDAC cells induced PaSC activation and promoted the transition of macrophages toward a protumorigenic state [142].

Furthermore, organoid-on-a-chip platforms can be used to construct PDAC immune microenvironment models. PDAC organoids, PaSCs, HUVEC-derived endothelial tubules, and PBMCs were separately integrated into the vascular, stromal, and tumor compartments to simulate the migration of immune cells from a vessel-like structure through the stromal region toward the tumor organoids. This model showed that PaSCs formed a physical stromal barrier and shaped a biochemical microenvironment through compartment-dependent secretion of immune mediators, retaining a subset of immune cells [73]. It models stroma-mediated immune exclusion and impaired infiltration of immune cells in PDAC.

Pancreatic cystic fibrosis modeling

Cystic fibrosis (CF) is a genetic disorder that affects multiple organs, including the lungs, liver, intestines, and pancreas. It is caused by mutations in the cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR) gene that disrupt the ion channels it encodes, leading to dysfunction of epithelial cell water–electrolyte homeostasis [143]. This imbalance causes obstruction due to viscous pancreatic ductal secretions, leading to inflammation and tissue damage.

Organoids provide an ideal model for studying pancreatic duct ion transport in a physiologically relevant context. Organoids derived from mouse pancreatic duct fragments exhibit ion transport activity comparable to that of freshly isolated primary tissues, confirming the reliability of this model for investigating ion transport in the pancreatic ductal epithelium [144]. Furthermore, studies using porcine CF organoids have revealed that chloride ions, rather than bicarbonate, serve as the primary driver of forskolin-induced swelling, highlighting the utility of this model for in-depth research on chloride permeability in pancreatic duct epithelial cells [145, 146].

The organoid model has significantly advanced our understanding of the mechanisms underlying pancreatic pathology in CF. In human systems, iPSC-derived CF organoids develop normally while recapitulating the phenotypic defects in CFTR function, suggesting that CFTR mutations may not affect pancreatic development but impair its function [147]. A study using mouse models and iPSC-derived pancreatic ductal organoids from patients with cystic fibrosis, together with super-resolution imaging, has demonstrated that CFTR not only functions as an ion channel but also serves as a pivotal organizer of apical membrane signaling microdomains [148]. CFTR deficiency disrupts both the physical and functional coupling between CFTR and plasma membrane calcium ATPase 4 (PMCA4), resulting in the mislocalization of calmodulin, loss of PMCA4 activity, and subsequent breakdown of intracellular calcium homeostasis. This “calcium toxicity” leads to mitochondrial dysfunction, increased apoptosis, and heightened susceptibility of pancreatic ductal cells to injury [148]. Organoid models have also been used to elucidate the cell-specific roles of CFTR in disease pathogenesis. Studies demonstrate that CFTR expression in ductal cells across different organs is precisely regulated by a cell type-specific “enhancer repertoire.” For instance, in primary pancreatic duct organoids, CFTR transcription primarily relies on distal enhancers characteristic of the airway epithelium, with minimal use of intestinal-characteristic intronic enhancers, whereas distinct regulatory patterns are observed in bile duct and sweat gland cells [149]. These findings provide a molecular basis for understanding organ-specific variations in CF-related pathologies.

Organoid-on-a-chip systems can be used to model CF complications, such as CF-related diabetes. Co-culture of CFTR-deficient pancreatic ductal cells with islets demonstrated impaired insulin secretion, linking exocrine dysfunction and endocrine failure [150].

Pancreatitis modeling

Chronic pancreatitis is a long-term evolutionary process that progresses from recurrent episodes of acute inflammation to gradual chronic fibrosis and glandular atrophy [151, 152]. This dynamic complexity poses a challenge to accurately recapitulating the full disease trajectory, spanning acute inflammation and chronic fibrosis, in in vitro models. This challenge is a key reason for the current lack of well-established organoid models specifically for chronic pancreatitis.

Existing organoid models of acute pancreatitis are relatively simplified and often lack key components of the immune microenvironment, limiting their ability to fully recapitulate complex pathophysiological interactions in vivo. Despite these limitations, simplified models have proven valuable as pathological tools, offering novel perspectives on the underlying disease mechanisms. For instance, Liu et al. [153] treated hESC-derived pancreatic organoids with streptozotocin, successfully inducing acute pancreatitis phenotypes, including reduced cell viability and reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation. This model was subsequently used to evaluate the anti-inflammatory and anti-pyroptotic effects of protective agents such as emodin and hUC-MSC-derived extracellular vesicles (hUC-MSC-EVs), demonstrating its utility in simulating parenchymal injury and facilitating drug screening. Consistent with the findings from pancreatic ductal organoids derived from patients with CF mentioned earlier, alcohol metabolites downregulate CFTR expression. This downregulation disrupts the functional coupling between CFTR and the plasma membrane calcium pump PMCA4, ultimately triggering the collapse of calcium homeostasis and mitochondrial damage. Consequently, ductal cells become exceptionally vulnerable to inflammatory attacks [148]. This discovery elucidates a pathogenic pathway shared across pancreatitis of different etiologies. Furthermore, organoid studies have delineated the adaptive responses of ductal cells to inflammatory conditions. In the early stages of inflammation, pro-inflammatory cytokines can transiently upregulate CFTR function to enhance secretion and clear irritants, likely representing a protective, compensatory mechanism. However, under sustained inflammatory attack, this compensatory response may eventually become exhausted, leading to functional decompensation and exacerbation of the injury [154].

Pancreatic organoids and organoid-on-a-chip platforms are increasingly becoming important bridges between basic research and therapeutic translation. This section discusses their treatment-related applications, including the use of islet organoids in regenerative medicine and cell replacement therapy, PDAC PDOs for drug screening and precision medicine, and organoid-on-a-chip platforms for high-throughput therapeutic screening, functional maturation, and scalable production. Islet organoids in regenerative medicine and personalized treatment strategies

Maturation optimization of stem cell-derived islet organoids

Stem cell-derived islet organoids require further optimization to achieve full functional maturation for transplantation. Recent studies have identified several strategies to promote the maturation of stem cell-derived β cells or islet organoids, including stage-specific modulation of TGF-β signaling to establish dynamic GSIS [37], activation of ERRγ- and WNT4-related metabolic maturation programs to enhance glucose responsiveness [155, 156], and synchronization of circadian rhythms to improve the function of hPSC-derived islets [157].

Besides intrinsic developmental programs and metabolic regulation, the islet microenvironment plays a critical role in β-cell maturation. Talavera-Adame et al. [158] used a 3D co-culture system of ECs and hPSC-derived embryoid bodies and found that ECs promoted the expression of mature β-cell markers and enhanced insulin secretion, possibly through the upregulation of BMP-2/4. Jun et al. [76] revealed that ECs form a vascular network and a basement membrane-like structure around β cells that contributes to the functional improvement of SC-β cells. Furthermore, they assembled SC-islet cells, human primary endothelial cells, and fibroblasts to generate vascularized SC-islet organoids. They optimized the co-culture conditions by adjusting the islet-to-vascular medium ratio and using a gradual medium-change strategy, thereby maintaining both vascular network formation and insulin content in SC-β cells. Vascularization enhances stimulus-dependent Ca²⁺ influx in SC-β cells, promotes the formation of an islet-like basement membrane, and accelerates diabetes reversal after transplantation in mice. Yoshihara et al. [156] generated human islet-like organoids from iPSC-derived endocrine progenitors in a 3D culture system. By incorporating hADSCs and HUVECs, the model introduced stromal- and vascular-like microenvironmental cues, whereas WNT4 treatment during the maturation stage activated an ERRγ-associated metabolic program, increased mitochondrial gene expression, and improved GSIS. Together, these studies indicate that vascular and stromal microenvironmental cues, particularly EC-related signals, are important for promoting the maturation of stem cell-derived organoids.

Immune-evasive engineering for islet replacement therapy

Current strategies to overcome immune rejection of pancreatic islet organoids focus on genetic engineering. Complete deletion of surface HLA-I in pancreatic organoids prevents immune recognition [159], whereas selective retention of HLA-A2 combined with non-classical HLA-E/F/G preserves immune surveillance while suppressing NK cell activity [160].

Immune protection can also be enhanced by overexpressing programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1), which suppresses T-cell activation through the PD-1/PD-L1 checkpoint pathway [161, 162]. However, porcine islet cells engineered to express human PD-L1 restored normoglycemia in only half of recipient mice, suggesting that PD-L1 alone provides incomplete protection [163]. IFN-γ can also induce endogenous PD-L1 expression in vitro [156, 159, 164, 165]. Additionally, engineering SC-β cells to secrete TGF-β, IL-10, and IL-2 promotes local immune tolerance by recruiting regulatory T cells [166]. Together, these strategies demonstrate that pancreatic organoids can be precisely engineered to counter multiple mechanisms of immune rejection while preserving endocrine function.

Biomaterial-based encapsulation is another key strategy for protecting islet grafts in replacement therapy. Common materials include alginate, chitosan, and polycaprolactone [167–169]. Although encapsulation can partially isolate grafts from host immune attacks, the performance of encapsulated organoids or islet-like cells is constrained by oxygen availability, nutrient diffusion, and metabolic waste exchange at the transplantation site. To address these mass-transport limitations, Ernst et al. [170] developed the SHARP computational framework, which rapidly predicts device-specific therapeutic efficacy and calculates islet-equivalence conversion coefficients based on device geometry, material properties, and cell-size distribution. Future optimization of encapsulation materials, pore architecture, and transplantation sites will be essential to enhance the translational potential of this approach.

Clinical translation of stem cell-derived islet therapies

Stem cell-derived islet therapy has successfully advanced into human clinical trials. In one example of personalized autologous transplantation, a patient with T1DM achieved insulin independence within three months after receiving islets derived from autologous chemically induced PSCs (ciPSCs). This was accompanied by a sustained time-in-range exceeding 98% and no transplant-related complications [171]. In parallel, allogeneic stem cell-derived islet cell therapy, VX-880 (zimislecel), represents a direction toward scalable therapeutic products. A recent phase 1/2 study in patients with T1DM showed that all 12 recipients who received a single full dose were free from severe hypoglycemia and maintained glycated hemoglobin levels below 7%. Among them, 83% (10/12) achieved insulin independence within one year after infusion. However, the main safety concerns include neutropenia and serious infections, which are associated with concomitant immunosuppressive therapy [10]. Although zimislecel is administered as a cell suspension, its clinical efficacy suggests that future transplantation of allogeneic stem cell-derived islet organoids may be feasible.

These two clinical translational routes have distinct advantages and limitations. Allogeneic cell products are more suitable for scalable manufacturing; however, their broad application remains limited by immune rejection and the need for systemic immunosuppression. In contrast, autologous ciPSC-derived islet therapy has the potential to reduce immune mismatch; however, its clinical feasibility still depends on shortening the manufacturing timeline, reducing production costs, and establishing reproducible individualized manufacturing and batch-release systems. Notably, for both autologous and allogeneic hPSC-derived products, batch-to-batch differences in differentiation efficiency, cellular composition, and maturation status, together with the tumorigenic potential of residual incompletely differentiated cells, remain key issues affecting product consistency and clinical controllability.

In addition, cell replacement therapy requires islet organoids to be amenable to scalable expansion, cryopreservation, recovery, and stable production. However, these processes may amplify the risk of phenotypic drift due to pancreatic cellular plasticity. Studies have shown that long-term cultured porcine islet-derived organoids can be passaged and cryopreserved, but gradually acquire pancreatic duct-like characteristics [172]. Therefore, standardized quality control systems should be established before therapeutic application, including lineage marker validation, functional assays such as GSIS, and single-cell or molecular analyses, to monitor cell identity, maturation status, and phenotypic stability.

PDAC PDOs in precision medicine

PDO-based drug-response prediction and clinical correlation

Current first-line treatment primarily relies on chemotherapy regimens, such as FOLFIRINOX and gemcitabine/nab-paclitaxel [173]. However, responses to these regimens vary considerably among patients, underscoring the need for models that can predict individual drug sensitivity and inform treatment selection. PDOs represent a patient-specific ex vivo platform for drug screening, as they are established directly from patient tumor tissue and can partially retain the molecular and phenotypic characteristics of the original tumor. One study showed that established PDOs retained typical PDAC mutational features and achieved 91% mutational concordance with the original tumors, with a predictive sensitivity of 83.3% and specificity of 92.9% in 34 patients with complete follow-up [174]. These findings support the use of PDOs as models of tumor drug response. However, conventional PDOs primarily enrich tumor epithelial cells during long-term passaging, whereas the TME and immune infiltrates are difficult to maintain, limiting the ability of these models to predict responses driven by stromal or immune microenvironmental components [72].

Clinically oriented PDO drug-response testing involves organoid establishment from biopsy or surgical specimens, brief in vitro expansion, exposure to concentration gradients of standard or targeted agents, and quantitative assessment of drug-induced changes in viability. The choice of drug-response metric directly influences interpretation. Although IC50 is suitable for evaluating in vitro drug potency, it is readily affected by organoid proliferation rate, drug concentration range, and nonspecific cytotoxicity. Accordingly, a lower IC50 does not necessarily translate to greater clinical benefits [175]. In contrast, AUC or normalized AUC reflects the overall inhibitory effect across the full dose range and better captures global response patterns. Wansch et al. [83] reported that AUC-based direct clustering of multi-drug testing achieved the highest predictive accuracy for first-line treatment response (85%), whereas log[IC50]-based multi-drug classification achieved 62% accuracy. Therefore, AUC-based metrics appear more suitable than IC50 for clinical correlation in PDO drug-sensitivity studies, although this conclusion requires prospective validation.

The clinical relevance of PDO drug screening is commonly assessed by comparing in vitro drug-sensitivity results with real-world patient outcomes, including radiographic response, pathological treatment response, CA19-9 decline, time to relapse, recurrence-free survival, and progression-free survival. Demyan et al. [176] reported good concordance between PDO-predicted chemotherapy sensitivity and post-NAT pathological response, as well as tumor cellularity. In a larger cohort, Shi et al. [58] stratified patients according to the AUC-based sensitivity of their paired organoids to the adjuvant chemotherapy agents they received. Patients treated with organoid-sensitive drugs had significantly longer recurrence-free survival, and ODX models further validated the concordance between in vitro organoid drug sensitivity and in vivo therapeutic response.

Collectively, these studies support an association between PDO pharmacotyping and clinical treatment response. However, heterogeneity in study design, sample size, drug-response metrics, and clinical endpoints continues to limit the use of PDO screening to guide clinical treatment decisions.

Technical and translational challenges of PDAC PDO platforms

Clinical translation of PDOs remains constrained by several technical limitations. Tumor tissues and their matched PDOs or PDO xenografts do not always maintain stable phenotypes [177]. Additionally, culture-medium composition can influence chemosensitivity; for example, PDOs cultured in WNT-based media often exhibit greater drug resistance than those cultured in PaTOM-based media, although this effect is not universal [7]. Establishment efficiency also varies widely across studies, with reported take rates of approximately 50–80% [72, 79, 83, 176]. Low-cellularity specimens, such as those obtained by EUS-FNA/FNB, further complicate organoid establishment and expansion due to the limited number of starting tumor cells. Even a successful initial establishment does not guarantee downstream drug testing. Seppälä et al. [72] reported that approximately one-third of established PDOs failed to progress from expansion to further characterization, primarily due to normal ductal epithelial overgrowth, microbial contamination, insufficient biomass, and growth arrest.

Turnaround time is another key barrier to clinical implementation. Advanced PDAC progresses rapidly, and treatment decisions often cannot be delayed for several weeks. In the ORGANOPREDICT study, the mean turnaround time for PDO chemograms was 6.8 weeks, which may be too long for patients with limited therapeutic windows [174]. The randomized phase II PASS-01 trial highlighted the same challenge: although pretreatment biopsy samples were collected for PDO development to inform later-line therapy, only 54% of patients ultimately received second-line therapy because some experienced rapid progression or were unable to proceed to subsequent treatments [178]. Accordingly, PDO drug-sensitivity data may have limited clinical utility if they are not available before treatment selection or early during therapy.

Clinical interpretation of PDO drug-sensitivity results also remains insufficiently standardized. Studies differ in drug-response metrics, concentration ranges, treatment duration, curve-fitting methods, and sensitivity or resistance threshold, making cross-study comparisons difficult even when PDO responses correlate with patient outcomes.

Workflow optimization and biomarker-guided PDO testing

PDO workflows are continuously refined to improve clinical applicability. One strategy is to optimize culture and assay procedures. To address the challenge of low-cellularity biopsy samples, Ekawa et al. [179] developed a low-input, non-additional invasive method for generating PDAC PDO from residual saline flush samples collected after routine EUS-FNA, enriching tumor cells under mutation-selective culture conditions. Shortening the drug-screening timeline is equally important. Demyan et al. [176] performed drug testing in low-passage, short-term expanded PDOs rather than waiting for long-term stable lines, reducing the average screening time to 15 days and generating drug-response data in as few as 7 days. Another strategy is to integrate molecular biomarkers with functional PDO testing. Molecular profiling can narrow the range of candidate drugs, whereas PDO assays can determine whether those agents are active in patient-derived tumor cells. Dantes et al. [177] analyzed cfDNA from PDO culture supernatants in parallel with organoid culture, providing a strategy for earlier genotype-directed drug testing and more rapid development of personalized treatment plans.

Collectively, PDOs continue to advance as “avatar” models for personalized PDAC therapy. The Phase III AVATAR trial, reported in 2025, integrated genomics with PDO-based drug screening to guide treatment selection, demonstrating the feasibility of precision oncology in advanced disease [180]. Expanded biobanks and high-resolution genomic profiling have improved the concordance between PDO responses and clinical outcomes, with reviews highlighting the potential of PDOs to predict therapy sensitivity and overcome resistance [181, 182]. Patient-specific avatars derived from EUS-guided biopsies enable rapid turnaround for timely clinical applications [25].

Organoid-on-a-Chip platforms for therapeutic screening and scalable production

Organoid-on-a-chip platforms for scalable islet organoid production

Organoid-on-a-chip technology addresses the limitations of islet organoid production, such as inconsistent size and uncontrolled maturity. Compared with static culture, microfluidic systems can provide a stable supply of oxygen and nutrients through continuous perfusion while removing metabolic waste, supporting the long-term culture and functional maintenance of islet organoids. Their miniaturized, array-based structures also help control organoid size and reduce culture heterogeneity. When combined with confocal imaging, ELISA, biosensors, and electrophysiological readouts, these platforms enable dynamic monitoring of morphological changes, insulin secretion, and functional maturation in islet organoids [78, 183–185]. Functionally, islet organoid-on-a-chip systems have been shown to enhance endocrine cell differentiation, as reflected by increased β-cell marker expression, improved GSIS, and elevated Ca²⁺ influx [68]. Additionally, one study developed a vascularized organoid-on-a-chip system based on HUVEC-lined serpentine microchannels. Organoids or spheroids were embedded in a fibrin hydrogel and precisely positioned within the chip, whereas HUVECs and fibroblasts formed a vascular/stromal niche under continuous perfusion. This platform promotes improved GSIS in pancreatic islet spheroids [82]. Collectively, organoid-on-a-chip platforms can facilitate the scalable production of mature and functionally improved islet organoids.

Organoid-on-a-chip platforms for PDAC therapeutic screening

PDAC is typically diagnosed at an advanced stage, and numerous patients can only provide limited tumor tissue through fine-needle aspiration or core needle biopsy, which restricts the establishment and expansion of PDOs. In addition, conventional PDO cultures often show marked size heterogeneity, affecting the stability and reproducibility of drug screening results. Therefore, generating sufficient, relatively uniform PDOs suitable for drug-response assessment from limited clinical samples remains a major challenge for personalized drug screening in PDAC. Compared with conventional static PDO culture systems, organoid-on-a-chip platforms use microfluidic, miniaturized, and array-based designs to reduce cell and reagent consumption and enable the parallel testing of multiple drug concentrations, drug combinations, or patient samples. More importantly, microfluidic systems can provide dynamic perfusion and generate stable drug concentration gradients, thereby more closely mimicking in vivo nutrient supply and drug delivery than traditional static immersion-based assays [24].

To improve the uniformity and controllability of patient-derived 3D tumor models, one study encapsulated primary pancreatic cancer cells in CMC/ALG core–shell hydrogel microcapsules, allowing them to form uniformly sized 3D tumor spheroids within a confined space. After integration with a concentration-gradient chip, this platform enabled dynamic drug exposure and dose–response analysis, supporting comparison of chemotherapy sensitivity across patient-derived models [74]. To achieve a dynamic drug evaluation that more closely reflects clinical treatment, an automated organoid-on-a-chip system combining a multiplexer fluid-control device with 3D culture chambers was developed. Unlike conventional static plate-based assays, this system can be programmed to simulate the sequential administration of drugs. Together with real-time imaging, this enables combinatorial drug screening across hundreds of pancreatic organoid culture units [75].

To shorten the drug screening timeline, future organoid-on-a-chip platforms should focus on automation, high-throughput capacity, and standardized operations. In addition, prospective validation in larger patient cohorts is required to determine the correlation between organoid-on-a-chip drug responses and actual clinical outcomes.

Barriers to the clinical translation of organoid-on-a-chip platforms

However, the clinical translation of organoid-on-a-chip platforms remains limited. Numerous platforms still lack standardized construction protocols, and substantial differences exist across studies in chip materials, channel geometry, extracellular matrices, and fluidic control, making reproducibility difficult to achieve [70]. In addition, organoid-on-a-chip fabrication often relies on specialized microfabrication techniques such as PDMS-based soft lithography. The construction of fluidic control systems, including pumps, valves, and tubing, also requires high precision and is often costly, which limits scalable manufacturing and broader use in routine laboratory settings [71]. Furthermore, dynamic perfusion may introduce non-physiological shear stress, which can affect organoid architecture, cell viability, and differentiation status, reducing the accuracy of chip-based models in predicting in vivo physiological states and therapeutic responses [55]. Although PDMS remains the dominant material for organoid-on-a-chip devices, its limited elasticity and spatial adaptability are not ideal for the long-term culture of large 3D organoids. PDMS can also absorb hydrophobic small molecules, altering their effective solution concentrations, thereby reducing the accuracy and reliability of drug-response assessments [186, 187]. Moreover, large-scale prospective clinical studies are needed to determine whether chip-derived readouts can accurately predict patient outcomes. Therefore, before organoid-on-a-chip platforms can be used as clinical decision-making tools, further efforts are required to improve platform standardization, quality control, scalable manufacturing, and systematic validation of their clinical relevance.

Applications of artificial intelligence in pancreatic organoids

In patients with PDAC, undergoing PDO-based drug-sensitivity testing is part of precision medicine; rapid disease progression often imposes a narrow window between biopsy collection and the generation of actionable pharmacological data. Moreover, high-throughput culture platforms and high-content imaging generate large, complex datasets that are labor-intensive and potentially subjective to analyze manually. AI, particularly computer vision and machine learning (ML), may help address these limitations by (i) automating image-based quantification of organoid morphology and drug response, (ii) enabling longitudinal tracking of individual organoids to capture phenotypic evolution and heterogeneity, and (iii) constructing digital organoid models to predict drug-sensitivity and prioritize candidate therapies, thereby supporting faster and more robust interpretation of screening results.

High-throughput organoid imaging and AI-based image analysis

The manual delineation of organoid boundaries is inefficient and poorly reproducible. Live-cell imaging enables the noninvasive monitoring of organoid dynamics, and when combined with AI-based computer vision methods, it provides a promising approach for high-throughput and automated image analysis. Kulkarni et al. [188] trained the StarDist and Cellpose models on large datasets of pancreatic cancer organoid images, enabling automated organoid detection and segmentation. These models achieved false-positive and false-negative rates within 10–20% and showed greater consistency than manual annotation. However, such approaches primarily provide snapshot-level detection of organoid populations and are limited in their ability to track individual organoids.

To address this limitation, Matthews et al. [189] developed the OrganoID platform, which performs pixel-level segmentation and longitudinal tracking of individual organoids using bright-field and phase-contrast microscopy. The platform achieved more than 95% agreement with manual annotation, and the single-organoid tracking accuracy remained above 89% over 4 days. Continuous monitoring of individual organoids enables quantitative assessment of dynamic changes in size and morphology during culture and captures inter-organoid variability in drug responses under treatment conditions.

Nevertheless, current AI models are often trained on datasets with limited diversity and relatively simple cellular compositions, restricting their generalizability. In more complex experimental settings, such as co-culture systems or matrix-remodeling models, the segmentation and tracking accuracy may decline substantially. Moreover, imaging conditions, culture protocols, and annotation strategies vary between studies. Therefore, the performance of existing models remains largely constrained by dataset diversity and the degree of methodological standardization. Collectively, these AI-driven tools may shorten analytical turnaround time and account for dynamic changes in PDOs during culture, thereby improving the feasibility of integrating PDO pharmacotyping into time-sensitive clinical decision-making for appropriately selected patients.

Machine learning for drug-response prediction

The establishment of organoid models is time-consuming and labor-intensive, limiting their application in high-throughput drug screening assays. The development of multi-omics databases based on PDOs provides important resources for data-driven drug-response prediction. Therefore, integrating genomic, transcriptomic, and pharmacological response data with ML models has become an important research direction.

Ovchinnikova et al. trained multiple ML models using genomic, transcriptomic, and drug-response data from pancreatic cancer PDOs. These models use the molecular features of organoids and the chemical structures of drugs as inputs to predict treatment responses [175]. Recently, the concept of Artificial Intelligence Virtual Organoids (AIVOs) has been proposed [190]. This approach aims to integrate multi-omics data to construct digital representations of organoids, enabling in silico simulation of patient-specific drug responses.

This field remains in its early stages of development. AI-based drug prediction platforms should be regarded as supportive tools rather than replacements for experimental systems. In particular, the “black-box” nature of many ML models limits their biological interpretability and applicability. Currently, these models are better suited for the preliminary screening of candidate drugs, followed by functional validation using organoid-based experiments. This integrated strategy may streamline the screening workflow while maintaining experimental reliability.

Challenges and future perspectives

Challenges

Although pancreatic organoid systems have revolutionized ex vivo modeling of pancreatic pathophysiology, several fundamental limitations remain.

Technical bottlenecks

Three primary barriers impede the clinical adoption of pancreatic organoids: the high cost of specialized cytokines and equipment, inconsistent reproducibility caused by protocol variability, and limited scalability compounded by phenotypic instability during expansion and cryopreservation, as evidenced by the emergence of ductal characteristics in porcine islet organoids during prolonged culture [172]. Although Good Manufacturing Practice (GMP)-compliant organoid derivation from cadaveric pancreata represents an important step toward clinical translation [191], high operational costs and batch-to-batch variability continue to hinder broader implementation.

Physiological oversimplification

Current organoid models exhibit simplifications in their physiological architecture, severely constraining their translational value. These limitations are primarily manifested in the following ways:

Lack of functional vascular and neural networks

Vascularization strategies have rapidly advanced. Simple HUVEC co-cultures are being superseded by more organized vascularized islet models that incorporate endothelial networks, perfusion, and engineered vascular niches. Quintard et al. [82] developed a serpentine microfluidic platform in which pre-vascularized insulin-secreting pancreatic islet spheroids were cultured under flow conditions within a neighbouring HUVEC vascular bed, leading to enhanced GSIS. Jun et al. [76] vascularized SC-islet organoids by loading SC-islets together with human primary endothelial cells and fibroblasts into a microfluidic perfusion device, in which interstitial flow induced the formation of a perfusable vascular network around SC-islets and prolonged stimulus-dependent Ca²⁺ influx in SC-β cells. These advances may inform the development of more physiologically relevant and functionally mature islet organoid models.

Innervation, which has long been neglected, is emerging as the next frontier. Pancreatic islets and ducts are densely innervated by sympathetic, parasympathetic, and sensory fibers that modulate insulin/glucagon release and bicarbonate secretion. Recent in vivo studies have demonstrated that pancreatic islets actively shape their sympathetic innervation through specialized integration and an endocrine-driven mechanism involving Reg-family proteins for neural regulation and regeneration [192]. While direct neural co-culture in human islet organoids remains underexplored, proof-of-concept approaches suggest the potential to incorporate iPSC-derived neural progenitors or ganglion-like structures to restore catecholaminergic and cholinergic innervation, enhance first-phase insulin secretion, and re-establish vagal-mediated cephalic phase responses. In parallel, optogenetic activation of channelrhodopsin-expressing vagal (parasympathetic) neurons has enabled real-time, light-controlled modulation of GSIS, as shown in models that stimulate autonomic input to islets [193]. These advances open new avenues for adapting optogenetics to islet-on-a-chip devices, facilitating the modeling of diabetic neuropathy and autonomic dysregulation.

Limitations in multi-organ interaction modeling

Current organ-on-a-chip platforms only partially simulate blood glucose regulation through basic liver-islet interactions [80], while failing to adequately account for critical inter-organ signaling crosstalk, such as enteroendocrine signaling, neural regulation, and adipose tissue interactions.

Ethical considerations

Commercialization raises unresolved questions regarding fair donor compensation [194]. Murine tumor-derived Matrigel introduces batch variability and a risk of xenograft contamination. Alternative matrices, such as decellularized ECM or synthetic hydrogels, can be considered [195, 196]. Furthermore, CRISPR-based genome editing in pancreatic organoids exhibits concerning off-target rates, which pose clinical safety concerns.

Outlook

The convergence of stem cell biology, microengineering, gene editing, and AI is rapidly transforming pancreatic organoids from research tools into clinically actionable platforms. We propose the following five concrete milestones that are technically feasible and likely to be achieved within the next decade.

GMP-compliant, fully vascularized stem cell-derived islet organoids in Phase 2/3 allogeneic trials

Building on the success of Zimislecel programs, next-generation islets incorporating prevascularized endothelial networks and inducible immune-evasive transgenes (PD-L1/CTLA-4-Ig, HLA-E/G) will be introduced into clinical practice. By integrating 3D bioprinting and organ-on-a-chip technologies, functional artificial islets can be constructed, advancing the feasibility of subcutaneous implantation. These products are expected to achieve sustained insulin independence in recipients with T1DM without systemic immunosuppression.

Regulatory approval of the first organoid-based companion diagnostic for PDAC

PDO drug-sensitivity testing, combined with matched genomic/cfDNA profiling, will be prospectively validated in randomized trials as a predictive biomarker panel that outperforms current guideline-based chemotherapy selection.

Routine deployment of AI-integrated, high-throughput organoid-on-a-chip platforms for precision oncology and toxicology

Fully automated systems (e.g., successors of HCS-3DX and Schuster et al. multiplexer chips [75, 197]) will screen > 500 PDOs in parallel within 10–14 days, using deep-learning phenotyping of morphology, metabolism, and secretome to guide first-line and adaptive therapies for PDAC [198, 199].

Multi-organ microfluidic devices recapitulating the entero-insular and gut–liver–pancreas axes

Standardized four- to six-organ chips linking intestinal, hepatic, adipose, and pancreatic islet organoids under physiological nutrient/hormonal gradients will become the gold standard for modeling T2DM, metabolic-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), and post-bariatric hypoglycemia. In addition, these systems will enable the direct evaluation of drug efficacy and hepatotoxicity, reducing the risk of late-stage clinical failure in therapeutic development.

Large-scale, ethically governed pancreatic organoid biobanks with > 10,000 cryopreserved, deeply annotated lines

International consortia will provide on-demand, genetically stratified organoids linked to longitudinal clinical data, enabling the retrospective validation of therapeutic hypotheses and accelerating basket trials for rare CFTR, HNF1A/B, and KRAS-negative pancreatic neoplasms.

Realizing this roadmap will require coordinated investments in GMP infrastructure, harmonized regulatory frameworks for living cellular diagnostics, and open-source AI tools for organoid image analysis. If achieved, these advances will make the treatment paradigm for pancreatic diseases truly predictive and mechanism-based, with the long-term goal of achieving a cure.

Conclusions

This review summarizes pancreatic organoid and organoid-on-chip technologies, comparing cell sources and construction methods to optimize their development. We have also discussed their applications in modeling endocrine/exocrine diseases and precision medicine. Future advancements will enhance physiological accuracy and multi-organ modeling, ultimately aiding the development of early diagnostic markers and effective treatment options for this disease. The production of GMP-compliant organoids will further enable their clinical translation into regenerative medicine.
