Saliency-R1 : Enforcing Interpretable and Faithful Vision-language Reasoning via Saliency-map Alignment Reward
Shizhan Gong, Minda Hu, Qiyuan Zhang, Chen Ma, Qi Dou

Abstract
Vision-language models (VLMs) have achieved remarkable success across diverse tasks. However, concerns about their trustworthiness persist, particularly regarding tendencies to lean more on textual cues than visual evidence and the risk of producing ungrounded or fabricated responses. To address these issues, we propose Saliency-R1 , a framework for improving the interpretability and faithfulness of VLMs reasoning. Specifically, we introduce a novel saliency map technique that efficiently highlights critical image regions contributing to generated tokens without additional computational overhead. This can further be extended to trace how visual information flows through the reasoning process to the final answers, revealing the alignment between the thinking process and the visual context. We use the overlap between the saliency maps and human-annotated bounding boxes as the reward function, and apply Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) to align the salient parts and critical regions, encouraging models to focus on relevant areas when conduct reasoning. Experiments show Saliency-R1 improves reasoning faithfulness, interpretability, and overall task performance. The code is available at https://github.com/peterant330/Saliency_R1 .

Introduction

Vision-language models (VLMs) have made impressive strides, demonstrating strong performance on tasks that require joint reasoning over images and text. As a result, VLMs have been widely adopted in various domains, including high-stakes areas such as autonomous driving
(
15
)
, finance
(
36
)
, and medical diagnosis
(
26
)
. Despite their superior performance, the trustworthiness of VLMs has raised concerns. For example, prior work show that VLMs often over-rely on textual cues, with visual signals playing a comparatively smaller role
(
53
;
29
)
. Another concern is hallucination
(
23
)
, where models produce ungrounded or fabricated content not supported by the image. These issues limit VLMs’ broader deployment in safety-critical settings.

To build trust, VLMs are frequently prompted to generate natural language explanations (NLEs) that describe their reasoning. For instance, Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompts
(
74
)
can be used to elicit step-by-step reasoning before producing a final answer. Recent reasoning-based language models, such as DeepSeek-R1
(
28
)
and Claude-Sonnet
(
5
)
, are explicitly trained to provide detailed reasoning traces. However, these NLEs still have limitations. Multiple studies report inconsistencies between the articulated reasoning process and the final answer
(
52
;
3
;
7
;
84
)
, indicating that the provided “thinking” may not faithfully reflect the process that led to the solution. Moreover, the explanations themselves can exhibit problems including misusing visual cues or hallucinating unsupported details.

Figure 1
:
Main motivation of this work.
Different thinking processes might focus on distinct regions of an image, even if they arrive at the correct answer. Unfaithful thinking processes either focus on irrelevant parts of the image or fail to consider the image.

In this work, we aim to enhance the interpretability and faithfulness of VLM reasoning. We introduce a saliency-map method that visualizes how visual information influences generated tokens. Specifically, we decompose token logits into first-order contributions
(
21
)
from all context tokens and render the contributions from visual tokens as saliency maps. These maps highlight image regions most relevant to each generated token and require no extra forward or backward passes, making the approach computationally efficient. We further extend the framework to trace how visual tokens propagate to answer tokens through the thinking tokens as a bottleneck, revealing image regions that drive both the reasoning process and the final answer. As illustrated in Fig.
1
, different reasoning traces pull from different parts of the image: some emphasize regions truly pertinent to the question, while others attend to irrelevant objects or background. We regard the latter as a potential misuse of visual information or a form of hallucination.

Building on these insights, we propose
Saliency-R1
, a novel framework that aligns VLM saliency maps with human annotations. We curate a VQA dataset with bounding boxes marking image regions essential for answering each question. For each generated response, we compute the ratio of saliency mass inside the annotated boxes to the saliency mass over the whole image and use this ratio as a reward signal in Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO)
(
60
)
. This training process encourages the model to concentrate on task-relevant regions. Extensive experiments across diverse benchmarks show that our approach not only steers reasoning towards critical visual areas but also enhances perceptual grounding and overall performance. Our main contribution can be summarized as:

•

We build a novel attention-based saliency-map technique, which highlights the token-wise important regions efficiently without additional forward or backward passes.

•

We propose an attention rollout mechanism that traces how visual tokens contribute to answer tokens, using the thinking tokens as a bottleneck for visualization.

•

We use the saliency–annotation alignment score as a reward and apply GRPO post-training to enhance the faithfulness and interpretability of VLM reasoning.

In contrast, our approach eliminates the need for additional forward or backward passes, enabling real-time computation and seamless integration into the training pipeline.

In parallel with advances in LLM reasoning capabilities

Attention Manipulation in Vision-language Models.

Our motivation for introducing saliency-map alignment rewards stems from prior findings that steering attention can enhance LLM and VLM performance. LLMs often display biased attention over long contexts, for example, underutilizing middle segments

The Saliency-R1 framework is designed to encourage the model to produce a more faithful vision-language reasoning. 3.1
Token-level Saliency-map Generation via Logits Decomposition

As most existing VLMs are based on the transformer architecture
(
70
)
, we first provide a brief review of the transformer structure. Transformer is a residual network composed of
L
layers, where each layer consists of a Multi-Head Self-Attention (MSA) mechanism followed by an FFN block. The input texts are first tokenized and then embedded into token embeddings. The images are also encoded into visual tokens with a visual encoder, usually a ViT
(
19
)
. Typical VLMs adopt a decoder-only paradigm, using input tokens along with previously generated tokens to predict the next tokens. At timestep
i
, we denote all past tokens as
\textbf{X}_{i}=[t_{1},t_{2},\cdots,t_{i}]
. With the residual connections, the output of layer
l
can be expressed as:

\textbf{h}_{i}^{l}=\textbf{h}_{i}^{l-1}+\textbf{A}_{i}^{l}+\textbf{F}_{i}^{l},

where
\textbf{A}_{i}^{l}=\text{ATTN}^{l}(\textbf{h}_{1}^{l-1},\cdots,\textbf{h}_{i}^{l-1})
represents the output of the MSA block, and
\textbf{F}_{i}^{l}=\text{FFN}^{l}(\textbf{A}_{i}^{l}+\textbf{h}_{i}^{l-1})
represents the output of the FFN block
1
1

1

For simplicity, we ignore the layer normalization layer in the derivation. We will explain it in the Appendix.
. The
L
-th layer output is used to compute the final probability distribution
y
by multiplying the unembedded matrix
\textbf{E}u\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N}
. Moreover, the output of the attention layer
\textbf{A}_{i}^{l}
can be represented as a sum of head outputs, each being a weighted sum of value-output vectors on all positions:

\begin{split}\textbf{A}_{i}^{l}&=\sum_{j=1}^{H}\sum_{p=1}^{i}\alpha_{i,j,p}^{l}{\mathbf{W}^{l}_{o{\color[rgb]{0,0,0},j}}}{{\mathbf{W}^{l}_{v{\color[rgb]{0,0,0},j}}}}\textbf{h}_{p}^{l-1},\\
\alpha^{l}_{i,j,p}&=\text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}^{l}_{q,j}\textbf{h}_{i}^{l-1}\cdot\mathbf{W}^{l}_{k,j}\textbf{h}_{p}^{l-1}),\end{split}

where
H
is the number of attention heads,
{\mathbf{W}_{o{\color[rgb]{0,0,0},j}}^{l}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d/H}
,
\mathbf{W}_{k,j}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d/H\times d}
,
\mathbf{W}_{q,j}^{l}\in\mathbb{R}^{d/H\times d}
,
{\mathbf{W}_{v{\color[rgb]{0,0,0},j}}^{l}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d/H\times d}
are the output, key, query, and value matrices. As a result, the final output can be decomposed as:

\textbf{h}_{i}^{L}=\textbf{h}_{i}^{0}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\textbf{F}_{i}^{l}+\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{H}\sum_{p=1}^{i}\alpha_{i,j,p}^{l}{\mathbf{W}^{l}_{o,j}}{{\mathbf{W}^{l}_{v,j}}}\textbf{h}_{p}^{l-1}.

While it is difficult to attribute the first two terms to specific context tokens, the third term consists of the direct contributions of individual context tokens. As a result, we can explicitly trace each token’s direct impact on the final logits (Fig.
2
(a)). Specifically, the total logits for predicting
t_{i+1}
are
\textbf{h}_{i}^{L}\textbf{E}_{u}
, where the direct contribution of the token
t_{p}
is

c_{p}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{H}\alpha_{i,j,p}^{l}{\mathbf{W}^{l}_{o{\color[rgb]{0,0,0},j}}}{{\mathbf{W}^{l}_{v{\color[rgb]{0,0,0},j}}}}\textbf{h}_{p}^{l-1}\textbf{E}_{u}.

(1)

We can select the logits contribution that corresponds to the visual tokens, and rearrange according to the orders of the visual patches. We also apply a
ReLU
function to ignore all the negative contributions. This gives saliency maps highlighting the important regions for the token prediction.

The way we build the saliency maps simplifies the problem by only considering the direct contribution of tokens. The generated saliency maps may not be the most faithful ones, compared with gradient-based methods or perturbation-based methods. However, as our ultimate goal is to use the saliency maps to guide the training, the efficiency is also very important. Our proposal does not requires additional backward process or multiple forward process. Many attention implementations naturally support returning attention weights, and the term
{{\mathbf{W}^{l}_{v,j}}}\textbf{h}_{p}^{l-1}
is also available within KV cache
(
62
)
. Therefore, the saliency maps do not incur extra computation. In addition, previous literature pointed out that the indirect contribution is minor compared to the direct contribution
(
21
;
86
;
24
)
. Therefore, aligning the dominant contribution is sufficient to steer the model’s attention to the correct regions.

3.2
Holistic Saliency-map Aggregation with Thinking Tokens as Bottleneck

A typical reasoning model first produces a chain of thought and then generates the final answers based on this reasoning context. We assume that the thinking process begins by extracting information from the input images and prompts, followed by summarization and reasoning. The final answers then structure the conclusions drawn from this process into the required output format. To ensure faithful reasoning, we aim for the answer generation to depend on the thinking process, while the thinking process itself should effectively focus on the relevant regions of the input images. To use saliency maps to visualize the process, we hope they could reflect how visual information flows to the answer tokens, with the thinking tokens serving as a bottleneck.

We utilize heuristic attention rollout to quantify how the visual information is transferred to answer tokens through thinking tokens (see Fig.
2
(b)). At layer
l
and head
h
, we use
\mathcal{A}^{l,h}_{vt}
to denote the attention weight matrix between visual tokens and thinking tokens, where the entry
(i,j)
denotes the attentions weights from the
i
-th visual token to the
j
-th thinking token. Similarly,
\mathcal{A}^{l,h}_{ta}
denotes the weight matrix between thinking tokens and answer tokens. Therefore,
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{l,h}_{va}=\mathcal{A}^{l,h}_{vt}\mathcal{A}^{l,h}_{ta}
measures the attention weight from visual tokens to the answer tokens, with thinking tokens as the bottleneck. For a specific answer token, we can utilize Eq.
1
to visualize the transitional contribution of visual tokens, by replacing the attention weights with the elements in the corresponding column of
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{l,h}_{va}
.

Finally, we form the holistic saliency map by directly summing the per-token maps. We do not normalize the columns of
\mathcal{A}^{l,h}_{vt}
,
\mathcal{A}^{l,h}_{ta}
or
\tilde{\mathcal{A}}^{l,h}_{va}
because some answer tokens (e.g., prepositions) receive little contribution from thinking tokens, and similarly some thinking tokens receive little contribution from visual tokens. Such tokens should naturally have a smaller influence on the holistic map. In addition, since the saliency maps reflect logit contributions, low-confidence answer tokens with small logits also contribute less to the holistic saliency map.

3.3
Boosting Saliency-map Alignment via GRPO

Once we obtain the holistic saliency map, we compare it with human annotation to measure the alignment. Specifically, we draw bounding box on the input image, which highlights the part of the figures important for answering the question. We resize the saliency map to be the same size as the input image, and calculate the recall to represent the alignment score, i.e.,

\text{Alignment\_Score}=\frac{\sum_{i\in\text{ Bounding Box}}\text{Saliency\_Score}(i)}{\sum_{i\in\text{Image}}\text{Saliency\_Score}(i)}.

A faithful vision-language reasoning process should achieve a high alignment score, as it actually look at the critical regions of the image relevant to the task. In contrast, hallucinated responses often disregard the image entirely or fixate on unrelated background elements. Likewise, unfaithful chain-of-thoughts rely on shortcuts from visual tokens directly to answer tokens, with minimal information flowing through the thinking bottleneck, leading to low alignment scores of the transitional saliency map. To enforce higher alignment score, we use GRPO
(
60
)
as the optimization algorithm (see Fig.
2
(c)). During the training process, GRPO samples a group of outputs based on the given question from the old policy
\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}
and then optimizes the policy model
\pi_{\theta}
by maximizing the following objective:

{\small\begin{split}&\mathcal{J}_{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}_{[q\sim\mathcal{D},\{o_{i}\}_{i=1}^{G}\sim\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(\cdot|q)]}\frac{1}{G}\sum_{i=1}^{G}\left(M_{i}-\beta\mathbb{D}_{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}})\right),\\
&M_{i}=\min\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}|q)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i}|q)}A_{i},\operatorname{clip}\left(\frac{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}|q)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(o_{i}|q)},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon\right)A_{i}\right),\\
&\mathbb{D}_{KL}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}})=\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{i}|q)}{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}|q)}-\log\frac{\pi_{\text{ref}}(o_{i}|q)}{\pi_{\theta}(o_{i}|q)}-1,\end{split}}

where
q
represents the input question drawn from the dataset
\mathcal{D}
, and
\{o_{1},o_{2},\cdots,o_{G}\}
denotes the generated text response.
\mathbb{D}_{KL}
is the KL-divergence measure, while
\epsilon
and
\beta
are hyper-parameters.
A_{i}
indicates the advantage, computed using a group of rewards
\{r_{1},r_{2},\cdots,r_{G}\}
corresponding to the outputs within each group. The standardized rewards are used as the advantage.

Different reward functions can lead the model toward different optimization directions and final performance outputs. The reward function in our Saliency-R1 framework consists of three components:

\mathcal{R}_{\text{overall}}=\mathcal{R}_{\text{accuracy}}+\mathcal{R}_{\text{format}}+\mathcal{R}_{\text{saliency}}.

\mathcal{R}_{\text{format}}
enforces the model to follow the instruction. The model receives a reward of 1 if the reasoning process is enclosed in “<think></think>” tags and the final answer is followed afterwards. Otherwise, the reward score is 0. The accuracy reward
\mathcal{R}_{\text{accuracy}}
is calculated using LLM-as-judge
(
80
)
, where 0 indicates an incorrect answer and 1 indicates a correct answer. We further use the alignment score as the saliency reward
\mathcal{R}_{\text{saliency}}
. To this end, responses with higher saliency alignment are treated as exhibiting better reasoning. The training process guides the model towards the direction of generating more responses with high alignment scores, thereby being more interpretable.

4.2
Evaluation of Saliency Maps Faithfulness

We begin by evaluating the faithfulness of our saliency methods using deletion and insertion tests. In deletion, we progressively remove the most salient image pixels in descending order; in insertion, we add those pixels back onto a blank canvas. To enable fair comparison, the model’s mean self-log-likelihood is linearly normalized using the blank image and the original, unperturbed output as references. We report normalized log-likelihood at 5%, 15%, and 30% pixel perturbation. Lower deletion scores and higher insertion scores indicate that the highlighted regions have a stronger causal effect on the model’s responses.

We conduct experiments using Qwen2.5-VL-3B as the VLM, and compare our method with several baselines, including CAM
(
97
)
, Grad-CAM
(
57
)
, raw attention, attention rollout
(
1
)
, ATTN-LRP
(
2
)
, and TAM
(
39
)
. The evaluation is performed on three datasets, namely the COCO Caption dataset
(
13
)
, GranDf
(
54
)
, and OpenPSG
(
98
)
. For each dataset, we sample 1k images for evaluation. The results are shown in Table
1
. Our method attains faithfulness on par with, or better than, the baselines. For example, on COCO Captions, the deletion score is lower than the second-best approach by 5.46%, 4.77%, and 2.57% at 5%, 15%, and 30% pixel perturbation, respectively. This indicates that, even though we focus solely on direct contributions, the method still faithfully captures the relative importance of visual patches. In addition, our method is very efficient. Unlike baselines such as ATTN-LRP (which requires backpropagation) and TAM (which solves an optimization problem), our approach relies only on simple matrix operations, making it especially suitable for integration into post-training pipelines.

Effects of SFT Cold Start.
We evaluate the performance of Saliency-R1 against Saliency-R1-zero, which leverages GRPO to train the model directly without an SFT cold start. The results show that SFT plays a crucial role in significantly enhancing model performance. Given the relatively small size of the base model, its initial reasoning capabilities are limited. Effects of Individual Reward Term.
We then investigate the impact of each reward term. We begin with standard GRPO training without incorporating the saliency alignment reward. This gives the same training protocol as Vision-R1
31
, but using different training data. The results reveal that Saliency-R1 outperforms Vision-R1 by 1.5% across 9 VQA benchmarks, highlighting the importance of the saliency alignment reward. By aligning saliency maps, the model concentrates on the most critical regions of the image, showing significantly better visual reasoning ability.

We also observed a general positive correlation between saliency alignment and accuracy: when the model attends to the correct region, it tends to produce the correct answer. Moreover, since computing the saliency reward depends on properly formatted outputs, its value already captures formatting correctness. This suggests the saliency reward could be used on its own. To test this hypothesis, we train Saliency-R1-pure using only the saliency alignment reward. The model performs well, with the average accuracy across nine benchmarks just 0.6% lower than the full Saliency-R1. It even surpasses Saliency-R1 on the MME benchmark. Training with only the saliency reward can lower costs by eliminating the need to evaluate generated answers with another LLM.

Finally, we evaluate the impact of our proposed saliency map technique. We replace it with a vanilla attention aggregation and retrain the model, yielding the variant Saliency-R1-attn. Still, the results are sub-optimal. Our saliency maps more faithfully capture the reasoning process, aligning them has a more direct and stronger effect on improving the visual ability of the model.

5
Limitation and Conclusion

In this work, we propose Saliency-R1, a novel protocol that improves the visual reasoning ability of VLMs via saliency map alignment. Specifically, we propose a real-time saliency-map generation algorithm via logits decomposition, and propose to align the saliency maps with human annotations through GRPO. Evaluation of Saliency-R1 on 10 VQA benchmarks shows its superior performance.

Our work has certain limitations. Due to the limited computational resources, the training, especially SFT, is conducted on relatively small datasets. As shown in Table 2, some metrics become lower after SFT. This is not desired and can be overcome by collecting more diverse cold-start data. Moreover, we use bounding boxes as the coarse labels to align saliency maps. Finer segmentation labels (e.g., from SAM 3
9
) may further improve performance. Finally, our evaluation is based on relatively small base models. The extension to larger models is left as future work.

Appendix A
Normalization Layers

In this section, we outline the modifications required to incorporate normalizations layer (usually RMSNorm) into our method. Normalizations are applied in two key locations: before the projection layer (on the output of the transformer) and before each layer in the transformer block (on the layer input). We detail how the attribution techniques should be adjusted accordingly.

A.1
Pre-unembedding Normalization

In typical language models, a normalization layer, usually RMSNorm, is applied to the transformer’s output before the unembedding layers. Formally, the final logits is given by
\text{RMSNorm}(\textbf{h}_{i}^{L})\textbf{E}_{u}
, and the normalization layer can be written as:

\text{RMSNorm}(x)=\gamma\times\frac{x}{\sigma_{l}^{2}+\epsilon},

(2)

where
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the input token,
\sigma_{l}\in\mathbb{R}
is the standard deviation, and
\gamma\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is learned vectors. As the normalization if a linear operation, when calculating the contribution of each visual tokens, we can directly apply the same operation to each token, i.e.,:

c_{p}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{H}\text{RMSNorm}(\alpha_{i,j,p}^{l}{\mathbf{W}^{l}_{o{\color[rgb]{0,0,0},j}}}{{\mathbf{W}^{l}_{v{\color[rgb]{0,0,0},j}}}}\textbf{h}_{p}^{l-1})\textbf{E}_{u}.

(3)

One major difference is that in this case, the standard deviation
\hat{\sigma}
should be calculated from the final representation
\mathbf{h}_{p}^{L}
, i.e.,
\hat{\sigma}=\text{s.d.}(\mathbf{h}_{p}^{L})
.

A.2
MSAs Input Layer Normalizations

In the main paper, we do not describe the normalization layers that are applied to each input of the MSA block in the model. More accurately, the complete representation of the MSA block are:

\begin{split}\tilde{\textbf{h}}_{p}^{l-1}&=\text{RMSNorm}(\textbf{h}_{p}^{l-1}),\\
\textbf{A}_{i}^{l}&=\sum_{j=1}^{H}\sum_{p=1}^{i}\alpha_{i,j,p}^{l}{\mathbf{W}^{l}_{o{\color[rgb]{0,0,0},j}}}{{\mathbf{W}^{l}_{v{\color[rgb]{0,0,0},j}}}}\tilde{\textbf{h}}_{p}^{l-1},\\
\alpha^{l}_{i,j,p}&=\text{softmax}(\mathbf{W}^{l}_{q,j}\tilde{\textbf{h}}_{i}^{l-1}\cdot\mathbf{W}^{l}_{k,j}\tilde{\textbf{h}}_{p}^{l-1}).\end{split}

(4)

Therefore, by the linearity of the normalization layer, we can rewrite the token-wise contribution to incorporate the normalization:

c_{p}=\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{j=1}^{H}\text{RMSNorm}(\alpha_{i,j,p}^{l}{\mathbf{W}^{l}_{o{\color[rgb]{0,0,0},j}}}{{\mathbf{W}^{l}_{v{\color[rgb]{0,0,0},j}}}}\tilde{\textbf{h}}_{p}^{l-1})\textbf{E}_{u}.

(5)

Appendix C
Implementation Details

In this section, we provide more details of the implementation for bot cold start and the saliency-map alignment.

C.1
SFT

We use llama-factory
96
as the framework for conducting SFT. We conduct SFT on Saliency-R1-cold for 2 epochs. The maximum learning rate is
5\times 10^{-5}
and then it is adjusted according to cosine annealing scheduler with warm-up ratio of 0.05. The batch size is 64. Lora with LoRA with rank 128 is used for optimization. Other settings are set to be the default values of llama-factory.

C.2
GRPO

GRPO is performed using TRL as the framework. We conduct GRPO on Saliency-r1-8k for 3 epochs. We utilize an original learning rate of
1\times 10^{-5}
and batch size of 128. LoRA of rank 8 is performed on the query and value projection layers. The number of rollouts is set to be 8. Other settings are set to be the default values of TRL.

Appendix D
Introduction of benchmark

In this section, we provide a brief overview of the benchmarks used in this paper. Our experiments are conducted on well-established or recently introduced visual understanding benchmarks. These benchmarks were selected based on two key criteria: (1) The tasks must be vision-centric, meaning that the questions and answers should be derived from the content of the image, requiring attention to relevant regions within the image. Benchmarks that primarily focus on other aspects, such as mathematical reasoning or code generation, often depend heavily on the intrinsic reasoning abilities of the language model, which falls outside the scope of this paper. (2) The questions must be in a multiple-choice or True/False format, allowing us to directly evaluate the model’s performance without being affected by the variability of LLM evaluation.

MMMU-Pro.
MMMU-Pro
88
is a strengthened version of the MMMU benchmark that more rigorously tests multimodal understanding and reasoning. It filters out questions solvable by text-only LLMs, expands multiple-choice options up to 10 to curb option-guessing, and introduces a vision-only setting where questions and options are embedded in photos/screenshots to require simultaneous “seeing” and “reading.”

MMBench.
MMBench
43
is a comprehensive benchmark for evaluating multimodal models’ understanding and reasoning abilities across fine-grained tasks. It spans a wide range of skills: visual perception, commonsense and logical reasoning, detail recognition, spatial relations, text recognition, and cross-modal alignment, primarily through carefully curated multiple-choice questions. With rigorous human verification and de-duplication to mitigate training data contamination, MMBench aims to provide a fair and reproducible assessment.

POPE.
POPE
40
is designed to evaluate object hallucination in vision–language models using binary-choice questions that verify the presence of specific objects in images. It reports metrics such as Accuracy, Recall, Precision, and F1 across three distinct sampling strategies, providing a robust and fine-grained assessment of hallucination tendencies. In our paper, we report the F1 score for POPE.

MME.
The MME benchmark
10
evaluates multimodal models across 14 subtasks that span both perceptual processing and cognitive reasoning. By using carefully designed instruction–response pairs, MME reduces the likelihood of training data leakage, supporting a fair and rigorous assessment.

MME-RealWorld.
MME-RealWorld
95
is a large, fully human-annotated benchmark for evaluating multimodal LLMs on high-resolution, real-world tasks. It comprises 13,366 images (avg
\sim
2000×1500) and 29,429 multiple-choice QA pairs across 43 subtasks in five domains: OCR in the wild, remote sensing, diagram/table understanding, video monitoring, and autonomous driving. Designed to stress fine-grained perception and reasoning, it includes an “E” option for unanswerable cases and enforces strict quality control by 25 annotators and 7 experts.

MMStar.
MMStar
11
is a vision-indispensable multimodal benchmark of 1,500 human-curated, multiple-choice samples designed to fairly evaluate Large Vision-Language Models. It targets six core capabilities (coarse/fine-grained perception, instance/logical reasoning, science & technology, mathematics) across 18 axes, with strict visual dependency, minimized data leakage, and balanced difficulty.

ChartQA.
ChartQA
48
is a benchmark for testing how effectively multimodal models answer questions about charts, emphasizing both visual comprehension and logical reasoning. It covers a range of chart types—such as bar charts and line graphs—and combines human-authored and automatically generated questions to probe complex reasoning. Importantly, ChartQA is highly OCR-dependent and demands fine-grained visual understanding, as models must extract textual information from charts and reason over it.

IllusionVQA.
IllusionVQA
58
is a two-part visual question answering benchmark designed to test Vision-Language Models on challenging optical illusions. It comprises 435 multiple-choice comprehension instances across 12 illusion categories curated from diverse real images, and a 1,000-sample “soft localization” task where models identify which of two side-by-side objects is geometrically impossible. In this paper, we mainly focus on the soft localization task.

ScienceQA.
ScienceQA
56
is a large-scale multimodal multiple-choice benchmark of about 21k K–12 science questions spanning natural, social, and language sciences, many with text and/or image contexts. Uniquely, it annotates most questions with “lectures” (background knowledge) and “explanations” (reasoning), enabling evaluation and training of chain-of-thought reasoning. The dataset covers 26 topics, 127 categories, and 379 skills, includes both diagrams and natural images, and supports both few-shot prompting and fine-tuning studies.

SalBench.
SalBench
16
is a vision-centric benchmark for Large Vision-Language Models that tests human-obvious visual saliency—finding an “odd-one-out” among distractors based on low-level features. Built by augmenting the P3 (synthetic) and O3 (natural) datasets with language instructions, it evaluates three tasks: Odd-One-Out Detection, Referring Odd-One-Out (text box coordinates), and Visual Referring Odd-One-Out (highlighted box). Features span color, orientation, size (synthetic) and extend to focus, shape, location, pattern (natural).
