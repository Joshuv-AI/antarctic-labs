1.0 Executive Summary
 Nanosecond pulsed discharges (typical pulse duration of about 10 nanoseconds) have attracted widespread
attention due to their wide range of applications in plasma-assisted combustion and aerodynamic flow control,
biomedicine, nanotechnology, and materials processing. This project investigated the plasma dynamics of
nanosecond discharges in a pin-to-pin configuration using a combined experimental and theoretical modeling
approach, leveraging the expertise and resources of Purdue University and the Princeton Collaborative Research
Facility (PCRF).

2.0 Background
 The project was conducted on Purdue University’s main campus in West Lafayette, IN, and at the Princeton
Collaborative Research Facility (PCRF) in Princeton, NJ.
 Purdue University has a modern, highly versatile campus that offers a wide range of services and resources
including laboratory, animal, computer and office facilities. The experimental portion of the project at Purdue was
conducted in Electric Propulsion and Plasma Laboratory, which occupies more than 1,000 sq.ft. in the Neil
Armstrong Hall of Engineering. One particularly important resource for the project was access to multiple machine
shops, including Aerospace Sciences Laboratory Machine Shop, Central Machine Shop, Artisan and Fabrication
Lab. These facilities were used for fabrication of custom-made equipment required for the project, such as
precisely aligned electrode assemblies. The machine shops are equipped with an extensive range of tools and
instrumentation, including mills, lathes (including CNC), welders, and grinders. Simple machining tasks were
performed by students participating in the project, while more complex work was caried out by professional
machinists.

3.0 Project Objectives
 Nanosecond pulsed discharges (typical pulse duration of about 10 ns) have attracted widespread attention
owing to variety of applications in plasma-assisted combustion and aerodynamic flow control, biomedicine,
nanotechnology, and material processing. The major goal of the project was to study plasma dynamics of
nanosecond discharges in pin-to-pin configuration using a joint experimental and theoretical modeling approach.
One objective of the project was to conduct time-resolved measurements of nitrogen vibrational level populations
using Coherent Anti-Stokes Raman Spectroscopy (CARS) diagnostics available at the Princeton Collaborative
Research Facility (PCRF). Another objective was to investigate the electron number density using optical
interferometry.

4.0 Description of Activities Performed
 A Michelson interferometry setup (532 nm CW laser) was employed to record time-resolved interferograms
of the discharge, enabling spatially resolved calculation of the electron number density. An ICCD camera was used
to capture the spatiotemporal evolution of the discharge (streamer formation and spark channel development),
and Coherent Anti-Stokes Raman Spectroscopy (CARS) was used to measure post-discharge N2 vibrational
temperatures. Discharge current and voltage were monitored with a back current shunt. The discharge was

initiated with simultaneous cathode-directed and anode-directed streamers that bridge the gap within ~1 ns and
form a luminous, filamentary plasma channel immediately after breakdown. Laser interferometry measurements
showed peak electron number densities on the order of 1017-1018 cm-3, occurring about 15–20 ns after pulse arrival
at the discharge gap. Shorter gap discharges yielded higher peak electron densities, consistent with the higher
energy density in the smaller gaps. Spatially, the electron density was highest near the electrodes and decreased
toward the mid-gap region, with 5 mm and 7 mm gaps exhibiting a pronounced drop in the central channel. CARS
indicated initial vibrational temperatures of approximately 4,000–6,000 K in the spark core at about 50 ns after
the discharge onset, which decayed over a timescale of roughly 500 ns as the plasma cooled and recombined.

5.0 Conclusions and Recommendations
 We experimentally characterized a pin-to-pin nanosecond pulsed discharge in largely unexplored yet
practically important regime of high-energy pulses (~20-30 mJ deposited over about 10 ns pulse to 3-7 mm air
gaps) and outlined several state-of-the-art diagnostic techniques relevant for these discharges, along with their
applicability limits. The measurements indicated that high degrees of ionization (with electron number densities
above 1018 cm−3) can be expected for short electrode gaps (≤3 mm).

6.0 Lessons Learned
 This work paved the way for the use of state-of-the-art diagnostic techniques to characterize nanosecond
discharges and analyzed their applicability limits.
 We found that the 1-mm probing region of the utilized CARS system—larger than the sub-millimeter diameter
of the nanosecond spark—introduced significant measurement uncertainties due to strong contributions from
surrounding cold gas to the measured signal. To obtain reliable vibrational temperature data, CARS system with a
sub-millimeter probing region size is required.
 The single-wavelength optical interferometry was shown to be an effective tool for evaluating plasma
dynamics within the nanosecond pulse. However, improved temporal resolution to sub-nanosecond levels is
required to accurately resolve the plasma dynamics, as well as the capability to separate the contributions of
plasma electrons and heavy particles. To achieve this, two-wavelength, picosecond or femtosecond optical
interferometry should be utilized.
 The back current shunt proved to be a highly effective tool for obtaining accurate, high-quality measurements
of discharge voltage and current. The coherent microwave scattering has been shown to be ineffective for
characterizing the dense plasmas (with 𝑛𝑛𝑒𝑒 >1016 cm-3) associated with high-energy nanosecond discharges.
