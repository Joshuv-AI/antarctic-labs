Executive Summary
We aimed to develop a catalytic membrane reactor (CMR) that couples water–gas shift (WGS) reaction
with in-situ H2 separation to produce blue H2 and a CO2-rich stream from coal- and biomass-derived syngas.
Our core approach employs high-permeance carbon molecular sieve (CMS) hollow-fiber membranes
(HFMs) with strong gas separation ability, which we further integrate into catalytic membrane reactors to
intensify H2 production and CO2 capture in a single unit. Three organizations with complementary skills
collaborated to achieve the goal, including the University at Buffalo (UB), Los Alamos National Laboratory
(LANL), and Trimeric Corporation (Trimeric).
 We fabricated CMS HFMs that maintain stable mixed-gas H₂/CO₂ separation (selectivity=103 and H2
permeance=1020 GPU at 375oC) under simulated WGS reaction conditions. We established repeatable
spinning/carbonization and acid-doping protocols, then fabricated multi-fiber bundles (≥32 fibers) and
demonstrated stable mixed-gas H₂/CO₂ separation at 200–300 °C with CO and steam. High H₂ permeance
was maintained while H₂/CO₂ selectivity remained in the targeted range, with multi-hundred-hour
durability.
 We synthesized flame-aerosol high-entropy metal-oxide (HEMO) WGS catalysts. A (FeCrNiCoMn)Ox
spinel outperformed a conventional Fe–Cr catalyst at high temperature (e.g., ~90% CO conversion at 450
°C), and a (FeCrNiCoMn)3O4 variant met our kinetic target with a CO2 reaction order of −0.42, mitigating
product inhibition. We demonstrated CMRs with a synthesized HEMO catalyst at ~350 °C, 30 psig, and
dried gas hourly space velocity (DGHSV)≈12,500 h⁻¹, increased CO conversion by ~20% over a packed
bed and achieved up to ~60% H₂ recovery. The CMRs also present strong H2 purification
(H2/(CO2+CO+H2) > 98%) and CO2 capture performance (CO2/(CO2+CO+H2) > 95%) at various conditions.
We completed 400-h long-term and multi-cycle stability tests without performance loss and >100-h
continuous operation of the CMRs for WGS reaction.
 We performed a model-based sensitivity analysis using our validated MATLAB membrane-reactor
framework (1-D, isothermal, plug-flow; counter-current permeation with WGS kinetics) to rank the
technical levers that control performance. At a fixed reactor volume, with the desired target H₂ recovery
and purity setpoint, the dominant driver of CO conversion is H₂ permeance through the membrane. H₂/CO₂
selectivity governs dry H₂ purity and CO₂ slip; maintaining selectivity in the ~40–60 band with sub-percent
module leakage enables > 90% dry H₂ without further downstream purification. Sweep ratio and staging
further enhance the transmembrane driving force, with counter-current flow consistently outperforming co-
current under otherwise identical conditions. These results define clear design targets for CMR, including
maximizing effective membrane area and permeance per footprint, minimizing leakage and permeate
pressure, and operating in the temperature window that balances kinetics and selectivity.
 We conducted a techno-economic analysis (TEA) and found that the CMR design evaluated for this
TEA was not economically favorable compared to the conventional WGS reactors from the NREL Base
Case. The hydrogen revenue for the CMR case was a few percentage points higher than the NREL Base
Case. The CMR produces a higher-purity stream than the Base Case, but both cases still require additional
purification, during which some hydrogen is lost regardless of inlet concentration. The CMR required
nearly six times as much steam as the conventional WGS, which is costly. Our findings show that reducing
the required steam flow would significantly lower operating costs associated with the CMR and reduce the
size of the equipment required to process the resulting carbon dioxide-rich and hydrogen-rich streams.
Alternate CMR designs with less steam should be pursued. One option is to retain the high temperature
shift (HTS) of the conventional WGS and replace the low temperature shift (LTS) with the CMR unit.
Additionally, increasing hydrogen permeance through the membrane without requiring additional steam
flow would reduce hydrogen losses and increase revenue. If the CMR could produce higher-purity
hydrogen, it could eliminate the need for a downstream purification unit, thereby reducing capital costs and
hydrogen losses associated with purification.

1. Project Accomplishments and Milestones
Our approach to developing a high-performance catalytic membrane reactor (CMR) combines carbon
molecular sieve (CMS) hollow fiber membranes with high-entropy WGS catalysts, followed by system
integration, physics-based modeling, and techno-economic assessment. Specifically, we designed and
optimized CMS membranes, achieving H₂/CO₂ separation performance that surpasses that of state-of-the-art
membranes. In parallel, we built and validated a 1-D MATLAB model of catalytic membrane reactors,
synthesized nanoscale high-entropy metal-oxide (HEMO) WGS catalysts, and integrated membranes and
catalysts into bench-scale CMRs for validation and >100-h operation. Finally, we conducted a techno-
economic analysis (TEA) to evaluate process feasibility and cost. We have also developed a Technology
Management Plan (TMP) by assessing the status of our technology and laying out a commercialization plan.

2. Simulate and Design CMRs
 Summary: We have successfully developed models to describe CMR performance based on catalyst
and membrane performance and validated them with experimental data. The model can be used to
conduct a sensitive study to optimize membrane and catalyst performance for syngas from different
biomass sources. The effects of pressure, gas hourly space velocity, membrane performance, and sweep
flow rate on CMR performance were investigated, and the steam sweep flow rate is critical for achieving
superior performance. Consequently, we met Milestone 5: Models for CMRs. The details are described
below.
 A MATLAB model was developed to simulate a conventional two-stage water-gas reactor system. The
model was used to identify operating parameters that minimize the total catalyst volume, providing a
baseline for process intensification by membrane reactor processes. Simulated comparisons of WGS
reactions between CMRs and packed bed reactors (PBRs) were conducted. For comparison purposes, two
feeds comparable to the effluent from small, stationary, downdraft, fixed-bed gasifiers operating at 800-
900 °C and 25-30 atm, with one feed comparable to the effluent from a commercial natural gas steam
reformer, were selected. The dry feed compositions are shown in Table 2.

 Constrained optimization has been performed on the steam-swept membrane reactor; the others have
been simulated, and initial trial-and-error optimization has been performed. These analyses have
highlighted the challenges of using a porous membrane:
 • A high steam flow rate is needed on the sweep side: This keeps the partial pressures of the other
 gases small, leading to good permeation rates.
 • A high steam flow rate entering the sweep side will rapidly permeate to the reaction side because
 steam has the largest permeability of all the gases: This dilutes the CO on the reaction side, leading
 to a low reaction rate and a large catalyst volume.
 • Attempting to reduce the permeation of steam to the reaction side by feeding less steam to the
 sweep side results in losing too much CO to the sweep side.
 • Attempting to reduce the permeation of steam by feeding more steam to the reaction side dilutes
 the CO on the reaction side, again leading to a low reaction rate and a large catalyst volume.
 The most promising configuration at present is the high-temperature packed bed reactor, followed by a
low-temperature MR. The DGHSV for this reactor is comparable to two packed-bed reactors in series. To
identify the optimal operating conditions for the membrane reactor, a single high-temperature WGS
membrane reactor (Fig. 1) was simulated to replace the conventional two-packed-bed reactor system
through process intensification and to produce a fraction of H2 at higher purity. In the simulation, it is
assumed that a bundle of hollow-fiber membranes will pass through the outer shell of the membrane reactor,
which is packed with a catalyst. A shell pressure of 25 atm is assumed. Two hydrogen streams are recovered.
To reap the potential purification benefit of a membrane reactor, it is essential that the sweep gas flowing
through the tubes is easily separated from the H2 and other gases that permeate into the tubes. Here it is
assumed that steam is used as the sweep gas.

 The Biomass 1 feed operating at 97% H2 yield was chosen to study the effect of SDGR. Fig. 3a-c shows
the molar flow rate profiles and the temperature profile in a membrane reactor with an SDGR of 10. The
large excess of steam on the tube side serves as a thermal sink, and therefore, the inlet temperature can be
high, leading to a high reaction rate and a small catalyst volume. A series of optimizations was performed
as above, in which the maximum SDGR varied from 1.7 to 10. Fig. 3d shows how DGHSV is affected by
the maximum SDGR, 10. It also shows the results of optimizing series-packed bed reactors for this feed at
97% H2 yield. As the maximum SDGR decreases from 10 to 1.7, the DGHSV decreases from about double
that of the series packed beds to around one-third of it (as shown in Fig. 3d). As SDGR increases from 3 to
10 (i.e., in the range where the membrane reactor DGHSV is greater than series packed beds) the fraction
of H2 recovered at high dry purity (89 to 91%), increases from 86 to 96%, compared to 59.3% for series
packed bed reactors. The tube pressure is relatively stable at 12.5-13 atm, while the optimum membrane
area per catalyst volume increases from ~4.9 to 12.8. Within the same range, the fraction of the steam fed
to the tubes increases from ~51 to 92%. This suggests that the intensification observed in Fig. 3d is largely
due to heat absorption by the steam in the tubes.

 Researchers have attempted to improve catalytic activity for HT-WGS by adding various functional
and structural promoters. Previous studies demonstrated that first-row transition metals can significantly
influence HT-WGS performance; for example, Cu has been shown to enhance activity,1, 2 while Mg2+, Mn2+,
Co2+, Ni2+, and Zn2+ have been systematically evaluated as promoters in Fe-Cr catalysts.3 Structural
promoters such as Co,4 Ni,5-7 and Mo8 have further been reported to stabilize magnetite-based catalysts by
increasing surface area, forming mixed oxide or alloy phases, and suppressing metallic Fe formation under
high CO concentrations. This showed that the catalyst can exhibit synergistic effects, in which the combined
presence of two or more promoters leads to catalytic performance greater than the sum of the individual
effects of each promoter. Based on this understanding, the selection of elements in this work was further
informed by the catalytic membrane reactor (CMR) model developed in Task 2.0, which showed that the
membrane reactor configuration was most effective in a second reactor, following an initial HT reactor.
This implies a high CO2 concentration and thus highlights the importance of reducing CO2 inhibition or
increasing the reaction rate. Transition metals such as Fe, Ni, Co, Mn, and V were therefore selected to
simultaneously address redox activity, structural stability, and resistance to CO2 inhibition, motivating the
use of high-entropy metal oxides (HEMOs) as a platform to integrate multiple functional and structural
roles within a single, entropy-stabilized oxide phase. This model-guided, multicomponent design strategy
represents a rational extension of conventional promoter-based approaches toward catalyst architectures
tailored specifically for HT-WGS operation in a CMR.9-12

base-case study of biomass-to-hydrogen. The energy and operating costs were examined, and aspects for
the CMRs to improve are identified. Consequently, we met Milestone 10: Final TEA.

conventional WGS reactors in a biomass gasification process designed to produce a high-purity hydrogen

this TEA, and the biomass gasification process it describes will be referred to interchangeably as the NREL

 Process Descriptions: This section will first describe the NREL biomass gasification process used as
the baseline case. Then, alterations to the base case through the replacement of the conventional WGS
reactor with the CMR will be described in subsequent sections.
 NREL Baseline Case Description: The NREL biomass gasification process receives biomass as an input
to produce a purified hydrogen product. A block flow diagram of this process is shown in Fig. 37. A process
flow diagram of the baseline case highlighting the scope of the TEA is shown in Fig. 38. A more detailed
block flow diagram of the NREL Case can be found in the executive summary of the NREL Report. The
biomass gasification process comprises five main operations:
 Gasification (Dryer, Gasifier, and Char Combustor) – Wood chips received as process feed are dried in
a rotary drier from 50 wt% moisture down to around 12 wt% before being conveyed to the gasifier. Inside
the gasifier, endothermic reactions take place using steam as a process medium to gasify the biomass. The
gasified biomass is a syngas that contains primarily hydrogen, water, carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide,
methane and trace amounts of other hydrocarbons, ammonia, and hydrogen sulfide. Hot sand is fed into the
gasifier as a motive fluid for the wood chips and to provide heat to fuel the reaction, then recycled to the
char combustor. Any remaining biomass in the form of char can then be burned in the char combustor to
reheat the sand.
 Syngas Processing (Tar Reformer, Syngas Compression, LO-CAT, and ZnO Bed) – Syngas vapor from
the gasification unit is processed through a tar reformer (a fluidized bed reactor) for cleanup prior to
compression. The syngas is cooled before being compressed from near-atmospheric conditions to over 400
psig using a five-stage centrifugal compressor. The pressurized syngas is then processed in a LO-CAT unit
followed by a ZnO bed to remove sulfur impurities from the syngas. Sulfur in the syngas must be reduced
from around 400 ppmv to below 1 ppmv since it poisons the low temperature shift (LTS) reactor catalyst.
 Hydrogen Production (Steam Reformer and HTS and LTS Reactors of the WGS) – Syngas from the
ZnO Bed is heated before entering the steam reformer, where reformation reactions take place to convert

                                                  38                                       DE-FE0032209

hydrocarbons in the syngas into hydrogen and carbon monoxide with the addition of steam and heat. The
syngas exiting the steam reformer is slightly cooled before entering the WGS reactors. The WGS reactors
are fixed bed equilibrium reactors that contain a high temperature shift (HTS) reactor followed by a low
temperature shift (LTS) reactor in series. The HTS uses an iron oxide/chromium oxide catalyst mixture,
and the LTS uses a copper oxide/zinc oxide mixture to carry out the WGS reaction to convert steam and
carbon monoxide into hydrogen and carbon dioxide. After the WGS reactors, the shifted gas primarily
contains steam, hydrogen, and carbon dioxide. The shifted gas is then cooled below its dew point to remove
the bulk of any water remaining.
 Hydrogen Processing (PSA, Combustor, Hydrogen Compression) – To create a high-purity hydrogen
product, a pressure swing adsorption (PSA) unit adsorbs impurities from the shifted gas. The PSA contains
multiple beds in parallel filled with activated carbon and zeolites that are cycled between adsorption and
desorption modes of operation. First, a bed will be in adsorption mode at elevated pressure, where water,
carbon dioxide, and trace amounts of unreacted carbon monoxide and hydrocarbons are adsorbed by the
bed, producing a hydrogen product with a purity that can be greater than 99.99 mol%. After a specified
period, the bed is placed in regeneration mode where pressure is let down to release the adsorbed species
as an off gas. The beds operate in pairs with one bed in each mode to ensure the PSA is always online and
capable of processing shifted gas. The off gas leaving the PSA is sent to the combustor where it is combined
with natural gas and air to be burned to provide the heat required by the steam reformer. The flue gas from
the combustor can then be vented to atmosphere. The purified hydrogen stream will be pressurized to over
1,000 psig with a two-stage reciprocal compressor where the hydrogen product leaves the facility via
pipeline.
 Steam Cycle and Power Generation – The biomass gasification process requires industrial quantities of
steam to be consumed as a resource in the gasifier and shift reactors, therefore a steam generation system
is required. To generate this steam, there are multiple series of cross exchangers to transfer heat from hot
process streams to boiler feed water. Boiler feed water (BFW) is fed from a condensate surge tank through
a deaerator before being pumped to a pressure of 1300 psig. The BFW then passes through the first series
of cross exchangers of various hot process streams, to heat the BFW to its saturation temperature. The
saturated water is then stored for holdup in the steam drum before getting sent to the next series of cross
exchangers to produce saturated steam and returning to the steam drum. The saturated steam is then sent
through the final series of cross exchangers to produce superheated steam. This superheated steam is then
fed to a steam turbine where it is let down to generate a portion of the power required by the gasification
process. High pressure steam is pulled off the generator to be sent to the steam reformer. Low pressure
steam is pulled off the generator to be fed to the gasifier. The remaining steam is let down to atmospheric
conditions in the turbine for power generation before it is condensed and returned to the condensate surge
drum. Process condensate from separators is also recovered to be used as boiler feedwater. A small amount
of make-up water is additionally required to feed the steam cycle to account for blowdown and other losses.

 The novel CMR developed by the recipient is an alternative to conventional WGS reactors. The CMR
is designed to replace the conventional WGS reactors while keeping the upstream biomass gasification to
hydrogen process identical to the NREL Base Case. A block flow diagram in Fig. 37 shows how the CMR
integrates into the biomass gasification process. The CMR is a CMS-based hollow-fiber membrane
embedded with conventional, metal oxide catalyst to carry out the WGS reaction. Additionally, the CMR
partially separates the shift gas by allowing the hydrogen gas product to permeate across the membrane.
The CMR results in a hydrogen-rich stream on the tube side of the membrane (permeate) and CO2-rich
stream on the shell side of the membrane (retentate). The syngas from the reformer is fed at 425 psig to the
shell side of the membrane where the WGS reaction takes place. Most of the carbon monoxide present in
the syngas is converted to carbon dioxide in an environment of excess steam, where steam is consumed to
produce hydrogen. The off gas leaving the shell side of the CMR combines with the off gas from the PSA
and is then fed to the combustor.
 Steam is required on both the shell-side and steam side of the CMR. On the shell side, steam is required
to drive the shift reaction; on the tube side, steam promotes hydrogen permeance across the membrane and
mitigates temperature rise in the reactor by providing thermal mass to absorb the exothermic heat of the
WGS reaction.
 The recipient quantified the steam feed to the CMR as the molar ratio of the overall steam feed (shell
+ tube side) to the dry gas feed to the CMR. This ratio is called the SDGR, i.e., steam to dry gas ratio.
SDGR values of 2 to 10 were modeled. At the lowest SDGR of 2, the desired maximum catalyst temperature
was not reached. For this TEA, Trimeric selected the smallest technically-feasible steam feed rate modeled
by the recipient (an SDGR value of 4). At an SDR value of 4, there was a significant increase in the CMR
steam requirement compared to the conventional WGS reactor. At an SDGR value of 4, the conventional
WGS reactor operates with 153,000 lb/hr of water (as steam) in the feed gas from the reformer; the CMR
requires additional steam for both the shell side (another 140,000 lb/hr of water) and the tube-side (576,000
lb/hr of water) to drive the reaction, promote hydrogen permeance across the membrane, and absorb the
heat of reaction.
 The steam generated in the NREL Base Case is not enough to satisfy the CMR steam requirement, so
additional steam must be generated externally. The heat input required for the CMR steam is significant;
for context, it is nearly on par with the biomass heat input required for the NREL Base Case biomass
gasification plant. For the sake of simplicity (i.e., to avoid significant changes to the upstream gasification
process), the cost of this steam was represented using the cost of natural gas to supply the heat, with boiler
feed water sourced from CMR condensate streams as well as make up water. This natural gas-fired boiler
option was the least expensive capital cost option available to produce the additional steam; for a carbon-
neutral steam supply, the flue gas from the natural gas boiler would need to be treated with a CO2 capture
process (shown on the process diagrams but not included in the cost analysis for this TEA). Alternatively,
biomass could be the fuel supply for the steam, but this would be in addition to the biomass feed for the
gasification process. At the modular scale, a custom electric boiler to supply steam at the required pressure
of 425 psig might be an option, but it would need a supply of cheap, low-carbon electricity to be cost
competitive with the natural gas option. Another option for the steam supply is to install a Combined Heat
and Power (CHP) (or cogeneration) plant for provision of electricity as well as steam for the CMR. The
amount of electricity generated relative to heat produced depends upon the CHP configuration chosen (e.g.,
gas-fired turbine or biomass-fired steam turbine); the CHP could provide electricity for the biomass
gasification process, and perhaps additional electricity for sale to the electricity market. However, the
addition of the CHP (or any steam generation method) increases the overall footprint of the gasification
system, offsetting any reductions in footprint from the use of less catalyst in the CMR. Overall, while the
installation of a CHP unit may make the production of steam more energy efficient, the capital cost
efficiency will be significantly lower.
 The tube side permeate leaving the CMR is a hydrogen-rich stream at a lower pressure of 206 psig, due
to pressure drop across the membrane. This pressure drop is significant, nearly 200 psi, compared to the
15-psi pressure drop across the HTS, LTS, and exchanger in the conventional WGS reactor. Since the

                                                   41                                         DE-FE0032209

hydrogen-rich stream exiting the CMR was at a lower pressure than the operating pressure of the PSA, it
was re-compressed in a 3-stage compressor to the PSA operating pressure of 400 psig.

 Option 1 for the CMR (Fig. 40) replaces both the HTS and LTS reactors with a single CMR unit. The

shell side of the membrane. The retentate stream combines with the PSA off gas to be fed to the combustor.
The tube side permeate is fed to the PSA to produce a purified hydrogen product. Optional steam turbines
downstream of both CMR outlet streams as well as on the tube side inlet recover energy by letting down
the streams (which are mostly steam) to their specified conditions.
 Option 2 for the CMR (Fig. 41) is identical to Option 1, except only the LTS is replaced by the CMR
(i.e., the HTS is retained). All other processing would remain the same as CMR Option 1. CMR Option 2
was developed by the recipient after realizing that the high steam consumption of CMR Option 1 was not
techno-economically favorable. CMR Option 2 was not analyzed for this TEA; it is presented as an
alternative option for future analysis, if design concepts to improve the economic viability of the CMR are
explored. While the steam consumption of CMR Option 2 would be lower than Option 1, it will still have
significantly higher steam consumption than the conventional WGS reactor.

 Trimeric used the results from the recipient’s CMR Option 1 modeling as an input to develop a
simulation of the process downstream of the CMR. The process model was developed in Schlumberger’s
Symmetry software. Trimeric first modeled CMR Option 1 as replacing both the HTS and LTS with a single
CMR unit.
 The streams leaving the CMR contain large amounts of water, so the streams were let down to recover
energy and cooled to condense as much water as possible.
 The carbon dioxide-rich stream leaving the CMR (which is mostly steam) was first let down to 20 psig
in a turbine to generate power. Then, it was cooled in an exchanger and fed to a knock-out vessel to remove

 48

condensed water. The vapor from the knock-out vessel was then fed to the combustor to fully combust any
remaining carbon monoxide, hydrogen, methane, or other trace species.
 The hydrogen-rich stream from the CMR was let down to 40 psig in a turbine to generate power. Then,
it was cooled in an exchanger and fed to a knock-out vessel to remove condensed water. The vapor from
the knock-out vessel was then repressurized in hydrogen compressor stages 1-3 up to 400 psig to feed the
PSA. The hydrogen compressor was modeled as a 5-stage compressor with the discharge temperature of
each stage specified to be below 300°F. The pressure ratio in each stage was approximately 2.1. Stages 1-
3 pressurize the hydrogen from 40 to 400 psig to feed the PSA, while stages 4-5 compressed the purified
hydrogen from the PSA from 400 to 1000 psig. The hydrogen-rich stream leaving stage 3 of the compressor
is fed to the PSA where any impurities are adsorbed into the bed. The adsorbed gases are released during
the regeneration stage of the PSA unit and then fed to the combustor alongside the carbon dioxide-rich
stream from the CMR. To maximize recovery of the high purity hydrogen feeding the PSA, the estimated
hydrogen recovery for the PSA was based on the highest hydrogen recovery cited in the NREL Report:
90%. Actual recovery will be dependent on operating conditions for the PSA. The remainder of the
hydrogen and all other remaining gases adsorbed into the bed were sent to the combustor. The hydrogen
leaving the PSA is assumed to have a purity of near-100%. The hydrogen product enters the compressor
stages 4-5 to be pressurized up to 1000 psig, the hydrogen product pressure specified in the NREL Base
Case. The pressurized hydrogen product then leaves the facility via pipeline.

Utility and Energy Summary
 The NREL Base Case leveraged heat recovery of hot process streams in the biomass gasification
process to generate all the required steam; however, there was not enough heat remaining in the hot process
streams to generate the additional steam required for the CMR. For the CMR case, a gas-fired boiler unit
was added to produce the additional required steam.
 A comparison of utility usage between the NREL Base Case and the CMR Option 1 is shown in Tables
20-24. The values from the NREL Base Case come from the NREL Report. The values from CMR Option
1 come from the Symmetry process model developed by Trimeric. In general, these tables show that the
CMR Option 1 case had significantly higher utility requirements than the NREL Base Case.
 Comparing the cooling duty summaries in Tables 20 and 21 highlights that the CMR Option 1 case
required over four times the cooling duty of the NREL Base Case. The increase in cooling duty is driven
by higher flow rates leaving the CMR than in the base-case shift reactors. The flow rates leaving the CMR
and shift reactors were identical on a water-free basis, but the CMR's additional steam requirement
increased the total flow around the unit. These additional steam flows added significant cooling duty
requirements to the CMR, as this steam must be condensed and removed from the non-condensable gases
leaving the CMR. The costs of this additional cooling duty (i.e., capital costs, parasitic power for pumps
and fans, water, and water treatment costs) were not quantified for this TEA.
 Comparing the electricity requirement summary in Tables 22-24 shows that the CMR Option 1 case
has almost three times the power demand of the base case. The increased electricity requirement in the
CMR Option 1 case stems from additional hydrogen compression in stages 1-3 to re-pressurize the tube-
side outlet of the CMR to the required PSA feed conditions. To address this, turbines could be added to
recover energy from streams around the CMR where a large pressure drop was already required by the
CMR process; these streams are the CMR tube-side feed, the CMR tube-side outlet, and the CMR shell-
side outlet. If these turbines were purchased and installed, they would generate sufficient electricity to make
the CMR process a net electricity producer.

Economic Evaluation for CMR Option 1
 The scope of this economic evaluation focused solely on the gasification process downstream of the
reformer to compare the CMR Option 1 to the conventional WGS reactor in the NREL Base Case. All
processing and sizing upstream of the WGS reactors were identical between cases, so the process upstream
of the reformer was not included in this economic comparison. This economic analysis focused on key
operating expenses (OPEX) and revenue values identified as having a large impact on overall costs,
including natural gas costs, electricity costs, catalyst costs, hydrogen revenue, and possible power
generation revenue. The opex/revenue analysis indicated that the CMR Case Option 1 was not favorable
compared to the NREL Base Case; therefore, other process design alternatives were considered (e.g., CMR
Case Option 2) for implementing the CMR. The economic evaluation for Option 1 included a cursory semi-
quantitative assessment of the equipment costs.

 The annualized capital costs of the key equipment in the baseline NREL WGS Case (i.e., WGS reactor,

 54

to supply the steam to the CMR. In the NREL WGS Case, the total installed costs for the equipment in the

Conclusions and recommendations from the TEA study
 Initial modeling indicated that the CMR could achieve similar reaction conversion to a conventional
water-gas shift (WGS) reactor while utilizing a smaller catalyst volume and creating a higher purity
hydrogen stream. However, the CMR requires significantly more steam and incurs a significantly higher
pressure drop than a conventional WGS reactor. While the CMR produces a higher purity hydrogen-rich
stream than the conventional WGS reactor, the purity is not sufficient to meet the DOE’s stated
requirements for transportation fuel cell use or for hydrogen for heating/boiler applications; additional
purification was still required for the hydrogen-rich stream.
 For the recipient’s early-stage process concept, Trimeric developed the TEA with a focus on the key
revenue streams (hydrogen) and utility requirements (heat, electricity, cooling) for the CMR process vs. the
conventional WGS reactor. The CMR design evaluated for this TEA was not economically favorable
compared to the conventional WGS reactors from the NREL Base Case. The key comparisons of revenues
and utility costs were as follows:
 • The hydrogen revenue for the CMR case was a few percentage points higher than the NREL Base
 Case. The CMR produces a higher purity stream than the Base Case, but both cases still require
 additional purification, where some hydrogen losses are incurred regardless of inlet concentration.
 The CMR also incurs some hydrogen loss that does not permeate the CMR and thus remains with
 a CO2-rich membrane retentate.
 • The CMR requires nearly six times more steam than the conventional WGS reactor. The heat input
 associated with this steam requirement was nearly the same as the biomass heat input to the
 gasification process. The cost to supply this additional steam poses a significant challenge to the
 economic viability of the process, even when using the lowest capital cost option of a natural gas
 boiler. The cost would further increase if carbon capture were employed to capture the CO2 from
 the boiler flue gas.
 • The CMR has significantly higher cooling duty requirements due to the increased steam
 requirement for the CMR; this incremental sweep steam must be condensed and removed from the
 non-condensable gases leaving the CMR.
 • The CMR has higher compression power requirements due to the significant (~200 psi) pressure
 drop across the CMR, which results in the need to re-pressurize the hydrogen-rich stream.
 • The CMR has a lower catalyst operating cost than the conventional WGS reactor, but this benefit
 was insignificant compared to the cost of supplying the additional steam for the CMR. Any savings
 in CMR equipment costs and footprint due to use of less catalyst will be eclipsed by increased
 capital costs associated with steam generation and the larger equipment sizes of exchangers and
 separators to handle the larger steam flows around the CMR.
 • The annualized capital costs of the key WGS-area equipment in the NREL Base Case (i.e., WGS
 reactor, PSA, and associated exchangers and knockouts) were significantly less than the cost to
 supply steam to the CMR. While cogeneration systems could be used to supply the steam for the
 CMR and, also generate electricity that could be used by the gasification process and/or sold to
 market, this steam supply system would increase the capital cost and footprint of the gasification
 process.

 55

 Reducing the required steam flow would significantly reduce the operating costs associated with the
CMR and would reduce the size of the equipment to process the resulting carbon dioxide-rich and hydrogen-
rich streams. Alternate design options for the CMR that would require less steam should be considered.
One option is to retain the HTS of the conventional WGS reactor and replace the LTS with the CMR unit.
 Other improvements to the CMR performance could also improve its viability. For instance, increasing
hydrogen permeance through the membrane without requiring additional steam flow would reduce the
amount of hydrogen lost and increase revenue. If the CMR could produce higher purity hydrogen, it could
eliminate the need for a downstream purification unit; this would decrease capital costs and purification-
related hydrogen losses.
