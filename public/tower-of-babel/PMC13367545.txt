Chronicity and comorbidities: Is estimated longer duration of obstructive sleep apnoea linked to higher cardiometabolic risk?
Authors: Krishnapriya S. Kumar, Kritarth Rawat, Lokesh Kumar Saini, Barun Kumar, Kalyani Sridharan, Ravi Gupta, Saikat Banerjee, Mayank Mishra, Ruchi Dua, Prakhar Sharma, Girish Sindhwani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and Objectives:Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is associated with an increased risk of cardiometabolic disorders. Still, the role of OSA duration in the development and severity of these conditions remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the association between estimated OSA duration and cardio-metabolic comorbidities.Materials and Methods:In this cross-sectional study, 279 adults (≥35 years; body mass index ≥20 kg/m2) with moderate–severe OSA diagnosed by level-1 polysomnography were included. Diagnosis of cardiometabolic disorders was based on examination, medical records, and laboratory investigations. Duration of OSA was estimated using the OSA-Onset algorithm, and comorbidity prevalence was compared across tertiles of estimated duration.Results:In this cross-sectional exploratory analysis, the median estimated OSA duration was 18.7 years, and the median estimated age of onset was 32.3 years. Systemic hypertension was the most prevalent comorbidity (79.6%) and showed a higher prevalence across tertiles of estimated OSA duration (68.8%, 83.9%, and 86%, respectively; P = 0.007). In multivariable models, estimated OSA duration was statistically associated with hypertension (odds ratio [OR] 1.039, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.012–1.068; P = 0.005). Sensitivity analysis excluding participants with implausible negative onset estimates yielded similar results (OR 1.036, 95% CI 1.006–1.066; P = 0.017). No statistically significant associations were observed for other cardiometabolic conditions. Given multiple comparisons and correlated outcomes, findings should be interpreted as exploratory.Conclusion:In this cross-sectional exploratory study, longer algorithm-estimated OSA duration was statistically associated with hypertension but not with other cardiometabolic comorbidities. These findings are hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in longitudinal studies.

INTRODUCTION

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a common yet underdiagnosed chronic sleep disorder and a major contributor to morbidity and mortality.[123] Its prevalence among Indian adults is high, substantially adding to the global disease burden.[4] Untreated OSA is associated with a wide range of cardiometabolic and systemic comorbidities, including hypertension, coronary artery disease (CAD), heart failure, arrhythmias, stroke, metabolic syndrome, diabetes mellitus (DM), chronic kidney disease (CKD), depression, etc.[56] Though these are primarily mediated by intermittent hypoxia, fluctuating intrathoracic pressure, and recurring micro-arousals, dominant mechanisms may vary across comorbidities.[7891011]

Whether treatment of OSA mitigates cardiometabolic risk remains controversial. While some studies have reported improvements in blood pressure, lipid profile, and insulin sensitivity with continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) therapy, large randomized trials, including the SAVE trial, have failed to demonstrate significant reductions in cardiovascular events, particularly among asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic patients.[61213141516171819]

The limited cardiovascular benefit of CPAP may reflect several factors, including prolonged duration of untreated OSA, irreversible end-organ damage from chronic intermittent hypoxia and inflammation, shared risk factors such as obesity, and methodological limitations of existing trials.[202122] Notably, the duration of untreated OSA is rarely quantified, as symptom-based estimates are unreliable due to interindividual variability, delayed recognition, and recall bias.[23]

To overcome this limitation, Olaithe et al. developed the “OSA-Onset algorithm,” which estimates duration of OSA and age of onset using apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) from a single diagnostic polysomnography (PSG), along with body mass index (BMI) and gender.[19] Despite growing interest in disease chronicity, the relationship between OSA duration and cardiometabolic comorbidities has not been systematically evaluated.

Therefore, this study aimed to examine the association between estimated duration of moderate-to-severe OSA, derived using the OSA-Onset algorithm, and the prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities. To our knowledge, this is the first study to address the relationship between OSA duration and cardiometabolic risk. Given the established influence of age, dietary intake, physical activity, smoking, and family history on cardiometabolic risk, these potential confounders were adjusted for in the analysis.[242526]

METHODS

This cross-sectional observational study was conducted at the Sleep Laboratory, Division of Sleep Medicine, between January 2024 and March 2025, after obtaining approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee (AIIMS/IEC/24/44). Data were collected anonymously after obtaining written informed consent from the participants. Data protection, privacy, and confidentiality were maintained throughout the study process.

Study population

Consecutive, treatment-naïve, adult patients (≥35 years) diagnosed with moderate to severe OSA based on International Classification of Sleep Disorders, third edition- text revision (ICSD-3-TR) criteria[27] through attended PSG and having BMI ≥20 kg/m2 were enrolled. These cut-offs for age and BMI were chosen because OSA-Onset algorithm works best for people who are ≥35 years of age and with a BMI ≥20 kg/m2.[19] However, participants having chronic liver disease, CKD, type-1 DM, uncontrolled hypothyroidism, or those already receiving PAP or other therapy for OSA were excluded.

Sample size

Sample size was calculated using GPower version 3.1.9.4 (Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf, Germany) for comparison of proportions across three independent groups (lower, middle, and upper tertiles of estimated OSA duration), assuming a medium effect size (w = 0.3), α = 0.05, power (1−β) = 80%, and df = 2. The minimum required sample size was 324 participants (108 per group). Although 328 patients were initially enrolled, application of the OSA-Onset algorithm eligibility criteria (age ≥35 years and BMI ≥20 kg/m2) resulted in 279 participants being included in the final analysis.

Study procedure

A structured proforma was completed that captured demographic data, lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol), physical activity (assessed via International Physical Activity Questionnaire[28] and classified based on Met minutes and type, frequency and duration of activity), dietary habits (Recommended Dietary Allowance for calorie intake based on a 3-day 24-h dietary recall using the HealthifyMe app[29]), past and current medical history, family history of comorbidities in first degree relatives, and physical examination including anthropometric measurements.

Polysomnography

Diagnosis of OSA was based on clinical information and attended diagnostic video-synchronised PSG study (Somnoscreen HD, Somnomedics GmbH, Germany). Studies were performed after participants were given adequate time to acclimatize to the sleep laboratory. Recording included six channels of electroencephalography, two channels of electrooculography, and two channels of chin electromyography for identifying wakefulness and sleep stages. Respiratory parameters included airflow (recorded by oro-nasal thermistor and nasal pressure transducers); respiratory efforts using respiratory inductance plethysmography belts for chest and abdomen, overnight oximetry and snore microphone. In addition, one channel of electrocardiogram was used to record cardiac activity and two channels of electromyogram from bilateral anterior tibialis muscles were used to assess leg movements. Other channels included body position sensor and synchronized audio-visual recording. Sensors were placed according to recommendations of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) scoring manual version 3.0.[30] The raw data were manually scored and report was prepared according to the AASM scoring manual by trained staff and supervised by trained sleep physicians.[30]

Diagnosis of obstructive sleep apnea

OSA was diagnosed as per ICSD-3-TR criteria.[27] The severity of OSA was determined by AHI (AHI <5/h = normal (for adults); 5–14.9/h = mild OSA; 15–29.9/h = moderate OSA; and ≥30/h = severe OSA).

Estimation of age of onset of obstructive sleep apnea and number of years with obstructive sleep apnea (estimated duration of obstructive sleep apnea)

Estimated duration of OSA was calculated using the OSA-Onset algorithm described by Olaithe et al.[19] This model provides a retrospective, equation-based estimate of time since predicted progression to moderate–severe OSA (AHI ≥15), rather than true biological onset. The model estimates years since AHI ≥15 using the equation: Estimated years since AHI ≥15= β0 + β1(sex) + β2(BMI category) + β3(AHI), where: Sex was coded as male = 0, female = 1; BMI categories were coded as: 0 (20–24.9 kg/m2), 1 (25–29.9 kg/m2), 2 (30–39.9 kg/m2), 3 (≥40 kg/m2); AHI was entered as a continuous variable derived from diagnostic level-1 polysomnography. The beta coefficients reported by Olaithe et al.[19] were applied without modification. Estimated age of onset was calculated as current age minus estimated years since AHI ≥15. The published Excel worksheet provided by the original authors was used to ensure consistency with the original model specification.

Participants with age <35 years or BMI <20 kg/m2 were excluded, as these categories fall outside the model’s validated parameter range. A small proportion (3.6%) yielded negative estimated onset ages; these values were retained in the primary analysis to preserve model fidelity but were excluded in a predefined sensitivity analysis to evaluate robustness.

Diagnosis of a cardio-metabolic disorder was made when (i) the patient has received the diagnosis in the past and, previous investigations and treatment records were available to confirm the diagnosis or (ii) newly diagnosed during our study, according to the following definitions:

Other investigations

All patients underwent 12-lead ECG and 2D echocardiography. Venous blood samples were collected the morning after overnight polysomnography, following an overnight fast, and analyzed for FBS, HbA1c, lipid profile, and thyroid-stimulating hormone.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Descriptive statistics were calculated as appropriate. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. The primary exposure variable was “estimated duration of OSA,” defined as the algorithm-derived estimated time since predicted progression to moderate–severe OSA (AHI ≥15 events/h). For comparative analyses, estimated OSA duration was stratified into tertiles (lower, middle, and upper). The prevalence of cardiometabolic comorbidities across tertiles was compared using Chi-square tests. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed separately for each cardiometabolic outcome. Covariates were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and included dietary intake, physical activity, genetic predisposition, smoking status, and age where appropriate. Confounding was assessed by comparing unadjusted and adjusted Odds ratios (ORs); a change ≥10% was considered meaningful. The level of statistical significance was set at 0.05. Because seven cardiometabolic outcomes were evaluated across tertiles of estimated OSA duration, Bonferroni correction was applied (m = 7), defining the family of tests as the set of cardiometabolic comorbidities analyzed. This conservative adjustment was chosen to control family-wise error rate despite the biological interrelatedness of outcomes. Given the correlated nature of these endpoints, results are interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding participants with implausible negative estimated onset ages. Primary regression models were re-run in this restricted cohort, and effect estimates were compared quantitatively with the main analysis.

RESULTS

Population characteristics

A total of 367 participants were screened for eligibility, and finally, 279 participants were enrolled in the study, as shown in Figure 1. All included participants had complete data for exposure and outcome variables; therefore, no additional exclusions for missing data were required and analyses were conducted using complete-case data without imputation.

Of all, 49.1% participants were male; 29% had moderate and rest had severe OSA. Based on the World Health Organization (WHO) BMI classification, 8.2% (n = 23) participants had normal weight, 32.6% (n = 91) were overweight, and 59.1% (n = 165) were classified as obese. Baseline demographic, clinical, and polysomnographic characteristics, along with their distribution across estimated OSA duration tertiles, are summarized in Tables 1 and 2. Gender-wise comparisons are shown in Supplementary Table 1. Most commonly reported symptoms were snoring (87.5%), followed by nocturia (50.5%) and excessive daytime sleepiness (47.7%). The prevalence of comorbid psychiatric illness in our study population was relatively low (6.8%).

Distribution of demographic and clinical parameters across genders

Among participants, median systolic BP was 132 mmHg (IQR: 129–140), diastolic BP was 84 mmHg (IQR: 77–90), and heart rate was 80 bpm (IQR: 76–86). Participants had a median neck circumference of 38 cm (IQR: 37–40) and a waist circumference of 102.5 cm (IQR: 98–112). The median FBS was 104 mg/dL (IQR: 90–121), HbA1c was 6.0% (IQR: 5.5–6.7), total cholesterol was 186 mg/dL (IQR: 158–217), triglycerides were 147 mg/dL (IQR: 112–206), LDL cholesterol was 102 mg/dL (IQR: 87–123), and HDL cholesterol was 43 mg/dL (IQR: 36–53).

Cardiometabolic comorbidities

Systemic hypertension was the most prevalent condition, affecting 79.6% (n = 222) of the study participants, followed by dyslipidemia (n = 219, 78.5%) and metabolic syndrome (n = 208, 74.6%). Diabetes mellitus was present in 50.9% of participants (n = 142). Less frequent conditions included CAD (n = 19, 6.8%), cardiac arrhythmia (n = 14, 5%), and heart failure (n = 12, 4.3%).

Duration of obstructive sleep apnea estimated by obstructive sleep apnea-onset algorithm

The overall median estimated duration of OSA was 18.7 years (IQR = 9.41–27.88 years). Estimated duration of OSA (range: 0.19–63.29 years) was stratified into tertiles for comparative analysis. The lower tertile included individuals with estimated duration of OSA <12.86 years (n = 93), the middle tertile comprised those with estimated duration of OSA ranging between 12.86 and 25.08 years (n = 93), and the upper tertile consisted of participants with estimated duration of OSA >25.08 years (n = 93). The overall median OSA onset age was 32.23 years (IQR: 19.9–42.19 years).

Association between cardio-metabolic comorbidities and estimated obstructive sleep apnea duration

Analysis of cardiometabolic comorbidities across tertiles of estimated OSA duration revealed significant trends for hypertension (Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.049), as shown in Figure 2. Although the proportions of participants with metabolic syndrome, heart failure, and CAD increased across tertiles, these differences did not reach statistical significance after Bonferroni correction (adjusted P = 1.000, 1.000, and 0.441, respectively). Similarly, no significant differences were observed for DM, cardiac arrhythmia, or dyslipidemia (all Bonferroni-adjusted P = 1.000).

Bar chart showing prevalence of cardio-metabolic comorbidities across tertiles of estimated duration of OSA. OSA = Obstructive sleep apnea

In the adjusted multivariable model [Table 3], accounting for dietary intake, physical activity, smoking status, genetic risk, and age, estimated OSA duration was statistically associated with hypertension (OR 1.039; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.012–1.068; P = 0.005). This corresponds to an approximately 3%–4% increase in the odds of hypertension per additional year of estimated OSA duration. Although statistically significant, the magnitude of effect was modest. Genetic risk was strongly associated with hypertension in univariate analysis (OR 4.361; 95% CI: 2.029–9.371; P < 0.001); however, its inclusion in the multivariable model did not materially alter the association between estimated OSA duration and hypertension. No statistically significant associations were observed between estimated OSA duration and other cardiometabolic comorbidities after adjustment [Supplementary Tables 2-7].

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first study to examine the association between algorithm-estimated duration of moderate-to-severe OSA and a range of cardiometabolic comorbidities. In this cohort, longer estimated OSA duration was statistically associated with a higher prevalence of systemic hypertension, with each additional year corresponding to a 3.9% increase in the odds of hypertension. However, no significant associations were observed between estimated OSA duration and DM, metabolic syndrome, dyslipidemia, CAD, heart failure, or arrhythmia.

The association with hypertension is biologically plausible and likely reflects the cumulative effects of prolonged intermittent hypoxia and sustained sympathetic activation in long-standing untreated OSA.[35] Recurrent hypoxia, hypercapnia, and sleep fragmentation promote chronic sympathetic overactivity, endothelial dysfunction, vascular remodeling, and reduced arterial compliance; mechanisms central to blood pressure dysregulation.[35] Previous longitudinal studies have demonstrated progressive blood pressure abnormalities in untreated OSA,[363738] although these investigations did not account for total disease duration, as enrolment occurred after diagnosis.[11] Our findings extend this literature by suggesting that disease chronicity, as estimated by the OSA-Onset algorithm, may contribute to hypertension risk.

Equally important are the null findings for other cardiometabolic outcomes. Despite established cross-sectional associations between OSA and disorders such as diabetes, dyslipidemia, and CAD,[5639] estimated OSA duration did not demonstrate similar relationships in this cohort. Several explanations may account for this pattern. First, cardiometabolic conditions such as diabetes and CAD may reflect multifactorial processes influenced by genetic predisposition, lifestyle factors, pharmacotherapy, and environmental exposures, potentially diluting any isolated contribution of estimated OSA duration.[40414243] Second, phenotype clustering may differ across populations; hypertension may represent a more immediately responsive vascular phenotype to sleep-disordered breathing, whereas metabolic and structural cardiac conditions may require longer cumulative exposure or interaction with additional risk modifiers. Third, statistical power may have been limited for lower-prevalence outcomes such as heart failure and arrhythmias, reducing the ability to detect modest associations.

Methodological factors may also have influenced these findings. The cross-sectional design restricts temporal inference, and logistic regression models may be less sensitive to gradual or cumulative effects. Dichotomization of comorbidities into binary variables may have reduced analytical sensitivity by ignoring disease severity and duration. In addition, because BMI is incorporated into the OSA-Onset algorithm, residual confounding and structural collinearity cannot be excluded, particularly for metabolically mediated outcomes.[4445]

Importantly, the absence of associations with several cardiometabolic conditions suggests that estimated OSA duration alone may not uniformly translate into broader cardiometabolic burden. As highlighted in the 2021 American Heart Association scientific statement,[46] OSA represents one component within a complex cardiometabolic network, and its contribution may vary across disease phenotypes. In our cohort, approximately one-fifth of participants did not have hypertension despite long estimated OSA duration, further underscoring the heterogeneity of cardiometabolic expression. Taken together, these findings indicate a selective association with hypertension rather than a generalized increase in cardiometabolic comorbidity across all domains.

A key limitation of this study relates to the OSA-Onset algorithm used to estimate disease duration. A small proportion (3.6%) of participants had implausible negative estimated onset ages, indicating potential imprecision in the model. The algorithm was originally developed and internally validated in the Wisconsin Sleep Cohort, and has not been externally validated in Indian populations.[1947] Differences in body habitus, craniofacial morphology, metabolic phenotype, genetic susceptibility, and healthcare-seeking behavior across ethnic groups may influence disease progression and affect the calibration of the model in South Asian cohorts. Furthermore, the reported prediction error metrics lack clear clinical interpretability.

Because BMI and AHI are embedded within the derivation of the OSA-Onset algorithm, estimated OSA duration represents a composite construct reflecting both severity and modelled progression rather than a purely independent measure of chronicity. Complete statistical separation between the exposure variable and related cardiometabolic risk factors is therefore not achievable. Residual confounding and structural collinearity cannot be fully excluded, and the observed associations should not be interpreted as demonstrating independence or causality.[48] Sensitivity analysis excluding participants with implausible negative onset estimates showed only minimal attenuation of effect size (OR 1.039 vs. 1.036), with overlapping CIs and preserved statistical significance, supporting numerical stability but not eliminating inherent methodological constraints.

Additional limitations warrant consideration. The cross-sectional design precludes confirmation of temporal sequencing between estimated OSA duration and cardiometabolic comorbidities. Both exposure and outcomes were assessed at a single time point, and the algorithm provides a retrospective, model-based estimate of time since predicted progression to moderate–severe OSA (AHI ≥15), rather than a prospectively validated measure of biological onset or cumulative exposure. Accordingly, “estimated OSA duration” represents a proxy construct and should not be interpreted as a precise measure of true chronicity or temporally verified disease duration.

Recruitment from a single tertiary-care sleep laboratory may introduce referral bias and limit generalizability to community-based or milder OSA populations. Patterns of healthcare access may influence the timing of diagnosis and therefore affect model-based onset estimation. In addition, the algorithm’s reliance on WHO BMI classifications may be suboptimal for Asian populations, where lower BMI thresholds are often recommended.[49]

Although an a priori sample size calculation was performed to detect medium effect size differences across tertiles (target n = 324), the final analyzed sample (n = 279) was slightly lower. The calculation was based on overall group comparisons rather than outcome-specific prevalence. While the study was likely adequately powered for common outcomes such as hypertension, power may have been insufficient for lower-prevalence conditions (e.g., CAD, heart failure, arrhythmias). Accordingly, null findings for these outcomes may reflect limited power and potential Type II error rather than true absence of association.

Multivariable models included a limited number of clinically selected covariates to reduce overfitting. For hypertension, the number of outcome events relative to predictors was adequate, suggesting reasonable model stability. However, for lower-prevalence outcomes, fewer events per predictor may have increased model imprecision. In addition, because BMI and AHI are embedded within the OSA-Onset algorithm, structural collinearity cannot be excluded. Formal collinearity diagnostics were not performed and remain a limitation.

These considerations highlight the need for prospective, multicenter longitudinal studies with external validation of onset-estimation models in diverse populations, incorporation of region-specific anthropometric thresholds, and direct assessment of temporal relationships. Future research should also examine treatment effects across varying stages of OSA and explore mechanistic pathways using objective biomarkers.

Despite these limitations, this study provides an exploratory evaluation of algorithm-estimated OSA duration in relation to cardiometabolic comorbidities within a well-characterized tertiary cohort. The relatively large sample of moderate-to-severe OSA patients, balanced gender distribution, use of level-1 polysomnography with manual scoring, and structured assessment of comorbidities enhance internal consistency of the findings.

CONCLUSION

This exploratory cross-sectional study evaluated the association between algorithm-estimated OSA duration and cardiometabolic comorbidities. A higher prevalence of hypertension was observed across tertiles of estimated OSA duration, whereas no statistically significant associations were identified for other cardiometabolic conditions. Given the conservative multiple-testing adjustment, structural limitations of the algorithm-derived exposure variable, and the cross-sectional design, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. The retrospective, model-based estimate of OSA duration does not establish temporal sequencing or cumulative risk. Accordingly, the results are hypothesis-generating and require confirmation in prospective, longitudinal, multicenter studies designed to directly assess temporal relationships and treatment effects.
