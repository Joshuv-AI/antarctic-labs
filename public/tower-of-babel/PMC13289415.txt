Integrin CD11b/CD18 reprograms macrophage polarization by suppressing ERK/STAT3 signaling to enhance antitumor immunity in colitis-associated colorectal cancer
Authors: Ying Lin, Yuan-Kun Liu, Peng Mi, Xian-Ling Zhao, Jun-Hui Lin, Xiao-Shen Cheng, Lai-Ying Liang, Yong-Dong Huang, Ya-Ni Huo, Gui-Jing Xie, Zhen-Yu Ye, Bayasi Guleng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic inflammation is a well-established driver of colorectal cancer (CRC), with the resulting inflammatory microenvironment facilitating tumor initiation and progression. The integrin CD11b/CD18, a leukocyte-specific heterodimeric adhesion receptor, mediates critical immunoregulatory functions during inflammatory responses. However, the roles and mechanisms of CD11b/CD18 in colitis-associated colorectal cancer (CAC) remain unclear.MethodsTo investigate the impact of CD11b/CD18 deficiency on colorectal carcinogenesis, an azoxymethane/dextran sodium sulfate-induced CAC model was established with CD11b and CD18 single-knockout and double-knockout mice. The tumor immune microenvironment was characterized using multicolor flow cytometry. Transcriptomic changes in tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) and macrophages (TAMs) on CD11b/CD18 ablation were profiled by RNA sequencing. Functional crosstalk between TANs and TAMs was assessed via co-culture experiments. The direct role of CD11b/CD18 in TAM polarization and antitumor activity was evaluated using in vitro agonist assays, and the involvement of the extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) axis was validated with pathway-specific inhibitors.ResultsBioinformatics analysis revealed significant downregulation of ITGAM (CD11b) and ITGB2 (CD18) expression in CRC tissues. In the CAC model, CD11b and CD18 exhibited non-redundant and cooperative functions, and double deficiency significantly exacerbated tumor progression, with increased STAT3 phosphorylation and reduced yes-associated protein phosphorylation. Flow cytometric analysis identified neutrophils as the predominant CD11b+CD18+ population within the tumor microenvironment (TME). Mechanistically, CD11b/CD18 deficiency promoted TME remodeling, including protumor skewing of TANs and TAMs, with TAM polarization mediated partly by TAN-TAM crosstalk. Beyond this indirect mechanism, direct activation of CD11b/CD18 in TAMs attenuated the immunosuppressive properties and enhanced their tumoricidal activity. At the molecular level, CD11b/CD18 deficiency activated janus kinase (JAK)-STAT and mitogen-activated protein kinase pathways in TAMs, whereas CD11b/CD18 activation effectively suppressed ERK1/2 and STAT3 signaling. Combined inhibition of ERK1/2 and STAT3 reversed M2 polarization and restored TAM-mediated tumor killing.ConclusionsOur findings establish that integrin CD11b/CD18 orchestrates antitumor immunity by modulating the TME, particularly through direct TAM reprogramming and indirect TAN-TAM crosstalk, highlighting its potential as an immunotherapeutic target for CAC.Keywords: Tumor Microenvironment, Colorectal Cancer, Immunotherapy, Macrophage, Neutrophil

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most prevalent malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide.1 Chronic inflammation is a well-established tumorigenic driver, with patients with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) having increased risk of developing colitis-associated colorectal cancer (CAC), the major mortality cause in this population.2 3 Although accumulating evidence indicates that CAC may be driven by an inflammatory microenvironment and profoundly influenced by gut microbiota dysbiosis and immune dysfunction,4,6 the precise molecular mechanisms remain to be fully elucidated.

Chronic inflammation drives tissue microenvironment remodeling, influencing all stages of colorectal tumorigenesis.7 The tumor microenvironment (TME), a complex ecosystem maintaining tumor survival and progression, comprises tumor, immune, stromal, and extracellular components.8 Within the TME, immune cells play critical roles in tumor behavior and therapeutic responses. Immunotherapies in CRC have demonstrated significant improvements in tumor control and survival outcomes.9 10 Nevertheless, a considerable proportion of patients remain refractory to current immunotherapies due to TME heterogeneity and immune subset diversity.11 Multiple studies have shown that tumor-infiltrating immunosuppressive myeloid cells are closely related to immunotherapy resistance.12 13 Therefore, targeting myeloid cell regulation emerges as a viable strategy to enhance therapeutic efficacy and benefit more patients with CRC.

Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) are highly plastic immune cells in the CRC microenvironment. Based on activation states, TAMs are traditionally divided into classically activated (M1) and alternatively activated (M2) subtypes.14 Furthermore, circulating monocytes infiltrate tissues and differentiate into Ly6Chi (pro-inflammatory), Ly6Cint (transitional/intermediate), and Ly6Clo (anti-inflammatory/reparative) populations in response to microenvironmental signals.15 16 M1-TAMs exert direct tumoricidal effects and enhance T cell-mediated tumor killing via inflammatory mediators.17 Conversely, M2-TAMs facilitate tumor progression by promoting angiogenesis, extracellular matrix remodeling and immunosuppression via production of factors such as transforming growth factor (TGF)-β, interleukin (IL)-10, vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), while also suppressing antitumor immunity through induction of T-cell exhaustion.18 19 However, the TME predominantly drives TAM polarization toward the tumor-promoting M2 phenotype.20 Despite the controversial role of TAMs in CRC due to their phenotypic plasticity,21 22 reprogramming TAMs toward an antitumor phenotype represents a promising therapeutic strategy for CRC.

Tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) constitute an important component of the TME. In CRC, high TAN infiltration correlates with poor prognosis.23 However, TANs display functional plasticity, exhibiting both tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressing capacities.24 25 Analogous to TAMs, TANs can be classified into antitumor (N1) and protumor (N2) phenotypes. The TME typically drives N2 polarization via TGF-β and extends TAN longevity through pro-survival factors.26 27 Intriguingly, studies demonstrate bidirectional neutrophil-macrophage crosstalk: Neutrophil-derived mediators (eg, NETs, CSF1) drive macrophage recruitment and Ly6Clo/M2 polarization28 29; while macrophages orchestrate resolution of inflammation via neutrophil efferocytosis.30

Integrin CD11b/CD18 (αMβ2, Mac-1, CR3), a key member of the β2 integrin family, is a heterodimer of non-covalently linked CD11b (αM) and CD18 (β2) subunits.31 As a critical adhesion receptor mediating both cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions, CD11b/CD18 is predominantly expressed on myeloid cells and natural killer (NK) cells, with minimal expression in lymphocyte subsets.32 33 It orchestrates diverse immune cell functions and bidirectionally regulates inflammatory responses in a context-dependent manner.34,36 CD11b/CD18 critically regulates neutrophil-intestinal epithelium interactions, with its dysregulation contributing to chronic mucosal inflammatory disorders.37 While CD11b/CD18 blockade attenuates inflammation in several animal models, current antagonists show limited clinical efficacy and notable toxicity.38,40 Conversely, small-molecule agonists that activate CD11b/CD18 can reduce leukocyte migration and tissue damage, suggesting integrin activation as a potential anti-inflammatory strategy.41 42 Nevertheless, the precise mechanistic roles of CD11b/CD18 in CAC remain largely elusive.

In this study, bioinformatics analysis revealed significant downregulation of CD11b and CD18 in CRC tissues. In vivo studies demonstrated that genetic ablation of CD11b/CD18 exacerbated CAC progression, suggesting that CD11b/CD18 exerts tumor-suppressive functions. Mechanistically, CD11b/CD18 deficiency reshaped the CAC immune landscape, driving TANs and TAMs toward a protumor phenotype, with TANs involved in TAM reprogramming. Furthermore, pharmacological activation of CD11b/CD18 in vitro attenuated the immunosuppressive activity of TAMs and enhanced their tumoricidal capacity, mediated through suppression of extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK)/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) signaling. Taken together, these findings indicate the therapeutic potential of targeting integrin CD11b/CD18 to augment antitumor immunity, providing a plausible immunotherapeutic strategy for CAC.

Materials and methods

Mice

CD11b−/− (B6.129S4-Itgamtm1Myd/J, 003991, RRID: IMSR_JAX:003991) and CD18−/− (B6.129S7-Itgb2tm1Bay/J, 002128, RRID: IMSR_JAX:002128) mice on the C57BL/6J background were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory, and crossed to obtain CD11b/CD18 double-knockout (CD11b/CD18-DKO, named DKO) homozygous mice. All mice were bred and maintained in a controlled animal facility under specific pathogen-free conditions, with a 12-hour light/dark cycle and free access to food and water. For the experiments, age-matched and sex-matched mice were used to ensure consistency, and mice were acclimatized for 1 week to adapt to the new environment prior to pharmacological treatment.

Murine colitis-associated colorectal cancer model

To induce CAC, 6–8 weeks old male mice were administered a single intraperitoneal injection of the azoxymethane (AOM) (12.5 mg/kg body weight; Sigma-Aldrich, USA). 1 week later, 3% dextran sodium sulfate (DSS) (MW 36–50 kDa; MP Biomedicals, USA) dissolved in drinking water was given for five consecutive days, followed by normal water for 14 days. This DSS-administration cycle (5 days of 3% DSS and 14 days interval between each cycle) was carried out three times in total. The body weight, stool consistency and rectal bleeding during CAC induction were monitored dynamically. At the end of the model, mice were subjected to a colonoscopy using the animal endoscopy system (Wolf GmbH, Germany). The colons were harvested to assess tumor burden and were photographed. Portions of the distal colons were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and paraffin-embedded for histopathological analysis, and the rest were used for RNA and protein analysis.

H&E staining

After euthanasia, 3 cm segments of colon tissue, located near the rectum, were collected from the experimental mice. The colonic segments were then rolled from the distal to the proximal end into a “Swiss roll” shape, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, China) for 24 hours, and subsequently embedded in paraffin and sectioned at 4 µm thickness.

For the H&E staining, colonic sections were deparaffinized in xylene, followed by rehydration through a descending ethanol series. Staining was performed with hematoxylin (BOSTER, China) for 2.5 min and eosin (BOSTER, China) for 30 s. After staining, sections were then dehydrated in graded alcohols, cleared with xylene, and mounted for analysis. The stained sections were scanned under a panoramic digital slide scanner (Leica Aperio Versa 200; Leica Biosystems, USA) at 20×magnification and analyzed using corresponding viewer software (Aperio Image Scope; Leica Biosystems, USA).

Immunohistochemistry staining

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining of paraffin-embedded mice colonic sections was performed in accordance with standard procedures. Following deparaffinization and rehydration, the tissue sections were submerged into citrate buffer and microwaved for antigenic retrieval. After cooling, sections were treated with 3% H2O2 and blocked with goat serum. The tissue sections were then incubated with primary antibody (Ki-67 (D3B5) rabbit mAb; Cell Signaling Technology, USA, Cat# 12202, RRID: AB_2620142) overnight at 4°C, followed by secondary antibody (SignalStain Boost IHC Detection Reagents (HRP, Rabbit); Cell Signaling Technology, USA, Cat# 8114, RRID: AB_10544930) incubation for 30 min at room temperature, and detection with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate (Cell Signaling Technology, USA, Cat# 8059) for 1.5 min. Counterstaining was carried out with hematoxylin (BOSTER, China) for 2 min. Images were acquired using Leica Aperio Versa 200 and analyzed by Aperio Image Scope Software. Ki-67 positivity was assessed in five random 20×fields per section, and the mean percentage of Ki-67-positive (Ki-67+) cells per section was used for statistical analysis. All IHC-stained sections were independently scored by two researchers blinded to the group allocation.

TUNEL staining

To evaluate in situ cellular apoptosis, terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick-end labeling (TUNEL) assay was performed using a commercial fluorescein-based detection kit (Servicebio, China, Cat# G1501) following the manufacturer’s instructions. TUNEL positivity was assessed by counting the number of TUNEL-positive (TUNEL+) cells in five random 20×fields per section, and the mean value per section was used for statistical analysis. All TUNEL-stained sections were independently scored by two researchers blinded to the group allocation.

Reverse transcription quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from tissues or cells using TRIzol reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, Cat# 15596018CN). Subsequently, complementary DNA was generated with a reverse transcription reagent kit (Vazyme, China, Cat# R323-01). The expressions of messenger RNA (mRNA) were analyzed using Hieff qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Yeasen, China, Cat# 11 201ES08) with a CFX384 TOUCH Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad, USA, RRID: SCR_018057). The primer sequences used were detailed in online supplemental table 1, and GAPDH was used for normalization. The relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blotting

Total protein from the samples was extracted with radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) lysis buffer (Solarbio, China, Cat# R0010) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The protein concentration was measured with a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China, Cat# P0012), and equal amounts of protein were separated on sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) gels followed by transfer to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were subsequently blocked with 5% skim milk or bovine serum albumin (BSA), and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Following incubation with the appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature, the protein bands were visualized using the ECL Chemiluminescence Detection Kit (Advansta, USA, Cat# K-12045-D50) and quantified with ImageJ software (RRID: SCR_003070). All antibodies used for western blotting are provided in online supplemental table 2.

Isolation of colonic lamina propria and tumor-infiltrating cells

The colon tissues were isolated from the CAC model and control mice, followed by dissection of tumor, para-cancerous tissue, and normal colon tissue. The colon tissues were cut into several pieces and collected in pre-digestive buffer (D-Hanks containing 5 mM EDTA (Solarbio, China, Cat# E1170) and 1 mM DTT (Solarbio, China, Cat# D8220)). The epithelial cells of the colons were removed by shaking for 15 min at 37°C and 200 rpm. Then colon tissues were further cut into very small pieces and digested in prewarmed digestion buffer (Roswell Park Memorial Institute (RPMI) 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1 mg/mL collagenase IV (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, Cat# 9001–12-1) and 0.15 mg/mL DNase I (Roche, Switzerland, Cat# 10104159001)) at 37°C for 90 min. Tumor tissues were chopped directly and incubated with the digestion buffer under the same conditions. Digested tissues were triturated and filtered through 100 µm and 70 µm cell strainers (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) sequentially to obtain single-cell suspensions, followed by immune cell enrichment on a 40%–80% Percoll (Cytiva, USA, Cat# 17089109) gradient.

Flow cytometry

For surface staining, cells were first incubated with Fixable Viability Stain solution for 15 min at room temperature, and incubated with an Fc receptor blocking antibody in fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) buffer (0.25% BSA in 1×phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)) at 4°C for 20 min. Subsequently, an appropriate combination of surface antigen antibody cocktail diluted in FACS buffer was added and incubated at 4°C in the dark for 30 min.

For intracellular staining, the cells were incubated with a lymphocyte activation cocktail (RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS, Brefeldin A (BD Biosciences, USA, Cat# 555029), 50 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, Cat# 16561–29-8) and 1 µg/mL ionomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, Cat# 56092–82-1)) for 5 hours in a humidified incubator (37°C, 5% CO₂). After stimulation, cells were stained for surface antibodies as described above, and then fixed and permeabilized using the Fixation and Permeabilization Solution (BD Biosciences, USA, Cat# 554722) for 30 min according to the manufacturer’s instructions, followed by incubation with corresponding fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies in the 1×Perm/Wash Buffer (BD Biosciences, USA, Cat# 554723) for 1 hour at 4°C.

For transcription factor staining, cells were fixed and permeabilized with the Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer (eBioscience, USA, Cat# 00–5523-00) according to the manufacturer’s instructions for 30 min, and then corresponding fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies were applied for 1 hour at 4°C.

An analytical flow cytometer BD LSRFortessa X-20 (BD Biosciences, USA, RRID: SCR_025285) was applied to detect the immune cell composition, and data analysis was performed using FlowJo software (RRID: SCR_008520). All antibodies used for flow cytometry are listed in online supplemental table 3.

RNA-sequencing analysis

TANs and TAMs from the tumor tissues of CAC mice were sorted by BD FACSAria III Flow Cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA). The sorted cells were then lysed with TRIzol reagent and submitted to BGI Genomics (Wuhan, China) for RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis. The raw transcriptomic sequencing reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome GRCm38 (mm10) with Bowtie2 (V.2.4.5), and gene expression quantification was subsequently conducted using RSEM software (V.1.3.1). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (V.1.34.0), with statistically significant thresholds set at q value ≤0.05 or false discovery rate ≤0.001. Functional enrichment analysis was conducted using a hypergeometric test to identify significantly enriched Gene Ontology terms and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways among differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Isolation and induction of murine bone marrow-derived macrophages

Male C57BL/6J mice aged 6–8 weeks were euthanized via cervical dislocation and disinfected by immersion in 75% ethanol. The skin and muscle tissues of the hind limbs were carefully removed to isolate the femurs and tibias. Bone marrow cells were flushed out from the bone cavities using RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco, USA). After erythrocyte lysis, the bone marrow cells were resuspended in bone marrow-derived macrophage (BMDM) induction medium (RPMI-1640 containing 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin and 20 ng/mL recombinant murine macrophage colony-stimulating factor (PeproTech, USA, Cat# 315–02)) and cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂. The BMDM induction medium was partially replaced (50%) on day 3 and completely refreshed on day 5. Typically, mature BMDMs were obtained after a 7-day culture period.

Cell culture and treatment

The mouse colon adenocarcinoma cell line MC38 (RRID: CVCL_B288) was cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and maintained in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37°C.

To prepare the conditioned medium (CM), MC38 cells at 80–90% confluency were washed three times with PBS and incubated in serum-free RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 1% penicillin-streptomycin for 24 hours. The culture supernatant was then collected and centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C. Following centrifugation, the supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 µm filter and supplemented with 1% FBS, which was defined as MC38-CM.

To investigate the effects of CD11b/CD18 activation on TAM polarization, BMDMs were treated with MC38-CM to mimic the formation of TAMs. Before inducing TAM-like polarization, BMDMs were pretreated with 10 μM GB1275 (Selleck Chemicals, USA, Cat# S0525), 7.5 µM leukadherin-1 (LA1, Selleck Chemicals, USA, Cat# S8306), or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (vector control) for 1 hour, followed by an 8-hour treatment with MC38-CM in the presence or absence of GB1275, LA1, or DMSO. To assess whether the ERK/STAT3 axis mediates these effects, separate groups were additionally pretreated for 2 hours with 1 µM SCH772984 (ERK1/2 inhibitor; Selleck Chemicals, USA, Cat# S7101) and/or 5 μM C188-9 (STAT3 inhibitor; Selleck Chemicals, USA, Cat# S8605) prior to agonist and CM stimulation, with inhibitors maintained throughout the subsequent treatments. All BMDMs were then harvested for reverse transcription quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR), flow cytometry, and western blotting; inhibitor efficacy was validated by western blot analysis of phosphorylated ERK1/2 and STAT3.

Preparation of conditioned media from BMDMs: the BMDMs were subjected to various treatments as described above. Subsequently, BMDMs were washed three times with PBS and then cultured in complete RPMI-1640 medium for an additional 24 hours to obtain different BMDM-CM.

Isolation of murine bone marrow neutrophils and co-culture with BMDMs

Bone marrow cells were isolated from the femurs and tibias of wild-type (WT) and DKO mice. Neutrophils were enriched first using a mouse bone marrow neutrophil isolation kit (Solarbio, China, Cat# P8550) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and then further purified using a BeaverBeads mouse neutrophil isolation kit (BeaverBio, China, Cat# 70907) following the manufacturer’s protocol. For apoptosis analysis, neutrophils were treated with RPMI-1640 medium or MC38-CM for 16 hours. For co-culture experiments, neutrophils were directly co-cultured with WT BMDMs in RPMI-1640 medium or MC38-CM. After 12 hours of co-culture, BMDMs were collected to evaluate macrophage polarization markers.

Annexin V/propidium iodide apoptosis assay

Apoptosis of cells was assessed using the Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit (Signalway Antibody, USA, Cat# CA011). After treatment, the supernatant was collected, and cells were detached with EDTA-free trypsin, centrifuged at 500×g for 5 min at 4°C, and washed two times with ice-cold PBS. Cells were resuspended in 1×Annexin V binding buffer at a concentration of 1×10⁶ cells/mL, and sequentially stained with Annexin V for 10 min on ice in the dark followed by propidium iodide (PI) for 5 min. After adding binding buffer, samples were filtered and analyzed immediately by flow cytometry (BD LSRFortessa X-20, RRID: SCR_025285). Data analysis was performed using FlowJo software.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

The concentrations of IL-10 and tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α in BMDM culture supernatants were measured using commercial ELISA kits specific for mouse IL-10 (Dakewe Biotech, China, Cat# 1211002) and mouse TNF-α (Solarbio, China, Cat# SEKM-0034), following the manufacturers’ protocols.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted by GraphPad Prism V.9 software. Data were expressed as mean±SD or SEM. Comparisons between two groups were performed using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t-test, while one-way or two-way analysis of variance was employed for comparisons involving multiple groups. Significant differences were represented as *p<0.05, **p<0.01, and ***p<0.001, with “NS” denoting no significant difference.

Results

Deficiency of integrin CD11b/CD18 exacerbates colitis-associated colorectal carcinogenesis

Integrin CD11b/CD18, a heterodimeric transmembrane protein formed by non-covalent association of CD11b and CD18 subunits, plays crucial roles in both innate and adaptive immune responses. To investigate the functional role and mechanistic basis of CD11b/CD18 in CRC development, we first analyzed RNA-seq data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) cohort obtained from the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) via the UCSC Xena platform, revealing significant downregulation of both ITGAM (encoding CD11b) and ITGB2 (encoding CD18) genes in CRC tissues (figure 1A). To further determine whether decreased CD11b and CD18 expression correlates with colorectal tumorigenesis, we used CD11b−/− and CD18−/− mice obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (online supplemental figure S1A). Intriguingly, flow cytometric analysis revealed the presence of CD18+ cell populations in CD11b−/− mouse peripheral blood, while CD11b+ cells were detectable in CD18−/− mice (online supplemental figure S1B), suggesting potential independent expression of these subunits.

In investigating the pathogenesis of CAC, an important question emerged regarding whether CD11b and CD18 subunits function independently or require cooperative interaction to execute their biological functions. To address this question, we generated CD11b/CD18 double-knockout (CD11b/CD18-DKO, DKO) mice by crossing single-knockout strains (online supplemental figure S1C). We subsequently established the AOM/DSS-induced CAC model in CD11b−/−, CD18−/−, CD11b/CD18-DKO mice and WT controls (figure 1B), monitoring disease progression through longitudinal measurements of body weight, stool consistency, and hematochezia. Comparative analysis revealed that while CD11b−/− and CD18−/− mice showed non-significant weight loss trends compared with WT controls in late disease stages, CD11b/CD18-DKO mice exhibited significantly accelerated weight reduction beginning from the second DSS cycle (figure 1C). The DKO group also demonstrated substantially elevated Disease Activity Index scores relative to WT, CD11b−/−, and CD18−/− mice (figure 1D), though no statistically significant differences in survival rates were observed among groups (online supplemental figure S1D).

At CAC study termination, endoscopic evaluation was conducted to assess colonic tumor formation across experimental groups (figure 1E). Quantitative analysis confirmed CD11b−/− mice developed significantly more tumors than WT controls, with CD11b/CD18-DKO mice showing further increased tumor incidence and larger tumor volumes (figure 1F–H). Moreover, AOM/DSS-treated DKO mice displayed significant splenomegaly, with elevated spleen weight and spleen index, indicating CD11b/CD18 deficiency exacerbates systemic inflammation (figure 1I–K). These findings collectively suggest that integrin CD11b/CD18 serves as an important protective factor against colitis-associated carcinogenesis.

CD11b/CD18 deficiency promotes malignant progression of colorectal tumors and upregulates pro-inflammatory cytokines

To explore the mechanistic basis by which CD11b/CD18 deficiency promotes CAC development, we performed a comprehensive histopathological evaluation of distal colorectal tissues in all study groups. H&E staining showed that CD11b−/−, CD18−/− and DKO mice developed colorectal tumors with larger dimensions, greater vascular density, and more pronounced histological dedifferentiation relative to WT controls. Notably, tumor cells in both CD11b−/− and DKO groups exhibited basement membrane penetration with submucosal invasion (figure 2A and online supplemental figure S2A). Subsequent immunohistochemical quantification of Ki-67 expression in serial colorectal sections demonstrated significantly increased proliferation indices in tumors of CD11b−/− and DKO mice, indicating enhanced tumor cell proliferative activity (figure 2B,D and online supplemental figure S2B). Parallel TUNEL staining for apoptosis assessment revealed a non-significant trend of reduced apoptotic cell numbers in tumors from all knockout groups compared with those in WT mice (figure 2C,E and online supplemental figure S2C).

Given the expression of CD11b/CD18 in immune cells and the critical role of immune cell-derived cytokines in colorectal carcinogenesis and progression, we further profiled cytokine expression via RT-qPCR to investigate the underlying molecular mechanisms. Quantitative analysis revealed that, following AOM/DSS treatment, CD11b−/−, CD18−/− and DKO mice exhibited upregulated mRNA levels of multiple pro-inflammatory/protumorigenic cytokines in colonic tissues, compared with WT controls. Specifically, Tgfb1 mRNA levels were uniformly elevated across all knockout genotypes (figure 2F), while Il17 expression was selectively increased in CD11b−/− and DKO groups (figure 2G). The DKO mice uniquely showed significant upregulation of Il6, Il1b, Tnfa, and Ifng mRNA compared with WT and single-knockout counterparts (figure 2H–K). These data suggest that CD11b/CD18 deficiency may promote local inflammation by amplifying pro-inflammatory cytokine production, thereby fostering a tumor-permissive microenvironment.

Loss of CD11b/CD18 elevates STAT3 phosphorylation and reduces YAP phosphorylation in CAC tumors

Chronic inflammation drives colorectal tumor initiation and progression through dysregulation of critical signaling pathways. The IL-6/signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) pathway, whose aberrant activation has been well-established to enhance tumor cell proliferation, survival, invasion and immune evasion, plays a pivotal role in inflammation-to-cancer transition.43 Yes-associated protein (YAP), whose activity is suppressed by the tumor-suppressive Hippo pathway, contributes to tumorigenesis by regulating cell proliferation and differentiation.44 Recent studies have identified complex signaling crosstalk between YAP and STAT3, wherein these molecules cooperatively drive tumor angiogenesis, proliferation and metastasis via a mutually reinforcing positive feedback loop.45 46

Our RT-qPCR data showing significant alterations in IL-6 and related cytokines prompted us to hypothesize that CD11b/CD18 might suppress tumor progression by regulating the IL-6/STAT3 and Hippo signaling pathways. Accordingly, we initially assessed total protein expression and phosphorylation status of STAT3 and YAP in mouse colonic lysates by western blotting (online supplemental figure S2D–J). Quantitative analysis indicated comparable total STAT3 and YAP protein levels across all groups (online supplemental figure S2F,J). However, following AOM/DSS induction, CD11b−/− and CD11b/CD18-DKO mice exhibited significantly enhanced phosphorylation of STAT3 at Tyr705 (online supplemental figure S2E), concomitant with markedly reduced phosphorylation of YAP at Ser127 and Ser397 residues (online supplemental figure S2H,I).

To determine whether these signaling alterations originated from the tumor compartment, we next examined STAT3 and YAP phosphorylation in colorectal tumor tissues isolated from CAC mice of each genotype (figure 2L–N). Consistent with the findings in whole colonic lysates, CD11b−/− and DKO tumors showed significantly increased p-STAT3Tyr705 levels (figure 2L), whereas p-YAPSer127 and p-YAPSer397 levels (figure 2M,N) were significantly decreased compared with WT controls. These results suggest that CD11b/CD18 deficiency promotes STAT3 activation and reduces YAP phosphorylation within the tumor compartment, raising the possibility that this signaling imbalance may contribute to CAC development.

Taken together, these data suggest that the CD11b subunit may modulate CAC progression through coordinated regulation of STAT3 and Hippo signaling pathways. The observation that CD11b deficiency alone partially recapitulates the protumorigenic phenotype of CD11b/CD18 DKO implies that CD11b plays a critical role in CAC suppression, while the full tumor-suppressive function likely requires synergistic cooperation between both subunits.

Neutrophils comprise the predominant population of CD11b+CD18+ immune cells in CAC tumors

Our findings demonstrate that CD11b/CD18 DKO significantly exacerbates CAC development and progression. The integrin CD11b/CD18 is predominantly expressed on myeloid cells, including neutrophils, monocytes, macrophages, and dendritic cells, with detectable expression also present on NK cells and specific subsets of B and T lymphocytes.32 33 This multifunctional adhesion molecule regulates both innate and adaptive immune responses through mediating cellular adhesion, migration, activation, and intercellular interactions.34

Given the critical role of integrins in immune cell recruitment and activation, we propose that CD11b/CD18 deficiency could significantly alter the composition of CD11b+CD18+ immune cell populations in the CAC microenvironment, thereby disrupting CD11b/CD18-dependent cellular crosstalk and subsequently reshaping the tumor immune landscape, which may ultimately drive CAC progression. To confirm this hypothesis, we performed flow cytometric analysis of CD11b+CD18+ immune cell infiltration patterns in colorectal tumor tissues, para-cancerous tissues and normal colon tissues from WT mice (figure 3A and online supplemental figure S3A,B).

Comparative analysis revealed significant alterations in CD11b+CD18+ immune cell composition within TMEs. Neutrophil infiltration was substantially increased in tumor tissues (figure 3B), accompanied by elevated monocyte proportions in both tumor and para-cancerous tissues (figure 3C). Conversely, antigen-presenting cells (macrophages and dendritic cells) were significantly reduced in tumor tissues (figure 3D,E). Among the relatively scarce CD11b+CD18+ lymphocyte population, both B and T cells exhibited decreased tumor infiltration, while NK cell frequencies remained comparable across groups (figure 3F,G). Notably, neutrophils emerged as the predominant CD11b+CD18+ immune subset in tumor infiltrates (figure 3H).

CD11b/CD18 deficiency skews tumor-associated neutrophils and macrophages toward a protumor phenotype in the CAC microenvironment

Studies have demonstrated that TANs exhibit high plasticity and can bidirectionally modulate both tumor-promoting and tumor-suppressing effects.24 25 To further elucidate the role of CD11b/CD18 in regulating TAN heterogeneity and their crosstalk with other immune cells, we isolated TANs from tumor tissues of WT, CD11b−/−, CD18−/− and DKO mice using FACS for RNA-seq (figure 3I). By analyzing the overlapping DEGs from three comparisons—CD11b−/− TANs versus WT TANs, CD18−/− TANs versus WT TANs, and DKO TANs versus WT TANs (online supplemental figure S4A)—we identified commonly upregulated DEGs in the knockout TANs. A threshold of log₂ fold change ≥1 and q value<0.05 was applied, followed by functional annotation-based screening of significant DEGs.

Hierarchical clustering heatmap analysis revealed that, compared with WT TANs, all three knockout TAN groups exhibited significant upregulation of genes associated with N2 polarization and immunosuppression (eg, Ccl5, Isg15, Socs1, Cxcr4, Mif), pro-angiogenesis (Flt1), and anti-apoptosis (Bcl2a1a) (figure 3J). These genes are implicated in key biological processes, including positive regulation of cell migration, chemotaxis (particularly monocyte chemotaxis), and angiogenesis (figure 3K). Furthermore, protein–protein interaction network analysis identified Ccl5 as the hub gene with the highest node degree (online supplemental figure S4B).

Elevated C-C motif chemokine ligand 5 (CCL5) expression has been shown to significantly correlate with the immunosuppressive N2 polarization of TANs.47 As a classical chemokine, CCL5 mediates monocyte chemotaxis and promotes their differentiation into M2-polarized macrophages, thereby facilitating tumor progression.48 49 Our data demonstrate that CD11b/CD18 deficiency markedly modifies the immune-related gene expression profile of TANs, including significant upregulation of Ccl5 (online supplemental figure S4C). Considering the documented immunomodulatory functions of CCL5, we propose that CD11b/CD18 deficiency may disrupt tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) homeostasis by modulating TAN phenotypic states and their cellular crosstalk. We therefore performed comprehensive immune profiling using multicolor flow cytometry in the CAC model, comparing knockout and WT mice (online supplemental figure S4D).

Flow cytometric evaluation revealed comparable overall infiltration levels of neutrophils, monocytes, and macrophages in colorectal tumor tissues across all genotypes (online supplemental figure S5A–C), indicating CD11b/CD18 deficiency may not intrinsically impair myeloid cell trafficking to tumor sites. Strikingly, detailed immunophenotyping uncovered profound alterations in TAM polarization within TMEs of CD18−/− and DKO mice. Specifically, we observed markedly reduced infiltration of pro-inflammatory Ly6Chi macrophages alongside significantly increased accumulation of immunosuppressive Ly6Clo macrophages and CD206+ M2-polarized macrophages (figure 4A,B). However, no significant differences were observed in CD86+ M1-polarized macrophage proportions among the groups (online supplemental figure S5D). Furthermore, all knockout strains exhibited significantly decreased dendritic cell infiltration in colorectal tumors relative to WT controls (online supplemental figure S5E).

Loss of CD11b/CD18 alters neutrophil-macrophage crosstalk and promotes immunosuppressive macrophage polarization in CAC

To further investigate the impact of CD11b/CD18 deficiency on the transcriptional profiles of TAMs, we sorted TAMs from colorectal tumor tissues of CAC mice across genotypes and performed RNA-seq analysis (online supplemental figure S6A). By comparing the gene expression of CD11b−/−, CD18−/−, and DKO TAMs with WT TAMs, we identified commonly upregulated DEGs (log₂ fold change ≥1, q value<0.05) (online supplemental figure S6B). These genes were primarily enriched in chemokine-mediated signaling pathways and cell chemotaxis (online supplemental figure S6C). Among these, the major chemotaxis-related genes were Ccr3 and Ccr8, both significantly upregulated in DKO TAMs (online supplemental figure S6D–F), with Ccr3 encoding an important receptor for CCL5.

The upregulation of Ccr3 and Ccr8 in TAMs suggests their potential to respond to exogenous chemotactic signals. This observation, together with the finding that DEGs in CD11b/CD18-deficient TANs are enriched in monocyte chemotaxis, further supports the possibility that TANs may influence macrophage polarization via chemokine signaling. To explore whether CD11b/CD18-deficient TANs directly regulate macrophage polarization, and considering that the CD11b/CD18 DKO most markedly enhanced Ccl5 expression in TANs and promoted macrophage polarization toward an immunosuppressive state, we focused on analyzing DEGs between DKO and WT TANs. The results showed that DKO TANs exhibited significant upregulation of a series of tumor-promoting genes (figure 4C), including those involved in anti-apoptosis (eg, Bcl2a1a, Bcl2a1b, Bcl2a1c, Bcl2a1d), inflammatory regulation (eg, Isg15, Socs1, Mif), chemotaxis/immunosuppression (eg, Cxcr4, Ccl1, Ccl4, Ccl5, Ccl6, Ccl17, Ccl22), and angiogenesis (eg, Flt1, Vegfa). Moreover, these DEGs were also significantly enriched in monocyte chemotaxis (online supplemental figure S5F). Collectively, we hypothesized that CD11b/CD18 deficiency enhances TAN survival and protumor polarization, which in turn promotes immunosuppressive macrophage polarization.

To validate this hypothesis, we isolated bone marrow neutrophils (BMNs) from WT and DKO mice. A subset of these BMNs was treated with MC38 cell-CM to generate TAN-like cells (hereafter TANs) (figure 4D,E). Compared with WT controls, DKO BMNs showed a significantly lower apoptosis rate under both control conditions and MC38-CM treatment. On MC38-CM treatment, DKO TANs exhibited an even lower total apoptosis rate than untreated DKO BMNs (figure 4F–I). In separate experiments, WT or DKO BMNs were co-cultured with WT BMDMs, either in the absence or presence of MC38-CM (figure 4D,J). Under MC38-CM treatment, CD206 expression was comparable among all BMDM groups (online supplemental figure S6G). However, co-culture with DKO TANs significantly upregulated IL-10 expression and downregulated CD86 expression in BMDMs (figure 4K,L), indicating that DKO TANs can drive macrophages toward an immunosuppressive phenotype.

CD11b/CD18 deficiency upregulates PD-L1 expression on specific myeloid subsets and compromises T-cell infiltration and function

Programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), a critical immune checkpoint molecule, potently suppresses T-cell activation, proliferation, and effector functions through its interaction with the programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) receptor, thereby promoting tumor immune evasion.50 PD-L1 is not only highly expressed on the surface of tumor cells but also prominently present in tumor-infiltrating myeloid cells. Importantly, experimental evidence demonstrates that PD-L1 expressed on myeloid cells, rather than on tumor cells, is essential for the efficacy of immune checkpoint blockade.51

To further elucidate the mechanistic role of CD11b/CD18 in modulating the TIME, we quantitatively assessed PD-L1 expression across myeloid cell subsets in different genotypes (online supplemental figure S7A). Comparative analysis revealed significant upregulation of PD-L1 on tumor-infiltrating neutrophils, macrophages, and monocytes in CD18−/− and DKO mice relative to WT controls (figure 5A–C), whereas dendritic cells maintained comparable PD-L1 levels across groups (figure 5D). These data indicate that CD11b/CD18 deficiency upregulates PD-L1 on myeloid cells (excluding dendritic cells), thereby potentially suppressing antitumor immunity in the TIME.

Comprehensive immunophenotyping further demonstrated markedly reduced T-cell infiltration in tumors of all knockout models compared with WT controls (figure 5E and online supplemental figure S7B). This reduction was particularly pronounced for CD4+ T cells, with a consistent tendency observed in para-cancerous tissues, peripheral blood, and spleen (online supplemental figure S8A–D). Nevertheless, the proportions of CD4+ T-cell subsets in tumor tissues did not differ significantly across all genotypes (online supplemental figure S9A–F). Although neither the relative abundance nor PD-1 expression of CD8+ T cells showed significant differences among the groups (online supplemental figure S9G), tumor-infiltrating CD8+ T cells from CD18−/− and DKO mice displayed significantly diminished interferon (IFN)-γ production, suggesting functional impairment of cytotoxic effector responses (figure 5F).

These experimental findings suggest that genetic ablation of CD11b/CD18 remodels the CAC immune microenvironment via multifaceted mechanisms. In CD11b/CD18-mediated immunoregulation, the CD18 subunit primarily executes effector functions, while the CD11b subunit plays an auxiliary regulatory role. Furthermore, we identified that CD11b modulates both STAT3 and Hippo signaling pathways in CAC development, establishing functional divergence between the two subunits. Notably, single-subunit deficiency fails to fully recapitulate the protumorigenic phenotype induced by double deficiency, indicating that the complete biological function of integrin CD11b/CD18 requires cooperative interaction between both subunits.

CD11b/CD18 agonist attenuates immunosuppressive properties of TAMs and enhances their antitumor activity

Our results have demonstrated that CD11b/CD18 deficiency promotes immunosuppressive macrophage polarization in the CAC TME, partially by altering the crosstalk between TANs and macrophages. However, whether CD11b/CD18 directly regulates macrophage polarization and function remains unclear. Given that CD11b/CD18 deficiency drives macrophages toward a protumor phenotype, we reasoned that pharmacological activation might counteract this effect and restore their antitumor functions. To this end, we performed in vitro agonist experiments to investigate the direct effects of CD11b/CD18 activation on macrophage polarization and tumoricidal activity.

BMDMs were isolated and differentiated from WT mice (online supplemental figure S10A), pretreated with either CD11b/CD18-specific small-molecule agonists (GB1275 or LA1) or DMSO vehicle control, and then induced to polarize into TAM-like cells (hereafter TAMs) using MC38-CM (figure 6A). RT-qPCR analysis of key molecular markers under various treatments revealed that CD11b/CD18 activation significantly suppressed expression of pro-inflammatory genes (Il1a, Il1b), M2-related markers (Il10, Tgfb1, Arg1, Cd206, Ccl17, Csf1), and pro-angiogenic/stroma-remodeling factors (Pdgf, Vegf, Mmp2) in TAMs (figure 6C), while upregulating Tnfa expression (online supplemental figure S10B). Additionally, cytokine production and polarization marker expression in BMDMs were assessed by flow cytometry (figure 6B). The results showed that treatment with CD11b/CD18 agonists significantly reduced IL-10 production (figure 6D) and concurrently increased IFN-γ secretion in TAMs (figure 6E). Furthermore, under both basal and MC38-CM-stimulated conditions, treatment with GB1275 or LA1 markedly decreased the percentage and mean fluorescence intensity of CD206+ cells in BMDMs, whereas CD86 expression showed no significant change (figure 6F and online supplemental figure S10C). These data demonstrate that CD11b/CD18 activation effectively reprograms the immunosuppressive properties and polarization of TAMs, potentially facilitating their transition toward an antitumor immune state.

To validate CD11b/CD18 agonism in enhancing TAM tumoricidal activity, BMDM-CM from the indicated treatment groups were collected and used for indirect co-culture with MC38 cells. In parallel, treated BMDMs were co-cultured directly with MC38 cells at various effector-to-target ratios for 24 hours. MC38 cell apoptosis was subsequently evaluated by Annexin V/PI staining. The results showed that under both indirect and direct co-culture conditions, TAMs treated with CD11b/CD18 agonists significantly increased the total apoptosis rate of MC38 cells (figure 6G and online supplemental figure S10D,E). In direct co-culture settings, both 1:1 and 2:1 (BMDMs:MC38) ratios significantly enhanced early and late apoptosis in MC38 cells. However, direct co-culture did not further increase total MC38 apoptosis compared with indirect co-culture (online supplemental figure S10D,E). Collectively, these findings indicate that CD11b/CD18 activation promotes TAM-mediated tumor killing primarily through remodeling the macrophage secretome rather than via direct phagocytosis.

CD11b/CD18 activation suppresses ERK1/2 and STAT3 signaling in TAMs

To delineate the molecular mechanisms by which CD11b/CD18 regulates macrophage polarization, TAMs sorted from each genotype were further analyzed by RNA-seq. We first investigated the respective roles of CD11b and CD18 in TAM signaling pathways. KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs in CD11b−/− and CD18−/− TAMs compared with WT TAMs revealed no significantly enriched signaling pathways in either single-knockout TAMs (q>0.05) (online supplemental figure S11A,B), indicating that deficiency of either subunit alone is insufficient to induce global alterations in TAM signaling.

In contrast, CD11b/CD18 DKO TAMs exhibited significant enrichment of the janus kinase (JAK)-STAT signaling pathway compared with WT controls (rich ratio=0.060, q=0.012), along with enrichment trends in multiple inflammation-related, angiogenesis-related, and cell survival-related pathways, including nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB), TNF, hypoxia-inducible factor 1 (HIF-1), VEGF, and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) (figure 7A). Consistent with these observations, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis further validated pronounced activation of the JAK-STAT, MAPK, and NF-κB signaling pathways in DKO TAMs. Of note, significant upregulation of the PD-L1/PD-1 checkpoint pathway (figure 7B) corroborated our previous flow cytometry findings showing elevated PD-L1 surface expression on DKO TAMs. These findings suggest that CD11b and CD18 synergistically regulate TAM signaling, and that the loss of both subunits is necessary to activate multiple protumor pathways.

To further dissect the individual contributions of CD11b and CD18 to the DKO phenotype, we performed subgroup comparison analyses. The VEGF signaling was significantly enriched in DKO compared with CD11b−/− TAMs (rich ratio=0.052, q=0.023) (online supplemental figure S11C), whereas JAK-STAT signaling was enriched in DKO compared with CD18−/− TAMs (rich ratio=0.030, q=0.004) (online supplemental figure S11D). These results indicate that activation of the JAK-STAT pathway is primarily associated with CD11b deficiency, while activation of the VEGF pathway is more dependent on CD18 deficiency. Collectively, these data further demonstrate that CD11b and CD18 exhibit both functional synergy and divergence in regulating TAM signaling pathways.

Current evidence establishes that the JAK/STAT3, MAPK/ERK, and NF-κB signaling pathways critically regulate M2 macrophage polarization.49 52 53 To examine the activation status of these pathways in TAMs and the regulatory effects of CD11b/CD18 agonism, we generated WT murine TAMs in vitro as described above and treated them with CD11b/CD18-specific agonists (GB1275 or LA1). Immunoblotting analysis demonstrated elevated phosphorylation of STAT3, ERK1/2, and p65 in untreated TAMs. Notably, both agonists significantly attenuated STAT3 and ERK1/2 phosphorylation, whereas p65 phosphorylation was selectively reduced by GB1275 treatment (figure 7C–H). Despite the established role of PI3K/AKT signaling in M2 polarization,54 neither agonist significantly altered the expression of key pathway components (total AKT, p-AKTSer473, or PI3K p110α subunit) (online supplemental figure S11E,F). These findings collectively suggest that CD11b/CD18 activation modulates macrophage polarization through inhibition of ERK and STAT3 signaling, leading to suppression of M2 polarization and consequent enhancement of antitumor immunity.

CD11b/CD18 regulates macrophage polarization and tumoricidal activity via the ERK/STAT3 axis

The above results indicate that integrin CD11b/CD18 is critical for macrophage polarization and tumoricidal activity, potentially through suppression of ERK and STAT3 signaling. To investigate this mechanism, we employed selective inhibitors of ERK1/2 and STAT3 (figure 8A,B). The results showed that treatment with the STAT3 inhibitor (C188-9, 5 µM) alone significantly downregulated the expression of M2 polarization-associated genes, including Il10, Cd206, and Arg1, in MC38-CM-induced TAMs, and further reduced Arg1 mRNA levels following CD11b/CD18 agonist treatment (figure 8C–E). Meanwhile, treatment with the ERK1/2 inhibitor (SCH772984, 1 µM) alone markedly suppressed Cd206 and Arg1 expression in TAMs, and significantly decreased Cd206 mRNA levels in both DMSO control and agonist-alone groups (figure 8D,E). Notably, combined inhibition of ERK1/2 and STAT3 reduced the mRNA expression of Il10, Cd206, and Arg1 to comparable levels across all treatment groups (figure 8C–E).

We next assessed the functional impact of ERK/STAT3 inhibition on macrophage-mediated tumor killing. CM from BMDMs treated with or without SCH772984 and/or C188-9 was used to culture MC38 cells for 24 hours, and apoptosis was evaluated by Annexin V/PI staining. BMDM-CM from SCH772984-treated groups significantly increased MC38 cell apoptosis, and notably potentiated the antitumor activity of BMDMs in both DMSO control and agonist-alone groups (online supplemental figure S12A–D). C188-9 treatment alone primarily enhanced MC38 cell apoptosis induced by TAM-polarized BMDM-CM, and further potentiated the pro-apoptotic effect of that from GB1275-treated or LA1-treated groups (online supplemental figure S12E–H). Moreover, combined inhibition of ERK1/2 and STAT3 consistently promoted early, late, and total apoptosis of MC38 cells across all BMDM-CM conditions, and the induced apoptosis levels converged to a similar range (figure 8F–I).

These findings demonstrate that ERK1/2 and STAT3 play critical and synergistic roles in CD11b/CD18-mediated macrophage polarization, and that this effect may mediate their tumoricidal function through modulation of macrophage cytokine secretion. To validate this mechanism, we measured the concentrations of IL-10 and TNF-α in macrophage culture supernatants from each treatment group by ELISA. The results showed that treatment with GB1275 or LA1 significantly suppressed IL-10 production (figure 8J) while enhancing TNF-α secretion (figure 8K) in TAMs. Combined ERK1/2 and STAT3 inhibition further potentiated this effect and significantly increased TNF-α secretion in both DMSO control and agonist-alone groups.

Taken together, our data reveal that CD11b/CD18 suppresses macrophage polarization toward an immunosuppressive M2 phenotype via regulation of the ERK/STAT3 axis, thereby enhancing macrophage-mediated antitumor immunity.

Discussion

The substantial disease burden and marked heterogeneity of CRC underscore the need for novel therapeutic targets. Chronic inflammation drives CRC progression by remodeling the TME and facilitating immune evasion,6 7 processes dependent on leukocyte migration. Integrin CD11b/CD18, a leukocyte-specific heterodimeric adhesion molecule, critically regulates inflammatory responses and immune homeostasis.34,36 However, the precise functions and mechanisms of CD11b/CD18 in CRC pathogenesis await comprehensive elucidation.

Bioinformatic analysis of the GDC TCGA-COAD dataset revealed significantly decreased expression levels of both ITGAM (CD11b) and ITGB2 (CD18) genes in tumor tissues compared with normal colon tissues, suggesting their downregulation may correlate with colorectal tumorigenesis and progression. To elucidate the role of integrin CD11b/CD18 in CAC, we generated CD11b and CD18 DKO mice and systematically evaluated their susceptibility to AOM/DSS-induced CAC in single-KO and DKO mice. Our results demonstrated that CD11b/CD18 deficiency markedly aggravated CAC development, as evidenced by increased colorectal tumor burden, enhanced cellular proliferation and upregulated mRNA expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines. Furthermore, we detected elevated STAT3 phosphorylation and reduced YAP phosphorylation, both known to promote cell proliferation and tumor progression, in CD11b−/− and DKO colorectal tumor tissues. However, the specific cellular source of the observed cytokine changes and the precise molecular mechanism linking CD11b/CD18 expression to STAT3/YAP phosphorylation remain to be clarified.

Collectively, these data support a tumor-suppressive role for integrin CD11b/CD18 in CAC. However, our findings differ from previous reports showing that CD11b deficiency suppresses intestinal adenoma growth in ApcMin/+ mice.55 This phenotypic divergence likely stems from distinct pathogenic mechanisms: The ApcMin/+ model recapitulates Wnt/β-catenin-driven “adenoma-carcinoma” progression with scant immune infiltration, whereas our CAC model follows the canonical “inflammation-dysplasia-carcinoma” sequence featuring robust immune recruitment.56 57 Importantly, since integrin CD11b/CD18 functions as a leukocyte-restricted immunoregulatory receptor, its biological effects may be highly dependent on the immunological characteristics of the TME, which underlies the model-specific phenotypic variances observed in colorectal carcinogenesis studies.

Integrating these findings, we propose that CD11b/CD18 deficiency exacerbates inflammation-driven colorectal carcinogenesis through TIME remodeling. In our AOM/DSS-induced CAC models, CD11b/CD18 deficiency drove a tumor-permissive transformation of the TIME, characterized by protumor TAN skewing, increased immunosuppressive macrophage polarization, upregulated PD-L1 expression on specific myeloid subsets, impaired CD8+ T-cell effector functions, and reduced infiltration of dendritic cells and CD4+ T cells. These collective findings suggest that CD11b/CD18 deficiency promotes CAC progression through coordinated immunoregulatory mechanisms.

Moreover, genetic ablation of CD11b/CD18 did not significantly alter the infiltration frequencies of neutrophils, monocytes, or macrophages within the TME. This observation aligns with a prior study demonstrating normal neutrophil migration to inflammatory sites in CD11b/CD18-deficient mice.58 In our study, molecular profiling of CD11b/CD18-deficient TANs revealed significant upregulation of Itgax (encoding CD11c) gene expression. These data suggest a compensatory mechanism mediated by alternative adhesion molecules (eg, CD11c) that sustains neutrophil migratory capacity during CD11b/CD18 deficiency. This molecular adaptation may explain the preserved immune cell infiltration dynamics in the TME of CD11b/CD18-knockout CAC mice.

This study further demonstrated that CD11b/CD18 double deficiency reprogrammed TANs into an N2-like protumor phenotype. Transcriptomic analysis revealed significant upregulation of multiple protumorigenic genes, including anti-apoptotic factors (eg, the Bcl2a1 family) and chemokines (eg, Ccl5), along with enrichment of biological processes associated with monocyte chemotaxis. Functionally, DKO neutrophils in vitro exhibited enhanced resistance to apoptosis, particularly on stimulation with tumor-CM, which may prolong their survival within the TME and amplify their immunosuppressive effects. Furthermore, CD11b/CD18-deficient TAMs showed significant enrichment of chemokine-mediated signaling pathways and cell chemotaxis, with increased expression of Ccr3 (encoding a receptor for CCL5) and Ccr8, suggesting potential chemokine-mediated crosstalk between TANs and TAMs. Functional co-culture experiments confirmed that DKO TAN-like cells drove macrophages toward an immunosuppressive phenotype. Taken together, our findings reveal that CD11b/CD18 regulates neutrophil-macrophage crosstalk, and its deficiency may promote a cascade characterized by enhanced neutrophil survival, protumor polarization, and macrophage reprogramming, thereby fostering immunosuppression and tumor progression in CAC. Although transcriptomic data suggest a potential involvement of CCL5, the exact molecular mediators of this crosstalk remain to be further investigated.

Mounting evidence has established TAMs as important components of the TME that drive tumor progression. Owing to their functional plasticity, reprogramming immunosuppressive TAMs toward antitumor phenotypes has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy.14 20 Our data showed that genetic ablation of CD11b/CD18 skewed TAM polarization toward tumor-promoting phenotypes, suggesting a role for CD11b/CD18 in regulating macrophage polarization. Mechanistically, we found that this effect was partially mediated via CD11b/CD18-deficient neutrophils, which acquired a protumor phenotype and drove macrophages toward an immunosuppressive state in CAC. Beyond this indirect mechanism, our gain-of-function experiments using two CD11b/CD18-specific small-molecule agonists (GB1275 and LA1) provided direct evidence that CD11b/CD18 activation suppressed immunosuppressive gene expression in TAMs, reduced IL-10 production and CD206 expression, increased IFN-γ secretion, and enhanced TAM-mediated tumoricidal activity.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the STAT3 and ERK pathways not only independently participate in M2 macrophage polarization but also form a positive feedback loop via ERK-mediated STAT3 phosphorylation, synergistically amplifying M2 polarization.59,61 Our mechanistic investigations revealed that CD11b/CD18 deficiency activated the JAK-STAT, MAPK, and NF-κB pathways in TAMs, whereas CD11b/CD18 activation significantly suppressed ERK1/2 and STAT3 signaling. Consistently, pharmacological inhibition of ERK1/2 or STAT3 alone significantly attenuated M2 polarization and enhanced TAM-mediated tumor killing, and combined inhibition of both pathways resulted in a more pronounced effect, supporting a synergistic role of these pathways downstream of CD11b/CD18. Through both loss-of-function and gain-of-function experiments, we thus identified CD11b/CD18 as an important regulator of TAM functional remodeling.

Notably, we observed in single-gene knockout mice that the expression of integrin CD11b and CD18 subunits in the peripheral blood exhibited mutual independence, as the genetic ablation of one subunit did not alter the expression level of the other. Building on this observation, we further investigated a critical scientific question regarding the functional interplay between CD11b and CD18 subunits: During CAC development, do CD11b and CD18 operate as independent functional units, or is their heterodimerization essential for biological activity? Resolving this question is important for elucidating integrin-mediated antitumor mechanisms. Previous work has reported distinct, non-redundant functions of CD11b and CD18 in cellular responses,62 suggesting that integrin activity arises from the coordinated contributions of its subunits. Nevertheless, prior studies lacked systematic examination of the individual and cooperative roles of CD11b and CD18 in in vivo models or pathological conditions.

Our study demonstrates a functional divergence between the two subunits of integrin CD11b/CD18 in CAC development. Strikingly, single knockout of CD11b partially recapitulated the protumorigenic phenotype observed in CD11b/CD18 DKO mice, significantly enhancing STAT3 phosphorylation while suppressing YAP phosphorylation in colorectal tumors. While previous studies have primarily focused on the immunoregulatory role of CD11b,63 64 we identified CD18 as playing a critical role in modulating the TIME in CAC. CD18 deficiency markedly exacerbated immunosuppressive remodeling of the TIME, suggesting CD18 as the dominant effector molecule in CD11b/CD18-mediated immune regulation. However, the full tumor-suppressive capacity of integrin CD11b/CD18 requires cooperative action of both subunits. Consistent with this, TAM signaling pathway analysis revealed that single deficiency of either CD11b or CD18 failed to effectively alter signaling, whereas double deficiency activated multiple protumor pathways, with distinct signaling pathways preferentially associated with each subunit, further demonstrating both functional synergy and divergence. This dual mechanism—maintaining functional autonomy while enabling synergy—may represent a fundamental paradigm for how integrin family members orchestrate complex physiological and pathological processes. However, a limitation of this study is that the precise molecular mechanisms by which CD11b and CD18 individually and cooperatively regulate TAM signaling and CAC development remain to be fully elucidated, and whether their functional divergence extends to other immune cell types requires further investigation.

The study by Schmid et al demonstrated that CD11b activation promotes pro-inflammatory macrophage polarization by upregulating microRNA Let7a, thereby suppressing tumor growth, whereas CD11b inhibition prevents Let7a expression and induces cMyc, leading to immunosuppressive macrophage polarization and accelerated tumor progression.65 Although these findings are partially consistent with our observations, our study builds on and complements their work in several critical aspects. First, while the previous study focused exclusively on macrophages, we identified a neutrophil-macrophage crosstalk axis, uncovering an indirect mechanism of CD11b/CD18-mediated macrophage polarization. Second, unlike their emphasis on the CD11b subunit alone, we systematically dissected the functional divergence between the two subunits of the integrin, revealing that the CD11b/CD18 heterodimer, rather than CD11b alone, is functionally critical in the CAC model, with both subunits exerting non-redundant and cooperative roles. Third, we identified ERK/STAT3 signaling as a key downstream synergistic pathway through which CD11b/CD18 directly regulates macrophage polarization and tumoricidal function, complementing the Let7a/cMyc mechanism described by Schmid et al.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that CD11b/CD18 deficiency promotes CAC progression by remodeling the TME toward an immunosuppressive state, whereas CD11b/CD18 activation enhances antitumor immunity by reprogramming TAMs. Importantly, we reveal a functional paradigm in which the CD11b and CD18 subunits exert both distinct and cooperative roles to suppress CAC pathogenesis. These findings advance our understanding of CD11b/CD18-mediated immune regulation, provide new insights into integrin functional diversity, and establish a mechanistic foundation for developing CD11b/CD18-targeted immunotherapies for CAC.
