Hybrid TD3: Overestimation Bias Analysis and Stable Policy Optimization for Hybrid Action Space
Thanh-Tuan Tran 1 , Thanh Nguyen Canh 2 , Nak Young Chong 2,3 and ∗ Xiem HoangVan 1

Abstract
Reinforcement learning in discrete-continuous hybrid action spaces presents fundamental challenges for robotic manipulation, where high-level task decisions and low-level joint-space execution must be jointly optimized. Existing approaches either discretize continuous components or relax discrete choices into continuous approximations, which suffer from scalability limitations and training instability in high-dimensional action spaces and under domain randomization. In this paper, we propose Hybrid TD3, an extension of Twin Delayed Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (TD3) that natively handles parameterized hybrid action spaces in a principled manner. We conduct a rigorous theoretical analysis of overestimation bias in hybrid action settings, deriving formal bounds under twin-critic architectures and establishing a complete bias ordering across five algorithmic variants. Building on this analysis, we introduce a weighted clipped Q-learning target that marginalizes over the discrete action distribution, achieving equivalent bias reduction to standard clipped minimization while improving policy smoothness. Experimental results demonstrate that Hybrid TD3 achieves superior training stability and competitive performance against state-of-the-art hybrid action baselines.



I
Introduction

Deep reinforcement learning (DRL) has shown strong performance in sequential decision-making and continuous control, making it a promising approach for robotic manipulation. Learned policies are expected to bridge high-level task objectives and low-level actuator commands, enabling adaptation to contact dynamics, uncertainty, and changing scenes. A fundamental design choice is the control space in which actions are defined:
task space
(Cartesian end-effector objectives) versus
joint space
(commands at the level of individual joints). Task-space control specifies goals in the robot workspace (
e.g
., end-effector position and orientation), whereas joint-space control directly outputs motor-level quantities such as joint torques, angles
[
19
]
, or velocities
[
6
,
11
]
. While both formulations are viable, the choice affects exploration, constraint handling, and robustness in unstructured manipulation settings.

Conventional RL algorithms are designed for either purely discrete or purely continuous action spaces. Common extensions to hybrid settings involve discretizing continuous components
[
9
]
, parameterizing action subspaces
[
10
]
, or relaxing discrete choices into continuous approximations
[
16
]
. These conversions suffer from scalability limitations: discretization leads to exponential action blowup, while relaxation introduces approximation errors that degrade policy quality. More principled approaches such as PADDPG
[
10
]
and P-DQN
[
21
]
directly model hybrid actions but overlook dependencies between discrete and continuous components, limiting convergence in high-dimensional settings. SAC-based hybrid variants
[
14
,
3
,
23
]
improve exploration but introduce redundancy in parameter modeling that exacerbates instability under aggressive domain randomization.

A critical and underexplored source of instability in hybrid action RL is
overestimation bias
. In standard Q-learning, the maximization operator over noisy value estimates systematically inflates target values
[
8
,
18
]
. In hybrid settings, this bias is compounded: the agent must simultaneously maximize over a discrete action set and optimize continuous parameters, coupling two sources of approximation error into a self-reinforcing feedback loop. Under full domain randomization — where object poses, masses, and scene configurations vary at every episode reset — this instability becomes particularly severe, as distributional shifts prevent critics from converging reliably

To address these challenges, we first conduct a systematic empirical comparison of four DRL algorithms — TD3
[
5
]
, SAC
[
7
]
, DDPG
[
13
]
, and PPO
[
17
]
— each adapted to hybrid action spaces under full domain randomization. TD3 consistently achieves superior stability and performance, owing to its twin-critic architecture, delayed policy updates, and deterministic policy structure, which jointly mitigate overestimation bias and gradient variance. Motivated by this finding, we extend TD3 to hybrid action spaces and introduce a weighted clipped Q-learning target that marginalizes the Bellman backup over the full discrete action distribution, rather than committing to a greedy discrete selection. We further provide a rigorous theoretical analysis establishing a complete ordering of expected estimation bias across five hybrid algorithmic variants, formally justifying clipped double Q-learning as the most stable bias mitigation strategy under dense rewards and heavy randomization. For experimental validation, we adopt the reward functions and state representations from our prior work
[
1
]
and evaluate Hybrid TD3 on four manipulation tasks on a UF850 robotic arm in simulation.

•

•

II
Proposed Method

This section presents our approach to learning hybrid action policies for robotic manipulation under full domain randomization. We begin by formulating the discrete-continuous hybrid action space and motivating the choice of TD3 as the most stable backbone through empirical baseline comparison. We then introduce our proposed modification — a weighted clipped Q-learning target — and provide a theoretical analysis of overestimation bias across five hybrid algorithmic variants. Finally, we describe the state representation, low-level control interface, and training protocol adopted for our manipulation tasks.

II-A

Problem formulation

We consider the robot manipulator setting in which an agent must make decisions spanning both discrete actions (
e.g.
grasp, release) and continuous joint-space parameterization (
e.g.
, joint velocities). This structure defines a
parameterized hybrid action space
, where an action

a
combines a discrete component
a_{d}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}=\{0,1\}

(selecting the suction mode) and a continuous component

a_{c}\in\mathcal{A}_{c}\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{m_{k}}
(mode-specific joint-space
parameters, where the dimensionality
m_{k}
may vary per mode
k
). We adopt a factorized policy that models the two action components independently:

\pi(a\mid s)=\prod_{a^{i}\in a_{d}}\pi_{d}(a^{i}\mid s)\prod_{a^{i}\in a_{c}}\pi_{c}(a^{i}\mid s),

(1)

where the discrete part
\pi^{d}
is parameterized as a categorical distribution implemented via a softmax over logits from a shared network trunk, and the continuous part
\pi^{c}
is a deterministic actor head producing joint velocities. This factorization allows each component to be trained with its appropriate objective while sharing a common state encoder.

As shown in Fig.

This empirical advantage is attributable to three structural properties of TD3: (i) its twin-critic architecture, which mitigates overestimation bias via clipped double Q-learning; (ii) delayed policy updates, which reduce the frequency of destructive gradient interference between actor and critic; and (iii) its deterministic policy, which avoids the entropy-regularization instability that hampers SAC under distributional shifts induced by heavy randomization. In contrast, DDPG suffers from unmitigated overestimation bias due to its single critic, SAC struggles to maintain entropy-regularized convergence under non-stationary distributions, and the on-policy nature of PPO makes it sample-inefficient under high-variance randomized environments. Motivated by these results, we build our hybrid extension upon the TD3 framework, as detailed in the following subsection.

II-B

Hybrid TD3: Weighted Clipped Q-Learning

We extend the standard TD3 to the hybrid action space by defining twin critics:
Q_{\phi_{i}}(s,a_{c},a_{d})
,
i\in\{1,2\}
, that jointly evaluate both action components. Both critics share the same regression loss:

L(\phi)=\mathbb{E}_{(s,a_{c},a_{d},r,s^{\prime})\sim\mathcal{D}}\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(Q_{\phi_{i}}(s,a_{c},a_{d})-y\right)^{2},

(2)

where
\mathcal{D}
is the relay buffer and
y
is the bootstrapped regression target described below. The standard TD3 target selects the discrete action greedily and applies the clipped minimum over two target critics:

y=r+\gamma\min_{i=1,2}\,(Q_{\phi_{i}^{\prime}}(s^{\prime},\,a_{c}^{\prime},\,a_{d}^{\prime}),

(3)

where
a_{c}^{\prime}=\mu_{\theta^{\prime}}(s^{\prime})+\epsilon
,
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})

is target policy smoothing noise, and
a_{d}^{\prime}=\arg\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{d}}p_{d}(k\mid s^{\prime})
is the greedy discrete action under the target policy.

Instead of committing to a single greedy discrete action, we propose to
marginalize
the clipped minimum target over the full discrete action distribution. The resulting weighted clipped Q-learning target is:

y=r+\gamma\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{d}(a_{d}^{(k)}\mid s^{\prime})\min_{i=1,2}Q_{\phi_{i}^{\prime}}(s^{\prime},\,a_{c}^{\prime},\,a_{d}^{(k)}).

(4)

This formulation propagates a soft, distribution-weighted value estimate rather than the hard-max target of standard TD3, providing smoother gradient signals during critic updates and reducing sensitivity to discrete action misclassification in early training. As established in Theorem
II.1
, this modification preserves the same expected bias as the standard TD3 target while improving policy smoothness.

To improve robustness in highly stochastic environments, we retain the clipped double-
Q
operator in the actor update, deviating from standard TD3, which relies on a single critic. Using a minimum of two critics reduces overestimation bias during policy improvement.
In the parameterized hybrid action setting, the actor objective marginalizes over the discrete action distribution while generating mode-conditioned continuous parameters. The objective is defined as:

\mathcal{L}_{\theta}=-\,\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_{d}(a_{d}^{(k)}\mid s)\,\min_{i=1,2}Q_{\phi_{i}}\!\big(s,a_{d}^{(k)},a_{c}\big)\right].

(5)

By marginalizing over the discrete policy
\pi_{d}
rather than applying a hard
\arg\max
selection, the objective enables differentiable credit assignment across all discrete modes. This soft aggregation improves optimization stability and accelerates convergence during early training stages, when the discrete policy remains diffuse.

II-C

Theoretical Analysis of Overestimation Bias

A central motivation for choosing TD3 as the backbone is its principled handling of overestimation bias. In standard Q-learning
[
20
]
, the greedy Bellman target:

y=r+\gamma\max_{a^{\prime}\in\mathcal{A}}Q(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})

suffers from systematic overestimation because the maximization operator over noisy estimates satisfies
\mathbb{E}[\max_{a^{\prime}}\hat{Q}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})]\geq\max_{a^{\prime}}Q^{\pi}(s^{\prime},a^{\prime})
by Jensen’s inequality applied to the convex
\max
operator. In deep Q-learning, this effect is amplified when the same network is used for both action selection and evaluation, creating a self-reinforcing feedback loop that inflates value estimates across updates
[
8
]

In hybrid action spaces, overestimation is further compounded: the agent must simultaneously maximize over a discrete mode set and optimize continuous parameters, coupling two sources of approximation error. Under aggressive domain randomization, the resulting distributional shifts make this instability particularly severe. To rigorously characterize these effects, we derive formal bounds on the expected estimation bias for five hybrid algorithmic variants: Our proposed (Hybrid TD3), ACC
[
4
]
, and DATD3
[
15
]
. In addition, We formalize the robotic environment as a parameterized Markov Decision Process (MDP), denoted by
\mathcal{M}(\omega)
, where the configuration vector
\omega\in\Omega

\mathcal{P}(\Omega)
at the onset of each episode. Since
\omega
is randomized per episode, the agent perceives the transition dynamics
\mathcal{P}(s^{\prime}\mid s,a)
and reward functions
\mathcal{R}(s,a)
as non-stationary across the training set. This inherent environmental noise significantly increases the value function’s susceptibility to large estimation errors, thereby increasing the likelihood of substantial bias.

II-D

Hybrid Action RL Formulation

II-D
1
State Representation

To enable effective decision making, the agent receives a structured state embedding
\boldsymbol{s}_{t}
that captures both robot kinematics, object geometry, and task-relevant relational information.
A 2-step history of joint position
\boldsymbol{j}_{t-2:t}
provides implicit velocity information through temporal differences. Relative position vectors
\boldsymbol{p}_{eo}
,
\boldsymbol{p}_{og}
, and
\boldsymbol{p}_{eg}
encode the geometric relationships between the end-effector, object, and goal, guiding reaching and manipulation while improving invariance to global coordinates. Suction observations
\boldsymbol{su}_{t}
include two binary indicators (activation and release) together with a continuously predicted continuous suction command, enabling joint reasoning over contact state and actuation intent. Previous actions
\boldsymbol{a}_{t-3:t-1}
are included to promote temporal smoothness and mitigate abrupt control transitions.

All state components are normalized using Welford’s algorithm, which maintains running estimates of the mean
\mu_{t}
and variance
\sigma_{t}^{2}
:

\displaystyle n_{t}

\displaystyle=n_{t-1}+1,\delta_{t}=\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mu_{t-1},\mu_{t}=\mu_{t-1}+\dfrac{\delta_{t}}{n_{t}},

(7)

\displaystyle\delta_{t}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{x}_{t}-\mu_{t},M2_{t}=M2_{t-1}+\delta_{t}\odot\delta_{t}^{\prime}.

The running variance and standard deviation are
\sigma_{t}^{2}=M2_{t}/n_{t}
and
\sigma_{t}=\sqrt{\sigma_{t}^{2}}
. Each observation
\mathbf{x}
is then standardized as:

\hat{\mathbf{x}}=\dfrac{\mathbf{x}-\mu_{t}}{\sigma_{t}+\epsilon},\qquad\epsilon=1e-7,

(8)

[-1,1]

II-D
2
Action Representation

The continuous action
\boldsymbol{a}_{t}
specifies predicted joint velocities
\dot{\boldsymbol{j}}_{t}
for all robot joints. Target joint positions are obtained by integrating these velocities over the simulation timestep
dt
:

\boldsymbol{j}_{t}=\boldsymbol{j}_{t-1}+\dot{\boldsymbol{j}}_{t}\cdot dt.

(9)

The resulting joint targets are then tracked by a low-level PD controller that enforces velocity bounds and joint-limit constraints. Note that the inclusion of a PD controller breaks the strict Markov assumption, as the next state
\boldsymbol{s}_{t+1}
depends on
(\boldsymbol{s}_{t},a_{t},a_{t-1})
; we handle this by including the previous action
\boldsymbol{a}_{t-2:t-1}
.

II-D
3
Reward & Penalty Design

We adopt the hierarchical reward and penalty formulation from our prior
work
[
1
]
and summarize it here for completeness. The total reward at each timestep is a weighted combination of three reward terms and six penalty terms:

R_{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{t},\boldsymbol{a}_{t})=\sum_{i=0}^{2}W_{i}\cdot r_{i}^{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{t},\boldsymbol{a}_{t})-\sum_{j=0}^{5}W_{j}\cdot c_{j}^{t}(\boldsymbol{s}_{t},\boldsymbol{a}_{t}),

(10)

normalized by a constant factor of
10
to stabilize critic gradient magnitudes.

The reward terms provide stage-dependent supervision:
r_{0,t}
encourages safe end-effector approach to the target object;
r_{1,t}
measures goal-directed transport progress conditioned on established object contact; and
r_{2,t}
rewards object velocity aligned with the goal direction. The penalty terms discourage collisions with the ground, the robot itself, or surrounding objects; penalize exceeding the maximum episode length; and constrain end-effector inclination, action jerkiness, object tilt, and workspace boundary violations

II-D
4
Training Protocol

The overall DRL framework is illustrated in Fig.
2
. The agent is trained using Hybrid TD3 across four manipulation tasks:
reach
,
pick
,
move
, and
put
. At the start of each episode, the simulator randomly instantiates a scene containing more than 21 distinct object categories, with randomized poses, masses, and friction coefficients. For the reaching task, the agent must bring the end-effector to a target object. For the remaining tasks, success requires completing two sequential sub-goals: correctly approaching the target object and transporting it to the goal location.

An episode terminates when the agent successfully completes the required task, or upon any of the following failure conditions: end-effector collision with the ground or self-collision, a joint angle exceeding its allowable range, the object leaving the workspace, or the episode length exceeding a maximum of
100
steps. This termination scheme encourages the agent to develop safe, efficient trajectories while penalizing unstable behaviors.

Average return

Hybrid TD3 achieves the highest final average return across all four tasks. The performance gap is most pronounced in the Pick and Put tasks, where the hierarchical nature of the reward and the precision requirements of contact-rich manipulation amplify the consequences of Q-value overestimation. HyDATD3, despite having the least negative bias, exhibits unstable training with high variance, consistent with the hypothesis that moving bias toward overestimation is detrimental under heavy domain randomization. HyACC and HyTQC achieve moderate performance but converge more slowly and with greater variance than Hybrid TD3, suggesting that their distributional corrections introduce additional instability under the non-stationary distributions induced by full randomization. HyDARC shows competitive performance in the simpler Reach task but degrades on the more complex manipulation sequences, indicating limited scalability to dense-reward, multi-stage tasks.

IV
Conclusion

This paper presented Hybrid TD3, a principled extension of TD3 to discrete-continuous hybrid action spaces for robotic manipulation under full domain randomization. We established a formal ordering of expected estimation bias across five hybrid algorithmic variants, proving that the proposed weighted clipped Q-learning target — which marginalizes over the discrete action distribution — provides smoother policy gradient signals. Our analysis further showed that the tightly bounded negative bias of clipped double Q-learning is actively beneficial under the non-stationary distributions induced by aggressive randomization, where methods that reduce this bias introduce additional variance and instability. Experiments on four manipulation tasks confirmed the theoretical bias ordering and demonstrated superior performance over both dedicated hybrid action baselines and bias-mitigation variants. The policy also generalized zero-shot to novel object categories and transferred successfully to an alternative robot platform without retraining. Future work will focus on relaxing the independence assumption in the bias analysis to account for correlated critic errors, and on bridging the sim-to-real gap to enable direct deployment on physical hardware.

Target policy smoothing is applied to the continuous action, while the discrete

a_{c}^{\prime}=\mu_{\theta^{\prime}}(s^{\prime})+\epsilon,\qquad\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}),

(13)

a_{d}^{\prime}=\arg\max_{k\in\mathcal{A}_{d}}p_{d}(k\mid s^{\prime}),

(14)

where
\mu_{\theta^{\prime}}
denotes the target actor network and
p_{d}(\cdot\mid s^{\prime})

is the discrete action distribution.

The actor is trained to maximize the critic value with respect to the continuous
policy while selecting the best discrete action:

\mathcal{L}_{\theta}=-\max_{a_{d}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}}Q_{\phi_{1}}(s,\mu_{\theta}(s),a_{d}).

(15)

A-B

Deep Deterministic Policy Gradient (DDPG)

DDPG employs a single critic
Q(s,a_{c},a_{d})
.
The critic loss is

\mathcal{L}_{\phi}=\left(Q(s,a_{c},a_{d})-y\right)^{2},

(16)

with target

y=r+\gamma Q(s^{\prime},a_{c}^{\prime},a_{d}^{\prime}),

(17)

where
a_{c}^{\prime}
and
a_{d}^{\prime}
are generated by the target policy.

The actor objective follows the same hybrid maximization structure:

\mathcal{L}_{\theta}=-\max_{a_{d}\in\mathcal{A}_{d}}Q(s,\mu_{\theta}(s),a_{d}).

(18)

A-C

Soft Actor-Critic (SAC)

SAC maintains two critics
Q_{\phi_{i}}
,
i\in\{1,2\}
, and augments the target with
entropy regularization for both action components.
The critic loss is

\mathcal{L}_{\phi}=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\left(Q_{\phi_{i}}(s,a_{c},a_{d})-y\right)^{2},

(19)

where the target value is

\displaystyle y=r+\gamma\bigg(\min_{i=1,2}Q_{\phi_{i}}(s^{\prime},a_{c}^{\prime},a_{d}^{\prime})-\alpha_{c}\log p_{c}(a_{c}^{\prime}\mid s^{\prime})

(20)

\displaystyle-\alpha_{d}\log p_{d}(a_{d}^{\prime}\mid s^{\prime})\bigg).

Here,
p_{c}
and
p_{d}
denote the continuous and discrete policy distributions,
and
\alpha_{c}
,
\alpha_{d}
are the corresponding entropy coefficients.

The actor objective jointly optimizes both action components:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\theta}=\mathbb{E}_{a_{c}\sim p_{c},\,a_{d}\sim p_{d}}\bigg[

\displaystyle\alpha_{c}\log p_{c}(a_{c}\mid s)+\alpha_{d}\log p_{d}(a_{d}\mid s)

(21)

\displaystyle-\min_{i=1,2}Q_{\phi_{i}}(s,a_{c},a_{d})\bigg].

A-D

Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO)

For PPO, the hybrid policy factorizes into continuous and discrete components.
The clipped surrogate objective is given by

\mathcal{L}_{\theta}=-\mathbb{E}\big[\min\left(r_{t}(\theta)A(s,a),\;\text{clip}(r_{t}(\theta),1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)A(s,a)\right)\big],

(22)

where
A(s,a)
denotes the advantage estimate and the importance sampling ratio
is

r_{t}(\theta)=\dfrac{p_{c}^{\mathrm{new}}(a_{c}\mid s)\,p_{d}^{\mathrm{new}}(a_{d}\mid s)}{p_{c}^{\mathrm{old}}(a_{c}\mid s)\,p_{d}^{\mathrm{old}}(a_{d}\mid s)}.

(23)

The DARC hybrid adaptation follows the same critic architecture and loss

Building upon Soft Actor-Critic, the TQC hybrid method maintains

Appendix C
Estimation Analysis

To analyze the overestimation bias of the selected algorithms within hybrid action spaces, we first establish the statistical properties of the estimation errors.

Assumption 1 (Synchronized Bias Shift).

Let
Q^{\pi}
denote the true
Q
function under the current target policy
\pi
, and let
Q_{\theta}
be its neural network approximation. And as we assume that the estimation error at stage
k
can be expressed as a step-wise random error
\epsilon^{k}_{\phi,\xi}
, where
\xi\in\{1,2\}
indexes the critic networks. This error is assumed to be independent of the state-action pair
(s_{k},a_{k})
under fully randomized environment and to have finite mean, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\epsilon^{k}_{\phi_{\xi}}\right]=\mu_{\xi}<\infty
for all
k
, and
\xi\in\{1,2\}
. Since all critics are trained on the same replay buffer
\mathcal{D}
using identical architectures and update rules, they are subject to the same environmental dynamics. Consequently, in a non-stationary environment, the critics exhibit a
synchronized bias shift
relative to the
Q_{true}^{\pi}
, leading to the approximation,
\mu_{1}\approx\mu_{2}\approx...\approx\mu_{k}\approx\mu
.

Lemma 1 (Expectation of Gaussian Min-Max Operators).

Let
X
and
Y
be independent random variables with

X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},\sigma^{2})
and

Y\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{2},\sigma^{2})
.
Using the identities

\displaystyle\min(X,Y)=\frac{X+Y-|X-Y|}{2},

(35)

\displaystyle\max(X,Y)=\frac{X+Y+|X-Y|}{2},

we define
Z=X-Y
.
Since
X
and
Y
are independent,

Z\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{z},\sigma_{z}^{2}),\qquad\mu_{z}=\mu_{1}-\mu_{2},\quad\sigma_{z}^{2}=2\sigma^{2}.

(36)

\mathbb{E}[|Z|]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|z|f_{Z}(z)\,dz=\int_{0}^{\infty}z\bigl(f_{Z}(z)+f_{Z}(-z)\bigr)\,dz,

(37)

where
f_{Z}(z)
denotes the probability density function of
Z
.
Evaluating the integral yields

\mathbb{E}[|Z|]=\sigma_{z}\sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}}\exp\!\left(-\frac{\mu_{z}^{2}}{2\sigma_{z}^{2}}\right)+\mu_{z}\,\mathrm{erf}\!\left(\frac{\mu_{z}}{\sqrt{2}\sigma_{z}}\right).

(38)

Substituting
36
this result yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\min(X,Y)]=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}}{2}-\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\pi}}\exp\!\bigg(-\frac{(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}\bigg)

(39)

\displaystyle-\frac{\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}}{2}\mathrm{erf}\!\bigg(\frac{\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}}{2\sigma}\bigg).

Applying the assumption at
C
we simplify the expression to the expectation of min and max operators:

\mathbb{E}[\min(X,Y)]\approx\mu-\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\pi}},\quad\mathbb{E}[\max(X,Y)]\approx\mu+\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\pi}}.

(40)

Lemma 2 (Expectation of Nested Operators).

Let
X
and
Y
be independent random variables with
X\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},\sigma^{2})
and
Y\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{2},\sigma^{2})
.
Let
E
and
F
be an independent copy of
(X,Y)

E\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{1},\sigma^{2})
and
F\sim\mathcal{N}(\mu_{2},\sigma^{2})
.
Define

W_{1}=\min(X,Y),\qquad W_{2}=\min(E,F),

where
W_{1}
and
W_{2}
are independent random variables.

Using the results on the expectation of the minimum and maximum of Gaussian random variables
C
, the expectation of the nested minimum is given by

\mathbb{E}[\min(W_{1},W_{2})]=\mu_{w}-\frac{\sigma_{w}}{\sqrt{\pi}},

(41)

where
\mu_{w}
and
\sigma_{w}
denote the mean and standard deviation of
W_{1}

(and equivalently
W_{2}

The mean of

\displaystyle\mu_{w}=\mathbb{E}[\min(X,Y)]=

\displaystyle\frac{\mu_{1}+\mu_{2}}{2}-\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\pi}}\exp\!\bigg(-\frac{(\mu_{1}-\mu_{2})^{2}}{4\sigma^{2}}\bigg)

(42)

\displaystyle-\frac{\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}}{2}\mathrm{erf}\!\bigg(\frac{\mu_{1}-\mu_{2}}{2\sigma}\bigg).

The variance of

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[\max(W_{1},W_{2})]\approx\mu-\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{\pi}}+\frac{\sigma_{w}}{\sqrt{\pi}}.
