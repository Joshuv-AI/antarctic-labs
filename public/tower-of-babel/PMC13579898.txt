Next-generation of mesothelin-targeted CAR-T cells secreting anti-PD-L1 scFv for potent immunotherapy against 3D patient-derived colorectal cancer organoids

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapies have revolutionized the landscape of cancer treatment, particularly in hematological malignancies. However, their successful translation to solid tumors remains limited by several barriers, including immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and on-target/off-tumor toxicity. One promising strategy to enhance efficacy of CAR-T cells is the rational selection of tumor-specific antigens coupled with engineering strategies that incorporate localized immune modulation, such as CAR-T cells secreting immune checkpoint-blocking anti-PD-L1 scFv.MethodsTo better model therapeutic responses, we established a dynamic real-time autologous co-culture platform integrating colorectal cancer (CRC) patient-derived organoids (PDOs) and CAR-T cells to assess infiltration, persistence, and cytotoxicity ex vivo. Although early clinical trials of mesothelin (MSLN)-directed CAR-T cells have demonstrated high safety, their anti-tumor efficacy remains modest, highlighting the need for improved constructs. Therefore, we engineered anti-MSLN-CAR4-T cells using fully human anti-MSLN scFv linked to a triple costimulatory backbone (CD28, 4-1BB, and CD27) fused to CD3ζ, and anti-MSLN-CAR5-T cells, incorporating an additional anti-PD-L1 scFv.ResultsBoth CAR4- and CAR5-T cells exhibited comparable cytotoxic efficacy against MSLNHigh/PD-L1High autologous PDOs. However, only anti-MSLN-CAR5-T cells were able to sustain potent killing activity against PD-L1High PDOs with high IFN-γ/cytolytic cytokine production at a low effector-to-target ratio (5:1), reflecting improved resilience to PD-L1-mediated suppression.ConclusionsFinally, our 14-day ex vivo CRC-PDOs/CAR-T platform provides a promising rapid and translational tool for tumor-associated antigen validation, streamlined PDO isolation, autologous CAR-T cytotoxicity testing, and personalized immunotherapy optimization in solid tumors.Graphical abstract Schematic overview of the experimental strategy for personalized evaluation of anti-mesothelin (MSLN)-specific CAR-T cells using patient-derived colorectal cancer organoids (CRC-PDOs). Surgically resected tumor and matched normal tissues are processed to establish 3D-PDO cultures that retain the architectural complexity, cellular heterogeneity, and tumor-associated antigen (TAA) landscape of the original tumor. In parallel, autologous T cells are isolated from patient blood, activated, and engineered via lentiviral transduction to express MSLN-targeting CAR4 or CAR5 with additionally secretes an anti-PD-L1 scFv. CAR-T cells are subsequently co-cultured with patient-matched PDOs to assess tumor killing dynamics, cytokine secretion, and persistence/exhaustion profiles under both short-term and long-term immune pressure. This integrated autologous PDO-CAR-T platform models patient-specific antigen burden and immune resistance mechanisms, providing a translational system for preclinical efficacy testing and functional stratification of individuals who may benefit from MSLN-directed cell therapy. Keywords: Colorectal cancer, CAR-T cells, MSLN, PD-L1, Patient-derived organoids

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is a major public health problem globally, is the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the USA, and accounts for 10.7% of all cancer deaths [1, 2]. The prognosis is strongly stage-dependent: patients with localized disease have a 5-year relative survival rate of up to 90%, whereas those with metastatic CRC (mCRC) experience much poorer outcomes with survival dropping to approximately 14% [3]. Historically, CRC treatment has involved surgery, radiation therapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, often in combination for non-resectable tumors [4]. Multiple lines of evidence have demonstrated the critical role of the immune system in controlling cancer progression and have paved the way for the development of active and passive immunotherapeutic strategies. However, the limited efficacy of conventional immunotherapies in CRC, particularly in microsatellite-stable (MSS) tumors, which comprise the majority of cases, highlights a critical need for next-generation, context-adapted cellular immunotherapies capable of overcoming local immune suppression and antigen heterogeneity [5, 6]. T cell-redirecting strategies, exemplified by chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cell therapies, represent a promising avenue to improve outcomes in this otherwise poorly responsive population [7].

CAR-T cell has emerged as a transformative modality in cancer treatment, offering potent antigen-specific cytotoxicity through engineered T cell redirection [8, 9]. It is an extraordinary concept proposed by Z Eshhar in 1989 [10] that attempts to express functional chimeric receptors that can directly recognize tumor antigens and eliminate cancer cells without requiring MHC recognition [11]. While CAR-T therapy has achieved remarkable clinical success in hematological malignancies, translating this efficacy to solid tumors such as CRC has proven substantially more challenging [6, 12]. Unlike leukemias and lymphomas, CRC exhibits pronounced antigen heterogeneity, stromal barriers to immune infiltration, and a profoundly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment, all of which diminish CAR-T cell persistence and function [12, 13]. Several tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) have been investigated as potential CAR targets in CRC, mesothelin (MSLN), with early-phase clinical studies demonstrating safety but only modest clinical benefit [14, 15]. Ongoing clinical trials including NCT04503980, NCT05089266, NCT05783089, and NCT06051695 continue to assess MSLN-directed CAR-T cells in CRC, though durable responses remain limited largely due to immune checkpoint upregulation, particularly PD-L1 expression at the tumor immune synapse [15–17].

The limited efficacy of CAR-T cells in solid tumors is driven by multiple barriers, including antigen heterogeneity, restricted trafficking and infiltration, metabolic suppression, and checkpoint-mediated exhaustion within the tumor microenvironment (TME). In CRC, MSLN has emerged as a relevant tumor-associated antigen due to its high expression in metastatic or invasive CRC subtypes, where it contributes to epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) and tumor aggressiveness [18]. PD-L1 expression is upregulated as an adaptive immune resistance mechanism, directly inhibiting T cell cytolytic function through PD-1 engagement [19]. To overcome these, researchers are actively developing dual-/multi-target CAR-T cells and logic-gated CARs [20]. In patients with solid tumors, the safety and effectiveness of PD-1 inhibition in combination with anti-MSLN-CAR-T cells is being studied. The relevant clinical trials are as follows: NCT05089266, NCT04503980, NCT04489862, NCT03615313, NCT03030001, NCT04577326, NCT03747965, NCT03545815, NCT03182803, NCT02414269 and NCT05373147 [20]. However, most of these studies focus solely on PD-1 inhibition rather than PD-L1 expression at the tumor site, and no report PD-L1 co-expression status as a biological stratification marker. The absence of PD-L1 profiling in these trials highlights a critical translational gap and reinforces the rationale that co-expression of MSLN and PD-L1 defines a subset of CRC patients who may particularly benefit from CAR-T cells engineered with intrinsic checkpoint-blocking capability.

In parallel with advances in CAR design, the development of three-dimensional (3D) ex vivo culture systems have dramatically improved the physiological relevance of preclinical testing [21]. Organoid technology, derived from patient tumor tissue, recapitulates key architectural, genetic, and immunological features of the native malignancy, including antigen expression profiles, intratumoral heterogeneity, epithelial polarity, matrix interactions, and therapy resistance pathways [22]. These 3D patient-derived organoids (PDOs) therefore solved the problems of conventional 2D cell lines and provide a robust platform for modeling immune-tumor interactions, enabling functional interrogation of CAR-T cell penetration, persistence, and cytotoxicity under conditions that closely resemble the in vivo TME [23].

To address the dual challenge of antigen targeting and immune evasion, our research group engineered two MSLN-specific CAR constructs: CAR4, consisting of a fully human anti-MSLN scFv coupled to a triple costimulatory backbone (CD28, 4-1BB, and CD27) fused to CD3ζ, and CAR5, also equipped to secrete an anti-PD-L1 scFv for localized checkpoint blockade. Using a dynamic real-time autologous co-culture platform integrating CRC PDOs with CAR-T cells, we demonstrate that although both constructs exhibit comparable baseline cytotoxicity, CAR5 sustains superior killing capacity and effector cytokine production under low effector-to-target ratios reflective of solid tumor constraints. Here, we describe the development of the 14-day PDO/CAR-T assay as a rapid and clinically relevant workflow for antigen validation, CAR-T functional profiling, and the optimization of personalized cell-based immunotherapies for CRC.

Methods

Human Ethical approval

The collection of venous blood, matched normal tissue, and tumor tissue samples from 15 CRC-patients, following the acquisition of written informed consent, received approval from the Siriraj Institutional Review Board (SIRB) of the Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, in Bangkok, Thailand. The approval was granted under COA no. Si 617/2024.

3D-CRC-patient-derived organoids (3D-CRC-PDOs) culture conditions

Surgically resected tissues were obtained from patients diagnosed with CRC and informed consent was obtained from all patients enrolled in this study. Fresh normal and tumor specimens matched to each other (approximately 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm × 0.5 cm) were collected intraoperatively in sterile collection medium and the dimensions and weight of each sample were recorded prior to processing (Additional file 1: Table 1). Briefly, each CRC specimen was minced on ice and digested with Liberase™ DH (Cat. No. 5401054001, Merck Ltd., Darmstadt, Germany) in the advanced Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12 medium (Cat. No. 12634010, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) at 37 °C for 2 h. Subsequently, the suspension was strained through a 100 μm nylon filter and centrifugated for 5 min at 300 × g. The organoid pellets were suspended in a cold Matrigel® Basement Membrane Matrix (Cat. No. 354234, Corning Inc., NY), and embedded in pre-warmed 12-well culture plates. Once solidified, the PDO culture medium (Additional file 1: Table 12) was added. The culture medium was changed twice a week. The PDOs were passaged every 1-2 weeks. For storage, the PDOs were dissociated and resuspended in Cellbanker™ 2 (GMP-grade) cryopreservation medium (ZENOAQ, Japan) and frozen according to standard procedures.

3D-CRC-PDOs characterization

On day 1 following isolation, both normal- and tumor-derived PDOs were dissociated into single-cell suspensions and analyzed by flow cytometry to determine baseline expression of tumor-associated antigens including PerCP/Cyanine5.5 anti-CD326 (EpCAM) antibody (clone 9C4, #324213, BioLegend, San Diego, CA), Kappa-eF570 anti-ACTA2 (α-SMA) antibody (clone 1A4, 41-9760-82, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA), FITC-anti-CD3 antibody (clone UCHT-1, 21270313, ImmunoTools GmbH, Friesoythe, Germany), together with FITC-anti-MSLN antibody (clone B35, LS-C179484, LSBio/LifeSpan BioSciences, Seattle, WA), and PE-PD-L1 antibody (clone 29E.2A3, 329706, BioLegend). CD3⁺ T cells were gated and subsequently analyzed for CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ expression. The percentages of CD3⁺/CD4⁺ and CD3⁺/CD8⁺ subsets were calculated independently and not as mutually exclusive populations (Additional file 1: Table 3). Immune cell populations identified at this stage were evaluated only during the initial isolation process and were not intended to represent stable immune components of established long-term PDO cultures, as CD3⁺ cells may gradually diminish during subsequent in vitro expansion. 15 cases of CRC patients were stratified into four molecular subgroups (MSLNLow/ PD-L1Low, MSLNLow/ PD-L1High, MSLNHigh/ PD-L1Low, MSLNHigh/ PD-L1High) for subsequent allocation to CAR4 or CAR5 specificity tests (Additional file 1: Table 3).

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining in Tumor-PDOs

Tumor-PDOs were fixed in 10% formalin, followed by dehydration and paraffin embedding. The sections were prepared for automated H&E staining at the Department of Pathology, Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, and scanned at a resolution of 0.12 μm/pixel using a 3DHistech Panoramic 1,000 microscope with a 40x objective lens. The resulting images were saved in mrxs format for further analysis.

Construction of anti-MSLN CAR

The pCDH.EF1. SIN.WPRE plasmid, a self-inactivated (SIN) lentiviral vector (RRID: Addgene_71708) [24], served as the transfer vector for carrying the coding sequences of anti-MSLN-CAR4 or anti-MSLN-CAR5. The fully human anti-MSLN single-chain variable fragment (scFv) sequence was obtained from the international patent publication WO 2015/090230 A1, ensuring high specificity and reduced immunogenicity for MSLN-expressing target cells. To ensure optimal human expression, the anti-MSLN scFv gene fragment was codon-optimized for human use and synthesized by Integrated DNA Technologies, Inc. (Coralville, Iowa). The synthesized fragment was subsequently cloned in-frame with a c-Myc peptide tag and fused to the CAR4 backbone, which consists of the human CD8α hinge region, the CD28 transmembrane domain, the intracellular costimulatory domains derived from CD28, 4-1BB and CD27, and the CD3ζ signaling domain. This construct is hereafter referred to as “anti-MSLN-CAR4”.

For “anti-MSLN-CAR5”, the cDNA encoding the anti-PD-L1 scFv was derived from the humanized monoclonal antibody atezolizumab [25]. The construct contained a signal peptide (SP) followed by the variable heavy (VH) and light (VL) chains joined by a (G₄S)₃ linker and fused to an N-terminal HA epitope tag. The synthesis of this scFv cDNA has been described previously [24]. The anti-PD-L1 scFv cassette was inserted downstream of a T2A self-cleaving peptide sequence, enabling bicistronic expression together with the anti-MSLN-CAR4 backbone. The expression of the CAR constructs was driven by the human EF-1α promoter. The finalized plasmid was propagated in Stbl3 E. coli and purified using a Midiprep Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The integrity and fidelity of the inserted sequences were verified by Sanger sequencing.

Lentivirus production

Lentiviral particles encoding anti-MSLN-CAR4 or anti-MSLN-CAR5 were generated using Lenti-X™ 293 T packaging cells (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan). Approximately 5 ×10⁶ cells were seeded in 10-cm culture dishes one day prior to transfection. Cells were co-transfected with the pCDH-CAR transfer vector, psPAX2 packaging plasmid, and pMD2.G envelope plasmid using the calcium phosphate precipitation method. Six to eight hours after transfection, the medium containing the transfection complexes was replaced with fresh complete medium. The viral supernatants were collected 48 and 72 h after transfection, clarified by centrifugation, and passed through a 0.45-μm syringe filter. The filtered supernatants were then concentrated by highspeed centrifugation at 20,000 x g for 180 min at 4°C and stored at -80°C for subsequent experiments.

T cell isolation and lentiviral transduction

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from CRC patients by density gradient centrifugation in Lymphocyte Separation Medium (Corning, Inc., NY). The isolated PBMCs were plated in AIM-V medium (Gibco, Waltham, MA) supplemented with 5% human AB serum (Sigma-Aldrich Corporation, St. Louis, MO) for 24 h to allow adherence of monocytes, after which non-adherent lymphocytes were collected. T cells were then stimulated for 72 h with 5 μg/mL phytohemagglutinin-L (PHA-L) (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) in the presence of recombinant human IL-2 (10 ng/mL), IL-7 (5 ng/mL), and IL-15 (20 ng/mL) (ImmunoTools, Friesoythe, Germany).

Activated T cells were transduced with lentiviral particles encoding anti-MSLN-CAR4 and -CAR5 constructs in the presence of 10 μg/mL of protamine sulfate (Sigma-Aldrich) and subjected to spinoculation at 1,200 x g for 90 min at 32°C. Following transduction, cells were maintained in cytokine-supplemented culture medium containing IL-2 (10 ng/mL), IL-7 (5 ng/mL), and IL-15 (20 ng/mL) to support expansion. CAR surface expression was evaluated by flow cytometry at 72 h post-transduction, and the immunophenotype of the transduced T cells was further characterized on day 7.

Analysis of anti-MSLN-CAR-T cells by flow cytometry and western blot

Anti-c-Myc-FITC (Clone ab1394; Abcam, Cambridge) was employed to evaluate the transduction efficiency of all generations of anti-MSLN-CAR-T cells. The T cell phenotype was characterized by staining with various antibodies, including anti-CD3-FITC (Clone UCHT-1), anti-CD3-PerCP (Clone UCHT-1), anti-CD4-APC (Clone MEM-241), anti-CD8-APC (Clone UCHT-4), anti-CD16-APC (Clone 3G8), anti-CD19-APC (Clone LT19), anti-45RA-APC (Clone HI100), and CD62L-PE (Clone HI62L), all of which were procured from Immuno-Tools GmbH (Friesoythe, Germany), and anti-CD56-PE (Clone AB_2563925) was obtained from BioLegend (CA).

The exhaustion marker of T cells was determined by staining with the following antibodies: anti-PD-1-PE (Clone EH12.2H7), anti-LAG3-PE (Clone C9B7W), and anti-TIM3-PE (Clone F38-2E2), which were purchased from BioLegend. Following staining, cell detection was performed utilizing flow cytometry (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter). Flow cytometric acquisition was performed using a CytoFLEX cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA), and data were analyzed with FlowJo v10.0 software (Tree Star Inc., Ashland, OR). Marker expression levels were reported as mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) normalized to fluorescence-matched isotype control antibodies.

Immunoblot analysis was performed to determine the expression of the full length of the anti-MSLN-CAR4 and anti-MSLN-CAR5 protein. Lenti-X™ HEK 293 T cells were transfected with anti-MSLN-CAR4 or -CAR5 using Lipofectamine ® 2000 Reagent (Life Technologies Corporation, Carlsbad, CA) for 48 h. The transfected Lenti-X™ HEK293T cells and culture supernatant were collected, and their proteins were separated using SDS-PAGE and then probed with anti-human CD3ζ (clone sc-166435; Santa Cruz Biotechnology Dallas, TX). The HA-tagged anti-PD-L1 scFv protein was examined using a mouse anti-HA-tag antibody (Clone 2-2.2.14; Invitrogen). Subsequently, the membrane was incubated with a secondary antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP) (Dako, Agilent Technologies, CA). Detection of the signal was carried out using the Western Enhanced Chemiluminescence (ECL) Kit (Bio-Rad Laboratories, CA) and photographed using the Gel Doc system (Bio-Rad Laboratories). The protein expression levels were quantified by normalizing their band intensities to that of the β-actin internal control (clone sc-32233; Santa Cruz Biotechnology) using ImageJ software version 1.53e (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent spot (ELISpot) assay

The production of IFN-γ by anti-MSLN-CAR4- and -CAR5-T cells was evaluated using a Human IFN-γ ELISpot BASIC kit (Mabtech, Inc., Cincinnati, OH) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, MultiScreen HTS IP plates (0.45-μm Immobilon-P membrane) were pre-wetted with 35% ethanol and subsequently coated with 15 μg/mL anti-IFN-γ capture antibody overnight at 4°C. The following day, 25 μM of MSLN peptide [26] were co-incubated with 1×105 T cells, then transferred into the antibody-coated ELISpot plates and incubated for an additional 24 h at 37 °C to permit cytokine secretion. PMA/ionomycin stimulation served as the positive control. After incubation, secreted IFN-γ was detected using a biotinylated anti-IFN-γ monoclonal antibody (clone 7-B6-1), followed by alkaline phosphatase (ALP)-conjugated streptavidin. Colorimetric development was performed by adding 0.45-μm filtered BCIP/NBT-Plus substrate (Mabtech AB, Nacka, Sweden). Plates were thoroughly washed, air-dried, and spot-forming units (SFUs) were quantified using a Bioreader 5000 Pro-F Gamma ELISPOT Reader (BioSys GmbH, Karben, Germany).

3D-CRC-PDOs co-killing efficiency of anti-MSLN-CAR-T cells

The CRC organoids were digested in 5 mL of TrypLETM Express at 37°C for 3 min. Trypsinization was discontinued by adding 5 mL of 20% FBS advanced DMEM/F-12 + ++ media, followed by centrifugation at 300 x g. The dissociated organoids were transfected with CellTrackerTM Green CMFDA Dye (C7025, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and plated in Ultra Low Attachment (ULA) 96-well flat-bottom plates (Corning) at 1 ×104 cells/well. CellTrackerTM Orange CMTMR Dye (C2927, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc.) labeled activated anti-MSLN-CAR-T cells (E) were added to target PDOs (T) at (E:T) ratios of 20:1 for 72 h, and of 5:1 for long term co-killing assay (1 h, 1 day, 3 days, and 5 days). Fluorescence was captured with a ZEISS LSM 800 confocal laser fluorescence scanning microscope (AxioObserver7, LSM 800, Zeiss). The results were normalized to vehicle controls consisting of non-transduced T cells (NT). Experiments were performed using PDOs derived from a single CRC patient.

Tumor long-term cytotoxicity assay

A repetitive antigen stimulation assay was conducted in vitro to evaluate the killing efficacy of CAR-T cells. CellTrackerTM Green CMFDA Dye-labeled tumor-PDOs were co-cultured with non-transduced T cells (NT), anti-MSLN-CAR4-T cells, or anti-MSLN-CAR5-T cells at an E:T ratio = 5:1 for 1 h, 1 day, 3 days, and 5 days. Flow cytometry was used to quantify the remaining viable target cells and dead cells.

Cytokine production analysis

Culture supernatants from NT, anti-MSLN-CAR4-, and anti-MSLN-CAR5-T cells co-cultured with autologous normal- and tumor-derived PDOs were collected in two conditions: (i) an E:T ratio of 20:1 after 72 h, and (ii) an E:T ratio of 5:1 after 3 days for long-term co-killing assessment. Supernatants were clarified by centrifugation to remove cellular debris prior to cytokine measurement. Cytokine concentrations were quantified using the LEGENDplex™ Human CD8/NK Cell Panel (741065, BioLegend, San Diego, CA), which enables multiplex detection of 13 analytes, including IL-2, IL-4, IL-6, IL-10, IL-17A, IFN-γ, TNF-α, soluble Fas, soluble FasL, granzyme A, granzyme B, perforin, and granulysin. Samples were acquired on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA), generating a comprehensive profile of CAR-T cell-mediated cytokine secretion in response to tumor antigen engagement within PDO co-cultures.

Gene expression profiling and clinical correlation analysis (GEPIA2)

The transcriptional expression of MSLN and PD-L1 (CD274) in CRC was analyzed using the Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis database (GEPIA2, http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn). The correlation analysis between MSLN and PD-L1 mRNA levels was performed using the “Correlation Analysis” module with Spearman’s rank correlation. Tumor versus paired-normal differential expression was evaluated using the “Expression DIY-Boxplot” module, applying the following thresholds: |log2 fold change (log2FC)| ≥ 1 and q-value < 0.01, with matched TCGA tumor and GTEx-derived normal tissue data. Gene expression values were displayed as log2(TPM + 1) for log-scale normalization.

To assess the distribution of cancer-type, the tissue-wise expression of MSLN and PD-L1 across multiple human malignancies was visualized using the GEPIA pan-cancer boxplot function. Disease-free survival (DFS) analysis was conducted using the “Survival Analysis” module, stratifying patients into high- and low-expression groups based on the median cutoff point. The association between gene expression and pathological stage was evaluated using the “Stage Plot” module to examine expression dynamics during clinical progression.

Statistical analysis

Data are shown as the mean ± SD Statistical evaluations between two groups were performed by Student’s t-test. Experiments with more than three groups were evaluated by one-way ANOVA followed Tukey’s post-hoc test. Statistical analyses were carried out using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 25 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY) and GraphPad Prism, version 9.0 (GraphPad Software Inc, San Diego, CA). A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

MSLN and PD-L1 expression profile and clinical correlation in CRC patients

Using GEPIA2, which integrates TCGA and GTEx RNA-seq, we first surveyed MSLN and PD-L1 (CD274) differential expression across cancer types (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). We then compared CRC tumors (n = 367) with normal colorectal tissues (n = 667). In CRC, which was distinguished by the location of the tumor in the colon (COAD) or rectum (READ) showed that MSLN and PD-L1 were significantly upregulated in tumors by one-way ANOVA applying |log2FC | ≥ 1 and q-value ≤ 0.01 thresholds (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A). The data revealed a statistically significant positive correlation between MSLN and CD274 expression levels in tumor samples (Spearman R = 0.11; p-value = 0.00053) (Additional file 1: Fig. S1B). The expression difference was significant in both COAD (tumor, n = 275 vs normal, n = 349) and READ (tumor, n = 92 vs normal, n = 318), indicating that upregulation of these genes is a consistent molecular feature across anatomical subtypes of CRC (Additional file 1: Figs. S1C and S1G). A box plot of log2(TPM + 1) values confirmed a higher MSLN in tumors versus normal colon (p-value < 0.05; Additional file 1: Fig. S1D). Overall survival analysis did not show a significant association between MSLN expression and outcome (p-value = 0.58), although higher expression qualitatively aligned with a poorer prognosis (Additional file 1: Fig. S1E). A pathological stage plot suggested an increase in MSLN expression with advancing tumor stage, consistent with a role in CRC progression (Additional file 1: Fig. S1F). To evaluate the relevance of PD-L1 in CRC, we queried GEPIA2. PD-L1 was expressed more strongly in CRC tumors (n = 275) compared to matched normal tissues (n = 349) (Additional file 1: Fig. S1G and S1H). Kaplan-Meier analysis showed a nonsignificant trend towards poorer overall survival with higher PD-L1 (p-value = 0.25; Additional file 1: Fig. S1I). The expression stratified by pathological stage showed a tendency toward higher PD-L1 in advanced stages, suggesting an association with progression (Additional file 1: Fig. S1J). The GEPIA2 data indicate that a statistically significant association is unlikely to arise by chance. Although the magnitude of correlation was moderate, the result suggests possible transcriptional co-regulation or coordinated expression within the CRC tumor microenvironment. Given their concurrent overexpression and correlation in CRC, these cell-surface proteins represent complementary targets for the development of dual-targeted immunotherapy.

Establishment of patient-derived colorectal cancer organoids (CRC-PDOs) ex vivo

Surgically resected tumor tissue, matched adjacent normal tissue, and peripheral blood were collected from an individual CRC patient. The tissue specimen was divided into two portions that were processed for standard histological diagnosis and another for PDO derivation. This workflow enables paired molecular and functional characterization while preserving patient-specific tumor architecture and antigenic landscape (Fig. 1A). On day 1 post-isolation, EpCAM⁺ epithelial cells (Fig. 1C, Additional file 1: Table 3) expressing tumor-associated antigens (TAAs) were readily identified and separated from stromal αSMA⁺ fibroblasts (Fig. 1D, Supplementary Table S3) and infiltrating CD3⁺ T cells (Fig. 1E, Additional file 1: Table 3), confirming successful enrichment of the epithelial tumor compartment for PDO derivation. Notably, αSMA expression was markedly higher in tumor-PDOs isolates than in matched normal-PDOs (p-value = 0.0047, Fig. 1D), consistent with fibroblast activation/remodeling within the tumor stroma [27]. The lymphocytic compartment also exhibited a tumor-skewed profile, with an overall CD3⁺/CD8⁺ T cell infiltration being higher in tumors compared to adjacent normal tissue (p-value = 0.0456, Fig. 1E), moreover, the CD3⁺/CD8⁺ population was proportionally lower than CD3⁺/CD4⁺ within the tumor microenvironment, reflecting a characteristic immunosuppressive phenotype commonly observed in CRC (p-value = 0.0016, Fig. 1E). Notably, CD3⁺ T cells were detected primarily during the initial PDO isolation phase, reflecting the presence of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) in the original surgical specimens. However, these immune cells are not maintained during long-term culture under standard submerged Matrigel conditions, which predominantly support epithelial cell expansion [28]. Therefore, the relatively high proportion of CD3⁺ T cells observed at day 1 does not represent the steady-state composition of established PDO cultures. Moreover, revealed overlapping CD3⁺/CD4⁺/CD8⁺ T cell populations in both normal organoids and PDOs at day 1 following organoid isolation, which may partially explain the cumulative CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ percentages exceeding 100%, as shown in Supplementary Fig. S2A–B. No statistically significant difference in CD3⁺/CD4⁺/CD8⁺ populations was observed between normal organoids and PDOs (Additional file 3: Fig. S2B). After 2 weeks of ex vivo culture, different well-formed 3D organoid gland-like structures and morphology were observed, indicating successful establishment and expansion of CRC-PDOs (Fig. 1B). The clinicopathological characteristics of the 15 CRC patients used for PDO generation, including sex, age, diagnosis, tumor stage, and metastatic status, are summarized in Additional file 1: Table 3.

Next, TAAs profiling of matched normal and tumor-derived PDOs was performed to determine whether CRC-PDOs appropriately recapitulate the antigenic landscape required for downstream αMSLN-CAR4- and αMSLN-CAR5-T cell targeting (Fig. 2). Next, we assessed the expression of MSLN and PD-L1 in the EpCAM+ PDOs population (Fig. 2A). As represented in Fig. 2B, the pooled analysis of all 15 CRC cases demonstrated that the MSLN⁺ (p-value = 0.0085), PD-L1⁺ (p-value = 0.0086) and double-positive MSLN⁺/PD-L1⁺ cell populations (p-value = 0.0049) were significantly more abundant in tumor-derived PDOs compared to their matched normal-PDO counterparts. In addition, we also demonstrated patient-level antigen profiles, showing that both MSLN and PD-L1 expression were consistently elevated on a case-by-case basis across individual tumor-PDOs relative to their paired normal controls (Fig. 2C-D). Tumor PDOs show markedly elevated MSLN and PD-L1 expression frequency, with many samples clustering above 40-60% positive cells, while normal PDOs remain close to baseline ( < 20% for most samples) (Fig. 2D). MSLN and PD-L1 markers are significantly higher in tumor PDOs compared with matched normal PDOs, as indicated by the p-values (MSLN p = 0.0085, PD-L1 p = 0.0086, and MSLN/PD-L1 co-expression p = 0.0049) (Fig. 2D). Moreover, correlation analysis revealed a significant positive association between MSLN and PD-L1 expression levels within the tumor-PDO epithelial compartment (r = 0.9878, r2 = 0.9757, p-value = 3.129e-10), indicating coordinated upregulation of these immune-evasive and tumor-promoting surface antigens (Fig. 2E).

Engineering and expression of MSLN-specific CAR in Lenti-X™ 293 T cells and primary patient T cells

To evaluate whether anti-MSLN-CAR4-T cells could overcome tumor-microenvironment-mediated immunosuppression, we further engineered the anti-MSLN-CAR5 construct to secrete an anti-PD-L1 single-chain variable fragment (scFv), enabling local blockade of PD-1/PD-L1 signaling at the tumor interface [29]. Figure 3E illustrates the schematic design of the lentiviral vectors encoding anti-MSLN-CAR4 and anti-MSLN-CAR5. The fully human anti-MSLN scFv was integrated into two generations of CAR constructs consisting of anti-MSLN-CAR4 with CD28, 4-1BB, CD27, CD3ζ and anti-MSLN-CAR5 (referred to anti-MSLN-CAR4 incorporating anti-PD-L1 scFv) (Fig. 3F). The anti-PD-L1 scFv fragment, derived from the humanized monoclonal antibody specific to PD-L1, Atezolizumab [25]. The gene fragment encoding an HA-tagged anti–PD-L1 scFv was positioned downstream of the anti-MSLN-CAR4 cassette via a T2A self-cleaving peptide sequence. Consequently, upon transfection of the anti-MSLN-CAR5 construct engineered T cells concurrently produce the anti-MSLN-CAR4-CAR on the cell surface while secreting the anti-PD-L1 scFv in the extracellular culture medium, where it can neutralize PD-L1 in the tumor microenvironment.

To examine the expression of the anti-MSLN-CAR4 and anti-MSLN-CAR5 proteins, both lentiviral constructs were transiently transfected into Lenti-X 293 T cells, and immunoblotting was performed on cell lysates and culture supernatants. A full-length CAR protein (72 kDa) was successfully detected in lysates from anti-MSLN-CAR4 (p-value = 0.0080) and anti-MSLN-CAR5 (p-value = 0.0156) constructs using an anti-CD3ζ antibody, whereas no corresponding band was observed in non-transduced (NT) controls, confirming correct of CAR expression (upper panel, Fig. 3G-H and Additional file 1: Table 6). In addition, a 30 kDa HA-tagged anti-PD-L1 scFv that was produced from the anti-MSLN-CAR5 lentiviral construct, was successfully detected in supernatant (p-value = 0.0068 compared to NT and p-value = 0.0013 compared to anti-MSLN-CAR4) (lower panel, Fig. 3G and I and Additional file 1: Table 6), demonstrating that the scFv is not only synthesized intracellularly, but is also actively secreted by the transfected Lenti-X 293 T cells.

To further examine the surface expression of anti-MSLN-CAR4 and anti-MSLN-CAR5 in transduced T cells, flow cytometric analysis was performed using an FITC-conjugated anti-c-Myc antibody, which detects the c-Myc tag positioned between the MSLN-specific scFv and the hinge region. The mean expression of the CAR protein expression on the surface of T cells from the anti-MSLN-CAR4 and anti-MSLN-CAR5 lentiviral constructs was 8.04 ± 37.80% (p-value = 0.0029) and 7.56 ± 69.96% (p-value = 0.0005), respectively, compared to untransfected cells (2.74 ± 11.8%) (upper panel, Fig. 3J–K and Additional file 1: Table 7). In parallel, secretion of the HA-tagged anti-PD-L1 scFv from anti-MSLN-CAR5-transduced T cells was confirmed using an anti-HA antibody. The representative histograms are shown in lower panel of Fig. 3J, M, and Additional file 1: Table 7, demonstrated %HA-tagged from anti-MSLN-CAR4 T cells (7.48 ± 7.09%) compared to NT (2.48 ± 1.90%, p-value < 0.0001) and anti-MSLN-CAR5 T cells (28.35 ± 29.36%) compared to NT (2.48 ± 1.90%, p-value < 0.0001), thereby demonstrating both membrane-bound CAR expression and successful extracellular release of the PD-L1 neutralizing scFv. Notably, surface staining with an anti-PD-L1 antibody revealed detectable PD-L1 signal on anti-MSLN-CAR4 (p-value < 0.0001) and anti-MSLN-CAR5-T cells (p-value < 0.0001) themselves, indicating that endogenous PD-L1 is present on the surface of activated CAR-T cells (middle panel, Fig. 3J and L).

Discussion

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common type of cancer diagnosed worldwide, with an incidence of 10.2% and a mortality rate of 9.2% of all cancers [2]. Since surgical resection and radiotherapy are often insufficient to completely eliminate CRC particularly in advanced or metastatic stages, systemic chemotherapy remains the cornerstone of treatment for CRC. Standard regimens typically include fluoropyrimidine-based combinations such as FOLFOX (5-fluorouracil, leucovorin, and oxaliplatin) or FOLFIRI (5-fluorouracil, leucovorin, and irinotecan) [30, 31]. However, their therapeutic efficacy is frequently limited by systemic toxicity and the development of drug resistance because approximately 33% of patients do not respond to treatment or they relapse after treatment [32, 33]. Moreover, the 3-year relative overall survival (OS) for patients with metastatic colorectal cancer (mCRC) is approximately 35-30% [34], and fewer than 15% beyond 5 years from diagnosis [35]. Multiple studies have revealed the crucial role of the immune system in regulating CRC progression and response to therapy, inspiring the development of active and passive immunotherapies that harness immune mechanisms to combat cancer [36]. Adoptive T cell therapy using chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T cells offers promising a better clinical benefit for patients with CRC, however, the pronounced heterogeneity of CRC remains a barrier to the efficacy of immunotherapy in those patients [37]. Hence a series of strategies have been proposed to restore therapeutic activity, including modulation of immune checkpoint molecule overexpression and suppression of inhibitory cytokine secretion [37]. These approaches have become to overcome the limitations of conventional therapies and represent key areas of focus in current CRC research.

CAR-T cell therapy starts by collecting T cells from the CRC patients (Graphical summary of the study concept). These cells are then genetically engineered to express a receptor that targets specific tumor antigens, mesothelin (MSLN), as supported by numerous studies [20]. First, the modified anti-MSLN-CAR4-T cells, containing CD3ζ plus CD137 (4-1BB) and CD28 signaling domains, are generated and expanded ex vivo. While this multi-costimulatory design is not conventional, it may provide advantages in overcoming T cell exhaustion and limited persistence in solid tumor settings. Both 4-1BB and CD28 incorporated CAR demonstrated synergistically enhance T cell functions such as proliferation, cytokine production, and persistence [38]. The CD3ζ/4-1BB-based CAR therapies that targeted MSLN have already entered clinical trials (NCT06051695, NCT01355965, NCT01897415, NCT05783089, and NCT04503980); however, our laboratory focuses on enhancing their efficacy by engineering CAR-T cells capable of secreting anti-programmed death ligand 1 (PD-L1) single-chain variable fragments (scFv) to counteract tumor-induced immunosuppression. As reported by our team, the successfully engineered CAR5 construct co-expresses the CAR and anti-PD-L1 scFv proteins, which are secreted into the culture medium (Fig. 3F) [24, 25, 29]. The secreted scFv retained functional activity, as evidenced by its ability to bind PD-L1 expressed on tumor cells and to competitively inhibit the interaction between PD-L1 and the anti-PD-L1 monoclonal antibody. Although baseline PBMC profiling was performed, correlation between initial T cell phenotypes and resulting CAR-T cell characteristics was not formally evaluated due to the limited sample size. Both constructs demonstrated comparable expansion kinetics and viability following transduction. Furthermore, no substantial phenotypic differences were observed between CAR4- and CAR5-T cells based on the markers analyzed, suggesting that the observed functional differences are not primarily driven by major phenotypic divergence. Future studies with larger cohorts will be required to more definitively correlate baseline PBMC characteristics with final CAR-T cell phenotypes, as limited cell yield in the current study precluded comprehensive multi-parameter profiling [39].

More recently, a three-dimensional (3D) organoid culture system has been shown to support the generation of mature and functional CD3+/CD8αβ+ cytotoxic T cells and CD3+/CD4+ helper T cells, including TCR-transgenic T cells, thereby offering a promising platform for further modification and optimization of this organoid-based immune culture strategy [40]. To test this hypothesis, we established autologous ex vivo models by generating 15 CRC patient-derived organoids (PDOs) to model immunotherapy function of paired patient-matched CAR-T cells. These PDOs faithfully preserved the histological and molecular characteristics of the original tumors and exhibited relevant patient-specific responses to treatment [41, 42]. With ex vivo model, we have demonstrated distinct functional behaviors between the CAR4 and CAR5 engineered T cell products, uncovered MSLN and PD-L1 expression pattern associated with serial killing, designed an optimal time-lapse framework of T cell therapy and provided proof-in-principle for a personalized preclinical testing. Through screening with GEPIA2 against recently identified targets in solid tumors, we observed that both MSLN and PD-L1 were markedly upregulated in tumor tissues. Elevated MSLN and PD-L1 expression was associated with poorer overall survival of CRC patients, including CRC tumor tissues (n = 275) and normal tissues (n = 349). Consistent with these bioinformatic findings, analyses of 15 CRC-PDOs revealed significantly higher MSLN/PD-L1 in tumor tissues compared with their matched normal counterparts. Additionally, alpha-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA) is typically significantly higher in tumor-PDOs than in normal, which correlate with more advanced tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) stages and worse patient outcomes [43, 44]. MSLN and PD-L1 expression levels in our isolated-PDOs’ system can serve as predictive biomarkers to guide personalized cancer treatment (Fig. 1A), helping to anticipate patient responses to anti-MSLN-CAR4- or CAR5-T cell immunotherapies. The expression profiles of MSLN and PD-L1 within our PDO system may serve as useful biomarkers for evaluating personalized treatment strategies (Fig. 2), providing a platform to investigate patient-specific responses to anti-MSLN-CAR4- or CAR5-T cells. While solid tumors frequently evade endogenous immunity through downregulation of HLA molecules or defects in antigen presentation machinery, the HLA-independent nature of CAR-T cell recognition may enable bypass of these mechanisms [45]. This suggests that MSLN-targeted CAR-T cells could represent a potential therapeutic option for “cold” CRC tumors with impaired antigen presentation [46]. Although CD3⁺/CD8⁺ T cells were detected more frequently in isolated tumor-PDOs than those normal-PDOs, doesn’t always guarantee tumor control, as they can become dysfunctional or exhausted within the tumor microenvironment [47], therefore, we assessed the proportion of not only T, B, NK, and NKT cells, but also exhausted T cells prior to generating each patient’s CAR-T cells.

Recent reports indicate the potential application of a co-culture system containing PDOs and immune cells for assessing the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy and precision medicine [48]. Despite relatively low transduction efficiencies commonly observed when manufacturing autologous CAR-T products from heavily pre-treated cancer patients, both CAR4- and CAR5-T cells exhibited comparable expansion kinetics and viability, suggesting that incorporation of the anti-PD-L1 scFv cistron does not adversely affect the manufacturing profile [49]. Functional assays were performed using whole cell populations normalized by cell number to preserve cell fitness and better reflect clinically relevant dosing conditions. However, we acknowledge that pre-enrichment of CAR-expressing cells in future studies may further isolate CAR-specific effects and reduce inter-sample variability. In addition, larger cohorts will be required to robustly correlate baseline PBMC characteristics with final CAR-T phenotypes, as current cell yield limitations precluded comprehensive multi-parameter profiling [49, 50]. Typically, high E:T ratios are used in cytotoxicity assays to achieve robust tumor cell lysis [29], however, at an E:T ratio of 20:1, our assay failed to discriminate the enhanced killing efficacy of anti-MSLN-CAR5-T cells over CAR4-T cells (Fig. 4). Accordingly, the superiority of CAR5 is not universal but emerges under immunosuppressive or effector-limited conditions. In our present report, secretion ability of anti-PD-L1 scFv from anti-MSLN-CAR5-T cells enhanced under specific conditions of selectively eliminate MSLNHigh/PD-L1High tumor-PDOs than normal-PDOs in a serial exposure model. Moreover, the superior persistence of anti-MSLN-CAR5-T cells compared with CAR4-T cells, at the low E:T ratio of 5:1, suggests a potential for sustained anti-tumor activity, particularly evident at day 3 of co-culture. Important, the functional superior of anti-MSLN-CAR5-T cells over CAR4-T cells is highly context-dependent. At high E:T ratios (20:1), both constructs demonstrated comparable cytolytic activity, suggesting a state of functional saturation where the abundance of effectors overcomes PD-L1-mediated inhibition regardless of scFv secretion. However, the distinct advantages of CAR5 become evidence under lower E:T conditions, which more closely simulate the clinical reality of solid tumors where T cells are often outnumbered and must sustain activity despite chronic antigen exposure [51] (Fig. 6). These findings suggest that while CAR5 may not offer a universal advantage in all scenarios, it provides critical resilience in ‘effector-strained’ environments, potentially enhancing the durability of the immune response in the face of a high tumor burden, particularly with PD-L1High tumors [52, 53]. The anti-MSLN-CAR4- and CAR5-T cells typically secrete higher levels of cytokines and cytolytic molecules, granzyme B and perforin, which perforin forming pores in the cell membrane to allow granzyme B to enter and trigger the killing target cell process [54]. However, anti-MSLN-CAR5-T cells still demonstrated significant release of IL-17A, IFN-γ, and granzyme A compared to target alone, while TNF-α, IL-2, and granulysin release were not significantly elevated. Interestingly, the secretion of anti-PD-L1 scFv from anti-MSLN-CAR5-T cells did not alter all cytokine release profile induced by MSLN and PD-L1 status in target cells. This indicates that activating CAR-T cells to target MSLN/PD-L1 creates a balanced immune response, with both helper T helper 1 (Th1) and Th2, which is effective against the specific cancer cells [55]. While CAR5-T cells exhibited elevated IL-17A production, it is important to interpret this finding with caution, as IL-17A has context-dependent roles in CRC, including both pro-inflammatory and pro-tumorigenic effects [56]. Therefore, the increase in IL-17A should not be assumed to be uniformly beneficial without further mechanistic validation. Consequently, this increase in IL-17A profile requires further evaluation to delineate the functional phenotypes of CAR-T cells in this system, particularly to distinguish between Th17-like responses and cytotoxic effector functions. It is important to note that the levels of IL-6 secretion from both anti-MSLN-CAR-T cells, exposed to autologous PDOs, were significantly lower than those of PDOs alone. Moreover, consistently lower IL-6 secretion from anti-MSLN-CAR5-T cells than from CAR4-T cells across all PDO groups (Fig. 6I). Accordingly, secretion of anti-PD-L1 scFv from anti-MSLN-CAR5-T cells was associated with a reduction in IL-6 levels, most notably in MSLNHigh/PD-L1High-PDOs (p-value = 0.0006) (Fig. 6I). Therefore, anti-MSLN-CAR5-T cells potent cytotoxic activity against PD-L1-High tumor cells, may offer therapeutic advantages by reducing the risk of cytokine release syndrome (CRS) when administered to real patients. Similarly, although reduced IL-6 levels may suggest a potentially favorable safety profile, the underlying mechanisms remain unclear and require further investigation. These observations should be considered hypothesis-generating rather than definitive conclusions.

While both CAR4 and CAR5 demonstrated selective cytotoxicity toward tumor-derived PDOs with minimal effects on matched normal-PDOs, it is important to acknowledge that MSLN is expressed at low levels in certain normal tissues. Therefore, on-target/off-tumor toxicity cannot be fully excluded based on ex vivo models alone [57]. Furthermore, although the anti-PD-L1 scFv is designed for localized secretion within the tumor microenvironment, its potential systemic diffusion and unintended immune activation remain to be investigated [58]. In particular, the risk of cytokine-related toxicities, including CRS, warrants further evaluation. Future studies using in vivo models, such as humanized mouse systems or advanced toxicity modeling platforms, will be essential to assess biodistribution, safety profiles, and potential off-tumor effects of this approach.

The ex vivo co-culture system, which incorporation of PDOs into CAR-T testing pipelines represents an important step toward functional precision immuno-oncology [59–61]. PDOs retain the genetic, phenotypic, and microenvironmental features of their parental tumors, enabling patient-specific assessment of immune responsiveness and prediction of clinical outcomes [62]. In our study, CD3⁺ T cells detected within PDO preparations were primarily observed during the initial isolation phase and likely represent residual tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) from the original tumor tissue (Fig. 1E and Additional file 1: Table 3). However, these immune populations are not maintained during long-term culture under standard submerged Matrigel-based conditions, which predominantly support epithelial expansion. As a result, this model becomes predominantly epithelial-dominant and does not fully recapitulate the multi-lineage complexity of the in vivo CRC tumor microenvironment, particularly the absence of endothelial cells, myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and other immunosuppressive subsets. Consequently, bystander activation of resident immune populations could not be evaluated in this study.

Furthermore, although Matrigel provides essential biochemical cues, it lacks the dense collagen I-rich extracellular matrix and cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) that constitute major physical barriers to T cell infiltration [63]. Importantly, our platform was intentionally designed as a reductionist and controlled autologous system, integrating patient-derived tumor organoids with autologous T cells, to specifically evaluate antigen-dependent CAR-T cell responses against MSLN/PD-L1-expressing tumor cells. This approach allows precise functional interrogation of CAR-T cytotoxicity, infiltration, and persistence, while minimizing confounding effects from heterogeneous stromal components. Future refinements incorporating air-liquid interface (ALI) models, organ-on-chip systems, or humanized mouse models will be essential to further validate these findings and to determine whether the IL-6-sparing effects observed ex vivo translate into a reduced risk of CRS in vivo. Moreover, our PDO co-culture model shows stromal and vascular components that are known to influence CAR-T infiltration and persistence ex vivo. Future studies incorporating PD-L1 expressing cancer-associated fibroblasts may further refine this system to better reflect the tumor microenvironment [64]. Additionally, longitudinal studies using patient-derived xenograft models or humanized mice could validate whether the IL-6 sparing effects observed ex vivo translate into reduced CRS in vivo [65]. Due to the intrinsic heterogeneity of patient-derived T cells, CAR transduction efficiency varied among samples. Samples with extremely poor expansion or insufficient viable CAR-positive populations were excluded from downstream analyses. Remaining CAR-T products were evaluated under the same experimental conditions to preserve biological variability in this preclinical study [66]. Although no strict clinical-grade release criteria were applied, variability in CAR transduction efficiency among patient-derived T cell products was observed, likely reflecting inter-patient differences in T cell quality and manufacturing susceptibility. This heterogeneity represents a common challenge in autologous CAR-T cell production and should be considered when interpreting functional variability across samples. In summary, we report a conceptual and experimental framework for advancing MSLN- and PD-L1-directed CAR-T therapies in CRC. This study establishes a patient-derived CRC organoid platform that enables functional assessment of CAR-T cell responses across individual patients, providing a valuable tool for investigating inter-patient variability and supporting the development of personalized immunotherapy strategies. By leveraging PDO-based personalized screening, we demonstrated the feasibility of comprehensive platform CAR design to individual tumor antigen profiles, paving the way toward safer, more effective, and patient-specific cellular immunotherapies for solid tumors. These preclinical findings provide critical insight into strategies for modulating antitumor immune responses and mitigating cytokine-associated toxicity. Collectively, our results offer a strong rationale for the clinical evaluation of anti-MSLN-CAR5-T cells in patients with CRC to assess their therapeutic potential and translational clinical applicability.

Conclusions

In summary, the data presented here demonstrate the feasibility of a 14-day ex vivo CRC-PDOs/CAR-T platform as a powerful translational tool for rapid validation of tumor-associated antigens, efficient PDO generation, and personalized evaluation of CAR-T cell efficacy, thereby supporting precision immunotherapy development in solid tumors. We also demonstrated a robust and reproducible workflow for generating anti-MSLN-CAR5-T cells with superior functional activity compared to CAR4-T cells using autologous patients’ blood. These optimized manufacturing settings could be broadly applied to other CAR-T cell products targeting diverse tumor antigens and disease indications currently under development in our institute. Moreover, this approach provides a proof-of-practical framework for establishing academic cell-manufacturing facilities, thereby facilitating the local production of CAR-T cells and improving accessibility of advanced immunotherapies in developing countries.
