1 Introduction
Superconducting qubits are one of the leading architectures for developing fault-tolerant utility-
scale quantum computers. However, achieving fault tolerance with these devices requires significant
improvements to the fundamental operations which act as the building blocks of quantum algo-
rithms. While gate fidelities have steadily improved over time through improved qubit and control
pulse design, errors in qubit state readout remain relatively high.
 In improving the control of these qubits, many approaches were developed for designing high
fidelity control pulses. Earlier works focused on analytical methods, such as derivative removal
by adiabatic gate (DRAG) pulses [1], which introduced quadrature components and dynamic fre-
quency adjustments to counteract leakage and dephasing errors. Subsequent works expanded upon
these concepts to generalize the construction of these types of pulses [2]. Around the same time,
interest began to build in using optimization for control pulse design. Early implementations of
this process used simple methods like time-slicing to parameterize pulses [3], but such methods
require an extremely large number of tunable control parameters to design complex pulses. Later
efforts utilized more sophisticated parameterizations such as B-Splines [4] and other analytical
functions [5].
 While this direction was pursued extensively for designing optimal control pulses, relatively little
effort has been put into designing high fidelity pulses for qubit readout. The earliest efforts in this
domain applied drives with square envelopes, which led to relatively slow resonator occupation
during the pulse, and required a long reset time to empty the resonator after the readout before
successive gates could be applied. The first issue was addressed by using “two-step” pulses which
increased the magnitude of the beginning of the drive to more rapidly populate the resonator [6].
This simple idea was later generalized by shaping the end of the pulse to quickly evacuate the

While optimization techniques have previously been employed to select qubit parameters such as
frequencies and coupling strengths [8], thus far no numerical approaches for readout pulse shaping
have been explored to the authors’ knowledge. In this work, we employ a multiphysics Maxwell-
Schrödinger technique to implement such an approach.
 The remainder of this report is structured as follows. In Section 2, we detail the dispersive
readout process used for measuring the state of superconducting qubits and the key sources of
error in state identification. Then we introduce the Maxwell-Schrödinger numerical method in
Section 3 and detail the implementation used in this work. Procedures for calculating gradients
of the readout objective using this method are described in Section 5, where both forward and
adjoint differentiation techniques are described and validated. In Section 6, we use this technique
to design optimized readout pulses and demonstrate the improved performance. Finally, we discuss
our conclusions and future directions for this work in Section 7.

2 Dispersive readout
In this Section, we detail the dispersive readout process used to measure the states of supercon-
ducting qubits. We begin by analyzing a Hamiltonian model of a superconducting qubit coupled to
a microwave resonator, and describe the approximate model that can be used when the resonances
of these components are significantly detuned from one another. We then discuss how this model
can be embedded into a larger experimental device to read out the qubit state. We also introduce
the various sources of error that can occur during this process, such as distribution overlap, qubit
relaxation, measurement-induced qubit state transitions, and excessive resonator population.
 We begin by considering the system of Fig. 1, where the superconducting qubit is capacitively
coupled to a resonator, which itself is coupled to the electromagnetic environment. This system is
operated in the “dispersive regime,” meaning the detuning ∆ = ωr − ωq is large, where ωr and ωq
are the resonator and qubit transition frequencies. By “large,” we mean that this detuning must
be significantly larger than the coupling rate g between devices [9]. The interaction between these

dispersive regime can be violated in a variety of ways that cause (7) to become invalid. Some of
these mechanisms will be covered later on in this report.
 For this procedure to work, we need a way to measure the resonator frequency accurately
enough to distinguish the qubit state. This is typically done using a measurement setup pictured
in Fig. 2. In this setup, an arbitrary waveform generator (AWG) at room temperature is used to
generate a microwave tone to probe the resonator. The center frequency of this probe is typically
selected to fall halfway between the resonator frequencies with the qubit in |0⟩ and |1⟩ to maximize
the fidelity of this procedure [9]. The probe is often applied using a rectangular window function,
although slightly more sophisticated functions have been explored in prior studies [6, 7]. This pulse
is then attenuated through several stages before it reaches the low temperature environment of the
qubit, which is typically O(10) mK. This attenuation is needed to avoid heating and to reduce the
number of photons in the pulse to prevent errors due to measurement-induced transitions, which
will be discussed in more detail later in Appendix A.
 When this pulse interacts with the resonator, it experiences state-dependent phase and fre-
quency shifts. The remaining hardware in Fig. 2 is intended to allow these shifts to be detected
and used to identify the qubit state. While the pulse needs to be low-power to avoid errors, it
must be amplified to allow it to be read by standard electronic systems. Like the attenuation, this
amplification is performed in several stages of the dilution refrigerator. The first stage amplifier is
typically implemented using a quantum-limited amplifier such as a Josephson Parametric Ampli-
fier (JPA), which is intended to amplify the signal while adding a minimal amount of noise. The
noise requirements on successive stages are less severe, so standard amplifiers such as high electron
mobility transistors (HEMTs) and room temperature low-noise amplifiers (LNAs) can be used.
 Once the signal power has been increased, it needs to be shifted to a much lower frequency
so it can be converted to a digital signal. This is performed by first mixing the signal with a
sinusoid near the drive frequency fd . Typically an IQ mixer is used to produce in-phase (I) and
quadrature (Q) components by mixing with a cosine and sine respectively. Along with the desired
low-frequency components, this mixing process produces tones at 2fd , which must be removed

 4

by filtering. The remaining low-frequency signals are then integrated, which in an experiment is
typically performed digitally following an analog to digital converter (ADC). The final result is
a single pair of IQ values which capture the state-dependent phase and frequency shifts in the
signal. As a result, these IQ values will also be functions of the qubit state, and thus they can be
used to determine what the state is.
 Ideally, repeated measurements with a fixed qubit state would produce the exact same IQ
values so that the measurement is perfectly reproducible. In reality, there are several mechanisms
which can cause these points to vary and lead to errors in identifying the qubit state. As mentioned
briefly earlier, the readout tone includes noise generated from many sources, including the AWG
itself, the attenuators, loss sources in the qubit system, and the amplifiers (with noise in the JPA
being the dominant contributor). These noise sources lead to variations in the IQ values, causing
repetitive measurements to produce a distribution of points rather than a single repeated value.
If the distributions associated with the |0⟩ and |1⟩ states overlap, then the state will be identified
incorrectly, which we refer to here as “separation error.”
 The measurement can also produce an incorrect result if the qubit state changes in the middle of
the measurement. For example, the qubit may relax from |1⟩ to |0⟩ during the measurement due to
its limited coherence time, which we refer to as “T1 error.” Alternatively, the qubit may transition
due to the measurement pulse itself. While the model of (7) does not provide a mechanism for
this to occur, this model becomes invalid when the number of photons in the resonator gets large
enough (typically O(100)), allowing so-called “measurement-induced state transitions” (MIST)
[13], which is also referred to as “ionization” [14]. The underlying mechanism behind this effect
will be discussed in significant detail in Appendix A.
 Overall, the variety of these error mechanisms demonstrates the need for optimizing readout
pulses using a high-fidelity numerical method. To push the mean values of the IQ distributions
further apart and reduce separation errors, the readout pulse should be applied with high power
and for as long of a time as possible. But increasing the readout time increases the probability of T1
errors, and using high power pulses can lead to MIST. To balance these tradeoffs successfully, the
underlying simulation needs to capture each of these effects accurately while being efficient enough
for optimization to be feasible. These requirements lead us to select a Maxwell-Schrödinger method
for this purpose, which will be discussed in detail in the following section.

3 Maxwell-Schrödinger modeling
In this Section, we outline the formulation, discretization, and implementation of the Maxwell-
Schrödinger method used for readout optimization here. Because the formulation is described in
significant detail in [15], we provide an overview of the general procedure and the key results,
but do not work through the underlying math in detail. We employ a modified strategy for the
discretization scheme to produce a nonlinear system of equations, so additional details are included
to describe that process. Finally, we discuss the strategy used to implement the time-marching
equation produced by this method.

3.1 Formulation
The fully quantum modeling scheme addressed in the previous section suffers from a few drawbacks.
The most obvious is that incorporating resonator modes in the quantum model causes the size of the
Hilbert space to grow substantially, which can make simulations far more expensive. Further, this
approach is rather restrictive in the types of systems it can simulate. If the Hamiltonian dynamics
are evaluated directly, the system cannot be coupled to loss sources such as input and output
electronics, which prevents the readout dynamics from being simulated accurately. This can be

resolved by instead evaluating the dynamics of the Lindblad master equation, which allows decay
rates to be mapped onto each component to account for these effects [16]. However, this requires
evaluating density matrices at each timestep, squaring the size of the system and making the scaling
problem even more egregious. While alternative methods based on probabilistic techniques are a
popular choice to avoid this additional cost [17], they typically require hundreds of simulations to
fully resolve the underlying dynamics, giving them limited applicability.
 For this problem, we can leverage key aspects of the hardware configuration used for readout to
alter the formulation and make the simulation far less expensive. In particular, the pulses used to
drive the resonator are generated at room temperature from classical microwave circuitry, meaning
they are coherent drives rather than pure states. As a result, a classical approximation of the
resonator dynamics is fully capable of describing the dynamics of these drives. And as described in
Section 2, the qubit is operated in the dispersive regime, meaning the resonator is strongly detuned
from the qubit transition frequency. In this regime, detailed photon statistics are not necessary
to properly model interactions between these devices, meaning that the classical resonator model
would be sufficient to account for these effects as well. Due to these factors, a model consisting of
the qubit coupled to a classical resonator is sufficient to accurately simulate readout dynamics, as
demonstrated by the results of [15].
 One method of modeling this interaction could be using the lumped circuit of Fig. 1 and
treating the inductor and capacitor as classical components. However, this lumped element model
is a simplification of the actual electromagnetic system pictured in Fig. 3, where a coplanar
waveguide is used to form a distributed microwave resonator. To capture the physics of this type
of device more accurately, we use the finite element method (FEM) to spatially discretize this
type of system. In general, this type of approach could be used to directly model interactions
between the qubit and an arbitrary 3D geometry of a superconducting circuit. For this work, we
instead focus on 1D transmission lines to simplify the EM modeling challenges while proving out
the application.

3.3 Implementation
Due to the nonlinearity in {J(x(n+1) )}, a nonlinear solver is required to solve for {x(n+1) } in

solves the problem iteratively by solving a linearized system for successive guesses until the solver
converges. While Newton’s method can struggle to converge when the initial guess is far from the
solution, this is not a significant concern in a time-marching method as the solution from the prior
step is typically close enough to that of the current step for the solver to converge.
 While convergence is not a major problem, using Newton’s method does require forming and
solving several nonlinear systems at each timestep, which leads to high computational costs for
readout simulations with hundreds of thousands of timesteps. However, we can exploit some of
the structure of the problem to reduce this cost. For these simulations, most of the unknowns
are typically associated with flux on the transmission lines rather than the qubit state coefficients.
Only the flux at nodes coupled to the qubit is affected by the nonlinear terms at a given timestep,
meaning we can perform the nonlinear updates on a much smaller subset of the state variables.

3.4 Fitting to Measurements
In order to use the Maxwell-Schrödinger method to model an experimental system, we need to be
able to set the circuit and qubit parameters to match the results from measurements of that system.
If a 3D design file of the geometry is provided, many of these parameters can be extracted from
electromagnetic simulations using commercial tools or custom codes. However, experimentalists
working with superconducting qubits often do not have immediate access to this detailed geometric
information, so the parameters of the model need to be calculated from quantities that can be easily
measured. In this Section, we describe a process for performing this fitting.

4 Readout Cost Function
With the basic ideas of dispersive readout and the Maxwell-Schrödinger method established, we
now move towards using optimization to design readout pulses. In this section, we discuss how we
construct the cost function for optimization. First in Section 4.1, we discuss how we incorporate
the separation error due to overlap between IQ distributions, as established in Section 2. Then in
Section 4.2, we introduce our approach for accounting for errors due to having an excess of photons
at the end of the readout simulation. We note that in Section 2, we also discussed the need to
account for errors due to limited coherence times (T1 errors) and due to measurement-induced
state transitions. In the case of T1 errors, we do not include a term in the cost function because
we perform the optimization over a fixed total length in this work, so the T1 error is constant
with respect to the controls. In contrast, qubit state transitions are extremely dependent on the
controls as they occur due to excess resonator population. For this report, we unfortunately did not
have time to incorporate these errors into the cost function due to their complex nature. Instead,
the optimization here is performed using only the separation and resonator errors, and ideas for
incorporating errors due to state transitions are discussed in Appendix A.

4.1 Separation Error
As discussed in Section 2, separation errors are due to overlap in the distributions of IQ points
for each of the qubit computational states. Because these distributions are Gaussian, accurately
calculating these errors requires obtaining both the mean IQ values and their variances. In this
section, we describe the basic procedure for calculating both quantities, starting with the mean
values. We note that this procedure is originally discussed in [21].

would like to minimize the resonator population at the end of our simulations while maintaining a

5 Computing the gradient of the cost function
In order to use the cost function established in the prior section for gradient-based optimization, we
need to evaluate derivatives of (58) with respect to the controls. There are several approaches for
calculating the derivatives, with the simplest being using finite differencing. While this is extremely
straightforward to implement, it is inherently approximate and can require multiple simulations
per control variable to calculate the gradient with high accuracy. This cost can be reduced to
only a single simulation per control variable using forward differentiation, but this approach is still
quite expensive when the number of controls gets large. Instead, we use the adjoint method to
calculate the gradient, which makes the number of simulations needed to calculate the gradient
independent of the number of controls. As will be shown in Section 6, this allows us to optimize
over significantly more complex pulse shapes than would be possible with finite differencing or
forward differentiation.

tests we used to validate them.

 While we believe these results demonstrate the potential utility of this approach, there are

issues shown in Fig. 6 must be resolved to ensure longer simulations can be run while maintaining a

higher accuracy to be achieved with a larger timestep by eliminating oscillations in the solution,
resulting in a faster and more effective procedure.

As an example, the real-valued controls used in this work could instead be replaced by complex
quantities to allow the phase of the envelope to be shifted over time, which could potentially allow
pulses to eliminate spectral content at undesired frequencies [25]. Further, while this work used a
single carrier frequency, we could instead apply drives at multiple carrier frequencies to produce
similar cancellation effects and other unique phenomena. Overall, we feel that this method has
significant potential to improve readout fidelity, and there are a number of ways that continued
efforts in this direction could help realize that potential.

A Measurement induced state transition errors
In this section, we discuss the underlying mechanisms which lead to measurement-induced state
transitions (MIST), and how we can analyze this behavior to understand when these transitions are
occurring in the Maxwell-Schrödinger method. Further, we discuss some potential options for how
these errors could be incorporated into the cost function so they are accounted for in optimization.

A.1 MIST / Ionization
The underlying behavior which produces MIST is studied in significant detail in [14]. We note
that this effect is referred to as “ionization” throughout that work, but we elect to follow the
convention of [13] and refer to it as MIST. This is because these transitions do not necessarily
lead to a physical ionization of the qubit where the transition is to a state outside the potential
well. More often, the transition is to a much higher state that is still within the well, making
the ionization label somewhat misleading. Despite the difference in labeling, our initial discussion
of this topic will largely focus on the procedures discussed in [14], which we will connect to our
Maxwell-Schrödinger simulations later on.
 As discussed in Section 2, increasing the population in the resonator leads to shifts in the qubit
frequencies. While we only consider the shift in the transition frequency between computational
states in that section, a more rigorous approach is used in [14] to derive dispersive shifts for the

we assign a label |q, r⟩, where q is the qubit state, and r is the number of photons in the resonator.

the overlap is maximized, we assume this state is also in branch q, and label it as |q, 1⟩. Once these
states are labeled for all q, this process can be repeated for the next r, which can be iterated until
the desired behavior is captured.
 The results of the branch analysis employed in [14] are shown in Fig. 15. As the number of
resonator photons Nr is increased, it eventually leads to “branch swapping,” which will later be
shown to produce ionization. For example, at Nr = 84, there is a swap between branches 1 and 7,
which can lead to transitions into qubit state |7⟩. In the bottom plot, this is shown to be due to a
resonance between the two dressed states.

 To conclude, we briefly comment on how this system is solved numerically. Due to the time-
dependent Jacobian terms, the system cannot be pre-factorized before time-marching to solve
efficiently at each timestep. Instead, we use GMRES to iteratively evaluate {λ(n−1) } at each
timestep. To accelerate this computation, we remove the Jacobian terms and factorize the linear
system to build a preconditioner for GMRES. Additionally, the Jacobian terms themselves are
implemented using linear operators to avoid needing to explicitly construct these matrices at each
timestep.
