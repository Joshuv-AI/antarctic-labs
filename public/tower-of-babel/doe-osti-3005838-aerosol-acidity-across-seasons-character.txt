1.0 Summary
 The goal of this project is to leverage the Coast-Urban-Rural Atmospheric Gradient Experiment
 (CoURAGE) campaign in the Baltimore area to characterize key aerosol life cycle processes and
 thermodynamics, enabling us to answer the following questions:

 How do aerosol liquid water content (ALWC) and aerosol pH vary along spatial (rural-urban) and
 temporal (dynamic seasonal transition) gradients?

 How are aerosol processes (secondary organic aerosol [SOA] formation, cloud condensation nuclei
 [CCN], organic acid partitioning) in typical eastern U.S. areas impacted by these transitions and
 gradients?

 Currently, earth system and chemical transport models do not provide well-tested representation of the
 urban impact on atmospheric chemistry, nor can they provide reliable depiction of the urban impact on
 the surrounding environment in the future. To investigate and provide observational data to inform
 models, the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE) Atmospheric Radiation Measurement (ARM) User
 Facility mobile laboratory set up measurements in and around Baltimore during the Coast-Urban-Rural
 Atmospheric Gradient Experiment (CoURAGE), in conjunction with the DOE-supported Urban
 Integrated Field Laboratory (UIFL) Baltimore Social-Environmental Collaborative (BSEC) during the
 period from December 2024 to November 2025, providing one-year continuous, detailed measurements
 of chemical and physical properties of the urban and surrounding environments of Baltimore.

 A key objective of the DOE ARM CoURAGE study is to investigate to what amount the Baltimore
 atmospheric environment responds to influence from the surrounding environment versus, or in
 conjunction with, feedback and interactions with perturbations within the urban agglomeration around
 Baltimore. Questions being pursued during the DOE ARM CoURAGE study include:
 1. What is the dependence of Baltimore’s atmospheric environment (e.g., aerosols and atmospheric
 composition) on the surrounding surface-atmosphere interactions and atmospheric environments?
 2. What processes govern the differences in surface-atmospheric interactions that lead to significant
 gradients in atmospheric properties in multiple environments surrounding Baltimore?
 3. How well can our current numerical modeling systems reproduce these gradients and associated
 processes? Can we improve earth system models to simulate the Baltimore atmospheric environment
 with more fidelity?

 We addressed those questions through ~12 weeks of intensive ambient measurements at the ARM S2
 (Mt. Airy) site and BSEC site in support of the CoURAGE campaign. The measurements conducted
 included PM2.5 chemical composition with an aerosol chemical speciation monitor (ACSM; measurement
 provided by ARM), gas‐phase ammonia measured with an AiRRmonia analyzer and a Picarro cavity ring‐
 down spectroscopy SI2103 analyzer, small organic gases via proton transfer reaction‐mass spectrometry,
 particle‐phase organic acids using frequency response masking (FRM) filter samples and offline analysis
 by liquid chromatograph mass spectrometer (LC/MS), CCN concentrations using a DMT Instruments
 CCN counter (ARM), and trace gases using reference gas analyzers. The measurements will be used for
 modeling products to: (1) characterize organic aerosol sources using PMF analysis of the ACSM mass

 1

 spectra, (2) quantify ALWC and pH using the E‐AIM and ISORROPIA thermodynamic equilibrium
 models, and (3) analyze closure of the CCN budget using kappa‐Kohler theory.

 The measurement systems described above were deployed from 19 March 2025 to 16 June 2025. From all
 possible times across the 12‐week deployment, data completion was above 75% for most measurements.
 The following data products have already been processed and submitted to the ARM data repository:
 NH3 (g) concentrations from both the AiRRmonia and Picarro instruments, aerosol liquid water content,
 aerosol pH, and ACSM data. Because the time-of-flight (TOF)‐ACSM‐X, scanning mobility particle sizer
 (SMPS), and AE‐33 data are part of the DOE UIFL BSEC project, the final data has been posted to the
 BSEC data repository (the MSD‐Live data repository). We have linked the DOI for this to the ARM data.
 The CCNC data measured at the BSEC site have been collected, analyzed, processed for quality control,
 and uploaded to the ARM Data Archive. In addition, offline filter sampling has been collected, analyzed,
 and processed. This data will also be added to the ARM data repository in December.

 We planned to use the JHU mobile laboratory to do mobile sampling in between the urban and rural
 CoURAGE sites. The mobile sampling has been delayed due to several instruments being down in the
 mobile laboratory. These issues continued into the fall. The instruments should be repaired by the end of
 2025; therefore, we plan to conduct these drives in spring, 2026. However, these drives will be informed
 by the results from CoURAGE and the airborne observations of aerosol composition collected by the
 National Aeronautics and Space Administration (NASA) in the summer of 2025. The PM2.5 filter‐based
 analysis was delayed due to a three‐month period of inactivity at the shared mass spectral facilities at the
 University of Maryland, College Park. This has extended the analysis timeline considerably.
 Consequently, the expenditure of funds has been delayed. However, the mass spectrometer has been
 repaired and returned to service, which should allow for a return to the planned schedule in the next
 budget period.

 Although our efforts to date have focused on completing the field measurements and processing and
 archiving the data, we have begun detailed analysis of the data and we have already identified notable
 findings – see section 2.0 for details. One highly interesting and unexpected outcome was the frequent
 occurrence of fog at the rural site but not the urban site. Owing to the urban heat island (UHI) and the
 associated differences in temperature and relative humidity (RH), there are many events throughout the
 CoURAGE campaign when fog occurs at the rural site but not the urban site. This offers the opportunity
 to characterize effects of fog processing on the aerosol processes. Our analysis of this phenomenon is
 ongoing. We also have an unexpected opportunity to characterize NH3 (g) emissions associated with
 agricultural activity. The rural S2 site was located on a working farm where wheat and corn were being
 grown throughout the campaign. Our deployment began before the fields were prepared for planting (e.g.,
 before plowing, fertilizer application, etc.), but spanned these activities. We see clear signs of agricultural
 activity in the NH3 data set, especially during late April and early May when tractor activity was greatest.
 We are currently working to characterize the NH3 emissions under these different activities.

2.0 Results
 The major impacts on the principle discipline of the project will be realized in budget period 2 of this
 project. Activities in budget period 1 primarily involved the deployment of aerosol and gas‐phase
 measurements during the CoURAGE campaign. Our intensive operational period (IOP) lasted from
 March to June, 2025. We have processed most of the data and have submitted many of our data products.

 2

Other data products are in progress and will be submitted in December, 2025. We have carried out
thermodynamic equilibrium modeling to quantify aerosol liquid water content and aerosol pH — these
products have been published. We are working on the CCN closure modeling and the organic aerosol
factor analysis modeling as well, and expect these to be complete in the next one to two months. Our
efforts now shift to analyzing the incredibly rich data set we have collected. Our analysis to date, while
early, has identified some unique and impactful observations:
 • The UHI has a strong and clear diurnal signature in Baltimore, with maxima in temperature and RH
 differences at night and minima in the afternoon. The UHI also shows a strong seasonal pattern, with
 much larger temperature and RH gradients in summer and winter than spring and fall (Figure 1,
 below).

Figure 1. Average diurnal profile of the temperature difference between the urban CoURAGE site and
 the rural (S2) CoURAGE site from December 2024 through August 2025. In all seasons, the
 maximum temperature difference occurs at night and early morning hours, while minima
 always occur in the afternoon. There is a seasonal pattern, as well, with strong UHIs during
 summer and weaker UHI during spring.

 • The UHI leads to systematic differences in aerosol liquid water content that are best predicted by the
 urban‐rural temperature gradient. Maxima in the aerosol liquid water differences occur when RH is
 high (> 0.9) at the rural site.
 • Owing to the UHI and the associated differences in temperature and RH, there are many events
 throughout the CoURAGE campaign when fog occurs at the rural site but not the urban site. This
 offers the opportunity to characterize effects of fog processing on the aerosol distribution.
 • Aerosol composition is often similar at the urban and rural sites, but there are systematic differences
 during winter, including higher aerosol nitrate concentrations at the rural site. This is likely from the
 lower temperature and higher RH that favor HNO3 partitioning to the condensed phase.

 Figure 2. Average diurnal profiles of NH3 (g) (top) during June 2025, and aerosol pH (bottom) from

  • Biogenic emissions show a strong season cycle and contribution to aerosol processes (composition
 and CCN), with much stronger effects in late‐spring and summer.
  • Agricultural NH3 (g) emissions show a distinct temperature‐driven emission pathway, as well as

4.0 Lessons Learned
 The major lesson learned related to local activity at the Mt. Airy (S2) site during CoURAGE. The site was
 a working farm where wheat and corn were being grown. We deployed instrumentation from March to
 June, which coincided with intense farm activity to prepare the fields, plant the crops, and perform regular
 field applications (e.g., fertilizer, pesticides). The DOE camera at the S2 site regularly documented a
 tractor working the fields directly surrounding the ARM instrument containers. While intermittent, this
 activity had a significant impact on aerosol and gas-phase concentrations at the site. For example,
 Figure 3 below shows a time series of PM10 concentrations at the S2 site and the BSEC site in Baltimore.
 While the 1-hr-averaged concentrations show many periods where PM10 concentrations are correlated at

 5

the two sites, there are also clear periods where PM10 at the rural S2 site far exceeds that at the urban site,
in some cases by a factor of 4 to 10. Some of these PM10 spikes also exhibited NH3 (g) spikes. To date, we
have cataloged each hour of our deployment using the DOE camera data, NH3 (g) data, and rural/urban
PM10 ratio. We have categorized each hour as ‘probable local farm influence’, ‘unlikely local farm
influence’, and ‘unclear’. We shared these data at the CoURAGE team meeting in June 2025 so other
investigators were aware of the potential local source influence in the S2 data set.

Figure 3. Time series of PM10 at the rural S2 site and the BSEC site during CoURAGE. At times, PM10
 at the two sites is correlated, but there are also many times when PM10 at the rural site is 4-10
 times higher than it is a the urban site. Our analysis reveals that many of these times are due
 to local tractor activity on the farm surrounding the S2 site.
