Development and Validation of a Computer-Aided Diagnostic System for Colorectal Lesions Based on the JNET Classification: Supporting the “Resect and Discard” Strategy
Authors: Shin Morimoto, Shigeto Yoshida, Yongfei Wu, Tetsushi Koide, Toru Tamaki, Yudai Takehara, Yuki Kamigaichi, Hidenori Tanaka, Ken Yamashita, Yoshihiro Kishida, Toshio Kuwai, Shiro Oka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objectives: Although the Japan NBI Expert Team (JNET) classification for colorectal lesions is clinically useful, standardization remains challenging owing to endoscopist variabilities. Therefore, we developed a computer-aided diagnosis (CADx) system to support JNET diagnoses, potentially facilitating a “Resect and Discard” strategy. The CADx system's diagnostic performance and its potential to support the “Resect and Discard” strategy were compared with those of endoscopists of varying experience. Methods: Forty-three patients (60 lesions) who underwent CADx system colonoscopies at Hiroshima University Hospital between September 2022 and July 2024 were evaluated. Endoscopists were grouped by experience: beginners (100–500 cases, n=4), intermediates (1,000–2,000 cases, n=5), and experts (>2,000 cases, n=3). In Study 1, JNET classification concordance between the CADx system and beginner/intermediate endoscopists was compared, using expert diagnosis as the gold standard. Study 2 included 38 <10 mm lesions, with histopathology as the gold standard. Lesions excluding advanced neoplasia were classified as “Resect and Discard”. The CADx system and endoscopists were evaluated for identifying JNET Type 1 and 2A lesions as non-advanced neoplasia. Results: In Study 1, CADx-expert concordance was 83.3% (Kappa=0.75)—significantly higher than beginners (66.7%) and outperforming intermediates (78.0%). In Study 2, the CADx system's accuracy was 92.1%—significantly higher than beginners (70.4%) and outperforming intermediates (82.6%) and experts (78.9%). The CADx system's advanced neoplasia negative predictive value was 100%. Conclusions: The CADx system showed high concordance with expert diagnoses. For lesions <10 mm, its high advanced neoplasia diagnostic accuracy indicates its potential utility in supporting the “Resect and Discard” strategy.

Introduction

The Japan NBI Expert Team (JNET) classification, based on narrow-band imaging (NBI) magnification, is an essential diagnostic framework for colorectal neoplasms[1,2]. However, its widespread standardization remains challenging owing to interobserver variability among endoscopists[3,4]. With advancements in artificial intelligence, computer-aided diagnosis (CADx) systems for colorectal lesions have emerged, demonstrating high diagnostic accuracy[5-7]. As these technologies evolve, they may contribute to the standardization of diagnoses based on the JNET classification by reducing variability.

Beyond standardization, the CADx system is also expected to support the “Resect and Discard” strategy. Therefore, most available CADx systems are primarily designed to classify diminutive (≤5 mm) polyps as neoplastic or non-neoplastic[8-10]. While this is clinically valuable, endoscopic resection requires more detailed information, including histological subtypes and depth of invasion, which are critical for treatment decisions and surveillance according to the major guidelines[11-13]. Recognizing this limitation, a modified “Resect and Discard” approach has been proposed, using NBI magnification diagnosis and the JNET classification to identify small polyps (<10 mm) with advanced histological features[14]. A CADx system tailored for the JNET classification could optimize this strategy by improving the accuracy and reliability of endoscopic decision-making.

However, despite several commercially available CADx systems, none incorporate the JNET classification. Therefore, we developed a novel real-time CADx system to support JNET classification diagnoses. This study aimed to validate the performance of our CADx system by comparing it with endoscopists of varying experience levels, using expert diagnosis as the gold standard. Further, it aimed to assess the applicability of the CADx system to the “Resect and Discard” strategy, assessing its potential to enhance decision-making in colorectal lesion management.

Methods

Study design

This was a retrospective, single-center validation study conducted at Hiroshima University Hospital in collaboration with Kure Medical Center and Chugoku Cancer Center, the Research Institute for Semiconductor Engineering at Hiroshima University, and the Department of Computer Science at Nagoya Institute of Technology. The study was performed in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was registered with the Hiroshima University Ethics Committee (approval number E2011-0407). Written informed consent was obtained from all the enrolled patients.

Development of the CADx system and training sets

This study used 4,156 NBI images obtained from 409 colorectal lesions resected either endoscopically or surgically, 227 diminutive lesions not resected, and 1,571 images of normal colorectal mucosa. All images were collected from patients who visited Hiroshima University Hospital between January 2006 and December 2018 (Table S1). The scopes used to capture the training images were the CF-H260AZI, PCF-Q260AZI, and CF-HQ290AZI (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), and the light sources were the EVIS LUCERA ELITE video systems CV-260 and CV-290 (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

The region of interest in the image was identified by an expert in endoscopic diagnosis, and each image was labeled with a JNET classification diagnosed by two experts. The proposed CADx system comprises a two-stage architecture consisting of a segmentation model followed by a classification model, as shown in Figure 1, Supporting Video 1, and Supporting Video 2. The network architecture of the segmentation model adopted a combination of DenseNet (Dense Convolutional Network)[15] and U-Net[16]. After DenseNet extracted the lesion's features, U-Net functioned as a segmentation tool to create a mask that showed its shape and location. The model was trained using 512×512 images, with Binary Cross-Entropy (BCEloss) as the loss function and Adam[17] as the optimization method. To enhance the detection of small lesions without magnification, CutMix[18] was applied using normal mucosa, along with basic augmentations like flipping and rotation. Inception V3[19] was used as the model for the classification. According to the outputs of the segmentation model, the lesion was randomly cropped into multiple patches of different sizes, and all patches were resized to a uniform size of 224×224 pixels. The diagnostic result was determined by comprehensively considering the classification results of each patch.

Diagnostic flow from an endoscopic image to the final prediction using the CADx system.

Video for NBI diagnosis

The videos were recorded with NBI using high-definition colonoscopes (CF-H260AZI, CF-HQ290ZI, CF-XZ1200I, and CF-EZ1500DI; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and videoendoscopic systems (EVIS LUCERA ELITE system and EVIS X1 system; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). The NBI endoscopic videos of 60 lesions in 43 patients were used to conduct the test. These lesions were not consecutive cases; to avoid overrepresentation of Type 2A lesions, Type 2B and 3 lesions were deliberately included. Among the videos, 38 lesions of <10 mm size were used to evaluate the CADx system's performance of “Resect and Discard.”

Outcome measures and definitions

First, using expert diagnoses as the gold standard, the concordance of the JNET classification of the CADx system was compared to that of beginner and intermediate endoscopists. Second, using histopathology as the gold standard, lesions excluding advanced neoplasia were defined as “Resect and Discard” lesions. The performance of the CADx system and endoscopists in identifying JNET Type 1 and Type 2A lesions as non-advanced neoplasia was evaluated using 38 lesions of size <10 mm. Advanced neoplasia was defined as adenomas ≥10 mm, villous histology, high-grade dysplasia (classified as intramucosal carcinoma [Tis] in Japan), or invasive carcinoma.

Endoscopists were classified based on their colonoscopy experience: beginners (100–500 cases, n=4), intermediates (1,000–2,000 cases, n=5), and experts (>2,000 cases, n=3). The validation videos were interpreted by the same three expert endoscopists, who were independent of the CADx system's development and training process and were not involved in selecting the validation videos. The gold standard was established by consensus among these three experts, all of whom were blinded to the patients' clinical data and histopathological findings. The histopathological diagnosis of each lesion, according to the Japanese Society for Cancer of the Colon and Rectum Guidelines, was made by two pathologists at the Hiroshima University Hospital.

Statistical analyses

The CADx system's concordance rate was calculated with expert, beginner, and intermediate endoscopists for each JNET type. Pearson's chi-squared test was used to compare the concordance rate. In the performance evaluation test of the CADx system, the diagnostic agreement rate with the diagnosis of the experts was calculated. The diagnostic agreement rate between the CADx system and the experts was evaluated using Kappa statistics, as follows: Kappa <0.4, poor agreement; 0.41–0.60, moderate agreement; 0.61–0.80, good agreement; and >0.8, excellent agreement. Kappa statistics were calculated using the Fleiss method in R (Version 4.3.2; R Core Team, 2023; R: A Language and Environment for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria. URL: https://www.R-project.org/). The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy of the CADx system and endoscopists for advanced neoplasia were calculated. Pearson's chi-squared test was used to compare the NPV and accuracy. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05, and all statistical analyses were performed using JMP version 18 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

Tumor characteristics and JNET classifications

Forty-three patients with 60 lesions who underwent colonoscopies at Hiroshima University Hospital between September 2022 and July 2024 were enrolled. Table 1 presents the clinicopathological findings of the 60 lesions classified according to the JNET classification. The JNET classifications of the lesions were as follows: 12 lesions of Type 1, 25 lesions of Type 2A, 18 lesions of Type 2B, and five lesions of Type 3. The median lesion size was 7 mm (interquartile range: 5–12 mm). The lesions were predominantly located in the right-sided colon (58.3%), followed by the left-sided colon (25.0%) and the rectum (16.7%). Diagnostic concordance rate of the CADx system compared to endoscopists with various levels of experience

The diagnostic concordance rate between the CADx system and experts (gold standard) was 91.6% for Type 1, 96.0% for Type 2A, 55.5% for Type 2B, 100% for Type 3, and 83.3% overall. In comparison, the concordance rates for beginners were 62.5% for Type 1, 73.0% for Type 2A, 58.3% for Type 2B, 75.0% for Type 3, and 66.7% overall (Table 2). For intermediate endoscopists, the concordance rates were 85.0%, 84.0%, 66.7%, 72.0%, and 78.0%, respectively (Table 3). The overall concordance rate for the CADx system was significantly higher than that for beginners (P=0.02). Furthermore, the diagnostic agreement rate between the CADx system and the experts was high (Kappa statistic = 0.75).

Diagnostic capability of the CADx system for advanced neoplasia in <10 mm lesions compared to endoscopists with various levels of experience

The pathological characteristics of the 38 lesions included in this analysis were as follows: 4 SSLs, 32 LGD lesions, one high-grade dysplasia to T1a carcinoma lesion, and 1 T1b carcinoma lesion. Table 4 compares the sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and accuracy of the CADx system and endoscopists for advanced neoplasia in lesions <10 mm. The sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and accuracy of the CADx system for advanced neoplasia were 100% (95% CI, 34.2–100), 91.7% (78.2–97.1), 40.0% (11.8–76.9), 100% (89.6–100), and 92.1% (79.2–97.3), respectively. Discussion

This validation study revealed that our newly developed CADx system, based on the JNET classification, demonstrated a high concordance rate with expert diagnoses, significantly outperforming beginners. Furthermore, for lesions <10 mm, the system achieved high accuracy (92.1%) in identifying advanced neoplasia, with an NPV of 100%.

The newly developed CADx system showed a high concordance with expert diagnoses, achieving an agreement rate of 83.3%. It also demonstrated significantly higher diagnostic agreement than that of beginner endoscopists (66.7%) and outperformed intermediate endoscopists (78.0%). The JNET classification is a diagnostic method based on NBI. Pathologically, JNET Type 1 lesions include hyperplastic polyps (HPs) and SSLs; Type 2A includes LGD lesions; Type 2B includes HGD and SM-s carcinoma lesions; and Type 3 includes SM-d carcinoma lesions[20-22]. The utility of the JNET classification has been shown not only among experts but also among endoscopists with limited experience in NBI magnifying endoscopy[23-25]. However, variability in diagnostic accuracy among endoscopists remains a challenge. Furthermore, although CADx is expected to contribute to the standardization of JNET classification diagnoses, only a few CADx systems focusing on this classification have been reported. Our previous study demonstrated that the CADx system exhibited good diagnostic performance for JNET classification using still images, aiding non-expert endoscopists in diagnosing colorectal lesions[26]. The same results were obtained in this study using video data. We previously reported interobserver agreement rates by experience level, with Kappa values ranging from 0.78–0.85 for experts, 0.39 for non-experts, and 0.32 for beginners with no endoscopy experience[2]. Another study reported interobserver agreement rates with Kappa values among experts ranging from 0.66 to 0.74[27]. Additionally, a separate study found 99% agreement between two of three experts, while the complete concordance rate (defined as agreement among all three experts) was 73%. Interobserver variability was noted in distinguishing JNET Type 2A from Type 2B, with a Kappa value of 0.72 indicating interobserver agreement[28]. These findings suggest that our CADx system may serve as a reference equivalent to a single expert opinion. However, it is important to note that the diagnostic accuracy for JNET Type 2B lesions remains suboptimal, posing challenges even in clinical practice. This discrepancy may be attributed to the difference between endoscopists' and the CADx system's diagnoses of JNET Type 2B lesions. More than half of the cases (5/8) diagnosed as Type 2B by experts but classified as Type 2A by the CADx system were high-grade adenomas, as defined in Japan. To address this issue, further improvements to the CADx system are necessary, and we intend to enrich the training dataset with a greater number of JNET Type 2B cases in future studies to enhance performance in this category. However, even considering these limitations, the CADx system demonstrated high diagnostic accuracy and has the potential to contribute to the standardization of the JNET classification. It is important to emphasize that this CADx system is not designed to supplant or undermine the significance of established diagnostic techniques such as pit pattern analysis or endoscopic ultrasonography (EUS). Instead, the accurate identification of lesions classified as JNET Type 2B or 3 may enhance the quality of endoscopic practice by encouraging more comprehensive evaluations, including crystal violet staining and EUS.

Furthermore, our CADx system demonstrated a high diagnostic accuracy of 92.1% for advanced neoplasia of ≤10 mm, with an NPV of 100%. Takeuchi et al. have proposed a modified “Resect and Discard” strategy by recognizing micro-invasive cancers using magnifying endoscopy[14]. That is, for small (<10 mm) polyps, the following management strategies were applied based on lesion characteristics and locations: Polyps suspected to be non-neoplastic and located in the rectosigmoid colon were managed with a “leave in situ” approach. For lesions predicted to be non-advanced neoplasia, the “Resect and Discard” strategy was adopted without formal histopathological examination. However, non-neoplastic polyps in the right-sided or descending colon, lesions with advanced histology, including invasive cancer, or polyps with challenging lesion-type prediction were deemed to require formal histopathological evaluation and were therefore managed using the “Resect and Send” strategy. They reported that 85.7% of small polyps were managed without formal histopathology using this “Resect and Discard” strategy. In this study, using the idea that JNET Type 1 and 2A lesions correspond to lesions up to LGD, histopathological assessment was omitted for 86.8% of small polyps, while all advanced neoplasia were sent for histopathological evaluation. Although the proportion of advanced neoplasia, particularly invasive cancer, in lesions ≤10 mm is low, its prevalence ranges from 1.3–4.6% in 1–5 mm lesions and 7.9–12.5% in 6–9 mm lesions[29-31]. Invasive cancer is observed in 0–0.07% of 1–5 mm lesions and 0.12–0.49% of 6–9 mm lesions[32]. Failure to diagnose advanced adenomas may lead to an inappropriately prolonged surveillance interval, while failing to diagnose invasive carcinoma is unacceptable owing to the risk of metastasis and recurrence. It should be noted that the definition of advanced neoplasia in the present study was based on the Japanese pathological classification system. Despite international efforts toward standardization, including the recent WEO Delphi consensus[33], differences in the interpretation of high-grade dysplasia and intramucosal carcinoma remain between Japanese and Western practices, which may affect the interpretation and generalizability of our findings.

The ASGE Technology Committee, in its Preservation and Incorporation of Valuable Endoscopic Innovations (PIVI) statement, recommended the implementation of the “Resect and Discard” strategy if it reaches ≥ 90% agreement with histopathology in determining post-polypectomy surveillance intervals, and if NPV for non-neoplastic lesions reaches ≥ 90% for diminutive polyps[34]. However, recent meta-analyses indicated that CADx provides limited added value not only in the “leave in situ” strategy but also in the “Resect and Discard” strategy[35,36]. In contrast, our CADx system demonstrated an NPV of 100%, indicating sufficient data compared to the ASGE statement. Accordingly, in this study, our findings suggest that the CADx system may assist in distinguishing lesions appropriate for a modified “resect-and-discard” strategy, thereby potentially supporting treatment selection and surveillance decisions. Additionally, identifying non-advanced neoplasia not only supports the potential for a “Resect and Discard” strategy but may also assist in determining the feasibility of cold snare polypectomies. Enabling the identification of advanced neoplasia could become a new strength of CADx. Peng et al. have reported on a CADx system that showed considerable efficacy in differentiating non-neoplastic polyps, non-advanced adenomas, and advanced neoplasia in vitro[37]. Further advancements in this field are anticipated, which may enhance the clinical utility of CADx diagnostic systems. As this study was based on a relatively small sample size, larger multicenter studies are warranted to validate the findings.

This study had some limitations. First, this was a retrospective, single-arm (i.e., not comparative) study conducted at a single institution. Second, the number of lesions was limited, particularly for lesions ≤10 mm, and the study was not designed with a predefined sample size calculation to formally assess the modified “Resect and Discard” strategy, rendering this analysis exploratory in nature. Accordingly, the observed NPV of 100% should be interpreted with caution, as it was derived from a limited number of lesions ≤10 mm. Third, this study did not use data from real-world clinical practice, as cases were not consecutively enrolled but selectively included based on image quality sufficient for JNET classification and were intentionally balanced across JNET categories. Therefore, the diagnostic performance observed in this study may not fully reflect real-world prevalence or case distribution. Fourth, the histopathological assessment was performed at a single center, and potential interobserver variability among pathologists may have influenced the evaluation, thereby limiting generalizability. Fifth, because the training dataset included images acquired with a previous-generation endoscopic platform and a newer-generation system was used in part for validation, the influence of platform differences (i.e., potential domain shift) on the results cannot be ruled out. In addition, the CADx system was developed and trained using data from a single institution, and the validation was also conducted using data from the same institution, which may have introduced bias and limited generalizability. Accordingly, the performance of the CADx system may vary depending on institutional environments, endoscopic platforms, and imaging settings. Nevertheless, the results suggest the potential usefulness of the CADx system for JNET classification and identifying advanced neoplasia. A multicenter prospective study in real-world settings with a sufficient number of cases is warranted to validate these results.

In conclusion, our newly developed CADx system showed sufficient concordance with expert diagnoses. For lesions <10 mm, the system demonstrated favorable diagnostic accuracy for advanced neoplasia, suggesting the possible applicability of the modified “Resect and Discard” strategy. To further confirm these findings, a prospective multicenter study in real-world settings is currently being planned.
