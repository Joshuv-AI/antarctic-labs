Let
{\mathbb{N}}
be the set of positive integers and put
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}={\mathbb{N}}\cup\{0\}
. For
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and given subsets
\mathcal{A},\mathcal{M}
of
{\mathbb{N}}
, Canfield and Wilf
[
CW12
]
introduced the restricted integer partition function
p(n,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{M})
, defined as the number of representations of
n
in the form

n=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}m_{a}a,

where
m_{a}\in\mathcal{M}\cup\{0\}
for all
a\in\mathcal{A}
, and only finitely many
m_{a}
are nonzero. The function
p=p(n,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{M})
is said to have polynomial growth if there exist a constant
c>0
and an integer
n_{0}
such that
p(n,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{M})\leq n^{c}
for all
n\geq n_{0}
.

Answering a question of Canfield and Wilf
[
CW12
]
in a stronger form, Alon
[
Al12
]
proved that there are two explicit infinite sets
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{M}
of positive integers such that
p(n,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{M})=1
for all
n\geq 1
. For
\mathcal{A}=\{n!\}_{n\geq 1}
or for
\mathcal{A}=\{n^{n}\}_{n\geq 1}
, Alon further proved that there exist
n_{0}
and an infinite set
\mathcal{M}
of positive integers such that

0<p(n,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{M})<n^{8+o(1)}

for all
n>n_{0}
, thereby answering two further problems of Ljujić and Nathanson
[
LN12
]
in the affirmative. In 2016, via an elementary inductive argument, Dai and Chen
[
DC16
]
improved Alon’s exponent from
8+o(1)
to
2+o(1)
. More generally, Dai and Chen obtained a unified result for subsets
\mathcal{A}=\{1=a_{1}<a_{2}<\cdots\}
satisfying

c_{1}(n+1)^{\theta_{1}}a_{n}\leq a_{n+1}\leq c_{2}(n+1)^{\theta_{2}}a_{n},\quad n=1,2,3,\ldots,

where
c_{2}>c_{1}>0
and
\theta_{2}\geq\theta_{1}>0
are constants. This led them to the following interesting question
[
DC16
, Question 3.5]
.

Question 1

(Dai–Chen)
.

Do there exist two infinite sets
\mathcal{A}=\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty}
and
\mathcal{M}
of positive integers such that

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log a_{n+1}-\log a_{n}}{\log n}=+\infty,

p(n,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{M})>0
for all sufficiently large
n
, and
p
has polynomial growth?

In this note, we show that the answer to Question
1
is affirmative. Before proving this corollary, we establish a more general result that relates additive complements to Question
1
. Let
\mathscr{B}
and
\mathscr{S}
be subsets of
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. We call
\mathscr{B}
and
\mathscr{S}

complete additive complements
if every nonnegative integer can be represented as the sum of an element of
\mathscr{B}
and an element of
\mathscr{S}
, i.e.,
\mathscr{B}+\mathscr{S}={\mathbb{N}}_{0}
.
For a set
\mathcal{Y}\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
, let
\mathcal{Y}(x)
denote the number of elements of
\mathcal{Y}
not exceeding
x
.
Our main result is stated as follows.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathscr{B}
and
\mathscr{S}
be complete additive complements satisfying

\mathscr{B}(x)\mathscr{S}(x)\ll x.

Suppose that

\mathcal{A}=\big\{2^{b}:b\in\mathscr{B}\big\}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{M}=\bigg\{\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}2^{d}:\mathcal{D}\subset\mathscr{S},\ 0<|\mathcal{D}|<\infty\bigg\}.

Then
p(n,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{M})>0
for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
p
has polynomial growth.

Proof.

By the hypothesis of the theorem we have

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}+\mathscr{S}=\mathbb{N}_{0}.

(1)

We first show that
p(n,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{M})>0
for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
. By the binary expansion of
n
, there is a finite nonempty set
\mathcal{J}\subset{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
such that

n=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}2^{j}.

By (
1
), each
j\in\mathcal{J}
can be written as
j=s_{j}+b_{j}
, where
s_{j}\in\mathscr{S}
and
b_{j}\in\mathscr{B}
. Thus

\displaystyle n=\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}}2^{s_{j}}2^{b_{j}}.

(2)

Combining the terms in (
2
) with the same value of
b_{j}
, we get

\displaystyle n=\sum_{b\in\mathscr{B}}\Big(\sum_{j\in\mathcal{J}:\ b_{j}=b}2^{s_{j}}\Big)2^{b}=\sum_{a\in\mathcal{A}}m_{a}a.

For each fixed
b
, the corresponding
s_{j}
are distinct, because the
j
are distinct. Hence each nonzero inner sum belongs to
\mathcal{M}
. Therefore
m_{a}\in\mathcal{M}\cup\{0\}
for every
a\in\mathcal{A}
, and only finitely many
m_{a}
are nonzero.

It remains to show that
p
has polynomial growth. Let
n
be sufficiently large and put
N=\log n/\log 2
. Take an arbitrary representation counted by
p(n,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{M})
as

\displaystyle n=\sum_{b\in\mathscr{B}}m_{2^{b}}2^{b}.

(3)

For each
b
with
m_{2^{b}}\neq 0
, the uniqueness of binary expansion gives a unique finite nonempty set
\mathcal{D}_{b}\subset\mathscr{S}
such that

m_{2^{b}}=\sum_{s\in\mathcal{D}_{b}}2^{s}.

Thus, the representation (
3
) determines a finite set

\mathcal{P}=\big\{(s,b):b\in\mathscr{B},\ s\in\mathcal{D}_{b}\big\}\subseteq\mathscr{S}\times\mathscr{B}

such that

n=\sum_{(s,b)\in\mathcal{P}}2^{s+b}.

Since all summands are positive, each pair in
\mathcal{P}
satisfies
s+b\leq N
. Hence the map from representations to such sets
\mathcal{P}
is injective, and so

p(n,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{M})\leq 2^{f(N)},

where

f(N)=\#\big\{(s,b)\in\mathscr{S}\times\mathscr{B}:s+b\leq N\big\}.

Therefore, we have

\displaystyle f(N)\leq\mathscr{S}(N)\mathscr{B}(N)\leq c_{0}N=\frac{c_{0}}{\log 2}\log n,

(4)

where
c_{0}>0
is a constant. It then follows from (
4
) that

p(n,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{M})\leq 2^{\frac{c_{0}}{\log 2}\log n}\leq n^{c_{0}},

completing the proof that
p
has polynomial growth.
∎

Now, we turn back to Question
1
. We shall use the following result of Ruzsa
[
Ru72
]
on additive complements of powers of two.

Proposition 1

(Ruzsa, 1972)
.

There is a subset
\mathcal{S}\subset\mathbb{N}
with
\mathcal{S}(x)\ll x/\log x
such that every sufficiently large integer is of the form
2^{k}+s
, where
k\in\mathbb{N}
and
s\in\mathcal{S}
.

Corollary 1
.

The answer to Question
1
is affirmative.

Proof.

Let

\mathcal{B}=\{0\}\cup\big\{2^{k}:k=0,1,2,\ldots\big\}.

By Proposition
1
, after adding finitely many nonnegative integers to
\mathcal{S}
if necessary, there exists some
\mathcal{S}\subseteq{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
such that
\mathcal{S}(x)\ll x/\log x
and

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{N}_{0}.

Indeed, finitely many missing values may be put into
\mathcal{S}
, since
0\in\mathcal{B}
, and this does not affect the estimate
\mathcal{S}(x)\ll x/\log x
. Since
\mathcal{B}(x)\ll\log x
for
x\geq 2
, we have
\mathcal{B}(x)\mathcal{S}(x)\ll x
.

Let

\mathcal{A}=\big\{2^{b}:b\in\mathcal{B}\big\}:=\{a_{n}\}_{n=1}^{\infty},

where the elements of
\mathcal{A}
are listed in increasing order. Then
a_{1}=1
and
a_{n}=2^{2^{n-2}}
for every
n\geq 2
. Hence

\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{\log a_{n+1}-\log a_{n}}{\log n}=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{(2^{n-1}-2^{n-2})\log 2}{\log n}=+\infty.

Put

\mathcal{M}=\bigg\{\sum_{d\in\mathcal{D}}2^{d}:\mathcal{D}\subset\mathcal{S},\ 0<|\mathcal{D}|<\infty\bigg\}.

Moreover,
\mathcal{S}
is infinite, otherwise
\mathcal{B}+\mathcal{S}
would have counting function
O(\log x)
and could not be all of
{\mathbb{N}}_{0}
. Hence
\mathcal{M}
is infinite. By Theorem
1
,
p(n,\mathcal{A},\mathcal{M})>0
for every
n\in{\mathbb{N}}
and
p
has polynomial growth, completing the proof of Corollary
1
.
∎
