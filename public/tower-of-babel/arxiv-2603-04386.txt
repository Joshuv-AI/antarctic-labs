The Gaussian Wave for Graphs of Finite Cone Type
Theo McKenzie

Abstract
We show that for any infinite tree of finite cone type satisfying a mild expansion condition, the only typical process on its vertices with covariance induced by the Green’s function is the Gaussian wave. This generalizes a result of Backhausz and Szegedy, who proved this for the infinite regular tree of degree d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 . We do this by giving a reduction to a statement concerning the distribution of the inner product of our process with columns of the Green’s function, which in turn are straightforward to calculate. As a consequence, for random bipartite biregular graphs, the distribution of local neighborhoods of eigenvectors must approximate the Gaussian wave. Moreover, for generic configuration models including random lifts, the local distribution of a uniformly chosen eigenvector from any arbitrarily small spectral window likewise converges to the Gaussian wave.

Introduction

Berry’s random wave conjecture is a foundational prediction in the study of quantum chaotic systems
[
10
,
11
]
. It theorizes that the local statistics of eigenfunctions converge to those of a Gaussian random wave. The discrete version of this question is well studied, and large sparse graphs have long been used as a system of quantum chaos
[
32
]
. Whereas a general heuristic is that these graphs will have their spectral measure converge to their local weak limit, the idea of Berry’s conjecture is that, for chaotic models, we can move past this weak convergence to a stronger notion dealing with local statistics.

We will make this explicit through the
Green’s function
. Define
\mathbb{C}^{+}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Im[z]>0\}
and its closure
\overline{\mathbb{C}^{+}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\Im[z]\geq 0\}
. We define the Green’s function for a graph
\mathcal{G}
with adjacency operator
A_{\mathcal{G}}
and
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
as

G(z,\mathcal{G})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=[A_{\mathcal{G}}-z\mathds{I}]^{-1}.

We can then extend the definition to the real line by saying that for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
,

G(\lambda,\mathcal{G})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 0^{+}}G(\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta,\mathcal{G}).

If
\mathcal{G}
is a finite graph on vertex set
[N]
, we define the
Stieltjes transform

m(z,\mathcal{G})
to be the normalized trace of the Green’s function,
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{x\in[N]}G_{xx}
. We can think of this as the expected value of the diagonal entry of the Green’s function for a vertex selected uniformly at random. More generally, if
\mathcal{G}
is a
unimodular random graph
(see
[
2
]
for a thorough overview), we can take the expectation over the root of the graph. Therefore, we define the
Stieltjes transform of
\mathcal{G}
for
z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}^{+}}
to be

m(z,\mathcal{G})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\mathbb{E}_{o}[G(z,\mathcal{G})_{oo}],

where
\mathbb{E}_{o}[\cdot]
refers to the expectation over the randomly selected root according to the unimodular measure.

We say that
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
is in the
purely absolutely continuous spectrum
if

0<\Im(m(\lambda))<\infty.

(1.1)

Whereas point spectrum corresponds to localized states, the absolutely continuous spectrum corresponds to dynamical delocalization of the quantum operator (e.g.
[
1
, Theorem 2.6]
), so it is where we witness chaotic behavior of eigenfunctions from Berry’s conjecture.
The impressive work of Backhausz and Szegedy
[
8
]
proved that every eigenvector, besides the trivial eigenvector of eigenvalue
d
, has the local statistics of a Gaussian process on the infinite regular tree
\mathcal{T}_{d}
(see
1.4
). As the spectrum of the adjacency operator on the infinite regular tree is purely absolutely continuous, this gives evidence towards Berry’s conjecture.

The
d
-regular graph benefits from a key simplifying feature: its local weak limit is the infinite regular tree, which has a large automorphism group. This is due to it being vertex transitive, and, more strongly, distance regular (for any pair of paths of the same length, there is an automorphism sending one path to the other). Any limiting wave must respect this symmetry, and this invariance plays a decisive role in identifying and solving the associated formulas of a limiting process.

Nevertheless, there is no reason to expect the symmetry of the regular tree to be essential to chaotic behavior, and we prove a result toward Berry’s conjecture for a broader class of random graph models generalizing the random regular graph. Specifically, for a large family of configuration models—random graphs with a prescribed degree distribution—we show that eigenvectors exhibit Gaussian-type statistics, driven solely by local expansion properties. A principal innovation is a structural decomposition of the Gaussian process on the infinite tree via the Green’s function, which makes this mechanism transparent. The Gaussian wave can be realized as a Gaussian process obtained by pushing forward i.i.d. Gaussian noise through the resolvent. Namely, for
z=\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta\in\overline{\mathbb{C}^{+}}
, graph
\mathcal{T}
, and a family
\{\xi_{x}\}_{x\in V(\mathcal{T})}
of i.i.d. random variables with
\xi_{x}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, the Gaussian wave
\Psi^{z}(\mathcal{T})
(see
1.1
) admits the representation

\Psi^{z}(\mathcal{T})\overset{d}{=}\sqrt{\eta}\sum_{x\in V(\mathcal{T})}\xi_{x}\,G(z,\mathcal{T})_{x},

(1.2)

where
G(z,\mathcal{T})_{x}(\cdot)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=G(z,\mathcal{T})_{\cdot x}
denotes the column of the kernel of
G(z,\mathcal{T})
corresponding to
x\in V(\mathcal{T})
. To show any candidate distribution must be Gaussian, we examine its entropy, and one key insight is that to find the entropy of the Gaussian wave, it is sufficient to consider moments of the form

\{\langle\Psi^{z}(\mathcal{T}),G(z,\mathcal{T})_{x}\rangle\}_{x\in V(\mathcal{T})}.

Of course, because
\Psi^{z}
has the decomposition from (
1.2
), the above is easy to compute.

While the Green’s function representation of covariance is known (see
[
26
, Section 1.1]
), treating this decomposition as a primary analytical tool allows for a direct comparison between the cases
z\in\mathbb{C}^{+}
and
z\in\mathbb{R}
and provides a transparent framework for analyzing the entropy of the Gaussian wave. (
1.2
) can be compared to the heuristic related to Berry’s conjecture that on a hyperbolic manifold, the Fourier coefficients of the Poisson kernel should be i.i.d. Gaussian
[
6
,
35
]
. Indeed, we would expect this type of decomposition to hold in many models and to be useful in future work.

In order to precisely state our results, we give the following definition of an eigenvector process with covariance determined by the Green’s function. This was defined for the regular tree in
[
21
]
, but a more general definition is as follows.

Definition 1.1
.

An
eigenvector process

\Phi^{z}(\mathcal{T})
on the (potentially infinite) graph
\mathcal{T}
for
z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}^{+}}
, is a real-valued process on
V(\mathcal{T})
that is automorphism invariant, is centered, and for every
x,y\in V(\mathcal{T})
,
\mathbb{E}[\Phi^{z}(\mathcal{T})_{x}\Phi^{z}(\mathcal{T})_{y}]=\Im[G(z,\mathcal{T})_{xy}]
.

The
Gaussian wave

\Psi^{z}(\mathcal{T})
is the unique real valued Gaussian eigenvector process.

By the fact that
z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}^{+}}
, the imaginary part of the Green’s function is positive semidefinite, and therefore forms a valid covariance matrix. Moreover, (
1.2
) has the correct covariance by the Ward identity (
B.4
). For
z\in\mathbb{R}
, the Gaussian wave is nontrivial with finite variance if and only if
z
is in the purely absolutely continuous spectrum.

For families of finite random graphs, we will be interested in the Gaussian wave on their universal cover, defined as follows.

Definition 1.2
.

For any finite graph
\mathcal{G}
, its
universal cover

\mathcal{T}
is the tree whose vertices are nonbacktracking walks in
\mathcal{G}
, with adjacency given by one-step extensions of such walks.
There is a canonical projection
\pi:V(\mathcal{T})\to V(\mathcal{G})

that maps each walk to its endpoint in
\mathcal{G}
.
To obtain a unimodular random rooted tree, we select a root
o
uniformly at random from
V(\mathcal{G})
and consider the component of
\mathcal{T}
corresponding to walks starting at
o
.

A
labeling
of
\mathcal{G}
is a function
f:V(\mathcal{G})\to Y
, where
Y
is a separable metrizable topological space.
Such a labeling induces a labeling
\hat{f}:V(\mathcal{T})\to Y
on the universal cover by

\hat{f}(x)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=f(\pi(x)).

We denote by
\textnormal{distr}^{*}(f)
the law of this randomly rooted, labeled universal cover.

We study the weak limits of the above defined distributions, therefore, we define the Lévy-Prokhorov metric for the weak topology (see
[
19
, Section 11.3]
).

Definition 1.3
.

For
m\in\mathbb{N}
and Borel set
A\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we denote
A^{\epsilon}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{m}:\inf_{y\in A}|x-y|<\epsilon\}
. For two random vectors
X,Y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}
, we define the
Lévy-Prokhorov distance

d_{LP}(X,Y)
between
X
and
Y
as

d_{LP}(X,Y)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\inf\bigg\{\epsilon\geq 0:\mathbb{P}(X\in A)\leq\mathbb{P}(Y\in A^{\epsilon})+\epsilon\textnormal{ for all Borel sets }A\bigg\}.

(1.3)

For process
\Phi
on
\mathcal{T}
, normalized so that
\textnormal{Var}[\Phi]=1
, we measure its distance to the Gaussian wave
\Psi^{\lambda}(\mathcal{T})
for
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
k\in\mathbb{N}
through the quantity

\Xi_{k}^{\lambda}(\Phi)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\inf_{0\leq\sigma\leq\textnormal{Var}[\Psi^{\lambda}]^{-1/2}}\bigg\{d_{LP}(B_{k}(\Phi^{\lambda}),B_{k}(\sigma\Psi^{\lambda}))\bigg\},

(1.4)

where
B_{k}(\cdot)
gives the distribution of the ball of radius
k
around a randomly selected vertex of
\mathcal{T}
.

\Xi_{k}^{\lambda}
therefore takes the infimum of the Lévy-Prokhorov distance of
\Phi
to any multiple of
\Psi^{\lambda}
with variance at most 1. In fact, based on its definition,
\textnormal{Var}[\Psi^{z}]=\Im[m(z)]
. Utilizing the above definitions, we can state the main theorem of
[
8
]
.

Theorem 1.4

(Theorem 2.2 of
[
8
]
)
.

For any
d\geq 3
,
\epsilon>0
, and
k\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist
N_{0}\in\mathbb{N}
and
\delta>0
such that for all
N\geq N_{0}
, with probability at least
1-\epsilon
over the choice of a uniformly random
d
-regular graph
\mathcal{G}
on
N
vertices, the following holds: for every vector
\psi
with
\|\psi\|=1
, if there exists
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
with
\lambda\leq d-\epsilon
such that

\|(A_{\mathcal{G}}-\lambda)\psi\|\leq\delta
,
then

\Xi_{k}^{\lambda}\!\left(\textnormal{distr}^{*}(\sqrt{N}\psi)\right)<\epsilon.

By Friedman’s theorem
[
23
]
, a regular graph selected uniformly at random has, with high probability, all nontrivial eigenvalues with magnitude at most
2\sqrt{d-1}+o_{N}(1)
. Thus,
1.4
encompasses all almost-eigenvectors separated from the all-ones vector.

In this paper we consider a broader family of graphs.
Specifically, we study random graphs whose universal cover has
finite cone type
, which is a well studied class of models defined as follows.

Definition 1.5
.

For an infinite tree
\mathcal{T}
with oriented edge
(o,x)
, define the cone determined by
(o,x)
to be the connected component of
\mathcal{T}\backslash{o}
containing
x
, rooted at
x
.
The tree
\mathcal{T}
is said to have
finite cone type
if there are only finitely many isomorphism classes of cones created in this fashion.

To see the utility of this constraint, note that in general, the value of the Green’s function on a tree
\mathcal{T}
can be deduced from the Schur complement formula (see (
B.1
)),

G(z,\mathcal{T})_{oo}=(-z-\sum_{x\sim o}G(z,\mathcal{T}^{(o)})_{xx})^{-1};\qquad G(z,\mathcal{T}^{(o)})_{xx}=(-z-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\sim x\\
y\neq o\end{subarray}}G(z,\mathcal{T}^{(x)})_{yy})^{-1}.

(1.5)

Therefore, given such a tree, it is straightforward to find the behavior of the spectrum at any given
\lambda
. Specifically, for any tree of finite cone type
\mathcal{T}
, there is a finite discrete set
\mathfrak{D}\in\mathbb{R}
such that away from this set, the spectrum and Green’s function are well behaved (see
Lemma

B.3
).

This tractable analysis has led to this class of trees being a fruitful area of study. Initially, this closed form was used to analyze the random walk on these trees
[
30
,
31
]
. More recently, the Anderson model on these trees was shown to have purely absolutely continuous spectrum
[
28
,
29
]
and the eigenfunctions are known to be quantum ergodic
[
3
,
5
,
4
]
. Moreover, in many examples we can
quantify
the portion of the spectrum that is absolutely continuous, both in the specific settings of the the infinite regular tree and the infinite biregular tree, but also more generally
[
9
,
13
,
29
]
.

To create a family of graphs whose universal cover is a given deterministic tree, we use a generalized random model with a fixed set of vertex types. Instead of allowing an arbitrary degree sequence, we fix a finite number of types of vertices and prescribe how many edges each type sends to every other type.

Definition 1.6
.

For
k\in\mathbb{N}
fixed, a matrix
\mathbf{d}=\{\mathbf{d}_{ij}\}_{i,j=1}^{k}\in\mathbb{N}^{k\times k}
is called an
expanding degree matrix
if:

1.

when
\mathbf{d}_{ij}>0
, then
\mathbf{d}_{ji}>0
;

2.

for any
i,j,\ell\in[k]
, if
\mathbf{d}_{ji},\mathbf{d}_{\ell j},\mathbf{d}_{\ell i}\neq 0
, then

\frac{\mathbf{d}_{ij}}{\mathbf{d}_{ji}}\cdot\frac{\mathbf{d}_{j\ell}}{\mathbf{d}_{\ell j}}=\frac{\mathbf{d}_{i\ell}}{\mathbf{d}_{\ell i}};

3.

\mathbf{d}
is irreducible;

4.

\max_{i}\sum_{j}\mathbf{d}_{ij}\geq 3
and
\min_{i}\sum_{j}\mathbf{d}_{ij}\geq 2
.

For
\mathbf{d}
an expanding degree matrix, we denote by
\mathcal{G}(N,\mathbf{d})
the random graph ensemble created by partitioning the vertices into sets
V=V_{1}\sqcup V_{2}\sqcup\cdots\sqcup V_{k}
, then sampling a graph uniformly at random under the constraint that for every
i,j\in[k]
, the degree of every vertex
x\in V_{i}
to
V_{j}
is
\mathbf{d}_{ij}
.

We denote the universal cover of any graph
\mathcal{G}(N,\mathbf{d})
as
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}}
, and we denote by
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbf{d}}\subset\mathbb{R}
the minimal set such that for all
\lambda\in\textnormal{spec}(A_{\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}}})\backslash\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbf{d}}
, for every edge
(x,y)\in\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}}
,
|G_{xx}(\lambda)|,|G_{xx}^{(y)}(\lambda)|<\infty
, and
\Im[G_{xx}^{(y)}(\lambda)]>0
, where
\textnormal{spec}(A)
is the
spectrum
of
A
, that is the set of values
\lambda
where
(A-\lambda\mathds{I})
is not invertible. By
Lemma

B.3
,
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbf{d}}
is finite.

\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}}
has finite cone type, as the cone is completely determined by entry
\mathbf{d}_{ij}
corresponding to the chosen edge
(o,x)
. Also, the size of the sets
|V_{i}|
are completely determined by
\mathbf{d}
, as
|V_{i}|/|V_{j}|=\mathbf{d}_{ji}/\mathbf{d}_{ij}
when these entries are nonzero, and by assumption,
\mathbf{d}
is irreducible. In broad terms, our conditions on
\mathbf{d}
are as general as possible. The first two ensure that
\mathbf{d}
is compatible with a configuration model. The third guarantees that the resulting random graph is connected. The fourth imposes
local expansion
: in the universal cover, every vertex has infinitely many descendants, ensuring that the graph size tends to infinity and spectral chaos can occur. For instance, in the
d
-regular model we require
d\geq 3
to observe chaotic behavior; similarly, bipartite biregular graphs
\mathcal{G}(N,d_{1},d_{2})
fit into this framework under our constraints as long as
\max\{d_{1},d_{2}\}\geq 3
and
\min\{d_{1},d_{2}\}\geq 2
.

This framework contains several ubiquitous graph families. Important examples include the following:

(1)

Random
d
-regular graphs
are of the form
\mathcal{G}(N,d)
.

(2)

Random bipartite biregular graphs
have the form
V=V_{1}\sqcup V_{2}
and
\mathbf{d}=\left[\begin{array}[]{cc}0&d_{1}\\
d_{2}&0\end{array}\right]
. We denote this model by
\mathcal{G}(N,d_{1},d_{2})
, where
d_{1}
and
d_{2}
are the degrees of vertices in
V_{1}
and
V_{2}
, respectively.

(3)

Random lifts of a fixed graph
correspond to the special case where the matrix
\mathbf{d}
is the adjacency matrix of an undirected multigraph. The chaotic behavior of the spectrum in lift models is itself an active area of study
[
14
,
12
,
17
]
.

Although bipartite biregular graphs arise from a configuration model with fixed degrees, they do not arise as a sequence of lifts of a fixed graph when
\mathbf{d}_{ij}\neq\mathbf{d}_{ji}
. Their spectral statistics are well studied, and several general results on spectral chaos are known
[
15
,
20
,
36
]
, motivated in part by applications to coding theory and to the singular vectors of random
0
–
1
matrices.

For
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}}
of finite cone type, it is straightforward to characterize the set
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbf{d}}
, and in fact
\textnormal{spec}(A_{\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}}})\backslash\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbf{d}}
corresponds to a nontrivial portion of the spectrum for any
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}}
(see
[
13
, Section 1.6]
and
[
9
, Theorem 3.1]
). Intuitively,
\textnormal{spec}(A_{\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}}})\backslash\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbf{d}}

is where we would expect chaotic behavior, as it avoids the point spectrum, and also guarantees that spectral statistics on each subtree are nontrivial. For example, by
[
5
, Section 4.2]
,
\textnormal{spec}(A_{\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}}})\backslash\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbf{d}}
is exactly the region where we would expect quantum ergodicity, another statistic of eigenvector delocalization.

Our first main result is about the most general model. For this generality we give up control over individual eigenvectors and instead study a randomly selected eigenvector from a small spectral window.

Definition 1.7
.

Let
\mathcal{G}
be a graph with vertex set
[N]
, and let

\{\psi_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
be an orthonormal eigenbasis of
A_{\mathcal{G}}
with
eigenvalues
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i\in[N]}
. For
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
and
\epsilon>0
, choose an eigenvalue

\lambda_{i}
uniformly at random (with multiplicity) from

\textnormal{spec}(A_{\mathcal{G}})\cap[\lambda-\epsilon,\lambda+\epsilon]
, and let

\psi_{i}
be the associated unit eigenvector. Define the labeling

f^{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda,\epsilon}:=\sqrt{N}\,\psi_{i}.

Also, define the random almost eigenvector

\widetilde{\psi}_{\lambda,\epsilon}^{\mathcal{G}}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|\{\lambda_{i}\in[\lambda-\epsilon,\lambda+\epsilon]\}|}}\sum_{\lambda_{i}\in[\lambda-\epsilon,\lambda+\epsilon]}g_{i}\,\psi_{i},

where the
g_{i}
are independent standard Gaussian random variables.

Our first result is that the above distributions have Gaussian statistics.

Theorem 1.8
.

For any expanding degree matrix
\mathbf{d}
, any
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and any sequence
\epsilon_{N}=o_{N}(1)
that decreases sufficiently slowly, with probability
1-o_{N}(1)
over
\mathcal{G}\sim\mathcal{G}(N,\mathbf{d})
, the following holds: for every
\lambda
such that
\textnormal{spec}(A_{\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}}})\cap[\lambda-\epsilon_{N},\lambda+\epsilon_{N}]
avoids the finite set
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbf{d}}
, we have
\Xi_{k}^{\lambda}\!\left(\textnormal{distr}^{*}(f^{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda,\epsilon_{N}})\right)<\epsilon_{N}
.

The same conclusion holds if
f^{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda,\epsilon_{N}}
is replaced by the almost eigenvector
\sqrt{N}\,(\widetilde{\psi}_{\lambda,\epsilon}^{\mathcal{G}}+v_{N}),

where
v_{N}
is any sequence satisfying

\|v_{N}\|\leq\delta_{N}
for sufficiently small
\delta_{N}>0
.

Our result can be viewed as reasoning about the “noisy” distribution of an eigenvector: rather than studying a specific eigenvector
\psi
, we examine the distribution of an eigenvector sampled from an arbitrarily small spectral window. The uniform sampling may be replaced by any distribution that is sufficiently flat over the window (every eigenvector has probability
O(1/N)
for fixed
\epsilon
).

It is not necessarily true that in this generalized setting every
\lambda\in\textnormal{spec}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}})\backslash\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbf{d}}
has eigenvectors with Gaussian statistics. For example, if we take
\mathcal{G}(N,\mathbf{d})
to be a random lift, there can be planted eigenvalues with eigenvectors with the same distribution as one in the base graph. For example, every lift of the 4 clique
K_{4}
has an (unnormalized) eigenvector of eigenvalue
-1
where a quarter of entries are
3
, and three quarters of entries are
-1
, despite the fact that
\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbf{d}}=\emptyset
for
\mathbf{d}=d\geq 3
.

The fact that we must take an average over a window stems from the lack of symmetry in general trees of finite cone type: there is not enough structure to guarantee that the empirical covariance converges to that of the eigenvector process. To have such structure, it is sufficient to impose
radial symmetry
, meaning that if
o
is a root, then re-rooting the graph at vertices
x
and
y
yield isomorphic rooted graphs whenever
\mathrm{dist}(o,x)=\mathrm{dist}(o,y)
. This symmetry condition has enabled other sharp spectral results on infinite trees
[
29
]
. In particular, it holds for random biregular graphs, allowing us to prove an analogue of
1.4
.

Theorem 1.9
.

For any
\epsilon>0
,
k\in\mathbb{N}
, and degrees
(d_{1},d_{2})
with
d_{1}\geq 3
and
d_{2}\geq 2
, there exist
N_{0}
and
\delta>0
such that for all
N\geq N_{0}
, with probability at least
1-\epsilon
over the randomly selected bipartite graph
\mathcal{G}_{N}\sim\mathcal{G}(N,d_{1},d_{2})
, the following holds: for every
\psi
with
\|\psi\|=1
satisfying

\|(A_{\mathcal{G}_{N}}-\lambda)\psi\|\leq\delta
for
|\lambda|\leq\sqrt{d_{1}d_{2}}-\epsilon
and, if
d_{1}\neq d_{2}
,
|\lambda|\geq\epsilon
, we have

\Xi_{k}^{\lambda}\!\left(\textnormal{distr}^{*}(\sqrt{N}\psi)\right)<\epsilon.

Our condition on
\lambda
avoids the point spectrum at
0
and the trivial eigenvalues
\pm\sqrt{d_{1}d_{2}}
. In fact, by
[
15
, Theorem 4]
, our condition is equivalent to requiring that for
d_{1}\geq d_{2}
,

\sqrt{d_{1}-1}-\sqrt{d_{2}-1}-o_{N}(1)\leq|\lambda|\leq\sqrt{d_{1}-1}+\sqrt{d_{2}-1}+o_{N}(1)
. Moreover, the same idea yields simplifications in the proof of
1.4
.

Our work can be compared to
[
26
]
, where the authors show convergence to the Gaussian wave for the random regular graph in the specific case where the eigenvalue is one of the
k
smallest or
k
largest for fixed
k
(therefore making it a statement for eigenvalues satisfying
||\lambda|-2\sqrt{d-1}|=N^{-2/3+o_{N}(1)}
). In fact, they do not need to take the infimum over all
\sigma
in (
1.4
), but rather can deterministically set
\sigma=\textnormal{Var}(\Psi^{z})^{-1/2}
, thereby showing full
\ell_{2}
delocalization. They do this by applying the local law universality of the Green’s function for random regular graphs shown in
[
27
]
. In contrast, our approach does not use any local Green’s function law; instead, we show that the Gaussian process in (
1.2
) has an easily analyzable entropy for any tree.

We also remark that (
1.2
) immediately implies that the Gaussian wave is the limit of a linear factor of i.i.d. process for any graph
\mathcal{G}
and any
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
lying in the purely absolutely continuous spectrum of
A_{\mathcal{G}}
(by adjusting the variance of
\xi_{x}
, an analogous statement holds when
\lambda
is in the point spectrum). This extends
[
25
, Theorem 4]
, which established the result under the additional assumption of vertex transitivity as a tool to study the independence ratio on infinite graphs. We therefore expect (
1.2
) to be of independent interest.

1.1
Key entropy inequality

The key observation in
[
8
]
is that the empirical distribution of an eigenvector in the configuration model is a
typical process
, meaning that it defines a labeling which, in the weak topology, must be close to a labeling realized by a randomly selected graph, with high probability.

Definition 1.10
.

Let
Y
be a separable, metrizable topological space, and let
\mu
be a probability distribution on processes in
Y^{V(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}})}
. We say
\mu
is
typical
if there exists an increasing sequence
N
such that for
\mathcal{G}_{N}\sim\mathcal{G}(N,\mathbf{d})
, with probability
1
, there are labelings
f_{N}:V(\mathcal{G}_{N})\to Y
for which
\textnormal{distr}^{*}(f_{N})
converges to
\mu
in the weak topology.

Our main theorem is the following.

Theorem 1.11
.

For any expanding degree matrix
\mathbf{d}
and any
\lambda\in\textnormal{spec}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}})\backslash\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbf{d}}
, the only typical eigenvector process on
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}}
at
\lambda
is the Gaussian wave
\Psi^{\lambda}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}})
.

The reduction of

and

to

follows from

; for completeness, we reproduce the proof in

Preliminaries

2.1
Notation and conventions

We list notation that is used throughout the paper. We fix expanding degree matrix
\mathbf{d}
and the corresponding universal cover
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}}
. We denote by
V_{\mathbf{d}}
the vertex set of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}}
. For vertex
u\in V_{\mathbf{d}}
, we define
\delta_{u}
to be the indicator vector on the vertex
u
. For a set of vertices
\Gamma\subset V_{\mathbf{d}}
, we define the boundary
\partial\Gamma\subseteq\Gamma
to be the subset of vertices adjacent to
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}}\backslash\Gamma
.

For
z\in\overline{\mathbb{C}^{+}}
, in our analysis of the Green’s function
G^{z}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=G(z,\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}})
, we will often consider the function
G_{x}^{z}(y)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=G^{z}(y,x)
, which is the column of the kernel of
G^{z}
. More generally, we have
G^{z}_{\Gamma}\in\mathbb{C}^{V_{\mathbf{d}}\times|\Gamma|}
. We will drop the dependence on
z
when
z
is clear from the context. We now introduce notation to consider the probability measure on marginals.

Definition 2.1
.

For any separable, metrizable, topological space
Y
and probability distribution
\mu
on
Y^{V_{\mathbf{d}}}
, define
\mu_{\Gamma}
to be the marginal distribution on
\Gamma\subset V_{\mathbf{d}}
. Under the motivation of our decomposition (
1.2
), for the probability measure
\mu
associated with the process
\Phi^{\lambda}
, we then define
\mu^{\eta}
to be the probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{V_{\mathbf{d}}}
, where
u\in V_{\mathbf{d}}
is labeled with
-\eta\mathrm{i}\langle\Phi^{\lambda},G^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta}_{u}\rangle
.

Following
[
8
]
, we reduce the problem to an entropy inequality. Some care is required in defining entropy for the following reasons:

(1)

As any distribution must satisfy the eigenvector equation at every vertex, the distribution on a connected subgraph
\Gamma
with
\Gamma\backslash\partial\Gamma\neq\emptyset
is not full rank.

(2)

We will want an entropic inequality associated with an arbitrary distribution, whereas Shannon entropy is defined for discrete distributions, and differential entropy for smooth distributions.

For (1), rather than calculate entropy in the full ambient space, we project onto the intrinsic subspace. For (2), we discretize the process then take the Shannon entropy of the resulting discrete distribution. The discretization is performed on the full-rank
|\partial\Gamma|
-dimensional subspace rather than coordinatewise, so that entropy reflects the true dimension of the distribution. To do this, we consider a basis that satisfies the eigenvector equation at each vertex in the interior.

Definition 2.2

(Discretization at scale
a
)
.

For any
\Gamma\subset V_{\mathbf{d}}
, we fix an orthonormal basis
\Pi(\Gamma)=(\chi_{1},\ldots,\chi_{|\partial\Gamma|})
of vectors orthogonal to all vectors of the form
\lambda\delta_{u}-\sum_{v\sim u}\delta_{v}
for
u\in\Gamma\backslash\partial\Gamma
. Note the law of
\mu_{\Gamma}
is determined by

\big(\langle\Phi^{\lambda},\chi_{1}\rangle,\ldots,\langle\Phi^{\lambda},\chi_{|\partial\Gamma|}\rangle\big).

Therefore, for
a>0
, we define
\mu_{\Gamma,a}
to be the discrete probability measure of

\frac{1}{a}\big(\lfloor a\langle\Phi^{\lambda},\chi_{1}\rangle\rfloor,\ldots,\lfloor a\langle\Phi^{\lambda},\chi_{|\partial\Gamma|}\rangle\rfloor\big).

As the quantity we are chiefly interested in is entropy, which is invariant under unitary transformation, it does not matter which basis of vectors we choose.

Specifically, the quantity we will be most interested in is the difference of entropies of a star and an edge. Therefore we consider the star
C_{o}
, which is the union of the vertex
o\in V_{\mathbf{d}}
with its neighbors. We denote the edge between
o
and
i
as
e_{oi}
. For a discrete probability measure
\mu
, we consider the Shannon entropy
\mathbb{H}(\mu)=\mathbb{E}[-\log(\mu(X))]
, where
X
is a random variable distributed according to
\mu
. Then our comparison is, for any fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\Delta_{k}(\mu)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\lim_{a\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{o}[\mathbb{H}(\mu_{B_{k}(C_{o}),a})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\sim o}\mathbb{H}(\mu_{B_{k}(e_{oi}),a})],

(2.1)

where once again,
B_{k}(\Gamma)
is the ball of radius
k
around the set of vertices
\Gamma
. We will often specifically consider
\Delta_{0}(\mu)=\lim_{a\rightarrow\infty}\mathbb{E}_{o}[\mathbb{H}(\mu_{C_{o},a})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\sim o}\mathbb{H}(\mu_{e_{oi},a})]
.

We have the relation (see
[
18
, Theorem 8.3.1]
) that for any law
\mu
with smooth density,

\mathbb{H}(\mu_{\Gamma,a})=|\partial\Gamma|\log a+\mathbb{D}(\mu_{\Pi(\Gamma)})+o_{a}(1).

(2.2)

where
\mathbb{D}(\mu)\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{|\partial\Gamma|}}f_{\mu_{\Pi(\Gamma)}}(x)\log f_{\mu_{\Pi(\Gamma)}}(x){\rm d}x
for density function
f_{\mu_{\Pi(\Gamma)}}
corresponding to
\mu_{\Pi(\Gamma)}
, is the differential entropy on the
|\partial\Gamma|
dimensional subspace orthogonal to vectors of the form
\lambda\delta_{u}-\sum_{v\sim u}\delta_{v}
.

Therefore, when
\mu
is the law of a smooth eigenvector process, we can equivalently write

\Delta_{k}(\mu)=\mathbb{E}_{o}[\mathbb{D}(\mu_{\Pi(B_{k}(C_{o}))})]-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\sim o}\mathbb{D}(\mu_{\Pi(B_{k}(e_{oi}))})],

(2.3)

using that the
\log a
terms cancel out exactly.
This will be useful specifically when we consider either the Gaussian wave or an eigenvector process smoothed by adding a Gaussian wave component to another eigenvector process.

2.2
Proof of
1.11

In this subsection, we prove
1.11
under the assumption of a handful of propositions. In the later sections, we will prove the individual propositions referenced in this section.

To start, our way of quantifying typicality is through the following generalization of
[
7
, Theorem 4]
.

Proposition 2.3
.

Recall the definition of
\Delta_{k}
from (
2.1
). Let
Y
be a separable, metrizable topological space, and let
\mu
be a probability distribution on processes in
Y^{V(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}})}
. If
\mu
is typical, then

\Delta_{0}(\mu)\geq 0.

(2.4)

Our restrictions on
Y
are loose enough for
2.3
to imply
\Delta_{k}(\mu)\geq 0
for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
, as we can label vertex
u
with the vector of labels in
B_{k}(u)
, meaning the entropy on a single vertex is that of the entire ball in the original process.
The next step is to show that for the Gaussian wave, this inequality is an equality.
One key observation is that for any
\ell_{2}
vector
\chi\in\mathbb{R}^{V(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}})}
, any eigenvector process
\Phi^{\lambda}
, and any
\eta
,

\langle\Phi^{\lambda},G^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta}\chi\rangle=-\mathrm{i}\eta^{-1}\langle\Phi^{\lambda},\chi\rangle.

(2.5)

This means that for any set of vectors
\chi_{1},\ldots\chi_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{V(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}})}
, the joint distribution of the random variables

\left\{-\mathrm{i}\eta\langle\Phi^{\lambda},G^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta}{\chi_{i}}\rangle\right\}_{i\in[k]}.

(2.6)

does not depend on
\eta
. Therefore, as we apply a fixed linear transform, for any eigenvector processes with measures
\mu
and
\nu
,

\Delta_{0}(\mu^{\eta})-\Delta_{0}(\nu^{\eta})=\Delta_{0}(\mu)-\Delta_{0}(\nu).

(2.7)

The advantage of considering different
\eta
is that the inner product of the vectors
G^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta}\chi
changes with
\eta
, adjusting the entropy of (
2.6
). Specifically, we show the following, which is one of the main novelties of our work.

Proposition 2.4
.

For the Gaussian wave
\Psi^{\lambda}
with probability measure
\mu
, recall that the probability measure
\mu^{\eta}
on
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}}
for the distribution where the value at vertex
u
is
-\mathrm{i}\eta\langle\Psi^{\lambda},G_{u}^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta}\rangle
. Then for any
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\Delta_{k}(\mu^{\eta})=0.

(2.8)

In fact, this proof is general enough that
\Delta
could be alternatively defined to take the difference in entropy of any two sets of the same boundary size. Next, we show the Gaussian is the unique maximizer of the differential entropy.

Proposition 2.5
.

\Delta_{0}(\mu)
is uniquely maximized over all eigenvector processes by
\mu
the probability measure corresponding to
\Psi^{\lambda}
the Gaussian wave.

To do this, we adapt the general argument of
[
8
, Section 11]
, where we show that heating the random variable increases
\Delta_{0}
for any other distribution. However, because there is much less symmetry, there are some required novelties. In the previous result, the authors use the de Bruijn identity (see
Lemma

5.2
) with an unknown basis, then deduce properties of the basis using symmetries of the graph. In this work, we find a canonical basis based on the Green’s function that is well behaved and works for any configuration model.

The final piece is to extrapolate from knowing that the Gaussian is the unique maximizer of
\Delta_{0}
to the unique maximizer on
\Delta_{k}
for all
k
. For this, we prove the following. This is the identical argument as in
[
8
, Section 10]
for regular graphs, but we include the proof for completeness in
Section

A.3
.

Lemma 2.6
.

For any probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{V_{\mathbf{d}}}
, we have

\Delta_{k}(\mu)\leq\Delta_{k-1}(\mu).

We have equality for every
k\in\mathbb{N}
if and only if the distribution is
2-Markov
, meaning that conditioned on any edge, the distributions on the two sides of the edge are independent.

Proof of
1.11
.

Consider a typical eigenvector process
\Phi^{\lambda}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}})
with probability measure
\mu
. For each
\eta
and
k
,
\mu_{B_{k}}^{\eta}
, the process where we label vertex
u
with the vector
\{-\mathrm{i}\eta\langle\Phi^{\lambda},G^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta}_{w}\rangle\}_{w\in B_{k}(u)}
, is typical.
Therefore, by
2.3
,
\Delta_{k}(\mu^{\eta})\geq 0
for all
k\geq 0
and
\eta\geq 0
. Therefore, combining
2.4
,
2.5
, and (
2.7
), the marginals on stars of
\Phi^{\lambda}
are Gaussian. If the distribution is 2-Markov, then the marginals on stars defines the entire distribution, and it is the Gaussian wave. Now, assume our measure
\mu
is not 2-Markov. We compare it to a measure
\nu
, which is the Gaussian wave. Then there exists some fixed radius
k
on which the marginals on the balls of radius
k
differ, so by
Lemma

2.6
,
\Delta_{k}(\mu^{\eta})<\Delta_{k}(\nu^{\eta})
for all
\eta\geq 0
. Using (
2.5
),
2.3
, and
2.4
again, we recover that
\mu
is not typical.
∎

The remainder of the paper is devoted to proving the propositions introduced in this section. In
Section

3
, we explain how typical processes can be characterized through their marginals, as in
2.3
. This is a straightforward extension of
[
7
, Theorem 5]
, but may give some intuition to those who are not familiar with the topic. In

, we prove

using elementary properties of the Green’s function. Finally, in

we establish

by analyzing the heated random variable through a well-chosen basis and more properties of the Green’s function.

3
Entropic identities for typical processes

For a discrete measure
\mu
, if there is exists a labeling of the vertices of a graph for which the distribution over vertices is
\mu
, then the number of ways to label a vertex set in such a way that the empirical distribution of vertex labels on
N
vertices is
\mu
is
{\rm e}^{(1+o_{N}(1))N\mathbb{H}(\mu)}
.
Along with labelings of vertices, we now wish to consider labelings on connected pairs
(i,j)
. For edges from
V_{i}
to itself, this is done previously.

Lemma 3.1

(Lemma 4.1 in
[
7
]
)
.

Let
V
and
S
be finite sets and
|V|=N
. Let
\mu_{e}
be a probability distribution on
S\times S
with one dimensional marginal measure
\mu
in both coordates. Define
M_{f}
to be the set of perfect matchings such that the distribution across random edges is
\mu_{e}
. If
M_{f}
is non-empty, it satisfies

|M_{f}|=(N!!){\rm e}^{(1+o_{N}(1))N(\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{H}(\mu_{e})-\mathbb{H}(\mu))}.

We now extend this result to a corresponding bipartite version.

Lemma 3.2

(Bipartite version)
.

Consider two finite vertex sets
|V_{1}|=|V_{2}|=N
, and
\mu_{e}
a probability distribution on
S\times S
. Define
\mu_{1}
and
\mu_{2}
to be the marginals on the first and second coordinates with respective distributions
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
. Define
M_{f_{1}f_{2}}
to be the set of perfect matchings from
V_{1}
to
V_{2}
for which, given a labeled graph, the marginal on edges is
\mu_{e}
. If
M_{f_{1}f_{2}}
is non-empty, then

|M_{f_{1}f_{2}}|=(N!){\rm e}^{(1+o_{N}(1))N(\mathbb{H}(\mu_{e})-(\mathbb{H}(\mu_{1})+\mathbb{H}(\mu_{2})))}.

Proof.

Consider the set
M^{\prime}
of labelings of perfect matchings that satisfy
\mu_{e}
. We will count
|M^{\prime}|
in two ways. In the first way, label with marginals
f_{1}
and
f_{2}
, then match edges. Under this counting, we can see
|M^{\prime}|=|M_{f_{1}f_{2}}|{\rm e}^{(1+o_{N}(1))N(\mathbb{H}(\mu_{1})+\mathbb{H}(\mu_{2}))}
.

The second way to count is that there are
N!
perfect matchings. Given a perfect matching, counting the number of viable labelings gives
|M^{\prime}|=(N!){\rm e}^{(1+o_{N}(1))N\mathbb{H}(\mu_{e})}
. Combining these two equalities gives the result.
∎

We can now use this to compare a star to edges.

Proof of
2.3
.

Consider a labeling
f:\vec{E}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}})\to S\times S
, where
\vec{E}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}})
is the set of half-edges of
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}}
. We require that if two half-edges
\vec{e_{1}}
and
\vec{e_{2}}
originate at the same vertex, then
f(\vec{e_{1}})
and
f(\vec{e_{2}})
share the same first coordinate.

We construct the configuration model by matching half-edges so that a half-edge of type
i\to j
with label
(s_{1},s_{2})
is paired with a half-edge of type
j\to i
labeled
(s_{2},s_{1})
. Define
\mathcal{G}_{\epsilon}(N,\mathbf{d},\mu)
to be the set of labeled graphs such that the star and edge marginals are within
\epsilon
in total variation of
\mu_{C_{i}}
and
\mu_{e_{ij}}
for all pairs
i,j
. We compare
|\mathcal{G}_{\epsilon}(N,\mathbf{d},\mu)|
with the total number of graphs in the configuration model,
|\mathcal{G}(N,\mathbf{d})|
. This is within a constant factor of the total number of simple graphs (see
[
34
, Theorem 2.2]
). Define
q_{i}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{|V_{i}|}{N}
to be the fraction of vertices of type
i
, we have

|\mathcal{G}(N,\mathbf{d})|=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\frac{(\mathbf{d}_{ii}q_{i}N)!!}{(\mathbf{d}_{ii}!)^{q_{i}N}}\right)\left(\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}\frac{(\mathbf{d}_{ij}q_{i}N)!}{(\mathbf{d}_{ij}!)^{q_{i}N}(\mathbf{d}_{ji}!)^{q_{j}N}}\right),

(3.1)

obtained by matching all half-edges while accounting for those of the same type.

We can construct a graph in
\mathcal{G}_{\epsilon}(N,\mathbf{d},\mu)
by first randomly sampling the labelings such that they have the correct distribution on stars, for which there are
{\rm e}^{(1+o_{N}(1))\,N\sum_{i}q_{i}\mathbb{H}(\mu_{C_{i}})}
. We then match edges between
V_{i}
and
V_{j}
in such a way that respects the matching. Since edges of type
i\to j
are independent of edges between other type pairs, we may compute
|\mathcal{G}_{\epsilon}(N,\mathbf{d},\mu)|
by evaluating, for each
(i,j)
,
|M_{ij}|
, the number of matchings within
\epsilon
of
\mu_{e_{ij}}
. Because the entropy of a half-edge equals that of the union of a half-edge with its paired reverse edge, we apply

Lemma

3.1
to obtain

|M_{ii}|=\frac{(\mathbf{d}_{ii}q_{i}N)!!\,{\rm e}^{(1+o(1))\mathbb{H}(\mu_{e_{ii}})\,\mathbf{d}_{ii}q_{i}N/2}}{(\mathbf{d}_{ii}!)^{q_{i}N}\,{\rm e}^{\mathbb{H}(\mu_{e_{ii}})\,\mathbf{d}_{ii}q_{i}N}},

where
o(1)
is a term that goes to zero when we send
N
to infinity, then
\epsilon
to 0. We have divided by
(\mathbf{d}_{ii}!)^{q_{i}N}
because we do not distinguish edges that stem from the same vertex. Similarly, by
Lemma

3.2
,

|M_{ij}|=\frac{(\mathbf{d}_{ij}q_{i}N)!\,{\rm e}^{(1+o(1))\mathbb{H}(\mu_{e_{ij}})\,\mathbf{d}_{ij}q_{i}N}}{(\mathbf{d}_{ij}!)^{q_{i}N}(\mathbf{d}_{ji}!)^{q_{j}N}\,{\rm e}^{(\mathbf{d}_{ij}q_{i}+\mathbf{d}_{ji}q_{j})N\,\mathbb{H}(\mu_{e_{ij}})}}.

Therefore, to estimate
\mathcal{G}_{\epsilon}(N,\mathbf{d},\mu)
, we have

\displaystyle\begin{split}|\mathcal{G}_{\epsilon}(N,\mathbf{d},\mu)|&=\exp\left((1+o(1))\,N\sum_{i}q_{i}\mathbb{H}(\mu_{C_{i}})\right)\left(\prod_{1\leq i\leq k}|M_{ii}|\right)\left(\prod_{1\leq i<j\leq k}|M_{ij}|\right)\\[3.00003pt]
&=\exp\!\left((1+o(1))\,N\sum_{i}q_{i}\Big(\mathbb{H}(\mu_{C_{i}})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}\mathbf{d}_{ij}\mathbb{H}(\mu_{e_{ij}})\Big)\right)\,|\mathcal{G}(N,\mathbf{d})|,\end{split}

(3.2)

where in the last line, we use (
3.1
).

Since
\mu
is typical, for any
\epsilon>0
we have

\limsup_{N\to\infty}\frac{|\mathcal{G}_{\epsilon}(N,\mathbf{d},\mu)|}{|\mathcal{G}(N,\mathbf{d})|}\geq c_{\mathbf{d}},

(3.3)

for
c_{\mathbf{d}}\geq\exp[(1-(\max_{i}\{\sum_{j}\mathbf{d}_{ij}\})^{2})/4]
the probability a graph sampled from the configuration model is simple. Combining (
3.3
) with (
3.2
) yields (
2.4
).

∎

4
Entropy of the Gaussian wave

We first need to confirm the rank of the covariance matrix is indeed the size of the boundary. This will also be a warm-up for the analysis to come in
Section

5
.

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
\Gamma\in\{B_{k}(C),B_{k}(e)\}_{k\in\mathbb{N}}
, the rank of
\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\Im(G_{\Gamma}^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta})
is
|\partial\Gamma|
.

Proof.

For any
\eta\geq 0
,
\Im[G_{\Gamma}^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta}]
is the Gram matrix of
\{\sqrt{\eta}G_{x}^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta}\}_{x\in\Gamma}
, by (
B.4
). Therefore, it is sufficient to lower bound the minimum singular value of this latter matrix.
We construct an explicit linear transformation giving

|\partial\Gamma|
orthogonal vectors.
Let
x\in\partial\Gamma
and let
y\in\Gamma
denote its parent.
By the Schur complement formula (
B.2
),

G_{x}^{(y)}=G_{x}-\frac{G_{xy}}{G_{yy}}\,G_{y},

(4.1)

where
G^{(y)}=[A_{\mathcal{T}^{(y)}}-z\mathds{I}]^{-1}
is the Green’s function of the graph with
y
removed.

If
x_{1}\neq x_{2}
are distinct vertices of
\partial\Gamma
with respective
parents
y_{1},y_{2}\in\Gamma
, then the vectors

G_{x_{1}}^{(y_{1})}
and
G_{x_{2}}^{(y_{2})}
are supported on disjoint forward cones,
hence are orthogonal. Therefore,
\{\sqrt{\eta}\,G_{x}^{(y)}\}_{x\in\partial\Gamma}

is an orthogonal family of size
|\partial\Gamma|
, and thus linearly
independent for every
\eta>0
.

Define a linear operator
T
by prescribing its rows as
(TG_{\Gamma})_{x}=G_{x}-\frac{G_{xy}}{G_{yy}}G_{y}
for
x\in\partial\Gamma
,
so that
T(\sqrt{\eta}\,G_{\Gamma})=\{\sqrt{\eta}\,G_{x}^{(y)}\}_{x\in\partial\Gamma}
.
Applying the singular value inequality

\sigma_{\min}(M)\geq\sigma_{\min}(TM)/\|T\|
yields

\sigma_{|\partial\Gamma|}(\sqrt{\eta}\,G_{\Gamma})\;\geq\;\frac{\min_{x\in\partial\Gamma}\Im[G_{xx}^{(y)}]}{\|T\|},

where we used that
\|\sqrt{\eta}\,G_{x}^{(y)}\|^{2}=\Im[G_{xx}^{(y)}]
.
As
\lambda\notin\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbf{d}}
, each quantity

\Im[G_{xx}^{(y)}]
admits a finite positive limit as
\eta\to 0^{+}
.
Moreover,
\|T\|
remains uniformly bounded in this limit since it
depends only on ratios
G_{xy}/G_{yy}
, which also converge. Therefore, the smallest nonzero singular value of
\sqrt{\eta}\,G_{\Gamma}

remains bounded away from zero as
\eta\to 0^{+}
.
∎

Given (
2.5
) and (
2.2
), we can analyze the entropy of the Gaussian by dealing directly with the differential entropy.

Proof of
2.4
.

As a Gaussian vector, the entropy is defined by the covariance matrix
\Sigma
and the Gram matrix
M
. Specifically, define
\Sigma^{\eta}
to be the matrix with
\Sigma_{uv}^{\eta}=\textnormal{Cov}(\langle\Psi^{\lambda},\eta G^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta}_{u}\rangle,\langle\Psi^{\lambda},\eta G^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta}_{v}\rangle)
, and define
M
to be the matrix with
M_{uv}=\eta^{2}\langle G^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta}_{u},G^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta}_{v}\rangle
. Assuming
M
is full rank, the multivariate Gaussian of dimension
m
has differential entropy

\frac{m}{2}\log(2\pi e)+\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\Sigma)-\frac{1}{2}\log\det(M).

Examining
M
, it reduces by a Ward identity (
B.4
) to

M_{uv}=\eta^{2}(G^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta}_{u})^{*}G^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta}_{v}=\eta\Im[G^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta}_{uv}].

(4.2)

On the other hand, by (
2.5
),

\Sigma_{uv}=\lim_{\zeta\rightarrow 0}\Im(G_{uv}^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\zeta}).

(4.3)

Therefore, for any set
\Gamma
, we can use (
2.2
) to write

\displaystyle\phantom{{}={}}\mathbb{D}(\mu_{\Gamma}^{\eta})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{D}(\Psi_{\Pi(\Gamma)})

\displaystyle=\frac{|\partial\Gamma|}{2}\log(2\pi e)+\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\Sigma_{\Pi(\Gamma)})-\frac{1}{2}\log\det(M_{\Pi(\Gamma)})

\displaystyle=\frac{|\partial\Gamma|}{2}\log(2\pi e)+\frac{1}{2}\log\det(\lim_{\zeta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\Im(G_{\Pi(\Gamma)}^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\zeta}))-\frac{|\partial\Gamma|}{2}\log(\eta)-\log\det(\Im(G_{\Pi(\Gamma)}^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta})).

In the difference of entropies
\mathbb{E}_{o}\left[\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k}(C_{o})}^{\eta})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\sim o}\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k}(e_{oi})}^{\eta})\right]
, for
o\sim i
, the boundary vertices of distance
k
from
i
and
k+1
from
o
appear once on the left-hand side, and twice on the right-hand side, each with prefactor
1/2
. Therefore, the terms
\frac{|\partial\Gamma|}{2}(\log 2\pi e-\log\eta)
cancel exactly, leaving us with

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\phantom{{}={}}\mathbb{E}_{o}\left[\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k}(C_{o})}^{\eta})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\sim o}\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k}(e_{oi})}^{\eta})\right]\\
&=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{o}\left[\log\det(\lim_{\zeta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\Im(G_{\Pi(B_{k}(C_{o}))}^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\zeta}))-\log\det(\Im(G_{\Pi(B_{k}(C_{o}))}^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta}))\right]\\
&-\frac{1}{4}\mathbb{E}_{o}\left[\sum_{i\sim o}\left(\log\det(\lim_{\zeta\rightarrow 0^{+}}\Im(G_{\Pi(B_{k}(e_{ij}))}^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\zeta}))-\log\det(\Im(G_{\Pi(B_{k}(e_{ij}))}^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta}))\right)\right].\end{split}

(4.4)

By
Lemma

4.1
,
\Pi

Maximizing the entropy inequality

We wish to use a de Bruijn identity for a heated random variable. Therefore, it will be convenient to reduce to only considering smooth processes.

(A.1)

Therefore, define

\mu_{oi}^{N}\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{\lambda\in\textnormal{spec}(A_{\mathcal{G}})}\mathbb{E}_{o}[\psi^{N}_{\lambda}(o_{N})\psi^{N}_{\lambda}(i_{N})]\delta_{\lambda},

and
\mu_{oi}
defined by its moments

\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{k}{\rm d}\mu_{oi}=\langle\delta_{i},A_{\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}}}^{k}\delta_{o}\rangle.

Since the spectrum is uniformly bounded and moments determine the measure, convergence of all moments implies weak convergence of
\mu_{oi}^{N}
to
\mu_{oi}
.

By the fact that
\lambda
is in the absolutely continuous part of the spectrum, by the Stieltjes inversion formula (see
[
1
, Equation 2.11]
), for sufficiently small
\epsilon
,

\int_{\lambda-\epsilon}^{\lambda+\epsilon}{\rm d}\mu_{oo}(x)\geq\frac{1}{2}\Im[G_{oo}]\epsilon.

Moreover, by weak convergence, for any
\alpha>0
there is some sufficiently large
N
such that

\left|\int_{\lambda-\epsilon}^{\lambda+\epsilon}{\rm d}\mu_{oi}^{N}(x)-{\rm d}\mu_{oi}(x)\right|,\left|\int_{\lambda-\epsilon}^{\lambda+\epsilon}{\rm d}\mu_{oo}^{N}(x)-{\rm d}\mu_{oo}(x)\right|<\alpha.

(A.2)

Therefore, as
\mathbb{E}_{o}[f^{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda,\epsilon}(o_{N})f^{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda,\epsilon}(i_{N})]=\int_{\lambda-\epsilon}^{\lambda+\epsilon}{\rm d}\mu_{oi}^{N}(x)
,
by (
A.2
), for
\alpha\leq\frac{1}{4}\Im[G_{oo}]\epsilon
, we use that
\frac{x+\alpha}{y-\alpha}-\frac{x}{y}=\frac{\alpha(x+y)}{y(y-\alpha)}
, giving

\left|\frac{\mathbb{E}_{o}[f^{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda,\epsilon}(o_{N})f^{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda,\epsilon}(i_{N})]}{\mathbb{E}_{o}[f^{\mathcal{G}}_{\lambda,\epsilon}(o_{N})^{2}]}-\frac{\int_{\lambda-\epsilon}^{\lambda+\epsilon}{\rm d}\mu_{oi}(x)}{\int_{\lambda-\epsilon}^{\lambda+\epsilon}{\rm d}\mu_{oo}(x)}\right|\leq 16\Im[G_{oo}]^{-1}\epsilon^{-1}\alpha.

(A.3)

Therefore, as
\alpha
is arbitrary, fixing
\epsilon
and sending
\alpha
to 0, we see this converges to 0.
Thus, (
1.1
) and (
A.3
) give

\lim_{\epsilon\rightarrow 0}\lim_{N\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\int_{\lambda-\epsilon}^{\lambda+\epsilon}{\rm d}\mu_{oi}^{N}(x)}{\int_{\lambda-\epsilon}^{\lambda+\epsilon}{\rm d}\mu_{oo}^{N}(x)}=\frac{\Im[G_{oi}]}{\Im[G_{oo}]}.

∎

We now give a similar decomposition for the bipartite biregular graph.

Lemma A.3
.

For
d_{1}\geq d_{2}
,
d_{1}\geq 3
, and
d_{2}\geq 2
, the following is true about the model
\mathcal{G}\sim\mathcal{G}(N,d_{1},d_{2})
.

(1)

The spectrum of the bipartite biregular infinite tree
\mathcal{T}_{d_{1},d_{2}}
for
d_{1}>d_{2}
is purely absolutely continuous away from
\mathfrak{D}_{d_{1},d_{2}}=0
. If
d_{1}=d_{2}
, it is purely absolutely continuous with
\mathfrak{D}_{d_{1},d_{2}}=\emptyset
.

(2)

All eigenvectors of eigenvalue
|\lambda|<\sqrt{d_{1}d_{2}}
are orthogonal to the set of vectors that are constant on each of the two parts
V_{1}\sqcup V_{2}
of the configuration model.

Proof.

(1) follows immediately from the decomposition of the spectrum in
[
24
, Corollary 4.5]
. For (2) we can do a similar argument to
Lemma

A.1
, as the
\pm\sqrt{d_{1}d_{2}}
eigenspaces correspond to vectors which are constant on each part.
∎

Lemma A.4
.

Fix
k\geq 0
. For the infinite biregular tree
\mathcal{T}_{d_{1},d_{2}}
, we fix a root
o
for
\mathcal{T}_{d_{1},d_{2}}
, and let
i
be any vertex at distance
k
from
o
. Then, for the random bipartite biregular graph
\mathcal{G}\sim\mathcal{G}(N,d_{1},d_{2})
, we consider the distribution over the randomly selected vertex
o_{N}
covered by
o
, and take
i_{N}
to be the image of
i
in the projection onto
\mathcal{G}
. For any
\lambda\in\textnormal{spec}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}})
with any eigenvector
\psi
,

\frac{\mathbb{E}_{o_{N},i_{N}}[\psi_{o_{N}}\psi_{i_{N}}]}{\mathbb{E}_{o_{N}}[\psi_{o_{N}}^{2}]}=\frac{\Im[G_{oi}]}{\Im[G_{oo}]}.

Proof.

We evaluate this covariance by lifting
\psi
to
\textnormal{distr}^{*}(\psi)
. Because the infinite biregular tree is radially symmetric, it suffices to compute the covariance between
o\in V
and any vertex at distance
k
, since all such covariances agree by automorphism invariance. Let
S_{o}(k)
denote the set of vertices at distance exactly
k
from
o
. Then

\mathbb{E}[\psi_{o}\psi_{i}]=\frac{1}{|S_{o}(k)|}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left[\psi_{o}\sum_{w\in S_{o}(k)}\psi_{w}\right].

Assume vertices at even distance from
o
have degree
d_{1}
and those at odd distance have degree
d_{2}
. Repeatedly applying the eigenvector equation yields the recurrence

\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\displaystyle\sum_{y\in S_{o}(2j+1)}\psi_{y}\\
\displaystyle\sum_{y\in S_{o}(2j)}\psi_{y}\end{array}\right)=\left[\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\[1.99997pt]
\lambda&-(d_{1}-1)\end{array}\right)\left(\begin{array}[]{cc}0&1\\[1.99997pt]
\lambda&-(d_{2}-1)\end{array}\right)\right]^{j}\left(\begin{array}[]{c}\lambda\,\psi_{o}\\
\psi_{o}\end{array}\right).

(A.4)

This means that
\mathbb{E}[\psi_{k}\psi_{o}]/\mathbb{E}[\psi_{o}^{2}]
can be expressed as an explicit polynomial in
\lambda,d_{1},d_{2}
, and that this must be satisfied by any eigenvector. As
\lim_{\eta\to 0}\|(A-(\lambda+i\eta)\mathds{I})(G_{o})\|/\|G_{o}\|=0
, the same recurrence holds for the Green’s function as well.

∎

Remark A.5
.

This proof also works for the regular tree (or indeed any graph with radial symmetry) by replacing (
A.4
) with the correct degree sequence.

A.2
Convergence to the Gaussian wave

Proof of
1.8
.

By
Lemma

A.1
, the process is centered, and by
Lemma

A.2
, the covariance matrix of our process is
\sigma^{2}\Im[G]
, for some
0\leq\sigma^{2}\leq\frac{1}{\Im[m(\lambda)]}
. Therefore, it is sufficient to show that any convergent subsequence is typical.

Define
\mathcal{P}
to be the set of Borel probability measures on
V(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}})
with second moment at most 1. As
\mathcal{P}
is tight, it is compact under the metric
d_{LP}
defined in (
1.3
). We then wish to define a corresponding metric on graphs. For any graph
\mathcal{G}
, we can take the set
S(\mathcal{G})\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\{\textnormal{distr}^{*}(f):f\in\mathbb{R}^{V(\mathcal{G})},\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(i)^{2}\leq 1\}
, which once again, is compact in the weak topology. Therefore, we can define
d_{N}(\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2})=d_{H}(S(\mathcal{G}_{1}),S(\mathcal{G}_{2}))
, where
d_{H}
is the Hausdorff distance under
d_{LP}
. Equivalently,
d_{N}(\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2})
is the infimum over
\delta
such that for any labeling
f_{1}
of
\mathcal{G}_{1}
satisfying
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in[N]}f_{1}(i)^{2}\leq 1
, there is a labeling
\hat{f}_{1}
of
\mathcal{G}_{2}
such that
d_{LP}(\textnormal{distr}^{*}(f_{1}),\textnormal{distr}^{*}(\hat{f}_{1}))\leq\delta
, and for every
f_{2}
a labeling of
\mathcal{G}_{2}
, there is a labeling
\hat{f}_{2}
of
\mathcal{G}_{1}
such that
d_{LP}(\textnormal{distr}^{*}(f_{2}),\textnormal{distr}^{*}(\hat{f}_{2}))\leq\delta
.

The Hausdorff distance of a compact metric between closed subsets is compact. Therefore
there exists a finite
\epsilon/3
cover
M
of the sets
S(\mathcal{G})
. This means there is set of subsets
T
such that with probability at least
1/|M|
, our randomly selected graph
\mathcal{G}
has
d_{H}(S(\mathcal{G}),T)\leq\epsilon/3
. Therefore, define the random variable
X(\mathcal{G})
to be
d_{H}(S(\mathcal{G}),T)
for the randomly selected graph
\mathcal{G}\sim\mathcal{G}(N,\mathbf{d})
. When we are performing a switch, we change the neighborhood of any fixed vertex with probability
O(1/N)
as
N\rightarrow\infty
. Therefore, for Lévy-Prokhorov distance, the change caused by this shift is at most
O(1/N)
. This means we can use a McDiarmid inequality on the Doob martingale (see
[
34
, Theorem 2.19]
for an explicit derivation) to show that for some fixed
c>0
, with probability at least
1-\exp(-cN^{1/3})
,
|X(\mathcal{G})-\mathbb{E}[X(\mathcal{G})]|\leq N^{-1/3}
. Therefore, for sufficiently large
N
we must have
\mathbb{E}[X]\leq\epsilon/2
. meaning, by the triangle inequality, with probability
1-\exp(-cN^{1/3})
,
d_{N}(\mathcal{G}_{1},\mathcal{G}_{2})\leq\epsilon
. By Borel-Cantelli II, this means that if there is an infinite subsequence that converges to a measure, then in fact this measure is
\epsilon
-typical. As
\epsilon
is arbitrary, this implies that the limit is typical.

∎

The proof of
1.9
is identical to that of
1.8
, we just replace
Lemma

A.1
and
Lemma

A.2
with
Lemma

A.3
and
Lemma

A.4
.

A.3
Comparing levels

Proof of
Lemma

2.6
.

We use the fact that
\mathbb{D}(X,Z)+\mathbb{D}(Y,Z)-\mathbb{D}(Z)\geq\mathbb{D}(X,Y,Z)
, with equality if and only if
X
and
Y
are conditionally independent given
Z
. This gives the following equations, where
d_{o}
is the degree of
o
:

\displaystyle\begin{split}\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k}(C_{o})})&\leq\left(\sum_{i\sim o}\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k}(e_{oi})})\right)-(d_{i}-1)\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k-1}(C_{o})})\\
\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k}(e_{oi})})&\leq\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k-1}(C_{o})})+\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k-1}(C_{i})})-\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k-1}(e_{oi})}).\end{split}

(A.5)

The first equation in (
A.5
) follows as we can cover
B_{k}(C_{o})
with
d_{o}
balls, one for each edge emerging from the central vertex. This will create an excess overlap of
d_{o}-1
balls of radius
k-1
. We have equality if and only if the
d_{o}
disconnected components of
B_{k}(C_{o})\backslash B_{k-1}(C_{o})
are conditionally independent.

Similarly, for the second equation of (
A.5
), we can cover
B_{k}(e_{oi})
with two balls surrounding each of the vertices, with an overlap of a ball of radius
k-1
. We only have equality if, when conditioned on
B_{k-1}(e_{oi})
, the two sides of
B_{k}(e_{oi})\backslash B_{k-1}(e_{oi})
must be independent.

Therefore, taking an average over the root, we obtain

\displaystyle\begin{split}&\phantom{{}={}}\mathbb{E}_{o}\left[\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k}(C_{o})})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\sim o}\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k}(e_{oi})})\right]\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}_{o}\left[\sum_{i\sim o}\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k}(e_{oi})})-(d_{o}-1)\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k-1}(C_{o})})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\sim o}\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k}(e_{oi})})\right]\\
&=\mathbb{E}_{o}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i\sim o}\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k}(e_{oi})})\right)-(d_{o}-1)\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k-1}(C_{o})})\right]\\
&\leq\mathbb{E}_{o}\left[\frac{1}{2}\left(\sum_{i\sim o}\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k-1}(C_{o})})+\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k-1}(C_{i})})-\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k-1}(e_{oi})})\right)-(d_{o}-1)\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k-1}(C_{o})})\right].\end{split}

(A.6)

We now track the number of terms
\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k-1}(C_{o})})
in (
A.6
). We have
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}{\mathbf{d}_{oi}}-d_{o}+1
from type
i
, and
\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\mathbf{d}_{oi}
from neighbors of
o
. As
\sum_{i}\mathbf{d}_{oi}=d_{o}
, the last row of (
A.6
) reduces to

\mathbb{E}_{o}\left[\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k-1}(C_{o})})-\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i\sim o}\mathbb{D}(\mu_{B_{k-1}(e_{oi})})\right].

We have equality for every
k
if and only if we have equality in (
A.5
) for every
k
, which means that the distribution is 2-Markov.
∎

Appendix B
Properties of the Green’s Function

Through this section, let
G=(H-z\mathds{I})^{-1}
denote the Green’s function of Hermitian operator
H
at
z=E+\mathrm{i}\eta
. We recall the fundamental recursive properties of the Green’s function. The first is the Schur complement formula.

Lemma B.1

(Lemma 7.2 of
[
22
]
)
.

For any subset of indices
\mathbb{T}
,

G|_{\mathbb{T}}=(H|_{\mathbb{T}}-z\mathds{I}-H|_{\mathbb{T}\mathbb{T}^{c}}G^{(\mathbb{T})}H|_{\mathbb{T}^{c}\mathbb{T}})^{-1}.

(B.1)

The next is the decomposition of the Green’s function.

Lemma B.2

(Lemma 8.3 of
[
22
]
)
.

The following is true for the Green’s function.

1.

For
y,w\neq x

G_{yw}^{(x)}=G_{yw}-\frac{G_{yx}G_{xw}}{G_{xx}}.

(B.2)

2.

If
x\neq y
, then

G_{xy}=-G_{xx}\sum_{w\neq x}H_{xw}G_{wy}^{(x)}.

(B.3)

3.

(Ward Identity)
We have that

\sum_{y}|G_{xy}|^{2}=\frac{\Im(G_{xx})}{\eta}.

(B.4)

We collect the following results from
[
5
, Proposition 4.2]
.

Lemma B.3
.

(1)

For any tree
\mathcal{T}
of finite cone type, there is a finite set
\mathfrak{D}_{1}\subset\mathbb{R}
such that for every edge
(x,y)\in\mathcal{T}
,
G_{xx}^{(y)}(\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta),G_{xx}(\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta)
is finite for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathfrak{D}_{1}
. The map
\lambda\mapsto G_{xx}^{(y)}(\lambda+i0)
is continuous on
\mathbb{R}\setminus\mathfrak{D}_{1}
.

(2)

Moreover,
there is a finite set
\mathfrak{D}_{2}
such that for
\lambda\in\textnormal{spec}(\mathcal{T})\backslash(\mathfrak{D}_{1}\cup\mathfrak{D}_{2})
, for every edge
(x,y)
,
\Im[G_{xx}^{(y)}]>0
and
\Im[G_{xx}]>0
.

We also a general property of the Green’s function for generically chosen
z
.

Lemma B.4
.

For expanding degree matrix
\mathbf{d}
,
\lambda\in\textnormal{spec}(\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}})\backslash\mathfrak{D}_{\mathbf{d}}
, consider
G\mathrel{\mathop{:}}=\lim_{\eta\rightarrow 0}G^{\lambda+\mathrm{i}\eta}
. Assume there is an automorphism on
\mathcal{T}_{\mathbf{d}}
mapping directed edge
(x,y)
to directed edge
(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
and the path has at least one vertex of degree 3. Then for the path
x=\gamma_{0},\gamma_{1},\ldots,\gamma_{t}=x^{\prime}
,

\prod_{i\in[t]}|G_{\gamma_{i}\gamma_{i}}^{(\gamma_{i-1})}|<1.

Proof.

By taking the norm of the Green’s function, we have that by iterating (
B.3
),

\|G_{y}^{(x)}\|^{2}\geq\left(\prod_{i\in[t]}|G_{\gamma_{i}\gamma_{i}}^{(\gamma_{i-1})}|^{2}\right)\|G_{y}^{(x)}\|^{2}+\|X\|^{2}

where
X
has nonzero norm since at least one new forward cone is introduced. This immediate implies the result.
∎
