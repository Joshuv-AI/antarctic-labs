Posterior fossa dynamic craniotomy utilizing NuCrani reversibly expandable bone flap fixation plates: patient series
Authors: Rohit Khanna, Paul R. Krafft, Gilberto Perez-Rodriguez-Garcia, Anjali Khanna, Patrick Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDThe authors report on the first clinical application of a posterior fossa dynamic craniotomy technique utilizing NuCrani reversibly expandable fixation plates designed to allow controlled outward bone flap displacement in response to postoperative cerebellar swelling while maintaining bone flap fixation under normal conditions. Eight patients underwent dynamic bone flap fixation for posterior fossa surgery. Indications included cerebellar hemorrhage (n = 2), traumatic cerebellar and subdural hemorrhage (n = 1), and cerebellar metastases with associated edema (n = 5). Three-month clinical and radiographic outcomes were evaluated.OBSERVATIONSAll patients demonstrated varying degrees of postoperative cerebellar swelling that was accommodated by outward bone flap migration, effectively maintaining decompression with bone flap stability. No patient needed reoperation for cerebellar swelling, CSF leakage, or wound complications. One patient developed postoperative hematoma, which was accommodated with immediate decompression aided by the reversible expansion of the dynamic construct and subsequent hematoma evacuation.LESSONSPosterior fossa dynamic craniotomy using reversibly expandable fixation plates is technically feasible, providing automatic volumetric decompression for postoperative cerebellar swelling and/or hematoma while preserving dural and wound integrity. This hybrid approach integrates the advantages of a suboccipital craniectomy with the reconstructive stability of a craniotomy, presenting another option in posterior fossa surgery aimed at reducing secondary brainstem compression and postoperative morbidity. https://thejns.org/doi/10.3171/CASE2616

Surgical approaches for posterior fossa lesions typically involve either a suboccipital craniectomy (SOC) or suboccipital craniotomy, both of which provide adequate exposure to posterior fossa structures. Because of the confined volume of the posterior fossa, even small lesions, hemorrhages, or infarcts can cause profound neurological compromise due to cerebellar and brainstem compression, hydrocephalus, or herniation.1–16

SOC remains the traditional decompressive approach, permitting expansion in response to postoperative edema or hemorrhage. However, SOC is associated with higher rates of CSF leakage, pseudomeningocele formation, and wound-healing complications.1–11 In contrast, suboccipital craniotomy allows for anatomical closure and rigid protection of the cerebellum, reducing the incidence of CSF leaks, wound dehiscence, and infection, but it does not accommodate postoperative swelling, which can lead to acute neurological decline and fatal brainstem compression.1–11,17

Dynamic craniotomy uses expandable fixation constructs that permit controlled, reversible outward migration of the bone flap in response to intracranial swelling, while preventing flap sinking.19–22 Supratentorial applications have demonstrated efficacy in maintaining intracranial pressure (ICP) and compensating for cerebral swelling after trauma and hemorrhagic stroke.18,19 Extending this principle to the posterior fossa may allow for physiological decompression while preserving the anatomical and reconstructive benefits of craniotomy.

This study presents the initial clinical experience with a novel posterior fossa dynamic craniotomy technique utilizing NuCrani reversibly expandable fixation plates. The technique provides secure anatomical closure while allowing controlled expansion in the setting of postoperative swelling or hemorrhage, thereby serving as a hybrid between traditional craniectomy and rigid craniotomy.

Study Description

Patient Selection

Eight patients underwent dynamic posterior fossa craniotomy using the NeuroVention Cranial Fixation System with NuCrani reversibly expandable fixation plates following resection or evacuation of posterior fossa mass lesions. Indications included spontaneous cerebellar hemorrhage (n = 2), traumatic cerebellar and infratentorial subdural hemorrhage (n = 1), and cerebellar metastasis with associated edema (n = 5). The NeuroVention Cranial Fixation System is intended for use as a burr hole cover and/or skull bone fixation following craniotomy, cranioplasty, or craniectomy surgery. Semirigid fixation is an off-label indication.

Surgical Technique

The surgical technique consists of elevating a suboccipital posterior fossa bone flap with burr holes and a craniotome. More recently, we have been using a fine-tip drill without burr holes to elevate the bone flap, which has been less cumbersome from a technical standpoint. Typically, a 6-cm or larger bone flap is recommended. The dura is opened in a Y-shaped format, and the posterior fossa mass lesion is removed. To allow for compensation of any postoperative cerebellar swelling, an expansile synthetic dural graft, which is typically not sutured along with a sealant, is used for dural closure. The bone flap is replaced and fixated using Khanna NuCrani reversibly expandable plates (NeuroVention, LLC), enabling controlled outward migration for any worsening postoperative edema or hemorrhage. Layered soft tissue closure is performed in the standard fashion.

Two patients demonstrated significant postoperative cerebellar swelling with 9 mm of outward migration of the bone flap. One patient developed a postoperative hematoma on day 3 following cerebellar metastasis resection. Initial imaging showed complete resection and the bone flap in the anatomical position; however, a subsequent hypertensive episode led to acute neurological decline (GCS score 15 to 5T). CT imaging revealed a new cerebellar hemorrhage with 7 mm of outward bone flap migration, indicating compensatory decompression. The patient’s neurological function improved spontaneously (GCS score 10T), and the family later elected to proceed with hematoma evacuation.

The remaining patients demonstrated between 2 and 7 mm of outward bone flap migration associated with cerebellar swelling. No patient required reoperation for cerebellar swelling, CSF leakage, or wound complications. One patient developed a small, asymptomatic pseudomeningocele after adjuvant radiotherapy that resolved without intervention. Importantly, none of the 8 patients developed a CSF leak, even when dural closure was nonwatertight and reinforced with an expansile synthetic graft and sealant. Bone flap retraction after resolution of the cerebellar swelling was seen in all surviving patients, and none of the patients experienced any cosmetic deformity.

Discussion

Yaşargil and others have long emphasized the importance of bony reconstruction in posterior fossa surgery.1,4,5,11,23 Suboccipital craniotomy bone flap elevation has evolved from placing burr holes and using a Gigli saw to connect the burr holes to using fine-tip drills with or without burr holes. Dural closure techniques have also evolved to sutured patch grafts or synthetic grafts with dural sealants. Bone flap fixation techniques, on the other hand, have consisted of rigid fixation using either sutures, wires, or rigid plates.

SOC has been consistently associated with higher complication rates, particularly pseudomeningoceles and CSF leaks, compared to posterior fossa craniotomy. Reported complication rates range from 24% to 55% after SOC versus 7% to 13% following craniotomy.1–11 The presence of a bone flap provides tamponade over the dural closure, limiting the expansion of minor defects and reducing CSF egress into subcutaneous tissues. Legnani et al.5 demonstrated a 33% complication rate following SOC compared to 7% after craniotomy, with significantly lower rates of recurrent pseudomeningocele formation after craniotomy repair.

While craniotomy reduces wound complications, it fails to accommodate postoperative cerebellar swelling—a potentially catastrophic limitation. Prior studies have shown that even 5 mm of supratentorial bone flap outward migration can significantly offset worsening cerebral edema and/or hemorrhage after trauma or hemorrhagic stroke.18,19,24–27 The NuCrani dynamic fixation system has demonstrated up to 15 mm of reversible outward migration while maintaining bone flap stability and ICP control in supratentorial applications.18–20 Other mobile bone flap techniques likehinge or free-floating bone flaps used in supratentorial craniotomy do not provide a controlled and stable bone flap migration like dynamic craniotomy and, therefore, are less likely to be utilized in posterior fossa craniotomy.

In the present series, posterior fossa bone flap displacement ranged from 2 to 9 mm, correlating with the degree of postoperative swelling. Although small degrees of outward migration may appear modest, the confined volume of the posterior fossa amplifies the physiological impact of even limited expansion. In general, an 8-cm bone flap with 9 mm of outward bone flap displacement provides approximately 30 ml of extra posterior fossa volume. In one case, spontaneous neurological improvement following delayed hemorrhage in the cerebellar neoplasm resection bed was temporally associated with outward flap migration, with immediate decompression and prevention of catastrophic brainstem compression and herniation.

Posterior fossa dynamic craniotomy also reduces postoperative CSF leakage and pseudomeningocele formation, likely due to the anatomically reconstituted rigid barrier provided by the bone flap and the tamponading effect of the expandable fixation system. Despite the use of nonwatertight dural closures, no patient in this series developed a CSF leak or required surgical intervention for pseudomeningocele. This suggests that dynamic fixation may maintain sufficient dural apposition and occipital skull integrity while accommodating cerebellar expansion. This experience aligns with prior literature demonstrating that posterior fossa bony reconstruction significantly mitigates the risk of postoperative CSF-related complications compared to SOC.1–11 The ability of the dynamic construct to provide pressure-responsive decompression, yet a sealed posterior fossa compartment, may represent an added advantage over both traditional SOC and fixed-plate craniotomy.

Observations

Posterior fossa craniotomy with bony reconstruction has been associated with lower rates of CSF leakage and pseudomeningocele formation compared with SOC, due to restoration of a rigid anatomical barrier that supports dural closure. In this series, use of a posterior fossa dynamic craniotomy allowed reversible outward bone flap displacement ranging from 2 to 9 mm in response to postoperative cerebellar swelling. Even small degrees of outward migration provided meaningful posterior fossa volume expansion, with one case of postoperatve hemorrhagic complication demonstrating neurological improvement temporally associated with bone flap expansion. Despite nonwatertight dural closures, no patient developed a CSF leak or required surgical treatment for pseudomeningocele.

Potential risks associated with expandable fixation constructs include mechanical failure, plate loosening, restricted bone flap mobility, malposition, late infection, and theoretical risks of under- or overdecompression. No device-related complications were observed in this small series; however, rare or delayed adverse events cannot be excluded. In larger clinical series of supratentorial dynamic craniotomy fixations, we have not noticed any plate mechanical failure, malposition, or loosening.18 As with all cranial implants, meticulous surgical technique, appropriate patient selection, and longitudinal surveillance are essential. Larger multicenter studies are required to characterize the long-term safety profile of expandable fixation systems more fully in the posterior fossa.

The NuCrani reversibly expandable fixation system is commercially available in the United States. Although the upfront cost exceeds that of standard rigid fixation plates, this must be weighed against the potential downstream costs associated with CSF leakage, wound complications, reoperation, and prolonged hospitalization commonly observed after SOC.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. The series is small, retrospective, and lacks a control group, precluding inference regarding comparative effectiveness relative to traditional SOC or fixed craniotomy. ICP measurements were recorded in 4 of 8 patients (Table 1) but not uniformly obtained, reflecting real-world variability in posterior fossa postoperative management. Postoperative ICP remained normal in monitored patients despite worsening cerebellar swelling. Future studies incorporating defined pressure thresholds, volumetric modeling, and integration with pressure-sensing technologies may provide more precise physiological correlations and guide postoperative escalation of adjunctive medical management needed to accommodate the swelling period.

Additionally, a proportion of the cited literature reflects prior work by us describing the same expandable fixation technology, reflecting the relative novelty of the device and the limited independent clinical experience currently available. Independent validation and multicenter comparative studies will be essential to reduce potential selection and reporting bias.

Finally, the applicability of posterior fossa dynamic craniotomy in emergency settings needs further investigation. In cases of rapid neurological deterioration or impending herniation, SOC remains the fastest and most definitive decompressive intervention. Dynamic craniotomy may be best suited for elective, urgent, or semiurgent cases in which postoperative swelling is anticipated but immediate maximal decompression is not required. As such, this technique should be viewed as complementary rather than a replacement for traditional SOC.

Lessons

Posterior fossa dynamic craniotomy represents a hybrid surgical strategy that integrates the decompressive capacity of SOC with the structural stability and dural protection of fixed craniotomy. By allowing automatic, pressure-responsive outward bone flap expansion, this technique accommodates postoperative cerebellar swelling while preserving anatomical reconstruction and dural integrity. In the constrained posterior fossa, even small increases in available volume may meaningfully reduce the risk of brainstem compression. Additionally, reversibly expandable fixation constructs may decrease CSF-related wound complications by maintaining a sealed posterior fossa compartment. Dynamic craniotomy does not require additional workflow modifications relative to fixed-plate craniotomy, yet provides the added advantage of controlled cranial decompression without the need for a secondary decompressive procedure, potentially improving procedural safety and facilitating broader adoption. Conceptualizing dynamic craniotomy as an incremental evolution of established decompressive principles, rather than a replacement, and emphasizing surgeon control with the ability to convert to standard decompressive craniectomy if required, may mitigate concerns related to novelty and facilitate integration into routine neurosurgical practice.

Posterior fossa dynamic craniotomy utilizing NuCrani reversibly expandable fixation plates is a novel, physiologically adaptive surgical strategy that integrates the decompressive benefits of SOC with the structural advantages of rigid craniotomy. By allowing controlled, reversible bone flap expansion in response to postoperative swelling, this technique offers a promising hybrid solution to reduce brainstem compression, CSF leakage, and wound complications in posterior fossa surgery.
