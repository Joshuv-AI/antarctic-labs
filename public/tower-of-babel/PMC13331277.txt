Improvement of persistent anuria by long-term percutaneous ventricular assist device management and successful bridge to left ventricular assist device implantation in a patient with acute myocardial infarction: a case report
Authors: Ryohei Ono, Goro Matsumiya, Taro Imaeda, Taka-Aki Nakada, Yoshio Kobayashi, Josip Andelo Borovac, Deepti Ranganathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiogenic shock secondary to acute myocardial infarction (AMI) is often complicated by acute kidney injury (AKI). Although percutaneous ventricular assist devices (pVADs) are used to treat organ failure in cardiogenic shock, it is difficult to predict whether AKI is reversible or not and how long treatment should be continued to improve renal function.Case summaryA 57-year-old woman with AMI complicated by cardiogenic and anaphylactic shock developed persistent anuria and required dialysis. Despite initial mechanical support, she remained dependent on advanced circulatory assistance. She received prolonged pVADs (Impella CP® and Impella 5.5®) for over 4 months. Remarkably, urine output resumed after 2 months of anuria, allowing discontinuation of dialysis. She underwent successful left ventricular assist device (LVAD) implantation on Day 138 and remained dialysis-free thereafter.DiscussionCardiogenic shock complicated with AKI limits options for durable mechanical circulatory support. While dialysis-dependent renal dysfunction is considered a contraindication for LVAD implantation in our country, our case illustrates that recovery of renal function may still be achievable even after prolonged anuria. By maintaining renal perfusion with extended pVAD support, dialysis withdrawal was ultimately successful, enabling LVAD implantation. This case highlights that prolonged circulatory support may be a feasible bridge to durable LVAD or heart transplantation.Keywords: Acute myocardial infarction, Acute kidney injury, Case report, Destination therapy, Left ventricular assist device, Percutaneous ventricular assist device

Introduction

Acute myocardial infarction (AMI) with left ventricular dysfunction is the most frequent cause of cardiogenic shock (CS). Such a case may require an implantable ventricular assist device (VAD) and heart transplantation (HTx); however, these strategies are contraindicated in patients with irreversible renal dysfunction requiring haemodialysis in our country.1 Acute kidney injury (AKI) is often associated with CS, and mechanical circulatory support (MCS) is used to treat organ damage, including AKI. Although the recent availability of percutaneous ventricular assist devices (pVAD), including Impella CP® or 5.5® (Abiomed, Danvers, MA, USA), has expanded treatment options for patients with organ failure due to low cardiac output,2,3 it is difficult to predict reversibility, and the optimal duration of treatment when organ damage persists is uncertain.

Case presentation

A 57-year-old woman with a history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, dyslipidaemia, and anaphylactic reaction to contrast media was transferred to our hospital due to CS after AMI. Her previous serum creatinine level was 0.65 mg/dL. She had been smoking 20 cigarettes per day for 37 years. Three days prior, she presented with an acute onset of chest pain and visited another hospital. Electrocardiogram showed ST elevation in leads I, aVL, and V1-6 (Figure 1A). She was diagnosed with AMI, and coronary angiography (CAG) was performed after hydrocortisone administration to prevent an allergic reaction. CAG revealed that the proximal left anterior descending was occluded (Figure 1B). However, she had dyspnoea and hypotension soon after contrast injection, and adrenaline was administered for anaphylactic shock. Finally, plain old balloon angioplasty was performed, and thrombolysis in myocardial infarction (TIMI) grade 3 flow was obtained. Intra-aortic balloon pumping (IABP) insertion, intubation, continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), and steroid and dexchlorpheniramine administration were started, but her hypotension and circulatory failure due to cardiogenic and anaphylactic shock persisted. Her peak creatinine kinase (CK) and CK-myocardial band levels were 8500 U/L and 1250 ng/mL, respectively. She was transferred to our hospital for further treatment.

(A) Electrocardiogram showing ST elevation in leads I, aVL, and V1-6. (B) Coronary angiography showing occlusion of the proximal left anterior descending artery.

On arrival, the patient’s blood pressure was 60/39 mm Hg under the support of catecholamine (dobutamine 6.3 μg/kg/min and norepinephrine 0.65 μg/kg/min) and IABP, and pulse rate was 105 beats per minute. Physical examination revealed no cardiac murmur, but cold extremities. The electrocardiogram showed sinus rhythm with QS pattern in leads V2-6. Chest radiograph showed pulmonary congestion (Figure 2). Transthoracic echocardiography showed a left ventricular ejection fraction of 30%, akinesis of the anteroseptal walls and apex with aneurysm, and pericardial effusions. Laboratory data showed elevated levels of serum creatinine (2.80 mg/dL) and N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide (15 571 pg/mL).

Chest radiograph showing pulmonary congestion.

IABP was converted to Impella CP® via the right femoral artery; however, the patient’s blood pressure was still low and cardiac index (CI) was only 1.0 L/min/m2. Therefore, veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) was subsequently added via the left femoral vessels. On Day 7, pericardiocentesis was performed for cardiac tamponade due to oozing rupture. After the pericardiocentesis, VA-ECMO was successfully removed on the same day. Because her cardiac function did not change and her anuria persisted, the Impella CP® was converted to Impella 5.5® via the right subclavian artery on Day 13.

The clinical course is shown in Figure 3. During the course, she had several infections, including ventilator-associated pneumonia, catheter-associated bloodstream infection, sepsis, pneumonia, and access site infection of Impella 5.5®, and she was treated with antibiotics for a long time. On Day 21, extubation was performed, and CRRT was switched to intermittent haemodialysis. Dobutamine was weaned off on Day 30. On Day 35, right heart catheterization (RHC) under P8 support (Impella performance level) showed: Aortic blood pressure, 100/79 (mean, 87) mm Hg; heart rate, 80 beats per minute; pulmonary capillary wedge pressure (PCWP), 26 mm Hg; mean right atrial pressure, 14 mmHg; mean pulmonary artery pressure, 35 mm Hg; CI, 3.02 L/min/m2; and mixed venous oxygen saturation, 72.5%. Since her haemodynamic status was still dependent on Impella 5.5® and required further support for AKI, Impella 5.5® was replaced on Day 41. Surprisingly, after 2 months from the AMI, small amounts of urine were observed. Urinary output gradually increased, and she was on intermittent haemodialysis every 2–3 days. RHC on Day 99 showed PCWP 29 mmHg and CI 2.77 L/min/m2 under P8 support, and PCWP 38 mmHg and CI 2.12 L/min/m2 under P2 support; therefore, Impella 5.5® withdrawal was considered difficult. After initiation of furosemide and tolvaptan, urine volume gradually increased. Finally, haemodialysis was stopped on Day 118, and her blood urea nitrogen and serum creatinine peaked out at 94 mg/dL and 6.07 mg/dL, respectively, without acidosis and hyperkalaemia. Therefore, the heart team conference and hospital ethical committee approved for left ventricular assist device (LVAD) implantation. After additional haemodialyses were performed for solute removal preoperatively, she finally underwent LVAD implantation (HeartMate3, Abbott, USA) for destination therapy on Day 138. After LVAD implantation, the patient no longer required haemodialysis. Although her rehabilitation was prolonged due to muscle weakness, she was discharged independently 6 months after surgery.

Discussion

The incidence of AKI as a complication of CS is significantly high, with studies reporting it to be between 20% and 35%.4,5 In addition, the presence of AKI in CS is associated with increased mortality.6 Irreversible renal dysfunction requiring haemodialysis is one of the contraindications for implantable LVAD or HTx in our country. Therefore, in the case of CS complicated by severe renal dysfunction, withdrawal from haemodialysis is necessary to bridge to implantable LVAD or HTx.

However, previous studies have shown that severe renal dysfunction complicating CS is not a contraindication to VAD, since clinical outcomes (including 30-day and 6-month survival, survival to transplantation, and 1-year post-transplant survival) in these patients were comparable to those in patients without severe renal dysfunction.7 A recent review of outcomes with LVADs in end-stage renal disease also showed that LVADs have an acceptable survival in carefully selected patients on dialysis, despite the limited data and publications on LVADs in patients with end-stage renal disease.1,8

In our case, we considered that the patient’s AKI was mainly caused by cardiogenic and anaphylactic shock, in addition to infections and antibiotic use. Of note, the patient had anuria for more than 2 months; therefore, predicting whether or not renal function would improve was difficult, and the prospect of dialysis cessation directly influenced the treatment decision for VAD implantation.

We previously experienced and reported a case of renal function recovery after 87 days of anuria in a patient with dilated cardiomyopathy9; thus, our strategy was to maintain renal perfusion with pVAD support for up to 3 months. In addition, we dared not replace Impella 5.5® for the third time because the replacement procedure may cause temporary hypotension due to anaesthesia and bleeding, which may subsequently cause deterioration of renal function again. Although we used prolonged Impella support beyond the approved duration (approved by the heart team conference and hospital committee), previous reports have demonstrated the feasibility of extended Impella 5.5® support as a bridge to durable LVAD or HTx, with no significant differences in mortality, major bleeding, or stroke between patients supported for >14 days (up to 133 days) and those supported for ≤14 days.10,11 Nevertheless, prolonged support may increase the risk of device-related complications, including bleeding, haemolysis, stroke, acute kidney injury, and infectious complications such as surgical site or bloodstream infection.10,11 In the present case, ventilator-associated pneumonia, catheter-associated bloodstream infection, and access site infection occurred during the prolonged support period. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first case of persistent anuria but successful haemodialysis withdrawal using pVAD for 138 days as a bridge to durable LVAD implantation.

In conclusion, our case showed that prolonged use of Impella achieved haemodialysis withdrawal even for 2 months of anuria. Although the duration of MCS use is controversial, prolonged percutaneous ventricular assist device use may be one of the options as a bridge to LVAD or HTx.
