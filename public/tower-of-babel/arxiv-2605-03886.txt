More Permutations Do Not Always Increase Power: Non-monotonicity in Monte Carlo Permutation Tests
Suman Cha, Seongchan Lee, Antonin Schrab, Ilmun Kim, [0.6em] Department of Statistics, Data Science, Yonsei University, Seoul, South Korea, [0.4em] These authors contributed equally to this work.

Abstract
Monte Carlo permutation tests are a cornerstone of valid, model-free statistical inference. A widely held practical intuition is that increasing the number of sampled permutations improves test performance, in particular that statistical power tends to increase with the Monte Carlo budget. In this paper, we show that these intuitions are false in general. Leveraging the saw-toothed structure of power arising from distributional discreteness, we provide a simple structural explanation for why power can decrease as the number of sampled permutations increases, and we prove that such decreases occur infinitely often as the Monte Carlo budget grows.



1
Introduction

Permutation tests, originated with
7
and
20
, provide exact or finite-sample-valid inference under exchangeability (or invariance) assumptions. Owing to this finite-sample validity, they are widely used across modern statistics, machine learning, and the sciences
(
1
;
23
)
. In practice, since the full permutation group is often too large to enumerate, practitioners typically employ a Monte Carlo approach by drawing
B
random permutations
(
5
)
. The prevailing heuristic suggests that increasing
B
yields a “better” test, as the Monte Carlo
p
-value concentrates around its full-group counterpart with an error shrinking on the order of
B^{-1/2}

(
15
)
.

Despite this intuition, the relationship between the Monte Carlo budget
B
and test power remains only partially understood. Historically, the literature has predominantly focused on exact size control, practical stability
(
18
)
, or conditions that guarantee monotonic power increases under the strict alignment condition
(B+1)\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
. Under this aligned setting, monotonicity has been established assuming monotone likelihood ratios
(
14
)
or concavity of the underlying power function
(
15
)
. More recently,
8
analyzed a broader resampling framework covering bootstrap and permutation tests, bounding rejection probabilities via properties of the
p
-value distribution such as convexity and the Bernstein property. Taken together, these studies clarify power behavior under specific structural constraints, but they do not characterize the general behavior of power for arbitrary choices of
B
. Crucially, the possibility of non-monotonicity, meaning that increasing the number of sampled permutations could actually reduce the rejection probability under the alternative, has not been systematically investigated.

Related phenomena have been observed in other simulation-based and discrete testing frameworks.
4
analyzed the power loss caused by using a finite number of bootstrap replications, and
3
documented a “sawtooth” pattern in the power function of exact proportion tests due to the discreteness of the sampling distribution. While this analysis was confined to the binomial setting without a general structural explanation, it hints at the broader impact of discreteness. Closer to our context,
22
demonstrated that within permutation testing, the
conditional
rejection probability for a fixed dataset
\mathcal{X}
need not increase with the Monte Carlo budget
B
. Our work extends these findings in two directions. First, we demonstrate that the
unconditional
power, averaged over
\mathcal{X}
under the alternative, also exhibits non-monotone behavior as a function of
B
. Second, we identify a discrete critical-count structure that explains when and why local downward steps arise along plateaus of the rejection threshold.

A separate line of work has generalized permutation tests along an algebraic axis.
21
established finite-sample validity for arbitrary subsets of the permutation group and non-uniform sampling distributions, and
16
showed that a carefully chosen subgroup can outperform the full group in multiple testing. The power gains in that literature arise from the algebraic structure of the chosen permutations, and are therefore orthogonal to the effect of the Monte Carlo budget
B
studied here.

Despite the theoretical emphasis on aligned choices with
(B+1)\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
, many applied implementations instead use round Monte Carlo budgets (e.g.,
B=10^{2},10^{3},10^{4}
) without calibrating
B
to the nominal level
\alpha
. The goal of this article is to show that non-monotonicity is not a pathological curiosity but a fundamental structural feature of Monte Carlo permutation tests, and to argue that choosing
B
so that
(B+1)\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
provides a simple design rule that places the test at a local power maximum under the non-degeneracy condition.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. In
Section

2
, we review the setup of Monte Carlo permutation tests. In
Section

3
, we present our main theoretical results on non-monotonicity and infinitely many local maxima in the power curve. In
Section

4
, we illustrate these phenomena in a simple closed-form example. We conclude with a discussion in
Section

5
, and all code to reproduce the figures and numerical results is available at
https://github.com/suman-cha/mc-perm-power
.

2
Monte Carlo permutation tests

We briefly review exact permutation tests and their Monte Carlo approximations, fix notation, and introduce the key objects that drive our subsequent analysis of power non-monotonicity. We refer to
17
and
12
for comprehensive treatments of permutation testing.

2.1
Exact permutation tests

Let
\mathcal{X}
be observed data (e.g., concatenated samples in a two-sample problem), let
T(\mathcal{X})\in\mathbb{R}
be a test statistic, with larger values indicating stronger evidence against the null hypothesis, and let
\mathcal{G}
be a finite group of transformations on the data. In permutation tests,
\mathcal{G}
is typically the full permutation group on the sample indices or a subgroup thereof induced by the null hypothesis.

The exact permutation
p
-value is defined as

p_{\mathrm{exact}}(\mathcal{X})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}\sum_{\pi\in\mathcal{G}}\mathds{1}\{T(\mathcal{X}^{\pi})\geq T(\mathcal{X})\}.

(1)

Under standard invariance or exchangeability assumptions, the exact
permutation
p
-value is super-uniform under the null hypothesis:

\mathbb{P}(p_{\mathrm{exact}}(\mathcal{X})\leq\alpha)\leq\alpha
for all

\alpha\in[0,1]
; see
13
.

2.2
Monte Carlo approximation

When the permutation group
\mathcal{G}
is too large to enumerate exhaustively, one approximates the exact
p
-value by random sampling from
\mathcal{G}
. Specifically, let
\pi_{1},\ldots,\pi_{B}
be i.i.d. random transformations drawn uniformly from
\mathcal{G}
(with replacement). The Monte Carlo permutation
p
-value is defined as

p_{B}(\mathcal{X})=\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{B}\mathds{1}\{T(\mathcal{X}^{\pi_{i}})\geq T(\mathcal{X})\}}{B+1},\qquad\text{reject if }p_{B}(\mathcal{X})\leq\alpha.

(2)

The additive constant “
+1
” treats the observed statistic as one of the
B+1
exchangeable draws (equivalently, it corresponds to augmenting the resampled transformations with the identity), which ensures that
p_{B}(\mathcal{X})
is never zero and that the resulting test is finite-sample valid under the null
(
19
;
12
)
.

To analyze the behavior of this test, it is convenient to separate the randomness coming from the data
\mathcal{X}
and the randomness coming from the Monte Carlo sampling. Conditional on the observed data
\mathcal{X}
, define the single-draw exceedance probability

\displaystyle q(\mathcal{X})\;\coloneqq\;\mathbb{P}(T(\mathcal{X}^{\pi})\geq T(\mathcal{X})\mid\mathcal{X})\in[0,1],

(3)

where
\pi
denotes an independent random transformation drawn uniformly from
\mathcal{G}
. Thus
q(\mathcal{X})
is the conditional probability that a single randomly sampled permutation produces a test statistic at least as extreme as the observed one.

Given
\mathcal{X}
, the Monte Carlo exceedance indicators
\mathds{1}\{T(\mathcal{X}^{\pi_{i}})\geq T(\mathcal{X})\}
are i.i.d. Bernoulli random variables with success probability
q(\mathcal{X})
. Consequently, the total number of exceedances

\displaystyle R_{B}(\mathcal{X})\;\coloneqq\;\sum_{i=1}^{B}\mathds{1}\{T(\mathcal{X}^{\pi_{i}})\geq T(\mathcal{X})\}

satisfies the conditional binomial law

R_{B}(\mathcal{X})\mid\mathcal{X}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}\bigl(B,q(\mathcal{X})\bigr).

This conditional binomial structure is the key property underlying all subsequent power calculations. Finally, define the integer-valued critical count

k_{B}\;\coloneqq\;\lfloor(B+1)\alpha\rfloor-1.

With this notation, the Monte Carlo rejection event can be written equivalently as

\{p_{B}(\mathcal{X})\leq\alpha\}=\{R_{B}(\mathcal{X})\leq k_{B}\}.

(4)

The non-smooth, integer-valued dependence of
k_{B}
on
B
is the fundamental source of the non-monotonic power behavior analyzed in the remainder of the paper.

Conditional and unconditional power.

For a fixed dataset
\mathcal{X}
, define the conditional rejection probability

\displaystyle\phi_{B}(\mathcal{X})\;\coloneqq\;\mathbb{P}(p_{B}(\mathcal{X})\leq\alpha\mid\mathcal{X})=\mathbb{P}(R_{B}(\mathcal{X})\leq k_{B}\mid\mathcal{X}),

and, under an alternative distribution for
\mathcal{X}
, the unconditional power

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pow}(B)\;\coloneqq\;\mathbb{E}[\phi_{B}(\mathcal{X})].

Our focus is on how
\mathrm{Pow}(B)
behaves as a function of the Monte Carlo budget
B
.

3
Power non-monotonicity and sawtooth behavior in
B

A central mechanism behind the non-monotonicity studied in this section is the integer-valued rejection threshold
k_{B}=\lfloor(B+1)\alpha\rfloor-1
. As
B
increases,
k_{B}
can change only at discrete indices and is otherwise constant over intervals of
B
. Whenever
k_{B}
remains unchanged as
B
increases by one, the rejection rule compares an additional Bernoulli trial to the same critical count. In other words, the exceedance count
R_{B}
becomes stochastically larger while the cutoff is held fixed. This mechanism makes rejection
strictly harder
, not easier, for datasets with
q(\mathcal{X})\in(0,1)
, and it is a key structural source of the downward steps that occur within each plateau of
k_{B}
, contributing to the sawtooth behavior of the power curve analyzed below.

Plateaus and jumps of
k_{B}
.

The sequence
(k_{B})_{B\geq 1}
is piecewise constant and increases by at most one at each step. More precisely, for
B\geq 2
,

\displaystyle k_{B}-k_{B-1}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\lfloor(B+1)\alpha\rfloor=\lfloor B\alpha\rfloor+1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We call the case
k_{B}=k_{B-1}
a plateau and the case
k_{B}=k_{B-1}+1
a jump. The evolution of
k_{B}
thus consists of flat stretches (plateaus) interspersed with upward increments (jumps), and this discrete structure induces the characteristic sawtooth behavior of the power curve.

Before formalizing this mechanism, we impose a mild non-degeneracy condition on the conditional exceedance probability.

Assumption 1

(Non-degeneracy of the conditional exceedance probability)
.

The conditional exceedance probability is not almost surely equal to one, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\bigl(q(\mathcal{X})<1\bigr)>0.

The most interesting regime for power analysis is the non-degenerate case in which the conditional exceedance probability
q(\mathcal{X})
can take values strictly below
1
with positive probability. Note that the lower bound
q(\mathcal{X})>0
holds automatically. Indeed, the permutation group
\mathcal{G}
always contains the identity transformation
\mathrm{id}
, for which
\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{id}}=\mathcal{X}
and hence
T(\mathcal{X}^{\mathrm{id}})=T(\mathcal{X})
. Consequently,

\displaystyle q(\mathcal{X})=\mathbb{P}\big(T(\mathcal{X}^{\pi})\geq T(\mathcal{X})\mid\mathcal{X}\big)\geq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}|}>0.

Thus, the substantive restriction in
1
is that
q(\mathcal{X})
is not almost surely equal to
1
.

If
q(\mathcal{X})
is almost surely equal to
1
, then for every dataset all permutations would produce statistics at least as large as the observed one, implying
p_{B}(\mathcal{X})=1
for all
B
. The Monte Carlo test would therefore never reject, and the unconditional power would be identically zero and hence trivially monotone in
B
. By contrast, when
q(\mathcal{X})<1
with positive probability, the permutation distribution sometimes contains values strictly smaller than the observed statistic. In this non-degenerate regime, the number of exceedances among the
B
sampled permutations varies with the Monte Carlo draws, allowing the discrete plateau–jump structure of
k_{B}
to translate into genuine non-monotonicity of the unconditional power.

Theorem 1

(Non-monotonicity and infinitely many local maxima)
.

Fix
\alpha\in(0,1)
and consider the Monte Carlo permutation test (
2
), with
q(\mathcal{X})
,
k_{B}
, and
\mathrm{Pow}(B)
as defined in
Section

2
. Under
1
and
B\geq 2
, the following hold.

(i)

If
B
satisfies

\displaystyle k_{B}-k_{B-1}=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad k_{B+1}-k_{B}=0,

then
\mathrm{Pow}(B)
is a strict local maximum, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pow}(B-1)<\mathrm{Pow}(B)\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Pow}(B+1)<\mathrm{Pow}(B).

(ii)

There exist infinitely many integers
B
satisfying the above jump–plateau pattern (that is, a jump at
B
followed by a plateau at
B+1
, so that
k_{B-1}<k_{B}=k_{B+1}
). Consequently, the power curve
B\mapsto\mathrm{Pow}(B)
has infinitely many strict local maxima.

Remark 1
.

When
\alpha\in(0,1/2]
, the two conditions in
Theorem

1
(i) are not independent: the jump condition
k_{B}-k_{B-1}=1
alone implies
k_{B+1}-k_{B}=0
. Indeed, letting
\mathrm{frac}\{x\}\coloneqq x-\lfloor x\rfloor
denote the fractional part of
x
, a jump at
B
means
\mathrm{frac}\{B\alpha\}\geq 1-\alpha
, which forces
\mathrm{frac}\{(B+1)\alpha\}=\mathrm{frac}\{B\alpha\}+\alpha-1\in[0,\alpha)
. A jump at
B+1
would additionally require
\mathrm{frac}\{(B+1)\alpha\}\geq 1-\alpha
. But for
\alpha\leq 1/2
we have
[0,\alpha)\subseteq[0,1-\alpha)
, so
\mathrm{frac}\{(B+1)\alpha\}<1-\alpha
, ruling out a jump at
B+1
. Hence, for
\alpha\in(0,1/2]
, every jump index
B
is automatically a local maximizer of the power curve.

A simple practical criterion.

A convenient sufficient condition for the jump–plateau pattern in
Theorem

1
(i) is
(B+1)\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
. Indeed, if
(B+1)\alpha
is an integer, then
B\alpha
cannot be an integer (otherwise
\alpha=(B+1)\alpha-B\alpha
would be an integer, contradicting
\alpha\in(0,1)
), so necessarily
k_{B}-k_{B-1}=1
and
k_{B+1}-k_{B}=0
.

Hence, under
1
, every such
B
is a strict local maximizer of the power curve. As we show below (
Corollary

1
), for the common choices
\alpha\in\{0.1,0.05,0.01\}
this alignment condition also completely characterizes the set of strict local maxima.

The next proposition quantifies the size of each downward step and shows that it is
O(B^{-1/2})
as
B\to\infty
.

Proposition 1
.

In the setting of
Theorem

1
, suppose that
k_{B+1}=k_{B}=:k
. Then

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pow}(B)-\mathrm{Pow}(B+1)=\mathbb{E}\bigl[q(\mathcal{X})\,\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Binomial}(B,q(\mathcal{X}))=k)\bigr],

where the inner probability is taken with respect to a
\mathrm{Binomial}(B,q(\mathcal{X}))
distribution conditional on
\mathcal{X}
. Moreover, the decrease admits the distribution-free bound

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pow}(B)-\mathrm{Pow}(B+1)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(B+1)}}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}.

A refined design criterion.

Theorem

1
guarantees that every jump–plateau index is a strict local maximum of the power curve. The following result provides an explicit characterization of the complete set of such indices. For
\alpha\in(0,1)
, let

\mathcal{B}_{\alpha}=\bigl\{B\in\mathbb{N}:\mathrm{frac}\{(B+1)\alpha\}<\min(\alpha,1-\alpha)\bigr\}.

(5)

Indeed, a jump at
B
is equivalent to
\mathrm{frac}\{B\alpha\}\geq 1-\alpha
, which forces
\mathrm{frac}\{(B+1)\alpha\}=\mathrm{frac}\{B\alpha\}+\alpha-1\in[0,\alpha)
. A subsequent plateau at
B+1
is equivalent to
\mathrm{frac}\{(B+1)\alpha\}<1-\alpha
. Combining these conditions yields (
5
).

For
\alpha\in(0,1/2]
,
Remark

1
shows the plateau condition is automatically satisfied, so (
5
) simplifies to

\mathcal{B}_{\alpha}=\bigl\{B\in\mathbb{N}:\mathrm{frac}\{(B+1)\alpha\}<\alpha\bigr\}=\bigl\{\lceil j/\alpha\rceil-1:j\in\mathbb{N},\,j\geq 1\bigr\},

(6)

where the second equality holds since
\mathrm{frac}\{(B+1)\alpha\}<\alpha
if and only if
B+1=\lceil j/\alpha\rceil
for
j\coloneqq\lfloor(B+1)\alpha\rfloor\geq 1
.

Corollary 1

(Characterization for rational levels)
.

When
\alpha=1/m
for an integer
m\geq 2
, equation (
6
) reduces to

\mathcal{B}_{\alpha}=\bigl\{B\in\mathbb{N}:(B+1)\alpha\in\mathbb{N}\bigr\}.

(7)

The common levels
\alpha\in\{0.1,0.05,0.01\}
are all of the form
\alpha=1/m
and thus fall under
Corollary

1
, which follows immediately from (
6
): when
\alpha=1/m
, the ceiling
\lceil j/\alpha\rceil=jm
is always an integer, giving
B=jm-1
, i.e.,
(B+1)\alpha=j\in\mathbb{N}
. Together with the direct argument above, this shows that for these levels the alignment condition
(B+1)\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
not only provides a sufficient condition for a strict local maximum, but also exactly characterizes the entire set
\mathcal{B}_{\alpha}
.

The results above establish that non-monotonicity of power is a structural feature of Monte Carlo permutation tests and provide sharp finite-
B
bounds on the size of individual downward steps. We now turn to concrete examples that make this mechanism fully explicit.

4
A concrete example

We now illustrate the non-monotonicity mechanism in a fully explicit example at level
\alpha=0.05
. This example serves two purposes: first, it yields a closed-form expression for the unconditional power; second, it makes transparent how the integer-valued rejection threshold induces a deterministic sawtooth dependence of the power on the Monte Carlo budget
B
.

Fix an integer
n\geq 2
and consider a two-group experiment with
n
units per group and binary outcomes. Under the alternative, the treated group has success probability
p_{1}\in(0,1)
and the control group has success probability
p_{0}=0
.

Let the first
n
indices correspond to the treated group and the remaining
n
to the control group. Specifically, define

\displaystyle X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Bernoulli}(p_{1}),\qquad Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathrm{Bernoulli}(0),

and form the pooled outcome vector

\displaystyle Z=(Z_{1},\ldots,Z_{2n})\coloneqq(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})\in\{0,1\}^{2n}.

Let

\displaystyle S\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{n}Z_{i}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(n,p_{1})

denote the observed number of treated successes.
Since
Z_{n+1}=\cdots=Z_{2n}=0
almost surely, the total number of successes in the pooled sample equals
S
. Let
\mathcal{I}\coloneqq\{I\subset[2n]:|I|=n\}
denote the collection of all possible treatment labelings of size
n
, so that
|\mathcal{I}|=\binom{2n}{n}
. The original treatment group is indexed by
I_{0}=\{1,\ldots,n\}.
For any
I\in\mathcal{I}
, define the treated success count

\displaystyle T_{I}(Z)\coloneqq\sum_{i\in I}Z_{i},

that is, the number of successes assigned to the treated group under labeling
I
. In particular, under the observed labeling we have
T_{I_{0}}(Z)=S
.

Figure 1:
Unconditional power curves for the closed-form Bernoulli example at level
\alpha=0.05
with
p_{0}=0
and
p_{1}=0.16
. Solid line: Monte Carlo power
\mathrm{Pow}(B)
. Dashed line: exact-test power
\mathrm{Pow}_{\mathrm{exact}}
. Dots mark the predicted local maxima where
(B+1)\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
.

Conditional exceedance probability.

Given
S=s
, the conditional exceedance probability is

\displaystyle q(s)\;\coloneqq\;\mathbb{P}(T_{\pi}(Z)\geq T_{I_{0}}(Z)\mid S=s)=\mathbb{P}(T_{\pi}(Z)=s\mid S=s),

where
\pi
is uniform on
\mathcal{I}
. The event
\{T_{\pi}(Z)=s\}
occurs if and only if the random labeling places all
s
success indices in the treated group. There are
\binom{2n-s}{n-s}
such labelings, hence

\displaystyle q(s)=\frac{\binom{2n-s}{n-s}}{\binom{2n}{n}}\in(0,1]\qquad\text{for $s=0,1,\ldots,n$}.

Closed-form power expression.

With Monte Carlo sampling with replacement,

\displaystyle R_{B}\mid S=s\sim\mathrm{Binomial}\bigl(B,q(s)\bigr),\qquad k_{B}\coloneqq\lfloor(B+1)\alpha\rfloor-1,

and the conditional rejection probability is

\displaystyle\phi_{B}(s)=\mathbb{P}(R_{B}\leq k_{B}\mid S=s)=F_{B,q(s)}(k_{B}),

where
F_{B,q(s)}
denotes the cumulative distribution function of
\mathrm{Binomial}(B,q(s))
. Averaging over the data-generating distribution of
S
yields the unconditional power

\mathrm{Pow}(B)=\mathbb{E}\big[\phi_{B}(S)\big]=\sum_{s=0}^{n}\binom{n}{s}p_{1}^{s}(1-p_{1})^{n-s}\,F_{B,q(s)}(k_{B}).

(8)

This expression is fully explicit and closed-form.

Non-monotonicity.

Here
q(0)=1
, while
q(s)\in(0,1)
for every
s\in\{1,\dots,n\}
. Thus the non-degeneracy condition
1
holds as soon as
\mathbb{P}(S\geq 1)>0
(i.e.,
p_{1}\in(0,1)
). In that case,
Theorem

1
implies that the unconditional power in (
8
) need not be monotone non-decreasing in
B
.

In this closed-form example, the dependence of
\mathrm{Pow}(B)
on
B
is entirely driven by the integer-valued cutoff
k_{B}
, and the power curve therefore exhibits the deterministic sawtooth pattern described earlier, with strict local maxima at those
B
for which
(B+1)\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
.

The qualitative shape of this sawtooth curve, however, does not by itself determine how the Monte Carlo test compares to the exact permutation test. Depending on
(n,p_{1})
, the Monte Carlo power can lie above or below the exact power curve for substantial ranges of
B
. We illustrate these two contrasting behaviors below and also in
Appendix

C
.

Case 1: Exact test has larger power (
n=15
).

Take
n=15
and
p_{1}=0.16
at level
\alpha=0.05
. In this setting,

\displaystyle q(3)\approx 0.1121>\alpha,\qquad q(4)\approx 0.0498<\alpha,

so the exact permutation test rejects if and only if
S\geq 4
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pow}_{\mathrm{exact}}=\mathbb{P}(S\geq 4)\approx 0.209218.

In this configuration, the Monte Carlo power curve lies entirely below the exact power curve for all
B
(see the left panel of
Figure

1
), although it converges to
\mathrm{Pow}_{\mathrm{exact}}
as
B\to\infty
, as shown in
Appendix

C
for a broader range of
B
.

Case 2: Exact test has smaller power (
n=25
).

Now let
n=25
and
p_{1}=0.16
at the same level
\alpha=0.05
. Then
S\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(25,0.16)
and

\displaystyle q(4)\approx 0.0549>\alpha,\qquad q(5)\approx 0.0251<\alpha.

Hence the exact permutation test rejects if and only if
S\geq 5
, giving

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pow}_{\mathrm{exact}}=\mathbb{P}(S\geq 5)\approx 0.370668.

In this configuration, for moderate values of
B
, the Monte Carlo test can be strictly more powerful than the exact test (see the right panel of
Figure

1
). Nevertheless, as
B\to\infty
, the Monte Carlo power converges to the exact power, i.e.,
\mathrm{Pow}(B)\to\mathrm{Pow}_{\mathrm{exact}}
(see
Appendix

C
for extended results).

Appendix
B
further demonstrates that this non-monotonic behavior also arises for a wider class of test statistics commonly used in practical applications.

5
Main takeaways

Our results should not be interpreted as “more permutations are bad.”
In many respects, the opposite is true: increasing the Monte Carlo budget
B
reduces the simulation variability of the Monte Carlo
p
-value and makes
p_{B}(\mathcal{X})
a more stable approximation to the full-group permutation
p
-value. It also refines the discrete set of attainable Monte Carlo
p
-values. The focus of this paper is more specific. Because the rejection threshold is discrete, increasing
B
does not in general guarantee a monotone increase in power. With that in mind, the main practical messages are as follows.

•

The number of permutations
B
is part of the test definition and should be prespecified. Although Monte Carlo error decreases as
B
grows, the induced decision rule
\mathds{1}\{p_{B}(\mathcal{X})\leq\alpha\}
need not become more powerful in a monotone way. Thus “more” does not necessarily mean “better” when power at level
\alpha
is the primary objective. A natural design recommendation is to choose
B
, subject to computational constraints, so that
(B+1)\alpha\in\mathbb{N}
. This alignment is consistent with earlier analyses
(
14
;
15
)
, and our results provide a complementary structural reason for it.

•

If one is willing to introduce auxiliary randomization, we recommend using the randomized Monte Carlo permutation
p
-value

\displaystyle p_{B}^{\mathrm{rand}}(\mathcal{X})=p_{B}(\mathcal{X})-U\frac{1+\sum_{i=1}^{B}\mathds{1}\{T(\mathcal{X}^{\pi_{i}})=T(\mathcal{X})\}}{B+1},

where
U\sim\mathrm{Uniform}(0,1)
is independent of
\mathcal{X}
. Under the null, this test rejects with probability exactly
\alpha

(
12
, Proposition 3)
and its power is no smaller than that of the nonrandomized test. Intuitively, the extra randomization smooths the discreteness and correspondingly dampens the sawtooth pattern in the power curve.

•

Rather than fixing
B
in advance, sequential procedures
(
2
;
9
;
6
)
can adaptively determine how many permutations to draw, based on the observed data, while controlling type I error at level
\alpha
. These methods can be more computationally efficient than fixed-
B
tests when the decision is relatively clear, that is, when the resulting
p
-value is likely to be very small or very large.

We hope that our results will encourage practitioners to think more carefully about the choice of
B
and to consider alternative approaches when appropriate. In particular, the non-monotonicity phenomenon highlights the importance of understanding the interplay between computational resources and statistical properties in permutation testing.

Proofs

This section contains the proofs of
Theorem

and
Proposition

A.1
Proof of
Theorem

1

Proof.

Recall that
k_{B}=\lfloor(B+1)\alpha\rfloor-1
. For
B\geq 2
, the difference between successive critical counts is given by

\displaystyle k_{B}-k_{B-1}=\begin{cases}1,&\text{if }\lfloor(B+1)\alpha\rfloor=\lfloor B\alpha\rfloor+1,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We prove the two claims in turn. Fix
B\geq 2
such that

\displaystyle k_{B}-k_{B-1}=1\qquad\text{and}\qquad k_{B+1}-k_{B}=0.

Let
A:=\{q(\mathcal{X})<1\}
, which satisfies
\mathbb{P}(A)>0
by
1
. Conditionally on
\mathcal{X}
, let

\displaystyle R_{B-1}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(B-1,q(\mathcal{X})),\qquad R_{B}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(B,q(\mathcal{X})).

Then

\displaystyle\phi_{B-1}(\mathcal{X})=\mathbb{P}(R_{B-1}\leq k_{B-1}\mid\mathcal{X}),\qquad\phi_{B}(\mathcal{X})=\mathbb{P}(R_{B}\leq k_{B}\mid\mathcal{X}).

Since
k_{B}=k_{B-1}+1
, moving from
B-1
to
B
adds one Bernoulli trial and relaxes the rejection threshold by one. Couple
R_{B}=R_{B-1}+Y
, where
Y\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(q(\mathcal{X}))
is independent of
R_{B-1}
given
\mathcal{X}
. Then we can decompose the probability as follows:

\displaystyle\phi_{B}(\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(R_{B-1}+Y\leq k_{B-1}+1\mid\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(Y=0\mid\mathcal{X})\mathbb{P}(R_{B-1}\leq k_{B-1}+1\mid\mathcal{X})+\mathbb{P}(Y=1\mid\mathcal{X})\mathbb{P}(R_{B-1}\leq k_{B-1}\mid\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=(1-q(\mathcal{X}))\big(\mathbb{P}(R_{B-1}\leq k_{B-1}\mid\mathcal{X})+\mathbb{P}(R_{B-1}=k_{B-1}+1\mid\mathcal{X})\big)

\displaystyle\quad+q(\mathcal{X})\mathbb{P}(R_{B-1}\leq k_{B-1}\mid\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(R_{B-1}\leq k_{B-1}\mid\mathcal{X})+(1-q(\mathcal{X}))\mathbb{P}(R_{B-1}=k_{B-1}+1\mid\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=\phi_{B-1}(\mathcal{X})+(1-q(\mathcal{X}))\mathbb{P}(R_{B-1}=k_{B-1}+1\mid\mathcal{X}).

On the set
A
, we have
1-q(\mathcal{X})>0
. Furthermore, since
k_{B-1}+1=k_{B}\leq B-1
, the binomial mass
\mathbb{P}(R_{B-1}=k_{B-1}+1\mid\mathcal{X})
is strictly positive. This strictly increases the binomial left-tail probability:

\displaystyle\phi_{B-1}(\mathcal{X})<\phi_{B}(\mathcal{X})\quad\text{on }A,

while equality holds on
A^{c}
where
q(\mathcal{X})=1
.
Taking expectations gives

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pow}(B-1)<\mathrm{Pow}(B).

Next, since
k_{B+1}=k_{B}=:k
, we are on a plateau. Couple

\displaystyle R_{B+1}=R_{B}+Y^{\prime},\qquad Y^{\prime}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(q(\mathcal{X})),

with
Y^{\prime}
independent of
R_{B}
given
\mathcal{X}
. Then

\displaystyle\phi_{B+1}(\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(R_{B}+Y^{\prime}\leq k\mid\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(R_{B}\leq k\mid\mathcal{X})-\mathbb{P}(Y^{\prime}=1\mid\mathcal{X})\mathbb{P}(R_{B}=k\mid\mathcal{X})

\displaystyle=\phi_{B}(\mathcal{X})-q(\mathcal{X})\,\mathbb{P}(R_{B}=k\mid\mathcal{X}).

For
q(\mathcal{X})\in(0,1)
and
0\leq k\leq B
(note that
k\geq 0
is naturally guaranteed by the preceding jump
k_{B}=k_{B-1}+1\geq 0
), the binomial mass
\mathbb{P}(R_{B}=k\mid\mathcal{X})
is strictly positive, hence

\displaystyle\phi_{B+1}(\mathcal{X})<\phi_{B}(\mathcal{X})\quad\text{on }A,

with equality again on
A^{c}
. Taking expectations yields

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pow}(B+1)<\mathrm{Pow}(B).

Thus
\mathrm{Pow}(B)
is a strict local maximum, proving part (i).

For part (ii), define the backward difference

\displaystyle d_{B}\coloneqq k_{B}-k_{B-1}\in\{0,1\}\qquad\text{for }B\geq 2.

Hence
(k_{B})
is nondecreasing and increases by at most one at each step. Moreover,

\displaystyle(B+1)\alpha-2<k_{B}\leq(B+1)\alpha-1,

so that
k_{B}/B\to\alpha\in(0,1)
.

We first show that both values of
d_{B}
occur infinitely often. If
d_{B}=1
for all sufficiently large
B
, then for some
B_{0}
and all
B>B_{0}
we would have

\displaystyle k_{B}=k_{B_{0}}+\sum_{j=B_{0}+1}^{B}d_{j}=k_{B_{0}}+(B-B_{0}),

so that

\displaystyle\frac{k_{B}}{B}=1+\frac{k_{B_{0}}-B_{0}}{B}\rightarrow 1,

contradicting
k_{B}/B\to\alpha<1
. Thus
d_{B}=0
must occur infinitely often. A symmetric argument shows that
d_{B}=1
must also occur infinitely often, since if
d_{B}=0
eventually, then
k_{B}
would be eventually constant and hence
k_{B}/B\to 0
, contradicting
\alpha>0
.

We now show that the pattern
(d_{B},d_{B+1})=(1,0)
occurs infinitely often. Suppose, for contradiction, that it occurs only finitely many times. Then there exists
B_{1}
such that for all
B\geq B_{1}
,

\displaystyle d_{B}=1\;\implies\;d_{B+1}=1.

In other words, beyond
B_{1}
, once a jump occurs it must be followed by another jump.

Since
d_{B}=1
occurs infinitely often, choose
B^{\star}\geq B_{1}
such that
d_{B^{\star}}=1
.
By the implication above, we then have

\displaystyle d_{B^{\star}+1}=1,\quad d_{B^{\star}+2}=1,\quad\ldots

and thus
d_{B}=1
for all
B\geq B^{\star}
.
But this contradicts the fact that
d_{B}=0
occurs infinitely often. Therefore the implication cannot hold, and the pattern
(1,0)
must occur infinitely many times.

By part (i), each such index
B
yields a strict local maximum of
\mathrm{Pow}(B)
. Hence the power curve has infinitely many strict local maxima.
∎

A.2
Proof of
Proposition

1

Proof.

Write
q\coloneqq q(\mathcal{X})
and assume
k_{B+1}=k_{B}=:k
. If
k=-1
, then
\phi_{B}(\mathcal{X})=\mathbb{P}(R_{B}\leq-1\mid\mathcal{X})=0
and likewise
\phi_{B+1}(\mathcal{X})=0
, meaning the power difference is trivially zero and the bound holds. Thus, we may assume
k\geq 0
. Since
\alpha\in(0,1)
, we also naturally have
k=\lfloor(B+1)\alpha\rfloor-1\leq B-1
.

Conditional on
\mathcal{X}
, we couple the binomial variables as
R_{B+1}=R_{B}+Y
, where
R_{B}\mid\mathcal{X}\sim\mathrm{Binomial}(B,q)
and
Y\mid\mathcal{X}\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(q)
are independent. Following the exact same logic as in the proof of
Theorem

1
, the difference in conditional power is exactly
q\,\mathbb{P}(R_{B}=k\mid\mathcal{X})
. Taking expectations over
\mathcal{X}
yields the claimed identity:

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pow}(B)-\mathrm{Pow}(B+1)=\mathbb{E}\big[q(\mathcal{X})\,\mathbb{P}(R_{B}=k\mid\mathcal{X})\big].

To bound this quantity uniformly, let
b(k;B,q)\coloneqq\binom{B}{k}q^{k}(1-q)^{B-k}
denote the binomial probability mass function. The expected decrease is bounded by the supremum
\sup_{q\in[0,1]}q\cdot b(k;B,q)
. To find this supremum, we maximize the logarithm of the objective,
\log\bigl(q^{k+1}(1-q)^{B-k}\bigr)=(k+1)\log q+(B-k)\log(1-q)
. Taking the derivative with respect to
q
and setting it to zero gives

\displaystyle\frac{k+1}{q}-\frac{B-k}{1-q}=0\implies(k+1)(1-q)=(B-k)q\implies k+1=(B+1)q.

This vanishes uniquely at
q^{\star}=(k+1)/(B+1)
. Since the log function is strictly concave on
(0,1)
, this critical point is the global maximizer.

Setting
m\coloneqq k+1
and
N\coloneqq B+1
, we have
q^{\star}=m/N
. We can relate the binomial mass at
B
to the mass at
N
through the identity
\binom{B}{k}=\binom{B}{m-1}=\frac{m}{B+1}\binom{B+1}{m}=q^{\star}\binom{N}{m}
. Using this relation, we observe the following crucial simplification:

\displaystyle b(k;B,q^{\star})

\displaystyle=\binom{B}{k}(q^{\star})^{k}(1-q^{\star})^{B-k}

\displaystyle=\bigg\{q^{\star}\binom{N}{m}\bigg\}(q^{\star})^{m-1}(1-q^{\star})^{N-m}

\displaystyle=\binom{N}{m}(q^{\star})^{m}(1-q^{\star})^{N-m}=b(m;N,q^{\star}).

We bound this probability mass using Robbins’ bounds for Stirling’s formula
(
24
)
, which guarantee that
n!
is bounded between
\sqrt{2\pi}\,n^{n+1/2}e^{-n+1/(12n+1)}
and
\sqrt{2\pi}\,n^{n+1/2}e^{-n+1/(12n)}
for any integer
n\geq 1
. Since
0\leq k\leq B-1
, both
m\geq 1
and
N-m\geq 1
are satisfied, allowing us to apply these bounds to
N!
,
m!
, and
(N-m)!
. The terms
e^{-N}
in the numerator and
e^{-m}e^{-(N-m)}
in the denominator cancel out perfectly. Substituting these into the definition of
b(m;N,q^{\star})
and extracting the fractional powers yields

\displaystyle b(m;N,q^{\star})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\sqrt{2\pi}\,N^{N+1/2}}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,m^{m+1/2}\,\sqrt{2\pi}\,(N-m)^{N-m+1/2}}\exp\Bigl(\tfrac{1}{12N}-\tfrac{1}{12m+1}-\tfrac{1}{12(N-m)+1}\Bigr)

\displaystyle\qquad\times(q^{\star})^{m}(1-q^{\star})^{N-m}.

By recognizing that
(q^{\star})^{m}(1-q^{\star})^{N-m}=(m/N)^{m}((N-m)/N)^{N-m}=m^{m}(N-m)^{N-m}/N^{N}
, we can explicitly cancel all the terms with exponents of
m
,
N-m
, and
N
. After cancellation, the bound simplifies to

\displaystyle b(m;N,q^{\star})\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\sqrt{\frac{N}{m(N-m)}}\exp\biggl(\frac{1}{12N}-\frac{1}{12m+1}-\frac{1}{12(N-m)+1}\biggr).

The argument inside the exponential function is strictly negative because

\displaystyle\frac{1}{12m+1}+\frac{1}{12(N-m)+1}\geq\frac{4}{(12m+1)+(12(N-m)+1)}=\frac{2}{6N+1}>\frac{1}{12N}.

Consequently, the exponential factor is strictly bounded by
1
. Since
\frac{m}{N}=q^{\star}
and
\frac{N-m}{N}=1-q^{\star}
, we can rewrite the remaining square root as
(2\pi N\,q^{\star}(1-q^{\star}))^{-1/2}
, meaning
b(k;B,q^{\star})\leq(2\pi N\,q^{\star}(1-q^{\star}))^{-1/2}
. Multiplying both sides by
q^{\star}
gives

\displaystyle q^{\star}\,b(k;B,q^{\star})\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(B+1)}}\sqrt{\frac{q^{\star}}{1-q^{\star}}}.

Finally, under our plateau assumption
k=\lfloor(B+1)\alpha\rfloor-1
, we have
q^{\star}\leq\alpha
. Because the function
x\mapsto x/(1-x)
is strictly increasing on
(0,1)
, we may replace
q^{\star}
by
\alpha
to achieve the desired distribution-free bound

\displaystyle\mathrm{Pow}(B)-\mathrm{Pow}(B+1)\leq\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi(B+1)}}\sqrt{\frac{\alpha}{1-\alpha}}.

This proves the claimed bound.
∎
