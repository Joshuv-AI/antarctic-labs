Fitting Horn DL Ontologies to ABox and Query Examples: A Tale of Simulation Quantifiers and Finite Models
Marvin Grosser, Carsten Lutz \affiliations Leipzig University

Abstract
We study the problem of fitting a description logic (DL) ontology to a given set of positive and negative examples in the form of an ABox and a Boolean query. While previous work has investigated this problem for the expressive DLs 𝒜ℒ𝒞 {\cal ALC} and 𝒜ℒ𝒞ℐ {\cal ALCI} , we here focus on the Horn DLs ℰ ​ ℒ {\cal E\!\!\>L} and ℰ ​ ℒℐ {\cal E\!\!\>LI} , as well as their extensions with the bottom concept. As the query language, we consider atomic queries (AQs), conjunctive queries (CQs), and unions thereof (UCQs). We provide characterizations of the existence of a fitting ontology based on simulations, use them to develop decision procedures, and clarify the exact computational complexity. For AQs, the problem is in PTime for both ℰ ​ ℒ {\cal E\!\!\>L} and ℰ ​ ℒℐ {\cal E\!\!\>LI} . For CQs and UCQs, it is Σ 2 P \Sigma^{\textsc{P}}_{2} -complete for ℰ ​ ℒ {\cal E\!\!\>L} and ExpTime -complete for ℰ ​ ℒℐ {\cal E\!\!\>LI} . Adding the bottom concept does not change any of these complexities. Interestingly, moving from 𝒜ℒ𝒞 {\cal ALC} and 𝒜ℒ𝒞ℐ {\cal ALCI} to ℰ ​ ℒ {\cal E\!\!\>L} and ℰ ​ ℒℐ {\cal E\!\!\>LI} introduces additional technical challenges rather than simplifying the matter.

Introduction

In knowledge representation
and databases, fitting problems aim to support
human engineers in constructing ontologies and queries. Given as input a collection of positively and
negatively labeled examples, a fitting algorithm outputs a formal object that fits all examples
or reports that no such object exists. The study of fitting problems originated in databases, where the formal object to be constructed is a query
[
32
]
. This line of work
is known as query-by-example, and sometimes as
reverse query engineering, and it continues to be studied today, see for instance
[
9
,
4
,
28
]
and references therein.
Although not the focus of this article, fitting problems are also closely
related to machine learning, where fitting a model to a set of examples
is linked to PAC-style generalization guarantees
[
26
]
.

Fitting problems have also attracted considerable interest in ontology-based KR,
particularly in the context of description logics (DLs).
There are at least two ways in which fitting problems can support ontology engineering. On the one hand,
they can help an engineer construct

concepts
that can then serve as building blocks in an ontology. On the other hand, fitting can be used to construct entire
ontologies
, either for
stand-alone use or as a module in a larger ontology under development.

Interest in concept fitting first emerged under the label of
DL concept learning
although, unlike in machine learning, generalization guarantees were typically not considered; see
[
29
]
for an exception. This research line led to the development of practical systems, most prominently DL Learner
[
20
,
8
]
. Starting with

[
14
]
, the
focus shifted to algorithms for concept fitting that are sound, complete and terminating, as well as to
investigations of the decidability and computational complexity of concept fitting in the presence of an ontology;
see for instance
[
19
]
and the references therein.

Ontology fitting, by contrast, has attracted attention only relatively recently. A key design choice
concerns the form of the examples.
One natural option, adopted in
[
18
]
, is to use interpretations (logical structures)
as examples. Another,
used in
[
13
]
, is to use pairs
(\mathcal{A},q)
where
\mathcal{A}
is an ABox and
q
is a (Boolean) query. For a positive example, a fitting ontology
\mathcal{O}
must then satisfy
\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{O}\models q
while for a negative example it must satisfy
\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{O}\not\models q
. The latter setting
is particularly relevant in the context of ontology-mediated querying
[
5
,
31
]
.

In this article, we study the problem of fitting ontologies to ABox-query examples. Whereas previous work has focused on
expressive DLs such as
{\cal ALC}
and
{\cal ALCI}
as the ontology language, we instead
consider Horn DLs, especially
{\cal E\!\!\>L}
,
{\cal E\!\!\>LI}
, and their extensions with the bottom concept ‘
\bot
’. These DLs are of particular interest because they are computationally more tractable than their expressive counterparts. In particular,
{\cal E\!\!\>L}
admits subsumption and querying in polynomial time for tree-shaped queries, and
both
{\cal E\!\!\>L}
and
{\cal E\!\!\>LI}
are supported by so-called consequence-based reasoning algorithms that are highly efficient in practice
[
10
]
.
This has contributed to an extension of
{\cal E\!\!\>L}
becoming one of three profiles of
the OWL 2 ontology language standard
[
23
]
. As query languages,
we consider atomic queries (AQs), conjunctive queries (CQs), and unions thereof (UCQs).
In addition, we consider a fitting problem
in which the examples consist of an ABox only and where we seek an ontology that is consistent with the positive examples and inconsistent with the negative ones. For all these fitting
problems, we
provide effective characterizations and
determine the precise complexity of deciding whether a fitting ontology exists. Our
algorithms also produce explicit
fitting ontologies when they exist.

Our characterizations resemble those
obtained in
[
13
]
for the expressive DLs
{\cal ALC}
and
{\cal ALCI}
, but they also exhibit notable differences.
To begin with, the
characterizations for
\mathcal{ALC}
and
{\cal ALCI}
are based on homomorphisms which ours replace with simulations. This introduces technical challenges, especially for
CQs and UCQs as the query language,
intuitively because homomorphisms are more local in nature than simulations. For example, a reflexive loop must map homomorphically to a reflexive loop, but it may be simulated by an infinite path. When turning our characterizations into fitting algorithms, we are therefore led to employ the
extension of
{\cal E\!\!\>L}
and
{\cal E\!\!\>LI}
with simulation quantifiers as studied
for the
{\cal E\!\!\>L}
case in
[
21
]
. In the case
of
{\cal E\!\!\>LI}
, an additional complication
arises: the non-local nature of
simulations clashes, in a certain sense, with the more local
nature of
{\cal E\!\!\>LI}
-concepts used in ontologies. We address this issue by working with finite interpretations rather than with unrestricted ones in our characterizations, and by considering finite model reasoning in
{\cal E\!\!\>LI}
extended with simulation quantifiers.

We also discuss, throughout the paper, the size and nature of fitting ontologies.
For AQs and for consistency-based fitting, the existence of a fitting ontology coincides with the existence of a fitting ontology that does not contain auxiliary symbols (i.e., concept or role names not present in the examples). For conjunctive queries and UCQs, this is not the case. In particular, a fresh role name is required to deal with disconnected queries.
We also observe that, in most cases, admitting auxiliary symbols enables the
construction of fitting ontologies of polynomial size while, without such symbols, they are of single exponential size. The sole exception is the
case of
{\cal E\!\!\>LI}
and (U)CQs, where we do not obtain any bounds on the size of fitting ontologies at all.

an individual.
A
union of conjunctive queries (UCQ)

q
is a disjunction of CQs. A CQ is
rooted

if every variable is reachable from an individual name in the undirected graph
G_{q}=(\mathsf{ind}(q)\cup\mathsf{var}(q),\{\{t,t^{\prime}\}\mid r(t,t^{\prime})\in q\})
. A UCQ is
rooted
if
every CQ in it is.

A CQ
q
gives rise to an interpretation

\mathcal{I}_{q}
with
\Delta^{\mathcal{I}_{q}}=\mathsf{ind}(q)\cup\mathsf{var}(q)
,
A^{\mathcal{I}_{q}}=\{t\mid A(t)\in q\}
, and
r^{\mathcal{I}_{q}}=\{(t,t^{\prime})\mid r(t,t^{\prime})\in q\}
for all
A\in{\sf N_{C}}
and
r\in{\sf N_{R}}
.
With a homomorphism from a CQ
q
to an
interpretation
\mathcal{I}
, we mean a homomorphism from
\mathcal{I}_{q}
to
\mathcal{I}
that is the identity
on all individual names.
For an interpretation
\mathcal{I}
and a UCQ
q
, we write

\mathcal{I}\models q
if there is a
homomorphism
h
from a CQ in
q
to
\mathcal{I}
. For an ABox
\mathcal{A}
and ontology
\mathcal{O}
, we write
\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{O}\models q
if
\mathcal{I}\models q
for all models

\mathcal{I}
of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{O}
.

We shall also consider queries that
in essence are DL concepts. Let
\mathcal{L}\in\{{\cal E\!\!\>L},{\cal E\!\!\>L}_{\bot},{\cal E\!\!\>LI},{\cal E\!\!\>LI}_{\bot}\}
. A
rooted
\mathcal{L}
-query
takes
the form
C(a)
with
C
an
\mathcal{L}
-concept and
a\in{\sf N_{I}}
. We write

\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{O}\models q
if
a\in C^{\mathcal{I}}
for all models

\mathcal{I}
of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{O}
. An
existential
\mathcal{L}
-query

takes the form
\exists x\,C(x)

with
C
an
\mathcal{L}
-concept. We set

\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{O}\models q
if
C^{\mathcal{I}}\neq\emptyset
for all models

\mathcal{I}
of
\mathcal{A}
and
\mathcal{O}
. When speaking only of an
\mathcal{L}
-query, we mean an
\mathcal{L}
-query that is either rooted or existential. There is also a finite
version of
\mathcal{L}
-query entailment,
denoted by ‘
\models^{\mathsf{fin}}
’. While the unrestricted and the finite version coincide for the DLs
\mathcal{L}
listed above,
this is not the case for some of their
extensions introduced later on.
It is easy to
see that every
{\cal E\!\!\>L}
- and
{\cal E\!\!\>LI}
-query can be expressed as a (tree-shaped) CQ.

Note that all queries introduced above are Boolean, that is, they evaluate to true or false instead of producing answers. This is without loss of generality since we
admit individual names in queries.

We use
||O||
to denote the
size
of a syntactic object
O
such as an ontology
or an ABox. It is defined as the length
of the encoding of
O
as a word over a suitable alphabet.

2.4
ABox Examples and the Fitting Problem

Let
\mathcal{Q}
be a query language such as
\mathcal{Q}=\text{AQ}
or
\mathcal{Q}=\text{CQ}
. An

such that all individual names that appear in

By a

and
\mathcal{A}\cup\mathcal{O}\not\models q
for all
(\mathcal{A},q)\in E^{-}
. The
following example illustrates this central

Example 1
.

Let

be the collection of labeled ABox-CQ examples where

contains the two examples

\begin{array}[]{l}(\ \{\,\mathsf{NewHire}(\mathsf{jane})\,\},\ \exists x\,\mathsf{mentor}(\mathsf{jane},x)\wedge\mathsf{Emp}(x)\ )\\[5.69054pt]
(\ \{\,\mathsf{NewHire}(\mathsf{alex}),\ \mathsf{RemoteWorker}(\mathsf{alex})\,\},\\[2.84526pt]
\qquad\qquad\exists x\ \mathsf{mentor}(\mathsf{alex},x)\wedge\mathsf{SeniorEmp}(x)\ ).\end{array}

and
E^{-}
contains the single
example

(\{\ \mathsf{NewHire}(\mathsf{bob})\,\},\ \exists x\ \mathsf{mentor}(\mathsf{bob},x)\wedge\mathsf{SeniorEmp}(x)\ ).

Then, the
{\cal E\!\!\>L}
-ontology
\mathcal{O}
that contains the CIs

\begin{array}[]{r@{\;}c@{\;}l}\mathsf{NewHire}&\sqsubseteq&\exists\mathsf{mentor}.\mathsf{Emp}\\[2.84526pt]
\mathsf{NewHire}\sqcap\mathsf{RemoteWorker}&\sqsubseteq&\exists\mathsf{mentor}.\mathsf{SeniorEmp}\end{array}

fits all examples in
E
.
If we further add the
CI
\mathsf{SeniorEmp}\sqsubseteq\mathsf{Emp}
, the ontology still fits.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be an ontology language, such as
\mathcal{L}={\cal E\!\!\>LI}_{\bot}
, and
\mathcal{Q}
a query
language. Then
(
\mathcal{L}
,
\mathcal{Q}
)-ontology fitting
is the problem
to decide, given as

labeled
ABox-

admits a fitting
\mathcal{L}
-ontology.
We generally assume that the ABoxes used in

have pairwise disjoint sets of individual names.
It is not hard to verify that this is without loss of generality because consistently renaming individual names in a collection of examples has no impact on the existence of a fitting ontology.

There is a natural variation of

(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{Q})
-ontology fitting
where one additionally requires the fitting ontology to be consistent with all ABoxes that occur in examples. We then speak of

consistent
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{Q})
-ontology fitting
. The following observation from
[
13
]
shows that it suffices to
design algorithms for
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{Q})
-ontology fitting as originally introduced.

Proposition 1
.

Let
\mathcal{L}
be any ontology language and
\mathcal{Q}\in\{\text{AQ},\text{CQ},\text{UCQ}\}
. Then there is a
PTime
-reduction from consistent
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{Q})
-ontology fitting
to
(\mathcal{L},\mathcal{Q})
-ontology fitting.

3
Consistency-Based Fitting

We start with a version of ontology fitting that is based on ABox consistency rather than on querying.
Besides being natural in its own right,
it also serves to showcase some central techniques used in this paper.
Here, an example is simply an ABox, and
an ontology
\mathcal{O}

fits
a collection

if
\mathcal{A}
is consistent with
\mathcal{O}
for all
\mathcal{A}\in E^{+}
and inconsistent with
\mathcal{O}
for all

\mathcal{A}\in E^{-}

\mathcal{L}
-ontology fitting
.

We only consider
{\cal E\!\!\>L}_{\bot}
and
{\cal E\!\!\>LI}_{\bot}

{\cal E\!\!\>L}
and
{\cal E\!\!\>LI}
are uninteresting. In fact, it is easy to see that a collection of labeled ABox examples

if and only if

does not contain negative examples, and the same is true for

Let

are equivalent, where

1.

E
admits a fitting
\mathcal{L}
-ontology;

2.

\mathcal{A}\not\preceq_{\mathcal{L}}\mathcal{A}^{+}
for every
\mathcal{A}\in E^{-}
.

The next example shows that the existence of a fitting
{\cal E\!\!\>L}_{\bot}
-ontology does not, in general, coincide with the
existence of a fitting
{\cal E\!\!\>LI}_{\bot}
-ontology. It is interesting to contrast this with the case of consistency-based fitting for
{\cal ALC}
and

[

Example 2

Consider the collection of ABox examples

-ontology, e.g. the one that

In contrast, it follows from Theorem

4
Atomic Queries

We consider atomic queries and present a characterization that resembles the one for consistency-based fitting, but has an additional ingredient. Informally, each positive example
(\mathcal{A},q)
now acts like a certain kind of implication
on the negative examples,
similar to an existential
rule with

as the (atomic unary) rule head
and
\mathcal{A}
as the rule body that, however, must be matched in terms of a simulation
rather than a homomorphism. We now make

Let

be a collection of labeled ABox-AQ-examples and
\mathcal{A}^{-}=\biguplus_{(\mathcal{A},Q(a))\in E^{-}}\mathcal{A}
.

of

In contrast to consistency-based fitting,
here also
{\cal E\!\!\>L}
and

for the ontology language.

Theorem 3
.

Let
E=(E^{+},E^{-})
be a collection of labeled ABox-AQ-examples

\mathcal{L}
-ontology

\mathcal{C}
of

such that

;

(b)

and

, then

.

Point (b) of Theorem

formalizes ‘positive examples as implications’.
It is not hard to find a variation of Example

5
UCQs and CQs

We start with characterizations of

(\mathcal{L},\text{UCQ})
-ontology fitting,
for
\mathcal{L}\in\{{\cal E\!\!\>L},{\cal E\!\!\>L}_{\bot},{\cal E\!\!\>LI},{\cal E\!\!\>LI}_{\bot}\}
,
which also cover the case
of CQs. These characterizations are more intricate
than in the AQ case. In particular,
the finite completions used for the AQ characterizations have to be replaced with potentially infinite ones, which we choose to represent
as interpretations. This
change is connected to the fact that the fitting
ontologies constructed in the AQ-case do not use existential quantifiers on the right-hand side of CIs while
this cannot be
achieved
for CQs and UCQs. Example
1

Since already for AQs the existence of a fitting ontology does not coincide
for any two of our four languages

\mathcal{L}\in\{{\cal E\!\!\>L},{\cal E\!\!\>L}_{\bot},{\cal E\!\!\>LI},{\cal E\!\!\>LI}_{\bot}\}
, the same is clearly true for CQs and
UCQs. In the following characterization,
we assume that there is at least one negative example. Note that the case without negative examples is not too interesting: for
\mathcal{L}\in\{{\cal E\!\!\>L}_{\bot},{\cal E\!\!\>LI}_{\bot}\}
,
\{\top\sqsubseteq\bot\}
is a fitting ontology; for
\mathcal{L}\in\{{\cal E\!\!\>L},{\cal E\!\!\>LI}\}
, either the
ontology
\{\top\sqsubseteq A\mid A\in{\sf N_{C}}\text{ occurs in }E\}\cup\{\top\sqsubseteq\exists r.\top\mid r\in{\sf N_{R}}\text{ occurs in }E\}

fits or there is no fitting ontology.

A model

6
Conclusion

We have obtained a relatively complete picture of ontology fitting problems for the most common Horn DLs based on ABoxes and queries.
It would, however, be interesting to extend our study to other relevant Horn DLs including
\mathcal{ELO}
, Horn-
{\cal ALC}
, and Horn-
{\cal ALCI}
. Existential rule languages such as guarded existential rules would be another interesting subject to study.

One may also be interested in the case of full UCQs, that is, UCQs that do not use existentially quantified variables. We conjecture that
it is not too difficult to extend the techniques that we have introduced for AQs to full UCQs, and that
(\mathcal{L},\text{full UCQ})
-ontology fitting is in
PTime
for all
\mathcal{L}\in\{{\cal E\!\!\>L},{\cal E\!\!\>L}_{\bot},{\cal E\!\!\>LI},{\cal E\!\!\>LI}_{\bot}\}
. In the case of
{\cal ALC}
and
{\cal ALCI}
, such an extension has been presented in
[
13
]
.

It should be straightforward to adapt
the characterizations and decidability results presented in this paper to the DLs

{\cal E\!\!\>L}^{\mathsf{sim}}_{\bot}
and

{\cal E\!\!\>LI}^{\mathsf{sim}}_{\bot}
. An interesting nuance is that
the qualifier ‘finite’ in Theorem
5
can then be dropped for both
{\cal E\!\!\>L}^{\mathsf{sim}}_{\bot}

and

{\cal E\!\!\>LI}^{\mathsf{sim}}_{\bot}
.

Another question left open is the size of fitting ontologies in
({\cal E\!\!\>LI},\text{UCQ})
-ontology fitting, where we have obtained no bounds at all. In particular, it would be interesting to understand whether polynomial size fittings always exist if auxiliary symbols are admitted.

Before we prove the results of Section
5
, we formalize the concepts used in the reduction queries.
These concepts encode tree-shaped queries in the spirit of the characteristic concepts introduced earlier.
Although the characteristic concept would suffice to capture the structure of a tree-shaped query, its size may be exponential in the size of the query.
This blow-up occurs in the presence of inverse roles, which enable the inductive definition to repeatedly traverse edges towards the root, resulting in a redundant encoding of identical substructures.
We therefore refine this construction to avoid such redundancies. The resulting concept is linear in the size of the tree and equivalent its characteristic concept.
