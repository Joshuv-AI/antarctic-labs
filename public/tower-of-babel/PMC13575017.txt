Factors Associated with Childhood Vaccination in Sub-Saharan African Countries Experiencing Armed Conflicts: A Scoping Review
Authors: Rodolfo Rossi, Matej Kovarik, Ariadna Monroy Davila, Kurt Barbé, Ives Hubloue
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArmed conflicts substantially disrupt health systems and undermine routine childhood immunization, increasing the risk of vaccine-preventable disease outbreaks. While declines in vaccination coverage in conflict settings are well documented, less is known about the multi-level determinants associated with childhood vaccination outcomes in African countries affected by armed conflict. This scoping review maps and synthesizes existing empirical evidence on factors associated with childhood vaccination in these settings.MethodsA scoping review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA-ScR guidelines. Systematic searches were performed in PubMed, Embase, and Scopus, with supplementary searches in Google Scholar. Peer-reviewed observational studies and systematic reviews published from January 2015 onwards were included if they examined determinants associated with childhood vaccination outcomes in African countries affected by armed conflict. Findings were synthesized narratively and grouped into thematic determinant domains encompassing caregiver characteristics, socioeconomic factors, geographic barriers, conflict-related determinants, and health-system constraints.ResultsTwenty-eight studies met the inclusion criteria. Evidence was geographically concentrated in a limited number of countries, particularly Ethiopia, Somalia/Somaliland, the Democratic Republic of Congo, and Nigeria. Maternal/caregiver education and empowerment, geographic access barriers/remoteness, and household/community poverty and wealth were the most frequently reported determinant categories. Across settings, maternal education, antenatal care attendance, and facility-based delivery were consistently associated with higher vaccination uptake. Conversely, poverty, rural residence, insecurity, displacement, and disruption of routine services were recurrent barriers to complete and timely immunization. Health-system constraints such as stock-outs, limited outreach services, and shortages of trained personnel further compounded inequities in vaccination access.ConclusionsChildhood vaccination in conflict-affected African countries is shaped by a complex interplay of socioeconomic vulnerability, caregiver characteristics, conflict dynamics, and health-system disruption. Armed conflict appears to amplify pre-existing inequities in access to routine immunization services. The current evidence base remains geographically uneven, highlighting important gaps in several conflict-affected settings. Strengthening context-specific research is essential to inform resilient, effective, and equitable immunization strategies in conflict-affected settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s44197-026-00600-4.Keywords: Childhood vaccination, Armed conflict, Immunisation coverage, Conflict-affected settings, Africa, Health systems, Scoping review

Background

Childhood vaccination is one of the most cost-effective public health interventions that has saved million lives worldwide. It is estimated that vaccination can prevent over two million deaths every year, as well as reducing childhood morbidity and disability. Since the establishment of the Expanded Programme of Immunization (EPI) in 1974, it is estimated that vaccination averted 146 million deaths among children younger than 5 years [1–3].

To achieve its highest potential and protect from vaccine-preventable diseases also individuals who cannot be vaccinated, vaccination coverage must be maintained at a sufficient level for the herd immunity to work. The herd immunity threshold (HIT) is the proportion of immune individuals that should lead to a decline in incidence of infection, protecting a population from the invasion of an infection. The HIT is calculated upon the disease’s Basic Reproduction Number (R0), the number of secondary cases of a disease generated on average by one infectious case in a totally susceptible population. For highly transmissible diseases, such as measles, the vaccination coverage should be at least of 95% to prevent an outbreak [4].

International agencies have been making major efforts to maintain a high level of childhood vaccination and to reach children who never received a vaccine. For example, GAVI, the Vaccine Alliance, has the goal to reduce the number of zero-dose children (defined as infants who have not received the first dose of diphtheria, tetanus, and pertussis-containing vaccine by the end of their first year of life) by 25% by 2025, and by 50% by 2030 [5]. Similarly, the World Health Organization (WHO) Immunization Agenda 2030 (IA2030) aims at an effective, efficient and resilient immunization services that are accessible to all people as an essential part of primary health care, and thereby contribute to universal health coverage [6].

However, the impact of such efforts can easily be jeopardized by armed conflict and related humanitarian crises. During the early phases of the COVID 19 pandemic, it was estimated that the overall vaccination coverage in the African and American regions declined by almost 40% and, specifically for measles and pentavalent 3, this decline in coverage reached 48% and 37%, respectively [7]. Acute and protracted armed conflicts have catastrophic effects on childhood vaccination. Countries affected by armed conflicts experience lower vaccination coverages, higher incidence and mortality due to vaccine preventable diseases, and higher risk of infectious diseases epidemics. In 2019, the Democratic Republic of Congo reported 36 public health emergencies linked to vaccine preventable diseases, South Sudan 13, while most stable countries in the same continent, such as Liberia and Zimbabwe, reported only 1 and 2, respectively [8]. A review published in 2019 analysed data from 16 conflict-affected countries which, representing just 12% of the global population, were responsible for 67% of global polio cases and 39% of global measles cases between 2010 and 2015. Fourteen out of the 16 countries were below the global average of 85% coverage for diphtheria, pertussis, and tetanus (DPT3) in 2014 [9].

To address these challenges, it is essential to understand the complex interplay of determinants associated with vaccination coverage in these settings. In fact, vaccination coverage, even if within a specific context, can vary depending on different determinants, being these at the individual, household, community and society or health system levels. Understanding these determinants can inform vaccination policies and strategies, support partners in prioritising interventions, and help identify disadvantaged groups. African countries were selected as the focus of this review because the continent includes a substantial proportion of the world’s fragile and conflict-affected settings, while continuing to bear a high burden of under-vaccinated and zero-dose children. In addition, recurrent outbreaks of vaccine-preventable diseases in several conflict-affected African countries highlight the importance of understanding how conflict interacts with other determinants of childhood vaccination in these settings [5, 8, 9].

Fragile and conflict-affected settings are commonly characterised by protracted insecurity, institutional fragility, population displacement, disruption of essential services, and reduced state capacity. The World Bank classifies Fragile and Conflict-Affected Situations (FCS) based on institutional and social fragility and/or the presence of high-intensity conflict [10], while the WHO refers to fragile, conflict-affected and vulnerable settings as contexts affected by humanitarian crises, protracted emergencies, or armed conflict [11]. Despite growing recognition of the impact of armed conflict on childhood immunisation, the determinants associated with vaccination outcomes in conflict-affected African settings remain fragmented across diverse study designs and contexts. In addition, evidence appears geographically concentrated in a limited number of countries, with important gaps in several conflict-affected settings. Given the heterogeneity of available evidence, a scoping review approach was considered appropriate to map and synthesise reported determinants of childhood vaccination outcomes in conflict-affected African countries.

The objectives of this review were therefore to:

identify and categorise determinants reported to be associated with childhood vaccination outcomes in conflict-affected African countries;

identify and categorise determinants reported to be associated with childhood vaccination outcomes in conflict-affected African countries;

describe the geographic distribution and characteristics of the existing evidence base; and.

describe the geographic distribution and characteristics of the existing evidence base; and.

identify gaps and priorities for future research in underrepresented settings.

Methods

Study Design

Given the heterogeneity of study designs, immunisation outcomes, and conflict contexts across the available literature, a scoping review methodology was considered more appropriate than quantitative synthesis to map the breadth and characteristics of available evidence. We conducted this scoping review based on the criteria set out by the PRISMA Statement for Scoping Reviews [12]. A scoping review methodology was chosen to map the existing evidence on determinants of childhood vaccination in African countries affected by armed conflict, to identify knowledge gaps, and to inform priorities for future research. A review protocol was not prospectively registered. We conducted systematic searches in PubMed, Embase, and Scopus. These databases were selected to ensure broad coverage of biomedical, public health, and interdisciplinary literature relevant to vaccination and conflict-affected settings. In addition, Google Scholar was used as a supplementary search source to identify potentially relevant studies not indexed in the primary databases and to screen reference lists of included studies. Screening was conducted at the title and abstract level using predefined search terms. Due to the dynamic nature of Google Scholar indexing, reproducibility may be limited. Countries were considered conflict-affected if they experienced active armed conflict, protracted insecurity, or humanitarian crises associated with organised violence during the study period, consistent with classifications used by the World Bank Fragile and Conflict-Affected Situations (FCS) framework and WHO definitions of fragile, conflict-affected and vulnerable settings. Countries included in the search strategy were selected from the World Bank FCS classification applicable during the review period. A list of countries considered eligible for inclusion is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Search Terms

The search strategy included the following key concepts:

Vaccination: vaccine OR vaccination OR immunization.

Population: child OR children OR childhood.

Outcome: childhood vaccination outcomes, including vaccination coverage, uptake, full immunisation, zero-dose status, timeliness, or immunisation status.

Setting: Africa and individual African countries affected by armed conflict.

Search strings were adapted to the syntax and indexing terms of each database. Individual African countries affected by armed conflict were entered separately into the search strategy to maximise sensitivity. The complete PubMed search strategy, including Boolean operators, limits, and the date of the final search, is provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Inclusion Criteria

eligible studies included observational studies (cross-sectional, case-control, cohort, ecological studies) and meta-analyses/systematic reviews. Eligible studies reported determinants associated with one or more childhood vaccination outcomes, including full immunisation, vaccination coverage, zero-dose status, timeliness, or vaccination dropout. The search was limited to peer-reviewed articles published from January 2015 onwards to capture contemporary evidence relevant to current immunisation programmes and conflict dynamics in African countries. Earlier studies were not included as immunisation systems, conflict contexts, and data availability have evolved substantially over time. We excluded publications such as case reports, case series and randomized-controlled trials or other studies that assessed the efficacy and effectiveness of new vaccines; we also excluded articles of research conducted in countries not affected by armed conflict. Reference lists of retrieved articles were additionally screened to identify other potentially relevant publications. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by three authors (RR, MK, AMD), followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible studies. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. The eligibility criteria applied in this review are summarised in Table 1.

Determinants reported by included studies were grouped into thematic domains based on conceptual similarity, including maternal/caregiver characteristics, socioeconomic determinants, geographic access barriers, conflict-related determinants, and health-system constraints. The thematic categories were developed inductively during data extraction and synthesis. Determinants reported by individual studies were reviewed and grouped according to their conceptual similarity and level of influence on childhood vaccination outcomes. This process resulted in six broad categories: maternal/caregiver education and empowerment, geographic access barriers/remoteness, household/community poverty and wealth, conflict exposure/displacement/insecurity, health system constraints/disruptions, and antenatal care attendance and facility-based delivery.

Findings were synthesised narratively and organised according to a multi-level conceptual framework encompassing individual, household, community, conflict-related, and health-system determinants. To strengthen descriptive synthesis, determinants reported across included studies were additionally grouped into thematic categories and their frequency of reporting across studies was summarised descriptively. Individual studies could contribute to multiple determinant categories.

Consistent with scoping review methodology, no formal risk-of-bias assessment was conducted, as the objective was to map the breadth and characteristics of available evidence rather than to estimate pooled effects or intervention effectiveness.

Results

The literature search identified 1,473 records, including 487 from PubMed, 421 from Embase, and 389 from Scopus. An additional 176 records were identified through Google Scholar and reference list screening. After removal of 412 duplicates, 1,061 records were screened based on title and abstract. Following screening, 155 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 127 articles were excluded because they did not assess factors associated with childhood vaccination, were conducted in non–conflict-affected settings, focused exclusively on vaccine efficacy or campaigns, or did not include children under five years of age. A total of 28 articles of empirical evidence were retained and included in the Results synthesis (Fig. 1).

Study selection flow (PRISMA-Scoping Review)

The included studies encompassed a range of quantitative designs, with the majority using cross-sectional analyses of household survey data. Most studies were conducted in a limited number of conflict-affected countries, notably Ethiopia (6 studies), Somalia/Somaliland (5 studies), Democratic Republic of Congo (5 studies), and Nigeria (3 studies). In contrast, little or no eligible evidence was identified for several countries included in the review framework, including Burkina Faso, Cameroon, the Central African Republic, Mali, Niger, and Sudan, while only one study was identified from South Sudan. The geographic distribution of included studies is illustrated in Supplementary Figure S1. Supplementary Table S3 summarises the characteristics of included studies, including study design, data source, vaccination outcome, and key findings [13–40].

Determinants reported across included studies were grouped into six thematic categories. Because individual studies could report more than one determinant, categories were not mutually exclusive. Maternal/caregiver education and empowerment was the most frequently reported determinant category, followed by geographic barriers/remoteness and household/community poverty and wealth.

Across the included studies, determinants associated with childhood vaccination outcomes were reported across multiple thematic domains (Fig. 2). Maternal/caregiver education and empowerment were the most frequently reported determinant category (16 studies), followed by geographic barriers/remoteness (11 studies) and household/community poverty and wealth (10 studies). Conflict-related determinants, including insecurity, displacement, and disruption of routine services, were identified in 8 studies, while health-system constraints/disruptions were reported in 7 studies. Antenatal care attendance and facility-based delivery were reported in 6 studies.

Frequency of thematic determinant categories reported across included studies (n = 28)

Maternal education, antenatal care attendance, and facility-based delivery were consistently associated with higher vaccination uptake and full immunisation. Conversely, household poverty, rural residence, and limited access to health facilities were frequently associated with incomplete, delayed, or zero-dose vaccination status. Conflict-related determinants—including insecurity, population displacement, geographic isolation, and disruption of routine services—were frequently reported as barriers across both country-specific and multi-country studies. Several studies also identified health-system constraints, such as vaccine stock-outs, reduced outreach activities, and shortages of trained health personnel, as barriers to routine immunisation delivery. Overall, findings were remarkably consistent across studies despite differences in country context and vaccination outcomes. Maternal education, household socioeconomic status, and utilisation of maternal health services emerged as recurrent determinants across multiple settings. Geographic barriers, including rural residence and distance from health facilities, were also consistently associated with lower vaccination uptake. Greater heterogeneity was observed for conflict-related determinants, partly reflecting differences in how studies measured conflict exposure, displacement, insecurity, and service disruption. Nevertheless, studies that explicitly assessed conflict-related factors generally reported negative associations with vaccination coverage or timeliness.

Discussion

Consistent with the review objectives, this scoping review provides a structured overview of determinants associated with childhood vaccination outcomes, the geographic distribution of available evidence, and priority areas for future research in conflict-affected African countries. The findings indicate that childhood immunisation in these settings is shaped by a complex, multi-level interplay of determinants, encompassing individual and caregiver characteristics, household socioeconomic conditions, conflict-related contextual factors, and health-system functioning. Across studies, maternal education, antenatal care attendance, and facility-based delivery emerged as consistently associated with higher vaccination uptake, while poverty, rural residence, insecurity, displacement, and disruption of health services were recurrent barriers to full immunisation. Taken together, these findings suggest that armed conflict rarely operates as an isolated determinant of under-vaccination, but rather interacts with and amplifies pre-existing socioeconomic, geographic, and health-system inequities [41, 42]. Importantly, the determinants most frequently identified in this review—including maternal education, household poverty, antenatal care attendance, and geographic access barriers—are not unique to conflict-affected settings. However, the findings suggest that armed conflict may alter the way these determinants operate by exacerbating existing vulnerabilities. For example, insecurity and population displacement can further limit access to health facilities among already disadvantaged populations, while disruption of routine services may reduce opportunities for vaccination even among households that would otherwise seek preventive care. In this sense, conflict appears less as an independent determinant and more as a multiplier of pre-existing socioeconomic and health-system inequities that shape childhood vaccination outcomes (Fig. 3) [41, 42].

Conceptual framework of pathways linking armed conflict and childhood vaccination outcomes in conflict-affected African settings. The framework was developed by the authors based on determinants identified across the included studies and informed by previous literature on vaccination in conflict-affected settings. It illustrates how armed conflict may influence childhood vaccination outcomes through multiple interacting pathways, including population displacement, insecurity, health-system disruption, and amplification of pre-existing socioeconomic and geographic vulnerabilities [41, 42]

The evidence base remains uneven and geographically concentrated. Most studies were conducted in a small number of conflict-affected countries—particularly Ethiopia, Somalia/Somaliland, and Nigeria—while several African countries experiencing armed conflict remain under-represented or not represented at all. The geographic concentration of evidence also highlights important research priorities. Several countries included in the conflict-affected framework, including Burkina Faso, the Central African Republic, Mali, Niger, and Sudan, were not represented in the included studies despite experiencing substantial insecurity and humanitarian challenges. This imbalance limits the generalisability of the current evidence base and may constrain the development of context-sensitive immunisation policies in settings where routine vaccination services face some of the greatest operational barriers. In addition, many studies relied on subnational samples or facility-based data, and relatively few explicitly integrated measures of conflict exposure with household-level determinants within a single analytical framework. These gaps limit the generalisability of findings and highlight the need for more comprehensive and geographically diverse research. This concentration of evidence in a limited number of countries also reflects broader inequities in research production and data availability across conflict-affected African settings (Supplementary Table S3).

The present review adds value by bringing together evidence that has previously been dispersed across individual countries, study designs, and conflict contexts. Beyond identifying determinants associated with childhood vaccination outcomes, it highlights how these determinants cluster across thematic domains and interact with conflict-related vulnerabilities. The review also provides a structured overview of the geographic distribution of evidence, revealing important research gaps in several conflict-affected African countries. By synthesising findings across diverse settings, the review offers a broader perspective that may help inform both future research priorities and context-sensitive immunisation strategies. The findings of this review are broadly consistent with the wider literature on immunisation in fragile and conflict-affected settings. Global and regional policy guidance has long emphasised the vulnerability of routine immunisation services to crises, particularly in contexts characterised by insecurity, population displacement, and weak governance structures, which were also recurrent determinants identified across the included studies [5, 43]. Systematic reviews of vaccination guidance in humanitarian responses further underline the operational challenges of sustaining routine immunisation during emergencies, including fragmented service delivery, limited coordination, and difficulties in reaching mobile or displaced populations [44].

Earlier conceptual work has highlighted the close link between armed conflict and the emergence or resurgence of infectious diseases, driven by breakdowns in health systems, population movements, and reduced access to preventive services [45]. More recent analyses from Sudan further illustrate how protracted conflict can severely constrain the delivery of essential services such as routine childhood vaccination through near-collapse of health systems [46, 47]. These observations align with the findings of the present review, in which service disruption, insecurity, displacement, and geographic access barriers were recurrently reported as determinants of under-vaccination.

Recent reviews provide additional context for interpreting these findings. A systematic review of empirical evidence concluded that armed conflict is consistently associated with declines in routine immunisation coverage, although the magnitude and pathways of impact vary considerably across settings [41]. Similarly, a narrative review highlighted how armed conflicts exacerbate pre-existing inequalities in vaccination coverage, particularly among marginalised and hard-to-reach populations [42]. The heterogeneity observed across the studies included in this review reinforces the importance of context-specific analyses rather than one-size-fits-all approaches to immunisation strategies in conflict-affected settings.

Evidence from humanitarian and programme-level analyses further demonstrates the systemic challenges affecting routine immunisation in fragile contexts. Studies have documented how repeated crises expose structural weaknesses in immunisation systems, including limited preparedness, weak integration with primary health care, workforce shortages, and reliance on external support [8, 48, 49]. In addition, evidence from the COVID-19 pandemic showed how large-scale shocks can sharply disrupt routine childhood immunisation services even outside active conflict settings, suggesting that conflict-affected countries may be particularly vulnerable to compounded disruptions [7, 50, 51]. The findings also have important policy and programmatic implications. Given the consistent role of geographic barriers, socioeconomic disadvantage, and health-system disruption, efforts to improve vaccination coverage in conflict-affected settings should extend beyond vaccine supply alone. Governments and immunisation programmes may benefit from strengthening outreach services, integrating vaccination with maternal and child health platforms, and prioritising underserved populations in insecure and hard-to-reach areas. Humanitarian agencies and global partners can support these efforts by investing in resilient delivery systems, strengthening routine services alongside emergency vaccination campaigns, and improving monitoring of vaccination equity among displaced and conflict-affected populations [6].

Strengths and Limitations

This scoping review has some strengths. It followed PRISMA guidelines for scoping reviews and applied a transparent, multi-level conceptual framework and thematic categorisation approach to synthesise evidence across diverse study designs and settings. By focusing on conflict-affected African countries, the review provides a targeted mapping of evidence relevant to some of the most vulnerable populations globally. However, the review also has limitations. Although multiple bibliographic databases were searched, the review was not intended to be exhaustive, and some relevant studies may not have been identified. Google Scholar was used as a supplementary search source, but its dynamic and non-transparent indexing structure may limit reproducibility. In addition, no formal risk-of-bias assessment was conducted, consistent with scoping review methodology. Finally, most included studies employed cross-sectional designs, limiting the ability to infer causal relationships between conflict exposure and vaccination outcomes. Several studies also relied on caregiver-reported vaccination histories, which may be subject to recall bias where vaccination cards were unavailable. In addition, heterogeneity in study designs, vaccination outcome definitions, and measures of conflict exposure precluded quantitative synthesis and limited comparability across studies.

Implications for Future Research

The findings of this scoping review point to important priorities for future research. There is a clear need for country-specific analyses in conflict-affected African countries for which recent nationally representative data are available, particularly Demographic and Health Surveys conducted within the last decade. Such analyses would allow for more robust examination of how conflict exposure interacts with individual, household, and community-level determinants of childhood vaccination. Countries experiencing protracted or recurrent conflict, but for which empirical evidence remains limited, should be prioritised to inform equitable and context-sensitive immunisation strategies. Future research should also incorporate explicit measures of inequity, displacement, conflict exposure, and health-system accessibility to better understand how vulnerabilities intersect to shape childhood vaccination outcomes. Future research also should prioritise conflict-affected countries for which little or no recent evidence is available, particularly Burkina Faso, Cameroon, the Central African Republic, Mali, Niger, and Sudan. Studies should move beyond indirect proxies and, where feasible, incorporate direct measures of conflict exposure, displacement, insecurity, and health-system resilience. Longitudinal and mixed-methods approaches may also help clarify how conflict interacts with socioeconomic and geographic determinants over time. Finally, implementation-focused research is needed to identify strategies that can sustain routine immunisation services among displaced, remote, and conflict-affected populations.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this scoping review demonstrates that childhood vaccination in conflict-affected African countries is influenced by a complex set of interrelated determinants operating across multiple levels. While armed conflict consistently undermines routine immunisation, its effects are mediated through socioeconomic vulnerability, caregiver characteristics, population displacement, and health-system disruption. Addressing gaps in the current evidence base through context-specific, country-level research is essential to inform resilient, effective, and equitable immunisation policies in conflict-affected settings.
