Locally Semi-Equitable Colourings of BIBDs
Andrea C. Burgess, William Kellough, David A. Pike

Abstract
We study ℓ \ell -colourings of ( v , k , λ ) (v,k,\lambda) -BIBDs (balanced incomplete block designs) where, within each block, one colour is absent and each of the ℓ − 1 \ell-1 other colours appears exactly k ℓ − 1 \frac{k}{\ell-1} times. We establish several necessary conditions for such colourings to exist. We also use these coloured BIBDs to provide new necessary conditions for the existence of Hadamard matrices, affine planes, and twin prime powers.



Keywords:
colouring; balanced incomplete block design; Hadamard matrices; affine planes; twin prime powers

MSC2020: 05B05, 05C15

Introduction

If
v,k,\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
with
2\leq k<v
, then a
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD (balanced incomplete block design)
is a pair
(V,\mathcal{B})
where
V
is a set of
v
points and
\mathcal{B}
is a collection of
k
-element subsets of
V
, called
blocks
, such that for any pair of distinct
x,y\in V
, there are exactly
\lambda
blocks containing both
x
and
y
. The value
\lambda
is called the
index
of the design. If
C=\{c_{1},\dots,c_{\ell}\}
, then an
\ell
-colouring
of a BIBD
(V,\mathcal{B})
is a surjective mapping
f:V\to C
, where the elements of
C
are called
colours
. The
colour class
of
c_{i}\in C
is the set of all points in
V
coloured
c_{i}
. A
locally equitable colouring
of
(V,\mathcal{B})
is a colouring such that for any
B\in\mathcal{B}
and any two colour classes
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
,
\big||C_{1}\cap B|-|C_{2}\cap B|\big|\leq 1
.

The study of locally equitably coloured designs initially started with cycle systems, which were considered in
[
1
,
2
,
8
,
9
,
18
,
35
,
36
]
. Locally equitable colourings of group divisible designs and maximum packing designs are studied in
[
7
]
. Luther and Pike
[
19
,
20
]
investigated such colourings on BIBDs and characterized all BIBDs which admit a locally equitable colouring. We briefly note that we are using different terminology than in
[
19
,
20
]
. For what they called “equitable” colourings, we use the term “locally equitable” because “equitable” colourings in the hypergraph colouring literature may instead refer to colourings such that for any two colour classes
C_{1}
and
C_{2}
,
\big||C_{1}|-|C_{2}|\big|\leq 1
. For a survey on these kinds of colourings for
(v,3,\lambda)
-BIBDs, see Sections 18.2–18.4 in
[
12
]
. In the literature on colouring block designs, an “equitable” colouring may also refer to a specific kind of colouring where the blocks are assigned colours instead of the points (see for example
[
14
]
), but such colourings are not studied in this paper.

Luther and Pike
[
19
,
20
]
showed that a BIBD admits a locally equitable
\ell
-colouring if and only if
\ell=v
or it is a
(k+1,k,u(k-1))
-BIBD such that
k\equiv\ell-1\pmod{\ell}
and
u\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Because of how restrictive admitting a locally equitable colouring is for the parameters of a BIBD, one could ask: Which BIBDs can be coloured in a way that is close to a locally equitable colouring?

In this paper, we generalize locally equitable colourings to
locally semi-equitable colourings
. Let
(V,\mathcal{B})
be a
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD with
\mathcal{B}=\{B_{1}.\dots,B_{b}\}
. For each
B_{i}\in\mathcal{B}
, let
n_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
and let
\Upsilon
be the multiset
\{n_{1},\dots,n_{b}\}
. An
\Upsilon
-locally semi-equitable colouring
, abbreviated
\Upsilon
-LSE
, of
(V,\mathcal{B})
is a colouring such that for each
B_{i}\in\mathcal{B}
:

(1)

one colour is represented exactly
n_{i}
times within
B_{i}
, and

(2)

for any two colours
c_{j_{1}}
and
c_{j_{2}}
that are not the colour from condition (1), their colour classes
C_{j_{1}}
and
C_{j_{2}}
satisfy
\big||C_{j_{1}}\cap B_{i}|-|C_{j_{2}}\cap B_{i}|\big|\leq 1
.

In the case that
n_{i}=t
for all
1\leq i\leq b
where
t\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
is a constant, we write
t
-LSE instead of
\Upsilon
-LSE. Notably, if for all
1\leq i\leq b
,
n_{i}\in\left\{\left\lfloor\frac{k}{\ell}\right\rfloor,\left\lceil\frac{k}{\ell}\right\rceil\right\}
, then an
\Upsilon
-LSE
\ell
-colouring is a locally equitable colouring.

Let
\Upsilon=\{n_{1},\dots,n_{b}\}
. We separate
\Upsilon
-LSE colourings into two types. An
\Upsilon
-LSE colouring of a
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD is
uniform
if
\frac{k-n_{i}}{\ell-1}\in\mathbb{Z}
for all
1\leq i\leq b
and
nonuniform
otherwise. We further abbreviate uniform and nonuniform
\Upsilon
-LSE to
\Upsilon
-ULSE and
\Upsilon
-NLSE respectively. In particular, in a
t
-ULSE
\ell
-colouring of a
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD,
(\ell-1)
divides
(k-t)
. The main focus of this paper is on
0
-ULSE colourings.

In Section
2
we give preliminary results on BIBDs. We also obtain results that apply to all
\Upsilon
-LSE coloured BIBDs and all
t
-LSE coloured BIBDs. The rest of the paper focuses on
0
-ULSE colourings. In Section
3
we establish necessary conditions for
0
-ULSE
\ell
-coloured
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBDs, including a relation between
v
,
k
, and
\ell
. In Section
4
we present the main result of our paper, which is a general framework for constructing
0
-ULSE coloured BIBDs. In Section
5
we show that Hadamard matrices and affine planes can be used with the general framework in Section
4
to construct families of
0
-ULSE coloured BIBDs. The results of Section
5
also establish new necessary conditions for the existence of Hadamard matrices and affine planes. In Section
6
we show how twin prime powers can be used to construct
0
-ULSE coloured BIBDs, which gives a new necessary condition for the existence of twin prime powers. We also show that there exist
0
-ULSE coloured BIBDs beyond those that can be obtained by the construction in Section
4
. We finish the paper with open problems and future work in Section
7
.

Preliminaries

In this section we present some elementary properties of BIBDs and
\Upsilon
-LSE colourings. For more background on BIBDs, we direct the reader to
[
30
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

If
x
is a point in a
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD then the number of blocks containing
x
is exactly

r=\frac{\lambda(v-1)}{k-1}.

We call
r
the
replication number
of a BIBD and we continue denoting the replication number with
r
throughout this paper.

Lemma 2.2
.

If
b
is the number of blocks in a
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD then

b=\frac{vr}{k}=\frac{\lambda v(v-1)}{k(k-1)}.

We continue denoting the number of blocks in a design with
b
throughout this paper. The following is a well known result by Fisher
[
13
]
.

Lemma 2.3

(Fisher’s Inequality)
.

If a
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD exists with replication number
r
and
b
blocks, then
v\leq b
and, consequently,
k\leq r
.

A
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD is called
symmetric
if
v=b
, or equivalently
k=r
.

Next, we show that given an
\Upsilon
-LSE coloured BIBD, we can obtain other designs with particular colourings of interest.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD. If
\mathcal{D}
admits an
\Upsilon
-LSE colouring, then for any
m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
there exists a
(v,k,m\lambda)
-BIBD that admits an
\Upsilon
-LSE colouring.

Proof.

Fix an
\Upsilon
-LSE colouring of
\mathcal{D}
. Let
\{B_{1},\dots,B_{b}\}
be the blocks of
\mathcal{D}
. Let
\mathcal{D}_{m}
be the
(v,k,m\lambda)
-BIBD with blocks
\{B_{1_{1}},B_{1_{2}},\dots,B_{1_{m}},B_{2_{1}},\dots,B_{2_{m}},\dots,B_{b_{m}}\}
where
B_{i_{j}}=B_{i}
. If we assign the same colouring to
\mathcal{D}_{m}
as we did to
\mathcal{D}
, then, since the blocks of
\mathcal{D}_{m}
are copies of the blocks of
\mathcal{D}
, the colouring will be an
\Upsilon
-LSE colouring.
∎

It is known that when given a BIBD, say
\mathcal{D}
, a different BIBD,
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, can be formed by letting the blocks of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
be the complement of the blocks in
\mathcal{D}
.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let
v,k,\lambda,b,r\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
with
k\leq v-2
. A
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD with
b
blocks and replication number
r
exists if and only if a
(v,v-k,b-2r+\lambda)
-BIBD exists.

Theorem 2.6
.

Let
v,k,\lambda,b,r,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that
k\leq v-2
and
\ell
divides
v
, let
\Upsilon=\{n_{1},\dots,n_{b}\}
, and let
\Upsilon^{\prime}=\{\frac{v}{\ell}-n_{1},\dots,\frac{v}{\ell}-n_{b}\}

\Upsilon
-LSE
\ell
-colouring of a

-BIBD with

\Upsilon^{\prime}
-LSE
\ell

Let

\left\lceil\frac{k-n_{i}}{\ell-1}\right\rceil

By Lemma
2.5
, we can construct a

-BIBD,

, by taking the complement of the blocks in
\mathcal{D}
. For each

, let

be the complement of the block

. Suppose we colour the points of

-LSE colouring of

. We claim that this yields an
\Upsilon^{\prime}
-LSE
\ell
-colouring of

. To see this, consider any block

of

. Since one colour, say

, is represented

\left\lfloor\frac{k-n_{i}}{\ell-1}\right\rfloor

will be represented

Suppose instead we fix an
\Upsilon^{\prime}
-LSE
\ell
-colouring of
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. By taking the block complement of

, we obtain

. By the same argument as above,

3
Necessary Conditions for
0
-ULSE Coloured BIBDs

Since
k<v
by the definition of a BIBD, every BIBD on
v
points admits a
0
-LSE
v
-colouring. We call
0
-LSE
v
-colourings of a
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD
trivial
and when
\ell<v
, we call
0
-LSE
\ell
-colourings
nontrivial
. It follows immediately from the definition of
0
-LSE
\ell
-colourings that
\ell>1
. We now show that
0
-LSE
2
-colourings do not exist.

Lemma 3.1
.

If there exists a
0
-LSE
\ell
-coloured BIBD, then
\ell>2
.

Proof.

If a
0
-LSE
2
-coloured BIBD exists, then every block contains points that are all the same colour. Suppose for a contradiction, we have a
0
-LSE
2
-coloured BIBD. Recalling that a colouring is surjective, there must be two points,
x
and
y
, that are coloured differently. By the definition of a BIBD, there exists a block containing both
x
and
y
and so we have a block containing both colours. This is a contradiction and so there are no
0
-LSE
2
-coloured BIBDs.
∎

In
[
19
,
20
]
, it was shown that if
\ell\geq k
, then a locally equitable
\ell
-coloured
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD exists if and only if
\ell=v
. We use an analogous argument to give a similar result for
0
-LSE colourings.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD and let
\ell\geq k+1
. A
0
-LSE
\ell
-colouring of
\mathcal{D}
exists if and only if
\ell=v
.

Proof.

If
\ell=v
, then assigning every vertex its own unique colour will result in a
0
-LSE
\ell
-colouring of
\mathcal{D}
.

Suppose instead that
k+1\leq\ell<v
and suppose for a contradiction there exists a
0
-LSE
\ell
-colouring of
\mathcal{D}
. Since there are fewer colours than points, there exist two points, say
x
and
y
, such that
x
and
y
are assigned the same colour, say
c_{1}
. Let
B
be a block containing the pair
xy
. By the definition of a
0
-LSE colouring, one colour, say
c_{2}
, is absent from
B
. Since
|B|=k
, this leaves at least
k-1
colours to be assigned to
k-2
elements in
B
that are neither
x
nor

. Thus, a second colour will be missing from
B
, say
c_{3}
. If
C_{1}
and
C_{3}
are the colour classes for the colours
c_{1}
and
c_{3}
respectively, then
|C_{1}\cap B|-|C_{3}\cap B|>1
. This contradicts our assumption that the BIBD is
0
-LSE
\ell
-coloured.
∎

We therefore have the following bound on the number of colours in any nontrivial
0
-LSE colouring of a BIBD.

Theorem 3.3
.

If a nontrivial
0
-LSE
\ell
-coloured
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD exists, then
3\leq\ell\leq k
.

We now focus on
0
-ULSE colourings and show that for any given colour, the size of its colour class and the number of blocks containing the colour are constants that are independent of the given colour. To do this, we define two new parameters.

In any
0
-ULSE
\ell
-colouring of a BIBD, every block has
(\ell-1)
colours and
(\ell-1)
divides
k
. For a given block and a given colour

,

either appears
\frac{k}{\ell-1}
times or
0

\alpha_{i}
(respectively

) denote the number of blocks where

appears
\frac{k}{\ell-1}
(respectively

) times.

Lemma 3.4
.

In a
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD with a
0
-ULSE
\ell
-colouring, each colour appears in exactly

\frac{r^{2}}{\lambda}-\frac{(\ell-1)r^{2}}{\lambda k}+\frac{(\ell-1)r}{k}

blocks and each colour class has size

.

Proof.

Let
q=\frac{k}{\ell-1}
. Let
c_{i}
be a colour with corresponding colour class
C_{i}
.

Since each colour appears either
q
or
0
times in a block,

\alpha_{i}+\gamma_{i}=b.

(1)

By counting, in two different ways, the number of pairs
(x,B)
where
x
is a point coloured
c_{i}
and
B
is a block containing

we obtain the following.

\alpha_{i}q=r|C_{i}|

(2)

By counting, in two different ways, the number of pairs
(xy,B)
where

is an unordered pair of points both coloured

and
B
is a block containing the pair

we obtain the following.

\alpha_{i}\binom{q}{2}=\lambda\binom{|C_{i}|}{2}

(3)

By combining Equations (

) and (

) we obtain

r|C_{i}|\frac{q-1}{2}=\frac{\lambda}{2}|C_{i}|(|C_{i}|-1).

Solving for

|C_{i}|=\frac{kr}{\lambda(\ell-1)}-\frac{r}{\lambda}+1.

(4)

Since the right-hand side is independent of

, every colour class has the same size. Therefore,

for every colour class

. Substituting

into Equations (

) and (

\displaystyle\alpha_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{r^{2}}{\lambda}-\frac{(\ell-1)r^{2}}{\lambda k}+\frac{(\ell-1)r}{k}

(5)

\displaystyle\gamma_{i}

\displaystyle=b-\frac{r^{2}}{\lambda}+\frac{(\ell-1)r^{2}}{\lambda k}-\frac{(\ell-1)r}{k}

(6)

∎

Notably,
\alpha_{i}
and

are also independent of

Theorem 3.5
.

If a
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD with a
0
-ULSE
\ell
-colouring exists, then

v=\frac{\ell(\ell-2)k}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}.

(7)

Proof.

Since
\gamma

\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\gamma=b.

(8)

Combining Equation (
5
), Equation (
1
) after summing both sides from

\ell

b(\ell-1)=\sum_{i=1}^{\ell}\alpha=\frac{\ell r^{2}}{\lambda}-\frac{\ell(\ell-1)r^{2}}{\lambda k}+\frac{\ell(\ell-1)r}{k}.

By using Lemmas

v=\frac{\ell(\ell-2)k}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}.\qed

If we substitute

. This is the same family of BIBDs, as mentioned in Section

. This is not a coincidence. Every block of a

-ULSE

-coloured BIBD will have one colour absent and the rest appearing exactly once. Therefore, for any block

and

,

. So every trivial

Notably, not every BIBD that satisfies Equation (

) admits a

-ULSE colouring. As an example, consider the BIBDs with parameters

. The complement of a

-BIBD is a

-BIBDs. In Table 1.30 of Section II.1.2 in

-ULSE

-colouring with all colour classes of size three while the rest do not. Therefore, by Corollary

, there is exactly one

-BIBD with a

-ULSE

-colouring while every other

-BIBD does not have such a colouring.

Using Theorem

, we can obtain bounds on the block size of any design that admits a

-ULSE colouring.

Lemma 3.6
.

If a
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD admits a nontrivial
0
-ULSE
\ell
-colouring, then

2(\ell-1)\leq k\leq(\ell-1)(\ell-2).

Proof.

Since the colouring is nontrivial, we have

by Lemma
3.2
. Necessarily,
k\equiv 0\pmod{(\ell-1)}
and so the smallest possible value of
k
is
2(\ell-1)
.

It must hold that the denominator of Equation (
7
) is positive since its numerator is always positive for all
\ell\geq 3
. Thus

k\leq(\ell-1)^{2}-1.

Again, since
k\equiv 0\pmod{(\ell-1)}
, the largest possible value for
k
is
(\ell-1)^{2}-(\ell-1)=(\ell-1)(\ell-2)
.
∎

As a consequence of Lemma
3.6
, we can give tighter bounds on
\ell
than in Theorem
3.3
when only considering
0
-ULSE colourings.

Theorem 3.7
.

If there exists a nontrivial
0
-ULSE
\ell
-coloured
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD, then

\frac{3+\sqrt{4k+1}}{2}\leq\ell\leq\frac{k}{2}+1.

Proof.

From Lemma
3.6
,
2(\ell-1)\leq k\leq(\ell-1)(\ell-2)
. Rearranging
k\leq(\ell-1)(\ell-2)
gives

\ell^{2}-3\ell+2-k\geq 0.

Applying the quadratic formula and eliminating the negative solution for
\ell
yields

\ell\geq\frac{3+\sqrt{4k+1}}{2}.

Rearranging the lower bound
2(\ell-1)\leq k
gives

\ell\leq\frac{k}{2}+1.\qed

It follows from Lemma
3.6
and Theorem

that in any
0
-ULSE
\ell
-coloured
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD,
\ell\geq 4
and
k\geq 6
. Observe that the necessary conditions of Lemma
3.6
are violated when
\ell\leq 3
. Likewise, the necessary conditions of Theorem
3.7
are violated when
k\leq 5
.

The following result shows that within the set of all blocks
\mathcal{B}_{j}
that do not contain any points from the
j
-th colour class, for any point
x
not in the
j
-th colour class,
x
is contained in a constant number of blocks in
\mathcal{B}_{j}
.

Lemma 3.8
.

Let
(V,\mathcal{B})
be a
0
-ULSE
\ell
-coloured
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD with colour classes
C_{1}
,
\dots
,
C_{\ell}
. For each
1\leq j\leq\ell
, let
\mathcal{B}_{j}=\{B\in\mathcal{B}\mid B\cap C_{j}=\emptyset\}
. If
i\neq j
and
x\in C_{i}
, then
x
occurs in exactly

\frac{\lambda(v-1)}{k-1}-\frac{\lambda v(\ell-1)}{k\ell}

blocks in
\mathcal{B}_{j}
.

Proof.

Fix
x\in C_{i}
. Since every pair of points occurs in
\lambda
blocks and
\mathcal{B}\backslash\mathcal{B}_{j}
contains every block that contains both
x
and points from
C_{j}
, we count the size of the following multiset:

\big|\big\{y\mid\{x,y\}\subset B\in\mathcal{B}\backslash\mathcal{B}_{j},y\in C_{j}\big\}\big|=\lambda|C_{j}|=\frac{\lambda v}{\ell}.

Because every block containing
x
and a point from
C_{j}
contains
\frac{k}{\ell-1}
pairs
xy
where
y\in C_{j}
, the number of blocks in
\mathcal{B}\backslash\mathcal{B}_{j}
containing
x
is

\frac{\lambda v}{\ell}\left(\frac{\ell-1}{k}\right)=\frac{\lambda v(\ell-1)}{k\ell}.

Note that the replication number of
x
in the BIBD is
\frac{\lambda(v-1)}{k-1}
. Therefore
x
is contained in exactly

\frac{\lambda(v-1)}{k-1}-\frac{\lambda v(\ell-1)}{k\ell}

blocks in
\mathcal{B}_{j}
.
∎

A direct consequence of Lemma
3.8
is that
\frac{r}{\ell-1}\in\mathbb{Z}
, which we will now show.

Theorem 3.9
.

If there exists a
0
-ULSE
\ell
-coloured
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD with replication number

, then
\frac{r}{\ell-1}\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a
0
-ULSE
\ell
-coloured BIBD with
\mathcal{B}
denoting the set of blocks of
\mathcal{D}
. Let
i,j\in\{1,\dots,\ell\}
with

. Let

and

be two colour classes and let
\mathcal{B}_{j}=\{B\in\mathcal{B}\mid B\cap C_{j}=\emptyset\}
.

From Lemma

, each point in

occurs in exactly

blocks in
\mathcal{B}_{j}
. Furthermore, there are

and there are

\mathcal{D}
containing

and so

Since

In every BIBD that admits a

-ULSE colouring, its colour classes induce a design.

If

is a

Let

From Lemma

,

. Consider the design

\lambda

Theorem 3.11
.

If there exists a symmetric

-ULSE

-coloured

\lambda=\frac{k((\ell-1)^{2}-k)}{(\ell-1)^{2}}

\gamma=\frac{v}{\ell}

(9)

Furthermore, in a symmetric BIBD,

. Substituting into Equation (

\displaystyle\gamma

\displaystyle=v-\frac{k^{2}}{\lambda}+\frac{(\ell-1)k^{2}}{\lambda k}-\frac{(\ell-1)k}{k}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\ell-2)k}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}

.

If there exists a

-ULSE
\ell
-coloured

-BIBD, then

Since

Following a conference presentation given by Kellough in December 2025 on the preliminary contents of this paper, Brett Stevens posed the following question: Is the smallest possible candidate for

in a

, we know the value of

. First, we show that

Lemma 3.13
.

If there exists a

-ULSE
\ell
-coloured

-BIBD, then
\frac{k((\ell-1)^{2}-k)}{(\ell-1)^{2}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.

Proof.

By the definition of uniform,

. So

. Since

,

Therefore
\frac{k((\ell-1)^{2}-k)}{(\ell-1)^{2}}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
.

Theorem 3.14
.

Let
v,k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
satisfy Equation (
7
). Then the smallest index
\lambda_{\min}
such that Lemmas

and

are satisfied for a BIBD on

-ULSE

\frac{k(k-1)}{v-1}=\frac{k((\ell-1)^{2}-k)}{(\ell-1)^{2}}
.

Proof.

It can be easily checked that

satisfies Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
for a
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD. Suppose

is the smallest integer described in the statement of the theorem. From Corollary
3.12
,

. From Lemma
3.13
, the lower bound from Corollary

is always an integer. Therefore,
\frac{k((\ell-1)^{2}-k)}{(\ell-1)^{2}}
is the smallest possible value for the index of a

as required.
∎

By the work done in the proof of Lemma
3.13
,
\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}{\ell-1}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Furthermore, if
\frac{k}{\ell-1}=1
, then a

-ULSE
\ell
-colouring of a

-BIBD is trivial. Thus, in any nontrivial

-ULSE
\ell
-colouring,

. Since

in any

-ULSE coloured
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD by Theorem

, it follows that

in any nontrivial
0
-ULSE coloured BIBD.

To finish this section, we show that
0
-ULSE colourings are unique in the sense that if a BIBD
\mathcal{D}
admits a nontrivial
0
-ULSE
\ell
-colouring, then there is no
\ell^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}\backslash\{\ell\}
such that
\mathcal{D}
also admits a nontrivial
0
-ULSE
\ell^{\prime}
-colouring.

Theorem 3.15
.

If a
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD exists, then there is at most one
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that a nontrivial
0
-ULSE
\ell
-colouring of the BIBD exists. Furthermore, if
\mathcal{D}_{1}
is a
(v,k,\lambda_{1})
-BIBD with a nontrivial
0
-ULSE
\ell_{1}
-colouring and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
is a
(v,k,\lambda_{2})
-BIBD with a nontrivial
0
-ULSE
\ell_{2}
-colouring, then
\ell_{1}=\ell_{2}
.

Proof.

Fix
v,k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that a
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD exists for some
\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Suppose there exists a
0
-ULSE
\ell
-colouring of this BIBD. By Theorem
3.5
,

v=\frac{\ell(\ell-2)k}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}.

Rearranging gives

(v-k)\ell^{2}-2(v-k)\ell-v(k-1)=0.

Applying the quadratic formula and simplifying yields

\ell=1\pm\frac{2\sqrt{k(v-1)(v-k)}}{2(v-k)}.

(10)

Since
k\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
,

1-\frac{2\sqrt{k(v-1)(v-k)}}{2(v-k)}\leq 1-\frac{2\sqrt{(v-k)(v-k)}}{2(v-k)}=1-1=0

which is not an admissible number of colours. Therefore we have at most one solution for
\ell
.

Notably, our proof works independently of the value of
\lambda
. Therefore for any two BIBDs on the same number of points with the same block size, there is at most one
\ell
such that they each have a
0
-ULSE
\ell
-colouring. In particular,
\mathcal{D}_{1}
and
\mathcal{D}_{2}
cannot have a
0
-ULSE
\ell_{1}
-colouring and a
0
-ULSE
\ell_{2}
-colouring respectively with
\ell_{1}\neq\ell_{2}
.
∎

, we have the following.

Corollary 3.16
.

If a
0
-ULSE
\ell
-coloured

Equation (

,

is a perfect square.
∎

A General Construction

In this section we give a framework for constructing
0
-ULSE coloured BIBDs. This framework requires two designs as input ingredients: resolvable BIBDs and transversal designs.

A BIBD
(V,\mathcal{B})
is
resolvable
if its blocks can be partitioned into sets
\Pi_{1},\dots,\Pi_{r}
such that for each
1\leq i\leq r
,
\cup_{B\in\Pi_{i}}B=V
and
B_{1}\cap B_{2}=\emptyset
for any distinct
B_{1},B_{2}\in\Pi_{i}
. The sets
\Pi_{1},\dots,\Pi_{r}
are called
resolution classes
. We abbreviate resolvable BIBD to RBIBD.

If
k,n,\lambda\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
n>2
, then a
transversal design
TD
{}_{\lambda}(k,n)
is a triple
(V,\mathcal{G},\mathcal{B})
where
V
is a set of
kn
points,
\mathcal{G}
is a partition of
V
into
k

n
-element subsets called
groups
,
\mathcal{B}
is a collection of
k
-element subsets, called
blocks
, that contain exactly one point from each group, and for every pair of distinct points
x,y\in V
that are not in the same group, there are exactly
\lambda
blocks containing both
x
and
y
. In particular, no two points in the same group are in the same block. If
\lambda=1
, then we write TD
(k,n)
instead of TD
{}_{1}(k,n)
. The following result follows from simple counting arguments.

Lemma 4.1
.

If a TD
{}_{\lambda}(k,n)
exists, then each point occurs in exactly
\lambda n
blocks and there are exactly
\lambda n^{2}
blocks.

Transversal designs and resolvable BIBDs can be used to construct

-ULSE coloured BIBDs if the parameters are chosen in a specific way. The following is the main result of this paper.

.

Let

\{E_{0},\dots,E_{\ell-1}\}
form an

-BIBD which we will call
\mathcal{E}
.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be a
\left(\frac{v}{\ell},\frac{k}{\ell-1},\frac{k\ell(\ell-2)}{v(\ell-1)}\right)

\mathcal{R}
contains

and

7

b_{\mathcal{R}}
and
s_{\mathcal{R}}
we obtain

b_{\mathcal{R}}=\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell-2)}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}

and

s_{\mathcal{R}}=\frac{(\ell-1)(\ell-2)}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}.

Consequently,

\frac{b_{\mathcal{R}}}{s_{\mathcal{R}}}=\ell-1.

Let
\mathcal{A}=\{A_{1},\dots,A_{b_{\mathcal{R}}}\}
be the blocks of
\mathcal{R}
. Without loss of generality, assume that
\{A_{i},A_{i+1},\dots,A_{i+s_{\mathcal{R}}-1}\}
is a resolution class for each

. For each
j\equiv i\pmod{s_{\mathcal{R}}}
where
1\leq i\leq s_{\mathcal{R}}-1
, let
A_{j}=\{a_{j}^{(i-1)\frac{k}{\ell-1}+1},\dots,a_{j}^{i\frac{k}{\ell-1}}\}
. For
j\equiv 0\pmod{s_{\mathcal{R}}}
, let
A_{j}=\{a_{j}^{(s_{\mathcal{R}}-1)\frac{k}{\ell-1}+1},\dots,a_{j}^{\frac{v}{\ell}}\}
. Note that if
a_{j_{1}}^{q_{1}}\in A_{j_{1}}
and
a_{j_{2}}^{q_{2}}\in A_{j_{2}}
, then it may be true that
a_{j_{1}}^{q_{1}}=a_{j_{2}}^{q_{2}}
since blocks in
\mathcal{R}
are not necessarily disjoint. In the case where
A_{j_{1}}
and
A_{j_{2}}
are in the same resolution class, then
a_{j_{1}}^{q_{1}}\neq a_{j_{2}}^{q_{2}}

Applying Theorem

Observe that a
\left(\frac{(\ell-2)k}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k},\frac{k}{\ell-1},\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}{\ell-1}\right)
-RBIBD satisfies the property that its replication number is equal to the sum of its block size and its index. Any RBIBD that admits this property is called
affine resolvable
. The following lemma concerning the intersection of blocks in affine resolvable BIBDs was proven by Bose
[
5
]
.

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be an affine resolvable
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD. If
B_{1}
and
B_{2}
are two blocks of
\mathcal{D}
in different resolution classes then
|B_{1}\cap B_{2}|=\frac{k^{2}}{v}
.

Lemma 4.4
.

If
k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
\ell\geq 4
, then an
\left(\frac{(\ell-2)k}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k},\frac{k}{\ell-1},\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}{\ell-1}\right)
-RBIBD is affine resolvable. Furthermore, for any two blocks
B_{i}
and
B_{j}
in different resolution classes,

|B_{i}\cap B_{j}|=\frac{k((\ell-1)^{2}-k)}{(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell-2)}.

Proof.

The replication number of the RBIBD is

\ell-1=\frac{k}{\ell-1}+\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}{\ell-1}

and so it is affine resolvable. By Lemma
4.3
, if
B_{i}
and
B_{j}
are in different resolution classes,

|B_{i}\cap B_{j}|=\frac{\left(\frac{k}{\ell-1}\right)^{2}}{\left(\frac{(\ell-2)k}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}\right)}=\frac{k((\ell-1)^{2}-k)}{(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell-2)}.\qed

to construct a symmetric

is a

\text{TD}_{\frac{k((\ell-1)^{2}-k)}{(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell-2)}}\left(\ell-1,\frac{(\ell-1)(\ell-2)}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}\right).

are duals of each other in the case where the resulting

Lemma 4.5
.

There exists an
\left(\frac{(\ell-2)k}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k},\frac{k}{\ell-1},\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}{\ell-1}\right)
-RBIBD if and only if there exists a TD
{}_{\frac{k((\ell-1)^{2}-k)}{(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell-2)}}\left(\ell-1,\frac{(\ell-1)(\ell-2)}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}\right)
.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{R}
be an
\left(\frac{(\ell-2)k}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k},\frac{k}{\ell-1},\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}{\ell-1}\right)
-RBIBD. Let
x_{1},\dots,x_{\frac{(\ell-2)k}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}}
be the points of
\mathcal{R}
and let
B_{1},\dots,B_{\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell-2)}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}}
be the blocks of
\mathcal{R}
. We construct the dual design
\mathcal{T}
on the points
y_{1},\dots,y_{\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell-2)}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}}
and with blocks
D_{1},\dots,D_{\frac{(\ell-2)k}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}}
where
y_{i}\in D_{j}
if and only if
x_{j}\in B_{i}
. We claim that
\mathcal{T}
is a TD
{}_{\frac{k((\ell-1)^{2}-k)}{(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell-2)}}\left(\ell-1,\frac{(\ell-1)(\ell-2)}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}\right)
.

Since
\mathcal{R}
has
\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell-2)}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}
blocks,
\mathcal{T}
has
\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell-2)}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}
points. Since
y_{i}\in D_{j}
if and only if
x_{j}\in B_{i}
,
|D_{j}|
is equal to the number of blocks containing
x_{j}
in
\mathcal{R}
. The replication number of
\mathcal{R}
is

\displaystyle\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}{\ell-1}\left(\frac{(\ell-2)k}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}-1\right)\left(\frac{1}{\frac{k}{\ell-1}-1}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\ell k-k-\ell^{2}+2\ell-1}{k-\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\frac{(\ell-1)(k-\ell+1)}{k-\ell+1}

\displaystyle=\ell-1.

Thus the block size of
\mathcal{T}
is also
\ell-1
.

Consider the
\ell-1
resolution classes of the RBIBD. There are
\frac{(\ell-1)(\ell-2)}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}
blocks in each resolution class. The blocks in each resolution class are pairwise disjoint and the union of all of the blocks in a resolution class is the entire point set of the RBIBD. Without loss of generality, let
\left\{B_{1},\dots,B_{\frac{(\ell-1)(\ell-2)}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}}\right\}
be a resolution class. Translating into
\mathcal{T}
, we observe that no block will contain any two of the points
y_{1},\dots,y_{\frac{(\ell-1)(\ell-2)}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}}
and every block will contain at least one of the points
y_{1},\dots,y_{\frac{(\ell-1)(\ell-2)}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}}
. Therefore the points
y_{1},\dots,y_{\frac{(\ell-1)(\ell-2)}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k}}
form a group. Since there are
\ell-1
resolution classes in the RBIBD, there are
\ell-1
groups in
\mathcal{T}
, as we desired.

All that remains to show is that every pair of points that do not share a group share exactly
\frac{k((\ell-1)^{2}-k)}{(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell-2)}
blocks. Let
y_{i}
and
y_{j}
be two points from different groups in
\mathcal{T}
. Then the pair
y_{i}y_{j}
is contained in exactly
\frac{k((\ell-1)^{2}-k)}{(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell-2)}
blocks if and only if
|B_{i}\cap B_{j}|=\frac{k((\ell-1)^{2}-k)}{(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell-2)}
where
B_{i}
and
B_{j}
are blocks in different resolution classes. We know this to be true by Lemma
4.4
and so
\mathcal{T}
is a TD
{}_{\frac{k((\ell-1)^{2}-k)}{(\ell-1)^{2}(\ell-2)}}(\ell-1,\frac{(\ell-1)(\ell-2)}{(\ell-1)^{2}-k})
.

Since the dual of the dual of a design is the original design, the dual of
\mathcal{T}
is
\mathcal{R}
. This proves the result.

5
Applications of Theorem
4.2

Theorem
4.2
states that given a specific transversal design and resolvable BIBD, we can construct a
0
-ULSE coloured BIBD. We now discuss two ways of furnishing these ingredient designs: Hadamard matrices and affine planes.

5.1
Hadamard Matrices

A
Hadamard matrix
of order
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, denoted
H(n)
, is an
n\times n
matrix with entries in
\{-1,1\}
such that the rows are pairwise orthogonal. It is commonly known that if a Hadamard matrix of order
n
exists, then
n\in\{1,2\}
or
n\equiv 0\pmod{4}
. Hadamard
[
15
]
conjectured that this necessary condition is also sufficient and the conjecture is still open. See Part V of
[
11
]
for more on Hadamard matrices. A Hadamard matrix
H(4n)
exists if and only if a
(4n-1,2n-1,n-1)
-BIBD exists; a proof of this fact can be found in Chapter 4 of
[
30
]
.

A Hadamard matrix is
regular
if the sum of the entries in each row and the sum of the entries in each column of the matrix are the same. A
Menon design
is a BIBD with parameters either
(4u^{2},2u^{2}+u,u^{2}+u)
or
(4u^{2},2u^{2}-u,u^{2}-u)
. It is well-known that there exists a regular
H(n)
if and only if a Menon design on
n
points exists (see Chapter 4 of
[
30
]
). Menon designs of the form
(4u^{2},2u^{2}-u,u^{2}-u)
satisfy Equation (
7
) when substituting
\ell=2u
. This substitution yields a design with parameters
\left(\ell^{2},\binom{\ell}{2},\frac{\ell(\ell-2)}{4}\right)
.

Using Theorem
4.2
and the following lemmas, we can construct
0
-ULSE
\ell
-coloured
\left(\ell^{2},\binom{\ell}{2},\frac{\ell(\ell-2)}{4}\right)
-BIBDs using Hadamard matrices of order
\ell
.

Lemma 5.1
.

For
\ell\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, a Hadamard matrix
H(\ell)
exists if and only if a TD
{}_{\frac{\ell}{4}}(\ell-1,2)
exists.

Lemma 5.2
.

For
\ell\equiv 0\pmod{4}
, a Hadamard matrix
H(\ell)
exists if and only if an
\left(\ell,\frac{\ell}{2},\frac{\ell}{2}-1\right)
-RBIBD exists.

Lemma
5.1
can be obtained by combining results from
[
16
,
33
]
. A proof for Lemma
5.2
can be found in
[
4
]
.

Theorem 5.3
.

Let
\ell\equiv 0\pmod{4}
. If there exists a Hadamard matrix
H(\ell)
then there exists a
\left(\ell^{2},\binom{\ell}{2},\frac{\ell(\ell-2)}{4}\right)
-BIBD with a
0
-ULSE
\ell
-colouring.

Proof.

The result follows directly from Lemma
5.1
, Lemma
5.2
, and Theorem
4.2
.
∎

5.2
Affine Planes

An
affine plane
of order
n
is an
(n^{2},n,1)
-BIBD. Using well known results on affine planes, we can obtain the ingredients for Theorem
4.2
.

Lemma 5.4

(See Theorems 6.32 and 6.44 in
[
30
]
)
.

There exists an affine plane of order
n
if and only if there exists a TD
(n+1,n)
.

A
projective plane
of order
n
is an
(n^{2}+n+1,n+1,1)
-BIBD. It is well known that a projective plane of order
n
exists if and only if an affine plane of order
n
exists; see
[
30
]
for a proof. It was originally proven in
[
34
]
that there exists a projective plane of order
n
when
n
is a power of a prime. Consequently, we have the following existence result for affine planes.

Lemma 5.5
.

If
n\geq 2
is a prime power, then there exists an affine plane of order
n
.

Determining if there exists a projective plane of an order that is not a power of a prime is a long-standing open problem. Proving that there is no projective plane of order
6
is credited to Tarry
[
32
]
and proving that there is no projective plane of order
10
was done by Lam, Thiel, and Swiercz
[
17
]
. It remains open whether there exists a projective plane of order
12
, which is the smallest open case.

Lemma
5.4
shows how we obtain one of the ingredient designs in Theorem
4.2
from an affine plane. The following known property of affine planes shows that affine planes can be used as the second ingredient.

Lemma 5.6

(See Theorem 5.9 in
[
30
]
)
.

If there exists an affine plane of order
n
, then it is resolvable.

Theorem 5.7
.

If
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that there exists an affine plane of order
\ell-2
, then there exists a
(\ell(\ell-2)^{2},(\ell-1)(\ell-2),\ell-2)
-BIBD with a
0
-ULSE
\ell
-colouring.

Proof.

The result follows by applying Lemmas
5.4
,
5.5
, and
5.6
to Theorem
4.2
.
∎

In
[
31
]
, it is proven that if a Hadamard matrix of order
4n
exists, then a
((4n)^{2},2n(4n-1),2n(2n-1))
-BIBD exists. It was shown in
[
3
]
that given a prime power
q
, a
((q+2)q^{2},(q+1)q,q)
-BIBD can be constructed. Theorems
5.3
and
5.7
strengthen these results by adding a colouring condition to the output BIBDs in the results from
[
3
,
31
]
.

6
Twin Prime Powers

Not every
0
-ULSE
\ell
-coloured BIBD is captured by Theorem
4.2
. In this section, we construct an infinite family that cannot be constructed using Theorem
4.2
. To do this, we start by stating an elementary result concerning finite fields.

Lemma 6.1
.

If
\mathbb{F}_{q}
is a finite field of order
q=p^{n}
where
p
is an odd prime and
n\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, then there are
\frac{q-1}{2}
nonzero squares in
\mathbb{F}_{q}
and
\frac{q-1}{2}
nonsquares.

A positive integer
n
is called a
prime power
if
n=p^{\omega}
where
p
is a prime and
\omega\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
. Two integers
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are called
twin prime powers
if
n_{1}
and
n_{2}
are prime powers, and
|n_{1}-n_{2}|=2
.

Let
G
be a finite group of order
v
with identity element
0
. A
(v,k,\lambda)

difference set

D\subseteq G
is a set of
k
group elements such that the multiset
\{x-y\mid x,y\in D,x\neq y\}
is the multiset of all elements of
G\backslash\{0\}
repeated
\lambda
times. Difference sets can be used to construct symmetric BIBDs (see Chapter 3 of
[
30
]
). Given twin prime powers,
\ell
and
\ell-2
, Stanton and Sprott in
[
29
]
prove the existence of an
\left(\ell(\ell-2),\frac{\ell^{2}-1}{2}-\ell,\frac{(\ell-3)(\ell+1)}{4}\right)
difference set.

Lemma 6.2

(
[
29
]
)
.

Let
\ell
and
\ell-2
be prime powers. The
twin prime power difference set
is defined to be

\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{F}_{\ell-2}\times\mathbb{F}_{\ell}\mid\text{$x$ and $y$ are both nonzero squares, or both nonsquares, or $y=0$}\}

where the differences are calculated over the additive group of the field. This set generates an
\left(\ell(\ell-2),\frac{\ell^{2}-1}{2}-\ell,\frac{(\ell-3)(\ell+1)}{4}\right)
-BIBD.

Lemma 6.3
.

Let
\ell
and
\ell-2
be twin prime powers. The
\left(\ell(\ell-2),\frac{\ell^{2}-1}{2}-\ell,\frac{(\ell-3)(\ell+1)}{4}\right)
-BIBDs with
\ell\geq 5
generated by twin prime power difference sets are
(\ell-2)
-ULSE
\ell
-colourable.

Proof.

For each
y\in\mathbb{F}_{\ell}
, let
C_{y}=\{(x,y)\mid x\in\mathbb{F}_{\ell-2}\}
be a colour class. For each
(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{F}_{\ell-2}\times\mathbb{F}_{\ell}
, let

B_{(z_{1},z_{2})}=\{(x+z_{1},y+z_{2})\mid\text{$(x,y)$ is in the twin prime power difference set}\}.

The set of blocks
\{B_{(z_{1},z_{2})}\mid(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{F}_{\ell-2}\times\mathbb{F}_{\ell}\}
form an
\left(\ell(\ell-2),\frac{\ell^{2}-1}{2}-\ell,\frac{(\ell-3)(\ell+1)}{4}\right)
-BIBD.

Fix
(z_{1},z_{2})\in\mathbb{F}_{\ell-2}\times\mathbb{F}_{\ell}
. Then
C_{z_{2}}\subseteq B_{(z_{1},z_{2})}
. Let
m\in\mathbb{F}_{\ell}\backslash\{z_{2}\}
. Either
m-z_{2}\in\mathbb{F}_{\ell}
is a nonzero square or a nonsquare. Suppose
m-z_{2}
is a nonzero square. By Lemma
6.1
there are
\frac{\ell-3}{2}
nonzero squares in
\mathbb{F}_{\ell-2}
. Therefore there are
\frac{\ell-3}{2}
elements
x\in\mathbb{F}_{\ell-2}
such that
(x,m-z_{2})
is in the twin prime power difference set. So
|C_{m}\cap B_{(z_{1},z_{2})}|=\frac{\ell-3}{2}
. By a nearly identical argument, if
m-z_{2}
is a nonsquare, then
|C_{m}\cap B{(z_{1},z_{2})}|=\frac{\ell-3}{2}
.

Thus, within every block of the
\left(\ell(\ell-2),\frac{\ell^{2}-1}{2}-\ell,\frac{(\ell-3)(\ell+1)}{4}\right)
-BIBD, one colour appears
\ell-2
times and the rest appear exactly
\frac{\ell-3}{2}
times. This forms an
(\ell-2)
-ULSE
\ell
-colouring.
∎

By taking the complement of the BIBDs from Lemma
6.3
, we obtain a family of BIBDs that admit
0
-ULSE colourings.

Theorem 6.4
.

If
\ell\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
\ell\geq 5
,
\ell
is a prime power, and
\ell-2
is a prime power, then there exists a
0
-ULSE
\ell
-coloured
\left(\ell(\ell-2),\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}}{2},\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}}{4}\right)
-BIBD.

Proof.

Applying Corollary
2.7
to Lemma
6.3
immediately gives the result.
∎

Regarding the
\left(\ell(\ell-2),\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}}{2},\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}}{4}\right)
-BIBDs from Theorem
6.4
, if we substitute
4n-1=\ell(\ell-2)
into the parameters of the BIBDs we obtain
(4n-1,2n,n)
. The complement of a
(4n-1,2n,n)
-BIBD is a
(4n-1,2n-1,n-1)
-BIBD which, recalling from Section
5.1
, exists if and only if a Hadamard matrix of order
4n
exists. Pairing this observation with Theorem
5.3
establishes that
0
-ULSE coloured BIBDs can be constructed from Hadamard matrices, and Hadamard matrices can be constructed from
0
-ULSE coloured BIBDs. It is already known that the twin prime power difference set can be used to construct Hadamard matrices (see for example
[
22
]
).

To see that the
0
-LSE
\ell
-coloured
\left(\ell(\ell-2),\frac{(\ell-2)^{2}}{2},\frac{(\ell-1)^{4}}{4}\right)
-BIBDs from Theorem
6.4
cannot be constructed from Theorem
4.2
, consider the ingredients if the construction were possible. Note that
\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}}{4(\ell-2)}\notin\mathbb{Z}
since gcd
(\ell-1,\ell-2)=1
and
\ell\geq 4
. However, the construction from Theorem
4.2
would a require a transversal design of index
\frac{(\ell-1)^{2}}{4(\ell-2)}
which is not possible.

As a final note regarding all three infinite families of
0
-ULSE
\ell
-coloured BIBDs from Theorems
5.3
,
5.7
, and
6.4
, it can be easily verified that the only BIBD that is contained in more than one family is the
0
-ULSE
4
-coloured
(16,6,2)
-BIBD built by Hadamard matrices and affine planes.

Question 7.1
.

How can we find more families of
0
-ULSE coloured BIBDs?

One possible candidate for a new family comes from projective geometries. Consider an odd integer
d\geq 3
. The complement of a projective geometry PG
{}_{d}(q)
is a

\left(\frac{q^{d+1}-1}{q-1},q^{d},q^{d-1}(q-1)\right)\text{-BIBD}.

It is known that there exists a projective geometry PG
{}_{d}(q)
for all
d\geq 2
and all prime powers
q
; see Section 2.3 of
[
30
]
for a proof. It can be verified that this BIBD satisfies Equation (
7
) when
\ell=\sqrt{q^{d+1}}+1
. It is impossible to create the ingredient designs in Theorem
4.2
from a
\left(\frac{q^{d+1}-1}{q-1},q^{d},q^{d-1}(q-1)\right)
-BIBD. The necessary RBIBD ingredient fails to exist since the block size does not divide the number of points, as is required of an RBIBD. So if
0
-ULSE coloured
\left(\frac{q^{d+1}-1}{q-1},q^{d},q^{d-1}(q-1)\right)
-BIBDs exist, they would constitute another infinite family that is not captured by Theorem
4.2
. In support of the hypothesis that this family does exist, we have the following result.

Theorem 7.2
.

There exists a
0
-ULSE
\left(\sqrt{q^{d+1}}+1\right)
-coloured
\left(\frac{q^{d+1}-1}{q-1},q^{d},q^{d-1}(q-1)\right)
-BIBD for
q=d=3
.

Proof.

Begin with the BIBD obtained from the
(40,13,4)
difference set

\{0,1,2,4,5,8,13,14,17,19,24,26,34\}

in
\mathbb{Z}_{40}
. Next, partition the points in
\mathbb{Z}_{40}
by the colour classes
C_{i}=\{i,10+i,20+i,30+i\}
for each
0\leq i\leq 9
. This construction forms a
4
-ULSE
10
-coloured
(40,13,4)
-BIBD. Taking the complement of this
4
-ULSE
10
-coloured
(40,13,4)
-BIBD produces a
0
-ULSE
10
-coloured
(40,27,18)
-BIBD.
∎

Question 7.3
.

Does there exist a
0
-ULSE
(\sqrt{q^{d+1}}+1)
-coloured
\left(\frac{q^{d+1}-1}{q-1},q^{d},q^{d-1}(q-1)\right)
-BIBD for all prime powers
q
and for all odd
d\geq 3
?

Another possible candidate is a family of BIBDs with parameters

\left(\frac{1}{2}\ell(\ell-2)(\ell-3),(\ell-1)(\ell-3),2(\ell-3)\right)

where
\ell\geq 5
. It can be verified that such a BIBD satisfies Equation (
7
). However, such a family cannot be obtained by Theorem
4.2
since it would require a transversal design of index
\frac{2(\ell-3)}{\ell-2}
, which is not an integer if
\ell\geq 5
.

Question 7.4
.

Does there exist a
0
-ULSE
\ell
-coloured
\left(\frac{1}{2}\ell(\ell-2)(\ell-3),(\ell-1)(\ell-3),2(\ell-3)\right)
-BIBD for all
\ell\geq 5
?

Not every BIBD satisfying Equation (
7
) has a
0
-ULSE colouring. As mentioned in Section
3
, there are five
(15,8,4)
-BIBDs up to isomorphism and by computer search we have determined that only one of them has a
0
-ULSE
5
-colouring. However, we have yet to find integers
v,k,\lambda,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
that satisfy Equation (
7
) and Lemmas
2.1
and
2.2
such that there is no
0
-ULSE
\ell
-coloured symmetric
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD. This motivates the following conjecture.

Conjecture 7.5
.

For every
v,k,\ell\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
satisfying Equation (
7
) such that there exists a symmetric BIBD on
v
points with block size
k
, there exists a
0
-ULSE
\ell
-coloured symmetric BIBD on
v
points with block size
k
.

The Bruck-Ryser-Chowla Theorem, proven by the work done in
[
6
,
10
,
28
]
, states that if a symmetric
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD exists, then
k-\lambda
is a perfect square if
v
is even and there exists a nontrivial solution to

x^{2}=(k-\lambda)y^{2}+(-1)^{\frac{v-1}{2}}\lambda z^{2}

if
v
is odd. From Theorem
3.11
, in a symmetric
0
-ULSE
\ell
-coloured
(v,k,\lambda)
-BIBD,
\lambda=\frac{k((\ell-1)^{2}-k)}{(\ell-1)^{2}}
. Therefore
k-\lambda=\left(\frac{k}{\ell-1}\right)^{2}
. Furthermore,
(x,y,z)=(k,\ell-1,0)
is a nonzero solution to the above equation. So, the Bruck-Ryser-Chowla Theorem does not prove the nonexistence of any symmetric
0
-ULSE coloured BIBD.

Our work gives new necessary conditions for the existence of Hadamard matrices, affine planes, and twin prime powers. As mentioned in Section
5
, determining when Hadamard matrices exist and determining when affine planes exist are two long-standing open problems. Whether there exist infinitely many twin prime powers, i.e. solutions to the equation

p^{n}-q^{m}=2

(11)

where
p
and
q
are primes and
n,m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, is also an open problem. If
n=m=1
in (
11
), then
p
and
q
are twin primes. For background on the Twin Prime Conjecture, which asserts that there are infinitely many twin primes, see
[
21
]
. Solutions for
p
when
n=1
and
m\geq 1
are listed in sequence A267945 of the On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences (OEIS)
[
25
]
and solutions for
q
when
n\geq 1
and
m=1
are listed in sequence A267944 in the OEIS
[
24
]
. If
n>1
and
m>1
, then (
11
) is a special case of Pillai’s Conjecture
[
26
,
27
]
, which states that for any fixed
c\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, there are finitely many solutions to the equation
x^{n}-y^{m}=c
where
x,y,n,m\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
and
n,m\geq 2
. There is only one known solution to
x^{n}-y^{m}=2
(see
[
23
]
) which is
(x,y,n,m)=(3,5,3,2)
; the solution
(p,q,n,m)=(3,5,3,2)
also satisfies (
11
). For the number of known solutions for each
c\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
, see the sequence A076427 in the OEIS
[
23
]
.

It is well known that there are infinitely many Hadamard matrices and affine planes (see
[
30
]
) and so the families of
0
-ULSE coloured BIBDs in Theorems
5.3
and
5.7
are infinite families. Since we are interested in determining if the family of
0
-ULSE coloured BIBDs in Theorem
6.4
is also infinite, we ask the following question.

Question 7.6
.

Are there infinitely many twin prime powers?

By Theorems
5.3
,
5.7
, and
6.4
, if a Hadamard matrix, an affine plane, or a pair of twin prime powers is given, then a
0
-ULSE coloured BIBD can be constructed. Consequently, the existences of the families of
0
-ULSE coloured BIBDs stated in Theorems
5.3
and
5.7
are necessary conditions for the existences of Hadamard matrices and affine planes respectively. Hence, proving the nonexistence of these
0
-ULSE coloured BIBDs of specific orders would prove the nonexistence of the corresponding Hadamard matrices and affine planes. Furthermore, proving that there are only finitely many of the
0
-ULSE coloured BIBDs from Theorem
6.4
would prove that there are only finitely many twin prime powers.

Question 7.7
.

Can
0
-ULSE coloured BIBDs be used to show the nonexistence of Hadamard matrices, affine planes, and twin prime powers?
