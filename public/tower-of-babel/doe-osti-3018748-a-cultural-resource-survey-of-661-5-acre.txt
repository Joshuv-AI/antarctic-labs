Project Description: This report presents the results of a Section 110 cultural resource survey of
Pedro Lake and the surrounding area. Sandia National Laboratories’ (Sandia) cultural resources
program, in collaboration with personnel from the cultural resource department from the
Northern Ranges of the Nevada Test and Training Range (NTTR), Nellis Air Force Base (NAFB)

equipped with modern electronic tracking instrumentation and data acquisition systems used to
characterize the performance of weapons and their delivery systems. These activities require a
remote location for both public safety and to maintain national security. The TTR lies within Cactus
Flat, a valley with minimal topographical relief flanked by mountains and hills.

INTRODUCTION
Sandia National Laboratories (Sandia) Cultural Resources Program, in collaboration with cultural
resource personnel from Northern Ranges of the Nevada Test and Training Range (NTTR), Nellis
Air Force Base (NAFB) conducted a Section 110 cultural resource survey of Pedro Lake and the
surrounding area, located on the Tonopah Test Range (TTR), Nellis Air Force Base, Nye County,
Nevada.

The project required multiple 30 meter (m) (100 feet [ft]) transects across a block survey area for
a total surveyed area of 267.71 hectares (ha) (661.50 acres [ac]). The survey area occurs on
withdrawn private lands administered by the Department of Defense (DOD), within a common
use area shared by the DOD, the Department of Energy (DOE)/Sandia Field Office (SFO), and the
National Nuclear Security Administration (NNSA), who operate the range under a US Department
of the Air Force (DAF) permit.

Sandia personnel, along with NTTR archaeologists conducted an intensive (100-percent)
pedestrian cultural resources survey between September 16, 2024, and October 24, 2024.
Christina Chavez, Stanley Kerr-Painter and Taressa Nield from Sandia were joined by Sarah Rice,
Lynn Robinson, and Zach Tellis from NTTR to complete the survey. Christina Chavez served as
Principal Investigator, while Stanley Kerr-Painter served as Crew Chief for the project. Taressa
Nield served as a field technician and oversaw data management and map production for the
project.

Sandia documented two previously recorded sites, 15 newly discovered sites, and 18 isolated
occurrences (IOs). The following sites are recommended as eligible for nomination to the National
Register of Historic Places (NRHP). Sites 26NY19405, 26NY19406, and 26NY19410 are prehistoric
lithic scatters of unknown aboriginal temporal and cultural affiliations. Site 26NY19413 is a
prehistoric lithic scatter with thermal features, and 26NY19414 is a prehistoric lithic scatter. Both
sites are of an Aboriginal: Middle Archaic (6,000 BP – 3,000 BP) period, based on the presence of
diagnostic projectile points. Avoidance of the above resources is recommended. These sites are
in an area where there are currently no planned undertakings or activities, and no impacts are
anticipated. If any future activities are undertaken in the area, avoidance or other mitigative plans
would be discussed and addressed prior to the activity.

The following sites are recommended as not eligible for nomination to the NRHP. Site 26NY1553
is a multicomponent site that consists of a low-density lithic scatter, a fire-cracked rock scatter,
and a historic berm. Site 26NY19404 is a prehistoric artifact scatter of an unknown aboriginal
cultural and temporal affiliation. Sites 26NY19401, 26NY19402, 26NY19407, 26NY19408,
26NY19409, 26NY19411, 26NY19412, and 26NY19415 are all ephemeral, two-track roads, with no
engineer controls that encompass early historic use (ca AD 1950s) up to present day. Site
26NY19403 is a historic site consisting of one feature with an unknown function. These sites are
in an area where there are currently no planned undertakings or activities, and no impacts are

be recommended for future undertakings. The 18 IOs do not meet the criteria for listing to the

ENVIRONMENT
Mountain ranges in this region are connected to their adjoining basins with alluvial aprons that
consist of piedmont surfaces and coalescing fans that grade down as multiple fans onto the valley
floors (Ezzo 1996a:12). The contact between the alluvial aprons and the mountain ranges is
distinctive, however the contact between the alluvial aprons and the valley fill is much harder to
distinguish (Bagley 1980).

Physiography
The proposed project area is in the southwest portion of the Cactus Flat basin, encompassing the
Pedro Lake Playa. Cactus Flat is a topographically closed basin flanked by the Cactus Range on the
west and southwest and the Kawich Range to the east and northeast. Low topographic divides
separate Cactus Flat from Stone Cabin and Ralston valleys to the north and Gold Flat to south. The
Pedro Lake Playa project area is topographically flat ground at an average elevation 1,640 m
(5,380 ft).

Soils
Soils are characterized by material derived from the Cactus Range. Relatively thin fan deposits of
chert-laden gravels overlie shallow bedrock/pediments that form undulating ridges and terraces
that extend almost to the playa margins (Dickerson 2009:111). The abundant chert and
chalcedony nodules found in these gravels were a likely source of lithic material for tool
manufacture by the indigenous inhabitants of the region (Dickerson 2003).

Climate
Although the project area lies within the Great Basin, biotically and climatically the area more
closely resembles the Mojave Desert region of the Basin and Range Province. The area is
characterized by extremely low rainfall (annual precipitation between 5 and 10 inches [in] [12–25
cm]), high evapotranspiration, and relatively low humidity (Ezzo 1996a:9). Most of the rains in the
area result from relatively longer-lasting winter storms that develop over the northern Pacific

 6

Ocean. During the summer, a shift in the overall storm pattern brings relatively more-intense,
short-term rains from the more distant Gulf of Mexico. Summer temperatures in the project area
routinely exceed 38 Degrees Celsius (°C) (100 Degrees Fahrenheit [°F]); evening temperatures
drop as much as -6.6 °C (20°F) to -1.1 °C (30°F). Winter temperatures are mild: daytime highs
frequently reach to 21 °C (70°F), and evening lows are often below freezing. Due in part to the
variations in topography, considerable microvariability in both annual precipitation and
temperature exists across the valley.

Vegetation
Temperature extremes and arid conditions of the Great Basin Desert limit both species diversity
and vegetation coverage. The vegetation within the current project area includes Shadscale
(Atriplex confertifolia), winterfat (Krascheninnikovia lanata), four-wing saltbush (Atriplex
canescens), ricegrass (Orzyopsis hymenoides), and budsage (Artemisia spinescens). Yellow
rabbitbrush (Chrysothamnus viscidiflorus) is also present and a few clumps of galleta grass
(Pleuraphis jamesii) were noted.

Fauna
The prehistoric inhabitants of the area hunted a variety of animals for food, hides, and bone. All
available environmental zones and landforms were exploited. Important game animals included
pronghorn, deer, wapiti, bighorn sheep, bison, cottontails, and jackrabbits. Mammals such as
bears, wolves, beavers, bobcats, foxes, and river otters were hunted for their pelts. Turkeys, owls,
hawks, eagles, and various perching birds were hunted or raised for their feathers. Quail and
waterfowl were procured as secondary food resources.

The Nevada Wild Horse Range and other wild horse land-use areas compose a significant portion
of the North Ranges of the Nevada Test and Training Range (NTTR) with herds common in Cactus
and Gold Flats, Kawich Valley, Goldfield Hills, and the Stonewall Mountains (Bureau of Land
Management [BLM] 2008). Hundreds of wild horses (Equus caballus) graze freely throughout TTR
and activities on-site have had little effect on the horse population or their grazing habits (DRI
1991; USAF 1978). Horses and pronghorn (Antilocapra americana) were observed in the vicinity
of the project area. Other mammals common to the area but not encountered while completing
the field inventory include mule deer (Odocoileus hemionus), kit fox (Vulpes macrotis), bobcat
(Zynx rufus), coyote (Canis latrans), and gray fox (Urocyon cinereoargenteus) (USAF 1978, DRI
1991). Small mammals such as antelope ground squirrels (Ammospermophilus leucurus),
kangaroo rats (Dipodomys deserti), pocket mice (Chaetodipus penicillatus), cottontails (Sylvilagus
audubonii), and jackrabbits (Lepus californicus) are widely distributed but not abundant.

Cultural Overview
Over the years, a number of alternative cultural frameworks have been proposed for southern
Nevada. This report follows the structure and terminology of the Southern Nevada Prehistoric
Context presented by Roberts and Ahlstrom (2012: Table 1.1), adapted perspective and synthesis
of chronology by Blair et al. (1999), Jennings (1986, fig. 2:115), Warren and Crabtree (1986), and
Winslow (1996). For the prehistoric period, this framework covers the time of the earliest

 7

documented human occupation of the area (ca. 13,000 BP) until the earliest European exploration
of the area (ca. AD 1600). This period is divided into six broad temporal units: Lake Mohave
(13,000 – 7000 BP), Pinto (7500 – 4000 BP), Gypsum (4000 BP – AD 400), Late Prehistoric (AD 1150
– 1600), ethnohistoric, and Historic. The primary characteristics and events of these periods are
summarized below.

Lake Mohave Period (13,000 – 7000 BP)
The Lake Mohave period corresponds to the earliest known human occupation of the area,
encompassing the terminal Pleistocene and early Holocene epochs. This includes the Paleoindian
period and the Early Archaic period. The Lake Mohave period, and the Pinto and Gypsum periods
that follow, constitute the period of human occupation of North America before the adoption of
agriculture in the area.

Paleoindian
Paleoindian groups are generally described as small, highly mobile bands of hunter-gatherers
adapted to a climate that was cooler and wetter than at present (Martin and Plog 1973:44).
Paleoindian sites are often associated with the remains of such extinct mammal species as
mammoth, North American camel, and giant ground sloth, an association that has led to
interpretations of subsistence strategies that emphasize big-game hunting, although species of
small animals probably were exploited as well. Archaeologically, the Paleoindian period is
characterized by a number of distinct traditions of fluted projectile points including Clovis, Folsom,
Agate Basin, and Hell Gap (Jennings 1980). Known sites of the Clovis tradition are extremely
widespread across North and South America. The other traditions listed above, referred to
collectively as Plano, are more regional in scope. Although debate continues about the timing of
the beginning of the Paleoindian period, the general agreement is that the period ends around
10,500 BC with the extinction of the Pleistocene megafauna. Some artifacts include Lake Mojave,
Parman, Silver Lake, and rare fluted projectile points (Clovis and Folsom); lunate and eccentric
crescents; small flake engravers; specialized scrapers; leaf-shaped knives; drills and heavy
choppers or hammerstones (Warren and Crabtree 1986:184).

In the U.S. Southwest, Paleoindian sites range in function, including camps with associated kill
sites, such as Murray Springs in southern Arizona (Haynes 1969); kill and butchering localities,
such as the Olsen-Chubbuck site in east-central Colorado (Wheat 1972); kill sites, such as the Naco
Mammoth site in southeastern Arizona (Haury 1953); and several scattered finds of individual
projectile points. In southern Nevada, archaeological evidence of the Paleoindian period is
limited. Most of these sites consist of isolated fluted points (Tuohy 1985). A notable exception to
this pattern is the Tule Springs site, first identified in the 1930s (Harrington 1930) and more
extensively investigated in the 1960s (Harrington and Simpson 1961; Shutler 1967). The
Paleoindian component at the site was initially documented through a series of radiocarbon dates
between 12,000- and 10,000-years BP, although no fluted points were identified at the site
(Shutler 1967). Several of the investigated loci contained the remains of megafauna species
including mammoth and camel. The lithic collection included a small number of choppers,
scrapers, and flakes. Several thermal features interpreted as hearths were also identified. Three
 8

of these features were associated with lithic artifacts or megafauna remains. Taken together,
these data have been used to support an interpretation that at least some of the components at
the Tule Springs site functioned as temporary campsites.

Bedwell (1970, 1973) and Hester (1973) have interpreted the same assemblages to reflect
specialized adaptations to lacustrine resources around the edges of pluvial lakes. J.O. Davis (1978)
provides a synthesis suggesting a more generalized hunting and collecting economy existed, in
which lakeside sites represent the exploitation of marsh resources. These varying interpretations
are considered a product of limited numbers of Paleoindian sites having adequate stratigraphic
assemblages. The majority of Paleoindian sites in the Great Basin are characterized as surface
sites commonly found along shores of pluvial lakes or Pleistocene waterways.

Early Archaic
The Early Archaic period is generally characterized by an economy focusing on the exploitation of
wild plant and animal resources. The Archaic tradition in the Great Basin, including the current
project area, was initially described as a period of relatively small nomadic groups (Warren 1967)
with stable foraging referred to as the Desert Culture (Jennings and Norbeck 1955).

The Archaic period differs from the preceding Paleoindian period largely in the greater diversity
of species that were used, reflecting in part the extinction of many species of large animals at the
end of the Pleistocene. The period also saw the appearance of more-diverse localized cultural
traditions that probably reflected the rapidly changing climatic patterns that occurred at the
beginning of the Holocene. These changes resulted in the creation of dramatically different
ecological zones across the continent. The changes are reflected in the archaeological record by
ever-increasing diversity in the tool kit. Fluted projectile points fell out of use and were replaced
with a variety of new types differing in both style and method of manufacture.

The diverse Archaic tool kit also included numerous tools for the processing of plant resources.
Although Paleoindian peoples probably exploited plant remains as well, their artifact assemblage
provided little in the way of direct evidence.

In Southern Nevada, some of the most common site types dating to the Early Archaic period
consists of surface finds of stemmed series projectile points such as the Lake Mohave and Silver
Lake types (Roth 2012:91). These sites are commonly, but not exclusively, found in areas that
formerly were lakeshores and marshes, suggesting the importance of wetland resources at this
time as argued by Hester (1973) and Bedwell (1970, 1973) who suggests a more specialized
adaptation to the pluvial lakes and waterways. In addition to these projectile points, Early Archaic
period sites also contain a variety of flaked stone scrapers and occasional rock features that may
represent hearths. A number of additional sites without the temporally diagnostic projectile
points have produced radiocarbon dates that fall within the Early Archaic period.

Middle to Late Archaic Periods (7500 BP – AD 400)
Pinto Period (7500 – 4000 BP)

 9

The Pinto period is coterminous with the Middle Archaic in southern Nevada. As the name implies,
the hallmark of the period is the Pinto lithic-tool complex, characterized by leaf-shaped knives,
scrapers, and projectile points with bifurcated, expanding stems, shallow basal notches, and
bulbous tangs (Ezzo 1996a:40). Climatically, the period is coterminous with the beginning of the
middle Holocene, which is characterized by an overall warming and drying (Antevs 1955). This had
an obvious impact on site distribution, with evidence of human occupation in the area
corresponding to areas with reliable water resources, and subsistence strategies, with evidence
of a broadening of the resource base (Roth 2012:95). This includes the more common presence
of ground stone artifacts, reflecting the increasing importance of plant resources, and associated
features containing fire-cracked rock. Flat milling slabs along with shallow basin and circular basin
milling slabs have been found at some sites.

Although they are somewhat more common than Early Archaic period and Paleoindian period
sites, the distribution of Pinto period sites and sites with Pinto period components in the project
area remains sparse.

Gypsum Period (4000 BP – AD 400)
The Late Archaic period, also referred to as the Gypsum period, saw a return to more-mesic
climatic conditions leading to an increase in the availability of large game animals, as well as an
increased availability of plant resources (Roth 2012:99). The primary diagnostic artifact types of
the period are a variety of large dart points including the eponymous Gypsum, Elko series, and
Humboldt series points. An increase in ground stone artifacts at Gypsum period sites further
speaks to the continuing importance of plant resources in the diet. The appearance of mortars
and pestles at this time is likely to signal the introduction of the seed pods of leguminous trees
into the diet (Roth 2012:99). The period is also characterized by an increase and elaboration of
ritual artifacts, including artifacts made from shell species native to the California coast (Ezzo
1996a:40). Also, the association of split-twig figurines and extensive rock art sites has been
interpreted as an expression of enriched ceremonial lifestyle and economic ties with outside areas
(Pippin 1986:51-52).

The number and diversity of sites increased significantly during the Gypsum period. Sites
interpreted as large base camps have been identified in presumably resource-rich areas. It has
been proposed that, in general terms, the Gypsum period saw a shift in preferred settlement
location from the pluvial lakeshore settings that characterized earlier Archaic periods to one that
included both lowland and mountain habitats (Davis 1963). By extension, this shift has been
argued to reflect the continuing importance of a generalist adaptive strategy, one that exploited
several ecological zones, probably seasonally (Ezzo 1996a:42).

Late Prehistoric Period (AD 1150–Contact)
With the end of the Virgin Anasazi occupation in southern Nevada, the area became the home of
peoples who followed a more mobile lifeway increasingly dependent on hunting and foraging, but
who continued to practice small-scale agriculture (Ezzo 1996a:55). The artifact assemblage for the
period includes Owens Valley Brown Wares, Desert Side-Notched and Cottonwood Triangular

 10

projectile points, and large triangular stone knives. The ground stone assemblage reflects the
apparent return to a foraging economy, dominated by unshaped metates, manos, and mortars
and pestles.

The Late Prehistoric period also saw the appearance of an identifiable Southern Paiute, or Numic-
speaking, presence in the Great Basin and southern Nevada. This was roughly concurrent with the
initial movement of Paiute peoples into the region from the Mojave Desert. The precise timing of
this process remains a point of debate among scholars in the region, exacerbated in part by the
relatively ephemeral nature of archaeological sites during this time. One school of thought holds
that the Numic-speaking peoples occupied the area as early as the Middle Archaic period, an
argument based largely on linguistic data (Aikens and Witherspoon 1986). An alternative
hypothesis suggested by Gross (1977) argues that the linguistic ancestors of the Numic were
occupying the Great Basin as early as 10,000 years ago. Aikens and Witherspoon (1986) have also
theorized that the expansion occurred many times during the last 5,000 years. Lastly, Rafferty and
Blair (1984), Rafferty (1989), Lyneis (1982:180), and others have proposed that Archaic hunter
and gatherer groups coexisted with the Anasazi and Fremont, becoming the ethnographically
known Southern Paiute and Western Shoshone described by the first Europeans entering the
region. Conversely, discussions and consultations by Nellis AFB with the Consolidated Group of
Tribes and Organizations (CGTO), the Great Basin descendants of the aboriginal population
disagree with the Numic concept theory. They argue their ancestral lineage was always here, and
their population was never replaced by other cultural groups.

Ethnographic Culture History
Early explorers and immigrants in the southern and central Great Basin encountered widely
scattered groups of hunters and gatherers currently known as the Southern Paiute, Western
Shoshone and Owens Valley Paiute (Fowler 1982, Kelly and Fowler 1986). The current project is
within the traditional territory of the Western Shoshone. However, the boundaries between these
hunter gatherer groups were fluid and characterized by regular contact and cooperation.
Subsistence strategies revolved around movements between environmental zones within the
territories (e.g., highlands and lowlands) according to seasonal availability of food resources
(Steward 1938:95-97; Wheat 1967). Criteria for the location of a primary camp included its
proximity to stored or cached foods; the availability of water, wood fuel, and house construction;
and a microclimate with relatively warm winter temperatures like that found in canyon mouths
or in the woodlands (Steward 1938:233). The presence of springs or river tributaries likely
stimulated the formation of larger semi-permanent camps, but generally, these groups moved
seasonally, occupying small camps populated by small groups of extended family members. The
cultivation of domesticated plants was not an important activity to Western Shoshone, although,
various Numic groups were observed broadcasting seeds of wild plants, constructing irrigation
canals that watered stands of wild plants, or pruning vegetation to increase the productivity of
wild plants (Steward 1938; Stewart 1951; Zedeño et al. 2006).

None of the major transportation corridors in Nevada passed through the land that was to
become the NTTR, and because of this, there were few early settlements in the area. There is little

 11

knowledge of the aboriginal inhabitants in this area in comparison to other areas of Nevada.
According to informants of Steward’s there were both aboriginal settlements and resources
within the boundaries of the NTTR. These included the territory of individual Western Shoshone
bands over hundreds of square kilometers, and the Southern Paiutes who maintained villages in
the Oasis Valley north of Beatty who also harvested pinyon nuts in the Belted and Kawich Ranges
(Haarklau 2003; Steward 1938).

Epidemics periodically swept through the region during the late 19th and early 20th Century
devastating the ever-shrinking indigenous population in the Great Basin and Southern Nevada.
There are many accounts of the role disease played in the decline of the Southern Paiute (Inter-
Tribal Council of Nevada 1976; Ruuska et al. 2011).

According to Holm (2005), Between the 1890s and 1920s, the US government thought it could
make Indians "vanish”. Reservations were established slowly and erratically for the Native
American inhabitants of Nevada. The government gave allotments of land to individual Native
Americans to farm, introduced the English language to Native American children, and introduced
Christianity. Federal officials sought to assimilate Native Americans through policies and
procedures within a generation or two (Holm 2005). Nevada was not immune to this trend and
reservations and Indian boarding schools were established throughout the state (Zedeño et al.
2006).

In more recent decades, both the DOD and DOE have initiated American Indian Programs for the
lands they administer on the NTTR and NNSS. Representatives from tribes or organizations
culturally affiliated with these withdrawn lands engage in government-to-government
consultations with the agencies through the CGTO. Seventeen different Shoshone and Southern
Paiute tribes consult with the agencies. Through these interactions, the CGTO and the agencies
can address American Indian concerns on a wide range of environmental and management issues
(Haarklau 2001; Stoffle et al. 2001).

Historic Period Culture History
The first Spanish explorers to see the Colorado River were the men of Francisco de Coronado’s
expedition in the 1540s. Coming north from New Spain (present-day Mexico), they were seeking
mineral wealth. In 1598, Juan de Oñate laid claim to the kingdoms and provinces of New Mexico—
La Provincia de Nuevo México—for the Spanish Crown, but Spanish occupation of other regions
in the southwest was much slower. Alta California (present-day California) was not settled by the
Spanish until the 1760s and 1770s. Until these settlements were established, the Spanish
developed travel and supply routes between New Spain and Alta California that crossed southern
portions of the Colorado River. During the latter half of the eighteenth century, the Spanish began
to search for travel routes between New Mexico and Alta California that would bypass the Grand
Canyon; these routes included those that traversed southern Nevada. Expedition leaders included
Fathers Francisco Garcés, Silvestre Vélez de Escalante, and Francisco Atanasio Domínguez. The
Spanish would not acquire influence over the region before the independence of Mexico (Sterner
and Ezzo 1996:79–82). Spain’s rule ended in 1821, with Mexico gaining independence. Now under

 12

control of the Mexican government, which sought commercial trade, the Great Basin opened to
trappers and explorers. One of the first explorers through the area was Jedediah Strong Smith. As
part owner of the Rocky Mountain Fur Company, he began to explore Utah and Nevada in August
1826, looking for waters that were well stocked with beaver (Castor canadensis).

Smith traveled along the Virgin River in the fall of 1826, passing through the area of present-day
Bunkerville, continuing south to the Virgin River’s convergence with the Colorado River, and
traveling into Mexican California. Smith passed through Clark County again along the same route
in 1827; however, his second meeting with the Mojave Indians was much less friendly, and Smith,
along with other survivors of an attack, proceeded back to California. After surviving tragic
encounters with Native Americans and a grizzly bear (Ursus arctos) attack, Smith discovered South
Pass and was the first American to travel overland to California, to cross the Sierra Nevada and
the Great Basin, and to travel from California to Oregon. Numerous traders and trappers followed
Smith’s routes, as Smith’s expedition opened the first reliable trade route between Santa Fe, New
Mexico, and California (NPS 1986:8–12; Sterner and Ezzo 1996:82–83).

Trails that radiated out of the Hispanic colonies in New Mexico were called Spanish trails and had
been established by the Tewa, the Navajo, the Ute, the Apache, the Paiute, Spanish traders, and
Mexican frontiersmen. The Spanish trails from New Mexico to California were used to trade New
Mexican goods for California pack animals. The first organized expedition was completed by
Antonio Armijo, a Mexican trader, who left Abiquiú, New Mexico, on November 6, 1829, with a
commercial caravan of 60 men and arrived in California by February 24, 1830. Armijo used routes
that had been established by Father Vélez de Escalante through northern Arizona and by Jedediah
Smith along the Virgin River. The expedition returned by the same route after 2 months of trading.
Even though a caravan left Abiquiú annually, the trail established by Armijo was not the primary
route of what became known as the Old Spanish Trail. Many used a modified route established
by fur trappers William Wolfskill and George C. Yount. This shortened Wolfskill-Yount route that
traveled from the Virgin River to the Mojave River by way of the Muddy River and Las Vegas
became the preferred route of the Old Spanish Trail. Traders eventually connected the Old
Spanish Trail to the Santa Fe Trail, which ran from Independence, Missouri, to Santa Fe, New
Mexico, and created a major trade route across the continent (Hafen and Hafen 1954:157–158;
Sterner and Ezzo 1996:83–86).

The United States acquired a large portion of the Southwest following the defeat of Mexico in
1848. The United States Congress passed a series of bills that created the Compromise of 1850,
which established California as a state and Utah and New Mexico as territories. The New Mexico
Territory included almost all of present-day New Mexico and Arizona, south-central Colorado, and
southern Nevada. The territorial capital was located in Santa Fe, more than 500 miles away from
this region. Southern Nevada would remain part of the New Mexico Territory until 1863, when it
became part of the Arizona Territory (Sterner and Ezzo 1996:86).

By the middle 1860s, ranches had been established in Pahranagat, Big Smokey, Monitor, Railroad,
Hot Creek and Stone Cabin valleys (Angel 1881:484, 515-516; Bowers and Muessig 1982:86-87;

 13

Elliott 1973:115-122; Kral 1951:1, 3-4; McCracken and Howerton 1996). The ranch headquarters
in the Kawich Range were likely established sometime later, but still during the late 19th Century.
Although Cactus Flat did not offer an environment conducive for the establishment of such early
ranching headquarters, the ranch site recorded by Allen et al. (1998) does reflect additional
ranching activities during the first part of the 20th Century. O.K. and Ed Reed had purchased both
the Hawes Canyon Reed Cattle Company headquarters and Breen Ranches from Jack Longstreet
by 1906 and by 1913 had merged with the slaughter, packing, and meat-market operation owned
by Herb and Jake Mumphrey in Goldfield to form the renowned United Cattle Company
(McCracken and Howerton 1996:186-191). The Kawich Cattle Association (John Connolly) also
apparently had a headquarters ranch at Breen Creek in the north end of Cactus Flat during the
early 1900s. Likewise, Jim M. Butler, who eventually married O.K. Reed’s oldest daughter
Florence, had ranching interests in the region during the early 1900s (McCracken 1987). Bergin et
al. (1978:36-38) report there were at least eleven different individuals or firms using the range
for grazing in 1941.

Mining
Mining was also an important historic-age activity in the region and by 1910 small mining camps
were established in the Kawich (Golden Arrow and Silverbow) and Cactus (Wellington, Antelope
Springs, and Cactus Spring) ranges; as well as in the Trappman Hills (Wilson's Camp and
Trappman’s Camp). With the possible exception of the Silverbow District, none of these mining
districts ever became major producers. Yet mining continued in the region up to and following its
withdrawal from public access in 1940. Although the current survey area is not directly located
within one of these mining properties, there is still a potential for the discovery of mining claims
and prospects. Mellan, which was established during the economic depression of the mid-1930s,
is approximately 8.05 km to the east/southeast. The older mining ventures at Nixon and Gold
Reed are less than 40 km away to the southeast. These early Twentieth Century mining locales lie
on opposing flanks of the Kawich Range at a pass just north of Quartzite Mountain.

Military
Nevada’s statehood stakes its roots in the Civil War, when it joined the union in 1864. Central
Nevada’s relationship with military training and national defense programs begins later with
World War II (WWII). The following narratives will discuss the components of the military and
national defense reservations in central Nevada and the developments and programs conducted
in Nevada within these timeframes.

Nevada Test and Training Range
The NTTR covers nearly three million acres of southern Nevada, encompassing the southwest
corner of Lincoln County, northwest corner of Clark County, and much of the southern stem of
Nye County. In addition, it is surrounded by airspace designated as Military Operations Areas
(MOAs). At one time, these MOAs were nearly twice the size of the NTTR, extending from
California into Utah (Loomis 1993:ix). The current MOAs do not reach the California border.

 14

The range was originally the Tonopah Bombing and Gunnery Range, withdrawn from private use
in 1940 and in active use starting from 1942 through 1946 (Myhrer 2012:1-2). The range was used
by the Tonopah Army Airfield (AAF) in the north and by Las Vegas and Indian Springs AAFs in the
south. Missions from Tonopah trained pilots and crew to fly P-39 fighters and B-24 bombers,
including air combat, air-to-ground support, and strategic bombing. Las Vegas AAF missions were
similar, but for crews of the B-17 and later B-29 bombers. Small support and emergency auxiliary
fields were established across the range for these bases.

Starting in 1950, the range was used for fighter pilot training out of Las Vegas (Myhrer 2012:1-2).
The name thus changed to the Las Vegas Bombing and Gunnery Range and changed again to Nellis
Bombing Range when the Las Vegas AFB became Nellis AFB (Myhrer 2012:1-2). Through the years
a wide variety of missions, including ground attack, strategic bombing with mock nuclear
weapons, and the complex Red Flag training school have made use of the range, which has been
managed out of Nellis AFB ever since closure of the Tonopah AAF at the end of WWII (Myhrer
2012:1-44). Although managed by the Air Force, the range has been used extensively to also train
Navy, Marine, and North American Treaty Organization (NATO) pilots and air crew.

Following withdrawal of this area from the surrounding gunnery range in 1950, the Air Force
worked closely with the Atomic Energy Commission (AEC, precursor of the DOE) in support of
testing on the Nevada Proving Ground/Nevada Test Site/Nevada National Security Site
(NPG/NTS/NNSS) using the airstrip at Camp Desert Rock as well as Indian Springs (Myhrer
2012:23-28). The northern part of the range served as the location for five plutonium-scattering
tests in 1963, called Operation Roller Coaster (Johnson 1996:47). These tests were conducted by
the AEC and the Army. Architectural remnants of support camps and testing facilities survive
(Myhrer 2012:29-40).

Tonopah AAF
Construction of the Tonopah AAF, located between Tonopah and the northwest corner of the
NTTR, began in 1940 (Henderson-Elder and Myhrer 2010:11). Originally sharing the same name
as the bombing and gunnery range, it did not receive its separate name as the Tonopah AAF until
1944. As noted earlier, the base offered training to P-39 fighter pilots and B-24 bomber crews
(Henderson-Elder and Myhrer 2010:57-70). In addition, it initiated the area’s history of weapons
development away from public view with a series of glide bomb tests in 1944-1945 (Henderson-
Elder and Myhrer 2010:101-120). These were early “smart bombs” that were heat seeking, radio-
controlled, or TV-controlled. Most were launched from B-17 bombers, which were a rarity at
Tonopah.

By the end of the war the facility included a triangular runway system and a complete range of
temporary wood-framed buildings capable of supporting flight operations and a maximum of
6,537 personnel (Henderson-Elder and Myhrer 2010). It also boasted a stone-veneer
administration building and three large hangars.

 15

The base was closed in 1946 and dismantled in 1949 (Henderson-Elder and Myhrer 2010:121-
134). A total of 560 buildings and structures were offered for sale. Most of them were designed
to be demountable and easily transportable to new locations. The hangars and airstrip were
turned over to Nye County, which maintained the structures for some time following the war
(Henderson-Elder and Myhrer 2010:131). Two runways are still maintained and remain active as
public-use airport.

Tonopah Test Range
The Tonopah Test Range (TTR) is located in the northwest corner of the NTTR (Johnson 1996). TTR
is within the Air Force bombing and gunnery range, and starting in 1956 it was managed entirely
by the DOE and SNL (Davies 2008; Johnson 1996; Peck 2012:65-69; Ullrich et al. 2005). During the
late 1940s and into the 1950s, weapons design research for America’s atomic and nuclear
inventory had been conducted largely at the Salton Sea testing base in southern California, but
haze problems forced the AEC to seek another location. The TTR was withdrawn from public use
in 1956.

The facility had a small complex of support buildings and had use of a short Air Force runway. The
Sandia work was highly classified, involving such matters as development and testing of nuclear
bomb cases and release systems (Johnson 1996), including rocket and artillery launches (Ullrich
et al. 2005). Sandia also performed plutonium-scattering tests in the surrounding areas of the
NTTR. Beginning in 1978, and in several phases thereafter, facilities and testing equipment across
the TTR was upgraded and modernized to meet mission needs. The range has continued in this
role for the DOE (the AEC’s successor agency) into the 21st Century. From Sandia’s mission control
center, the range can draw upon a variety of communications systems, tracking radars, optical
tracking systems, telemetry stations and ground targets to fulfill mission requirements (SNL 2017).

From 1979 through the 1980s, TTR saw an immense increase of activity and development as it
became home to two spectacular Device Assembly Facility (DAF) top-secret programs unrelated
to the Sandia work, which are now declassified. First on the scene was called Constant Peg, a
program to develop a squadron of Soviet aircraft for use in training pilots out of Nellis AFB, on
how to cope with their Cold War adversaries (Yenne 2014:114-123). The program was extremely
successful and operated until 1988 (Davies 2008; Peck 2012).

In a brilliant piece of Cold War misdirection, the top-secret Constant Peg operation provided cover
for the simultaneous development of another even more sensitive program Senior Trend, which
involved testing of the F-117A Nighthawk stealth fighter (Davies 2008; Peck 2012: 61-63; Yenne
2014:132). While tame Communist Block Mikoyan-Gurevichs’ (MiGs) filled the air during the day,
the stealth F-117 only came out at night. The stealth F-117 operation was active at TTR until 1989
(Myhrer 2012:42-44), when the squadrons were transferred to Holloman AFB, New Mexico
(Yenne 2014:137). In 2008, the operational F-117 fleet was retired following a federal budget
decision. Unlike most other Air Force aircraft, the F-117s were not sent to Davis-Monthan AFB
where retired planes are stored, scrapped or loaned to museums. Instead, the F-117s were retired
to their original hangers at the TTR and various locations (Yenne 2014:137).The increased

 16

operations necessitated an extensive runway improvement, construction of hangers, and
replacement of temporary facilities. These improvements resulted in an impressive base with
extensive cinder block buildings, including a large recreation center (Davies 2008; Johnson 1996;
Peck 2012). The base, and previously Tonopah TTR, has always maintained close ties with the
nearby town of Tonopah. At one time, Sandia maintained a subdivision of houses in Tonopah,
which has been relocated from the Hawthorne Ammunition Depot, in Western Nevada. In the
early 1980’s, prior to the housing facility known as Mancamp was constructed, many base
personnel commuted to the base from Tonopah. Today, marking the turnoff from the main
highway to the TTR/Nellis AFB entrance is the bullet-scarred rocket sign, a distinctive local
landmark.

Research Design and Data Requirements
Cultural resources identified during this survey were evaluated for significance under the
following federal legislation and regulations: the NHPA of 1966, as amended (36 CFR 800), and
the NRHP (36 CFR 60). This legislation ensures the protection of historical-period and prehistoric
sites.

Under the NHPA, cultural resources may be eligible for listing in the NRHP if they are not
associated with “properties that have achieved significance within the last 50 years,” if they
“possess integrity of location, design, setting, materials, workmanship, feeling, and association,”
and if they

 (a) are associated with events that have made a significant contribution to the broad
 patterns of our history; or
 (b) are associated with the lives of persons significant in our past; or
 (c) embody the distinctive characteristics of a type, period, or method of construction, or
 that represent the work of a master, or that possess high artistic values, or . . .
 represent a significant and distinguishable entity whose components may lack
 individual distinction; or
 (d) have yielded, or may be likely to yield, information important in prehistory or history
         [NRHP, 36 CFR 60.4].

One of the goals of the current project was to evaluate the eligibility of cultural resources
identified in the survey area for possible NRHP listing. Of the criteria used to evaluate the eligibility
of a property or resource for listing in the NRHP, the most commonly used is Criterion D, which
states that a cultural resource is considered eligible if it can contribute to our understanding of
the past. Criterion D is often cited for prehistoric resources, for which linking the resource to a
specific individual or event is difficult, if not impossible. In the context of Criterion D, the eligibility

 18

of a particular resource is often evaluated by its potential to address important regional research
issues. This includes those questions that can be effectively addressed with data observable on
the site’s surface and/or the individual site’s potential to contain intact buried cultural deposits.
The acquisition of data beyond that directly observable, i.e. buried, during the survey would
require additional investigations. Listed below are examples of pertinent regional research
questions and the kinds of data, both directly observable during the survey as well as those classes
of data only available through expanded investigations that would be required to address them.

Chronology
Questions of chronology are often central to the archaeological investigation of any area. Indeed,
chronological control is often a critical component of a wide range of research issues. Roberts et
al. (2012) proposed several possible research questions for this theme. For the Paleoindian and
Archaic period, these questions include the timing of the entry of Paleoindian hunters into the
region, the temporal extent of the Early Archaic period occupation, and the timing of the
introduction of maize agriculture.

For cultural resource surveys, the investigation of chronological questions depend largely on an
examination of temporally diagnostic artifacts such as projectile points and other tools. Sites with
a high likelihood of containing buried cultural deposits can potentially provide additional
chronometric data in the form of materials amenable to radiocarbon or other direct forms of
dating.

Settlement Patterns and Settlement Systems
The related research themes of settlement patterns and settlement systems refer to how human
settlement is situated on the landscape. Proposed research questions for this theme include
refining the nature and distribution of sites of the Late Archaic period and identifying variability
in site function based on survey data (Roberts et al. 2012:281). For example, is there a temporal
component to any variability that is observed?

Kolvet et al. (2000b) in their study of the sites in Cactus Flat, focused on testing a
geomorphological model stating that landforms and natural resources form the basis for
determining the location, type, and integrity of sites (Nials 1999). Their data largely supported
this model. Kolvet et al. (2000a) hypothesized that prehistoric land use on the bajadas around
Cactus Flat, and by extension also around Gold Flat and Kawich Valley, would have been
minimized and primarily restricted to travel across the area along with short-term exploitation of
seasonal and/or ephemeral resources.

Primary data appropriate for the investigation of questions pertaining to settlement focus largely
on the spatial distribution of sites and isolates. For these data to be useful in addressing these
questions, fine-grained estimates of site function and age are required.

 19

Subsistence and Diet
Roberts et al. (2012:283–284) proposed several research questions relating to the theme of
subsistence and diet. Many of these questions require the kinds of data obtained from testing and
data recovery excavations. Nevertheless, several questions pertaining to subsistence and diet can
be addressed through survey data. For example, can site-distribution data inform on preferred
procurement locations, and can changes through time be discerned? In our assessment of the
project sites, we consider both the directly observable data in addition to the site’s potential to
yield additional information through expanded data recovery investigations.

Many of the direct indicators of subsistence behavior, such as macrobotanical remains and pollen,
were not observable during the survey. Fully investigating this theme would require additional
subsurface data collection. However, several indirect indicators, such as stone tools, faunal bone,
and food-processing features, would likely to be observable if present. The presence of thermal
features may also suggest that additional data recovery activities could provide additional data
for the investigation of diet and subsistence themes. Site location may indirectly inform on
questions of subsistence and diet, as well. Additionally, changes in the locations of sites in relation
to agricultural lands can also serve as proxy data demonstrations the importance of domesticates
in the diet.

Technology
Technology, in an archaeological context, refers to modifications of the environment to meet the
needs of the individual or group. This includes items as small as projectile points and other tools,
to large- scale water-control devices or other such modifications to the landscape. A research
question that could be addressed through survey data is that of determining the distribution of
features and tools associated with agriculture, which also may contribute to the investigation of
land-use patterns. Variability in the size and distribution of habitation features may also be
discernible from survey data alone. Other types of questions, including in-depth studies of lithic
technology, requires the implementation of an expanded data recovery program.

In terms of agricultural technology, water-control features such as check dams may indicate
increasing agricultural technological sophistication. However, considerable variability in
architectural features may also be evident in the analysis of survey data. In these cases, further
interpretation would require additional data recovery efforts. In-field analysis of artifacts can be
used to collect data on variability in tool manufacture and use.

Archaeological Cultures, Ethnicity, and Ethnogenesis
Several ethnographically recorded winter villages are known in the Kawich Range and on Pahute
Mesa (Hohmann 2002; Steward 1938). During previous consultations with Native Americans,
Black Mountain has been identified as a special place of pilgrimage (Arnold et al. 1997; Carroll et
al. 2006). For Cactus Flat and Gold Flat, Steward (1938) notes that the month-long rabbit and
antelope drives through those areas were attended by various neighboring Shoshone groups

 20

during fandangos usually held around November, whereas the seed gathering expeditions in
those areas usually occurred around May and June.

The kinds of data useful for the archaeological investigation of ethnicity and ethnic identity that
may be recovered during survey projects include ethnohistoric-age archaeological sites,
traditional properties, a variety of portable artifacts and their spatial distribution and the joint
presence of these items.

Field Methods
Sandia personnel, along with NAFB archaeologists conducted an intensive (100-percent) Section
110 archaeological resources survey between September 16, 2024, and October 24, 2024.
Christina Chavez, Stanley Kerr-Painter and Taressa Nield from Sandia were joined by Sarah Rice,
Lynn Robinson, and Zach Tellis from NTTR to complete the survey. Christina Chavez served as
Principal Investigator, while Stanley Kerr-Painter served as Crew Chief for the project. Taressa
Nield served as a field technician and oversaw data management and map production for the
project. The project required multiple 30 m (50 ft) transects across a block survey area for a total
surveyed space of 267.71 ha (661.50 ac). The block survey area for this inventory utilized a 500
square meter grid system for controls throughout the survey. Archaeological resources and IOs
were mapped with a Trimble Trex-10 Global Position System (GPS) unit with real-time accuracy.
At a minimum, site maps included the locations of all identified features, point-provenience
artifacts, datums, site boundaries, and important or diagnostic artifacts. The location and extent
of significant natural or human-made disturbances such as drainages or two-track roads were also
mapped. Each site is photographed and may include cultural features or tools.

Archaeological sites were defined as physical locations of purposeful human activities or events
that are at least 50 years old. An activity is considered to have been purposeful if it resulted in a
deposit of cultural material beyond the level of one or a few accidentally lost artifacts. Cultural
resources that qualify as sites exhibit at least one of the following:

 • One or more features
 • One formal tool if associated with other cultural materials
 • An occurrence of artifacts (such as pottery sherds, chipped stone, or historic items) that
 contains one of the following: (a) three or more types of artifacts or materials; (b) two
 types of artifacts or materials in a density of at least 10 items per 100 square meters (sq
 m); (c) a single type of artifact or material in a density of at least 25 items per 100 sq m.

Cultural manifestations that were more than 50 years old but did not meet any of the above
criteria were recorded as IOs, which usually consist of a location with fewer than 10 artifacts.

Information collected during site recording was put onto Intermountain Antiquities Computer
System (IMACS) forms, as is appropriate for federal lands according to the Nevada SHPO.
Appropriate photo documentation was taken in the field and is included with the site forms.

 21

Artifacts encountered in the field were subjected to in-field analysis with attributes recorded on
artifact forms. No artifacts were collected during the current archaeological survey. When
diagnostic, unique, or unusual artifacts were encountered, the items were photographed, and
their location was recorded with the GPS unit. If a site contained an artifact density greater than
100 items, a representative sample of the artifacts was analyzed and recorded.

Material type was recorded for all lithic artifacts encountered in the project area. Attributes
recorded for the cores, tools, and ground stone consisted of artifact type; condition (whole or
fragment); measurements of length, width, and thickness (in millimeters [mm]); and the presence
or absence of cortex. Attributes recorded for lithic debitage consisted of technological type,
condition (whole or fragment), and material type.

Core length was determined by measuring along the major flaking axis (top to bottom), width was
measured perpendicular to this axis (side to side), and thickness consisted of the remaining
dimension, from front to back. Length for flaked stone tools was measured from the proximal
margin to the distal margin of each artifact. For ground stone artifacts, length was measured
parallel, and width perpendicular, to the orientation of the grinding surface. Additionally, the
presence of burning was noted for ground stone artifacts. Measurements for projectile points
consisted of maximum length, width, and thickness. Photographs were taken of all projectile
points recorded in the project area. Projectile point styles and diagnostic dates were defined by
the attributes as outlined by Justice (2002).

The artifact-type definitions used to classify lithic artifacts recorded during the in-field analysis
include the following. Cores are nodules that have faceted platforms from which specific kinds of
flakes are removed. Tested materials are nodules with a single flake removed from an unprepared
cortical platform at one or more isolated locations. A cobble uniface has two or more flakes
unifacially removed along a single edge margin, usually at one end of the pebble or cobble. Cobble
unifaces probably are unprepared cobble cores. A cobble biface has two or more flakes bifacially
removed from a single edge at the end of a pebble or cobble. Cobble bifaces presumably are
formal heavy-duty chopping tools (i.e., choppers) but might also have been used as sources for
flakes. Cobble bifaces differ from bifacial cores in that bifacial cores are generally made of siliceous
materials and have more than one continuous bifacially retouched edge perimeter.

Hammerstones are defined as nodules that exhibit battering on otherwise unmodified cortical
portions of the surface. This battering is usually located on the end or along the perimeter of the
pebble or cobble. Debitage consists of the by-products of core reduction and tool production.
Flakes are pieces of material that have been detached from a core or tool by percussion or
pressure, as opposed to angular debris, which are pieces that are incidentally broken off during
core reduction. These pieces of shatter lack definable flake characteristics, such as a platform,
bulb of percussion, ventral/dorsal surface, and proximal/distal ends. A core flake is a flake that
has been detached from a core. A polythetic set (Clarke 1968:36–37) of attributes for core flakes
consists of a single or dihedral platform; a platform that is approximately as wide as the flake; a
platform angle of greater than 75°; cortex present on the dorsal surface; dorsal scars that may be

 22

absent, parallel, or perpendicular to the platform; a thickness greater than about 5 mm; and a
pronounced bulb of percussion. To be classified as a core flake, the flake must exhibit at least six
of the eight defining attributes. Biface flakes are flakes that have been detached from a bifacially
retouched artifact. A polythetic set of attributes for biface flakes consists of a multifaceted
platform, an isolated platform, a lipped platform, platform angle less than 75 degrees, a weak
bulb of percussion, cortex absent on the dorsal surface, dorsal scars that are roughly parallel to
each other and perpendicular to the platform, a thickness of less than 5 mm that is relatively even
from proximal to distal ends, and a pronounced ventral curvature.

A flake must exhibit at least six of the nine attributes to be classified as a biface flake.
Undetermined flake fragments are fragments for which flake type cannot be determined. A
retouched tool is the result of the secondary percussion or pressure flaking of a piece to produce
a specific tool shape. A marginally retouched piece is a piece of debitage with retouch that extends
over less than one-third of the surface of the artifact (Chapman and Schutt 1977:86). This
noninvasive retouch is limited to the edge margin but may be unidirectional or bidirectional. A
uniface is an artifact that exhibits retouch scars over one-third or more of only one surface. This
type of retouch can be defined as invasive. A uniface exhibits initial edge retouch that lacks a
formal overall shape. In contrast, a scraper is a specialized form of uniface that exhibits secondary
edge retouch producing a formal shaped tool with an edge angle between 60° and 80°. A biface
is an artifact that exhibits retouch scars extending over one-third or more of both surfaces
(Chapman and Schutt 1977:93). Most generalized bifaces are ovate or lanceolate in shape, with
edge angles of approximately 30–50°. A projectile point is a specialized form of biface that exhibits
hafting modifications that distinguish the stem from the blade.

Ground stone tools are artifacts that exhibit ground and/or abraded surfaces. A mano is a cobble
or slab with at least one surface characterized by one or more smooth facets produced through
grinding. Manos are handheld implements that were primarily used to crush and grind vegetal
foodstuffs against metates (Chapman and Schutt 1977:95; Christenson 1987:44). A polished
surface on a mano may indicate a function other than vegetal processing (e.g., hide processing
[Adams 1988]). A one-handed mano is less than 170 mm in length; a two-handed mano has a
length equal to or greater than 170 mm. An undetermined mano is a fragment the projected
length of which cannot be determined.

Following field investigations, all GPS data were downloaded and differentially corrected to
ensure submeter accuracy. Project area maps and site maps were produced using shapefiles
created from the downloaded data and existing background layers. In addition, digital
photographs were downloaded. However, due to security measures and restrictions
implemented at TTR by both NTTR and NNSA/DOE, some cultural resource overviews do not have
photographs.

CULTURAL RESOURCE MANAGEMENT

Sandia personnel, along with NTTR archaeologists conducted an intensive (100-percent)
pedestrian cultural resources survey between September 16, 2024, and October 24, 2024.
Christina Chavez, Stanley Kerr-Painter and Taressa Nield from Sandia were joined by Sarah Rice,
Lynn Robinson, and Zach Tellis from NTTR to complete the survey. Christina Chavez served as
Principal Investigator, while Stanley Kerr-Painter served as Crew Chief for the project. Taressa
Nield served as a field technician and oversaw data management and map production for the
project.

Sandia documented two previously recorded sites, 15 newly discovered sites, and 18 isolated IOs.
The following sites are recommended as eligible for nomination to the NRHP. Sites 26NY19405,
26NY19406, and 26NY19410 are prehistoric lithic scatters of unknown aboriginal temporal and

 60

cultural affiliations. Site 26NY19413 is a prehistoric lithic scatter with thermal features, and
26NY19414 is a prehistoric lithic scatter. Both sites are of an Aboriginal: Middle Archaic (6,000 BP
– 3,000 BP) period, based on the presence of diagnostic projectile points. Avoidance of the above
resources is recommended. These sites are in an area where there are currently no planned
undertakings or activities, and no impacts are anticipated. If any future activities are undertaken
in the area, avoidance or other mitigative plans would be discussed and addressed prior to the
activity.

The following sites are recommended as not eligible for nomination to the NRHP. Site 26NY1553
is a multicomponent site that consists of a low-density lithic scatter, a fire-cracked rock scatter,
and a historic berm. Site 26NY19404 is a prehistoric artifact scatter of an unknown aboriginal
cultural and temporal affiliation. Sites 26NY19401, 26NY19402, 26NY19407, 26NY19408,
26NY19409, 26NY19411, 26NY19412, and 26NY19415 are all ephemeral, two-track roads, with no
engineer controls that encompass early historic use (ca AD 1950s) up to present day. Site
26NY19403 is a historic site consisting of one feature with an unknown function. These sites are
in an area where there are currently no planned undertakings or activities, and no impacts are
anticipated. However, since these are recommended as not eligible, no further treatment would
be recommended for future undertakings. The 18 IOs do not meet the criteria for listing to the
NRHP and no further treatment is recommended for these resources.

Site 26NY11151 is a previously recorded lithic scatter with various thermal features dating to an
Aboriginal: Early Archaic (9,000 BP – 6,000 BP) period. The site was previously recommended as
not eligible during the original recording; however, the site is recommended as undetermined for
nomination to the NRHP. The site is located within ~ 300 meters of past testing activities;
therefore, at risk of being inadvertently impacted by future testing activities. Testing to determine
the deposition and integrity of the site is recommended. If testing is not feasible, avoidance or
monitoring of any future activities in the area is recommended. These are summarized in Table
