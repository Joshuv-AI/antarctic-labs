Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS) produce clean energy by circulating fluid through hot rock
deep underground and bringing that heat to the surface to generate electricity. For this process to
work reliably, fluids must be able to move efficiently through networks of natural or engineered
fractures in the rock. Enhancing and maintaining subsurface permeability over time is essential for
sustainable energy production. However, fluid injection changes the underground temperature,
pressure, rock stress, and chemistry, which can alter permeability and sometimes trigger
earthquakes. Predicting these interconnected processes remains a key challenge.

To address this, we combined high-temperature laboratory experiments with high-fidelity
simulations to better understand how fractures in geothermal reservoirs evolve over time. Our
experiments measured how fractures respond to stress, slip, slip rate, and chemical reactions under
geothermal conditions. These data were integrated into coupled thermal-hydrological-mechanical-
chemical and earthquake (THMC+E) models tailored to the Utah FORGE site. The validated
modeling framework improves predictions of reservoir performance and seismic response and
helps guide operational decisions. This work reduces technical risk and strengthens the scientific
foundation needed to make geothermal energy a reliable and scalable clean energy resource.

High-level accomplishments at the conclusion of the project are provided below:
• Performed a comprehensive set of high-temperature laboratory experiments on FORGE cores
 (double-direct shear, triaxial shear, and core-flood tests) to quantify frictional properties,
 permeability evolution, and geochemical effects under coupled geothermal conditions.
 Demonstrated that fracture frictional stability and permeability can be significantly altered with
 stress, temperature, and fluid conditions.
• Developed a probabilistic (Bayesian/MCMC) framework and applied it to integrate laboratory
 and field data and constrain key parameters including frictional properties and in situ stress.
• Established and validated a THMC+E workflow that integrates GEOS and RSQSim for a
 coupled numerical study of fluid flow, heat transport, geochemistry reaction, geomechanics,
 and seismic slip. Applied the modeling framework to FORGE operations including hydraulic
 stimulation and circulation to identify multiphysics coupling (stress change, permeability
 evolution, seismic response) as key controls on reservoir performance and induced seismicity.

Keywords: Enhanced geothermal system; hydraulic stimulation; circulation; fracture permeability; rate-
and state-dependent friction; reactive transport; THMC coupled modeling

1. Executive Summary
This report documents and summarizes the research including laboratory experiments, uncertainty
quantification, and high-fidelity numerical modeling performed to investigate the evolution of
fracture friction and permeability under coupled thermo-hydro-mechanical and chemical
conditions and to evaluate their potential impacts on mechanical and seismic behavior of the Utah
FORGE reservoir when subject to certain injection activities.

1.1 Background
Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS) are created by injecting fluid into hot, low-permeability
rock to stimulate fractures and then circulating fluid to extract heat for energy production. These
injection activities increase pore pressure, cool the rock, alter in situ stresses, and trigger fluid–
rock chemical reactions. Together, these processes can change fracture permeability and, in some
cases, induce earthquake events. Because fluid flow, heat transfer, mechanical deformation,
geochemical reactions, and seismic slip are mutually coupled, effective EGS development requires
an integrated approach to understand their interactions.

The Utah FORGE project near Milford, Utah, is a U.S. Department of Energy initiative focused
on advancing enhanced geothermal system (EGS) technology. Following an initial stimulation
campaign (Stages 1–3) conducted in April 2022, a second campaign consisting of eight stimulation
stages (Stages 3R–10) was carried out in April 2024 in injection well 16(A)78-32. Subsequently,
short-term and extended circulation tests were performed to examine the conductivity and
connectivity between injection and production wells. Induced seismicity during these operations
was monitored using a combination of surface geophone patches (Niemz et al., 2025) and
downhole geophones deployed in wells 58-32, 56-32, and 78B-32 (Dyer et al., 2023). In addition,
fiber optic cables installed in wells 16(B)78-32, 78-32, and 78B-32 were used to complement
microseismic data and detect fracture hits.

1.2 Objectives
The primary objective is to develop, apply and validate a thermal, hydrologic, mechanical, and
chemical (THMC) workflow that also includes evaluation of induced seismic slip in EGS
reservoirs. Both experimental and modelling approaches are integrated to reduce parameter
uncertainty and better predict and mitigate seismic hazard at Utah FORGE and future EGS sites.
For this purpose, three interrelated technical tasks are defined for this project.

Laboratory measurements of frictional strength, permeability evolution, and geochemical effects
under representative stress and temperature conditions are conducted and used to constrain
probabilistic model inputs. These data inform reservoir-scale simulations that explicitly represent
discrete fracture networks and coupled THMC processes, enabling prediction of pressure and
temperature evolution, stress changes, fracture aperture and permeability evolution, and overall

system performance during stimulation and circulation. Time-dependent stress and pressure
outputs from these models are then used to simulate induced seismic slip, providing a physics-
based framework to assess seismic hazard and support optimized, risk-informed operational
strategies at Utah FORGE and future EGS sites.

1.3 Key findings
Key findings are summarized below for each technical task and for the overall project:
• Double-direct shear experiments show that fractures follow rate- and state-dependent frictional
 behavior that evolves with stress, temperature, and fluid conditions, with modest temperature-
 dependent strengthening, significantly enhanced frictional healing at elevated temperature, and
 variability in (𝐴−𝐵) indicating transitions between near-neutral, velocity-weakening, and
 velocity-strengthening regimes, implying evolving fault stability.
• Triaxial direct-shear experiments under representative FORGE reservoir conditions imply that
 highly conductive natural fractures may exist in FORGE reservoir and also demonstrate that
 shear deformation can increase fracture permeability by several orders of magnitude for most
 cases, with one exception though that exhibits reduced permeability due to continued slip.
 Elevated-temperature shear produces relatively smooth, low-dilation fractures, which may
 limit the long-term effectiveness of shear stimulation.
• Core-flood experiments under high temperature analogous to EGS conditions indicate that
 fracture permeability evolution is highly variable and influenced by initial permeability,
 fracture type, and fluid–rock interactions, with irregular natural fractures likely promoting
 enhanced reaction due to increased surface complexity compared with the sawcut smooth
 fracture. Geochemical observations indicate increased dissolved silica with decreasing
 permeability and evolving fluid chemistry, but permeability changes cannot be uniquely
 attributed to dissolution or precipitation alone, highlighting the need for reactive transport
 modeling to interpret coupled processes.
• Probabilistic analysis using Bayesian MCMC methods integrates laboratory measurements and
 field observations to constrain key parameters such as rate- and state- friction properties and
 in situ stress. The results show significant uncertainty in parameters (e.g., (𝐴−𝐵)), with values
 often near neutral stability, highlighting the difficulty of uniquely determining fault behavior
 from available data. The in-situ stress analysis further indicates a predominantly strike-slip
 stress regime with spatial variability and possible stress rotation with depth, providing
 improved constraints for subsequent reservoir and seismic modeling.
• Reactive transport modeling of core-flood experiments shows that mineral reactions can
 strongly alter fracture conductivity, with precipitation dominated by phlogopite, causing rapid
 porosity and permeability reduction and corresponding flow restriction near fracture inlets.
 The simulations reproduce the major trends in injection pressure and flow-rate evolution
 observed in the experiments, indicating that geochemical effects can significantly degrade
 fracture conductivity over time.
• The coupled reservoir–seismic modeling workflow demonstrates a unified framework for
 linking injection-driven THMC processes to earthquake response in EGS systems. By
 integrating laboratory-constrained parameters with reservoir-scale simulations and earthquake
 modeling, the approach enables prediction of pressure and temperature evolution, permeability
 changes, geochemistry reactions, mechanical response, and induced seismicity. This workflow
 has been applied to study FORGE hydraulic stimulation and circulation activities and

 6

 highlights the value of multiphysics coupling for improving forecasting capability and
 supporting risk-informed design and operation of geothermal reservoirs.

Overall, this project demonstrates that reliable prediction of reservoir performance and induced
seismicity associated with injection operations in EGS requires an integrated, multiphysics
framework in which fracture strength and permeability evolution as well as fault stability are
jointly governed by coupled THMC processes. A key insight is that laboratory measurements,
probabilistic analysis, and numerical modeling must be tightly linked: experiments constrain rate-
and state- friction parameters, permeability evolution, and geochemical reaction effects, while
Bayesian approaches quantify their uncertainty and translate them into model-ready inputs. The
study further highlights the importance of essential geologic and field-scale information, including
in situ stress state and orientation, fracture network geometry, initial permeability structure, and
fluid/rock chemistry, for constructing realistic models. These findings provide a pathway for future
coupled multiphysics studies by emphasizing the need for systematic measurement and calibration
of frictional, hydraulic, and reactive transport parameters, along with consistent integration of field
observations, to develop predictive and risk-informed modeling workflows for EGS operations.

2. Introduction
This section provides the introduction to the report, outlining the purpose, scope of work, and the
overall structure.

2.1 Purpose of the report
The primary purpose of the report is to document, integrate, and interpret the laboratory
experimental efforts and numerical simulations that have been performed to investigate how
fracture strength, deformation behavior, and permeability evolve under coupled THMC processes
and how they impact the seismic response for Utah FORGE operations. More specifically, the
report summarizes key findings collectively obtained from (i) double-direct shear, triaxial direct
shear experiments and core flood experiments on FORGE samples as well as integration of
experimental and field data into probabilistic study, and (ii) reactive transport modeling and
coupled THMC+E modeling to characterize the geochemistry and reservoir behavior as well as
induced seismicity for representative FORGE operations. Finally, the report consolidates the
lessons learnt from each task, identifies the limitations of the current study, and proposed potential
future enhancements, with the ultimate goal to inform the design of EGS reservoirs with a
comprehensive experiment and modeling framework to better understand the THMC+E processes.

2.2 Scope of work
The project comprises a high-level project management task (Task 1) followed by three technical
tasks designed to develop an integrated experimental and numerical framework to improve
understanding of fracture behavior, reservoir performance, and corresponding seismic behavior
under coupled THMC processes. The specific technical tasks include:

Task 2 – Perform triaxial direct-shear, double-direct shear experiments, and core-flood experiments
to measure frictional strengths, permeability and their evolutions due to stress and temperature
change and geochemical processes under geothermal conditions. Experimental measurement and
existing Utah FORGE field data are integrated into a stochastic model to further constrain the
THMC+E parameters that are subsequently fed into numerical simulations.

Task 3 – Conduct reactive transport and geomechanics simulations in GEOS that explicitly account
for discrete fracture networks (DFNs). The modeling task aims to predict the thermal-poro-elastic
stress evolution and fracture aperture/permeability, so as to analyze reservoir performance and
quantify the impact of coupled processes and permeability evolution in Utah FORGE operations.

Task 4 – Conduct earthquake simulations with RSQSim driven by the time history of THMC stress
and pressure results. Simulations are performed with a sufficient number of realizations of initial
conditions drawn from property PDFs derived from Task 2 to improve understanding of the
impacts of coupled processes on induced seismicity.

2.3 Structure of the report
The remainder of report is organized as follows:
• Section 3 presents laboratory experiments and probabilistic study including double-direct
 shear experiments for rate- and state-frictional property measurement, triaxial direct shear
 experiments for fracture permeability measurements, and core flood experiments on mineral
 reactions.

• Section 4 provides the numerical results from core-scale reactive transport modeling and
 coupled reservoir geomechanics simulations for representative FORGE injection activities as
 well as the earthquake simulations to study their corresponding seismic response.
• Section 5 consolidates the overall findings from all technical tasks and provides
 recommendations for future research and development in stress characterization and hydraulic
 fracturing modeling for crystalline EGS reservoirs.

3.1.1 Summary of Methodologies
All experiments were performed in a double-direct shear (DDS) configuration using the Penn State
BIAX biaxial deformation apparatus (Figure 3-1). Gouge layers were prepared with a uniform
thickness of 3 mm and a nominal contact area of 7.15 cm2. Normal and shear loads were measured
with custom beryllium–copper load cells with a measurement resolution of ~5 N. Shear and normal
displacements were recorded using direct-current displacement transducers (DCDTs) located both
external and internal to the pressure vessel, with a displacement resolution of ~0.1 micron. Loads
and displacements were recorded continuously at up to 10 kHz and then averaged for storage at 1
Hz to 1 kHz, depending on the imposed shear rate.

Experiments were conducted across three boundary-condition classes: (1) room-humidity
conditions (RH 35% at 25C), (2) water-saturated conditions at nominally zero pore pressure
(“bath” configuration) spanning 25–75 C, and (3) fluid-pressurized conditions with elevated pore
pressure (Pf) and confining pressure, spanning 25–125C. Applied normal stress ranged from 5–
100 MPa. For pressurized experiments, effective normal stress was defined as 𝜎𝑛′ = 𝜎𝑛 − 𝑃𝑓 and
ranged from 5–35 MPa. In the fluid-pressurized series, fluid boundary conditions were adjusted to
increase sensitivity to slip-induced changes in fault-zone hydraulic properties. Results were
interpreted within the framework of RSF (Dieterich, 1979; Ruina, 1983).

Each experiment followed the same general protocol (Figure 3-1):

 11

 1. Compaction: Apply effective normal stress and allow the gouge layer to compact; saturate
 the layer when applicable.
 2. Run-in: Shear at a constant velocity of 10 micron/s for 10 mm of displacement to reach
 steady-state friction and establish baseline strength.
 3. Slide–Hold–Slide (SHS): Halt sliding for incremental hold durations (1–3000 s), then re-
 shear at 10 micron/s with 500 microns of displacement between holds to quantify healing.
 4. Velocity-step tests: Impose step changes in sliding velocity to quantify velocity dependence
 and estimate RSF parameters under each set of boundary conditions.

3.2 Triaxial Direct-Shear Experiments on Fracture Permeability Measurement
Here, we present laboratory triaxial direct-shear experiments to characterize thermal, hydraulic,
mechanical, and chemical (THMC) coupled processes related to the stimulation of and flow of
fluid through shear-induced fractures. Results from this work reveal insights into the potential of
hydraulic shear stimulation to enhance flow and quantify key properties for fractures, including
strength, frictional properties, dilation tendencies, and chemical reactivity. Detailed discussions on
the experiments and results can be referred to

3.2.1 Summary of Methodologies
We prepared cylindrical specimens with 38.1 mm (1.5 inch) diameter and 38.1 mm length from
subsurface cores retrieved from Well 16A at the FORGE site. The key information of the specimens
along with the experimental conditions are summarized in Table 3-1. The specimens were
primarily granite, gneiss, and granodiorite. Samples were prepared to have their fabric oriented
parallel to the axis of the core so that we could measure the weakest orientations with respect to
shear. The injectate fluid was synthesized in the laboratory to mimic the chemistry of the water
sourced from the Milford golf course, which was a previously planned injectate fluid. The fluid is

 14

a 0.5 mM solution consisting of 3.2 mg/L KCl, 47.7 mg/L CaCl2.2H2O, 33.5 mg/L MgCl2.6H2O,
53.2 mg/L Na2SO4, and 0.032 mg/L NaHCO3 added to deionized water.

Table 3-1 Properties of rock specimens from 16A(78)-32 with experimental conditions.

Triaxial-direct shear uses two opposed L-shaped platens to induce shear stress along the axis of a
cylindrical rock sample (Figure 3-6). When sufficient axial load is applied, a fracture is created
through the diameter of the core. The amplitude of en-echelon structures in the fracture (i.e.,
roughness) is a function of the confining stress, and also possibly the temperature, with higher
stresses during failure leading to flatter fracture surfaces. More crucially, this end-to-end fracture
is diametric and, therefore, suited for permeability measurements parallel to shear. We
continuously measure flow and permeability using up to four syringe pumps and up to five pressure
transducers. This allows instantaneous assessment of the permeability and its uncertainty on a
point-by-point basis. Steady-state flow is neither required nor desired, as we seek to understand
the dynamics of flow through actively evolving fractures.

More details of sample preparation and how the experiment mimics the in situ conditions are
described in Frash et al. (2024); KC et al. (2025). The general procedures followed to perform the
experiments are:
 1. Pre-confinement: Set-up the specimen prepared in subsection 2.2 on the triaxial cell base
 and vacuum test to confirm seal of the assembly. Fill the cell with high temperature silicone
 oil, i.e. confining fluid.
 2. Heating: Start heating the sample by activating the heater on the triaxial cell to gradually
 increase the temperature of the confining fluid inside the cell. Confining pressure of 5 MPa
 was maintained during the heating process to prevent the steam pressure from fracturing
 the rock sample when temperature is above 90 °C. Heating rate was maintained at 0.75
 °C/min until the target temperature of 195 °C was reached then the system was allowed to
 equilibrate for 2 hours.
 3. In-situ confinement: The confining pressure was increased to the target value of 36.1 MPa,
 and the pore pressure to 22 MPa.
 4. Intact rock permeability: We measured the intact rock permeability at in-situ conditions by
 injecting golf course water from top of the specimen.
 5. Fracturing and incremental shear: We sheared the sample by applying shear stress on the
 sample. The displacement rate of the axial piston was maintained at 0.002 mm/sec during
 all shearing stages. The fracture was displaced up to 2 mm to characterize the fracture
 permeability at different shear displacement levels.
 6. Confining stress cycle: Confining stress cycling (36.1 MPa – 24 MPa – 36.1 MPa – 48
 MPa – 36.1 MPa) was performed to observe the hysteresis in the permeability due to
 change in normal stress.

 15

 7. Mohr-Coulomb shear: The confining stress acting on the specimen was changed, and the
 fracture was sheared further by 0.3 mm steps to characterize the frictional properties of the
 fracture.
 8. Finally, the system was allowed to cool to ambient temperature before depressurizing and
 ending the experiment.

3.3 Fracture Permeability Measurement under Geochemical Alterations
This section summarizes high-temperature geochemical core-flooding experiments to investigate
fluid-rock interactions and permeability evolution in core samples collected from Utah FORGE
well 16A(78)-32. The experimental results are further used to validate the reactive transport model
in Section 4.1, where a reaction-aware permeability model is derived and calibrated.

3.3.1 Summary of Methodologies
We prepared cylindrical specimens from subsurface cores provided from well 16A at the FORGE
site for both triaxial testing and for core-flood reactive experiments. Nearby sections from the
subsampled cores were destructively sampled to analyze the mineralogic composition of the rocks
via X-ray detection (XRD) and converted to volumetric percentages via semi-quantitative Rietfeld
refinement method. The rocks were generally classed as granites and gneisses, with the darker
gneissic rock having a significantly higher percentage of anorthitic plagioclase and biotite,
compared to the potassium-feldspar and muscovite granitic samples.

For consistency across core-flood experiments, we developed a synthetic dilute brine, based on a
charge-balanced recasting of the “Milford Golf Course” groundwater composition referenced in
the North Milford Valley groundwater chemistry report (Simmons et al., 2019). This fluid was
prepared by dissolving KCl, CaCl2·2H2O, MgCl2·6H2O, Na2SO4, and NaHCO3 inorganic salts into
de-aired water, resulting in ~5 mM solution with solution pH of 8.3 at 20 °C. Liquid samples were
regularly taken and preserved for major ion analysis by ion chromatography, acidified and
preserved for trace element analysis by inductively coupled plasma-mass spectroscopy, and a split
allowed to cool to room temperature for pH measurement. Solution pH at 200 °C was modeled
from these analyses using speciation model EQ3/6 and associated high-temperature database
(Wolery, 1992).

Rock cores used for reactive experiments were generally 38.1 mm (1.5 in) in diameter and a factor
of 2 times longer in the lengthwise direction (parallel to fractures). A series of methods for creating
fractures within the rock samples were employed, ranging from:
 • saw-cutting a single, roughly parallel planar ‘fracture’ though the rock core; no other
 surface finishing was employed;

 18

 • utilizing ‘natural’ fractures created during tri-axial experiments conducted in Task 3.2,
 resulting in non-planar, rough fracture surfaces with much wider variation in aperture and
 some high-temperature reaction (“weathering”) noted at induced fracture surfaces;
 • and fresh ‘natural’ fractures created by overcoring a sample and subjecting it to targeted
 pressure within a rock press to create a single, non-planar fracture surface with generally
 lower aperture variation compared to the triaxially-induced fractured samples.
Fracture surfaces were analyzed for surface elevation using an optical stylus and profilometer
before and after experiments. A 1:1 registration was not desired; rather, the surface elevations or
linear data subsets were analyzed en masse to derive roughness coefficients.

3.3.2 Summary of Key Findings
Key findings from experiments are summarized below:
 • Through collaboration with our LANL colleagues, a fluoropolymer material resistant to
 sustained 200 °C temperature was identified, procured, and found satisfactory for use in
 high temperature/high pressure flow-through experiments representative of subsurface
 FORGE site conditions. Lead times and prices for jacketing materials are not expected to
 decrease so future workers may want to consider alternative methods for isolating samples
 from confining pressure fluids.
 • Initial permeability varied widely across the samples used in these experiments but did not
 trend with the type of fracture (though we note that a sample size of n=3 does not permit
 thorough investigation). However, the magnitude of the initial measured permeability did
 correlate with the trend of permeability change; the experiments with the highest measured
 initial permeability (10955-B, 10974) experienced the least decrease in permeability. The
 correlation of a permeability response with fracture ‘type’ or rock type in such a small
 sample set is unlikely, but this does not mean that these experiments do not have value; on
 the contrary, a dataset of constrained reactive transport experiments has proven extremely
 valuable to the development of reaction chemistry within the flow and transport simulator
 (see Section 4.1).
 • The response of permeability with time and resulting fluid volume was variable despite
 noting similarly elevated concentrations of aqueous components. We hypothesize that the

 20

 irregular fracture surfaces of the ‘natural’ (triaxial and Brazil-fractured) samples may
 provide additional fresh mineral surfaces for reaction, while the sawcut fractures may as a
 result of their relatively planar geometry simply provide more mineral-fluid access.
 Inspection of fracture surfaces post-experiment does not show visible evidence of large-
 scale mineral precipitation, but reacted (‘weathered’) regions are evident. In-depth
 examination of the fracture surfaces was beyond the scope and timeline of this project.
 • Dissolved silica concentrations varied inversely with permeability; as permeability
 decreased and fluid residence times increased, dissolved silica concentrations increased.
 Some other common rock-forming elements followed a similar trend (e.g., Al, Mg), while
 others did not (e.g., K, Fe). Regardless, the highest dissolved silica concentrations noted in
 these experiments resulted in model saturation indices, SI of only -0.8 at the highest (where
 SI is a measure of the magnitude of the thermodynamic driver for a given mineral to
 precipitate when SI > 0 or dissolve when SI < 0).
 • Modeled solution pH at the in situ 200 °C temperature in these experiments was higher
 than the neutral value of ~5.6 at this elevated temperature. This slightly alkaline fluid pH
 is a result of neutralization of hydrogen with multiple mineral phases and supports
 continued low-level release of dissolved silica from fracture-accessible mineral grains.
 • Across the experiments conducted at 200 °C, thermodynamic modeling of solution
 chemistry samples indicated that effluent brine becomes most saturated with respect to
 sheet silicates like biotite and chlorite and sorosilicates like epidote, with SI values for
 epidote noted ranging from 8-15 (notable as a unit increase in SI represents a logarithmic
 increase in saturation). Despite this fluid saturation, no precipitates were noted on fracture
 surfaces post-experiment.
 • It is not possible to ascribe permeability response to either mineral dissolution or
 precipitation processes from solution chemistry data alone. Reactive transport modeling
 such as that performed and discussed in Section 4.1 is necessary to evaluate the relative
 magnitude of each reaction.

3.4 Integration of Experimental Measurements and Probabilistic Study
The objective of this task is to integrate a very wide variety of characterization data from the Utah
FORGE site into a probabilistic model of the state of stress and RSF parameters to be used in other
modeling Tasks 3 and 4. In this case, “probabilistic” should be understood in the Bayesian sense—
that is reflecting a degree of confidence or belief in a particular value. The core method used to
perform this integration is the Metropolis-Hastings Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method.

The posterior distribution is computed via Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) sampling.
MCMC sampling uses a Markov chain, which is a sequence of random variables in which the
probability of each variable depends only on the state of the previous variable. The sampling
workflow is, generally:
1. Start with an initial set of parameter values drawn from the prior distributions in Equation 3-6.
2. Generate a proposal distribution for each parameter based on how well the previous values fit the

3. Accept or reject the proposed values based on an acceptance criterion (e.g. Metropolis-Hastings

 values. If the proposed values are rejected, the values remain unchanged.
4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 to generate a Markov chain.

target parameter distribution. For each dataset, we first applied a series of smoothing,
filtering, and sectioning operations to reduce noise without compromising data integrity.

3.4.2 MCMC Analysis Method and Key Findings for the State of Stress

 24

This section describes the application of the MCMC approach to the state of stress at Utah FORGE.
The method employed here is a generalization of the method implemented in the SOSAT Python
library (https://github.com/pnnl/SOSAT) and web-based tool (https://sosat.pnnl.gov/) originally
developed for CO2 storage applications. SOSAT is essentially a Bayesian expression of standard
and well-validated borehole-based stress characterization methods.

An analytical solution for the joint probability distribution of 𝜎𝐻 and 𝜎ℎ using the stress polygon
approach was derived by Burghardt (2018). That solution assumed a uniform or lognormal
distribution of 𝜇 and a deterministic value for 𝜎v and 𝑃𝑝 . Analytical solutions were also presented
for cases where a probability density for the minimum principal stress is available from hydraulic
fracturing based measurements, and regional information about the faulting regime. As part of the
development of SOSAT, likelihood functions were also developed for the presence or absence of
drilling-induced tensile fractures (DITF) and borehole breakouts (BO). These likelihood functions,
however, result in non-analytic posterior distributions and so require a numerical sampling
procedure.

The SOSAT Python library uses these semi-analytical methods to constrain the state of stress with
the limitation that the principal stresses must be vertical/horizontal and the borehole where DITF
or BO observations are made is vertical. This latter limitation made the tool inappropriate to use
as-is for the Utah FORGE data set because boreholes 16A(78)-32 and 16B(78)-32 are highly
deviated over the regions of interest. To overcome this limitation, we implemented the analytical
solution (Zoback, 2007), which allows for arbitrary orientations of boreholes and principal
stresses. This solution has considerably more parameters and is much more complex than the
vertical borehole Kirsch solution used in SOSAT. Because of this, we opted to implement the
analysis routines in the Julia programming language, which is computationally much faster than
Python. The additional complexity also makes the semi-analytical approach used by SOSAT
intractable, so we replaced it with a MCMC methodology that uses the Peska and Zoback analytical
borehole stress solution to compute the likelihood of a given stress state and parameter set.
Following constraints were applied in this MCMC analysis of the Utah FORGE boreholes 56-32,
58-32, 78B-32, 16A(78)-32, and 16B(78)-32: (i) Frictional stability constraint that requires three
principal stresses resulting in a state of static equilibrium with frictional faults and fractures that
are presumed to be pervasive in any significant volume of the earth’s crust; (ii) Faulting regime
prior constraint imposed on the state of stress that assigns equal likelihood to normal faulting and
strike-slip stress regimes, and zero likelihood to thrust faulting regimes; (iii) SHmax azimuth prior
constraint assigned to the direction of the maximum horizontal stress using a von Mises angular
distribution with a mean of 20° NNE and a standard deviation of 20°, based on the regional
observations of stress directions from borehole and seismic observations; (iv) Borehole breakout
stress constraint that identifies the presence or absence of borehole breakouts to place constraints
on the relative magnitudes of the principal stresses (Zoback, 2007); (v) Drilling-induced tensile
fracture constraint that identifies the presence or absence of DITF to inform the state of stress,
similar to borehole breakouts.

4.1.3 Summary of Results for Core 5477 ft
This experiment was conducted over five days. Injection was performed at a constant flow rate
during the first day, then switched to constant pressure for the remainder of the test. The
simulations followed the same protocol, using the measured rate and pressure histories to define
the boundary conditions. In addition, prior to injection, the system was initialized by equilibrating
the brine with the core mineral for one day to better represent the starting geochemical conditions.

As shown in Section 3.3, experimental measurements of injection rate and pressure drop across
the core indicated strong precipitation. During the first day, the pressure differential increased
rapidly to above 8 MPa from an initial value of approximately 0.3 MPa, which motivated the
transition to constant-pressure injection to avoid damaging the experimental system. The
simulations likewise predicted strong precipitation, dominated by phlogopite. Phlogopite
precipitation developed as a reaction front, with an initial precipitation spike at the fracture inlet
that migrated deeper into the crack as local reactive surface area decreased. Anorthite also showed
localized precipitation at the same location, but at rates orders of magnitude lower than phlogopite.
Downstream of the precipitation front, anorthite and other minerals exhibited slight dissolution.
Quartz showed the strongest dissolution, followed by anorthite, K-feldspar, and kaolinite. Overall,
phlogopite precipitation overwhelmingly controlled the dynamics, producing rapid porosity and
permeability reductions within approximately the first 0.75 mm from the crack inlet.

To reproduce the experimentally observed magnitude and timing of the first-day pressure increase
and the subsequent flow-rate decline under constant pressure, free parameters in the surface-area
formulation (Equation 4-4) and permeability model (Equation 4-10) were calibrated. Figure 4-3
compares simulated and experimental data. The experiment exhibited multiple sharp pressure
spikes during the first day. After switching to constant pressure, the injection rate dropped quickly
below the initial value of 0.4 mL/min and continued to decline. The simulations did not reproduce
the short pressure spikes but did capture the overall rapid pressure rise and the peak value at the
time of switching to constant-pressure injection. The modeled rate decline under constant pressure
followed the same qualitative trend as the experiment, with an immediate drop and continued
reduction thereafter, but the magnitude of the decline was underpredicted. Achieving closer
agreement was challenging because parameter changes that strengthened the rate drop, such as
reducing residual permeability or increasing the exponent, also tended to amplify the first-day
pressure response beyond what was observed. The final calibration, therefore, reflects a
compromise that captures the dominant trends in both injection phases.

4.1.4 Summary of Results for Core 10955 ft
The experiment was conducted over seven days with injection applied as a sequence of constant
flow-rate intervals throughout the test. The simulations used the same injection-rate schedule. Prior
to injection, the chemical system was initialized by equilibrating the brine with the core mineral

experiment, although the slope of the subsequent pressure increase is captured well. During the

For both tests, the exponent controlling reactive surface-area reduction due to precipitation was
set to 8. This choice mitigates the tendency for precipitation to localize at the inlet. Without
sufficiently rapid surface-area decay, reactive species are consumed within the first few grid cells,
producing an unrealistically sharp permeability collapse, abrupt pressure increase, and severe flow
restriction. Enforcing a strong reduction in reactive surface area as porosity approaches the residual
value shifts the reactive front deeper into the fracture, distributes permeability loss over a broader
region, and yields smoother pressure-rise and rate-decline behavior.

As shown in Figure 4-3, the measured injection rate did not fall to zero even after five days,
indicating a small but persistent residual flow through the core. Numerical sensitivity tests
indicated that a residual permeability of 1 × 10−19 m2 is low enough to strongly reduce flow while
still allowing nonzero throughput. The permeability exponent controls how quickly permeability
decreases with porosity loss and therefore directly affects the modeled timing and magnitude of
the pressure increase and rate decline. Notably, the fitted permeability exponents differ
substantially between the two experiments, with the first test requiring a less steep permeability
decline than the second. This is consistent with the much shorter reactive zone in the second test
(approximately 0.02 mm) compared with the first (approximately 0.75 mm). Because the pressure
drop reflects an effective integration of permeability along the fracture length, a more localized
reactive region requires a steeper permeability reduction to reproduce a sharp pressure response.

4.2 Coupled Reservoir Geomechanics and Seismic (THMC+E) Simulations
4.2.1 Summary of Coupled THMC+E Workflow
The modeling workflow integrates a coupled reservoir simulation with an earthquake simulation,
coupled in a one-way manner through following three steps:
1. Reservoir simulation: The workflow begins with the reservoir simulation in GEOS to model
 FORGE injection activities. To account for the effects of geochemical processes, we
 incorporate an upscaled, reduced-order permeability model derived from core-scale reactive
 transport modeling study into the reservoir model.
2. Processing reservoir model results: Upon completion of the reservoir simulation, we extract
 the time histories of pressure and effective stress (include shear and effective normal stresses
 which capture the full poroelastic response) and project the stresses onto the fault planes
 defined for the subsequent earthquake simulation.
3. Earthquake simulation: The stress and pressure data are then transferred to an earthquake
 simulator based on quasi-dynamic boundary element method, RSQSim (Richards-Dinger &
 Dieterich, 2012), for conducting simulations of earthquake activity on pre-defined fault planes.
We note that a similar one-way coupled workflow has been employed successfully for other EGS
projects (Kroll et al., 2021).

4.2.2 Summary of Results for Utah FORGE Stimulation Activities
We first present a numerical study of Utah FORGE stimulation activities, more specifically, Stages
3–6 on Well 16A. Since the stimulation period is relatively short, we ignore the chemical effect for
simplicity in the above modeling framework. As a result, the workflow is reduced to a THM+E
version. This numerical investigation aims to validate two hypotheses proposed below:
• Hypothesis 1 The new stimulation stages 3R-6 were reopening and extending a preexisting
 fracture zone instead of creating new fractures.
• Hypothesis 2 The re-stimulated preexisting fracture zone is possibly the one that was originally
 generated during Stage 3 stimulation in 2022.
Details of how these two hypotheses are proposed, numerical model setups, and material
parameters adopted can be referred to Fei et al. (2026). Note that the modeling parameters used in
both the reservoir and the earthquake simulator are based on existing Utah FORGE references and
laboratory measurements in this project.

The THM reservoir simulation focuses on history matching the field measurement of injection
pressure for all stimulation stages from 3 to 5. Note that Stage 6 is excluded from this study due
to unexpectedly high injection pressure obtained in the field. Figure 4-7 compares the modeled
injection pressure with the field data for Stages 3–5. As can be seen, all cases show good agreement
between the simulation results and the observed bottom-hole pressure evolution, including both

Figure 4-17 to Figure 4-19 display the comparison in wellhead pressure evolutions between field

The producer pressure curves reasonably match those observed in the field as well for the HM and

obtained by either HM or THM model. It is because both HM and THM models consider the

 48

poroelastic effect to account for the coupling between pore pressure and rock effective stress. The
fluid inflow will lead to local volume expansion near the injection region, thereby generating
additional compression of the surrounding region, leading to potential pressure increase in the far-
field even though the injected fluid has yet to arrive. This far-field pressure increase will also
enhance permeability, consequently reducing the pressure response at the producer. An opposite
mechanism can be used to explain the slight difference between the HM and THM results, where
the THM model shows a bit larger wellhead pressure at the producer. This is due to the circulation
which allows fluid to cool the reservoir and cause volume contraction in the local injection zone,
thus alleviating the poroelastic effect. As a result, the wellhead pressure becomes slightly higher
in the THM case, because of less permeability enhancement due to reduced poroelastic response
in the far-field region. Considering these competing mechanisms, this numerical study suggests
that it is vital to use appropriate multiphysics coupling to reliably predict the EGS reservoir
response.

5. Conclusions and Recommendations
5.1 Key Findings and Summary of Conclusions
This project constructs a comprehensive experimental, probabilistic, and modeling framework to
investigate fracture permeability evolution and fault stability under coupled thermal, hydrological,
mechanical, chemical (THMC), and earthquake (E) processes in EGS. The integrated approach
demonstrates that these processes are inherently interdependent and jointly control reservoir
performance and induced seismicity.

Laboratory experiments demonstrate that fractures of FORGE core samples exhibit RSF stability
evolving as a function of stress, temperature, and fluid conditions. In particular, elevated
temperature promotes stronger frictional healing and modest increases in steady-state friction,
which tends to lead to more stick-slip events. In parallel, triaxial direct-shear and core-flood
experiments demonstrate that fracture permeability is highly dynamic, with shear stimulation
capable of enhancing permeability by several orders of magnitude, while geochemical reactions
may degrade fracture conductivity due to mineralization in core fractures, with the extent
controlled by the fracture type (natural v.s. sawcut) and fluid–rock interactions. Reactive transport
modeling demonstrates that geochemical effects play a critical role in fracture evolution, as mineral
precipitation can significantly reduce permeability and restrict flow. These findings indicate that
permeability evolution cannot be treated as a solely mechanical process, but should incorporate
coupled chemical effects and their feedback on fluid flow. Additionally, probabilistic analysis
using Bayesian MCMC methods highlights substantial uncertainty in rate- and state- friction
properties, while also constraining the stress regime to be predominantly strike-slip with spatial
variability and possible stress rotation along depth.

A major outcome of this work is the development and validation of a coupled THMC+E modeling
workflow that integrates laboratory-constrained parameters, reservoir-scale simulations, and
earthquake modeling. This workflow demonstrates the capability to link injection-driven processes
to seismic response and to capture the key controls on reservoir performance, including pressure
evolution, permeability changes, stress variations, and induced seismicity. The results consistently
indicate that multiphysics coupling and earthquake simulations rooted in RSF (as opposed to
simpler Coulomb friction) are essential for accurately predicting reservoir behavior and induced
seismicity. The successful application of this workflow to Utah FORGE stimulation and circulation
tests highlight the importance of well-constrained input data, including RSF parameters,
permeability evolution laws, and geochemical reaction kinetics derived from laboratory
experiments, as well as essential geologic information such as in situ stress state and fracture
network geometry, including slip kinematics. In addition, the workflow relies on field-scale
observations, including injection pressure and rate records, fiber data (e.g., DTS, DSS, DAS),
and microseismic data, to calibrate, validate, and interpret model predictions, enabling more
reliable and predictive multiphysics simulations for EGS applications.

Overall, this project concludes that reliable prediction of EGS reservoir performance and seismic
hazard requires an integrated, physics-based framework that combines experimental
measurements, probabilistic parameter estimation, and coupled numerical modeling. The insights
gained from this work provide a foundation for developing predictive workflows that produce risk-
informed guides lines for the design and operation of future geothermal systems.

5.2 Implications of Findings for Utah FORGE and EGS Technologies
The experimental results from this study provide a comprehensive basis for understanding the
behavior of crystalline granite reservoirs under geothermal conditions, with direct relevance to
future EGS development. The measured rate- and state-dependent frictional properties
demonstrate that fault strength and stability in granite evolve with stress, temperature, and fluid
conditions. In particular, the observed temperature dependence of frictional healing and stability
parameters highlights the importance of accounting for geothermal conditions when assessing fault
reactivation and seismic hazard. Permeability measurements from triaxial direct-shear and core-
flood experiments further show that fracture conductivity in granite is highly dynamic and
controlled by coupled mechanical and chemical processes. While shear stimulation can
significantly enhance permeability, the experiments also demonstrate that permeability evolution
depends on fracture morphology, dilation behavior, and geochemical reactions, which may either
enhance or degrade conductivity over time. These observations provide critical insight into the
mechanisms governing fracture development and fluid transport in crystalline reservoirs.

More broadly, the experimental dataset generated in this project establishes a set of quantitative
relationships and parameter constraints, including frictional properties, permeability evolution
trends, and geochemical reaction kinetics, that can be directly incorporated into reservoir and
seismic models. These data enable more realistic representation of granite behavior in EGS
simulations and reduce reliance on poorly constrained assumptions. As such, the results contribute
to improving the predictive capability of models used to design and operate geothermal systems.

The development of the coupled THMC+E workflow provides a systematic framework for
integrating experimental measurements, geologic characterization, and numerical modeling to
predict reservoir behavior and seismic response. This workflow highlights the critical data
requirements needed to constrain predictive models for future EGS development, including
frictional parameters, permeability evolution relationships, and reaction kinetics, as well as field-
scale information such as in-situ stress magnitude and orientation, fracture network geometry, and
initial reservoir conditions (e.g., pressure, temperature, chemical composition). Furthermore, the
workflow emphasizes the importance of comprehensive field monitoring data, including wellhead
pressure and temperature record, pumping rate, fiber data, and microseismic observations, for
model calibration and validation. Collectively, these findings provide guidance for future EGS
development by identifying the key physical processes and data requirements necessary to support
predictive, multiphysics modeling and risk-informed reservoir management.

5.3 Recommendations for Future Work
The experimental and modeling efforts in this project have provided fundamental insights
into fracture strength, fault stability, and permeability evolution, as well as their impacts on
reservoir performance and associated seismic response in EGS systems. Building on this
foundation, future work should focus on advancing both experimental characterization and
integrated multiphysics modeling to further improve predictive capability.

Continued laboratory investigations may be necessary to better characterize injection-induced slip
under RSF framework and how key parameters and frictional healing vary over a range of loading
paths (e.g., cyclic injection). As there were ample microseismic events observed during the
stimulation and circulation phase and because experimental results indicate RSF (b-a)> 0, this

 56

project did not evaluate the propensity for aseismic slip. Additionally, more experiments are
needed to further constrain the impacts of proppant breakage and associated geochemical reactions
as well as their interactions on fracture permeability under geothermal conditions.

The probabilistic analysis indicates potential rotation of the in-situ stress state with depth. This
conclusion, however, is primarily based on image log interpretations of borehole breakouts and
DITFs. Recent analyses of distributed fiber optic data from the 16A stimulation (Han et al., 2026)
also imply similar stress rotation behavior, providing independent support for this observation.
Future work should focus on integrating multiple data sources, including image logs, fiber
measurements (DAS/DSS), and microseismic observations, within a unified framework to better
constrain stress orientation and its spatial variability.

On the modeling side, reactive transport modeling should be extended from sawcut fractures
to rough, naturally formed fractures as experimental data interpretation becomes available. Further
development of permeability models that couple mechanical deformation and chemical alteration
as well as proppant breakage are needed to better capture the full range of fracture behavior in
reality. The coupled THMC+E workflow should be extended to incorporate more comprehensive
and flexible coupling strategies, including potential two-way coupling between reservoir processes
and seismic response, as well as improved representation of fracture network complexity instead
of just considering upscaled permeability heterogeneity.

A key recommendation is the continued integration of field-scale data for model calibration and
validation. Future studies should leverage expanded datasets from ongoing Utah FORGE
operations, including injection and production data, distributed fiber optic measurements (DTS,
DAS, DSS), and high-resolution microseismic catalogs, to refine model parameters and reduce
uncertainty. Systematic workflows for data assimilation and model updating will be critical for
improving predictive performance.
