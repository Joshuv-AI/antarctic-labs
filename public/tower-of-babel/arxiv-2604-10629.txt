Searching for Gamma Ray Bursts associated with CHIME Fast Radio bursts Astropy ( 5 ; 3 ; 4 ) , h5py ( 22 ; 23 ) , skymap [ https://github.com/kadrlica/skymap ], healpy ( 60 ; 61 )
Yifang Liang, Ye Li †, Bao Wang, Xuan Yang, Yuan-Pei Yang, Xuefeng Wu

Abstract
Fast radio bursts (FRBs) and gamma-ray bursts (GRBs) are both linked to compact-object activity, yet their possible connection remains unclear. Here we perform a systematic search for spatial and temporal associations between FRBs in the second CHIME/FRB catalog and Swift GRBs. Instead of using the positional ellipses reported in the catalog, the full CHIME localization probability maps are adopted for spatial cross-matching. This yields 130 candidate pairs and increases the number of spatially consistent matches by a factor of several. A redshift consistency requirement reduces the sample to 45 pairs. Applying an additional temporal criterion, requiring long GRBs to precede FRBs and short GRBs to follow them, further reduces the sample to 26 candidates. Monte Carlo simulations show that the overall excess of associations is not statistically significant, and the distribution of matches across localization confidence levels is consistent with random expectations. However, potential associations may be diluted by localization uncertainties and a dominant background of chance coincidences. These results place constraints on any FRB–GRB connection and highlight the need for improved localization and larger samples.

I
Introduction

Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs) are luminous (
\sim 10^{44}
erg s
-1
), millisecond-duration radio transients of extragalactic origin
(
33
;
48
)
. Despite the rapidly growing sample—with more than 5000 events reported to date—their physical origin remains an open question. The all-sky event rate of FRBs, estimated to be
\sim 10^{3}
day
-1

(
19

, implies a volumetric occurrence comparable to or exceeding that of several classes of stellar end-of-life explosions. This suggests that most FRB sources are capable of repeating activity, even though only a small fraction have been observed to do so
(
44
;
18

7

.
Recent observational advances, particularly from the Canadian Hydrogen Intensity Mapping Experiment (CHIME)
(
15

, have significantly improved the localization capabilities for FRBs. In addition to arcsecond-level localizations for a subset of sources, CHIME provides pixelized probability localization maps that encode the full, and often highly non-Gaussian, positional uncertainty of each burst
(
20
;
14
)
. These developments enable more sophisticated counterpart searches beyond traditional methods based on simplified positional uncertainties.

A growing body of evidence suggests that at least a fraction of FRBs originate from magnetars
(
58
;
59
;
53
;
43
)
. The repeating source FRB 121102, located in a low-metallicity star-forming dwarf galaxy, points to a young and highly active progenitor
(
6

. More directly, an FRB-like burst was detected from the Galactic magnetar SGR 1935+2154, accompanied by a coincident X-ray flare
(
17
;
10
)
, demonstrating that magnetars can produce coherent radio bursts with properties analogous to extragalactic FRBs. Population studies further show that FRBs preferentially occur in star-forming galaxies, consistent with progenitors linked to recent stellar evolution
(
32
;
11
;
8
;
29
;
9
)
. These results, together with the inferred FRB event rate, suggest that young magnetars formed in energetic transients constitute a dominant channel for FRB production.

Gamma-ray bursts are among the most energetic transients in the universe and are broadly classified into short GRBs (SGRBs) and long GRBs (LGRBs) based on their duration
T_{\rm 90}

(
56
)
. SGRBs are generally associated with compact binary mergers, while LGRBs are linked to the collapse of massive stars. Both channels are capable of producing highly magnetized neutron stars under certain conditions. Several theoretical scenarios predict that FRBs may arise in connection with GRBs, either promptly or with significant time delays. For example, FRBs may be produced during the collapse of a supra-massive magnetar formed in a GRB (the “blitzar” scenario), or during the subsequent spin-down evolution of a long-lived magnetar remnant
(
54
;
28
;
39
;
50
)
.
In these scenarios, the delay between the GRB event and observable FRB activity can range from nearly simultaneous emission to timescales of years or longer, depending on the evolution of the central engine and its surrounding environment.

Previous studies provide useful initial constraints on potential FRB–GRB connections, using spatial coincidence analyses
(
38
;
36
;
52
;
34
;
26
;
41
;
27
;
13

. For example, several works have searched for positional overlaps between FRBs and archival GRB samples by comparing their angular separations with reported localization uncertainties, and have identified a small number of candidate pairs that are consistent in position and, in some cases, temporal proximity
(
52
)
. In particular, FRB 171209 has been reported to be spatially consistent with the long-duration GRB 110715A, with its afterglow properties interpreted in the context of a magnetar central engine.

While previous studies have identified several candidate FRB–GRB pairs, the statistical significance of these associations remains uncertain due to the lack of a uniform and self-consistent treatment of localization uncertainties across different datasets. In this work, we revisit the FRB–GRB association problem using a self-consistent statistical framework. We evaluate the spatial consistency between FRBs and GRBs by directly utilizing the probability localization information provided in CHIME Catalog 2, and further impose redshift and physically motivated temporal constraints to construct a robust sample of candidate associations. The statistical significance of these associations is assessed through Monte Carlo simulations that account for observational selection effects.

II
Samples

II.1
FRB Sample

We use the second CHIME/FRB catalog
(
14
)
, which contains a total of 4539 FRBs, including both one-off events and repeating sources. Among them, 981 bursts are associated with 83 repeating sources, while the remaining 3558 events are classified as apparently non-repeating bursts.

The catalog provides sky positions for all bursts, along with associated localization uncertainties. In addition to the commonly used central coordinates and their elliptical errors, CHIME also releases localization probability maps for each FRB. These maps encode the full positional probability distribution on the sky, which can be highly non-Gaussian and irregular due to the instrument beam response. Among them, the localizations of 138 FRBs were replaced with more precise measurements at the
\sim 10^{\prime\prime}
level, for which channelized raw voltage (“baseband”) data are available
(
21
)
.

For this study, we select 4513 FRBs for which reliable localization maps are available and suitable for cross-matching analysis. Bursts from the same repeating source are identified but treated as independent events in the analysis. The dispersion measure (DM) of each burst is used as a proxy for distance, enabling an approximate inference of the FRB redshift, as described in Section
III
.

II.2
GRB Sample

The GRB sample is constructed from the
Swift
GRB catalog
1
1

1

https://swift.gsfc.nasa.gov/archive/grb_table.html/
, covering the period from 2005 to 2025.

To ensure reliable spatial association with FRBs, we restrict the sample to GRBs with high-precision localization obtained from either the X-Ray Telescope (XRT) or the Ultraviolet/Optical Telescope (UVOT). This selection yields 1436 GRBs with arcsecond-level positional accuracy, which is critical for meaningful cross-matching with FRB localization regions.

Among these GRBs, 464 events have spectroscopically measured redshifts reported in the literature. For the remaining GRBs without direct redshift measurements, redshifts are estimated using empirical correlations, as described in Section

III
Methodology

We identify candidate FRB–GRB associations through a multi-stage procedure based on spatial, redshift, and temporal consistency. The overall workflow is illustrated in Figure
1
.

Figure 1:
The workflow of the FRB–GRB association procedure. The input consists of FRB and GRB samples, which are processed through spatial and temporal selection steps to identify potential associations. The procedure evaluates positional consistency within localization uncertainties and applies additional constraints (e.g., time delay and redshift consistency) to obtain the final matched sample.

III.1
Localization-based Matching

Instead of adopting a simple angular separation criterion, we utilize the full localization probability information provided by the CHIME/FRB localization maps.
For each FRB, the CHIME pipeline produces a sky map characterized by a
\Delta\chi^{2}
surface. This surface is empirically calibrated using a sample of localized pulsars, allowing
\Delta\chi^{2}
values to be mapped to confidence level (CL)
(
14
)
. Specifically, the CL at a given sky position can be interpreted as the fraction of the total localization probability contained within regions of higher likelihood.

For each FRB–GRB pair, we evaluate the CL at the GRB sky position by interpolating the FRB localization map. Only GRBs located within the valid footprint of the map are considered. A pair is defined as spatially consistent if the GRB position lies within the 68% highest-probability region (i.e., CL
<0.68
) of the FRB localization.

We perform a global search over all FRBs and GRBs, identifying all candidate pairs that satisfy this spatial criterion.

III.2
Redshift Consistency

For spatially consistent FRB--GRB pairs, we further require consistency in redshift. When spectroscopic redshifts are available, we directly compare the measured values
2
2

2

https://swift.gsfc.nasa.gov/archive/grb_table.html/
. Otherwise, redshifts are inferred probabilistically for both FRBs and GRBs.

III.2.1
FRB Redshift Inference from Dispersion Measure

For FRBs without host galaxy redshift measurements, we infer their redshifts from the observed dispersion measure (DM) based on the Bayesian inference method
(
46
)
. The total DM is decomposed as

\mathrm{DM}_{\rm obs}=\mathrm{DM}_{\rm ISM}^{\rm MW}+\mathrm{DM}_{\rm halo}^{\rm MW}+\mathrm{DM}_{\rm IGM}(z)+\frac{\mathrm{DM}_{\rm host}}{1+z}.

(1)

The interstellar medium (ISM) of the Milky Way contribution
\mathrm{DM}_{\rm ISM}^{\rm MW}
is estimated using the NE2001 model
(
24
)
, while a fixed Galactic halo term
\mathrm{DM}_{\rm halo}^{\rm MW}
is assumed as 50
\rm pc~cm^{-3}

(
35
)
. The intergalactic medium (IGM) contribution
\mathrm{DM}_{\rm IGM}(z)
is computed under a flat
\Lambda
CDM cosmology, with stochastic fluctuations modeled as
\sigma_{\rm IGM}\propto z^{-1/2}
. The host-galaxy contribution
\mathrm{DM}_{\rm host}
is described by a log-normal distribution.
For details, please refer to
(
46
)
.

For each FRB, we construct a likelihood function
\mathcal{L}_{\rm FRB}(z)
and sample the posterior distribution using a Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) method with a flat prior over
0.001<z<9
. The median and 16th–84th percentiles are adopted as the inferred redshift and uncertainty.

III.2.2
GRB Redshift Inference from Prompt-emission Correlations

For GRBs without spectroscopic redshifts, we estimate their redshifts using the Amati relation
(
1
;
2
)
, which links the rest-frame peak energy
E_{\rm p,i}
and isotropic-equivalent energy
E_{\rm iso}
.
For a trial redshift
z
, the isotropic energy is computed as

E_{\rm iso}(z)=\frac{4\pi d_{L}^{2}(z)}{1+z}\,S_{\rm obs}\,k(z),

(2)

where
d_{L}(z)
is the luminosity distance,
S_{\rm obs}
is the observed fluence, and
k(z)
is the
k
-correction factor that converts the observed bandpass to a fixed rest-frame energy range (typically 1–10,000 keV) based on the best-fit spectral model.
The likelihood is constructed as

\mathcal{L}(z)\propto\exp\left[-\frac{1}{2}\frac{\left(\log E_{\rm p,i}-\mu(E_{\rm iso})\right)^{2}}{\sigma_{\rm A}^{2}}\right],

(3)

where
\mu
represents the excepted value of the best-fit correlation from Amati relation,
\sigma_{A}
is the intrinsic scatter of the relation.
The posteriors are sampled via MCMC with a prior uniform in
\log z
over
0.01<z<10
. The median and 16th–84th percentiles are adopted as the redshift estimate and uncertainty.

III.3
Temporal Consistency

In addition to spatial and redshift consistency, we impose temporal constraints motivated by plausible physical scenarios linking GRBs and FRBs. These criteria are designed to reflect different evolutionary pathways of the central engine. In particular, LGRBs, commonly associated with massive star collapse, may give rise to a long-lived compact object (e.g., a magnetar)
(
54
)
, from which FRB activity can emerge on delayed timescales. In contrast, SGRBs, typically linked to compact binary mergers, are expected to occur either contemporaneously with or after any potential pre-merger radio activity
(
57
)
.

Specifically, we require that long GRBs precede FRBs by at least 2 years, while short GRBs occur at least 2 years after the FRB. The 2-year criterion just serves as a conservative timescale to avoid contamination from prompt or early-time emission.

III.4
Chance-coincidence Simulation

To evaluate the probability that the observed associations arise by chance, we perform Monte Carlo simulations that replicate the same selection procedure.

The FRB sample is kept fixed, including their localization maps, event times, and redshift estimation. In each simulation, a mock GRB sample is generated with the same size as the observed sample. The sky positions are drawn isotropically, burst times are sampled from the observed temporal distribution of
Swift
GRBs, and redshifts are drawn from an empirical GRB formation rate model.
For each simulated GRB, we apply the same selection criteria as for the real data, requiring spatial, redshift, and temporal consistency.

We perform 5000 realizations of the simulation. In each realization, we record the number of FRB–GRB pairs that satisfy all criteria, as well as their distribution in localization confidence levels. The statistical significance of the observed associations is then evaluated by comparing with the simulated distributions.

Discussion

V.1
The Candidates

The candidates discussed here satisfy both the spatial consistency based on localization maps and the redshift criteria. Among them, two systems are associated with SGRBs, including FRB 20190309A–sGRB 060502B previously reported by
(
34

, and FRB 20200227A–GRB 051221A identified in this work. GRB 051221A is a short burst with

(
25

f_{\rm FRB}\sim 1.7

\eta=f_{\rm GRB}/f_{\rm FRB}\sim 2\times 10^{11}

\sim 400~

Such systems are broadly consistent with merger-driven scenarios in which FRB activity, if present, is expected to precede the neutron star-neutron star binary (see Section 5.3 for more discussion) and be suppressed after the merger. Given the temporal configuration adopted here, these cases are treated primarily as illustrative comparisons.

The majority of the sample consists of LGRB-associated candidates, which are more directly relevant to delayed FRB emission scenarios. For instance, FRB 20210917A–GRB 140318A involves a relatively low-energy GRB (
E_{\gamma,\rm iso}\sim 8\times 10^{50}

(
12

47

f_{\rm FRB}\sim 2.0

\sim 7.5

A contrasting case is FRB 20201111B–GRB 140607A, which is associated with a long-duration GRB (
T_{90}=109.9
s) with typical fluence
(2.2\pm 0.3)\times 10^{-6}~\rm erg~cm^{-2}

(

f_{\rm FRB}\sim 1.15

\mathrm{DM}_{\rm exc}\sim 261~\rm pc~cm^{-3}

\eta\sim 5\times 10^{11}

The GRB exhibits a relatively long duration and typical energy, with the associated FRB showing comparable properties to others, indicating no evident correlation between prompt gamma-ray and later radio emission.

Across all candidates, the fluence ratio remains at the level of
\eta\sim 10^{10}
–
10^{11}

, but consistent with a scenario in which the FRB emission represents a small fraction of the total energy budget. This behavior is compatible with models in which FRBs originate from magnetospheric processes of a long-lived compact object (e.g., a magnetar) formed in the GRB event, where the radio emission is only weakly coupled to the prompt gamma-ray energetics.

Taken together, the candidate pairs’ properties also do not reveal clear signatures that would distinguish them from random coincidences. Therefore, although individual systems may be suggestive, the current sample does not provide compelling evidence for a direct physical association.

V.2
Effects of CHIME Sidelobe Structure on Spatial Matching

A key systematic in the spatial association analysis arises from how FRB localization uncertainties are represented.
To assess this effect, we compared two approaches: (1) a Gaussian approximation based on the central coordinates and 68% uncertainties reported in the CHIME Catalog 2
(
14
)
, and (2) a map-based method that utilizes the full localization probability distribution.

Under the Gaussian approximation, spatial consistency is evaluated using a normalized elliptical distance assuming a two-dimensional Gaussian error model. In contrast, the map-based approach directly identifies GRB positions that fall within the reported 68% confidence region of the FRB localization map. Applying the Gaussian prescription yields 30 spatially consistent FRB–GRB pairs. In comparison, the map-based method identifies 130 pairs when considering spatial coincidence alone, indicating a substantial increase in candidate associations.

The catalog-reported positions correspond to the peak response of the synthesized beam and its local uncertainty, but do not capture the full posterior structure of the localization. Owing to CHIME’s cylindrical, East–West interferometric configuration, the beam response can produce multiple sidelobes, leading to spatially extended and multi-modal probability distributions
(
16
)
. As a result, significant localization probability may reside in regions that are spatially offset from the catalog-reported position.

This effect is illustrated in Figure
4
. In some cases (e.g., FRB 20211030A–GRB 090813 and FRB 20200314H–GRB 160228A), the GRB positions coincide with the catalog-reported FRB locations. However, in other cases (e.g., FRB 20221202D–GRB 100816A and FRB 20210917A–GRB 140318A), the GRBs are clearly offset from the catalog positions and lie within sidelobe-supported probability regions. These regions remain within the 68% confidence contours but would be excluded under a Gaussian approximation centered on the catalog coordinates.

Therefore, the Gaussian elliptical method systematically underestimates the true localization support and may miss physically plausible associations. The map-based approach, by construction, preserves the full probability distribution and thus provides a more complete and conservative treatment of spatial uncertainties. Although the use of localization maps increases the number of candidate matches, this does not necessarily inflate the statistical significance, as subsequent redshift and temporal constraints substantially reduce the sample. Instead, the map-based method minimizes the risk of excluding genuine associations due to oversimplified localization models. For this reason, it is adopted as the fiducial approach throughout this work.

The red crosses mark the FRB positions reported in the CHIME catalog, while the shaded contours indicate the probabilistic localization regions derived from the FRB beam response, with the 68% and 95% confidence levels shown, and the green star symbols denote the positions of the associated GRBs. In the cases of FRB 20211030A–GRB 090813 and FRB 20200314H–GRB 160228A, the GRBs coincide closely with the catalog-reported FRB positions. In contrast, for FRB 20221202D–GRB 100816A and FRB 20210917A–GRB 140318A, the GRBs are offset from the catalog positions and fall into sidelobe regions; however, they remain within the 68% confidence localization contours of the FRBs.

V.3
Physical Motivation for Temporal Constraints

The temporal constraints adopted in this work are motivated by physically plausible evolutionary connections between GRBs and FRBs, particularly in scenarios involving magnetar formation and compact object mergers
(
30
;
31
;
51
;
49
;
58
;
59
)
.

For LGRBs, the standard picture associates them with the core collapse of massive stars, leading to the formation of a rapidly rotating, highly magnetized neutron star or black hole. In magnetar-powered scenarios, the newborn magnetar may initially be embedded in dense ejecta, suppressing coherent radio emission. FRB activity is therefore expected to emerge only after the surrounding medium becomes sufficiently optically thin. This delay depends on the expansion and ionization state of the ejecta, as well as the magnetar wind evolution, and can naturally span timescales of years to decades
(
54
;
40
)
.
Under this framework, the FRB represents a later-stage manifestation of the same central engine that powered the LGRB. Consequently, a physically consistent association requires that the FRB occurs after the LGRB. The adopted criterion
T_{\rm FRB}-T_{\rm GRB}>2\,\mathrm{yrs}
is intended to exclude prompt or causally inconsistent associations, while remaining conservative with respect to the uncertain timescale for radio transparency.

In contrast, SGRBs are widely attributed to compact binary mergers, such as binary neutron star systems
(
55
;
57
;
58
)
. In some evolutionary scenarios, FRB activity may originate from a long-lived neutron star prior to merger, either through magnetospheric activity or interaction with the surrounding environment. The merger event then produces the SGRB and terminates the FRB engine. In this picture, the FRB precedes the SGRB, and no FRB activity is expected after the merger.

V.4
Distribution of Matches in Localization Confidence

An interesting feature of our results is that the excess of FRB–GRB associations does not concentrate in the lowest CL bins—corresponding to the highest-probability localization regions—but instead appears at intermediate CL values.
At first glance, this behavior is consistent with expectations from random spatial coincidence, where no strong preference for the highest-probability regions is present. This is also reflected in the relatively low overall statistical significance obtained from Monte Carlo simulations, suggesting that the majority of identified pairs are likely chance alignments.

The localization uncertainties of GRBs—particularly those without precise afterglow-based positions—can further broaden the effective matching region. When combined with the extended and structured FRB probability maps, this can shift genuine associations toward intermediate CL bins.
If only a small fraction of the sample corresponds to physically associated FRB–GRB pairs, their statistical signature can be easily diluted by a dominant background of random matches. In this regime, even real associations may not produce a strong excess in the highest-probability bins, but instead appear as a mild and distributed enhancement across multiple CL intervals.

Under this interpretation, the observed distribution does not provide strong evidence for a physical association, but it is also not in clear contradiction with scenarios in which a subdominant population of true FRB–GRB pairs is present. Distinguishing between these possibilities will require improved localization precision, larger sample sizes, and more stringent temporal or physical constraints.

VI
Conlucsion

We conducted a systematic search for spatial and temporal associations between CHIME FRBs and
Swift
GRBs. By adopting full localization probability maps instead of simplified catalog positions, the analysis accounts for the complex and multi-modal nature of CHIME localizations, leading to a substantially larger set of spatially consistent candidate pairs. Physically motivated temporal criteria were applied based on plausible evolutionary scenarios linking magnetar activity and compact object mergers to FRBs and GRBs. These constraints provide a consistent framework for evaluating potential associations while reducing clearly unphysical coincidences.

Despite these considerations, Monte Carlo simulations indicate that the overall excess of FRB–GRB associations is not statistically significant. The distribution of matches across localization confidence levels is broadly consistent with expectations from random coincidence, although a small contribution from genuine associations cannot be excluded given current uncertainties.
These results suggest that, within the present sample and methodology, there is no compelling statistical evidence for a direct FRB–GRB connection. Future progress will rely on improved localization precision, more uniform sky coverage, and larger samples, which will enable more stringent tests of potential physical links between these two classes of transients.
