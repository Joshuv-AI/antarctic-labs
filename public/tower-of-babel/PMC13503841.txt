Utilization of advanced machine learning models for analysis of pharmaceutical cocrystals by prediction of solubility parameters using Dragonfly algorithm optimization
Authors: Hadil Faris Alotaibi, Tareq Nayef AlRamadneh, Mahendihasan S Heera, Anupam Yadav, Ahmed Kareem Shakir, T Ramachandran, Swati Mishra, Navin Kumar Tailor, Divya Singhal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPharmaceutical cocrystals have emerged as an effective strategy for enhancing the solubility and bioavailability of poorly water-soluble drugs. The successful design of cocrystals largely depends on the selection of suitable coformers, which requires an understanding of the molecular interactions governing cocrystal formation during the process. The objective of this study was to develop robust machine learning models for predicting Hansen solubility parameters of pharmaceutical cocrystal using molecular descriptors generated from COSMO-RS and group contribution methods. A dataset consisting of 181 samples and 86 input features was utilized, incorporating molecular descriptors related to hydrogen-bonding capability, van der Waals interactions, and structural functional groups. Data preprocessing included outlier detection using the Isolation Forest algorithm and feature selection through Sequential Floating Forward Selection (SFFS). Three tree-based ensemble learning models, namely Random Forest (RF), Extra Trees (ET), and Gradient Boosting Regression Trees (GBRT), were developed and optimized using the Dragonfly algorithm for hyperparameter tuning. The obtained results demonstrated that the Extra Trees model consistently outperformed the RF and GBRT models in predicting all three Hansen solubility parameters. For the three target outputs, the ET model achieved test-set R2 values of 0.9175, 0.8661, and 0.9815, respectively, while also exhibiting the lowest RMSE and MAE values. Feature importance analysis revealed that both group contribution descriptors and COSMO-RS-derived molecular properties play significant roles in determining the Hansen solubility parameters of coformers. Overall, the proposed machine learning framework provides an efficient and accurate approach for predicting Hansen solubility parameters and screening pharmaceutical cocrystals. The findings highlight the superior predictive capability of the Extra Trees model and demonstrate the potential of combining molecular thermodynamic descriptors with advanced machine learning techniques for pharmaceutical cocrystal design.Keywords: Pharmaceutical cocrystals, Machine learning, Solubility, Random forest, Extra trees, Gradient boosting regression treesSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

For preparation of cocrystals with enhanced solubility, selection of coformer is a key step which should be selected based on analysis of intermolecular forces so that successful crystal would be formed. Given that a variety of entities can be used as coformer, development of advanced models to select the best coformer is valuable for development of pharmaceutical industry1. Some molecular-based models have been reported and implemented for selection and analysis of pharmaceutical coformers to better understand how formation can occur. Also, the process for crystallization can operate in either batch or continuous mode in which the model can help to improve the process parameters2.

Although thermodynamic and molecular-level models have been successfully offered and tested for analysis of pharmaceutical crystallization, more generalized models are needed which can account for a wide variety of molecules with less time and computational costs. In this regards, data-driven models can be exploited which have been shown to be powerful in modeling pharmaceutical processes and formulations3,4. These models are generally easier to apply in finding correlations between inputs and process responses, however they do not give any process understanding and physical views in pharmaceutics. Therefore, combination of data-driven models with thermodynamic models can be considered as a new approach for analysis of pharmaceutical crystallization. In this approach, the input data for analysis by data-driven models can be generated via thermodynamic methods such as activity models or COSMO-RS (COnductor like Screening MOdel for Real Solvents) technique5.

For building hybrid models in analyzing pharmaceutical cocrystals, machine learning methods are great choices for screening coformers since large dataset can be evaluated via these models. The molecular dataset for machine learning analysis can be obtained via COSMO-RS technique and group contribution (GC) method to have a full description of molecular properties (molecular descriptors) for the model. In recent years, Machine Learning (ML) models, especially tree-based ensemble models, have gained popularity in predictive modeling. These models are recognized for their capability to handle extensive datasets, address intricate relationships among variables, and produce dependable predictions6–9.

The models we employ as the basis of our research utilize several Regression Trees (decision trees) to boost the predictive precision and mitigate the likelihood of overfitting for the dataset. The employed models in this study include:

Data preprocessing is essential for the quality and effectiveness of machine learning models. In this study, several steps were taken to prepare the dataset for modeling. Missing values were handled through imputation techniques, ensuring dataset completeness. Data normalization scaled the features to a standard range, crucial for models sensitive to input data scale. Outlier detection used the Isolation Forest (IF) method, identifying and removing rare anomalies to enhance model robustness. Feature selection was performed using Sequential Floating Forward Selection (SFFS), dynamically including and excluding features based on their contribution to model performance.

Hyperparameter optimization employed the Dragonfly algorithm, a meta-heuristic technique inspired by dragonfly swarming behavior. This algorithm balances exploration and exploitation to efficiently search for optimal hyperparameter values, enhancing the predictive capabilities of the machine learning models.

Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to develop accurate machine learning models for predicting the Hansen solubility parameters of pharmaceutical cocrystal coformers using molecular descriptors generated from COSMO-RS and group contribution methods. To achieve this objective, three advanced tree-based ensemble learning algorithms, namely RF, ET, and GBRT, were developed and compared. The study further incorporates Isolation Forest for outlier detection, Sequential Floating Forward Selection (SFFS) for feature selection, and the Dragonfly algorithm for hyperparameter optimization. In addition to evaluating predictive performance, the importance of molecular descriptors and functional groups is analyzed to identify the key factors governing Hansen solubility parameters and coformer selection. The proposed framework provides an efficient data-driven tool for screening pharmaceutical cocrystal coformers and offers insights into the molecular characteristics influencing cocrystal formation.

Materials and methods

The objective of this study was to determine three Hansen parameters, i.e., , , and via the developed ML models. A wide range of entities were considered in the modeling with different structures and functional groups8. Group contribution was used to account for the functional groups in the structure of coformer, while COSMO-RS thermodynamic model was used to obtain the molecular descriptors for the entities such as hydrogen bond and Wan der Waals forces. The dataset, comprising 86 features and 181 data points, was obtained from a published source10. The detailed definition of input and output parameters are reported previously10. The same data of cocrystals was used in other works8,9, and their methodology is implemented in this study for building the ML methods and execution of models as well as assessments. The following sections describe the methodology and techniques employed in this research, including data preprocessing, feature selection, model training, and evaluation.

Initially, the dataset underwent preprocessing steps to handle missing values, normalize the data, and detect outliers. Outlier detection was performed using the Isolation Forest (IF) method. This technique isolates observations to identify outliers, focusing on anomalies which are rare and distinct from normal data points. The IF algorithm identifies outliers by randomly choosing features and splitting values to create a tree structure, isolating anomalies more efficiently due to their unique characteristics.

To improve model performance and interpretability, feature selection was carried out using the Sequential Floating Forward Selection (SFFS) method. SFFS is an iterative feature selection technique that dynamically adds and removes features based on their contribution to model performance. This method achieves an optimal subset of features by combining the advantages of forward and backward selection methods.

Following that, the RF (Random Forest), ET (Extra Trees), and GBRT (Gradient Boosting Regression Trees) models were trained and fine-tuned. The Dragonfly algorithm, a meta-heuristic optimization technique inspired by the swarming behavior of dragonflies, was used for hyperparameter tuning. This algorithm employs a strategy that effectively balances the act of exploring different options and exploiting the best ones in order to efficiently search for the most optimal hyperparameter values.

In order to prevent data leakage in the modeling dataset of solubility parameters, the dataset was first divided into training and testing subsets. Feature selection using Sequential Floating Forward Selection (SFFS) with 5-fold cross-validation and hyperparameter optimization using the Dragonfly algorithm were performed exclusively on the training data. The final optimized models were subsequently evaluated on the independent test set, which was not used during feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, or model training. The reported 5-fold mean and standard deviation values represent cross-validation results obtained from the training stage and are provided to assess model stability and robustness.

Outlier detection using isolation forest

Isolation Forest (IF) is an anomaly method that isolates observations to identify outliers. It focuses on isolating anomalies, which are few and different from normal data points11,12. The IF algorithm operates by employing a random selection process to choose a feature, followed by another random selection process to determine a split value within the range of the minimum and maximum values of the chosen feature. The aforementioned procedure is iterated to construct a hierarchical arrangement, based on the notion that anomalies, due to their infrequency and distinctiveness, are more prone to being identified and separated earlier in the tree structure compared to normal data points. The shorter the average path length from the root to a leaf for a data point, the more likely it is to be an anomaly13.

In this study, outliers were identified based on the anomaly scores generated by the Isolation Forest algorithm. Observations exhibiting significantly shorter average path lengths than the majority of samples were considered anomalous because they could be isolated more easily within the feature space. Such observations were classified as outliers and removed prior to feature selection and model development to improve the robustness and reliability of the predictive models. IF is efficient and effective for high-dimensional data and large datasets due to its linear time complexity and small memory requirement. The algorithm’s effectiveness stems from its simplicity and the insight that anomalies are few and different, thus easier to isolate.

Feature selection with sequential floating forward selection (SFFS) method

Feature selection is a critical step in developing predictive models, aimed at identifying the most significant features influencing the target variable. This process enhances model performance and interpretability. In our study, we employed the Sequential Floating Forward Selection (SFFS) method to select features for three outputs14.

Dynamic inclusion and exclusion of features depending on their contribution to the performance of the model is achieved in SFFS by iterative feature selection. In addition, the SFFS procedure was performed separately for each target variable (δd, δp, and δh) to obtain feature subsets specifically optimized for predicting each Hansen solubility parameter.

Dragonfly algorithm for hyper-parameter tuning

The dragonfly algorithm replicates the social actions of dragonflies, differentiating between stationary (feeding) and mobile (migratory) swarming stages, which correspond to exploration and exploitation in optimization. During their investigation, dragonflies congregate in tiny clusters and systematically hunt a specific region for food, thereby attracting flying prey. On the other hand, during the exploitation phase, there is a significant movement of dragonflies in big groups over extended distances to divert attention from potential dangers16–18. Mathematically, these behaviors are modeled using five key patterns: attraction to food, distraction from enemies, separation, alignment, and cohesion.

For hyperparameter optimization, the Dragonfly algorithm was employed to search within predefined parameter ranges for each machine learning model. For the RF and ET models, the optimized hyperparameters included the number of trees (n_estimators), maximum tree depth (max_depth), minimum number of samples required to split an internal node (min_samples_split), minimum number of samples required at a leaf node (min_samples_leaf), and the number of features considered at each split (max_features). For the GBRT model, the optimization process included n_estimators, max_depth, min_samples_split, min_samples_leaf, learning_rate, and subsample ratio. The Dragonfly algorithm explored the specified search space for each parameter and selected the combination that yielded the best cross-validation performance, thereby improving the predictive accuracy and generalization capability of the developed models. It is particularly noted for outperforming single decision tree algorithms, such as CART, while maintaining computational efficiency. The fundamental concept underlying Random Forest is to construct a collection of varied decision trees, each trained on random subsets of data and features. This approach effectively mitigates overfitting and enhances the ability to generalize.

Gradient boosting regression trees (GBRT)

GBRT uses an iterative process which progressively reduces the model’s error for estimation of response parameters. In order to mitigate the issue of overfitting, GBRT include a parameter known as the learning rate. This parameter is responsible for adjusting the impact of each weak learner in the model. The ultimate prediction of a GBRT model is the aggregate of the predictions from all weak learners (models), with weights set by the learning rate23,24.

The careful selection of the learning rate and the number of boosting rounds is crucial for balancing the model’s bias-variance trade-off and achieving high predictive performance without overfitting.

Although RF, ET, and GBRT are all tree-based ensemble learning methods, they differ substantially in their learning strategies. RF constructs multiple decision trees using bootstrap sampling and random feature selection, which improves generalization and reduces overfitting. ET further increases randomness by using the entire training dataset and selecting split thresholds randomly, often resulting in lower variance, faster training, and improved predictive performance. In contrast, GBRT builds trees sequentially, with each new tree correcting the errors of the previous ones, enabling the model to capture complex nonlinear relationships. RF is generally recognized for its robustness and stability, ET for its computational efficiency and strong generalization capability, and GBRT for its high predictive power and ability to model intricate patterns. Comparing these three approaches provides a comprehensive assessment of different ensemble learning strategies for predicting Hansen solubility parameters of pharmaceutical cocrystal coformers.

Conclusion

This study successfully demonstrates the application of tree-based ensemble learning techniques, specifically RF, ET, and GBRT, for predicting the outputs , , and . Inputs are generated from group contribution and COSMO-RS models. The Extra Trees model consistently outperformed both RF and GBRT models, achieving superior predictive accuracy and robustness as evidenced by higher R2 scores and lower RMSE and MAE values across all outputs. The methodology incorporated effective data preprocessing, outlier detection using Isolation Forest, and feature selection with Sequential Floating Forward Selection, enhancing model performance and interpretability. Hyperparameter tuning was efficiently conducted using the Dragonfly algorithm, further optimizing the models’ predictive capabilities. These results show the potential of the Extra Trees model in complex predictive tasks, offering a powerful tool for future research and practical applications in fields that require precise prediction of three delta values. The study provides a comprehensive framework for leveraging machine learning techniques to achieve high-performance predictions, contributing to advances in predictive modeling and machine learning applications.
