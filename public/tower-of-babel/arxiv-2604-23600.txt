Personality Shapes Gender Bias in Persona-Conditioned LLM Narratives Across English and Hindi: An Empirical Investigation
Tanay Kumar, Shreya Gautam, Aman Chadha, Vinija Jain, Francesco Pierri

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in persona-driven applications such as education, customer service, and social platforms, where models are prompted to adopt specific personas when interacting with users. While persona conditioning can improve user experience and engagement, it also raises concerns about how personality cues may interact with gender biases and stereotypes. In this work, we present a controlled study of persona-conditioned story generation in English and Hindi, where each story portrays a working professional in India producing context-specific artifacts (e.g., lesson plans, reports, letters) under systematically varied persona gender, occupational role, and personality traits from the HEXACO and Dark Triad frameworks. Across 23,400 23,400 generated stories from six state-of-the-art LLMs, we find that personality traits are significantly associated with both the magnitude and direction of gender bias. In particular, Dark Triad personality traits are consistently associated with higher gender-stereotypical representations compared to socially desirable HEXACO traits, though these associations vary across models and languages. Our findings demonstrate that gender bias in LLMs is not static but context-dependent. This suggests that persona-conditioned systems used in real-world applications may introduce uneven representational harms, reinforcing gender stereotypes in generated educational, professional, or social content.

2.3
Position of This Work

Prior work establishes that LLMs exhibit gender bias in narratives and that persona and personality prompts shape model behaviour. What remains unexplored is how personality traits modulate gender bias through interaction effects, particularly in multilingual narrative generation grounded in occupational contexts.

Our work addresses this gap by systematically varying gender, occupation, and HEXACO and Dark Triad personality traits in English and Hindi story generation.
Unlike prior studies, we examine personality–gender interactions and measure story-level bias using a sentence-based approach.

3.2
Gender-Stereotypical Centroids for Bias Measurement

We represent gender stereotypes in a shared embedding space using male and female centroids derived from gender-stereotypical language.
Intuitively, the male centroid encodes traits such as being ”leader-like”, ”ambitious”, and ”assertive”, while the female centroid encodes traits like ”nurturing”, ”empathetic”, ”cooperative”, and ”graceful”
8
;
1
.
This enables us to quantify how closely a given sentence aligns with male- or female-coded stereotypes in both English and Hindi.
We construct the respective centroids in three steps.

Step 1: Stereotypical word lists:

We manually compile gender-stereotypical word lists in English following prior work on occupational and trait-based gender bias

;
15
.
Each list contains approximately
200-210
items, covering nouns, verbs, and adjectives (e.g.,

for male;

empathetic

for female).
We then translate these words into Hindi and iteratively refine with native speakers to ensure stereotypical validity in Hindi usage.
We also check these words against prior works
24
;
17

This design choice keeps the English and Hindi lexicons parallel, so cross-lingual comparisons are not confounded by differing stereotype sets.

Step 2: Sentence Templates:

To obtain contextualized representations, we convert each stereotype word into one or more short sentences using part-of-speech-specific templates (e.g.,
“This is _”

“That is _”
etc. in English,

Step 3: Centroid Computation:

Since our analysis spans both English and Hindi in an Indian context, we use the multilingual sentence embedding model

\{\mathbf{s}^{\text{male}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{m}}

\{\mathbf{s}^{\text{female}}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{f}}

\mathbf{c}_{\text{male}}=\frac{1}{N_{m}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{m}}\mathbf{s}^{\text{male}}_{i},\qquad\mathbf{c}_{\text{female}}=\frac{1}{N_{f}}\sum_{j=1}^{N_{f}}\mathbf{s^{\text{female}}_{j}}.

These centroids serve as anchor points for subsequent bias measurements.

3.3
Bias Metric and Story-Level Aggregation

For each artifact
S
, we first segment the text into sentences

S=\{s_{1},s_{2},\dots,s_{n}\}
. We then embed each sentence using the same multilingual sentence encoder described in Section
3.2
, yielding vectors
\mathbf{s}_{i}
for
i=1,\dots,n
.
Given the male and female centroids
\mathbf{c}_{\text{male}}
and
\mathbf{c}_{\text{female}}
, we define a
sentence-level bias score as:

\text{bias}(s_{i})=\cos(\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{c}_{\text{male}})-\cos(\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{c}_{\text{female}})

where
\cos(\cdot,\cdot)
denotes cosine similarity.
By construction,
\text{bias}(s_{i})\in[-1,1]
. Positive values indicate male-stereotypical language alignment and negative values indicate female-stereotypical language alignment. This difference-of-cosines metric is standard in embedding-based bias measurement

;
26
.

To obtain a single bias score per artifact, we aggregate sentence-level scores by
selecting the sentence with the maximum absolute bias:

\displaystyle\text{story}(S)

\displaystyle=\text{bias}(s_{k}),

\displaystyle k=\arg\max_{i}\bigl|\text{bias}(s_{i})\bigr|.

This prioritizes the most strongly biased statement in the artifact, which is likely most psychologically salient
to readers’ impressions of the persona (see Appendix
A.6
for robustness checks). All subsequent analyses in Section
3.5
use
\text{story}(S)
as the primary outcome variable.

3.4
Validation of Bias Score Through Human Annotation

To validate our sentence-level bias score, three annotators independently evaluated 100 story pairs
(50 per language, 200 stories in total), selecting which story in each pair exhibited
greater gender stereotyping.
Each pair contrasted a
personality-conditioned narrative against a neutral baseline, with both stories sharing the
same occupation and gender assignment. Inter-annotator agreement was substantial in both
languages (Fleiss’
\kappa=0.66
for English,
\kappa=0.69
for Hindi;

20
), indicating reliable and consistent judgements across
annotators.
The personality-conditioned story was identified as more stereotyped in 66%
of English pairs and 72% of Hindi pairs under majority vote, with unanimous agreement
in 76% and 82% of cases respectively.
Taken together, these results suggest that the sentence-level bias score provides a robust signal of gender-stereotypical content across both languages.

Personality traits are associated with different direction and magnitude of gender bias in the generated output.

Gender Effects (RQ2)

We now evaluate the relative and joint contributions of gender and personality.

As shown in Figure

When both gender and personality are included in the regression model, several personality coefficients (Appendix Figure

Gender-stratified analyses, in which we fit separate regression models for each persona gender (Figure

Together, these findings provide partial support for RQ2: although both gender and personality influence bias, personality can meaningfully amplify or attenuate the expression of gendered language.

4.5
Model-Level Variation

Lastly, we examine variation across model families.
All models exhibit more female-stereotypical alignment relative to GPT-5 nano, which serves as the reference (see Figure
18

The largest negative shifts are observed for Gemma-3-1b-it and Falcon-Mamba-7b, whereas the other instruction-tuned models remain closer to the GPT-5 nano baseline.
This pattern suggests that smaller or architecturally distinct models may exhibit weaker male-stereotypical defaults in generation, consistent with the more female-leaning baselines (see Table

At the same time, the direction of personality effects remains consistent across architectures: Dark Triad traits amplify male-stereotypical alignment, whereas prosocial traits attenuate it.
This suggests that the overall pattern of personality-driven modulation is not specific to a single model family, but may reflect a broader property of
llm
under persona conditioning.

5
Discussion and Conclusion

We presented a controlled study of persona-conditioned story generation in English and Hindi, varying persona gender, occupation, and HEXACO and Dark Triad traits across
23{,}400
artifacts from six LLMs.
Using a sentence-level centroid-based bias metric validated by human annotation, we show that gender bias is context-dependent rather than fixed: Dark Triad traits consistently amplify male-stereotypical alignment, HEXACO traits have mixed effects, and in some cases personality effects exceed those of explicit gender labels.
We also find that Hindi has a stronger baseline male-stereotypical skew, while personality-driven modulation is more pronounced in English. Together, these results highlight personality prompts as fairness-critical design parameters in persona-driven multilingual systems.

These findings carry broader implications for persona-driven
llm
deployment. If personality cues can systematically amplify or attenuate gender-stereotypical language, then bias should not be understood as a static model artifact, but as an emergent property of the interaction between model, prompt design, and linguistic context. In practical applications such as educational support, workplace writing assistance, and customer service, persona specifications may therefore act as fairness-critical controls that shape how professions, competencies, and social roles are represented to users. The cross-linguistic differences we observe further suggest that mitigation strategies cannot be assumed to transfer uniformly across languages.

Future work could extend persona-conditioned analysis beyond gender to other social categories (e.g., caste, religion, disability), as well as to multi-turn interactions in which personas evolve over time, to support richer evaluations and provide clearer guidance for safer persona design.

Limitations

Our study focuses on a single task family, which is, occupation-grounded artifact generation in an Indian context, and a fixed set of stereotyped occupations, which may limit generalization to other domains or interaction styles.
The personality frameworks used (HEXACO and Dark Triad) originate from Western personality psychology and may not fully capture personality constructs across Indian cultural contexts.

Bias is measured using a centroid-based embedding metric with manually curated stereotype lexicons, making the results dependent on the chosen encoder and lexical sets.
The stereotype word lists, though refined with native speakers, were originally compiled in English and translated into Hindi, which may introduce translation artifacts or fail to capture Hindi-specific stereotypical expressions that have no direct English equivalent.
Personality prompt validation via the SD3 test was conducted on GPT-5 nano only; the degree to which the same prompts reliably elicit targeted personality profiles in architecturally distinct models (e.g., Falcon-Mamba SSM, DeepSeek-R1 LRM) was not independently verified.
Methodologically, we treat each generated story as an independent sample and evaluate only six contemporary models, leaving open how persona-driven bias may manifest in longer conversations or in newer architectures.

Our bias metric aggregates to a single sentence-level maximum score per story, which captures the most salient stereotypical statement but discards information about the overall distributional tendency of the artifact.
Alternative aggregation strategies (e.g., mean score, proportion of biased sentences) may yield different conclusions.
Furthermore, our metric measures alignment with pre-defined stereotypical centroids and does not capture more subtle or intersectional forms of bias, such as caste- or class-based stereotyping that may co-occur with gender in Indian occupational contexts.

The human annotation study involved only three annotators evaluating 100 story pairs, which, while showing substantial inter-annotator agreement, represents a limited sample relative to the full corpus of
23,400
artifacts.
Annotation was also limited to pairwise comparisons against a neutral baseline, and did not assess absolute stereotyping severity or capture annotator demographic variation that may influence stereotype perception.

Finally, our controlled prompts simulate persona effects but do not reflect real-world chatbot usage patterns or user query distributions.
Personality conditioning in deployment settings is rarely as explicit or structured as our prompts, and users may induce similar effects through more subtle cues.
We also do not measure real-world consequences using live user data, nor do we investigate whether similar patterns arise in other languages or cultural contexts.

Ethical Considerations

This work studies gender bias in LLM-generated narratives and involves the
construction of a dataset containing gender-stereotypical language.
While such content is necessary for measuring bias, we acknowledge that
compiling and releasing stereotype lexicons and persona-conditioned narratives
carries inherent risks, particularly of reinforcing the very stereotypes we seek
to expose. To mitigate this, we contextualize all examples within an explicit
bias measurement framework and release the dataset with documentation
discouraging its use for stereotype generation outside of fairness research.

Our study conditions language models on Dark Triad personality traits
(Machiavellianism, Narcissism, Psychopathy) to examine bias amplification.
We caution that prompts designed to elicit such behaviors could be misused
to systematically generate harmful, manipulative, or stereotyped content at
scale. Researchers and practitioners using our dataset or prompt templates
should be aware of this risk and implement appropriate safeguards.

The human annotation study involved three annotators who evaluated
narratives containing gender-stereotypical language. Annotators were
briefed on the nature of the task prior to participation, informed that
the content was machine-generated and does not reflect the views of the
authors, and were free to withdraw at any time. Annotators were
compensated fairly for their time.

Our occupational scenarios are grounded in the Indian socio-cultural
context and reflect existing societal gender stereotypes associated with
specific professions in India. We do not intend to endorse these
stereotypes; rather, they are drawn from documented occupational
gender associations to create ecologically valid test conditions.
The personality frameworks used (HEXACO and Dark Triad) originate from
Western psychology and may not fully represent personality constructs
across all cultural contexts, a limitation we acknowledge explicitly.

Finally, all experiments were conducted using publicly available or
API-accessible language models. No private user data was collected or
used at any stage of this study.

We used AI-based writing assistants to improve clarity and language in the manuscript; however, all research ideas, methodological decisions, analyses, and interpretations are solely those of the authors.

Occupational Prompt Design

Gender-Stereotype Classification of Occupations

The occupations listed in Table
7
are categorized based on widely observed gender stereotypes in the Indian socio-cultural context, following prior work on occupational gender bias in language models and social perception
1
;
36
.
Specifically, we construct two sets of 25 occupations each, representing predominantly male-stereotyped and female-stereotyped roles.
These categorizations are not intended to reflect actual workforce distributions or normative judgments, but rather to capture commonly held societal associations that may influence language model behavior.

In the Indian context, occupational roles are often strongly gender-coded due to historical labor divisions and socio-cultural norms
24
;
12
. For example, professions such as construction work, driving, and mechanical labor are commonly associated with men, whereas caregiving, domestic work, and service-oriented roles are more frequently associated with women. Prior work has shown that such associations are reflected and often amplified in language technologies, particularly in multilingual settings involving Hindi and English.

Each occupation is paired with a corresponding artifact and scenario (e.g., lesson plans, reports, logs), providing contextual grounding for generation and ensuring ecological validity of the produced narratives. As described in Section 3.1, these occupations are used both in baseline prompts (without personality conditioning) and in persona-conditioned settings, allowing us to isolate the contribution of personality traits to gender bias beyond occupational priors.

We emphasize that these labels represent stereotypes rather than ground truth, and are included solely to facilitate bias measurement. The goal is not to reinforce such associations, but to systematically evaluate how they are reproduced or amplified in LLM-generated content.

A.3
Model Selection Rationale

To ensure that our findings are not specific to a single model architecture, we evaluate a diverse set of contemporary language models spanning different design paradigms and capability levels.
The selected models, that are, GPT-5 nano, Llama-3.3-70B-Instruct, Gemma-3-1B-it, DeepSeek-R1, Mixtral-8x7B-Instruct, and Falcon-Mamba-7B-Instruct represent different architectural families, including standard transformer-based
llm
, small language models, reasoning-oriented models, mixture-of-experts architectures, and state-space models.
This diversity allows us to examine whether personality-conditioned gender bias patterns generalize across fundamentally different model designs rather than being an artifact of a specific architecture.
We include models with different parameter sizes and training objectives to assess whether the interaction between personality conditioning and gender bias is consistent across models with different capacities and inductive biases.

Choice of Sentence Embedding Model

Our bias metric relies on sentence embeddings to measure semantic similarity between generated artifacts and gender-stereotypical reference centroids.
Since our study focuses on Hindi and Indian occupational contexts, we use
IndicSBERT
, a multilingual sentence encoder trained specifically on a large collection of Indian languages.
IndicSBERT has been shown to better capture semantic relationships in Indian languages compared to general-purpose multilingual encoders
5
.

We also experimented with LaBSE, a widely used multilingual sentence embedding model
9
.
However, preliminary experiments indicated that LaBSE produced less distinct separation between male- and female-aligned similarity distributions across personality conditions.
In contrast, IndicSBERT yielded clearer and more stable bias patterns, hence, we use IndicSBERT as the primary encoder in our bias computation pipeline.

A.5
Gendered Stereotypical Words

Validation of Gender Associations in the Embedding Space

To quantify gender associations in the embedding space, we computed a WEAT-style Cohen’s d effect size measuring the differential association between male and female target words and gender-associated attribute sentences.
Target word embeddings were generated using the IndicSBERT model.
For each target word, an association score was calculated as the difference between its mean cosine similarity with male attribute sentences and its mean cosine similarity with female attribute sentences.
Attribute sentences were constructed by inserting gender-associated adjectives and verbs into simple sentence templates (e.g., “This is _”).
The effect size was then computed as the difference between the mean association scores of the male and female target word groups, normalized by the pooled standard deviation of all association scores.
All embeddings were L2-normalized prior to cosine similarity computation.

The resulting Cohen’s d effect sizes indicate strong gender association patterns in both Hindi (d =
0.9302
) and English (d =
0.9447
), both exceeding the conventional threshold for a large effect size (d ¿ 0.80).
These results suggest that gender-associated semantic patterns are strongly encoded in the model’s multilingual embedding space for both languages.

A.7
Annotation Guidelines and Inter-Annotator Agreement

Annotators were presented with pairs of LLM-generated first-person
narratives (Story A and Story B). Each pair describes a character of
the
same occupation and gender
, generated under two
conditions: a
baseline
prompt and a

personality-conditioned
prompt based on the HEXACO and Dark Triad
framework. The task was to judge
which story exhibits more
gender stereotyping
.

The task followed a
forced pairwise comparison
design.
A “neither” option was deliberately excluded to enforce a
discriminative judgment in every case, as relative comparisons reduce
subjectivity relative to absolute rating scales.

Annotators were given the following definition:

Gender stereotyping is making assumptions about a person’s
behavior, emotions, abilities, or role based solely on their
gender, not their individual skills, choices, or character.
