1. Executive Summary
Renewable alcohols derived from air, water, and renewable energy offer a transformative
solution for utilizing captured atmospheric carbon dioxide to produce liquid fuels. Among these,
methanol stands out as a superior C1 product due to its liquid state, high energy density, and
versatile applications. With global methanol consumption reaching 64 million metric tons
annually and over 35% used directly as fuel, methanol generates an impressive $55 billion in
economic activity, making it a key player in the transition to sustainable energy.
This project pioneered a groundbreaking reactor design to produce green methanol by
harnessing the electrochemical CO₂ reduction reaction (eCO₂RR), a cornerstone of power-to-
fuels technology. The effort integrated three innovative technologies to achieve carbon-neutral
methanol production at a target cost of under $800/ton:
 1. Direct Air Capture (DAC): Using a cutting-edge sorbent material developed at
 Holocene, scalable models were developed to integrate captured atmospheric CO₂ into
 the reactor system.
 2. Intermediate-Temperature CO₂ Electrolyzer: Developed by the University of
 Tennessee (UTK), this electrolyzer utilizes a cost-effective, proton-conducting solid acid
 electrolyte (CsH₂PO₄, CDP) and a mixed-metal oxide cathode. It achieves high faradaic
 efficiencies (>98%) by effectively suppressing hydrogen evolution at high current
 densities, converting CO₂ to CO with remarkable selectivity.
 3. Catalysis and Reactor Engineering: Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL)
 contributed world-class expertise in heterogeneous catalysis and reactor design. Their
 advanced ASPEN modeling drove systems integration and supported techno-economic
 and life cycle analyses.
This effort was further bolstered by partnerships with industry leaders Air Company and Plug
Power, who provided critical guidance on scaling, systems engineering, and the integration of
water electrolyzers into large-scale operations.
During Phase 1, the team focused on modeling and validating a lab-scale reactor demonstrating
the feasibility of the integrated approach. Key accomplishments include a 52% increase in
current density at 0.8 V while maintaining >98% CO faradaic efficiency, successful 10× scale-up
of the electrolyzer with performance within 5% of coin-cell results, best-in-class durability (168-
hour test at 0.6 V with 0.14 mA/cm²-h degradation), validated TEA confirming the $800/ton
methanol target, and completed preliminary LCA showing potential for net-negative GHG
emissions under renewable energy scenarios.

3. CO₂ Electrolyzer Development and Performance
Phase 1 experimental efforts for the CO₂ electrolyzer had three primary goals: improvements in
electrode microstructure, increasing the scale of the benchtop electrolyzer, and improvements in
durability. These are discussed in detail below.

3.1 Electrode Microstructure Improvements
Optimizing tri-phase boundaries by reducing the particle size of the electrolyte/electrode support
enhanced catalyst utilization. This strategy led to a 52% increase in current density (rate of CO
production) at 0.8 V while maintaining a faradaic efficiency of CO generation of over 98%.
Experimental efforts addressed electrode porosity, catalyst dispersion, and nano-templating of
CDP electrolyte.

3.3 Electrolyzer Scale-Up

 Page 5

NETL Final Technical Report — DE-FE0032415

The electrolyzer was scaled up from a cell area of approximately 3 cm² to 25 cm² during Phase
1 to assess the reproducibility of performance for larger cells. Good agreement (within 7%) in
CO production was found as judged by current density at a given voltage. Comparable to results
obtained with analogous fuel cell studies, the scaled-up electrolyzer performance was within 5%
of the coin-cell-sized studies.
Scaling from bench to industrial scale presents additional challenges. Key insights from the
Nelson et al. (2024) study on scaling CO₂ electrolyzers highlight that maintaining consistent
current density and faradaic efficiency across an expanded electrode surface area requires
attention to compression uniformity and CO₂ diffusion limitations. Scaling from 5 cm² to 800 cm²
active area resulted in a 20% drop in selectivity due to CO₂ starvation effects. A multi-channel
serpentine flow field design can mitigate these issues by ensuring uniform gas distribution while
preventing stagnant CO₂ zones that lead to excessive hydrogen evolution.

Figure 1. Cell hardware and sample cells showing Phase 1 scaleup efforts.

3.3.1 Cell Geometry Considerations
Circular electrolyzer cells are advantageous for catalyst screening and development due to
uniform current and potential distribution, consistent mass transport conditions, easier sealing
and fabrication, efficient use of small electrode samples, and simplified electrolyte and gas flow
control. However, rectangular cells are preferred for stack-scale deployment due to higher
packing efficiency, easier modular stacking, simplified manifold and flow field design, better
manufacturing material utilization, improved structural integrity under compression, and easier
integration with balance-of-plant systems.

3.4 Durability Testing
Most reported studies of CO₂ electrolyzers show cell performance with very limited
potentiostatic holds, often for less than 4 hours, making it difficult to adequately benchmark and
forecast long-term performance critical for commercial applications. During Phase 1, the team
used chronoamperometry as a diagnostic, holding the cell voltage at 0.6 V corresponding to a
current density of approximately 553 mA cm⁻², still over 2× higher than a commercial target rate
of CO production.
In a 168-hour test, a degradation rate of 0.14 mA/cm²-h was observed. Based on competing
electrolyzers, this level of stable operation suggests best-in-class performance. While that rate
is higher than what would be commercially viable, it represents an important step forward and
provides a baseline for accelerated stress test development.

4. Modeling Studies
4.1 COMSOL Flow-Field Modeling
COMSOL was used to model current distribution in the CO₂ electrode and gas flow through
serpentine flow fields. The simulations provided insight into how the CO₂ electrolyzer must
evolve from bench to stack scale.
The lab model relied on a simple dead-end channel, which quickly becomes transport-limited:
CO₂ is depleted at the inlet while H₂ evolution accelerates near the outlet, depressing faradaic
efficiency and per-pass conversion. Introducing a serpentine manifold distributes the gas more
uniformly, lengthens the reactant path, and maintains shear that sweeps product bubbles away
from the catalyst.
At projected stack flow rates, this geometry balances three competing targets CO₂ utilization,
single-pass conversion, and FE by mitigating thickness-wise concentration gradients inside the
gas-diffusion electrode. Ongoing work is extending these 2-D results into full 3-D models and
testing alternative interdigitated patterns to further reduce pressure drop while preserving mass-
transfer gains.
The CO₂ consumption profile illustrates how the reactant is depleted as it flows through
serpentine channels. Sharp concentration variations along the flow path indicate regions of high
electrochemical activity and highlight that mass transport limitations could arise if reactant flow
is not properly managed. The CO mole fraction distribution across the cathode shows gradient
from high CO concentration near the inlet to lower concentration at the outlet, suggesting that
reactant depletion and product accumulation impact system performance.

Figure 2. Sample COMSOL modeling results illustrating flow field design and reaction yield /
uniformity across the cathode for electrochemical reduction of CO₂.

4.2 ASPEN/HYSYS Process Modeling
The ASPEN/HYSYS model translates the electrolyzer’s CO-rich effluent into methanol-synthesis
reactor design rules. A 62 mol% H₂ / 31 mol% CO / 6 mol% CO₂ feed at 250°C and 75 bar

enters a cooled, tube-in-shell reactor where exothermic hydrogenation drives 40% of the gas
phase into liquid methanol.
By the reactor outlet, the syngas has shifted to approximately 18 mol% CO, 37 mol% H₂, and 10
mol% CO₂, while methanol reaches 34 mol%. A downstream flash condenser operating near
ambient pressure polishes the product to >99.6 mol% methanol at 130 kg/h, with residual light
gases recycled.
The heat removal and cooling system maintains reactor stability with an outlet cooling duty of
299.8 MJ/h. The process leverages a multi-stage cooling approach to facilitate methanol
condensation while minimizing energy losses. The methanol product stream at 25°C and 101.3
kPa contains 99.63 mol% methanol, confirming high product purity. The overall process
achieves a high methanol yield (185.3 kg/h) with minimal impurities.
Explicitly accounting for the electrolyzer’s CO fraction lets the model size heat-removal duty,
pressure-relief capacity, and recycle ratios without resorting to idealized stoichiometric feeds,
ensuring the hydrogenation section remains robust to electrolyzer upsets or intentional
operating-point shifts.

4.3 Scale-Up Roadmap from Modeling
Taken together, the two models delineate a clear scale-up roadmap. Electrolyzer expansion will
proceed from 15 cm² single cells to 50–125 cm² plates arrayed in stacks using the serpentine
field proven in COMSOL; aluminum or stainless frames with polymer gaskets could be used.
Maintaining the modeled flow regime at stack level requires modest blower power (<2 kPa ΔP)
and humidified CO₂ at approximately 3% RH, conditions readily supplied by the DAC unit.
On the synthesis side, the ASPEN case will be re-run with larger stack throughputs to validate
that reactor cooling and separation equipment still meet the 1,000 t/yr methanol target. By
iterating between the multiphysics and process models, the team can converge on an integrated

5. Key Design Features for a Scaled-Up Integrated Reactor
Scaling up the integrated reactor for producing green methanol from DAC CO₂, solid acid
electrolysis, and methanol synthesis requires a meticulously designed system to ensure high
efficiency, economic viability, and sustainable operation. The following subsections detail the
key design features necessary for successful scaling.

5.1 Integrated Process Looping and Reactor Configuration
To maximize CO₂ utilization and minimize energy losses, the reactor employs a closed-loop
system that efficiently recycles unreacted gases and intermediates. The DAC unit captures
atmospheric CO₂ using an amino acid-based sorbent, which is then desorbed and fed into a
solid acid CO₂ electrolyzer operating at 250°C. The produced CO is subsequently combined
with hydrogen from a PEM electrolyzer for methanol synthesis.
Key looping strategies include:
 • CO₂ Recycling: Since the per-pass CO₂ conversion in the electrolyzer is typically low
 (5–10%), unconverted CO₂ must be separated and recirculated to improve overall
 utilization.
 • CO Recycle Loop: Given that CO conversion to methanol is not 100% per pass, a
 looping configuration ensures that unreacted CO is recycled and reintroduced into the
 reactor.
 • H₂ Optimization: Hydrogen supply from PEM electrolysis is dynamically adjusted based
 on CO₂ electrolysis output, ensuring a stoichiometric balance that minimizes excess
 hydrogen loss.

5.2 Optimized Per-Pass Conversion Efficiencies
 • CO₂-to-CO Electrolysis: The solid acid electrolyzer operates at 750 mA/cm² and 0.8 V,
 achieving approximately 75% conversion efficiency per pass. A significant portion of CO₂
 remains unconverted and requires recycling.
 • CO-to-Methanol Conversion: CO and H₂ react over a Cu/ZnO/Al₂O₃ catalyst at 50–100
 bar and 200–300°C. Per-pass CO conversion efficiency is typically 40–60%,
 necessitating gas separation and recycling.
 • Hydrogen Utilization: A PSA unit or membrane-based separation system recovers
 excess hydrogen for recycling back into the methanol reactor.

5.3 Thermal Integration and Heat Recovery
 • Heat Recovery from Electrolysis: The solid acid electrolyzer operates at 250°C,
 providing an opportunity to preheat the DAC desorption unit, reducing external heating
 requirements.
 • Exothermic Methanol Synthesis: The methanol reactor generates significant heat that
 can drive DAC CO₂ desorption or provide heat to the electrolyzer preheating system.
 • Steam Generation: Water produced in methanol synthesis and electrolysis waste heat
 can generate steam for thermal DAC regeneration or process heat.

5.4 Reactor Design for High Selectivity and Durability
 • Catalyst Optimization: The Cu/ZnO/Al₂O₃ catalyst must be optimized for high CO
 conversion selectivity to minimize side product formation (higher alcohols and
 hydrocarbons).
 • Electrolyzer Stability: Electrodes must be engineered to withstand long-term operation
 at high current densities, minimizing degradation and electrolyte leaching.
 • Gas Purity Management: A gas purification and drying system ensures that only high-
 purity CO and H₂ enter the methanol reactor.

5.5 Scalability and Process Control
 • Modular Reactor Design: Electrolyzers and methanol reactors are scaled using parallel
 or stacked configurations, allowing gradual expansion without significant re-engineering.
 • Automated Process Control: Advanced process control monitors reaction
 temperatures, pressures, gas flow rates, and catalyst activity for real-time optimization.
 • Dynamic Load Management: Electrolysis and DAC processes are dynamically
 adjusted based on renewable energy availability for efficient intermittent operation.

6. Techno-Economic Analysis
To ensure economic viability, the reactor design balances capital costs (CAPEX) and operating
costs (OPEX) while targeting competitive methanol production costs at or below $800/ton.

6.1 DAC System Economics
CO₂ capture energy requirements are estimated at 1.8 kWh/kg CO₂ for the preliminary analysis.
Two candidate DAC systems were evaluated:

6.1.1 m-BBIG Amino Acid-Based DAC System
This system uses aqueous amino acids (potassium glycinate, potassium sarcosinate) combined
with solid bis-iminoguanidines (BIGs). The technology offers lower regeneration temperatures
(60–120°C), enabling use of low-grade waste heat or renewable energy. The estimated
regeneration energy is 360 kJ/mol CO₂ (8.2 GJ/ton CO₂), and the m-BBIG sorbent costs
approximately $2/kg.

6.3 PEM Electrolyzer
CapEx for PEM electrolyzers is estimated at $1,000–$2,500 per m². The estimated H₂
requirement is 1.15 × 10⁸ moles (approximately 230 tons). The electrical energy required to
produce hydrogen is approximately 50 kWh per kilogram of H₂ for a PEM electrolyzer, giving
1.15 × 10⁷ kWh/year or an electrolyzer with power capacity of 1.31 MW. At typical power
densities of approximately 2 kW/m², the required electrolyzer size is around 654 m².

6.4 Overall System Economics
Energy consumption optimization is paramount given that electrolysis energy requirements
dominate production costs. Key economic strategies include maximizing system efficiency
through process integration, using earth-abundant catalysts and corrosion-resistant materials to
reduce long-term costs, and exploring additional revenue streams such as carbon credits or
government subsidies. The TEA validates the $800/ton MeOH target at pilot scale (1,000 t/yr),
confirming the economic feasibility of the integrated system design.

7. Life Cycle Assessment
7.1 Preliminary LCA
A high-level carbon and energy balance for the integrated reactor was conducted. Key
assumptions include that CO₂ is captured and fed into the CO₂ electrolyzer after desorption
from the DAC sorbent (effectively storing CO₂ as methanol in the power-to-fuels process), and
that both thermal and electrical energy are generated from renewable sources.
The GHG emissions of this process are expected to be negative assuming energy inputs come
from renewable electricity and heat produced by hydrogen combustion. In the FOA and
Appendix M provided by NETL, two scenarios were considered:

Thermal energy requirements represent approximately 6% of total energy (thermal + electricity),
so only slight increases in GHG are expected from thermal contributions.

8. Technology Gap Analysis
8.1 Process Overview
The integrated process combines four major components: Direct Air Capture using an amino
acid-based sorbent system; CO₂ electrolysis using a CsH₂PO₄-based solid acid electrolyzer
operating at 250°C (750 mA/cm², 0.8 V); PEM water electrolysis supplying high-purity hydrogen;
and methanol synthesis via catalytic reactor at elevated temperatures and pressures. This
system creates a closed-loop, potentially carbon-negative fuel cycle.

8.3 Detailed Component Assessments
8.3.1 Direct Air Capture (DAC)
Several experimental setups have demonstrated successful CO₂ capture with >90% efficiency
using amino acid-based sorbents. However, long-term sorbent stability and regeneration
efficiency require further validation. Required research for scale-up includes testing at larger
scales to evaluate mass and energy balance, optimization of sorbent regeneration conditions to
reduce energy input, and integration with downstream CO₂ electrolysis.

8.3.2 CO₂ Electrolysis
The solid acid electrolysis system has demonstrated stable operation at 250°C, achieving 75%
CO₂-to-CO conversion efficiency. Primary challenges involve improving catalyst stability and
reducing system degradation. Required advances include development of durable electrode and
electrolyte materials, balancing per-pass conversion efficiencies, testing at higher current
densities (>1 A/cm²), and integration with industrial-scale power electronics.

8.3.3 PEM Water Electrolysis
Commercial PEM electrolyzers are available (TRL 7–9), but improvements in durability and
efficiency under dynamic operation coupled with variable renewable energy sources are
needed. Pre-commercial systems at scale for 1,000 tons methanol per year require some
modification of existing larger commercial systems.

8.3.4 Methanol Synthesis
TRL 9 systems exist for stand-alone reactors, but challenges remain in catalyst longevity and
reaction kinetics at lower pressures. Required research includes development of catalysts with
higher selectivity and resistance to poisoning, reactor design optimization for efficient heat
management, and integration with CO₂ electrolysis output gas composition.

10. Conclusions and Future Work
10.1 Summary of Key Findings
Phase 1 of this project has successfully demonstrated the feasibility of an integrated system for
producing green methanol from atmospheric CO₂ via direct air capture, solid acid CO₂
electrolysis, and catalytic CO hydrogenation. The major findings are:
 4. The CDP-based CO₂ electrolyzer achieved 750 mA/cm² at 0.8 V with >98% CO faradaic
 efficiency, representing a 52% improvement in current density over baseline.
 5. Successful 10× scale-up (3 cm² to 15 cm²) with performance within 5–7% of coin-cell
 results demonstrates scalability of the electrode architecture.
 6. A 168-hour durability test at 0.6 V yielded a degradation rate of 0.14 mA/cm²-h,
 representing best-in-class stability among comparable electrolyzers.
 7. COMSOL and ASPEN/HYSYS modeling established a clear scale-up roadmap from lab
 cells to 50–125 cm² stack plates targeting 1,000 t/yr methanol production.
 8. TEA validates the $800/ton methanol cost target at pilot scale, with electrolyzer CapEx
      of $1,850–$4,000/m² and DAC costs of $280–$493/ton CO₂.
 9. Preliminary LCA indicates net-negative GHG emissions (−0.91 kg CO₂e/kg MeOH) when
 powered entirely by renewable electricity.

10.2 Remaining Challenges
The most critical gaps exist in CO₂ electrolysis and DAC integration. Specifically:
 • Electrolyzer degradation rate must decrease from approximately 3%/1,000 h to
 <1%/1,000 h for commercial viability
 • Single-pass CO₂ conversion must be improved from 5–10% to reduce recycle costs
 • DAC sorbent stability and regeneration efficiency require further validation at pilot scale
 • Catalyst longevity in the methanol synthesis unit needs optimization under realistic feed
 compositions

10.3 Path Forward
The project has enabled a clear picture of the strengths and weaknesses of the system
components in meeting objectives both as a system and as individual components. Future work
should focus on advancing the CO₂ electrolyzer to larger active areas (50–125 cm²),
demonstrating integrated operation of all three subsystems, and validating long-term durability
under realistic operating conditions. Successful commercialization will rely on strategic
partnerships with technology providers and continued investment in advanced catalytic and
electrochemical processes.
The team elected not to target Phase II/pilot-scale funding; however, the comprehensive
