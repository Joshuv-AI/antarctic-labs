Differential regulation of autophagy and metabolic pathways by dietary methionine and lysine deficiency in goats
Authors: Shabbir Ahmed, Jian Wu, Zixin Liu, Aoyu Jiang, Chuanshe Zhou, Zhiliang Tan, Liang Chen, Jinhe Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research aimed to explore the effect and mechanism of dietary methionine and lysine deficiency on autophagy of gastrointestinal tract and liver tissues. Thirty goats were divided into three groups: control (Con), methionine deficiency (MD), and lysine deficiency (LD) groups. The expression levels of autophagy marker proteins LC3 and p62 were analyzed by immunohistochemistry (n = 5), and mRNA expression levels of autophagy-related genes ATF4, FOXO3, Beclin-1, Atg13 and eIF2α were analyzed through qPCR. No significant differences were found for LC3, but optical density for p62 of jejunal mucosa in LD was significantly higher (P < 0.01) than Con and MD. The mRNA expression level of rumen epithelial ATF4 in LD was significantly higher compared to control and MD, but FOXO3 in LD was significantly higher than control (P < 0.01). In the jejunal mucosa, LD had the highest ATF4 mRNA expression level, while Beclin-1 mRNA expression level was lowest (P < 0.01) compared to control. Metabolomics of jejunal mucosa showed that sorbitol and N-acetylglucosamine in MD and DL-Homocystine and eight other metabolites in LD were significantly increased compared to control. Overall, the degradation process of autophagy was blocked in the jejunum when the diet was lacking of lysine in jejunum, and ATF4 was upregulated to maintain the metabolic balance of amino acids in rumen, jejunum and ileum. Introduction

Autophagy is a catabolic process where eukaryotic cells sequester cargo in autophagosomes for lysosomal degradation, maintaining metabolic balance [1]. This evolutionarily conserved starvation response includes macroautophagy, microautophagy, and chaperone-mediated autophagy [2, 3]. Macroautophagy is the most characterized pathway, whereas the degraded components are wrapped by autophagosome and then transferred to the lysosome for degradation [4, 5]. Autophagy supplies energy to produce new proteins and membranes, essential for the survival and adaptation of cells during starvation [6–8].

Amino acids, the basic elements of protein synthesis, are essential for growth and development [9, 10]. When amino acids are deficient, autophagy and other unfavourable body reactions are induced [11, 12]. Deprivation of amino acids in perfused rat liver activated autophagy [13, 14]. While amino acids typically suppress autophagy, their specific effects vary in the gastrointestinal tract and liver due to the unique metabolism and nutrient availability in each body part [15, 16].

Methionine and lysine are the first and second limiting amino acids for goats in corn soybean meal diet model, and have a positive effect on the growth performance as well as physiological metabolism [17]. Methionine and lysine deficiency played crucial roles in cellular processes, especially in protein synthesis. Methionine regulates methylation of DNA, protein, lipid and oxidative stress in cells. Lysine deficiency disrupts cellular homeostasis and protein turnover [18, 19]. Methionine participates in the synthesis of peptides and proteins, regulates related gene expression in the animal body, and produces S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) under the action of methionine adenosyltransferase [20]. Lysine is a ketogenic and sugar-producing amino acid in animals, involved in lipid metabolism and protein synthesis. It is one of the main energy substances in animals during starvation, and plays an important role in enhancing the body’s resistance [21]. In previous research, methionine and lysine deficiencies have been imposed at levels ranging from zero to a certain percentage of the animal’s requirements. In studies utilizing rumen-protected methionine (RPMet) and lysine (RPLys), researchers typically adopt a relative deficiency paradigm. The basal diet is intentionally formulated to supply the specific percentage of the animal’s metabolizable protein (MP) requirements for methionine and lysine [22, 23].

In ruminants, the relationship between specific amino acid deficiencies and tissue-specific autophagy responses remains poorly understood. A previous study has indicated that different nitrogen sources (e.g., non-protein nitrogen vs. amino acids like methionine) differentially regulated autophagy and apoptosis in rumen epithelial cells [24]. However, a comprehensive understanding of the underlying molecular mechanisms and the comparative impact of lacking methionine and lysine on autophagy regulation in goats is still unclear. We hypothesized that dietary methionine and lysine deficiency had different effects on autophagy in goats. The present research was focused on the effect of methionine and lysine deficiencies on the autophagy of gastrointestinal tract, liver tissues and metabolomics of jejunum tissues in goats.

Materials and methods

Ethical approval

All animal experiment procedures were accomplished in accordance with IACUC (Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee); approval number: ISA-2020-0096.

Source of the animals

All experimental goats used in this study were purchased from a local commercial breeding farm.

Eutanesia method

Goats were transported to the experimental slaughter room and humanely euthanized by intravenous injection of an overdose sodium pentobarbital.

Experimental design and feeding management

Thirty healthy growing Xiangdong black goats, average body weight of 18 ± 0.5 kg, were randomly divided into three groups: control group (Con), fed with a normal diet; methionine deficiency group (MD); lysine deficiency group (LD). The diets were prepared according to NRC [25]. The addition of methionine and lysine in the control group was based on the amino acid pattern of growing goat muscle (methionine: lysine 3:1). 0.253 g of effective methionine and 0.5 g of lysine per 100 g of basal diet (DM basis) were added. To achieve the corresponding low amino acid content and amino acid deficiency group, lysine and methionine supplementation in the MD and LD groups were subtracted. MD and LD groups were supplemented with 0.5 and 0.204 g urea per 100 g (DM basis) respectively, to avoid differences in dietary N content due to the addition of amino acids. Diet ingredients and nutrient compositions are shown in (Table 1). Crude protein (CP, method 954.01; CP = N × 6.25) was determined according to AOAC 2004 [26]. Neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and Acid detergent fiber (ADF) were measured as described by Van Soest et al. [27].

The rumen-protected methionine (RPMet) and rumen-protected lysine (RPLys) used in this study were supplied by Evonil Industries AG (Hanan, Germany) and Kemin Industries, Inc. (Iowa, USA), respectively. The RPMet contained 85% DL- methionine whereas the coating material was ethyl cellulose, and the RPLys contained 47.5% lysine hydrochloride including 52.5% lipids. Rumen passage rates for RPMet and RPLys were > 80% and 55–66%, moreover, small intestine digestion and absorption rates were > 90% and 90–95%, respectively. The ratio of concentrate to roughage was 45: 55. The adaptation period was 10 d and the experimental period was 30 d. Goats were accessed for ad libitum feeding and drinking, and daily residual feed was 5%.

Sample collection

At the end of the experiment period, all goats were slaughtered, chest and abdomen were opened, gastrointestinal tract and liver were taken out and placed on ice. Liver, rumen, jejunum and ileum samples were taken, rinsed with 4 °C saline solution, and fixed in 10% formalin solution for immunohistochemistry analysis. Liver, rumen and ileum samples were taken again, rinsed with saline solution, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and then stored to -80 °C for RT-PCR analysis. The jejunum sample was collected and stored at -80 ℃ for RT-PCR and non-targeted metabolomics analysis.

Immunohistochemistry

Collected tissue samples (n = 5) were fixed with 4% buffered formaldehyde for 24 h. Then tissue samples were subjected to dehydration by successive immersion in ascending concentration of alcohols (50%, 75%, 85%, 95% and 100%), xylene and finally embedded in paraffin. Serial sections were made using a rotary microtome (Leica, Germany) with a thickness of 5 μm from the paraffin block, to prepare slides [29]. Slides were dewaxed and blocked with 3% H2O2 (Endogenous peroxidase activity) and then, 10% bovine serum (non-specific binding) at room temperature for 30 min. Subsequently, the slides were incubated with primary antibodies (supplementary Table S1) at 4 °C overnight. Then, the slides were cleaned in PBS solution (3 × 5 min) and incubated with biotinylated anti-rabbit IgG antibody (Birmingham, AL, USA), and diluted 1:200 in PAV buffer for 50 min at 4 °C. Slides were washed with PBS buffer and stained with diaminobenzidine tetrahydrochloride (DAB). Immunostained slides were examined with an optical microscope.

RNA extraction and qPCR

Total RNA extraction from samples was performed using ScriptTM KT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) to convert RNA into cDNA according to the manufacturer’s instructions and obtained cDNA was stored at -20 ℃ for subsequent qPCR. The expression of autophagy-related genes was determined using SYBR® Premix Ex Taq qPCR Kit (TaKaRa, Dalian, China) based on a Light Cycler 480 system (Roche, Castle Hill, NSW, Australia) according to the manufacture’s specification [30]. The primer information was shown in supplementary Table S2. The β-actin was used to normalize the relative expression levels of target genes, and the 2−∆∆Ct method was used for data analysis, as previously described [31].

Non-targeted metabolomics

Six jejunal tissue samples from each group were used for analysis. 50 mg of ground jejunal tissue samples were taken to a 1.5 ml of EP tube. Two extraction solutions were prepared by mixing methanol, acetonitrile and water in a ratio of 2:2:1 (extraction solution I) and acetonitrile and water in a ratio of 1:1 (extraction solution II). These solutions were stored at 4 ℃ for later use. To extract the metabolites of the jejunal tissue samples, 1000 µl of extraction solution I was added to each sample containing EP tube. Samples sonicated for 5–7 min at ice-water bath. The ultra-sonicated samples were placed in a -20 ℃ refrigerator for 60 min and centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 ℃. The supernatant was transferred to a 1.5 ml EP tube for freeze drying and concentration. 100 µl of extraction solution II was added to freeze dried concentrated sample, homogenized for 30s, sonicated for 10–12 min at ice-water bath then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 ℃. After microporous filtration, supernatant was taken into glass vials for metabolomics. For the accuracy of the analysis, a quality control (QC) sample was prepared by mixing equal aliquots of supernatants from all samples. Metabolites were analyzed through Ultra High Performance Liquid Chromatography (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with Quadrupole Time-of-Flight Mass Spectrometry (UHPLC Q-TOF MS). Q-TOF MS allowed accurate automated mass measurement of product ions for metabolomics. All MS data were acquired with reference masses via negative and positive ESI modes to ensure the mass accuracy and reproducibility. The metabolites expressed differentially were recognized with variable importance for projection (VIP) values > 1.0 and adjusted P-values < 0.05.

Data statistics

The data were analyzed by SAS (version 9.2) for analysis of variance (ANOVA), and the differences between mean values were determined by Student’s t-test. The significance of the difference was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

Gene and protein expression profiles of gastrointestinal tract and liver tissues under dietary methionine and lysine deficiency

Rumen epithelium

It was found that dietary methionine and lysine deficiency did not affect the protein expression levels of autophagy-related proteins LC3 (microtubule-associated protein 1 light-chain 3) and p62 (protein Sequestosome 1) (Fig. 1A, B) in rumen epithelium of goats (P > 0.05). The mRNA expression level of ATF4 (activating transcription factor 4) was significantly higher in the rumen epithelium of goats in LD group compared to control and MD groups (P < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). Meanwhile, the mRNA expression level of FOXO3 (forkhead box O3) in LD group was significantly higher than control group, but there are no difference between LD and MD group (P < 0.05). However, dietary methionine and lysine deficiency had no significant effect on the mRNA expression levels of Beclin-1, Atg13 (autophagy-related genes 13), and eIF2α (eukaryotic initiation factor 2α) in the rumen epithelium of goats (P > 0.05).

Jejunum mucosa

Dietary methionine and lysine deficiency did not affect the protein expression levels of autophagy-related proteins LC3 (Fig. 1C, D) in jejunum mucosa of goats (P > 0.05). It was found that dietary methionine deficiency has no significant effect on the protein expression levels of autophagy-related proteins p62 in the jejunum mucosa, while lysine deficiency significantly increased (P < 0.01) the p62 content in the jejunum mucosa (Fig. 1C, D). Simultaneously, the LD group (Fig. 2B) had the highest mRNA expression level of ATF4 (P < 0.01), while the mRNA expression level of Beclin-1 was lowest (P < 0.01). However, dietary methionine and lysine deficiency had no effect on the mRNA expression levels of FOXO3, Atg13 and eIF2α in the jejunum mucosa (P > 0.05).

Ileum mucosa

It was found that dietary methionine and lysine deficiency did not affect the protein expression levels of autophagy-related proteins LC3 and p62 (Fig. 1E, F) in ileum mucosa of goats (P > 0.05). Meanwhile, the mRNA expression level ATF4 in LD group was significantly higher compared to control and MD group (P < 0.01). Regardless, dietary methionine and lysine deficiency had no significant effect on the mRNA expression levels of FOXO3, Beclin-1, Atg13, and eIF2α in the ileum mucosa of goats (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2C).

Liver tissue

Dietary methionine and lysine deficiency did not affect the protein expression levels of autophagy-related proteins LC3 and p62 (Fig. 1G, H) in liver tissues of goats (P > 0.05). Similarly, dietary methionine and lysine deficiency had no significant effect on the mRNA expression levels of ATF4, FOXO3, Beclin-1, Atg13 and eIF2α in the liver tissues of goats (P > 0.05) (Fig. 2D).

Metabolomics analysis of jejunal mucosa under dietary methionine and lysine deficiency

The orthogonal partial least squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) showed clear separations in metabolite profiles between MD group and the control group, and also between LD group and the control group (Fig. 3A-B). Furthermore, the distribution of differential metabolites between different groups were visualized through a volcano plot (Fig. 3C, D). Compared with MD and the control group, the levels of sorbitol and N-acetylglucosamine were increased significantly (P < 0.05), on the other hand, twelve metabolites, including 5-aminopentanoic acid, D-citrulline, valeric acid, phenylethylamine, resveratrol, Met-Ala, Ile-His, Leu-Ala, etc., were decreased significantly in the jejunal tissue (Table 2). Furthermore, compared with LD and the control group, levels of nine metabolites comprising dimethyluric acid, Leu-Ala, homocysteine etc., were increased significantly P < 0.05), conversely, levels of seven metabolites, comprising 5-aminopentanoic acid, L-cystine etc., were decreased significantly in the jejunal tissue (Table 3). Key metabolic pathways with the highest correlation involved in the jejunal mucosa were shown with bubble charts (Fig. 3E, F). KEGG analysis of the differential metabolites between the MD group and the control group showed that the metabolic pathways mainly involved in four metabolic pathways: D-arginine and D-ornithine metabolism, phenylalanine metabolism, pentose phosphate pathway and amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism pathways (Fig. 3E). However, the metabolic pathways of differential metabolites between the LD group and the control group included cysteine and methionine metabolism and purine metabolism (Fig. 3F).

Discussions

It was found that more than 40 autophagy-related genes (Atg), corresponding proteins, protein complexes and associated pathways play main role in different phases of autophagy [2, 11, 32, 33]. Among various Atg core proteins, the MAP1LC3 (microtubule-associated protein 1 light-chain 3, referred as LC3), protein Sequestosome 1 (p62/SQSTM1) are essential for the biogenesis, elongation and maturation of the autophagosome [34]. It is well known that p62 is a substrate molecule for autophagy and also used as an autophagy marker molecule to detect autophagic flux [35]. The protein degradation process caused by autophagy, occurs mainly due to p62. At the same time, p62 operates as a scaffold and adaptor protein in the cell signaling process [36]. p62 directly interacts with the cell autophagy marker molecule LC3 and participates in the formation and degradation of autophagosomes [35, 37].

In the current study, it was found that dietary methionine and lysine deficiency did not affect the protein expression levels of autophagy-related proteins LC3 and p62 in rumen, ileum mucosa and liver tissues of goats, indicating that dietary methionine and lysine deficiency have no significant effect on autophagy in these body parts.

The upregulated expression of p62 and the unchanged expression of LC3 in the jejunum mucosa of LD group in the present study indicated that the formation of autophagosomes was normal, but the degradation process of autophagy was blocked in the jejunum when the diet was lacking of lysine. However, dietary methionine deficiency did not affect the autophagy in the current study. This result was inconsistent with previous studies that lysine suppressed cellular autophagy through the mTORC1 signaling pathway in C2C12 myotubes [38], and amino acid starvation induced cellular autophagy in mammalian cells [39, 40]. These discrepancies may stem from variations in animal species, distinct physiological stages, and differences in the supplemental dosages of amino acids across studies [41, 42].

The decrease in cell autophagy in the jejunum during lysine deficiency is likely due to the jejunum’s primary role in absorbing small nutrient molecules, making it more sensitive to amino acid deprivation than other tissues. As an essential amino acid for protein synthesis in mammals, lysine deprivation significantly reduces the viability of muscle satellite cells, as well as their rates of protein synthesis and cell cycling [43].

The rumen relies on microbial fermentation, and the microbial community is the primary source of nutrient breakdown [44]. Although lysine deficiency would eventually affect the microbes, the rumen’s primary role is not the direct absorption of amino acids, therefore, the immediate impact of lysine deficiency is less pronounced than in the jejunum [45]. The ileum absorbs distinct nutrients such as bile salts and vitamin B12, while the liver possesses more complex regulatory mechanisms that buffer against short-term nutritional deficiencies, including lysine deprivation [46]. Consequently, similar autophagy responses to lysine deprivation were not observed in the rumen, ileum, or liver.

On the other hand, dietary methionine deficiency may not affect autophagy as the rumen microbes can provide enzymes necessary for fermentation of feedstuffs and synthesis of amino acids including methionine [47, 48]. Moreover, some previous research indicated that rumen microbes can convert methionine into other metabolites, which may then be used by the host animal for various cellular functions, such as oxidative stress resistance and antioxidant defenses [22, 49].

Leucine or arginine starved for a long time, mTORC1 activity is reactivated, thereby inhibiting the occurrence of cellular autophagy [50]. Increased p62 concentration also enhanced mTORC1 activity [37]. The gene Beclin-1 is considered a downstream gene of the mTORC1 autophagy regulatory pathway. The reduced mRNA expression level of Beclin-1 in the jejunum of LD group suggested the activation of mTORC1 pathway in this study as mTORC1 inhibits autophagy, and Beclin-1 is a key regulator of autophagy [51].

Autophagy is firmly operated by proteins encoded by autophagy-related genes [34]. ATF4 belongs to the ATF/CREB (activating transcription factor/cyclic AMP response element binding protein) family of basic region-leucine zipper (bZip) transcription factors [52, 53]. As a transcription factor, it plays an important role in adapting to stress, regulating the transcription of multiple genes [54–56]. ATF4 can adapt to stress due to amino acid deficiency by regulating the GCN2/eIF2 pathway [56]. During the formation of autophagosomes, the eIF2α/ATF4 pathway increases the transcription of autophagy related genes to promote autophagy formation, elongation, and autophagosome maturation. In addition, eIF2α can induce the transcription of ATF4 [57]. In the experiment, dietary lysine deficiency significantly increased the expression levels of ATF4 mRNA in rumen and ileum tissues, while the expression levels of eIF2α mRNA in these tissues were not affected compared with the control group. It was shown that ATF4 regulatory mechanism can regulate ATF4 transcription without relying on traditional eIF2α phosphorylation [57]. Therefore, the results in this experiment might indicated that ATF4 had an independent effect on transcription factor eIF2α. Previous studies have shown that when mTORC1 activity was inhibited, the body could reduce ATF4 mRNA levels by increasing the degradation rate of ATF4 mRNA. In brief, the proposed hypothesis is that during prolonged dietary lysine deficiency, mTORC1 activity is reactivated. This activated mTORC1 then enhances ATF4 mRNA expression in the rumen, jejunum, and ileum, thereby helping to maintain amino acid metabolic balance in these tissues.

In the current study, FOXO3 mRNA expression in the rumen epithelium was significantly higher in the LD group than in the control group, whereas no significant difference was found between the LD and MD groups. These results suggest that the rumen epithelium of goats is more sensitive to intracellular lysine deprivation than to methionine deprivation, leading to a more rapid and pronounced transcriptional activation of FOXO3 under lysine-deficient conditions [58].

Metabolomics profiling analysis helps decipher the complex regulatory mechanisms of autophagy, as metabolites play a critical role in its regulation [59]. In the current research, the increased levels of sorbitol and N-acetylglucosamine were found in the jejunal tissue of MD group compared with the control group. Sorbitol is a sugar alcohol produced from glucose via the polyol pathway, a process that becomes more active during metabolic stress or nutrient deprivation [60]. N-acetylglucosamine is a monosaccharide derivative that plays a key role in gut health and energy signaling [61]. The elevation of these compounds represents a shift in metabolism where the jejunal tissue increases the production of protective sugars N-acetylglucosamine and sorbitol to counteract the oxidative stress and impaired barrier function that often results from a shortage of methionine. This is the compensatory mechanism to maintain energy homeostasis in the jejunal epithelial cells when methionine is not available for protein synthesis and mitochondrial energy metabolism [62, 63].

Citrulline is a non-protein amino acid that is converted to arginine by argininosuccinate synthase [64]. Meanwhile, arginine can also generate citrulline under inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS) induction, accompanied by the production of nitric oxide (NO) [65]. Citrulline has a negative feedback regulatory effect on NO [66]. Therefore, the downregulation of citrulline in the jejunal tissue of MD group in this experiment indicated that the dietary deficiency of methionine might lead to an increase in NO production. NO can act as a signaling molecule to regulate many biological processes within cells [67].

In the present study, down regulation of resveratrol in methionine deficiency group was found. Resveratrol may regulate autophagy by clearing damaged components and improving cellular function. Resveratrol’s antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties may protect cells and tissues from the damaging effects of this deficiency [68, 69]. Studies showed that resveratrol can promote apoptosis of subcutaneous adipocytes in beef cattle by activating the SIRT1/AMPK signaling pathway and its downstream FOXO1 [70]. Resveratrol also has regulatory effect on cellular autophagy. Moreover, resveratrol regulates cellular autophagy through various pathways, including mTORC1, AMPK, SIRT1/ SIRT3-FOXO and STAT3 signaling pathways [71]. Compared with the control, content of the metabolites citrulline and resveratrol was downregulated in the jejunal tissue of methionine deficiency group. Combining with the expression of autophagy proteins and genes in jejunal mucosa as well as metabolomics of jejunum tissues, it was found that there was no significant difference in the expression of autophagy marker proteins between the control group and the methionine deficiency group. This result suggested that dietary methionine deficiency primarily affected metabolic pathway rather than autophagy.

Generally, small peptides assist the absorption of amino acids in the animal digestive tract and increase nitrogen deposition [72]. In this study, the methionine deficiency group showed a significant decrease in the content of small peptides (Met-Ala, Ile-His and Leu-Ala) in the jejunal tissue compared with the control group. This result was compatible to the studies reported before, and these occurred as part of a conserved metabolic response in animals to protect limited methionine for critical functions like protein synthesis and cellular methylation [73, 74].

According to multivariate data analysis, differential metabolites screening and metabolic pathway analysis of jejunum tissue, significant differences were found in the energy metabolism related pentose phosphate pathway of the dietary methionine deficiency group compared to control group.

Therefore, it can be concluded that, deficiency of dietary methionine has an impact on the energy metabolism of the jejunal tissue of goats. Due to the deficiency of methionine, energy metabolism was blocked and energy supply was insufficient. Levels of differential metabolites sorbitol and N-acetylglucosamine were upregulated, rapidly provide energy through lactate metabolism and pentose phosphate pathways. In addition, the differential metabolic pathways in the jejunal tissue caused by dietary methionine deficiency included D-arginine and D-ornithine metabolism, phenylalanine metabolism, and amino sugar and nucleotide sugar metabolism pathways, indicating that methionine deficiency affected the related metabolism of arginine, ornithine, and phenylalanine.

Homocysteine (Hcy) is an important intermediate product of the methionine and cysteine metabolism. In this study, the Hcy content of the jejunal tissue was upregulated in the dietary lysine deficiency group, compared with the control group. At the same time, significant differences in the cysteine and methionine metabolism pathways related to Hcy metabolism were found, indicating that dietary lysine deficiency promoted methionine metabolism and produced Hcy. It suggested that lysine deficiency could affect the metabolism of other amino acids in the goats through a complex amino acid metabolic network. This result in this study was consistent with the previous reports that a lysine-deficient diet led to the upregulation of Hcy via disruptions in methionine metabolism and impaired autophagy. This process involved a critical imbalance in the methylation cycle, leading to the accumulation of Hcy and a subsequent cascade of cellular stress responses in mouse [75, 76]. Previous result showed that Hcy might reduce autophagy levels in mouse cardiomyocytes through the mTOR signaling pathway [77]. So, the increased content of Hcy might one of the reasons why the lysine-deficient diet led to a reduced autophagy levels at the current study.

Hcy is directly related to purine metabolism and acts as a central control point. A shortage of lysine triggers cellular stress responses that influences the one-carbon metabolism cycle, directly increases homocysteine (Hcy) levels, which further inhibits autophagy, and also impacts purine metabolism, as purine synthesis is a downstream user of the one-carbon metabolism pathway [78, 79]. Moreover, the small intestinal mucosa absorbs nutrients from feed and is responsible for the synthesis and catabolism of purine nucleotides [80]. Therefore, maintaining normal absorption and metabolism function of the gastrointestinal tract is of great importance for purine metabolism.

Conclusion

Dietary methionine and lysine deficiency did not alter the protein expression levels of autophagy-related proteins LC3 and p62 in rumen epithelium, ileal mucosa, or liver tissue. However, dietary lysine deficiency alone reduced autophagy in the jejunal mucosa of goats. In response, the body upregulated ATF4 gene expression to help maintain amino acid metabolic balance in rumen, jejunum, and ileum tissues. Metabolomics analysis indicated that the observed reduction in cellular autophagy resulting from lysine deficiency might be mediated by upregulation of homocysteine, and dietary methionine deficiency primarily affected metabolic pathway rather than autophagy.
