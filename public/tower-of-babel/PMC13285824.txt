ERBB2/HER2 landscape and prognostic impact: large-scale, real-world analysis across solid cancers
Authors: A. Shreenivas, A. Elliott, T. Corbiere, H.-Z. Chen, B. George, M. Kamgar, S. Chakrabarti, A. Desai, D. Bracken-Clarke, E. Xue, C.S. Floudas, J. Thomas, J. Xiu, G. Sledge, E.S. Antonarakis, Y. Boumber, R. Kurzrock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe evaluated the frequency and prognostic impact of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) protein expression [immunohistochemistry (IHC)] and correlations with ERBB2 genomic alterations in a large database of molecularly profiled real-world patients (Caris Life Sciences, Phoenix, AZ).Materials and methodsWe analyzed 206 750 tumors for ERBB2 copy number alterations and pathogenic mutations (using next-generation sequencing) and 88 530 samples for HER2 IHC data (stratified as 0, 1+, 2+, or 3+). RNA (whole transcriptome) was also evaluated. Overall survival (OS) was based on real-world insurance claims.ResultsERBB2 amplifications and/or mutations were found in a subset of multiple cancer types. ERBB2 amplifications were often but not always associated with higher HER2 IHC expression. ERBB2 pathogenic mutations and ERBB2 amplifications correlated significantly across cancers and in some cancer subtypes. Statistically significant associations with HER2 IHC positivity were also identified in a subgroup of tumors exhibiting mutations, even without amplifications. ERBB2 transcripts were markedly increased among HER2 IHC 3+ tumors. Median OS for HER2 (3+, 2+, 1+, and 0+) tumors were 30.6, 26.9, 26.5, and 23 months, respectively [hazard ratio (HR) 0.80, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.77-0.83; HR 0.89, 95% CI 0.87-0.91; and HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.88-0.92, respectively (P < 0.001)]. However, survival advantages observed in the overall cohort appeared largely driven by breast and gastric cancers for which HER2-targeted therapies are available.ConclusionComplex relationships between ERBB2 alterations and HER2 IHC expression exist, including increased expression in non–ERBB2-amplified tumors with pathogenic mutations. Importantly, ERBB2/HER2 amplification and/or mutation rates as well as transcript and protein expression varied between cancers and within cancer types, emphasizing the need for individual assessment.

Introduction

Human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) is a tyrosine kinase receptor encoded by the ERBB2 gene. HER2 expression is observed across diverse types of tumors. In an analysis of ∼37 000 cases of cancers, as well as prior literature surveys, a subset of almost every tumor type expressed HER2, suggesting that tumor-agnostic therapeutic targeting may be important.1,2 Notably, a recent study showed that gastric and esophageal cancers with low [immunohistochemistry (IHC) 1-2+] and high (IHC 3+) HER2 expression have distinct molecular and immune profiles.3 The prevalence of ERBB2 amplification and mutation in HER2 ultra-low (IHC 0+) and HER2-low (IHC 1-2+) tumors across different cancer subtypes remains largely unknown. Concordance between ERBB2 amplification and gain-of-function ERBB2 mutation identified by next-generation sequencing (NGS) studies, with HER2 expression assessed through IHC, also varies across different tumor types,4, 5, 6, 7 as does discordance between HER2 IHC status in ERBB2-amplified tumors, all of which merit additional investigation in a pan-tumor setting.8 These issues are of special importance because of the availability of agents that effectively target HER2 IHC 3+ expression in multiple cancer types, as well as ERBB2 mutations and amplification, and even HER2 IHC 1+/2+ (at least in breast cancer).9, 10, 11

The treatment paradigm for HER2-overexpressing (IHC 3+) tumors has dramatically changed over the years. HER2 is now recognized as a tumor-agnostic biomarker. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recently granted accelerated approval to fam-trastuzumab deruxtecan-nxki (Enhertu, Daiichi Sankyo, Inc., Basking Ridge, NJ) for patients with unresectable or metastatic HER2-high (IHC 3+) solid tumors who have received prior systemic treatments. This approval was primarily based on encouraging results of the DESTINY-PanTumor02,10 DESTINY-CRC02,12 and DESTINY-Lung01 (NCT03505710) trials.13 HER2-low (IHC 1/2+) expressing tumors may also respond well to novel anti-HER2-based therapies such as antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs).14,15 For example, administration of the ADC trastuzumab deruxtecan was associated with remarkable improvement in both overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with HER2-low expressing advanced-stage breast cancer, regardless of its hormone receptor status, compared with the physician’s choice of chemotherapy.16,17 Trastuzumab deruxtecan also received accelerated FDA approval for patients with advanced-stage lung cancers whose tumors harbor activating ERBB2 mutations, regardless of their HER2 IHC status.13 The DESTINY-PanTumor02 trial also showed some activity in HER2 (IHC 2+) non-amplified tumors across several histologies.10 The impact of ADCs in HER2-low expressing tumors has varied.18, 19, 20 Physician–scientists are now evaluating potential efficacy biomarkers for trastuzumab deruxtecan beyond IHC in both HER2-low and ultra-low expressing advanced breast cancer populations in the DESTINY-Breast15 (NCT05950945) and other studies.21

Despite the encouraging results mentioned above, the true scope of the efficacy of trastuzumab deruxtecan and other anti-HER2-based therapies in managing several HER2-low expressing tumors is still being determined. Earlier studies have compared HER2 protein expression by IHC and ERBB2 mRNA expression through RT–PCR or probe hybridization techniques, which suggested that mRNA expression could provide complementary means of subcategorizing HER2 expression when IHC results are ambiguous.11,22, 23, 24 The landscape of HER2-targeted therapy is expanding significantly with the development of novel therapeutic approaches, including next-generation ADCs,25, 26, 27 bispecific antibodies,28 tyrosine kinase inhibitors,29 bispecific T cell engagers,30 and adoptive T cell-based therapies and cancer vaccines.31 HER2-low expression is widely observed in solid tumors, suggesting a potentially broader application for HER2-directed therapies than previously recognized.

In this study, we evaluated HER2 IHC expression and its correlation with ERBB2 genomic alterations and ERBB2 transcripts, as well as the prognostic impact of ERBB2/HER2 expression, in a very large database of molecularly profiled tumors from real-world patients.

Materials and methods

Patient cohort

We performed a review of solid tumor specimens from real-world patients with a wide range of cancers that were submitted for molecular profiling to a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA)-certified laboratory (Caris Life Sciences). The present study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki, Belmont Report, and US Common Rule and in compliance with policy 45 CFR 46.101(b). The analysis utilized retrospective, deidentified clinical data, thereby negating the necessity for patient consent.

NGS

After tumor enrichment by manual microdissection, NGS was performed on isolated genomic DNA using the NextSeq platform (Illumina, Inc., San Diego, CA) for 592 cancer-relevant genes or the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, Inc.) for whole exome sequencing. A custom-designed SureSelect XT assay was used to enrich exonic regions of 592 whole-gene targets (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). All variants were detected with >99% confidence based on allele frequency and amplicon coverage, with an average sequencing depth of coverage >500 and an analytic sensitivity threshold of 5% established for variant calling. ERBB2 variants (single-nucleotide variants/indels) were classified by board-certified molecular geneticists according to criteria established by the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics.32 ERBB2 copy number (CN) amplification was determined by calculating the average depth of the sample, along with the sequencing depth of each exon, in comparison to a precalibrated reference value, with CN ≥6 classified as amplified. The other category that was used was non-amplified, which referred to CN 1 to <4. Tumors with CN 4 to <6 were considered intermediate. Further details of methods can be found in the section “Supplemental Methods” in Supplementary Materials, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107772.

IHC

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded slides were stained using the Agilent DAKO Link 48 automated platform and staining techniques, per the manufacturer’s instructions, and were optimized and validated per CLIA/College of American Pathologists and International Organization for Standardization requirements. PATHWAY anti-HER2/neu (4B5) antibody was utilized. Staining was scored for intensity (0 = no staining; 1+ = weak staining; 2+ = moderate staining; 3+ = strong staining) and percentage of stained cells, as evaluated by a board-certified pathologist.

Real-world clinical outcomes

Real-world OS information was obtained from insurance claims data, with OS calculated from the date of initial diagnosis (first International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision code for cancer-related diagnosis identified in the claims data) or date of tissue biopsy collection to the last contact or death. Censoring was applied for patients with last contact <100 days from the data cut-off date (October 2023). Hazard ratios (HRs) and P values were calculated using the Cox proportional hazards model and log-rank test, respectively, for patients with HER2 IHC 1+, 2+, and 3+ tumors compared with those with IHC 0+ tumors.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed with JMP V18.0.1 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). Continuous data were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test, while categorical data were examined employing either the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test (two-sided), as appropriate. The P values were adjusted for multiple hypothesis testing using the Bonferroni correction method.

Results

A total of 206 750 pan-tumor samples were evaluated for ERBB2 CN alterations and mutations and 88 530 samples for HER2 IHC data.

Pan-cancer distribution of HER2 IHC staining intensities

Of 88 535 patient tumors with HER2 IHC data, 61.27% were HER2 IHC 0+, 19.91% HER2 IHC 1+, 13.52% HER2 IHC 2+, and 5.30% HER2 IHC 3+ staining intensity (Figure 1A). Concordance between ERBB2 mRNA expression and IHC score is illustrated in Figure 1B and is discussed in detail in a different section of the manuscript. The distribution of HER2 IHC intensity varied significantly by cancer type (Figure 1C), with the largest proportion of HER2 IHC 3+ tumors observed for bladder cancer (37.59%), which also had the smallest proportion of HER2 IHC 0 samples (18.31%). The proportion of HER2 IHC 3+ tumors was also notably higher in cervical (25.64%) and other gynecological malignancies (13%-16% in uterine, endometrial, and ovarian cancers) compared with tumor types commonly associated with HER2, such as breast (6.77%) and gastric cancers (5.92%). ERBB2 CN amplification correlated with higher HER2 IHC expression

Among the subset of 63 404 samples with ERBB2 CN amplification results, we next examined the association between HER2 IHC expression and ERBB2 amplification. Across all cancers, 80.47% of HER2 IHC 3+ cases were ERBB2 amplified (≥6 copies), compared with 5.69% of IHC 2+, 0.40% of IHC 1+, and 0.16% of IHC 0+ cases, with similar results across most cancer types (Figure 2A and Table 1). For HER2 IHC 2+ expression, high ERBB2 amplification frequencies were observed in cervical cancer (57.14%), head and neck cancers (35.00%), vulvar cancer (25.00%), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (18.60%), and ovarian epithelial carcinoma (18.02%). Among samples with HER2 IHC 1+ expression, ERBB2 amplification was most prevalent in head and neck cancers (25.00%) and bladder cancer (11.11%), with relatively low rates (<5%) observed for all other tumor types. It is noteworthy that HER2 IHC 0+ expressing tumors seldom exhibited ERBB2 amplification. Interestingly, however, 7.41% of bladder cancer samples had HER2 IHC 0 expression but still had ERBB2 amplification; nonetheless, there was a statistically significant association between higher HER2 IHC expression levels and ERBB2 amplification. The overall pattern demonstrated that although ERBB2 amplification is predominant in HER2 IHC 3+ tumors, there is notable heterogeneity across cancer types, with variable ERBB2 amplification rates observed across different HER2 expression levels.

ERBB2 pathogenic mutations correlated with higher HER2 IHC levels

We next evaluated the prevalence of pathogenic or likely pathogenic ERBB2 mutations and their association with HER2 IHC expression across various tumor types from a large cohort of 74 018 samples with available data. A diverse group of ERBB2 exon 20 insertion mutations in the kinase domain or ERBB2 point mutations in the extracellular domain, transmembrane domain, or the kinase domain was observed. There was an overall trend of increasing ERBB2 mutation prevalence with higher HER2 expression, with a marked increase from 2.03% in HER2 IHC 0+ to 4.53% in 3+ cases (Table 2). As depicted in Figure 2B, the HER2 IHC expression pattern in ERBB2-mutated cases showed heterogeneity, with most tumor types displaying variable distribution across HER2 IHC 3+ (red), 2+ (yellow), 1+ (green), and 0+ (gray) expression levels. Small intestinal malignancies showed notably high mutation rates in HER2 IHC 3+ cases, where the mutation rate reached 37.04% (P < 0.001). This aligns with the prominent peak seen in Figure 2B and represents one of the most statistically significant findings in the dataset. Overall, the strongest statistical associations between HER2 expression and ERBB2 mutations appeared in small intestinal malignancies, cancer of unknown primary (CUP), and breast carcinoma, all with highly significant Q values after Bonferroni correction. Small intestinal malignancies showed the most dramatic relationship, with mutation rates rising from 6.16% in HER2 IHC 0+ to an exceptional 37.04% in HER2 IHC 3+ tumors. Interestingly, not all cancers followed a linear pattern. Breast carcinoma and non-small–cell lung cancer (NSCLC) showed peak ERBB2 mutation rates in HER2 IHC 2+ (4.57% and 5.99%, respectively) rather than HER2 IHC 3+ tumors. Gastric adenocarcinoma displayed higher mutation rates in HER2 IHC 0+ (4.09%) than in HER2 IHC 1+ (1.94%) or HER2 IHC 2+ (3.14%) categories. Colorectal adenocarcinoma, with over 20 000 samples analyzed, also showed a robust statistical relationship between mutation and IHC expression, enhancing confidence in these findings. It displayed higher mutation rates in HER2 IHC 3+ (5.54%) than in HER2 IHC 2+ (1.80%) or HER2 IHC 1+ (1.52%) categories. Cholangiocarcinoma exhibited distinct subtype-specific patterns of ERBB2 mutations, with extrahepatic variants showing a high mutation rate of 20.69% in HER2 IHC 3+ cases (P = 0.00011), while intrahepatic cholangiocarcinomas demonstrated a lower but still significant 6.78% mutation rate at the same HER2 expression level (P = 0.00026).

The pattern varied significantly across cancer types, including some tumors showing high mutation rates even in HER2-low/ultra-low expressor cases (additional details for statistically significant associations in Table 2). Notably, bladder cancer (664 samples) showed a relatively balanced distribution between HER2 IHC 1+ and 2+ expression in ERBB2-mutated cases, with each representing about 12% of cases. Lower mutation frequencies (5%-8%) were observed in several other tumor types with lower IHC expression, as depicted in Table 2.

ERBB2 pathogenic mutations and ERBB2 amplifications correlate with each other across cancers and in some cancer subtypes

We also evaluated the prevalence of ERBB2 pathogenic mutations by ERBB2 amplification status among a much larger cohort of patient samples that included samples without HER2 IHC testing. Of 206 750 total cases with DNA sequencing for ERBB2 variants and CN changes across all cancer types, 4856 cases (2.35%) had ERBB2 amplifications, and a total of 3315 cases (1.60%) had pathogenic/likely pathogenic ERBB2 mutations, with a higher percentage observed in cases with ERBB2 CN amplification compared with non-amplified cases (5.31% versus 1.51%, P < 0.0001). Several cancer types showed a strong association between ERBB2 amplification and increased mutation frequency (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107772). For example, small intestine malignancies: 33.33% mutation in amplified versus 6.10% in non-amplified (P < 0.0001), colorectal cancers: 8.30% versus 1.85% (P < 0.0001), cholangiocarcinoma (extrahepatic): 18.75% versus 4.23% (P < 0.001), lung adenocarcinoma (NSCLC): 7.25% versus 1.48% (P < 0.0001), and gastric carcinoma: 5.96% versus 0.09% (P < 0.0001). A few other cancers showed trends toward higher mutation rates in amplified cases, but with smaller sample sizes, such as melanoma: 11.11% versus 0.65% (P < 0.001). In contrast, many cancer types showed no significant difference in mutation rates between amplified and non-amplified cases, including bladder, cervical, endometrial, esophageal, ovarian, and uterine serous carcinoma.

ERBB2 mutations correlated with increased HER2 IHC expression in the absence of ERBB2 amplification across cancers and in several specific tumor types

We also analyzed 74 018 non-amplified (CN <4) tumors to provide a more nuanced view of the relationship between pathogenic or likely pathogenic ERBB2 mutations and HER2 IHC expression (Figure 2C). We focused our evaluation on non-amplified (CN <4) samples and left out the intermediate CN gain in this analysis. Although the statistical power was limited by the rarity of non-amplified HER2 IHC 3+ cases, there was a significant increase in mutation frequency among HER2 IHC-high tumors across all cancers and in some tumor types (Supplementary Table S2, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107772). Among HER2 IHC 3+ tumors, salivary gland tumors demonstrated the highest mutation rate, which was significantly increased compared with HER2 IHC 0-2+ tumors (16.67% versus 1.04%-3.79%, P < 0.05), with similar results observed in cholangiocarcinoma/intrahepatic tumors (14.29% in HER2 IHC 3+ versus 0.35%-1.03% in IHC 0-2+, P < 0.01) and CUP tumors (11.11% in HER2 IHC 3+ versus 1.33%-3.56% in IHC 0-2+, P < 0.001) as well as in breast cancer (5.8% in HER2 IHC 3+ versus 1.96-3.77% in IHC 0-2+, P < 0.001). Further investigation may focus on the functional impact of mutation and its correlation with its clinical outcome. Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107772, shows a detailed illustration of types of ERBB2 mutations per domain per cancer type in our study.

ERBB2 mRNA correlates with HER2 IHC expression

As heterogeneity in HER2 IHC testing can arise from differences in tissue processing methods, staining protocols, and interpretation by pathologists, we assessed the association between ERBB2 transcript and HER2 protein expression to determine if HER2 mRNA quantification could serve as a valuable complement to IHC, potentially offering a more standardized assessment. Among 61 443 samples with ERBB2 RNA and HER2 IHC data available, the median ERBB2 expression progressively increased with HER2 IHC intensity, with notably high expression in 401.32 transcripts per million (TPM) in HER2 IHC 3+ tumors compared with a range of 23.87-49.35 TPM across HER2 IHC 0+, 1+, and 2+ categories (Figure 1B; Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.107772). However, there were no clear RNA expression thresholds that could reliably predict HER2 IHC level. Median ERBB2 RNA expression in HER2 IHC 3+ tumors was consistently higher than that in HER2 IHC 0-2+ tumors, often by an order of magnitude. This pattern was observed across breast, esophageal, and gastric cancers, which corresponds with the current use of HER2-targeted therapies in these malignancies. Significantly increased ERBB2 transcriptomic expression was also observed in HER2 IHC 3+ NSCLC and bladder cancers, along with less common cancers, such as cholangiocarcinoma, uterine serous carcinoma, and small intestinal malignancies, suggesting ERBB2 mRNA expression may be a useful surrogate for protein expression by IHC in a wide range of solid tumors.

Prognostic impact of HER2 IHC status

Using insurance claims data to infer real-world outcomes, we evaluated OS from the time of tissue collection or the time of diagnosis to the last contact or death for patients stratified by HER2 IHC status. Across all cancers, the median OS from tissue collection was longest for patients with HER2 IHC 3+ tumors (30.6 months), followed by 2+ (26.9 months), 1+ (26.5 months), and 0+ (23.0 months) (Figure 3A). Higher HER2 expression, especially HER2 IHC 3+, was associated with significantly longer OS compared with HER2 IHC 0+ tumors in several cancer types, including breast [HR 0.65, 95% confidence interval (CI) 0.60-0.71, P < 0.0001], esophageal/gastroesophageal junction (GEJ) (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.69-0.84, P < 0.0001), and gallbladder cancer (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.55-0.88, P < 0.01), while significant associations were not observed in other cancer types, including bladder (HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.64-1.28, P = 0.59) and ovarian surface epithelial carcinomas (HR 1.10, 95% CI 0.85-1.42, P = 0.48). In contrast, high HER2 (IHC 3+) expression in endometrial cancer was associated with a shorter median OS of 33.5 months compared with 44.4 months in HER2 IHC 0+ (HR 1.33, 95% CI 0.69-0.84, P < 0.0001). The findings suggest that the prognostic impact of HER2 expression depends on the specific cancer context and is most prominent in breast and esophageal/GEJ cancers, perhaps because of the effect of HER2-targeting treatments in those diseases. Discussion

Our study interrogated a very large database of 206 750 tumors and showed that ERBB2 amplifications and mutations, as well as variable HER2 IHC expression, are discernible across a broad subset of malignancies and vary both between and within cancer types, emphasizing the pan-tumor nature of these aberrations and the need for individual tumor genomic and IHC assessment. These findings further suggest opportunities for expanding HER2-targeted therapeutic approaches beyond traditional indications. Our results expand significantly on earlier studies1,2,7 and support the biology behind the recent solid cancer-agnostic FDA approval of the ADC trastuzumab deruxtecan for HER2 IHC 3+ solid cancers.33,34

The associations between ERBB2 amplifications and IHC expression have been explored in other studies; there is a strong correlation, but they are not always concordant.6,8 In line with prior reports,6,35,36 we found a strong correlation between ERBB2 amplification and HER2 IHC 3+ expression. However, some cancers with ERBB2 amplification had low HER2 IHC expressions; the latter observation needs further evaluation regarding its biologic underpinnings. For example, previous studies have shown that nonfocal amplifications may be less likely to produce overexpression.37

The association between ERBB2 mutation and IHC expression was weaker than that between ERBB2 amplification and HER2 IHC expression and was not statistically significant in many individual cancer types, particularly among tumors without ERBB2 amplification. Even so, perhaps surprisingly, ERBB2 mutations did correlate with higher HER2 IHC expression (in the absence of ERBB2 amplification) across cancers and in breast, salivary, CUP, and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. The mechanism behind increased HER2 protein expression is unclear, as ERBB2 mutations might be expected to activate the kinase domain without causing overexpression of the HER2 protein.38 The presence of pathogenic ERBB2 mutations in a subset of HER2-overexpressing but non-ERBB2 amplified tumors39,40 suggests an alternative pathway to induce HER2 expression.

The exact mechanism explaining the association between ERBB2 mutations and increased HER2 expression in ERBB2 non-amplified tumors is not well understood, but some possible theories have been proposed.38,41,42 For example, some activating mutations in the extracellular domain, including G309E, S310F, C311R, and C334S, appear to disrupt the receptor’s normal intramolecular architecture in ways that favor constitutive dimerization. Work by Greulich et al43 showed that extracellular domain mutants of ERBB2 are activated by two distinct mechanisms: S310F and S310Y cause hyperphosphorylation of the receptor’s C-terminal tail, while G309E and cysteine-disrupting mutations such as C311R and C334S instead promote intermolecular disulfide bond formation leading to constitutive receptor dimerization, without requiring C-terminal hyperphosphorylation. These mechanisms lead to phosphorylation of intracellular proteins and ultimate activation of tumorigenic pathways.38

For kinase domain mutations such as YVMA, V777L, and D769H/Y, a different but complementary mechanism may be at work. These mutations produce constitutively heightened kinase activity, driving strong downstream activation of the MAPK and AKT pathways.38,44 Earlier studies have also outlined that sustained MAPK activation phosphorylates and activates ETS family transcription factors, including ER81 and PEA3, which are known positive regulators of the HER2 promoter.38,45 In principle, this could create a self-reinforcing loop in which the mutant receptor amplifies its own transcription without altering gene CN. This is a mechanistically plausible hypothesis, specifically in the context of somatic point mutations without amplification, but strong evidence supporting this theory is lacking and requires further investigation.

There may be some additional mechanisms that are not yet known. From a clinical perspective, regardless of mechanism, our data suggest that ERBB2 mutational status may provide complementary information to ERBB2 CN in predicting HER2 protein expression. This has potential implications for patient selection for HER2-targeted ADCs, which depend on surface protein expression for efficacy. We need further investigation of these mechanisms.

In addition, it is notable that ERBB2 mutations were found in a subset of many cancers and have important implications for therapeutic targeting, regardless of expression level, especially considering recent FDA approvals for HER2-directed therapies in tumors (such as NSCLC) harboring such mutations.13 Novel tyrosine kinase inhibitors, such as BAY 2927088 and zongertinib (BI 1810631), that inhibit ERBB2 exon 20 insertions and ERBB2 point mutations have also shown promising activity in NSCLC.46, 47, 48, 49 Moreover, HER2 expression is heterogeneous,50,51 changing over time and by site of recurrence or metastasis,52,53 and understanding these fluctuations is crucial across multiple cancer subtypes. There have been some concerted efforts in understanding the HER2 models (using natural language processing),54 but prevalence and clinical outcome data of HER2 low and ultra-low expressing tumors and correlations with ERBB2 alterations are lacking in a pan-cancer setting. The real-world data generated from our study should be useful in better appreciating the ERBB2/HER2 landscape across cancers.

HER2 IHC expression analyses are often user- and tissue sample-dependent, and their interpretations may differ between individuals and institutions.55,56 Notably, we found a statistically significant association between ERBB2 mRNA levels and HER2 IHC expression, with ERBB2 mRNA expression markedly increased among HER2 IHC 3+ tumors. However, there were no clear RNA expression thresholds that could reliably predict lower HER2 IHC levels. These findings were again consistent with prior studies in which mRNA expression was not always reliable in predicting HER2 (IHC 2+) expression in breast cancer samples.57 Even so, there are cases reported wherein high HER2 RNA correlated with response to HER2-targeted therapy.11 Future developments in the field may also include digital pathology platforms utilizing artificial intelligence algorithms.58

Higher versus lower HER2 IHC expression was associated with longer median survival in this heterogeneous group. It followed a linear trend when median survival was calculated from the time of tissue collection and a nonlinear trend when median OS was calculated from the time of tissue collection. Overall, the median survival of HER2-high (IHC 3+) and HER2-low expressors (IHC 1/2 +) was better than that of ultra-low expressors (IHC 0). However, there was no significant difference between HER2-low expressors and ultra-low expressors after excluding breast and gastric cancers. This could partially be explained by the fact that, historically, anti-HER2-based therapies15,26,59, 60, 61 were primarily FDA-approved for the management of HER2-positive breast and gastric cancers and may have improved the survival of patients with those tumors. An important limitation of our study was the absence of detailed treatment data, which precluded a definitive assessment of whether survival differences are directly attributable to HER2-targeted therapies versus other factors across all cases analyzed in this data set.

This study, while providing valuable insights, has important limitations, including a real-world study design. For some cancer types, the total number of HER2 IHC 2+ and 3+ non-amplified cases with mutation data was quite small, and the prevalence reported for some rare tumors should be interpreted cautiously. The stage at diagnosis or response to treatments was not available, which limits the ability to control for potential confounding factors. The dataset represents a snapshot in time, and the prevalence of ERBB2 mutations and association with HER2 IHC status may change over time and could differ between distinct metastatic sites in the same patient. There may be additional biological factors that influence HER2 IHC expression, which require further evaluation. Despite these limitations, the analysis provides valuable insights into the prevalence of ERBB2 mutations and amplifications across different HER2 IHC categories and cancer types, which can guide future research and inform clinical decision making.

In summary, our current report demonstrates strong associations between ERBB2 amplification and HER2 IHC expression, while highlighting more complex relationships between ERBB2 mutations and protein expression, particularly in ERBB2 non-amplified tumors. The identification of pathogenic ERBB2 mutations in HER2-expressing but non-amplified tumors may imply alternative pathways for HER2 expression induction that need further evaluation. The strong correlation between ERBB2 mRNA levels and HER2 IHC expression, particularly in HER2 IHC 3+ tumors, suggests the potential utility of RNA-based testing as a complementary diagnostic tool, although clear mRNA expression thresholds for IHC categories remain elusive. Figure 3C and D provide evidence that the survival advantage observed in the overall cohort (Figure 3A and B) may be largely driven by the availability of HER2-targeted therapies in the management of breast and gastric cancers. Although randomized controlled trials provide the highest level of evidence for treatment efficacy, real-world data from our study can complement these studies and help understand outcomes across heterogeneous patient populations that may be underrepresented in clinical trials. Importantly, higher HER2 IHC expression was associated with improved survival outcomes across multiple cancer types, but this effect, as noted earlier, was attenuated when excluding gastric and breast cancer patients, perhaps highlighting the impact of approved anti-HER2-based therapies on survival. Future studies with access to detailed treatment-level data collected through electronic health records or clinical trial data registries may allow investigators to further evaluate if receipt of HER2-targeted therapies accounts for OS difference across different HER2 IHC categories. Finally, our findings support the emerging paradigm of HER2 as a tumor-agnostic therapeutic target while highlighting the need for individual molecular testing.
