Polymer-Zn(II) sunscreens for protection against harmful blue ray
Authors: Rui Yuan, Sa Du, Jieyao Cheng, Zeyu Ma, Ruoxi Wang, Xiue Yan, Yonghui Huang, Yuwei Wu, Lei Tao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Harmful blue rays (HBRs, 400-450 nm) originating from the sun and numerous electronic devices are similar to ultraviolet rays (UVRs) and can induce skin photoaging. Ordinary sunscreens exhibit poor HBR-blocking capability, and traditional anti-HBR materials are unsuitable for skin protection. Herein, we develop a polymer-metal complex that can be applied to the skin to effectively block HBRs. Hydroxyquinoline is introduced into the polymer backbone via the Biginelli reaction. This polymer (P) exhibits strong coordination with Zn(II). The resulting P-Zn(II) complex undergoes a spectral redshift through metal-ligand orbital transitions, enabling effective shielding of both UVRs and HBRs. The P-Zn(II) complex protects fibroblasts from HBR-induced cell death, oxidative stress, and cellular aging by preventing HBR-triggered dysregulation of multiple photoaging-related signaling pathways and genes. In mouse experiments, the P-Zn(II) complex exhibits minimal skin irritation and low hepatic and renal toxicity. It can be applied to the skin in a manner similar to sunscreens and effectively prevents HBR-induced skin damage, which fills the gap in HBR protection left by current commercial products. This study highlights the potential of polymer-metal ion complexes as advanced photoprotective materials and provides valuable insights into the development of anti-HBR biomaterials.

Introduction

Ultraviolet rays (UVRs, 280-400 nm) are a well-known cause of skin aging [[1], [2], [3]]. Compared to UVRs, which primarily originate from sunlight, harmful blue rays (HBRs, a high-energy visible light within 400-450 nm) originate from multiple sources and can induce oxidative stress, inflammatory responses, and various forms of tissue injury [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. These effects may promote collagen degradation and the expression of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP), ultimately leading to skin photoaging [[8], [9], [10]] (Fig. 1a). In addition to sunlight—where HBR intensity has been measured as approximately 1230.43 μW/cm2 (10-day average measured at 12:00 in June, Beijing)—many artificial devices used in daily life also emit HBRs [11,12]. The HBR intensities of mobile phones, computers, and indoor light-emitting diode (LED) lighting are approximately 3.00, 1.01, and 45.08 μW/cm2, respectively. Currently, there are limited methods available to effectively prevent HBR-induced skin damage (Fig. 1b). Existing materials for sunscreens are primarily designed to protect against UVRs and have demonstrated high efficacy in this regard [[13], [14], [15]]; however they provide minimal protection against HBRs. HBRs are considered a significant cause of many eye diseases [16,17]. Traditional anti-HBR lenses and films have been developed for eye protection [[18], [19], [20]]; however, these materials are not suitable for skin application. Several anti-blue-ray additives, such as aromatic azo compounds, cadmium sulfide (CdS), and cesium lead halides (CsPb(Cl/Br)3), have been reported [[21], [22], [23]], but these lipophilic small molecules or toxic heavy-metal salts pose potential health risks to users [[24], [25], [26]]. Recently, certain nanomaterials, including carbon dots, have been shown to provide shielding against HBRs. However, they require high loading levels (up to 18 wt%) to achieve effective HBR shielding (98.6%) and may also exhibit phototoxicity and long-term cumulative toxicity [27,28]. In addition, numerous available actives in sunscreen products claim anti-blue light properties as well as protecting the skin from photoaging [29], but they are mainly used to alleviate photoaging damage by providing antioxidant effects instead of directly shielding HBR. Therefore, the development of safe and efficient anti-HBR materials that can be conveniently applied like sunscreens for human skin protection to directly shield HBR remains both highly necessary and challenging for fundamental research and practical applications.

In this study, we developed a low-toxicity polymeric sunscreen to efficiently prevent HBR-induced skin damage. A monomer containing a hydroxyquinoline moiety was synthesized via the Biginelli reaction and subsequently copolymerized with poly(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate (PEGMA) to obtain a water-soluble polymer (P). This polymer was then coordinated with Zn(II) to produce an anti-HBR sunscreen suitable for skin protection in an acute photodamaged mouse model (Fig. 1c).

We selected the Biginelli reaction, hydroxyquinoline, Zn(II), and polyethylene glycol (PEG) for the following reasons. First, a series of anti-UV polymers based on the Biginelli reaction have been reported, and the resulting 3,4-dihydropyrimidin-2(1H)-one (DHPM) structures in these polymers play a crucial role in conferring UV-protective properties [30,31]. By incorporating hydroxyquinoline moieties with strong UV absorption into the polymer backbone through the DHPM structure, the synergistic combination of hydroxyquinoline and DHPM is expected to generate a polymer with broad-spectrum absorption spanning both UVRs and HBRs. Second, hydroxyquinoline derivatives possess excellent chelating ability toward a variety of metal ions owing to their unique molecular structures. Based on evidence in previous studies that metal ion coordination can induce significant spectral red-shifts [32,33], we hypothesized that coordination between hydroxyquinoline and metal ions may enable orbital transitions, thereby endowing the resulting complexes with HBR-absorption capability. Third, zinc is an essential trace element in the human body, exhibiting high biosafety and a strong, specific binding affinity for hydroxyquinoline. In this study, various metal ions were screened, and the P-Zn(II) complex demonstrated the most effective HBR-blocking performance and the lowest cytotoxicity among the tested P-metal ion complexes. Finally, PEG has been widely used in biomaterials owing to its excellent biocompatibility, appropriate viscosity, and moisturizing properties [34]. The PEG segments introduced into the polymer chains may enhance the water solubility, biosafety, and skin adhesion of the resulting anti-HBR polymers.

The resulting P-Zn(II) complex exhibited low cytotoxicity and efficiently shielded both UVRs and HBRs. By blocking HBRs, the P-Zn(II) complex protected cells from oxidative stress, aging, and death. RNA-sequencing analysis demonstrated that HBR-induced dysregulation of multiple photoaging-associated signaling pathways was markedly prevented in the presence of the P-Zn(II) complex. In a high-dose HBR-induced acute photodamaged mouse model, topical application of the P-Zn(II) complex effectively prevented skin damage. These results demonstrate that this rationally designed polymer-metal ion complex is a safe and effective sunscreen for HBR skin protection with potential for practical applications.

Results and discussion

In vitro HBR-blocking performance of the polymer-metal ion complexes

We first synthesized a monomer containing a hydroxyquinoline-DHPM structure via the Biginelli reaction (details are provided in the Supplementary Information; Fig. S1 and S2). This monomer was then copolymerized with the water-soluble monomer PEGMA (Mn: 950 g mol−1) to yield a water-soluble polymer (P) with a monomer conversion rate approaching 100% (Fig. S3). The 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectrum of P clearly showed the characteristic signals of the methyl group in the PEG segment (CH3: 3.17 ppm), the aromatic protons of the hydroxyquinoline moiety (Ph-Hs: 7.05-8.20 ppm), and the characteristic protons of the DHPM structure (N-Hs: 10.45 and 9.64 ppm; PhCH: 5.43 ppm) (Fig. 2a). Polymer P exhibited a relatively high molecular weight (Mn: 21,000 g mol−1, determined by gel permeation chromatography (GPC); Fig. S4), confirming the successful synthesis of a polymer containing multiple functional groups. P showed a low glass transition temperature (Tg: −31.6°C, determined by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC); Fig. S5) and moderate viscosity (η: 21.1 cP, approximately 8.8-fold higher than that of deionized water, measured by rotational viscometry; Fig. S6). When applied to pig skin, P (20 mg/mL in aqueous solution, 2.0 mg/cm2) remained on the skin surface and formed a coating (Fig. S7). We next evaluated the UVR- and HBR-shielding performance of P at different concentrations (2.5, 5.0, 10.0, 20.0, and 40.0 mg/mL in aqueous solution) using UV-visible (UV-vis) transmittance measurements. P exhibited a concentration-dependent light-blocking capability and almost completely blocked both UVRs (280-400 nm) and HBRs (400-450 nm) at concentrations ≥20 mg/mL (Fig. 2b). Subsequently, common high-valence metal ions present in the human body (Ca(II), Mg(II), Mn(II), Ni(II), Zn(II), Cu(II), Fe(II), and Fe(III); 1 mM) were mixed with P (5 mg/mL), and the HBR-shielding performance of the resulting P-metal ion complexes was evaluated (Fig. 2c). P alone (5 mg/mL) blocked approximately 70% of HBRs, whereas coordination with Ni(II), Zn(II), Cu(II), Fe(II), and Fe(III) considerably enhanced its HBR-shielding ability (Fig. S8). Notably, the complexes formed with Zn(II), Cu(II), and Fe(III) blocked more than 95% of HBRs, corresponding to an enhancement in HBR-shielding efficiency exceeding 35% compared with P alone (Fig. 2d). These results confirm that the polymer containing the hydroxyquinoline-DHPM structure effectively shields both UVRs and HBRs, and that this protective ability can be considerably enhanced through complexation with metal ions, particularly Zn(II), Cu(II), and Fe(III). Consequently, the complexes formed by P and these three metal ions were used in cell experiments.

We evaluated the cytotoxicity of P, Zn(II), Cu(II), and Fe(III) using the cell counting kit (CCK-8 assay, a classic cell viability test). L929 cells (mouse dermal fibroblasts), serving as model fibroblasts, were cocultured with the samples at various concentrations for 24 h, with cells in medium alone used as a blank control. The safe concentrations (cell viability >90%) were determined to be 20 mg/mL for P, 2 μg/mL for Zn(II), 0.125 μg/mL for Cu(II), and 2 μg/mL for Fe(III) (Fig. S9 and S10), and the lethal doses of 50% (LD50) were calculated as 5.59 μg/mL for Zn(II), 0.78 μg/mL for Cu(II), and 14.43 μg/mL for Fe (III) using the [Inhibitor] vs. normalized response model. Next, P (20 mg/mL) was mixed with Zn(II), Cu(II), and Fe(III), and the cytotoxicity of the resulting P-metal ion complexes was assessed. Notably, P considerably increased the safe concentration of Zn(II) from 2 to 280 μg/mL while maintaining cell viability above 90% (Fig. 2e), and the P-Zn(II) complex exhibited nearly complete shielding of both UVRs and HBRs (∼100%) in the UV-vis transmittance spectrum (Fig. S11).

The reduced cytotoxicity of Zn(II) is due to the formation of the P-Zn(II) complex that has no high cytotoxicity and can effectively chelate Zn(II), thereby ensuring that the released and unchelated free Zn(II) does not exceed the safety threshold (2 μg/mL). In contrast, at low concentrations of Cu(II) and Fe(III) (10 μg/mL), the P-Cu(II) and P-Fe(III) complexes remained cytotoxic (cell viability <60%), and these metal ions at this low concentration (10 μg/mL) provided only minimal enhancement to the HBR-shielding ability of P (Fig. S12). The pronounced cytotoxicity of the P-Cu(II) and P-Fe(III) complexes could be attributed to the released and unchelated free metal ions during the 24 h co-culture with cells, as well as the cytotoxicity of the P-Cu(II) and P-Fe(III) complexes themselves [[35], [36], [37]]. Therefore, the P-Zn(II) complex (P: 20 mg/mL, Zn(II): 280 μg/mL) was selected for subsequent HBR-blocking studies.

In the HBR-shielding test using blue light (425 nm, 2000 μW/cm2) as a strong HBR source (Fig. 2f), commercial anti-HBR eye cream (Estée Lauder) and sunscreens (LANCOME, L’ORÉAL, and ANESSA; 2.0 mg/cm2) demonstrated poor HBR-shielding ability, with shielding ratios below 10%. Raw sunscreen agents including TiO2 (20 mg/mL; 2.0 mg/cm2) and ZnO (20 mg/mL; 2.0 mg/cm2) showed poor HBR-shielding ability (<15%) (Fig. S13), which might be due to the fact that TiO2 and ZnO are unable to form a continuous and dense protective layer when lacking other components in sunscreens. In contrast, P alone (20 mg/mL, 2.0 mg/cm2) exhibited moderate HBR-shielding capability, with a shielding ratio of 60.5%, while the P-Zn(II) complex (P: 20 mg/mL, Zn(II): 280 μg/mL, 2.0 mg/cm2) blocked the majority of HBRs, achieving a shielding ratio of 97.6%. These results demonstrate that the rationally designed polymer-Zn(II) complex is not only safe for cells but also fills the gap in HBR protection left by current commercial products.

We investigated the coordination between P and Zn(II) and the resulting spectral redshift induced by this interaction. Atomic absorption spectroscopy (AAS) indicated a coordination ratio of 1.79, suggesting the coexistence of both 1:1 and 2:1 coordination forms in the P-Zn(II) complex. The spectral redshift was further analyzed using the projector augmented-wave (PAW) method based on DFT (Fig. 2g). Before coordination with Zn(II), the hydroxyquinoline-DHPM structure exhibits a HOMO (highest occupied molecular orbital)-LUMO (lowest unoccupied molecular orbital) energy gap of 4.25 eV. Upon binding with Zn(II), the HOMO energy increases while the LUMO energy decreases, resulting in a reduced energy gap of 3.11 eV for the 1:1 P-Zn(II) complex and 2.86 eV for the 2:1 P-Zn(II) complex. This decrease in excitation energy causes a spectral redshift of the P-Zn(II) complexes relative to P, thereby extending the absorption band from the UVR region to the HBR region.

Protection of cells by the P-Zn(II) complex against HBR-induced damage

We evaluated the protective effect of the P-Zn(II) complex against HBR-induced damage in L929 cells using CCK-8 assay. Cells were cocultured with P-Zn(II) (P: 20 mg/mL, Zn(II): 280 μg/mL) and subsequently exposed to different HBR doses (5, 10, and 20 J/cm2) under a 425 nm HBR source (8620.83 μW/cm2). P (20 mg/mL) and Zn(II) (2 μg/mL) were tested in parallel. Cells directly exposed to HBR were used as the control, while cells cultured only in the medium served as the blank. Increasing HBR dosage led to a significant decrease in cell viability (Fig. 3a). At an HBR dose of 20 J/cm2—roughly estimated as continuous mobile phone exposure for ∼80 days, computer exposure for ∼118 days, indoor LED lighting for ∼7 days, or ∼4.5 h of sunlight (Fig. 3b)—cell viability decreased below 60%. At their safe concentrations, Zn(II) alone offered minimal protection against HBR, whereas P provided partial but incomplete protection. We investigated in detail the cellular damage induced by high-dose HBRs (20 J/cm2), including cell death, severe oxidative stress, pronounced cellular senescence, and subsequent secretion of the SASP characterized by changes in inflammatory and growth factors. Cells were separately cocultured with P (20 mg/mL), Zn(II) (2 μg/mL), and the P-Zn(II) complex (P: 20 mg/mL, Zn(II): 280 μg/mL) to evaluate their protective effects in parallel. Cells cultured in medium alone served as the blank.

We observed live (green) and dead (red) cells using fluorescein diacetate (FDA) and propidium iodide (PI) double staining. HBRs clearly inhibited cell proliferation and induced cell death (Fig. 3c). Dead cells were also present in cultures treated with Zn(II) or P alone. Among the three samples, the P-Zn(II) complex provided the most effective protection, with the ratios of live and dead cells comparable to those in the blank (Fig. 3d).

We assessed HBR-induced oxidative stress in different treatment groups using the dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) probe. Following HBR irradiation, cells exhibited strong green fluorescence, indicating elevated intracellular ROS levels (Fig. 3e and Fig. S14). Zn(II) alone had minimal effect on reducing this fluorescence, whereas P partially decreased the fluorescence intensity. Notably, the P-Zn(II) complex almost completely suppressed intracellular ROS generation, with fluorescence intensity comparable to that of unirradiated cells in the blank group (Fig. 3f).

We further investigated HBR-induced cellular photoaging by staining (48 h after the HBR-irradiation) senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal), a typical marker of cellular senescence. Following HBR irradiation, cells exhibited strong blue staining, indicating that HBR induced considerable cellular photoaging (Fig. 3g). Cells treated with Zn(II) or P alone still showed noticeable blue SA-β-Gal signals. In contrast, cells treated with the P-Zn(II) complex displayed almost no blue staining, with SA-β-Gal intensity comparable to that of the unirradiated blank group (Fig. 3h).

We further used an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to investigate HBR-induced photoaging with the SASP [[38], [39], [40]], focusing on TGF-β1 (a representative growth factor in the TGF-β signaling pathway), IL-6 (an inflammatory factor in the IL-17 pathway), and TNF-α (an inflammatory factor in the TNF pathway) (Fig. 3i). HBR-irradiated cells exhibited the clear SASP, characterized by a considerable decrease in TGF-β1 and increases in IL-6 and TNF-α. Treatment with the P-Zn(II) complex effectively restored TGF-β1 levels to promote tissue repair and reduced IL-6 and TNF-α levels to suppress HBR-induced inflammation, bringing their expression close to that of the blank group. These results indicate that HBRs can induce cellular damage and senescence via multiple pathways, and that the P-Zn(II) complex efficiently mitigates these HBR-induced effects at the cellular level (Fig. 3j).

In our design, the P-Zn(II) complex could block HBRs to avoid the HBR-induced dysregulation of signaling pathways in cells. Therefore, we further performed RNA-sequencing analysis to investigate the protective effects of the P-Zn(II) complex against HBR-induced alterations in signaling pathways and gene expression. Cells were cocultured separately with P, Zn(II), or P-Zn(II) and then exposed to HBR (20 J/cm2), with cells exposed to HBR alone serving as the control (HBR group). Volcano plots showed few differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between the HBR and Zn(II) groups, whereas a large number of DEGs were observed in comparisons between the HBR group and the P or P-Zn(II) groups (Fig. 4a). These results indicate that, following HBR irradiation, cells treated with P or the P-Zn(II) complex exhibit gene expression profiles considerably different from unprotected cells. Gene Ontology (GO) analysis (Fig. 4b) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis (Fig. 4c) revealed that many DEGs between the HBR group and the P-Zn(II) group were enriched in classical senescence-related pathways, including those associated with oxidative stress, inflammation, and cell growth. Notably, the P-Zn(II) complex protected cells by preventing the HBR-induced upregulation of key signaling pathways involved in oxidative stress and inflammatory responses, which are known contributors to cellular senescence [[41], [42], [43], [44]], including MAPK & MAP kinase tyrosine/serine/threonine phosphatase activity, Fc-gamma receptor, NF-κB, TNF, and IL-17 signaling pathways. Meanwhile, signaling pathways related to cell growth and metabolism [[45], [46], [47]]—such as glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis (chondroitin sulfate/dermatan sulfate), heteromeric SMAD protein complex formation, SMAD protein-mediated signal transduction, and the TGF-β signaling pathway—were considerably prevented from being downregulated by HBR. These results can be attributed to the excellent HBR-shielding ability of the P-Zn(II) complex, thereby preventing HBR-induced cellular damage. We also analyzed DEGs in these senescence-related pathways between the HBR group and the P group using GO (Fig. S15) and KEGG (Fig. S16) analyses. The number of DEGs and the magnitude of their enrichment in these pathways were generally less pronounced in the P-treated cells compared with the P-Zn(II)-treated cells. We further investigated the direct impact of the P-Zn(II) complex on HBR-induced cellular senescence using gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). In addition to considerably suppressing the HBR-activated oxidative stress response signaling pathway (Fig. S17), the P-Zn(II) complex resisted HBR-induced senescence by preventing the upregulation of the transcription repressor complex signaling pathway and the downregulation of the DNA mismatch repair signaling pathway (Fig. 4d). These effects were not observed between the HBR group and the P group (Fig. S18 and S19). We also evaluated the relative transcript levels of selected DEGs across different treatment groups, focusing on genes previously reported to be associated with cellular senescence [[48], [49], [50], [51], [52]]. The transcripts of these DEGs between the HBR and P-Zn(II) groups exhibited an almost opposite expression pattern (Fig. 4e), indicating that the HBR-blocking P-Zn(II) complex effectively prevents photoaging at the genetic level.

Overall, the RNA-sequencing results indicate that the P-Zn(II) complex blocks HBR to prevent HBR-induced activation of oxidative stress- and inflammation-related signaling pathways. Specifically, the P-Zn(II) complex prevents the dysregulation of the TGF-β/SMAD signaling landscape by blocking HBR, thereby averting the pro-inflammatory and senescence-associated responses while preserving the expression of key regenerative mediators, such as Smad6, Smad9, and Itgb6. Consequently, HBR-induced ROS accumulation and the secretion of SASP factors (IL-6 and TNF-α) are prevented, and TGF-β level is maintained to promote tissue repair. These findings reveal the protective effects of the P-Zn(II) complex against HBR-induced photodamage at genetic levels.

In vivo Anti-HBR evaluation of the P-Zn(II) complex

We further evaluated the anti-HBR performance of the P-Zn(II) complex in vivo. All animal procedures in this study were performed in conformity with the Guidelines for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of Tsinghua University (approval number: 20-TL1.G25-1) and the 3R principles (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement), and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee. All experimental animal procedures were performed under anesthesia, and all efforts were made to minimize suffering.

We further examined the skin samples using histological and immunoregulatory analyses. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining revealed that HBR irradiation caused pronounced infiltration of inflammatory cells in the dermis (black arrows) and evident atrophic damage to hair follicles (blue arrows), including considerable alterations in their morphology and size. Anessa provided minimal protection against these damages, whereas skin treated with the P-Zn(II) complex exhibited negligible inflammatory cell infiltration and healthy hair follicles (Fig. 5a, middle). Masson's trichrome staining showed that HBRs induced extensive fragmentation and loss of collagen fibers (stained blue), accompanied by hyperkeratosis and fibrinous exudates (stained red), indicative of typical photoaging. Again, Anessa offered little protection, while the P-Zn(II)-treated skin retained almost intact collagen fibers with minimal keratin and fibrin deposition (Fig. 5a, bottom). Quantitative analysis confirmed that collagen fiber content in the P-Zn(II) group was comparable to that in the blank group (Fig. 5b). These results demonstrate that the P-Zn(II) complex effectively prevents HBR-induced tissue damage.

We further evaluated typical aging markers in skin tissues using immunofluorescence staining. HBR irradiation caused a pronounced decrease in Lamin B1 (green) and an increase in p21 (red), indicating considerable photoaging (Fig. 5c). Anessa provided limited protection against these changes, whereas the P-Zn(II) complex effectively preserved the levels of Lamin B1 and p21, maintaining them comparable to those observed in the unirradiated blank group (Fig. 5d).

We further examined HBR-induced photoaging in vivo by assessing SASP components, including TGF-β1, IL-6, and TNF-α (Fig. 5e and f). Following direct HBR irradiation, TGF-β1—closely associated with collagen deposition and regeneration—was considerably reduced, while the inflammatory factors IL-6 and TNF-α were strongly upregulated in the skin tissue. Treatment with Anessa had minimal effect on preventing the decrease of TGF-β1 or the increase of IL-6 and TNF-α. In contrast, levels of TGF-β1, IL-6, and TNF-α in the P-Zn(II) group were comparable to those in the blank group, demonstrating that the P-Zn(II) complex effectively blocks HBRs to inhibit HBR-induced SASP.

Initial biosafety evaluation of the P-Zn(II) complex

Many commercial sunscreens contain small-molecule UV filters that can penetrate the skin, raising safety concerns. Therefore, we assessed the transdermal penetration of the P-Zn(II) complex using a Franz diffusion cell system and pig skin as previously reported [53,54]. We evaluated the transdermal penetration of the P-Zn(II) complex using Rhodamine B (479.01 g mol−1) as a small-molecule control. Pig skin was fixed in a Franz diffusion cell (3.14 cm2, 37°C, 300 rpm) with 8.0 mL of normal saline in the receiving compartment. The P-Zn(II) complex (P: 20 mg/mL, Zn(II): 280 μg/mL, 0.314 mL, 2.0 mg/cm2) was applied to the donor compartment, while Rhodamine B (20 mg/mL, 2.0 mg/cm2) was tested in parallel. After 24 h, Rhodamine B penetrated the skin, turning the normal saline in the receiver purple, whereas the normal saline in the receiver of the P-Zn(II) complex remained colorless (Fig. 6a). Standard curves of absorbance versus concentration were constructed for both the P-Zn(II) complex (detection wavelength of P: 342 nm) (Fig. S21) and Rhodamine B (detection wavelength: 535 nm) (Fig. S22). Quantitative analysis showed that the transdermal rate of Rhodamine B reached 53.7% over 24 h, while that of the P-Zn(II) complex was only 3.5% (Fig. 6b). To measure the penetration depths of different samples in the skin, we further collected skin tissue sections after the experiment, and examined them by a fluorescence microscopy. The red fluorescence signal of Rhodamine B was observed throughout all skin layers of porcine skin, from the epidermis to the subcutaneous tissue. Conversely, the green fluorescence signal of the P-Zn(II) complex was only observed at the skin surface and in the infundibulum region of hair follicles, without penetrating through the dermis (Fig. S23). Although the P-Zn(II) complex exhibits limited transdermal penetration, it is important to evaluate its biosafety. Hemolysis assay showed that the hemolysis rate of red blood cells co-cocultured with the P-Zn(II) complex was below 1%, which was comparable to that of the negative control (normal saline), confirming the excellent hemocompatibility of the P-Zn(II) complex (Fig. S24).

We further studied the preliminary phototoxicity of the P-Zn(II) complex at the cellular level using a reported method [55]. L929 cells were co-cultured with the P-Zn(II) complex (P: 20 mg/mL, Zn(II): 280 μg/mL, in medium) for 1 h, and then were exposed to a high-dose UVA irradiation (365 nm, 20 mW/cm2, ∼12 J/cm2). After that, cells were co-cultured with a normal fresh medium for 24 h, and the cell viability was assessed through the CCK-8 assay. The unirradiated cells in the normal medium and the irradiated cells in the normal medium were parallelly tested. The irradiated cells in the normal medium showed a low cell viability (∼50%) due to the UVA damage, and the irradiated cells in the medium with the P-Zn(II) complex showed a high cell viability (>90%) (Fig. S25). These results indicate that the P-Zn(II) complex has excellent UVA-shielding ability at the cellular level, and it has low phototoxicity.

We continued to evaluate the biosafety of the P-Zn(II) complex in vivo. The P-Zn(II) complex (P: 20 mg/mL, Zn(II): 280 μg/mL, in normal saline) was applied to the dorsal skin of BALB/c mice (2.0 mg/cm2) for 24 h. After washing, almost no erythema or allergic reaction was observed, and the skin appeared comparable to untreated skin (Fig. 6c). Histological analysis of H&E-stained skin sections also showed no inflammatory infiltration among fibroblasts in the tissue, and the morphology of fibroblasts in the P-Zn(II)-treated skin showed no significant difference compared with those in untreated skin. These results indicate that P-Zn(II) complex has little effect on the activity of fibroblasts.

We further administered a single high-dose injection of the P-Zn(II) complex in vivo to assess the emergency toxicity when a large amount of P-Zn(II) entered the bloodstream in an extreme situation where the skin lost its protective function. Mice were injected with 600 μL of the P-Zn(II) complex (P: 20 mg/mL, Zn(II): 280 μg/mL, in normal saline) via the tail vein, and no mortality was observed within 72 h. Major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, were collected for histopathological analysis using H&E staining (Fig. 6d), showing no observable abnormalities compared with healthy mice. Serum levels of key functional biomarkers, including albumin (ALB), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine (Crea), and uric acid (UA), were also measured (Fig. 6e), and no significant differences were found between treated and healthy mice. These results demonstrate that the P-Zn(II) complex exhibits minimal skin irritation, low toxicity, and is a safe HBR-blocking sunscreen for potential users.

Possible extensions

The P-Zn(II) complex has demonstrated excellent biosafety and anti-HBR efficacy in mice. In this research, the P-Zn(II) complex is a polymer that is applied on the skin to block harmful blue rays. Its transdermal ability is much poor than that of small molecules. Therefore, we administered a single high-dose injection to assess the emergency toxicity when a large amount of P-Zn(II) entered the bloodstream in an extreme situation where the skin lost its protective function. However, when P-Zn(II) is repeatedly applied on damaged skin (such as users with skin wounds), evaluating its subacute toxicity through multiple administration can provide a rigorous safe dosage for use.

In cell-protection experiment, given the short co-culture time with the cells (only during HBR irradiation, 40 min) and the high molecular weight of P-Zn(II), only a few P-Zn(II) complex may enter the cells. Therefore, the P-Zn(II) complex mainly prevents HBR-induced damage by physically blocking HBRs as we designed, rather than by entering cells to directly regulate the relevant signaling pathways. Nevertheless, we cannot completely rule out the possibility that small amount of P-Zn(II) complex may enter cells and exert regulatory effects. In this research, the P-Zn(II) complex with Zn(II) at some concentration ranges increased the cell viability exceeding 100%, which could be attributed to the cell proliferation promoting effect of the released and unchelated free Zn(II) at certain low concentrations [56,57], as well as the P-Zn(II) complex that may enter cells during prolonged culture [58,59]. However, we cannot fully distinguish whether this effect originates from the small amount of released free Zn(II) or from the P-Zn(II) complex that enters cells during prolonged culture, or from both. In future research, we will systematically investigate the proliferative and reparative activities of the P-Zn(II) complex with different molecular weights using a post HBR repair model.

Although the P-Zn(II) complex exhibits limited skin penetration (3.5% over 24 h), this residual permeation may be attributed to the broad polydispersity index (PDI: 2.10) of the polymer prepared via free radical polymerization, allowing the lower-molecular-weight fraction to penetrate the skin. Purifying polymers through dialysis or fractionation precipitation techniques can yield polymers with high molecular weights and narrow PDIs. This should be beneficial for reducing the transdermal risk of the polymers and facilitating their practical application. Meanwhile, advanced controlled radical polymerization (CRP) techniques offer a powerful strategy to synthesize well-defined polymers with narrow PDIs. These approaches include single-electron transfer atom transfer radical polymerization (SET-ATRP) [[60], [61], [62]], sulfur-free reversible addition-fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT) emulsion polymerization [[63], [64], [65]], photoinduced electron/energy transfer-RAFT (PET-RAFT) [[66], [67], [68]], and photoinduced CRP et [[69], [70], [71]]. In future studies, these CRP techniques may be used to develop anti-HBR polymers with high molecular weights and narrow PDIs, thereby minimizing transdermal absorption while maintaining effective skin surface protection. Moreover, both molecular weight and polydispersity of P may affect the cellular uptake of the P-Zn(II) complex. In this work, P-Zn(II) complex is applied on the skin to block HBR. It has poor transdermal properties and little adverse effect on fibroblasts. Thus, we did not conduct a thorough study on the cellular uptake of the P-Zn(II) complex. In future research, polymers with different molecular weights and narrow PDIs can be prepared and used to further investigate the cellular uptake of P-Zn(II) complexes.

In in vivo anti-HBR study, the strong HBR dose (21910 μW/cm2, 1.0 h, ∼80 J/cm2) caused extensive sunburn-like injury on unprotected skin, resulting in animal suffering. We adopted 3 mice/group based on a careful balance between the 3R principles (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement) of ethical animal experimentation and the experimental objectives, and this animal number has been approved for certain studies that may cause suffering [[72], [73], [74]]. Under our experiment conditions, the protective effect of the P-Zn (II) complex was obvious (it completely prevented visible skin damage and nearly completely preserved collagen), and statistically significant inter-group differences were detectable with 3 mice/group while ensuring the welfare of the animals. In future, detailed studies on the differences between the sunburn groups should adopt more mice in each group. Meanwhile, we will try low-dose and long-term HBR exposure models in mice or use large animal models, and more animals per group will be included to obtain reliable results while avoiding severe harm to animals.

The bio-models (L929 mouse fibroblasts and a short-term female BALB/c mouse model) used in this research still have limitations to mimic human. The short-term high-dose HBR exposure is also different from the long-term daily HBR exposure. Thus, results obtained in this research can only be used as a reference for the development of anti-HBR sunscreens. In future research, we will study the anti-HBR performance of the P-Zn(II) complex using more skin-relevant models, such as primary human dermal fibroblasts, simple co-culture systems, and reconstructed human skin. Meanwhile, we will systematically study the genotoxicity of the P-Zn(II) complex using these skin-relevant models to complete the safety assessment. Based on these studies, we will try to recruit volunteers to evaluate the safety and the protective effect of the P-Zn(II) complex against long-term daily HBR doses.

This water-soluble P-Zn(II) complex efficiently blocks both UVRs and HBRs, making it a promising single active ingredient for sunscreens with a comfortable skin feel. However, under conditions of heavy sweating or water exposure, the current P-Zn(II) formulation may not remain on the skin surface, leading to reduced photoprotection. In future research, strategies such as tuning the ratio of hydrophilic to hydrophobic segments in the polymer or incorporating functional groups with strong skin affinity could be used to develop sunscreens with enhanced adhesion, providing prolonged protection.

Recently, antioxidant polymers have demonstrated considerable potential in managing the wound microenvironment and promoting tissue repair [75,76]. The P-Zn(II) complex contains anti-inflammatory zinc(II) and antioxidant DHPM structure [77,78]. Future studies could explore this potential by evaluating the P-Zn(II) complex in wound models, including sunburn-induced skin injury.

Conclusion

In summary, we have developed an anti-HBR polymer-metal ion complex, P-Zn(II), through a combination of multicomponent reactions, metal ion chelation, and polymer chemistry. Coordination between the hydroxyquinoline-DHPM moieties in the polymer and Zn(II) not only dramatically enhanced the polymer's HBR-blocking ability but also considerably improved the cellular safety of Zn(II). In cellular studies using L929 mouse fibroblasts, the P-Zn(II) complex effectively protected fibroblasts from HBR-induced cell death, oxidative stress, and senescence, including the formation of the SASP. RNA-sequencing analysis further confirmed that by blocking HBRs, the P-Zn(II) complex inhibited HBR-induced upregulation of signaling pathways associated with oxidative stress and inflammation. The P-Zn(II) complex also blocked HBRs to inhibit HBR-induced downregulation of pathways related to tissue repair, thereby genetically preventing HBR-induced photoaging. In mouse experiments (female BALB/c mouse) using an acute photodamaged model (short-term and strong HBR exposure), the P-Zn(II) complex was applied to the mouse skin like a conventional sunscreen and preliminarily prevented HBR-induced acute skin photodamage. In contrast, mouse skin covered with a commercial sunscreen exhibited noticeable sunburn, whereas the mouse skin protected by the P-Zn(II) complex showed no considerable difference compared with healthy, unirradiated skin. These results demonstrate that the P-Zn(II) complex is a promising anti-HBR ingredient for next-generation sunscreens and skincare products. This study highlights the potential of polymer-metal ion complexes as advanced photoprotective materials to mitigate HBR-induced skin damage, providing new insights for the development of value-added biomedical materials in skincare and light-protection applications.

Materials and methods

Materials

8-Hydroxyquinoline-2-carboxaldehyde (98%, Bide Pharmatech), 2-(acetoacetoxy) ethyl methacrylate (AEMA, 95%, Aladdin), thiourea (98%, Aladdin), chlorotrimethylsilane (98%, J&K), acetic acid (98%, Aladdin), poly(ethylene glycol) methyl ether methacrylate (PEGMA, Mn ∼ 950 g mol−1, Sigma), 2,2′-azobis(2-methylpropionitrile) (AIBN, 99%, Aladdin), N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, AR, Aladdin), tetrahydrofuran (AR, Aladdin), phosphate buffered solution (PBS, pH ∼ 7.2-7.4, 0.01 M, Solarbio), 2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid) (ABTS, 98%, J&K), Roswell Park Memorial Institute-1640 (RPMI-1640) culture medium (Corning-Cellgro), serum-free RPMI-1640 culture medium (Gibco), fluorescence live/dead viability assay kit (KeyGEN Bio TECH), cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime), reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay kit (Beyotime), senescence β-galactosidase (SA-β-Gal) staining kit (Beyotime), mouse proinflammatory cytokine multiplex ELISA kit including IL-6 and TNF-α (Arigobio), mouse TGF-β1 ELISA kit (Beyotime), TRIzol (Invitrogen), HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies (Servicebio), and IL-6/TNF-α/TGF-β1/p21/Lamin B1 antibody (Proteintech) were used as purchased. L929 cells were purchased from Wuhan Pricella Biotechnology Ltd.

Instruments

Proton nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR) spectra and carbon nuclear magnetic resonance (13C NMR) spectra were obtained using a JEOL JNM-ECA400 (400 MHz) spectrometer for all samples. The ESI-MS data were collected using a Micro TOF-QII Bruker mass spectrometer. The viscosity data were obtained by a Brookfield DV-II + PRO rotational viscometer. Gel permeation chromatography (GPC) analyses were performed using DMF containing 50 mM LiBr as the eluent. The GPC system was a Shimadzu LC-20AD pump system consisting of an auto-injector, a PLgel 5 μm guard column (50 × 7.5 mm) followed by three PL gel 5 μm MIXED-D columns (300 × 7.5 mm), and a Shimadzu RID-20A refractive index detector. The GPC system was calibrated with polystyrene standards of narrow molecular weight distribution (Mn: 1300 to 2,630,000 g mol−1). Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was performed using TA Instruments Q2000 at a scanning rate of 10°C min−1. UV-vis transmittance spectra were obtained by a PerkinElmer Lambda 750 spectrometer. The phototoxicity evaluation was performed by a SCOUT UVLED (NX3035-15K, Yancheng Nuoxin Electronic Equipment Co., Ltd.) with the emission wavelength range of 365 ± 3 nm. The concentration of Zn(II) was detected by a Thermo Scientific iCETM3500 atomic absorption spectrometer. Cell viability was measured via the CCK-8 assay by a VICTORTM X3 PerkinElmer 2030 multilabel plate reader. All the fluorescence images were collected using a Zeiss LSM 780 laser scanning confocal microscopy. All the histological and immunohistochemical (IHC) images of mice were taken by a Nikon Eclipse Ci-E/Ci-L/Ci-S microscope.

HBR exposure system

The HBR irradiance levels were monitored and calibrated using an HBR detector (LH-137, Shenzhen Lianhuicheng Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) with a spectral response range of 400-450 nm and a measurement range of 1 μW/cm2 to 1000 W/cm2. For cell experiments, the HBR light source was a custom LED array (Shenzhen Boxing Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) with a centre wavelength of 425 nm (bandwidth: 420-430 nm) and overall dimensions of 225 × 185 × 150 mm. This source was parallelly fixed ∼8.0 cm above the cell culture plate to fully illuminate the well area, and the actual HBR irradiance at the plate surface was calibrated to be 8620.83 μW/cm2 through the HBR detector. For the in vitro HBR-shielding test and in vivo anti-HBR evaluation, a custom flashlight-type LED source (Shenzhen Yinghuochong Semiconductor Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China) with a wavelength of 425 ± 2.5 nm and an emission aperture (diameter: ∼3.5 cm) was used. In the shielding test, the source was parallelly fixed at ∼3.5 cm above the samples, and the HBR irradiance at the sample position was calibrated to be 2000 μW/cm2 through the HBR detector. For the in vivo evaluation, the source was parallelly fixed ∼1.0 cm above the dorsal skin of the mice, and the calibrated HBR irradiance at the skin surface was 21910 μW/cm2 through the HBR detector.

Synthesis of the monomer (M)

8-Hydroxyquinoline-2-carboxaldehyde (3.46 g, 20 mmol), AEMA (4.28 g, 20 mmol), thiourea (2.28 g, 30 mmol), and chlorotrimethylsilane (4.35 g, 40 mmol) were added in DMF (10.0 mL) in a round flask. The mixture was stirred in an oil bath (80°C) for 4 h. The mixture was precipitated in deionized water and washed with a diethyl ether/petroleum ether solution (1:2, v/v) for three times to obtain the target monomer (M, 8.14 g, 95.3%). Details for the synthesis of M were provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Preparation of P

M (4.27 g, 10 mmol), PEGMA (9.50 g, 10 mmol), and AIBN (32.84 mg, 0.2 mmol) were dissolved in DMF (20.0 mL) in a round flask. The mixture was purged by N2 for 30 min then stirred in an oil bath (75°C) for 24 h. The polymerization was quenched in an ice-water bath, and a sample (∼0.5 mL) was taken out for 1H NMR and GPC analyses. The target polymer (P) was obtained after precipitation in diethyl ether and dried to constant weight in a vacuum oven (12.9 g, yield: ∼93.8%).

Cellular HBR-protection experiments

L929 cells (a fibroblast cell line from mice) were cultured in a RPMI-1640 medium with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin and streptomycin. The cells were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2. Culture medium was changed every day to maintain the exponential growth of cells.

P-Zn(II) (P: 20 mg/mL, Zn(II): 280 μg/mL), P (20 mg/mL), and Zn(II) (2 μg/mL) were prepared in culture medium as different samples. We assessed the protective capacity of the P-Zn(II) against HBR-induced damage on L929 cells using the CCK-8 assay. L929 cells (∼105 cells/mL) were seeded in a 96-well plate in culture medium (100 μL) and incubated for 24 h. The culture medium was replaced by different samples, and the cells were exposed to different doses of HBR (5, 10 and 20 J/cm2) under a HBR source (425 nm, 8620.83 μw/cm2) at 37°C. After HBR irradiation, cells were washed three times using PBS and cultured in new culture medium at 37°C for 24 h. Then, cells were cultured by 100 μL of culture medium with 10% CCK-8 reagent for 2 h before observation. The plate was put into a microplate reader to record the absorbance at 450 nm. HBR-irradiated cells without samples served as a control. Unirradiated cells were used as a blank.

We investigated in detail the cellular damage caused by the high-dose HBR (20 J/cm2). L929 cells (∼105 cells/mL) were seeded and cultured in a 12-well plate in culture medium (1.0 mL) for 24 h, then the culture medium was replaced by different samples. Cells were exposed to the high doses of HBR (20 J/cm2). HBR-irradiated cells without any sample served as a control. Unirradiated cells were used as a blank. After HBR irradiation, cells were washed three times using PBS and cultured in new culture medium.

HBR irradiation-induced ROS was evaluated 6 h after the HBR-irradiation using DCFH-DA probes. Cells were washed three times with PBS and incubated with DCFH-DA (10 μmol/L) in serum-free RPMI-1640 culture medium for 20 min followed by observation using a confocal laser scanning microscopy.

24 h after the irradiation, the culture media in different groups were collected and centrifuged (5000 r/min, 5 min) to obtain the supernatant. The supernatant was evaluated by the corresponding enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to detect the levels of TGF-β1, IL-6, and TNF-α in cells. The normalized concentrations of TGF-β1, IL-6, and TNF-α were obtained by dividing the cytokine concentrations in the supernatant by the relative viable cell fraction from the CCK-8 assay. Meanwhile, fluorescein diacetate (FDA)/propidium iodide (PI) double staining was used to visualize the live-dead state of cells 24 h after the HBR-irradiation. Briefly, cells were incubated with a staining solution (FDA: 5 μg/mL, PI: 5 μg/mL, PBS as the solvent) for 10 min, then washed three times with PBS followed by observation under a fluorescence microscope. Live cells appeared green, while dead cells were stained red. We counted live cells (stained green) and dead cells (stained red) by manual visual counting, and cross-checked the data by using the Image J software.

HBR irradiation-induced cellular senescence was evaluated 48 h after the HBR-irradiation using SA-β-Gal staining. Cells were washed once with PBS and fixed with β-galactosidase staining fixative for 15 min at 25°C. After three washes with PBS, cells were incubated with the SA-β-Gal staining working solution at 37°C overnight in a CO2-free environment, followed by observation under a light microscope.

Whole transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) and bioinformatic analysis

RNA-Seq analysis was performed to analyze the RNA of L929 cells. Before tests, P-Zn(II) (P: 20 mg/mL, Zn(II): 280 μg/mL), P (20 mg/mL), and Zn(II) (2 μg/mL) were prepared with culture medium as different samples. L929 cells (∼105 cells/mL) were seeded in a 6-well plate in culture medium (2 mL) and incubated for 24 h. The culture medium was replaced by different samples, and the cells were exposed to HBR (20 J/cm2) under a HBR source (425 nm, 8620.83 μw/cm2) at 37°C. Then, the cells were washed three times by PBS and cultured in new culture medium for 24 h. Cells were washed three times, and treated by a TRIzol solution (2 mL) to obtain RNA samples. These samples were thoroughly ground with a glass grinder. The RNA purity and quantification were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. The RNA integrity was assessed using an Agilent 2100 bioanalyzer. The libraries were then built using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Library Preparation Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. Transcriptome sequencing was performed by OE Biotech (Shanghai, China). The llumina Novaseq 6000 platform was used to sequence the libraries. And 150bp of paired-end reads were generated. Fastp [1] was used to manipulate the raw reads in fastq format, underlying ving low quality reads to obtain clean reads. HISAT22 was applied to map the clean reads to the reference genome. Calculate FPKM3 for each gene and obtain read counts for each gene using HTSeq-count4. PCA analysis was subjected to R (version 3.2.0) in order to assess the biological reproducibility of the samples. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2. Fold change >2 or <0.5 and P value < 0.05 was set as the threshold for significantly differential expression genes (DEGs). Hierarchical clustering analysis of DEGs was carried out using R (v 3.2.0) to show the expression pattern of genes in different groups. DEGs were enriched using R (v 3.2.0) for Gene Ontology (GO) analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis. Multiple-testing correction was applied to the DEGs and enrichment analyses using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. Histograms and bubble plots of significantly enriched terms were plotted using R (v 3.2.0). Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using GSEA software. The analysis used a predefined set of genes, and the genes were sorted according to their degree of differential expression in different groups. The raw sequence data reported in this paper have been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive (GSA) in National Genomics Data Center, China National Center for Bioinformation/Beijing Institute of Genomics, Chinese Academy of Sciences (GSA accession number: CRA044407) that are publicly accessible at https://ngdc.cncb.ac.cn/gsa.

HBR-protection experiments in vivo

BALB/c mice (8 weeks old, female, 18-21 g, 3 mice in every group) were used in the HBR-protection experiments. All animal procedures in this study were performed in conformity with the Guidelines for Care and Use of Laboratory Animals of Tsinghua University (approval number: 20-TL1.G25-1) and the 3R principles (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement), and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee. All experimental animal procedures were performed under anesthesia, and all efforts were made to minimize suffering.

The dorsal hairs of the mice were removed by an electric shaver, and the hair removal cream was applied to the shaved area. After 5 min, the hair removal cream was wiped off and the dorsal skin was cleaned by PBS. 24 h after that, the P-Zn(II) complex (P: 20 mg/mL, Zn(II): 280 μg/mL, 2.0 mg/cm2) was applied on the skin of mice. When the P-Zn(II) complex formed a coating on the skin after 5 min, the skin was irradiated by a strong HBR (425 nm, 21910 μw/cm2, 1.0 h, 80 J/cm2). The commercial sunscreen Anessa (2.0 mg/cm2) was parallelly tested. The HBR-irradiated mice without protection served as a control, and the unirradiated mice served as a blank. Skins in different groups were photographed 24 h after HBR-irradiation. The skin tissues were analyzed by histology and immunohistochemistry. Skin specimens were fixed in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde for 24 h at 25°C. Then these specimens were embedded in paraffin. Histomorphology staining and analysis were performed on sections (4 μm thickness) of the HBR-irradiated skin tissues and the HBR-unirradiated skin tissues. These sections were prepared for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining and Masson staining according to the manufacturer's protocols. For immunohistochemistry detection, skin sections were incubated in primary antibodies (IL-6, TNF-α, and TGF-β1) for 12 h at 25°C. Then, the sections were incubated with HRP-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibodies, and then stained with 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) solution. Finally, hematoxylin staining was used to detect the nucleus. The samples were sealed with a neutral adhesive for observation using an Olympus microscope. For the immunofluorescence detection, skin sections were incubated in primary antibodies (Lamin B1 and p21) for 12 h at 25°C. The samples were washed three times with PBS, and the corresponding fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied followed by incubation for 50 min at 25°C in dark. Subsequently, autofluorescence quenching reagent was applied. After three additional PBS washes, the sections were mounted with DAPI-containing anti-fade mounting medium. Images were acquired using a Nikon Eclipse Ci-E/Ci-L/Ci-S microscope.

In vitro transdermal study

The transdermal penetration of the P-Zn(II) complex was evaluated using a Franz diffusion cell system (TK-12D, 3.14 cm2 effective diffusion area) with Rhodamine B (479.01 g/mol) serving as a small-molecule control. Excised porcine skin was mounted between the donor and receiver compartments. The receiver compartment was filled with normal saline (8.0 mL) and maintained at 37°C with continuous magnetic stirring at 300 rpm. The P-Zn(II) complex (P: 20 mg/mL, Zn(II): 280 μg/mL) was applied to the donor compartment at a dose of 2.0 mg/cm2 (0.314 mL), while Rhodamine B (20 mg/mL, 2.0 mg/cm2) was tested in parallel. At 0 h and 24 h, the receiver fluid was photographed to record any visible colour change. Standard curves correlating absorbance to concentration were established for both P-Zn(II) complex (342 nm) and Rhodamine B (535 nm) to enable quantitative analysis. After 24 h, the skin samples were removed and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and tissue sections were prepared for observation by a fluorescence microscopy to assess penetration depth. Samples were counterstained with DAPI (blue) for nuclear visualization. Rhodamine B was detected with excitation at 557 nm and emission at 570 nm (red), and P-Zn(II) complex was recorded with excitation at 491 nm and emission at 516 nm (green).

Theoretical calculation

Density functional theory (DFT) as implemented in the Materials studio (MS) was used in all calculations. The exchange-correlation potential is described by using the generalized gradient approximation of Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (GGA-PBE). The projector augmented-wave (PAW) method is employed to treat interactions between ion cores and valence electrons. The plane-wave cutoff energy was fixed to 500 eV. Given structural models were relaxed until the Hellmann-Feynman forces smaller than −0.02 eV/Å and the change in energy smaller than 10−6 eV was attained. The vacuum thickness was set to be 20 Å to minimize interlayer interactions. Grimme's DFT-D3 methodology was used to describe the dispersion interactions among all the atoms in adsorption models.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 26.0 and Image J softwares were employed for statistical analysis and all charts were generated using GraphPad Prism 9.0. For all image-based assays, images were randomly captured from ≥5 non-overlapping fields per sample, and the thresholds were set using the Otsu automatic thresholding method by Image J to ensure consistent thresholding across all samples in the same experiment. Each test was performed in triplicate and are presented as the mean ± SD. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test was used to determine statistical significance.
