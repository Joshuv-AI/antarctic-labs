Opinions of digital technology held by independent-living older people: a survey and generational comparison
Authors: Stephen Potter, Mark Hawley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Services delivered using digital technologies, including technology-enabled care services (TECS), have been proposed to help people age independently and well in their own homes. However, the uptake of digital services by older users appears uneven, suggesting that opportunities to assist people are being missed. A better understanding of older people’s attitudes towards digital technologies and TECS could help to address this issue. Evidence of the attitudes held by independent-living older people was gathered through a cross-sectional questionnaire survey of people aged 55 and over. The participants were residents of independent-living schemes and community-dwelling members of local communities. A total of 230 people completed the questionnaire. The participants’ responses were analysed in aggregate and in comparative terms, according to the ‘technology generation’ to which each participant belongs. The participants were generally positive about digital technologies and TECSs, which suggests that digital services play a role in supporting people in their ability to age well. Considering these results should aid in the development of effective TECS. However, participants also express misgivings about digital technologies; this was particularly marked in the case of the generation aged 75 and older. Generational differences suggest that different strategies are required that consider older people’s distinct opinions and experiences with technologies.

Introduction

In societies around the world, supporting older people to live well and healthily in their own homes for longer would reduce the utilisation of health and care services such as hospitals and care home facilities. Moreover, research suggests that such ‘ageing in place’ coincides with the wishes of many people as they grow older (Hatcher et al., 2019; Sixsmith & Sixsmith, 2008), even among the ‘very old’, that is, those aged 85 and older (Stones & Gullifer, 2016). However, bringing this about requires the provision of effective support services at a time when demographic and social changes are placing unprecedented burdens on conventional health, care and social systems. The deployment of effective technology-enabled care services represents one way by which older people might be supported to remain living independently for longer.

Technology-enabled care services

The term ‘technology-enabled care services’ (TECS) is here used to refer to services built around digital devices and infrastructure for the purpose of providing support or assistance for everyday living. While not exclusively intended for supporting older people (here considered to be those aged 55 and older), this is one common application area of TECS. In its widest sense, TECS incorporates everyday general-purpose consumer products such as smartphones, smart watches and smart speakers, as well as more specialist services that target specific daily challenges. A recent survey (Alden, 2024) categorised the different roles that TECS can play in people’s lives; these include aiding communication (e.g. through social media, smartphones and more specialised tools such as speech-generation devices), helping with household tasks (e.g. with robot vacuum cleaners, smart home systems), providing personal safety (e.g. using fall alarm systems and GPS tracker devices), and maintaining health and wellbeing (e.g. through domestic passive sensor monitoring systems). The range of TECS is wide, and they do not share any specific design concept, features or functionality. TECS might be used directly by older people and/or indirectly by their formal and informal care networks. They might be provided and maintained (and, in some cases, paid for) by health, care or housing service providers; alternatively, the TECS might be chosen by, paid for, and provided directly to the older person or family members themselves on a commercial basis.

In part because of the potentially extensive and fluid nature of what constitutes TECS, it is unclear to what extent TECS are currently used by older people, how they are delivered, what their key outcomes are and whether they are achieving these, and what the facilitators and barriers to implementation and delivery are (Gibson et al., 2025). As a proxy for usage statistics, recent data suggest that older people’s use of smart and mobile phones—which provide generic means of everyday support – is relatively high but decreases with increasing age and is lower than that of younger age groups (Charness & Boot, 2022). In the UK, it was estimated that, in 2024, 89% of those aged 55–64 and 67% of those aged 65 and older used smartphones (OFCOM, 2025). However, 21% of 65s and over said that they did not have access to the internet in their homes, for which the main reason given (by 88%) was that they had no need to go online or else were not interested in doing so. On the other hand, the ability to access to health services online is acknowledged to be the spur most likely to encourage (or coerce) this group to arrange home access.

Alongside this, there is evidence that suggests that opportunities, even using relatively simple technologies, are being missed for deploying TECS to help people (TEC Action Alliance, 2024). The evidence suggests that ‘TECS do not achieve their therapeutic goals and are not cost-effective’ (Gibson et al., 2025). Possible reasons for this include a lack of interoperability, of training and of digital literacy more generally (in both users and their caregivers), of personalisation and co-developed solutions, and of trust and confidence in digital technologies, which in turn are related to the need for reliable evidence of the benefits of TECS and for safeguards to be placed around its use (Keck & Godec, 2024; Mistry, 2024).

This is the backdrop against which to consider the conditions necessary for the deployment of successful digital TECS for older people. Clearly, the development of effective TECS relies on a thorough understanding of the support needs and wants of older people. It also requires an appreciation of the digital resources and literacy of prospective users. However, this is, in itself, insufficient to ensure the success of a TECS; to gain a better idea of the full range of factors involved, we must turn to theories of technology adoption.

Technology adoption and age

The reasons why older people adopt digital technology into their lives have been extensively researched in recent decades. A ‘persistent age-related digital divide’ was first recognised in 1995, shortly after the advent of the World Wide Web, and still exists (Charness & Boot, 2022), which suggests that age is a factor in technology uptake. Adopting a psychological viewpoint, Charness and Boot go on to discuss how both ‘person factors’ (an individual’s background, characteristics and affordances) and the ‘system features’ of the technology in question can affect the adoption and use of that technology. Drawing on a qualitative study, Marston et al. (2019) identify practical activities that older people cite as reasons for digital usage, and set these against uptake ‘facilitators’ (access and opportunity, feeling secure) and ‘detractors’ (difficulty, feeling apprehension, lack of interest).

From a technical perspective, The Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (UTAUT), and its extended UTAUT2 variant, two widely cited technology acceptance models (Venkatesh et al., 2003, 2012), propose that the successful adoption of new technology is dependent on a network of factors, including its perceived usefulness (and pleasure it could give), the effort required, the availability of the required resources, and value for money. The individual’s experience is explicitly acknowledged to play a role in modifying these constructs (although less clear is how to represent this experience), that is, the experience of growing up, living, and working with technologies that are old and new. Seen from this perspective, there is an inescapable relationship between an individual’s age and their experience with technology. Exposure to technology can play an important role in shaping attitudes; this role is more related to cohort differences in the developmental experiences of technology than it is to age per se (Charness & Boot, 2022). Building on earlier notions of ‘technology generations’ (Sackmann & Winkler, 2013), recent work has sought to make this relationship explicit and explore the extent to which membership of a particular generation affects the use of digital technologies (Ivan et al., 2020; Loos & Ivan, 2024). ‘Technology generations’ are defined as ‘birth cohorts whose conjunctive experience with technology is differentiated [i.e. from other generations] by social change’ (Sackmann & Winkler, 2013). A particular birth cohort – those born in a particular society during a defined period – will share certain experiences; when the formative years of late adolescence and young adulthood coincide with the experience of the widespread adoption of particular technologies considered to have a transformative effect on society, we can postulate that the cohort constitutes a technology generation. To make this more tangible, the technology generations postulated by Sackmann and Winkler (2013) are summarised in Table 1. It will be noted, however, that since the transformative technologies will not have been introduced at the same time in all places, the corresponding cohort period is subject to regional variations according to the availability of those technologies (Sackmann’s original work analysed the experiences of citizens of the German Democratic Republic).

Regardless of the precise definition, it seems reasonable to assume that the formative experience of certain technologies influences our adoption and use of new technologies as we grow older. If so, awareness of older people’s opinions and attitudes towards digital technologies, as viewed through a prism of generational differences in their experiences, will be useful for the development and adoption of new digital TECS.

Methods

Aims

With the elements of the survey described here, the principal objective was to gather evidence about the attitudes towards digital technologies held by older people living independently in the UK, and about their experience and opinions of digital TECS. A secondary aim was to explore differences in these attitudes and opinions across different technology generation cohorts in the sample of participants.

Design

The study used a cross-sectional survey design.

Survey development and content

A prior search of the literature revealed no existing survey instruments that addressed the topics covered here. Accordingly, a qualitative study was conducted, which involved 8 in-person focus groups attended by a total of 56 participants drawn from a representative sample of the target cohort, in order to identify relevant themes and then formulate corresponding questions. Hence, the questionnaire was designed specifically for this study: it did not reuse, either in whole or in part, existing survey instruments. It consisted of three distinct sections relating to demographics, to challenges faced in daily life, and to digital technology and TECS. The content of each section is described below. The responses were anonymous, and the participants were able to skip any questions they did not wish to answer. The questionnaire was independently checked for readability, comprehensibility and appropriateness and piloted with members of the project Patient and Public Involvement and Engagement (PPIE) panel.

The questionnaire was developed and – for some participants – distributed and completed using the online Qualtrics survey platform (Qualtrics, Provo, UT). An accessible paper-based version of the questionnaire was created for in-person completion by participants.

Demographics

A section devoted to the participant’s demographics, with closed questions covering gender, age (group), ethnic group or background, and employment status. For each question, participants were given the option of ‘prefer not to say’ (in addition to the option of simply not answering the question).

Everyday challenges

The next section of the questionnaire surveyed the challenges faced by respondents in their daily lives. In addition to providing a general understanding of the self-reported daily obstacles that independent-living older people encounter, this information can be used to orient wider co-development activities. The details of this section and the data collected are not discussed in this paper.

Digital technology and TECS

This section covers the respondents’ opinions about digital technology, and their awareness of and opinions about TECS.

The first subsection, entitled ‘Digital devices and you’, was intended to gauge participants’ attitudes towards digital technologies in general. It consisted of two questions. The first question asked participants to indicate the extent to which they agreed (using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from ‘strongly disagree’ to ‘strongly agree’) with each of the 10 statements about their use of digital devices. ‘Digital devices’ were defined as ‘devices that use digital data, which includes things such as computers, smartphones and smart speakers’. The statements (shown in Table 2) were, in general, phrased so as to present the use of digital devices in positive terms. In other words, the stronger the agreement, the greater the participant’s positive perception of aspects of the digital world, its ease of use, or its benefits.

The next question asked participants about their agreement with each of 10 statements about ‘potential dangers or drawbacks to the use of digital devices’, once again indicating the extent of their agreement using a 5-point Likert scale. Digital devices were defined as above; however, in contrast to the previous question, here, the statements were generally phrased in more negative terms (Table 3) – in other words, a stronger agreement would indicate a greater negative perception of aspects of the digital world and its effects on everyday life.

The second subsection, entitled ‘TECS and you’, focused specifically on digital TECS and was intended to gauge participants’ awareness, experience, and opinions of TECS, and to collect information about the importance they attached to different features of TECS development and use. As this paper focuses on general perceptions of digital technologies, a short summary of its contents; full details can be found in the supplementary material. Following a definition of TECS, participants were asked whether they were familiar with TECS, and given three possible responses, namely: ‘Yes, I have used TECS’; ‘Yes, but I have not used any TECS’; and ‘No’. Those who answered yes, regardless of whether they had direct experience with TECS, were then asked about the extent, again using a Likert scale, to which they agreed with 10 statements about the use of TECS. These included 4 positive statements about TECS, 4 negative and 2 phrased in neutral terms. The third and final question in this subsection asked participants to rate, using a Likert scale, the importance of 10 different features of the development and application of TECS. This question was asked of all participants, regardless of whether they were previously aware of the TECS.

Consent

The questionnaire was prefaced by an information sheet, and the purpose and content of the study were explained at in-person sessions. Participant consent was implied by completion and submission of the questionnaire.

Study population

The target participants for the survey were drawn from two distinct populations. The first of these comprises residents aged 55 years or over of independent-living housing schemes run by a project partner, a housing provider organisation (55 is the threshold age for residency in the provider’s independent living schemes). Typically, residents want and have the capability, perhaps with additional support, to maintain independent lives, and live in single-occupancy apartments with basic domestic telecare alarm services, located in residential schemes with shared communal areas.

Reflecting the historical usage of such housing services in the UK and, possibly, the cultural and religious unsuitability of the available housing for people from minority ethnic and religious backgrounds, scheme residents are recognised to belong predominantly to white British ethnic groups. Accordingly, to balance potential biases and to increase generality by looking beyond the residents and context of residential schemes, a second target population was chosen, consisting of community-dwelling people aged 55 years or over belonging to ‘seldom-heard’ communities found in the vicinity of the researchers’ university. ‘Seldom-heard’ communities are those that are typically underserved by conventional health, care, housing and other social services (and often underrepresented in research such as this). Their members usually live in areas with high levels of socioeconomic deprivation and often (but not necessarily) belong to minority ethnic background groups.

Data collection settings and methods

Data were collected in three distinct ways: online, in-person by project researchers, and in-person by community researchers.

Online data collection

An email with an attached information sheet explaining the purpose of the study was sent, via the housing provider, to all scheme residents for whom the provider had an email address. The email contained a link to the online version of the questionnaire. A reminder email was sent approximately 3 weeks after the first.

In-person data collection by project researchers

To redress any bias that might result from excluding residents who did not possess either an email address or the skills and confidence to complete an online questionnaire, several weeks after the reminder email had been sent, online participants’ postal codes were analysed to identify a convenience sample of 4 residential schemes from which no responses had been received. A visit to each scheme was organised, at which residents were invited to complete questionnaires with the assistance, where needed, of the researcher and local wellbeing and housing officers.

In-person collection by local community researchers

Eleven community researchers were recruited, all belonging to local ‘seldom-heard’ (and, in this case, ethnic minority) communities felt to be underrepresented in the University’s research work. The community researchers were told the purpose of the study, and given training in the ethical recruitment of study participants and the completion of the questionnaire. They were given printed copies of the questionnaire and each asked to survey up to 10 eligible members of their respective communities over the following month. They were each paid a fixed amount of GB£300 (approximately US$400) for their work.

Ethics approval

Following a review, this study was granted approval by the ethics committee of the School of Medicine and Population Health at the University of Sheffield (ref: 047766). The research was conducted in accordance with the principles stated in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection and analysis

Data were collected between May 2024 and September 2024. The online responses were stored on the Qualtrics platform. The completed paper questionnaires were manually entered into the same Qualtrics platform. The data were then downloaded from the platform for analysis. Data preprocessing and analysis of Likert-scale responses were performed using Microsoft Excel and R; analysis of open-ended textual responses was performed using Excel and NVivo. Missing values were not imputed.

In order to consider generational differences among the participants, we have adapted the technology generations of Sackmann and Winkler (2013), adjusting the cohort periods to align with the age groups in the demographics section of the questionnaire and excluding those under 55 years (Table 4). For convenience, we label these, from youngest to oldest, the ‘computer’, ‘electronic’ and ‘household’ technology generations.

To examine differences among the survey samples for the three generations, and with unequal sample sizes and no assumptions made about normality, Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to determine if the samples appeared to be drawn from different distributions. When a significant difference (p < 0.05) was found, Dunn’s pairwise tests, adjusted using the Bonferroni correction, were carried out for the three pairs of groups. Unless otherwise indicated, significant differences between pairs of generations reported below are at p < 0.05. Cronbach’s alpha was applied to measure the internal consistency of responses to grouped survey items, with a value of α > 0.7 interpreted as indicating sufficient internal consistency to justify the creation and application of a composite measure from the items.

Results

Participant demographics

The email invitation was sent to a total of 1321 residents, from whom 110 responses were received (8% response rate). An additional 32 residents completed questionnaires during in-person visits to residential schemes. The local community researchers collected a further 88 completed questionnaires in person from members of their communities. Hence, a total of 230 completed questionnaires were collected.

Table 5 presents the demographics of all participants, including the numbers belonging to each of the technology generations. There was a reasonably even distribution with 34% belonging to each of the computer and electronic generations (78/230 in each case) and 31% to the household generation (71/230). The 2 participants who preferred not to give their age group and the one for whom the age group is missing were all surveyed in person and confirmed that they were aged 55 years or over before completing the questionnaire; hence, while their data are included here in overall analyses it is excluded from generational comparisons.

Digital technologies: ‘Digital devices and you’

The first question in this section concerns the extent of participants’ agreement with positive statements about the use of digital technologies in general. Unless otherwise stated, for numerical analyses, we encode Likert responses incrementally from 1 for ‘Strongly disagree’ to 5 for ‘Strongly agree’.

Positive aspects of digital technologies

Across the 10 statements which comprise the first question, 90% (208/230) of participants rated all statements. Their responses demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.81, N = 208), suggesting that the items are suitable for forming a composite score. We postulate that this composite score indicates greater relative positivity about digital devices (‘digital positivity’ for short), with scores ranging from 10, indicating minimum digital positivity, to 50, indicating maximum digital positivity. Across all participants who rated all 10 statements, the mean digital positivity score was 35.33 (median = 36.0), indicating a tendency towards greater positivity. Among the different technology generations (Table 6), the household generation has the lowest mean and median (that is, less digital positivity).

Kruskal–Wallis and Dunn’s pairwise tests suggest very strong evidence (p < 0.001) of a difference between computer generation and household generation, and strong evidence (p < 0.01) of a difference between the electronic and household generations. There was no evidence of a difference between the computer and electronic generations. In other words, the data suggests that the household generation displays significantly less digital positivity than either of the younger generations.

Next, considering each of the statements independently, Figure 1 shows the mean agreement responses for each statement across all participants and across each generation. Kruskal–Wallis tests and subsequent Dunn’s pairwise tests were performed to explore significant differences among the responses to each statement. Table 7 summarises the significant differences in the responses of the generations, where these exist. In all cases, the significant difference was between the household generation and one of the other two generations, with the mean of the household generation being the lesser of the two.

Negative aspects of digital technologies

The second question in this section concerns the extent of participants’ agreement with negative statements about the use of digital technologies in general. Across the 10 statements which comprise this question, 89% (205/230) of the participants rated all the statements. Their responses demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.86, N = 205), justifying the formation of a composite score. We postulate that this composite score indicates greater relative discomfort with or mistrust of digital devices (‘digital negativity’ for short), with scores ranging from 10, indicating minimum digital negativity, to 50, indicating maximum digital negativity. Across all the participants, the mean digital negativity score is 30.24 (median = 31.0), indicating a slight tendency towards greater negatively across the participants.

Comparing the different technology generations (Table 8), there is a significant difference between the electronic and household generations (with the latter displaying greater digital negativity).

Next, considering each of the statements independently, Figure 2 shows the mean agreement for each statement across all participants and across each generation. Table 9 summarises the significant differences in the responses of the generations, where these exist. As for the positive statements, in all cases, the significant difference was between the household generation and one of the other two generations; however, here, the mean of the household generation was invariably the greater of the two (i.e. greater agreement with the negative statement).

TECS: ‘TECS and you’

The second half of the survey elements discussed here are related to TECS. Here, we present a summary of the results; the full results can be found in the supplementary material.

Among the 230 participants, 71% (163/230) stated that they were aware of TECS (as defined in the questionnaire), with 46% (106/230) stating that they had used TECS and 25% (57/230) saying that, although aware, they had not used TECS (and hence, the remaining 29% (67/230) said that they were not aware of TECS). All the participants responded to this question. TECS use (and overall awareness) is higher among the younger generations, although a χ2 test does not indicate significant differences.

Opinions of TECS

The participants who stated that they were aware of TECS were then asked about the extent of their agreement with various statements about TECS. Tests suggest a significant difference between generations only in their agreement with the statement, ‘TECS would probably be too difficult for people like me to use’: here, the household generation was significantly more in agreement with the statement than was the electronic generation.

TECS development and use features

All participants, regardless of whether they were previously aware of TECS, were asked to complete the final section, which asked for the importance of participants’ different aspects of the development and application of TECS. The tests suggested a difference only in agreement with ‘There are independent studies that show the benefits of using the TECS’: the household generation considered this feature to be significantly less important (p < 0.01) than did the immediately preceding generation.

Discussion

The present study explored the various attitudes held by people aged 55 and over living independently in the UK towards different aspects of digital technologies and TECS. The results are presented in terms of all the participants, which provides some idea of general attitudes, and in terms of three sequential generations of older people, namely, computer generation (those aged 55–64 at the time of the study), electronic generation (aged 65–74) and household generation (aged 75 years and over), which besides giving some idea of how attitudes vary among those generations, lend support to the idea that the experiences of different cohorts can significantly affect the ways in which their members view technologies. While there is a substantial literature investigating the opinions held of digital technology by older users, the particular lens adopted here of age and generational differences in experience among users at the particular time the study was conducted makes it difficult to make direct comparisons with the findings of any prior work.

Digital technologies: ‘Digital devices and you’

This section explored participants’ agreement with a series of statements that cast the use of digital technologies in, variously, a positive or a negative light.

Positive aspects of digital technologies

According to the composite measure of ‘digital positivity’, the household generation showed significantly less digital positivity and, indeed, the first quartile lies below the midpoint of the scale, suggesting that a not inconsequential number of this generation do not see digital technologies in a positive light. The computer and electronic generations were more similar in their responses, with the former displaying slightly more positivity.

As suggested by the composite measure, participants tended to agree with the individual positive statements about digital technologies (Figure 1). However, note that in each case, the mean response of the computer generation was greatest and that of the household generation the least, with the exceptions of ‘My family or friends help me to use digital devices’, for which the household generation had the highest mean agreement (and the computer generation the second highest, which is perhaps more surprising), and ‘Digital devices help me to maintain my independence’, for which the electronic generation has the highest mean agreement, suggesting that it was this ‘middle’ generation that best appreciated the implications of digital technologies for their (notion of) independence. The positive statement for which there was most agreement, both within each generation and across all participants, was ‘Digital devices help me to stay connected or engaged’: the participants understood and appreciated the practical social aspect of digital technologies. However, even here, it should be noted that there was a significantly less agreement seen in the household generation than in the other generations. This accords with previous work that suggest that older people leverage (intergenerational) relationships with family and friends to both provide practical support for technology adoption and as a way to remain connected with those family and friends (Freeman et al., 2020). Seen in this light, these relationships both facilitate and justify digital uptake, and, in turn, can play a key role in helping users to maintain their independence.

Another statement for which there were statistically significant differences in the responses was ‘Digital devices are designed with people like me in mind’, for which there was very strong evidence (p < 0.001) of a difference in opinion between the household and both the computer and electronic generations: participants from the oldest generation are (very highly) significantly less likely to feel that digital devices are made for people like them. Furthermore, the household generation considered themselves significantly less confident users of digital devices and to have significantly less facility when it comes to picking up how to use digital devices. The household generation is also significantly less inclined to feel safe using digital and to consider important the ‘look and feel’ of devices than are the computer generation. Together these add to the impression that the household generation feels itself to be more excluded by both the form and the function of digital technologies.

Negative aspects of digital technologies

After considering the positive aspects of digital technologies, participants were asked for the extent of their agreement with more negative aspects of digital technologies. Complementing the results for digital positivity, the composite measure of digital negativity is greatest among the household generations, although here, only the difference from the electronic generation is statistically significant. Taken together, the digital positivity and digital negativity results suggest that older people’s relationship with digitaltechnologies is, as might be expected, nuanced: while members of all generations can appreciate certain benefits of digital technology, they are also well aware of the possible pitfalls and downsides of its use, and the often-negative effects it can have on them and the society around them.

Considering the separate statements about negative aspects (Figure 2), where significant differences in the responses of the different generations occur, these are between the household generation (which invariably shows greater agreement with negative aspects) and both other generations. Specifically, there is evidence that members of the household generation are in greater agreement with ‘I feel frustrated, inadequate or afraid when I use digital devices’ and ‘Overall, my experiences of using digital devices have been unsatisfactory’, and strong evidence (p < 0.01) for greater agreement with ‘I feel negative or uncomfortable feelings when I use digital devices’. It is noteworthy that these statements (which overlap to some extent) all relate to the individual’s reflection on the feelings provoked by the use of digital technologies: it appears that significantly more members of the household generation feel themselves denigrated or belittled by their unsatisfactory experiences of digital technology.

Noticeable among other results is the high degree of (cross-generational) agreement with the statement ‘Nowadays it is necessary to use digital devices’, and, to a lesser degree, with the statement ‘I don’t always want to use a digital device but sometimes there is no alternative’: together, this suggests that the participants consider that digital technology has already become an essential component of everyday life but that this brings with it, if not necessarily a loss of agency, then a resigned lament for earlier, more conventional ways of doing things.

The different generations also show a similar degree of greater-than-average agreement that ‘Digital devices make me more vulnerable to fraud, scams or theft’: while the awareness of the digital space as a new medium for property crime undoubtedly contributes to negative reactions to digital technologies, this also reassuring, in the sense that participants are not oblivious or naïve when it comes to the risks to which the use of digital can make us vulnerable. A recent survey of people aged over 70 (Pérez-Rugosa et al., 2025) suggested that poor technical literacy and limited exposure to digital devices are correlated with greater degrees of technophobia. Less certain is whether it is causation or correlation that is at play here.

TECS: ‘TECS and you’

There was relatively high awareness of digital TECS (as defined here) across the participants, even in the case of the household generation, the generation with the least direct experience of TECS. Among those with some prior awareness, there is, in general, a positive view of TECS: irrespective of generation, participants generally agree that, for people such as them, TECS can be beneficial to health and wellbeing; can help maintain independence; and can help avoid social isolation. The participants also tended to disagree that TECS would be too difficult to use, that the use of TECS necessarily results in a loss of dignity or privacy, and that it brings with it social stigma. However, and perhaps more pointedly, they were less inclined to agree that TECS would be their preference where non-digital alternatives exist, and there was greater agreement with the view that TECS would be too expensive: it seems that perceived cost could deter potential beneficiaries. Finally, the household generation seems more likely to assume or accept that TECS will be too difficult for them to use.

There was high support for the importance of co-development of TECS; something that, perhaps, harks back to the earlier finding that the participants are ambivalent (with the household generation particularly sceptical) about whether digital technologies are designed with people like them in mind.

In terms of TECS development and use features, there is appreciation among potential users of a rounded, ‘best-practice’ approach to TECS co-development, and the wish to see evidence of benefits, consideration of technical and social compatibility, and personalisation, good information and ongoing support. The only significant generational difference was that the household generation was (highly) significantly less in agreement with the need for independent studies demonstrating the benefits of TECS than was the electronic generation.

Technology generations

Significant differences between the responses of the different generations were not encountered in all the aspects and features surveyed. However, it is noticeable that, where a significant difference was found, it was invariably a difference between the response of the household generation (the oldest) and that of one (or both) of the two younger generations. Members of the younger two generations seem more positive (and less negative) about digital technologies, and they are more likely to have used digital TECS. Hence, while this study does provide some evidence (in the UK in 2024) for the existence of a ‘technology generational break’ around the age of 75, there is little evidence here of such a break around the age of 65: where differences exist between the computer and electronic generations, they were less pronounced and never significant.

In this context, it would have been interesting to compare the responses of people aged under 55 to see whether differences can be discerned with those of younger generations (who, of course, are the older people of the future). The results might lead one to conclude that, as the current generations age, we will see more ‘digital positivity’ among even the oldest people. However, such a narrative fails to take into account the continuous development of digital technologies: while the computer and electronic generations might seem more at ease with the current generation of digital devices and services, this does not necessarily mean that they will adapt to and feel similarly at ease with the digital technologies of the future.

It is to adhere to a facile, ageist stereotype to assume that all older people are technophobes and digitally illiterate, often with cognitive deficits and – simply because of their age –they lack the facilities and discernment to incorporate them into their lives (Mannheim et al., 2023). This view attributes lack of uptake to a failure in older people themselves rather than any shortcomings in the technology, a display of ageism that unhelpfully places older people irretrievably on the wrong side of the digital divide (McDonough, 2016). Other studies have suggested that the perceived value and benefit of new technologies are more significant for technology acceptance than chronological age is (Berkowsky et al., 2017; Hauk et al., 2018): rather than being technophobic as a rule or having low levels of digital literacy, older people seem less likely to invest time in new digital technologies whose value for them is not apparent. This view is supported by the current study: the participants, regardless of generation, evidenced a nuanced but generally positive view of digital technologies, both appreciating the benefits they bring and recognising their downsides. When those downsides outweigh the real or perceived benefits, digital devices are placed on one side. It seems likely that, as people age, they value their time too much to squander it trying to use newfangled stuffs whose value is unclear: viewed in this light, older people are more discriminating adopters of new technology.

If a qualitative change exists in the attitudes of older people towards digital technologies, this study suggests that it occurs – at the time the study was conducted – at or after the age of 75. However, rather than being an inevitable consequence of chronological ageing, we would propose that it is experience, rather than chronological age, that is the influential factor: this, too, is supported by this study, which considers the shared experience of generations growing up as particular transformative technologies became prevalent in society. It is worth noting that many of the participants, especially those in the computer and electronic generations, had extensive experience with digital technology, primarily in the form of personal computers, throughout their working (and social) lives (indeed, some 20% of the participants were still in employment). It is the household generation, whose members will not have this same transformational experience of digital technologies, which displays the most marked differences in attitudes.

Other works have included older people in studies of generational differences in attitudes towards technology (e.g. Offerman et al., 2023), but this study is, as far as we are aware, unique in looking at the differences between distinct generations of older people. Notwithstanding local variations in lived experience, the findings are expected to translate with reasonable accuracy to the situation of older people in other high-income economy countries that have seen similar technological advances in the lifetimes of today’s older generations.

Limitations

Participants were self-selecting, and as such, the results might not be representative of independent-living older people in the UK. Having been made aware of the purpose of the study, potential participants who are more sceptical about digital technologies or TECS might have chosen not to participate. Hence, this study has not investigated the digital divide (or, rather, the ‘digital divides’ (Charness & Boot, 2022)) that exists between those older adults who have access to and are comfortable using digital technologies and those who lack access to and feel uneasy using them. Those who were less likely, for whatever reason, to be reached by the various data collection methods might not see their opinions mirrored here. Most participants had chosen to reside in independent-living schemes, which may also have implications for their opinions. While the data collection methods attempted to address some of the anticipated biases in the data, these biases remain: there is a gender imbalance towards females (this can, in part, be attributed to a greater number of females living in the independent living schemes surveyed here (and to more older females than older males in the UK population as a whole (ONS, 2025)). The data are not representative of the ethnic background of older people in the population more generally, according to the 2021 census of England and Wales (ONS, 2022). It was known that residents were predominantly White British; however, the decision to employ researchers from particular local ethnic minority communities for data collection resulted in a dataset containing a greater overall proportion of participants with minority ethnic backgrounds than that found in the population as a whole, but in which, nonetheless, some minority ethnic groups are overrepresented, and others are underrepresented.

The questionnaire treated digital technologies and TECS at a high level and each as a coherent application category. In reality, each category contains quite diverse elements; further work exploring participants’ experiences of and attitudes towards different types of devices and applications might well bring out nuances that could help to inform future developments.

This paper focuses on differences among the different age cohorts formed by the participants. It is very possible that other demographic characteristics—such as age, ethnic background, health status, educational attainment and professional background, or living environment and arrangements—are also instrumental in some of the differences observed, but these have not been examined here. This points to another limitation: the notion of ‘technology generation’ is itself somewhat reductive and deterministic, serving to overshadow an individual’s unique experiences and to diminish their agency in choosing whether to engage with new technologies, a limitation pointed out by König et al. (2018), who suggest that other factors, including gender, education, socioeconomic status, profession and career progression, are also important. Nonetheless, by introducing the lived experience of older people into the discourse in a relatively simple manner that does not require detailed knowledge of the life course of every potential user of TECS and other digital services, it can be argued that the idea of technology generation provides a practical instrument for developers that, at the very least, can help provide a useful initial corrective to even more reductive and dismissive ageist narratives.

Conclusions

This study has demonstrated that there is little evidence for conventional ageist narratives about the relationships of current generations of older people with digital technologies. Independent-living over-55s in the UK were generally positive about digital technologies: in particular, value is placed on the role of digital in maintaining independence and social engagement. Nonetheless, they are also aware of the downsides: the threat of digital property crimes looms large, and participants see the advance of digital services and accompanying marginalisation of conventional services as something inevitable but not necessarily welcome. The participants are also generally positive about digital TECS: this is encouraging for those who view TECS as one way to enable and prolong independent living in older age. However, evident in the results is a generational divide: those aged 75 and over seem significantly less comfortable in the current digital era.

The results also provide some useful pointers for the developers of TECS about the feature and service preferences among this cohort. Although developers are increasingly aware of the need to engage with potential users at all stages of development, awareness of the value placed in, in particular, the socialisation and autonomy promised by digital technologies could help to design features and shape positive narratives about how these can benefit older people’s lives, while having a compelling, trustworthy and comprehensible policy when it comes to security and privacy can help to alleviate some of their legitimate concerns. However, it should not be assumed that the use of digital services will always be welcomed: where perfectly serviceable conventional services already exist, digital ‘solutions’ might not find favour among this cohort. In more general terms, this study has demonstrated that older people’s views are just as complex and dynamic as anyone else’s, and developers and commissioners would do well to take this into account when making decisions about TECS. Policy makers might consider steps to address some of the negative aspects of digital technologies: providing courses and support to build skills and confidence of, in particular, older digital users have previously proven effective in encouraging uptake (see, for example, (Czaja et al., 2018)), although it is less clear whether these are sufficient to alleviate the diverse negative feelings that can be produced by the digital use. One recent study, focusing on TECS uptake, emphasised the ongoing importance of adequate training covering practical knowledge of the availability, use and assessment of services (Gibson et al., 2025); while another recent study, concerned with trust in technology, concluded that training focused on the ‘emotional comfort’ of older users might be more effective than purely technical training (Pérez-Rugosa et al., 2025).

Further research could include a more comprehensive investigation of the role that demographics, background and current circumstances play in shaping an individual’s attitudes towards TECS, helping to elaborate the idea of ‘technology generation’ beyond its current (rather simplistic) definition. An examination of specific categories of digital technology or TECS would help provide a more complete picture of older users’ opinions, provide more guidance for developers about the most appropriate means for delivering care and support, and in ways that promote greater ‘digital comfort’ among as wide a range of prospective users as possible. In this context, the increasing application of generative and agentic AI presents a particular challenge, as well as offering new opportunities for assistive technologies.

Finally, the findings of this study highlight that gerontechnology, as a field, needs to be agile and dynamic, and continuously questioning its premises, since its objects of study – older people, technologies and the intersection of the two – are themselves continuously changing and adapting under their mutual influence, and exist against the backdrop of societies in which digital technologies are playing an ever greater role.

Impact statement

This study asked older people who live in their own homes what they think about digital technology. We found that older people are generally positive about digital technology, and value how it can help them stay independent and socially engaged. However, they are also uncomfortable with some aspects of digital technology, with people aged 75 or over more likely to feel this way. This matters because services – including care and support services – are increasingly provided using digital technology, and we need to make sure that everyone feels able to use these services. Our results should help build better services, and to provide the right advice and support to use them.
