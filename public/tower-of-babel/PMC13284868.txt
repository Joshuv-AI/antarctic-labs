Application of a Novel Desirability of Outcome Ranking Endpoint Incorporating a Framework for Resistance Assessment and Microbiologic Evaluation in Antibiotic Trials: A Proof-of-Concept in a Post Hoc Analysis of the BALANCE Trial
Authors: Sean W X Ong, Aditi Patrikar, Ruxandra Pinto, Robert K Mahar, Asgar Rishu, Joshua S Davis, Robert A Fowler, Steven Y C Tong, Nick Daneman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDesirability of Outcome Ranking (DOOR) with Response Adjusted for Duration of Antibiotic Risk (RADAR) has been suggested as an outcome for antibiotic duration trials. An endpoint incorporating a measure of antimicrobial resistance may better reflect trade-offs between different antibiotic strategies.MethodsWe constructed a novel endpoint, DOOR-FRAME (Framework for Resistance Assessment and Microbiologic Evaluation), and applied it as a proof-of-concept in a post hoc analysis of the BALANCE trial comparing 7- versus 14-day antibiotic duration for uncomplicated non-Staphylococcus aureus bloodstream infection (BSI). The DOOR comprised mortality, readmission, and reinfection, while the FRAME component was a quantitative composite measure of incident Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) and positive cultures for antibiotic-resistant organisms (AROs). We included BALANCE patients in Ontario who consented to data linkage to provincial health administrative databases. We calculated the probabilistic index (PI), the probability of a randomly selected patient in the 7-day group having a better outcome at 180 days.ResultsTwo hundred seventy-one participants were included. The DOOR distributions were similar between the 7- and 14-day groups, with PI of 46.8% (95% CI 40.4%–53.2%) using DOOR alone. Nine (6.9%) and 12 (8.5%) patients in the 7 and 14-day group respectively had ARO/CDI within 180 days. Probabilistic index was 53.6% (95% CI 46.7%–60.4%) with DOOR-RADAR and 47.4% (95% CI 40.9%–53.8%) with DOOR-FRAME.ConclusionsThere were no significant differences in DOOR outcomes at 180 days between patients with uncomplicated BSI treated with 7 versus 14-day antibiotic duration. Incorporating a novel measure of antimicrobial resistance did not change interpretation of the trial.

Appropriate antibiotic therapy is essential for the treatment of severe bacterial infections. However, antibiotic use is associated with adverse effects for both individual patients (harmful side effects, secondary infections such as Clostridioides difficile infection [CDI]) and the broader community by increasing selection pressure for antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [1–3]. Antimicrobial stewardship operates on the principle of administering the minimum “amount” of antibiotics required to treat an infection to minimize the collateral impact of antibiotic use [4]. This can be done by choosing narrower-spectrum agents, using monotherapy (instead of combination therapy), or shortening antibiotic durations.

Evidence supporting shorter antibiotic durations largely derives from non-inferiority clinical trials, which aim to establish if a new intervention is as good (or no worse) than a current standard of care treatment, within a pre-defined non-inferiority margin [5]. This margin quantifies the acceptable “loss” of efficacy that clinicians or patients are willing to accept, given the assumed benefits of the experimental intervention (eg, fewer side effects, improved convenience, lower cost). For example, the BALANCE trial randomly assigned patients with uncomplicated non-Staphylococcus aureus bacterial bloodstream infection (BSI) to 7 versus 14 days of antibiotic duration, with a primary outcome of 90-day mortality and a 4% non-inferiority margin (on the absolute scale) [6]. Worded differently, this means that we accept that the benefits of a shorter treatment duration are “worth” at most a potential 4% increase in mortality. However, non-inferiority margins in many trials are poorly justified, and wider non-inferiority margins are often used to improve sample size feasibility, potentially compromising study validity [7, 8]. This issue, along with other methodologic pitfalls with non-inferiority trials (eg, the impact of non-adherence) [5, 7, 9], has led some investigators to consider alternative study designs.

Trials using a hierarchical composite endpoint (HCE) such as Desirability of Outcome Ranking (DOOR) aim to directly capture the ancillary benefits of the experimental intervention in one of the outcome components, thus re-framing the non-inferiority question as a superiority question [10–12]. For example, in the RADAR (Response Adjusted for Duration of Antibiotic Risk) outcome, antibiotic duration is combined with DOOR, with the assumption that shorter antibiotic durations are superior to longer durations [10]. However, one issue with incorporating antibiotic duration in the outcome (namely, the RADAR component) is that this stacks the odds in favor of the experimental shorter intervention in the case of trials studying antibiotic duration. That is, a component of the outcome is already built into the study design, which may unfairly advantage the short treatment arm [13–15].

Instead of directly measuring antibiotic duration as an outcome component, investigators of antibiotic minimization trials may wish to directly capture the anticipated benefits of minimizing antibiotics as an outcome component [12]. We propose that such trials, when using a HCE or DOOR outcome, should consider incorporating a measure of antimicrobial resistance, since minimizing this is a key motivation for infectious disease clinicians wanting to reduce antimicrobial use. In this paper, we aim to (1) describe a novel outcome, DOOR-FRAME (DOOR combined with Framework for Resistance Assessment and Microbiologic Evaluation), (2) demonstrate use of the DOOR-FRAME outcome in a post hoc analysis of the BALANCE trial, and (3) evaluate longer-term outcomes (beyond 90 days) among a subset of BALANCE trial patients who consented to data linkage with health administrative databases.

METHODS

Study Cohort

This study is an exploratory post hoc analysis of the BALANCE trial. Detailed methods and results of the BALANCE trial have been previously published [6, 16]. In brief, participants were adults hospitalized with a BSI, defined as a blood culture positive for pathogenic bacteria. Patients with S. aureus bacteremia, candidemia, severe immunocompromise, endovascular prostheses, or a focus of infection separately requiring prolonged therapy were excluded. Participants were randomized 1:1 to 7 or 14 days of antibiotic treatment; exact agent, dose, and route were left to individual treating physicians. The primary outcome for the BALANCE trial was 90-day all-cause mortality.

For this study, as we wanted to explore an expanded outcome set not captured in the primary BALANCE case report form, we focused on the subset of BALANCE patients enrolled in Ontario, Canada who provided written informed consent for data linkage with health administrative databases (where these expanded outcomes were available). No sample size calculation was performed for this post hoc analysis, since the number of patients was based on the logistical availability of data linkage among this subset of BALANCE patients.

Data Sources

Baseline data captured prospectively using the standardized BALANCE case report form were obtained from the BALANCE dataset. Expanded outcome data were obtained from linked population-wide administrative databases housed at ICES, Ontario, linked using unique encoded identifiers and analyzed at ICES. The full list of databases, along with specific variable definitions, is provided in the Supplementary Methods. ICES is an independent, nonprofit research institute whose legal status under Ontario's health information privacy law allows it to collect and analyze healthcare and demographic data, without consent, for health system evaluation and improvement.

Primary and Secondary Outcomes

The primary outcome was a DOOR outcome, assessed at 180 days. We chose the time point of 180 days as we were interested in longer-term outcomes beyond the initial 90 days assessed in the BALANCE trial. Additionally, ICES privacy restrictions preclude public reporting of outcomes with ≤5 patients, and rarity of some of the outcome components (especially ARO-positive cultures) means that at some ordinal levels, there could be ≤5 patients if the outcome was measured too early. The DOOR outcome was constructed based on clinical consensus among investigators in the BALANCE trial steering committee and includes the following 4 ranks from best to worst: (1) neither readmission nor reinfection, (2) reinfection or all-cause readmission, (3) reinfection and all-cause readmission, and (4) all-cause death (Table 1). Reinfection was defined as a positive blood culture for the same organism as the index positive blood culture, collected more than 7 days after the index positive blood culture. In a DOOR-RADAR outcome, the duration of antibiotics received is used as an additional tiebreaker for patients with the same DOOR level, with patients receiving a shorter duration ranked above those receiving a longer duration. For the DOOR-FRAME outcome, instead of using antibiotic duration as a tiebreaker, we used a separate FRAME (Framework for Resistance Assessment and Microbiologic Evaluation) score, an aggregate score based on the number of unique antimicrobial-resistant organisms (AROs) and diagnosis of CDI (Table 1). Antibiotic-resistant organisms were defined as any culture (regardless of reason for ordering) being positive for a highly resistant organism as defined by Dutch nosocomial infection surveillance guidelines [17]. As we could not arbitrate between infection versus colonization using health administrative data, we only stratified cultures by sterile versus non-sterile sites, with AROs in sterile site cultures weighted above non-sterile site cultures. Similar to DOOR-RADAR, for patients tied with the same DOOR, the aggregate FRAME score was used as a tiebreaker, with those with a lower FRAME score ranked higher than those with a higher FRAME score. Thus, clinical outcomes take priority, but for patients with the same clinical outcome, the antimicrobial resistance outcome is the secondary consideration. To evaluate the longer-term impact of 7- versus 14-day antibiotic duration, we also evaluated the DOOR, DOOR-RADAR, and DOOR-FRAME outcomes at 365 days (in addition to 180 days) from index blood culture.

Statistical Analysis

For each DOOR outcome (DOOR alone, DOOR-RADAR, and DOOR-FRAME), we calculated the probabilistic index (PI), which quantifies the probability that a randomly selected patient in the 7-day group has a better outcome than a randomly selected patient in the 14-day group [10, 12]. This is also variably referred to in the literature as the better DOOR probability (BDP) or concordance probability c [11]. 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were determined using the Wilcoxon rank sum method [12]. The null hypothesis of no difference between groups is 50%, while a point estimate above 50% indicates that the treatment effect favors the 7-day group, and a statistically significant difference is concluded when the entirety of the 95% CI is above or below 50%.

We plotted a Kaplan–Meier curve to compare survival over time between the 7- and 14-day groups and separately constructed cause-specific cumulative incidence function (CIF) curves, accounting for the competing risk of death, to compare incidence over time between groups for each of the other DOOR component outcomes: (1) time to first readmission, (2) time to reinfection, and (3) time to first positive ARO culture. Differences between the 7- and 14-day groups in hazard of occurrence of the event of interest were tested using the log-rank test for death and Gray's test for the other three events (accounting for competing risk of death).

Sensitivity Analysis

As a sensitivity analysis, we excluded incident positive ARO cultures from the outcome if the patient had a positive culture for that same organism in the index blood culture or any other culture in the preceding 365 days. For example, if a patient had a previous MRSA culture 6 months prior, any positive MRSA culture within the 365 days from the index blood culture would not be counted as meeting the outcome. We recalculated FRAME scores for all patients with this additional criterion and re-conducted the DOOR-FRAME analyses using the same method above.

Exploratory Outcomes

We also collected the following exploratory outcomes evaluating clinical efficacy and healthcare utilization: (1) the individual components of the DOOR outcome (ie, mortality, readmission, and reinfection) at 180 and 365 days, (2) number of readmissions among patients who had any readmission, (3) emergency department visits at 180 and 365 days, (4) number of emergency department visits among patients who had emergency department visits, and (5) antibiotic-free days at 180 and 365 days (among patients >65 years old in whom we had access to outpatient medication data). To minimize multiple hypothesis testing, these exploratory outcomes are reported descriptively, and no statistical testing was performed.

RESULTS

Of 3608 participants in the primary BALANCE cohort, 1389 participants were enrolled in ten Ontario sites that participated in data linkage, of whom 433 were enrolled after approval of data linkage at each site (approval dates ranging from October 2021 to March 2023). Of these, 275 participants provided informed consent for data linkage. Four participants were excluded due to missing health card number (which was required for data linkage), leaving 271 participants included in this post hoc analysis (study flowchart in Supplementary Figure 1). Baseline characteristics of these patients, stratified by treatment allocation group, are summarized in Table 2. There were no significant baseline differences between treatment groups. The subset of patients in this post hoc analysis was representative of the larger BALANCE cohort, with a similar age distribution (median 71 years [interquartile range 62–83] in this study vs 70 [IQR 59–80] years in BALANCE), sex distribution (141/271 [52.0%] male in this study vs 1922/3608 [53.3%] in BALANCE), and comorbidity burden. Like the BALANCE cohort, Enterobacterales organisms were the most frequent causative pathogens, while urinary tract and intra-abdominal infections were the top 2 most common sources of infection. However, the population in this study had milder disease presentation, with 89/271 (32.8%) admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) on admission compared to 1986/3608 (55.0%) in the larger BALANCE cohort.

DISCUSSION

In this exploratory post hoc analysis of the BALANCE trial, we constructed and demonstrated the use of a hierarchical composite endpoint combining a DOOR (evaluating clinical efficacy) with a novel quantitative assessment of antimicrobial resistance (the FRAME score). This novel DOOR-FRAME outcome builds upon the DOOR-RADAR outcome previously described in the literature, with the additional benefit of attempting to measure the collateral impact of antibiotic use. DOOR-RADAR may be over-sensitive in using antibiotic duration as a tiebreaker, and this can result in erroneous conclusions of superiority if the tiebreaking effect of antibiotic duration overwhelms the DOOR ranking (especially in a scenario where event rates are low) [12, 14, 15]. The FRAME component in DOOR-FRAME can provide a more important measure of the benefit of reducing antibiotic durations and may thus be better suited for antibiotic duration trials in the future. A further benefit of the DOOR-FRAME outcome is that it permits harmonized use across all antibiotic minimization trials, and not just those assessing antibiotic duration alone, which RADAR is designed for. For example, the DOOR-FRAME outcome could also be applied to a trial comparing a narrow-spectrum to a broad-spectrum antibiotic or a trial comparing monotherapy to combination therapy. The FRAME component directly measures the potential benefit of the experimental intervention (ie, reduction in antimicrobial resistance) and combines this benefit with the trade-offs of any changes in clinical efficacy.

In this cohort, inclusion of the FRAME component in DOOR-FRAME did not demonstrate superiority with the 7-day arm, even though the incidence of ARO positive cultures and CDI was numerically lower in the 7-day group. There are 3 possible explanations: (1) reducing antibiotic duration does not actually have a significant effect on reducing antimicrobial resistance, (2) reducing antibiotic duration does reduce antimicrobial resistance, but this effect size is small, and this study was underpowered to demonstrate this, or (3) the impact of reducing antibiotic duration is primarily on colonization by AROs, which was underestimated in our study due to the absence of routine surveillance cultures. In our cohort, rates of incident positive ARO cultures and CDI were low, at 7.7% (21/271) and 9.2% (25/271) at 180 and 365 days, respectively. This could be due to low rates of antimicrobial resistance in Ontario, and studies conducted in other settings with a higher burden of antimicrobial resistance may be able to detect a greater effect. A previous modeling study showed that the impact of reducing antibiotic duration will likely have a greater impact on reducing antimicrobial resistance in high-transmission settings compared to low-transmission settings [18]. A separate BALANCE sub-study evaluated gut microbiome diversity and carriage of antimicrobial resistance genes in 41 participants (all also enrolled in Ontario) and did not find a difference between the 7-day and 14-day treatment groups, though that study faced a similar issue with a limited sample size [19]. While often assumed and cited as a rationale for limiting antibiotic durations, there is limited real-world evidence that shortening antibiotic durations actually has a positive impact on reducing antimicrobial resistance and/or improving other microbiologic outcomes, and further research should attempt to better quantify this benefit [18, 20]. Nonetheless, reducing antibiotic duration has other benefits beyond mitigating antimicrobial resistance, such as reducing adverse events, hospital length of stay, and healthcare costs [2, 21].

The BALANCE trial concluded that a shorter 7-day antibiotic duration is non-inferior to a longer 14-day duration for the primary outcome of 90-day mortality. However, clinicians and patients may have other concerns beyond mortality, such as potentially increased readmission, healthcare utilization, or reinfection with shorter course therapy, especially beyond the initial 90 days. Our post hoc analysis with linkage to population-wide administrative databases should allay some of these concerns, by demonstrating that these longer-term outcomes up to 180 and 365 days were similar between the 7-day and 14-day treatment groups. Furthermore, the benefit of shorter antibiotic durations was maintained up to 365 days, with more antibiotic-free days in the 7-day group, demonstrating that the initial reduction in antibiotic use was not subsequently compensated by increased recurrence and/or delayed antibiotic use. Our study adds to the body of evidence supporting that 7-day antibiotic durations should be the established standard of care for most patients with uncomplicated non-S. aureus BSI.

There are several limitations to our study. First, there was a low proportion (7.5%) of the overall BALANCE cohort included in this analysis. Linkage to the comprehensive set of health administrative databases was only possible in Ontario and required additional written informed consent from patients, which was only added to the informed consent form in the last 2 years of the trial. Participants in this sub-study had milder disease presentation with a lower proportion admitted to ICU on admission, which may be related to the additional requirement for written informed consent for data linkage (and surrogate decision makers for ICU patients being less willing to provide this additional consent). Study findings may therefore not be generalizable to settings outside of Ontario, or to patients more severely ill at presentation. The small sample size may also explain the fact that the point estimate for 180-day mortality in this analysis favors the 14-day group, which might be due to random chance, since in the larger BALANCE cohort of 3608 patients, the point estimate for 90-day mortality favored the 7-day group. While there were no significant differences in baseline characteristics between groups in the 271 patients in this analysis, there were numerically fewer patients in the 7-day group (40.8% vs 51.1% in 14-day group) with urinary tract source of infection; this may underestimate the benefit associated with 7-day treatment since non-urinary tract source of infection may be associated with more severe disease and worse clinical outcomes. Similarly, there were numerically more patients in the 7-day group with malignancy or immunosuppression, which are both associated with worse clinical outcomes. Second, we defined reinfection only by positive repeat blood cultures and did not consider cultures from other non-blood sites (eg, positive urine cultures for urinary tract infection). We may thus underestimate the true frequency of recurrence, since not all recurrent infections would be expected to be associated with bacteremia. Third, we relied on health administrative databases which collect routine laboratory data. Antibiotic-resistant organism rates were thus passively collected based on routine cultures ordered for clinical reasons, and there was no additional active ARO surveillance as part of this study. Balancing logistical realities, our study design is reflective of daily clinical practice, with actual positive clinical ARO cultures and CDI rates being the most direct collateral impact of antibiotic use encountered outside of a clinical research setting. Future studies evaluating the impact of different antibiotic minimization strategies could conduct regular routine testing of participants at fixed intervals to prospectively identify ARO colonization and could also employ more comprehensive testing of the microbiome and resistome. The DOOR-FRAME approach could easily incorporate active ARO surveillance or even detailed quantitative microbiome testing, with minor adjustments to the FRAME score. Lastly, we highlight that the DOOR-FRAME outcome only captures outcomes at the individual patient level and does not capture the broader public health or societal impact in reducing transmission of antimicrobial resistance between patients.

CONCLUSION

In this exploratory post hoc analysis, there was no significant difference in a DOOR outcome (incorporating death, readmission, and reinfection) at 180 and 365 days between patients with uncomplicated BSI treated with 7 versus 14 days of antibiotics. Combining a novel measure of antimicrobial resistance, FRAME, with the DOOR did not demonstrate the superiority of shorter treatment. This novel DOOR-FRAME outcome may be used to concurrently evaluate benefits and trade-offs of different antimicrobial strategies in future antibiotic minimization trials.
