Influence of high-power light-curing modes on the mechanical and optical properties of no-cap bulk-fill resin composites
Authors: Betül Kübra Kurucu Karadeni̇z, Merve Kütük Ömeroğlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe clinical performance of no-cap bulk-fill resin composites depends strongly on adequate polymerization, which influences both mechanical durability and optical stability. Although contemporary high-power light-curing units (LCUs) provide ultra-fast curing modes, evidence regarding their effects on modern bulk-fill materials remains limited. This in vitro study aimed to evaluate the influence of different polymerization modes of VALO and Bluephase PowerCure on the microhardness and color stability of no-cap bulk-fill resin composites.Materials and methodsFour no-cap bulk-fill resin composites (Omnichroma Bulk Flow, Charisma Bulk Flow ONE, Simplishade Bulk Fill, and Admira Fusion x-tra) were investigated. Two hundred cylindrical specimens (3 mm thickness) were prepared and polymerized using two LCUs (VALO and Bluephase PowerCure) under five curing modes (VALO Standard, High Power, and Xtra; Bluephase High Power and PowerCure). Vickers microhardness was measured on the top and bottom surfaces after 24 h, and bottom-to-top hardness ratios were calculated as an indicator of depth of cure. Baseline color was recorded and remeasured after 12-day coffee immersion at 37 °C, and color change (ΔE₀₀) was calculated. Data were analyzed using two-way ANOVA and Aligned Rank Transform ANOVA (α = 0.05).ResultsComposite type, polymerization mode, and their interaction significantly affected bottom-to-top hardness ratio, microhardness, and color change values (p < 0.001). VALO Standard and VALO High Power generally produced higher bottom-to-top hardness ratios and bottom-surface microhardness values than the 3-second ultra-short curing modes. Admira Fusion x-tra and Simplishade Bulk Fill generally showed higher microhardness values and/or lower ΔE₀₀ values under the tested conditions, whereas Omnichroma Bulk Flow and Charisma Bulk Flow ONE showed lower bottom-to-top hardness ratios or higher ΔE₀₀ values in some ultra-short curing groups. No consistent correlation was observed between ΔE₀₀ and bottom-to-top hardness ratio across the tested material–curing mode combinations.ConclusionsPolymerization mode and total radiant exposure influenced the mechanical and optical outcomes of the tested no-cap bulk-fill resin composites. Longer exposure protocols with higher radiant exposure generally resulted in more favorable bottom-to-top hardness ratios and bottom-surface microhardness values than the 3-second ultra-short curing protocols. The findings suggest that ultra-rapid curing modes may not provide equivalent outcomes for all no-cap bulk-fill resin composites. Therefore, curing protocols should be selected with consideration of material-specific behavior, exposure time, and total radiant exposure.Keywords: Bulk-fill composites, Light-curing units, Polymerization mode, Microhardness, Color stability, ΔE₀₀

Introduction

Bulk-fill resin-based composites (BFRCs) were introduced to simplify restorative procedures and enhance clinical efficiency.

These materials are designed to be placed in thicker layers, typically up to 4–5 mm, while still achieving adequate polymerization depth and satisfactory mechanical performance. By allowing placement in a single increment, bulk-fill composites help shorten clinical working time and simplify the restorative technique, which is particularly beneficial for posterior teeth where access is limited and patient cooperation may be more difficult to maintain [1].

Bulk-fill resin composites are available in two main viscosity types: low-viscosity (flowable) and high-viscosity formulations. Flowable bulk-fill composites are primarily used as a base, especially in regions exposed to high occlusal stress, and usually require an overlying layer of either a high-viscosity bulk-fill or a conventional resin composite to provide adequate mechanical support [2, 3]. This capping layer is essential for improving both wear resistance and the overall durability of the restoration. In addition, the relatively limited color stability of flowable bulk-fill materials has been cited as another reason for applying a high-viscosity composite overlay [4].

Bulk-fill RBCs were developed to enable placement in 4–5 mm increments, thereby reducing chair time and technique sensitivity compared to conventional 2-mm layering [2, 5]. This deeper curing capability is achieved through increased translucency, reduced filler content, and the use of more efficient photoinitiators that enhance light transmission [6, 7]. Despite these advances, light scattering and absorption within bulk-fill composites still reduce the energy reaching deeper regions, raising concerns regarding insufficient depth of cure [8].

In response to the limitations of conventional flowable bulk-fill composites, no-cap flowable bulk-fill resin composites have been developed to eliminate the need for an additional covering layer. These materials are intended for use as a single restorative layer in direct posterior restorations, particularly when simplified application, improved cavity adaptation, and reduced chair time are desired. Their low viscosity may improve adaptation to cavity walls and internal irregularities, thereby reducing void formation and potentially enhancing marginal integrity [9]. However, since these materials are used without a protective capping layer, their mechanical performance, wear resistance, polymerization efficiency at depth, and optical stability become clinically critical. Therefore, the long-term performance of no-cap bulk-fill composites may be strongly influenced by material composition, filler characteristics, translucency, resin matrix chemistry, and the selected light-curing protocol.

Despite these clinical advantages of no-cap bulk-fill composites, their performance depends critically on the efficiency of light-activated polymerization. Adequate polymerization is essential for achieving satisfactory mechanical properties and long-term clinical performance. Insufficient curing can reduce microhardness, compromised wear resistance, and increase susceptibility to degradation. Microhardness values and depth of cure have been widely used as indirect indicators of polymerization effectiveness, with the bottom-to-top hardness ratio frequently employed to assess the degree of polymerization at clinically relevant depths. Studies show that bulk-fill composites generally achieve satisfactory depth of cure values and acceptable hardness ratios at 4-mm increments, although these properties vary with material composition and curing conditions [7, 10].

Color stability is another critical outcome that may be influenced by the adequacy of polymerization and material formulation. While some studies show that bulk-fill composites demonstrate comparable or even superior color stability relative to conventional composites when exposed to staining agents, the results are mixed and depend on resin matrix, filler content, and external challenges [11, 12].

Resin-based composites polymerize through light-activated free-radical reactions; however, the degree of conversion is influenced by several factors, including material composition, photoinitiator system, light spectrum, irradiance, and exposure time [13–15].

Incomplete polymerization may reduce hardness, increase water sorption and discoloration, and compromise the long-term performance of restorations [16–18]. Therefore, optimization of light-curing protocols is essential for achieving adequate mechanical reliability and esthetic stability [13–15].

Contemporary resin composites may contain different photoinitiators with distinct wavelength absorption characteristics [15, 19, 20].

Thus, the spectral emission and irradiance distribution of light-curing units can influence polymerization efficiency [21, 22]. Although polywave LCUs are designed to activate both conventional and alternative photoinitiators, their superiority over high-power monowave devices is not consistent and appears to be material dependent [23].

In addition to irradiance and spectral output, the distance between the light-curing tip and the restorative material is an important determinant of polymerization efficiency. As the distance from the light source increases, the irradiance reaching the composite surface may decrease and the light beam may become less concentrated, potentially reducing the degree of conversion and bottom-surface hardness. This issue is particularly relevant in deep posterior cavities, where ideal positioning of the light-curing tip directly over the restoration may not always be achievable.

Although high-power and ultra-fast curing modes may reduce chair time, the increased radiant exitance and rapid energy delivery may raise concerns regarding temperature elevation within the restorative material and underlying pulp tissue. Therefore, the potential thermal effects of these curing protocols should also be considered, particularly in deep cavities with reduced remaining dentin thickness.

Although the effects of different LCUs and curing parameters on conventional and bulk-fill resin composites have been widely investigated, evidence remains limited regarding the performance of no-cap bulk-fill resin composites polymerized with contemporary high-power ultra-fast curing modes. This gap is particularly relevant because these materials are used without a protective capping layer, and inadequate polymerization may directly affect both their mechanical reliability and optical stability. Moreover, limited information is available on how different polymerization modes simultaneously influence microhardness, bottom-to-top hardness ratio, and color stability in no-cap bulk-fill composites, including single-shade and ORMOCER-based materials. Therefore, further investigation is required to determine whether ultra-fast curing protocols provide adequate polymerization and acceptable color stability under clinically relevant conditions.

In this in vitro study, we aimed to evaluate the effect of different polymerization modes of two contemporary high-power light-curing units (VALO and Bluephase PowerCure) on the microhardness, depth of cure (bottom-to-top hardness ratio), and color stability (ΔE₀₀) of four no-cap bulk-fill resin composites. The null hypotheses tested were as follows:

Different polymerization modes would not significantly affect the top or bottom surface microhardness of the no-cap bulk-fill resin composites;

Polymerization mode would not significantly affect the color stability (ΔE₀₀) of the materials after coffee aging; and

Materials used in this study

The specimens were polymerized using two light-curing units: VALO (Ultradent Products Inc., South Jordan, UT, USA) and Bluephase PowerCure (Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein). Five polymerization modes were evaluated according to radiant exitance and exposure time: VALO Standard (1000 mW/cm² for 20 s), VALO High Power (1400 mW/cm² for 12 s), VALO Xtra Power (3200 mW/cm² for 3 s), Bluephase High Power (1200 mW/cm² for 10 s), and Bluephase PowerCure mode (3000 mW/cm² for 3 s).

The required sample size was calculated using G*Power 3.1 (Heinrich-Heine-Universität Düsseldorf, Germany) based on a two-way ANOVA model. The bottom-to-top hardness ratio was selected as the primary outcome variable for sample size calculation because it directly reflects polymerization efficiency and depth of cure under different curing protocols. Using an effect size of f = 0.25, α = 0.05, and a power of 0.80, the analysis indicated a minimum of 10 specimens per subgroup. Therefore, a total of 200 specimens were prepared (4 composites × 5 curing modes × 10 specimens).

A total of 200 cylindrical specimens (n = 50 per composite) were fabricated using stainless-steel molds (6 mm diameter, 3 mm thickness). The composite materials were inserted in a single increment, covered with a Mylar strip and glass slide, and gently pressed to obtain flat, void-free surfaces. Polymerization was performed through the Mylar strip and glass slide to standardize the specimen surface and thickness.The 3 mm thickness was not selected to represent the maximum curing depth claimed by manufacturers, as bulk-fill resin composites are generally marketed for placement in thicker increments. Instead, this thickness was chosen as a clinically relevant bulk-fill increment that would allow assessment of polymerization efficiency at the bottom surface while avoiding extreme under-polymerization that might occur with thicker specimens under ultra-short high-power curing protocols. This design enabled comparison of the tested curing modes under a standardized and clinically meaningful depth condition [7, 18].

The light-curing unit was positioned perpendicular to the specimen surface at 1-mm distance. The nominal light guide diameter was 10 mm for VALO Cordless and 9 mm for Bluephase PowerCure, according to the manufacturers’ specifications. Polymerization was performed according to the assigned curing mode. The 1-mm distance between the light-curing tip and the specimen surface was used to standardize light delivery and minimize experimental variability among groups, as recommended in previous in vitro studies [18]. The light-curing tip was positioned perpendicular to the specimen surface, and the 1-mm distance between the light guide tip and the specimen surface was standardized using a fixed positioning setup during polymerization. The irradiance output of each LCU was verified before and after every 10 specimens using a Bluephase Meter II (Ivoclar Vivadent), and deviations greater than ± 5% were corrected.

Radiant exposure was calculated for each polymerization mode using the formula: radiant exposure (J/cm²) = irradiance (mW/cm²) × exposure time (s) / 1000. The calculated radiant exposure values were 20.0 J/cm² for VALO Standard, 16.8 J/cm² for VALO High Power, 9.6 J/cm² for VALO Xtra Power, 12.0 J/cm² for Bluephase High Power, and 9.0 J/cm² for Bluephase PowerCure.

To ensure uniform surface finishing, all specimens were polished by a single operator using silicon carbide abrasive papers of 400, 600, and 1200 grit under constant water irrigation. Polishing was performed for 10 s per grit at a rotational speed of 150 rpm using a benchtop grinding and polishing unit (Ecomet 6, Buehler, Lake Bluff, USA). After polishing, samples were rinsed with distilled water, lightly dried, and their thickness was verified with a digital micrometer (MDC-250, Mitutoyo, Kawasaki, Japan) to confirm dimensional standardization. The specimens were then stored in a dark, closed box at room temperature to prevent exposure to ambient light until further processing.

Vickers microhardness was measured on both the top (irradiated) and bottom (non-irradiated) surfaces using a microhardness tester (HMV-2, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) with a 200 g load applied for 15 s. Five indentations were made in the central region of each specimen surface, with a minimum spacing of 1 mm between indentations and away from the specimen margins to avoid overlap or edge-related measurement errors. Mean Vickers hardness numbers (VHN) were then calculated for each surface [24].

The bottom-to-top hardness ratio (B/T, %) was calculated to assess the depth of cure. A ratio ≥ 80% was considered indicative of adequate polymerization.

All specimens were immersed in a coffee solution prepared with Nescafé Classic (Nestlé, Vevey, Switzerland; 1.8 g/150 mL distilled water) at 37 (±) 1 C for 12 days to simulate extrinsic staining. This immersion period was selected based on previous reports suggesting that one cup of coffee is consumed over approximately 15 min and that coffee drinkers consume an average of 3.2 cups per day. Accordingly, 24 h of continuous coffee immersion may approximate one month of coffee exposure, and the 12-day immersion period used in the present study may be considered to simulate approximately 12 months of coffee consumption under standardized laboratory conditions [25].Each sample was individually placed into a well of a 24-well culture plate to ensure complete submersion. The plates were kept in an incubator at 37 (±) 1 °C throughout the staining periods. To maintain consistency and prevent microbial growth, the coffee solution was renewed daily, and the specimens were repositioned to ensure complete exposure. This daily renewal protocol is consistent with procedures described in previous studies [25, 26].

After staining, specimens were rinsed thoroughly with distilled water and gently dried prior to color measurements.

Color measurements were performed using a SpectroShade Micro spectrophotometer (MHT Optic Research AG, Italy) under D65 illumination and a 2° standard observer [27]. All color measurements were performed on a standardized white background to minimize the influence of backing color on spectrophotometric readings.Three consecutive readings were recorded for each specimen, and mean L*, a*, and b* values were calculated.

Color was measured at baseline (T0) and after coffee aging (T1). Color difference (ΔE₀₀) was calculated using the CIEDE2000 formula with kL = kC = kH = 1. Thresholds of perceptibility (0.800) and acceptability (1.800) were applied for clinical interpretation [28].

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using R software and Minitab v14. For normally distributed data, two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test was applied. For non-normal data, Aligned Rank Transform (ART) ANOVA with Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons was used. Correlations between ΔE₀₀ and B/T ratios were evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficients. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Specifically, bottom-to-top hardness ratio and color change (ΔE₀₀) were analyzed using parametric two-way ANOVA with composite type and polymerization mode as factors, whereas top and bottom surface Vickers microhardness values were analyzed using non-parametric Aligned Rank Transform (ART) ANOVA with composite type, polymerization mode, and surface as factors.

The relationship between ΔE₀₀ and bottom-to-top hardness ratio was evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficients within each composite × curing mode subgroup. A total of 20 subgroup-specific correlations were performed (4 composites × 5 curing modes), with n = 10 specimens per subgroup. This correlation analysis was considered exploratory and was conducted to examine whether color change was associated with polymerization efficiency within specific material–mode combinations.

Based on the CIEDE2000 acceptability threshold (ΔE₀₀ = 1.8), most groups cured with ultra-short, high-power modes—particularly Omnichroma Bulk Flow and Charisma Bulk Flow ONE cured with Bluephase PowerCure and VALO Xtra—exhibited clinically unacceptable color changes, whereas specimens cured with VALO Standard and VALO High Power generally remained within or close to the acceptability limit.

Discussion

The present in vitro study evaluated the effect of different polymerization modes of contemporary high-power light-curing units on the microhardness, bottom-to-top hardness ratio, and color stability of no-cap bulk-fill resin composites. The findings showed that polymerization mode, composite type, and their interaction significantly influenced the mechanical and optical outcomes. Therefore, the null hypotheses regarding the effects of polymerization mode on microhardness, bottom-to-top hardness ratio, and color stability were rejected.In contrast, the null hypothesis regarding the relationship between ΔE₀₀ and the bottom-to-top hardness ratio was not rejected, as no consistent correlation was observed across most material–curing mode combinations. This suggests that color stability was not determined solely by polymerization efficiency but was also influenced by material-related factors.

The interpretation of partial eta-squared values clarified the relative contribution of the tested factors. For the bottom-to-top hardness ratio, the composite × polymerization mode interaction showed the largest effect size (ηp² = 0.949), followed by polymerization mode (ηp² = 0.875) and composite type (ηp² = 0.784). Similarly, for color change, the composite × polymerization mode interaction had the largest effect size (ηp² = 0.867), followed by polymerization mode (ηp² = 0.697) and composite type (ηp² = 0.460). These findings indicate that both depth of cure and discoloration were mainly determined by the material-specific response to the curing protocol rather than by material type or curing mode alone. For microhardness, surface had the strongest main effect (ηp² = 0.772), confirming that bottom surfaces were more affected than top surfaces. The substantial composite × mode × surface interaction (ηp² = 0.674) further indicates that the reduction in bottom-surface hardness depended on both the material and the curing strategy. Overall, these findings suggest that no-cap bulk-fill resin composites should not be considered interchangeable with respect to curing requirements. Ultra-short 3-second curing modes did not provide uniformly favorable outcomes across all materials, indicating that high irradiance alone may not compensate for insufficient exposure time, especially in highly translucent single-shade flowable composites.

The differences among curing modes should also be interpreted in relation to radiant exposure. Although the ultra-short modes had the highest irradiance values, their total radiant exposure was lower than that of the longer exposure protocols. For example, VALO Xtra Power and Bluephase PowerCure delivered 9.6 and 9.0 J/cm², respectively, whereas VALO Standard and VALO High Power delivered 20.0 and 16.8 J/cm². Therefore, the reduced bottom-surface hardness and increased discoloration observed in some ultra-short curing groups may be partly explained by the lower total radiant exposure, indicating that irradiance alone is insufficient to describe curing effectiveness.

Surface microhardness and the bottom-to-top hardness ratio are widely accepted indirect indicators of polymerization efficiency and depth of cure in bulk-fill composites [30]. A hardness ratio of at least 0.80 is generally considered to indicate adequate polymerization throughout the bulk of the material. Inadequate curing at the bottom surface compromises mechanical integrity and increases susceptibility to chemical degradation and microbial colonization [31].

These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that shortened exposure times and different LCU characteristics can influence microhardness, degree of conversion, and depth of cure in bulk-fill composites [19, 31–33]. However, the present results further emphasize the material-dependent nature of this effect. While some composites maintained acceptable bottom-to-top hardness ratios under high-power protocols, Omnichroma Bulk Flow cured with Bluephase PowerCure and Charisma Bulk Flow ONE cured with VALO Xtra showed marked reductions. This suggests that the effectiveness of ultra-fast curing protocols depends on the interaction between light-curing mode and material formulation, including translucency, filler characteristics, resin matrix, and photoinitiator system.

Shortened curing protocols may compromise polymerization efficiency despite high irradiance. Previous studies have shown that reduced exposure time can adversely affect microhardness, degree of conversion, and curing depth in resin composites, and that these effects may vary according to material type and curing mode [34, 35].

In agreement with these reports, the present study showed that ultra-short high-power protocols did not consistently provide favorable bottom-surface hardness or bottom-to-top hardness ratios. This finding supports the concept that adequate exposure time remains critical for effective polymerization and that increased irradiance alone does not guarantee sufficient curing at depth.

The degree of conversion is a critical determinant of the chemical stability of resin-based composites because it reflects the extent of monomer conversion into a cross-linked polymer network. A reduced degree of conversion may increase residual monomer content, water sorption, solubility, and susceptibility to discoloration, while also impairing mechanical properties [36–38]. Although microhardness and the bottom-to-top hardness ratio are widely used as indirect indicators of polymerization efficiency, they do not directly quantify the degree of conversion [39]. Therefore, future studies using Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy or Raman spectroscopy are needed to directly evaluate the relationship between curing mode, degree of conversion, chemical stability, and color stability in no-cap bulk-fill resin composites.

Coffee aging further demonstrated the combined influence of curing strategy and material composition on optical stability. Previous studies reported that immersion in staining beverages, particularly coffee, can increase color change and reduce surface microhardness in bulk-fill resin composites [40]. In the present study, several no-cap bulk-fill composites showed clinically relevant ΔE₀₀ values after coffee immersion, particularly when ultra-short curing protocols were used. This suggests that insufficient or less homogeneous polymerization may increase susceptibility to staining; however, the material-dependent differences observed in ΔE₀₀ values indicate that resin matrix composition, filler characteristics, translucency, and hydrophilicity also contribute substantially to color stability.

Material formulation also appears to be an important determinant of the response to high-power curing protocols. Structurally colored and highly translucent single-shade composites may differ from conventional composites in terms of filler architecture, optical behavior, and surface characteristics, which may influence both polymerization efficiency and staining susceptibility [41]. In addition, exposure duration remains a critical factor in photopolymerization, as shortened curing times may fail to achieve optimal hardness despite increased irradiance [42]. Therefore, the reduced bottom-surface hardness and increased discoloration observed in some ultra-short curing groups in the present study are likely related to the combined effects of material formulation, light transmission, and insufficient exposure time.

The decrease in microhardness from the top to the bottom surface observed in the present study is consistent with previous evidence showing that polymerization efficiency decreases with increasing composite depth [43]. However, the extent of this reduction differed among materials and curing protocols, indicating that depth-related hardness loss is not solely a function of specimen thickness but is also influenced by material composition and light-curing parameters. Although LED curing units can provide effective polymerization under appropriate conditions [44], the present findings suggest that ultra-short high-power modes may compromise bottom-surface hardness in certain no-cap bulk-fill composites. Therefore, adequate curing of these materials requires optimization of exposure time and irradiance according to material-specific characteristics.

Light delivery characteristics may also contribute to the material-dependent differences observed in this study. Previous studies have shown that non-uniform irradiance distribution, beam profile, light guide design, and monowave or polywave emission characteristics can influence top and bottom microhardness values in resin composites [45–47]. In agreement with these reports, the present findings suggest that polymerization efficiency is not determined solely by nominal irradiance, but by the combined effects of irradiance distribution, exposure duration, curing mode, and composite formulation. This may partly explain why ultra-short high-power protocols produced acceptable outcomes in some material–mode combinations but compromised bottom-surface hardness or color stability in others.

Previous studies have shown that curing mode, exposure duration, and material composition can significantly influence the microhardness, degree of conversion, and color stability of resin composites [48–50]. Consistent with these findings, the present study demonstrated that shortened curing protocols may compromise polymerization efficiency and increase discoloration in a material-dependent manner. These results reinforce the importance of balancing irradiance and exposure time when using advanced LED LCUs, particularly for no-cap bulk-fill composites with high translucency or single-shade formulations.

While the CIELab (ΔE*ab) system remains the most commonly used color analysis method in studies evaluating color differences in the dental literature, the CIEDE2000 (ΔE00) system has been reported to more accurately represent human color perception. This is because ΔE00 can detect even small color differences and provides better indicators for perceptibility (PT) and acceptability (AT) thresholds [51]. In a multicenter study conducted on this subject, the PT and AT values for ΔE00 in dentistry were reported to be 0.80 and 1.80 respectively [29].

According to CIEDE2000 thresholds [52], clinically unacceptable color change (ΔE₀₀ > AT) was primarily observed in highly translucent single-shade no-cap bulk-fill composites polymerized with ultra-short (3-seconds) high-power protocols. In contrast, Admira Fusion x-tra, when polymerized with standard curing, exhibited the lowest ΔE₀₀ values and remained within clinically acceptable limits.

These findings are consistent with previous studies reporting that curing-related parameters significantly influence both color stability and microhardness of contemporary bulk-fill composites [53].

In the present study, color stability differed significantly among the tested groups after coffee aging and appeared to be influenced by both curing protocol and material composition. Gonder and Fidan [54] reported that reducing polymerization time in bulk-fill resin composites decreased surface hardness and increased color change, accompanied by alterations in translucency parameters. Similarly, previous studies have shown that light-curing unit characteristics and polymerization protocols may affect both microhardness and color stability, although the magnitude of these effects varies depending on material formulation and experimental conditions [55–57]. In agreement with these reports, ultra-short high-power curing protocols were associated with increased ΔE₀₀ values in some material groups, particularly highly translucent single-shade composites, suggesting that insufficient or less homogeneous polymerization may contribute to staining susceptibility. However, the absence of a consistent correlation between ΔE₀₀ values and bottom-to-top hardness ratios indicates that discoloration was not solely governed by polymerization efficiency. Intrinsic material characteristics, including filler type, resin matrix composition, translucency, hydrophilicity, water sorption, and solubility, may also influence optical stability after staining, as supported by previous studies showing material-dependent differences in microhardness, color change, translucency, water sorption, and solubility of resin composites, including no-cap low-viscosity bulk-fill materials [58, 59]. Therefore, color stability in no-cap bulk-fill resin composites should be interpreted as the result of combined effects of curing strategy, polymer network formation, and material composition.

The increased ΔE₀₀ values observed in some ultra-short curing groups suggest that inadequate or less homogeneous polymerization may increase susceptibility to water sorption, staining agent penetration, and optical changes. However, the absence of a consistent correlation between ΔE₀₀ and bottom-to-top hardness ratio indicates that discoloration cannot be explained solely by polymerization efficiency and is also influenced by intrinsic material characteristics such as resin matrix hydrophilicity, filler morphology, and translucency.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the investigation was conducted under in vitro conditions and therefore cannot fully replicate the complex oral environment. In clinical situations, saliva, thermal fluctuations, pH changes, masticatory forces, biofilm formation, toothbrushing abrasion, dietary variability, and oral clearance may influence the polymerization behavior, mechanical performance, and color stability of resin composites.

Second, a specimen thickness of 3 mm was selected to create a clinically relevant and standardized condition for comparing high-power and ultra-short curing modes. However, bulk-fill resin composites are often marketed for placement in increments up to 4–5 mm. Therefore, the present findings should not be directly extrapolated to thicker increments, and future studies should evaluate the performance of no-cap bulk-fill composites at greater depths.

Third, microhardness and bottom-to-top hardness ratio were used as indirect indicators of polymerization efficiency and depth of cure. Although these parameters are widely accepted, they do not directly measure the degree of conversion or polymer network structure. Future studies using spectroscopic methods, such as Fourier-transform infrared or Raman spectroscopy, are needed to directly assess the relationship between curing mode, degree of conversion, chemical stability, and color change.

The aging protocol was also limited to immersion in a single staining medium, coffee. Although the 12-day coffee immersion period may approximate 12 months of coffee consumption based on an accelerated exposure model, this equivalence should be interpreted cautiously because intraoral coffee exposure is intermittent and influenced by saliva, oral clearance, temperature changes, dietary habits, and oral hygiene procedures. Moreover, thermocycling, mechanical loading, and toothbrushing simulation were not performed. Therefore, future studies incorporating combined aging protocols are needed to better simulate intraoral conditions.

Additional methodological limitations should also be noted. Polymerization was performed through a Mylar strip and glass slide to standardize specimen thickness and surface smoothness; however, the glass slide may have influenced light transmission or caused scattering of the light beam. Similarly, although the 1-mm curing distance allowed standardization of light delivery under laboratory conditions, it may not fully reproduce deep posterior cavities, where the curing tip is often positioned farther from the restorative material. Pulpal temperature rise was also not evaluated, although high-power and ultra-fast curing modes may increase heat generation during polymerization.

Finally, only two high-power light-curing units and a limited number of curing modes were investigated. Although the nominal light guide diameters were reported according to the manufacturers’ specifications, the beam profiles of the LCUs were not independently measured. Therefore, possible variations in irradiance distribution across the light tip should be considered when interpreting the findings. Further studies evaluating different LCUs, curing distances, exposure times, beam profiles, and clinically relevant cavity configurations are warranted.

From a clinical perspective, the results support the use of curing protocols that provide a balance between irradiance and exposure time rather than relying solely on ultra-rapid high-power modes. Admira Fusion x-tra and Simplishade Bulk Fill showed more favorable overall performance under the tested conditions, whereas Omnichroma Bulk Flow and Charisma Bulk Flow ONE were more susceptible to reduced depth of cure or discoloration depending on the curing mode. Therefore, clinicians should exercise caution when selecting ultra-short curing protocols for highly translucent no-cap bulk-fill composites.
