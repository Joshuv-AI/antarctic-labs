Stability of Vortex Patches in Channels
Zelin Dong, Chenyun Luo

Abstract
. In this paper, we investigate the orbital stability of vortex patches for the two-dimensional incompressible Euler equations in both a class of domains that satisfy the “weak finite volume condition” and a strip of arbitrary width. We establish that for suitable parameters ( μ , λ ) (\mu,\lambda) , the penalized kinetic energy functional admits a minimizer, and that every such minimizer satisfies the elliptic equation ω = λ ​ ( ψ − W ​ x 2 − γ ) + \omega=\lambda(\psi-Wx_{2}-\gamma)_{+} . Furthermore, we demonstrate that the set of minimizers is orbitally stable under the Eulerian dynamics. This work extends the variational framework developed by Abe and Choi [ 3 ] to domains that lack both spatial scaling invariance and horizontal translation invariance. The absence of these properties introduces substantial difficulties in the proof, as classical rearrangement and scaling arguments are no longer applicable. We overcome these obstacles by comparing the Green’s function with that of the half-plane and exploiting the decay condition to formulate a concentration-compactness argument that ultimately yields the desired stability result.



1.
Introduction

The two-dimensional Euler equation for the motion of an inviscid, incompressible fluid on a fixed domain
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
is given in vorticity form by

(1.1)

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\omega+u\cdot\nabla\omega

\displaystyle=0,~\quad\omega(x,0)=\omega_{0}(x)\quad\text{ in }\Omega.

Here,
\omega
is the vorticity of the flow, and the fluid velocity
u
satisfies the slip boundary condition:

\displaystyle u\cdot\mathcal{N}=0,

where
\mathcal{N}
is the unit outward normal. When the appropriate Biot-Savart law is applicable,
u
is determined from
\omega
in the sense that

u(x,t)=\nabla^{\perp}\int_{D}G(x,y)\omega(y,t)dy,

and
G
is the Green’s function for the Dirichlet problem in
\Omega
. It will be convenient for us to define the stream function associated with
\omega
:

(1.2)

\displaystyle\psi(x,t)=\int_{\Omega}G(x,y)\omega(y,t)dy,

so that
u(x,t)=\nabla^{\perp}\psi
. In addition, during this paper, when conducting a discussion at a fixed time, the time
t
will be omitted, i.e.,
\omega(\cdot,t)=\omega(\cdot),\psi(\cdot,t)=\psi(\cdot)
and
u(\cdot,t)=u(\cdot)
.

A natural question is whether solutions remain stable over long time intervals. In general, the answer is negative. For 2D Euler equations in a disk, Nadirashvili, Alexander, and Šverák
[
18
]
constructed solutions whose vorticity gradient grows double‑exponentially. Similar mechanisms were exploited on the torus
\mathbb{T}^{2}
by Zlatoš, Andrej
[
24
]
, applied to smooth domains with an axis of symmetry by Xu
[
23
]
, and also for free-boundary problems by Hu, Luo, and Yao
[
17
]
, demonstrating that instability can occur generically.

Nevertheless, stability can be established for certain domains with suitable classes of initial data. Cao and Wang
[
6
]
proved nonlinear stability of highly concentrated vortex patches near non‑degenerate minima of the Robin function; Cao, Wan, and Wang
[
5
]
showed orbital stability for kinetic‑energy‑maximising patches; Choi, Jeong, and Lim
[
7
]
studied the stability of monotone, nonnegative, and compactly supported vorticities in the half cylinder. Earlier work by Burton
[
4
]
and Turkington
[
22
]
established foundational stability results for vortex patches using rearrangement techniques. However, all these results highly rely on the specific geometry of the domain, the assumptions about the initial data, and the techniques used (such as rearrangement or translation invariance).

In recent years, a variational approach based on minimizing a penalized energy has been successfully applied to prove the orbital stability of vortex patches. Abe and Choi
[
3
]
established the stability of Lamb dipoles in the half‑plane
\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}
by considering the minimization problem

(1.3)

\displaystyle I_{\mu,\nu,\lambda}=\inf_{\omega\in K_{\mu,\nu}}\{-E_{2,\lambda}\},

where
K_{\mu,\nu}
is a class of admissible vorticities with fixed first moment and bounded
L^{1}
norm, and
E_{2,\lambda}
is the penalized kinetic energy. Their proof relies on two key properties of the half‑plane: i) the translation invariance property, which allows the use of rearrangement techniques, and ii) the spatial scaling invariance, which is used to obtain monotonicity and negativity and strictly decreasing in
\mu
of
I_{\mu,\nu,\lambda}
to control minimizing sequences.

The variational approach of Abe and Choi
[
3
]
has recently been extended to more complex symmetric configurations. Choi, Jeong, and Yao
[
8
]
studied the orbital stability of a pair of opposite-signed Lamb dipoles under odd-odd symmetry, as well as concentrated vortices in a quadrant, using the monotonicity of the first moment and pointwise kernel estimates. Abe, Choi, Jeong, Sim, and Woo
[
1
]
demonstrate the existence and stability of Sadovskii vortices. However, those results are heavily reliant on the convenience of the (half) plane, i.e., the explicit structure of the Green’s function, the scaling property, and rearrangement techniques. In contrast, domains meet the “weak finite volume condition” and infinite strip considered here lack these structures, requiring new tools to establish weak continuity of the kinetic energy and consequently the existence and stability of minimizers.

The main goal of this paper is to extend the variational approach to two classes of domains:

1.

\Omega=\mathcal{D}=D_{u}\cup D_{l}
,
where

(1.4)

\displaystyle D_{u}:=\mathcal{D}\cap\{x_{2}\geq 1\},

\displaystyle D_{l}:=\mathcal{D}\cap\{x_{2}<1\},

that satisfies the following two conditions:

•

The “weak finite volume condition”, i.e.,

(1.5)

\displaystyle\int_{D_{l}}x_{2}^{p}dx<\infty,\text{ for some }p\geq 0,\text{ and }~\text{Vol}(D_{u})<\infty.

•

The Biot-Savart law is applicable on
\mathcal{D}
.

Without loss of generality, it suffices to study the case when
p\geq 2
. This class includes a wide range of domains, including but not limited to all simply connected domains with (piecewise) smooth boundary
[
19
]
. The weak tangency condition on non-smooth domains is discussed in
[
16
]
and
[
15
]
. In particular, this class covers all bounded domains, domains with periodic boundaries, and domains whose boundaries are graphs of some appropriate functions. These are of considerable importance in the study of free-boundary problems.

2.

\Omega=\mathcal{S}
, where
\mathcal{S}
is the infinite strip

(1.6)

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}=\mathbb{R}\times(0,L),~L>0,

whose Green’s function is given explicitly by

(1.7)

\displaystyle G(x,y)=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{4\pi}\ln\Big(1+\frac{\sin(\frac{\pi}{L}x_{2})\sin(\frac{\pi}{L}y_{2})}{\sinh(\frac{\pi}{2L}(x_{1}-y_{1}))^{2}+\sin(\frac{\pi}{2L}(x_{2}-y_{2}))^{2}}\Big).

Such strips can be regarded as a generalization of the half-plane model to domains of finite width, or as a limit
p\to\infty
of Class
1.
. Nevertheless, due to the different widths
L
and the absence of decay of the domain itself prevent a direct application of the methods from
[
3
]
or from Class
1.
.

More precisely, we introduce the set of admissible vorticities for parameters
0\leq\mu,\nu,\lambda<\infty
:

\displaystyle K_{\mu,\nu}=\left\{\omega\in L^{2}(\Omega)\big|\omega\geq 0,\int_{\Omega}x_{2}\omega(x)dx=\mu,\int_{\Omega}\omega(x)dx\leq\nu\right\}.

The kinetic energy and the penalized energy are given by

\displaystyle E[\omega]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}G(x,y)\omega(x)\omega(y)dxdy,\quad E_{2,\lambda}[\omega]=E[\omega]-\frac{1}{2\lambda}\|\omega\|_{2}^{2},

where
G
is the Green’s function for the domain
\Omega
. Define
S_{\mu,\nu,\lambda}\subseteq K_{\mu,\nu}
by the set of minimizers for (
1.3
) in
K_{\mu,\nu}
. By the measure scaling,
\hat{\omega}(x)=\omega(x)/\nu,
the problem reduces to the case
\nu=1
. We abbreviate the notation as

\displaystyle K_{\mu}=K_{\mu,1},~I_{\mu,\lambda}=I_{\mu,1,\lambda},\text{ and }S_{\mu,\lambda}=S_{\mu,1,\lambda}.

Recall the the stream function associated with vorticity
\omega
in (
1.2
),

E[\omega]=\frac{1}{2}\int\psi\omega dx
. Adapting the proof of
[
3
]
to our setting encounters three main difficulties that demand new analytical tools.

1.1.
Loss of spatial scaling.

Due to the loss of spatial scaling invariance, the Green’s function on
\Omega
does not satisfy the property that
G(cx,cy)=G(x,y)
. Therefore, for any
c>0
and scaling
\widehat{\omega}(x)=\omega(cx)
, there is no possible connection between
E[\widehat{\omega}|_{\Omega}]
and
E[\omega]
. Meanwhile, if we consider the synchronous changes of the domain
\Omega
during scaling and regard
\widehat{\omega}
as a function defined on
c^{-1}\Omega
, the penalized energy of
\widehat{\omega}
should be:

\displaystyle E^{c}_{2,\lambda}[\widehat{\omega}]=

\displaystyle\int_{c^{-1}\Omega}\int_{c^{-1}\Omega}G^{c}(x,y)\widehat{\omega}(x)\widehat{\omega}(y)dxdy-\frac{1}{2\lambda}\int_{c^{-1}\Omega}\widehat{\omega}^{2}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c^{-4}\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}G(x,y)\omega(x)\omega(y)dxdy-\frac{1}{2\lambda}\int_{c^{-1}\Omega}\widehat{\omega}^{2}dx

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle c^{-4}E[\omega]-c^{-2}\frac{1}{2\lambda}\int_{\Omega}\omega^{2}dx,

where we temporarily use
E^{c}
and
G^{c}
to represent the kinetic energy and the Green’s function for
c^{-1}\Omega=\left\{c^{-1}x:x\in\Omega\right\}
. To compare
E_{2,\lambda}^{c}[\widehat{\omega}]
with
E_{2,\lambda}[\omega]
, it is necessary to let
c^{-4}=c^{-2}
with an unique solution
c=1
, which is meaningless.

This issue directly led to the failure of the methods used in
[
3
]
to study kinetic energy and penalized energy. Fortunately, based on the inclusion relationship
\Omega\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
, we have

(1.8)

\displaystyle 0\leq G(x,y)\leq G_{H}(x,y)\text{ for any }x,y\in\overline{\Omega},

where
G_{H}
is the Green’s function for the upper half plane, and this comparison allows us to derive estimates similar to those in
[
3
]
. By choosing appropriate parameters
\mu,\lambda
and constructing explicit trial functions in
K_{\mu,\lambda}
, we prove negativity of
I_{\mu,\nu,\lambda}
. Other impacts of the loss of scaling will be mentioned and solved below.

1.2.
Loss of horizontal translation invariance (Class
1.
).

The Green’s function of domains in this class
cannot be written as
G(x,y)=g(|x_{1}-y_{1}|,x_{2},y_{2})
for some function
g
, so that the kinetic energy is not translation invariant. Meanwhile, such difficulty also undermines all techniques that rely on rearrangement methods.

To overcome this, we introduce the weak finite volume condition of
\mathcal{D}
itself to show that the weighted mass of any minimizing sequence concentrates on a bounded region. In particular, for any minimizing sequence
\{\omega_{n}\}
and
\epsilon>0
, there exists a bounded domain
D_{0}\subseteq\mathcal{D}
such that the kinetic energy, as well as crossing terms, involving
\omega_{n}|_{\mathcal{D}\setminus D_{0}}
is in
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
. Under such a split, the Green’s function is square-integrable on
D_{0}\times D_{0}
, so that we can pass the limit in kinetic energy using weak
L^{2}
convergence and obtain the existence of a minimizer and a general convergence theorem.

1.3.
Difficulty in proving strict monotonicity of
I_{\mu}
(Class
2.
).

The method in
[
3
]
, as well as
[
13
]
and
[
12
]
, applies to prove compactness of a minimizing sequence satisfying

(1.9)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} &\omega(x_{1},x_{2})=\omega(-x_{1},x_{2}),\\
&\omega(x_{1},x_{2})\text{ is non-increasing for }x_{1}>0.\end{aligned}

Under such an assumption, these authors discuss the weighted mass of the minimizing sequence of
I_{\mu,\nu,\lambda}
is concentrated under a proper
x_{1}
-translation, and the key to this lies in the rearrangement and the strictly decreasing nature of
I_{\mu,\nu,\lambda}
. The former can be resolved by two points: i) the structure of the Green’s function for
\mathcal{S}
meet the criteria of standard rearrangement inequality (see Proposition
4.5
), ii) the comparison (
1.8
) leads to a comparison between stream functions by viewing
L^{i}(\mathcal{S})=\{\omega\in L^{i}(\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2})~|~\operatorname{spt}\omega\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{S}}\}
, then for any proper
i>1
and
\omega\in L^{i}(\mathcal{S})
can generate a stream function
\psi^{H}
on
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
in the sense that

\displaystyle\psi^{H}(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}G_{H}(x,y)\omega(y)dy=\int_{\mathcal{S}}G_{H}(x,y)\omega(y)dy,

with

(1.10)

\displaystyle\psi(x)\leq\psi^{H}(x)\text{ for any }x\in\mathcal{S}.

The latter is more complicated due to the lack of scaling invariance. We cannot follow the same route, instead, it is necessary to proof it alone a new path: prove the existence of a minimizer (see Sec
4.1
), and then demonstrate more properties of minimizers as
\mu
is sufficiently small, such as an uniform
L^{1}
bound, to establish the strict monotonicity of
I_{\mu,\nu,\lambda}
(Proposition
4.12
). This strict monotonicity is finally employed in a concentration-compactness framework to obtain a general convergence theorem for minimizing sequences (Theorem
4.14
).

A Minimization Problem

We establish the basic properties of the minimization problem. Thanks to the observation (

Proposition 2.1
.

The following estimates hold for

\displaystyle E[\omega]\leq C\|x_{2}\omega\|_{1}^{1/2}\|\omega\|_{1}\|\omega\|_{2}^{1/2},

(2.3)

\displaystyle\Bigg|\int_{\Omega}\int_{\Omega}G(x,y)\omega_{1}(y)\omega_{2}(x)dxdy\Bigg|\leq C\|\omega_{1}\|_{1}^{1/2}\|\omega_{1}\|_{2}^{1/2}\|x_{2}\omega_{2}\|_{1}^{1/2}\|\omega_{2}\|_{1}^{1/2},

\displaystyle|E[\omega_{1}]-E[\omega_{2}]|\leq C\|\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}\|_{1}^{1/2}\|\omega_{1}-\omega_{2}\|_{2}^{1/2}\|x_{2}(\omega_{1}+\omega_{2})\|_{1}^{1/2}\|\omega_{1}+\omega_{2}\|_{1}^{1/2}.

Use the inequality

\displaystyle\Bigg(\int_{\Omega}G(x,y)^{q}dy\bigg)^{1/q}\leq\Bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}}G_{H}(x,y)^{q}dy\Bigg)^{1/q}\leq Cx_{2}^{2/q}.

Then all estimates (
2.1
)-(
2.4
) can be obtained through the same arguments in Proposition 2.1,
[
3
]
.

\Box

(2.7)

\displaystyle I_{\mu,\lambda}<0,

\displaystyle\text{ for any }(\mu,\lambda)\in\bigcup_{K\subseteq\Omega\text{ and }|K|<\infty}(0,\mu_{K}]\times(\lambda_{K},\infty).

3.
General Convergence Theorem for the Class
1.

With the preliminary estimates and the negativity of
I_{\mu,\lambda}
established, the next step is to prove that a general minimizing sequence actually converges to a minimizer (Theorem
3.2
). To achieve this goal, it is necessary to study the convergence of the kinetic energy
E[\omega]
under weak
L^{2}
convergence.

Proposition 3.1

(Convergence of the kinetic energy)
.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be a domain in
\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
satisfy the weak finite volume condition. Assume
\{\omega_{n}\}\subset L^{1}\cap L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
and
\omega\in L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
satisfy:

•

\sup_{n}\|\omega_{n}\|_{1}+\sup_{n}\|\omega_{n}\|_{2}+\|\omega\|_{1}+\|\omega\|_{2}\leq M
for some constants
M
, and

•

\omega_{n}\rightharpoonup\omega
weakly in
L^{2}(\mathcal{D})
.

Then for any
\epsilon>0
, there exists
N=N(\epsilon)>0
, such that

(3.1)

\displaystyle\sup_{n}\int_{D_{l}\cap\{|x_{1}|>N\}}x_{2}\omega_{n}dx\leq\epsilon,

hence

E[\omega_{n}]\to E[\omega]
.

Proof:

Suppose that (
3.1
) is false. Then there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
and a subsequence
\{\omega_{n_{k}}\}
such that for any
k\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 1}
,

\displaystyle\int_{D_{l}\cap\{|x_{1}|>k\}}x_{2}\omega_{n_{k}}dx>\epsilon_{0}.

Thus

\displaystyle\epsilon_{0}<\int_{D_{l}\cap\{|x_{1}|>k\}}x_{2}\omega_{n_{k}}dx\leq\Bigg(\int_{D_{l}\cap\{|x_{1}|>k\}}x_{2}^{p}dx\Bigg)^{1/p}\Bigg(\int_{D_{l}\cap\{|x_{1}|>k\}}\omega_{n_{k}}^{q}dx\Bigg)^{1/q}.

where
\frac{1}{p}+\frac{1}{q}=1
with
p\geq 2
, we see
q\in(1,2]
. By the weak finite volume condition (
1.5
) for
D_{l}
,

\displaystyle\int_{D_{l}\cap\{|x_{1}|>k\}}x_{2}^{p}dx\rightarrow 0,\text{ as }k\rightarrow\infty.

Then

•

\sup_{n}\|\omega_{n}\|_{1}+\sup_{n}\|\omega_{n}\|_{2}\leq M
for some constants

, and

•

\omega_{n}\rightharpoonup\omega
in
L^{2}(\mathcal{S})
.

If each
\omega_{n}
is nonnegative and satisfies (

E[\omega_{n}]\to E[\omega]
as
n\rightarrow\infty
.

Proof.

We decompose the energy into two terms

\displaystyle 2E[\omega_{n}]=\int_{\mathcal{S}}\psi_{n}(x)\omega_{n}(x)\,dx=\int_{Q}\psi_{n}(x)\omega_{n}(x)\,dx+\int_{\mathcal{S}\setminus Q}\psi_{n}(x)\omega_{n}(x)\,dx,

and observe that

\displaystyle\int_{Q}\psi_{n}(x)

\displaystyle\omega_{n}(x)dx

\displaystyle=\int_{Q}\omega_{n}(x)\int_{Q}G(x,y)\omega_{n}(y)\,dydx+\int_{Q}\omega_{n}(x)\int_{\mathcal{S}\setminus Q}G(x,y)\omega_{n}(y)dydx,

\displaystyle=\int_{Q}\omega_{n}(x)\int_{Q}G(x,y)\omega_{n}(y)\,dydx+\int_{\mathcal{S}\setminus Q}\omega_{n}(y)\int_{Q}G(x,y)\omega_{n}(x)dxdy,

\displaystyle\leq\int_{Q}\omega_{n}(x)\int_{Q}G(x,y)\omega_{n}(y)\,dydx+\int_{\mathcal{S}\setminus Q}\psi_{n}(x)\omega_{n}(x)\,dx.

Applying (
4.11

\displaystyle\Bigl|E[\omega_{n}]-\frac{1}{2}\int_{Q}\int_{Q}G(x,y)\omega_{n}(x)\omega_{n}(y)\,dx\,dy\Bigr|\leq

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{S}\setminus Q}\psi_{n}(x)\omega_{n}(x)\,dx,

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\frac{C}{\min\{A,R\}^{1/2}}.

By estimating
E[\omega]
in the same way,

\displaystyle 2|E[\omega_{n}]-E[\omega]|\leq\Bigl|\int_{Q}\int_{Q}G(x,y)(\omega(x)\omega(y)-\omega_{n}(x)\omega_{n}(y))\,dx\,dy\Bigr|+\frac{C}{\min\{A,R\}^{1/2}}.

Since

and
\omega_{n}(x)\omega_{n}(y)\rightharpoonup\omega(x)\omega(y)
in
L^{2}(Q\times Q)
, sending

and

imply the desired result.

∎

Before stating the next result, we pause to note that the path to Lemma 3.2 in
[
3
]
has now been fully cleared in our strip setting. Specifically, Proposition
4.5
provides the Steiner symmetrization with proper properties, Proposition

guarantees that the weak

[

Proposition 4.8
.

For
0<\nu<\infty,0<\mu<M_{1}\nu,\lambda>\frac{\pi^{5}}{2L^{2}}
, there exists a minimizer

(4.12)

\displaystyle E_{2,\lambda}[\tilde{\omega}]=\inf_{\tilde{\omega}\in\tilde{K}_{\mu,\nu}}\left\{-E_{2,\lambda}[\tilde{\omega}]\right\},

where

Moreover,

with a compact support. In particular, it is also a minimizer of (

Proof.

The result follows by reproducing the proof of Lemma 3.2 in
[

]
in our setting. That argument rests on two pillars: i) a Steiner symmetrization that preserves the constraints while raising the penalized energy (given by Proposition

In the final step, one can use the same argument in Lemma 3.2 in

∎

4.2.
General Convergence Theorem

Having established the existence of a minimizer, we now aim for the main result of this section: proving that any minimizing sequence is (up to a subsequence) strongly convergent to a minimizer. The concentration-compactness principle (Proposition
4.13
) will be the primary tool. However, to apply this principle and, in particular, to exclude the dichotomy scenario, the strict decrease of
I_{\mu,\lambda}
with respect to
\mu
is required.
We begin by deriving important uniform bounds for all minimizers with small impulse. These bounds, especially the
L^{1}
bound, are indispensable for the perturbation argument that proves the strict decreasing (Proposition
4.12
).

Proposition 4.9
.

(
L^{1}
estimate of minimizers)
For any
0<\nu<\infty,0<\mu<M_{1}\nu
and
\lambda\geq\pi^{5}(2L^{2})^{-1}
, we have

(4.13)

\displaystyle\sup_{\omega\in S_{\mu,\nu,\lambda}}|\operatorname{spt}\omega|\leq\frac{5}{2}\pi^{5}\nu^{-2}L^{2}.

Consequently,

(4.14)

\displaystyle\sup_{\omega\in S_{\mu,\nu,\lambda}}\|\omega\|_{1}\leq C^{3}\mu\nu^{-3}\lambda^{2}L^{3},

(4.15)

\displaystyle\sup_{\omega\in S_{\mu,\nu,\lambda}}\|\omega\|_{2}\leq C^{3}\mu\nu^{-2}\lambda^{2}L^{2},

for some constant
C
independent from
\mu,\nu,\lambda,L
.

Proof.

It is sufficient to consider the case when
\nu=1
. For any
\omega\in S_{\mu,\lambda}
, with
\mu\leq M_{1}
and
\lambda\geq\pi^{5}(2L^{2})^{-1}
. Recall the equation (
2.10
) with
W=W(\omega)>0
and
\gamma=0
. Then, multiplying the equation
-\Delta(\psi-Wx_{2})=\lambda(\psi-Wx_{2})_{+}
by
\psi-Wx_{2}
and integrating by parts,

\displaystyle\int_{\operatorname{spt}\omega}|\nabla\psi|^{2}-2W\partial_{2}\psi+W^{2}dx=

\displaystyle\int_{\operatorname{spt}\omega}|\nabla(\psi-Wx_{2})|^{2}dx,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\lambda\int_{\operatorname{spt}\omega}|\psi-Wx_{2}|^{2}dx=\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\operatorname{spt}\omega}|\omega|^{2}dx.

Since
\psi-Wx_{2}=0
on

, we have

\displaystyle\int_{\operatorname{spt}\omega}\partial_{2}\psi=

\displaystyle\int_{\operatorname{spt}\omega}\partial_{2}(\psi-Wx_{2})+W|\operatorname{spt}\omega|,

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\int_{\partial(\operatorname{spt}\omega)}n_{2}(\psi-Wx_{2})+W|\operatorname{spt}\omega|=W|\operatorname{spt}\omega|.

Then

\displaystyle 2E[\omega]-W^{2}|\operatorname{spt}\omega|

\displaystyle\geq\int_{\operatorname{spt}\omega}|\nabla\psi|^{2}-W^{2}|\operatorname{spt}\omega|=\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_{\operatorname{spt}\omega}|\omega|^{2}dx.

Hence,

\displaystyle W^{2}|\operatorname{spt}\omega|

\displaystyle\leq 2E_{2,\lambda}[\omega]\leq-2I_{\mu,\lambda}.

Meanwhile,

(4.16)

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} -I_{\mu,\lambda}=&E_{2,\lambda}[\omega]=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\operatorname{spt}\omega}\psi\omega-\frac{1}{2\lambda}\int_{\operatorname{spt}\omega}\omega^{2},\\
=&\frac{1}{2}\int_{\operatorname{spt}\omega}(\psi-Wx_{2})\omega+\frac{1}{2}W\mu-\frac{1}{2\lambda}\int_{\operatorname{spt}\omega}\omega^{2}=\frac{1}{2}W\mu,\end{aligned}

then

\displaystyle|\operatorname{spt}\omega|

\displaystyle\leq\mu/W.

Recall there exists a lower bound for
-I_{\mu,\lambda}\geq\frac{\mu^{2}}{5\pi^{5}L^{2}}
, then the identity (
4.16
) implies

\displaystyle W\geq\frac{2\mu}{5\pi^{5}L^{2}}.

Hence

\displaystyle|\operatorname{spt}\omega|

\displaystyle\leq\mu/W\leq\frac{5}{2}\pi^{5}L^{2},

and the proof finished by the Cauchy inequality and the
L^{2}
uniform bound (
2.9
) while
\omega_{n}\equiv\omega
for all
n
:

\displaystyle\|\omega\|_{1}\leq

\displaystyle\|\omega\|_{2}|\operatorname{spt}\omega|^{1/2}\leq C\mu^{1/3}\lambda^{2/3}L\|\omega\|_{1}^{2/3},

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\|\omega\|_{1}\leq

\displaystyle C^{3}\mu\lambda^{2}L^{3},

\displaystyle\Rightarrow\|\omega\|_{2}\leq

\displaystyle C\mu^{1/3}\lambda^{2/3}\|\omega\|_{1}^{2/3}\leq C^{3}\mu\lambda^{2}L^{2}.

∎

Remark 4.10
.

The proof shows that, for a small

Remark 4.11

Combined with estimates (

Before proving the strict monotonicity, we recall that in Proposition

Proposition 4.12
.

(Strictly decreasing of

)
For any
0<\nu<\infty
and

Proof.

It is sufficient to consider the case

. Note that
\omega_{\mu}
is compactly supported has an

given in Proposition

. For

\displaystyle\omega_{s}(x)=\omega_{\mu}(x)+s\omega_{0}(x+\tau e_{1}),

\displaystyle-I_{\alpha,\lambda}

∎

Proposition 4.13
.

Let
0<\mu<\infty
. Let
\{\rho_{n}\}\subset L^{1}(\mathcal{S})

\displaystyle\rho_{n}\geq 0,\quad n\geq 1,\qquad\int_{S}\rho_{n}\,dx=\mu_{n}\to\mu\quad\text{as }n\to\infty.

satisfying one of the following:

(1)

(Compactness)

\displaystyle\liminf_{k\to\infty}\int_{B(y_{k},R)\cap\mathcal{S}}\rho_{n_{k}}\,dx\geq\mu-\epsilon.

In addition, it is possible to assume

for all

.

(2)

(Vanishing)
For each

\displaystyle\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{y\in\mathcal{S}}\int_{B(y,R)\cap\mathcal{S}}\rho_{n_{k}}\,dx=0.

(3)

(Dichotomy)
There exists

such that for every
\epsilon>0
there exist

and
\{\rho_{k}^{1}\},\{\rho_{k}^{2}\}\subset L^{1}(\mathcal{S})
such that
\operatorname{spt}\rho_{k}^{1}\cap\operatorname{spt}\rho_{k}^{2}=\emptyset

\displaystyle\limsup_{k\to\infty}\Bigl\{\|\rho_{n_{k}}-\rho_{k}^{1}-\rho_{k}^{2}\|_{L^{1}}+\Bigl|\int_{S}\rho_{k}^{1}\,dx-\alpha\Bigr|+\Bigl|\int_{S}\rho_{k}^{2}\,dx-(\mu-\alpha)\Bigr|\Bigr\}\leq\epsilon,

\displaystyle d(\operatorname{spt}\rho_{k}^{1},\operatorname{spt}\rho_{k}^{2})\to\infty\quad\text{as }k\to\infty.

Proof.

The assertion is proved by Lemma I.1 in
[

for

and the fixed mass
\mu_{n}=\mu

[

for

[
3

by view

. From this perspective, the sequence of

meets the requirement of Lemma 4.1 in

and then yields three possibilities: compactness, vanishing, or dichotomy.

(1)

(Compactness)
In this case, the tightness is originally stated with centres
y_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}

, then for large

, forcing the integral to vanish and contradicting tightness. Therefore,
\sup_{k}y_{k,2}<\infty

for all

for all

(2)

(Vanishing)
In this case, one can use the set relation

to conclude for any

\displaystyle 0\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{y\in\mathcal{S}}\int_{B(y,R)\cap\mathcal{S}}\rho_{n_{k}}\,dx\leq\lim_{k\to\infty}\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\int_{B(y,R)\cap\mathbb{R}_{+}^{2}}\rho_{n_{k}}\,dx=0.

(3)

(Dichotomy)
In this case, due to

\operatorname{spt}\rho_{n_{k}}^{i}\subseteq\operatorname{spt}\rho_{k}\subseteq\overline{\mathcal{S}}

∎

Under the strict decreasing of
I_{\mu,\lambda}
(Proposition
4.12
), the convergence of kinetic energy for Steiner-symmetrized sequences (Proposition
4.7
), and the concentration-compactness alternatives (Proposition
4.13
), we can now outline the proof of the general convergence theorem for the strip. This theorem parallels the strategy of Abe & Choi
[
3
]
for the half-plane, but its validity in the strip domain
\mathcal{S}
hinges on the adaptations made in the preceding propositions.

The proof proceeds by eliminating vanishing and dichotomy for a minimizing sequence
\{\omega_{n}\}
. Vanishing is ruled out by the negativity of
I_{\mu,\lambda}
. The more delicate case is dichotomy, where a minimizing sequence splits into two well-separated components. By applying Steiner symmetrization to these components and using the strict monotonicity of
I_{\mu,\lambda}
, we deduce a contradiction to its minimality, forcing compactness instead. We now formalize this result.

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
0<\nu<\infty,\mu<M_{2}\nu
and
\lambda\geq\pi^{5}(2L^{2})^{-1}
. For any minimizing sequence
\{\omega_{n}\}
satisfying
\omega_{n}\in K_{\mu_{n},\nu}
,
\mu_{n}\rightarrow\mu
and
-E_{2,\lambda}[\omega_{n}]\rightarrow I_{\mu,\nu,\lambda}
, then there exists a subsequence, still denoted by
\{\omega_{n}\}
, s.t. there exists
\omega\in K_{\mu,\nu}
,

\omega_{n}\rightarrow\omega
and
x_{2}\omega_{n}\rightarrow x_{2}\omega
strongly in
L^{2}(\mathcal{S})
and
L^{1}(\mathcal{S})
respectively.

Proof.

The proof follows the concentration-compactness argument of Theorem 1.3 in
[
3
]
, which we briefly outline. By Proposition
4.13
, the sequence
x_{2}\omega_{n}
(up to a subsequence) satisfies one of three scenarios: compactness, vanishing, or dichotomy. Vanishing cannot occur since
I_{\mu,\lambda}<0
. If dichotomy were to occur, the sequence would split into two non-trivial parts with strictly smaller impulses. Applying Steiner symmetrization (Proposition
4.7
) and the strict monotonicity of
I_{\mu,\lambda}
(Proposition
4.12
), one derives a contradiction to the minimizing property of
\{\omega_{n}\}
, as shown in detail in the Theorem 1.3,
[
3
]
. Therefore, the sequence must be locally compact up to horizontal translations along the boundary
\mathcal{S}
. The strong convergence of the kinetic energy then follows from Proposition
4.7
, which identifies the limit as a minimizer.
∎

5.
Stability respect to the minimizer

Having established the existence of minimizers and a general convergence theorem, we now turn to their orbital stability with
C_{c}^{\infty}(\Omega)
initial data. Using the strong convergence shown in Theorem
3.2
for
\Omega=\mathcal{D}
and Theorem
4.14
for
\Omega=\mathcal{S}
, we can show that the set of minimizers is orbital stable in the sense that any flow starting close to a minimizer will remain close to the set of minimizers during its lifespan. We conclude this section by deriving the precise functional form of these minimizers.

The proof here is the same as that for Theorem 1.4 in
[
3
]
, but to ensure completeness, we present it below.

Theorem 5.1
.

Let
\nu,\mu,\lambda
given in the Theorem
3.2
for the case

and given in the Theorem
4.14
for the case
\Omega=\mathcal{S}
, then

is orbitally stable in the sense that: for any

\displaystyle\inf_{\omega\in S_{\mu,\nu,\lambda}}\Big\{\|\xi_{0}-\omega\|_{2}+\|x_{2}(\xi_{0}-\omega)\|_{1}\Big\}\leq\delta,

the solution

satisfy:
\forall~t\in[0,T_{\text{lifespan}})
,

(5.1)

\displaystyle\inf_{\omega\in S_{\mu,\nu,\lambda}}\Big\{\|\xi(t)-\omega\|_{2}+\|x_{2}(\xi(t)-\omega)\|_{1}\Big\}\leq\epsilon.

Proof:
It suffices to prove the case when
\nu=1
. Suppose that (
5.1
) is false. Then there exists
\epsilon_{0}>0
, a sequence
\{\xi_{0,n}\}\subseteq C_{c}^{\infty}(\mathcal{D}),~\xi_{0,n}\geq 0,~\|\xi_{0,n}\|_{1}\leq 1
and
t_{n}
in the lifespan of the solution
\xi_{n}
based on the initial data
\xi_{0,n}
s.t.

\displaystyle\inf_{\omega\in S_{\mu,\lambda}}\Big\{\|\xi_{0,n}-\omega\|_{2}+\|x_{2}(\xi_{0,n}-\omega)\|_{1}\Big\}

\displaystyle\leq 1/n,

\displaystyle\inf_{\omega\in S_{\mu,\lambda}}\Big\{\|\xi_{n}(t_{n})-\omega\|_{2}+\|x_{2}(\xi_{n}(t_{n})-\omega)\|_{1}\Big\}

\displaystyle>\epsilon_{0}.

We write
\xi_{n}=\xi_{n}(t_{n})
by suppressing

Thus

be a minimizing sequence s.t.

as

Knowing that
\xi
conserved on particle trajectory map, then

and

for any

Hence
\{\xi_{n}\}
be a minimizing sequence s.t.
\xi_{n}\in K_{\mu_{n}},\mu_{n}\rightarrow\mu

\displaystyle 0=

\Box

6.
Discussion

In the end, we discuss some generalizations of Theorem
5.1
, and state some open questions.

6.1.
More general domains.

A natural question arises as to whether the stability result applies to more general domains. It would be valuable to identify optimal conditions on the domain that ensure both the weak continuity of kinetic energy and the existence of minimizers. This is particularly relevant for domains with even weaker volume conditions and small perturbations of the strip model. Examples of such domains include shapes defined by functions like
(\ln(2+|x_{1}|))^{-1}
,
1/x
, and
e^{-x^{2}/2}+1
, along with
\partial\mathbb{R}^{2}_{+}
as boundaries, among others. Their behavior is unpredictable due to the absence of technical tools, such as rearrangement, and the concentrated nature resulting from the decay of the domain itself.

6.2.
Free-boundary problems.

Since the Class
1.
is very general, for a free-boundary problem whose initial domain satisfies the decay assumption (
1.5
), it is believed that within a certain time, the free-boundary domain
\mathcal{D}_{t}
will still satisfy the condition
1.5
. This holds particularly for the domains studied in Hu–Luo–Yao
[
17
]
.
A pertinent question arises regarding whether the stability issues discussed in this paper can be extended to the free-boundary case. This concept may be further explored by referencing the approximate Biot-Savart law proposed in
[
17
]
. However, it is important to note that their study focuses on local properties, whereas stability studies necessitate a reliance on global considerations, which requires more advanced techniques.

6.3.
Multiple patches and more study of minimizers.

Our results extend the work of
[
3
]
from a single vortex patch to more general domains. A natural next step is to consider configurations with multiple patches, as studied for dipole pairs in
[
8
]
. However, extending our approach to dipole pairs presents two major difficulties.

First, the structure of the minimizer set
S_{\mu,\nu,\lambda}
remains largely unknown for both domain classes
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{S}
. Consequently, a vortex evolving under the Euler dynamics might approach minimizers with different spatial structures over its lifespan, making stability analysis significantly more challenging.

Second, the method of
[
8
]
