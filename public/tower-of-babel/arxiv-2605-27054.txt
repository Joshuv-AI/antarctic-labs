Lack of Gevrey solvability for a model operator
Enrico Bernardi

Abstract
We prove that the Cauchy problem for the model hyperbolic operator in ℝ 4 \mathbb{R}^{4} Q = − D t 2 + 2 ​ x ​ D t ​ D y + D x 2 + x 3 ​ D y 2 + D z 2 + z 2 ​ D y 2 Q=-D_{t}^{2}+2xD_{t}D_{y}+D_{x}^{2}+x^{3}D_{y}^{2}+D_{z}^{2}+z^{2}D_{y}^{2} is not locally solvable at the origin, in the Gevrey s s class if s > 6 s>6 .



Keywords:
Cauchy Problem, Hyperbolic Operators,
Gevrey classes, Hamilton systems, Analysis of ODEs.

1
Introduction and main theorem

Dating back to the foundational papers
[
7
,
10
]
, the
theory of well-posedness for the Cauchy problem of linear hyperbolic
operators with double characteristics in the
C^{\infty}
category has progressed towards an ever
more complete understanding of the subtle interplay between the
symplectic invariants of the double set and the functional analytic
features of the problem.

In
[
11
]
, to which we refer for all the standard notations and
terminology, one finds a comprehensive account of the state of the art
in the case of
non-effectively hyperbolic
operators. These are
identified as those operators whose symbol at a double point has its
fundamental matrix—i.e. its Hessian read via
\sigma=d\xi\wedge dx
, the
symplectic 2-form in the cotangent of
\mathbb{R}^{n+1}
—without real eigenvalues. When the principal
symbol’s spectral behavior never changes, thus avoiding transition
cases, the general picture is today fairly clear. For some recent work on the
transition cases see however

[
2
]
and

[
3
]
.

The only situation not yet fully understood in the domain where
the spectral type of the fundamental matrix never varies, is when
two phenomena occur simultaneously: there exists a null
bicharacteristic tangent to the double manifold,
and
the
positive trace

\mathrm{Tr}^{+}F_{p}

of the principal symbol — the sum of positive imaginary
eigenvalues of the fundamental matrix — is strictly positive.

On the other hand it is known that the
existence of a null tangent bicharacteristic, as shown e.g. in

[
1
]
or proven again in
[
11
]
, modifies the
natural Gevrey threshold (which is
2
for general operators with
double characteristics, see
[
4
]
for the case of operators
with any multiplicity) beyond which the Cauchy problem
well-posedness may cease to exist.

Furthermore this type of unstable Hamilton system may happen in only
one of the symplectic types in which the algebraic
structure of the non-effectively hyperbolic symbols can be classified: the complete list
of these cases
can be found for example in
[
8
]
.

In this regard we recall a simple example, where
the positive trace is
0
and the necessary
Ivriĭ–Petkov–Hörmander conditions on the lower order terms
are verified, and yet the Cauchy problem at the origin for

P=-D_{t}^{2}+2xD_{t}D_{y}+D_{x}^{2}+x^{3}D_{y}^{2},

(1.1)

fails to be locally solvable in
\gamma^{(s)}
(see below
Definition
1.2
for the definition of the Gevrey spaces

\gamma^{(s)}
) for
s>5
, with the convention

D_{j}=\frac{1}{i}\partial_{x_{j}}

for derivatives.
This
is essentially due to the existence of a simple null bicharacteristic

x=-t^{2}/4,\ y=t^{5}/8,\ \tau=0,\ \xi=t^{3}/8,\ \eta=1,

(1.2)

parametrized by
t
and landing tangentially onto

\Sigma=\{\tau=x=\xi=0,\,\eta\neq 0\}
.

Moreover this phenomenon happens even in some transition
cases, i.e. when the spectral type of the
principal symbol of the operator varies along the double manifold, see
the recent result

[
3
]
.

As explained in
[
11
, p. 141]
, after renaming the
variables, one model in this yet undecided category where the positive
trace

\mathrm{Tr}^{+}F_{p}>0
and admitting a null tangent
bicharacteristic, with
(t,x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{4}
, is given by

Q=-D_{t}^{2}+2xD_{t}D_{y}+D_{x}^{2}+x^{3}D_{y}^{2}+D_{z}^{2}+z^{2}D_{y}^{2}.

(1.3)

This model, which can be easily characterized in a symplectically invariant
way, has analytic coefficients, verifies the necessary
Ivriĭ–Petkov–Hörmander conditions on the lower-order terms,
its subprincipal symbol being
0

like in (
1.1
), and comes with a null tangent bicharacteristic to
its double manifold

\Sigma=\{\tau=x=z=\zeta=\xi=0,\,\eta\neq 0\}
. However the presence
of the added harmonic
oscillator in the
z
variable is enough to completely disrupt the
technique to show lack of Gevrey
s
solvability when
s>5
,
which was used in
[
11
]
and was based on a fine study of the zeros of the
Stokes coefficients (see e.g.
[
13
]
for some of their
general properties) of a second order ODE attached to (
1.1
).
As noted in

[
11
, p. 141]
, until now it was only known that the Cauchy problem
for
Q
is
\gamma^{(6)}
-well-posed.

The purpose of this paper is to prove the following result. (See
Definition
1.2
below for the Gevrey space
\gamma^{(s)}
).

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
s>6
. The Cauchy problem for
Q
at the origin is not locally
solvable, in the sense of Definition
1.3
, in the
(Roumieu) Gevrey class
\gamma^{(s)}
.

Theorem
1.1
thus shows that
s=6
is the precise
Gevrey threshold for the local solvability problem for
Q
, just as

s=5
was the precise
Gevrey threshold for the local solvability problem for
P
.

The proof of Theorem
1.1
follows the usual
closed graph and contradiction strategy utilized in
[
1
]
and
[
11
]
in the study of
tangent bicharacteristics, but the
model considered here requires a different scaling and a different
one-dimensional reduction.

We construct a family of exact analytic null solutions depending on a large
real parameter
\lambda
:

V_{\lambda}(t,x,y,z)=e^{i\tau_{\lambda}t}e^{i\lambda^{6}y}\psi_{\lambda}(z)W_{\lambda}(\lambda^{2}x),\qquad\tau_{\lambda}=\sigma\lambda^{2}-i\gamma\lambda,

where
\sigma>0
,
\gamma>0
, and
\psi_{\lambda}
is the ground state of the
harmonic oscillator in the
z
-variable at frequency
\lambda^{6}
. The
function
W_{\lambda}
is the recessive solution at
+\infty
of the reduced
ordinary differential equation

\left[-\partial_{X}^{2}+\lambda^{2}q_{\lambda}(X)\right]W_{\lambda}(X)=0,\qquad X=\lambda^{2}x,

chosen with its natural WKB or Liouville–Green normalization on the right. More precisely, the
branch of
q_{\lambda}^{1/2}
is fixed by

q_{\lambda}(0)^{1/2}=1+O(\lambda^{-2}),

and the normalization is chosen so that, for
X\geq 0
,

W_{\lambda}(X)=q_{\lambda}(X)^{-1/4}\exp\left(-\lambda\int_{0}^{X}q_{\lambda}(r)^{1/2}\,dr\right)\left(1+O(\lambda^{-1})\right).

In particular,

W_{\lambda}(0)=q_{\lambda}(0)^{-1/4}\left(1+O(\lambda^{-1})\right)=1+O(\lambda^{-1}),

so no additional normalization at
X=0
is needed. This gives a uniform lower
bound for the cutoff test function near the
origin

|W_{\lambda}(\lambda^{2}x)|\geq c,\qquad|x|\leq c_{0}\lambda^{-3}.

The reduced equation has a polynomial potential with a single simple complex
turning point
X_{\lambda}
, at
O(\lambda^{-1})
distance to the
unique real negative solution

X_{\ast}
of
q_{\infty}(X)=0
, see Figure
2
. We use the
standard Liouville–Green (in the sequel shorthanded by LG) and Airy connection machinery near
X_{\lambda}
to
continue the subdominant solution at
+\infty
across the turning point region and into the
relevant negative real region. In fact the connection relation among
the solutions tells us that continuing the recessive branch
through the Airy neighborhood does not create an exponentially large
coefficient. This eventually yields the upper bounds

\left|\partial_{x}^{k}W_{\lambda}(\lambda^{2}x)\right|\leq C^{k+1}k!\lambda^{M+6k}\exp\left(A_{\sigma}\lambda+C(\sigma)\gamma\sqrt{\delta}\,\lambda\right),\qquad|x|\leq\delta,

where

A_{\sigma}=\int_{X_{\ast}}^{0}\sqrt{V_{\sigma}(r)}\,dr.

The factor
e^{A_{\sigma}\lambda}
arises when continuing the
subdominant LG solution from
X=0
leftward toward the turning
point, while the
e^{C(\sigma)\gamma\sqrt{\delta}}
factor is due to the lower order perturbations in
\lambda^{-1}
in
q_{\lambda}(X)
. We
no longer need to use fine information about the zeros of Stokes coefficients for
this particular ODE: the continuation is controlled throughout by the

dominant
LG mode, whose modulus is insensitive to the Stokes constant relating
the two sides of the turning point (see
Proposition
5.5
).
This is the real reason the method survives the addition
of the harmonic oscillator.
The tangential frequency is
\lambda^{6}
. After multiplying by compactly
supported Gevrey cutoffs, we have the
norms of the data bounded by

\exp\left(A_{\sigma}\lambda+C(\sigma)\gamma\sqrt{\delta}\,\lambda+C_{\rho}\lambda^{6/s}\right).

The term
\lambda^{6/s}
is the source of the Gevrey threshold
6
.

Finally, assuming local solvability in
\gamma^{(s)}
, we pass from the
solvability statement to a localized closed graph estimate on a fixed
Banach space of compactly supported Gevrey functions. Writing

X^{\prime}=(x,y,z),\qquad\chi_{X^{\prime}}(X^{\prime})=\chi_{x}(x)\chi_{y}(y)\chi_{z}(z),

we apply this estimate directly to cutoff test functions

v_{\lambda}(t,X^{\prime})=\chi_{t}(t)\chi_{X^{\prime}}(X^{\prime})V_{\lambda}(t,X^{\prime}).

Since
V_{\lambda}
is an exact solution, we have to estimate the commutator

Qv_{\lambda}=[Q,\chi_{t}\chi_{X^{\prime}}]V_{\lambda}.

This commutator is a first order differential operator acting on
V_{\lambda}
. It generates only polynomial powers of
\lambda
, and the nested choice
of cutoffs prevents the
appearance of
the large positive-time factor

e^{\gamma t_{\ast}\lambda}
. Thus the commutator is controlled by the same
exponential bound as the Cauchy data. On the other hand, the cutoff test function has size

e^{\gamma t_{\ast}\lambda}

at the observation time
t=t_{\ast}
. Comparing the two sides gives

\gamma t_{\ast}\leq A_{\sigma}+C(\sigma)\gamma\sqrt{\delta}+C_{\rho}\lambda^{6/s-1}+o(1).

For
s>6
, the last term tends to zero. We then use the fact that

A_{\sigma}=O(\sigma^{-1})
as
\sigma\to+\infty
,
and finally choose
\delta
small. This gives the desired contradiction.

We have also organized a number of standard results, adapted and tuned to
our setup in the Appendix,
where we have recalled and proven some results on the LG-Airy transition machinery
needed in order to estimate globally the solutions constructed in
Section
2
.

We now briefly recall some classical definitions of the basic spaces and estimates used in the
next sections.

Definition 1.2
.

We say that
f\in\gamma^{(s)}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
, the (Roumieu) Gevrey class of
order
s\geq 1
, if for every compact
K\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
there exist

C>0
,
h>0
such that

\left|\partial_{x}^{\alpha}f(x)\right|\leq Ch^{-\left|\alpha\right|}(\alpha!)^{s},\qquad x\in K,\ \forall\alpha\in\mathbb{N}^{n}.

(1.4)

Definition 1.3
.

We say that the Cauchy problem for a second order operator is locally solvable
in the Gevrey class
\gamma^{(s)}
at the origin,
if
for every datum

(u_{0},u_{1})\in(\gamma^{(s)}(\mathbb{R}^{n}))^{2}
supported sufficiently close to the origin,
there is a unique local solution
u
satisfying

\begin{cases}Qu=0,\\
D_{0}^{j}u(0,X^{\prime})=u_{j}(X^{\prime}),\qquad j=0,1.\end{cases}

In the case of our model
Q
in (
1.3
) we relabel the coordinates
x_{0}=t
, and with
n=3,X^{\prime}=(x,y,z)\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
.

2.1
The coordinates

We choose a set of asymptotic coordinates. We notice how the
eigenfunctions of the harmonic oscillator naturally force us to
choose the scale
(1,2,6)
in the frequency parameter
\lambda
.

Let

\eta=\lambda^{6},\qquad\tau_{\lambda}=\sigma\lambda^{2}-\mathrm{i}\gamma\lambda,\qquad\sigma>0,\quad\gamma>0,\quad\lambda\to+\infty.

(2.1)

Then

e^{\mathrm{i}\tau_{\lambda}t}=e^{\mathrm{i}\sigma\lambda^{2}t}e^{\gamma\lambda t},

(2.2)

which is the future-time growth responsible for the instability.

To take care of the added harmonic oscillator consider the normalized
Hermite eigenfunction

\psi_{\lambda}(z)=\pi^{-1/4}\lambda^{3/2}e^{-\lambda^{6}z^{2}/2}.

(2.3)

Then
\left\|\psi_{\lambda}\right\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R}_{z})}=1
, and

(D_{z}^{2}+\lambda^{12}z^{2})\psi_{\lambda}=\lambda^{6}\psi_{\lambda}.

(2.4)

Indeed
D_{z}^{2}=-\partial_{z}^{2}
, so this is the ground state of the harmonic oscillator

-\partial_{z}^{2}+\lambda^{12}z^{2}
.
We look for exact null solutions in the separated variables form

V_{\lambda}(t,x,y,z)=e^{\mathrm{i}\tau_{\lambda}t}e^{\mathrm{i}\lambda^{6}y}\psi_{\lambda}(z)w_{\lambda}(x).

(2.5)

Substituting into
Qu=0
, and using (
2.4
), gives

\left[-\partial_{x}^{2}+\lambda^{12}x^{3}+2\tau_{\lambda}\lambda^{6}x+\lambda^{6}-\tau_{\lambda}^{2}\right]w_{\lambda}(x)=0.

(2.6)

Now set

x=\lambda^{-2}X,\qquad w_{\lambda}(x)=W_{\lambda}(X).

(2.7)

Since
\partial_{x}=\lambda^{2}\partial_{X}
, division by
\lambda^{4}
gives

\left[-\partial_{X}^{2}+\lambda^{2}V_{\sigma}(X)-2\mathrm{i}\gamma\lambda X-\sigma^{2}+2\mathrm{i}\sigma\gamma\lambda^{-1}+\gamma^{2}\lambda^{-2}\right]W_{\lambda}=0,

(2.8)

where

V_{\sigma}(X)=X^{3}+2\sigma X+1.

(2.9)

Equivalently,

\left[-\partial_{X}^{2}+\lambda^{2}q_{\lambda}(X)\right]W_{\lambda}=0,

(2.10)

with

q_{\lambda}(X)=V_{\sigma}(X)-\frac{2\mathrm{i}\gamma}{\lambda}X-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}+\frac{2\mathrm{i}\sigma\gamma}{\lambda^{3}}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\lambda^{4}}.

(2.11)

The cubic potential

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
\sigma>0

V_{\sigma}(X)=X^{3}+2\sigma X+1

X_{*}=X_{*}(\sigma)<0

V_{\sigma}(X)<0\quad(X<X_{*}),\qquad V_{\sigma}(X)>0\quad(X>X_{*}).

Define

A_{\sigma}=\int_{X_{*}}^{0}\sqrt{V_{\sigma}(r)}\,\,dr.

(2.12)

Then
A_{\sigma}=O(\sigma^{-1})
as
\sigma\to+\infty

Proof.

Since

V_{\sigma}(X)\to+\infty

For the estimate when

Then

At the zero this gives

X_{*}=-\frac{1}{2\sigma}+O(\sigma^{-4}).

(2.13)

On

This proves the lemma. Notice how the presence of the harmonic

which is responsible for the asymptotic behavior of

Theorem 2.2
.

Fix
\sigma>0
,
\gamma>0

\delta>0

Let
W_{\lambda}

\left[-\partial_{X}^{2}+\lambda^{2}q_{\lambda}(X)\right]W_{\lambda}(X)=0

(2.15)

at

W_{\lambda}(X)=q_{\lambda}(X)^{-1/4}\exp\left(-\lambda\int_{0}^{X}q_{\lambda}(r)^{1/2}\,dr\right)\left(1+O(\lambda^{-1})\right)

(2.16)

locally uniformly for
X\geq 0
, with the branch chosen so that

q_{\lambda}(0)^{1/2}=1+O(\lambda^{-2}).

w_{\lambda}(x):=W_{\lambda}(\lambda^{2}x).

Then there are constants

,
M>0
,

, and
\lambda_{0}

\sigma,\gamma,\delta

for all

\left|\partial_{x}^{k}w_{\lambda}(x)\right|\leq C^{k+1}k!\,\lambda^{6k+M}\exp\left(\lambda A_{\sigma}+C(\sigma)\gamma\sqrt{\delta}\,\lambda\right)

(2.18)

for

\left|W_{\lambda}(X)\right|\geq\frac{1}{2}\qquad\text{for }|X|\leq c_{0}\lambda^{-1},

(2.20)

Proof of Theorem
2.2
.

The standard Airy estimates and the
Liouville–Green estimates ( see Chapter 6 in
[
12
]

5

Throughout the proof,
M
denotes a polynomial-loss exponent depending only on

\sigma,\gamma,\delta
, but not on
\lambda
or
k

from line to line as harmless polynomial powers of
\lambda
are absorbed.

The function
q_{\lambda}
is an analytic perturbation of
V_{\sigma}
. Since

V_{\sigma}^{\prime}(X_{\ast})\neq 0
, the implicit function theorem gives a unique simple
zero
X_{\lambda}
of
q_{\lambda}
near
X_{\ast}
. Moreover,

X_{\lambda}=X_{\ast}+O(\lambda^{-1}),\qquad q_{\lambda}^{\prime}(X_{\lambda})=V_{\sigma}^{\prime}(X_{\ast})+O(\lambda^{-1})\neq 0.

(2.21)

Thus the reduced equation has a simple complex turning point close to
the real one.
It is also easy to check that
\Im X_{\lambda}<0
, as proven in
this Lemma.

Lemma 2.3
.

Let
X_{\ast}=X_{\ast}(\sigma)<0
be the unique real zero of

V_{\sigma}(X)=X^{3}+2\sigma X+1.

Let
X_{\lambda}
be the unique zero of
q_{\lambda}
near
X_{\ast}
, where

q_{\lambda}(X)=V_{\sigma}(X)-\frac{2i\gamma}{\lambda}X-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}+\frac{2i\sigma\gamma}{\lambda^{3}}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\lambda^{4}}.

Then, as
\lambda\to+\infty
,

X_{\lambda}=X_{\ast}-i\,\frac{2\gamma|X_{\ast}|}{3X_{\ast}^{2}+2\sigma}\,\lambda^{-1}+O(\lambda^{-2}).

In particular,

\Im X_{\lambda}=-\frac{2\gamma|X_{\ast}|}{3X_{\ast}^{2}+2\sigma}\,\lambda^{-1}+O(\lambda^{-2})<0

for all sufficiently large
\lambda
. Thus the perturbed turning point lies
slightly below the real axis.

Proof.

Put
\varepsilon=\lambda^{-1}
and write

q_{\lambda}(X)=Q(X,\varepsilon),

where

Q(X,\varepsilon)=V_{\sigma}(X)-2i\gamma\varepsilon X-\sigma^{2}\varepsilon^{2}+2i\sigma\gamma\varepsilon^{3}+\gamma^{2}\varepsilon^{4}.

Since

Q(X_{\ast},0)=0,\qquad\partial_{X}Q(X_{\ast},0)=V_{\sigma}^{\prime}(X_{\ast})=3X_{\ast}^{2}+2\sigma>0,

the holomorphic implicit function theorem gives a unique zero

X_{\lambda}=X(\varepsilon)

near
X_{\ast}
, analytic in
\varepsilon
. Write

X(\varepsilon)=X_{\ast}+\varepsilon X_{1}+O(\varepsilon^{2}).

Substituting into
Q(X(\varepsilon),\varepsilon)=0
and comparing the
coefficient of
\varepsilon
, we find

V_{\sigma}^{\prime}(X_{\ast})X_{1}-2i\gamma X_{\ast}=0.

Therefore

X_{1}=\frac{2i\gamma X_{\ast}}{V_{\sigma}^{\prime}(X_{\ast})}=-i\,\frac{2\gamma|X_{\ast}|}{3X_{\ast}^{2}+2\sigma}.

This gives the claimed expansion.

A Gevrey non-stationary phase estimate

We now need an extension of the classical preliminary theorems on the stationary

To convert the previously obtained derivative bounds into a Gevrey estimate for the
Cauchy data in suitable Banach spaces we use the following localization lemma. The role of
the large Fourier frequency parameter is played by

\Lambda=\lambda^{6}.

Also define

H_{\Lambda}=A_{\sigma}\lambda+C(\sigma)\gamma\sqrt{\delta}\,\lambda.

We state the result with a generic Gevrey index
s_{0}>1
, since in the
application we use
s_{0}=s^{\prime}<s
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
1<s_{0}<\infty
, let
I\Subset\mathbb{R}
, and let

F_{\Lambda}(x)=\sum_{\nu=1}^{N_{0}}A_{\Lambda,\nu}(x)\exp\!\bigl(B_{\Lambda,\nu}(x)\bigr)\exp\!\bigl(i\Theta_{\Lambda,\nu}(x)\bigr),\qquad\Lambda\geq 1,

on
I
, where
N_{0}
is fixed. Assume that
B_{\Lambda,\nu}
and

\Theta_{\Lambda,\nu}
are real-valued, that there is a
frequency
scale

\mu=\mu_{\Lambda}
with

1\leq\mu\leq C_{0}\Lambda,

that the phase gradient satisfies

|\Theta^{\prime}_{\Lambda,\nu}(x)|\leq C_{0}\Lambda,\qquad x\in I,

and that the amplitudes and phases obey the symbol-type Gevrey bounds

|\partial_{x}^{k}A_{\Lambda,\nu}(x)|\leq C^{k+1}(k!)^{s_{0}}\mu^{k}\Lambda^{M},\qquad k\geq 0,

and

|\partial_{x}^{k}B_{\Lambda,\nu}(x)|+|\partial_{x}^{k}\Theta_{\Lambda,\nu}(x)|\leq C^{k+1}(k!)^{s_{0}}\mu^{k},\qquad k\geq 1.

Assume moreover that

\sup_{I}B_{\Lambda,\nu}\leq H_{\Lambda}.

Then, for every
\chi\in\gamma^{(s_{0})}_{0}(I)
, there exist constants

C_{1}>C_{0}
,
C>0
,
a>0
, and
M^{\prime}>0
, independent of
\Lambda
, such that

|\widehat{\chi F_{\Lambda}}(\xi)|\leq C\Lambda^{M^{\prime}}e^{H_{\Lambda}}\exp\!\left(-a\,\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\xi,[-C_{1}\Lambda,C_{1}\Lambda]\bigr)^{1/s_{0}}\right).

Proof.

It is enough to prove the estimate for one term of the finite sum; the
finite number of terms is absorbed into the constants. We therefore write

A_{\Lambda}=A_{\Lambda,\nu},\qquad B_{\Lambda}=B_{\Lambda,\nu},\qquad\Theta_{\Lambda}=\Theta_{\Lambda,\nu}.

Let

g_{\Lambda}(x)=\chi(x)A_{\Lambda}(x).

Since
\chi\in\gamma^{(s_{0})}_{0}(I)
and
A_{\Lambda}
satisfies the
symbol-type Gevrey bounds above, the product
g_{\Lambda}
satisfies

|\partial_{x}^{k}g_{\Lambda}(x)|\leq C^{k+1}(k!)^{s_{0}}\mu^{k}\Lambda^{M},\qquad k\geq 0,

where we used
\mu\geq 1
to absorb the derivatives of
\chi
.

We study

I_{\Lambda}(\xi)=\int e^{B_{\Lambda}(x)}e^{i\Theta_{\Lambda}(x)}e^{-ix\xi}g_{\Lambda}(x)\,dx.

Let

\Psi_{\Lambda,\xi}(x)=B_{\Lambda}(x)+i\Theta_{\Lambda}(x)-ix\xi.

be the complex phase.

Then

\Psi^{\prime}_{\Lambda,\xi}(x)=B^{\prime}_{\Lambda}(x)+i\bigl(\Theta^{\prime}_{\Lambda}(x)-\xi\bigr).

Because
B_{\Lambda}
is real-valued, we have

|\Psi^{\prime}_{\Lambda,\xi}(x)|\geq|\Theta^{\prime}_{\Lambda}(x)-\xi|.

Choose
C_{1}>C_{0}+1
. If

|\xi|\leq C_{1}\Lambda,

then the desired estimate follows from the trivial bound

|I_{\Lambda}(\xi)|\leq\int_{I}e^{B_{\Lambda}(x)}|g_{\Lambda}(x)|\,dx\leq C\Lambda^{M}e^{H_{\Lambda}},

because the distance term is zero.

We may therefore assume that

|\xi|>C_{1}\Lambda.

Set

d=d(\xi):=\operatorname{dist}\bigl(\xi,[-C_{1}\Lambda,C_{1}\Lambda]\bigr).

Since

|\Theta^{\prime}_{\Lambda}(x)|\leq C_{0}\Lambda,

we have, for every
x\in I
,

|\Theta^{\prime}_{\Lambda}(x)-\xi|\geq d+(C_{1}-C_{0})\Lambda.

Put

D:=d+(C_{1}-C_{0})\Lambda.

Then

|\Psi^{\prime}_{\Lambda,\xi}(x)|\geq D,\qquad D\geq c(\Lambda+d).

Define the operator

L=\frac{1}{\Psi^{\prime}_{\Lambda,\xi}(x)}\partial_{x}.

to be used in iterated integration by parts. Then

Le^{\Psi_{\Lambda,\xi}(x)}=e^{\Psi_{\Lambda,\xi}(x)}.

Since
g_{\Lambda}
is compactly supported in
I
, integration by parts gives

I_{\Lambda}(\xi)=\int e^{\Psi_{\Lambda,\xi}(x)}(L^{*})^{N}g_{\Lambda}(x)\,dx

for every
N\in\mathbb{N}
.

We now estimate
(L^{*})^{N}g_{\Lambda}
. Since

\Psi^{\prime}_{\Lambda,\xi}=B^{\prime}_{\Lambda}+i(\Theta^{\prime}_{\Lambda}-\xi),

the symbol-type bounds on
B_{\Lambda}
and
\Theta_{\Lambda}
imply, for
k\geq 1
,

|\partial_{x}^{k}\Psi^{\prime}_{\Lambda,\xi}(x)|\leq C^{k+1}(k!)^{s_{0}}\mu^{k}.

Together with
|\Psi^{\prime}_{\Lambda,\xi}|\geq D
,
D\geq c\Lambda
, and

\mu\leq C_{0}\Lambda\leq CD
, the standard differentiation formula for the
reciprocal gives

\left|\partial_{x}^{k}\left(\frac{1}{\Psi^{\prime}_{\Lambda,\xi}}\right)\right|\leq C^{k+1}(k!)^{s_{0}}D^{-1}\left(\frac{\mu}{D}\right)^{k}\leq C^{k+1}(k!)^{s_{0}}D^{-1},\qquad k\geq 0.

Indeed each derivative either differentiates
\Psi^{\prime}_{\Lambda,\xi}
, producing
a factor
O(\mu)
, or produces another denominator; since

\mu/D\leq C
, all such factors are absorbed into the constant.

The operator
L^{*}
has the form

L^{*}h=-\partial_{x}\left(\frac{h}{\Psi^{\prime}_{\Lambda,\xi}}\right).

Using the preceding bounds, the Gevrey bounds for
g_{\Lambda}
, and induction on

N
, we obtain

|(L^{*})^{N}g_{\Lambda}(x)|\leq C^{N+1}(N!)^{s_{0}}\Lambda^{M}D^{-N},

the powers
\mu^{N}
produced by the derivatives being absorbed through

\mu/D\leq C
.
Therefore

|I_{\Lambda}(\xi)|\leq C^{N+1}(N!)^{s_{0}}\Lambda^{M}e^{H_{\Lambda}}D^{-N}.

It remains to optimize in
N
. By Stirling’s formula,

(N!)^{s_{0}}\leq CN^{s_{0}N}e^{-s_{0}N}.

Choose

N=\left\lfloor\varepsilon D^{1/s_{0}}\right\rfloor

with
\varepsilon>0
sufficiently small. Then

C^{N+1}(N!)^{s_{0}}D^{-N}\leq C\exp(-aD^{1/s_{0}})

for some
a>0
. Hence

|I_{\Lambda}(\xi)|\leq C\Lambda^{M}e^{H_{\Lambda}}\exp(-aD^{1/s_{0}}).

Since
D\geq d
, we conclude

|I_{\Lambda}(\xi)|\leq C\Lambda^{M}e^{H_{\Lambda}}\exp\!\left(-a\,d(\xi)^{1/s_{0}}\right).

This proves the estimate for one term. Summing over the fixed finite number of
terms only changes the constants, and the lemma follows.

\displaystyle\leq C\lambda^{M}\exp\left(A_{\sigma}\lambda+C(\sigma)\gamma\sqrt{\delta}\,\lambda+C_{\rho}\lambda^{6/s}\right).

Proof.

Since
\chi_{t}\equiv 1
on a neighborhood of
t=0
, we have

v_{\lambda}(0,\cdot)=\chi_{X^{\prime}}V_{\lambda}(0,\cdot)
and

D_{t}v_{\lambda}(0,\cdot)=\chi_{X^{\prime}}D_{t}V_{\lambda}(0,\cdot)
. These are exactly the
cut-off Cauchy data
(g_{0,\lambda},g_{1,\lambda})
of
(

), with the product cutoff
\chi=\chi_{X^{\prime}}
and the
compact
K=K_{2}
. The bound (

∎

Lemma 4.6
.

With the nested cutoffs chosen above, after increasing
C,M
if necessary,

\left\|Qv_{\lambda}\right\|_{C^{m}((-2t_{\ast},2t_{\ast})\times K_{1})}\leq C\lambda^{M}\exp\left(A_{\sigma}\lambda+C(\sigma)\gamma\sqrt{\delta}\,\lambda+C_{\rho}\lambda^{6/s}\right).

(4.5)

Proof.

We write

\chi=\chi_{t}\chi_{X^{\prime}},\qquad v_{\lambda}=\chi V_{\lambda}.

Since
V_{\lambda}
is an exact solution of the model equation,

QV_{\lambda}=0,

we have

Qv_{\lambda}=Q(\chi V_{\lambda})=[Q,\chi]V_{\lambda}.

Thus the commutator is produced by derivatives of the fixed cutoffs. By
construction, all spatial derivatives of
\chi_{X^{\prime}}
vanish on
K_{1}
. Hence,
inside the cylinder
I_{*}\times K_{1}
, the only nonzero part of the commutator is
the time commutator, whose support is contained in

[-2\varepsilon_{t},-\varepsilon_{t}]\times K_{1}
. In particular, on the
support of the commutator inside
I_{*}\times K_{1}
we have

|e^{i\tau_{\lambda}t}|=e^{\gamma\lambda t}\leq 1\leq\exp(C(\sigma)\gamma\sqrt{\delta}\,\lambda).

Recall that

Q=-D_{t}^{2}+2xD_{t}D_{y}+D_{x}^{2}+x^{3}D_{y}^{2}+D_{z}^{2}+z^{2}D_{y}^{2}.

Since
Q
is second order, the commutator
[Q,\chi]
is a differential
operator of order at most one. More explicitly, it is a finite sum of terms of
the form

a_{\alpha,\lambda}(t,x,y,z)D^{\alpha}V_{\lambda},\qquad|\alpha|\leq 1,

plus zero order terms. The coefficients
a_{\alpha,\lambda}
are smooth,
compactly supported, and depend only on the fixed cutoffs and on the polynomial
coefficients
x,x^{3},z^{2}
of
Q
. On the fixed compact set
K_{1}
, all these
coefficients are uniformly bounded, together with all derivatives. Therefore it
is enough to estimate
D^{\alpha}V_{\lambda}
for
|\alpha|\leq 1
on the support
of the commutator.

The exact separated solution has the form

V_{\lambda}(t,x,y,z)=e^{i\tau_{\lambda}t}e^{i\lambda^{6}y}\psi_{\lambda}(z)W_{\lambda}(\lambda^{2}x),

where

\tau_{\lambda}=\sigma\lambda^{2}-i\gamma\lambda,\qquad\psi_{\lambda}(z)=\left(\frac{\lambda^{6}}{\pi}\right)^{1/4}e^{-\lambda^{6}z^{2}/2}.

Hence

|e^{i\tau_{\lambda}t}|=e^{\gamma\lambda t}.

The preceding support observation is the only place where the time
growth of the exact separated solution enters the estimate of the commutators.

We now record the polynomial losses coming from the derivatives. First,

D_{t}V_{\lambda}=\tau_{\lambda}V_{\lambda},\qquad|\tau_{\lambda}|\leq C\lambda^{2}.

Second,

D_{y}V_{\lambda}=\lambda^{6}V_{\lambda}.

Third,

D_{x}V_{\lambda}=e^{i\tau_{\lambda}t}e^{i\lambda^{6}y}\psi_{\lambda}(z)\partial_{x}\bigl(W_{\lambda}(\lambda^{2}x)\bigr),

and Theorem
2.2
gives

\left|\partial_{x}W_{\lambda}(\lambda^{2}x)\right|\leq C\lambda^{M}\exp\left(A_{\sigma}\lambda+C(\sigma)\gamma\sqrt{\delta}\,\lambda\right),

after increasing
M
. More generally, any derivative w.r.t.
x
which appears in
the commutator is controlled by the same bound, with only a polynomial increase
in
\lambda
.

Finally, for the Gaussian factor,

\partial_{z}\psi_{\lambda}(z)=-\lambda^{6}z\,\psi_{\lambda}(z).

As an
L^{2}_{z}
-estimate this costs only the harmonic-oscillator scale:

\|\partial_{z}\psi_{\lambda}\|_{L^{2}_{z}}\leq C\lambda^{3}\|\psi_{\lambda}\|_{L^{2}_{z}}.

Equivalently, on fixed compact
z
-sets one may use the elementary bound

|\partial_{z}\psi_{\lambda}(z)|\leq C\lambda^{3}\lambda^{3/2}e^{-c\lambda^{6}z^{2}},

which is again only polynomial after integration.

Combining all these estimates, every term in
[Q,\chi]V_{\lambda}
is bounded in

L^{2}((-2t_{\ast},2t_{\ast})\times K_{1})
by

C\lambda^{M}\exp\left(A_{\sigma}\lambda+C(\sigma)\gamma\sqrt{\delta}\,\lambda\right).

The possible powers
\lambda^{2}
,
\lambda^{3}
, and
\lambda^{6}
coming from

D_{t}
,
D_{z}
, and
D_{y}
are absorbed into the single polynomial factor

\lambda^{M}
.

The
C^{m}
-norm in Proposition
4.3
requires finitely many
additional derivatives of these commutator terms. Each such derivative can fall on a
fixed cutoff, on the polynomial coefficients of
Q
, or on one of the separated factors
of
V_{\lambda}
. The cutoffs and polynomial coefficients contribute constants depending
on
m
,
K_{1}
, and
\delta
, while derivatives of the separated factors contribute
only polynomial powers of
\lambda
. The exponential factor is unchanged, because the
time-commutator support remains in
t<0
. Hence

\left\|Qv_{\lambda}\right\|_{C^{m}((-2t_{*},2t_{*})\times K_{1})}\leq C\lambda^{M}\exp\left(A_{\sigma}\lambda+C(\sigma)\gamma\sqrt{\delta}\,\lambda\right).

Finally, the factor
\exp(C_{\rho}\lambda^{6/s})
is larger than one and may be inserted
without changing the estimate. This gives (
4.5
).
∎

4.4
The contradiction for

We now collect the previous estimates and finally conclude.

Assume, for contradiction, that the localized closed graph estimate

Hence we choose

(4.6)

Next fix, for instance,

. With this

and

all constants in the ODE estimates are fixed. Choose

so small that

5
Appendix

The results collected here are standard consequences of the
Liouville–Green method away from simple turning points and of the
classical Airy normal form near a simple turning point. They can be
found for instance in Chapters 6, 11 and 13 of
[
12
]
.

We have included the
proofs only to make explicit their uniformity in
\lambda
and the derivative
losses on the expanding interval
|X|\leq\delta\lambda^{2}
.

We study the equation

-Y^{\prime\prime}(X)+\lambda^{2}q(X)Y(X)=0,

(5.1)

with the proviso that the intended application is to a
\lambda
-dependent analytic family

q=q_{\lambda}.

More precisely, in the proof of Theorem
2.2

we use

q_{\lambda}(X)=V_{\sigma}(X)-\frac{2i\gamma}{\lambda}X-\frac{\sigma^{2}}{\lambda^{2}}+\frac{2i\sigma\gamma}{\lambda^{3}}+\frac{\gamma^{2}}{\lambda^{4}},\qquad V_{\sigma}(X)=X^{3}+2\sigma X+1.

For fixed
\sigma>0
and
\gamma>0
, this is a small analytic
perturbation of
V_{\sigma}
on every fixed complex neighborhood of the
real turning point. Since
V_{\sigma}
has a simple zero
X_{*}=X_{*}(\sigma)
,
the implicit function theorem gives a unique simple zero

X_{\lambda}=X_{*}+O(\lambda^{-1})

of
q_{\lambda}
near
X_{*}
, and

q_{\lambda}^{\prime}(X_{\lambda})=V_{\sigma}^{\prime}(X_{*})+O(\lambda^{-1})\neq 0

for all sufficiently large
\lambda
. Hence all the tools used
below, the Airy coordinate,
the Liouville–Green amplitudes, and the error estimates below
are uniform in
\lambda
, after increasing
\lambda_{0}
if necessary.

Thus every statement below for
q
should be read as a uniform statement
for
q_{\lambda}
, provided the following hypotheses hold:

(i)

q_{\lambda}
is analytic in the chosen complex neighborhood;

(ii)

q_{\lambda}
has exactly one simple zero
X_{\lambda}
in that
neighborhood;

(iii)

q_{\lambda}^{\prime}(X_{\lambda})
is bounded away from
0
;

(iv)

q_{\lambda}\to V_{\sigma}
analytically as
\lambda\to+\infty
,

all of which are satisfied in our case.

The two relevant regimes therefore are:

(1)

away from
X_{\lambda}
, where
q_{\lambda}\neq 0
, and ordinary
Liouville–Green estimates apply uniformly;

(2)

near
X_{\lambda}
, where the equation is reduced to Airy form by
a uniform Liouville transformation.

5.1
Away from the turning point

Proposition 5.1
.

Let
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval and suppose that
q
is analytic in a complex
neighborhood of
I
, with
|q|\geq c_{0}>0
there. Choose a branch of
q^{1/2}
, and
put

S(X)=\int_{X_{0}}^{X}q(r)^{1/2}\,\,dr.

Then solutions of (
5.1
) admit a representation by two LG modes

Y_{\pm}(X)=q(X)^{-1/4}e^{\pm\lambda S(X)}b_{\pm}(X,\lambda),

(5.2)

where, on compact subintervals of
I
,

\left|\partial_{X}^{k}b_{\pm}(X,\lambda)\right|\leq C^{k+1}k!

(5.3)

for
\lambda\geq\lambda_{0}
. Moreover, along any subinterval
[a,b]\subset I
, the
fundamental matrix grows at most like

C\lambda^{M}\exp\left(\lambda\int_{a}^{b}\left|\Re q(r)^{1/2}\right|\,\,dr\right).

(5.4)

Derivative estimates satisfy the rule that one
X
-derivative costs at most

C\lambda|q(X)|^{1/2}
, up to analytic Cauchy factors.

Proof.

We give the standard proof. Introduce the Liouville variable (see, for instance,
[
12
]
).

s=S(X),\qquad\frac{\,ds}{\,dX}=q(X)^{1/2},

and write

Y(X)=q(X)^{-1/4}U(s).

A direct computation gives

\frac{\,d^{2}U}{\,ds^{2}}=\lambda^{2}U+R(s)U,

(5.5)

where

R(s)=\frac{5}{16}\frac{(q^{\prime})^{2}}{q^{3}}-\frac{1}{4}\frac{q^{\prime\prime}}{q^{2}},

(5.6)

with the right-hand side evaluated at
X=X(s)
. Since
q
has no zeros in the
chosen complex neighborhood,
R
is analytic and bounded on the corresponding

s
-domain.

For the plus branch, write

U(s)=e^{\lambda s}B(s).

Then (
5.5
) becomes

B^{\prime\prime}+2\lambda B^{\prime}=R(s)B.

(5.7)

Solving this by variation of constants from a base point
s_{0}
, with normalization

B(s_{0})=1
, gives the equation

B(s)=1+\int_{s_{0}}^{s}\frac{1-e^{-2\lambda(s-t)}}{2\lambda}R(t)B(t)\,\,dt.

(5.8)

The path is chosen inside the analytic neighborhood and along a direction for which the
kernel remains bounded; on real intervals this is obtained by subdividing into finitely
many monotone pieces for
\Re s
. Since the kernel is
O(\lambda^{-1})
, the Volterra
operator is a contraction for
\lambda
large, after subdivision if necessary. Hence

B=1+O(\lambda^{-1})
, and all derivatives of
B
are bounded by Cauchy’s formula.
This gives (
5.2
) and (
5.3
). The same argument with

e^{-\lambda s}
gives the minus branch.

The growth estimate (
5.4
) follows from the exponential factors

e^{\pm\lambda S}
and the fact that the amplitudes are bounded, with a polynomial
factor allowing for finite subdivision and changes of normalization. The derivative rule
follows by differentiating (
5.2
); the derivative of the exponential gives

\lambda q^{1/2}
, and derivatives of the analytic amplitudes are controlled by Cauchy
inequalities.
∎

5.2
Near the simple turning point

Proposition 5.2
.

Let
q
be analytic in a disk
D\subset\mathbb{C}
, and assume that
q
has a unique simple
zero
X_{0}\in D
, so that
q(X_{0})=0
,

\zeta=\zeta(X)

\zeta(X_{0})=0

\zeta^{\prime}(X_{0})\neq 0

\frac{2}{3}\zeta(X)^{3/2}=\int_{X_{0}}^{X}q(r)^{1/2}\,\,dr.

(5.9)

In this coordinate, every solution of (

Y(X)=\left(\zeta^{\prime}(X)\right)^{-1/2}W(\zeta(X)),

(5.10)

where

W^{\prime\prime}(\zeta)=\lambda^{2}\zeta W(\zeta)+r(\zeta)W(\zeta),

(5.11)

with

analytic and bounded. There is a solution corresponding to the Airy branch

\operatorname{Ai}

(5.12)

where
\mathcal{A}
has the same sectorial size as
\operatorname{Ai}
. The estimate is uniform in
closed Airy sectors and remains valid after differentiating a fixed number of times, with
polynomial losses in
\lambda

only polynomial powers of
\lambda
, not a new exponential factor.

Proof.

Again we follow
[
12
]
. First define
\zeta
. Since
q(X)=(X-X_{0})g(X)
, with
g(X_{0})=q^{\prime}(X_{0})\neq 0
, we have

\int_{X_{0}}^{X}q(r)^{1/2}\,\,dr=\frac{2}{3}g(X_{0})^{1/2}(X-X_{0})^{3/2}(1+O(X-X_{0})).

Thus (
5.9
) defines an analytic coordinate
\zeta
near
X_{0}
, after
choosing a branch. It satisfies

(\zeta^{\prime}(X))^{2}\zeta(X)=q(X).

(5.13)

Set

Y(X)=\left(\zeta^{\prime}(X)\right)^{-1/2}W(\zeta(X)).

A direct calculation, using (
5.13

-Y^{\prime\prime}+\lambda^{2}qY=-(\zeta^{\prime})^{3/2}\left(W^{\prime\prime}-\lambda^{2}\zeta W-r(\zeta)W\right),

where

r(\zeta)=\frac{1}{2}\{X,\zeta\},

(5.14)

up to the conventional sign, and
\{X,\zeta\}
is the Schwarzian derivative. Since

\zeta
is an analytic coordinate,

is analytic and bounded after shrinking the

We now solve (

) by variation of constants around the Airy equation

W^{\prime\prime}=\lambda^{2}\zeta W.

Let

A(\zeta)=\operatorname{Ai}(\lambda^{2/3}\zeta),\qquad B(\zeta)=\operatorname{Bi}(\lambda^{2/3}\zeta),

in an Airy sector where these functions form a fundamental system. Their Wronskian in

\zeta
is

W_{\zeta}(A,B)=\lambda^{2/3}\pi^{-1}.

The Green kernel for the Airy operator is therefore

G_{\lambda}(\zeta,\omega)=\pi\lambda^{-2/3}\big(A(\zeta)B(\omega)-B(\zeta)A(\omega)\big).

(5.15)

The Airy branch solution satisfies the equation

W(\zeta)=A(\zeta)+\int_{\Gamma(\zeta)}G_{\lambda}(\zeta,\omega)r(\omega)W(\omega)\,\,d\omega,

(5.16)

where
\Gamma(\zeta)
is an Airy admissible path ending at
\zeta

decays along

Standard Airy estimates give

\left|G_{\lambda}(\zeta,\omega)\right|\leq C\lambda^{-1}\,\frac{\mathcal{A}(\lambda^{2/3}\zeta)}{\mathcal{A}(\lambda^{2/3}\omega)}

(5.17)

The final statement follows from the standard asymptotics of

\lambda

5.4
Derivative estimates on expanding intervals

The preceding results are local in the complex domain. However in Theorem

2.2
, the interval in the
X
-variable expands as

|X|\leq\delta\lambda^{2}
. We explain why the derivative bound used
there is still valid.

Lemma 5.6
.

On
|X|\leq\delta\lambda^{2}
, solutions controlled by the LG/Airy estimates above satisfy

\left|\partial_{X}^{k}Y(X)\right|\leq C^{k+1}k!\left(\lambda\langle X\rangle^{3/2}\right)^{k}\sup_{\widetilde{X}\in\mathcal{N}_{X}}|Y(\widetilde{X})|,

where
\mathcal{N}_{X}
is a complex neighborhood of radius comparable to

(\lambda\langle X\rangle^{3/2})^{-1}
. Near the turning point, the factor

\lambda\langle X\rangle^{3/2}
may be replaced by
C\lambda
, which is larger than the Airy
scale
\lambda^{2/3}
.

Proof.

Away from the turning point, LG modes have the form

a(X,\lambda)e^{\pm\lambda\int^{X}q^{1/2}}.

On the expanding interval,
|q(X)|^{1/2}\leq C\langle X\rangle^{3/2}
. Therefore the exponential
varies on the local length scale
(\lambda\langle X\rangle^{3/2})^{-1}
. The amplitudes are
analytic on a comparable complex scale because the only singularity of the LG
representation is the turning point, which has been excluded in this part of the
argument. Cauchy’s inequalities on that disk give the asserted bound.

In the turning point region, Proposition
5.2
gives an Airy
representation. Differentiating
\operatorname{Ai}(\lambda^{2/3}\zeta(X))
produces

O(\lambda^{2/3})
per derivative, times fixed analytic factors. Since

X
remains in a fixed compact neighborhood of
X_{*}
, this is bounded by

C\lambda\langle X\rangle^{3/2}
after increasing
C
. This proves the lemma.
∎
