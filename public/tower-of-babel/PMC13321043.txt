Coronary plaque beyond calcium: advancing precision risk assessment in South Asians
Authors: Izza Shahid, Bashir Hanif, Khurram Nasir
Published: 2026

South Asians face a disproportionately high burden of premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) compared with other ethnic groups, reflecting earlier cardiometabolic disease, adverse lipid and adiposity phenotypes, and risk that is not fully captured by traditional risk factors [1]. However, despite growing recognition of this excess risk, accurately identifying which South Asian individuals are most likely to develop early coronary atherosclerosis remains a clinical challenge. This persistent mismatch between predicted and observed risk is why contemporary prevention guidelines identify South Asian ancestry as a risk-enhancing factor when considering lipid-lowering therapy [2]. The 2026 ACC/AHA/Multi-society dyslipidemia guidelines also emphasize personalization of risk using risk enhancers, selective coronary artery calcium (CAC) testing, and biomarkers such as Lp(a) and apoB when treatment uncertainty remains [2].

Within this context, coronary imaging has become central to the conversation. CAC scoring provides a simple, low-cost, reproducible measure of calcified coronary atherosclerosis [3]. A CAC score of 0 indicates low short-term risk of an ASCVD event and may inform shared decision-making regarding the initiation or intensification of preventive therapies [4]. However, CAC scoring is unable to detect non-calcified plaque, low-attenuation plaque, or characterize high-risk plaque phenotypes that may be more common earlier in the disease course, especially among younger individuals. Prior evidence from MASALA and DILWALE studies have shown higher earlier CAC development and higher CAC prevalence among South Asian men and women compared with other racial/ethnic groups [5,6]. But uncertainty persists about what lies beneath CAC, particularly whether South Asians harbor a meaningful burden of non-calcified plaque even when CAC is absent.

In this issue, Satish et al. provide timely data from the DILWALE CTA study, one of the largest U.S.-based CTA cohorts evaluating coronary plaque characteristics among South Asians [7]. The investigators analyzed 341 clinically indicated coronary CT angiograms (CCTA) from the Baylor Scott & White DILWALE registry using AI-enabled quantitative coronary plaque analysis [7]. The cohort was predominantly middle-aged and male, with a high prevalence of cardiometabolic risk factors. Overall, 63% of individuals had detectable coronary plaque, and non-calcified plaque was the dominant plaque subtype, accounting for approximately 86% of plaque composition. Notably, plaque was not confined to older adults: AI-enabled analysis identified plaque in nearly half of individuals aged 40–54 years and in 15% of those younger than 40 years after applying a threshold to reduce false positives [7]. Men developed plaque earlier and had higher plaque burden across most age strata, whereas women demonstrated a marked increase in total plaque volume after age 65 [7].

Importantly, the DILWALE CTA study observed that 10% of individuals with CAC score of zero had detectable plaque on CCTA [7]. The presence of plaque despite CAC=0 suggests that a subset of South Asian adults may harbor earlier, predominantly non-calcified atherosclerosis that is not captured by calcium scoring alone. Notably, this finding is consistent with emerging CCTA data in South Asians. In a matched cohort study by Manubolu et al., South Asian adults had greater total plaque burden and higher non-calcified plaque percent atheroma volume compared with non-Hispanic White adults, with differences most pronounced among younger participants, supporting the concept that plaque may develop earlier and with distinct composition in this population [8]. At the same time, the clinical implications of detecting non-calcified plaque in CAC=0 individuals require caution. In the population-based SCAPIS analysis, CCTA-derived measures modestly improved prediction of first coronary events beyond traditional risk factors and CAC, including improved discrimination and reclassification (NRI 0.133); however, absolute event rates were low, with only 304 events occurring among 24,791 participants over ∼8 years, and only 17 individuals classified as high risk after addition of CCTA-derived data [9]. These findings are consistent with prior data suggesting that while CCTA can enhance anatomic risk characterization, its incremental prognostic value beyond CAC may be modest and context dependent. In CONFIRM, for instance, plaque characteristics such as non-calcified plaque did not significantly improve discrimination beyond traditional risk factors and CAC, with a reported C-statistic of 0.721 (p = 0.286) [10].

The DILWALE CTA findings refine how coronary imaging may be interpreted for ASCVD risk assessment in South Asians. While CAC=0 in reassuring, in select South Asian individuals, particularly younger patients or those with diabetes, strong family history, elevated Lp(a), persistent LDL-C exposure, or symptoms, absence of calcification may not fully exclude clinically relevant early atherosclerosis. The increased prevalence of non-calcified plaque at younger ages may reflect an earlier and potentially more accelerated atherosclerotic phenotype in South Asians, consistent with prior CCTA studies showing ethnic differences in coronary plaque composition [11]. Accordingly, the key clinical question is not whether CTA should replace CAC as a broad screening strategy, but when South Asian-specific risk, symptoms, or risk-enhancing features should prompt escalation beyond CAC to CTA-based plaque assessment.

Addressing these questions requires prospective, population-based data with standardized imaging, longitudinal follow-up, and adjudicated clinical outcomes. In this context, the PAK-SEHAT (PAKistan Study of prEmature coronary atHerosclerosis in young AdulTs) study represents an important next step [12]. Whereas DILWALE provides valuable real-world insights from clinically indicated CTAs, PAK-SEHAT is a nationwide prospective cohort of 2000 individuals from Pakistan without baseline ASCVD [12]. By integrating serial CCTA, biomarker profiling, genomic data, carotid imaging, and adjudicated ASCVD outcomes, PAK-SEHAT may help clarify which plaque phenotypes progress, which predict clinical events, and which clinical, metabolic, genetic, or imaging features identify South Asian individuals who may benefit from escalation beyond CAC-based risk assessment.

Ultimately, these findings support a more nuanced approach to coronary imaging in South Asian adults. The DILWALE CTA study highlights that South Asian individuals may warrant additional plaque characterization beyond calcium scoring, particularly those at higher risk for premature ASCVD. The predominance of non-calcified plaque also raises important questions about whether South Asians may express atherosclerosis earlier in the disease course through plaque phenotypes not fully captured by calcification alone. Clinically, this has implications for patient counselling: CAC=0 remains reassuring, but should be interpreted alongside symptoms, family history, lifetime LDL-C exposure, diabetes, Lp(a), and other risk-enhancing factors. More broadly, these observations support a shift from generalized risk estimation toward more individualized, ethnicity-informed prevention strategies. Future studies are needed to determine how these imaging findings can be translated into targeted prevention strategies that meaningfully reduce premature ASCVD risk in South Asian populations.
