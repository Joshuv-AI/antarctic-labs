Non clinic-Based Interventions to Mitigate Post-Intensive Care Syndrome: A Scoping Review
Authors: Yasmeen Abu Fraiha, James Devanney, Megan McNichol, Thomas Cummiskey, Abirami Kumaresan, Somnath Bose
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVES:Survivors of critical illness and their caregivers often experience a complex constellation of sequelae in the aftermath of their recovery, termed post-intensive care syndrome (PICS) and PICS-Family (PICS-F), respectively. These can have a dire impact on patients and families, and thus require coordinated rehabilitative efforts. However, optimal modes of providing support services remain unclear, with traditional clinic-based strategies showing mixed results. This scoping review aimed to summarize the feasibility and effectiveness of nonclinic-based follow-up and rehabilitation strategies for adult survivors of critical illness and their caregivers.DATA SOURCES:Databases were searched on July 8, 2025, and included Cochrane, Embase, PubMed, and Web of Science.STUDY SELECTION:Studies reporting interventions and follow-up options for adult survivors of critical illness requiring ICU stay and targeting PICS and PICS-F were included. Studies examining interventions in the outpatient clinic or ICU settings exclusively were excluded.DATA EXTRACTION:Two team members used Covidence to screen all the deduplicated citations, with a third team member acting as a tiebreaker. The studies included were reviewed in full to extract general data, the studied syndrome (PICS, PICS-F, or both), component of PICS studied, study population, study objectives, methods, inclusion and exclusion criteria, intervention details, general results, and study conclusions.DATA SYNTHESIS:We screened 8608 studies; 45 studies met criteria and were included in this review. Most (64%) focused on the physical and psychological sequelae of PICS. Interventions varied widely, by setting, duration, and approach. Outcome measures were heterogeneous, limiting comparisons. Most studies evaluated the feasibility of interventions and not effectiveness. Only six articles targeted PICS-F exclusively.CONCLUSIONS:This scoping review summarizes the status of different nonclinic-based follow-up and rehabilitation strategies for adult ICU survivors. Future research focused on studies that evaluate effectiveness of these strategies in mitigating the burden of PICS and PICS-F, especially within the cognitive domain, is likely to improve aftercare for both patients and their families.

KEY POINTS

Question: Are there any feasible and effective interventions to mitigate post-intensive care syndrome (PICS) and PICS-Family (PICS-F) that do not require an in-person clinic follow-up?

Findings: This scoping review summarizes the existing scientific literature on nonclinic-based interventions for PICS and PICS-F, which can be in-hospital, at-home, and/or remote/virtual. The most promising interventions are those that cover more than one aspect of PICS and include multiple settings along the recovery pathway of ICU survivors.

Meaning: Nonclinic-based interventions to mitigating PICS and PICS-F are feasible and acceptable. Further research is needed to explore specific interventions and their effectiveness, primarily focusing on cognitive recovery and PICS-F.

Advances in intensive care medicine have decreased in-hospital mortality despite an overall increase in average age and severity of illness in ICU admissions (1). This progress has led to a growing population of survivors who endure significant impairment in physical function, cognitive capabilities, and psychological well-being long after their discharge from the ICU. This constellation of impairments is known as post-intensive care syndrome (PICS) (2). Family and informal caregivers of critically ill patients also suffer from the experiences in the ICU—the trauma of decision-making during the acute illness and the long-term burden of caregiving frequently lead to collateral psychological sequelae, termed PICS-Family (PICS-F) (3).

To mitigate the growing burden of PICS and PICS-F, specialized multidisciplinary clinics have been established across healthcare systems around the world (4–6), designed to meet the constellation of unique challenges faced by survivors of critical illness. They are primarily focused on physical, cognitive, and psychological rehabilitation, medication management, and comprehensive coordination of care (6). However, the efficacy of this model remains to be established.

A 2018 Cochrane review on ICU follow-up services reported little to no difference in quality of life, mortality after ICU discharge, physical and cognitive function, and psychological symptoms such as depression, anxiety and post-traumatic stress disorder, and rates of returning to work (7). More recent trials have shown inconsistent benefits of clinic-based follow-up strategies (8, 9), thereby questioning the effectiveness of this approach. Furthermore, the logistics of an in-person office visit are often challenging to patients recovering from the aftermath of critical illness and may incur selection bias favoring the more functional patients who are able to attend visits. At the same time, many remote and digital follow-up strategies were developed in the past decade to monitor patients’ progress, and even more so since the COVID-19 pandemic (10).

This scoping review aims to comprehensively analyze the feasibility and effectiveness of nonclinic-based follow-up services and rehabilitation options for adult survivors of critical illness requiring ICU stay and their caregivers. Furthermore, it seeks to identify future areas for study based on current clinical practice.

METHODS

Protocol and Registration

This scoping review was conducted with the guidance of the latest version of the Joanna Briggs Institute Manual for Evidence Synthesis (11). Utilizing the framework as outlined by Arksey and O’Malley (12), our scoping was conducted with Arksey’s five stages: 1) identifying the research question, 2) identifying relevant studies, 3) study selection, 4) charting the data, and 5) collating, summarizing and reporting the results. For transparency and reproducibility, our scoping review follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews checklist to ensure the rigor of this study (13) (Appendix S1, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B643). The protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework and is available online (https://osf.io/qpbmw).

Eligibility Criteria

There were no limits applied to the search regarding the date or country of publication. The search was restricted to publications in English, and gray literature was excluded. Studies that focused on follow-up interventions for adult survivors or family members of adult survivors of critical illness and requiring ICU stay were included. For the purposes of this study, a follow-up intervention was defined as any intervention aimed at mitigating PICS or PICS-F after ICU discharge. Clinic-based interventions were defined as interventions that required ICU survivors or their caregivers to travel to outpatient facilities for an in-person office visit. To address possible preventive interventions, together with the understanding that many ICU survivors are first transferred to hospital wards before their discharge home or to a rehabilitation facility, we have included interventions that started in the ICU and continued in the wards, as well as interventions that started in the hospital and continued at home or at another inpatient facility. However, studies in which the intervention was exclusively taking place in the ICU were excluded, as well as studies on patients who did not require ICU stay. Reviews were also excluded due to repetitiveness of evidence, as well as ongoing registered studies and/or published protocols that did not report any results by the time of the search. Studies that focused on ICU diaries were excluded as there have been several well-conducted meta-analyses on this topic, and this intervention is exclusively taking place in the ICU and not considered a follow-up intervention (14, 15).

Information Sources and Search Process

Initial search strategies were developed by knowledge specialist (T.C.). Building on the previous work, knowledge specialist (M.M.) developed and ran the final search strategy in four databases: Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews and CENTRAL, Wiley Online Library, London), Embase (Elsevier, Amsterdam, Netherlands), Medline (PubMed, National Library of Medicine, Bethesda, MD), and Web of Science—Core Collection (Clarivate, Philadelphia, PA). The search was executed on July 8, 2025.

A complete list of search strategies by database name, date, and results is available in Appendix S2 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B643). With exemplar articles from the team, knowledge specialist (T.C.) used the PubMed ReMiner tool (https://hgserver2.amc.nl/cgi- bin/miner/miner2.cgi; Amsterdam, Netherlands) to extract keywords and index terms to develop the initial search strategy. The agreed-upon search strategy was first run in PubMed, and then, with guidance from the Polyglot search accelerator, the search strategy was translated for the three remaining databases. Final search strategies were checked against the uniform standards of each database while maintaining reproducibility of the search.

Screening Process and Data Extraction

Two team members (Y.A.F., J.D.) used Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) to screen all the deduplicated citations by title and abstract, with a third team member (S.B.) acting as a tiebreaker. Then, two team members (Y.A.F., J.D.) reviewed the full-text articles, deciding on exclusions as appropriate, and a third team member (S.B.) resolving conflicts.

The studies included were reviewed in full to extract the following data: title, first author’s last name, publication journal, year of publication, study type, country in which the study was conducted, the studied syndrome (PICS, PICS-F, or both), which element of PICS was the focus of the study (physical, psychological, cognitive, or coordination of care), study population (survivors, caregivers, or both), study objectives, methods of participant recruitment, recruitment duration (the period of time during the study in which patients were enrolled to participate), inclusion and exclusion criteria, intervention details, number of participants, number and percentage of participants who were lost to follow-up or withdrew from the study, follow-up duration, scores used for outcomes, general results, and study conclusions. Feasibility, acceptability and effectiveness of interventions are extracted from the reported conclusions of the respective studies. Descriptive statistics were used to report the results.

RESULTS

Our search identified 8608 titles and abstracts. After removal of 880 duplications and exclusion of 7617 titles, 111 articles were screened for eligibility by reviewing their full text. After reaching consensus, 45 articles were included in the study (Fig. 1). The list of all included articles, along with an abbreviated version of the extracted data, is available in Appendix S3 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B643).

Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram. This figure describes the screening process according the PRISMA checklist. Excluded titles included systematic or narrative reviews, abstracts or protocols without any published results, studies that focused on ICU diaries (eight articles), clinic-based interventions (five articles), ICU-only interventions (one article) or no intervention at all (one articles), studies that were not published in English, and studies that did not focus on ICU survivors (two articles) or on post-intensive care syndrome (PICS)/PICS-Family (two articles).

Most of the studies originated in the United States (19/45, 42%), followed by European Union member states (7/45, 16%), the United Kingdom (4/45, 9%), and China (4/45, 9%). Only three studies (7%) were international and collected data from multiple countries. The earliest article included was published in 2003, and 89% of the articles (40/45) were published in the past decade (since 2015).

Study Population

Thirty-four studies (75%) focused exclusively on ICU survivors, while only six studies (13%) focused exclusively on families and caregivers, and five studies (11%) focused on dyads of ICU survivors and their caregivers. Seven studies (16%) included only survivors of medical ICUs, one study (2%) included only survivors from surgical ICUs, and 13 studies (29%) included both or mixed ICUs, while 14 studies (31%) did not mention the type of ICU. Inclusion and exclusions criteria varied among the studies, with notable exclusion of non-English speaking participants, survivors with cognitive impairment, and patients who were discharged to nursing homes or rehabilitation facilities. Appendix S4 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B643) summarizes the common inclusion and exclusion criteria for both ICU survivors and caregivers.

Mode of recruitment varied and included in-person visitation while inpatient in the ICU or hospital wards (25/45, 55%), in an outpatient setting (8/45, 18%), via the telephone (9/45, 20%), online methods such as social media and email (7/45, 15%), or mail (1/45, 2%).

Interventions Identified

Study interventions varied considerably and could be classified according to their location (post-ICU acute care facility, home, electronic or telehealth, and hybrid processes that start in the ICU), duration (from days to months), approach (diagnostic and assessment measures, preventative initiatives, treatment and rehabilitation interventions, digital follow-up, and coordination of care), and focus (psychological function, physical function, cognitive function, and transitional and care management issues). All interventions were found to be feasible and acceptable, although efficacy was rarely assessed (Fig. 2 and Appendix S5, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B643). Clinical outcomes were measured heterogeneously among the different studies, and the variety of scores used is summarized in Appendix S6 (https://links.lww.com/CCX/B643).

Psychological Interventions

Of the 34 studies (34/45, 76%) focusing on ICU survivors, 21 (21/34, 62%) focused on the adverse psychological sequela of PICS. These could be divided into four main themes: professional psychological support, mindfulness and coping skills training, peer support groups, and assessments of psychological needs and outcomes after nonpsychological interventions. Four studies explored proactive in-person or virtual professional psychological support, delivered by either trained psychotherapists or the ICU team (16–19). In-person psychological consultations in ICUs that continued in acute care settings (16, 17), home-based virtual reality sessions to process traumatic experiences (18), and 1-hour remote psychological sessions (19) were shown to be feasible, safe, and acceptable, but were not powered enough to assess efficacy.

Five studies (5/34, 15%) focused on mindfulness and coping skills training, mostly through mobile applications or telephone visits (20–24), originating from a single research group in the United States. Each study demonstrated the feasibility, acceptability, and usability of the mobile applications, but their effectiveness was only minor and at least noninferior to mindfulness sessions (22) or cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) sessions (23) with a trained therapist.

Four studies (4/34, 12%) investigated peer support groups for ICU survivors (25–28). These were usually operated as part of ICU follow-up clinics. However, the COVID-19 pandemic caused some of them to switch to a virtual format. McPeake et al (25) described six general models of peer support groups that were identified in ICU groups in the United States, United Kingdom, Australia, and Canada, and were based on combinations of timing (in-ICU, immediate post-ICU, or at any point of the recovery process), leadership (purely patient-led, mental health professional, or ICU clinician), and location of services (at hospital, elsewhere in-person in the community, or online). Lassen-Greene et al (26) studied ICU survivors who participated in a virtual peer support group during the COVID-19 pandemic and showed that 92% of participants felt comfortable using their devices to attend the group and endorsed fewer barriers to attendance with the virtual group. Furthermore, 90% of participants stated that they would continue attending the virtual group, as it helped them gain a greater understanding of PICS, how to manage its symptoms better, and build relationships with others who had similar experiences.

Finally, eight studies (8/34, 24%) explored psychological outcomes of nonpsychological interventions, such as physical therapy and rehabilitation (29–33), comprehensive follow-up (34, 35), and general screening for PICS (36) that will be discussed below.

Physical Interventions

Sixteen studies (16/45, 36%) focused on physical recovery and outcomes. These could be divided into five themes: physical therapy starting in the hospital, home-based in-person rehabilitation, exclusively telerehabilitation, combined cognitive and physical therapy home rehabilitation, and comprehensive follow-up programs.

In two studies (2/16, 12·5%), interventions began in the hospital and continued with in-person visits for a month, by either standard physical therapy (31) or virtual reality (37). Both did not demonstrate a benefit in objective physical outcomes, but one study showed a statistically significant benefit in self-reported physical and mental components of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) after a month compared with standard of care (31).

Three additional studies (3/16, 19%) explored the benefits of in-person home-based rehabilitation for ICU survivors, augmented by phone calls or virtual sessions to motivate survivors to perform unsupervised training sessions (29, 33, 38). The REVIVE trial examined the effect of two supervised and one unsupervised exercise sessions per week for 6 weeks and found no statistically significant difference in physical function by the end of the program (39). Patients participating in this trial reported the supervision to be the main facilitator of their perceived benefit from the program, while the biggest barriers were mental health issues and physical pain (29). Two studies (2/16, 12·5%) focusing specifically on pulmonary rehabilitation for COVID-19 patients showed benefits in reducing kinesiophobia (38), anxiety, depression, and dyspnea (33).

Exclusive telerehabilitation was the focus of five studies (5/16, 31%) (30, 32, 40–42), three of which reported a significant improvement in physical capacity and HRQoL with pulmonary rehabilitation via video conferencing (30, 41, 42). Jones et al (32) conducted a randomized controlled trial comparing 6 weeks of rehabilitation exercises guided by a manual and routine follow-up telephone calls with telephone calls only for the control group and showed statistically significant improvement in physical recovery and a decrease in depression symptoms. Capin et al (40) showed that a program of 12 telerehabilitation sessions with video conferencing for COVID-19 survivors is safe and feasible but their study was not powered to show significant benefit over usual care.

One study (1/16, 6%) combined cognitive and physical rehabilitation at home, concluded that it is safe and feasible, and showed a trend toward benefit in cognitive and physical outcomes after 3 months of home rehabilitation, but it was not statistically significant (43).

Last, five studies (5/16, 31%) explored the effect of remote PICS screening and follow-up on physical outcomes, but were all designed to test feasibility rather than efficacy (34–36, 44, 45). Therefore, it is still unclear whether these interventions are effective in improving clinical outcomes.

Cognitive Interventions

Only six studies (6/45, 13%) focused on cognitive outcomes in ICU survivors (34, 43, 46–49), and four of them (47–49) (4/6, 67%) designed their intervention exclusively targeting cognitive recovery. Most studies focused on cognitive interventions via video conferencing (43, 48), web-based software (49), and virtual reality (46).

Two studies (2/6, 33%) evaluated telemedicine-based remote cognitive assessments (48, 49) and reported both feasibility and acceptability from the patients’ perspective; however, neither study included a comparison with in-person assessments. Three other studies (3/6, 50%) were randomized controlled trials that explored remote cognitive rehabilitation interventions—home-based rehabilitation (which combined in-person visits at home and telemedicine visits) (43), virtual reality-based rehabilitation (46), and fully remote digital rehabilitation (47)—and compared them to in-person usual care. All three studies showed significant improvements over time in multiple cognitive domains—including general cognition, executive function, fluency, and visuospatial ability—either with no significant differences between control and intervention groups or with significant benefits favoring the intervention group in specific domains such as the Tower test and Functional Activities Questionnaire (43), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (47), and visuospatial function (46). However, all of these studies were relatively small (participant numbers ranging from 20 to 143) and excluded patients with severe cognitive disorders, indicating that further research is required to determine the effectiveness and broader applicability of these interventions.

Interventions Focused on Care Coordination

Nonclinic-based follow-up was the focus of 11 studies (11/45, 24%), which explored remote PICS screening by phone or online (3/11, 27%) (36, 44, 50) and comprehensive multidisciplinary follow-up through telemedicine and/or home visits (8/11, 73%) (34, 35, 45, 51–55). A study from the Netherlands developed a web-based triage tool to screen for PICS and found implementation challenging due to low rates of engagement from both ICU survivors and healthcare professionals (36). Nine studies (9/11, 82%) used telephone (35, 44, 50, 52), video conferencing (45, 51), and digital follow-up (36, 53, 55) for PICS screening and care planning in ICU survivors and showed that all these strategies are feasible; however, their impact on clinical outcomes is still unclear.

Three interventions (3/11, 27%) that focused on comprehensive follow-up showed mixed results. Khan et al (34) reported a randomized controlled trial in which patients were randomized to a nurse-led coordinated care with a multidisciplinary team that was based on home visits and phone calls for 12 months but was underpowered to show efficacy in clinical outcomes. On the other hand, Taylor et al (52) reported that a nurse-led telephone follow-up and care coordination with sepsis survivors reduced 30-day mortality and readmissions compared with usual care. Kovaleva et al (54) reported a pilot intervention of multidisciplinary video conferences with ICU survivors, which was acceptable and beneficial according to the participants.

Interventions for PICS-F

Four interventions were identified for caregivers of ICU survivors: PICS-F screening via telephone or mail (50, 56), mobile application for CBT (21, 57–59), in-person psychological intervention (60), and storytelling (61). Choi et al (56) showed that caregivers are able to share their needs and lifestyle restrictions remotely, and phone interviews might be effective in screening for PICS-F. Providing CBT via a mobile application was feasible and acceptable, and demonstrated some benefit in decreasing anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress symptoms (57, 58). An ultra-brief manualized psychological intervention to help caregivers cope with the ICU experience was shown to be feasible, acceptable, and associated with improvements in psychological outcomes overtime, although it was not compared with untreated controls (60). In addition, a home- or telephone-based storytelling session with bereaved family members of ICU patients was found to be feasible, acceptable and tolerable, and seemed to lower the prevalence of PICS-F among participants and reduce symptom burden (61).

DISCUSSION

This scoping review examines the existing literature on nonclinic-based interventions designed to prevent or reduce the harmful effects of PICS and PICS-F. This is topical given the inconsistent adoption of PICS clinics, a focus on mainly post-hospital discharge care, and a wide distribution of rehabilitative approaches that lead to the variable benefit seen in survivors. Our results show that most studies involve small cohorts and focus primarily on feasibility and acceptability of interventions in survivors, with only a handful measuring efficacy itself. This trend is reflective of the relatively nascent nature of rehabilitative services following discharge from an ICU stay. Additional identified gaps include the small number of participants enrolled in most studies, the short duration of interventions and follow-up, and the exclusion of patients with cognitive impairments, a population that may paradoxically benefit the most from targeted intervention.

We have identified multiple key points along the recovery pathway of ICU survivors, which present an opportunity for possible interventions in the ICU setting, inpatient acute care wards, and at home with telerehabilitation (Fig. 2). Some interventions—such as mobile applications for mindfulness and coping skills training, manuals and virtual physical therapy, peer support groups, and psychological consultations—could be implemented at any location acutely and continued long-term. Additionally, of the 15 interventions that were focused on more than one component of PICS or on coordination of care, seven (46%) showed effectiveness or a positive trend toward effectiveness, compared with 10 of 24 studies that focused only on one PICS component (41%; Appendix S5, https://links.lww.com/CCX/B643). This could suggest that survivors and caregivers may benefit most from multicomponent interventions that extend longitudinally through their path to recovery, but further research is needed on such interventions to substantiate this claim.

Nonclinic-based rehabilitation and follow-up have been efficacious in other diseases such as cancer (62), stroke (63), traumatic brain injury (64), and cardiac rehabilitation (65). These methods of follow-up care offer significant advantages to patients and their families who sometimes are too sick to travel to the hospital or clinic for a follow-up visit, live too far away, or suffer from significant financial distress following the hospitalization, which makes it infeasible for them to attend. Additionally, remote follow-up has the potential to be more cost-effective than a fully operational multidisciplinary clinic (66, 67). However, the era of telehealth is still in its infancy, and more research is needed on larger samples of patients and for longer periods of time to assess whether these interventions are more beneficial than in-person follow-up or usual outpatient care.

Among our included studies, more than a third of the studies excluded patients who did not speak English. This percentage increases to 82% when considering studies focusing on PICS-F. Most participants included in these studies were White, well-educated, and from a high socioeconomic status background. This excludes significant portions of the ICU survivors’ population, as approximately 20% of the patients admitted to ICUs in the United States alone are non-White (68). Similarly, 18% of the studies excluded patients who were discharged to nursing or rehabilitation facilities, and almost no studies investigated interventions in these facilities. While it is conceivable that patients discharged to home are less sick at discharge and are more likely to be able to participate in nonclinic-based interventions, this approach excludes approximately half of ICU patients who are discharged to post-acute care facilities (69). These patients are usually older and sicker and therefore likely to have a higher burden of PICS (70). Therefore, more research is needed on mitigating PICS in these high-risk populations, who also have even more barriers in approaching follow-up clinics.

Notably, very few studies were focused on cognitive recovery. These studies showed at least noninferiority of virtual assessments with in-person assessments, and some found benefits in cognitive function and quality of life. Specifically, combined physical and cognitive rehabilitation showed promising results (43) and is supported by previous studies that examined combined physical and cognitive rehabilitation in patients with multiple sclerosis (71), stroke (72), and Parkinson’s disease (73). A possible explanation could be that improvement in physical function accelerates improvement in executive function and problem solving, and vice versa. Providing this type of rehabilitation via telemedicine and home visits may facilitate rehabilitation further, as the home environment provides a more natural and supportive environment, which can enhance patient engagement and satisfaction (74). Factors such as social support, access to assistive technologies, and modifications to the physical environment are critical facilitators and are essential for cognitive rehabilitation (75). Therefore, further research should focus on cognitive rehabilitation in PICS, and specifically on home-based rehabilitation.

Our scoping review has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, as in many scoping reviews, the quality of the studies was not assessed, which limits discussion about their outcomes and results. Second, our results were limited by the primary database search strategy and the search date. Relevant studies published after July 8, 2025, are not included in this review and may limit our results and conclusions. Additionally, since we did not include gray literature and reviews, there is still a possibility that some important research was not assessed. This decision was made consciously with the intent of only including peer-reviewed literature. Third, this review did not include data on ICU diaries, despite it being a well-known intervention to mitigate PICS and PICS-F that is not clinic-based. This was justified based on several well-conducted systematic reviews and meta-analyses on this topic, which have already been published (14, 15); therefore, this review was unlikely to provide additional insights. Finally, the exclusion of non-English language literature is an important limitation, which is also acknowledged.

CONCLUSIONS

This scoping review offers a comprehensive analysis of the current literature regarding nonclinic-based follow-up and rehabilitation strategies to mitigate PICS and PICS-F. No clear strategies emerged as superior to others, although most remote/digital interventions were found to be noninferior to in-person interventions. Currently, no standard has been established, and combined strategies that intervene upon multiple PICS components warrant further investigation. Future research should focus on evaluating the effectiveness of these strategies in mitigating the burden of PICS, specifically on cognitive recovery of ICU survivors and on interventions targeted at PICS-F. These studies should attempt to include larger samples of patients, longer interventions, more inclusive cohorts (non-English speakers and patients discharged to nursing and rehabilitation facilities), and long-term follow-up of efficacy and outcomes of these interventions.
