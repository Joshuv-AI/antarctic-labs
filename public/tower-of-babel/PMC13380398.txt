A 4‐week community‐implemented diabetes prevention program is associated with improved metabolic outcomes at 12 months after participation
Authors: J. E. Bourne, K. Cranston, T. Dineen, M. MacPherson, S. Locke, A. Santos, N. Grieve, A. Colyer, K. Falkenhain, A. N. Yildirim, M. E. Jung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsSmall Steps for Big Changes (SSBC) is a 4‐week, evidence‐based diabetes prevention program delivered by non‐medical personnel in community settings. This study explored changes in type 2 diabetes risk factors and health behaviours over 12 months following SSBC program completion.MethodsA single‐arm pragmatic trial with repeated measures was conducted in three urban locations in Canada and was delivered virtually during COVID‐19. Participants were adults at risk of developing type 2 diabetes (HbA1c 39‐46 mmol/mol[5.7–6.4%]). SSBC consists of 6 one‐hour sessions involving behavioural counselling and exercise. The primary outcome was HbA1c at 12‐month post‐program. Secondary outcomes included weight, waist circumference, functional exercise capacity, diet and physical activity behaviour at 12‐month post‐program.ResultsThree hundred and eighty‐nine individuals at risk of developing type 2 diabetes were enrolled (mean age = 58.8(8.7) years, 61% White, 68% women), with a 93% session attendance rate. Of 389 enrolled, 172 completed 12‐month assessments, of whom 117 had HbA1c at baseline and the 12‐month follow‐up. At 12 months, participants had significant reductions in HbA1c (−1.91 mmol/mol; −0.19% [95% CI: −2.57, −1.36 mmol/mol; −0.25, −0.13%]), weight (−3.03 [−4.15, −1.93] kg), waist circumference (−4.07 [−5.27, −2.91] cm) and improved distance walked on the six‐minute walk test (37.55 [28.58, 46.75] m). Favourable changes in diet and physical activity were also sustained at 12‐month post‐program.ConclusionsSSBC participation was associated with improvements in key diabetes risk factors at 12‐month post‐program. These findings suggest that brief, community‐based, lay‐led programs may have potential for supporting diabetes prevention.

What is already known? Community‐delivered type 2 diabetes prevention programs (DPPs) can improve weight outcomes but are typically long, resource‐intensive and led by health professionals, with limited evidence for brief, lay‐led programs improving glycaemic control. What this study has found? A four‐week, lay‐delivered community DPP showed positive changes in HbA1c, weight, waist circumference, functional exercise capacity, physical activity behaviour and dietary intake over 12 months. At follow‐up, 42% of participants had HbA1c <39 mmol/mol (normoglycaemia threshold) and an additional 16% had HbA1c at 39 mmol/mol. What are the implications of the study? Brief, non‐medical, community‐based DPPs may offer a lower‐cost approach to supporting improvements in diabetes risk factors, with potential for broader implementation.

INTRODUCTION

The global prevalence of diabetes is projected to increase from 529 million cases in 2021 to 1.31 billion by 2050, 1 with type 2 diabetes (T2D) accounting for approximately 96% of cases. 1 T2D is associated with obesity, poor dietary intake and physical inactivity (PA). 1 , 2 Randomized trials demonstrate that structured diet and PA interventions can reduce T2D risk 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 ; however, translating these intensive trials into community settings is challenging. 7

Community‐based translational diabetes prevention programs (DPPs) can reduce T2D by 26–29% compared to control conditions, 8 , 9 and achieve significant weight loss and improved glucose control. However, they typically report less weight loss than clinic‐based trials. Specifically, community DPPs pooled weight loss ranges from 1.57–2.32 kg at 12 months 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 compared to 5.6 kg in the US DPP and 4.6 kg in the Finnish DPP. 6 , 12 Importantly, evidence indicates that even modest weight loss significantly reduces T2D risk, with every kilogram lost associated with a 43% reduction in the odds of developing T2D, 9 challenging the 5–7% weight loss benchmark for clinical relevance. 13

Glycaemic control is another key outcome of DPPs. Community DPPs achieve modest HbA1c reduction at 12 months (−1.4[95% CI: −2.4, −0.5]mmol/mol, −0.13[95% CI: −0.22, −0.05%]), 11 while the UK DPP reported a decrease of 2.04(95% CI: −1.96, −2.12)mmol/mol (−0.19[95% CI: −0.18, −0.19]%) among participants completing all 13 sessions. 14 However, the clinical relevance of these reductions in people living with prediabetes remains uncertain due to limited data on HbA1c response to interventions in community settings. 14 Most glycaemic data come from clinically delivered DPPs led by healthcare professionals, 9 , 11 while community programs frequently omit HbA1c measurement due to resource constraints. 9 Such data are essential for informing program guidelines and evaluating the clinical value of low‐cost, scalable DPPs. Current NICE 15 and IMAGE 16 guidelines prioritize weight loss outcomes, despite inconsistent associations between adherence and diabetes incidence. 8 Although intensive DPPs (≥13 sessions, 1–2 h each, over 9–18 months) are recommended to maximize clinical impact, 8 these models, typically delivered in clinical settings, 9 are often inaccessible to populations most in need. 17 , 18

This study examined whether a low‐cost, six‐session DPP, delivered by non‐medical personnel in a community setting, reduced T2D risk among adults with prediabetes, 12 months after program completion.

METHODS

Study design

A single‐arm pragmatic trial with repeated measures was conducted at three YMCA facilities in British Columbia, Canada, with a virtual delivery offered during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Ethical approval was obtained from the research ethics committee at the University of British Columbia (H16‐02028), and the trial was registered at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT03096002).

Small Steps for Big Changes (SSBC)

SSBC is a four‐week DPP delivered by trained non‐medical coaches, including YMCA staff and volunteers. Coaches are not required to have formal clinical training. Training is specific to SSBC and includes intervention content, foundational diabetes prevention knowledge, behaviour change theory, motivational interviewing and supervised practice. Training delivery has evolved from an initial two‐day in‐person workshop to an online format consisting of seven asynchronous modules with embedded quizzes and a virtual mock session. Coaches are required to demonstrate competency before delivering the program. The program includes 6 one‐hour, one‐on‐one sessions combining 30 min of counselling and 30 min of aerobic exercise, underpinned by motivational interviewing‐informed behaviour change techniques. 19 High fidelity of intervention delivery has been demonstrated. 20 , 21

Participants

Adults (≥18 years), with an HbA1c value between 39 and 46 mmol/mol (5.7–6.4%), or a T2D risk score (≥5) on the American Diabetes Association diabetes risk questionnaire 22 were eligible. Exclusion criteria included a diagnosis of T2D, explicit contraindications to exercise (e.g., musculoskeletal injury), uncontrolled major depression or lack of medical clearance for physical activity when required. Use of glucose‐lowering or weight‐loss pharmacotherapy was not an exclusion criterion for program completion, but was recorded at recruitment for analysis purposes. Recruitment occurred between September 2017 and January 2023, with a recruitment pause between February 2020 and October 2021 due to the COVID‐19 pandemic. Recruitment occurred through physician recommendation based on HbA1c results from routine tests, health assessment days in the community and online advertisements. Informed consent was obtained.

Outcomes and data collection

Demographic data were collected at baseline. The primary outcome, HbA1c, measured by venous blood sample collected at a local certified laboratory using a turbidimetric inhibition immune assay, was collected at baseline and 12 months post‐program. Secondary clinical outcomes, including waist circumference, weight and functional exercise capacity, were measured at baseline, 6‐ and 12‐month post‐program. Secondary behavioural outcomes, including physical activity and dietary intake, were assessed at baseline, 1‐, 6‐ and 12‐month post‐program.

Demographic data were collected in a baseline questionnaire. Age was collected as a continuous variable. Sex was recorded as men or women. Ethnicity was classified as Asian, Indigenous, Latin, Central, South American, North American, White or Black. Education was classified as high school or less, a non‐university certificate or diploma, or higher education. Income was classified as $0–$49,999, $50,000–$99,999 and $100,000+. Employment status was classified as working full‐time, working part‐time, retired or other. The location in which the participant completed SSBC was recorded.

Waist circumference was measured following the Canadian Society of Exercise Physiology measurement guidelines. Weight was recorded without shoes using a calibrated scale (700 seca, UK). Individuals who completed the virtual program were provided with a scale, measuring tape, instructions for self‐measurement, and were offered the opportunity to receive virtual instructions. Functional exercise capacity was assessed using the 6‐min walk test. 23 The total distance covered over 6 min was recorded.

Participants' moderate‐to‐vigorous PA (MVPA) score was assessed using the Godin Leisure Time Exercise Questionnaire. 24 Participants reported the number of light, moderate and strenuous PA bouts lasting more than 30 min in the past week. The frequency of each moderate and strenuous intensity activity was multiplied by the corresponding metabolic equivalents and summed to give an overall MVPA score.

Dietary intake was assessed using five summary questions on eating habits from the Block Questionnaire. 25 Participants reported their weekly consumption of vegetables, fruits, sweets, bread, and white rice or pasta. Vegetable and fruit intake was rated on a six‐point scale: 1 (once per week or less), 2 (2–3 times per week), 3 (4–6 times per week), 4 (once a day), 5 (2 times or servings per day) and 6 (3+ times or servings per day). Scale values 1 and 2 were collapsed due to low response rates (n < 10). Sweets, bread, and white rice or pasta consumption were recorded on a scale of 1 (<1 per week), 2 (once per week), 3 (2–3 times per week), 4 (4–6 times per week), 5 (once a day) and 6 (2 or more per day). Scale values 5 and 6 were collapsed due to low responses (n < 10).

A dummy variable was created to assess the impact of COVID‐19 on outcomes and attrition. Participants were classified as pre‐COVID‐19 or COVID‐19‐impacted based on program timing relative to January 25, 2020, with classifications adjusted for participants who withdrew before the pandemic onset.

Sample size and data analysis

Given the pragmatic nature of the study, the YMCA, in discussion with the research team, set the target sample size for the cohort of people receiving SSBC at 500 to reflect implementation capacity. Descriptive baseline demographic, behavioural and clinical outcomes are reported. Differences between participants retained at 12 months and those who dropped out were examined using t‐tests or chi‐squared tests, as appropriate. A linear mixed‐effects regression model with restricted maximum likelihood estimation was conducted to examine the impact of time on HbA1c and the association of demographic factors (age, sex, ethnicity, income and highest educational attainment), baseline weight and COVID‐19 status with change in HbA1c. Participant and program location were treated as random effects. The proportion of participants whose 12‐month HbA1c values were below 39 mmol/mol (5.7%), post‐intervention is reported. The proportion of participants whose 12‐month HbA1c values were ≥48 mmol/mol (6.5%) is reported, and the cumulative incidence calculated.

Secondary clinical outcomes and PA changes were analysed using linear mixed‐effects models controlling for covariates identified above (except in the weight model, where waist circumference was included as a covariate). Dietary intake changes were examined using ordinal mixed models (cumulative linked mixed models fitted with the Laplace approximation) with food group consumption frequency as the dependent variable. Odds ratios and associated confidence intervals were derived from raw coefficients. Model assumptions were checked with normal probability plots, and collinearity of independent variables was assessed through variance inflation factors.

The amount and pattern of missing data in the raw data were explored. Predictors of missingness at 12‐month follow‐up were examined using logistic regression models to assess the plausibility of the missing at random (MAR) assumption. Variables associated with dropout and missingness were included in the multiple imputation model. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using multiple imputations with multivariate chained equations to impute missing covariate and outcome data under the MAR assumption. Imputed results were compared with the primary analysis. To assess the robustness of the 12‐month HbA1c change to departures from the missing‐at‐random assumption, a tipping‐point sensitivity analysis was performed. For each of the 20 imputed datasets, we incrementally increased the imputed 12‐month HbA1c values (∆ = 0.5–4 mmol/mol) to simulate progressively worse outcomes due to missing values. Each shifted dataset was converted to long format and analysed using a linear mixed‐effects model. The tipping point was defined as the smallest ∆ at which the time effect became non‐significant (p > 0.05).

The proportion of individuals with a 12‐month HbA1c value below 39 mmol/mol (5.7%) was recalculated using multiple imputation to account for missing 12‐month HbA1c values. A conservative sensitivity analysis was also performed in which all participants with missing 12‐month HbA1c were assumed not to have achieved remission. Results in the manuscript are presented as means and 95% confidence intervals or proportions as appropriate. All data analyses were completed in R (version 4.4.0).

RESULTS

Retention and participant characteristics

Of the 397 individuals who consented and enrolled, eight were excluded from the analysis due to being on diabetes medication at baseline. Of the 389 included in the study analyses, 93% completed SSBC, 65% completed 6‐month follow‐up and 44% completed 12‐month follow‐up measures (File S1). Fifty‐four percent of the sample were engaged in SSBC during or after the COVID‐19 pandemic. In total, 41% of the data were missing across all confounders and outcomes of interest. File S2 details the missingness by variable.

Participants had a mean age of 58.8 ± 8.7 years. Of those who provided demographic data, 75% were women and 84% identified as White (Table 1). Eighty‐five percent of the sample completed SSBC in person, while 14% completed the program virtually. Retention at 12 months differed by program location (Table 1). Session attendance was high at 5.6 out of 6 sessions. Those who dropped out engaged in significantly fewer sessions than those who completed the 12‐month follow‐up.

Change in clinical outcomes

Of the 126 participants with 12‐month follow‐up HbA1c data, 42% had values below the ADA prediabetes threshold (<39 mmol/mol[5.7%]) and 16% had an HbA1c equal to 39 mmol/mol (5.7%). One new case of T2D was identified at 12‐month follow‐up. Using multiple imputation to account for missing data, the pooled estimate of participants with an HbA1c <39 mmol/mol (5.7%) was 56% (range across 20 imputations: 49–62%). In a worst‐case sensitivity analysis, assuming all missing participants had HbA1c ≥39 mmol/mol, the proportion achieving below the threshold was 14%. Using observed data, differences in the baseline characteristics of those who were below the 39 mmol/mol threshold at 12‐month follow‐up compared to those who were equal to or above are reported in File S3.

Linear mixed‐effects modeling revealed a significant effect of time (F(1, 143.59) = 40.43, p < 0.001), after controlling for demographic factors, COVID‐19 impact, baseline weight and the random intercepts of location and subject. Specifically, HbA1c decreased by 1.91 mmol/mol (95% CI: −2.57, −1.36 mmol/mol, p < 0.0001, [−0.19%, 95% CI: −0.25, −0.13%]) (see Figure 1a). There were no main effects of age, education, ethnicity, income or whether the participant was impacted by the COVID‐19 pandemic (Table 3). Sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation showed no substantive change in the direction and magnitude of the associations. Tipping‐point analysis indicated that the reduction in HbA1c remained statistically significant unless the missing 12‐month HbA1c values were assumed to be ≥1.5 mmol/mol higher than imputed, suggesting the time effect is robust to moderate departures from the missing‐at‐random assumption (see File 4).

There was a significant main effect of time on waist circumference (F(2, 211) = 38.41, p < 0.001) and weight (F(2, 203) = 30.3, p < 0.001). Specifically, there was a significant decrease in waist circumference of 3.53 (95% CI: −4.45, −2.61) cm and 4.07 (95% CI: −5.27, −2.91) cm from baseline to 6 and 12 months, respectively (Figure 1b). For weight, there was a significant decrease of 3.10 (95% CI: −3.95, −2.26) kg and 3.03 (95% CI: −4.15, −1.93) kg from baseline to 6 and 12 months, respectively (Figure 1c).

There was a significant main effect of time on distance covered in the 6‐min walk test (F(2, 177) = 61.19, p < 0.001). Significant increases in the distance covered were seen at 6 (38.54[95% CI: 31.12, 46.24]m) and 12 months (37.55[95% CI: 28.58, 46.75]m; Figure 1d) post‐program (see File S5). Sensitivity analysis using multiple imputation, including predictors of dropout and missingness, showed no substantive change in the direction and magnitude of the associations.

Change in behavioural outcomes

There was a significant main effect of time on moderate to vigorous physical activity scores (F(3, 411) = 15.34, p < 0.001). Specifically, MVPA scores significantly increased from baseline to 1 (9.50[95% CI: 5.83, 13.18]MVPA score) and 12‐month follow‐up (11.14[95% CI: 6.68, 15.54]MVPA score). There was a main effect of sex (F(1, 242) = 15.70, p < 001), with descriptive data revealing that men participants had higher MVPA scores than women (12‐month mean MVPA scores: 32.9[95% CI: 22.29, 43.60] and 25.7[95% CI: 15.69, 35.7]; respectively). See File S6. Sensitivity analysis with multiple imputations increased the magnitude of the change in MVPA scores from baseline to 1‐, 6‐ and 12‐month follow‐up.

For dietary intake, the likelihood of greater fruit intake increased significantly from baseline to 6 months post‐intervention with an odds ratio of 1.85 (95% CI: 1.15, 2.99, p = 0.01). Although fruit intake remained higher at 12‐month post‐intervention, the likelihood of greater fruit intake did not reach statistical significance (OR = 1.63, 95% CI: 0.90, 2.96). The likelihood of greater vegetable intake increased significantly from baseline to 6 and 12 months, OR = 2.08 [95% CI: 1.29, 3.37] and OR = 1.84 [95% CI: 1.01, 3.35], respectively. The likelihood of greater sweet intake decreased at 1‐, 6‐ and 12‐month post‐program, OR = 0.14 [95% CI: 0.05, 0.39], OR = 0.35 [95% CI: 0.21, 0.56], OR = 0.60 [95% CI: 0.34, 1.05] respectively. The likelihood of greater bread intake decreased significantly from baseline to 6 and 12 months, OR = 0.48 [95% CI: 0.30, 0.77] and OR = 0.52 [95% CI: 0.29, 0.92] respectively. Similarly, the likelihood of greater white rice and pasta intake decreased significantly from baseline to 6 and 12 months, OR = 0.37 [95% CI: 0.23, 0.60] and OR = 0.41 [95% CI: 0.23, 0.74] respectively. These findings suggest participants decreased their intake of sweets, bread, and white rice or pasta and maintained this change 12 months post‐program. See File S7. Likelihood Ratio Tests for all dietary intake models comparing the full models with null models indicated that all models, inclusive of predictors of interest, significantly improved model fit over the null models (p's < 0.001). Sensitivity analyses with multiple imputations did not significantly impact the magnitude or direction of associations.

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this study is among the first to evaluate a brief community‐delivered DPP provided by non‐medical personnel on HbA1c levels 12 months post‐program. In this cohort, HbA1c decreased by 1.91 mmol/mol (0.19%), and 42% of participants had values below the prediabetes threshold of 39 mmol/mol (<5.7%) at 12‐month follow‐up. The cumulative incidence for T2D was 0.79% 12 months post‐program. Observed reductions in HbA1c were similar across the demographic and socioeconomic groups in this sample and were not associated with COVID‐19 timing. These outcomes appear favourable compared with estimates reported in meta‐analyses of ‘real‐world’ translational DPPs 8 , 9 , 11 and are comparable to the more time‐intensive UK DPP. 14 , 26 However, these comparisons should be interpreted cautiously due to non‐random attrition in the current study and substantial missing data.

Participants also experienced a mean weight reduction of 3.03 kg (4.4% body weight) at 12 months, aligning with reported changes in the UK and US DPPs, 26 , 27 and slightly larger than pooled estimates from prior reviews. 8 , 9 , 11 Given the established association between modest weight loss and reduced T2D risk, 9 these findings are potentially clinically meaningful, particularly as SSBC was not designed as a weight loss program.

Improvements in clinical outcomes were accompanied by favourable changes in health behaviours. At 12 months, participants increased their MVPA score by 11.14, equating to approximately 55 additional minutes of MVPA per week. Given the independent role of exercise in improving insulin sensitivity in individuals with prediabetes, 28 this may have contributed to HbA1c reductions. Observed increases in the six‐minute walk test distance further support increased PA. Regarding dietary intake, participants reported small but statistically significant reductions in sugar and carbohydrate intake and increased vegetable intake, SSBC's primary dietary focus. These changes are consistent with dietary changes reported in other DPPs, 29 , 30 though comparisons should be interpreted cautiously.

High adherence (93%) was observed in the present study, exceeding rates in more frequent DPPs. 14 , 27 Although the single‐arm design precludes causal inference, high engagement may have contributed to the observed changes over time. These findings are notable given that existing guidelines recommend a minimum of 13 sessions based on evidence suggesting a dose–response link relationship between program attendance, weight loss and reduced diabetes incidence. 8 , 9 , 11 SSBC involves six hours of contact, yet participants in the current study experienced changes of a magnitude that appears comparable to those reported in more intensive programs, including the UK DPP (≥16 contact hours) and the US DPP (≥22 contact hours). 14 , 27 This observation may indicate that program duration alone may be less critical for program effectiveness than embedding evidence‐based behaviour change strategies. SSBC incorporates combined diet and PA behaviour change strategies delivered using motivational interviewing techniques and including 43 specific behaviour change techniques, including self‐regulatory methods. 19 These components align with best practice recommendations for maximizing the clinical impact of DPPs 15 , 16 and are consistently identified as active ingredients leading to success. Previous evaluations have also reported that coaches have high adherence to the SSBC intervention components, 21 likely contributing to program success.

In addition to program characteristics, differences in participant baseline characteristics may have influenced the glycaemic changes observed. Specifically, participants in the present study had baseline HbA1c values near the lower threshold of the prediabetes range, averaging 41 mmol/mol (5.9%), compared to 43–53 mmol/mol (6.1–7.0%) 14 , 31 in some of the clinical translational studies. These differences in baseline glycaemic status may partly explain the high proportion of participants whose HbA1c values fell below the prediabetes threshold at follow‐up. Evidence suggests that individuals with lower baseline levels of glycaemic dysregulation are more likely to return to normoglycemic ranges, even in the absence of substantial weight loss, and may demonstrate greater improvements in insulin sensitivity compared to individuals with higher baseline glycaemia who do not achieve this shift. 32 However, the single‐arm design and non‐random attrition in the current study limit causal inference, and these baseline differences should be considered when interpreting the observed outcomes.

Improved insulin sensitivity may be an important consideration in prediabetes remission, emphasizing the need to identify individuals with prediabetes as early as possible to begin preventive treatment. Further supporting the need for early detection, prediabetes is associated with elevated long‐term risk of all‐cause mortality and cardiovascular disease, independent of progression to T2D. 33 Mounting evidence of the detrimental impacts of prediabetes and the added risk reduction from achieving normal glucose control has led to calls for prediabetes remission to be the primary treatment target for DPPs. 34

An unexpected finding of the present study was that greater reductions in HbA1c were observed among participants who completed SSBC virtually compared with those attending in‐person sessions (mean HbA1c change: −3.1[95% CI: −4.7, −1.4]mmol/mol (−0.28[95% CI: −0.43, −0.13]%), −2.2[95% CI: −3.0, −1.4]mmol/mol (−0.20[−0.27, −0.13]%)) for virtual and in‐person delivery, respectively. These observations are consistent with evidence from digital adaptations of the UK DPP. 31 However, they should be considered exploratory, given that the study was not designed to formally compare outcomes between delivery modes and the virtual sample was small (n = 56). Several contextual factors may help explain this observation. The development of the virtual SSBC program occurred during the COVID‐19 pandemic, a period characterised by heightened health awareness 35 and substantial changes in daily routines. It is possible that some participants had greater flexibility or time available for health behaviours, which may have supported greater engagement in lifestyle changes. In addition, virtual delivery may reduce barriers related to travel, scheduling or discomfort with gym settings, which could enhance participation for some individuals. Technology‐supported DPPs have the potential to further reduce delivery costs and expand program reach, particularly for individuals living in rural and remote areas or those unable to attend in‐person programs. 7 At the same time, it is important that the expansion of digital delivery models does not exacerbate existing health inequities related to access to technology or digital literacy. 7 Future studies designed specifically to compare virtual and in‐person delivery of DPPs are needed to better understand whether modality influences participant engagement and clinical outcomes.

While the present study observed improvements in both clinical and behavioural outcomes at 12 months, evidence has shown that lifestyle interventions, although associated with a lower risk of T2D compared to controls up to 5–9 years after program completion, often show attenuated effects over time. 36 Therefore, the durability of SSBC's effects beyond 12 months remains uncertain. Long‐term follow‐up and booster interventions may be necessary to maintain improvements in glycaemic control, weight and health behaviours over time. Future studies should evaluate the sustainability of program outcomes and identify strategies to support continued behaviour change beyond the first year.

Strengths and limitations

This study is among the first to use a clinical measure of glucose control in a community‐delivered DPP, providing valuable data on the impact of such programs in low‐cost settings. However, the study has significant limitations. As a single‐arm prospective study, this design does not permit causal inference. The study experienced high attrition in the follow‐up data collection period, with over half of the participants affected by the COVID‐19 pandemic. This could have contributed to the loss to follow‐up due to disruptions in program delivery (including four closures and reopenings over 20 months), changes in participant availability and challenges with continued engagement. Non‐response to follow‐up assessments was classified as loss to follow‐up rather than documented program withdrawal, and as such, the reasons for non‐attendance are not fully known. Specific reasons for missing HbA1c measurements at 12‐month follow‐up were not collected. HbA1c testing required participants to attend an external laboratory, which may have contributed to missing data. While those who dropped out did not differ demographically from those retained, they had higher baseline weight, waist circumference and lower functional exercise capacity, factors that independently increase risk of T2D. 37 As a result, and given the single‐arm design and high attrition, leading to substantial missing data, the observed improvements in study outcomes may be overestimated. Sensitivity analyses assuming data were missing at random supported the robustness of the findings, but the potential for effect size inflation should be considered when interpreting the results. Weight and waist circumference measurements were self‐reported for participants in the virtual program, which may have introduced variability or measurement error despite providing equipment and guidance. Physical activity behaviours were also assessed by self‐report, which is subject to recall and social desirability bias and may overestimate true activity levels. However, the use of self‐report measures reflects the pragmatic, community‐based design of the intervention and enhances real‐world feasibility. The lack of a control group limited causal interpretation, and the impact of additional confounders that could have impacted the results cannot be ruled out. Finally, the sample was primarily White and women, reducing the generalisability to the wider Canadian population. Future research should examine the impact of the program on a more diverse study population and formally assess the cost‐effectiveness of this brief DPP.

CONCLUSIONS

Participation in SSBC was associated with improvements in diet, PA, and clinical outcomes relevant to T2D prevention over 12 months. These results indicate that a brief, community‐based DPP delivered by non‐medical personnel may offer a feasible approach to supporting lifestyle change at a population level. Given the study's high attrition and missing data, the true magnitude of effect remains uncertain, and further research is required to confirm these findings.
