Polygenic scores in the diagnosis of abnormal complete blood count values
Authors: Marc Srour, Estelle Lecluze, Elisha Chacowry, Manon Saby, Thomas Pincez
Published: 2026

Abnormal complete blood count (CBC) values can persist overtime and remain unexplained, 1 even after extensive investigation to identify an underlying disease. 2 , 3 Individuals have different setpoints of baseline CBC values, with great inter‐individual variability.3, 4, 5, 6 These CBC setpoints are largely genetically determined, and polygenic scores (PGS) summarize the genetic contribution to a given CBC trait.7, 8, 9 However, whether genetic contribution can, on its own, explain an abnormal CBC value is unknown. Determining that an abnormal CBC value can be due to the individual's genetic setpoint would ultimately allow for tailored management, notably by avoiding unnecessary investigations for an underlying disease. To evaluate the clinical relevance of PGS in the interpretation of CBC, we investigated whether an extreme PGS value could cause an abnormal CBC value.

We leveraged the UK Biobank (UKBB) as our main cohort and included the 200,816 patients (108,745 females and 92,071 males) with no condition(s) or treatment(s) that could affect CBC (Table S1). We also gathered the 46,362 individuals (30,163 females and 15,762 males, 436 intersex or not specified) from the All of Us (AoU) cohort meeting the same criteria. We used the AoU cohort only to validate the pattern seen in UKBB, as the lower number of individuals in the former limits the representativeness for extreme PGS percentiles. Moreover, likely due to the electronic health record‐based structure of AoU, patients had several CBC values available, with high frequencies of abnormal values. To limit potential confounding factors and to select the values more likely to represent genetic setpoints, we selected the less abnormal CBC value (i.e., the lower value of absolute z‐score). Nevertheless, a high frequency of abnormal CBC values remained for red blood cell (RBC) traits (e.g., 21% of individuals were anemic), and results for RBC traits should be analyzed with caution in this cohort.

Using genotyping data from the two cohorts, we use plink to compute 14 PGS for 14 CBC traits using published multi‐ancestry PGS (each of the 14 PGS is hereafter named PGSCBC, in which CBC designates the CBC trait to which the PGS corresponds to). 8

First, we aimed to identify which CBC traits should be separately analyzed based on sex. To test the impact of sex on PGSCBC, we fitted linear regression models for each CBC trait with the corresponding PGSCBC for this trait, sex, sex–PGSCBC interaction, age, and the first 10 principal components as covariates (Table S2). In the UKBB, we confirmed that PGSCBC and sex were associated with trait value for all CBC traits and found that sex–PGSCBC interaction was significant (P < 0.05) in 10/14 traits (exceptions were mean corpuscular hemoglobin [MCH], mean corpuscular volume [MCV], mean platelet volume [MPV], and white blood cell [WBC] count). The direction of effect for PGSCBC, sex, and the interaction term was consistent in AoU in 34/38 cases (89%). Therefore, we analyzed the ten traits with significant sex–PGSCBC interaction separately for males and females and jointly for the remaining four (total number of CBC parameters: 44). We considered high and low values for each trait except for basophil and eosinophil counts, for which no lower threshold exists. We computed the percentage of individuals with abnormal CBC value among those with extreme PGSCBC values (PGS value ≤ 0.1 percentile [p], ≤1p, ≤2.5p, ≤5p, ≥95p, ≥97.5p, ≥99p, and ≥99.9p) for two CBC value thresholds: abnormal CBC value (above or below the reference range using the World Health Organization and other published ranges),10, 11, 12, 13 and markedly abnormal CBC value (above or below a more extreme threshold that we defined as no specific ranges exist).

For all the 44 CBC parameters analyzed, we found in both the UKBB and AoU that the frequency of abnormal CBC values increased as the PGSCBC became more extreme (Figures 1, 2, and S1, complete data are shown in Table S3 for the UKBB and Table S4 for AoU). This suggests that an extreme PGSCBC can lead, by itself, to abnormal CBC values for all parameters.

The frequency of abnormal CBC values greatly varied among parameters (Figure 2B). For 28 of the 44 CBC parameters (64%), abnormal value was frequent among individuals with extreme PGSCBC and was found in ≥10% of individuals PGSCBC ≥ 99.9p in the UKBB. For 14 CBC parameters (32%), an abnormal value was present in ≥20% of individuals with PGSCBC ≥ 99.9p in the UKBB. We found a similar distribution pattern of CBC parameters associated or not with frequent abnormal values in the AoU cohort, supporting the specificity of these findings.

We found opposite patterns for several RBC parameters depending on sex. Polycythemia was found in 23% of men with PGSRBC ≥ 99.9p but was virtually absent in women. Conversely, anemia was noticed in 21% of women but only 7% of men with PGSRBC ≤ 0.1p. The observed pattern for RBC was opposite: women frequently have high RBC values (up to 43%), whereas men frequently have low RBC values (up to 30%). HCT pattern was close to the one of Hb with more pronounced differences for women (less than 1% with low hematocrit and up to 32% with high hematocrit). MCV, MCH, and mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC) had frequent high values (up to 19%, 38%, and 30%, respectively), whereas low values were found in less than 10% of individuals (except MCHC in females with up 16% of abnormal values).

Regarding platelet (PLT) traits, we found that thrombocytopenia was frequent with extreme PGSPLT values and was present in 29% of males and 14% of females with PGSPLT ≤ 0.1p. Conversely, thrombocytosis was rare (less than 10%). We found the opposite pattern for MPV. High MPV was even almost pervasive in individuals with high PGSMPV (98% of individuals with PGSMPV ≥ 99.9p). This opposite pattern between platelet count and MPV is consistent with the regulation of platelet mass rather than platelet count, in which a higher platelet count is associated with lower MPV. 14 , 15 Indeed, we found that PGSPLT was negatively correlated with MPV (R 2 = 8%, P < 10−16).

Regarding WBC traits, we found similar figures for leukocytosis and leukopenia, present in 16% and 18% of individuals with PGSWBC ≥ 99.9p and ≤0.01p, respectively. The frequency of neutrophilia increased with the PGSNEU value in both cases (up to 14%), and neutropenia was rare (less than 10%). Lymphocytosis was rare (less than 10%), and lymphopenia was found in up to 20% of females and 14% of males. Monocytosis was present in up to 20% of females and 40% of males, and eosinophilia was identified in up to 10% of females and 20% of males. Basophilia and monocytopenia were rare (less than 3% and 9%, respectively). We also investigated self‐declared African‐ancestry individuals, as the high frequency of Duffy‐negative individuals in this population requires using Duffy‐negative‐specific reference ranges for neutrophil and WBC counts. 8 We found a similar pattern in African‐ancestry individuals as in the general population, but their low representation in the UKBB hampers precise estimation (Figure S2).

Finally, we examined the magnitude of abnormal CBC values found in individuals with extreme PGSCBC values. For 21 of 28 CBC parameters with frequent abnormal values (75%), abnormal values found in individuals with extreme PGSCBC were mild (i.e., outside of the published reference range but not of the markedly abnormal range that we defined). The seven parameters with ≥10% of individuals with markedly abnormal values were high RBC in women (up to 22%), high MPV (up to 81%), high MCH (up to 18%), high MCHC in men (up to 15%), neutrophil count in males (up to 10%), and monocytosis in males (up to 17%). MPV was a significant exception with frequent markedly high values: 98%, 81%, and 50% of individuals with PGSMPV ≥ 99.9p had an MPV > 9, >10, and >11 fL, respectively.

In sum, we identified that abnormal CBC values can be due to extreme PGSCBC. Our results suggest that extreme PGSCBC should be considered in the differential diagnosis of abnormal CBC values. PGSCBC can be clinically relevant in CBC interpretation in some settings depending on the specific CBC parameter, the magnitude of the abnormal value, and sex for most parameters. We can define two clinical settings: (1) Two‐thirds (28/44) of CBC parameters have frequent abnormal values in case of extreme PGSCBC (listed in Figure 2 legend). In these cases, testing for the corresponding PGSCBC can be clinically useful, as an extreme PGSCBC value may provide an explanation for an abnormal CBC value. This value is only mildly abnormal in three‐quarters of these parameters (21/28) and can be markedly abnormal in the other quarter. Markedly abnormal MPV values were even found in almost all individuals with extreme PGSMPV. (2) For the other 16/44 parameters, an extreme PGSCBC value does not appear to explain abnormal CBC values. In such cases, clinical PGSCBC testing offers limited utility. This finding also has a direct clinical consequence: in these cases, a genetically determined setpoint outside the reference range should not be considered in the differential diagnosis.

Our results also suggest that sex influences the genetic determinants of most CBC values through an epistatic effect. As reported for other traits, the underlying mechanisms could involve sex‐specific gene expression or gene architecture, 16 , 17 as is the case with sex hormones, 18 which are known to affect CBC values. As a clinical consequence, we found that the frequency of abnormal values may substantially differ between males and females (e.g., for most WBC traits) and even present an opposite pattern for several RBC traits.

Our results pave the way toward a future individualization of CBC, which would consider the PGSCBC value of each individual for increased CBC accuracy. Ultimately, CBC reference ranges based on PGSCBC values may allow optimal interpretation. Given the increasing availability of genetic testing, computing PGSCBC may represent a new tool in clinical hematology.

Our analyses have some limitations. Despite the consistent patterns identified between the UKBB and AoU cohorts for most parameters, we found some differences in the frequency of abnormal values, especially for RBC traits due to the unusual prevalence of anemia in the AoU cohort. This difference could be due to the lower sample size of AoU but also the way CBC values were collected. For inclusion in the UKBB, patients had to attend an assessment center where blood was drawn. This is more likely to reflect the baseline CBC setpoint. In contrast, AoU collected data from electronic health records. CBC values obtained in this manner are more susceptible to being affected by clinical indications and concurrent conditions that led to the CBC, despite our efforts to mitigate this impact. Moreover, the precise frequencies of abnormal CBC values may fluctuate over time and be affected by other parameters, such as ancestry, 8 , 19 that the current sample size of existing biobanks does not allow to capture. Finally, iron deficiency could also affect RBC traits, particularly in females due to its high prevalence in this population, but iron studies were not performed in the UKBB.
