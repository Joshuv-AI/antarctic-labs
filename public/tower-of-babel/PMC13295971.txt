Quality education initiative to reduce pressure ulcers in high-risk-admitted patients in low-resource settings
Authors: Almas Fasih Khattak, Sulaiman Hussain, Bashir Ahmad, Madiha Khattak, Wagma Viqar Afridi, Barkatullah Khan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPressure injuries, although preventable, are a persistent challenge for admitted patients in hospitals globally, especially in low-resource settings. High-risk patients include the elderly, the immobile and those with terminal illness. A hospital-wide quality education initiative was launched at a tertiary care hospital after an increasing trend of pressure injuries was seen among inpatients across different units. This initiative aimed to assess the effectiveness of a simple, easy-to-use Turning Clock Tool to reduce the occurrence, severity and undesired outcomes of pressure injuries.MethodsThe quality education initiative was implemented over 6 months in three stages: the preintervention stage from 1 November 2023 to 31 January 2024, the implementation and training stage during February 2024, and a postintervention stage from 1 March 2024 to 31 May 2024. All inpatients admitted for at least 24 hours were included in the study. Nurses and caregivers were trained on the use of the tool to reposition patients every 2 hours. The incidence of pressure injuries, location, staging and the duration of hospital stay were compared before and after the implementation of the tool.ResultsThere was a 77.36% reduction in the incidence of pressure injuries from 53 to 12 cases. All stage-3 pressure injuries were eliminated while a significant decrease in stage-4 pressure injuries was seen. The gluteal and sacral regions were the most affected parts of the body. Patients with advanced age were the most affected. Average length of stay dropped from 11 days to 5 days.ConclusionThe Turning Clock Tool evaluated effectiveness, feasibility and contextual significance for preventing pressure injuries in low-resource hospital settings. Its use not only decreased the incidence and severity of pressure injuries but also reduced the average length of hospital stays. The Turning Clock Tool can be used as a simple yet cost-effective model to prevent pressure injuries in similar resource-constrained settings globally.Keywords: Patient safety, Quality improvement, Pressure Ulcer

Introduction

Pressure injuries (PUs) are a common and critical medical condition affecting hospitalised patients globally.1 Before February 2024, our hospital experienced a significant increase in hospital-acquired PUs. Such injuries were very common among patients who are immobile, elderly, and those with limited consciousness and usually appeared over bony prominences because of pressure or pressure combined with shear, affecting both the skin and underlying tissues.

Advanced age, immobilisation, altered mental status, faecal or urinary incontinence, fractures, friction, moisture, surgical interventions, poor appetite, low serum albumin levels, unhealthy eating habits, diabetes, pre-existing skin conditions and reliance on nasogastric or intravenous nutrition are all risk factors for hospital-acquired PUs.2 All these factors can increase the likelihood of developing PUs. Since 10% to 23% of hospitalised patients in Western settings are affected by PUs, identifying those at highest risk is crucial for prevention and patient care.3,5

PUs have a multifaceted effect, resulting in discomfort, scarring and interference with daily activities. Not only the physical but also the economic burden is substantial as well; the annual PU-related expenses are estimated at $11 billion worldwide. Complications from PUs result in 60 000 fatalities annually in the USA alone, out of 2.5 million hospitalised patients.6 7 PU-related treatment is responsible for 25% of the total cost of managing spinal cord injuries (SCI) and disorders in Africa.8 In addition, the occurrence of PU causes a delay in the delivery of essential hospital services, such as acute treatment, home-based care, rehabilitation and long-term support.9

PUs cause significant morbidity, extend hospital stays and add immense cost. Globally, the overall impacted population almost doubled from almost 300 000 in 1990 to 6 45 000 in 2021, despite a slight decrease in age-standardised prevalence. In South Asia alone, an estimated 55 449 individuals experienced active PUs in 2021.10

Several Asian studies have indicated that systematic repositioning can significantly lower the incidence of PUs. A study performed in a neurosurgery critical care unit in Pakistan showed that a structured repositioning approach resulted in a significant difference in PUs between the intervention and non-intervention groups (1.35±0.52 vs 1.73±0.79, p<0.01) at day 10.11 Similarly, a rehabilitation study in Pakistan revealed repositioning patients every 2 hours with a 30° lateral tilt rather than every 4 hours with a complete lateral tilt improves PU healing in paraplegic patients.12 A 10‐week quality improvement initiative implementing an evidence-driven toolkit with critical care patient automated turning beds, reducing the incidence of PUs.13 In contrast, only 4.8% of at-risk individuals received appropriate repositioning and skin care, emphasising low compliance with prescribed preventative measures.14

There is a significant amount of variation in the prevalence of PUs because of the different clinical settings. Intensive care unit (ICU) patients are at a greater risk than patients in other settings, with incidence rates ranging from 3.3% to 52.9%.15 16 PUs that are associated with SCI are common in developing countries; however, the prevalence and incidence numbers that have been reported are highly variable. In both the developed and developing countries, the risk factors for PUs are equal; however, in developing countries, many of the risk factors are more prevalent than in developed countries.17 Therefore, it warrants cost-effective efforts and resources to prevent PUs in low-resource settings of the developing countries.

PUs can heal slowly and recur, especially in wheelchair users and bedridden patients. Regularly changing sitting or lying positions can relieve skin pressure. Observing susceptible body parts and positions can help determine relief. Using cushions, adjusting chair positions and moving frequently, even if lying in bed, can help reduce pressure.18

Most of the prevention and treatment strategies in underserved populations have been descriptive in nature and have primarily focused on the improvement of acute care, staffing ratios and supportive infrastructure, such as specialised mattresses. The potential benefits of involving patients and their families in the prevention of PUs include a reduction in the risk of complications, a decrease in healthcare costs and an improvement in the overall quality of life of patients.19 20 Nurses and the hospital staff also play a critical role in the prevention of PUs in hospital-admitted patients. Tools like adequate resources, teamwork and guidance from leaders and wound care experts can guarantee the provision of high-quality and effective care.21 In reality, turning strategies could be boosted by visual aids. Bedside turning clocks or charts illustrating each patient’s specific turning schedule increase caregiver compliance with 2-hourly turning.22

Despite the implementation of established preventive measures, PUs continued to be a persistent concern among patients who were admitted for extended stays at a tertiary care hospital in Peshawar, Pakistan. Between 1 November 2023 and 31 January 2024, 53 cases were recorded. This represented an increase from the 122 cases that were documented during the previous audit, which took place from June 2020 to July 2022.23 24 The hospital’s quality committee responded by prioritising the prevention of PUs in high-risk patients and launching a targeted, cost-effective quality improvement project across multiple departments. The initiative focused on patients who were most susceptible to the development of PUs, including those with reduced consciousness, terminal illnesses or limited mobility, especially in the ICUs.

Methodology

Context and setting

This quality improvement project was carried out in a major tertiary care hospital located in Peshawar, Pakistan. It was implemented across 14 clinical departments in the hospital, including cardiology, the COVID ward, endocrinology, gastroenterology, general medicine, general surgery, gynaecology, nephrology, neurology, neurosurgery, orthopaedics, pulmonology, paediatrics and the ICUs, caring for a variety of high-risk patients, including elderly, immobile and neurologically impaired.

Before the intervention, practices for preventing PUs differed across departments, with the repositioning of high-risk patients being performed inconsistently by nursing staff and patient care assistants. This disparity led to the occurrence of PUs. The hospital utilised regular foam mattresses in most wards, with only limited air-bubble mattresses available for critically ill patients in the ICU.

In this resource-limited context, it was important to implement a cost-effective and easy-to-use intervention to ensure consistent turning for all high-risk patients.

Improvement model

The Quality Improvement Department of our hospital provided direction for this quality improvement project and followed the Institute for Healthcare Improvement’s (IHI) Model for Improvement, a widely accepted approach for developing measurable and sustainable improvements in healthcare settings. The model indicates three key components: developing a clear and attainable goal, identifying indicators illustrating progress and determining specific, achievable improvements that enable improved results. These guidelines were employed to establish the project’s objectives, to reduce hospital-acquired PUs among high-risk inpatients by using the Turning Clock tool as the intervention, and to compare preimplementation and postimplementation outcomes to determine improvement.

A single Plan–Do–Study–Act (PDSA) cycle was employed to implement and evaluate the intervention. In the plan phase, the quality improvement department and research team observed an increased incidence of PUs and established the Turning Clock Tool as a viable, affordable preventive measure. During the Do phase, the Turning Clock Tool was implemented at the ward level, led by nurse supervisors and unit team leaders. The study phase entailed the analysis of preintervention and postintervention data collected from the Hospital Management Information System (HMIS) to assess variation in the incidence and severity of PUs. Finally, the act phase prioritises sustainability by conducting audits of compliance every 6 months, unit on-shift supervision by unit leaders, and intending to integrate a specific 2-hourly turning checklist into the hospital management system.

Intervention

The primary intervention was to introduce the ‘Turning Clock Tool’, a visual aid that encourages changing positions of high-risk patients every 2 hours to avoid PUs. The tool is a spherical diagram with six 2-hour sections, each of which is labelled with an ideal placement (such as the back, left side or right side) (figure 1).

The 2-hourly Turning Clock Tool as displayed on patients' bedside in the hospital under study.

This project was implemented by a multidisciplinary team supervised by the Quality Improvement Department, which included nursing supervisors, ward in-charges, physicians and quality officers. Institutional data and incident reports revealed inconsistent repositioning and growing PU rates across the wards. In the initial brainstorming session, the team identified limited reminders, inconsistent documentation and a lack of awareness of evidence-based turning intervals as major concerns, prioritising repositioning as the most significant area for improvement. Although no formal Pareto chart was generated, team consensus identified the ‘Keep Moving’ component of the Surface, Skin inspection, Keep Moving, Incontinence, and Nutrition (SSKIN) framework as the most inconsistently implemented preventive strategy.

Before this quality improvement initiative, several components corresponding with the SSKIN bundle, like application of appropriate support surfaces, routine skin examination, maintenance of dryness and cleanliness and attention to patient nutrition and hydration, were already utilised as part of routine nursing care at the hospital, though not under standardised SSKIN protocols. During the planning stage, the ‘keep moving’ element is lacking consistently. Thus, the Turning Clock Tool was developed explicitly to enhance and standardise this area of care. While other existing nursing practices remained unchanged throughout the study period.

A routine ward-based educational session preceded implementation of the tool. These sessions were organised and supervised by the Quality Improvement Department and facilitated by nursing supervisors and the unit team leader. Each ward received an on-site session, including brief demonstrations on correct tool placement, turning techniques, and documentation. Unit leaders gave bedside orientations during shift handovers to reinforce accurate use.

Nursing supervisors and unit team leaders performed an on-ward demonstration, indicating appropriate use and deploying the tool at each high-risk patient’s bedside.

On-duty nursing staff and care assistants were reminded during each shift to hand over and follow the prescribed turning schedule. Despite no formal pre-training and post-training evaluation of staff knowledge or behaviour being conducted, ongoing assessment by team leaders across routine shifts enhanced compliance and consistency of practice.

Project phases

The intervention was implemented in three stages; in the preintervention stage, 3 months of retrospective data were collected from 1 November 2023 to 31 January 2024. In the implementation stage during February 2024, the Turning Clock Tool was implemented, and nursing staff and patient care assistants were trained on its use. In the postintervention stage, another 3 months of prospective data were collected from 1 March to 31 May 2024.

Participants and risk classification

There is no standardised risk assessment scale routinely used in this hospital. In this study, all patients admitted for at least 24 hours in the above-mentioned departments were included. Patients with pre-existing PUs on admission were excluded.

Outcome measures, data collection and analysis

Data were collected from the HMIS for two particular time periods: preintervention phase and postintervention phase, using a standardised data collecting form including patient demographics, gender, age, medical record number, ward of admission, total length of hospital stays, number of PUs, stage of the injury and anatomical location. To ensure patient confidentiality, data were deidentified before analysis. The project aimed to assess the overall reduction in incidence following the implementation of the Turning Clock Tool.

All the data were collected from the hospital’s online management system and entered into an Excel sheet. Patient identifiers, including name, MR number and contact details, were removed. The final deidentified dataset was used exclusively by the research team and was not disclosed publicly. Data accuracy was confirmed by cross-checking with the original HMIS records.

Results from the preintervention to postintervention stages were compared using descriptive statistics. The Turning Clock Tool’s effectiveness was evaluated by analysing changes in PUs rates, severity and length of hospital stay.

Ethical statement

This quality improvement initiative involved human participants; however, the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Northwest General Hospital & Research Centre, Peshawar, Pakistan, exempted this study from ethical review because it was carried out as part of a hospital-wide quality improvement (QI) initiative approved by the hospital. Formal approval from the IRB was not required, as an exemption was given in accordance with the hospital policy for QI activities that do not qualify as human subject research. For more information and clarification, the IRB Coordinator can be reached at coordinator.irb@nwgh.pk.

Results

Preintervention stage (November 2023–January 2024)

During the preintervention period, a total of 53 cases of PUs were documented. The majority were categorised as stage I (23 cases) and stage II (18 cases), whereas more advanced cases comprised stage III (eight cases) and stage IV (four cases) of PUs. The gluteal region was the predominant anatomical site, comprising 41 PUs. Other sites included the sacral region (5 cases), scapulae (4 cases), malleoli (2 cases) and heels (1 case). These patterns highlight the susceptibility of regions exposed to extended stress and insufficient repositioning. The average length of hospital stay for patients with PUs was 11 days. Patients with stage III injuries experienced significantly prolonged hospital stays, with a maximum of 90 days, indicative of the increased complexity of care required for more severe PUs. The patient cohort consisted of 33 males and 25 females, with a mean age of 61.1 years (range: 18–101 years). The interquartile age range of 53–73 years indicates increased vulnerability among older adults. These baseline findings suggested the urgent need for a standardised, hospital-wide, cost-effective preventive strategy, especially in our low-resource setting, to reduce both the occurrence and severity of PUs in high-risk patients.

Implementation and training stage (February 2024)

In February 2024, the Turning Clock Tool was implemented throughout the hospital, and training sessions were arranged for nurses and the patient care assistants. The unit team leaders started monitoring compliance with the 2 hourly repositioning schedule after placing the tool at the patients’ bedsides. Compliance reports indicated uniform adoption across departments by month’s end, but no official data collection on outcomes was conducted during this time.

Postintervention stage (1 March–31 May 2024)

After 1 month of the implementation of the Turning Clock Tool, data were collected again. There was a marked decrease from 53 cases before the intervention to 12 cases of PUs in high-risk inpatients during the 3 months after the Turning Clock Tool was implemented. According to severity, there were six cases of stage I PUs, four cases of stage II and two cases of stage IV PUs. The intervention was successful in preventing more severe PUs, as stage III PUs were entirely eradicated. Both the gluteal and sacral regions were the most common sites of PU development (seven cases and five cases, respectively). Although there has been a decrease in the variety and spread of PUs compared with the period before the intervention, this consistent pattern still indicates that high-pressure zones remain vulnerable. Patients suffering from PUs had a shorter than expected hospital stay of an average of 5 days, compared with 11 days before the intervention. The average age of the patient population was 70 years, with a range of 27–88 years. There were 8 males and 4 females in total. Once again, the older adults were more vulnerable, with an interquartile age range of 63.7–82.2 years. In high-risk populations, these results highlight the need for structured preventive strategies such as the Turning Clock Tool to decrease PU incidence, minimise severity and improve clinical outcomes (figure 2).

Contextual factors and unintended effects

This intervention was implemented in a dynamic, resource-limited hospital setting, where teamwork and supervisor engagement were essential to its success. Active monitoring by the nursing team, and constant bedside display of the Turning Clock Tool, with consistent adherence despite staff fluctuation. This initiative promoted improved interaction among staff members, heightened awareness of preventive care and improved documentation practices, creating a more effective patient safety culture. Although a few staff members first observed an increased work drive, which was temporary, as evidenced by the reported decrease in new PUs and a more defined role distribution. This project included no significant additional financial costs, demonstrating that in inexpensive, low-resource healthcare settings, behavioural interventions can provide significant and long-lasting quality improvement.

Sustainability and future directions

To ensure the long-term sustainability of the Turning Clock tool, intervention will require continuous reinforcement, periodic evaluation and integration with hospital workflows. To ensure adherence, unit nursing team leaders will continue to monitor compliance during routine shifts and provide reminders to the staff. Hospital-based assessments of PU incidence and turning compliance are scheduled every 6 months to assess progression and locate areas that require improvement. Furthermore, the implementation of a dedicated ‘2-hourly turning checklist’ within the hospital management system is intended to facilitate electronic accountability and monitoring. These measures intend to integrate the Turning Clock Tool into nursing practice as a standard approach and to promote long-term progress in preventing PUs and improving patient safety.

Discussion

Implementing the Turning Clock Tool, a structured repositioning intervention, significantly reduced the incidence, severity and length of hospital stay linked to pressure injuries among high-risk admitted patients in this quality improvement project. As an example of the effectiveness of low-cost, context-sensitive solutions in resource-limited settings, the intervention reduced pressure injuries by 77.36% within 3 months and eliminated stage III pressure injuries.

Results from comparable studies corroborate our findings. For example, Al-Otaibi et al found that hospital-acquired pressure injuries decreased after a targeted prevention programme, which included staff education and pressure injury audits, was implemented in a hospital in Saudi Arabia.2 Similarly, Bereded et al highlighted the necessity for systematic prevention strategies after reporting a prevalence of 16.8% of pressure injuries in adults hospitalised in Ethiopia.4 Pressure injury rates were 12.4% in a Jordanian point prevalence study by Tubaishat et al, mostly among immobilised patients. The study also showed that regular risk assessment and prevention tools were underused because of a lack of knowledge and a shortage of personnel.6 Our intervention offered a long-term answer to those problems by decreasing reliance on constant observation and documentation; it centred on involving nurses and patient care assistants, already involved in the care of those patients as part of their job, with an easy-to-use and cost-effective visual and time-based repositioning tool.

Several studies have shown that not every preventative measure has been successful. According to Schutt et al, typical bedside turn clocks and timers had not achieved high compliance with turning protocols.25 Similarly, Yoo Chang et al established an educational toolkit for patient care technicians that improved knowledge and audit performance but did not help to reduce pressure injury prevalence,26 and a study by Rich et al reported no association between the 2-hour turning clock and pressure injury reduction.27

Timely and regular repositioning not only prevents pressure injury development but also expedites patient recovery and discharge readiness. The dramatic reduction in hospital stay duration—from an average of 11 days to 5 days—reinforces the use of this tool.7 A Brazilian multicentre study showed that vulnerable patients, such as those suffering from malnutrition, are at increased risk of pressure injuries during the first week of hospitalisation. The study emphasised the importance of promptly and proactively implementing preventive measures, such as repositioning and nutritional assessment, to decrease the occurrence of pressure injuries and shorten hospital stays. This approach would be both cost-effective and impactful.28 Our study’s results corroborate those of the Brazilian study, showing that easy-to-use tools and teamwork approaches can improve outcomes, enhance quality of life and optimise resource use. We were able to eliminate Stage III pressure injuries and significantly reduce advanced-stage pressure injuries.

The simplicity, affordability and scalability of this intervention were its notable strengths. In low and middle-income countries, where advanced pressure-relieving surfaces and nurse-patient ratios often fall short of optimal standards, the Turning Clock Tool becomes all the more relevant because it does not require any additional human resources or digital infrastructure.

Several limitations should be recognised before the findings of this study can be generalised. One limitation that might limit the study’s applicability is that it only utilised data from one centre. Second, there is a chance of bias due to the observational nature of the data collection and the absence of a concurrent control group. The substantial improvement in the reduction of pressure injuries in 3 months could be due to factors like the Hawthorne effect, wherein better outcomes could be the result of awareness of being observed rather than the intervention itself, although fulfilling our study objective.

It would be ideal for future research to use control groups or stepped-wedge designs to replicate this intervention across various departments and units with varying levels of resources. Additional measures to improve results could include using electronic compliance tracking in hospitals and formally including family members and informal caregivers in the prevention process as well. Future research could also assess the long-term compliance levels, cost-effectiveness and patient satisfaction with the use of the Turning Clock Tool.

It is a promising model for hospital-wide quality improvement strategies in low-resource settings from a policy and system perspective and is an ideal candidate for inclusion in standard nursing protocols and hospital accreditation frameworks due to its adaptability across departments, minimal training requirement and ease of use.

Limitations

This study had several limitations that should be considered before interpreting the results. First, it was conducted in a single centre, restricting applicability of the findings to other settings, keeping in view the different populations and clinical environments, resources and staffing levels. To minimise this limitation, we implemented the intervention across all medical and surgical departments to increase representativeness, although within the context of a single centre.

Second, this initiative used an observational design without a control group, and a Hawthorne effect could have contributed to the reduction in the number of pressure injuries. To control this risk, the project employed a consistent predesign and postdesign using comparable timeframes, maintaining routine operations without introducing additional interventions.

Third, adherence to the Turning Clock Tool was supervised informally through ward-level monitoring without systematic measurement or inter-rater verification, with possible observer bias. To reduce this limitation, unit in-charges, team leaders and nursing supervisors carried out regular bedside checks, shift handoffs and verbal reminders to reinforce compliance with the 2-hourly turning schedule.

Fourth, this improvement initiative did not assess potential confounding factors that might affect the outcomes of pressure injuries, such as comorbidities, nutritional status of patients and the baseline skin condition, which could influence the results. To address this issue and to ensure consistency, patients with pre-existing pressure injuries were excluded, and data were extracted from the centralised HMIS from hospital-wide departments.

Finally, as we relied on data extracted from the HMIS, therefore, under-reporting and documentation errors might have occurred. To account for this and maintain confidentiality, data were deidentified before analysing them as well as cross-checked by the QI department to ensure accuracy.

Despite these limitations, consistent collection of data, hospital-wide roll-out and the significant improvement in outcomes suggest that the Turning Clock Tool was a cost-effective strategy for reducing pressure injuries in low-resource settings.

Conclusion

This quality improvement initiative proved that the Turning Clock Tool, a simple and no-cost intervention, can produce significant results in preventing pressure injuries in resource-constrained hospital environments. The Turning Clock Tool significantly reduced the incidence of pressure injuries and average hospital stays and eliminated stage III pressure injuries altogether, demonstrating its ability to improve patient safety, reduce morbidity and increase operational efficiency. Its scalability, affordability and ease of use make it a feasible framework for widespread hospital-wide and system-level adoption. Future studies should explore cost-effectiveness, patient and family members’ engagement in the use of this tool and long-term sustainability. The Turning Clock Tool could be a crucial component in improving patient safety and pressure injury prevention, especially in low-resource but high-commitment hospital settings.
