Tumor microenvironment–specific nanomedicine: from biology-driven to multi-omics–guided precision engineering
Authors: Jee-Eun Hwang, Jiwoo Park, Hong-Sik Kim, Shengjun Li, Hyung-Jun Im
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The tumor microenvironment (TME) is a structurally and functionally heterogeneous ecosystem in which extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling, vascular–lymphatic dysfunction, hypoxia, acidosis, and spatially organized immunosuppressive networks jointly constrain nanoparticle accessibility, intratumoral distribution, and therapeutic efficacy. Conventional nanomedicine strategies based on passive accumulation, ligand-mediated targeting, and stimulus-responsive release have produced important advances, but they remain limited by empirical design principles that often overlook interpatient and intratumoral heterogeneity. Recent advances in single-cell profiling, spatial transcriptomics (ST), and complementary multi-omics technologies have revealed that the TME comprises regionally distinct stromal architectures, endothelial states, metabolic niches, proteolytic landscapes, and immune–stromal interaction networks. These datasets provide an opportunity to move beyond tumor-averaged descriptors by identifying cell-type-specific receptors, stromal and vascular barriers, spatially restricted release cues, and immune-excluded niches that govern nanoparticle transport, activation, and therapeutic response. In this review, we first summarize key TME barriers relevant to nanomedicine delivery, including ECM architecture, vascular pathophysiology, hypoxia, acidosis, and tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) dynamics. We then propose a closed-loop omics-to-design framework that translates spatially resolved biological features into actionable nanoparticle engineering parameters, including ligand selection, stromal-penetrating and barrier-modulating architectures, subtype-informed vascular delivery, and omics-defined responsive release strategies. Finally, we discuss the translational requirements for implementing this paradigm, including patient stratification, companion diagnostic development, chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC) considerations, regulatory pathways, and the practical shift from fully personalized formulations toward stratification-matched libraries of pre-validated nanomedicines. By combining TME biology, spatial multi-omics, functional engineering, and translational planning, this review outlines an integrated strategy for next-generation precision nanomedicine designed to overcome tumor heterogeneity and enhance therapeutic efficacy.

Background

The tumor microenvironment (TME) constitutes a complex and continuously evolving ecosystem in which malignant cells coexist with stromal components, infiltrating immune populations, extracellular matrix (ECM), and a dynamic milieu of soluble mediators [1, 2]. Rather than serving as a passive scaffold, it actively shapes tumor progression through reciprocal signaling between cancer and host cells [3, 4]. Structural abnormalities such as disorganized vasculature, dense desmoplastic stroma, and elevated interstitial fluid pressure (IFP) form substantial physical barriers that hinder the penetration of therapeutic agents [5]. At the same time, metabolic stressors including hypoxia and acidosis trigger adaptive programs that sustain tumor growth and reinforce therapeutic resistance [6]. The accumulation of immunosuppressive cell subsets such as tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), and regulatory T cells (Tregs) further establishes tolerogenic niches that attenuate antitumor immunity and reduce the likelihood that intratumoral delivery translates into durable therapeutic benefit [7, 8]. These interlinked biochemical and structural perturbations are particularly pronounced in immune-desert and desmoplastic tumors such as pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), glioblastoma (GBM), and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC), where physical inaccessibility and immune dysfunction converge to create formidable barriers to therapy [9–11]. Together, these pathological features highlight why a single, universal drug delivery strategy remains ineffective and emphasize the need for precision designs tailored to the unique architecture and dynamics of each TME [12, 13].

To overcome the multifaceted barriers imposed by the TME, a wide range of nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems (DDS) has been developed to enhance therapeutic precision [14]. Early strategies primarily relied on the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, allowing nanocarriers to passively accumulate within tumors through leaky vasculature [15]. However, variability in vascular permeability, IFP, and stromal density across tumor types often led to inconsistent outcomes and limited clinical translation [15, 16]. To address these limitations, active targeting approaches were introduced, employing monoclonal antibodies, peptides, aptamers, or small molecules to selectively engage tumor-associated receptors or stromal constituents [17, 18]. Representative targets include growth factor receptors such as epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2), and insulin-like growth factor 1 receptor (IGF1R) on tumor cells, integrins that mediate adhesion within angiogenic vasculature, fibroblast activation protein (FAP) expressed by cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs), and ECM components such as collagen and laminin receptors [19–21]. In parallel, immune-related targets such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), Toll-like receptor 9 (TLR9), and stimulator of interferon genes (STING) have been increasingly exploited to reprogram tumor-induced immunosuppression, enhance antigen presentation and stimulate effector T cell or natural killer (NK) cell responses for improved therapeutic efficacy [22, 23]. Stimuli-responsive nanocarriers were also designed to react to microenvironmental cues such as acidic pH, hypoxia, or elevated protease activity, enabling controlled payload release at the tumor site, while biomimetic systems using cell membrane-coated nanoparticles or immune cell-derived vesicles have leveraged endogenous homing attributes and immune-evasive properties to improve targeting specificity and biocompatibility [24–28]. Despite these advances, most design approaches remain predominantly empirical, guided by limited histological and phenotypic observations rather than comprehensive molecular and spatial insight [29, 30]. The pronounced heterogeneity of the TME, both between patients and within individual tumors, continues to hinder predictable drug distribution and therapeutic efficacy [31, 32]. These challenges call for a shift from intuition-driven formulation toward data-informed nanomedicine design grounded in high-resolution biological mapping and integrated omics analysis [33, 34].

Recent advances in single-cell, spatial, and complementary multi-omics profiling have substantially expanded our understanding of the TME by resolving its cellular, molecular, functional, and spatial heterogeneity at unprecedented resolution [35]. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables the dissection of cellular populations within the TME into distinct cell states, thereby revealing immune and stromal subtypes, candidate receptor landscapes, and intercellular signaling programs that may inform target discovery for nanomedicine-based DDS [36, 37]. Spatial transcriptomics (ST) further preserves tissue architecture while profiling gene expression, allowing these cellular and molecular features to be mapped onto spatially organized niches such as desmoplastic stromal boundaries, perivascular regions, hypoxic regions, immune-excluded interfaces, and tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) [38–40]. Together, these approaches shift the design problem from identifying isolated tumor-associated markers to defining spatially organized and functionally relevant TME niches that influence nanoparticle targeting, transport, penetration, payload release, and therapeutic response. However, transcriptome-centric profiling alone is insufficient to define fully actionable nanoparticle design parameters. Transcript abundance does not necessarily reflect protein-level receptor availability, enzymatic activity, metabolite concentration, membrane composition, or the functional accessibility of a target niche. Consequently, complementary proteomic, metabolomic, lipidomic, and epigenomic layers are increasingly needed to validate candidate targets, identify proteolytic or metabolic release cues, refine stromal and vascular barrier maps, and distinguish correlative molecular signatures from functionally relevant design variables [33, 41]. In this context, multi-omics datasets provide not only descriptive maps of TME heterogeneity but also a multidimensional biological blueprint for precision nanomedicine, linking cell-type-specific receptor landscapes, stromal and endothelial states, interaction interfaces, delivery-relevant barriers, and responsive trigger landscapes to nanoparticle design principles [42–46].

Building on this concept, the translation of spatially resolved TME information into nanoparticle engineering parameters is redefining cancer nanomedicine from an empirical formulation paradigm toward an iterative, design-oriented framework [47–51]. Rather than using omics data only for post hoc biological interpretation, we propose that an omics-guided workflow can support data-informed target and ligand discovery, spatial barrier mapping, penetration-enhancing design, omics-defined trigger selection, functional validation, parameterized nanoparticle engineering, and spatially resolved back-validation of intratumoral distribution. Together, these steps provide a structured route for translating TME heterogeneity into actionable nanoparticle design variables, including ligand selection, particle size and surface properties, barrier-modulating or penetration-oriented architectures, responsive linker chemistry, release mechanisms, and payload strategies [49–54].

The central message of this review is that cancer nanomedicine is moving beyond generalized TME-responsive strategies toward more precise, omics-informed engineering approaches. Several recent reviews have summarized TME-targeted nanomedicine from the perspectives of tumor abnormality-responsive design, immunomodulatory nanomaterials, or platform-based therapeutic strategies [55–57]. However, these studies have generally emphasized pathological stimuli, therapeutic modality, or nanocarrier type rather than the translational logic by which high-resolution biological data can be converted into engineering principles for nanoparticle design. In contrast, the present review integrates TME barrier biology, omics-guided nanoparticle engineering, and clinical translation into a design-oriented framework for precision nanomedicine. Rather than cataloging TME-targeted nanomedicines as isolated examples, we aim to provide a reusable conceptual framework for interpreting TME heterogeneity as a set of actionable design variables. Through this structure, we clarify how molecular and spatial features of the TME can inform biologically grounded drug delivery strategies while also addressing key translational considerations, including patient stratification, companion diagnostic feasibility, manufacturing scalability, and regulatory challenges relevant to clinical implementation. Intratumoral transport constraints and microenvironmental heterogeneity: limits of empirical nanomedicine design

Intratumoral structural and biochemical barriers to nanomedicine transport

Dense ECM as a primary physical barrier

ECM is a structurally and biochemically complex network composed of fibrous proteins, glycoproteins, proteoglycans, and hyaluronic acid (HA), which together regulate tissue architecture, hydration, and cell behavior [58–62]. In solid tumors, however, ECM homeostasis is profoundly dysregulated, resulting in abnormal accumulation, crosslinking, and spatial reorganization of matrix components [63, 64]. Compared with normal tissues, tumor ECM is typically denser, stiffer, and mechanically abnormal, and these changes are closely associated with tumor progression, metastasis, and poor clinical outcome [65–67] (Fig. 1A). Beyond its biological role in malignancy, this remodeled matrix also constitutes a major physical barrier to nanomedicine delivery.

Among the barriers that limit nanoparticle transport in solid tumors, remodeled ECM is particularly important because it directly restricts physical accessibility after vascular extravasation [45]. Excess deposition and crosslinking of collagen, fibronectin, laminin, proteoglycans, and HA reduce interstitial space, increase transport tortuosity, limit pore accessibility, and elevate resistance to both diffusion and convection [68, 69]. As a result, nanoparticle movement through tumor tissue becomes markedly constrained, leading to shallow penetration and highly uneven intratumoral distribution, as supported by studies showing improved penetration or intratumoral distribution after enzymatic depletion or remodeling of collagen- or hyaluronan-rich matrix barriers [70–73]. Importantly, this barrier is not uniform across the tumor mass. Desmoplastic stromal regions, invasive fronts, perivascular zones, and hypoxic or necrotic areas can differ substantially in collagen architecture, HA content, fiber alignment, and stiffness, such that nanoparticles encounter not a single stromal obstacle but a heterogeneous landscape of microregions with distinct transport properties [74–76]. This spatial variability has direct consequences for local penetration depth, intratumoral distribution, and drug accessibility [72, 77].

Such heterogeneity is dynamically generated through tumor–stroma interactions. CAFs are major regulators of matrix deposition, contractile remodeling, and reciprocal stromal signaling, thereby contributing to tumor survival, invasion, angiogenesis, and therapeutic resistance [78–81]. Importantly, CAF states are themselves spatially heterogeneous. Myofibroblastic CAFs (myCAFs), typically enriched adjacent to tumor cells, promote matrix deposition and stiffening, whereas inflammatory CAFs (iCAFs), more often located in distal stromal regions, preferentially support inflammatory and immune-regulatory programs [82–84] (Fig. 1B). This regional specialization contributes to localized differences in ECM rigidity and stromal function within the same lesion. Matrix stiffening is further reinforced by lysyl oxidase (LOX) family enzymes, which catalyze oxidative crosslinking of collagen and elastin and thereby promote desmoplastic remodeling [85–87] (Fig. 1B). In cancer, LOX-driven matrix stiffening is associated with aggressive phenotypes and poor clinical outcome across multiple tumor types [88–91]. The resulting rigid matrix can also promote integrin-dependent mechanotransduction, further sustaining stromal activation and tumor progression [87, 88, 92, 93]. As summarized in Fig. 1C, focal adhesions serve as integrated mechanotransductive units that couple ECM architecture to intracellular signaling responses [94–96]. In parallel, metalloproteinases, including matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), a disintegrin and metalloproteinases (ADAMs), and a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin motifs (ADAMTS), remodel ECM architecture and matrix-associated signaling in a spatially restricted manner [97–100], generating localized proteolytic niches that may partially relax matrix constraints while adjacent heavily crosslinked regions remain poorly penetrable [101–104] (Fig. 1B). Together, these processes create a mechanically and biochemically heterogeneous matrix environment that impedes interstitial transport and ultimately contributes to nonuniform nanoparticle accessibility and drug distribution within solid tumors.

This complexity has prompted the development of diverse ECM-oriented nanomedicine strategies aimed at improving intratumoral transport. Guo et al. developed the Tenascin-C-targeting CFH/OM-L platform, in which a CFH peptide enabled selective engagement of CAF-associated stromal regions, promoted receptor-mediated uptake, epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT), reduced collagen deposition, and remodeled the TME [105]. Sasaki et al. designed Dox-CBD-SA, a collagen-binding serum albumin–doxorubicin conjugate containing the A3 domain of von Willebrand factor (vWF), to exploit exposed collagen types I and III within tumor stroma as anchoring sites, thereby increasing stromal retention, enabling acidic drug release, and enhancing antitumor efficacy with immune activation [106]. Kanapathipillai et al. reported LOXAbNP, a LOX-directed nanoplatform that binds lysyl oxidase in the tumor ECM to inhibit collagen crosslinking, improve intratumoral antibody retention, and suppress tumor growth [107]. Collectively, these studies illustrate the dominant empirical design logic underlying current ECM-oriented nanomedicine: therapeutic benefit is pursued by targeting stromal cellular architects, binding selectively to abundant matrix components, or modulating matrix mechanics to improve retention and transport. Yet these advances also expose an important limitation of empirically driven design: because ECM composition, architecture, and remodeling activity are not uniformly distributed within the same lesion, broadly applicable ECM-targeted nanoplatforms may not operate with equal consistency across distinct intratumoral compartments [82, 108, 109].

This limitation is supported by studies directly demonstrating the spatial heterogeneity of tumor stroma. In PDAC, Öhlund et al. identified spatially segregated CAF populations, with myCAFs enriched adjacent to tumor cells and iCAFs occupying more distal regions, indicating that matrix-producing and inflammatory stromal programs are regionally organized within the same tumor [82]. In breast carcinoma, Conklin et al. showed that collagen organization varies substantially across tumor regions and identified perpendicular collagen alignment at the tumor boundary as a distinct and clinically adverse stromal architecture [110]. More recently, Arora et al. used ST to resolve tumors into core and edge compartments with distinct ECM-associated and stromal interaction programs, further supporting the view that stromal composition and function differ according to intratumoral location [111]. Taken together, these findings suggest that ECM is better understood not as a single desmoplastic entity, but as a heterogeneous landscape of localized matrix niches that differentially regulate nanoparticle penetration, anchoring, and drug accessibility (Fig. 1D). For nanomedicine design, this view implies that tumor ECM should be considered a regionally organized stromal framework that generates heterogeneous transport conditions within the same lesion, rather than a uniformly targetable feature of the TME. Distinct ECM-rich regions may differ in matrix architecture and stiffness, with important consequences for nanoparticle penetration and cellular accessibility [46, 112]. The central design challenge, therefore, is not simply to target ECM, but to identify which matrix contexts are functionally accessible and therapeutically actionable.

Vascular–lymphatic dysfunction and elevated interstitial fluid pressure

The tumor vasculature is a central determinant of nanoparticle accessibility because it governs whether circulating nanocarriers can extravasate, spread through the interstitium, and reach therapeutically relevant tumor regions. Unlike normal vessels, tumor blood vessels exhibit disorganized branching, irregular lumen diameters, unstable endothelial junctions, abnormal pericyte–endothelial association, and aberrant basement membrane architecture, collectively producing leaky but inefficient transport routes [113–115]. Endothelial surface structures such as the glycocalyx have also been implicated in vascular barrier function, although their tumor-specific status is less consistently defined in this context [116, 117]. In parallel, the tumor lymphatic system is often structurally and functionally compromised, with intratumoral lymphatic vessels frequently compressed, collapsed, or effectively nonfunctional, whereas patent drainage is often restricted to the tumor periphery [118–120]. Together, these vascular and lymphatic abnormalities elevate IFP, attenuate convective transport, and limit uniform nanoparticle redistribution after extravasation [121–124] (Fig. 2A). The vascular-lymphatic compartment should therefore be understood not simply as an entry route for nanomedicine, but as a structurally abnormal transport interface that actively determines where delivery is permitted and where it fails.

Importantly, these transport abnormalities are not spatially uniform within a single tumor. Tumor regions can differ substantially in perfusion, vessel functionality, permeability, and local transport conditions, generating marked differences in blood flow, residence time, extravasation efficiency, and interstitial redistribution [125, 126]. Endothelial cells themselves also display contextual heterogeneity, with distinct angiogenic and mesenchymal-like programs [127, 128], as well as specialized endothelial states such as high endothelial venule (HEV)-like or TLS-associated programs [129, 130], together with more quiescent arterial-, venous-, or capillary-like endothelial phenotypes that may differ in local vascular function and baseline barrier properties, thereby potentially shaping microregional nanoparticle access and distribution [131, 132] (Fig. 2B). Vascular accessibility should therefore not be treated as a global tumor property but as a microregional one, and tumor-averaged descriptors such as “leaky vasculature” may overestimate functional delivery coverage. This intratumoral heterogeneity provides a mechanistic explanation for why passive accumulation based on the enhanced EPR effect often yields inconsistent results across tumors and even across regions within the same tumor.

Early passive nanomedicine largely relied on the EPR effect under the assumption that leaky tumor vasculature would broadly support nanoparticle accumulation. To improve selectivity beyond passive accumulation, ligand-guided vascular targeting strategies were subsequently introduced. For example, Qi et al. further extended this logic by simultaneously targeting αvβ3 integrin through arginine-glycine-aspartate (RGD) and the lactoferrin receptor in the RGD-Lf-LP-DTX platform to enhance receptor-specific uptake, blood-brain barrier transport, and docetaxel delivery [133]. Zhang et al. applied an RGD-mediated targeting strategy in M(a)D@PI-PEG-RGD while additionally incorporating pH-sensitive and near-infrared-assisted drug release [134]. In parallel, Jiang et al. designed CREKA-Lipo-Dox to target fibrin-fibronectin complexes present in ECM and vasculature, thereby exploiting the vessel-associated matrix interface as a niche for selective doxorubicin accumulation and deposition [135]. Collectively, these studies illustrate how empirical vascular targeting strategies have attempted to convert leaky vasculature, angiogenic endothelial receptors, and vessel-associated matrix components into actionable delivery handles for nanomedicine.

However, several imaging, transport, and biodistribution studies suggest that these strategies remain constrained by the spatial heterogeneity of vascular access. Torosean et al. showed that nanoparticle uptake across different tumor models was associated not with vascularity alone, but with the interplay of vessel density, collagen content, and IFP, with collagen-rich and high-pressure tumors showing lower nanoparticle uptake overall [136]. Stapleton et al. further demonstrated that intratumoral liposome accumulation correlated positively with regional perfusion parameters measured by dynamic contrast-enhanced computed tomography (DCE-CT), indicating that delivery is governed by spatially restricted perfused territories rather than by tumor-level vascular averages [137]. Consistent with this, Chauhan et al. found that vascular normalization improved the delivery of smaller nanoparticles while not improving, and in some cases hindering, the delivery of larger ones, showing that the simplistic assumption that leakier vessels universally favor nanomedicine delivery is incomplete and highly size dependent [138]. Importantly, limitations are also evident for active vascular targeting. Kunjachan et al. reported, in the context of ~ 10 nm polymeric nanocarriers, that RGD- and asparagine-glycine-arginine (NGR)-mediated vascular targeting enhanced early binding to tumor blood vessels, but passive targeting ultimately resulted in higher overall tumor levels and more efficient retention [139]. More broadly, limitations are also evident for active nanomedicine targeting beyond the vascular compartment. Kirpotin et al. showed that antibody-targeted anti-HER2 immunoliposomes increased tumor-cell internalization without increasing total tumor localization, indicating that ligand-mediated cellular engagement does not necessarily translate into greater whole-tumor accumulation [140]. Together, these findings suggest that empirical vascular targeting can improve access to selected delivery-permissive regions, but cannot by itself resolve the broader constraints imposed by heterogeneous perfusion, interstitial pressure, stromal resistance, and incomplete coupling between ligand binding and whole-tumor accumulation [136–140]. This shifts the design question from simply asking what to target to asking where targetable states emerge, how stably they are maintained, and whether they coincide with regions that are permissive for nanoparticle transport.

Hypoxia as a spatially heterogeneous biochemical barrier

Hypoxia in solid tumors should be understood not as a uniform microenvironmental condition, but as a spatially and temporally heterogeneous biochemical barrier that emerges within a single lesion from uneven perfusion, limited oxygen diffusion, and region-specific metabolic demand imposed by structurally abnormal and functionally unstable tumor vasculature [141–147]. While normal tissues typically maintain oxygen levels of approximately 5–8% (partial pressure 40–60 mmHg), oxygen concentrations in solid tumors frequently fall below 1% (partial pressure < 10 mmHg) in poorly perfused regions [148]. Because tumor oxygenation is governed by both distance from functional blood vessels and instability of tumor blood flow, intratumoral hypoxia arises through at least two overlapping processes: chronic, diffusion-limited hypoxia in regions distal to perfused vessels, and acute or cycling hypoxia caused by transient perfusion failure [142–147]. As a result, tumors are partitioned into heterogeneous microregions, including relatively oxygenated perivascular areas, intermittently hypoxic territories, and severely hypoxic peri-necrotic compartments [146, 147, 149] (Fig. 3A). These spatially distinct compartments are likely to impose different constraints on therapeutic delivery and response [150–152].

This intratumoral heterogeneity is highly consequential for nanomedicine because hypoxic niches often coincide with regions that are already poorly accessible owing to abnormal perfusion, elevated interstitial resistance, and limited drug redistribution after extravasation [118, 151, 153]. Hypoxia therefore acts not only as a marker of metabolic stress, but also as a functional determinant of treatment failure layered onto primary limitations in physical accessibility. In these regions, stabilization of hypoxia-inducible factor-1α (HIF-1α) promotes glycolytic reprogramming, angiogenic signaling, and survival pathways that support adaptation to oxygen deprivation and reinforce aggressive phenotypes [141, 154–158]. At the same time, hypoxia reshapes the local immune and metabolic landscape by inducing immunosuppressive mediators such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β), PD-L1, lactate, and adenosine, thereby suppressing antitumor immune activity and establishing regionally immunosuppressive niches [159–164] (Fig. 3B). Consequently, intratumoral oxygen heterogeneity is associated with localized differences not only in oxygen availability, but also in metabolic state, immune suppression, and therapeutic vulnerability across the same tumor mass [152, 165, 166].

From a drug delivery perspective, these region-specific effects make hypoxic tumor compartments especially difficult to treat [167–174]. Oxygen-dependent modalities such as radiotherapy, photodynamic therapy, and sonodynamic therapy are directly compromised because insufficient oxygen limits reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation, whereas oxygen-independent therapies may also be attenuated because hypoxic cells often exhibit slow proliferation, stress-adapted survival programs, and reduced drug sensitivity [168–173]. Importantly, nanoparticle accumulation at the whole-tumor level does not necessarily translate into effective payload access to all intratumoral compartments, particularly when hypoxic zones reside in poorly perfused or diffusion-limited regions [118, 150, 175] (Fig. 3C). Hypoxia should therefore be viewed as a compounding intratumoral barrier that amplifies resistance and produces spatially variable treatment response.

This complexity has motivated extensive empirical efforts to develop nanomedicine platforms that either alleviate tumor hypoxia or exploit hypoxia-associated biological features for therapeutic gain. For example, Wang et al. reported a mitochondria-targeted lonidamine liposome (TPP-LND@Lip) that alleviated hypoxia by suppressing oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS)-mediated oxygen consumption, thereby enhancing radiotherapy-induced ROS generation while concurrently downregulating PD-L1 expression [176]. Li et al. developed a dual hypoxia-targeting small interfering RNA (siRNA) nanoplatform that combined hypoxia-responsive nanoparticle disassembly with silencing of cell division cycle 20 (CDC20), a hypoxia-correlated protumorigenic gene, thereby enabling efficient gene silencing and tumor suppression in breast cancer [177]. Budi et al. engineered multifunctional superparamagnetic iron oxide nanoparticle (SPION)-based nanocarriers for dual silencing of HIF-1α and signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3), demonstrating that simultaneous disruption of hypoxia- and inflammation-associated signaling more effectively suppressed tumor progression and improved antitumor immunity than single-target approaches [178]. Collectively, these studies illustrate the major empirical design logic underlying current hypoxia-oriented nanomedicine and suggest that therapeutic benefit can be achieved through modulation of oxygen consumption, hypoxia-responsive delivery and gene silencing, and inhibition of hypoxia-adaptive and inflammation-associated signaling. However, these advances also reveal an important limitation of empirically driven design: because tumor hypoxia is spatially heterogeneous and can fluctuate over time within the same lesion, hypoxia-targeted nanoplatforms may not operate with equal consistency across distinct intratumoral compartments [179–181].

This concern is supported by several studies illustrating the spatial and temporal heterogeneity of intratumoral hypoxia. In head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC), pimonidazole-based analyses revealed marked regional variation in hypoxic binding and vascular architecture within tumors, and subsequent clinical studies linked pimonidazole binding and tumor vascularity to treatment outcome, supporting the view that hypoxia-related treatment resistance is unlikely to be uniformly distributed across the lesion [179, 182]. In xenograft models, sequential administration of two hypoxia markers showed that a measurable fraction of tumor cells experienced transient hypoxia, providing direct evidence that hypoxia can fluctuate over time within the same tumor [180]. In glioblastoma, pseudopalisading cells surrounding necrotic foci represent a classical histologic example of region-specific hypoxia associated with local vaso-occlusive pathology, forming hypoxic and actively migratory niches distinct from better-perfused tumor areas [183, 184]. More recently, ST analyses have shown that individual tumors can be resolved into spatially distinct compartments or niches with divergent hypoxia-related transcriptional and immune-regulatory programs [44, 185]. Together, these findings indicate that hypoxia is not a single averaged tumor property, but a mosaic of spatially and temporally distinct ecological niches within the same tumor. For nanomedicine design, hypoxia should therefore be treated as an intratumoral design variable rather than a generalized TME hallmark, as distinct hypoxic compartments may impose different constraints on particle delivery, activation, and payload efficacy.

Acidosis as a spatially heterogeneous biochemical barrier

Solid tumors commonly maintain a mildly acidic extracellular milieu, typically around pH 6.5–6.8, relative to the near-neutral pH of most normal tissues [186]. This acidification arises from the coupled effects of malignant metabolic rewiring and abnormal tissue architecture. Cancer cells preferentially rely on aerobic glycolysis and produce excess lactate, while hypoxia, poor perfusion, and inefficient waste clearance further favor proton accumulation [187–189]. To preserve intracellular pH homeostasis despite this metabolic burden, tumor cells upregulate proton pumps, Na+/H+ exchangers, monocarboxylate transporters, and carbonic anhydrases that extrude protons and lactate or facilitate bicarbonate handling [187–189]. As a result, the TME becomes acidic even while intracellular pH is maintained within a comparatively permissive range. This extracellular acidosis is not merely a metabolic byproduct. It promotes matrix remodeling, protease activity, invasion, immune dysfunction, and reduced efficacy of several anticancer drugs, while also providing an attractive pathological cue for nanomedicine design because it is more disease-associated than many generic physicochemical triggers [186, 190]. In this context, extracellular acidosis functions not only as an exploitable trigger but also as a biochemical barrier, because it can reduce the cellular uptake and efficacy of weakly basic drugs through ion trapping while also narrowing the effective activation window and tumor selectivity of pH-responsive nanocarriers [191–193]. In this sense, low pH should be viewed as both a driver of malignant progression and a conditional design handle for selective drug release (Fig. 4A).

Importantly, however, tumor acidosis is not spatially uniform within a single lesion. Microregional differences in perfusion, oxygenation, and metabolic activity generate uneven extracellular pH (pHe) landscapes across tumors [194–196]. Regions near functional vessels may remain less acidic than poorly perfused, hypoxic, or densely packed territories, and these gradients can shift further during treatment [194–196]. Accordingly, acidic tumor pH is better understood as a spatial gradient than as a tumor-wide constant. This spatial view is supported by in vivo nanosonophore-assisted multispectral optoacoustic imaging, which enabled quantitative mapping of tumor pHe as a resolved feature across the lesion rather than as a single bulk value [194]. In glioma, intratumoral and peritumoral pHe can likewise be visualized as spatially varying and therapy-responsive parameters [195]. Tumor acidity therefore represents a regional microenvironmental state that must be interpreted in spatial context rather than as a uniformly distributed hallmark (Fig. 4B).

These features have nevertheless inspired a broad range of empirical pH-responsive nanomedicine designs. One representative strategy is to use mildly acidic pHe to trigger selective destabilization or drug release. For example, Park et al. developed folate receptor beta (FRβ)-targeted pH-sensitive liposomes for non-small cell lung cancer that combined folate-mediated targeting with acid-responsive drug release under mildly acidic conditions [197]. A second common strategy is to exploit acid-labile linkers that remain stable during circulation but are cleaved in acidic tumor regions. Zhao et al. reported a multistage pH-responsive codelivery liposomal platform, D-Lsi-DTX, that used imine bond cleavage in mildly acidic conditions to destabilize the outer structure and facilitate subsequent release of siRNA and docetaxel [198]. A third strategy is to exploit structure-switchable or size-transformable assemblies that disassemble under acidic conditions. Ye et al. reported a DNA-programmed gold nanoparticle assembly in which pH-sensitive triplex DNA promoted disassembly and size reduction under mildly acidic conditions, thereby improving tumor penetration, while a separate telomerase-responsive module mediated cancer-specific intracellular drug release [199]. A fourth strategy is to couple acidity sensing with enhanced membrane interaction or tumor penetration. Zhang et al. reported pH (low) insertion peptide (pHLIP)-modified mesoporous organosilica nanoparticles for acidic TME-targeted delivery, using pH-dependent membrane insertion behavior to enhance tumor-directed transport [200]. Collectively, these studies illustrate the central logic of empirical low-pH-responsive design, where tumor acidosis serves as an enabling signal for selective destabilization, linker cleavage, structural transformation, membrane insertion, or progressive release, often in combination with a receptor ligand or secondary trigger to improve apparent specificity.

However, several lines of evidence suggest that purely empirical pH-responsive design remains intrinsically limited if it relies on bulk acidity alone. First, the relevant pH window is narrow, as the difference between blood pH and tumor pHe is often modest and therefore makes reliable activation difficult without premature release or incomplete triggering [196, 201]. Second, this differential is heterogeneous within and across tumors, meaning that a formulation tuned to one trigger pH may underperform in large fractions of the lesion [194–196]. Third, mild acidity is not uniquely tumor-specific, because other pathological settings, including inflammatory and ischemic tissues, can also become acidic, raising the risk of off-target activation [192, 193, 201]. At the translational level, imaging reviews have noted that there is currently no established clinical tool for routinely mapping the spatial distribution of tumor pHe [202]. These concerns have already been recognized in the pH-responsive drug delivery literature, including the challenge of defining a universal trigger threshold for systemic therapy [190, 193, 201]. More recent studies sharpen this point. Quader et al. improved efficacy by designing a nanomedicine that progressively released drug by correctly sensing heterogeneous tumor pHe rather than assuming a uniform acidic field [196], whereas Rao et al. showed that intratumoral pHe can shift with therapy, underscoring that acidity is not only heterogeneous but dynamically remodeled [195]. Finally, Ye et al. recently argued that single-gate stimuli-responsive systems are inadequate for differentiating tumor versus normal tissue signals and therefore developed an AND-gated nanoparticle requiring both acidic pH and hypoxia, underscoring the limitations of bulk pH alone as a precision trigger [203]. Taken together, these observations suggest that low pH is an important but incomplete design variable. The next question is no longer simply whether a nanocarrier can respond to acidity, but which acidic niches are functionally relevant, which co-states they overlap with, and whether those combined states are stable and specific enough to support precision design (Fig. 4C).

The complex role of the immune microenvironment in nanomedicine delivery and distribution

Context-dependent immune niches as indirect constraints on nanomedicine efficacy

The relevance of immunosuppressive niches to nanomedicine is best understood in terms of how they shape the therapeutic consequences of delivery after nanoparticles reach tumor tissue [204, 205]. In this setting, the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) does not merely accompany drug delivery as background context, but actively determines whether delivered payloads encounter conditions that support antigen presentation, effector-cell activation, and durable tumor killing, or instead enter regions in which suppression, exhaustion, and non-responsiveness dominate [204, 205]. This distinction is especially important for nanomedicine platforms whose efficacy depends not only on physical access to the tumor but also on local immune competence [204, 206]. Immunosuppressive niches therefore act primarily as indirect constraints on efficacy because they regulate whether intratumoral delivery can be translated into sustained therapeutic benefit [204–206].

This problem becomes more pronounced when the marked intratumoral heterogeneity of the immune microenvironment is taken into account. Immune-inflamed, immune-excluded, and immune-desert phenotypes reflect distinct spatial and functional states of immune engagement within the same tumor [207, 208] (Fig. 5A). In inflamed regions, abundant cytotoxic lymphocytes and active immune signaling may create a more permissive context for immune-modulatory therapies [208, 209]. By contrast, excluded regions often contain immune cells that remain confined to stromal or peripheral compartments, whereas desert-like regions lack sufficient priming and chemokine support for effective immune activation [207, 208]. Tissue-specific immune structures such as TLS, perivascular immune neighborhoods, and macrophage-rich perivascular niches further illustrate how microregional spatial organization can shape distinct local immune states [210–212]. As a result, tumor-averaged descriptors of the immune microenvironment may overestimate the likelihood that a delivered payload will encounter conditions capable of supporting therapeutic amplification [204, 205, 208–212]. This helps explain why one-size-fits-all immune-targeting strategies frequently show variable performance across tumors and across distinct immune contexts within the TME [206–208].

Within these heterogeneous immune contexts, distinct suppressive cell populations and dysfunctional immune states can further limit the extent to which intratumoral delivery is translated into durable therapeutic benefit. Treg suppress antitumor immunity through cytokine- and metabolic mechanisms [213–215] (Fig. 5B). MDSCs further constrain effector T-cell function through nutrient depletion and oxidative stress [216] (Fig. 5C). TAMs likewise reinforce immune suppression through checkpoint-related, cytokine-mediated, and stromal-supportive programs [217, 218] (Fig. 5D). Immunoediting, tumor-cell-intrinsic immune escape, and defective antigen presentation reduce the likelihood that delivered agents will generate durable responses, while T-cell exhaustion, metabolic competition, hypoxia or nutrient stress, and matrix-mediated immune restriction can further blunt therapeutic amplification within the tumor bed [219–226] (Fig. 5E).

To address these challenges, a range of empirical nanomedicine strategies has been developed to target or reprogram immunosuppressive niches. Wang et al. designed inflammation-responsive DNA nano-cocoons for the programmed co-delivery of anti-PD-1 antibody and cytosine-phosphate-guanine oligodeoxynucleotide (CpG), thereby localizing immune stimulation to the postsurgical inflammatory setting and prolonging survival in relapse models [22]. Dane et al. subsequently showed that tumor-penetrating PEG-lipid nanodiscs carrying a STING agonist enabled broad intratumoral distribution and primed robust anticancer immunity, illustrating how nanocarrier design can support local innate immune activation within tumors [23]. In the myeloid compartment, Glass et al. reported macrophage association and anti-tumor effects of mannose-decorated nanoparticles in ovarian cancer models, supporting TAM-directed reprogramming as an empirical design strategy [227]. In parallel, Ou et al. developed tLyp1-conjugated hybrid nanoparticles to target intratumoral Tregs, and in combination with anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4) blockade this approach was associated with reduced intratumoral Treg abundance, enhanced cluster of differentiation 8-positive (CD8+) T-cell responses, and improved tumor control [228]. Taken together, these representative studies show that empirical immune-targeting designs have sought to exploit checkpoint-, macrophage-, and Treg-associated suppressive states as therapeutic intervention points for nanomedicine [22, 23, 228].

However, these approaches remain limited because immune niches are spatially uneven, functionally divergent, and temporally dynamic [204–206, 208, 209]. Arlauckas et al. showed by intravital imaging that anti-PD-1 antibodies initially engaged PD-1+ CD8+ T cells but were then rapidly captured from the T-cell surface by TAMs through Fcγ receptor-dependent processes, thereby shortening productive target engagement and revealing an immune-cell-mediated resistance pathway [7]. Kumagai et al. further demonstrated that in highly glycolytic tumors, lactic acid promoted PD-1 expression in Tregs through monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1)-dependent uptake, and PD-1 blockade could therefore also reinvigorate these PD-1+ Tregs and, in some contexts, limit therapeutic benefit [8]. These findings underscore that local immune and metabolic context can invert or blunt the intended effects of immune-modulatory interventions even after tumor access is achieved [7, 8]. At the same time, the immune compartment should not be viewed as uniformly hostile to nanomedicine. Zheng et al. showed that apoptotic body-encapsulated nanomedicines could be phagocytosed by inflammatory Ly-6 C+ monocytes, which then infiltrated the tumor center and enhanced intratumoral accumulation [229]. Su et al. likewise demonstrated that photoinduced inflammation could recruit neutrophils that transported paclitaxel nanocrystals into tumors, thereby improving antitumor efficacy without abolishing neutrophil infiltration [230]. These examples are especially important because they show that immune-cell-mediated nanoparticle hitchhiking can coexist with immune suppression and may, under specific conditions, turn immune populations into trafficking partners rather than only barriers or sinks [229, 230]. Simply targeting immunosuppressive niches is therefore not sufficient by itself. Progress will require a more spatially and functionally resolved framework capable of distinguishing which immune states should be inhibited from those that should be preserved, redirected, or even leveraged for intratumoral drug action [204–206, 208–212].

Intratumoral dynamic and adaptive pathological barriers to nanomedicine transport

Temporal evolution of the tumor microenvironment

The TME is not a static background against which nanomedicines operate, but a continuously evolving transport landscape [69, 231]. As tumors progress, cellular composition, matrix architecture, vascular function, and interstitial mechanics are progressively reorganized, thereby altering both nanoparticle accessibility and regional drug permissiveness [65, 118, 231]. This temporal dimension is important because many empirical nanomedicine designs are optimized against a static pathological snapshot. Accordingly, temporal evolution is better understood not as a wholly distinct barrier category, but as a continuous remodeling process that reshapes the configuration, severity, and spatial distribution of pre-existing transport barriers within the tumor. This view is supported by accumulating evidence across multiple tumor types. In PDAC, malignant progression is associated with dynamic changes in tumor-infiltrating immune cells, pancreatic stellate cells, and fibroblast-related stromal components, supporting the idea that transport-relevant properties of the TME may shift during disease progression [232, 233]. Complementing this view, integrative single-cell analyses further indicate that pancreatic cancer subtypes are reinforced by coordinated TME programs linked to stromal activation states and immune composition [234]. Comparable progression-associated remodeling has also been described in other tumor contexts, including breast cancer, where the transition toward invasive disease is accompanied by stromal reorganization [235], and lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), where progression from ground-glass or early-stage lesions toward more advanced disease is associated with shifts in multicellular ecotypes, stromal organization, and immune contexture [236].

From a transport perspective, these observations support a model of continuous barrier drift, in which evolving vascular dysfunction, matrix densification, and immune reprogramming progressively alter how nanoparticles enter, move through, and are retained within the tumor. Functionally, tumor progression often reinforces ECM organization, elevates interstitial constraints, compresses vascular function, and worsens both convective and diffusive transport, thereby reducing uniform penetration and biasing nanoparticle accumulation toward more accessible peripheral or perivascular regions [118, 136, 231, 237]. Intratumoral heterogeneity should therefore be understood not only as a matter of spatial variation, but also as a temporally shifting distribution of transport-favorable and transport-restrictive regions. Temporal evolution is thus not merely a descriptive feature of tumor biology, but a delivery-relevant process that progressively shifts the balance among permeability, retention, stromal hindrance, and local target accessibility over the course of lesion progression [65, 118, 231, 238]. This process-oriented perspective provides the conceptual basis for distinguishing continuous temporal remodeling from the treatment- and stage-associated transport states discussed in the following sections.

Treatment-induced microenvironmental remodeling as an adaptive transport barrier

A second layer of complexity arises because therapy does not simply act on the tumor; it also remodels the TME that subsequent nanomedicines must traverse [239–241]. Cytotoxic therapy, radiotherapy, anti-angiogenic treatment, and stromal interventions can each alter vascular integrity, matrix organization, cytokine signaling, and immune-cell distribution [239, 241–243]. These effects may transiently improve transport in some settings, but they can also generate adaptive barriers that reduce later delivery. Treatment-induced remodeling is therefore better framed not as a separate barrier type, but as an intervention-driven process that reconfigures the vascular, stromal, and immune conditions governing subsequent nanomedicine transport. Mechanistically, therapy can reshape the TME in several nonexclusive ways. Radiation can activate TGF-β-associated fibrotic programs that increase matrix deposition and stiffness, thereby creating conditions less permissive for later penetration [244–246]. Therapy-induced inflammatory repair responses may also reprogram fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and myeloid populations, leading to altered vessel permeability, uneven perfusion recovery, and new spatial gradients of stress signaling [246–248]. At the same time, selected interventions can transiently normalize vascular and stromal abnormalities, indicating that treatment-induced remodeling is not uniformly detrimental [138, 249–251]. Rather, its transport consequences depend on timing, sequence, and local tissue context. Prior therapy should therefore be treated as a delivery-relevant variable, because it may either create a transient window of improved access or leave behind a remodeled residual niche with shallow penetration and regionally uneven payload exposure.

This adaptive view is supported by evidence at both the functional transport and molecular microenvironmental levels. In breast cancer models, vascular normalization after vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2) blockade increased transvascular flux for smaller nanomedicines during a finite normalization window, whereas larger nanoparticles derived limited benefit, indicating that therapy can impose new size-dependent constraints on subsequent delivery [138]. Similarly, in glioma models, therapeutic remodeling of the vasculature and ECM improved nanoparticle delivery and intratumoral distribution by establishing a remodeled transport state with altered perfusion and matrix properties, rather than simply removing pre-existing barriers [239]. Complementing these functional studies, human molecular profiling further suggests that therapy can generate biologically distinct post-treatment microenvironments rather than simply debulking the original lesion. In PDAC, spatial transcriptomic analysis has revealed therapy-associated remodeling of stromal, immune, and intercellular signaling programs, while matched pre- and post-treatment hormone receptor-positive (HR-positive) breast cancers likewise exhibit chemotherapy-associated immune and transcriptional reprogramming [252, 253]. Together, these findings support the view that post-treatment lesions may represent adaptively reconfigured transport environments in which the dominant constraints on subsequent nanomedicine delivery are qualitatively altered rather than merely reduced. For nanomedicine design, the key implication is that treatment history is itself part of the transport landscape. A formulation optimized in a treatment-naïve model cannot be assumed to behave similarly after chemotherapy, radiotherapy, anti-angiogenic therapy, or stromal modulation, because prior intervention may already have rewritten vascular patency, matrix architecture, immune-cell localization, and stress-response signaling. This helps explain why designs derived from static pretreatment models often translate poorly to advanced clinical settings. Accordingly, subsequent nanomedicine design should take into account the remodeled barrier state created by prior therapy, rather than relying solely on pretreatment assumptions.

Disease stage–specific niche remodeling and transport permissiveness

Beyond continuous temporal evolution and treatment-induced remodeling, discrete disease stages may also present markedly different transport logic. Early, intermediate, and advanced tumors may present distinct stromal and vascular transport conditions, including differences in matrix abundance, IFP, and vessel permeability, such that the same nanoparticle properties are unlikely to be optimal across all stages [136, 238, 254]. Disease stage should therefore be understood not merely as a clinical descriptor of tumor burden, but as a practical surrogate for a distinct transport niche. In this sense, stage-specific remodeling does not simply make delivery progressively harder; rather, it reshapes which barriers dominate, which routes remain accessible, and which design features are most likely to succeed at a given point in disease progression [12, 68, 255]. Recent work directly supports this view. In an orthotopic breast cancer model, stage-dependent differences in stromal looseness, IFP, and receptor expression were associated with distinct nanoparticle delivery behaviors, such that smaller particles were favored in earlier lesions with relatively permissive transport conditions, whereas larger ligand-modified particles could perform better in more advanced tumors through stronger multivalent interactions [256]. Additional studies extend this principle by showing that progression can also create anatomically and biologically distinct delivery niches beyond the primary tumor. In orthotopic colorectal cancer (CRC) models, metastatic lymph node lesions represent such a niche, as a multistage delivery system can first accumulate in the primary tumor and then release smaller particles that more effectively penetrate the tissue and drain through tumor lymphatics to reach metastatic nodes. This finding suggests that progression does not simply intensify a single transport barrier, but can redistribute delivery constraints across spatially distinct disease compartments that require sequential or niche-matched design logic [257]. Together, these observations argue that stage-linked niche remodeling alters not only the severity of transport restriction, but also the relative importance of penetration and regional accessibility. This stage-dependent shift in dominant transport constraints has direct implications for how nanomedicine design should be prioritized across the disease continuum. The TME should not be treated as a single uniformly targetable entity across the disease continuum. Instead, each stage presents a different barrier–permissiveness profile and therefore a different engineering problem. Earlier or less fibrotic lesions may favor strategies optimized for deep penetration, low-pressure traversal, and broader intralesional distribution, whereas more advanced and receptor-rich desmoplastic tumors may require stronger multivalent retention, barrier-modulating components, or sequential delivery logic to overcome matrix-dominated restriction [256, 257]. Framed this way, disease stage becomes not just a pathological descriptor but a delivery-relevant variable that should guide particle size selection, binding strategy, release behavior, and niche-matched functionality. This progression-aware perspective supports the need for nanomedicine design strategies matched to the dominant transport constraints of each disease stage.

Collectively, these structural, biochemical, immunological, and dynamically evolving transport constraints indicate that the TME cannot be treated as a single averaged design space, but must instead be resolved into distinct and actionable pathological states if nanomedicine is to be rationally matched to the barriers that govern delivery and response. These complexities also help explain the limitations of empirical nanomedicine design, which has often relied on generalized assumptions about the TME rather than on the molecular and spatial determinants of delivery-relevant niches. This need provides the rationale for omics-guided design frameworks that can define, stratify, and prioritize delivery-relevant microenvironmental states for precision nanomedicine engineering.

From omics insights to executable design principles for precision nanomedicine in the TME

Omics-to-design translation framework: a closed-loop workflow

A systematic framework that translates high-dimensional omics readouts into quantitative nanoparticle design parameters has not yet been widely consolidated into an actionable workflow in the field [258]. Conventional TME-responsive nanomedicines have largely followed an empirical trial-and-error paradigm, in which a broadly expressed TME cue such as acidic pH or an overexpressed MMP is selected from the literature, incorporated into a responsive material design, and then evaluated in one or a limited number of tumor models [259, 260]. This broader empirical logic is also consistent with the classical EPR framework, which encouraged the view that tumor selectivity could be achieved by leveraging shared pathophysiologic features of tumor tissues [261]. Although this strategy has been productive, it is fundamentally constrained by its disconnection from the patient- and region-specific molecular reality of the TME, as underscored by the preceding discussion of intratumoral heterogeneity. Multi-omics technologies, including scRNA-seq, ST, proteomics, and metabolomics, now provide the resolution needed to address this gap, yet their translation into a systematic and actionable nanomedicine design pipeline remains incomplete [49, 50]. Here, we propose a minimal but actionable closed-loop workflow, schematized in Fig. 6, that defines a continuous path from tissue acquisition to spatially validated nanoparticle delivery and provides an organizing framework for the omics-guided design logic developed across Sect. "Target discovery in a heterogeneous landscape" to "Beyond transcriptome-centric design: complementary multi-omics layers and translational challenges for DDS engineering".

Steps 1 and 2: sample acquisition and multimodal omics profiling with spatial stratification

Before initiating new tissue acquisition, investigators can first survey existing public datasets to determine whether resources relevant to the tumor type, profiling modality, and biological or clinical context are already available. These resources can be obtained from general repositories such as GEO, SRA, and ArrayExpress/BioStudies, or from cancer-focused portals such as the NCI Genomic Data Commons/TCGA and the Human Tumor Atlas Network [262–266]. Single-cell and spatial data portals, including CZ CELLxGENE Discover and STOmicsDB, can further support the discovery and reuse of cell-resolved or spatially resolved datasets [267, 268]. When such public resources are unavailable, incomplete, limited in sample size, or do not provide the required spatial resolution, clinical annotation, or disease context, the prospective workflow proceeds with tumor tissue acquisition through surgical resection, core-needle biopsy, or, where feasible, multi-region sampling to capture spatial heterogeneity, followed by multimodal omics profiling to generate a comprehensive molecular atlas of the TME (Fig. 6, Steps 1 and 2). At the cellular level, scRNA-seq resolves the TME into discrete populations, including cancer and stromal cells as well as immune subsets, and identifies their transcriptional programs, putative target-gene expression patterns, and intercellular signaling networks [269–272]. However, scRNA-seq alone cannot determine how these populations are spatially arranged within intact tissue architecture [273]. Spatial transcriptomic platforms address this limitation by mapping gene expression onto native tissue coordinates, thereby revealing how cell types and molecular programs are compartmentalized into functionally distinct niches, including desmoplastic barriers, immune-excluded boundaries, perivascular regions, and hypoxic cores. Current technologies include sequencing-based platforms such as 10x Visium and Visium HD, which provide broad whole-transcriptome spatial profiling, as well as imaging-based in situ platforms such as Xenium, Atera, MERFISH/MERSCOPE, and Bruker CosMx SMI, which resolve transcript localization at single-cell to subcellular resolution [34, 39, 274–276]. Notably, Atera, recently introduced by 10x Genomics, extends in situ spatial biology toward high-throughput whole-transcriptome analysis with single-cell sensitivity, whereas Bruker CosMx SMI provides subcellular whole-transcriptome spatial imaging and same-cell multi-omics capabilities [277, 278]. Beyond transcriptomic mapping, Step 2 can further integrate spatial proteomics, such as co-detection by indexing (CODEX) or MIBI-TOF, and spatial metabolomics, such as MALDI-MSI, where available [43, 271, 279]. This multimodal atlas can capture not only transcriptional identity but also the functional state and biochemical milieu of each niche, thereby providing a richer design substrate than any single modality alone. Regardless of the exact modality combination, this stage generates a spatially resolved TME map that stratifies the tumor into actionable niches defined by design-relevant molecular, structural, and functional features, including candidate receptor expression patterns, intercellular interaction programs, delivery-relevant barrier architectures, protease- or metabolism-related signatures for responsive release, and, when available, protein- or metabolite-level measurements from complementary omics modalities.

Step 3: functional-layer confirmation

Even when omics-derived features appear highly promising, they remain correlative unless functionally validated, a step that remains inconsistently implemented in the transition from sequencing data to nanoparticle synthesis [280]. Transcriptomic overexpression of a surface receptor does not guarantee protein-level accessibility, ligand-binding competence, or relevance to nanoparticle uptake. Post-transcriptional regulation, protein turnover, and steric occlusion by the glycocalyx or ECM can all decouple mRNA abundance from actual surface availability [281]. Functional confirmation therefore represents an indispensable intermediate step (Fig. 6, Step 3), in which candidate targets and microenvironmental features are validated through orthogonal experimental approaches. At the molecular level, orthogonal spatial validation of transcriptomically identified targets can be performed using multiplex immunofluorescence (mIF), immunohistochemistry (IHC), or high-resolution in situ expression platforms on serial tissue Sects [282, 283]. At the functional level, three-dimensional (3D) tumor models, including patient-derived organoids, multicellular spheroids incorporating stromal and immune components, and ex vivo tumor slice cultures, allow direct assessment of whether candidate receptors mediate nanoparticle binding, internalization, and payload delivery in physiologically relevant settings [284–286]. For intercellular interaction targets, such as ligand-receptor pairs inferred by CellChat or NicheNet [287, 288], clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR)-based knockout or knockdown in co-culture systems can establish causal dependency. The use of spatial CRISPR screening to map regulators of the TME further illustrates how functional genomics can be spatially resolved to confirm that omics-identified features are not merely markers, but mechanistic drivers of microenvironmental state [289]. Only features that pass this validation gate, by demonstrating both molecular accessibility and causal relevance, should advance to the parameterized design stage.

Steps 4 and 5: parameterized design implementation and spatially resolved back-validation

Once targets and microenvironmental features have been functionally validated, the workflow advances to the translation of biological information into concrete nanoparticle design specifications (Fig. 6, Step 4). This translation operates across several tunable parameters. Surface ligand identity and density are informed by the receptor landscape of the target niche [290]. Particle size and surface properties can be tuned in light of the physical accessibility constraints of the spatial compartment. For example, in some contexts, smaller particles may favor penetration through dense ECM, whereas near-neutral to slightly negative surface charge may facilitate diffusion through ECM-rich stromal regions [291, 292]. Stimuli-responsive elements can likewise be selected according to niche-specific biochemical triggers, such as a particular protease isoform rather than a generic enzyme class [293]. Finally, payload selection can also be informed by both the functional state and spatial organization of the target cell population, with immune-activating or cytotoxic payloads considered according to the dominant biology, vascular proximity, and tissue penetration constraints of the target niche [49, 294].

Beyond individual parameter selection, high-throughput screening technologies can further accelerate this design-implementation cycle. DNA-barcoded nanoparticle libraries enable simultaneous in vivo testing of hundreds of nanoparticle formulations in a single animal, with barcode sequencing yielding organ- and cell-type-resolved biodistribution data that could, in principle, be integrated with omics-defined niche characteristics [295, 296]. Such combinatorial approaches have the potential to transform Step 4 from a serial, hypothesis-driven process into a more parallel, data-driven optimization framework that is well suited to the multidimensional design space defined by omics profiling.

The final, and arguably most critical, step is spatially resolved back-validation (Fig. 6, Step 5), in which the engineered nanoparticle is administered in vivo and its intratumoral distribution is mapped by spatial imaging modalities, including fluorescence microscopy, mass spectrometry imaging, or autoradiography, and then quantitatively correlated with the original omics-defined target niche coordinates [297]. This spatial correlation closes the design loop by allowing concordance between predicted and observed accumulation to support the omics-to-design translation, while discordance prompts iterative refinement of the design parameters and re-entry into the workflow.

Although these studies were not originally designed as fully prospective omics-guided optimization workflows, two studies from our group provide methodological precedents for spatially resolved back-validation by showing how nanoparticle distribution can be mapped, molecularly interpreted, and evaluated against ST-defined tumor niches [34, 298]. Park et al. first showed, in an ST-based nanoparticle distribution study, that 10x Visium ST could be integrated with fluorescence-based imaging of nanoparticle distribution in 4T1 tumor models. Using the deep-learning algorithm SPADE, they correlated gene expression patterns with intratumoral nanoparticle accumulation [34]. Notably, nanoparticle uptake was not fully explained by conventional vascular markers such as Pecam1 and Cd34, but was instead associated with metabolically defined tissue regions characterized by hypoxia-, glycolysis-, and apoptosis-related signatures, including markers such as Vdac1, suggesting that intratumoral nanoparticle distribution cannot be explained solely by conventional vascular features [34].

In a subsequent glycan-tuned albumin nanoplatform study, Park et al. extended this spatial mapping strategy to engineered glycosylated albumin nanoparticles [298]. The nanoparticles themselves were engineered through azadibenzocyclooctyne (ADIBO)-based click chemistry with controlled mannose, galactose, or glucose conjugation, whereas positron emission tomography (PET), fluorescence imaging, and ST were used to evaluate how these predefined glycosylation patterns mapped onto distinct TME compartments and receptor-associated signatures. Orthogonal imaging and cell-type-level analyses further supported the association between glycosylated albumin uptake and specific cellular compartments, including Man-Alb enrichment in macrophage/TAM-associated and mannose receptor C-type 1 (Mrc1)-high regions and Gal-/Glc-Alb uptake in glycolytic/hypoxic, solute carrier family 2 member 1 (Slc2a1)-associated tumor regions [298]. Spatial back-validation therefore showed that distinct glycosylation patterns were associated with spatially segregated TME niches, rather than demonstrating a fully prospective omics-driven design process from the outset. Together, these studies illustrate how the spatial back-validation layer of the proposed workflow can be implemented using currently available imaging and ST technologies. In this sense, they serve as methodological examples for mapping, interpreting, and validating nanoparticle biodistribution against molecularly defined tissue niches, whereas the broader translation of omics-defined features into tunable design parameters is synthesized in Sect. "Omics-to-design translation map for nanoparticle engineering". Collectively, the closed-loop framework proposed here transforms the omics-to-nanomedicine pipeline from a linear and largely descriptive exercise, in which omics data serve as post hoc justification for empirically designed nanoparticles, into an iterative engineering cycle in which each design decision is traceable to a validated molecular observation and each outcome is spatially benchmarked against the original biological blueprint. In the following sections, this framework is applied to three interconnected design challenges, beginning with the redefinition of target discovery within a heterogeneous TME landscape (Sect. "Target discovery in a heterogeneous landscape"), followed by spatially informed nanoparticle delivery that maps delivery barriers and therapeutic opportunity zones (Sect. "Spatially informed nanoparticle delivery: mapping delivery barriers and therapeutic opportunity zones"), and then by spatially informed responsive release that moves beyond generic triggers toward omics-defined molecular signatures (Sect. "Spatially informed responsive release: beyond generic triggers to omics-defined signatures"). These discussions are then synthesized into an omics-to-design translation map that links spatially resolved biological features to tunable nanoparticle design parameters and validation strategies (Sect. "Omics-to-design translation map for nanoparticle engineering"). Finally, we extend the discussion beyond transcriptome-centric design to examine how complementary proteomic, metabolomic, lipidomic, and epigenomic layers can inform DDS engineering, while also critically assessing the translational distance between multi-omics data and integrated nanoparticle design (Sect. "Beyond transcriptome-centric design: complementary multi-omics layers and translational challenges for DDS engineering").

Target discovery in a heterogeneous landscape

Conventional nanomedicine targeting has largely relied on the overexpression of a single receptor on tumor cells, an approach that does not fully account for the cellular and spatial heterogeneity revealed by scRNA-seq and ST [17]. For decades, a common design logic has been to identify a receptor overexpressed on cancer cells relative to normal tissue, such as folate receptor-α (FRα), HER2, or transferrin receptor, and to functionalize nanoparticle surfaces with the corresponding ligand to achieve tumor-selective accumulation [259, 260]. This paradigm has yielded clinically relevant targeted-delivery strategies, including ligand-decorated nanocarriers and related receptor-guided therapeutic platforms. Yet an important limitation of this paradigm lies in two assumptions that are increasingly recognized as overly simplistic in light of single-cell omics data [299, 300]. First, receptor expression is often heterogeneous within tumors, and single-cell as well as intratumoral analyses increasingly indicate that candidate target molecules may be enriched only in subsets of malignant cells rather than uniformly expressed across an entire lesion [299–301]. Second, the tumor mass is not composed solely of cancer cells. A substantial fraction of solid tumors is composed of stromal and immune cell populations rather than cancer cells alone, yet these populations have rarely been treated as primary targeting substrates in conventional nanoparticle design [9, 35]. Taken together, these observations suggest that average expression across bulk tissue does not equate to uniform accessibility at single-cell resolution [302]. Effective target discovery must therefore move beyond cancer-cell-centric and receptor-centric paradigms. Building on Steps 1 and 2 of the framework outlined in Sect. "Omics-to-design translation framework: a closed-loop workflow", the following discussion examines how single-cell and spatial omics are redefining the target landscape for nanomedicine beyond single cancer-cell receptors, with particular emphasis on stromal subpopulations, dysfunctional endothelial states, and spatially organized cell-cell interaction interfaces.

Spatial omics-guided identification of stromal subtype targets

CAFs provide a useful example of how single-cell and spatial profiling have transformed a seemingly homogeneous stromal population into a landscape of therapeutically distinct subtypes with divergent functions and spatial distributions [79, 303]. Integrated single-cell studies have resolved CAFs into multiple functionally specialized states, with myCAFs and iCAFs serving as two widely used reference states rather than universally fixed categories [79, 303]. In many contexts, myCAF-like populations are associated with ECM production and desmoplastic remodeling, whereas iCAF-like populations are linked to cytokine- and chemokine-rich inflammatory signaling; their spatial distribution also appears to vary by tumor context, with myCAF-like populations often enriched near desmoplastic tumor borders and iCAF-like populations occupying more distal stromal regions [304–308]. This distinction has important implications for nanomedicine design, because a pan-CAF targeting strategy could engage multiple CAF states simultaneously and may therefore perturb both immunosuppressive and potentially tumor-restraining stromal functions [79].

Several spatial omics studies now provide a molecular framework for subtype-selective CAF targeting [308]. Chen et al. used integrated scRNA-seq and ST to map periostin-positive (POSTN⁺) CAF clusters in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), showing that these cells form spatially confined immunosuppressive niches associated with reduced CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and poor prognosis [305]. Similarly, Wu et al. identified POSTN as a key mediator of ECM-remodeling CAF activity in PDAC through integrative single-cell and spatial analysis, revealing that POSTN⁺ CAFs concentrate at the tumor-stroma interface, where they deposit a dense collagen-rich matrix [306]. Li et al. extended this logic to HNSCC, identifying a spatial CAF subset that physically restricts CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration and is associated with impaired local antitumor immunity [304]. In CRC, Qi et al. further demonstrated through scRNA-seq and spatial co-localization analysis that FAP⁺ fibroblasts and secreted phosphoprotein 1-positive (SPP1⁺) macrophages form a spatially organized interaction hub that may reinforce local immunosuppression through paracrine TGF-β- and IL-1-related signaling [309]. These omics-defined stromal programs therefore support subtype-selective CAF target discovery and suggest that stromal targets should be interpreted as spatially organized niches rather than isolated marker-positive populations [310].

Recent studies have also begun to link omics-defined CAF targets with nanomedicine design within a broader translational framework in which spatial and cellular context are treated as functionally relevant rather than merely descriptive features of the TME [311]. Two recent studies provide proof-of-concept examples that connect single-cell target discovery to functional perturbation and nanoparticle-based therapeutic testing [312, 313]. Zhang et al. leveraged single-cell transcriptomics in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) to identify CXCL12-expressing CAFs as a prominent axis associated with CD8⁺ T-cell exclusion through the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis. Translating this insight into nanoparticle design, the authors engineered FAPα-antibody-decorated lipid nanocarriers loaded with CXCL12-targeting siRNA, achieving selective CAF engagement, CXCL12 knockdown, restoration of CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration, and significant tumor growth inhibition in vivo (p < 0.01) [312]. In a complementary study, Yan et al. applied scRNA-seq to NSCLC and identified ACTN1 as a CAF-enriched gene whose overexpression correlated with poor patient prognosis (hazard ratio = 1.5, p = 0.011). Rather than relying on a conventional ligand-targeting strategy, they exploited the stromal homing properties of CAF membranes to construct biomimetic nanovesicles camouflaged with CAF-derived membranes and loaded with ACTN1-targeting siRNA, thereby reducing pro-tumorigenic cytokine secretion, including IL-6 and CCL2, and attenuating tumor growth and metastasis in vivo [313]. Taken together, these two studies move beyond descriptive target nomination by linking single-cell target discovery to functional perturbation and nanoparticle-based therapeutic testing. If CAFs illustrate how stromal heterogeneity expands the target landscape beyond malignant cells, endothelial atlases extend the same logic to the vascular compartment.

Endothelial state heterogeneity as a guide to vascular target discovery

Compared with CAF-directed strategies, endothelial cells-subtype-selective nanomedicine remains less experimentally mature, but recent endothelial atlases have made this space more tractable for hypothesis-driven design. Beyond stromal targets, tumor-associated endothelial cells represent a second major target class whose therapeutic significance has been redefined by single-cell omics [314]. Conventionally, tumor vasculature has been treated as a monolithic entity, either to be normalized through anti-angiogenic therapy or exploited for passive accumulation through the EPR effect, with little distinction among the diverse endothelial states that coexist within a single tumor [314]. Goveia et al. generated a landmark scRNA-seq atlas of tumor endothelial cells across human lung tumors and resolved the vasculature into phenotypically and functionally distinct subpopulations, including tip cells, stalk cells, immature endothelial cells, breach cells with compromised barrier function, and activated post-capillary venule endothelial cells that regulate immune extravasation [315]. These endothelial cell phenotypes also appear to be spatially patterned, although their precise distribution is likely to vary by tumor type and local microenvironment; angiogenic tip- or breach-like states may be enriched in actively remodeling vascular niches, whereas activated venular endothelial cell programs may be more relevant at immune-trafficking interfaces [314, 315].

This endothelial heterogeneity suggests that tumor vasculature should not be treated as a uniform delivery interface. Plasmalemma vesicle-associated protein (PLVAP), a marker of fenestrated and immature tumor endothelial cells, is enriched in tumor vasculature relative to normal vessels and is associated with regions of enhanced vascular permeability, suggesting that PLVAP may help define permeability-associated vascular segments within tumors [315]. Atypical chemokine receptor 1 (ACKR1, also known as Duffy antigen), selectively expressed on post-capillary venular endothelial cells, mediates chemokine transcytosis and leukocyte trafficking; ACKR1⁺ venular states may therefore mark vascular segments that regulate immune entry into the tumor parenchyma [316, 317]. In parallel, endothelial anergy, characterized by the downregulation of adhesion molecules such as intercellular adhesion molecule 1 (ICAM-1) and vascular cell adhesion molecule 1 (VCAM-1) in response to sustained angiogenic signaling, constitutes an additional vascular state that may shape immune infiltration [318, 319]. Although nanoparticle platforms directed specifically against omics-defined endothelial cell subtypes remain largely conceptual, the molecular coordinates provided by endothelial single-cell atlases make such vascular-state-selective targeting increasingly testable as a design objective [131]. More broadly, however, single-cell and spatial omics are shifting the design unit itself from discrete cell populations to the signaling interfaces that organize multicellular behavior within the TME.

Cell–cell interaction interfaces as spatially organized targeting nodes

An important conceptual advance of single-cell and spatial omics for nanomedicine target discovery is the shift from individual cell-surface markers to intercellular communication interfaces as primary substrates for design [320]. Computational frameworks such as CellChat and NicheNet infer ligand-receptor interaction networks from scRNA-seq data, quantify signaling strength between cell populations, and identify the molecular pairs that mediate functionally meaningful crosstalk within the TME [287, 288]. When these analyses are integrated with ST, they reveal not only which cell types communicate, but also where those interactions occur within intact tissue architecture, thereby adding a spatially actionable layer to interaction networks [320].

The PD-1/PD-L1 axis illustrates how spatial interaction analysis can reshape targeting logic. Conventional immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) targets PD-L1 irrespective of its cellular source, yet spatial profiling has shown that PD-L1 is not uniformly distributed across tumor cells and is, in many cancer types, prominently expressed on TAMs, with functional consequences that depend on both cell type and spatial context [217, 321]. Wang et al. reported that PD-L1-expressing TAMs in human breast cancer are spatially concentrated at the tumor-immune interface and, paradoxically, are associated with immunostimulatory rather than immunosuppressive activity [321]. These findings suggest that the consequences of PD-L1 targeting may vary according to the cellular source and spatial context of PD-L1 expression; accordingly, future PD-L1-directed nanomedicines may benefit from incorporating additional cell-type or spatial selectivity beyond PD-L1 binding alone. Achieving such selectivity would likely require the added spatial and cellular resolution provided by single-cell and spatial omics, beyond what conventional marker-based approaches alone can typically capture [312].

Beyond checkpoint signaling, paracrine signaling hubs inferred from interaction analysis provide additional opportunities for intervention. In CRC, Qi et al. showed that FAP⁺ fibroblasts and SPP1⁺ macrophages form a spatially correlated stromal–myeloid hub and proposed multiple interaction axes, including COL1A1/LAMA1–ITGB1, TGFB1/INHBA-associated signaling, WNT5A–FZD2, CCL3–CCR5, and RARRES2–CMKLR1, that may reinforce desmoplastic remodeling and immune exclusion [309]. In this setting, macrophage accumulation and polarization are better described as associated or potentially reinforced processes rather than as a single conclusively established recruitment mechanism [309]. Similarly, the CXCL12-CXCR4 axis, highly active within the iCAF compartment, drives T-cell exclusion from the tumor core by establishing chemokine gradients that divert lymphocytes into stromal trapping zones [312, 322]. Targeting such interaction interfaces, rather than isolated markers on single cell types, could motivate nanoparticle designs aimed at disrupting functionally important signaling nodes in the TME. In this framework, the relevant design variable is not simply receptor abundance on one cell type, but the spatially localized interaction node itself [310].

Taken together, these examples suggest that single-cell and spatial omics have broadened the target repertoire for TME-directed nanomedicine from a limited set of overexpressed cancer-cell receptors to a richer, spatially resolved landscape of stromal subtypes, endothelial states, and intercellular signaling interfaces [310]. Translating these targets into engineered nanoparticles, however, requires not only molecular specificity but also spatial precision. The challenge is no longer simply what to target, but where within the tissue to deliver, and that spatial dimension is the focus of the following section.

Spatially informed nanoparticle delivery: mapping delivery barriers and therapeutic opportunity zones

Spatial heterogeneity of nanoparticle accumulation revealed by spatial omics

Beyond identifying molecular targets, ST has revealed that the TME is organized into functionally distinct spatial compartments, while complementary imaging studies have shown that nanomedicine accumulation can vary markedly across tumor regions. Together, these findings underscore the need for delivery strategies informed not only by what to target, but also by where to deliver [32, 111, 323]. The classical EPR effect predicts passive nanoparticle accumulation through leaky tumor vasculature and impaired lymphatic drainage, with vascular leakiness often attributed to passive extravasation through inter-endothelial gaps. Yet an EPR-centered framework can obscure the spatial heterogeneity of vascular permeability, interstitial transport, and other delivery barriers across the TME [324, 325]. Sindhwani et al. challenged this paradigm by showing that, across the tumor models and human tumor samples examined, inter-endothelial gaps were not the dominant route of PEGylated gold nanoparticle entry into solid tumors; instead, most nanoparticles entered tumors through an active trans-endothelial process [326]. Building on this trans-endothelial entry model, Kingston et al. identified nanoparticle transport endothelial cells (N-TECs), a distinct subset of tumor endothelial cells heterogeneously distributed along a limited subset of tumor vessels, as gatekeepers that determine nanoparticle entry points, intratumoral distribution, and tumor-cell accessibility [327]. Together, these studies support a spatially resolved model of nanoparticle delivery in which endothelial transport routes, vessel-level heterogeneity, and regional barrier architecture jointly determine where nanoparticles enter, accumulate, and gain access to tumor cells.

Spatial transcriptomic studies further extend this spatial framework to the tissue level by mapping regional variation in TME organization and barrier-associated programs. Arora et al. applied integrated scRNA-seq and ST to oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) and identified architecturally distinct tumor core and leading-edge compartments with divergent transcriptional programs, cellular neighborhoods, and ligand–receptor interactions. The partial conservation of leading-edge programs across cancer types and their association with clinical outcomes and predicted targeted-therapy response suggest that regional TME architecture can define spatially distinct therapeutic contexts that may be relevant to delivery-barrier mapping [111]. Consistent with this delivery-relevant view of spatial heterogeneity, Park et al. integrated 10x Visium ST with fluorescence imaging of nanomedicine distribution to demonstrate spatially heterogeneous intratumoral accumulation of PEGylated liposomal nanomedicine and to identify associated molecular programs, including hypoxia, glycolysis/glucose metabolism, and apoptosis-related signatures [34]. More broadly, spatial omics can identify therapeutically important regions even when delivery is not directly measured. In a complementary spatial-omics study, Wu et al. used Stereo-seq to delineate a 500 μm-wide invasive zone centered on the liver tumor border, characterized by damaged hepatocytes, hepatocyte–tumor cell crosstalk, metabolic reprogramming, macrophage-associated local immunosuppression, and tumor progression. Although not a nanoparticle-delivery study, this work highlights how spatially restricted pathological niches can nominate therapeutically important regions that may also require region-specific delivery strategies [328]. Together, these studies support the view that nanomedicine delivery should be interpreted within a spatially resolved TME framework, in which regional differences in vascular access, stromal organization, molecular programs, and microenvironmental state shape both nanoparticle accumulation and therapeutic opportunity. Accordingly, rational nanoparticle design should integrate Step 2-derived spatial barrier maps with the spatially and cellularly resolved target landscape defined in Sect. "Target discovery in a heterogeneous landscape", so that nanoparticle engineering is guided not only by what to target but also by where delivery barriers and therapeutic opportunity zones are located.

Spatial omics-guided refinement of barrier-disrupting and penetration-enhancing strategies

Once spatial barriers have been mapped, the next engineering challenge is to determine how best to traverse, remodel, or bypass them to access otherwise poorly accessible TME compartments. In practice, barrier-disrupting and penetration-enhancing strategies can be organized into three principal classes, each targeting a complementary barrier layer through particle architecture, stromal remodeling, or endothelial transport.

First, size-switchable nanoparticle systems attempt to address a central size trade-off in tumor delivery, in which particle sizes that favor systemic circulation, tumor accumulation, or retention are not necessarily optimal for penetration through the dense tumor ECM [12]. Consistent with this rationale, Wong et al. reported a proof-of-concept multistage delivery system in which 100-nm gelatin nanoparticles bearing 10-nm quantum dots on their surface undergo MMP-2-mediated degradation after extravasation into the TME, thereby releasing smaller particles that diffuse more readily through the tumor interstitium [329]. Li et al. later extended this size-switching strategy with the pH-responsive clustered nanoparticle system iCluster, in which approximately 100-nm nanoclusters disassemble under mildly acidic tumor extracellular conditions, experimentally modeled at pH 6.8, to release approximately 5-nm platinum(IV) prodrug-conjugated poly(amidoamine) (PAMAM) dendrimers; in BxPC-3 human pancreatic tumor xenografts, iCluster/Pt achieved at least seven-fold higher intratumoral platinum accumulation than free cisplatin and PAMAM/Pt [330]. Together, these studies suggest that the performance of size-switchable systems depends not only on the switching mechanism itself, but also on whether the size transition occurs at the appropriate spatial barrier interface. Spatial omics adds an important new dimension to this strategy by converting trigger selection from a generic material choice into a spatially informed design variable. Rather than relying on broadly applied stimuli such as acidic pH or broadly defined MMP activity as presumed universal cues, ST or spatial extracellular proteomic profiling could first nominate regionally enriched ECM-remodeling or putative protease-associated programs at tumor-stroma and invasive barrier regions [331, 332]. These candidate triggers should then be functionally validated with spatial protease activity assays to determine where the corresponding enzymatic activity is actually present within the tissue [333]. Importantly, spatial omics information can be used not only to nominate protease-rich barrier regions, but also to assess whether the intended therapeutic target and the release-triggering enzyme are spatially aligned within the same or adjacent tumor niches. For example, co-localization of target-cell populations with MMP- or other protease-associated programs at tumor–stroma interfaces or invasive-front regions could guide the paired selection of nanoparticle-targeting ligands and enzyme-responsive release strategies, ensuring that ligand-mediated localization and protease-responsive disassembly occur within therapeutically relevant tissue compartments. This target–trigger co-mapping concept is supported by spatial studies showing distinct tumor core and leading-edge architectures, ECM-remodeling programs at invasive regions, and MMP14-associated stromal matrix remodeling. A recent spatial transcriptomics-based ADC modeling study reported by Bae et al. provides a directly relevant methodological example. Using Visium spatial transcriptomics from 36 lung adenocarcinoma tissues, they developed spatial indices to evaluate ADC-related features, including target specificity, spatial homogeneity, bystander-effect potential, and a Target-Linker Match Score (TLM), which measures the spatial overlap between ADC target regions and linker enzyme activity. This approach illustrates how spatial transcriptomics can quantify whether a therapeutic target and its corresponding linker-activating enzyme are co-localized within the same tumor region or positioned in neighboring niches [334]. Extending this logic to nanoparticle design, target–linker enzyme matching could guide the paired selection of nanoparticle-targeting ligands and protease- or enzyme-cleavable release strategies, such as MMP-, cathepsin-, or β-glucuronidase-responsive linkers. In this framework, protease-responsive size-switchable nanoparticles could be engineered so that disassembly or size reduction is preferentially coupled to protease-active barrier interfaces where deep penetration is required, rather than being triggered broadly across the tumor.

Second, ECM-degrading strategies directly target the desmoplastic barrier by incorporating or co-delivering matrix-modifying enzymes to remodel stromal ECM and improve intratumoral drug delivery [77, 335]. Hyaluronidase-functionalized nanoparticles provide representative preclinical examples of nanoparticle-enabled ECM remodeling, whereas systemic PEGylated hyaluronidase PEGPH20 (pegvorhyaluronidase alfa) represents the most clinically advanced hyaluronan-depleting enzymatic strategy evaluated in PDAC [336–338]. However, the phase III HALO-109-301 trial failed to translate this preclinical rationale into clinical benefit; adding PEGPH20 to nab-paclitaxel plus gemcitabine did not improve overall survival in patients with hyaluronan-high metastatic pancreatic adenocarcinoma, despite prior preclinical evidence that enzymatic hyaluronan depletion could remodel the desmoplastic stroma and enhance drug delivery [77, 335, 338]. This instructive failure underscores a central lesson for omics-guided barrier disruption: ECM degradation without spatial guidance may dismantle tumor-restraining stromal architecture alongside delivery-limiting barriers, potentially favoring more aggressive tumor behavior or an immunosuppressive stromal state [339–341]. This concern is further supported by preclinical studies showing that broad stromal depletion or Hedgehog-pathway disruption can accelerate pancreatic tumor progression, promote aggressive tumor behavior, and induce immunosuppressive remodeling [339, 340, 342]. Given the context-dependent roles of tumor stroma in cancer progression and therapy response, ST could help reduce this risk by resolving spatially distinct stromal states and prioritizing putative tumor-promoting desmoplastic niches, where controlled ECM remodeling may improve nanoparticle access, while flagging stromal compartments with potential tumor-restraining functions, where broad barrier disruption may be harmful [109, 343]. More specifically, ST-based CAF and ECM-associated signatures, including POSTN⁺ stromal programs, FAP⁺ fibroblast–SPP1⁺ macrophage niches, collagen-rich desmoplastic regions, and immune-excluded stromal boundaries, could help identify candidate ECM-rich compartments that may function as immunosuppressive barriers and potential delivery-limiting regions [305, 309]. Together, these spatial maps could then guide where ECM-degrading, matrix-softening, or penetration-enhancing nanoparticles could be preferentially deployed or activated, while sparing stromal regions whose structural role may restrain invasion. In this regard, the spatial CAF subtyping described in Sect. "Target discovery in a heterogeneous landscape" provides a molecular framework for prioritizing candidate stromal regions for controlled ECM remodeling. In particular, the identification of ECM-remodeling POSTN⁺ CAF programs in PDAC supports a shift from systemic matrix degradation toward spatially selective barrier modulation [305, 306].

Third, transcytosis-inducing strategies seek to overcome the vascular entry barrier by exploiting active trans-endothelial transport pathways, thereby reducing reliance on passive extravasation through leaky tumor vessels [344]. Following Sindhwani et al.’s paradigm-shifting demonstration that active trans-endothelial transport accounts for a substantial fraction of nanoparticle entry into tumors [326], nanoparticles can be engineered to engage endothelial transcytotic pathways or transport-associated targets, such as the transferrin receptor, neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn), and caveolae-associated mechanisms, thereby improving vascular entry in tumor regions where passive, EPR-dependent extravasation is limited [325]. When integrated with the endothelial subtype atlas described in Sect. "Target discovery in a heterogeneous landscape", such ligands could be prioritized according to the endothelial cell phenotype enriched within a given vascular segment, thereby reframing transcytosis from a generic transport route into a vascular-state-informed delivery strategy tailored to the endothelial context of each tumor region [345]. Taken together, these strategies indicate that effective nanoparticle penetration cannot rely on generic barrier-responsive mechanisms alone, but instead requires spatially informed design logic matched to the dominant vascular, stromal, and interstitial constraints within each tumor region.

Spatial opportunity zones as targets for precision nanoparticle intervention

Having mapped delivery barriers and the strategies needed to traverse them, spatial analysis can next shift the design focus from overcoming physical access limitations to identifying therapeutic opportunity zones where localized nanoparticle interventions could modulate disease-relevant biology [111, 346]. One compelling example is provided by TLS, spatially organized ectopic lymphoid aggregates that arise within or adjacent to tumors [347]. Liu et al. used integrated scRNA-seq and ST to show that TLS-associated cellular states and their spatial relationships with tumour cell aggregates in nasopharyngeal carcinoma are linked to tumour progression and immunotherapy response. These findings support the idea that TLS may act as localized immune-active hubs with therapeutic relevance for enhancing antitumor immunity [348]. This spatial organization provides a conceptual rationale for engineering nanomedicines capable of selectively engaging and modulating these immune-priming microenvironments. In principle, spatially programmed nanomedicines could be designed to act within TLS-rich or TLS-adjacent immune niches by leveraging TLS-associated molecular cues, such as CXCL13-enriched chemokine signals, to inform the local delivery of antigens, immune adjuvants, or other immunomodulatory payloads. Such strategies could support local immune priming and, where biologically appropriate, promote TLS maturation toward more functional germinal-center-like states [347, 349, 350].

Beyond immune-active niches such as TLS, spatial maps can further delineate structurally defined interfaces that shape spatial access, transport, and barrier properties within the TME [111, 153]. Feng et al. showed that the spatial organization and immune status of the tumor–stroma boundary are associated with ICB response in CRC. This finding supports the broader concept that boundary regions can function as therapeutically relevant interfaces where stromal barrier features and immune accessibility converge [283]. For nanomedicine design, this spatial signature suggests that the tumor–stroma boundary may provide a strategic interface for coordinating barrier modulation with immune access.

Practically, tumor–stroma boundary targeting would rely on molecular features enriched at the interface rather than on the anatomical boundary itself. Boundary-enriched CAF or ECM-associated markers, such as FAP, POSTN, CXCL14-associated stromal programs, collagen-rich matrix, or fibronectin-rich regions, could guide nanoparticle localization, whereas boundary-associated protease activity, including MMP14, MMP2/9, cathepsins, or FAP-linked proteolytic programs, could trigger local release or size switching [111, 153]. Thus, boundary-directed nanomedicine could integrate ligand-mediated localization with spatially restricted activation, allowing barrier-modulating or immunostimulatory payloads to act specifically at tumor–stroma interfaces. More broadly, such spatially resolved mapping can nominate boundary regions as candidates for spatially and temporally ordered delivery strategies, thereby generating design-relevant hypotheses without prematurely specifying nanoparticle formulations or therapeutic payloads.

Spatially informed responsive release: beyond generic triggers to omics-defined signatures

Whereas Sect. "Spatially informed nanoparticle delivery: mapping delivery barriers and therapeutic opportunity zones" focused on spatial barriers that govern nanoparticle access and penetration, this section addresses spatially defined molecular cues that determine where and when responsive systems should release their payload.

Spatial heterogeneity of conventional responsive triggers revealed by spatial omics

Conventional stimulus-responsive nanomedicines have primarily exploited generic TME cues, such as bulk acidity, hypoxia, or broadly expressed enzymes, as release triggers [351–353]. Collectively, spatial omics and TME-focused studies now indicate that these cues can vary across tumor regions and among patients, supporting a shift from one-size-fits-all responsive designs toward data-driven, signature-specific release systems [43, 202, 279, 354, 355]. Among generic TME-responsive strategies, pH-responsive DDS are typically designed to exploit the mildly acidic extracellular pH of tumors (pH 6.5 to 6.8) relative to normal tissue (pH 7.4) [202, 356]. Yet this “average tumor pH” is a bulk measure that masks substantial intratumoral heterogeneity [355, 357]. Spatial metabolomics and integrated spatial multi-omics studies have shown that metabolic features are spatially compartmentalized within tumors, with distinct glycolytic, oxidative, and lipogenic programs emerging across anatomically and cellularly defined regions [43, 279]. Sun et al. applied an integrated spatially resolved multi-omics approach to gastric cancer and revealed cell-specific metabolic remodeling and interactions across heterogeneous tissue regions, highlighting the spatial organization of metabolic heterogeneity within the TME [43]. These spatially heterogeneous metabolic programs, together with the known spatial variability of tumor acidosis, suggest that acidity-related release cues are unlikely to be uniform across the entire tumor. Accordingly, a pH-responsive nanoparticle calibrated to release around pH 6.5 may activate efficiently in some tumor regions but less effectively in others, potentially resulting in spatially non-uniform drug release [355, 356].

A similar limitation affects enzyme-responsive systems. MMPs are among the most widely used enzymatic triggers for TME-responsive drug delivery [358–360], but they represent a diverse protease family with isoform-specific functions, cellular sources, and activity patterns across malignant and stromal compartments [361, 362]. In cancer tissues, MMP-2 and MMP-9 have been linked to stromal and inflammatory compartments, whereas MMP-14, also known as membrane-type 1 matrix metalloproteinase (MT1-MMP), is closely associated with pericellular proteolysis, matrix remodeling, and invasive behavior [361, 363]. Spatial and single-cell studies further show that CAF-rich tumor-stromal interfaces are heterogeneous and spatially organized, suggesting that MMP-responsive systems should not assume uniform protease activity across the tumor [304, 305]. A nanoparticle carrying a broadly MMP-responsive peptide substrate may be activated across multiple MMP-rich regions and thus may lack the spatial precision needed to distinguish an invasive niche from stromal regions where premature release would be undesirable [358, 359]. More broadly, Song et al. highlighted spatial tumor ecosystem heterogeneity in NSCLC, demonstrating that stromal and tumor features are spatially organized at the intratumoral level, a pattern that is consistent with the broader view that spatially agnostic responsive designs may overlook therapeutically relevant microregional variation [364].

Spatially resolved molecular signatures as precision release triggers

Multi-omics provides the spatial and molecular resolution needed to support a more refined design logic. Instead of selecting a trigger from a generic list of textbook TME hallmarks, this framework derives the trigger from spatially resolved molecular signatures of the target tumor niche. Within the closed-loop workflow proposed in Sect. "Omics-to-design translation framework: a closed-loop workflow", the candidate signature would be identified during Step 2, functionally validated in Step 3, and then translated in Step 4 into selection of a responsive material with the appropriate specificity.

At the protease level, this logic translates into isoform-informed cleavable linkers [359, 360]. If ST together with orthogonal proteomic or activity-based validation identifies MMP-14, rather than MMP-2 or MMP-9, as the dominant protease at the invasive front of a given patient’s tumor [363, 365], the nanoparticle linker can be engineered with a peptide substrate designed to preferentially respond to MMP-14 [360, 366]. Such a design could, in principle, enable spatially restricted release at the invasion interface while improving stability in adjacent MMP-2-rich stromal regions, where premature activation would be undesirable [360, 363, 366]. Cathepsin B, a lysosomal cysteine protease overexpressed in invasive tumor cells and TAMs, offers another promising trigger for niche-specific prodrug activation [367, 368]. Shim et al. showed that carrier-free nanoparticles assembled from cathepsin B-cleavable peptide-doxorubicin conjugates (FRRG-DOX) undergo cathepsin B-dependent intracellular activation in tumor cells, with reduced systemic toxicity relative to free doxorubicin [369]. When cathepsin-responsive systems are informed by spatial profiling that localizes cathepsin B–associated niches within the tumor, particularly at invasive fronts and TAM-dense regions, the responsive element can be spatially contextualized rather than deployed in a generic manner [358, 367, 368].

At the metabolic level, spatial metabolomics opens the possibility of niche-specific metabolic triggers [354]. Spatial metabolomics studies in gastric cancer and hepatocellular carcinoma, including those by Sun et al. [43] and Chen et al. [279], reveal spatially heterogeneous metabolic landscapes and tumor-host metabolic interactions that could, in principle, inform the selection of metabolite-based release cues. A lactate-oxidase-gated nanoparticle, for example, could be engineered to preferentially release its payload in lactate-enriched regions, with the target lactate range informed by spatial metabolomics [43, 279, 354]. Such a module could promote preferential release in glycolytically active tumor regions while potentially reducing off-target activation in adjacent stromal or immune compartments with lower lactate levels [357, 370]. Although such metabolite-responsive materials remain at an early stage, emerging spatial metabolic maps can begin to inform rational materials design by identifying candidate metabolites, regional enrichment patterns, and approximate concentration landscapes relevant to trigger selection [354].

Spatial omics-guided trigger selection: trade-offs and a subtype-informed path forward

To clarify the paradigm shift proposed here, it is useful to compare generic and omics-defined triggers across key design parameters. Generic triggers, such as pH ~ 6.5, H₂O₂, or pan-MMP-cleavable peptides, offer several practical advantages. They rely on well-characterized stimuli and exploit established responsive chemistries, including hydrazone, acetal, and disulfide linkages, pan-MMP-cleavable peptides, and ROS-sensitive thioketal motifs, whose synthesis and stimulus-response properties have been widely studied [356, 359, 371]. Generic triggers further require no patient-specific molecular profiling, making them operationally attractive for use across heterogeneous tumor settings. Their core limitation, however, lies in spatial non-specificity, as activation can occur wherever the stimulus is present, regardless of whether that region corresponds to a therapeutically relevant niche, thereby diluting efficacy and increasing the risk of off-target effects [202, 354, 355].

Omics-defined triggers, such as niche-specific protease isoforms or tumor-state-specific metabolite signatures, offer the potential for greater spatial selectivity. In principle, release would occur preferentially at biologically defined target niches, thereby reducing off-target activation and potentially improving the therapeutic index [354, 358]. This specificity, however, introduces several practical design constraints. First, omics-defined triggers should ideally be selected from recurrent and biologically validated trigger classes rather than engineered de novo for every individual patient, because excessive trigger customization would undermine scalability [372]. Second, the TME is dynamic, meaning that a trigger landscape captured at a single profiling time point may not remain stable during tumor progression or therapy. Third, transcriptomic evidence alone is insufficient to define a functional release trigger. Consistent with the functional-layer confirmation logic outlined in Sect. "Omics-to-design translation framework: a closed-loop workflow", protease transcripts should be linked to enzymatic activity, and metabolite-defined triggers should be converted into quantitative threshold ranges through spatial proteomics, metabolomics, or activity-based assays [354, 365, 367, 373]. Fourth, omics-defined trigger selection requires upstream multi-omics profiling and orthogonal functional confirmation for each subtype, expanding the experimental and analytical requirements relative to off-the-shelf generic-trigger designs [372].

In this context, a more practical near-term model may be subtype-informed trigger selection rather than fully bespoke responsive nanomedicine. Tumors could be grouped into recurrent responsive states, such as MMP-14-dominant invasive fronts [363], cathepsin B/S-rich macrophage-enriched invasive niches [367, 368], or lactate-high glycolytic regions [43, 279, 354, 357]. Each state would then be matched to a corresponding pre-engineered module. For example, MMP-14-cleavable peptide linkers could be deployed for MMP-14-dominant fronts [363, 365, 366], FRRG-based or related cathepsin-cleavable prodrug designs for cathepsin-rich niches [369], and lactate-responsive modules, where available, could be calibrated to spatially measured threshold concentrations in glycolytic regions [43, 279, 354, 357, 370]. This modular strategy is also consistent with translational design principles that favor standardized, reproducible, and comparable nanoparticle variants that can be validated and iteratively optimized while preserving the spatial selectivity sought in omics-informed release systems [372]. Building on this validation logic, the next subsection synthesizes how spatially resolved omics features, including cell-state subtypes, interaction interfaces, delivery barriers, responsive trigger landscapes, and closed-loop anchor examples, can be organized into an omics-to-design translation map for nanoparticle engineering.

Omics-to-design translation map for nanoparticle engineering

Building on the preceding discussion across Sect. "Target discovery in a heterogeneous landscape"–"Spatially informed responsive release: beyond generic triggers to omics-defined signatures", the central question now shifts from whether omics can identify spatially restricted cell states, interaction patterns, delivery barriers, or responsive triggers to how such observations can be organized into testable design hypotheses within the closed-loop framework outlined in Sect. "Omics-to-design translation framework: a closed-loop workflow". Accordingly, we summarized representative biology-to-design relationships in an omics-to-design translation map (Table 1). In this framework, the first three columns, Design-relevant omics feature, Omics modality, and TME niche/context, are mainly based on reported omics-based studies and can be viewed as spatially defined TME features associated with tumor progression, immune organization, or therapeutic response. In contrast, the subsequent columns are presented as hypothesis-driven engineering extrapolations that explore how these biological observations could be translated into tunable nanoparticle design parameters, rationales, and validation strategies. Therefore, rather than serving as a simple target list or as evidence that all proposed nanoparticle systems have already been experimentally validated, Table 1 is intended to provide a structured basis for discussing how niche-level biological observations can inform testable nanoparticle design hypotheses. Below, we discuss how this translation logic can guide nanoparticle design across the representative design-relevant omics features summarized in Table 1, including cell-state subtypes, interaction interfaces, spatial barrier or trigger landscapes, and closed-loop anchors.

The POSTN⁺ myCAF cluster provides a stromal-subtype example in which spatially resolved CAF heterogeneity can be translated into a stroma-guided nanoparticle design hypothesis. Chen et al. identified POSTN⁺ CAFs in NSCLC as a fibrotic and immunosuppressive stromal program associated with ECM remodeling, proximity to SPP1⁺ macrophages, reduced T-cell infiltration, and poor clinical outcome [305]. Wu et al. further supported the relevance of CAF-derived POSTN in PDAC, linking it to ECM-remodeling stromal biology and tumor-promoting stromal–epithelial crosstalk [306]. Together, these findings position POSTN-rich myCAF regions as desmoplastic stromal interfaces rather than simple marker-positive compartments. This shifts the design problem toward POSTN-rich stroma-guided delivery, in which recognition of POSTN-rich stromal regions is paired with formulations designed to navigate fibrotic barriers, such as matrix-responsive, ECM-modulating, or penetration-oriented architectures. For instance, such formulations could incorporate ECM-responsive linkers, matrix-degrading or matrix-normalizing payloads, or surface chemistries that enhance diffusion through POSTN-rich desmoplastic stroma. Accordingly, validation should focus not only on increased bulk tumor uptake, but also on whether the formulation preferentially localizes to POSTN-rich stromal regions and improves movement across desmoplastic boundaries, for example using 3D CAF spheroids and ST-correlated nanoparticle distribution analyses. This example therefore illustrates how omics-defined stromal subtypes can inform both where nanoparticles should engage the TME and which local barrier properties their design should address.

For endothelial subtype–guided design, PLVAP-associated endothelial cell heterogeneity illustrates how tumor vasculature can be stratified into functionally distinct delivery niches. Zeng et al. synthesized single-cell omics evidence showing that tumor endothelial cells are composed of molecularly diverse states rather than a uniform vascular compartment [314], while Goveia et al. used lung tumor endothelial profiling to show that classical angiogenic tip or proliferating endothelial phenotypes represent only a minority of the tumor endothelial compartment [315]. Across these endothelial studies, PLVAP has been reported as a recurrently enriched tumor endothelial marker associated with immature or permeability-related vascular states [314, 315]. Because PLVAP has also been implicated in endothelial permeability, VEGF-associated vascular leakage, and barrier alteration, PLVAP-rich vascular regions may represent candidate transport-associated niches, although direct validation of PLVAP-guided nanoparticle delivery remains necessary. Accordingly, this example reframes vascular delivery as a segment-selective access problem rather than generic tumor vascular targeting. Relevant validation should therefore extend beyond bulk tumor deposition to include colocalization with PLVAP⁺ vascular segments and segment-specific nanoparticle accumulation relative to non-targeted controls. This example highlights how endothelial omics can refine vascular targeting from organ- or vessel-level delivery toward molecularly defined vascular segments with potentially distinct transport properties.

As an interaction-interface example, the SPP1⁺ macrophage–FAP⁺ fibroblast hub illustrates how spatially organized stromal–myeloid circuits may inform nanoparticle design hypotheses. Qi et al. showed that SPP1⁺ macrophages and FAP⁺ fibroblasts are positively correlated and spatially co-localized in CRC, and linked this stromal–myeloid coexistence to ECM organization, desmoplastic structure, reduced lymphocyte infiltration, and an immune-excluded phenotype [309]. High FAP or SPP1 expression was further associated with poorer outcome or reduced benefit in an external anti-PD-L1-treated cohort [309]. These observations suggest that the actionable feature is not merely the presence of either macrophages or fibroblasts, but the formation of a spatially organized stromal–myeloid hub that contributes to immune exclusion. This converts the design focus from single-marker targeting to a dual-compartment or niche-focused intervention strategy, such as co-targeting stromal and myeloid components or delivering immunomodulatory payloads intended to remodel the suppressive interface. For instance, a niche-focused formulation could combine fibroblast- or macrophage-facing recognition with an immunomodulatory payload designed to weaken stromal–myeloid coupling and improve CD8⁺ T-cell access. Validation should therefore assess evidence of stromal–myeloid niche remodeling, including reduced FAP–SPP1 spatial proximity or functional coupling, increased lymphocyte or CD8⁺ T-cell access, and macrophage-state remodeling, rather than simple binding to one marker-positive population. This example emphasizes that omics-informed nanomedicine design can operate at the level of multicellular organization, not only at the level of single-cell marker enrichment.

As an example of a spatial barrier–opportunity landscape, Feng et al. used integrated scRNA-seq and ST to show that the spatial organization and immune status of the tumor–stroma boundary are closely associated with immunotherapy response in CRC [283]. Specifically, boundary regions enriched in LAMP3⁺ dendritic cells and CXCL13⁺ T cells were associated with more favorable ICB responses, whereas CXCL14⁺ CAF-associated ECM organization marked a more structured stromal barrier in less responsive tumors. Biologically, this suggests that the tumor–stroma boundary can be viewed not merely as a passive structural interface, but a spatially defined niche where ECM architecture, immune-cell accessibility, and therapeutic responsiveness converge. From a nanoparticle engineering perspective, this type of boundary can be interpreted as a design-relevant interface that simultaneously presents both a delivery barrier and a therapeutic opportunity. For example, a sequential nanoparticle strategy could be envisioned, in which a first nanoparticle is designed to modulate ECM-associated barrier features at the tumor–stroma boundary, followed by a second nanoparticle delivering immune checkpoint inhibitors or immune-activating payloads. In this framework, the key validation readout would extend beyond overall tumor accumulation to include boundary-localized nanoparticle distribution, reduction of ECM-associated barrier features, and improved CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration or immunotherapy response across the interface in ex vivo or in vivo settings. Thus, the main design implication of this example is that spatial omics can define not only where physical delivery barriers exist, but also where boundary-targeted modulation may support improved immune access and therapeutic efficacy.

Beyond such boundary-defined barriers and opportunity zones, spatial omics can also identify local biochemical trigger landscapes that may inform conditional nanoparticle activation. As an example of a spatially resolved trigger landscape, Liu et al. identified conserved CAF spatial subtypes and cellular neighborhoods across cancer types using single-cell spatial multi-omics [374]. Among these, a matrix-remodeling CAF program showed elevated expression of MMP7 and MMP14, together with ECM-remodeling features within CAF-enriched stromal regions. This type of omics-defined niche can be translated into nanoparticle engineering by treating MMP7/MMP14 activity not merely as a stromal marker, but as a local biochemical activation cue. For instance, nanoparticles could be designed with an MMP7/MMP14-cleavable peptide linker connecting a protective outer shell, PEG layer, or matrix-penetration-regulating component to the nanoparticle core. Such particles would be expected to remain relatively stable during circulation and early tumor entry, but undergo protease-triggered shell shedding, exposure of a penetration-enhancing surface, size/charge transition, or payload release after encountering MMP-enriched CAF neighborhoods. In this strategy, spatial selectivity would arise less from directing nanoparticles exclusively to MMP7/MMP14-high regions and more from restricting functional activation to these protease-rich stromal niches after tumor accumulation. Therefore, the key validation readout would not simply be higher bulk tumor accumulation, but spatially preferential nanoparticle activation or payload release within MMP7/MMP14-high CAF-rich regions, ideally accompanied by improved penetration through remodeled ECM or enhanced local therapeutic efficacy. Thus, the main design implication of this example is that spatial multi-omics can help define not only where nanoparticles should accumulate, but also where and under what local biochemical conditions they should become functionally activated.

As a final closed-loop anchor example, the glycan-tuned albumin nanoplatform summarized at the end of Table 1 represents one of the closest implementations of the proposed framework, because it links tunable glycan engineering with ST-based receptor-distribution mapping and spatial back-validation within a single experimental trajectory. Park et al. first showed, in a ST-based nanoparticle distribution study, that intratumoral nanoparticle distribution was not adequately explained by conventional vascular markers such as Pecam1 and Cd34, but instead mapped to metabolically defined regions associated with hypoxia- and glycolysis-related programs [34]. In a subsequent glycan-tuned albumin nanoplatform study, Park et al. further demonstrated that distinct glycan modifications altered biodistribution and intratumoral routing, with mannose-associated distribution linked to Mrc1-high TAM/ECM-rich regions and galactose- or glucose-associated uptake mapping to Slc2a1-high glycolytic or hypoxic tumor clusters [298]. These findings suggest that nanoparticle routing can be shaped by spatially organized receptor, transporter, and metabolic microdomains rather than by vascular access alone. From a design perspective, this example moves beyond review-level extrapolation by experimentally tuning the nanoparticle surface and then using spatial omics to evaluate how those modifications redirected intratumoral distribution. The key validation readout in this example is therefore spatial concordance between glycan-dependent nanoparticle routing and the corresponding Mrc1- or Slc2a1-associated tissue regions. Even in this more advanced example, however, the point is not to present these mappings as universal design rules, but to show how spatial biology can be incorporated into structured design–validation cycles with different degrees of translational maturity.

Taken together, the examples discussed in this section illustrate how spatial omics can help move nanoparticle engineering beyond marker-based target selection toward a structured, hypothesis-driven omics-to-design framework, in which spatially defined biological niches guide the generation of formulation hypotheses, the prioritization of tunable nanoparticle design parameters, and the proposal of spatially resolved validation readouts across different levels of translational maturity.

Beyond transcriptome-centric design: complementary multi-omics layers and translational challenges for DDS engineering

Although the translation map presented in Sect. "Omics-to-design translation map for nanoparticle engineering" provides a structured route from spatial omics features to nanoparticle design hypotheses, most actionable features cannot be defined reliably from transcriptomic information alone. This limitation motivates the incorporation of complementary proteomic, metabolomic, lipidomic, and epigenomic layers to refine DDS target discovery, trigger selection, and design validation.

Complementary multi-omics layers for DDS target discovery and design refinement

Transcript abundance often correlates only imperfectly with protein abundance and, in some contexts, the two can diverge substantially. This discordance arises from multiple layers of regulation, including post-transcriptional control, alternative splicing, translational efficiency, protein folding, post-translational modification (such as glycosylation, phosphorylation, and proteolytic processing), and differential protein stability [281, 375]. For nanoparticle targeting, this distinction is not merely technical but directly relevant to design, because ligand engagement depends more directly on receptor availability and accessibility at the protein level than on transcript abundance alone [281, 376, 377]. This consideration highlights the importance of protein-level and spatially resolved validation. Consistent with this, Liu et al. used single-cell spatial multi-omics to define conserved spatial subtypes and cellular neighborhoods of CAFs, illustrating how the integration of spatial transcriptomic and proteomic information can refine the characterization of CAF functional states and spatial organization beyond transcript-only analysis [374].

Beyond the proteome, spatial metabolomics may provide an additional layer of information with potential relevance to DDS design. Spatial metabolomics, particularly when combined with mass spectrometry imaging, can reveal intratumoral metabolic heterogeneity and metabolite–cell type interactions across heterogeneous tissue regions. For example, Sun et al. integrated ST with MSI-based spatial metabolomics and lipidomics in gastric cancer and identified a tumor–normal interface with distinct immunometabolic features [43]. Other omics layers, including spatial lipidomics and chromatin profiling, may further expand this design space [271, 378]. Spatial lipidomics can map membrane composition and lipid metabolic gradients at high, and in some implementations near-single-cell, spatial resolution, potentially informing our understanding of nanoparticle–membrane interactions in ways not readily accessible from transcriptomic or proteomic data alone [379, 380]. Likewise, spatial chromatin accessibility profiling can reveal epigenetic states that govern surface receptor expression and cell identity within intact tissue architecture [381]. However, the translation of these modalities into quantitative nanoparticle design parameters remains at an early stage, and a broadly adopted systematic framework has not yet emerged for integrating lipidomic and epigenomic information with the proteogenomic and metabolomic layers described above [33, 271, 382, 383].

Critical assessment: the distance from multi-omics data to integrated nanoparticle design

Despite this promise, a substantial gap remains between current technical capabilities and the fully integrated, multi-omics-driven nanoparticle design pipeline envisioned in this review. A central challenge is the absence of a unified framework for converting heterogeneous omics layers into quantitative design inputs [33, 384, 385]. Transcriptomic, proteomic, metabolomic, and spatial datasets differ fundamentally in dimensionality, dynamic range, and noise structure [271, 384], and mismatched resolution, for example between spot-level ST and single-cell spatial proteomics, further complicates cross-modal alignment [386, 387]. Methods such as MOFA+ [388] and MaxFuse [386] illustrate the potential of current computational strategies for cross-modal integration, but robust integration across three or more modalities while preserving spatial context remains challenging. These difficulties are compounded by the lack of standardized workflows across spatial omics platforms [271, 389]. Sequencing-based and imaging-based ST approaches differ in gene coverage, spatial resolution, and tissue compatibility [390], yet cross-platform benchmarking remains limited [391]. Downstream analytical steps, including cell segmentation, normalization, and batch correction, also lack consensus, creating sources of bias that can propagate into target nomination [392, 393]. Although frameworks such as SpatialData [394] and Squidpy [395] are improving the storage, integration, and analysis of spatial omics data, standardized benchmark datasets and validation pipelines for translating multi-omics data into nanoparticle design parameters remain limited. As a result, inferred design rules may reflect pipeline-specific artifacts as much as underlying biology, underscoring the need for experimentally validated design criteria.

Even if heterogeneous multi-omics datasets can be successfully integrated computationally, experimental validation of the resulting in silico predictions remains challenging [396]. Current pipelines often proceed on the assumption that receptors enriched within a target niche will contribute to nanoparticle uptake, yet this assumption is not always tested rigorously [397]. Mechanistic validation approaches, including competitive inhibition, receptor-level perturbation, and carefully controlled uptake assays, are not yet consistently applied across receptor-targeted nanomedicine studies [397–399]. Studies by Park and colleagues provide proof-of-concept that spatial transcriptomic signatures can be used to identify molecular correlates of nanoparticle distribution and to inform subsequent targeting strategies, although direct functional validation of the underlying uptake mechanisms remains incomplete [34, 298]. Accordingly, the attrition rate from computationally nominated targets to experimentally validated design parameters remains difficult to define. Translation is further constrained by platform-specific tissue and assay requirements. Although early spatial transcriptomic workflows were often constrained by tissue-preservation requirements, newer formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE)-compatible approaches have expanded access to archival clinical specimens, albeit with platform- and assay-dependent trade-offs in sensitivity, design, and scope [400]. Moreover, the interval from tissue acquisition to profiling, data integration, and design iteration may span weeks to months, potentially limiting alignment with clinical decision-making timelines [401, 402]. High per-sample costs may restrict cohort size, while current regulatory frameworks provide limited specific guidance for design rationales derived from complex multi-omics workflows [403, 404]. Taken together, these challenges, spanning data integration, standardization, validation, and clinical feasibility, indicate that the omics-to-nanomedicine pipeline remains an emerging rather than fully operational framework. The following section therefore considers the translational infrastructure required to close this gap, including clinical precedents, companion diagnostic development, regulatory and chemistry, manufacturing, and controls (CMC) considerations, and remaining translational limitations.

Translational landscape, challenges, and future perspectives

Clinical validation and patient stratification

A critical prerequisite for omics-guided nanomedicine, if it is to move beyond empirical formulation design, is the demonstration that molecular profiling can be translated into clinically actionable treatment decisions [405]. Although developed outside nanomedicine, transcriptomic assays that have already entered routine oncology practice provide an important precedent for this logic. In the landmark Trial Assigning Individualized Options for Treatment (Rx; TAILORx), the 21-gene recurrence score assay (Oncotype DX®) was used to guide adjuvant treatment decisions in patients with HR-positive, HER2-negative, node-negative early breast cancer. Among patients with intermediate recurrence scores, endocrine therapy alone was noninferior to chemoendocrine therapy in the overall intention-to-treat population, indicating that many patients could avoid chemotherapy without compromising clinical outcome, although a modest chemotherapy benefit was observed in some younger patients [406]. These findings established a clinically important precedent: transcriptomic readouts can guide treatment selection and reduce unnecessary toxicity. Although TAILORx does not provide a direct precedent for nanomedicine, it supports the broader clinical logic that molecular profiles can stratify patients into treatment-relevant groups. For omics-guided nanomedicine, the analogous task is to stratify tumors by delivery-relevant TME states and match those states to appropriate nanoparticle formulations [407].

Spatial profiling technologies and remaining translational barriers

To extend patient stratification from conventional transcriptomic assays to TME-guided nanoparticle selection, profiling technologies must be compatible with routine clinical specimens and capable of preserving spatial tissue architecture. The NanoString GeoMx Digital Spatial Profiler (DSP) enables targeted region of interest (ROI)-based transcriptomic and proteomic profiling from FFPE tissue, the standard specimen type in routine clinical pathology [408]. Importantly, this platform does not require fresh-frozen tissue or single-cell dissociation, thereby lowering a major practical barrier to translational adoption [408]. Other spatial platforms further broaden this translational landscape. The 10x Genomics Visium HD platform enables whole-transcriptome spatial profiling on FFPE sections using 2-µm barcoded bins, providing near single-cell-scale spatial information without requiring tissue dissociation [282]. For single-molecule in situ detection, the 10x Genomics Xenium platform provides subcellular-resolution multiplexed transcript imaging on FFPE tissue, with panels ranging from several hundred to several thousand genes, thereby enabling high-resolution mapping of TME architecture at the single-cell level [275]. More recently, the 10x Genomics Atera platform has expanded in situ spatial biology toward near whole-transcriptome profiling of more than 18,000 genes with subcellular resolution and single-cell sensitivity, supporting broad spatial discovery [277]. In parallel, the CODEX method, originally described by Goltsev et al. using a 30-antibody panel on murine splenic cryosections, established a framework for iterative, barcoded mIF immunofluorescence at single-cell resolution [409]. CODEX has since been implemented in commercial multiplex imaging platforms, including the Akoya Biosciences PhenoCycler/CODEX system, and panels of more than 50 protein markers have been applied to FFPE clinical tumor specimens [410]. These multiplex protein-imaging approaches provide complementary spatial readouts at the protein level. Despite this rapid progress, as of April 2026, the spatial profiling platforms discussed here remain primarily marketed for research use only (RUO), and none of these platforms has been cleared or approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for clinical in vitro diagnostic (IVD) use in spatial omics applications. Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments (CLIA)-certified laboratories may, in principle, develop and validate laboratory-developed tests (LDTs) using such technologies, but assay standardization, analytical reproducibility, clinical utility, and regulatory oversight remain unresolved challenges [276, 400, 411, 412]. This gap represents a major translational bottleneck and will be revisited in greater detail in Sect. "Limitations and evidence gaps".

Lessons from the current nanomedicine clinical landscape

Representative oncology nanomedicines that have achieved clinical approval, including pegylated liposomal doxorubicin (PLD; Doxil®/Caelyx®) and albumin-bound paclitaxel (Abraxane®), were developed through empirical formulation optimization rather than omics-guided rational design [413, 414]. Nonetheless, their clinical trajectories offer several important lessons for the paradigm proposed here. First, the clinical experience with PLD illustrates that nanoparticle pharmacokinetics and lesion exposure can vary substantially across patients. An early clinical pharmacokinetic study showed that drug levels in malignant effusions were approximately 4- to 16-fold higher than those obtained with free doxorubicin, whereas subsequent pharmacokinetic studies revealed substantial inter-patient variability in PLD clearance [415, 416]. These observations suggest that even clinically approved nanoparticles may not achieve equivalent circulation, clearance, or disease-site accumulation across patients, reinforcing the need for delivery-relevant patient stratification. Second, Abraxane provides a complementary lesson from a protein-based oncology nanomedicine: clinical translation depends not only on therapeutic rationale, but also on reproducible formulation, robust physicochemical characterization, manufacturing scalability, and control of product-specific quality attributes [414, 417]. Third, personalized neoantigen RNA/mRNA vaccine programs demonstrate that tumor sequencing can already be used to select patient-specific molecular payloads for oncology applications. Autogene cevumeran (BNT122) in resected PDAC and intismeran autogene (V940; formerly mRNA-4157) combined with pembrolizumab in resected melanoma illustrate that omics-selected payloads can be incorporated into RNA-lipoplex (RNA-LPX) or mRNA-lipid nanoparticle (mRNA-LNP) platforms for clinical testing [418–421]. However, these programs personalize the antigenic payload rather than the nanoparticle formulation itself, and therefore do not yet establish a clinical precedent for spatial TME-guided nanoparticle selection.

Taken together, the oncology-focused precedents summarized in Table 2 show that nanoparticle products can reach clinical approval, that their delivery behavior and manufacturability remain dependent on product and patient factors, and that omics can already be used to individualize therapeutic payloads. However, none has yet linked spatially resolved TME profiling to the selection or optimization of the nanoparticle formulation itself. This remains the central translational gap addressed by the omics-guided, subtype-matched strategy proposed in this review.

These precedents provide a practical basis for the next translational question: whether delivery-relevant patient stratification can improve the clinical performance of nanoparticle platforms. In this context, the modest clinical benefit achieved by many oncology nanomedicines relative to their preclinical promise further supports patient selection as a key translational lever [407, 422]. Insufficient stratification according to the TME features that govern nanoparticle accumulation and efficacy may have contributed to the limited clinical translation of many nanomedicine candidates [414]. Omics-guided nanomedicine offers a principled strategy to address this gap, but meaningful clinical validation will require prospective trials in which the nanoparticle formulation and its companion diagnostic are developed in parallel.

Companion diagnostics and biomarker feasibility

The feasibility of translating complex molecular, immune, and spatial features into deployable clinical assays is illustrated by several established biomarker paradigms. The PD-L1 IHC 22C3 pharmDx assay (Agilent/Dako) is an FDA-approved companion diagnostic that assesses PD-L1 protein expression in FFPE tumor tissue and helps identify patients who may be eligible for immune checkpoint inhibitor therapy across multiple tumor types [425]. In several indications, PD-L1 expression is interpreted using the Combined Positive Score (CPS), which incorporates PD-L1 staining in tumor cells, lymphocytes, and macrophages into a single clinically actionable scoring metric [426, 427]. Similarly, the Immunoscore demonstrates that spatial immune-cell distribution, particularly CD3⁺ and CD8⁺ T-cell density in the tumor center and invasive margin, can be converted into a clinically informative immune score [428]. In breast cancer, the Oncotype DX® 21-gene Recurrence Score, validated in the TAILORx trial, illustrates how multigene expression data can be distilled into a quantitative treatment-decision tool [406]. Together, these precedents support the broader translational premise that complex molecular, cellular, and spatial information can be reduced into standardized, clinically interpretable assays. For omics-guided nanomedicine, an analogous companion diagnostic would need to translate high-dimensional spatial omics signatures into an equally interpretable and deployable format that can stratify patients according to delivery-relevant TME states. This translational challenge motivates the tiered pathway proposed below.

A tiered pathway from spatial omics signatures to deployable companion diagnostics

We propose a three-tiered pathway for converting spatial omics signatures into clinically deployable companion diagnostics, progressing from near-term surrogate assays to longer-term spatial multi-omics implementations.

Tier 1 (near-term translational bridge): surrogate IHC and mIF panels. Key stromal and immune markers featured in ST-based characterization of the TME, including POSTN, FAP, SPP1, PD-L1, and CD8, can already be assessed on FFPE sections by conventional IHC or mIF using commercially available antibodies [429]. Analogous to the Immunoscore principle, which uses immune-cell spatial distribution as a clinically informative readout, a spatial scoring algorithm for omics-guided nanomedicine could integrate marker positivity with spatial relationships between delivery-relevant compartments [428]. Candidate features may include CD8⁺ cell density at the tumor-stroma boundary or stromal-myeloid proximity patterns, such as FAP⁺ stromal regions adjacent to SPP1⁺ myeloid compartments. Such scores would initially serve as research-stage surrogates for the full ST-derived signature and would require validation before clinical deployment. Machine-learning models trained on paired ST and mIF datasets from the same tissue blocks could support the development of these surrogate scoring systems, an approach already being explored for immune-phenotype classification [430, ].

Tier 2 (medium-term implementation): targeted gene panels on spatial platforms. Platforms such as NanoString GeoMx DSP and 10x Genomics Visium-based spatial assays enable spatially resolved transcriptomic profiling in FFPE tissue, either through targeted panels or whole-transcriptome workflows, depending on the platform and assay configuration [282, 408]. As a proposed framework, a curated panel of approximately 100 to 200 genes enriched for nanoparticle design-relevant features, including receptor expression, protease isoforms, metabolic enzyme markers, and immune checkpoint molecules, could provide a standardized and focused spatial readout for stratifying patients into predefined nanoparticle-responsive subtypes [431].

Tier 3 (longer-term implementation): full spatial multi-omics in CLIA-certified laboratories. As ST and spatial proteomics technologies improve in analytical reproducibility, workflow efficiency, and cost-effectiveness, their integration into clinical laboratory workflows becomes increasingly conceivable [276, 400]. Reaching this stage, however, will require more than incremental technological refinement. It will also demand regulatory frameworks for spatially resolved diagnostics, including standardized tissue-handling protocols, reference datasets, and external proficiency-testing programs [432].

Regulatory considerations for omics-based companion diagnostics

For omics-based companion diagnostics, the regulatory route will depend on whether the assay is implemented as a laboratory-developed test within a CLIA-certified laboratory or marketed as an IVD device requiring FDA review [433]. LDTs are designed, validated, and performed within a single CLIA-certified laboratory. Historically, they have been overseen primarily under CLIA by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS), while the FDA generally exercised enforcement discretion rather than routinely applying premarket review. Although the FDA attempted to expand its oversight of LDTs through a 2024 final rule, that rule was vacated by a federal district court in March 2025. The FDA therefore issued a September 2025 final rule restoring the prior regulatory text [434, 435]. Within this framework, near-term clinical deployment of Tier 1 surrogate assays may be more realistic than that of Tier 2 or Tier 3 spatial omics diagnostics, either as analytically validated LDTs within CLIA-certified laboratories or through established regulatory paradigms for IHC-based companion diagnostics [436]. For Tier 2 and Tier 3 spatial omics-based diagnostics that are intended to be marketed as IVDs, the regulatory path has historically involved the premarket approval (PMA) pathway for high-risk companion diagnostics [437]. However, the regulatory landscape is evolving. In November 2025, the FDA issued a proposed order to reclassify oncology therapeutic nucleic acid-based test systems from Class III, typically subject to PMA, to Class II with special controls, subject to 510(k) requirements under the proposed classification regulation at 21 CFR 866.6075 [438]. If finalized, this reclassification could, in principle, provide a more streamlined 510(k) pathway for nucleic acid-based spatial omics diagnostics that fall within the final device definition and intended-use scope. However, analytical performance, including precision, reproducibility, and inter-laboratory concordance, would still require rigorous validation, and clinical utility would need to be demonstrated prospectively through an association between the diagnostic result and nanoparticle treatment outcome. For this reason, the co-development of omics-guided nanomedicines and their companion diagnostics from the earliest stages of clinical development will be essential if these evidentiary and regulatory requirements are to be met efficiently [439].

Chemistry, manufacturing, and controls considerations and regulatory pathways

The escalating structural and functional complexity of omics-guided nanomedicines, including multi-ligand targeting, stimuli-responsive release, and patient-stratified formulation selection, imposes correspondingly greater demands on manufacturing scalability, batch-to-batch reproducibility, and regulatory compliance [440]. Even clinically established nanomedicines, such as Doxil/Caelyx and Abraxane, have encountered substantial manufacturing challenges [417]. As discussed in Sect. "From omics insights to executable design principles for precision nanomedicine in the TME", each additional functional module, whether in the form of targeting-ligand choice, responsive-linker chemistry, or spatial-niche specificity, increases not only formulation complexity, but also the analytical burden associated with defining and controlling critical quality attributes, including particle size distribution, polydispersity, surface charge, ligand density, payload loading, release kinetics, sterility, and potency [441].

From fully personalized to stratification-matched nanomedicine

The concept of fully personalized nanomedicine, in which each patient receives a bespoke nanoparticle formulation derived from that individual’s omics profile, is conceptually appealing but remains impractical under current technological and clinical constraints [442]. The cost, manufacturing time, and quality-control infrastructure required to produce, characterize, and release a distinct nanoparticle for every patient are difficult to reconcile with the operational realities of clinical oncology [442]. At the same time, as summarized in Table 2, personalized RNA/mRNA neoantigen vaccine programs provide a partial proof-of-concept that patient-specific molecular payloads can be selected using tumor sequencing and manufactured at clinical grade, even though they do not personalize the nanoparticle formulation itself [418, 420, 443]. Sahin et al. first demonstrated this concept in melanoma, reporting that individualized RNA mutanome vaccines could be manufactured, quality-controlled, and administered within a clinically relevant timeframe while eliciting poly-specific antitumor immunity [443]. More recent programs, including autogene cevumeran and intismeran autogene, further show that patient-specific RNA/mRNA payloads can be incorporated into RNA-LPX or mRNA-LNP platforms for clinical oncology testing [418, 420].

Extrapolating from this precedent, the most realistic translational path for omics-guided nanomedicine is unlikely to be fully bespoke nanoparticle design for each patient, but rather a stratification-matched model based on a finite library of pre-validated formulations. Under this model, spatial omics profiling would classify each patient’s tumor into predefined TME subtypes, such as desmoplastic-dominant, immune-excluded, or highly vascularized states, with each subtype linked to a corresponding standardized nanoparticle formulation. This approach preserves the specificity advantages of omics-informed treatment selection while retaining the manufacturing scalability of standardized products. The logic is analogous to HER2 IHC or fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) testing for trastuzumab selection: the diagnostic stratifies the patient, but does not require the creation of a new therapeutic for each individual [444]. This model is operationalized through the closed-loop workflow introduced in Sect. "Omics-to-design translation framework: a closed-loop workflow" (Fig. 6). Within this framework, the companion diagnostic approach outlined in Sect. "Companion diagnostics and biomarker feasibility" would assign patients to the corresponding pre-validated formulation.

Navigating the regulatory landscape for complex nanomedicines

The regulatory pathway for omics-guided nanomedicines will depend on both product classification and formulation complexity. The FDA’s 2022 guidance on drug products containing nanomaterials, finalized from a 2017 draft, provides an initial framework for physicochemical characterization, biocompatibility testing, and manufacturing controls, but does not yet address the added complexity introduced by omics-informed and multifunctional nanomedicine designs [404]. Although outside oncology, the clinical deployment of COVID-19 mRNA-LNP vaccines provides an important platform-level precedent for scalable manufacturing, quality control, and regulatory approval of LNP-enabled mRNA products [414, 445–447]. This precedent does not establish efficacy for TME-targeted oncology applications, but it supports the broader translational feasibility of LNP-based nucleic acid delivery platforms that can be adapted to oncology programs, including individualized mRNA neoantigen therapies.

Several regulatory issues deserve particular attention in the context of omics-guided nanomedicine. First, product classification may become more complex. Multifunctional nanoparticles that combine therapeutic payloads, targeting modules, and diagnostic-linked selection logic may raise combination-product or coordinated-review considerations, particularly when drug, biologic, device, or companion diagnostic components are developed together [448]. Second, the analytical characterization burden expands with design complexity. Each functional module, including the targeting ligand, responsive linker, and therapeutic payload, must be independently characterized for identity, purity, potency, and stability, and these same attributes must also be shown to remain controlled in the fully assembled nanoparticle [12]. Third, when nanoparticle selection depends on a companion diagnostic result, the drug and diagnostic must be co-developed and, in many cases, co-approved [449]. Although this adds regulatory complexity, it also provides an embedded patient-selection strategy that may improve the likelihood of clinical trial success [405].

Despite these challenges, the regulatory environment is evolving in ways that may ultimately favor precision nanomedicine [450]. Increasing regulatory acceptance of real-world evidence, adaptive trial designs, and biomarker-driven enrichment strategies creates a framework within which omics-guided nanomedicine trials could be designed more efficiently [451]. The critical requirement, however, is that CMC and regulatory planning be integrated into the earliest stages of the omics-to-design pipeline, rather than deferred until after the scientific concept has been established [452]. Nevertheless, early CMC and regulatory integration does not by itself resolve the evidence gaps that will determine whether omics-guided formulation selection is biologically robust, systemically safe, and clinically testable.

Limitations and evidence gaps

Although the framework outlined in Sect. "Clinical validation and patient stratification" to "Chemistry, manufacturing, and controls considerations and regulatory pathways" provides a coherent translational pathway, four interrelated gaps currently constrain its clinical implementation: the spatiotemporal limitations of TME profiling, incompletely characterized systemic safety and clearance, methodological weaknesses in current preclinical studies, and the lack of clinical evidence from trials in which spatial omics prospectively guides nanoparticle formulation design. This section addresses each in turn.

Spatiotemporal TME dynamics challenge the robustness of snapshot-based designs

A major vulnerability of omics-guided formulation selection is its dependence on a molecular snapshot of the TME, typically derived from a single biopsy obtained at a single time point, to guide the design of a nanoparticle that may be administered days or weeks later. The TME, however, is inherently dynamic. It undergoes continuous remodeling in response to tumor progression, hypoxic adaptation, immune editing, and, critically, therapeutic intervention itself [13]. Chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immunotherapy can rapidly alter cellular composition, receptor expression, and spatial tissue architecture. As a result, an omics-derived design blueprint may no longer fully reflect the relevant TME state by the time the engineered nanoparticle is delivered. In parallel, a single core-needle biopsy samples only a limited fraction of the total tumor volume and may therefore fail to capture the full extent of intratumoral spatial heterogeneity, yielding designs optimized for one region but suboptimal for others [453].

Addressing these temporal and spatial sampling limitations will require dynamic TME monitoring strategies that extend beyond conventional tissue biopsy. Liquid biopsy approaches, including circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), extracellular vesicles, and circulating immune-cell profiling, offer the possibility of minimally invasive longitudinal monitoring that could detect treatment-induced shifts in the TME and guide adaptive nanoparticle selection, for example by enabling a switch from one formulation to another within the stratification-matched framework proposed in Sect. "Chemistry, manufacturing, and controls considerations and regulatory pathways" [454]. Imaging modalities, including PET-based receptor mapping and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based perfusion imaging, may provide complementary whole-tumor spatial information [455]. However, neither liquid biopsy nor imaging currently approaches the combined molecular and spatial resolution of tissue-based omics profiling, and their integration into the closed-loop workflow remains unresolved [454].

Systemic safety, clearance, and long-term toxicity

The safety profile of increasingly complex multifunctional nanoparticles also remains insufficiently characterized [456]. A meta-analysis by Wilhelm et al. reported that a median of only 0.7% of the administered nanoparticle dose reaches the tumor, whereas a large fraction of the remaining dose is cleared by mononuclear phagocyte system (MPS)-rich organs, including the liver and spleen [457]. For multifunctional nanoparticles carrying bioactive payloads, including immune agonists, protease inhibitors, or epigenetic modulators, this off-target distribution represents not only an efficacy limitation, but also a potentially significant safety liability [456, 457]. Moreover, because stimuli-responsive mechanisms must balance circulation stability against intratumoral responsiveness, a portion of the payload may be released in the systemic circulation or within MPS-rich organs such as the liver and spleen, compounding the safety concerns raised above [458, 459].

PEGylation, one of the most widely used strategies for prolonging nanoparticle circulation, introduces an additional immunological complication. Repeated administration of PEGylated nanoparticles can induce anti-PEG antibodies, immunoglobulin M (IgM), leading to the accelerated blood clearance (ABC) phenomenon. This response can reduce efficacy upon repeat dosing and may also contribute to hypersensitivity reactions [460]. Because omics-guided nanomedicines may require serial administration in order to address evolving TME states, this issue is particularly relevant in the present context.

In vivo imaging-based pharmacokinetic and biodistribution analysis may help address several of these safety and clearance gaps. Quantitative PET, SPECT, MRI, fluorescence imaging, or multimodal imaging can enable longitudinal whole-body tracking of circulation kinetics, tumor uptake, MPS-organ accumulation, renal or hepatobiliary clearance, and repeated-dose distribution changes within the same subject. In particular, radiolabeled nanoparticle PET/SPECT imaging can provide quantitative, time-resolved information on biodistribution and nonspecific liver or spleen uptake, complementing single-time-point ex vivo biodistribution studies [461, 462]. When integrated with ex vivo gamma counting, autoradiography, histology, and spatial omics, imaging-based PK analysis can further distinguish whether nanoparticles remain confined to vascular or MPS-rich compartments, penetrate target tumor regions, or accumulate in normal organs that may become sites of delayed toxicity. Therefore, image-guided PK and safety assessment should be incorporated into the preclinical evaluation of omics-guided nanomedicines, especially for multifunctional or repeatedly administered formulations.

More broadly, many published studies still emphasize short-term efficacy endpoints, often based on tumor growth inhibition in one or a limited number of animal models, without systematic assessment of chronic toxicity, repeated-dose pharmacokinetics, long-term organ accumulation, or the immunological consequences of sustained exposure [456]. As a result, many published preclinical studies still fall short of the dose-response characterization and comprehensive toxicology datasets expected for investigational new drug (IND)-enabling development [456, 463]. Addressing these gaps will be essential to support broader clinical translation.

Methodological limitations of current preclinical studies

Beyond safety, the current preclinical evidence base is limited by methodological weaknesses and an incomplete mechanistic understanding of nanoparticle-tumor interactions, both of which restrict interpretability and translational relevance [326, 464].

First, many nanoparticle studies still employ subcutaneous xenograft models in immunodeficient mice [65], despite the fact that these models poorly recapitulate the architecturally complex and immunologically active microenvironment of human tumors [465]. They lack functional adaptive immunity, display artificially simplified stromal composition, and develop vasculature that differs fundamentally from clinical tumors in permeability, perfusion, and endothelial phenotype [465]. Orthotopic implantation models, patient-derived xenografts (PDX), and humanized mouse systems offer progressively more realistic TME reconstruction [465, 466]. However, their higher cost, longer study duration, and increased technical difficulty have limited their broader adoption in nanomedicine research [463]. For omics-guided nanomedicine, this limitation is particularly consequential. The validity of the design logic depends on how faithfully the omics data capture the relevant TME state. If the preclinical model does not reproduce the relevant human TME states, then the design rationale remains only partially tested.

Second, systematic dose-response analysis is notably absent from most nanoparticle studies [463]. The standard design typically compares a single nanoparticle dose against free drug and untreated controls, without examining dose-dependent efficacy, dose-dependent toxicity, or the therapeutic window [463]. Under these conditions, it becomes difficult to determine whether an omics-guided design genuinely improves the therapeutic index or merely changes the apparent dose-response relationship without widening the therapeutic window.

Third, direct head-to-head comparisons between omics-guided nanoparticles and conventionally designed nanoparticles remain rare, even though such comparisons represent the clearest test of whether spatial omics information adds tangible value to nanoparticle engineering [463]. In the absence of these studies, it remains uncertain whether the additional cost and complexity of omics-guided design translate into clinically meaningful gains. Future studies should therefore incorporate such comparisons as a routine component of experimental design rather than treating them as secondary analyses.

Future studies should align model selection with the specific TME feature being targeted, using desmoplastic orthotopic or patient-derived models for stromal-penetrating designs and immunocompetent or humanized systems for immune-modulatory formulations. Mechanistic validation should extend beyond total tumor accumulation to include receptor engagement, linker cleavage, payload activation, and spatial back-validation within the intended omics-defined niche. Dose-response studies should also be paired with PK/PD analyses to determine whether improved efficacy reflects enhanced exposure, penetration, release, or true TME-specific action. Finally, omics-guided nanoparticles should be benchmarked against non-targeted and conventionally targeted nanoparticles under matched formulation conditions to isolate the added value of omics-guided design.

The clinical translation gap: lack of trials for omics-guided nanoparticle design

Perhaps the most notable gap in the current translational landscape is the lack of clinical evidence from trials in which multi-omics data are prospectively used to guide nanoparticle formulation selection or delivery-related design-parameter optimization [49, 407]. The closest precedents arise not from nanoparticle formulation engineering, but from personalized mRNA vaccine programs in which omics profiling determines the therapeutic payload delivered by an otherwise fixed nanoparticle platform [418, 420]. Autogene cevumeran exemplifies this approach by using tumor-normal whole-exome sequencing (WES), tumor RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), and human leukocyte antigen (HLA) typing to computationally select up to 20 patient-specific neoantigens. These selected neoantigens are then encoded into an individualized mRNA vaccine and delivered via an RNA-LPX nanoparticle [418]. In the Phase I evaluation of autogene cevumeran in resected PDAC, vaccine-induced neoantigen-specific T-cell responses were associated with longer recurrence-free survival (RFS) at 3.2-year follow-up, supporting further Phase II evaluation [419, 423]. Separately, a Phase IIb follow-up study of intismeran autogene, an individualized neoantigen therapy guided by tumor WES, matched normal blood WES, tumor RNA-seq, and HLA typing, reported sustained improvements in RFS and distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) when intismeran autogene was combined with pembrolizumab in resected melanoma. The program is currently being evaluated in Phase III [420, 421, 424].

These programs establish an important proof-of-principle: omics data can be translated into a patient-specific therapeutic payload and incorporated into a nanoparticle-delivered therapy within clinical development workflows [418, 420, 421]. At the same time, they differ fundamentally from the paradigm proposed in this review. Personalized neoantigen vaccines individualize the therapeutic cargo, namely the mRNA payload sequence, while leaving the delivery platform itself fixed as a standardized LNP or RNA-LPX formulation. By contrast, the omics-guided nanomedicine framework envisioned here seeks to individualize the delivery strategy itself, including targeting ligands, size, surface charge, and responsive release mechanisms, on the basis of each patient’s spatially resolved TME profile. This added layer of design complexity, which requires co-optimization of both payload and platform, represents a qualitatively greater translational challenge and explains, in large part, why such trials have not yet emerged [407].

Three structural barriers further impede clinical entry. First, as discussed in Sect. "Chemistry, manufacturing, and controls considerations and regulatory pathways", the co-development of an omics-based companion diagnostic and a matched nanoparticle formulation may raise combination-product or coordinated-review considerations, increasing regulatory complexity and development cost [448, 449]. Second, the spatial omics assays that underlie the design logic have not yet achieved the degree of analytical standardization required for clinical deployment. Most spatial omics platforms have not yet been validated as LDTs for this intended use or cleared or approved by the FDA for clinical spatial-omics-based diagnostic applications. In addition, per-assay costs remain high for routine deployment, and rapid changes in platform availability, assay chemistry, and vendor-specific workflows introduce additional uncertainty into clinical implementation [276, 400, 411, 412]. Third, although preclinical proof-of-concept studies have demonstrated that spatial TME features can correlate with intratumoral nanoparticle distribution, biomarker-guided patient stratification strategies linking tumor biology to clinical nanoparticle treatment outcomes remain to be established at scale, particularly in the spatial omics context [310, 407, 422]. Without such evidence, the rationale for launching prospective omics-guided trials remains difficult to justify economically and operationally.

Bridging this translation gap will require pragmatic and incremental strategies rather than a direct leap toward early-phase trials in which nanoparticle formulation selection is prospectively guided by spatial omics profiles. Two pragmatic and near-term strategies are particularly feasible. One is to incorporate spatial-omics-based stratification arms into ongoing or planned basket and umbrella trials of existing nanomedicines, thereby generating paired omics-response datasets within established clinical trial frameworks [467]. The other is to perform retrospective spatial profiling of archival tumor tissue from completed trials of clinically established nanomedicines, such as PLD, Abraxane, or liposomal irinotecan (Onivyde®), in order to identify spatial TME features associated with differential response to nanoparticle therapy [276, 400]. Together, such efforts could provide the correlative evidence base needed to support future prospective studies.

Future directions: toward a closed-loop translational workflow

Enabling spatial technologies and computational infrastructure

Realizing the full potential of omics-guided precision nanomedicine will require convergent progress across interlocking technological, computational, and infrastructural fronts. On the technology side, the rapid maturation of spatial profiling platforms is beginning to lower technical barriers to adoption [276, 468]. Next-generation ST platforms and imaging-based methods, including 10x Visium HD, MERFISH, and Stereo-seq, now offer substantially improved spatial resolution, in some cases reaching near-subcellular or subcellular scales, while commercial accessibility and technical performance continue to improve [276, 468]. Although spatial profiling has not yet matched the scale or cost trajectory of next-generation sequencing, recent improvements in resolution, FFPE compatibility, and commercial accessibility suggest a gradual movement toward broader adoption [469].

Equally important are advances in computational infrastructure. Scalable frameworks such as Scanpy for single-cell analysis [470] and Squidpy for spatial omics [471] have made sophisticated analytical workflows more accessible, while emerging foundation models may further generalize biological patterns across datasets, tissues, and disease contexts. Models such as scGPT, pretrained on over 33 million cells, demonstrate that generative artificial intelligence (AI) can learn transferable representations of single-cell multi-omics [472]. For omics-guided nanomedicine, a key extension would be to link these representations to delivery-relevant variables, such as receptor accessibility, stromal barriers, immune-excluded niches, and spatially restricted uptake patterns. As foundation-model approaches expand into spatial biology, they may eventually support nanoparticle design-specific tasks, including prediction of uptake from spatial receptor profiles or identification of optimal trigger signatures from multimodal datasets. However, the expertise required to interpret high-dimensional spatial multi-omics remains concentrated in well-resourced academic centers, creating a workforce bottleneck that technical accessibility alone cannot resolve [276]. Lowering this interpretive barrier will therefore require automated end-to-end analysis pipelines and domain-specific foundation models tailored to spatial biology [473]. Early examples already point in this direction. Nicheformer, a transformer-based model trained on more than 110 million cells, integrates single-cell and spatial modalities [474], whereas Novae provides a graph-based foundation model for tissue segmentation in ST data [475]. In parallel, emerging conceptual frameworks for spatial-omics foundation-model development are likely to accelerate broader adoption of omics-guided nanomedicine design across both research and clinical environments [473]. Ultimately, improved experimental and computational cost-effectiveness will be essential if omics-guided nanomedicine is to become a routine design paradigm rather than remain a specialized academic exercise [469].

AI-driven design optimization and high-throughput screening

The central challenge of the closed-loop workflow, namely the translation of omics data into nanoparticle design parameters, is fundamentally a mapping problem. It requires connecting high-dimensional biological input spaces to multiparametric nanoparticle design spaces, a task for which machine learning is particularly well suited [476, 477]. When coupled with high-throughput experimental platforms, AI-driven optimization could substantially accelerate the design-test-learn cycle [476]. DNA-barcoded nanoparticle libraries, pioneered by Dahlman and colleagues, enable multiplexed in vivo screening of nucleic acid delivery across dozens of nanoparticle formulations in a single experiment [295]. Sago et al. further extended this strategy by developing the Fast Identification of Nanoparticle Delivery (FIND) platform, which quantified functional mRNA delivery from more than 250 LNPs in vivo and identified endothelial-tropic formulations capable of supporting Cas9 mRNA/single guide RNA (sgRNA)-mediated gene editing [296]. Together, these high-throughput platforms can generate large-scale paired datasets linking nanoparticle composition, biodistribution, and functional delivery, thereby providing the experimental substrate needed to train predictive design models. If these high-throughput functional delivery and biodistribution measurements are integrated with spatially resolved omics data from matched tumor models, they could form the basis of machine-learning systems that predict or prioritize nanoparticle accumulation and activity in defined TME niches as a function of particle properties, thereby making the parameterized nanoparticle design step (Step 4) of the closed-loop workflow illustrated in Fig. 6 more systematic and experimentally guided.

Looking further ahead, the concept of a digital twin, that is, a patient-specific computational model of the TME that integrates multimodal data to simulate nanoparticle behavior in silico before physical synthesis, could in principle reduce the number of experimental iteration cycles needed to converge on an optimized design [478]. Fully realized spatial-omics-parameterized nanoparticle digital twins remain aspirational. However, intermediate implementations that couple agent-based models of nanoparticle-tumor interactions with mechanistic mathematical frameworks are already feasible and could provide useful design guidance as they are progressively integrated with spatial omics data [479, 480]. In line with this direction, a recent spatial transcriptomics based tumor microenvironment pharmacokinetic modeling study provides a relevant intermediate example. Bae et al. developed an in silico model incorporating spatial target expression, endothelial density, vessel distribution, and linker-cleavage enzyme activity to simulate antibody-drug conjugate payload distribution. After validation against experimental antibody distribution imaging, the model was applied to spatial transcriptomic datasets from 52 stomach adenocarcinoma patients and showed that optimal antibody binding affinity depends on both target-expression level and spatial tumor microenvironment organization [294]. Although developed for antibody-drug conjugate payload dynamics rather than nanoparticle formulation selection itself, this study illustrates how spatial omics, kinetic modeling, and imaging-based validation can provide a practical stepping stone toward digital twin-like nanomedicine design.

Standardization and community infrastructure

The maturation of omics-guided nanomedicine will also require field-specific community standards and shared infrastructure, which remain underdeveloped [481]. Three priorities are especially important. First, benchmark datasets: the field needs publicly available paired datasets linking spatial omics profiles of tumor tissue with nanoparticle biodistribution, efficacy, and toxicity data generated in the same models. Such resources would allow direct evaluation of how well omics-derived predictions translate into actual delivery outcomes. Second, standardized reporting guidelines: analogous to the minimum information about a microarray experiment (MIAME) framework that helped standardize gene expression research, the field would benefit from consensus criteria defining the minimum omics, nanoparticle characterization, and in vivo validation data required for a study to credibly claim omics-guided design [481]. Third, open-source computational platforms: an integrated and openly accessible software environment connecting spatial omics analysis, target and trigger prioritization, nanoparticle parameter mapping based on the logic outlined in Table 1, and spatial back-validation analyses would substantially reduce the barrier to entry and facilitate broader implementation of the closed-loop workflow.

The convergence of these developments, including increasingly accessible spatial profiling, AI-augmented design, high-throughput screening, and community standardization, positions omics-guided nanomedicine as an increasingly tractable experimental frontier rather than a distant aspiration [458, 482]. Realizing this potential will require not only continued technological progress, but also a cultural shift toward integrated and interdisciplinary research programs that bring together spatial biologists, materials scientists, computational engineers, and clinical investigators from the earliest stages of the design process [458]. Together, these technological, computational, regulatory, and collaborative foundations will be essential for converting the closed-loop omics-to-design workflow into a clinically actionable strategy in which spatially defined TME states guide the selection of pre-validated, subtype-matched nanomedicine formulations.

Conclusions

The TME constitutes a hierarchically organized and spatially heterogeneous ecosystem in which ECM architecture, vascular–lymphatic dysfunction, hypoxia, acidosis, and immunosuppressive cellular networks collectively impose major biophysical and biological barriers to nanoparticle transport and therapeutic efficacy. Although extensive efforts have produced tumor microenvironment-targeted and stimulus-responsive nanomedicine platforms, most current designs remain largely empirical and rely on generalized tumor cues rather than patient- and region-specific determinants of nanoparticle access, activation, and response. Recent advances in single-cell profiling, spatial transcriptomics, and complementary multi-omics technologies now provide the resolution needed to redefine the TME as a collection of delivery-relevant niches, including stromal subtypes, endothelial states, proteolytic or metabolic trigger landscapes, immune-excluded interfaces, and spatially organized cell–cell interaction hubs.

The next frontier of precision nanomedicine lies not simply in generating richer tumor maps, but in converting these maps into executable design and validation logic. Multi-omics can integrate transcriptomic information with proteomic, metabolomic, lipidomic, and epigenomic layers, while spatial multi-omics can position these features within native tissue architecture. This integration is essential because transcript abundance alone cannot reliably define receptor accessibility, enzymatic activity, metabolite availability, membrane composition, or functional targetability. Accordingly, omics-guided nanomedicine should move beyond using spatial datasets as descriptive or post hoc explanatory tools and instead use them to nominate actionable targets and triggers, prioritize tunable nanoparticle parameters, and define spatially resolved validation readouts.

The evidence discussed in this review supports a conceptual shift from empirical tumor microenvironment-responsive nanomedicine toward a closed-loop, data-driven engineering workflow. In this framework, spatially and functionally resolved omics data are used to define delivery-relevant tumor states, nominate and validate targets or release triggers, translate these features into parameterized nanoparticle design criteria, and back-validate the resulting formulations against observed biodistribution, payload release, and therapeutic response. Such back-validation can be strengthened by incorporating imaging-based pharmacokinetic and biodistribution assessment, autoradiography, histology, multiplex immunostaining, and spatial omics overlays, allowing nanoparticle behavior to be evaluated against the molecular tissue niches that originally motivated the design.

Clinical implementation, however, will require pragmatic simplification. Although precision nanomedicine ultimately aims to tailor therapy to the biological context of each tumor, near-term translation is unlikely to depend on fully bespoke formulations for individual patients. A more feasible model is stratification-matched nanomedicine, in which tumors sharing recurrent and clinically meaningful microenvironmental features are assigned to a finite library of pre-validated nanoparticle formulations. This approach preserves the biological specificity of omics-informed patient selection while remaining more compatible with manufacturing scalability, batch reproducibility, quality control, and regulatory requirements. Its success will depend on companion diagnostics that reduce high-dimensional spatial omics signatures into clinically interpretable assays or scores, as well as on studies linking spatial TME states to nanoparticle biodistribution and clinical outcomes.

Realizing this vision will require stronger evidentiary standards, including human-relevant tumor models, mechanistic target and trigger validation, dose-response and pharmacokinetic/pharmacodynamic analyses, head-to-head benchmarking against conventional nanoparticle designs, and systematic assessment of systemic safety, clearance, repeated dosing, and long-term organ accumulation. CMC considerations should also be incorporated from the earliest stages of design, because each added targeting ligand, responsive linker, or niche-specific module increases analytical and regulatory complexity. Ultimately, the future of precision nanomedicine will depend not only on mapping the TME with increasing resolution, but also on translating those maps into simplified, testable, manufacturable, and clinically tractable design frameworks that enable reproducible, biologically matched therapeutic implementation and meaningful patient benefit.
