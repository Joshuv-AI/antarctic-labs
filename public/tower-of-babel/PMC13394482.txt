Catalytic asymmetric synthesis of inherently chiral tricyclic diazepinones: Access to (+)-telenzepine
Authors: Cui Zhang, Xiang-Tao Kong, Chuanjun Song, Yun-Xuan Chen, Xiao Xiao, Shi-Kun Jia, Guang-Jian Mei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Molecules with inherent chirality, which stems from their overall conformation rather than local stereogenic centers, present a notable synthetic challenge. This is especially evident for bioactive, saddle-shaped tricyclic diazepinones, whose conformational flexibility has hampered asymmetric synthesis. Herein, we address this issue through a late-stage organocatalytic asymmetric N-H alkylation strategy. By introducing torsional strain via strategic N-H functionalization, we lock the molecular chiral conformation and enable direct synthesis of diverse enantioenriched tricyclic diazepinones from prochiral precursors. The modular nature of this approach is demonstrated through the kinetic resolution of racemic substrates, as well as in, to our knowledge, the first enantioselective synthesis and absolute configuration confirmation of the drug molecule (+)-telenzepine.

INTRODUCTION

Chirality, a fundamental molecular property describing non-superimposable mirror images, is a cornerstone of chemistry and pharmacology. The profound, often dramatic differences in biological activity between enantiomers underscore a central tenet: molecular shape governs function (1–3). This principle drives the continuous pursuit of stereoselective synthesis. Traditionally, chirality is defined by local stereogenic elements, such as stereocenters, axes, or planes (Fig. 1A). Consequently, molecules featuring these classical motifs have been extensively explored (4–7). In contrast, an often-overlooked class of chiral molecules exists that lacks any local stereogenic unit, deriving its asymmetry instead from the system’s global topology or conformation. This is exemplified by a distinct class of conformationally rigid, sp2-rich organic architectures, including helicenes (8), annulenes (9), and sumanenes (10), where well-defined three-dimensional shapes endow them with inherent chirality (11–14). For a subset of these, particularly medium-sized rings, chirality emerges not from a single atom or bond, but from the spatial arrangement of the entire framework (15–17). Such molecules, whose conformational space is distinct from that of their mirror images, are often classified as “inherently chiral” in literature, representing a paradigm beyond classical stereogenic elements (18–30).

Tricyclic diazepinones, core scaffold of many tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), embody this concept (Fig. 1B) (31–33). As medium-sized rings with a high degree of sp2-hybridization, they can exhibit inherent chirality by adopting stable saddle-shaped chiral conformations, which interconvert via a sequence of concerted bond torsions (34). The pharmacological significance of this chirality is starkly illustrated by telenzepine, where the (+)-enantiomer exhibits a remarkable 500-fold higher potency at the muscarinic receptor than its (−)-counterpart (35–37). Such dramatic efficacy differences underscore the critical need for stereoselective synthesis. However, accessing these scaffolds in enantiopure form remains a notable challenge with most approaches relying on post-synthetic resolutions (38). A recent advance by Miller employed an iminophosphorane-catalyzed SNAr cyclization to construct chiral seven-membered rings (Fig. 1C) (39). While elegant, this method requires a strategically positioned blocking group on the nucleophile for the reaction to proceed effectively, narrowing its substrate scope. Therefore, the development of a general and versatile catalytic asymmetric route to such intriguing tricyclic architectures remains a highly desirable yet unmet goal.

Herein, we report a modular strategy for the construction of chiral tricyclic diazepinones, pivoting on a key late-stage organocatalytic asymmetric N-H alkylation (Fig. 1D). The conformational flexibility of these scaffolds often obscures their inherent chirality, posing a persistent challenge for asymmetric synthesis. We envisioned that strategic N-H functionalization could introduce sufficient torsional strain to enforce a stable chiral conformation. This approach efficiently afforded a diverse array of enantioenriched tricyclic diazepinones directly from prochiral precursors in good yield and with excellent enantioselectivity. The methodology also proved effective for the kinetic resolution of racemic substrates. To demonstrate its synthetic utility, we achieved, to our knowledge, the first enantioselective synthesis of (+)-telenzepine and unequivocally confirmed its absolute configuration.

RESULTS

Thermal racemization studies

Our investigation began with the study of the ring-inversion barrier of the tricyclic system, which revealed key structural influences on conformational stability (Fig. 2). Substrate 1a (R = H), prepared according to literature methods (40–42), displayed a low ring-inversion barrier (21.7 kcal/mol), consistent with the failure to resolve its enantiomers by chiral HPLC. Given that the non-planar conformation is governed by 1,3-bond interactions, we systematically examined methyl substitution at the 6′-, 6-, 3-, and 3′-positions. Incorporating a methyl group at the 6′-position increased the inversion barrier near a threshold range (23.2 kcal/mol) (43), allowing partial HPLC separation, though the chiral conformer remained labile at ambient temperature. A methyl substituent at the 6-position induced greater steric constraint, resulting in a ring-inversion barrier of 25.7 kcal/mol. In contrast, methylation at the 3- or 3′-position substantially enhanced conformational stability, affording high inversion barriers (ΔG‡ = 30.4 and 30.5 kcal/mol, respectively). These findings establish the position effect trend as 6′ < 6 < 3 ≈ 3′. Notably, N-H alkylation using Morita−Baylis−Hillman (MBH) adducts further raised conformational stability across the series (all ≥28.0 kcal/mol). To explain why N-alkylation substantially raises the racemization barrier, we have analyzed the DFT-optimized ground and transition state geometries as well as the Laplacian bond orders (see fig. S10 and table S13 for details). Therefore, prochiral 1a is amenable to direct catalytic asymmetric N-H alkylation, whereas 6′-methyl derivative is suitable for dynamic kinetic resolution, and 6-, 3- or 3′-methyl analogs are applicable to kinetic resolution.

Reaction condition optimizations

Optimization of the catalytic asymmetric N-alkylation of 1a is summarized in Fig. 3. Our initial conditions employing hydroquinidine (C1) in dichloromethane (DCM) at room temperature provided product 3a in 90% yield and 83% ee (entry 1). A systematic investigation of solvent effects identified chloroform (CHCl3) as optimal (entries 2–6). Subsequent screening of readily accessible cinchona alkaloid-based catalysts confirmed their general efficacy but afforded no further enhancement (entries 7–11). Ultimately, the addition of 3 Å molecular sieves (30 mg) proved critical, delivering 3a in 96% yield and 93% ee (entry 12).

Substrate scope investigation

Having established the optimal reaction conditions, we proceeded to evaluate the substrate scope. As illustrated in Fig. 4, the reaction demonstrated broad applicability across a range of MBH adducts. Although high yields were consistently achieved, the ee of the products decreased with diminishing steric bulk of the ester group (3a–3e). We next investigated the influence of substituents on the benzoyl moiety. Substituents at the para- and meta-positions were well tolerated, with electronic properties exerting minimal impact on the reaction outcome. Under these conditions, tricyclic products 3f–3n were obtained in 90%–98% yields and 90%–93% ee. In contrast, ortho-substituents exhibited a pronounced electronic effect: an electron-donating methyl group (3o) afforded a product with higher ee than an electron-withdrawing fluoro substituent (3p). Additionally, substrates bearing disubstituted (3q) or trisubstituted (3r) benzoyl groups proved compatible. The method was also extended to other arylformyl substrates, including furanoyl (3s), thienoyl (3t), and naphthoyl derivatives (3u and 3v), all of which delivered the corresponding products in good yields with excellent enantioselectivities. Replacement of the benzoyl group with an acetyl group maintained a comparable level of enantiocontrol; however, a notable reduction in yield was observed.

We then examined the influence of substituents on rings A and C (Fig. 5). The A ring was found to exert a subtle yet discernible effect on the reaction outcome. Substituents at the 5-position were largely insensitive to electronic properties, affording products 3x–3a’ in 91%–95% yields with 91%–94% ee. At the 4-position, both methoxy (3b’) and halogen groups (3c’–3e’) were well tolerated; however, a strongly electron-withdrawing trifluoromethyl group (3f’) led to a slight reduction in enantioselectivity. A similar electronic trend was observed in disubstituted substrates, a dimethoxylated analog (3g’) gave higher ee than its difluoro-substituted counterpart (3h’). In contrast, the C ring proved insensitive to both the position and nature of substituents. Mono-, di-, and fused-ring substrates were all compatible, delivering the corresponding products 3l’–3s’ in 91%–97% yields and 92%–94% ee. Notably, the standard conditions also enabled dynamic kinetic resolution of 6′- and 6-substituted tricyclic diazepinones (3t’–3w’).

Kinetic resolution

Building on these successful results, we sought to apply the catalytic strategy to the kinetic resolution of racemic tricyclic diazepinones (Fig. 6). After further optimization, catalyst C2 was identified as optimal (see tables S2 and S3 for details), enabling the reaction to proceed efficiently and deliver N-alkylated products with good to excellent selectivity factors. For example, modifying the ester moiety of the MBH adducts 2 from CO2tBu to CO2nBu or CO2Me afforded products 5a–5c in good yields and high enantioselectivities (91%–93% ee). The N-protecting group on the B ring exerted a discernible influence on enantiocontrol. Switching from phenylacetyl to benzoyl (5d), acetyl (5e), or pivalyl (5f) led to a slight decrease in ee. In addition to methyl, other substituents at the 3-position, such as fluoro, methoxy, and even a fused phenyl ring, were well tolerated, affording the corresponding products 5g–5i in 91%–99% ee. Substrates featuring an additional methyl group at the 4- or 5-position (5j and 5k) were also suitable, yielding selectivity factors ranging from 115 to 133. Replacement of the A ring with a heteroaromatic system such as thiophene (5l), a motif of relevance in drug synthesis, proved feasible. Substituents on the C ring had minimal impact on the kinetic resolution efficiency (5m–5p), with s factors spanning 37 to 129. We further evaluated the effect of blocking group position on the reaction outcome. A methyl group at the 3′-position was well accommodated, delivering the N-alkylated product 5q in 92% ee and the recovered starting material 4q’ in 91% ee. The absolute configuration of the product was unambiguously established by single-crystal x-ray diffraction analysis of compound 5f (CCDC 2499723; see table S14 for details).

Density Functional Theory (DFT) calculations

To elucidate the chiral selection mechanism, DFT calculations were performed (see figs. S11 and S12 for details), and the results are summarized in Fig. 7. In these calculations, C2′ undergoes C–N coupling with deprotonated (±)-4d via transition states TS-5d and TS-ent-5d, yielding 5d and ent-5d, respectively. The formation of 5d is thermodynamically more favorable, with an energy barrier difference of 2.3 kcal/mol between the two transition states, consistent with the experimental enantiomeric excess (ee) values. Furthermore, IGMH (independent gradient model based on Hirshfeld partition) and AIM (atoms in molecules) analyses confirm the presence of intramolecular weak interactions in both TS-5d and TS-ent-5d. Notably, TS-5d exhibits substantially stronger OH···O hydrogen bonding interactions, whereas such strong interactions are absent in TS-ent-5d.

Further elaborations

To demonstrate the synthetic utility, large-scale experiments were conducted. Under standard conditions, both the enantioselective synthesis of 3a and the KR of racemic 4a were successfully performed on a 2 mmol scale while maintaining excellent enantioselectivity (Fig. 8A). The resulting tricyclic diazepinones proved to be versatile intermediates, as illustrated by further transformations (Fig. 8B). For example, hydrogenation of 3a provided diastereomeric 6, which was hydrolyzed to furnish acid 7. Treatment of 4a’ with Lawesson’s reagent yielded thioamide 8. Subsequent alkylation with iodoethane led to the S-ethylated product 9 without erosion of enantiopurity, whereas direct alkylation of 4a’ occurred regioselectively at the N-position (10). Notably, N-benzylation product underwent Pd-catalyzed intramolecular C − H arylation to deliver bowl-shaped nitrogen-containing polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon 11 in 82% yield and 93% ee (44).

Moreover, our methodology enabled, to our knowledge, the first catalytic asymmetric access to (+)-telenzepine, a potent and selective M1 antagonist (Fig. 9). Currently, enantiopure telenzepine is prepared via optical resolution, and its absolute configuration remains unconfirmed (45). Our synthetic route began with the Pd-catalyzed coupling of 4-amino-thiophene 12 and 1-iodo-2-nitrobenzene 13. The resulting product 14 was readily hydrogenated to o-phenylenediamine 15, which then underwent intramolecular amidation to give, after two steps, the tricyclic intermediate 17. Subsequent acylation with 2-chloroacetyl chloride afforded racemic 18 (46). Under the standard conditions, the anticipated KR proceeded smoothly, delivering enantioenriched precursor 20 in 93% ee. Finally, nucleophilic substitution with N-methylpiperazine completed the enantioselective synthesis of (+)-telenzepine.

DISCUSSION

In summary, we have developed a general and efficient organocatalytic asymmetric N-H alkylation strategy that enables the direct and highly enantioselective construction of inherently chiral tricyclic diazepinone scaffolds. Central to our approach is the strategic introduction of specific N-substituents to impart sufficient torsional strain, thereby stabilizing the inherent chiral conformation and overcoming the long-standing challenge associated with the conformational flexibility of such molecules. Broad substrate scope, successful kinetic resolution, and application to the synthesis of pharmacologically relevant molecules including, to our knowledge, the first catalytic asymmetric route to (+)-telenzepine, underscore the versatility and synthetic utility of this method. Biological activity assays of the synthesized compounds are underway and will be reported in due course.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Unless otherwise specified, all reactions were conducted under an inert atmosphere and anhydrous conditions. All the solvents were purified according to the standard procedures. All chemicals which are commercially available were employed without further purification. Thin-layer chromatography (TLC) was performed on silica gel plates (60F-254) using UV-light (254 nm). Flash chromatography was conducted on silica gel (200–300 mesh). 1H and 13C NMR spectra were recorded at ambient temperature in CDCl3 or DMSO- d6 on a 400 MHz NMR spectrometer. Chemical shifts were reported in parts per million (ppm). The data are reported as follows: for 1H NMR, chemical shift in ppm from tetramethylsilane with the solvent as internal standard (CDCl3 δ 7.26 ppm), multiplicity (s = singlet, d = doublet, t = triplet, q = quartet, m = multiplet or overlap of non-eqalent resonances), integration; for 13C NMR, chemical shift in ppm from tetramethylsilane with the solvent as internal indicator (CDCl3 δ 77.1 ppm), multiplicity with respect to protons. All high-resolution mass spectra were obtained on a Q-TOF Micro LC/MS System ESI spectrometer to be given in m/z. Enantiomeric excess values were determined with HPLC (chiral column; mobile phase n-hexane/i-PrOH).

General procedure for enantioselective synthesis of 3

1 (0.1 mmol), C1 (10 mol%) and 3 Å (30 mg) were dissolved in CHCl3 (1 ml), then 2 (0.11 mmol, 1.1 equiv) was added. The reaction was stirred at room temperature until 1 was completely consumed (detected by TLC). The solvent was evaporated in vacuo, the residue was treated with EtOAc. The combined organic layer was washed with brine, dried over Na2SO4 and concentrated under reduced pressure. The resulting residue was purified by flash column chromatography on silica gel (petroleum ether/ethyl acetate = 3:1) to afford pure product 3.

General procedure for KR

Rac-4 (0.1 mmol), C2 (10 mol%) and 3 Å (50 mg) were dissolved in CHCl3 (5 ml), then 2 (0.055 mmol. 0.55 equiv) was added. The reaction was stirred at room temperature for 48 hours. The solvent was evaporated in vacuo, the residue was treated with EtOAc. The combined organic layer was washed with brine, dried over Na2SO4 and concentrated under reduced pressure. The resulting residue was purified by flash column chromatography on silica gel (petroleum ether/ethyl acetate = 3:1) to afford pure product 4′ and 5.
