Determinants of systolic murmurs without significant valvular heart disease in an unselected Norwegian adult population: the Tromsø Study
Authors: Stian Andersen, Anne Herefoss Davidsen, Per Niklas Waaler, Peder Andreas Halvorsen, Henrik Schirmer, Hasse Melbye, Juan Carlos Aviles Solis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSystolic murmurs are common in adults without significant valvular heart disease (VHD) and are more prevalent in women than in men, but the determinants of murmur audibility remain unclear. We aimed to identify the physiological determinants of innocent murmurs and examine the basis for sex differences in murmur prevalence.MethodsIn this cross-sectional population-based study, we analysed data from 1680 participants in Tromsø7 without significant VHD who underwent echocardiography and heart sound recording. We used multivariable logistic regression with complete-case data from 1643 participants to identify determinants of innocent murmurs.ResultsAmong 1680 participants, 339 (20.2%) had an innocent murmur. In multivariable analysis, mean aortic valve gradient was the strongest determinant (OR 23.79 per 5 mm Hg, 95% CI 15.01 to 38.71), followed by female sex (OR 3.42, 95% CI 2.38 to 4.99), age (OR 1.41 per 10 years, 95% CI 1.22 to 1.64), systolic blood pressure (OR 1.12 per 10 mm Hg, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.20) and haemoglobin (OR 0.86 per 10 g/L, 95% CI 0.75 to 0.98). Murmur probability increased steeply with increasing gradient and exceeded 50% at 6–8 mm Hg, depending on sex. Women had a higher murmur probability than men across the observed gradient range.ConclusionsIn adults without significant VHD, systolic murmurs were strongly associated with mean aortic valve gradient and occurred at lower gradients in women than in men. These findings support interpreting systolic murmurs as a probabilistic and context-dependent clinical finding rather than a binary marker of VHD.

Introduction

Systolic murmurs are typically heard in aortic stenosis (AS), yet in unselected adult populations most occur in individuals without valvular heart disease (VHD).1 2 In a recent population-based study, nearly one in four adults had an audible murmur, but the overlap between VHD and murmurs was limited. Only about one-third of individuals with VHD had a murmur, and fewer than one-third of those with a murmur had VHD.2 This leaves a large group of adults with murmurs but no significant VHD—commonly termed innocent murmurs. In primary care, detection of a murmur frequently prompts referral for echocardiography.3 4 Yet the determinants of such innocent murmurs are incompletely understood, limiting interpretation of auscultatory findings and rational use of imaging.

Sex differences further complicate murmur interpretation. Systolic murmurs are more prevalent in women than in men, yet women have lower rates of VHD both in the general population and among those with murmurs.2 3 This makes murmurs an even less reliable indicator of VHD in women.

Previous studies have linked transaortic flow velocity to systolic murmurs,5 6 but were largely conducted in selected populations referred for echocardiography or with known VHD,3–6 and with gradients overlapping with mild AS,6 7 limiting insight into murmur generation in adults without significant VHD. Although sex differences in valvular pathology are increasingly recognised,8 9 sex differences in murmur prevalence remain unexplained.

We therefore examined the determinants of systolic murmurs in a general population without significant VHD, with particular focus on mean aortic valve gradient, sex differences and implications for clinical murmur interpretation.

Methods

Study population

The Tromsø Study is an ongoing population-based health study conducted in Tromsø municipality in northern Norway. Initiated in 1974 as a cardiovascular risk study, it has expanded into a multipurpose epidemiological study with repeated population surveys.10 The Seventh Survey of the Tromsø Study (Tromsø7, 2015–2016) invited all residents of Tromsø municipality aged 40 years or older to a health screening, and a randomly selected subsample underwent transthoracic echocardiography and digital heart sound recording. Study design and data collection are described elsewhere.2 11

Among 2131 participants with both echocardiography and heart sound recordings, we excluded 49 participants as their recordings were unclassifiable due to noise, leaving 2082 suitable for analysis. For the main analysis, we excluded 392 participants (18.8%) with significant VHD. We also excluded nine participants with a mean aortic valve gradient ≥15 mm Hg without meeting guideline criteria for AS to avoid misclassification of mild AS, consistent with previous work from the Tromsø Study.12 One remaining participant with a diastolic murmur was also excluded.

The study population for the main analysis comprised 1680 participants without significant VHD. Of these, 1643 (97.8%) had complete data on all model covariates (figure 1). Missing data were consistent with missing completely at random (Little’s MCAR p=0.54).

Heart sound recording and classification

Heart sounds were recorded at four standard auscultation sites (aortic, pulmonic, tricuspid and mitral) using a modified acoustic stethoscope (Littmann Classic II, 3M) with a wireless microphone (MKE 2-EW, Sennheiser) inserted into the tubing. Recordings lasted 10 s and were obtained after at least 3 min of rest.

Two general practitioners independently classified recordings while blinded to echocardiographic findings. Recordings were reviewed as digital audio files with simultaneous visual spectrogram display to aid detection and timing of murmurs. Categories were ‘normal heart sounds’, ‘systolic murmur’, ‘diastolic murmur’ or ‘unclassifiable’. Disagreements were adjudicated by a senior general practitioner and cardiologist. A murmur was classified as present if detected at one or more auscultation sites; the full process has been described elsewhere.2 13 For the present analysis, we included only systolic murmurs.

Echocardiography

Echocardiography was performed on the same day as heart sound recording using a GE Vivid E9 scanner (GE Vingmed Ultrasound). Image acquisition and measurements followed the American Society of Echocardiography and the European Association of Cardiovascular Imaging guidelines.14 15 Images were analysed by a physician blinded to auscultation findings, and murmur classification was performed blinded to echocardiographic results, minimising detection bias.

Definitions of key variables

We defined VHD according to European Society of Cardiology and European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery guidelines.16 Significant VHD was defined as AS of any degree, moderate or severe aortic or mitral regurgitation or moderate or severe mitral stenosis.2 Mild VHD was defined as trace or mild regurgitation or stenosis of any valve, not meeting criteria for significant VHD.

We defined innocent murmur as a systolic murmur in the absence of significant VHD and with a mean aortic valve gradient <15 mm Hg.

Blood pressure was measured three times after rest; we used the mean of the second and third readings.

Anaemia was defined according to local laboratory reference ranges (haemoglobin <115 g/L in women and <130 g/L in men, University Hospital of North Norway).

Self-reported variables

Information on previous or current disease history was obtained from standardised questionnaires administered as part of the Tromsø7 Study11 and recorded using the question format ‘Have you ever had, or do you have…’. Self-reported variables included diabetes, myocardial infarction, heart failure, stroke, hypertension and atrial fibrillation. All variables were treated as binary, with yes indicating current or previous disease and no indicating never.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean with SD, and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Unadjusted group differences were assessed using Welch’s two-sample t-test for continuous variables and Pearson’s χ2 test for categorical variables.

We used multivariable logistic regression to identify determinants of innocent murmur using complete-case data. Candidate predictors were selected based on physiological relevance and prior literature.

To explore potential mechanisms underlying the sex difference, we examined whether adjustment for additional variables attenuated the sex effect, quantified as percentage change in the sex OR. These included haemodynamic measures (cardiac index, left ventricular outflow tract peak velocity), anatomical measures (left ventricular mass index, left ventricular outflow tract diameter, aortic root diameter, aortic valve area), diastolic function (ratio of early mitral inflow velocity (E) to early diastolic mitral annular velocity (e′), E/e’ ratio) and body mass index. Anatomical measures were examined both as absolute values and indexed to body surface area. Interactions between gradient, age and sex were tested.

Results are reported as ORs with 95% CIs. Age and systolic blood pressure were scaled per 10-unit increase, haemoglobin per 10 g/L and mean aortic valve gradient per 5 mm Hg. Linearity of continuous predictors was assessed, multicollinearity was evaluated using variance inflation factors and influential observations were examined using Cook’s distance.

Model discrimination was quantified using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) with 95% CIs estimated by DeLong’s method. Optimism in discrimination was estimated by bootstrap resampling with full model refitting, and an optimism-corrected AUC was reported. Calibration was assessed graphically and with the Hosmer-Lemeshow test.

Sensitivity analyses included alternative anaemia definitions, restriction to different gradient ranges and alternative haemodynamic measures. To test robustness to VHD definitions, we repeated the main multivariable analysis after excluding all participants with mild VHD (N=1145). We also tested whether VHD status modified the sex-murmur association by adding a sex×VHD interaction term to a logistic regression model in the full Tromsø7 sample (N=2029 with complete covariate data), adjusting for mean aortic valve gradient, VHD status, age, systolic blood pressure and haemoglobin.

Finally, we examined gradient distribution among all participants with systolic murmur in the full Tromsø7 sample (N=487), including those with VHD, stratified by sex and age (<70 and ≥70 years).

Analyses were conducted using R V.4.5.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). This study is reported according to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for reports of cross-sectional studies.17

Results

Compared with those with normal heart sounds, participants with murmurs were older, more often female and had higher systolic blood pressure (table 1). There were no significant differences in self-reported cardiovascular comorbidity except hypertension.

Determinants of innocent murmurs

The multivariable model included 1643 complete cases and 331 events. Higher mean aortic valve gradient (OR 23.79 per 5 mm Hg, 95% CI 15.01 to 38.71), female sex (OR 3.42, 95% CI 2.38 to 4.99), older age (OR 1.41 per 10 years, 95% CI 1.22 to 1.64) and higher systolic blood pressure (OR 1.12 per 10 mm Hg, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.20) were associated with odds of innocent murmur, whereas higher haemoglobin (OR 0.86 per 10 g/L, 95% CI 0.75 to 0.98) was associated with lower odds (table 2).

The gradient-murmur relationship

Innocent murmur prevalence increased progressively with mean aortic gradient (figure 3). At gradients below 3 mm Hg, murmurs were uncommon. Prevalence then increased markedly across gradient categories and remained consistently higher in women than in men. However, the highest gradient categories included few participants, particularly from 10 mm Hg and above, and these estimates should therefore be interpreted with caution (online supplemental table S5).

After excluding mild VHD, the gradient effect was unchanged and sensitivity analyses using binary anaemia definitions, restricted gradient ranges and alternative echocardiographic flow parameters yielded consistent results (online supplemental table S1). There was no significant interaction between gradient and sex (p=0.083), age (p=0.898) or haemoglobin (p=0.118).

Sex differences

Mean aortic gradient did not differ significantly between women and men (4.03 vs 3.98 mm Hg, p=0.56), whereas murmur prevalence was 26.7% in women and 11.7% in men (risk ratio 2.30, 95% CI 1.84 to 2.89). At any given gradient, women had a higher predicted probability of murmur than men.

In multivariable analysis, female sex was strongly associated with murmur presence (OR 3.42, 95% CI 2.38 to 4.99). Adjustment for additional echocardiographic and clinical variables changed the sex OR by ≤2.8% (online supplemental table S2).

In the full Tromsø7 sample including participants with significant VHD (N=2029), VHD status modified the sex-murmur association (sex×VHD interaction p=0.018; online supplemental table S3). Murmur prevalence in men was 10.2% without VHD and 25.2% with VHD, compared with 26.2% and 30.2% in women.

Discussion

In this population-based study, systolic murmurs among participants without significant VHD were strongly associated with mean aortic valve gradient and female sex. Murmur probability increased steeply with gradient in a dose-response pattern, with murmurs occurring at lower gradients in women than in men.

The haemodynamic continuum

Systolic murmur audibility was strongly associated with mean aortic valve gradient regardless of how VHD was defined, with a stable association whether participants with VHD were included or excluded. We reported mean aortic valve gradient per 5 mm Hg to make the estimate clinically interpretable. The wider upper end of the CI likely reflects the small number of participants with higher gradients after exclusion of those with significant VHD and gradients ≥15 mm Hg; the exact magnitude of the association at higher gradients is therefore uncertain.

Hirayama et al reported similar findings in a referred population, where transaortic flow velocity was an important determinant of systolic murmurs.6 However, the patients with murmur in their cohort had substantially higher gradients (mean aortic gradient 18.7 mm Hg). We demonstrate that this relationship holds in an unselected general population even at gradient levels below those typically associated with mild AS.

The gradients associated with murmurs in our study are not clinically trivial. Longitudinal data from the Tromsø Study show that approximately one-third of individuals with mean gradients between 10 and 15 mm Hg develop AS within 7 years, whereas progression is uncommon below 10 mm Hg.7 However, murmurs were present across the full measured gradient range and cannot distinguish between gradients associated with low and high progression risk alone.

Sex differences

Murmur prevalence increased with mean aortic gradient in both sexes, but women had higher murmur prevalence than men at comparable gradient levels.

Although women had smaller absolute left ventricular outflow tract dimensions than men, mean aortic gradients were nearly identical. Adjustment for differences in cardiac output, aortic and outflow tract dimensions and body size resulted in minimal attenuation of the sex association.

VHD modified the sex-murmur association, with a stronger association between VHD and murmur presence in men than in women. This is consistent with earlier findings from the Tromsø7 cohort, where male sex was independently associated with underlying VHD among individuals with a murmur.2

What underlies the lower audibility threshold in women remains uncertain. One hypothesis relates to sex differences in valvular tissue. Women with VHD show greater fibrosis and less calcification than men at comparable disease severity,8 9 18 and sex hormones influence valve tissue composition.18 19 Whether similar differences are present in normal heart valves, potentially affecting murmur audibility, remains speculative.

Clinical implications

Our study helps explain why systolic murmurs do not have a uniform clinical meaning. Other studies from both primary care and echocardiography-referred populations have shown that murmurs do not map reliably onto VHD.1 3 4 In one study, women were more frequently referred for murmur evaluation despite a lower proportion of valve abnormalities.3 Our findings suggest that this mismatch may partly reflect transvalvular flow conditions even below conventional AS threshold. This may also help explain the substantially lower positive predictive value of murmurs for VHD in women, who had a higher prevalence of innocent murmurs in our cohort.

Among individuals with a murmur in the Tromsø7 Study, the probability of underlying VHD ranged from 10% in women younger than 70 years without previous myocardial infarction to 67% in men aged 70 years or older with previous myocardial infarction.2 Consistent with this, only 2.5% of women younger than 70 years with a murmur had a mean aortic gradient ≥10 mm Hg, compared with 40.7% of men aged 70 years or older (online supplemental table S4). While mean aortic valve gradient is associated with both murmur audibility and risk of progression to AS, the gradient itself cannot be inferred from cardiac auscultation alone in patients without VHD.

Conversely, the absence of a murmur provides limited reassurance, as nearly two-thirds of participants with significant VHD in Tromsø7 had no audible murmur.2 However, earlier Tromsø studies indicate that echocardiographic screening of asymptomatic adults does not reduce mortality or cardiovascular events compared with symptom-initiated evaluation,20 suggesting that the low sensitivity of auscultation may be of limited clinical consequence in asymptomatic populations.

Strengths and limitations

This study is based on a large, randomly selected population-based sample with paired echocardiography and heart sound recordings in all participants. Murmurs were classified by general practitioners using a standardised three-step consensus procedure described in detail elsewhere,2 13 with all classifiers blinded to echocardiographic findings. The use of general practitioners as auscultators reflects routine clinical practice in primary care.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Heart sounds were recorded and classified digitally with spectrogram assistance, which may detect fainter murmurs than routine bedside auscultation and prevalence estimates may therefore exceed those observed in clinical practice.

While murmurs were graded by intensity (faint vs distinct), other acoustic characteristics such as detailed timing or tonal quality, which may aid discrimination between murmurs of different origins, were not systematically recorded.

Body habitus might influence both acoustic transmission and echocardiographic image quality, but more detailed assessment of chest wall composition was not available. As in many population-based studies, participation may be influenced by health status, potentially limiting external validity if participants were healthier than non-participants and introducing selection bias.

The study population comprised adults aged 40 years and older of predominantly Northern European ancestry. This may limit generalisability to younger adults, other ethnic groups and settings where rheumatic heart disease is common, rather than age-related non-rheumatic VHD.21

Conclusion

Systolic murmurs were strongly associated with mean aortic valve gradient in individuals without significant VHD, and women had higher murmur prevalence than men at comparable gradient levels. Cardiac auscultation should therefore be interpreted as a probabilistic, context-dependent screening tool rather than a binary diagnostic test.
