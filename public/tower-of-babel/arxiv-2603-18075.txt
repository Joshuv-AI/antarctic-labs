Waveforms and Fluxes of Generic Extreme-Mass-Ratio Inspirals with a Spinning Secondary
Qiuxin Cui, Wen-Biao Han

Abstract
Extreme mass-ratio inspirals (EMRIs), comprising a stellar-mass compact object (CO) orbiting a supermassive black hole (BH), are key targets for future space-based gravitational-wave (GW) observatories. Incorporating the spin of the secondary body into waveform models not only enhances measurement precision but also offers insight into the spin distribution of stellar-mass COs. In this work, we construct the flux and waveform for an EMRI with a spinning secondary in a Kerr background under the linear-spin approximation. Using the radiative prescription (half-retarded minus half-advanced field), we derive orbit-averaged evolution equations for the fundamental constants of motion, including the energy, angular momentum, Carter-like constant, and the parallel spin component. This framework provides a tractable route to generating waveforms that incorporate the secondary spin, with the potential for further simplification in future work.



I
Introduction

Since the first direct detection of a gravitational wave (GW) signal from a binary black hole merger in 2015
1
, the LIGO-Virgo-KAGRA (LVK) collaboration has announced more than 100 compact binary merger events
2
. The gravitational waves from these stellar-mass compact binaries at coalescence are in a relatively high frequency band. The future space-borne gravitational wave detectors, such as LISA
3
, Taiji
38
, and Tian-Qin
25
, are most sensitive in the milli-Hertz (mHz) band. One of the primary sources for these space-borne interferometers is extreme mass-ratio inspirals (EMRI)
4
, which consists of a stellar-mass compact object, e.g., a stellar-mass black hole (sBH) with mass
\mathcal{O}(1-100)

M_{\odot}
, and a supermassive black hole (SMBH).

It relies on the matched-filter technology to dig out the EMRI event in the continuous long signal or resolve system parameters, which requires the precise system simulation. Due to the extreme mass ratio
\epsilon
, we can apply the two-timescale analysis to the EMRI system
18
. Generally, the orbit motion
q_{\alpha}
evolves on the orbit timescale
\sim 2\pi/\omega_{\alpha}
as

\frac{dq_{\alpha}}{d\tau}=\omega_{\alpha}^{(0)}(Q_{\lambda})+\epsilon\omega^{(1)}_{\alpha}(Q_{\lambda},q_{i})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})

(1)

where
\omega_{\alpha}
are the orbit frequencies and
Q_{\lambda}
are the constants of motion. While
Q_{\lambda}
evolve on the radiation timescale
\sim 2\pi/\epsilon\omega
as

\frac{dQ_{\lambda}}{d\tau}=\epsilon G^{(1)}_{\lambda}(Q_{\sigma},q_{i})+\epsilon^{2}G^{(2)}_{\lambda}(Q_{\sigma},q_{i})+\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{3})

(2)

Since the detected signal will persist for about one year, e.g.,
\sim 1/\epsilon
cycles for the orbit motion, the leading-order simulation named adiabatic approximation (0PA) should involve
\omega^{(0)}_{\alpha}
and
\epsilon G^{(1)}_{\lambda}
. And the subleading-order effects are
\epsilon\omega^{(1)}_{\alpha}
and
\epsilon^{2}G^{(2)}_{\lambda}
, named the first post-adiabatic approximation (1PA). By Lindblom’s criteria
23
, the 1PA simulation should fulfill the precision requirement of future detection.

Ignoring the perturbation of the background metric, the motion of the test body is described by the Dixon laws
9
;
10
. For compact objects (such as the BH), the zero-order term is the geodesic motion, which contributes to
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{0})
, and the spin of the compact object (
s
, named the secondary spin, as the spin of SMBH,
a
is named the primary spin) contributes to at
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
. Other higher-pole moments should contribute to
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
. When it comes to the gravitational perturbation induced by the secondary body (i.e., the self-force effect), Ref.
17
showed that the motion still follows the same equation as a test body, but in an effective metric instead of the background metric. Thus, considering the extreme mass ratio
\epsilon
, the geodesic motion should contribute to the
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
self-force effect, including the dissipative part and the conservative one. While the secondary spin should contribute to the
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
self-force effect. For the 1PA approximation, we should take into account all the
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon)
self-force effects and the dissipative part of the
\mathcal{O}(\epsilon^{2})
one, so other higher-pole moments can be safely discarded.

In this paper, we concentrate on the effect of the secondary spin instead of the full 1PA effect, i.e., only the dissipative self-force effects are taken into account. Specifically speaking, although
s/M\sim\epsilon\ll 1
, we will handle these two approximations separately and take their linear approximations respectively. Practically speaking, we compute the linear metric perturbation induced by the orbit of the Mathison-Papapetrous-Dixon (MPD) equation
30
;
33
(which is the dipole approximation of the Dixon law) with the linear-spin approximation, and then make use of this to compute the inspiral, i.e., orbit-averaged evolution of the constants of motion.

The secondary spin is an interesting and significant component in the EMRI system, not only because it is necessary for the 1PA simulation but also because it can reveal certain important characteristics of the EMRI system. As Ref.
8
showed, the secondary spin can be used to distinguish dry (loss cone) EMRIs (where the secondary BHs were born in the collapse of individual massive stars and are of low spin) and Hills EMRIs (where the secondary BHs are remnants of massive star binaries and the secondary spins follow a bimodal distribution). Besides, Ref.
36
;
59
suggested that the secondary spin may also be used to distinguish the stellar-mass BH from other kinds of compact objects. This is why we first focus our attention on the secondary spin in the 1PA effect.

For a spinning secondary, some special orbits have been carefully studied, such as the straight falling one in Ref.
31
, the circular-aligned one in Ref.

;
15
;
35
;
24
;
16
, the eccentric equatorial one in Ref.

;
46
, the Schwarzschild one in Ref.

;

, and the quasi-spherical one in Ref.
47
. Although some research
19
;
34
argued that the secondary spin is unmeasurable for LISA with a typical mass ratio
\sim 10^{-5}
, the motion of the secondary is strictly constrained to an equatorial and further circular orbit in their discussion. Recently, Ref.

44
;
37

A few days before the release of this work, an article

\displaystyle\left<h^{\text{rad}}_{\mu\nu}\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu}_{0}\right>=\bigg<\int dw

\displaystyle\sum_{lm}\frac{1}{2iw^{3}}\bigg(N_{\text{out}}\Pi^{\text{out}}_{lmw,\mu\nu}(z^{\mu}(\tilde{\lambda}))\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu}_{0}(z^{\mu}(\tilde{\lambda}))Z^{\infty}_{lmw}

(77)

\displaystyle+\text{the "down" part}\bigg)+(c.c.)\bigg>

We define the ”out” part of the above equation as

\phi^{\text{out}}_{lmw}(\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu}_{0},\tilde{\lambda}):=\tilde{\Sigma}N_{\text{out}}\Pi^{\text{out}}_{lmw,\mu\nu}(z^{\mu}(\tilde{\lambda}))\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu}_{0}(z^{\mu}(\tilde{\lambda}))

(78)

Performing the Fourier transformation to it

\displaystyle\phi^{\text{out}}_{lmw}(\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu}_{0},\tilde{\lambda})=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2\pi}\int d\eta\Phi^{\text{out}}_{lmw}(\eta)e^{i\eta\tilde{\lambda}}

(79)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\text{out}}_{lmw}(\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu}_{0},\eta):=

\displaystyle\int d\tilde{\lambda}\phi^{\text{out}}_{lmw}(\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu}_{0},\tilde{\lambda})e^{-i\eta\tilde{\lambda}}

Using the Dirac function
\delta^{(4)}
,
\Phi^{\text{out}}_{lmw}
can be reorganized into a form similar to Eq.
66

\displaystyle\Phi^{\text{out}}_{lmw}(\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu}_{0},\eta)=\int d^{4}x

\displaystyle\sqrt{-g}N_{\text{out}}\Pi^{\text{out}}_{lmw,\mu\nu}(x^{\mu})

(80)

\displaystyle\int\tilde{\Sigma}d\tilde{\lambda}\frac{\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu}-z^{\mu}(\tilde{\lambda}))}{\sqrt{-g}}\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu}_{0}(z^{\mu}(\tilde{\lambda}))e^{-i\eta\tilde{\lambda}}

if we treat
\int d\tau\delta^{(4)}\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu}_{0}/\sqrt{-g}
as the virtual stress-energy tensor and label the corresponding virtual amplitude (in Eq.
59
) as
Z^{\infty/H,E_{0}}_{lmwknj}
. Thus we have

\Phi^{\text{out}}_{lmw}(\mathfrak{t}_{0}^{\mu\nu},\eta)=\frac{16\overline{N}_{\text{out}}}{C_{\infty}\mathcal{C}}\tilde{\Gamma}\sum_{knj}\overline{Z}^{\infty,E_{0}}_{lmwknj}\delta(w\tilde{\Gamma}-m\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\varphi}-k\tilde{\Upsilon}_{z}-n\tilde{\Upsilon}_{r}-j\tilde{\Upsilon}_{\psi}-\eta)

(81)

where the
-\eta
in the
\delta
function is induced by
e^{-i\eta\tilde{\lambda}}
in Eq.
80
. So

\phi^{\text{out}}_{lmw}(\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu}_{0},\tilde{\lambda})=\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}}{2\pi}\sum_{knj}\overline{Z}^{\infty,E_{0}}_{lmwknj}e^{i\tilde{\Gamma}(w-w_{mknj})\tilde{\lambda}}

(82)

For terms about
\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu\rho}_{1}
or
\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}_{2}
, the situation is quite similar by using the Stokes’ theorem

\displaystyle\Phi^{\text{out}}_{lmw}(\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu\rho}_{1},\eta)

\displaystyle=\int\tilde{\Sigma}N_{\text{out}}d\tilde{\lambda}\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\Pi^{\text{out}}_{lmw,\mu\nu;\rho}(x^{\mu})

(83)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu}-z^{\mu}(\tilde{\lambda}))}{\sqrt{-g}}\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu\rho}_{1}(z^{\mu}(\tilde{\lambda}))e^{-i\eta\tilde{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}N_{\text{out}}\Pi^{\text{out}}_{lmw}(x^{\mu})

\displaystyle\int\tilde{\Sigma}d\tilde{\lambda}\nabla_{\rho}\left(\frac{-\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu}-z^{\mu}(\tilde{\lambda}))}{\sqrt{-g}}\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu\rho}_{1}(z^{\mu}(\tilde{\lambda}))e^{-i\eta\tilde{\lambda}}\right)

\displaystyle\Phi^{\text{out}}_{lmw}(\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}_{2},\eta)

\displaystyle=\int\tilde{\Sigma}N_{\text{out}}d\tilde{\lambda}\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}\Pi^{\text{out}}_{lmw,\mu\nu;\rho\sigma}(x^{\mu})

(84)

\displaystyle\frac{\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu}-z^{\mu}(\tilde{\lambda}))}{\sqrt{-g}}\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}_{2}(z^{\mu}(\tilde{\lambda}))e^{-i\eta\tilde{\lambda}}

\displaystyle=\int d^{4}x\sqrt{-g}N_{\text{out}}\Pi^{\text{out}}_{lmw}(x^{\mu})

\displaystyle\int\tilde{\Sigma}d\tilde{\lambda}\nabla_{\sigma}\nabla_{\rho}\left(\frac{\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu}-z^{\mu}(\tilde{\lambda}))}{\sqrt{-g}}\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}_{2}(z^{\mu}(\tilde{\lambda}))e^{-i\eta\tilde{\lambda}}\right)

Therefore, for the full terms
\left<h^{\text{rad}}_{\mu\nu}\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu}_{0}+h^{\text{rad}}_{\mu\nu;\rho}\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu\rho}_{1}+h^{\text{rad}}_{\mu\nu;\rho\sigma}\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}_{2}\right>
, the virtual stress-energy tensor is

\displaystyle T^{\mu\nu}_{\text{E}}(x^{\mu})=

\displaystyle\int d\tau\Bigg[\frac{\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu}-z^{\mu}(\tau))}{\sqrt{-g}}\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu}_{0}(z^{\mu}(\tau))

(85)

\displaystyle-\nabla_{\rho}\left(\frac{\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu}-z^{\mu}(\tau))}{\sqrt{-g}}\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu\rho}_{1}(z^{\mu}(\tau))\right)

\displaystyle+\nabla_{\rho}\nabla_{\sigma}\left(\frac{\delta^{(4)}(x^{\mu}-z^{\mu}(\tau))}{\sqrt{-g}}\mathfrak{t}^{\mu\nu\rho\sigma}_{2}(z^{\mu}(\tau))\right)\Bigg]

which is exactly the form we have introduced in Eq.
52
, and the corresponding virtual amplitude is labeled as
Z^{\infty/\text{H}}_{\text{E},lmw}
. Thus, we have

\displaystyle\left<\frac{dE}{d\tilde{\lambda}}\right>=

\displaystyle\lim_{T\rightarrow\infty}\frac{1}{2T}\int_{-T}^{T}d\tilde{\lambda}\int dw\sum_{lmknj}\frac{1}{2iw^{3}}

(86)

\displaystyle\left(\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}}{2\pi}\overline{Z}^{\infty}_{\text{E},lmwknj}Z^{\infty}_{lmw}e^{i\tilde{\Gamma}(w-w_{mknj})\tilde{\lambda}}+\text{the "down" part}+\right)

\displaystyle+(c.c)

Again, since
Z^{\infty}_{lmw}
is composed of discrete spectra with
Z^{\infty}_{lmw}=\sum_{k^{\prime}n^{\prime}j^{\prime}}Z^{\infty}_{lmwk^{\prime}n^{\prime}j^{\prime}}\delta(w-w_{mk^{\prime}n^{\prime}j^{\prime}})
, the integration in Eq.
86
will be finally reduced to

\sum_{lmknj}\frac{\tilde{\Gamma}}{2\pi w_{mknj}^{3}}\text{Im}\left[\overline{Z}^{\infty}_{\text{E},lmwknj}Z^{\infty}_{lmwknj}+\alpha_{lmw_{mknj}}\overline{Z}^{\text{H}}_{\text{E},lmwknj}Z^{\text{H}}_{lmwknj}\right]

(87)

IV.1.2
The specific method

For the second method, let’s go back to Eq.
74
. In the Boyer-Lindquist coordinate, the Killing vector
\xi^{(t)\mu}
is
\{1,0,0,0\}
. Therefore, expanding the covariant derivative by
\Gamma^{\mu}_{\alpha\beta}
, we will have

\left<\frac{dE}{d\tilde{\lambda}}\right>=-\frac{1}{2}\left<v^{\rho}v^{\sigma}\partial_{t}h^{\text{E}}_{\rho\sigma}-v^{(\alpha}s^{\beta)\gamma}\partial_{t}h^{\text{E}}_{\alpha\beta;\gamma}\right>

(88)

As for the radiative field
h^{\text{rad}}_{\mu\nu}
, it is totally composed of modes
e^{im\varphi-iwt}
as can be seen from Eq.
63
. So we have
\partial_{t}h^{\text{rad}}_{\rho\sigma}=-iwh^{\text{rad}}_{\rho\sigma}
,
\partial_{t}h^{\text{rad}}_{\alpha\beta;\gamma}=-iwh^{\text{rad}}_{\alpha\beta;\gamma}
and

\left<\frac{dE}{d\tilde{\lambda}}\right>=\frac{1}{2}\left<iw\left(v^{\alpha}v^{\beta}h^{\text{rad}}_{\alpha\beta}+s^{\gamma(\beta}v^{\alpha)}h^{\text{rad}}_{\alpha\beta;\gamma}\right)\right>

(89)

Comparing the above equation with Eq.
85
, in this case, the virtual stress-energy tensor
T^{\mu\nu}_{\text{E}}
returns to the original one in Eq.
51
, and so does the amplitude
Z^{\infty/\text{H}}_{\text{E},lmwknj}
. Therefore, we have

\left<\frac{dE}{d\tilde{\lambda}}\right>=\sum_{lmknj}\frac{-\tilde{\Gamma}}{4\pi w^{2}_{mknj}}\left(|Z^{\infty}_{lmwknj}|^{2}+\alpha_{lmw_{mknj}}|Z^{\text{H}}_{lmwknj}|^{2}\right)

(90)

which provides the flux-balance law by relating the orbit evolution to the asymptotic amplitude mode
52
.

IV.2
The angular momentum
J_{z}

For the angular momentum
J_{z}
, the procedure follows the same manner as we replace
\xi^{(t)}_{\mu}
with
\xi^{(\varphi)}_{\mu}
. Therefore, the result is

\left<\frac{dJ_{z}}{d\tilde{\lambda}}\right>=\sum_{lmknj}\frac{-\tilde{\Gamma}m}{4\pi w^{3}_{mknj}}\left(\lvert Z^{\infty}_{lmwknj}\lvert^{2}+\alpha_{lmw_{mknj}}\lvert Z^{\text{H}}_{lmwknj}\right)

(91)

IV.3
The parallel spin
s_{||}

Using the relation between the Yano-Killing tensor
Y_{\mu\nu}
and the conformal Yano-Killing tensor
f_{\mu\nu}

20

f_{\mu\nu}=\frac{1}{2}Y^{\alpha\beta}\epsilon_{\alpha\beta\mu\nu}

(92)

the parallel spin
s_{||}
can also be rewritten as

s_{||}=-\frac{1}{2}\frac{f_{\mu\nu}s^{\mu\nu}}{\sqrt{K_{\mu\nu}v^{\mu}v^{\nu}}}

(93)

Therefore, its orbit-averaged evolution can be expressed as

\left<s_{||}\right>=-\frac{1}{2\sqrt{K_{\mu\nu}v^{\mu}v^{\nu}}}\left<f_{\mu\nu;\alpha}s^{\mu\nu}v^{\alpha}+f_{\mu\nu}n^{\mu\nu}-\frac{f_{\mu\nu}s^{\mu\nu}}{K_{\mu\nu}v^{\mu}v^{\nu}}K_{\mu\nu}v^{\mu}f^{\nu}\right>

(94)

The first part will finally be reduced to

\frac{1}{\sqrt{K_{\mu\nu}v^{\mu}v^{\nu}}}\left<Y^{\gamma\beta}s_{\gamma}u^{\alpha}u^{\delta}h^{\text{rad}}_{\alpha\beta;\delta}\right>

(95)

the second part will be

\frac{1}{2\sqrt{K_{\mu\nu}v^{\mu}v^{\nu}}}\left<v^{\rho}v^{\lambda}Y^{\sigma\beta}s_{\beta}h^{\text{rad}}_{\lambda\rho;\sigma}+v^{\rho}Y^{\beta\sigma}v_{\beta}s^{\lambda}\left(2h^{\text{rad}}_{\lambda(\rho;\sigma)}-h^{\text{rad}}_{\rho\sigma;\lambda}\right)\right>

(96)

and the third part will be

\frac{f_{\mu\nu}s^{\mu\nu}}{4\left(K_{\mu\nu}v^{\mu}v^{\nu}\right)^{3/2}}\left<K_{\mu\nu}v^{\nu}g^{\mu\lambda}h^{\text{rad}}_{\rho\sigma;\lambda}v^{\rho}v^{\sigma}+2h^{\text{rad}}_{\lambda\rho}v^{\rho}g^{\mu\lambda}v^{\nu}v^{\sigma}K_{\mu\nu;\sigma}\right>

(97)

Agian, we can apply the generic method to resolve the evolution numerically. Let’s turn to a more specific case: if the spin vector is fully parallel. Under the assumption of
s^{\mu}=s_{||}e^{\mu}_{(3)}
, the above three equations will be

\displaystyle-\frac{s_{||}}{K_{\mu\nu}v^{\mu}v^{\nu}}

\displaystyle\left<h_{\alpha\beta;\rho}^{\text{rad}}v^{\rho}v^{\alpha}g^{\beta\gamma}K_{\gamma\mu}v^{\mu}\right>

(98)

\displaystyle\frac{s_{||}}{2K_{\mu\nu}v^{\mu}v^{\nu}}

\displaystyle\left<h^{\text{rad}}_{\lambda\rho;\sigma}K^{\sigma\beta}v_{\beta}v^{\rho}v^{\lambda}\right>

\displaystyle-\frac{s_{||}}{2K_{\mu\nu}v^{\mu}v^{\nu}}

\displaystyle\left<h^{\text{rad}}_{\rho\sigma;\lambda}K_{\mu\nu}v^{\nu}g^{\mu\lambda}v^{\rho}v^{\sigma}-2h^{\text{rad}}_{\lambda\rho;\sigma}v^{\rho}g^{\mu\lambda}v^{\nu}v^{\sigma}K_{\mu\nu}\right>

Obviously, the three parts will cancel each other out to zero. This cancellation explicitly demonstrates that, within our linear-spin approximation, the projection of the spin onto the orbital angular momentum,
s_{||}
, remains constant under radiation reaction for an equatorial orbit (where the spin vector must be parallel), consistent with the expectation that the orbital plane is preserved.

IV.4
The Carter-like constant
K

We rewrite the expression of
K
as

K=K_{\mu\nu}v^{\mu}v^{\nu}-2v^{\mu}s^{\rho\sigma}L_{\mu\rho\sigma}

(99)

K
is conserved to
\mathcal{O}(s)
as
dK/d\tau=o(s)
, which requires

2v^{\mu}v^{\nu}s^{\rho\sigma}\left(-\frac{1}{2}R^{\delta}_{\ \ \nu\rho\sigma}K_{\mu\delta}-L_{\mu\rho\sigma;\nu}\right)=o(s)

(100)

From the integrability condition of the Yano-Killing tensor, it can be proved that
L_{\mu\rho\sigma}=Y_{\mu\rho;\kappa}Y^{\kappa}_{\ \ \sigma}+Y_{\rho\sigma;\kappa}Y^{\kappa}_{\ \ \mu}
satisfies Eq.
100
.
Under radiation reaction, the evolution of
K
will be

\displaystyle\left<\frac{dK}{d\tilde{\lambda}}\right>=

\displaystyle\bigg<K_{\mu\nu;\alpha}v^{\mu}v^{\nu}v^{\alpha}+2K_{\mu\nu}v^{\mu}f^{\nu}

(101)

\displaystyle-2v^{\mu}v^{\nu}s^{\rho\sigma}L_{\mu\rho\sigma;\nu}-2L_{\mu\rho\sigma}\left(f^{\mu}s^{\rho\sigma}+v^{\mu}n^{\rho\sigma}\right)\bigg>

The first three items will be reduced to

\displaystyle\Bigg<

\displaystyle K_{\mu\nu}v^{\nu}g^{\mu\lambda}v^{\rho}v^{\sigma}h^{\text{rad}}_{\rho\sigma;\lambda}+2g^{\mu\lambda}v^{\rho}\frac{DK_{\mu\nu}v^{\nu}}{d\tau}h_{\lambda\rho}^{\text{rad}}+v^{\lambda}v^{\rho}v^{\mu}\frac{DK_{\mu\nu}v^{\nu}}{d\tau}h_{\lambda\rho}^{\text{rad}}

(102)

\displaystyle+K_{\mu\nu}v^{\nu}v^{\alpha}g^{\mu\lambda}s^{\beta\lambda}\left(-R^{\rho}_{\ \ \gamma\lambda\alpha}h_{\rho\beta}^{\text{rad}}-h_{\alpha\beta;\gamma\lambda}^{\text{rad}}+h^{\text{rad}}_{\lambda\beta;\gamma\alpha}\right)\Bigg>

The last item will be

\displaystyle\Bigg<

\displaystyle-L_{\mu\alpha\beta;\nu}s^{\alpha\beta}v^{\mu}v^{\nu}v^{\rho}v^{\sigma}h^{\text{rad}}_{\rho\sigma}-2L_{\mu\alpha\beta;\sigma}s^{\alpha\beta}g^{\mu\lambda}v^{\rho}v^{\sigma}h_{\lambda\rho}^{\text{rad}}

(103)

\displaystyle-L_{\mu\alpha\beta}s^{\alpha\beta}g^{\mu\lambda}v^{\rho}v^{\sigma}h_{\rho\sigma;\lambda}^{\text{rad}}

\displaystyle-2L_{\alpha[\mu\nu]}v^{\alpha}v^{\rho}s^{\sigma\mu}g^{\nu\lambda}\left(2h^{\text{rad}}_{\lambda(\rho;\sigma)}-h^{\text{rad}}_{\rho\sigma;\lambda}\right)\Bigg>

Using Eq.
100
,
v^{\lambda}v^{\rho}v^{\mu}\frac{DK_{\mu\nu}v^{\nu}}{d\tau}h_{\lambda\rho}^{\text{rad}}
in Eq.
102
and
-L_{\mu\alpha\beta;\nu}s^{\alpha\beta}v^{\mu}v^{\nu}v^{\rho}v^{\sigma}h^{\text{rad}}_{\rho\sigma}
in Eq.
103
will be canceled. However, we didn’t find an appropriate method to further simplify the equation, under the assumption of
L_{\mu\rho\sigma}=Y_{\mu\rho;\kappa}Y^{\kappa}_{\ \ \sigma}+Y_{\rho\sigma;\kappa}Y^{\kappa}_{\ \ \mu}
. Also, we find it difficult to extend the method in Ref.
40
, which associates the evolution of the Carter constant with the asymptotic wave, to the MPD orbit presented here. Therefore, we choose to stop here and substitute this somewhat rough formula into the general method for calculation. The corresponding virtual amplitude is labeled as
Z^{\infty/\text{H}}_{K,lmwknj}
.

V.1
Code Validation and Consistency Checks

Before presenting our main results, we perform several checks to validate the numerical implementation of our Teukolsky-based perturbation calculation. The orbital motion of the spinning secondary is handled analytically using the closed-form expressions derived in Ref.
49
, which have been independently verified. Therefore, our validation focuses exclusively on the perturbation sector—specifically, on the computation of the source term and the resulting asymptotic amplitudes. Three complementary tests are carried out: (i) a comparison with the published energy, angular momentum and Carter constant flux data for generic (eccentric and inclined) orbits in the nonspinning limit in Ref.
11
; (ii) a comparison with published energy flux data (a mode where
l=2
and
m=2
) for circular, equatorial orbits with full-spin effects in Ref.
35
; and (iii) an internal consistency check for generic orbits, where two independent methods implemented in our code are compared.

V.1.2

The circular (equatorial and spin-aligned) case of MPD orbits has been detailedly studied as in Ref.

. We wish to compare our results with theirs, but the orbit in Ref.
35

). So the mode summation of

disappears, and we only consider the mode where

V.1.3
consistency check

From Sec.
IV.1
, there are two methods to resolve the orbit-averaged evolution. The general one can be applied to almost any case, while the specific one relies on further simplification with the properties of the constant of motion. For the energy
E
, both methods should be applicable and equivalent, although they are structured differently at the code level. Therefore, we compare these two different methods for generic MPD orbits. The resulting flux shows
complete consistency
, at least up to 11 significant digits. This consistency provides us with certain confidence for the calculation of the Carter-like constant flux, for which we have not found a specific method for further simplification yet.

VI
Summary

In this work, we calculated the gravitational perturbation induced by the linearized MPD orbit, and then used the radiative field to derive the evolution of the MPD orbit—namely, the evolution equations for its constants of motion. The core method employed for this derivation is presented in Sec.
IV.1.1
.

There are several aspects of this work that can be further optimized. For instance, extracting the spin corrections from the amplitude calculations separately would greatly facilitate the future integration of spin corrections with other 1PA effects. Furthermore, in the evolution equation for the Carter-like constant, if applying the flux-balance law
14
, it should be significantly simplified, analogous to the expression for the energy. In addition, all calculations in this work are currently performed on CPUs. Migrating the code to GPUs would greatly enhance computational efficiency, as done in codes like
FastEMRIWaveforms
.
