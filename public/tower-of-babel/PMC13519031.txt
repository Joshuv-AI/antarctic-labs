Contrastive unlearning via representation editing for graph neural networks
Authors: Zhifan Huang, Hanyu Lu, Yu Yang, Yongyi Yuan, Hongfa Ding
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graph unlearning (GU) aims to eliminate the influence of specific nodes, edges, or features from a trained graph neural network (GNN), and is of great importance for privacy protection and data quality management. However, existing GU methods often struggle to achieve a satisfactory balance between model utility and unlearning effectiveness, and they also find it difficult to remove the influence of target data and its propagated effects over the graph. To address these issues, we propose a GU framework named CURE based on contrastive representation editing. CURE introduces an adaptive sample selection module to accurately identify key nodes that are highly related to the unlearning targets from both structural and semantic perspectives. It then adopts a contrastive unlearning strategy to decouple the representations of the forgotten nodes from those of their related nodes in the embedding space, thereby effectively removing the information of the forgotten nodes. In addition, to preserve the predictive capacity of the model on the remaining data, we further introduce a personalized PageRank-based stability preservation module, which constrains the consistency of prediction distributions for affected nodes before and after unlearning. The experimental results show that CURE achieves a favorable trade-off among model utility, unlearning efficiency, and unlearning effectiveness, and outperforms existing baseline methods in most experimental settings, while also exhibiting superior privacy protection and robustness.

Introduction

With the widespread adoption of graph-structured data in domains such as social networks1,2, financial risk control3, bioinformatics4, and recommender systems5,6, graph neural networks7–9 have achieved remarkable success in tasks such as node classification, link prediction, and graph classification, owing to their powerful ability to model relational dependencies. However, in real-world applications, training data are not immutable. Some graph data may need to be removed from a trained model due to privacy requirements, annotation errors10, or poisoned data11. In particular, as the “right to be forgotten”12 is increasingly incorporated into data governance and privacy protection frameworks, users are entitled to request the deletion of their personal data and its associated influence. For example, both the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR)13 and the California Consumer Privacy Act (CCPA)14 explicitly impose requirements on personal data deletion and privacy protection. Against this backdrop, how to efficiently eliminate specific graph data and their influence from trained models has become an important and urgent problem in graph unlearning (GU).

Machine unlearning15 refers to the requirement that, when a request to forget certain data is received, not only should those data be removed from the training set, but their influence on the trained model must also be erased16. Compared with image or text data in Euclidean space, graph data involve complex topological dependencies among nodes. As a result, deleting a node or an edge affects not only its own information, but also propagates to neighboring nodes and even higher-order neighborhoods through the message-passing mechanism. Therefore, GU must forget not only the target data themselves, but also further mitigate the influence of the forgotten data on other nodes17, making this task significantly more challenging than conventional machine unlearning.

Existing GU methods can generally be divided into exact unlearning and approximate unlearning. Exact unlearning guarantees that the deleted data are completely forgotten, whereas approximate unlearning is usually more efficient. Retraining a model from scratch on the remaining data can achieve the best performance, but it is computationally prohibitive and impractical for large-scale datasets. Exact GU methods such as GraphEraser18 and GUIDE19 improve efficiency by partitioning the graph into shards, locally retraining the affected subgraphs, and then aggregating the results. However, such partitioning damages the topological structure of the graph, resulting in suboptimal model utility. More recently, various approximate GU methods have been proposed to achieve higher efficiency and better model utility. For example, CGU20, Projector21, and GIF22 exhibit strong model dependence. These methods typically rely on specific model architectures and corresponding optimization assumptions (e.g., the theoretical analysis of GIF applies only to convex models), making them difficult to generalize to more general nonlinear GNNs. GNNDelete23 requires forgotten nodes to be continuously recorded and additional layers to be activated during inference, leading to high deployment costs and poor scalability. MEGU24 mainly performs unlearning by imposing penalties on the prediction results of target samples in the loss function. AGU25 performs unlearning by maximizing the divergence between the prediction distributions of the unlearned model and the teacher model. Therefore, both MEGU and AGU mainly adjust the model’s predictive behavior, which may still introduce potential privacy leakage risks.

Based on the above analysis, although existing GU methods alleviate the high cost of retraining from scratch to some extent, they still struggle to simultaneously balance unlearning effectiveness, model utility, and practical applicability. On the one hand, some approximate GU methods depend on specific model structures or lack generality, making them difficult to adapt directly to a broader range of GNNs and thus resulting in inflexibility and suboptimal performance26. On the other hand, due to the ubiquitous topological dependencies in graph data, the information of unlearning targets can spread to neighboring nodes through message passing. Merely imposing penalties at the prediction level is often insufficient to remove such influence and may even lead to further privacy leakage27.

To address these issues, we propose CURE (Contrastive Unlearning via Representation Editing), a flexible GU framework that performs unlearning at the representation level and can be adapted to multiple GNN backbones without relying on a specific model architecture. Unlike existing GU methods that rely on specific architectures or optimization forms, CURE models the unlearning process at the representation level, thereby improving both effectiveness and generality. Due to the influence of graph topology, information to be forgotten often propagates through the structure and affects related nodes. To tackle this challenge, we design an adaptive sample selection module and a contrastive unlearning strategy, which identify key samples that are highly correlated with the unlearning targets and weaken their informational associations with the targets in the representation space, thus enabling more effective unlearning. Meanwhile, the model should preserve as much discriminative ability as possible on the remaining data during unlearning. To this end, we further design a simple yet effective stability-preserving module based on personalized PageRank, which maintains the overall model performance by constraining the consistency of prediction distributions for affected nodes. Related work

Graph unlearning

GU aims to efficiently remove the influence of specific nodes, edges, or node features from a trained GNN without retraining the entire model, and constitutes an important extension of machine unlearning to graph-structured data. Existing methods can generally be categorized into exact GU and approximate GU.

The exact methods guarantee complete forgetting of deleted data17. Representative works such as GraphEraser18, inspired by the SISA paradigm16, partition the training graph into multiple shards and retrain only the affected shards to improve unlearning efficiency. Building upon this idea, GUIDE19 further introduces fairness-aware graph partitioning and similarity-based aggregation strategies to alleviate performance degradation caused by partitioning. Nevertheless, these partition-based methods inevitably disrupt the original graph topology, thereby impairing the model’s performance. To achieve a better trade-off between unlearning efficiency and model utility, approximate GU methods have recently attracted increasing attention. For instance, Projector21, GST28, and ScaleGUN29 attempt to achieve theoretically guaranteed unlearning through closed-form solutions, but they typically rely on linear GNN assumptions. GIF22 estimates the influence of deleted targets on neighborhood structures and model parameters via graph-based influence functions. GNNDelete23 eliminates deleted information while preserving the original model knowledge through hierarchical unlearning operators. IDEA30 further extends influence estimation to a wider range of unlearning tasks, while MEGU24 improves unlearning performance by jointly optimizing prediction objectives and unlearning objectives and by identifying highly influential neighbors. Recently, Ding et al. proposed AGU25, an adaptive GU framework that mainly considers the task differences among node-level, edge-level, and feature-level unlearning, as well as the differences in affected-neighbor ranges caused by different GNN architectures.

Although existing GU methods have made encouraging progress, they still struggle to achieve a satisfactory balance among unlearning effectiveness, model performance, and methodological generality. In particular, for complex GNNs and tightly coupled graph structures, how to efficiently and thoroughly remove the deleted targets and their influence remains a key open problem.

Graph contrastive learning

Graph contrastive learning is an important approach in graph self-supervised representation learning. It usually does not rely on a large number of manual labels, but learns discriminative node or graph representations by pulling positive sample pairs closer and pushing negative sample pairs apart. GMI31 extends traditional mutual information from vector space to graph-structured data, while jointly considering node features and topological information, thereby achieving unsupervised graph representation learning by maximizing graph mutual information. In recent years, related studies have further improved graph contrastive learning from the perspectives of sample construction, data augmentation, and robustness. MSCL32 points out that traditional contrastive loss does not sufficiently distinguish the importance of positive and negative samples, and therefore proposes a multi-sample contrastive loss to make fuller use of multiple positive samples. NCLA33 argues that directly treating neighboring nodes as negative samples conflicts with the homophily assumption of graphs. Therefore, it incorporates both intra-view neighbors and cross-view neighbors into the positive sample set through learnable graph augmentation and neighbor contrastive loss. CGSRL34 further focuses on category-aware positive sample selection. It selects more reliable positive samples by integrating feature similarity and structural similarity, and preserves category-related information during feature augmentation. GRAIL35 addresses the problem of graph contrastive learning under class-imbalanced scenarios by proposing balanced negative sampling and high-quality positive sample selection strategies, so as to alleviate the dominance of majority classes in contrastive optimization. In addition, PAGCL36 studies graph contrastive learning from the perspective of robustness, showing that unsupervised poisoning attacks can inject malicious nodes and optimize perturbations using gradients from the contrastive loss, thereby degrading the graph representations learned by the model.

Although the above methods effectively improve the performance of graph representation learning, most of them focus on how to learn better representations rather than how to remove the influence of specific data from a trained model. In GU tasks, the representation relationships among forgotten nodes, neighboring nodes, and retained nodes need to be reshaped. Therefore, although contrastive learning provides useful insights for representation-level GU, it still needs to be specifically redesigned according to the objectives of unlearning.

Methodology

Figure 2Overview of our proposed CURE.

Overview of our proposed CURE.

Framework overview

As illustrated in Fig. 2, this section presents CURE, a unified representation-space-driven GU framework. Specifically, the framework first employs an adaptive sample selection module to identify key related nodes from the neighborhood of the forgotten nodes. Then, it applies a contrastive unlearning mechanism to adjust the relationships between the forgotten nodes and their related nodes in the representation space, thereby reducing the influence of the forgotten nodes on the model. Finally, a personalized PageRank-based stability preservation module is introduced to constrain the predictive distributions of the affected nodes, ensuring the stability of the overall performance of the model.

Adaptive sample selection

Existing studies22,24 have shown that the neighboring nodes of the forgotten nodes can significantly affect the performance and effectiveness of unlearning. Current GU methods37 construct sample pairs only based on class labels within the K-hop neighborhood. However, this strategy ignores the differences in propagation strength and predictive semantic consistency among nodes in the graph structure, making it difficult to accurately identify key nodes that are highly coupled with the unlearning targets. We argue that relying solely on label information for sample selection is insufficient, and that the correlations at both the representation level and the predictive behavior level should be considered. To this end, we propose an adaptive sample selection module, which jointly measures the relevance between candidate nodes and forgotten nodes from the perspectives of representation similarity and predictive distribution similarity.

Following the conventional definition of contrastive learning, this paper defines neighboring nodes that are highly related to the forgotten nodes in terms of both feature representations and predictive semantics as positive samples. For each forgotten node and its neighboring nodes, we calculate the cosine similarity between their propagated representation vectors and prediction distributions, respectively. The two types of similarity scores are then normalized and fused through a weighted scheme, and the nodes with the highest scores are adaptively selected as positive samples. These positive samples usually have strong informational coupling with the forgotten nodes. Therefore, in the subsequent contrastive unlearning process, these positive samples are not used to pull the representations closer. Instead, they serve as reference nodes that need to be decoupled from the forgotten nodes. Meanwhile, this paper randomly selects nodes with labels different from those of the forgotten nodes from the remaining training nodes as negative samples. Since these nodes are semantically different from the forgotten nodes in terms of class labels, their representations are usually distributed outside the original class cluster. Thus, they can provide cross-class references for repositioning the representations of the forgotten nodes. During contrastive unlearning, the introduction of heterogeneous negative samples can guide the forgotten nodes to gradually move away from their original semantic neighborhood and shift toward the class boundary region, thereby reducing their coupling with the original class cluster and related neighboring nodes. This design enables the unlearning process to focus not only on changes in prediction results, but also on the local structural relationships in the representation space, thus providing an effective optimization direction for subsequent contrastive unlearning.

Contrastive unlearning

Conventional contrastive learning aims to learn representations by pulling positive samples closer and pushing negative samples farther apart33,35. In contrast, in GU, we reversely exploit this mechanism. After identifying the key contrastive nodes, we further perform contrastive unlearning in the embedding space. Unlike approaches that simply reduce the prediction confidence of the target nodes, our goal is to reposition the forgotten nodes in the representation space, such that they move away from both the original decision boundaries and the original structural neighborhoods. This design is motivated by two key observations. First, in supervised GNNs, embeddings of nodes from the same class are typically clustered in nearby regions, while embeddings of nodes from different classes remain well separated in the representation space39. Second, due to the message-passing mechanism of graph neural networks, node embeddings usually exhibit high similarity to those of their structural neighbors. Therefore, to effectively forget the target nodes, merely penalizing their prediction outputs is often insufficient. To address this issue, we further conduct contrastive unlearning in the representation space. Existing theoretical studies have shown that the sigmoid loss can reshape the embedding space by pulling similar samples closer and pushing dissimilar samples apart, and that its optimal representations possess analytically tractable structures40. Therefore, we adopt a log-sigmoid-based contrastive loss in the representation space and perform targeted editing of the forgotten nodes through a nonlinear sigmoid mapping.

Specifically, for each forgotten node u ∈ F, this paper constructs the contrastive unlearning objective using the positive sample set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_u$$\end{document}, obtained through adaptive sample selection, and the heterogeneous negative sample set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_u$$\end{document}. Since the nodes in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_u$$\end{document} are highly coupled with the forgotten node in terms of structural representations and predictive semantics, they are regarded as reference nodes that need to be decoupled from the forgotten node. Therefore, this paper introduces a negative log-sigmoid loss with a negative inner product between the forgotten node and the nodes in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_u$$\end{document}, encouraging their representations to gradually separate in the embedding space. Meanwhile, the nodes in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_u$$\end{document} are heterogeneous nodes whose labels are different from that of the forgotten node. These nodes provide cross-class references for repositioning the representation of the forgotten node. By introducing a negative log-sigmoid loss with a positive inner product between the forgotten node and the nodes in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$N_u$$\end{document}, the representation of the forgotten node is guide the forgotten nodes away from their original class cluster and reduce their coupling with the original semantic neighborhood. The contrastive unlearning loss is defined as9\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} {L}_c = -\frac{1}{|F|}\sum _{u\in F} \left[ \frac{1}{|P_u|}\sum _{p\in P_u} \log \sigma \left( -{h_u^*}^{\top }h_p^*\right) + \frac{1}{|N_u|}\sum _{n\in N_u} \log \sigma \left( {h_u^*}^{\top }h_n^*\right) \right] . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_u^*$$\end{document}, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_p^*$$\end{document}, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$h_n^*$$\end{document} are the embeddings of node u, positive node p, and negative node n after unlearning. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma (\cdot )$$\end{document} denotes the sigmoid function. Under the assumptions of differentiability, convexity, and bounded gradients, we can ensure that this loss function separates the positive and negative dot products of node41 better.

PPR-guided stability preservation

Although contrastive unlearning can effectively alter the representations of forgotten nodes, due to the message-passing mechanism of GNNs, changes in node representations may further affect their neighboring nodes, thereby degrading the model performance on the remaining nodes. To mitigate this issue, we introduce a preservation mechanism based on Personalized PageRank (PPR)42, which is used to identify key nodes that are highly influenced by the forgotten nodes. We then use the original outputs of a teacher model as retained knowledge to constrain these nodes, ensuring that their prediction distributions remain consistent before and after unlearning.

Specifically, for each forgotten node u, we constrain the predictive distributions of the affected nodes to remain consistent before and after unlearning. Let P denote the transition matrix of the graph, and let \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha \in (0,1)$$\end{document} denote the random restart probability. Based on the random walk process with restart, the corresponding Personalized PageRank stationary distribution can be written as10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} p = \alpha \left( I - (1-\alpha ) P^{\top } \right) ^{-1} p^{(0)}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}It should be noted that the closed-form solution in Eq. (10) is mainly used to theoretically define the structural influence intensity of the forgotten nodes on other nodes. In practical computation, matrix inversion is not performed directly, because the explicit computation of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\left( I-(1-\alpha )P^{T}\right) ^{-1}$$\end{document} would incur high time and space costs on large-scale graphs, making it difficult to meet the scalability requirements of real-world applications. Therefore, this paper adopts a truncated random walk with restart strategy to approximate the PPR scores. Specifically, this paper extracts a local L-hop subgraph centered on the forgotten node and performs only a limited number of sparse propagation steps within this local subgraph, thereby estimating the structural influence of the forgotten node on its surrounding nodes. The iterative process can be expressed as:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} p^{(t+1)} = (1-\alpha )P_L^{T}p^{(t)} + \alpha p^{(0)} \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$P_L$$\end{document} denotes the transition matrix on the local L-hop subgraph, and α denotes the random restart probability. After a finite number of iterations, the obtained PPR score is used as a measure of the extent to which a node is influenced by the forgetting target. A higher score indicates a stronger structural association between the node and the forgotten node, suggesting that the node is more likely to be affected during the unlearning process. Based on the above influence scores, we select a subset of non-forgotten training nodes with the highest scores to form the set S. These nodes are structurally more affected by the forgotten nodes, yet they do not belong to the unlearning targets, and therefore their predictive behavior should be preserved. During optimization, we freeze the original model as the teacher model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{\theta }$$\end{document} and treat the unlearned model as the student model \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f_{\theta ^{*}}$$\end{document}. We then enforce consistency between their predictive distributions on the set S, which is achieved by minimizing the KL divergence between the predictive distributions of the teacher and student models12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L_k = \sum _{i \in S} \textrm{KL}\!\left( f_{\theta }(x_i) \,\Vert \, f_{\theta ^{*}}(x_i) \right) . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}Based on Eqs. (9) and (12), we design the overall optimization objective of CURE as13\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L = (1-\gamma )L_c + \gamma L_k, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}which consists of the contrastive unlearning loss and the preservation loss, and γ is the hyperparameter.

The detailed pseudo-code of the proposed CURE framework is presented in Algorithm 1. For each forgotten node u, CURE computes relevance scores only within its candidate node set \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$C_u$$\end{document}. Experiments

Experimental setup

Datasets. We selected four widely used graph datasets: Cora, Citeseer43, PubMed44, and Physics45. Table 1 provides specific details of these datasets. Following the guidelines in22,24, each dataset was split such that 80% of the data were used for training and the remaining 20% for testing.

GNN Backbones. To further support the generality of CURE across different GNN architectures, we evaluate our method on five representative GNN backbones. These models can be divided into two groups: layer-wise message-passing GNNs, including GCN43, GAT46, and GraphSAGE47, and propagation-decoupled GNNs, including APPNP48 and SIGN49. The former performs neighborhood aggregation during each GNN layer, while the latter separates graph propagation from feature transformation or precomputes multi-hop propagated features. This setting allows us to evaluate whether CURE can generalize beyond conventional message-passing GNNs.

Baselines. We compared CURE with the following baselines: Retrain, which retrains the model from scratch after removing the data to be forgotten; GUIDE19, an exact GU method with two graph partition strategies, GUIDE-SR and GUIDE-Fast; and several approximate GU methods, including GNNDelete23, GIF22, MEGU24, and AGU25. For all methods, the hidden dimension of every model was set to 64, and the number of GNN layers was fixed at 2. To reduce randomness and ensure fair comparisons, each experiment was repeated 10 times, and the average performance together with the standard deviation was reported. Meanwhile, the number of training epochs for each GU strategy was tuned to its optimal value to ensure convergence.

Unlearning Targets. In our experiments, GU requests were categorized into three types. (1) Feature-level. We randomly selected 5% of the nodes from the training set and masked their entire feature vectors. (2) Node-level. We randomly selected 5% of the nodes from the training set and removed all edges connected to these nodes. (3) Edge-level. We randomly selected 5% of the edges from the training graph. The two nodes connected by each removed edge were then regarded as the unlearning targets.

Evaluation Metrics. We evaluated different unlearning methods from three perspectives: model utility, unlearning efficiency, and unlearning efficacy. Model utility was measured by the F1-score, unlearning efficiency was assessed by running time, and unlearning efficacy was evaluated through membership inference attacks50 and edge attack Node classification performance mainly reflects the predictive ability of the unlearned model on the remaining data. An ideal GU method should not only sufficiently reduce the influence of the target data, but also preserve the discriminative ability of the model on the remaining data as much as possible. Therefore, we evaluate model utility on the remaining dataset using the semi-supervised node classification task, with the F1-score as the evaluation metric. Tables 2, 3, and 4 present the performance comparison of different GU methods under node unlearning, feature unlearning, and edge unlearning settings, respectively. From the overall results, it can be observed that our method achieves competitive performance across multiple datasets and different GNN backbones.

Taking the node unlearning task in Table 2 as an example, CURE shows a clear utility advantage over exact unlearning methods. Compared with GUIDE, CURE achieves an average improvement of 4.48%, effectively alleviating the performance degradation caused by the disruption of the original graph topology in exact unlearning methods. Compared with approximate unlearning methods, CURE improves the F1-score by an average of 2.62% over MEGU, which is the second-best baseline. Compared with the strongest baseline AGU, CURE outperforms AGU on Cora and Citeseer, and achieves the second-best performance on the other two datasets. In addition, CURE achieves performance comparable to Retrain on all datasets, and even surpasses Retrain on Cora and Citeseer. To further evaluate the adaptability of CURE to different GNN architectures, Table 3 compares node unlearning and edge unlearning performance on two propagation-decoupled GNNs, APPNP and SIGN. Compared with the recent unlearning method AGU, CURE improves the average F1-score by 7.32 percentage points on APPNP and by 1.88 percentage points on SIGN. Moreover, CURE achieves the best performance under all settings on SIGN. These results indicate that CURE can also effectively adapt to propagation-decoupled models such as APPNP and SIGN, demonstrating its generality across different GNN architectures. For feature unlearning and edge unlearning in Table 4, CURE also shows strong performance. Under both GCN and GAT backbones, the results of CURE are overall very close to those of Retrain. In some cases, CURE outperforms all baselines, while in other cases it ranks second only to AGU. These results can be attributed to the PPR-based preservation mechanism, which helps maintain the discriminative ability of the remaining nodes and prevents the model utility from being severely degraded during unlearning.

Evaluation of unlearning efficiency

In addition to model utility, lower time cost helps GU methods handle frequent unlearning requests. To evaluate the computational efficiency of different GU methods, we compare the running time required by each method to perform the unlearning operation. Tables 2 and 3 present the average unlearning time of different methods on various datasets and GNN backbones.

Specifically, compared with Retrain and the exact GU method GUIDE, our method achieves a speedup ranging from 8.78× to 134.44×. This is because Retrain needs to retrain the model on the remaining data, while GUIDE needs to train multiple models, resulting in substantially higher time cost. Compared with GNNDelete, CURE achieves a speedup ranging from 1.24× to 79.47×. Although Delete does not require full retraining, it usually requires 200 to 300 epochs to reach convergence. GIF performs unlearning without training by directly modifying model parameters, while MEGU benefits from its mutual evolution module and only requires dozens of training epochs to complete unlearning. AGU also shows relatively high efficiency in some settings. For example, on the GCN and GAT backbones in Table 2, its running time is close to that of CURE, but it still incurs higher time cost on some large-scale datasets or specific backbones.

Compared with approximate unlearning methods such as GIF, MEGU, and AGU, CURE achieves comparable computational efficiency. The experimental results show that CURE has high running efficiency on small-scale datasets and can also maintain acceptable time cost on large-scale datasets. This is because CURE only performs targeted editing on the representations of forgotten nodes during unlearning, enabling the model to converge quickly. Evaluation of unlearning efficacy

To evaluate the extent to which the model forgets the target data, namely unlearning efficacy, we adopt two commonly used attack-based evaluation methods: membership inference attack50 and edge attack22,24.

Following the settings of existing GU studies, we perform membership inference attacks on the unlearned model to evaluate whether it still leaks information about the forgotten nodes. Specifically, the attacker obtains the differences between the GNN output embeddings before and after unlearning, identifies the nodes with larger representation changes, and infers them as forgotten nodes. An AUC closer to 0.5 indicates that the attack model can only make random guesses, implying better unlearning performance. The experimental results are shown in Table 5. Overall, existing approximate GU methods still suffer from certain privacy leakage risks. For example, on multiple datasets, the attack AUCs of GIF and Delete are higher than the random-guessing level of 0.5, indicating that the information of forgotten nodes may still be identified by the attacker. Compared with GIF and Delete, AGU achieves improvements on some datasets, with its AUC values being generally closer to 0.5. In contrast, CURE obtains lower attack AUCs in most experimental settings and is overall closer to the random-guessing level of 0.5, demonstrating that it can more effectively reduce the risk of information leakage from forgotten nodes.

For edge attack, we follow the protocols used in GIF and MEGU by adding heterophilic edges between nodes of different classes in the Cora graph and treating these heterophilic edges as the unlearning targets. We then evaluate the model utility of different methods under edge-noise conditions. As shown in Fig. 3, as the attack ratio increases, the performance of Vanilla Train directly trained on the noisy graph drops significantly. For example, on GAT, its performance decreases to as low as 66.78%. In contrast, GU methods can mitigate the impact of noise to some extent. GIF shows a clear performance drop under high noise levels, Delete performs relatively poorly overall, and MEGU remains relatively stable but still suffers from performance degradation under certain attack intensities. AGU can also effectively alleviate the negative impact caused by edge noise. Compared with AGU, CURE achieves comparable performance. On GCN, CURE obtains the highest F1-score under most attack ratios and still maintains a performance of around 87% under high-noise conditions. On GAT, CURE maintains a performance above 86% under relatively high attack ratios and overall outperforms most baselines.

In summary, CURE not only effectively reduces potential privacy leakage risks, but also mitigates the negative impact caused by structural noise. Ablation study

To validate the effectiveness of the components in CURE, we conduct ablation studies on Cora, Citeseer, and PubMed by constructing two variants. (1) w/o ASS. removing the adaptive sample selection module; (2) w/o PSP. removing the PPR-based stability preservation module. For both variants, we replace the selected nodes with an equal number of randomly chosen nodes. The results in Table 6 show that CURE outperforms both variants in most cases. Removing ASS leads to performance degradation and increased AUC, indicating that adaptive sample selection can more accurately identify nodes highly correlated with the target nodes, thereby improving unlearning effectiveness. Similarly, removing PSP results in a performance decline, demonstrating that this module effectively alleviates the impact of unlearning on neighboring nodes and helps preserve the model’s overall predictive capability.

Robustness analysis

To evaluate the stability of GU methods under different unlearning ratios, we conduct experiments with varying scales of unlearning requests. As shown in Fig. 4, when the unlearning ratio increases from 10% to 40%, all GU methods exhibit performance degradation to varying degrees. This decline is mainly caused by the removal of a relatively large proportion of data, which significantly affects model prediction51. Notably, the performance of GIF on the GCN backbone decreases sharply as the unlearning ratio increases, indicating that this method is sensitive to large-scale unlearning requests and lacks robustness. MEGU and AGU can alleviate the performance degradation in some settings; however, as the unlearning ratio increases, MEGU still shows a certain decline in classification performance. In contrast, CURE only exhibits a slight decrease in F1-score and outperforms other methods in some settings, while ranking second only to AGU in a few cases. These results demonstrate that CURE can still maintain good model utility under large-scale unlearning requests and achieves an effective trade-off between different unlearning scales and model inference capability.

Unlearning visualization

To further intuitively analyze the unlearning effect of our method, Fig. 5 presents a two-dimensional visualization of node hidden representations before and after unlearning, where green points denote forgotten nodes and orange points denote their related nodes. In the original model, the forgotten nodes and their related nodes show clear overlap in the representation space and are clustered in nearby regions. After unlearning, the forgotten nodes gradually deviate from their original clusters and exhibit a more evident separation trend from the related nodes. This suggests that CURE can adjust the representation distribution of forgotten nodes and reduce their representation association with related nodes. The visualization provides intuitive evidence for the effectiveness of CURE from the representation-space perspective, indicating that it helps weaken the coupling between the unlearning targets and their original structural neighborhoods.

Conclusion and discussion

Conclusion

In this paper, we study the unlearning problem in GNNs and observe that existing methods struggle to achieve a desirable balance among unlearning efficiency, utility, and effectiveness, particularly in eliminating the influence of target data and its propagation in graph structures. To address this issue, we propose CURE, a unified GU framework based on representation editing. Specifically, we design an adaptive sample selection module to identify key nodes highly correlated with the target nodes from both structural representations and predictive distributions. Furthermore, we employ contrastive representation reconstruction to push forgotten nodes away from their original structural-semantic neighborhoods, thereby removing their influence. In addition, we introduce a stability preservation module based on personalized PageRank to maintain prediction consistency for affected nodes and preserve model performance on the remaining data. Experimental results demonstrate that CURE achieves a favorable trade-off among model utility, unlearning efficiency, and unlearning effectiveness, and performs better than or comparably to existing baselines in most experimental settings. Moreover, it exhibits strong advantages in privacy protection and robustness analysis.

Limitations and future work

Despite the promising overall performance achieved by CURE in our experiments, it still has several limitations. First, this paper transforms edge-level unlearning into endpoint-node-level unlearning. Although this strategy facilitates the unification of different unlearning tasks, it may lead to over-unlearning in certain cases. Nevertheless, the results of the edge attack experiments show that CURE can effectively mitigate the impact of structural noise on model performance. Second, CURE randomly selects heterogeneous nodes as negative samples to reposition the forgotten nodes, which may introduce slight semantic interference and disturb the embedding space. However, large-scale unlearning requests are not common in practical scenarios, and the stability preservation mechanism constrains the prediction distributions of affected nodes. Therefore, such interference usually does not cause severe performance degradation. In future work, we will explore more reliable negative reference node selection strategies to further reduce this risk. In addition, CURE is an approximate GU method. Although it achieves a favorable balance among efficiency, model utility, and unlearning effectiveness, it still cannot provide a strict guarantee of data deletion.
