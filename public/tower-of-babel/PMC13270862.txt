Feasibility and acceptability of the Health Assessment Tool (HAT) in Swedish primary care: insights into older patients’ needs and professional perspectives
Authors: Emmanouil Kokoroskos, Ahmad Abbadi, Amaia Calderón-Larrañaga, Caroline Wachtler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Health Assessment Tool (HAT) is a validated instrument designed to comprehensively assess the health of older adults. This study examinedthe feasibility and acceptability of implementing the HAT in Swedish primary care, while exploring older patients’ needs and professional perspectives, drawing on experiences from a multicenter prospective validation study.MethodsA qualitative design was employed using reflexive thematic analysis. Data were collected through semi-structured interviews with patients and healthcare managers, and a focus group discussion with healthcare staff familiarized with the HAT in six Swedish primary care centers. Analysis followed Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic approach, integrating inductive and deductive reasoning.ResultsThe analysis constructed one overarching theme: ‘For the needs left waiting, the HAT is a feasible bridge to holistic care of older adults’, supported by two main themes: ‘Voices of aging, unmet needs unveiled’ and ‘The HAT: a dependable compass for decision making in the care of older people’. Findings revealed challenges for older adults, including rapid health decline, loss of independence, loneliness, and gaps in primary care. The HAT was positively received for its comprehensive, person-centered approach, despite some concerns regarding time required for its assessment.ConclusionsThe HAT was perceived as feasible and acceptable by older patients and healthcare providers, with the potential to transform care of older adults through proactive, holistic assessments. Its use may foster collaboration between health and social care professionals, a key prerequisite for meeting the complex needs of older people. Future research should evaluate a HAT-based model to support its integration into routine primary care.

Background

The development of geriatric assessment tools is highly prioritized in research on older adults [1]. The aging process and care needs vary significantly among older adults [2], whose health status should be assessed multidimensionally, taking functional aspects into account [3]. The concept of frailty, an increased vulnerability to external homeostatic threats, emerged precisely in response to this paradigm shift [4]. Despite relevant applications of frailty indices in gerontology, these tools typically categorize older persons in few mutually exclusive frailty states (e.g. robust, prefrail, frail), limiting the ability to monitor subtle yet clinically meaningful changes in health status over time. Moreover, the concept of frailty has often been criticized for its potential to contribute to the medicalization of aging when used as a formal medical diagnosis [5]. In the transition from the current fragmented care systems to a model of integrated person-centered care, tools for the comprehensive assessment of older people’s health and social care needs are urgently warranted [6]. These are especially important in primary care, where most complex older patients are managed and monitored over time.

One such instrument designed to capture the complexity and variability of the health among older adults is the Health Assessment Tool (HAT). The HAT is a validated instrument [7] for comprehensively assessing the health status of older adults on a continuous scale using multiple indicators, developed using data from the Swedish National study on Aging and Care in Kungsholmen (SNAC-K) [8]. Designed to evaluate and visualize age-related variations in health among older adults, the HAT incorporates five indicators: disabilities in personal and instrumental activities of daily living (P-ADL and I-ADL), gait speed, cognitive function, and number of chronic diseases. HAT-scores predict unplanned hospitalizations and mortality better than each of their individual components and other geriatric health indices [7,9,10]. Considering these features, the HAT has the potential to support primary care clinicians in detecting older patients with impending health decline and high care needs, facilitating informed, person-centered, and holistic clinical decision making.

Despite evidence supporting the tool’s predictive performance [7,8,10,11], the extent to which the HAT can be applied in primary care as a clinical prediction tool remains unknown, and its implementation potential may vary depending on the level of acceptance by end-users [12] and the perceived benefits of its use in routine practice. In Sweden, a more extensive comprehensive geriatric assessment instrument was recently tested in primary care; however, its implementation was deemed suboptimal because of lack of partnership among stakeholders, poor acceptance and awareness among family physicians and patients, and challenges related to operationalization [13,14]. Indeed, to mitigate the risk of unsuccessful implementation of prognostic tools across care settings, the literature suggests consulting end-users to assess acceptability, identify possible barriers to feasibility, and inform tailoring strategies [15–18].

In this study, we aimed to assess the acceptability of the HAT among patients and healthcare staff across different primary care settings in Sweden, and to explore intrinsic and organizational barriers and opportunities related to its feasibility. We also sought to map the needs of the end-users for tailoring its future implementation in routine primary care practice.

Methods

Study setting

The current study was performed in the context of an ongoing prospective cohort study to assess the predictive performance of the HAT in Swedish primary care. Of the six participating primary care centers, four were in Stockholm (Danderyds akademiska vårdcentral, Jakobsbergs universitetsvårdcentral, Liljeholmens universitetsvårdcentral & Husläkarmottagning i Täby centrum), one in Nordanstig (Premicare Bergsjö Hälsocentral), and one in region Kalmar in Öland (Färjestadens hälsocentral). Quantitative data were collected by four nurses and two family physicians working in the primary care centers between 30th of November 2022 and 10th of December 2024, for the purposes of the prospective cohort study. Adults aged 60 and older visiting or registered in the primary care centers were invited to undergo two clinical assessments at baseline and follow-up after 6–12 months. The clinical assessments included the five components of the HAT—global cognition (using the Mini Mental State Examination), gait speed, I-ADL, P-ADL, and number of chronic diseases—as well as examination of the health related quality of life using the EuroQoL 5 dimensional-5 level questionnaire (EQ-5D-5L) [19]. Additional information on the two clinical assessments and the construction of the prospective cohort study is available in the supplementary file.

Study design

To address the aims of this study, a qualitative design principled in constructivism was utilized. Within this study, one researcher (EK) took a ‘participant as observer’ role. He worked in one of the primary care centers, had access to the participants, was in contact with the data collectors, and was also data collector himself [20]. The rest of researchers (AA, ACL, CW) took an ‘observer as participant’ role and were in contact with the quantitative data collection team but had no interactions with patients themselves [20]. To enhance the analytical consciousness of the group, a reflexivity exercise was done inspired by Braun and Clark’s recommendations [21,22] and the results are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Co-authors’ previous experiences, both in health care and research, had a major role on their perceptions and expectations of the project, mainly characterized by the question of how we can improve primary healthcare for older patients with complex health.

Data collection

One year after project start, between November-December 2023, a focus group discussion (FGD) was held with the data collectors, and one-to-one semi-structured interviews were held with the managers of the primary care centers and patients. Using purposive sampling [23], we aimed to recruit participants across age, sex, and region. Given the heterogeneity in health status across age groups among older adults, as well as inequities in access to health care between rural areas in Sweden and Stockholm, we anticipated that the relevance of the HAT might differ by age, sex, and region. The sampling strategy therefore aimed to include four participants from each region: one man and one woman younger than 75 years, and one man and one woman aged 75 years or older. To achieve this, EK identified 24 eligible individuals across the participating sites and invited them to participate. Of these, 20 responded to the invitation, and 19 agreed to participate and were interviewed. The initial interview guide was piloted with seven older adults; it was later revised to include additional open-ended questions gathering richer data. Pilot interviews were included in the analysis. While all six primary care managers were invited for the semi-structured interviews, only three accepted to participate. One follow-up interview with a healthcare manager was additionally conducted because the first interview recording was partially lost. The FGD was carried out with the nurses and the family physician involved in the quantitative data collection. Four nurses and one family physician participated, and it lasted 2 h and 11 min. The interviews with older adults lasted between 4 and 21 min and those with healthcare managers between 18 and 48 min. They were all performed by EK, with assistance from CW for the interviews with primary care managers. All interviews were performed in Swedish and administered through Microsoft Teams, telephone, or in physical meetings for few participants who lived in Stockholm. The latter were conducted in person to facilitate and expedite data collection, as quantitative data were collected in parallel. No differences in data richness between in‑person and telephone interviews were noted in EK’s reflexivity journal. Details on the domains of the interviews and the interview guides are available in the supplementary file. Interviews were transcribed verbatim by a private company (Spoken AB) and were translated to English through DeepL. Translation accuracy was checked by CW, a native English and fluent Swedish speaker. The characteristics of the participants in the semi-structured interviews and FGD are summarized in Table 1.

Data analysis

Transcripts were analyzed using Braun and Clarke’s six-phase thematic analysis process [21,24]. An abductive approach was used [25]. Reassessment to understand the data was done by iterating between a primarily inductive approach but also deductive reflections based on frameworks and theories, such as the Normalization Process Theory [26,27] and the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [28] to support interpretation and theme construction.

During the analysis, three interviews were coded in pairs by EK and AA using Nvivo 14. The codes were then discussed with ACL and CW to assess concerns and share reflections regarding the coding process. Following agreement on the coding method, EK coded the remaining interviews alone. EK and AA met over a three-month period to discuss codes and iteratively define, review, and reconstruct themes and sub-themes across the entire dataset, with group reflections assisting the iterative process. The iterative process continued until consensus was reached among co-authors. To keep track of the analytical process, EK kept a reflexivity journal throughout the study. The Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) checklist was followed in reporting this study and is provided in the supplementary file.

Results

We generated one overarching theme: For the needs left waiting, the HAT is a feasible bridge to holistic care of older adults. Figure 1 presents the architecture of our results.

The development of the overarching theme.

The overarching theme, ‘For the needs left waiting, the HAT is a feasible bridge to holistic care of older adults’, captures how essential health, functional, cognitive, and psychosocial needs of older adults often remain unrecognized or insufficiently addressed within routine primary care, due to limited continuity, resource constraints, and the absence of systematic multidimensional assessment. Within this context, participants perceived the HAT as a feasible bridge that makes these needs visible earlier, structures the assessment of complex health problems, and supports more holistic, informed, and proactive care planning.

The ‘needs left waiting’ refer to unmet needs for timely identification of health decline, coordinated responses across professionals, and person‑centered conversations that address what matters most to older adults. The notion of ‘waiting’ reflects delays in comprehensive assessment, proactive follow‑up, and care coordination, described by participants as common features of current practice.

The overarching theme integrates two main themes: ‘Voices of aging, unmet needs unveiled’, reflecting how lived experiences of aging shape diverse and often unaddressed care needs; and ‘The HAT: a dependable compass for decision making in the care of older adults’, illustrating how participants envision the HAT supporting clinical decision-making in routine practice. The use of the HAT was seen to stimulate solution-oriented discussions, positioning it as both a guide for decision-making and a structured entry point for identifying older adults’ care needs; ‘With other instruments you might evaluate nutrition, fall risk and so on. But here [with the HAT], you can get a more holistic view’. – Nurse 3.

Theme 1 ‘voices of aging, unmet needs unveiled’

This theme describes the limited capacities and gaps of primary care compared to the map of care needs of older adults. Building on the multifaceted challenges faced by older adults as they navigate the aging process—rapid health decline, loss of independence, and pervasive loneliness—this theme highlights the current systemic obstacles that hinder primary care’s efforts to deliver holistic care to older adults. The needs of older adults, as narrated in the subtheme ‘Aging unfolded’, are often compounded by insufficient support from a primary care system constrained by limited resources, leading to lack of continuity and limited access—issues further explored in the subtheme ‘Gaps widen where care feels thin’; ‘That is one thing among many, to find accessibility that works better for these older people. That’s where they easily get stuck, I think that there are more channels for younger people.’ – Nurse 1.

Subtheme 1 ‘aging unfolded’

This subtheme reflects the lived experiences of older adults as they narrate the uncertainties and challenges of aging transitions, which are simultaneously witnessed by the healthcare workers. The effects of aging are reflected in fast health decline that jeopardizes functional independence, often requiring adaptations or sacrifices by the older individuals; ‘But I can’t take a bus there. I don’t have a car; I got rid of it because I’m too old to drive. And I can’t walk from home to the forest and back again. [.] it’s impossible’ – Woman (patient), 70.

Aging is often described as being accompanied by loneliness, which has damaging effects on health and well-being. Loneliness often emerges with aging, as familiar sources of meaning and connection—such as work, friends, spouse, the role of caregiver—gradually disappear, leaving the individual vulnerable and searching to fill the void, with burdensome consequences for health and healthcare; ‘They [lonely older people] are the ones who put an incredible burden on society. Then you [the lonely older person] go to the health center looking for diseases, trying to fix [the loneliness]’ – Man (patient), 79.

With health and social network declines, older people prioritize keeping their existing levels of physical resilience and social interactions. Staying physically and mentally robust requires, however, a personal investment; ‘Then you become sedentary. And I want to avoid that… So, yes. Two days a week I volunteer at the museum’. – Man (patient), 78 – but also easier access to care; ‘Well, it’s that you get in touch with doctors when you need to and well that’s the most important thing’. – Man (patient), 78.

Subtheme 2 ‘gaps widen where care feels thin’

As the challenges of aging unfold, older adults experience a lack of support from primary care. This subtheme illustrates how limitations and gaps in the primary healthcare system prevent it from meeting the care needs of older people, as well as how healthcare workers try to make up for these shortcomings. Access to primary care and navigation within the healthcare system are fraught with obstacles that older adults are to overcome with minimal help and often without continuity or a clear follow-up plan; ‘Because you can get stuck somewhere in this carousel, [.] First the family physician, referral, something, investigations, back to family physician and then it’s not granted that you get back to the same family physician or anywhere at all’. – Woman (patient), 74. Primary care is described by healthcare workers as underfunded, with resulting understaffing and overload as main factors for lack of continuity; ‘I also recognize that with just the continuity. [.] many described that they do not remember [their family physician]. They barely know their doctor has changed. They don’t know which doctor they have. They are simply passed around’ – Nurse 3. Efforts to improve the access of older adults seem to have been lost in the vastness of the family physicians’ duties; ‘The care center has had an elderly care team, but I don’t know how active they are now’. – Manager 2.

Addressing the needs of older adults is of high clinical importance to healthcare workers, particularly regarding the health decline and loneliness; ‘Otherwise, the elderly are a main target group for us, [.] since they often are to a greater extent more in need of care’. – Manager 3. However, older patients themselves, in their contact with primary care, experience that their needs are not heard or that there is insufficient time for them. Lack of person or family centeredness is recognized by healthcare workers and explained by time restrictions due to the scarceness of resources but also because of the neglect of informal caregivers. Systematic health assessments, which could help identify the often-overlooked needs of older patients or even serve as a solution to access issues, are largely absent from routine primary care; ‘What is it called then? A full health check, the whole thing, without me specifying that I have a problem. There is no such thing’. – Woman (patient), 70.

Furthermore, primary care services are primarily oriented to older people who are considered to have significant or urgent needs. Home healthcare is overburdened, often performing services beyond the duties and expertise of primary care, such as blood transfusions, while for example dementia care continues to exhibit substantial deficiencies. While patients with robust family support are often detected in the early phases of dementia, those who are lonely and vulnerable remain unnoticed by healthcare staff, reflecting the absence of systematic dementia screening: ‘Obviously most people who [.] have a family member with them are identified, but still we sometimes find people who are quite advanced in dementia who are unknown to the care system’. – Family physician. Moreover, problematic communication with municipality care has hindered effective collaboration in caring for patients with dementia or other vulnerable groups. In most Swedish regions, municipalities are responsible for home assistance and nursing homes, while primary care provides home healthcare and medical services. Because these two sectors operate under different administrative structures, participants described communication and coordination between them as suboptimal.

The challenges of aging, interpreted also as challenges for the healthcare system, highlight the public health dimension and the urgency of addressing the needs of older people; ‘But it’s a tough situation, you could say. And given the demographics, it will be even tougher. There is no doubt about that’ – Family physician.

Theme 2 ‘the HAT: a dependable compass for decision making in the care of older people’

By meeting users’ needs and inspiring trust, the HAT has the potential to guide decision-making in the care of older adults, especially when applied as intended by its end-users to capitalize on its strengths. This theme, constructed upon two subthemes ‘Tested and trusted’ and ‘Guiding care, bridging gaps’, captures the added value of the HAT as reflected in participants’ experiences and preferences, and its anticipated role within the challenging context of primary care. The subtheme ‘Tested and trusted’ explores participants’ reflections on the acceptability and feasibility of the tool, while the subtheme ‘Guiding care, bridging gaps’ reflects the intention to use this trusted instrument in practice; ‘What I have understood from the tool itself is that it will be able to identify early risk factors for when you need help. And yes, what do I think about that? It’s absolutely fantastic’. – Manager 1.

Subtheme 3: ‘tested and trusted’

The use of the HAT in primary healthcare offered many advantages, highlighting its added value and practical utility despite some limitations. Compared to routine care, participants expressed several benefits of using the HAT: assessment of physical and daily functioning, preventive capacity, quantitative interpretation of health, and holistic, person-centered approach.

The HAT is valued for assessing aspects of health that are highly significant for older patients, yet not typically examined in routine practice. In particular, the evaluation of physical function through gait speed and daily functioning was perceived as an important strength of this tool for geriatric health assessment; ‘Usually when you go to a health center, you have so little time [.] Then [with the HAT] it’s good to check up on little things like that, I think it’s important—just the everyday stuff, it affects a lot’. – Woman (patient), 65. Cognitive decline and the risk of dementia are common concerns among older adults, and cognitive assessment with the HAT provided them with reassurance. Moreover, healthcare workers reported that a significant number of study participants were referred for further dementia investigations after being screened for cognitive impairment using the HAT.

Besides its value for the early detection of dementia, the HAT was also appreciated for its other preventive functions, such as fall risk assessment, detecting health decline, and promoting measures to reverse or detain its progression; ‘Well that would be the purpose I guess of this. To find them [those with health decline] quickly. So, it would be a good thing to add this in routine [care] in connection with an annual check-up’. – Man (patient), 71. Furthermore, improved visualization and understanding of health decline may enhance health awareness and motivate lifestyle changes; ‘You can motivate the patient in a different way if you have this tool [.], when the patient gets it in black and white, you can strengthen the patient’s integrity and desire to do something themselves, and also to draw the patient’s attention to the risks that exist’. – Manager 2. The interpretation of health as a continuous variable offers an additional benefit for follow-up, enabling the detection of changes over time.

Participants found that care needs, such as incontinence, which are often left unspoken due to stigma, can be more easily addressed in conversations facilitated by the HAT. Moreover, the HAT was perceived as a tool that supports a more holistic understanding of older adults’ needs. The multidimensional nature of the HAT, along with the time invested in its assessment, seemed to create an open and inviting atmosphere during patient encounters, bringing participants closer to person- or family-centered care; ‘I think a conversation or meeting like this can open up more because you have time to ask what’s important to the patient. You may feel that they have a problem with this, but the person seeking care feels something completely different [.] So in this way, I think it could be that you get closer to family centered [care]’. – Nurse 4.

As for the feasibility of the tool, the time needed to complete the HAT assessment was interpreted in several ways. The long duration of the session was at times perceived positively, offering an in-depth understanding of the complex needs of older adults and providing them with the valuable experience of feeling heard; ‘We have seen that many people have found it nice to sit and talk and that someone is listening. I think a lot of people have appreciated that, that they’ve been able to talk’ – Nurse 4. On the other hand, the HAT assessment sessions were seen by some as too comprehensive to be integrated into a routine doctor’s appointment. Time estimates for completion ranged between 10 and 60 min, including tasks such as obtaining informed consent, collecting demographic data, and administering the EQ5D5L, none of which would be needed in a pragmatic, real-world application. Despite this, participating patients generally perceived the length of the HAT assessment session as appropriate; ‘It was totally okay. It was quite reasonable’ – Man (patient), 71.

Healthcare workers considered themselves adequately skilled to administer the HAT, yet they appreciated the instructional video provided. Only a short amount of time was needed at the beginning of the study to familiarize themselves with the tool and establish a working flow: ‘I think it went smoothly even if you hadn’t practiced much before. But you noticed that, after assessing just a few patients, you could take it with much better flow. But it went pretty fast anyway to get there’. – Nurse 3.

There were some objections about ‘things missing from the tool’, the greatest shortcoming being the lack of assessment of loneliness and social isolation. The HAT was also considered easy to administer by the healthcare staff, and participating patients reported no difficulties with its completion.

Subtheme 4:’ ‘guiding care, bridging gaps’

The HAT was seen as having potential applications in primary care and beyond to transform the care of older adults and overcome the limitations of current care models. Participants envisioned the HAT being implemented in routine practice, with care interventions guided by its assessment results. Each component of the HAT generated clinically meaningful findings that required attention, which often extended the duration of the HAT sessions; ‘But then there have been secondary findings [.]; we have not been able to leave those we have talked to [.] And we have helped with this and that, booking doctor’s appointments and district nurse appointments, referring to psychologists, counselors. We have given advice on self-care’. – Nurse 2.

Participants anticipated that the HAT assessment could be carried out by district nurses, who in their role as ‘spiders in the web’, can orchestrate care by contacting and delegating tasks to other primary care professionals; ‘I think that if you want it to work, it has to fall to the district nurse and then you collaborate depending on what comes up, that you contact the doctor or the person who needs to be contacted [.] physiotherapist, occupational therapist…’ – Nurse 1. Furthermore, nurses can take independent actions, such as prescribing incontinence aids or offering lifestyle recommendations, and have access to communication channels with municipal social care services.

In line with the emerging trend of the elderly care clinics within primary care centers—an initiative aimed at proactively addressing older patients’ care needs—participants considered the central role that the HAT could play in this model. By leveraging the tool’s preventive properties, they suggested using the HAT as an annual, systematic health assessment to identify older adults with significant health decline and to offer timely interventions to slow of reverse it; ‘It’s called a health check… a health talk. Yes, everyone who is over 70 or maybe even older, over 75. To discover [health changes] and maybe if you already have ill health to keep you as healthy as possible’. – Nurse 2.

However, considering their experiences with younger older adults, many of whom considered themselves too healthy for the tool, healthcare workers suggested targeting the HAT toward patients over the age of 70, whose care needs are typically higher. By using the HAT findings, physicians would be able to make more informed clinical decisions and arrange appropriate follow-up. Overall, the HAT was seen as a potential compass for clinical decision-making and care planning: initiating contact with patients, identifying care and intervention needs, and serving as both an outcome measure and a monitoring tool. Healthcare professionals also envisioned the HAT as a means to improve collaboration with municipal services, providing a common ground for shared decision-making in the care of patients with dementia or those in need for assisted living.

Participants noted that the future implementation of the HAT largely depends on decision-makers and therefore requires studies proving its benefits. However, they emphasized that cost-effectiveness alone should not be the sole measure of value: ‘But there is a little bit of a trap there, which is that you think that everything must lead to cost savings [.] improvements cost money too [.] because we find things that need to be fixed and that does not necessarily lead to cost savings. [.] The main thing is that they lead to some benefit that is worth the money you spend on it’. – Family physician.

Discussion

In this qualitative study exploring the feasibility and acceptability of the HAT in Swedish primary care, the tool was perceived as acceptable by end-users, as it addressed the unmet needs of older adults in this setting, particularly regarding holistic health assessment and the proactive identification of age-related health issues, cognitive decline, and risks to independent living. The added value of the HAT is four-dimensional, encompassing the assessment of physical and daily functioning, its preventive capacity, the quantitative interpretation of health status, and its holistic, person-centered approach. Although social connections and access to care were highly prioritized needs among older adults, these aspects are not covered by the tool. However, participants claimed that applying the tool for annual health assessments in primary care patients above a certain age threshold could partially mitigate issues related to access. The comprehensiveness of the HAT does not match with the limited time available per visit for family physicians, presenting a feasibility challenge in routine primary care. Participants suggested that the HAT should be administered by nurses instead and used as a targeted screening tool for health decline in older adults. It is envisioned as a starting point for clinical decision-making and the prescription of non-pharmacological interventions that support healthier aging, as well as a common ground for collaboration between primary and municipal social care.

Our findings indicate that the limited capacities of primary care, primarily stemming from underfunding, hinder the support of older people in managing their struggles associated with aging, including rapid health decline, increased propensity to falls, frailty, and incontinence. These findings are aligned with reports from the WHO[6,29]. The unmet need among older adults for integrated, multidisciplinary, and continuous care—alongside the involvement of informal caregivers—is also consistent with current evidence [30]. Participants’ concerns about the public health implications of aging, grounded in demographic trends, align with the World Report on Aging and Health [29], as well as with evidence highlighting the inadequate preparedness of the current healthcare workforce to address the consequences of population aging [31]. As reflected in our overarching theme, the importance of employing comprehensive assessments to detect early signs of health decline—within an integrated care model led by primary care—has been reiterated in several studies and policy reports [6,32–35].

The qualitative methodological approach of our study enabled the research team to explore the added value of the HAT as experienced by end-users, revealing it to be a meaningful instrument with the potential to improve healthcare delivery. In contrast, similar tools have shown varying perceived utility in previous quantitative or mixed-method studies, with some clinicians placing greater trust in their own judgement over standardized risk stratification tools, or finding such tools overly sensitive and nonspecific [36,37]. Clinicians’ skepticism, particularly regarding the reliability of self‑administered surveys [37], appears misaligned with international strategies emphasizing person‑centered approaches to the clinical assessment of older people [38,39]. Although the perceived inferiority of self‑administered tools compared with clinician‑administered assessments remains debated, indices that incorporate at least one clinically assessed component of physical functioning—such as the locomotion domain of intrinsic capacity or gait speed in the HAT—consistently demonstrate higher predictive validity for mortality [7,9,40]. By integrating clinical and patient‑reported data, the HAT may help bridge the gap between predictive performance and person‑centeredness. Previous studies also report contradictory attitudes regarding feasibility and acceptability of frailty identification tools, with concerns about time constraints and logistic challenges—even for unidimensional tests such as the four-meter gait speed test [36,37]. Unlike other feasibility and acceptability studies that offer limited guidance on mitigation strategies for future implementation [37,41,42], our study drew on the experiences of family physicians, nurses, and patients who had long-term exposure to the HAT. This approach provides insights into the conditions needed to overcome feasibility barriers and informs strategies for the sustainable integration of the HAT in routine practice [43,44].

For example, participants’ proposal to assign nurses the role of geriatric care coordinators, which is supported by both Swedish and international literature [45,46], could mitigate the challenges of interprofessional collaboration and logistic barriers mentioned in our study. Finally, participants’ vision for adapting and applying the tool in the future reflects a process of reflexive monitoring [26,27], and indicates their firm engagement with the proposed change and intention to adopt the tool moving forward.

Strengths and limitations

Our study demonstrates trustworthiness through a transparent design and the use of reflexive thematic analysis. Credibility is enhanced by the proximity of the researchers to the study population and by the structured reflexivity exercise, which unfolded the researchers’ positionings in relation to the data through the sharing of personal ‘mirrors’ and ‘backpacks’ [47]. This process offers readers greater insight into the interpretative lens applied in the analysis [48,49], which was primarily grounded in health sciences reasoning aimed at clinical and public health applications, rather than in informing social or psychological theories. Prolonged engagement with the data, combined with the triangulation across researchers, and data collection methods, further strengthen the study’s credibility [50]. Dependability is supported by the clear and open description of the iterative analytic process [48], while the inclusion of illustrative excerpts from the data contributes to the confirmability of the findings. Our inclusive purposive sampling of a defined population—individuals exposed to the HAT in primary care—and the alignment of our results with current evidence support the transferability of findings [50].

All data collectors in our study had prior research experience; while this high level of expertise greatly enriched the study, it also represents a deviation from the realities of routine clinical practice. Notably, some nurses, despite their extensive clinical and research background and their role as key informants on the use of the HAT, had only limited prior experience in primary care settings. Finally, this qualitative study explored acceptability and feasibility beyond the conventional quantitative approaches, as part of a larger implementation strategy that engaged all relevant stakeholders. As such, the study offered an in-depth perspective on the barriers and facilitators influencing the feasibility and acceptability of implementing the HAT in routine primary care.

Conclusions

The HAT was deemed feasible and acceptable by patients, healthcare workers, and managers in Swedish primary care, with the potential to effectively address the unmet needs of older adults through a holistic care approach. Although challenges such as extended session duration were noted, the tool was positively received by patients and healthcare providers alike. Importantly, participants contributed valuable insights and practical suggestions for the tool’s implementation in primary care. The findings suggest that the HAT is particularly appropriate for older adults with complex health needs, including those over 70 years and/or those at risk of functional or cognitive decline. In primary care, the tool could be used as a targeted annual health assessment administered by nurses to systematically detect early signs of deterioration and guide individualized follow‑up. By providing a multidimensional picture of health, the HAT may support more proactive care planning, strengthen coordination across care providers, and complement existing clinical decision-making processes. Future research should focus on integrating the HAT into routine practice and evaluating the effectiveness of care models based on its use.
