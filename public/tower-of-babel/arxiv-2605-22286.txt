EmoTrack: Robust Depression Tracking from Counseling Transcripts across Session Regimes
Zhaomin Wu, Jiayi Li, Bingsheng He

Abstract
Text-based counseling is an important interface for AI mental-health support, where transcripts may be used to monitor depression severity and flag sessions requiring timely human review. However, robust PHQ-8 prediction across session regimes remains challenging: fine-tuning-based methods can exploit richer supervision but may generalize poorly under data scarcity, while prompt-based LLM methods are data-efficient but usually treat each transcript holistically and provide limited support for longitudinal context. We study robust depression tracking from counseling transcripts across single-session and multi-session regimes. We introduce LongCounsel-8 , a multi-session counseling dataset with session-level PHQ-8 supervision for evaluating repeated-session tracking under partial symptom disclosure and cross-session continuity. We further propose EmoTrack , a PHQ-8 prediction framework that combines LLM-extracted clinical signals with frozen turn-level semantic embeddings and trains symptom-specific predictors over the resulting transcript representation. When prior sessions are available, EmoTrack can further incorporate them through compact cross-session memory. Experiments on LongCounsel-8 and Daic-Woz show that EmoTrack achieves a clear gain on the real single-session benchmark, including a 13.5% relative MAE reduction over the strongest Daic-Woz baseline, and remains competitive with the strongest longitudinal baseline on LongCounsel-8 .

Introduction

Text-based counseling is becoming an important interface for AI mental-health support, as reflected by recent counseling-oriented dialogue systems and datasets
[
16
,
26
,
38
]
. In such settings, depression assessment is not only an offline prediction problem: a system may need to estimate the client’s current PHQ-8
[
11
]
severity during or after a counseling conversation, flag high-risk sessions when the predicted score exceeds a predefined threshold, and support timely human review. We study this problem across session regimes, including
single-session
settings, where only the current transcript is available, and
multi-session
settings, where prior counseling sessions from the same client may provide additional context. Here, a
turn
refers to a single utterance in a counseling dialogue, produced by either the client or the counselor, and a
session
refers to one complete counseling encounter consisting of multiple turns.

Existing text-based depression assessment methods mainly follow two directions. The first uses trainable neural or fine-tuning-based models to learn task-specific predictors from labeled data
[
13
,
22
,
29
]
. The second uses prompt-based or holistic LLM methods to directly score a transcript, extract clinical features, or complete structured questionnaires
[
30
,
14
]
. These two directions reflect different assumptions about supervision: the former relies on task-specific labeled data, while the latter relies more on the general clinical reasoning ability of LLMs.

However, these methods face a two-sided cross-regime limitation. Fine-tuning-based methods can benefit from data-rich multi-session settings, but may generalize poorly when labeled data are scarce or the target regime changes. Prompt-based holistic methods are more data-efficient and can work better in scarce-data settings, but they usually treat each transcript as a whole and provide limited mechanisms for learning turn-level evidence or exploiting cross-session continuity. The key problem is therefore not only longitudinal tracking, but robust PHQ-8 prediction across session regimes.

To address these limitations, we pursue a hybrid and regime-adaptive approach. Rather than using LLM prompting as the final zero-shot predictor, we use it only to extract structured clinical cues; rather than fine-tuning a large encoder on scarce depression labels, we train supervised predictors on top of frozen semantic representations. The goal is to build a strong current-session model that remains effective in single-session settings, while allowing prior-session context to be added when available.

Realizing this idea raises three challenges: (i) depression evidence is distributed across turns and often expressed indirectly; (ii) prior-session context is useful but noisy, so direct history concatenation can introduce stale or irrelevant evidence; and (iii) existing resources are insufficient for evaluating both regimes, since
Daic-Woz
provides real single-session interviews
[
7
]
, while existing multi-session counseling resources rarely provide standardized session-level PHQ supervision
[
26
,
24
,
34
]
.

We propose
EmoTrack
, a PHQ-8 prediction framework, together with
LongCounsel-8
, a multi-session counseling dataset, to address these challenges.
(i) For distributed and indirect within-session evidence, EmoTrack represents each session as structured clinical-feature tokens and turn-level semantic tokens, then uses self-attention to integrate heterogeneous evidence and PHQ-8 symptom queries to attend to symptom-relevant cues.
(ii) For noisy prior-session context, EmoTrack avoids direct history concatenation and instead compresses the previous session into compact attention-based memory, which is selectively injected through gated symptom-level updates and skipped when no history is available.
(iii) For the evaluation gap, we construct
LongCounsel-8
, which provides session-level
PHQ-8
labels for multi-session counseling conversations, enabling evaluation under partial symptom disclosure and multi-session settings.

Our contributions are threefold: (1) we frame PHQ-8 prediction across single- and multi-session counseling settings and propose EmoTrack, a practical model that combines structured clinical signals, turn-level semantics, and compact prior-session memory; (2) we introduce
LongCounsel-8
, a controlled synthetic benchmark with known symptom trajectories, partial disclosure, cross-session continuity, and standardized session-level PHQ-8 supervision; and (3) we evaluate EmoTrack on
LongCounsel-8
and
Daic-Woz

[
7
]
, showing a clear gain on the real single-session benchmark and competitive performance on the longitudinal benchmark.

3
Problem Formulation

We study PHQ-8 depression tracking from counseling conversations in a cross-regime setting, covering both
single-session
settings, where only the current transcript is available, and
multi-session
settings, where prior counseling sessions from the same client may provide additional context. Following the terminology introduced above, a
turn
is a single utterance within a counseling conversation, and a
session
is one complete counseling encounter. Intra-session modeling concerns turn-level interaction within the current session, while cross-session modeling concerns dependencies between the current session and prior sessions from the same client. Given the transcript of the current session and, when available, prior-session context, the goal is to predict the client’s PHQ-8 depression severity at the current session.

Clinical target.

Depression severity is measured by the widely used PHQ-8
[
11
]
, which contains
J{=}8
symptoms assessing anhedonia, depressed mood, sleep disturbance, fatigue, appetite disturbance, low self-worth or guilt, concentration difficulty, and psychomotor change. Each symptom is scored on a four-point ordinal scale
y_{j}\in\{0,1,2,3\}
, and the total score is
Y=\sum_{j=1}^{J}y_{j}\in[0,24]
.

Task definition.

Given the current counseling transcript
\mathcal{T}^{(t)}
at session
t
and optional prior-session context
\mathcal{H}^{(t-1)}
, the task is to predict the client’s PHQ-8 severity at the current session. Here,
\mathcal{H}^{(t-1)}
denotes the set of all previous-session counseling transcripts for the same client, i.e.,

\mathcal{H}^{(t-1)}=\{\mathcal{T}^{(1)},\mathcal{T}^{(2)},\ldots,\mathcal{T}^{(t-1)}\},

which is empty when
t=1
. This formulation allows access to all prior sessions from the same client; in EmoTrack, we instantiate this setting with the immediately previous session as the memory source, since it provides the most recent longitudinal context while avoiding the noise and cost of full-history modeling. Formally, the goal is to learn a predictor

f_{\theta}\bigl(\mathcal{T}^{(t)},\mathcal{H}^{(t-1)}\bigr)\rightarrow\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{(t)}\in[0,3]^{J},

(1)

where
\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{(t)}
denotes the predicted PHQ-8 symptom scores, and the total predicted severity is

\hat{Y}^{(t)}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\hat{y}_{j}^{(t)}.

4
Approach: EmoTrack

We propose
EmoTrack
, a transformer-based PHQ-8 predictor for counseling transcripts in single- and multi-session settings. For the current session, EmoTrack builds two client-side token sequences: LLM-extracted clinical-feature tokens and turn-level dialogue embeddings (Section
4.1
). An encoder–decoder backbone integrates both sources and uses learned PHQ-8 symptom queries to read out symptom-specific representations (Section
4.2
), which a prediction head maps to per-symptom severities with an aggregate-plus-symptom-wise objective (Section
4.3
). When history is available, a compact memory module selectively refines these representations with previous-session evidence (Section
4.4
). Figure
1
summarizes the pipeline, with formal Algorithm
1
in Appendix
B
.

4.1
Input Representation

Given the client-side current-session transcript
\mathcal{T}^{(t)}
, EmoTrack constructs two complementary token sequences, one from structured clinical features and the other from client turns; we focus on client turns because they yield the most informative dialogue view for PHQ-8 prediction, while counselor-only and combined views perform worse (Figure
2(c)
). We extract
F{=}23
clinician-style feature scores from
\mathcal{T}^{(t)}
by prompting an LLM with the AIDA feature schema
[
14
]
, organized into three clinically motivated groups (8 PHQ-aligned symptom indicators, 5 linguistic-pattern features, 10 cognitive-distortion markers); each score is rated on a
[0,10]
scale and z-normalized to
\{z_{i}\}_{i=1}^{F}
using training-split statistics. The two sources are complementary, and removing either consistently degrades performance on both benchmarks (Figure
2(b)
).

Clinical-feature branch.

For each normalized feature value
z_{i}
, we construct a token

\mathbf{h}_{i}=\mathrm{MLP}_{\mathrm{score}}(z_{i})+\mathbf{e}^{\mathrm{feat}}_{i}+\mathbf{e}^{\mathrm{group}}_{g(i)}
,
where
\mathrm{MLP}_{\mathrm{score}}(\cdot)
projects the scalar feature value into the model space,
\mathbf{e}^{\mathrm{feat}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a learned embedding identifying the
i
-th feature, and
\mathbf{e}^{\mathrm{group}}_{g(i)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is a learned embedding indicating the clinical group of that feature. Here,
g(i)\in\{1,2,3\}
maps each feature to one of the three groups defined above. Collecting all feature tokens gives the structured clinical representation
\mathbf{H}^{\mathrm{c}}=[\mathbf{h}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{h}_{F}]\in\mathbb{R}^{F\times d}
.

Dialogue branch.

Each client turn is encoded by a frozen sentence encoder, yielding
\mathbf{u}_{n}\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{e}}
for turn
n
, then projected as
\mathbf{s}_{n}=\mathrm{LayerNorm}(W_{\mathrm{proj}}\mathbf{u}_{n}+\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{proj}})
. Counselor turns and empty utterances are removed first; sessions with more than
N_{\max}
remaining client turns are truncated by keeping the first
N_{\max}
turns in chronological order, and each utterance is encoded as a single fixed-size embedding regardless of its length. The default
N_{\max}{=}80
is selected on validation MAE, and shorter or much longer windows degrade performance on both benchmarks (Appendix
E.4
). This gives the client-turn token sequence
\mathbf{S}=[\mathbf{s}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{s}_{N}]\in\mathbb{R}^{N\times d}
.

4.2
Encoder–Decoder Backbone

The encoder–decoder backbone takes the clinical tokens
\mathbf{H}^{\mathrm{c}}
and client-turn tokens
\mathbf{S}
as input and produces one latent representation for each PHQ-8 symptom.

We first concatenate the two token sequences into a single current-session memory,
\mathbf{X}=[\mathbf{H}^{\mathrm{c}};\mathbf{S}]\in\mathbb{R}^{(F+N)\times d}
. A padding mask is applied so that self-attention ignores padded client turns. The concatenated sequence is then processed by Transformer encoder layers. In each encoder layer,
\mathbf{X}
is projected into queries, keys, and values, and self-attention updates each token by attending to all other clinical and client-turn tokens in the current session. This yields a contextualized representation in which each token aggregates relevant evidence from both branches. We use
L_{\mathrm{enc}}{=}2
encoder layers and
L_{\mathrm{dec}}{=}4
decoder layers, selected on validation MAE; this moderate depth is competitive with both shallower and deeper variants on the two benchmarks (Appendix
E.2
). Replacing the EmoTrack predictor with standard regressors over the same features substantially worsens PHQ-8 prediction, supporting the attention-based backbone (Table
3
).

PHQ-symptom query decoder.

EmoTrack uses
J{=}8
learned query vectors, one for each PHQ-8 symptom, collected in
\mathbf{Q}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times d}
. In the decoder, these symptom queries cross-attend to the encoder output
\mathbf{X}
, allowing each symptom to retrieve its own relevant evidence from the current-session tokens. The decoder returns symptom representations
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times d}
, where each row corresponds to one PHQ-8 symptom. Appendix
E.8
compares this design with a mean-pooled encoder head that first compresses the transcript into a single session vector.

4.3
Training Objective

The backbone produces symptom-specific representations
\mathbf{D}=[\mathbf{d}_{1},\ldots,\mathbf{d}_{J}]^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times d}
. A linear-plus-sigmoid head maps each
\mathbf{d}_{j}
to a per-symptom severity
\hat{y}_{j}=3\cdot\sigma(\mathbf{w}_{j}^{\top}\mathbf{d}_{j}+b_{j})\in[0,3]
, and the predicted total PHQ-8 severity is
\hat{Y}=\sum_{j=1}^{J}\hat{y}_{j}
. We supervise these predictions at two granularities: a Huber loss on the aggregate severity,
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{agg}}=\mathrm{Huber}(\hat{Y},\,Y)
with
Y=\sum_{j}y_{j}
, and an auxiliary per-symptom Huber loss
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sym}}=\tfrac{1}{J}\sum_{j}\mathrm{Huber}(\hat{y}_{j},\,y_{j})
that anchors each PHQ-8 dimension to its own evidence, following symptom-resolved depression estimation
[
22
,
2
]
. The full objective combines them with a scalar weight
\lambda_{\mathrm{sym}}\geq 0
,

\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{agg}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{sym}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sym}},

(2)

where
\lambda_{\mathrm{sym}}{=}0.5
is the default selected on validation aggregate MAE and
\lambda_{\mathrm{sym}}{=}0
recovers aggregate-only supervision (Appendix
E.7
). We optimize with AdamW
[
18
]
, using a warmup–decay learning-rate schedule, gradient clipping, and early stopping on validation performance.

4.4
Previous-Session Memory

The components above define the main current-session prediction pathway. To incorporate longitudinal context, EmoTrack refines the symptom representations using client-side information from the immediately previous session, which provides the most relevant recent context while avoiding the noise and cost of full-history modeling. Extending the memory module to selectively use all prior sessions is left for future work.

Let
\{\mathbf{u}^{(t-1)}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
denote the client-turn embeddings from session
t\!-\!1
. A dedicated projection maps them into the model space, yielding previous-session client tokens
\mathbf{V}^{(t-1)}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times d}
.

Retrieval path.

To avoid directly feeding the full previous-session client-side transcript, we compress it into a fixed-size memory. A set of learned slot queries
\mathbf{P}\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times d}
attends to the projected previous-session client tokens and produces a compact memory

\mathbf{M}=\mathrm{LN}\!\bigl(\mathrm{MHA}(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{V}^{(t-1)},\mathbf{V}^{(t-1)})\bigr)\in\mathbb{R}^{S\times d},

(3)

where
\mathrm{MHA}(\cdot)
denotes multi-head attention and
\mathrm{LN}(\cdot)
denotes layer normalization. Each current-session symptom representation in
\mathbf{D}
then attends to this compressed memory, yielding a retrieved history representation
\mathbf{C}\in\mathbb{R}^{J\times d}
. We incorporate it through a gated residual update,

\mathbf{D}\leftarrow\mathbf{D}+\sigma\!\bigl(W_{g}[\mathbf{D};\mathbf{C}]\bigr)\odot W_{o}\mathbf{C},

(4)

so that relevant historical information can be selectively injected for each PHQ-8 symptom. We use
S{=}16
slots by default, selected on validation MAE; the retrieval path together with the summary path provides the strongest configuration, and removing either component degrades performance (Appendix
E.5
,
E.6
).

Summary path.

In addition to slot-based retrieval, we construct a coarse summary of the previous session by mean-pooling the projected previous-session client tokens and mapping the result into the model space. Let
\bar{\mathbf{v}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
denote this summary vector. We broadcast it to all
J
symptoms and apply a second gated residual update,

\mathbf{D}\leftarrow\mathbf{D}+\sigma\!\bigl(W_{g^{\prime}}[\mathbf{D};\bar{\mathbf{v}}]\bigr)\odot\bar{\mathbf{v}}.

(5)

This provides each symptom with a coarse global view of the prior client-side session context. After these refinements, the updated symptom representations are passed to the prediction head described in Section
4.3
.

History dropout.

During training, previous-session memory is randomly dropped with probability
p_{\mathrm{hist}}
. This prevents over-reliance on history and ensures that the model remains effective when no prior session exists. In single-session settings, this module is simply skipped. The default
p_{\mathrm{hist}}{=}0.1
is selected on validation MAE; light dropout regularizes effectively, while
p_{\mathrm{hist}}{=}0
or large values both degrade performance (Appendix
E.3
).

5
Evaluation:
LongCounsel-8

Existing datasets do not support controlled evaluation of longitudinal PHQ-8 tracking in counseling: real counseling resources are scarce, mostly single-session, and rarely provide standardized per-session PHQ supervision
[
7
,
34
]
, while synthetic counseling corpora lack known longitudinal symptom trajectories
[
15
,
10
,
24
]
. We therefore construct
LongCounsel-8
, a synthetic longitudinal counseling corpus in which each client returns across five visits with prescribed hidden PHQ-8 states; Appendix
C
provides the construction and evaluation details.

Data Generation.

A longitudinal counseling corpus must specify a connected generation chain: (1) who the client is, (2) how the client’s overall depression severity changes across visits, (3) how each visit-level PHQ-8 total score is decomposed into eight symptom-level scores, and (4) how these symptom scores are expressed in counseling dialogue.

LongCounsel-8
grounds every step in empirical sources rather than arbitrary prompts: (1) we instantiate client backgrounds, presenting concerns, and session-stage structures from PsychEval-derived cases
[
24
]
; (2) we reconstruct five-visit PHQ-8 total-score trajectories from PSYCHE-D
[
19
]
so that longitudinal change follows empirical depression patterns; (3) we decompose each visit-level total into an eight-dimensional PHQ symptom profile using NHANES DPQ responses
[
4
]
; and (4) we translate each symptom score into private client behaviors that determine
what
symptom evidence appears in dialogue. RealCBT counseling conversations
[
34
]
then calibrate
how
this evidence is expressed, aligning generated turns with real-dialogue statistics over speaker role, counseling stage, utterance length, question form, opening pattern, and lexical overlap.

Evaluation target and corpus.

Because real depression datasets only provide questionnaire-derived labels, we adopt a realistic protocol: models are trained on simulated self-report PHQ-8 scores but evaluated against the latent PHQ-8 state that drives dialogue generation. The latent values appear only as private generation conditions, so models must infer symptom severity from conversational evidence. After validity filtering,
LongCounsel-8
contains 3,599 trajectories (mostly five labeled sessions per trajectory, with some contributing 1–4 labeled sessions due to generation interruptions; 17,122 labeled sessions in total) covering stable, improving, worsening, and fluctuating symptom courses. Audits along trajectory diversity, self-report-to-latent fidelity, and dialogue realism against RealCBT and synthetic counseling baselines
[
34
,
15
,
10
]
support the benchmark validity: final cue-bank pruning improves item exact match from
0.77
to
0.85
and reduces total MAE from
1.69
to
1.21
, while
LongCounsel-8
is closest to RealCBT under both judge-based indistinguishability and psycholinguistic distance.
LongCounsel-8
is designed for controlled benchmark evaluation rather than clinical validation; real longitudinal clinical validation remains future work. Detailed construction prompts, fidelity checks, and similarity analyses are provided in Appendix
C
.

6.1
Experiment Settings

Datasets.

We evaluate on two depression-assessment benchmarks.
For
LongCounsel-8
, we use the cleaned 3,599 principle-guided synthetic multi-session counseling trajectories (17,122 valid self-report-labeled sessions) constructed to cover diverse severity ranges, progression patterns, and degrees of history dependence under standardized PHQ-8 supervision.

Daic-Woz

[
7
]
contains 189 real single-session clinical interviews with self-reported PHQ-8 labels.
Daic-Woz
uses the official participant-level split of 107 train, 35 validation, and 47 test interviews;
LongCounsel-8
is split by profile–trend trajectories into 80% train/validation and 20% test.

Models.

All utterance embeddings are extracted using a frozen embedding model (default: Qwen3-Embedding-8B
[
28
]
), which produces 4,096-dimensional vectors for each utterance.
The 23 AIDA clinical feature scores are extracted by prompting Qwen3.5-35B-A3B
[
28
]
with structured scoring rubrics.
Each extraction run uses a distinct random seed, and all downstream models are trained and evaluated on matched-seed AIDA features to ensure a fair comparison.

Evaluation.

All results are reported as 5-seed means
\pm
standard deviation.
The primary metric is mean absolute error (MAE) on the total PHQ-8 score against ground-truth labels.
We additionally report per-session MAE on
LongCounsel-8
to assess longitudinal tracking ability.

Baselines.

We include state-of-the-art baselines spanning LLM-based clinical feature extraction, LLM questionnaire completion, neural text encoding, and symptom-level depression-score prediction.

•

AIDA

[
14
]
: extracts 23 LLM-rated clinical features covering PHQ-aligned symptoms, linguistic patterns, and cognitive distortions, and fits a ridge regressor.

•

LMIQ

[
30
]
: prompts an LLM to complete structured mental-health questionnaires on behalf of the participant, and trains a random forest on the resulting questionnaire features.

•

Lau et al.

[
13
]
: uses a parameter-efficiently tuned RoBERTa question–response encoder with a sentence-transformer branch for depression-severity prediction.

•

Milintsevich et al.

[
22
]
: predicts PHQ-8 symptom scores with a hierarchical attention model over sentence embeddings, and sums symptom predictions into the total PHQ-8 score.

•

EnsemBERT

[
29
]
: retrieves symptom-relevant utterances and predicts each questionnaire symptom with attention over utterance, symptom-description, and response-choice embeddings. We adapt it from BDI-II prediction to PHQ-8 symptom prediction.

•

Self-report
: (
LongCounsel-8
only) uses the client’s own PHQ-8 self-assessment as the estimate.

For LLM-feature and questionnaire-completion baselines
[
14
,
30
]
, we report two history variants:

Input-source ablation.

Combining LLM-extracted clinical features with turn-level semantic embeddings yields the best PHQ-8 prediction across both benchmarks. As shown in Figure

These results show that semantic dialogue evidence and structured clinical features capture distinct but complementary signals, with dataset-dependent relative importance.

Speaker-view ablation.

Client utterances provide the most informative and least noisy dialogue view for PHQ-8 prediction. As shown in Figure

Appendix C
Construction and Evaluation of
LongCounsel-8

LongCounsel-8
is constructed as a controlled longitudinal counseling corpus for session-level PHQ-8 tracking. The construction is organized as a traceable generation chain: (1) define a stable client profile and session plan, (2) assign a five-visit depression trajectory, (3) decompose each visit-level severity anchor into eight PHQ-8 symptom scores, and (4) render those private symptom states as natural counseling dialogue. Each step is anchored in empirical sources or deterministic validation rules, so the resulting benchmark is not driven by unconstrained prompting.

C.1
Construction

Generation chain.

Each trajectory contains five counseling sessions for one client. For session
t
, the latent symptom state is a symptom-level PHQ-8 vector

\mathbf{y}^{(t)}=[y_{1}^{(t)},\ldots,y_{8}^{(t)}],\qquad y_{j}^{(t)}\in[0,3],

(6)

whose sum gives the visit-level PHQ-8 total score. PsychEval-derived cases
[
24
]
provide stable client backgrounds, presenting concerns, and session-stage structures. PSYCHE-D
[
19
]
provides the longitudinal severity anchors, ensuring that the five-visit changes follow empirical depression trajectories rather than prompt-selected trends. NHANES DPQ responses
[
4
]
then provide observed symptom co-occurrence patterns: for each anchor, we sample a complete PHQ-style response vector and retain the first eight symptoms as the latent PHQ-8 state.

The client-profile step is a deterministic conversion of PsychEval-derived cases rather than a free-form client-invention prompt. It preserves stable identity, biography, presenting concern, topic, growth history, beliefs, and target behaviors, while removing counselor-only administrative material and process instructions. Session themes are rewritten as client-side disclosure beats that specify a concrete event, bodily or contextual triggers, feared meaning, avoidance cost, and desired help. Profiles without valid client information or session plans are removed before trajectory assignment.

For trajectory reconstruction, PSYCHE-D quarterly observations are chained into five anchors: the first visit uses the first available start score, and subsequent visits use the corresponding quarter-end scores. When adjacent quarterly windows disagree on a shared boundary, the two boundary values are averaged and rounded. For each reconstructed PHQ-9 anchor, NHANES DPQ rows with complete symptom responses are grouped by total score. A deterministic hash of the global seed, participant id, visit index, and anchor score selects one matching response vector. The first eight PHQ symptoms form the PHQ-8 vector, and their sum becomes the latent PHQ-8 total. The decomposition uses exact observed NHANES total-score buckets, with all-zero and all-maximum anchors handled as deterministic boundary cases.

Condensed prompt templates.

The following templates are used only after deterministic profile and trajectory construction. The labels name the generation stage; they are not part of the prompt text. Bracketed spans denote instance-specific content instantiated from one client’s profile, one session’s private symptom cues, the public dialogue history, retrieved memories, and the current turn plan.

Behavior-cue generation.

Given a PHQ-8 symptom, an ordinal severity level, and the intended direction when applicable, write short candidate behavioral cues that could naturally appear in a counseling client. Each cue must describe one lived-experience signal in 12–30 words. Do not mention PHQ, questionnaires, scores, symptom names, response options, or measurement language. Do not write first-person questionnaire answers or direct paraphrases of the PHQ symptom. Avoid cues that primarily express another PHQ-8 symptom.

Counselor-turn generation.

You are the counselor. Given the public conversation so far, the session focus, a brief carryover note, relevant past-session memories, the target counseling move for this turn, and the RealCBT-derived turn form, write only the next counselor utterance in English. Follow the specified surface form, continue the session naturally, avoid repetition and premature closure, and do not mention latent states, PHQ, scores, or hidden generation instructions. Return only the visible counselor message in the required structured form.

Client-turn generation.

You are the client described by the private profile. Given the public conversation so far, the current session disclosure beat, private symptom cues, stable background, carryover note, relevant memories, and the RealCBT-derived turn form, write only the next client utterance in English. Express the private cues indirectly through ordinary speech, partial disclosure, hesitation, affect, or concrete examples. Do not name PHQ, scores, questionnaire symptoms, cue lists, or hidden instructions; do not speak like a counselor. Return the visible client message and a short emotion phrase in the required structured form.

Self-report generation.

After the session, answer PHQ-8 as the same client using the private symptom cues, stable profile, carryover note, and completed transcript. For each shuffled symptom, choose exactly one of the provided shuffled option texts. Base the answer on what the client would endorse, not on what was explicitly disclosed. Return only the required structured response, with no rationale, commentary, paraphrased options, or extra text.

From symptom scores to dialogue.

To express symptom-level scores in dialogue without leaking labels, each PHQ-8 symptom score is translated into private client behaviors rather than visible symptom names or numeric values. The behavior-cue prompt generates short, atomic, naturalistic cues for each PHQ-8 symptom–score cell. It emphasizes lived experience over questionnaire wording and excludes PHQ names, option labels, numeric scores, direct symptom paraphrases, first-person self-report phrasing, and strong cross-symptom contamination. These cues cover affect, functioning, cognition, somatic burden, and interaction style. Candidate cues are first checked within their target cell and then pruned under joint composition tests that sample full PHQ-8 profiles and retain cues whose induced self-reports recover the intended symptom and total scores. After pruning, each PHQ-8 symptom–score cell contains ten validated behavioral cues. At generation time, each symptom contributes five cues; fractional scores are represented by sampling from adjacent integer severity levels in proportion to the fractional value. The client simulator receives these cues as private self-beliefs and is explicitly instructed not to reveal questionnaire language or numeric labels.

The conversation generator conditions on the private symptom cues, the PsychEval-derived client profile, memory from earlier visits, and turn-level constraints calibrated from RealCBT
[
34
]
. Counselor prompts receive only public dialogue context, a short carryover note, retrieved memories, the session focus, the current counseling-move target, and a RealCBT-derived turn-form card. Client prompts receive the public context plus private symptom cues, stable background, session beat guidance, carryover, retrieved memories, and the same style of turn-form card. RealCBT provides surface calibration rather than clinical supervision: speaker- and stage-specific statistics determine sentence-count patterns, utterance length, question form, discourse starters, and lexical overlap, while the client profile and symptom cues determine the clinical content. This design prevents generated sessions from becoming symptom checklists and instead embeds PHQ-related evidence into realistic counseling exchanges.

Visible counselor and client turns are generated with Qwen3.5-35B-A3B under moderate sampling, using temperature
0.6
, nucleus mass
0.95
, top-
k
sampling at
20
, and a 512-token cap per turn. Longitudinal run assignment uses seed
42
, and the inference backend uses a fixed server seed. Each run contains five sessions with 20 counselor turns and 20 client turns per session. Generated turns must be English, role-consistent, non-empty, non-repetitive, free of questionnaire or score leakage, and not prematurely closing; violations trigger targeted repair attempts.

Latent target and self-report supervision.

The target of depression tracking is the underlying latent symptom state, which PHQ questionnaires can only approximate through self-report. In
LongCounsel-8
, this latent PHQ-8 state is known by construction and therefore serves as the controlled evaluation target. Ideally, models would be trained and tested on this latent state. Real depression datasets, however, cannot observe a person’s true latent depression level and instead provide questionnaire-derived labels. We therefore adopt a realistic and stricter setting: models are trained on simulated self-report PHQ-8 scores but evaluated against the latent PHQ-8 state that drives dialogue generation. The latent values are used only as private generation conditions and never appear in the visible dialogue, so models must infer symptom severity from conversational evidence.

After each completed session, the client simulator privately completes PHQ-8 using the same behavior cues, stable profile, carryover note, and transcript. Symptom and option order are deterministically shuffled for each pass, and the prompt requires exact option texts without rationale or extra prose. We generate five deterministic self-report passes and average them symptomwise. Invalid structure, missing symptoms, or paraphrased options trigger a repair prompt; sessions without a valid self-report are excluded from split construction. Session summaries and self-report responses use deterministic decoding, with 1024 tokens allowed for summaries and 1024–1536 tokens for self-report responses depending on the generation setting.

Dataset statistics and use.

The complete corpus contains 3,690 five-session client trajectories and 18,450 labeled counseling sessions. A trajectory-level audit assigns generated clients to fluctuating, stable, improving, and worsening patterns, with all four classes represented. All model experiments use trajectory-level partitions rather than individual-session splits, preventing sessions from the same client from appearing in more than one partition.

Filtering is applied before split construction: trajectory reconstruction keeps strict five-visit PSYCHE-D participants, NHANES decomposition uses only complete valid symptom-response rows, the cue bank is pruned to a uniform ten cues per symptom–score cell, and generated runs must contain a valid transcript, latent target, and simulated self-report. The released split manifest is generated over the resulting 3,599 usable trajectories: 2,519 train, 360 validation, and 720 test. Split assignment is produced by sorting run ids, applying a seed-42 random shuffle within the
LongCounsel-8
parent group, and assigning the 0.7/0.1/0.2 train/validation/test ratios at the trajectory level. The manifest stores each run id with its split, so the exact partition can be recovered without relying on filename order.

LongCounsel-8
is intended as a controlled evaluation resource, not a substitute for clinical validation. Its role is to expose longitudinal PHQ-8 tracking behavior under known symptom trajectories, partial disclosure, and cross-session continuity. We therefore pair experiments on
LongCounsel-8
with evaluation on
Daic-Woz

[
7
]
, which provides a real single-session anchor for text-based depression assessment.

Appendix F
Limitations

Real longitudinal clinical validation.

Although
LongCounsel-8
enables controlled evaluation of longitudinal PHQ-8 tracking and
Daic-Woz
provides a real-data anchor, real multi-session counseling datasets with standardized session-level PHQ supervision remain limited. Therefore, our results should be interpreted as benchmark evidence for robust transcript-based depression tracking, rather than as clinical validation. We plan to extend the evaluation to real longitudinal patient data in collaboration with clinical partners.

Open-source upstream models.

EmoTrack uses open-source upstream models for utterance embedding and clinical-feature extraction. This design improves accessibility and reproducibility, but may not reflect the strongest performance attainable with frontier closed-source models. Future work can evaluate whether more capable proprietary models improve clinical-feature quality, calibration, and downstream PHQ-8 prediction.

PHQ-8 prediction versus clinical decision support.

Our evaluation focuses on PHQ-8 score prediction. PHQ-8 is a widely used and standardized depression-severity measure, making it an appropriate target for controlled comparison; however, predicting PHQ-8 scores does not by itself cover all requirements of clinical decision support. Practical deployment would also require calibration, uncertainty estimation, threshold-based triage analysis, false-negative analysis, privacy safeguards, and human-in-the-loop review. EmoTrack should therefore be viewed as a depression-tracking component rather than an autonomous diagnostic or intervention system.
