Correlation between fecal eosinophil cationic protein and cow’s milk protein allergy in extremely preterm infants and its value in auxiliary diagnosis
Authors: Hongjuan Jin, Yuandan Chen, Ying Lin, Yufang Lin, Deyi Zhuang, Xiaoyan Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To explore the correlation between fecal Eosinophil Cationic Protein (ECP) and Cow’s Milk Protein Allergy (CMPA) in extremely preterm infants, and to evaluate its value in auxiliary diagnosis. This prospective controlled study enrolled 60 extremely preterm infants (< 32 weeks gestational age), comprising 30 with confirmed CMPA and 30 matched controls. Fecal ECP levels and routine blood tests were monitored longitudinally. Statistical analyses included one-way repeated-measures ANOVA and independent samples t-test. Fecal ECP levels in the experimental group were significantly elevated during allergic manifestation (7.08 ± 1.22 µg/g) compared with all other timepoints (all P < 0.001). No significant differences in ECP levels were observed among non-allergic timepoints (all P > 0.05). The control group maintained stable ECP levels throughout all observed periods (P > 0.05). The mean fecal ECP level during non-allergic periods in the experimental group (3.18 ± 1.01 µg/g) showed no significant difference from the control group (P = 0.262). In contrast, ECP levels during active allergy were significantly higher than those in the control group (P < 0.001). Fecal ECP level is significantly positively correlated with CMPA onset in extremely preterm infants, which provides a clinical basis for non-invasive auxiliary diagnosis of CMPA in extremely preterm infants.

Introduction

Extremely preterm infants (gestational age < 32 weeks at birth) are prone to Cow’s Milk Protein Allergy (CMPA) through multiple pathways due to immature development of the immune system and intestinal mucosal barrier1–3. Clinically, the clinical manifestations of CMPA in extremely preterm infants are non-specific, mainly including abdominal distension, vomiting, gastric retention, hematochezia, and poor weight gain, which are easily confused with neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), lactose intolerance, and other diseases3. Currently, the diagnosis relies on “exclusion diagnosis + food avoidance challenge test”, which may be accompanied by unnecessary fasting, invasive blood sampling, imaging examinations, and even abuse of antibiotics, increasing the risk of injury to infants3. If allergic factors are not intervened in a timely manner, it may lead to secondary long-term allergic diseases, growth retardation, and metabolic syndrome in adulthood4. Therefore, there is an urgent need to find non-invasive and specific auxiliary diagnostic indicators.

Eosinophil Cationic Protein (ECP) is a toxic protein released by activated eosinophils (EOS) after degranulation, and it is an important marker of EOS-mediated inflammatory response5. While other granule proteins like eosinophil-derived neurotoxin (EDN) serve as valid indicators of eosinophil activation, ECP was selected as the sole biomarker in this study due to its potent cytotoxic effects that directly disrupt epithelial tight junctions—closely mirroring the mucosal damage seen in infantile intestinal allergies—and its established precedent in pediatric gastrointestinal allergy monitoring6. In recent years, fecal ECP has been confirmed as a predictive indicator for the active phase of inflammatory bowel disease6, and studies have suggested that it may have diagnostic value in intestinal allergic diseases7. This study prospectively detected fecal ECP levels in extremely preterm infants with CMPA before allergy, at the time of allergy, after remission, and in normal extremely preterm infants, to explore its correlation with CMPA and its potential as an indicator of intestinal allergic inflammation to support clinical evaluation.

Materials and methods

Study subjects

Extremely preterm infants admitted to the Department of Neonatology of Xiamen Children’s Hospital from May 1, 2022 to April 30, 2024 were enrolled. Inclusion criteria: ① Gestational age < 32 weeks at birth; ② Postnatal age < 14 days at admission. Exclusion criteria (excluded if any of the following is met): ① Complicated with neonatal necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), tuberculosis infection, ulcerative colitis, congenital intestinal malformations (intestinal stenosis, intestinal atresia), congenital immunodeficiency, or genetic metabolic diseases; ② Mothers with immune system diseases such as ulcerative colitis, Crohn’s disease, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), or rheumatoid arthritis (RA).

The infants were divided into two groups according to whether CMPA was diagnosed: Experimental group: 30 cases, confirmed CMPA. The diagnosis was based on the 2022 edition of Guidelines for Diagnosis and Nutritional Intervention of Mild to Moderate Non-IgE-Mediated Cow’s Milk Protein Allergy in Chinese Infants8: presence of symptoms such as abdominal distension, vomiting, gastric retention, hematochezia, and poor weight gain, which were relieved after cow’s milk protein avoidance implemented primarily via dietary transition to an extensively hydrolyzed formula (eHF), and other diseases such as infection, necrotizing enterocolitis, digestive system malformations, and lactose intolerance were excluded. Notably, although the reference guidelines recommend a formal oral food challenge (OFC) to confirm the diagnosis, a re-challenge was not performed in this study due to safety and ethical constraints associated with the extreme fragility of this preterm cohort; instead, diagnosis relied strictly on symptom resolution upon elimination and comprehensive differential exclusion. Control group: 30 cases, extremely preterm infants hospitalized during the same period with no allergic symptoms. Regarding participant selection, strict restrictions were applied based on our inclusion criteria, limiting enrollment to infants with a gestational age of less than 32 weeks and an admission age of less than 14 days to minimize confounding clinical variables. To ensure baseline comparability, controls were selected from the same neonatal unit during the identical study period and matched to the experimental group based on a similar gestational age range. Because these entry restrictions achieved robustly balanced baseline characteristics across groups, no further post-hoc statistical adjustments were required in the final analysis.

Study methods

Sample collection and detection

Fecal sample collection: For all subjects, starting from 2 weeks after birth (or from 28 weeks of gestational age for infants with gestational age < 26 weeks at birth), fecal samples (volume ≥5 g) were collected using sterile specimen bottles and immediately stored in a −80℃ refrigerator. For the experimental group, additional fecal samples were collected on Day 1 (time of allergy), Day 8, and Day 15 of the food avoidance test (during which infants received an eHF-based diet); for the control group, samples were collected until 6 weeks after birth (samples from discharged infants were collected through outpatient follow-up).

Pretreatment of fecal samples: Before detection, fecal samples were thawed. 1 g of feces was weighed and added to 1mL of phosphate-buffered saline, thoroughly mixed, and allowed to stand for 10 min, then centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 30 min, and the supernatant was reserved for use.

Fecal ECP detection: Fecal ECP detection: Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA) was used, and a commercial human eosinophil cationic protein ELISA kit (Wuhan MSK Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China; Catalog No. KT-98952) was used to detect the concentration of fecal ECP in the supernatant. The detection range of the kit was 4ng/mL – 220 ng/mL. The intra-assay coefficient of variation (CV) ranged from 4.69% to 7.78% (mean 6.6%); inter-assay CV was not evaluated as no inter-plate replicates of the same sample were performed. For sample processing and dilution, stool samples were first homogenized in PBS at 10% (w/v) (1 g feces + 9 mL PBS), yielding a 10-fold dilution. The resulting supernatant was then diluted another 5-fold during the ELISA procedure (40 µL diluent + 10 µL sample), resulting in a total sample dilution factor of 50. Raw concentration readings obtained from the microplate reader in ng/mL were converted to final wet-weight fecal values (µg/g) using the following formula: Final concentration (µg/g) = ELISA reading (ng/mL) × 50/1000. All assay operations strictly followed the manufacturer’s instructions. To ensure methodological objectivity, the laboratory technician who performed the ELISA was completely blinded to the clinical group allocation. All samples were recoded with unique numeric identifiers before analysis, and the assignment key code was not revealed until after the final dataset was completely generated.

Sample size determination

The sample size for this study was determined based on a formal sample size calculation derived from a preliminary pilot study. In the pilot phase, fecal samples from 14 infants clinically diagnosed with cow’s milk protein allergy (CMPA) and 14 matched non-CMPA controls were analyzed. The pilot data revealed a significant difference in fecal eosinophil cationic protein (ECP) levels between the two groups (t = 6.08, P < 0.001). Based on these pilot findings, a sample size estimation for a two-independent-sample means design was performed using PASS 15.0 software (NCSS, LLC, Kaysville, Utah, USA). Setting the statistical significance level (alpha) at 0.001 and the statistical power (1-beta) at 0.90, the calculation indicated that a minimum of 23 infants per group was required to achieve sufficient statistical power. To account for an anticipated 20% sample attrition or dropout rate during the longitudinal stool collection process, the final sample size was expanded to 30 infants per group, resulting in a total enrollment of 60 extremely preterm infants.

Statistical methods

SPSS 28.0 statistical software was used for data analysis, following the Intent-to-Treat (ITT) principle. Measurement data were expressed as “mean ± standard deviation (x ± s)”, and categorical data were expressed as “cases (%)”. Independent samples t-test was used for comparison of measurement data between the two groups, and Fisher’s exact test was used for comparison of categorical data; one-way repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for comparison of fecal ECP levels at different time points, followed by Bonferroni post-hoc tests for multiple pairwise comparisons. For peripheral blood EOS%, which did not follow a normal distribution, the non-parametric Friedman test was utilized for longitudinal comparisons. A P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 60 extremely preterm infants were evaluated in this study. There were no statistically significant differences in gender, gestational age at birth, or feeding method (breastfeeding, mixed feeding, preterm formula) between the two groups (all P > 0.05), indicating that the baseline data were balanced and comparable (Table 1).

Fecal ECP levels at different time points in the control group

Fecal ECP levels in the control group at 2 weeks, 4 weeks, and 6 weeks after birth were (3.12 ± 0.48) µg/g, (3.16 ± 0.46) µg/g, and (3.08 ± 0.48) µg/g, respectively. One-way repeated-measures ANOVA showed no statistically significant differences among different time points (2 weeks vs. 4 weeks after birth: P = 0.732; 2 weeks vs. 6 weeks after birth: P = 0.767; 4 weeks vs. 6 weeks after birth: P = 0.524), indicating that fecal ECP levels in normal extremely preterm infants remained stable from 2 to 6 weeks after birth (Table 3).

Discussion

Through a prospective controlled design, this study focused on the high-risk group of “extremely preterm infants with gestational age < 32 weeks at birth” for the first time, and systematically analyzed the correlation between fecal ECP and CMPA as well as its auxiliary diagnostic value. The results showed that fecal ECP increased significantly during CMPA allergy in extremely preterm infants, and its level at non-allergic time points was consistent with that in normal extremely preterm infants. In addition, the detection is non-invasive and repeatable, indicating that fecal ECP has clinical value as an auxiliary diagnostic indicator.

Correlation mechanism between fecal ECP and CMPA in extremely preterm infants

CMPA in extremely preterm infants is mainly dominated by non-IgE-mediated immune response, and EOS is the core effector cell of this response9,10. EOS are mainly distributed in the intestinal mucosal layer (accounting for more than 90% of total EOS in the body)11. When cow’s milk protein acts as an allergen to stimulate the intestinal mucosa, EOS are activated and degranulated, releasing ECP, which damages the tight junctions of intestinal epithelium (such as occludin protein) and increases intestinal permeability12. This is consistent with the clinical manifestation of hematochezia in 60% (18/30) of CMPA infants in this study. In this study, the fecal ECP level of the experimental group at the time of allergy (7.08 ± 1.22) µg/g was significantly higher than that at non-allergic time points and in the control group, and fecal ECP gradually returned to the normal level after food avoidance. This is consistent with the conclusion of Saarinen KM et al.7 that “fecal ECP increases in patients with positive food challenge test”, confirming that fecal ECP can dynamically reflect the inflammatory state related to intestinal allergy and has a close correlation with CMPA.

Clinical advantages and potential of fecal ECP as a biomarker

The current diagnosis of CMPA in extremely preterm infants faces many difficulties: (1) Non-specific clinical manifestations, which are easily confused with necrotizing enterocolitis and lactose intolerance13; (2) Limitations of traditional detection methods: Serum-specific IgE detection is only applicable to IgE-mediated allergy, and has a low diagnostic rate for non-IgE-mediated CMPA in extremely preterm infants14; Endoscopic examination can observe mucosal EOS infiltration, but it is an invasive procedure and not suitable for extremely preterm infants15; Peripheral blood EOS% is easily interfered by factors such as infection and bronchopulmonary dysplasia, and this study also confirmed that it is not related to the allergic state of CMPA (P = 0.836); Compared with fecal calprotectin (an active protein released after neutrophil activation), which is interfered by intestinal infection and necrotizing enterocolitis, its clinical application is limited16.

In contrast, fecal ECP has significant advantages: (1) Potential to differentiate allergic states from healthy baselines: This study showed that the difference in fecal ECP level at the time of allergy between the experimental group and the control group was statistically significant (P < 0.001), while there was no difference between non-allergic time points and the control group (P = 0.262). However, because this study excluded other inflammatory neonatal gastrointestinal conditions (such as NEC and infectious colitis), the true diagnostic specificity of fecal ECP against clinical mimics remains to be fully verified. Our current data support its role in association and dynamic monitoring rather than providing a validated diagnostic threshold; (2) Non-invasiveness and convenience: Fecal sample collection does not require blood sampling and can be obtained repeatedly, which is especially suitable for extremely preterm infants with poor vascular conditions and low tolerance; (3) Value in dynamic monitoring: Fecal ECP can reflect the remission process of allergy (gradually decreasing on Day 8 and Day 15 of avoidance), which can not only assist in diagnosis but also evaluate the efficacy of food avoidance treatment using extensively hydrolyzed formula (eHF). By removing intact protein allergens, eHF successfully halts eosinophil activation and mucosal degranulation, directly contributing to the downward trend of fecal ECP as the intestinal barrier repairs. This establishes fecal ECP as a highly sensitive tool meeting the clinical needs of “diagnosis-treatment-monitoring” throughout the process.

Study limitations and prospects

Limitations of this study: (1) Small sample size (30 cases in each group), which may affect the extrapolation of conclusions; (2) The diagnostic threshold of fecal ECP was not explored, and further expansion of the sample size is needed to establish a reference range; (3) The diagnostic efficacy of fecal ECP was not compared with other intestinal inflammatory markers, so its advantages and disadvantages cannot be clarified. (4) The lack of a formal oral food challenge (OFC). While an OFC is the gold standard for confirming non-IgE-mediated CMPA, re-introducing the allergen was omitted in our cohort due to the high clinical risk of inducing severe mucosal injury or feeding setbacks in extremely preterm infants in the NICU. Relying solely on elimination and clinical exclusion may introduce diagnostic bias or result in an over-diagnosis of CMPA, which represents a key limitation of our clinical framework.

Future studies can focus on the following directions: (1) Enroll extremely preterm infants from multiple centers with a larger sample size to establish the diagnostic threshold and ROC curve of fecal ECP for CMPA; (2) Compare the diagnostic value of fecal ECP with other biomarkers (such as fecal calprotectin, serum IL-5); (3) Explore the application of fecal ECP in other food allergies (such as soy protein allergy) to further expand its clinical application scope.

Additionally, our control group consisted solely of extremely preterm infants without gastrointestinal allergic symptoms. While this design allowed us to successfully map baseline fecal ECP fluctuations and establish a significant difference during active CMPA episodes, it restricts our ability to evaluate the specific performance of fecal ECP in differential diagnosis. In clinical practice, CMPA can easily be confused with other neonatal gastrointestinal pathologies, such as necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC), infectious enteritis, or feeding intolerance, which also manifest with overlapping signs like hematochezia and abdominal distension. Because eosinophil activation can occur during various types of mucosal injury, comparing ECP levels against a disease-control group (e.g., infants with NEC) would provide far greater diagnostic specificity. This lack of a disease-control cohort represents a major limitation of our current work. Finally, this study is a single-center study with a relatively small sample size. In the future, multi-center, large-sample prospective studies incorporating diverse disease-control cohorts are still needed to further investigate the clinical application and differential diagnostic value of fecal ECP.
