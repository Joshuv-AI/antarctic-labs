Effect of firings on the lithium disilicate and zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate dental ceramics for CAD-CAM procedures
Authors: Pedro Giorgetti Montagner, Antonio Marcos Montagner, Mário Alexandre Coelho Sinhoreti, Rafael Leonardo Xediek Consani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe aim of this study was to verify the effect of multiple firing cycles on lithium disilicate and zirconia reinforced lithium silicate ceramics.Materials and MethodsSixty discs measuring 12 mm x 1.2 mm made with IPS E.max CAD (n=30) or Vita Suprinity (n=30) ceramic systems were divided into 3 subgroups submitted to 2, 3 or 5 firing cycles (C2, C3, and C5, respectively). Surface roughness, gloss, biaxial flexural strength, and Vickers hardness were evaluated, as well as scanning electron microscopy (SEM) analysis. Data was submitted to two-way ANOVA and Tukey's test with a 5% significance level.ResultsFor surface roughness, Suprinity showed a significant decrease at C5 (p<0.05). For gloss, there was no statistical interaction and C5 showed higher gloss value for both ceramics (p>0.05). For flexural strength, Suprinity showed significant increase (p<0.05) and IPS E.max CAD decreased significantly at C5 (p<0.05). For Vickers hardness, there was no statistical interaction and hardness decreased at C5 for both ceramics (p>0.05). IPS E.max CAD showed no morphological difference while Suprinity showed micro irregularities for C5 in the SEM analysis.ConclusionFiring cycles promoted better effect on roughness and biaxial flexural strength parameters for the Suprinity compared with IPS E.max CAD. Both ceramics showed negative effects on the morphological aspect of the crystalline matrix and similar gloss or hardness values.

Introduction

With the enhancement of technological systems related to computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD-CAM), indirect restorations have been made using these materials to achieve better aesthetic results. CAD-CAM ceramics have made easier to make indirect restorations, promoting greater patient's satisfaction. However, considering that aesthetic or functional adjustments may be required after fabrication, the restoration may be subjected to several additional firings (1).

The clinical selection of ceramic systems for making indirect restorations has been based on the mechanical and optical properties of the materials (2). Lithium disilicate has emerged as a suitable material for this type of dental application, with several studies demonstrating excellent success rates associated with better mechanical and aesthetic properties. This ceramic material has been used by prosthodontics because it has satisfactory mechanical properties and dimensional changes over the long term. However, additional aesthetic or functional adjustments may be required after fabrication, and subsequently subjected to repeated firings during the grinding process with varying effects on the chemical stability of the ceramic (3).

For CAD-CAM manufacturing techniques, lithium disilicate and lithium silicate reinforced with zirconium dioxide are generally available in prefabricated, partially crystallized blocks requiring milling and/or machining and subsequent crystallization to finish the restoration (4). These materials have similar clinical indications and are all used across a broad spectrum, including anterior and posterior full crown restorations, implant substructures, indirect veneers, inlays, and onlays.

New generations of block glass ceramics were developed after the technology was used in restorative dentistry, involving computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD-CAM). Lithium disilicate or lithium silicate glass ceramics were developed to meet the aesthetic requirements of restorations in anterior teeth. Thus, block making, milling and heat treatment are necessary steps for ceramics to be used in the fabrication of indirect restorations. Occlusal adjustment, color refinement and glaze application are some of the procedures required to mimic the crown anatomy and translucency of natural teeth, requiring additional firing cycles before cementing the ceramic restoration (5, 6).

Each ceramic material has a different manufacturing method and manufacturers generally recommend a single firing cycle with extrinsic characterization before finalizing the restoration. However, it has been claimed that ceramic restorations can be subjected to two or different firing cycles before final cementation (7).

On the other hand, repeated glaze firing cycles can compromise the long-term clinical success of lithium disilicate glass ceramic restorations due to chemical degradation (1). The manufacturers that produce these ceramic types claim that only one cycle for extrinsic characterization is necessary before finalizing the restoration. However, dental laboratory technicians often use additional cycles to make color corrections, pigmentation and layering after adjustments. The disadvantage claimed was that multiple firing cycles can concentrate stress on the ceramic surface due to phase transformation that occur in the crystals (8).

Changes in the crystal lattice structure can occur depending on the heat treatment used in the crystallization, pigmentation and glaze application phases. Additional crystallization during firing procedures in the manufacture of glass ceramics results in changed thermal behaviors and formation of crystalline phases (9).

Clinical survival of the ceramic restoration depends on the thermal characteristics of the material as demonstrated by the coefficient of thermal expansion and contraction. Thermal processing in the laboratory to apply the glaze may be associated with structural and mechanical changes that reduce the flexural strength of the ceramic during firing for layering. Therefore, these thermal procedures should be considered when predicting the long-term success of ceramic restorations (10, 11).

Given these considerations, it would be timely and current to evaluate the effect of the number of firing cycles on the roughness, gloss, biaxial flexural strength and Vickers hardness of dental glass ceramics for CAD-CAM. The hypothesis of the study was that increasing the number of firing cycles would not affect the mechanical and morphological properties of the ceramic materials.

Materials and Methods

Sample preparation

Thirty discs of lithium disilicate ceramics (IPS E.max CAD; Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein - shade A2 HT) and thirty discs of zirconia reinforced lithium silicate (Suprinity; Vita Zahnfabrik, Bad Sackingen, Germany - shade 2M2 HT) were prepared (Table 1).

The partially crystallized milling CAD/CAM blocks (18 x 14 x 12 mm) were machined into a cylindrical shape using UA121 grinding machine (Ferdimat, Sao Jose dos Campos, SP, Brazil) with a diamond grinding wheel with 12 mm diameter. The cylinders were subsequently cut into discs approximately 12 mm in diameter and 1.35 mm thick in a precision metallographic cutting machine (Isomet 1000, Buehler, Lake Bluff, Illinois, USA) with 0.48 mm thick diamond blade (ERIOS Equipments, Eireli EPP, Sao Paulo, SP, Brazil) at low rotation speed and water cooling. The samples were polished with #200, 400 and 800 grit water sandpaper (Aropol 2V Polisher, Arotec, Sao Paulo, SP, Brazil) until they reached dimensions of 12 mm in diameter by 1.2 mm in thickness, checked with digital caliper (Mitutoyo, Jundiai, SP, Brazil) with 0.01 mm accuracy. After machining and preparation, the samples were washed under running water, rinsed in deionized water for three five-minute periods, ultrasonically washed with 70% isopropyl alcohol (Ultrassonica Cristofoli Biosafety, Campo Mourao, PR, Brazil) and stored dry at room temperature for 48 h.

Firing protocols

According to manufacturers` recommendations, at least two firings are necessary for glass-ceramics materials, being one for crystallization and another for pigments and glaze. The samples were divided into subgroups (n=10) according to the number of firing cycles for each type of ceramic material (n=30): C2 (2 firing cycles recommended by the manufacturers, C3 (3 firing cycles), and C5 (5 firing cycles) as showed in the Figure 1. The sample size was calculated based on the literature (5, 9, 12).

The samples were subjected to thermal crystallization cycles in a furnace (Programat EP3010, Ivoclar Vivadent, Schaan, Liechtenstein) according to the parameters recommended by the ceramic manufacturers (Figure 2).

The crystallization firing and the glaze application firing were carried out according to the parameters recommended by the manufacturers (Figure 3).

In the groups with additional firings, the glaze was applied after the last cycle. A thin layer of paste with a creamy consistency resulting from mixing equal proportions of ceramic powder and liquid from each manufacturer was applied with micro brush to standardize the consistency and dilution of the material in all the samples.

Surface roughness

The surface roughness was measured with a Roughness meter (Surfcorder SE1700; Tokyo, Japan) after the proposed treatment related to the number of firings of each ceramic material. Three measurements were taken in same regions of all samples, with reading length of 3 mm and speed of 0.2 mm/s. The analysis was carried out according to ISO 4287:1997 standards (13), with a Gaussian filter and a cut-off wavelength value of 0.8 mm. The arithmetic means of the values obtained for each sample was taken as the group value.

Gloss was measured with Glossmeter (Zehntner GmbH, Testing Instruments, Swiss) with an angle of incidence of 60º to assess the gloss value. The ISO 2813:2014 specification for ceramic materials (14) was followed for measurement and the gloss unit (GU).

Biaxial flexural strength test was performed in universal testing machine (Instron, Model 4411, Sao Jose dos Pinhais, PR, Brazil) following ISO 6872:2015 (15). Each sample was placed over three steel balls (3.2 mm in diameter) positioned to 120º away from the balls circle with diameter of 10 mm. The load was applied by a circular flat piston (1.5 mm in diameter) at 1 mm/min until failure. Ten samples from each group were tested. The biaxial flexural strength of the samples was calculated according to equation:

S=−0.2387P(X−Y)/d2

In which: S= maximum tensile stress (MPa), P= load applied to cause fracture (N), and d=sample thickness at the fracture origin (mm); X and Y = parameters related to the elastic properties of the material (Poisson coefficient and elastic modulus), being X = 0.25 and Y = 64 GPa, according to previous studies (5, 6, 12).

Samples were subjected to the Vickers hardness test using a durometer (HMV-2; Shimadzu, Tokyo, Japan) with a diamond tip and load of 4.9 N for 5s, promoting indentation verified under 40x magnification. The procedure was repeated in three different areas of the sample and the average calculated. The values were obtained based on the calculation involving the factors applied force, diagonal length of the indentation, and opposite angles of the indentation.

Morphological analysis

Micro-structural morphological analysis was carried out using SEM (LEO 438 VPi; Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) in two samples (n=2) randomly chosen per group. The samples were evaluated after fracture (6, 7, 16) placed over metal stubs, the fractured surface coated with gold in a vacuum sputtering device, and analyzed with a secondary electron detector (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany) at 15.0 kV, reading distance of 8mm, and 1600x magnification.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed by two-way ANOVA test using material (Lithium disilicate and Zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate) and number of firings (C2, C3, and C5) as factors. Pos-hoc multiple comparisons were performed using Tukey's test (α=0.05). The SANEST software program (SANEST - Statistical Analysis System, Brazil) was used for this purpose.

Results

Surface roughness results are shown in Table 2. When comparing multiple cycles, there was statistically significant difference for roughness value only for Suprinity, being higher in cycle C2 and lower in C5, while C3 showed an intermediate value (p<0.05). For IPS E.max CAD, there was no change in the roughness values when multiple firing cycles were applied. When comparing ceramics, there was a statistical difference in cycles C2 and C3 (p<0.05) with the highest value for Suprinity, while the values were similar between ceramics in the cycle C5 (p>0.05).

Gloss results are shown in Table 3. There was no interaction or statistical difference when comparing firing cycles and ceramics. The pooled mean values were statistically evaluated for the isolated factors and difference was shown between ceramic types and number of firing cycles. When comparing the mean values of isolated factors, there was statistical difference between the ceramics, with higher value for IPS E.max CAD (p<0.05). When comparing the mean values of isolated factors between firing cycles, the C5 cycle showed the highest value, C2 lowest and C3 intermediate value, showing that gloss increased significantly (p<0.05) according to the number of firing cycles, regardless of the ceramic system.

Flexural strength results are shown in Table 4. IPS E.max CAD ceramic showed statistical similarity between cycles, while Suprinity showed highest value in C5, lowest in C2 and intermediate in C3. When the number of firings increased, IPS E.max CAD showed no change and Suprinity showed a change in flexural strength. When comparing ceramics, there was statistical similarity in cycle C2 (p>0.05), and difference between the cycles C3 and C5 (p<0.05), with higher value for Suprinity.

Hardness results are shown in Table 5. There was no interaction or statistical difference when comparing firing cycles and ceramics. The pool mean values for the individual factors were evaluated. When comparing the mean values of the isolated factors, there was statistical difference between ceramics, with higher value for Suprinity. When comparing the mean values of isolated factors between cycles, C2 showed highest value, C5 lowest and C3 intermediate value. Vickers hardness decreased significantly (p<0.05) according to the number of firing cycles, regardless of the ceramic system.

Images from SEM of IPS E.max CAD and Suprinity ceramics illustrating the characteristics of the crystals in cycles C2, C3, and C5 are shown in Figure 4. The morphological aspect of the crystalline matrix of IPS E.max CAD ceramic showed in the cycles C2 (Figure A), C3 (Figure B), and C5 (Figure C) are similar, showing formation of micro irregularities in all the firing cycles. The crystalline matrix morphological aspect of Suprinity ceramic is illustrated in the Figure 4. Cycle C2 (Figure D) shows crystalline matrix with homogeneous relief, cycle C3 (Figure E) shows large amount of amorphous material, probably glaze over the crystalline matrix, and cycle C5 (Figure F) shows the same morphological aspect found in the cycle C2 (Figure D); however, with depressions and micro cracks (arrow).

Discussion

Current ceramics have enhanced significantly compared to first generation, meeting the aesthetic and mechanical demands of clinical dental restoration practices. When making dental restorations, ceramics may need adjustments that require additional firing to make final corrections. This study evaluated the effect of firing cycles on the roughness, gloss, biaxial flexural strength, and Vickers hardness of the commercial ceramics IPS E.max CAD and Suprinity, both materials with similar clinical indications.

Roughness and flexural strength results showed statistical difference between firing cycles only for Vita Suprinity. However, both ceramic materials showed similar gloss values between firing cycles. There was also statistical similarity in the flexural strength values between cycles only for the IPS E.max CAD and the hardness was similar between firing cycles in both ceramics. Therefore, the study hypothesis that firing cycles would promote similar roughness, gloss, flexural strength or surface hardness values between IPS E.max CAD and Vita Suprinity ceramics was not confirmed.

A previous study showed that ceramic types, multiple cycles (2 or 4 firings), and the interaction between material and number of firing cycles differently affected the roughness level of CAD-CAM ceramics (7). This previous result corroborates with the current study only for Vita Suprinity.

The different roughness values (Table 2) between the ceramics subjected to the effect of multiple firings may be due to composition of the materials. In addition, previous study showed that additional firings improve the level of glaze infiltration into the irregularities of the ceramic, increasing the smoothness of the material surface. However, the effect of repeated firings on the chemical stability of ceramics for CAD-CAM was different and no trend was observed in the elution of different ions in the material/firing association before thermal cycling. However, this procedure increased the number of eluted ions while repeated firings decreased the roughness of lithium disilicate glass ceramics (1).

It has been claimed that roughness values are different between ceramics, probably caused by the type of characterization and firing cycles. However, single-stage characterization showed roughness stability after repeated firings (8). In addition, the number of firing cycles increased significantly the roughness and the formation of crystalline phases, being the changes different and also related to the ceramic types (9).

The ceramic surface of dental prosthetic restorations must provide sufficient level of roughness to maintain aesthetics, color stability, favorable biological condition, longevity in use, and patient's satisfaction. Another interesting fact was that there is insignificant difference in roughness between ceramics before and after thermal cycling. However, significant increase in roughness can also occur after the thermal procedure (4). In addition, ceramic restorations with rougher surface can accumulate different biofilm levels and facilitate the marginal staining.

When comparing the mean values of isolated gloss factor (Table 3) there was statistical difference between the ceramics with highest value for IPS E.max CAD with C5 showing highest gloss value. Another important factor that could be related to the gloss value is the color of the ceramics. It has been claimed that the color value of the ceramic is also affected by the firing cycles, as well as by the shade of the veneering ceramic. Therefore, increasing the firing number in ceramic of different shades resulted in light, green or yellow colors. However, the color changes that occurred were considered clinically acceptable (10).

In addition, multiple firings can significantly alter the color and translucency of CAD-CAM ceramics (6), and the ceramic material type, since the firing regime had statistically significant effect on the gloss of fully crystallized CAD-CAM ceramics (17).

Gloss value can be evaluated in lithium disilicate and lithium silicate ceramics reinforced with zirconium dioxide. However, the clinical effect of gloss was more significant in lithium disilicate ceramics, with higher value. Thus, lower glass content and higher proportion of metal oxides may be responsible for the instability of ceramic color and gloss. Therefore, single firing would be enough to alter the gloss of ceramics, while additional firings would cause greater change in color (18). In addition, the color value was also negatively affected by increasing the firing cycle, change also influenced by the ceramic type (9).

It has been claimed that IPS E.max CAD ceramic showed higher biaxial flexural strength compared with Vita Suprinity, whatever the firing cycle. In addition, significant increase in biaxial flexural strength occurred in these ceramics when subjected to 2 or 7 firings (6). In the current study, although IPS E.max CAD ceramic showed similarity between firing cycles, Suprinity provided higher value in cycle C5, lower in C2 and intermediate in C3. When comparing the mean values of the isolated factors between cycles, C5 was lower than C2 and similar to C3 (Table 4).

On the other hand, the flexural strength value of the Suprinity ceramic was lower when subjected to two firing cycles and higher when three or five firing cycles were applied (2). However, increasing the number of firing cycles did not reduce the flexural strength of samples made with lithium disilicate ceramic (19), while vitro lithium disilicate ceramic and zirconia-reinforced lithium silicate ceramics were not negatively affected by increasing the firing cycle (9). Another important factor claimed is that although the firing cycle increases flexural strength, a lower temperature range can also increase the heterogeneity of ceramic materials, cause increased risk of mechanical failure, and consequently greater risk of cracks and fissures occurring in the material (18).

The statistical similarity in the flexural strength values of IPS E.max CAD in the firing cycles C2, C3 and C5 seems to confirm the results of previous articles showing that repeated firings did not affect the flexural strength of lithium disilicate glass ceramic (5, 11). However, the current study also showed that the statistical similarity of values between firing cycles was not observed for Suprinity ceramic, a fact that probably occurred considering the hypothesis that the zirconia in the composition of the material would limit the growth and maintain the uniformity of the glass crystal grains.

In general, glass ceramics are made from specific glasses by melting ceramic crystals. Controlled heating protocols form nuclei and promote crystal growth within the glass matrix. The glass ceramic material can consist of several glassy and crystalline phases. This process increases the properties of lithium disilicate, making it a more resistant and durable ceramic material (5).

The negative effect of repeated firings on dental ceramics and the decrease in biaxial flexural strength after prolonged sintering and vitrification treatments have also been observed. Although vitrification improves the surface texture of ceramics, this procedure can generate stress, leading to the initiation and propagation of microcracks. In addition, the type of material and the firing cycle had significant negative effect on the biaxial flexural strength value of lithium disilicate ceramics (17), fact that corroborates only the results of IPS E.max CAD in this current study.

There was no interaction or statistical difference when comparing firing cycles and ceramics related to hardness. However, when comparing the mean values of isolated factors, there was statistical difference between ceramics, with higher value for Suprinity. This fact may have been due to zirconia in the composition of the material. When comparing the average values of isolated factors between cycles, the more firing cycles applied to the ceramic materials, the lower the hardness value (Table 5).

Previous research has shown that the hardness value of lithium disilicate ceramics for fully crystallized CAD-CAM, zirconia-reinforced silicate or lithium aluminum disilicate was negatively influenced by the material and firing cycles (17). However, Suprinity showed higher hardness value compared with IPS E.max CAD (2), corroborating the current study that also showed higher hardness for Suprinity in all firing cycles. In addition, it was also found that C2 promoted greater hardness, C5 lower and C3 was intermediate, meaning that the increase of cycles had negative effect on the hardness of both ceramic types, fact that confirms previous study showing that hardness was negatively affected by the increase of firing cycles (9).

Although the hardness results in each firing cycle did not show statistical difference between ceramics, the absolute averages showed difference with higher value for Suprinity (Table 5). In addition, Suprinity showed different biaxial flexural strength value between firing cycles (Table 4) and higher hardness (Table 5) compared with IPS E.max CAD. This result may be due to the effect of firing cycles causing different formation of micro irregularities in the crystalline matrix.

On the other hand, previous study showed that the highest hardness and fracture toughness values before the sintering process and repeated firings occurred for IPS E.max CAD (20). However, the SEM images of the IPS E.max CAD (Figure 4 - A, B, C) show similar tendency of structural formation, with Suprinity images showing changes in the surface after different firing cycles (Figure 4 - D, E, F).

Morphological aspect of the crystalline matrix of the IPS E.max CAD showed in the cycles C2 (Figure 4A), C3 (Figure 4B) and C5 (Figure 4C) identifies similar formation of micro irregularities. On the other hand, the morphological aspect of the crystalline matrix of the Suprinity shows structural difference among the cycles. C2 exhibits crystalline matrix with completely homogeneous relief (Figure 4D) similar to that of the IPS E.max CAD. Amorphous material, probably glaze, covers part of the crystalline matrix in C3 (Figure 4E), and C5 (Figure 4F) shows the same morphological aspect found in C2; however, with the formation of depressions and microcracks.

Previous study showed that the ceramic subjected to the 900°C firing cycle and analyzed by SEM presented homogeneous structure, indicating that the firing cycle was completed (21). Furthermore, the microstructural characterization of highly translucent zirconia prepared by milling technology and subjected to four firing cycles also showed homogeneous structure, without porosities and with crystalline grains of different sizes (22).

The development of residual stress could be a potential cause of premature fractures in glassy ceramic materials, with special interest being lithium silicate glass-ceramics that require crystallization firing to achieve better mechanical properties (23). However, good agreement was found between the biaxial strength results for the different sample geometries (plates vs. discs) using the B3B test (24). On the other hand, ceramics with a single-fired glaze showed greater changes in color, roughness, and microhardness when compared with those subjected to two firings (3).

Limitations of the current study include the in vitro experimental design, which does not simulate the oral environment. Clinical conditions, including masticatory forces and mechanical aging were not verified in the study and may affect the research results, considering that the flexural strength test applied would not be the only means to simulate the clinical performance of the material. Another limitation of the study was the fact that the greater number of repeated firings was 5 times (cycle C5); therefore, future investigations involving greater numbero of cycles and other variables are necessary to corroborate the findings of the current work.

Conclusion

Despite the limitations of the study, the following conclusions can be drawn: Firing cycles promoted better effect on the roughness and biaxial flexural strength parameters for Suprinity compared with IPS E.max CAD. Both ceramics showed negative effects on the morphological aspect of the crystalline matrix and similar gloss or hardness values.
