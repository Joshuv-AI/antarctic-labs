Executive Summary
To ensure safe, reliable, and affordable power for our country, utilities are evolving in response
to a wide variety of natural events, technological advancements, and societal trends. Retail
electricity rate design has developed over decades as a mechanism to ensure the fair, efficient
recovery of the costs to maintain the system and serve customer load. This research investigates
the inception and current state of one retail electricity rate design element— demand charges—
which are based on a customer’s level of electrical demand over a specific period, denominated
in kilowatts (kW).

Widely used in the commercial and industrial (C&I) electricity sectors, demand charges are
being integrated into residential rates in a modest but growing number of locations. Although the
debate regarding their use as a rate recovery mechanism is longstanding, the conversation about
demand charges has intensified with recent technological advancements and trends. While there
are various uses of the term “demand charge,” this report focuses on demand charges that are
components of retail rate structures and that are based on the individual customer’s
noncoincident peak (NCP) demand. 1,2

The purpose of this effort is to describe the development of retail electricity demand charges and
their relationship to the current electricity landscape. The paper includes a literature review about
this topic and an analysis of retail electricity rates using the National Laboratory of the Rockies’s
(NLR)’s publicly accessible Utility Rate Database (URDB) to describe how demand charges
vary across the United States. In addition, the authors curate the results of semi-structured
interviews with experts to describe the current breadth of opinions about demand charges. These
interview results are categorized using four widely accepted principles of electricity ratemaking:
utility cost recovery, customer understanding and acceptance, equitable cost allocation, and
efficient price signals to encourage customer behavior. The authors conclude by summarizing
future research avenues and research gaps in this area of study.

This report does not take a stance on whether demand charges are an effective or ineffective
mechanism or provide specific recommendations. Instead, the report describes the various forces
that have shaped demand charges over time, which could help the reader develop or refine their
own perspectives.

relevance given current changes in the electricity sector. This report explores the historical
context of and motivations for demand charges, describes their implementation and impacts in
today’s context, and uses a variety of opinions collected from relevant parties through semi-

Electric utilities design and propose rates that are then approved or denied by regulators, city
councils, and others with oversight authority (referred to hereafter as “oversight bodies”). Both
utilities and oversight bodies seek to balance key ratemaking principles and policy goals in their
respective ratemaking and decision-making processes. The following four principles help guide
the design and approval of a “just and reasonable” rate:

 • Effective cost recovery (i.e., effectiveness in yielding total revenue requirements)
 • Customer understanding and acceptance
 • Equitable cost allocation (including the avoidance of undue discrimination)
 • Encouraging customer response (e.g., through price signals).
To determine a rate, utilities first determine the revenue requirement—that is, the amount of
money that should be collected annually through electricity rates. The revenue requirement is
divided across different utility functions such as generation, transmission, distribution, and
billing and customer service. This step is called functionalization. The next step classifies costs
into demand-related, energy-related, or customer-related costs. Finally, costs are allocated to
different customer classes (e.g., residential, commercial, industrial) and subclasses and the final
rates for each class are established.

Demand charges entered the ratemaking conversation as early as the late 1890s, after the
invention of the maximum demand meter. Throughout the midcentury, demand charges
remained a part of many large C&I electricity rates and were already the subject of a variety of
debates regarding their effectiveness in reducing system peaks and their alignment with the costs
of serving customer loads.

Significant rate design reforms were debated and implemented in the 1970s and 1980s. New
metering technologies emerged, and a push to increase competition led to the passage of the
Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act (PURPA) and the restructuring of some electricity
markets. During this time, many jurisdictions rolled generation and transmission costs into per
kilowatt-hour (kWh) rates instead of demand charges.

Between the 1990s and 2010s, smart metering technology such as advanced metering
infrastructure (AMI) was introduced, increasing the feasibility of implementing both residential
demand charges and time-varying demand charge alternatives. The 2010s also saw the
widespread adoption of net metering compensation.

New metering technology paired with utility concerns about rising electricity delivery costs,
stagnant electricity sales, and customer savings from net metering spurred increased utility
interest in residential demand charge pilot programs in the 2010s. Many rate cases with
residential demand charges were rejected during this period, but some were also passed. Today,
most residential customers in the United States do not have residential demand charges;
however, there are several examples of longstanding default or opt-in residential demand charges
in the United States that date back to the 2010s or earlier. Several current trends continue to push
the evolution of demand charges.

To demonstrate how demand charges can vary across the United States, we compared the 2023
revenue requirement calculations for two utilities: a distribution-only utility in a fully
restructured market and a large, vertically integrated investor-owned utility. In both utility
examples, we examined how revenue is recovered through rates across all sectors and within the
C&I and residential sectors. These examples represent the ways that revenue recovery may occur
for two investor-owned utility types. In both cases, demand charges make up a larger portion of
the revenue recovered from the C&I sectors than the total revenue recovered through rates
overall. When a utility owns generation—such as is the case in the vertically integrated utility—
demand charges are more likely to recover costs associated with energy production.

distribution-only utility to the revenue recovered in just the C&I sectors. The total revenue
recovered is approximately $1 billion, which includes pass-through charges for energy. The
percentage recovered through demand charges based on total revenue from all sectors, including
revenue from residential and lighting sectors, which do not include demand charges, is smaller
than the percentage recovered through demand charges for C&I customers. 3

This report adds to a body of work on how demand charges have changed throughout history and
how they correspond to different ratemaking principles. The key takeaways of this report are as
follows:

 • Since the inception of demand charges as a rate element, experts have discussed their
 ability to reduce system peak load when applied to individual noncoincident customer
 peaks.
 • For both vertically integrated and distribution-only investor-owned utilities, demand
 charges are significant in cost recovery, especially in the C&I sectors. If demand charges
 were to go away, that revenue would still need to be recovered through other avenues.
 • For customers with low load factors, demand charges can result in particularly high costs.
 • Most demand charges in the United States are applied to the C&I sector, but with
 increasing use of residential demand charges in certain utility territories, there is debate
 about whether the technology exists to enable customer response and the level of
 response that would occur even with that technology.
 • In locations that have residential demand charges as an option, opt-in rates are low (often
 less than 1% of residential customers).
 • Given AMI technology, other price signals can achieve similarly effective cost recovery
 to demand charges.
 • With the increase of new technologies such as battery energy storage, there is a question
 about the continued applicability of demand charges as a revenue stability mechanism for
 utilities in the future.
 • Locations with higher rates in general tend to have higher demand charges, with a few
 exceptions.
 • Although all demand charges encourage the reduction of customer billing demand, flat
 demand charges tend to smooth customer load whereas peak or peak window demand
 charges may move load to low-cost hours and encourage the moderating of load during
 high-cost hours. All time-varying rates (including peak or peak window demand charges
 and TOU volumetric rates) can also encourage customers to reduce energy use at high-
 cost times.

1 Introduction
Electric utilities are evolving in response to a wide variety of natural events, technological
advancements, and societal trends. How are the costs of supplying electric service recovered by
the entities that maintain this critical infrastructure? How might cost recovery mechanisms
evolve to reflect societal and technological changes?

Retail electricity rate design has developed over decades as a mechanism to ensure the fair,
efficient recovery of the costs of serving electric customer load. Over the years, the process of
setting electricity rates and the rate designs themselves have been refined and, in many cases,
become more complex. How rates should evolve in response to technological and societal
changes is a topic of debate. It is clear, however, that rate design remains a critical element in
ensuring a safe, reliable, and affordable electricity system into the future.

1.1 Scope of Research
This research investigates the inception and current state of one retail electricity rate design
element: demand charges. Demand charges are based on a customer’s level of electrical demand
over a specific period, denominated in kilowatts (kW). This report explores the historical context
of and motivations for demand charges, describes their implementation and impacts in the
current day context, and discusses how demand charges align with four widely accepted rate
design principles. The research draws on semi-structured interviews with rate design experts that
represent a wide variety of perspectives as well as literature and utility data.

Demand charges are typically used in conjunction with energy charges (measured in kilowatt-
hours [kWh]) and fixed charges, to comprise the utility rate structure. They are widely used for
commercial and industrial (C&I) electricity customers to recover significant portions of utility
revenue and are being integrated into residential rates in a modest but growing number of
locations.

This report focuses on retail rate noncoincident peak (NCP) demand charges that are based on
the individual customer’s NCP demand. Coincidence refers to the degree to which the timing of
a customer’s (or a group of customers’) peak demand aligns with the timing of system peak
demand. The measurement of coincidence is used by utilities to size their assets and can be
expressed as a percentage, called the coincidence factor.

Coincidence factor is inversely related to the load diversity factor, which is equal to the sum of
the maximum load rating of a group of customers over the maximum load rating in that group.
As the area within which load is served (and thus the number of customer loads) increases away
from the local transformer, the load diversity factor increases, and the coincidence factor
decreases.

The following sections categorize various types of NCP demand charges that have been
implemented in the United States, present the perspectives of rate design experts, use several
widely accepted ratemaking design principles as a framework to discuss their effectiveness as a
rate recovery mechanism, and identify areas for further investigation.

 1

This paper does not take a stance on whether demand charges are an effective or ineffective
mechanism or provide specific recommendations. Instead, the focus is on describing the various
forces that have shaped demand charges over time, which may help the reader develop or refine
their own perspectives.

1.2 Variations of Demand Charges
Demand charges can vary in the way they are structured and implemented. The following terms
are important to understanding the structural variations of demand charges:

 • Integration period. The length of time over which the highest demand (in kW) is
 averaged is called the integration period. This is often 15, 30, or 60 minutes for industrial
 sector customers. Advanced metering technology allows more flexible methods in setting
 the integration period, such as taking the average of a customer’s two highest demand
 hours during a month.
 • Time variation. Demand charges ($/kW) may vary by the hour in which the demand is
 used. The demand charge may vary by the hour of the day, the day type (i.e., weekend vs.
 weekday, holidays), or both.
 • Seasonal variation. Demand charges ($/kW) may vary by the month of the year or the
 season. For example, a demand charge may be higher in summer in locations with
 summer system load peaks.
 • Ratchet application. This refers to whether the demand charge employs a ratchet (i.e., a
 billing method wherein the customer pays the higher of the monthly demand
 measurement or a percentage of the highest value in the previous 12 months). If there is
 not a ratchet, the customer demand is typically measured at each billing period.
 • Contract terms. This refers to whether the demand billed for is predefined by agreement
 or measured at the meter.
 • Categories of costs recovered. This refers to whether the demand charge is recovering
 generation, transmission, primary voltage distribution facility, or secondary voltage
 distribution facility costs.
These different structural dimensions of demand charges lead to different types of demand
charges that can be incorporated into rate structures. The most common demand charges used in
recent decades include the following:

 • Customer NCP demand charge. This is based on a customer’s individual peak and
 measured every billing period.
 • Peak or peak window demand charge. The billing demand is measured in a subset of
 hours (e.g., peak period or off-peak period) akin to a time-of-use kWh rate.
 • Daily as-used demand charge. The customer’s individual NCP billing demand is
 measured every day, sometimes within a peak window (LeBel et al. 2020).
 • Ratcheted demand charge. The customer pays the higher of the monthly demand
 measurement or a percentage of the highest value in the previous 12 months (typically
         between 85% and 100%).
 • Contract demand charge. The billing demand is predefined by agreement. Sometimes
 called a “connected load charge” based on the size of the service panel of the premises.

 2

 • Hours use of demand charge. Also known as a Wright demand charge, these types of
 demand charges divide a customer’s energy usage (kWh) by their maximum demand
 (kW) within a billing period (Vogt 2009). In other words, these demand charges represent
 the customer’s average load factor to indicate a customer’s energy use relative to their
 peak demand. 8,9
Each of these types of demand charges has been shaped by the customer classes they apply to,
policy and regulatory priorities, the opinions of customers and consumer advocates, and other
factors. Any single type of demand charge can be designed to recover a variety of system costs.
For example, customer NCP demand charges can be used to recover either the costs associated
with local facilities, e.g., local transformers, or could be designed to recover more upstream
system costs (i.e., generation, distribution, and transmission).

2 Background on Ratemaking
Traditional economic regulation of electric utilities involves the oversight body’s review of rates
proposed by utilities against certain legal and regulatory standards, including the commonly cited
“just and reasonable” statutory standard. Under this standard, the utility should be able to recover
its cost to serve customers, including a reasonable return on investment, through electricity rates.

The utility ratemaking process can be summarized in three steps:

 1. Determine the revenue requirement, i.e., the amount of money that rates are intended to
 collect annually.
 2. Allocate costs across customer classes (e.g., residential, commercial, industrial, and street
 lighting) using policy factors.
 3. Design the final rates for each customer class.

Oversight bodies and other relevant parties refer to a wide range of principles or guidelines when
deciding whether to approve or deny a rate proposed by a utility, and utilities also use such
principles when designing rates. The most famous list of principles were compiled by economist
Dr. James C. Bonbright. 11 We use four accepted principles of rate design to frame the
discussions in this work (LeBel et al. 2021):

 • Effectiveness in yielding total revenue requirements. The utility expects it will
 approximately recover its revenue requirement from customer rates, with a reasonable
 amount of stability from year to year.
 • Customer understanding and acceptance. Prices should not be overly complex or
 convoluted such that customers cannot understand how their bills are determined or how
 they should respond to manage their bills. Customers and the public should generally
 accept the prices they are charged for electricity service as fair for the service they are
 receiving.
 • Equitable allocation of costs and the avoidance of undue discrimination. The
 apportionment of total costs of service among the different customers and customer
 allocations should be done fairly and equitably. While this principle has always
 incorporated broader notions of fairness, it typically focuses on the idea that cost
 recovery should come from the customers who are responsible for incurring the costs.
 • Efficient price signals that encourage optimal customer behavior. On a forward-
 looking basis, electricity prices should encourage customers to use, conserve, store, and
 generate energy in ways that are most efficient (i.e., most optimal for the electric system
 and society). Efficient price signals are often informed by the concepts of cost causation
 and marginal cost pricing.
Although each of these four principles individually is important in ratemaking, the oversight
body must often weigh and balance these objectives when deciding whether to approve or deny a
rate proposed by a utility, recognizing significant trade-offs between them may exist (Roe 2023).
We use these four principles as a framework for our discussion of relevant parties’ opinion on
demand charges in Section 5.

This section provides a brief history of demand charges in the electricity system, including
factors contributing to the evolution of this rate structure over time. Shifts in demand charge
structures and theory are related to changes in technology, policy, market structures, or a
combination of more than one of these. An abbreviated timeline of how demand charges have
evolved is displayed in Figure 3, and the following sections describe these events in more detail.

3.1 The Birth of Demand Charges (1890–1930)
The 1890–1930 period saw the dramatic expansion of electricity service and consumption and
the beginning of regulated utilities in many states. Throughout this time period, electricity was
primarily consumed by large manufacturing customers and expanded into residential lighting. In
the 1890s, electric utilities were emerging in the United States, and inventors George
Westinghouse, Nikola Tesla, and Thomas Edison argued in their “war of the currents” about
whether to use alternating or direct current to supply electrical power to customers—which
would inform how to design U.S. transmission and distribution systems (U.S. DOE 2014). The
debate over direct versus alternating current would also determine how a large base of electricity
customers would be served. Simultaneously, utilities were aiming to expand their customer bases
over which to spread their fixed costs and competed for customers who were considering self-
generation (i.e., sources of electricity production that are not connected to the central grid)
(Neufeld 1987).

As large manufacturing customers began using electricity for lighting and other operations,
discussion arose about how to price and sell this new commodity. Demand charges entered the
conversation in the late 1890s and early 1900s during early debates about electricity pricing and
the invention of the maximum demand meter. In the 1890s in England, British engineers John

 8

Hopkinson and Arthur Wright each theorized about their own flavor of a “two-part tariff” to
recover costs associated with electricity production (Roe 2023; Neufeld 1987). The first “part”
was a flat charge that measured total energy consumption, and the second was a “demand
charge” wherein customers’ individual peaks were measured. The two agreed that one part of the
tariff should measure energy use but argued for two flavors of a demand charge as the second
component. Hopkinson was a proponent of an NCP demand charge based on peak demand,
whereas Wright was a proponent of a declining block rate that depends on the number of hours
that the maximum demand is used by the customer (Neufeld 1987; Lewis 1941). 12

Arthur Wright also invented and implemented a demand meter that could measure the maximum
power consumption of an individual (Neufeld 1987). After demand metering became possible,
demand charges allowed utilities to monetize a customer’s individual maximum consumption
(Byatt 1963). In 1894, Samuel Insull—an English entrepreneur and businessman who had moved
to the United States to act as Thomas Edison’s secretary—used the two-part tariff and Wright’s
demand meter in Chicago, Illinois (Cudahy and Henderson 2005).

According to modern historian John L. Neufeld, individual customer peaks may have aligned
well with system peak when Hopkinson and Wright were first theorizing about the two-part tariff
in the late 1800s to early 1900s. During this time, electricity was mostly used for lighting, and
because it was still fairly expensive, lighting was used only in the evening—resulting in a very
specific peak period (Neufeld 1987). However, demand charges were adopted between 1906 and
1917, and by this time the uses of electricity had altered so customers’ individual peaks did not
necessarily coincide with system peaks (Neufeld 1987).

As the usefulness of electricity began to be realized, large C&I customers evaluating their
options for how to meet new electricity demand considered installing their own self-generation
rather than procuring electricity from a local utility. Utilities knew retaining C&I customers
would spread their fixed costs across a wider customer base. Neufeld posited that demand
charges were originally a method used by utilities to recover costs while retaining the business of
large industrial customers (Neufeld 1987). This rate element allowed utilities to supply
generation to industrial customers at a lower cost than the self-generation option (Neufeld 1987).
In other words, utilities had an advantage over many customers who were considering self-
generation because of the benefits of economies of scale and peak diversity (Neufeld 1987). In
this way, the concept of the demand charge was solidified by the marketing strategies used with
large C&I customers.

Hopkinson, Wright, and Insull argued demand charges could help utilities reduce system peak
load and therefore total system costs (Neufeld 1987). However, as early as the 1920s, engineer
Hugo Eisenmenger postulated that an individual customer’s peak demand is not necessarily

3.2 Demand Charges in the Midcentury (1930–1970)
In the mid-1900s, the United States undertook a large push for electrification, especially in rural
areas. The New Deal Era promoted rural electrification across the United States through
legislation such as the Rural Electrification Act of 1936 and creation of the Tennessee Valley
Authority in 1933. The significant electrification efforts led to the doubling of demand in the
1940s and 1950s with utilities building out significant generation across the nation (PBS 2001).
After World War II, electric power use steadily grew as costs continued to decline, providing
customers with inexpensive and abundant electricity (PBS 2001). In addition, advertisement
campaigns encouraging electricity use promoted increased electrification of end uses (PBS
2001).

In the 1960s–1970s, marginal cost theory began to be applied to electricity pricing, paving the
way for increasingly competitive electricity markets and the restructuring that would occur later
in the twentieth century. 14 Two early contributors to marginal cost theory were Alfred E. Kahn
and James C. Bonbright. Bonbright’s 1961 Principles of Utility Ratemaking includes a chapter
about marginal costs, which argues that while they promote efficient price signals, they should
be balanced with other priorities—and bills should involve other cost components (such as fixed
costs) to support effective cost recovery and ensure fairness. Similarly, Kahn’s 1970 book The
Economics of Regulation also includes insights about marginal costs, noting they promote
economically efficient price signals for electric utilities. Like Bonbright, Kahn saw marginal cost
pricing as a starting point for electricity pricing that would require adjustments related to
ensuring recovery of the revenue requirement, practicality, customer acceptance, and fairness.

Throughout the midcentury, demand charges remained a part of many large C&I electricity rates.
A demand charge portion of the two-part tariff was justified by the argument that because
electricity could not be stored for long periods, the equipment necessary to serve electricity
customers was set by peak demand periods—the cost for which should be recovered separately
from the cost of a customer’s total energy consumption (Lewis 1941). Another notable trend is
that throughout this time period, electricity costs were declining each year because of economies
of scale (Cudahy and Henderson 2005).

Residential demand charges were uncommon in the mid-1900s for several reasons. For
individual households, demand meters were initially expensive to implement. In addition,
common thought dictated that demand charges were inappropriate for small, low-load factor
customers because of a lack of customer understanding and the possibility of high customer costs
resulting from electricity use during off-peak hours (Bolton 1951). For example, economist D. J.
Bolton argued in 1951 that while demand charges may appropriately represent cost for large
industrial customers whose peak aligns well with the system peak, they were likely inappropriate
for smaller customers (Bolton 1951).

Even when they applied to the C&I sectors, demand charges were subject to critiques during this
period. In the early 1940s, the Saint Lucian and British economist and Nobel Prize winner Sir W.
Arthur Lewis called Hopkinson and Wright’s maximum demand portion of the two-part tariff
“fallacious in so far as they claim to be exactly allocating to each customer the costs he causes,”
and argued volumetric TOU rates presented a better representation of costs (Lewis 1941). Lewis
theorized that since the customer’s peak was not necessarily coincident with the system peak, the
cost of contributing to peak implied by a demand charge could be inaccurate (Lewis 1941). In
other words, Lewis argued that if the customer’s individual maximum demand laid outside of the
peak period when the costs of selling to the customer are theoretically lower, a demand charge
could be overcharging the customer (Lewis 1941). The assertion that demand charges “fail to
adequately solve the peak problem” represents a common critique of demand charges that was
presented by other experts during this period, including D. J. Bolton, who described the
alignment between the component loads of customers that make up the total system load in terms
of load factor (Bolton 1951).

3.3 New Regulations and Restructuring (1970–2010)
In the 1970s through 2010, changes in policy and technology led to shifts in theory and practice
related to retail electricity rates. Significant rate design reforms were debated and implemented
in the 1970s and 1980s, including a move away from declining block tariffs in some jurisdictions
as energy efficiency became a higher priority (Berg and Tschirhart 1989). New metering
technologies emerged and developed, global conflicts involving member countries from the
Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC) led to significant increases in fuel
costs, most of the nuclear generating capacity that is currently operating in the United States was
installed, and a broader trend of restructuring U.S. markets to increase competition reached the
electricity sector and led to restructuring of certain electricity markets.

Between 1973 and 1974, members of OPEC in Southwest Asia and North Africa enforced an oil
embargo on the United States and other countries whose governments supported Israel in the
October 1973 Arab-Israeli war, which led to a stark increase in U.S. fuel costs. Between 1977
and 1979, turmoil related to the Iranian Revolution further increased the cost of oil. These
events, in addition to varying costs of nuclear generating capacity and other influences,
collectively led to a reverse of the trend of electricity cost decline that had been observed until
the 1970s (Cudahy and Henderson 2005).

The Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act (PURPA) of 1978 was spurred by rising electricity
costs (and related conversations about strategies such as competition to help bring costs back
down), as well as the emerging environmental movement (Cudahy and Henderson 2005).
PURPA allowed small energy producers to supply electricity by requiring large utilities to buy

 11

their output at a reasonable rate, and it also promoted energy conservation and energy efficiency
(Public Utility Regulatory Policies Act of 1978 1978). The passage of PURPA was also the
beginning of a path toward a broader legislative trend of electricity restructuring (Cudahy and
Henderson 2005; Lazar et al. 2020).

In addition, PURPA required state public utility commissions to consider broadly whether rates
should be based on the cost of service, and within that, the implementation of TOU rates,
seasonal rates, interruptible rates, and the elimination of declining-block rates (Public Utility
Regulatory Policies Act of 1978 1978). Previously, ratemaking was largely based on the utility’s
cost of generating electricity, without competition from independent power providers defined
under PURPA. In the 1970s, TOU rates and other forms of demand response were initiated, in
part driven by the increasing adoption of air conditioning—which created short-term spikes in
demand that were concentrated into a common period for all customers and reduced customer
load factors (Cappers et al. 2009). However, chart metering and paper recordings that required
manual translation still made time-varying rates burdensome, and it was not until metering
technology improved that TOU rates were widely deployed. Although there were several early
pilots to reflect new loads such as air conditioning, residential demand charges were still
uncommon (Hledik 2014).

Also during this time period, the U.S. federal government passed restructuring policies for U.S.
industries such as air travel, telecommunications, and natural gas (Cudahy and Henderson 2005).
The 1992 Energy Policy Act, spurred partially by concerns of large electricity consumers, added
electricity to that list and encouraged the development of wholesale power markets (Lazar et al.
2020; Costello et al. 1993). A series of Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) Orders
throughout the 1990s further promoted competition among power suppliers and allowed
generators to compete for transmission on a more price-competitive playing field (Cudahy and
Henderson 2005). In 1999, FERC Order 2000 encouraged the development of regional
transmission organizations (RTOs) to coordinate the electric power system across multiple states
(Costello and Burns 2000).

In restructured markets, many jurisdictions rolled generation and transmission costs into per
kWh rates instead of demand charges. However, demand charges were still applied to industrial
customers in some jurisdictions to recover some distribution system costs. 15 Schweppe wrote
about restructuring in 1988 and described demand charges as having “essentially no role in the
energy marketplace” in which marginal costs dictate rates (Schweppe et al. 1988).

to reflect the increasing focus on clean energy and decarbonization goals through renewable
portfolio standards, clean energy standards, federal initiatives, and cap-and-trade programs, and

By enabling efficient and affordable two-way communication between utilities and customers
and more detailed information about customer electricity use, AMI began to replace automated
meter reading (AMR), which refers to one-way electricity meters that collect data for billing
purposes only (U.S. EPA 2024). AMR technology use peaked in 2009 when it served about 34%

time-varying rates—such as TOU pricing, critical peak pricing, and real-time rates—which were
significant because they presented alternatives to demand charges and triggered a new round of
debates between retail electricity pricing experts (Borenstein 2016; Faruqui et al. 2014). In
addition, as AMI became more widespread, the ability and feasibility of implementing residential
demand charges increased as well.

The 2010s also saw the widespread adoption of net metering compensation for rooftop solar
photovoltaics (PV) customers. Net metering refers to a billing mechanism wherein residential or
commercial solar PV customers can offset their grid electricity demand with their own supply
(generally solar PV) and receive retail rate crediting for sending excess generation back to the
grid (Shah 2014). By July 2013, according to data by the Database of State Incentives for
Renewables and Efficiency (DSIRE), 43 states, 4 territories, and Washington, D.C. had adopted
a net metering policy (Database of State Incentives for Renewables & Efficiency 2013).

Standard net metering presented an opportunity for early solar PV adopters to be compensated
for supplying their own electricity. High levels of behind-the-meter (BTM) solar deployment had
the potential to exaggerate the trend of slowing electricity sales across the United States during
this period because of the Great Recession, energy efficiency standards, and demand side
management programs (Hledik 2014; J. McLaren et al. 2015). Simultaneously, the cost of
electricity delivery—which includes money spent on building, operating, and maintaining the
electric transmission and distribution systems—was trending upward. In other words, utilities

 13

were paying more to maintain the electricity grid while sales remained stagnant (U.S. EIA 2021).
Because most residential solar PV customers remained connected to the electricity grid, many
electric utilities already felt that changes to retail rates in the residential sector might be
necessary to recover fixed costs across stagnant to declining sales (J. McLaren et al. 2015).
Utilities initially explored addressing this issue through increasing fixed charges, but demand
charges began to emerge as a potentially fairer alternative (e.g., by not automatically increasing
bills for customers with low loads) (Hledik 2014). Taken together, these factors led to increased
utility interest in residential demand charges (J. McLaren et al. 2015).

After 2010, several utilities began to propose residential demand charge pilot programs in their
rate cases. In 2014, at least 9 utilities in the United States had a voluntary demand charge option
for residential customers, and by 2016, one report cited 25 utilities in 15 U.S. states with
residential demand charge offerings (Hledik 2014; Sherwood et al. 2016).

Proposals for residential demand charges were met with significant concerns from a variety of
groups including advocates for underserved communities, solar PV customers, clean energy
groups, and oversight bodies as fears emerged that they singled out customers with lower usage
(sometimes resulting from installation of rooftop solar) (Brodie 2016). This resulted in some
backlash including a community coalition formed in protest of Arizona Public Services’
proposed mandatory demand charge as well as class action suits filed by solar owners, including
in Salt River Project and Wisconsin’s We Energies (Randazzo 2015; Toll 2016). Arizona Public
Service has multiple rate options today, including a standard rate, a TOU rate, and a demand
charge rate.

Many rate cases with residential demand charges were rejected during this period, but some were
also passed. According to data from EQ Research, no state oversight body approved mandatory
residential demand charges for any investor-owned utility, but some small municipal utilities and
electric cooperatives did implement mandatory residential demand charges (EQ Research 2016;
Green Tech Media Squared and EQ Research 2016). 16

States that date back to the 2010s or earlier. These include both opt-in and opt-out/default
demand charges for residential customers.

4 Cost Recovery and Compensation in the Current
  Era
4.1 Current Issues and Influences
Several current trends continue to push the evolution of demand charges, including load
flexibility, battery energy storage systems, heating in the built environment, transportation, and
microgrids.

Some are questioning current demand charges as they apply to new technologies. Demand
charges may positively or negatively impact the cost effectiveness of new technologies and are
being considered during business model development. For electric vehicle charging (in particular
direct-current fast charging (DCFC) at low utilization rates), demand charges may reduce cost
effectiveness, while high demand charges may reduce the payback period for battery energy
storage systems. These various interplays are discussed in Sections 4.1.1 through 4.1.5.

4.1.1 Load Shifting
Load shifting refers to the ability of customers to adjust or shift their load in response to grid
conditions through price signals or control signals during system events. The price signals
provided from demand charges do not necessarily incentivize load shifting from customers. For
example, non-coincident peak demand charges can be levied against a customer during low-cost
times of the day and month due to their lack of temporal differentiation. In other words, NCP
demand charges may in effect during times when it is cheaper to serve customers from the
utility’s perspective and thus send conflicting signals about when customers should shift their
load (LeBel et al. 2021). To comprehensively incentivize beneficial load shifting from the
system’s perspective, a combination of marginal, time-differentiated price signals and
programmatic features, such as control signals, are needed so that utilities can send price signals
that correspond with times when cost to serve is high.

4.1.2 Battery Energy Storage Systems
Deployment of battery energy storage systems (BESS) at high levels may make demand charges
less effective as a revenue stability mechanism because deploying BESS allows customers to
shift energy usage to avoid times of high demand. Battery storage can be an economically
attractive option for residential, commercial, and industrial customers that are otherwise unable
to shift their electricity load patterns to avoid high demand charges (J. A. McLaren et al. 2017).
Hledik and Greenstein (2016) investigated the extent to which energy storage can reduce the
electricity bill of a residential battery storage customer with demand charges, finding that
batteries can lead to savings of 13% to 28% depending on the magnitude of the demand charge
and the customer’s ability to respond to rates using demand management strategies. The higher
the demand charge element of the rate, the higher the savings potential from a battery (Hledik
and Greenstein 2016). Batteries can also help customers reduce bills under other time-varying
rates, such as critical peak pricing. In some cases, the utility may control the customer battery
during system peak events, in exchange for a customer bill credit or other compensation (Eden et
al. 2024). Despite recent reductions in battery costs, BTM battery storage economics are still
highly dependent on the individual application and applicable utility rate structure, and financing
continues to be a challenge because of complexities and uncertainties in calculating payback on

 16

the investment (NREL 2024; Eden et al. 2024). In the United States, an estimated 0.8 gigawatts
(GW)/1.6 gigawatt-hours (GWh) of BTM battery storage was installed at the end of 2020
(Prasanna et al. 2021). While this level of BESS capacity is unlikely to significantly impact the
utility’s ability to recover costs, an important area of future research is exploring the role that
advanced rate structures and flexible interconnection agreements can play in ensuring cost
recovery at higher levels of BTM BESS deployment (McDonnell et al. 2025).

4.1.3 Heating in the Built Environment
Many locations are incentivizing customers to replace combustion-driven heating
boilers/furnaces with electric heat pumps in residential and commercial buildings to support state
emission reduction goals. Electricity rate design impacts the cost effectiveness of heat pumps
because long-term economic evaluation of these technologies depends on reduced operating
costs. Sergici et al. (2023) performed a case study of 80 single-family customers in a cold
climate and single weather year and found seasonal demand-based and volumetric TOU rates can
both reduce operating costs and payback period compared to a natural gas heater, leading to
positive economics (Sergici et al. 2023). Similar case studies with different geography, customer
allocations (i.e., both residential and commercial, multi-family housing), and rate design
alternatives are likely needed for utilities and other market players to understand and verify the
impact of different types of retail rates on heat pump economics.

4.1.4 Transportation
Traditional demand charges can make the business model for public direct-current fast charging
(DCFC) stations uneconomic due to these stations’ low utilization rates. 18,19 DCFC has a high
associated power usage, which can range between 50 and 350 kW, compared to less than 20 kW
for Level 2 chargers (U.S. DOT 2023). Demand charges are often part of the C&I default tariff
and may be applied to electric vehicle charging in the absence of an electric vehicle-specific rate.
Because most vehicle chargers are currently not being used more than a few hours per day
(subject to “low utilization rates”), the high-power usage of DCFC during short time intervals
can result in high spikes in electricity use. When demand charges are applied at stations with low
utilization rates, demand charges can make up a majority of the electricity bill, and the cost to
operate a DCFC station can outpace the revenue that the stations can obtain (McFarlane et al.
2019). Thus, a growing number of DCFC sites are using on-site batteries to avoid demand
charges and high initial electric service installation costs (Edelstein 2021). As utilization rates
increase over time, the impact of demand charges on DCFC sites will be lessened. A 2022 white
paper presents several possible demand charge relief strategies at DCFC sites, including
temporarily discounting demand charges, varying targeted electric vehicle charging station

holidays. Just two of the active rates with demand charges that were surveyed apply to the
residential sector (Cappers et al. 2023).

Electric vehicle charging can be shifted or limited to low-cost periods by programming the in-
vehicle charge-controller or charger, allowing utilities to control customer charging, or
encouraging customer response to price signals when time-varying retail rates are in effect. For
example, customers or the utility can limit the current drawn during vehicle charging, thus
reducing the short-duration power requirement. For customers who are on a flat demand charge,
limiting the current can reduce peaks in load, thus reducing demand charges. In addition,
customers who are on a time-varying rate such as a peak window demand charge or TOU
volumetric rate can save money by shifting their charging away from the times of day when
retail rates are the highest or when demand charges are levied.

4.1.5 Microgrids
A microgrid refers to “a group of interconnected loads and distributed energy resources that acts
as a single controllable entity with respect to the grid” (NREL Grid Mod., n.d.). Microgrids can
combine several of the technologies discussed in Section 4.1 and can disconnect, or island from
the grid to provide resilience and reliability during times of grid disturbances. Data centers have
been exploring microgrids to completely avoid grid charges (Reuters 2024).

Microgrids can add complexity to the pricing and regulatory paradigm. However, microgrids
also offer opportunities to control peak demand and grid costs by supplementing or substituting
for utility grid capacity and other services. For example, combining flexible connections for
export and import with microgrid functionalities could reduce distribution system capacity
requirements (Adeyi and Kossmann 2024). Given the fast advancement and adoption of
technologies that enable microgrid functionality, understanding the interplay of demand charge

4.2 Demand Charge Variation in the United States
The historical factors, policy priorities, and current trends discussed in Sections 3 and 4 frame
this report’s discussion on how demand charges are adopted and implemented throughout the
United States. In this section, we discuss how demand charges recover utility revenue differently
in vertically integrated utilities versus utilities in fully restructured markets and use the URDB to
visualize how demand charges vary throughout the United States.

To demonstrate how demand charges can vary across the United States, we compared the 2023
revenue requirement calculations for two utilities to determine how revenue is recovered through
rates across all sectors and within the C&I and residential sectors (Figure 7). In both cases,
demand charges recover a significant amount of overall utility revenue, and the percent of
revenue recovered increases significantly when looking at just the C&I sectors. The first utility is
a distribution-only IOU in a restructured market where customers may choose to procure their
power from a competitive retail supplier. The distribution-only utility still purchases power for
customers that do not choose a competitive retail supplier and provides delivery services for all
customers. Because this utility purchases generation from the market and passes it on to
customers, it is allowed only to recover costs, not earn a rate of return on the generation (i.e., it
does not profit from supply charges). Thus, the demand charges in the distribution-only utility
are predominately recovering the utility’s capital investments, on which they are allowed to
recover a rate of return. 21

5 Demand Charge Perspectives
This section presents expert opinions on demand charges, which were gathered through
individual semi-structured interviews and focus groups of a variety of experts from academia,
utilities, public utility commissions, consultancies, research institutions, and nonprofit
organizations. A total of 30 individuals contributed to a broad understanding of how demand
charges are currently implemented in various jurisdictions across the United States. The purpose
of these interviews was to gain a robust understanding of the opinions about demand charges and
use them to advance the conversation about demand charges in the current era. We formulated a
list of questions in several categories, including the history, goals, and motivations of demand
charges, current and future trends in demand charges, and examples of specific cases or
electricity sectors where demand charges align or misalign with key ratemaking principles.

Our selection of interviewees was based on the interviewees’ expertise on relevant areas of
interest. The diversity of expertise that is represented by our interviews represents a microcosm
of the larger scope of opinions about demand charges that have shaped the evolution of this rate
design over time. While these interviews may not represent the full range of opinions on this
topic, they are important in showing a breadth of opinions about demand charges among
electricity experts who have spent decades researching the topic of retail rates.

Electric utilities design and propose rates which are then approved or denied by oversight bodies.
Both utilities and oversight bodies seek to balance key ratemaking principles and policy goals in
their respective ratemaking and decision-making processes. We use four widely accepted rate
principles to categorize the insights from the expert interviews and to discuss the trade-offs
between these regulatory objectives that may be relevant as the electricity system evolves. The
principles are 1) effective revenue recovery, 2) customer understanding, 3) equitable cost
allocation, and 4) efficient marginal cost pricing.

5.1 Effective Revenue Recovery
The first principle acknowledges the longstanding regulatory goal of recovering electric utility
revenue requirements (LeBel et al. 2021). The annual revenue requirement for a utility is set
based on the cost of providing service, which typically includes operating expenses, depreciation
expense (a measure of the annual loss in value of utility capital assets), taxes, and an explicit
element for a rate of return (LeBel et al. 2021).

The revenue stability provided by employing demand charges has been a motivation for their
implementation since demand charges were introduced (Neufeld 1987). However, while designs
such as ratcheted demand charges yield predictable revenue, there is a tension with other
ratemaking principles. For example, several interview participants argued that ratcheted demand
charges provide customers with less ability to control costs through behavioral changes
(Interviewee A1, “Demand Charges Interview,” July 29, 2024, Virtual; Interviewee A2,
“Demand Charges Interview,” September 11, 2024, Virtual).

The extent to which demand charges result in effective cost recovery is sensitive to changes in
technology availability and affordability. For instance, as noted in Section 3.4, widespread
availability of affordable BESS, smart thermostats, home energy management systems, smart
charging, or load-limiting devices could enable customers to manage and reduce their demand

 29

charges. These technological changes could reduce the effectiveness of demand charges as a
utility mechanism for revenue stability.

5.2 Customer Understanding and Acceptance
The second principle states that electricity rates should be designed such that customers can
understand and accept them as fair and reasonable. 24 Large C&I customers are often
sophisticated and well equipped to understand and respond to demand charges and have accepted
demand charges as the status quo for many decades. Some large C&I customers—for example,
oil refineries, hospitals, and paper mills—have energy management systems and energy
managers to minimize utility costs (Interviewee A5, “Demand Charges Interview,” August 23,
2024, Virtual).

However, Chernick et al. (2016) pointed to low levels of understanding among small commercial
customers that make demand charges less favorable for this customer class (Chernick et al.
2016). Small commercial customers often are served through individual meters, have higher
levels of individual load diversity, and have less technological sophistication than their large
counterparts (Chernick et al. 2016). These factors can impact their ability to reduce both their
individual NCP demand and system peak demand (Chernick et al. 2016). New technology
applications can also impact the acceptance of demand charges. One interviewee observed
consistently low levels of understanding and acceptance of demand charges by entities that aim
to adopt electric vehicle fleets (Interviewee A3, “Demand Charges Interview,” August 9, 2024).

A common critique of demand charges that was expressed in our interviews by customer
advocates and other related groups is that demand charges are difficult for residential customers
to understand, respond to, and accept. A 2016 paper by Hledik and Faruqui summarizes
consumer advocacy groups’ opinions about the advantages and disadvantages of residential
demand charges. Opponents cite a lack of customer understanding and customer acceptance to
residential demand charges (Hledik and Faruqui 2016), whereas other experts point to potentially
effective solutions such as high-level messaging to educate customers about how to manage
demand. One cited example was a message on Georgia Power’s website that told customers to
avoid the simultaneous use of major appliances. Several case studies are cited wherein customer
response to such messaging has been observed (Hledik and Faruqui 2016).

Chernick et al. (2016) expand on the argument, stating that even if residential customers
understand demand charges and how to reduce their impact on electricity bills, the daily routines
of families preparing for work and school make it difficult to avoid the simultaneous use of
household appliances—particularly in the absence of energy management systems typically
available to larger C&I customers (Chernick et al. 2016). While some load-controlling devices
are commercially available, they have not yet been widely adopted by residential customers.

Along with residential customers, fleet managers and DCFC station owners may also lack

been cases in which customers have incurred large electricity bills before realizing the need to
adjust charging patterns to limit costs or change to a rate structure more appropriate for their
specific vehicle charging patterns (Interviewee A3, “Demand Charges Interview”).

Customer understanding and acceptance can be related to how quickly a new rate design or rate
concept is introduced (LeBel et al. 2021). One expert stated that as utilities introduce new rates
there may be an assumption of understanding and attention on the part of the customer, which is
not always manifested. Because of this, several interviewees pointed to the importance of
intentional and targeted customer education as the sector moves toward twenty-first century rate
designs (Interviewee A3, “Demand Charges Interview”). A highlighted best practice was utility
educational strategies that are supported by public utility commissions (PUCs) (Interviewee A4,
“Demand Charges Interview,” August 9, 2024, Virtual).

The form that customer education strategies can take varies by utility and depends on factors
such as their goals, customer class, and customer distribution. One strategy is to require utilities
that offer multiple residential rates annually to educate customers about the best rate for their
load profile. For example, Arizona Public Service notifies residential customers about their most
cost-efficient rate based on their historical use. 25 On-bill notifications and short videos that help
residential customers understand how to use their devices most cost-efficiently are other
strategies. Utilities could also offer free or subsidized smart home technologies for those on
residential demand charges so customers can respond to the rate (Interviewee A5, “Demand
Charges Interview”). Vehicle fleet electrification assessment services that help fleet managers
understand the investment and infrastructure requirements of electrifying are offered by several
utilities, including PG&E, National Grid, and Southern California Edison (Ackerman 2022;
PG&E, n.d.; National Grid, n.d.; Southern California Edison, n.d.).

However, even with comprehensive education campaigns, not all customers are interested in or
have the time and resources to learn about and respond to rates. In addition, educational outreach
expenditures may not be in proportion with customer need. One expert noted a 2024 energy
burden testimony and exhibit filed on behalf of the Alliance for a Green Economy in response to

communications budget (which includes customer education) went toward energy affordability
programs between 2021 and 2023—even though 24% of the utility’s customers live in
designated disadvantaged communities (DACs) (Niagara Mohawk Power Corporation d/b/a
National Grid - Exhibit 21 for Testimony on Energy Burden 2024).

5.3 Equitable Cost Allocation
The third principle states that classification of the total cost of service across customer
allocations should be fair and equitable. As noted in Section 2, the term “equitable” has always
incorporated broader notions of fairness but also means customers should pay for the costs that
they cause, which is related to the principle of efficient pricing.

The critique that demand charges are inadequate in representing the costs of actually serving the
peak is a relevant consideration for intraclass cost allocation (Interviewee A5, “Demand Charges
Interview”). Even in the case of the C&I sectors, a common argument is that the individual
customer’s peak is not necessarily aligned with the overall system peak and that other time-
varying rates may better reflect system costs. The counterpoint to this argument is that the
customer’s peak may be a reasonable proxy for local peaks on the distribution system, given
physical proximity of customers within the same class (Interviewee A10, “Demand Charges
Interview,” August 5, 2024, Virtual).

Historically, experts have accepted that NCP demand charges accurately reflect the cost to serve
large customers with high load factors, although there have been critiques about their accuracy in
capturing system peak load (Neufeld 1987; Borenstein 2016; LeBel et al. 2020). However,
although NCP demand charges may not capture system peak load, they may accurately represent
utility costs—including the grid impact of C&I customers that operate outside of peak hours
(Interviewee A10, “Demand Charges Interview”).

Demand charges are less common for residential customers. Although there was a resurgence in
residential demand charge interest in the mid-2010s as noted in Section 3.4, very few residential
customers are served on demand charge tariffs today. Shifting residential customers from
volumetric ($/kWh) to demand charge ($/kW) rates may result in wider variation across
residential customer electric bills because of lifestyle differences (Interviewee A7, “Demand
Charges Interview,” August 30, 2024, Virtual). Some experts note, however, that this level of
variation is heavily dependent on how the demand charge is designed (Interviewee A10,
“Demand Charges Interview”).

A 2016 pilot program in Colorado studied the implementation of residential demand charges and
residential volumetric TOU charges. The demand charge resulted in significant customer bill
volatility compared to bills before the pilot, sometimes creating bill changes of ±40%
(Interviewee A7, “Demand Charges Interview”). The bill volatility may be exacerbated for
customers in multifamily dwellings. In this case, the maximum load associated with short-
duration appliance use across multiple units within an hour is likely to exceed the maximum load
as measured at the transformer level. This means the maximum demand at a unit level may be
larger than that unit’s contribution at the transformer peak time, which determines transformer

 32

sizing requirements. Thus, under a demand charge rate, an individual customer’s bill may exceed
the cost of serving that individual customer (Chernick et al. 2016).

There is a key social equity issue illustrated by this. Multifamily tenants are mostly renters and
are unlikely to invest in on-site storage to avoid adverse cost impacts of demand charges
(Interviewee A5, “Demand Charges Interview”). These same customers have individual demands
that may be highly variable, but their service is provided at a point of delivery where other
customers’ demands are also served—and that diversity smooths out the variability of individual
customers. Because the utility transformer and service drop costs are based on the combined
loads of multiple customers, assigning these costs to individual customers based on individual
NCP demands is neither cost-based nor equitable. Ultimately, this and other factors led the
Colorado state utility commission to support a volumetric TOU charge rather than a demand
charge in the residential sector (Interviewee A7, “Demand Charges Interview”).

The functionalization of costs recovered (generation, transmission, distribution, etc.) also relates
to the principle of equitable cost allocation. Some interviewees argue residential demand charges
more accurately reflect the cost to maintain local distribution systems and may be inappropriate
to apply to generation and transmission cost recovery. The crux of this argument is that
residential customers’ individual NCP demand could impact the size of the utility’s transformer
but does not impact utility equipment beyond the transformer (Interviewee A5, “Demand
Charges Interview”). In cases where each customer is attached to one transformer (which is more
common for rural areas and C&I customers), there is a better correlation between individual
NCP demand and the sizing of the transformer (Interviewee A5, “Demand Charges Interview”).
However, the circumstances are different in urban and suburban spaces, where the transformer
serves 5 to 10 customers in areas comprising single-family homes—or significantly more in the
case of apartments (Interviewee A5, “Demand Charges Interview”).

Some utility territories offer demand charge holidays, wherein a utility reduces or negates the
phase-in rate of demand charges on charging stations in the first several years of the station’s
operation, allowing customers to pay volumetric charges instead. Although demand charge
holidays are widely viewed as a useful strategy for gradualism, implementing them in a fair way
that accounts for different customer needs while retaining the ability to equitably allocate cost
was noted as an important challenge in the electric vehicle fleet space (Interviewee A3, “Demand
Charges Interview”).

To address the issue of cost allocation, several interviewees expressed the opinion that demand
charges should always be opt-in or optional, and others supported offering a wide variety of rate
options to suit different customer lifestyles and preferences (EPRI 2011; Interviewee A8,
“Demand Charges Interview,” August 6, 2024, Virtual; Interviewee A9, “Demand Charges
Interview,” August 8, 2024, Virtual). While no one directly disagreed on this point, one
interviewee noted the opt-in percentages for customers on electricity rates are usually low.
Several oversight bodies have required that utilities periodically inform customers about their
optimal rate choice (APS 2017).

Finally, some experts feel that demand charges are not necessarily needed with metering that can
handle TOU volumetric rates, which are closer to real-time pricing and to cost causation
(Borenstein 2016). Others maintain that even with the ability to administer volumetric TOU

 33

rates, demand charges could still have a place in the electricity system as they relate to electric
vehicles or the local distribution system.

5.4 Encouraging Customer Response
The fourth principle holds that rates should encourage customers’ efficient use of energy that is
optimal for the electric system and society. This necessitates customer responsiveness to an
implemented rate and requires that the rate is designed to reduce system costs.

As noted in Section 1, a variety of rate designs include what we categorize as a “demand
charge.” Further, different demand charge designs encourage different customer behaviors.
Although all demand charges encourage the reduction of customer billing demand, flat demand
charges tend to smooth customer load while peak or peak window demand charges may move
load to low-cost hours and encourage the moderating of load during high-cost hours. All time-
varying rates (including peak or peak window demand charges and TOU volumetric rates) can
also encourage customers to reduce energy use at high-cost times—for example, through
delaying home vehicle charging and clothes drying. In the residential sector, NCP demand
charges often encourage customers to avoid the simultaneous use of multiple appliances;
however, at low coincidence factors, this does not necessarily reduce system costs or relate to
cost causation.

Another aspect of encouraging customer response is the concept of energy efficiency. Demand
charges do not directly incentivize energy efficiency because they do not encourage customers to
reduce their energy consumption; rather, they encourage customers to smooth their consumption
over a longer time period (or shift their consumption with peak window demand charges), which
could include shifting use to system peak hours when a noncoincident peak demand charge is
applied (Interviewee A2, “Demand Charges Interview”).

In the C&I sectors, some large customers have energy management systems that facilitate
their ability to respond to utility signals through rates, but many interviewees expressed the
need to reform high and rising residential retail electricity rates. However, there were various
opinions on what specific strategies to use in rate design. For example, some noted that,
because the average residential customer does not spend significant time or effort
understanding their electricity bill, rates should be kept simple—especially if encouraging
customer response to price signals is a goal (Interviewee A8, “Demand Charges Interview”).

Opinions also varied between the ability of residential customers to respond to demand charges.
Some interviewees expressed the need for transparency in residential rates and emphasized that
customers should have the opportunity to respond to rates, which is something they feel is not
always provided via individualized, noncoincident demand charges, even if customers have
technology such as smart meters. One expert in the nonprofit sector argued that noncoincident
residential demand charges do not give customers as much opportunity to change their behavior
as other alternatives, such as TOU rates. In terms of medium- and heavy-duty fleets, some are
more able shift load in response to a demand charge than others (e.g., large commercial fleets
with backup vehicles vs. small school bus fleets) (Interviewee A3, “Demand Charges
Interview”).

 34

However, some evidence suggests that residential customers will respond to price signals under
certain circumstances. Several studies have explored customer response to demand charges in the
residential sector. One study cites three early residential demand charge pilots—one from
Norway in 2006, one in Wisconsin in 1977 to 1978, and one in North Carolina in 1978 to 1983
(Hledik 2014). In these three pilots, customers reduced kW demand by 5%, 29%, and between
17% and 41%, respectively, and used a variety of load-shifting techniques to respond to demand
charges (Hledik 2014). Further research is needed to understand the applicability of these results
in the current electricity landscape as well as their dependency on price signal level (Hledik
2014).

Another study demonstrates that certain levels of utility price signals may elicit changes in
residential energy consumption patterns (McKenna et al. 2021). Changes included increasing air
conditioning setpoints during summer evenings, turning off air conditioning during summer
evenings, cooking later or earlier in the evening, limiting water heating during winter mornings,
and using laundry machines outside of peak hours (McKenna et al. 2021). The study finds
customers are more likely to shift energy consumption to off-peak hours when off-peak
electricity prices are at least 40% lower than on-peak prices (McKenna et al. 2021).

Although utility companies may encourage customer behavior modification using cost signals,
behavior modification may not always significantly lower electricity bills (Anderson et al. 2023).
The level of impact of behavioral changes is influenced by factors such as the efficiency of the
building envelope and its location (Anderson et al. 2023). For example, increasing the summer
cooling setpoint is less effective at reducing electricity use in a poorly insulated building located
in an urban heat island than an efficient building surrounded by trees (Anderson et al. 2023).

5.5 Trade-Offs Between Ratemaking Principles
As stated by several interviewees, ratemaking is not an exact science and involves balancing
many interests into a streamlined rate design or set of rate options. There are notable trade-offs
between the four principles that are discussed previously. For example, rate designs that make
revenue recovery more certain, such as ratcheted demand charges, could discourage customer
response and customer acceptance.

Although discouraging the use of demand-heavy technologies may encourage efficient behavior
from a grid-wide perspective, it could discourage customer acceptance, economic development
of nascent technologies, and electrification. For example, depending on specific load
characteristics of different electrification and DER end uses, demand charges could encourage
the development of certain technologies (e.g., heat pumps) while inhibiting others (e.g., DCFC
stations).

Another example, described by one interviewee, is that the optimal time to charge electric
vehicles may be a concentrated period overnight—particularly in wind-rich utility systems. This
would use low-cost energy and occur when the transmission and distribution system is lightly
loaded. However, a demand charge may encourage the consumer to spread that load across all
hours or move load to hours when other major appliances are not in use. This could put the
demand charge signal in tension with encouraging customers to charge at times that reduce strain
on the electricity system (Interviewee A5, “Demand Charges Interview”).

 35

Tension also exists between customer understanding and customer behavior. Even if a customer
understands the potential financial impacts of responding to a rate, certain enabling technologies
might be needed to make it convenient for the customer to respond. For example, several
interviewees suggested that automation of devices to enable “setting and forgetting it,” or
locationally targeted virtual power plants, could be a boon to customer response and efficient
behavior in the residential sector. Although AMI continues to become widespread, this level of
demand-response automation beyond AMI—to provide control and not just measurement—is not
yet widely available (Interviewee A1, “Demand Charges Interview”; Interviewee A3, “Demand
Charges Interview”; Interviewee A4, “Demand Charges Interview”; Interviewee A7, “Demand
Charges Interview”).

5.7 Demand Charge Alternatives
As the current energy transition proceeds and technologies change, policy experts expect
alternative retail rate structures to continue to develop. Although most experts see a continued
place for demand charges in the electricity sector going forward, they also argue that more
dynamic and time-based pricing might be needed to provide efficient signals in a future grid with
more variable resources and flexible loads. Some demand charge alternatives must be adopted
gradually to avoid tension with the “customer understanding and acceptance” principle.
Throughout the interviews that we conducted, commonly mentioned demand charge alternatives
included TOU volumetric rates, critical peak pricing, grid access charges, real-time pricing,
variable peak pricing, coincident peak charges, and subscription rates. These are each briefly
described next. 26 This following list is provided for informational purposes only and is not
intended to explore trade-offs, and the authors do not endorse any of the rate alternatives on the
list.

5.7.1 Other Types of Time-Varying Rates
 • Time-of-use volumetric rates. The magnitude of this electricity rate varies by the time
 of day. There is usually at least one “peak period” associated with a higher rate and an
 “off-peak period” with a lower rate. The rate is presented in units of $/kWh. Xcel Energy
 has a TOU program; during weekdays, the cost of energy varies based on the time of day.
 Salt River Project offers an EZ-3 rate, where customers choose their own 3-hour
 afternoon window (e.g., some choose 3–6 p.m., some 4–7 p.m., others 5–8 p.m.) All have
 5–6 p.m. in their peak window—the typical system peak hour—and the other benefits are
 spread across the rest of the 3–8 p.m. period.
 • Variable peak pricing. The energy prices are determined and may fluctuate daily,
 depending on the time of day and system conditions. This is similar to a TOU volumetric
 structure, except TOU is set in advance over a given period and varies only according to
 hour of day and day type (i.e., weekend vs. weekday, holidays). Oklahoma Gas and
 Electric offers variable peak pricing to customers in a program called SmartHours Daily,
 where the on-peak price changes by day (OG&E, n.d.).
 • Real-time pricing (RTP). The prices charged to customers frequently fluctuate to match
 either the wholesale market or utility production cost (U.S. DOE FEMP, n.d.). Georgia
 Power and Niagara Mohawk both helped pioneer RTP for C&I customers (Barbose et al.
 2005). Commonwealth Edison Company and Ameren in Illinois have offered optional
 RTP to residential customers for more than a decade, but uptake has been low (Star et al.
 2010). Octopus Energy, based in the United Kingdom but also operating as an electricity

 retailer in Texas, offers customers a combination of real-time prices and real-time
 controls and has gained customers by guaranteeing savings (Octopus Energy 2024).
 • Coincident peak (CP) charges. These rates are based on the customer’s energy
 consumption during the system peak hour every month of the year (12CP rates), or the
 system peak hour in the three or four highest demand months of the year (3CP or 4CP
 rates). This rate structure provides a signal of the cost associated with system constraints
 and encourages customers to reduce demand during system peak periods. For example,

 • Critical peak pricing (CPP). The utility notifies customers ahead of time (usually a day

 events, the kWh charge may be higher than during non-event hours (SMUD, n.d.).

5.7.2 Other Related Cost Recovery Mechanisms
 • Subscription pricing. This is an alternative rate structure that offers customers a
 monthly fixed billing option (Fox-Penner et al. 2020). Customers choose a subscription
 level, and their usage is limited to a certain amount of electricity usage per month, which
 can be measured in either kWh or kW. 27 Subscription pricing reduces variability in
 billing and may be easy for customers to understand. From the utility’s perspective, it
 provides revenue stability.
 • Variations on fixed charges. This is a line item that is often present in a rate structure to
 recover the cost of connection to and maintenance of the electricity grid. It can also be
 referred to as a grid access charge, customer charge, monthly minimum charge, basic
 charge, or service charge and can relate to several costs that cover a customer’s grid
 connection. An alternative to demand charge application is to increase the fixed charge or
 use a grid access charge to recover similar costs.
Going forward, rate design could evolve in several directions. One option is the use of time-
varying rates that send customers increasingly accurate signals about system operating
conditions (i.e., TOU rates, CPP). On the other hand, very simple rates (such as subscription
rates) could be combined with direct utility-/aggregator-control of certain aspects of loads (i.e.,
thermostats, vehicle charging, batteries). Different types of customers may be better equipped to
respond to and understand various pricing signals.

6 Conclusion and Future Work
In this report, we explore the historical context and current implementation of electric utility
demand charges, document how demand charge rate structures have evolved over time, and
organize interview insights from relevant parties to understand the benefits and challenges of
demand charges amid the backdrop of an evolving electricity technology mix. This report adds to
a body of work on how demand charges have changed throughout history and how they
correspond to different ratemaking principles. Although we relay some expert opinions that
speculate about how demand charges might change or stay the same in the future, a forward-
looking analysis is largely left as an area of future work.

The key takeaways of this report are as follows:

 • Since the inception of demand charges as a rate element, experts have discussed their
 ability to reduce system peak load when applied to individual noncoincident customer
 peaks.
 • For both vertically integrated and distribution-only investor-owned utilities, demand
 charges are significant in cost recovery, especially in the C&I sectors. If demand charges
 were to go away, that revenue would still need to be recovered through other avenues.
 • For customers with low load factors such as DCFC station owners experiencing low
 utilization rates, demand charges can result in particularly high costs.
 • The majority of demand charges in the United States are applied to the C&I sector, but
 with increasing use of residential demand charges in certain utility territories, there is an
 ongoing debate about whether the technology exists to enable customer response,
 customer understanding, and the level of response that would occur even with that
 technology.
 • In locations that have residential demand charges as an option, opt-in rates are low (often
 less than 1% of residential customers).
 • Given AMI technology, other price signals can achieve similarly effective cost recovery
 to demand charges.
 • With the increase of new technologies such as battery energy storage, there is a question
 about the continued applicability of demand charges as a revenue stability mechanism for
 utilities in the future.
 • Locations with higher rates in general tend to have higher demand charges, with a few
 exceptions.
 • Although all demand charges encourage the reduction of customer billing demand, flat
 demand charges tend to smooth customer load while peak or peak window demand
 charges may move load to low-cost hours and encourage the moderating of load during
 high-cost hours. All time-varying rates (including peak or peak window demand charges
 and TOU volumetric rates) can also encourage customers to reduce energy use at high-
 cost times.
This report discusses the factors that led to the current state of demand charges and provides
context on how we got to where we are today. Further work is necessary to answer the “whys”
associated with demand charge implementation, motivations, and approval processes. As we
look to the future, the evolving landscape for technology and public policy points to important

 40

research questions about the merits of various demand charge options as well as the relative
merits of alternatives:

 1. How will twenty-first century aspirations and events influence the evolution and
 prioritization of ratemaking principles and policy goals? What adjustments may be called
 for in the near or longer term?

 2. How can customer energy management technology, including batteries, improve the
 ability of customers to respond to or avoid demand charges?

 3. How do technological responses to demand charges improve or degrade the alignment of
 demand charges with cost causation? With policy goals?

 4. How does the signal contained within demand charges to moderate or smooth high
 electricity usage over short periods potentially benefit the electric system? Is it consistent
 with a transition to a high level of variable renewable energy, such as day-centric solar or
 night-centric wind power?

 5. What innovations might be possible with respect to demand charge structure
 implementation?

 6. If the customer can significantly reduce or avoid demand charges using battery energy
 storage, does this proportionally reduce system costs, or does it negatively impact the
 ability of demand charges to be a stable cost recovery mechanism?

 7. How do factors such as changes in weather patterns and the increased length of heat
 waves interplay with different modern retail rate options such as demand charges?

 8. How could widespread application of demand charges into a population with
 heterogeneous ability to respond to such rates lead to cost shifting between customers?

 9. What are the impacts of demand charges on various technological investments, such as
 smart appliances, distributed solar panels, battery storage, and heat pumps? What will the
 effect of these technologies be on the viability of demand charges as a stable revenue
 source for utilities?

 10. How does the length of the peak period relate to the magnitude of the demand charge?

 11. What is the average demand charge rate on-peak versus off-peak in key regions of the
 United States? To what extent are on-peak versus off-peak ratios designed (i.e., chosen
 ahead of time based on assumptions about how people will respond) versus calculated
 (i.e., based on observed or projected costs and noncoincident peaks in the on- and off-
 peak periods)?

These questions will be used to guide future research.

 variable costs) and ensure a fair rate of return (to both cover its costs

 43

 and earn a profit) on capital investments. The public utility
 commission determines the allowable rate of return for each utility.

 Residential sector An energy-consuming sector that consists of living quarters for private
 households. Common uses of energy associated with this sector
 include space heating, water heating, air conditioning, lighting,
 refrigeration, cooking, and running a variety of other appliances.

 Restructured electricity A type of electricity market wherein regulated utilities divest their
 market generation, and independent generators participate in the wholesale
 markets.

 Retail electricity rates Define the price paid by an electricity consumer via their electricity
 bill.

 Revenue requirement The total cost for an investor-owned utility to serve customers,
 including a reasonable rate of return that is determined by the
 regulating body. The utility collects the revenue requirement from
 customers in the form of electricity rates.

 Time varying demand charge This refers to whether the demand charge amount ($/kW) varies by
 hour of the day or day type (i.e., weekend vs. weekday, holidays).

 Time-of-use volumetric rate The magnitude of this electricity rate varies by the time of day. There
 is usually at least one “peak period” associated with a higher rate and
 an “off-peak period” associated with a lower rate. The rate is presented
 in units of $/kWh.

 Utilization rate The percentage of time used compared to the total time available. This
 is often applied to vehicle charging stations because the utilization rate
 can be low (only in use for a few hours per day). This high-power
 usage during short time intervals can result in peaky demand. When
 demand charges are applied at stations with low utilization rates,
 demand charges can make up a majority of the electricity bill, and the
 cost to operate a DCFC station can outpace the revenue that the
 stations can obtain.

 Vertically integrated utility A utility that owns generation, transmission, and distribution and
 provides electricity to customers.

 Volumetric rate A type of electricity rate that is based on a metric of kilowatt-hour
 (kWh) energy usage.

 Wholesale electricity rates Define the price paid by an electricity distributor to a power generator
 at a bulk level.
