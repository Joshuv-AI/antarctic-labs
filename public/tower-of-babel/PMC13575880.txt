Effects of Chemotherapy on Survival Outcomes in Older Patients with Metastatic Pancreatic Cancer: A Nationwide Population-Based Study
Authors: Kangju Son, Byung Kyu Park, Chan Su Park, Joung-Ho Han, Kyong Joo Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/AimsClinical data on chemotherapy outcomes in older patients with pancreatic cancer are limited. This population-based study aimed to evaluate the effects of chemotherapy on survival outcomes in older patients with metastatic pancreatic cancer (mPC).Methods Data from the National Health Insurance Service linked to the Korea Central Cancer Registry (2012 to 2019) were used. We assessed chemotherapy utilization and overall survival (OS) across various age groups and chemotherapy regimens.ResultsAmong 20,549 patients with mPC, 8,652 received chemotherapy, including 711 and 163 patients aged 76–80 and ≥81 years, respectively. In the chemotherapy group, the median OS (mOS) was 9, 8, 7, and 6 months for the 21–70, 71–75, 76–80, and ≥81-year age groups, respectively (p<0.001). In contrast, untreated patients had an mOS of only 2 months across all age groups. Propensity score matching for patients aged ≥76 years revealed that chemotherapy was associated with a significantly longer mOS compared with no treatment (7 months vs 2 months hazard ratio, 0.3; 95% confidence interval, 0.271 to 0.333; p<0.001). Among patients treated with gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel, the mOS was 11, 10, 9, and 7 months across the age groups, respectively (p<0.001). For those receiving the FOLFIRINOX regimen, the mOS was 12, 11, and 10 months in patients aged 21–70, 71–75, and 76–80 years, respectively (p=0.001).ConclusionsThese real-world data suggest that chemotherapy is effective in older patients with mPC. Notably, chemotherapy with gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel and FOLFIRINOX provided substantial survival benefits even in patients aged 76–80 years, with only marginal differences compared to younger cohorts.Keywords: Pancreatic neoplasms, Aged, Antineoplastic combined chemotherapy protocols, Survival analysis, Cohort studies

INTRODUCTION

The population is aging, with the number and proportion of older people increasing in almost every country worldwide. As health improves and life expectancy increases globally, the overall incidence of pancreatic cancer (PC) is increasing.1 Over the past few decades, the global burden of PC in older people has clearly risen.2

Most patients with PC are diagnosed in an unresectable state. Chemotherapy is the mainstay treatment for patients with PC and has evolved over the past decade. After gemcitabine monotherapy (Gem) became the first-line chemotherapy for unresectable PC in 1997,3 FOLFIRINOX (fluorouracil, leucovorin, irinotecan, and oxaliplatin) and gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel (GnP) were subsequently introduced as the standard treatment.4,5

Although PC treatment is advancing, treatment in older patients remains challenging due to the lack of evidence for the safety and efficacy of chemotherapy in clinical trials including this population. The older age group has been underrepresented in oncological clinical trials.6,7 The median age of participants in clinical trials is 6–8 years younger than that of the overall patient population.8,9 Thus, most randomized clinical trials investigating appropriate PC treatments often exclude older patients. Patients with PC aged >75 years are rarely included in phase III clinical trials.4,5 Therefore, studies on the efficacy of chemotherapy in older patients are limited, and a standard chemotherapy regimen for older patients with metastatic PC (mPC) has not yet been established. As older patients have more comorbidities and poorer physical performance status (PS) than younger patients, the results of trials conducted mainly among younger patients cannot be extrapolated to older patients. Retrospective studies on chemotherapy in older patients with PC are limited.10-20 The definition of “older patients” varies across studies, the sample sizes are small, and the results are variable. Most significantly, data regarding FOLFIRINOX or GnP efficacy in patients aged >75 years are exceedingly limited.10,11,20 Furthermore, nationwide population-based studies on the treatment of older patients with PC are scarce. In particular, the lack of clear evidence-based guidelines is concerning, given the data suggesting the survival benefit of chemotherapy in older patients. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate survival outcomes in older patients with mPC receiving chemotherapy using a national population-based cohort.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

1. Data sources

Data from the National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) from 2011 to 2020, linked to data from the Korea Central Cancer Registry (KCCR) from 2011 to 2019 (NHIS-2022-8-009), were used. To prevent personal identification, the linked dataset provided ages in 5-year intervals, aggregating dates by month. These two data points are detailed in our recently published report.21 This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of National Health Insurance Service Ilsan Hospital (IRB number: NHIMC 2023-09-011). Informed consent was waived by the board because anonymous raw data were obtained from the NHIS.

2. Study population

Patients diagnosed with mPC who initiated chemotherapy between January 1, 2012, and December 31, 2019, were included. Patients with PC were identified by cross-referencing the KCCR and NHIS databases. Specifically, we included patients registered with PC in the KCCR between 2011 and 2019, who also held both the ICD-10 (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision) code C25 and the V193 code in the NHIS from 2012 to 2019. The V193 code is mandatory for all cancer patients in South Korea to receive expanded health insurance benefits. Among the patients with PC, those aged <20 years, those with missing data, and those with pancreatic neuroendocrine tumors (PNETs) were excluded. Missing data were defined as instances where key variables, such as the specific month of PC diagnosis or chemotherapy initiation, were absent from the database. PNETs were identified based on histological confirmation registered in the KCCR or the presence of at least three separate claims under the PNET-specific diagnosis code (C254) in the NHIS. mPC was defined as cases in which the summary stage in the KCCR was classified as “distant.” Among patients with mPC, those who had undergone pancreatic resection (identified by procedure codes) or initiated chemotherapy after 2019 were excluded from the study. Older patients were defined as those aged ≥76 years.

3. Chemotherapy and study outcome

Administration of chemotherapy was defined as cases in which a chemotherapy injection prescription code (KK151–156) or anticancer agent was claimed within 6 months of PC diagnostic code. To ensure consistency in treatment, patients were defined as having received chemotherapy only if they had at least three claims for the same anticancer agent.21,22 The chemotherapy regimens were divided into Gem, gemcitabine plus erlotinib (Gem-E), GnP, FOLFIRINOX, S-1, and other drugs. The number of chemotherapy cycles administered to each patient was calculated by reviewing the claim frequency for each anticancer agent and considering the regimen cycle. The duration of chemotherapy was defined as the interval from the registration of the initial claim to that of the final claim for the anticancer agents. The study outcome was overall survival (OS), defined as the time from the month of PC diagnosis to the month of death or last follow-up. The patients were followed up until December 2020 or until death.

4. Propensity score matching analysis in older patients with mPC

Propensity score matching was performed to compare the effect of chemotherapy on survival in older patients (≥76 years) with mPC. The adjusted variables included sex, age group, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), and year of diagnosis.

5. Statistical analysis

The patients were categorized into the following age groups: 21–70, 71–75, 76–80, and ≥81 years. The years of PC diagnosis were divided into four periods: 2012–2013, 2014–2015, 2016–2017, and 2018–2019. Chemotherapy trends were analyzed every 2 years across different age groups; however, for patients aged ≥81 years, claims were aggregated every 4 years due to the small number of cases and concerns regarding personal identification. Descriptive statistics for each variable are presented for all patients and by age group, with differences analyzed using the chi-square test. Survival was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier survival curves, and differences were tested using the log-rank test. The median OS (mOS) and survival rates at 6 months and 1, 2, and 3 years were reported in each analysis. The chi-square test was conducted to examine the homogeneity of matching variables before and after matching. Homogeneity was considered to be achieved if the absolute value of the standardized difference was ≤0.1. After matching, survival outcomes between the groups were compared using Kaplan-Meier survival curves and the log-rank test. Hazard ratios between groups were calculated using Cox regression analysis, excluding confounding variables used in the matching process. p-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant. All analyses were conducted using the SAS software (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

RESULTS

1. Study population

Between 2011 and 2019, 54,396 patients were registered in the KCCR for PC, of whom 49,589 were identified with PC codes and V193 code in the NHIS between 2012 and 2019. After excluding patients under 20 years of age, with PNET, and with missing data, 47,303 patients were included in the PC cohort. From this cohort, 21,899 patients were identified as having metastatic disease, categorized as “distant” according to the KCCR summary stage. After excluding patients who underwent pancreatic resection (n=1,281) and those who initiated chemotherapy after 2019 (n=69), a total of 20,549 patients with mPC were eligible for inclusion in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of the study population.

2. Baseline characteristics of patients with mPC by age group

The baseline characteristics of 20,549 patients with mPC are presented in Table 1. According to age group, 10,763 patients (52.4%) were aged 21–70 years, 3,584 patients (17.4%) were aged 71–75 years, 3,197 patients (15.6%) were aged 76–80 years, and 3,005 patients (14.6%) were aged ≥81 years. Overall, males accounted for 55.7% of the patients, and 44.3% were female. In patients aged 76–80 years, 49.1% were male and 50.9% were female. In patients aged ≥81 years, females were dominant at 63.2%. The number of patients increased with time, with the lowest number being 4,095 (19.9%) in 2012–2013; in contrast, 2018–2019 had the highest number of patients at 6,191 (30.1%). In particular, the increase in the number of patients was more evident with increasing patient age. Of all patients with mPC, 8,652 (42.1%) received chemotherapy. The chemotherapy rate by age group was 59.9% for patients aged 21–70 years, 37.2% for those aged 71–75 years, 22.2% for those aged 76–80 years, and only 5.4% for those aged ≥81 years. The rate of chemotherapy decreased with age.

3. Chemotherapy rates in age groups by year

The chemotherapy rate increased across all age groups from 2012 to 2019. The rate increased from 56.0% in 2012 to 64.6% in patients aged 21–70 years, from 32.5% to 47.0% in those aged 71–75 years, and from 19.7% to 32.2% in those aged 76–80 years. The rate of increase was the smallest in those aged ≥81 years, from 4.9% to 6.0% (Supplementary Fig. 1).

4. Chemotherapy trends and cycles by age group

In all age groups, Gem and Gem-E were the main anticancer regimens used until 2016. In Korea, GnP was introduced in 2016 and FOLFIRINOX in 2017, and their usage increased thereafter (Supplementary Fig. 2). Regarding chemotherapy regimens in 2018–2019, GnP and FOLFIRINOX were administered to 57.9% and 33.4% of patients aged 21–70 years, respectively. As age increased, the use of FOLFIRINOX declined notably, recorded at 22.9% and 14.5% for patients aged 71–75 years and 76–80 years, respectively. During the same period, the combined utilization of these two regimens also decreased across age groups: 91.3% (21–70 years), 86.8% (71–75 years), and 74.5% (76–80 years). In the oldest cohort (≥81 years, 2016–2019), only 37.1% received either GnP (32.7%) or FOLFIRINOX (4.4%). Due to the insufficient sample size (n=5 for FOLFIRINOX), this group was excluded from subsequent statistical analyses to ensure integrity.

The median cycles of GnP administered by age group were 5 for patients aged 21–70 years and 4, 3.7, and 3 cycles for those aged 71–75, 76–80, and ≥81 years, respectively. In the case of FOLFIRINOX, there were 9, 8, and 7 cycles for those aged 21–70, 71–75, and 76–80 years, respectively, indicating that the older the patient’s age, the shorter the administered chemotherapy cycle. The chemotherapy duration was similar to that of the chemotherapy cycle (Table 2).

5. OS by age group, according to chemotherapy status

In patients who received chemotherapy, the mOS was 9, 8, 7, and 6 months for those aged 21–70, 71–75, 76–80, and ≥81 years, respectively (p<0.001). The corresponding 1-year survival rates in these age groups were 37.9%, 28.4%, 26.6%, and 22.7%, indicating a longer survival time in younger age groups. In patients not receiving chemotherapy, the mOS was 2 months in all age groups, showing no significant difference, and the corresponding 1-year survival rates were 7.5%, 3.4%, 2.9%, and 2.0% (Fig. 2).

6. OS among age groups by chemotherapy regimen

The OS was compared by age group for each chemotherapy regimen. In the Gem group, there was no significant difference in the survival curves according to age group (p=0.082). In the Gem-E group, the mOS was 7, 6, 5, and 5 months in patients aged 21–70, 71–75, 76–80, and ≥81 years, respectively, showing a slight difference (p<0.001). In the GnP group, the mOS by age group was 11, 10, 9, and 7 months, respectively (p<0.001). In the FOLFIRINOX group, the mOS by age group was 12, 11, and 10 months, in patients aged 21–70, 71–75, and 76–80 years, respectively (p=0.001) (Fig. 3).

7. OS of chemotherapy regimens across age groups

Next, the mOS was analyzed according to chemotherapy regimens in each age group. In patients aged 21–70 and 71–75 years, chemotherapy with GnP and FOLFIRINOX was associated with a significant survival benefit compared to chemotherapy with Gem and Gem-E. In the older age group, the mOS according to the chemotherapy regimens in patients aged 76–80 years was 7, 6, 9, and 10 months for Gem, Gem-E, GnP, and FOLFIRINOX, respectively (p<0.001). Similar to the ≤75-year age group, survival improvement with GnP and FOLFIRINOX was observed in patients aged 76–80 years. In patients aged ≥81 years, it was 5.5, 5, and 7 months for Gem, Gem-E, and GnP, respectively (p=0.079) (Fig. 4).

8. Propensity score matching in older patients with mPC

In patients with mPC aged ≥76 years, a 1-to-1 propensity score matching was performed using sex, age group, CCI, and the year of diagnosis. Before matching, 5,328 patients did not receive chemotherapy and 874 patients received chemotherapy. Moreover, there were statistically significant differences among all variables between the two groups, except for CCI (p<0.001). After 1:1 matching, 874 patients from the group not receiving chemotherapy and 874 patients from the group receiving chemotherapy were selected. No statistically significant difference was noted between the two groups; the p-value of each variable was 1.0, and the standardized difference was 0 (Supplementary Table 1). After matching, the mOS of the group not receiving chemotherapy (n=874) was 2 months, and that of the group receiving chemotherapy (n=874) was 7 months. The 6-month survival rates were 12.0% and 66.7%, and the 1-year survival rates were 2.8% and 25.9%, respectively. The hazard ratio for death was 0.3 (95% confidence interval, 0.271 to 0.333; p<0.001) in the group receiving chemotherapy (Supplementary Fig. 3).

DISCUSSION

Most clinical trials on PC treatment have been conducted primarily in younger patients, resulting in a lack of evidence regarding optimal treatment strategies for older patients. In routine clinical practice, patients and physicians must often make treatment decisions for older patients without solid empirical guidance, which may lead to excessive or insufficient treatment. Consequently, this study aimed to provide real-world evidence regarding the survival outcomes of chemotherapy in older patients with mPC.

This population-based study focused solely on patients with mPC, evaluating survival outcomes through a comparative survival analysis categorized by age group. Older patients with mPC who received chemotherapy showed improved survival compared to those who did not. While GnP and FOLFIRINOX in patients aged 76–80 years yielded an mOS that was 1–2 months shorter than in younger cohorts, both regimens conferred a significant survival advantage over Gem and Gem-E.

The age threshold considered when defining “older patients” varies across studies; while some studies employed a 65-year cutoff,12,18,23 the majority used 7015,17,19,24-28 or 7510,11,14,16,29-33 years as the cutoff. In particular, the pivotal phase III trial of FOLFIRINOX restricted enrollment of patients only up to the age of 75 years, thereby excluding those aged ≥76 years.4 To address this gap in clinical evidence, the present study specifically defined “older patients” as those aged ≥76 years. Furthermore, we divided the patients into four age groups. This stratification was strategically adopted to allow for a comparative evaluation of patients aged 71–75 years and those in the most advanced age group (≥81 years). Among the 20,549 patients with mPC, 6,202 (30.2%) were aged ≥76 years, and 874 of them received chemotherapy. To date, chemotherapy studies for older patients with PC have involved relatively small cohorts, with a maximum of 344 participants including those aged ≥65 years.23 When the definition of older patients was restricted to those aged ≥75 years, the sample size was substantially reduced, ranging from 9 to 178.10,11,14,16,29-33 To the best of our knowledge, the 874 older patients with mPC treated with chemotherapy in this study constitute the largest number of patients.

Older patients tend to receive less chemotherapy than younger patients.14,24,34,35 This finding was consistently observed in this study. Older patients with PC who received chemotherapy survived longer than those who did not. Previous studies on patients with mPC aged ≥75 years demonstrated that chemotherapy significantly extended mOS compared to conservative care (5.8 months vs 2.6 months and 7.5 months vs 4.1 months; p<0.001).14,16 In the present study, the mOS for patients receiving chemotherapy was 7 and 6 months in patients aged 76–80 and ≥81 years, respectively. While these figures were slightly lower than those of younger cohorts (8–9 months), they remained substantially longer than the 2 months observed across all age groups without chemotherapy. Following propensity score matching among patients aged ≥76 years (n=874), the group receiving chemotherapy exhibited a significantly longer mOS of 7 months, compared to 2 months in the group not receiving chemotherapy. The hazard ratio for death was 0.3, indicating substantially reduced mortality risk. These real-world findings demonstrate that chemotherapy confers a survival benefit even in older patients with mPC.

In this study, Gem did not result in a significant difference in survival according to age group, and the mOS was 5.5 to 7.0 months (p=0.082). Gem has been the most commonly used chemotherapy for older patients, especially those with an Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score of 2 or higher.36 In patients with mPC aged ≥75 years, Gem showed an mOS of 4.7 and 6.9 months.26,30 Gem remains the standard treatment for older patients even after the introduction of GnP and FOLFIRINOX.36

The phase III trial of GnP (MPACT) included only 10% of patients aged ≥75 years. Therefore, the evidence for GnP chemotherapy in patients ≥75 years is insufficient.5 In a study reporting the outcome of GnP in 116 patients aged ≥75 years with advanced PC in Japan, the mOS was 24.8 months in patients with locally advanced PC and 13.3 months in those with mPC.11 Several retrospective studies involving patients aged ≥75 years with unresectable or metastatic PC have reported an mOS of 9.2 to 12.0 months with GnP, showing comparable outcomes to previous overall patient populations.31-33 In this study, 247 patients aged 76–80 years and 37 patients aged ≥81 years received GnP for mPC, representing a substantially larger sample size of older patients compared to prior literature. The mOS was 9 months for patients aged 76–80 years and 7 months for those aged ≥81 years. These figures are comparable to the 8.5 months reported by the MPACT study,5 although shorter than the 13.3 months observed in the previous Japanese study.11 Therefore, GnP could be a preferable chemotherapy regimen for older patients who maintain a favorable PS.

Evidence supporting the use of FOLFIRINOX in older patients is remarkably limited, with only one study conducted in patients aged ≥75 years. Mizrahi et al.10 reported an mOS of 11.6 months among 24 patients aged ≥75 years with unresectable PC treated with FOLFIRINOX. When the age threshold is lowered, the mOS ranged from 11.6 to 13.3 months in patients aged ≥70 years,17,28 reaching 14.4 months in those aged ≥65 years.12 In the present study, the 56 patients aged 76–80 years treated with FOLFIRINOX achieved an mOS of 10 months, representing a marginal reduction by 1 to 2 months compared to younger cohorts. As this study exclusively enrolled patients with mPC, these outcomes are largely consistent with the mOS of 11.6 months reported by Mizrahi et al.10 in patients with unresectable PC. Furthermore, the observed OS was longer than that associated with Gem or Gem-E. Consequently, FOLFIRINOX demonstrates significant clinical efficacy in patients aged 76–80 years with a favorable PS, indicating a survival benefit comparable to that observed in younger populations. However, FOLFIRINOX was administered to only 14.5% of the patients aged 76–80 years between 2018 and 2019, thus limiting the overall cohort size. Further research is necessary to optimize patient selection for FOLFIRINOX in this age group. The clinical efficacy of FOLFIRINOX in patients aged ≥81 years could not be reliably assessed due to the insufficient sample size (n=5).

Among patients aged ≥81 years, chemotherapy remained notably infrequent and showed negligible growth between 2012 and 2019. Although survival was prolonged in patients who received chemotherapy compared to those who did not, analyzing the comparative efficacy of specific regimens was challenging because of the remarkably small number of treated patients in this group.

In this study, the number of chemotherapy cycles for GnP and FOLFIRINOX, which indirectly reflects the treatment intensity, decreased proportionally with advancing age. This finding indicates that sustained exposure to GnP and FOLFIRINOX is closely linked to improved survival, highlighting the importance of treatment tolerability in older patients.

Adverse events associated with chemotherapy are pivotal considerations in determining chemotherapy in older patients. Despite their importance, information on these events was not accessible in this study owing to limitations of the claim data. In a study of 116 Japanese patients aged ≥75 years treated with GnP, grade 4 hematological toxicity occurred in 8.6%, and grade 3–4 nonhematological toxicity occurred in 19.8%, suggesting tolerable toxicity.11 However, the safety of FOLFIRINOX in older patients has not been established. The discontinuation rates of FOLFIRINOX due to adverse events in older patients were 31.0% to 41.7%.10,28 FOLFIRINOX displays a 45.8% incidence of grade 3–4 hematological adverse events in patients aged ≥75 years, but the results are similar to those in the general population.10 The administration of chemotherapy in older patients with PC demands rigorous clinical attention; consequently, further investigations are necessary for establishing the safety profile of chemotherapy, particularly FOLFIRINOX.

While this study lacked direct data on PS, the strict reimbursement criteria for GnP and FOLFIRINOX in South Korea, which are limited to ECOG score 0 or 1, suggest that patients treated with these regimens likely maintained a favorable PS. Beyond a simple ECOG assessment, current geriatric oncology guidelines advocate for geriatric assessment to detect underlying vulnerabilities.37,38

This study has some limitations that warrant further consideration. First, the median age could not be presented because it was provided in 5-year increments. Second, because day-specific records were absent, survival was measured in months. While this may reduce the accuracy of the survival period slightly, the overall trends and relative differences remain statistically valid. Third, due to data limitations, we were unable to account for PS, a critical prognostic factor that influences survival outcomes.36 Fourth, an analysis of the safety of chemotherapy could not be performed due to data limitations. Fifth, because comorbidities were identified based on diagnostic coding records, there is an inherent risk of underestimation due to potential undercoding of pre-existing conditions. Sixth, older patients frequently experience dose reductions or treatment delays;10,11,28 however, specific data regarding these modifications were not available in the present study. Instead, this study analyzed the cycle and duration of chemotherapy as proxy measures for treatment exposure and intensity. Seventh, because patients in the chemotherapy group had to survive until the initiation of treatment, the possibility of immortal-time bias cannot be entirely excluded. Eighth, OS was calculated from diagnosis rather than chemotherapy initiation. Delayed chemotherapy in older patients may have introduced lead-time bias, potentially overestimating OS. Finally, survival may have been influenced by residual unmeasured confounding factors, including disease burden (e.g., tumor size and metastasis sites), detailed socioeconomic status, supportive care, and subsequent treatment. Furthermore, survival outcomes may have been influenced by physical and social factors that determine treatment eligibility rather than by the chemotherapy itself; therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution due to potential selection bias.

Nevertheless, this study has several distinct strengths. First, it is the first national-scale study to focus on older patients with PC since the introduction of GnP and FOLFIRINOX. Second, we employed 5-year intervals, enabling a more granular and precise comparison of survival outcomes across age groups. Third, this study selected only mPC by linking the NHIS and KCCR, and provided real-world data with statistical significance by including a large number of patients nationwide. Finally, we provided a comprehensive overview of chemotherapy trends and age-specific survival outcomes, offering valuable clinical insights into the management of older patients with mPC.

In conclusion, this nationwide population-based study demonstrated that chemotherapy is associated with significantly improved survival in older patients with mPC, providing robust real-world evidence that supports its clinical utility in this age group. Specifically, chemotherapy with GnP and FOLFIRINOX showed substantial survival benefits even in patients aged 76–80 years, with a marginal difference compared to younger cohorts. While improved survival was also observed in patients aged ≥81 years, the clinical uptake of chemotherapy remains extremely low. Chemotherapy must be strongly recommended for older patients with a favorable PS. Future prospective studies incorporating geriatric assessment tools are warranted to refine patient selection and develop optimized chemotherapeutic strategies for older patients.
