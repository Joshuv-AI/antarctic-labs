The impact of large language models on collaborative learning processes and outcomes in higher education for English translation
Authors: Xunlian Quan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Collaborative learning is an important teaching method in English translation in Chinese higher education and is affected by the development of Large Language Models (LLMs). Previous studies mainly focus on the application of LLMs by individual students, but there is a lack of information about the influence of these models on the group dynamics when they are used in collaborative work. To fill this gap, our research examines the effect of an LLM, as a third participant, on the collaborative learning procedures and results. A comparative experimental design is conducted, involving 372 undergraduate and graduate students majoring in English in China, who are randomly divided into two groups: a control group working together without the LLM and an experimental group where the LLM is included in a human-human-AI collaboration team. The translation quality, questionnaire data and process records of both groups are analyzed by a mixed-method approach to make a comparison. The findings show that there is no significant difference in the final translation quality between the two groups, but the experimental group reports a significantly higher perceived efficiency and task satisfaction. A qualitative analysis clarifies this result, indicating that the participation of the LLM changes the collaborative procedure. This change involves a transition from a generative to an evaluative negotiation in the decision-making process, and also leads to the formation of informal roles. The study concludes that in collaborative learning, LLMs affect the process rather than enhance the content directly. These findings are of great importance for the design of effective human-ai English translation teaching in the Chinese higher education environment.

Introduction

In the field of higher education for English translation, collaborative learning is considered an important part of constructivist teaching methods1. According to the social constructivism theory, knowledge is obtained through communication and interaction. The translation of English documents is not only an algorithmic process, but it includes complex cognitive and social activities like cultural understanding, meaning negotiation and creative problem-solving2. Thus, the translation problem can be effectively solved by considering different viewpoints3. Group work provides an appropriate setting for these activities, enabling students to communicate, express their opinions and reach an agreement on the original text. Therefore, this cooperative approach is significant for enhancing the professional skills needed for handling complexities in English translation4.

The current educational system is influenced by the application of Generative Artificial Intelligence (AI), especially Large Language Models (LLMs)5. These models are trained on a vast amount of English and other language data and are commonly used by students in higher education, performing excellently in content creation and instant feedback6. These technological developments provide many opportunities for personalized learning and improve efficiency. However, they also bring some problems such as the issue of academic integrity and the potential of cognitive offloading, where students may depend on AI without fully participating in the cognitive processes7. In the teaching of translation, it is worried that the frequent use of AI may hinder the growth of critical negotiation and problem-solving abilities, which are supposed to be improved by cooperative learning8.

Most previous studies focus on the advantages of LLMs as auxiliary tools to enhance the efficiency and quality of individual translators in English translation education9. But these studies ignore the possible changes of LLMs in collaborative situations. The ability of current LLMs to carry out deep, dialogical conversations makes them have characteristics similar to participants, allowing them to actively participate in the process. This transformation from a tool to a participant turns LLMs into a ‘third collaborator’ in a student group, forming a new 'Human-Human-AI’ triad which has the potential to redefine collaborative learning10.

Though the capability of this model is evident, the specific impacts of the newly formed cooperative system on the educational procedures have not been studied thoroughly up to now. Currently, the application of AI in education mainly focuses on the interaction between an individual learner and the AI, while cooperative applications are seldom observed11. However, systematic reviews carried out in the last two decades indicate that long-term use of AI techniques can support collaborative learning12. Moreover, the appearance of 'social generative AI’ offers a new theoretical basis for integrating these models in joint educational programs13. However, from the latest research, there is a considerable shortage of data concerning the functions of these models in a social learning setting14. Without a thorough knowledge of these novel tripartite relationships, the teachers might establish unsuitable teaching strategies and fail to adequately prepare the students for the future workplace where human-AI cooperation will be frequent15. Consequently, the primary research question is how the presence of an LLM affects the interaction between human learners and thereby influences their learning results?

This study investigates the impact of an LLM’s involvement on the collaborative processes and ultimate accomplishments in a higher education context for English translation. By using a comparative experimental design, the investigation presents empirical evidence and theoretical views to assist in improving the efficient Human-AI cooperative teaching methods in the intelligent era. In the subsequent part of this paper, the related literature will be summarized, followed by the description of the method, then the comparative results will be exhibited, and finally the findings and their implications will be discussed.

Literature review

Collaborative learning in English translation education

Cooperative learning in English translation education has greatly changed from passive supportive tools to autonomous educational agents because of the development of LLMs. Historically, the field has mainly been influenced by an "AI-controlled" paradigm, where intelligent tutoring systems were built to reflect the behaviors of individual learners and provide guiding feedback16. However, the presence of LLMs has led to a shift towards an "AI-assisted" paradigm. Unlike the previous rule-based or specialized artificial intelligence systems, LLMs possess much autonomy and can initiate complex dialogues, imitate different professional roles and produce delicate linguistic contents17.

Recently, researchers have studied artificial intelligence from the aspect of Socially Shared Regulation of Learning (SSRL), indicating that multiple group members collaborate and rely on each other to regulate their cognition, metacognition and motivation towards a common goal11. Compared with the individual self-regulation, SSRL includes joint discussions about the task perception and shared supervision of progress. For example, in a cooperative translation task, SSRL takes place when the group members jointly evaluate the appropriateness of a technical term and modify their translation strategies based on the group’s feedback. Under this framework, the intelligent agent can affect the group’s supervision and regulation of their collaborative tasks. This change implies the emergence of hybrid intelligence, in which intelligence is no longer owned solely by humans or machines, but comes from their dynamic interaction18.

At present, though significant progress has been achieved, most studies still concentrate on the relationship between humans and artificial intelligence, mainly focusing on the enhancement of individual performance or personalized instructions19. There is still insufficient understanding regarding the operation of the agent in multi-personal collaborative systems, particularly the details of interaction between an agent playing as a third collaborator and the existing social dynamics, negotiation patterns and shared regulatory strategies in group learning. The aim of this research is to fill this gap by examining the tripartite "Human-Human-AI" interaction and considering the LLM not as a secondary auxiliary but as a socio-cognitive partner that modifies the classroom environment.

The evolving role of artificial intelligence in education: from tools to social agents

The origin of artificial intelligence in education traces back to the combination of cybernetics and cognitive science in the middle of the twentieth century20. The 1956 Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence plays a crucial role21,22, aiming to simulate human learning through accurate algorithms. After the post-“cognitive revolution” in psychology, the early educational applications were based on the concept that human intelligence could be improved by systematic, computer-assisted feedback loops23. These initial paradigms treated the learner as a recipient of AI-guided services, including intelligent tutoring systems designed to model individual knowledge states and provide rule-based feedback16. At present, this paradigm has transformed into a cybernetic approach which emphasizes the significance of AI in promoting collaborative learning and knowledge integration among adult learners24.

With the development of the field, the appearance of LLMs results in a transition to an "AI-assisted" paradigm. Unlike the previous narrow systems, modern LLMs possess strong autonomy - the ability to initiate complex, multi-round dialogues and imitate various professional roles17. Current research is increasingly examining these models from the viewpoint of Socially Shared Regulation of Learning, showing that artificial intelligence has evolved from a passive tool to an active participant capable of facilitating group monitoring and social-cognitive regulation11. This evolution is supported by the introduction of multimodal AI, which improves more natural and human-like interactions in text, voice and visual modes, thus realizing the ideal of “hybrid intelligence”18.

Although these developments exist, there is still a great difference in the advancement of cooperative systems involving both humans and artificial intelligence. Current research mainly focuses on the assistance provided by the LLM to individual performance or personalized teaching19, but does not thoroughly investigate the 'Human-Human-AI’ model. Specifically, the influence of an active participant on the existing social structures and general regulatory methods in group learning is uncertain. The aim of this study is to bridge this gap by considering the LLM as a socio-cognitive partner affecting the classroom atmosphere rather than a peripheral service provider.

LLMs in English translation: shifting collaborative workflows

In the field of English translation education, the integration of technology generally follows the machine translation post-editing way. The linear workflow usually treats the human’s role as ‘error correction’, which is a production-oriented procedure where the machine gives a fixed output for the human to modify25. Although this model enhances the efficiency, recent studies indicate the issue of ‘cognitive offloading’. During this process, students may accept the machine-generated drafts without careful consideration and weaken some important translation skills such as source text analysis and stylistic judgment7. Furthermore, the ‘agency’ characteristic of LLMs may bring a psychological risk - the Dunning-Kruger effect. This cognitive bias occurs when individuals with insufficient knowledge about a certain field overestimate their abilities due to lack of self-awareness26. In the cooperation between humans and AI, the fluent and authoritative tone of AI-generated drafts may create a false sense of authority, leading to students being too confident in their untested outputs and concealing their real weaknesses.

To overcome the disadvantages of the linear post-editing model, this research suggests an integrated collaborative translation method which utilizes the dialogic and evaluative features of modern AI27. Compared to the one-sided characteristics of traditional tools, the collaborative application of LLMs is iterative and process-oriented28. This change is also supported by the emergence of multimodal LLMs, which transform AI into a context-sensitive partner. For example, recent studies show that multimodal capabilities are beneficial for personalized vocabulary acquisition by combining textual and visual supports29 and improve performance in specialized tests by incorporating complex visual information30. In the 'human-human-AI’ triad, the model acts as an evaluator performing the function of ‘evaluative negotiation’. It implies a higher level of mental activity where the learners not only correct their errors but also investigate, explain and combine various linguistic options generated by several AI partners9. Research in 2025 indicates that the effect of AI on evaluation leads to more profound critical thinking than the conventional evaluation techniques31–33.

The advantage of this approach is to shift the focus from the whole quality of the translated result to the organization of the ‘translation event’ itself34. By altering the main task of the group from writing to evaluating, this model redistributes the social and cognitive workload among the student team. This investigation offers a significant progress in product-oriented research, investigating how new working procedures can improve the skills of the professionals through systematic and critical communications with intelligent agents, which is appropriate for the current condition when cooperation between humans and AI is frequent35.

Theoretical framework: distributed cognition in the triad

The theoretical foundation of the triadic interaction in this study comes from the concept of Distributed Cognition which is connected with the cybernetic view on systems. One of the basic principles of Distributed Cognition is that cognitive activities take place not only within an individual’s mind but also socially and materially, being distributed over a network of people, cultural artifacts and technological environment36. According to this point of view, the focus of cognitive analysis shifts from an individual learner to the whole socio-technical system, stressing the coordination between internal mental images and external technological aids.

When applying Distributed Cognition to the "human-human-AI" triad, the cooperative group can be regarded as a single hybrid functional system. In this system, the Large Language Model (LLM) is re-interpreted as a dynamic cognitive form bearing the responsibility of memory, problem solving and preliminary language assessment. This modification indicates a considerable change in the relationship between the human and AI team, from a tool-oriented one to a multi-level and self-renewing feedback system with cooperative interactions among the socio-technical network13,24. According to this perspective, the cooperation involves three participants, where both humans and machines modify the cognitive tasks and create knowledge jointly37. This approach is especially suitable for the study of complex and ambiguous tasks such as translation, where the language ability may be different among the members of the group.

A important aspect of our study is the combination of historical and professional progress through the use of Distributed Cognition theory to follow the “information pathway” in the triad. The uniqueness of our research lies in applying Distributed Cognition to observe the “information pathway” in the triad, investigating how a prompt is generated by the students, processed by the AI and then evaluated by the human partners. Moreover, this framework can also be used to examine the "transfer of authority", analyzing the influence of the AI’s authoritative suggestions on the distribution of power and knowledge exchange among the cooperating humans. This theoretical idea suggests that the LLMs have the function of regulating the cognitive process. By controlling the internal cognitive flow and sharing the cognitive load, the model improves the collaborative experience and changes the knowledge authority, offering a new approach for dealing with the evaluation results in previous works.

Based on the theoretical foundation of Distributed Cognition and the existing limitations in the literature regarding the cooperation between humans and artificial intelligence, this investigation intends to investigate the role of LLMs as the ‘third collaborator’. The main objectives of this study are to investigate the differences in the collaboration patterns and outcomes:

RQ1

How does the presence of an LLM influence the communication methods and knowledge creation in collaborative English translation learning compared to the single human group?

RQ2

What are the impacts of the human-artificial intelligence cooperative system on the final learning achievements and individual students’ experiences?

Methods

Sample and sampling technique

In the first method, the participants were selected from the School of Foreign Languages in a big public university by purposive sampling. In order to guarantee that the participants have the necessary background in translation, the main targets were senior undergraduate students (third and fourth years) and graduate students of English major who have passed at least one semester of specialized courses in “Written Translation” or "Business English Translation". A total of 372 students were chosen and then randomly divided into 124 groups of three people. As shown in Table 1, most of the sample consists of females (87.4%), which reflects the gender distribution in the related programs in this area. The group contains 162 third-year undergraduate students (43.5%), 180 fourth-year undergraduate students (48.5%) and 30 graduate students (8.0%). Concerning the digital literacy, 90.3% of the respondents indicated previous experience with Language Learning Models (LLM), among whom 50% are beginners and 40.3% are experienced users. All participants were informed about the aims of the research, the procedures and their rights before the experiment started. They signed a written informed consent form. In order to maintain confidentiality, all the collected data were anonymized and used exclusively for the present academic study. Research design and procedure

The second method involves the research design and the process. We used a comparative experimental design to compare the differences between human-human and human-human-AI collaboration models. There were 124 cooperative teams, each consisting of three students, which were randomly allocated to either the control group (62 teams) or the experimental group (62 teams). The control group, representing traditional human-human collaboration, could only use standard tools like online dictionaries and terminology database. However, the experimental group, representing the human-human-AI collaboration model, was given the same traditional tools along with a shared LLM account named “third collaborator”.

The task materials in both groups comprised a 500-word business text including technical terms, specific business pragmatics and persuasive strategies to encourage comprehensive discussion. The experiment was conducted in an experimental environment equipped with audio and screen recording software and lasted for 90 minutes. As shown in Figure 1, the procedure started with the selection and random assignment for all the participants. Afterward, the teams were asked to complete the collaborative translation task within the given time limit. When they finished, each group submitted their final result, and afterwards, the members answered an online questionnaire and participated in a group discussion to give their opinions about their experience.Fig. 1Flowchart of the research design and data collection procedure.

Data collection instruments

To investigate the collaborative process and its results, a multi-source data collection method has been implemented in this study. All the group meetings have been recorded by using common screen and voice recording software to observe the dynamic collaboration process. Besides, the inputs given to the LLM and their corresponding outputs are also collected to record the interaction between human and LLM. In addition to the aforementioned data, the main results include the final translated text of each group and a brief team report. The configuration and prompting strategies of the LLM used in our research are introduced in Table 2. The ‘third collaborator’ role is defined by a strict system instruction forming a scaffolding-based interaction model. For example, the LLM’s “temperature” is set at 0.7. This parameter affects the randomness of the output in generative models and a value of 0.7 is selected to balance the linguistic diversity and creative expression while maintaining sufficient consistency and factual accuracy for the translation task. Moreover, the learning outcomes are represented by the final translated documents and organized team reports. To ensure the objectivity and consistency of the assessment, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) is calculated using a two-way mixed-effects model. The overall ICC value is 0.86 (95% confidence interval [0.82, 0.89]). The ICC values for each evaluation criterion are also computed to guarantee the transparency of the measurement: fidelity (ICC = 0.88), fluency (ICC = 0.84), terminology accuracy (ICC = 0.89) and pragmatic style appropriateness (ICC = 0.83). These scores indicate a high degree of intra-rater agreement according to standard psychometric criteria38. If the scores of any one dimension vary more than 10%, another experienced translator will recalculates them without informing the previous one. Then, the final result will be evaluated by the three experts to guarantee the accuracy of the data. In order to obtain the personal views of the students, a questionnaire is completed by each one after the experiment to assess their cooperation ability, satisfaction and total cognitive load. To investigate the variations of cognitive resources in real time, a one-item momentary cognitive load scale is given every 15 minutes during the experiment. This scale is chosen because of its reliability and ability to recognize the present mental effort, which can decrease the influence of retrospective memory. As a new tripartite 'Human-Computer-Assisted’ system, this method focuses on observing the changes in cognitive resource allocation over time rather than giving a certain psychological measurement value39. The momentary results are verified with the total cognitive load scores of the whole experiment to keep consistency. The detailed list of questionnaire items and their respective scales is given in Appendix A Questionnaire Instruments.

In this study, a convergent parallel mixed-methods design is applied for data analysis according to Fig. 2. For quantitative analysis, the differences between the two groups in important outcome and experience variables are focused. Firstly, the descriptive statistics such as means (M) and standard deviations (SD) are calculated for the translation quality scores and the questionnaire data about perceived efficiency, task satisfaction and cognitive load. Afterwards, independent samples $t$-tests are performed to investigate the statistical differences in the means of these indicators between the control group and the experimental group40.Fig. 2Flowchart of the mixed-methods data analysis procedure.

Flowchart of the mixed-methods data analysis procedure.

For qualitative analysis, a thematic analysis based on grounded theory is conducted on the transcripts of cooperative sessions41, log files of LLM interactions and interview records. The coding process is both inductive and deductive, aiming to identify emerging patterns and predefined concepts such as team’s communication patterns, decision-making mechanisms, conflict resolution methods and the different roles played by the LLM in the experimental group. Finally, the results from both the quantitative and qualitative analyses are integrated and verified to gain a comprehensive understanding of the effect of LLM on cooperative translation learning.

Results

Analysis of translation quality

An independent-samples t-test is also carried out on the final translation quality scores to evaluate the impact of human-AI collaborative system on the objective learning outcomes. The results do not show a significant difference between the experimental group (M=85.2, SD=5.4) and the control group (M=84.5, SD=5.9), with t(122)=0.78 and p=0.437. The calculated Cohen’s d value is 0.12, which indicates a small effect size42. As shown in Fig. 3, the medians and interquartile ranges of both groups are very close, suggesting that the introduction of LLM as a ‘third collaborator’ does not have a significant effect on the final output quality. The non-significant result together with the smaller variance in the experimental group’s scores indicates that the artificial intelligence maintains a stable performance level but does not improve the group’s creativity.Fig. 3Box plot of translation quality scores by group.4 results.

Box plot of translation quality scores by group.4 results.

It should be noted that although there is no significant difference in the mean scores, the experimental group has less score variability and a more concentrated distribution than the control group. From Fig. 3, it can be observed that the scores of the experimental group have a narrower range and fewer outliers. This may imply that the intervention of LLM has different effects on groups at different levels: it may serve as a ‘floor guarantee’ for groups with low levels, preventing them from making serious mistakes by providing high quality initial drafts; however, it may restrict the translation creativity of groups with high levels, resulting in similar outcomes.

We got the learners’ opinions through a questionnaire. The statistical results indicated that there were significant differences between the two groups. As shown in Table 3, the experimental group scored higher than the control group in the areas of "Perceived Collaboration Efficiency" and “Task Satisfaction” (p < 0.01). It implies that the group using LLM thought their collaborative process was more efficient and satisfactory. This result was also confirmed by the qualitative interviews. After conducting the thematic analysis on the interview data, we discovered that the experimental group generally believed that the LLM greatly reduced the time and effort required for information collection and the writing of the first draft. Thus, they could focus more on complex decision-making and revising the text, resulting in a better feeling of efficiency and accomplishment. Nevertheless, this might be affected by the Dunning-Kruger effect26, since though the experimental group experienced an obvious increase in efficiency, the actual translation quality was not higher than that of the control group. This inconsistency indicates a difference between ability and confidence. The convenience of using LLM makes people overestimate their abilities. This phenomenon is similar to the Dunning-Kruger effect, causing the learners to think that they have made greater progress in improving their language skills than they really have. Therefore, even if the LLM ensures the basic quality of the translation, it may be difficult for the students to consider the output generated by the LLM as their own improvements.

The change in the cooperative decision-making approach

After a thorough analysis of the collaborative recordings’ transcripts, we have discovered that the introduction of LLM has led to a significant modification in the team’s decision-making process. In the control group, the main subject of conversation was "How should we translate?" and each person had to propose his/her own translation methods separately, which can be referred to as “generative negotiation”. Conversely, the experimental group discussed the problem of "Which of these options suggested by the LLM is more appropriate?" or "What difficulties exist in the LLM’s translation?", leading to a new type of negotiation named “evaluative negotiation”.

Reconfiguration of team interaction dynamics

The introduction of a LLM has greatly modified the interaction pattern among the team members. Firstly, regarding the distribution of tasks, our qualitative study on the collaborative process showed an informal division of roles. In the control group, the members had a flexible and shared division of labour. However, in the experimental group, the team interactions were mainly concentrated on human-machine dialogues, with one member often taking the role of a ‘prompt engineer’, responsible for communicating with the LLM, leading to a tendency towards task centralisation.

Secondly, the introduction of the LLM brought about a more fundamental change in the authority of knowledge. To measure this change, we recorded the ultimate basis for each key translation decision in the audio transcripts and classified them into three categories: ‘peer consultation’, ‘external resources’ and ‘AI suggestions’. From Fig. 4, it is evident that the distribution of codes has caused a notable difference in the authority of knowledge. In the control group, the majority of the decisions were made according to ‘peer consultation’ (58.7%) and ‘external resources’ (41.3%). However, in the experimental group, ‘AI recommendations’ occupy the principal position as the source of authority, which accounts for 50.4% of the decisions. On the other hand, ‘peer consultation’ reduces to 28.4% and the use of ‘external resources’ decreases to 21.2%. These results show that the LLM not only acts as a passive information source but also plays a new role as a ‘knowledge authority’ when cooperating, thereby having a great effect on the team’s decision-making.Fig. 4Distribution of knowledge authority sources in decision-making progress.

Learners’ perception model of AI roles

Based on a systematic thematic analysis of interview data and collaborative reports, we have identified several core functions that learners attribute to LLMs during cooperative translation, categorizing them into a multidimensional role model. This model shows that learners generally consider LLMs as efficient auxiliary tools rather than equal partners. The main roles include ‘Draft Generator’ used for quickly generating basic text; ‘Information Retriever’ used for retrieving specialized terminology and background knowledge; ‘Creativity Catalyst’ used to provide diversified expressions; and ‘Quality Checker’ used to check the linguistic coherence of the translated text at the later stage of the task. Further analysis reveals that the importance of these roles varies with the progress of the task.

We counted the number of times each participant talked about the interview topics and divided the data into several stages. As shown in Fig. 5, each stage has some unique features: At the start of the task, ‘Draft Generator’ and ‘Information Retriever’ were frequently mentioned and were the main subjects of the discussion. When the task proceeded to the latter part which required more processing and creative ideas, ‘Creativity Catalyst’ became more significant. During the checking process, ‘Quality Checker’ was referred to more often. These results suggest that the learners changed their roles according to the different stages in the computer cooperative work. Discussion and implications

Concerning the modification of cooperative strategy and knowledge construction

According to the first research question, the application of a LLM not only enhances the efficiency but also considerably alters the method of cooperation. A notable change has taken place in the decision-making procedure of the group, changing from the former “generation negotiation” to the “evaluation negotiation”. In the groups containing both humans and artificial intelligence, the main mental activities have transferred to the evaluation, assessment and revision of the proposals presented by the AI. This sentence agrees with some recent studies44, showing that the effective use of Generative Artificial Intelligence in complex and interactive teaching situations necessitates the students to engage in higher order thinking such as assessing the results generated by the AI. Our proposal indicates that the students should improve their ability to evaluate and make decisions, which is important in the current intelligent society. This proposal follows the hierarchical levels of Bloom’s Revised Taxonomy45, in which the former “production” stage has been upgraded to the “evaluation” level, being the second highest in the taxonomic sequence. Therefore, the cognitive process has been altered, leading to changes in the group’s interpersonal relationships.

The establishment of an "informal prompt engineer" position indicates a new kind of task division, where one person is responsible for communicating with the AI. Additionally, the introduction of the Large Language Model (LLM) as a new “knowledge authority” has significantly affected the power distribution within the group. Their viewpoints are frequently considered as external references when solving disputes, which affects the way classmates work together cooperatively. This shows that the AI is not only an external tool, but it has also become an indispensable part of the cooperative process as an active participant in the cognitive system, forming a “distributed intelligence” network including "human-human-AI" where knowledge is shared among the learners and the technology37. However, it should be pointed out that “AI suggestions” take up 50.4% of the decision-making power, raising a serious controversial question. Though12 stresses the benefits of AI in cooperation, our data disclose a possible risk of epistemic submission. For groups with relatively lower initial abilities, the LLM is generally regarded as a "single authority," which may lead to an inefficient cooperative procedure ignoring the discussion between peers and encouraging blind use of AI. It is crucial to take into account the "trust calibration," the procedure by which students learn to adjust their reliance on the AI according to its reliability.

This change in authority promotes the development of metacognition, since the LLM functions as a cognitive “mirror” which externalizes various linguistic possibilities. Based on recent frameworks on human-AI cooperation, interacting with an agent-like collaborator requires the participants to improve the frequency of self-explanation and conflict resolution46. The evaluation negotiation in this research is a typical example of controlling the learning process. By evaluating the “reasoning” of the AI and identifying “hallucinations”, the students can enhance their metacognitive abilities. The comprehensive evaluation avoids the knowledge authority being a single and unthinking system, but turns it into a distributed one.

It is important to consider the function of Large Language Models (LLMs) as a source of knowledge. On the one hand, they can offer correct and rapid answers which are beneficial for solving simple language problems and promote the progress of the group. On the other hand, there exists a risk of epistemic dependence as the learners may accept the outcomes of the artificial intelligence without thorough investigation. This issue is particularly worrying due to the algorithmic bias and “hallucinations” in the LLMs. If the LLM contains implicit cultural biases or factual mistakes, its credibility might lead to an uncritical acceptance and consequently reinforce these wrong information, which causes serious ethical and educational problems47. The danger is not only in accepting factual “hallucinations”, but also in gradually enhancing the cultural and linguistic biases present in the training data of the LLM. Since the LLM usually represents the dominant Western-oriented or standard linguistic norms, considering it as a source of knowledge may unintentionally ignore the diversified practical aspects necessary for professional translation. This leads to a 'bias feedback loop’, in which the students accept and transmit these hidden prejudices in their final works. Hence, ‘critical skepticism’ should be considered an important aspect of AI literacy, prompting the students to examine the underlying cultural logic of the LLM rather than focusing solely on its surface-level fluency.

Thus, this paper considers ‘evaluative negotiation’ as the best learning objective. At present, in the translation research of 2024, it is shown that in a professional environment based on mixed workflow systems, ‘evaluative agency’ is equally important as linguistic proficiency48. Although the risk of cognitive offloading still exists, a structured triadic model transforms the student from a ‘linguistic transcriber’ to a ‘strategic mediator’. Evaluative negotiation does not obstruct the progress, but rather helps the learners to prepare for the future of the profession, in which the ability to evaluate and improve the AI generated materials will be an important feature of an expert49. In fact, the ‘process change’ found should not be considered as the final goal, but as the start of a training procedure guided by human feedback. The information about the satisfaction and efficiency, showing which language models (LLM) are helpful for effective ‘evaluative negotiation’ or indicating the cognitive obstacles, gives the necessary human preference data for Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF)16. In a prospective model, these process data act as a reward signal to improve specialized pedagogical LLMs50. Therefore, the AI can be used as a flexible helper, giving criticism to promote constructive analysis instead of merely obeying. Besides, this interactive system is beneficial in transforming the traditional teaching methods into an AI-assisted flipped classroom, where the questions provided by AI create a better learning environment. Combining our empirical results with the advanced technology of RLHF, this research suggests a clear method to produce suitable AI assistants for their users.

Influence on academic results and personal perception

We observe a difference between the actual outcomes and the participants’ feelings. Though the translation quality of the group which employs human-computer cooperation does not improve significantly, the scores of cooperative efficiency and task satisfaction are relatively higher. Such high satisfaction is mainly due to the “cognitive offloading” idea, where the LLM bears the major part of the repetitive cognitive tasks like writing and information search. By transferring these repetitive tasks to the LLM, the students can concentrate on more important activities like strategic planning and style modification, which are considered more interesting and valuable. This conclusion is consistent with some recent studies51 which show that technological feedback can enhance the emotional state and perceived helpfulness of the students, even if their performance does not significantly increase.

Furthermore, the decline in the output quality indicates that the LLM has a complex and dual effect in cooperative learning. On the one hand, it serves as a "floor-guarantee" to ensure that the weaker groups cannot generate unsatisfactory translations. On the other hand, it may act as a "ceiling-constraint", because its fluent but sometimes general responses may discourage the better groups from exploring more detailed and innovative solutions. This viewpoint is supported by the literature7, which emphasizes the adverse impact of too much reliance on AI on personal and creative thinking. Hence, the teachers should design tasks to make the students use the AI at the initial stage of creativity rather than as the last stage of the work. Additionally, the remarkable improvement in the sense of efficiency raises an important educational issue: is a reduction in the cognitive load always beneficial? Although cognitive offloading simplifies the collaborative process, research in the field of learning science suggests that “desirable difficulties” are generally necessary for a thorough understanding and long-term memory. If the AI-human team frequently meets with efficient challenges, like dealing with syntactic ambiguities, there is a possibility of superficial processing. In future instructional designs, efforts should be made to maintain an appropriate balance, providing cognitive assistance from the LLM while keeping the necessary challenging thoughts needed for acquiring professional knowledge.

This "floor-guarantee, ceiling-constraint" situation requires a careful reconsideration of the concept of “quality” in an intelligent environment. Although the final results from the different groups have the same statistical data, the cognitive loads borne by the two groups are fundamentally different. The control group performs traditional generation and autonomous solutions, while the experimental group focuses on higher-order cognitive functions, including systematic reviews, quality controls and iterative prompt designs. A single evaluation of a specific task may conceal these differences, but a long-term analysis will reveal a significant difference in professional ability. The skills gained under the joint condition of human and machine, especially the abilities in evaluation and adjustment towards automated results, are more appropriate for the present demands of the translation industry. Therefore, this study suggests that the present standard based on the products is inappropriate in the information age; it cannot fully reflect the essential "human-in-the-loop" skills which are crucial for the educational value of AI-assisted collaborative learning.

Implications for English translation education

The results of this research possess important practical value for the arrangement of curricula, teaching methods and evaluation standards in higher education regarding English translation. The main purpose is to change the teaching objectives from just enhancing translation skills at the beginning to improving abilities in a human-computer cooperative environment. Hence, the courses should include some systematic modules such as 'AI-aided post-editing’, 'evaluation of machine output’ and 'translation prompt engineering’ to meet the demands of the industry. Therefore, the teachers’ roles should be transformed from mere instructors to ‘guides’ in the complex learning process.

In this position, the teachers should not only give explanations which may not be fully understood by the students, but should adopt a practical approach. The findings reveal a significant problem: should the integration of AI be restricted to advanced students who have acquired the basic translation skills? Our analysis indicates that without a strong linguistic basis, ‘evaluative negotiation’ is superficial. Thus, the teaching methods need to be modified according to the students’ abilities. For beginners, extra difficulties should be introduced to prevent early dependence, while for advanced students, they should be trained to utilize the large language model as a strategic partner to improve the quality of translation. This suggests that teaching students to use the LLM is not only about giving general instructions, but also includes skill-practice which promotes true critical thinking through careful and practical verification. To avoid ‘uninformed’ dependence, the professors should clearly explain these procedures and incorporate them into the class activities. For example, 'Adversarial Translation Auditing’ can be performed by assigning groups to detect the subtle cultural biases in the LLM outputs. Another effective activity is 'Triadic Prompt Iteration’, requiring groups to record and explain each step of improvement based on the feedback from the LLM. By integrating ‘evaluative negotiation’ into these structured tasks, the educators ensure that the students gain competence through practice instead of relying on abstract guidelines.

To further investigate these theoretical implications and guide the future development of educational AI, the communication should be transformed from a static suggestion box to an interactive, participatory and fact-based discussion system. According to Sharples’13 concept of 'Social Generative AI’, the LLM should be redefined as an active ‘interlocutor’ which encourages the exchange of ideas among the three members. In this capacity, the AI plays the role of a ‘Socratic provocateur’ by prompting students to justify their language choices and find logical inconsistencies in peer discussions. Additionally, following Tilak’s24 cybernetic viewpoint, the AI functions as a ‘systemic regulator’ which maintains the group’s ‘productive struggle’. It does not directly provide solutions but offers strategic hints or alternative cultural interpretations which change the team’s overall control. Based on the newly established models for individualized learning and fact-checking29,52, we propose that the LLM’s role as an evaluative collaborator can be improved through structured, LLM-based questioning. In this framework, student groups can actively ‘question’ the model, for instance, by requesting it to 'explain this alternative translation’ or 'compare these two expressions for formality’. Furthermore, to guarantee the factual accuracy and reduce hallucinations, the evaluating negotiation process should be strengthened by incorporating Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) and knowledge graphs. As shown in graph-based models like TrumorGPT52, future translation systems may enable real-time verification of the AI-generated content against authoritative linguistic databases.

Although the main purpose of this research is to apply the LLM as a text transforming tool, the rapid progress of the MLLM indicates a new revolutionary trend. Unlike the text communication mentioned here, the MLLM can combine visual, contextual and domain-specific clues, for example, analyzing the visual rhetoric of an advertisement or the technical diagrams in a manual, to offer context-sensitive help which is more efficient than linguistic expressions29,30. This change means that ‘evaluative negotiation’ will develop into a multilevel semiotic adjustment. In order to make full use of the AI in such situations, the students should learn to switch different modes and connect the AI generated text with the visual evidence and cultural background in order to keep the consistency of semiotics. According to the “storyboarding” technique24, the teachers can assist the students by assigning them to draw multimodal translation maps. During these processes, the students have to compare the AI suggestions with the original images and explain why their selected linguistic methods resolve the semiotic ambiguity. This kind of organized practice will change the AI from a “textual consultant” to a “perceptual partner”, so that the human-human-AI triad can focus on the higher level pragmatic synthesis while guaranteeing that the final product is coherent in terms of multiple modes.

Moreover, the information obtained from this research agrees well with the human preference data required for Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), the method used for aligning the advanced models16. By considering the observed “process transformation” as a feedback-driven training cycle, we can use the aggregated process data, showing which Large Language Models (LLM) interventions lead to effective discussions or dead-ends, as a reward signal53. Through this structured approach which can be improved by RLHF, the interaction can be transformed into a dynamic and factual dialogue54,55. These adjustable collaborators may bring about significant educational changes such as the flipped classroom based on LLMs, where the AI-assisted peer questioning forms a more student-centered and autonomous learning environment56. By implementing these technical steps, where student-led evaluations guide the model’s continuous improvement, the pedagogical AI can play its role as a reliable cognitive support, enhancing the high-level evaluation ability necessary in modern industries.

Additionally, the assessment methods for translation ability should undergo a paradigm shift to meet these changing educational objectives. With the LLMs able to produce high quality initial results, the traditional product-oriented evaluation is no longer sufficient to evaluate a student’s participation and performance. This study recommends a transition towards process-oriented, authentic assessments which emphasize the ‘journey’ rather than the ‘destination’. Moreover, the evaluation criteria should consider not only the final fixed text but also various evidences related to the procedures, such as the records of the iterative prompts, the history of AI-generated comments and the logical reasoning of the decisions. It is important to use a 'step-by-step integration’ approach to keep the basic abilities. In the initial stage of the students’ training, they should do the tasks in a supervised or encrypted online manner to prevent unauthorized copying and to help them understand the difficulty in segmenting and translating the original text. After reaching the basic level, the course should gradually introduce the LLMs as special conversation partners, beginning with simple question-answer pairs and ending with the triadic cooperation which has been discussed in this research. By considering the translation portfolio as both the final result and the continuous record of the development from independent to assisted practice, the teachers can guarantee that the ‘evaluation ability’ is based on a strong base of language skills.

For example, students need to submit a “translation portfolio” to show their progress. It should include the first draft generated by AI, the prompts used to improve the language, and a "translator’s note" explaining the final decision after considering the suggestions of AI. Consequently, the evaluation standards will be shifted from the quality of output to the ability of students in assessing and controlling metacognition. According to the interaction records, teachers can accurately evaluate a learner’s ability to critically analyse the AI outputs, which is consistent with the concepts of sustainable assessment and modern industrial requirements. This change meets the general educational need57 to reform the assessment methods in the age of AI, moving from a single aspect of academic integrity to promoting reflective and integrated professional behaviour.

The obtained results are beneficial for teacher training courses. Future English teachers are supposed to not only learn to apply these cooperative devices but also use them in their teaching. Hence, the teacher training curriculum should incorporate the training of "AI educational content knowledge", helping the prospective teachers to design, conduct and assess the cooperative learning activities between humans and AI. This includes instructing them to foresee and deal with novel social circumstances, such as the position of “prompt engineer” and the appearance of "AI as authority", so that all the students can take an equal and critical part in their future classes.

Limitations and Future Research

This paper provides empirical support for the human-human-AI trinity, but some restrictions indicate potential areas for further research. Taking into account the design constraints, the present study mainly investigates the immediate cognitive and social changes during a single cooperative meeting. Future work may use longitudinal designs to investigate how these relationships change as the students acquire more complex AI-assisted social procedures. Concerning the generalization of the results, the obtained data are based on a particular situation where English majors and graduate students perform special translation tasks in higher education. Although the tripartite model offers a useful approach for analyzing socio-technical interactions, the findings mainly focus on the linguistic and intercultural mediation conditions. Additional studies are necessary to determine if these patterns vary in different language settings or among various academic communities. Moreover, as the translation task is not precisely specified and assessed, the ratio of human involvement to AI assistance observed here may differ when applied to other professional areas.

Important ethical and cognitive risks, such as trust calibration and the Dunning-Kruger effect26, must be taken into consideration. Due to the strong “authority effect” of AI agents, the students might become too confident in the incorrect AI results and ignore the critical evaluation of the biases or practical issues. To deal with these problems, it is necessary to develop educational approaches to improve the ‘AI literacy’ and assist the students in adapting to the use of automatic partners. In addition, when modifying the writing procedure, further research should be carried out to find out the changes in the evaluation methods which should include the process-related evidence, like AI criticism logs and rationales, to effectively assess the students’ evaluation ability. Furthermore, some restrictions in the experimental groups need to be pointed out. Since this study focuses on the triadic model including two human collaborators and one large language model, it does not include a one-to-one human-AI (dyadic) relationship. This is a significant restriction as it makes us unable to decide whether the tendency of ‘evaluative negotiation’ is due to the peer-to-peer social interaction or the interaction between language models. Hence, further research should design a more comprehensive comparison, which consists of human beings only, human-AI dyads and human-human-AI triads. Such comparisons are essential to discover the special socio-cognitive advantages of mixed cooperation and to find out the best arrangement for assisted translation teaching with AI.

Conclusion

After completing this research, we find that the integration of an LLM into translation cooperation is not mainly used to improve the quality of the output, but rather to change the way the team works fundamentally. Similar to the general rules of automation, the introduction of an AI member into the cooperative environment modifies the original power relationships and converts the traditional “generative negotiation” into an “evaluative negotiation” mode. This kind of system change can free up the members from performing routine tasks so that learners can focus more on the metacognitive examination and strategic decisions. However, we need to point out that there is a limitation in our observation due to the fact that the present experimental arrangement did not include a “pure human” condition or a "one-on-one human-AI pair". Therefore, the variations in authority and negotiation cannot be completely separated from the effects of group dynamics. Hence, our results can only represent a particular type of "human-human-AI" triad model instead of a general guideline for the influence of AI. To further confirm the effect of automation on individual abilities or team performance, more research with additional comparison groups is needed. In the future, we should also consider the significant position of AI as a new “knowledge authority”. It is necessary to improve the ability to coordinate human rational thinking with automatic procedures, and to maintain basic language skills by regular practice. This will make experts in the intelligent age.
