Attentional bias abnormalities and their association with cognitive load in obsessive-compulsive disorder
Authors: Zijun Yan, Xu Ma, Si Mi, Xiangyun Yang, Jinyu Zhang, Yu Chen, Danfeng Yuan, Pengchong Wang, Zhanjiang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAttentional bias is a significant factor in the onset and persistence of obsessive-compulsive symptoms in individuals with obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). However, the determinants of attentional bias in OCD remain inadequately understood. Research has indicated that certain goal-directed cognitive control factors, particularly working memory, affect the modulation of attentional bias. This study aimed to explore the relationship between cognitive load and attentional bias in OCD.MethodA total of 58 OCD patients (age: 31.34 ± 7.77, male:22, female: 36) and 59 healthy controls (HC) (age: 28.81 ± 6.19, male:19, female: 40) were enrolled. This study integrated a visuospatial working memory task with a free-viewing paradigm and utilized eye-tracking. The fixation data and gaze data were collected. Repeated measures analysis of variance and temporal-course analysis were used to explore the association between attentional bias and cognitive load.ResultsOCD patients exhibited attentional bias to Contamination pictures, manifested as early attentional vigilance, and difficulty in attentional disengagement in the middle stage. For the direction of initial fixation (DIF), only the main effect of group was significant, F(1, 115) = 21.792, p < 0.001. Analysis of the initial fixation duration (IFD) revealed a significant effect for group, F(1, 115) = 10.352, p = 0.002, and load, F(1.831, 210.57) = 4.726, p = 0.012, as well as significant group × load interaction, F(1.831, 210.57) = 7.614, p = 0.001. The results of temporal-course analysis showed that the proportion of OCD patients’ attention time on Contamination pictures was significantly higher than that of HC at 678-1751 ms under the condition of no cognitive load. Under low cognitive load conditions, the difference between the two groups began at 662 ms, while in the high cognitive load condition, significant differences appeared as early as 635 ms.ConclusionThe present study provides novel evidence that cognitive load modulates attentional bias towards contamination-related stimuli in OCD patients. With increasing cognitive load, OCD patients demonstrated greater difficulties disengaging attention from threatening stimuli and an earlier onset of these difficulties. These findings suggest that the sustained attentional bias toward threatening or symptom-related stimuli in OCD patients may be associated with limited cognitive control. It provides a basis for further exploration of the mechanism of attentional bias in OCD patients.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08306-4.Keywords: Obsessive-compulsive disorder, Attentional bias, Cognitive control, Cognitive load, Eye tracking

Introduction

Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD) is characterized by the presence of obsessions, compulsions, or both, with obsessions involving recurrent and intrusive thoughts, urges, or images, and compulsions involving repetitive behaviors or mental acts that the individual feels driven to perform [1]. The cognitive model [2], posits that OCD is maintained by inflated responsibility concerning the prevention of harm. This appraisal directly leads to heightened attentional vigilance towards perceived threats and difficulties in disengaging attention from symptom-related stimuli, as individuals actively “look for trouble” to prevent feared negative outcomes. Consistent with this view, a growing body of evidence suggests that OCD patients exhibit heightened attentional vigilance and avoidance, as well as difficulties in disengaging attention from threaten or symptom-related stimuli [3, 4]. Aberrant attentional biases are considered a significant factor contributing to the provocation and maintenance of OCS [5–8].

A substantial body of research has examined attentional bias in OCD, showing that individuals with OCD may preferentially allocate attention to threat- or symptom-relevant information, particularly stimuli related to their dominant symptom dimensions, such as contamination-related cues [9]. These biases have been investigated using various experimental paradigms, including emotional Stroop, dot-probe, visual search, and eye-tracking tasks [6]. However, findings across studies have not always been consistent, and attentional bias in OCD may vary depending on the type of stimulus, symptom dimension, task demands, and the temporal stage of attention being measured [10, 11]. In particular, attentional bias may reflect different components, including initial vigilance, sustained attentional maintenance, avoidance, and difficulty disengaging attention from symptom-related stimuli [12]. Therefore, although the existence of attentional bias in OCD has been widely investigated, the cognitive mechanisms that modulate the temporal dynamics of this bias remain insufficiently specified.

According to the theory of attentional control [13], increased cognitive load diminishes attentional control due to competition for limited executive resources. Specifically, high working memory load may occupy the resources required for goal-directed attentional control, thereby reducing the ability to maintain task-relevant goals, inhibit task-irrelevant stimuli, and flexibly shift attention away from salient distractors [14]. In this context, threat- or symptom-related stimuli may exert a stronger bottom-up influence on attention, leading to prolonged attentional maintenance or impaired attentional disengagement. This interpretation is also consistent with load theory, which suggests that working memory load increases distractor interference by weakening cognitive control over irrelevant information [15]. Moreover, in conjunction with the Cognitive-Motivational Framework [16], attentional bias can be understood as the outcome of interactions between stimulus-driven salience and goal-directed cognitive control processes, including working memory, inhibitory control, and cognitive flexibility. Therefore, when working memory resources are depleted, individuals may have greater difficulty inhibiting threat- or symptom-related cues and disengaging attention from them.

Consistent with this notion, evidence indicates that OCD patients do indeed exhibit deficits in goal-directed cognitive control, particularly in working memory, as evidenced by reduced memory span and lower accuracy on working memory tasks compared to healthy controls (HC) [17, 18]. However, relatively little is known about how goal-directed cognitive control, particularly working memory resources, shapes the expression of attentional bias in OCD.

Although studies in non-clinical populations indicate that increased cognitive load can promote attentional maintenance toward threat-relevant stimuli and influence the efficacy of attentional bias modification (ABM) training, it remains unclear whether these effects generalize to OCD, a disorder characterized by symptom-related threat appraisals and deficits in goal-directed cognitive control. Studies highlight that depleted cognitive control resources increase the likelihood of task-irrelevant threat cues capturing and maintaining attention [19, 20], and that cognitive load had an impact on both attentional bias and ABM training [4, 21–23]. These studies suggest the need to determine whether similar relationships exist in OCD, where goal-directed cognitive control may be particularly vulnerable. Therefore, exploring the relationship between cognitive load and attentional bias in OCD, specifically in the context of goal-directed cognitive control, is crucial for a more comprehensive understanding of the cognitive mechanisms driving attentional bias in this disorder. Furthermore, it may identify potential targets for improving ABM training tailored to the specific cognitive profile of OCD patients.

To address the complex relationship between cognitive load and attentional bias in OCD, the present study adopted a dual-task paradigm to directly examine how experimentally manipulated working memory load influences attentional bias. Specifically, we integrated a visuospatial working memory task with a free-viewing eye-tracking paradigm under symptom-provocation conditions. This design allowed us to investigate not only whether cognitive load modulates attentional bias toward symptom-relevant stimuli, but also which components of attentional processing are affected.

We hypothesized that OCD patients would exhibit initial attentional vigilance to contamination related stimuli, and subsequent difficulty in attentional disengagement. Moreover, the early component was not affected by cognitive load, while difficulty in attentional disengagement was aggravated with the increase of cognitive load.

Methods

Participants

A total of 66 patients with OCD were recruited from the outpatient department of Beijing Anding Hospital. Participants in the OCD group were required to meet the following criteria: 18–60 years of age; at least a junior high school education; diagnosis of OCD according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition, Text Revision (DSM-5-TR); a Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) total score > 7; presence of contamination symptoms; right-handedness; normal or corrected-to-normal vision; no self-reported history of color blindness or color vision deficiency; and willingness to participate with written informed consent. Exclusion criteria included current comorbid psychiatric disorders, such as schizophrenia or mood disorders; recent modified electroconvulsive therapy or neuromodulation treatment; a history of organic brain disease or major physical illness; and evidence of substance dependence or psychoactive substance use. All included patients with OCD had contamination/washing-related symptoms as their primary symptom dimension, which was first identified by the outpatient psychiatrist and then independently confirmed by the psychiatrist responsible for study enrollment. Right-handedness was used as an inclusion criterion to reduce potential heterogeneity related to handedness-associated functional lateralization and motor-response differences, which may influence cognition processing and task performance.

A total of 60 healthy controls (HC) were recruited through social advertisement. All potential HC were screened using the Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) to exclude current psychiatric disorders. Eligible HCs were required to be 18–60 years old, have at least a junior high school education, have an Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory-Revised (OCI-R) total score < 21 to exclude clinically elevated obsessive-compulsive symptoms, score ≤ 15 on both the Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI) and Beck Depression Inventory (BDI), be right-handed, have normal or corrected-to-normal vision, report no history of color blindness or color vision deficiency, and provide written informed consent. Individuals were excluded if they met diagnostic criteria for any psychiatric disorder, including OCD, schizophrenia, or mood disorders, had a previous diagnosis of OCD, had a history of organic brain disease or major physical illness, or showed evidence of substance dependence or psychoactive substance use.

To minimize the potential influence of extreme values on statistical estimates and inference, outliers were screened separately within each group for each eye-tracking index using a ± 3SD criterion, a commonly used approach for identifying extreme observations, and values exceeding this threshold were excluded from the final analyses [24]. Consequently, data from eight patients with OCD (age: 26.13 ± 8.48, Male: 5, Female: 3, Y-BOCS: 21.88 ± 5.33) and one healthy control (age = 23, Male) were excluded from the final analyses: one healthy control and five patients with OCD were excluded because their IFD values exceeded the ±3SD threshold, and three patients with OCD were excluded because their DIF values exceeded this threshold. Finally, 58 patients with OCD and 59 HC were included in the statistical analysis. In the OCD sample, 25 patients were medication-free, whereas 33 patients were receiving stable Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors (SSRIs) treatment.

Measures

The Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (M.I.N.I.)

The M.I.N.I. [25] was used to diagnose and screen out for mental disorders. It has been updated to the 7.0.2 version, and we use the Chinese version of 7.0.2, which can screen for OCD based on DSM-5 diagnostic criteria.

The Yale-Brown Obsessive-Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS)

Y-BOCS [26] was used to assess the severity of OCS in terms of obsession and compulsion. The Chinese version of the Y-BOCS [27] shows good interrater reliability (r = 0.75) and test-retest reliability (r = 0.91) [27]. In the OCD sample of the present study, Cronbach’s α was 0.768. The Y-BOCS total score ranges from 0 to 40, with higher scores indicating greater OCD symptom severity; scores of 0–7, 8–15, 16–23, 24–31, and 32–40 are commonly interpreted as subclinical, mild, moderate, severe, and extreme symptoms, respectively.

The Obsessive-Compulsive Inventory-Revised (OCI-R)

OCI-R [28] was used to assess specific OCS. It is an 18-item self-report measure of obsessive-compulsive symptoms (OCS), and a total score of 21 has been recommended as the clinical cutoff for probable OCD. The Chinese version of OCI-R [29] has good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.84) and test-retest reliability (r = 0.96) [29]. In the OCD sample of the present study, Cronbach’s α was 0.902.

Beck Anxiety Inventory (BAI)

BAI [30] was used to assess the severity of anxiety symptoms. It consists of 21 items rated on a 4-point scale. In the present study, the Chinese version of the BAI [31] was used, which has demonstrated good internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.95). In the present study, Cronbach’s α was 0.947 in the full sample, 0.949 in the OCD group, and 0.797 in the HC group. The BAI total score ranges from 0 to 63, with scores of 0–7, 8–15, 16–25, and 26–63 indicating minimal, mild, moderate, and severe anxiety, respectively.

Beck Depression Inventory (BDI)

BDI [32] is a 21-item self-report scale used to assess the severity of depressive symptoms. In the present study, the Chinese version of the BDI [33] was used, which has shown good test-retest reliability (r = 0.55) and internal consistency reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.94). In the present study, Cronbach’s α was 0.920 in the full sample, 0.915 in the OCD group, and 0.837 in the HC group. The BDI total score ranges from 0 to 63, with scores of 0–13, 14–19, 20–28, and 29–63 indicating minimal, mild, moderate, and severe depressive symptoms, respectively.

Attentional bias assessment task

This study used a dual-task paradigm of free-viewing combined with visuospatial working memory (WM) task (Fig. 1). The pictures were selected from Chinese obsessive-compulsive symptom provocation picture set (COCSP-PS) [34], from which 16 pairs of Contamination-Neutral pictures, 16 pairs of Neutral-Contamination pictures, and 16 pairs of Neutral-Neutral pictures were randomly selected. Nine-point calibration was used in eye-tracking experiment, and drift correction was performed before each trial.

Attentional bias assessment task. Note: when no red square appears, it indicates a condition without cognitive load. When the number of red squares is 1 to 3, it indicates a low cognitive load condition. The condition of high cognitive load is the appearance of 4 to 6 red squares. The pictures are Neutral-Neutral, contamination-Neutral and Neutral-contamination picture pairs

There were 12 irregularly positioned gray squares on the screen and 0–6 red squares would randomly appear at these positions (0 for no cognitive load condition, 1–3 for the low cognitive load condition and 4–6 for the high cognitive load condition). Participants were asked to remember the position and order in which the red squares appeared. A “+” fixation appeared on the screen for 500 ms, followed by a random pair of pictures for 2000 ms. Participants should freely view the pictures on the screen. Next, the “+” fixation appeared on the screen again. After 500 ms, 12 gray squares were displayed on the screen. Participants need to click on the positions where the red squares appeared before in sequence. The fixed maximum reaction time is 8000 ms. If participants complete all clicks within the fixed time, they will directly proceed to the next trial after 1200 ms.

There were 4 exercises before the formal experiment, and the corrections were recalibrated after the exercises. The formal experiment consisted of 144 trials, with 48 trials for each type of picture pairs. For each type of picture pairs, there were 16 trials in no cognitive load condition, low cognitive load condition, and high cognitive load condition, respectively, presented in random order. It took approximately 45 minutes to complete 144 trials. After completing 72 trials, the participants can choose to rest for 5 minutes and then continue the experiment.

The fixation data of participants under the Contamination-Neutral and Neutral-Contamination picture pairs were selected to analyze the two components of attentional bias. The indicators included the direction of initial fixation (DIF) reflecting initial attentional vigilance, and the initial fixation duration (IFD) reflecting difficulty in attentional disengagement.

Meanwhile, gaze data were used to calculate the proportion of attention time in Contamination-Neutral and Neutral-Contamination picture pairs to investigate how attention time changed over time.

Equipment

Eyelink1000 produced by SR Research Company was used to collect eye tracking data of left eye (sampling rate: 1000 Hz). Participants sat 60 cm away from the screen (screen resolution: 1280 × 1024 pixels). All pictures were presented in the same size (1024 × 768 pixels resolution). Following the recommendation in the EyeLink 1000 User Manual regarding the treatment of short fixations, fixation events shorter than 80 ms were excluded during offline preprocessing.

Data analysis

Data Viewer 4.3.1.0 and MATLAB were used to preprocess the eye tracking data. SPSS 26.0 and R 4.4.3 were used for statistical analysis.

Independent t-tests and chi-square tests were used to compare differences between OCD and HC in demographics, clinical indicators and attentional bias characteristics. To explore the potential influence of medication status, additional exploratory analyses were conducted to compare DIF-related measures between medicated and unmedicated patients. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Although some DIF-related variables in the HC group deviated from normality, parametric tests were retained as the primary analyses because the two groups had moderate and nearly equal sample sizes, and parametric tests are generally considered robust to violations of the normality assumption under such conditions. In addition, Mann-Whitney U tests were conducted as non-parametric sensitivity analyses for variables that were not normally distributed to examine the robustness of the findings. Repeated measures analysis of variance was used to compare attentional bias characteristics, with group (OCD vs. HC) as a between-subjects factor and cognitive load condition as a within-subjects factor. For repeated-measures ANOVA and simple-effect analyses, effect sizes were reported as partial eta squared, ηp2. When follow-up pairwise comparisons were conducted, Bonferroni correction was applied to control for multiple comparisons. Adjusted p values are reported. The Pearson correlation coefficient was used to analyze the relationship between attentional bias and clinical indicators.

To further explore how attentional allocation changed over time for each group, we adopted a data-driven temporal-course analysis based on a moving-average approach [35, 36], combined with a cluster-based permutation test to control the family-wise error rate. Specifically, each trial contained 2000 samples in total, corresponding to the 2000-ms stimulus presentation period. A 250-ms moving window was applied with a step size of 1 ms, yielding 1751 overlapping time windows per trial (2000−250+1 = 1751). For each moving window, the proportional attention time allocated to contamination-related stimuli was calculated as the dependent variable, thereby generating a time-series signal of proportional attention time. Since adjacent time points were likely to exhibit similar effects, cluster-based permutation tests with 1000 random permutations were used to correct for multiple comparisons across time.

Differences in attentional bias characteristics between OCD and HC

We compared the differences in attentional bias characteristics between the two groups using independent t-tests. However, under both low (t = 2.202, p = 0.030) and high cognitive load conditions (t = 4.005, p < 0.001), IFD was significantly higher in the OCD group than in HC (Table 2). To further assess the robustness of these findings, Mann-Whitney U tests were conducted as non-parametric sensitivity analyses for the DIF-related variables that deviated from normality. The non-parametric sensitivity analyses showed that DIF (U = 1162.50, Z = −2.99, p = 0.003), as well as DIF under no cognitive load (U = 1140.00, Z = −3.12, p = 0.002), low cognitive load (U = 1253.00, Z = −2.50, p = 0.013), and high cognitive load (U = 1106.00, Z = −3.30, p < 0.001), remained significantly different between the two groups. The pattern of results was consistent with that obtained from the parametric analyses (Supplementary Table S1).

Temporal-course analysis

Temporal-course analyses demonstrated distinct time windows of group differences in attention allocation toward contamination-related stimuli. Under the no-load condition, OCD patients showed significantly higher proportions of attention time compared to healthy controls between 678 and 1751 ms (Fig. 5), with no significant differences outside this window. Under the low-load condition, a significant difference emerged at 662 ms and dissipated after 1751 ms (Fig. 6). Under the high-load condition, group differences emerged earlier, at approximately 635 ms (Fig. 7). Notably, across all cognitive load conditions, OCD patients differed significantly from healthy controls only during the middle stage of stimulus presentation, with no significant differences observed in the early or late stages.

Discussion

The present study provides novel evidence that cognitive load modulates attentional bias towards contamination-related stimuli in individuals with OCD. With increasing cognitive load, attentional disengagement difficulties were exacerbated, and disengagement impairments occurred earlier in the time course of attentional processing. These findings suggest that the sustained attentional bias toward threatening or symptom-related stimuli in OCD patients may be associated with limited cognitive control.

Compared with HC, OCD patients exhibited a significantly difference in DIF for contamination-related stimuli, reflecting the heightened attentional vigilance. Notably, cognition load did not significantly modulate the DIF, suggesting that attentional vigilance to contamination cues in OCD may be relatively independent of concurrent cognitive control. Consistent with the attentional vigilance hypothesis, individuals with OCD were thought to exhibit facilitated automatic detection of symptom-relevant stimuli at an early stage of attentional processing, along with decreased perceptual thresholds for identifying such stimuli [37]. Neurobiological evidence supports this interpretation, with the amygdala playing a central role in early threat processing by receiving rapid sensory input and projecting feedback signals to sensory cortices. Through these reciprocal connections, the amygdala can detect threatening stimuli earlier and bias perceptual competition toward threat-related cues [38]. Consequently, attentional vigilance is thought to reflect a bottom-up, stimulus-driven process, which is largely automatic and minimally influenced by higher-order cognitive control mechanisms.

OCD patients exhibited prolonged initial fixation duration (IFD) on contamination-related stimuli, reflecting difficulty in attentional disengagement in the early stage of attention processing. This aligns with both prior research [39, 40] and schema theory [41], which posits that symptom-relevant stimuli may preferentially activate the symptom-related schemas in patients, resulting in prolonged processing. Furthermore, Individuals typically exhibit difficulty disengaging attention from complex and detailed stimuli [42]. Given that OCD patients may perceive greater threat from contamination-related stimuli, this could contribute to attentional biases during early processing stages. Research also indicates a significant relationship between perceived threat level and attentional disengagement difficulties in OCD [43]. Although IFD is a duration-based eye-tracking index that has been used to reflect early attentional maintenance, it does not provide a direct measure of attentional disengagement. Therefore, the interpretation regarding disengagement should be considered preliminary. Future studies should include additional indices, such as latency-based measures and overall dwell time, to provide more convergent evidence for attentional disengagement processes.

This study identifies cognitive load as an additional factor influencing attentional disengagement in individuals with OCD. Temporal-course analysis further revealed that higher cognitive load prompted an earlier onset of these disengagement impairments. This suggests a model which early attentional processing relies on strategic target mechanisms [44], regulated by cognitive control and inhibition [12]. The dual-process model further posits that attentional bias stems from hyperactivation of the reflexive/reward system [45]. Contextual factors, such as cognitive load, may selectively impair the reflective/control system, leading to reflexive/reward system dominance [46].

This study also found that IFD was positively correlated with the scores of OCI, BAI, and BDI in all subjects under high cognitive load conditions. While previous studies in non-clinical samples linked OCS to attentional bias [5, 47], this study extends those findings by supporting a correlation between OCS and early attentional maintenance for symptom-relevant stimuli in clinical OCD patients. Moreover, in line with Attentional Control Theory [13], anxiety appears to impact top-down attentional control processes by depleting cognitive resources. Empirical studies suggest that anxiety not only enhances attentional capture by threatening stimuli but also delays disengagement from such stimuli [48–50]. These findings also demonstrate the influence of cognitive control on attentional processes.

Limitation

There are several limitations in current study. First, the sample size in this study was relatively small. Second, emotional responses to stimuli and cognitive load may interact bidirectionally, potentially influencing working memory performance. In addition, medication effects could not be fully controlled. Although exploratory analyses showed no significant differences between medicated and unmedicated patients in most fixation-related measures, except for the no-load DIF index, the limited subgroup sample size reduced statistical power. Therefore, future studies with larger medication-free samples and detailed dose-response analyses are needed to clarify the potential influence of medication on eye tracking and task performance. Moreover, inter-subject designs assigning distinct cognitive load conditions to different groups prior to attentional bias assessment may help further address the effects of cognitive load manipulation.

Conclusion

This study explored the effect of cognitive load on attentional bias in OCD patients. We found that OCD patients exhibited attentional bias to symptom related stimuli. The difficulty in disengagement was affected by cognitive load, which suggest that the sustained attentional bias toward threatening or symptom-related stimuli in OCD patients may be associated with limited cognitive control. This study provided a basis for further exploration of the mechanism of attentional bias in OCD patients. It will help us further understand OCD and optimize clinical treatment.
