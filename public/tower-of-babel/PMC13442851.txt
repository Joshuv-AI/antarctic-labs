MiT fusions, TSC1–TSC2 divergence, and stem-like programs reveal distinct origins and vulnerabilities in PEComa
Authors: Justine Gantzer, Xiaofan Lu, Céline Charon-Barra, Charles Vinson, Noëlle Weingertner, Antonin Fattori, Marie-Pierre Chenard, Damien Plassard, Serge Vicaire, François Le Loarer, Maud Toulmonde, Marie Karanian, Mehdi Brahmi, Carine Ngo, Axel Le Cesne, Alice Hervieu, Lenaïg Mescam-Mancini, François Bertucci, Juliette Beaujot, Thomas Ryckewaert, Philippe Rochaix, Thibaud Valentin, Nicolas Macagno, Florence Duffaud, Matthias Tallegas, Bruno Chetaille, Isabelle Valo, Emmanuelle Bompas, Marick Lae, Flore Delalande, Francisco Llamas-Gutierrez, Nathalie Rioux-Leclercq, Jean-Yves Blay, Jean-Emmanuel Kurtz, Gabriel G Malouf

ABSTRACT
AbstractPerivascular epithelioid cell neoplasms (PEComas) are ultra-rare mesenchymal tumors lacking a molecular classification to guide therapy. Here we perform comprehensive multi-omic profiling of an unselected PEComa cohort. We identify frequent MITF rearrangements involving actin gene partners (ACTA2, ACTG1 and ACTB). Anatomical stratification reveals cyclin-dependent kinase module mutations in gynecologic tumors, whereas soft tissue, gastrointestinal, and pelvic tumors lacked mTOR pathway alterations but are enriched for TFE3/MITF rearrangements. Transcriptomic analysis defines four subtypes with distinct lineage programs—melanocytic, mesenchymal, or adipogenic—as well as unique mutational patterns and clinical behaviors. Notably, an aggressive stem-like subtype enriched for TP53/RB1 mutations exhibits high proliferation, activation of embryonic and Hedgehog signaling, immune infiltration, and resistance to mTOR inhibitors, but potential responsiveness to immunotherapy. Single-nucleus RNA sequencing reveals intra-tumoral heterogeneity within this subtype, including divergent inflammatory states. Together, these findings establish a molecular classification framework and identify actionable vulnerabilities in PEComa.Subject terms: Sarcoma, Cancer genomics, Tumour heterogeneity

Introduction

Perivascular epithelioid cell neoplasms (PEComas) are ultra-rare mesenchymal tumors that co-express smooth muscle and melanocytic markers1–8. PEComas predominantly arise sporadically, with an estimated incidence of 0.2 per million population per year, and can develop in virtually any anatomical location9,10. They occur more frequently in women, with a 6:1 female to male ratio and a median age at diagnosis ranging from 43–67 years11–13. Clinically, PEComas display a wide spectrum of behavior, ranging from indolent lesions to aggressive metastatic disease, with a reported 5-year event-free survival of approximately 58%14. To assist in prognostic stratification, a histological classification based on six tumor characteristics, known as the Folpe criteria, was proposed in 200511. More recently, we developed the PEC-PRO score, a prognostic tool designed to estimate the risk of postoperative recurrence—including both local relapse and distant metastasis—in patients with localized PEComa, based on clinicopathologic variables such as necrosis, vascular invasion, margin status and sex14.

Currently, the systemic treatment of advanced PEComas primarily relies on mTOR inhibitors, which yield a median progression-free survival (PFS) of approximately 10 months13,15–18. This therapeutic strategy in PEComas is grounded in the frequent activation of the mTOR signaling pathway, driven by recurrent molecular alterations. These include bi-allelic inactivation of TSC1 and TSC2—observed in approximately 10–20% and 30–40% of cases, respectively—as well as truncating mutations in FLCN, found in fewer than 5% of cases17,19–22. These alterations are largely mutually exclusive and converge on dysregulated mTOR pathway activation, providing a mechanistic rationale for mTOR inhibition. Additionally, TFE3 gene rearrangements, genetically confirmed by fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) or sequencing, occur in approximately 6–23% of PEComas across studies and define a distinct molecular subset19. To date, only isolated cases of MiT family rearrangements have been reported in PEComas, including one SFPQ::TFEB fusion in a melanocytic PEComa and one ACTG1::MITF fusion in an MITF-overexpressing PEComa23,24. However, the prevalence of MiT family fusions in the unselected PEComa cohort has not yet been systematically assessed. In parallel, recurrent mutations in tumor suppressor genes such as TP53, RB1, and ATRX have been described, particularly in metastatic or high-grade PEComas17,21,22,25,26.

Despite these advances, several key questions remain unresolved. First, the distribution and prognostic significance of these genetic alterations have not been comprehensively assessed in an unselected cohort. Second, the potential mutual exclusivity between canonical oncogenic drivers and tumor suppressor mutations—particularly in localized disease—remains unclear. This raises the possibility that a subset of PEComas may lack known driver events (“molecular wild-type”), a population that remains poorly characterized. Importantly, while mTOR pathway activation provides a strong rationale for mTOR inhibitor therapy and has been associated with durable responses in a subset of patients (e.g., the AMPECT trial)27, clinical benefit is not uniform, underscoring the need for predictive biomarkers and alternative therapeutic strategies. Moreover, unlike epithelial cancers such as breast and endometrial carcinoma, where transcriptomic classifications have complemented genomic driver alterations to improve disease stratification and therapeutic decision-making, no such framework exists for PEComas—despite their pronounced clinical, anatomical, and molecular heterogeneity28–31. In sarcomas, emerging transcriptomic studies have already revealed clinically meaningful molecular subgroups in specific subtypes, suggesting that a similar approach could be fruitful in PEComas32–34.

To address these gaps, we performed the multi-omic characterization of a large, unselected cohort of primary PEComas, integrating targeted genomic profiling, bulk and single-nucleus RNA sequencing (snRNA-seq), and DNA methylation analysis. Our study identifies frequent MITF rearrangements involving actin gene partners, representing a previously unrecognized molecular driver in PEComa biology. We developed a transcriptomic classification system delineating four subtypes with distinct molecular signatures, biological programs, and prognostic implications. Notably, we identified a highly aggressive, stem-like subtype enriched for TP53/RB1 alterations and immune infiltration, which exhibited limited benefit from mTOR inhibition but a high likelihood of responding to immunotherapy. Together, these findings establish a framework for the biological classification of PEComas and open avenues for personalized therapeutic strategies.

Results

Study population characteristics

Among the 150 patients identified in the NETSARC+ database (https://netsarc.sarcomabcb.org/), 90 primary tumors were collected and diagnosis confirmed centrally by an expert pathologist (C. C-B). Among these, sufficient material for nucleic acid extraction was available in 80 samples (Fig. S1, Supplementary Data 1). The demographic distribution was predominantly female, with a male-to-female ratio of 1:3. Patients presented a median age of 54 years at diagnosis, with an age range from 13–94 years. The anatomical origins of the primary tumors were varied, including gynecological organs (n = 22), kidneys (n = 13), soft tissues (n = 10), gastrointestinal organs (n = 9), retroperitoneal organs (n = 9), peritoneum (n = 10), pelvis (n = 4) and lungs (n = 3). For the subset of patients with available initial diagnostic assessments, the median primary tumor size was 65 mm (range: 13–300 mm), and the disease was predominantly localized at diagnosis (n = 65/72, 90.3%). All 80 patients underwent a TFE3 break-apart FISH assay, revealing seven positive cases (8.75%). The median follow-up was 31.5 months (interquartile range: 17.3–61.8), during which 29 patients (39%) died, including 22 who died from the disease (Fig. S1).

Within the study cohort, 27 patients had metastatic disease at some point, including 7 who presented with metastasis at diagnosis. All these patients received first-line metastatic treatment, with 18 patients receiving mTOR inhibitors—a treatment that remains ongoing for one patient (Supplementary Data 2). Other therapeutic approaches involved chemotherapy for seven patients and anti-angiogenic therapy for two. Subsequent therapeutic management involved second-line systemic treatments in 20 patients: 12 received chemotherapy, five underwent anti-angiogenic therapy, and three received mTOR inhibitors. Overall, eight patients were treated with three or more lines of therapy. Across all lines, 23 patients received mTOR inhibitors, 10 received anthracycline-based chemotherapy, and two received immunotherapy.

For genomic analyses, DNA and RNA of sufficient quality were successfully extracted from 78 and 69 tumor samples, respectively. We performed targeted gene panel sequencing (TGPS), a hybrid-capture approach covering the complete exonic regions of 468 clinically relevant genes, on 78 PEComa samples. Based on RNA quality and library metrics, 53 samples underwent paired-end (PE) RNA sequencing (RNA-seq), while 16 underwent single-end (SE) RNA-seq. Additionally, DNA methylation profiling was conducted in 46 tumor samples.

Discovery of MITF fusion transcripts in PEComa

We explored the fusion transcript landscape of samples subjected to paired-end RNA-seq using two independent software tools. Only fusions detected by both tools were considered for reporting (Supplementary Data 3). Among the 37 identified fusions, 29 were unique and distributed across 22 different samples, with one sample alone harboring 13 distinct fusions. Remarkably, we identified fusion transcripts involving MITF, the founding member of the MiT family of transcription factors, which plays a crucial role in promoting gene expression related to cell proliferation and growth. Analogous to TFE3 fusions, the identified MITF fusions involve the 3’ region of MITF and various 5’ partners, preserving the basic helix-loop-helix leucine zipper (bHLH-LZ) domains required for dimerization and DNA binding, and are therefore likely to confer oncogenic activity.

We identified seven cases of MITF rearrangements (13.2%), all involving actin coding genes: five with ACTA2::MITF, one with ACTG1::MITF and one with ACTB::MITF (Fig. 1a). FISH confirmed MITF rearrangements in four of the seven cases for which material was available (Fig. S2). The predicted breakpoints occurred within introns immediately upstream of MITF exon 3 and downstream of exon 3 of the respective actin genes (ACTA2, ACTB, and ACTG1), thereby positioning the actin gene promoter upstream of the fusion point. In addition, we identified two TSC2 rearrangements predicted to cause loss of tumor-suppressor function. One case harbored a TSC2::CCNF fusion, with predicted breakpoints within intron 3 of TSC2 and intron 9 of CCNF, and the other carried a TSC2::PSMD9 fusion involving the same TSC2 breakpoint and a junction after exon 4 of PSMD9.

Within the cohort of 53 RNA-seq samples, TFE3-associated gene fusions were confirmed in four cases (7.5%) by FISH—three with SFPQ::TFE3 and one with a newly described PSPC1::TFE3 fusion (Fig. 1a)35–37. Across all 80 samples with available material, we assessed TFE3 and MITF protein expression by immunohistochemistry (IHC). TFE3-rearranged tumors showed markedly higher TFE3 H-scores compared with MITF-rearranged tumors and all other groups (all, p < 0.005; Fig. 1b). In addition to elevated expression levels, TFE3 staining was strictly nuclear in all TFE3-rearranged cases (Fig. S3a). MITF H-scores were significantly higher in MITF-rearranged tumors compared with TFE3-rearranged tumors (p = 0.030) and showed a trend toward higher expression compared with other categories (both, p < 0.1) (Fig. 1c). Consistently, analysis of RNA-seq data from the 53 samples demonstrated that fusion-positive tumors exhibited significantly higher mRNA expression of the corresponding MiT family transcription factor compared with fusion-negative tumors (TFE3: p = 0.033; MITF: p = 0.024; Fig. S3b). Morphologically, PEComas with MITF rearrangements were indistinguishable from other types of PEComa (Fig. S3c).

Genomic landscape and anatomical correlations

In our investigation of 78 PEComa samples, we identified 229 high-confidence somatic mutations, predominantly non-synonymous, identified through tumor-only sequencing using computational germline filtering against population and cancer variant databases (Supplementary Data 4). The majority of these non-synonymous mutations were categorized as missense mutations (69%), followed by frameshift deletions (15%), nonsense mutations (5%) and splice site (5%). Using the dNdScv algorithm, we identified 32 significantly mutated genes (false discovery rate [FDR] <0.05; Supplementary Data 5), of which seven were classified as putative driver mutations, present in at least 5% of the samples (Fig. 2a). Among those, we identified mutations of TSC2 (n = 18, 23%) and TSC1 (n = 8, 10%), along with notable tumor suppressor genes such as TP53 (n = 14, 18%), RB1 (n = 8, 10%) corroborating recent findings38. In addition, we found mutations in chromatin remodelers, including KMT2D (n = 6, 8%) and ATRX (n = 4, 5%), along with MED12 (n = 4, 5%), a component of the cyclin-dependent kinase module (CKM) of the mediator complex.

Because TP53 has a well-described protein-expression phenotype, we also documented p53 IHC patterns in TP53-mutant tumors. Among the 14 TP53-mutant tumors, 11 carried missense and 3 carried truncating variants (frameshift, nonstop, splice-site). Of the missense mutations, 6 showed diffuse p53 overexpression, whereas all three truncating mutations exhibited complete loss of p53 expression (Fig. 2a). While concordance between IHC and sequencing of TP53 is known to be variable across tumor types39–41, the staining patterns observed here aligned with known protein-expression phenotypes associated with missense and truncating TP53 mutations.

Besides TSC2 and TSC1 mutations, additional alterations were identified in other mTOR pathway genes, including MTOR, RPTOR, and PTEN. Specifically, mutations affecting the mTOR pathway were observed in 28 cases (36%), underscoring the pathway’s central role in PEComa tumorigenesis (Fig. 2a). In addition to mTOR pathway alterations, we observed that mutated genes were frequently involved in pathways related to DNA repair (23%), chromatin remodeling (18%), SWI/SNF complex (8%), and CKM complex (8%). Of note, in addition to MED12, CKM complex involves another gene CDK8 that significantly mutated in 3% of the samples (2 cases) (Fig. 2a). TSC1 and TSC2 mutations exhibited marginal mutual exclusivity (odds ratio [OR]: 0.45, p = 0.083; Fig. 2b), with mutations detected in 7 tumors harboring TSC1 alterations only, 17 harboring TSC2 alterations only, and 1 tumor harboring alterations in both genes. In this case, the TSC1 mutation was a truncating frameshift with high variant allele frequency, consistent with a clonal event, whereas the TSC2 variant was a missense alteration detected at lower variant allele frequency, suggesting a subclonal event of uncertain functional significance. Mutations in the mTOR pathway demonstrated significant mutual exclusivity with both TFE3 (OR: 0.10, p = 0.005) and MITF rearrangements (OR: 0.27, p = 0.041), as well as chromatin remodeling mutations (OR: 0.43, p = 0.015), suggesting divergent tumorigenic mechanisms. TP53/RB1 mutations showed marginal mutual exclusivity with TSC1, TSC2, and mTOR pathway alterations but significantly co-occurred with SWI/SNF (OR: 22.7, p = 0.043) and chromatin remodeling genes (OR: 58, p = 0.002), potentially contributing to a more aggressive tumor phenotype by affecting cell cycle regulation and apoptosis.

To further investigate whether mutual exclusivity patterns could be influenced by differences in tumor grade, we stratified the genomic landscape according to the Folpe classification, which incorporates key histologic features to define PEComas as benign, of intermediate malignancy, or malignant11. Among the 78 tumors, 8 were classified as benign, 52 as malignant, 7 as intermediate, and 11 as unclassified. Focusing on the benign and malignant subsets, we compared the prevalence of selected genomic alterations (Fig. S4, Supplementary Data 6). Mutations or rearrangements involving TP53, RB1, ATRX, and genes related to chromatin remodeling, DNA repair, SWI/SNF, and CKM complexes were observed exclusively or more frequently in malignant cases, although none reached statistical significance (all p > 0.1). TP53 mutations were present in 1 of 8 benign tumors (12.5%) and 13 of 52 malignant tumors (25%; p = 0.667), while TSC2 mutations were detected in both groups (3/8 [37.5%] vs. 8/52 [15.4%]; p = 0.154). These findings suggest that certain genomic alterations were enriched in malignant PEComas but were not specific to high-grade disease, and that mutual exclusivity patterns might not be solely attributable to differences in histologic grade20,21,24,42,43.

Previous studies reported that approximately half of malignant TSC2-mutant PEComas exhibit TP53 alterations20. In our cohort of eight malignant TSC2-mutant tumors, three (38%) carried TP53 missense mutations and exhibited diffuse p53 overexpression. Among the five cases without TP53 mutations, one showed complete absence of p53 staining, whereas the others displayed a wild-type p53 immunostaining pattern (Fig. 1C). In total, four of eight malignant TSC2-mutant tumors (50%) displayed either a TP53 mutation or an abnormal p53 IHC pattern.

While studies investigating the genomic landscape of PEComa have made significant progress in recent years, the potential influence of the tumor’s anatomical origin on its mutational profile remains less explored19,44. We discovered distinct molecular fingerprints associated with different anatomical origins (Fig. 2c). Tumors from gynecologic organs exhibited a pattern of exclusive CKM complex mutations (MED12 and CDK8) (6 of 20 [30%] vs. 0 of 58; p < 0.001), contrasting with previous findings in uterine PEComas42. Conversely, tumors arising in soft tissues, gastrointestinal organs and the pelvis lacked mTOR pathway mutations (0 of 23 vs. 28 of 55 [51%]; p < 0.001) but were enriched for MITF/TFE3 fusions (8 of 23 [35%] vs. 6 of 55 [11%], p = 0.021). Pelvic PEComas exhibited no significant mutations.

While prior literature has primarily associated ACTIN::MITF fusions with cutaneous tumors45, in our cohort, two MITF-rearranged cases were located in the skin: one superficial lesion of the left calf harboring an ACTG1::MITF fusion, and another involving the left leg with an ACTB::MITF fusion. The remaining MITF-rearranged tumors arose in visceral or deep soft tissues, expanding the known anatomical spectrum of this fusion type. Notably, none of the MITF-rearranged tumors showed S100 protein expression by IHC, while only one TFE3-rearranged tumor exhibited weak S100 positivity (H-score 5; Supplementary Data 1). Together with their distribution across multiple anatomic sites, these findings suggest that MITF-rearranged PEComas share overlapping molecular features with previously described cutaneous ACTIN::MITF fusion neoplasms, while differing in S100 protein expression and anatomic distribution46.

To validate the genomic landscape and anatomical distribution of PEComa, we analyzed the GENIE cohort, which includes TGPS data for 109 primary PEComa-related tumors, comprising 61 non-uterine PEComas, 19 uterine PEComas, and 29 renal angiomyolipomas (AML; a renal PEComa subtype). Among these tumors, six harbored TFE3 rearrangements, while no MITF rearrangements were identified. Among the significant mutations identified in our cohort, TSC2, TP53, ATRX, TSC1, RB1, and MED12 were altered in more than 5% of the samples (Fig. S5a). Notably, consistent with our findings that tumors originating from gynecologic organs exhibit an exclusive pattern of CKM complex mutations, all five MED12 mutations were restricted to uterine cases in the GENIE cohort (p < 0.001). Additionally, we observed mutual exclusivity between TSC1 and TSC2 mutations (OR: 0.20, p = 0.030) and between TP53/RB1 and TSC2 mutations (OR: 0.87, p = 0.041) (Fig. S5b).

Prognostic role of TP53/RB1 mutations

Among the driver mutations identified, TP53 mutations or TP53/RB1 pathway mutations were notably prevalent in cases with poor disease-specific survival (DSS) (Fig. 3a). Specifically, of the 14 TP53 and eight RB1 mutations detected, four TP53 and two RB1 were present in metastatic cases (Fig. 3b). We also highlighted other mutations occurring in at least two metastatic cases, including TSC2 and ATRX. The occurrence of TP53 mutations in metastatic cases was significantly higher compared to localized tumors (4 [67%] vs. 9 [14%], p = 0.0093), similarly to TP53/RB1 (4 [67%] vs. 13 [20%], p = 0.028). While this finding contrasts with a recent report where TP53 alterations were confined to renal PEComas22, it aligns with multiple other studies demonstrating TP53 pathway disruption in malignant PEComas of diverse extra-renal origins20,21,24,42. In addition, we found that metastatic cases showed more ATRX mutations as compared to localized tumors (2 [33%] vs. 1 [2%], p = 0.018). To discern whether the prognostic impact of TP53 or TP53/RB1 pathway mutations is independent of metastatic status—a strong prognostic factor of DSS itself (HR: 5.92, 95% CI: 2.24–15.68, p = 0.0003; Fig. 3c)—we performed multivariate Cox regression analyses including metastatic status as a covariate. In this model, TP53 mutations remained independently associated with poorer DSS (HR: 3.33, 95% CI: 1.21–9.12, p = 0.019), whereas metastatic status showed a trend toward significance (HR: 2.95, 95% CI: 0.92–9.40, p = 0.068). Similarly, TP53/RB1 pathway alterations retained independent prognostic significance (HR: 3.41, 95% CI: 1.30–8.92, p = 0.013), with metastatic status also independently associated with DSS in this model (HR: 3.11, 95% CI: 1.01–9.57, p = 0.048). Therefore, our data indicate that TP53/RB1 mutations not only correlate with metastasis but also contribute independently to a poorer prognosis in PEComa.

Impact of genomic alterations on mTOR inhibitor response

Among the 18 patients who received mTOR inhibitors as first-line therapy, the median PFS was 5.5 months (range: 0.1–22), which is shorter than the PFS reported in prior retrospective series (approximately 9 months) and in the AMPECT trial of nab-sirolimus (10.6 months)27,47. To explore the genomic impact of response, patients were initially stratified into three genomic categories: (i) TFE3/MITF-rearranged tumors without concurrent TP53/RB1 mutations (n = 2), (ii) TSC2-mutant tumors (n = 6), and (iii) TP53/RB1-mutant tumors (n = 10). Notably, in our cohort, TSC1-mutated tumors did not recur and therefore did not receive systemic therapy, precluding evaluation of mTOR inhibitor response in this subgroup.

Despite a trend in PFS across these categories, the differences did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.138; Fig. S6). Median PFS was 16 months for TFE3/MITF-rearranged tumors, 7.5 months for TSC2-mutant tumors, and 4.5 months for TP53/RB1-mutant tumors. Clinical benefit rate (CBR), defined as complete or partial response or stable disease lasting ≥12 months, was 100%, 50%, and 20%, respectively (p = 0.071; Fig. S6). To increase statistical robustness, we subsequently stratified patients according to TP53/RB1 mutational status. Patients harboring TP53 and/or RB1 mutations (n = 10) had shorter PFS compared with those without these alterations (n = 8) (median 4.5 vs. 13.0 months; p = 0.060; Fig. 3d). Consistently, CBR was lower in the TP53/RB1-mutant group (20%) compared with TP53/RB1–wild-type tumors (62.5%), although this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.088; Fig. 3d).

Transcriptomic classification of PEComa

Despite considerable progress in characterizing the genomic landscape of PEComas, the transcriptomic diversity of these tumors remains underexplored. To address this, we developed a comprehensive strategy to analyze the transcriptomes of both non-metastatic and metastatic PEComa cases (Fig. S7). Our study included 69 PEComa samples, analyzed using two RNA-seq technologies: 53 samples with PE and 16 with SE RNA-seq. To effectively manage the variability introduced by the differing sequencing methodologies and the varied number of samples, we organized the data into three distinct datasets: a discovery dataset comprising 48 cases with no confirmed metastasis and sequenced with PE technology, a validation dataset consisting of 15 cases with no confirmed metastasis and sequenced with SE technology, and an exploration dataset containing six cases with confirmed metastatic disease.

In our analysis using the discovery dataset, we determined four potential subtypes based on the highest stability index generated through Monte Carlo reference-based consensus clustering using the most variable genes (Fig. S8a). Subsequent consensus non-negative matrix factorization (NMF) identified four well-defined transcriptomic subtypes—labeled as C1 (n = 10), C2 (n = 12), C3 (n = 12), and C4 (n = 14)—each characterized by distinct connectivity matrices (Fig. S8b). Principal component analysis (PCA) corroborated the molecular uniqueness of these subtypes (Fig. S8c).

Molecular characterization and prognostic implications of transcriptomic subtypes

Having identified four distinct transcriptomic subtypes in PEComa, our focus shifted to understanding the molecular drivers and clinical implications of these subtypes (Fig. 4a, b). Our analysis commenced with the Melanocytic subtype (C1), marked by the overexpression of melanocytic markers such as TYR, TYRP1, and SLC45A2. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed modest enrichment of the estrogen response pathway in C1 compared with other subtypes, suggesting a lineage responsive to hormonal signaling. This subtype predominantly harbored TFE3/MITF gene rearrangements, accounting for 80% of all identified translocations, and notably lacked TSC1, TSC2, or other mTOR pathway alterations. The Mesenchymal subtype (C2) was characterized by elevated expression of mesenchymal markers (DES, COL1A1, MMP2, and CLDN1) and strong enrichment of the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway. The Adipogenic subtype (C3) showed markers indicative of adipogenic lineage (MYL3, MYRIP, GPX3, SOX6, PPARG, APOC1, APOE, and LDHD). Despite this adipocyte-like transcriptomic signature, histopathologic re-review did not reveal overt adipocytic differentiation, suggesting that the observed lineage program reflects transcriptional priming rather than morphological differentiation—a pattern similarly observed in other mesenchymal tumors where transcriptomic lineage signals are used to infer putative cells of origin47. This subtype also demonstrated strong enrichment of the oxidative phosphorylation pathway, suggesting enhanced metabolic activity that may support tumor growth and survival under unfavorable conditions. Interestingly, both subtypes showed a significant mutation burden in the mTOR pathway (16 [67%] vs. 1 [5%], p < 0.001), yet each displayed unique mutational profiles. Specifically, TSC1 mutations were primarily confined to Mesenchymal C2 (4 [33%] vs. 1 [3%]; p = 0.014), while TSC2 mutations were more common in Adipogenic C3 (9 [75%] vs. 1 [3%]; p < 0.001) compared to other subtypes.

The most compelling findings emerged from the Stem-like subtype (C4), which overexpressed genes linked to cell cycle control and embryogenesis (TOP2A, MKI67, BRCA1, CDK1, CCNB1, AURKA, MCMC10, GMNN and NEK2), together with strong enrichment of the embryonic stem cell signature and modest enrichment of the hedgehog signaling pathway, consistent with a stem-like transcriptional program. The proliferative and stem-cell-like features of this subtype were further supported by a significantly higher Ki67 IHC staining (p = 0.006; Fig. 4c) and elevated stemness index score (p = 0.009; Fig. 4d). Of note, this subtype also demonstrated modest enrichment of immune response pathways, including the interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) response and the MHC class II antigen presentation pathway, suggesting enhanced antigen processing and immune activation. Genetically, C4 was significantly enriched for TP53 mutations (5 [42%] vs. 2 [6%]; p = 0.01) and alterations in the TP53/RB1 pathway (6 [50%] vs. 2 [6%]; p = 0.002), consistent with p53 pathway suppression.

To evaluate the clinical implications of these findings, we employed the Complexity Index in SARComas (CINSARC) signature, which has demonstrated consistent prognostic value across sarcoma cohorts, sample types, and transcriptomic platforms48. Our analysis revealed that almost all cases in the Stem-like subtype were classified as high-risk for metastatic outcomes (13 [93%] vs. 10 [29%], p < 0.001; Fig. 4a). This observation corroborates our finding that metastatic PEComa samples are enriched for TP53 mutations, suggesting a mechanistic link between TP53 mutations and the metastatic potential of this subtype. Of note, this subtype also showed the shortest median survival time to recurrence and disease-specific death compared to the other subtypes (both, p < 0.05; Fig. 4e, f). Importantly, in multivariate Cox analysis adjusting for TP53/RB1 mutation status, this subtype remained an independent predictor of poor outcome (adjusted HR = 7.5, p = 0.006 for recurrence-free survival [RFS]; adjusted HR = 6.3, p = 0.020 for DSS), underscoring that its prognostic impact extends beyond TP53/RB1 mutation alone (Fig. S9).

Therefore, through this detailed molecular and clinical characterization, we introduced a classification system, the MMAS system, categorizing PEComas into Melanocytic (MelPEC), Mesenchymal (MesPEC), Adipogenic (AdiPEC), and Stem-like (StemPEC) subtypes. This system reflects not only the transcriptomic diversity but also correlates strongly with distinct genomic landscapes, highlighting specific vulnerabilities that could be targeted therapeutically.

Independent validation of the classification system

To investigate the reproducibility of our MMAS classification system and its associated molecular traits, we identified the top 200 uniquely and significantly upregulated genes for each subtype (FDR < 0.05), yielding 134 genes for MelPEC and 200 genes for each of the other three subtypes—MMAS classifier (734 genes; Fig. S10a, Supplementary Data 7). Using nearest template prediction (NTP) on our discovery cohort, we demonstrated robust classification accuracy, misclassifying only one sample within StemPEC, confirming the system’s reliability (accuracy: 98%, Kappa: 0.972, p < 0.001; Fig. S10b).

To evaluate whether histologic grade or inclusion of benign tumors may have influenced MMAS subtype definition, we reanalyzed the discovery cohort using Folpe classification. Among the 48 tumors originally used to define MMAS, 5 were benign, 5 intermediate, and 32 malignant, with the remainder unclassified or lacking annotation. No significant association was observed between MMAS subtypes and Folpe grade categories (benign vs. malignant, p = 0.247), even when combining intermediate and malignant tumors (p = 0.263), suggesting that MMAS subtypes are not primarily determined by histologic grade. We next assessed whether the MMAS signature could be reliably applied to tumors with malignant histology but no documented metastasis at diagnosis. Using the 734-gene MMAS classifier, we predicted subtypes in 32 malignant, non-metastatic tumors (Fig. S11a). The predicted subtypes showed near-perfect concordance with their original subtype designations (accuracy = 97%, Kappa = 0.956, p < 0.001; Fig. S11b), confirming the robustness of the MMAS classification framework in malignant PEComas, independent of metastatic status.

In the validation dataset, the subtypes were consistently recapitulated (Fig. S12a). Notably, TFE3 gene rearrangements were exclusively found in MelPEC (2 [50%] vs. 0, p = 0.057), and all TP53/RB1 mutations were involved in StemPEC (2 [67%] vs. 0, p = 0.055). Although mutations in the mTOR pathway were prevalent in both MesPEC and AdiPEC, statistical significance was not achieved (4 [67%] vs. 1 [20%], p = 0.242), and the polarization of TSC1 and TSC2 was not observed, potentially due to limited sample size.

Significantly, StemPEC in the validation dataset enriched in CINSARC high-risk tumors (4 [100%] vs. 1 [9%], p = 0.003; Fig. S12a). Among the 63 PEComa samples without confirmed metastasis at presentation and profiled by transcriptomics, StemPEC consistently emerged as the subtype with the poorest outcomes (Fig. S12b), characterized by a high incidence of necrosis (8 [50%] vs. 11 [28%], p = 0.11). Furthermore, the disproportionate representation of male patients in StemPEC compared to other subtypes (8 [44%] vs. 7 [16%], p = 0.023) introduces an additional layer of sex-based complexity to its aggressive phenotype that warrants further exploration.

High metastatic risk of the StemPEC subtype

To deepen our understanding of the metastatic propensity within PEComa subtypes, we applied a multiclassification random forest classifier on the exploration dataset of six metastatic PEComa. This approach was chosen to preserve the integrity of the raw molecular signals. Our analysis revealed that five out of the six metastatic samples (83%) closely resembled the StemPEC subtype, presenting the highest prediction probability for this group (Fig. S13). Of note, the one metastatic sample not assigned to the StemPEC was devoid of a TP53 or RB1 mutation. This finding lends strong support to our hypothesis that StemPEC harbors the highest metastatic risk in the MMAS system.

Predictive value of MMAS classification for mTOR inhibitor response

Within our study cohort, transcriptomic profiles were available for 15 patients with metastatic PEComa who had received mTOR inhibitors across various treatment lines (2 MelPEC, 2 MesPEC, 4 AdiPEC and 7 StemPEC). PFS varied across MMAS subtypes, although statistical significance was not reached (p = 0.162). Median PFS was 16 months for MelPEC, 5 months for MesPEC, 13 months for AdiPEC, and 4 months for StemPEC (Fig. S14a). Interestingly, MesPEC tumors showed shorter median PFS compared to AdiPEC. This pattern is directionally consistent with findings from the AMPECT trial, in which TSC2-mutant tumors derived greater benefit from mTOR inhibition than TSC1-mutant tumors27. Notably, although MesPEC is enriched for TSC1 alterations in the broader cohort, the two MesPEC cases in this treated subset did not harbor TSC1 mutations, whereas all four AdiPEC cases harbored TSC2 mutations, which might suggest that transcriptomic features associated with TSC1-enriched subtypes could potentially relate to reduced sensitivity to mTOR inhibition.

Given the relatively larger representation and clinically aggressive phenotype of StemPEC, we compared this subtype against all others. Patients with StemPEC exhibited a shorter median PFS (4 months) compared to non-StemPEC subtypes (13 months), with a trend toward significance (p = 0.069; Fig. S14b). Consistently, CBR was significantly lower in StemPEC (1/7, 15%) than in the remaining subtypes (6/8, 75%; p = 0.032; Fig. S14c). In multivariate analysis adjusting for TP53/RB1 mutation status, StemPEC remained associated with an increased risk of progression (adjusted HR = 17.0, p = 0.032; Fig. S14d). Although limited by small sample size, these findings suggest that MMAS classification may capture transcriptomic programs associated with differential response to mTOR inhibition.

Unique tumor microenvironment landscape of StemPEC

The upregulation of the antigen-presenting process in StemPEC suggested a distinct tumor microenvironment (TME) landscape. To further investigate TME composition across PEComa subtypes, we analyzed transcriptomic data to estimate the relative abundance of nine cellular populations in both the discovery and validation datasets using MCPcounter49. Unsupervised clustering revealed two distinct TME subtypes among these samples (Fig. 5a). One TME subtype, notably enriched in the StemPEC (p = 0.007), was characterized by remarkably higher immune cell infiltration, especially of T cells, cytotoxic lymphocytes, B lineages and NK cells (all, p < 0.001), suggesting immune activation. Notably, the co-enrichment of B cells and tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) signature suggests the presence of B-cell-rich TLS, which has been associated with enhanced efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs)50. Additionally, this immune-enriched subtype exhibited upregulated expression of immune checkpoint genes, including PDCD1, CTLA4, HAVCR2 and LAG3 (all, p < 0.05). These tumors also displayed greater vascularization (p = 0.008), consistent with previous observations in melanoma and kidney cancer, where increased vasculature has been linked to improved responses to ICIs51–53.

Epigenetic underpinnings of StemPEC aggressiveness

Considering the distinct transcriptomic landscapes observed across the MMAS system, we sought to determine if these transcriptional deregulations were reflective of underlying epigenetic alterations. Differential methylation analysis was performed on 37 tumors with matched DNA methylation and RNA-seq data (11 StemPEC vs 26 other subtypes). This analysis revealed marked subtype-specific variation, with StemPEC exhibiting a total of 3025 significantly hypomethylated probes, the majority of which were localized within enhancer regions (Fig. 5b). Notably, this loss of DNA methylation in StemPEC was significantly correlated with accelerated cellular proliferation (Fig. 5c), consistent with a highly proliferative phenotype and a greater frequency of large tumors ( ≥ 5 cm; 15 [88%] vs. 24 [60%], p = 0.033), features associated with aggressive clinical behavior.

Given the critical role of enhancers in gene expression modulation and their link to oncogenic activity through hypomethylation, we integrated DNA methylation with transcriptome profiles to elucidate pivotal connections and identify key transcription factors (TFs) that might be instrumental in driving its aggressive behavior and metastatic propensity. Our integrated analysis led to the identification of 1,991 putative enhancer probe-gene pairs, where we found the overall gene expression in StemPEC to be significantly higher than in other subtypes (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Data 8). Intriguingly, the genes associated with these hypomethylated enhancers were significantly enriched in interferon-γ response and proliferative pathways (Fig. 5e), particularly those involving E2F targets, G2/M checkpoints, mitotic spindle and DNA repair (FDR < 0.05). Furthermore, by analyzing probe-enriched motifs and their association with the expression of all known TFs, we pinpointed several TFs closely linked to the unique characteristics of the StemPEC (Supplementary Data 9). Among these, FOSL1 emerged as a key factor associated with cellular proliferation, while BACH2 was identified as a TF related to immune responses.

Inter/intra-tumor heterogeneity of MMAS through single-nucleus sequencing

To investigate the inter-tumor heterogeneity of the MMAS classification system at the cellular level, we performed snRNA-seq on five patient samples (S01–S05) for which sufficient material was available. These samples included two from the MesPEC subtype (S01 and S02), two from the AdiPEC subtype (S03 and S04) derived from primary tumors, and one from the StemPEC subtype (S05) derived from a metastatic tumor (Fig. 6a). After stringent quality control, we obtained high-quality nuclei counts of 3873, 3789, 4631, 3150, and 6367 from samples S01 to S05, respectively. To validate the MMAS classification in these samples, we performed pseudo-bulk analysis by aggregating the expression profiles of all cells per sample and applying the MMAS classifier through NTP. This analysis confirmed that each sample corresponded to its expected MMAS subtype (Fig. 6b). We then merged the 21,810 nuclei and conducted global clustering analysis. Based on the expression of canonical cell-type markers (Fig. 6c, d), cells were broadly categorized into 11 major cell types, including PEComa tumor cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, T cells, macrophages, mast cells, and five normal renal epithelial cell types primarily originating from sample S04, which was collected from kidney tissue.

To examine the distribution of MMAS classifier genes at the single-cell level, we calculated MMAS subtype scores for each cancer cell using the classifier gene sets. Remarkably, all five tumors exhibited homogeneous signals, with individual cancer cells corresponding closely to their respective MMAS classifications (Fig. 6e). This finding suggests that tumor cells are the primary contributors shaping the MMAS classification system. Further sub-clustering of PEComa tumor cells revealed four subgroups that were predominantly driven by their MMAS classifications (Fig. 6f). Intriguingly, the StemPEC sample (S05) was divided into two distinct subgroups, indicating higher intra-tumor heterogeneity compared to the other MMAS subtypes. Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) highlighted the functional plasticity of these subgroups (Fig. 6g, h). The snMesPEC subgroup showed high expression of EMT and TGF-β signaling pathways, with overexpression of mesenchymal markers such as CCN1, SAT1, ACTA2, and CALD1. The snAdiPEC subgroup exhibited activation of adipogenesis and myogenesis pathways, with overexpression of genes like SORBS1, APOE, and MYLK.

Notably, two distinct clusters were identified within the StemPEC sample. One cluster, named snStemPEC-Hh⁺, showed overactivation of the Hedgehog signaling pathway with overexpression of NRP2, RTN1, and CDK6, and demonstrated a significantly higher stemness index compared to other clusters (Fig. 6i). Notably, enrichment of Hedgehog signaling using bulk expression profiles was closely associated with poorer outcomes across all 63 patients (Fig. 6j). The other cluster, named snStemPEC-inflam, showed unique overexpression of the interferon-γ response pathway and HLA genes (HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-DRA), consistent with the activation of both MHC class I and II antigen presentation pathways. This inflamed cell state likely reflects an intrinsic activation of immune-related pathways within the tumor cells. The upregulation of HLA Class I molecules suggests that these tumor cells have an enhanced capacity for antigen presentation, potentially increasing their immunogenicity (Fig. 6k).

Proximity among tumors with MiT family rearrangements

To elucidate the transcriptomic relationships among tumors with MiT family translocations, we analyzed a cohort of various tumors profiled by PE RNA-seq54. We included 35 tumors with TFE3 translocations plus nine conventional PEComas without reported rearrangements. The 35 tumors comprised eight alveolar soft part sarcomas (ASPS), 14 melanotic Xp11 neoplasms, and 13 translocation renal cell carcinomas (TRCC). To minimize cross-cohort batch effects and enhance our analysis, we incorporated an additional ASPS case and nine pathologically confirmed TRCCs (six with TFE3 rearrangements, three with TFEB rearrangements) in our local cohort. This integration resulted in a merged cohort totaling 102 cases, including our original discovery PEComa cohort.

Upon mapping the nine conventional PEComas with the MMAS system, we categorized them as follows: one as MelPEC, one as MesPEC, five as AdiPEC, and two as StemPEC. Subsequent unsupervised clustering, based on the most variable genes, delineated seven distinct clusters (Fig. 7a). Remarkably, cluster C1 predominantly included TRCCs with TFE3 rearrangements, except for one case. Cluster C2 encompassed all TFE3-rearranged PEComas and melanotic Xp11 neoplasms. Cluster C3 was a mix of MITF-rearranged PEComas, TFEB-rearranged TRCCs, and several PEComas without TFE3/MITF translocations. Cluster C4 exclusively contained ASPS cases, while C5 was enriched in StemPEC, C6 in MesPEC, and C7 in AdiPEC. The batch effect was effectively addressed, evidenced by the clustering of external TRCCs with our TRCCs, external ASPSs with our one ASPS, and external PEComas with corresponding predicted subtypes, including one classified as MelPEC clustering close to our MITF PEComa. Lineage-specific gene expression patterns were largely preserved across clusters: C1 tumors overexpressed renal lineage genes such as PAX8, while C2 showed enrichment of melanogenesis-related genes, including TYR and PMEL. Among subtype-specific markers, Delta-like ligand 3 (DLL3) expression was modestly but significantly higher in C2 tumors (Fig. 7b), while MET was relatively upregulated in the MITF-rearranged C3 subtype (Fig. 7b). These findings underscore molecular distinctions between PEComa subtypes and suggest candidate pathways of potential clinical interest.

TFE3 rearrangements, immune infiltration, and estrogen receptor activation

Given the distinct features observed between TFE3 and MITF-rearranged PEComas, we proceeded to analyze their transcriptomic differences. Differential expression analysis revealed upregulation of TFE3 and downregulation of MITF in TFE3-rearranged relative to MITF-rearranged PEComas (Fig. 7c). GSEA revealed modest but statistically significant enrichment of inflammatory and interferon-γ response pathways in TFE3-rearranged PEComas (inflammatory response: NES = 1.7; interferon-γ response: NES = 1.4; FDR < 0.05; Fig. 7d). In addition, estrogen response pathways showed modest enrichment (early estrogen response: NES = 1.3; late estrogen response: NES = 1.4; FDR < 0.1; Fig. 7d), suggesting that the signal observed in MelPEC is largely driven by TFE3 rearrangement.

At the epigenetic level, TFE3-rearranged PEComas exhibited a notable presence of enhancer-enriched hypomethylation compared to MITF-rearranged PEComas (Fig. 7e). Integrative methylation–expression analysis identified 583 enhancer probe-gene pairs associated with these hypomethylated regions (Fig. 7f, Supplementary Data 10). The linked genes were significantly enriched in inflammatory and estrogen-response pathways (Fig. 7g), with the corresponding probes enriched in 12 motifs (Supplementary Data 11). Notably, one of these motifs corresponded to estrogen receptor-related receptor γ (ESRRG), which was significantly overexpressed in TFE3-rearranged PEComas compared to MITF-rearranged PEComas. These findings suggest that TFE3 translocations promote hormonal and immune dysregulation through coordinated transcriptional and epigenetic mechanisms in PEComa.

We extended our investigation to include IHC analysis of hormone receptors, including estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR), in three TFE3-rearranged PEComas and three MITF-rearranged PEComas with available materials (Fig. 7h). Remarkably, none of the MITF-rearranged PEComas exhibited positive staining for ER or PR. In contrast, all three TFE3-rearranged PEComas showed positive PR staining, with 70 to 90% of cells expressing PR at intensity levels ranging from 2 to 3, which was statistically significant compared to the MITF-rearranged group (p = 0.020 for intensity, p = 0.006 for percentage of positive cells). The H-index, which combines both staining intensity and the proportion of positive cells, varied from 140 to 270, significantly higher than in the MITF-rearranged PEComas (p = 0.036). Additionally, one TFE3-rearranged PEComa exhibited ER positive staining in 30% of cells, albeit at a lower intensity level and without reaching statistical significance.

Considering the similarity in the transcriptomic program between MITF and TFEB55, we further explored whether these dysregulations could be mirrored in TRCC. Analysis of both our local TRCC cohort (six TFE3 vs. three TFEB rearrangements) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) cohort (15 TFE3 vs. three TFEB rearrangements) revealed similar activation of interferon-γ response and estrogen response pathways (Fig. S15a, b). These transcriptomic similarities suggest that TFE3 translocations drive a conserved program of immune activation and hormone receptor signaling across both PEComa and renal tumor contexts.

Discussion

In this comprehensive multi-omic study of a large, unselected cohort of PEComas, we identified frequent MITF rearrangements involving actin family genes, revealed the association between anatomical site and mutational landscapes, and established a prognostic role for TP53 and RB1 mutations. Furthermore, we proposed the transcriptomic classification of PEComas, delineating four biologically relevant and clinically distinct subtypes with therapeutic implications—thereby addressing several key knowledge gaps in this ultra-rare tumor type.

Notably, we discovered MITF rearrangements in 13.2% of PEComas, representing 50% of all MiT family-rearranged cases in our cohort. These rearrangements, involving actin gene partners (ACTA2, ACTG1, or ACTB), retained MITF’s functional domains and were mutually exclusive with canonical drivers such as TSC1, TSC2, and FLCN. Structurally, the rearrangements preserve the transcriptional activation domains of MITF while removing its N-terminal region, which contains a phospho-degron motif (ESGIV) recently shown to mediate FBXW7-dependent MITF degradation56. As a consequence, the resulting fusion transcripts are predicted to stabilize the MITF protein and maintain its transcriptional activity. The transcriptomic profiles of MITF-rearranged tumors were distinct, enriched for melanocytic markers, and closely resembled those of TFE3-rearranged PEComas, reflecting overlapping downstream transcriptional targets regulated by MITF and TFE3. Importantly, unlike TFE3-rearranged PEComas, MITF-rearranged PEComas lacked homogeneous morphological or immunohistochemical features. While MITF IHC tended to show stronger staining in fusion-positive cases, the overlap with other categories limited its diagnostic specificity. These findings suggest that MITF IHC alone might be insufficient for reliable identification of MITF-rearranged PEComas, necessitating molecular assays for reliable confirmation24.

Our genomic analyses confirmed that TFE3/MITF rearrangements and mTOR pathway mutations are mutually exclusive, consistent with divergent tumorigenic mechanisms. Furthermore, TP53/RB1 mutations, while previously described, were shown here to be mutually exclusive with canonical mTOR-pathway drivers (TSC1, TSC2), and to co-occur with mutations in SWI/SNF chromatin-remodeling genes—an association that may contribute to tumor aggressiveness through impaired cell cycle control and apoptosis57,58. Crucially, we validated the independent prognostic value of TP53/RB1 mutations, supporting their role as biomarkers of poor outcome regardless of primary tumor site. We also identified significant anatomical associations with molecular features. PEComas arising in soft tissues, gastrointestinal organs, and pelvis were enriched for TFE3/MITF rearrangements and lacked mTOR pathway mutations. Conversely, tumors of gynecologic origin exhibited exclusive alterations in the CKM complex genes MED12 and CDK8, highlighting potential site-specific oncogenic programs. To our knowledge, this analysis focusing on the genomic profiles of tumors based on their primary site has not been conducted in the largest genomic profiling studies, nor in studies focused on other histological subtypes of sarcomas59,60. However, it has to be noted, MED12 deficiency has been previously reported in other uterine muscle tumors, both benign (MED12 mutation) and malignant (more likely due to epigenetic modifications)60,61.

Through transcriptomic profiling, we uncovered four robust molecular subtypes, each reflecting distinct lineage programs, mutation profiles, and clinical behaviors. Interestingly, TSC1- and TSC2-mutated tumors did not cluster together, challenging the assumption that these alterations produce equivalent biological effects. Instead, TSC1 mutations were enriched in the MesPEC subtype, associated with EMT, while TSC2 mutations were enriched in the AdiPEC subtype, characterized by adipogenic differentiation. In our cohort, only patients harboring TSC2 mutations developed recurrence and required mTOR inhibitor treatment, whereas TSC1-mutated cases did not recur and therefore did not receive systemic therapy, suggesting a more indolent clinical behavior for TSC1-mutated tumors. This pattern is consistent with findings from the AMPECT trial, where TSC2-mutated tumors exhibited greater clinical benefit from mTOR inhibition than those with TSC1 mutations, supporting the notion that these genotypes differ both in biological aggressiveness and therapeutic sensitivity17,27. Mechanistically, it is possible that, beyond promoting mTOR-dependent proliferation similar to TSC2 loss, TSC1 inactivation also engages the TGFβ/Smad2/3 signaling pathway, thereby enhancing EMT and fostering a distinct transcriptomic state62. Alternatively, these differences could reflect divergent cells of origin, with TSC1- and TSC2-mutated tumors arising from separate lineages that imprint their molecular and phenotypic identity.

Our transcriptomic classification also carried prognostic significance. The StemPEC subtype—marked by enrichment in TP53 and RB1 mutations—was associated with the poorest clinical outcomes and demonstrated limited response to standard-of-care mTOR inhibitors. These findings challenge the current uniform application of mTOR inhibition for advanced PEComas and suggest that biomarker-driven treatment selection may be more appropriate. Biologically, StemPEC appeared to be the most aggressive subtype, exhibiting strong activation of proliferative pathways, including upregulation of cell cycle regulators and significantly higher Ki67 staining by IHC, likely driven in part by TP53 dysfunction63. This subtype also uniquely activated the Hedgehog signaling pathway, which is known to support stem-like phenotypes and has been implicated in other aggressive sarcomas. Preclinical studies have shown that Hedgehog pathway inhibitors can reduce proliferation in sarcomas overexpressing pathway components64. Beyond its proliferative program, StemPEC tumors displayed an immunologically active microenvironment, including upregulation of interferon signaling, MHC class II antigen presentation, and enrichment in B cell–rich TLS. While the link between TP53 mutations and immune infiltration is well documented in carcinomas, its role in sarcomas remains emerging and may be context-dependent65,66. Finally, both our DNA methylation and single-nucleus RNA-seq analyses highlighted the distinct epigenetic deregulation and intratumoral complexity of this subtype. Together, these features point to immunotherapy as a promising strategy for this aggressive PEComa subtype and support the development of personalized treatment approaches guided by molecular profiling.

By extending our transcriptomic analysis to other MiT family–rearranged tumors, we found that TFE3 and MITF rearrangements in PEComas induce distinct transcriptomic programs that align more closely with other tumor types than with each other. MITF-rearranged PEComas clustered with TFEB-rearranged TRCCs, suggesting a shared molecular program despite differences in histopathology. In contrast, TFE3-rearranged PEComas clustered closely with melanotic Xp11 neoplasms and showed co-activation of melanocytic differentiation pathways, including overexpression of canonical melanocytic markers such as TYR and PMEL. Within the specific context of PEComa, however, TFE3- and MITF-rearranged tumors consistently clustered together under the MelPEC subtype, reflecting a shared MiT-driven transcriptional identity that defines the principal axis separating MiT-driven from mTOR-driven or TP53/RB1-driven PEComas. This overarching relationship captures the major biological distinction in PEComa, whereas the finer transcriptional differences between TFE3 and MITF fusions become more evident only when analyzed within the broader MiT family across tumor types.

Interestingly, in the pan-MiT family analysis, the TFE3-rearranged PEComas that clustered with melanotic Xp11 neoplasms exhibited elevated expression of DLL3, a Notch pathway ligand currently under investigation as a target for antibody–drug conjugates (ADCs) in small cell lung cancer67. Although the magnitude of DLL3 upregulation was modest, its consistent overexpression in a molecularly defined subgroup suggests a possible rationale for exploratory evaluation of DLL3-targeted approaches in preclinical models. Likewise, MET expression was relatively increased in MITF-rearranged PEComas, raising the possibility that MET tyrosine kinase inhibitors could have therapeutic relevance in this subset. While these observations alone do not imply drug sensitivity, they highlight potentially actionable signaling programs that warrant further functional investigation.

Beyond these findings, transcriptomic comparisons revealed activation of estrogen response pathways in TFE3-rearranged PEComas, suggesting a degree of hormonal dependency. Notably, clinical benefit from combining mTOR inhibition with aromatase inhibitors has been reported in patients progressing on mTOR monotherapy68, an approach supported by known crosstalk between mTOR and estrogen signaling, as well as preclinical evidence from lymphangioleiomyomatosis (LAM) models69. Finally, we observed similar estrogen/progesterone receptor pathway activation in TFE3-rearranged TRCCs, suggesting that TFE3 translocations confer a conserved and targetable transcriptional program across tumor types, distinct from those driven by MITF or TFEB fusions.

This study has several limitations, including its retrospective design and the use of targeted gene panels rather than whole-exome or whole-genome sequencing, which may limit the detection of new variants. In the present study, copy-number alterations were not assessed, as the targeted sequencing assay (NovoPM) is optimized for detection of sequence-level variants and does not support reliable identification of deep deletions or amplifications. In addition, the long inclusion period and variable sample quality inherent to rare tumor biobanking could introduce confounding factors. The availability of DNA methylation data was restricted to a subset of tumors; although it covered the majority of the discovery cohort, these findings warrant validation in larger independent datasets. Nevertheless, the study benefits from a centralized pathological review and the inclusion of a large, unselected cohort collected through a national network with comprehensive clinical annotation and follow-up data.

In summary, our work redefines PEComas as a group of transcriptionally and genetically heterogeneous diseases driven by distinct oncogenic mechanisms, with corresponding implications for prognosis and therapy. The identification of MITF rearrangements, the MMAS classification, and the delineation of an aggressive, immunogenic stem-like subtype provide a framework for molecular stratification and therapeutic innovation in this ultra-rare sarcoma. We propose an integrated model summarizing these findings and their clinical implications (Fig. 8). Further prospective validation is warranted to assess the clinical utility of these findings and support biomarker-guided clinical trials.

Methods

Sample collection and ethical approval

This study was approved by Strasbourg University Hospital ethical committee (FC/dossier 2019-76) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. PEComas refers to a family of ultra-rare mesenchymal tumors encompassing AML, LAM, clear cell “sugar” tumor (CCST) and PEComas-not other specified (NOS). In this study we focused our work on those PEComas-NOS, the most heterogeneous subgroup. This is a retrospective, multicentric analysis of PEComas cases included in the French Sarcoma Clinical Reference Network NETSARC+ (http://www.netsarc.org) centers of the French Sarcoma Group. Except for deceased patients, all included patients provided written informed consent for the use of their samples for research purposes in a molecular profiling study and for publication of anonymized data. The consent framework did not include broad consent for public deposition, controlled-access repository deposition, or redistribution of raw genomic/omics data outside the original approved study framework. Sex and age were obtained from clinical records. No sex-stratified analyses were performed due to limited sample size and because the study was not designed to assess sex-specific effects. No participants received compensation. De-identified data from patients, including patients’ and tumor characteristics, treatments, and outcomes, were collected for patients treated between 2003 and 2021. Included patients had to have a diagnosis of PEComas-NOS with an available formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor sample. All samples were reviewed by a sarcoma expert pathologist and classified according to the 5th WHO classification of soft tissue and bone tumors.

DNA/RNA extraction

Starting with 3 mm2 cores biopsies of PEComa selected by the expert pathologist, nucleic acids were extracted using the AllPrep DNA/RNA FFPE kit from Qiagen. Prior to the actual extraction, the punches were deparaffinized following the protocol described previously70. Using a motorized tissue homogenizer, the cores were ground into fine tissue particles. After the sample preparation, the purification of genomic DNA and total RNA was performed following the kit instructions (AllPrep DNA/RNA FFPE Handbook, 08/2020).

Library preparation and RNA sequencing

RNA-seq libraries were generated either using Illumina TruSeq RNA Exome kit (TruSeq RNA Exome Reference Guide #1000000039582 v01, March 2018) or Lexogen 3’mRNA Seq Library Prep Kit (015, QuantSeq 3’ mRNA-Seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina FWD) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The choice of the protocol depends on the samples. Concerning the first one, cDNA was generated from RNA fragments using random priming during first and second strand synthesis. Sequencing adapters were ligated to the resulting double-stranded cDNA fragments to produce a library that was amplified by PCR. The coding regions of the transcriptome were then captured from this library using sequence-specific probes to create the final library. The following specific conditions were applied for library preparation. For samples with DV200 values of <30%, 100 ng input RNA was used, while for samples with DV200 values of 30%–50% or > 50%, 80 and 40 ng input RNA were used, respectively, as indicated by the manufacturer’s protocol. The fragmentation step was omitted. DNA fragments before capture were enriched by PCR with 15 cycles of amplification for all samples with DV200 values > 30% and with 18 cycles for low-quality RNA with DV200 values < 30%. The exome enrichment was conducted in either 4-Plex or 5-Plex reactions, maintaining a total mass of 800 ng of DNA library in each pool. As far as possible, the pools were constituted with libraries from starting RNA of equivalent quality to optimize the balancing of the reads of each library in the pools. Concerning the Lexogen kit, 3’mRNA-Seq libraries were generated from 100 to 150 ng of total RNA. Library generation was initiated by oligo(dT) priming. After first strand synthesis, the RNA was removed and second strand synthesis was initiated using a random primer containing a UMI (081, UMI Second Strand Synthesis Module for QuantSeq FWD) (Lexogen, Vienna, Austria). The DNA library were amplified using 14 to 20 cycles of PCR, adding Lexogen i7 6nt single indexes. Surplus PCR primers were further removed by a first purification using PB beads and a second purification using SPRIselect beads (Beckman-Colter, Villepinte, France). The final libraries were checked for quality and quantified using the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, Les Ulis, France). Libraries were sequenced either on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 sequencer or an Illumina NextSeq 2000 sequencer, as paired-end 100 base reads for the first protocol and as 75-length single reads for the second protocol. Image analysis and base calling were performed using RTA (v2.7.7) and either bcl2fastq (v2.20.0.422) for the data generated from HiSeq 4000 or BCL Convert (v3.8.4) for the data generated from NextSeq 2000. For the data generated from the Lexogen kit, the first six bases of the reads, corresponding to UMI, were extracted and added in the read identifier using UMI-tools (v1.1.2). The following four bases were discarded using cutadapt (v1.10)71, and this same tool was used to remove adapters, polyA and low-quality sequences (Phred quality score below 20) in all the samples. After this preprocessing, reads shorter than 40 bases were discarded for further analysis. Reads were mapped to rRNA sequences using Bowtie 2 (v2.2.8)72, and reads mapping to rRNA sequences were removed for further analysis. The raw reads in FASTQ format were aligned to the reference human genome, GRCh38/hg38, using STAR (v2.5.3a)73. PCR duplicates in the data generated from the Lexogen kit were removed using the umi_tools dedup command of UMI-tools. Gene expression quantification was performed from uniquely aligned reads using htseq-count (v0.6.1p1)74, with annotations from Ensembl (v107) and “union” mode. Only non-ambiguously assigned reads to a gene have been retained for further analyses. For all samples with raw count data, the number of fragments per kilobase of non-overlapped exon per million fragments mapped (FPKM) was computed first and transferred into transcripts per kilobase million (TPM) values, the signals of which were more similar to those quantified from microarrays and were more comparable between cases75.

Fusion detection

We used STAR-Fusion (v.1.10.0) to identify fusion genes in tumor samples with paired-end RNA-seq profiles76. By enabling the STAR option to report the chimeric read alignments, these data will be readily available for running STAR-Fusion, quickly providing access to lists of candidate fusion transcripts. The fusions were labeled based on FusionAnnotator annotations and then filtered by removing fusions involving chrM, fusions containing the tags “GTEx_recurrent_StarF2019,” “BodyMap,” “DGD_PARALOGS,” “HGNC_GENEFAM,” “Greger_Normal,” “Babiceanu_Normal,” and “ConjoinG,” and those detected in normal samples, considering what has already been described in the literature77. Those fusions that pass the filters are reported in a tab-delimited summary file identifying the fusion pairs, the inferred fusion breakpoint (chromosomal exon boundaries), counts of supporting split reads and spanning fragments, and identification of the RNA-seq reads that support the fusion prediction. The parameters of “–min_junction_reads” and “–min_sum_frags” were assigned default values. Another software (Arriba v.2.4.0, default settings) was used to confirm the presence of these fusions, and the only fusions common to both software programs are the reported fusions of interest, including TFE3 and MITF (Supplementary Data 3).

Targeted gene panel sequencing (TGPS)

For the identification of single-nucleotide variants (SNVs) and small insertions and deletions (InDels) in PEComa samples, we utilized TGPS with the NovoPM™ 2.0 panel. This hybrid-capture panel interrogates the complete exonic regions of 484 cancer-relevant genes. Sequencing reads were aligned to the human reference genome (hg19) using the Burrows-Wheeler Aligner (BWA)78. The aligned reads were sorted, and duplicates were marked using samtools and Picard78, respectively. The final alignments were stored in Binary Alignment Map (BAM) format for downstream analyses. Variant calling for SNVs and InDels was conducted using Varscan2 (v1.0.0)79, along with NovogeneSGZ, an in-house algorithm designed to distinguish somatic from germline variants in tumor-only sequencing data. Germline filtering was performed computationally within the NovoPM pipeline based on population and cancer databases, including 1000 Genomes (2015 Aug release), ExAC, dbSNP (build 150), ClinVar, and COSMIC, together with functional prediction scores from SIFT and PolyPhen-280–83. Variants were annotated using the Variant Effect Predictor (VEP v109)84. A variant was considered non-synonymous if classified as one of the following: “Frame_Shift_Del”, “Frame_Shift_Ins”, “Splice_Site”, “Translation_Start_Site”, “Nonsense_Mutation”, “Nonstop_Mutation”, “In_Frame_Del”, “In_Frame_Ins”, and “Missense_Mutation”. To ensure high confidence, stringent filtering criteria were applied: mutation frequency (MutFreq) > 5%, mutation depth (MutDepth) ≥ 15, ExAC_ALL frequency <0.0001, and exclusion of variants annotated as “Likely benign” or “Benign” in ClinVar. Variants that passed these filters included both pathogenic alterations and variants of uncertain significance (VUS) and were retained uniformly at the gene level without variant-level functional interpretation. For external validation of somatic mutation profiles, we analyzed data from GENIE (v17.0), retrieving TGPS profiles from 109 primary PEComa cases85.

DNA methylation profiling

DNA integrity, quantity, and purity were assessed by capillary electrophoresis using the Agilent High Sensitivity DNA Kit and a Qubit fluorometer for quantification. Based on the manufacturer’s recommendations, genomic DNA (250–500 ng) was subjected to bisulfite conversion using the EZ DNA Methylation-Lightning Kit (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA), which is optimized for FFPE-derived DNA by ensuring effective bisulfite conversion of damaged DNA. The completeness of bisulfite conversion was verified using a MethyLight-based real-time PCR assay designed to distinguish between methylated and unmethylated alleles. The converted DNA was then processed for hybridization to the Illumina Infinium HumanMethylation850 (EPIC) BeadChip Array. This process involved whole-genome amplification, followed by enzymatic fragmentation. Post-amplification, samples were denatured and hybridized overnight to the BeadChip. The array facilitates the annealing of whole-genome amplified DNA to locus-specific oligomers linked to individual bead types, enabling comprehensive methylation profiling.

Quantification and filtering of DNA methylation profiles

To those cohorts with available raw *.IDAT files, we used the R package ChAMP (v2.28.0) to first extract the raw signal intensities from the *.IDAT files and calculate the β value for each probe86. By default, ChAMP performed comprehensive filtering procedures, and the following filtering criteria were applied for both 450 K and EPIC arrays: removal of probes with a detection of P value > 0.01, probes with <3 beads in at least 5% of samples per probe, all non-CpG probes, all SNP-related probes, all multi-hit probes, and probes located in chromosome X and Y87.

MITF fluorescent in situ hybridization

Dual color MITF Break Apart Probes (Empire genomics-MITFBA-20-ORGE, AMPLITECH, France) were used according to manufacturer’s protocol. Firstly, the FFPE blocks of interest were cut to a thickness of 4 µm. The samples were deposited on slides, which was dried before technical at 37 °C in an incubator. To avoid background noise, a passage of one hour at 60 °C was necessary. Then, tissues were dewaxed and rehydrated in successive baths in the HE600, Roche. After washes with buffer, slides were incubated with the pre-treatment solution from Dako, boiled using a microwave. After further buffer washing, digestion was performed with pepsin solution for three minutes at 37 °C. A new series of rinses with wash buffer DAKO were performed. Samples were dehydrated by immersing in 70%, 90%, and 100% alcohol for one min each at room temperature, then dried again. The two probes were applied to each tumor in the darkness, covered with a thin layer of sealed glass. Slides with the different samples were then placed in the Hybridizer. Denaturation lasted three minutes at 85 °C. Then, 20 h at 37 °C overnight were necessary for hybridization in this humidified chamber. All steps post-hybridization were done in the dark. The following day, the slides were dissembled in order to take away the excess of the probe. For this, a stringent bath with Wash Buffer 2 Clinisciences was applied for 10 min at 37 °C. Finally, after a new dehydration and drying in ambient air, the slides were mounted and counterstained with DAPI. After hybridization, all slides were maintained in the fridge at 4 °C and in the dark. Slides were analyzed with a Leica DM 6000B fluorescence microscope equipped with PL Fluotar 10×, 20×, 60×, and 100× objectives and specific filters for fluorescein isothiocyanate, Texas Red, and 4,6-diamino-2-phenylindole and also double and triple band-pass filters. Images were captured using Cytovision FISH software. For each sample, a hundred nuclei were analyzed. The interpretation of intact and split signals was based on generally accepted guidelines recommended for all other commercially available break-apart FISH assays. Space between two signals greater than one signal was considered as a split signal. A positive result was considered when greater than 20% of the tumor nuclei had rearrangement.

Immunohistochemistry

IHC was performed on 4-μm-thick tumor sections prepared from FFPE tissue blocks. After spreading the sections on a distilled water bath, they were mounted on silanized slides and dried. All staining procedures were carried out using the Benchmark Ultra automated stainer (Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland), following protocols optimized for each antibody. Slides were deparaffinized at 72 °C using Roche’s EZ Prep solution. Antigen retrieval was performed using Tris/Borate/EDTA (TBE) buffer (Ultra CC1, pH 8.4) under different conditions depending on the antibody. For estrogen receptor (ER; clone SP1, pre-diluted, Roche), retrieval was conducted at 95 °C for 36 min, while for progesterone receptor (PR; clone 1E2, pre-diluted, Roche) and KI67 (clone MIB-1, Dako; 1:50 dilution), the retrieval time was extended to 64 min.

MiTF (clones C5 and D5, RTU, Bio SB) and TP53 (clone DO7, RTU, Roche) were also assessed using the same antigen retrieval buffer (pH 8.4, Ultra CC1), with incubation parameters pre-set by the automated platform. The UltraView DAB detection kit was used for MiTF, while OptiView was used for TP53 detection. Following antibody incubation, counterstaining was performed using Hematoxylin II and Bluing Reagent, with final mounting on the HE 600 platform. For ER and PR, a 24-min incubation at 37 °C was applied, whereas the KI67 protocol involved a coverslipped incubation at room temperature for 32 min. TP53 and MiTF antibodies were processed according to manufacturer-recommended protocols for fully automated staining. For TP53 (p53) IHC, staining interpretation followed the criteria commonly applied in gynecologic malignancies. Tumors were classified as aberrant if they showed either (i) diffuse nuclear overexpression in more than 80% of tumor cells, irrespective of staining intensity, or (ii) complete absence of nuclear staining (null pattern). All other staining patterns, including variable or focal nuclear positivity below this threshold, were considered consistent with a wild-type expression pattern. Scoring was performed by an experienced board-certified pathologist to ensure consistency and reproducibility.

External positive controls were included for ER and PR to ensure staining quality. For these markers, staining intensity was graded on a scale from 1 (weak) to 3 (strong), and the percentage of positively stained tumor cells was recorded. TP53 expression was interpreted based on nuclear staining patterns, distinguishing between complete loss, overexpression, or wild-type patterns. MiTF was evaluated based on nuclear immunoreactivity without semi-quantitative scoring.

External RNA-seq cohort of PEComa and tumors with TFE3 rearrangements

We assembled a PE RNA-seq cohort from GSE150474, comprising 35 tumors with TFE3 rearrangements and nine conventional PEComas without any reported fusions54. This specific subset included eight cases of ASPS, 14 melanotic Xp11 neoplasms, and 13 cases of TRCC. Mirroring the approach taken with our discovery cohort, the clean reads were mapped to the hg38 genome assembly using STAR (version 2.5.3a). Following this, FPKM values were obtained and subsequently transformed into TPM values to improve comparability across datasets.

To mitigate cross-cohort batch effects and enrich our analysis, we expanded the cohort to include an additional case of ASPS and 17 pathologically confirmed TRCCs from our local database. This addition comprised six TRCCs with TFE3 rearrangements, three with TFEB rearrangements, and eight with unidentified fusions. The integration of these cases resulted in a comprehensive merged cohort of 110 cases, which includes our initial discovery PEComa cohort with PE RNA-seq profiles.

Transcriptomic and epigenetic profiles of TRCC

Misclassified TRCC cases with either TFE3 or TFEB rearrangements were identified according to our previous study from TCGA projects88,89. Transcriptome expression and epigenetic DNA methylation profiles were downloaded from the UCSC Xena platform (https://xenabrowser.net/datapages/). Our analysis encompassed 15 TFE3 TRCC cases (7 from TCGA-KIRC, 8 from TCGA-KIRP) and 3 TFEB TRCC cases (1 from TCGA-KIRC, 2 from TCGA-KIRP). While transcriptomic data in the form of TPM were available for all 18 TRCC cases, DNA methylation profiles, quantified using the Illumina HumanMethylation 450 K array, were accessible for only four TFE3 TRCC cases. Additionally, 24 normal adjacent kidney samples with both transcriptomic and epigenetic profiles were included for comparative purposes.

Batch effect removal in transcriptomic profiles

To mitigate potential batch effects introduced by different cohorts, we employed the ComBat algorithm, a function included in the R package sva (v3.46.0), using default parameters. ComBat is an empirical Bayes framework that estimates and removes batch-specific variability, thus allowing us to effectively correct for any potential batch effects introduced by different cohorts. We included the cohort as a covariate in our statistical models to further account for any variability associated with the different cohorts.

Driver mutation identification and mutual exclusivity analysis

To identify driver mutations of PEComa, the dNdScv algorithm (v0.0.1.0) was applied to infer significantly mutated genes90. Reference database was set to hg19 and background was limited to the targeted gene panel; other parameters were set by default. A Poisson–Binomial distribution-based analysis implemented in the R package Rediscover (v0.3.2) was performed to identify co-occurring or mutually exclusive pairs of genomic alterations, corresponding odds ratio was estimated by a Fisher’s exact test. A Haldane–Anscombe correction (adding 0.5) was applied to zero-count cells. Mutational OncoPrint was illustrated using the R package ComplexHeatmap (v2.13.4)91.

Pathway mutation

Mutation in five pathways were investigated, including mTOR pathway (MTOR, RPTOR, RICTOR, TSC1, TSC2, PI3K, AKT1, PTEN), SWI/SNF chromatin-remodeling complexes (SMARCC1, SMARCC2, SMARCA4, SMARCA2, SMARCB1, SMARCE1, SMARCD1, SMARCD2, SMARCD3, ACTB, ACTL6A, ACTL6B, BCL7A, BCL7B, BCL7C, SS18, ARID1A, ARID1B, DPF2, DPF3, BCL11A, BCL11B, ARID2, PBRM1, BRD7, PHF10, BRD9, BICRA, BICRAL), DNA repair (ATM, ATRX, BRCA1, BARD1, DOT1L, ERCC1, ERCC4, FANCD2, FANCM, MRE11, RAD50, WRN, XPC, CUL4A, FH, MSH3, NUDT1, PARP3, PARP4, TRRAP), chromatin remodeling (ARID1A, ARID1B, ATRX, BCOR, RB1, SMARCA4, KDM5C, PBRM1, SF3B1) and CKM complex (MED12, CDK8). Pathway was considered mutated if one of the genes involved was mutated.

Clustering analysis

The optimal clustering number was estimated by calculating the stability index using Monte Carlo reference-based consensus clustering through R package M3C (v1.20.0)92. Consensus NMF was performed using the R package NMF (v0.24.0) for 100 runs with the offset method, which includes an intercept to capture a common baseline and shared patterns in order to produce cleaner basis components for matrix factorization. Unsupervised hierarchical clustering of the samples was analyzed using 1-Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient as distance measures with Ward’s clustering method, which was implemented in the R package ClassDiscovery (v3.4.0).

Nearest template prediction and random forest classifier for subtype classification

NTP was employed to classify each sample into one of the defined transcriptomic subtypes using the R package CMScaller (v2.0.1)93. For NTP, we established a template for each subtype in the MMAS classification by identifying the top 200 (if available) significantly upregulated genes (log2FoldChange > 0, FDR < 0.05) within each subtype. This method involves standardizing the expression data (TPM) through log2 transformation and z-scoring. Standardization is crucial for NTP as it normalizes the data across samples, thereby allowing for a relative comparison across predefined templates. This method is particularly effective in reproducing the heterogeneity within the non-metastatic cases, where the goal is to match samples to the closest subtype template.

In contrast, for assessing the metastatic potential of samples and their alignment with specific subtypes, we utilized the random forest classifier using the R package randomForest (v4.7-1.1). This method does not require the standardization of data; instead, it uses raw log2-transformed TPM values directly. The choice of random forest was driven by its ability to handle the raw molecular signals, preserving the unique expression profiles of metastatic samples. With ntree set at 500, the classifier evaluates the closeness of each metastatic sample to the subtype profiles as probabilities. This is particularly advantageous when hypothesizing that one transcriptomic subtype, identified as having higher metastatic risk, might closely resemble the molecular profile of metastatic samples. By avoiding data standardization, the random forest method provides a probability score for each subtype, offering a more precise measure of how closely each metastatic sample resembles our identified high-risk subtype, thereby enhancing our understanding of metastatic progression.

Differential and enrichment analysis

Differentially methylated probes (DMPs) were identified using the standard process of ChAMP, considering a probe as significantly hypermethylated or hypomethylated if the average β value was respectively larger or smaller than that of other samples (Δβ > 0.1, p < 0.05, FDR < 0.25), with the one in the reference group being less than 0.3. For differential expression analyses, the R package MOVICS (v0.99.17) was used with the limma approach94–96, and for GSEA based on transcriptome expression data, a pre-ranked gene list was prepared according to the descending ordered log2FoldChange value derived from differential expression analysis, and used the R package clusterProfiler (v4.6.0) to determine functional enrichment based on the Hallmark pathways97. Functional enrichment analysis based on the gene list was performed by Enrichr (https://maayanlab.cloud/Enrichr/) for Hallmark pathways98. Pathways and terms were considered significantly enriched at p < 0.05 and FDR < 0.2.

Estimation of tumor microenvironment cell abundance

To infer the relative abundance of specific cell types within complex tissues based on gene expression profiles for each tumor, we used the R package MCPcounter (v1.2.0)49, which provides abundance scores for eight immune populations (T cells, CD8 + T cells, cytotoxic lymphocytes, natural killer cells, B cell lineage, monocytic lineage, myeloid dendritic cells, and neutrophils) and two stromal populations (endothelial cells and fibroblasts). Of note, the fibroblast signature was removed as all PEComa exhibited high and homogeneous scores for this cell population. A 29-gene TLS imprint signature was analyzed using single-sample GSEA, with enrichment score per sample calculated by the R package GSVA (v1.53.5)99.

Calculation of stemness index

We applied a one-class logistic regression (OCLR) technique to estimate the stem cell index (mRNAsi) for our samples100. Initially, we retained only the gene expression data from embryonic stem cells (ESCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), hereafter collectively referred to as stem cell (SC) samples. To mitigate sample-specific bias, we centered the gene expression data by subtracting the average expression value of each gene across all samples from its corresponding expression value in individual samples. Subsequently, the centralized gene expression profiles of protein-coding genes were processed using the R package gelnet (v1.2.1) to compute the weight vector for each gene. We then calculated Spearman’s correlation coefficients to measure the similarity between the gene expression profile of each PEComa sample and the model gene weight vectors. These values were subsequently rescaled across samples using min–max normalization to derive the mRNAsi score. The resulting mRNAsi values range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating lower degrees of cellular differentiation and stronger stem cell–like characteristics.

CINSARC signature

To assess the metastatic potential of different PEComa subtypes, we implemented the CINSARC signature, originally devised for prognostic stratification in sarcomas. This signature comprises of 67 genes, which are all related to chromosome biogenesis, mitosis control, and chromosome segregation, but do not include TP53 or RB1. Leveraging the nearest centroid method, we calculated centroids that encapsulated the mean expression levels of the signature genes for both metastatic and non-metastatic samples. These centroids were derived from the comprehensive GSE21050 sarcoma dataset, which included both the original training and validation cohorts. The classification of each PEComa sample was performed by calculating the Spearman’s rank correlation between the sample’s gene expression profile and the predetermined centroids. A sample was classified as CINSARC low-risk (C1) or high-risk (C2) based on the correlation that was more strongly aligned with either centroid48.

Assessment of cellular proliferation acceleration

We estimated the relative cumulative replicative histories of human cells using “RepliTali” (Replication Times Accumulated in Lifetime), a method based on analyzing DNA methylation patterns to infer cellular replication events101. To evaluate cellular proliferation acceleration, we applied a linear regression model using chronological age as the independent variable and the RepliTali score as the dependent variable. The acceleration in cellular proliferation was determined by calculating the residual for each sample: the difference between the predicted RepliTali value (as estimated by the regression model) and the actual RepliTali value. This residual was interpreted as cellular proliferation acceleration, where positive values indicated an increased rate of cellular replication relative to chronological age, and negative values indicated a decreased rate.

Inference of enhancer-associated transcription factors

To identify functional changes at transcriptional enhancers based on the DNA methylation profile, we performed integrative analysis combining methylation and gene expression data by using the R package ELMER (v2.25.2)102. Specifically, ELMER analyses have five main steps:

1) identify enhancers on DNA methylation arrays;

2) identify enhancers with a significantly hypomethylated level in the group of interest as compared to the reference group (β difference ≥ 0.1, p < 0.05 and FDR < 0.25);

3) Identify putative target genes that were significantly upregulated for hypomethylated enhancers. Specifically, the closest 20 upstream and downstream genes were collected for each probe, and for each candidate probe-gene pair, the Mann-Whitney U test was used to test the null hypothesis that overall gene expression in the group of interest is greater than or equal to that in the reference group;

4) Identify enriched motifs for the enhancers that are significantly hypomethylated and linked to a putative target gene in the group of interest. Specifically, all probes occurring in significant probe-gene pairs are combined for motif enrichment analysis. HOMER (Hypergeometric Optimization of Motif EnRichment) was used to find motif occurrences in a ± 250 bp region around each probe103, using HOCOMOCO (HOmo sapiens COmprehensive MOdel Collection, Version 11)104. For each probe set tested (i.e., the set of all probes occurring in significant probe-gene pairs), ELMER quantifies enrichments using Fisher’s exact test (where a is the number of probes within the selected probe set that contains one or more motif occurrences; b is the number of probes within the selected probe set that do not contain a motif occurrence; c and d are the same counts within the entire array probe set drawn from the same set of distal-only probes using the same definition as the primary analysis), and multiple testing correction occurs using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure. A probe set was considered significantly enriched for a particular motif if the 95% confidence interval of the odds ratio was greater than 1.1, the motif occurred at least 10 times in the probe set, and FDR < 0.05;

5) Identify regulatory transcription factors (TFs) whose expression was negatively associated with DNA methylation at enriched motifs. Specifically, for each candidate motif-TF pair, the Mann-Whitney U test is used to test the null hypothesis that overall gene expression in the group of interest is greater than or equal to that in the reference group. Finally, all TFs were ranked by the |−log10P, and those falling within the top 5% of this ranking were considered candidate upstream regulators; the highest known ranking TF with a recognized motif was considered as the potential enhancer-associated TF105.

Single-nuclei RNA sequencing

Library preparation

To ensure the loading of 6,000 nuclei, a total of 30,000 nuclei were placed onto a microfluidic chip (Singleron GEXSCOPE® Single Nucleus RNA Library Kit V2). Single-nucleus RNA-seq libraries were constructed using the GEXSCOPE™ Single Nucleus RNA-seq Library Kit (Singleron Biotechnologies) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Paramagnetic beads conjugated to oligo-dT probes carrying unique molecular identifiers (UMIs) and unique barcodes were added, after which the nuclei were lysed. The beads bound to polyadenylated mRNA were extracted from the chip and reverse-transcribed into cDNA at 42 °C for 1.5 h. The cDNA was then amplified by PCR, fragmented, and ligated to indexed Illumina adapters. The fragment size distribution of the final amplified library was assessed using an Agilent Fragment Analyzer.

Library sequencing

Library concentrations were measured using a Qubit 4.0 fluorometer, and the libraries were pooled equimolarly. The single-nucleus libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using a 2 × 150 bp paired-end approach to a final depth of 45 GB per library. Reads were demultiplexed according to the multiplexing index sequencing on Illumina’s BaseCloud platform.

Raw data processing and quality control

CeleSCOPE® (v1.14.1) was employed to process the raw data for each patient tumor sample, demultiplex cellular barcodes, map reads to the transcriptome of the human reference genome GRCh38, and downsample reads (www.github.com/singleron-RD/CeleScope; Singleron Biotechnologies). Briefly, FASTQ files were demultiplexed based on their respective cell barcodes and UMIs. Adapter sequences and poly-A tails were trimmed using Cutadapt (v3.7), and the trimmed Read2 reads were aligned to the GRCh38 human genome using STAR (v2.6.1a) with Ensembl (v110) gene annotations via featureCounts (v2.0.1)71,73,106. The filtered feature-barcode matrix for each patient tumor sample was then converted into a Seurat object using the R package Seurat (v5.0.0)107.

To ensure high-quality cells, quality control and doublet removal were performed individually for each patient sample. First, CellBender (v0.3.0) was applied to eliminate empty droplets and technical artifacts108. Second, matrices were filtered to retain only high-quality cells by applying the following criteria: number of detected genes between 200 and 8000; number of UMIs between 500 and 10,000; mitochondrial reads less than 20% (matching pattern “^MT-“); ribosomal reads less than 5% (matching pattern “^RP[SL]”); and hemoglobin reads less than 5% (matching pattern “HB[^(P)]”)109. To reduce false positives in doublet calling, only cells marked as doublets by both DoubletDecon (v1.1.6) and DoubletFinder (v2.0.4) were excluded from downstream analysis110,111. After quality control filtering, 21,810 single nuclei were obtained and merged from five patient samples into one Seurat object for downstream analyses.

Data normalization, feature selection, cell clustering and annotation

The gene expression matrices from the merged Seurat object were normalized using Seurat’s NormalizeData() function with the method set to “LogNormalize.” Feature selection was performed using Seurat’s FindVariableFeatures() function with the selection method “vst”, which identified the top 2000 variable features. These genes were summarized via PCA into 50 principal components (PCs). Batch correction was conducted in each cell compartment using the RunHarmony() function from the Harmony R package, with the batch key (group.by.vars) set to patients.

Clustering analysis was based on the integrated joint embedding produced by Harmony. A shared nearest-neighbor graph was computed using the FindNeighbors() function (k.param = 20) with all 50 PCs. The Louvain algorithm (resolution = 0.7), implemented in Seurat’s FindClusters() function, was used for clustering112. Major clusters were initially visualized on a 2D map produced with the Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) method using the RunUMAP() function and further refined using the R package plot1cell (v0.0.0.9000)113. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) among clusters were extracted using the FindAllMarkers() function with the default non-parametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test and Bonferroni correction. Major cell types were annotated based on the expression of canonical markers and DEGs in various clusters.

Cancer cell sub-clustering

For sub-clustering analysis of cancer cells, SCTransform was applied for data normalization while regressing out the mitochondrial read percentage per cell, identifying the top 3000 variable genes. Subsequent steps included PCA calculation, Harmony integration, unsupervised clustering, and visualization via UMAP. To functionally annotate the sub-cell clusters, both DEGs and pathway enrichment were considered. GSVA was used to assess relative pathway activities in tumor cells based on the 50 Hallmark pathways using the R package GSVA (v1.53.5). The average relative expression of each set of MMAS predictor genes minus the average relative expression of all genes was used to calculate an initial subtype score for each cell114.

Cell-to-cell communication

Cell-to-cell interactions between clusters were systematically inferred and visualized using the R package CellChat (v2.1.2), following the standard framework described in the manuscript references. CellChat infers biologically significant cell-cell communication from single-nucleus RNA-seq data based on a curated ligand-receptor interaction database115.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using R (v4.2.2). The Mann-Whitney U test was used for analyzing continuous data, while Fisher’s exact test was used for analyzing categorical data. Survival rates were analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves, with differences determined using a log-rank test through the R package “survival” (v3.4.0)116. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all unadjusted analyses.
