A Weak Solution of Inverse Treblecross
Kai Liang, Muxi Li

Abstract
. We give a weak solution for an impartial game we called “Inverse Treblecross”. We have determined which of its starting positions are 𝒫 \mathscr{P} -position and give a reasonable strategy.



1.
Introduction

Combinatorial games of “Making and avoiding” constitute a well-studied and richly varied domain within combinatorial game theory.
While the majority of classic positional games in this category—such as Tic-Tac-Toe, Gomoku, and Connect-4—are partisan,
featuring players utilizing distinct sets of pieces, there is profound mathematical interest in their impartial counterparts,
where both players share a single reservoir of indistinguishable pieces.

A paradigmatic example of such an impartial game is “Treblecross”, extensively analyzed by Berlekamp, Conway, and Guy in
Winning Ways

[
2
]
.
Played on a
1\times n
strip of squares, both players take turns placing an “X”, and the first player to complete a contiguous line of three wins.
In the framework of CGT, Treblecross is equivalent to the octal game
0.007
.
Despite its apparent simplicity and decades of study, it remains notoriously open whether the Sprague-Grundy sequence of Treblecross is ultimately periodic.

In this paper, we direct our attention to the avoidance version, or following the terminology of Beck
[
1
]
, the “inverse” version of Treblecross.
In
Inverse Treblecross
, the objective is reversed: completing a line of three is strictly prohibited. Consequently, the game is played under the normal play convention,
where a player loses when they are faced with a board configuration and possess no legal moves that do not create a three-in-a-row.

To properly contextualize this game, it is instructive to compare it with its lower-order analogue, which one might call “Inverse Duocross” (avoiding two-in-a-row).
In Inverse Duocross, placing a piece effectively renders the played square and its immediate adjacent squares unplayable. Thus, a single move cleanly severs the board into independent contiguous empty segments.
This property makes Inverse Duocross structurally equivalent to (the normal play of) Dawson’s Chess (the octal game
0.137
), which effortlessly breaks down into disjunctive sums of independent subgames and possesses a famously periodic SG sequence.

The primary difficulty in analyzing Inverse Treblecross arises fundamentally from its resistance to such straightforward disjunctive sum decomposition.
Unlike Dawson’s Chess, a move in Inverse Treblecross does not inherently isolate the remaining empty spaces into independent subgames.
Because pieces are permitted to be adjacent (up to a block of two), the legality of a move within an empty segment depends heavily on the proximity and specific configuration of the pieces bounding that segment.
Consequently, an arbitrary move cannot be trivially evaluated as the nim-sum (direct XOR sum) of two completely independent smaller components, necessitating a much more nuanced state-space analysis to evaluate the game’s SG values.

It is also highly relevant to point out the game’s conceptual kinship with “Notakto” (also known as Neutral or Impartial Tic-Tac-Toe), which has generated considerable interest in recent literature
[
3
]
.
While Notakto is typically played on one or more 2D grids with the objective of forcing the opponent to complete a three-in-a-row, Inverse Treblecross serves as its foundational 1D analog. Resolving its mathematical structure provides crucial groundwork for understanding higher-dimensional impartial avoidance games.

The ultimate objective of this paper is to evaluate the game from its initial state. In the standard terminology of game solving,
our work provides a
weak solution
for Inverse Treblecross. While evaluating the exact SG value for an arbitrary, partially filled board (a
strong solution
) remains an intricate open problem due to the aforementioned boundary complexities, we successfully resolve the SG sequence for initially empty boards.
Surprisingly, we demonstrate that the SG values for an empty board are strictly restricted to
0
and
1
.
Specifically, we prove the following main theorem:

Theorem 1.1
.

An empty board with
n
points has Sprague-Grundy value
0
(i.e. second player wins) if and only if
n\equiv 0,2,3,6,9(\mathrm{mod}\ 10)
.
For the rest cases, i.e when
n\equiv 1,4,5,7,8(\mathrm{mod}\ 10)
, the Sprague-Grundy value is
1
.

Lemma 3.1
.

Given any

[X1Xw]=[Xw]

(2)

(3)

(4)

(5)

A position is single if and only if it does not contain subsequence

The following properties will also be useful to simplify the position, but we will not consider them as reducible:

Lemma 3.3

(2)

Our approach to proving the main theorem is as follows: we present an action strategy for “regular positions” (we will provide a specific definition later). Regardless of how the previous player make the move, the next player always has at least one move that can maintain the regularity of the board by utilizing the aforementioned reductions. In this way, we can recursively prove the Sprague-Grundy value for all regular positions.

While some degree of case analysis is inevitable,
our approach efficiently restricts it to a manageable scale,
allowing us to replace an extensive computer search with a compact hand-checked proof.

4.
Main Theorem

To proceed with the proof of the main theorem, we first establish the following algebraic lemma regarding the characteristic function
\chi_{1}

Lemma 4.1
.

For any
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}_{10}
, at least one of the following two equalities holds:

\displaystyle\chi_{1}(a)\chi_{1}(b)

\displaystyle=\chi_{1}(a+b+4),

\displaystyle\chi_{1}(a+1)\chi_{1}(b-1)

\displaystyle=\chi_{1}(a+b+4).

Proof.

While the following detailed algebraic proof is possible, we present a more
intuitive argument based on the structure shown in Figure
4.2
.

Recall that the map
x\mapsto 7x
acts as the inverse of
x\mapsto x/3
in
\mathbb{Z}_{10}
.
Let
I=\{-2,-1,0,1,2\}
be a symmetric set of integer representatives modulo
10

, we can uniquely decompose

as a sum
c_{x}+d_{x}\pmod{10}
, where
c_{x}\in\{0,5\}
and
d_{x}\in I

By the definition of
\chi_{1}

\chi_{1}(x)=1
if and only if

, which is strictly equivalent to

\chi_{1}(x)=1

\chi_{1}(x)=-1

To express this compactly, we define a sign function

\sigma(y)=1

\sigma(y)=-1
if

. It follows immediately that
\chi_{1}(x)=\sigma(7x-2)
and
\sigma(y_{1}+y_{2})=\sigma(y_{1})\sigma(y_{2})\sigma(d_{1}+d_{2})
.

Now, consider the term
a+b+4

\displaystyle\chi_{1}(a+b+4)

\displaystyle=\sigma(7(a+b+4)-2)

\displaystyle=\sigma(7a+7b+26)

\displaystyle=\sigma((7a-2)+(7b-2)).

Applying our decomposition yields:

\displaystyle\chi_{1}(a+b+4)

\displaystyle=\sigma(c_{a}+d_{a}+c_{b}+d_{b})

\displaystyle=\sigma(c_{a})\sigma(c_{b})\sigma(d_{a}+d_{b})

\displaystyle=\chi_{1}(a)\chi_{1}(b)\sigma(d_{a}+d_{b}).

We now evaluate the integer sum
d_{a}+d_{b}
over
\mathbb{Z}
. Since
d_{a},d_{b}\in I
, their sum lies in the interval
[-4,4]
.
If
d_{a}+d_{b}\in I

\sigma(d_{a}+d_{b})=1

\chi_{1}(a+b+4)=\chi_{1}(a)\chi_{1}(b)

Otherwise, we must have
d_{a}+d_{b}\notin I

and

to be non-zero and share the same sign. Specifically, either

or

\sigma(d_{a}+d_{b})=-1

(4.2)

\chi_{1}(a+b+4)=-\chi_{1}(a)\chi_{1}(b).

In this case, we turn our attention to the shifted parameters

\displaystyle\chi_{1}(a+1)

\displaystyle=\sigma(7(a+1)-2)=\sigma((7a-2)-3)=\chi_{1}(a)\sigma(d_{a}-3),

\displaystyle\chi_{1}(b-1)

\displaystyle=\sigma(7(b-1)-2)=\sigma((7b-2)+3)=\chi_{1}(b)\sigma(d_{b}+3).

To satisfy the second equality, it suffices to show that
\sigma(d_{a}-3)\sigma(d_{b}+3)=-1

•

If

4.1.
Empty Board States
R_{s}^{(0)}

Let
[a]\in R_{s}^{(0)}
be an empty board of length
a\geq 1
. A legal move by the Next Player places a single piece, transitioning the game to a state
[a_{1}Xa_{2}]
such that
a_{1}+a_{2}=a-1
.
Depending on the geometric placement of the piece, the move falls strictly into one of three distinct categories:

(1)

Single-cell terminal board:

a=1
, yielding the terminal state
[X]
with no remaining empty cells (
a_{1}=a_{2}=0
).

(2)

Interior move:
The piece is placed such that neither adjacent cell is the board’s strict boundary (i.e.,
a_{1}\geq 1
and
a_{2}\geq 1
).

(3)

Boundary move:
The piece is placed at the immediate edge of the board (i.e., exactly one of
a_{1},a_{2}
is
0
).

To establish the SG values of these states, we analyze the replying strategies available to the subsequent player. The following two lemmas provide a concrete “reduction move” strategy for cases (2) and (3), ensuring the game can always be fractured into favorable independent components.

Lemma 4.4
.

Suppose an empty board
[a]\in R_{s}^{(0)}
transitions to
[a_{1}Xa_{2}]
via an interior move (
a_{1},a_{2}\geq 1
). Then the responding player can always place a piece immediately adjacent to the newly placed one such that the reduced state
[v]
decomposes into a disjunctive sum
[v_{1}]\oplus[v_{2}]
, where
[v_{1}]\in R_{s_{1}}^{(0)}
,
[v_{2}]\in R_{s_{2}}^{(0)}
, and
s_{1}s_{2}=s
.

Proof.

By playing immediately adjacent to the new piece, the responding player has two candidate moves, yielding either
[(a_{1}-1)XXa_{2}]
or
[a_{1}XX(a_{2}-1)]
. By the reduction rules outlined in Lemma
3.1
, these states naturally decompose as follows:

\displaystyle[(a_{1}-1)XXa_{2}]=[(a_{1}-2)1XX1(a_{2}-1)]

\displaystyle\cong[(a_{1}-2)]\oplus[(a_{2}-1)],

\displaystyle[a_{1}XX(a_{2}-1)]=[(a_{1}-1)1XX1(a_{2}-2)]

\displaystyle\cong[(a_{1}-1)]\oplus[(a_{2}-2)].

By definition, it suffices to prove that at least one of these two configurations satisfies the target parity condition. Since
s=\chi_{1}(a)=\chi_{1}(a_{1}+a_{2}+1)
, we require either:

\displaystyle\chi_{1}(a_{1}-2)\chi_{1}(a_{2}-1)

\displaystyle=\chi_{1}(a_{1}+a_{2}+1),\quad\text{or}

\displaystyle\chi_{1}(a_{1}-1)\chi_{1}(a_{2}-2)

\displaystyle=\chi_{1}(a_{1}+a_{2}+1).

Letting
x=a_{1}-2
and
y=a_{2}-1
, we observe that
x+y+4=a_{1}+a_{2}+1
. The required condition then perfectly matches the algebraic identity proven in Lemma
4.1
, guaranteeing that a valid reduction move always exists.
∎

Lemma 4.5
.

Suppose an empty board
[a]\in R_{s}^{(0)}
transitions to
[X(a-1)]
via a boundary move (by symmetry, assume the left edge). Then the responding player can always place a piece at distance 1 or 2 from the newly placed piece such that the resulting state

R_{s}

Proof.

The responding player has two candidate moves near the edge piece: placing a piece to form
[XX(a-2)]
or
[X1X(a-3)]
. According to Lemma

, these states simplify to:

\displaystyle[XX(a-2)]=[XX1(a-3)]

\displaystyle\cong[a-3],

\displaystyle[X1X(a-3)]

\displaystyle\cong[X(a-3)].

We must verify that for any
a\geq 2
, at least one of
[a-3]
or
[X(a-3)]
belongs to
R_{s}
.
If
\chi_{1}(a-3)=\chi_{1}(a)=s
, then the first option trivially yields
[a-3]\in R_{s}^{(0)}
. Direct evaluation of the characteristic function
\chi_{1}
reveals that
\chi_{1}(a-3)=\chi_{1}(a)
holds for all residue classes modulo

, except when

and
a\equiv 5\pmod{10}
.

For these two exceptional classes, we evaluate the second option,
[X(a-3)]
, which formally writes as
[0X(a-3)]

•

If
a\equiv 0\pmod{10}
, then
s=\chi_{1}(0)=1
. The second option yields
[0X7]
. Since
\chi_{2}(0)=1
and
\chi_{2}(7)=1
, we have
\chi_{2}(0)\chi_{2}(7)=1=s

[0X7]\in R_{1}^{(1)}\subset R_{1}

•

If

\chi_{2}(0)=1

4.2.

R_{s}^{(1)}

[aX_{1}b_{1}X_{2}b_{2}]

Depending on the exact placement of

(1)

X_{2}

X_{1}
(i.e.,

(2)

Interior move:

X_{2}
is placed far from
X_{1}
and strictly within the interior of segment

b_{1}\geq 2
and
b_{2}\geq 1

(3)

X_{2}

The following three lemmas address these cases respectively. We demonstrate that a proximity move by the Next Player is fundamentally a fatal blunder that immediately surrenders the target parity, while for interior and boundary moves, the responding player can invariably execute a reduction move to restore the desired parity.

Lemma 4.7
.

If the Next Player makes a proximity move (Case 1), the resulting state
[w^{\prime}]
immediately decomposes into a disjunctive sum
[w^{\prime}_{1}]\oplus[w^{\prime}_{2}]
where
[w^{\prime}_{1}]\in R_{s_{1}}
,
[w^{\prime}_{2}]\in R_{s_{2}}
, and importantly,
s_{1}s_{2}=-s
. Thus, this move transitions the game directly to the losing parity for the Next Player.

Proof.

A proximity move to the right of
X_{1}
restricts
[w^{\prime}]
to two possible configurations:

\displaystyle[aX_{1}X_{2}(b-1)]=[aX_{1}X_{2}1(b-2)]

\displaystyle\cong[(a-1)]\oplus[(b-2)],\quad(\text{if }b_{1}=0),

\displaystyle[aX_{1}1X_{2}(b-2)]=[aX_{1}1X_{2}1(b-3)]

\displaystyle\cong[aX]\oplus[X(b-3)],\quad(\text{if }b_{1}=1).

For the first configuration, given
\chi_{2}(a)\chi_{2}(b)=s
, Proposition 2.1 dictates that
\chi_{1}(a-1)=\chi_{2}(a)
and
\chi_{1}(b-2)=-\chi_{2}(b)
. Consequently,
[(a-1)]\in R_{\chi_{2}(a)}^{(0)}
and
[(b-2)]\in R_{-\chi_{2}(b)}^{(0)}
. The product of their parities is
\chi_{2}(a)(-\chi_{2}(b))=-s
, as desired.

For the second configuration, evaluating the characteristic functions similarly yields
\chi_{2}(a)\cdot\chi_{1}(b-3)=-s
for all non-exceptional residue classes. For exceptional boundary cases (e.g., when
a\equiv 3\pmod{10}
), the components naturally map into the corresponding exceptional subsets
R^{*}
, preserving the total parity
s_{1}s_{2}=-s
.
∎

Lemma 4.8
.

If the Next Player makes an interior move (Case 2), resulting in
[aX_{1}b_{1}X_{2}b_{2}]
with
b_{1}\geq 2,b_{2}\geq 1
, the responding player can always place a piece adjacent to
X_{2}
to force a reduction
[v]\cong[v_{1}]\oplus[v_{2}]
such that
v_{1}\in R_{s_{1}}
,
v_{2}\in R_{s_{2}}
, and
s_{1}s_{2}=s
.

Proof.

The responding player has two candidate moves adjacent to
X_{2}
, producing either:

\displaystyle[aX(b_{1}-1)XXb_{2}]

\displaystyle\cong[aX(b_{1}-2)]\oplus[(b_{2}-1)],\quad\text{or}

\displaystyle[aXb_{1}XX(b_{2}-1)]

\displaystyle\cong[aX(b_{1}-1)]\oplus[(b_{2}-2)].

We must prove that at least one of these resulting parities matches
s
, meaning either:

(4.9)

\displaystyle\chi_{2}(a)\chi_{2}(b_{1}-2)\chi_{1}(b_{2}-1)

\displaystyle=s,\quad\text{or}

(4.10)

\displaystyle\chi_{2}(a)\chi_{2}(b_{1}-1)\chi_{1}(b_{2}-2)

\displaystyle=s.

Since
\chi_{2}(a)\chi_{2}(b)=s
, we can factor out
\chi_{2}(a)
by requiring the remaining terms to equal
\chi_{2}(b)
.
By the definition of
\chi_{2}
, the values
\chi_{2}(b_{1}-1)
and
\chi_{2}(b_{1}-2)
cannot simultaneously be
0
. Thus, there exists an index
i\in\{1,2\}
such that
\chi_{2}(b_{1}-i)\in\{1,-1\}
.
Recall that
b=b_{1}+b_{2}+1
, which implies
b_{2}-(3-i)=b-(b_{1}-i)-2
. By evaluating the behavior of
\chi_{1}(x-y-2)
for fixed
\chi_{2}(x)
and
\chi_{2}(y)
, it rigorously follows that
\chi_{2}(b_{1}-i)\chi_{1}(b_{2}-(3-i))=\chi_{2}(b)
. Multiplying both sides by
\chi_{2}(a)
exactly satisfies condition (
4.9
) or (
4.10
) depending on the chosen
i
.
∎

Lemma 4.11
.

If the Next Player makes a boundary move (Case 3), resulting in
[aX_{1}(b-1)X_{2}]
with
b\geq 3
, the responding player can always find a reduction move yielding
[v]\cong[v_{1}]\oplus[v_{2}]
where
s_{1}s_{2}=s
.

Proof.

The primary candidate move for the responding player is to place a piece to the immediate left of the newly placed
X_{2}
(acting as
X_{3}
). Note that placing it adjacent to
X_{1}
is handled symmetrically by the left segment
a
.
If a valid move can be made adjacent to
X_{1}
without bridging the gap to
X_{2}
or hitting the left boundary, we obtain:

[(a-1)XX(b-1)X_{2}]\cong[(a-2)]\oplus[(b-2)].

Given
\chi_{2}(a)\chi_{2}(b)=s
, Proposition 2.1 implies
\chi_{1}(a-2)=-\chi_{2}(a)
and
\chi_{1}(b-2)=-\chi_{2}(b)
. The product is
(-\chi_{2}(a))(-\chi_{2}(b))=s
. Hence,
[(a-2)]\in R_{s_{1}}^{(0)}
and
[(b-2)]\in R_{s_{2}}^{(0)}
with
s_{1}s_{2}=s
.

This specific reduction move is structurally obstructed only if
a=0
(the boundary is reached) or
a=1
(the move would illegally create a three-in-a-row).
In such restricted boundary configurations, the responding player simply places a piece to the immediate right of
X_{1}
instead:

[XX(b-2)]\cong[\emptyset]\oplus[(b-3)]=[(b-3)].

Since
a\in\{0,1\}
, we have
\chi_{2}(a)=1
. Thus
\chi_{2}(b)=s
. By definition of the characteristic functions,
\chi_{1}(b-3)=\chi_{2}(b)=s
holds for all non-exceptional cases, yielding
[(b-3)]\in R_{s}^{(0)}
. For the few exceptional residue classes (e.g.,
b\equiv 2\pmod{10}
), the reduced component rigorously falls into
R_{s}^{*}
, completing the proof.
∎

4.3.
Exceptional Boundary States
R_{s}^{*}

Let
[w]\in R_{s}^{*}
be an exceptional boundary state. Up to spatial reflection, these states are entirely represented by the four residue classes
[X\bar{6}]
,
[X\bar{4}X]
,
[X\bar{1}]
, and
[X\bar{9}X]
.
Suppose the Next Player makes a valid move. Depending on the geometry of the placement, the transition falls into one of three distinct categories:

(1)

Terminal state exhaustion:
No further valid moves exist. This applies exclusively to the configurations
[X2]
and
[2X]
(within the classes
[X\bar{2}]
and
[\bar{2}X]
when the empty segment has exactly length
2
).

(2)

Proximity move:
The Next Player places a piece at distance
1
or
2
from an existing piece (i.e., adjacent to or with a single-gap separation from an existing boundary piece).

(3)

Interior move:
The Next Player places a piece strictly in the interior of the empty segment, maintaining a distance of at least
3
from all existing pieces.

The following two lemmas address cases (2) and (3), demonstrating that proximity moves inherently surrender the parity, while interior moves can invariably be countered with a reduction move to preserve the parity.

Lemma 4.12
.

If the Next Player makes a proximity move (Case 2), the game reduces to a state
[w^{\prime}]
or decomposes into a disjunctive sum
[w^{\prime}_{1}]\oplus[w^{\prime}_{2}]
such that the resulting parity exactly aligns with
R_{-s}
.

Proof.

A proximity move structurally anchors to the existing boundary piece(s). Evaluating the reduction rules for each equivalence class yields:

•

For
[w]\in[X\bar{6}]
, the Next Player’s proximity move yields
[XX\bar{4}]\cong[\bar{3}]\in R_{-1}^{(0)}
.

•

For
[w]\in[X\bar{4}X]
, the move yields
[XX\bar{3}X]\cong[\bar{2}X]
. Since
\chi_{2}(2)\chi_{2}(0)=(-1)(1)=-1
, this reduced state belongs to
R_{-1}^{(1)}
.

•

For
[w]\in[X\bar{1}]
, the move yields
[XX\bar{9}]\cong[\bar{8}]\in R_{1}^{(0)}
.

•

For
[w]\in[X\bar{9}X]
, the move yields
[XX\bar{8}X]\cong[\bar{7}X]
. Since
\chi_{2}(7)\chi_{2}(0)=(1)(1)=1
, this reduced state belongs to
R_{1}^{(1)}
.

In all cases, the initial parity
s
flips to
-s
, confirming that the Next Player transitions the game to a losing configuration.
∎

Lemma 4.13
.

If the Next Player makes an interior move (Case 3), the responding player can always place a piece adjacent to the newly placed one such that the reduced state decomposes as
[v]\cong[v_{1}]\oplus[v_{2}]
, where
v_{1}\in R_{s_{1}}
,
v_{2}\in R_{s_{2}}
, and
s_{1}s_{2}=s
.

Proof.

The algebraic structures of
[X\bar{1}]
and
[X\bar{9}X]
are identical to those of
[X\bar{6}]
and
[X\bar{4}X]
, respectively, differing solely in sign arithmetic. For brevity, we detail the proofs for
[X\bar{6}]
and
[X\bar{4}X]
, where the target parity is
s=1
.

Case 3.1:
[w]\in[X\bar{6}]
.

The state can be written as
[X_{1}(10k+6)]
for some
k\in\mathbb{N}
. An interior move places
X_{2}
, splitting the empty segment into
a
and
b
, yielding
[X_{1}aX_{2}b]
with
a+b=10k+5
. Since it is an interior move,
a\geq 2
.
The responding player has two candidate moves adjacent to
X_{2}
, yielding either:

\displaystyle[X(a-1)XXb]

\displaystyle\cong[X(a-2)]\oplus[(b-1)],\quad\text{or}

\displaystyle[XaXX(b-1)]

\displaystyle\cong[X(a-1)]\oplus[(b-2)].

By the definition of the characteristic function
\chi_{2}
, the values
\chi_{2}(a-2)
and
\chi_{2}(a-1)
cannot simultaneously be
0
. Thus, there exists an index
i\in\{1,2\}
such that
\chi_{2}(a-i)=\sigma\in\{1,-1\}
.
We evaluate the corresponding right-side component:

\displaystyle\chi_{1}(b-(3-i))

\displaystyle=\chi_{1}((10k+5-a)-(3-i))

\displaystyle=\chi_{1}(2-a+i)

\displaystyle=-\chi_{1}(a-i-2)\quad(\text{by Proposition 2.1})

\displaystyle=\chi_{2}(a-i)=\sigma.

This establishes that
[X(a-i)]\in R_{\sigma}^{(1)}
and
[(b-(3-i))]\in R_{\sigma}^{(0)}
. Their combined parity is
\sigma\times\sigma=1=s
, satisfying the condition.

Case 3.2:
[w]\in[X\bar{4}X]
.

The state can be written as
[X(10k+4)X]
. An interior move yields
[XaX_{2}bX]
with
a+b=10k+3
, where
a\geq 2
and
b\geq 2
.
The responding player’s two candidate moves adjacent to
X_{2}
yield:

\displaystyle[X(a-1)XXbX]

\displaystyle\cong[X(a-2)]\oplus[(b-1)X],\quad\text{or}

\displaystyle[XaXX(b-1)X]

\displaystyle\cong[X(a-1)]\oplus[(b-2)X].

As before, there exists an index
i\in\{1,2\}
such that
\chi_{2}(a-i)=\sigma\in\{1,-1\}
.
We evaluate the corresponding right-side component:

\displaystyle\chi_{2}(b-(3-i))

\displaystyle=\chi_{2}((10k+3-a)-(3-i))

\displaystyle=\chi_{2}(-a+i)

\displaystyle=\chi_{2}(a-i)\quad(\text{by Proposition 2.1})

\displaystyle=\sigma.

This establishes that
[X(a-i)]\in R_{\sigma}^{(1)}
and
[(b-(3-i))X]\in R_{\sigma}^{(1)}
. Their combined parity is
\sigma\times\sigma=1=s
, completing the proof.
∎

4.4.
Synthesis and the Sprague-Grundy Evaluation

Having exhaustively analyzed all possible legal moves across the three regular sub-families (
R_{s}^{(0)},R_{s}^{(1)}
, and
R_{s}^{*}
), we consolidate these tactical maneuvers into a comprehensive structural theorem.

Theorem 4.14
.

Let
[w]\in R_{s}
be a non-terminal regular position. The game tree from
[w]
admits the following universal reduction mechanism:

(1)

Reactive reduction:
Regardless of where the Next Player places a piece, the responding player can always secure a reply that strictly fractures the resulting state into a disjunctive sum
[v]\cong\bigoplus_{j}[v_{j}]
, such that every
[v_{j}]\in R_{s_{j}}
and the multiplicative parity is preserved:
\prod_{j}s_{j}=s
.

(2)

Proactive reduction:
The Next Player currently facing
[w]
always possesses at least one valid outbound move that strictly transitions the game into a disjunctive sum
[u]\cong\bigoplus_{j}[u_{j}]
, such that every
[u_{j}]\in R_{s_{j}}
and the multiplicative parity is inverted:
\prod_{j}s_{j}=-s
.

Proof.

The reactive mechanism (1) is the synthesis of our interior and boundary response lemmas (Lemmas
4.4
,
4.5
,
4.8
,
4.11
, and
4.13
). For proximity moves made by the Next Player, Lemmas
4.7
and
4.12
demonstrate that the state inherently fractures into the desired inverted parity components without requiring a response. If the Next Player makes such a blunder, the responding player simply treats the resulting independent smaller components as new initial states and applies the proactive mechanism to them.
The proactive mechanism (2) is guaranteed for empty boards by Lemma
4.6
, and naturally extends to
R^{(1)}
and
R^{*}
by intentionally making proximity moves.
∎

We are now equipped to formally evaluate the Sprague-Grundy values for all regular positions, which serves as the final proof of our main theorem.

Theorem 4.15
.

The SG values for states strictly satisfy
\mathcal{G}(R_{1})=0
and
\mathcal{G}(R_{-1})=1
. Consequently, an empty board of length
n
has an SG value of
0
if
\chi_{1}(n)=1
, and
1
if
\chi_{1}(n)=-1
.

Proof.

We proceed by strong induction on the structural length of the game state. It is trivial to verify that the terminal base cases conform to the theorem. Assume the theorem holds for all regular positions strictly shorter than the current state
[w]\in R_{s}
.

Notice the profound algebraic isomorphism linking our parity index
s\in\{1,-1\}
to the nim-sum operation over SG values
\{0,1\}
. By the inductive hypothesis,
v_{j}\in R_{s_{j}}
implies
\mathcal{G}([v_{j}])=\frac{1-s_{j}}{2}
. Therefore, for any disjunctive sum of regular positions, the nim-sum evaluation is precisely governed by their multiplicative parity:

(4.16)

\displaystyle\bigoplus_{j}\mathcal{G}([v_{j}])=0

\displaystyle\iff\prod_{j}s_{j}=1,\quad\text{and}

\displaystyle\quad\bigoplus_{j}\mathcal{G}([v_{j}])=1

\displaystyle\iff\prod_{j}s_{j}=-1.

Case 1:
[w]\in R_{1}
.

By Theorem
4.14
(1), for any arbitrary move
[w]\mapsto[w^{\prime}]
chosen by the first player, the second player can always reply with
[w^{\prime}]\mapsto\bigoplus_{j}[v_{j}]
such that
\prod_{j}s_{j}=1
. By equation (
4.16
),
\mathcal{G}(\bigoplus_{j}[v_{j}])=0
.
By the definition of the SG function,
\mathcal{G}([w^{\prime}])=\mathrm{mex}\{0,\dots\}>0
for all possible first moves
[w^{\prime}]
. Consequently,

\mathcal{G}([w])=\mathrm{mex}_{w^{\prime}}\{\mathcal{G}([w^{\prime}])\}=0.

Case 2:
[w]\in R_{-1}
.

Similarly, by Theorem
4.14
(1), for any arbitrary move
[w]\mapsto[w^{\prime}]
chosen by the first player, the second player can reply with a reduction yielding
\prod_{j}s_{j}=-1
. By equation (
4.16
),
\mathcal{G}(\bigoplus_{j}[v_{j}])=1
. Thus,
\mathcal{G}([w^{\prime}])=\mathrm{mex}\{1,\dots\}\neq 1
for all
[w^{\prime}]
.
This strictly implies
\mathcal{G}([w])\leq 1
.
To prove
\mathcal{G}([w])=1
, we must show there exists at least one option
[w^{\prime}]
such that
\mathcal{G}([w^{\prime}])=0
. By Theorem
4.14
(2), the first player has a proactive move
[w]\mapsto\bigoplus_{j}[u_{j}]
such that the resulting multiplicative parity is
-(-1)=1
. By equation (
4.16
), this specific option has an SG value of
0
. Therefore:

\mathcal{G}([w])=\mathrm{mex}\{0,\dots\}=1.

This completes the induction. Since any initial empty board
[n]
is, by definition, an element of
R_{\chi_{1}(n)}^{(0)}
, the main theorem follows directly.
∎

5.
Open Problems and Further Extensions

Although the initial empty-board problem for Inverse Treblecross has been resolved, our investigation has uncovered several intriguing structural patterns that extend beyond our current theoretical framework.
Because our “reduction move” strategy strictly relies on immediate parity responses, it is insufficient to fully characterize these broader phenomena.
In this section, we propose several conjectures and extensions that highlight promising avenues for future research, likely requiring more sophisticated algebraic or combinatorial tools.

5.1.
Coverage of Regular Positions

First, we observe that the regular positions defined in our main theorem almost exclusively contain no more than one placed piece (with
[X\bar{4}X]
and
[X\bar{9}X]
being the only exceptions).
Computational evidence strongly suggests that our definition of
R
nearly exhausts the universe of simple states with an SG value of
0
or
1
.
Specifically, we propose the following completeness conjecture:

Conjecture 5.1
.

The regular set
R
contains all board states that possess at most one piece and satisfy
\mathcal{G}\leq 1
, with exactly four isolated exceptions:

[1X1],\quad[1X4],\quad[4X1],\quad\text{and}\quad[4X4].

These four exceptional states act as isolated anomalies: they are the unique known states within their respective residue classes (
[\bar{1}X\bar{1}]
,
[\bar{1}X\bar{4}]
,
[\bar{4}X\bar{1}]
, and
[\bar{4}X\bar{4}]
) that evaluate to an SG value of
1
. Empirical data indicates that all other sufficiently long states in these classes strictly evaluate to
\mathcal{G}>1

5.4.
Circular Chessboards

Finally, analyzing Inverse Treblecross on a circular ring introduces nontrivial boundary conditions. Let
(l)
denote an empty circular board of length
l
. For
k=3
, the SG values exhibit at least partial periodicity.

Proposition 5.4
.

The SG value of an empty circular board evaluates to:

\mathcal{G}((l))=\begin{cases}0,&l\pmod{10}\in\{0,2,3,4,6,7,8\};\\
1,&l\pmod{10}=5.\end{cases}

Proof.

For
l\pmod{10}\in\{4,6,7,0,3\}
, an arbitrary first move by the Next Player places a piece, strictly transforming the ring into a linear segment. The responding player can immediately execute a proximity move adjacent to the first piece, effectively absorbing
4
spaces and reducing the game to the linear configuration
[l-4]
. By Theorem
4.15
,
\chi_{1}(l-4)=1
, yielding
\mathcal{G}([l-4])=0
.

When
l
is strictly even (
l\pmod{10}\in\{0,2,4,6,8\}
), the standard Tweedledum-Tweedledee strategy applies seamlessly: the responding player universally mirrors the first player’s move across the circle’s axis of symmetry.

For the exceptional case
l\pmod{10}=5
, analogous to our linear analysis, the responding player can invariably find a reduction move that fractures the circle into a regular linear board
[v]\in R_{1}
, strictly maintaining an SG value of
0
.
∎

However, for
l\equiv 1\pmod{10}
, computational data reveals that the SG sequence is not perfectly periodic, as
\mathcal{G}((21))=0
while
\mathcal{G}((11))=1
.

It is also worth highlighting why the traditional Tweedledum-Tweedledee symmetry strategy fails on linear boards of even length, yet succeeds on circular ones. On a linear board, mimicking a move symmetrically can result in an illegal placement. For instance, if the first player places a piece near the center to create
[w1X4w^{R}]
, the symmetric mirror requires placing a piece separated by exactly two empty spaces, forming
[w1X2X1w^{R}]
. Subsequent moves in this central zone frequently force three-in-a-row infractions that break the symmetry.
