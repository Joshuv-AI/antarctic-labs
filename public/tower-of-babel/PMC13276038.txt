BLAST demonstrates higher prognostic accuracy for survival than CPSS in Chinese patients with chronic myelomonocytic leukemia
Authors: Xin Yan, Gege Feng, Zhimei Cai, Jiale Ma, Shuqian Xu, Zheng Ge
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

Chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML) is a hematopoietic stem cell-derived myeloid malignancy that exhibits marked biological and clinical heterogeneity, with substantial variability in patient prognosis [1–3]. Early identification of high-risk patients who may benefit from timely allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (allo-HSCT) is therefore critical. Over the past two decades, several prognostic models have been developed for CMML, ranging from systems based solely on clinical parameters to those incorporating cytogenetic and molecular features [4–13]. Among these, clinical tools such as CMML-specific Prognostic Scoring System (CPSS) and CPSS-Molecular (CPSS-mol) are the most widely used. Recently, Tefferi et al. [14] developed the Blasts, Leukocytes, and Anemia-based Survival Tool for Chronic Myelomonocytic Leukemia (BLAST), a prognostic model based exclusively on easily accessible clinical variables, as well as an updated version integrating molecular data, the Blasts, Leukocytes, and Anemia-based Survival Tool with Molecular Integration for Chronic Myelomonocytic Leukemia (BLAST-mol), and showed that they outperform existing tools. However, the BLAST and BLAST-mol have been validated only in Western patient cohorts, and their performance in Chinese patients remains unclear. Therefore, this study aims to compare the prognostic predictive performance of the BLAST, BLAST-mol, CPSS, and CPSS-mol in Chinese CMML patients.

We included all patients with CMML who were diagnosed or reclassified according to the 2022 World Health Organization (WHO) classification between July 20, 2016, and December 1, 2025, across four hospitals. Patient enrollment and analysis schema were described in Supplementary Fig. 1. Patient samples were obtained with written informed consent, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The protocol was approved by the human research ethics committee of the four participating hospitals.

DNA from bone marrow mononuclear cells was analyzed by next-generation sequencing (NGS) as previously described [15]. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq platform, and the genes listed in Supplementary Table 1 were assessed in all cases. Molecular risk categories and adverse karyotype were defined according to Tefferi et al. [14].

Baseline clinical characteristics were summarized in Supplementary Table 2. All 127 patients were informative for genetic mutations (Supplementary Fig. 2). The most frequently mutated genes were ASXL1 (66/127, 51.9%), TET2 (51/127, 40.2%), SRSF2 (43/127, 33.9%), NRAS (29/127, 22.8%), KRAS (23/127, 18.1%), RUNX1 (21/127, 16.5%). Regarding treatment, 17 patients received hydroxyurea, 18 received best supportive care alone, 58 received hypomethylating agent (HMA) monotherapy (decitabine or azacitidine), 15 received HMA-based combination therapy (with lenalidomide or ruxolitinib), 14 received azacitidine plus venetoclax, and 5 underwent allo-HSCT. At the last follow-up, 68 patients (54.4%) had died, and 25 (20%) had transformed to blast phase.

Results of the univariate and multivariable analyses were summarized in Supplementary Table 3. In univariate analysis, the following variables were associated with inferior OS: moderate anemia (HR 2.55, 95% CI 1.66–5.60, P = 0.02), severe anemia (HR 3.17, 95% CI 1.56–6.41, P = 0.001), PB blast ≥2% (HR 1.79, 95% CI 1.10–2.92, P = 0.019), adverse karyotype (HR 1.85, 95% CI 1.09–3.14, P = 0.023), ASXL1 mutation (HR 1.66, 95% CI 1.01–2.72, P = 0.044) and TP53 mutation (HR 3.21, 95% CI 1.65–6.25, P = 0.001). WBC ≥ 13 × 109/L (HR 1.57, 95% CI 0.98–2.53, P = 0.064) and high molecular risk (HR 3.21, 95% CI 0.99–10.42, P = 0.052) showed a trend toward statistical significance, and thus were included together in the multivariate analysis. In multivariate analysis, severe anemia (HR 2.39, 95% CI 1.12–5.11, P = 0.025) and TP53 mutation (HR 2.54, 95% CI 1.23–5.22, P = 0.012) remained independent poor prognostic factors for OS.

Risk stratification by CPSS, CPSS-mol, BLAST, BLAST-mol prognostic scoring system

A total of 125 patients were evaluable for risk stratification using the CPSS- and BLAST-based prognostic scoring systems, According to CPSS, 27 cases (21.6%) were assigned to the low-risk group, 40 cases (32.0%) to the intermediate-1 (Int-1) group, 50 cases (40.0%) to the intermediate-2 (Int-2) group, and 8 cases (6.4%) to the high-risk group. The median OS (mOS) was not reached (NR) in the low-risk group, 17 months (95% CI 9.78–24.22) in the Int-1 group, 16 months (95% CI 11.50–20.50) in the Int-2 group, and 8 months (95% CI 0.98–15.02) in the high-risk group. Difference in mOS across all CPSS risk subgroups was statistically significant (P = 0.0049; Fig. 1A); however, no statistically significant difference was observed between the Int-1 and Int-2 groups (P = 0.649), suggesting limited discriminatory ability of the CPSS for the two intermediate subgroups in this Chinese cohort.

For the BLAST scoring system, the 125 patients were stratified to the low-risk group (11, 8.8%), intermediate-risk group (24, 19.2%), and high-risk group (90, 72.0%). The corresponding mOS were NR, 30 months (95% CI 4.25–55.75), and 14 months (95% CI 10.59–17.41), respectively, with a statistically significant difference across all BLAST risk strata (P = 0.0005; Fig. 1B). Results of the univariate and multivariable analyses for the BLAST high-risk group (n = 90) are summarized in Supplementary Table 4. In univariate analysis, adverse karyotype (HR 2.03, 95% CI 1.14–3.62, P = 0.017) and TP53 mutation (HR 2.45, 95% CI 1.25–4.81, P = 0.009) were associated with inferior OS. In multivariable analysis, TP53 mutation (HR 2.81, 95% CI 1.42–5.60, P = 0.003) remained an independent adverse prognostic factor for OS.

Using the CPSS-mol model, 13 (10.4%), 18 (14.4%), 55 (44.0%), and 39 (31.2%) patients were classified as low-, intermediate-1 (Int-1), intermediate-2 (Int-2), and high-risk, respectively. The mOS was NR in the low-risk group, 22 months (95% CI 13.60–30.40) in the Int-1 group, 16 months (95% CI 13.01–18.99) in the Int-2 group, and 12 months (95% CI 2.66–21.34) in the high-risk group, respectively. Overall differences across CPSS-mol categories were significant (P = 0.0049; Fig. 1C), but mOS did not differ significantly between the Int-2 and high-risk groups (P = 0.568), indicating limited discrimination between these two higher-risk categories.

With the BLAST-mol system, the same 125 patients were stratified to low- (12, 9.6%), intermediate- (47, 37.6%), and high-risk (66, 52.8%) groups. The mOS was NR, 24 months (95% CI 16.53–31.47), and 12 months (95% CI 9.23–14.77), respectively, with highly significant differences across BLAST-mol risk categories (P < 0.0001; Fig. 1D).

Comparison of BLAST versus CPSS and BLAST-mol versus CPSS-mol

When comparing CPSS with BLAST (by merging CPSS intermediate-1 and intermediate-2 risk to BLAST intermediate risk), 95 of 125 subjects (76%) were reclassified. Among these subjects, 92 (96.8%) were upstaged, and 3 (3.2%) were downstaged (Fig. 2A). Specifically, 19 of 27 patients (70.4%) initially classified as CPSS low risk were upstaged to higher-risk BLAST categories (intermediate or high). Among the 90 patients in the combined CPSS intermediate-1/2 group, 3 (3.3%) were reassigned to BLAST low risk and 73 (81.1%) to BLAST high risk, while all patients in the CPSS high-risk group remained BLAST high risk.

We further compared OS between patients whose BLAST risk category was upgraded and those whose category was not, relative to CPSS. After excluding patients initially classified as high risk by CPSS, patients whose BLAST risk category was upgraded had significantly shorter OS than those whose BLAST risk category was unchanged (NR vs. 16 months, 95% CI 12.18–19.82, P < 0.001; Fig. 2B).

Moreover, we analyzed the reclassification of patients from CPSS-mol to BLAST-mol (again merging the CPSS-mol intermediate-1 and intermediate-2 categories to the BLAST-mol intermediate-risk group). Fifty-seven of 125 subjects (45.6%) were reclassified. Of these subjects, 42 (73.7%) were upstaged and 15 (26.3%) downstaged (Fig. 2C). Specifically, 8 of 13 patients (61.5%) in the CPSS-mol low-risk category were upstaged to BLAST-mol higher-risk categories (intermediate or high). In the CPSS-mol intermediate-1/2 group, 7 of 73 patients (9.6%) were shifted to the BLAST-mol low-risk category and 34 of 73 (46.6%) to BLAST-mol high-risk category. 8 of 39 (20.5%) patients of the CPSS-mol high-risk categories were downstaged to the lower-risk group in BLAST-mol.

In addition, we compared the OS between patients whose risk category was upgraded by BLAST-mol and those whose category was not elevated relative to CPSS-mol. After excluding patients who were initially classified as high risk by CPSS-mol, patients whose BLAST-mol risk category was upgraded had significantly shorter OS than those whose BLAST-mol risk category was unchanged (27 months, 95% CI 6.89–47.11; vs. 16 months, 95% CI 10.06–21.94; P = 0.025; Fig. 2D). These findings suggest that the BLAST/BLAST-mol prognostic scoring system more effectively identifies potential high-risk patients who are not recognized as such by the CPSS/CPSS-mol models.

The areas under the ROC curve (AUC) for CPSS, CPSS-mol, BLAST, and BLAST-mol at 2 years were 0.578, 0.587, 0.637, and 0.664, respectively. At 3 years, the corresponding AUCs were 0.567, 0.596, 0.637, and 0.658, respectively (Fig. 2E). Together, these results indicated that BLAST and BLAST-mol exhibited superior predictive performance for survival compared with the CPSS and CPSS-mol prognostic scoring systems.

In this retrospective study of Chinese patients with CMML, we found that the BLAST and BLAST-mol scoring systems provided superior prognostic discrimination compared with CPSS and CPSS-mol. BLAST-based models more clearly separated overall survival across risk categories and identified patients with poor outcomes who were classified as low or intermediate risk by CPSS/CPSS-mol, as shown by the adverse survival of upstaged patients. These findings suggest that BLAST and BLAST-mol offer a more refined and practical framework for risk assessment in this population and may better inform the selection of candidates for allogeneic stem cell transplantation or clinical trials. Larger, multicenter studies are warranted to further validate these findings and to confirm the broader applicability of BLAST and BLAST-mol in diverse CMML populations.
