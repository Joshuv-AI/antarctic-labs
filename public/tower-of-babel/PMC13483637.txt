Coenzyme Q4 gene compound heterozygous mutations cause hereditary spastic paraplegias: a case report
Authors: YaLan Rong, LiZi Ye, Ci Liu, XiaoYan Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundComplicated hereditary spastic paraplegias (complicated HSPs) are a subtype of hereditary spastic paraplegias (HSPs), which refer to a group of hereditary neurodegenerative diseases of the nervous system characterized by stiffness of the lower limbs and hyperreflexia, and are accompanied by other symptoms. HSPs exhibit high clinical and genetic heterogeneity, making diagnosis challenging, especially in early-onset cases. Mutations in the COQ4 gene have been rarely reported to be associated with complicated HSPs, and most previously reported COQ4-related HSP cases lack detailed clinical descriptions or involve novel mutations. Through this case report, we aim to expand the genotype spectrum of complicated HSPs caused by COQ4 gene variants, provide detailed clinical and genetic data for early-onset HSP, and help improve the understanding and diagnosis of COQ4-related neurodegenerative diseases in clinical practice.Case presentationA 12-year-and-9-month-old Chinese boy presenting with progressive spastic paraplegia of both lower limbs, gait disturbance, and mild tremor who had an undetermined genotype, as well as his parents were recruited. Whole-exome sequencing (WES) was performed on the proband and his parents. Two COQ4 variants, c.433C > T (p.Arg145Cys) and c.719G > T (p.Arg240Leu), were identified, among which c.719G > T (p.Arg240Leu) represented the first reported case in complicated hereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP), and c.433C > T (p.Arg145Cys) had been previously documented.ConclusionsOur case expanded the genotype of complicated HSPs caused by COQ4 compound heterozygous mutations. Introduction

Hereditary spastic paraplegias (HSPs) are a group of hereditary neurodegenerative diseases of the nervous system characterized primarily by lower limb spasticity, with or without other neurological abnormalities, such as peripheral neuropathy, intellectual disability, cerebellar ataxia, tremor, and seizure [1–3].

Based on clinical characteristics, the phenotypes of HSP can be initially categorized into two major types: pure and complicated [2, 3]. By inheritance pattern, HSP can be divided into autosomal dominant (AD), autosomal recessive (AR), X-linked (XL), and mitochondrial inheritance [4]. By age of onset, HSP can be classified into early-onset type (onset < 35 years, Type I) and late-onset type (onset ≥ 35 years, Type II) [4].

Due to the heterogeneous features of HSP, it is overwhelmingly difficult to distinguish HSP from other neurogenetic diseases, such as mitochondrial diseases [5].

Despite the growing recognition of mitochondrial dysfunction in neurodegenerative disorders, COQ4-related hereditary spastic paraplegia (HSP) remains exceptionally rare, with only a handful of cases reported globally. The clinical spectrum of COQ4 mutations is broad, ranging from isolated spasticity to severe multisystem involvement, yet the specific genotype–phenotype correlations, particularly regarding compound heterozygous variants, remain poorly defined. Most reported COQ4 cases involve homozygous mutations or present with early-onset encephalopathy rather than pure or complicated HSP phenotypes. Here, we describe the clinical and molecular characterization of a 12-year-and-9-month-old Chinese boy with a novel COQ4 mutation.

Case presentation

We reported a 12-year-and-9-month-old Chinese male, the only child of healthy, non-consanguineous parents. He was born via normal vaginal delivery at term with normal birth weight and developmental milestones until school age. There is no family history of neuromuscular disorders, consanguinity, or sudden unexplained deaths. Psychosocial history revealed no significant stressors, and the patient attended regular school with average academic performance prior to symptom onset.

A detailed timeline of the clinical course was as follows: T = 0 (1 year prior to presentation): The patient first noticed difficulty running and frequent tripping during physical education classes. T = about 5 months: Symptoms progressed to an inability to run. He began experiencing soreness and cramping pain in both calves and ankles specifically after physical activity (exercise-induced myalgia). No sensory numbness, sphincter dysfunction, or cognitive decline was reported. T = about 12 months (current visit): The patient presented to our department due to worsening gait instability and frequent falls while walking.

Physical examination findings on admission, vital signs were stable. No abnormalities were found in cardiopulmonary. Neurological examination revealed: Alert, oriented, with normal speech and cognitive function. Cranial Nerves: Normal ocular movements; no ptosis or ophthalmoplegia. Motor System: Muscle strength was grade 5/5 in all limbs. However, increased muscle tone was evident in both lower extremities. A scissoring gait was observed. Involuntary mild tremors were noted in both upper extremities, exacerbated during goal-directed tasks (object holding). Pes cavus deformity was present (Fig. 1). Reflexes: Hyperreflexia in lower limbs (+ + +), positive bilateral patellar clonus, and a positive right Babinski sign. Upper limb reflexes were normal ( +). Hoffman's signs were negative. Coordination: Finger–nose test was steady; heel–knee–tibia test showed mild dysmetria on the left (Modified Ashworth Scale, MSA 1 level).

To establish a comprehensive differential diagnosis for pediatric spastic paraplegia, a targeted laboratory workup was performed. Serum ceruloplasmin was measured to exclude Wilson disease, given the patient's tremor and gait disturbance. Serum lactate levels were assessed to screen for underlying mitochondrial dysfunction, while thyroid function tests and autoantibody profiles (including ANCA) were included to rule out metabolic encephalopathies and autoimmune myelitis, respectively.

Results showed that routine investigations (complete blood counts, electrolytes, liver/renal function), muscle enzymes, myocardial enzymes, lactate, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, ceruloplasmin, thyroid function, and autoantibody profiles were all within normal limits. Echocardiography and MRI of the brain and whole spinal cord also revealed no structural or signal abnormalities. Nerve conduction velocities, including motor and sensory conduction of all four limbs, F-waves, and H-reflexes, were within normal limits.

Due to the unclear etiology of spasticity in both lower limbs, finally, the patient underwent whole-exome sequencing, WES identified compound heterozygous variants in the COQ4 gene (NM016035.5): c.433C > T (p.Arg145Cys) in exon 5, inherited from the mother. c.719G > T (p.Arg240Leu) in exon 7, inherited from the father. Both variants were confirmed by Sanger sequencing (Fig. 2). According to the ACMG guidelines, c.433C > T (p.Arg145Cys) was classified as a likely pathogenic variant (PM2, PM3, PP3), and c.719G > T (p.Arg240Leu) was classified as a novel likely pathogenic variant (PM2, PP3, PP4). Neither parent exhibited clinical symptoms, supporting an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern (Fig. 3).

Combined with the patient's clinical manifestations and genetic testing results, the child was diagnosed with COQ4-related disorder presenting as Hereditary Spastic Paraplegia (HSP). Although COQ4 mutations are classically associated with Spastic Ataxia Type 10 (SPAX10, OMIM #613939), characterized by prominent cerebellar ataxia, our patient presented primarily with progressive lower limb spasticity, hyperreflexia, and Babinski signs, with only mild cerebellar signs (mild dysmetria on heel–knee–tibia test, no significant gait ataxia beyond spasticity). Given that the clinical boundary between complicated HSP and spastic ataxia can be overlapping, we classified this case as complicated HSP based on the predominance of pyramidal tract signs over cerebellar dysfunction, consistent with recent literature describing COQ4-related HSP phenotypes.

To manage spasticity, the patient was treated with oral Baclofen (After the first visit, baclofen 10 mg three times daily was administered). Follow-up at T = 2 weeks: No improvement was observed in the patient’s spasticity of both lower limbs by the patient's father reported (WES indicated mutations in COQ4); therefore, Coenzyme Q10 was added (30 mg three times daily). At T = 4 months: The patient’s lower limb spasticity still showed no improvement, and the patient developed low mood and school avoidance (MSA 1 + level). Regrettably, the patient refused to attend the psychological specialist clinic for further evaluation and intervention.

Discussion

The prevalence of autosomal recessive hereditary spastic paraplegia (AR-HSP) ranges from 0.3 to 5.3 per 100,000 population, with significant variations across different ethnic groups [6]. While SPG11, SPG15, and SPG5 are the most common subtypes in European populations [6], SPG11, SPG35, and SPG5 predominate in Chinese cohorts [7, 8]. COQ4-related disorders represent a rare but increasingly recognized cause of complicated HSP [9].

Historically, biallelic COQ4 variants were primarily associated with severe, lethal infantile encephalomyopathy. However, the phenotypic spectrum was now understood to be much broader, ranging from early-onset lethal disease to adult-onset ataxia-spasticity disorders. A seminal study identified COQ4 mutations as the cause of Spastic Ataxia Type 10 (SPAX10), characterized by the triad of cerebellar ataxia, spasticity, and cognitive decline [9]. Yet, emerging evidence suggested that COQ4 deficiency could present predominantly as hereditary spastic paraplegia with minimal cerebellar involvement [5]. In our case, the patient exhibited severe progressive spasticity, hyperreflexia, and prominent pyramidal signs, contrasted by only mild dysmetria and an absence of significant gait ataxia or cognitive impairment. Given this distinct predominance of pyramidal tract dysfunction over cerebellar features, we classified this case as complicated HSP rather than classic SPAX10. This classification aligned with recent findings by Wei et al.[5], who reported similar HSP-predominant phenotypes associated with COQ4 variants. Furthermore, Cordts et al. highlighted the existence of an "ataxia-spasticity spectrum," noting that phenotypic expression could vary significantly even among patients with similar genetic backgrounds [9].

At the molecular level, the COQ4 protein lacked enzymatic activity itself but serves as a critical organizer (scaffold) for the CoQ biosynthetic complex (synthome) within the inner mitochondrial membrane [10, 11]. Casarin et al. demonstrated that human COQ4 is essential for the stability and assembly of this complex; its absence leads to secondary CoQ10 depletion and subsequent failure of oxidative phosphorylation [10]. Similarly, Marbois et al. reported that without the COQ4 polypeptide acting as a scaffold, the biosynthetic enzymes cannot function efficiently [11]. We hypothesized that the compound heterozygous variants identified in our patient—c.433C > T (p.Arg145Cys) and the novel c.719G > T (p.Arg240Leu)—were hypomorphic alleles. Both affect conserved arginine residues, likely disrupting the structural integrity of the COQ4 scaffold without completely abolishing it. This partial loss of function might allow for residual complex assembly, thereby preventing the lethal infantile phenotype seen in null mutations [12], but resulting in insufficient CoQ10 levels to sustain high-energy neuronal tissues during childhood.

Clinically, this metabolic insufficiency manifested as progressive gait instability and spasticity. Notably, our patient reported exercise-induced calf pain and soreness, an under-reported feature that may serve as a specific clinical clue for mitochondrial dysfunction in muscle metabolism. Regrettably, direct assays for CoQ10 levels in muscle or fibroblasts were not performed, which represented a limitation of this study. Nevertheless, the clinical presentation strongly supports a diagnosis of mitochondrial-related HSP.

Regarding management, two interventions were utilized: Baclofen: As a first-line symptomatic treatment, baclofen acts as a selective GABA-B receptor agonist, inhibiting excitatory neurotransmitter release and dampening spinal reflex hyperexcitability [13]. While effective for symptom management in some cases, it does not alter the underlying neurodegenerative process. Coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10): Supplementation is currently the only potential disease-modifying therapy. CoQ10 functions as an essential electron carrier in the respiratory chain and a potent lipid-soluble antioxidant [14]. Recent evidence, including the comprehensive review by Xie et al. [13], highlights that high-dose CoQ10 supplementation can significantly stabilize or even improve neurological outcomes in patients with pathogenic COQ4 variants. Specifically, early initiation of therapy is associated with better preservation of motor function and cognitive development, potentially halting the progression of spasticity and ataxia. In COQ4 deficiency, high-dose oral CoQ10 aims to bypass the biosynthetic bottleneck, restore ATP production, and mitigate oxidative damage [13].

Crucially, our patient did not exhibit immediate motor improvement after 4 months of combined therapy. This lack of response highlights a critical aspect of treating mitochondrial disorders: Therapeutic efficacy appears to be highly time-dependent. Literature suggests that early initiation, prior to irreversible neuronal loss, is crucial for stabilizing or improving neurological function [12, 13]. The absence of improvement in our case may be attributed to the delayed diagnosis (> 1 year post-symptom onset) or the potential need for higher dosages to effectively penetrate the central nervous system. In addition, the emergence of psychosocial symptoms (low mood, school avoidance) during follow-up underscores the need for holistic management beyond motor symptoms.

We acknowledge several limitations in this study. First, we did not perform functional validation (e.g., enzymatic assays or cell models) to confirm the pathogenicity of the novel c.719G > T variant. However, the variant's rarity, its location in a conserved domain, and the patient's compatible clinical phenotype strongly support its pathogenicity, consistent with ACMG guidelines. Second, long-term follow-up data regarding the patient's response to CoQ10 supplementation are currently unavailable. Despite these limitations, this case provides the first genetic evidence linking the c.719G > T mutation to COQ4-related HSP, expanding the mutational spectrum of this rare disorder. More importantly, it highlights the critical need for early genetic diagnosis to enable timely therapeutic intervention, even in the absence of complete functional data or long-term outcomes.

Conclusion

Our case expanded the genotypic and phenotypic spectrum of complicated HSP caused by COQ4 compound heterozygous mutations. It highlighted that COQ4-related disorders may present with progressive spasticity, tremor, and distinct exercise-induced myalgia, even in the absence of prominent ataxia. This case underscored the critical value of WES in diagnosing atypical pediatric spastic paraplegias and emphasizes the importance of early genetic diagnosis to facilitate timely initiation of CoQ10 therapy before irreversible neurological damage occurs.
