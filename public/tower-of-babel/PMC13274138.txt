Muribaculum as a microbial contributor of rifaximin-induced mucosal protection during chemotherapy
Authors: Carmen Correale, Martina Morandi, Antonio Gil-Gomez, Alessandra Silvestri, Andrea Gatti, Daniele Braga, Annalisa Maroli, Caterina Foppa, Stefano De Zanet, Francesca Algieri, Michela Lizier, Riccardo Rossetti, Sara Carloni, Giuseppe Penna, Antonino Spinelli, Paola Brescia, Maria Rescigno
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTChemotherapy-induced intestinal mucositis is a frequent and dose-limiting toxicity that compromises cancer treatment outcomes and lacks effective targeted interventions. Here, we investigate the mechanisms by which the nonabsorbable antibiotic rifaximin mitigates chemotherapy-induced intestinal injury, focusing on microbiota-mediated preservation of epithelial barrier integrity. In a murine model of 5-fluorouracil (5-FU)-induced intestinal injury, rifaximin pretreatment reduced mucosal inflammation and tissue damage, preserved epithelial and mucus barrier integrity, and limited systemic endotoxemia. Importantly, rifaximin did not impair the antitumor efficacy of 5-FU in ApcMin/+C3arKO mice. To assess translational relevance, we employed a human intestinal ex vivo organ culture system (EVOC) and found that rifaximin preserved mucosal architecture, mucus balance, and tight junction integrity following inflammatory challenge. Microbiome profiling revealed that rifaximin reshaped the intestinal microbial community, preventing the depletion of health-associated taxa, including Muribaculum and Parasutterella. Functional experiments demonstrated that Muribaculum intestinale supplementation alone attenuated 5-FU–induced injury, reproducing key protective features of rifaximin treatment. Together, these findings identify Muribaculum as a microbial contributor to rifaximin's protective effects, supporting its potential role as a safe adjunctive strategy to improve gastrointestinal tolerability of cancer treatment.

Introduction

Gastrointestinal toxicity remains a major limitation in cancer chemotherapy, with intestinal mucositis representing one of the most common and debilitating complications. 1 Clinically, intestinal mucositis manifests with symptoms as abdominal pain, diarrhea, malabsorption and increased risk of infection, which collectively impair quality of life and may necessitate treatment interruptions or dose reductions, compromising oncological outcomes. 2 , 3

The gastrointestinal tract is particularly vulnerable to the toxic effects of chemotherapy, as many anticancer drugs target rapidly dividing cells like intestinal epithelial cells. These cells are essential for maintaining the gut barrier and interact closely with the gut microbiota to support immune and metabolic functions. Chemotherapy disrupts this balance, causing damage to the epithelial lining, increased permeability, immune cell infiltration, and significant shifts in the microbial community. 4

Among chemotherapeutic agents, 5-fluorouracil (5-FU) is widely employed for the treatment of a variety of solid tumors, including breast cancer, gastric cancer and especially colorectal cancer, 5 but it is also notably associated with mucosal damage in both small and large intestine. 6-8 Its mechanism of action, primarily the inhibition of thymidylate synthase and subsequent disruption of DNA and RNA synthesis, also triggers apoptosis in rapidly dividing intestinal epithelial cells. This epithelial injury contributes to a breakdown of the intestinal barrier, which is critical in maintaining host–microbiota homeostasis and preventing luminal antigens and pathogens from translocating into systemic circulation. 9 This toxicity manifests as villus atrophy, crypt loss, epithelial barrier dysfunction, and gut microbial dysbiosis, collectively contributing to mucosal injury and inflammation. 10 , 11

The intestinal barrier comprises multiple integrated components. The mucus layer, primarily composed of mucins like MUC2, provides the first line of defense, acting as a physical and biochemical barrier to microbial contact with the epithelium. 12 Beneath this, tight junctions (TJs) between epithelial cells, involving proteins such as Zonula Occludens-1 (ZO-1), regulate paracellular permeability and maintain barrier integrity. Disruption of either of these layers by chemotherapy can result in increased intestinal permeability, systemic dissemination of bacterial products such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), and the activation of inflammatory cascades. 9

Recent advances highlight the gut microbiota as a critical modulator of the efficacy of anticancer therapies and their toxic side effects, particularly those associated with chemotherapy-induced intestinal damage. 13 The administration of chemotherapeutic agents results in a reduction of microbial diversity and a decline in the microbial populations that confer protective benefits, support barrier integrity, modulate immune responses, and produce short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) which provide nourishment to epithelial cells, concurrently altering the equilibrium towards potentially pathogenic taxa. 14 These alterations can compromise barrier repair mechanisms, exacerbate inflammatory responses, and prolong mucosal damage. 15

Despite its prevalence and impact on patient outcomes, therapeutic strategies for preventing or mitigating intestinal mucositis remain limited. 16 , 17

Recent insights into the gut microbiota's role in modulating both chemotherapy efficacy and mucosal health have suggested that interventions capable of preserving or restoring a healthy microbiota may offer therapeutic benefit and prompted investigations into microbiome-targeted therapies as adjunctive strategies in oncology. Prophylactic interventions aimed at mitigating intestinal mucositis include the use of antibiotics and probiotics, which are intended to enhance gastrointestinal functionality, protect the intestinal epithelium, manage inflammatory responses, and promote overall gut health. 18 , 19 Preclinical studies have demonstrated that probiotics, particularly strains such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, can effectively alleviate symptoms associated with chemotherapeutic agents, including 5-FU. 20 Additionally, evidence suggests that antibiotic administration may diminish the severity of intestinal mucositis and associated inflammation. 19 However, conventional antibiotics can profoundly disrupt the gut microbiota, leading to dysbiosis with potential long-term consequences, while certain probiotics may pose risks of systemic translocation when the intestinal barrier is compromised by chemotherapy.

In this context, there is growing interest in microbiota-targeted, non-systemic therapies to reduce mucosal damage. Some poorly absorbable antibiotics, for instance, do not affect the balance between symbionts and pathobionts and are particularly attractive for managing chemotherapy-induced intestinal mucositis, as their activity is largely restricted to the intestinal lumen, minimizing systemic side effects and reducing the risk of drug–drug interactions. Among these, rifaximin has garnered attention due to its dual capacity to modulate gut microbiota and exert local anti-inflammatory effects without systemic absorption. Moreover, rifaximin can positively modulate the gut microbiota favoring the growth of beneficial bacterial species, 21 , 22 making the intestinal mucosa resistant to bacterial adherence and limiting translocation or migration of intestinal bacteria. 23 Clinically, rifaximin has been approved for the treatment of various gastrointestinal disorders, including small intestinal bacterial overgrowth (SIBO), irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), and diverticular disease (DD). It is also considered an effective option for managing Clostridium difficile infection (CDI). 24 , 25 Moreover, rifaximin has shown benefits in treating conditions associated with the gut‒liver‒brain axis, such as hepatic encephalopathy (HE) in patients with advanced liver disease, 26 , 27 as well as alcohol-related liver disease. 28

However, its potential to mitigate chemotherapy-induced mucosal injury, and the underlying mechanisms, remain poorly explored, with very limited available preclinical evidence. 29

Here, we investigated the impact of rifaximin on 5-FU-induced intestinal mucositis using a murine model, with focus on histological damage, gut barrier integrity, and microbial composition. Additionally, we observed that Muribaculum, a gut commensal, was enriched by rifaximin and contributed to mucosal protection. To evaluate translational relevance, we tested whether rifaximin interfered with the anticancer efficacy of 5-FU using a genetic model of colorectal cancer. Our results highlight rifaximin as a microbiota-modulating agent capable of mitigating mucosal injury without compromising chemotherapeutic efficacy, offering a potential strategy to enhance the tolerability of cancer treatment.

Methods

Animal model

C57BL/6J male, 8-week-old mice were purchased from Charles River Laboratories and maintained at Humanitas Clinical and Research Center on a 12-hour light‒dark cycle with controlled temperature and under specific pathogen-free conditions, with 3–5 animals housed per cage. ApcMin/+C3arKO mice were obtained by crossing C57BL/6J-ApcMin/+with C3arKO mice in our facility. 30 All animal experiments were performed under protocols (598/2019-PR, 1054/2015-PR, 139/2015-PR, 588/2021-PR, 757/2023-PR, 581/2025-PR) approved by the Italian Ministry of Health, and consistent with national (D.L. N. 26, G.U. March 4, 2014) and international law and policies (EEC Council Directive 2010/63/EU). Sample size was defined on the basis of past experience with the models, to detect differences of 20% or greater between the groups (10% significance level and 80% power). For ethical reasons, the minimum number of animals necessary to achieve the scientific objectives was used. Clinical signs and histological scoring of mucosal injury were defined as the primary outcome measure and used to estimate sample size.

Mice were fed with either a rifaximin (provided by Alfasigma S.p.A.) supplemented diet (100 mg/kg/mouse) ad libitum or Muribaculum intestinale (2 × 109 CFUs/day) by oral gavage for two weeks and then treated intraperitoneally (i.p.) with 5-FU for 4 days. Control animals received an equivalent volume of sterile saline. Animals received a dose of 300 mg/kg 5-FU on day 1 and then 50 mg/kg on the 3 following days; saline solution was injected as vehicle in control groups.

Mice were randomly allocated to cages to minimize cage effects across experimental groups. All groups were handled according to the same standardized experimental conditions to ensure environmental consistency across treatments. Blinding was not implemented during the group assignment. Outcome evaluation was performed in a blinded manner for histopathological scoring, as previously described. 31 Other endpoints, including ELISA, permeability assays, and sequencing analyzes, are based on objective quantitative measurements and were therefore not blinded.

Muribaculum intestinale YL27 (DSM 28989) was obtained from DSMZ (Leibniz Institute DSMZ, Germany). Bacteria were cultured under strictly anaerobic conditions at 37 °C in liquid Anaerobe Chopped Meat medium (Anaerobe Systems) using an anaerobic workstation (Baker Ruskinn). Prior to administration, bacterial cultures were centrifuged, washed twice in sterile saline, and resuspended in sterile saline solution to eliminate residual culture medium before oral gavage. Diarrhea severity was assessed by using a score for each mouse using the following scale: 0-normal (normal stool), 1-minimal (soft stool), 2-slight (slightly wet and soft stool), 3-moderate (wet and unformed stool with moderate perianal staining of the coat), 4-severe (watery stool with severe perianal staining of the coat). Fecal occult blood was assessed with Hemoccult SENSA slides. Both assessments were performed based on stool availability at the time of evaluation; animals without evaluable stool samples were not included in these analyzes.

Histopathological assessment

Intestinal specimens were fixed overnight either in 10% formaldehyde or Periodate-Lysine-Paraformaldehyde (PLP) buffer overnight. After processing and paraffin embedding, 3 μm sections were prepared and stained with hematoxylin and eosin for morphological evaluation. Tissue injury was scored under blinded conditions using established criteria that included assessment of inflammatory infiltrates, villus/crypt integrity, edema, ulceration, hemorrhage, and hyperemia. Quantitative measurements of small intestinal villus height and colonic crypt depth were obtained using Fiji (ImageJ). Mucin content and apoptosis were evaluated on FFPE sections using Alcian Blue/Periodic Acid–Schiff staining (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and TUNEL assay (Abcam), respectively, following the manufacturers' protocols.

Cell proliferation was examined by Ki67 immunohistochemistry on 3 μm FFPE sections. Following deparaffinization and rehydration, heat-induced epitope retrieval was carried out in 10 mM citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was suppressed with 0.3% hydrogen peroxide. Sections were incubated with Rodent Block M (Biocare Medical) for 30 minutes prior to exposure to rabbit monoclonal anti-Ki67 antibody (D3B5, Cell Signaling Technology) for 1 hour at room temperature. Detection was achieved using MACH 1 Universal Polymer Detection (Biocare Medical), followed by DAB chromogen development and hematoxylin counterstaining. Slides were dehydrated through graded ethanol and mounted with Eukitt.

Bright-field images were captured using an Olympus VS120 digital slide scanner with 10 × or 20 × objectives. Quantitative analyzes were performed using QuPath software (https://qupath.github.io).

For immunofluorescence analyzes, OCT-embedded intestinal samples fixed in PLP were cryosectioned (10 μm thickness). After rehydration, sections were permeabilized and blocked in Tris-HCl buffer (0.1 M, pH 7.4) containing 2% FBS and 0.3% Triton X-100. Tight junction integrity was assessed using anti-ZO-1 antibody (clone ZO1-1A12, Invitrogen). To exclude endothelial-associated ZO-1 signal from analysis, CD34 (clone RAM34, eBioscience) staining was used to generate a digital exclusion mask during image quantification in Fiji. MUC-2 was detected by immunofluorescence on 10 μm cryosections. Sections were rehydrated in PBS containing 0.05% Tween-20, subjected to antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) for 20 minutes, and incubated with anti-MUC-2 antibody (EPR23479-47, Abcam). Immune cell infiltrates were characterized using antibodies against CD45 (eBioscience), Ly6G (BD Biosciences), F4/80 (Thermo Fisher Scientific), CD3 (Invitrogen), and B220 (eBioscience). Appropriate fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied, and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Images were acquired using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope equipped with a 40 × oil-immersion objective, and quantitative analysis was conducted with Fiji (ImageJ). For histological and immunostaining analyzes, only samples meeting predefined quality criteria (e.g., adequate tissue integrity and staining quality) were included in quantitative evaluations.

FITC-dextran permeability assay

Intestinal barrier function was evaluated using a fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)–dextran permeability test. Mice were fasted for 4 hours prior to administration of 4 kDa FITC–dextran (TdB Labs) by oral gavage at a dose of 400 mg/kg body weight. Four hours following gavage, peripheral blood samples were obtained from the tail vein. Plasma fluorescence was measured using a microplate reader (Clariostar Plus, BMG Labtech), and FITC–dextran concentrations were determined by interpolation from a standard calibration curve. Only samples with reliable fluorescence measurements were included in the analysis; samples affected by technical variability (e.g., inconsistent gavage or suboptimal sample quality) were excluded.

Biochemical analysis in serum or fecal samples

Endotoxin lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and LPS binding protein (LBP) were assessed in serum samples obtained after centrifugation of blood collected via cardiac puncture in vacutainer clot activator tube immediately after euthanized the mice with CO2. LPS was measured with Pierce Chromogenic Endotoxin Quant Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and LBP by ELISA (Abcam) following manufacturers' instructions.

Lipocalin-2 and Calprotectin were assessed in fecal pellets mechanically crashed in PBS buffer with 0.5% Tween. Lipocalin-2 and Calprotectin ELISA (R&D Systems) were performed following manufacturers' instructions.

In vitro IC₅₀ determination

Primary tumor cells were isolated from colonic tumors of ApcMin/+C3arKO mice and cultured under standard conditions. Cells were treated in vitro with increasing doses of 5-FU (10−10 to 10−4 M) in the presence of increasing doses of rifaximin (6.25 to 50 µM) for 48 hours. Cell viability was assessed using the MTT assay, and IC₅₀ values for 5-FU were calculated by nonlinear regression analysis.

Ex vivo human colon mucosa explants (EVOC)

Ex vivo colon mucosa explants were collected from colorectal cancer patients from Humanitas Research Hospital (Rozzano, Milano, Italy). Patients included in the study did not receive neoadjuvant treatment, but they undergone surgery. Colon mucosa resection was performed in the healthy margin of the tumor after surgery. Colon mucosa was washed in HBSS (Hanks' Balanced Salt Solution) 1X without Calcium and Magnesium with 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin and 100 μg/mL Gentamycin and transported to the laboratory. The specimen was transferred in DMEM (Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium) without serum, with 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin and 100 μg/mL Gentamycin pre-warmed at 37 °C and incubated for 5 minutes at 37 °C. The specimen was plated with the luminal side facing upwards into a clean petri dish and cleaned of any residual submucosa parts using a sterile scalpel. The mucosa was cut in stripes at least 1 cm wide and as long as possible and a plastic cylinder was placed (adding surgical glue) on the apical side of the mucosa, as close to one of the borders of the strip as possible. After filling 60 × 15 mm IVF One Well Dish (Falcon # 353653; Colorado Springs, Colorado) with DMEM supplemented with Glutamine (2 mM), 15% FBS-Na (fetal bovine serum - North American), 1% ITSX (insulin-transferrin-selenium-ethanolamine) and 200 ng/ml EGF (epidermal growth factor) at the time of use, the specimen with the cylinder was plated on top of a metal grid resting in the center of IVF One Well Dish. The plate was transferred in the incubator with 5% CO2 at 37 °C for 1 hour to allow mucus reconstitution. After this period, 30 μL of the stimulus (LPS, 400 μg/mL) and either Rifaximin (100 μM) or its vehicle (DMSO) were added to the cylinder, and the specimen was incubated in an oxygen-filled jar at 1 atm pressure in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂ at 37 °C. After a total culture time of 4 hours, the cylinder was carefully removed from the tissue using a scalpel, and the tissue was fixed for histological analysis. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin and Alcian/PAS, or processed for immunofluorescence staining of MUC2 and ZO-1.

Gut microbiota analysis

To characterize gut microbial composition, we performed 16S rRNA gene sequencing on fecal samples. The number of biological replicates varied slightly between experiments; exact sample sizes for each analysis are indicated in the corresponding figure legends.

DNA extraction

Genomic DNA was isolated from fecal samples using the DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer's instructions. Frozen pellets (−80 °C) were suspended in the provided lysis buffer and subjected to mechanical disruption with 0.1 mm zirconia/silica beads (BioSpec) using a FastPrep-24 instrument (MP Biomedicals) to ensure efficient bacterial cell breakage.

Following bead-beating, debris was pelleted, and the clarified lysate was processed through the silica membrane–based spin columns supplied in the kit. After sequential washing steps to remove contaminants and inhibitors, microbial DNA was eluted in the kit's elution buffer and stored at −80 °C until further analysis.

DNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

The quantity and integrity of DNA were checked through TapeStation (Agilent Technologies) and sample library preparation for Next Generation. Sequencing was done using the QIAseq 16S/ITS Region Panels kit (Qiagen), targeting the V3V4 hypervariable regions of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene and the fungal Internal Transcribed Spacer (ITS) region. The V3–V4 hypervariable region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using the primer pair 341F (5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′) and 806 R (5′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3′), each containing a sample-specific barcode. PCR reactions were performed in a total volume of 15 µL containing 0.2 µM of each primer, approximately 10 ng of template DNA, and Phusion® High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix (New England Biolabs). Amplicons of the expected size were verified by 2% agarose gel electrophoresis. Equimolar amounts of PCR products from each sample were pooled, end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated with Illumina sequencing adapters. Libraries were checked through TapeStation 4200 (Agilent Technologies), quantified by using MicroPlate Reader GloMax (Promega), and sequenced on an Illumina paired-end platform to generate 250 bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing reads were processed in the following bioinformatics pipeline. Raw reads were subjected to adapter trimming and quality control using Trimmomatic (v0.39; parameters: ILLUMINACLIP:TruSeq_PE.fa:2:30:7 MINLEN:250 AVGQUAL:30) or alternatively Fastp (v0.23.4) with a minimum mean quality score of Q30. Residual primer sequences and low-quality bases were further removed with Cutadapt (v3.7), and reads containing more than two ambiguous nucleotides were discarded.

To exclude potential host-derived contamination, filtered reads were mapped to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38) using Bowtie2 (v2.2.5), and aligned sequences were eliminated prior to downstream analysis.

Microbial community analyses were carried out using QIIME2. A Naïve Bayes classifier was trained on the SILVA 138 reference database (99% OTUs), restricted to the V3–V4 region. Amplicon sequence variants (ASVs) were inferred from quality-filtered paired-end reads using the DADA2 algorithm. Read pairs were merged during the denoising pipeline. The resulting ASVs had an average length of 409 bp (median: 405 bp). Prior to ecological analyzes, ASVs assigned to the family Muribaculaceae using a naïve Bayes classifier were further evaluated to improve taxonomic resolution within this family by sequence alignment against the SILVA database using a BLAST-based approach. Taxonomic assignment was assessed using a lowest common ancestor (LCA) method. However, this procedure did not yield improved taxonomic resolution, reflecting the limited discriminatory power of the V3–V4 region and the incomplete representation of this family in reference databases. Accordingly, some sequences were retained at the family-level classification. For ecological analyses, samples were rarefied to different sequencing depths for each experimental run (28,000 and 40,131 reads per sample, respectively). Alpha- and beta-diversity metrics were then computed using the core-metrics-phylogenetic pipeline in QIIME 2 (v2024.5). Rarefaction depths were selected to retain the majority of samples while ensuring adequate sequencing coverage. 32

Alpha diversity comparisons among groups were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn's post hoc multiple-comparison test. Beta diversity was calculated using Bray–Curtis dissimilarity and visualized through principal coordinates analysis (PCoA). Group-wise differences in community structure were statistically tested using PERMANOVA (999 permutations) and PERMDISP implemented in the QIIME2 beta-group-significance function. Differential abundance analysis was performed on count data at the genus level using the DESeq2 package in R. Statistical significance was assessed using Wald statistical tests and multiple testing correction was applied; taxa with adjusted FDR values below 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Spearman correlation of microbes with colon length, villus and crypt length, MUC2, ZO1, and Ki67 was evaluated using the corr.test function in the psych (v2.2.5) R package for each pairwise combination of microbial genus abundances and biological measurements. Significance of the correlation (FDR) and correlation coefficients were represented in a bubble plot.

Predictive functional profiling was performed on ASV count data using PICRUSt2 (v2.5.3) with the default evolutionary placement algorithm pipeline, generating predicted MetaCyc pathway abundances. Differential abundance of predicted functional features between comparison groups was assessed using edgeR (v4.0.16) and DESeq2 (v1.42.1) independently, and only features reaching statistical significance in both methods (FDR < 0.05) were considered as robustly differentially abundant.

Statistical analysis

All statistical computations were conducted using GraphPad Prism, QIIME2 (PERMANOVA and PERMDISP), and R (DESeq2 package) as appropriate. Differences between groups were assessed using parametric or nonparametric tests based on data distribution, applying two-way ANOVA with Tukey's post hoc test or Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn's multiple-comparison test, as appropriate. Extreme values were screened using Grubbs' test (α = 0.05) and excluded when justified. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) from at least three independent experiments or biological replicates, with the sample size reported in the corresponding graph or figure legend. Statistical significance was defined as follows: *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Rifaximin protects against intestinal damage induced by chemotherapy

To assess the protective potential of rifaximin, we induced intestinal damage in mice using 5-FU and monitored outcomes following rifaximin administration, which began prior to and continued throughout chemotherapy (Figure 1A). The selection of 5-FU doses and treatment schedule was determined by evaluating their safety and efficacy in reducing body weight or causing temporary intestinal damage, aiming to replicate the pathological conditions observed in cancer patients undergoing chemotherapy. Mice were euthanized either 24 hours (day 4, Figure 1A) or 96 hours (day 7, Figure S1A) after the cessation of 5-FU treatment. By day 7, the intestinal damage, characterized by diarrhea, the presence of occult blood in the feces, and villus/crypt morphology, had fully recovered (Figure S1). Therefore, our focus was centered on the acute effects of 5-FU on intestinal damage, which were most evident at day 4.

Rifaximin's protective effects were assessed through symptom monitoring and histological evaluations, aiming to provide insights into its potential role in mitigating chemotherapy-induced intestinal mucositis.

Mice treated with 5-FU showed a decrease in body weight at day 4, after which they started to recover. Rifaximin was not able to prevent body weight loss (Figure S1B) but reduced the frequency of diarrhea (Figure 1B) and the detection of occult blood in the feces (Figure 1C). A notable decrease in the length of the small intestine and colon was observed following 5-FU treatment, which was mitigated by rifaximin treatment (Figure 1D−E).

Morphological assessments were performed in both the small intestine and colon. Mice treated with 5-FU showed atrophy of villi in the small intestine and crypts in the colon, while rifaximin improved both (Figure 1F−G). Immunohistochemical analysis of Ki67 showed a marked reduction in crypt cell proliferation following 5-FU treatment. Rifaximin protected from loss of proliferative activity, particularly in the small intestine, suggesting a protective effect on epithelial regeneration. (Figures 1H−I).

5-FU induces cytotoxicity primarily by inhibiting thymidylate synthase, disrupting DNA and RNA synthesis, and leading to apoptosis. 33 Using TUNEL assay, we observed a significant increase in apoptotic cells in the small intestine following 5-FU treatment. Rifaximin co-treatment showed a trend towards a reduction of apoptotic cells, suggesting a protective effect against chemotherapy-induced epithelial cell death (Figure 1J–K).

These findings suggest that rifaximin has a protective role in mitigating intestinal damage and enhancing cellular proliferation in the context of 5-FU-induced mucositis.

Rifaximin attenuates 5-FU–induced intestinal inflammation

Given the strong association of mucositis with inflammatory responses, we investigated whether rifaximin could modulate immune cell infiltration and inflammatory markers triggered by 5-FU. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained small intestinal and colon sections were evaluated using established scoring criteria, assessing inflammatory infiltrates, villus/crypt integrity, edema, ulceration, hemorrhage, and hyperemia. 34 , 35 5-FU treatment significantly increased histopathological damage, while rifaximin markedly reduced these alterations (Figure 2A−B), reinforcing its relevance as a supportive agent in chemotherapy. Immunofluorescence analysis further revealed that 5-FU induced an increase in CD45-positive immune cells in the intestine, along with increased infiltration of F4/80-positive macrophages and Ly6G-positive neutrophils. At the same time, the levels of CD3-positive T cells and B220-positive B cells remained unchanged (Figure 2C−D). Rifaximin attenuated innate immune cell recruitment, particularly macrophages and neutrophils, likely contributing to attenuation of inflammation and mucosal damage induced by 5-FU. Consistent with these findings, the fecal inflammatory markers lipocalin-2 and calprotectin, 36-38 both released predominantly by activated neutrophils and monocytes, were strongly elevated after 5-FU and significantly reduced by rifaximin (Figure 2E−F). Since calprotectin is also widely used as a clinical biomarker of intestinal barrier dysfunction, these results suggest that rifaximin not only limits mucosal inflammation but may also contribute to preserving barrier integrity.

Rifaximin protects intestinal barrier integrity upon 5-FU treatment

Chemotherapy is known to markedly impair the intestinal barrier in both mouse models and humans. Therefore, we investigated whether rifaximin could preserve mucus layer integrity and epithelial barrier function following 5-FU–induced damage. Mice treated with 5-FU showed an alteration in the balance between neutral and acid mucins, which is critical for maintaining the protective mucus layer of the gut barrier. However, following rifaximin treatment, we observed a restoration of this balance, as evidenced by the Alcian Blue/PAS staining patterns (Figure 3A). Furthermore, the analysis of MUC2 expression, the predominant mucin in the mouse colon, showed that rifaximin effectively preserved MUC2 levels in the context of 5-FU treatment, suggesting a protective role in maintaining mucosal integrity (Figure 3B−C).

To evaluate epithelial barrier integrity, intestinal sections were stained for ZO-1, a key tight junction protein and established marker of barrier function. 5-FU treatment significantly reduced ZO-1 expression in both the small intestine and colon, whereas rifaximin administration restored its expression to levels comparable to those of untreated controls (Figure 3D−E).

Intestinal permeability was assessed by orally administering 4-kDa FITC-dextran and measuring its passage into the bloodstream. Mice treated with 5-FU exhibited significantly elevated plasma dextran levels, indicating a compromised intestinal barrier. Conversely, rifaximin-treated mice showed significantly lower dextran concentrations in the circulation, suggesting that rifaximin helps preserve intestinal barrier integrity (Figure 3F).

Plasma levels of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and LPS-binding protein (LBP) are key markers of intestinal barrier dysfunction. LPS, from Gram-negative bacteria, triggers immune responses when it leaks into the bloodstream due to barrier damage. Elevated LBP reflects the body's response to this leakage. In mice, 5-FU treatment significantly increased serum LPS and LBP, while rifaximin reduced their levels (Figure 3G-H), suggesting it helps restore barrier integrity and potentially reduce systemic inflammation.

Collectively, these findings indicate that rifaximin not only alleviates the intestinal damage induced by 5-FU but also plays a pivotal role in reinstating barrier function and mucosal integrity.

Rifaximin treatment does not interfere with 5-FU chemotherapeutic activity on colorectal cancer

5-FU efficacy in colorectal cancer may be modulated by gut bacteria that metabolize or modify the drug. 39 , 40

To investigate whether rifaximin impacts on the activity of 5-FU, we utilized a genetic mouse model that spontaneously develops colon tumors. Specifically, we crossed ApcMin/+ mice, known for their intestinal tumor development, with mice carrying a knockout mutation in the C3aR (complement anaphylatoxin C3a receptor) gene, resulting in ApcMin/+C3arKO mice. 30 These mice exhibited a shift in spontaneous tumor growth from the small intestine to the colon, developing approximately eight tumors in the colon by fourteen weeks of age.

We treated ApcMin/+C3arKO mice with a rifaximin-supplemented diet for two weeks prior to administering 5-FU and analyzed tumor growth three days post-treatment (Figure 4A). The total count of tumors in the colon was significantly reduced in mice treated with 5-FU, whether used alone or in conjunction with rifaximin, when compared to untreated mice. Interestingly, rifaximin treatment on its own also showed a potential decrease in the total tumor count (Figure 4B). Detailed analysis revealed no significant changes in the number of small tumors (<2 mm) regardless of treatment; however, both 5-FU alone and in combination with rifaximin significantly decreased the number of larger tumors (>2 mm) (Figure 4C), indicating no detrimental effect of rifaximin on 5-FU antitumoral activity. Rifaximin alone did not lead to a significant reduction in large tumor number, and histological examination of colon tissue indicated no effect on tumor size (Figure 4D). Additionally, primary cancer cell cultures from ApcMin/+C3arKO tumors treated in vitro with 5-FU and increasing doses of rifaximin showed no alterations in the 5-FU IC50 (Figure 4E), suggesting no direct effect of rifaximin on tumor cell growth.

Overall, these findings suggest that rifaximin does not interfere with the efficacy of 5-FU on tumor cells, but it could affect tumor cell growth through changes in gut microbiota, warranting further investigation.

Rifaximin treatment protects ex vivo human colon mucosa explants

The protective effects of rifaximin on human intestinal tissue were investigated using an ex vivo organ culture (EVOC) system, which allows for directional stimulation (from luminal to basolateral side) of human intestinal mucosa explants. 41 , 42

Treating human explants with 5-FU posed significant challenges due to their restricted culture duration, which limited our ability to observe measurable damage indicative of treatment efficacy. To address this limitation, we opted for a more immediate and potent mucosal insult by utilizing bacterial LPS. This approach allowed us to effectively simulate mucosal damage, thereby facilitating the evaluation of rifaximin's protective effects on intestinal integrity. Indeed, the LPS has a well-documented role in eliciting inflammatory responses that compromise mucosal barriers. We have shown that increased serum LPS levels, a marker of barrier dysfunction, after 5-FU treatment were reduced by rifaximin, suggesting a protective effect on the intestinal barrier and a role in maintaining mucosal homeostasis (Figure 2G−H).

Thus, human colonic mucosa was exposed to LPS and rifaximin for 4 hours, with the luminal side facing upwards (Figure 5A). Rifaximin maintained tissue integrity disrupted by LPS and preserved the balance of the mucus layer (Figure 5B). Additionally, rifaximin restored the LPS-induced decrease in MUC2 expression (Figure 5C−D) and the tight junction protein ZO-1 (Figure 5E and F), demonstrating its capability to support intestinal barrier integrity also in human tissue. These findings suggest that rifaximin may serve as a protective agent for human intestinal mucosa against external damage, indicating its potential application in clinical settings for patients undergoing chemotherapy. Its ability to enhance mucosal integrity and modulate inflammatory responses highlights its therapeutic potential in maintaining gut health.

Rifaximin modifies gut microbiota composition and partially prevents 5-FU-induced dysbiosis

Given rifaximin's known influence on gut microbiota, we investigated whether its protective effects against 5-FU-induced intestinal damage were mediated through microbiota modulation. To this end, we performed 16S rRNA gene sequencing to characterize fecal microbiota dynamics during treatment.

As expected, rifaximin reduced total bacterial mass per gram of feces, with a more significant effect when combined with 5-FU (Figure S2A). Alpha-diversity analysis revealed that rifaximin was associated with a decrease in bacterial richness (Chao1) and diversity (Shannon and Simpson indices), particularly in combination with 5-FU (Figure 6A, S2B). In contrast, 5-FU alone did not show significant differences in alpha-diversity. These findings indicate that rifaximin, particularly in combination with 5-FU, alters the gut microbiota composition.

Principal-coordinate analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis dissimilarity was used to visualize differences in microbial community structure across treatment groups. Statistical testing confirmed significant differences in community composition across all pairwise comparisons (PERMANOVA, q < 0.01 for all pairwise comparisons; Table S1). 5-FU treatment induced a marked compositional shift relative to untreated controls, without a concomitant increase in community dispersion, indicating a genuine restructuring of the gut microbiota rather than increased variability. Rifaximin-treated samples displayed significantly greater community homogeneity relative to other groups, as supported by dispersion analysis. Notably, the combination of rifaximin with 5-FU resulted in a distinct compositional profile relative to 5-FU alone, consistent with a modulatory effect of rifaximin on chemotherapy-associated microbial dysbiosis (Figure 6B).

At the phylum level, 5-FU treatment led to a marked reduction in Bacteroidota, accompanied by an expansion of Firmicutes, Cyanobacteria, Proteobacteria, and Verrucomicrobiota. Notably, rifaximin co-treatment attenuated most of these alterations. Interestingly, rifaximin alone induced a pronounced expansion of Bacteroidota, largely at the expense of Firmicutes, a shift that persisted to some extent also after the addition of 5-FU (Figure 6C). At the genus level, rifaximin treatment appeared to promote the abundance of potentially beneficial bacteria, partially mitigating microbiota alterations induced by 5-FU. Specifically, 5-FU reduced the abundance of several genera, including Bifidobacterium, Lactobacillus, and Faecalibaculum, as well as members of the Muribaculaceae family. Notably, a subset of sequences could only be classified at the family level (Muribaculaceae) due to the limited taxonomic resolution of the 16S rRNA V3–V4 region. Rifaximin co-treatment was associated with partial preservation of Faecalibaculum and members of the Muribaculaceae family (Figure S2C). Contrariwise, we observed a rise of Lachnospiraceace in the 5-FU treated group which was unaltered by rifaximin.

Quantitative analysis at the genus level identified significant changes in Parasutterella and Muribaculum, together with broader alterations in the Muribaculaceae family, following 5-FU treatment; these changes were partially preserved by rifaximin (Figure 6D). Heatmap and volcano plot analyses further showed that rifaximin modulated the microbiota composition toward a control-like profile (Figure 6D−E). Specifically, 5-FU decreased beneficial taxa such as Muribaculum, Parasutterella, Alloprevotella, and unclassified members of the Muribaculacae family, while increasing taxa that have been associated with altered microbial composition in inflammatory or dysbiotic conditions, such as Bacteroides, Ruminococcaceae, and Lachnospiraceae, consistent with previous reports. 43 , 44 Rifaximin induced a shift of the gut microbial community, counteracting many of these alterations without a broad taxa depletion, suggesting a protective role in maintaining microbial balance during chemotherapy (Figure 6E).

Spearman correlation of different taxa with relevant intestinal mucositis markers is shown in Figure 5F. Among the identified taxa, Muribaculum demonstrated significant positive correlations with almost all analyzed markers of rifaximin-improved intestinal structure during 5-FU mucositis (e.g., colon length, villus length, colon crypt depth, Ki67, MUC2, and ZO-1 expression), indicating a potential protective role of this microbial population in mitigating chemotherapy-induced damage.

Muribaculum intestinale supplementation mitigates chemotherapy-induced intestinal damage

One significant change in the gut microbiota composition, particularly linked to most alterations counteracted by rifaximin during intestinal mucositis, was preservation of the abundance of Muribaculum, a key anaerobic genus in the mouse gut, known for its beneficial effects on intestinal health, particularly in the context of inflammation. 45 The first and most researched species of this genus is Muribaculum intestinale, recognized as a dominant member of the mouse gut bacterial community and a key player in gut health and disease. 46 , 47 To understand whether Muribaculum represented a direct contributor of the protective effect of rifaximin, we administered Muribaculum intestinale YL27 to mice for 14 days followed by a concomitant treatment with 5-FU (Figure 7A).

Clinical signs of intestinal mucositis, including diarrhea and the presence of occult blood in feces, were significantly improved following supplementation with M. intestinale (Figure 7B and C). Additionally, key indicators of intestinal damage and inflammation, such as small intestine and colon length, were significantly enhanced in comparison to mice treated with 5-FU (Figure 7D). To further substantiate these findings, a morphological analysis of intestinal tissue revealed enhancements in villus length within the small intestine and crypt depth in the colon upon administration of M. intestinale (Figure 7E and F). These results collectively underscore the bacterium's role in promoting gut health during chemotherapy.

Muribaculum species are recognized for their ability to effectively colonize the gut mucus layer, utilizing mucins as a nutrient source, and to up-regulate the expression of thight junction proteins, supporting the maintenance of intestinal barrier structure and function. 45

Our observations confirmed that both the mucus and epithelial barriers were significantly preserved following the administration of M. intestinale, compared to solely 5-FU-treated, as supported by enhanced expression of mucins (Figure 7G-I) and improved levels of ZO-1 in both small intestine and colon (Figure 7J-K).

Microbiota sequencing of fecal samples showed that M. intestinale administration did not significantly affect overall bacterial richness or diversity compared to control groups (M.int vs NT; M.int + 5-FU vs 5-FU) (Figure S3A). However, beta-diversity analysis revealed significant differences in microbial community structure between groups (pairwise PERMANOVA; Table S2). In particular, M. intestinale supplementation induced compositional shifts relative to both NT and 5-FU-treated mice without significant changes in dispersion, indicating genuine but relatively subtle alterations in community structure. In contrast, rifaximin treatment was associated with more pronounced separation in ordination space and, in several comparisons, reduced dispersion, consistent with a broader effect on the gut microbiota and reduced community variability (Figure S3B). Taxonomic analysis at the phylum level demonstrated a similar distribution pattern between the M. intestinale-treated mice and control groups, whereas rifaximin treatment was associated with a marked increase in Bacteroidota and a decrease in Firmicutes, consistent with our previous results (Figure S3C).

Overall, the beneficial effects of M. intestinale on intestinal morphology and barrier function were evident but less pronounced than those of rifaximin, supporting a role for this bacterium in the protective effects of rifaximin. Still, rifaximin's broader modulation of the gut microbiota, including multiple components of the Muribaculacae family, may account for its greater efficacy in alleviating chemotherapy-induced intestinal mucositis.

To investigate whether microbiota remodeling was associated with functional changes relevant to host physiology, we performed PICRUSt2-based prediction of microbial metabolic pathways from 16S rRNA gene sequencing data. In 5-FU–treated mice, rifaximin administration was associated with alterations in pathways involved in carbohydrate metabolism, including glycolysis and glyoxylate-related processes, as well as sugar acid degradation pathways (e.g., glucarate and galactarate metabolism). Additional changes were observed in pathways related to fermentation, fatty acid biosynthesis, amino acid metabolism, and energy metabolism, including respiration and electron transport processes (Figure S4). These predicted functional changes indicate a broad shift in microbial metabolic potential across multiple functional categories. Supplementation with Muribaculum intestinale resulted in a more restricted functional profile, primarily affecting pathways related to carbohydrate metabolism, alcohol metabolism, fermentation, and central energy metabolism (e.g., TCA-related processes), with limited impact on lipid biosynthesis and other metabolic functions (Figure S5). These changes partially overlapped with those observed following rifaximin treatment.

Discussion

Chemotherapy-induced mucositis remains a major clinical challenge, limiting treatment intensity and compromising patient quality of life. Despite its prevalence, effective strategies to prevent or mitigate mucosal injury without interfering with antitumor efficacy are lacking. In this study, we show that rifaximin, a nonabsorbable antibiotic widely used in gastrointestinal disorders, protects against 5-FU-induced intestinal injury through preservation of epithelial barrier integrity, attenuation of inflammation, and selective remodeling of the gut microbiota. Beyond demonstrating the protective potential of microbiota-targeted interventions, our study identifies Muribaculum intestinale as a contributor to the protective effects associated with rifaximin treatment and as a key microbial modulator of host barrier resilience under cytotoxic stress.

Using a murine model of 5-FU–induced mucositis, we show that rifaximin conferred broad histological and functional protection, preserving villus and crypt architecture, reducing epithelial damage, and limiting intestinal inflammation. These effects reflect a coordinated preservation of both the mucus and epithelial barriers, which together prevented systemic translocation of bacterial products, such as LPS, thereby limiting inflammatory responses beyond the gut. These effects collectively indicate stabilization of the intestinal barrier, a key determinant of chemotherapy-induced toxicity. Importantly, rifaximin did not prevent chemotherapy-associated body weight loss, underscoring that its protective effects are not global but are specifically linked to mucosal structure and function.

A central concern for any supportive intervention in oncology is the potential to compromise anticancer efficacy. Notably, rifaximin did not compromise the antitumor efficacy of 5-FU in the ApcMin/+C3arKO mice, distinguishing it from broad-spectrum antibiotics that impair chemotherapeutic responses by depleting immunostimulatory commensals. 48 The observed reduction in tumor number upon rifaximin monotherapy further suggests potential interactions with tumorigenic pathways, warranting additional investigation in models with longitudinal tracking of tumor progression. These results support rifaximin as a microbiota-modulating adjunct that improves chemotherapy tolerability without compromising antitumor efficacy.

To explore translational relevance, we tested rifaximin on ex vivo human colon explants using the EVOC system. Although direct testing with 5-FU would have been more relevant to chemotherapy-induced mucositis, this was limited by the restricted viability window of human explants, which did not allow measurable tissue damage to develop under these conditions. We therefore used LPS as an alternative inflammatory challenge to assess whether rifaximin could preserve barrier integrity in human intestinal tissue. Under these conditions, rifaximin preserved mucus organization, tight junction integrity, and overall tissue architecture, supporting a barrier-protective effect in human intestinal mucosa and its potential relevance in clinical settings where mucosal injury contributes to chemotherapy toxicity. However, this model does not fully capture microbiota-based interactions central to our in vivo findings, and LPS represents a distinct inflammatory stimulus compared with 5-FU. These results should therefore be interpreted as supportive, partly representative of chemotherapy-associated mucosal damage.

Mechanistically, our data indicate that rifaximin's protective effects in mice are predominantly mediated through modulation of the gut microbiota rather than direct host signaling. Consistent with this interpretation, the murine pregnane X receptor (PXR), a canonical host target of rifaximin in humans, is not activated in mice, 49 , 50 supporting a microbiota-dependent mechanism in this model. Importantly, we did not evaluate a direct interaction between rifaximin and Muribaculum intestinale, such as drug metabolism or bacterial susceptibility, and further studies would be required to address these aspects.

Microbiome profiling revealed that 5-FU disrupted the intestinal community, depleting commensal anaerobes such as Muribaculum and Parasutterella, while expanding taxa associated with inflammatory or dysbiotic states, including Bacteroides. 51 , 52 Rifaximin partially counteracted these changes, through both reduction of taxa associated with dysbiosis and preservation of microbial features linked to mucosal integrity. Notably, this effect was accompanied with changes in alpha diversity, suggesting a selective remodeling of the microbial community rather than a global restoration of diversity. Consistent with its antibiotic activity, rifaximin may therefore exert protective effects in part through suppression of specific microbial taxa; however, the ability of Muribaculum intestinale alone to reproduce key aspects of barrier protection in vivo supports a role for microbiota remodeling beyond simple depletion.

Among the taxa associated with these changes, Muribaculum emerged as a key microbial contributor. Initially described as a rodent commensal 46 , 47 and predominantly abundant in murine models, Muribaculum presence in humans appears limited, 46 , 53 warranting caution in translational interpretation. Nevertheless, its functional repertoire, including mucin turnover, 45 resilience to inflammatory conditions and competitive exclusion of pathogens, 54 highlights mechanisms relevant to maintain barrier integrity and host–microbe interactions.

To further contextualize these taxonomic changes within a functional framework, we investigated the predicted metabolic capacity of the microbiota. Rifaximin treatment was associated with alterations in pathways related to carbohydrate metabolism and fermentation, extending to lipid biosynthesis, amino acid metabolism, and energy-related processes, including respiration. Together, these changes indicate a broad shift in microbial metabolic potential. These pathways are central to microbial processing of luminal substrates and generation of metabolites known to support epithelial homeostasis, including regulation of tight junction assembly, mucus production, immune responses, epithelial membrane integrity, and host–microbe signaling. 55 , 56 Muribaculum intestinale supplementation reproduced a subset of these functional changes, primarily involving carbohydrate metabolism and fermentation, consistent with its known specialization in polysaccharide utilization and mucin-associated carbohydrate processing. However, its more limited functional impact compared to rifaximin indicates that additional microbial taxa and pathways are required to fully recapitulate the observed protective phenotype.

Consistently, reduced Muribaculum abundance has been reported in several models of gut barrier dysfunction, including diet-induced and environmental stress conditions, 57-60 where it is associated with impaired tight junction expression, increased intestinal permeability, and systemic inflammation. Restoration of its abundance, through interventions applied in these models, correlates with improved barrier function, 57 , 58 , 61 supporting a role in intestinal homeostasis.

Our findings extend this evidence by demonstrating a direct protective effect of Muribaculum in chemotherapy-induced injury. Oral supplementation with M. intestinale reproduces key aspects of barrier protection, including preservation of mucus and epithelial integrity, although with a more limited impact compared to rifaximin. This supports a model in which Muribaculum contributes to, but does not fully account for, the protective effects of rifaximin. Notably, contribution analysis did not identify Muribaculum as a dominant contributor to predicted metabolic pathways, consistent with functional redundancy within the gut microbiota and supporting a community-level mechanism. Regional differences observed between the small intestine and colon may further reflect spatial heterogeneity in Muribaculaceae distribution and activity along the gastrointestinal tract. 62

These findings suggest that microbiota-mediated preservation of mucosal barrier integrity may represent a common mechanism underlying some of the beneficial gastrointestinal effects attributed to rifaximin in different clinical contexts across gastrointestinal and gut–liver–brain axis disorders. In this framework, Muribaculum serves as a useful mechanistic model to illustrate how specific commensal functions, such as support of mucus dynamics or epithelial barrier stability, can contribute to host resilience under inflammatory or cytotoxic stress. Despite promising results, some limitations of the present study must be acknowledged. First, murine models do not fully recapitulate human microbiota complexity and immune responses, and the predominance of Muribaculaceae in mice limits direct translational relevance. Second, functional prediction based on 16S rRNA gene sequencing reflects inferred metabolic potential rather than direct measurement of microbial activity, and therefore requires validation through targeted metabolomic and host-level analyzes. Finally, although rifaximin is generally considered to have a low risk of resistance development, recent reports of cross-resistance with daptomycin in vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus faecium highlight the need for careful evaluation in clinical settings. 63

In conclusion, we identify Muribaculum intestinale as one microbiota-derived contributor to rifaximin's mucosal protective effects during chemotherapy. This work supports a model in which microbiota modulation underlies therapeutic benefit and suggests that specific microbial functions may contribute to intestinal resilience during cytotoxic treatment. These findings may support future development of targeted microbial strategies to preserve gut health during cancer therapy and beyond.
