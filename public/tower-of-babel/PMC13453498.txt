A 3– 2–1 reflective journaling method for cultivating caring behaviors and compassionate empathy in clinical nursing education
Authors: Jeffrey V. Esteron, Jerry Ligawen, Therese Camille Rivera, Brenda Policarpio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clinical nursing programs use reflective journals to connect patient encounters with caring behavior and compassionate empathy, yet open-ended journals often vary in depth, confidentiality safeguards, and faculty feedback. This method article presents a low-cost 3– 2–1 reflective journaling method for medical-surgical clinical education. After each clinical session, students write three realizations, two discoveries, and one challenge with an action plan. Faculty deliver a shared orientation, protect confidentiality, use a formative rubric, provide timely feedback, and conduct debriefing to reinforce patient-centered learning. The method was implemented with 139 third-year nursing students and validated using pre-post CBI-16 Student and KCES-R scores. Caring behavior improved from 90.71 ± 4.98 to 91.62 ± 5.69 (Wilcoxon p = 0.0002; estimated r = 0.32), and compassionate empathy improved from 94.26 ± 5.77 to 95.50 ± 3.66 (p = 0.0001; estimated r = 0.33). Because baseline scores were already high, the findings should be interpreted as modest but educationally meaningful gains rather than large clinical shifts.•Structures reflection into three realizations, two discoveries, and one challenge/action plan for consistent clinical learning and assessment.•Standardizes orientation, confidentiality guidance, rubric-based feedback, debriefing, fidelity checks, and troubleshooting for faculty implementation across rotations.•Provides validation evidence supporting reproducible adoption in nursing programs seeking measurable caring and empathy outcomes.

Background

Caring behavior and compassionate empathy are core clinical competencies in nursing education because they shape how students listen, communicate, protect dignity, explain care, and respond to the human meaning of illness [1]. In clinical rotations, students are expected not only to perform procedures correctly but also to interpret patient experiences, recognize emotional cues, and translate professional values into observable patient-centered actions. These competencies are therefore difficult to develop through lectures alone; they require repeated contact with real or simulated clinical situations followed by guided reflection on what happened, why it mattered, and how care can be improved.

Reflective journaling is commonly used to support this type of learning in nursing and health-professions education [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6]]. However, many journal activities are implemented as broad writing assignments rather than as reproducible teaching methods. When prompts, feedback criteria, confidentiality procedures, submission schedules, and debriefing practices are not specified, the method becomes difficult to replicate across clinical groups and difficult to evaluate as an educational intervention. Students may produce descriptive narratives, faculty may respond inconsistently, and programs may be unable to determine whether journaling is strengthening the intended caring and empathy outcomes.

The 3– 2–1 reflective journaling method addresses this practical gap by converting reflective writing into a simple, repeatable, and auditable clinical teaching procedure. After each clinical exposure, students document three realizations, two discoveries, and one challenge or unresolved concern, then link the challenge to a concrete action plan. The structure is intentionally brief so that it can be used during demanding related learning experience rotations, yet it is sufficiently specific to move students beyond event description toward interpretation, self-assessment, and planned behavioral change.

The implementation of 3– 2–1 reflective journaling among 139 third-year nursing students was associated with statistically significant improvement in caring behavior and compassionate empathy scores. The purpose here is different from the original outcome report. Rather than emphasizing the study results, this article emphasizes the method itself: how the journal was designed, how students were oriented, how faculty feedback was standardized, how confidentiality was protected, how fidelity was monitored, and how the approach can be adapted by other clinical educators.

The method draws from the original 3– 2–1 reflective journaling approach [7], caring theory [8], and reflective practice literature [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Its contribution is operational. It gives educators a replicable protocol with prompts, rubrics, implementation timing, feedback rules, validation measures, and troubleshooting guidance. These details are essential because reflective learning interventions are highly sensitive to local delivery: a journal assignment without feedback, a feedback process without faculty calibration, or a reflection prompt without privacy safeguards may produce a substantially different learning experience.

This article therefore describes the 3– 2–1 reflective journaling method in a form that can be adopted, adapted, and tested in other nursing and health-professions education settings. The intended users are clinical instructors, course coordinators, nurse educators, and researchers who need a low-cost but structured method for strengthening students' caring behaviors and compassionate empathy while maintaining ethical handling of patient-related reflective writing.

Method details

Reflective journaling is widely recommended in nursing education, but many implementations remain under-specified: students may be asked to write reflections without a consistent prompt architecture, feedback rubric, confidentiality protocol, or fidelity check. The 3– 2–1 reflective journaling method addresses that gap by turning reflection into a repeatable clinical teaching procedure. Its value is not merely that students write; its value is that they write in a structured sequence that repeatedly moves from observation to interpretation to future action.

The method is intentionally concise. The three realizations help students notice meaning in clinical events; the two discoveries require translation of experience into new knowledge, skill, or professional attitude; and the one challenge with action plan prevents reflection from ending as description alone. This structure is especially useful in busy related learning experience rotations because it reduces ambiguity for students while giving faculty a stable basis for formative feedback.

The methodological contribution is therefore operational rather than conceptual only: the article specifies the preparation, orientation, journal template, feedback process, debriefing, fidelity safeguards, validation measures, and adaptation rules needed for another nursing program to reproduce the intervention. The method is also ethically practical because it can be separated from clinical grading, anonymized for research use, and adapted to existing institutional learning platforms.

Theoretical and pedagogical basis

The protocol is grounded in caring theory and reflective learning. Watson's caring perspective positions the nurse-patient relationship as more than task completion; it includes intentional presence, respect, responsiveness, and moral attention to the whole person [8]. Reflective writing creates a bridge between that theory and clinical action by asking students to examine what happened, what they felt or assumed, how the patient may have experienced the encounter, and what a more caring response would require next time [[2], [3], [4], [5], [6]].

Unlike unstructured journaling, the 3– 2–1 format makes the reflection traceable. It provides a consistent minimum data structure across entries while preserving the student's voice. This balance is important in clinical education: excessive standardization can flatten reflection into compliance, whereas insufficient structure can leave the depth of reflection to chance. The method uses structure to support, not replace, professional self-examination.

Setting, participants, and use case

The method was applied in a university clinical nursing program in Angeles City, Philippines, with third-year students assigned to a medical-surgical unit for related learning experience training during Academic Year 2024–2025. The validation implementation included 139 students, predominantly female, all single, with a mean age of 20.65 years. The intervention ran from August 5 to October 30, 2024, followed by post-intervention measurement in November 2024. Students completed a minimum of one 3– 2–1 entry per week across the 12-week intervention period, for 12 required entries, with additional entries permitted after clinical exposures according to the rotation schedule. The method is suitable for clinical courses in which students have repeated patient encounters and faculty can review short reflective entries at regular intervals.

Although developed for clinical nursing education, the procedure can be adapted for other health-professions programs where empathy, patient-centered care, communication, and ethical responsiveness are explicit competencies. Examples include midwifery, pharmacy, physical therapy, medicine, and interprofessional simulation, provided that the prompts are aligned with the learner's scope of practice and local confidentiality requirements.

Rubric and feedback logic

The rubric should be formative rather than punitive. A simple three-level scoring guide is sufficient: emerging, developing, and proficient. The goal is not to reward polished prose, but to make depth of reflection visible. Data collection and analysis for validation

The validation implementation used two established instruments. Caring behavior was measured using the Caring Behavior Inventory-16 Student version, a 16-item self-assessment tool rated on a six-point scale with a total score range of 16–96 [9]. Compassionate empathy was measured using the Kiersma-Chen Empathy Scale-Revised, a health-professions empathy measure scored on a seven-point response format with a total score range of 15–105 [10]. Prior use in nursing or health-professions education supports the content relevance of both instruments for this population, and pilot testing in the local context supported internal consistency, with Cronbach's alpha values of 0.879 for CBI-16 and 0.871 for KCES-R. Baseline scores were collected before the first journal entry, and post-intervention scores were collected after completion of the journaling period. Students completed both self-report instruments; trained assistant researchers and the primary researcher exported the secure survey responses and computed total scores according to instrument scoring rules.

For programs that validate the method, paired analysis should compare baseline and post-intervention scores for the same students. When normality cannot be assumed or Likert-type aggregate scores are analyzed conservatively, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test is appropriate. Effect sizes should be reported with p values; using r = Z/sqrt(N), the published validation p values imply small-to-moderate absolute effects for caring behavior (estimated Z = 3.72, r = 0.32, N = 139) and compassionate empathy (estimated Z = 3.89, r = 0.33, N = 139). Programs should also report attrition, missing data handling, timing of surveys, whether reflective journal completion was required for the course or optional for research inclusion, and the number and proportion of students whose paired total scores improved, remained unchanged, or declined.

The validation evidence supports the practical usefulness of the method, but it should be interpreted as method validation rather than definitive causal proof. The pre-post design measures change within the same learners and is efficient for educational implementation, yet it cannot fully rule out maturation, concurrent clinical learning, or testing effects. This limitation is openly acknowledged because transparent method reporting strengthens, rather than weakens, reproducibility.

Directional change should also be reported from paired student-level total scores: improved when the post-test total is higher than baseline, unchanged when totals are equal, and declined when the post-test total is lower. The published aggregate article provides means, standard deviations, Wilcoxon statistics, and p values, but the improved/unchanged/declined proportions require the de-identified paired dataset and should not be inferred from aggregate means alone.

The strength of the method lies in its operational clarity. Many reflective practice studies report that students journaled, but fewer describe the exact architecture of the entry, the feedback pathway, the debriefing cadence, and the fidelity safeguards needed for implementation. The 3– 2–1 reflective journaling method makes each of those elements visible. This increases reproducibility and allows other educators to adopt, compare, or modify the procedure without guessing how reflection was actually performed.

The method is also appropriately matched to the target outcomes. Caring behavior and compassionate empathy are not isolated psychomotor tasks; they require noticing, interpretation, perspective-taking, and deliberate future action. The 3– 2–1 sequence follows that developmental logic. Realizations help students name meaning, discoveries help them convert experience into learning, and the challenge/action-plan element requires behavioral transfer. This is why the method is more defensible than simply asking students to write an unstructured journal entry.

Another strength is feasibility. The intervention does not require simulation centers, specialized software, paid licenses, or additional clinical placement hours. It can be inserted into an existing rotation using a one-page template, a brief orientation, and short faculty feedback. For resource-constrained programs, this matters: a method that depends on expensive infrastructure may be educationally attractive but difficult to sustain.

Validity, reliability, and trustworthiness of implementation

The improvement in caring behavior was statistically significant but numerically modest: the total score increased by 0.91 points on a 16–96 scale, from 90.71 to 91.62. Baseline scores were already near the upper end of the scale, so a ceiling effect likely limited the size of possible gains. The estimated Wilcoxon effect size (r = 0.32) suggests a small-to-moderate change, which should be interpreted as educational reinforcement of already high caring behaviors rather than a large clinical transformation. For a method intended to strengthen professional consistency, modest gains can still be meaningful if they reflect more dependable patient teaching, confidence, timeliness, and symptom relief.

The compassionate empathy findings should be interpreted similarly. The total score increased by 1.24 points on a 15–105 scale, from 94.26 to 95.50, with an estimated Wilcoxon effect size of r = 0.33 and reduced post-intervention variability. Because the baseline mean was already high, the educational significance lies less in a large score shift and more in the possibility that repeated structured reflection helped students express empathy more consistently. In clinical education, this consistency is valuable because patient-centered care should not depend solely on individual disposition; it should be cultivated through repeatable educational practice.

Interpretation of the validation results

The improvement in caring behavior was statistically significant even though baseline scores were already high. This pattern is plausible in clinical nursing education because students often enter patient-care rotations with positive self-perceptions of caring. A ceiling effect can make large numerical gains unlikely. For a method intended to reinforce professional behaviors, even modest score increases can be meaningful if they indicate greater consistency in behaviors such as patient teaching, confidence, timeliness, and symptom relief.

The compassionate empathy findings are particularly important because the significant post-intervention increase was accompanied by reduced variability in overall scores. This suggests that the method may help students converge toward a more consistent empathic stance, not merely raise the average for a small subgroup. In a clinical education setting, this consistency is valuable because patient-centered care should not depend solely on individual disposition; it should be cultivated through repeatable educational practice.

Transferability

The method can be transferred to other clinical courses if three conditions are maintained: students must have meaningful encounters to reflect on, faculty must provide timely formative feedback, and confidentiality rules must be explicit. The core 3– 2–1 architecture should remain stable during initial adoption. After fidelity is established, programs can adapt the prompt bank to specialty areas such as maternal-child nursing, psychiatric nursing, community health, simulation, interprofessional care, or end-of-life communication.

Transferability is strengthened by the method's modular design. Programs that cannot collect research data can still use the journal as a teaching method. Programs with strong evaluation capacity can add validated measures, qualitative thematic analysis, or objective structured clinical examination stations. Programs with limited faculty time can use a sampling strategy for detailed comments, such as providing full rubric feedback on selected weekly entries while reviewing all submissions for completion and confidentiality. For planning purposes, faculty should budget approximately 5 min per entry, or about 75–90 min per week for 15 students submitting one entry each.

Limitations

The main limitation of the validation is the one-group pretest-post-test design. Without a comparison group, outcome change cannot be attributed only to reflective journaling. The method also relied on self-report instruments, was implemented at one university, and involved students in one clinical context. In addition, baseline caring and empathy scores were high, creating a ceiling effect that makes large numerical gains unlikely. The student-faculty relationship may also create perceived pressure despite safeguards for voluntary participation. These limitations should be reported transparently in any MethodsX submission.

Future development should test the method in multi-site samples, compare it with unstructured journaling or standard clinical debriefing, examine longer-term retention of caring behaviors, and combine self-report instruments with performance-based assessment. Qualitative analysis of anonymized entries could also clarify which types of realizations, discoveries, and action plans are most strongly associated with empathy and caring behavior development.
