Impact of Stabilizing Osmolytes on the Conformational Dynamics of Human and Rat Islet Amyloid Polypeptides
Authors: Kiara A. Kidman, Claudio Pedrick, Cara A. Kreck, Ricardo L. Mancera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe aggregation of human islet amyloid polypeptide (hIAPP) into cytotoxic oligomers and amyloid fibrils is a hallmark of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), leading to pancreatic β‐cell dysfunction. In contrast, rat IAPP (rIAPP) is largely non‐amyloidogenic. Osmolytes such as glucose, glycerol, and sorbitol are known to stabilize globular protein structures; however, in the case of intrinsically disordered proteins (IDPs), they modulate amyloidogenic aggregation in a concentration‐dependent manner. Understanding the molecular mechanism of action of these osmolytes on IDPs remains limited. Well‐tempered bias exchange metadynamics (WT‐BEMD) simulations were used to study the conformational energy landscape of hIAPP and rIAPP in solution across varying osmolyte concentrations (125, 250, and 500 mM). The addition of osmolytes resulted in subtle changes in secondary structure propensity and content in both hIAPP and rIAPP. In the case of hIAPP, a general reduction in the likelihood of α‐helical conformations was observed, particularly in the amyloidogenic core, suggesting a molecular mechanism for reduced aggregation in the presence of osmolytes. There was a notable lack of significant direct H‐bonding and hydrophobic protein‐osmolyte interactions, confirming the presence of a strong osmophobic effect. These findings suggest that these stabilizing osmolytes influence the conformational ensemble of hIAPP and rIAPP through exclusion from the protein surface, rather than by directly stabilizing specific conformations. The potential osmolyte‐mediated reduction in aggregation‐prone conformations in IDPs such as hIAPP may disrupt early aggregation and offer a potential strategy to mitigate hIAPP cytotoxicity.

Introduction

Human islet amyloid polypeptide (hIAPP) is an amyloidogenic intrinsically disordered protein (IDP), whose aberrant aggregation into cytotoxic soluble oligomers and amyloid fibrils is a hallmark feature of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). Under physiological conditions, hIAPP is co‐synthesized and secreted with insulin by pancreatic β‐cells and aids in glucose homeostasis by slowing gastric emptying, reducing glucagon secretion, and promoting satiety [1, 2, 3, 4].

IDPs lack a well‐defined secondary structure, fluctuating across predominantly disordered conformations, which predisposes these proteins to misfolding and aggregation [5, 6, 7]. These properties make IDPs susceptible to being affected by cellular conditions such as pH, temperature, oxidative stress, and ionic strength, which may further promote misfolding and aggregation [8, 9]. Additional factors, such as molecular crowding, mechanical stress, metal‐ionic interactions, chemical modifications, and altered levels of chaperone proteins, can also trigger the aggregation of IDPs into amyloid fibrils [10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. In T2DM, the accumulation of hIAPP, along with biochemical changes in the pancreas, can promote its aggregation into amyloid fibrils, contributing to β‐cell dysfunction and the progression of disease pathology [16, 17, 18].

The aggregation of hIAPP follows a nucleation‐dependent mechanism involving three key phases: nucleation, elongation, and stationary phase. During the nucleation phase, monomeric hIAPP adopts transient secondary structures conducive to self‐association, resulting in the formation of a nucleation seed and soluble oligomers [19, 20]. This process leads to the rapid elongation of protofibrils and the formation of mature fibrils [21].

Prefibrillar oligomers of hIAPP are toxic and contribute to β‐cell dysfunction by disrupting cellular membranes, inducing oxidative stress, and activating inflammatory pathways [22, 23, 24]. Characterizing these early oligomeric conformations and the nucleation dynamics is essential to understanding their cytotoxicity [9]. Numerous transient oligomeric intermediates have been identified along the fibril formation pathway; however, the structural heterogeneity of hIAPP and the metastable nature of its myriad conformational states make it challenging to link these intermediates to their cytotoxic properties [9].

Experimental studies have characterized late‐stage protofibrils and mature fibrils to identify key regions and structural elements essential for amyloid formation. The amyloidogenic core region of hIAPP, comprising residues 20SNNFGAILSS29, is critical for aggregation. This region exhibits a propensity to form β‐sheet structures, which are critical for both nucleation and fibril stability [19, 25, 26, 27, 28]. However, X‐ray diffraction (XRD) and solid‐state nuclear magnetic resonance (ssNMR) protofibril models have revealed that this amyloidogenic core region does not form part of the typical cross‐β structure; rather, it adopts a partially ordered bend connecting the β‐strands. These findings suggest that the amyloidogenic core region is an early structural feature that controls the kinetics of amyloid formation and which can be significantly influenced by amino acid substitutions [27, 29, 30, 31].

Compared to hIAPP, rat IAPP (rIAPP) exhibits significantly reduced amyloid fibril formation and lacks pathogenic features associated with T2DM [32]. Six amino acid substitutions distinguish rIAPP from hIAPP: His18Arg, Phe23Leu, Ala25Pro, Ile26Val, Ser28Pro, and Ser29Pro [33, 34]. Thioflavin‐T (ThT) fluorescence aggregation experiments have demonstrated that the Ala25Pro, Ser28Pro, and Ser29Pro substitutions diminish the rate of amyloid formation in rIAPP, with Ser28Pro exerting the greatest effect. The His18Arg substitution increases the time required for fibril formation, while the Phe23Leu substitution extends the initial lag phase by hindering β‐sheet formation, the point at which monomers become nucleation seeds [33, 35, 36, 37].

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have been used to investigate the conformational behavior of monomeric hIAPP and rIAPP [31, 38, 39, 40, 41, 42]. Monomeric hIAPP was predicted to exhibit a propensity to form β‐sheet structures within its amyloidogenic core region. Proline substitutions in this region disrupt β‐sheet formation, consistent with the absence of well‐defined β‐sheet conformation in rIAPP [38, 39, 41, 42]. Three dominant conformational states in hIAPP have been identified: a compact α‐helical/coil structure, an extended anti‐parallel β‐sheet, and a random coil. Among these, the β‐sheet conformation was found to be the most energetically favorable [38, 39, 41, 42]. In contrast, rIAPP predominantly adopts either a compact α‐helical or random coil structure, with the α‐helical form favored at room temperature and the random coil conformation becoming more prevalent at elevated temperatures due to greater enthalpic stabilization [38, 39, 41, 42].

Stabilizing osmolytes, such as methylamines, polyols, sugars, and specific amino acids, are small naturally occurring molecules that cells accumulate in response to cellular stress in order to counteract destabilizing physico‐chemical forces [43, 44, 45, 46]. These molecules can also affect the aggregation of IDPs, such as hIAPP. Stabilizing osmolytes are believed to act through an osmophobic effect, resulting in their exclusion from the protein surface and thereby promoting interactions between the protein and surrounding water molecules [43, 44, 47]. This exclusion raises the free energy of proteins, stabilizing them by shifting the folding equilibrium toward more compact, native‐like conformations with reduced solvent‐accessible surface area (SASA) [48]. While stabilizing osmolytes increase the free energy of the unfolded state of globular proteins, promoting compact structures, their effects on IDPs vary [49, 50]. Depending on the type and concentration of the osmolyte, as well as the surrounding physico‐chemical environment, they may either promote or inhibit the aggregation of IDPs [10, 51, 52, 53, 54].

Experimental techniques have explored how stabilizing osmolytes affect the aggregation kinetics of hIAPP and alter its average structure. Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy studies have shown that hIAPP rapidly transitions from a random coil to β‐sheet conformation as fibrils mature. In the presence of 20% glycerol (v/v), the hIAPP fragment spanning residues 20–29 undergoes slower conformational changes over time, indicating that glycerol inhibits the transition to β‐sheet structures by reducing the shift from random coil to β‐sheet formation [54]. Similarly, other studies have demonstrated that choline‐O‐sulfate (COS) delays the β‐sheet transition [13]. Trehalose displays a concentration‐dependent effect, whereby at lower concentrations it inhibits or delays the α‐helix to β‐sheet transition, whereas at higher concentrations, it paradoxically reduces the lag phase, thereby promoting faster β‐sheet formation. Molecular crowding at high trehalose concentrations may be responsible for enhancing aggregation [55].

Studies using thioflavin T (ThT) fluorescence assays have examined how stabilizing osmolytes affect the aggregation kinetics of hIAPP. Trimethylamine N‐oxide (TMAO) has been shown to slow fibril elongation and growth without affecting the lag phase. Similarly, betaine reduces the fibril growth rate, albeit less significantly than TMAO [56]. L‐proline slows the elongation phase at concentrations up to 500 mM and markedly inhibits fibril formation at concentrations as high as 1 M [57]. MD simulations at 2 M TMAO concentration revealed that TMAO stabilizes hIAPP by preserving hydrogen bonds and maintaining the hydration shell, promoting compact and ordered structures [53].

Although research on the effect of sorbitol on hIAPP aggregation is limited, a study on γ‐synuclein reported that lower concentrations of sorbitol increase the lag time for fibrillation, while at higher concentrations decrease the lag time, thereby promoting fibrillation [58].

Characterizing the influence of various polyols, sugar alcohols, and sugar‐based stabilizing osmolytes on the solution structures of monomeric hIAPP and rIAPP will help to elucidate whether they induce an osmophobic effect and if they alter the conformations adopted by aggregation‐prone hIAPP and non‐amyloidogenic rIAPP. A better understanding of the mechanism of interaction between these two peptides and osmolyte‐containing aqueous solutions may reveal key molecular mechanisms that drive their contrasting aggregation propensities. Such knowledge is crucial for the development of targeted strategies to inhibit hIAPP aggregation into toxic oligomers and insoluble fibrils, offering a potential therapeutic intervention for T2DM and related amyloid diseases. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the effects of the stabilizing osmolytes glucose, glycerol, and sorbitol on the conformational landscape of hIAPP and rIAPP, and how this may impact their aggregation properties.

Methods

Initial Structures and Simulation Setup

Equilibrated monomeric structures of hIAPP and rIAPP derived from conventional molecular dynamics (cMD) simulations were used. To align with typical experimental conditions, each peptide was capped with an NH3 + group at the N‐terminus and an NH2 group at the C‐terminus. For each simulation system described further below, a single rIAPP monomer with a net charge of +4, or a single hIAPP monomer with a net charge of +3, was placed in a cubic simulation box, ensuring a minimum distance of 1.0 nm from the peptide to the edge of the cell. To neutralize the charges of hIAPP and rIAPP, 4 Cl− and 3 Cl− ions were added, respectively. The temperature was maintained at 310 K using the Nosé‐Hoover thermostat with a time constant of 2.0 ps [59, 60]. Pressure was kept at 1 atm with the Parrinello‐Rahman barostat, using a time constant of 5.0 ps and the experimental compressibility of water of 4.5 × 10−5 bar−1 [61, 62].

Electrostatic interactions were computed using the particle mesh Ewald (PME) method, with a Coulomb interaction cutoff of 1.0 nm [63, 64]. Lennard–Jones interactions were treated with a twin‐range cutoff scheme, also set at 1.0 nm. Periodic boundary conditions were applied in all directions, with a time step of 2.0 fs throughout the simulation. The Amber99SB*‐ILDNP force field [65], which has been shown to accurately replicate experimental properties of IDPs like rIAPP and hIAPP, was employed [66, 67, 68, 69]. The TIP4PD water model, developed specifically for IDP simulations, was used [70, 71]. General AMBER Force Field (GAFF2) [72] parameters were used for sorbitol and Glycosidic Linkage Conformational Amplitude Model (GLYCAM) [73] parameters were used for glucose and glycerol. Table 1 list the osmolyte concentrations and corresponding compositions (number of molecules in the simulation cell) investigated. The two dominant anomeric forms of glucose [74] and the various conformers of glycerol [75] were explicitly included in accordance with their experimentally determined relative proportions.

Well‐tempered bias exchange metadynamics (WT‐BEMD) simulations were performed using the Plumed 2.3.5 plugin integrated with GROMACS 2023.3 [76, 77, 78]. This method utilizes a history‐dependent bias potential, known as a Gaussian energy function (hill), to reduce the probability of revisiting previously explored states. By doing so, it facilitates the crossing of energy barriers, enabling transitions from local minima to higher‐energy configurations that contribute to the free energy of the system. A bias factor of 15 was used during well‐tempering to gradually lower the height of the Gaussian function as different areas of phase space were sampled [79, 80, 81].

WT‐BEMD simulations make use of multiple replicas, each biased by collective variables (CVs) to enhance sampling efficiency and allow for configuration swaps between replicas via random exchanges. For each peptide, three replicas were biased using two CVs: α‐RMSD and anti‐parallel β‐RMSD, to explore α‐helical and β‐sheet conformations in monomeric hIAPP and rIAPP in the presence of stabilizing osmolytes, similarly to previous studies of IAPP and amyloid β42 (Aβ42) [6, 39, 66, 71, 82]. These CVs are well suited to study the conformational properties of IDPs because they quantify local α‐helical and β‐sheet propensity, rather than global similarity to single conformations. A slider is used to compute the RMSD of multiple segments of the peptide with respect to ideal geometries for α‐helical or β‐sheet conformations (six consecutive residues for α‐RMSD and three + three residues for β‐RMSD), which is then added to give a metric of total α‐helical or β‐sheet content [83]. Higher CV values reflect larger number and/or length of amino acid sequence regions with the corresponding secondary structure. Plumed computes the RMSD of these segments relative to the corresponding ideal geometry and combines the local comparisons into a single CV value using a switching‐function sum. The parameters used for the switching function were r0 = 0.1, M = 8, and N = 4 for α‐RMSD, and r0 = 0.1, M = 12, and N = 6 for β‐RMSD [84, 85].

To focus exploration on specific regions, sampling of CVs was constrained with upper limits of 25 for α‐RMSD and 10 for β‐RMSD, and lower limits of 0.2 for both α‐RMSD and β‐RMSD. Gaussian energy hills were deposited periodically every 500 steps, with random exchanges between replicas and trajectory frames saved every 20 ps. Convergence of the free energy calculations was assessed using the METAGUI 3.0 plugin in VMD [86, 87], by comparing free energy surfaces at 20 ps intervals. To obtain accurate ensemble averages from the biased simulations, observables (O) were corrected for bias by reweighting the post‐convergence portion of the simulation trajectory. The average value of a bin α for a particular microstate, represented by Oα, and the free energy Fα of the bin were recalculated at a fixed temperature (T) to account for the applied biases [88, 89, 90]. O=Oαα∑expFα/Texpα∑−Fα/T

The convergence of the free energy surface was assessed using the sum_hills tool in PLUMED 2.5.3 [76, 77] to compute the volume under the surface, facilitating the evaluation of free energy changes. Upon convergence, the simulation had sufficiently sampled all regions of the conformational free energy surface, resulting in a reduction of the applied bias potential in the sampled regions. Simulations were extended by an additional 300 ns beyond the point of convergence. The convergence plot of each simulation is available in Figures S1 and S2. To identify regions within the CV space exhibiting large changes in free energy, plots of the computed free energy against each CV were generated. This also allowed a better understanding of the shape of the converged free energy surface for each individual CV.

The METAGUI 3.0 plugin for VMD [86, 87] was used to compute the free energy surface using the K‐medoids clustering method. A sieving value of 20 000 was applied, resulting in the generation of 1000 microstates. The weighted histogram analysis method (WHAM) was used with the equilibrium time (Teq) set to the convergence time point of the system. The free energy of each microstate was calculated with a delta value of 4 (maximum allowed difference in kBT units between free energy estimates) and a kBT of 2.57 kJ/mol (thermal energy at 310 K), where kB is Boltzmann's constant and T is the temperature.

Statistical uncertainties in the reweighted observables were estimated from post‐convergence trajectories using the weighted block standard error analysis method of Flyvbjerg and Petersen [91] adapted for biased simulations [91, 92]. This approach was applied to all hydrogen‐bonding analyses.

Structural and Interaction Analysis

The hydrophobic and hydrophilic solvent‐accessible surface areas (SASA) were calculated using the gmx_sasa module in GROMACS. These measurements were related to the compactness of the peptide and its residue interaction preferences. NMR Cα chemical shifts for the peptides were calculated using SHIFTX2 [93]. Secondary Cα chemical shifts were computed by subtracting random coil values, including sequence corrections [94, 95]. These were used to determine secondary structure propensities for each amino acid in the peptide sequence. Predictions were compared with experimental NMR values for rIAPP. The secondary structure content per frame was quantified using the DSSP (Define Secondary Structure of Proteins) method, executed via the gmx_dssp module in GROMACS, and used to calculate simulation averages per amino acid residue. Hydrogen‐bonding (H‐bonding) analysis was conducted using the gmx_hbond module in GROMACS, with a hydrogen‐donor‐acceptor (H‐D‐A) angle cut‐off of 37 degrees and a maximum donor‐acceptor distance of 0.35 nm, covering intra‐peptide, peptide‐solvent, and solvent‐solvent interactions [96, 97]. As an indicator of hydrophobic interactions, the radial distribution function (RDF, or g(r)) and coordination number between hIAPP side‐chain non‐polar C atoms (in methylene or methine groups) and osmolyte C atoms in each simulation system was calculated over the entire trajectory using the gmx_rdf module in GROMACS. Polar or charged functional‐group carbons were omitted; instead, the most proximal non‐polar C atoms (i.e., methylene or methine) within the sidechain was selected for all residues. RDFs were computed with a maximum radius of 1.5 nm and a bin width of 0.01 nm. The hydrophobic coordination number (i.e., the average number of non‐polar atoms within a given distance) was taken as the value of cumulative number RDF at a cut‐off distance of 0.6 nm.

Results

Conformational Free Energy Landscapes

The conformational free energy surface (FES) of rIAPP in pure water (Figure 1a) reveals the presence of a flat region with the lowest free energy (within 10 kJ/mol of the minimum, shown in dark purple) corresponding to low CV values of both α‐RMSD (< 5) and β‐RMSD (< 2), indicating a strong preference for disordered conformations, as expected. The corresponding FES of hIAPP in pure water (Figure 2a) also reveals that the most energetically favorable region is located within low value ranges of both α‐RMSD (< 4) and β‐RMSD (< 0.5), also corresponding to free energy values within 10 kJ/mol. This suggests that, while disordered conformations are also the most thermodynamically favorable for hIAPP, they are more narrowly defined than in rIAPP. The FES for hIAPP (Figure 2a) exhibits a somewhat more diffuse CV region within 20 kJ/mol (shown in light and dark purple) than the corresponding one in rIAPP (Figure 1a), such that conformations with higher CV values for α‐RMSD (< 9) and β‐RMSD (< 3) are more thermodynamically accessible.

FES of monomeric rIAPP in different solvent conditions: (a) pure water, (b–d) glucose at 125 mM, 250 mM, and 500 mM, respectively; (e–g) glycerol at 125 mM, 250 mM, and 500 mM; (h–j) sorbitol at 125 mM, 250 mM, and 500 mM.

FES of monomeric hIAPP in different solvent conditions: (a) pure water, (b–d) glucose at 125 mM, 250 mM, and 500 mM, respectively; (e–g) glycerol at 125 mM, 250 mM, and 500 mM; (h–j) sorbitol at 125 mM, 250 mM, and 500 mM.

The conformational FES of both rIAPP and hIAPP appear to be affected differently by each osmolyte and in a concentration‐dependent manner. At some concentrations there is an overall flattening of the entire FES, manifested as lower relative free energies at high values of both α‐RMSD and β‐RMSD, although this effect was observed more consistently in hIAPP than in rIAPP. While this in principle makes more structured conformations (i.e., with high CV values) thermodynamically more accessible, particularly in the case of hIAPP, it is primarily conformations within a free energy range of 20 kJ/mol that are significant and hence of more relevance.

Addition of glucose at a concentration of 125 mM (Figure 1b) led to the largest flattening of the conformational FES of rIAPP, with a major expansion of CV values for both α‐RMSD (< 10) and β‐RMSD (< 5) within 20 kJ/mol (colored in dark and light purple). This suggests that 125 mM glucose promotes a substantially broader range of thermodynamically accessible conformational states. The increase in concentration to 250 mM (Figure 1c) led to the opposite effect: a very large reduction in CV range within 20 kJ/mol for both α‐RMSD (< 4) and β‐RMSD (< 2), which is even lower than in pure water. Interestingly, a further increase in concentration to 500 mM (Figure 1d) increased the range in CV values within 20 kJ/mol for α‐RMSD (< 7) but not for β‐RMSD.

Addition of glycerol at a concentration of 125 mM (Figure 1e) led to an expansion of the CV range within 20 kJ/mol for both α‐RMSD (< 10) and β‐RMSD (< 5), alongside a flattening of the conformational FES of rIAPP. Increasing the concentration to 250 mM (Figure 1f) resulted in a further expansion of this low‐free energy CV range but only for α‐RMSD (< 14). Interestingly, a further increase in concentration to 500 mM (Figure 1g) led to a contraction of CV values within 20 kJ/mol for both α‐RMSD (< 10) and β‐RMSD (< 3).

Addition of sorbitol at a concentration of 125 mM (Figure 1h) did not affect in any significant way the conformational FES of rIAPP with respect to CV values within 20 kJ/mol for both α‐RMSD and β‐RMSD. Further increases in concentration to either 250 mM (Figure 1i) or 500 mM (Figure 1j) led to a gradual but relatively minor decrease in the CV range within 20 kJ/mol for both α‐RMSD (< 6) and β‐RMSD (< 4).

The addition of glucose at a concentration of 125 mM (Figure 2b) resulted in the most significant flattening of the conformational FES of hIAPP, with a substantial expansion of CV values for both α‐RMSD (< 12) and β‐RMSD (< 5) within 20 kJ/mol. The same effect was observed with rIAPP, as described above. Increasing the concentration to 250 mM (Figure 2c) led to an overall reduction in CV range within 20 kJ/mol, except for β‐RMSD with corresponding low α‐RMSD values (< 9). Increasing the concentration to 500 mM (Figure 2d) led to a contraction of low‐free energy regions, with a shift toward higher free energy values and a restriction of low‐free energy states to lower CV values for both α‐RMSD (< 3) and β‐RMSD (< 4).

Addition of glycerol at a concentration of 125 mM (Figure 2e) resulted in a significant reduction in the CV range within 10 kJ/mol (colored in dark purple), alongside a flattening of the conformational FES of hIAPP in β‐RMSD but only for corresponding low α‐RMSD values. Increasing the concentration to 250 mM (Figure 2f) resulted in an expansion of the low‐free energy CV range for α‐RMSD (< 5). A further increase in concentration to 500 mM (Figure 2g) led to substantial flattening of the FES, with an expansion of CV values within 20 kJ/mol for both α‐RMSD and β‐RMSD. Interestingly, this low‐free energy CV region is similar to that observed upon addition of glucose at a concentration of 250 mM.

Addition of sorbitol at a concentration of 125 mM (Figure 2h) resulted in the flattening of the conformational FES of hIAPP, with an expansion of CV values for both α‐RMSD (< 5) and β‐RMSD (< 4) within 10 kJ/mol. Further increases in concentration to either 250 mM (Figure 1i) or 500 mM (Figure 2j) decreased the extent of flattening of the FES, with CV ranges within 10 kJ/mol reduced in extent, particularly for β‐RMSD (< 2).

Secondary Cα Chemical Shifts

Secondary Cα chemical shifts provide an indicative measure of the average dominant secondary structure propensity for each residue. Values greater than 0 (upfield shifts) indicate α‐helical/turn propensity; values near 0 indicate an average propensity for random coil conformations; and values lower than 0 (downfield shifts) indicate β‐sheet propensity.

The predicted secondary Cα chemical shifts of rIAPP in pure water reveal the presence of distinct conformational propensities across different regions of the peptide. N‐terminal residues Thr4‐Phe15 predominantly exhibit a propensity for α‐helical conformations, with a few residues trending toward random coil conformations. The remainder of the peptide does not display a clear structural propensity, except for weak α‐helical/turn conformations in the C‐terminal region (Figure 3a). This is largely consistent with experimental secondary Cα chemical shifts, which show the presence of a transient α‐helix in N‐terminal residues Ala5‐Ser19, while the C‐terminal region is largely disordered with the presence of an unstructured coil‐turn conformation for residues Arg18‐Tyr37 [98]. Agreement between predicted and experimental Cα secondary chemical shifts was assessed by computing the Pearson's correlation coefficient (0.640) and the RMSD Δδ Cα (0.727 ppm), which are consistent with previously reported values for this force field [71].

Secondary chemical shifts of monomeric rIAPP: (a) pure water and experimental, (b–d) glucose at 125 mM, 250 mM, and 500 mM; (e–g) glycerol at 125 mM, 250 mM, and 500 mM; (h–j) sorbitol at 125 mM, 250 mM, and 500 mM.

In the presence of glucose at a concentration of 125 mM, rIAPP shows an increased propensity for α‐helical conformations for residues Asn14‐Gly24 and a more consistent propensity for random coil conformations of C‐terminal Asn31‐Tyr37 residues (Figure 3b). An increase in concentration to 250 mM resulted in conformational propensities similar to those observed in pure water (Figure 3c). At a concentration of 500 mM, rIAPP returned to exhibiting increased α‐helical propensity but for a shorter region encompassing residues Asn14‐Asn21, as well as more consistent α‐helical propensity for N‐terminal residues Asn31‐Tyr37 (similar to rIAPP in pure water) (Figure 3d).

In the presence of glycerol at a concentration of 125 mM, rIAPP exhibits a general reduction in α‐helical preference in the C‐terminal region, while N‐terminal residues increased their random coil propensity (Figure 3e). At a concentration of 250 mM, rIAPP displayed a clear increase in α‐helical propensity across most of the N‐ and C‐terminal regions, compared to that predicted in pure water (Figure 3f). At a concentration of 500 mM, there was again a reduction in α‐helical propensity in the C‐terminal region (but retaining the increased α‐helical propensity in the Asn14‐Asn22 region), but to a lesser extent than at a glycerol concentration of 125 mM, while rIAPP retains its α‐helical propensity at N‐terminal residues to a similar extent as that in pure water (Figure 3g).

In the presence of sorbitol at a concentration of 125 mM, rIAPP shows a substantial reduction in the α‐helical propensity of C‐terminal residues compared to that predicted in pure water and, instead, displays an increased propensity for random coil conformations (Figure 3h). These conformational propensities remain largely the same at a concentration of 250 mM, although rIAPP exhibits an increased propensity for random coil conformation compared to that predicted at a concentration of 125 mM and in pure water, as well as an increased α‐helical propensity in N‐terminal residues Asn31‐Tyr37 (Figure 3i). An increase in concentration to 500 mM resulted in rIAPP showing an increase in α‐helical propensity in C‐terminal residues Thr4‐Phe15, although less so than that predicted in pure water (Figure 3j).

The predicted secondary Cα chemical shifts of hIAPP in pure water (Figure 4a) reveal the presence of α‐helical propensity across most regions of the peptide, with a moderate reduction in α‐helical propensity in the N‐terminal region, but with a consistent increase in α‐helical propensity in C‐terminal residues His18‐Tyr37. This is largely consistent with previous MD simulations [71].

Secondary chemical shifts of monomeric hIAPP: (a) pure water, (b–d) glucose at 125 mM, 250 mM, and 500 mM; (e–g) glycerol at 125 mM, 250 mM, and 500 mM; (h–j) sorbitol at 125 mM, 250 mM, and 500 mM.

In the presence of 125 mM glucose, secondary Cα chemical shift analysis of hIAPP shows a slight increase in helical propensity at the N‐terminal region, particularly at residues Thr6 and Cys7. A reduction in helical propensity is observed for residues Ser20‐Tyr37 (Figure 4b). At a concentration of 250 mM glucose, secondary chemical shift analysis reveals an increase in helical propensity for residues Phe15‐Ser20, alongside a marked reduction in helicity for N‐terminal residues and an increase in β‐sheet propensity for residues Val32, Ser34, and Asn34 (Figure 4c). In 500 mM glucose, hIAPP showed an increase in overall helical tendencies of most residues, with a substantial increase in C‐terminal residues, compared to that seen in pure water and at lower concentrations of glucose (Figure 4d).

In solutions of 125 mM glycerol, hIAPP showed an increase in the helical propensity of residues Phe15‐Ser29 and an increase in random coil and β‐sheet propensity of C‐terminal residues (Figure 4e). In 250 mM, hIAPP notably showed an overall reduction in helical propensity of N‐terminal residues compared to pure water and instead displayed an increased β‐sheet and random coil propensity (Figure 4f). Further increases in concentration to 500 mM promoted increased helical propensity of C‐terminal residues and an increased random coil tendency for N‐terminal residues compared to in water (Figure 4g).

In 125 mM sorbitol, hIAPP exhibited an increased propensity for random coil and β‐sheet conformation compared to that seen in water, and an overall reduction in α‐helical propensity across the protein (Figure 4h). These conformational propensities are maintained at a concentration of 250 mM, although there is an increased helical propensity for residues Ile27‐Ser29 (Figure 4i). Conversely, at 500 mM hIAPP shows an increase in random coil propensity for N‐terminal residues compared to that seen in pure water, while C‐terminal residues also display a decrease in both α‐helical and β‐sheet propensities (Figure 4j).

Solvent‐Accessible Surface Area (SASA)

Changes in the computed SASA in rIAPP upon addition of all osmolytes at all concentrations considered exhibited a clear pattern, whereby all residues showed a reduction in solvent exposure (SASA) except for Leu16, Asn21, Asn22, and Leu23, which always exhibited an increase in solvent exposure (Figure 7). Addition of glucose follows an inverted U‐shaped trend in relation to the magnitude of the change in SASA (Figure 7b–d), whereas addition of glycerol follows a U‐shaped trend (Figure 7e–g). The changes in SASA largely retain the same magnitude at all concentrations of sorbitol (Figure 7h–j).

Hydrogen‐Bonding Interactions

No distinct pattern emerged in protein–protein and protein‐water H‐bonding interactions, likely due to the fact that intra‐protein and protein‐water H‐bonding reflect average changes across all secondary structures (Figures 9a and 10a). However, the relatively higher number of protein–protein H‐bonds and lower number of protein‐water H‐bonds in the presence of glucose at a concentration of 125 mM appears to be related to the increase in β‐sheet content at that concentration at the expense of random coil conformations (Tables 2 and 5). Similarly, the relatively higher number of protein–protein H‐bonds and lower number of protein‐water H‐bonds in the presence of glycerol at a concentration of 250 mM also correlates with a significant increase in turn, β‐sheet, and helical content at the expense of random coil conformations (Tables 3 and 6). In the case of sorbitol, the lower overall secondary structure content is only partially reversed at a concentration of 125 mM with a significant increase in β‐sheet content (Tables 4 and 7), which appears to correlate with a relatively higher number of protein–protein H‐bonds and a lower number of protein‐water H‐bonds.

Interestingly, the addition of any of the osmolytes resulted in only a small number of protein‐osmolyte H‐bonds, highlighting a lack of direct interactions between the surface of the protein (rIAPP or hIAPP) and osmolyte molecules (Figures 9b and 10b). This observation was confirmed through visualization of the simulation trajectories.

In the case of rIAPP, the number of protein‐osmolyte H‐bonds shows a clear linear increase with higher osmolyte concentrations. This trend was found to be identical for glycerol and sorbitol, and was somewhat more pronounced for glucose, which also exhibited more H‐bonds with the protein (Figure 9c). However, normalization of the number of H‐bonds per hydroxyl group (Figure 9c, inset) reveals that sorbitol has the weakest H‐bonding capacity of the three osmolytes. In the case of hIAPP, addition of glucose followed a very similar trend to that observed in rIAPP. Interestingly, addition of glycerol or sorbitol at all concentrations did not result in any changes in the small number of H‐bonds formed with the protein (Figure 10c), with normalization of the number of H‐bonds per hydroxyl group (Figure 10c, inset) again revealing that sorbitol has the weakest H‐bonding capacity.

Further analyses of H‐bonds, such as a differential analysis of H‐bonds made by protein mainchain groups or side chains with the osmolyte molecules, were deemed unnecessary given the very small number of H‐bonding interactions between the protein and the osmolytes.

Radial Distribution Functions (RDFs)

Protein‐osmolyte RDFs were computed to assess the presence of hydrophobic interactions between methylene/methine groups in non‐polar regions. When present, such interactions exhibit well‐defined peaks in their pair RDF, with a maximum at a distance of 0.4 nm and a minimum at 0.6 nm, which has been used to describe protein‐solute interactions [99, 100].

In the case of glucose, for both rIAPP (Figure 11a) and hIAPP (Figure 11d), a relatively minor, broad peak in the RDFs at r ~ 0.50 nm was observed across all concentrations, with g(r) values decreasing from 1.40 in rIAPP and 1.50 in hIAPP (125 mM) to 1.37 in rIAPP and 1.42 in hIAPP (250 mM), and 1.30 in rIAPP and 1.36 in hIAPP (500 mM), indicating that increasing glucose concentration nonetheless resulted in a smaller proportion of glucose molecules in the vicinity of both proteins. Additionally, a comparable reduction in the plateau region is observed in both proteins. The inset plots further show that the hydrophobic coordination number is small but increases with glucose concentration. This suggests the presence of a sparse and weak hydrophobic interaction between rIAPP/hIAPP and the non‐polar regions of glucose, which further decreases in a concentration‐dependent manner.

Radial distribution functions (RDFs) between sidechain non‐polar carbon atoms in rIAPP (a–c) and hIAPP (d–f) and osmolyte carbon atoms: (a, d) glucose at 125 mM, 250 mM and 500 mM; (b, e) glycerol at 125 mM, 250 mM and 500 mM; and (c, f) sorbitol at 125 mM, 250 mM and 500 mM.

In the case of glycerol, for both rIAPP (Figure 11b) and hIAPP (Figure 11e), a narrower but lower RDF peak was observed at ~0.52 nm across all concentrations. The g(r) values decreased from 1.26 in rIAPP and 1.23 in hIAPP (125 mM) to 1.19 in rIAPP and 1.22 in hIAPP (250 mM), to 1.19 for rIAPP and 1.20 in hIAPP (500 mM), indicating that at all concentrations a small proportion of glycerol molecules is found in the vicinity of both proteins. Furthermore, the inset plots show that the hydrophobic coordination number is smaller (compared to glucose) but also increases with glycerol concentration. The hydrophobic interaction between rIAPP/hIAPP and the carbon backbone of glycerol is also sparse, weak, and largely concentration‐insensitive.

In the case of sorbitol, for both rIAPP (Figure 11c) and hIAPP (Figure 11f), no RDF peak was observed at any of the concentrations. The g(r) values remained below bulk values, indicating that at all concentrations a very small proportion of sorbitol is found within the vicinity of both proteins. The inset plots show that the hydrophobic coordination number is small (similar in magnitude to that of glycerol), rising as the concentration of sorbitol increases. Similarly to glycerol, this shows that the hydrophobic interaction between the carbon backbone of sorbitol and rIAPP/hIAPP is very sparse, weak, and concentration‐insensitive.

Figure 12 shows the hydrophobic coordination number between rIAPP/hIAPP and each one of the osmolytes normalized by the number of carbon atoms in each osmolyte molecule (i.e., six for glucose and sorbitol and three for glycerol). All osmolytes exhibit a linear, concentration‐dependent increase in coordination number, yet the values remain very low, reflecting minimal hydrophobic contacts with both proteins. For example, glucose reaches a maximum average value at a concentration of 500 mM of only ~1.4–1.5 molecules in the vicinity of rIAPP and hIAPP (Figure 12a,d, insets). Glucose exhibits the highest values, closely followed by glycerol, whereas sorbitol displays substantially lower values. This reveals that sorbitol has the weakest tendency of the three osmolytes to form hydrophobic contacts with both proteins. The modest rise in coordination number with concentration largely reflects the increased number of osmolyte molecules present in solution at higher concentrations, rather than stronger intrinsic interactions.

Discussion

This study aimed to investigate how stabilizing osmolytes, specifically glucose (glucopyranose), sorbitol (6‐C sugar alcohol), and glycerol (3‐C polyol), interact with non‐amyloidogenic rIAPP and amyloidogenic hIAPP in their monomeric forms, and how they affect the conformational propensities of these peptides in solution. In particular, understanding the behavior of hIAPP under different physico‐chemical conditions is important because this peptide readily aggregates in aqueous solution into cytotoxic oligomers and amyloid fibrils, both of which contribute to pancreatic β‐cell dysfunction and the progression of T2DM [1, 2, 3, 4, 51]. Given that rIAPP does not form amyloid fibrils and is non‐amyloidogenic, comparative studies between hIAPP and rIAPP under similar osmolyte conditions can provide valuable insights into the physico‐chemical factors driving aggregation [32].

Our simulations revealed subtle yet distinct conformational preferences between hIAPP and rIAPP, highlighting key differences in their conformational behavior. In pure water, rIAPP predominantly adopts random coil conformations, reflecting a lower tendency for ordered secondary structures. Its substantially reduced tendency for aggregation has been attributed to residue substitutions, particularly proline substitutions at positions 25, 28, and 29, which disrupt secondary structure formation. These proline residues act as structure breakers by inducing turns and preventing the regular alignment necessary for β‐sheet or indeed α‐helix formation, ultimately inhibiting the formation of the characteristic cross β‐sheet structure observed in amyloidogenic fibrils [33, 34, 36, 39, 42]. In contrast, while hIAPP in water also exhibits a predominantly random coil conformation, it also shows a greater propensity for α‐helical conformations, particularly in C‐terminal residues His18‐Tyr37, which includes its amyloidogenic core (20SNNFGAILSS29). This amyloidogenic core is not in fact part of the cross β‐sheet structure of fibrils but it has been suggested that it may be involved in the formation of α‐helical structures that facilitate peptide self‐recognition during the nucleation phase, thereby promoting the subsequent formation of β‐sheet rich aggregates [35, 38, 101, 102, 103, 104]. Similar observations have been made for other IDPs such as Aβ42 [15, 105].

Experimental studies of the effect of glucose on the aggregation of IDPs have found that at low concentration glucose increases the stability of the hydration shell around proteins, resulting in increases in conformational flexibility and solubility, thereby reducing aggregation, whereas at a higher concentration of 1 M, it induces aggregation through a different molecular mechanism due to changes in solution viscosity and crowding [106, 107, 108]. In our simulations at the lower concentrations (125–500 mM), glucose exerted different and nuanced effects on hIAPP and rIAPP. In rIAPP, glucose enhanced α‐helical propensity at 125 and 500 mM, promoting secondary structure formation, although the underlying changes in secondary structure content revealed not only a modest increase in helical/turn content but also a more substantial increase in β‐sheet content (the latter was not observed at 250 or 500 mM). This shift toward ordered structures suggests that glucose may stabilize more ordered conformations of rIAPP. In the case of hIAPP, glucose at 125 mM by contrast decreased α‐helical propensity, particularly within the amyloidogenic core (residues 20–29), an effect that became more pronounced as the concentration increased to 250 and 500 mM. At the same time, the underlying changes in secondary structure content showed a progressive increase in random coil and β‐sheet content (the latter in the amyloidogenic core) at the expense of helical and turn content at concentrations of 125 and 250 mM, although the effect was mostly reversed at 500 mM. These conformational shifts indicate a destabilizing effect of glucose on the helical regions of hIAPP, potentially encouraging the stabilization of metastable intermediate conformations with decreased α‐helical structure but increased β‐sheet structure, or otherwise decreasing the transition from random coil to α‐helix, thereby decreasing aggregation.

Several studies on the influence of glycerol on IDPs suggest that it also has a concentration‐dependent effect [58, 109, 110]. A CD spectroscopy study on Aβ42 showed that at increasing high concentrations of 3 to 6 M, glycerol increased the rate of β‐sheet formation and promoted the formation of Aβ42 aggregates [109]. However, another CD spectroscopy study of the hIAPP 20–29 fragment in 20% glycerol (v/v) indicated a potential inhibitory effect on aggregation by reducing the formation of β‐sheet structures [54]. A fluorescence spectroscopy study of the effect of glycerol on the aggregation of hIAPP proposed that at concentrations of 3 to 4 M, glycerol has an inhibitory effect, while at higher concentrations (6 M) it may induce aggregation [111]. In our simulations glycerol exerted its largest effect on rIAPP at a concentration of 250 mM, increasing α‐helical propensity, while at lower (125 mM) and higher (500 mM) concentrations there was a reduction in α‐helical propensity. The underlying changes in secondary structure were only substantial at a concentration of 250 mM, with substantial increases in α‐helix, turn and β‐sheet content, primarily in the C‐terminal region. This suggests that glycerol promotes multiple ordered conformations in rIAPP. In the case of hIAPP, glycerol first increased slightly the α‐helical propensity in the amyloidogenic core region (residues 20–29) at a concentration of 125 mM, but then reduced it substantially at 250 mM, particularly within the amyloidogenic core. At a concentration of 500 mM this α‐helical propensity partially returned to similar levels as predicted in pure water. The underlying changes in secondary structure content confirmed that a significant reduction in α‐helical and turn content occurred at 250 mM, with a concomitant increase in β‐sheet content (the latter occurring primarily in a central region including the amyloidogenic core). This shift toward β‐sheet structures in hIAPP under the action of glycerol only at a concentration of 250 mM indicates that it can destabilize helical conformations (and promote β‐sheet conformations) in the amyloidogenic core. However, lower (125 mM) or higher (500 mM) glycerol concentrations do not appear to have a significant effect on the conformational preferences of hIAPP. The contrasting effect of the action of glycerol on rIAPP and hIAPP at a concentration of 250 mM suggests the possibility of a concentration‐dependent inhibitory effect on the aggregation of hIAPP.

CD spectroscopy and ThT fluorescence aggregation kinetics studies of α‐synuclein found that 2 M sorbitol preserved the random coil conformation of this protein with minimal shifts toward β‐sheet or α‐helical conformations. Higher sorbitol concentrations reduced fibril formation, with aggregation rates initially increasing at lower concentrations but declining at 2 M. It was suggested that the hydroxyl groups of sorbitol likely stabilize α‐synuclein in a less hydrophobic state, limiting interactions essential for aggregation and thereby inhibiting fibrillation [110]. In our simulations across all tested concentrations, sorbitol consistently reduced the α‐helical propensity in both rIAPP and hIAPP. In the case of rIAPP, the underlying changes in secondary structure were rather similar at 125 and 250 mM, with a small decrease in α‐helical content and an increase in β‐sheet content. At 500 mM the changes in secondary structure content were minimal. These findings indicate that lower concentrations of sorbitol exert a somewhat more pronounced effect on the conformational preferences of rIAPP. In the case of hIAPP, at all concentrations of sorbitol there was a substantial decrease in α‐helical content and an accompanying increase in β‐sheet content (including a large portion of the amyloidogenic core), although the latter diminished with increasing sorbitol concentration in favor of an increase in random coil content. Consequently, at 250 and 500 mM concentrations there was an increase in disordered conformations compared to the 125 mM concentration. These shifts in secondary structure propensity and content suggest a destabilization of α‐helical structures in hIAPP in the presence of sorbitol, which may result in decreased aggregation.

Computation of hydrogen‐bonding interactions, SASA, and hydrophobic interactions for all systems considered revealed the clear presence of a strong osmophobic effect and a stabilization of the hydration shell around both hIAPP and rIAPP. This was evidenced by the formation of minimal protein‐osmolyte H‐bonds and hydrophobic contacts, with rather stable intra‐protein and protein‐water hydrogen bonds across all osmolyte interactions. The latter is consistent with the small average decreases in SASA observed in both hIAPP and rIAPP at all osmolyte concentrations, suggesting any of the changes in secondary structure discussed above did not result in meaningful reductions in solvent exposure of the protein surface. It is interesting to note that a clear concentration‐dependent increasing trend in protein‐glucose H‐bonds was observed for both rIAPP and hIAPP, which may be related to the predicted increased structural stabilization of these proteins with increasing glucose concentration. A much weaker trend was predicted for rIAPP‐glycerol and rIAPP‐sorbitol H‐bonds, while no changes in hIAPP‐glycerol and hIAPP‐sorbitol H‐bonds with the increase in osmolyte concentration were observed. Visual inspection of the simulation trajectories revealed the very occasional hydrophobic interaction of the osmolytes with the proteins through their non‐polar regions, particularly for the linear molecules glycerol and sorbitol, which was confirmed by the computation of pair RDFs between non‐polar methylene/methine groups and the associated hydrophobic coordination numbers.

All three osmolytes clearly exhibit a very strong osmophobic effect in their interactions with rIAPP and hIAPP. On a per C‐OH group basis, glucose is predicted to form the highest number of H‐bonding interactions and hydrophobic contacts with both proteins, while sorbitol is predicted to form the lowest number of H‐bonding interactions and hydrophobic contacts, especially with hIAPP. This behavior is correlated with predicted decreases in α‐helical content and concomitant increases in β‐sheet and/or random coil content for all osmolytes.

It is important to emphasize that this study focused on characterizing the conformational landscape of the monomeric forms of hIAPP and rIAPP and, consequently, it did not directly investigate changes to the mechanism and kinetics of self‐aggregation upon addition of any of the osmolytes. However, changes in secondary structure propensity and content in monomers upon the addition of osmolytes are likely to impact their self‐aggregation.

Conclusions

Molecular dynamics simulations of the interaction of aqueous solutions of the stabilizing osmolytes glucose, glycerol, and sorbitol at various concentrations revealed subtle differences in the conformational propensities of rIAPP and hIAPP. Decreased helical propensity, particularly in the amyloidogenic core of hIAPP, accompanied by increases in either random coil and/or β‐sheet content, may be related to a decrease in aggregation propensity. The underlying molecular mechanism of action of these osmolytes was shown to involve a strong osmophobic effect, with very limited H‐bonding and hydrophobic interactions with any of the proteins.

In addition, an atypical (non‐linear) concentration‐dependent effect was observed with the addition of glycerol and sorbitol. Glycerol at a concentration of 250 mM destabilized α‐helical conformations and promoted β‐sheet formation in the amyloidogenic core of hIAPP. Similarly, higher concentrations of sorbitol (250 mM and 500 mM) substantially reduced α‐helical content and increased disordered conformations in hIAPP, potentially limiting its aggregation tendency.

These findings underscore the potential role of osmolyte type and concentration in modulating IDP aggregation pathways, suggesting that osmolytes may both favor specific conformational states and promote structural transitions that reduce aggregation. Future research should explore the impact of osmolytes on the self‐aggregation of hIAPP, potentially through simulations involving multiple aggregation‐prone monomers. Additionally, examining a broader range of osmolyte concentrations and incorporating other stabilizing agents could further clarify how cellular environments influence the behavior of IDPs, ultimately informing therapeutic strategies targeting amyloid aggregation in diseases like T2DM.
