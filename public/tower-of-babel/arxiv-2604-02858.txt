Random Reshuffling-Based Distributed Nash Equilibrium Seeking
Jun Hu, Chao Sun, Chen Bo, Jianzheng Wang, Zheming Wang

Abstract
This paper studies random reshuffling (RR)-based distributed Nash equilibrium seeking for noncooperative games. The game is motivated as a sample-average approximation of an underlying expected-value stochastic game, while the algorithmic focus is placed on the resulting finite-sum equilibrium problem. Unlike existing distributed stochastic Nash equilibrium methods that mainly rely on with-replacement sampling, the proposed approach incorporates without-replacement component updates into equilibrium computation over networks. We first consider a full-information benchmark, for which an intermediate reference trajectory and a shuffling variance are introduced to characterize the epoch-wise dynamics induced by RR. The method is then extended to the more practical partial-decision-information setting, where each player updates its action using local estimates of the joint action profile. For the full-information case, a descent-type bound is established for the RR iterates. For the distributed partial-decision-information case, it is shown that, under constant parameters, the proposed algorithm converges linearly to a neighborhood of the Nash equilibrium, while under diminishing parameters, it converges exactly to the Nash equilibrium almost surely and in mean square. Numerical experiments on an EV charging game and a nonquadratic edge resource admission game demonstrate that RR consistently outperforms the conventional with-replacement SGD baseline in both steady-state accuracy and long-horizon performance.

Introduction

Game theory provides a framework for modeling strategic interactions among multiple self-interested decision makers.
Among the solution concepts for noncooperative games, Nash equilibrium (NE) is one of the most fundamental, as it characterizes operating points at which no player can unilaterally improve its own objective. NE seeking has found wide applications in
communication networks
[
1
]
, smart grids
[
15
]
, resource allocation
[
9

, and multi-robot coordination
[

, where multiple agents are coupled through their decision variables and cost functions while pursuing individual objectives.
In many modern engineering systems, however, these agents are distributed via a network and can communicate only with neighbors. As a result, each player may not have direct access to the full action profile of all the others, which naturally leads to the problem of distributed NE seeking under partial-decision information.

To solve such networked games,distributed Nash equilibrium seeking has been widely studied under both full-decision and partial-decision information. In stochastic settings, players often update their strategies using noisy or sampled observations of their own objective functions, which has made stochastic-approximation and SGD-type methods a prevailing algorithmic framework.
Representative examples include:

[

[
7

developed a distributed stochastic Nash equilibrium learning algorithm for locally coupled network games with unknown parameters.

[
17

proposed a consensus-based gradient-free distributed Nash equilibrium seeking method that requires only local cost-function measurements, rather than explicit knowledge of the cost-function expressions or their gradients.

Despite these advances, existing stochastic distributed NE methods are still primarily built on stochastic-approximation and related independently sampled updates. While the distributed game literature has not yet fully separated the role of the sampling policy, related stochastic optimization and variational-inequality results indicate that, compared with without-replacement passes, independently sampled updates may induce larger steady-state neighborhoods under constant step-sizes

However, the role of random reshuffling in distributed Nash equilibrium seeking
remains largely unexplored, especially under partial-decision information.
Existing distributed stochastic NE methods mainly rely on independently sampled
stochastic approximation updates, with primary emphasis on pseudo-gradient
design, local estimation, and communication mechanisms. In contrast, the effect
of without-replacement sampling on coupled pseudo-gradient dynamics and network
disagreement has received much less attention. This motivates the present work,
where we study whether random reshuffling can serve as a principled sampling
mechanism for distributed stochastic NE seeking and improve solution accuracy
over conventional with-replacement SGD schemes.

To the best of our knowledge, random reshuffling has not been systematically
studied in distributed stochastic Nash equilibrium seeking. The main
contributions of this paper are threefold.

(1) We propose a random-reshuffling-based framework for distributed stochastic
Nash equilibrium seeking in non-cooperative games. The framework covers both a
full-information benchmark and the more practical distributed setting under
partial-decision information.

(2) We develop an epoch-wise analysis for reshuffling-based game dynamics. In
particular, we introduce an intermediate reference trajectory and a shuffling
variance to capture the cumulative error induced by without-replacement
sampling, and we further combine this characterization with the network
disagreement recursion in the distributed setting.

(3) We establish convergence guarantees for the proposed algorithms. For the
full-information case, we derive a descent-type bound for the RR iterates. For
the partial-decision-information case, we show linear convergence to a
neighborhood of the Nash equilibrium under constant parameters, and exact
convergence almost surely and in mean square under diminishing parameters.
Numerical experiments on an EV charging game and a nonquadratic edge resource
admission game show that RR consistently outperforms the with-replacement SGD
baseline in both steady-state accuracy and long-horizon performance.

2.2
random reshuffling

Unlike existing methods for solving (
2

Specifically, at the beginning of epoch

i

\{\pi_{0}^{i},\pi_{1}^{i},\dots,\pi_{m-1}^{i}\}

\{1,2,\dots,m\}

\displaystyle x_{i,k}^{\ell+1}=x_{i,k}^{\ell}-\alpha_{k}\nabla_{x_{i}}f_{i}(\mathbf{x}_{k}^{\ell};\pi_{\ell}^{i}),

\displaystyle\text{where}\quad i\in[n],\ \ell=0,1,\dots,m-1.

Key Difference between RR and SGD

Moreover, Random reshuffling is related to the full-gradient method in that both
fully exploit the finite-sum structure of the sample-average objective; see,

[
20

Remark 3

Under with-replacement SGD, the stochastic gradient at each iteration can be
viewed as an unbiased estimator of the full gradient, and the analysis is often
based on its one-step variance. This viewpoint is no longer adequate under
random reshuffling. Indeed, within one epoch, the component indices are visited
without replacement, and the inner iterates evolve along the realized
permutation; hence the sampling error is accumulated across the whole pass
rather than appearing as an independent one-step fluctuation around the full
gradient. This motivates us to characterize the residual error of reshuffling
through an epoch-wise quantity associated with the reference trajectory

\{x_{\star}^{\ell}\}_{\ell=0}^{m}

4.1
Numerical Experiments

We evaluate the proposed reshuffling-based stochastic pseudo-gradient method on two distributed Nash games under partial-decision information. In all experiments, we compare four schemes: RR with constant stepsizes, SGD with constant stepsizes, RR with diminishing stepsizes, and SGD with diminishing stepsizes. The communication/tracking recursion, feasible-set projection, initialization, and stopping horizon are kept the same for all methods, and only the stochastic pseudo-gradient oracle is changed. In particular, SGD samples local components with replacement, whereas RR randomly permutes all local samples at the beginning of each epoch and processes them without replacement. One epoch contains
m
component-gradient evaluations per agent; hence, the epoch-matched comparison adopted below is also oracle-complexity matched. The performance metric is

e_{k}=\log_{10}\!\left(\frac{\|x_{k}-x^{\star}\|}{\|x_{0}-x^{\star}\|}\right),

where
x^{\star}
denotes the Nash equilibrium. Unless otherwise stated, each curve is averaged over
20
independent runs, and the shaded region represents
\pm 1
standard deviation.

4.1.1
EV Charging Game

Figure 2:
Convergence comparison for the EV charging game under constant and diminishing stepsizes.

We first consider a data-driven EV charging game, where each player is interpreted as a regional charging aggregator and the scalar decision
x_{i}
denotes its aggregate charging level over one scheduling interval. The local objective of player
i
is modeled in finite-sum form as

f_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}f_{i}(x;\ell),

where
\ell
indexes the local historical charging scenarios. In our implementation, each local sample corresponds to one historical realization of charging demand, electricity price, and local operating condition. The component cost is chosen as

f_{i}(x;\ell)=\frac{q_{i,\ell}}{2}(x_{i}-d_{i,\ell})^{2}+b_{i,\ell}x_{i}+x_{i}\sum_{j=1}^{N}C_{ij}x_{j}.

The first term penalizes the deviation from the scenario-dependent desired charging level
d_{i,\ell}
, the second term captures the scenario-dependent marginal charging cost or incentive, and the coupling term models the interaction among aggregators induced by shared distribution-network usage. Such a formulation is consistent with sample-driven and game-theoretic EV charging models, congestion-aware pricing, and distributed gradient-based charging coordination reported in
[
12
,
13
,
4
]
.

Fig.
2
shows the convergence behavior. Since RR and SGD use the same communication/tracking mechanism and the same number of component-gradient evaluations per epoch, the performance gap can be directly attributed to the sampling rule. It is observed that RR consistently improves over with-replacement SGD under both stepsize policies. Under constant stepsizes, RR converges to a smaller residual neighborhood. Under diminishing stepsizes, RR continues to reduce the error in the long-horizon regime and attains the best terminal accuracy.

4.1.2
Edge Resource Admission Game

Figure 3:
Convergence comparison for the edge resource admission game under constant and diminishing stepsizes.

To further verify that the benefit of random reshuffling is not restricted to a quadratic benchmark, we next consider a nonquadratic edge resource admission game. In this setting, player
i
represents an edge server, and
x_{i}
denotes its admitted workload level over a scheduling window. The local objective is again written as a finite sum,

f_{i}(x)=\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}f_{i}(x;\ell),

where each local sample
\ell
corresponds to one historical service scenario, including workload intensity, local service cost, and congestion condition. We use the component function

\displaystyle f_{i}(x;\ell)=a_{i,\ell}(x_{i}\log x_{i}-x_{i})-\kappa_{i,\ell}\log(\bar{c}_{i}-x_{i})+b_{i,\ell}x_{i}

\displaystyle+d_{i,\ell}\log\!\Bigl(1+\exp\bigl(\beta_{i}x_{i}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}C_{ij}x_{j}-r_{i,\ell}\bigr)\Bigr).

Here, the entropy-type term regularizes the local admission decision, the logarithmic barrier prevents the admitted workload from approaching the service-capacity limit
\bar{c}_{i}
, the linear term captures the local operating cost, and the smooth log-sum-exp coupling term models congestion induced by neighboring servers. This modeling choice is aligned with game-theoretic and distributed resource management formulations in edge computing and vehicular edge networks
[
29
,
24
,
11
,
22
]
.

The corresponding numerical results are reported in Fig.
3
. The same qualitative conclusion is observed as in the EV charging case: RR uniformly outperforms the with-replacement SGD baseline under the same stepsize policy. Moreover, the superiority of diminishing-step RR becomes more pronounced in this nonquadratic setting, where it achieves the smallest terminal error among all four methods. This supports the claim that the advantage of reshuffling is not confined to affine or quadratic pseudo-gradient structures.

5
Conclusion

This paper investigated random reshuffling based Nash equilibrium seeking for noncooperative games. We first considered the full-information setting and then extended the method to the distributed partial-decision information case. By introducing an intermediate reference trajectory and the corresponding shuffling variance, we characterized the epoch-wise structure of RR and established its convergence properties. In particular, under constant parameters, the proposed algorithm converges linearly to a neighborhood of the Nash equilibrium, while under diminishing parameters, exact convergence to the Nash equilibrium is achieved almost surely and in mean square. Numerical results further showed that RR consistently outperforms SGD with replacement under both oracle-complexity-matched and epoch-matched comparisons. Future work will focus on relaxing the strong monotonicity requirement and extending the proposed framework to broader classes of distributed stochastic games.
