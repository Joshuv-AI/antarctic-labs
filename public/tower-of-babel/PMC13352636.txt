Silicon Flavylium Polymethine Dyes for Shortwave Infrared Imaging
Authors: Quintashia D. Wilson, Emily B. Mobley, Eric Y. Lin, Barry Y. Li, Ash S. Hua, Diankai Liu, Justin R. Caram, Ellen M. Sletten
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The shortwave infrared (SWIR, 1000–2000 nm) region offers significant advantages for in vivo fluorescence imaging, including reduced scattering, minimal autofluorescence, and deep-tissue penetration. However, the development of small-molecule fluorophores with high brightness and maximum emission above 1100 nm remains a key challenge. Here, we report a new class of silicon-containing flavylium (SiliFlav) polymethine dyes that address this limitation. By incorporating a silicon heteroatom into the established Flav scaffold, we achieve unprecedented bathochromic shifts in emission up to 200 nm, while maintaining respectable fluorescence quantum yields (up to 0.30%). A modular synthetic route enables diverse functionalization at the 1-, 2-, and 7-positions of the heterocycle, allowing for systematic tuning of photophysical properties and improved dye performance. The lead fluorophore, SiliFlav5, formulated into canola oil nanoemulsions, enables bright SWIR emission above 1300 nm. In vivo imaging in mice demonstrates high-resolution visualization of the vasculature and spleen, confirming the utility of SiliFlav polymethine dyes as a new platform for deep-tissue SWIR imaging.

Introduction

The shortwave infrared (SWIR, 1000–2000 nm) region of the electromagnetic spectrum has emerged as the premier region for fluorescence imaging in mammals (Figure a). − Compared to the visible (VIS, 400–700 nm) and near-infrared (NIR, 700–1000 nm) regions, the SWIR region offers reduced photon scattering, minimal autofluorescence, and deeper tissue penetration, enabling enhanced contrast and resolution in vivo. − Since the initial reports of SWIR imaging, significant progress has been made toward contrast agents that have maximum emission (λmax,em) in this region, with small-molecule fluorophores distinguished for their low toxicity and favorable pharmacokinetics. − ,,− Among these, polymethine dyes are one of the few scaffolds that readily achieve SWIR wavelengths due to their conjugated heterocycles and extendable polymethine chain (Figure a). ,,,− Additionally, their large absorption coefficients (ϵmax) and moderate fluorescence quantum yields (ϕF) produce high brightness values (ϵmax × ϕF) necessary for in vivo imaging. We showcased these advantages in 2017 with the report of Flav7, a dimethylamino flavylium heptamethine dye with λmax,em = 1045 nm and ϕF = 0.53%. As the brightest SWIR fluorophore of its time, we detected a strong signal from Flav7 micelles in the vasculature of mice, and were able to quantify the heart rate of an anesthetized mouse, exemplifying the enhanced spatiotemporal resolution obtained in the SWIR region.

Since Flav7, the number of SWIR polymethine dyes (and SWIR small-molecule fluorophores, in general) has increased considerably. We have incorporated modifications at the 2- and 7-positions of the Flav heterocycle as well as the polymethine chain to achieve bright, multicolor SWIR imaging across organic, aqueous and fluorous phases (Figure a). − Others have designed SWIR fluorophores based on FDA-approved indocyanine green (ICG), , other N-containing polymethine dyes, ,− and various xanthene architectures, − increasing both the structural diversity and clinical aptitude of this region. Despite this tremendous growth, very few small-molecule fluorophores reach λmax,em above 1100 nm, limiting their capacity for enhanced imaging in deeper SWIR windows, e.g., 1300–1450 nm, where water absorption of scattered light enhances image contrast. ,,− As such, the development of small-molecule fluorophores with λmax,em above 1100 nm provides the opportunity for improved SWIR imaging.

Design strategies to red-shift polymethine dyes can be divided into two general categories: extending the polymethine chain and modifying the heterocycle. Between these, lengthening the polymethine chain is the most straightforward approach, yielding an ∼100 nm red-shift in λmax with each vinylene addition. We recently adapted this methodology to the Flav scaffold, obtaining linear nonamethine derivatives with λmax,em spanning 1088–1188 nm and ϕF ranging from 0.15 to 0.50%. Lengthening the polymethine chain even further will impart larger bathochromic shifts, but also promotes ground-state desymmetrization. Ground-state desymmetrization is charge polarization and increased bond length alternation along the polymethine chain, which results in a broad, often bimodal, absorption spectrum, decreased brightness, and decreased fluorophore stability. − Therefore, chain extension is often paired with heterocycle modifications to increase λmax without compromising brightness and stability. Of the various heterocycle modifications known to red-shift λmax, heteroatom exchange typically provides the greatest increase. In the Flav scaffold, exchanging the oxygen heteroatom to sulfur can induce bathochromic shifts in emission up to ∼90 nm, comparable to chain extension. ,,, However, sulfur incorporation also promotes intersystem crossing and nonradiative decay, causing up to a 6-fold decrease in ϕF. , Another common heteroatom, typically employed in xanthene scaffolds, is silicon. Compared to their oxygen analogues, silicon xanthene dyes exhibit red-shifted emission (∼70–100 nm) while maintaining high ϕF. − This is an attractive quality for designing SWIR fluorophores, which inherently possess lower ϕF due to energy gap law constraints. To evaluate these effects on the polymethine scaffold, we designed the first class of silicon polymethine dyes featuring two silicon xanthene (SiXan) heterocycles. SiXan polymethine dyes exhibit an ∼100 nm red-shift in λmax,em and an ∼2-fold decrease in ϕF compared to the oxygen derivatives, demonstrating that silicon incorporation is a viable strategy in polymethine dyes. However, their broad absorption bands containing significant xanthene character and overall low brightness preclude the use of this scaffold in vivo.

Given the imaging success with flavylium polymethine dyes, we believe that silicon incorporation in the Flav scaffold has the potential to readily generate SWIR fluorophores with λmax,em above 1100 nm while retaining high ϕF. However, the current synthetic route for Flav fluorophores is not amenable to silicon incorporation. Notably, all of the Flav derivatives arise from one modular synthesis featuring a Mentzer pyrone condensation of commercially available (or synthetically accessible) aniline analogues with various β-keto esters. , Herein, we report a general synthetic method to achieve a new class of silicon flavylium (“SiliFlav”) polymethine dyes (Figure a). We prepared a panel of SiliFlav derivatives featuring modifications at the 1-, 2- and 7-positions of the heterocyclewhich are deemed the most influential in tuning photophysical propertiesas well as the polymethine chain. We used these derivatives to evaluate the effects of silicon incorporation in the Flav scaffold. Finally, we performed in vivo imaging with this new class of dyes, achieving strong detection of the vasculature and spleen above 1300 nm (Figure b).

Results and Discussion

Designing Functionalized Silicon Flavones

Generating SiliFlav fluorophores requires the synthesis of a silicon-flavone precursor that can undergo methylation and subsequent condensation with a polymethine linker (Scheme a). Importantly, a phenyl group (or any bulky, nonacidic substituent) is required at the 2-position for steric protection, and an electron-donating group is required at the 7-position to impart the positive charge. Literature examples of silicon flavones are exceedingly sparse, with only two reports of relevant benzosilinone precursors (Scheme b,c). In one report, benzosilinone derivatives functionalized at the 2-position were synthesized via palladium (Pd)-catalyzed carbonylation of hydrosilanes with various terminal alkynes. While this approach enabled the synthesis of several functionalized benzosilinones, the severe reaction conditions limited its practicality. In another report, benzosilinones were synthesized via lithium exchange of a dihalogenated precursor followed by intramolecular cyclization with dimethyl dichlorosilane (DMDCS) and subsequent oxidation. However, the benzosilinones in this work do not possess the required functionalities at the 2- and 7-positions. To address this, we designed a modular synthetic route featuring a key acetylide addition to install 2-position functionality, an intramolecular cyclization to install the 1-position silicon heteroatom, and a strategic fluorine handle to enable late-stage nucleophilic aromatic substitution (SNAr) at the 7-position with various amines (Scheme d). Additionally, this work addresses the lack of synthetically accessible silicon flavone derivatives, in contrast to the well-established flavone, 4-quinolone, and thioflavone analogues, enabling fundamental investigations of this underexplored class of compounds.

Synthesizing and Investigating SiliFlav Vinylogs

We initiated the synthesis of a functionalized silicon flavone precursor addit phenyl acetylide into ion bromo-4-fluorobenzaldehyde. Starting with this commercially available aldehyde afforded 1a in 82% yield at the multigram scale (Scheme a). With the 2-position phenyl group installed, 1a was then subjected to deoxygenation conditions with Et3SiH and trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), yielding 2a, which readily underwent regioselective hydroiodination using Et3SiH and iodine to produce 3a in a 1:3 mixture of E- and Z-isomers. Lithium–halogen exchange of isomeric 3a followed by treatment with DMDCS formed cyclized precursor 4a, which was rapidly oxidized with SeO2 to form fluorinated silicon flavone 5a.

With the 1-position silicon heteroatom installed, we then turned our attention to the advantageous fluorine handle at the 7-position of 5a. We expected this site to be sufficiently electrophilic due to the electron-withdrawing ketone and allow for late-stage SNAr with different amines. Aromatic substitution of 5a in the presence of dimethylamine and diisopropylethylamine (DIPEA) produced trace amounts of aminated silicon flavone 6a, with the major product being desilylated enone S1 (Figure S1). We suspected that S1 is formed upon reaction of 6a with the fluoride anion generated in situ, causing desilylation and subsequent ring-opening. To counteract this, we used silica gel as an additive to scavenge the reactive fluoride anions. Remarkably, this completely suppressed fluoride-promoted desilylation, exclusively generating 6a in 92% yield (Scheme b).

Achieving an appropriate functionalized silicon flavone, we set out to prepare silicon-containing polymethine dyes. We treated 6a with MeMgBr followed by an aqueous quench with 50% fluoroboric acid (HBF4), producing heterocycle 7a, but in very low yields due to poor stability (Figure S2). Based on the broad NMR spectrum and general insolubility of the resulting compound, we suspect that HBF4 promotes polymerization. Quenching with saturated NH4Cl improved solubility, but did not amend the overall stability of the resulting heterocycle. Thus, we pursued in situ formation of 7a followed by immediate subjection to dye condensation conditions. Gratifyingly, this was a successful approach, wherein acid-promoted condensation of 7a with electrophilic linkers 8–10 readily afforded pentamethine 11 (SiliFlav5), heptamethine 12 (SiliFlav7), and nonamethine 13 (SiliFlav9) in 12–31% yield after purification (Scheme b).

Having synthesized a panel of SiliFlav vinylogs, we measured their photophysical properties in dichloromethane (DCM) to evaluate the effects of silicon substitution in the Flav scaffold (Figures a,b and S3, S4) (Tables S1 and S2). The biggest advantage of silicon incorporation is its red-shifting properties, with silicon xanthene derivatives exhibiting λmax,em increases up to 100 nm. Interestingly, we observed an unprecedented 200 nm increase in λmax,em for the SiliFlav scaffold (Figure c,d), placing 10 (SiliFlav5), 11 (SiliFlav7), and 12 (SiliFlav9) among the most red-shifted vinylogs in the polymethine family (Figures S5 and S6). ,,,,,,− To further understand this substantial bathochromic shift, we performed time-dependent Hartree–Fock (TDHF@vW) calculations using a CAM-B3LYP basis set (Figures e and S7). We employ TDHF@vW for spectral calculations because long-chain polymethine dyes can be challenging for standard TD-density functional theory (TD-DFT) descriptions, , whereas the many-body-theory-based, screened-exchange TDHF@vW framework captures electron–hole interactions underlying excitonic physics with good accuracy and computational efficiency for conjugated systems. − Comparing the highest occupied molecular orbitals (HOMOs) of 11–13 to their Flav analogues, there is a noticeable increase in electron density along the polymethine chain with silicon incorporation. Since the silicon atom does not participate in π-delocalization, the electron density is shifted between the terminal nitrogen atoms, increasing the effective length of conjugation. Additionally, there is favorable σ*−π* conjugation in the lowest unoccupied molecular orbitals (LUMOs) of 11–13, resulting in a smaller energy gap (E gap) compared to their Flav analogues. While σ*−π* conjugation is often cited as the crucial orbital interaction in silicon fluorophores, , we did not observe this interaction in the SiXan polymethine dyes, suggesting that the orbital effects of silicon are not uniform across small-molecule scaffolds.

Another benefit of silicon incorporation is the ability to achieve long wavelengths while maintaining high brightness. The ϕF for 11 (0.13%), 12 (0.091%), and 13 (0.012%) are higher than most small-molecule scaffolds that emit above 1100 nm (Figures S5 and S6), with noteworthy exceptions being flavylium nonamethine derivatives. ,,,,,,,,,, We believe that advantageous electron donation from the oxygen heteroatom and reduced vibronic coupling in the excited state (evidenced by the small Stokes shift) result in the uncharacteristically high ϕF of flavylium nonamethine dyes. Considering 11–13 do not have favorable heteroatom donation, and they exhibit relatively large Stokes shifts for polymethine dyes (∼60–80 nm), their ϕF are quite respectable for this region of the SWIR. The biggest downside to these dyes is their low ϵ (37,700–118,000 M–1 cm–1). We observed similarly low ϵ for the SiXan dyes, suggesting that silicon incorporation may weaken the transition dipole moment in the polymethine dyes. However, the respectable ϕF produces brightness values for 11 and 12 (34–160 M–1 cm–1) that are on par with small-molecule fluorophores with similar λmax,em (Figure S6). We achieved these brightness values with less structural complexity than the extended xanthene dyes and rigidified polymethine dyes in this region, enabling the development of more complex SiliFlav derivatives to investigate structure–property relationships that affect the ϕF of this scaffold (vide infra).

Achieving Enhanced SiliFlav5 Derivatives

To access enhanced SiliFlav fluorophores, we returned our attention to fluorinated precursor 5a to expand the scope of the SNAr reaction (Scheme a). Modulating the 7-position of flavin fluorophores has enabled brighter, red-shifted probes with enhanced solubility. By leveraging the SNAr reaction, these advantageous functionalities can be installed late stage, increasing the utility of this synthetic route. We found that SNAr substitution of 5a with small amines readily occurred, affording dimethylamine-d 6 6b, pyrrolidine 6c, and azetidine S2 using the same conditions as 6a (Figure S8). For bulkier amines, we found that SNAr substitution of 5a with allylamine to S3, followed by deprotection to S4 and substitution expanded the scope of silicon flavones (e.g., S5, Figure S8). All of the modified silicon flavones were stable to methylation, generating heterocycles 7b, 7c, S6, and S8, but the condensation with 8 proved to be variable. In the case of S7 (Azet-SiliFlav5), we suspect that the 4-membered ring is too strained to stabilize the positive charge, leading to fluorophore degradation (Figure S8). In the case of S9 (F104-SiliFlav5), the condensation proceeded smoothly, but the fluorophore began to degrade during purification (Figure S8). Fortunately, 7b and 7c were stable to condensation with 8, readily affording pentamethine dyes 14 (SiliFlav5-d 12) and 15 (Py-SiliFlav5) (Scheme b).

Beyond the 7-position, the 1- and 2-positions are also synthetically accessible sites that permit fine-tuning of λmax and brightness. Starting with the Li–halogen exchanged intermediate of 3a, we found that cyclization with methylphenyl dichlorosilane to yield 4b, followed by oxidation to 5b and SNAr substitution to 6e, occurred with comparable efficiency to the dimethylsilane analogues. Methylation of 6d to give 7d followed by condensation with 8 also proceeded smoothly, yielding silicon functionalized pentamethine 16 (Sili­(MePh)­Flav5). Unlike the previous modifications, substituting the 2-position required us to return to the beginning of the synthetic route. Fortunately, we found that the tert-butyl substituent was well-tolerated throughout the entire synthesis, affording compounds 1b–6e with negligible changes to the reaction conditions, purification methods, or obtained yields. Methylation of 6e to yield 7e followed by condensation with 8 afforded the final pentamethine of the lineup, silicon chromenylium 17 (SiliChrom5). In total, we prepared seven silicon flavylium fluorophores modified at the 1-, 2-, and 7-positions using this modular synthetic route.

To determine the effects of substituent modification on ϕF, we measured the photophysical properties of 14–17 in DCM (Figures a,b and S4) (Tables S1 and S3). Additionally, their fluorescence lifetimes (τ) were obtained using time-correlated single-photon counting (TCSPC), and the rates of radiative (k r) and nonradiative (k nr) decay were used to determine their contributions to the change in ϕF relative to 11 (Figure b,c). Based on previous work with deuterated Flav dyes, we expected 14 (SiliFlav5-d 12) to have a higher ϕF due to the suppression of nonradiative C–H vibrational frequencies. , Gratifyingly, 14 has a higher ϕF (0.17%) compared to 11, with the largest contribution coming from an increase in k r. Another strategy to increase ϕF is to inhibit twisted intramolecular charge-transfer (TICT) in the excited state through rigidification. Interestingly, we found that 15 (Py-SiliFlav5) has a lower ϕF (0.10%), despite having a longer τ (43 ps). Since the λmax of 15 is longer than 11, it is possible that the advantages of rigidification and TICT suppression are counteracted by the effects of the energy gap law, producing an overall lower ϕF. Moving to other sites of the SiliFlav scaffold, we found that the 1-position phenyl substituent in 16 (Sili­(MePh)­Flav5) caused an ∼30 nm red-shift in λmax,em, but a small decrease in ϕF (0.12%) compared to 11. These results indicate that the 1-position could be useful for installing functional handles without disrupting the properties of the fluorophore core. In contrast, the 2-position tert-butyl substituent in 17 (SiliChrom5) produced an ∼40 nm blue-shift in λmax,em and a 2-fold increase in ϕF (0.30%) compared to 11. This is consistent with the trends observed for the Flav versus Chrom heterocycles, in which the bulky tert-butyl group suppresses internal conversion, leading to a decrease in k nr. Overall, the structure–property relationships observed for Flav fluorophores generally translate to the SiliFlav scaffold, making the design and optimization of future SiliFlav derivatives a promising endeavor.

In Vivo Investigations with SiliFlav5

After elucidating the photophysical properties of SiliFlav fluorophores, we assessed the utility of this scaffold for in vivo imaging. We employed 11 for imaging studies, as it could be readily excited with a standard 1060 nm laser, an exceptionally low-energy excitation for a pentamethine dye. Previous work with SWIR-emissive silicon xanthene dyes suggests that canola oil nanoemulsions are required for prolonged stability of silicon fluorophores in vivo. Considering the general hydrophobicity of 11 alongside its concerning stability in the presence of methanol and biological nucleophiles (Figure S9), a nanoemulsion formulation seemed necessary for imaging. For such formulations, solubility of the dye in the oil phase is often enabled by counterion pairing with more lipophilic, weakly coordinating anions. Accordingly, we exchanged the strongly coordinating tetrafluoroborate counterion for the more lipophilic, weakly coordinating tetrakis­[3,5-bis­(trifluoromethyl)­phenyl]­borate (BArF24) ion. Sonicating 11 in a biphasic solution of DCM and aqueous NaBArF24 followed by column chromatography afforded counterion-exchanged 11·BArF 24 in 87% yield (Figure a). We then measured the photophysical properties of 11·BArF 24 and were pleased to find that the λmax remained unaffected by the bulky counterion while the ϕF increased to 0.17% (Figures b and S10). Additionally, 11·BArF 24 exhibited excellent solubility across a range of solvents (Figure S10). In our previous investigation of a fluorous pentamethine dye, we observed an increase in photostability with counterion exchange. To determine if the effects were similar for the SiliFlav5 scaffold, we continuously irradiated 11 and 11·BArF 24 at 786 nm for 5 min and measured the loss in emission over time (Figures c and S11). We observed a 25% decrease in signal intensity for 11 and a 10% decrease for 11·BArF 104 , representing a 2-fold increase in relative photostability after counterion exchange. Comparing their photostability to Flav5, 11 exhibits comparable stability while 11·BArF 24 is ∼2-fold more stable (Figure S11). To further investigate possible photodegradation pathways, we also performed comparative photobleaching studies of 14 (SiliFlav5-d 12) and 15 (Py-SiliFlav5) to probe whether photoinduced dealkylation contributes to fluorophore degradation (Figure S11). We observed a slight increase in photostability for 15 and a decrease for 14 compared to 11, suggesting that dealkylation is not the primary pathway of photodegradation. Overall, these results showcase that the photostability of the SiliFlav scaffold is sufficient for in vivo imaging, with 11·BArF 24 boasting enhanced stability.

To formulate 11·BArF 24 into canola nanoemulsions, we first prepared empty nanoemulsions by sonicating canola oil in a solution of Pluronic F-68 surfactant and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) (Figures d and S12). The empty nanoemulsions were then rocked with a solution of dye in acetone for 1 h and concentrated to afford 11·BArF 24 nanoemulsions with an average size of ∼210 nm and a polydispersity of ∼0.026. This approach was not successful with unexchanged 11, further illustrating the effects of counterion exchange on fluorophore utility (Figure S12). We assessed the chemical stability of 11·BArF 24 emulsions in biological settings by incubating them in fetal bovine serum (FBS) at 37 °C and monitoring the decrease in monomer absorption over time (Figure e). We found that 11·BArF 24 exhibited adequate chemical stability under these conditions, retaining over 60% monomer absorption after 90 min. Additionally, 11·BArF 24 nanoemulsions showed strong SWIR emission in FBS, with a detectable signal up to 1700 nm (Figure S12).

We evaluated nanoemulsions containing 0.5 and 1 mg of 11·BArF 24 to assess the effect of dye loading on optical performance under formulation conditions (Figure S12). We observed that doubling the fluorophore concentration resulted in an approximately 1.6-fold increase in brightness rather than a strictly linear response. This behavior suggests that although higher dye loading increases overall fluorescence intensity, the enhancement is partially offset by concentration-dependent effects within the nanoemulsion. Ultimately, the 0.5 mg formulation was selected for in vivo studies because it provided a strong signal intensity while requiring less material.

After evaluating the stability of the SiliFlav scaffold in vitro, we assessed its toxicity in cellulo. A375 cells were incubated with empty nanoemulsions and 11·BArF 24 nanoemulsions at varying concentrations, and their viability was assessed after 24 h using the MTT assay (Figures f and S13). The cells exhibited good viability (75–95%) for both the empty and loaded nanoemulsions at relevant in vivo concentrations. The 11·BArF 24 nanoemulsions exhibited comparable viability to empty nanoemulsions, suggesting negligible toxicity from the dye itself. With these promising results, 11·BArF 24 nanoemulsions were intravenously injected into 7-week-old nude mice and immediately imaged using 1060 nm excitation (Figure g). We imaged the mice using both 1200 nm long-pass (LP) and 1300 nm LP filters. While more signal was obtained in 1200 nm LP images (Figures S14–S15), the hallmark high resolution of the SWIR region is evident in the 1300 nm LP images. From the dorsal view, we detected excellent labeling of subcutaneous veins in the head and back, as well as a strong signal in the spleen. The spleen is also very apparent in the lateral left view. The lateral right and ventral views showcase additional vasculature labeling as well as the liver. With time, the vasculature signal diminishes and strong emission from the spleen is apparent, characteristic of the biodistribution of ∼200 nm nanoemulsions (Figures S14–S15). After 2 days, ex vivo analysis confirms near-exclusive localization to the spleen (Figure S16). Taken together, these imaging results show that SiliFlav formulations are capable of serving as bright, stable, red-shifted probes for high-resolution SWIR imaging above 1300 nm.

Conclusions

In this work, we report the development of a new class of SWIR polymethine fluorophores based on a silicon flavylium (SiliFlav) scaffold. Through the incorporation of a silicon heteroatom into the established Flav framework, we achieved unprecedented red-shifts in emission up to 200 nm, placing the SiliFlav scaffold among the most red-shifted small-molecule fluorophores to date. Importantly, these fluorophores retain respectable ϕF, demonstrating that silicon substitution can effectively tune optical properties without fully compromising brightness, even in the challenging SWIR region. We developed a modular and scalable synthetic route that enables diversification at the 1-, 2-, and 7-positions of the SiliFlav core. This strategy not only allowed us to explore structure–property relationships in detail but also led to several enhanced derivatives with improved photophysical performance. Notably, the 2-tert-butyl derivative (17, SiliChrom5) exhibited a 2-fold increase in ϕF, while deuteration at the 7-position (14, SiliFlav5-d 12) improved radiative decay rates. These findings suggest that many of the design principles established for flavylium dyes are transferable to the silicon scaffold, offering a clear roadmap for future optimization. We also demonstrated the practical utility of this scaffold for in vivo imaging. A nanoemulsion formulation of counterion-exchanged SiliFlav5 (11·BArF 24 ) provided excellent chemical stability, low toxicity, and good signal using a 1300 nm LP filter. Following intravenous injection in mice, this fluorophore formulation enabled high-resolution imaging of the vasculature and spleen, with ex vivo analysis confirming organ-specific accumulation consistent with the expected biodistribution of nanoemulsions. Overall, this work establishes silicon flavylium dyes as a promising new platform for bright, red-shifted SWIR imaging. The modular synthetic approach, combined with the favorable photophysical and biological performance, opens the door to a new generation of functional small-molecule probes for deep-tissue, high-resolution imaging in the SWIR window.
