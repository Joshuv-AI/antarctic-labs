Subcutaneous Sacrococcygeal Lipoma in a 7 Months Old Female Mimicking Myelomeningocele and Sacrococcygeal Teratoma, A Case Report
Authors: Rajab Msemo, Boniphace Marwa, Mussa Binge, Nanyokye Kidoko, Neema Bayyo, Mathayo Shadrack, Rashid Mwatebela, Ummehani Akberali, Ally Mwanga, Mohammed Sultan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 7‐month‐old female presented with a congenital sacrococcygeal mass. While SCT and MMC are common, MRI ruled them out, leading to simple excision. Histopathology revealed a rare subcutaneous sacrococcygeal lipoma. This case emphasizes the use of advanced imaging to differentiate benign soft‐tissue lesions from spinal or neoplastic pathologies in infants.

Introduction

Congenital masses arising in the sacrococcygeal region represent a broad differential diagnosis, where prompt identification of the lesion type is crucial for determining prognosis and management [1]. The overwhelming majority of these masses are either Sacrococcygeal Teratoma (SCT), often malignant or containing significant intrapelvic components, or Myelomeningocele (MMC), a form of spinal dysraphism requiring neurosurgical management [2]. The clinical presentation of a smooth, mobile mass, while sometimes overlapping with these common diagnoses, can occasionally mask rarer, benign lesions [3]. We present a case of a sacrococcygeal mass in a 7‐month‐old infant successfully managed by Simple Complete Excision at a tertiary teaching hospital which, through advanced imaging, was determined to be neither SCT nor MMC, ultimately confirmed to be sacrococcygeal lipoma on histopathological evaluation.

Case History and Physical Examination

A 7‐month‐old female infant was referred to Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH) with a chief complaint of a slowly progressive swelling in the sacrococcygeal area, first noted by the parents shortly after birth. There was no history of trauma, fever, or constitutional symptoms. The mass did not demonstrate rapid growth.

On physical examination, a soft, smooth‐surfaced mass measuring approximately 14 cm wide was observed in the midline, superior to the intergluteal cleft. The mass was notably mobile, with a smooth surface, soft, non‐tender to palpation, and the overlying skin was intact with no discoloration, sinus tracts, or tufts of hair (Figure 1). Digital rectal examination was unremarkable. Neurological examination of the lower limbs was unremarkable, and anal tone was normal.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Initial investigations commenced with imaging to rule out the primary differentials. MRI of the lumbosacral spine demonstrated a well‐circumscribed, superficial soft‐tissue mass confined to the subcutaneous plane, completely separate from the coccyx, sacrum, and the underlying dura/spinal canal (Figure 2). The conus medullaris was noted to be in its normal anatomical position. These findings provided definitive exclusion of both SCT and MMC.

MRI demonstrates a large, well‐defined encapsulated subcutaneous mass within the sacrococcygeal/posterior pelvic region, measuring approximately 10.4 × 13.4 cm. The lesion shows uniform fatty signal characteristics, with homogeneous hyperintensity on T1 (A) and T2 (B & F)‐weighted sequences and complete signal suppression on fat‐saturated imaging (D), confirming its lipomatous composition. Post‐contrast sequences (C & E) reveal no internal, septal, or nodular enhancement. No non‐adipose soft‐tissue elements, thickened septa, cystic degeneration, calcification, or other suspicious features are identified. The mass remains confined to the subcutaneous compartment without extension into the presacral space or spinal canal. Surrounding pelvic organs are preserved, with no evidence of local invasion. Smooth osseous remodeling of the sacrum and coccyx is present, consistent with long‐standing mass effect, without cortical breach or aggressive bony destruction. Overall imaging features are in keeping with a benign lipomatous lesion.

Following a multidisciplinary team review, the mass was planned for surgical excision under general anesthesia. A wide local excision was performed, and the mass was easily dissected from the surrounding subcutaneous fat and underlying fascia. The procedure was uncomplicated, and the wound was closed primarily. The resected specimen was sent for histopathological examination. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, and the patient was discharged on the third postoperative day with favorable wound healing. The final histopathological report confirmed the mass to be a lipoma, characterized by mature adipocytes surrounded by a thick capsule (Figure 3).

Outcome and Follow Up

The patient discharged 1 week following surgery without any complications. At 6‐month follow‐up, the infant was thriving with no signs of recurrence or neurological deficit.

Discussion

The differential diagnosis of a sacrococcygeal mass requires a systematic approach, led by clinical evaluation and imaging [3]. SCTs are the most common tumors of this region, varying widely in presentation (from benign solid masses to highly vascular malignant tumors) and necessitating a detailed assessment of intrapelvic extension [4]. The smooth, soft, and mobile nature of our patient's mass, combined with the normal neurological status, suggested a benign tumor with a low risk of neurogenic involvement. However, imaging was paramount.

The MRI findings—specifically the intact spinal cord and absence of sacral bony defects—effectively ruled out MMC and other forms of occult spinal dysraphism. Furthermore, the homogeneous fatty signal characteristics and the complete absence of mixed tissue densities, calcifications, or fluid components on the imaging sequences strongly argued against SCT [1].

The eventual diagnosis of subcutaneous sacrococcygeal lipoma (SSL) is extremely rare. While sacrococcygeal tumors as a broader category occur in approximately 1 in 27,000 to 40,000 live births (roughly 0.025 to 0.037 per 1000 live births), pure SSL is an exceptionally rare subset, often classified under the miscellaneous type accounting for only about 15%–20% of all sacrococcygeal tumors [2]. SSL is a benign lesion found in the midline sacrococcygeal subcutaneous plane posterior to the sacrum comprising adipose tissues [5].

Clinically SSL presents with a spontaneous slowly growing Pre sacral mass, soft with smooth surface, mobile and nontender with no palpable mass on digital rectal examination and intact distal neurological status [3]. SL are best diagnosed using imaging modalities preferably like MRI of Lumbosacral spine which will show a distinct mass posterior to the sacrum with regular margin and no involvement of adjacent structures, in addition images are useful in ruling out other causes of sacrococcygeal masses [1].

The management is by observation or simple complete excision [1]. Simple complete excision is recommended in symptomatic tumors or with a diameter of more than 8 cm, which is associated with a risk of malignant transformation [1, 5]. Following this, a tissue specimen should be submitted for histopathological evaluation to rule out the presence of malignancy [5]. This case serves as an important reminder that while SCT and MMC dominate the differential, rare benign lesions must be considered when imaging exclusions are clear.

Conclusion

We report the successful diagnosis and management of a rare Subcutaneous sacrococcygeal lipoma presenting as a sacrococcygeal mass in a 7‐month‐old infant. The case underscores the essential nature of preoperative MRI and CT in narrowing the differential diagnosis, especially in excluding the serious diagnoses of Myelomeningocele and Sacrococcygeal Teratoma, thereby guiding appropriate surgical planning and ensuring a favorable outcome.
