 Abstract
 The goal of this internship is to develop a fluid dynamics characterization of the
high-pressure flow used for cleaning the internal surface of the SRF cavities. Initially,
the High Pressure Rinsing (HPR) process is introduced, focusing on the problems
of the SRF cavities and how this method can be used to increase their acceleration
efficiency and performance. The characterization of the problem is then presented,
including an introduction to the multiphase flow modeling and the analysis of the
Fermilab facility for the HPR. To accomplish the goal, a preliminary estimation
of the variables was performed using MATLAB. Then, multiple 2D simulations
were developed with Ansys Fluent, and the influence of the main parameter was
investigated. The simulation results are then presented and analysed, with a focus on
the possible future developments of the HPR process fluid dynamic characterization.

 Field Emission and High-Pressure Rinsing Process

In this chapter, the High-Pressure rinse will be introduced, with a focus on the pro-
cess methodology, the main challenges, and the weak points. To better understand
the utility of this process for the production of the SRF cavities, a brief introduction
to the field emission problem is provided in the following section.

1.1 Field Emission
Field emission is one of the biggest problems affecting SRF cavities in particle accel-
erators. Field emission is a physical phenomenon in which electrons are extracted
from a solid material under the influence of a strong applied electric field. Un-
like thermoionic emission, this process does not require heating; instead, it is a
quantum-mechanical process in which electrons tunnel through the material’s po-
tential barrier.
 Once a very intense electric field is applied (normally < 108 V /m) over a conduc-
tive surface, the potential barrier holding the electrons decreases its efficiency, and
two of the following phenomena can occur:

 • Quantum Tunneling: the electrons near to the surface can pass through the
 potential barrier even if they do not have enough energy to do it classically.

 • Vacuum Emission: the electrons can separate and move away from the
 metallic surface into the vacuum area around the metal. In SRF cavities, in
 proximity to the niobium surface, a noble gas is used for cryogenic applications,
 and it is pumped to a very low pressure value.

 In the SRF cavities, the field emission is an active problem since dust parti-
cles, microscopic imperfections, high surface roughness, impurities, or contaminants
create intensification points for the electric field. These superficial imperfections
generate an unwanted electron emission called dark emission. When the emitted

1.2 High-Pressure Rinsing (HPR)
In the High-Pressure Rinsing process, a high-pressure and velocity liquid jet is used
to clean the internal surfaces of the cavities.
 The liquid usually used for the HPR process in clean room applications is ul-
trapure water. The resistivity of the water should be close to theoretically pure
(> 18M Ohm − cm), and the water inlet should be filtered to eliminate particles
with an average diameter > 0.3µm [4].

 Figure 1.3: HPR system [3]

 Fig 1.3 shows a scheme with the relevant components of the HPR system used
for SRF applications:

 • Ultrapure Water System: It is usually a vessel that contains pre-prepared
 ultrapure water. The ultrapure water is maintained inside the tank at ambient
 pressure. The vessel is designed to avoid any external contamination of the
 ultrapure water.

9 1.2. High-Pressure Rinsing (HPR)

 • High Pressure Pump: thanks to this pump, the ultrapure water is pumped
 at a high level of pressure inside the ducts. Typical values for operative pres-
 sure are around 80-100 bar.
 • Flowmeter: it controls the mass flow rate of ultrapure water that can be
 ejected from the HPR system.
 • Filter: After the ultrapure water has passed through the pump and the flow
 meter, the filter removes any contaminants that may come from the previous
 stages.
 • Drive System: the relative motion between the nozzle and the SRF cavity
 must be maintained to have an efficient cleaning process. The drive system
 can be applied both to the HPR system and the SRF cavity.
 • Wand: it is a small rigid duct that can be inserted inside the SRF cavity.
 Due to its rigidity, the wand easily reaches parts of the internal surface that
 could be difficult to clean because of the cavity geometry.
 • Nozzle: the wand finishes with the nozzle head. Usually, the nozzle is a
 hollow element screwed on the end of the wand, and it has several holes with
 different dimensions and inclinations with respect to the wand axis.
 • Aspirator: it is used to remove water from the SRF cavities. It is not a
 mandatory element, since the SRF cavities can be moved and inclined in mul-
 tiple directions to drain the water. Moreover, after the HPR process, the
 niobium cavities complete an oven cycle to let all the residual water particles
 evaporate.

 The importance of the HPR can be summarised in these 2 bullet points:

 • It ensures thorough cleaning of the inner high-purity niobium surface of SRF
 cavities. [5]

 • It unlocks the full potential of SRF cavities for achieving peak performance. [5]

 The factors that describe the efficiency of the pressure rinse process are:

  1. Angle between the water jet and normal vector to the cavity sur-
 face: angle of incidence plays a pivotal role in the cleaning process, where
 certain angles may enhance the rinse effectiveness, while others may lead to
 suboptimal results [5].

  2. Rinsing time: it is the duration for which specific areas are subjected to a
 high-pressure water jet. It is a critical parameter that determines the force
 exerted by the water jet, and consequently, the cleaning efficiency [5].

  3. Distance from nozzle exit area and the surface at the point of impact:
 as the rinsing time, it also determines the force exerted by the water jet and
 the cleaning efficiency [5].

 One of the main challenges for obtaining a high cleaning efficiency with the
HPR process regards the complex geometry of the cavities. In particular, due to the
sophisticated geometry, the visible total surface area that can be directly impinged
by the water jet during the process decreases. Besides, at the Applied Physics and
Superconducting Technology Department in Fermilab, the HPR process has always
been modeled as a ray-tracing problem [5]. With this assumption, the water jet is
modeled as a beam of rays. At each iteration of the simulation, the impingement
point of each ray can be calculated, since the geometry of the internal surface of the
SRF cavity is discretized using mesh tools. From the simulation, for each element

2.4 Atomization Process Study
In a liquid injected at high velocity through a small orifice, atomization is the process
by which the injected liquid is broken into droplets.
 Atomization has a strong influence on spray vaporization rates because it in-
creases the total surface area of the injected liquid. Fast vaporization is desirable
in some applications, but it is undesirable in HPR cleaning process, where the liq-
uid is required to impinge on the surface target. The injection process defines the
trajectories of the spray drops, the momentum of the drop, drag forces, interactions
between the drops and the surrounding gas.

19 2.4. Atomization Process Study

 Atomization is generally achieved by forcing a liquid or a liquid-gas mixture
through a small hole or slit under pressure to create thin liquid sheets or jets moving
at high relative velocity with respect to the surrounding ambient gas.
 Atomizers can be broadly characterized depending on the type of spray they
produce, in particular:

 • Solid cone sprays: the spray liquid is concentrated along the spray axis. It
 is useful in applications requiring high spray penetration.

 • Hollow cone spray: the axis region is relatively free of drops, giving wide
 spray dispersal. They are often used in furnaces, gas turbines, and spray-
 coating applications.

 Multiphase Flow

The HPR process is described by a high-velocity jet of Ultra Pure Water which is
moving in the ambient air inside the SRF cavity. For this reason, the problem can
be described using multiphase flow dynamics. In this chapter, a small introduction
to the theory of the multiphase flow will be presented. After this introduction, a
brief explanation of how multiphase flow physics is implemented in Ansys Fluent is
provided.

3.1 Theory of Multiphase Flow
A phase refers to states of matter which are generally classified as solid, liquid
and gases. The behaviour of a single phase can be described using Navier-Stokes
equation:

 • Continuity equation

 • Momentum equation

 • Energy equation

 A multiphase flow is a simultaneous flow of a mixture of phases, such as gases
(bubbles) in liquid or liquid (droplets) in gases. Multiphase flows are found in many
industrial applications (like chemical reactors or process flow industries) and natural
phenomena (rivers or cloud formation). In real applications, it is difficult to isolate
a single phase from a mixture; it is more practical to study the entire phenomenon
composed of multiple phases considered as a single flow.
 While working with multiphase flows, a particular terminology must be taken
into account:

 • Continuous/Primary phase: this is the principal phase, the carrier one.

 The types of multiphase flows can be classified as:
 1. Dispersed phase flow: one of the two phases in the form of a discrete
 element. There is no connection between individual discrete phase elements,
 which may be in the form of particles or droplets. This is the case of droplets in
 gas or bubbles in liquid. In this flow, the particle motion is mainly controlled
 by the fluid forces (drag and lift).
 2. Separated flow: the 2 phases are separated by a distinct line of contact. It
 is basically possible to travel from one location to another in the same phase
 and remain in the same medium.
 3. Gas-liquid flows: one phase is a gas, and the other is in a liquid state. They
 can be in different forms, like bubbly flow or annular flow.
 4. Gas-solid flow: in this flow, the solid phase is generally suspended in the gas
 phase. This is the case of granular flows, where particle-particle and particle-
 wall interactions are more important than the forces due to the interstitial
 gas.
 5. Liquid-solid flow: the solid particles are carried by the liquid phase. This
 type of flow is also called slurry flow.
 6. Three-phase flow: in this type of flow, all 3 types of phases are involved at
 the same time. This is the case of bubbles in a slurry flow, which give rise to
 three phases flowing together.
 7. Dense flow: in this flow, collisions play a major role in controlling particle
 motion. Under dense flows also there are further classifications:
 • Collision dominated: collisions between the particles control the fea-
 tures of the flow.
 • Contact dominated: the particle motion is controlled by continuous
 contact.

3.2 Multiphase Flow Modeling
Two types of specifications can be used while modeling flows:

 • Eulerian specification: it focuses on specific locations in space through
 which fluid flows in time.

 • Lagrangian specification: the observer follows an individual fluid parcel as
 it moves through space and time.

 Figure 3.1: Representation of different types of specification

 There are several approaches to model the multiphase flow, considering different
types of combinations in the choice of the specification method. These approaches
are reported and analysed in the following paragraphs.

3.2.1 Eulerian-Lagrangian Approach
For this approach, a combination of the 2 specification methods is used. In particu-
lar, the Eulerian framework is used to model the continuous phase. The trajectories
of the dispersed phase are calculated by solving the equation of motion. Indeed, the
Lagrangian approach is used for the dispersed phase. The volume displacement due
to the dispersed phase motion is ignored, and the momentum and continuity equa-
tions are solved for each element of the continuous phase. The Eulerian-Lagrangian
approach is characterized by the coupling of the momentum and energy equations
between the particles and continuous flow. It is suitable for a very low volume
fraction of the dispersed phase.
 Fig. 3.2 shows a representation of the Eulerian-Lagrangian approach.

 Figure 3.2: Representation of Eulerian-Lagrangian approach

3.2.2 Eulerian-Eulerian Approach

In this configuration, the Eulerian equations is used to solve all the phases. Within
the Eulerian approach, the dispersed phase is also treated as a continuum. In par-
ticular, the control volume is used to define the phase velocities, while the equations
are solved considering the phase volume fraction. This type of approach is suitable
for high dispersed phase volume loading (< 10%). The coupling between the phases
is represented by various interphase transport models, in which viscous and fluid
dynamic forces are considered.
 Fig. 3.3 shows a representation of the Eulerian-Eulerian approach.

 Figure 3.3: Representation of Eulerian-Eulerian approach

3.2.3 Volume of Fluid Approach
In this type of approach, the Eulerian framework is used for both phases with
specialized interface treatment. The dispersed phase is completely separated from
the continuous phase. In particular, the flow around individual dispersed particles
is resolved. All phases share a single set of governing equations that use combined
mixture properties. The volumetric forces and the interface tracking between the
2 phases are included in the model. This approach is suitable for stratified and
separated flows, in which a distinct interface can be defined.

3.3 Multiphase Flow Model in Ansys Fluent
In Ansys Fluent, there are 4 different types of models for modeling multiphase flow:

 • Discrete Phase Model (DPM): this model uses the Eulerian-Lagrangian
 approach. This is suitable for bubbly, droplet, and particle-laden flows with
 the volume fractions less than or equal to 10%. In particular, this model can
 be used for very low or intermediate loading, where the coupling between the
 phases is one-way or two-way.

 • Mixture Model: this model uses the Eulerian-Eulerian approach. This
 model is suitable for bubbly, droplet, and particle-laden flows in which the
 phases mix and/or dispersed-phase volume fractions exceed 10%. As for the
 DPM, the Mixture model can be used for very low or intermediate loading
 flows, where the coupling between the phases is one-way or two-way.

 • Volume of Fluid Model (VOF): this model uses the Volume of Fluid model.
 This is the typical choice to model slug or stratified/free-surface flows.

29 3.3. Multiphase Flow Model in Ansys Fluent

 • Eulerian Multiphase Flow Model: this model also uses the Eulerian-
 Eulerian approach. The difference from the Mixture Model is that the Eulerian
 Model is more suitable for high loading flow, and when the pressure and viscous
 stress between particles must be taken into account. Typical examples of
 applications for the Eulerian Model are:

 – Bubbly or droplets flow in which the dispersed-phase volume fraction
 exceeds 10%.
 – Fluidized beds.
 – Slurry flows and hydro-transport.
 – Sedimentation.

 After several attempts and trade-off considerations, the model used for the HPR
simulations is the Eulerian Multiphase Flow Model. The main characteristics of the
model chosen are reported in the following bullet points:

 • Mass, momentum, and energy equations are solved for each phase.

 • Energy equations are coupled between the 2 phases.

 • Momentum and continuity equations are solved separately for each phase.

 Increasing the relative angle between the flow axis and the normal vector to

contour plots, it is visible how the flow is mostly contained inside the upper part of
the axis while the relative angle increases. The inclination of the surface moves the

simulations, since the rotation of the surface wall is taken clockwise, the impingement

turbulence increases. In particular, the turbulence is higher in the surface portion

the impact point, since a phenomenon of recirculating flow begins because of the flow
asymmetry. The static pressure maximum value on the wall, which is reached at the
impact point, decreases as the surface inclination increases. In particular, the static
pressure profile also enlarges as soon as the relative angle increases. This means
that the impact area on the wall where the static pressure is different from zero
increases. In other words, for a higher surface inclination, the cleaning efficiency is
lower, but the directly impacted wall area is higher with respect to the purely axial
flow.

 Conclusion

In this report, a fluid dynamics characterization of the flow for HPR cleaning process
has been performed. In particular, a preliminary estimation of the variable has been
developed using MATLAB. After that, different configurations have been simulated
with Ansys Fluent. In particular, the simulations had the goal of understanding how
different parameters influence the dynamics of the flow and the cleaning efficiency.
The main parameters that have been investigated are:

 • Nozzle-wall distance

 • Pressure

 • Relative angle between flow axis and normal vector to the wall surface

 • Hole diameter

 The results obtained with the MATLAB analysis have been used as a starting
point for the input information needed for the Ansys setup. In particular, with the
chosen setup, the best input for easy convergence is the velocity inlet. The velocity
input defined for the Ansys simulation is the one obtained by combining the infor-
mation of the volumetric flow rate used at Fermilab facilities and the results of the
MATLAB code. The variation of the mass flow rate has been considered only in
the group of simulations concerning the variation of the hole diameters. Indeed, the
velocity inlet has been assumed constant while the diameter was changing, and to
satisfy the condition, the mass flow rate must vary. For the other group of simula-
tions, the mass flow rate was assumed to be constant, since both the velocity and
the nozzle diameter were fixed.

The main results are reported below with a series of bullet points.

The increment of the nozzle-wall distance:

 53

 • Decrease the velocity along the flow axis.

 • Increase the flow turbulence near the impact point on the wall.

 • Increase the flow dispersion (the flow cross-section).

 • Decrease the static pressure peak on the wall.

the wall surface:

 • Increase the asymmetry of the flow.

 Future Developments

The results of this report can be elaborated to have a better estimation of the
cleaning efficiency. The cleaning efficiency depends on multiple factors such as the
velocity at the impact point, the static pressure profile on the wall, the dimension
of the jet cross-section, and the time spent to clean the same surface portion on the
wall. The next step could be the determination of the cleaning efficiency starting
from the considerations obtained from this characterization. Besides, the roughness
of the niobium surface, the effective wall shear stress (needed to obtain the right
friction coefficient between the flow and the wall), and the surface energy deposition
can be added in the model and considered in the cleaning efficiency estimation.

 A preliminary investigation that has been performed during this characterization
is the implementation of a real nozzle geometry with respect to the holes. Fig. 7.1
and Fig. 7.2 show respectively the velocity contours obtained respectively with a
converging nozzle and a simplified plug nozzle.
