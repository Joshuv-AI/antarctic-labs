Transconductance as a Probe of Valley Thermodynamics in Multilayer WSe 2
Katsunori Wakabayashi, Souren Adhikary, Tomoaki Kameda

Abstract
Transconductance is a central figure of merit in field-effect transistors, typically governed by charge accumulation and carrier mobility. In multilayer WSe 2 transistors, however, it is shown to carry a nonlinear transport signature of inter-valley carrier redistribution between the K K and Γ \Gamma valleys. This valley-crossover contribution suppresses transconductance in bilayer WSe 2 and reverses sign in trilayer, while remaining absent in single-valley systems. Unlike extrinsic mechanisms such as trap-state filling or contact resistance, the anomaly leaves the subthreshold swing unchanged and cannot be reproduced within conventional single-valley transport models. Introducing the valley susceptibility χ v ≡ ∂ f Γ / ∂ V GS \chi_{v}\equiv\partial f_{\Gamma}/\partial V_{\rm GS} , bounded by an intrinsic thermodynamic limit ( 4 ​ k B ​ T ) − 1 (4k_{B}T)^{-1} , we quantify this response and show that it reaches ∼ 0.20 ​ V − 1 {\sim}0.20\,\mathrm{V}^{-1} in bilayer WSe 2 near threshold at room temperature. The sign, magnitude, and temperature dependence of the anomaly provide directly measurable fingerprints of valley thermodynamics, establishing transconductance as an electrical probe of internal electronic degrees of freedom and revealing a previously hidden nonlinear response in standard transistor measurements.

Introduction

Transconductance
g_{m}=dI_{D}/dV_{GS}
is a central figure of merit in
field-effect transistors, quantifying the gate response of channel current
and governing amplifier gain and switching speed.
In conventional semiconductor devices,
g_{m}
is determined by charge
accumulation and carrier mobility; its non-monotonic behavior is attributed

The transconductance of multilayer WSe

When the

\Gamma

64

g_{m,v}
that suppresses
g_{m}
in the bilayer, reverses sign

Non-monotonic structure in

is conventionally attributed to the
extrinsic mechanisms cited above, each of which simultaneously degrades the
subthreshold swing
SS
.
In contrast, the valley-crossover contribution identified here is
qualitatively distinct: it leaves

cannot be reproduced within conventional
single-valley transport models

II
Two-Valley Model and Valley Susceptibility

II.1
Electrostatics and carrier statistics

We model a WSe
2
GAA
FET
43
;
57
;
17

with gate oxide capacitance
C_{ox}
per unit area and
interface-trap capacitance
C_{it}
.
The relation between gate voltage
V_{GS}
and surface potential
\psi_{s}
is

V_{GS}=\psi_{s}+\frac{Q(\psi_{s})}{C_{ox}},\qquad Q=q\,p+C_{it}\psi_{s},

(1)

where
p=p_{K}+p_{\Gamma}
is the total hole density.
Each valley contributes via the two-dimensional Fermi–Dirac
integral
23
;
36
;
7

p_{\nu}=N_{2D,\nu}\ln\!\left(1+e^{\eta_{\nu}}\right),\quad N_{2D,\nu}=\frac{m_{\nu}^{*}k_{B}T}{\pi\hbar^{2}},

(2)

with reduced chemical potentials

\eta_{K}=\eta_{K,0}+q\psi_{s}/k_{B}T
and

\eta_{\Gamma}=\eta_{K}-\Delta_{K\Gamma}/k_{B}T
.

II.2
Valley fraction and susceptibility

We define the
\Gamma
-valley fraction

f_{\Gamma}=p_{\Gamma}/p
and the
valley susceptibility

\chi_{v}\equiv\frac{\partial f_{\Gamma}}{\partial V_{GS}}=\frac{\partial f_{\Gamma}}{\partial\psi_{s}}\cdot\alpha_{S},\quad\alpha_{S}=\frac{C_{ox}}{C_{ox}+C_{Q}+C_{it}},

(3)

where
C_{Q}=q\,\partial p/\partial\psi_{s}
is the quantum capacitance and

\alpha_{S}
is the electrostatic screening factor.
In the nondegenerate (subthreshold) limit both valleys obey Boltzmann
statistics and
\partial f_{\Gamma}/\partial\psi_{s}=0
exactly, because the
gate shifts
p_{K}
and
p_{\Gamma}
by the same multiplicative factor.
The susceptibility
\chi_{v}
is therefore a
threshold phenomenon
:
it activates only where Fermi–Dirac statistics become nonlinear
(
\eta_{K}\gtrsim 0
), making it sensitive precisely in the regime where the
device switches on.

Three properties follow from Eq. (
2
) in the Boltzmann limit:
(i)
f_{\Gamma}
is governed by the dimensionless parameter

x\equiv\Delta_{K\Gamma}/k_{B}T
via

f_{\Gamma}=\frac{r_{\rm DOS}\,e^{-x}}{1+r_{\rm DOS}\,e^{-x}},\quad r_{\rm DOS}=m_{\Gamma}^{*}/m_{K}^{*};

(4)

(ii) the sign rule
\mathrm{sgn}(\chi_{v})=\mathrm{sgn}(\Delta_{K\Gamma})

holds universally; and
(iii) the response is maximized at
x^{*}=\ln r_{\rm DOS}
, giving the
intrinsic bound

|\chi_{v}^{\rm max}|=\frac{1}{4k_{B}T}.

(5)

This bound—not directly observable because
\alpha_{S}<1
—serves as a
reference scale for device efficiency.
For the WSe
2
GAA structure studied here
(
\mathrm{EOT}=0.7\,\mathrm{nm}

34
;
5
,

C_{ox}\approx 49\,\mathrm{mF\,m}^{-2}
),
the numerically computed peak
\chi_{v}
for 2L at
300\,\mathrm{K}
is

\approx 0.20\,\mathrm{V}^{-1}
, a factor of
{\approx}50
below
the bound
9.7\,\mathrm{V}^{-1}
, reflecting the suppression by

\alpha_{S}\ll 1
near threshold.

Figure
2
shows the resulting
f_{\Gamma}(V_{GS})
and

\chi_{v}(V_{GS})
for 1L, 2L, and 3L at
T=300\,\mathrm{K}
and for 2L
across temperatures.
The bilayer displays a positive peak
\chi_{v}^{\rm peak}\approx 0.20\,\mathrm{V}^{-1}

near threshold; the trilayer shows an opposite-sign peak of

\approx-0.10\,\mathrm{V}^{-1}
; the monolayer (
x\approx 19
) gives

\chi_{v}\approx 0
.
These behaviors directly reflect the sign rule and the exponential
suppression of
f_{\Gamma}
at large positive
x
.

III
Transconductance Decomposition and Valley Anomaly

III.1
Effective mobility and drain current

The
K
and
\Gamma
valleys carry hole mobilities
\mu_{K}
and
\mu_{\Gamma}
,
with
\mu_{K}>\mu_{\Gamma}
because
m_{K}^{*}<m_{\Gamma}^{*}

4
;
19
;
42
;
14
;
27
;
32
.
The valley-averaged effective mobility

\mu_{\rm eff}=\mu_{K}-\Delta\mu\,f_{\Gamma},\quad\Delta\mu\equiv\mu_{K}-\mu_{\Gamma}>0,

(6)

decreases as holes transfer into the heavier
\Gamma
valley.
In the linear regime,

I_{D}=\frac{W}{L}\,\mu_{\rm eff}\,qp\,V_{DS}.

(7)

III.2
Transconductance decomposition

Differentiating Eq. (
7
) with respect to
V_{GS}
and using

\partial\mu_{\rm eff}/\partial V_{GS}=-\Delta\mu\,\chi_{v}
yields

\displaystyle g_{m}

\displaystyle=g_{m,0}+g_{m,v},

(8)

\displaystyle g_{m,0}

\displaystyle=\frac{W}{L}\,V_{DS}\,\mu_{\rm eff}\,C_{Q}\,\alpha_{S},

(9)

\displaystyle g_{m,v}

\displaystyle=-\frac{W}{L}\,V_{DS}\,\Delta\mu\,\chi_{v}\,qp.

(10)

The charge-accumulation term
g_{m,0}
is the standard transconductance.
The valley-crossover term
g_{m,v}
is a purely internal-degree-of-freedom
contribution whose sign is set by the sign of
\chi_{v}
:

\mathrm{sgn}(g_{m,v})=-\mathrm{sgn}(\Delta_{K\Gamma}).

(11)

For bilayer WSe
2
(
\Delta_{K\Gamma}>0
,
\chi_{v}>0
):

g_{m,v}<0
and gate-induced transfer of holes from high-mobility
K
to
low-mobility
\Gamma

suppresses

g_{m}
.
For trilayer (
\Delta_{K\Gamma}<0
,
\chi_{v}<0
):

g_{m,v}>0
and the reverse transfer
enhances

g_{m}

anomalously above the charge-accumulation expectation.

The most direct demonstration of the sign rule [Eq. (
11
)]
is the parametric plot of
({\chi_{v}},\,g_{m,v})
shown in
Fig.
3
(c), where
V_{GS}
is encoded in color.
The 2L trace occupies exclusively the fourth quadrant (
\chi_{v}>0
,

g_{m,v}<0
) and the 3L trace the second quadrant (
\chi_{v}<0
,

g_{m,v}>0
): the two layers are confined to
opposite
quadrants
across the entire gate-voltage range.
This anti-correlation is a direct consequence of Fermi–Dirac valley
thermodynamics and cannot be reproduced within conventional single-valley
transport models, nor mimicked by interface traps or contact resistance,
both of which degrade
SS
simultaneously—whereas
g_{m,v}
leaves
SS

strictly unchanged.

The individual gate sweeps are shown in Fig.
3
(a,b).
For bilayer,
\chi_{v}
(blue, left axis) peaks near

V_{GS}\approx 0.15\,\mathrm{V}

\chi_{v}^{\rm peak}\approx 0.20\,\mathrm{V}^{-1}
, and

(red, right axis) grows negatively

g_{m,v}\approx-1.1\,\mu\mathrm{S}
at its
minimum.
For trilayer, the sign is reversed throughout:
\chi_{v}<0
and
g_{m,v}>0
,
with a peak enhancement of
\approx+1.1\,\mu\mathrm{S}
.

III.3
Why bilayer WSe
2
is special

The magnitude of
g_{m,v}
is maximized when
|\chi_{v}^{\rm peak}|
is
largest, which from Eq. (
5
) occurs at

x^{*}=\ln r_{\rm DOS}
.
For 2L WSe
2
at room temperature and zero strain,

\Delta_{K\Gamma}/k_{B}T\approx 1.0
and

\ln r_{\rm DOS}\approx 0.93
, placing the bilayer at the optimal
thermodynamic condition essentially without any external tuning.
The monolayer (
x\approx 19
) and trilayer (
x<0
) are far from optimal.
Biaxial strain shifts
\Delta_{K\Gamma}

continuously

;

;
46
;
66
;
20
;
29
;
1
;
52

and provides experimental access to different regimes; compressive strain
moves the 2L system slightly closer to

, while tensile strain drives

III.4
Distinguishing valley crossover from trap effects

A key diagnostic criterion follows from the independence of
SS
from

g_{m,v}
.
The subthreshold swing

SS=\ln(10)(k_{B}T/q)(1+(C_{Q}+C_{it})/C_{ox})

is governed by electrostatics and is insensitive to the valley character
of the carriers: in the subthreshold regime
C_{Q}\ll C_{ox}
and

f_{\Gamma}
varies negligibly, so
\chi_{v}\approx 0
and

g_{m,v}\approx 0
.
Interface traps degrade both
SS
and the overall
g_{m}
profile
simultaneously; by contrast, the valley anomaly in
g_{m,v}
persists
even when
C_{it}
substantially degrades
SS
, because
g_{m,v}

depends on
\chi_{v}\cdot qp
rather than on
\alpha_{S}

30
;
2
;
22
.
An anomaly in
g_{m}
that survives trap degradation unscathed, reverses
sign between 2L and 3L, and grows in amplitude with decreasing temperature
provides a robust signature of valley-crossover origin.
The growth follows a
{\sim}T^{-1}
power law—opposite to the thermally
activated suppression
\propto\exp(-E_{a}/k_{B}T)
expected for deep-level
interface traps—making temperature sweeps a direct experimental
discriminant (Supplemental, Sec. S9).

IV
Universal Scaling and Device Design

The intrinsic valley response is universal: in the Boltzmann limit,

\chi_{v}^{\rm peak}\propto\Phi(x)/(k_{B}T)
, where

\Phi(x)=\frac{r_{\rm DOS}\,e^{-x}}{(1+r_{\rm DOS}\,e^{-x})^{2}}

(12)

peaks at
\Phi_{\max}=1/4
independent of
r_{\rm DOS}
.
Device-level screening breaks this universality:

4

versus

x=\Delta_{K\Gamma}/k_{B}T

at
T=300\,\mathrm{K}
or temperature at zero strain.
The two routes do not collapse onto a single curve—temperature
simultaneously tunes
x
and reduces
C_{Q}\propto N_{\rm 2D}\propto T
,
relaxing the screening factor
\alpha_{S}
, whereas strain modulates only

\Delta_{K\Gamma}
.
All data lie well below the theoretical bound
4\Phi(x)
(dotted),
confirming the
{\sim}50\times
screening suppression.
This non-universality is not a breakdown of the thermodynamic
framework; rather, it quantifies the role of device geometry.
Increasing
C_{ox}
(thinner or higher-
\kappa
gate
dielectric
58
;
33
;
48
)
or operating at lower temperature are the most direct routes to
approach the bound and amplify the observable valley anomaly.

V
Discussion

The transconductance decomposition [Eqs. (
8
)–(
10
)]
predicts three experimentally testable fingerprints:
(i)
g_{m,v}
is absent in the monolayer but pronounced in the bilayer
and trilayer, with opposite signs;
(ii)
|g_{m,v}^{\rm peak}|
grows as temperature decreases following

(4k_{B}T)^{-1}
scaling;
(iii) the anomaly persists under interface-trap degradation that suppresses

SS
, providing an unambiguous valley-crossover criterion.
For a
W=L=1\,\mu\mathrm{m}
device at 300 K,

|g_{m,v}^{\rm peak}|\approx 1\,\mu\mathrm{S}
, representing a 7% anomaly
relative to
g_{m,0}
, growing to
\approx 17\%
at 100 K.
Lock-in measurement of the numerically differentiated
I_{D}
–
V_{GS}
curve
provides sufficient sensitivity for detection.

The quasi-equilibrium treatment assumes that the intervalley scattering
time
\tau_{\rm iv}
is much shorter than the carrier transit time

\tau_{\rm tr}=L/v_{d}

55
;
6
.
When
\tau_{\rm iv}\gg\tau_{\rm tr}
, the valley population is frozen at
the source-contact value and
g_{m,v}
is suppressed; this provides an
additional observable in the channel-length dependence of the anomaly.

The framework extends beyond WSe
2
.
The essential requirement is that multiple valleys with distinct
effective masses are separated by
\Delta\sim k_{B}T
.
Analogous physics is expected in other few-layer
TMDCs
40
;
26
;
49
;
18
;
68
;
15
,
including systems where electrical valley control has been demonstrated via
ferroelectric gating
31
,
and in conventional multi-valley semiconductors under uniaxial stress.
In each case, the transconductance serves as a direct electrical meter
for internal valley thermodynamics—a probe that requires no optical
or magnetic field, and that is fully compatible with CMOS-compatible
transistor characterization
9
;
54
;
17
;
45
;
5
.

VI
Conclusion

We have shown that gate-induced redistribution of holes between the
K

and
\Gamma
valleys of WSe
2
produces a distinct contribution

g_{m,v}=-\Delta\mu\,(W/L)V_{DS}\,qp\,\chi_{v}
to the transconductance.
This valley-crossover term suppresses
g_{m}
in bilayer and enhances it in
trilayer WSe
2
, in both cases without affecting the subthreshold swing.
The valley susceptibility
\chi_{v}
vanishes exactly in the subthreshold
regime, peaks at threshold following the universal bound
(4k_{B}T)^{-1}
,
and is suppressed by electrostatic screening by a factor of
{\sim}50
in
realistic GAA devices.
The condition
\Delta_{K\Gamma}\approx k_{B}T\ln r_{\rm DOS}
identifies
bilayer WSe
2
at room temperature as the optimal material configuration.
These results establish the transconductance as a direct probe of valley
thermodynamics in multi-valley two-dimensional transistors, providing
an electrical fingerprint of internal electronic degrees of freedom
that is accessible through standard characterization techniques.
