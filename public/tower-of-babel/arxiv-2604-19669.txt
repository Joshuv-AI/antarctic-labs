Abstract
Enforcing constraint satisfaction in neural network outputs is critical for safety, reliability, and physical fidelity in many control and decision-making applications. While soft-constrained methods penalize constraint violations during training, they do not guarantee constraint adherence during inference. Other approaches guarantee constraint satisfaction via specific parameterizations or a projection layer, but are tailored to specific forms (e.g., linear constraints), limiting their utility in other general problem settings. Many real-world problems of interest are nonlinear, motivating the development of methods that can enforce general nonlinear constraints. To this end, we introduce HardNet++, a constraint-enforcement method that simultaneously satisfies linear and nonlinear equality and inequality constraints. Our approach iteratively adjusts the network output via damped local linearizations. Each iteration is differentiable, admitting an end-to-end training framework, where the constraint satisfaction layer is active during training. We show that under certain regularity conditions, this procedure can enforce nonlinear constraint satisfaction to arbitrary tolerance. Finally, we demonstrate tight constraint adherence without loss of optimality in a learning-for-optimization context, where we apply this method to a model predictive control problem with nonlinear state constraints.



I
INTRODUCTION

Neural networks have demonstrated remarkable success in modeling complex physical dynamics and learning surrogate representations of computational processes, often with improved efficiency compared to traditional numerical methods. As a result, they are increasingly used in scientific machine learning, where models are expected to represent underlying physical systems. In these settings, outputs must often satisfy domain-specific constraints derived from first principles, such as conservation of mass or energy, symmetry relationships, or governing equations. Similarly, safety-critical applications require strict enforcement of bounds on process variables or stability constraints. Standard neural networks, however, do not inherently guarantee adherence to such constraints, which can lead to physically inconsistent or infeasible predictions, limiting the practicality of such models. This limitation motivates the development of principled constraint enforcement methods that preserve feasibility in learned models.

Beyond scientific modeling, neural networks are also increasingly used to approximate optimization solvers. Many applications, including model predictive control, resource allocation, and real-time decision making, require repeatedly solving high-dimensional optimization problems online. Although modern optimization solvers are highly optimized, repeated real-time solutions can still be computationally expensive, motivating the use of neural networks as fast surrogate solvers. However, these optimization problems typically include equality and inequality constraints that define the feasible solution set. Consequently, the practical deployment of neural network surrogate solvers depends on the ability to ensure that predicted solutions remain feasible.

Despite the importance of constraint satisfaction in both scientific machine learning and learned optimization, enforcing constraints in neural network outputs remains challenging. Soft-constrained approaches encourage constraint satisfaction by penalizing violations during training
[
15
]
, but they generally cannot guarantee feasibility at inference time, particularly for inputs outside the training distribution. To address this limitation, hard-constrained methods have been developed to enforce feasibility by design
[
11
]
. This is often done via specific parameterizations
[
1
]
or projection-based methods that project the network output onto a feasible set defined by the constraints. For linear
equality
constraints, a closed-form projection exists to minimize the Euclidean distance between the network output and a projected feasible point
[

. HardNet
[
11
]
introduced a closed-form parallel projection that enforces both linear equality and inequality constraints while provably retaining the network’s expressivity. Beyond linear constraints, ECO
[
6
]
enforces a convex quadratic constraint in closed form.

While closed-form projections offer efficient implementation and guaranteed constraint satisfaction, there are problems for which, inevitably, a single closed-form projection onto the feasible set does not exist, in which case iterative projection methods are often applied. PiNet
[
7
]
uses Douglas-Rachford to alternate projections onto sets whose Euclidean projections are known. LMI-Net
[
16
]
satisfies linear matrix inequality (LMI) constraints by alternating between an affine equality projection and the projection onto the positive semidefinite cone, both of which can be computed in closed form.
Other alternating projection methods use Dykstra’s projection algorithm with success in solving optimization problems with linear constraints
[
3
,
4
]
.
For arbitrary nonlinear equality constraints, ENFORCE
[
9
]
iteratively projects onto local linearizations of the constraints with a closed-form Euclidean projection
[
2
]
, but does not handle inequality constraints. FSNet
[
13
]
uses vanilla gradient descent to minimize the constraint residual.

Despite the success of these projection methods in constraint satisfaction, many are limited by the form of the constraints or lack theoretical convergence guarantees. In this work, we introduce HardNet++, a framework for enforcing nonlinear equality and inequality constraints on neural network outputs via a differentiable projection with constraint satisfaction guarantees. Specifically, HardNet++ iteratively enforces constraints using a closed-form damped projection based on local linearizations. Our contributions are:

1.

A differentiable projection framework that simultaneously enforces nonlinear equality and inequality constraints on neural network outputs.

2.

Convergence guarantees showing that the proposed iterative projection achieves arbitrarily small constraint violations for nonlinear constraints.

3.

Experimental validation on a nonlinear MPC task demonstrating reliable constraint satisfaction with minimal degradation of optimal performance.

The goal of this work, therefore, is to develop a methodology that ensures neural network outputs satisfy nonlinear constraints. Specifically, we map the unconstrained neural network output to a point that simultaneously satisfies nonlinear equality and inequality constraints, as in Figure

. Consider a neural network output
\hat{y}_{\theta}=f_{\theta}(x)
, where

denote
q
input-dependent inequality constraints. We aim to map
\hat{y}_{\theta}

P_{x}(\hat{y}_{\theta})=\bar{y}_{\theta},\quad\bar{y}_{\theta}\in\left\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:b^{l}_{x}\leq g_{x}(y)\leq b^{u}_{x},\;h_{x}(y)=0\right\}.

(1)

Here,
b^{u}_{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
and

.

II-A

Notation and Definitions

We define the stacked constraint vector

c(y):=\begin{bmatrix}h(y)\\
g(y)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+q}.

To measure constraint violation, we define the constraint residual

r(y):=\begin{bmatrix}h(y)\\
\mathrm{ReLU}\left(g(y)-b^{u}\right)-\mathrm{ReLU}\left(b^{l}-g(y)\right)\end{bmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{m+q},

where the
\mathrm{ReLU}(\cdot)=\mathrm{max}(0,\cdot)
operator is applied elementwise. Note that we drop the dependence of
h
and
g
as well as
b^{l}
and
b^{u}
on the network input
x
for notational convenience, although we emphasize that this method handles input-dependent constraints, as demonstrated in Section
V
.

The Jacobian of the constraints with respect to
y
is
J_{c}(y)
where
J_{c,ij}=\frac{\partial c_{i}(y)}{\partial y_{j}}
.
The Jacobian of the constraint residuals
r(y)
is
J_{r}(y)
where
J_{r,ij}=\frac{\partial r_{i}(y)}{\partial y_{j}}
. A subscript
k
denotes the
k
-th iterate of an iterative method, where
k\in\{0,1,2,\dots\}
.

III
Constrained Learning Methodology

III-A

Constraint Enforcement via Damped Local Linearizations

While no closed-form solution exists for projections onto general nonlinear constraints
h(y)=0
and
b^{l}\leq g(y)\leq b^{u}
, several projection methods can project onto linear equality or inequality constraints in closed form. To leverage this, we adopt a differentiable iterative constraint satisfaction scheme, as is common in literature
[
7
,
9
,
13
]
. We satisfy constraints asymptotically by performing an update step based on local linearizations of the constraints. That is, we wish to simultaneously satisfy

\begin{split}h(y_{k})+J_{h}({y_{k}})[y_{k+1}-y_{k}]=0,\\
b^{l}\leq g(y_{k})+J_{g}(y_{k})[y_{k+1}-y_{k}]\leq b^{u}.\end{split}

(2)

Here,
J_{h}
and
J_{g}
are the Jacobians of the equality and inequality constraints, respectively. In the case where the number of independent constraints is less than or equal to the number of variables, HardNet
[
11
]
admits a solution to the linearized projection. That is, when
J_{c,k}:=J_{c}(y_{k})
has full row rank, a closed-form projection exists that satisfies Equation (
2
). Specifically, the HardNet projection is

y_{k+1}=y_{k}-J_{c,k}^{T}(J_{c,k}J_{c,k}^{T})^{-1}r(y_{k}).

(3)

Equation
3
is well-suited for linear constraints due to the parallel projection geometry inherent to HardNet. That is, points are projected parallel to the boundaries of the satisfied constraints, allowing satisfaction of violated constraints without violating these already-satisfied constraints. In the case of nonlinear constraints, where the constraint boundary does not extend across the entire domain of the variables, a strictly parallel projection may fail to move toward violated constraints. See Figure
2a
for an example of this failure mode.

(a)
Vanilla HardNet iterates

(b)
HardNet++ iterates

Fig. 2:
Projection steps based on local linearizations under two projection methods. The feasible set is the overlap between the red and blue regions. The red point is the initial point, and points get darker in subsequent iterations. An iterative projection using HardNet is forced to stay parallel to the satisfied linear constraint, preventing it from reaching the constraint. The damped method allows movement orthogonal to the constraint boundary, converging to the feasible set.

To circumvent this phenomenon inherent to a vanilla HardNet implementation, we add a damping term to our projection.

y_{k+1}=y_{k}-J_{c,k}^{T}(J_{c,k}J_{c,k}^{T}+\varepsilon I)^{-1}\left[r(y_{k})\right]\quad\varepsilon>0

(4)

The regularization term,
\varepsilon I
, offers several key advantages. First, it damps the restriction on the direction orthogonal to satisfied constraints, allowing the projection method to converge to the feasible set. See Figure
2b
for an example of this improvement. It also allows
(JJ^{T}+\varepsilon I)x=b
to have a unique solution for
x
, regardless of the number of constraints and variables. That is,
JJ^{T}+\varepsilon I
is invertible, even when
JJ^{T}
is not invertible, although in practice we can use efficient linear solvers to compute the projection in Equation (
4
) rather than explicitly computing the inverse. Finally, the addition of the
\varepsilon I
term behaves as a regularizer, penalizing steps far away from the region in which the linear approximation is valid. This particular effect is discussed further in the next section.

Importantly, this iterative procedure is differentiable. This enables an end-to-end training scheme, where the constrained output is considered during training, rather than only being applied at inference time. This is important because training seeks to ensure the constrained output minimizes the loss function, rather than the unconstrained output, which could be pushed away suboptimally when constraints are enforced only during inference.

III-B

Connection to Levenberg-Marquardt

The damped projection method has parallels to the Levenberg-Marquardt damped least-squares algorithm
[
10
]
, although our projection method is fundamentally different in how it handles inequality constraints. Levenberg-Marquardt, applied to solve nonlinear least squares problems, also involves iterative linearizations. Specifically, it minimizes a square objective function, which, in our case, can be defined as the constraint residual.

y_{k+1}=\underset{y}{\text{argmin }}\sum_{i}r_{i}(y)^{2}=\underset{y}{\text{argmin }}r(y)^{T}r(y)

(5)

While this minimization problem in Equation (
5
) does not necessarily admit a closed-form solution, a local linearization does.

\displaystyle\delta_{k}=y_{k+1}-y_{k}

\displaystyle=\underset{\delta}{\text{argmin }}[r(y_{k})+J_{r,k}\delta]^{T}[r(y_{k})+J_{r,k}\delta]

\displaystyle=\underset{\delta}{\text{argmin }}2r_{k}^{T}J_{r,k}\delta+\delta^{T}J_{r,k}^{T}J_{r,k}\delta

(6)

Here,
r(y_{k})
is replaced with
r_{k}
for notational convenience. Importantly, the Jacobian
J_{r,k}
here is the Jacobian of the
constraint residual
, rather than the constraint itself, since the optimization problem in Equation (
5
) seeks to minimize the residual instead of the constraint value. Taking the derivative of the objective in Equation (
III-B
) and setting it to 0 gives

J_{r,k}^{T}J_{r,k}\delta_{k}=-J_{r,k}^{T}r_{k}.

(7)

This system admits a unique solution when
J_{r,k}
has full column rank, which is only possible when the number of constraints is greater than or equal to the number of variables. In the case where the number of constraints is fewer than the number of variables (and
J_{r,k}
has full row rank), the system has infinite solutions and requires additional specifications to identify a unique solution. The Levenberg-Marquardt correction gives

(J_{r,k}^{T}J_{r,k}+\varepsilon I)\delta_{k}=-J_{r,k}^{T}r_{k}.

(8)

Equation (
8
) is equivalent to the solution to the linearized objective optimization problem in Equation (
III-B
) with a penalty on
||\delta||^{2}_{2}
. That is, it is the solution to the problem,

\delta_{k}=\underset{\delta}{\text{argmin }}[r(y_{k})+J_{r,k}(y_{k})\delta]^{T}[r(y_{k})+J_{r,k}(y_{k})\delta]+\varepsilon\delta^{T}\delta.

(9)

Clearly, the solution in Equation (
8
) minimizes the local linearization with a penalty to restrict steps too far from where the local linearization is valid.

Despite the fact that Equation (
8
) has a similar form to our projection in Equation (
4
), these projections are
fundamentally different in their geometries
. Importantly, the Levenberg-Marquardt projection uses the Jacobian of the residual, while our projection uses the Jacobian of the constraint. These are the same for equality constraints but different for inequality constraints because of the ReLU in the residual calculation. The geometric intuition here can be understood in the context of the advantage of the HardNet parallel projection. In the Levenberg-Marquardt step, inequality constraints that are already satisfied are effectively ignored, as their corresponding rows in the Jacobian matrix are all zeros. In our projection step, we explicitly consider all constraints. In the HardNet projection, this manifests as constraining projections to run parallel to the boundary of already satisfied constraints. For linearizations of nonlinear functions, this is equivalent to restricting projections in the direction of maximum increase in the constraint value. Our damping approach then softens this so that the step is not exactly parallel to the linearized satisfied constraint boundaries.

III-C

Learning with Hard Constraints for Optimization

The proposed methodology for enforcing constraints in neural network outputs is particularly relevant to learned optimization solvers. Consider an optimization problem of the form

\underset{y}{\text{min }}Z_{x}(y)\quad{\text{subject to}\quad g_{x}(y)\leq 0,\;h_{x}(y)=0}

(10)

that needs to be solved for different instances of
x
. The idea is to train a neural network to map an optimization context
x
to a candidate solution
y_{\theta}
, rather than solving a constrained optimization independently at each instance. Often, the objective function
Z_{x}(y)
itself is chosen as the loss function during training. Our methodology is well-suited for learned optimization problems because we can ensure the feasibility of the candidate solution
y_{\theta}
via our iterative projection method.

IV
Convergence Guarantees

In this section, we show that, under a standard set of assumptions, our projection method converges asymptotically to a point that satisfies the constraints. We define the violation energy as

V(y):=\frac{1}{2}\|r(y)\|_{2}^{2},

We establish two standard optimization conditions for our objective function

V(y)
.

Assumption 1

(
L
-Smoothness): The violation energy
V(y)
is
L
-smooth, meaning its gradients are
L
-Lipschitz continuous.

Lemma
1
shows how this assumption provides a quadratic upper bound on the function’s growth, controlling the region where our linear approximation is valid.

Lemma 1

Let
V(y)
be an
L
-smooth function on a convex domain, then the following quadratic upper bound holds for any
y_{1},y_{2}
:

V(y_{2})\leq V(y_{1})+\nabla V(y_{1})^{T}\Delta y+\frac{L}{2}\|\Delta y\|_{2}^{2},

where
\Delta y=y_{2}-y_{1}
.
[
12
]

It is worth noting that although Assumption
1
is stated globally for notational simplicity, the proof only uses the quadratic upper bound from Lemma
1
along the iterates.
Therefore, it is sufficient that
V
admit an upper bound
L
on its local smoothness over any convex region containing the iterates (e.g., the initial sublevel set).

Assumption 2

(PŁ Condition
[
14
]
) The function
V(y)
with minimizer
y^{\star}
satisfies the
\mu
-PL condition

\forall y:\frac{1}{2}\|\nabla V(y)\|_{2}^{2}\geq\mu\left(V(y)-V(y^{\star})\right)

This assumption connects gradients at any point to sub-optimality, allowing us to translate arguments from gradient space to sub-optimality. If we assume there exists a point satisfying all the constraints, then
V^{\star}=0
, and violation can be upper bounded by the norm of the gradient. Further note that the function does not need to be convex to satisfy this condition; however,
\mu
-strongly convexity implies this condition.

Assumption 3

(Lipschitzness of the Constraints)
The stacked constraint vector

c(y)=\begin{bmatrix}h(y)\\
g(y)\end{bmatrix}

is differentiable and
G
-Lipschitz on the domain of interest, i.e.,

\|c(y_{1})-c(y_{2})\|_{2}\leq G\|y_{1}-y_{2}\|_{2}\qquad\forall y_{1},y_{2}.

This assumption implies an upper bound on the Gram Matrix
H_{k}=J_{k}J_{k}^{\top}
, where
J_{k}=J_{c}(y_{k})
. Lemma
2
gives a bound on the maximum eigenvalue of
H_{k}
.

Lemma 2

Under Assumption
3
, the maximum eigenvalue of
H_{k}=J_{k}J_{k}^{\top}
satisfies

\lambda_{\max}(H_{k})\leq G^{2}.

Proof:

Fix
y
and let
J(y)=J_{c}(y)
. For any unit vector
v
, differentiability
and
G
-Lipschitzness of
c
imply

\|J(y)v\|_{2}=\lim_{t\to 0}\frac{\|c(y+tv)-c(y)\|_{2}}{|t|}\leq G.

Taking the supremum over all
\|v\|_{2}=1
gives
\|J(y)\|_{2}\leq G
. Therefore,

\lambda_{\max}(H_{k})=\|J_{k}\|_{2}^{2}\leq G^{2}.

This proves the claim.
∎

Under the aforementioned assumptions, Theorem
1
shows that the violation energy decays exponentially fast as a function of the number of iterations.

Theorem 1

Consider the violation energy
V(y)=\frac{1}{2}\|r(y)\|_{2}^{2}
, with minimizer
y^{\star}
. Suppose
V(y)
is
L
-smooth and satisfies the
\mu
-PL condition, and the stacked constraint vector
c(y)
is
G
-Lipschitz. If the update step is defined as

\Delta y_{k}=-J_{k}^{\top}(J_{k}J_{k}^{\top}+\varepsilon I)^{-1}r(y_{k}),\qquad J_{k}:=J_{c}(y_{k}),

then for any regularization parameter choice
\varepsilon>\frac{L}{2}
, the violation energy decays as follows:

V(y_{k+1})-V(y^{\star})\leq\bigl(1-2\mu c(\varepsilon)\bigr)\bigl(V(y_{k})-V(y^{\star})\bigr),

where

c(\varepsilon):=\min\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}-\frac{L}{2\varepsilon^{2}},\;\frac{1}{\varepsilon+G^{2}}-\frac{L}{2(\varepsilon+G^{2})^{2}}\right\}>0.

In particular, if there exists a feasible point, then
V(y^{\star})=0
and

V(y_{k})\leq\bigl(1-2\mu c(\varepsilon)\bigr)^{k}V(y_{0}),

so
V(y_{k})\to 0
exponentially fast.

Proof:

We begin by defining

r_{k}:=r(y_{k}),\qquad J_{k}:=J_{c}(y_{k}),\qquad H_{k}:=J_{k}J_{k}^{\top},

and substituting the update step

\Delta y_{k}=-J_{k}^{\top}(H_{k}+\varepsilon I)^{-1}r_{k}

into the quadratic upper bound:

V(y_{k+1})\leq V(y_{k})+\nabla V(y_{k})^{\top}\Delta y_{k}+\frac{L}{2}\|\Delta y_{k}\|_{2}^{2}.

Now,

\begin{split}V(y)&=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}h_{i}(y)^{2}\\
&+\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j}[\mathrm{ReLU}(g_{j}(y)-b^{u})-\mathrm{ReLU}(b^{l}-g_{j}(y))]^{2}.\end{split}

Since the scalar function
\phi(z)=\frac{1}{2}[\mathrm{ReLU}(z-b^{u})-\mathrm{ReLU}(b^{l}-z)]^{2}
is continuously differentiable with derivative
\phi^{\prime}(z)=\mathrm{ReLU}(z-b^{u})-\mathrm{ReLU}(b^{l}-z)
, the chain rule gives

\nabla V(y_{k})=J_{k}^{\top}r_{k}.

Therefore,

\nabla V(y_{k})^{\top}\Delta y_{k}=-r_{k}^{\top}H_{k}(H_{k}+\varepsilon I)^{-1}r_{k},

and

\|\Delta y_{k}\|_{2}^{2}=r_{k}^{\top}(H_{k}+\varepsilon I)^{-1}H_{k}(H_{k}+\varepsilon I)^{-1}r_{k}.

Substituting into the quadratic upper bound yields

\displaystyle V(y_{k})-V(y_{k+1})

\displaystyle\geq r_{k}^{T}H_{k}(H_{k}+\varepsilon I)^{-1}r_{k}

\displaystyle-\frac{L}{2}r_{k}^{T}(H_{k}+\varepsilon I)^{-1}H_{k}(H_{k}+\varepsilon I)^{-1}r_{k}

Let
H_{k}=Q_{k}\Lambda_{k}Q_{k}^{\top}
be the eigendecomposition of
H_{k}
, where

\Lambda_{k}=\mathrm{diag}(\lambda_{k,1},\dots,\lambda_{k,m+\ell}),

and let
z_{k}=Q_{k}^{\top}r_{k}
. Lemma
2
, implies

\forall i:0\leq\lambda_{k,i}\leq G^{2}
therefore,

V(y_{k})-V(y_{k+1})\geq\sum_{i}\left(\frac{\lambda_{k,i}}{\lambda_{k,i}+\varepsilon}-\frac{L\cdot\lambda_{k,i}}{2(\lambda_{k,i}+\varepsilon)^{2}}\right)z_{k,i}^{2}.

Let

x_{i}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{k,i}+\varepsilon},\qquad f(x)=x\left(1-\frac{L}{2}x\right).

Then

x_{i}\in\left[\frac{1}{\varepsilon+G^{2}},\,\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\right].

Since
f
is a concave quadratic, its minimum over this interval is attained at an endpoint. Therefore,

f(x_{i})\geq c(\varepsilon)=\min\left\{\frac{1}{\varepsilon}-\frac{L}{2\varepsilon^{2}},\;\frac{1}{\varepsilon+G^{2}}-\frac{L}{2(\varepsilon+G^{2})^{2}}\right\}.

Substituting this lower bound gives

\displaystyle V(y_{k})-V(y_{k+1})

\displaystyle\geq c(\varepsilon)\sum_{i}\lambda_{k,i}z_{k,i}^{2}

\displaystyle=c(\varepsilon)\,r_{k}^{\top}H_{k}r_{k}

\displaystyle=c(\varepsilon)\,\|J_{k}^{\top}r_{k}\|_{2}^{2}

\displaystyle=c(\varepsilon)\,\|\nabla V(y_{k})\|_{2}^{2}.

Applying the
\mu
-PL condition yields

V(y_{k})-V(y_{k+1})\geq 2\mu c(\varepsilon)\bigl(V(y_{k})-V(y^{\star})\bigr),

or equivalently,

V(y_{k+1})-V(y^{*})\leq\bigl(1-2\mu c(\varepsilon)\bigr)\bigl(V(y_{k})-V(y^{\star})\bigr).

Iterating this inequality gives

V(y_{k})-V(y^{*})\leq\bigl(1-2\mu c(\varepsilon)\bigr)^{k}\bigl(V(y_{0})-V(y^{\star})\bigr).

Importantly, the decay factor
1-2\mu c(\varepsilon)
remains between
0
and
1
. Because
\varepsilon>\frac{L}{2}
, we have
c(\varepsilon)>0
and therefore
1-2\mu c(\varepsilon)<1
. Additionally, since the global maximum of
f
is
\frac{1}{2L}
and
L\geq\mu

[
8
]
,
f(x_{i})\leq\frac{1}{2\mu}
. This guarantees that the decay factor
1-2\mu c(\varepsilon)
remains non-negative, validating the monotonic decay. Finally, since
V(y_{k+1})\leq V(y_{k})
, the iterates remain in the initial sublevel set of
V
, so the proof works under a local smoothness bound over that region. This completes the proof.
∎

V
Empirical Validation

We demonstrate the ability of our proposed methodology to approximate a constrained optimization solver, as described in Section
III-C
. We use an illustrative model predictive control example with a nonlinear constraint on the state
x
.

\begin{split}\underset{x,u}{\text{min }}&\sum_{k=1}^{N}||x_{k}-x_{\text{target}}||_{2}^{2}+||u_{k}||_{2}^{2}\\
&\text{subject to}\\
&x_{k+1}=Ax_{k}+Bu_{k}\quad\forall k\in\{0,1,...N-1\}\\
&|u_{k}|\leq u_{b}\quad\forall k\in\{0,1,...N-1\}\\
&x^{T}Qx\leq x_{b}\\
&x_{0}=x_{\text{in}}\end{split}

(11)

We use
n_{x}=5,\;\;n_{u}=4
for
x_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{x}}
,
u_{k}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{u}}
. The linear equality constraint is the system dynamics, and we use

A=I_{5},\;B=\begin{bmatrix}I_{4}\\
e_{4}^{T}\end{bmatrix}\;Q=\operatorname{blkdiag}\!\left(\begin{bmatrix}0.05&0.025\\
0.025&0.1\end{bmatrix},\,0.1I_{3}\right)

with
e_{4}^{T}=\begin{bmatrix}0&0&0&1\end{bmatrix}
. Box constraints are enforced on the controller

with

, and the bound on the quadratic constraint is

. We use

. We aim to find a mapping from the initial condition

is
[3,12,10,10,10]
, which lies outside the constraint boundary. When constraints are enforced, the state will therefore never reach the target.

We use a feed-forward neural network with 2 hidden layers and 200 neurons each. For the constraint satisfaction layer, we use

and
500

Intuitively, this promotes minimization of the constrained output, which is the MPC goal, rather than minimizing an unconstrained output and then projecting back onto a feasible set during inference only.

[
5
]

\begin{split}S=\frac{\max(0,Z(\bar{x},\bar{u})-Z(x^{*},u^{*}))}{Z(x^{*},u^{*})}.\end{split}

(12)

Here

is the objective function
\underset{x,u}{\text{min}}\sum_{k=1}^{N}||x_{k}-x_{\text{target}}||_{2}^{2}+||u_{k}||_{2}^{2}
,

and

VI
Conclusion

To address the challenge of enforcing constraints in neural networks, we introduced HardNet++, a differentiable enforcement method for nonlinear inequality and equality constraint satisfaction. HardNet++ uses damped closed-form updates based on local linearizations of the constraints. Unlike many existing approaches, this method can handle nonlinear inequality and equality constraints simultaneously. We provide theoretical guarantees showing that under standard regularity assumptions, our constraint satisfaction method converges exponentially to a feasible point satisfying the constraints to arbitrary tolerance. Empirical evaluation on a model predictive control problem with nonlinear state constraints demonstrates reliable constraint enforcement without sacrificing optimality.

The theoretical and empirical results of this paper suggest that HardNet++ provides a practical mechanism for incorporating hard constraints into network models, with applications in learned optimization and scientific machine learning. Future work could explore the applications of HardNet++ to larger-scale control and learning problems.
