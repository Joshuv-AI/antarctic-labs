Single-cell multiomic approaches define a gradual, spatially regulated epigenetic and transcriptional transition from embryonic to adult neural stem cells
Authors: Beatrix S Wang, Konstantina Karamboulas, Nareh Tahmasian, Daniel J Dennis, David R Kaplan, Freda D Miller
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHere, we ask how adult neural stem cells (NSCs) arise developmentally, focusing on murine cortical precursors that generate excitatory neurons embryonically and interneurons and glial cells postnatally. Using complementary single-cell spatial, transcriptomic, and epigenomic approaches, we show that postnatal NSC state acquisition involves a gradual transcriptional and epigenetic shift in the entire embryonic cortical precursor cell population and identify a distinct transition precursor state at E17/18 when both embryonic and the first postnatal progeny are being generated. Non-proliferative adult NSCs are also first seen at this transition timepoint, but they arise in a spatial domain distinct from that of the first postnatal progeny, indicating that NSC state acquisition is not a necessary prelude to the switch in cell genesis. These findings support a gradual epigenetically continuous model for the transition from developing cortical precursors to NSCs and show that this is spatially separable from the transition to generating postnatal cell types.Keywords: neural stem cells, cortical development, stem cell development, single-cell transcriptomics, single-cell spatial transcriptomics, single-cell epigenetics, stem cell epigenetics, radial glia, olfactory neurogenesis

Introduction

One of the two well-studied mammalian neural stem cell (NSC) populations resides in the forebrain ventricular-subventricular zone (V-SVZ). These NSCs, which make olfactory bulb interneurons and glial cells, have two distinct embryonic origins. Those in the lateral V-SVZ largely derive from ganglionic eminence (GE) radial precursors (RPs) that also make interneurons and oligodendrocytes embryonically. NSCs in the dorsal V-SVZ instead derive from cortical RPs that make excitatory neurons embryonically, and switch to making their adult progeny postnatally (Menn et al., 2006; Ventura and Goldman, 2007). This cell genesis switch occurs around birth and is coincident with a second transition where embryonic RPs become slowly dividing dormant/quiescent postnatal NSCs (Borrett et al., 2020; Kessaris et al., 2006; Merkle et al., 2004; Winkler et al., 2018; Young et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2020). We previously showed that this dormancy transition starts by postnatal day 6/7 (P6/7) and ends by P21 (Borrett et al., 2020).

One model explaining these transitions is the “set-aside” model, which posits two developing cortical RP populations, one that generates excitatory neurons and a second that, during mid-embryogenesis, is set aside as slowly dividing cells that later emerge as postnatal NSCs. This set-aside model is widely accepted for lateral GE-derived V-SVZ NSCs (Fuentealba et al., 2015; Furutachi et al., 2015), but it may not explain what happens in the cortex. In particular, most cortical NSCs originate from precursors that proliferate perinatally (Marcy et al., 2023), and some cortical precursors make both embryonic excitatory neurons and postnatal olfactory bulb interneurons (Cai et al., 2013; Fuentealba et al., 2015; Gao et al., 2014). How then do embryonic cortical RPs become postnatal NSCs, and what is the relationship between this state change and the genesis of postnatal cell types?

Here, we used single-cell approaches to address these questions at the transcriptional, epigenetic, and spatial levels. We show that the acquisition of an NSC state involves a gradual transcriptional and epigenetic shift in the entire RP population and define an RP/NSC transition state that occurs at E17/18 when both embryonic and postnatal progeny are generated. We also show that the first NSCs and the first postnatal progeny arise in different developing V-SVZ spatial domains, thereby dissociating the acquisition of an NSC state from the cell genesis transition.

Results

At P2, cortical precursors have switched to making glia and interneurons

To determine when cortical precursors transition from embryonic to postnatal cell genesis, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from embryonic day 14 (E14) to P6/7, starting with P2, since the first cortically derived interneurons are observed in early postnatal life (Borrett et al., 2020; Harkins et al., 2025). To identify cortically derived cells, we performed lineage tracing with mice carrying Cre knocked into the Emx1 locus and a floxed Eyfp allele in the Rosa26 locus; we and others have shown that cortically derived cells are specifically tagged in these Emx1Cre;R26Eyfpfl/fl mice (called Emx1-Eyfp mice) (Borrett et al., 2020; Cocas et al., 2009; Gorski et al., 2002). We dissected the dorsal/lateral V-SVZ from these mice at P2, sequenced single cells using the 10× Genomics Chromium platform, and analyzed transcriptomes using our previously described pipeline (see STAR Methods). We removed doublets and low-quality cells, then used genes with high variance to compute principal components as inputs for UMAP visualization. Clustering was performed at a range of resolutions using a shared nearest neighbors-cliq (SNN-cliq)-inspired approach built into the Seurat R package (Hao et al., 2024).

We identified all predicted cell types within this dataset, including NSCs, transit-amplifying precursors (TAPs), neuroblasts, oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs), and astrocytes, as well as excitatory neurons, interneurons, and microglia (Figure S1A). We defined postnatal NSCs as cells expressing Aldoc, Slc1a3/Glast, Tfap2c, Vnn1, and Veph1 but low levels of astrocyte mRNAs such as Agt and Htra1. We confirmed this assignment using a gene signature (Borrett et al., 2020; see STAR Methods) that distinguishes postnatal NSCs from astrocytes (Figure S1B). We identified TAPs as proliferative cells expressing Ascl1 and Egfr, together with other TAP genes we previously described (Borrett et al., 2020; Willis et al., 2025). We defined early intermediate progenitors (IPs) as cells expressing Eomes and Insm1, but not neuronal mRNAs such as Neurod1 and Neurod6, and considered these to be a reflection of ongoing excitatory neurogenesis. The P2 dataset did not include early IPs (Figure S1A), indicating excitatory neurogenesis was largely complete. Finally, we identified newly generated neurons as cells expressing early neuronal mRNAs such as Neurod1 and Neurod6, and the IP mRNA Eomes (termed late IPs/newborn neurons).

We increased the power of our analysis by merging these transcriptomes with our previously published P2 Emx1-Eyfp mouse V-SVZ dataset (Borrett et al., 2020; GEO: GSE152281) after reanalyzing it similarly (Figure S1C). We ensured integration using two iterations of Harmony batch correction (Korsunsky et al., 2019). We identified the same cell types as in the individual datasets (Figures 1A and 1B). Analysis of cell cycle status using Cyclone (Scialdone et al., 2015) (Figure 1C) predicted that many TAPs were proliferating, as were some OPCs and astrocytes, but no NSCs nor late IPs/newborn neurons.

We next analyzed Eyfp expression (Figure 1D); approximately 80% of cortically derived late IPs/newborn excitatory neurons were Eyfp-positive while GE-derived cortical/striatal interneurons displayed only low ambient Eyfp levels, confirming the lineage tracing efficacy and specificity. Many TAPs, neuroblasts, OPCs, and astrocytes were also Eyfp-positive, indicating they were generated by cortical precursors. Since there were no early IPs (Figure 1A), this indicates that P2 cortical precursors have switched from generating embryonic to postnatal progeny.

By P2, cortical precursors acquire an NSC transcriptional state

We better defined the transcriptional state of P2 cortical precursors by comparing them to cortical embryonic RPs and postnatal NSCs. To do this, we subsetted out Eyfp-positive RP/NSCs from the merged P2 dataset (cluster 12, Figure S1D), and merged these with subsetted cortical Emx1-Eyfp lineage-traced E14 RPs and P6/7 NSCs transcriptomes that we previously published (Borrett et al., 2020; GEO: GSE152281) (Figures S1E–S1G). In this merged cortical precursor dataset (Figure 1E), P2 and P6/7 NSCs co-clustered, while E14 RPs were completely distinct. Pearson’s correlations of averaged gene expression (Figures 1F and 1G) confirmed P2 cells were highly similar to P6/7 NSCs, with r = 0.965, but not to E14 cortical RPs, with r = 0.865.

These data indicate P2 cortical precursors have acquired an NSC transcriptional state and switched to generating postnatal progeny. We extended these conclusions by performing Monocle-based trajectory analysis on P2 cortical NSCs and their immediate progeny (Qiu et al., 2017). We enhanced the power of this analysis by including neural cell transcriptomes from our published, specifically dissected P2 cortex scRNA-seq dataset (Dennis et al., 2024; GEO: GSE255405) (Figure S1H). We merged these different datasets and performed batch correction and cell cycle regression (Figures 1H and 1I) to ensure results were not driven by cell cycle status. Monocle analysis of this dataset (Figure 1J) predicted that cortical NSCs give rise to TAPs, which then bifurcate to generate OPCs and neuroblasts, a trajectory similar to that seen with adult NSCs.

At E17/18, cortical precursors are in a unique RP/NSC transition state and make both embryonic and postnatal progeny

These findings confirm that the cortical RP to NSC transition occurs between E14 and P2. We more precisely defined the timing of this transition by performing scRNA-seq on the E18 cortex and obtaining transcriptomes of all cortical cell types, including cortical precursors (RP/NSCs) (Figure S2A). We merged these E18 neural cell transcriptomes with our previously published E17 cortically derived Emx1-Eyfp-positive neural cell dataset (Borrett et al., 2020; GEO: GSE152281) (Figure S2B). This merged dataset (Figures 2A–2C) included Eyfp-positive TAPs, neuroblasts, and OPCs, demonstrating that cortical precursors generate postnatal progeny at E17/18. Early IPs were also present (Figure S2C), indicating excitatory neurogenesis was ongoing. Moreover, Cyclone analysis indicated some RP/NSCs were proliferating (Figure S2D). Thus, E17/18 cortical precursors proliferate and, as a population, generate both embryonic and postnatal progeny.

We characterized the transcriptional state of E17/18 cortical precursors (clusters 8 and 10 in Figure 2A) by merging them with E14 cortical RPs (Figure S1E) and P2 cortical NSCs (cluster 2 from Figure 1H). We also included P6/7 cortical NSCs subsetted from a merger of the aforementioned P6/7 Emx1-Eyfp datasets and our published P7 dorsal cortex scRNA-seq dataset (Dennis et al., 2024; GEO: GSE255405) (cluster 3 from the merged dataset in Figures S2E and S2F). This comparison (Figures 2D and 2E) showed most E17/18 precursors clustering separately (cluster 3) from E14 RPs and postnatal NSCs. We termed these distinct cells transition precursors.

We characterized this E14-P6/7 stem cell dataset using four approaches. First, we performed single-cell correlations, comparing each cortical precursor transcriptome to the averaged gene expression of E14 RPs versus P6/7 NSCs. This analysis (Figure 2F) defined a gradual transcriptional transition from E14 to P6/7, with E17/18 precursors almost equally similar to RPs and NSCs. Second, we analyzed the expression of a postnatal NSC gene signature we previously defined and modified slightly (see STAR Methods). NSC signature expression was relatively high in P2 and P6/7 NSCs and low in E14 RPs (Figure 2G). E17/18 cells were somewhat heterogeneous, but most expressed intermediate NSC signature levels. Consistent with this, violin plots showed E17/18 precursors were just starting to express postnatal NSC genes such as Gfap and Aqp4 (Figure S2G). Third, we performed Monocle-based trajectory analysis after regressing out cell cycle genes. This analysis (Figure 2H) identified a single gradual transition with E14 RPs followed by E17/18 transition precursors and then followed by overlapping P2 NSCs and P6/7 NSCs. A similar trajectory emerged when only Emx1-Eyfp-positive E14, E17, and P2 precursors were analyzed, in spite of the low number of Emx1-Eyfp-positive E17 precursors (data not shown). Thus, cortical precursors shift gradually from RPs to NSCs between E14 and P2, with a transition midpoint around E17/18.

Single-cell multiomic analyses define transcriptional states and chromatin accessibility of cortical precursors from E14 to P7

We asked whether the RP to NSC transcriptional transition was driven at the chromatin level by acquiring single-nucleus RNA-seq (snRNA-seq) and single-nucleus assay for transposase accessible chromatin-seq (snATAC-seq) from the same single nuclei using the 10× Genomics platform. We analyzed cortical cells at E14, E17, and P2 (before, during, and after the transition), and at P6/7, when dormant postnatal NSCs are well-established (Borrett et al., 2020). The transcriptomic data were processed through our Seurat-based pipeline, while the epigenomic data were processed using a separate pipeline based on the R package ArchR (Granja et al., 2021). In this ArchR-based pipeline, after removing low-quality cells based on metrics such as fragment count and transcription start site enrichment, we performed dimensionality reduction using an iterative latent semantic indexing. We clustered the data across a range of resolutions using Seurat’s graph-based clustering approach (Hao et al., 2024) and visualized the datasets in two-dimensional space using UMAP embeddings.

We first analyzed the E14 cortex, obtaining 4,780 paired transcriptomes and epigenomes. Clustering based on the transcriptional data identified all expected cell types (Figure S3A). We subsetted out and reanalyzed V-SVZ neural cells, defining proliferative and non-proliferative IPs and RPs using Cyclone (Figures 3A and S3B). We then reclustered this dataset based on chromatin accessibility and used marker gene accessibility to define the same cell types (Figure 3B). We compared the two clustering approaches, overlaying barcodes for a given epigenetically defined cell type on the transcriptome-based UMAP (Figure 3C). Cellular assignments using transcriptomic versus chromatin accessibility data were generally the same, with two exceptions. First, proliferative and non-proliferative cells were not distinguishable in the chromatin accessibility data (see IPs in Figures 3A–3C). Second, epigenome-defined changes in cell type sometimes preceded those observed transcriptionally. For example, some transcriptionally defined RPs clustered with early IPs epigenetically, presumably because differentiation genes become accessible prior to detectable mRNA expression.

We next analyzed the E17 cortex, obtaining 4,752 paired transcriptomes and epigenomes. RNA-based clustering defined all expected cell types (Figure S3C). We subsetted and reanalyzed V-SVZ neural cells and identified TAPs, neuroblasts, and some proliferative IPs and RP/NSCs (Figures 3D and S3D) as in the E17/18 scRNA-seq analysis (Figures 2A–2C and S2D). We confirmed that multiome-derived E17 RP/NSCs were transcriptionally similar to the scRNA-seq-derived E17/18 RP/NSCs by including them in the same single-cell correlation analysis (Figure 3E). We then reclustered E17 V-SVZ neural cells on the basis of chromatin accessibility (Figure 3F). As at E14, this analysis defined the predicted cell types, but did not distinguish proliferative from non-proliferative cells.

Finally, we acquired single-cell multiomic V-SVZ datasets at P2 and P6/7, dissecting the dorsal and immediately lateral V-SVZ. At P2, we performed two independent runs, transcriptionally identifying all predicted cell types in both runs (Figures S3E and S3F). For P6/7, we acquired one new P7 V-SVZ dataset (Figure S3G) and reanalyzed our previously published P6 V-SVZ dataset (Dennis et al., 2024; GEO: GSE255405) (Figure S3H). At both timepoints, we merged the two independent datasets, then subsetted and reclustered V-SVZ neural cells on the basis of the transcriptional data (Figures 3G, 3I, S3I, and S3J).

At P2, as seen in the scRNA-seq analysis (Figures 1H and 1I), there were TAPs, neuroblasts, and OPCs, but not early IPs (Figure 3G), confirming the cortical genesis of postnatal but not embryonic progeny. P2 NSCs expressed NSC but not RP genes and were predicted to be non-proliferative (Figure S3K), confirming they had acquired an NSC state. Reclustering of P2 V-SVZ cells on the basis of chromatin accessibility (Figure 3H) defined the same cell types, but again did not distinguish proliferative and non-proliferative cells. Similar results were obtained at P6/7 (Figures 3I, 3J, and S3L), although there were relatively more oligodendrocytes and fewer late IPs/newborn neurons.

The transition from cortical RPs to NSCs involves a gradual epigenetic switch and epigenetically distinct E17 transition precursors

We defined epigenetic changes during the RP to NSC transition by merging the transcriptionally defined E14, E17, P2, and P6/7 cortical precursors across multiomic datasets. Transcription-based clustering (Figures 4A and S4A) demonstrated that P2/6/7 NSCs were similar to each other but distinct from the embryonic precursors. The E14 and E17 precursors were also largely distinct from each other, although some proliferative E14 and E17 precursors co-clustered.

We reclustered the E14-P7 precursor dataset on the basis of chromatin accessibility (Figures 4B and S4B). As seen transcriptionally, all postnatal NSCs co-clustered, while E14 and E17 precursors were completely distinct from each other. To characterize the epigenetic differences underlying this clustering, we examined global genomic accessibility, defining differentially accessible genes as log2FC > 0.5 with an FDR < 0.05 (Table S1). This analysis showed that E14 RPs were more accessible at 6-fold more genes than E17 precursors (Figure 4C). However, this difference was reversed postnatally so that E14 RPs were only preferentially accessible at 1.4-fold more genes than P6/7 NSCs.

We examined the individual genes involved in these changes (Table S1). This analysis demonstrated that E14 RP chromatin was preferentially open for many transcription factor genes, including Hoxc12, Zic1, Irx1, Foxb1, Lhx1, Myt1, Neurog2, Zic4, Snai2, Hoxb2, and Tlx2 (Table S1). Among these were transcription factors associated with excitatory (Neurog2, Neurod1/2/6, Eomes) and inhibitory (Dlx1/2/5, Sp8/9, Nkx2.1) neurogenesis, suggesting that E14 cortical RPs are epigenetically primed to make inhibitory neurons even when they only generate excitatory neurons. By contrast, P6/7 NSCs were enriched for fewer transcription factor genes, and many of these (Stat2, Gli1, and Crem) were associated with signaling pathways. P6/7 NSCs instead showed enhanced accessibility for postnatal NSC genes such as Vnn1, Gfap, Pdgfrb, Aqp4, Mt3, and Veph1 (Table S1).

Three further analyses defined a gradual RP to NSC epigenetic shift with E17 transition precursors at the midpoint. First, heatmaps of genes preferentially accessible in E14 RPs versus P6/7 NSCs (Figure 4D) showed E17 transition precursors having a hybrid accessibility state that included genes from both timepoints. By contrast, P2 NSCs were very similar to P6/7 NSCs. The second analysis involved an E14 RP gene signature that we defined from a comparison of differentially expressed mRNAs in E14 RPs versus P6/7 NSCs (Table S2). We showed that 15 mRNAs, including Neurog2, Dmrt1a, and Bcl11b, were sufficient to specifically identify embryonic RPs versus NSCs (Figures 4E and S4C). Further analysis showed the chromatin of these 15 signature genes was also more accessible in E14 RPs than postnatal NSCs, and that E17 transition precursor gene accessibility was midway between the two (Figure 4F).

Third, we performed single-cell correlations of the merged multiomic dataset, initially comparing each precursor’s transcriptome to averaged gene expression for E14 RPs versus P6/7 NSCs (Figure 4G). As in the scRNA-seq analysis (Figure 2F), there was a continuous transition from E14 to P6/7, with E17 transition precursors positioned midway. We then did a similar comparison at the chromatin accessibility level, comparing individual epigenomes against averaged gene accessibility scores of E14 RPs and P6/7 NSCs (Figure 4H). This analysis defined a gradual transition in chromatin accessibility from E14 to P6/7, with E17 precursors again located midway.

The RP to NSC transition is reflected in the accessibility of regulatory transcription factor binding motifs

We asked how the gene regulatory landscape changed during the RP to NSC transition by calculating openness for transcription factor binding motifs within each nucleus, using chromVAR to analyze the chromatin accessibility data (Schep et al., 2017). This analysis showed that E14 RPs versus P6/7 NSCs were preferentially open for 495 and 293 transcription factor motifs, respectively (Table S3). E14 RP chromatin was preferentially open for motifs involved in excitatory neurogenesis, including binding sites for LHX2, PAX6, NEUROD1/2, NEUROG1, POU3F1/2, and ARX (Figure 4I). Unexpectedly, E14 RP chromatin was also highly accessible for motifs associated with (1) inhibitory neurogenesis, such as DLX1/2 and SP8, (2) oligodendrogenesis, such as OLIG1/2, SOX9/10, and TCF4, and (3) ventral forebrain identity, such as GSX2 and NKX2.1 (Figure 4I). This is consistent with our finding that E14 chromatin is more accessible around many of the same genes (Figure 4D; Table S1).

By contrast, P6/7 NSC chromatin was more open for transcription factor binding motifs associated with signaling pathways such as the GLIs and two transcription factor families important for astrogenesis, NFIA/B and STAT1/3 (Figure 4I) (Barnabé-Heider et al., 2005; Bonni et al., 1997; Deneen et al., 2006). Indeed, the analysis of the Gfap gene showed STAT binding motifs were more accessible in postnatal NSCs than in E14 RPs (Figure 4J), consistent with previous work (Fan et al., 2005; Takizawa et al., 2001).

We characterized motif accessibility in individual E14 to P6/7 cortical precursors (those in Figures 4A and 4B) by plotting accessibility in a heatmap (Figure 4K) or overlaying motif accessibility on the RNA-based UMAP projection (Figures 4L, S4D, and S4E). These analyses confirmed differences in motif accessibility and demonstrated that E17 precursors were in a transition state midway between E14 and P2. Some highly accessible E14 motifs were still open at E17, including those for SP8, NEUROD1/2, NEUROG1, and OLIG1, while others were already less accessible, including those for GSX2, POU3F1, PAX6, and DLX2. All E14-enriched motifs were less accessible in P2 NSCs. Conversely, some P6/7 accessible motifs, such as those for GLI1, GLI3, and NFIA/B, were already becoming accessible at E17.

Single-cell spatial transcriptomic analyses identify spatially distinct embryonic cortical V-SVZ domains at E18

To ask if the RP to NSC transition is spatially regulated, we used the Xenium platform for single-cell multiplexed in situ gene expression analysis. We analyzed E18 coronal fixed-frozen sections at the level of the lateral ventricles using a probeset targeting 347 genes: 247 from the Xenium murine brain panel plus a 100-gene custom probeset allowing better resolution of NSCs, astrocytes, and TAPs (Table S4) (Dennis et al., 2024). We defined a region of interest (ROI) including the dorsal cortex and dorsal V-SVZ (Figure 5A) and analyzed one section each from 4 mice. We processed individual sections, removed cellular doublets and objects with low transcript counts, and merged cells from different sections, obtaining 71,596 transcriptomes.

UMAPs and marker overlays (Figures 5B, S5A, and S5B) showed that transcriptomes were well-integrated and identified all predicted cell types, including excitatory neurons, interneurons, early IPs, late IPs/newborn neurons, OPCs, microglia, and vasculature cells. Spatial plots (Figures 5C–5H) demonstrated that cortical neurons were localized superficially, while early IPs and late IPs/newborn neurons were located in the SVZ and intermediate zone, respectively. Interneurons, OPCs, microglia, and vasculature cells were localized throughout the ROI, with a higher density of interneurons in the SVZ and intermediate zone.

This analysis also identified E18 RP/NSCs and cortical TAPs. To better characterize these cells, we reclustered the RP/NSCs, IPs, TAPs, and late IP/newborn neurons (Figure 5I). This analysis identified four spatially distinct RP/NSC groups, all expressing Aldoc, Slc1a3, Vnn1, Tspan18, and Hes5 (Figures 5J and S5C). One group included septal RP/NSCs located ventral to the cortical V-SVZ medial boundary, while a second included Gsx2-positive GE RP/NSCs ventral to the Emx1-positive lateral cortical boundary (Figures 5J and 5K). Also present at the cortical-GE boundary were cells highly enriched for Ano1 and Sema6a (Figure S5D). We speculate these comprise the antihem, a signaling center involved in cortical specification (Assimacopoulos et al., 2003).

The two other populations were spatially distinct dorsal cortical RP/NSCs, one including more laterally localized cells ending at the GE border (dorsolateral) and the other more medially localized cells ending at the septal border (dorsomedial) (Figure 5J). These two populations were highly enriched at the border regions but intermingled in the central region (Figures 5J, 5L, and 5M). Differential gene expression showed that dorsolateral E18 RP/NSCs were enriched for Veph1, and dorsomedial RP/NSCs for Slit2, Gfap, and Rmst (Figures 5L and S5E; Table S5). We confirmed the segregation of these mRNAs within the combined E17/18 scRNA-seq dataset (Figure S5F). We also characterized Ki67 expression as an indicator of precursor proliferation (Figures 5M and 5N). About 25–35% of E18 cortical RP/NSCs were Ki67-high, and nuclei of the positive cells were located along the ventricle wall in both dorsomedial and dorsolateral domains.

The switch to cortical olfactory neurogenesis first occurs dorsolaterally

We asked whether these spatially distinct E18 cortical RP/NSC populations were differentially associated with olfactory neurogenesis. To do so, we analyzed cortical TAPs, as defined by their enrichment for Ascl1, Egfr, Gsx2, and Ki67 (Figures 5I and S5G) combined with the expression of at least one of the four genes expressed in cortical but not GE TAPs, Emx1, Tfap2c, Neurod6, or Fezf2 (Figure S6A; data not shown). Most TAPs in the E18 ROI were cortical TAPs (Figures 6A and S6B), and 80% of these were located dorsolaterally (Figure 6B). Higher resolution analysis showed that within the V-SVZ the cortical TAPs were frequently associated with dorsolateral and not dorsomedial RP/NSCs (Figures 6C and 6D). Thus, the first cortical TAPs are likely generated by dorsolateral RP/NSCs.

Spatially distinct cortical RPs are also present at E15

The scRNA-seq analyses showed that most E17/18 RP/NSCs were transition precursors, but that some were more RP-like, and some more NSC-like. To define the spatial location of these distinct E17/18 precursor populations, we acquired Xenium-based transcriptomes for E15 RPs and P2 NSCs to allow a comparative analysis. At both timepoints, we analyzed sections and ROIs similar to those at E18 (Figure 6E), merging all individual section datasets for a given timepoint.

At E15, Xenium analysis identified all predicted cell types (Figures 6F–6H and S6C), and showed they were appropriately localized (Figures 6G–6L). Excitatory neurons were in the cortical plate and intermediate zone, late IP/newborn neurons in the intermediate zone, and early IPs in the SVZ. Cortical interneurons were enriched in the SVZ and intermediate zone, and OPCs were localized more laterally, consistent with the recent GE-to-cortex migration of both cell types. Vasculature cells and microglia were distributed throughout the ROI.

This analysis also defined three E15 RP groups, all expressing Aldoc, Slc1a3/Glast, Tspan18, Hes5, and Ki67 (Figures 6F, S6C, and S6D). One of these included Gsx2-positive GE RPs ventral to the Tfap2c-positive lateral cortical boundary (Figures 6M and 6N). The other two were cortical RPs that were spatially analogous to the E18 dorsolateral and dorsomedial precursors, with one extending to the GE border, and the other to the medial cortical border (Figures 6M and 6O). Dorsolateral RPs were enriched for Mt3, Tox, and Veph1, and dorsomedial RPs for Slit2 and Rmst (Figures 6O and S6D). Moreover, cortical cells adjacent to the GE were enriched for Ano1 (Figure S6D), potentially comprising the antihem. We confirmed the segregation of dorsolateral versus dorsomedial RP mRNAs within our E14 multiomic dataset; at both RNA and chromatin accessibility levels, Rmst was enriched in different RPs than were Veph1 and Tox (Figures S6E and S6F). Thus, at both E15 and E18, there are two spatially distinct cortical precursor populations.

At P2, non-proliferative NSCs and proliferative TAPs extend along the entire dorsal V-SVZ

A similar analysis at P2 identified all predicted cell types (Figure 7A) and showed they were appropriately localized (Figures 7B, 7C, and S7A–S7E). Excitatory neurons and interneurons were located within the cortical parenchyma, and late IP/newborn neurons were apparently migrating from the SVZ to the cortical layers. OPCs, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes were located in the parenchyma and nascent corpus callosum, with oligodendrocytes enriched in the latter. Vasculature cells were located throughout the ROI while microglia were enriched within the SVZ/nascent corpus callosum, consistent with a potential role in oligodendrogenesis (Shigemoto-Mogami et al., 2014; Willis et al., 2025).

This dataset also included NSCs. To ensure we could distinguish these from cortical ependymal cells, we subsetted out NSCs, ependymal cells, TAPs, IPs, and early neuroblasts (Figure S7F). This analysis defined Foxj1-high ependymal cells that only partially lined the cortical VZ at this age (Figures 7D and S7F), consistent with previous reports (MacDonald et al., 2021). There were also three groups of NSCs expressing Aldoc, Slc1a3/Glast, Hes5, and Tspan18 (Figures 7E and S7G). One included GE/lateral wall NSCs expressing Gsx2 but not Tfap2c or Emx1, and a second included septal NSCs located on the medial wall. The third included dorsal cortical NSCs expressing Tfap2c and Emx1 but not Gsx2. We did not observe distinct dorsolateral and dorsomedial populations at this age. Moreover, the majority of cortical NSCs were Ki67-negative (Figure 7F).

This analysis also demonstrated that the cell genesis situation had evolved by P2. By comparison with E18, Ki67-positive TAPs were more numerous and were, together with olfactory neuroblasts, located along the entire dorsal V-SVZ (Figures 7F–7H). There was also a group of dorsolateral corner precursors extending into the adjacent cortical parenchyma (Figures 7A–7C and S7H). These clustered separately from NSCs, and their location suggests they may be early neurogenic TAPs.

At E18, transition precursors are distributed along the entire cortical V-SVZ, whereas the first non-proliferative NSCs are located medially

We used these E15 and P2 Xenium datasets to comparatively define the transcriptional states of E18 precursors by merging RP/NSCs, TAPs, IPs, and late IP/newborn neurons from all three timepoints (Figure S7I). Most RP/NSCs clustered in 3 groups (Figures 7I and 7J). One (cluster 15, termed NSCs) included most P2 dorsal cortical NSCs and a small proportion of E18 precursors. A second (cluster 7, termed transition precursors) included most E18 RP/NSCs and small proportions of E15 and P2 precursors. The third (clusters 0 and 11, termed RPs) included most E15 RPs and about 20% of the E18 precursors. To validate these assignments, we analyzed the expression of Xenium probeset genes that distinguish RPs and NSCs (Figures 7K and S7J). Relative to the RPs, the NSCs were significantly enriched for the NSC mRNAs Vcam1, Vnn1, Aqp4, Thbs4, Slc1a3, Clu, Mdfi, Gfap, and Ttyh1. Conversely, the RPs were significantly enriched for Neurod6, Eomes, Cdh6, Ki67, and Fn1, genes associated with excitatory neurogenesis, cell adhesion, neuroepithelial function, or proliferation. Notably, transition precursors expressed intermediate levels of many of these genes.

These Xenium-based analyses defined most E18 precursors as transition precursors, some as RPs, and a small proportion as NSCs, in agreement with the scRNA-seq and multiomic analyses. We asked about the spatial locations of these three populations, quantifying dorsomedial versus dorsolateral distributions relative to total RP/NSCs (Figures 7L and 7M). We found that transition precursors and the less-abundant RPs were evenly distributed along the dorsal V-SVZ. By contrast, NSCs were highly enriched dorsomedially. A higher resolution analysis defined two additional differences between E18 transition precursors and NSCs. First, transition precursors were generally located closer to the ventricle than NSCs (Figure 7N). Second, many transition precursors were Ki67-positive while NSCs were almost all Ki67-negative (Figure 7N). Thus, at E18, cortical NSCs arise dorsomedially, while cortical TAPs occur dorsolaterally (see Figure 7O), implying the switch in cell genesis and acquisition of an NSC state are separable transitions.

Discussion

Here, we asked how developing cortical precursors switch from proliferative embryonic RPs making excitatory neurons to non-proliferative postnatal NSCs making interneurons and glial cells. We show that embryonic cortical RPs gradually acquire epigenetic and transcriptional characteristics of an NSC state so that by E17/18, they are in a transition state midway between RPs and NSCs (Figure 7O). These transition precursors are proliferative and, as a population, make both embryonic and postnatal progeny. By P2, only non-proliferative NSCs are present, and while not studied here, these newly generated NSCs will subsequently undergo a gradual transition to full dormancy, as we previously described (Borrett et al., 2020).

Our data also demonstrate that the RP to NSC transition is spatially dissociable from the coincident switch in cell genesis (Figure 7O). At E18, the first cortical NSCs arise dorsomedially while the first postnatal progeny are located dorsolaterally, where they are likely made by transition precursors. These findings imply that before they become NSCs, cortical precursors are likely able to generate both embryonic and postnatal progeny. In support of this idea, we show that embryonic cortical precursors are epigenetically primed to make both embryonic and postnatal progeny, and previous work showed they will make postnatal progeny when their local environment is changed by transplantation, exogenous ligand exposure, or manipulation of downstream signaling pathways (Barnabé-Heider et al., 2005; Hitoshi et al., 2002; Naruse et al., 2006; Voronova et al., 2017; Willaime-Morawek et al., 2006; Zhang et al., 2020). Thus, the cell genesis switch is likely due to a changing environment rather than intrinsic biases. What might induce this switch? One explanation involves GE-derived cortical interneurons; our spatial transcriptomic data identify increased dorsolateral density of these cells at E18 (see Figure 5G), and interneuron-derived factors such as fractalkine and Shh are important for the genesis of cortical OPCs and olfactory neuroblasts (Voronova et al., 2017; Winkler et al., 2018; Zhang et al., 2020).

If transition precursors make cortical glia and interneurons during late embryogenesis, then what happens postnatally when there are only NSCs? In adults, the lineage is clear; dormant forebrain NSCs are activated to generate TAPs, and these make glia and olfactory interneurons. The situation is less clear in early postnatal life when cell genesis requirements are high and cortical NSCs are becoming dormant. Perhaps some transition precursors become NSCs, whereas others become multipotent proliferative TAPs that support early postnatal cell genesis. Our P2 trajectory analyses support this idea (see Figure 1J), with neonatal TAPs rather than NSCs at the hub of differentiating glial and interneuron lineages. This idea is further supported by work identifying a transient postnatal pool of proliferative cortical precursors generating both interneurons and glial cells (Harkins et al., 2025). If this is the case, then perhaps most OPCs and interneurons generated in the first weeks after birth derive from transition precursors and not NSCs. Nonetheless, a switch to the adult lineage must ultimately occur, perhaps after P15 when NSCs have attained their final adult state (Borrett et al., 2020).

How do our findings relate to the previously proposed set-aside model? This model is based upon label-retaining studies identifying a population of slowly proliferating, set-aside embryonic RPs that generate the adult lateral V-SVZ NSC pool (Fuentealba et al., 2015; Furutachi et al., 2015). This work did not, however, identify set-aside RPs within the embryonic cortex, and a later study found that dorsal adult NSCs derive from precursors that proliferate perinatally (Marcy et al., 2023), consistent with our finding that dorsal NSCs arise between E18 and P2. It may be that the set-aside model makes biological sense for the GE, where developmental cell genesis is temporally separable from postnatal establishment of the NSC pool. By contrast, the continuous model we propose here (Figure 7O) may make more sense for the cortex and likely the hippocampus (Berg et al., 2019), where both events occur within the same perinatal time frame.

Our identification of spatially distinct embryonic cortical precursor populations is unexpected. Notably, two transcription factors that distinguish the dorsolateral and dorsomedial precursors were previously reported as differentially localized; SP8 is enriched in the medial cortex and is involved in cortical patterning (Borello et al., 2014), while TOX is enriched in the lateral embryonic cortical V-SVZ (Artegiani et al., 2015). Moreover, previous work implicated medial-lateral cortical gradients of the transcription factors Pax6, Sp8, Emx2, and Nr2f1 in establishing telencephalic identity and neurogenic potential (O’Leary et al., 2007). However, these gradients are thought to be important in earlier embryogenesis, whereas we document dorsolateral and dorsomedial precursors at E15 and E18. Nonetheless, it is intriguing that the first TAPs originate from dorsolateral precursors, while the first NSCs arise dorsomedially.

One final open question involves mechanisms driving the transition from proliferative cortical RPs to non-proliferative postnatal NSCs. While we haven’t defined underlying mechanisms here, it is likely the transition involves an interplay between the evolving cortical environment and precursor epigenetic state. Future studies elucidating this interplay may perhaps ultimately define strategies for promoting brain repair by allowing adult NSCs to reacquire their embryonic multipotency.

Method details

Single-cell isolation & 10x genomics

For single-cell isolation of the embryonic (E14, E17, E18) cortex, the meninges were removed and tissue immediately dorsal to the lateral ventricles—including the cortical layers and the V-SVZ—was collected. For the E18 cortex scRNA-seq dataset, thick coronal sections rostral to the hippocampus were created and the tissue dorsal to the lateral ventricles was dissected out. For single-cell isolation of the postnatal (P2, P7) V-SVZ, the cortex was removed to expose the lateral ventricles, and the dorsolateral V-SVZ was microdissected out and collected. During dissections, collected tissue was placed in either DMEM (Gibco) or Hibernate A without calcium (BrainBits) supplemented with 2% B27 (Life Technologies) and 500 μM L-glutamine (Cedarlane) and left on ice until subsequent processing steps.

For some datasets (E18 cortex scRNA-seq, E17 cortex multiome, P2 V-SVZ n2 multiome, P7 V-SVZ multiome), tissue collection was immediately followed by an enzymatic digestion step. Samples were incubated in 20 units/mL Papain with 1 mM L-cysteine, 0.5 mM EDTA, and 0.005% DNase I (Worthington) dissolved in Hibernate A medium without calcium (BrainBits) and DRAQ5 (Abcam) diluted 1:3000 for 30 min at 37°C to simultaneously enzymatically digest the tissue and label nuclei. Following enzyme treatment, cells were spun down at 300 g for 5 min and resuspended in media, either DMEM (Gibco) or Hibernate A medium without calcium, supplemented with 2% B27 (Life Technologies) and 500 μM L-glutamine (Cedarlane).

Following enzymatic digestion or immediately following collection, tissue was triturated using blunt needles with sequentially decreasing diameters (McMaster-Carr). Cells were then spun down at 300 g for 5 min and resuspended in 0.25% BSA (Jackson) in HBSS, after which the cell suspension was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer and incubated in 1:1000 propidium iodide (PI) (Abcam) to label dead/damaged cells. Samples were then FACS sorted using a MoFlo Cell Sorter (Beckman Coulter) or Aria Fusion A (Becton Dickinson) to enrich for viable (PI-negative, DRAQ5-positive) cells, which were collected in media, either DMEM or Hibernate A without calcium, supplemented with 2% B27 and 500 μM L-glutamine.

Single-cell capture and cDNA library preparation were then performed at the Princess Margaret Genomics Center (Toronto, ON) using the relevant 10x Genomics Chromium system protocol. Libraries were then sequenced using Illumina HiSeq 2500 (E18 cortex scRNA-seq), Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (P2 Emx1 scRNA-seq, P2 V-SVZ n1 multiome, and P7 V-SVZ multiome), and Illumina NovaSeq X (E14 cortex multiome, E17 cortex multiome, P2 V-SVZ n2 multiome) systems.

For wild-type (CD1 or C56BL/7) scRNA-seq samples, FASTQ reads were aligned to the wild-type mouse genome (mm10). For Emx1-Eyfp lineage-traced scRNA-seq samples, FASTQ reads were aligned to a custom reference genome containing the Eyfp open reading frame concatenated to the Ensembl mm10 reference genome. All scRNA-seq datasets were processed using 10x Genomics Cell Ranger v4 software (cellranger count function) with settings as recommended by the manufacturer.

For multiomic samples, FASTQ reads were processed and aligned to the wild-type mouse genome (mm10) using 10x Genomics Cell Ranger ARC v2 software (cellranger-arc count function) with settings as recommended by the manufacturer.

scRNA-seq data analysis

10x Genomics scRNA-seq data was analyzed using a previously described computational pipeline (Borrett et al., 2020; Carr et al., 2019; Dennis et al., 2024; Ebert et al., 2025; Innes and Bader, 2019; Mahmud et al., 2022; Storer et al., 2020; Willis et al., 2025; Yuzwa et al., 2017). In brief, cells with low unique molecular identifier (UMI) counts, red blood cells, cells with relatively high mitochondrial DNA content, and putative doublets were removed. Genes detected in fewer than three cells were removed and cell transcriptomes were normalized using the scran R package to account for read depth and library size (Lun et al., 2016). Cyclone was used to predict cell cycle phase (Scialdone et al., 2015). The Seurat package (v5.2.0) (Hao et al., 2024) was then used to process the normalized expression matrices. Following this, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using the top 2,000 highly variable genes. The 30 top principal components were used (i) to project the dataset into two-dimensional space as Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) embeddings using the Seurat RunUMAP, and (ii) to iteratively carry out SNN-Cliq-inspired clustering using the Seurat FindClusters function, at resolutions ranging from 0.4 to 2.4. Each dataset was analyzed by choosing the most conservative resolution at which cell populations of known identity separated into distinct clusters; resolution was only increased to interrogate cell heterogeneity. For further information on the resolutions used within this paper, see resolutions used for scRNA-seq data annotation and visualization below.

Cell types were identified based on expression of canonical markers. We used the following gene expression definitions: embryonic radial precursors (RPs), postnatal neural stem cells (NSCs), and astrocytes expressed Aldoc, Dbi, Fabp7, Hes1, Hes5, and Slc1a3; postnatal NSCs expressed Mdfi, Shroom3, Tfap2c, Tspan18, Veph1, and Vnn1; astrocytes expressed Agt, Aqp4, Hbegf, and Htra1; all intermediate progenitors (IPs) expressed Eomes; early IPs expressed Insm1 and Neurog1 in addition to Eomes, and were negative for late IP markers; late IPs expressed Neurod1 and Tbr1 in addition to Eomes; cortical excitatory neurons expressed combinations of Cux2, Fezf2, Ldb2, Pcp4, Satb2, Slc17a6, Tbr1, and Unc5d; transit-amplifying precursors (TAPs) expressed Ascl1, Egfr, and Gsx2; olfactory bulb neuroblasts expressed Dlx1, Dlx2, Gad1, Gad2, Sp8, and Sp9; oligodendrocyte lineage cells expressed Olig2 and Sox10; oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) expressed Cspg4 and Pdgfra; immature oligodendrocytes expressed Enpp6, Fyn, and Gpr17; mature oligodendrocytes expressed Mag, Mbp, Mog, Opalin, and Trf; striatal interneurons expressed Gad1, Ppp1r1b, Bcl11b, and Calb1; cortical interneurons expressed Sst, Pvalb, or Vip in addition to Gad1; Cajal-Retzius cells expressed Cacna2d2, Ebf3, Tmem163, and Trp73; pericytes expressed Cspg4, Pdgfrb, Des, and Rgs5; endothelial cells expressed Pecam1, Esam, and Plvap; ependymal cells expressed Acta2, Ccdc153, Foxj1, and Rarres2; microglia expressed Cd68, Cx3cr1, and Aif1; and proliferating cells expressed Ki67 and Top2a. Cells expressing markers like Gad1/2, Dlx1/2, and Sp9 in the absence of Sp8, cortical interneuron markers, or striatal interneuron markers were broadly categorized as interneurons.

UMAPs and gene expression overlays, generated using the DimPlot and FeaturePlot functions, were used to investigate gene expression and interpret cell clustering patterns. Heatmaps and violin plots were generated using the DoHeatmap and VlnPlot functions, respectively.

To merge or subset datasets, barcodes corresponding to the cells or cell types of interest were used to extract relevant gene expression information from filtered gene expression matrices. For the merging of datasets, the gene expression information from each respective dataset was then combined and the cells were then run through the pipeline as described above.

In brief, the RunHarmony function was used to reduce batch effects that manifested between cells originating from different datasets within a Seurat object. All scRNA-seq datasets were assigned to the following batches, which corresponded to datasets that were generated at similar times by the same individuals, and run through Harmony accordingly. Batch 1 consisted of all datasets from Borrett et al. After using RunHarmony, new UMAP embeddings were generated using the RunUMAP function, and the data were reclustered using the FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions at a range of resolutions.

Trajectory inference using transcriptomic data

Trajectory inference and pseudotime ordering of scRNA-seq data was performed using the Monocle 2 package (v2.20.0) (Qiu et al., 2017; Trapnell et al., 2014) in R as previously described (Borrett et al., 2020; 2022; Mahmud et al., 2022; Storer et al., 2020). In short, the raw expression data for the cells of interest was extracted and merged. Following this, the data were normalized using Monocle’s size factor normalization method via the estimateSizeFactors function. PCA was then performed using the same 2,000 highly variable genes obtained from our computational pipeline, as described above, with slight modification if cell cycle regression was to be performed (see cell cycle regression). Afterward, the cells were projected into two-dimensional space and clusters were assigned using Monocle’s density peak clustering algorithm via the reduceDimension (max_components = 2, reduction_method = “tSNE”) and clusterCells functions in Monocle. Genes to be used for pseudotime ordering were obtained by identifying the top 1,000 differentially expressed genes based on clustering at the relevant resolution (Figure 1J) or timepoints (Figure 2H) using the differentialGeneTest function. Expression profiles were then reduced to two dimensions via the DDRTree algorithm using the reduceDimension function in Monocle (max_components = 2, method = “DDRTree”), and cells were then ordered using these genes to generate trajectories via the orderCells function. The function plot_cell_trajectory was subsequently used for trajectory visualization.

Cell cycle regression

Cell cycle regression, in the context of clustering and visualizing scRNA-seq data (see Figure 1H), was performed by assigning cell cycle scores to the cells within the dataset based on their expression of cell cycle phase markers. Cell cycle scoring was performed using the CellCycleScoring function in the Seurat package. The ScaleData function was then used to generate a scaled expression matrix in which cell cycle-related heterogeneity was removed from the data by regressing out S and G2/M scores. All upstream and downstream steps were performed as previously discussed.

In order to regress cell cycle related genes from trajectories generated using Monocle, cell cycle associated genes used by Cyclone to assign cell cycle phase (Scialdone et al., 2015), along with S phase- and G2/M phase-related genes identified by Kowalczyk et al. (2015); Tirosh et al. (2016) were subtracted from the set of 1,000 ordering genes used to order trajectories in pseudotime, as described by Borrett et al. (2020). Upstream and downstream steps were performed as described in the relevant section.

Multiomic data analysis

snRNA-seq data was processed through the Seurat-based scRNA-seq pipeline described earlier. snATAC-seq data was analyzed using the ArchR R package (v1.0.3) (Granja et al., 2021). In short, fragments output files generated by 10x Genomics Cell Ranger were read and processed using ArchR, excluding low-quality cells with <3000 fragments and a TSS enrichment score <4. Cells were also removed based on high nucleosome ratio (>4) and blacklist ratio (>0.04) values. Genes were defined as a region containing the gene body as well as 5 kb upstream of the TSS and gene accessibility scores, which we used as a measure of the overall accessibility of a particular gene, were calculated using ArchR via the addGeneScoreMatrix function using default parameters. In short, fragments that mapped to a region 100 kb upstream and downstream of a given gene (unless this window overlapped with an adjacent gene, in which case it would be truncated accordingly) were used to calculate a gene score in a distance-dependent fashion, with distal fragments contributing less to accessibility calculations. Calculations were also normalized based on gene body size.

Dimensionality reduction was performed using an iterative LSI approach via the ArchR function addIterativeLSI with the recommended parameters. Clustering of the data, as implemented by the Seurat package (Hao et al., 2024), was performed across a range of resolutions (from 0.4 to 2.4) using the addClusters function. Each dataset was analyzed by choosing the most conservative resolution in which cell types of known identity separated into distinct clusters as expected; resolution was only increased to interrogate cell heterogeneity or if two cell populations of known identity, based on the expression of canonical markers, were not separating into distinct clusters as expected. The data were projected into two-dimensional space as UMAP embeddings using the addUMAP function. Imputation, using the addImputeWeights function in ArchR with default parameters, was performed using Markov affinity-based graph imputation of cells (MAGIC) (van Dijk et al., 2018) for clearer visualization of accessibility data. UMAPs and UMAP overlays, generated using the plotEmbedding function, were used to investigate gene expression and interpret cell clustering patterns.

Some clusters with unusually high fragment counts were considered to be low-quality cells and were removed. To identify cell types, we used the following marker genes: oligodendrocyte lineage cells expressed Olig2 and Sox10; OPCs expressed Pdgfra and Cspg4; mature oligodendrocytes expressed Mbp, Mog, Mag, Trf, and Opalin; immature oligodendrocytes expressed Fyn, Enpp6, and Gpr17; both NSCs and astrocytes expressed Slc1a3, Gfap, and Aldoc; NSCs expressed Veph1, Shroom3, Tspan18, Tfap2c, and Vnn1; astrocytes expressed Htra1, Aqp4, and Agt; TAPs expressed Ascl1, Egfr, and Gsx2; olfactory bulb neuroblasts expressed Dlx1, Dlx2, Sp8, Sp9, Gad1, and Gad2; excitatory neurons expressed Kitl, Ldb2, Neurod1, Satb2, Tbr1, and Unc5d; microglia expressed Cd68, Cx3cr1, and Aif1; striatal interneurons expressed Gad1, Ppp1r1b, Bcl11b, and Calb1; cortical interneurons expressed Sst, Pvalb, and/or Vip in addition to Gad1; Cajal-Retzius cells expressed Cacna2d2, Ebf3, Tmem163, and Trp73; pericytes expressed Cspg4, Pdgfrb, Des, and Rgs5; endothelial cells expressed Pecam1, Esam, and Plvap; proliferating cells expressed Ki67 and Top2a.

Merging and subsetting of the snRNA-seq data was performed as described above for scRNA-seq analyses. To subset snATAC-seq data, barcodes corresponding to the cells of interest were first obtained and the subsetArchRProject function was then used to remove all other cells; the data were then run through the pipeline as described above. To merge snATAC-seq data, an ArchRProject was first generated using all datasets to be merged. Afterward, if relevant, barcodes corresponding to the cells of interest were subsetted out using the subsetArchRProject function and the data were run through the pipeline as described above.

For some downstream analyses, gene accessibility scores, embeddings, and cluster assignments, which were calculated using ArchR, were imported into Seurat/Signac (Stuart et al., 2021). To do so, some code from the ArchRtoSignac R package (Shi et al., 2022) was consulted and adapted.

Peak calling and motif analysis

Cells were grouped into pseudo-bulk replicates via the addGroupCoverages function in ArchR. Pseudo-bulk replicates were generated within either clusters at a resolution sufficient to distinguish between the cell types of interest or the annotated cell types themselves, depending on what was more appropriate. The default settings (minCells = 40, maxCells = 500, minReplicates = 2, maxReplicates = 5) were used. Peak calling was subsequently performed with MACS2 (v2.2.7.1) (Zhang et al., 2008) using the addReproduciblePeakSet function in ArchR.

Transcription factor binding motif inference was performed using the implementation of chromVAR (v1.24.0) (Schep et al., 2017) in the ArchR package. A set of murine motifs were retrieved from the CIS-BP database (Weirauch et al., 2014) using the ArchR addMotifAnnotations function. Z-scores of the bias-corrected deviations of binding motif accessibility were used as a measure of the enrichment or depletion of motifs within individual cells. Z-scores were used for subsequent pairwise testing of differential motif accessibility between cell populations (see differential accessibility analysis below). For binding motif visualization, position weight matrices were extracted from the dataset’s motif matrix, converted into position probability matrices, and plotted using the ggseqlogo (v0.1) package (Wagih, 2017). Binding motifs included a pseudocount of 0.008 (Schep et al., 2017).

To identify accessible peaks containing specific transcription factor binding motifs (see Figure 4J), the ArchR getMatches function was used; the output (namely the matches assay) was then examined to identify peaks containing the motifs of interest. Chromatin and peak accessibility were then visualized using the ArchR plotBrowserTrack function.

Xenium tissue preparation & data processing

Brain tissue was collected from mice at E15, E18, and P2. Samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences) for 24 h at 4°C and subsequently cryopreserved in 30% sucrose for 48 h. Samples were embedded in O.C.T. (Tissue-Tek) and cryosectioned coronally onto Xenium slides (chemistry v1) at a thickness of 10 μm. Slides were then stored at −70°C until downstream steps.

All samples were processed as per the Xenium workflow for fixed-frozen tissue. In brief, slides were incubated at 37°C for 1 min and subsequently underwent a series of rehydrating washes in RNase-free PBS (Thermo Fisher), RNase-free water (Wisent), 100% ethanol (Sigma), and 70% ethanol (Sigma). Samples were then incubated in a decrosslinking buffer for 30 min at 80°C, followed by 10 min at 22°C. Following three PBS-T washes, tissue was incubated in a probe solution for 16–24 h at 50°C. For all datasets, the solution contained the same set of 347 gene targeting probes (see Table S4) in TE buffer (Fisher). Nonspecific probe binding was next washed off via PBS-T, a 30-min incubation in post-hybridization buffer at 37°C, and additional PBS-T washes.

Ligation of the hybridized probes was performed by incubating the samples in ligation enzymes for 2 h at 37°C. After a series of PBS-T washes, the ligated probes were amplified via rolling circle amplification; to achieve this, samples were incubated with amplification enzyme for 2 h at 30°C. Sections were then washed twice in TE buffer.

Autofluorescence quenching and nuclei staining steps were carried out next. Sections were washed in PBS, incubated for 10 min in a reducing agent, washed in 70% and 100% ethanol, and incubated in Xenium autofluorescence solution for 10 min. Following this, sections were washed three times in 100% ethanol, dried at 37°C for 5 min, and rehydrated via subsequent washes in PBS and PBS-T. Sections were then incubated in a nuclear staining buffer for 1 min and washed in PBS-T.

Following this, the sample was loaded into the Xenium analyzer instrument (software v2.0–3.1, depending on the sample) and subjected to multiple cycles of reagent application, probe hybridization, imaging, and probe removal. Initial pre-processing of captured z stack images was performed using the Xenium onboard analysis pipeline (v2.0–3.1, depending on the sample), as outlined by Janesick et al. (2023). In brief, our custom Xenium codebook was used to decode puncta into transcripts, with each codeword assigned to genes in the gene panel. Quality scores (Q-Scores) were assigned to transcripts based on maximum likelihood codewords, and negative controls were used to ensure accurate calibration. On-instrument nuclei segmentation was performed based on DAPI morphology, with each segmented nuclei assigned a unique cell ID.

All data were subsequently run through 10x Genomics Xenium Ranger v3 software (xeniumranger resegment function) with settings as recommended by the manufacturer to assign transcripts to the closest nucleus within a maximum distance of 2 μm.

Xenium data analysis

Xenium data were processed using a previously described computational pipeline (Dennis et al., 2024; Ebert et al., 2025; Willis et al., 2025). Xenium data files were first visualized in Xenium Explorer (v1.3-4), which was used to define regions of interest (ROIs) and export the IDs of cells corresponding to these regions; across all timepoints, these ROIs encompassed the dorsal V-SVZ, some more ventral regions of the V-SVZ, and cortical tissue dorsal to the lateral ventricles. The cell IDs were then imported into R alongside transcript count, feature, and coordinate data via the Seurat package (v5.2.0) to generate Seurat objects. Low quality cells were identified based on the mean number of detected genes and total transcript counts, and cells with metrics that were greater than ±2.5 standard deviations from the mean were filtered out.

Filtered data were transformed using SCTransform and dimensionality reduction was performed using PCA based on highly variable genes. This was subsequently used to construct a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph using the FindNeighbors function in Seurat. The data were then clustered using the FindClusters function across a range of resolutions (0.4–2.4) and visualized in two-dimensional space using UMAP embeddings. Spatial plots and UMAPs, generated using the ImageDimPlot, FeaturePlots, and ImageFeaturePlots functions, were used to investigate gene expression and interpret cell clustering patterns.

Each section was initially processed separately. Afterward, data corresponding to different sections were merged together in one of two ways. For merged datasets of the same timepoint (e.g., all E15 sections), data was integrated using the Seurat SelectIntegrationFeatures, FindIntegrationAnchors, and IntegrateData functions. For the dataset containing data from all three Xenium timepoints (see Figures 7I and S7I), merging was performed using the Seurat merge function, so as to avoid the loss of biologically significant differences between the timepoints. Where necessary for improved resolution of certain cell types (see Figures 5I and S7F), the Seurat subset function was used on merged datasets to subset out clusters of interest. Normalization, transformation, PCA, and clustering was performed on each merged dataset and/or subset as previously described.

Cell types were identified based on expression of canonical markers and positional information. We used the following gene expression definitions: oligodendrocyte lineage cells expressed Olig1, Olig2, and Sox10; OPCs expressed Pdgfra and Cspg4; immature oligodendrocytes expressed Enpp6, and Gpr17; mature oligodendrocytes expressed Mbp and Mag; NSCs expressed Tspan18, Veph1, Shroom3, Tfap2c, Vnn1, and Thbs4; astrocytes expressed Hbegf, Agt, Lcat, and Gjb6; TAPs expressed Ascl1, Egfr, and Gsx2; olfactory bulb neuroblasts expressed Dlx1, Dlx2, Dlx5, Sp8, and Sp9; cortical excitatory neurons expressed Neurod6 and Slc17a7 as well as Bcl11b, Cux2, Fezf2, and/or Satb2; early IPs highly expressed Eomes and only lowly expressed Neurod1; late IPs expressed Eomes and Neurod1; microglia expressed Aif1, Trem2, and Cd68; striatal interneurons expressed Ppp1r1b, Calb1, and Bcl11b; cortical interneurons expressed Sst, Pvalb, and/or Vip in addition to Gad1; septal interneurons expressed Gad1, Sp8, Cacna2d2, and Calb2; endothelial cells expressed Pecam1, Acvrl1, Adgrl4, and Car4; ependymal cells expressed Foxj1; choroid plexus expressed Clic6; proliferating cells expressed Ki67. Dorsolateral cells in the P2 dataset that expressed NSC markers and localized relatively far from the ventricle were termed “corner precursors”.

Each dataset was analyzed by choosing the most conservative resolution; resolution was only increased to interrogate cell heterogeneity or if two cell populations of known identity, based on the expression of canonical markers, were not separating into distinct clusters as expected.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Differential gene expression analysis

Differential gene expression analysis of scRNA-seq data were performed using the FindMarkers function in Seurat. Expression levels were tested using a Wilcoxon Rank-Sum Test. A Bonferroni-adjusted p-value smaller than 0.05 (padj < 0.05) was considered statistically significant, and data was further filtered to only include genes with an average fold-change of greater than 1.3 (FC > 1.3), expression in at least 10% of cells within a given cluster, and a difference in the percent of cells expressing the gene between the groups being compared of at least 10%.

Differential gene expression analysis of Xenium data was performed using SCT-normalized expression data. This was performed using the PrepSCTFindMarkers function followed by the FindMarkers function in Seurat. Expression levels were tested using a Wilcoxon Rank-Sum Test. A Bonferroni-adjusted p-value smaller than 0.05 (padj < 0.05) was considered statistically significant.

Differential accessibility analysis

Bias-matched differential accessibility testing (of genes and transcription factor binding motifs) of multiomic data was performed using the ArchR getMarkerFeatures function. For differential gene accessibility comparisons, E14 RPs were compared against precursors from other timepoints (E17 RP/NSCs, P2 NSCs, and P6/7 NSCs); cells used for the comparisons were selected based on TSS enrichment values in order to reduce potential bias. Accessibility levels were tested using a Wilcoxon Rank-Sum Test. A false detection rate smaller than 0.05 (FDR <0.05) combined with a log fold-change greater than 0.5 (log2FC > 0.5) was considered statistically significant. For transcription factor motif accessibility comparisons, E14 RPs were compared against P6/7 NSCs; cells used for the comparison were selected based on TSS enrichment values, log-normalized fragment counts, and log-normalized transcript counts in order to reduce potential bias. Z-scores were tested using a Wilcoxon Rank-Sum Test. A false detection rate smaller than 0.05 (FDR <0.05) was considered statistically significant.

Gene signature analysis

Scaled expression or accessibility of multiple genes was calculated using the AddModuleScore function in R (see Figures 2G, 4E, 4F, S1B, and S4C). Module scores were calculated using the log-normalized gene expression or gene accessibility scores. To calculate the overall expression/accessibility of the postnatal NSC signature, the following signature from Borrett et al. (2020) was used, with modifications: Sfrp1, Veph1, Tnc, Thbs4, Vnn1, Cpe, Igfbp5, Sparc, Mdfi, and Ccdc80. Genes were removed from the original signature if they were (i) non-specific to NSCs, (ii) sparsely expressed, or (iii) broadly expressed in cortical RP/NSCs.

The followed embryonic RP signature was defined based on differential expression analyses comparing E14 RPs and P6/7 NSCs (Table S2): Col2a1, Neurog2, Car14, Sema5a, Bcl11b, Basp1, Neurod2, Eomes, Neurod6, Sdc1, Trp53i11, Jpt2, Nme4, Dmrta1, and Myo1b.

Pearson correlation analysis

Two types of correlation analyses were performed within this study. In the first, the expression of each gene was averaged within specific clusters/cell types of interest to obtain averaged transcriptomes for each group. Cell cycle associated genes used by Cyclone to assign cell cycle phase (Scialdone et al., 2015), along with S phase- and G2/M phase-related genes identified by Kowalczyk et al. (2015); Tirosh et al. (2016) were excluded from this analysis. Normalized expression values were then correlated via Pearson correlation using the cor.test function in R. For each comparison, the Pearson correlation coefficient (r) value is provided in the figure, figure legend, and results text.

In the second type of correlation analysis, transcriptomes or epigenomes of individual cells were compared against averaged transcriptomes/epigenomes, once more using the cor.test function in R. For transcriptomic data, correlations were performed using log-normalized gene expression data; for epigenetic data, correlations were performed using log-normalized gene accessibility scores as calculated using the ArchR package. Individual cells were then plotted as a scatterplot, where placement along each axes represents the difference between the cell’s correlation to one averaged transcriptome/epigenome versus another.

Xenium-based quantification & statistical analysis

All Xenium-based quantification was performed using the E18 dataset, which contained 1 coronal section each from 4 CD1 E18 brains collected across two litters. Roughly equivalent ROIs were used for each section; these ROIs all contained the entirety of the dorsal wall of the V-SVZ, the dorsal regions of the lateral and septal walls, the intermediate zone, and part of the cortical plate.

To quantify highly Ki67-expressing cells within dorsolateral and dorsomedial RP/NSCs in the E18 cortex (Figure 5N), we extracted the barcodes of cells that detectibly expressed 4 or more Ki67 mRNAs. These Ki67-expressing cells were then cross-referenced with dorsolateral and dorsomedial RP/NSCs, as defined by the E18 subset (see Figure 5I), and the data were expressed as the proportions of cells within each RP/NSC population that expressed 4+ Ki67 mRNAs. Statistical significance was determined using a paired parametric t test, wherein values corresponding to the same section were paired, with p < 0.05 considered to be statistically significant.

To quantify the distribution of cortically derived TAPs within the E18 cortex (see Figures 6A and 6B), we used TAPs as identified by the E18 subset (Figure 5I). We defined TAPs of cortical origin as those expressing at least one Emx1, Fezf2, or Neurod6 transcript, or at least two Tfap2c transcripts (to account for higher overall expression). The dorsal V-SVZ was then divided into dorsolateral and dorsomedial regions of equal length, and the number of cortically derived cells dorsal to these regions were quantified. TAPs were excluded if they were medial or lateral to the dorsal V-SVZ. The data were expressed as the proportion of cortical TAPs across both regions that were adjacent to either the dorsolateral or dorsomedial V-SVZ. Statistical significance was determined using a paired parametric t test, wherein values corresponding to the same section were paired, with p < 0.05 considered to be statistically significant.

To quantify the identities of RP/NSCs adjacent to TAPs at E18 (see Figures 6C and 6D), we used TAPs, dorsolateral RP/NSCs, and dorsomedial RP/NSCs as defined by the E18 subset (Figure 5I). Cell type annotations were imported into and visualized in Xenium Explorer (v4) and manually quantified using Photoshop (Adobe). A TAP was considered adjacent to an RP/NSC if it was either (i) directly touching an RP/NSC, (ii) in close proximity to an RP/NSC without any cells directly between them, or (iii) directly touching another TAP considered to be adjacent to an RP/NSC. TAPs were excluded if they were medial or lateral to the dorsal V-SVZ. The data were expressed as the proportion of total RP/NSC-adjacent TAPs that were adjacent to either dorsolateral RP/NSCs, dorsomedial RP/NSCs, or at least one of each. Statistical significance was determined using a paired parametric t test, and values corresponding to the same section were paired, with p < 0.05 considered to be statistically significant.

To quantify the proportions of E15, E18, and P2 RP/NSCs that fell into different cortical precursor subtypes (see Figure 7J), we determined the number of precursors per section within the E15, E18, P2 precursor merge (see Figures 7I and S7I) that were present in clusters 0/11 (cortical RPs), cluster 7 (transition precursors), and cluster 15 (cortical NSCs). These values were then divided by the total number of RP/NSCs per section within the merge, which included all cortical and GE/lateral RP/NSCs (Figure S7I, clusters 0, 7, 10, 11, 15, 20), in order to obtain the proportion of RP/NSCs per section within each cluster of interest.

To quantify the distribution of RPs, transition precursors, and NSCs (see Figure 7L), we defined cells as per the E15/E18/P2 precursor merge (Figure 7I) and visualized E18 cells that clustered with cortical RPs (clusters 0 and 11), cortical transition precursors (cluster 7), and cortical NSCs (cluster 15) within the E18 cortex. As per the TAP localization analysis, the dorsal V-SVZ was then divided into dorsolateral and dorsomedial regions of equal length, and the number of cortically derived cells dorsal to these regions were quantified. Precursors were excluded if they were medial or lateral to the dorsal V-SVZ. The precursors present within the dorsolateral and dorsomedial V-SVZ were quantified using Illustrator (Adobe); the proportions of precursors per group that were localized to each region was then quantified. To account for differences in overall precursor abundance between the dorsomedial and dorsolateral V-SVZ, dorsolateral and dorsomedial proportions were divided by the overall distribution of precursors (clusters 0, 7, 11, 15) per section. In the resulting normalized data, a value of 1 represents a cluster distribution that closely mirrors the overall distribution of precursors; values greater or lower than 1 represent either an over- or under-representation of precursors, respectively. Statistical significance between differentially distributed precursors within each cluster was determined using a two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc comparisons, with p < 0.05 considered to be statistically significant.

For all plots, error bars indicate standard deviation (SD). Statistical analyses were performed using Prism 10 (GraphPad). Statistics used for computational analyses are described in the relevant sections.
