Matrices with cyclically monotone rows and Cantor numeration systems
Pavel Šťovíček, Edita Pelantová

Abstract
We study a class of square matrices with non-negative elements which have cyclically monotone rows in the sense that each row of a matrix from the class consists of a cyclically non-increasing sequence of numbers starting from a maximal element on the diagonal. We prove that if every diagonal element is strictly larger than all other elements in the respective row, then the matrix is regular. This property enables us to solve an open problem that comes from the theory of non-standard numeration systems, also called Cantor numeration systems. The problem concerns a one-to-one relationship between Cantor real bases, which are supposed to be alternate, that is, periodic with a period p p , and lists of p p sequences of non-negative integers satisfying the so-called Parry condition.

Introduction

Our main motivation in this paper is an open problem concerning the Cantor numeration systems.
To solve it, we introduce and study a special class of square matrices whose rows are
cyclically monotone. In more detail, each row of a square matrix from this class
consists of a cyclically non–increasing sequence of non-negative numbers
starting at the maximal element lying on the diagonal,
see Section
2
for a formal definition.
Theorem
13
, also in Section
2
, contains the main result
of our article stating that the determinant of every matrix from this class is non-negative and,
under a somewhat stricter assumption, the matrix is even regular.
Let us note that the theorem may also be regarded as a partial generalization of a known result
valid for circulant matrices
[
13
, Prop. 24]
.

Let us now briefly describe the nature and history of the above mentioned open problem.
The history goes back to Cantor, who was the first to use positional number systems
in which instead of one fixed integer
\beta>1
chosen as a base,
as customary in the decimal or binary system,
a sequence
\mathcal{B}=(\beta_{i})_{i\geq 0}
of integers
\beta_{i}>1
is employed.
In this numeration system, the
\mathcal{B}
-expansion of a number
x\in[0,1)

- usually denoted
(x)_{\mathcal{B}}
- is a string

a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots
of integers
a_{n}
,
0\leq a_{n}<\beta_{n}
, satisfying

x=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{a_{n}}{\beta_{0}\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{n}}\,.

(1)

Cantor focused on the problem of specifying conditions that guaranty the number defined
by the series (
1
) to be irrational.

It seems that Cantor’s original ideas had to wait almost a hundred years
for further intensive development.
In 1954, Cantor’s criterion for the irrationality of numbers defined by series
(
1
) was generalized by Oppenheim
[
15
]
.
Further generalizations have been later presented, for example, in papers by Hančl and Tijdeman,
see
[
11
,
12
]
. Another problem related to the Cantor series (
1
)
concerns statistical properties of the distribution of coefficients
a_{n}
. This topic
was already addressed in the 1950s by Erdős and Rényi
[
9
]
, by Turán
[
20
]
,
and later in the 1960s by Šalát
[
19
]
.
Furthermore, the question of the normality of numbers in this numeral system has become the focus
of recently published articles due to Airey and Mance and their collaborators, see
[
1
]
.
The numeration system, as originally introduced by Cantor, is a frequent subject of research
up to the present time, for instance, from the point of view of the theory of number approximations.
In this regard, let us just mention a very recent paper
[
5
]
.

While Cantor in
[
4
]
admitted only integer sequences
\mathcal{B}
,
Caalim and Demegillo in
[
3
]
and, independently of them, Charlier and Cisternino
in
[
6
]
,
being inspired by the beta expansion of real numbers introduced by Rényi
[
18
]
,
began to study
real Cantor numeration systems
.
In such systems
\mathcal{B}
is a sequence of real numbers greater than one satisfying

\prod_{n=0}^{+\infty}{\beta_{n}}=+\infty
.
Furthermore, condition
0\leq a_{n}<\beta_{n}
is replaced by another one that requires

a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots
to be the lexicographically greatest string
such that (
1
) holds (in the case of integer bases, both conditions
are equivalent).
\mathcal{B}
is called a
Cantor real base
.
If the sequence
\mathcal{B}
is purely periodic, the number system is called

alternate
. As shown in
[
3
]
and
[
6
]
, to an alternate base
\mathcal{B}
with period
p
one can assign
p
integer sequences

{\bf d}^{(\ell)}=(d_{n}^{(\ell)})_{n\geq 0}
,
\ell=0,1,\ldots,p-1
,
with the help of which it can be decided whether a string
a_{1}a_{2}a_{3}\cdots
represents
some number
x\in[0,1)
.

An important characteristic of sequences
{\bf d}^{(\ell)}
assigned to a base
\mathcal{B}

is that they satisfy the so-called Parry condition.
However, conversely, the question remained unanswered until recently
whether a base
\mathcal{B}
exists for any list of sequences that satisfies
the Parry condition and, if so, whether it is uniquely given.
In a recently published article
[
8
]
, the question of existence has been answered in the affirmative.
Furthermore, uniqueness has also been proven, but in some particular cases only, namely,
in
[
7
]
, in the case
where every
{\bf d}^{(\ell)}
is eventually periodic and, in
[
8
]
,
in the case where every sequence
{\bf d}^{(\ell)}={d}^{(\ell)}_{0}{d}^{(\ell)}_{1}\cdots

starts with a number
{d}^{(\ell)}_{0}\geq 2
.

In the present paper, we also contribute to the solution of this problem.
In Sections
3
and
4
,
with the aid of algebraic tools developed in Section
2
,
we are able to prove the uniqueness of the base
\mathcal{B}
for every list

{\bf d}^{(\ell)}=(d_{n}^{(\ell)})_{n\geq 0}
,
\ell=0,1,\ldots,p-1
,
of sequences that satisfy the Parry condition.

2
Matrices with cyclically monotone rows

Notation 1
.

As common,
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
,
p\in\mathbb{N}
, stands for the cyclic
group of order
p
. As a set,

\mathbb{Z}_{p}:=\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\},

and the group operation in
\mathbb{Z}_{p}
is the modular addition.

To simplify notation,
whenever an addition
j+k
, with
j,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}
,
occurs in any expression, the addition is understood as
j+k\mod p
.

The symbol
\mathbb{N}
stands for the set of positive integers, no special symbol
will be used for non-negative integers.

In this section, we introduce and deal with a special class of square matrices.

Definition 2
.

A square matrix

A=(a_{j,k})_{0\leq j,k<p},\ p\in\mathbb{N},

is said to have
cyclically monotone rows
if its entries are all non-negative
and its rows are cyclically non–increasing in the following sense:

\forall j\in\mathbb{Z}_{p},\forall k\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}\setminus\{p-1\},\ a_{j,j+k}\geq a_{j,j+k+1}.

(2)

If, moreover, either

p=1\ \text{ and }\ a_{0,0}>0

(3)

or

p\geq 2\ \text{ and }\ a_{j,j}>a_{j,j+1}\ \ \text{for every\ }j\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}

(4)

we say that
A
is a matrix with
strictly cyclically monotone rows
.

Example
.

Consider the matrices

\left(\begin{matrix}5&4&3\\
1&2&2\\
2&2&3\\
\end{matrix}\right)\ \ \text{and}\ \ \left(\begin{matrix}5&4&4\\
1&2&1\\
2&2&3\\
\end{matrix}\right)\!.

The former has cyclically monotone rows, but not strictly, while
the latter one has strictly cyclically monotone rows.

Remark 3
.

Let us list some simple properties of matrices with (strictly) cyclically monotone rows
that we will use in some proofs to follow.

1.

Both conditions (
2
) and (
4
)
are invariant with respect to
simultaneous cyclic permutation of rows and columns. Formally, let

A^{\prime}=(a^{\prime}_{j,k})_{0\leq j,k<p},\ a^{\prime}_{j,k}:=a_{j+1,k+1}\ \,\text{for all}\ j,k\in\mathbb{Z}_{p}.

(5)

If
A
has cyclically monotone rows or strictly cyclically monotone rows,
then the same is true for
A^{\prime}
. Moreover,
\det A^{\prime}=\det A
.

2.

If
A_{1},\ldots,A_{m}
are matrices with cyclically monotone rows and

\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{m}
are non-negative numbers, then the sum

\sum_{k=1}^{m}\lambda_{k}A_{k}

is a matrix with cyclically monotone rows. If, moreover, there exists a summation index

k
such that
A_{k}
has strictly cyclically monotone rows and
\lambda_{k}>0

then the sum is also a matrix
with strictly cyclically monotone rows.

3.

Let
B
be a principal submatrix of a
p\times p
matrix
A
(that is, a submatrix determined by a proper subset
I\subset\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
and obtained from
A

by removing rows and columns whose row index, respectively, column index belongs to
I
).
If
A
has (strictly) cyclically monotone rows, then
B
has (strictly) cyclically
monotone rows as well.

4.

Let
A
be a matrix of size
p\in\mathbb{N}
with (strictly) cyclically monotone rows and

c=(c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{p-1}).

be a non–increasing sequence of non-negative numbers. Fix an index
j
,
0\leq j\leq p-1
, and modify
A
by adding to its row with index
j
sequence
c
. Denote the resulting
matrix by
B
. Then
B
is a matrix with (strictly) cyclically monotone rows if and only if

j=0
or
j\geq 1
and

c_{0}-c_{p-1}\leq a_{j,p-1}-a_{j,0}.

(6)

Indeed, we only have to check the row of
B
with index
j
. Then
b_{j,k}=a_{j,k}+c_{k}
.
The situation is obvious for
j=0
. Suppose
j\geq 1
. Then surely

b_{j,j}\geq b_{j,j+1}\geq\ldots\geq b_{j,p-1}\ \text{ and }\ b_{j,0}\geq b_{j,1}\geq\ldots\geq b_{j,j-1}.

Hence, it is sufficient and necessary to guarantee that
b_{j,p-1}\geq b_{j,0}
.
But this is exactly the condition (
6
). Of course, if
a_{j,j}>a_{j,j+1}

then
b_{j,j}>b_{j,j+1}
.

Theorem 4
.

If a square matrix
A
of size
p\in\mathbb{N}
has cyclically monotone rows,
particularly, if condition (
2
) holds, then
\det A\geq 0
.

If, moreover,
A
satisfies (
3
) or (
4
),
that is, if
A
has strictly cyclically monotone rows, then
\det A>0
.

Proof.

We proceed by induction on the size of
A
. The case
p=1
is trivial.

Let
p\geq 2
. For an arbitrary but fixed
\ell\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-2\}
, we consider an array

A^{(\ell)}(x)=\big(a_{j,k}^{(\ell)}(x)\big)_{0\leq j,k<p}
of matrix-valued functions of
a real variable
x
defined by

a_{j,k}^{(\ell)}(x):=\begin{cases}x&\text{if}\ \,j=0,\,0\leq k\leq\ell,\\
a_{j,k}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(7)

Using the induction hypothesis we first show two auxiliary statements.

Claim 1.

The function
\det A^{(\ell)}(x)
of real variable
x
is non–decreasing. If, moreover,

A
satisfies (
4
) then the function is strictly increasing.

To demonstrate the claim, we realize that

\det A^{(\ell)}(x)
is a linear function of
x
, say
\det A^{(\ell)}(x)=\alpha x+\beta
.
Let us show that coefficient
\alpha
standing at
x
is non-negative, respectively positive if (
4
) takes place.

Modify matrix
A^{(\ell)}(x)

as follows. Subtract the first column from the columns with indices
k=1,\ldots,\ell
,
and add to the same columns the last column. Of course, this modification
does not influence the determinant. Let us denote the resulting matrix as

\tilde{A}^{(\ell)}(x)=\big(\tilde{a}_{j,k}^{(\ell)}(x)\big)_{0\leq j,k<p}

(in particular, for
\ell=0
there is no modification at all and so

\tilde{A}^{(0)}(x)=A^{(0)}(x)
). Then
\tilde{a}_{0,0}^{(\ell)}(x)=x

and
x
does not occur in any other entry of
\tilde{A}^{(\ell)}(x)
.

Hence the sought coefficient
\alpha
equals the minor of
matrix
\tilde{A}^{(\ell)}(x)
corresponding to indices
(0,0)
, that is

\alpha
equals the determinant of the principal submatrix
\tilde{M}
of

\tilde{A}^{(\ell)}(x)

obtained by removing the first row and column. Let us denote by
M
the submatrix of
A
also corresponding to indices
(0,0)
. We enumerate entries of both
M
and
\tilde{M}
by indices from
the range
{0,1,\dots,p-2}
. As observed in Remark
3
ad(3),

M
has (strictly) cyclically monotone rows.

By construction of
\tilde{A}^{(\ell)}(x)
, for a fixed row index
j
,

0\leq j\leq p-2
,

\tilde{m}_{j,k}=m_{j,k}+c_{k},\ \,k=0,1,\ldots,p-2,

where
m_{j,k}=a_{j+1,k+1}
and

c_{k}=\begin{cases}a_{j+1,p-1}-a_{j+1,0}&\text{if}\ \,0\leq k\leq\ell-1,\\
\vskip 3.0pt plus 1.0pt minus 1.0pt\cr 0&\text{ if }\ell\leq k\leq p-2.\end{cases}

In particular, if
\ell=0
then
\tilde{M}=M
is a matrix with (strictly) cyclically
monotone rows.
Suppose
\ell\geq 1
. We observe that
(c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{p-2})
is a non–increasing
sequence of non-negative numbers and, if
j\geq 1
,

c_{0}-c_{p-2}=a_{j+1,p-1}-a_{j+1,0}\leq a_{j+1,p-1}-a_{j+1,1}=m_{j,p-2}-m_{j,0}.

Thus we can apply Remark
3
ad(4), repeatedly for each row index
j
,
to conclude that
\tilde{M}
has (strictly) cyclically monotone rows.

We have found that, in any case,
\tilde{M}
is a matrix with (strictly) cyclically
monotone rows. Therefore, by the induction hypothesis,
\alpha\geq 0
(respectively,
\alpha>0
). Claim 1 is proved.

Claim 2.

Let
B=(b_{j,k})_{0\leq j,k<p}
be the matrix defined by

b_{j,k}:=\begin{cases}a_{0,p-1}&\text{if}\ \,j=0,\\
a_{j,k}&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

(8)

Then
B
has cyclically monotone rows and
\det A\geq\det B
.
If
A
has strictly cyclically monotone rows then
\det A>\det B
.

To prove this claim we exploit Claim 1 saying that
\det A^{(\ell)}(x)
is non–decreasing function
in the real variable
x
for every
\ell
,
0\leq\ell\leq p-2
. Hence,

\det A^{(\ell)}(a_{0,\ell})\geq\det A^{(\ell)}(a_{0,\ell+1}).

Moreover, it is clear from definition (
7
) that, for each
\ell
,
0\leq\ell\leq p-3
,

A^{(\ell)}(a_{0,\ell+1})=A^{(\ell+1)}(a_{0,\ell+1}).

Thus, for
0\leq\ell\leq p-3
,

\det A^{(\ell)}(a_{0,\ell})\geq\det A^{(\ell+1)}(a_{0,\ell+1}).

Furthermore, it is also clear that
A=A^{(0)}(a_{0,0})
and
A^{(p-2)}(a_{0,p-1})=B
.
It follows that

\det A=\det A^{(0)}(a_{0,0})\geq\det A^{(p-2)}(a_{0,p-2})\geq\det A^{(p-2)}(a_{0,p-1})=\det B.

If
A
satisfies even condition (
4
), in particular if
a_{0,0}>a_{0,1}
, then

\det A^{(0)}(a_{0,0})>\det A^{(0)}(a_{0,1}),

and so
\det A>\det B
. The proof of Claim 2 is now complete.

Under the induction hypothesis we showed that the determinant of any matrix
A

with cyclically monotone rows is not smaller than the determinant of the matrix
obtained from A by replacing all entries in the first row by the smallest element in
the row. Obviously, the resulting matrix
B
has again cyclically monotone rows.

To complete the proof of the theorem, denote by
B^{\prime}
the matrix obtained from
B
by
the simultaneous cyclic permutation of rows and columns described in (
5
).
As stated in Item 1 of Remark
3
,
B^{\prime}
has cyclically monotone
rows and
\det B=\det B^{\prime}
.
Denote by
B^{\prime\prime}
the matrix obtained from
B^{\prime}
by replacing all entries in the first row
by the smallest element in the row. Then
B^{\prime\prime}
has two constant rows, the first one and
the last one, and thus
\det B^{\prime\prime}=0
.

Claim
2
applied to
A
and
B^{\prime}
gives

\det A\geq\det B=\det B^{\prime}\geq\det B^{\prime\prime}=0,

where the first inequality is strict if
A
has strictly cyclically monotone rows.
∎

Recall that a matrix of the form

C=\left(\begin{array}[]{ccccc}c_{0}&c_{1}&\cdots&c_{n-2}&c_{n-1}\\
c_{n-1}&c_{0}&\cdots&c_{n-3}&c_{n-2}\\
\vdots&&&&\vdots\\
c_{1}&c_{2}&\cdots&c_{n-1}&c_{0}\end{array}\right)

(9)

is called
circulant
. Let us also mention that, among other well known facts concerning
circulant matrices, an explicit formula exists for its determinant
(see, for example, Chapter 5 in
[
21
]
),

\det C=\prod_{k=0}^{n-1}(c_{0}+c_{1}\omega_{k}+c_{2}\omega^{\,2}_{k}+\cdots+c_{n-1}\omega^{\,n-1}_{k}),\quad\text{where }\omega_{k}=\exp(2\pi ik/n).

Regarding circulant matrices, Theorem
4
has a direct corollary if we assume
that the first row of a circulant matrix represents a monotone sequence. More
precisely, sticking to the notation of equation (
9
), suppose that

c_{0}\geq c_{1}\geq c_{2}\geq\cdots\geq c_{n-1}\geq 0.

(10)

Corollary 5
.

Let the entries of a circulant matrix
C
defined in (
9
) satisfy (
10
).
Then
\det C\geq 0
. If, in addition,
c_{0}>c_{1}
then
C
is regular.

More is known in this particular case.

Theorem 6

(
[
13
]
)
.

Let the entries of a circulant matrix
C
defined in (
9
) satisfy (
10
).
Then
C
is singular if and only if
n=kd
where
k,d\in\mathbb{N}
,
d\geq 2
, and
the vector
(c_{0},c_{1},\ldots,c_{n-1})
consists of
k
consecutive constant blocks of length

.

We just note that the easy corollary following from Theorem

is in agreement
with Theorem

3
Alternate power series

Definition 7
.

Let
p
be a positive integer. A mapping
f:[0,1)^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
is said to be

alternate power series of type
p
if
for every
\vec{y}=(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{p-1})^{T}\in[0,1)^{p}

f(\vec{y})=f(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{p-1})=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}a_{n}\prod_{i=0}^{n}y_{i\!\!\!\mod p}

(11)

where
(a_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a bounded sequence of non-negative numbers such that
a_{0}>0
and

a_{n}>0
for infinitely many indices
n
.

Obviously,
f
is differentiable in any point
\vec{y}\in[0,1)^{p}
.

Lemma 8
.

Let
f:[0,1)^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
be an alternate power series of type
p\in\mathbb{N}
.
Then for all
\vec{y}=(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{p-1})^{T}\in(0,1)^{p}
and

j\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
the following inequalities take place

y_{j}\,\frac{\partial f(\vec{y})}{\partial y_{j}}\geq 0\qquad\text{and }\qquad y_{j}\,\frac{\partial f(\vec{y})}{\partial y_{j}}-y_{j+1}\,\frac{\partial f(\vec{y})}{\partial y_{j+1}}\ \ \left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}>0&\text{ if }j=0;\\
&\\
\geq 0&\text{ if }j>0.\end{array}\right.

Proof.

For a non-negative integer
n
let us write
n=kp+r
, with
k
and
r
being non-negative integers,
r<p
.
Then

\prod_{i=0}^{n}y_{i\!\!\!\!\mod p}=\prod_{m=0}^{p-1}y_{m}^{\,\,\sigma_{m}},\ \text{where}\ \sigma_{m}=\begin{cases}k+1&\text{if}\ \,0\leq j\leq r,\\
k&\text{if}\ \,r<j\leq p-1.\end{cases}.

For the purposes of this proof it suffices to notice that, for any given
n
,
the respective sequence of powers
\{\sigma_{0},\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{p-1}\}
is
non-increasing and, in addition,
\sigma_{0}>\sigma_{1}
if
n=0
.
Since

y_{j}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial y_{j}}\,\prod_{m=0}^{p-1}y_{m}^{\,\,\sigma_{m}}=\sigma_{j}\,\prod_{m=0}^{p-1}y_{m}^{\,\,\sigma_{m}},

all
y_{m}
are positive, all
a_{n}
are non-negative, and
a_{0}>0
, the lemma follows.
∎

Definition 9
.

Let
f^{(0)},f^{(1)},\ldots,f^{(p-1)}
be a
p
-tuple of alternate power series
of type
p\in\mathbb{N}
.
For
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
, we define
\psi_{i}:[0,1)^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
as follows:

\psi_{i}(\vec{y})=f^{(i)}(y_{i},y_{i+1},\ldots,y_{i+p-1})\text{ \quad for every $\vec{y}=(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{p-1})\in[0,1)^{p}$ }.

(12)

The mapping
\Psi=(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{p-1})^{T}:[0,1)^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}

is called
the vector-valued function associated with
f^{(0)},f^{(1)},\ldots,f^{(p-1)}
.

Lemma 10
.

Let
\Psi
be as in Definition
9
.
Then the Jacobian matrix
J_{\Psi}(\vec{y})
of
\Psi
multiplied from the right
by the diagonal matrix

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diag}\nolimits(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{p-1})
has strictly cyclically monotone rows
at every
\vec{y}\in(0,1)^{p}
.

Proof.

Since
\psi_{i}(\vec{y})=f^{(i)}(y_{i},y_{i+1},\ldots,y_{i+p-1})
, Lemma
8
implies

y_{i}\,\frac{\partial\psi_{i}(\vec{y})}{\partial y_{i}}>y_{i+1}\,\frac{\partial\psi_{i}(\vec{y})}{\partial y_{i+1}}\geq\cdots\geq y_{i+p-1}\,\frac{\partial\psi_{i}(\vec{y})}{\partial y_{i+p-1}}\geq 0.

(13)

This means exactly that the product of matrices
J_{\Psi}(\vec{y})
and

\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diag}\nolimits(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{p-1})
is a matrix with strictly cyclically monotone rows.
∎

Corollary 11
.

Let
\Psi
be as in Definition
9
, and
\vec{y}\in(0,1)^{p}
.
Then every principal minor of the Jacobian matrix
J_{\Psi}(\vec{y})
is positive.

Proof.

For ease of notation, in this proof we put
J:=J_{\Psi}(\vec{y})
,

D:=\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diag}\nolimits(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{p-1})
and
C:=JD
. Let
I\subset\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}

be an arbitrary proper subset and
J^{\prime}
be the principal submatrix of
J

corresponding to
I
, as described in Remark
3
ad(3).
Similarly,
D^{\prime}
is the principal submatrix of
D
corresponding to
I
, and
C^{\prime}

is the principal submatrix of
C
corresponding to
I
. Because
D

is diagonal, we clearly have
C^{\prime}:=J^{\prime}D^{\prime}
.

Lemma
10
guarantees that
C
has strictly cyclically monotone
rows and, as observed in Remark
3
ad(3), the same is true
for every principal submatrix of
C
.
Therefore, by Theorem
4
,
\det(C^{\prime})>0
. As all components of
\vec{y}
are
positive,
\det(D^{\prime})>0
. Consequently,
\det(J^{\prime})>0
.
∎

The paper
[
10
]
by Gale and Nikaido is devoted to mappings whose Jacobian
matrix has the property described in Corollary
11
.
Let us quote their main result.

Theorem 12

(

)

Let
\Phi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a differentiable mapping on a closed rectangular

\mathbb{R}^{p}
. If every leading principal minor of the Jacobian matrix

.

The following theorem is a straightforward consequence of the previous theorem and Corollary

Theorem 13
.

The vector-valued function

.

Proof.

Choose

Additionally, we can propose an alternative proof of Theorem

Theorem 14

(
[
14
]
)
.

Let
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
be a continuous vector-valued function, and assume that

f
is continuously differentiable on
(0,1)

p

\xi_{1},\xi_{2},\ldots,\xi_{p}\in(0,1)
and
p

\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{p}

Remark 15
.

Theorem
14
has an immediate extension that can be formulated as follows.

Let
\Omega
be an open convex region in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{p}

be a continuously differentiable vector-valued function. Then for every couple of points

\vec{a},\vec{b}\in\Omega
,
\vec{a}\neq\vec{b}
, there exist
p
points
\vec{\eta}_{1},\vec{\eta}_{2},\ldots,\vec{\eta}_{p}

lying in the interior of the segment
[\,\vec{a}\,,\vec{b}\,]
and
p
non-negative numbers

\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{p}
such that

\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2}+\cdots+\lambda_{p}=1

and

F(\vec{b})-F(\vec{a})=\bigg(\sum_{k=1}^{p}\lambda_{k}\,J_{F}(\vec{\eta}_{k})\bigg)(\vec{b}-\vec{a}),

(14)

where
J_{F}(\vec{\eta})
is the Jacobian matrix of
F
at a point
\vec{\eta}
.

Of course, to see it, it suffices to consider the vector-valued function
f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
,
f(t):=F\big((1-t)\vec{a}+t\vec{b}\,\big)
.

Combining the observation from Remark
15
with Theorem
4
we have
the following theorem.

Theorem 16
.

Let
\Omega
be an open convex region in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
and
F:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{p}

be continuously differentiable.
If the Jacobian matrix
J_{F}(\vec{a})
has strictly cyclically monotone rows at every point
\vec{a}\in\Omega
then
F
is injective.

Proof.

Suppose that
\vec{a},\vec{b}\in\Omega
,
\vec{b}-\vec{a}\neq\vec{0}
. Equation (
14
)
is applicable to
F
and, as noted in Remark
3
ad(2), the convex combination
of Jacobian matrices on the RHS is also equal to a matrix with strictly cyclically monotone rows.
Hence, by Theorem
4
, this convex combination represents a regular
p\times p
matrix.
Therefore,
F(\vec{b})-F(\vec{a})\neq\vec{0}
.
∎

An alternative proof of Theorem

.

Consider the mapping
T:(-\infty,0)^{p}\to(0,1)^{p}
,

T(x_{0},x_{1},\ldots,x_{p-1}):=\big(\exp(x_{0}),\exp(x_{1}),\ldots,\exp(x_{p-1})\big)^{T}.

is one-to-one and thus

is injective on

if and only if

is injective
on

J_{\Psi\circ T}(\vec{x})=J_{\Psi}(\vec{y})\,\mathop{\mathrm{}}{diag}\nolimits(y_{0},y_{1}\ldots,y_{p-1})\ \,\text{where}\ \,\vec{y}=(y_{0},y_{1}\ldots,y_{p-1})=T(\vec{x}).

By Lemma

is

Theorem

.
∎

4
Sequences satisfying the Parry condition

Let
\mathcal{B}=(\beta_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a Cantor real base, as explained in Introduction.
The definition of
\mathcal{B}
-expansion preserves the ordering of numbers, i.e. if
0\leq x<y<1
, then

(x)_{\mathcal{B}}\prec_{lex}(y)_{\mathcal{B}}
. This property enables us to define
the
quasi-greedy
\mathcal{B}
-expansion of unity

d_{\mathcal{B}}^{*}(1):=\lim_{x\to 1_{-}}(x)_{\mathcal{B}}

where the limit is meant in the product topology.
Let us recall some properties of
d_{\mathcal{B}}^{*}(1)=d_{0}d_{1}d_{2}\ldots
.

1.

d_{n}
is a non-negative integer for every index
n
;

2.

d_{0}>0
and
d_{n}>0
for infinitely many indices
n
;

3.

\displaystyle 1=\,\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{d_{n}}{\beta_{0}\beta_{1}\cdots\beta_{n}}
.

To formulate the Parry condition, we will use the notation

\sigma(\mathcal{B}):=(\beta_{n+1})_{n\geq 0}\ \ \text{where}\ \ \mathcal{B}=(\beta_{n})_{n\geq 0},

that is,
\sigma(\mathcal{B})
is a shift of a sequence
\mathcal{B}
.

Theorem 17

(
[
3
]
,
[
6
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}=(\beta_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a Cantor real base. A string
a_{0}a_{1}a_{2}\cdots

of non-negative integers is the
\mathcal{B}
-expansion of a number
x\in[0,1)
if and only if

a_{n}a_{n+1}a_{n+2}\cdots\prec_{lex}d_{\sigma^{n}(\mathcal{B})}^{*}(1)\quad\text{for every }\ n\geq 0.

(15)

Obviously, if the base
\mathcal{B}
is alternate of period
p
, then

d_{\sigma^{n}(\mathcal{B})}^{*}(1)=d_{\sigma^{n+p}(\mathcal{B})}^{*}(1)
for every
n\geq 0
.

Recall that the numeration system introduced by A. Rényi in
[
18
]
is a special case of the
alternate numeration system for
p=1
. This means that Theorem
17
, if specialized to

p=1
, is concerned with Rényi numeration systems. In this particular case, it has been derived
by Parry in
[
16
]
. But Parry’s name is now associated with condition (
15
)
in the general case as well.

Example
.

The classical decimal system is an alternate base system of period
p=1
and
d_{\mathcal{B}}^{*}(1)=d_{\sigma^{n}(\mathcal{B})}^{*}(1)=999999\cdots
for every non-negative integer
n
.

For the original Cantor base
\mathcal{B}=(\beta_{n})_{n\geq 0}
consisting of integers

\beta_{n}>1
, the quasi-greedy expansions of unity have the form

d_{\sigma^{n}(\mathcal{B})}^{*}(1)=(\beta_{n}-1)(\beta_{n+1}-1)(\beta_{n+2}-1)\cdots

for every
n\geq 0
.

Definition 18
.

Let
p\in\mathbb{N}
and
{\bf d}^{(\ell)}=d^{(\ell)}_{0}d^{(\ell)}_{1}d^{(\ell)}_{2}\cdots

be a sequence of non-negative integers for each
\ell=0,1,\ldots,p-1
.
For every index
n\geq 0
, define
{\bf d}^{(n)}:={\bf d}^{(\ell)}
where
n=\ell\mod p
.

We say that the list
{\bf d}^{(0)},{\bf d}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf d}^{(p-1)}
satisfies

the Parry condition
if for each
\ell=0,1,\ldots,p-1
the following inequalities
take place:

•

d^{(\ell)}_{0}>0
,

•

d^{(\ell)}_{n}>0
for infinitely many indices
n
,

•

d_{n}^{(\ell)}d_{n+1}^{(\ell)}d_{n+2}^{(\ell)}\cdots\preceq_{lex}{\bf d}^{(\ell+n)}

for every
n\geq 0
.

Remark 19
.

We emphasize that the lexicographic inequality in the Parry condition enforces the boundedness
of the sequences
{\bf d}^{(\ell)}
for every
\ell=0,1,\ldots,p-1
. In particular,
each term
d^{(\ell)}_{n}
is less than or equal to

H=\max\{d^{(0)}_{0},d^{(1)}_{0},\ldots,d^{(p-1)}_{0}\}
.

It is well known that the Parry condition holds for every list of sequences that is
associated with an alternate Cantor real base.

Proposition 20

(
[
3
]
,
[
6
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{B}
be an alternate real base of period
p\in\mathbb{N}
. Then the list

d_{\mathcal{B}}^{*}(1),d_{\sigma(\mathcal{B})}^{*}(1),\ldots,d_{\sigma^{p-1}(\mathcal{B})}^{*}(1)

satisfies the Parry condition.

The inverse relationship is a more demanding question. The existence problem has already been solved.

Theorem 21

(
[
8
]
)
.

Let
{\bf d}^{(0)},{\bf d}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf d}^{(p-1)}
satisfy the Parry condition.
Then there exists an alternate real base
\mathcal{B}
of period
p
such that

d_{\sigma^{\ell}(\mathcal{B})}^{*}(1)={\bf d}^{(\ell)}
for each
\ell=0,1,\ldots,p-1
.

Let us mention that in
[
8
]
, some combinatorial properties of the set of so-called

\mathcal{B}
-integers were used to prove the existence of a base
\mathcal{B}
associated with
a given list of sequences satisfying the Parry condition.
We are able to provide an alternative proof of the existence theorem. Since our proof relies solely on some
topological arguments we postpone it to Appendix.

In the article
[
8
]
, the question of the ambiguity of the base
\mathcal{B}

has been addressed, but only for lists

{\bf d}^{(0)},{\bf d}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf d}^{(p-1)}
of a particular form. Having
equipped ourselves with the tools developed in the preceding sections, we are now ready to deal
with the general case.

Theorem 22
.

Let
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
be two alternate real bases of period
p
with the same
list of quasi-greedy expansions of unity. Then
\mathcal{B}_{1}=\mathcal{B}_{2}
.

Proof.

Let
{\bf d}^{(\ell)}=(d^{(\ell)}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
, for
\ell=0,1,\ldots,p-1
form the list
of quasi-greedy expansions of unity. In particular,
d^{(\ell)}_{0}>0

for every
\ell=0,1,\ldots,p-1
.
Let
\Psi
be the vector-valued function associated with the alternate power series

f^{(0)},f^{(1)},\ldots,f^{(p-1)}
, where

f^{(\ell)}(\vec{y})=f^{(\ell)}(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{p-1})=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}{d^{(\ell)}_{n}}\prod_{i=0}^{n}y_{i\!\!\!\!\mod p}

Theorem
13
implies that there exists at most one
p
-tuple

\vec{y}=(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{p-1})\in(0,1)^{p}
such that
for each
\ell=0,1,\ldots,p-1
,

1=\psi_{\ell}(\vec{y})=f^{(\ell)}(y_{\ell},y_{\ell+1},\ldots,y_{\ell+p-1})=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}d^{(\ell)}_{n}\prod_{i=0}^{n}{y_{\ell+i\!\!\mod p}}.

Defining
(\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p-1}):=(y_{0}^{\,-1},y_{1}^{\,-1},\ldots,y_{p-1}^{\,-1})
,
we deduce that there exists at most one
p
-tuple
(\beta_{0},\beta_{1},\ldots,\beta_{p-1})

of numbers
>1
such that the alternate base
(\beta_{n})_{n\geq 0}
of period
p
satisfies

1=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}\frac{d^{(\ell)}_{n}}{\beta_{\ell}\beta_{\ell+1}\cdots\beta_{\ell+n}}\quad\text{ for each $\ell=0,1,\ldots,p-1.$}

This proves the theorem.
∎

5
Concluding comments

To show the irrationality of a real number, one must prove that the number does not belong
to the subfield
\mathbb{Q}
. This concept can be generalized to the irrationality with respect
to other subfields of real numbers, e.g., to the extension of the field
\mathbb{Q}
by
an algebraic number
\alpha
, which is usually denoted as
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
.
It would be interesting to find criteria for this type of irrationality using a real Cantor basis.

However, it should be noted that when applying the irrationality criterion, a Cantor base

\mathcal{B}
is used for this purpose that is not periodic, i.e.,
\mathcal{B}
is not alternate.
In such a case, the list of quasi-greedy expansions of unity associated with
\mathcal{B}

is infinite and also satisfies Parry’s condition. But the question we have solved here
for alternate Cantor bases has not been addressed yet at all in the case of non-periodic
bases.

A
The range of
\Psi

Let us discuss the range of the mapping
\Psi=(\psi_{0},\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{p-1})^{T}:[0,1)^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}

associated with a
p
-tuple of alternate power series of type
p\in\mathbb{N}
,
as introduced in Definition
9
.
For each
i=0,1,\ldots,p-1
we assume that the coefficients
a^{(i)}_{n}
in

\psi_{i}(\vec{y})=\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty}a^{(i)}_{n}\prod_{k=0}^{n}y_{i+k\!\!\!\mod p}

have these two properties:

\mathcal{P}1
:

(a^{(i)}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is a bounded sequence of non-negative numbers,

\mathcal{P}2
:

a^{(i)}_{0}\geq 1
and
a^{(i)}_{0}+a^{(i)}_{n}>1
for some
n\geq 0
.

Our goal is to prove the following theorem.

Theorem A.1
.

\Psi\big((0,1)^{p}\big)\supset(0,1]^{p}
.

Before we start with the proof, we derive an auxiliary statement and recall
two theorems from topology on which our proof is based.

Lemma A.2
.

Let
\delta\in(0,1/2)
and
r\in(0,1)
. Then there exist
\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}\in(0,\delta)

such that the
p
-dimensional cube
[r,1]^{p}
and the image
\Psi(\Sigma)
are disjoint
where
\Sigma

is the boundary of the cube
[\varepsilon^{\prime},1-\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}]^{p}
.

Proof.

By
\mathcal{P}1
,
(a^{(i)}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is bounded, which implies that each of
the series
\psi_{i}
is convergent even if
y_{j}\in[0,1)
for only one index
j
,

0\leq j\leq p-1
, while all other variables
y_{k}
,
k\neq j
, are equal to
1
.
Therefore,
\Psi
is continuous in the domain

[0,1]^{p}\setminus\{(1,1,\ldots,1)\}

Moreover, we can factor
y_{i}
out of
\psi_{i}
, which means that there exists
another power series
\omega_{i}
such that

\psi_{i}(\vec{y})=y_{i}\,\omega_{i}(\vec{y})
.
Consequently,

\psi_{i}(\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{i\,times},\ 0,\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{p-1-i\,times})=0.

This allows us to choose
\varepsilon^{\prime}\in(0,\delta)
such that

\psi_{i}(\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{i\,times},\ \varepsilon^{\prime},\underbrace{1,\ldots,1}_{p-1-i\,times})<r.\quad\text{for each }i,\,1\leq i\leq p-1.

By
\mathcal{P}2
, the function
\psi^{(i)}(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{p-1})
is non-decreasing separately in each variable.
Hence,

\psi_{i}(\vec{y})<r\ \ \text{for every\ }\ \vec{y}\in(0,1]^{i}\times\{\varepsilon^{\prime}\}\times(0,1]^{p-1-i}\,.

(A1)

Properties
\mathcal{P}1
and
\mathcal{P}2
guarantee that

\omega_{i}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime},\ldots,\varepsilon^{\prime})>\omega_{i}(\vec{0})\geq 1
.
Thus, we can choose
\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}\in(0,\delta)
sufficiently small so that

(1-\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})\,\omega_{i}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime},\ldots,\varepsilon^{\prime})>1\quad\text{for each }i,\,1\leq i\leq p-1.

Hence, for
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
we can estimate

\psi_{i}(\vec{y})\geq(1-\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})\,\omega_{i}(\varepsilon^{\prime},\varepsilon^{\prime},\ldots,\varepsilon^{\prime})>1\ \ \text{for every}\ \ \vec{y}\in[\varepsilon^{\prime},1]^{i}\times\{1-\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}\}\times[\varepsilon^{\prime},1]^{p-1-i}.\quad

(A2)

Now it suffices to realize that a vector
\vec{y}=(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{p-1})^{T}

belongs to
\Sigma
if and only if
y_{i}\in[\varepsilon^{\prime},1-\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}]

for each
i\in\{0,1,\ldots,p-1\}
and
y_{j}
equals
\varepsilon^{\prime}
or
1-\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}

for at least one index
j
.
Inequalities (
A1
) and (
A2
) mean that for every
point
\vec{y}\in\Sigma
.
\psi_{j}(\vec{y})<r
or
\psi_{j}(\vec{y})>1
hold.
Consequently,
\Psi(\vec{y})
does not belong to the cube
[r,1]^{p}
.
∎

We shall need a classical theorem due to E. L. J. Bouwer that is recalled below

[
2
]
.

Theorem A.3

(Brouwer’s Theorem on the Invariance of Domain)
.

Let
U
be an open subset of the Euclidean space

\mathbb{R}^{p}
,

p\in\mathbb{N}
, and
\Phi\!:U\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an injective
continuous mapping. Then
\Phi(U)
\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
is an
open subset and
\Phi
is even a homeomorphism of
U
onto
\Phi(U)
.

We now have all the ingredients needed to prove the inclusion

\Psi\big((0,1)^{p}\big)\supset(0,1]^{p}
.

Proof of Theorem
A.1
.

Due to properties
\mathcal{P}1
and
\mathcal{P}2
, whenever
\vec{y}\in(0,1)^{p}
,
all components of the vector
\Psi(\vec{y})
are positive.
As
\Psi
is continuous on
[0,1)^{p}
and
\Psi(\vec{0})=\vec{0}
, there exists

\vec{z}\in(0,1)^{p}
such that
\Psi(\vec{z})\in(0,1)^{p}
, too.

Choose
\delta\in(0,1/2)
and
r\in(0,1)
satisfying

\vec{z}\in(\delta,1-\delta)^{p}
and
\Psi(\vec{z})\in(r,1)^{p}
.
Let us consider
\varepsilon^{\prime}
and
\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}
assigned by Lemma
A.2

to such
\delta
and
r
.

Let
K:=[\varepsilon^{\prime},1-\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}]^{p}
.
We again denote by
\Sigma
the boundary of the cube
K
and by
{\rm Int}(K)

its interior
(\varepsilon^{\prime},1-\varepsilon^{\prime\prime})^{p}
.
By the choice of
\varepsilon^{\prime}
,
\varepsilon^{\prime\prime}
and
r
,
\vec{z}\in{\rm Int}(K)

and
\Psi(\vec{z})\in[r,1]^{p}
. Hence

[r,1]^{p}\cap\,\Psi\big({\rm Int}(K)\big)\neq\emptyset.

(A3)

Recall that alternate power series
\Psi
is continuously differentiable on
(0,1)^{p}

and, by Theorem
13
, also injective on this domain.
On the other hand, by Lemma
A.2
,
[r,1]^{p}
is disjoint with
\Psi(\Sigma)
.
Thus we find that

\mathbb{R}^{p}=\big(\mathbb{R}^{p}\setminus\Psi(K)\big)\cup\Psi(\Sigma)\cup\Psi\big({\rm Int}(K)\big).

where the union is disjoint, and

[r,1]^{p}\subset\mathbb{R}^{p}\setminus\Psi(\Sigma)=\big(\mathbb{R}^{p}\setminus\Psi(K)\big)\cup\Psi\big({\rm Int}(K)\big).

By Brouwer’s Theorem on the Invariance of Domain,

\Psi\big({\rm Int}(K)\big)
is an open subset of
\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
Note also that
\Psi(K)
is compact.
This means that the connected set
[r,1]^{p}
is contained in a disjoint union
of two open sets and, therefore, it must be entirely contained within one of them.
In regard of (
A3
), we conclude that

[r,1]^{p}\subset\Psi\big({\rm Int}(K)\big)\subset\Psi\big((0,1)^{p}\big).

(A4)

Let us emphasize that when choosing
r
in
(0,1)
we had to respect only one condition,
namely that
\Psi(\vec{z})\in(r,1)^{p}
. Of course,
r
can be replaced by any smaller
positive number. In other words, (
A4
) holds true for every
r\in(0,1)
, i.e.,

(0,1]^{p}\subset\Psi\big((0,1)^{p}\big)
, as desired.
∎

We have an obvious corollary.

Corollary A.4
.

There exists
\vec{y}\in(0,1)^{p}
such that

\psi_{0}(\vec{y})=\psi_{1}(\vec{y})=\cdots=\psi_{p-1}(\vec{y})=1.

Remark A.5
.

Note that Properties
\mathcal{P}1
and
\mathcal{P}2
are fulfilled for the alternate power
series associated with a list
{\bf d}^{(0)},{\bf d}^{(1)},\ldots,{\bf d}^{(p-1)}
of
sequences satisfying the Parry condition, see Definition
18
.
If we use the solution
\vec{y}=(y_{0},y_{1},\ldots,y_{p-1})^{T}
from the previous corollary and put

\beta_{i}:=y_{i}^{\,-1}
,
0\leq i\leq p-1
, we get a new proof of Theorem
21
.
