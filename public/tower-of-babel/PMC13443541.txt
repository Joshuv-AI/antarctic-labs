Creation of an air-stable surface electrene and its application to ammonia synthesis
Authors: Zhujun Zhang, Shiyao Wang, Jiang Li, Masato Sasase, Masaaki Kitano, Hideo Hosono
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractElectrenes are unique materials characterized by a surface floating electron layer, which results in an ultra-low work function, making them ideal candidates for catalyzing chemical reactions via electron donation. However, these materials are extremely sensitive to air and moisture, thereby limiting their potential applications. Here, we demonstrate that oxygen-doped BaSiN2 is an air-stable surface electrene. Its highly active surface, with an ultra-low WF of approximately 1.5 eV, can donate surface floating electrons to adsorbed N2, forming an N2-passivated surface, which can be constantly regenerated through hydrogenation of surface-activated N2. This alternating pathway, particularly with the assistance of Ru loading, effectively produce ammonia: the Ru/BaSiN2:O catalyst exhibits high ammonia synthesis rate of 43 mmol gcat−1 h−1 at 300 °C and 0.9 MPa, and much superior air stability compared to the most active electrides and hydrides-based heterogeneous catalysts reported to date. This discovery opens a new way for electrene-driven heterogeneous catalysis.Subject terms: Catalyst synthesis, Heterogeneous catalysis, Porous materials, Catalytic mechanisms

Introduction

Two-dimensional electron gases (2DEGs) are generated by the large dipole moment of semiconductors, which are commonly employed to enhance electron mobility1–3. However, since 2DEGs are created at the embedded hetero-interface of compound semiconductors, there has yet to be a successful application in catalytic reactions that occur exclusively at the surface. Electrenes are considered a type of material featuring surface 2DEGs, as they possess a surface floating electron layer (SFEL) with unique properties derived from their intrinsic nature, including ultra-low work functions (WFs), high electrical conductivities, high nonlinear optical susceptibility and diverse surface chemistries4–7. These attributes present significant potential for catalytic applications. Nevertheless, a major challenge lies in their extreme sensitivity to ambient atmosphere and the complexities involved in their synthesis and handling8–12. Addressing these limitations necessitates rigorous efforts to explore air-stable electrenes, thereby advancing the frontiers of material science and industrial applications.

Ammonia synthesis is a vital industrial process essential for the advancement of agriculture, chemical engineering, pharmaceuticals, and the renewable energy sector13. However, the activation of dinitrogen (N2), the critical step in ammonia synthesis, still relies heavily on the use of iron (Fe) and ruthenium (Ru) catalysts, which must operate under high temperatures and pressures at industrial conditions. This reliance perpetuates high energy costs and stringent equipment safety requirements14–17. The scaling relationship between the chemical adsorption of N2 and the transition-state energies of intermediates on transition metals (TMs) poses a challenge, making it difficult to achieve high-efficiency low-temperature ammonia synthesis with traditional TM-based catalysts18. Over the past decade, electride and hydride-based catalysts have demonstrated enhanced activity for low-temperature ammonia synthesis19–25. However, nearly all of these materials are chemically unstable in an ambient atmosphere. In essence, while these catalysts offer high catalytic efficiency for low-temperature ammonia synthesis, they are extremely sensitive to atmospheric exposure, leading to substantial activity loss even after brief contact with air. This represents a significant barrier to their industrial application.

Here we report a type of electrene, BaSiN2:O, which features an SFEL in its as-prepared state. This material demonstrates remarkable capabilities for spontaneous N2 activation and high efficiency during low-temperature ammonia synthesis, significantly enhanced by Ru-loading that accelerates the hydrogenation of activated N2. Our findings reveal that the strong dipole moment of the polar Ba surface, stabilized by a rigid Si-N layer with an opposing dipole moment, generates unique surface orbitals that can accommodate floating electrons through the natural doping of a small amount of oxygen. The WF for BaSiN2:O with an SFEL is as low as approximately 1.5 eV; however, when the SFEL interacts with N2, an N2-passivated surface forms, leading to an increase of WF to around 3.5 eV. This SFEL can be regenerated by hydrogen treatment to eliminate the N2-passivation layer. The hydrogenation of N2 on the surface of BaSiN2:O is significantly enhanced with the addition of Ru nanoparticles, resulting in superior low-temperature ammonia synthesis activity and remarkable air stability compared to most active electrides and hydride-based heterogeneous catalysts.

Result and discussion

Synthesis and characterization

We synthesized BaSiN2:O powders through a solid-state reaction involving Ba2N and Si3N4 in a stoichiometric ratio under a flow of NH3, N2, or Ar (Fig. 1a). The powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns show that each diffraction peak of the synthesized BaSiN2:O sample aligns closely with the standard pattern for BaSiN2 (Fig. 1a). The color of the prepared BaSiN2:O powders transitions from light yellow (NH3) to dark brown (N2) and black (Ar). The elementary analysis of BaSiN2:O (NH3) powder, conducted via Auger electron spectroscopy (AES) depth profile analysis (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1), quantitatively reveals that the atomic concentration of oxygen at the external surface reaches approximately 12%, remaining constant at around 4% in the bulk region. This spontaneous oxygen doping likely arises from minimal oxygen contamination that cannot be eliminated from the raw material (Supplementary Fig. 1a) or gas paths. The surface oxidized layer of the BaSiN2:O (Ar) powder becomes thicker (Supplementary Fig. 2), leading to a darker color. The total electron concentration of the BaSiN2:O powders reaches 1.98×1019 g−1 (NH3), 6.20×1019 g−1 (N2), and 7.70×1019 g−1 (Ar) (Supplementary Tables 1-3), as estimated by iodometry26, which correlates positively with the amount of oxygen doped in the samples. However, the electron concentrations in BaSiN2:O powders determined by electron paramagnetic resonance analysis (Supplementary Fig. 3) are as low as approximately 1016 g−1, three orders of magnitude smaller than their total electron concentration. Furthermore, the electron density (in units of g−1) of the as-prepared BaSiN2:O (NH3) samples increases linearly with an increase in their specific surface area, while the specific electron densities (in units of m−2) remain constant at approximately 0.5×1019 m−2 for all samples (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Tables 1–3). This value corresponds to about 70% of the number of surface nitrogen atoms. These results suggest that the electrons are primarily located at the surface of BaSiN2:O (NH3) rather than in the bulk. Notably, the number of electrons in samples prepared in N2 and Ar atmospheres significantly exceeds the number of surface atoms (Supplementary Table 2), indicating a substantial incorporation of electrons in the bulk, likely due to the formation of vacancies with high oxygen content (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Electronic structure and physical properties

The electronic structure of BaSiN2 is predicted by density functional theory (DFT) calculations. The (010)-Ba2 terminated slab, which has the lowest surface energy (Supplementary Fig. 4a), is the most stable surface and displays a novel surface state (SS) situated in the gap and close to the conduction band, unrelated to Ba, Si, or N atoms (Supplementary Fig. 5). The corresponding partial charge distribution reveals that this SS arises from additional surface orbitals (Supplementary Fig. 5c). When surface N³⁻ is partially substituted by O2−, the Fermi level shifts upwards and crosses the SS (Fig. 2a), leading to the formation of surface floating electrons that extend approximately 2.0 Å beyond the surface (Fig. 2b, Supplementary Figs. 6 and 7). This suggests surface floating electrons can be readily generated when surface N³⁻ is partially substituted by O2−, consistent with the surface electrons presence shown in Fig. 2a, b. Notably, even with a thick slab of seven layers of BaSiN2, substituting nitrogen in the center layer with oxygen still results in excess electrons residing at the surface rather than between the Ba layers near the oxygen atom (Supplementary Fig. 7). No significant surface Ba reconstruction is observed, primarily because the surface Ba layer is supported by a stable Si2N4 layer, where Si is coordinated with four nitrogen atoms and one Si atom. Additionally, the (010)-Ba2 terminated surface exhibits a notable negative dipole moment (−0.09 e·Å), creating an electric field directed toward the bulk that stabilizes the suspended surface orbitals (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 8). The synthesized BaSiN2:O sample shows no preferred orientation, with various surfaces, including (010), (100), (112), (022), and (111), observed via scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) (Supplementary Fig. 9). No significant surface reconstruction is noted for BaSiN2:O (Supplementary Fig. 10), supporting our theoretical findings that the strong surface dipole and stable Si2N4 layer enhance the stability of the Ba-terminated surface, facilitating the formation of surface floating electrons (Fig. 2b). Each Ba-terminated surface can accommodate excess electron introduced by oxygen substitution (Supplementary Fig. 11). Additionally, the emergence of floating electrons is linked to the formation of surface nitrogen vacancy (Supplementary Fig. 12). Previous reports suggest that leveraging surface dipole moments could facilitate the formation of 2DEGs, but this has primarily been observed in internal hetero-interface of semiconductors with polar surface1–3. BaSiN2:O represents the first-ever electrene exhibiting surface 2DEGs (or SFEL), distinctly different from 2D electrides that confine 2DEGs within inter-layer spaces.

The calculated WF of BaSiN2 in a (010)-Ba2 configuration is as low as 1.77 eV (Supplementary Fig. 8b), closely matching the experimental value (~1.5 eV) obtained via the Kelvin probe method27, as shown in Fig. 2c, d. Additionally, the ionization potential (IP) of BaSiN2 (1.99 eV) is only slightly greater than its WF, indicating that the Fermi level of BaSiN2 is situated near the valence band maximum (Supplementary Fig. 5a). This unique band structure endows BaSiN2 with promising physical properties, such as high carrier mobility. This study focuses on the application of its ultra-low WF for catalysis. Given that the WF is even lower than the electron affinity of the N2 molecule (~ 2.1 eV), direct N2 activation through electron donation in the absence of TM sites may be feasible11. To test this hypothesis, we conducted the following experiment: the as-prepared BaSiN2:O powders (approximately 0.25 g) were first pressed into a rectangular pellet (10 × 7 mm2) within an Ar-filled glove box. The WF of BaSiN2:O pellets was measured as a function of exposure time in an N2 environment (25 °C, 0.1 MPa) (Fig. 2c, d). The initial WFs of BaSiN2:O samples prepared in NH3 and Ar flow are around ~1.5 eV, close to the calculated value. However, this ultra-low WF cannot be maintained once exposed to N2, gradually increasing to ~3.5 eV over several minutes. In comparison, the WF of BaSiN2:O (N2) exhibits a stable high WF of ~3.5 eV (Fig. 2d) as it was already exposed to the N2 environment during the synthesis process. These results suggest that N2 molecules spontaneously adsorb onto the surface of BaSiN2:O to form an N2-passivated surface with higher WF. This finding is further corroborated by theoretical simulations (Supplementary Fig. 13). The active surface with SFEL on BaSiN2:O (N2) can be readily regenerated by H2 treatment at ammonia synthesis temperature (300 °C) to remove the surface N2-passivation layer (Fig. 2c), indicating that the active (010)-Ba2 surface does not undergo reconstruction during N2 exposure and H2 treatment. Ba2N is a 2D electride with floating electrons confined in the inter-layer space, and the electrene is obtained by peeling the cationic layer. However, this electrene is highly sensitive to air, likely due to its lack of chemical stability in maintaining its Ba2 polar surface28. In contrast, the crystal structure of BaSiN2:O (Ar) remains intact after exposure to air for one week (Supplementary Fig. 14). To the best of our knowledge, BaSiN2:O represents the first-ever example of an air-stable surface electrene. We propose that the regular (010)-Si2N4 inter-layer with rigid Si-N coordination plays a crucial role in preserving the (010)-Ba2 polar surface without reconstruction.

Catalytic performance

The BaSiN2:O (NH3) electrene demonstrates a noteworthy and stable activity of approximately 0.12 mmol gcat−1 h−1 at 0.9 MPa and 400 °C. In contrast, Y5Si3, known for its air stability, shows no ammonia synthesis activity (Supplementary Fig. 15). Ba2N, another 2D electride11, initially displays a high activity of 9.0 mmol gcat−1 h−1 but experiences a drastic decline to approximately 0.04 mmol gcat−1 h−1 due to the conversion to BaH2, caused by the reaction of anionic electrons with H2 during ammonia synthesis (Supplementary Fig. 16). To enhance ammonia synthesis activity, Ru nanoparticles (NPs) were loaded onto various samples (Supplementary Fig. 17). The Ru/BaSiN2:O electrene catalysts demonstrated significantly improved catalytic performance, with activation energies (Eas) ranging from 58.7 to 63.7 kJ mol−1, which is considerable lower than that of conventional Ru/MgO (95.6 kJ mol−1) and the Ru/Si3N4 151.8 kJ mol−1 catalysts. The optimized Ru/BaSiN2:O (HS) catalyst, with a surface area of 14.8 m2 g−1, achieved a catalytic activity two orders of magnitude greater thanRu/MgO catalyst at 300 °C and 0.9 MPa (Supplementary Fig. 17). This remarkable performance at low temperatures is attributed to its low apparent Ea and a positive reaction order for H2 ( ~ 0.64) (Supplementary Fig. 18), contrasting sharply with traditional Ru-based catalysts that generally experience high activation barriers and hydrogen poisoning23,29,30. The Ru/BaSiN2:O (HS) catalyst maintained a constant NH3 synthesis rate of ~23 mmol gcat−1 h−1 for 105 h (H2:N2 = 3:1 (75% H2)), and ~34 mmol gcat−1 h−1 for an additional 125 hours (H2:N2 = 5:7 (42% H2)), respectively, under a weight hourly space velocities (WHSV) of 36,000 mL g−1 h−1 at 300 °C and 0.9 MPa (Fig. 3a). No significant changes in crystal phases were observed (Supplementary Fig. 19), and Ru NPs remained highly dispersed with an average size of 3 nm after long-term stability tests (Supplementary Fig. 20), indicating exceptional catalytic and chemical stability under ammonia synthesis conditions.

The majority of highly active catalysts, such as metal hydride-based and electride-based materials, are often extremely sensitive to ambient air18,20–25,27, leading to significant catalytic deactivation and serious safety hazards upon exposure to air. Supplementary Fig. 21 shows that the Ru/BaSiN2:O (NH3) catalyst retained its activity after exposure to air through 7 cycles, maintaining comparable catalytic performance at temperatures between 275 °C and 340 °C, with Ea values between 50 and 53 kJ mol⁻¹. This indicates a relative insensitivity to ambient air. XRD analysis confirmed preservation of the crystal structure post-air resistance testing (Supplementary Fig. 22). Further stability tests using low-purity N2 gas (99.998%, ~5 ppm O2) showed a ~ 14% decrease in activity after 200 h, but the ammonia production rate could be restored to baseline levels with high-purity gas (Supplementary Fig. 23a). XRD measurements indicated that the crystal structure of Ru/BaSiN2:O remained intact after this duration under oxygen exposure at high temperature (Supplementary Fig. 23b), highlighting its durability against oxygen impurity. Ultimately, the optimized Ru/BaSiN2:O (HS) catalyst achieved an ammonia synthesis rate of 43 mmol gcat−1 h−1 at 300 °C and 0.9 MPa under a WHSV of 60,000 mL g−1 h−1 (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 24, Supplementary Table 4), which is higher than that of the most active reported catalyst, Ru/Ba3SiO5-xNyHz_HS (40.1 mmol gcat−1 h−1 at 1.0 MPa; specific surface area: 105 m2 g−1)23,31–37. There remains significant potential for further enhancing the low-temperature ammonia synthesis activity of Ru/BaSiN2:O by increasing its specific surface area. The high catalytic activity, stability, and air robustness of this catalyst suggest promising applications for the on-site production of ammonia powered by renewable energy in the near future.

Catalytic mechanism

Figure 4a presents the in situ diffuse-reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) analysis results of Ru/BaSiN2:O (NH3) catalyst under a mixture gas flow of N2 and H2 (or D2) at 30 °C and 0.1 MPa. Before introducing the gas mixture, the Ru/BaSiN2:O (NH3) catalyst exhibits a strong vibration band centered at 1966 cm−1 (Supplementary Fig. 25), corresponding to the N ≡ N stretching vibration of adsorbed N2. This band shifts significantly to lower frequencies compared to the stretching frequency of a free N2 molecule (2331 cm−1) and that of N2 adsorbed on negatively charged Ru nanoparticles, such as Ru/C12A7:e− (2176 cm−1)19. When the Ru/BaSiN2:O (NH3) catalyst is exposed to a flow of H2 and N2 mixture gases from 1 to 20 min at room temperature, two broad bands emerge at ~1418 cm−1 and ~3325 cm−1, which increase in intensity as the N ≡ N band at 1965 cm−1 diminishes (negative signal). The former broad band remains almost unchanged, while the latter one shifts considerably to 2431 cm−1 when the gas flow of N2 and H2 is replaced with N2 and D2. The broad band centered at around 3325 cm−1 is thus attributed to the NH stretching, as this peak is significantly red-shifted due to the isotope effect from NH to ND stretching (3325 cm−1 × [(14×16)/(15×28))]1/2 = 2427 cm−1). The more intense band at ~1418 cm−1 remains almost unshifted, suggesting it is unlikely to originate from the corresponding NH bending vibration. This band position agrees well with N = N stretching vibration in organic compounds with N = N bonds38. A similar intermediate formation was observed when the Ru/BaSiN2:O catalyst was exposed to the pure H2 (or D2) flow at 30 °C (Supplementary Fig. 26). These findings strongly indicate that N2Hx is the primary intermediate formed through the efficient hydrogenation of N ≡ N over the Ru/BaSiN2:O catalyst, which contrasts with other TM-based catalysts that produce ammonia via direct N ≡ N dissociation mechanism with significantly higher activation barriers. Notably, the formation of the N2Hx intermediate occurs even at room temperature, highlighting its remarkable low-temperature ammonia synthesis efficiency. As shown in Fig. 4b, a sustained ammonia formation rate of 13.5 μmol gcat−1 h−1 was observed over the Ru/BaSiN2:O catalyst at 30 °C and 0.1 MPa.

Although the bare BaSiN2:O sample can activate and reduce N2 molecules (Supplementary Fig. 15), its ammonia synthesis activity remains relatively low, primarily due to its limited hydrogen migration capability, as evidenced by a high H2 desorption peak at 600 °C (Supplementary Fig. 27). Upon loading of Ru onto BaSiN2:O, the H2 desorption peak significantly decreases to 338 °C. Furthermore, the addition of Ni, which exhibits a weak interaction with N2, markedly enhances the ammonia synthesis rate to 1.3 and 6.7 mmol g−1 h−1 at 300 °C and 400 °C, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 28). These findings underscore the necessity of loading transition metals to facilitate the formation of active dissociated hydrogen species. Consequently, the Ru/BaSiN2:O electrene catalyst demonstrates substantially higher catalytic activity for ammonia synthesis at low temperature than both the Ru/Y5Si3 electride and conventional Ru/MgO catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 29).

DFT calculations were conducted to elucidate the active sites, reaction mechanisms, and rate-determining steps (Fig. 4c, Supplementary Figs. 30–33). Initially, we found that N2 preferentially adsorbs at the interface of Ru cluster and BaSiN2:O (*N2-1: N2 coordinates with both Ru and Ba and is perpendicular to the side of Ru) with an adsorption energy of −2.0 eV, which is more stable than adsorption on Ru surface (−0.6 eV) (Supplementary Fig. 30). Due to activation by the surface floating electrons, the N − N bond length elongates to 1.17 Å compared to molecular N2 (1.08 Å). The significant energy barriers along the overall reaction pathway primarily arise from the sequential hydrogenation of N2 (*N2-2: N2 coordinates with both Ru and Ba and runs parallel to the side of Ru) to form *HN2 and *HN2H intermediates via an alternating pathway (TS2 → TS3: 1.2-1.4 eV), indicating that these reaction steps are kinetically relevant. This result aligns with the in situ DRIFTS analysis result, which indicates that N2Hx is the main intermediate. Following this, hydrogen is progressively added to *HN2H to produce intermediates of *H2N2H, *H2N2H2, and 2*NH3 via an alternating pathway. In contrast, the hydrogenation of *N2 to form *N2H and *N2H2 via a distal pathway must overcome significantly higher reaction energy barriers (TS2 → TS3: 1.5-2.2 eV), suggesting that ammonia synthesis on the Ru/BaSiN2:O catalyst predominantly follows the alternating hydrogenation pathways (Supplementary Fig. 33). However, the nitrogen isotope exchange rate is 1.7 mmol g−1 h−1 for the Ru/BaSiN2:O catalyst at 300 °C, while it is under detect limitation for the bare BaSiN2:O catalyst even at 400 °C (Supplementary Fig. 34). These results demonstrate the nitrogen dissociation on the Ru surface, and hence the dissociation route for the ammonia formation cannot be ruled out for the present catalyst.

Origin of catalytic activity

The stability of SFEL under reaction conditions is confirmed by measuring the WF of the samples subjected to various treatments. As shown in Fig. 5a, the WF of the as-prepared BaSiN2:O (NH3) sample remains stable at 1.2–1.5 eV in an Ar-filled glove box. After treatment in N2 flow at 300 °C, the WF rises to a constant 3.5 eV, indicating that surface floating electrons are captured by N2. Following this, the N2 passivated sample was treated in H2 flow at 300 °C, resulting in a WF curve similar to that of the as-prepared sample. We also measured the WFs for the Ru(0.5 wt%)/BaSiN2:O sample under identical conditions (Fig. 5b). The WF for Ru metal is ~4.7 eV, decreasing to 4.1 eV for the Ru(0.5 wt%)/BaSiN2:O sample, suggesting surface floating electrons transfer from the support to Ru. After N2 treatment, the WF increased to 4.4 eV, indicating that electron transfer occurs from the Ru metal to the N2 molecule or the surface floating electrons mainly combine with adsorbed N2 rather than transfer to Ru metal. These findings demonstrate that surface-floating electrons can be repeatedly generated under ammonia synthesis conditions. DFT calculation results indicate that Ru atoms on the top surface are strongly negatively charged by the surface floating electrons, whereas the bottom Ru atoms, which are coordinated with lattice N3−, exhibit positive charge owing to the strong Ru-N interaction. This charge distribution enables the Ru cluster to maintain an almost neutral (metallic) state (Supplementary Fig. 35). These findings are further corroborated by the results from Ru K edge extended X-ray absorption fine structure analysis (Supplementary Fig. 36) and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy analysis (Supplementary Fig. 37). The WFs of as prepared BaSiN2:O samples are experimentally estimated to be ~1.5 eV, which is slightly lower than the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy level of the N2 molecule (~2.1 eV), and much lower than that of Ru metal (~4.7 eV). This energy level difference enables electron donation from the BaSiN2:O to N2 molecule more easily (Fig. 5c).

The catalytic performance is influenced not only by the WF but also significantly by electron concentration. For supported Ru catalysts, both a low WF and a high electron concentration are essential for optimal ammonia synthesis activity27,39. We calculated the electron concentrations of SFEL and plotted them against surface oxygen content, along with the corresponding WF, using the Ba32Si32N64-xOx model (Supplementary Figs. 38 and 39). Our findings indicate that both WF and electron concentration decrease as surface oxygen content decreases. BaSiN2 without surface oxygen exhibits the lowest WF but lacks excess electrons. We investigated N2 adsorption on BaSiN2 with and without surface oxygen. As illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 40, the adsorbed N2 gains an electron from BaSiN2:O (with a Bader charge of -0.5), resulting in elongation of the N-N bond to 1.16 Å due to the presence of a surface floating electron. In contrast, N₂ adsorption on bare BaSiN2 without a floating electron is significantly hindered, where the Bader charge of adsorbed N₂ is only −0.2. This marked difference indicates that each floating electron acts as an active site for N₂ activation. Consequently, BaSiN2 without surface oxygen is expected to be less active due to a limited number of floating electrons. Accordingly, as surface oxygen content increases, both WF and surface electron concentration rise (Supplementary Figs. 38). While the increase in WF reduces reaction activity, the rise in electron concentration enhances the number of active sites, thereby increasing the reaction rate. This trade-off indicates that ammonia synthesis activity likely follows a volcano-type dependence, as shown schematically in Supplementary Fig. 41. Our experimental data reveal that as surface oxygen content increases from 12 to 22%, the WF rises from approximately 1.6 to 3.5 eV, while ammonia synthesis activity decreases from 16.9 to 7.8 mmol g−1 h−1 at 300 °C and 0.9 MPa (see Fig. 5d and Supplementary Fig. 42). These results are positioned on the right side of the volcano plot, suggesting that lower oxygen-doping concentrations could yield more effective catalysts. However, synthesizing such samples remains challenging due to unavoidable oxygen contamination from raw materials (Si₃N₄ and Ba₂N) and during the synthesis process.

The energy barrier for the direct dissociation of the N ≡ N triple bond at the edge of the Ru cluster is calculated to be 1.1 eV (Supplementary Fig. 32), which is comparable to that observed at the B5 site of the Ru catalyst40. However, it is important to consider that competitive hydrogen adsorption occurs on the Ru surface due to its lower adsorption energy (~ −1.0 eV). As a result, ammonia synthesis on the Ru surface via NN dissociation processes is relatively more challenging than that at the interface of Ru and BaSiN2:O through alternating hydrogenation processes. Additionally, we have ruled out the possibility of a lattice nitrogen-mediated ammonia synthesis process20, because the dominant product is 15NH3 (mass weight of 18) under 15N2 + H2 atmosphere (Supplementary Fig. 43), and our calculations show that its activation barrier exceeds the formation energy (~2.4 eV) of nitrogen-vacancy on the BaSiN2 surface (Supplementary Fig. 44). Consequently, it is evident that the exceptional low-temperature ammonia synthesis activity observed over the Ru/BaSiN2:O catalyst is primarily governed by an associative hydrogenation mechanism. The BaSiN2:O surface, characterized by floating electrons and ultra-low WF, plays a crucial role in the spontaneous adsorption and activation of N2. The activated N2 can then be readily hydrogenated to ammonia with the assistance of neighboring Ru nanoparticles (Fig. 5e).

The present work developed a highly active and chemically stable surface electrene, BaSiN2:O, featuring an SFEL with ultra-low WF achieved by doping with a small amount of oxygen at N-sites. This highly active SFEL can be spontaneously stabilized through N2-passivation, which preserves its floating electrons within the adsorbed N2 molecule and can be easily regenerated when the activated N2 is hydrogenated to form ammonia, particularly with the aid of Ru-loading. The Ru/BaSiN2:O catalyst demonstrates outstanding catalytic activity and air stability for low-temperature ammonia synthesis. Our work not only discovers a new type of surface electrene with significant potential for diverse applications in physics and electronics but also demonstrates a novel catalyst that functions with both high activity and air stability for the efficient synthesis of green ammonia at low temperatures and pressures.

Methods

Synthesis of BaSiN2:O

The BaSiN2:O powders were prepared by a solid-state reaction using Ba2N and Si3N4. Firstly, Ba2N was prepared by directly heating pure Ba metal nuggets (99.99%, Aldrich Co.) in pure N2 flow at 650 °C for 15 h, and the obtained brittle blocks were crushed into black powders. Then, the obtained Ba2N powder with dehydrated Si3N4 nanopowder (<50 nm particle size (spherical), ≥98.5% trace metals basis; Aldrich Co.) in a stoichiometric ratio was well mixed by grinding in an agate mortar set. The mixed powder was then set in a molybdenum metal crucible and transferred to a quartz tube reactor. All the grinding processes were performed in the Ar-filled glove box to avoid contamination with air. Pure NH3, N2, or Ar gas (80 mL min−1) flowed through this reactor under ambient pressure, heated to the target temperature (800 °C) within 4 h and held for another 20 h. After cooling naturally, the powder product was collected in an Ar-filled glove box for further use. The samples synthesized in different gas environments were named as BaSiN2:O (NH3), BaSiN2:O (N2), and BaSiN2:O (Ar), respectively. The high surface area sample BaSiN2:O (HS) was synthesized by a solid-state reaction of Ba2N with a slight excess of Si3N4 (0.4 g Si3N4 + 0.8 g Ba2N) at 800 °C under NH3 flow. To prepare BaSiN2:O (NH3) samples with lower surface area, a larger size of Si3N4 was used.

Loading of Ru on BaSiN2:O

The loading of Ru nanoparticles (NPs) on various supports (BaSiN2:O, Si3N4, MgO, and Ba2N) was realized by the hydrogen reduction method using triruthenium dodecacarbonyl (Ru3(CO)12, 99%, Aldrich) as the Ru precursor. Typically, a mixture of support powder and Ru3(CO)12 with the desired weight ratio (the amount of Ru precursor on various supports was kept at 5 wt.% in this work) were mixed by grinding in an Ar-filled glove box and set in a silica reactor tube and then heated under a pure H2 flow (4 mL min−1) to 250 °C within 2 h and keep for another 1.5 h. The samples were collected after naturally cooling to room temperature and stored in an Ar-filled glove box.

Sample characterization

Powder XRD patterns for all the samples were recorded on a diffractometer (D2 PHASER, Bruker) with monochromated Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å) at a voltage of 40 kV and a current of 40 mA. An X-ray-transmitting airtight specimen holder was used to avoid oxidation of the samples. The specific surface areas of the as-prepared BaSiN2:O powders were evaluated by the analysis of N2 adsorption−desorption isotherms collected on a BELSORP-mini II (MicrotracBEL) at 77 K using the Brunauer−Emmett−Teller (BET) method. The composition of the BaSiN2:O catalyst (Ba/Si = 1.0) was determined by an electron probe micro analyzer (EPMA; JXA-8530F, Jeol). Auger electron spectroscopy (AES) depth profile analysis was on a scanning Auger nanoprobe system (PHI 710, Ulvac-Phi) with 10 keV primary electrons. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements of the as-prepared BaSiN2:O powder samples were conducted using a spectrometer (E580 X-band, Bruker; (ca. 9.7 GHz) in an Ar atmosphere at room temperature. High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) observations were conducted on a microscope (ARM-200F, JEOL) operated at 200 kV. The actual amount of loaded Ru on the support was determined by an X-ray fluorescence spectrometer (XRF; S8 TIGER, Bruker) (the values are summarized in Supplementary Table 4). Ru K-edge X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) measurements were performed on a synchrotron radiation ring at the NW-10A beamline (Photon Factory, KEK, Japan) using a Si(311) double-crystal monochromator and ionization chambers. A mixture of Ru/BaSiN2:O and dehydrated boron nitride powders was pressed into a pellet with a diameter of 10 mm and well-sealed in a polyethylene pack in an Ar-filled glove box. The collected data were processed using the ATHENA software package. The filtered k3-weighted χ spectra were Fourier transformed into r space with a k range of 3.0–16.0. XPS analysis was conducted on a Kratos-ULTRA2 (Shimadzu) using an apparatus equipped with a charge neutralization system. An air-isolated sample vessel was used to transfer the sample from the Ar-filled glove box to the XPS analysis system. The binding energy was corrected against the signal of C 1 s at 284.8 eV.

Iodometry

Iodometry was performed to estimate the concentration of electrons incorporated in the as-synthesized BaSiN2:O powders. Typically, around 25 mg of BaSiN2:O sample was placed into a glass vial containing 1 mL of tetrahydrofuran (THF) in an Ar-filled glove box to avoid the reaction with air. Then, 2 mL of I2 solution (3 mmol L−1) and 3 mL of HCl solution (1 mol L−1) were added to the vial under magnetic stirring. After magnetic stirring for another 10 min, a standard sodium thiosulfate (Na2S2O3) solution (1.6 mmol L−1) was added drop-wise into the glass vial to react with the remaining I2, and the volume of the consumed Na2S2O3 solution was recorded. The actual concentration of the I2 solution was measured by the same process without the BaSiN2:O sample. All of the samples were measured three times to obtain the average values.

Work function measurements

The work function of BaSiN2:O samples was evaluated on a RIKEN FAC-2 Kelvin probe force microscopy (RIKEN, Japan) under ambient pressure in an N2-filled or Ar-filled glove box. First of all, the powder samples (ca. 0.25 g) for WF measurement were pressed into a rectangular pellet (10 × 7 mm2) in an Ar-filled glove box. The above pallets were transferred to an N2-filled glove box without being exposed to air or N2 and then placed in contact with the electrode. The WF values of BaSiN2:O samples as a function of N2 exposure time were recorded and summarized as shown in Fig. 2c, d. The work function of BaSiN2:O and Ru/BaSiN2:O samples as a function of measurement time was recorded in an Ar-filled glove box and summarized in Fig. 5a, b, and Supplementary Fig. 42e. All samples were measured four times, and the average values were used. An Au plate was checked as a reference with a WF of 5.11 eV before and after each measurement to confirm the reliability of the measurement. H2 treatment (0.1 MPa, 300 °C/2 h) was performed for the BaSiN2:O (N2) sample to evaluate the regeneration of the ultra-low work function surface.

Ammonia synthesis reactions

Ammonia synthesis over various catalysts (0.1 g) was conducted in a stainless-steel reactor (0.9 MPa) or a quartz glass tube reactor (0.1 MPa) in a gas flow of N2 (purity >99.99995%; 15 mL min−1) and H2 (purity >99.99999%; 45 mL min−1) with a WHSV of 36,000 ml g−1 h−1, and under steady-state temperature in the range from 30 °C to 500 °C. A 5 mM sulfuric acid solution was used to trap the produced ammonia gas to obtain the NH4+ solution. The concentration of NH4+ was then quantitatively monitored by ion chromatography (Prominence, Shimadzu) with a conductivity detector. All the catalysts were treated under ammonia synthesis conditions at 400 °C for at least 2 h to remove the surface remaining carbon species during the loading of Ru, as well as to reach a stable catalytic state. The apparent Ea of catalysts were calculated from the Arrhenius plots of the MH3 synthesis rates at different temperatures with ammonia yield less than 20% of that at equilibrium.

Kinetics analysis

The reaction orders of N2 (α), H2 (β), and NH3 (γ) were measured at a steady-state temperature of 300 °C and pressure of 0.9 MPa under a WHSV of 36,000 mL g-1 h-1. The activity of the catalysts measured under these conditions was far from the thermal equilibrium. Calculation of the ammonia synthesis rate (r) was based on Eq. 1, while Eq. 2 − 5 were used for the kinetics analysis, where r, W, y0, q, and (1-m) represent the NH3 synthesis rate, the catalyst weight, the outlet NH3 mole fraction, the total volume flow rate, and the reaction order with respect to NH3 (γ). Finally, the α and β parameters can be determined by plotting the logarithm of C vs. the partial pressure of N2 (PN2) or H2 (PH2).

In situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform (DRIFTS) observations

The used Ru/BaSiN2:O catalyst was placed in an alumina sample cup and introduced into a stainless-steel heat chamber equipped with a BaF2 window (160–1147, S.T. Japan) in an Ar-filled glove box. The background of the used Ru/BaSiN2:O catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 25) was measured at 30 °C under a pure Ar flow (10 mL min−1). N2 (3 mL min−1) and H2 or D2 (9 mL min−1) were flowed at 30 °C, and the spectrum at different reaction times was recorded by a spectrometer (FT/IR-6XV, JASCO Corporation) equipped with a mercury-cadmium-tellurium (MCT) detector at a resolution of 0.25 cm−1. The spectrum shown in Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 26 are those collected after deducting the background.

DFT calculations

The first-principles calculations based on DFT were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP version 6.4.0.)41. The projector augmented wave (PAW) method was used to describe the interactions between valence electrons and ionic cores42. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was adopted to account for electronic exchange and correlation43. The cutoff energy of the plane-wave basis set was set to 520 eV for the bulk structure and 450 eV for the slab models of different surfaces. A Γ-centered Monkhorst-Pack sampling grids with a resolution of 2π×0.03 Å−1 were used for Brillouin-zone integrations44. The energy convergence precision was set as 1×10−5 eV. The atoms in the bulk and slab models were relaxed until the forces on all the atoms became less than 0.02 eV/Å and 0.05 eV/Å, respectively. For slab models, a large vacuum thickness of 20 Å is set to avoid the interaction between repeated units. The crystal structures of all models were drawn using the VESTA code45.
