Application of hydrogel technologies in the treatment of musculoskeletal disorders
Authors: Jiahao Li, Xin Su, Yuan Dong, Wentao Wan, Jingjing Tian, Yu Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMusculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) represent a diverse group of conditions affecting bones, cartilage, intervertebral discs, tendons, muscles, and related structures, and continue to be a leading cause of global disability. Despite advancements in surgery and pharmacotherapy, issues such as incomplete regeneration, limited longevity, and procedure-related complications remain unresolved. As a result, hydrogel technologies have garnered increasing attention due to their hydrated three-dimensional networks, customizable mechanical properties, and extracellular matrix (ECM)-mimicking features. Hydrogels have been developed in various forms, including injectable systems, scaffolds, and patches, to facilitate tissue regeneration, deliver bioactive molecules, modulate inflammation, and provide mechanical or interfacial support. This review comprehensively examines recent advancements in hydrogel technologies for MSD treatment, focusing on bone and cartilage repair, osteoarthritis management, intervertebral disc regeneration, tendon and muscle healing, and spinal dural repair. It covers laboratory studies, preclinical animal trials, and clinically approved or translational products. By critically evaluating representative materials, design strategies, and disease-specific requirements, this review highlights the therapeutic potential and translational barriers of current hydrogel systems. Challenges such as mechanical durability, spatiotemporal control of bioactivity, scalable manufacturing, and clinically relevant evaluation are addressed, providing insights into the development of next-generation hydrogels for MSDs.

Introduction

Musculoskeletal tissues, including muscles, bones, cartilage, tendons, and ligaments, account for over 40% of body mass, providing structural support and shaping the body.[1] With rising life expectancy, there has been a notable increase in debilitating conditions affecting these tissues. Musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) are a leading cause of global disability and reduced quality of life, encompassing fractures, osteoarthritis, intervertebral disc degeneration (IVDD), muscle and tendon injuries, and more.[2] Despite advances in surgical techniques and pharmacotherapy, challenges such as incomplete tissue repair, suboptimal long-term outcomes, and postoperative complications remain prevalent.[3] Consequently, the development of novel therapeutic strategies has become a key focus in musculoskeletal treatment research.

MSDs also impose a significant social, economic, and medical burden, prompting the exploration of various treatment strategies, including implantable materials, drug therapies, and tissue engineering approaches.[4] However, traditional methods such as metallic implants and bone grafts face major limitations, including immune rejection, infection risks, and poor biocompatibility.[5] This highlights the urgent need for the development of new materials and technologies to enable more efficient and safer musculoskeletal tissue repair. Recently, bioactive materials, including bioceramics,[6,7] bioactive glass,[8,9] and hydrogels,[10,11] have gained considerable attention. Hydrogels, in particular, have garnered significant interest due to their three-dimensional hydrophilic polymer networks, high water content, excellent biocompatibility, tunable physicochemical properties, and ECM-mimicking characteristics, making them suitable for replicating the microenvironment of human tissues.[12] In addition, hydrogels can function as carriers for cells and growth factors, scaffolds for tissue defect filling, and injectable repair materials, offering promising potential for tissue regeneration and functional restoration within the musculoskeletal system.[13,14] However, comprehensive reviews on the application of hydrogel technologies in MSD treatment remain scarce.

This review summarizes recent studies on the biomedical application of various hydrogels and hydrogel systems for treating MSDs, including bone, cartilage, intervertebral disc, tendon, muscle, and spinal dural repair. The challenges and future prospects of hydrogel systems as musculoskeletal tissue repair materials are also discussed. It is hoped that this review will encourage greater attention to hydrogels in MSD treatment and advance their application in biomedical fields.

Hydrogel Technology for Bone Repair

Bone tissue, a vital component of the human body, plays an essential role in various physiological processes. Bone injuries or defects resulting from trauma, disease, or other factors significantly impact public health.[15–17] Bone is a natural nanocomposite composed of organic proteins (primarily type I collagen), inorganic minerals (mainly calcium phosphate), and multiple cell types.[18,19] The bone vascular system not only supplies oxygen and nutrients to anabolic skeletal cells, but also contributes to bone formation and regeneration. However, large or irregular defects, infection-prone wounds, and compromised host conditions (such as aging or oxidative stress) can surpass the intrinsic regenerative capacity of bone, making effective bone repair a persistent clinical challenge.

Recent advancements in biomedical hydrogels have led to substantial mechanical improvements. Techniques such as double-network toughening, salting-out or ionic densification, microphase separation, and nanofiller or fiber reinforcement have enhanced their modulus and strength from the kilopascals (kPa) to megapascals (MPa) range, even reaching tens of MPa, with improved crack resistance and fatigue tolerance. These innovations expand the potential of hydrogels for musculoskeletal therapies, including cartilage repair, bone defect filling, and tendon or ligament regeneration. Hydrogels possess revolutionary potential in bone repair due to their unique physicochemical properties, including high water content, adjustable mechanical properties, extracellular matrix (ECM)-like structural features, and viscoelasticity.[20–22] Furthermore, hydrogels can be engineered with stimulus-responsive behaviors triggered by pH, temperature, near infrared (NIR) irradiation, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and ultrasonic (US) stimulation, enabling microenvironment-adaptive or on-demand delivery.[23–25]

Hydrogel-based scaffolds for bone defect repair

Hydrogels can enhance bone regeneration through various mechanisms, such as promoting osteogenic differentiation and mineralization, stimulating angiogenesis, and regulating the expression of osteogenesis-related genes.[26–28] A representative approach involves combining a mechanically competent framework with a bioactive hydrogel phase to create a soft–hard hybrid construct. For example, a 3D-printed polycaprolactone scaffold coated with a copper sulfide(CuS) nanoparticle-polyethylene glycol (PEG) hydrogel, loaded with dexamethasone sodium phosphate (Dexp), has been designed for bone regeneration [Figure 1A].[29] This scaffold exhibited exceptional mechanical and photothermal properties; under NIR irradiation, Dexp was released in a controlled manner to enhance the osteogenic differentiation of bone mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), while antibacterial activity was facilitated by the release of active copper ions. Additionally, it demonstrated significant antibacterial efficacy due to the copper ion release. The multifunctional hydrogel scaffold with a soft–hard hybrid structure holds potential as an advanced drug delivery platform for treating large bone defects. In addition to printed hybrid scaffolds, supramolecular hydrogels can be fabricated into directionally structured plugs with spatially programmed properties. For instance, a patterned UPy-modified gelatin hydrogel film was functionalized with kartogenin@polydopamine and miRNA@calcium phosphate nanoparticles, then rolled into a cylindrical scaffold with a Haversian canal-like architecture, providing layer-specific bioactivity in vivo [Figure 1B].[30] In a rabbit osteochondral defect model, this architecture-guided construct improved defect repair and promoted subchondral bone formation, demonstrating the feasibility of spatial control at the bone-cartilage interface. However, the evidence remains preclinical, and clinical translation will require scalable manufacturing and validation of long-term mechanical and interfacial durability under cyclic joint loading.

This hybrid strategy clearly divides functions: the printed framework provides structural support, while the hydrogel coating enables controlled delivery and creates a cell-permissive microenvironment, integrating osteogenesis, anti-infection, and tunable release within a single platform. Nonetheless, several key translational factors remain insufficiently addressed, including the interfacial stability of hydrogel coatings on rigid scaffolds under cyclic loading, practical challenges related to photothermal activation (penetration depth, safety margins, and compatibility with surgical procedures), and the scalability and reproducibility of multistep manufacturing. Thus, beyond short-term histological outcomes, clinically relevant studies should report fatigue-related mechanical outcomes, quantitative interface integrity, and long-term defect bridging in standardized critical-sized models. Furthermore, trigger-based systems should be compared with simpler sustained-release designs to justify their added value and safety.

Hydrogel adhesives for bone repair

Stable retention and intimate contact at wet and bleeding interfaces are critical for bone repair, particularly in irregular defects or minimally invasive procedures where mechanical fixation may be limited. Bioadhesive hydrogels have been explored to improve local fixation, minimize micromotion, and enhance the integration between scaffolds and host bone. Current designs typically use catechol/mussel-inspired chemistry, dynamic bonding, or interpenetrating networks to achieve wet adhesion while maintaining a hydrated microenvironment. Some systems also integrate hemostatic, antibacterial, or osteoinductive functions to address complex defect conditions. A representative example is the homogeneous “hard–soft” biphasic injectable bone adhesive, which combines phosphate-calcium-mediated wet adhesion with tetracalcium phosphate (TTCP)-driven mineral deposition. This adhesive achieves strong wet bonding on bone (lap-shear = 270 kPa; end-to-end = 206 kPa), along with high fatigue resistance and rapid gelation (20–60 s), making it promising for injectable fixation of comminuted fragments [Figure 1C].[31]

From a translational perspective, adhesive hydrogels have the potential to improve local retention and tissue coupling without significantly complicating surgical procedures. However, overly strong or nonspecific adhesion could complicate revision surgeries, and the application of swelling pressure or certain crosslinking chemistries may impair local microcirculation or raise concerns about increased toxicity. Additionally, adhesion is often evaluated under simplified conditions that do not account for blood contamination, surface heterogeneity, or cyclic loading at bone interfaces. Therefore, clinically relevant studies should assess wet or blood adhesion under dynamic loading, track interface integrity over time, and consider sterilization, shelf stability, and controllable debonding/removal when necessary.

Injectable/in situ-forming hydrogels for irregular defects

Injectable hydrogels are among the most widely studied biomaterials, as they can be delivered through syringe and rapidly form a gel within the body.[32–34] They enable minimally invasive filling of irregular defects, local retention, and tunable release, and can also serve as carriers for cells or bioactive factors while shaping a favorable immune microenvironment for bone regeneration.[35] For instance, a multihierarchy ROS scavenging system was designed for aged bone regeneration by fabricating an injectable PEGylated poly(glycerol sebacate)/poly(γ-glutamic acid) hydrogel loaded with rapamycin-containing poly(diselenide-carbonate) nanomicelles [Figure 1D].[10] This hydrogel is highly sensitive to ROS in a senescent microenvironment and dynamically releases drug-loaded nanomicelles to reduce intracellular ROS in senescent BMSCs, protecting DNA replication under oxidative stress.

Microenvironment-responsive injectable designs move beyond generic delivery systems toward mechanism-guided therapy. However, most injectable formulations still lack mechanical robustness for load-bearing applications, and gelation kinetics need optimization to balance injectability with in situ retention. Additionally, degradation rates can vary across patients and microenvironments, while the long-term biosafety of nanocarriers and degradation products is often underreported. As such, claims of “immunomodulation” should be substantiated with immune profiling (e.g., macrophage polarization dynamics), and degradation should be linked to measurable healing outcomes. For load-bearing repairs, injectable hydrogels are more viable as bioactive depots used in conjunction with fixation or structural support rather than as stand-alone structural substitutes.

Hydrogels for spinal fusion

Spinal fusion is a common surgical treatment for spinal disorders, and hydrogels show considerable potential as carriers to localize osteoinductive signals, enhancing the handling and retention of bone graft substitutes. In this context, hydrogels typically do not serve as the primary load-bearing element but rather as injectable or moldable matrices that stabilize osteoconductive particles and regulate the release of osteoinductive factors. Preclinical studies indicate that hydrogel-based composites can deliver osteoinductive factors with improved spatial control and potentially fewer off-target effects compared to conventional carriers. Additionally, these composites improve conformal filling of fusion beds and mitigate burst release. Clinically, bone morphogenetic protein-2 (BMP2) has long been combined with collagen carriers, but supraphysiological dosing and rapid release have been associated with inflammatory complications, edema, osteolysis, inappropriate adipogenesis, and ectopic bone formation, highlighting the need for more effective carrier systems. A representative example is the nanoclay gel (NC)/BMP2 system,[36] where a thixotropic nanoclay hydrogel retained BMP2 through physical entrapment and electrostatic interactions, thereby prolonging local exposure. In a rat posterolateral fusion model, both NC and collagen sponge (CS) achieved comparable fusion rates, but NC resulted in a more robust fusion mass with improved trabecular architecture, whereas CS showed less favorable bone formation at higher BMP2 doses. Consistently, CS induced a dose-dependent inflammatory response, while NC exhibited lower and less dose-sensitive inflammation, suggesting that improved retention can mitigate perilesional inflammatory toxicity while maintaining osteoinductive efficacy.

Despite these promising results, the evidence supporting hydrogel-enabled fusion remains largely preclinical, and translation requires more clinically relevant benchmarking. Given the mechanically demanding fusion environment and the known safety concerns with osteoinductive factors, hydrogel carriers should be evaluated not only by fusion rate but also by the quality of fusion mass (e.g., micro-CT architecture and mechanical competence), risk of ectopic ossification, inflammatory and osteolytic responses, and neurologic safety with appropriate follow-up.

Hydrogel matrices for bone organoids

Hydrogels are increasingly used as engineered matrices for musculoskeletal organoid culture, offering a controllable three-dimensional microenvironment in which key biophysical properties (e.g., stiffness, viscoelasticity, and stress relaxation) and biochemical signals can be tuned to guide lineage specification and tissue maturation. A notable example is the large-scale self-mineralizing bone organoid strategy,[37] which developed a bone-matrix-inspired hybrid bioink composed of gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA), methacrylated alginate (AlgMA), and hydroxyapatite for digital light processing (DLP ) bioprinting. This bioink better mimics the organic-mineral features of bone ECM and supports long-term, multicellular osteogenic maturation [Figure 1E]. In a rat critical-sized (5 mm) calvarial defect model, the printed bone organoid constructs demonstrated significantly higher new-bone indices (e.g., BV/TV) at 8 weeks compared with hydrogel-only controls, suggesting that organoid-like maturation within a mineral-informed hydrogel matrix can improve defect repair outcomes in vivo.

However, organoid systems remain primarily at the laboratory-to-animal stage and should be considered within this context in translational reviews. Even in advanced demonstrations, functional maturation often requires in vivo conditioning, and current organoids do not fully replicate key clinical features, such as vascular, immune, and multi-tissue interactions essential for bone regeneration. Furthermore, implantable organoid products face substantial challenges in scaling up, ensuring manufacturing consistency, sterilization, logistics, and regulatory approval. Thus, in the short term, organoids should be viewed as human-relevant discovery and screening platforms that can be used to investigate how hydrogel design parameters regulate osteogenesis and prioritize candidate formulations, rather than as immediately deployable clinical solutions for musculoskeletal repair.

Clinical translation and future challenges

Most advanced hydrogel systems for bone repair remain at the preclinical stage, and their successful translation will depend on meeting load-bearing requirements, maintaining stable retention at wet interfaces, and ensuring predictable degradation that aligns with mineralized tissue formation. Bone hydrogel research is progressively shifting from passive fillers to multifunctional, mechanism-driven systems.

From a materials perspective, these challenges correspond to recurring failure modes associated with different crosslinking mechanisms. Physically crosslinked hydrogels often exhibit significant viscoelastic creep, stress relaxation, and fatigue softening under cyclic loading, which can compromise defect-site stability and fixation unless reinforced by rigid stabilization. In contrast, chemically crosslinked in situ-forming networks may offer higher initial strength, but translation could be limited by curing depth, intraoperative procedures, and potential biocompatibility concerns related to crosslinking chemistry and long-term integration stability.

Future bone-directed hydrogel platforms should prioritize mechanically relevant testing, including fatigue resistance, interface strength, controlled degradation linked to mineralized tissue formation, immune-vascular integration metrics, and the compatibility of scalable manufacturing and sterilization processes.

Hydrogel Technology for Cartilage Repair and Osteoarthritis Management

Articular cartilage (AC) is a specialized connective tissue known for its high strength and low friction, providing essential mechanical support, lubrication, and antiwear functionality.[38,39] However, due to its avascular nature, lack of lymphatics, and absence of nerves, AC has limited intrinsic regenerative capacity, especially under the harsh biomechanical conditions of joints, making cartilage repair a persistent clinical challenge. Osteoarthritis (OA),[40] a globally prevalent and debilitating condition, is characterized by progressive cartilage loss accompanied by subchondral bone remodeling, osteophyte formation, and synovial inflammation.[41–43] Epidemiologically, OA affects approximately 10% of males and 18% of females aged 60 years or older, imposing a significant socio-economic burden.[42,43] Current OA management primarily relies on medications and surgery,[44] with oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) associated with gastrointestinal and cardiovascular side effects, while intra-articular injections often face challenges such as limited residence time and procedure-related risks. Hydrogel strategies for cartilage-related disorders can thus be explored in two clinically relevant directions: (1) regenerative repair for focal defects and osteochondral units and (2) OA-oriented intra-articular depots and tribological interventions.

Hydrogel scaffolds/patches for focal cartilage regeneration

Hydrogel scaffolds and patches offer ECM-mimetic, hydrated microenvironments and can be engineered to combine mechanical resilience with local biochemical regulation. For instance, a multiple-hydrogen-bond crosslinked hydrogel loaded with tannic acid and kartogenin was developed as a cell-free scaffold for in vivo cartilage regeneration [Figure 2A and B].[41] This system exhibited durable mechanical behavior, stage-dependent release, and local anti-inflammatory and antioxidative properties to support a prohealing environment. Designing cartilage-mimetic materials with both high strength and toughness remains a challenge. A “molecular Velcro”-inspired peptide-gelatin co-assembly strategy [Figure 2C] enhanced cell proliferation and chondrogenic differentiation, while BMSC-loaded hydrogels were stably retained in rabbit knee defects through light curing, promoting hyaline-like cartilage regeneration after three months.[45]

However, several gaps limit translational confidence. First, photocuring-based fixation raises practical concerns about light penetration in deeper defects, initiator safety, and curing uniformity. Second, many studies focus on histology while underreporting tribological recovery (friction or wear) and long-term functional outcomes, which are critical for durable joint performance. Third, integration at the defect margin remains a frequent failure mode, often not quantified. Future scaffold studies should, therefore, report defect-edge integration strength, surface durability, and friction and wear under joint-like loading, in addition to standardized histological scoring, to substantiate clinically meaningful cartilage repair outcomes.

Hydrogels for focal osteochondral defect repair

Osteochondral regeneration, involving both cartilage and subchondral bone, remains a major clinical challenge due to the substantial differences in composition, vascularity, and remodeling kinetics between these compartments, complicating integrated repair.[25,46,47] Conventional osteochondral strategies often involve fabricating cartilage- and bone-mimicking layers separately before assembling them. However, weak interfacial bonding and poor load transfer across the interface frequently hinder performance under shear and compression in vivo.[48] To address these challenges, interface-aware and architecture-guided scaffolds have been developed to spatially control bioactive factors and anisotropic structures at the osteochondral unit. Additionally, defect-filling hydrogels provide a minimally invasive alternative that can conformally fill irregular osteochondral lesions. For example, a biodegradable injectable piezoelectric hydrogel, which incorporates short electrospun poly(L-lactic acid) (PLLA) nanofibers within a collagen matrix, has been shown to support osteochondral repair. In a rabbit critical-size defect model, the system generated local electromechanical properties and enhanced osteochondral healing when activated remotely by low-intensity ultrasound [Figure 2D].[32]

From a critical perspective, focal osteochondral repair should be considered a structural regeneration challenge rather than a means of controlling intra-articular symptoms. Interface-aware scaffolds address key failure modes of layered constructs, such as interfacial delamination and poor integration, while defect-filling injectables offer conformal filling and optional on-demand stimulation. However, translation is limited by the lack of long-term testing under joint-like cyclic loading, insufficient quantification of integration strength and interfacial toughness, and challenges in synchronizing degradation and remodeling across cartilage and bone. Future evaluations of osteochondral hydrogels should focus on interfacial toughness, load-bearing durability, tribological recovery, and time-resolved degradation–healing coupling, rather than relying on histology alone.

Injectable intra-articular hydrogels for OA management

OA is a prevalent and debilitating condition, with current therapies (oral NSAIDs, intra-articular injections, surgery) limited by systemic side effects, procedure-related risks, and short intra-articular residence times. Hydrogels are attractive for use as intra-articular depots, as they can prolong local exposure and integrate anti-inflammatory, antioxidative, and lubricating functions.[49,50] Representative natural and synthetic components include Hyaluronic acid, collagen, alginate, chitosan, gelatin,[51,52] as well as poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA), poly(acrylic acid) (PAA), poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAM), PEG, and polyphosphazenes.[53–56] For instance, Han et al[57] developed force-electroconverting hydrogel microspheres to enhance chondrogenic differentiation, and Yu et al[58] reported cartilage-targeting ROS-responsive microspheres that scavenge ROS and trigger Dexamethasone/kartogenin release. Lubrication-oriented systems aim to reduce friction-driven wear and synovitis, as demonstrated by injectable lubricating and ROS-scavenging hydrogels.[59] Notably, most disease-modifying hydrogel concepts remain preclinical, while HA-based viscosupplement products are already clinically used for OA pain management, providing primarily symptomatic rather than regenerative benefits.

A central issue in OA hydrogel research is the discrepancy between claims and endpoints, as OA is a whole-joint pathology involving cartilage, synovium, subchondral bone, and meniscus. Many studies report short-term biomarker changes or histological improvements without demonstrating durable cartilage restoration or clinically meaningful outcomes. It is recommended that OA hydrogel studies explicitly distinguish symptom relief (e.g., pain relief and improved lubrication/viscosupplementation) from true disease modification, benchmark against clinically used comparators such as HA viscosupplementation, and adopt clinically relevant endpoints, including pain management, imaging-based outcomes, and long-term follow-up.

Hydrogel microneedles for periarticular delivery

Hydrogel microneedles have been explored for the local delivery of anti-inflammatory and chondroprotective agents, aiming to alleviate OA-related symptoms while avoiding repeated intra-articular injections. Hydrogel-assisted acupuncture and microneedling have been shown to penetrate cartilage cell layers in rat and rabbit models, reducing inflammation and subchondral bone thickening.[60] Beyond symptom management, microneedle constructs can also be engineered as implantable microdevices for osteochondral lesions. For example, Wang et al[61] developed a bilayer GelMA microneedle patch coencapsulating protoporphyrin IX and chondral organoids to enable ultrasound-activated sonodynamic therapy, followed by organoid-driven repair [Figure 2E]. In a rabbit knee osteochondral defect model, the MN(+PpIX/CO) group achieved improved integrated osteochondral regeneration and demonstrated acceptable systemic biosafety at 12 weeks.

From a critical perspective, microneedles offer convenient localization, but their target compartment and exposure profile need to be clearly defined. Many microneedle-based platforms for OA primarily deliver to the skin or periarticular tissues rather than the joint space, and they are limited by dose capacity, reproducibility, and skin tolerability. Implantable microneedle constructs provide better control at the lesion site but introduce additional surgical and stimulation-related variables. Therefore, studies should align claims with their intended use (symptom control vs. structural repair), quantify local exposure, and substantiate regeneration with rigorous imaging and integration endpoints.

Clinical translation and future challenges

Cartilage-directed hydrogel technologies serve two main clinical purposes: focal defect repair using scaffolds, patches, or defect-filling constructs, and whole-joint OA management through intra-articular depots that combine sustained delivery with microenvironment modulation and, in some cases, tribology-oriented lubrication. Although most regenerative hydrogel designs remain preclinical, clinical adoption is currently most established for viscosupplementation and selected scaffold- and cell-based cartilage repair approaches.

A critical limitation in translation is often hydrogel-type dependent. For focal defect repair, physically crosslinked fillers (e.g., fibrin/collagen/alginate/HA gels) are prone to shear-driven delamination, washout, and time-dependent softening, which compromise margin integration under joint-like cyclic loading. Chemically crosslinked in situ-forming systems can enhance initial retention and integrity, but practical constraints, along with long-term challenges such as tribology mismatch, surface wear, and interface durability, can still restrict sustained performance. Thus, the primary bottleneck is not static stiffness alone, but the coupled issue of integration strength and surface durability under physiological tribology.

To improve translational relevance, future studies should match claims with the level of evidence. For defect repair, studies should prioritize quantitative assessments of integration strength, surface durability, and friction and wear under joint-like loading. For OA-oriented systems, clinically aligned endpoints such as pain and function measures, imaging-based cartilage and bone readouts, and long-term follow-up should be the standard. Finally, head-to-head comparisons against current clinical standards, rather than only against untreated controls, should be routine, as incremental benefit over established care will determine clinical value.

Hydrogel Technology for Intervertebral Disc Repair

IVDD is a major contributor to chronic low back pain, one of the leading causes of disability worldwide.[62] Current clinical treatments, including conservative management and surgical procedures such as discectomy or spinal fusion, focus primarily on symptom relief rather than biological restoration of disc function. Moreover, invasive procedures can accelerate adjacent segment degeneration, highlighting the need for regenerative and minimally invasive therapeutic strategies.[63]

The complex anatomy of the intervertebral disc, comprising the nucleus pulposus (NP), annulus fibrosus (AF), and cartilage endplates (CE), presents distinct and sometimes conflicting design requirements for biomaterials. Hydrogels have emerged as particularly promising candidates due to their high water content, tunable viscoelasticity, injectability, and ability to deliver bioactive molecules. Based on their mode of application and functional objectives, disc repair hydrogels can be broadly categorized into scaffold hydrogels for structural reconstruction and injectable hydrogels for biological modulation and localized therapy.

Scaffold hydrogels for disc structural support

Scaffold-based hydrogels aim to restore the biomechanical integrity of degenerated discs while providing a supportive microenvironment for cellular activity and matrix regeneration. For load-bearing NP and tensile AF regions, achieving adequate mechanical strength without compromising biological compatibility remains a central challenge. Double-network hydrogels, with their high compressive strength, are able to partially replicate the native disc’s viscoelastic behavior [Figure 3A and B].[11,64] These scaffolds are designed to withstand physiological loading conditions.[64,65]

For NP repair, resistance to compressive loading and the maintenance of hydration are critical. Materials such as alginate-based and hyaluronic acid-based hydrogels meet these requirements, offering excellent water retention and viscoelasticity.[66] Incorporating mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) into these hydrogels has shown promise in promoting NP regeneration and restoring shock-absorbing functionality.[67] However, excessive stiffness can impair cellular viability and ECM remodeling, highlighting the need for a balanced mechanical design.

For AF repair, scaffold hydrogels must withstand tensile and shear stresses while supporting collagen fiber alignment. GelMA-, collagen-, and composite-based scaffolds have been shown to promote cell adhesion and allow for tunable crosslinking density.[68,69] Functionalization with bioactive factors, such as transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) or BMPs, enhances collagen deposition and annular tissue maturation.[70] However, long-term integration at the AF-bone interface remains insufficiently addressed.

An emerging trend in disc repair involves integrated NP–AF composite scaffolds, aiming to restore disc functionality holistically. Hybrid designs that combine soft, highly hydrated cores with fiber-reinforced peripheral regions better mimic the regional heterogeneity of the native disc.[71] Composite hydrogels, incorporating materials such as collagen, alginate, and poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid)(PLGA), have demonstrated their potential in preclinical studies by enhancing the viscoelastic properties and promoting cell infiltration into both AF and NP regions.[7] Some scaffold systems are designed to meet the structural needs of the NP and AF while providing a favorable microenvironment for cellular activity.[72] For example, bioactive motifs in peptide-functionalized hydrogels enhance ECM production,[11] and sustained release mechanisms in polyphenol-enriched scaffolds ensure prolonged anti-inflammatory effects.[73] Although these systems are conceptually promising, they face translational challenges related to fabrication complexity, reproducibility, and surgical implantation feasibility.

Overall, scaffold hydrogels show considerable potential for mechanical reconstruction in advanced IVDD but are limited by long-term durability, interfacial integration, and surgical practicality. Current studies are still largely confined to animal experiments.

Injectable hydrogels for disc regeneration

Injectable hydrogels offer a minimally invasive approach for early- to mid-stage IVDD, where partial disc structure is preserved. Key advantages include ease of delivery, adaptability to irregular defects, and the ability to provide localized therapeutic release.[65] Polyphenol nanosphere-encapsulated hydrogels[74] and multifunctional gene delivery systems[75] enable sustained release of therapeutic molecules, promoting ECM regeneration and reducing inflammation. Exosome-releasing hydrogels[76,77] specifically target CE and NP regions to deliver bioactive factors, reversing disc degeneration, and serve as reliable vehicles for disc regeneration.

Recent developments have focused on stimuli-responsive injectable hydrogels that dynamically adapt to the challenging disc microenvironment. Self-gelling nucleobase-modified hydrogels enhance structural resilience and stem cell retention under dynamic spinal loading [Figure 3C].[78,79] Oxygen-regulating hydrogels use laccase-mediated systems to maintain oxygen levels in the hypoxic NP,[80,81] while ferroptosis-targeting hydrogels alleviate oxidative stress by chelating iron ions.[82]

Thermosensitive hydrogels are particularly effective in preventing post-injection leakage, a significant safety concern for NP applications.[72,83] Additionally, multifunctional injectable platforms capable of sustained anti-inflammatory drug release and pain modulation have proven effective in animal models of disc herniation [Figure 3D].[83] Natural product-based scaffolds, incorporating polyphenols or herbal extracts, add antioxidant and anti-inflammatory functions essential for addressing the chronic inflammatory state of degenerated discs.[73]

Although several injectable systems have shown efficacy in reducing inflammation, enhancing cell survival, and alleviating pain in preclinical disc degeneration models, they typically offer limited mechanical reinforcement. Material leakage or degradation mismatch further restricts their use as standalone solutions in advanced degeneration.

Clinical translation and future challenges

Compared with scaffold-based constructs, injectable hydrogels offer superior clinical feasibility due to their minimally invasive delivery and compatibility with existing spinal procedures. Notably, injectable NP augmentation hydrogels, designed to restore disc hydration and mechanical support, have progressed to early human studies and regulatory-approved clinical trials, showing preliminary safety and pain relief benefits in patients with degenerative disc disease.[84] Although injectable hydrogels currently offer higher clinical feasibility due to their compatibility with minimally invasive procedures, scaffold-based constructs remain more suitable for severe disc degeneration requiring mechanical reconstruction. However, no single hydrogel system fully meets both the mechanical and biological demands.

Future disc repair strategies should prioritize multifunctional hydrogel systems that combine mechanical support, biological modulation, and long-term stability. Standardized biomechanical testing and long-term in vivo evaluation under physiological loading conditions will be essential to bridge the gap between experimental success and clinical application. In particular, long-term studies under these conditions are critical to validate durability and safety. Additionally, scalable manufacturing techniques, such as 3D printing, and surgical compatibility will ultimately determine clinical feasibility.

Advances in Hydrogel for Tendon and Muscle Repair

Tendon injuries, such as rotator cuff tears, Achilles tendon ruptures, and patellar tendon injuries, are prevalent in both athletic and general populations. Studies estimate that rotator cuff tears alone affect approximately 30–40% of adults aged 60 years and older, with a rising incidence among younger people engaged in high-impact sports.[85] Similarly, muscle injuries, including strains, tears, and contusions, are common in sports, trauma, and surgeries. These injuries can cause significant pain, dysfunction, and prolonged recovery, often resulting in muscle fibrosis and incomplete regeneration if not adequately treated. Muscle and tendon injuries present substantial clinical challenges due to their hierarchical structure, high mechanical demands, and limited intrinsic regenerative capacity. Conventional surgical repairs often fail to restore native structure and function, highlighting the need for biomaterial-based regenerative strategies. Hydrogels have attracted increasing attention as scaffolds, patches, and injectable systems capable of supporting tissue repair and functional recovery.

Patch-type and scaffold hydrogels for tendon, muscle repair

Recent advances in patch-type and scaffold hydrogels have highlighted their potential to address the complex biomechanical and biological requirements of muscle and tendon repair. These hydrogel systems facilitate tissue integration, enhance cellular activity, and contribute to the restoration of mechanical function in injured musculoskeletal tissues.

In the context of muscle and tendon defects, several studies have focused on biomimetic designs aimed at replicating native tissue properties. For example, glycosaminoglycan-based scaffolds have significantly improved skeletal muscle regeneration in volumetric muscle loss models by closely mimicking ECM properties.[86] Similarly, sandwich-structured biomimetic scaffolds have been shown to promote tendon regeneration by guiding tendon progenitor cell alignment within a three-dimensional microenvironment [Figure 4A].[87]

For tendon or ligament reconnection, hydrogel-based scaffolds play a pivotal role in ligament regeneration by promoting cellular adhesion, integration, and mechanical continuity, as evidenced by advanced biomechanical evaluations.[88] A novel fiber-reinforced hydrogel (FRH) that mimics the structure of human tendon has been developed as a synthetic tendon graft material, offering enhanced load-bearing capacity [Figure 4B].[89] Marine collagen-based scaffolds, despite their relatively limited mechanical strength, have shown high biocompatibility, making them promising candidates for tendon repair.[90] To address mechanical insufficiency, FRH composites and nanofiber scaffolds have emerged as effective solutions, combining high tensile strength with improved biological performance to enhance both structural stability and functional recovery.[91] Stratified composite hydrogel biomaterials further facilitate tendon-to-bone integration by establishing graded biomechanical transition zones.[92] Additionally, tough adhesive hydrogels with bilayer structures provide both anti-inflammatory effects and mechanical reinforcement, thereby improving tendon repair efficiency.[2]

Beyond structural support, gene-hydrogel patches represent a promising strategy for tendon regeneration. By integrating localized gene delivery, these patches have been shown to suppress fibroblast overactivation while enhancing the mechanical properties of repaired tendons.[93] At the myotendinous junction, bioactive hydrogels incorporating MSCs and bioactive molecules significantly reduce fibrosis and improve structural integration.[94] Moreover, tendon assembloids augmented with hydrogel layers have demonstrated promise in improving multicellular crosstalk, thereby enhancing repair outcomes.[88]

Despite significant progress, most hydrogel-based patches and scaffolds for muscle and tendon repair still face limitations in long-term mechanical stability under repetitive loading, restricting their applicability in load-bearing environments. Additionally, mismatches between hydrogel degradation kinetics and native tissue remodeling often compromise sustained mechanical support and functional integration.

Injectable hydrogels for tendon and muscle regeneration

Injectable hydrogels are particularly suitable for repairing partially damaged tendons and muscles. High-adhesion, shape-adaptive hydrogels have shown superior performance in tendon repair by conforming to irregular defect geometries and maintaining intimate contact with surrounding tissues.[95] DNA hydrogels, designed to mimic ECM structures, have proven effective in promoting tendon cell proliferation and tissue repair in preclinical studies.[96] Thermoresponsive chitosan-based hydrogels undergo rapid sol-gel transitions at physiological temperatures, providing structural support at injury sites.[97] Similarly, peptide-based thermoresponsive hydrogels facilitate cell migration and proliferation, contributing to improved tendon healing outcomes.[98]

In muscle regeneration, hydrogels incorporating insulin-like growth factor 1 have shown considerable promise in mitigating muscle atrophy by enhancing cellular proliferation and tissue repair processes [Figure 4C].[99] Dual-gradient growth factor-releasing hydrogels exhibit multifunctional therapeutic effects, simultaneously modulating inflammatory responses and accelerating muscle repair.[100] Additionally, silicate-releasing hydrogels promote angiogenesis and support myogenic regeneration, addressing critical vascularization challenges in muscle repair.[101]

Magnetic hydrogels have been explored to guide cellular alignment during skeletal muscle regeneration, improving tissue organization and functional recovery. Magnetic nanoparticles combined with collagen-based hydrogels represent an innovative strategy for directing cell orientation and enhancing tendon repair outcomes.[102] The combination of mesoporous silica nanoparticles and natural materials improves tendon repair by regulating macrophage polarization, highlighting the application potential of nanoparticles [Figure 4D].[103] Considering the electromechanical nature of muscle tissue, injectable bioresponsive hydrogel-based tissue prostheses have been developed to enable real-time neuromuscular rehabilitation through closed-loop signal responsiveness.[104] Additionally, powder hydrogel-based injectable microenvironments have proven highly effective in enhancing regenerative capacity in muscle tissue.[105]

Despite their versatility, injectable hydrogels for tendon and muscle repair still face challenges related to insufficient long-term mechanical stability under dynamic loading and limited control over spatial tissue organization postinjection. One promising development involves gradient metallic ion-releasing hydrogels, which enhance tendon-to-bone interface regeneration by facilitating tissue remodeling.[106]

Clinical translation and future challenges

Although significant progress has been made in hydrogel-based strategies for tendon and muscle repair, most advanced systems remain confined to preclinical studies. From a translational perspective, injectable hydrogels offer clear advantages in minimally invasive delivery and surgical adaptability.

However, several key challenges must be addressed to bridge the gap between experimental success and clinical implementation. First, achieving a balance between mechanical strength and biological activity remains challenging, particularly in load-bearing and highly dynamic musculoskeletal environments. Second, many studies rely on short-term animal models that fail to fully capture the scale, heterogeneity, and mechanical demands of human tendon-muscle units, limiting the predictive value of preclinical outcomes. Last, insufficient vascularization, innervation, and functional integration continue to constrain the restoration of native tissue performance.

Adhesive Hydrogel for Dural Repair

Spinal MSDs often require surgical intervention, during which cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leakage is a common and serious complication, with an incidence rate of up to 30%.[107,108] Effective dural repair is thus critical for the successful treatment of these conditions. CSF leakage can lead to wound dehiscence, surgical site infections, intracranial infections, and spinal cord-related complications, posing significant risks to patient safety and, in severe cases, even resulting in mortality. Despite the development of various dural repair strategies, such as suturing, biological adhesives, hydrogels, and dural substitutes, postoperative CSF leakage remains a clinically unresolved challenge.[109]

Direct suturing is the most widely used method for dural closure, but leakage can still occur around the suture site. Conventional biological adhesives, including fibrin glue, gelatin, and CS, have several inherent limitations, such as weak performance in wet environments, rapid degradation, excessive swelling, and mechanical mismatch with the native dura mater.[110] In contrast, hydrogel tissue adhesives, which mimic the natural ECM with high water content and appropriate mechanical properties, can enhance wound healing by promoting cell proliferation.[111,112] The adhesion of hydrogels to dural tissues is influenced by a combination of surface chemistry, interfacial topology, and the physicochemical properties of the adhesion.[113] To achieve strong and stable adhesion at the hydrogel-tissue interface, various strategies have been explored, including material selection, fabrication methods, and application-specific considerations.[114–116]

Injectable hydrogel adhesives for dural sealing

Injectable hydrogel adhesives have gained increasing attention due to their ability to rapidly adhere to wet tissues and resist swelling in moist environments, which is essential for hemostasis and watertight sealing in confined surgical spaces.[117,118] Many injectable hydrogels, such as those based on PEG, collagen, chitosan, alginate, and hyaluronan, are inherently cytocompatible and promote cellular growth and proliferation. The minimally invasive nature of injectable hydrogels makes them particularly useful in difficult-to-access or hazardous areas, such as the central nervous system, for applications such as spinal cord injury repair and dural tear sealing. A tissue-adhesive, low-swelling hydrogel sealant composed of gelatin and o-phthalaldehyde-terminated 4-armed poly(ethylene glycol) through the OPA/amine condensation reaction exhibited an adhesive strength of 79.9 ± 12.0 kPa and a burst pressure of 208.0 ± 38.0 cmH2O on porcine intestine. This hydrogel demonstrated a very low swelling rate in saline, minimizing the risk of nerve compression in the limited spinal and intracranial spaces [Figure 5A].[119] These results highlight the hydrogel’s potential in sealing dural defects and preventing CSF leakage in rat and rabbit models of lumbar and cerebral dural defects.

A dual-performance hydrogel with rapid adhesion and anti-swelling properties, which cures in seconds under ultraviolet irradiation [Figure 5B], has been used for dural wound closure in wet environments. It effectively avoids spinal nerve compression compared to typical swelling-bonded hydrogels.[120] A biocompatible tetra-PEG hydrogel, designed as a sutureless sealant [Figure 5C], offers advantages such as ease of handling, high safety, fast coagulation time, easy injection, high mechanical strength, and strong tissue adhesion, making it suitable for sutureless dural closure during surgery.[121] In a rabbit model of dural rupture, the hydrogel was shown to rapidly gel and tightly occlude the dural defect intraoperatively. However, the uncontrollable nature of the injectable hydrogel’s shape presents a risk of spinal cord compression when applied epidurally.

Hydrogels bioadhesive patch for dural repair

Inspired by the practicality and efficacy of duct tape in non-medical applications, hydrogel patches for sealing the dural membrane offer a convenient, atraumatic, fluid-tight, robust, and sutureless solution for dural defects, addressing key limitations of sutures and commercially available tissue adhesives and sealants.[122–125] A bioactive patch composed of an alginate and polyacrylamide hydrogel matrix crosslinked by calcium ions, combined with chitosan adhesive [Figure 5D], demonstrates excellent mechanical properties and adhesion strength, with stable adhesion in wet environments, achieving a seal burst pressure exceeding 790 mmH2O.[126] Additionally, the hydrogel patch exhibits anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and analgesic properties and prevents tissue adhesion, attributed to a significant reduction in the expression of glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP), ionized calcium-binding adapter molecule 1 (IBA-1), myelin basic protein (MBP), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and cyclooxygenase-2 (COX-2) during the early postoperative period. A recent advancement in dural repair includes the development of a dynamically adaptive Janus hydrogel patch, which enables rapid, suture-free sealing while minimizing postoperative adhesion.[127]

Despite these innovations, achieving precisely tunable and durable tissue adhesion remains a challenge. Although strong initial adhesion can be achieved, adhesive performance may degrade over time due to factors such as interfacial water infiltration, tissue motion, hydrogel swelling or dehydration, and material degradation. Furthermore, balancing postoperative tissue conglutination prevention with sufficient adhesion adds complexity to material design. Future bioadhesive hydrogel patches should, therefore, integrate mechanical compatibility with native dura mater, stable wet adhesion, controlled degradation, and effective anti-adhesion properties.

Clinical translation and future challenges

Adhesive hydrogels for dural repair represent one of the most clinically advanced hydrogel applications in spinal and neurosurgical procedures. Several polymer-based adhesive hydrogels, particularly those based on PEG and hyaluronic acid, have been approved and are routinely used in spinal and neurosurgical procedures to achieve watertight dural closure and prevent CSF leakage,[128] highlighting the translational feasibility of hydrogel systems when material performance aligns with clinical needs.

However, broader clinical adoption of next-generation dural sealants is constrained by several factors. Long-term adhesive durability under pulsatile CSF pressure and tissue motion has not been sufficiently validated, and excessive swelling or mechanical mismatch may still pose risks of neural compression. Moreover, although multifunctional hydrogel systems incorporating bioactive or responsive components show promise in preclinical studies, they face challenges related to manufacturing scalability, sterilization, reproducibility, and regulatory approval. Future research should prioritize clinically scalable hydrogel sealants with simplified compositions, predictable degradation behavior, and standardized evaluation protocols.

Conclusion

This review highlights recent advancements in hydrogel-based biomaterials for treating MSDs across various tissues and clinical contexts. A key observation is the frequent use of similar hydrogel systems, particularly injectable and ECM-mimetic formulations, for diverse MSDs due to shared needs such as minimally invasive delivery and local microenvironment modulation. Conversely, distinct hydrogel formats—such as injectable depots, load-bearing scaffolds, or adhesive interfaces—are required for different disease stages and mechanical demands. The design of hydrogels is thus tailored to specific application scenarios, rather than being restricted to particular diseases. Additionally, research on hydrogels as matrices for culturing musculoskeletal organoids shows significant potential for development and application. The integration of artificial intelligence (AI) technology is also expected to expedite the rapid evolution of hydrogel technology, enabling better alignment with the treatment needs of MSDs.

Despite promising progress, clinical translation remains hindered by several challenges. Load-bearing applications, such as bone and intervertebral disc repair, necessitate long-term mechanical stability paired with controlled degradation, while adhesion applications, such as dural repair, require durable wet adhesion with minimal swelling and reduced neural risk. Mismatches between degradation rates, bioactive release, and tissue remodeling often compromise outcomes, and clinically relevant endpoints are frequently underreported in animal studies.

Future hydrogel development should focus on clinically pragmatic designs that balance functionality with translational feasibility. Key priorities include mechanically reinforced yet biologically permissive materials, simplified multifunctional systems, and standardized evaluation protocols. Incorporating large animal studies, early clinical trials, and interdisciplinary collaboration will be crucial for accelerating the clinical adoption of hydrogel technologies in MSDs.
