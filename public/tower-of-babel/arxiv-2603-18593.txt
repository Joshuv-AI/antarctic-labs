Language Model Maps for Prompt-Response Distributions via Log-Likelihood Vectors
Yusuke Takase, Momose Oyama, Hidetoshi Shimodaira

Abstract
We propose a method that represents language models by log-likelihood vectors over prompt-response pairs and constructs model maps for comparing their conditional distributions. In this space, distances between models approximate the KL divergence between the corresponding conditional distributions. Experiments on a large collection of publicly available language models show that the maps capture meaningful global structure, including relationships to model attributes and task performance. The method also captures systematic shifts induced by prompt modifications and their approximate additive compositionality, suggesting a way to analyze and predict the effects of composite prompt operations. We further introduce pointwise mutual information (PMI) vectors to reduce the influence of unconditional distributions; in some cases, PMI-based model maps better reflect training-data-related differences. Overall, the framework supports the analysis of input-dependent model behavior.

Large language models (LLMs) are becoming increasingly diverse, creating a growing need for frameworks that systematically compare the behaviors of different models
Zhu et al. 2025
;
Yax et al. 2025
;
Zhuang et al. 2025
;
Horwitz et al. 2025
;
Jiang et al. 2025
. Quantifying distances between models provides a foundation for a wide range of applications, including model selection, performance prediction, and the analysis of distillation and evolution.

A log-likelihood vector
Oyama et al. 2025b
is a feature representation that characterizes a language model by its log-likelihoods over a predefined set of texts. In this representation, each language model is embedded as a point in a Euclidean space. The squared Euclidean distance in this space is approximately proportional to the Kullback–Leibler (KL) divergence between models, making distances between models interpretable. Dimensionality reduction of these vectors yields a model map that reflects relationships among probability models and enables model behavior to be analyzed from various perspectives.

In contrast, the method of
Oyama et al. 2025b
focuses on unconditional probability distributions, which limits the scope of possible analyses. Extending this framework to conditional probability distributions is therefore important for broadening its applicability. In fact, large language models behave as conditional probability models that define a response distribution
p(y|x)
conditioned on an input prompt
x
. Major tasks such as dialogue and question answering are naturally formulated as conditional generation, and model behavior is likewise governed by
p(y|x)
. It is therefore important to compare language models from the perspective of conditional distributions.

Jiang et al. 2025
proposed a method for measuring differences between language models based on distances in the embedding space of response texts generated by the models for a given input. Although this approach directly addresses the practical setting of conditional generation, it can be affected by the randomness of text generation and the variability of embedding representations.

In this work, we propose a method for comparing a large number of language models in the practical setting of conditional generation, without using texts generated by the models themselves. Specifically, for a set of text pairs
D=\{(x_{s},y_{s})\}_{s=1}^{N}
, we characterize each model by the vector
\{\log p(y_{s}|x_{s})\}_{s=1}^{N}
, which we call a conditional log-likelihood vector. This representation directly evaluates the response distribution conditioned on the input and thus naturally fits the setting of conditional generation. We further show that the squared distance between these vectors approximates the KL divergence between conditional distributions averaged over the input distribution.

Furthermore, to remove the contribution of the unconditional distribution, we introduce a representation based on the difference between the conditional and unconditional log-likelihoods, namely
\log p(y|x)-\log p(y)
. This quantity coincides with pointwise mutual information (PMI) and can be interpreted as a measure of the strength of the relationship between the input and the response.
As we show later, model maps based on PMI vectors can in some cases reflect training-data-related differences more clearly than conditional model maps.

The contributions of this paper are as follows.

1.

We propose a framework for model maps based on conditional log-likelihood vectors over prompt-response text pairs, and show that distances in this space approximate the KL divergence between conditional distributions averaged over the input distribution.

2.

We show that the proposed shared space captures changes in model position induced by prompt modifications (Prompt Shift) and their additive compositionality, suggesting a way to analyze and approximately predict the effects of composite prompt operations.

3.

We introduce PMI vectors to remove the contribution of the unconditional distribution, and show that model maps based on PMI vectors can in some cases reflect training-data-related differences more clearly than conditional model maps.

This study reframes language model comparison from the perspective of conditional probability distributions, and clarifies the relationship between this view and existing unconditional model maps and generation-based methods.
It also suggests a practical use of the shared space: predicting how prompt modifications change model behavior, including the effects of composite prompt operations.

2
Related Work

Existing studies comparing a large number of language models can be broadly categorized by the type of information they use into approaches based on model internals, generated texts, and log-likelihood vectors.

Comparison based on model internals.

Comparisons based on model weights and intermediate representations can directly examine similarities in internal structure. For example,
Zhu et al. 2025
analyzed relationships among models based on statistical similarities of their parameters, and
Horwitz et al. 2025
compared the weights of many models on Hugging Face to reveal relationships that are not easily visible from publicly available information alone. In addition,
Zhou et al. 2025
measured similarities among models using changes in activations induced by linguistic minimal pairs.
While these approaches are useful for analyzing model internals, it is not straightforward to provide a common basis for comparison across models with different architectures or representation spaces.

Comparison based on generated texts.

Comparisons based on generated texts can directly address the practical setting of conditional generation.
Jiang et al. 2025
mapped generated responses into a text embedding space and measured differences among models based on distances in that space. In addition,
Yax et al. 2025
introduced a similarity measure based on conditional probabilities and estimated phylogenetic distances among language models.
However, such approaches can be affected by the randomness of the generation process and by the choice of embedding-based representations for generated outputs.

Comparison based on log-likelihood vectors.

Methods that represent models as vectors based on their log-likelihoods on a pre-defined set of texts
Oyama et al. 2025b
;
Oyama et al. 2025a
;
Kishino et al. 2026
are useful in that they enable many models to be compared in a shared space without relying on generation, and they also allow distances between vectors to be interpreted in relation to the KL divergence between probability distributions. However, existing methods mainly focus on unconditional distributions and do not directly address the setting of conditional generation.

Our study aims to fill this gap. Specifically, we introduce comparisons based on conditional log-likelihood vectors and use PMI-vector representations that subtract unconditional components, thereby providing a framework for comparing the conditional probability distributions of many language models.

3
Our Method: Mapping Conditional Probability Distributions

In this section, we introduce a method for comparing the conditional probability distributions of autoregressive language models based on conditional log-likelihoods. Figure
3
shows the overall setting considered in this study, and Table
1
summarizes the representations used in our method.

3.1
Autoregressive language models

For a language model
p_{i}
, we consider the conditional probability distribution
p_{i}(y|x)
, where
x
denotes a prompt and
y
denotes a response.
In an autoregressive language model,
p_{i}(y|x)
is factorized as

p_{i}(y\mid x)=\prod_{t=1}^{|y|}p_{i}\bigl(z_{t}\mid x,z_{<t}\bigr).

(1)

Here,
|y|
denotes the number of tokens in the response, and we write
y=(z_{1},\dots,z_{|y|})
. In addition,
z_{<t}=(z_{1},\dots,z_{t-1})
denotes the sequence of tokens preceding position
t
.
In the usual use of a language model, the response
y
is generated by sampling. In contrast, our main use in this paper is to fix the response
y
in advance and compute its conditional probability using (
1
).

3.2
Conditional log-likelihood vector

We consider a pre-defined dataset consisting of
N
prompt-response pairs,

D=\{(x_{1},y_{1}),\dots,(x_{N},y_{N})\}.

As a vector representation of a language model
p_{i}
based on this dataset
D
, we define the log-likelihood vector
\bm{\ell}_{i}
as

\bm{\ell}_{i}=(\ell_{i1},\dots,\ell_{iN})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}.

Here,
\ell_{is}
is the log-likelihood assigned by the conditional probability distribution
p_{i}(y|x_{s})
to the pair
(x_{s},y_{s})
, and it is computed using (
1
) as

\ell_{is}=\log p_{i}(y_{s}|x_{s}).

(2)

Furthermore, by subtracting the mean log-likelihood

\bar{\ell}_{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s=1}^{N}\ell_{is}

from each component, we define the centered log-likelihood vector

\bm{\xi}_{i}=(\xi_{i1},\dots,\xi_{iN})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},\qquad\xi_{is}=\ell_{is}-\bar{\ell}_{i}.

In contrast, the log-likelihood vectors introduced in
Oyama et al. 2025b
were based on the log-likelihood

\log p_{i}(y_{s})

(3)

assigned by the unconditional probability distribution
p_{i}(y)
to
y_{s}
. In general, the unconditional definition (
3
) is appropriate when
y_{s}
is an unconditional text chunk, whereas the conditional definition in (
2
) is more appropriate when we are interested in the response
y_{s}
to a prompt
x_{s}
.

3.3
Kullback–Leibler divergence

First, for a given prompt
x
, we measure the difference between the conditional probability distributions of two language models
p_{i}
and
p_{j}
by the conditional KL divergence

\displaystyle\KL(p_{i},p_{j}\mid x)=\bbE_{y\sim p_{i}(y|x)}\left[\log\frac{p_{i}(y|x)}{p_{j}(y|x)}\right].

(4)

We assume that the unknown data-generating distribution underlying the dataset
D
is given by
(x,y)\sim p_{0}(x,y)=p_{0}(y|x)p_{0}(x)
.
Then, we consider the average of this conditional KL divergence over the prompt distribution
x\sim p_{0}(x)
:

\displaystyle\KL(p_{i},p_{j})

\displaystyle=\bbE_{x\sim p_{0}(x)}\Bigl[\KL(p_{i},p_{j}\mid x)\Bigr]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bbE_{(x,y)\sim p_{i}(y|x)p_{0}(x)}\left[\log\frac{p_{i}(y|x)}{p_{j}(y|x)}\right].

(5)

Furthermore, assuming that both
p_{i}(y|x)
and
p_{j}(y|x)
are sufficiently close to
p_{0}(y|x)
, we can apply the argument of
Oyama et al. 2025b
to the joint distribution over
(x,y)
, so that this KL divergence is approximated by

\displaystyle\KL(p_{i},p_{j})\approx\frac{1}{2}\Var_{(x,y)\sim p_{0}(x,y)}\left[\log\frac{p_{i}(y|x)}{p_{j}(y|x)}\right].

(6)

Details of the derivation are given in Appendix
A
.
Approximating this variance by the empirical variance on the dataset
D
, we obtain

\displaystyle\KL(p_{i},p_{j})\approx\frac{1}{2N}\|\bm{\xi}_{i}-\bm{\xi}_{j}\|^{2}

(7)

using the centered log-likelihood vectors
\bm{\xi}_{i}
.
Therefore, the squared Euclidean distance between centered log-likelihood vectors can be interpreted as a quantity that approximates the difference between the conditional probability distributions of language models.

3.4
PMI vector

In Section
3.2
, we introduced the conditional log-likelihood vector

\bm{\ell}_{i}=(\ell_{i1},\ldots,\ell_{iN})^{\top}.

Our definition is based on the conditional probability distribution
p_{i}(y|x)
, whereas
Oyama et al. 2025b
used the unconditional probability distribution
p_{i}(y)
. The difference between the two is whether the association between the prompt
x
and the response
y
is taken into account. We consider the difference between these two log-likelihood vectors and, because it is closely related to pointwise mutual information, refer to it as the PMI vector for convenience:

\Delta\bm{\ell}_{i}=(\Delta\ell_{i1},\ldots,\Delta\ell_{iN})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N},

where each component is the increase in log-likelihood obtained by taking
x
into account:

\displaystyle\Delta\ell_{is}=\log p_{i}(y_{s}|x_{s})-\log p_{i}(y_{s}).

(8)

We also define the centered vector

\bm{\eta}_{i}=(\eta_{i1},\ldots,\eta_{iN})^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{N}

by subtracting the mean
\overline{\Delta\ell}_{i}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s=1}^{N}\Delta\ell_{is}
from each component, where
\eta_{is}=\Delta\ell_{is}-\overline{\Delta\ell}_{i}
.

To interpret
\Delta\bm{\ell}_{i}
, we rewrite the right-hand side of (
8
) as

\Delta\ell_{is}=\log\frac{p_{i}(y_{s}|x_{s})p_{0}(x_{s})}{p_{i}(y_{s})p_{0}(x_{s})}.

The numerator of this expression is the probability given by the joint distribution

p_{i}(x_{s},y_{s})=p_{i}(y_{s}|x_{s})p_{0}(x_{s}),

and the two terms in the denominator are in the form of marginal probabilities. If
p_{i}(y_{s})
were actually given by the marginal probability distribution

\displaystyle p_{i}(y_{s})=\bbE_{x\sim p_{0}(x)}[p_{i}(y_{s}|x)]

(9)

then
\Delta\ell_{is}
would represent the pointwise mutual information (PMI) between
x
and
y
.

In this study, instead of using (
9
) exactly, we use the probability obtained by inputting only the response
y_{s}
into the language model
p_{i}
as an approximation to the marginal probability. We call the resulting vector
\Delta\bm{\ell}_{i}
the PMI vector. The PMI vector is a representation obtained by subtracting the contribution of the unconditional distribution from the conditional log-likelihood vector, and it can be regarded as a feature that more strongly emphasizes information about the dependency structure between prompts and responses.

3.5
Mutual information

The extent to which a language model
p_{i}
captures the association between a prompt
x
and a response
y
can be quantified by mutual information (MI), which is the average of pointwise mutual information:

\displaystyle\textrm{MI}(p_{i})=\bbE_{(x,y)\sim p_{i}(y|x)p_{0}(x)}\left[\log\frac{p_{i}(y|x)}{p_{i}(y)}\right].

(10)

We approximate MI as

\displaystyle\textrm{MI}(p_{i})\approx\overline{\Delta\ell}_{i}.

(11)

This approximation replaces the expectation with respect to
(x,y)\sim p_{i}(y|x)p_{0}(x)
in (
10
) by the empirical average based on
(x,y)\sim p_{0}(x,y)
.

By analogy with (
7
), MI may be heuristically approximated by

\displaystyle\textrm{MI}(p_{i})\approx\frac{1}{2N}\|\bm{\eta}_{i}\|^{2},

(12)

which in turn suggests an interpretation of the centered PMI vector
1

1

MI is the KL divergence between the joint distribution
p_{i}(y|x)p_{0}(x)
and the product of marginals
p_{i}(y)p_{0}(x)
. Therefore, the same variance approximation used for (
6
) applies here as well. On the Tulu3 text set, (
11
) and (
12
) have Pearson and Spearman correlations of
0.945
and
0.930
, respectively, across

models for which both quantities could be computed.

Experimental Settings

4.1
Selection of text data

In our experiments, we use two text sets, denoted by
D=\{(x_{s},y_{s})\}_{s=1}^{N}
.
The first consists of 10,000 prompt-response pairs extracted from
tulu-3-sft-mixture

Lambert et al. 2025
2
2

2

https://huggingface.co/datasets/allenai/tulu-3-sft-mixture
.
The second consists of 10,000 prompt-response pairs extracted from
Infinity-Instruct-7M

Li et al. 2025
3
3

3

https://huggingface.co/datasets/BAAI/Infinity-Instruct
.
For each of these two text sets, we construct a model map and conduct experiments on mutual information in order to analyze how the observed tendencies differ across text sets.

4.2
Selection of language models

To construct the model maps, we considered text generation models listed on Open LLM Leaderboard v2.
Among them, we restricted our analysis to models with at least 1,000 downloads and an available
chat_template
.
We also restricted the model size to no larger than 70B due to computational resource constraints.
In addition, for each model, we attempted to compute log-likelihoods and retained only those for which the computation was numerically stable, without overflow or related issues.
The resulting number of models differs across experiments; specifically, we use
K=552
models for the conditional model map and prompt-shift analyses, and
K=481
models for the PMI model map analysis.

4.3
Computation of the log-likelihood

For each language model, we computed the conditional log-likelihood
\log p(y|x)
and the unconditional log-likelihood
\log p(y)
for each text pair
(x,y)
.
To compute
\log p(y|x)
, we fed each prompt-response pair into the model using the corresponding
chat_template
.
To compute
\log p(y)
, we used the same
chat_template
with the user input left empty, and computed the log-likelihood of the response.
All computations were performed using bfloat16 precision.
In addition, to reduce the influence of extremely small log-likelihood values, we applied clipping separately to
\log p(y|x)
and
\log p(y)
using the lower 2% threshold computed from all model-text combinations.

5.2
Prompt shift

Unlike
Oyama et al. 2025b
, which focuses on unconditional distributions, the use of conditional log-likelihood vectors allows us to directly examine how the generation distribution of each language model changes on the model map in response to changes in the input prompt.
Let
T(x)
denote an operation that transforms an input
x
.
In our framework,
p(\cdot|x)
and
p(\cdot|T(x))
can be compared in the same space as distinct conditional probability models.
We refer to the systematic movement of the conditional probability distribution in response to a change in the prompt as
prompt shift
.

To investigate prompt shift, we consider three prompt transformations obtained by concatenating text to the original input
x
:

T_{\mathrm{cot}}(x)=x+c
,

T_{\mathrm{rep}}(x)=x+x
,
and

T_{\mathrm{rep+cot}}(x)=x+x+c
.
Here,
c
is the CoT-inducing phrase “Let’s think step by step”
Kojima et al. 2022
.
The transformation
T_{\mathrm{rep}}
corresponds to repeating the prompt twice, which has recently been reported to improve the performance of non-reasoning models
Leviathan et al. 2025
.
In the following, we denote the base setting and the settings corresponding to these transformations by

base
,
cot
,
repeat
, and
repeat+cot
, respectively.

For each setting, we construct the corresponding dataset
\{(T(x_{s}),y_{s})\}_{s=1}^{N}
from
\{(x_{s},y_{s})\}_{s=1}^{N}
and compute the conditional log-likelihood vector for each model.
The conditional log-likelihood vectors for model
p_{i}
under the four settings are denoted by
\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{base},i}
,
\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{cot},i}
,
\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{rep},i}
, and
\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{rep+cot},i}
, respectively.
We also define three shift vectors
\bm{v}_{\mathrm{cot},i}=\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{cot},i}-\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{base},i}
,

\bm{v}_{\mathrm{rep},i}=\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{rep},i}-\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{base},i}
, and

\bm{v}_{\mathrm{rep+cot},i}=\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{rep+cot},i}-\bm{\ell}_{\mathrm{base},i}
.

7
Conclusion

We proposed a framework for constructing model maps for prompt-response distributions using conditional log-likelihood vectors. In this shared space, distances between models approximate KL divergence between conditional distributions, extending previous log-likelihood-vector-based model maps to conditional settings. We also introduced PMI vectors to reduce the contribution of unconditional distributions and examined their relation to training-data-dependent differences. We further showed that prompt modifications induce systematic shifts in model position and that these shifts exhibit approximate additive compositionality. These results suggest that the proposed framework is useful for comparing conditional model behavior across language models and analyzing prompt transformations in a common space.

Limitations

•

This study focuses on models whose weights are publicly available, and extending the method to closed models remains future work.

•

The experiments are conducted mainly on instruction-style prompt-response datasets such as Tulu3 and Infinity-Instruct. It remains unclear to what extent the proposed framework generalizes to other settings, such as non-instructional text, multilingual data, code generation, or longer and more complex interactions.

•

The effectiveness of PMI-based quantities is not uniform across all analyses. Although PMI vectors can in some cases reflect training-data-related differences more clearly than conditional log-likelihood vectors, this tendency is not consistently observed, and the practical advantage of PMI-based model maps may depend on the dataset and models being compared.

•

The prompt-shift analysis is limited to a small number of prompt transformations, namely appending a CoT suffix and repeating the prompt. It remains an open question whether similar geometric regularities and approximate additive compositionality hold for a broader class of prompt edits.

•

The visualizations in this paper rely on low-dimensional projections such as t-SNE and PCA. While these are useful for qualitative inspection, they do not fully preserve the geometry of the original high-dimensional space, so apparent proximity or clustering in two dimensions should be interpreted with caution.

•

The empirical validation in Section
6
is conducted on a limited set of eight language models. Although the observed correlations support the proposed approximation, the robustness of these findings across a broader range of models, decoding settings, and evaluation prompts remains to be investigated.

•

The proposed framework depends on specific formatting choices for prompt-response pairs, including prompt templates and prompt transformations. Such implementation details may affect the resulting log-likelihood vectors and model positions, and disentangling these effects from more intrinsic behavioral differences remains future work.

•

p_{0}(y|x)

Appendix A
Details of the Variance Representation of KL Divergence

In this section, we derive the variance representation of KL divergence in (
6
) and the corresponding approximation in (
7
).
First, we transform the right-hand side of (
5
) as follows:

\displaystyle\KL(p_{i},p_{j})

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bbE_{(x,y)\sim p_{i}(y|x)p_{0}(x)}\left[\log\frac{p_{i}(y|x)}{p_{j}(y|x)}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\bbE_{(x,y)\sim p_{i}(y|x)p_{0}(x)}\left[\log\frac{p_{i}(y|x)p_{0}(x)}{p_{j}(y|x)p_{0}(x)}\right].

This expression represents the KL divergence between the joint distributions
p_{i}(y|x)p_{0}(x)
and
p_{j}(y|x)p_{0}(x)
over
(x,y)
.
We assume that both
p_{i}(y|x)
and
p_{j}(y|x)
are sufficiently close to
p_{0}(y|x)
. Then, the corresponding joint distributions
p_{i}(y|x)p_{0}(x)
and
p_{j}(y|x)p_{0}(x)
can also be regarded as sufficiently close to
p_{0}(y|x)p_{0}(x)=p_{0}(x,y)
. Therefore, we can apply Eq. (2) of
Oyama et al. 2025b
to the joint distribution over
(x,y)
, obtaining

\displaystyle 2\KL(p_{i},p_{j})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\Var_{(x,y)\sim p_{0}(x,y)}\left[\log\frac{p_{i}(y|x)p_{0}(x)}{p_{j}(y|x)p_{0}(x)}\right]

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\Var_{(x,y)\sim p_{0}(x,y)}\left[\log\frac{p_{i}(y|x)}{p_{j}(y|x)}\right].

This yields (
6
).
To approximate this variance from the dataset
D
, we use the empirical variance:

\displaystyle 2\KL(p_{i},p_{j})

\displaystyle\approx

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s=1}^{N}\biggl[\log\frac{p_{i}(y_{s}|x_{s})}{p_{j}(y_{s}|x_{s})}-

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s^{\prime}=1}^{N}\log\frac{p_{i}(y_{s^{\prime}}|x_{s^{\prime}})}{p_{j}(y_{s^{\prime}}|x_{s^{\prime}})}\biggr]^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{s=1}^{N}\Bigl(\xi_{is}-\xi_{js}\Bigr)^{2}

\displaystyle=

\displaystyle\frac{1}{N}\|\bm{\xi}_{i}-\bm{\xi}_{j}\|^{2}.

This yields (
7
).
