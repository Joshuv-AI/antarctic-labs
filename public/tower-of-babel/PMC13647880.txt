Chitosan-Coated Oleuropein Nanoliposomes Enable Dose-Sparing
Suppression of Serum Inflammatory Markers in a TNF-α-Induced
Vascular Inflammation Model

Roi Martin B Pajimna, John Wilfred T Malabanan, Nonthaneth Nalinratana, Yilin Song, Pranee Rojsitthisak, Pornchai Rojsitthisak, Agnes L Llamasares-Castillo

Abstract

Vascular inflammation,
marked by endothelial dysfunction and overexpression
of proinflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and monocyte
chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1), is a key contributor to atherosclerosis.
Oleuropein (Ole), a phenolic compound with proven anti-inflammatory
properties, suffers from poor aqueous stability and limited oral bioavailability,
constraining its clinical application. To overcome these limitations,
we developed and optimized chitosan (CS)-coated liposomes for oral
delivery of Ole. Using a Box-Behnken design, the optimized formulation
exhibited a particle size of 168.73 ± 3.86 nm, a polydispersity
index (PDI) of 0.17 ± 0.01, a moderate negative zeta potential
(−22.50 ± 2.39 mV), an encapsulation efficiency of 87.17
± 0.27%, and a loading capacity of 12.22 ± 0.25%. The CS-coated
formulation retained acceptable colloidal stability under refrigerated
storage, enabled pH-responsive sustained release governed by Fickian
diffusion, and enhanced endothelial nanocarrier uptake in EA.hy926
cells. The formulation was well tolerated in vitro, with cell viability
exceeding 90% across a 500–4000 ng/mL concentration range.
In a TNF-α-induced murine model of acute vascular inflammation,
daily oral administration of CS-Ole-Lip (20 mg/kg) significantly suppressed
serum IL-6 by 6.90-fold (p < 0.05) and MCP-1 by
2.45-fold relative to TNF-α controls (p <
0.0001), with numerically greater suppression than that observed with
free Ole and uncoated Ole-Lip at the same dose. CS-Ole-Lip at 2 mg/kg,
one-tenth of the 20 mg/kg dose evaluated in this study, produced substantial
serum cytokine suppression, suggesting a potential dose-sparing effect.
No treatment-associated elevations in the evaluated serum markers
of hepatic or renal function or overt histological abnormalities in
the examined organs were observed. These findings support CS-Ole-Lip
as a stable oral nanocarrier with enhanced anti-inflammatory pharmacodynamic
activity. The mechanistic basis for the dose-sparing effect requires
direct pharmacokinetic and molecular validation.

Introduction

Vascular inflammation
is a central pathophysiological process in
the development and progression of numerous cardiovascular diseases
(CVD), including atherosclerosis, hypertension, and vascular complications
of metabolic diseases.
,
 Endothelial activation promotes
barrier disruption, oxidative stress, and a proinflammatory secretome
that includes interleukin-6 (IL-6) and monocyte chemoattractant protein-1
(MCP-1), thereby programming leukocyte recruitment and amplifying
downstream inflammatory cascades.
,
 Despite the
causal role of endothelial inflammation, therapeutic development in
CVD has remained dominated by lipid lowering and systemic immunomodulation,
leaving a persisting need for interventions that directly and durably
attenuate endothelial inflammatory signaling at the vascular interface.

Oleuropein (Ole), a phenolic secoiridoid abundant in olive leaves
and unrefined olive oil, has been reported to mitigate endothelial
inflammatory activation by suppressing nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB)
signaling, thereby reducing TNF-α-induced cytokine production
and the expression of adhesion-related mediators.
,
 However,
the translational trajectory of Ole remains constrained by chemical
instability and low oral bioavailability, which reduce the fraction
of intact compound available for vascular exposure and generate inconsistent
pharmacodynamic readouts.
,
 An enabling delivery
strategy must therefore address both compound integrity and exposure
control to realize reproducible anti-inflammatory activity.

Nanoliposomal encapsulation provides a versatile platform in which
bilayer composition, surface structure, and interfacial chemistry
can be modified to protect labile polyphenols, modulate release kinetics,
and direct biological interactions at the endothelial interface.
−

 Yet conventional liposomes are prone to leakage, aggregation, and
rapid clearance, particularly under electrolyte-rich or protein-containing
conditions that challenge bilayer integrity and colloidal stability.
,
 Surface functionalization with biopolymers provides a mechanistically
grounded approach to overcome these limitations by stabilizing the
interface and controlling cargo retention during transit across gastrointestinal
and physiological environments. Chitosan (CS) is a cationic polysaccharide
that has been the subject of GRAS notices for specific food uses and
is described in pharmacopeial monographs; these regulatory contexts
do not constitute US FDA approval as a pharmaceutical excipient for
oral drug products.
−

 CS exhibits pH-responsive solubility and mucoadhesive
behavior, enabling prolonged intestinal residence and electrostatic
association with negatively charged biological surfaces. These features
position CS coating as a promising strategy to strengthen liposome
stability and promote prolonged gastrointestinal residence, which
may improve the likelihood of maintaining an intact payload during
transit.

Several CS-Lip systems have been reported for other
therapeutic
contexts: CS-Lip coloaded with exemestane and genistein was developed
for combination breast cancer chemotherapy without response surface
optimization; CS-coated curcumin liposomes
were stabilized against degradation using high-pressure processing
to improve bioactivity retention; and
resveratrol-loaded chitosan nanoparticles were engineered for anti-Helicobacter pylori activity via a gastric-targeted
delivery route. To our knowledge, no
previously reported CS-Lip polyphenol system has been evaluated in
a TNF-α-induced vascular inflammation model with a quantified
pharmacodynamic dose-sparing factor, nor optimized using response
surface methodology for this application. Related precedents include
a Box-Behnken-optimized, chitosan-coated liposomal system developed
for a nonpolyphenol anticancer agent, design of experiments (DoE)-optimized chitosan-based polyphenol
nanocarriers evaluated in nonvascular inflammatory models,
,
 and chitosan nanoparticle-encapsulated oleuropein, which achieved
a comparable dose-sparing pattern in a gastroprotective, nonvascular
model. These studies collectively underscore
the distinct contribution of the present BBD-optimized CS-Ole-Lip
system for TNF-α-induced vascular inflammation. In this context,
we hypothesized that surface modification with CS would increase Ole
stability and retention within nanoliposomes across gastrointestinal
and physiological environments, thereby improving dose integrity and
enhancing suppression of TNF-α-driven endothelial inflammatory
activation relative to uncoated Ole-loaded liposomes (Ole-Lip) and
free Ole. To test this hypothesis, we developed Ole-Lip with and without
CS coating and optimized key formulation variables using Box-Behnken
design (BBD). We then established a relationship between formulation
attributes and biological outcomes by assessing physicochemical performance,
Ole retention and release across biorelevant media, and anti-inflammatory
efficacy in a biological system. Therapeutic activity and systemic
safety were further examined in a TNF-α-induced acute vascular
inflammation mouse model, supported by serum biochemical indices (ALT,
AST, BUN, creatinine) and histopathological evaluation.

Experimental Section

Materials
and Reagents

Ole (98% purity)
was purchased from BLD Pharm, Shanghai, China. Soybean lecithin (90%
purity) was obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific Chemicals, Inc.,
Eindhoven, Netherlands, while cholesterol (>95% purity) was sourced
from Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan. Chitosan (CS),
with a molecular weight of approximately 540 kDa and a degree of deacetylation
greater than 90%, was obtained from Marine Bio-Resources Co., Ltd.,
Samut Prakan, Thailand. EA.hy926 endothelial cells were acquired from
the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC), Manassas, VA, USA. Dulbecco’s
Modified Eagle’s Medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin
(100 U/mL), and streptomycin (100 μg/mL) were purchased from
Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA. MTT solution was
obtained from Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA. All other reagents
and solvents used were of at least analytical grade and employed without
further purification.

Preparation of CS-Ole-Lip

The preparation
of CS-Ole-Lip was carried out according to previously established
protocols with minor modifications.
,
 The preweighed
soy lecithin and cholesterol were dissolved in 5 mL chloroform and
were subjected to rotary evaporation at 40 °C, 120 rpm, 200 mbar
(G3 Heidolph Hei-VAP Core, Heidolph Instruments GmbH & Co. KG,
Bavaria, Germany) to form the thin lipid film (). The flask was kept in a desiccator overnight
to ensure full removal of chloroform via evaporation. Subsequently,
the lipid film was detached from the flask wall and rehydrated in
a rotary evaporator at 55 °C and 120 rpm for 15 min using 3 g
of glass beads (diameter: 3 mm) and 10 mL of phosphate-buffered saline
(PBS, pH 7.4) containing Ole at a known concentration. The resulting
liposome suspension was subjected to high-pressure homogenization
using a high-shear benchtop homogenizer (LV1 Microfluidizer, Microfluidics
Corp., Westwood, MA, USA) at 500 bar for one passage.

After homogenization, 10 mL of a CS solution of known concentration
in 1% (v/v) acetic acid was added dropwise at a rate
of 40 mL/h using an automatic syringe pump (NE-1000 Single Syringe
Pump, New Era Pump Systems, Inc., Farmingdale, NY, USA) under continuous
stirring at 1200 rpm (MS-H380-Pro Hot plate Magnetic Stirrer, Onilab
LLC Scientific Inc., Riverside, CA, USA). Stirring was continued for
an additional 1 h to ensure uniform surface coating. The formulation
was then equilibrated at 4 °C overnight to ensure stabilization
prior to analysis. A blank CS-coated liposome formulation was prepared
following the same procedure, excluding Ole.

Optimization
Using BBD

The experiment
was focused on three critical formulation factors: amount of Ole (A), lecithin/cholesterol mass ratio (B),
and CS concentration (C). To evaluate the effects
of these variables on particle size (Y
1), polydispersity index (PDI) (Y
2), and
loading capacity (LC) (Y
3) of the CS-Ole-Lip,
a three-factor, three-level BBD was employed as summarized in . A total of 15 experimental
runs were generated using Design-Expert software (Version 13, Stat-Ease
Inc., Minneapolis, MN, USA). Each run was performed in triplicate
and conducted in randomized order.

Analysis
of variance (ANOVA) was carried out to determine the statistical
significance of the model terms (Table S1). Response surface plots were used to visualize the interaction
effects among variables and to identify the optimal formulation conditions
with desirability closest to 1.00. In the numerical optimization, Y
1 (particle size) and Y
2 (PDI) were set to minimize, whereas Y
3 (loading capacity) was set to maximize; importance levels
of +++++, ++++, and +++++ were assigned to Y
1, Y
2, and Y
3, respectively, and default lower and upper weight values
of 1 were retained for all responses. The optimized formulation was
then prepared and experimentally validated, and the predicted values
for the selected responses were confirmed. Model robustness was further
evaluated by preparing the optimized formulation in six independent
replicates. In addition to the 95% prediction intervals (PI), the
percentage error for each response was calculated using .

Physicochemical Characterization of CS-Ole-Lip

The
particle size, PDI, and zeta (ζ) potential of the CS-Ole-Lip
were measured using a DLS instrument (Zetasizer Nano ZS, Malvern Instruments
Ltd., Worcestershire, U.K.) at 25 °C. Samples were gently equilibrated
for 10 s and measured in triplicate. Encapsulation efficiency (EE)
was determined indirectly by ultracentrifugation (45,000 rpm for 1
h at 4 °C). Free Ole in the supernatant was collected and quantified
via ultraviolet–visible (UV–Vis) spectrophotometry (Agilent
Cary 60, Santa Clara, CA, USA) at 281 nm using a PBS (pH 7.4) calibration
curve (y = 0.0234x + 0.0184). Potential
spectral interference from lipid and CS components was excluded by
confirming negligible absorbance at 281 nm in supernatants obtained
from blank CS-coated liposomes (CS-Lip) processed under identical
ultracentrifugation conditions. The EE and LC were calculated using and , respectively.

The morphology of the nanoparticles
was examined via transmission electron microscopy (TEM; JEM-1400,
JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) using 1% uranyl acetate solution as the negative
stain. For TEM grid preparation, 10 μL of the CS-Ole-Lip dispersion
was placed onto a copper grid and allowed to stand for 5 min to permit
sample adsorption. Excess sample was gently blotted from the edge
of the grid using filter paper. The grid was then negatively stained
by adding 10 μL of 1% uranyl acetate solution and allowing it
to stand for 2 min. Excess stain was carefully removed using filter
paper, and the grid was left to dry overnight at room temperature
prior to TEM imaging. The sample was prepared directly from the PBS-based
dispersion without prior dialysis or buffer exchange. To compare the
surface functional groups of CS-Ole-Lip with those of Ole, lecithin,
cholesterol, and CS, Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy
was conducted using a Spectrum One FTIR spectrometer (PerkinElmer
Inc., Waltham, MA, USA) in the 400–4000 cm–1 range. Furthermore, the crystalline structure of the nanoparticles
was analyzed using powder X-ray diffraction (XRD; PANalytical X’Pert
Pro, Kassel-Waldau, Germany) operated at 40 kV and 40 mA, scanned
in continuous mode at a step size of 0.02° 2θ at 25 °C.

Colloidal Stability Studies

The colloidal
stability of the optimized CS-Ole-Lip suspension was evaluated under
refrigerated (4.0 ± 2.0 °C) and room temperature (RT; 25.0
± 2.0 °C) storage conditions for a period of three months.
At predetermined intervals, samples were collected to assess changes
in particle size, zeta potential and EE.

In Vitro
Release Study

Ole release
from CS-Ole-Lip was evaluated in enzyme-free simulated gastric fluid

In Vitro
Cellular Uptake Study

The
cellular internalization of CS-Lip was qualitatively assessed by confocal
laser scanning microscopy (CLSM; LSM 980 with Airyscan 2, Carl Zeiss,
Jena, Germany) using a previously reported method with minor modifications. For fluorescence tracing, FITC was used as a
model fluorescent probe in place of Ole to enable visualization of
liposome internalization. FITC-loaded CS-Lip was prepared by substituting
Ole with FITC using the procedure described in . It is acknowledged that
FITC differs from Ole in molecular weight, hydrophobicity, and charge,
and therefore the encapsulation behavior and intracellular distribution
of FITC may not fully replicate those of Ole. Nevertheless, FITC loading
serves as a well-established qualitative indicator of nanocarrier
uptake. EA.hy926 cells were seeded to ∼50% confluence and incubated
for 4 h with either free FITC or FITC-loaded CS-Lip. Cells treated
with serum-free medium served as the negative control. Following incubation,
cells were washed three times with cold PBS, fixed with 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde,
and imaged by CLSM.

In Vitro Cytocompatibility
Studies

Cell Culture

Human endothelial
EA.hy926 (American Type Culture Collection, ATCC CRL-2922, passages
23–25) cells were cultured in DMEM, supplemented with 10% FBS,
100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. The cells were
maintained in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 at 37
°C (Forma Series 3 Water Jacketed CO2 Incubator, Thermo
Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA).

MTT
Cytocompatibility Assay

EA.hy926
cells were seeded into a 96-well plate at a density of 2 × 105 cells/mL and incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Then, the
cells were treated with varying concentrations of CS-Lip, CS-Ole-Lip,
or free Ole and were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. After the treatment
period, the medium was carefully removed, and 100 μL of MTT
solution (0.5 mg/mL) was added to each well. The cells were incubated
for 2 h at 37 °C to allow for formazan formation. The MTT solution
was then discarded, and the resulting formazan crystals were dissolved
in 100 μL of DMSO. Absorbance was recorded at 570 nm using a
microplate reader (CLARIOstar Plus, BMG Labtech, Ortenberg, Germany).
Cell viability was assessed by comparing the absorbance values of
the treated wells to those of the control group.

In Vivo Efficacy and Safety Evaluation

Animals

Male C57BL/6 mice (10 weeks
old; 25.0–30.0 g; specific pathogen-free grade) were purchased
from Changsheng Biotechnology (Shenyang, China). All animal procedures
were conducted in accordance with institutional and national guidelines
for the care and use of laboratory animals and were approved by the
Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shenyang Pharmaceutical
University (SYPU-IACUC-S2025-1121-102). Animals were housed in a controlled
environment (22 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 2% relative humidity,
12 h light/dark cycle) and were maintained on a standard pelleted
diet and ad libitum access to drinking water.

Drug Administration and Treatments

After a 7-day acclimatization
period, the mice were randomly assigned
into six groups (n = 8 per group): Group 1 (G1):
Normal group receiving only phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 7.4);
Group 2 (G2): TNF-α group receiving PBS + TNF-α; Group
3 (G3): Free Ole group (20 mg/kg/day Ole + TNF-α); Group 4 (G4):
Low-dose CS-Ole-Lip group (2 mg/kg/day CS-Ole-Lip + TNF-α);
Group 5 (G5): High-dose CS-Ole-Lip group (20 mg/kg/day CS-Ole-Lip
+ TNF-α); and Group 6 (G6): Ole-Lip group (20 mg/kg/day Ole-Lip
+ TNF-α).

Assigned interventions were administered once
daily via oral gavage for 10 consecutive days (days 1–10).
To assess preventive efficacy, acute vascular inflammation was induced
by intraperitoneal TNF-α administration once daily (25 μg/kg/day)
from day 4 through day 10 in G2–G6, whereas G1 received the
same volume of PBS (pH 7.4). Body weight was recorded daily throughout
the study. Two hours after the final TNF-α injection on day
10, mice were euthanized and whole blood was collected for serum preparation.
Serum was obtained by centrifugation and stored at −80 °C
until subsequent analysis. Of the 8 mice per group, serum samples
from 5 mice were used for MCP-1 and IL-6 analyses. Serum samples from
the remaining 3 mice were used for alanine aminotransferase (ALT),
aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine
(CREA) analyses, and the same 3 mice were used for histopathological
evaluation. All designated samples were included in the respective
analyses, with no exclusions.

Expression
of Proinflammatory Cytokines
in Serum

Whole blood was allowed to clot at RT (25.0 ±
2.0 °C), and serum was collected by centrifugation at 3000 rpm
for 5 min at 4 °C. Serum concentrations of MCP-1 and IL-6 were
quantified using commercially available ELISA kits (Invitrogen, Thermo
Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s
instructions. Analyte concentrations were determined by interpolation
from assay-specific standard curves. Serum MCP-1 and IL-6 concentrations
were analyzed using five mice per group (n = 5).

Safety Evaluation

Serum samples
were collected as described in . Serum biochemical markers of hepatic
and renal function, including ALT, AST, BUN, and CREA, were quantified.
Serum biochemical markers were evaluated using three mice per group
(n = 3), with no exclusions. Histopathological evaluation
was performed on the same three mice, and representative sections
are presented for G1, G3, and G5, selected to compare the normal control,
the free-drug treatment, and the highest-dose CS-Ole-Lip treatment.
Major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys) were subsequently
harvested and processed for histopathological examination using hematoxylin
and eosin (H&E) staining. The main biochemical comparison focused
on G1 and the treated groups (G3−G6), because this assay was
designed to evaluate potential hepatic and renal toxicity associated
with the oleuropein formulations. To provide context for the effect
of TNF-α challenge alone, biochemical marker values for G2 are
reported separately in Supporting Information Table S3.

Statistical Analysis

Data are presented
as mean ± SD for nanoparticle physicochemical characterization
and in vitro experiments, and as mean ± SEM for in vivo studies
unless otherwise stated. Group differences were assessed by one-way
ANOVA followed by the appropriate post hoc comparisons. For , each formulation
series was analyzed independently using one-way ANOVA followed by
Dunnett’s multiple-comparisons test against the corresponding
untreated (zero-concentration) control; no comparisons were statistically
significant (p > 0.05). For , Bonferroni-corrected pairwise comparisons
were performed specifically for G1 versus G2 and for G2 versus each
treatment group (G3–G6). Statistical analyses and graphical
visualization were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.4 (GraphPad Software,
San Diego, CA, USA) and Origin (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA).
A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically
significant.

Results and Discussion

Response Surface Model via BBD

Formulation
optimization was performed first to define a CS-Ole-Lip system with
controlled Ole content and reproducible baseline colloidal attributes,
which is required to interpret TNF-α-responsive anti-inflammatory
outcomes without confounding from dose variability. BBD was selected
to evaluate three formulation variables that provide orthogonal control
over composition and surface structure: Ole amount (A), lecithin-to-cholesterol mass ratio (B), and CS
concentration (C). Factor A defines
the drug level introduced during preparation and sets the payload
range explored, Factor B controls membrane composition
through cholesterol-dependent bilayer packing, and Factor C specifies the polymer available for coating formation
and thus the extent of surface modification. Responses were chosen
to establish a quantitative baseline before retention and bioactivity
studies. Particle size (Y
1) and PDI (Y
2), measured in ultrapure water under standardized
conditions, were used to quantify the initial dispersion state and
population uniformity at preparation. Loading capacity (LC, Y
3) quantified Ole incorporated per carrier mass,
providing a defined starting payload against which subsequent retention
and efficacy outcomes can be interpreted. Across the BBD space (), Y
1 ranged from 159 to 303 nm, Y
2 from 0.19 to 0.33, and Y
3 from 4.5%
to 13.7%, indicating that the selected factor ranges generated substantial
separation in both dispersion attributes and Ole payload within the
experimental domain.

The BBD data set was
then fitted to polynomial models to quantify
factor effects and support numerical optimization. Model adequacy
was evaluated by ANOVA using prespecified criteria: significant regression
(p < 0.05), nonsignificant lack-of-fit (p > 0.05), agreement between adjusted and predicted R2
 (difference ≤ 0.2), and adequate precision
>4. All selected response surface
methodology
(RSM) models satisfied these requirements (Table S1). With model validity established, response surface analysis
was used to visualize how A, B,
and C jointly influence Y
1, Y
2, and Y
3. The 3D response surface plots () summarize the main effects and interaction
patterns that define the trade-offs between dispersion uniformity
and Ole incorporation, providing the quantitative basis for identifying
an operating region appropriate for downstream retention studies and
TNF-α-responsive efficacy assessment.

In evaluating interaction effects
of independent variables on response
variables, Y
1 was adequately captured
by a second-order RSM model, indicating that particle size in the
CS-Ole-Lip system is governed by coupled compositional and interfacial
assembly processes rather than by independent, monotonic factor effects
(A–C; ). B produced
the largest magnitude linear effect on Y
1 (−47.65), establishing the lecithin-to-cholesterol mass ratio
as the dominant size-controlling factor within the explored space.
This dependence is chemically consistent with sterol-dependent modulation
of bilayer packing and membrane physical properties, where changes
in cholesterol content can alter lipid chain ordering and curvature-sensitive
membrane behavior, thereby influencing vesicle assembly and the resulting
hydrodynamic size distribution.
,
 The strong positive
curvature (+46.60) indicates a pronounced nonlinear response, supporting
the presence of a composition window that favors smaller particle
sizes and flanking regions where size increases as membrane rigidity
and restructuring behavior change. C increased Y

mild convexity (+3.04), consistent with CS coating increasing hydrodynamic
diameter through growth of the interfacial layer and, at higher polymer
availability, greater propensity for polymer-mediated association. In contrast, A contributed
only a small positive effect (+2.71) with weak curvature (+0.8938),
indicating that the amount of Ole has limited direct influence on
size under average formulation conditions and primarily modulates
size through composition-dependent interfacial effects rather than
as an intrinsic driver of vesicle enlargement.

The dominant
interaction was BC (−11.10), indicating that
the size consequence of CS depends
strongly on bilayer composition. This observed behavior is supported
by the established role of cholesterol in modulating bilayer packing,
ordering, rigidity, and liposome stability, thereby altering the interfacial
conditions that govern CS adsorption and particle association during
coating.

CS effect on loading capacity in the present system.

The mechanistic contrast among the three response

for future formulation development. Y

changes in particle size distribution and dispersion uniformity. In

is governed primarily by drug feed availability rather than by the
coupled interfacial assembly dynamics that control the colloidal responses.

modifications to lecithin/cholesterol ratio or CS concentration intended
to tune particle size will have limited, predictable effects on Ole
payload, which can instead be controlled independently through the
Ole input quantity during preparation. The practical significance
of the three optimized response values connects the formulation properties
with the downstream biological studies that follow. Specifically, Y
1 (168.73 nm) places the formulation within
the sub-200 nm range associated with enhanced mucosal adhesion, intestinal
epithelial transcytosis, and endothelial cell internalization, all
relevant to the intended oral-to-vascular delivery route. Y
2 (PDI = 0.17) indicates a monomodal population
essential for reproducible release kinetics and consistent dose delivery
across individual animals in the in vivo model. Finally, Y
3 (LC = 12.22%) establishes the Ole payload per unit carrier
mass that determines the Ole concentrations in the dosing suspensions
to be used in subsequent experiments. Preliminary range-finding trials
conducted prior to the Box-Behnken design indicated that Ole amounts
above 20 mg (25–40 mg) resulted in visible precipitation of
the liposomal suspension, and CS concentrations below 0.05 mg/mL (0.025
and 0.0125 mg/mL) resulted in unstable, highly variable zeta potential
values; these observations informed the factor ranges selected for
the design space ().

Optimization and Validation

The above
interaction analysis shows that response behavior is governed by coupled
changes in membrane composition and surface modification, producing
nonadditive shifts in size distribution and payload. This motivates
a model-guided optimization step to identify a single operating point
that satisfies the multiresponse criteria simultaneously. The optimized
condition and its confirmatory outcomes are summarized in . The optimized settings,
20 mg Ole amount, a lecithin-to-cholesterol mass ratio of 3.97:1,
and 0.05 mg/mL CS, define a composition expected to control dispersion
state while maintaining adequate payload incorporation. Numerical
optimization identified this compromise solution with an overall desirability
of D = 0.899. The predicted LC at this compromise point (12.2%) is
lower than the highest single-run LC in the design matrix (13.69%,
run 6, ), because
the desirability function constrained particle size and PDI simultaneously
rather than maximizing LC alone. Model reliability at this point was
evaluated using the 95% prediction intervals and by preparing the
formulation in six independent replicates to capture run-to-run variability.

All observed
means fell within their respective 95% prediction
intervals, with errors below 10% across responses. Y
1 showed close agreement with the model (5.19% error),
supporting accurate capture of the dominant determinants of hydrodynamic
size at the optimum. Y
2 was similarly
well predicted (5.56% error), indicating that the optimized setting
yields a reproducible, monomodal dispersion. Y
3 exhibited near-identical agreement between prediction and
experiment (0.16% error), consistent with well-resolved parametrization
of Ole incorporation at the optimized composition and low sensitivity
to run-to-run variation.

Structural and Physicochemical
Characterization
of the Optimized Formulation

Following numerical optimization
and experimental confirmation, the optimized CS-Ole-Lip formulation
was subjected to structural and physicochemical characterization to
verify key material attributes and define parameters relevant to dispersion
stability and payload performance. The optimized system showed a hydrodynamic
diameter of 168.73 ± 3.86 nm (A) with a PDI of 0.17 ± 0.01, indicating
a relatively narrow size distribution. The zeta potential was −22.50
± 2.39 mV (B), consistent with a moderately negative surface charge.

Although CS is cationic, the negative zeta potential
is mechanistically
plausible and may be explained in two ways: first, protonation of
CS amino groups is pH-dependent, and partial deprotonation at neutral
to slightly basic conditions reduces the positive surface contribution. Second, negatively charged lipid components
in the formulation, including phospholipid headgroups, can dominate
the net interfacial charge and outweigh the contribution of CS, resulting
in an overall negative zeta potential.
,
 As a direct
control for CS coating, the zeta potential of uncoated Ole-Lip was
measured as −24.65 ± 1.09 mV (n = 6),
compared with −22.50 ± 2.39 mV (n = 6)
for CS-Ole-Lip. The shift toward a less negative value upon CS addition
is directionally consistent with partial surface charge neutralization
by adsorbed CS, but the difference did not reach statistical significance
(unpaired two-tailed t test, p =
0.07). We therefore do not present the zeta potential shift as independent
evidence of coating and instead rely on the FTIR and XRD data described
below as the primary indication of CS association with the liposome
surface. In addition, we note that this net negative zeta potential
reflects the balance of surface charge integrated over the entire
particle and does not preclude a fraction of CS amino groups remaining
protonated at the polymer–lipid interface; this local, partially
protonated character underlies the short-range electrostatic interactions
described in and . At this magnitude, the
zeta potential suggests sufficient electrostatic repulsion to support
colloidal stability and reduce aggregation tendency during storage
and handling, which is important for preserving nanoparticle performance. In terms of payload metrics, the optimized formulation
achieved an EE of 87.17 ± 0.27% and a LC of 12.22 ± 0.25%,
indicating efficient Ole incorporation under the optimized preparation
conditions.

TEM imaging provided qualitative morphological evidence
of nanoscale
vesicular particles with generally spherical morphology (C). The needle-like
textured background observed in C is most consistent with crystallization of PBS salts
during negative staining, as the sample was imaged directly from the
PBS-based dispersion without prior dialysis or buffer exchange; this
preparation artifact limits morphological interpretation, and the
retained image cannot exclude sample heterogeneity. The apparent particle
size observed by TEM was smaller than the hydrodynamic diameter measured
by dynamic light scattering (DLS), which is expected because DLS measures
hydrated particles in suspension, including the solvation layer and
any surface-associated polymer contribution, whereas TEM visualizes
dried particles under vacuum conditions after staining.

FTIR analysis further substantiated assembly
of the CS-Ole-Lip
system at the molecular level through band broadening, shifts, and
intensity attenuation relative to the individual components (D). In particular,
the broad band near 3355 cm–1, assigned to overlapping
O–H and N–H stretching vibrations of CS, became broader
and slightly shifted in the nanoparticle spectrum, which is consistent
with altered hydrogen bonding interactions after formulation, suggesting
that CS hydroxyl and amino groups participated in intermolecular interactions
within the assembled carrier, rather than behaving as free/unassociated
CS. Moreover, the Ole-associated CO band at 1734 cm–1 decreased in intensity in the formulated system, while the CS amide
I band around 1645 cm–1 was also attenuated. Although
FTIR alone does not define the exact binding mode, the concurrent
changes in these bands support noncovalent interactions, most plausibly
hydrogen bonding and secondary polar interactions, among Ole, CS,
and the lipid phase. Importantly, the spectrum of the CS-Ole-Lip showed
band overlap and reduced signal definition relative to the isolated
raw materials, which argues against simple spectral superposition
of physically separated constituents. Instead, these spectral features
support successful incorporation of Ole within an interacting polymer–lipid
matrix and are consistent with complexation in the optimized formulation.
XRD analysis (E) further supports this observation wherein characteristic sharp
crystalline peaks of Ole, CS, soy lecithin, and cholesterol were evidently
reduced in the diffractogram of CS-Ole-Lip. Instead, a broad diffuse
peak around 20° (2θ) was observed, indicative of an amorphous
structure. This transition from crystalline to amorphous form confirms
that Ole was incorporated within the lipid–polymer matrix in
a less crystalline state, which is consistent with successful nanoparticle
formation.

Colloidal Stability

The colloidal
stability of nanoparticulate drug carriers is a critical factor influencing
their shelf life, delivery performance, and clinical applicability.
In this study, the stability of the optimized CS-Ole-Lip was systematically
assessed under refrigerated (4.0 ± 2.0 °C) and RT (25.0
± 2.0 °C) conditions over a three-month period. The parameters
evaluated included particle size, PDI, zeta potential, and EE, all
of which serve as indicators of nanoparticle integrity and storage
stability.

As shown in A–C, CS-Ole-Lip maintained relatively consistent
particle size, PDI, and surface charge under refrigeration, with minimal
fluctuations observed throughout the 3-month storage period. This
suggests a stable colloidal system with low aggregation propensity
at reduced temperatures. In contrast,
samples stored at 25 °C showed a statistically significant increase
in particle size and PDI as early as one month of storage (p < 0.05), suggesting the onset of structural destabilization
and particle aggregation. This temperature-dependent behavior likely
reflects the impact of increased molecular motion and collision frequency
at higher thermal energy, which promotes vesicle fusion or swellinga
phenomenon commonly reported in polymer-coated nanocarriers.
,,

Similarly, EE values remained
largely unchanged at 4.0 ± 2.0
°C, suggesting effective retention of Ole within the lipid bilayer
under cold storage. However, a noticeable decline in EE was observed
at 25.0 ± 2.0 °C, particularly during the later stages of
storage. This reduction may be attributed to progressive Ole leakage
from the liposomal matrix, potentially accelerated by lipid rearrangement
or CS loosening at elevated temperatures.
,

These findings reinforce the importance of low-temperature
storage
in preserving the physicochemical attributes of CS-Ole-Lip. The results
demonstrate that refrigerated storage is preferable over the three-month
period evaluated for minimizing particle destabilization and active
compound loss, particularly for temperature-sensitive polyphenolic
drugs like Ole. These findings are consistent with previous reports
where nanoparticle destabilization was driven by temperature-dependent
changes in hydrophilic–hydrophobic interactions and surface
charge dynamics.
,
 It should be noted that the present
stability assessment monitors colloidal attributes (particle size,
PDI, zeta potential) and Ole retention (EE) but does not directly
quantify Ole chemical degradation products. The observed decline in
EE at 25 °C could reflect either physical leakage of intact Ole
from the liposomal matrix or chemical degradation of Ole within the
carrier.

In Vitro Release Study

To evaluate
the release characteristics of Ole from the CS-Ole-Lip, an in vitro
release study was conducted in SGF (pH 1.2), SIF (pH 6.8), and physiological
buffer (pH 7.4) to compare how the formulation behaves as pH shifts
from the stomach to the intestine and then to physiological conditions
(). The study
was extended to 96 h to capture the full sustained release phase and
approach to a plateau, rather than to replicate gastrointestinal transit
time. Release increased steadily in all media and followed a biphasic
pattern, with an early rise followed by a slower phase approaching
a plateau. Cumulative release increased from ∼2–3% at
0.5 h to ∼20–23% at 6 h, and no differences were detected
within this early window (p > 0.05). At 12 h,
values
remained closely aligned at 37.7 ± 0.8% (pH 1.2), 35.4 ±
1.3% (pH 6.8), and 39.7 ± 1.9% (pH 7.4) (n =
3), consistent with early release dominated by an accessible Ole fraction.
Clear pH effects appeared later, when transport is dominated by diffusion
through the liposomal membrane and the CS coating. From 8–12
h, release at pH 7.4 became higher than at pH 6.8 (p < 0.05), and by 48–96 h all media were distinct (p < 0.0001). At 96 h, cumulative release reached 55.0
± 1.3% at pH 7.4, 48.5 ± 2.2% at pH 1.2, and 43.6 ±
1.6% at pH 6.8, giving the late-phase order pH 7.4 > pH 1.2 >
pH 6.8.
Practically, this means the formulation limits rapid early leakage,
then releases Ole gradually in a pH-dependent manner as exposure continues.

The 55.0 ± 1.3% cumulative release at pH 7.4 after 96 h indicates
a sustained and incomplete release profile for CS-Ole-Lip. Although
previous studies on chitosan- or polymer-coated phospholipid vesicles
commonly evaluated release over shorter periods, their findings support
the mechanistic interpretation observed in the present study. For
instance, CS-coated genistein-loaded liposomes showed reduced drug
release under acidic gastric conditions and controlled release under
intestinal pH, with release behavior attributed to Fickian diffusion. Similarly, multilayered quercetin-loaded liposomes
demonstrated that increasing polymer coating layers reduced drug release,
indicating that the external polymer layer can act as a diffusion-controlling
barrier. In the present formulation,
the low early phase release (∼20–23% at 6 h), followed
by gradual progression toward a plateau, suggests that Ole release
was restricted by both its retention within the phospholipid bilayer
and the barrier effect of the chitosan coating. This sustained, incomplete
release behavior may be relevant for the intended oral delivery application,
as it may limit the risk of premature drug release during gastric
exposure while maintaining sustained Ole availability under intestinal
pH conditions. However, whether the released Ole fraction translates
to pharmacologically sufficient systemic exposure depends on absorptive
processes at the intestinal epithelium, which are beyond the scope
of the in vitro dialysis model and will be assessed directly in the
planned pharmacokinetic study.

This ordering is compatible with
a CS-Lip because the coating is
an interfacial layer whose barrier strength depends on CS ionization
and how tightly it remains associated with the liposome surface. At
pH 6.8, CS is still partly protonated and can remain strongly associated
with negatively charged lipid headgroups, forming a more coherent
barrier that slows diffusion.
,
 At pH 7.4, CS is less
protonated, which can weaken interfacial cohesion and make the coating
less continuous, increasing bilayer accessibility and net diffusion
over time.
,
 The intermediate behavior at
pH 1.2 is consistent with strong protonation and hydration of CS,
which supports coating association but can also increase water penetration,
producing neither the slowest nor the fastest release.
−

 Our results indicate that CS-Ole-Lip limits early Ole release in
SGF and exhibits sustained, pH-dependent release behavior across the
tested media.

To further elucidate the underlying release mechanism,
cumulative
release data were fitted to zero-order, first-order, Higuchi, Korsmeyer–Peppas,
and Hixson–Crowell kinetic models using DDSolver (Table S2). The Korsmeyer–Peppas model
demonstrated the best overall fit (R
2 =
0.8835–0.9227), followed by the Higuchi model (R
2 = 0.7200–0.8221), indicating a diffusion-controlled
release process. The n values for the CS-Ole-Lip
(0.3071–0.3396) remained below 0.45, confirming Fickian diffusion.
This slow diffusional rate is attributed to the combined barrier effect
of the lipid bilayer and the CS shell, which collectively restrict
molecular transport.

These findings are consistent with previous
reports on polymer-coated
nanoparticle systems, where diffusion remains the dominant release
mechanism when polymer erosion or matrix relaxation is minimal. In contrast to systems exhibiting anomalous
transport due to extensive polymer swelling or degradation, the kinetic
fits are consistent with diffusion-controlled transport; carrier integrity,
however, was not measured directly during the release experiment. The poor fit of the zero-order, first-order,
and Hixson–Crowell models (R
2 <
0.48) further indicates that constant-rate release and erosion-controlled
mechanisms were not predominant. The observed model fit supports diffusion-dominated
release under the tested in vitro conditions but does not by itself
establish improved oral bioavailability. A limitation of the present
release study is that the three pH media were evaluated in parallel
rather than sequentially (SGF → SIF → physiological
buffer). The parallel design characterizes the CS coating’s
pH-dependent barrier properties at each discrete condition under controlled,
decoupled conditions, but does not simulate the dynamic sequential
transition in medium composition and enzyme exposure that occurs during
gastrointestinal transit, a distinction that is particularly relevant
for a system intended for oral administration. A sequential biorelevant
cascade study using SGF with pepsin for 2 h, transferred to SIF with
pancreatin for 6 h, then PBS pH 7.4 to 96 h, is planned as the next
step to assess whether the CS coating maintains its release-modulating
behavior under conditions more closely approximating gastrointestinal
transit. Additionally, the use of enzyme-free media in the present
study does not account for lipase-mediated disruption of the lipid
bilayer. This enzyme effect will also be characterized in the planned
sequential study.

In Vitro Cellular Uptake
in EA.hy926 Cells

The internalization of CS-Lip was qualitatively
assessed using
CLSM. As shown in , EA.hy926 endothelial cells treated with FITC-loaded CS-Lip
exhibited markedly stronger green fluorescence in the cytoplasm compared
to those treated with free FITC. Only the free FITC and FITC-loaded
CS-Lip groups were imaged in this experiment. The left panel shows
nuclear staining with Hoechst 33342 (blue), the middle panel shows
FITC fluorescence (green), and the merged images illustrate intracellular
localization.

Confocal micrographs revealed qualitatively greater
intracellular
green fluorescence in EA.hy926 cells treated with FITC-loaded CS-Lip
compared to those treated with free FITC, providing qualitative evidence
of CS-Lip nanocarrier internalization into vascular endothelial cells.
This enhanced uptake is consistent with the mucoadhesive and membrane-interactive
properties of CS, in which locally protonated amino groups on the
CS chain can interact electrostatically with negatively charged membrane
components, facilitating adsorptive endocytosis and increasing cellular
contact time.
,
 However, the limitations of the
FITC surrogate must be acknowledged explicitly. FITC (MW 389 Da; log P ≈ −1.4; anionic at physiological pH) differs
substantially from Ole (MW 540 Da; log P ≈
−0.1; neutral) in molecular weight, lipophilicity, and charge,
such that its encapsulation behavior within CS-Lip, its intravesicular
localization, and its intracellular redistribution following nanocarrier
internalization may not replicate the behavior of Ole. Accordingly,
these CLSM observations must be interpreted strictly as evidence of
enhanced CS-Lip nanocarrier internalization into EA.hy926 cells, and
not as a direct measure of Ole uptake efficiency or intracellular
Ole distribution. Quantitative characterization via flow cytometric
quantification of mean fluorescence intensity from FITC-loaded CS-Lip
across treatment groups or LC-MS/MS quantification of intracellular
Ole in EA.hy926 lysates may be further explored in future studies
to provide rigorous cellular pharmacokinetic data for this nanocarrier
system.

In Vitro Cytocompatibility Studies

The cytotoxicity of CS-Ole-Lip, free Ole, and blank CS-Lip was evaluated
in EA.hy926 human vascular endothelial cells using the MTT assay to
assess the cytocompatibility of the developed nanocarrier system.
As shown in , all formulations maintained cell viability
values above 90% across the tested concentration range (500–4000
ng/mL), indicating negligible cytotoxicity.

Notably, blank CS-Lip exhibited no significant
cytotoxic effect
at any tested dose, indicating cytocompatibility of the delivery vehicle
under the specific in vitro conditions tested. This observation is
consistent with earlier reports that CS-coated nanocarriers and liposomal
systems exhibit minimal toxicity in endothelial and epithelial cell
lines under selected in vitro conditions. The absence of toxicity in the blank formulation supports its potential
as a safe platform for vascular-targeted delivery, although broader
biocompatibility requires integrated in vivo safety, immune-response,
and hemocompatibility evaluation.

Free Ole showed slightly lower
mean viability than CS-Ole-Lip at
some concentrations, but all mean values remained above 90%. Because
the results do not show direct between-formulation significance, this
pattern should not be interpreted as evidence that encapsulation protected
mitochondrial function.

Importantly, no dose-dependent decrease
in viability was observed
across the 500–4000 ng/mL range, suggesting that both free
and encapsulated Ole maintain a favorable safety profile even at the
higher tested concentrations. The ability to maintain >90% cell
viability
in endothelial cells is particularly important for formulations intended
for vascular or circulatory system applications, where prolonged exposure
to the vascular endothelium requires minimal cytotoxicity to ensure
safety and therapeutic efficacy.

The concentration range evaluated
(500–4000 ng/mL) was selected
based on effective anti-inflammatory concentrations observed in preliminary
experiments. Although all formulations showed >90% viability across
this range, the narrow concentration window precludes determination
of IC50 values. Expanded dose–response studies across
a wider concentration range (e.g., up to 100 μg/mL) would provide
a more complete cytotoxicity profile and define the therapeutic index
of CS-Ole-Lip in endothelial cells.

CS-Ole-Lip
Suppresses Serum MCP-1 and IL-6
in TNF-α-Induced Vascular Inflammation in Mice

The
anti-inflammatory efficacy of CS-Ole-Lip was evaluated in vivo using
a TNF-α-induced vascular inflammation mouse model. Serum levels
of proinflammatory cytokines, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1 (MCP-1)
and interleukin-6 (IL-6), were quantified by ELISA to assess the systemic
inflammatory response following treatment (). Treatment with free Ole (20 mg/kg) moderately reduced MCP-1
by 1.67-fold compared with the TNF-α group (p < 0.01). Although IL-6 levels were reduced by 3.50-fold, this
difference was not statistically significant. CS-Ole-Lip at 20 mg/kg
produced the strongest suppression of both cytokines, reducing MCP-1
by 2.45-fold (p < 0.0001) and IL-6 by 6.90-fold
(p < 0.05) compared with the TNF-α group,
nearly restoring cytokine levels to those observed in healthy controls.
Low-dose CS-Ole-Lip (2 mg/kg) also retained substantial activity,
reducing MCP-1 by 1.76-fold (p < 0.001) and IL-6
by 4.45-fold (p < 0.05) compared with the TNF-α
group. Ole-Lip at 20 mg/kg reduced MCP-1 and IL-6 levels by 2.21-fold
(p < 0.0001) and 6.51-fold (p < 0.05), respectively, relative to the TNF-α group.

Together, these results indicate that liposomal encapsulation
and
CS coating are associated with improved anti-inflammatory pharmacodynamic
performance relative to free Ole. However, the present in vivo design
did not include a chitosan-only, drug-free comparator group. Therefore,
comparisons between Ole-Lip and CS-Ole-Lip reflect the combined effects
of nanoliposomal encapsulation and CS coating, and the relative contribution
of CS intrinsic antioxidant or anti-inflammatory activity cannot be
fully isolated from carrier-related functions such as improved Ole
retention, mucoadhesion, and oral exposure.

The numerically
greater pharmacodynamic response of CS-Ole-Lip
(20 mg/kg) relative to Ole-Lip (20 mg/kg) across both cytokines, 6.90-fold
versus 6.51-fold IL-6 reduction and 2.45-fold versus 2.21-fold MCP-1
reduction, may be consistent with a CS coating contribution beyond
simple liposomal encapsulation; however, no direct statistical comparison
between the two liposomal groups was reported. The magnitude of this
difference was modest at the 20 mg/kg dose, suggesting that both liposomal
formulations delivered Ole in pharmacodynamically effective quantities
and that the incremental benefit of CS coating at this dose may reflect
marginal rather than dramatic differences in effective exposure. This
pattern is qualitatively consistent with reports of CS-coated or CS-modified
lipid nanocarriers improving antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity
relative to uncoated comparators, including soluble soybean polysaccharide/chitosan-encapsulated
quercetin and chitosan-oligosaccharide-modified cordycepin nanoliposomes.
,
 The review of CS-coated liposome systems by Alcantara et al. identifies CS-mediated electrostatic interaction
with negatively charged mucosal and cellular surfaces and prolonged
gastrointestinal residence as primary mechanistic contributors to
pharmacodynamic enhancement.

The most pharmacodynamically informative
finding is the dose-sparing
effect, where CS-Ole-Lip at 2 mg/kg, one-tenth of the 20 mg/kg dose
evaluated in this study, achieved IL-6 suppression of 4.45-fold and
MCP-1 suppression of 1.76-fold, both statistically significant relative
to the TNF-α control group (p < 0.05) and
numerically exceeding the activity of free Ole at 20 mg/kg (3.50-fold
IL-6; 1.67-fold MCP-1). These pharmacodynamic contributors are mechanistically
consistent with the physicochemical profile established in and : the sub-200 nm hydrodynamic diameter (168.73
nm) and narrow size distribution (PDI = 0.17) fall within the range
reported to favor mucoadhesive retention and epithelial transcytosis,
while the CS surface layer, evidenced by FTIR/XRD (), zeta potential shift
relative to uncoated Ole-Lip, and the pH-dependent release barrier
(), is
compatible with prolonged intestinal residence and gradual Ole release
that could affect systemic exposure. Although plasma Ole concentration–time
profiling after oral gavage was not included in the present study,
the marked cytokine suppression observed with low-dose CS-Ole-Lip
provides pharmacodynamic evidence compatible with enhanced oral performance
of the CS-coated formulation. The absence of pharmacokinetic data
precludes a formal bioavailability claim; therefore, the dose-sparing
effect is interpreted as a pharmacodynamic finding consistent with,
but not proof of, CS-mediated enhancement of oral Ole delivery.

The suppression of serum MCP-1 and IL-6 is pharmacodynamically
relevant to the vascular inflammatory context. MCP-1 (CCL2) is the
principal chemoattractant driving monocyte recruitment to activated
endothelium and subsequent foam cell formation, a rate-limiting early
step in atherosclerotic plaque development,
,
 and
IL-6 amplifies the systemic acute-phase response and drives STAT3-mediated
endothelial dysfunction. While the present study did not directly
measure endothelial NF-κB activation in vascular tissue, reduction
of both markers by CS-Ole-Lip treatment thus addresses upstream drivers
of endothelial inflammatory activation relevant to cardiovascular
disease. This provides a coherent pharmacodynamic readout that aligns
with the anti-inflammatory action of Ole attributed to NF-κB
pathway inhibition in endothelial cells, reducing transcriptional
drive for MCP-1 and IL-6 expression.
,
 Direct confirmation
of the local endothelial pathway will require vascular tissue immunofluorescence
for NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation, VCAM-1, and ICAM-1, together
with in vitro NF-κB pathway assays in TNF-α-stimulated
EA.hy926 cells. These experiments are therefore positioned as mechanistic
validation studies in .

Safety Evaluation in Mice

To evaluate
the systemic toxicity of the CS-Ole-Lip, serum biochemical markers
of liver (ALT, AST) and kidney (BUN, CREA) function were quantified
following treatment in the TNF-α-induced vascular inflammation
mouse model. As shown in , no statistically significant differences were observed
in ALT, AST, CREA, and BUN levels among the treated groups and the
healthy control group (p > 0.05; n = 3 per group). These findings indicate that administration of CS-Ole-Lip
did not induce detectable hepatocellular injury or nephrotoxicity
under the study conditions. To provide context for the effect of TNF-α
challenge alone, ALT, AST, BUN, and CREA values for the G2 (TNF-α
+ PBS) group are reported in Supporting Information Table S3.

This systemic safety profile is consistent with prior reports demonstrating
the biocompatibility of CS-based delivery systems and their minimal
interaction with off-target organs at therapeutic doses.
,,

Histological evaluation
was performed in G1, G3, and G5 to establish
an organ-specific safety profile. The heart, liver, spleen, lungs,
and kidneys were harvested and processed using hematoxylin and eosin
(H&E) staining for microscopic observation (). No overt signs of inflammation,
necrosis, tissue degeneration, or leukocyte infiltration were observed
across all examined organs in the CS-Ole-Lip-treated group. The liver
exhibited well-preserved lobular architecture, while the kidney glomeruli
and tubules appeared morphologically intact. Similarly, pulmonary
alveolar structures, splenic white pulp, and cardiac muscle fibers
remained unaffected. These histological findings reinforce the conclusion
from the biochemical assays that CS-Ole-Lip possesses a favorable
safety profile, at least under the dosage and treatment duration evaluated.
Importantly, the histological normalcy of the kidneys and liver is
particularly noteworthy given their critical roles in drug metabolism
and excretion.

Limitations
and Future Directions

The present study establishes the initial
formulation, release, cellular
interaction, and acute anti-inflammatory profile of CS-Ole-Lip. These
findings provide a basis for the next stage of preclinical characterization,
particularly in defining how chitosan coating influences oral exposure,
endothelial delivery, and vascular inflammatory signaling. The current
data support pharmacodynamic activity under controlled experimental
conditions, while several additional studies can further refine the
mechanistic and translational interpretation of the system.

A key next step is pharmacokinetic validation. The observed response
pattern, in which CS-Ole-Lip at 2 mg/kg produced greater serum MCP-1
and IL-6 suppression than free Ole at 20 mg/kg, is consistent with
enhanced oral performance after chitosan-coated liposomal delivery.
Direct plasma Ole concentration–time profiling would strengthen
this interpretation by determining whether the pharmacodynamic advantage
is primarily exposure-driven. A future study comparing free Ole, Ole-Lip,
and CS-Ole-Lip after oral gavage in C57BL/6 mice, with plasma sampling
at 0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 24 h and LC-MS/MS quantification of
Ole, would allow calculation of Cmax, AUC0–24, Tmax,
and relative oral bioavailability against an intravenous reference
dose. In addition, the present in vivo design did not include a chitosan-only,
drug-free comparator group. Because chitosan has documented intrinsic
antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity, part of the enhanced efficacy
of CS-Ole-Lip relative to Ole-Lip could reflect a direct contribution
of chitosan itself, in addition to CS-mediated improvements in Ole
retention, mucoadhesion, and oral exposure. A future study incorporating
a CS-only gavage arm, dose-matched to the chitosan content of the
CS-Ole-Lip 20 mg/kg group, is needed to isolate this contribution.

The acute TNF-α-induced murine model provides a reproducible
first-tier platform for evaluating anti-inflammatory nanoformulations
because it generates defined cytokine end points through a controlled
inflammatory stimulus. Within this scope, the model is appropriate
for demonstrating systemic anti-inflammatory activity and comparing
formulation-dependent pharmacodynamic responses. A logical extension
would be validation in ApoE–/– or LDLr–/– mice maintained on a high-fat diet, where
the performance of CS-Ole-Lip can be examined in the context of chronic
lipid-driven vascular inflammation, plaque formation, and lesion progression.

The two-dose in vivo design was useful for identifying a preliminary
pharmacodynamic dose-sparing pattern. Since CS-Ole-Lip at 2 mg/kg
produced substantial cytokine suppression relative to free Ole at
20 mg/kg, a more detailed dose–response study would help define
the magnitude of this effect quantitatively. A future five-dose comparison
of CS-Ole-Lip and free Ole at 0.5, 2, 5, 10, and 20 mg/kg in the same
TNF-α model would permit construction of dose–response
curves for MCP-1 and IL-6, estimation of ED50 values, and calculation
of a dose-sparing factor as the ED50 ratio between formulations. Future
work extending the design space beyond the practically constrained
boundaries identified here () may further clarify whether higher LC
is achievable without compromising particle size, PDI, coating integrity,
or physical stability.

The cellular uptake experiment provides
qualitative evidence of
CS-Lip association with endothelial cells using a fluorescent FITC
surrogate. This result supports the plausibility of chitosan-mediated
cellular interaction, but quantitative confirmation would further
define the contribution of the coating to nanocarrier uptake and Ole
delivery. Flow cytometric analysis of fluorescently labeled CS-Lip
cellular association, combined with LC-MS/MS quantification of intracellular
Ole in EA.hy926 lysates after treatment with CS-Ole-Lip, Ole-Lip,
and free Ole, would provide a stronger link between carrier uptake
and payload delivery. The cellular uptake comparison was also limited
to free FITC and FITC-loaded CS-Lip; inclusion of FITC-loaded uncoated
liposomes in future experiments would more directly isolate the contribution
of CS coating to endothelial uptake.

The serum cytokine results
indicate suppression of MCP-1 and IL-6,
both of which are relevant to vascular inflammatory activation. To
connect this systemic response more directly with endothelial signaling,
future studies should assess NF-κB p65 nuclear translocation,
VCAM-1, and ICAM-1 expression in aortic sections using immunofluorescence.
Complementary in vitro assays in TNF-α-stimulated EA.hy926 cells,
such as NF-κB luciferase reporter analysis or nuclear p65 Western
blotting, would clarify whether CS-Ole-Lip reduces inflammatory signaling
through endothelial NF-κB pathway inhibition at matched Ole
concentrations. The present study quantified only two serum cytokines,
MCP-1 and IL-6, selected as representative markers of monocyte chemoattraction
and the acute-phase inflammatory response, respectively. Additional
mediators relevant to vascular inflammation and endothelial activation,
including TNF-α, IL-1β, ICAM-1, VCAM-1, and C-reactive
protein (CRP), were not measured and represent important extensions
for future work.

The in vitro release study defined pH-dependent
release behavior
under controlled enzyme-free conditions, which was useful for isolating
the influence of environmental pH on Ole release from CS-Ole-Lip.
Further biorelevant testing can expand this release characterization
by simulating gastrointestinal transit. A sequential model using simulated
gastric fluid with pepsin at pH 1.2 for 2 h, followed by simulated
intestinal fluid with pancreatin at pH 6.8 for 6 h, and subsequent
incubation in PBS at pH 7.4 would determine whether the chitosan coating
maintains its release-modulating function under digestive conditions,
including enzyme-mediated effects on the liposomal bilayer. Formal
saturation-solubility determinations of Ole in each release medium
were not performed in this study; future work should confirm sink
conditions experimentally by measuring Ole solubility in SGF, SIF,
and pH 7.4 buffer containing 0.1% Tween 80. Future TEM characterization
should also include desalting or buffer exchange before negative staining
to avoid PBS salt-crystallization artifacts and provide clearer morphological
confirmation.

Overall, the present findings position CS-Ole-Lip
as an optimized
oral liposomal formulation with sustained release behavior, endothelial
cellular association, and acute systemic anti-inflammatory activity.
The next phase of investigation should focus on pharmacokinetic profiling,
quantitative uptake analysis, molecular pathway confirmation, dose–response
modeling, and chronic atherosclerosis validation to define the formulation’s
therapeutic potential with greater mechanistic and translational precision.

Conclusions

This study developed and optimized
a CS-Ole-Lip system for oral
anti-inflammatory delivery. The resulting uniform lipid–polymer
nanocarrier, obtained using Box-Behnken design, yielded a well-characterized
nanoscale formulation with high Ole EE and LC. The formulation further
showed Fickian diffusion-governed sustained release behavior, refrigerated
colloidal stability, and favorable in vitro cytocompatibility and
in vivo safety profiles under the conditions evaluated.

In the
TNF-α-induced murine vascular inflammation model,
CS-Ole-Lip produced numerically greater serum IL-6 and MCP-1 suppression
than free Ole and uncoated Ole-Lip at equivalent doses. Notably, CS-Ole-Lip
at 2 mg/kg, one-tenth of the 20 mg/kg dose evaluated in this study,
retained substantial cytokine suppression, suggesting a potential
dose-sparing effect. These findings support the pharmacodynamic activity
of the optimized CS-Ole-Lip system and support its potential as a
stable oral nanocarrier with enhanced anti-inflammatory activity.
Further plasma pharmacokinetic studies are required to determine whether
this response is associated with improved oral Ole exposure, followed
by expanded dose–response profiling, sequential biorelevant
release testing, and validation in chronic atherosclerosis models.
