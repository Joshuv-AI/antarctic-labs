 Executive Summary of CRADA Work:

 This project will conduct biomass deconstruction (pretreatment and enzymatic saccharification)
 on two representative biomass feedstocks and four different pretreatment processes, including
 three high temperature steam/chemical pretreatments and one low temperature
 chemical/mechanical pretreatment. A third biomass feedstock will undergo biomass
 deconstruction with three different pretreatment processes, including two high temperature
 chemical pretreatments and one low temperature chemical/mechanical pretreatment. Several
 pretreatment conditions will be performed in a screening study using NLR pilot-scale
 pretreatment equipment to generate a range of pretreated biomass substrates. A selected number
 of these substrates will be chosen for enzymatic saccharification evaluation, based on standard
 compositional analysis of the pretreated substrates as a primary indicator of pretreatment
 efficacy. Resulting enzymatic hydrolysis slurries will be analyzed to determine overall biomass
 sugar yields. Additional compositional analysis will be performed to determine oligomeric sugar

 1

 composition and structure, to analyze structural characteristics of solids fractions on native,
 pretreated, and enzymatically saccharified biomass residues for one of the biomass feedstocks,
 corn stover. The enzymatically saccharified materials will undergo 2,3-butanediol fermentation
 in a shaker flask as bench scale. Using relevant process performance data collected in these
 various conversion steps, technoeconomic analysis activities will be performed to compare the
 economic potential of the various biomass feedstock and pretreatment processes and to identify
 key economic drivers and sustainability metrics.

 TASK DESCRIPTIONS:

 Purpose: This project will generate a comprehensive set of process data and underlying
 knowledge for conversion of several wide-ranging biomass types across a variety of
 deconstruction processes and conditions to generate biomass sugar hydrolysates. These
 hydrolysates are expected to have widely different properties regarding monomeric sugar
 concentrations, oligomeric sugar concentrations and structural properties, and other compounds
 derived from the feedstock and pretreatment conditions that could significantly impact the ability
 of conversion microorganisms to grow and produced targeted intermediates from these
 hydrolysates. One crucial aspect of this project is to generate knowledge about the structure-
 properties relationships across the various biomasses, pretreatment options and sugar
 compositions. In the long term, such knowledge might be extremely useful for combining the
 right bioconversion to the appropriate feedstock composition or blend different feeds to
 accommodate for some of the bioconversion limitations, similarly to what is currently done in
 the oil and gas industry in the application of catalysts for different crude compositions. The
 resulting process performance data will be utilized in technoeconomic and life-cycle assessment
 models to identify overall commercialization potential, with accompanying sensitivity analysis to
 identify the most important cost drivers for improving overall process performance and
 economics and reduce emissions. The outcomes of this project will be of significant interest to

 2

 the broader biomass conversion community, so the dissemination of key findings will occur via
 presentations at leading scientific conferences and publications in high-impact scientific journals.

 Task 1: Hydrolysate Generation (steam, dilute acid, deacetylation-dilute acid
 pretreatments)

 Objective: Task 1 will generate multiple sets of hydrolysate streams from three different
 feedstocks: corn stover, a representative woody biomass such as poplar, and a representative
 energy crop feedstock such as switchgrass. Feedstocks will be sourced through Idaho National
 Laboratory (INL) and will be appropriately pre-processed at INL to achieve a pre-determined
 specification for each feedstock. Pre-processed feedstock will be shipped to NLR in supersacks.
 For corn stover and switchgrass, three different high temperature pretreatments will be
 performed:

 • Steam pretreatment
 • Dilute acid pretreatment
 • Deacetylation-dilute acid pretreatment

 For poplar, two different high temperature pretreatments will be performed:

 • Dilute acid pretreatment

 • Deacetylation-dilute acid pretreatment

 3

 The high temperature pretreatments will be conducted using NLR’s pilot scale continuous
 pretreatment reactor(s). For each feedstock and high temperature pretreatment method, up to 12
 specific pretreatment conditions (combinations of temperature, residence time, and pretreatment
 chemical loading (as appropriate)) will be conducted by allowing each pretreatment condition to
 achieve a steady-state operating condition followed by collecting a representative pretreated
 slurry sample over a 30 minute period. The following steps for each feedstock and high
 temperature pretreatment method will be conducted:

 1. Dilute alkali deacetylation (only for deacetylation-dilute acid pretreatment)
 2. Water/chemical pre-impregnation
 3. Pilot scale pretreatment (up to 12 conditions)
 4. Standard compositional analysis and yield calculations (liquid and solids fractions)

 Up to six pretreated slurries from each feedstock and high temperature pretreatment method will
 be selected for enzymatic saccharification, which will be conducted using a single commercial
 enzyme (cellulase/hemicellulase) preparation at a single enzyme loading. The selection of the
 pretreated slurries to be used in enzymatic saccharification will be based upon an assessment of
 pretreatment performance, including the yield of oligomeric and monomeric sugars in the liquid
 fraction of the pretreated slurry, the amount of sugar degradation products (primarily furfural and
 5-HMF) in the liquid fraction of the pretreated slurry, and the overall carbohydrate component
 mass closure in the pretreated slurry solid and liquid fractions. The enzymatic saccharification
 will be conducted in laboratory-scale roller bottle-type reactors at a solids loading of 20% total
 solids. At the endpoint of enzymatic saccharification, the saccharified slurries will be harvested
 and samples will undergo standard compositional analysis and sugar yield calculations.

 The harvested saccharified slurry samples will be measuring filtration flux rates and washing
 efficiencies. The liquid fractions will be retained and stored in a frozen state for biological
 characterization and fermentation process studies (Task 4).

 Task 1 Results:

 1.1.1 Corn Stover Pretreatment

 Pretreatment of corn stover was completed using steam, dilute acid (DA), and deacetylated dilute
 acid (DDA). There were significant delays during pretreatment of corn stover. The first delay
 was NLR closing for COVID-19 and even when it reopened, labor within the IBRF Pilot Plant
 was limited for a few months. The second delay was feeding the deacetylated acid impregnated
 corn stover into the Large Horizontal Reactor (LHR). The deacetylated acid impregnated corn
 stover did not form a strong enough plug on LHR’s screw plug feeder which resulted in multiple
 blow backs from the steam inside the horizontal reactor. The deacetylated acid impregnated corn
 stover was switched to the South Vertical Reactor (SVR) where the horizontal section was
 utilized as a horizontal reactor, see Figure 1. Due to these delays, the corn stover steam/DA/DDA
 pretreatments were not completed until 2.5 years into the project.

 1.2.1 Poplar Pretreatment

 The only poplar pretreatment performed was DA Low Acid. The poplar pretreatment was
 scheduled to run immediately following the corn stover pretreatment campaigns. Unfortunately,
 the corn stover was not completed until 2.5 years into the project. On the second campaign for
 poplar, DA High Acid, the poplar plug failed and blow back occurred that was severe enough to

 pretreatment compared to corn stover and switchgrass, limited time and funding, the remaining
 DA/DDA pretreatment poplar campaigns were abandoned.

 Task 2: Hydrolysate Generation (deacetylation-mechanical refining (DMR) pretreatment)

 Objective: Task 2 will generate hydrolysate streams from the same three feedstocks utilized in
 Task 1. In Task 2, a low temperature pretreatment process involving dilute alkali deacetylation
 followed by a mechanical refining operation will be used. The mechanical refining step will be
 performed in a 12-inch pilot-scale disk refiner.

 For each feedstock, up to 12 specific pretreatment conditions (combination of deacetylation
 conditions, such as residence time, alkali concentration, and mechanical refining conditions) will
 be conducted to collect representative pretreated samples. Each pretreatment sample will be
 enzymatically saccharified using a single commercial enzyme (cellulase/hemicellulase)
 preparation at a single enzyme loading. The enzymatic saccharification will be conducted in
 laboratory-scale roller bottle-type reactors at a solids loading of 20% total solids. At the endpoint
 of enzymatic saccharification, the saccharified slurries will be harvested and samples will
 undergo standard compositional analysis and sugar yield calculations. The predictor of
 pretreatment efficacy used in the Task 1 high temperature pretreatment processes (solubilization
 profile of carbohydrate components and associated component mass closure of carbohydrate
 components in pretreated slurry solid and liquid fractions) cannot be used for the deacetylation-
 mechanical refining pretreatment due to limited carbohydrate solubilization occurring in that
 pretreatment process. Therefore, the enzymatic saccharification results from the Task 2
 pretreated substrates will be utilized along with more detailed compositional activities in Task 3
 (including imaging and structural analysis) to develop a predictive function of saccharification
 performance.

 11

 The harvested saccharified slurry samples’ lignin-rich residual solids will be separated from the
 sugar-rich liquid fraction using laboratory-scale filtration devices. Filtration flux rates and
 washing efficiencies will be measured. The liquid fractions will be retained and stored in a
 frozen state for biological characterization and fermentation process studies (Task 4).

 Task 2 Results:

 2.1 Deacetylation & Disc Refining

 Figure 8 displays the soluble lignin and acetic acid yields during deacetylation and disc refining
 for the DMR process. Deacetylation was performed using 5kg of dry biomass in a 90L paddle
 reactor. The concentration of NaOH, residence time was varied during deacetylation. The lignin
 rich black liquor was separated from the deacetylated slurry and the remaining solids were disc
 refined in a 12 inch disc refiner. The space between discs was varied during disc refining. The
 nomenclature of the samples are as follows: CS/Pop/SWG are the feedstocks corn
 stover/poplar/switchgrass, 60/80/100 is the concentration of NaOH in g per kg of dry biomass,
 120/240 is the residence time during deacetylation in min, and 0.001/0.005/0.015 is the gap
 space during disc refining in in. The poplar had to have smaller gap space during disc refining
 because the 0.015 in gap was too large and the poplar fell through the disc. Deacetylation is
 meant to remove inhibitory acetic acid and lignin while not producing furfural and
 Hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF) during pretreatment. Figure 8 illustrates that all of the acetic acid
 was removed from the biomass solids during deacetylation. The acetic acid yields are larger than
 100% because the compositional analysis of the raw biomass underestimates acetyl functional
 groups. The corn stover lignin yields are over 100%, indicating that all of the lignin was
 removed. However, at this time, the current methods for measuring lignin in black liquor are
 inadequate and overestimate the concentrations and thus overestimate the yields. The removal of
 lignin from corn stover during deacetylation was much more effective than that of poplar and
 switchgrass. Higher severity deacetylation removed all the lignin from corn stover, according to
 this data, which it peaked at 55% and 60% respectively for poplar and switchgrass.

 Fig. 8. Soluble lignin and acetic acid yields during DMR pretreatment

 2.2 Enzymatic Hydrolysis

 Because DMR sugar yields can only be determined after enzymatic hydrolysis, all of the DMR
 slurries underwent enzymatic hydrolysis. The results of the enzymatic hydrolyses are displayed
 in Figure 9. Corn stover was clearly the best performing feedstock from DMR enzymatic
 hydrolysis. Once the NaOH concentration reached 80 g/kg the xylose yield was near or above
 90% and the glucose yields near or above 70%. The poplar xylose yield peaked at 60% and the
 switchgrass peaked at 80%. The poor enzymatic hydrolysis performance for poplar in DMR was
 another reason the DA/DDA pretreatment campaigns for poplar were abandoned.

 Task 3: Detailed Compositional and Imaging Analysis

 Objective: While NLR standard Laboratory Analytical Procedures will be performed on samples
 generated in Tasks 1 and 2, additional detailed analysis methods will also be performed on
 selected samples generated in Tasks 1 and 2.

 Liquid-phase sample analysis performed under Task 3 will involve detailed oligomeric sugar
 speciation and structure analysis, including degree of polymerization and branching pattern of
 high temperature pretreated corn stover. This information will help identify the differences in
 oligomeric sugar from corn stover in different pretreatment processes and conditions and can
 identify the types of bond cleavage and associated enzymatic activities necessary to produce
 monomeric sugars from these oligomer structures.

 14

 Task 3 Results:

 Oligomeric sugar speciation was determined using an Liquid Chromatography Mass
 Spectrometry (LCMS) with a zinc column and an Evaporative Light Scattering Detection
 (ELSD). Due to branching of the polymers and unknown response factors of those branches,
 quantification of the polymers was not possible. This was an exercise to determine how large the
 polymer chains of pentose and hexose were in the liquid fraction of the corn stover pretreated
 slurries. The results are displayed in Figure 10. Figure 10 has steam pretreatment on the left, DA
 pretreatment in the middle, and DDA pretreatment on the right. For DA and DDA, the low acid
 pretreatment (0.5% sulfuric acid versus dry biomass) is on top and the bottom is high acid
 pretreatment (0.8% sulfuric acid vs dry biomass). All charts start with lowest severity
 (temperature and residence time) on the left and increase in severity to the right. CS Steam
 170/20 was an underperforming pretreatment, which is displayed with pentose chains with 12
 degrees of polymerization. There was also only monomeric hexose present in all the steam
 pretreated corn stover samples. Most of the liquors’ longest pentose chains ranged from 8 to 11
 degrees of polymerization. The DA pretreated corn stover hexose’s longest chains ranged from 5
 to 8 degrees of polymerization, while the DDA pretreated corn stover ranged from 3 to 5 degrees
 of polymerization for hexose.

 Fig. 10. Oligomeric ranges of pretreated corn stover

 Task 4: Fermentation of Enzymatic Hydrolysate (modified from Task 4: Lignin
 Characterization in Mod 4)

 Objective: The various pretreatment approaches used in this project will generate different types
 of sugar rich enzymatic hydrolysates with different monomeric sugar concentrations as
 inhibitory and degradation product concentrations such as acetic acid and furfural. To measure
 the digestibility of the various hydrolysates, each enzymatic hydrolysate will undergo laboratory
 scale batch fermentation using NLR’s 2,3-butanediol strain of Zymomonus mobilis. Each
 fermentation will be sampled throughout the fermentation process to provide data on production

 15

 rates, and total fermentation conversion. Work in Task 4 is structured to occur in sequence with
 the generation of samples from Task 1 and Task 2 activities.

 Task 4 Results:

 4.1 Steam/DA/DDA Pretreated Corn Stover

 The scope was modified from lignin analysis to fermentation because it was determined that
 understanding the fermentation conversion of the material after enzymatic hydrolysis provided
 more value than lignin analysis.

 Fermentations were performed in shake flasks and therefore were not pH controlled. Samples
 were collected at various points during the procedure. Fermentations were performed after
 enzymatic hydrolysis. Figure 11 displays the final process yields for fermenting steam/DA/DDA
 pretreated corn stover. This strain of Zymomonus mobilis (Z. mobilis) produces 2,3-butanediol
 (2,3-BDO) and acetoin. Both compounds can be upgraded and are thus both considered products.
 The maximum process yields for both 2,3-BDO and acetoin from both glucose and xylose is
 50%. The low temperature steam pretreated corn stover has the strongest fermentation, reaching
 44% yield. This is due to the lack of furfural present during fermentation. Overall, the DDA
 pretreated corn stover was most consistent and had less affected by pretreatment conditions, all
 over which had process yields greater than 35%.

 Fig. 11. Fermentation process yields of steam/DA/DDA pretreated corn stover

 4.2 DA/DDA Pretreated Poplar

 For the reason previously mentioned, the poplar pretreatment campaigns were dropped to

 4.3 DA/DDA Pretreated Switchgrass

 Figure 12 displays the fermentation of DA/DDA pretreated switchgrass. The DA pretreated
 switchgrass had very poor results, with a maximum process yield of 10%. This was most likely
 due to an excess amount of acetic acid present in the pretreated liquor. Both DDA pretreatments
 of switchgrass at 170 deg C also had poor fermentation performance. The pretreatments had
 degradation products like furfural present which appear to have inhibited the fermentation. The
 best performing fermentation was the poorest pretreatment of switchgrass, SWG DDA
 150/12/0.5 and SWG DDA160/20/0.5. While these pretreatment conditions had poor
 pretreatment and enzymatic hydrolyses results, the lack of inhibitors allowed for optimal
 fermentation conditions. Even these two samples did not perform as well as steam pretreated
 corn stover. In hindsight, it would have been optimal to pretreat switchgrass with steam to see
 how it would perform compared to corn stover.

 4.4 DMR Pretreated Biomass

 Figure 13 displays the fermentation results for all DMR pretreated feedstocks. Some of the
 poplar fermentation could not be run because the enzymatic hydrolysis did not produce enough

 Task 5: Process Engineering/Technoeconomic Analysis

 Task 5 Results:

 Technoeconomic analysis activities compare the economic potential of the various biomass
 feedstock and pretreatment processes and to identify key economic drivers and sustainability
 metrics. Material and energy balances for each feedstock and pretreatment process were
 constructed that utilize NLR’s existing Aspen Plus process simulation models. Appropriate
 financial parameter assumptions were developed in collaboration between NLR and EMRE to
 allow for reporting of modeling results in a mutually useful manner.

 Sensitivity analysis involving selected individual and collective process variables were
 conducted to identify the most significant factors that impact overall process operating and
 capital costs. This information will provide a basis for strategic evaluation of process potential
 based on technoeconomic and life-cycle considerations. Work in Task 5 was structured to occur
 in sequence with the generation of process conversion results and characterization from the other
 project tasks. Technoeconomic analysis (TEA) based on prior task results provided an
 understanding of the entire process and provide a direct comparison between feedstocks and
 other sugar sources that are commercially available.

 5.1 Steam/DA/DDA Pretreated Corn Stover

 NLR’s ASPEN model was utilized to provide a TEA report for all pretreated feedstocks that
 underwent enzymatic hydrolysis. Because the fermentations were performed in a shake flask, the
 TEA report was used to estimate cost and production cost of the cellulosic sugars in for each
 feedstock and conditions.

 For steam/DA/DDA pretreated corn stover, the TEA results were generally found to be most
 favorable for the DA pretreatment cases, followed by steam and then DDA pretreatment, largely
 reflecting similar trends in sugar yields. For a hypothetical commercial-scale biorefinery
 processing 2,000 dry metric tonne per day of biomass under nth-plant economic projections, the
 DA cases translated to modeled minimum sugar selling prices (MSSPs) ranging between 21 to
 28 cents/lb total sugars with one outlier at 32 cents/lb, while the steam pretreatment cases varied
 28 to 32 cents/lb and the DDA cases from 31 to 34 cents/lb. The DDA cases were somewhat
 further confounded by higher capital and operating expenses than the other pretreatment
 scenarios, tied to elevated capital costs for wastewater treatment attributed to generation of
 deacetylation black liquors as well as elevated operating costs for the use of deacetylation
 caustic. Results are displayed in Figure 14.

 19

 Fig. 14. Sugar TEA results for steam/DA/DDA pretreated corn stover

 5.2 DA/DDA Pretreated Poplar

 For the reason previously mentioned, the poplar pretreatment campaigns were dropped to
 prioritize more promising feedstocks and pretreatments.

 5.3 DA/DDA Pretreated Switchgrass

 The NLR TEA sugar models were exercised to incorporate experimental data generated across
 36 pretreatment cases for deconstruction of switchgrass biomass to hydrolysate sugars. After
 updating key financial/cost parameters, the assessment found that sugar costs for DA
 pretreatment are most favorable largely reflecting similar trends in relatively higher sugar yields.
 The models will also be provided to EMRE collaborators upon request. Sugar yield is the
 dominant key cost driver. For a hypothetical commercial-scale biorefinery processing 2,000 dry
 metric tonne per day of biomass under nth-plant economic projections, MSSP ranges from
 $0.21-$0.25/lb for DA pretreatment and ranges from $0.26-$0.34/lb for DDA pretreatment.
 Results are displayed in Figure 15.

 5.4 DMR Pretreated Biomass

 The NLR TEA sugar models were exercised to incorporate experimental data generated across
 36 pretreatment cases for deconstruction of corn stover, poplar and switchgrass biomass to
 hydrolysate sugars. After updating key financial/cost parameters, the assessment found that sugar
 costs for corn stover and switchgrass using DDR pretreatment are most favorable largely
 reflecting similar trends in sugar yields. Sugar cost for poplar is less favorable because sugar
 yields were found generally lower using poplar. For a hypothetical commercial-scale biorefinery
 processing 2,000 dry metric tonne per day of biomass under nth-plant economic projections, the
 corn stover cases translated to modeled minimum sugar selling prices (MSSPs) ranging between
 29 to 49 cents/lb total sugars, while the switchgrass cases resulted in MSSPs ranges from 33 to
 53 cents/lb total sugars. However, the poplar cases translated to modeled minimum sugar selling
 prices (MSSPs) unfavorably ranging between 66 to 1.52 cents/lb total sugars, mainly due to
 lower sugar yields. The MSSP trends largely were a function of sugar yields. Results are
 displayed in Figure 16.
