 ABSTRACT

 With the United States Department of Energy (DOE)’s goal of quadrupling the nation’s nuclear
energy supply by 2050 [1], and with the Advanced Fuels Campaign [2] pushing for new types of advanced
reactor fuels and geometries, the need has arisen for new nuclear fuel designs. One such design is to swap
out current nuclear fuel geometries in exchange for another type of geometry, called a Triply Periodic
Minimal Surface (TPMS). TPMSs are self-supporting, infinitely repeating lattices—attributes that lend
themselves well to additive manufacturing. These surfaces also possess enhanced heat transfer properties
thanks to their internal area changes and large surface-area-to-volume ratios. Their drawback, however, is
an increased pressure drop. Given the small amount of correlations and data (Reynolds numbers in the
2,000–8,000 range), and the minimal amount of experience so far obtained by modeling TPMS structures
using 1D systems codes such as the Reactor Excursion and Leak Analysis Program (RELAP5-3D), further
research into this topic was needed. Using data from the University of Wisconsin - Madison (UW),
curve fits were created for both a Heat Transfer Coefficient (HTC) correlation and a Darcy friction factor
empirical coefficient correlation. The curves’ coefficients and multipliers were then output and utilized in
RELAP5-3D models of two upcoming experiments—Flow Loop for INFLUX Pressure drop (FLIP) and
Microreactor Agile Non-nuclear Experimental Test (MAGNET)—aimed at increasing the available data
for Reynolds numbers to the 16,000–36,000 range for TPMS structures. The models were run under the
conditions utilized by a Computational Fluid Dynamics (CFD) analysis performed by another group at
Idaho National Laboratory. Only CFD pressure drop values were obtained from the FLIP test, and those
values showed that the RELAP5-3D models had a lower rate of pressure increase in comparison to the
CFD values. In addition, there seemed to be a vertical shift upward in the pressure drop for both models
whenever the TPMS porosity decreased, and the RELAP5-3D models showed a higher vertical shift in
comparison to the CFD values. The MAGNET results did not correspond to any CFD or experimental
results against which they could be compared, so they were instead compared against the proposed CFD
input conditions. These values were then compared with each other to make sure the model seemed to be
performing as expected, paving the way for future tests that can be run for the purpose of further analyses
and comparisons. The pressure drop increased with temperature and mass flow rate independently. The
temperature change would decrease with increasing mass flow rate and temperature, which was just as we
expected based on the fact that the lower viscosity and decreased density would result in higher friction
and churning losses. The last metric that was assessed was the enthalpy flow change, which increased
with increasing mass flow rate and decreasing temperature.

 1. INTRODUCTION

 The United States Department of Energy (DOE) is working to achieve its goal of quadrupling the nation’s
current nuclear power output by 2050 [1]. In tandem with this need for expanded energy capacities, the
Advanced Fuels Campaign is underway to help drive innovation in advanced nuclear reactors [2]. New
reactors and fuels are crucial for achieving DOE’s overarching goal by increasing reactor deployment rates,
power densities, etc. As specifically regards the fuels, certain design elements can also be changed, such as the
fuel material and geometry. In current nuclear reactors, most of the fuel geometries are cylindrical or spherical
in nature. However, the rise of additive manufacturing has opened up the possibility for more complex
geometries to be manufactured and used as nuclear fuel, prompting new investigations into such geometries.
One candidate is the Triply Periodic Minimal Surface (TPMS), which features a large surface-area-to-volume
ratio and a constantly changing mass flow area and is an infinitely repeating, self-supporting structure. These
properties open up the possibility for smaller and more power-dense reactors. TPMSs are defined by different
versions of level-set equations and can be transformed by changing the pitch of the structure, in turn affecting
the porosity and angle of the lattice (see Figures 1 and 2).

Figure 1. Cylindrical diamond TPMS structure. Figure 2. Diamond lattice TPMS structure.

 This allows for TPMS structures to be additively manufactured. Advancements in additive manufacturing
have enabled simultaneous printing of multiple types of materials. As a result, a fuel and a cladding can be
made at the same time, using different materials. This then allows for the fuel to be a single connected lattice,
as shown in Figure 3.
 TPMS structures are also useful in heat transfer applications because of the high surface area involved,

 1

along with the changing mass flow area. These characteristics lend themselves to a high heat transfer rate,
broken thermal boundary layers, and fluid turbulence, as visualized in Figure 4. Thanks to the combination of
all those factors, TPMS geometries have a high Heat Transfer Coefficient (HTC) as compared to a similar
volume of material for a different geometry (e.g., pipes, rod bundles, and spheres). But there are drawbacks to
TPMS geometries as well—particularly the high pressure drop values, which would require more or larger
pumps or blowers to push the fluids through the geometry. This tradeoff could reduce the gains afforded by
the enhanced heat transfer in terms of both economics and performance.

Figure 3. TPMS fuel design. Figure 4. CFD of diamond TPMS.

 As these geometries have only recently begun being tested for heat transfer and fluid applications, few
correlations and datasets pertaining to them exist, especially when it comes to volumetrically heated TPMS
structures. Having these is important for properly modeling and predicting the pressure drop and HTC values
in potential new nuclear reactor designs. For the present work, we used the Reactor Excursion and Leak
Analysis Program (RELAP5-3D) systems code in trying to model a diamond TPMS geometry’s pressure drop
and heat transfer characteristics. We also explored the error between the experimental, Computational Fluid
Dynamics (CFD) simulation, and RELAP5-3D simulation results.

 2. METHODOLOGY

 In beginning the present work, datasets from [3] at the University of Wisconsin - Madison (UW) were
used, because those experiments were focused on the possibility of using TPMS geometries for nuclear
fuel applications by volumetrically heating the TPMS. These data came from thermocouples, with air being
employed as the working fluid and the focus being on 50 and 75 mm diamond lattices. These datasets, both
separately and combined, were curve-fit using Python. The curve fits were used to determine coefficients for
the Darcy friction factor, given in Equation (1), and correlation multipliers for HTCs from Dittus-Boelter,
shown in Equation (2), so that these could then be inputted to the RELAP5-3D models.

 These three Prandtl number values were used to assess the outputs’ sensitivity to slight variations in
Prandtl number. After obtaining all these equations and curve fitting them for coefficients and multipliers, the
values were put into the two RELAP5-3D models for use in the Flow Loop for INFLUX Pressure drop (FLIP)

uses water as its working fluid and MAGNET uses helium and air.
 The FLIP model, shown in Figure 6, was nodalized as a time-dependent volume, with a time-dependent
junction connected to a single volume for the top pressure sensor. A junction connecting the single volume to
a circular pipe section with a square end at the bottom was used, and thus this created a junction to the test
section itself. The test section was a 70 mm square nodalized into three separate sections of a pipe. A final
junction led to the last single volume for the bottom pressure sensor location, and finally went into the output
time-dependent volume. This is visually represented in Figures 5 and 6.
 The MAGNET test, shown in Figure 8, was nodalized as two separate systems: one that used air and one
that used helium, as shown in Figure 7. A heat structure was used for the heat exchanger. The TPMS was
modeled as a slab geometry. Inclusion of a surface area multiplier in modeling this geometry was necessary
to allow the model to run while also capturing the differences in area effects. There were two time-dependent
volumes, each with time-dependent junctions to the input volume. The nodalization was then broken up into
two columns of five pipes each so that different sections of the TPMS could be compared against each other.
A visual representation is given below in Figures 7 and 8.

 Figures 16–18 present the results obtained from the MAGNET simulations, based on a ≈ 6.9 mm wall

 4. CONCLUSIONS/SUMMARY

 The FLIP results show that RELAP5-3D has a lower rate of pressure drop increase compared to CFD. This
most likely stems from the coefficients for the pressure drop being incorrect due to significant extrapolation
of the curve fit from the Reynolds numbers tested at UW. With decreasing porosity, both the CFD and
RELAP5-3D shift upward for pressure drop. However the RELAP5-3D values shift up higher than the CFD
values, as is seen when comparing Figures 12–15. Also, the internal expansion and contraction effects of
the fluids are most likely being improperly captured by the forward and reverse loss coefficients within the
test section. With future experimental data from FLIP that will extend the Reynolds number range, we are
expecting greater agreement between RELAP5-3D and CFD once the new coefficients are used within the
models. The MAGNET values, despite us having nothing to compare them against, still serve as a launchpad
for quickly moving forward with data from CFD or experiments that will foster future comparisons similar
to what has been shown with FLIP. There is also interest in using other HTC correlations contained in
RELAP5-3D so as to compare their performance next to the Dittus-Boelter equation. Much work remains
to be done to flesh out our understanding of TPMS geometries, their effects, and the most effective way to
model them, but this work gets us one step closer to achieving those goals.
