Late phase transfusion after CAR T therapy is associated with persistent hematotoxicity and non-relapse mortality in multiple myeloma: a post hoc analysis
Authors: Fan Xia, Bin Lv, Xinyi Li, Mingxi Tian, Xuejiao Gu, Dian Zhou, Qian Sun, Jieyun Xia, Yuekun Qi, Huanxin Zhang, Kunming Qi, Yang Liu, Ying Wang, Wei Chen, Jiang Cao, Zhiling Yan, Ming Shi, Depeng Li, Feng Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundChimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T cell) therapy is highly effective for relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (R/R MM), but hematologic toxicity and the need for blood transfusions remain common. This study examined the patterns of transfusion needs in patients receiving CAR-T cell therapy, analyzed risk factors, and assessed the effects of transfusions on clinical outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective study included 155 R/R MM patients treated with CAR-T cells targeting different antigens. We used multivariate regression and survival analysis to identify risk factors for early (≤ 30 days) and late (> 30 days) transfusion requirements and analyzed their prognostic impact.ResultsOf the 155 patients, 38.7% required transfusion in the early phase (≤ 30 days) and 35.5% in the late phase (> 30 days). Early transfusion was associated with acute inflammation and baseline bone marrow reserve but had limited impact on survival. In contrast, late transfusion, particularly red blood cell (RBC) transfusion, was associated with poor outcomes, including significantly reduced overall survival (median OS: 10.87 vs. 37.87 months; P < 0.0001) and progression-free survival (median PFS: 4.93 vs. 17.17 months; P < 0.0001). Late transfusion also correlated with a shallower depth of response (P < 0.001) and higher non-relapse mortality (P = 0.026).ConclusionsTransfusion requirement after CAR-T cell therapy is a key prognostic factor in MM. The need for early transfusion indicates high inflammation and severe acute toxicity, whereas the need for late transfusion is associated with poor remission, higher non-relapse mortality, and shorter survival. Reducing the need for transfusions could be important for lowering patient burden and improving outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12916-026-04988-5.

Background

Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T cell) therapy has shown revolutionary efficacy in relapsed/refractory multiple myeloma (R/R MM) [1–3]. However, its safety can be compromised by unique adverse effects, including cytokine release syndrome (CRS), immune effector cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS), and hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH)-like syndrome [4–6]. Real-world data show that hematologic toxicities (HTs) are the most common grade 3 or higher adverse events in patients receiving CAR-T cell therapy. In studies of B-cell mature antigen (BCMA) CAR-T cell therapy, up to 95% of patients experienced prolonged cytopenia (low blood cell counts), which persisted for at least 30 days in more than half of these patients [7, 8]. Such effects lead to prolonged hospital stays, elevated rates of infection, and increased non-relapse mortality (NRM) [9].

Comparing HT rates across trials is difficult due to differences in disease types and cell targets for CAR T-cell therapy. To address this, the European Hematology Association (EHA)/EBMT group proposed the concept of immune effector cell-associated hematotoxicity (ICAHT), categorized as early and late phases [10]. A study applying a neutrophil-based scoring system (N-ICAHT) found that approximately 15% of MM patients develop severe early N-ICAHT, which is linked to prolonged neutropenia, severe thrombocytopenia, and anemia [11]. Recently, Rejeski et al. [12] introduced the thrombocytopenia-based T-ICAHT grading system. Studies of both systems confirmed that patients with higher grades of ICAHT experience a higher burden of platelet and red blood cell (RBC) transfusions and shorter survival.

Clinical management of ICAHT is challenging. Our center has shown that treatment with granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) could reduce the risk of neutropenia after CAR-T cell therapy [13]. In addition to G-CSF, blood transfusion is the main supportive treatment. However, true transfusion needs depend on the primary disease and the CAR-T cell type. Statistics show that 23.1%–66% of patients need RBC or platelet transfusions after CAR-T cell therapy [14–16]. Such requirements are indicative of a patient’s bone marrow reserve. Long-term transfusion can cause iron overload as well as circulatory overload, reducing quality of life. Also, transfusion-related immunomodulation (TRIM) can affect the efficacy of CAR-T cell therapy [17]. Early studies in patients with solid tumors showed that allogeneic blood transfusion increases mortality and tumor recurrence [18–20]. Recent research also suggests that transfusions may reduce response rates to immunotherapy [21]. In large B-cell lymphoma, both early transfusion and late platelet transfusion were found to be associated with shorter progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS), as well as higher mortality [22]. Although high rates of cytopenia were previously attributed to bone marrow infiltration and the effects of multiple prior lines of therapy, later findings suggest that disease-specific differences are more important [23].

Currently, research on the transfusion needs of R/R MM patients after CAR-T cell therapy remains limited. Because understanding these needs and their impact on survival is crucial for optimizing treatment, the present study examined the patterns of transfusion needs among R/R MM patients who received CAR-T cell therapy, analyzed risk factors for transfusion need, and evaluated the effects of transfusion events on clinical outcomes.

Methods

Patients and data collection

We conducted a retrospective study on patients with R/R MM who received CAR-T cell therapy at the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University between May 2018 and December 2023 (clinical trial numbers: ChiCTR1900026219, ChiCTR2000033194, ChiCTR2100048888). We have previously described the methods applied for lymphodepletion chemotherapy and CAR-T cell preparation [1, 2].

We collected baseline data, including age, sex, MM type, International Staging System (ISS) stage, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG PS), tumor burden, cytogenetics, extramedullary disease (EMD), and prior treatments. Laboratory data were retrieved from electronic medical records and included blood counts, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) concentration, and β2-microglobulin level before lymphodepletion. We also tracked absolute neutrophil counts (ANCs), hemoglobin (Hb) levels, and platelet counts from Day 0 to Day 30, and peak concentrations of inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin (IL)-2, IL-6, and IL-10; tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α); and interferon-α (IFN-α) and IFN-γ within the first month after infusion.

We recorded the number, frequency, timing, and type of all blood product transfusions from 6 months before to 6 months after CAR-T cell infusion (D-6 M to D6M). Transfusion of packed RBCs (PRBCs) or single-donor platelets (SDPs) was counted as one event. Administration of other products such as cryoprecipitate or small volumes of fresh frozen plasma was not counted. Blood products were prepared by fresh apheresis, leukoreduction, and irradiation. Transfusion decisions were based on clinical judgment and standard guidelines.

The patient inclusion criteria were adapted from Vic et al. [22] to include R/R MM patients treated with at least one CAR-T cell infusion (targets: G protein-coupled receptor class C group 5 member D [GPRC5D], BCMA, CD19) at our center, with at least 6 months of follow-up and detailed transfusion data. The early transfusion phase was defined as Day 0 to Month 1 (1 M), and the late phase was defined as Month 1 to Month 6 (1 M-6 M).

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Hospital of Xuzhou Medical University (approval number XYFY2021-KL154-01). Written informed consent was obtained from each patient before study entry in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Hematologic toxicity

CRS and ICANS were diagnosed according to official American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy (ASTCT) criteria [24]. Neutropenia was evaluated based on the 2023 EHA/EBMT consensus guidelines for ICAHT [10]. According to the guidelines, early N-ICAHT is characterized by neutropenia (ANC ≤ 500/µL) from the day of CAR-T cell infusion (D0) to D28, whereas late N-ICAHT is defined as neutropenia beyond D28. Severe N-ICAHT involves persistence of ANC ≤ 500/µL for at least 14 days or ANC ≤ 100/µL for at least 7 days during the first 28 days after CAR-T cells infusion (early N-ICAHT), or ANC ≤ 500/µL at two or more time points or nontransient neutropenia after D28 (late N-ICAHT). Early T-ICAHT was graded based on the depth and duration of thrombocytopenia in the first 30 days. Late T-ICAHT represents the thrombocytopenia severity between Day 31 and Day 100 [12].

Severe infection was defined as an infectious event requiring hospitalization, intravenous antimicrobial therapy, ICU admission, or resulting in infection-related death.

Clinical outcomes

Treatment response in the included R/R MM patients was assessed using the International Myeloma Working Group (IMWG) criteria. PFS was defined as the time from the date of CAR-T cell infusion to disease progression or all-cause death. OS was defined as the time from the date of CAR-T cell infusion to death due to any cause.

Statistical analysis

Intergroup differences in continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test, and percentage differences were identified using Fisher’s exact test. For univariate analysis, P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and factors with significant P values were retained in the multivariate analysis. P-values for the overall model significance and for non-linearity were derived from the likelihood ratio test. A linear Cox model was employed when RCS modeling was inapplicable for the threshold analysis, and optimal cutoffs were determined by the maximally selected rank statistic method. PFS and OS survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. The following software programs were used for statistical analysis and data visualization: R Resources (version 4.4.2).

Results

Basic and clinical characteristics of R/R MM patients

This retrospective study included 155 patients with R/R MM who received CAR-T cell therapy during the study period of May 2018 to December 2023 according to the inclusion criteria. The types of CAR-T cell therapy administered to the study population included BCMA CAR-T cell therapy for 38 patients, GPRC5D CAR-T cell therapy for 38 patients, BCMA+CD19 CAR-T cell therapy for 33 patients, bispecific BCMA/CD19 CAR-T cell therapy for 31 patients, and bispecific BCMA/GPRC5D CAR-T cell therapy for 15 patients. The patient characteristics are presented in Table 1 (and Additional file 1: Tables S1A-S1F). The study design flow is depicted in Fig. 1. At the time of CAR-T cell infusion, the median patient age was 58 years (range, 53–63 years), and the median number of prior lines of therapy was 4 (range, 3–6). Of the 155 included patients, 23 (14.1%) had a ECOG PS > 0, 67 (43.2%) had extramedullary disease, 62 (40.0%) had high-risk genetic factors, 42 (27.1%) had prior hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, and 32 (20.6%) had received bridging therapy. Thirty-nine patients (25.2%) experienced infections within 3 months prior to lymphodepletion.

Transfusion requirements among patients receiving CAR-T cell therapy

We recorded all transfusion events that occurred during the month preceding CAR-T cell reinfusion, during the early transfusion phase following CAR-T cell reinfusion, and in the late transfusion phase. Within 1 month prior to CAR-T cell infusion, 34 (21.9%) patients received transfusions, which included RBC transfusions for 30 (19.4%) patients and platelet transfusions for 17 (11.0%) patients. Among these 34 patients, 13 (8.4%) required both RBC and platelet transfusions (Fig. 2A).

During the 6-month follow-up period after CAR-T cell infusion, transfusion requirements persisted (Fig. 2A). A total of 74 (47.7%) patients received transfusions, which included RBC transfusions for 59 (38.1%) patients and platelet transfusions for 58 (37.4%) patients. Among the 74 patients, 30 (40.5%) needed both RBC and platelet transfusions.

Transfusion requirements were concentrated in the first month post-reinfusion (i.e., the early transfusion phase), during which 60 (38.7%) patients required transfusions [RBCs for 46 (29.7%) patients and platelets for 45 (29.0%) patients]. The mean RBC transfusion volume during this phase was 1.8 units (range, 0–30 units), and the mean platelet transfusion volume was 1.3 units (range, 0–5 units). Thereafter, transfusion requirements showed a declining trend (Fig. 2B and C). During the 1- to 6-month post-transfusion period (i.e., the late transfusion phase), 55 (35.5%) patients received transfusions, with 36 (23.2%) receiving RBC transfusions and 47 (30.3%) receiving platelet transfusions. Although the average transfusion volume did not decrease further between 5 and 6 months post-CAR-T cell infusion, the proportion of patients requiring transfusions did drop to 9.7%, with only 15 patients needing at least one transfusion in this period (Fig. 2A).

Early ICAHT grading and transfusion requirements

In our patient cohort, transfusion requirements increased with higher early N-ICAHT grades. Among patients with Grade 0 N-ICAHT (n = 83), 69.9% required no transfusion, whereas 10.8% received RBC transfusion alone, 7.2% received platelet transfusion alone, and 12.0% required both RBC and platelet transfusion (Fig. 2D and Additional file 1: Fig. S2C). Patients with Grade 1 N-ICAHT (n = 36) showed greater transfusion needs: 55.6% required no transfusion, while 2.8% received RBC alone, 13.9% received platelets alone, and 27.8% required both products (Fig. 2D and Additional file 1: Fig. S2D). In Grade 2 N-ICAHT (n = 18), transfusion was required in 77.8% of patients, including 11.1% with RBC alone, 22.2% with platelets alone, and 44.4% with both RBC and platelets, whereas 22.2% required no transfusion (Fig. 2D and Additional file 1: Fig. S2E). Notably, among patients with severe early N-ICAHT (Grade 3–4, n = 18), 88.9% required combined RBC and platelet transfusion and 11.1% received RBC alone, with no patients requiring platelet-only transfusion or no transfusion (Fig. 2D and Additional file 1: Fig. S2F).

Similar to the pattern observed with N-ICAHT grading, the frequency of transfusion needs increased with higher T-ICAHT grades (Fig. 2E and Additional file 1: Fig. S2G–J). All 36 patients with early severe (Grade 3–4) T-ICAHT received at least one transfusion of RBCs and/or platelets, with 83.3% requiring transfusion of both blood products.

Early transfusion mirrors inflammatory toxicity and intensive supportive care

Our analysis revealed that early transfusion events were strongly associated with heightened inflammatory responses and clinical toxicity (Fig. 2F-I and Additional file 1: Table S2). Early RBC transfusion was significantly associated with elevated levels of multiple proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-2, IL-6, IL-10, and IFN-α. Concurrently, patients in this group exhibited higher CRS grades (P = 0.040) and a longer CRS duration (P = 0.036). Early platelet transfusion was also significantly associated with elevated levels of cytokines, including IL-2, IL-6, IL-8, and IFN-γ. Regarding clinical toxicity, patients in this group had higher ICANS grades (P = 0.001) and CRS grades (P = 0.026), with a significantly extended CRS duration (P = 0.019) and ICANS duration (P = 0.030) (Fig. 2H, I and Additional file 1: Table S2).

Regarding adverse events and supportive care (Figs. 2F, G), the rates of severe infection in the groups requiring early RBC and early platelet transfusion were 57% and 58%, respectively. These patients also required frequent supportive interventions, particularly corticosteroids (72% and 82% of those requiring early RBC or platelet transfusion, respectively) and tocilizumab (41% and 47% of those requiring early RBC or platelet transfusion, respectively) (Fig. 2F). The rates of severe infection among the patients who received late RBC and late platelet transfusions were 58% and 52%, respectively. Among the patients who required late transfusion of RBCs or platelets, the percentages who were treated with corticosteroids were 67% and 70%, respectively, and the percentages who received tocilizumab were 31% and 33%, respectively.

Late transfusion identifies poor response depth and excess nonrelapse mortality

Transfusion events were associated with the depth of therapeutic remission, particularly those occurring in the late phase. Early RBC and platelet transfusions showed no significant correlation with final efficacy (Fig. 2J). Late RBC transfusion (overall response [ORR]: 61.1% vs. 86.6%; P < 0.001) and late platelet transfusion (ORR: 63.0% vs. 88.1%; P < 0.001) were both significantly associated with poorer treatment response depth, which manifested as a significantly lower response depth in patients who received transfusions than in those who did not.

In the analysis of causes of death, disease-related mortality (DRM) showed no significant difference across all transfusion subgroups compared with that among non-transfused patients (Table 2A). However, NRM was significantly elevated in multiple transfusion subgroups compared with the non-transfusion group, including in the groups that received early RBC transfusion (26.1% vs. 10.1%; P = 0.021), late RBC transfusion (27.8% vs. 10.9%; P = 0.026), and late platelet transfusion (26.1% vs. 10.1%; P = 0.021) (Table 2B).

Additionally, EMD was a risk factor specific to early platelet transfusion (Table 3A).

Transfusion and survival outcomes

The last follow-up date was August 15, 2025, and the median time duration among all 155 patients was 41.7 months (95% confidence interval [CI], 35.1–48.3 months). The need for transfusion, particularly RBC transfusion, was a significant risk factor for shortened PFS (Fig. 3B). Patients receiving late RBC transfusions demonstrated a significantly worse median PFS than those who did not require transfusion (4.93 vs. 17.17 months, P < 0.0001). Early RBC transfusion (median PFS: 8.17 months vs. 17.17 months, P = 0.0172) and late platelet transfusion (median PFS: 5.25 months vs. 17.53 months, P = 0.0038) also were significantly associated with poorer PFS compared with no transfusion. However, the effect of early platelet transfusion on PFS did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.0972) (Fig. 3B and Table S3).

Similar and more pronounced associations were observed for OS (Fig. 3A). Patients who received late RBC transfusions had the shortest median OS (10.87 months vs. 37.87 months for patients who did not receive transfusions, P < 0.0001). Early RBC transfusion (median OS: 18.63 months vs. 34.97 months, P = 0.0003) and late platelet transfusion (median OS: 19.20 months vs. 38.77 months, P = 0.0003) were similarly significantly associated with reduced OS. Consistent with the result for PFS, early platelet transfusion had no significant impact on OS (P = 0.1146). Overall, the need for transfusion of RBCs and platelets was significantly associated with poor survival outcomes.

In the group that received both types of transfusions in the early phase, the 5-year OS was 16.3% (95% CI: 5.2%–51.5%) (Fig. 3C), which was significantly lower than that in the single type-transfusion group (26.5%) and in the no-transfusion group (43.5%; P = 0.0126). PFS also was significantly worse for patients who received both types of transfusions in the early phase (P = 0.0287). These differences between the groups that received both types of transfusions versus a single type of transfusion or no transfusions became even more pronounced in the late phase (OS, P < 0.0001; PFS, P = 0.0002), with 5-year OS dropping to 6.8% (95% CI: 1.1%–42.1%) for the group that received both types of transfusions, which was far below the OS for both the single type-transfusion group (25.4%) and no-transfusion group (46.0%).

A nonlinear, dose- and time-dependent association was observed between transfusion volume post-CAR-T cell therapy and survival outcomes (Figs. 2K, L and Additional file 1: Fig. S3). A total RBC transfusion volume exceeding 1 unit (hazard ratio [HR] 1.19; 95% CI, 1.06–1.34; P < 0.001) and a total platelet transfusion volume exceeding 10.1 units (HR, 1.76; 95% CI, 1.02–3.05; P < 0.001) were significantly associated with increased patient mortality risk (Figs. 2K, L). Stratified analysis by transfusion timing showed that both early and late RBC and platelet transfusions were significantly associated with reduced OS (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A-D and Additional file 1: Table S4), but only late transfusions were associated with shortened PFS (Additional file 1: Fig. S3E-H).

Time-dependent Cox proportional hazards regression showed that in multivariable time-dependent models early RBC transfusion remained independently associated with worse PFS (HR 1.677, 95% CI 1.034–2.719; P = 0.036) and OS (HR 2.307, 95% CI 1.208–4.407; P = 0.011). Late transfusion of any type was significantly associated with inferior OS (HR 2.534, 95% CI 1.303–4.927; P = 0.006), but showed no significant association with PFS (HR 1.546, 95% CI 0.905–2.641; P = 0.111). Landmark analyses yielded consistent findings: in the 30-day landmark analysis, early RBC transfusion was associated with significantly poorer PFS (P = 0.040) and OS (P < 0.001); in the 90-day landmark analysis, late transfusion was linked to inferior PFS and OS (P = 0.020 and P < 0.001, respectively) (Additional file 1: Table S4; Additional file 1: Fig. S4–S5).

Discussion

In this large retrospective study of patients with R/R MM who were treated with CAR T-cell therapy based on five different cell targets, we comprehensively characterized the landscape of transfusion requirements. Within our cohort, 21.9% of patients required transfusion support in the month preceding CAR-T cell infusion, reflecting widespread hematopoietic impairment driven by prior multi-line therapies and inherent bone marrow involvement. Following CAR-T cell infusion, 47.7% of patients required transfusion within the first 6 months. The demand peaked in the first month (38.7%) and gradually declined, with only 9.7% of patients requiring transfusion by month 6. This trajectory aligns with the known kinetics of lymphodepletion-induced myelosuppression and ICAHT [10, 25]. Our findings parallel those of Jin et al.,16 who reported that 30 days after BCMA CAR-T cell reinfusion, 23.1% of patients received RBC transfusions and 26.9% received platelet transfusions. Notably, we observed a distinct temporal shift in the type of blood product required. While RBC and platelet needs were comparable in the first month (RBC needs vs. platelet needs: 29.7% vs. 29.0%), platelet transfusion became the dominant requirement in the late phase (RBC needs vs. platelet needs: 23.2% vs. 30.3%). This supports the concept that early anemia is primarily associated with the lymphodepletion regimen, whereas persistent thrombocytopenia is a hallmark of late-phase toxicity. The occurrence of long-term thrombocytopenia after CAR-T cell therapy can be caused by multiple factors including CAR target structure, prior lines of therapy, bridging regimens, baseline intramedullary tumor burden, and levels of endothelial inflammation markers such as baseline D-dimer, and the precise pathophysiology remains to be fully elucidated [1, 7, 15, 26, 27].

Our data indicate a robust correlation between high-grade early N-ICAHT and increased transfusion requirements. The neutrophil count–based N-ICAHT grading likely captures the impact of the CAR-T-driven systemic inflammatory storm on the functional reserve of the bone marrow microenvironment [10]. Clinically, this manifests as a higher transfusion burden, particularly in the early post-infusion period, consistent with previous findings [28]. Patients with severe (Grade 3–4) early N-ICAHT in our study exhibited significantly higher transfusion needs. Regarding the relationship between inflammation and cytopenia, Jain et al. [15] and Rejeski et al. [29] found no association between the serum level of IL-6 and prolonged cytopenias. However, Juluri et al. [30] reported conflicting conclusions. Our results support the former conclusion, with early inflammatory adverse events not significantly predicting late transfusion requirements. This discrepancy demonstrates that the mechanisms underlying late cytopenias are complex, potentially involving persistent immune dysregulation, stem cell exhaustion from prolonged therapy, or sustained suppression of hematopoietic progenitor cells by CAR-T cells, rather than merely the initial cytokine storm [25, 31, 32]. Additionally, neutropenia and lymphopenia heighten the risk of infections. In patients with high N-ICAHT grades, lower baseline granulocyte levels and greater antibiotic exposure may exacerbate post-CAR-T hematologic toxicity, negatively impacting long-term survival and indirectly increasing transfusion needs [28, 33–37]. Recently, Rejeski et al. [12] established the T-ICAHT grading system based on platelet counts and showed that higher grades correlate with increased transfusion burden and inferior survival, conclusions corroborated by the results of the present study. Moreover, predictive models such as Early ICAHT Prediction Models (eIPMs) [38] have been developed to forecast severe early N-ICAHT, and thus, could facilitate risk stratification to guide interventions including transfusion and infection prophylaxis.

Multivariate analysis identified that, during the early phase, a history of transfusion within the preceding month was a significant predictor, as was a history of HSCT, which likely reflects cumulative damage to hematopoietic progenitor cells [27, 39]. The roles of G-CSF and granulocyte–macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) in modulating CAR-T cell therapy efficacy and toxicity remain topics of debate [40–42]. Our data indicate that neither factor predicted early transfusion requirements, although G-CSF use was associated with late-phase platelet transfusion needs. In the late phase, risk factors diverged. Late platelet transfusion was linked to markers of marrow exhaustion such as G-CSF use, whereas late RBC transfusion was primarily determined by baseline marrow reserve. Lower baseline MK counts in transfused patients may reflect pre-existing megakaryopoietic vulnerability, potentially contributing to persistent platelet transfusion needs after CAR-T cell infusion. As mentioned previously, older age and/or poorer performance status may be associated with the duration of hematologic toxicity. The MM patient population is predominantly middle-aged and elderly, often presenting with comorbidities such as cardiac and pulmonary diseases or reduced organ function. These factors impose limitations on anemia tolerance. Studies on large B-cell lymphoma have demonstrated that advanced age and/or poor performance status do not elevate transfusion risk. While our conclusions are consistent with these findings, this may be partly attributable to the relatively low proportion of elderly patients in our transfusion cohort [22, 43]. We recommend assessment of transfusion volume tolerance in these patients prior to treatment. CAR-T target was reviewed as a baseline treatment characteristic. However, given the limited number of patients in each target subgroup, it was not incorporated into the final multivariable survival models to avoid unstable estimates and model overfitting.

Transfusion requirements were strongly associated with inferior clinical outcomes. The persistent need for late RBC transfusion was closely linked to reduced survival. This contrasts with lymphoma studies showing that early transfusion or late platelet dependence is associated with poor prognosis [22]. More recently, late hematologic toxicity after CAR-T has been systematically characterized in large B-cell lymphoma. In a cohort of 290 LBCL patients, Corona et al. [44] reported that late ICAHT remained frequent by day 100 (45% for any grade; 22% for grade ≥ 3), and late severe ICAHT was associated with a higher hazard of late infections. These data highlight that late cytopenias after CAR-T are clinically meaningful rather than isolated laboratory abnormalities. Rejeski et al. [25, 45] defined persistent severe neutropenia (> 14 days) following CAR-T cell infusion as an aplastic phenotype and found that such patients experience poorer PFS and OS. Combined with our study results, this indicates that some aplastic phenotype patients overlap with thrombocytopenia, leading to similar outcomes. Furthermore, we observed that patients who required late-stage transfusions exhibited significantly poorer disease remission depth and bore a higher risk of NRM. For patients who have undergone multiple lines of therapy and suffered severe bone marrow dysfunction, the relatively slow-recovering erythroid hematopoietic function remains unreconstructed after cell reinfusion. This may indicate irreversible depletion of bone marrow reserve. In vitro studies have indicated that activated CAR-T cells secrete pro-inflammatory mediators, impair hematopoietic differentiation, and remodel transcriptional programs, contributing to persistent hematopoietic toxicity [25, 44, 46]. Previous animal and clinical studies also have revealed the involvement of transfusion-related immune modulation (TRIM) effects after allogeneic transfusion, which in multiple scenarios lead to significant immune downregulation and may further promote tumor relapse [21, 47]. The hypothesis that blood product processing methods such as pre-storage leukoreduction can mitigate TRIM effects requires further validation. Furthermore, restricted cubic spline analysis confirmed a dose-dependent mortality risk with increasing transfusion volume. Although sparse data at the upper extremes limited precision, patients accumulating substantial transfusions represent a distinct subgroup with persistent marrow failure warranting further study.

Given that transfusion exposure after CAR-T therapy is inherently time-dependent, we performed additional time-dependent Cox and landmark analyses to further examine the robustness of the survival findings. These analyses indicate that the prognostic impact of transfusion is not constant over time: early RBC transfusion appears to identify an early post-infusion vulnerable period, whereas late transfusion exhibits a broader and more sustained adverse association, particularly with overall survival. Considered alongside its associations with poorer response depth and higher non-relapse mortality in the primary analyses, late transfusion appears to represent a clinically meaningful marker of persistent hematologic vulnerability.

To our knowledge, this investigation represents the largest international retrospective cohort study to date examining transfusion requirements following CAR-T cell therapy in patients with R/R MM. However, our study has limitations. First, its retrospective design may introduce selection bias. Second, heterogeneity among CAR-T cell products may contribute to variability in associations and outcomes. For example, clinical meta-analyses indicate that CD19-targeted CAR-T cell therapy is associated with a higher incidence of complications compared with BCMA-targeted therapy [48]. While the use of different targets enhances the universality of our core findings across therapeutic products, it may also influence transfusion requirements. Furthermore, the potential impacts of blood product processing methods and storage duration on hematologic toxicity and prognosis after CAR-T cell therapy remain unknown. In addition, differences in transfusion thresholds and outpatient follow-up patterns across centers may influence the incidence and timing of transfusion events, which should be considered when interpreting the external generalizability of our findings.

Conclusions

In summary, this study identifies post-CAR-T transfusion dependency as a key prognostic factor in R/R MM. Early transfusion indicates severe inflammation, whereas late dependence predicts inferior survival and increased NRM. Future research should focus on mechanisms and predictive models to optimize supportive care and improve long-term outcomes.
