A universal dichotomy for concentration in randomly colored graphs.
Nicola Apollonio

Abstract
Let ζ \zeta be Euclidean norm of the degree sequence of a graph normalized by the graph size. We prove that when the vertices of a graph are randomly colored with s s colors such that the fraction of vertices in each color class is bounded away from zero, only two asymptotic regimes emerge. If ζ = o ⁡ ( 1 ) \zeta=o(1) , then the sizes of the subgraphs induced by the color classes concentrate around their expected values. If ζ = Θ ⁡ ( 1 ) \zeta=\Theta(1) , then concentration depends on the color balance: for colorings with persisting imbalance, the total number M M of monochromatic edges stays bounded away from its mean with positive probability; otherwise, for vanishing imbalance, M M still concentrates. The same dichotomy holds for a broad class of randomly colored random graphs.



Keywords
: random coloring, second order method, elementary symmetric polynomials, random graphs,
Paley–Zygmund inequality.

Complementarily to the Edge Statistic Theorem, which gives a universal upper bound on the probability that an induced subgraph has exactly

the number of monochromatic and bichromatic edges, respectively.

We show that the asymptotic behavior of these statistics is sharply governed by the parameter

\zeta(G)={\displaystyle\frac{\sqrt{\Sigma_{2}(G)}}{m}}

where
\Sigma_{2}(G)
is the sum of squared degrees of

When

When
\zeta(G)=\Theta(1)
, concentration holds precisely for balanced colorings, while persistent imbalance forces fluctuations of order comparable to the mean, yielding anti-concentration.

Specifically, we prove the following.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\left(G_{n}\right)_{n\geq 1}
be a sequence of randomly
\mathbf{c}_{n}(s)
-colored graphs of order

where
\mathbf{c}_{n}(s)=(c_{1,n},\ldots,c_{s,n})
and
\sum_{i}c_{i,n}=n

\hat{c}_{n}(s)=\min_{i\in[s]}c_{i,n}
and
\zeta_{n}=\zeta(G_{n})

\boldsymbol{M}(G_{n})=\left(M_{1}(G_{n}),\ldots,M_{s}(G_{n})\right)\quad\text{and}\quad\widebar{\boldsymbol{M}}(G_{n})=\left(\mathbb{E}\left(M_{1}(G_{n})\right),\ldots,\mathbb{E}\left(M_{s}(G_{n})\right)\right).

If

\liminf_{n}\frac{\hat{c}_{n}}{n}>0\quad\text{and}\quad m_{n}\to\infty

then the following hold.

(i)

If
\zeta_{n}=o(1)
, then

\boldsymbol{M}(G_{n})=(1+o_{P}(1))\boldsymbol{I}_{s}\widebar{\boldsymbol{M}}(G_{n})

Hence, in particular, both

(ii)

If
\zeta_{n}=\Theta(1)

\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{s}=s^{-1}\mathbf{1}_{s}

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(|M(G_{n})-\mathbb{E}\left(M(G_{n})\right)|>0\right)>0

and, similarly,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(|L(G_{n})-\mathbb{E}\left(L(G_{n})\right)|>0\right)>0

or
\|n^{-1}\mathbf{c}_{n}(s)-\boldsymbol{\upsilon}_{s}\|\to 0
, and both

and

concentrate in probability around their expected values regardless of
\zeta_{n}

The dichotomy established in Theorem

The heart of the proof is the exact computation of the second moment structure which requires some combinatorial work and a convenient asymptotic estimates of the leading coefficient of the asymptotic variance. Applying these results, along with some additional probabilistic tool, shows that for a rather large class of random graphs, Theorem

Remark 4
.

By counting in two ways, for any graph
G_{n}
with
n
vertices,
m_{n}
edges and maximum degree
\Delta_{n}
, it holds that

\Sigma_{2}(G_{n})=\sum_{v\in V(G)}d^{2}_{G_{n}}(v)=\sum_{uv\in E(G_{n})}(d_{G_{n}}(u)+d_{G_{n}}(v)).

Since for
uv\in E(G_{n})
it holds that
2\leq d_{G_{n}}(u)+d_{G_{n}}(v)\leq 2\Delta_{n}
and
\Delta_{n}\leq\min\{n,m_{n}\}
, this yields

\Sigma_{2}(G_{n})=\begin{cases}\Theta(m_{n}^{2})&\text{if $m_{n}=\Theta(1)$},\\
\Theta(n)&\text{if $\Delta_{n}=\Theta(1)$ and $m_{n}\to\infty$},\\
O(nm_{n})&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Therefore, if we assume that
m_{n}\to\infty
, then the conditions
\zeta_{n}=o(1)
and
\zeta_{n}=\Theta(1)
in Theorem
1
, imply that
m_{n}=\Omega(n)
and
m_{n}=O(n)
, respectively. In particular, the condition
\zeta_{n}=o(1)
is realized when
\Delta_{n}=\Theta(1)
or when
m_{n}=\omega(n)
. On the other hand,
\zeta_{n}=\Theta(1)
is realized only if
m_{n}=\Theta(n)
and the degree sequence of
G_{n}
does not concentrate around the mean degree
2m_{n}/n
. This happens, for instance, for sparse threshold graphs (see
[
12
,
5
]
) which are in fact maximizers of
\Sigma_{2}(\cdot)
.

Remark 5
.

Consider an arbitrary sequence
\left(G_{n}\right)_{n\geq 1}
of randomly
\mathbf{c}_{n}(s)
-colored graphs of order
n

where

. This condition can be realized by taking

or

with
\alpha+2\beta<2
. However, as we will show in the next section, even assuming
m_{n}c_{i,n}^{\kern 0.81949pt\underline{2}\phantom{i,n}}=\Theta(n^{\underline{2}})
but allowing

yields concentration regardless of the structure of

(see Remark
6
). The regimes for

and
\mathbf{c}_{n}(s)

Proof.

Let

–

Let

–

Let
\mathcal{G}_{n}
be a random graph on

is such that there exists a vanishing fraction of vertices whose degrees are of order

never concentrates around its mean, because the contribution of the hubs keeps the ratio

1/(2n)&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}.

Lemma 4

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and

{\displaystyle\frac{\mathbb{E}(X_{n})}{\mathbb{E}(Y_{n})}}\to 0\Longleftrightarrow{\displaystyle\frac{X_{n}}{Y_{n}}}\xrightarrow{P}0.

(*)

(**)

in other words, under the assumption of the lemma, the sufficient condition (

.

By Theorem

and, by the second part of Lemma

5
Concluding remarks

In this paper we have studied concentration/anti-concentration phenomena of the counts of monochromatic and bichromatic edges in randomly colored graph and randomly colored random graphs identifying a single parameter which sharply governs the emergence of these two regimes. The motivation for studying these phenomena comes both from structural and extremal graph theory, where the count of monochromatic edges in a single color class (the random variables
M_{i}(G))
is the density of induced subgraph sizes, and from the practical need to assess homophily and the existence of community structures in network science. As a side effect, the analysis shed new light on the so-called first Zagreb index, a notion central to the mathematical chemistry community. The random coloring model can be complicated and enriched in several ways by considering more general probability spaces
\mathbb{G}\otimes\mathbf{C}
where
\mathbb{G}
is the sample probability space for the random graph and
\mathbf{C}
is the sample probability space for the random colorings. Even when keeping
\mathbf{C}
at its simplest form, as the one given here with the uniform model, it would be already interesting to investigate the extent to which Theorem
2
can be lifted by weakening condition
(enumi)
.
