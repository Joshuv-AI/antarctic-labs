Safactory: A Scalable Agentic Infrastructure for Training Trustworthy Autonomous Intelligence
Shanghai AI Laboratory ∗

Abstract
As large models evolve from conversational assistants into autonomous agents, challenges increasingly arise from long-horizon decision making, tool use, and real environment interaction. Existing agenticinfrastructure remain fragmented across evaluation, data management, and agent evolution, making it difficult to discover risks systematically and improve models in a continuous closed loop. In this report, we present Safactory , a scalable agent factory for trustworthy autonomous intelligence. Safactory integrates three tightly coupled platforms: a Parallel Simulation Platform for trajectory generation, a Trustworthy Data Platform for trajectory storage and experience extraction, and an Autonomous Evolution Platform for asynchronous reinforcement learning and on-policy distillation. As far as we know, Safactory 1 1 1 https://github.com/AI45Lab/Safactory is the first framework to propose a unified evolutionary pipeline for next-generation trustworthy autonomous intelligence.

1
Introduction

1.1
A Panoramic View of AI Agent Safety Risks

With the rapid development of applications such as companion AI, DeepResearch, intelligent terminals, and embodied AI, large language models are moving from ”conversational assistants” to ”autonomous agents.” These systems are not only responsible for answering questions but also continuously perceive their environment, plan tasks, invoke tools, and directly influence external systems.

This brings stronger application capabilities, but also imposes higher safety requirements. Unlike traditional single-turn models, the risks of agents are not limited to harmful outputs; they accumulate progressively throughout the process of ”perception → decision-making → execution → feedback,” ultimately manifesting as real-world environmental consequences.

As shown in Figure
2
, violations, jailbreak inductions, and explicit attacks are merely the risks above the surface. Beneath the waterline lie more insidious issues such as tool misuse, state drift, process loss of control, data contamination, cross-turn inducement, and cascading errors. These risks are often harder to detect and more difficult to cover with static testing.

Such risks typically exhibit three characteristics. First, they often emerge in long-horizon, multi-step interactions rather than single-turn input-output pairs. Second, they are closely tied to environmental states, tool interfaces, and execution contexts, making them difficult to characterize using static benchmarks alone. Third, when they do occur, the consequences often go beyond degraded response quality — they may further lead to erroneous operations, privacy breaches, system damage, or even real-world losses. Therefore, trustworthy AI for high-value scenarios requires not just ”safer responses,” but more importantly, the ability to remain controllable, auditable, and recoverable while taking continuous action.

1.2
Limitations of Existing Systems

To address the above challenges, existing AI agent safety systems still exhibit significant gaps across three foundational dimensions: evaluation, data, and evolution. These issues are not isolated from one another. Collectively, they result in safety efforts often remaining in a passive ”find a problem, fix a problem” mode, making it difficult to form a continuous closed loop oriented toward real-world deployment scenarios.

Limitations of Evaluation Infrastructure

Existing security evaluations are still dominated by static question-answering, single-round adversarial examples, or fixed task sets. Such approaches are suitable for answering whether a model ”will say something wrong,” but struggle to answer whether an agent ”will do something wrong during continuous execution.” When risks depend on tool invocation chains, changes in environmental states, and the accumulation of long-horizon decisions, single-turn interactive evaluations can only capture the surface of the iceberg, making it difficult to systematically expose deep failure modes. Even in executable environments, many existing frameworks remain centered around fixed tasks and offline logs, lacking capabilities such as high-concurrency retesting, dynamic perturbation injection, and joint analysis of process–consequence coupling. For the relevant technical landscape, see Section
2.1
.

Limitations of Data Infrastructure

Currently, many evaluation and training frameworks treat data merely as a byproduct of the runtime process, with common practices relying on in-memory caches and temporary local files for storage. This approach struggles to stably support large-scale heterogeneous data management, nor does it facilitate cross-task, cross-version, or cross-environment retrieval, reuse, and auditing. More importantly, agents continuously generate a large volume of trajectories, reward signals, and failure contexts during evaluation, execution, and training. These are essentially high-value data assets, yet existing systems generally lack a unified mechanism for their structured accumulation, automated analysis, and subsequent feedback into model optimization. For further discussion, see Section
2.2
.

Limitations of Evolution infrastructure

At the level of model evolution, training, evaluation, and deployment remain largely disconnected from one another. Issues identified during evaluation are difficult to promptly convert into training samples, and real-world feedback generated during execution is also hard to smoothly reintegrate into subsequent optimization workflows. Meanwhile, reinforcement learning and online distillation for agent scenarios are transitioning from standalone algorithm implementations to system-level orchestration problems. However, most existing training frameworks are still designed around local coordination between rollout and trainer components, and are insufficient to stably support complex training closed loops that involve multi-environment concurrency, trajectory staleness control, asynchronous sampling, and online policy updates. For a comprehensive review, see Section
2.3
.

1.3
Safactory: Building a Complete Closed Loop for Trustworthy AI

To address the above limitations, Safactory proposes an infrastructure framework for trustworthy autonomous agents. Centered around three core platforms — a Parallel Execution Middle Platform, a Trustworthy Data Middle Platform, and an Autonomous Evolution Middle Platform — it establishes a complete closed loop that integrates risk discovery, data accumulation, and capability repair and reinforcement. Overall, as shown in Figure
1
, Safactory does not treat security evaluation as a one-time tool-based process, but rather organizes it as a data and training system that can run continuously, be iteratively optimized, and be repeatedly reused.

Among these, the Parallel Execution Middle Platform targets executable sandboxes for Android, computer environments, and embodied scenarios. It supports environment pool preheating, asynchronous scheduling, and concurrent re-evaluation of multiple trajectories, as well as the injection of attacks and perturbations during execution, enabling more systematic discovery of safety risks in long-horizon interactions. The Trustworthy Data Middle Platform is responsible for consolidating the multimodal data, step-level trajectories, and behavioral signals generated during evaluation and training into retrievable, analyzable, and auditable data assets, and performs safety auditing, sample filtering, and experience distillation through an intent-driven data engine. The Autonomous Evolution Middle Platform further integrates these data assets into asynchronous reinforcement learning and online policy optimization workflows, continuously generating trainable samples under version control, trajectory filtering, and group consistency constraints, thereby supporting the simultaneous improvement of model capabilities and safety.

Therefore, Safactory’s goal is neither to improve a single evaluation metric in isolation, nor to merely provide point solutions such as a data tool or a training framework. What it aims to establish is a closed-loop pathway of ”discovering risks through execution, consolidating evidence through data, completing repairs through evolution, and verifying effectiveness through re-execution.” Along this path, safety is no longer a one-time check conducted before model deployment, but rather becomes an infrastructure capability throughout the entire lifecycle of an agent.

Related Work

2.1
Agent Evaluation Framework

As model capabilities and deployment scenarios continue to expand, safety evaluation must evolve toward deeper and more comprehensive paradigms. We therefore define a five-level progression of AI safety evaluation (L1–L5):

Figure 3
:
The evolutionary trajectory of safety evaluation capabilities.

•

L1 — Q&A Evaluation
: Static, single-turn benchmarks focusing on alignment, jailbreak robustness, and domain-specific knowledge.

•

L2 — Adversarial Evaluation
: Dynamic red teaming via human or automated agents that generate multi-turn attack scenarios.

•

L3 — Process-Level Evaluation
: Multi-turn interaction assessment that captures risks emerging task execution.

•

L4 — Sandbox Evaluation
: Evaluation in executable environments where real-world consequences of agent actions are observable.

•

L5 — Evolutionary Evaluation
: Adaptive evaluation that co-evolves with model capabilities and generalizes across scenarios.

2.1.1
Q&A - and Adversarial-Level Infrastructure

Most existing evaluation infrastructure operate at L1, organizing benchmarks around task registries and standardized scoring pipelines. Representative systems include LM-Evaluation-Harness
lm-eval-harness
, OpenCompass
opencompass
, and OpenAI Evals
openai-evals
. Despite differences in scale and engineering maturity, they share a common paradigm: evaluation is conducted on fixed, single-instance tasks, inherently limiting coverage.

To overcome this limitation, L2 adversarial evaluation introduces dynamic attack generation. Systems such as Giskard Hub
giskard
, Garak
garak
, and promptfoo
promptfoo
expand the test space through continuous red teaming and structured probing. However, their evaluation unit remains single-turn interaction.

Both L1 and L2 fundamentally rely on single-turn interactions.
In contrast, many critical agent failures emerge over multi-step tool usage and long-horizon execution
, motivating the shift toward process-level evaluation.

2.1.2
Process-Level Infrastructure

L3 evaluation extends the unit of analysis from single responses to full interaction trajectories. Langfuse
langfuse
emphasizes observability, enabling real-time monitoring of agent traces. MLflow
mlflow
integrates intermediate decision steps—such as tool selection and recovery—into lifecycle evaluation. DeepEval
deepeval
evolves from Q&A-style testing by introducing agentic and conversation-level metrics.

Process-level evaluation enables trajectory-based analysis of where and how risks arise. However, its scope largely remains within abstract task execution, lacking direct validation of real-world consequences.

2.1.3
Sandbox-Level Infrastructure

Many agent risks—such as unsafe tool usage, improper file operations, and cascading execution errors—cannot be fully captured without executable environments. L4 sandbox evaluation addresses this by placing agents in real environments where outcomes are directly observable. Systems such as Inspect AI
inspect-ai
, Terminal-Bench
terminal-bench
, Harbor
harbor
, and Claw-Eval
claweval2026
advance this paradigm across tool use, terminal tasks, and trajectory auditing.

However, such systems generally operate with fixed evaluation tasks as their basic unit, lacking the ability to dynamically perturb the environment state, and store results primarily as logs rather than reusable structured data. Environment support is often domain-specific, and safety is treated as a secondary metric rather than a first-class objective. These limitations hinder scalability and reuse in rapidly evolving model settings.

To address these gaps,
Safactory
introduces three key capabilities: (1) dynamic injection of attacks and perturbations during execution to systematically expose failure modes; (2) structured storage of interaction trajectories and reward signals as reusable data assets for downstream SFT or RL; and (3) service-oriented environment interfaces that decouple evaluation from execution, enabling seamless integration of diverse environments (e.g., industrial systems, simulators, embodied settings).

Unlike prior L4 systems, The core distinction of Safactory is not whether it supports execution in real environments, but rather its transformation of security evaluation from a one-time tool-based process into a sustainably evolving infrastructure capability.

2.2
Trustworthy Data Services

As the training scale of AI systems and agentic application scenarios continue to expand, the data service infrastructure that underpins their operation must also evolve from static pipelines toward orchestrable, perception-aware, and self-evolving intelligent systems. We categorize existing work into three stages according to capability progression:

•

Static Data Management
: Offline workflow scheduling built around DAGs, ensuring data availability and reproducibility.

•

Data Cleaning Pipelines
: Orchestrable pipelines centered on rules, scoring functions, and operators, automating collection, cleaning, deduplication, filtering, alignment, and quality verification to reliably produce training-ready data.

•

Intelligent Data Engines
: Systems centered on data assets and behavioral signals, transforming production-side feedback into a continuous optimization driver and forming a closed-loop evolutionary cycle.

2.2.1
Static Data Management: DAG-centric Workflow Scheduling

Most mainstream data infrastructure revolves around task definition, dependency orchestration, scheduling, and status monitoring, with DAG-based batch processing at the core. Apache Airflow
airflow
is the de facto standard for scheduling-oriented ETL, providing a DAG-based framework with a rich operator library and cron scheduling. Prefect
prefect
advances the paradigm from
DAG as configuration
to
Python as flow
, offering dynamic process construction and event-driven scheduling. Dagster
dagster
places software-defined assets at the core, treating datasets, models, and features as traceable products. All three share the same model: execution is driven by static scheduling and predefined dependencies.

The limitation is that DAGs only answer
when to run what
, not
whether the resulting data is worth using
. Dirty data, duplicate samples, and low-quality content can pass through unchecked. This gap gave rise to quality-oriented data cleaning pipelines.

2.2.2
Data Cleaning Pipelines: Scoring-centric Quality Assurance

Data cleaning pipelines treat data quality as the primary objective, building workflows around operators for cleaning, deduplication, filtering, and scoring. Data-Juicer
datajuicer
provides composable operators covering text, image, audio/video, and multimodal data. OpenDataArena
cai2025opendataarena
characterizes instructions and responses along more than ten dimensions, making data value quantifiable. NeMo Curator
nemo-curator
uses a GPU-accelerated stack to handle cleaning, deduplication, and PII redaction with dedicated multimodal pipelines.

These systems shift from task scheduling to quality-centered content selection. However, processing flows and scoring thresholds are fixed before the pipeline runs. Data risks surface only when task objectives change, domains shift, or compliance boundaries tighten, which is a structural limitation that static pipelines cannot overcome.

2.2.3
Intelligent Data Engines: Closed-loop Optimization Centered on Data Assets and Behavioral Signals

Intelligent data engines treat data as continuously accumulable assets and use behavioral signals as the core driver of data production. TensorZero
tensorzero
unifies an LLM gateway, evaluation, and optimization into one system, persisting each inference and feedback while supporting SFT and RLHF based on real-world metrics. Arena Learning
luo2024arena
simulates a chatbot arena to automatically generate preference pairs. Agent-in-the-Loop
zhao2025agent
embeds human annotation into production workflows. Both validate the closed loop of “behavioral signals
\rightarrow
data assets
\rightarrow
model iteration”.

These systems, however, remain at the tool execution layer. Users must still describe execution flows via declarative configurations, feedback is only aggregated as training signals rather than used to evolve the toolchain, and data security relies on environment isolation rather than native substrate capabilities. In regulated and multi-party scenarios, this means task expression is locked within engineering teams, closed-loop experience cannot be distilled into reusable capabilities, and privacy constraints render many high-value tasks infeasible.

Safactory models data tasks as a complete chain: natural language intent
\rightarrow
dynamically generated pipeline
\rightarrow
tool invocation
\rightarrow
execution
\rightarrow
tracking
\rightarrow
asset accumulation. For explicit tasks it uses single-planning execution. For ambiguous tasks it enters autonomous closed-loop mode, continuously trying, judging, and correcting around the goal. Stable multi-step combinations can be distilled into new tools, continuously evolving the tool ecosystem. For data security, Safactory supports encrypted packaging for local execution, outputting results without exposing raw data. This transforms data services from static pipeline construction to intent-driven continuous evolution, which is the essential difference between existing intelligent data engines and Safactory.

2.3
Agentic Model Training Services

With the expansion of large models into new application domains, training is no longer just about optimizing model parameters. It now encompasses organizing the rollout process, feedback modeling, and policy updates within the training pipeline. Traditional monolithic training frameworks, which are designed for static data, are increasingly inadequate to support more complex training processes. Consequently, model training services for agents have gradually evolved into frameworks that emphasize orchestration, decoupling, and scalability.

Early reinforcement learning training frameworks for large language models (LLMs) mainly focused on the standard Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF) process, as exemplified by systems such as TRL
trl
, DeepSpeed-Chat
yao2023deepspeedchat
, and OpenRLHF
openrlhf2024
. These frameworks aimed to organize supervised fine-tuning, reward modeling, and policy optimization into reproducible training pipelines. The training paradigm in this phase was relatively standardized, typically following the SFT–RM–PPO (Supervised Fine-Tuning, Reward Modeling, Proximal Policy Optimization) pipeline: first, the model was aligned using demonstration or preference data, then further optimized through reinforcement learning with a reward model and PPO-based methods. This approach closely mirrored earlier representative works such as InstructGPT
ouyang2022instructgpt
and Constitutional AI
bai2022constitutional
. Thus, the core task during this phase was the engineering and encapsulation of standard components like SFT, RM, and PPO, rather than explicitly addressing multi-round interactive training in more complex environments.

As model scale, training costs, and online sampling expenses continued to rise, the focus of training frameworks shifted from simply ”executing RLHF” to ”organizing large-scale post-training systems.” From GPT-3
brown2020gpt3
and GPT-4
openai2023gpt4
to Qwen2
qwen2_2024
, DeepSeek-V2
deepseek_v2_2024
, and DeepSeek-V3
deepseek_v3_2024
, these works demonstrated that model training is evolving into a multi-stage, systematized engineering process: model improvement no longer solely depends on a single training script, but increasingly relies on reusable data pipelines, clearly defined training phases, and parallelism and scheduling mechanisms that scale stably with both model and cluster sizes. At the framework level, verl
verl_github
has begun to prioritize data flow organization, modular decoupling, and resource scheduling, with a focus that has gradually shifted from supporting a specific optimization method to providing unified system support for complex post-training processes such as PPO and DPO.

In recent years, the rapid development of reasoning and agent-based scenarios has further accelerated this evolution. While early RLHF efforts primarily addressed ”how to make models answer better,” recent technical reports have focused more on ”how to enable models to act continuously in complex environments and learn from interactions.” On the one hand, works such as DeepSeek-R1
deepseek_r1_2025
and Kimi k1.5
kimi_k1_5_2025
show that reinforcement learning has shifted from being a tool for alignment to becoming a key method for enhancing reasoning capabilities. Corresponding optimization methods have moved beyond the classic RLHF-PPO pipeline, adopting new approaches such as GRPO that are better suited to large-scale online sampling and relative comparison signals. On the other hand, reports such as Qwen3
qwen3_2025
, GLM-4.5
glm45_2025
, Kimi K2
kimi_k2_2025
, Tongyi DeepResearch
tongyi_deepresearch_blog_2025
, Scaling Agents via Continual Pre-training
scaling_agents_cpt_2025
, GLM-5
glm5_2026
, and DeepSeek-V3.2
deepseek_v32_2025
have broadened the training narrative to incorporate elements like thinking modes, agentic capabilities, tool usage, continual pre-training, and the decoupling of generation and training. In other words, the training system must now address not just a single parameter update, but a continuous loop of rollout, data generation, optimization, and evaluation.

Against this backdrop, training frameworks are transitioning from execution frameworks focused on a single training cycle to systematic orchestration frameworks designed for complex agent workflows. Works such as slime
slime_github
, AReaL
areal_github
;
fu2025areal
, and RollArt
rollart2025
explore scalable organizational methods for more complex training workflows, from customizable data generation interfaces and asynchronous training execution to decoupling heterogeneous infrastructures. These frameworks share the characteristic of no longer being restricted to implementing specific optimization algorithms like PPO or GRPO; instead, they focus on system-wide coordination among rollout, reasoning, training, and resource orchestration. Along this path, the core challenge in large model training services has shifted from ”how to complete a single training run” to ”how to organize a sustainable and scalable training system.”

Compared to previous frameworks that were mainly designed around the collaboration between rollout and trainer, Safactory’s advantage lies not in introducing new single-point optimization modules, but in explicitly reconfiguring the structural relationships between the operational pipeline, data deposition, and optimization loops through layered orchestration. This makes the system more suited to handling the training requirements of agent-based scenarios, where continuous interaction, generation, and optimization co-exist.

3
Parallel Simulation Platform

As agents extend toward multi-step, complex tasks, continuous-interaction evaluation in real-world environments faces two core challenges. The first is long runtime and low efficiency. Long decision chains make even minor deviations highly prone to accumulation, so repeated trials are required to ensure confidence. Taking OpenClaw as an example, a single task takes about 20 minutes on average. If 100 tasks are each re-evaluated five times, the total serial runtime would be roughly seven days. The second challenge is insufficient assessment of safety capabilities. The current evaluation paradigm, based on single-sample, linear execution, fundamentally conflicts with the exponential growth of multi-turn interactive decision branches in multi-step tasks. In OpenClaw, for instance, an agent may trigger high-risk operations such as accidentally deleting files during task execution. However, single-path testing can cover only a very small number of fixed trajectories, making it easy to create an illusion of safety simply because the model happened to avoid risk in one particular decoding attempt. Since existing evaluations lack mechanisms for traversing decision branches in multi-step tasks, they struggle to systematically expose risk blind spots in complex interactions, resulting in inadequate safety-evaluation coverage and distorted conclusions.

To systematically address these pain points, the Parallel Rollout Platform was developed. As a sandbox rollout framework designed for evaluation and reinforcement learning, the platform uses pre-warmed environment pools and asynchronous scheduling mechanisms to advance massive numbers of long-horizon trajectories under high concurrency, effectively alleviating efficiency and cost bottlenecks. At the same time, it natively supports the dynamic injection of red-team attacks, enabling comprehensive and systematic evaluation of agents’ safety and robustness boundaries in complex interactive scenarios.

In addition, the platform continuously accumulates reusable data and experience during operation. All step-level trajectories generated through rollouts can be seamlessly fed back into the system, forming a closed data loop of “evaluation–training–re-evaluation.” In this way, the Parallel Rollout Platform fundamentally breaks through the limitations of isolated evaluation and establishes a unified technical foundation for the continuous improvement of agent capabilities and the strengthening of safety defenses.

3.2
System Architecture

Figure 4
:
Architecture of Parallel Simulation Platform.

To support multi-step continuous-interaction evaluation and address issues such as long evaluation time and insufficient evaluation coverage, we designed and implemented a parallel rollout platform based on state awareness and elastic scheduling (Figure
4
). This platform is designed for the scaled execution of evaluation tasks and multi-path analysis. It centrally manages Agent instances, sandbox environments, large-model requests, and the circulation of evaluation data. It consists of four core modules: a parallel scheduler, a concurrent Agent rollout foundation, an elastic large-model gateway, and data storage and evaluation. To reduce integration costs, the platform provides a dual-track access solution: lightweight Agents can be directly launched through Python source code, while complex Agents can be seamlessly mounted through Docker images. Developers only need to implement three basic interfaces—Reset, Start, and Close—to bring custom Agents under the platform’s unified scheduling and carry out large-scale concurrent repeated evaluation and rollout analysis under a standardized evaluation protocol.

Focusing on the efficiency bottleneck in repeated evaluation of multi-step tasks, the platform improves evaluation throughput through an end-to-end mechanism of “pre-warming—pooled scheduling—asynchronous persistence.” First, the sandbox manager starts the underlying sandboxes in batches in advance according to the configured pool capacity, completing the pre-warming of the task execution resource pool and reducing cold-start time from the source. Second, based on the ready resource pool, the system relies on ActorPool to concurrently create a large number of Agent instances, and achieves seamless transitions between tasks through asynchronous hot-switching scheduling. This reduces CPU idle time caused by Agent initialization, environment loading, and resource waiting, allowing limited computing resources to be used more for the actual evaluation process. Finally, in the face of request surges to large models caused by repeated evaluation with multiple Agents, the elastic large-model gateway is responsible for unified handling, dynamic scaling, and request forwarding, and transparently writes interaction trajectories into the storage module. With built-in buffering and a “fire-and-forget” asynchronous persistence mechanism, the main evaluation process does not need to block while waiting for trajectory writing. In this way, repeated evaluation of long tasks that was originally advanced serially is transformed into a multi-instance concurrent execution process, significantly reducing overall evaluation time while ensuring that trajectories remain traceable.

To address problems in safety evaluation such as insufficient coverage under single-path execution, high costs of repeated restarts, and difficulty reproducing abnormal scenarios, the platform further introduces a “backtracking sandbox” mechanism, building a version management system similar to a Git dedicated to Agents. This mechanism can perceive file changes, context states, and key environment operations made by the Agent during the task process, and automatically generate checkpoints at important nodes. Like game saves, it preserves a complete behavior timeline and an environment “version tree.” During evaluation, when an Agent enters a multi-path decision node, triggers a potentially high-risk operation, or causes environment damage due to incorrect execution, the system does not need to fully restart from the initial state. Instead, it can precisely locate a historical safety anchor point and quickly roll back to the corresponding state, then continue rolling out different action paths as if “pulling a new branch.” As a result, safety evaluation is upgraded from single-sample, linear execution to branch-based exploration around key states. On the one hand, this avoids repeated execution of prerequisite steps, so the large model only consumes incremental tokens for newly explored branches. On the other hand, it enables multi-path repeated evaluation around safety-sensitive nodes such as accidental file deletion, incorrect modification, permission abuse, abnormal recursion, and infinite loops, more systematically exposing risk blind spots in long-horizon interaction and improving the coverage, reproducibility, and reliability of conclusions in safety capability evaluation.

3.3.1
QA Gym

The QA Gym environment can support conventional large model evaluation workflows. In this work, we adopt a red-teaming safety evaluation setup. Within this environment, agents are not required to perform complex external operations; instead, they engage in multi-turn dialogue, strategy prompting, and sustained interaction to simulate attack–defense dynamics. This makes it particularly suitable for characterizing the safety boundaries of models.

Based on this setup, we reproduce the multi-strategy, multi-round, and multi-agent red-teaming configuration proposed in OpenRT
wang2026openrt

3.3.2
OS Gym

OS Gym encapsulates a real Ubuntu desktop virtual machine as a standardized evaluation environment, enabling agents to execute long-horizon tasks in multi-application GUI scenarios and exposing potential security risks through real system interactions. This environment emphasizes cross-application operations, complex interface understanding, and system-level sensitive behaviors (e.g., file access and command execution), making it well-suited for desktop automation and safety evaluation.

At the execution level, OS Gym relies on a virtualized desktop environment combined with containerized scheduling to support reproducible system-level interactions. While this design provides high-fidelity execution, it also introduces substantial system dependencies. To reduce deployment complexity, we provide pre-built Docker images, allowing users to run evaluations without manually configuring virtual machines or runtime environments.

3.3.3
Android Gym

Android Gym encapsulates a real Android emulator as a standardized evaluation environment, enabling agents to execute tasks within UI workflows that closely resemble real user interactions, while exposing potential security risks during execution. The environment targets typical mobile scenarios such as app operation, interface navigation, and environment-injected attacks, allowing simultaneous evaluation of task performance and safety robustness.

3.4
SATraj: Open-Source Agent Trajectory Datasets

Safactory is not only an execution framework for agent evaluation and training, but also capable of continuously depositing agent interaction processes into data assets. During each environmental operation, the framework automatically records the complete interaction trajectories between agents and the environment, including task instructions, dialogue history, model responses, action execution, environmental states, screenshot observations, stepwise rewards, and final task outcomes. Consequently, each evaluation or training session generates not only performance metrics but also high-quality trace data that is traceable, analyzable, and reusable.

This mechanism extends Safactory from a single-round experimental platform to an infrastructure for continuous data asset accumulation. For complex agent tasks such as Computer-Using Agents, real interaction trajectories are of great value. They can be utilized for behavioral imitation in supervised fine-tuning, provide rollout data for reinforcement learning, and further support failure case analysis, experience extraction, safety alignment, and capability boundary diagnosis. Equipped with a unified data management module, Safactory structurally persists trajectories generated across diverse environments, models, and tasks, significantly reducing the cost of data sorting and reuse after multiple rounds of experiments.

4.2
System Architecture

The core design of the system follows a triple logic of ”decoupled storage and computing, intelligent governance closed-loop, and compliance with trustworthiness,” aiming to construct an intelligent hub that supports the full-lifecycle management of PB-scale security data assets.

Figure 14
:
Architecture of the Data Infrastructure and Trustworthy Data Intelligence Engine.

The underlying architecture relies on the LanceDB Serverless system to build a high-performance multi-modal data lakehouse, addressing the storage challenges of heterogeneous and unstructured data generated by the security wind tunnel system during massive evaluation processes. This storage foundation achieves the decoupling of the Persistent layer, Object Storage, and multi-dimensional indexing layers through a tiered design. It not only supports the reliable storage and computation of ultra-large-scale data but also ensures low-latency data access via a self-developed Multi-layer Cache mechanism. This enables the platform to support a spectrum of queries ranging from simple business tag filtering to complex semantic-level similarity recall, providing precise, versioned, and reproducible data slices for upstream model training.

Sitting atop the physical storage, the data platform layer acts as a ”data asset factory,” responsible for transforming raw, scattered deduction logs into high-value training assets. The platform defines a Unified Schema to achieve comprehensive standardization and alignment of core data, including events, samples, traces, and evaluation results. To shorten the cycle from data ingestion to availability, the system implements a batch-stream integrated ingestion framework that supports second-level data reporting and batch processing via an SDK. Within this process, a series of highly customized ETL jobs automatically complete data pre-processing, format cleaning, and labeling. Furthermore, the platform is equipped with a comprehensive management and operations toolchain, covering lineage tracking, metadata lifecycle management, and hot/cold tiered storage strategies, ensuring that data assets maintain high-performance retrieval capabilities while achieving an optimal cost-to-performance ratio.

The top layer of the system is driven by the DataElf Trustworthy Data Intelligence Engine, which enables dynamic orchestration and automated execution from natural language user intents to complex governance pipelines. DataElf is not merely an execution engine but an agentic system with self-evolutionary capabilities. It translates ambiguous governance objectives into executable task Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) through an intent interpreter, with specialized agents—namely the Planner, Optimizer, and Judge—collaborating to complete task planning, strategy optimization, and result determination. To address extremely sensitive data environments in security scenarios, the engine introduces Isolated Execution Sandbox technology. All dynamically generated pipelines or code operators run within restricted sandboxes. Through permission declaration verification and I/O isolation mechanisms, the system ensures deep governance and value extraction while keeping data ”usable but invisible.” This architecture allows the system to output auditable and provable governance products without exposing raw privacy samples.

The entire system achieves continuous capability growth through an ”execution-verification-evolution” closed-loop mechanism. The DataElf engine is responsible for both executing predefined tools and deriving new tool operators or optimizing existing pipeline links through the Toolsmith agent based on execution feedback. These derived capabilities undergo rigorous benchmark drills and compliance evaluations within the DataElf Testbed before being promoted to formal system assets by the Capability Manager, thus enriching the base tool library. Through the synergy of the distributed execution foundation and the intelligent orchestration hub, the system achieves heterogeneous power scheduling and streaming task feedback. This ensures that long-running, high-latency tasks possess robust self-repair and checkpoint recovery capabilities in the face of node fluctuations, ultimately forming an end-to-end, automated, and continuously evolving security data governance ecosystem.

4.3.1
Data Value Assessment Tool

The data value assessment tool offers an end-to-end pipeline for judging the training value of a dataset and distilling it into a high-quality subset.
It comprises two complementary stages: a
scoring
stage that assigns quality scores through pluggable scorers, and a
selection
stage that turns these scores into a diverse, high-value subset of the requested size.
Although designed to work together, the two stages are fully decoupled and can also be invoked independently: users may run scoring alone to obtain multi-dimensional quality signals for their downstream workflows, or run selection alone on top of pre-computed scores.

Scoring.

The scoring stage assigns a quality score to every sample in a dataset.
It supports pluggable scoring methods: users can switch between scorers or run multiple scorers on the same dataset to obtain multi-dimensional quality signals.

The platform currently integrates 9 scorers that cover complementary quality dimensions
cai2025opendataarenafairopenarena
.

PPL

jelinek1977perplexity
and
Norm_Loss

measure the target model’s predictability of a training sample from a probabilistic and information-compression perspective:
the more natural and fluent a passage appears to the model, the higher the score, whereas a low score indicates that the sample is unnatural to the model.

IFD

li2024quantity
compares the relative difficulty of generating the same response with and without its instruction, using the gap as a proxy for how much the instruction actually helps: the more it helps, the higher the quality.

DEITA_Q
and
DEITA_C

rate the intrinsic properties of a sample directly: the former focuses on whether the instruction-response pair is clear and accurate, while the latter independently evaluates instruction difficulty and complexity.

adopts the most direct approach, simply prompting an off-the-shelf large language model to judge whether the sample is high-quality.

as the platform’s default scorer:
it converts the

This makes large-scale quality assessment efficient and keeps the cost of extending or combining scorers low.

Selection.

Given the quality scores produced above, the selection stage draws a target number of high-value samples from the dataset.
Naively taking the top-

applies K-means clustering, allocates the overall selection quota across clusters in proportion to their sizes,

This simple yet effective strategy preserves data diversity while keeping the selected subset locally high-quality within every cluster.

4.3.3
Data Security Audit Tool

The data security audit tool is designed to detect security risks across multiple dimensions in post-training datasets, helping practitioners identify and mitigate harmful content, privacy leakage, and adversarial attacks before data enters the training pipeline. Built around a pluggable checker architecture, the tool ships with 23 checkers spanning 13 risk types: harmful content, toxicity, bias, PII leakage, secret leakage, label flipping, factual inconsistency, self-contradiction, instruction mismatch, backdoor injection, prompt injection, jailbreak prompts, and sycophancy. Checkers can be freely composed through configuration files to suit different auditing needs.
The 23 checkers are grouped into four categories based on their detection approach.

Autonomous Evolution Platform

The autonomous evolution platform focuses on the ”continuous enhancement of model capabilities.” It centers around the RL training loop, organizing sample generation and policy updates into an iterative feedback loop: one end continuously integrates environmental feedback and multi-source trajectories, while the other end employs the OPD mechanism to convert usable signals from the teacher model into stable updates. A unified metric system tracks both gains and degradations. The core value of this system lies in its ability to support large-scale asynchronous training tasks: even under conditions with complex data sources and frequent configuration changes, it can consistently perform sample production, assembly, and policy updates, while ensuring the training process is observable, controllable, and reproducible.

5.2
Agentic RL System Architecture

Figure 18
:
Agentic RL System Architecture

The Agentic RL system architecture, as shown in Figure
18
, is designed around three core modules: Bridge, Trajectory Transformation, and Buffer Server. These modules jointly support a fully asynchronous ”Sampling-Trajectory Transformation-Training” pipeline. The architecture separates the rollout and training processes and uses versioned metadata and group consistency mechanisms to ensure that the system can reliably generate high-quality training samples under complex, long-duration, multi-environment concurrent tasks.

5.2.1
Bridge Module: Asynchronous Control and Unified Inference Entry

In traditional reinforcement learning architectures, interactions with the environment, inference requests, and training consumption are often tightly coupled, limiting system scalability and flexibility. In a fully asynchronous setup, issues such as policy version misalignment with sampled data, missing sample groups, trajectory and supervision signal misalignment, and imbalanced production-consumption in data buffers can arise. To address this, the system introduces the LLM Proxy and Rollout Manager at the Bridge layer, forming a closed loop with the Buffer Server: The LLM Proxy writes and returns trajectory and model version metadata in the request path, enabling the training endpoint to perform data staleness filtering and mitigate policy lag. The Rollout Manager samples by instance/group and submits in groups to prevent incomplete sample groups from entering training. The Bridge unifies the routing of LLM sessions and links trajectory recording and transformation processes, reducing the chances of trajectory and supervision signal mismatch. The Buffer Server dequeues data as needed, balancing production and consumption rates to prevent queue buildup and starvation. As a result, Rollout and Train are decoupled temporally, while maintaining consistency in data semantics and quality constraints.

At the same time, the Bridge supports the use of the OPD (Online Policy Distillation) module, which distills policies from the teacher model to real-time optimize the student model’s learning process, improving its generalization and training efficiency. This module works in collaboration with submodules for teacher model policy generation and other training signals to implement policy distillation.

5.2.2
Alignment Module: Semantic Alignment from Trajectories to Trainable Samples

In the Agentic RL system, trajectory data generated by the environment cannot be directly used for training. It needs to go through a series of processing and transform steps to become suitable training samples. The Trajectory Transformation module is designed to address this issue. Through a fine-grained processing pipeline, it converts raw trajectories generated from multi-round interactions into samples that meet training requirements.

The design of the Trajectory Transformation module focuses on ensuring data semantic consistency and correctness. Through incremental encoding and prefix matching of trajectories, the system effectively avoids redundant encoding of historical context while handling multimodal inputs (e.g., image and text data). Additionally, the system ensures that losses are only computed within the areas generated by the model through Mask Painter and Sample Packer, ensuring effective control of data quality during the training process. Ultimately, the samples are packed and annotated with metadata to maintain consistency in batch processing and prevent training bias due to data issues.

5.2.3
Summary

The Agentic RL system achieves asynchronous orchestration through the Bridge module, maintains data lifecycle consistency, ensures semantic correctness of data through the Trajectory Transformation module, and guarantees data supply through the Buffer Server. Together, these modules enable the system to maintain the usability, timeliness, and statistical stability of training data under fully asynchronous conditions, successfully supporting unified training for various task types.

5.3
On-policy Distillation

In the unified architecture above, OPD (On-policy Distillation) is implemented as an optional training route rather than a prerequisite for the system’s establishment. Its primary role is to provide additional distribution constraints and teacher signal correction for current policy updates under asynchronous rollouts and long-range agent interactions, thereby enhancing the consistency, stability, and controllability of the training process. Recent reviews of OPD also highlight that its core idea is to distill from the student’s own accessed state and trajectory distributions, rather than only performing offline imitation from static teacher data. Accordingly, OPD is particularly suited for training scenarios with more apparent asynchronous sampling, policy delays, and distribution shifts.

;

From a system implementation perspective, OPD integrates into the existing five-layer architecture as a plugin, without altering the main execution flow of Safactory. The
teacher bridge

in the figure should be understood as optional extension points: The

In the current implementation, Safactory adopts a white-box, token-level form of OPD. Specifically, the system introduces token-level log probabilities provided by the teacher during the rollout phase and incorporates a reverse-KL regularization term into the training objective to impose additional constraints on the advantage-driven policy updates.

From an architectural perspective, Safactory’s interface can further accommodate other forms of teacher signals, such as step-level, trajectory-level, or higher granularity feedback. This allows Safactory to maintain the system’s simplicity by using the same training base for both Gym-style and agentic scenarios, while also reserving sufficient space for more complex distillation, correction, and auxiliary supervision methods.
xu2026opdsurvey

OPD can be completely disabled in Safactory; however, for agentic tasks with more pronounced issues of asynchronous sampling, long-range decision making, multi-round interactions, and complex credit assignment, OPD can enhance the stability and controllability of the training process without disrupting the main execution flow. This enables Safactory to support both conventional reinforcement learning processes and naturally extend to more complex autonomous evolution training paradigms, all while maintaining a unified system abstraction.

5.4
Experiments

5.4.1
Safactory V.S. slime-native

The Safactory system supports both synchronous and asynchronous rollout execution modes. In terms of architectural design, Safactory explicitly decouples the rollout and training lifecycles: the data producer continuously generates trajectory data and writes it to storage, while the training process is triggered by the Bridge module once a sufficient amount of trainable trajectories has been accumulated. This asynchronous mechanism effectively prevents the ”producer” from idling its computational resources, thereby improving overall resource utilization.

In terms of data management, Safactory introduces a ”trajectory staleness” mechanism to filter samples in the buffer. Staleness is defined as the difference between the policy version used for trajectory generation and the current policy version. For example, when the acceptable staleness is set to 0, only trajectories generated by the current policy can be used for training; when the acceptable staleness is set to 1, trajectories generated by both the current and previous versions of the policy are allowed. This mechanism ensures training stability while allowing developers to customize the combination of off-policy and on-policy data, thereby increasing data reuse efficiency.

The experimental settings included a maximum of 10 environment interaction steps, with a total of 300 training steps. In contrast, Safactory’s fully asynchronous architecture continuously generates trajectories during the training phase, thus preventing GPU resource waste and achieving higher overall system throughput and resource utilization efficiency.

7
Conclusions and Discussions

Safactory is motivated by a simple observation: once large models become autonomous agents, safety can no longer be treated as a single benchmark score or a one-time release check. Many important failures emerge only when agents interact with tools, environments, and long decision chains. As a result, trustworthy agent development requires more than isolated evaluation tools or standalone training frameworks. It requires an infrastructure that can continuously discover risks, preserve evidence, convert execution traces into reusable assets, and feed them back into model improvement.

In this report, we present Safactory as such an infrastructure. Its
Parallel Simulation Platform
moves safety evaluation from static samples to large-scale sandbox-based rollout and risk discovery. Its
Trustworthy Data Platform
turns trajectories, rewards, and intermediate logs into structured, auditable, and retrievable data assets, while supporting safety auditing and experience extraction. Its
Autonomous Evolution Platform
further connects these assets to asynchronous reinforcement learning and online policy optimization, making it possible to organize rollout, data transformation, and policy update in a unified loop. Supported by DeepLink computing technologies, Safactory also provides the computing, storage, and system foundations needed to run this loop at scale.

From this perspective, the main contribution of Safactory is not a single algorithm or benchmark. Rather, it is a system view of trustworthy autonomous intelligence:
risk discovery, data deposition, capability repair, and re-validation should be designed as one continuous process
. This design helps bridge the long-standing gap between evaluation, data, and training, and provides a practical foundation for building safer and more capable agents in OS, mobile, embodied, and other interactive environments.

At the same time, Safactory should be viewed as a starting point rather than a finished solution. Several challenges remain open. First, environment coverage and scenario realism still need to expand, especially for high-stakes domains with richer human, tool, and system interactions. Second, automatically judging agent safety and utility in long-horizon tasks remains difficult, and stronger metrics and oversight mechanisms are still needed. Third, turning execution traces into stable training gains is still an evolving problem, especially under asynchronous sampling, distribution shift, and complex credit assignment. We hope Safactory can serve as a shared foundation for future work on scalable agent evaluation, trustworthy data construction, and continual post-training for autonomous systems.
