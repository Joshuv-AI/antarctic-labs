An Improved Bipartition Cover Bound for the Multispecies Coalescent Model
Zachary McNulty

Abstract
Bipartition cover probabilities quantify whether a collection of gene trees contains every bipartition of the underlying species tree, a condition that underlies finite-sample guarantees for summary methods such as ASTRAL. We study this problem under the multispecies coalescent (MSC) model and derive topology-free upper bounds on the number of loci needed to obtain a bipartition cover with prescribed confidence, improving upon the existing bounds of 20 . Practically, our bounds remain below biologically realistic numbers of loci across a substantially broader range of parameter settings, expanding their usefulness for empirical datasets. Theoretically, our analysis sharpens our understanding of coalescence under the MSC model and develops new asymptotics for these bounds and absorption times under Kingman’s coalescent in the natural short branch regime. We further compare our new bounds with existing work using simulations under a variety of different species-tree topologies.



Keywords
bipartition cover; multispecies coalescent model; species-tree inference; ASTRAL

Background

Consensus methods have grown in popularity in phylogenetics as large genomic datasets have revealed the limits of single-gene tree inference. Evolutionary processes such as incomplete lineage sorting, gene duplication and loss, and horizontal gene transfer can cause individual gene trees to differ from the true species tree. Consensus and summary-based methods aim to reconcile this discordance by aggregating information across many loci, providing statistically consistent estimates of species relationships under realistic models of gene tree variation.

The goal of consensus methods is to aggregate information across these gene trees to produce an estimate of the species tree. But even for a relatively small number of species, the space of possible species tree topologies is intractably large. Because of this, it is often necessary to reduce the search space somehow. One common strategy is to restrict to species trees whose set of bipartitions are contained in the set of bipartitions generated by the gene trees, possibly expanding this set with some collection of heuristics. This is the case for many modern phylogenetic algorithms including the popular ASTRAL
11
and its extensions ASTRAL II
12
, ASTRAL III
24
, and wASTRAL
23
.

In the case the true species tree lies in this restricted space, we say the gene trees form a
bipartition cover
of the species tree. When this occurs, ASTRAL comes with some strong finite-sample guarantees
19
. When it does not occur, ASTRAL provides no guarantees on how the estimated tree will relate to the true species tree. As a result, it is important to know how likely such a bipartition cover occurs. Since the species tree topology is unknown at inference time, the paper by
20
attempts to answer this question in a topology-free way, developing a bound that is a function only of two simple parameters: the number of species and the minimum branch length of the species tree.

In this paper, we identify a few areas where the bound of
20
is lossy and develop some topology-free improvements. Our main contributions come from careful analysis of various “worst-cases” of the MSC model, species tree topologies that somehow make coalescence difficult and certain bipartitions challenging to recover. Beyond this, since all these bounds are complicated functions of the underlying parameters, we develop some asymptotics to describe the growth rates of these bounds as functions of these parameters in a few natural regimes.

1.1
The Multispecies Coalescent Model

We start with a brief discussion of the
multispecies coalescent (MSC) model
, which is the probabilistic model underlying all the theoretical guarantees of ASTRAL and many other phylogenetic inference algorithms. The MSC model describes how gene tree topologies are generated from a given species tree
(
22
;
16
)
. It is in a sense the simplest model that can explain the phenomenon of
incomplete lineage sorting (ILS)
, the observation that the most common gene tree topology can differ from the species tree topology. This phenomenon makes species tree inference difficult: the intuitive “majority vote” estimator is not consistent. Figure
1
gives an example of the MSC and illustrates how gene tree topologies can differ from the species tree.

The MSC model is essentially just a constrained version of Kingman’s coalescent: going backwards in time along each branch of the species tree, we assume the surviving gene lineages coalesce according to Kingman’s coalescent. Namely, each pair of lineages coalesce at rate one. More concretely, let:

(1.1)

g_{ij}(T):=\sum_{k=j}^{i}\frac{e^{-{k\choose 2}T}(2k-1)(-1)^{k-j}j_{(k-1)}i_{[k]}}{j!(k-j)!i_{(k)}}

be the probability that
i
lineages coalesce into exactly
j
lineages in time
T
under Kingman’s coalescent. Here
a_{(k)}=a(a+1)\cdot...\cdot(a+k-1)
and
a_{[k]}=a(a-1)\cdot...\cdot(a-k+1)
denote the rising and falling factorial respectively, and the time
T
is measured in coalescent units. The function
g_{ij}(T)
is the basis of the original bound in
20
and all the new bounds we develop. In the setting of the MSC model,
T
represents the length of a given branch of the species tree, and
g_{ij}
describes the distribution of the number of lineages that remain uncoalesced at the end of that branch.

Our work relies on careful analysis of various “worst-case” topologies when it comes to phylogenetic inference. For this purpose, we define two tree types: a

A

These two tree topologies represent opposite extremes for coalescent behavior: one maximally balanced and the other maximally unbalanced. Caterpillar trees present a combinatorial bottleneck for phylogenetic inference, as many of their bipartitions involve very large subsets of taxa. Balanced trees, however, present a more subtle and severe difficulty: they induce a
coalescent bottleneck
, in which lineages are so evenly dispersed that coalescence is systematically delayed.

This distinction is made precise in Lemma
2.1
, which identifies caterpillar trees as extremal for increasing functions of descendant counts, and in Lemma
2.8
, which shows that balanced trees are stochastically worst-case for the number of surviving lineages under the multispecies coalescent. Together, these lemmas underpin our main result, which appears as Theorem
2.9
below:

Theorem
.

If we have a species tree with
k
species and minimum branch length
T_{min}
, then:

\inf\left\{n\geqslant 0:\sum_{\ell=2}^{k-2}(1-\mathbb{E}g_{X_{\ell},1}(T_{min}))^{n}\leqslant 1-q\right\}\leqslant\frac{\log\left(\frac{1-q}{k-3}\right)}{\log(1-\mathbb{E}g_{X_{k-2},1}(T_{min}))}

gene trees sampled independently under the MSC model form a bipartition cover with probability at least
q
. Here
X_{\ell}
is number of lineages reaching the root of the balanced tree with
\ell
leaves and all branches length
T_{min}
.

Due to the recursive structure of a balanced tree, these expectations are easy to compute in a dynamic fashion. Empirically, we observe the improvement can sometimes be several orders of magnitude. Moreover, through careful asymptotic analysis of the coalescent probabilities
g_{k,1}(T)
, we will show in some regimes this improves upon the original bound by a factor of
O(T^{-1})
asymptotically. Lemma
4.22
below makes this precise.

Since we want to avoid making any assumptions about the topology of the species tree, we will not assume the values

are known. As we will see in Lemma

Lemma 2.1

(Caterpillars Maximize Increasing Sums)
.

Suppose
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k-3}
are the descendant counts of our rooted binary tree
T
. Moreover, suppose

is nondecreasing. Then
\sum_{i}f(\alpha_{i})
is maximized when
T
is the caterpillar tree, in which case
\{\alpha_{i}\}=\{2,3,...,k-2\}
.

The proof of Lemma
2.1
can be found in the Appendix. There, we also prove Lemma
4.15

2.1
, which shows balanced trees are the minimizers (at least when

is also convex), adding to the idea that balanced and caterpillar trees are the two main extremes of this space of trees. From this result, we get the immediate corollary:

Corollary 2.2
.

Suppose
h:\mathbb{N}\to[0,1]
is decreasing and
\mathbb{P}(E_{i,1})\geqslant h(k_{i})
. Then:

\mathbb{P}(E_{n})\geqslant 1-\sum_{\ell=2}^{k-2}\Big[1-h(\ell)\Big]^{n}.

Proof of
2.2
.

By union bound and independence of the gene trees:

1-\mathbb{P}(E_{n})\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k-3}(1-\mathbb{P}(E_{i,n}))=\sum_{i=1}^{k-3}\Big[1-\mathbb{P}(E_{i,1})\Big]^{n}\leqslant\sum_{i=1}^{k-3}\Big[1-h(k_{i})\Big]^{n}.

As
h
is decreasing and bounded in

f(x)=[1-h(x)]^{n}
is increasing. Applying Lemma
2.1
to this

gives the desired result.
∎

Theorem 2.3
.

Note that if

Corollary 2.4

(Caterpillar Bipartition Cover Bound)

If we have a species tree with

(2.2)

M_{c}(k,T_{min}):=\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\sum_{\ell=2}^{k-2}\left[1-g_{\ell,1}(T_{min})\right]^{n}\leqslant 1-q\right\}

2.2
A Second Improvement: Accounting for Deeper Coalescent Events

Theorem
2.3
suggests one avenue for improvement is to just get a tighter bound on
\mathbb{P}(E_{i,1})\geqslant h(k_{i})
than choosing
h(x)=g_{x,1}(T_{min})
. As we discussed above, for small values of
T_{min}
the bound in Theorem
2.4
is dominated by the terms
g_{k_{i},1}
for which
k_{i}
are quite large. These are the bipartitions corresponding to edges
e
close to the root of the tree which have many descendants. In
20
, to develop the bound
1.1
the authors essentially assume
none
of the descendants coalesce before they reach edge
e
. This is a bit pessimistic, especially when
e
is far from the leaves: in this setting, descendants have a long distance to travel through the tree and potentially coalesce.

Hence to improve this bound we should try to account for coalescent events that occur below the edge
e
. Namely, we ought to somehow replace
g_{k_{e},1}(T_{min})
by
\mathbb{E}g_{X_{e},1}(T_{min})
where
X_{e}
is the (random) number of remaining lineages entering edge
e
. Clearly
\mathbb{P}(E_{i,1})\geqslant\mathbb{E}g_{X_{e_{i}},1}(T_{min})
by the same logic Theorem
1.1
employs. However,
X_{e}
will of course depend on the topology of the species tree below the edge

Recall that random variable
X
is said to
first-order stochastically dominate

Y

X\geqslant_{st}Y

\mathbb{P}(X>t)\geqslant\mathbb{P}(Y>t)

\mathbb{E}u(X)\geqslant\mathbb{E}u(Y)
for all nondecreasing functions

. It is said to

\mathbb{E}u(X)\geqslant\mathbb{E}u(Y)
for all nondecreasing concave functions, and denote this by
X\geqslant_{sst}Y
. First-order stochastic dominance naturally implies second-order, but not conversely.

Since
x\mapsto g_{x,1}(T)
is decreasing by Corollary
4.5
, and convex by Lemma

, we can lower bound
\mathbb{E}g_{X_{e},1}(T_{min})
by
\mathbb{E}g_{U_{e},1}(T_{min})
where
U_{e}\geqslant_{sst}X_{e}
is any second-order stochastic upper bound for the number of lineages entering
e
. This observation is so important we list it as a Lemma. It is an immediate consequence of these observations and our caterpillar bound Theorem
2.3
:

Lemma 2.5
.

Suppose

. Then
\mathbb{P}(E_{i,1})\geqslant\mathbb{E}g_{U_{e_{i}},1}(T_{min})
. In particular, if

Here we are choosing

2.3
A Final Improvement: Going All the Way Down the Tree

Lemma
2.5
shows that we can improve our bipartition cover bound by developing stochastic upper bounds
X_{e}\leqslant_{st}U_{e}
, where
X_{e}
is the number of remaining lineages actually entering
e
. This of course depends on the topology of the species tree, but we observed if we take the worst-case topology we can get a bound that works over all trees.

In the last section, we generated our upper bound
U_{e}
by only marginalizing over the vertices immediately below
e
and ignoring all deeper coalescents. There, we saw that the in some sense the worst-case was when descendants were evenly split across the two subtrees. Here, we extend the results to account for coalescents occuring anywhere below the edge
e
. By iterating our previous observation, it is natural to believe the “worst-case” occurs when
all
the splits are even, namely when the topology below the edge
e
is the balanced tree. This is the content of the following lemma.

Lemma 2.8
.

Given a binary tree
\mathcal{T}
(with branch lengths) let
X_{\mathcal{T}}
denote the number of lineages that have not coalesced by the time they reach the root. If
\mathcal{B}_{k}
is the balanced tree with
k
leaves and all branch lengths equal to
T_{min}
then
X_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}\geqslant_{st}X_{\mathcal{T}}
for any other tree
\mathcal{T}
with
k
leaves and minimum branch length
T_{min}
.

A proof of the lemma is provided in the Appendix, reducing the result to an extension of Lemma
2.6
which allows for random subpopulation sizes. Lemma
2.8
suggests a natural way of upper-bounding the number of lineages
X_{e}
entering a given edge
e
: in the worst-case the subtree below edge
e
is balanced, so
X_{e}\leqslant_{st}X_{\mathcal{B}_{k_{e}}}
. From this, we get our final bound.

Theorem 2.9

(Balanced Bipartition Cover Bound)
.

If we have a species tree with
k
species and a set
\mathcal{G}
of at least:

(2.7)

M_{b}(k,T_{min}):=\inf\left\{n\in\mathbb{N}:\sum_{\ell=2}^{k-2}\left[1-w_{\ell}\right]^{n}\leqslant 1-q\right\}

gene trees sampled independently under the MSC model, where:

(2.8)

w_{\ell}:=\mathbb{P}(W_{\ell}=1)

and where the distributions of
W_{\ell}:=Z_{X_{\mathcal{B}_{\ell}}}
are defined recursively as:

\mathbb{P}(W_{\ell}=j\;|\;\{W_{i}\}_{i<\ell})=g_{W_{\lceil\ell/2\rceil}+W_{\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor}^{\prime},j}(T_{min}),\qquad W_{1}\equiv 1,

then
\mathcal{G}
forms a bipartition cover with probability at least
q
. Here
W_{i}^{\prime}
is an iid copy of
W_{i}
and
T_{min}
is the minimum branch length (in coalescent units) in the species tree.

Reduction to Lemma
2.8
.

First, we show the recursion above does produce the distribution of
W_{\ell}=Z_{X_{\mathcal{B}_{\ell}}}
. We do this by induction on
\ell
. The base case of
\ell=1
is trivial as
W_{1}=Z_{X_{1}}=Z_{1}\equiv 1
. For the inductive step, note that the balanced tree with
\ell
leaves has subtrees with
\lceil\ell/2\rceil
and
\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor
leaves respectively. By induction,
W_{\lceil\ell/2\rceil},W_{\lfloor\ell/2\rfloor}
give the distribution of lineages exiting these subtrees respectively. Together, these
W_{\lceil i/2\rceil}+W_{\lfloor i/2\rfloor}^{\prime}
lineages traverse one more branch of length
T_{min}
, giving the above recurrence.

Lemma
2.8
above would imply that
X_{e}\leqslant_{st}W_{k_{e}}
so by Lemma
2.5
it suffices to show that
W_{\ell}
is stochastically increasing in
\ell
. But again this is obvious via the same coupling argument as before: the tree
\mathcal{B}_{\ell+1}
can be generated from the tree
\mathcal{B}_{\ell}
by adding a single new leaf (and branch) at the bottom of the tree, without any other topological changes. By introducing new lineages at the bottom of the tree, we can only make the number of lineages exiting the root stochastically larger. Formally, we can couple the exponential clocks determining the coalescent events in
W_{\ell}
and
W_{\ell+1}
exactly as before in our proof of Theorem
2.7
.
∎

Note that because the subtrees of a balanced tree are balanced, we were able to calculate the quantities
w_{\ell}
in a recursive fashion. This makes the bound above reasonable to compute. In our asymptotic analysis below, we will discuss the following convenient upper bound:

M_{b}(k,T)\leqslant\frac{\log\left(\frac{1-q}{k-3}\right)}{\log\left(1-g_{\mathbb{E}X_{k-2},1}(T)\right)}.

Hence our analysis has allowed us to replace
k-2
by
\mathbb{E}X_{k-2}
, which depending on
T
may be a large improvement.

Observe that by incorporating coalescent events deeper in the subtrees, the balanced bound of Lemma
2.9
must strictly improve upon the one-step bound Theorem
2.7
. This in turn must, by construction, improve upon our original improved bound Corollary
2.4
, which itself strictly improves the original Theorem
1.1
. In the next section, we look to explore the level to which each step in this chain improves the overall bound.

Here we confirm our intuition that our new bounds drastically improve performance in the small

Lemma 3.1

(Asymptotics of

)
.

The function

(3.1)

\begin{cases}1-g_{k,1}(T)\sim\frac{3(k-1)}{k+1}e^{-T},&T\uparrow\infty,\\

Moreover, for fixed
T

g_{k,1}(T)\downarrow\mathbb{P}(S_{\infty}\leqslant T)=:s(T)
where
S_{\infty}:=\sum_{i=2}^{\infty}\tau_{i}
for
\tau_{i}\sim\mathrm{Exp}\left({i\choose 2}\right)
independent. Furthermore, the gap
\Delta_{k}(T):=g_{k,1}(T)-s(T)
satisfies:

\Delta_{k}(T)=\frac{2f_{S_{\infty}}(T)}{k}+o\left(\frac{1}{k}\right)\sim\frac{2f_{S_{\infty}}(T)}{k}.

Using this, we develop the following asymptotics for our two bounds. Recall that
M_{o}(k,T)
denotes the original bound of

and
M_{b}(k,T)
our balanced bound (Theorem
2.9
).

Lemma 3.2

(Bound Asymptotics)
.

Let
\kappa_{k,q}:=\log\left(\frac{k-3}{1-q}\right)
and
u(T):=\frac{2-e^{-T/2}}{1-e^{-T/2}}

M_{o}(k,T)\sim\begin{cases}\frac{\kappa_{q,k}}{T},&T\uparrow\infty,\\
\frac{2^{k-3}\kappa_{q,k}}{(k-2)!T^{k-3}},&T=o(k^{-2}),\\
\frac{\kappa_{q,k}}{-\log(1-s(T))},&T\text{ fixed},k\to\infty\end{cases}

M_{b}(k,T)\lesssim\frac{\kappa_{q,k}}{-\log(1-g_{u(T),1}(T))},\qquad T\text{ fixed},k\to\infty

Moreover, in the fixed
T

\frac{\log(1-g_{u(T),1}(T))}{\log(1-s(T))}\sim\frac{\pi^{2}}{2T}

To see why the

growth rate is natural for the

is roughly constant and equal to

for all large

\mathbb{P}(\mathrm{Geo}(s)>m)=(1-s)^{m}

we see

4.1
Basic Properties Log-Concavity

A sequence
\{x_{k}\}
is
log-concave
if
x_{k}^{2}\geqslant x_{k-1}x_{k+1}
. We say a random variable
X
is log-concave if the sequence
x_{k}=\mathbb{P}(X=k)
is log-concave.

In order to apply Theorem
4.12
, we will need to determine various variables of interest are log-concave. For this purpose, we introduce a few basic closure properties of log-concave random variables. It will turn out to be useful to work with a strengthening of log-concavity. We say a sequence
\{x_{k}\}
is
ultra log-concave (ULC)
if the sequence
\{k!\cdot x_{k}\}
is log-concave, or equivalently
kx_{k}^{2}\geqslant(k+1)x_{k-1}x_{k+1}
. Clearly ultra log-concavity implies log-concavity. The following result, and much more, can be found in Chapter 4 of
17
.

Theorem 4.1

(Prékopa)
.

If
X,Y
are independent, (ultra) log-concave, integer-valued random variables, then
X+Y
is (ultra) log-concave. Equivalently, the class of (ultra) log-concave distributions is closed under convolution.

The following shows us that Kingman’s coalescent preserves log-concavity in our setting of interest.

Theorem 4.2

(Preservation of Ultra Log-Concavity)
.

Suppose
P_{t}
is the transition semigroup of a pure-death process with death rates
\lambda_{i}
so that (a)
\lambda_{i}
is convex (b)
\eta_{i}:=\frac{\lambda_{i}}{i}
is concave and (c) at least one of these are strictly convex/concave. Moreover, suppose that
\pi
is ultra log-concave with either
\mathrm{supp}(\pi)=\{2,...,M\}
or
\mathrm{supp}(\pi)=\{1,...,M\}
for some
M\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
. Then
\pi(t):=\pi P_{t}
is ultra log-concave for all
t\geqslant 0
. In particular, this is true for Kingman’s coalescent with
\lambda_{i}={i\choose 2}
.

Proof of Theorem
4.2
.

Recall Kolmogorov’s forward equations imply:

\frac{d}{dt}\pi_{i}(t)=\lambda_{i+1}\pi_{i+1}(t)-\lambda_{i}\pi_{i}(t).

First, we handle the interior. For
i\in\mathrm{Int}(\mathrm{supp}(\pi))
we can define the ratios:

v_{i}:=\lambda_{i}\cdot\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{i-1}},\qquad r_{i}:=\frac{i\pi_{i}^{2}}{(i+1)\pi_{i-1}\pi_{i+1}}=\frac{v_{i}}{v_{i+1}}\cdot\frac{\eta_{i+1}}{\eta_{i}}.

From here, using the forward equations we can see:

\displaystyle\frac{\dot{r_{i}}}{r_{i}}

\displaystyle=\frac{d}{dt}\log(r_{i})

\displaystyle=2\frac{\dot{\pi_{i}}}{\pi_{i}}-\frac{\dot{\pi}_{i-1}}{\pi_{i-1}}-\frac{\dot{\pi}_{i+1}}{\pi_{i+1}}

\displaystyle=\left(-2\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{i-1}+\lambda_{i+1}\right)+2\lambda_{i+1}\frac{\pi_{i+1}}{\pi_{i}}-\lambda_{i}\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{i-1}}-\lambda_{i+2}\frac{\pi_{i+2}}{\pi_{i+1}}

\displaystyle=\left(\lambda_{i-1}-2\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{i+1}\right)+2v_{i+1}-v_{i}-v_{i+2}.

By assumption,
r_{i}(0)\geqslant 1
for all
i
. Since
r_{i}(t)
is continuous in
t
, the only way for
r_{i}(t)<1
to occur is if
r_{i}(s)=1
for some
0\leqslant s<t
. We will show at this time that
\dot{r}_{i}(s)>0
, and hence we can never drop below one. Suppose
r_{i}(s)=1
and
r_{k}(s)\geqslant 1
for all other
k
. From these, we see respectively:

v_{i}=\frac{\eta_{i}}{\eta_{i+1}}\cdot v_{i+1},\qquad v_{i+2}\leqslant\frac{\eta_{i+2}}{\eta_{i+1}}\cdot v_{i+1}.

Using these two bounds, we can see:

\frac{\dot{r}_{i}}{r_{i}}\geqslant(\lambda_{i-1}-2\lambda_{i}+\lambda_{i+1})-\left[\eta_{i}-2\eta_{i+1}+\eta_{i+2}\right]\cdot\frac{v_{i+1}}{\eta_{i+1}}.

Since
\lambda_{i}
is convex, the first term is nonnegative. Since
\eta_{i}
is concave, the second term is nonnegative. Moreover, by assumption one of these must be strictly positive, and thus
\frac{\dot{r}_{i}}{r_{i}}>0
as desired.

Now, we handle the boundaries. In the case
M<\infty
, we trivially have
i\pi_{M}(t)\geqslant(M+1)\pi_{M-1}(t)\pi_{M+1}(t)=0
as
\pi_{M+1}(t)=0
for all
t
– in a pure-death process on
\mathbb{N}
the support can never increase. For the lower boundary, first consider the case
\mathrm{supp}(\pi)=\{1,...,M\}
. Then again we trivially have
\pi_{1}(t)\geqslant 2\pi_{0}(t)\pi_{2}(t)=0
for all
t
as we can never drop below one in a pure-death process on
\mathbb{N}
. In the case
\mathrm{supp}(\pi)=\{2,...,M\}
note that
2\pi_{2}(0)>3\pi_{1}(0)\pi_{3}(0)=0
. By continuity, we must have
2\pi_{2}(t)>3\pi_{1}(t)\pi_{3}(t)
for small enough
t>0
. For such a
t
, we see that
\pi(t)
is ultra log-concave. Moreover, since
\pi_{1}(t)>0
for any positive
t
we see
\mathrm{supp}(\pi(t))=\{1,...,M\}
so we can now apply the previous case to show ultra log-concavity is preserved for all further times.
∎

4.2
Basic Properties of
g_{i,j}(T)
and
Z^{T}_{i}

We will start by proving some basic properties about the function
g_{i,j}(T)
and the associated random variables
Z_{i}^{T}
with distribution
\mathbb{P}(Z_{i}^{T}=j)=g_{i,j}(T)
. Recall that
Z^{T}_{i}
gives the distribution of the number of lineages remaining after starting with
i

T
seconds.

Moreover, recall
X_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}
denotes the number of lineages reaching the root of a balanced tree with
k
leaves and branch lengths all equal to
T
. For convenience, we denote
X_{k}:=X_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}
. Using the above, we will show that all the variables of interest in this paper are log-concave.

Lemma 4.3
.

The random variables
X_{m},Z_{X_{m}}
are ultra log-concave, and hence log-concave.

Proof.

We will prove this by induction on
m
. Trivially
X_{1}=Z_{1}\equiv 1
are ultra log-concave. Now suppose for induction that
X_{i},Z_{X_{i}}
are log-concave for
i<m
. Note:

X_{m}=Z_{X_{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}}+Z_{X_{\lceil m/2\rceil}}.

By Prékopa’s Theorem
4.1
, the sum of independent ultra log-concave distributions is ultra log-concave, so
X_{m}
is ultra log-concave. Since
\mathrm{supp}(X_{m})=\{2,...,m\}
, by Theorem
4.2
, Kingman’s coalescent preserves ultra log-concavity, so
Z_{X_{m}}
is ultra log-concave. This completes the induction.
∎

Now we show our variables
Z_{i}^{T}
have some simple monotonicity properties.

Lemma 4.4
.

Let
i\leqslant j
and
t\leqslant T
. Then
Z_{i}^{T}\leqslant_{st}Z_{j}^{T}
and
Z_{i}^{T}\leqslant_{st}Z_{i}^{t}
.

Heuristically this just states the obvious: the more lineages we start with at the beginning of a branch and the less time we have to coalesce, the more lineages we tend to end with.

Proof of Lemma
4.4
.

Again the fact the function is decreasing in
T
follows immediately via a coupling argument. To see its increasing in
i
, we just condition on the first coalescence event. Assume
z\leqslant i
, otherwise both
\mathbb{P}(Z_{i}^{T}\geqslant z)=\mathbb{P}(Z_{i-1}\geqslant z)=0
. Note if
\tau\sim\exp\left({i\choose 2}\right)
is the time of the first coalescent event:

\mathbb{P}(Z_{i}^{T}\geqslant z)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(Z_{i-1}^{T-t}\geqslant z)\tau(dt)\geqslant\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(Z_{i-1}^{T}\geqslant z)\;\tau(dt)=\mathbb{P}(Z_{i-1}^{T}\geqslant z)

where we let
\mathbb{P}(Z^{T}_{i-1}\geqslant z)=1
if
T<0
. This gives
Z_{i}^{T}\geqslant Z_{i-1}^{T}
stochastically as desired.
∎

Note that
g_{i1}(T)=1-\mathbb{P}(Z_{i}^{T}\geqslant 2)
, from which we get the immediate corollary:

Corollary 4.5
.

The function
g_{i1}(T)
is decreasing in
i
and increasing in
T
.

Thus
g_{i,j}(T)
has some natural monotonicity properties. The following Lemmas show that most of the variability in
g_{i,j}(T)
occurs when both
T
and
i
are small.

Lemma 4.6
.

For fixed
n,k
, the function
\mathbb{P}(Z^{T}_{n}=k)=g_{n,k}(T)
is log-concave in
T
.

Proof of Lemma
4.6
.

We prove this by induction on
n
. For
n=1
we see
g_{nk}(T)=\mathbf{1}\{k=1\}
which is trivially log-concave. For the inductive step, recall that by conditioning on the first coalescent event and using the memoryless property of Kingman’s coalescent, we observed:

g_{n,k}(T)=\int_{0}^{T}g_{n-1,k}(T-t){n\choose 2}\exp\left(-{n\choose 2}t\right)\;dt.

Namely, we are just convolving the log-concave function
g_{n-1,k}
with the log concave function
\exp(-{n\choose 2}t)
. By Prékopa’s theorem
4.1
, convolutions preserve log-concavity. Thus
g_{n,k}
is log-concave.
∎

Lemma 4.7
.

For fixed
T
, the function
i\mapsto g_{i,1}(T)
is convex.

Proof of Lemma
4.7
.

It is a classical result of Karlin that the transition matrix
P_{T}:=[g_{i,j}(T)]_{i,j\in\mathbb{N}}
is totally positive (of any order) for any fixed
T
. See for example
6
. Theorem 2 of the paper
5
, again a result of Karlin, then shows such matrices preserve convexity. Since
e_{1}=(1,0,0,...)
is convex and
(g_{i,1}(T))=P_{T}e_{1}
this implies the sequence
\{g_{i,1}(T)\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is convex.
∎

Next, we study the expected number of lineages that survive under Kingman’s coalescent when the number of starting lineages is large. Since the initial coalescent rate
{i\choose 2}
grows so fast in the number of lineages, eventually adding more lineages has barely any influence on the number remaining at time
T
. This is essentially the well-known observation that Kingman’s coalescent can come down from infinity in any positive time.

Lemma 4.8

(Upper Bound for Expected Number of Lineages)
.

For any
i\in N
and
T>0
:

\mathbb{E}Z_{i}^{T}\leqslant\frac{1}{1-(1-1/i)\exp(-\frac{T}{2})}\leqslant\frac{1}{1-\exp\left(-\frac{T}{2}\right)}.

In particular, for any fixed
T>0
we see
\mathbb{E}Z_{i}^{T}=\Theta_{T}(1)
.

Note that in the small
T
regime, where
\exp(-T/2)\approx 1-T/2
, this bound is roughly
\frac{2}{T}
, recovering the well-known asymptotics of Kingman’s coalescent as it comes down from infinity. Hence in a sense this bound is roughly tight in that regime.

Proof of Lemma
4.8
.

Kolmogorov’s backwards equations followed by Jensen’s inequality implies:

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}Z_{i}^{t}\bigg|_{t=T}=\mathbb{E}\left(-{Z_{i}^{T}\choose 2}\right)\leqslant-{\mathbb{E}Z_{i}^{T}\choose 2}.

Hence letting
z(t):=\mathbb{E}Z^{T}_{i}
we see
z(t)
satisfies:

\dot{z}(t)\leqslant-\frac{z(z-1)}{2},\qquad z(0)=i.

Let
\dot{y}=-y(y-1)/2
with
y(0)=i
. Applying separation of variables:

y(t)=\frac{1}{1-\left(1-1/i\right)e^{-t/2}}.

Since
\dot{z}\leqslant\dot{y}
and
z(0)=y(0)
, applying the comparison principle yields:

z(t)\leqslant y(t)\leqslant\frac{1}{1-e^{-t/2}},

as we desired.
∎

In particular, in the setting of our one-step bound (Theorem

\mathbb{E}(Z^{T}_{\lceil k/2\rceil}+Z^{T}_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor})\leqslant\frac{2}{1-\exp(-T/2)}

Corollary 4.9

Suppose that

4.3
Basic Properties of Stochastic Orderings

The following results are just simple general properties of stochastic orderings which we have sometimes reinterpreted in our current setting. The book
18
contains more information on properties of such stochastic orderings, far beyond what we cover here.

4.3.1
First-Order Stochastic Dominance

In this section, we prove some lemmas relating to first-order stochastic dominance and the random variables
Z^{T}_{i}
. Below we typically suppress the
T
notation unless it is explicitly necessary. The following generalizes Lemma
4.4
by allowing for the number of initial lineages to be random.

Corollary 4.10

(Stochastic Dominance of Compositions)
.

If
X\geqslant_{st}Y
then:

Z_{X}\geqslant_{st}Z_{Y}.

Moreover, if
X^{\prime}\geqslant_{st}Y^{\prime}
and
X\perp\!\!\!\perp Y,X^{\prime}\perp\!\!\!\perp Y^{\prime}
, or more generally if
(X,Y)\geqslant_{st}(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
coordinate-wise then:

Z_{X}+Z_{Y}\geqslant_{st}Z_{X^{\prime}}+Z_{Y^{\prime}}.

In our context, the first statement above just says that if we tend to have more lineages entering a given edge of our tree, then we tend to also have more lineages exiting it. The second statement says that if an edge
e
tends to have more lineages uncoalesced in both its subtrees, then the edge
e
tends to have more lineages entering it.

Proof of Corollary
4.10
.

For the first statement, note that:

\mathbb{P}(Z_{X}\geqslant t)=\mathbb{E}_{i\sim X}\mathbb{P}(Z_{i}\geqslant t)\geqslant\mathbb{E}_{i\sim Y}\mathbb{P}(Z_{i}\geqslant t)=\mathbb{P}(Z_{Y}\geqslant t),

as by Lemma
4.4
the function
i\mapsto\mathbb{P}(Z_{i}\geqslant t)
is increasing. Hence
Z_{X}\geqslant Z_{Y}
stochastically. The second claim follows immediately by conditioning on
Z_{X}
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(Z_{X}+Z_{Y}\geqslant t)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{z\sim Z_{X}}\mathbb{P}(Z_{Y}\geqslant t-z)

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbb{E}_{z\sim Z_{X}}\mathbb{P}(Z_{Y^{\prime}}\geqslant t-z)

\displaystyle\geqslant\mathbb{E}_{z\sim Z_{X^{\prime}}}\mathbb{P}(Z_{Y^{\prime}}\geqslant t-z)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(Z_{X^{\prime}}+Z_{Y^{\prime}}\geqslant t)

so that
Z_{X}+Z_{Y}\geqslant_{st}Z_{X^{\prime}}+Z_{Y^{\prime}}
. The joint statement follows similarly just from the fact
\varphi(a,b):=\mathbb{P}(Z_{a}+Z_{b}\geqslant t)
is trivially increasing coordinate-wise.
∎

The following tells us that increasing mixtures of increasing variables are again increasing.

Lemma 4.11
.

Suppose that we have collections of random variables
\{V_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}},\{\Theta_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
so that for each
i\leqslant j
we have:

a)

\Theta_{i}\leqslant_{st}\Theta_{j}
,

b)

V_{i}\mid\{\Theta_{i}=\theta\}\leqslant_{st}V_{j}\mid\{\Theta_{j}=\theta\}
for every fixed
\theta
,

c)

V_{i}\mid\{\Theta_{i}=\theta\}\leqslant_{st}V_{i}\mid\{\Theta_{i}=\theta^{\prime}\}
for every
\theta\leqslant\theta^{\prime}
and
i
.

Then
V_{i}\leqslant_{st}V_{j}
for
i\leqslant j
.

Proof of Lemma
4.11
.

For
i<j
note by conditioning on
\Theta_{i}
we get:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(V_{i}\geqslant x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(V_{i}\geqslant x\;|\;\Theta_{i}=\theta)\;\Theta_{i}(d\theta)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(V_{j}\geqslant x\;|\;\Theta_{j}=\theta)\;\Theta_{i}(d\theta)

\displaystyle\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}}\mathbb{P}(V_{j}\geqslant x\;|\;\Theta_{j}=\theta)\;\Theta_{j}(d\theta)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(V_{j}\geqslant x)

by first using property (b) and then properties (a),(c) together. For the latter, we are using the fact that (c) implies
\mathbb{P}(V_{j}\geqslant x|\Theta_{j}=\theta)
is an increasing function in

\Theta_{i}\leqslant_{st}\Theta_{j}
this ordering is preserved when you take the expectation with respect to any increasing function.
∎

4.3.2
Likelihood Ratio Ordering

We say a random variable
X
is less than
Y
with respect to the
likelihood ratio ordering (LRO)
, and denote this by
X\leqslant_{lr}Y
, if the likelihood ratios
f_{Y}(t)/f_{X}(t)
are non-decreasing in
t
. Here,
f_{X},f_{Y}
are the mass functions of
X,Y
respectively. Equivalently, we require:

f_{X}(s)f_{Y}(t)\geqslant f_{X}(t)f_{Y}(s),\quad\forall s\leqslant t

It is not hard to see that
X\leqslant_{lr}Y
implies
X\leqslant_{st}Y
, so this is a strictly stronger ordering than that of first-order stochastic dominance. The LRO has some convenient closure properties. The following appear as Theorems 1.C.9 and 1.C.17 of
18
respectively:

Theorem 4.12

(LRO preserved by Convolutions)
.

Suppose we have two collections
\{X_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n},\{Y_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
of independent, log-concave random variables. If
X_{i}\leqslant_{lr}Y_{i}
for all
i
then
\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}\leqslant_{lr}\sum_{i=1}^{n}Y_{i}
.

Theorem 4.13

(LRO preserved by Mixtures)
.

Suppose we have a collection
\{V_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
of random variables so that
V_{i}\leqslant_{lr}V_{j}
for
i\leqslant j
. Moreover, suppose that we have random variables
M,N
independent of
\{V_{i}\}
with
M\leqslant_{lr}N
. Then
V_{M}\leqslant_{lr}V_{N}
.

Using these, we can see our random variables of interest are increasing with respect to the LRO:

Lemma 4.14
.

For
i\leqslant j
we have
Z_{i}\leqslant_{lr}Z_{j}
,
X_{i}\leqslant_{lr}X_{j}
and
Z_{X_{i}}\leqslant_{lr}Z_{X_{j}}
.

Proof.

First, we show
\{Z_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{N}}
is increasing in the LRO, namely:

\mathbb{P}(Z_{i}=m)\cdot\mathbb{P}(Z_{j}=n)\geqslant\mathbb{P}(Z_{i}=n)\cdot\mathbb{P}(Z_{j}=m),\quad\forall i\leqslant j,\;\forall m\leqslant n.

But this is a consequence of the total positivity of the transition kernels for birth-death processes (see for example
6
).

We will prove the other statements by induction. Trivially
X_{1}\equiv 1\leqslant_{lr}2\equiv X_{2}
. Thus suppose for induction that
X_{1}\leqslant_{lr}...\leqslant_{lr}X_{i}
. Then Theorem
4.13
implies
Z_{X_{1}}\leqslant_{lr}...\leqslant_{lr}Z_{X_{i}}
. Using this, Theorem
4.12
implies:

X_{i}=Z_{X_{\lfloor i/2\rfloor}}+Z_{X_{\lceil i/2\rceil}}\leqslant_{lr}Z_{X_{\lfloor(i+1)/2\rfloor}}+Z_{X_{\lceil(i+1)/2\rceil}}=X_{i+1}

since
Z_{X_{m}}
are log-concave by Lemma
4.3
. Hence the result follows from induction.

∎

We can provide a partial complement to the above Lemma

We will not use this result in this paper, but it adds to the idea that the balanced tree and the caterpillar tree are in a sense the two extreme points in our space of trees.

Lemma 4.15
.

Suppose
\{\alpha_{i}\}_{i=1}^{2k-2}
are the descendant counts of our rooted binary tree

Proof of Lemma
4.15

We will describe a natural greedy balancing operation on the tree and prove it decreases

Say that a given vertex

This type of iterative subtree balancing is reminiscent of measures of tree balance in phylogenetics, where metrics such as the Colless or Sackin indices quantify how balanced a tree is relative to the maximally balanced or maximally imbalanced (caterpillar) configurations. Tree balance statistics have long been used in phylogenetics to characterize the shapes of evolutionary trees, compare them to null models, and infer evolutionary processes such as speciation or extinction biases
8
.

Classical tree-balancing procedures in computer science—such as AVL trees
1
and weight-balanced trees
14
—use local rotations to enforce constraints on subtree depths or sizes. Our algorithm shares the same conceptual goal of redistributing leaves to reduce imbalance, but it is purely combinatorial and does not maintain any search property.

Due to the bijections full (planar) binary trees share with many other combinatorial objects, we can view these descendant counts
\{\alpha_{i}\}
through various lenses. To do so, we must extend a natural duality operation on planar binary trees to these combinatorial objects. Given such tree
T
, define its dual
T^{\prime}
by swapping the right and left subtree of every vertex. To extend this to other objects, given a bijection
\varphi:\mathcal{T}\to\mathcal{C}
from our binary trees to some class
\mathcal{C}
of combinatorial objects we can define the dual via
C^{\prime}:=\varphi(\varphi^{-1}(C)^{\prime})
. Using this, we can equivalently interpret our descendant counts
\{\alpha_{i}\}
as:

•

Dyck-paths
: To each edge
i
of a Dyck-path, associate the number
\alpha_{i}
of steps it takes to drop strictly below the level of edge
i
again. So in this setting, the
\alpha_{i}
correspond to the lengths of excursions above a certain level.

•

Non-Crossing Perfect Matchings:
A perfect matching
M
of
[2n]
is

if no matched pairs
(a,b),(c,d)\in M
have
a<c<b<d
where this ordering is interpreted modulo
2n
. In this setting,
\{\alpha_{i}\}=\{b-a\}_{(a,b)\in M}\sqcup\{b^{\prime}-a^{\prime}\}_{(a^{\prime},b^{\prime})\in M^{\prime}}
captures the distance between matched pairs in both the matching and its dual
M^{\prime}
.

•

Non-crossing Partitions:
A partition
\Pi
of
[n]
is non-crossing if there does not exist distinct parts
\pi_{1},\pi_{2}\in\Pi
and elements
a,b\in\pi_{1},c,d\in\pi_{2}
so that
a<c<b<d
. In this setting, if
i\in\pi_{i}\in\Pi
is the part containing
i
we see
\{\alpha_{i}\}=\{|\pi_{i}\setminus[i-1]|\}\sqcup\{|\pi^{\prime}_{i}\setminus[i-1]|\}
captures how many larger elements exist in each part of
\Pi
and its dual
\Pi^{\prime}
.

Hence, Lemmas
2.1
and

tell us what the maximizer and minimizer of such functions look like in a variety of combinatorial settings.

4.5
Proof Lemma
2.6
: Deterministic Balancing Lemma

Recall we aim to prove:

Lemma:
Let
k\geqslant 4
be fixed. Then for any fixed
T
:

Z^{T}_{i}+Z^{T}_{k-i}\leqslant_{st}Z^{T}_{j}+Z^{T}_{k-j},\quad 1\leqslant i\leqslant j\leqslant\frac{k}{2}.

Namely, the more balanced the subtrees are below edge
e
, the more lineages you typically enter
e
.

Proof Lemma
2.6
.

The statement is vacuously true for
k=2,3
, so for induction assume it is true for all
k^{\prime}<k
. Let
S_{k,m}^{T}:=Z_{m}^{T}+Z^{T}_{k-m}
denote the total number of lineages entering an edge
e
with subtrees of size
m,k-m
, the setup in Figure
4
. Let
\tau_{k,m}
be the time of the first coalescent event in either of our two subpopulations of size
m,k-m
, allowing the possibility
\tau_{k,m}>T
.

The basic idea is that if we start with
k
lineages, we can reduce to
k-1
lineages by just waiting for the first coalescence event to occur in either subpopulation. There is of course the chance no coalescent event occurs, so we must handle this separately. The proof then relies on a few main ideas. Firstly, for more balanced splits we tend to wait longer until the first coalescent event.

Claim 1
.

For fixed
k
,
\tau_{k,m}
is stochastically increasing in
m
for
1\leqslant m\leqslant\frac{k}{2}
.

Next, conditional on a specific first coalescent time, the more balanced our split is, the more lineages tend to remain uncoalesced by the end of the branch.

Claim 2
.

For fixed
k,x,t
then
\mathbb{P}(S_{k,m}^{T}\geqslant x\;|\;\tau_{k,m}=t)
is an increasing function of
m
for
1\leqslant m\leqslant\frac{k}{2}
.

Lastly, the more time we wait until the first coalescent event, the less time the remaining lineages have to coalesce and the more lineages we tend to have by the end of the branch.

Claim 3
.

For fixed
k,x,m
then
\mathbb{P}(S_{k,m}^{T}\geqslant x\;|\;\tau_{k,m}=t)
is an increasing function of
t
.

Proof of Claim
1
.

Let
h_{k,m}:={m\choose 2}+{k-m\choose 2}
. Then
\tau_{k,m}\sim\mathrm{Exp}\left(h_{k,m}\right)
. This comes from the fact that coalescents occur at rate
{m\choose 2}
in the population of size
m
and at rate
{k-m\choose 2}
in the population of size
k-m
. Together, these produce coalescent events at rate
h_{k,m}
. Since
\tau_{k,m}\sim\exp(h_{k,m})
:

\mathbb{P}(\tau_{k,m}>t)=\exp\left(-th_{k,m}\right)

As
h_{k,m}
is decreasing in
m
for
1\leqslant m\leqslant\frac{k}{2}
, the above is increasing in
m
. Hence
\tau_{k,m}
is stochastically increasing in
m
. This proves Claim 1.
∎

Proof of Claim
2
.

For
t>T
then we
\mathbb{P}(S_{k,m}^{T}\geqslant x|\tau_{k,m}=t)=1
so the claim is trivially true. For
t<T
, by the strong memoryless property of the exponential distribution underlying Kingman’s coalescent, we know:

(4.1)

\mathbb{P}(S_{k,m}^{T}\geqslant x\;|\;\tau_{k,m}=t)=p_{k,m}\cdot\mathbb{P}(S_{k-1,m-1}^{T-t}\geqslant x)+(1-p_{k,m})\cdot\mathbb{P}(S_{k-1,m}^{T-t}\geqslant x)

where:

p_{k,m}=\mathbb{P}\left[\exp\left({m\choose 2}\right)\leqslant\exp\left({k-m\choose 2}\right)\right]=\frac{{m\choose 2}}{{m\choose 2}+{k-m\choose 2}}

is the probability the first coalescence occurs in the population of size
m
. This essentially just says that after the first coalescent event occurs in either subpopulation, what remains is still a Kingman’s coalescent just with one less lineage. By our inductive hypothesis, for
1\leqslant m\leqslant\frac{k}{2}-1
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(S_{k,m}^{T}\geqslant x\;|\;\tau_{k,m}=t)

\displaystyle=p_{k,m}\cdot\mathbb{P}(S_{k-1,m-1}^{T-t}\geqslant x)+(1-p_{k,m})\cdot\mathbb{P}(S_{k-1,m}^{T-t}\geqslant x)

\displaystyle\leqslant\mathbb{P}(S_{k-1,m}^{T-t}\geqslant x)

\displaystyle\leqslant p_{k,m+1}\cdot\mathbb{P}(S_{k-1,m}^{T-t}\geqslant x)+(1-p_{k,m+1})\cdot\mathbb{P}(S_{k-1,m+1}^{T-t}\geqslant x)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{P}(S_{k,m+1}^{T}\geqslant x\;|\;\tau_{k,m+1}=t)

so that
\mathbb{P}(S_{k,m}^{T}\geqslant x\;|\;\tau_{k,m}=t)
is increasing in
m
for each fixed
t
. This proves Claim 2.
∎

Proof of Claim
3
.

By equation (
4.1
) it suffices to show for fixed
k,m
that
S^{T}_{k,m}
is stochastically decreasing in
T
. But this follows from a simple coupling argument. For
s\leqslant T
, couple
S_{k,m}^{T}
to
S_{k,m}^{s}
by using the same exponential clocks for the coalescent events. At time
s
the number of lineages remaining agree, and
S^{T}_{k,m}
can only decrease from here if more coalescents occur between times
s
and
T
. This proves Claim 3.
∎

With the claims proved, we are ready to finally finish our proof of Lemma
2.6
. Note that our above claims are exactly the conditions of Lemma
4.11
where
\{S_{k,m}^{T}\}_{m=1}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}
are playing the role of our
V_{i}
and
\{\tau_{k,m}\}_{m=1}^{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}
are playing the role of our
\Theta_{i}
. Hence the result just follows from applying this lemma. This completes the proof of Lemma
2.6
.

∎

4.6
Proof of Lemma
2.8
: Balanced is Worst-Case

Recall we aim to prove:

Lemma:
If
\mathcal{B}_{k}
is the balanced tree with
k
leaves and all branches length
T_{min}
, then
X_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}\geqslant_{st}X_{\mathcal{T}}
for any other tree
\mathcal{T}
with
k
leaves and minimum branch length
T_{min}
.

To simplify notation a bit, denote
X_{m}:=X_{\mathcal{B}_{m}}
. To start, we will prove two extensions of our deterministic balancing lemma
2.6
. The first allows the difference between the subpopulation sizes to be random. Heuristically, it says that conditional on the total number of species in the two subtrees below a given vertex, the more even the split tends to be, the more lineages tend to reach the vertex.

Lemma 4.16
.

Suppose for
s\in\mathbb{N}
that
0\leqslant_{st}D\leqslant_{st}D^{\prime}<s
and
D,D^{\prime}\equiv s\;\mathrm{mod}(2)
. Then:

Z_{\frac{s+D}{2}}+Z_{\frac{s-D}{2}}\geqslant_{st}Z_{\frac{s+D^{\prime}}{2}}+Z_{\frac{s-D^{\prime}}{2}}.

Proof of Lemma
4.16
.

Since the sum
s=\frac{s+d}{2}+\frac{s-d}{2}
is fixed, our deterministic balancing lemma
2.6
implies that:

Y_{d}:=Z_{\frac{s+d}{2}}+Z_{\frac{s-d}{2}}

is decreasing in
d
with respect to
\leqslant_{st}
for
0\leqslant d<s
. Lemma
4.11
then implies the result.
∎

The next extension generalizes this even further by allowing the sum of the subpopulation sizes to be random as well. Heuristically, it captures the same idea as before: more balanced subpopulations tend to produce less coalescence.

Lemma 4.17

(Balancing Lemma)
.

If
A,A^{\prime},B,B^{\prime}
are mutually independent, positive integer-valued random variables so that
A\leqslant_{lr}A^{\prime},B\leqslant_{lr}B^{\prime}
, then:

Z_{A+B}+Z_{A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}}\leqslant_{st}Z_{A+B^{\prime}}+Z_{A^{\prime}+B}.

Note when
A,B,A^{\prime},B^{\prime}
are all deterministic, this reduces Lemma
2.6
.

Proof of Balancing Lemma
4.17
.

For convenience, define:

\Delta_{A}:=A^{\prime}-A,\quad\Delta_{B}:=B^{\prime}-B,\quad S_{A}:=A^{\prime}+A,\quad S_{B}=B^{\prime}+B.

Now, note in both
Z_{A+B}+Z_{A^{\prime}+B^{\prime}}
and
Z_{A+B^{\prime}}+Z_{A^{\prime}+B}
the total number of lineages
S=S_{A}+S_{B}
is the same. Hence by conditioning on
S_{A},S_{B},|\Delta_{A}|,
and
|\Delta_{B}|
, Lemma
4.16
implies it suffices to show
D^{\prime}\geqslant_{st}D
where
D,D^{\prime}
are the conditional differences:

D^{\prime}=|\Delta_{A}+\Delta_{B}|\mid\{|\Delta_{A}|,|\Delta_{B}|,S_{A},S_{B}\},\qquad D=|\Delta_{A}-\Delta_{B}|\mid\{|\Delta_{A}|,|\Delta_{B}|,S_{A},S_{B}\}.

After this conditioning,
\{D,D^{\prime}\}=\{|\Delta_{A}|+|\Delta_{B}|,||\Delta_{A}|-|\Delta_{B}||\}
. Let:

q=\mathbb{P}(D^{\prime}=|\Delta_{A}|+|\Delta_{B}|)=\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{A}\Delta_{B}\geqslant 0\mid|\Delta_{A}|,|\Delta_{B}|,S_{A},S_{B})

be the probability
D^{\prime}
takes on the larger of these two values. Then it suffices to show
q\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
. Since
\Delta_{A},\Delta_{B}
are conditionally independent given
S_{A},S_{B}
:

q=p_{A}p_{B}+(1-p_{A})(1-p_{B})\geqslant\frac{1}{2}\iff\left(p_{A}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\left(p_{B}-\frac{1}{2}\right)\geqslant 0,

where
p_{A}=\mathbb{P}(\Delta_{A}\geqslant 0\mid|\Delta_{A}|,S_{A})
and likewise for
p_{B}
. We will show that
p_{A},p_{B}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
, completing the proof. Note conditional on
\{|\Delta_{A}|=m,S_{A}=s\}
then
p_{A}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
occurs precisely when:

\mathbb{P}\left(A^{\prime}=\frac{s+m}{2},A=\frac{s-m}{2}\right)\geqslant\mathbb{P}\left(A^{\prime}=\frac{s-m}{2},A=\frac{s+m}{2}\right).

Since
A,A^{\prime}
are independent, this follows from
A\leqslant_{lr}A^{\prime}.
Similarly,
p_{B}\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
. Thus
D^{\prime}\geqslant_{st}D
.
∎

Now we are ready to return to the main result. First, we show that the result follows from the following stochastic extension of Lemma
2.6
:

Lemma 4.18
.

For fixed
k\in\mathbb{N}
, then
Z_{X_{i}}+Z_{X_{k-i}}\leqslant_{st}Z_{X_{\lfloor k/2\rfloor}}+Z_{X_{\lceil k/2\rceil}}
for
1\leqslant i\leqslant\lfloor k/2\rfloor
.

We conjecture that
Z_{X_{i}}+Z_{X_{k-i}}
is increasing in
i
rather than just maximized at the even split. But for our current purposes, the above suffices. Assuming Lemma
4.18
for now, it is easy to prove the our desired Lemma
2.8
.

Reduction of Lemma
2.8
to Lemma
4.18
.

We induct on the number of species
k
.

Base Case (
k=4
)

Again there are only two different tree topologies, the ones in Figure
2
. Lemma
2.6
shows the even split
Z_{2}+Z_{2}^{\prime}\geqslant_{st}Z_{1}+Z_{3}^{\prime}
, and clearly
Z_{1}+Z_{3}^{\prime}\geqslant_{st}Z_{1}+Z_{X_{3}}^{\prime}
by Corollary
4.10
.

Inductive Step

Suppose
\mathcal{T}
is a tree with
k>4
leaves. As usual, the main idea is to reduce to smaller trees by looking at the subtrees of the root. We perform the induction in two steps: we first reduce to the case the two subtrees of the root are themselves balanced trees, and then we show in such a setting the worst-case is when the two subtrees have the same number of leaves.

Let
L
and
R
be the two subtrees of the root of
\mathcal{T}
which are of sizes
m
and
k-m
respectively. By our inductive hypothesis,
X_{L}\leqslant_{st}X_{\mathcal{B}_{m}}
and
X_{R}\leqslant_{st}X_{\mathcal{B}_{k-m}}
. Let
\mathcal{T}^{\prime}
be the tree whose two subtrees are
\mathcal{B}_{m},\mathcal{B}_{k-m}
, and whose branch lengths are all
T_{min}
. Then as
X_{\mathcal{T}}=Z_{X_{L}}+Z_{X_{R}}
we see Corollary
4.10
implies
X_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}\geqslant_{st}X_{\mathcal{T}}
stochastically. Hence all that is left to show is that
X_{\mathcal{B}_{k}}\geqslant_{st}X_{\mathcal{T}^{\prime}}
. But this is exactly just the content of Lemma
4.18
. Hence Lemma
2.8
follows from Lemma
4.18
.
∎

Now, all that is left to do is prove Lemma
4.18
.

Proof of Lemma
4.18
.

We prove this statement by induction on
k
. Again the base case is trivial:

Z_{1}+Z_{X_{3}}\leqslant_{st}Z_{1}+Z_{3}\leqslant_{st}Z_{2}+Z_{2}=Z_{X_{2}}+Z_{X_{2}}.

Now suppose the statement is true for
k^{\prime}<k
. Note:

X_{m}=Z_{X_{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}}+Z_{X_{\lceil m/2\rceil}},\qquad X_{k-m}=Z_{X_{\lfloor(k-m)/2\rfloor}}+Z_{X_{\lceil(k-m)/2\rceil}}.

By Lemma
4.14
we have for
1\leqslant m\leqslant\frac{k}{2}
:

Z_{X_{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}}\leqslant_{lr}Z_{X_{\lceil m/2\rceil}}\leqslant_{lr}Z_{X_{\lfloor(k-m)/2\rfloor}}\leqslant_{lr}Z_{X_{\lceil(k-m)/2\rceil}}.

Note that if
k
is even then:

\lfloor m/2\rfloor+\lceil(k-m)/2\rceil=\lceil m/2\rceil+\lfloor(k-m)/2\rfloor=\frac{k}{2}.

The balancing lemma
4.17
then implies:

Z_{X_{m}}+Z_{X_{k-m}}\leqslant_{st}\left(Z_{Z_{X_{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}}+Z_{X_{\lceil(k-m)/2\rceil}}}\right)+\left(Z_{Z_{X_{\lceil m/2\rceil}}+Z_{X_{\lfloor(k-m)/2\rfloor}}}\right).

The inductive hypothesis applied to
k^{\prime}=k/2
shows the subscripts of each term are maximized at
m=k/2
. Hence Corollary
4.10
implies:

\leqslant_{st}\left(Z_{Z_{X_{\lfloor k/4\rfloor}}+Z_{X_{\lceil k/4\rceil}}}\right)+\left(Z_{Z_{X_{\lceil k/4\rceil}}+Z_{X_{\lfloor k/4\rfloor}}}\right)=Z_{X_{k/2}}+Z_{X_{k/2}},

as desired. Likewise, if
k
is odd then:

\lfloor m/2\rfloor+\lfloor(k-m)/2\rfloor=\frac{k-1}{2},\quad and\quad\lceil m/2\rceil+\lceil(k-m)/2\rceil=\frac{k+1}{2}

so we can rearrange using the balancing lemma
4.17
as:

Z_{X_{m}}+Z_{X_{k-m}}\leqslant_{st}\left(Z_{Z_{X_{\lfloor m/2\rfloor}}+Z_{X_{\lfloor(k-m)/2\rfloor}}}\right)+\left(Z_{Z_{X_{\lceil m/2\rceil}}+Z_{X_{\lceil(k-m)/2\rceil}}}\right)

Applying induction with
k^{\prime}=\frac{k-1}{2}
on the left term and
k^{\prime}=\frac{k+1}{2}
on the right term we see both terms are maximized at
m=\lfloor k/2\rfloor=\frac{k-1}{2}
. Hence:

\leqslant_{st}\left(Z_{Z_{X_{\lfloor(k-1)/4\rfloor}}+Z_{X_{\lfloor(k+1)/4\rfloor}}}\right)+\left(Z_{Z_{X_{\lceil(k-1)/4\rceil}}+Z_{X_{\lceil(k+1)/4\rceil}}}\right)=Z_{X_{(k-1)/2}}+Z_{X_{(k+1)/2}}

as desired.
∎

4.7
Proof of Lemma
3.1
: Asymptotics
g_{k,1}(T)

In this section, we develop some asymptotics for the time
g_{k,1}(T)
to coalescence of Kingman’s coalescent, specialized to the topic of interest. More properties of this coalescent time are discussed in
13
and
4
. The paper
7
further generalizes some of these ideas to
\Lambda
-coalescents.

We will work in slightly more generality than this paper requires. Namely, suppose that we have positive
distinct
rates
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
. Define the random variables
\{S_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}
by
S_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}X_{i}
for
X_{i}\sim\mathrm{Exp}(\lambda_{i})
independent (namely
X_{i}
has density
\tau_{i}(x):=\lambda_{i}e^{-\lambda_{i}x}
). Moreover, define
F_{k}(T):=\mathbb{P}(S_{k}\leqslant T)
to be their CDFs and
f_{k}(T)
their densities. If we define:

C_{i,k}:=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}m=1\\
m\neq i\end{subarray}}^{k}\frac{\lambda_{m}}{\lambda_{m}-\lambda_{i}}

then we have the following well-known explicit formulas for the CDFs and densities:

(4.2)

F_{k}(T)=1-\sum_{i=1}^{k}C_{i,k}e^{-\lambda_{i}T},\qquad f_{k}(T)=\sum_{i=1}^{k}C_{i,k}\lambda_{i}e^{-\lambda_{i}T}.

See
21
, for example. Moreover, we have the natural recursive relation:

(4.3)

F_{k}(T)=(F_{k-1}\ast\tau_{k})(T):=\int_{0}^{T}F_{k-1}(x)\lambda_{k}e^{-\lambda_{k}(T-x)}\;dx.

Since
S_{k}
are monotonically increasing, we see
S_{k}\uparrow S_{\infty}:=\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}X_{i}
almost surely, where the limit may be infinite. To quantify the rate of convergence, define the partial sums
s_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}
, the remainder
R_{k}:=\sum_{i=k+1}^{\infty}X_{i}
, and the remainder’s expectation
r_{k}:=\mathbb{E}R_{k}=\sum_{i=k+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{-1}
. We start by getting some explicit formulas for the derivatives of CDF in terms of these rates
\{\lambda_{i}\}
:

Lemma 4.19
.

If we have positive, distinct rates
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{\infty}
:

(i)

At zero, the one-sided derivatives
F_{k}^{(m)}(0)=0
for
0\leqslant m\leqslant k-1
. The nonzero derivatives take the form:

F_{k}^{{(k+r)}}(0)=(-1)^{r}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\right)\left(h_{r}(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{k})\right),

where
h_{r}
are the complete homogeneous symmetric polynomials:

h_{r}(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{k}):=\sum_{i_{1}\leqslant i_{2}\leqslant...\leqslant i_{r}=1}^{k}\prod_{j=1}^{r}\lambda_{i_{j}}.

In particular, for
r=0,1
we have:

F_{k}^{(k)}(0)=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i},\qquad F_{k}^{(k+1)}(0)=-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\right)\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\right).

(ii)

The derivatives are uniformly bounded. Namely, for fixed
m
:

||F_{k}^{(m)}||_{\infty}\leqslant M_{m}=\max_{i=1,...,m}|F_{i}^{(m)}(0)|.

Proof.

(i) It is easy to see that for
m\geqslant 1
:

F_{k}^{(m)}(0)=(-1)^{m-1}\sum_{i=1}^{k}C_{i,k}\lambda_{i}^{m}

which vanishes by Lemma 2 of
21
when
m\leqslant k-1
. To calculate the first nonzero derivatives, we will rely on the recurrence in Equation
4.3
. Note that given two smooth functions
g,h:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
and their convolution:

g\ast h(T):=\int_{0}^{T}g(T-t)h(t)\;dt=\int_{0}^{T}g(t)h(T-t)\;dt.

Hence, by Leibniz’s integral rule we have:

(4.4)

(g\ast h)^{\prime}(T)=(g^{\prime}\ast h)(T)+g(0)h(T)=(g\ast h^{\prime})(T)+g(T)h(0).

By iteratively applying this to
F_{k}=F_{k-1}\ast\tau_{k}
, we see:

(4.5)

F_{k}^{(m)}(T)=(F_{k-1}^{(m)}\ast\tau_{k})(T)+\sum_{j=0}^{m-k}F_{k-1}^{(k-1+j)}(0)\tau^{(m-k-j)}_{k}(T),\\

since by
(ii)
the first
k-2
derivatives of
F_{k-1}
vanish. In particular, if
k>m
we see
F_{k}^{(m)}=F^{(m)}_{k-1}\ast\tau_{k}
. Using this, we can prove
F_{k}^{(k+m)}(0)
has the desired form by induction. Note
F_{1}(T)=1-e^{-\lambda_{1}T}
so
F_{1}^{m+1}(0)=(-1)^{m}\lambda_{1}\cdot\lambda_{1}^{m}
as desired. Moreover,
\tau_{k}^{(r)}(0)=(-1)^{r}\lambda_{k}^{r+1}
. Thus by induction and equation (
4.5
):

F_{k}^{(k+m)}(0)=\sum_{j=0}^{m}F_{k-1}^{(k-1+j)}(0)\,\tau^{m-j}_{k}(0)=(-1)^{m}\left(\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}\right)\sum_{j=0}^{m}\lambda_{k}^{m-j}a_{k-1,j}

where
a_{k,m}:=h_{m}(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{k})
are our symmetric polynomials. Hence it suffices to show these polynomials satisfy the recurrence:

a_{k,m}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\lambda_{k}^{m-j}a_{k-1,j}.

But this is almost immediate, just by grouping terms according the number of
i_{\ell}
that equal
k
:

a_{k,m}=\sum_{i_{1}\leqslant i_{2}\leqslant...\leqslant i_{m}=1}^{k}\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}\lambda_{i_{\ell}}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\;\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i_{1}\leqslant i_{2}\leqslant...\leqslant i_{j}=1\\
i_{j+1}=...=i_{m}=k\end{subarray}}^{k-1}\prod_{\ell=1}^{m}\lambda_{i_{\ell}}=\sum_{j=0}^{m}\lambda_{k}^{m-j}a_{k-1,j}.

Hence
F_{k}^{(k+m)}(0)=(-1)^{m}a_{k,m}\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}
takes the desired form.

(ii) Since we saw in the previous section that for
k>m
we have
F_{k}^{(m)}=F_{k-1}^{(m)}\ast\tau_{k}
, by iteratively applying Young’s convolution inequality we get:

||F_{k}^{(m)}||_{\infty}\leqslant||F_{k-1}^{(m)}||_{\infty}\cdot||\tau_{k}||_{1}=||F_{k-1}^{(m)}||_{\infty}\to||F_{k}^{(m)}||_{\infty}\leqslant||F_{m}^{(m)}||_{\infty},\quad\forall k\geqslant m.

Hence we can see
||F_{k}^{(m)}||_{\infty}\leqslant\max_{i=1,...,m}||F_{i}^{(m)}||_{\infty}=:M_{m}<\infty
.

To quantify
M_{m}
, note that using the expression for the derivatives of
\tau_{k}
, we can see equation (
4.5
) can be re-expressed as:

F_{k}^{(m)}(T)=F_{k-1}^{(m)}\ast\tau_{k}(T)+F_{k}^{(m)}(0)e^{-\lambda_{k}T}.

Then Young’s convolution inequality implies:

||F_{k}^{(m)}||_{\infty}\leqslant||F_{k-1}^{(m)}(0)||_{\infty}\cdot(1-e^{-\lambda_{k}T})+|F_{k}^{(m)}(0)|e^{-\lambda_{k}T}\leqslant\max\{||F_{k-1}^{(m)}||_{\infty},|F_{k}^{(m)}(0)|\}.

From here, by inducting we see
M_{m}=\max_{i=1,...,m}|F_{i}^{(m)}(0)|
, whose formuli are given in (ii) above.

∎

4.7.1
CDF Asymptotics

Now we that we have these bounds, the main results follow. We in fact show something slightly stronger: that the relative error in the Taylor series approximation is negligible in the
T=o((s_{k}/k)^{-1})
regime no matter where we truncate it.

Theorem 4.20
.

If
m=\arg\min_{i=1,...,k}\lambda_{i}
, then the CDFs have asymptotics:

\begin{cases}1-F_{k}(T)\sim C_{m,k}e^{-\lambda_{m}T},&T\uparrow\infty\text{ and k fixed,}\\
F_{k}(T)\sim\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}}{k!}T^{k},&T=o((s_{k}/k)^{-1}),\\
\end{cases}

where
T=o((s_{k}/k)^{-1})
denotes any sequence of pairs
(T_{n},k_{n})
so that
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{s_{k_{n}}T_{n}}{k_{n}}=0
. More generally, in the same
T=o((s_{k}/k)^{-1})
regime and for any finite
m\geqslant 0
:

\frac{F_{k}(T)-\sum_{r=0}^{m}a_{r,k}}{a_{m,k}}\to 0,\qquad a_{r,k}:=(-1)^{r}\frac{h_{r}(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{k})\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}}{(k+r)!}T^{k+r}.

Proof.

For
k
fixed, the exponential in equation
4.2
with the smallest rate decays the slowest, so
1-F_{k}(T)\sim C_{m,k}e^{-\lambda_{m}T}
as
T\to\infty
. For the other asymptotics, first fix
k
and let
h_{r,k}:=h_{r}(\lambda_{1},...,\lambda_{k})
be our symmetric polynomials and
p_{k}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}
. Since
F_{k}(T)
is the sum of a finite number of exponentials, its Taylor series at zero converges to
F_{k}(T)
for all
T
. Moreover, (i) of Lemma
4.19
above tells us
F_{k}^{(k+r)}(0)=(-1)^{r}p_{k}h_{r,k}
. Thus, let:

a_{r,k}=(-1)^{r}\frac{p_{k}h_{r,k}}{(k+r)!}T^{k+r}

denote the
(r+1)^{th}
nonzero term in the Taylor series expansion about zero. Suppose that we approximate
F_{k}(T)
by the first
(m+1)
nonzero terms of its Taylor series about zero. We can measure the approximation error relative to the last term
a_{m,k}
to see:

\frac{F_{k}(T)-\sum_{r=0}^{m}a_{r,k}}{a_{m,k}}=\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{r}\frac{h_{m+r}/h_{m}T^{r}}{(k+m+r)_{r}}

where
(x)_{r}=x(x-1)\cdot...\cdot(x-r+1)
denotes the falling factorial. We will now bound the right hand side by a geometric series.

Note that trivially
h_{r+1}\leqslant h_{r}s_{k}
as the former includes all monomials of degree
(r+1)
in
\{\lambda_{i}\}_{i=1}^{k}
with coefficient one, and the latter has all the monomials with coefficient at least one. Hence
h_{m+r}/h_{m}=\prod_{i=0}^{r-1}\frac{h_{m+i+1}}{h_{m+i}}\leqslant s_{k}^{r}
. Using this and the simple bound
(k+m+r)_{r}\geqslant k^{r}
we get:

\bigg|\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}(-1)^{r}\frac{h_{m+r}/h_{m}T^{r}}{(k+m+r)_{r}}\bigg|\leqslant\sum_{r=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{s_{k}T}{k}\right)^{r}=\frac{s_{k}T/k}{1-s_{k}T/k}

for
|s_{k}T/k|<1
. When
T=o((s_{k}/k)^{-1})
, this tends to zero. Thus in this regime:

\frac{F_{k}(T)-\sum_{r=0}^{m}a_{r,k}}{a_{m,k}}\to 0,

as desired. In particular:

F_{k}(T)=(1+o(1))\frac{\prod_{i=1}^{k}\lambda_{i}}{k!}T^{k}.

∎

4.7.2
Gap Asymptotics

The following statement gives us a sense of how fast our coalescent probabilities
g_{k,1}(T)
will saturate as
k\to\infty
.

Theorem 4.21

(Gap Asymptotics)
.

If
\sum_{i=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{-1}<\infty
, then
F_{k}\to F_{\infty}
uniformly. Quantitatively, the gap
\Delta_{k}(T)=F_{k}(T)-F_{\infty}(T)
has asymptotics:

\Delta_{k}=(f_{\infty}+o(1))r_{k}.

Proof.

Dini’s theorem already guarantees that
F_{k}\to F_{\infty}
uniformly. Here, we will get quantitative control on the rate of convergence. Note that (ii) of Lemma
4.19
implies the family
\{f_{k}\}
is uniformly bounded by
M_{1}
and are all
M_{2}
-Lipschitz. Thus they form a uniformly equicontinuous family which is uniformly bounded. Since
S_{k}\Rightarrow S_{\infty}
, as a consequence of
2
converse to Scheffe’s lemma, we see
f_{k}\to f_{\infty}
uniformly on
[0,\infty)
. Now, note that by conditioning on the tail
R_{k}
, as
S_{k}\perp\!\!\!\perp R_{k}
:

\mathbb{P}(S_{\infty}\leqslant T)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(S_{k}\leqslant T-r)\mathbb{P}(R_{k}\in dr)=\mathbb{E}F_{k}(T-R_{k}).

Hence by Taylor expanding the CDF
F_{k}
we get:

\Delta=\mathbb{E}_{R_{k}}[F_{k}(T)-F_{k}(T-R_{k})]=f_{k}(T)\cdot\mathbb{E}R_{k}+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\left(f^{\prime}_{k}(\xi(R_{k}))R_{k}^{2}\right),

where
\xi(r)\in(T-r,T)
for every
r
. Note that
\mathrm{Var}(R_{k})=\sum_{i=k+1}^{\infty}\lambda_{i}^{-2}\leqslant r_{k}^{2}
, implying
\mathbb{E}R_{k}^{2}\leqslant 2r_{k}^{2}
. Since
||f^{\prime}_{k}||_{\infty}\leqslant M_{2}
is uniformly bounded by (iii) and as
f_{k}(T)\to f_{\infty}(T)
uniformly as
k\to\infty
:

\Delta_{k}(T)=f_{\infty}(T)r_{k}+(f_{k}(T)-f_{\infty}(T))r_{k}+O(r_{k}^{2})=f_{\infty}(T)r_{k}+o(r_{k}).

More specifically, since
M_{2}=\max\{|F_{1}^{\prime\prime}(0)|,|F_{2}^{\prime\prime}(0)|\}=\lambda_{1}\max(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})
:

||\Delta_{k}(T)-f_{\infty}r_{k}||_{\infty}\leqslant||f_{k}-f_{\infty}||_{\infty}r_{k}+\lambda_{1}\max(\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2})r_{k}^{2}=o(r_{k})

so the rate of convergence is uniform in
T
.
∎

Lemma
3.1
then just follows from the special case of Kingman’s coalescent, where we have rates
\tilde{\lambda}_{i}={i+1\choose 2}
. Note the indexing is slightly different here than in the main paper: its shifted down by one, but otherwise the same. Here we have:

r_{k}=\sum_{i=k+1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{{i+1\choose 2}}=\frac{2}{k+1},\qquad s_{k}=\sum_{i=1}^{k}{i+1\choose 2}=\frac{k(k+1)(k+2)}{6}=\Theta(k^{3}).

Moreover, a simple induction shows that
C_{1,k}=\frac{3k}{k+2}
. Hence we get asymptotics:

F_{k}(T)\sim\begin{cases}1-\frac{3k}{k+2}e^{-T},&T\uparrow\infty\text{ and k fixed,}\\
\frac{(k+1)!}{2^{k}}T^{k},&T=o(k^{-2}).\\
\end{cases}\qquad\Delta_{k}(T)=(f_{\infty}(T)+o(1))\cdot\frac{2}{k+1}

4.7.3
Improvement Ratio Asymptotics

In Section
3.4
above, we saw that asymptotically our balanced bound (Theorem
2.9
) improves upon the original bound by a factor of:

\beta_{T}:=\frac{-\log(1-g_{u(T),1}(T))}{-\log(1-s(T))}

In this section, we develop small
T
asymptotics for
\beta_{T}
. In particular:

Lemma 4.22

(Small
T
Improvements)
.

As
T\downarrow 0
, we have:

\beta_{T}\sim\frac{\pi^{2}}{2T}

Proof of Lemma
4.22
.

Since
-\log(1-x)\sim x
as
x\downarrow 0
:

\beta_{T}\sim 1+\frac{\Delta(T)}{s(T)},\qquad\Delta(T):=g_{u(T),1}(T)-s(T).

Lemma
3.1
and Corollary
4.9
imply as
T\downarrow 0
:

\frac{\Delta(T)}{s(T)}\sim\frac{2f_{S_{\infty}}(T)}{s(T)}\cdot\frac{1}{u(T)}\sim T\cdot r(T),

where
r:=\frac{d}{dt}\log(s(t))
is the reversed hazard rate of
S_{\infty}
. To control this, we develop asymptotics for
K(x):=\log\mathbb{E}e^{-xS_{\infty}}
and use a classical Tauberian theorem to relate this to the log CDF. By independence, for
x>0
:

K(x)=\log\left(\prod_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{\lambda_{n}}{\lambda_{n}+x}\right)=-\log\left(\prod_{n=2}^{\infty}\left[1+\frac{x}{\lambda_{n}}\right]\right).

Since
(n-1)^{2}\leqslant 2\lambda_{n}\leqslant n^{2}
, using the infinite product expansion of
\sinh
, we see:

0\leqslant K(x)+\log\left(\frac{\sinh(\pi\sqrt{2x})}{\pi\sqrt{2x}}\right)\leqslant\log(1+2x).

Hence as
\log(y^{-1}\sinh(y))\sim y
as
y\to\infty
, we see
K(x)\sim-\pi\sqrt{2x}
as
x\to\infty
. Applying Corollary 2 of
3
with
A=1,P(x)=s(x),B=-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2},
and
\beta=-1
we see this implies that
\log(s(T))\sim-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}T^{-1}
as
T\downarrow 0
.

Let
h(t):=\log s(T)
. Prékopa’s theorem implies that
S_{k}
is log-concave for all
k
and, as log-concavity is preserved under weak limits, so is
S_{\infty}
. Hence its distribution function
s(T)
is log-concave, and thus
h(t)
is concave. Thus for fixed
\epsilon\in(0,1)
, its derivative is bound by the two secant slopes:

\frac{h((1+\epsilon)t)-h(t)}{\epsilon t}\leqslant h^{\prime}(t)\leqslant\frac{h((1-\epsilon)t)-h(t)}{\epsilon t}.

Our asymptotics for
h
then imply:

\frac{1+o_{\epsilon}(1)}{1+\epsilon}\leqslant\frac{h^{\prime}(t)}{\pi^{2}/2t^{2}}\leqslant\frac{1+o_{\epsilon}(1)}{1-\epsilon},

and thus:

\frac{1}{1+\epsilon}\leqslant\lim\inf_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{h^{\prime}(t)}{\pi^{2}/2t^{2}}\leqslant\lim\sup_{t\downarrow 0}\frac{h^{\prime}(t)}{\pi^{2}/2t^{2}}\leqslant\frac{1}{1-\epsilon}

Sending
\epsilon\downarrow 0
shows
r(t)\sim\frac{\pi^{2}}{2t^{2}}
as desired.
∎

Note that above we showed:

s(T)=\exp\left(-\frac{\pi^{2}}{2}T^{-1}(1+o(1))\right).

Since for typical trees (e.g. Yule trees) we have
T_{min}=O(k^{-1})
, this heuristically tells us the original bound is
O(\log(k)\exp(k))
.

4.7.4
Bound Asymptotics (Lemma
3.2
)

Now that we have analyzed the core function
g_{k,1}(T)
we are ready to analyze our bounds. Let
\kappa_{q,k}=-\log\left((1-q)/(k-3)\right)
be the coefficient that occurs in the numerator of all our bounds. Moreover, let
s(T):=\mathbb{P}(S_{\infty}\leqslant T)
be our limiting CDF,
\Delta_{k}(T):=g_{k,1}(T)-s(T)
our gap, and
c(T):=2f_{S_{\infty}}(T)
our gap’s asymptotic constant. Recall that
M_{o}(k,T)
denotes the original bound from Theorem
1.1
and
M_{b}(k,T)
our balanced bound Theorem
2.9
.

We start with
M_{o}
. Note Lemma
3.1
implies:

(4.6)

\log(1-g_{k,1}(T))\sim\begin{cases}-T,&T\uparrow\infty,\\
-\frac{k!}{2^{k-1}}T^{k-1},&T=o(k^{-2}).\end{cases}

From this:

M_{o}(k,T)\sim\begin{cases}\frac{\kappa_{q,k}}{T},&T\uparrow\infty,\\
\frac{2^{k-3}\kappa_{q,k}}{(k-2)!T^{k-3}},&T=o(k^{-2}).\end{cases}

In particular, we recover the small
T
bound found in Appendix
B
of
19
, although here we exactly quantify the asymptotic constant.

For the fixed
T
and asymptotic
k
bound, we Taylor expand
\log(x)
about
1-s
to observe:

\log(1-g_{k,1}(T))=\log(1-s)-\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{\Delta^{n}}{n(1-s)^{n}}=\log(1-s)-\frac{\Delta}{1-s}-o(\Delta^{2}).

Hence this and Lemma
3.1
imply as
k\to\infty
:

M_{o}(k,T)=\frac{\kappa_{k,q}}{\left(\frac{c(T)}{1-s}+o(1)\right)\cdot\frac{1}{k}-\log(1-s)}\sim\frac{\kappa_{k,q}}{-\log(1-s)}.

For
M_{b}(k,T)
, note as
g_{k,1}(T)
is decreasing in
k
we have:

\sum_{\ell=2}^{k-2}(1-h(\ell,T))^{n}\geqslant(k-3)(1-g_{2,1}(T))^{n}=(k-3)e^{-nT}

Hence
M_{b}(k,T)\geqslant\kappa_{q,k}T^{-1}
as before.
Moreover, as
k\mapsto g_{k,1}(T)
is decreasing and convex by Lemma
4.7
:

\sum_{\ell=2}^{k-2}(1-\mathbb{E}g_{U_{\ell},1}(T))^{n}\leqslant(k-3)(1-g_{\mathbb{E}U_{k-2},1(T)})^{n}

Applying Corollary
4.9
:

M_{b}(k,T)\leqslant\frac{\kappa_{q,k}}{-\log(1-g_{u(T),1}(T))},\quad u(T):=\frac{2-e^{-T/2}}{1-e^{-T/2}}

Combining these two previous results:

\frac{\kappa_{q,k}}{T}\leqslant M_{b}(k,T)\leqslant\frac{\kappa_{q,k}}{-\log(1-g_{u(T),1}(T))}

Since
M_{b}(k,T)\leqslant M_{o}(k,T)
, the lower bound shows us
M_{b}(k,T)\sim\kappa_{q,k}T^{-1}
as
T\to\infty
. Since
u(T)\sim\frac{2}{T}
as
T\downarrow 0
, the upper bound does not provide any provable improvement in the regime
k^{2}T=o(1)
that we analyzed previously. More careful analysis of the regime
kT=o(1)
is likely necessary to observe a noticeable improvement, but this is outside the scope of our current analysis.

For fixed
T
, the upper bound implies
M_{b}(k,T)
improves upon
M_{o}(k,T)
by a factor:

\beta_{T}:=\frac{\log(1-g_{u(T),1}(T))}{\log(1-s(T))}

In Lemma
4.22
, we show that
\beta_{T}\sim\frac{\pi^{2}}{2T}
as
T\downarrow 0
, so heuristically we get a factor
O(T^{-1})
improvement over the original bound.

Repeating our above analysis shows that
any
bound built using the union bound and the coalescent function
g_{k,1}(T)
cannot be asymptotically better than
\frac{\log(k)}{T}
. This is true,
even if the descendant counts
\alpha_{i}
are known
, as we can still upper bound
g_{\alpha_{i},1}(T)\leqslant g_{2,1}(T)
. Hence the asymptotics in
k
are fundamentally limited to
\Theta(\log(k))
by our union bound argument, and we can only hope to improve upon the constant down to the limit
T^{-1}
. Still, we see some finite-sample improvements empirically, as we observed in Figure
6
.
