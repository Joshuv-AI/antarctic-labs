Juvenile lumbar disc herniation: impact of delayed diagnosis and surgical treatment on outcome in a 10-year single-center series
Authors: Christina Høstmælingen, Jarle Sundseth, Anna Latysheva, Radek Frič
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLumbar disc herniation (LDH) is rare in juveniles and may present atypically, potentially delaying referral for neurosurgical evaluation. We aimed to assess diagnostic and treatment delays and to evaluate short-term surgical outcomes in pediatric patients undergoing microdiscectomy for LDH at our department over a 10-year period.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed consecutive patients under 18 years of age who underwent microdiscectomy for LDH at our institution between 2015 and 2025. Clinical presentation, timing of imaging and surgery, and postoperative outcomes were analyzed.ResultsTwenty-one patients were included (mean age 14.6 ± 2.1 years, 57% females). Median body mass index (BMI) was 21.2 (range 18.7–36.9). All patients presented with low back pain, and 92.5% had radicular symptoms. The median time from symptom onset to MRI was 6 months (range 0–12 months), and to surgery 12 months (range 0–36 months). Most patients (90.5%) were operated at one level; L4/L5 and L5/S1 were the most commonly affected levels. At 3 months after surgery, 95.2% of patients reported improvement of back pain, while all patients experienced relief of radicular symptoms.ConclusionIn our cohort of pediatric patients with LDH, short-term outcomes following microdiscectomy were generally favorable despite substantial delays in diagnosis and referral for surgical treatment. These findings may suggest that, unlike in adults, prolonged symptom duration does not necessarily compromise recovery in children, although this should be interpreted with caution given the limited sample size and follow-up period. Nevertheless, increased awareness of pediatric LDH could facilitate earlier neurosurgical evaluation, streamline care pathways, and potentially further optimize patient management.Keywords: Lumbar disc herniation, Microdiscectomy, Juvenile, Delay

Introduction

Lumbar disc herniation (LDH) in children and adolescents is uncommon. Population-based studies are lacking, but juvenile LDHs constitute approximately 0.5–6.8% in surgical series [3, 4, 19, 29, 30].

The presenting symptoms are most often low back pain with or without radiating pain in the legs. Sensory or motor deficits may follow, but are less frequent. Associated bowel or bladder disturbances are rare in the pediatric population [16, 22, 25, 26, 29].

Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is now considered the standard investigative tool, with prior studies consistently identifying L4/L5 and L5/S1 as the most commonly affected levels in children and adolescents [4, 14, 18, 26, 28, 32].

In the absence of neurological deficits, initial management is typically conservative, including bed rest, analgesia, anti-inflammatory medication, and physiotherapy. In pediatric patients, surgical intervention is generally reserved for cases refractory to prolonged conservative management or in the presence of significant neurological impairment, such as motor deficit or bowel/bladder disturbance [4, 14, 18, 28, 32].

Due to the somewhat diffuse nature of the symptoms and the fact that the condition is by most physicians rarely encountered in children, delays in diagnosis and treatment seem quite typical [1, 3, 16]. We wanted to explore this delay further by performing a retrospective analysis of patients younger than 18 years, who underwent microdiscectomy for LDH at our institution during a 10-year period. Particular attention was given to variables associated with delayed diagnosis and treatment, including symptom duration prior to diagnosis, time to initial MRI, radiological findings, and time to surgery. Postoperative outcomes were evaluated to assess the effectiveness of surgical management in this cohort.

Methods and material

Study design

This is a retrospective study of all consecutive patients younger than 18 years, who underwent lumbar microdiscectomy (LMD) at one or more disc levels at the Department of Neurosurgery, Oslo University Hospital-Rikshospitalet (OUH-RH), between January 2015 and July 2025. The patients were identified by review of the department´s database. The study excluded patients with achondroplasia. All other patients were included, encompassing revision cases, recurrent disc herniations, and prior lumbar surgery.

Variables

Demographic factors registered were age (at the time of surgery), sex (female/male), comorbidity, participation in sport activities, presence of trauma immediately preceding diagnosis of LDH, and body mass index (BMI) as measured at the time of surgery. Symptoms (back pain, radiating pain in legs) and clinical signs (sensory, motor, or bowel or bladder disturbances) at the time of surgery were noted, based on information in patients´ medical reports.

All MRI scans were reviewed independently by both neurosurgeon (C.H.) and neuroradiologist (A.L.), who were blinded to clinical outcome at the time of the evaluation. Disc herniations at all lumbar levels were recorded along with their morphology, location, presence of sequester migration, and grade/severity of compression of the spinal canal.

The duration of symptoms from the time of onset to the first MRI scan, to the first assessment of a neurosurgeon, and to the surgery was noted. The level and the number of levels operated on were recorded.

Outcome parameters

Postoperative evaluation was assessed retrospectively based on patients` medical reports. By conducting a comprehensive review of postoperative clinical notes from both physicians and physiotherapists, outcome with regard to back pain and radiating pain was categorized into five groups: significant improvement, some improvement, no change, some impairment, and significant impairment. Although this grading system is not validated, it may be loosely comparable to thresholds used in validated pain scales such as the Numeric Rating Scale and Visual Analog Scale, where a ≥ 30% reduction is typically considered clinically meaningful and ≥ 50% substantial. This comparison should be interpreted with caution, as direct equivalence cannot be assumed. Motor deficit was graded using the MRC Muscle Power Scale 0–5 [17].

All patients were examined on the first postoperative day by a physiotherapist, noting symptom relief and changes in any neurological deficits. All complications and length of stay were recorded.

Later, all patients underwent a follow-up control at the outpatient clinic after 3 months, where complications, symptom relief, changes in any neurological deficits, return to school, activities of daily living (ADL), and ability to participate in sport activities were registered.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS version 30.0 (IBM Corporation, Chicago, IL, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation for presumed normally distributed variables. Presumed non-normally distributed data are reported providing median and range of values.

Use of large language models

Large Language Models, specifically ChatGPT, were used to improve the readability of the manuscript and to correct grammar and spelling errors. The authors reviewed and approved all final content.

Results

Patient characteristics

After review of the department´s database, we identified 21 patients ≤ 18 years of age, who underwent microdiscectomy for LDH at our institution between January 2015 and July 2025. Mean age at the time of surgery was 14.6 ± 2.1 years (range 9–17 years). There were 12 females (57.1%). The median body mass index (BMI) at the time of surgery was 21.2 (range 18.7–36.9); six patients (28.6%) had a BMI > 25 (i.e., above the threshold for overweight). Fifteen children (71.4%) participated in organized sport activities, defined as structured athletic training with competitive events. Most participants played football (six patients), the others handball and gymnastics (two patients each), basketball, and track and field (one patient each), which are all considered high impact sports, while sailing, swimming, and cross-country skiing (one patient each) are generally considered low impact sports. Two patients (9.5%) had a preexisting scoliosis, and three (14.3%) had other comorbidities (asthma, factor V Leiden mutation, hypothyroidism, autism and albinism, respectively). None of the patients had undergone back surgery previously. Three children (14.3%) had a history of trauma before the debut of symptoms attributable to LDH (Table 1).

Clinical symptoms and physical examination

The median time to the first assessment by a neurosurgeon at OUH-RH was 9 months (range 0–23 months), where 16 patients (76.2%) were evaluated within 12 months. Low back pain (100%) and radiating pain in one or both legs (95.2%) were the dominating symptoms on first evaluation; 38.1% of patients had a sensory and 28.6% a motor deficit. None of the patients complained of bowel or bladder disturbances (Table 11).

Radiological findings

All patients were examined with lumbosacral MRI. The median time to the first MRI scan was 6 months (range 0–12 months), where 19 patients (90.5%) underwent an MRI scan within 9 months after the debut of symptoms. Fifteen patients (71.4%) had had two or more MRI scans before surgery. Eighteen patients (85.7%) had disc herniation at one level; L4/L5 and L5/S1 were the two levels most often affected (Table 2).

Surgery and postoperative results

All patients were operated on with standard microdiscectomy. The median time to surgery from the debut of symptoms was 12 months (range 0–36 months). Nineteen patients (90.5%) were operated at one level. The typical intraoperative finding was a disc protrusion located centrally and at disc level with a moderate to severe compression of the spinal canal (Table 22).

No surgical complications were noted during the first 3 months. Additionally, no recurrent LDH or reoperations were recorded within the 3-month follow-up period. The length of stay in hospital was median 3 days (range 2–6 days) (Table 22).

On the first postoperative day, all patients were examined by a physiotherapist: 8 (38.1%) reported significant improvement and 12 (57.1%) some improvement of back pain (Fig. 1), while 14 (66.7%) reported significant improvement and 7 (33.3%) some improvement of the radiating pain in leg(s) (Fig. 1).

Postoperative outcomes regarding back pain (A) and radiating pain (B) at 1 day and 3 months after surgery. The bars represent number of patients in each outcome category

At 3 months postoperatively, 20 patients (95.2%) reported significant improvement of back pain, and all patients reported significant improvement of radiating pain in leg(s) (Fig. 1). The 6 patients with motor deficits at the time surgery had all improved at 3 months. Four patients with an initial MRC Grade 4 paresis and one with an initial MRC Grade 3 all achieved an MRC Grade 5 at 3 months. The one patient with an initial MRC Grade 2 paresis at the time of surgery had an MRC Grade 4 at 3 months.

After 3 months, all patients were participating in full school days and reported no problems in activities of daily living. Six out of the 15 patients (40%) who previously had participated in organized sport activities were back in training after 3 months.

Discussion

The main findings from this present study are generally favourable outcomes after surgical treatment of LDH in children and adolescents, but with a significant delay in diagnosis and treatment.

To our knowledge, there are no comprehensive studies reporting the incidence of LDH in the population younger than 18 years. One cohort study from Finland followed 12,058 individuals from birth to the age of 28 years, where the incidence of hospitalization due to LDH was 0.1–0.2% by the age of 20 years [33]. In surgical series, children and adolescents constituted approximately 0.5–6.8% of patients [3, 4, 19, 29, 30]. Although we do not know the exact number of surgeries for LDH in our referral population (approximately 3.2 million inhabitants of all ages in South-East region of Norway) during the study period, reports from the Nordic spine surgery registries indicate 58 surgeries for LDH per 100.000 inhabitants of all ages in Norway [13]. Thus, approximately 17.400 surgeries may have been performed in our referral population during the last 10 years, out of which our 21 pediatric patients would represent 0.12%. However, this is only an estimate based on the fact that most patients < 18 years with LDH and presumed indication for surgery are usually all referred to our department.

Regarding the demographic characteristics, the mean age at the time of surgery in our study (14.6 ± 2.1 years) was in line with other studies including patients < 18 years [19, 20, 25, 26] (Table 3). Comparison with other studies was difficult due to the inclusion of patients of different age spans. Similar to other studies, we found no significant sex predominance in our study (57.1% females) [2, 7, 9, 12, 14].

More interesting was the distribution of potential risk factors for LDH within the study population. In our cohort 28.6% of the patients were overweight (BMI > 25) and 9.5% had a preexisting scoliosis. Furthermore, almost three-quarters of the patients participated in organized sport activities, and 14.3% reported a history of trauma before the debut of symptoms. While we could not compare these figures with an age-appropriate control group of healthy children, several other studies have established an association between trauma, family history, and spine abnormalities, such as scoliosis, respectively, and LDH in young patients [4, 14, 18, 21, 26, 32]. These findings may offer some insight into characteristics that distinguish this cohort from the general pediatric population. The relatively high proportion of patients engaged in organized sport activities, of which the majority in high-impact sports, may suggest a role of increased mechanical load and repetitive stress on the lumbar spine during a period of skeletal development, potentially contributing to disc pathology. Similarly, a history of trauma, although reported in a minority of patients, may act as a precipitating factor in individuals with underlying susceptibility. The presence of scoliosis in a subset of patients may indicate altered spinal biomechanics, which could predispose to asymmetric disc loading and herniation.

With regard to BMI, our findings align with previous studies suggesting that elevated body mass may increase the risk of LDH in younger individuals. Qi et al. performed a case–control study comparing patients < 25 years diagnosed with LDH on MRI with healthy controls and found that BMI > 30 was a significant risk factor for LDH. Some other studies also suggested that high BMI increases the risk of LDH in very young patients, but the age span and BMI cut-off values in these studies varied too greatly to draw any robust conclusions [32].

Taken together, these observations suggest that LDH in pediatric patients is likely multifactorial, with mechanical, anatomical, and possibly lifestyle-related factors contributing to disease development.

Also, the presenting symptoms seen most often in our study are in line with existing literature: low back pain (82–100% of cases), radiating pain in leg/legs (35–95%), and motor or sensory deficits (16–40%) [16, 22, 25, 26, 29].

MRI is now the standard radiological modality of investigation when LDH is suspected (Fig. 2). In line with observation in the present study, the most often affected spine levels according to the literature are L4/5 (43.8–56.7%) and L5/S1(34–53%) [4, 14, 18, 26, 28, 32].

For symptomatic LDH without motor or sensory deficits, conservative treatment consisting of bed rest, analgesic medication, anti-inflammatory drugs, and physiotherapy can be successful in 25–50% of cases [5, 11, 31]. The use of intradiscal therapy in children and adolescents is scarcely reported: some studies on the use of chemonucleolysis in adolescents were conducted in the 1980 s and 1990 s, with somewhat favorable results [4], but to the best of our knowledge, its use today is limited, particularly in the pediatric population. Surgery for a LDH in the pediatric population is considered relevant when significant motor deficits and/or bowel or bladder disturbances are present, or if conservative treatment over a longer period of time has failed in patients, even without neurological deficits [6, 15, 24].

A key finding of our study was the substantial delay between symptom onset and both imaging and surgical intervention. The median time from symptom onset to the first MRI examination was 6 months, while the median time to surgery was 12 months, which is considerably longer than reported in adult populations, where median time to surgery has been estimated at 21 weeks (IQR 12–37) [23] and a mean of 22.5 weeks [8].

This pronounced delay suggests that LDH may be under-recognized in pediatric patients. Several factors may contribute to this fact. Parents may attribute early symptoms, such as back pain or leg pain, to benign causes like muscle soreness due to exercise or “growing pains,” potentially contributing to a delay in seeking medical attention. Furthermore, LDH is relatively rare in children and, therefore, may not be initially considered by general practitioners and pediatricians, leading to late referrals to diagnostic MRI. Finally, there seems to be a tendency to prolonged conservative management to avoid surgery and its possible negative impact on the developing spine and paravertebral musculature.

Prolonged waiting for surgery in LDH (> 2–12 months) has been associated with poorer long-term outcomes in adult populations. [23, 27]. However, the findings of the present study suggest that this relationship may not translate to pediatric patients. Despite extended delays prior to surgical intervention, no corresponding negative impact on short-term outcomes was observed in the pediatric cohort. This discrepancy may reflect important differences in underlying pathology and recovery potential between age groups. Pediatric patients are more likely to present with relatively isolated disc herniations, often with less degenerative change and a larger soft disc component, which may contribute to more favorable and time-insensitive outcomes. In contrast, outcomes in adults may be influenced by more complex and multifactorial conditions, including degenerative changes and comorbidities affecting lower limb function.

Limitations

This study has the typical limitations of a retrospective cohort design, including the risk of selection bias and limited control of potential confounding factors. In addition, causal relationships cannot be firmly established due to the observational nature of the study. The study is further limited by a low number of cases, reflecting the rarity of the condition in this particular age group. On the other side, the small number of patients left us with no missing data. Given the overall favorable results at 3-month follow-up as described above, there was from a clinical point of view no need for further follow-up, which thus remains relatively short for the whole group. Finally, and as also mentioned above, there was no control group of healthy children to compare our findings to; given the low number of cases, however, this would probably not yield any more robust conclusions.

Conclusion

In our cohort of pediatric patients with LDH, short-term outcomes following microdiscectomy were generally favorable despite substantial delays in diagnosis and referral for surgical treatment. These findings may suggest that, unlike in adults, prolonged symptom duration does not necessarily compromise recovery in children, although this should be interpreted with caution given the limited sample size and follow-up period. Nevertheless, increased awareness of pediatric LDH could facilitate earlier neurosurgical evaluation, streamline care pathways, and potentially further optimize patient management.
