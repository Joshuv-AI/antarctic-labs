Introduction

The optimal control of hyperbolic partial differential equations has been extensively studied because of its theoretical importance and numerous applications in acoustics, vibration control, structural dynamics and wave engineering
[
2
,
7
,
8
,
13
]
. In many settings, the objective is to determine controls minimizing quadratic performance criteria while steering the system toward a prescribed target or tracking desired trajectories. Depending on the application, the control action may be distributed over a subregion, imposed through the boundary, or concentrated at a finite number of localized devices. Finite-dimensional and finite-band formulations are particularly attractive when only selected modal components are relevant.

Among localized mechanisms, point or Dirac-type actuators provide a convenient mathematical idealization of highly concentrated sources and have motivated substantial developments in controllability, stabilization and optimization. However, these actuators are typically postulated at the model level rather than derived from an underlying physical mechanism. This observation motivates the present work. We exploit the effective behavior of resonant bubble clusters, whose collective subwavelength dynamics generate localized monopole fields that can be interpreted as internal actuators for the acoustic wave equation. Building on recent asymptotic developments, we investigate the associated finite-band quadratic optimal control problem and quantify the interaction between resonant actuation and optimal control design.

The control of acoustic waves by small internal devices is usually formulated after an idealization: one prescribes localized sources, boundary inputs, or distributed controls, and then analyzes the corresponding control-to-state map. For resonant bubbly media there is an additional modelling layer. The localized sources are not imposed directly; they are generated by the oscillations of small high-contrast bubbles. Near Minnaert resonance these oscillations are strong, frequency selective, and governed by the multiple scattering between the bubbles. Hence the actuator is not a static source but a resonant internal system whose parameters are determined by the geometry and material contrast of the bubbles.

The mathematical modeling of wave propagation in bubbly media has a rich history; see, e.g., the foundational work on effective macroscopic properties

The starting point of the present work is the time-domain asymptotic description of waves in the presence of finitely many small bubbles. If
u^{\varepsilon}
denotes the acoustic pressure and
u^{\rm in}
the incident field, then, away from the bubbles and on fixed time intervals, the scattered field is approximated by a retarded Foldy–Lax expansion

u^{\varepsilon}(x,t)-u^{\rm in}(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}G_{0}(x,z_{i})q_{i}^{\varepsilon}\bigl(t-c_{0}^{-1}|x-z_{i}|\bigr)+r^{\varepsilon}(x,t),

(1)

where the amplitudes

solve a delayed Foldy–Lax system. The construction used in
[

distributes the bubbles into local clusters: bubbles belonging to the same cluster are close, whereas different clusters are separated at the macroscopic scale. Consequently, the Laplace-domain Foldy–Lax matrix is dominated by local intra-cluster blocks. Each block has a principal collective Minnaert pole

s_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}=-\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}+\mathrm{i}\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon},\qquad\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}>0,

(2)

which gives one effective collective actuator channel. The real part

After projecting the field generated by these collective channels onto a finite acoustic spectral band, one obtains the finite-dimensional wave–actuator system. This finite-band restriction preserves the uniform control properties of the low-frequency dynamics, which are typically lost in unfiltered discrete wave approximations

\displaystyle\ddot{a}+\Lambda a

\displaystyle=C_{\varepsilon}\eta,

(M)

\displaystyle=u.

For comparison, if localized sources at macroscopic cluster centers

Several natural choices of the observation matrix arise in practice:

1.

Full-state observation:
Choosing

2.

3.

Weighted observations:
A diagonal matrix

Pointwise measurements:
If the acoustic field is observed at spatial locations

\mathcal{R}_{ik}=\psi_{k}(x_{i}),

More generally, if observations correspond to averages over

so that each output component represents a spatially averaged measurement.

Thus,

In

12

Here the effective resonant channels are incorporated into an optimal-control problem: the desired acoustic output is prescribed, the collective source amplitude

The contribution is therefore a quantitative analysis of how the collective pole locations of the bubble clusters enter the quadratic control cost, the detuning estimates, and the finite-band stabilization mechanism. This distinction is essential for applications, since it identifies not only which source profiles can be produced, but also how efficiently they can be produced through the physical resonant actuator dynamics.

In this form, the analysis separates four aspects that are often combined in idealized formulations: the finite-band acoustic dynamics, the collective resonant actuator dynamics, the spatial modal coupling matrix, and the frequency-dependent input cost. This separation makes the role of resonance explicit while keeping the control problem finite-dimensional and fully quantitative.

This provides a rigorous bridge between the physics of resonant microstructured actuators and optimal control theory, showing how the location of the collective resonances directly influences tracking performance, control cost, and the efficiency with which desired finite-band wave fields can be generated.

Let

\kappa(x)^{-1}\partial_{t}^{2}u^{\varepsilon}-\nabla\cdot\big(\rho(x)^{-1}\nabla u^{\varepsilon}\big)=F\quad\hbox{in }(\mathbb{R}^{3}\setminus\partial D^{\varepsilon})\times(0,T),

(12)

with continuity of pressure and normal velocity across each

On compact observation sets separated from the bubbles and for fixed

u^{\varepsilon}(x,t)-u^{\rm in}(x,t)=\sum_{i=1}^{M}G_{0}(x,z_{i})q_{i}^{\varepsilon}\bigl(t-c_{0}^{-1}|x-z_{i}|\bigr)+r^{\varepsilon}(x,t),

where

In the normalization used in

D_{i}(\partial_{t}):=1+\omega_{M,i}^{-2}\partial_{t}^{2},\qquad\tau_{ij}:=c_{0}^{-1}|z_{i}-z_{j}|,\qquad q_{ij}^{\varepsilon}:=\frac{\varepsilon C_{j}}{4\pi|z_{i}-z_{j}|},\quad i\neq j.

Here

is a scalar interaction strength and contains no additional time differentiation. Equivalently,

\left(1+\omega_{M,i}^{-2}\partial_{t}^{2}\right)q_{i}^{\varepsilon}(t)+\sum_{j\neq i}\frac{\varepsilon C_{j}}{4\pi|z_{i}-z_{j}|}\partial_{t}^{2}q_{j}^{\varepsilon}(t-\tau_{ij})=\partial_{t}^{2}f_{i}(t).

The precise constants depend on the chosen amplitude normalization; the form (

2.3
Spectral isolation of principal Minnaert poles and collective channels

We now explain why the internal part of the reduced model can be written in diagonal form, although the microscopic Foldy–Lax system is fully coupled inside each cluster. This point is important: the diagonal matrices used later are not imposed at the bubble level; they are obtained after selecting one isolated collective pole from each local Foldy–Lax block.

For
i,j\in I_{\alpha}
,
i\neq j
, the construction in
[
12
]
uses close intra-cluster distances of order
\varepsilon^{p}
,
0<p<1
, while
|z_{i}-z_{j}|
is of order one when
i
and
j
belong to distinct clusters. After reordering the bubbles by clusters, the Laplace-domain Foldy–Lax pencil

A^{\varepsilon}(s):=D(s)+s^{2}Q^{\varepsilon}(s)

(25)

therefore has the block-dominant structure

A^{\varepsilon}(s)=\operatorname{diag}\big(A_{1}^{\varepsilon}(s),\ldots,A_{N_{c}}^{\varepsilon}(s)\big)+E_{\rm inter}^{\varepsilon}(s).

(26)

Here
A_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(s)
is the restriction of
A^{\varepsilon}(s)
to the bubbles of
I_{\alpha}
, while
E_{\rm inter}^{\varepsilon}(s)
contains the interactions between distinct clusters. The blocks
A_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(s)
are not diagonal: their off-diagonal entries contain the strong local interactions between the bubbles of the same cluster. The inter-cluster part is lower order in the regime considered in
[
12
]
and is treated perturbatively in the pole analysis.

Proposition 2.1 of
[
12
]
gives the following spectral picture for the microscopic transfer matrix. In the relevant right half-plane,
H_{b}^{\varepsilon}(s)
is meromorphic and its poles are contained in the zero set of

\det A^{\varepsilon}(s)=0.

(27)

For each local cluster
I_{\alpha}
there is a distinguished simple pole

s_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}=-\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}+\mathrm{i}\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon},\qquad\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}>0,

(28)

near the corresponding Minnaert frequency. This pole is separated from the other local poles by the asymptotic cluster gap proved in

12
]

is used as well when real-valued time-domain signals are reconstructed.

Let

denote the right and left principal resonant vectors associated with the pole

and normalized so that the corresponding residue is non-zero. For a simple pole of the matrix pencil

H_{b}^{\varepsilon}(s)=\frac{r_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\,l_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}{}^{*}}{s-s_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}}\,\kappa_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}+H_{\rm reg,\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(s)\qquad\text{near }s_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon},

(29)

where

is holomorphic. The scalar factor
\kappa_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{C}
is the generalized derivative trace, defined exactly by the first-order Taylor expansion of the pencil at the pole:

\kappa_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}=\big(l_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}{}^{*}\frac{\partial A^{\varepsilon}}{\partial s}\big|_{s_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}}r_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\big)^{-1}.

Equivalently, after applying the cluster-output map
B_{\rm out}
and the transducer trace map

H_{\rm cl}^{\varepsilon}(s)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{c}}\frac{b_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\ell_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}{}^{*}}{s-s_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}}+H_{\rm reg}^{\varepsilon}(s),

(30)

H_{\rm reg}^{\varepsilon}

and
\ell_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}
are the projected right and left principal cluster modes; they include the effects of

The principal collective coordinate can be represented, at leading order, by testing the microscopic monopole vector on the corresponding resonant mode. In time-domain notation we write

\eta_{\alpha}(t):=\sum_{i\in I_{\alpha}}\ell_{\alpha i}^{\varepsilon}q_{i}^{\varepsilon}(t).

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\beta_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}=-\omega_{M,\alpha}^{2}\rho_{\alpha}^{(0)}
, with
\rho_{\alpha}^{(0)}:=\big(B_{\rm out}r_{\alpha}^{(0)}\big)\big(l_{\alpha}^{(0)}{}^{*}G_{\rm tr}\big)
.

Proof.

We begin by recalling the fact that the local cluster transfer function
H_{\rm cl,\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(s)
admits the exact local decomposition

H_{\rm cl,\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(s)=\frac{P_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}}{s-s_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}}+H_{\rm reg,\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(s),

where the scalar residue is exactly
P_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}=\kappa_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(B_{\rm out}r_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})(l_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}{}^{*}G_{\rm tr})
.

We now analyze the asymptotic limits as
\varepsilon\to 0
. We start with analyzing
\kappa_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}=\big(l_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}{}^{*}\frac{\partial A^{\varepsilon}}{\partial s}\big|_{s_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}}r_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\big)^{-1}
at the unperturbed state (
\varepsilon\to 0
). The unperturbed local block is the diagonal matrix
A_{\alpha\alpha}^{(0)}(s)=\operatorname{diag}(1+s^{2}/\omega_{M,i}^{2})_{i\in I_{\alpha}}
and due to the generic assumption
\omega_{M,i}=\omega_{M,\alpha}
, we derive

\left.\frac{\partial A^{(0)}}{\partial s}\right|_{s=\mathrm{i}\omega_{M,\alpha}}=\operatorname{diag}\left(\frac{2s}{\omega_{M,\alpha}^{2}}\right)_{s=\mathrm{i}\omega_{M,\alpha}}=\frac{2\mathrm{i}}{\omega_{M,\alpha}}\mathbf{I}_{|I_{\alpha}|}.

Let
r_{\alpha}^{(0)}
and
l_{\alpha}^{(0)}
be the unperturbed right and left Perron-Frobenius eigenvectors. As the effective capacitance matrix of the cluster is real and symmetric, it follows that
r_{\alpha}^{(0)},l_{\alpha}^{(0)}\in\mathbb{R}
. Due to
B_{\rm out},G_{\rm tr}\in\mathbb{R}
, the unperturbed geometric projection
\rho_{\alpha}^{(0)}:=\big(B_{\rm out}r_{\alpha}^{(0)}\big)\big(l_{\alpha}^{(0)}{}^{*}G_{\rm tr}\big)\in\mathbb{R}
. Adopting the standard inner-product normalization
l_{\alpha}^{(0)}{}^{*}r_{\alpha}^{(0)}=1
, we further derive that

\big(\kappa_{\alpha}^{(0)}\big)^{-1}=l_{\alpha}^{(0)}{}^{*}\left(\frac{2\mathrm{i}}{\omega_{M,\alpha}}\mathbf{I}_{|I_{\alpha}|}\right)r_{\alpha}^{(0)}=\frac{2\mathrm{i}}{\omega_{M,\alpha}}\implies\kappa_{\alpha}^{(0)}=\frac{\omega_{M,\alpha}}{2\mathrm{i}}=-\mathrm{i}\frac{\omega_{M,\alpha}}{2}.

(33)

Crucially,
\kappa_{\alpha}^{(0)}
is strictly purely imaginary.

Now, as proven in
[
12
]
, the perturbed eigenvectors satisfy
r_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}=r_{\alpha}^{(0)}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1-p})
and
l_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}=l_{\alpha}^{(0)}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1-p})
for
p\in(0,1)
. Consequently, the perturbed residue admits the strict asymptotic expansion

P_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}=\kappa_{\alpha}^{(0)}\rho_{\alpha}^{(0)}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1-p})=-\mathrm{i}\frac{\omega_{M,\alpha}}{2}\rho_{\alpha}^{(0)}+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1-p}).

Because the physical time-domain system is real-valued, the principal expansion must include the complex conjugate pole
\overline{s_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}}=-\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}-\mathrm{i}\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}
. Combining the conjugate pair yields the rational singular part:

\Pi_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(s)=\frac{P_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}}{s-s_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}}+\frac{\overline{P_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}}}{s-\overline{s_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}}}=\frac{2\operatorname{Re}(P_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})(s+\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})+2\operatorname{Im}(P_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}}{(s+\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})^{2}+(\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})^{2}}.

(34)

Consequently, due to the fact that
\operatorname{Re}(P_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1-p})
and
\operatorname{Im}(P_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})=\mathcal{O}(1)
, defining
\beta_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}:=2\operatorname{Im}(P_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}
, the numerator becomes
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1-p})(s+\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})+\beta_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}
. On the compact acoustic band
K\subset\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}
, the dynamic factor
|s+\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}|
is uniformly bounded, reducing the numerator to
\beta_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1-p}))
. We also establish the limit of the coupling coefficient as
\varepsilon\to 0

\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\beta_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}=2\left(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\operatorname{Im}(P_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})\right)\left(\lim_{\varepsilon\to 0}\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\right)=2\left(-\frac{\omega_{M,\alpha}}{2}\rho_{\alpha}^{(0)}\right)\omega_{M,\alpha}=-\omega_{M,\alpha}^{2}\rho_{\alpha}^{(0)}.

Finally, by continuity, the regular remainder
H_{\rm reg,\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(s)
is uniformly bounded by an
\mathcal{O}(1)
constant on the compact set
K
. Because the distance to the principal pole scales as
|s-s_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}|=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1-p})
while the distance to the conjugate pole is macroscopic (
|s-\overline{s_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}}|=\mathcal{O}(1)
), the singular denominator scales strictly as
\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1-p})
. Thus,
|\Pi_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(s)|\sim\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-(1-p)})
.

Following that, due to the uniform ratio bound,

\left|\frac{H_{\rm reg,\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(s)}{\Pi_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(s)}\right|\leq\frac{\mathcal{O}(1)}{\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{-(1-p)})}=\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1-p}),

factoring out the singular principal part
\Pi_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(s)
yields

H_{\rm cl,\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(s)=\Pi_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}(s)\big(1+\mathcal{O}(\varepsilon^{1-p})\big).

Substituting the uniform numerator expansion established beforehand concludes the proof of the canonical representation.
∎

Applying the input renormalization

u_{\alpha}:=\beta_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}V_{\mu},

(35)

the principal band-projected relation maps directly to the canonical scalar channel

\ddot{\eta}_{\alpha}+2\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\dot{\eta}_{\alpha}+\big((\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})^{2}+(\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})^{2}\big)\eta_{\alpha}=u_{\alpha}.

(36)

The characteristic roots of (
36
) are exactly
-\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\pm\mathrm{i}\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}
. The damping coefficient is therefore the negative real part of the collective pole and is inherited from the bubble-cluster scattering mechanism.

Collecting one principal channel from each cluster gives

\ddot{\eta}+2\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\dot{\eta}+K_{\varepsilon}\eta=u,\qquad\Gamma_{\varepsilon}=\operatorname{diag}(\gamma_{1}^{\varepsilon},\ldots,\gamma_{N_{c}}^{\varepsilon}),

(37)

with

K_{\varepsilon}=\operatorname{diag}\big((\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})^{2}+(\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})^{2}\big)_{\alpha=1}^{N_{c}}.

(38)

The diagonal structure in (
37
)–(
38
) is a consequence of the choice of isolated principal-pole coordinates. The discarded terms are precisely the regular part of the transfer matrix, the higher local modes, and the lower-order inter-cluster corrections; these will be collected in the remainder
\rho_{\varepsilon}
after projection onto the acoustic band. The coupling between the selected acoustic modes and the selected resonant channels is not diagonal in general and is encoded in the matrix
C_{\varepsilon}
.

2.4
Finite-band Galerkin projection and the induced control system

Let
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{3}
be a bounded
C^{1,1}
domain such that the actuator region
D^{\varepsilon}=\bigcup_{i=1}^{M}D_{i}^{\varepsilon}\Subset\Omega
. For a fixed control horizon
T>0
, we assume the finite propagation constraint

\operatorname{dist}(D^{\varepsilon},\partial\Omega)>c_{0}T.

(39)

Let
-\Delta_{D}
denote the Dirichlet Laplacian on
\Omega
, equipped with an
L^{2}(\Omega)
-orthonormal eigenbasis
\{\psi_{k}\}_{k=1}^{\infty}\subset H_{0}^{1}(\Omega)\cap H^{2}(\Omega)
and discrete spectrum
0<\lambda_{1}\leq\lambda_{2}\leq\cdots\to\infty
. We define the acoustic angular frequencies
\omega_{k}:=c_{0}\sqrt{\lambda_{k}}
.

The macroscopic scattered pressure
p^{\varepsilon}:=u^{\varepsilon}-u^{\rm in}
satisfies the Dirichlet boundary-value problem

\begin{cases}(\partial_{tt}-c_{0}^{2}\Delta)p^{\varepsilon}(x,t)=c_{0}^{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{c}}\beta_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\eta_{\alpha}(t)\delta_{y_{\alpha}}(x),&(x,t)\in\Omega\times(0,T),\\
p^{\varepsilon}(x,t)=0,&(x,t)\in\partial\Omega\times(0,T),\end{cases}

(40)

where
y_{\alpha}\in\Omega
are the cluster centers and
\eta_{\alpha}(t)\in\mathbb{R}
is the canonical collective coordinate of the
\alpha
-th cluster corresponding to the input-output coefficient
\beta_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}
(see Section
2.3
).

Given a finite target spectral band
J\subset(0,\infty)
, we define the index set
K_{J}:=\{k\in\mathbb{N}:\omega_{k}\in J\}
with cardinality
n:=|K_{J}|
, and the associated finite-dimensional subspace
H_{J}:=\operatorname{span}\{\psi_{k}\}_{k\in K_{J}}
. Because
H^{2}(\Omega)\hookrightarrow C^{0}(\overline{\Omega})
in three dimensions, the point evaluations
\psi_{k}(y_{\alpha})
are well-defined. The Galerkin projection
p_{k}(t):=\langle p^{\varepsilon}(\cdot,t),\psi_{k}\rangle_{L^{2}(\Omega)}
reduces (
40
) to the modal system

\ddot{p}_{k}(t)+\omega_{k}^{2}p_{k}(t)=c_{0}^{2}\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{c}}\beta_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\eta_{\alpha}(t)\psi_{k}(y_{\alpha}),\quad\forall k\in K_{J}.

(41)

Collecting the modal coordinates into the acoustic state vector
a(t):=(p_{k}(t))_{k\in K_{J}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the projected dynamics admit the vectorized second-order representation

\ddot{a}(t)+\Lambda a(t)=C_{\varepsilon}\eta(t).

(42)

Coupling this with the internal macroscopic cluster dynamics (
37
) yields the complete finite-dimensional control system

\begin{cases}\ddot{a}(t)+\Lambda a(t)=C_{\varepsilon}\eta(t),\\
\ddot{\eta}(t)+2\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\dot{\eta}(t)+K_{\varepsilon}\eta(t)=u(t).\end{cases}

(43)

Here, the structural matrices
\Lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}
and
C_{\varepsilon}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times N_{c}}
are strictly defined by

\Lambda:=\operatorname{diag}(\omega_{k}^{2})_{k\in K_{J}},\qquad(C_{\varepsilon})_{k\alpha}:=c_{0}^{2}\beta_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\psi_{k}(y_{\alpha}),

(44)

and
u\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}})
acts as the external control input.

We formulate the coupled finite-band system as an abstract Cauchy problem on a finite-dimensional state space
X
. The generation of the associated continuous semigroup and the properties of the control operator follow from standard linear systems theory
[
2
,
13
,
15
]
.

3
Linear-quadratic optimal tracking for the finite-band system

Let

x=(a,v,\eta,\zeta):=(a,\dot{a},\eta,\dot{\eta})\in X:=\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{n}\times\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}}\times\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}}.

Then (
43
) is

\dot{x}=A_{\varepsilon}x+Bu,

(45)

with

A_{\varepsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}0&I&0&0\\
-\Lambda&0&C_{\varepsilon}&0\\
0&0&0&I\\
0&0&-K_{\varepsilon}&-2\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix},\qquad B=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
0\\
0\\
I\end{pmatrix}.

(46)

Let
\mathcal{R}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
be an observation matrix and define

\mathcal{J}_{\mu}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert\mathcal{R}a(t;u)-a_{d}(t)\right\rVert^{2}\,dt+\frac{\mu}{2}\int_{0}^{T}\left\lVert u(t)\right\rVert^{2}\,dt.

(47)

Theorem 3.1
.

For every
\mu>0
,
x_{0}\in X
and
a_{d}\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{m})
, there exists a unique minimizer

u_{\mu}^{*}\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}})

of (
47
) subject to (
45
) and
x(0)=x_{0}
.

Proof.

Let
\Pi_{a}
denote the projection from the full state

x=(a,v,\eta,\zeta)\in X

onto the acoustic modal component
a\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For a given control

u\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}})
, the solution of the finite-dimensional linear system is given by
the variation-of-constants formula

x(t;u)=e^{tA_{\varepsilon}}x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)A_{\varepsilon}}Bu(s)\,ds.

Therefore

a(t;u)=\Pi_{a}e^{tA_{\varepsilon}}x_{0}+\Pi_{a}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)A_{\varepsilon}}Bu(s)\,ds.

Set

a^{0}(t):=\Pi_{a}e^{tA_{\varepsilon}}x_{0}

and define

(\mathcal{K}u)(t):=R\Pi_{a}\int_{0}^{t}e^{(t-s)A_{\varepsilon}}Bu(s)\,ds.

Then

\mathcal{R}a(\cdot;u)=\mathcal{R}a^{0}+\mathcal{K}u.

The map

\mathcal{K}:L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}})\longrightarrow L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{m})

is bounded and linear, since the system is finite-dimensional and

t\mapsto e^{tA_{\varepsilon}}B
is bounded on
[0,T]
. Hence the control-to-output map

u\longmapsto\mathcal{R}a(\cdot;u)

is continuous and affine. It is affine, rather than linear, because of the free evolution

\mathcal{R}a^{0}
generated by the initial condition
x_{0}
. With this notation, the cost functional can be written as

\mathcal{J}_{\mu}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\mathcal{K}u-(a_{d}-\mathcal{R}a^{0})\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{m})}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}})}.

This functional is continuous and convex. Moreover, since
\mu>0
, the term

\frac{\mu}{2}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}})}

is strictly convex. Therefore
\mathcal{J}_{\mu}
is strictly convex, independently of whether

\mathcal{K}
has a nontrivial kernel. The same term also gives coercivity:

\mathcal{J}_{\mu}(u)\geq\frac{\mu}{2}\|u\|^{2}_{L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}})}.

Let
(u_{j})
be a minimizing sequence. By coercivity,
(u_{j})
is bounded in

L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}})
. Hence, after extracting a subsequence, there exists

u_{\mu}^{*}\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}})
such that
u_{j}\rightharpoonup u_{\mu}^{*}\quad\text{weakly in }L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}}).

Since
\mathcal{J}_{\mu}
is convex and continuous, it is weakly lower semicontinuous. Therefore

\mathcal{J}_{\mu}(u_{\mu}^{*})\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}\mathcal{J}_{\mu}(u_{j})=\inf_{u\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}})}\mathcal{J}_{\mu}(u).

Hence
u_{\mu}^{*}
is a minimizer. Finally, the strict convexity of
\mathcal{J}_{\mu}
implies that
this minimizer is unique.
∎

Proposition 3.2

(Pontryagin optimality system)
.

Let
u_{\mu}^{*}
be the minimizer and let
(a,v,\eta,\zeta)
be the corresponding state. There are adjoint variables
(p_{a},p_{v},p_{\eta},p_{\zeta})
satisfying

p_{a}(T)=p_{v}(T)=p_{\eta}(T)=p_{\zeta}(T)=0

and

\displaystyle-\dot{p}_{a}+\Lambda p_{v}

\displaystyle=R^{\top}(Ra-a_{d}),

(48)

\displaystyle-\dot{p}_{v}-p_{a}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle-\dot{p}_{\eta}-C_{\varepsilon}^{\top}p_{v}+K_{\varepsilon}p_{\zeta}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle-\dot{p}_{\zeta}-p_{\eta}+2\Gamma_{\varepsilon}p_{\zeta}

\displaystyle=0.

The optimal control is

u_{\mu}^{*}(t)=-\alpha^{-1}p_{\zeta}(t).

(49)

Proof.

We recall the finite-dimensional Pontryagin minimum principle in the form used here. For a system

\dot{x}=f(t,x,u),\qquad x(0)=x_{0},

and a functional

J(u)=\int_{0}^{T}L(t,x(t),u(t))\,dt+\Phi(x(T)),

if
u^{\star}
is optimal and
x^{\star}
is the corresponding trajectory, then there is an adjoint variable

p:[0,T]\to X
such that, with the Hamiltonian

\mathcal{H}(t,x,u,p)=L(t,x,u)+p^{\top}f(t,x,u),

one has

-\dot{p}(t)=\partial_{x}\mathcal{H}(t,x^{\star}(t),u^{\star}(t),p(t)),\qquad p(T)=\nabla\Phi(x^{\star}(T)),

(50)

and, for a.e.
t\in(0,T)
,

\partial_{u}\mathcal{H}(t,x^{\star}(t),u^{\star}(t),p(t))=0.

(51)

In the present problem,

f(t,x,u)=A_{\varepsilon}x+Bu,\qquad L(t,x,u)=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{R}a-a_{d}(t)\|^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|u\|^{2},\qquad\Phi\equiv 0.

Thus the Hamiltonian is

\mathcal{H}(t,x,u,p)=\frac{1}{2}\|\mathcal{R}a-a_{d}(t)\|^{2}+\frac{\mu}{2}\|u\|^{2}+p^{\top}(A_{\varepsilon}x+Bu).

(52)

Since there is no terminal cost, the transversality condition in (
50
) gives

p(T)=0.

The
x
-derivative of (
52
) is

\partial_{x}\mathcal{H}(t,x,u,p)=A_{\varepsilon}^{\top}p+\begin{pmatrix}R^{\top}(\mathcal{R}a-a_{d}(t))\\
0\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix}.

Consequently the adjoint equation is

-\dot{p}=A_{\varepsilon}^{\top}p+\begin{pmatrix}\mathcal{R}^{\top}(\mathcal{R}a-a_{d})\\
0\\
0\\
0\end{pmatrix},\qquad p(T)=0.

(53)

The derivative with respect to
u
gives

\partial_{u}\mathcal{H}(t,x,u,p)=\alpha u+B^{\top}p.

The stationarity condition (
51
) therefore yields

\alpha u_{\mu}^{*}+B^{\top}p=0.

(54)

It remains only to write (
53
) in components. Let

p=(p_{a},p_{v},p_{\eta},p_{\zeta}),\qquad p_{a},p_{v}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},\quad p_{\eta},p_{\zeta}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}}.

From (
46
),

A_{\varepsilon}^{\top}=\begin{pmatrix}0&-\Lambda&0&0\\
I&0&0&0\\
0&C_{\varepsilon}^{\top}&0&-K_{\varepsilon}\\
0&0&I&-2\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix},\qquad B^{\top}p=p_{\zeta}.

Substituting this expression in (
53
) gives exactly

\displaystyle-\dot{p}_{a}+\Lambda p_{v}

\displaystyle=\mathcal{R}^{\top}(\mathcal{R}a-a_{d}),

\displaystyle-\dot{p}_{v}-p_{a}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle-\dot{p}_{\eta}-C_{\varepsilon}^{\top}p_{v}+K_{\varepsilon}p_{\zeta}

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle-\dot{p}_{\zeta}-p_{\eta}+2\Gamma_{\varepsilon}p_{\zeta}

\displaystyle=0,

with terminal conditions

p_{a}(T)=p_{v}(T)=p_{\eta}(T)=p_{\zeta}(T)=0.

Finally, (
54
) and
B^{\top}p=p_{\zeta}
give

u_{\mu}^{*}(t)=-\alpha^{-1}p_{\zeta}(t),

which proves the claimed optimality system.
∎

4
Resonant Source-Lifting and Quantitative Optimal Bounds

4.1
Source-Lifting Estimates and Frequency Detuning

Let
a_{r}\in H^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{n})
be a prescribed finite-band trajectory. An exact collective source output
\eta_{r}
must satisfy

C_{\varepsilon}\eta_{r}(t)=\ddot{a}_{r}(t)+\Lambda a_{r}(t).

(55)

We impose the finite-band source rank condition

\operatorname{rank}C_{\varepsilon}=n.

(56)

This is the same modal point-source rank condition as in finite-band Dirac control. For generic cluster centers
y_{\alpha}
and
N_{c}\geq n
, it holds outside a proper analytic variety. Let
C_{\varepsilon}^{\dagger}
be a fixed right inverse and set

\eta_{r}=C_{\varepsilon}^{\dagger}(\ddot{a}_{r}+\Lambda a_{r}).

(57)

The paid control needed to generate
\eta_{r}
through the collective channels is

u_{r}=(\partial_{t}^{2}+2\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}+K_{\varepsilon})\eta_{r}.

(58)

Definition 4.1

(Finite-time band class)
.

Let
I\subset(0,\infty)
be compact. A function
g\in L^{2}(0,T)
belongs to
\mathcal{B}_{T}(I)
if there exists
\widetilde{g}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
such that
\widetilde{g}|_{(0,T)}=g
and

\operatorname{supp}\widehat{\widetilde{g}}\subset I\cup(-I).

The norm is

\|g\|_{\mathcal{B}_{T}(I)}=\inf\{\|\widetilde{g}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}:\widetilde{g}|_{(0,T)}=g,\ \operatorname{supp}\widehat{\widetilde{g}}\subset I\cup(-I)\}.

For each channel define

d_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(\nu)=\left|(\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})^{2}+(\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})^{2}-\nu^{2}+2\mathrm{i}\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\nu\right|.

(59)

For compact bands
I_{\alpha}
set

\Delta_{\varepsilon}(I_{1},\ldots,I_{N_{c}})=\max_{1\leq\alpha\leq N_{c}}\sup_{\nu\in I_{\alpha}}d_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(\nu).

(60)

Theorem 4.2
.

If
(\eta_{r})_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{B}_{T}(I_{\alpha})
for all
\alpha
, then

\|u_{r}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}\leq\sum_{\alpha=1}^{N_{c}}\left(\sup_{\nu\in I_{\alpha}}d_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(\nu)\right)^{2}\|(\eta_{r})_{\alpha}\|_{\mathcal{B}_{T}(I_{\alpha})}^{2}.

(61)

In particular,

\|u_{r}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}\leq\Delta_{\varepsilon}(I_{1},\ldots,I_{N_{c}})\|\eta_{r}\|_{\prod_{\alpha}\mathcal{B}_{T}(I_{\alpha})}.

(62)

Proof.

Fix
\alpha\in\{1,\dots,N_{c}\}
. Let
\widetilde{\eta}_{\alpha}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
be an arbitrary extension of
(\eta_{r})_{\alpha}
matching on
(0,T)
with
\operatorname{supp}\widehat{\widetilde{\eta}}_{\alpha}\subset I_{\alpha}\cup(-I_{\alpha})
. Define

\widetilde{u}_{\alpha}=(\partial_{t}^{2}+2\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}+(\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})^{2}+(\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})^{2})\widetilde{\eta}_{\alpha}

on
\mathbb{R}
, yielding
\widetilde{u}_{\alpha}|_{(0,T)}=(u_{r})_{\alpha}
. With the convention
\widehat{\partial_{t}g}(\nu)=\mathrm{i}\nu\widehat{g}(\nu)
, taking the Fourier transform gives

\widehat{\widetilde{u}}_{\alpha}(\nu)=\big((\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})^{2}+(\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})^{2}-\nu^{2}+2\mathrm{i}\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\nu\big)\widehat{\widetilde{\eta}}_{\alpha}(\nu).

Because
\operatorname{supp}(\widehat{\widetilde{\eta}}_{\alpha})\subseteq I_{\alpha}\cup(-I_{\alpha})
and
d_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(\nu)
is an even function (
d_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(\nu)=d_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(-\nu)
), we can bound the integral using the supremum over the positive frequency interval
I_{\alpha}
, and Plancherel’s theorem yields:

\|\widetilde{u}_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}=\int_{I_{\alpha}\cup(-I_{\alpha})}|d_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(\nu)|^{2}|\widehat{\widetilde{\eta}}_{\alpha}(\nu)|^{2}\,d\nu\leq\left(\sup_{\nu\in I_{\alpha}}d_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(\nu)\right)^{2}\|\widetilde{\eta}_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}.

By the property of restriction,
\|(u_{r})_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}\leq\|\widetilde{u}_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}
, yielding:

\|(u_{r})_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}\leq\left(\sup_{\nu\in I_{\alpha}}d_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(\nu)\right)^{2}\|\widetilde{\eta}_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(\mathbb{R})}^{2}

Because the left-hand side is invariant with respect to the choice of the extension, taking the infimum over all admissible extensions
\widetilde{\eta}_{\alpha}\in L^{2}(\mathbb{R})
on the right-hand side preserves the inequality. By the definition of the
\mathcal{B}_{T}(I_{\alpha})
norm, this gives:

\|(u_{r})_{\alpha}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}\leq\left(\sup_{\nu\in I_{\alpha}}d_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(\nu)\right)^{2}\|(\eta_{r})_{\alpha}\|_{\mathcal{B}_{T}(I_{\alpha})}^{2}

Summing over all channels
\alpha=1,\dots,N_{c}
and applying the definition of
\Delta_{\varepsilon}(I_{1},\dots,I_{N_{c}})
establishes the final bound:

\|u_{r}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}\leq\Delta_{\varepsilon}(I_{1},\dots,I_{N_{c}})\|\eta_{r}\|_{\prod_{\alpha}\mathcal{B}_{T}(I_{\alpha})}.

∎

At exact matching,

d_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})=\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\sqrt{(\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})^{2}+4(\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})^{2}}=2\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}+O((\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})^{3}).

(63)

Thus the pole real part makes the resonant gain finite. In the cluster regimes where
\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}=O(\varepsilon)
, the exact-match input energy is
O(\varepsilon^{2})
relative to the squared output amplitude.

4.2
Upper Bounds for the Optimal Value Function and Asymptotic Gain

Let

V_{\mu}(a_{d};x_{0}):=\inf_{u\in L^{2}(0,T;\mathbb{R}^{N_{c}})}\mathcal{J}_{\mu}(u).

(64)

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
x_{0}=0
,
a_{d}=Ra_{r}
, and let
\eta_{r}
be given by (
57
). Suppose the compatibility conditions for exact tracking hold and
(\eta_{r})_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{B}_{T}(I_{\alpha})
. Then

V_{\mu}(a_{d};0)\leq\frac{\mu}{2}\sum_{\alpha^{\prime}=1}^{N_{c}}\left(\sup_{\nu\in I_{\alpha^{\prime}}}d_{\alpha^{\prime},\varepsilon}(\nu)\right)^{2}\|(\eta_{r})_{\alpha^{\prime}}\|_{\mathcal{B}_{T}(I_{\alpha^{\prime}})}^{2}.

(65)

Consequently,

V_{\mu}(a_{d};0)\leq\frac{\mu}{2}\Delta_{\varepsilon}(I_{1},\ldots,I_{N_{c}})^{2}\|\eta_{r}\|_{\prod_{\alpha}\mathcal{B}_{T}(I_{\alpha})}^{2}.

(66)

Proof.

The input
u_{r}
defined in (
58
) generates
\eta_{r}
and therefore
a_{r}
. Hence the tracking term vanishes for this admissible control. Thus

V_{\mu}(a_{d};0)\leq\mathcal{J}_{\mu}(u_{r})=\frac{\mu}{2}\|u_{r}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}.

The result follows from
Theorem

4.2
.
∎

Corollary 4.4
.

Let
\tau>0
. Under the hypotheses of
Theorem

4.3
, the condition

\mu\leq\frac{2\tau}{\Delta_{\varepsilon}(I_{1},\ldots,I_{N_{c}})^{2}\|\eta_{r}\|_{\prod_{\alpha}\mathcal{B}_{T}(I_{\alpha})}^{2}}

(67)

implies
V_{\mu}(a_{d};0)\leq\tau
. Therefore a smaller complex-pole detuning permits a larger regularization parameter for the same tracking tolerance.

Remark 4.5
.

Corollary
4.4
shows that the admissible size of the Tikhonov
regularization parameter is determined by the resonant detuning factor

\Delta_{\varepsilon}
. In particular, near resonance one has

\Delta_{\varepsilon}=O(\gamma^{\varepsilon})
, so that smaller damping
permits substantially larger values of
\alpha
while maintaining the
same tracking tolerance. Thus, beyond reducing the required control
input, appropriately designed resonant actuators enlarge the range of
regularization parameters compatible with a prescribed level of
performance.

For the direct model (
4
), the paid source needed for
a_{r}
is

q_{r}=C_{\varepsilon}^{\dagger}(\ddot{a}_{r}+\Lambda a_{r})=\eta_{r}.

(68)

The direct energy is
\|q_{r}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}
, whereas the resonator-mediated energy is
\|u_{r}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}
.

Theorem 4.6
.

If
(q_{r})_{\alpha}\in\mathcal{B}_{T}(I_{\alpha})
, then

\|u_{r}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}\leq\Delta_{\varepsilon}(I_{1},\ldots,I_{N_{c}})^{2}\|q_{r}\|_{\prod_{\alpha}\mathcal{B}_{T}(I_{\alpha})}^{2}.

(69)

Thus, on matched collective Minnaert bands, the cost of producing a given effective source is controlled by the small real part of the corresponding cluster pole.

Proof.

This is
Theorem

4.2
with
q_{r}=\eta_{r}
.
∎

Remark 4.7
.

The estimate in Theorem
4.6
quantifies the difference between
direct point-source actuation and resonator-mediated actuation. In the direct model,
the prescribed source
q_{r}
is injected into the wave equation itself, so that the
required control effort is measured by
\|q_{r}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}
. In contrast, in the
resonator-mediated model the control acts only through the collective resonant dynamics,
and the quantity that is paid for is the input

u_{r}=(\partial_{t}^{2}+2\Gamma^{\varepsilon}\partial_{t}+K^{\varepsilon})\eta_{r}.

Theorem
4.6
therefore shows that the cost of generating the
same effective source can be bounded by

\|u_{r}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}\leq\Delta_{\varepsilon}^{2}\|q_{r}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2},

where the factor
\Delta_{\varepsilon}
is determined entirely by the locations of the
collective scattering resonances. In particular, near resonance one has

\Delta_{\varepsilon}=O(\gamma^{\varepsilon})
, so that when

\gamma^{\varepsilon}=O(\varepsilon)
the required input energy scales like

O(\varepsilon^{2})
relative to the direct-source energy. Thus, subwavelength resonators
can generate a prescribed effective source while requiring only a small external input,
with the energy reduction being governed by the damping of the collective resonant modes. To have an idea on the gain we can expect, suppose that producing a prescribed effective source directly requires unit energy,
namely
\|q_{r}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}=1
. If the target frequency is tuned to a collective
resonance and
\gamma^{\varepsilon}=10^{-2}
with
\omega^{\varepsilon}=O(1)
, then

\Delta_{\varepsilon}^{2}\approx 4(\gamma^{\varepsilon})^{2}(\omega^{\varepsilon})^{2}=O(10^{-4}),

and Theorem
4.6
predicts that the corresponding resonator-driven
input satisfies

\|u_{r}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}=O(10^{-4}).

This illustrates quantitatively that, once tuned to resonance, the actuator needs only a
very small external input to generate the same effective source.

5
Exponential stabilization via cluster-induced radiation damping

Unlike classical space-time multiplier methods
[
5
]
, we establish exponential stabilization via frequency-domain spectral criteria
[
4
,
14
]
. Since the reduced system is finite-dimensional, verifying the absence of purely imaginary eigenvalues, i.e.,
\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}\subset\rho(\mathcal{A})
, strictly guarantees exponential decay.

The same pole real part that regularizes the optimal-control multiplier also produces damping in the homogeneous coupled system. This section records the finite-dimensional stabilization result, demonstrating why the small attenuation generated by local clustering is structurally important.

Consider the system:

\begin{cases}\ddot{a}+\Lambda a=C_{\varepsilon}\eta,\\
\ddot{\eta}+2\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\dot{\eta}+K_{\varepsilon}\eta=C_{\varepsilon}^{\top}a.\end{cases}

(70)

By introducing the augmented state vector
X=(a^{\top},\eta^{\top})^{\top}
, this can be expressed as:

\ddot{X}+\mathbb{D}_{\varepsilon}\dot{X}+\mathbb{G}_{\varepsilon}X=0,

(71)

where the stiffness matrix
\mathbb{G}_{\varepsilon}
and damping matrix
\mathbb{D}_{\varepsilon}
(acting exclusively on the
\eta
-components) are defined as:

\mathbb{G}_{\varepsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda&-C_{\varepsilon}\\
-C_{\varepsilon}^{\top}&K_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix},\quad\mathbb{D}_{\varepsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&2\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}.

(72)

To establish structural stability,
\mathbb{G}_{\varepsilon}
must be symmetric positive definite. Since the acoustic modal matrix satisfies
\Lambda>0
, the block LDU factorization yields:

\begin{pmatrix}I&0\\
C_{\varepsilon}^{\top}\Lambda^{-1}&I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda&-C_{\varepsilon}\\
-C_{\varepsilon}^{\top}&K_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}I&\Lambda^{-1}C_{\varepsilon}\\
0&I\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda&0\\
0&K_{\varepsilon}-C_{\varepsilon}^{\top}\Lambda^{-1}C_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}.

Then
\mathbb{G}_{\varepsilon}>0
is guaranteed by a positive Schur complement:

K_{\varepsilon}-C_{\varepsilon}^{\top}\Lambda^{-1}C_{\varepsilon}>0.

(73)

Let
\lambda_{\min}
and
\lambda_{\max}
denote the strictly positive minimum and maximum eigenvalues of
\mathbb{G}_{\varepsilon}
. Consequently, the total energy of the system,

E_{\varepsilon}(t)=\frac{1}{2}\left(\dot{X}^{\top}\dot{X}+X^{\top}\mathbb{G}_{\varepsilon}X\right),

(74)

is bounded by the standard Euclidean energy
E_{\text{euc}}=|\dot{a}|^{2}+|\dot{\eta}|^{2}+|a|^{2}+|\eta|^{2}
via the Rayleigh quotient:

\min(1,\lambda_{\min})E_{\text{euc}}\leq 2E_{\varepsilon}(t)\leq\max(1,\lambda_{\max})E_{\text{euc}}.

(75)

This topological equivalence ensures that
E_{\varepsilon}(t)\to 0
if and only if all physical state variables asymptotically decay to zero.

Lemma 5.1
.

Every solution of the coupled system satisfies the dissipation identity:

\frac{d}{dt}E_{\varepsilon}(t)=-2\dot{\eta}(t)^{\top}\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\dot{\eta}(t).

(76)

Proof.

Differentiating
E_{\varepsilon}(t)
with respect to time and applying the product rule yields:

\frac{d}{dt}E_{\varepsilon}(t)=\dot{X}^{\top}\ddot{X}+\dot{X}^{\top}\mathbb{G}_{\varepsilon}X.

Substituting the acceleration
\ddot{X}=-\mathbb{D}_{\varepsilon}\dot{X}-\mathbb{G}_{\varepsilon}X
from (
71
) gives:

\frac{d}{dt}E_{\varepsilon}(t)=\dot{X}^{\top}(-\mathbb{D}_{\varepsilon}\dot{X}-\mathbb{G}_{\varepsilon}X)+\dot{X}^{\top}\mathbb{G}_{\varepsilon}X.

The conservative stiffness terms cancel identically, leaving:

\frac{d}{dt}E_{\varepsilon}(t)=-\dot{X}^{\top}\mathbb{D}_{\varepsilon}\dot{X}.

Expanding
\mathbb{D}_{\varepsilon}
back into its block components yields:

\frac{d}{dt}E_{\varepsilon}(t)=-\begin{pmatrix}\dot{a}\\
\dot{\eta}\end{pmatrix}^{\top}\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&2\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\dot{a}\\
\dot{\eta}\end{pmatrix}=-2\dot{\eta}^{\top}\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\dot{\eta}.

Since
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}
is a diagonal matrix of positive damping coefficients, this derivative is non-positive, completing the proof.
∎

Lemma
5.1
shows that the energy is non-increasing. Because damping acts exclusively on
\dot{\eta}
, we apply the finite-dimensional LaSalle/Huang–Prüss principle to establish global exponential stability.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
\dot{z}=\mathcal{A}z
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
. Suppose that there exists a quadratic energy

\mathcal{E}(z)=\frac{1}{2}z^{\top}Pz,\qquad P=P^{\top}>0,

such that along all solutions

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(z(t))=-z(t)^{\top}\mathcal{D}z(t)\leq 0,\qquad\mathcal{D}=\mathcal{D}^{\top}\geq 0.

Then the following statements are equivalent.

(i)

There exist
M\geq 1
and
\delta>0
such that

\mathcal{E}(z(t))\leq Me^{-\delta t}\mathcal{E}(z(0)),\qquad t\geq 0.

(ii)

The matrix
\mathcal{A}
has no eigenvalue on the imaginary axis
\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}
.

(iii)

The largest
\mathcal{A}
-invariant subspace contained in
\operatorname{Ker}\mathcal{D}
is
\{0\}
.

Proof.

To prove the equivalence of statements (i), (ii), and (iii) under the finite-dimensional LaSalle/Huang–Prüss principle, we map the dissipation trajectory onto the Continuous Algebraic Lyapunov Equation (CALE), see (
77
) below.

Consider the abstract energy functional
\mathcal{E}(z)=\frac{1}{2}z^{\top}Pz
along the linear trajectory
\dot{z}=\mathcal{A}z
:

\frac{d}{dt}\mathcal{E}(z(t))=\frac{1}{2}\dot{z}^{\top}Pz+\frac{1}{2}z^{\top}P\dot{z}=\frac{1}{2}z^{\top}(\mathcal{A}^{\top}P+P\mathcal{A})z.

Equating this to the defined dissipation
-z^{\top}\mathcal{D}z
for all vectors
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
yields the identity:

\mathcal{A}^{\top}P+P\mathcal{A}=-2\mathcal{D}.

(77)

Let
(\lambda,v)
be any eigenpair of
\mathcal{A}
, such that
\mathcal{A}v=\lambda v
for
v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
. Multiplying identity (
77
) on the left by the conjugate transpose
v^{*}
and on the right by
v
gives:

v^{*}(\mathcal{A}^{\top}P+P\mathcal{A})v=-2v^{*}\mathcal{D}v\implies\bar{\lambda}v^{*}Pv+\lambda v^{*}Pv=-2v^{*}\mathcal{D}v,

which further implies

2\Re(\lambda)(v^{*}Pv)=-2v^{*}\mathcal{D}v\implies\Re(\lambda)=-\frac{v^{*}\mathcal{D}v}{v^{*}Pv}

Because
P>0
and
\mathcal{D}\geq 0
, we have
v^{*}Pv>0
and
v^{*}\mathcal{D}v\geq 0
. Consequently,
\Re(\lambda)\leq 0
for all
\lambda\in\sigma(\mathcal{A})
.

Equivalence of (i) and (ii):

Because
P
is symmetric positive definite
\big(P>0\big)
, its eigenvalues satisfy
0<\lambda_{\min}(P)\leq\lambda_{\max}(P)<\infty
. The energy functional is strictly bounded by the standard Euclidean norm
\|z\|^{2}
:

\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\min}(P)\|z\|^{2}\leq\mathcal{E}(z)\leq\frac{1}{2}\lambda_{\max}(P)\|z\|^{2}

Thus, the exponential decay of
\mathcal{E}(z(t))
is topologically equivalent to the exponential stability of the state trajectory
z(t)=e^{\mathcal{A}t}z(0)
. In finite dimensions,
\dot{z}=\mathcal{A}z
is exponentially stable if and only if
\Re\lambda<0
for all eigenvalues. Given that
\Re\lambda\leq 0
globally,
\Re\lambda<0
holds if and only if
\sigma(\mathcal{A})\cap\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}=\emptyset
.

Proof that (ii) implies (iii):

We proceed via a contradiction argument. Assume (iii) is false; therefore, the maximal
\mathcal{A}
-invariant subspace contained in
\ker D
, denoted
\mathcal{M}
, is non-trivial (
\mathcal{M}\neq\{0\}
).

As an invariant subspace, the restriction
\mathcal{A}|_{\mathcal{M}}
possesses at least one eigenvalue
\lambda_{0}\in\sigma(\mathcal{A})
with a corresponding eigenvector
v_{0}\in\mathcal{M}\setminus\{0\}
. Because
\mathcal{M}\subset\ker\mathcal{D}
,
\mathcal{D}v_{0}=0
, leading to
v_{0}^{*}Dv_{0}=0
. Substituting this into the spectral real-part equation forces:

\Re(\lambda_{0})=-\frac{0}{v_{0}^{*}Pv_{0}}=0

Thus,
\lambda_{0}
is purely imaginary (
\lambda_{0}\in\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}
), contradicting statement (ii).

Proof that (iii) implies (ii):

Assuming (ii) is false, there exists a purely imaginary eigenvalue
\lambda=\mathrm{i}\mu
(
\mu\in\mathbb{R}
) with an eigenvector
v\in\mathbb{C}^{d}\setminus\{0\}
. The spectral identity dictates:

-\frac{v^{*}\mathcal{D}v}{v^{*}Pv}=0\implies v^{*}\mathcal{D}v=0

Since
\mathcal{D}\geq 0
, this implies
v\in\ker\mathcal{D}
. Decomposing
v=v_{R}+\mathrm{i}v_{I}
for
v_{R},v_{I}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we find
\mathcal{D}v_{R}=0
and
\mathcal{D}v_{I}=0
, situating both in
\ker D
. Evaluating
A
on these components gives:

A(v_{R}+\mathrm{i}v_{I})=\mathrm{i}\mu(v_{R}+\mathrm{i}v_{I})=-\mu v_{I}+\mathrm{i}\mu v_{R}

Equating real and imaginary parts reveals
\mathcal{A}v_{R}=-\mu v_{I}
and
\mathcal{A}v_{I}=\mu v_{R}
. Thus, the non-trivial subspace
\mathcal{W}=\text{span}\{v_{R},v_{I}\}
is strictly
\mathcal{A}
-invariant and fully contained within
\ker\mathcal{D}
. This contradicts statement (iii).
∎

The damping acts only through
\dot{\eta}
. The next theorem verifies the invariant-set condition of Lemma
5.2
. The condition says that no acoustic eigenmode in the selected finite band is invisible to the collective cluster channels.

Theorem 5.3
.

Suppose (
73
) holds,
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}>0
, and

\operatorname{Ker}(\Lambda-\lambda^{2}I)\cap\operatorname{Ker}(C_{\varepsilon}^{\top})=\{0\}\quad\text{for every }\lambda^{2}\in\sigma(\Lambda).

(78)

Then there exist
M_{\varepsilon}\geq 1
and
\delta_{\varepsilon}>0
such that every solution of (
70
) satisfies

E_{\varepsilon}(t)\leq M_{\varepsilon}e^{-\delta_{\varepsilon}t}E_{\varepsilon}(0),\qquad t\geq 0.

(79)

Moreover, for fixed
\Lambda,K_{\varepsilon},C_{\varepsilon}
and
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}=\gamma_{\varepsilon}\Gamma_{0}
with
\Gamma_{0}>0
and
0<\gamma_{\varepsilon}\ll 1
, one has

\delta_{\varepsilon}=O(\gamma_{\varepsilon}).

(80)

The condition (
78
) is a finite-dimensional observability (or non-invisibility) assumption for the reduced acoustic modes. It requires that no nontrivial eigenmode of the acoustic operator contained in the selected spectral band be orthogonal to all resonant actuator channels. Equivalently, every eigenspace of
\Lambda
must be detected by the coupling matrix
C_{\varepsilon}
. This means that no retained acoustic eigenfunction (or linear combination of eigenfunctions corresponding to the same eigenvalue) vanishes simultaneously at all cluster centers
y_{\alpha}
. Such a condition is generic with respect to the placement of the resonator clusters and excludes only exceptional configurations in which an acoustic mode is completely decoupled from the actuators.

Proof.

Set

q=\begin{pmatrix}a\\
\eta\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbb{D}_{\varepsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&2\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbb{G}_{\varepsilon}=\begin{pmatrix}\Lambda&-C_{\varepsilon}\\
-C_{\varepsilon}^{\top}&K_{\varepsilon}\end{pmatrix}.

Then (
70
) can be written as

\ddot{q}+\mathbb{D}_{\varepsilon}\dot{q}+\mathbb{G}_{\varepsilon}q=0.

(81)

By the Schur complement condition (
73
),
\mathbb{G}_{\varepsilon}>0
; hence the energy (
74
) is a positive definite quadratic form in
(q,\dot{q})
. By Lemma
5.1
,

\frac{d}{dt}E_{\varepsilon}(t)=-2\dot{\eta}^{\top}\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\dot{\eta}\leq 0.

Thus the hypotheses of Lemma
5.2
are satisfied for the first-order form of (
81
). It remains to prove that the largest invariant set contained in the zero-dissipation set is trivial.

Let a trajectory satisfy

\dot{\eta}(t)^{\top}\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\dot{\eta}(t)=0,\qquad t\geq 0.

Since
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}>0
, the identity

\dot{\eta}(t)^{\top}\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\dot{\eta}(t)=0

implies
\dot{\eta}(t)=0
, hence
\eta(t)=\eta_{0}
. Substituting this into

\ddot{q}+\mathbb{D}_{\varepsilon}\dot{q}+\mathbb{G}_{\varepsilon}q=0

gives

\ddot{a}+\Lambda a-C^{\varepsilon}\eta_{0}=0,\qquad K^{\varepsilon}\eta_{0}-(C^{\varepsilon})^{\top}a=0.

The second identity implies
(C^{\varepsilon})^{\top}a(t)
is constant. Therefore the only possible zero-dissipation trajectories are undamped acoustic oscillations satisfying

\ddot{a}+\Lambda a=0,\qquad(C^{\varepsilon})^{\top}a(t)=0.

By the observability condition

\ker(\Lambda-\lambda^{2}I)\cap\ker((C^{\varepsilon})^{\top})=\{0\},

such a trajectory must be
a\equiv 0
. Then

K^{\varepsilon}\eta_{0}=(C^{\varepsilon})^{\top}a=0.

Since
K^{\varepsilon}>0
, it follows that
\eta_{0}=0
. Hence the largest invariant set contained in the zero-dissipation set is trivial. Consequently

\ddot{a}+\Lambda a=0,\qquad C_{\varepsilon}^{\top}a(t)=0,\qquad\eta(t)=0.

(82)

Use the spectral decomposition of
\Lambda
:

\mathbb{R}^{n}=\bigoplus_{\lambda^{2}\in\sigma(\Lambda)}E_{\lambda},\qquad E_{\lambda}=\operatorname{Ker}(\Lambda-\lambda^{2}I).

Every solution of
\ddot{a}+\Lambda a=0
has the form

a(t)=\sum_{\lambda^{2}\in\sigma(\Lambda)}\left(\cos(\lambda t)a_{\lambda}+\frac{\sin(\lambda t)}{\mu}b_{\lambda}\right),\qquad a_{\lambda},b_{\lambda}\in E_{\lambda}.

The identity
C_{\varepsilon}^{\top}a(t)=0
for every
t
implies, by linear independence of the functions
\cos(\lambda t)
and
\sin(\lambda t)
for the distinct positive frequencies
\lambda
, that

C_{\varepsilon}^{\top}a_{\lambda}=0,\qquad C_{\varepsilon}^{\top}b_{\lambda}=0\qquad\hbox{for every }\lambda^{2}\in\sigma(\Lambda).

The modal coupling condition (
78
) gives
a_{\lambda}=b_{\lambda}=0
for every
\lambda
. Hence
a\equiv 0
and, by (
82
),
\eta\equiv 0
. The invariant subset of the zero-dissipation set is therefore
\{0\}
.

By Lemma
5.2
, the first-order semigroup generated by (
70
) is exponentially stable. Since
E_{\varepsilon}
is equivalent to the Euclidean energy, there exist
M_{\varepsilon}\geq 1
and
\delta_{\varepsilon}>0
such that (
79
) holds.

It remains to discuss the dependence on the small cluster-induced damping. Let
\Gamma_{\varepsilon}=\gamma_{\varepsilon}\Gamma_{0}
with
\Gamma_{0}>0
. The first-order matrix associated with (
70
) depends analytically on
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
. At
\gamma_{\varepsilon}=0
the system is conservative and its spectrum lies on the imaginary axis. The perturbation in the generator is linear in
\gamma_{\varepsilon}
and acts only on the collective velocities. Therefore the spectral abscissa can move into the left half-plane only at the scale
O(\gamma_{\varepsilon})
. For every fixed
\gamma_{\varepsilon}>0
, the invariant-set argument above excludes eigenvalues on
\mathrm{i}\mathbb{R}
; hence the spectral abscissa is strictly negative. This proves that the exponential decay rate has the natural order

\delta_{\varepsilon}=O(\gamma_{\varepsilon}),

which is (
80
).
∎

Remark 5.4
.

The decay in
Theorem

5.3
is generated by the real parts
\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}
of the collective poles. Resonant tuning affects stabilization in two ways: it increases the transfer of acoustic energy to the damped collective channels through the same frequency matching that appears in the multiplier
d_{\alpha,\varepsilon}
, and it determines the size of the damping scale. Since
\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}
is small for small bubbles, the finite-band decay is weak, typically of order
O(\varepsilon)
in the cluster regimes considered in
[
12
]
.

6
Conclusion

Starting from the bubbly acoustic transmission problem, the time-domain Foldy–Lax approximation gives

u^{\varepsilon}-u^{\rm in}=\sum_{i}G_{0}(\cdot,z_{i})q_{i}^{\varepsilon}(\cdot-c_{0}^{-1}|\cdot-z_{i}|)+r^{\varepsilon},

where
q^{\varepsilon}
solves the delayed system (
16
). The local-cluster geometry turns the Laplace-domain Foldy–Lax matrix into a block-dominant matrix, and Proposition 2.1 of
[
12
]
provides principal collective poles

s_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}=-\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}+\mathrm{i}\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}.

Projection onto the associated collective channels and onto a finite acoustic band yields the system

\ddot{a}+\Lambda a=C_{\varepsilon}\eta,\qquad\ddot{\eta}+2\Gamma_{\varepsilon}\dot{\eta}+K_{\varepsilon}\eta=u.

For the quadratic functional (
47
), the minimizer exists uniquely and satisfies the adjoint system (
48
)–(
49
). The finite-band source-lifting estimate is

\|u_{r}\|_{L^{2}(0,T)}^{2}\leq\sum_{\alpha}\left(\sup_{\nu\in I_{\alpha}}d_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(\nu)\right)^{2}\|(\eta_{r})_{\alpha}\|_{\mathcal{B}_{T}(I_{\alpha})}^{2},

with

d_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(\nu)=\left|(\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})^{2}+(\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})^{2}-\nu^{2}+2\mathrm{i}\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\nu\right|.

This gives the optimal-value bound (
65
). At frequency matching,
d_{\alpha,\varepsilon}(\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})=2\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}\omega_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon}+O((\gamma_{\alpha}^{\varepsilon})^{3})
, which quantifies the finite resonant gain. The same pole real parts also yield the finite-band stabilization identity (
76
) and, under the modal coupling condition (
78
), exponential decay with rate proportional to the cluster-induced damping scale.

The analysis therefore shows that the collective Minnaert poles play a double role. Their imaginary parts determine the frequency windows in which the actuator channels couple efficiently to the selected acoustic modes, while their real parts determine both the finite gain in the source-lifting estimate and the stabilization scale. In this sense the resonant microstructure is not treated as a passive scattering correction to an otherwise ideal actuator, but as part of the control mechanism itself. The framework suggests a systematic route for designing finite-band wave controls in which the geometry and material parameters of the clusters are chosen together with the optimal input, rather than after the control problem has been idealized.

An interesting feature of the present framework is that the same collective scattering poles govern both the efficiency of resonator-mediated actuation and the intrinsic stabilization of the reduced dynamics through their nonzero real parts.
