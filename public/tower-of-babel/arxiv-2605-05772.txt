UD-DML: Uniform Design Subsampling for Double Machine Learning over Massive Data
Yuanke Qu, Xiaoya Xu, Hengtao Zhang, [4pt] School of Computer Science, Engineering, Guangdong Ocean University, Guangdong 529500, China

Abstract
Double machine learning (DML) delivers valid inference on low-dimensional causal parameters while permitting flexible nuisance estimation, but its computational cost becomes prohibitive once cross-fitted learners must be trained on massive observational data. Applying DML to a uniformly drawn subsample alleviates this burden, yet such a reduction disregards the geometry of the covariate space and can exacerbate treated–control imbalance as well as overlap deficiency. We propose Uniform Design Double Machine Learning (UD-DML), a design-based subsampling strategy for average treatment effect (ATE) estimation. UD-DML first constructs a low-discrepancy skeleton in a PCA-rotated covariate space under the mixture-discrepancy criterion, and then assigns, to each skeleton point, the nearest treated and control units via KD-tree search. The resulting matched subsample is, by construction, both representative of the full covariate distribution and balanced across treatment arms; cross-fitted DML is subsequently applied to it. We establish discrepancy-based guarantees for representativeness and balance, and prove that the UD-DML estimator is r \sqrt{r} -asymptotically normal under mild conditions, where the selected subsample size r ≪ n r\ll n . The dominant nuisance-fitting cost is thereby reduced from the n n -scale to the r r -scale. Monte Carlo experiments show that UD-DML attains lower RMSE, narrower confidence intervals and more reliable coverage than uniform subsampling, with the largest gains in low-overlap and misspecified regimes. An application to a large observational dataset further demonstrates its practical feasibility.



Keywords:
Causal Inference, Double Machine Learning, Massive Data, Uniform Design, Subsampling.

1
Introduction

Modern observational datasets are increasingly produced at massive scale in a wide range of causal applications, including comparative effectiveness studies based on electronic health records and claims data, policy optimization on digital platforms using large-scale user logs, and population-level evaluation from linked administrative records
(
28
;
10
;
19
)
. In such settings, the objective extends beyond prediction: valid estimation and inference for causal effects are required on samples whose size commonly lies between
10^{5}
and
10^{7}
. This massive-data regime poses a fundamental computational challenge for classical causal inference procedures, since many modern estimators require repeated fitting of treatment and outcome models on very large samples, often compounded by cross-validation, repeated sample splitting or cross-fitting, and bootstrap-based uncertainty quantification
(
43
;
16
)
. Consequently, procedures that are statistically well understood in moderate samples may become prohibitively expensive in large observational studies, raising the central question of how to scale classical causal inference procedures to massive datasets without sacrificing inferential validity.

Among contemporary approaches to causal inference with observational data, double/debiased machine learning (DML) has emerged as a leading framework for valid inference on low-dimensional causal parameters
(
9
)
. The framework couples two key ingredients: Neyman-orthogonal scores
(
7
;
21
;
4
)
, which are locally insensitive to first-order nuisance-estimation error, and cross-fitting, which decouples nuisance learning from target-parameter evaluation and thereby controls overfitting bias. This construction accommodates flexible machine-learning estimators for nuisance components—such as the propensity score and the conditional outcome regressions—while retaining root-
n
inference on the target causal estimand under mild rate conditions. The same orthogonal-learning paradigm has since been extended to richer causal targets, including conditional and heterogeneous treatment effects
(
27
;
22
)
; see
15
for a broader review of semiparametric and doubly robust machine-learning-based causal inference.

Despite these statistical advantages, standard DML does not scale automatically to massive datasets. Its computational burden is driven by the repeated estimation of nuisance functions across cross-fitting folds: when the nuisance components are learned by flexible nonlinear methods, refitting these models across folds can be prohibitively expensive on very large samples. A natural remedy is to replace full-sample nuisance learning with estimation on a smaller working sample. If this reduction is carried out by naive uniform subsampling, however, a non-trivial statistical difficulty arises—the selected subset need neither preserve the covariate geometry of the full data nor maintain adequate treated–control balance or overlap. In observational settings, such deficiencies can destabilize nuisance estimation and, in turn, degrade the performance of orthogonal-score-based inference
(
25
;
18
)
. Subsampling is therefore a natural route towards scalable causal inference, but these observations indicate that simple random reduction is unlikely to suffice.

Motivated by this computational tension, a substantial literature has developed principled subsampling strategies for large-scale inference. One major strand is model-based optimal subsampling, which assigns non-uniform inclusion probabilities or deterministic selection rules so as to optimize a model-specific efficiency criterion, typically an asymptotic-variance or information-based objective
(
37
;
38
)
. Under this rationale, once a working model and target estimator are specified, one preferentially retains the observations that are most informative for the chosen inferential task. The idea has proved effective across a range of models, including linear and generalized linear regression
(
33
;
2
)
, logistic regression
(
34
;
35
)
, quantile regression
(
32
)
, additive hazards models
(
44
)
, and quasi-likelihood estimation
(
39
)
. At the same time, the strength of this line of work is also its chief limitation: its performance is inherently tied to the working model and the estimator for which the rule is optimized. In problems such as observational causal inference, where the nuisance structure is typically complex and learned flexibly rather than specified a priori, this model dependence can erode robustness. Such considerations have motivated a complementary strand of research on geometric or model-free subsampling.

Unlike model-based optimal subsampling, which is tailored to a prespecified model and estimator, the geometric strand seeks subsets that are globally representative of the full covariate distribution and space-filling in the design space. Rooted in uniform design (UD) and quasi-Monte Carlo methodology
(
12
;
23
;
11
)
, recent developments based on the generalized empirical
F
-discrepancy, the mixture discrepancy, and related UD constructions
(
41
;
40
;
42
)
have shown that such approaches can be substantially more stable and more robust to model misspecification than naive random sampling
(
36
)
. More closely related to the present paper are the recent causal subsampling methods of
29
;
30
, which extend the subsampling paradigm to causal-effect estimation through staged nuisance estimation and calibration. Taken together, these contributions demonstrate that carefully designed subsampling can substantially reduce computational cost while improving robustness relative to naive uniform subsampling. However, none is designed to construct a geometrically representative and treatment-balanced working sample for observational DML, and thus none directly addresses the instability that arises when poor overlap and treated–control misalignment undermine cross-fitted nuisance learning and the subsequent orthogonal-score inference.

Motivated by these gaps, we propose Uniform Design Double Machine Learning (UD-DML), a design-based subsampling framework for scalable causal inference. UD-DML integrates uniform-design ideas with the DML paradigm, simultaneously addressing computational reduction, covariate representativeness, and treated–control balance. The method operates in a retained PCA-rotated covariate space. A low-discrepancy skeleton is first constructed to capture the dominant geometry of the full sample; for each skeleton point, the nearest treated and control observations are then identified through efficient nearest-neighbour search
(
5
;
6
)
. The resulting working sample is substantially smaller than the original data while remaining representative of the full covariate distribution and balanced across treatment arms. Standard cross-fitted DML is subsequently performed on the selected original observations.

Our contributions are fourfold. First, we develop a novel subsampling algorithm for large-scale ATE estimation that couples uniform-design-based geometric coverage with orthogonal-score-based causal inference. Second, we establish a discrepancy-based theory showing that the selected subsample inherits favourable representativeness and treated–control balance properties within a kernel framework. Third, under mild regularity conditions, we prove that the UD-DML estimator is asymptotically normal, thereby supporting valid Wald inference. Fourth, extensive simulations demonstrate that UD-DML consistently outperforms naive uniform subsampling in both statistical efficiency and inferential stability, most notably in low-overlap regimes and under nuisance-model misspecification, while a real-data application further illustrates its practical feasibility for large-scale observational analysis.

The remainder of this article is organized as follows. Section
2
first reviews the notation and frameworks for causal inference and DML, and then details the UD-DML algorithm, its implementation, as well as its theoretical properties. Section
3
presents our extensive simulation studies, comparing UD-DML to benchmarks across various data-generating processes. Section
4
applies our proposed method over a real dataset to demonstrate its practical properties. Section
5
concludes the paper with a brief discussion.

2
Methodology

2.1
Causal Inference and Double Machine Learning

Consider
n
i.i.d. observations
\bm{O}_{i}=(Y_{i},W_{i},\bm{X}_{i})
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, where
Y\in\mathbb{R}
denotes the observed outcome,
W\in\{0,1\}
the binary treatment assignment, and
\bm{X}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
a vector of pre-treatment covariates. We work within the potential-outcomes framework
(
26
)
: for each unit
i
,
Y_{i}(1)
and
Y_{i}(0)
denote the potential outcomes that would be realised under treatment (
W_{i}=1
) and control (
W_{i}=0
), respectively, so that
Y_{i}=W_{i}Y_{i}(1)+(1-W_{i})Y_{i}(0)
. The causal parameter of interest is the average treatment effect (ATE),
\theta_{0}={E}[Y(1)-Y(0)]
. Identification of
\theta_{0}
from observational data relies on the standard conditions of unconfoundedness,
(Y(1),Y(0))\perp W\mid\bm{X}
, and overlap (positivity),
0<{P}(W=1\mid\bm{X})<1
almost surely.

The DML framework of
9
targets
\theta_{0}
through a Neyman-orthogonal moment condition
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\psi(\bm{O};\theta_{0},\bm{\eta}_{0})\right]=0
, in which
\bm{\eta}_{0}
collects the unknown nuisance functions to be estimated from data. For the ATE, these typically comprise the conditional outcome regressions
m_{g}(\bm{x})={E}[Y\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x},W=g]
,
g\in\{0,1\}
, and the propensity score
e(\bm{x})={P}(W=1\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x})
. A standard choice of
\psi
is the Augmented Inverse Propensity Weighting (AIPW) score:

\psi_{\text{AIPW}}(\bm{O};\theta,\bm{\eta})=\bigl(m_{1}(\bm{X})-m_{0}(\bm{X})\bigr)+\frac{W\{Y-m_{1}(\bm{X})\}}{e(\bm{X})}-\frac{(1-W)\{Y-m_{0}(\bm{X})\}}{1-e(\bm{X})}-\theta.

(1)

The nuisance components
\widehat{\bm{\eta}}=(\widehat{m}_{0},\widehat{m}_{1},\widehat{e})
are estimated by flexible machine-learning learners. To prevent regularisation biases in
\widehat{\bm{\eta}}
from propagating to the estimator of
\theta_{0}
, DML employs a
K
-fold cross-fitting scheme: for each fold
k
, the nuisance functions
\widehat{\bm{\eta}}^{(-k)}
are trained on the observations outside fold
k
and then evaluated on the observations within fold
k
. The final estimator
\widehat{\theta}
is the solution to the empirical moment equation aggregated across folds,

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi\!\left(\bm{O}_{i};\widehat{\theta},\widehat{\bm{\eta}}^{(-k_{i})}\right)=0,

where
k_{i}
denotes the fold index of observation
i
.

The DML estimator
\widehat{\theta}
enjoys two key properties. First, it is
\sqrt{n}
-consistent and asymptotically Gaussian, provided that
\widehat{\bm{\eta}}
converges at a sufficient rate, so that valid Wald-type inference is available. Second, the AIPW-based estimator is doubly robust
(
4
)
: consistency of
\widehat{\theta}
for
\theta_{0}
is preserved provided that either the outcome regressions
(\widehat{m}_{0},\widehat{m}_{1})
or the propensity score
\widehat{e}
is consistent, rendering the estimator robust to partial nuisance misspecification.

Despite this appeal, standard DML becomes computationally prohibitive for massive datasets, because cross-fitting entails repeated training of complex nuisance learners on samples of size
n
. A natural workaround is to apply DML to a uniformly drawn subsample of size
r\ll n
, but this strategy is statistically inefficient: simple random subsampling does not guarantee favourable covariate balance, a stable propensity-score distribution, or adequate treated–control overlap. The resulting computational savings may thus be offset by a loss of precision and inflated variance, underscoring the need for a subsampling strategy that is simultaneously scalable, representative, and balanced.

2.2
Uniform Design Subsampling

To reduce the computational burden while preserving the geometric structure of the covariates, we construct a balanced subsample by uniform-design subsampling. A small set of skeleton points is first generated so as to be evenly distributed over the covariate domain; treated and control observations are then anchored to these skeleton points. The resulting subsample therefore retains the dominant covariate geometry of the original data while also maintaining treated–control balance, both of which are essential for subsequent DML estimation.

Let
\bm{X}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denote the
p
-dimensional covariate vector for unit
i
,
i=1,\ldots,n
, and let
\bm{X}=(\bm{X}_{1}^{\top},\ldots,\bm{X}_{n}^{\top})^{\top}
be the corresponding
n\times p
covariate matrix. Since observational covariates are typically correlated, performing the UD construction in a rotated space obtained by principal component analysis (PCA) is preferable to operating directly in the original coordinates. Let
\bar{\bm{X}}=n^{-1}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bm{X}_{i}
be the sample mean of the covariates, and let
\widehat{\bm{D}}
be the diagonal matrix of sample standard deviations. We standardize the covariates by

\widetilde{\bm{X}}_{i}=\widehat{\bm{D}}^{-1}(\bm{X}_{i}-\bar{\bm{X}}),\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.

Write the singular value decomposition of the standardized matrix as
\widetilde{\bm{X}}=\bm{U}\bm{\Sigma}\bm{V}^{\top}
, where
\bm{\Sigma}=\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{p})
with
\sigma_{1}\geq\cdots\geq\sigma_{p}\geq 0
. Let
\rho_{0}\in(0,1)
be a prescribed cumulative variance threshold, and define
q
to be the smallest integer such that

{(\sum_{d=1}^{q}\sigma_{d}^{2})}/{(\sum_{d=1}^{p}\sigma_{d}^{2})}\geq\rho_{0}.

In practice, one may take
\rho_{0}=0.85
, following the recommendation in
40
;
42
. Let
\bm{V}_{q}
be the first
q
columns of
\bm{V}
. We then define the retained rotated covariates by

\bm{Z}_{i}=\bm{V}_{q}^{\top}\widetilde{\bm{X}}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{q},\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.

Here
q\leq p
is the working dimension used in the UD construction. When
q=p
, no dimension reduction is performed.

We now construct a uniform design on the retained rotated space. For the given pair number
r_{p}
, an
r_{p}
-run
q
-factor uniform design is a set of points in
[0,1]^{q}
that spreads as evenly as possible over the unit hypercube. These points serve as a low-discrepancy skeleton, which will later be mapped to the empirical distribution of the rotated covariates. We generate such designs by the leave-one-out good lattice point method with a power generator. Let
\gcd(\cdot,\cdot)
denote the greatest common divisor.
A positive integer
\gamma
is said to be admissible if
\gcd(\gamma,r_{p}+1)=1
and the
q
residues
1,\gamma,\gamma^{2},\ldots,\gamma^{q-1}\pmod{r_{p}+1}
are mutually distinct. For an admissible
\gamma
, define the power generator vector by

\bm{\gamma}=\bigl(\gamma^{0},\gamma^{1},\ldots,\gamma^{q-1}\bigr)^{\top}.

The corresponding candidate
r_{p}
-run
q
-factor design is

\mathcal{U}_{r_{p}}(\gamma)=\bigl\{\bm{u}_{j}^{(\gamma)}:j=1,\ldots,r_{p}\bigr\}\subset[0,1]^{q},

where

\bm{u}_{j}^{(\gamma)}=\frac{\mathrm{mod}(j\bm{\gamma},\,r_{p}+1)}{r_{p}}-\frac{1}{2r_{p}}\bm{1}_{q},\qquad j=1,\ldots,r_{p},

\bm{1}_{q}
is the
q
-dimensional vector of ones, and
\mathrm{mod}(\cdot,\cdot)
is applied componentwise. Different admissible values of
\gamma
lead to different candidate designs. It remains to specify how to quantify the uniformity of these candidate designs and how to select the best one.

To measure the uniformity of a point set on
[0,1]^{q}
, we adopt the mixture discrepancy, which has been shown to be an effective generalized
L_{2}
-discrepancy criterion for space-filling designs
(
41
;
40
)
. Let
\mathcal{D}=\{\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{r_{p}}\}\subset[0,1]^{q}
be a candidate design, and let
F_{\mathcal{D}}
and
F_{u}
denote the empirical distribution of
\mathcal{D}
and the uniform distribution on
[0,1]^{q}
, respectively. For a reproducing kernel
\mathcal{K}:[0,1]^{q}\times[0,1]^{q}\to\mathbb{R}
, define the squared discrepancy of
\mathcal{D}
with respect to
F_{u}
by

\displaystyle D^{2}(\mathcal{D};F_{u},\mathcal{K})

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{2q}}\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{t})\,d\{F_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{u})-F_{u}(\bm{u})\}\,d\{F_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{t})-F_{u}(\bm{t})\}

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]^{2q}}\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{t})\,d\bm{u}\,d\bm{t}-\frac{2}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\int_{[0,1]^{q}}\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{u}_{j})\,d\bm{u}

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{r_{p}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\sum_{k=1}^{r_{p}}\mathcal{K}(\bm{u}_{j},\bm{u}_{k}),

where the second analytic equation is obtained by expanding the quadratic form. A smaller value of
D^{2}(\mathcal{D};F_{u},\mathcal{K})
indicates that the design points are more uniformly scattered over
[0,1]^{q}
. We then take
\mathcal{K}
to be the mixture-discrepancy kernel

\mathcal{K}_{M}(\bm{u},\bm{t})=\prod_{d=1}^{q}\left(\frac{15}{8}-\frac{1}{4}\left|u_{d}-\frac{1}{2}\right|-\frac{1}{4}\left|t_{d}-\frac{1}{2}\right|-\frac{3}{4}|u_{d}-t_{d}|+\frac{1}{2}|u_{d}-t_{d}|^{2}\right),\quad\bm{u},\bm{t}\in[0,1]^{q}.

The corresponding discrepancy

D_{M}(\mathcal{D}):=D(\mathcal{D};F_{u},\mathcal{K}_{M})

is called the mixture discrepancy. Since
\mathcal{K}_{M}
is a product kernel, the above integrals can be evaluated coordinatewise, which yields the closed-form expression

\displaystyle D_{M}^{2}(\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{19}{12}\right)^{q}-\frac{2}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\prod_{d=1}^{q}\left[\frac{5}{3}-\frac{1}{4}\left|u_{jd}-\frac{1}{2}\right|-\frac{1}{4}\left(u_{jd}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{r_{p}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\sum_{k=1}^{r_{p}}\prod_{d=1}^{q}\left(\frac{15}{8}-\frac{1}{4}\left|u_{jd}-\frac{1}{2}\right|-\frac{1}{4}\left|u_{kd}-\frac{1}{2}\right|-\frac{3}{4}|u_{jd}-u_{kd}|+\frac{1}{2}|u_{jd}-u_{kd}|^{2}\right).

The detailed derivations for the discrepancy are attached in the Appendix
A.1
. Accordingly, we choose the final UD skeleton by minimizing the mixture discrepancy over admissible generators. Let
\mathcal{A}_{r_{p},q}
denote the set of admissible
\gamma
’s. In the practical implementation, exhaustive enumeration over
\mathcal{A}_{r_{p},q}
can be unnecessarily expensive when
r_{p}
is moderately large.
We thus implement the quasi-optimal search over a budgeted random subset of size
B_{\gamma}
,

\mathcal{G}^{(B_{\gamma})}_{r_{p},q}\subset\mathcal{A}_{r_{p},q},
with
|\mathcal{G}^{(B_{\gamma})}_{r_{p},q}|=\min\{B_{\gamma},|\mathcal{A}_{r_{p},q}|\}
.
We define

\widehat{\gamma}\in\arg\min_{\gamma\in\mathcal{G}^{(B_{\gamma})}_{r_{p},q}}D_{M}^{2}\!\bigl(\mathcal{U}_{r_{p}}(\gamma)\bigr),

and set
\mathcal{U}_{r_{p}}=\mathcal{U}_{r_{p}}(\widehat{\gamma})=\{\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{r_{p}}\}
.
This yields a fast and effective way to generate a nearly uniform set of
r_{p}
skeleton points in
[0,1]^{q}
.

Once the design points
\{\bm{u}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r_{p}}
are obtained, we map them from the unit hypercube back to the retained rotated covariate space through the empirical inverse transformation. For each coordinate
d=1,\ldots,q
, let
\widehat{F}_{Z^{(d)}}
denote the empirical cumulative distribution function of
\{Z_{i}^{(d)}\}_{i=1}^{n}
, and define the marginal empirical transformation

T_{\bm{Z}}(\bm{z})=\bigl(\widehat{F}_{Z^{(1)}}(z^{(1)}),\ldots,\widehat{F}_{Z^{(q)}}(z^{(q)})\bigr)^{\top}.

The corresponding skeleton points in the rotated space are

\bm{v}_{j}=T_{\bm{Z}}^{-1}(\bm{u}_{j})=\Bigl(\widehat{F}_{Z^{(1)}}^{-1}(u_{j1}),\ldots,\widehat{F}_{Z^{(q)}}^{-1}(u_{jq})\Bigr)^{\top},\qquad j=1,\ldots,r_{p}.

Each
\bm{v}_{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{q}
thus serves as a representative anchor point in the PCA-rotated covariate space.

We then use the skeleton points to determine the subsample indices. Because the causal estimand is defined on the original observations, the skeleton points
\bm{v}_{j}
serve only to locate representative units and are not substituted for the observed data. For each
j=1,\ldots,r_{p}
, we select one treated and one control observation whose rotated covariates are closest to
\bm{v}_{j}
:

i_{j}^{1}=\arg\min_{i:W_{i}=1}\|\bm{Z}_{i}-\bm{v}_{j}\|_{2},\qquad i_{j}^{0}=\arg\min_{i:W_{i}=0}\|\bm{Z}_{i}-\bm{v}_{j}\|_{2}.

This step is performed entirely in the rotated space, where distance calculations are more stable and computationally efficient. When the indices
i_{j}^{1}
and
i_{j}^{0}
are obtained, the actual selected observations are the original units
(Y_{i_{j}^{g}},W_{i_{j}^{g}},\bm{X}_{i_{j}^{g}})
,
g\in\{0,1\}
, on the original scale. We define the treated and control index sets by
\mathcal{S}_{1}=\{i_{j}^{1}\}_{j=1}^{r_{p}}
and
\mathcal{S}_{0}=\{i_{j}^{0}\}_{j=1}^{r_{p}}
, respectively, and let
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{1}\cup\mathcal{S}_{0}
be the final subsample index set. The resulting subsample size is
r=2r_{p}
. The final UD-selected sample used for DML is then

\bigl\{(Y_{i},W_{i},\bm{X}_{i}):i\in\mathcal{S}\bigr\}.

In summary, PCA and UD act purely as a design device for identifying informative and balanced indices; the subsequent estimation step continues to operate on the original observed data. The discrepancy-based theoretical justification of this construction, including the representativeness and balance properties induced by the UD skeleton, will be established in Section
2.4
through the generalized empirical
F
-discrepancy and the empirical Koksma–Hlawka inequality.

2.3
The UD-DML Algorithm and Implementation

The practical implementation of UD-DML comprises three phases. Phase 1 constructs a low-discrepancy skeleton in the retained PCA-rotated covariate space and uses it to select a balanced set of treated–control pairs. Phase 2 runs the standard cross-fitted DML procedure on the selected original observations. Phase 3 performs Wald inference based on the cross-fitted pseudo-outcomes. The complete procedure is summarised in Algorithm

https://github.com/BobZhangHT/UD-DML

\mathcal{S}_{1}\leftarrow\mathcal{S}_{1}\cup\{i_{j}^{1}\},

\mathcal{S}_{0}\leftarrow\mathcal{S}_{0}\cup\{i_{j}^{0}\}.

27:

end

for

28:

Set
\mathcal{S}=\mathcal{S}_{1}\cup\mathcal{S}_{0}
.

29:

Phase 2: Cross-fitted DML on the selected original observations

30:

Run the standard
K
-fold cross-fitting DML procedure on

\{(Y_{i},W_{i},\bm{X}_{i}):i\in\mathcal{S}\}.

31:

Obtain the cross-fitted nuisance estimators
\widehat{\bm{\eta}}^{(-k)}
and the pseudo-outcomes

\widehat{\psi}_{i}^{*}=\psi^{*}\!\left(\bm{O}_{i};\widehat{\bm{\eta}}^{(-k_{i})}\right),~i\in\mathcal{S}.

32:

Phase 3: Estimation and inference

33:

Compute

\widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\widehat{\psi}_{i}^{*}.

34:

Compute the empirical variance of the pseudo-outcomes:

\widehat{\sigma}_{\psi,r}^{2}=\frac{1}{r-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\left(\widehat{\psi}_{i}^{*}-\widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}\right)^{2}.

35:

Set

\widehat{V}_{\mathrm{UD}}=\frac{1}{r}\widehat{\sigma}_{\psi,r}^{2},
and construct the asymptotic
(1-\alpha)
confidence interval

\widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat{V}_{\mathrm{UD}}}.

We now analyse the computational complexity of UD-DML. The first component is a one-time preprocessing step comprising standardisation, PCA, and the empirical marginal transformation. For moderate
p
, this step is inexpensive relative to repeated ML fitting on the full sample. Once the rotated covariates
\{\bm{Z}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{n}
have been computed, the
q
marginal empirical CDFs are obtained by sorting
q
vectors of length
n
, costing
O(qn\log n)
.
The second component is the construction of the UD skeleton. The design is generated via the leave-one-out good-lattice-point construction with a power generator under the mixture-discrepancy criterion. For each admissible generator
\gamma
, evaluating
D_{M}^{2}(\mathcal{U}_{r_{p}}(\gamma))
via the closed form incurs a cost of
O(r_{p}^{2}q)
. In the implementation we inspect a randomly drawn candidate set of size
B_{\gamma}
and retain the quasi-optimal generator, giving a cold-start design-search cost of
O(B_{\gamma}r_{p}^{2}q)
.
The third component is the paired nearest-neighbour matching. A naive implementation would scan, for each of the
r_{p}
skeleton points, every treated and every control observation in the rotated space, leading to an overall cost of order
O(r_{p}nq)
. To circumvent this, we construct two spatial indices—specifically the
cKDTree
from the
scipy.spatial
library—one for the treated arm and one for the control arm, and query them separately. For exact matching without replacement, each query returns a candidate set of nearest neighbours that is expanded adaptively until the nearest available unit is found. In low-to-moderate retained dimensions
q
, the average query cost is approximately logarithmic in the arm size, so the total matching cost is
O(r_{p}q\log n)
once the indices have been built.
After the subsample
\mathcal{S}
of size
r=2r_{p}
is constructed, the algorithm proceeds to the DML stage.
This stage applies standard
K
-fold cross-fitting only to the selected original observations, so its
computational cost depends on
r
, not on
n
. Denote this cost by
C_{\mathrm{DML}}(r,p,K)
.
Finally, the inference step uses only the
r
cross-fitted pseudo-outcomes and therefore costs
O(r)
.

Combining the above components, the total cold-start computational cost of UD-DML is

C_{\mathrm{PCA}}(n,p,q)+O(qn\log n)+O(B_{\gamma}r_{p}^{2}q)+O(r_{p}q\log n)+C_{\mathrm{DML}}(r,p,K),

where
C_{\mathrm{PCA}}(n,p,q)
denotes the one-time PCA preprocessing cost.
In repeated Monte Carlo experiments the UD skeleton depends only on
(r_{p},q,B_{\gamma})
; once the quasi-optimal design has been generated and cached, the amortised cost per replication reduces to

C_{\mathrm{PCA}}(n,p,q)+O(qn\log n)+O(r_{p}q\log n)+C_{\mathrm{DML}}(r,p,K),

up to negligible cache lookup overhead. The dominant iterative nuisance-fitting cost is thus
C_{\mathrm{DML}}(r,p,K)
rather than
C_{\mathrm{DML}}(n,p,K)
, rendering the comparison with full-data DML transparent.
If one applies standard DML directly to the full sample, the leading computational cost is

C_{\mathrm{FULL}}(n,p,K)\asymp C_{\mathrm{DML}}(n,p,K),

up to a lower-order inference cost.
By contrast, UD-DML replaces this full-sample training burden by the much smaller term
C_{\mathrm{DML}}(r,p,K)
and adds only a quasi-linear preprocessing overhead in
n
.
Therefore, when
r\ll n
and nuisance fitting is the dominant computational bottleneck, UD-DML can be viewed as replacing a full-data learning problem by a preprocessing step on all
n
observations followed by a learning problem on only
r
selected observations.

To build learner-specific intuition, the computational advantage of UD-DML is clearest when nuisance functions are fitted by nonlinear learners commonly used in causal inference, such as random forests or gradient-boosted trees—both of which typically exhibit nonlinear effects and higher-order interactions on observational data. For tree-ensemble learners, the training cost on a sample of size
m
is typically of order
C_{\mathrm{DML}}(m,p,K)\approx c_{K,T}\,m\log m
, where
c_{K,T}
depends on the cross-fitting scheme, the number of trees, and other tuning parameters. Full-data DML therefore costs
c_{K,T}\,n\log n
, whereas UD-DML replaces this with
O(pn\log n)+c_{K,T}\,r\log r
up to lower-order terms. When
r\ll n
, the reduction from
n\log n
to
r\log r
in the nuisance-fitting stage substantially outweighs the one-time
O(pn\log n)
preprocessing cost. The effect is even more pronounced for costlier nonlinear learners—for example, kernel-based methods whose training cost scales quadratically or cubically with the sample size. In large-scale regimes with complex nonlinear nuisance structure, UD-DML can therefore be substantially more efficient than full-data DML while preserving the design-induced representativeness and balance properties required for valid inference in Section
2.4
.

2.4
Theoretical Properties and Inference

We now turn to the theoretical properties of the UD-selected subsample and of the resulting UD-DML estimator. The analysis proceeds in three steps. We first establish that the selected subsample is representative of the full data in the original covariate space. We then show that the same construction induces covariate balance between the treated and control subsamples. Finally, combining these design properties with the orthogonality structure of DML, we prove the asymptotic normality of the UD-DML estimator.

The key analytical device is the generalized empirical
F
-discrepancy (GEFD) of
40
. In our setting, the discrepancy is evaluated on the retained PCA-rotated covariates
\mathcal{Z}=\{\bm{Z}_{1},\ldots,\bm{Z}_{n}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
, where
\bm{Z}_{i}=\bm{V}_{q}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{D}}^{-1}(\bm{X}_{i}-\bar{\bm{X}})
.
For any point set

\mathcal{P}=\{\bm{\xi}_{1},\ldots,\bm{\xi}_{m}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}

and any reproducing kernel
\mathcal{K}:[0,1]^{q}\times[0,1]^{q}\to\mathbb{R}
, the squared GEFD of
\mathcal{P}
with respect to
\mathcal{Z}
is defined by

\displaystyle D^{2}(\mathcal{P};\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{K})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{i^{\prime}=1}^{n}\mathcal{K}\!\left(T_{\bm{Z}}(\bm{Z}_{i}),T_{\bm{Z}}(\bm{Z}_{i^{\prime}})\right)-\frac{2}{nm}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\mathcal{K}\!\left(T_{\bm{Z}}(\bm{Z}_{i}),T_{\bm{Z}}(\bm{\xi}_{k})\right)

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{m^{2}}\sum_{k=1}^{m}\sum_{k^{\prime}=1}^{m}\mathcal{K}\!\left(T_{\bm{Z}}(\bm{\xi}_{k}),T_{\bm{Z}}(\bm{\xi}_{k^{\prime}})\right),

(2)

where

T_{\bm{Z}}(\bm{z})

is the marginal empirical transformation. By the empirical Koksma–Hlawka inequality on
\mathbb{R}^{q}

(
40
;
13
)
, if
f
has bounded generalized variation
V_{2}(f,\mathcal{K})
on
[0,1]^{q}
, then

\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f\!\left(T_{\bm{Z}}(\bm{Z}_{i})\right)-\frac{1}{m}\sum_{k=1}^{m}f\!\left(T_{\bm{Z}}(\bm{\xi}_{k})\right)\right|\leq D(\mathcal{P};\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{K})\,V_{2}(f,\mathcal{K}).

(3)

This inequality provides a deterministic discrepancy bound in the rotated space. Since the selected subsample consists of original observations indexed by
\mathcal{S}_{1}
,
\mathcal{S}_{0}
, and
\mathcal{S}
, we next transport this control back to the original covariate space.

Define the PCA projection map

\Pi_{q}(\bm{x})=\bm{V}_{q}^{\top}\widehat{\bm{D}}^{-1}(\bm{x}-\bar{\bm{X}}),~\bm{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{p},

and the induced transformation

\mathcal{T}(\bm{x})=T_{\bm{Z}}\!\bigl(\Pi_{q}(\bm{x})\bigr)\in[0,1]^{q}.

For each
g\in\{0,1\}
, recall that the selected indices are given by

\mathcal{S}_{g}=\{i_{j}^{g}\}_{j=1}^{r_{p}},\qquad i_{j}^{g}=\arg\min_{i:W_{i}=g}\|\bm{Z}_{i}-\bm{v}_{j}\|_{2},

where
\mathcal{V}_{r_{p}}=\{\bm{v}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
is the UD skeleton constructed in Section
2.2
. We also define the group-specific matching radius in the rotated space,

\delta_{g}^{(\mathrm{rot})}:=\max_{1\leq j\leq r_{p}}\|\bm{Z}_{i_{j}^{g}}-\bm{v}_{j}\|_{2},\qquad g\in\{0,1\}.

The following theorem shows that, for functions induced by the retained PCA coordinates, the UD-selected subsample is representative of the full covariate distribution in the sense of empirical integration on the original covariate space.

Theorem 1

(Representativeness of the UD-selected subsample)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}_{r_{p}}=\{\bm{v}_{j}\}_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{q}
be the UD skeleton, and let
\mathcal{K}
be a reproducing kernel on
[0,1]^{q}\times[0,1]^{q}
. Suppose
f:[0,1]^{q}\to\mathbb{R}
has bounded generalized variation
V_{2}(f,\mathcal{K})<\infty
, and define the induced function on the original covariate space by

\phi_{f}(\bm{x})=f\!\bigl(\mathcal{T}(\bm{x})\bigr)=f\!\left(T_{\bm{Z}}\!\bigl(\Pi_{q}(\bm{x})\bigr)\right).

Assume moreover that the function

\varphi_{f}(\bm{z})=f\!\left(T_{\bm{Z}}(\bm{z})\right),\qquad\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{q},

is Lipschitz continuous on the support of
\mathcal{Z}\cup\mathcal{V}_{r_{p}}
with Lipschitz constant
L_{f}
, namely,

|\varphi_{f}(\bm{z})-\varphi_{f}(\bm{z}^{\prime})|\leq L_{f}\|\bm{z}-\bm{z}^{\prime}\|_{2}\qquad\text{for all }\bm{z},\bm{z}^{\prime}\in\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{Z}\cup\mathcal{V}_{r_{p}}).

Then, for each
g\in\{0,1\}
,

\left|\int\phi_{f}(\bm{x})\,dP_{n,\bm{X}}(\bm{x})-\int\phi_{f}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S}_{g},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right|\leq D(\mathcal{V}_{r_{p}};\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{K})\,V_{2}(f,\mathcal{K})+L_{f}\,\delta_{g}^{(\mathrm{rot})}.

(4)

Consequently, for the total subsample empirical measure

P_{\mathcal{S},\bm{X}}=\frac{1}{2}P_{\mathcal{S}_{1},\bm{X}}+\frac{1}{2}P_{\mathcal{S}_{0},\bm{X}},

we have

\left|\int\phi_{f}(\bm{x})\,dP_{n,\bm{X}}(\bm{x})-\int\phi_{f}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right|\leq D(\mathcal{V}_{r_{p}};\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{K})\,V_{2}(f,\mathcal{K})+\frac{L_{f}}{2}\bigl(\delta_{1}^{(\mathrm{rot})}+\delta_{0}^{(\mathrm{rot})}\bigr).

(5)

Theorem
1
states that, for any
\phi_{f}(\bm{x})=f(\mathcal{T}(\bm{x}))
induced by the retained PCA coordinates, the empirical average under the UD-selected subsample is close to that under the full data. Thus, although the selected sample contains only
r\ll n
observations, it preserves a broad class of functionals of the dominant covariate structure. The approximation is distributional, in the sense of empirical integration, rather than pointwise in
\bm{x}
, which is precisely the regime relevant for subsampling-based inference, where downstream estimators depend on the data through empirical averages of suitable functions.

Theorem
1
has several causal implications. Taking
f(\bm{u})=u_{d}
or
f(\bm{u})=u_{d}u_{\ell}
shows that the UD-selected subsample preserves first- and second-order features of the transformed coordinates, while smooth indicator-type choices of
f
control smoothed distributional features such as tail mass or quantile-related characteristics. Since the DML nuisance functions—the outcome regressions
m_{g,0}(\bm{x})
and the propensity score
e_{0}(\bm{x})
—depend on covariates through such low-dimensional features, the UD-selected subsample reproduces the empirical feature distribution most relevant for nuisance learning. Consequently, the subsample-trained learners operate on a covariate distribution close to that of the full data, which stabilises the orthogonal-score-based estimation that follows.

Our second result shows that the same UD construction simultaneously induces balance between the treated and control subsamples.

Theorem 2

(Inherent covariate balance of the UD-selected subsample)
.

Let the conditions of Theorem
1
hold. Then, for the induced function

\phi_{f}(\bm{x})=f\!\bigl(\mathcal{T}(\bm{x})\bigr),

the empirical measures of the treated and control UD-selected subsamples satisfy

\left|\int\phi_{f}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S}_{1},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})-\int\phi_{f}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S}_{0},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right|\leq L_{f}\bigl(\delta_{1}^{(\mathrm{rot})}+\delta_{0}^{(\mathrm{rot})}\bigr).

(6)

Theorem
2
formalises the balancing effect induced by the common UD skeleton. The bound in (
6
) indicates that the treated and control subsamples are close because they are matched to the same reference points. In observational studies, instability in propensity-score and outcome-regression estimation frequently arises from the treated and control samples occupying different regions of the covariate space. Theorem
2
establishes that, after UD subsampling, the empirical covariate distributions of the two arms are automatically aligned along the dominant latent directions identified by PCA. This design-induced alignment mitigates the extrapolation required in nuisance estimation, improves the effective overlap for the AIPW score, and yields a more favourable sample for the orthogonalised DML procedure analysed below.

With these two deterministic design properties in hand, we now turn to the asymptotic behaviour of the UD-DML estimator. Based on the orthogonal AIPW score (
1
), it is convenient to introduce the associated pseudo-outcome

\psi^{\ast}(\bm{O};\bm{\eta}):=\psi_{\text{AIPW}}(\bm{O};0,\bm{\eta})=\bigl\{m_{1}(\bm{X})-m_{0}(\bm{X})\bigr\}+\frac{W\{Y-m_{1}(\bm{X})\}}{e(\bm{X})}-\frac{(1-W)\{Y-m_{0}(\bm{X})\}}{1-e(\bm{X})}.

Letting
\bm{\eta}_{0}=(m_{0,0},m_{1,0},e_{0})
denote the true nuisance functions, the target parameter satisfies
\theta_{0}={E}[\psi^{\ast}(\bm{O};\bm{\eta}_{0})]
.
For the UD-selected sample indexed by
\mathcal{S}
, the cross-fitted UD-DML estimator can be written as

\widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\widehat{\psi}_{i}^{\ast},\qquad\widehat{\psi}_{i}^{\ast}:=\psi^{\ast}\!\bigl(\bm{O}_{i};\widehat{\bm{\eta}}^{(-k_{i})}\bigr),

where
k_{i}\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
is the fold label of observation
i
, and
\widehat{\bm{\eta}}^{(-k_{i})}
is the nuisance estimator trained on the complement of fold
k_{i}
within the selected sample.

We begin with the regularity conditions imposed on the nuisance estimators. For any measurable scalar function
h(\bm{X})
, write

\|h\|_{L_{2}}:=\Bigl(E\{h(\bm{X})^{2}\}\Bigr)^{1/2},

where the expectation is taken with respect to the population law of
\bm{X}
.

Assumption 1

(Cross-fitted nuisance regularity and overlap)
.

The cross-fitted nuisance estimators computed on the selected sample
\mathcal{S}
satisfy

\|\widehat{m}_{0}-m_{0,0}\|_{L_{2}}=o_{p}(1),\qquad\|\widehat{m}_{1}-m_{1,0}\|_{L_{2}}=o_{p}(1),\qquad\|\widehat{e}-e_{0}\|_{L_{2}}=o_{p}(1).

Moreover, there exists a constant
c_{e}>0
such that

c_{e}\leq e_{0}(\bm{X})\leq 1-c_{e}\qquad\text{a.s.},

and, with probability tending to one,

c_{e}/2\leq\widehat{e}(\bm{X})\leq 1-c_{e}/2\qquad\text{for all evaluation observations in the cross-fitting folds.}

Finally, the nuisance estimators obey the product-rate condition

\|\widehat{e}-e_{0}\|_{L_{2}}\Bigl(\|\widehat{m}_{1}-m_{1,0}\|_{L_{2}}+\|\widehat{m}_{0}-m_{0,0}\|_{L_{2}}\Bigr)=o_{p}(r^{-1/2}).

Assumption
1
is a direct analogue of the standard DML requirement in
9
, with the effective sample size now being the selected-sample size
r
. The first line imposes only
L_{2}
-consistency of the nuisance estimators, a requirement weaker than parametric-rate estimation and compatible with modern ML learners. The overlap condition ensures that the inverse-probability weights remain bounded and prevents the AIPW score from becoming unstable in regions where treatment assignment is nearly deterministic. Finally, the product-rate restriction is the key orthogonality condition: it guarantees that the second-order remainder generated by plugging the estimated nuisance functions into the orthogonal score is asymptotically negligible at the
r^{-1/2}
scale.

The next assumption connects the discrepancy-based design results in Theorems
1
and
2
to the oracle score in a primitive way. Define the oracle pseudo-outcome by

\psi_{i}^{\ast}:=\psi^{\ast}(\bm{O}_{i};\bm{\eta}_{0}),\qquad i=1,\ldots,n,

and let

\tau_{0}(\bm{x}):=E\bigl\{\psi^{\ast}(\bm{O};\bm{\eta}_{0})\mid\bm{X}=\bm{x}\bigr\}=m_{1,0}(\bm{x})-m_{0,0}(\bm{x})

denote the conditional mean of the oracle pseudo-outcome.

Assumption 2

(Approximate low-dimensional oracle structure compatible with the design)
.

There exist a function
f_{\tau}:[0,1]^{q}\to\mathbb{R}
and a remainder function
b_{\tau,r}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

\tau_{0}(\bm{x})=\phi_{\tau}(\bm{x})+b_{\tau,r}(\bm{x}),\qquad\phi_{\tau}(\bm{x}):=f_{\tau}\!\bigl(\mathcal{T}(\bm{x})\bigr),

where
\mathcal{T}(\bm{x})=T_{\bm{Z}}\!\bigl(\Pi_{q}(\bm{x})\bigr)
is the transformed PCA coordinate. Assume that
f_{\tau}
has bounded generalized variation,

V_{2}(f_{\tau},\mathcal{K})<\infty,

and that the associated function

\varphi_{\tau}(\bm{z}):=f_{\tau}\!\bigl(T_{\bm{Z}}(\bm{z})\bigr),\qquad\bm{z}\in\mathbb{R}^{q},

is Lipschitz continuous on
\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{Z}\cup\mathcal{V}_{r_{p}})
with Lipschitz constant
L_{\tau}
.

Assume further that the approximation remainder is negligible in empirical integral form:

\left|\int b_{\tau,r}(\bm{x})\,dP_{n,\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right|+\left|\int b_{\tau,r}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S}_{0},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right|+\left|\int b_{\tau,r}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S}_{1},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right|=o_{p}(r^{-1/2}),

and that the geometric approximation error satisfies

D(\mathcal{V}_{r_{p}};\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{K})=o_{p}(r^{-1/2}),\qquad\delta_{1}^{(\mathrm{rot})}=o_{p}(r^{-1/2}),\qquad\delta_{0}^{(\mathrm{rot})}=o_{p}(r^{-1/2}).

(7)

In addition,
E\{\tau_{0}(\bm{X})^{2}\}<\infty
, and the massive-data regime satisfies

r\to\infty,\qquad n\to\infty,\qquad r/n\to 0.

Assumption
2
is most naturally read as an approximate low-dimensional structure condition on the oracle signal
\tau_{0}(\bm{x})=m_{1,0}(\bm{x})-m_{0,0}(\bm{x})
: the dominant variation of
\tau_{0}
is captured by the retained transformed coordinates
\mathcal{T}(\bm{x})
, while the residual component is negligible under empirical integration. This reflects the sufficient-dimension-reduction perspective in causal inference. The conditions
V_{2}(f_{\tau},\mathcal{K})<\infty
and the Lipschitz continuity of
\varphi_{\tau}
are smoothness requirements for discrepancy-based approximation and for transferring error control from the ideal skeleton to the matched observations. The rate conditions in (
7
) are mild: they only require the deterministic design-side error to be asymptotically dominated by the stochastic
r^{-1/2}
scale. Here
D(\mathcal{V}_{r_{p}};\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{K})
captures the global representativeness error of the UD skeleton, while
\delta_{1}^{(\mathrm{rot})}
and
\delta_{0}^{(\mathrm{rot})}
measure the local matching errors of the treated and control samples relative to the common skeleton. When
q
is low to moderate, the skeleton is low-discrepancy, and each treatment arm becomes increasingly dense as
r/n\to 0
, these geometric errors may reasonably be expected to vanish; requiring them to be
o_{p}(r^{-1/2})
simply ensures that they remain asymptotically negligible and do not enter the first-order limit.

Assumption 3

(Design-compatible smoothness of nuisance estimators)
.

There exist functions
f_{m_{1}},f_{m_{0}}:[0,1]^{q}\to\mathbb{R}
such that,

\widehat{m}_{g}(\bm{x})-m_{g,0}(\bm{x})=\phi_{m_{g}}(\bm{x})+\rho_{g}(\bm{x}),\qquad\phi_{m_{g}}(\bm{x})=f_{m_{g}}(\mathcal{T}(\bm{x})),\qquad g\in\{0,1\},

where the decomposition residuals satisfy

r^{-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\rho_{g}^{2}(\bm{X}_{i})=o_{p}(r^{-1})
,
each
f_{m_{g}}
has finite generalized variation

V_{2}(f_{m_{g}},\mathcal{K})<\infty
, and the associated function

\varphi_{m_{g}}(\bm{z})=f_{m_{g}}(T_{\bm{Z}}(\bm{z}))
is Lipschitz continuous on

\mathrm{supp}(\mathcal{Z}\cup\mathcal{V}_{r_{p}})
with Lipschitz constant

L_{m_{g}}
.

Assumption
3
is a mild smoothness condition on the
nuisance estimators. It is
structurally analogous to Assumption
2
, which imposes
the same type of geometric representability on the oracle signal

\tau_{0}
; the present assumption imposes it on the estimation-error
functions
\widehat{m}_{g}-m_{g,0}
.

The final assumption is a conditional central limit theorem condition for the oracle residuals on the generated selected sample. Let

\mathcal{G}_{n}=\sigma\!\bigl((\bm{X}_{1},W_{1}),\ldots,(\bm{X}_{n},W_{n}),\mathcal{S}\bigr),

denote the
\sigma
-algebra for data and define the oracle residual

\varepsilon_{i}:=\psi_{i}^{\ast}-\tau_{0}(\bm{X}_{i}),\qquad i=1,\ldots,n.

Because the selected index set
\mathcal{S}
is constructed solely from
\{(\bm{X}_{i},W_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}
, the random variables
\{\varepsilon_{i}:i\in\mathcal{S}\}
are conditionally independent and conditionally mean-zero given
\mathcal{G}_{n}
.

Assumption 4

(Conditional triangular-array regularity)
.

There exists a constant
\sigma_{\psi}^{2}\in(0,\infty)
such that

\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}E(\varepsilon_{i}^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n})\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\sigma_{\psi}^{2}.

(8)

Moreover, for every fixed
\epsilon>0
,

\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}E\!\left[\varepsilon_{i}^{2}\mathbf{1}\{|\varepsilon_{i}|>\epsilon\sqrt{r}\}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n}\right]\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

(9)

Assumption
4
is a conditional Lindeberg–Feller condition for the triangular array generated by the selected sample; its role is purely probabilistic. The variance-stabilisation condition (
8
) identifies the limiting variance of the oracle score, while the Lindeberg condition (
9
) rules out asymptotic domination by a vanishing number of unusually large residuals.

The following lemma isolates the effect of replacing the oracle nuisance functions
\bm{\eta}_{0}
by their cross-fitted estimators on the UD-selected sample, thereby controlling the nuisance-estimation error at the sub-sampling scale.

Lemma 1

(Negligibility of the estimation error on UD)
.

Let

\widetilde{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\psi_{i}^{\ast}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\psi^{\ast}(\bm{O}_{i};\bm{\eta}_{0})

be the oracle UD estimator. Under Assumption
1
,

\widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}-\widetilde{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}=o_{p}(r^{-1/2}).

Lemma
1
formalises the same orthogonality principle that underlies standard DML, now instantiated on the UD-selected sample of size
r
. We next establish the asymptotic normality of the oracle UD estimator, which plays a pivotal role in the proof of Theorem
3
: once the estimation error has been shown to be negligible by Lemma
1
, the limiting behaviour of the feasible UD-DML estimator is driven by that of its oracle counterpart.

Proposition 1

(Conditional CLT for the oracle UD estimator)
.

Under Assumptions
2
and
4
, we have

\sqrt{r}\,\bigl(\widetilde{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}-\theta_{0}\bigr)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N(0,\sigma_{\psi}^{2}).

Proposition
1
elucidates the stochastic mechanism underlying the oracle UD estimator. The UD selection step does not perturb the first-order limit of the oracle score, provided that the selected subsample preserves the dominant signal in
\tau_{0}(\bm{X})
. Once the design-approximation error is sufficiently small, the large-sample uncertainty of the oracle estimator is governed by the residual variation of the orthogonal score on the selected sample, rather than by the geometry of the selection itself.

Theorem 3

(Asymptotic normality of UD-DML)
.

Under Assumptions
1
–
4
, we have

\sqrt{r}\,\bigl(\widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}-\theta_{0}\bigr)\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N(0,\sigma_{\psi}^{2}).

Theorem
3
establishes that the UD-DML estimator retains a valid root-
r
Gaussian limit. From a causal-inference perspective, this clarifies the statistical role of the UD subsampling step: its purpose is not merely computational compression but the construction of a smaller sample that still preserves the functionals of the covariate distribution most relevant for nuisance estimation and treatment-effect learning. Once this design objective is met, the orthogonal-score machinery delivers the same type of large-sample Gaussian inference as in standard DML, with the asymptotic variance now indexed by the selected sample. In this sense, UD-DML couples design-based representativeness with orthogonal-score robustness, yielding a computationally efficient yet inferentially valid approach for large-scale causal estimation.

For practical inference, the UD-DML point estimator is

\widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\widehat{\psi}_{i}^{\ast},

and a natural plug-in estimator of the asymptotic variance
\sigma_{\psi}^{2}
is the empirical variance of the cross-fitted pseudo-outcomes on the selected sample,

\widehat{\sigma}_{\psi,r}^{2}=\frac{1}{r-1}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\left(\widehat{\psi}_{i}^{\ast}-\widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}\right)^{2}.

The corresponding variance estimator for
\widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}
is

\widehat{V}_{\mathrm{UD}}=\frac{1}{r}\widehat{\sigma}_{\psi,r}^{2}=\frac{1}{r(r-1)}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\left(\widehat{\psi}_{i}^{\ast}-\widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}\right)^{2},

and the asymptotic
(1-\alpha)
Wald confidence interval for
\theta_{0}
is

\widehat{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\sqrt{\widehat{V}_{\mathrm{UD}}},

where
z_{1-\alpha/2}
denotes the
(1-\alpha/2)
-quantile of the standard normal distribution.

3
Simulated Studies

We now evaluate the finite-sample performance of UD-DML through a series of simulation experiments. The main benchmark is DML applied to a subsample drawn via simple uniform random sampling, hereafter denoted UNIF-DML.

3.1
Data-Generating Processes

We design three observational data-generating processes (DGPs) of increasing complexity in order to assess the finite-sample behaviour of UD-DML across varying covariate geometries, treatment-effect heterogeneities, and treated–control overlap regimes. Table
1
summarizes the key mathematical structures. Across all configurations, the full data size is denoted by
n
, and the covariate dimension is fixed at
p=10
. The observed outcome
Y
is generated as
Y=g(\bm{X})+W\cdot\Delta(\bm{X})+\varepsilon
with
\varepsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, and the true Average Treatment Effect
\theta_{0}=\mathbb{E}[\Delta(\bm{X})]
is analytically fixed at
1.0
in all three scenarios.

The first scenario (OBS-1) serves as a baseline of low heterogeneity and high overlap. The covariates are mutually independent and uniformly distributed,
X^{(d)}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}U[-2,2]
for
d=1,\ldots,p
. The outcome mechanism is linear, with
g(\bm{X})=0.5X^{(1)}+0.3X^{(2)}
and conditional average treatment effect (CATE)
\Delta(\bm{X})=1.0+0.2X^{(3)}
. Treatment is confounded by the logistic propensity
\operatorname{logit}(e(\bm{X}))=0.2X^{(1)}-0.2X^{(2)}
; the small coefficients keep propensity scores close to
0.5
, thereby ensuring a high degree of overlap between the treated and control groups.

The second scenario (OBS-2) introduces moderate heterogeneity alongside non-uniform covariate densities. The first five covariates follow
X^{(1)},\dots,X^{(5)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}U[-2,2]
, while the remaining five follow
X^{(6)},\dots,X^{(10)}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\mathrm{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mathcal{N}(0,1.5^{2})

g(\bm{X})=0.5(X^{(1)})^{2}+0.5X^{(2)}X^{(3)}+\sin(X^{(6)})

\Delta(\bm{X})=1.0+0.5X^{(1)}X^{(2)}
. The propensity score model,
\text{logit}(e(\bm{X}))=0.5X^{(1)}-0.3(X^{(2)})^{2}+0.4\sin(X^{(6)})+0.2X^{(7)}

The third scenario (OBS-3) is designed to stress-test the estimators by combining strong heterogeneity with severe overlap deficiency. The first five covariates are drawn from an equally weighted two-component Gaussian mixture with component means
(-2,-2,0,0,0)
and
(2,2,0,0,0)
and per-coordinate standard deviation
0.5

X^{(6)},\ldots,X^{(10)}\overset{\text{i.i.d.}}{\sim}\mathcal{N}(0,1)

g(\bm{X})=\sin(\pi X^{(1)})+0.5X^{(2)}X^{(3)}+0.1(X^{(6)})^{3}+0.2\cos(X^{(7)})
and
\Delta(\bm{X})=1.0+0.5\tanh(X^{(1)})+0.2X^{(6)}X^{(7)}
. The propensity score
\operatorname{logit}(e(\bm{X}))=0.3X^{(1)}+0.3X^{(2)}-0.5X^{(6)}
acts on the bimodal mixture variables, systematically driving treatment probabilities towards the boundaries
0
and
1
and thus inducing a highly challenging low-overlap environment in which naive subsampling is expected to yield unreliable inference.

To isolate the effect of overlap severity on estimator performance, we additionally consider a tunable variant of OBS-3, in which the propensity score is rescaled as
\operatorname{logit}(e(\bm{X}))=c\cdot(0.3X^{(1)}+0.3X^{(2)}-0.5X^{(6)})
for a multiplier
c\geq 0
. Setting
c=0
yields perfect overlap (
e(\bm{X})\equiv 0.5
),
c=1
recovers the default OBS-3, and larger values drive propensity scores towards
0
and
1

Experimental Settings

Unless stated otherwise, the DML nuisance functions

Finite-sample performance is evaluated through four standard criteria. The root mean squared error,

Results and Analysis

Figure

The underlying mechanism is as follows. In the poor-overlap regimes illustrated in Figure

The practical consequence is that, at any given sample size, UD-DML yields sharper point estimation and tighter yet still valid confidence intervals; the gains therefore materialise as improved inferential efficiency rather than as undercoverage, and they grow precisely in the regimes where naive subsampling is most costly.

4
Real Data Application

Having established the finite-sample advantages of UD-DML by simulation, we now illustrate its practical utility on a large-scale observational dataset. Specifically, we apply UD-DML to estimate the causal effect of maternal smoking on infant birth weight using the 2021 US Natality records, thereby demonstrating the method’s ability to deliver statistically efficient and computationally tractable inference under realistic conditions.

5
Conclusions

This paper has addressed a problem at the intersection of modern causal inference and massive observational data. While double machine learning delivers valid and flexible inference on causal estimands, its cross-fitted machine-learning machinery becomes computationally prohibitive at full-sample scale. The natural alternative of running DML on a uniformly drawn subsample is computationally attractive but, as we have shown, statistically fragile: uniform subsampling disregards the covariate geometry and offers no protection against treated–control imbalance, producing unstable estimates precisely in the low-overlap regimes that are common in observational studies.

To resolve this tension, we have introduced UD-DML, a design-based subsampling framework that treats the choice of the working sample as a statistical design problem rather than as a probabilistic reduction. UD-DML first constructs a low-discrepancy skeleton in a PCA-rotated covariate space under the mixture-discrepancy criterion, and then assigns, to each skeleton point, the nearest treated and control units via KD-tree search. The resulting matched subsample is, by construction, both representative of the full covariate distribution and balanced across treatment arms, and is subsequently plugged into the standard cross-fitted DML pipeline without any further methodological change.

On the theoretical side, we have established discrepancy-based guarantees that translate the UD construction into deterministic control of two distinct geometric errors: a global representativeness error governed by the generalised empirical
F
-discrepancy of the UD skeleton, and a within-subsample treated–control balance error governed by the common matching radius in the rotated space. Building on these design properties and on the Neyman-orthogonality of the AIPW score, we have proved that the UD-DML estimator is
\sqrt{r}
-asymptotically normal under the standard DML nuisance-rate conditions, where
r\ll n
is the selected subsample size, so that Wald inference based on the selected sample remains asymptotically valid.

The empirical evidence reinforces and refines these theoretical statements. Our simulation study spans three observational DGPs of increasing complexity (OBS-1 to OBS-3), a unified misspecification protocol in which all wrong components are estimated on a single uninformative covariate
X^{(5)}
, and four experiments targeting subsample-size efficiency, scalability in
n
, preservation of double robustness under nuisance misspecification, and sensitivity along a tunable overlap gradient. Across all four dimensions, UD-DML delivers lower RMSE, narrower confidence intervals, and more reliable coverage than UNIF-DML, with the largest gains in the low-overlap and jointly-misspecified regimes. The supplementary Q–Q and SMD diagnostics further confirm that these gains stem from a genuinely Gaussian sampling distribution of the estimator and from the design-induced treated–control balance, rather than from opportunistic interval width. The natality application on the CDC 2021 micro-data reproduces the same qualitative pattern on a genuine observational dataset: UD-DML is statistically close to full-data DML, computationally close to uniform subsampling, and substantially more efficient than uniform subsampling in both bias and variance.

Several directions remain open for future research. First, the current implementation relies on KD-tree nearest-neighbour matching in the retained PCA-rotated space, which is effective when the working dimension
q
is low to moderate but is eroded by the curse of dimensionality when
p\gg r
or when the relevant signal is genuinely high-dimensional; extending the UD construction to learned low-dimensional embeddings is a natural next step, for example, using sufficient dimension reduction, representation learning, or random projections. Second, the present framework is tailored to a binary treatment via 1:1 paired matching; generalising the geometric matching principle to continuous or multi-valued treatments, for instance by anchoring the skeleton to a dose–response grid, would broaden its applicability to policy-evaluation problems. Third, hybrid strategies that combine UD-DML’s geometric balance with the variance-minimising ideas of model-based optimal subsampling could further improve efficiency, particularly when partial prior information on the nuisance structure is available. Finally, embedding UD-DML into richer causal targets beyond the ATE, including conditional average treatment effects, distributional effects, and dynamic treatment regimes, would extend its scalable design-based perspective to the broader semiparametric and machine-learning-based causal-inference literature.

A.1
Derivation of the Closed Form of the Mixture Discrepancy

Let

\mathcal{D}=\{\bm{u}_{1},\ldots,\bm{u}_{r_{p}}\}\subset[0,1]^{q},\qquad\bm{u}_{j}=(u_{j1},\ldots,u_{jq})^{\top},

and let

F_{\mathcal{D}}=\frac{1}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\delta_{\bm{u}_{j}}

be the empirical distribution of the design points. Let
F_{u}
denote the uniform distribution on
[0,1]^{q}
. For a reproducing kernel
\mathcal{K}:[0,1]^{q}\times[0,1]^{q}\to\mathbb{R}
, the squared discrepancy is

D^{2}(\mathcal{D};F_{u},\mathcal{K})=\int_{[0,1]^{2q}}\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{t})\,d\{F_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{u})-F_{u}(\bm{u})\}\,d\{F_{\mathcal{D}}(\bm{t})-F_{u}(\bm{t})\}.

(10)

We now derive the closed form of the mixture discrepancy
D_{M}^{2}(\mathcal{D})
.

Proposition 2

(Closed form of the mixture discrepancy)
.

Let
\mathcal{K}_{M}
be the mixture kernel

\mathcal{K}_{M}(\bm{u},\bm{t})=\prod_{d=1}^{q}\kappa_{M}(u_{d},t_{d}),

where

\kappa_{M}(u,t)=\frac{15}{8}-\frac{1}{4}\left|u-\frac{1}{2}\right|-\frac{1}{4}\left|t-\frac{1}{2}\right|-\frac{3}{4}|u-t|+\frac{1}{2}|u-t|^{2},\qquad u,t\in[0,1].

Then

\displaystyle D_{M}^{2}(\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{19}{12}\right)^{q}-\frac{2}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\prod_{d=1}^{q}\left[\frac{5}{3}-\frac{1}{4}\left|u_{jd}-\frac{1}{2}\right|-\frac{1}{4}\left(u_{jd}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{r_{p}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\sum_{k=1}^{r_{p}}\prod_{d=1}^{q}\left(\frac{15}{8}-\frac{1}{4}\left|u_{jd}-\frac{1}{2}\right|-\frac{1}{4}\left|u_{kd}-\frac{1}{2}\right|-\frac{3}{4}|u_{jd}-u_{kd}|+\frac{1}{2}|u_{jd}-u_{kd}|^{2}\right).

Proof.

Write
\mu=F_{\mathcal{D}}-F_{u}
. Expanding the quadratic form in
(
10
) gives

D^{2}(\mathcal{D};F_{u},\mathcal{K})=\int_{[0,1]^{2q}}\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{t})\,d\bm{u}\,d\bm{t}-\frac{2}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\int_{[0,1]^{q}}\mathcal{K}(\bm{u},\bm{u}_{j})\,d\bm{u}+\frac{1}{r_{p}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\sum_{k=1}^{r_{p}}\mathcal{K}(\bm{u}_{j},\bm{u}_{k}),

(11)

where we used
F_{\mathcal{D}}=r_{p}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\delta_{\bm{u}_{j}}
and the symmetry of

\mathcal{K}
.

Now specialize to the mixture kernel

\mathcal{K}_{M}(\bm{u},\bm{t})=\prod_{d=1}^{q}\kappa_{M}(u_{d},t_{d}),\qquad\kappa_{M}(u,t)=\frac{15}{8}-\frac{1}{4}\Bigl|u-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr|-\frac{1}{4}\Bigl|t-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr|-\frac{3}{4}|u-t|+\frac{1}{2}|u-t|^{2}.

By Fubini’s theorem, the integrals in (
11
) factorize coordinatewise. Define

A_{0}:=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{0}^{1}\kappa_{M}(u,t)\,du\,dt,\qquad A_{1}(t):=\int_{0}^{1}\kappa_{M}(u,t)\,du.

Then

\int_{[0,1]^{2q}}\mathcal{K}_{M}(\bm{u},\bm{t})\,d\bm{u}\,d\bm{t}=A_{0}^{q},\qquad\int_{[0,1]^{q}}\mathcal{K}_{M}(\bm{u},\bm{u}_{j})\,d\bm{u}=\prod_{d=1}^{q}A_{1}(u_{jd}).

A direct calculation yields

\int_{0}^{1}\Bigl|u-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr|\,du=\frac{1}{4},\qquad\int_{0}^{1}|u-t|\,du=\Bigl(t-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)^{2}+\frac{1}{4},\qquad\int_{0}^{1}|u-t|^{2}\,du=\Bigl(t-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)^{2}+\frac{1}{12}.

Substituting these identities into the definition of
A_{1}(t)
gives

A_{1}(t)=\frac{5}{3}-\frac{1}{4}\Bigl|t-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr|-\frac{1}{4}\Bigl(t-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr)^{2},

and therefore

A_{0}=\int_{0}^{1}A_{1}(t)\,dt=\frac{19}{12}.

Finally,

\mathcal{K}_{M}(\bm{u}_{j},\bm{u}_{k})=\prod_{d=1}^{q}\left(\frac{15}{8}-\frac{1}{4}\Bigl|u_{jd}-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr|-\frac{1}{4}\Bigl|u_{kd}-\frac{1}{2}\Bigr|-\frac{3}{4}|u_{jd}-u_{kd}|+\frac{1}{2}|u_{jd}-u_{kd}|^{2}\right).

Substituting the above expressions for
A_{0}
,
A_{1}(\cdot)
, and
\mathcal{K}_{M}(\bm{u}_{j},\bm{u}_{k})

into (
11
) yields

\displaystyle D_{M}^{2}(\mathcal{D})

\displaystyle=\left(\frac{19}{12}\right)^{q}-\frac{2}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\prod_{d=1}^{q}\left[\frac{5}{3}-\frac{1}{4}\left|u_{jd}-\frac{1}{2}\right|-\frac{1}{4}\left(u_{jd}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}\right]

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{r_{p}^{2}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\sum_{k=1}^{r_{p}}\prod_{d=1}^{q}\left(\frac{15}{8}-\frac{1}{4}\left|u_{jd}-\frac{1}{2}\right|-\frac{1}{4}\left|u_{kd}-\frac{1}{2}\right|-\frac{3}{4}|u_{jd}-u_{kd}|+\frac{1}{2}|u_{jd}-u_{kd}|^{2}\right),

which is the desired result.
∎

A.2
Proof of Theorem
1

Proof.

Fix
g\in\{0,1\}
. Since
\bm{Z}_{i}=\Pi_{q}(\bm{X}_{i})
for every
i
, the left-hand side of (
4
) can be written as

\displaystyle\int\phi_{f}(\bm{x})\,dP_{n,\bm{X}}(\bm{x})-\int\phi_{f}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S}_{g},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f\!\left(T_{\bm{Z}}(\bm{Z}_{i})\right)-\frac{1}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}f\!\left(T_{\bm{Z}}(\bm{Z}_{i_{j}^{g}})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{f}(\bm{Z}_{i})-\frac{1}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\varphi_{f}(\bm{Z}_{i_{j}^{g}}).

Add and subtract the skeleton average
r_{p}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\varphi_{f}(\bm{v}_{j})
to obtain

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{f}(\bm{Z}_{i})-\frac{1}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\varphi_{f}(\bm{Z}_{i_{j}^{g}})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\varphi_{f}(\bm{Z}_{i})-\frac{1}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\varphi_{f}(\bm{v}_{j})\right|+\left|\frac{1}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\varphi_{f}(\bm{v}_{j})-\frac{1}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\varphi_{f}(\bm{Z}_{i_{j}^{g}})\right|

\displaystyle=:A_{n,g}+B_{n,g}.

We first control
A_{n,g}
. By the empirical Koksma–Hlawka inequality (
3
) with
\mathcal{P}=\mathcal{V}_{r_{p}}
and
m=r_{p}
,

A_{n,g}=\left|\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f\!\left(T_{\bm{Z}}(\bm{Z}_{i})\right)-\frac{1}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}f\!\left(T_{\bm{Z}}(\bm{v}_{j})\right)\right|\leq D(\mathcal{V}_{r_{p}};\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{K})\,V_{2}(f,\mathcal{K}).

Next consider
B_{n,g}
. By the Lipschitz continuity of
\varphi_{f}
,

\displaystyle B_{n,g}

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\left|\varphi_{f}(\bm{v}_{j})-\varphi_{f}(\bm{Z}_{i_{j}^{g}})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{L_{f}}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\|\bm{v}_{j}-\bm{Z}_{i_{j}^{g}}\|_{2}\leq L_{f}\,\delta_{g}^{(\mathrm{rot})}.

Combining the bounds for
A_{n,g}
and
B_{n,g}
yields (
4
).

It remains to prove (
5
). Since

P_{\mathcal{S},\bm{X}}=\frac{1}{2}P_{\mathcal{S}_{1},\bm{X}}+\frac{1}{2}P_{\mathcal{S}_{0},\bm{X}},

we have

\displaystyle\int\phi_{f}(\bm{x})\,dP_{n,\bm{X}}(\bm{x})-\int\phi_{f}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left\{\int\phi_{f}(\bm{x})\,dP_{n,\bm{X}}(\bm{x})-\int\phi_{f}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S}_{1},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right\}

\displaystyle+\frac{1}{2}\left\{\int\phi_{f}(\bm{x})\,dP_{n,\bm{X}}(\bm{x})-\int\phi_{f}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S}_{0},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right\}.

Applying the triangle inequality and then (
4
) with
g=1
and
g=0
gives

\left|\int\phi_{f}\,dP_{n,\bm{X}}-\int\phi_{f}\,dP_{\mathcal{S},\bm{X}}\right|\leq D(\mathcal{V}_{r_{p}};\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{K})\,V_{2}(f,\mathcal{K})+\frac{L_{f}}{2}\bigl(\delta_{1}^{(\mathrm{rot})}+\delta_{0}^{(\mathrm{rot})}\bigr),

which completes the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem
2

Proof.

By construction, for each
g\in\{0,1\}
,

\int\phi_{f}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S}_{g},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})=\frac{1}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\phi_{f}(\bm{X}_{i_{j}^{g}})=\frac{1}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\varphi_{f}(\bm{Z}_{i_{j}^{g}}),

where
\varphi_{f}(\bm{z})=f\!\left(T_{\bm{Z}}(\bm{z})\right)
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\left|\int\phi_{f}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S}_{1},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})-\int\phi_{f}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S}_{0},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right|=\left|\frac{1}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\varphi_{f}(\bm{Z}_{i_{j}^{1}})-\frac{1}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\varphi_{f}(\bm{Z}_{i_{j}^{0}})\right|.

Add and subtract the common skeleton average
r_{p}^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\varphi_{f}(\bm{v}_{j})
to obtain

\displaystyle\left|\frac{1}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\varphi_{f}(\bm{Z}_{i_{j}^{1}})-\frac{1}{r_{p}}\sum_{j=1}^{r_{p}}\varphi_{f}(\bm{Z}_{i_{j}^{0}})\right|

Using the Lipschitz continuity of

A.5
Proof of Proposition
1

Proof.

By definition of the oracle UD estimator,

\widetilde{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}-\theta_{0}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\psi_{i}^{\ast}-\theta_{0}.

We now add and subtract the selected-sample and full-sample averages of
\tau_{0}(\bm{X}_{i})
:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}-\theta_{0}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\psi_{i}^{\ast}-\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\tau_{0}(\bm{X}_{i})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\tau_{0}(\bm{X}_{i})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{0}(\bm{X}_{i})

\displaystyle\quad+\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{0}(\bm{X}_{i})-\theta_{0}.

Hence

\widetilde{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}-\theta_{0}=\mathcal{B}_{1,r}+\mathcal{B}_{2,r}+\mathcal{B}_{3,n},

where

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{1,r}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\bigl\{\psi_{i}^{\ast}-\tau_{0}(\bm{X}_{i})\bigr\}=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\varepsilon_{i},

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{2,r}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\tau_{0}(\bm{X}_{i})-\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{0}(\bm{X}_{i}),

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{3,n}

\displaystyle:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{0}(\bm{X}_{i})-\theta_{0}.

We analyze these three terms in turn. We first consider
\mathcal{B}_{1,r}
. By Assumption
4
, conditional on

\mathcal{G}_{n}=\sigma\!\bigl((\bm{X}_{1},W_{1}),\ldots,(\bm{X}_{n},W_{n}),\mathcal{S}\bigr),

the collection
\{\varepsilon_{i}:i\in\mathcal{S}\}
is conditionally independent and conditionally mean-zero. Therefore,

E\!\left(\varepsilon_{i}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n}\right)=0,\qquad i\in\mathcal{S},

and so

E\!\left(\mathcal{B}_{1,r}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}E\!\left(\varepsilon_{i}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n}\right)=0.

Its conditional variance is

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\sqrt{r}\,\mathcal{B}_{1,r}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\varepsilon_{i}\Bigm|\mathcal{G}_{n}\right).

Using conditional independence, the conditional covariance terms vanish, so

\displaystyle\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\sqrt{r}\,\mathcal{B}_{1,r}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\mathrm{Var}\!\left(\varepsilon_{i}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n}\right)=\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}E\!\left(\varepsilon_{i}^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n}\right),

because
E(\varepsilon_{i}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n})=0
. By (
8
),

\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}E\!\left(\varepsilon_{i}^{2}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n}\right)\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}\sigma_{\psi}^{2}.

Next, still conditional on
\mathcal{G}_{n}
, the triangular array

\left\{{\varepsilon_{i}}/{\sqrt{r}}:i\in\mathcal{S}\right\}

satisfies the Lindeberg condition by (
9
). Indeed, for every fixed
\epsilon>0
,

\frac{1}{r}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}E\!\left[\varepsilon_{i}^{2}\mathbf{1}\{|\varepsilon_{i}|>\epsilon\sqrt{r}\}\mid\mathcal{G}_{n}\right]\overset{p}{\longrightarrow}0.

Therefore, since the conditional convergence in distribution holds in probability over
\mathcal{G}_{n}
by the conditional Lindeberg–Feller central limit theorem, the unconditional convergence in distribution follows

\frac{1}{\sqrt{r}}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{S}}\varepsilon_{i}=\sqrt{r}\,\mathcal{B}_{1,r}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N(0,\sigma_{\psi}^{2}).

Next consider
\mathcal{B}_{2,r}
. By definition,

\mathcal{B}_{2,r}=\int\tau_{0}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})-\int\tau_{0}(\bm{x})\,dP_{n,\bm{X}}(\bm{x}).

Under Assumption
2
, write

\tau_{0}(\bm{x})=\phi_{\tau}(\bm{x})+b_{\tau,r}(\bm{x}),\qquad\phi_{\tau}(\bm{x})=f_{\tau}\!\bigl(\mathcal{T}(\bm{x})\bigr).

Then

\displaystyle|\mathcal{B}_{2,r}|

\displaystyle\leq\left|\int\phi_{\tau}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})-\int\phi_{\tau}(\bm{x})\,dP_{n,\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right|

\displaystyle\quad+\left|\int b_{\tau,r}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right|+\left|\int b_{\tau,r}(\bm{x})\,dP_{n,\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right|.

Since

P_{\mathcal{S},\bm{X}}=\frac{1}{2}P_{\mathcal{S}_{1},\bm{X}}+\frac{1}{2}P_{\mathcal{S}_{0},\bm{X}},

we further have

\displaystyle\left|\int b_{\tau,r}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{2}\left|\int b_{\tau,r}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S}_{1},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right|+\frac{1}{2}\left|\int b_{\tau,r}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S}_{0},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right|.

Moreover, by Theorem
1
applied to
\phi_{\tau}
,

\displaystyle\left|\int\phi_{\tau}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})-\int\phi_{\tau}(\bm{x})\,dP_{n,\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right|

\displaystyle\leq D(\mathcal{V}_{r_{p}};\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{K})\,V_{2}(f_{\tau},\mathcal{K})+\frac{L_{\tau}}{2}\bigl(\delta_{1}^{(\mathrm{rot})}+\delta_{0}^{(\mathrm{rot})}\bigr).

Therefore,

\displaystyle|\mathcal{B}_{2,r}|

\displaystyle\leq D(\mathcal{V}_{r_{p}};\mathcal{Z},\mathcal{K})\,V_{2}(f_{\tau},\mathcal{K})+\frac{L_{\tau}}{2}\bigl(\delta_{1}^{(\mathrm{rot})}+\delta_{0}^{(\mathrm{rot})}\bigr)

\displaystyle\quad+\left|\int b_{\tau,r}(\bm{x})\,dP_{n,\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right|+\frac{1}{2}\left|\int b_{\tau,r}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S}_{1},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right|+\frac{1}{2}\left|\int b_{\tau,r}(\bm{x})\,dP_{\mathcal{S}_{0},\bm{X}}(\bm{x})\right|.

By Assumption
2
, each term on the right-hand side is
o_{p}(r^{-1/2})
. Hence

|\mathcal{B}_{2,r}|=o_{p}(r^{-1/2}),\qquad\text{so that}\qquad\sqrt{r}\,\mathcal{B}_{2,r}=o_{p}(1).

It remains to analyze
\mathcal{B}_{3,n}
. Since

\theta_{0}=E\{\psi^{\ast}(\bm{O};\bm{\eta}_{0})\}=E\!\left[E\{\psi^{\ast}(\bm{O};\bm{\eta}_{0})\mid\bm{X}\}\right]=E\{\tau_{0}(\bm{X})\},

we may write

\mathcal{B}_{3,n}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\tau_{0}(\bm{X}_{i})-E\{\tau_{0}(\bm{X})\}.

Because Assumption
2
imposes
E\{\tau_{0}(\bm{X})^{2}\}<\infty
, the classical central limit theorem, or equivalently the weaker statement implied by Chebyshev’s inequality, gives

\mathcal{B}_{3,n}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2}).

Indeed,

E(\mathcal{B}_{3,n})=0,\qquad\mathrm{Var}(\mathcal{B}_{3,n})=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Var}\{\tau_{0}(\bm{X})\},

so

E\!\left(n\,\mathcal{B}_{3,n}^{2}\right)=\mathrm{Var}\{\tau_{0}(\bm{X})\}<\infty,

which implies
\mathcal{B}_{3,n}=O_{p}(n^{-1/2})
. Since
r/n\to 0
by Assumption
2
,

\sqrt{r}\,\mathcal{B}_{3,n}=\sqrt{r}\,O_{p}(n^{-1/2})=O_{p}\!\left(\sqrt{\frac{r}{n}}\right)=o_{p}(1).

Combining the three parts, we obtain

\sqrt{r}\,(\widetilde{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}-\theta_{0})=\sqrt{r}\,\mathcal{B}_{1,r}+\sqrt{r}\,\mathcal{B}_{2,r}+\sqrt{r}\,\mathcal{B}_{3,n}.

We have shown that

\sqrt{r}\,\mathcal{B}_{1,r}\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N(0,\sigma_{\psi}^{2}),\qquad\sqrt{r}\,\mathcal{B}_{2,r}=o_{p}(1),\qquad\sqrt{r}\,\mathcal{B}_{3,n}=o_{p}(1).

Therefore, by Slutsky’s theorem,

\sqrt{r}\,(\widetilde{\theta}_{\mathrm{UD}}-\theta_{0})\overset{d}{\longrightarrow}N(0,\sigma_{\psi}^{2}).

This completes the proof.
∎
