Direct Asymmetric α-C Conjugate Addition of Aminomethylphosphonate Enabled by Carbonyl/Iminium Double Organoactivation
Authors: Pengwei Ji, Xingdie Yang, Weibo Ling, Songnan Zhang, Tongyin Chen, Wen-Wen Chen, Siqi Liu, Baoguo Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As important bioisosteres of α-amino acids, chiral α-aminophosphonic acids exhibit broad bioactivities and are widely employed in pharmaceuticals. Direct asymmetric α-C conjugate addition of aminomethylphosphonate to α,β-unsaturated ketones is an intriguing strategy to make bioactive chiral phosphonic 1-pyrrolines. However, this transformation remains challenging due to the low α-C–H acidity of the aminomethylphosphonate, significant steric hindrance from the phosphonate moiety and interference by side reactions. In this work, we report the direct enantioselective α-C conjugate addition of NH2-unprotected aminomethylphosphonate to α,β-unsaturated ketones, by applying an organo-organo double activation strategy that employs pyridoxal to activate aminomethylphosphonates into reactive carbanions, while concurrently using pyrrolidine to convert α,β-unsaturated ketones into active iminium species. Various chiral phosphonic 1-pyrrolines are synthesized in high yields with excellent diastereo- and enantioselectivity (up to 99% yield, > 20:1 dr, 99% ee). Furthermore, the methodology can be extended to a one-pot sequential synthesis of structurally diverse quaternary α-aminophosphonate derivatives.

Introduction

Chiral α-aminophosphonic acids are important bioisosteres of α-amino acids, possessing diverse biological activities1. They are valued structural motifs widely present in pharmaceuticals2–6 and also serve as privileged scaffolds for chiral catalysts7 (Fig. 1a). Thus, enantioselective construction of structurally diverse chiral α-substituted aminophosphonic acids has attracted considerable research interest in organic chemistry, prompting the development of new synthetic strategy8–15. Asymmetric α-C–H functionalization of aminomethylphosphonates with electrophiles represents an efficient strategy to access α-substituted chiral α-aminophosphonates16–23. Nevertheless, this approach, which is normally facilitated by chiral auxiliaries16, chiral metal complex catalysts17–20, or chiral phase-transfer catalysts21–23, typically requires protecting group manipulations to prevent interference from the reactive NH2 group. By applying carbonyl catalysis24–47, it is promising to achieve direct asymmetric α-C–H functionalization of aminomethylphosphonates without pre-protection of the NH2 group, making this transformation more attractive in terms of atom- and step-economies. However, compared to glycinates, which have been successfully applied to a series of chiral aldehyde-catalyzed α-C–H functionalization24,31,33,34,37–47, aminomethylphosphonates are a type of much more challenging substrates for these reactions due to the following facts (Fig. 1b). First, although aminomethylphosphonates contain a phosphoryl (P = O) group as an electron-withdrawing motif, the phosphonate group exhibits significantly weaker electron-withdrawing ability compared to the carboxylate ester in glycinates. As a result, the acidity of the α-C–H proton in aminomethylphosphonates is substantially lower (pKa ~ 38.4 in DMSO, see SI) than that in glycinates (pKa ~ 24 in H2O)48, impeding proton abstraction under mild catalytic conditions. Second, glycinate forms a planar enolate intermediate after deprotonation, which allows a facile approach of electrophiles. In contrast, the corresponding anion derived from aminomethylphosphonate is sterically encumbered by the adjacent bulky, spherical phosphonate group. This steric shielding creates a significant kinetic barrier to the attack of electrophiles, thereby hindering the desired α-C–H functionalization. When α,β-unsaturated ketones are applied, the reaction likely suffers from interference by several potential side reactions, including conjugate N-addition49, [3 + 2] cycloaddition between α,β-unsaturated ketones and aldehyde-aminomethylphosphonate imine50, and Wittig-Horner reaction of aminomethylphosphonate51 (Fig. 1c).

Recently, we have identified a class of chiral pyridoxals bearing an ortho-substituted aryl group are effective carbonyl catalysts, demonstrating versatile capability in multiple transformations. They have been successfully applied to enantioselective α C–H conjugate addition of propargylamines to α,β-unsaturated ketones to deliver chiral polysubstituted 1-pyrrolines35. Moreover, introducing a methyl group to the pyridine nitrogen of the pyridoxals led to direct asymmetric α-alkylation of NH₂-unprotected amino acid esters to construct chiral quaternary α-amino acid esters34. Based on these findings, we envision that this chiral pyridoxal can activate aminomethylphosphonate 1a through imine formation, facilitating α-C–H deprotonation into active carbanion52. Due to the considerable steric hindrance imparted by the ortho-substituent, both the [3 + 2] cycloaddition and Wittig-Horner reaction pathways are expected to be suppressed. Meanwhile, pyrrolidine can be employed simultaneously to activate the α,β-unsaturated ketone via iminium ion formation53–56, enhancing its electrophilicity.

In this work, we have successfully achieved the asymmetric α-C conjugate addition of aminomethylphosphonate 1a to α,β-unsaturated ketones 2 based on an organo-organo double activation strategy57–71. The conjugate adducts undergo in situ intramolecular cyclization, producing a wide range of chiral phosphonic 1-pyrrolines (3) featuring two contiguous stereocenters in high yields with excellent stereoselectivities (up to 99% yield, >20:1 dr, 99% ee) (Fig. 1d). Results

Reaction optimization

We began our study by examining the reaction of aminomethylphosphonate 1a with α,β-unsaturated ketone 2a in the presence of chiral pyridoxal (R)-4a as the catalyst and LiOTf as the Lewis acid (Table 1). Despite the screening of a range of bases, the reaction activity remained low, albeit with respectable enantioselectivity (Table 1, entries 1–4). The results revealed that K2CO3 was the best choice in view of both activity and stereocontrol, providing the chiral phosphonic 1-pyrroline product 3a in 10% yield with high dr value (>20:1 dr) and 94% ee (Table 1, entry 1 vs. 2-4). Among the solvents examined, MTBE proved optimal, affording the desired product 3a in 19% yield with high stereoselectivity (Table 1, entry 5 vs. 1, 6–7). The low reaction yield is probably attributed to the low reactivity of α,β-unsaturated ketone 2a. To enhance the electrophilicity of 2a, pyrrolidine was added with the aim of forming a more reactive iminium ion species. To our delight, this modification led to a significant improvement in the reaction yield as anticipated, generating the product 3a in a 77% yield with good stereoselectivity (Table 1, entry 8). The use of other activating reagent such as azetidine and piperidine resulted in lower yields in comparison to pyrrolidine (Table 1, entries 9-10 vs. 8). In the absence of pyridoxal catalyst 4a, the reaction proceeded predominantly via classical N-addition pathway, with no detectable formation of the desired α-C-addition product (Table 1, entry 11). The results underscored the essential role of the pyridoxal catalyst in promoting the α-C selective pathway. Subsequent catalyst screening revealed that (R)-4e, which features a benzene–pyridine biaryl skeleton and a bulky lateral amide side chain, exhibited the highest enantioselectivity in the reaction (Table 1, entry 15 vs. 8, 12-14, 16). Further optimization of reaction conditions identified ZnF₂ as the optimal Lewis acid, enabling the formation of the target product 3a in 94% isolated yield with >20:1 dr and 98% ee (Table 1, entry 17 vs. 15, 18). Without the addition of Lewis acid ZnF2, a slight decrease in reaction activity was observed (Table 1, entry 19 vs. 17). Moreover, several ester variants on the aminomethylphosphonate were also examined. The results indicated that the ester group has a minor impact on stereocontrol but a significant effect on the reaction yield (see Table S1 in SI). The ethyl ester gave only a 33% yield, and only a trace amount of the product was observed for the benzyl ester. The low yields were primarily attributed to the hydrolysis of the benzyl ester and ethyl ester products under the reaction conditions. The isopropyl ester proved optimal, delivering high yield alongside the superior stereocontrol. Substrate scope

With the optimized conditions in hand, the scope of α,β-unsaturated ketones 2 was investigated in the asymmetric conjugate addition with aminomethylphosphonate 1a (Fig. 2). A broad range of α,β-unsaturated ketones bearing electron-withdrawing or electron-donating groups at the ortho-, meta-, and/or para-positions of the aromatic ring underwent the reaction smoothly, delivering the corresponding chiral phosphonic 1-pyrrolines (as for 3b-r) in good to high yields (55–99%) with consistently high diastereoselectivities (> 20:1 dr) and excellent enantioselectivities (93–98% ee). It is noteworthy that aryl-substituted α,β-unsaturated ketones featuring polar functional groups, including cyano (as for 3d) and hydroxyl (as for 3f), were also well tolerated under the standard reaction conditions. 1-Naphthyl (as for 3s), 2-naphthyl (as for 3t) and heteroaryl (as for 3u-y) substituted α,β-unsaturated ketones were all effective substrates for the transformation, producing the desired products in good to excellent yields (68-99%) with high chiral induction (> 20:1 dr, 97-99% ee). Moreover, an α,β-unsaturated ketone incorporating a biologically active moiety derived from β-Estradiol72 was also compatible with the reaction conditions, affording the corresponding product 3z in good yield with excellent stereocontrol (75% yield, >20:1:-:- dr). α,β-Unsaturated ketones bearing alkenyl (as for 3aa) or alkynyl (as for 3ab) substituents were also viable substrates for this reaction, albeit with a moderate reduction in yield. Remarkably, alkyl-substituted α,β-unsaturated ketones with lower electrophilicity were viable substrates for this transformation in the presence of (R)-4b as the catalyst at a higher reaction temperature, affording alkyl-substituted 1-pyrroline products 3ac-ae in acceptable yields (54-60%) with high diastereo- and enantioselectivities (> 20:1 dr, 93-94% ee). Tri-substituted α,β-unsaturated ketones such as 4,4,4-trifluoro-1,3-diphenylbut-2-en-1-one and 1,3-diphenylbut-2-en-1-one were also investigated, which were inactive in the reaction with aminomethylphosphonate. Further studies showed that α-substituted aminomethylphosphonate displayed very low activity in the reaction with α,β-unsaturated ketone 2a, likely due to their bulky steric hindrance. For example, when diisopropyl (1-aminoethyl)phosphonate was utilized, only a trace amount of the α-C addition product was observed. The stereoselective construction of quaternary stereocenters remains a challenge in synthetic chemistry. Catalytic asymmetric methods for accessing enantioenriched quaternary α-aminophosphonate derivatives are still limited73–75. By applying this organo-organo double activation strategy to a one-pot sequential reaction, we also achieved the direct asymmetric difunctionalization of aminomethylphosphonate 1a to construct enantioenriched quaternary α-aminophosphonates 6 (Fig. 3). This process involves the initial conjugate addition of 1a with electrophile 2 to form phosphonic 1-pyrroline 3. The acidic α-amino C–H bond in 3 allows for further functionalization. Deprotonation of 3 with a strong base generates a carbanion that reacts with a second electrophile 5 to generate a chiral quaternary carbon center, enabling access to chiral α-quaternary phosphonic 1-pyrrolines 6. The substrate scope of electrophiles 5 was explored in this one-pot cascade reaction (Fig. 3). A variety of alkyl halides such as benzyl bromide (as for 6a), allyl bromide (as for 6b-c), and propargyl bromide (as for 6d), smoothly underwent the sequential process, affording the desired chiral quaternary α-substituted aminophosphonates 6 in good yields (55-88%) with high diastereoselectivities (7:1–>20:1 dr) and excellent enantioselectivities (92-97% ee) (Fig. 3a). This protocol was also compatible with α,β-unsaturated compounds, such as acrylates (as for 6e-f) and acrylamides (as for 6g-h) (Fig. 3b). Notably, the reaction also demonstrated excellent compatibility with acrylates and acrylamides derived from structurally complex alcohols and amines commonly found in natural products and pharmaceuticals76–82, such as Icaridin83, Cholesterol84, and Paroxetine85. The corresponding quaternary α-aminophosphonates 6i-r were obtained in good yields (up to 85%) with high diastereoselectivities (up to >20:1:-:- dr) (Fig. 3c). The absolute configuration of product 6b was determined as (2S,3R) by X-ray analysis. Synthetic utility

To demonstrate the potential synthetic application of this protocol, a gram-scale reaction was performed using aminomethylphosphonate 1a and α,β-unsaturated ketone 2u under standard conditions, producing chiral 1-pyrroline 3u in comparable yield (92%) with excellent diastereo- and enantioselectivities (> 20:1 dr, 98% ee) (Fig. 4a). The synthetic transformations of product 3u or 3a into valuable derivatives with potential bioactivities were shown in Fig. 4. Deprotection of the isopropyl group of 3u with HCl furnished chiral phosphoric acid-substituted 1-pyrroline 7 in 90% yield without any loss of enantiopurity (Fig. 4b). Catalytic hydrogenation of compound 3a using Pd/C as the catalyst under hydrogen atmosphere induced reductive ring-opening, affording chiral α-substituted aminophosphonate 8 in almost quantitative yield with complete retention of enantiopurity86 (Fig. 4c). Treating 1-pyrroline 3a with borane-THF complex gave borane-coordinated polysubstituted pyrrolidine 9 featuring both aryl and phosphate functional groups in 70% yield with >20:1 dr and 95% ee (Fig. 4d).Fig. 4Synthetic applications.a Gram-scale synthesis. b Hydrolysis. c Reductive ring-opening. d Reductive with borane.

Mechanistic studies

To further elucidate the role of pyrrolidine, we evaluated the effects of both its loading and its structural modification. The investigation on pyrrolidine loading revealed that even a catalytic amount of pyrrolidine can activate the α,β-unsaturated ketone to facilitate the conjugate reaction (Fig. 5a). Although the catalytic conditions afforded a lower yield than the stoichiometric use of pyrrolidine, the results indicate the involvement of an iminium organocatalytic cycle. When replacing pyrrolidine with 1-methylpyrrolidine, the reaction yield decreased dramatically (Fig. 5a), strongly suggesting that pyrrolidine operates primarily through an iminium catalysis mechanism rather than acting as a base. The result indicates that the activation of α,β-unsaturated ketone via iminium ion formation facilitates the conjugate addition53–56. To further confirm the proposed iminium-based organocatalytic cycle, we monitored the reaction mixture by high-resolution mass spectrometry (HRMS), which successfully detected a peak corresponding to the key iminium ion intermediate 13 (For details, see SI). Computational studies also support the formation of the iminium intermediate with the assistance of ZnF2 during the catalysis (For details, see SI).Fig. 5Mechanistic studies.a Impact of pyrrolidine. b Proposed reaction mechanism. c Kinetic isotope effect studies. d The calculated pKa.

Therefore, the conjugate addition reaction was proposed to proceed via carbonyl catalysis/iminium double activation pathway as illustrated in Fig. 5b24–30. Chiral pyridoxal 4e undergoes condensation with aminomethylphosphonate 1a to afford the imine 10. Deprotonation of the α C−H bonds in 10 generates the delocalization-stabilized carbanion intermediate 1148,52,87–94. This carbanion then participates in an asymmetric 1,4-conjugate addition to the highly electrophilic α,β-unsaturated iminium species 13, which is formed in situ from the condensation of 2a and pyrrolidine (12). This addition step followed by hydrolysis affords the α-substituted α-aminophosphonate 15 and concurrently regenerates the pyridoxal catalyst 4e and pyrrolidine, thereby completing both the carbonyl and iminium catalytic cycles. Finally, compound 15 undergoes an intramolecular cyclization to afford the target product 3a.

In order to elucidate the observed stereochemical outcome, the transition state 14 was proposed for the step of asymmetric conjugate addition of carbanion 11 to iminium intermediate 13. As shown in the transition state 14 (Fig. 5b), the pyridoxal moiety converts aminomethylphosphonate 1a into an active carbanion 11 via the formation of an imine. The amide side chain is oriented beneath the pyridine ring, functioning as a rigid and bulky group that sterically blocks the bottom side of the pyridine ring. As a result, the iminium intermediate 13 approaches the carbanion preferentially from the upper side of the pyridine ring to avoid the steric repulsion with the amide side chain, leading to the formation of chiral product 3 with (2R,3R) configuration from (R)-4e.

To identify the rate-determining step, kinetic isotope effect (KIE) studies were conducted using equimolar amounts of aminomethylphosphonate 1a and the deuterated analog 1a-d (Fig. 5c). The ratio of products 3a and deuterated 3a-d was determined as 5.3:1, suggesting that deprotonation of imine 10 to generate the active carbanion intermediate 11 is likely the rate-determining step for the transformation. This is consistent with the low acidity of the α-C–H bond of aminomethylphosphonate 1a. DFT calculations estimate that the pKa value is approximately 38.4 (Fig. 5d) (see SI for details), indicating that the aldehyde plays a critical role in facilitating the reaction. Further computational studies reveal that upon imine formation between aminomethylphosphonate 1a and the chiral pyridoxal 4e, the acidity of the α C–H bonds increases dramatically by up to 1022 times (pKa ~ 16.3 of 10, see SI for details) (Fig. 5d). This marked enhancement in acidity enables α C–H deprotonation under standard reaction conditions. These results confirm the remarkable activation capability of the pyridoxal catalyst 4e towards the α C–H bonds of aminomethylphosphonate.

Discussion

In summary, we have accomplished the direct α-C conjugate addition of aminomethylphosphonate to α,β-unsaturated ketones by applying a carbonyl/iminium double activation strategy. In the transformation, pyridoxal activates the aminomethylphosphonate by generating an active amino anion equivalent, while pyrrolidine activates the α,β-unsaturated ketone via iminium formation to generate a more reactive electrophile. This approach bypasses competitive side reactions, providing efficient access to valuable chiral phosphonic 1-pyrrolines bearing two contiguous stereocenters (up to 99% yield, >20:1 dr, 99% ee). Moreover, this method can be extended to a one-pot sequential synthesis of chiral α-quaternary phosphonic 1-pyrroline derivatives, offering a robust and versatile platform for the synthesis of biologically relevant α-aminophosphonate derivatives. This study not only demonstrates a notable example of organo-organo double activation but also advances the field of vitamin B6-inspired asymmetric biomimetic catalysis53–56,95–97.

Methods

General procedure for asymmetric α-C conjugate addition (Fig. 2)

To a 10 mL vial were successively added α,β-unsaturated ketone 2 (0.10 mmol), K2CO3 (0.0249 g, 0.18 mmol), and ZnF2 (0.0031 g, 0.03 mmol). A solution of (R)-4e (0.0048 g, 0.01 mmol), aminomethylphosphonate 1a (0.0234 g, 0.12 mmol), and pyrrolidine (0.0071 g, 0.10 mmol) in MTBE (0.15 mL) was then added, followed by the addition of H2O (0.03 mL). After stirring at 70 °C for 48 h, saturated ammonium chloride (NH4Cl) solution (3 mL) was added to quench the reaction and diluted with 5 mL DCM. The resulting mixture was extracted with DCM (15 mL × 3). The combined organic layers were dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, filtered, concentrated, and purified via column chromatography on silica gel to give the desired product 3. For some specific substrates, the reaction conditions were slightly modified; the details are provided in the Supplementary Information.

General procedure for asymmetric one-pot sequential synthesis (Fig. 3)

To a 10 mL vial were successively added α,β-unsaturated ketone 2a (0.0208 g, 0.10 mmol), K2CO3 (0.0249 g, 0.18 mmol), and ZnF2 (0.0031 g, 0.03 mmol). A solution of (R)-4e (0.0048 g, 0.01 mmol), aminomethylphosphonate 1a (0.0234 g, 0.12 mmol) and pyrrolidine (0.0071 g, 0.10 mmol) in MTBE (0.15 mL) was then added, followed by addition of H2O (0.03 mL). After stirring at 70 °C for 48 h, the reaction mixture was cooled to room temperature, followed by the addition of electrophile 5 (0.20 mmol), CsOH·H₂O (0.2687 g, 1.60 mmol), and THF (0.4 mL). The resulting mixture was stirred at room temperature for 30 min until the full conversion of the compound 3a by TLC analysis. Saturated ammonium chloride (NH4Cl) solution (5 mL) was added to quench the reaction. The resulting mixture was extracted with DCM (15 mL × 3). The combined organic layers were dried over anhydrous Na2SO4, filtered, concentrated, and purified via column chromatography on silica gel to give the desired product 6. For some specific substrates, the reaction conditions were slightly changed, the detailed information of which can be found in the Supplementary Information.
