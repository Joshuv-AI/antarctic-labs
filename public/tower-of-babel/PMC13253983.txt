The Subjective Assessment Paradox in Health Literacy: Implications for Intervention Design in Primary Care Settings
Authors: Kong Qingfei, Xiao Wen
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

My colleagues and I read with great interest the insightful mixed‐methods study by Rambure et al., titled “Determinants of Health Literacy‐Tailored Communication Practices Adoption in French Community Pharmacies,” published in Health Science Reports. As practitioners deeply involved in primary care and chronic disease management, we commend the authors for rigorously investigating a critical yet underexplored facet of patient‐centered care. The study's application of the theoretical domains framework (TDF) to unpack the determinants of community pharmacy workers' (CPWs) behavior provides a nuanced and actionable map of the challenges at hand. We wish to elaborate on a central paradox illuminated by the findings and propose a pathway for transformative intervention. The study reveals a profound disconnect that resonates with our clinical experience: while 84% of CPW respondents initially lacked awareness of the health literacy (HL) concept, most claimed to informally assess patient HL and adapt communication after a brief introduction [1]. However, this assessment was overwhelmingly described as subjective and unstructured, based on intuition rather than validated tools or systematic protocols. This “subjective assessment paradox” is the study's most crucial finding. It underscores that the primary barrier is not a lack of willingness or recognition of importance post‐hoc, but rather the absence of structured, efficient, and integrable methods to translate awareness into consistent, high‐quality action within the constraints of real‐world practice. The TDF‐based analysis powerfully corroborates this by identifying key determinants that are inherently systemic rather than purely individual. The barriers of “environmental context and resources” (notably lack of time, staff, specific remuneration, and dedicated tools) and “behavioral regulation” (non‐habitual, non‐routine assessment influenced by time of day) point directly to the failure of the current work system to support HL‐sensitive care. CPWs are asked to perform a complex cognitive and communicative task—assessing an invisible trait and dynamically adjusting communication—relying solely on memory, motivation, and personal skill amidst significant time pressure. This is an unsustainable model for achieving equitable, high‐quality patient education.

Therefore, we argue that solutions focusing primarily on traditional training to increase knowledge or even skills, while necessary, will be insufficient to create large‐scale, lasting change. The study's own conclusion hints at the necessary shift: interventions must move “beyond awareness‐raising” to modify the practice environment and workflows. We propose that future intervention development must explicitly target the integration of HL assessment and communication support into the very fabric of the pharmacy's operational system. Concretely, we envision and support the development of multi‐component interventions that include: workflow‐embedded digital prompts: Leveraging the existing pharmacy dispensing software to integrate brief, structured prompts or checklists. For instance, a pop‐up reminder for new or complex medications could suggest using the “Teach‐back” method or prompt a quick assessment using an embedded ultra‐brief screening tool (e.g., a single‐item question) [2]. This addresses “memory, attention and decision processes” and helps form new habits (“behavioral regulation”). Standardized, quick‐reference tools co‐designed with end‐users: Developing a suite of simple, visual aids (e.g., infographics on common conditions, medication pictograms) and scripted communication guides for frequent scenarios. These tools must be co‐designed with CPWs and patients to ensure they are truly time‐efficient, relevant, and acceptable, thus mitigating the “resources” barrier. Re‐engineered physical and temporal space: Advocating for and testing models that protect dedicated, uninterrupted consultation time, possibly linked to specific service reimbursements. This directly tackles the “environmental context” barrier of time pressure and inadequate remuneration. Fostering a supportive social and professional environment: Creating “HL champion” roles within pharmacy teams and facilitating peer‐learning networks can strengthen “social influences” and “professional role” identity, providing ongoing motivation and support beyond initial training. In conclusion, Rambure et al.'s study successfully diagnoses the patient: the HL practice gap stems from a system that relies on professional intuition instead of supporting systematic practice. The prescription must be system redesign. We urge researchers, policymakers, and healthcare leaders to invest in developing and rigorously evaluating such embedded, socio‐technical interventions. The goal should be to make health‐literate communication the default, easy choice for every CPW in every patient interaction, thereby moving from subjective guesswork to supported, standardized excellence in patient education.
