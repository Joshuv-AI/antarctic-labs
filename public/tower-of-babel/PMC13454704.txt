Acute progression of extrinsic outflow graft obstruction associated with interval relocation of pliable biodebris in a patient with left ventricular assist device
Authors: Shotaro Komeyama, Tasuku Hada, Tatsuya Nishii, Yasumasa Tsukamoto, Cristiano Spadaccio, So Hirata
Published: 2026

Case description

Extrinsic outflow graft obstruction (eOGO) is a life-threatening left ventricular assist device (LVAD) complication typically caused by biodebris accumulation between the bend relief (BR) and the outflow graft.1,2 However, the acute progression mechanism remains unclear. We report a case of acute eOGO secondary to interval relocation of the stenotic segment within the BR.

A 64-year-old man, 4 years post-HeartMate II (Abbott) implantation, presented for routine follow-up. He was asymptomatic with stable vital signs [blood pressure (BP) 80/66 mmHg, SpO2 99%]. LVAD parameters (flow 3.8 L/min, power 5.0 W; no low-flow alarm) and right heart catheterization [cardiac index (CI) 2.79 L/min/m2, pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP) 8 mmHg] remained at his baseline values. Laboratory data showed a brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) level of 98.7 pg/mL and no haemolysis [lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) 356 U/L]. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CCT) revealed 50% stenosis at the proximal BR (Figure 1A–F).

One month later, he developed progressive dyspnoea on exertion, despite no interval changes in body weight or aortic insufficiency severity. Vital signs (BP 87/75 mmHg, SpO2 99%) and LVAD parameters (flow 3.6 L/min, power 4.7 W) remained relatively stable. Laboratory data revealed no haemolysis (LDH 286 U/L); however, BNP was significantly elevated to 806.0 pg/mL. Repeat CCT revealed the proximal stenosis had regressed, with new >90% stenosis identified at the distal BR (Figure 1G–L). Emergent surgery confirmed gelatinous biodebris, successfully removed via suction debridement with immediate symptom resolution.

These findings highlight the interval relocation of the stenotic segment within the BR, whereby an initially compensated 50% proximal stenosis progressed to severe distal obstruction. The biodebris was gelatinous and easily removed by suction, indicating high deformability.2 We hypothesize that within the confined space of the BR, factors such as haemodynamic changes or graft deformation triggered the relocation of this pliable biodebris, leading to focal entrapment and critical obstruction (alternative mechanisms such as progressive external compression cannot explain the proximal regression). This limited cardiac output augmentation under exertion, causing abrupt clinical deterioration. While moderate eOGO may have minimal resting impact,3 this case further demonstrates that such pliable biodebris can precipitate life-threatening occlusion. Clinicians should recognize the pliable nature of biodebris; even with stable pump parameters, significant BNP elevation should be considered a warning sign for flow limitation potentially caused by biodebris relocation.
