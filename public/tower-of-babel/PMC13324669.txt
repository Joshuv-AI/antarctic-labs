Condition-specific neural signatures of reactivation during post-retrieval rest: An EEG study
Authors: Luz Bavassi, Germán Campos-Arteaga, Ismael Palacios-García, Mario Villena-Gonzalez, Libertad Campassi, Emiliano Marachlian, Cecilia Forcato, Eugenio Rodriguez Balboa, Maria E Pedreira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryMemory reactivation mediates memory malleability, enabling memories to be updated or rendered susceptible to interference. Contemporary theories emphasize that reactivation outcomes depend on the degree and quality of neural reinstatement elicited by retrieval cues. Here, we analyzed how reminders with varying degrees of overlap with prior learning shape neural dynamics during the post-retrieval resting state period. Using electroencephalography (EEG), we compared cue (RX) and context (RCTX) reminders, which differ in their sensitivity to interference. Both reminders were associated with reductions in high beta-band power (25–40 Hz). An exploratory analysis of phase-synchronization network organization differentiated the two reminder types; the RCTX reminder, which was less similar to the original learning episode, showed increased frontal betweenness centrality, suggesting a prominent role for frontal regions in information transfer. Together, these findings identify high beta-band activity and network topology as signatures of post-retrieval neural states that differentiate between reminder types.Subject areas: Physiology, Neuroscience, Cognitive neuroscience

Introduction

Memory updating is a crucial aspect of adaptability in both humans and non-human animals, enabling flexible behavior in a constantly changing environment. The presentation of cues that serve as reminders triggers memory retrieval, re-expressing specific aspects of the spatiotemporal neural activity pattern that occurred during the initial experience.1 This reactivation has been proposed to mediate memory malleability by opening a time window during which the original memory trace can be modified.2 Although memory malleability is fundamental to everyday life, the underlying neural mechanisms remain largely unexplored.

Depending on the information embedded in the reminder and the characteristics of the original memory, the reminder’s content can be updated, enhanced3 or weakened.4 It has been proposed that memory fate, in part, depends on the strength of its reactivation and the state of memory.5,6 Moreover, the degree of overlap between the current input and the existing memory is thought to modulate the extent of reactivation.7 Accordingly, the degree of overlap between a reminder and the original learning episode should influence both the strength and the consequences of memory reactivation, particularly in reminder-based paradigms.

Memory reactivation is not confined to a single moment; different neural patterns are observed across multiple timescales during retrieval. Within the first seconds after cue onset, the level of neural reactivation predicts both immediate8 and long-term recall performance.9 Considering that neural reinstatement also occurs during rest periods after encoding, facilitating memory retention,10 it is reasonable to assume that this mechanism extends beyond the retrieval period. Supporting this notion, several studies suggest that spontaneous activity during post-retrieval rest reflects memory updating11 and memory generalization.12 Conversely, presenting new information immediately after a reminder increases long-term errors compared to presenting it before the reminder.13 Furthermore, the impact of interference introduced immediately after incomplete reminders varies with the content of the reminders. Specifically, reminders with moderate overlap with the acquired information tend to render the memory more sensitive to disruption, whereas both very low and very high overlaps result in reduced susceptibility to interference.14,15 Altogether, these findings indicate that brain activity during post-retrieval rest periods following cue presentation may critically influence long-term memory performance.

Brain oscillations play a central role in long-term memory processes, including consolidation, reactivation, and retrieval.16,17 In episodic memory, decreases in low-frequency power, particularly in the alpha/beta range (8−30 Hz), have been associated with successful encoding and retrieval, and are commonly interpreted as a marker of reduced neural noise and enhanced information processing across sensory modalities.18,19,20,21,22 While power decreases are not stimulus-specific, oscillatory phase can convey task- and content-related information: during sensory reinstatement, alpha/beta phase patterns encode stimulus-specific details, often accompanied by concurrent power reductions in the same regions.23,24 Thus, the power and phase of alpha/beta oscillations provide complementary neural signatures of successful memory retrieval.

Like other higher cognitive processes, memory relies on anatomical and functional interactions among distributed brain regions.25 The topology of functional networks reflects the multidimensional nature of episodic memory retrieval: anterior temporal interactions preferentially correlate with item-specific and emotional associations, whereas posterior medial subnetworks represent precise contextual information.26 Moreover, we previously showed that different types of reminders, which differ in their degree of overlap with the original learning episode, imprint distinct long-term connectivity patterns within the global retrieval network. For instance, incomplete reminders have been shown to induce more connected and efficient network configurations than complete reminders.27 Given that specific patterns of phase synchronization underlie different types of memories and memory processes,28,29 the topological organization of phase synchronization networks should reflect the quality and fidelity of episodic memory retrieval. However, because the relationship between reminder overlap and specific network properties remains underspecified, the present study adopts an exploratory, network-level approach to identify which aspects of functional topology differentiate reminder-induced reactivation states.

Building on this background, our working hypothesis is that incomplete reminders, with varying degrees of overlap, give rise to distinct patterns of neural signatures. Some of these signatures might facilitate memory reactivation that is susceptible to interference, whereas others do not elicit sufficient reactivation to permit modification. This study seeks to characterize the neural signatures associated with memory reactivation during post-retrieval resting periods following reminders and to examine their relationship with long-term behavioral performance. To this end, we employed a well-established behavioral paradigm developed in our laboratory. This task follows a three-day protocol during which participants learn five pairs of nonsense syllables, each presented within an enriched multisensory context that includes images and music.14,15,30,31 Using electroencephalography (EEG), we analyzed the resting periods following two types of reminders presented on day 2. The first reminder, the context plus one cue syllable, known as the cue reminder (RX), has been shown to increase susceptibility to interference. In contrast, the second reminder, with only context cues, referred to as the context reminder (RCTX), has shown relative insensitivity to such interference.14,15 Specifically, we assessed the frequency, power, and phase synchronization functional connectivity induced by each reminder and examined their impact on long-term memory performance.

Results

To characterize the neural signatures of memory reactivation associated with long-term memory performance, we implemented a syllable-learning protocol comprising three separate sessions.14,15,30,31 During the training session, participants learned five pairs of nonsense syllables presented in a rich multisensory environment that included music and images. They completed ten trials to encode the syllable pairs. The following day, during the treatment session, participants were reminded of the information learned on the first day. Finally, their memory retention was assessed 24 h later during the testing session (Figure 1A). On day 2, we recorded EEG activity pre- and post-reminder presentation (bl1 and bl2, respectively). We recorded two post-retrieval resting state periods: a 4 min baseline rest before the reminder and a 4 min post-reminder rest to examine any lingering neural effects. Participants were assigned to one of two experimental groups. The first group received the RX reminder, which included contextual information and the cue syllable, and was interrupted before participants could write the response syllable. In contrast, the other group received the RCTX reminder, which consisted solely of the contextual cues from the learning environment. It is important to note that the only difference between the two reminders is the degree of overlap with the information acquired in the training session (the presence/absence of the cue syllable).

Behavioral results

We characterized the behavioral results of the task. The learning rate was similar across groups; participants improved their performance across training trials, reaching near-perfect scores in the final two trials (Figure 1B). During the testing session, we observed high memory retention for both groups. The long-term memory test did not show significant differences between the mean percentage of correct responses for both groups, although the effect approached significance (U = 158, p = 0.06, Mann-Whitney test, Cliff’s δ = 0.3; Figure 1C). Notably, the two groups exhibited different levels of performance variability. Levene’s test confirmed a significant difference in variance between the groups (F[2, 29] = 9.02, p = 0.006), with the RX reminder group showing substantially lower variability. These findings indicate that both reminders elicited comparable levels of memory retention, although the RX group exhibited reduced performance variability.

Spectral power modulations

First, we examined the neural signatures elicited by reminders with different degrees of overlap with the encoded information across experimental groups, focusing on neural markers associated with memory modification. We applied a cluster-based permutation test across spatial and spectral dimensions to compare resting state EEG activity after (bl2) and before (bl1) each reminder presentation (Table 1; Figures 2A and 2B). Surprisingly, the overall spectral patterns observed across the two experimental groups were quite similar, suggesting that both types of reminders produced comparable frequency-power signatures. In both groups, the reminders triggered a significant decrease in high beta power. The group that received the RX reminder exhibited a localized cluster in the left parietal-occipital region between 25 and 45 Hz (p = 0.05, clusterstat = −353.5, cluster-based permutation test, Table 1). On the other hand, the group receiving the RCTX reminder showed a bilateral central cluster, including frequencies from 20 to 45 Hz (p = 0.02, clusterstat = −565.7, cluster-based permutation test, Table 1). Given that alpha/beta power reductions have been associated with information processing and successful subsequent retrieval, the observed effects of both reminders might be consistent with memory reactivation.

Moreover, the RX reminder produced a parietal beta power decrement, whereas the RCTX reminder induced a beta power reduction over central regions. The largely non-overlapping topographical distributions of the clusters, both clusters shared only three channels (less than 10%), suggest that the two reminders may engage distinct brain regions, likely related to different aspects of the previously learned declarative memory.

To further explore the relationship between beta power reduction and long-term memory performance, we divided each group by percentage of correct responses in the testing session (low vs. high performers; Figure S1). We evaluated the magnitude of the reduction in beta power for each significant cluster (Figures 2C and 2D). We conducted a two-way ANOVA with rate (low, high) and condition (RX, RCTX) as between-participant factors and beta power as the dependent variable. The analysis revealed no significant main effect of rate (F[1,27] = 3.69, p = 0.065, ηp2 = 0.12), although there was a trend toward greater reduction in the high condition. There was no significant main effect of condition (F[1,27] = 0.27, p = 0.61, ηp2 = 0.010), nor a significant interaction between rate and condition (F[1,27] = 0.064, p = 0.80, ηp2 = 0.0020), indicating that the relationship between rate and power did not differ between RX and RCTX. In summary, both reminders elicited reductions in high beta power, with a trend toward larger decreases in participants with higher subsequent long-term memory performance.

Unexpectedly, the RX reminder also revealed a widespread, significant cluster (7−12 Hz, p = 0.05, clusterstat = 293.8, cluster-based permutation test, Figures S2; Table 1). To relate this finding to behavior, we again split participants in this group according to testing performance (low vs. high) and did not observe differences in alpha power (SF2; U = 20, p = 0.85; Cliff’s δ = −0.091, Mann-Whitney test), likely indicating no clear relationship with long-term memory performance.

Finally, to distinguish between the power patterns induced by both reminders, we performed a cluster-based permutation test, but this time comparing RX and RCTX post-retrieval resting states. No significant clusters were found, suggesting that spectral power captures neural patterns elicited by reminder presentation but is insufficient to differentiate between reminder types.

Phase synchronization networks organization

Our previous results showed that both reminders (RX and RCTX) modulate high-beta power, without differentiating between conditions. Given that these reminders differ in their sensitivity to interference, we next examined differences in the phase organization of the underlying neural dynamics, which may relate to their degree of overlap with the original learning episode. To identify differences between the experimental groups, considering that oscillatory phase conveys complementary information about memory and that functional connectivity plays a central role in memory processes, we examined the global network structure based on phase synchronization within the frequency range identified in the power analysis. Specifically, EEG signals were bandpass filtered between 25 and 40 Hz. We then applied the Hilbert transform and computed the Phase Lag Index (PLI) between all channel pairs,32 generating a 64 × 64 symmetric matrix for each participant (Figure 3A).

To compare the connectivity patterns of networks with the same density, we constructed binary, bidirectional graphs by thresholding each matrix while fixing the number of edges (Figure 3A). Network densities ranged from ρ = 1% to ρ = 10% to ensure sparse network configurations, yielding a set of 364 graphs per participant. To characterize network organization for each condition, we explore a set of commonly used attributes of Graph Theory.33 We calculated four global measures: (1) efficiency, which reflects the network’s capacity for information exchange based on the shortest path length between nodes; (2) assortativity, which captures the tendency of nodes to connect with others of similar degree; (3) the size of the giant component, which quantifies the number of connected nodes and reflects the extent of the networks; and (4) the clustering coefficient, which indicates the degree to which the neighbors of a given node are interconnected. These global properties provide information on network robustness, information exchange, hierarchical organization, and network size. In addition, we examined the spatial organization of the nodes by grouping electrodes into bilateral anterior and posterior regions. Specifically, we divided the scalp into four sections: bilateral frontal and parietal-occipital regions (Figure 3B). We considered these four sections based on an anterior/posterior distinction following Cooper and Ritchey (2019).26 We also differentiate laterally because memory includes syllable information. Nodes were assigned to each section based on a trade-off of EEG sensor localization and their similarity, such that within-section signals were more similar than expected by chance (see Figure S3). For each section, we calculated the mean of two local measures: (1) degree centrality, defined as the number of direct connections a node has and (2) betweenness centrality, which quantifies the extent to which a node lies on the shortest paths between other nodes, thus reflecting its role in mediating information flow.

In total, we computed 12 attributes per network, resulting in 4368 values per participant (12 × 364). Our aim was to identify the attribute or attributes that best distinguish between the two experimental conditions, reflecting differences in their neural fingerprints. To discard redundant information in our subsequent analysis due to variable colinearity, we assessed the variability of each attribute across all networks and calculated its correlation. The variability was defined as the coefficient of variation (σx/x¯; Figure 3C). This parameter revealed that bilateral frontal betweenness centrality exhibited high variability and appeared to be a promising marker for distinguishing between experimental conditions. Next, recognizing that many attributes of graph theory are inherently correlated, we assessed pairwise correlations between features (Figure 3D). Efficiency showed strong correlations with both assortativity and giant component size, while betweenness centrality was highly correlated with degree centrality within each brain region. Based on these results, we selected six attributes for further analysis: efficiency, clustering coefficient, and betweenness centrality (computed for the four brain regions).

Once we obtained the set of six not correlated attributes, we examined the importance of these network measures in differentiating the two experimental conditions using a classifier model.34 The classification was performed across network densities ranging from 1% to 10%. Given the limited data, we employed a random forest classifier, a robust method that does not require data scaling and performs well with small datasets. The performance of each model was evaluated using leave-one-out cross-validation (see STAR Methods for more details). The random forest classifier consisted of 50 shallow trees (maximum depth of 2), each considering only two features at each split, to reduce the risk of overfitting. Figure 4A presents the model’s mean accuracy across all network densities (the model’s accuracy on the training set was ∼0.85). We found that the classifiers performed above chance in 119 of the 364 network densities (33% of the time), particularly for densities of 4%, 6%, and 8% (gray areas in Figure 4A).

We analyzed the relative importance of six attributes in distinguishing between the two experimental conditions. For graph densities in which the classifier performed significantly above chance, we conducted model-based feature selection by computing permutation feature importance using 50 random shuffles. This method assesses the extent to which the model’s performance drops when each feature is randomly permuted. Our analysis revealed that the frontal betweenness measures, both right and left, emerged as the most essential attributes for distinguishing the RX and RCTX networks. Notably, the left frontal betweenness consistently ranked as the top feature across all densities. Additionally, the right frontal centrality was significant in 95% of the well-classified densities (Figure 4B). Overall, bilateral frontal betweenness centrality emerged as the primary feature distinguishing the two reminder conditions. Given that betweenness centrality reflects a node’s role in mediating information flow within a network, the frontal regions play distinct roles in orchestrating global brain communication across conditions.

As a follow-up, we examined the centrality measure in both frontal regions for each participant and across all significant densities (Figures 4C and 4D). We conducted a two-way ANOVA with Side (left and right) and Condition (RX, RCTX) as factors and frontal betweenness as the dependent variable. The analysis revealed a significant main effect of Side, F(1, 7,374) = 87.17, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.012, indicating a small effect. A significant main effect of Condition was also observed, F(1, 7,374) = 643.50, p < 0.001, ηp2 = 0.080. The Side × Condition interaction was statistically significant, F(1, 7,374) = 5.26, p = 0.022; however, the effect size was negligible (ηp2 = 0.001). Accordingly, the RCTX reminder was associated with higher bilateral frontal betweenness centrality than the RX condition in both hemispheres.

Finally, we fitted a generalized linear model (GLM) to assess behavioral performance during the testing session (low vs. high performers) using both frontal betweenness centrality and network link density per reminder. For the RX reminder, the overall model was statistically significant (Pseudo R2 = 0.099; log likelihood = −1,108.4). Among the predictors, frontal left centrality showed a strong positive effect on the probability of the outcome (β = 81.76, z = 10.15, p < 0.001), while density exhibited a significant negative association (β = −9.36, z = −4.26, p < 0.001). In contrast, the frontal right centrality was not a significant predictor (β = −4.16, p = 0.62). The intercept was also significant (β = 0.34, p = 0.006). To evaluate the model’s discriminative capacity, we calculated the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC-ROC). Although it was moderate (AUC-ROC = 0.60), it was above-chance (Figure 4E). Besides, the linear model for the RCTX reminder provided a good overall fit (Pseudo R2 = 0.095; log likelihood = −1224.9). Both frontal centralities showed significant negative effects on the probability of the outcome (β = −68.72, z = −12.31, p < 0.001; and β = −13.25, z = −3.36, p = 0.001, right and left, respectively). In contrast, density had a significant positive effect (β = 14.24, z = 6.12, p < 0.001). The intercept was also statistically significant (β = 0.28, p = 0.018). Figure 4F shows the AUC-ROC, which exceeds chance, indicating reliable performance in distinguishing test performance. Together, these results indicate that frontal subnetwork centrality and connectivity density differentiate subsequent testing performance across conditions, but in opposite patterns across reminders.

Discussion

In this work, by combining spectral and network-level analyses, we show that reminders with different degrees of overlap with the acquired information exhibit distinct post-retrieval neural patterns. We found that both reminders elicited reductions in high beta power (25−40 Hz), with a tendency toward stronger reductions among participants with high long-term performance in the testing session. Importantly, an exploratory analysis revealed that the two reminders differed primarily in the organization of the phase-synchronization networks, with bilateral frontal regions playing distinct roles across conditions.

Memory reactivation can induce significant changes in stored information. A recent model, the Nonmonotonic Plasticity,35 offers a compelling explanation for how varying degrees of memory reactivation can lead to distinct memory outcomes, including integration, no change, or differentiation of memory traces. This model provides a valuable perspective on the differential effects observed following the presentation of cue and context reminders, which show distinct sensitivity to interference.14,15 We assume that memory retrieval triggers reactivation of the original memory trace through a diffusive propagation of activation.6 Given that the degree of overlap between current input and prior memory modulates reactivation strength,7 both reminder types likely initiated memory retrieval by reactivating the original trace, albeit to different extents. Importantly, although the RX reminder is more similar to the original learning episode than the RCTX reminder, stimulus similarity alone is unlikely to fully account for the behavioral and neural differences observed. A critical aspect of our paradigm is that the RX reminder implicitly encourages participants to complete the learned association upon reading the cue syllable. This internal retrieval operation is likely to lead to greater reactivation. On the other hand, the RCTX reminder does not prompt this demand, leading to weaker reactivation. According to the nonmonotonic plasticity framework, these differences in reactivation strength may help explain why the RX reminder is more sensitive to interference than the context reminder. Together, our results indicate that, beyond stimulus similarity, task demands and the cognitive operations they engage play a pivotal role in shaping reactivation strength and subsequent memory modification.

While our results showed subtle differences in behavioral performance, previous studies using this paradigm have demonstrated distinct outcomes following interference. Given that beta’s phase carries stimulus-specific memory, we explored phase-synchronization network connectivity to further differentiate between the two types of retrieval. This analysis revealed that the frontal betweenness centrality is a key attribute in differentiating the two conditions. Specifically, the RCTX reminder group presented higher frontal betweenness centrality than the RX reminder group. In other words, the RCTX reminder induces a network in which the left and right frontal regions play pivotal roles, serving as hubs for information transfer throughout the brain. Compared to RX, this reminder provides fewer retrieval cues directly linked to the original learning, which may limit direct access to the original memory trace. Retrieval in the RCTX group could therefore rely more strongly on internally driven search processes, potentially engaging prefrontal mechanisms to a greater extent. Neuroimaging studies have indeed indicated that activation of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex increases when memory strength is lower or when cues are insufficient, reflecting the need for controlled retrieval and attentional support.36,37 Furthermore, Kuhl et al. (2011) demonstrated that memory strength influences prefrontal cortex activation during retrieval, emphasizing that a reduced overlap between cues and memories correlates with greater retrieval effort.38 Importantly, behavioral performance showed limited variability (near-ceiling), which may have reduced sensitivity to detect robust neural-behavior relationships. Therefore, the associations between network properties and performance should be interpreted with caution.

The relationship between subsequent memory performance and frontal centrality suggests that the two reminders engage distinct post-retrieval mechanisms, likely reflecting their differential sensitivity to interference. In the RX condition, better performance was associated with greater frontal centrality, whereas in the RCTX condition, the opposite pattern was observed. This dissociation is consistent with prior work showing that similar retrieval structures can give rise to distinct neural patterns supporting memory updating, as demonstrated by the well-established testing effect, in which retrieval practice, relative to restudy, enhances learning and promotes memory updating.39 Moreover, memory updating appears to rely on distinct neural representations depending on the retrieval condition: while restudy-related improvements have been linked to ventral temporal regions, retrieval practice engages medial prefrontal cortex.3 Within this framework, the RX condition may be interpreted as a retrieval-practice-like process, and the positive association between frontal centrality and subsequent performance is consistent with the involvement of prefrontal networks in memory updating and consolidation.5 On the other hand, although the RCTX reminder is characterized by higher overall frontal centrality at the group level, better performance within this condition is associated with lower centrality. This pattern suggests that, in RCTX, excessive reliance on a frontal hub-like organization may be suboptimal, and that performance is better captured by network configurations with reduced frontal centrality, particularly at higher network densities. One possible explanation is that, because the RCTX reminder lacks the cue syllable, it may not strongly engage retrieval practice mechanisms or the completion of the learned association, thereby favoring a more context-driven mode of processing.

Hanslmayr and colleagues40 proposed that the reductions in alpha/beta power reflect memory reactivation. The authors argue that decreases in alpha/beta power serve as a proxy for increased neuronal firing, thereby facilitating the brain’s ability to represent information and reducing noise.18 In this sense, presenting both reminders was associated with a significant decrease in high beta power (25–40 Hz), which may reflect memory reactivation processes. Our findings reflected a tendency toward greater reductions in beta power, associated with improved long-term accuracy, with both reminders. This phenomenon has been reported during encoding, where decreases in alpha and beta power correlate with the number of items successfully retrieved.41

Interestingly, the reminders triggered clusters of significant decreases in high beta power, which were differently localized across the brain. The RX reminder, which received the syllable plus the context information, presented a left parietal-occipital cluster, while the RCTX reminder (the one that only includes the context information) exhibited a bilateral central cluster. Recent evidence suggests that beta-band power reductions during memory retrieval are not uniform but instead reflect the modality and content of the memory. Griffiths et al.20 demonstrated that alpha/beta power decreases during successful retrieval of visual and auditory information are localized in modality-specific sensory areas: occipital cortex for visual memories and parietal/temporal cortices for auditory memories. These results suggest that memory retrieval involves reinstatement of activity in regions initially engaged during encoding. Supporting this view, a previous MEG study using crossmodal associative memory paradigms found that retrieval of an associated item (e.g., retrieving a visual image from an auditory cue) activated the sensory-specific cortex corresponding to the retrieved (not the presented) stimulus.21,42 Together, these findings suggest that beta desynchronization accompanies cortical reinstatement and is related to the fidelity of retrieved sensory content. Our task involved multisensory information, comprising semantic, visual, and auditory information. In this sense, we proposed that the two reminders trigger distinct sensory aspects of the encoded memory. It is possible that the RX group primarily reactivates visual information containing semantic content due to left lateralization. In contrast, the RCTX reminder induces a power reduction in auditory cortex regions.

An alternative interpretation of our findings is that reminders on day 2 may act as retro-cues, directing attention to internal representations rather than triggering memory reactivation.43 Previous research indicates that retro-cues focus attention on stored auditory or visual information, accompanied by changes in beta-band activity across fronto-temporal and parietal networks.43,44,45 This suggests that the observed decreases in high beta power may reflect attentional prioritization of internal representations. Despite their apparent exclusivity, retro-cue-driven attention and memory reactivation could work together, with focused attention facilitating reactivation when partial cues prompt retrieval. In this framework, beta band dynamics may reflect top-down control mechanisms that facilitate access to stored information, extending beyond cue presentation into the post-retrieval resting period. Lastly, an unresolved question is whether the extent of overlap between the reminder and the original learning episode influences the effectiveness of the retro-cue.

In contrast to previous studies that propose that decreases in alpha power during the perception of sequential stimuli index improved memory performance,20,46 this report found that the RX group exhibited a significant, widespread increase in alpha power. It is essential to note that the RCTX reminder group also showed, although not statistically significant, an increase in alpha power. Alpha activity was initially interpreted as a sign of cortical inactivity.47 However, later studies demonstrated that alpha oscillations are associated with attention by inhibiting processing in task-irrelevant areas, thereby enhancing selective attention.48 Consistent with these findings, because our paradigm did not explicitly direct participants to focus on any step of the protocol, we speculate that the RX group focuses on completing the response syllable following the presentation of the cue syllable. This expectation is lower in the RCTX group because there is no such information.

Many memory paradigms focus on reactivation during encoding and retrieval. In this work, we propose a novel approach, investigating the post-retrieval rest period. In encoding, the processes unfolding during awake periods enhance memory performance. The interruption of the endogenous reactivation during the post-encoding resting periods impaired memory retention.49 Additionally, during this offline period, reactivation of successfully recalled objects is greater than that of forgotten ones.50 The spontaneous activity post-retrieval modulated the subsequent outcome, triggering fear memory updating11 and episodic memory generalization.12 Interestingly, our paradigm reveals that high beta (25−40 Hz) power and phase-synchronization act as neural signatures of memory reactivation during the resting period after both reminder presentations, indicating that reactivation extended beyond stimulus presentation.

All in all, our study characterizes condition-specific neural dynamics of memory reactivation during the post-retrieval resting state. This approach contrasts with previous studies, which have primarily focused on the moment of cue presentation.51,52 We found that both RX and RCTX reminders reduced beta power in parieto-occipital regions, a pattern associated with reactivation and updating of internal representations. Notably, sparse phase-synchronization networks in the high beta band were effective at distinguishing between the two types of reminders, indicating that the brain activates distinct wiring patterns in response to retrieval reactivation to varying degrees. Crucially, the extent to which frontal nodes functioned as hubs, facilitating communication across the network, may reflect long-term behavioral outcomes. These findings underscore the importance of early network topology in influencing memory persistence, emphasizing that frontal regions play a vital role not only in retrieval and reactivation but also in the effective integration and dissemination of mnemonic information. Collectively, our results contribute to the growing body of evidence linking transient neural dynamics beyond the stimulus presentation to subsequent memory performance, highlighting how transient network configurations during offline periods may bias the long-term stability of mnemonic representations. Despite the exploratory nature of some aspects of this study, we identify condition-specific neural configurations underlying post-retrieval states, representing a meaningful step toward understanding how memory is shaped after retrieval and opening promising avenues for future research.

Limitations of the study

Several limitations of the present study should be acknowledged. First, the behavioral protocol was designed to ensure robust learning and memory reactivation, but it does not allow for a fine-grained assessment of memory accuracy. The relatively small number of syllable pairs and the high level of training led to near-ceiling performance during the testing session, limiting our ability to detect subtle differences in memory precision or graded changes in performance. Second, although previous work using this paradigm has demonstrated differential sensitivity to interference across reminder types, the present study did not directly assess interference effects. This is because neural activity was recorded during resting-state periods following reminder presentation, precluding the inclusion of an explicit interference manipulation. As a result, sensitivity to interference could only be inferred indirectly from neural signatures rather than measured behaviorally. Future studies combining richer behavioral measures with online or post-reminder manipulations will be necessary to establish a more direct link between neural states induced by reminders and memory malleability. Finally, an additional limitation is that we did not collect ancestry, race, and ethnicity information as part of the original study design. Furthermore, although sex was recorded, the study was not designed or statistically powered to evaluate sex-related differences or interactions with the reminder condition. Therefore, the generalizability of the present findings across demographic groups should be interpreted with caution.

Experimental model and study participant details

Human participants

Thirty-three healthy undergraduate students from Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile volunteered for the study (19 women). Their ages ranged from 18 to 35 years, and they were native Spanish speakers. Information regarding ancestry, race, and ethnicity was not collected as part of the original study design. Sex and basic clinical information were recorded (see Table S1); however, sex-related effects were not explicitly evaluated because the study was not designed or statistically powered to test differences as a function of sex or interactions between sex and reminder condition.

All participants had to achieve at least 60% correct responses in the last four trials of the training session. Data from two participants were excluded from analysis because they either did not complete the entire experiment or had excessively noisy EEG signals.

Before their participation, all participants signed a written informed consent form. Both the protocol and the consent form were reviewed and approved by the Comité Ético Científico de la Facultad de Medicina, Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile (CEC MED UC), approval/project number 16-032, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Method details

Task and stimuli

We adapted the experiment used in Forcato et al.53 The experiment consisted of three sessions separated by 24 hours, Figure 1A. On day 1, participants learned a list of five pairs of nonsense syllables associated with a context (Training). The session on day 2 consisted of presenting reminders of the learned list and recording the neurophysiological data with an electroencephalogram (EEG). On day 3, we assessed the participants’ memory (Testing).

The participants were divided into two groups based on the type of reminders they received on day 2.

Each trial consisted of presenting an image, playing classical music, and presenting the five pairs of syllables. In this sense, the list of syllables was presented in an enriched context (image and music).

The protocol was run on a standard desktop computer using Psychtoolbox software.

Training session

Each trial began with a black screen (3 seconds), followed by the context period, which included the presentation of an autumn forest image accompanied by Vivaldi’s music (5 seconds), and then the presentation of the list of syllables. The syllable period started with the presentation of a cue-syllable on the left-hand side of the screen and an empty box on the right side. Each cue-syllable was taken randomly from the list of five pairs. Participants had 5 s to write the corresponding response syllable. After this, if no syllable was written, the correct one was shown for 5 s; if the response syllable was wrong, it was replaced by the correct one for 5 s; else, if the proper response was given, it stayed for 5 s longer. Immediately after that, another cue-syllable was shown, and the process was repeated until the list was over. Altogether, each trial lasted 58 s (3 s black screen, 5 s for context period, and 50 s for syllable presentation).

The training consisted of 10 trials. In each trial, we shuffled the presentation order of the pairs of syllables. In all the trials, participants were asked to write down the corresponding response syllable for each cue syllable presented. The list of five pairs of nonsense syllables was: ITE-OBN, ASP-UOD, FLI-AIO, NEB-FOT, COS-GLE (bold type: cue-syllable; italic type: response-syllable).

Reminder session

Participants came to the same room used in the Training session. They sat in a chair in front of the computer, and we recorded their brain activity during a 4-minute resting period (bl1). Then, they received a reminder of the encoded material. Finally, we recorded the EEG signal during a 4-minute post-treatment resting period (bl2).

Participants were divided into two groups based on the reminder received. The RX group received the cue reminder (15 participants), while the RCTX group saw the context reminder (16 participants).

•Cue reminder (RX group)

Cue reminder (RX group)

It consisted of presenting the context period (5 s, image + sound), followed by only one of the cue-syllables on the left-hand side of the monitor screen and the box on the right. After 5 seconds, the trial was interrupted, and an error message (’ERROR 323’) appeared on the screen.

•Context reminder (RCTX group)

Context reminder (RCTX group)

It consisted of presenting the context period for 5 seconds (image + sound). The trial was then interrupted, and an error message (‘ERROR 323’) appeared on the screen.

The error message was used to make the interruption appear unexpected and unrelated to the experimental manipulation. After the message, participants were informed that the experimental software had crashed and that the EEG recording would continue, followed by the session scheduled for the next day.

Testing session

The testing session consisted of 4 trials identical to the one presented during the Training session (each trial: 3 s black screen, 5 s for context period, and 50 s for syllable presentation). The testing session lasted 232 s.

Electrophysiological (EEG) recording and analysis

EEG activity was recorded on a dedicated PC at a sampling frequency of 2048 Hz, with 64 Ag-AgCl active electrodes - mounted in an elastic cap according to the extended International 10–20 System, using the Biosemi Active-Two system (Biosemi, Amsterdam, the Netherlands, http://www.biosemi.com/products.htm). Two extra electrodes were placed at both mastoids. During the recording, impedances were kept below 20kΩ.

The preprocessing of the EEG recordings followed the pipeline used in.54 After acquisition, the data were imported into MATLAB using the EEGLAB toolbox.55 First, they were digitally downsampled to 512 Hz using a fifth-order sinc filter to prevent aliasing, and referenced to the mean of the mastoid signal. The data were then bandpass filtered between 0.1 and 100 Hz using high-pass and low-pass filters, respectively. For this, we used the eegfiltnew function implemented in EEGLAB, which filters the signal forward and backward to achieve a zero-phase (flat frequency) response. Line noise, at 50 Hz, was removed by subtracting the evoked potential of the 50Hz signal from the original signal. To discard artifacts related to eye movements, we applied a global signal decomposition technique known as Independent Component Analysis (ICA), which is a source separation method that decomposes the EEG time series into a set of components aimed at identifying independent sources of variance in the data.55 From the original 64 components, a subset of 1.9 ± 0.9 was discarded. Finally, we applied a spherical channel interpolation at most 3 channels per participant after visual inspection.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Behavioral data

We computed as a correct response every time a participant wrote down the correct response syllable per trial. To analyze long-term memory assessment, we calculated the mean of the correct responses throughout the four trials.

To compare the rate of correct responses of the two groups, we performed a Mann-Whitney test. To compare the variance between the groups, we used a Levene’s test.

To explore the relationship between long-term behavioral performance and neural markers, we divided each experimental group into two, based on the level of correct responses in the Testing session. We defined the low and high rate performers depending on whether their number of correct responses was below or above the median of all members in each experimental group (see Figure S1 for participants distribution).

Frequency decomposition and statistical analysis

After the data were preprocessed, we transformed all the neurophysiological time series into the time-frequency domain. For this analysis, we used the FieldTrip toolbox for MATLAB.56 Specifically, we applied the Wavelet method, with 7 cycles, spanning the frequency range of 2 to 100 Hz. Power was calculated with moving steps of 0.5 s. Finally, the transformed data were averaged over the time dimension.

To study the modulation of the power oscillatory brain activity, we compared the activity before (bl1) and after (bl2) the presentation of the reminder, for each experimental group. A cluster-based statistical test with permutation correction was applied to address the multiple comparisons problem.57 This non-parametric test detects clusters of adjacent samples (channel, frequency) that show a statistically significant difference between bl1 and bl2. Clusters consisted of at least three neighboring channels and were considered significant within specific frequency bands. p-values were estimated using the Monte Carlo method, based on 500 random permutations (shuffling data between the two blocks), and the observed cluster statistics were compared against this distribution (the critical alpha level was set to 0.05). Through this method, we identified frequency bands and electrode locations that simultaneously exhibited statistically significant activity.

To examine whether beta power reduction predicts long-term memory performance, we analyzed the mean beta power within the significant cluster as a function of performance and experimental condition. We conducted a two-way ANOVA with performance (Low, High) and condition (RX, RCTX) as between-participant factors, and beta power as the dependent variable.

Finally, we compared the spectral power induced by the two reminder conditions by repeating the cluster-based permutation test using an independent-samples approach. Before the statistical analysis, bl2 was normalized relative to bl1.

Classification analysis and feature importance

For further analysis, we used Scikit-Learn, a library for machine learning in Python.60

First, we applied a classification algorithm to distinguish between the networks and the RX and RCTX reminder groups based on the local and global attributes that presented a higher level of variability and low correlation between them (efficiency, clustering, and the four betweenness centralities). Considering the low amount of data, we only had 31 networks per density, we performed a Random Forest Classifier, which is a robust algorithm that does not need data scaling, and works well with a small amount of data. The Random Forest classifier consisted of 50 shallow trees (maximum depth = 2), with only two features considered at each split, to minimize overfitting. To assess the classifier’s performance, we employed Leave-One-Out Cross-Validation (LOOCV). We computed two evaluation metrics: macro-averaged F1 score and overall accuracy. Both training and test scores were recorded across all LOOCV folds. Final performance (Figure 4A) was reported as the average and standard error of the cross-validated scores across all participants. This allowed us to estimate the model’s generalization performance while ensuring robustness to data imbalance.

Then, to capture the most relevant network’s measure in distinguishing between both groups, in the densities where the previous classifier was above chance, we applied a Random Forest-based feature selection. We evaluated feature importance using a permutation-based approach. The data were randomly split into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%), and the Random Forest model was fitted to the training data. Feature importance was then assessed using the permutation importance method with 50 random shuffles per feature, computed on the held-out test set. For each feature, we stored both the mean importance and its standard error. In parallel, for each density, we performed automatic feature selection using a model-based method. The trained Random Forest model provides internal estimates of feature importance based on the Gini impurity reduction associated with each split. We used scikit-learn’s SelectFromModel function, which selects features whose importance scores exceed a predefined threshold (by default, the mean importance across all features). The selected features, frontal right and left betweenness, presented scores above the threshold in 95% of the densities.

Frontal betweenness centrality was computed for the left and right frontal regions for each participant across all significant network densities. To assess the effects of hemisphere and experimental condition on frontal betweenness centrality, we performed a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Side (left, right) and Condition (RX, RCTX) as fixed factors and frontal betweenness centrality as the dependent variable. All the observations were treated as independent.

Finally, we aimed to identify Low vs. High performance during the Testing session based on bilateral frontal centrality. For each group, we applied a generalized linear model (GLM) with a binomial distribution and a logit link function to assess the contributions of left and right frontal centrality and connectivity density in predicting the binary outcome variable (Low/High). The analysis was conducted in Python using the statsmodels package. A constant term was included in the model to estimate the intercept, and parameters were optimized using the Iteratively Reweighted Least Squares (IRLS) method. To evaluate the models’ discriminative capacity, we calculated the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for each model. Then, we analyzed whether the observed model performance was above chance level; therefore, we generated a null distribution of AUC values using a randomization procedure. We randomly permuted 1000 times the outcome variable (Low/High), and for each iteration, a logistic GLM with the same predictors was refitted using the randomized labels.
