A Benchmark for Strategic Auditee Gaming Under Continuous Compliance Monitoring Thanks: We thank colleagues at the University of Bath for feedback on earlier drafts. The authors declare no competing interests.
Florian A. D. Burnat, Brittany I. Davidson

Abstract
Continuous post-deployment compliance audits, mandated by emerging regulations such as the EU AI Act and Digital Services Act, create a class of strategic gaming distinct from the one-shot input/output gaming studied in prior work. Regulated systems can delay outcome reporting, drift their reports within plausible noise envelopes, exploit longitudinal sample attrition, and cherry-pick among ambiguous metric definitions. We formalize continuous auditing as a T T -round Stackelberg game between an auditor that commits to a temporal policy and an adaptive auditee, and identify a structural feature of any noise-aware static-auditor design: a cover regime in which coverage gaps and granularity gaps cannot be closed simultaneously. We make this formal as Observation 1 and show that two minimal extension policies, each derived from the observation, close the regime along orthogonal axes: a sample-size-aware static rule (Periodic-with-floor) closes the granularity-failure case, while a history-conditioned suspicion-escalation policy closes the coverage-failure case for the naive Drift strategy — and neither closes both, exactly as the observation predicts; an audit-aware OffAuditDrift strategy that exploits Stackelberg commitment defeats both. To support empirical study we contribute a non-additive harm decomposition (welfare loss W W , coverage loss C C ) that exposes how attrition shifts harm from the regulator-accountable surface to a regulator-invisible one; an initial library of five auditee strategies (Delay, Drift, Cherry-pick, Attrition, OffAuditDrift) and five auditor policies, calibrated to summary statistics from published audits of the DSA Transparency Database; and a reproducible simulator with a small, extensible Python interface. The artifact is pip-installable, runs the full headline grid in under a minute on a laptop, and is designed for community-contributed strategies and policies. Keywords: algorithmic auditing, AI accountability, Digital Services Act, strategic compliance, Stackelberg games, benchmark.

Introduction

The European Union’s AI Act mandates
post-market monitoring
for high-risk AI systems (Article 72): providers must continuously document and analyze their systems’ real-world behavior throughout the system’s lifetime, not merely at the point of release. The Digital Services Act (DSA) imposes related governance obligations on large online platforms (VLOPs/VLOSEs), including transparency reports on content moderation (Article 15), statements of reasons for moderation actions (Article 24), six-month publication cycles for VLOPs (Article 42), systemic-risk assessment (Article 34) and mitigation (Article 35), and annual independent audits (Article 37). These regimes share a common structural feature:
the audit relationship is longitudinal
. A regulated firm reports outcome metrics across many rounds, an auditor decides which rounds to scrutinize, and the firm can adapt over time.

This longitudinal structure creates a class of strategic auditee behavior that prior work on AI auditing has largely sidestepped. The strategic classification literature

3
The Benchmark

3.1
Audit Game Setup

We model continuous compliance audits as a
T
-round Stackelberg game between an
auditor
(regulator) and an
auditee
(regulated firm operating an AI system). At round
t\in\{0,1,\dots,T-1\}
:

1.

The latent ground-truth fairness metric
m_{t}\in[0,1]
evolves according to a clipped baseline drift,
m_{t}=\mathrm{clip}(m_{t-1}+\eta_{t},0,1)
with
\eta_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2})
(
\sigma=0.02
in our experiments) representing natural population-level variation.

2.

The auditee chooses a reported metric
\hat{m}_{t}\in[0,1]
and a sample size
n_{t}\in\{n_{\min},n_{\max}\}
, possibly conditioned on its own prior actions and audit-detection history.

3.

The auditor decides whether to audit round
t
. We restrict attention here to
static
policies, where the audit set
\mathcal{A}_{\pi}\subseteq\{0,1,\dots,T-1\}
is committed at
t=0
and the audit decision conditions only on the round index
t
, not on observed reports.

Information structure.

The auditee observes the auditor’s committed policy
\pi
(including any pseudo-random seed determining the audit schedule) at
t=0
, but does not modify its own committed strategy mid-game. Detection events are observable to the auditee but do not trigger strategy changes within a single play of the game; multi-game reputation effects are deliberately bracketed (§
6
). This corresponds to the
full-information Stackelberg
setting in which the auditor moves first by committing. Strategies in §
3
are canonical policy probes, not equilibrium best-responses (§
6
).

Three information regimes.

To make the dependence of our results on this assumption explicit, we distinguish three regimes the same simulator can instantiate:
(R1) public deterministic schedule
— the audit set
\mathcal{A}_{\pi}
is fixed at
t=0
and known to the auditee;
(R2) public randomized policy, private realized draw
— the auditor commits to a distribution over schedules but the realized
\mathcal{A}_{\pi}
is drawn privately, so the auditee knows the policy distribution but not which rounds will be audited;
(R3) adaptive secret schedule
— neither the policy nor the realized draw is disclosed. Our static policies with seeded pseudo-random schedules place the headline experiments in
R1
, so the OffAuditDrift result of §
5
(5) is most precisely read as a
worst-case-auditee bound
: under
R2
the same OffAuditDrift would face expected coverage
\Pr_{A\sim\pi}[t\in A]>0
on each off-audit round and could no longer guarantee zero gap on audited rounds. The policy interface accepts arbitrary stochastic implementations of
audit_this_round
, so an R2/R3 audit of the same library is a one-config-file extension (§
6
).

The auditor’s decision rule on audited rounds is a noise-aware Wald-style threshold (with regulatory tolerance
\epsilon\geq 0
and
z=1.96
for a 95% Wald CI half-width): a gap is flagged when

|\hat{m}_{t}-m_{t}|>\epsilon+z\cdot\sqrt{\hat{m}_{t}(1-\hat{m}_{t})/n_{t}}.

(3.1)

This rule captures the auditor’s honest uncertainty: when
n_{t}
is small, the noise envelope is wide and the rule is forgiving; when
n_{t}
is large, even small gaps are visible. At
\hat{m}=0.5
the threshold equals
\approx 0.031
for
n_{t}=1000
and
\approx 0.098
for
n_{t}=100
. We interpret
m_{t}
as the verified reference estimate the auditor recovers on an audited round (e.g., from an independent re-sample or compelled disclosure of the underlying records) and
n_{t}
as the effective sample size of the auditee’s claim. The Wald rule is therefore not a full two-sample test; it is a deliberately conservative acceptance band around the auditee’s reported metric that scales with the precision the auditee itself claimed.

3.2
Auditee Gaming Strategies

We provide five canonical strategy primitives that abstract recurring patterns in regulatory practice:

•

Delay
(
k=2
): the auditee reports the metric value from
k
rounds prior, lagging unfavorable shifts. Models reporting frictions and the strategic use of submission cycles to push bad news beyond the regulator’s attention window. In our (symmetric-drift) experiments the gap can take either sign; in adverse-shift regimes (e.g., monotonically degrading systems) Delay produces consistently positive gaps.

•

Drift
(
\delta=0.05
): the auditee adds a fixed bias to the truth, reporting
\hat{m}_{t}=m_{t}+\delta
. Models the strategic choice of a single fixed operationalization of an ambiguous regulatory metric.

•

Cherry-pick
(
K=5
candidates,
\sigma_{\text{pick}}=0.04
): each round the auditee samples
K
candidate metric estimates from
\mathcal{N}(m_{t},\sigma_{\text{pick}}^{2})
and reports the maximum. We treat Cherry-pick as Drift’s
stochastic-gap
counterpart: it produces a per-round gap whose expectation is
\sigma_{\text{pick}}\cdot\mathbb{E}[\max_{i\leq K}Z_{i}]
for
Z_{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,1)
, which evaluates to
\approx 0.0465
at
K=5
,
\sigma_{\text{pick}}=0.04
(consistent with the empirical mean of
+0.045
in Table
2
), with sample-to-sample variance, motivated by DSA Article 15 ambiguity over subgroup-parity definitions. Cherry-pick’s distinctive value in the benchmark is not a separate gaming pathway but a sensitivity probe: it is the only strategy whose detection latency depends qualitatively on the auditor’s choice of multiple-comparisons correction (Table
2
).

•

Attrition
(
\tau=0.40
,
\delta_{\text{cover}}=0.05
): when
m_{t}<\tau
the auditee restricts to a smaller verified sub-sample, dropping
n_{t}
from
n_{\max}=1000
to
n_{\min}=100
, and additionally drifts the report by
\delta_{\text{cover}}
. The reduced sample widens the auditor’s tolerance band per (
3.1
), allowing a small drift to escape.

•

OffAuditDrift
(
\delta=0.05
): an audit-aware variant of Drift that exploits the Stackelberg commitment of the auditor’s schedule. The auditee observes the committed audit set
\mathcal{A}_{\pi}
at
t=0
and reports honestly (
\hat{m}_{t}=m_{t}
) on rounds in
\mathcal{A}_{\pi}
; on every other round it drifts by
\delta
(
\hat{m}_{t}=m_{t}+\delta
). Directly instantiates Case 1 of Observation
4.1
(coverage failure) for the deterministic-schedule policies (One-shot, Periodic, Scheduled-random, Min-sample floor), and for Suspicion-escalation it uses the base-cadence schedule as the auditee’s a-priori commitment.

A single common interface (
choose_report(t, m_t, history) -> (m_hat_t, n_t)
) lets users compose, parameterize, or extend these. We provide an
HonestAuditee
baseline that returns
(m_{t},n_{\max})
unconditionally as the no-strategic counterfactual.

3.3
Auditor Policies

We provide five auditor policies; the first three are
static temporal-coverage
policies that condition only on the round index
t
and total horizon
T
, while the last two extend along orthogonal axes (sample-size and gap-history conditioning):

•

One-shot
(
t^{\star}=5
): audit at a single committed round
t^{\star}
. Models traditional regulatory inspections.

•

Periodic
(period
k=3
, phase
k-1
): audit at
t\in\{2,5,8,11\}
over the
T=12
horizon. The phase offset corresponds to auditing at the end of each three-round reporting cycle (the natural cadence after a six-month DSA reporting interval has accumulated material). Models the de-facto pattern in most ongoing reporting regimes (DSA Article 15 / 42 transparency cycles, financial audit cycles).

•

Scheduled random
(
K=4
rounds, seed
42
): audit at
K
pseudo-randomly selected rounds, with the schedule fixed and known to the auditee at
t=0
via a committed seed. We avoid calling this a “surprise” audit because, under Stackelberg commitment, the realized audit set is observable to the auditee from the start; the term denotes a regime that is undisclosed only to outside parties, not to the auditee itself.

•

Min-sample floor
(
n_{\text{floor}}=500
, period
k=3
, phase
k-1
):
Periodic-with-floor
— audit on the same temporal cadence as the Periodic policy (
t\in\{2,5,8,11\}
for
T=12
), and on those audited rounds apply both the standard Wald rule and a sample-size floor: flag any audited round in which the auditee’s reported
n_{t}
falls below
n_{\text{floor}}
as ipso facto non-compliant. Conditions detection on
n_{t}
— a non-temporal lever orthogonal to the Periodic cadence.

•

Suspicion-escalation
(base period
4
, suspicion threshold
0.04
): audit periodically with first audit at
t=\text{base period}=4
and subsequent base-cadence audits at
t\in\{8\}
for
T=12
; once a prior audit observes a gap above the suspicion threshold, escalate to auditing every subsequent round. A deliberately simple history-conditioned threshold-escalation policy that conditions on observed gaps rather than
n_{t}
(richer Bayesian / CUSUM / SPRT alternatives are deferred to §
6
).

The first three policies are the scope to which Observation
4.1
applies; the latter two are minimal exits along the two orthogonal axes (sample-size and gap-history conditioning) the observation identifies. Richer adaptive families (Bayesian posteriors, SPRT, learned/RL) are follow-on work.

3.4
Evaluation Metrics

We report three primary metrics on each auditee–auditor pair, averaged over
S
random seeds:

•

Gaming gap

G(\pi,\sigma)=\overline{(\hat{m}_{t}-m_{t})}_{t\in\mathcal{A}}
, the mean reported-vs-true difference over audited rounds
\mathcal{A}
. Positive values indicate over-reporting (favorable to the auditee).

•

Time-to-detection

\tau_{d}(\pi,\sigma)\in\{0,1,\dots,T\}
: the first audited round at which
|\hat{m}_{t}-m_{t}|
exceeds the threshold of (
3.1
);
\tau_{d}=T
encodes “never detected within the horizon.” We report two variants:
\tau_{d}^{\text{uncorr}}
uses
z=1.96
(per-round 95% rule), and
\tau_{d}^{\text{Bonf}}
replaces
z
with
z_{\text{Bonf}}=\Phi^{-1}(1-\tfrac{\alpha}{2K})
where
K=|\mathcal{A}_{\pi}|
and
\alpha=0.05
, providing family-wise control across the audit set. Detection events do not trigger auditee strategy changes.

•

Welfare loss

W(\pi,\sigma)=\sum_{t\notin\mathcal{A}}n_{t}\cdot|\hat{m}_{t}-m_{t}|
measures harm to individuals
inside
the verified report, accumulated over unaudited rounds. Because
n_{t}
is auditee-controlled,
W
alone undercounts the harm of attrition; we therefore pair it with:

•

Coverage loss

C(\pi,\sigma)=\sum_{t}(N-n_{t})\cdot|\hat{m}_{t}-m_{t}|
, the harm to individuals
excluded
from the verified report, where
N
is the underlying affected population (we set
N=n_{\max}
for headline tables).
W
and
C
measure two distinct, non-overlapping accountability surfaces (the counted-and-unaudited population for
W
; the entirely excluded population for
C
); they are
not
components of a single total-loss quantity, and we do not interpret
W+C
as a sufficient statistic for harm. The substantive object of study is the
reallocation
of harm between the two surfaces across strategies (§
5
).

4
The Cover Regime: A Structural Observation

The simulator runs are organized by a single structural observation about noise-aware static auditors. Observation
4.1
below is conditional, not universal: it identifies a quantifiable interval (the
cover regime
) in which the auditor design space exposes a binding trade-off, and it identifies the analytical exit from that interval. We state it as an observation rather than a theorem because the proof is immediate from the Wald-CI threshold formula; the contribution is the framing and the exit, not the proof.

We restrict attention to
static temporal-coverage policies
: policies
\pi
that specify an audit set
\mathcal{A}_{\pi}\subseteq\{0,\dots,T-1\}
at
t=0
, condition only on the round index, and apply the noise-aware Wald rule (
3.1
) as their sole acceptance criterion. This class stylizes current reporting and audit regimes (DSA Articles 15/37 transparency reports and audits; AI Act Article 72 post-market monitoring) in which cadence is often specified more explicitly than verified-sample construction. A precision floor is regulatorily available but has not been prescribed in practice for reasons — false-positive inflation on legitimately small operating populations, legal contestability of any specific
n_{\text{floor}}
value, and methodological discretion left by existing DSA / AI Act obligations — that we discuss in Appendix
D
. The
Min-sample floor
baseline of §
3
demonstrates the analytical exit from this class while making these adoption barriers empirically visible.

Observation 4.1

(Coverage–granularity trade-off in the cover regime)
.

Fix horizon
T\geq 2
, regulatory tolerance
\epsilon\geq 0
, and the noise-aware decision rule
(
3.1
)
with sample-size bounds
n_{\min}<n_{\max}
. Define the
cover regime
at
\hat{m}\in(0,1)
as the half-open interval

\Delta(\hat{m},n_{\min},n_{\max})\;=\;\bigl(\epsilon+z\sqrt{\hat{m}(1-\hat{m})/n_{\max}},\;\epsilon+z\sqrt{\hat{m}(1-\hat{m})/n_{\min}}\bigr],

i.e., drift magnitudes large enough to exceed the full-sample threshold but small enough to fit within the small-sample threshold (worked example below). Define
h(p,n)=\epsilon+z\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}
. For any static temporal-coverage policy
\pi
and any
\delta
such that
\delta\in\Delta(\hat{m}_{t},n_{\min},n_{\max})
uniformly in
t
(i.e.,
\max_{t}h(\hat{m}_{t},n_{\max})<\delta\leq\min_{t}h(\hat{m}_{t},n_{\min})
across the realized trajectory), an evading auditee strategy
\sigma
exists in either of two structurally distinct configurations:

1.

Coverage failure.
If
\mathcal{A}_{\pi}\subsetneq\{0,\dots,T-1\}
, strategy
\sigma_{1}
drifts by
\delta
at any unaudited round
t^{\star}\notin\mathcal{A}_{\pi}
. The drift is never observed, so
\tau_{d}(\pi,\sigma_{1})=T
and welfare loss
W(\pi,\sigma_{1})\geq\delta\cdot n_{\max}>0
.

2.

Granularity failure.
If
\mathcal{A}_{\pi}=\{0,\dots,T-1\}
, strategy
\sigma_{2}
(Attrition of §
3
) reports
n_{t}=n_{\min}
and drifts by
\delta
on every round. The threshold widens beyond
\delta
, so the gap is never flagged:
\tau_{d}(\pi,\sigma_{2})=T
and
G(\pi,\sigma_{2})=\delta>0
.

The trade-off is unavoidable for any
static temporal-coverage

\pi
: shrinking Case 1 (auditing more often) cannot shrink Case 2 (which depends on the auditor’s ability to demand full-sample reporting via an independent constraint outside the Wald rule), and vice versa. A policy that imposes a minimum-sample-size floor (e.g., requiring
n_{t}\geq 500
) lies outside the class and can defeat Case 2 directly; the observation’s contribution is to delineate exactly which static-policy class the trade-off binds.

Proof.

Case 1.
Suppose
\mathcal{A}_{\pi}\subsetneq\{0,\dots,T-1\}
and pick any
t^{\star}\notin\mathcal{A}_{\pi}
. Define
\sigma_{1}
to drift by
\delta
at
t^{\star}
and report honestly otherwise:
\hat{m}_{t^{\star}}=m_{t^{\star}}+\delta
and
\hat{m}_{t}=m_{t}
for
t\neq t^{\star}
, with
n_{t}=n_{\max}
throughout. Detection requires
|\hat{m}_{t}-m_{t}|
to exceed the threshold of (
3.1
) on some round in
\mathcal{A}_{\pi}
; but on every
t\in\mathcal{A}_{\pi}
we have
|\hat{m}_{t}-m_{t}|=0
, so detection cannot fire and
\tau_{d}(\pi,\sigma_{1})=T
. The welfare-loss bound follows from
W(\pi,\sigma_{1})\geq|\hat{m}_{t^{\star}}-m_{t^{\star}}|\cdot n_{\max}=\delta\cdot n_{\max}>0
.

Case 2.
Suppose
\mathcal{A}_{\pi}=\{0,\dots,T-1\}
and consider
\sigma_{2}
with
n_{t}=n_{\min}
and
\hat{m}_{t}=m_{t}+\delta
on every round. The threshold of (
3.1
) at round
t
is
h(\hat{m}_{t},n_{\min})
. By the uniform hypothesis
\delta\leq\min_{t}h(\hat{m}_{t},n_{\min})
, so
|\hat{m}_{t}-m_{t}|=\delta\leq h(\hat{m}_{t},n_{\min})
at every round; the rule does not fire and
\tau_{d}(\pi,\sigma_{2})=T
. The audited-round gap satisfies
G(\pi,\sigma_{2})=\mathbb{E}_{t\in\mathcal{A}_{\pi}}[\hat{m}_{t}-m_{t}]=\delta>0
by construction. (The uniform condition is automatic when the trajectory
\hat{m}_{t}
stays bounded away from
0
and
1
, as in the empirical anchor with
\hat{m}\in[0.30,0.55]
; near the boundary,
p(1-p)
shrinks and the regime can close.)

The proof is constructive and immediate from the Wald threshold; the contribution is the taxonomy of failure modes, runnable witnesses (§
5
), and the orthogonal-axes minimal exits.
∎

Reading the observation.

The cover regime is non-empty whenever
n_{\max}/n_{\min}
is bounded away from
1
; at the benchmark parameters (
n_{\min}=100
,
n_{\max}=1000
,
\hat{m}=0.5
) the regime is
(0.031,0.098]
and contains the standard
\delta=0.05
. Outside it the observation is silent:
\delta
below the full-sample threshold escapes regardless of coverage, and
\delta
above the small-sample threshold is detected regardless of attrition. The trade-off vanishes for adaptive auditors who can respond to a sample-size drop with an out-of-cycle audit; the benchmark is designed to make such adaptive policies the natural object of comparison. Figure
2
maps the cover regime in
(n_{\min},\delta)
space for the periodic policy.

Five patterns dominate the table.

(1) Drift is detected, but coverage gaps leak welfare.
Against the Drift strategy, the static temporal-coverage policies detect with latencies governed by their audit cadence: scheduled-random in
\tau_{d}=1.3
rounds (mean over independent committed schedules), periodic in
2.0
, one-shot in
5.0
. For a
K=4
schedule from
T=12
rounds, the analytical expected minimum audited round is
(T+1)/(K+1)-1=1.6
; the
1.3
empirical mean over
30
schedule seeds is within Monte-Carlo variation. Detection works for above-envelope drift, but the
0.05
gap persists across unaudited rounds, producing
W=400
–
550
person-rounds. This is a non-adaptive manifestation of Case 1 of Observation
4.1
: detection occurs, but unaudited rounds still leak welfare under our no-remediation convention; the
OffAuditDrift
strategy below provides the direct empirical witness for the audit-aware Case 1 construction.

(2) The Bonferroni-correction tax is small but visible on stochastic-gap strategies.
Cherry-pick is the stochastic counterpart to Drift: its purpose here is to expose how detection latency depends on the auditor’s choice of decision rule. Cherry-pick’s mean per-round gap is
\approx+0.045
but the per-round draw varies (Table
2
shows seed-level SE on the gap of
0.003
–
0.005
). Under uncorrected per-round Wald-CI (
z=1.96
), the periodic policy detects in
2.7
rounds; under Bonferroni correction across
K=4
audits,
z_{\text{Bonf}}=\Phi^{-1}(1-0.05/8)\approx 2.50
, and detection slips to
4.2
rounds — the auditor pays a
\approx 1.5
-round latency tax for family-wise error control. Drift’s fixed
\delta=0.05
sits well above both thresholds and is unaffected; Cherry-pick’s lower-variance draws sometimes fall below the corrected threshold. The general lesson is that any strategy whose typical gap is just above the full-sample threshold loses detection under multi-comparisons correction, and Cherry-pick’s role in the benchmark is to make this dependence visible by construction.

(4) Two orthogonal exits along sample-size and history axes; neither closes both failure modes.
The Min-sample floor (Periodic+floor) and Suspicion-escalation policies validate Observation
4.1
along orthogonal axes.
Min-sample floor
(
n_{t}
-conditioning) closes Case 2: against Attrition (which defeats all three static temporal-coverage policies with
\tau_{d}=12

when the auditee’s reported
n_{t}=100<n_{\text{floor}}=500
; the Honest row confirms no spurious firing at

would be flagged.

(history-conditioning) closes Case 1 against the naive Drift strategy: it detects in

by escalating audits after observing a gap above the suspicion threshold. Suspicion-escalation does

close Case 2 (Attrition row:

(5) OffAuditDrift instantiates Case 1 directly: detection fails for every policy.
OffAuditDrift exploits Stackelberg commitment by observing
\mathcal{A}_{\pi}
at
t=0
and reporting honestly precisely on audited rounds. The audited-round gap is zero by construction across all five policies (Suspicion-escalation’s base-cadence audits at

never observe a non-zero gap, so escalation never triggers),
\tau_{d}=12
uniformly, and welfare loss is monotone in coverage (

4.1
Case 1.

6
Limitations and Discussion

We name four next-iteration directions explicitly.
(i) Honest sampling noise — validated.

HonestNoisyAuditee
(binomial draws at
n_{t}=1000
,
m_{t}
observed exactly) gives per-round uncorrected FPR
0.044
–
0.054
at nominal
\alpha=0.05
and Bonferroni-corrected family-wise FPR
0.035
–
0.065
across the five policies (Appendix
E
); auditor-side reference noise is a v2 extension.
(ii) Information regime.
Under R2 (public randomized policy, private realized draw, §
3
) we expect OffAuditDrift to lose its zero-gap guarantee on audited rounds.
(iii) Domain transfer.
The DSA calibration of Table
1
is one regulatory anchor; recalibration to algorithmic hiring
(
8
)
, credit scoring, or post-market clinical decision support follows the same parameter map.
(iv) Adaptive-auditor frontier.
A richer frontier (Bayesian posteriors over
(n_{t},\text{gap})
trajectories, SPRT, learned/RL) should Pareto-dominate on the (detection, FPR) plane, and a strategy library covering best-response, collusion, and target-population manipulation is the natural follow-on.

A.3
Auditor / Auditee protocol

Both interfaces are deliberately minimal so that user-supplied strategies and policies plug in without modification:

•

Auditee.choose_report(t, true_metric, audit_history) -> (reported, n_t)
: returns the reported metric and sample size for round
t
. Stateful strategies (e.g.
DelayStrategy
, which buffers past true metrics) maintain state on the instance.

•

Auditor.audit_this_round(t, T, history) -> bool
: returns whether to audit round
t
. Static policies ignore
history
entirely; the parameter is provided for adaptive extensions.

A.4
Detection rule

The implementation of equation (
3.1
):
Ωdef detection_threshold(reported_p, n, epsilon=0.0, z=1.96):Ωp = max(0.0, min(1.0, reported_p))Ωse = math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / max(n, 1))Ωreturn epsilon + z * seΩ

At
p=0.5
and
z=1.96
, this gives
\sim 0.031
for
n=1000
,
\sim 0.098
for
n=100
. Detection fires when
|\hatm_{t}-m_{t}|
exceeds this threshold on an audited round.

Drift envelope.

The Drift strategy is parameterized by
\delta\in[0,0.5]
. As
\delta
shrinks, the gap narrows linearly and time-to-detection grows sharply once
\delta
falls below the noise envelope at
n_{\max}
(
\sim
0.031). Below this threshold, even a fully-auditing surprise policy cannot detect (Case 2 of Observation
4.1
kicks in even without attrition, because the drift fits within the noise envelope at full
n
). At
\delta\geq 0.05
, surprise audit detects on its first audited round under both configs.

Detection tolerance
\epsilon
.

Setting
\epsilon=0.05
(a permissive regulatory tolerance) widens the threshold by an additive
0.05
, allowing both Drift and Attrition strategies at
\delta=0.05
to escape all three policies. In practice, regulators typically operate at
\epsilon=0
(the noise-aware Wald rule) with the understanding that the auditor adjusts her decision threshold for sample-size-driven uncertainty.

Attrition threshold
\tau
.

Setting
\tau=0.5
in the default config causes attrition to fire about half the rounds (whenever the natural drift dips
m_{t}
below
0.5
). The attrition gap is then a weighted average of zero-gap (when
m_{t}\geq\tau
) and
+0.05
(when
m_{t}<\tau

n_{t}
on cover.

Sample-size ratio.

The attrition cover effect requires
n_{\min}/n_{\max}\lesssim 0.1
to widen the threshold from
\sim
0.031 to
\sim
0.098 (the value needed to swallow
\delta=0.05

n_{\min}=500
, the threshold is only
\sim
0.044, and a
\delta=0.05

Appendix C
Bonferroni
K
accounting and Scheduled-random schedule-seed handling

Static policies.

For static policies
K=|\mathcal{A}_{\pi}|
, the committed audit-set cardinality, identical across seeds and trajectories:
K=1
for One-shot,
K=4
for Periodic / Scheduled-random / Min-sample floor,
K=2
for Suspicion-escalation under base cadence absent escalation.

Suspicion-escalation.

For the history-conditioned Suspicion-escalation policy we use the
realized per-trajectory

K
, since escalation can grow
K
beyond the base value. Under the seeds reported in Table
2
, escalation triggers within the first base period for Drift / Cherry-pick (so realized
K
approaches
T=12
) and never triggers for Honest / Attrition / OffAuditDrift (so
K
remains
2
). This is statistically conservative: a sequential or alpha-spending alternative would be tighter but is deferred to follow-on work.

Scheduled-random.

The Scheduled-random policy’s audit schedule seed is varied independently of the auditee stochasticity seed (offset by
1009\cdots
) so each trial draws an independent committed schedule. Prior versions reused the YAML-configured
SurprisePolicy
seed across all trials, which collapsed the audit-schedule distribution to a single committed schedule and produced misleading point estimates; the offset removes that confound.

Appendix D
Why precision floors are regulatorily available but rarely prescribed

\parThe

static-temporal-coverage class scoped in §
4
is a stylization of current reporting and audit regimes (DSA Articles 15/37 transparency reports and audits; AI Act Article 72 post-market monitoring) in which audit cadence is often specified more explicitly than verified-sample construction. A precision floor is regulatorily available but has not been prescribed in practice for at least three reasons immediate from the sensitivity analysis (§
5
, Figure
3
):

•

(i) FPR on legitimately small populations.
A precision floor inflates false-positive rates on operating populations that are legitimately small — low-traffic content categories, platforms in early growth phases, or product lines serving narrow user segments — creating administrative friction the regulator must adjudicate round-by-round.

•

(ii) Legal contestability of the threshold.
Any specific
n_{\text{floor}}
value is contestable as arbitrary in legal proceedings without a calibration to the noise-aware threshold, and the noise-aware threshold itself depends on the very
n_{t}
the floor is meant to constrain.

•

(iii) Methodological discretion in existing obligations.
DSA and AI Act obligations leave substantial methodological discretion over how reported metrics are operationalized, including which populations are included in a report, presumably to accommodate heterogeneous platform sizes.

\parThe

MinSampleFloor
baseline of §
3
demonstrates the analytical exit from the static-temporal-coverage class while making these adoption barriers empirically visible: Figure
3
(a) shows the binary trade-off in
n_{t}
that any level-conditioned floor exposes, motivating richer adaptive frontiers conditioning on
n_{t}

trajectories
rather than levels.
