Anti-lipid peroxidation attenuates Schwann cell repair phenotype and attenuates PNI-like behavior in oral squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Jing Long, Youzhi Wang, Mengqi Zou, Gangyong Jiang, Zhongwei Jiang, Wanxiao Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerineural invasion (PNI) is a critical feature of oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), in which Schwann cells are increasingly recognized as active contributors rather than passive structures. However, the mechanisms underlying Schwann cell phenotypic remodeling in the tumor microenvironment remain incompletely understood. This study aimed to investigate whether OSCC-derived signals induce lipid peroxidation and repair-associated phenotypic remodeling in Schwann cells that promote PNI-like tumor cell behavior, and whether inhibiting lipid peroxidation can attenuate these effects.MethodsAn OSCC-conditioned microenvironment was established to investigate whether tumor-derived signals induce lipid peroxidation and phenotypic changes in human Schwann cells (hSCs). Redox status was assessed by lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), malondialdehyde (MDA), glutathione (GSH), and C11-BODIPY assays. Repair-associated markers (c-Jun and NGFR) were evaluated by Western blot, and RSL3 was used as a positive inducer of lipid peroxidation. Schwann cell-conditioned medium (SCM) was applied to assess its effects on OSCC cell migration and invasion. Ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) was used to inhibit lipid peroxidation.ResultsOSCC-conditioned medium induced significant lipid peroxidation and redox imbalance in hSCs, as evidenced by increased LDH and MDA levels and decreased GSH (all P<0.001), along with elevated c-Jun and NGFR expression (P<0.001). RSL3 produced a stronger lipid peroxidation response and similar repair-marker changes. Conditioned medium derived from these cells enhanced migration and invasion of OSCC cells (P<0.001), without affecting cell viability. Fer-1 treatment partially reversed these changes, reducing oxidative stress, suppressing repair-marker expression (P<0.001), and attenuating the pro-migratory and pro-invasive effects (P<0.001).ConclusionsThese findings support lipid peroxidation as a contributory regulator of Schwann cell repair-associated remodeling and suggest that redox modulation may attenuate Schwann cell-mediated PNI-like behavior in OSCC in vitro.Keywords: Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC), Schwann cells, lipid peroxidation, ferroptosis, perineural invasion (PNI)

Highlight box

Key findings

• Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC)-conditioned medium induces lipid peroxidation and redox imbalance in human Schwann cells, with upregulation of repair-associated markers c-Jun and NGFR.

• Direct induction of lipid peroxidation by RSL3 recapitulates part of this response.

• Conditioned medium from these Schwann cells promotes OSCC cell migration and invasion without altering viability.

• Ferrostatin-1 partially reverses redox changes, attenuates repair-marker induction, and weakens the pro-migratory/pro-invasive paracrine effects.

What is known and what is new?

• Schwann cells can adopt repair-associated states that are hijacked in cancer and may facilitate perineural invasion (PNI)-related tumor behavior; however, upstream triggers of this remodeling in OSCC remain incompletely defined.

• This study links OSCC-derived signals to Schwann cell lipid peroxidation and repair-associated marker changes, and shows that anti-lipid peroxidation with Fer-1 attenuates Schwann cell-mediated PNI-like behavior in vitro.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Lipid peroxidation in Schwann cells may be a targetable contributor to nerve-tumor interaction in OSCC.

• Future work should validate these findings in co-culture and in vivo PNI models and identify the soluble mediators driving the paracrine effect.

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) remains one of the most aggressive malignancies in the head and neck region, not only because of its local invasiveness but also due to its unique interaction with the peripheral nervous system (1). Among its pathological features, perineural invasion (PNI) has drawn increasing attention, as it is closely associated with tumor recurrence, pain, and poor prognosis (2). Traditionally, PNI has been viewed as a passive process in which tumor cells spread along pre-existing nerve structures (3). However, this view is gradually shifting. Emerging evidence suggests that nerve-associated cells, particularly Schwann cells, are not merely structural components but active participants in tumor progression (4,5). Still, the mechanisms by which Schwann cells undergo tumor-associated phenotypic remodeling remain insufficiently understood.

Schwann cells exhibit remarkable plasticity. Under physiological injury, they can transition into a repair state characterized by coordinated changes, including c-Jun activation, p75NTR/NGFR expression, myelin-related suppression, altered morphology, increased motility, and secretion of trophic factors that facilitate axonal regeneration and tissue repair (6). Yet, in the context of cancer, elements of this adaptive program may be hijacked (7). Tumor-derived signals can induce Schwann cells to adopt a repair-like but functionally altered state, which may, in turn, support tumor cell migration and invasion (8,9). Several studies have hinted at this bidirectional interaction, although the underlying triggers that initiate such phenotypic remodeling are still under investigation (10). In particular, metabolic and redox-related stressors have been increasingly implicated as upstream regulators of cellular state changes (11).

Lipid peroxidation, as a central component of oxidative stress and ferroptosis-related processes, has emerged as a modulator of cell fate and function (12). Excessive accumulation of lipid peroxides can disrupt membrane integrity, alter signaling pathways, and reshape the secretory profile of cells (13). In tumor biology, lipid peroxidation is often discussed in the context of cancer cells themselves (14). By contrast, its role in stromal or nerve-associated cells has received less attention. It remains unclear whether OSCC-derived factors induce lipid peroxidation in Schwann cells and whether such changes contribute to repair-associated marker changes and downstream tumor cell behavior. More importantly, it is not yet known whether targeting lipid peroxidation can reverse these alterations and thereby influence tumor-nerve interactions.

In this study, we constructed an OSCC-conditioned microenvironment to examine how tumor-derived signals affect Schwann cell behavior. By integrating biochemical assays, protein expression analysis, and conditioned-medium functional assays, we evaluated lipid peroxidation status, repair-associated markers, and the downstream impact on OSCC cell migration and invasion. RSL3 was included to test whether direct induction of lipid peroxidation could mimic part of the OSCC-conditioned medium (OSCC-CM) effect, and ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) was applied to test whether these changes were reversible. Through this approach, we aimed to delineate a mechanistic axis linking lipid peroxidation, Schwann cell phenotypic remodeling, and PNI-like tumor behavior, while acknowledging that this in vitro system does not fully reproduce the in vivo PNI microenvironment. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1148/rc).

Methods

Experimental design and grouping

Human Schwann cells (hSCs; cat. No. 1700) were cultured in Schwann Cell Medium (all from ScienCell, USA). CAL27 and SCC9 cells were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, USA; CAL27: CRL-2095; SCC9: CRL-1629) and maintained in DMEM/F12 with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (both from Gibco, USA). Fer-1 (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, cat. No. SML0583) and RSL3 (MedChemExpress, USA, cat. No. HY-100218A) were dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO; Sigma-Aldrich), and the final DMSO concentration was kept at 0.1% in all groups.

In Experiment 1 (intervention safety assessment), hSCs were treated with vehicle (0.1% DMSO) or Fer-1 at 0.5, 1, 2, and 4 µM for 24 h or 48 h after cell attachment (15-17).

In Experiment 2 (mechanistic validation), hSCs were divided into five groups (18,19): control (Ctrl), OSCC-CM, OSCC-CM+Fer-1, RSL3, and RSL3+Fer-1. CAL27 and SCC9 cells, once reaching 70–80% confluence, were cultured in medium containing 1% FBS for 24 h, after which the supernatants were collected, centrifuged, and mixed at equal volumes to generate OSCC-conditioned medium (OSCC-CM). hSCs were then exposed to OSCC-CM at 50% (v/v) with fresh medium (final 1% FBS), and DMSO was adjusted to 0.1%. For the intervention group, hSCs were pretreated with Fer-1 (2 µM) for 2 h, followed by OSCC-CM with Fer-1 maintained for a total of 24 h. RSL3 (0.5 µM), used as a positive inducer of lipid peroxidation, was applied for 12 h, with or without Fer-1 pretreatment.

In Experiment 3 (functional assessment of Schwann cell-mediated paracrine effects relevant to PNI-like behavior), treated hSCs were washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then cultured in fresh medium containing 1% FBS for 24 h, allowing the collection of Schwann cell-conditioned medium (SCM). Based on treatment origin, five groups were defined (5,8,20,21): Ctrl-SCM, OSCC-CM-SCM, OSCC-CM+Fer-1-SCM, Positive-SCM, and Positive+Fer-1-SCM.

Cell viability assay

Schwann cells (hSCs) or OSCC cells (CAL27 and SCC9) were seeded in 96-well plates at an appropriate density and left to attach overnight under standard conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2), after which they were subjected to the indicated conditions. Subsequently, the medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 10% Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Dojindo, Japan, CK04) solution and incubated at 37 °C for 1–2 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was then recorded using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, USA). Absorbance readings were normalized to the control group and are expressed as relative cell viability.

Biochemical assays for lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), malondialdehyde (MDA), and glutathione (GSH)

To evaluate cell injury, culture supernatants, which were collected after treatment, were used for LDH detection with an LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (Beyotime, China, C0017), following the manufacturer’s instructions. After the reaction mixture had been added and incubated for the recommended time, absorbance at 490 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad), and membrane damage was inferred from the absorbance values.

For intracellular redox status, cells that had undergone the indicated treatments were lysed on ice, and the resulting supernatants were applied to determine MDA and GSH levels using commercial kits (Beyotime; MDA: S0131S; GSH: S0053). MDA content was quantified based on the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reaction at 532 nm; GSH levels were measured colorimetrically at 412 nm.

Lipid peroxidation assay

Lipid peroxidation in Schwann cells was assessed using the C11-BODIPY 581/591 fluorescent probe (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA, D3861). After treatment, cells, which were maintained under standard conditions, were incubated with the probe (2 µM) at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark, after which they were gently washed with PBS to remove excess dye. The fluorescence signals were then detected using a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan), and the ratio of green to red fluorescence, which increases as the probe becomes oxidized, was calculated; this ratio, in turn, reflects the extent of lipid peroxidation in the cells.

Western blot analysis

Treated cells were first washed with cold PBS, and lysis was then carried out using radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer (Beyotime) supplemented with protease inhibitors (Beyotime), from which total protein was obtained following centrifugation. Protein concentration was subsequently determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23225), after which equal amounts of protein were loaded and separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE), and then transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA, IPVH00010).

Blocked with 5% non-fat milk, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Abcam, UK), including c-Jun (ab31419, 1:1,000), and NGFR (ab245134, 1:1,000); β-actin (ab20272, 1:5,000) served as the loading control. After washing, incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology, USA, 1:5,000) was performed at room temperature for 1 h, and protein bands were then visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Band intensities were quantified by ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA), and relative expression levels were calculated accordingly.

Transwell migration and invasion assays

To evaluate the effects of conditioned media on tumor cell behavior, Transwell assays were performed using chambers with 8-µm pore size (Corning, USA).

For migration analysis, CAL27 or SCC9 cells were seeded into the upper chamber in serum-free medium, while conditioned media, which had been collected from treated Schwann cells, were added to the lower chamber as chemoattractants. After incubation for 16 h at 37 °C, non-migrated cells on the upper surface were removed, and those that had passed through the membrane were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Beyotime) and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Beyotime).

For invasion assays, which were carried out in a similar manner, the upper chambers were pre-coated with Matrigel (BD Biosciences, USA), and cells were allowed to invade for 24 h. After staining, cells that had migrated or invaded were observed under a microscope (Olympus), and the number of cells, which were counted in randomly selected fields, was used to assess migratory and invasive capacities.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 23.0, IBM, USA). Data from at least three independent experiments are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). For comparisons between multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was applied, after which post hoc tests were conducted when appropriate. A value of P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Fer-1 shows no obvious cytotoxicity or membrane damage in hSCs

To determine a suitable working concentration, the potential cytotoxicity of Fer-1 in Schwann cells was first evaluated. As shown in Figure 1A, CCK-8 results indicated that, compared with the Vehicle group, Fer-1 at 0.5, 1, and 2 µM did not significantly affect cell viability at either 24 h or 48 h. However, at 4 µM, a mild decrease was observed at 24 h, which became more pronounced at 48 h (P<0.001). These findings suggest that lower concentrations of Fer-1 are generally well tolerated by hSCs.

Fer-1 shows low cytotoxicity in hSCs. (A) CCK-8 analysis of cell viability after Fer-1 treatment (24 h, 48 h). (B) LDH levels in culture supernatant under the same conditions. Data are expressed as mean ± SD. ***P<0.001 vs. Vehicle. CCK-8, Cell Counting Kit-8; Fer-1, ferrostatin-1; hSCs, human Schwann cells; LDH, lactate dehydrogenase; SD, standard deviation.

Consistently, LDH release assays (Figure 1B) showed no significant difference between the Vehicle group and the 0.5–2 µM Fer-1 groups, whereas a marked increase in LDH level was detected at 4 µM (P<0.001), indicating membrane damage at this higher concentration. Taken together, these results demonstrate that Fer-1 at 2 µM and below does not induce obvious cytotoxicity or membrane injury in hSCs, and this concentration was therefore selected for subsequent experiments.

Lipid peroxidation and redox alterations in hSCs under OSCC-CM stimulation and Fer-1 intervention

Several sets of experiments were designed to explore whether tumor-derived factors disturb the redox state of Schwann cells. As reflected by LDH release (Figure 2A), exposure to OSCC-CM resulted in a clear elevation compared with the Ctrl condition, suggesting compromised membrane integrity (P<0.001). The effect became more pronounced under RSL3 treatment, with LDH levels reached a higher range (P<0.001). When Fer-1 was introduced, LDH values shifted downward in both contexts (P<0.001), indicating partial protection.

(C) Representative C11-BODIPY fluorescence images and quantification of the oxidized/reduced (green/red) fluorescence ratio in hSCs. Data are expressed as mean ± SD. ***P<0.001 vs. Ctrl; ##P<0.01, ###P<0.001 vs. OSCC-CM; &&&P<0.001 vs. RSL3. Ctrl, control; Fer-1, ferrostatin-1; GSH, glutathione; hSCs, human Schwann cells; LDH, lactate dehydrogenase; MDA, malondialdehyde; OSCC, oral squamous cell carcinoma; OSCC-CM, OSCC-conditioned medium; SD, standard deviation.

Changes within the cells followed a related but not identical pattern. MDA content rose under OSCC-CM and showed a further increase in the RSL3 group, while GSH moved in the opposite direction (Figure 2B, P<0.001). With Fer-1 present, these alterations were moderated—MDA declined and GSH recovered to some extent, though neither fully returned to baseline (P<0.01 or P<0.001).

C11-BODIPY fluorescence further demonstrated enhanced lipid peroxidation in hSCs following OSCC-CM exposure, characterized by increased oxidized (green) fluorescence and decreased reduced (red) fluorescence (Figure 2C). The oxidized/reduced fluorescence ratio was significantly increased by OSCC-CM and was further elevated following RSL3 treatment. Fer-1 significantly reduced this ratio in both OSCC-CM- and RSL3-treated cells, indicating effective suppression of lipid peroxidation.

In summary, these findings indicate that OSCC-derived signals induce lipid peroxidation and redox imbalance in hSCs, whereas Fer-1-mediated inhibition of lipid peroxidation partially restores redox homeostasis.

OSCC-CM is associated with repair-marker changes in hSCs, with partial normalization by Fer-1

Attention was next directed to markers associated with the Schwann cell repair program. Exposure to OSCC-CM led to a noticeable rise in c-Jun and NGFR protein levels (Figure 3A,3B, P<0.001), suggesting activation of repair-associated signaling rather than establishment of a full repair phenotype. Under RSL3 treatment, the signal became more pronounced, indicating that direct lipid peroxidation induction can mimic part of the OSCC-CM effect. With Fer-1 treatment, this elevation was reduced (P<0.001), and protein levels shifted closer to baseline, though not fully restored. The same trend appeared across both stimulation conditions.

Changes in repair-associated markers in hSCs under different conditions. (A) Protein expression of c-Jun and NGFR detected by Western blot. (B) Quantitative analysis of c-Jun and NGFR expression levels. Data are presented as mean ± SD. ***P<0.001 vs. Ctrl; ###P<0.001 vs. OSCC-CM; &&&P<0.001 vs. RSL3. Ctrl, control; Fer-1, ferrostatin-1; hSCs, human Schwann cells; OSCC, oral squamous cell carcinoma; OSCC-CM, OSCC-conditioned medium; SD, standard deviation.

Densitometric analysis (Figure 3B) reflected the same tendency. Relative expression values showed a clear separation between stimulated and untreated conditions, while Fer-1 consistently shifted them downward (P<0.001). These findings indicate that OSCC-derived signals can induce repair-associated marker changes in Schwann cells and that modulation of lipid peroxidation alters this response.

SCM promotes OSCC cell migration and invasion, an effect attenuated by Fer-1

Before assessing functional changes, cell viability was first examined to exclude potential interference. Across all conditioned media groups, neither CAL27 nor SCC9 showed obvious changes in viability (Figure 4A), indicating that the subsequent functional differences were not driven by cytotoxic effects.

Migration assays revealed a marked response. When exposed to conditioned medium derived from OSCC-CM-treated Schwann cells, both cell lines displayed enhanced motility, while the positive control group exhibited an even stronger effect (Figure 4B,4C, P<0.001). When Fer-1 was applied during Schwann cell treatment, this increase was clearly reduced, and fewer cells were observed passing through the membrane.

Invasion followed a similar but slightly more pronounced pattern. The number of invading cells rose under OSCC-CM-SCM and peaked in the positive control condition (Figure 4D,4E, P<0.001). This tendency weakened after Fer-1 intervention, with invasion counts shifting downward. Quantitative data were consistent with the imaging results, showing that the pro-invasive influence of Schwann cell-derived factors can be partially restrained when lipid peroxidation is suppressed.

Discussion

The present study delineates a sequence of events linking tumor-derived signals to Schwann cell repair-associated remodeling and, ultimately, to enhanced tumor cell motility. Three main observations stand out. First, OSCC-conditioned medium induced a clear shift in the redox state of Schwann cells, reflected by increased lipid peroxidation and altered antioxidant balance. Second, this biochemical disturbance coincided with activation of repair-associated markers, including c-Jun and NGFR, suggesting a partial repair-like response. Third, the conditioned medium derived from these altered Schwann cells promoted migration and invasion of OSCC cells, whereas inhibition of lipid peroxidation by Fer-1 attenuated these effects. Together, these findings support a functional axis in which oxidative stress within Schwann cells contributes to tumor-nerve interaction.

The involvement of oxidative stress in tumor-associated stromal remodeling has been described in several contexts. In neural injury models, activation of c-Jun is considered a hallmark component of the repair phenotype, enabling Schwann cells to support axonal regeneration (22). A similar upregulation has been observed in tumor settings, where Schwann cells acquire properties that facilitate cancer progression rather than repair (23). In line with this, the increase in c-Jun and NGFR observed here under OSCC-CM exposure suggests that tumor-derived factors may co-opt elements of an otherwise protective program. Because the present study did not directly assess myelin protein downregulation, Schwann cell morphology, or neurotrophic factor secretion, we have described the observed changes as repair-associated marker changes rather than a complete repair phenotype. Studies in pancreatic and head and neck cancers have reported that Schwann cells, once activated, can secrete chemotactic factors and extracellular matrix components that favor tumor cell dissemination (24,25). While these reports do not always focus on redox regulation, they provide indirect support for the idea that phenotypic plasticity of Schwann cells contributes to PNI-related behavior.

What appears more specific in the current work is the positioning of lipid peroxidation as a contributory upstream event in this process. The parallel changes observed between redox markers and repair-associated proteins point to a possible regulatory link. This interpretation is consistent with emerging evidence that oxidative stress can influence transcriptional programs through redox-sensitive signaling pathways (11). For example, reactive oxygen species (ROS)-dependent activation of AP-1 family members, including c-Jun, has been reported in various cell types, where it modulates stress responses and phenotypic transitions (26). In this context, lipid peroxidation may not simply represent cellular damage but could act as a signaling amplifier that reshapes Schwann cell behavior. The RSL3-positive group further supports this interpretation by showing that direct lipid peroxidation induction can reproduce part of the OSCC-CM response, whereas partial reversal by Fer-1 suggests that limiting lipid peroxidation is sufficient to dampen, though not entirely abolish, the phenotypic shift. These data support involvement of lipid peroxidation but do not prove that it is the sole or dominant regulator.

The functional consequence of this repair-associated remodeling becomes evident when considering the effects on tumor cells. The use of SCM allowed isolation of paracrine influences, avoiding direct cell-cell contact (24). Under these conditions, enhanced migration and invasion were consistently observed, indicating that soluble factors released by Schwann cells are sufficient to promote PNI-like behavior in vitro. Similar paracrine interactions have been reported in other tumor microenvironments, where stromal cells exposed to stress or injury signals acquire a pro-tumorigenic secretory profile (27). Although the specific mediators were not identified in the present study, candidates such as neurotrophic factors, cytokines, or matrix-remodeling enzymes are likely involved. It is also plausible that redox-dependent modifications alter the composition or activity of these secreted molecules, thereby influencing tumor cell responsiveness.

From a broader perspective, this study provides a framework that connects metabolic stress, cellular plasticity, and tumor progression within the nerve niche. Rather than viewing PNI solely as a structural phenomenon, the findings suggest that it may be driven, at least in part, by dynamic changes in Schwann cell state. The ability of Fer-1 to interfere with this process highlights lipid peroxidation as a potentially targetable node in vitro. While ferroptosis inhibitors have been primarily explored in the context of protecting normal tissues, their role in modulating tumor-stroma interactions remains less defined. The present data indicate that such intervention may not directly act on tumor cells but instead reshape the microenvironment in a way that attenuates pro-migratory and pro-invasive signaling.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the study relied on in vitro models, which, although controlled, cannot fully capture the complexity of in vivo nerve-tumor interactions. The use of conditioned medium simplifies the system and may overlook contributions from direct cell-cell contact, axonal guidance, extracellular matrix architecture, and mechanical cues; therefore, the results should be interpreted as PNI-like functional behavior rather than direct validation of PNI. Second, the current evidence for Schwann cell repair-associated remodeling was based mainly on c-Jun/NGFR expression and functional conditioned-medium assays. Morphological changes, myelin protein downregulation, and neurotrophic factor secretion were not examined and should be incorporated in future work. Third, downstream and upstream mechanisms linking OSCC-derived signals to lipid peroxidation were not dissected. Candidate pathways include ROS-producing enzymes, mitochondrial redox stress, iron-handling mechanisms, and ferroptosis-related regulators such as GPX4/SLC7A11 and ACSL4, which warrant direct testing by Western blot, transcriptomic, or proteomic approaches. Finally, animal models of OSCC-associated PNI are needed to determine whether lipid peroxidation and repair-associated Schwann cell markers are modulated in vivo and whether Fer-1 or related interventions can reduce true PNI.

Conclusions

This study shows that OSCC-derived signals disrupt the redox balance of Schwann cells and induce repair-associated changes that are functionally altered changes, which in turn enhance the migratory and invasive behavior of OSCC cells in vitro. When lipid peroxidation is inhibited by Fer-1, these changes are partially reversed, and the pro-tumorigenic effect is weakened. These findings indicate that lipid peroxidation is not only a marker of cellular stress but also a contributory regulator of Schwann cell remodeling within the tumor microenvironment, and they suggest that targeting this process may help attenuate nerve-tumor interactions and PNI-related progression in OSCC, pending validation in direct co-culture and in vivo PNI models.
