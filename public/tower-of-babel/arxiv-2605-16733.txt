Concentration Inequalities for Sample Cross-Covariances
Jiaheng Chen and Daniel Sanz-Alonso

Abstract
This paper establishes sharp dimension-free concentration and expectation bounds for the deviation of a sample cross-covariance matrix from its mean. For sub-Gaussian random vectors, we prove a high-probability operator-norm bound governed by the effective ranks of the two marginal covariance matrices. In the Gaussian case, we prove a matching expectation lower bound, allowing arbitrary correlation between the two random vectors.

1
Introduction

Sample covariance matrices are among the most fundamental objects in probability and statistics. Concentration inequalities for sample
covariances play a central role in covariance estimation, principal component
analysis, regression, empirical process theory, and many related problems.
A now-standard message is that the relevant dimension parameter is often not
the ambient dimension, but rather the effective rank of the covariance matrix
\Sigma,
defined by

r(\Sigma)=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma)}{\|\Sigma\|},

where
\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma)
denotes the trace and
\|\Sigma\|
denotes the operator norm. For instance,
[
6
]
proved that, if
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
are independent copies of a centered
sub-Gaussian random vector
X
with covariance matrix
\Sigma
, then the
sample covariance satisfies, for every
u\geq 1
, with probability at least
1-\exp(-u)
,

\bigg\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\Sigma\bigg\|\lesssim\|\Sigma\|\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{r(\Sigma)+u}{N}}+\frac{r(\Sigma)+u}{N}\bigg).

This paper studies the corresponding problem for sample cross-covariances. Sample cross-covariance matrices arise just as naturally in multivariate statistics and
data analysis. They are central in multivariate regression, canonical correlation analysis, instrumental variables, and
operator learning problems where one observes paired input-output data.

Let
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
be independent copies of a centered random vector

(X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{X}+d_{Y}}
, with marginal covariance
matrices
\Sigma_{X}
and
\Sigma_{Y}
. We are interested in sharp
high-probability bounds for

\bigg\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}Y_{i}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}XY^{\top}\bigg\|.

Although this deviation is formally similar
to the corresponding deviation for the
sample covariance, its sharp tail behavior is not yet fully understood.
Indeed,
the off-diagonal structure and possible dependence between
X
and
Y
make the analysis of sample cross-covariances more delicate. Our goal is to put sample cross-covariances on the same theoretical footing as sample covariances.

2
Main Result

The Orlicz
\psi_{2}
norm and the

L_{p}
norm of a real-valued random variable
Z
are defined by

\|Z\|_{\psi_{2}}:=\inf\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{t>0:\mathbb{E}\exp(Z^{2}/t^{2})\leq 2}}\right\},\qquad\|Z\|_{L_{p}}:=\big(\mathbb{E}|Z|^{p}\big)^{1/p}.

We say that a random vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is sub-Gaussian if there exists
K>0
such
that

\|\langle X,v\rangle\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K\|\langle X,v\rangle\|_{L_{2}},\qquad\text{for any }v\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1},

where
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
denotes the unit sphere in
\mathbb{R}^{d}.

The smallest such constant
K>0
is called the sub-Gaussian constant of
X
. The sub-Gaussian constant of a centered Gaussian random variable is
K=\sqrt{8/3}
. For positive sequences
\{a_{n}\},\{b_{n}\}
, we write
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
to denote that, for some constant
c>0
,
a_{n}\leq cb_{n}.
If both
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
and
b_{n}\lesssim a_{n}
hold, we write
a_{n}\asymp b_{n}.
Throughout the paper, all covariance matrices are allowed to be singular, but are assumed to be nonzero.

Our main result establishes operator-norm concentration bounds for sample cross-covariances.

Theorem 2.1
.

Let
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
be i.i.d. copies of a centered random vector

(X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{X}+d_{Y}}
. Assume that
X
and
Y
are
sub-Gaussian random vectors with covariance matrices
\Sigma_{X}
and

\Sigma_{Y}
, and sub-Gaussian constants
K_{X},K_{Y}
. Set

r_{X}:=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{X})}{\|\Sigma_{X}\|},\qquad r_{Y}:=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{Y})}{\|\Sigma_{Y}\|}.

For every
u\geq 1
, it holds with probability at least
1-\exp(-u)
that

\bigg\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}Y_{i}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}XY^{\top}\bigg\|\lesssim K_{X}K_{Y}(\|\Sigma_{X}\|\|\Sigma_{Y}\|)^{1/2}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{r_{X}+r_{Y}+u}{N}}+\frac{\sqrt{(r_{X}+u)(r_{Y}+u)}}{N}\bigg).

As a corollary,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}Y^{\top}_{i}-\mathbb{E}XY^{\top}\bigg\|\lesssim K_{X}K_{Y}(\|\Sigma_{X}\|\|\Sigma_{Y}\|)^{1/2}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{r_{X}+r_{Y}}{N}}+\frac{\sqrt{r_{X}r_{Y}}}{N}\bigg).

Moreover, if
(X,Y)
is jointly Gaussian, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}Y^{\top}_{i}-\mathbb{E}XY^{\top}\bigg\|\asymp(\|\Sigma_{X}\|\|\Sigma_{Y}\|)^{1/2}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{r_{X}+r_{Y}}{N}}+\frac{\sqrt{r_{X}r_{Y}}}{N}\bigg).

We make a few remarks on Theorem
2.1
.

Remark 2.2
.

1.

The upper bound in Theorem
2.1
only requires
X
and
Y
to be
individually sub-Gaussian, while allowing arbitrary dependence between them.
The matching lower bound is proved in the jointly Gaussian case, again allowing
arbitrary dependence between
X
and
Y
. In particular, when
X=Y
, Theorem
2.1
recovers the dimension-free concentration inequalities for sample
covariances established in

[
6
, Theorems 4 and 9]
.

2.

In the case where
X
and
Y
are independent,

[
1
, Lemma A.3]
obtained a high-probability upper bound by
applying the covariance bound of
[
6
]
to
the concatenated vector
(X,Y)
. This argument, however, does not capture
the product structure of the cross-covariance fluctuation, such as the
factors

\sqrt{\|\Sigma_{X}\|\|\Sigma_{Y}\|}
and
\sqrt{r_{X}r_{Y}}
. In the more general setting where
X
and
Y
may be arbitrarily
dependent,
[
3
, Theorem 2.1]
obtained an in-expectation
upper bound for sub-Gaussian random vectors as a corollary of more general
concentration results for asymmetric tensors, without capturing the sharp tail behavior. The new contributions of
Theorem
2.1
are twofold. First, it gives a sharp high-probability
upper bound for sample cross-covariances; the proof is short and transparent, combining a standard quadratic
empirical process inequality with a scaled polarization argument and an
optimization over the scaling parameter. Second, in the jointly Gaussian
case, it establishes a matching lower bound allowing arbitrary correlation
between
X
and
Y
.

3.

The sharp upper and lower bounds in Theorem
2.1
show
that, in the Gaussian case, the size of the fluctuation is essentially
insensitive to the strength of the dependence between
X
and
Y
.
To see the intuition, write

\bigg\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}Y_{i}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}XY^{\top}\bigg\|=\sup_{\|v\|_{2}\leq 1,\,\|h\|_{2}\leq 1}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle X_{i},v\rangle\langle Y_{i},h\rangle-\mathbb{E}\langle X,v\rangle\langle Y,h\rangle}}\right|.

For fixed directions
v
and
h
,

(\langle X,v\rangle,\langle Y,h\rangle)
is jointly Gaussian, and
Isserlis’ formula
[
5
]
gives

\mathsf{Var}\bigl(\langle X,v\rangle\langle Y,h\rangle\bigr)=\mathbb{E}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\langle Y,h\rangle^{2}+\bigl(\mathbb{E}\langle X,v\rangle\langle Y,h\rangle\bigr)^{2}.

Therefore, by Cauchy–Schwarz,

\mathbb{E}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\langle Y,h\rangle^{2}\leq\mathsf{Var}\bigl(\langle X,v\rangle\langle Y,h\rangle\bigr)\leq 2\mathbb{E}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\langle Y,h\rangle^{2}.

Thus the variance of the product
\langle X,v\rangle\langle Y,h\rangle

is comparable to

\mathbb{E}\langle X,v\rangle^{2}\,\mathbb{E}\langle Y,h\rangle^{2}
, with universal
constants, independently of the covariance

\mathbb{E}\langle X,v\rangle\langle Y,h\rangle
. This explains why, in the
Gaussian case, the fluctuation scale is governed by the marginal
covariances of
X
and
Y
, rather than by the size of the
cross-covariance. Theorem
2.1
makes this intuition rigorous in
operator norm, where one takes the supremum over all unit vectors
v

and
h
.

∎

Proof of Theorem
2.1
.

The high-probability upper bound in the sub-Gaussian case follows from Theorem
3.1
. Write

X=\Sigma_{X}^{1/2}Z,\qquad Y=\Sigma_{Y}^{1/2}W,

where
Z
and
W
are isotropic sub-Gaussian random vectors. We apply Theorem
3.1
with

T=\Sigma_{X}^{1/2}\mathbb{S}^{d_{X}-1},\qquad S=\Sigma_{Y}^{1/2}\mathbb{S}^{d_{Y}-1}.

Using that

\operatorname{rad}(T)=\|\Sigma_{X}\|^{1/2},\qquad\gamma(T)\leq\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{X})^{1/2}=\|\Sigma_{X}\|^{1/2}r_{X}^{1/2},

and

\operatorname{rad}(S)=\|\Sigma_{Y}\|^{1/2},\qquad\gamma(S)\leq\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{Y})^{1/2}=\|\Sigma_{Y}\|^{1/2}r_{Y}^{1/2},

gives the desired bound.
The in-expectation upper bound follows by integrating the tail bound. The matching lower bound in the jointly Gaussian case follows from Proposition
4.1
.
∎

3
Upper Bound

We say that a random vector in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
is
isotropic if its covariance matrix is the identity matrix
I_{d}
. Next, let

g\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
be the standard multivariate Gaussian random vector. For a set
T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we define its radius, Gaussian complexity,
and stable dimension by

(3.1)

\displaystyle\operatorname{rad}(T):=\sup_{v\in T}\|v\|_{2},\qquad\gamma(T):=\mathbb{E}\sup_{v\in T}|\langle g,v\rangle|,\qquad d(T):=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\frac{\gamma(T)}{\operatorname{rad}(T)}}}\right)^{2}.

We refer to

[
11
, Chapter 7]
for background on these geometric
quantities.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
(Z_{i},W_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
be i.i.d. copies of a centered random
vector
(Z,W)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}+d_{W}}
. Assume that
Z
and
W
are isotropic
sub-Gaussian random vectors with sub-Gaussian constants
K_{Z}
and
K_{W}
. Let

T\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{Z}}
and
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d_{W}}
be bounded sets. For every
u\geq 1
, it holds with probability at least
1-\exp(-u)
that

\displaystyle\sup_{v\in T,\,h\in S}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle Z_{i},v\rangle\langle W_{i},h\rangle-\mathbb{E}\langle Z,v\rangle\langle W,h\rangle}}\right|

(3.2)

\displaystyle\lesssim K_{Z}K_{W}\operatorname{rad}(T)\operatorname{rad}(S)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\frac{d(T)+d(S)+u}{N}}+\frac{\sqrt{(d(T)+u)(d(S)+u)}}{N}}}\right).

Our proof of Theorem
3.1
is based on the following standard quadratic
empirical process bound (see e.g.,
[
4
, Corollary 5.7]
,
[
2
, Theorem 1]
,
[
6
, Theorem 8]
), combined with a scaled polarization argument and an
optimization over the scaling parameter. The scaling parameter in the polarization identity is used to preserve the
separate complexities of the two index sets
T
and
S
, as well as the
dependence on the confidence level
u
. This is essential for obtaining the
product term in the final bound.

Recall that, for a metric space
(T,d)
, Talagrand’s
\gamma_{2}
-functional
[
10
]
is defined by

\gamma_{2}(T,d):=\inf_{\{T_{n}\}}\sup_{t\in T}\sum_{n\geq 0}2^{n/2}d(t,T_{n}),

where
d(t,T_{n}):=\inf_{s\in T_{n}}d(t,s)
, and the infimum is taken over all
admissible sequences
\{T_{n}\}_{n\geq 0}
of subsets of
T
, that is, sequences
satisfying
|T_{0}|=1
and
|T_{n}|\leq 2^{2^{n}}
for
n\geq 1
.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\xi,\xi_{1},\ldots,\xi_{N}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\text{i.i.d.}}}{{\sim}}\mu
be a sequence of random variables on a probability space
(\Omega,\mu)
, and let
\mathcal{F}
be a class of functions on
(\Omega,\mu)
. Set

\Delta(\mathcal{F}):=\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\|f\|_{\psi_{2}},\qquad\Gamma(\mathcal{F}):=\gamma_{2}(\mathcal{F},\|\cdot\|_{\psi_{2}}).

Then, for every

u\geq 1
, with probability at least
1-\exp(-u)
,

\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f^{2}(\xi_{i})-\mathbb{E}f^{2}(\xi)}}\right|\lesssim\frac{\Gamma(\mathcal{F})\Delta(\mathcal{F})}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{\Gamma^{2}(\mathcal{F})}{N}+\Delta^{2}(\mathcal{F})\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\frac{u}{N}}+\frac{u}{N}}}\right).

Now we are ready to prove Theorem
3.1
.

Proof of Theorem
3.1
.

For
\lambda>0
, define the two function classes

\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{+}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(z,w)\mapsto\langle z,v\rangle+\lambda\langle w,h\rangle:v\in T,\ h\in S}}\right\},

and

\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{-}:=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\{(z,w)\mapsto\langle z,v\rangle-\lambda\langle w,h\rangle:v\in T,\ h\in S}}\right\}.

Since
Z
and
W
are centered, all functions in

\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\pm}
are centered. Moreover, by the triangle inequality
for the
\psi_{2}
-norm,

\Delta(\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\pm})\leq K_{Z}\operatorname{rad}(T)+\lambda K_{W}\operatorname{rad}(S).

Indeed, for
v\in T
and
h\in S
,

\|\langle Z,v\rangle\pm\lambda\langle W,h\rangle\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq\|\langle Z,v\rangle\|_{\psi_{2}}+\lambda\|\langle W,h\rangle\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K_{Z}\|v\|_{2}+\lambda K_{W}\|h\|_{2}.

We next bound the
\gamma_{2}
-functional of
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\pm}
.
For

f_{v,h}^{\pm},f_{v^{\prime},h^{\prime}}^{\pm}\in\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\pm}
,

\|f_{v,h}^{\pm}-f_{v^{\prime},h^{\prime}}^{\pm}\|_{\psi_{2}}\leq K_{Z}\|v-v^{\prime}\|_{2}+\lambda K_{W}\|h-h^{\prime}\|_{2}.

Hence admissible chains for
T
and
S
can be combined, with a one-level
shift to preserve admissibility, to form an
admissible chain for
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\pm}
, yielding

\Gamma(\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\pm})\lesssim K_{Z}\gamma_{2}(T,\|\cdot\|_{2})+\lambda K_{W}\gamma_{2}(S,\|\cdot\|_{2}).

By the majorizing measure theorem
[
10
, Theorem 2.10.1]
,

\gamma_{2}(T,\|\cdot\|_{2})\lesssim\gamma(T),\qquad\gamma_{2}(S,\|\cdot\|_{2})\lesssim\gamma(S),

where
\gamma(T)
and
\gamma(S)
are the Gaussian complexities of
T
and

S
, as defined in (
3.1
).
Consequently,

\Gamma(\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\pm})\lesssim K_{Z}\gamma(T)+\lambda K_{W}\gamma(S).

Set

A:=K_{Z}\gamma(T),\qquad B:=K_{W}\gamma(S),\qquad C:=K_{Z}\operatorname{rad}(T),\qquad D:=K_{W}\operatorname{rad}(S).

We may assume that
C>0
and
D>0
, as the case
\operatorname{rad}(T)=0
or
\operatorname{rad}(S)=0
is
trivial.
Then

\Gamma(\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\pm})\lesssim A+\lambda B,\qquad\Delta(\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{\pm})\leq C+\lambda D.

For every
v\in T
and
h\in S
, the scaled polarization identity gives

\langle z,v\rangle\langle w,h\rangle=\frac{(\langle z,v\rangle+\lambda\langle w,h\rangle)^{2}-(\langle z,v\rangle-\lambda\langle w,h\rangle)^{2}}{4\lambda},\qquad\lambda>0.

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sup_{v\in T,h\in S}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle Z_{i},v\rangle\langle W_{i},h\rangle-\mathbb{E}\langle Z,v\rangle\langle W,h\rangle}}\right|

\displaystyle\leq\frac{1}{4\lambda}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{+}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f^{2}(Z_{i},W_{i})-\mathbb{E}f^{2}(Z,W)}}\right|+\frac{1}{4\lambda}\sup_{f\in\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{-}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f^{2}(Z_{i},W_{i})-\mathbb{E}f^{2}(Z,W)}}\right|.

Applying Lemma
3.2
to
\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{+}
and

\mathcal{F}_{\lambda}^{-}
, and absorbing the union bound into the constants,
we obtain with probability at least
1-\exp(-u)
,

\displaystyle\sup_{v\in T,h\in S}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle Z_{i},v\rangle\langle W_{i},h\rangle-\mathbb{E}\langle Z,v\rangle\langle W,h\rangle}}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim\frac{1}{\lambda}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left[\frac{(A+\lambda B)(C+\lambda D)}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{(A+\lambda B)^{2}}{N}+(C+\lambda D)^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\frac{u}{N}}+\frac{u}{N}}}\right)}}\right].

For every fixed
u\geq 1
, we optimize over
\lambda
. Write the right-hand side as

\frac{P}{\lambda}+Q\lambda+R,

where

P:=\frac{AC}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{A^{2}}{N}+C^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\frac{u}{N}}+\frac{u}{N}}}\right),

Q:=\frac{BD}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{B^{2}}{N}+D^{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\frac{u}{N}}+\frac{u}{N}}}\right),

and

R:=\frac{AD+BC}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{2AB}{N}+2CD\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\frac{u}{N}}+\frac{u}{N}}}\right).

Choosing

\lambda=\sqrt{\frac{P}{Q}},

we get

\frac{P}{\lambda}+Q\lambda=2\sqrt{PQ}.

It remains to bound
\sqrt{PQ}
. Let

x:=\frac{A}{C\sqrt{N}},\qquad y:=\frac{B}{D\sqrt{N}},\qquad r:=\sqrt{\frac{u}{N}}.

Then

P=C^{2}(x+x^{2}+r+r^{2}),\qquad Q=D^{2}(y+y^{2}+r+r^{2}).

Since

x+x^{2}+r+r^{2}\leq(x+r)+(x+r)^{2},

and similarly for
y
, the elementary inequality

\sqrt{(a+a^{2})(b+b^{2})}\lesssim a+b+ab,\qquad a,b\geq 0,

with
a=x+r
and
b=y+r
, gives

\sqrt{PQ}\lesssim CD\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(x+y+r+xy+rx+ry+r^{2}}}\right).

Returning to
A,B,C,D
, this yields

\sqrt{PQ}\lesssim\frac{AD+BC+\sqrt{u}\,CD}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{(A+\sqrt{u}\,C)(B+\sqrt{u}\,D)}{N}.

The same expression also dominates
R
, up to universal constants. Hence

\displaystyle\sup_{v\in T,h\in S}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle Z_{i},v\rangle\langle W_{i},h\rangle-\mathbb{E}\langle Z,v\rangle\langle W,h\rangle}}\right|\lesssim\frac{AD+BC+\sqrt{u}\,CD}{\sqrt{N}}+\frac{(A+\sqrt{u}\,C)(B+\sqrt{u}\,D)}{N}.

Substituting the definitions of
A,B,C,D
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sup_{v\in T,h\in S}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle Z_{i},v\rangle\langle W_{i},h\rangle-\mathbb{E}\langle Z,v\rangle\langle W,h\rangle}}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim K_{Z}K_{W}\bigg(\frac{\gamma(T)\operatorname{rad}(S)+\gamma(S)\operatorname{rad}(T)+\sqrt{u}\,\operatorname{rad}(T)\operatorname{rad}(S)}{\sqrt{N}}

\displaystyle+\frac{(\gamma(T)+\sqrt{u}\,\operatorname{rad}(T))(\gamma(S)+\sqrt{u}\,\operatorname{rad}(S))}{N}\bigg).

Finally, using

\gamma(T)=\operatorname{rad}(T)\sqrt{d(T)},\qquad\gamma(S)=\operatorname{rad}(S)\sqrt{d(S)},

we get

\displaystyle\sup_{v\in T,h\in S}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\langle Z_{i},v\rangle\langle W_{i},h\rangle-\mathbb{E}\langle Z,v\rangle\langle W,h\rangle}}\right|

\displaystyle\lesssim K_{Z}K_{W}\operatorname{rad}(T)\operatorname{rad}(S)\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sqrt{\frac{d(T)+d(S)+u}{N}}+\frac{\sqrt{(d(T)+u)(d(S)+u)}}{N}}}\right).

This proves the theorem.
∎

4
Lower Bound

We now prove the matching lower bound for Gaussian data in
Theorem
2.1
. Importantly, the result does not require
X
and

Y
to be independent; arbitrary correlation between them is allowed.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let
(X_{i},Y_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
be i.i.d. copies of a centered Gaussian random
vector
(X,Y)\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{X}+d_{Y}}
.
Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\bigg\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}Y^{\top}_{i}-\mathbb{E}XY^{\top}\bigg\|\gtrsim(\|\Sigma_{X}\|\|\Sigma_{Y}\|)^{1/2}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{r_{X}+r_{Y}}{N}}+\frac{\sqrt{r_{X}r_{Y}}}{N}\bigg).

Proof of Proposition
4.1
.

Write the covariance matrix of the jointly Gaussian vector
(X,Y)
as

\begin{pmatrix}\Sigma_{X}&\Sigma_{XY}\\
\Sigma_{YX}&\Sigma_{Y}\end{pmatrix}.

By the Gaussian regression formula
[
9
, Section 2.1.7]
, there exist a matrix

L=\Sigma_{YX}\Sigma_{X}^{\dagger}
and a centered Gaussian vector
Z
,
independent of
X
, such that

Y=LX+Z.

Here
\Sigma_{X}^{\dagger}
denotes the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of
\Sigma_{X}
. Moreover,

\Sigma_{Y}=L\Sigma_{X}L^{\top}+\Sigma_{Z},

where
\Sigma_{Z}=\mathbb{E}ZZ^{\top}
. Hence, substituting
Y_{i}=LX_{i}+Z_{i}

for each
i
, we obtain

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}Y_{i}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}XY^{\top}=\underbrace{\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}XX^{\top}\bigg)L^{\top}}_{:=A_{N}}+\underbrace{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}Z_{i}^{\top}}_{:=B_{N}}.

Here
(X_{i},Z_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
are i.i.d. copies of
(X,Z)
with
X_{i}
independent
of
Z_{i}
.

We first note that the two terms
A_{N}
and
B_{N}
cannot cancel in expectation. Indeed,
conditioning on
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
, the matrix
A_{N}
is deterministic and

B_{N}
is a centered Gaussian random matrix. Thus, by Jensen’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}_{Z}\|A_{N}+B_{N}\|\geq\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|A_{N}+\mathbb{E}_{Z}B_{N}}}\right\|=\|A_{N}\|.

On the other hand, conditionally on
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
, the random matrices

B_{N}
and
-B_{N}
have the same distribution. Hence, by the triangle inequality,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{Z}\|A_{N}+B_{N}\|

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\|A_{N}+B_{N}\|+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\|A_{N}-B_{N}\|

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{Z}\big\|(A_{N}+B_{N})-(A_{N}-B_{N})\big\|

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{Z}\|B_{N}\|.

Taking expectation over
X_{1},\ldots,X_{N}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\|A_{N}+B_{N}\|\geq\max\{\mathbb{E}\|A_{N}\|,\mathbb{E}\|B_{N}\|\}\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\mathbb{E}\|A_{N}\|+\mathbb{E}\|B_{N}\|}}\right).

Therefore it remains to lower bound the two pieces separately.

Set

P:=L\Sigma_{X}L^{\top},\qquad Q:=\Sigma_{Z}.

Then
P,Q\succeq 0
and
P+Q=\Sigma_{Y}
.

For the correlated term
A_{N}
, Lemma
4.2
yields, if
P\neq 0,

\mathbb{E}\|A_{N}\|\gtrsim(\|\Sigma_{X}\|\|P\|)^{1/2}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{r_{X}+r_{P}}{N}}+\frac{\sqrt{r_{X}r_{P}}}{N}\bigg)=\|\Sigma_{X}\|^{1/2}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\|P\|r_{X}+\mathrm{Tr}(P)}{N}}+\frac{\sqrt{r_{X}\mathrm{Tr}(P)}}{N}\bigg),

where
r_{P}:=\mathrm{Tr}(P)/\|P\|.
If
P=0,
we simply bound
\mathbb{E}\|A_{N}\|\geq 0.

For the independent residual term

B_{N}
, the independent Gaussian cross-covariance bound
[
3
, Theorem 2.1]
gives

\mathbb{E}\|B_{N}\|\asymp(\|\Sigma_{X}\|\|Q\|)^{1/2}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{r_{X}+r_{Q}}{N}}+\frac{\sqrt{r_{X}r_{Q}}}{N}\bigg)=\|\Sigma_{X}\|^{1/2}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\|Q\|r_{X}+\mathrm{Tr}(Q)}{N}}+\frac{\sqrt{r_{X}\mathrm{Tr}(Q)}}{N}\bigg),

where
r_{Q}:=\mathrm{Tr}(Q)/\|Q\|.

It remains to combine the two scales.
Since
P,Q\succeq 0
and
P+Q=\Sigma_{Y}
, we have

\|P\|+\|Q\|\geq\|\Sigma_{Y}\|,\qquad\mathrm{Tr}(P)+\mathrm{Tr}(Q)=\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{Y}).

Consequently,

\sqrt{\|P\|}+\sqrt{\|Q\|}\geq\sqrt{\|\Sigma_{Y}\|},\qquad\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(P)}+\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(Q)}\geq\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{Y})}.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\|A_{N}+B_{N}\|\geq\frac{1}{2}(\mathbb{E}\|A_{N}\|+\mathbb{E}\|B_{N}\|)\gtrsim\|\Sigma_{X}\|^{1/2}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{\|\Sigma_{Y}\|r_{X}+\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{Y})}{N}}+\frac{\sqrt{r_{X}\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{Y})}}{N}\bigg).

Using
r_{Y}=\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{Y})/\|\Sigma_{Y}\|
,
the right-hand side is equivalent, up to universal constants, to

(\|\Sigma_{X}\|\|\Sigma_{Y}\|)^{1/2}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{r_{X}+r_{Y}}{N}}+\frac{\sqrt{r_{X}r_{Y}}}{N}\bigg).

This proves the desired lower bound.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
(X_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
be i.i.d. copies of a centered Gaussian random
vector
X\in\mathbb{R}^{d_{X}}
with covariance matrix
\Sigma_{X}
, and let

L
be a fixed matrix such that
P:=L\Sigma_{X}L^{\top}
is nonzero. Then

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\mathbb{E}XX^{\top}\bigg)L^{\top}}}\right\|\gtrsim(\|\Sigma_{X}\|\|P\|)^{1/2}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{r_{X}+r_{P}}{N}}+\frac{\sqrt{r_{X}r_{P}}}{N}\bigg),

where

r_{X}:=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{X})}{\|\Sigma_{X}\|},\qquad r_{P}:=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(P)}{\|P\|}.

Proof of Lemma
4.2
.

We first prove the contribution of order
N^{-1}
. By conditioning on

X_{1}
and averaging over
X_{2},\ldots,X_{N}
, Jensen’s inequality gives

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\Sigma_{X}\bigg)L^{\top}}}\right\|\geq\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\|(XX^{\top}-\Sigma_{X})L^{\top}\|.

By the triangle inequality,

\mathbb{E}\|(XX^{\top}-\Sigma_{X})L^{\top}\|\geq\mathbb{E}\|XX^{\top}L^{\top}\|-\|\Sigma_{X}L^{\top}\|.

Since

\|XX^{\top}L^{\top}\|=\|X\|\,\|LX\|,

we lower bound
\mathbb{E}\|X\|\,\|LX\|
using the Gaussian correlation
inequality
[
8
,
7
]
. Indeed, for
s,t\geq 0
, the sets

\{x:\|x\|\leq s\},\qquad\{x:\|Lx\|\leq t\}

are symmetric and convex. Hence the Gaussian correlation inequality implies

\mathbb{P}(\|X\|\leq s,\|LX\|\leq t)\geq\mathbb{P}(\|X\|\leq s)\mathbb{P}(\|LX\|\leq t).

Equivalently,

\mathbb{P}(\|X\|>s,\|LX\|>t)\geq\mathbb{P}(\|X\|>s)\mathbb{P}(\|LX\|>t).

Using the layer-cake representation,

\mathbb{E}\|X\|\,\|LX\|=\int_{0}^{\infty}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}(\|X\|>s,\|LX\|>t)\,ds\,dt,

we obtain

\mathbb{E}\|X\|\,\|LX\|\geq\mathbb{E}\|X\|\,\mathbb{E}\|LX\|.

Moreover, for centered Gaussian vectors,

\mathbb{E}\|X\|\asymp\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{X})},\qquad\mathbb{E}\|LX\|\asymp\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(L\Sigma_{X}L^{\top})}=\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(P)}.

Therefore

\mathbb{E}\|X\|\,\|LX\|\geq c_{0}\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{X})\mathrm{Tr}(P)}.

On the other hand,

\|\Sigma_{X}L^{\top}\|=\|\Sigma_{X}^{1/2}\Sigma_{X}^{1/2}L^{\top}\|\leq\|\Sigma_{X}\|^{1/2}\|\Sigma_{X}^{1/2}L^{\top}\|=\|\Sigma_{X}\|^{1/2}\|L\Sigma_{X}L^{\top}\|^{1/2}.

Thus, writing

(4.1)

A:=(\|\Sigma_{X}\|\|P\|)^{1/2},\qquad R:=\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{X})\mathrm{Tr}(P)}=A\sqrt{r_{X}r_{P}},

we have shown

(4.2)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\Sigma_{X}\bigg)L^{\top}}}\right\|\geq\frac{c_{0}R-A}{N}.

We next prove the contribution of order
N^{-1/2}
. Choose

w\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{Y}-1}
such that

w^{\top}Pw=\|P\|.

Set

\ell:=L^{\top}w,\qquad\eta:=\langle X,\ell\rangle,\qquad\sigma^{2}:=\mathbb{E}\eta^{2}=\ell^{\top}\Sigma_{X}\ell=\|P\|.

Then

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\Sigma_{X}\bigg)L^{\top}}}\right\|\geq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\Sigma_{X}\bigg)\ell}}\right\|=\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(X_{i}\eta_{i}-\mathbb{E}X\eta\bigr)}}\right\|.

We orthogonalize
X
with respect to
\eta
. Write

X=\frac{\Sigma_{X}\ell}{\sigma^{2}}\eta+X^{\perp},

where
X^{\perp}
is centered Gaussian and independent of
\eta
, with covariance

\Sigma_{\perp}=\Sigma_{X}-\frac{\Sigma_{X}\ell\ell^{\top}\Sigma_{X}}{\sigma^{2}}.

Indeed,

\mathbb{E}X^{\perp}\eta=\mathbb{E}X\eta-\frac{\Sigma_{X}\ell}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\eta^{2}=\Sigma_{X}\ell-\Sigma_{X}\ell=0,

and uncorrelated jointly Gaussian random vectors are independent.
Thus

X\eta-\mathbb{E}X\eta=\frac{\Sigma_{X}\ell}{\sigma^{2}}(\eta^{2}-\sigma^{2})+X^{\perp}\eta.

Consequently,

\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(X_{i}\eta_{i}-\mathbb{E}X\eta\bigr)=U_{N}+V_{N},

where

U_{N}:=\frac{\Sigma_{X}\ell}{\sigma^{2}}\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\eta_{i}^{2}-\sigma^{2}),\qquad V_{N}:=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}^{\perp}\eta_{i}.

Conditionally on
(\eta_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
,
U_{N}
is deterministic and
V_{N}

is a centered Gaussian vector. Hence, by Jensen’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}_{X^{\perp}}\|U_{N}+V_{N}\|\geq\|U_{N}\|.

Also, by symmetry of
V_{N}
,

\mathbb{E}_{X^{\perp}}\|U_{N}+V_{N}\|=\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{X^{\perp}}\|U_{N}+V_{N}\|+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}_{X^{\perp}}\|U_{N}-V_{N}\|\geq\mathbb{E}_{X^{\perp}}\|V_{N}\|.

Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\|U_{N}+V_{N}\|\geq\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\|U_{N}\|+\frac{1}{2}\mathbb{E}\|V_{N}\|.

We now estimate the two terms. Since
\eta_{i}/\sigma
are i.i.d. standard
Gaussians,

\mathbb{E}\|U_{N}\|=\frac{\|\Sigma_{X}\ell\|}{\sigma^{2}}\mathbb{E}\bigg|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\eta_{i}^{2}-\sigma^{2})\bigg|\asymp\frac{\|\Sigma_{X}\ell\|}{\sqrt{N}}.

Conditionally on
(\eta_{i})_{i=1}^{N}
,

V_{N}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigg(0,\,\frac{1}{N^{2}}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\eta_{i}^{2}\,\Sigma_{\perp}\bigg).

For a centered Gaussian vector
G
with covariance matrix
\Gamma
, we use

\mathbb{E}\|G\|\asymp\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\Gamma)}.

Hence

\mathbb{E}\|V_{N}\|\asymp\frac{1}{N}\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\sum_{i=1}^{N}\eta_{i}^{2}}}\right)^{1/2}\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{\perp})}\asymp\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{N}}\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{\perp})}.

It follows that

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(X_{i}\eta_{i}-\mathbb{E}X\eta\bigr)}}\right\|\gtrsim\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|\Sigma_{X}\ell\|+\sigma\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{\perp})}}}\right).

Now

\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{\perp})=\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{X})-\frac{\|\Sigma_{X}\ell\|^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}.

Therefore, setting
a:=\|\Sigma_{X}\ell\|/\sigma
, we have

(4.3)

\|\Sigma_{X}\ell\|+\sigma\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{\perp})}=\sigma\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(a+\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{X})-a^{2}}}}\right)\geq\sigma\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{X})}.

Since
\sigma^{2}=\|P\|
, we obtain

(4.4)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\Sigma_{X}\bigg)L^{\top}}}\right\|\gtrsim\sqrt{\frac{\|P\|\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma_{X})}{N}}=(\|\Sigma_{X}\|\|P\|)^{1/2}\sqrt{\frac{r_{X}}{N}}.

We now prove the other
N^{-1/2}
contribution. Choose

v\in\mathbb{S}^{d_{X}-1}
such that

v^{\top}\Sigma_{X}v=\|\Sigma_{X}\|.

Set

\xi:=\langle X,v\rangle,\qquad\tau^{2}:=\mathbb{E}\xi^{2}=\|\Sigma_{X}\|.

Since

\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\Sigma_{X}\bigg)L^{\top}}}\right\|=\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|L\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\Sigma_{X}\bigg)}}\right\|,

we have

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\Sigma_{X}\bigg)L^{\top}}}\right\|\geq\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|L\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\Sigma_{X}\bigg)v}}\right\|.

The right-hand side equals

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(LX_{i}\xi_{i}-\mathbb{E}LX\,\xi\bigr)}}\right\|.

We now orthogonalize
LX
with respect to
\xi
. Write

LX=\frac{L\Sigma_{X}v}{\tau^{2}}\xi+Z,

where
Z
is centered Gaussian and independent of
\xi
, with covariance

P_{\perp}=P-\frac{L\Sigma_{X}vv^{\top}\Sigma_{X}L^{\top}}{\tau^{2}}.

Repeating the preceding argument gives

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\bigl(LX_{i}\xi_{i}-\mathbb{E}LX\,\xi\bigr)}}\right\|\gtrsim\frac{1}{\sqrt{N}}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left(\|L\Sigma_{X}v\|+\tau\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(P_{\perp})}}}\right).

Since

\mathrm{Tr}(P_{\perp})=\mathrm{Tr}(P)-\frac{\|L\Sigma_{X}v\|^{2}}{\tau^{2}},

the same elementary inequality as in (
4.3
) gives

\|L\Sigma_{X}v\|+\tau\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(P_{\perp})}\geq\tau\sqrt{\mathrm{Tr}(P)}.

Thus

(4.5)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\Sigma_{X}\bigg)L^{\top}}}\right\|\gtrsim\sqrt{\frac{\|\Sigma_{X}\|\mathrm{Tr}(P)}{N}}=(\|\Sigma_{X}\|\|P\|)^{1/2}\sqrt{\frac{r_{P}}{N}}.

Combining (
4.4
) and (
4.5
) yields

(4.6)

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\Sigma_{X}\bigg)L^{\top}}}\right\|\gtrsim(\|\Sigma_{X}\|\|P\|)^{1/2}\sqrt{\frac{r_{X}+r_{P}}{N}}.

Finally, we combine the
N^{-1}
estimate (
4.2
) with the

N^{-1/2}
estimate (
4.6
) using the convex-combination trick. From
(
4.6
) and
r_{X},r_{P}\geq 1
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\Sigma_{X}\bigg)L^{\top}}}\right\|\geq c_{1}A\sqrt{\frac{r_{X}+r_{P}}{N}}\geq c_{1}\frac{A}{N}.

Together with (
4.2
), for every
t\in[0,1]
,

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\Sigma_{X}\bigg)L^{\top}}}\right\|\geq t\frac{c_{0}R-A}{N}+(1-t)c_{1}\frac{A}{N}.

Choose
t>0
sufficiently small so that

(1-t)c_{1}-t\geq 0.

Then, using (
4.1
),

(4.7)

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\Sigma_{X}\bigg)L^{\top}}}\right\|\geq\frac{tc_{0}R}{N}\gtrsim(\|\Sigma_{X}\|\|P\|)^{1/2}\frac{\sqrt{r_{X}r_{P}}}{N}.

Combining (
4.6
) and (
4.7
), and recalling that

P=L\Sigma_{X}L^{\top}
, we obtain

\mathbb{E}\mathopen{}\mathclose{{\left\|\bigg(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}X_{i}X_{i}^{\top}-\Sigma_{X}\bigg)L^{\top}}}\right\|\gtrsim(\|\Sigma_{X}\|\|L\Sigma_{X}L^{\top}\|)^{1/2}\bigg(\sqrt{\frac{r_{X}+r_{P}}{N}}+\frac{\sqrt{r_{X}r_{P}}}{N}\bigg).

This proves the lemma.
∎
