Wrestler's Heart: Cardiac Remodeling in World Wrestling Entertainment Athletes
Authors: Lorenzo‐Lupo Dei, Nithiwat Tangchompoo, Tiziana Cristina Minopoli, Michael Papadakis, Sanjay Sharma, Gherardo Finocchiaro
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence on cardiac adaptation in elite wrestlers remains limited. This study assessed electrical and structural cardiac remodeling in elite wrestlers, with particular focus on sex‐specific differences.MethodsElite World Wrestling Entertainment athletes undergoing electrocardiographic and echocardiographic evaluation during routine cardiovascular screening were included. Left ventricular (LV) geometry was classified according to relative wall thickness and LV mass on the basis of international recommendations. This was a retrospective analysis of prospectively acquired data.ResultsThe cohort included 68 athletes (mean age, 28±6 years; 47 [69%] men), with similar weekly training volume between sexes (11.6±5 h/wk). No athlete reported performance‐enhancing drug use, although 1 admitted high‐energy drink consumption. Electrocardiographic abnormalities were observed in 2 athletes (3%). Women showed smaller absolute LV end‐diastolic diameter (46±4 versus 52±5 mm, P<0.001), but greater indexed LV dimensions (27±3 versus 25±3 mm/m2, P=0.003). Two female athletes had LV ejection fraction <50%. Right ventricular enlargement was observed in 23% of men and 14% of women (P=0.52), without sex differences in right ventricular fractional area change (39±6% versus 40±6%, P=0.42). Two male athletes had right ventricular fractional area change <34%. LV geometry was predominantly normal (89% men, 95% women), while concentric remodeling was identified in 6 athletes (9%), including 5 men and 1 woman.ConclusionsElite wrestlers demonstrate predominantly normal cardiac electrical and structural profiles, with low prevalence of pathological findings. Sex‐related differences were observed in cardiac dimensions, particularly after indexation to body surface area.Keywords: echocardiography, electrocardiography, energy drinks, performance‐enhancing substances, retrospective studies, stroke volume, ventricular remodelingSubject Categories: Epidemiology, Exercise, Women

Long‐term high‐intensity training induces a well‐characterized spectrum of structural, functional, and electrical cardiac adaptations collectively referred to as “athlete's heart.” 1 , 2 , 3 Differentiating physiological adaptations from pathologies such as cardiomyopathies represents a central challenge in sports cardiology, particularly in the context of preparticipation screening and prevention of exercise‐related sudden cardiac death. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8

Different types of sports are associated with distinct patterns of cardiac remodeling. 9 Endurance disciplines, such as cycling, are linked to increased left ventricular (LV) cavity size and eccentric remodeling. 10 In contrast, power sports, such as weightlifting, are historically associated with concentric remodeling, characterized by increased LV wall thickness. 9 However, this binary classification has been increasingly challenged. 11 Emerging evidence suggests that cardiac adaptations in power athletes are more heterogeneous than previously thought, with some individuals exhibiting mixed or even eccentric features 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 (Table 1). Sex‐based differences further complicate the interpretation of cardiac remodeling. In both sexes, the degree of physiological remodeling appears proportional to exercise volume, regardless of whether the sport is endurance or strength based. 19 Female athletes typically demonstrate less pronounced LV remodeling, with LV wall thickness and cavity dimensions rarely exceeding thresholds suggestive of primary cardiomyopathies. 19 Wrestling participation has risen markedly in recent years, with male youth involvement increasing significantly across Europe and female participation showing sustained growth, particularly in adolescence. 20 This shift underscores the need to better understand the cardiovascular demands and adaptations associated with wrestling, especially given its unique blend of dynamic and static exertion and the paucity of sex‐specific data. This study aims to evaluate cardiac anatomic and electrical remodeling in highly trained male and female wrestlers using both echocardiography and electrocardiography, with a focus on identifying sex‐related differences.

Methods

Data Availability

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Setting

The United Kingdom does not support a state‐sponsored cardiac screening program for the general population or athletes. However, the charitable organization Cardiac Risk in the Young (www.c‐ry.org.uk) has an established cardiac screening program for young individuals and also serves many professional sporting organizations in the United Kingdom. The preliminary investigation in athletes comprises a health questionnaire, physical evaluation, ECG, and echocardiogram, and is performed by cardiologists experienced in athletes' evaluation. The details of the screening program are described elsewhere. 21 Between 2020 and 2022, all professional WWE European training division athletes who underwent cardiovascular screening performed by Cardiac Risk in the Young were included in the study. These years correspond to the entire period during which Cardiac Risk in the Young provided cardiovascular evaluations for this organization. No specific exclusion criteria were applied, and no data were missing: Clinical questionnaires, physical examinations, 12‐lead ECGs, and echocardiograms were available and complete for all 68 athletes.

Electrocardiography

Standard 12‐lead ECGs were performed as described elsewhere. 22 Electrocardiographic criteria for LV hypertrophy were defined via the Sokolow–Lyon voltage criteria as the sum of S in V1+R in V5 or V6 ≥35 mm. ST‐segment shift was considered significant if ≥0.1 mV in ≥2 contiguous leads. T‐wave inversion (TWI) was considered abnormal if ≥0.1 mV in ≥2 contiguous leads. The Q wave was defined as abnormal when having a duration ≥40 milliseconds or a Q/R ratio >0.25. The QT interval was considered prolonged if >480 milliseconds. Left atrial (LA) enlargement was defined by a P‐wave duration ≥0.12 seconds associated with a terminal P negativity in lead V1 of duration ≥0.04 seconds and depth ≥1 mm. The ECG was interpreted according to the 2017 International Criteria. 23

Echocardiography

Two‐dimensional echocardiography was performed in every athlete. Digitized images of 3 beats were stored and reported by expert sonographers. They were subsequently revaluated by 2 expert cardiologists in accordance with the European Society of Echocardiography guidelines. The second analysis was blinded to the clinical characteristics of the athletes. Furthermore, the echocardiographic evaluation was performed independently, thereby ensuring a second level of blinding. Standard views were obtained as previously described. 24 LV diastolic diameter, septal thickness, and posterior wall thickness were measured from 2‐dimensional images in the parasternal long‐axis view at end‐diastole. Care was taken to exclude accessory structures (eg, septal bands and posterior wall chordae) from measurements. For this analysis, LV wall thickness >11 mm in women and >12 mm in men was considered above the typical reference range, acknowledging that such values may still fall within the spectrum of physiological remodeling in athletes.

LV cavity size was indexed by body surface area (BSA) and considered increased if >31 mm/m2. Relative wall thickness (RWT) was defined as the ratio of the sum of the interventricular septum and posterior wall thickness in end diastole to the LV end‐diastolic diameter (LVEDD). RWT was considered abnormal if >0.42. 24

In accordance with the American Society of Echocardiography, LV mass was calculated as follows: LV mass = [0.8 × 1.04 [(LVEDD + interventricular septum + posterior wall thickness)3 − (LVEDD)3]] + 0.6 g. LV mass >95 g/m2 in women and >115 g/m2 in men was deemed abnormal. 24 LV volumes and LV ejection fraction were assessed from the apical views, using the Simpson's method. LV systolic function <50% was considered to be reduced. 25

Athletes were subsequently divided into 4 groups based on the RWT and LV mass 14 , 16 : (1) normal (normal LV mass/normal RWT); (2) concentric hypertrophy (increased LV mass/increased RWT); (3) eccentric hypertrophy (increased LV mass/normal RWT); (4) concentric remodeling (normal LV mass/increased RWT).

Diastolic function was assessed by pulsed‐wave Doppler across the mitral valve and tissue Doppler velocity imaging of the septal and lateral mitral valve annulus 26 and considered altered if average septal/lateral E' <10 cm/s or E/E' ratio >15.

Right ventricular (RV) assessment was performed using the apical 4‐chamber view and its RV‐focused modification. RV basal diameter, tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion, tissue Doppler S′ velocity of the lateral tricuspid annulus, and fractional area change were measured. RV diameters (RVDs) were obtained at 3 levels from the apical 4‐chamber view: basal (RVD1), midcavity (RVD2), and longitudinal length from base to apex (RVD3). RV end‐diastolic area (RVEDA) was traced in the apical 4‐chamber view, and the indexed value (RVEDAi) was calculated as RVEDA divided by body surface area. RV outflow tract (RVOT) dimensions were measured in the parasternal long‐axis view for the proximal portion (RVOT1) and in the parasternal short‐axis view for the distal portion (RVOT2). Abnormal RV function was defined according to American Society of Echocardiography guidelines 27 as follows: tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion <17 mm, systolic velocity at the lateral tricuspid annulus (S′) <9.5 cm/s, and fractional area change <35%. Abnormal RV size was defined as RVEDA >20 cm2 and RVEDAi >11 cm2/m2. 27

LA volume, right atrial area, and anteroposterior LA diameter were assessed from apical 4‐chamber and parasternal long‐axis views, respectively. To obtain LA volume index LA volume was indexed by BSA and considered abnormal if >34 mL/m2. Right atrial area >18 cm2 and LA anteroposterior diameter >2.3 cm were considered abnormal.

Ethical Approval/Consent

Ethical approval was granted by the National Research Ethics Service, Essex 2 Research Ethics Committee in the United Kingdom. Written consent was obtained from individuals aged ≥16 years; no athlete aged <16 years was included in the study.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the SPSS software version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY). The results are expressed as a mean±SD for continuous variables and as number of cases and percentages for categorical variables. For comparison between the groups, t tests were used for continuous variables with adjustment for unequal variance if needed; for categorical variables, χ2 tests were used. Echocardiographic measurements were performed by 3 independent observers (sonographer plus 2 different cardiologists), while ECG interpretation was conducted by a single investigator. Interobserver variability was quantified with the use of mean differences and intraclass correlation coefficients.

Results

Athlete Demographics

The study population consisted of 68 wrestlers (47 [69%] men); 56 (82%) were classified as White individuals, 7 (10%) as Black individuals (5 men, 2 women), and 5 (7%) as Asian individuals (4 men, 1 woman). The mean age of the athletes was 28±6 years in men and 24±4 in women; all athletes were aged >18 years. Men exhibited a greater BSA than women (2.1±0.2 versus 1.7±0.2, P=<0.001).

Male athletes reported an average weekly training time of 11.5±4.4 hours; female athletes trained for 12.0±6.4 h/wk (P=0.49). The average number of years of sport participation was 16.4±6.2 years for men and 9.7±5.8 years for women (P=< 0.001).

All athletes reported being asymptomatic from a cardiac standpoint, with no history of chest pain, dyspnea, palpitations, or syncope. However, 2 male athletes reported experiencing palpitations; notably, 1 of them disclosed regular consumption of “high doses” of energy drinks. Out of the entire cohort, 4 athletes reported regular medication use. Two individuals used inhalers for asthma management, 1 was taking ursodeoxycholic acid, and 1 reported use of an oral contraceptive pill. Importantly, no athlete disclosed the use of performance‐enhancing drugs.

Left Ventricle

LVEDD was significantly lower in women (46±4 versus 52±5 mm, P<0.001). When LVEDD was indexed for BSA, women showed higher LV size (27±3 versus 25±3 mm/m2, P=0.015). Two (9%) female athletes exhibited indexed LVEDD >31 mm/m2. Overall, LV ejection fraction was normal and similar between groups (men, 59±7%, women, 58±6%), with only 2 female athletes, showing an LV ejection fraction <50% (48% in both cases).

Most athletes exhibited normal LV geometry, with no significant difference between sexes (95% of women compared with 89% of men, P=0.431). Women demonstrated significantly lower LV mass compared with men (58±15 compared with 82±24 g/m2, P<0.001), whereas RWT was similar between sexes. An RWT >0.42 was observed in 5% of women and 11% of men (P=0.658). While no athletes met the criteria for concentric hypertrophy or eccentric remodeling, concentric remodeling was identified in 6 athletes (9%), including 5 men and 1 woman (Figure 1).

Diastolic function parameters, including E/A ratio, lateral e' velocity, and E/e' were similar between the sexes and within normal ranges for all athletes. None of the athletes showed abnormal indices of diastolic function.

Right Ventricle

Male athletes demonstrated significantly larger RVOT dimensions at both the proximal (RVOT1: 30±3 versus 27±4 mm, P=0.001) and distal levels (RVOT2: 21±2.5 versus 19±4 mm, P=0.083). All measured RV diameters (RVD1, RVD2, and RVD3) were significantly greater in men compared with women (all P<0.001), with the largest difference observed in RVD3 (76±8 versus 63±7 mm, P<0.001; Figure 2). However, when indexed to BSA, these sex‐related differences were no longer statistically significant.

Mean RVEDA was 20±4 cm2 and mean RVEDAi 10±2 cm2/m2. There was no significant difference between sexes as far as RVEDAi was concerned (P=0.37). An enlarged RVEDAi (>11 cm2/m2) was observed in 11 of 47 men (23%) and in 3 of 21 women (14%).

Male athletes showed significantly higher tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion values (25±4 versus 22±3 mm, P=0.006) and tricuspid S′ velocities (14±3 versus 12±2 cm/s, P=0.002), indicating more pronounced longitudinal RV systolic excursion.

RV systolic function, assessed by fractional area change, was also comparable between groups (40±6% in women versus 39±6% inmen, P=0.529), with only 2 female athletes (10%) exhibiting fractional area change values <35%.

Follow‐Up

Further evaluation was not deemed necessary at the time of initial screening, as none of the athletes showed overtly pathological findings or reported cardiac symptoms. The athlete with inferior TWI (lead III and aVF with spared lead II) had a normal echocardiogram, and further investigations were not performed. Athletes with borderline or mildly reduced resting systolic function underwent brief on‐the‐spot brisk exercise, after which repeat echocardiography demonstrated improved contractility. All athletes underwent routine annual occupational medical surveillance including 12‐lead ECG and echocardiography. None of the athletes exhibited abnormal findings to warrant further investigations (including cardiac magnetic resonance imaging).

Discussion

This study provides the first dedicated evaluation of sex‐specific cardiac remodeling in WWE European training division wrestlers, a population that has been largely overlooked in the sports cardiology literature. Our findings demonstrate that most WWE European training division wrestlers exhibit normal cardiac geometry and function, with subtle sex‐related differences in both left and right heart parameters.

Previous studies on athlete's hearts have primarily focused on endurance and traditional power sports, such as cycling, rowing, and weightlifting. 10 , 28 , 29 The Morganroth hypothesis, 9 which proposed divergent remodeling patterns, eccentric hypertrophy in endurance athletes and concentric hypertrophy in strength athletes, has long served as a conceptual framework. However, subsequent research has challenged this binary model, revealing a more nuanced spectrum of adaptation influenced by training load, sport type, and individual factors such as sex and body size. 19 , 30 Our study adds to this evolving narrative by characterizing the cardiac phenotype of wrestlers, who engage in a unique combination of anaerobic bursts, isometric strain, and high‐impact maneuvers.

In our cohort, concentric remodeling was observed in 9% of athletes, predominantly men, while no cases of eccentric or concentric hypertrophy were identified. This aligns with prior echocardiographic studies in strength‐trained populations. 15

Notably, while absolute LV dimensions were larger in men, indexed LVEDD was significantly greater in women. While such sex‐based differences in indexed parameters have been described in endurance athletes, 31 they have not been reported in strength‐focused disciplines such as wrestling. Potential mechanisms for sex‐related differences in cardiac remodeling include anthropometrics, 32 sex hormone influence, 33 and peak exercise blood pressure. 34 However, recent work has shown that sex‐related differences in cardiac dimensions diminish when indexing is based on fat‐free mass rather than BSA, highlighting the importance of body composition in interpreting remodeling patterns. 35

Diastolic function was preserved across all athletes, with no evidence of impaired relaxation or elevated filling pressures. This finding reinforces the role of diastolic assessment in differentiating physiological cardiac adaptation from early cardiomyopathic processes, particularly in contexts where wall thickness and chamber geometry may overlap. 36

RV remodeling appeared more pronounced in men when assessed using absolute values; however, these sex‐based differences were no longer evident after indexing for BSA. This indexing effect stands in contrast to endurance athletes, where sex‐based differences in RV remodeling often persist even after adjustment for body size. Male endurance athletes consistently demonstrate larger indexed RV volumes and mass, reflecting a more robust chamber adaptation to prolonged dynamic exercise. 37 Importantly, RV systolic function remained preserved across the cohort, with only 2 female athletes exhibiting a mild reduction in RV fractional area change, neither of whom had associated ECG abnormalities, LV systolic dysfunction or clinical symptoms. Only 1% of athletes had an abnormal ECG according to international criteria. 23 Inferior TWI was observed in only 1 male athlete and the echocardiogram did not reveal any structural or functional abnormality.

None of the athletes in our cohort declared the use of performance‐enhancing or recreational substances; however, substance use was assessed solely through self‐report, and no objective testing was performed as part of the screening protocol. As such, underreporting cannot be excluded. In response to high‐profile cases in the sport, organizations such as WWE have introduced wellness policies that include drug testing, rehabilitation pathways, and mental health support. While these initiatives may reflect increased attention to athlete health, systematic data on substance use prevalence in this population remain limited and were not collected in the present study.

Clinical Implications

This study provides novel insights into the cardiovascular phenotype of elite wrestlers, a population previously underrepresented in sports cardiology literature. Despite high training volumes and prolonged exposure to strength‐dominant activity, most wrestlers exhibit physiological cardiac remodeling with preserved biventricular function. The identification of concentric remodeling in a minority of male athletes, in the absence of pathological hypertrophy, supports the notion that such adaptations represent a normal adaptive finding in these athletes. Importantly, the observation that female athletes demonstrated proportionally larger indexed LV cavity dimensions highlights the need for sex‐specific reference values when interpreting echocardiographic data in athletes. This is particularly relevant in preparticipation screening, where misclassification of physiological adaptation as pathology can lead to unnecessary investigations or restriction from sports.

Limitations

Limitations include the retrospective design, modest sample size, and absence of follow‐up or longitudinal data. Echocardiographic assessment did not include strain analysis. Data on training regimens were self‐reported. Direct assessment of body composition was not performed. Blood pressure data were not available, precluding assessment of hypertension prevalence. The potential influence of supplement use cannot be excluded; however, no athlete reported use of anabolic agents, and only 1 reported energy drink consumption. Ethnic diversity was limited, precluding meaningful subgroup analysis. Given that WWE wrestling includes choreographed and intermittent performance elements, the nature of exertion may differ from other power or mixed‐discipline sports involving more sustained cardiovascular loading, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Despite these limitations, the use of standardized protocols, complete data sets, and blinded analysis in a defined elite cohort strengthens the validity of our observations.

Conclusions

The cardiac phenotype observed in elite wrestlers is largely physiological, shaped by sustained hemodynamic stress and high‐intensity resistance training. A minority of athletes, primarily men, exhibit concentric remodeling; however, in the present cohort, none exhibited LV wall thickness exceeding 12 mm, supporting the absence of overt pathological hypertrophy. Sex‐specific differences were evident in absolute LV and RV dimensions, with men demonstrating larger chamber sizes. However, after indexing to BSA, these differences were no longer apparent for RV measurements, while indexed LV dimensions were greater in women.
