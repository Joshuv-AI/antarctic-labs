A pan-cancer functional atlas of ADH1A reveals its conserved role in the tumor microenvironment and immunity
Authors: Yunna Zheng, Fan Yang, Lu Yuan, Jihong Huang, Dong Yu, Junyi Lu, Laiyu Liu, Wenqi Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe human alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) gene family is increasingly implicated in tumorigenesis, yet the roles of ADH1A across diverse solid tumors remain largely uncharacterized. This study aimed to explore the expression landscape, regulatory networks, and conserved biological functions of ADH1A through a pan-cancer analysis.MethodsWe analyzed ADH1A expression across solid tumors using integrated The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) transcriptomic data, with protein-level validation from the Human Protein Atlas, 36 paired human tumor-normal tissues, and multiple tissues from BALB/c mice. To characterize ADH1A-associated biological contexts, we performed differential expression, co-expression, principal component, enrichment, and network analyses, together with survival and immune infiltration analyses.ResultsADH1A was significantly downregulated in most solid tumors relative to their normal counterparts, suggesting its potential diagnostic value. Co-expression network analysis identified that ADH1A was associated with angiogenesis, cell adhesion, and leukocyte migration, implicating its potential association with the tumor microenvironment and providing a rationale for future investigation into its potential predictive value for immunotherapy. We constructed a putative upstream regulatory network, including transcription factors and microRNAs, and a downstream protein interaction network for ADH1A based on integrative bioinformatics predictions. Notably, differential analysis suggested potential functional divergence of ADH1A between lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC). Furthermore, our cross-species comparison reveals both conserved and divergent ADH1A expression between humans and BALB/c mice, providing a partial contextual baseline for future preclinical studies.ConclusionsOur findings suggest ADH1A may serve as a potential diagnostic and prognostic biomarker across various solid tumors. By mapping its functional and regulatory landscape, this work provides a new rationale for investigating ADH1A as a candidate predictive biomarker for immunotherapy and uncovers novel avenues for exploring its biological mechanisms in cancer.Keywords: ADH1A, pan-cancer, tumor microenvironment, multi-omics

Highlight box

Key findings

• ADH1A is widely downregulated across most solid tumors and serves as a potential favorable prognostic biomarker.

• Pan-cancer co-expression analysis identifies conserved ADH1A-associated biological processes: angiogenesis, leukocyte migration, and cell adhesion.

• ADH1A exhibits strikingly divergent co-expression architectures and prognostic values between lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC).

• Construction of an ADH1A-centered transcriptional/miRNA regulatory network in LUAD.

What is known and what is new?

• The ADH gene family is associated with cancer risk and ethanol metabolism. Previous single-cancer studies indicated that ADH1A is downregulated in specific tumors (e.g., hepatocellular carcinoma, gastric cancer) and may act as a tumor suppressor.

• This study provides the first comprehensive pan-cancer multi-omics atlas of ADH1A, deconvoluting its complex roles into conserved microenvironmental functions and tumor-specific pathways. It underscores distinct subtype-specific functional trajectories between LUAD and LUSC. Furthermore, it constructed a predicted miRNA/TF-driven upstream regulatory network for LUAD.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Our findings indicate that ADH1A downregulation is a prevalent pan-cancer alteration with potential relevance to immune microenvironment remodeling, suggesting it may serve as a candidate diagnostic or prognostic biomarker, as well as a predictor for immunotherapy response. These potentials, along with the putative upstream regulatory network we constructed in LUAD, warrant further validation in corresponding independent clinical cohorts and experimental models.

Introduction

Cancer constitutes a primary global health burden and ranks as the second leading cause of death from non-communicable diseases worldwide. According to GLOBOCAN 2022, solid tumors, including lung, liver, colorectal, breast, prostate, and stomach cancers, are the predominant drivers of this cancer-related mortality (1). Indeed, the advent of tumor genomic profiling has provided profound insights into the molecular mechanisms of tumorigenesis, paving the way for the design of novel targeted therapies to combat these lethal malignancies.

The human alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) gene family, comprising seven enzymes (ADH1A, ADH1B, ADH1C, ADH4, ADH5, ADH6, and ADH7), is primarily involved in ethanol metabolism (2). It has been also significantly associated with a spectrum of human diseases, including alcoholism and various cancers. Notably, genetic variations within the ADH cluster are reported to modulate cancer risk (3-5). Specific family members have been linked to distinct oncologic outcomes; for instance, low expression of ADH1A may contributes to hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) progression (6). In gastric cancer (GC), low expression of ADH1A correlates with poor prognosis, and overexpression of ADH1A not only suppresses tumor cells but also induces anti-tumor M1 polarization of macrophages via the cGMP/PKG signaling pathway, thereby reshaping the tumor microenvironment (7). Furthermore, a 2025 study revealed for the first time that ADH1A inhibits the proliferation, migration, and invasion of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells by suppressing the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway (8). ADH1C is a favorable prognostic factor for liver cancer (9), and ADH1C down-regulation may harbor important clues to adenoma and CRC risk (10). ADH5 plays a protective role in leukemia (11). ADH1B and ADH1C may become metabolic markers for screening and diagnosis of breast cancer (12). Similarly, in lung adenocarcinoma, elevated expression levels of ADH1B, ADH4, and ADH5 were associated with a better prognosis (13). However, some studies had shown that patients with low expression of ADH1C and ADH7 in non-small cell lung cancer have longer survival times (14,15).

Collectively, these findings indicate that the ADH1 gene cluster plays a pivotal, albeit complex role across various solid tumors, motivating a deeper investigation into the specific functions and biological processes of its constituent members. Although there is a high transcriptional coherence between ADH1A, ADH1B, ADH1C, ADH4, ADH5, and ADH6 (16), all of which are involved in ethanol metabolism, ADH1A is the only one highly expressed in fetal liver with extremely low expression levels in adulthood (17). While classical onco-fetal antigens are typically resurrected in malignancy, ADH1A seemingly contradicts this paradigm, exhibiting further silencing rather than reactivation. This suggests ADH1A loss is not merely collateral to dedifferentiation but potentially facilitates tumorigenesis. Thus, we prioritized ADH1A for an in-depth pan-cancer analysis to explore its role in malignancy. In this study, we employed co-expression analysis, a powerful strategy for identifying disease-associated gene modules (18-20), to investigate the functions of ADH1A within the tumor biological process and to determine its expression profile in both human and murine tissues via immunohistochemistry. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-0432/rc).

Methods

Human specimen collection

Paired tumor and adjacent normal tissue specimens were obtained from 36 patients who underwent surgical resection at Nanfang Hospital (Guangzhou, China) between January 2017 and January 2018. The cohort included cases of LUAD (n=4), LUSC (n=3), lung adenosquamous carcinoma (n=1), liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) (n=4), cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL) (n=1), GC (n=3), colon cancer (n=4), rectum adenocarcinoma (READ) (n=3), breast cancer (BRCA) (n=4), renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) (n=2), esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCA) (n=3), and head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSC) (n=4). None of the patients had received anti-tumor therapy prior to surgery. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital (project number NFEC-2015-010) and informed consent was obtained from all patients on the day of admission.

Animal tissue preparation

Five male BALB/c (Bagg albino/c) mice (10 weeks old) were purchased from Southern Medical University (Guangzhou, China) and housed in the Laboratory Animal Center of Southern Medical University under SPF condition. The mice were observed for one week and if showing no abnormalities were subsequently euthanized via cervical dislocation, then a comprehensive set of organs and tissues—including the stomach, gut, lung, trachea, esophagus, ureter, heart, spleen, liver, pancreas, and thymus—were harvested for subsequent analysis. All animal experiments were performed under a project license (IACUC-LAC-20220712-008) granted by the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee of Nanfang Hospital, in compliance with the institutional guidelines for the care and use of animals.

Immunohistochemistry staining and analysis

Tissue specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 hours, processed through a serial alcohol gradient for dehydration, and embedded in paraffin. The resulting paraffin blocks were sectioned into 5-µm-thick slices. For IHC staining, sections were first deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series, followed by a wash in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the sections in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) at 120 ℃ for 5 minutes. Subsequently, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with a rabbit anti-ADH1A primary antibody (ab108203, Abcam, China) at specific dilutions (1:200 for murine tissues; 1:50 for human tissues). Following primary antibody incubation, sections were treated with a secondary antibody for 45 minutes at 37 ℃. The immunoreactive signal was visualized using 3,3’-diaminobenzidine (DAB) as the chromogen. Finally, the sections were sealed and imaged under an optical microscope (Leica, Germany).

Immunohistochemical staining was evaluated independently by two pathologists who were blinded to the clinicopathological information. The immunoreactive score (IRS) proposed by Remmele and Stegner was applied. Briefly, the staining intensity (SI) and the percentage of positive cells (PP) were assessed in at least five representative high-power fields (400× magnification). SI was scored as: 0 (negative), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), or 3 (strong). PP was scored as: 0 (0% positive cells), 1 (≤10%), 2 (11–50%), 3 (51–80%), or 4 (≥81%). The final IRS was calculated as the product of SI and PP, yielding a score ranging from 0 to 12. In cases of disagreement between the two observers, a consensus score was reached after joint discussion.

Bioinformatics mining of ADH1A

The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov) has 33 cancer datasets with publicly available RNAseqV2 data of cancer tissue and adjacent tissue, while the Genotype-Tissue Expression (GTEx) (https://www.gtexportal.org/home/index.html) database has publicly available RNA-seq data of normal tissue. For integrated tumor-normal expression analyses, RNA-seq data for TCGA tumors and GTEx normal tissues were obtained from the UCSC Xena platform. For cross-dataset comparison, we used the Toil-processed expression matrix, in which TCGA and GTEx RNA-seq samples were uniformly reprocessed to reduce computational batch effects and facilitate integrative analysis. Expression values were log2-transformed as provided by the platform or after standard transformation when applicable. We separately distinguished TCGA tumors, TCGA adjacent normal tissues, and GTEx normal tissues based on the sample annotations (Table 1). The expression profile of ADH1A in various human tumor tissues and the correlation of ADH1A expression with immune infiltration level in diverse cancer types were examined using TIMER database (https://cistrome.shinyapps.io/timer) (21,22). The correlated gene datasets were downloaded from https://www.cbioportal.org (23,24). To compare ADH1A-associated co-expression landscapes across cancers, we constructed a cancer-by-gene matrix using the union set of ADH1A-associated genes across the included tumor types. Each row represented one cancer type, each column represented one gene, and each matrix entry was the signed Spearman correlation coefficient between ADH1A and that gene in the corresponding cancer. Genes not retained as ADH1A-associated in a given cancer were coded as zero. Principal component analysis (PCA) was then performed on this matrix to project each cancer type to a single point in lower-dimensional space. The ClustVis tool (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/clustvis) (25) was used for PCA. Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed using STRING database (http://string-db.org) (version 11.0). The Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis for biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), molecular function (MF) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway were generated from https://david.ncifcrf.gov (26). The transcription factor (TF) data was downloaded from http://cistrome.org (27). The binding site of TF in the promoter sequence was predicted from http://jaspar.genereg.net (28). MicroRNAs were predicted using mirDIP, miRWalk, miRTarBase, miRDB (29-32) and TCGA databases.

Data visualization

Protein-protein interaction (PPI), gene correlation, and competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) networks were constructed and visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.7.0) (33). Key modules within these networks were identified using the Molecular Complex Detection (MCODE) plugin (version 1.5.1) (34), with the following parameters: Degree Cutoff =2, Node Score Cutoff =0.2, K-Core =2, and Max. Depth =100. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed, with a Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05 set as the threshold for significance. GO enrichment plots and heatmaps were generated via the imageGP web server (http://www.ehbio.com/ImageGP), and Venn diagrams were created using jvenn (http://jvenn.toulouse.inra.fr/app/example.html) (35). Survival analyses were performed using the Kaplan–Meier Plotter platform, with overall survival as the endpoint. For each tumor type, the optimal cut-off for ADH1A mRNA expression was automatically selected by the platform based on expression percentiles. For LUAD and LUSC, Cox regression analyses were further conducted using the available clinical covariates, including tumor stage and smoking history. For the remaining tumor types, only univariate analyses based on ADH1A mRNA expression were performed because sufficient clinicopathologic covariates were not available in the platform. As an exploratory extension, we queried immunotherapy-treated survival cohorts available in the Kaplan-Meier Plotter platform to assess whether ADH1A expression was associated with outcomes under immune checkpoint blockade.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism and Microsoft Excel. Data distribution normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. For normally distributed data, comparisons between two groups were performed using the Student’s t-test, while comparisons among three or more groups were carried out using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by the least significant difference (LSD) post hoc test. For non-normally distributed data, appropriate non-parametric tests were applied. A two-tailed, adjusted P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Co-expression between ADH1A and other genes was evaluated using Spearman’s correlation. Based on the Spearman’s correlation coefficient (ρ) and an adjusted P value <0.05, correlated genes were stratified into four tiers: highly correlated (|ρ| ≥0.8), moderately correlated (0.5≤ |ρ| <0.8), low correlated (0.3≤ |ρ| <0.5), and almost irrelevant (|ρ| <0.3).

Results

Expression profile analysis of ADH1A in human and BALB/c mice

To establish a baseline expression profile of ADH1A, we first analyzed transcriptomic data from the UCSC Xena browser, which integrates data from sources like the GTEx project. This analysis revealed that ADH1A mRNA expression was predominantly localized to the liver, with significantly higher levels in hepatocytes compared to all other normal human tissues (Figure 1A). To validate these findings at the protein level, we performed immunohistochemistry (IHC) on a panel of nine normal human tissues. Consistent with the mRNA data, ADH1A protein was strongly expressed in the cytoplasm of hepatocytes. Furthermore, moderate-to-strong positive staining was observed in the cytoplasm of glandular cells within the stomach, colon, and rectum. Weak positive staining was detected in alveolar and renal tubular epithelial cells. In contrast, ADH1A expression was undetectable in the breast and esophagus (Figure 1B).

(B) Representative IHC images showing ADH1A protein expression in paired human tumor and adjacent normal tissues (scale bar: Magnification: ×100 left; ×400 right). (C) Representative IHC images showing ADH1A protein distribution in a panel of tissues from BALB/c mice. GTEx, Genotype-Tissue Expression; IHC, immunohistochemical; TCGA, The Cancer Genome Atlas.

To evaluate the expression pattern in a common preclinical model, we next performed ADH1A IHC on a range of tissues from BALB/c mice. The analysis revealed a distinct distribution compared to humans. ADH1A was strongly positive in esophageal smooth muscle and myocardium, with moderate staining in gastric gland cells and hepatocytes, and weak staining in the ureteral epithelium. Conversely, ADH1A was not detected in the murine lung, trachea, spleen, thymus, or gut. A unique finding in the mouse model was the strong nuclear localization of ADH1A in pancreatic islet cells, in sharp contrast to the cytoplasmic staining observed in all other positive cell types (Figure 1C).

ADH1A is widely downregulated in human solid tumors

To systematically compare ADH1A expression between cancerous and non-cancerous states, we first analyzed transcriptomic data from the TIMER database. This initial screen revealed that ADH1A expression was significantly downregulated in most solid tumor types relative to their corresponding normal tissues, with the notable exceptions of ESCA and stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), which showed no significant difference (Figure 2A). To expand upon this and overcome the limitation of missing normal tissue data for some cancers in TIMER, we performed a more comprehensive analysis using an integrated dataset from TCGA and GTEx (Table 1). Across integrated TCGA/GTEx analyses, ADH1A expression was generally lower in many tumor tissues than in matched GTEx normal tissues, including bladder urothelial carcinoma​ (BLCA), BRCA, LIHC, LUAD, and LUSC. Glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) was the only exception, showing no significant differential expression. We also observed differences between TCGA adjacent normal tissues and GTEx normal tissues in several cancer types. For instance, in colon adenocarcinoma (COAD), ESCA, thyroid carcinoma (THCA), and prostatic adenocarcinoma (PRAD), the adjacent normal tissues displayed significantly lower ADH1A levels than their GTEx counterparts. Conversely, in LIHC and CHOL, adjacent normal tissues showed higher expression. Finally, no significant difference in ADH1A expression was observed between primary and metastatic tumors for BRCA and skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) (Figure 2B).

To corroborate these transcriptomic findings at the protein level, we queried IHC data from the HPA database. Quantitative analysis of the HPA dataset revealed that ADH1A protein expression was significantly downregulated in tumor tissues compared to normal counterparts across all seven available cancer types, including COAD, LIHC, LUAD, LUSC, KIRC, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC), and endometrioid adenocarcinoma (UCEC), which was highly consistent with our transcriptomic downregulation pattern (Figure 2C,2D). Additionally, we performed IHC on a preliminary set of paired tumor and adjacent normal tissues. Given the limited sample size, our analysis was restricted to descriptive observations. In alignment with the HPA data, ADH1A protein expression was visually higher in adjacent normal tissues than in tumor tissues for LUAD, LIHC, COAD, and KIRC. This observation was also extended to GC and READ, which were not covered by the HPA pathology dataset. However, we also noted notable discordances. In stark contrast to the transcriptomic data, protein expression of ADH1A appeared higher in the tumors of ESCA and HNSC than in their adjacent normal tissues. Furthermore, in LUSC, both the tumor and adjacent normal tissues were negative for ADH1A expression (Figure 1B).

Pan-cancer co-expression analysis reveals diverse and conserved functions of ADH1A

To elucidate the functional roles of ADH1A across different malignancies, we first constructed its co-expression landscape in TCGA cohorts where ADH1A was differentially expressed. The number of genes significantly correlated with ADH1A varied widely, with testicular germ cell tumors (TGCT), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD), LUAD, and BRCA being among the top eight cancers with the richest co-expression networks, suggesting that ADH1A may exert particularly diverse functions in these diseases (Figure 3A).

(C) Subsequent PCA of the main tumor cluster from (B), identifying LIHC and CHOL as secondary outliers. (D) Comparison of number of ADH1A-correlated genes in various correlated degrees in LIHC, CHOL, KICH, and BRCA. PCA, principal component analysis; TCGA, The Cancer Genome Atlas.

To compare ADH1A-associated co-expression patterns across cancer types, we first performed PCA using the co-expression gene sets identified in each tumor. Most cancer types clustered together, suggesting broadly shared ADH1A-associated co-expression features across tumors. In contrast, TGCT, LUAD, and PAAD were separated from the main cluster, indicating that their ADH1A-associated gene profiles were relatively distinct from those of other cancers (Figure 3B). These three tumor types were therefore retained as outlier cancers for subsequent analysis. To further select a main-cluster cancer type for comparison, we performed a second PCA focusing on cancers within the major cluster. In this analysis, BRCA still clustered with most cancer types, suggesting that its ADH1A-associated co-expression profile was consistent with the main-cluster pattern. Moreover, BRCA contained a relatively large number of moderately and highly ADH1A-correlated genes (Figure 3C,3D). Therefore, BRCA was selected as one main-cluster cancer type for comparison with the three outlier cancers. These four cancer types were subsequently used for detailed comparative functional analysis.

Identification of both tumor-specific and conserved ADH1A-associated pathways

Venn diagram analysis of the co-expression networks from these four selected cancers allowed us to separate tumor-specific from conserved functions (Figure 4A). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of the uniquely correlated genes in each cancer revealed distinct, context-specific roles. Notably, in BRCA, ADH1A-associated genes were enriched in the PPAR signaling pathway; in TGCT, they were linked to ribosome biogenesis; and in PAAD, to complement and coagulation cascades. In LUAD, the unique genes were associated with respiratory gaseous exchange, an organ-specific biological process (Figure 4B-4F).

In contrast, analysis of the co-expression genes common to all four cancers identified a core set of conserved biological processes. These included angiogenesis, leukocyte migration, and cell adhesion, suggesting that ADH1A is consistently associated with tumor microenvironment-related biological processes across multiple cancer types (Figure 4G). The Kaplan-Meier Plotter platform shows that higher ADH1A expression was associated with improved overall survival in a BRCA, and PAAD cohort. In addition, higher ADH1A expression was associated with a favorable but non-significant trend in a TGCT cohort (Figure 4H).

Hub-gene analysis of conserved functions and correlation with immune infiltration

To dissect these conserved functions further and pinpoint the central drivers within the enriched pathways, we constructed PPI networks and identified key modules and hub genes. The analysis identified PECAM1 for angiogenesis (Figure 5A), SELP—a critical adhesion molecule mediating leukocyte tethering and rolling—as the hub gene for leukocyte migration (Figure 5B) and VWF for cell adhesion (Figure 5C). Interestingly, although the shared ADH1A-associated genes were initially identified based on absolute correlation strength, their correlation patterns were tumor-specific. In the angiogenesis-related gene set, TEK and PTPRB showed relatively strong correlations with ADH1A in BRCA, LUAD, and PAAD, but much weaker correlations in TGCT, whereas S1PR1 showed relatively consistent correlations across the four tumors. In the leukocyte migration gene set, SELP and TEK were strongly correlated with ADH1A in BRCA, LUAD, and PAAD but showed attenuated correlations in TGCT. In the cell adhesion gene set, VWF and SVEP1 displayed stronger correlations with ADH1A in BRCA and LUAD than in PAAD or TGCT. Furthermore, expression heatmaps revealed that these genes were not uniformly expressed across tumor types, and relatively high expression did not necessarily correspond to uniformly strong correlations with ADH1A. Together, these results suggest that shared ADH1A-associated genes may participate in common vascular remodeling, leukocyte migration, and cell adhesion programs, while the strength of their association with ADH1A is shaped by tumor-specific contexts (Figure 5D-5I).

Given that successful leukocyte migration is a prerequisite for immune cell infiltration into tumors, we next investigated the correlation between ADH1A expression and tumor-infiltrating immune cells via the TIMER database. In LUAD, ADH1A expression showed a significant positive correlation with the infiltration levels of all major immune cell types analyzed (B cells, CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, macrophages, neutrophils, and DCs). Similarly, in PAAD and BRCA, ADH1A was positively correlated with CD8+ T cells, CD4+ T cells, and macrophages. In TGCT, ADH1A expression was positively correlated with CD4+ T cells and macrophages but negatively correlated with CD8+ T cells. Across most of these cancers, higher ADH1A expression was also significantly associated with lower tumor purity, consistent with a more robust immune infiltrate (Figure 5J). To provide preliminary context for the potential relationship between ADH1A and immunotherapy outcomes, we performed an exploratory analysis using immunotherapy-treated cohorts from the Kaplan–Meier Plotter platform. Higher ADH1A expression was associated with improved overall survival and progression-free survival in a pembrolizumab-treated melanoma cohort. In addition, higher ADH1A expression was associated with improved overall survival in an atezolizumab-treated esophageal adenocarcinoma cohort, whereas progression-free survival exhibited a favorable but non-significant trend (Figure S1A-S1D).

ADH1A exhibits divergent functional roles in LUAD versus LUSC

Prompted by the PCA results suggesting distinct roles for ADH1A in LUAD versus LUSC, we performed a differential co-expression analysis to investigate these differences. We identified genes uniquely correlated with ADH1A in each subtype (Figure 6A) and subsequently analyzed their PPI networks and key functional modules (Figure 6B-6D).

This analysis revealed stark functional divergences in co-expression architecture. For the conserved gene set shared across subtypes (Figure 6E), the associated network was significantly enriched for fundamental cellular and signaling processes, including the plasma membrane, protein coupled receptor signaling pathway, integral component of plasma membrane, and chemotaxis. In LUSC, the ADH1A-associated network was enriched for biomineral tissue development (Figure 6F), a process not typically associated with cancer progression. In sharp contrast, the LUAD-specific network was significantly enriched for core oncogenic processes, including the cell cycle, mitotic nuclear division, and the p53 signaling pathway (Figure 6G). These divergent functional associations were accompanied by differences in patient outcomes. Kaplan-Meier analysis indicated that high ADH1A expression was associated with significantly favorable overall survival in LUAD, whereas no such survival difference was observed in LUSC (Figure 6H).

Construction of a putative ADH1A regulatory network in LUAD

Given that ADH1A showed a clearer prognostic association in LUAD than in LUSC, we next focused on LUAD as a priority setting for downstream regulatory exploration. To generate a candidate framework for future mechanistic studies, we constructed a putative ADH1A-centered regulatory network using the LUAD RNA dataset. First, candidate TFs were predicted using the Cistrome database. The top ten TFs most highly correlated with ADH1A are detailed in Table 2, with MEF2C identified as the most closely correlated. The predicted binding sequence for MEF2C within the ADH1A promoter is “TTTTTAAAAATAATA” (Table 3).

Discussion

While the broader association between the ADH gene family and cancer risk is well-established, the specific functions of individual members within the tumor microenvironment have remained largely undefined. Our study addresses this gap by providing a comprehensive, pan-cancer functional and regulatory map for ADH1A. We demonstrate that ADH1A is not only a universally downregulated gene across most solid tumors but also a potential prognostic biomarker whose function is deeply context-dependent.

A central finding of this study is the consistent downregulation of ADH1A in tumor tissues compared to their normal counterparts, a trend observed at both the mRNA and protein levels for a majority of cancers, including LUAD, LIHC, and BRCA. This widespread downregulation suggests that ADH1A may act as a potential tumor suppressor, the loss of which could facilitate tumorigenesis. This hypothesis is strongly supported by our survival analyses, where higher ADH1A expression correlated with significantly longer overall survival in patients with BRCA, PAAD, and LUAD. Collectively, these data position ADH1A as a robust candidate for both diagnostic and prognostic assessment in multiple cancer types, requiring further experimental validation.

Our cross-species comparison highlights significant inter-species divergences in ADH1A expression. This indicates that while BALB/c mice may serve as a referential model for investigating ADH1A in specific tissues with conserved expression patterns (e.g., gastric and hepatic tissues), their application should be restricted to these contexts rather than broadly applied. Future studies utilizing murine models must rigorously account for these baseline expression differences. In addition, our multi-layered analysis also uncovered important complexities. The observed discordance between RNA and protein expression in ESCA and HNSC, for instance, points towards potential post-transcriptional or post-translational regulation. However, constrained by our limited IHC sample size and the absence of quantitative references for these malignancies in the HPA database, these observations remain preliminary. Future studies must first employ larger cohorts to robustly confirm this decoupling before delving into the underlying transcriptional and translational mechanisms.

Furthermore, we noted significant expression differences between adjacent normal tissues from TCGA and normal tissues from GTEx, particularly in LIHC and COAD. The observed differences between TCGA adjacent normal tissues and GTEx normal tissues may reflect of “field cancerization”, where morphologically normal tissue adjacent to a tumor already harbors molecular alterations, although potential technical confounders from dataset integration cannot be fully ruled out. It is consistent with previous findings that normal adjacent to tumor (NAT) tissues are not truly “normal”; rather, they exhibit a distinct intermediate transcriptional state between healthy and tumor tissues (36). Their pan-cancer analysis revealed that tumors actively reshape their adjacent microenvironment by spreading pro-inflammatory signals (such as TNF-α and TGF-β), which induces a specific set of tumor-adjacent specific activation (TASA) genes. Therefore, the divergent ADH1A expression patterns we observed between GTEx normal and TCGA adjacent normal tissues likely reflect this active tumor-adjacent interaction and the reprogramming of the adjacent stroma, rather than simple baseline physiological differences.

By employing a co-expression network analysis, we successfully deconvoluted the complex functions of ADH1A into both conserved and tumor-specific roles. Crucially, we identified three conserved ADH1A-associated biological functions—angiogenesis, cell adhesion, and leukocyte migration—across several cancer types, with PECAM1, SELP, and VWF pinpointed as the respective hub genes that may serve as candidate molecular targets for investigating ADH1A’s role in the tumor microenvironment. Similarly, the positive correlations between ADH1A expression and estimated immune-cell infiltration suggest that ADH1A may be linked to immune-related tumor microenvironment features.

While the correlative association between ADH1A and immune-related features in our pan-cancer analyses offers valuable biological insights, we concur that translating these into definitive immunotherapy predictive value requires further substantiation. To bridge this knowledge gap, we performed exploratory supplementary analyses of currently accessible immunotherapy-treated cohorts available in the Kaplan-Meier Plotter platform. Higher ADH1A expression was associated with more favorable outcomes in melanoma treated with pembrolizumab and in esophageal adenocarcinoma treated with atezolizumab. These findings provide preliminary external support for a possible link between ADH1A and immunotherapy-related biology, but they remain exploratory and require validation in dedicated, cancer-specific immunotherapy cohorts. Accordingly, our results provide a rationale for testing this possibility in future studies that include immunotherapy-treated cohorts and direct response data. Beyond these conserved functions, our analysis revealed striking, context-specific functional associations of ADH1A. In BRCA, the ADH1A co-expression network was uniquely enriched for the PPAR signaling pathway, a known regulator of cell proliferation and apoptosis in breast cancer (37-39). Even more striking was the divergent co-expression pattern observed between LUAD and LUSC. In LUAD, ADH1A co-expression was associated with core oncogenic pathways like cell cycle and p53 signaling, consistent with its prognostic significance in this disease. In LUSC, however, the co-expression network was associated with biomineralization, and its expression held no prognostic value. This finding highlights the possibility that LUAD and LUSC feature distinct ADH1A-associated network architectures, which provides a hypothesis for future investigations into their different underlying biologies.

To mechanistically elucidate ADH1A’s context-specific role in LUAD, we constructed a predicted upstream regulatory network which suggests MEF2C as a putative key activating TF and specific microRNAs as potential post-transcriptional tuners. Given that promoter hypermethylation canonically silences tumor suppressors, we analyzed ADH1A’s epigenetic regulation. Interestingly, our LUAD methylation analysis revealed no significant tumor-normal difference (Figure S1E), aligning with hepatocellular carcinoma reports of ADH1A’s insensitivity to DNA methylation and histone deacetylation inhibitors (40). Therefore, ADH1A downregulation appears independent of DNA hypermethylation, highlighting our predicted miRNA networks and TFs as a hypothetical alternative silencing mechanisms that warrants future experimental validation.

In conclusion, this study suggests that ADH1A has a certain role in pan-cancer biology. We have established its potential as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker, uncovered its conserved role in shaping the tumor microenvironment, and highlighted its strikingly divergent functions in different cancer subtypes. The integrated regulatory network constructed for LUAD provides a hypothetical framework for future mechanistic studies to dissect the upstream and downstream pathways governing ADH1A’s tumor-suppressive functions.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study establishes ADH1A as a pivotal and context-dependent gene in pan-cancer biology. Its widespread downregulation and correlation with patient survival highlight its dual potential as a diagnostic and prognostic biomarker. By systematically mapping its co-expression networks, we elucidate the functional associations underlying its diverse biological effects, providing a strong rationale for investigating ADH1A as a candidate predictive biomarker for immunotherapy response. Furthermore, the distinct networks and prognostic implications between LUAD and LUSC underscore its context-dependent nature, though future studies are needed to validate these mechanistic differences. Ultimately, this work uncovers new avenues and provides a comprehensive resource for future mechanistic and translational studies of ADH1A.
