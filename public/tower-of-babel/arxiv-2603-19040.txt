When Differential Privacy Meets Wireless Federated Learning: An Improved Analysis for Privacy and Convergence
Yaoling Chen, Hao Liang, Xiaotong Tu

Abstract
Differentially private wireless federated learning (DPWFL) is a promising framework for protecting sensitive user data. However, foundational questions on how to precisely characterize privacy loss remain open, and existing work is further limited by convergence analyses that rely on restrictive convexity assumptions or ignore the effect of gradient clipping. To overcome these issues, we present a comprehensive analysis of privacy and convergence for DPWFL with general smooth non-convex loss objectives. Our analysis explicitly incorporates both device selection and mini-batch sampling, and shows that the privacy loss can converge to a constant rather than diverge with the number of iterations. Moreover, we establish convergence guarantees with gradient clipping and derive an explicit privacy-utility trade-off. Numerical results validate our theoretical findings.

Introduction

Federated learning (FL) has emerged as a standard paradigm for distributed learning, enabling multiple devices to collaboratively train a shared model without exchanging raw data
[
14
]
. With the growing prevalence of wireless networks, FL has been increasingly deployed over wireless channels, giving rise to wireless federated learning (WFL). A prominent approach is over-the-air FL (AirFL)
[
21
]
, which leverages the superposition property of radio-frequency waveforms to allow simultaneous transmission and efficient model aggregation. Despite these advantages, WFL remains vulnerable to inference attacks, as sensitive information can still be revealed from the shared model parameters
[
16
]
.

To address such privacy risks, differential privacy (DP)
[
4
]
has become the de facto standard for ensuring individual privacy, offering strict theoretical guarantees. The key idea of DP is to inject carefully calibrated randomness, typically in the form of additive noise, to mask the contribution of any single data sample. This mechanism has also been adopted in WFL, yielding differentially private WFL (DPWFL). A natural problem then arises:
What is the privacy loss of DPWFL as a function of the number of iterations?

Several works have attempted to answer this. Building on advanced composition
[
4
]
, Liu and Simeone
[
10
]
provided privacy guarantees along with convergence analysis under smooth and Polyak-Lojasiewicz (PL) conditions. In
[
6
]
, the effect of device and mini-batch sampling was analyzed, yielding privacy guarantees via Rényi DP (RDP) composition. Later, Park and Choi
[
17
]
exploited the inherent randomness of local gradients to tighten privacy bounds. More recently, Liu
et al.

[
12
]
investigated DP in multiple-input multiple-output (MIMO) systems using the moment accountant
[
1
]
, and derived convergence guarantees for strong convexity and smooth objective. However, these approaches primarily rely on composition theorems or the moment accountant, both of which assume that all intermediate models are revealed during training and thus tend to over-estimate privacy loss. In practice, this leads to overly conservative noise injection, degraded model utility, and introduces unnecessary communication overhead. This raises a key question:
Can we more accurately track the privacy trajectory throughout training?

While substantial efforts have been made to strengthen privacy guarantees by avoiding the release of intermediate results
[
5
,
3
,
2
]
, these methods often lead to privacy bounds that increase ad infinitum with iterations or require restrictive (strongly) convexity assumptions. To address this, Liang
et al.
recently showed that privacy loss can converge under general smooth objectives and established a tight convergence guarantee in the centralized setting. However, its implications for wireless federated learning remain unexplored. Meanwhile, existing convergence analyses for DPWFL either ignore the critical role of gradient clipping (an essential mechanism for enforcing DP) or hinge on restrictive assumptions (e.g., convexity or bounded sample-wise gradient) which are often violated in practice, especially in training deep neural networks. This motivates another fundamental question:
How can we establish convergence guarantees for general non-convex objectives while explicitly accounting for gradient clipping?

The purpose of this paper is to address these questions. In particular, we provide a precise characterization of the privacy trajectory for DPWFL while incorporating both device selection and mini-batch sampling. By leveraging inherent channel noise, we establish rigorous privacy guarantees and prove that the privacy loss converges to a constant rather than growing unboundedly with iterations. In addition, we analyze the role of gradient clipping on the convergence under general smoothness assumptions, and derive an explicit privacy-utility trade-off. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: (i) we derive a precise privacy characterization for DPWFL by jointly incorporating device-level and data-level sampling, and prove that it can converge to a constant; (ii) we rigorously characterize the effect of gradient clipping on DPWFL, and establish an improved privacy-utility trade-off for general non-convex smooth objectives; (iii) we experimentally validate our theoretical findings

2
Preliminaries

2.1
System Model

Learning Protocol.

We consider an FL system that consists of a single-antenna server and
n
clients. Each device
i
has its own dataset
\mathcal{D}_{i}
, which is independent and identically distributed (
i.i.d.
) across clients, and we assume that each device has the same number of samples. The server and clients cooperatively learn a global model, parameterized by
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, by solving the minimization problem:
f^{*}=\min_{\bm{\theta}}f(\bm{\theta})\triangleq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}f_{i}(\bm{\theta})
, where
f_{i}(\bm{\theta})\triangleq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}_{i}|}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{D}_{i}}l(\bm{\theta};\xi)
with
|\mathcal{D}_{i}|
and
l(\cdot;\xi)
denoting the cardinality of
\mathcal{D}_{i}
and sample-wise loss function, respectively. In this work, we employ federated averaging (FedAvg) with distributed stochastic gradient descent (SGD)
[
14
]
to solve this problem iteratively across clients.

Specifically, at the
t
-th global communication round, the server uniformly selects
|\mathcal{I}^{(t)}|=pn
(
p\in(0,1]
) active devices at random and broadcasts the current global model parameter
\bm{\theta}^{(t)}
to all devices as initialization of the local models. To reduce communication overhead, clients participating in the
t
-th iteration simultaneously transmit their stochastic gradients, obtained from a mini-batch
\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(t)}\subseteq\mathcal{D}_{i}
, to the server, i.e.,
\widetilde{\nabla}f_{i}(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{(t)})\triangleq\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(t)}|}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(t)}}l(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{(t)};\xi)
, with
|\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(t)}|\triangleq q|\mathcal{D}_{i}^{(t)}|
(
q\in(0,1]
). The server then receives an estimate
\hat{\nabla}f(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})
of the superposition of global gradient
\widetilde{\nabla}f(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})\triangleq\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{(t)}}|\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(t)}|\widetilde{\nabla}f_{i}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})
. For example, we have
\hat{\nabla}f(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})=\widetilde{\nabla}f(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})
while assuming ideal, noiseless communications. Finally, the server updates the global model parameters,
\bm{\theta}^{(t+1)}
, with a learning rate
\eta
as
\bm{\theta}^{(t+1)}=\bm{\theta}^{(t)}-\frac{\eta}{pqn}\hat{\nabla}f(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})
. This therefore suggests an iterative procedure until a suitable criterion is satisfied.

Communication Model.

We consider a wireless system in which all devices communicate via the uplink to the server over a multiple-access fading channel via over-
the-air computing. As in
[
19
,
10
]
, we assume perfect channel state information at clients, and the downlink communication is ideal and noiseless. The uplink assumes block flat-fading channels, where the gains
\{h_{i}^{(t)}\}
remain constant within a communication round. To start, at each iteration
t
, the active device
i\in\mathcal{I}^{(t)}
transmits a scaled and per-sample clipped version of the gradient as
\bm{x}_{i}^{(t)}=s_{i}^{(t)}\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(t)}}\operatorname{clip}_{c}(\nabla l(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{(t)};\xi))
where
\operatorname{clip}_{c}(x)=x\min(1,c/\|x\|)
and
s_{i}^{(t)}
is the scaling factor. Hence, the signal received at the server can be expressed as
\bm{y}^{(t)}=\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{(t)}}h_{i}^{(t)}\bm{x}_{i}^{(t)}+\bm{\zeta}^{(t)}
, where
\bm{\zeta}^{(t)}\sim\mathcal{N}(\bm{0},\sigma^{2}\bm{I})
is the inherent channel noise.
Note that the scaling factors
s_{i}^{(t)}
need to satisfy
h_{i}^{(t)}s_{i}^{(t)}=\gamma^{(t)}
to ensure gradient alignment. Finally, the server estimates the global gradient as
\hat{\nabla}f(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})=(\gamma^{(t)})^{-1}\bm{y}^{(t)}
.
Therefore, the update procedure of the proposed method, termed DPWFL, can be written as

\!\bm{\theta}^{(t+1)}\!=\!\bm{\theta}^{(t)}\!-\frac{\eta}{pqn}\!\left(\!\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{(t)}}\!\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(t)}\!}\!\operatorname{clip}_{c}(\nabla l(\bm{\theta}_{i}^{(t)};\xi))\!+\!(\gamma^{(t)})^{-1}\!\bm{\zeta}^{(t)}\!\right)\!.\!

2.2
Differential Privacy

Threat Model.

Our threat model assumes a
curious third-party
adversary. The attacker aims to compromise privacy by inferring information about a specific data sample from the final released model
\bm{\theta}^{(T)}
. As in previous work
[
5
,
2
,
9
]
, we consider a black-box setting in which the attacker is restricted to accessing only
\bm{\theta}^{(T)}
and has no knowledge of the intermediate process. We then provide preliminaries on DP.

Definition
2.1
(Differential Privacy
[
4
]
)

For
\epsilon\geq 0
,
\delta\in[0,1]
, the DPWFL is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP if, for any pair of adjacent global datasets,
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, and for any subset of outputs
\mathcal{S}
, we have

\operatorname{Pr}[\bm{\theta}^{(T)}\in\mathcal{S}|\mathcal{D}]\leq\exp(\epsilon)\operatorname{Pr}[\bm{\theta}^{(T)\prime}\in\mathcal{S}|\mathcal{D}^{\prime}]+\delta.

(1)

where
\bm{\theta}^{(T)}
and
\bm{\theta}^{(T)\prime}
denote the output of DPWFL based on
\mathcal{D}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, respectively. Adjacent global datasets here refer to those where only a single client’s data point is changed, i.e.,

\mathcal{D}=(\cup_{i\neq j}\mathcal{D}_{i})\cup\mathcal{D}_{j}
and
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}=(\cup_{i\neq j}\mathcal{D}_{i})\cup\mathcal{D}_{j}^{\prime}
, with
\|\mathcal{D}_{j}-\mathcal{D}_{j}^{\prime}\|=1
for a device
j\in[n]
.

Our privacy analysis is based on the RDP framework
[
15
]
, which is well-suited for expressing privacy guarantees.

Definition
2.2
(Rényi Differential Privacy
[
15
]
)

For
\alpha>1
,
\varepsilon\geq 0
, the DPWFL satisfies
(\alpha,\varepsilon)
-RDP if, for any pair of adjacent global datasets,
\mathcal{D}
,
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
, it holds that

D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}^{(T)}|\mathcal{D}}||\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\theta}^{(T)\prime}|\mathcal{D}^{\prime}})\leq\varepsilon,

(2)

where
\mathbb{P}_{\mu}
denotes the law of the random variable
\mu
, and
D_{\alpha}(\cdot||\cdot)
is Rényi divergence defined as
[
18
]

D_{\alpha}(\mathbb{P}_{\mu}||\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\prime}})=\frac{1}{\alpha-1}\log\mathbb{E}_{s\sim\mathbb{P}_{\mu^{\prime}}}\left\{\left(\frac{\operatorname{Pr}[\mu=s]}{\operatorname{Pr}[\mu^{\prime}=s]}\right)^{\alpha}\right\}.

(3)

We emphasize that
(\alpha,\varepsilon)
-RDP can imply standard
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP.

Lemma
2.1
(From RDP to Standard DP
[
15
]
)

If the above system model is an
(\alpha,\varepsilon)
-RDP protocol, it also satisfies
(\varepsilon+\frac{\log 1/\delta}{\alpha-1},\delta)
-DP for any
0<\delta<1
.

2.3
Assumptions

Next, we outline the assumptions for our analysis, which are commonly used in non-convex optimization and FL analysis
2
2

2

We emphasize that Assumption
2
is mild and practically justified. Many optimization problems naturally involve bounded parameter domains due to intrinsic problem structure or practical requirements. Further, some unconstrained problems may be addressed by solving a sequence of constrained sub-problems with only a minor computation and privacy overhead
[
13
,
8
]
.
.

Assumption 1
(
L
-smooth)

The loss function
l(\cdot;\xi)
is
L
-smooth, i.e., for all
\bm{\theta},\bm{\theta}^{\prime}
, we have
\left\|\nabla l(\bm{\theta};\xi)-\nabla l\left(\bm{\theta}^{\prime};\xi\right)\right\|\leq L\left\|\bm{\theta}-\bm{\theta}^{\prime}\right\|
.

Assumption 2
(Bounded Parameter Domain)

For any iteration
t\in\{0,\ldots,T-1\}
, the domain of the model parameters has diameter
D>0
.

Assumption 3
(Bounded Variance)

For any
i\in[n]
and
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the gradient of loss function,
\nabla l(\bm{\theta};\xi)
, has bounded variance, i.e.,
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla l(\bm{\theta};\xi)-\nabla f_{i}(\bm{\theta})\|^{2}\right]\leq\sigma_{l}^{2}
.

Assumption 4
(Gradient Dissimilarity)

For any
i\in[n]
and
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, the gradient of local empirical loss function,
\nabla f_{i}(\bm{\theta})
, satisfies
\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f_{i}(\bm{\theta})-\nabla f(\bm{\theta})\|^{2}\right]\leq\sigma_{g}^{2}
.

3
Main Results

3.1
Privacy Analysis

In this subsection, we quantify the RDP loss for DPWFL. Our theoretical result builds upon the recent work
[
9
]
, which establishes a rigorous privacy characterization of centralized learning under ideal communication settings. In DPWFL, the combination of inherent channel noise, mini-batch sampling, and device active set selection, along with the evolving trajectory of privacy loss, makes it challenging to bound the overall privacy budget. To address this issue, we first decouple the scaled channel noise at each iteration
t
,
(\gamma^{(t)})^{-1}\bm{\zeta}^{(t)}
, into two components, i.e.,
\sqrt{\beta^{(t)}}\bm{\zeta}^{(t)}/\gamma^{(t)}
and
\sqrt{1-\beta^{(t)}}\bm{\zeta}^{(t)}/\gamma^{(t)}
with
\beta^{(t)}\in(0,1]
. The first component, together with the server aggregation procedure, forms the wireless noisy mechanism, whose specific definition and privacy reduction property are characterized by the following lemma.

Lemma
3.1
(Wireless Noisy Reduction)

Define

\!\psi^{(t)}(\bm{\mu})\triangleq\bm{\mu}-\frac{\eta}{pqn}\!\left(\!\sum_{i\in\mathcal{I}^{(t)}}\!\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(t)}\!}\!\operatorname{clip}_{c}(\nabla l(\bm{\mu};\xi))\!+\!\frac{\sqrt{\beta^{(t)}}}{\gamma^{(t)}}\bm{\zeta}^{(t)}\!\right),\!

as the wireless noisy mechanism based on the dataset
\mathcal{D}
at iteration
t
, and we can similarly define
\psi^{(t)\prime}(\cdot)
based on its adjacent dataset
\mathcal{D}^{\prime}
. If Assumption
1
holds, then for any random variables
\bm{\mu}
and
\bm{\nu}
, and for any
\beta^{(t)}\in(0,1]
, we have

\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}^{((1+\eta L)z)}\!(\mathbb{P}_{\psi^{(t)}(\bm{\mu})}||\mathbb{P}_{\psi^{(t)\prime}(\bm{\nu})})\leq\mathcal{D}_{\alpha}^{(z)}(\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\mu}}||\mathbb{P}_{\bm{\nu}})+\frac{2\alpha pqc^{2}(\gamma^{(t)})^{2}}{\beta^{(t)}\sigma^{2}}.\!

Proof (sketch):
The proof is motivated by
[
5
,
9
]
, which leverages the properties of the
\infty
-Wasserstein distance and shifted Rényi divergence. Further, by analyzing the sensitivity of the disclosed update function and exploiting the randomness introduced by mini-batch sampling and active set selection, we obtain the stated bound. We omit the detailed proof here due to space limitations and refer to the full version.
\blacksquare

Combining with the shift-reduction lemma in
[
5
]
, which characterizes the shifted Rényi divergence between two distributions convolved with additive noise, we establish the following RDP guarantee.

Theorem
3.1
(
(\alpha,\varepsilon)
-RDP Guarantee for DPWFL)

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, for any
\alpha>1
, DPWFL satisfies
(\alpha,\varepsilon)
-RDP, where

\varepsilon=\frac{2\alpha pqc^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\min\left\{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(\gamma^{(t)})^{2},\Phi\right\},

(4)

with
\Phi=(\gamma^{(T-1)}+\frac{(1+\eta L)\sqrt{pq}Dn\gamma^{(T-1)}}{2\eta c})^{2}
.

Proof (sketch):

We first decompose the server aggregation process at the
t
-th round into the wireless noisy mechanism,
\psi^{(t)}(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})
, and a partial Gaussian perturbation,
\frac{\sqrt{1-\beta^{(t)}}}{\gamma^{(t)}}\bm{\zeta}^{(t)}
. By applying Lemma
3.1
together with the shifted-reduction lemma, we derive inductive updates of the privacy loss. Further, under Assumption 2, we introduce an auxiliary variable sequence to control the perturbation and optimize the variable
\beta^{(t)}
, which enables us to characterize the trajectory of the RDP loss. The detailed proof is also omitted here.
\blacksquare

By leveraging Lemma
2.1
, the standard DP guarantee for DPWFL is established as follows.

Corollary
3.1
(
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP Guarantee for DPWFL)

Under Assumptions
1
and
2
, for any
\delta\in(0,1)
, DPWFL satisfies
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP, where

\epsilon=\frac{2pqc^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Gamma+2\sqrt{\frac{2pqc^{2}}{\sigma^{2}}\Gamma\log(1/\delta)},

(5)

with
\Gamma=\min\{\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}(\gamma^{(t)})^{2},\Phi\}
.

This corollary shows that, even in the wireless FL setting, the DPWFL can achieve a converged privacy loss, i.e., the bound remains constant as
T\to\infty
when the parameter domain has bounded diameter. This stands in sharp contrast to prior analyses of wireless FL
[
10
,
20
]
, where privacy loss goes to infinity with the number of iterations. Our result demonstrates that non-convex smooth objectives can obtain rigorous DP guarantees without long-term degradation, thereby enabling sustainable training without exhausting the privacy budget. Moreover, the presence of
p
and
q
in the bound explicitly quantifies the privacy amplification by device selection and mini-batch sampling, respectively.

3.2
Convergence Analysis

We now proceed to derive our convergence results for DPWFL. Our analysis is motivated by a recent result
[
7
]
, which rigorously analyzes the impact of the per-sample clipping operation on the convergence of FedAvg for the ideal communication scenario. Building upon this foundational work, we derive the convergence bound for general DPWFL settings.

Theorem
3.2
(Convergence Analysis for DPWFL)

Under Assumptions
1
,
3
, and
4
, the DPWFL algorithm running for
T
global rounds with step-size
\eta=\mathcal{O}(1/L)
satisfies

\displaystyle\min_{t\in[1,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})\|\right]\leq\mathcal{O}\biggl(\underbrace{\frac{f(\bm{\theta}^{(0)})-f^{*}}{\eta cT}+\sqrt{\frac{f(\bm{\theta}^{(0)})-f^{*}}{\eta T}}}_{\text{Initialization error }C_{1}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\sqrt{\frac{\eta L(\sigma_{l}^{2}+\sigma_{g}^{2})}{pqn}}+\min\left\{\eta L\sqrt{\sigma_{l}^{2}+\sigma_{g}^{2}},c\right\}}_{\text{Stochastic noise \& dissimilarity \& clipping error }C_{2}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\min\left\{\sigma_{l}+\sigma_{g},\frac{\sigma_{l}^{2}+\sigma_{g}^{2}}{c}\right\}}_{\text{Unavoidable clipping bias }C_{3}}

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\frac{d\eta L\sigma^{2}}{pqnc}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{1}{(\gamma^{(t)})^{2}}+\sqrt{\frac{d\eta L}{pqn}}\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\sigma}{\gamma^{(t)}}}_{\text{Estimation \& channel noise error }C_{4}}\biggr).

Proof (sketch):

To start, we incorporate the scaled channel noise
(\gamma^{(t)})^{-1}\bm{\zeta}^{(t)}
by leveraging the

L
-smooth assumption and combine the fact that both the active set,
\mathcal{I}^{(t)}
, and mini-batches,
\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(t)}
, are uniformly selected with
|\mathcal{I}^{(t)}|=pn
and
|\mathcal{B}_{i}^{(t)}|=q|\mathcal{D}_{i}|
, yielding at the desired result.
\blacksquare

The derived bound provides insights into the learning performance of DPWFL by explicitly accounting for four kinds of error: (i) the initialization error,
C_{1}
(capturing optimization-related factors); (ii) the error due to the stochastic gradient randomness, gradient dissimilarity, and clipping operation,
C_{2}
(dependent on
\sigma_{l}
,
\sigma_{g}
, and
c
); (iii) the unavoidable bias from gradient clipping,
C_{3}
(which decreases with larger
c
but cannot be reduced by smaller step-size,
\eta
, or more iterations,
T
); and (iv) the wireless communication error,
C_{4}
(arising from received signal estimation and channel noise).

Using our derived privacy guarantee, we can immediately obtain the privacy-utility trade-off.

Proposition
3.2
(Privacy-Utility Trade-off for DPWFL)

Under Assumptions
1
-
4
, the DPWFL algorithm is
(\alpha,\varepsilon)
-RDP, and satisfies

\displaystyle\min_{t\in[1,T]}\mathbb{E}\left[\|\nabla f(\bm{\theta}^{(t)})\|\right]\leq\mathcal{O}\biggl(C_{1}+C_{2}+C_{3}

(6)

\displaystyle+\frac{\alpha d\eta L}{n\varepsilon}\frac{c}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\Gamma}{(\gamma^{(t)})^{2}}+\sqrt{\frac{\alpha d\eta L}{n\varepsilon}}\frac{c}{T}\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\frac{\sqrt{\Gamma}}{\gamma^{(t)}}\biggr).

Equivalently, the DPWFL is
(\epsilon,\delta)
-DP, and its privacy-utility trade-off can be directly obtained by a similar analysis.

This proposition characterizes the privacy–utility trade-off of DPWFL. Specifically: (i) unlike previous work
[
10
,
11
]
that ignores gradient clipping by assuming bounded sample-wise gradients or (strongly) convex loss functions, our analysis rigorously incorporates the clipping effect into the convergence analysis; (ii) the bound in (
6
) explicitly reveals the inherent privacy–utility trade-off, where tighter privacy inevitably degrades convergence; (iii) these results can be further optimized and simplified by carefully selecting the iteration number
T
and clipping threshold
c
(see, e.g.,
[
7
,
9
]
).

5
Conclusion

In this paper, we provided a comprehensive analysis of privacy and convergence for DPWFL. We showed that by leveraging inherent channel noise and incorporating both device and mini-batch sampling, the privacy loss can converge to a constant. We further established the convergence result with gradient clipping, derived an explicit privacy–utility trade-off, and validated our theory with experiments.
