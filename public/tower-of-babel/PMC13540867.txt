SMN deficiency contributes to osteoporosis in spinal muscular atrophy by impairing Snap23 meditated muscle-derived extracellular vesicle secretion
Authors: Weishan Lin, Wenyuan Sui, Yaolong Deng, Jian Chen, Xiexiang Shao, Zifang Huang, Wenqing Wei, Liang Cheng, Xin Zhang, Rongcheng Hu, Jingfan Yang, Bingyu Li, Junlin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSpinal muscular atrophy (SMA), caused by mutations in survival motor neuron 1 (SMN1), presents with severe muscle atrophy and prevalent osteoporosis. Transcriptomic profiling of patient muscle biopsies revealed enrichment of extracellular vesicle genes, yet the contribution of SMA-EVs to SMA-associated bone loss and their link to SMN deficiency remain undefined.MethodsClinical CT/MRI images of SMA and control subjects were acquired to quantify osteoporosis and muscle atrophy. SMA model mice (Smn1hSMN2/hSMN2ROSA26hSMN2/+) were phenotyped at 6 weeks by micro-CT and histology. EVs were isolated from muscles, validated (western blot, transmission electron microscope, nano-flow cytometry, BCA protein assay), and compared between genotypes. DiL-labelled EV biodistribution was tracked in vivo; uptake by BMSCs/BMMs was confirmed by confocal microscopy. Cytotoxicity was assessed by live/dead staining. Dose–response experiments evaluated the osteogenic and anti-osteoclastic activity of SMA-EVs. Comparison of the effects of SMA-EVs and CON-EVs were performed with adequate doses in vitro and in vivo, followed by EV replenishment in SMA mice. Osteogenic and osteoclastogenic gene expression was quantified by qPCR; ALP activity by ELISA. Bone and cell parameters were assessed by HE staining, TRAP staining, COL-1 immunofluorescence staining, and micro-CT. RNA-seq data were validated by Western blot. Lentiviral shRNA and over-expression plasmids were used to generate muscle cells with stable SNAP23 knock-down or up-regulation, and AAV-mediated muscle-specific Snap23 over-expression was employed in mice to define the role of muscular SNAP23 in EV secretion and its impact on bone mass. Mice carrying extra SMN2 transgenic copies were analyzed to delineate the SMN–SNAP23 relationship.ResultsSMA patients and mice exhibited a significantly diminished capacity of skeletal muscle to secrete EVs, which were readily internalized by BMSCs and BMMs, dose-dependently promote osteogenic differentiation and suppress osteoclast formation. Adequate-dose SMA-EVs matched CON-EVs efficacy, and SMA-EVs supplementation effectively rescued the osteoporotic phenotype in SMA. Transcriptomics indicated impaired SNARE complex-mediated vesicle secretion pathway. We further demonstrated that deficiency of SMN protein drives downregulation of its downstream key SNARE component, SNAP23, thereby impairing the efficiency of SMA-EV secretion.ConclusionOur work elucidates a novel disease-specific mechanism for SMA osteoporosis—dysfunction of the SMN-SNAP23-EVs axis—and highlights the therapeutic potential of replenishing SMA-EVs or targeting this axis, offering a promising strategy to improve skeletal health in SMA.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12967-026-08475-6.

Introduction

Spinal muscular atrophy (SMA) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the survival motor neuron 1 (SMN1) gene, leading to a systemic deficiency of the SMN protein [1, 2]. With an incidence of approximately 1 in 10,000 live births and no sex predominance, SMA is classically defined by spinal motor neuron degeneration and progressive muscle atrophy, yet it is increasingly recognized as a multisystem disease [1, 3]. Among its systemic complications, osteoporosis represents a major clinical burden. SMA patients—particularly those with type I/II disease—exhibit a significantly higher prevalence of osteoporosis, with an estimated 50%–80% showing reduced bone mineral density (BMD), a finding especially prominent in children [1, 3]. Clinically, this manifests as low BMD, bone fragility, pathological fractures, and progressive spinal deformity, severely compromising quality of life in long-term survivors [4, 5].

The pathogenesis of osteoporosis in SMA remains incompletely elucidated. While disuse secondary to profound muscle weakness likely contributes, accumulating evidence points to an intrinsic, disease-specific mechanism. Notably, in SMA mouse models, skeletal abnormalities—including reduced BMD and impaired longitudinal bone growth—precede overt neuromuscular symptoms, directly implicating SMN deficiency in skeletal development [6]. Therefore, the skeletal problems exhibited by SMA patients are at least partially related to abnormalities in the SMN gene of the disease itself, rather than simply caused by disuse. This aligns with the established paradigm of muscle-bone crosstalk, in which skeletal muscle regulates bone metabolism not only through mechanical loading but also via secreted substances [7–9].

Extracellular vesicles derived from skeletal muscle have recently emerged as key mediators of muscle-bone crosstalk. These lipid bilayer nanoparticles, released by nearly all cell types including skeletal muscle cells, transport proteins, nucleic acids, and lipids to recipient cells near and far, thereby modulating cellular function [10, 11]. EVs secreted by healthy muscle have been shown to support bone homeostasis by promoting osteogenesis and suppressing osteoclastogenesis [10, 12]. Physiological bone remodeling depends on a tight balance between these processes; a sustained excess of osteoclastic resorption over osteoblastic formation leads to pathological bone loss [13]. Whether muscle derived EVs of SMA (SMA-EVs) are involved in the disrupted bone metabolism of SMA, however, is unknown.

EV biogenesis and secretion are governed by conserved cellular machinery, central to which are the soluble NSF attachment protein receptor (SNARE) complexes [14]. These proteins mediate the final fusion of multivesicular bodies with the plasma membrane—a critical step for EV release [14]. The core SNARE machinery in non-neuronal cells includes the widely expressed and universal SNARE protein SNAP23 [15]. Intriguingly, in the neuronal context of SMA, SMN deficiency is known to disrupt the assembly of SNARE complexes centered on SNAP25, the neuronal paralog of SNAP23 [16]. This raises a compelling, yet untested, hypothesis: that SMN may similarly regulate SNARE-dependent EV secretion in skeletal muscle, thereby contributing to non-neuronal pathologies such as osteoporosis.

Here, we hypothesize that SMN deficiency in skeletal muscle impairs EV secretion via downregulation of SNAP23, leading to a deficit in osteogenic SMA-EVs and consequent disruption of bone homeostasis in SMA. Integrating clinical observation, animal models, and molecular investigations, this study aims to: (1) characterize muscle derived EV alterations in SMA patients and mice; (2) define the functional role of SMA-EVs in bone remodeling; (3) establish a causal link between SMN deficiency, SNAP23 downregulation, and impaired EV secretion; and (4) evaluate the therapeutic potential of restoring SMA-EVs or SNAP23 to ameliorate SMA-associated osteoporosis. Our findings reveal a novel, extra-neurological role for SMN and identify a druggable axis for treating skeletal deficits in SMA.

Methods

Ethical statement

Human muscle specimens were obtained from bilateral paraspinal muscles discarded during surgery, collected from the apical vertebra in patients with SMA-associated scoliosis or from analogous vertebral levels in non-SMA controls. All enrolled patients underwent full-spine CT and MRI examinations as part of their clinical management. No additional risks were incurred during sample acquisition. This study was approved by the local institutional ethics committee (XHEC-D-2019-093) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to inclusion.

B6-3*hSMN2 mice (Catalog C001681, Cyagen), with the genotype Smn1hSMN2/hSMN2ROSA26hSMN2/+, were used to model SMA patients carrying three copies of the SMN2 gene. In humans, the SMN protein is encoded by two genes, SMN1 and SMN2. The loss of SMN1 function is the primary cause of spinal muscular atrophy (SMA). Its homologous gene, SMN2, carries a splicing defect; only about 10%–15% of its transcripts produce full-length, functional SMN protein, providing a limited compensatory function. Consequently, in SMA patients, a higher copy number of the SMN2 gene is associated with milder disease severity, ranging from the most severe form (often fatal before 2 years of age) to a nearly normal phenotype. However, mice possess only the Smn1 gene, and its knockout is embryonically lethal. Therefore, to model the human disease, SMA mouse models are generated by knocking out the murine Smn1 gene and inserting the human SMN2 gene, thereby recapitulating the genetic context and phenotypic spectrum observed in SMA patients. Wild-type C57BL/6 mice of the same strain served as healthy controls. Animals were housed under standard conditions (12-hour light cycle, conventional diet) in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment. A mixed-sex cohort of 6-week-old mice was randomly selected for experiments. All animal procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines and the ARRIVE guidelines (Animals in Research: Reporting In Vivo Experiments), and were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University (ZJU20241013).

Small animal micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT)

Femoral microarchitecture was analyzed in accordance with guidelines from the American Society for Bone and Mineral Research [17] and as previously described [18]. Distal femurs were scanned using a U-CT-XUHR small animal imaging system (Milabs, The Netherlands) with the following parameters: voltage 55 kV, current 170 µA, and exposure time 75 ms. Acquired images were reconstructed to generate representative 3D models of bone structure. Key bone parameters were quantified, including bone volume fraction (BV/TV), connectivity density (Conn.D), trabecular number (Tb.N), trabecular separation (Tb.Sp), cortical bone thickness (Cb.Th), and bone mineral density (BMD). Image processing and data analysis were performed using Imalytics Preclinical and ImageJ software.

Transcriptome sequencing

Transcriptome sequencing services were provided by Wuhan IGeneBook Biotechnology. Total RNA was extracted from patient skeletal muscle tissues, followed by quantification of RNA concentration and integrity assessment via agarose gel electrophoresis. High-throughput sequencing was subsequently performed on the Illumina NovaSeq platform (Illumina, USA). Differential expression analysis of RNA was conducted using R software packages, and Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed on the identified differentially expressed genes. The Benjamini–Hochberg procedure was applied to control the false discovery rate (FDR), yielding adjusted p‑values (p.adjust).

Extraction, characterization, and quantification of muscle derived EVs

Extracellular vesicles (EVs) were isolated from skeletal muscle tissue according to previously reported method [12]. All muscle tissues were collected, transported, and processed in a timely manner using identical protocols, ensuring comparable cell viability across groups. Briefly, weighed muscle samples (0.4–0.6 g) were minced and rinsed with saline. The tissue fragments were transferred to suspension culture flasks and incubated for 48 h at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. The supernatant was filtered through a 70 μm cell strainer and centrifuged at 3,500 × g for 30 min. Sequential filtration was then performed using 5 μm, 0.45 μm, and 0.22 μm filters. The filtrate was concentrated using a 100 kDa ultrafiltration tube at 3,500 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The concentrate was further centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was subjected to ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 60 min at 4 °C. The pellet was resuspended in 200µL PBS and washed by another round of ultracentrifugation at 100,000 × g for 60 min at 4 °C. The final pellet, representing isolated muscle-derived EVs, was resuspended and stored at -80 °C for subsequent experiments. For EV isolation from cultured cells, media were collected and processed using the same protocol.

EV characterization and quantification were performed according to guidelines from the International Society for Extracellular Vesicles (ISEV) [11]. Particle size distribution and concentration were analyzed using a Flow NanoAnalyzer (Xiamen Fuliu Bio-Technology, China), calibrated with standard beads prior to use. EV samples were diluted in PBS to fall within the instrument’s optimal detection range. Morphological assessment was conducted via Tecnai Spirit transmission electron microscopy (FEI, USA) operating at 120 kV. Samples were negatively stained using glow-discharged copper grids prior to imaging. The expression of EV marker proteins (Alix, CD9, TSG101) and the negative marker GM130 was verified by western blotting. Total protein concentration of EVs was determined using a BCA assay kit (P0010S, Beyotime Biotechnology, China), and total protein content was calculated.

For tissue‑derived EVs: muscles were weighed prior to EV isolation. EV release is expressed as particles per gram of tissue (particles/g tissue) and µg of EV protein per gram of tissue (µg EV protein/g tissue). For cell‑derived EVs: when isolating primary muscle cells, cells were counted before seeding to ensure an equal number of cells per well. This ensures that EV secretion data reflect cell‑autonomous differences rather than variations in cell input.

Isolation, culture, and induction of BMSCs and BMMs

Isolation, culture, and osteogenic induction of BMSCs

Bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs) were isolated, cultured, and induced as previously described [12]. Mice were euthanized, and femurs and tibiae were aseptically harvested. Bone marrow was flushed with α-MEM medium, and the cell suspension was plated in complete culture medium (α-MEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum and 1% penicillin-streptomycin) and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator. Medium was changed every 3 days. Fourth-passage BMSCs were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 1 × 10⁴ cells/well. After 24 h of culture in complete medium, the medium was replaced with osteogenic differentiation medium (#MUBMX-90021, Cyagen, USA). The differentiation medium was refreshed every 3 days.

Isolation, culture, and osteoclastogenic induction of BMMs

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMMs) were isolated and cultured according to Hou et al. [19]. Bone marrow cells flushed from femurs and tibiae were cultured overnight in complete medium. Adherent cells were discarded, and 20 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF, 416-ML, R&D Systems, USA) was added to the non-adherent cell fraction to obtain BMMs. For osteoclast differentiation, BMMs were treated with complete medium supplemented with 20 ng/mL M-CSF and 10 ng/mL receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL, #462-TEC, R&D Systems, USA), following the method of Huang et al. [20]. The medium was replaced every 2 days.

EV treatment

To investigate the effects of EVs on osteogenic differentiation of BMSCs and osteoclast differentiation of BMMs, cells were treated with EVs for 24 h prior to the induction of differentiation.

Distribution of EVs

EVs were labeled with the lipophilic fluorescent dye DiL (#D282, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After centrifugation to remove unincorporated dye, 200 µg of DiL-labeled EVs (1 µg/µL) were injected into mice via the tail vein. Mice were euthanized via pentobarbital sodium overdose. Major organs (brain, heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, bone, muscle) were harvested and visualized using an Ami HT small animal in vivo imaging system (Spectral Instruments Imaging, USA).

According to previously reported methods [12, 20], BMSCs or BMMs were incubated with 100 µg/mL DiL-labeled EVs at 37 °C for 1 h. Cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde, and stained with iFluor 488 Phalloidin (#40736ES75, Yeasen, China) for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (#G1012, Servicebio, China) for 15 min. Cellular uptake of EVs was visualized using a confocal microscope (Leica, Germany).

Live/dead cell staining

BMSCs or BMMs were treated with EVs for 24 h. Cell viability was assessed using a Calcein-AM/PI cell viability/cytotoxicity assay kit (#C2015M, Beyotime, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were observed under an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan). Fluorescent cell counts were performed using ImageJ software. Cell viability (%) was calculated as (Number of Calcein-AM-positive cells) / (Number of Calcein-AM-positive cells + Number of PI-positive cells) × 100.

Cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay

BMSCs were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 4 × 10³ cells/well and induced for osteogenic differentiation. Using a CCK-8 kit (#C0037, Beyotime, China), CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 1 h. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Thermo, USA) to establish the baseline (Day 0). Subsequently, cells were treated with EVs, and absorbance readings were taken every 24 h for 7 days following the same CCK-8 protocol.

Alkaline phosphatase (ALP) activity assay

ALP activity was measured using an ALP assay kit (#P0321S, Beyotime, China). Following the manufacturer’s instructions, reactions were set up in a 96-well plate with blank, standard, and sample wells. A mixture of sample/standard, chromogenic substrate, and assay buffer was incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. The reaction was terminated by adding 100 µL of stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 405 nm.

Reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR)

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (#15596018CN, Invitrogen, USA). RNA concentration and quality were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo, USA). Reverse transcription was performed with HiScript II RT SuperMix (#R323, Vazyme, China). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) was carried out using Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (#Q712, Vazyme, China) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex Real-Time PCR System (ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). Gene expression levels were calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table S1.

Staining and quantitative analysis of cells and tissues

Muscle tissue

Muscle samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining kit (#G1005, Servicebio, China). Bone Tissue: Bone samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and decalcified in 17% ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA). After paraffin embedding and sectioning (5 μm), sections were stained with H&E (#G1005, Servicebio, China) or for tartrate-resistant acid phosphatase (TRAP) activity using a TRAP staining kit (#G1050, Servicebio, China). For immunofluorescence staining, sections were incubated with a rabbit monoclonal anti-COL1 antibody (1:200, #ab270993, Abcam) overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with a goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 555 secondary antibody (1:500, #A21428, Invitrogen) for 30 min at 37 °C. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (#D8200, Solarbio) for 10 min. Cells: Cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and stained for TRAP activity (#G1050, Servicebio, China) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. All stained sections and cells were imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (Olympus, Japan) or a light microscope. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software (v1.8.0).

Primary muscle cell isolation

Primary muscle cells were isolated via enzymatic digestion as described by Xiao et al. [21]. Briefly, muscle tissues were harvested aseptically from 2-week-old euthanized mice, minced thoroughly, and digested with trypsin (#PB180225, Procell system, China) and collagenase type II (#LS004174, Worthington, USA) in a 37 °C water bath for 30 min. The digest was filtered through a 70 μm strainer and centrifuged at 500 × g for 4 min. The pellet was resuspended in specialized complete medium for mouse primary skeletal muscle cells (#CM-M084, Procell system, China) and plated for subsequent experiments. Primary muscle cells were isolated from Smn1 hSMN2/hSMN2 ROSA26 hSMN2/+ mice and their wild-type C57BL/6 littermates, designated as SMA and CON groups, respectively. Primary cells were also isolated from progeny of Smn1 hSMN2/hSMN2 ROSA26 hSMN2/+ mice identified to carry four copies of hSMN2, designated as the SMA + SMN group. For primary cell cultures, the same procedures were applied to all groups.

Gene knockdown and overexpression

CRISPR-Cas9-mediated SNAP-23 knockdown in cells

SNAP-23 knockdown was achieved using the CRISPR-Cas9 system (service provided by Genomeditech). Lentivirus was produced by transfecting HEK293T cells with the lentiCRISPR v2 vector containing a sgRNA targeting SNAP-23 (sequence: GGTCCAAGCCTTCTTCTATG) using Lipofectamine 3000 (#L3000015, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA). Viral supernatant was collected 36 h post-transfection, centrifuged to remove debris, and used to infect primary muscle cells, BMSCs, or BMMs. Infected cells were selected with 2.5 µg/mL puromycin (#P8230, Solarbio) for 7 days starting 48 h post-infection. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by western blotting.

SNAP-23 overexpression in cells

A plasmid for SNAP-23 overexpression was constructed (service provided by Genomeditech). The full-length SNAP-23 coding sequence (residues 1-211) was cloned into the pEGFP-C1 vector. Plasmids were transfected into muscle cells using Lipofectamine 3000. Overexpression was confirmed by western blotting 24 h post-transfection.

SNAP-23 overexpression in mouse muscle

For in vivo SNAP-23 overexpression, an AAV vector was constructed (service provided by Genomeditech). SNAP-23 cDNA was fused to the N-terminus of EGFP under the control of the muscle-specific MHCK7 promoter to generate the AAV plasmid (AAV9-mhck7-mSnap23-EGFP). The plasmid, along with pAAV-RC and pHelper plasmids, was co-transfected into HEK293T cells. AAV9 particles were harvested and purified 72 h later. A total of 100 µL AAV9 virus (titer: 1.0 × 10¹² vg/mL) was injected intramuscularly into the bilateral hindlimb muscles of mice (10 injections per limb, 10 µL per injection). Mice were sacrificed 4 weeks post-injection, and SNAP-23 overexpression in muscle tissue was confirmed by western blotting.

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from EVs, cells, or tissues using RIPA lysis buffer (#G2002, Servicebio, China) supplemented with 1% PMSF (#G2008, Servicebio, China). Samples were lysed on ice for 30 min and centrifuged at 4 °C for 15 min. Protein concentration in the supernatant was determined using a BCA assay kit (#P0010S, Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Proteins were denatured by boiling for 10 min, separated by 10% SDS-PAGE, and transferred onto PVDF membranes (#ISEQ00010, Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4 °C. After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:3000, #511203, Zenbio, China) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an ECL chemiluminescence kit (#G2074, Servicebio, China) and quantified using ImageJ software. Primary antibodies used in this study are listed in Table S2. Full uncropped western blot images are displayed in Figure S1.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. For in vitro experiments, ‘n’ represents the number of independent biological replicates. For in vivo experiments, ‘n’ represents the number of animals. Differences between two groups were analyzed using unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and Tukey’s HSD post‑hoc test was applied after one‑way ANOVA to correct for multiple comparisons. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Osteoporosis and muscle atrophy are prevalent in SMA patients and mice

Consistent with clinical observations, SMA patients exhibited significantly reduced bone density at the L1 vertebra compared to non-SMA controls with similar scoliosis, as quantified by CT grayscale values (Fig. 1A, B). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed severe muscle atrophy in SMA patients (Fig. 1C). Quantitative analysis confirmed a marked reduction in both total muscle cross-sectional area (CSA) and functional muscle CSA relative to vertebral size (Fig. 1D).

Mice with the genotype Smn1hSMN2/hSMN2ROSA26hSMN2/+ were used to model SMA patients carrying three copies of the SMN2 gene. Micro-CT of the distal femur revealed osteoporotic phenotypes, including decreased BV/TV, Conn.D, Tb.N, Cb.Th, and BMD, along with increased Tb.Sp (Fig. 1E, F). Histological analysis of quadriceps muscle confirmed significant muscle atrophy in SMA mice (Fig. 1G).

Intrigued by the muscle-bone coupling, we performed transcriptome sequencing on muscle from SMA patients. GO analysis of differentially expressed genes revealed a striking enrichment of pathways related to extracellular vesicles and secretion in the cellular component category (Fig. 1H), prompting us to investigate the role of muscle-derived EVs.

Secretion of muscle-derived EVs is impaired in SMA

We isolated EVs from human and mouse muscle tissue using a well-established differential ultracentrifugation protocol (Fig. 2A). Transmission electron microscopy confirmed the classic cup-shaped morphology of vesicles from both control (CON-EVs) and SMA (SMA-EVs) groups (Fig. 2B, H). Nanoflow analyzer showed a monodisperse size distribution peaking at 60–80 nm, with no significant difference between groups (Fig. 2C, I). Western blot analysis confirmed the presence of EV-positive markers (ALIX, CD9, TSG101) and the absence of the negative marker GM130 (Fig. 2D, J). The particle-to-protein ratio was comparable between groups (Fig. 2G, M).

Critically, however, both the particle number and total protein content of EVs secreted per gram of muscle tissue were significantly lower in SMA samples compared to controls, in both human and mouse models (Fig. 2E, F, K, L). This indicates an intrinsic defect in EV secretion capacity in SMA muscle, compounded by overall tissue loss.

SMA-EVs traffic to bone marrow and are internalized by BMSCs and BMMs

To trace the in vivo distribution, DiL-labeled SMA-EVs were intravenously injected into mice. Organ analysis revealed strong fluorescent signals in the bone marrow, liver, spleen, and lungs within 1 h, with signals persisting for up to 24 h (Fig. 3A, B). Notably, no signal was detected in skeletal muscle, suggesting a paracrine mode of action.

We then investigated whether bone marrow cells could take up these vesicles. In vitro, DiL-labeled SMA-EVs were efficiently internalized by both BMSCs and BMMs within 1 h of co-culture (Fig. 3C-F). These results demonstrate that SMA-EVs can systemically reach the bone marrow niche and be taken up by key cellular regulators of bone homeostasis.

SMA-EVs promote osteogenesis and inhibit osteoclastogenesis in a dose-dependent manner

Initial viability assays confirmed that SMA-EVs were not cytotoxic to BMSCs or BMMs (Fig. 4B). Functionally, treating BMSCs with SMA-EVs during osteogenic induction significantly upregulated the expression of key osteogenic markers (Runx2, Osterix, Ocn, Col-1, Alp, Bsp) and increased ALP activity (Fig. 4C, D). Conversely, treating BMMs with SMA-EVs during osteoclastogenic induction downregulated osteoclast-specific genes (TRAP, NFATc1, Cathepsin K and Mmp9) and reduced the number of TRAP-positive multinucleated cells (Fig. 4E, F). In vivo, intraperitoneal administration of SMA-EVs to SMA mice over three weeks increased Col-1 deposition and osteoblast numbers while decreasing TRAP activity and osteoclast numbers in bone sections (Fig. 4G-I). This pro-osteogenic and anti-osteoclastic activity was dose-dependent. Whether in vitro or in vivo, lower doses of SMA-EVs elicited diminished effects, and a saturating effect was observed at higher concentrations (Fig. 4).

At adequate doses, the bioactivity of SMA-EVs is comparable to control EVs

We next asked whether the function of EVs was altered in SMA beyond simple quantitative deficiency. At a maximally effective concentration (100 µg/mL in vitro, 200 µg per dose in vivo), SMA-EVs and healthy control CON-EVs showed largely comparable efficacy in promoting BMSC proliferation (CCK-8 assay) and osteogenic gene expression, and in suppressing osteoclast formation and related gene expression (Fig. 5C-H). Minor differences in a subset of markers (e.g., Osterix, Col-1) did not translate to significant differences in overall ALP activity, bone matrix formation (Col-1 immunofluorescence), or osteoblast/osteoclast numbers in vivo (Fig. 5I-K). These data suggest that the primary defect in SMA is a reduction in muscle derived EVs quantity rather than a severe qualitative impairment of their bone-modulating cargo.

Replenishing SMA-EVs ameliorates osteoporosis in SMA mice

To establish a direct causal link, we performed a therapeutic intervention. SMA mice received repeated injections of SMA-EVs over three weeks. This treatment significantly rescued the osteoporotic phenotype, as evidenced by increased Col-1 expression, increased osteoblast numbers, decreased osteoclast numbers in bone histomorphometry (Fig. 6A-C), and improvement in key Micro-CT parameters including BV/TV, Conn.D, Tb.Sp, Tb.N, Cb.Th, and BMD (Fig. 6D, E). This confirms that the deficit in muscle EVs is a major contributor to osteoporosis in SMA and that its replenishment is therapeutically viable.

Transcriptomic profiling implicates SNARE-mediated secretion pathways in SMA muscle

To elucidate the mechanism behind reduced EV secretion, we analyzed our muscle RNA-seq data from patients. Principal component analysis clearly separated SMA from control samples (Fig. 7A). SMN1 was among the most downregulated genes, while SMN2 showed compensatory upregulation in some patients (Fig. 7B, C), a finding confirmed at the protein level by Western blot (Fig. 7D, E). GO analysis again highlighted vesicle-related terms, especially secretory vesicles (Fig. 7F). Focusing on secretion machinery, we found significant dysregulation of several genes encoding SNARE complex proteins, with notable downregulation of three SNAP family members: SNAP29, SNAP47, and SNAP23 (Fig. 7G, Fig. S1). Among them, Snap29 and Snap47 mediate the membrane fusion of autophagosomes with lysosomes, a process that lies outside the scope of the present study [22, 23]. Given the established role of SNAP23 in exocytosis and EV release in non-neuronal cells, we validated the downregulation of SNAP23 at the protein level in SMA muscle (Fig. 7H, I).

SNAP23 is a key regulator of EV secretion in skeletal muscle

To test the functional role of SNAP23, we performed gain- and loss-of-function experiments in primary skeletal muscle cells. Knockdown of Snap23 using CRISPR-Cas9 significantly reduced EV secretion, while its overexpression enhanced secretion, in both control and SMA muscle cells (Fig. 8A-C). Critically, overexpressing Snap23 specifically in SMA muscle cells was sufficient to restore EV secretion to near-control levels without altering SMN protein expression (Fig. 8D-F). Conversely, Snap23 knockdown in BMSCs or BMMs did not affect their osteogenic or osteoclastogenic differentiation capacity (Fig. 8L, M), indicating its effect on bone is indirect via EV secretion.

In vivo, muscle-specific overexpression of Snap23 in SMA mice via AAV9 delivery successfully increased muscle SNAP23 levels and EV secretion (Fig. 8I-K) and, importantly, ameliorated the osteoporotic bone phenotype (Fig. 8G, H). These data establish SNAP23 as a crucial downstream effector impaired in SMA muscle, responsible for the EV secretion defect.

SMN protein regulates SNAP23 expression and EV secretion in muscle

Finally, we investigated the hierarchy between the primary genetic defect (SMN deficiency) and the secretory defect (SNAP23 downregulation). We analyzed muscle from SMA mice carrying an additional SMN2 transgene copy (SMA + SMN). Increased SMN expression in these mice correlated with elevated SNAP23 protein levels and a concomitant increase in muscle EV secretion (Fig. 9A-E). This relationship was recapitulated in primary muscle cells (Fig. 9F-J). Notably, manipulating SNAP23 did not affect SMN levels (Fig. 8D, E), placing SNAP23 downstream of SMN. Together, these results define a novel regulatory axis in skeletal muscle: SMN protein regulates SNAP23 expression and EV secretion in muscle. Considering that the core function of the SMN protein is to act as a molecular chaperone involved in the assembly of ribonucleoproteins, its deficiency may lead to abnormal pre‑mRNA splicing [24, 25]. We therefore further explored the mechanism by which SMN regulates Snap23 expression. The results showed that, compared with the CON group, the SMA group exhibited no significant change in pre‑mRNA levels of Snap23, but a marked decrease in mRNA levels (Fig. 9K-L). When comparing the SMA + SMN group with the SMA group, restoration of SMN expression did not significantly alter pre‑mRNA levels, but significantly increased mRNA levels (Fig. 9K-L). These findings suggest that SMN may regulate Snap23 expression by affecting the splicing of its pre‑mRNA.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the reduction of SMA-EVs disrupts their physiological regulation of bone homeostasis via muscle-bone crosstalk, representing a key mechanism underlying osteoporosis in SMA. This reduction in SMA-EVs is secondary to muscle atrophy on one hand, and on the other hand, results from SMN protein deficiency in skeletal muscle — SMN impairs SMA-EV secretion by downregulating the critical SNARE protein SNAP23 (Fig. 10). Our findings not only reveal a novel disease-specific mechanism for osteoporosis in SMA but also confirm the therapeutic potential of supplementing SMA-EVs or targeting the SMN-SNAP23 axis.

Skeletal health in SMA patients has garnered increasing attention in recent years. Previously, the greatest challenges for SMA patients were early mortality and loss of motor function due to neuromuscular disorders. It was only after the advent of SMN-targeting drugs for the nervous system since 2017, which altered the disease course, that ensuring patients’ quality of life began to receive gradual attention. In 2020, German researchers found reduced bone mineral density and Z-scores at the L1 vertebra in a cohort of SMA children (31 cases) [6]. A 2021 Chinese study reported that 67.5% (27/40) of type II/III SMA children with an average age of 5.5 years were diagnosed with low bone density [26]. A 2022 German study found that all children and adolescents with SMA had smaller vertebral body sizes compared to age-matched healthy controls [27]. This indicates the presence of abnormal skeletal development in SMA patients, with clinical phenotypes emerging as early as childhood. Similarly, features such as reduced bone density, osteoporosis, decreased bone volume, and caudal vertebral dysplasia have been observed in multiple SMA mouse models [28, 29]. Our results also show that the grayscale value of the L1 vertebra is lower in SMA patients compared to the CON group, and reduced bone density and osteoporosis were found in SMA model mice.

Whether osteoporosis in SMA is solely a secondary consequence of disuse due to motor neuron and muscle defects, an inherent mechanism of SMA independent of disuse, or a combination of both, remains inconclusive. Traditionally, reduced mechanical loading resulting from muscle atrophy has been considered a major factor. However, this study and recent literature suggest that SMN deficiency itself can directly impair skeletal development, indicating the existence of a disease-inherent mechanism independent of “disuse“ [6]. Skeletal muscle and bone are synergistic organs in movement, structurally and functionally coupled from the embryonic stage to old age, communicating bidirectionally through multiple pathways including mechanical forces and myokines [7–9]. This interaction is termed muscle-bone crosstalk [7–9]. The traditional view held that muscle-bone crosstalk relied on mechanical regulation, as proposed by Frost’s “Mechanostat Theory” in 1987 [30]. However, recent research reveals that muscle-bone interactions extend far beyond mechanics: skeletal muscle secretes hundreds of myokines, and merely upregulating specific myokines can improve bone loss [31]. Among these, myokines with a molecular weight less than 40 kDa can passively diffuse through the semi-permeable periosteum to reach adjacent tissues, while those larger than 40 kDa primarily rely on systemic circulation or muscle-derived EVs for transport [32]. Our transcriptome sequencing of muscle tissue from SMA patients indicated that differentially expressed genes were significantly associated with vesicles. We found that in both SMA patients and model mice, atrophied muscle also exhibits a significant decrease in the “EV secretory output per unit mass,” collectively leading to a severe systemic deficiency of circulating muscle derived EVs. Therefore, bone loss in SMA may result from a “double hit”: reduced muscle mass coupled with impaired secretory function of the remaining muscle.

We confirmed that SMA-EVs can be taken up by BMSCs and BMMs in the bone marrow, demonstrating the potential of muscle EVs to mediate muscle-bone crosstalk. It is well-established that skeletal muscle secretes a large number of EVs [12, 20], a finding corroborated by our results. These muscle EVs can be transported via the circulatory system and reach recipient tissues and cells to exert biological functions [12]. The abundant vascular connections between the periosteal circulation outside the bone cortex and the bone marrow provide a plausible route for circulating EVs to reach the marrow and exert effects [33, 34]. Our study found that intravenously injected muscle EVs can enter the bone marrow and be internalized by BMSCs and BMMs, where they perform corresponding functions.

Our subsequent research revealed that SMA-EVs promote osteogenesis and inhibit osteoclastogenesis in a dose-dependent manner, further indicating the capacity of muscle EVs to mediate bone remodeling. Bone remodeling involves bone formation and bone resorption, and its imbalance is considered a core pathogenic mechanism of osteoporosis [13]. Bone formation is carried out by BMSCs, which ultimately differentiate into osteocytes, while bone resorption is mediated by osteoclasts derived from BMMs [13]. Our experimental evidence shows that SMA-EVs taken up by BMSCs and BMMs in the marrow promote osteogenic differentiation and inhibit osteoclast formation, ultimately favoring bone formation. Huang et al. [20] demonstrated that EVs from healthy muscle have similar effects. As for the functional cargo, previous studies have shown that skeletal muscle-derived EVs can transport glycolytic enzymes to mediate muscle–bone crosstalk [12]. The cargo composition of muscle EVs is complex, involving thousands of proteins as well as various miRNAs. Sequencing datasets (e.g., PXD068287, PXD045797, PXD000022) may facilitate future investigations. Therefore, the specific functional components within muscle EVs that are responsible for promoting osteogenesis and inhibiting osteoclast activity are still at an exploratory stage.

We further compared the efficacy of SMA-EVs and CON-EVs to determine whether disease-specific alterations in SMA-EV composition affect bone formation. The results showed that at equivalent doses, their effects were largely comparable. This differs from the reported functional changes in EVs from a muscle atrophy model induced by botulinum toxin A (BoNT/A) [12]. We offer the following perspectives on this discrepancy: First, differences in results are understandable given the different models studied. Second, the dose used in our experiments was the dose required for maximal EV efficacy, which is not necessarily the physiological dose (the physiological dose is difficult to assess and thus cannot be determined). A sufficient quantity of EVs might compensate for differences in biological effects caused by compositional variations, but this precisely suggests that changes in EV quantity have a greater impact on their role in bone metabolism. Furthermore, from a therapeutic translation standpoint, quantitative supplementation is far safer and more reliable than modifying EV composition. Restoring EV quantity alone may be sufficient to produce therapeutic effects, which is more straightforward and feasible than attempting to correct their complex cargo. This also highlights the greater significance of focusing our research on the mechanisms underlying the reduced vesicle secretion in SMA.

In this study, we observed reduced EV secretion from atrophic muscles, and our in vitro and in vivo experiments suggested that muscle-derived EVs can directly affect bone cell function. However, we cannot ignore that part of the bone loss in SMA patients and animal models may result from reduced mechanical loading. In fact, these factors are likely interconnected: muscle atrophy and reduced physical activity lead to both decreased mechanical loading [7] and reduced EV secretion [35]; even reduced mechanical loading alone can lead to decreased EV secretion [36]. These factors coexist in SMA and collectively contribute to the development of osteoporosis. From a translational perspective, not everyone is able to engage in physical activity and muscle-strengthening exercises, which is particularly challenging for SMA patients. Therefore, this study investigated the therapeutic efficacy of directly supplementing muscle-derived EVs and found that it effectively ameliorates osteoporosis in SMA, representing a promising biological therapy. Our study demonstrates that reduced muscle EVs in SMA is one of the important factors contributing to osteoporosis, adding a new dimension to the classic muscle–bone crosstalk paradigm.

Mechanistically, we linked SMN deficiency to defective EV secretion, revealing for the first time in a non-neuronal tissue (skeletal muscle) that SMN is a key regulator of SNARE-mediated vesicle secretion. Dysfunction of the SNARE complex can lead to the accumulation of multivesicular bodies near the plasma membrane, preventing their fusion and subsequent EV release [37]. SNAP23 has been identified as a core SNARE protein essential for EV secretion in various non-neuronal cells [38]. Our functional experiments demonstrate that downregulation of SNAP23 is the direct cause of reduced muscle EV secretion in SMA, and overexpressing SNAP23 is sufficient to rescue the secretion defect and improve bone phenotypes. Furthermore, we explored how SMN regulates Snap23. Our experimental results demonstrate that SMN deficiency leads to a decrease in Snap23 mRNA levels by affecting the splicing process of Snap23 pre‑mRNA. This confirms the important function of SMN in RNA processing and metabolism [24, 25]. This expands the functional understanding of the SMN protein: beyond regulating the assembly of the SNAP25-centric SNARE complex in neurons [16], SMN similarly regulates its homolog SNAP23 in muscle, thereby influencing a novel downstream pathological process—impaired EV-mediated inter-organ communication.

This mechanistic discovery has straightforward translational value. Current disease-modifying therapies for SMA (such as SMN gene replacement or splicing modulation) primarily aim to increase SMN levels in the central nervous system to rescue motor neurons [2, 3]. Our research indicates that increasing SMN (or its downstream effector SNAP23) in muscle tissue is equally crucial, holding promise for concurrently ameliorating osteoporosis—a severe peripheral complication—by restoring SMA-EV secretion, achieving a multifaceted therapeutic effect from a single target. Furthermore, directly supplementing SMA-EVs themselves represents a promising biological therapy. Compared to traditional anti-resorptive drugs or exogenous stem cell transplantation, SMA-EVs offer potential advantages such as high stability, low immunogenicity, and ease of storage and administration [32].

It is necessary to clarify the key methodological choices in this study.

(1) Patient Controls and Bone Density Assessment: The control group consisted of non-SMA scoliosis patients with curvature types and angles similar to those of the SMA patients. This selection aimed to exclude the secondary effects of scoliosis itself—such as muscle atrophy and biomechanical imbalance—on bone density, thereby allowing us to focus on disease-specific mechanisms [39]. Due to ethical constraints, no additional imaging was performed solely for research purposes. Bone density was assessed by analyzing vertebral CT values from pre-existing, pre-operative spinal CT scans, a method demonstrated to be reliable [13].

(2) Animal Model Selection: Experiments were conducted using a mouse model corresponding to human type II SMA. In SMA, type 0-I patients have severe disease with over 90% mortality before age 2, while type III-IV patients exhibit milder symptoms and can walk independently. Type II patients, representing an intermediate phenotype, show significant motor function limitations and prominent musculoskeletal complications, making them highly relevant for clinical study [1, 2]. This patient group is prone to scoliosis or pathological fractures requiring surgical intervention, and the clinical specimens and imaging data were sourced from such patients. As SMA is an autosomal recessive disorder with no sex predilection [1], mice were not separated by sex in the experiments.

(3) EV Administration Route: Intraperitoneal injection was adopted for EV supplementation instead of tail vein injection, as SMA model mice exhibit tail defects (e.g., shortening, necrosis) that preclude repeated, high-dose intravenous administration. Studies indicate that both routes enable EVs to enter the systemic circulation and subsequently reach the bone marrow [40, 41]. Intramuscular injection was not used due to volume limitations.

Limitations and future perspectives of this study

We have not yet identified the specific cargo components (e.g., particular miRNAs, proteins, or lipids) within SMA-EVs responsible for their pro-osteogenic and anti-osteoclastogenic activities. Future studies are planned to perform sequencing of EVs to analyze and identify the key effector molecules. Secondly, skeletal muscle is composed of heterogeneous muscle fiber types, which may secrete functionally distinct EV subpopulations [42]. Developing techniques to isolate EVs from specific muscle fiber types in the future will deepen our understanding of muscle-bone communication. Thirdly, although we removed the vast majority of impurities from the skeletal muscle samples, trace amounts of adipose tissue or vascular endothelial cells within the muscle may be difficult to completely remove. More precise and efficient isolation techniques need to be developed in the future. It is worth noting that SNARE family proteins (e.g., Syntaxin 4, SNAP23, Syntaxin 12/13) are also involved in regulating inflammatory signaling and cytokine release. For instance, LPS stimulation of macrophages rapidly upregulates the expression of the Syntaxin 4/SNAP23/Munc18c complex, which directly controls the rate-limiting secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNFα [43]; Syntaxin 12/13, in turn, participates in the regulation of endolysosomal morphology and inflammaging [44]. Based on these findings, the downregulation of SNAP23 and the defect in vesicle secretion observed in our study may also contribute to SMA pathology by affecting immune cell function or systemic inflammation. This represents a potential research direction that warrants further investigation in the future, and would provide new perspectives for understanding the multisystem mechanisms of SMA.
