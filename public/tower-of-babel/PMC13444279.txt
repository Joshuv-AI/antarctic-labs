Life threatening acute complication after routine catheter exchange—The importance of planning and preparedness in risk cases
Authors: Yngve Forslin, David Andrei Patrauceanu, Maria Vinell, Octavian Petru Nitescu, Alexia Maria Neagu, Martin Delle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEndovascular procedures after abdominal surgery may carry risks for serious complications. Radical nephrectomy is preferred for renal cell carcinomas but carries risks including hemorrhage (1.2%), abscesses (0.7%-6.1%). Percutaneous abdominal drainage procedures carry hemorrhagic risks in 1%-2% of cases. An 88-year-old male with recent left sided nephrectomy developed a retroperitoneal abscess requiring drainage. During routine catheter upsizing, massive hemorrhage occurred due to injury of the left renal artery stump by the larger catheter. Emergency endovascular intervention was performed. Initial aortic stent graft placement alone proved insufficient due to anatomical constraints. Complete hemostasis was achieved through adding coil embolization of the arterial stump, while the partial stent coverage prevented coil migration. This case emphasizes that routine interventional procedures can precipitate life-threatening complications even in experienced hands.Keywords: Hemorrhage control, Abdominal compartment syndrome, Retroperitoneal abscess, Percutaneous catheter drainage, Endovascular intervention, Renal artery stump, Radical nephrectomy

Introduction

Radical nephrectomy, sometimes considered beneficial for more extensive renal cell carcinomas (RCC), entails undesirable effects and risks that the clinician must take into account [1]. Such risks include immediate severe hemorrhage, 1.2% of cases, surgical site infection (0.7%-6.1%), sepsis (3.1%) and acute thromboembolism (0.9%) [2]. Within 30 days of surgery, patient death occurred in 1.3% of cases [2].

Percutaneous drainage is a basic part of the standard treatment for most intra-abdominal abscesses. The primary drainage methods include percutaneous needle aspiration and percutaneous catheter drainage [3]. Catheter upsizing by using Seldinger technique represents a salvage technique for persistent poorly drained abscesses despite adequate catheter positioning. Studies indicate that upsizing is required in at least 1.5% [4,5]. However, as this case will show, careful handling of guidewires and catheters is essential, as inflamed and fragile tissues near major vessels increase the risk of hemorrhage.

Case presentation

A male patient, 88 years old, with a history of left sided nephrectomy as treatment for RCC (Fig. 1A), developed a left-sided retroperitoneal abscess 3 months after surgery. The abscess, containing thick, feces-like material, pus, and air, was initially managed with a 10 French (F) (Merrit Resolve) locking pigtail catheter, which was placed with ultrasound guidance, using a 1-step technique (Fig. 1B).

The area marked within the blue line is the abscess). (C) Blue Arrow = Guidewire; Green Arrow = 16F Catheter on its way to the abscess; Red Arrow = Left renal artery stump; Yellow Arrow = Surgical clips. (D) Blue Arrow = Amplatz guidewire; Green Arrow = 16F Catheter; Red Arrow = Left renal artery stump; Yellow Arrow = Surgical clips. The patient was admitted for drainage catheter exchange and upsizing due to suboptimal drainage of fecal content and remaining clinical signs of infection. Preprocedural computed tomography (CT) imaging was performed to delineate the abscess cavity with anatomical surroundings and plan the catheter placement. The abscess cavity was adherent to the left renal artery (LRA) stump. The chronic inflammatory process had created a direct interface between the abscess wall and the arterial stump. Moreover, suspected fistulas were seen from the small bowel and colon.

The initial catheter was removed over an Amplatz guidewire using standard technique. CT confirmation of guidewire position was obtained before proceeding with tract dilation.

The drainage tract was successfully dilated using a balloon dilatator through the abdominal wall to accommodate a 16F (Cook Gordon large-bore drainage) catheter. However, immediately following insertion of the larger catheter (Fig. 1C), large volumes of pulsating blood emerged from the catheter, indicating an arterial bleeding. Emergency measures implemented included immediate catheter capping and manual abdominal compression at the drainage site and emergency call to the acute anesthesiology team.

As the patient was already on the CT-table, an initial noncontrast CT imaging could promptly be performed once the patient achieved hemodynamic stabilization, followed by arterial phase contrast-enhanced CT angiography (Fig. 1D-F). Imaging revealed active extravasation from the LRA stump, with the drainage catheter positioned in close proximity to this vascular structure (Fig. 2A).

(A) Extravasation is seen from the left renal artery (Red arrow); Blue Arrow = Drainage catheter. (B) After stent placement, it can be seen that the stump was not completely covered and extravasation had not ceased; Red Arrow = Left renal artery stump; Orange Arrow = Stent graft; Purple Arrow = Right renal artery. (C) Deployment of the coils in order to further stop the bleeding.

Following multidisciplinary consultation, the patient was transferred to the hybrid angiography suite for emergency endovascular intervention.

Upon arrival in the angiography suite, the patient developed hypotension and continued bleeding. Under the lightest sterile conditions, a 7F introducer was placed in the left femoral artery, and a resuscitative endovascular balloon occlusion of the aorta (ER-REBOA PLUS, Prytime Medical Boerne, TX) was deployed suprarenally for proximal hemorrhage control.

The 20 × 48 mm balloon-expandable stent graft (BeGraft, Bently InnoMed) was appropriately sized for the infrarenal aortic diameter. However, due to a 60-degree angulation at the renal segment of the aorta, the proximal end of the stent graft could not be positioned high enough to achieve complete coverage of the LRA ostium without risking coverage of the contralateral right renal artery, which originated only 7 mm above the LRA stump. Positioning the stent graft more proximally would have at least partially occluded the right renal artery, which was unacceptable in a patient with a solitary functioning kidney. Aortic angiography confirmed continued extravasation through the uncovered superior portion of the LRA ostium (Fig. 2B). The stent graft did, however, partially cover the inferior aspect of the LRA ostium, creating a mechanical scaffold across the lower portion of the branch origin. This partial coverage was then used deliberately as a platform for a coil-jailing technique: a 14 × 40 mm Concerto coil was deployed into the LRA stump, followed by 2 additional 6 mm Concerto coils. The partial stent graft coverage across the inferior ostium served as a physical barrier that prevented the coils from migrating or being displaced into the aortic lumen by the high-flow arterial environment (Fig. 2C). Postembolization angiography confirmed complete cessation of extravasation with no residual bleeding. The patient showed immediate signs of recovering from hemodynamic instability.

However, due to the significant blood loss and development of abdominal compartment syndrome with visible intraperitoneal fluid on ultrasound examination, an additional 7F pigtail catheter was placed in the right paracolic gutter for drainage of accumulated blood.

Wide spectrum antibiotics were kept during the aftercare. At discharge from the hospital the patient continued antibiotic treatment with metronidazole and ciprofloxacin. The patient showed postoperatively fast recovery and declining signs of the infection. CT scan after 9 months showed full regression of the abscess (Fig. 3).

Nine-months postprocedural follow up—no signs of any remaining abscess.

Discussion

This case shows that a seemingly uncomplicated procedure has the potential to end up in a life-threatening situation. It is always important to, as far as possible, try to be mentally prepared for severe complications and to have a number of endovascular options “on shelf” as a bail out.

The risk for hemorrhage that occurred in this case during the catheter exchange could perhaps have been predicted. The literature confirms that hemorrhage as a complication during percutaneous procedures can occur in 1%-2% of cases [6]. In this case a couple of factors may have contributed. The use of the stiff amplatz-guidewire made a curve that probably pushed against the artery stump. When the 16F catheter was inserted, the mechanically pushed against the stump when going along the stiff amplatz-guidewire trauma most likely ruptured LRA stump (Figs. 1C and D).

The decision to upsize to a 16F catheter was driven by the nature of the collection, which contained thick, feces-like material and debris that had proven refractory to drainage through the initial 10F catheter. While standard practice surveys indicate that 8-12F catheters are most frequently used for initial percutaneous abscess drainage, larger-bore catheters (14F and above) are well-recognized as necessary for collections containing viscous, particulate, or necrotic material [7].

It is likely that the vessel residual/LRA-stump was fragile due to the abscess and inflammation in the area, which increased its sensitivity to trauma from the insertion of the 16F catheter. Abscess drainage carries intrinsic risks of hemorrhagic complications. If bleeding occurs, it is by many generally considered important to obtain follow-up imaging prior to repositioning or withdrawing the instrument. Premature removal from a major vessel can precipitate life threatening hemorrhage. By keeping the catheter in place and cap the outflow of blood, might have been an important factor that gave enough time to save the patient in our case [8,9]. There are cases when the removal of the catheter in a similar situation with acute bleeding is thought to have factored into the death of the patient [9].

Among the procedural factors that may have contributed to the vascular injury, the absence of real-time fluoroscopic guidance during the catheter exchange warrants discussion. Although CT confirmation of guidewire position was obtained before proceeding with tract dilation, the dynamic phases of the procedure—dilation and catheter insertion—were performed without continuous imaging. Real-time fluoroscopy would have provided continuous visualization of the guidewire trajectory and catheter tip during advancement, potentially revealing the deflection toward the renal artery stump before the larger catheter caused mechanical trauma. The use of CT for the exchange, while providing superior anatomical detail for preprocedural planning, did not offer this critical real-time safety advantage during the most hazardous phases of the procedure [10].

The proximal aortic balloon occlusion (REBOA) gave immediate hemodynamic control and gave some time to plan the next steps. The initial attempt at stent graft placement alone was unsuccessful due to inadequate proximal seal at the orifice of the LRA, a known limitation when treating short and angled infrarenal necks, like in this case. Positioning of the stent graft more proximally would have at least partially covered to the right renal artery. The addition of selective coil embolization provided definitive vessel occlusion while the stent graft minimized the risk of coil migration into the aorta, which was a significant concern given the short length of the arterial stump. Coil embolization solely was not the first choice of treatment because of the high risk of coil migration due to the high flow in the arterial system and the short landing area. This could have resulted in embolization in the lower legs or pelvic region. Furthermore, a vascular plug was excluded due to the presence of arterial wall inflammation, which may have rendered the device insufficient vessel wall support for achieving effective vessel occlusion.

In this case, the presence of foreign material near the infected area may be a potential (but acceptable) problem (graft infection, fiber coil infection, etc.). In the context of urgent therapeutic needs (massive hemorrhage, arterial rupture on a septic background), in the temporary control of infection or vascular injury, or in the case of contraindications for open surgery, the implantation of endovascular devices in infected sites may be acceptable [11,12].

Conclusion

This case demonstrates that extra vigilance must be maintained during routine endovascular procedures close to large vessels, as complications can occur unpredictably despite appropriate technique and planning. First, the proximity of the drainage tract to the renal artery stump was identifiable on preprocedural imaging, suggesting that alternative approaches or additional precautionary measures might have been considered. Second, the inflammatory tissues surrounding chronic abscesses may increase vascular fragility and bleeding risk during mechanical manipulation.

Last but not least, the technical challenges associated with treating vessel segments with complex anatomy are well-recognized, often requiring creative solutions and hybrid approaches as demonstrated in this case [13]. Preprocedural imaging, material selection and planning as well as bail out plans are of importance, especially if the anatomical region to be treated is hostile in terms of anatomy and inflammation.
