Identifying diagnostic markers and constructing a prognostic model for pancreatic cancer based on microarray and bioinformatic analysis
Authors: Chang Liu, Dan Qian, Ye Tian, Ying Yang, Menglu Li, Yiqin You, Liqun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPancreatic cancer (PC) is one of the leading causes of cancer-related death worldwide. The lack of effective diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets makes PC difficult to screen and treat. The aim of this study was to develop a diagnostic and survival-related gene signature for PC to construct a prognostic model.MethodsAn Arraystar RNA microarray was used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in clinical plasma samples between the PC group and the control group. We performed weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) to identify significant modules of DEGs in the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) cohort and to obtain potential diagnostic hub genes by intersecting the significant module genes with microarray-derived DEGs. In addition, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) cox regression analysis were performed to construct a prognostic model. Moreover, clinical samples were analyzed to evaluate the expression levels of the independent risk genes.ResultsOur microarray data revealed 228 significantly upregulated mRNA in plasma samples. FERMT1, S100A14, KCNN4, PKM, and ITGA3 were identified as robust diagnostic biomarkers. Integrating these hub genes, we constructed a prognostic model, with the nomogram exhibiting prognostic value in TCGA cohort. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazard analyses revealed that the expression of FERMT1, S100A14, and ITGA3 was an independent risk factor for poor prognosis. RT-qPCR validation in clinical plasma sample demonstrated concordant expression patterns of the independent risk gene, further supporting its potential prognostic relevance.ConclusionOur results revealed the potential biomarkers for the prediction of PC prognosis in addition to clinicopathological factors. Moreover, this study identifies potential biomarkers warranting further functional investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-04933-1.Keywords: Pancreatic cancer (PC), Microarray, Prognosis, Bioinformatics analysis, Diagnostic markers

Introduction

Pancreatic cancer (PC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Due to the subtle and asymptomatic nature of localized disease and the paucity of diagnostic biomarkers for early-stage tumors, routine tumor office-based screening remains a challenge, leading to low surgical resection rates and distant metastasis. Chemotherapy offers limited efficacy for advanced PC, with a 5-year overall survival rate of just 13% [2]. Although numerous treatments for PC have been proposed in recent years, patient survival remains poor due to therapeutic resistance [3]. Therefore, a more in-depth study of the underlying molecular framework and alterations that drive PC oncogenesis is necessary to provide effective treatment strategies for patients. Moreover, the initial diagnostic stage of the tumor is significantly related to disease prognosis. Thus, further research on molecular markers for the early diagnosis, prognosis, and therapeutic targeting of PC is needed.

With the development of transcriptomic analysis and genomic microarrays [4, 5], bioinformatics analysis has provided an effective way to explore the etiology, diagnosis, and novel targets for clinical treatment. Previous studies demonstrating that PC arises through a multistep series of precursor lesions have generated substantial interest in the discovery of disease-related biomarkers for early detection, especially in high-risk cohorts. Numerous studies [6–8] have shown the potential of circulating tumor-derived analytes as potential biomarkers for PC clinical applications. Notably, the potential role of messenger RNA (mRNA) in the prognosis and diagnosis of PC remains poorly understood.

In this study, we investigated differentially expressed mRNA from plasma samples of PC patients using Arraystar Microarray Technology (KANGCHEN, Shanghai, China). Moreover, we integrated the data with the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/) and The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) databases, The Clinical Proteomic Tumor Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) databases (https://pdc.cancer.gov/pdc/) and The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) (https://www.proteinatlas.org/) through systematic bioinformatics methods to explore hub genes that are closely associated with the pathogenesis and progression of PC. The expression level of the independent risk biomarkers were quantified by qPCR in clinical plasma samples from PC patients and healthy controls.

The findings of this study are expected to improve the diagnosis and prognosis of PC and provide a novel platform for molecular therapeutic strategies. The study workflow is shown in Fig. 1.

Materials and methods

Data collection

All PC-related datasets, including the TCGA and GEO databases, were obtained from public databases. The RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) expression pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) data of 169 PC tissue samples and 4 normal tissue samples with corresponding clinical information were downloaded from TCGA. In addition, GSE62452 (n = 69 pancreatic tumors, n = 61 adjacent nontumor tissues) and GSE71989 (n = 14 pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma tissues, n = 8 normal pancreatic tissues) were retrieved from the GEO database and merged as training datasets for weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA). For the validation dataset, the gene expression matrix of the GSE16515 dataset (n = 36 pancreatic tumors, n = 16 non-tumor tissue) and GSE15471 datasets (n = 36 pancreatic tumors, n = 36 normal pancreatic tissue) were obtained. Proteomic profiling was performed using the CPTAC database (n = 137 pancreatic tumors, n = 74 normal pancreatic tissue), along with immunohistochemistry (IHC) data for normal pancreatic and cancerous tissues from the HPA.

Clinical sample collection and array expression analysis

A total of 30 patients diagnosed with PC and 30 healthy volunteers were enrolled in the present study. The inclusion criteria for the PC cohort were as follows: (1) Patients prior to surgical resection in our hospital; (2) Patients who had been pathologically diagnosed with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma; (3) No previous chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and other special treatment. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients younger than 18 years of age; (2) Unclear pathological diagnosis; (3) Patients with other types of tumors or chronic diseases. Healthy controls were enrolled based on criteria: (1) Age-matched to the PC group. (2) No history of cancer, chronic diseases and major systemic diseases. Subjects were excluded for neuropsychiatric conditions and acute inflammation. The serum tumor marker CA199 (0-37u/ml) was tested in PC patients and healthy volunteers using the Abbott i2000SR system.

The plasma samples used in this study were obtained with informed consent from the participants, and the research procedures were approved by the Ethics Review Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Army Medical University (Approval No.2024-154-01).

Peripheral venous blood was collected from each patient and healthy volunteer between February to May 2023. The red blood cells and plasma were separated by centrifuging peripheral blood samples at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 ℃. The separated plasma was immediately transferred to RNasefree tubes and stored at − 80 ℃ until RNA extraction, with a median storage duration of 4 months prior to analysis. Before the microarray analysis, 30 plasma samples were mixed into one with an equal volume from the patient group, and the same was performed for the healthy group. A total of 3 replicate chips were set up for each group. Arraystar mRNA Microarray Technology was used to analyze the differential expression of mRNA between the PC group and the control group.

Co-expression analysis

WGCNA [9] was performed with the “WGCNA” R package (Version 4.3.1; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) to screen significant modules of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the training datasets. In the WGCNA analysis, all paired genes were subjected to Pearson correlation analysis to construct a correlation matrix, soft-threshold power values(β) and the best parameter were set to establish a scale-free network. In general, there were six steps to screen the hub module with WGCNA: (1) calculation of the expression and connectivity correlations of mRNAs between samples and paired samples; (2) election of the soft threshold power (β) in the light of the scale-free topology criterion; (3) estimation of the topological overlap matrix dissimilarity between genes to establish the dendrogram and distinguish the modules by the dynamic tree cut method; (4) evaluation of the preservation (Z-score > 5 and p < 0.05) of modules in samples and paired samples with the module preservation statistics; (5) enrichment of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) to modules with the hypergeometric algorithm [f (k, N, M, n) = C (k, M)*C (n-k, N-M)/C (n, N)]; and (6) relation of coexpression modules with pancreatic cancer. According to the results of module-trait relationships, modules showing high correlation were chosen as further research objects, and genes in the key module were denominated as hub genes relevant with pancreatic cancer. The gene significance (GS) and module membership (MM) were also measured to evaluate the significance of genes and the correlation of gene expression profile with module eigengenes.

Enrichment analysis

To further investigate the potential biological functions of the merged significant module genes, the “cluster Profiler, enrichplot, DOSE, ggplot2” R package (Version 4.3.1) was utilized for Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways [10]. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Screening and validation of critical markers

Genes in the module were selected as candidate hub markers using GS > 0.50 and MM > 0.80 as the screening criteria. The significant module genes were intersected with the differentially expressed Arraystar-derived mRNA. The overlapping DEGs were subjected to least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) algorithm analysis with the “glmnet” package (Version 4.3.1) to screen the hub genes as critical markers. Finally, the expression levels and the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis of the hub genes were used to evaluate the diagnostic value of the identified hub genes in the training datasets and validation datasets. The GSE16515 and GSE15471 datasets was selected as the validation cohort to evaluate the expression level and diagnostic value of the pivotal genes by using ROC curves and area under the curve (AUC) values. The protein expression of hub genes was validated using CPTAC datasets and IHC analysis from HPA database. A P-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Establishment and validation of profiling based on critical markers

With the predictive critical DEGs signatures, the risk scores for the PC patients were calculated with the Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) method, featured with preserving valuable variables and avoiding overftting. Subsequently, every patient’s risk score for OS was built based on a linear combination of the regression coefficient derived from the multivariate Cox regression model and the expression level of the optimized genes (i.e., the signature genes). That is, risk score=∑ iCoefficient(i)×Expression of signature gene(i). Coefficient of gene (i) represents the regression coefficient of gene (i) in LASSO-Cox regression model and Expression of gene (i) is the expression value of gene (i) for each patient. According to the median of risk score, patients were grouped into high- and low-risk groups. Besides, the Kaplan-Meier survival was drawn to evaluate the predictive value of the prognostic gene signature for OS using the software “Graphpad prism8.0”.

Construction and assessment of the nomogram

To further estimate the availability of the risk score, patients with clinicopathological information including the age at diagnosis, and tumor stage from the TCGA datasets, were enrolled. All characteristics were evaluated as categorical variables. Also, the TCGA cohort was used to establish a nomogram for predicting 3-, and 5‐year OS rates of PC patients. Calibration plot was constructed with R packages “pROC” to evaluate the nomogram’s efficiency.

Correlation analysis between the critical markers and clinical features

To further verify whether the survival of PC patients was related to hub genes, the datasets of TCGA were used to estimate the relationships between the hub genes and survival rate. Survival curves of the hub genes were plotted with the “survival” R package (Version 4.3.1). A P-value < 0.05 for the survival-related hub genes was considered to indicate statistical significance. Correlations between candidate biomarker genes and clinical parameters were also evaluated. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted with clinical features, including age, sex, grade, and stage, to analyze the independent prognostic value of the survival-related genes.

RNA extraction and quantitative real-time PCR

Total RNA was isolated from peripheral blood using the RNA simple Total RNA Kit (TIANGEN, Beijing, DP419) following the manufacturer’s protocol. qRT-PCR were performed using a one-step TB Green PrimeScript RT-PCR Kit II (Takara, Japan, RR086A). Amplification cycles were carried out on an Applied Biosystems QuantStudio (Hongshi, Shanghai, SLAN96P) under the following conditions: 95 °C for 10 s, 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s, 55 °C for 30 s and 72 °C for 30 s. β-actin served as the endogenous control for normalization. Fresh whole blood samples were collected from 8 PC patients and 8 healthy controls, followed by qPCR analysis to validate the differential expression of the independent risk gene in the patient cohort. Relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method. All reactions were performed in independent triplicates. Primer sequence is shown in Supplementary Table S1.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3.1) and GraphPad Prism 8.0. Overall survival (OS) was analyzed by Kaplan-Meier curves and LASSO-Cox regression. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to evaluate the associations of clinical characteristics and prognosis. The statistical significance of differences between independent groups was assessed using unpaired t-test or Chi-square test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with * indicating P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001, and ns denoting not statistically significant.

Functional enrichment analysis of significant module genes

To elucidate the functions and potential pathways associated with the brown module DEGs, functional enrichment analysis was conducted. In total, 142 GO terms (P < 0.05), including 106 biological process (BP) terms, 34 cellular component (CC) terms and 2 molecular function (MF) terms were identified and 74 significantly enriched KEGG pathways were detected. GO analysis revealed that a total of 71 DEGs in the key module were involved mainly in chromosome segregation, nuclear division, organelle fission, and cell–substrate junctions (Fig. 4a). KEGG analysis indicated that these genes were also enriched in the extracellular matrix (ECM)–receptor interaction, focal adhesion, and PI3K-Akt signaling pathways (Fig. 4b). These core genes predominantly regulate cell-matrix communication and dynamic control of cell motility. Notably, these molecular processes may collectively contribute to oncogenic progression, particularly in facilitating tumor cell invasion and metastasis.

Identification and validation of the diagnosis-related genes

The “Venn Diagram” R package (Version 4.3.1) was used to screen the potential hub genes between the significant co-expression module and the DEGs in our clinical microarray dataset. The DEGs were intersected with the brown module genes to obtain 8 intersecting genes, including FERMT1, S100A14, PLEK2, KCNN4, RHBDL2, PKM, UBE2T, ITGA3 (Fig. 5a). Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox analysis was further applied to identify the most valuable differentially expressed mRNA (DEmRNA) from the intersection genes in the 2 datasets. In total, 5 possible diagnostic markers were identified, namely, FERMT1, S100A14, KCNN4, PKM, and ITGA3 (Fig. 5b).

Construction and verification of a comprehensive gene signature in PC

The LASSO-Cox regression model was employed to verify the prognostic gene signatures. Thus, the risk score was calculated as follows: the combination mRNA panel= (0.455×expression value of FERMT1 + −0.237×expression value of S100A14 + 1.955×expression value of KCNN4 + 0.887×expression value of PKM + 0.214×expression value of ITGA3). Subsequently, the patients were stratified into high- or low‐risk groups on account of the median value in TCGA set. The number of recurrent patients increased with the rise of the risk score. Consistently, the expression levels of FERMT1, S100A14, KCNN4, PKM, and ITGA3 were upregulated (Fig. 10a). Kaplan-Meier analysis manifested that patients in the low-risk group had prolonged OS compared to those in the high-risk group (P < 0.01) (Fig. 10b). Collectively, our results indicated an applicable performance of the 5 gene signature for recidivation prediction.

Discussion

PC is an aggressive malignant tumor that often lacks early symptoms and is associated with a poor prognosis [11]. Although significant progress has been achieved in surgical resection and chemotherapy, these advancements have not translated into effective therapies for the majority of patients. Consequently, there is and therapeutic strategies for PC patients. Currently, the mechanisms underlying gene regulation that contributes to disease progression are not fully understood, and prognostic evaluations for PC patients remain unsatisfactory.

With the development of bioinformatics, several studies have explored potential prognostic and diagnostic biomarkers related to PC [12–14]. Furthermore, researchers identified a set of prognostic RNA expression signatures based on a large-scale meta-analysis [15–17]. Numerous studies found that cell-free nucleic acids (cfNAs) isolated from the plasma and serum of cancer patients have demonstrated significant potential as minimally invasive diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers. Therefore, we performed an initial investigation aimed at exploring PC-associated serum diagnostic biomarker and identifying hub genes to construct specific multigene prognostic signatures that could enhance the prediction of clinical outcomes in PC.

In this study, we analyzed transcriptome data related to PC from clinical plasma samples alongside public databases. The included study subjects underwent rigorous screening criteria and exhibited consistency with conventional PC tumor marker detection levels of CA199. Our results showed that 16,091 mRNAs (228 upregulated and 315 downregulated mRNAs) were identified in serum sample between the PC patients and HC groups with a |logFC|≥1 and P < 0.05. The mRNA signatures derived from microarray analysis were systematically validated across multiple independent public databases. Using WGCNA on the merged datasets GSE62452 and GSE71989, we identified significant gene modules associated with PC. Furthermore, KEGG pathway and GO enrichment analyses of the genes in the identified modules revealed their involvement in ECM–receptor interaction processes and the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway. The gene enrichment profile demonstrates significant extracellular matrix remodeling activity, consistent with established desmoplastic mechanisms in PC pathogenesis.

After overlapping the DEGs of our clinical microarray dataset and the significant modules, we obtained 8 genes that were highly expressed in patients with PC. Then, through LASSO regression, we identified 5 genes (FERMT1, S100A14, KCNN4, PKM, and ITGA3). The validation datasets GSE16515 and GSE15471 confirmed the elevated mRNA expression of these 5 hub genes in tumors, suggesting their potential diagnostic utility for PC. ROC analysis demonstrated robust diagnostic performance for the 5 hub genes in the training cohorts (GSE62452 and GSE71989), with AUC values ranging from 0.806 to 0.892. However, in the validation cohort (GSE15471), the performance of FERMT1 (AUC: 0.790) and S100A14 (AUC: 0.685) was notably attenuated. This discrepancy may be attributed to the cohort heterogeneity in population origin and tumor grade distribution. Specifically, the training cohorts predominantly comprised early-stage (I–II) pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cases from Caucasian populations [18, 19], whereas GSE15471 consisted mainly of stage III tumors from Asian patients [20]. These discrepancies suggest that the expression of FERMT1 and S100A14 may be influenced by tumor stage and ethnic background. Further validation in larger, multi-ethnic cohorts is needed to confirm these findings.

Moreover, significant differential protein expression between tumor and adjacent normal tissues were validated by CPTAC and HPA database. However, the comparable IHC staining intensities of KCNN4 between groups may associated with the activity of calcium channels and tumor histological grade. A study demonstrated hypomethylation of the KCNN4 promoter and increased KCa3.1 channel activity were closely correlated with highly aggressive lung adenocarcinoma cells [21]. Separately, recent finding indicated comparable KCNN4 protein expression between differentiated hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and adjacent normal tissues, revealed its significant association with tumor histological grades [22]. Notably, S. Jiang et al. [23]. reported stepwise elevation of KCNN4 immunoreactivity from normal acinar cells to pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma tissues, concluding that KCNN4 dysregulated during malignant transformation. Increased KCNN4 channel activity induces membrane potential changes and modulates intracellular calcium levels, thereby affecting cancer-related downstream signaling pathways.

Several studies have sought to combine key gene expressions with clinical parameters to develop models that aid in predicting patient prognosis. By LASSO-Cox regression analysis, we established and verified a robust independent prognostic signature based on 5-genes (FERMT1, S100A14, KCNN4, PKM, and ITGA3) that were significantly related to the predictive value for PC. We established a novel nomogram consisting of risk score, the calibration plots illustrating an excellent agreement between the prediction and observation in the TCGA cohort.

Moreover, according to the multivariate model, the expression of FERMT1, S100A14, and ITGA3 was an independent risk factor for poor prognosis, which was further confirmed by qRT-PCR in clinical blood sample. In this study, we examined the correlations between the expression of prognosis-related genes and clinicopathological characteristics. The results indicated that lymph node metastasis was an independent prognostic factor in PC patients.

Our findings contribute further evidence for the importance of the key molecules currently under investigation for PC. Based on our data, several studies have reported associations between these hub genes and the progression of PC to a certain extent. Metastasis is the main cause of cancer-associated death. Activation of the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT)-associated program is regarded as a central driver of tumorigenesis to metastasis [24]. Currently, the role of EMT in KRAS-driven PC development has been intensively studied [25–27]. Previous studies have indicated that S100A14 and ITGA3 may be important determinants of EMT in PC. Most S100 proteins are considered EMT facilitators or EMT markers in carcinoma cells [28, 29]. The depletion of S100A14 strongly increased cell invasion [30]. Consistent with the EMT and mesenchymal–epithelial transition states, ITGA3 [31] was reported to upregulate the expression of ZIP4 in a PC cell line through the ZEB1/YAP1-ITGA3 signaling axis to promote EMT plasticity. In our research, the high expression of S100A14 and ITGA3 are associated with a poor prognosis of PC, their potential roles in EMT regulation require further experimental validation through functional assays.

Fermitin family member 1 (FERMT1) is associated with immune cell infiltration, and its N6-methyladenosine modification might be a marker of the treatment response to PC chemotherapy [32]. In addition, studies have demonstrated that FERMT1 functions as a methylation-driven gene and participates in PC oncogenesis [33, 34]. Potassium calcium-activated channel subfamily N member 4 (KCNN4) is a type of intermediate conductance calcium-activated potassium channel, and numerous studies have demonstrated that KCNN4 closely relates to the invasion and metastasis in many types of tumors [35–37]. Mo et al. [38] illustrated that KCNN4 promotes pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma cell proliferation, migration, and invasion through the Ca2+/MET/AKT axis and identified KCNN4 as a potential therapeutic target in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. As a new potential therapeutic target, the splicing of pyruvate kinase (PKM) is correlated with drug resistance in PC patients [39] Numerous studies have affirmed the significance of hub genes in the landscape of PC. When considered alongside our previous analyses, data suggest that the differential expression of FERMT1, S100A14, KCNN4, PKM, and ITGA3 in tumor and normal tissues might be related to the underlying mechanisms of PC, which must be further explored.

Although some of our identified hub genes were previously reported to be associated with PC progression, no study has systematically analyzed the specific prognostic role of circulating biomarkers in PC. It is noteworthy that most existing findings were derived from in vitro cellular experiments or tumor tissue analyses, our prognostic model was established using clinical serum samples from PC patients. This strengthens the clinical utility of our findings for non-invasive diagnostic and prognosis applications.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. First, the findings are primarily derived from microarray analysis of clinical samples and bioinformatics approaches, lacking in vitro or in vivo experimental validation. Although we performed PCR validation in a small cohort, the limited sample size reduces statistical power and may increase bias. Second, the patients enrolled in this study were recruited from a single medical center and only included those with pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. Moreover, the validation datasets we used exhibited demographic diversity and pathological heterogeneity, which may affect cross-validation reliability and introduce bias. This variability may partially explain the attenuated performance observed in external validation. Additionally, the pooling strategy used in microarray analysis may have obscured biological variability, including the loss of gene expression information and reduced sensitivity to subgroup-specific patterns. Therefore, future work will include functional experiments, qPCR validation, cross-validation with public datasets (including both tissue and blood samples), and multi-center verification using large-scale clinical samples with consistent sequencing technology to minimize heterogeneity.

Conclusions

This study identified signature genes as potential biomarkers for the diagnosis of PC and showed great value for clinical prognosis. We focused on genes significantly associated with PC. These findings suggest potential research avenues for experimental studies, which could facilitate the discovery of novel targets for PC diagnosis and therapy.
