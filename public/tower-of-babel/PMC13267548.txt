Colovesical fistula following sigmoid resection: A complex postoperative complication assessed by multimodal imaging
Authors: Manuela Montatore, Federica Masino, Rossella Carpentiere, Gianmichele Muscatella, Marina Balbino, Giuseppe Guglielmi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractColovesical fistula (CVF) is an uncommon but clinically significant complication of diverticular disease and colorectal surgery, particularly in the postoperative setting. We report the case of a 49-year-old man who developed pneumaturia and fecaluria following elective sigmoid resection for diverticulitis. Sequential imaging with contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) and a water-soluble contrast enema documented the progression from pelvic abscess formation to a well-defined colovesical fistula, with associated extensive soft tissue involvement. Serial CT examinations were crucial for identifying the fistulous tract, evaluating disease evolution, and guiding conservative management. This case highlights the key role of longitudinal multimodal imaging in the diagnosis and management of complex postoperative CVF.Keywords: Colovesical fistula, Diverticulectomy, Postoperative complication, Multimodal imaging, Colorectal surgery

Introduction

Colovesical fistula (CVF) is an uncommon but clinically significant complication most frequently associated with diverticular disease and, less commonly, colorectal surgery. Its diagnosis may be challenging, particularly in postoperative settings where clinical and imaging findings can overlap with expected surgical changes. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) plays a central role in detecting fistulous tracts and associated complications, while complementary imaging modalities may provide additional functional information. However, the sequential radiologic evolution of postoperative CVF, especially in the presence of extensive pelvic and soft tissue involvement, is not widely documented. We report a case of postoperative CVF assessed through multimodal and longitudinal imaging, highlighting key diagnostic features and their impact on clinical management.

Case report

A 49-year-old man with a history of recurrent sigmoid diverticulitis underwent elective laparoscopic sigmoid resection. The immediate postoperative course was uneventful. However, 2 weeks after surgery, he presented with dysuria, pneumaturia, fecaluria, and low-grade fever. Laboratory tests revealed leukocytosis and a urinary tract infection caused by Escherichia Coli. Based on these findings, a colovesical fistula (CVF) was suspected.

A contrast-enhanced CT scan performed on September 19, 2025 (Fig. 1) demonstrated marked inflammatory changes in the sigmoid region with extensive pericolic fat stranding and a large, heterogeneous pelvic collection containing feculent material. The collection extended to both the sigmoid colon and the dome of the urinary bladder. Marked inflammatory involvement of the surrounding fat and soft tissues was present, with multiloculated abscesses tracking into the subcutaneous and muscular layers of the lower anterior abdominal wall.

A contrast-enhanced CT performed on 28/09/2025 (Fig. 2) showed postoperative changes. Subsequently, a right-sided diverting colostomy was created to protect the anastomosis.

A water-soluble contrast enema (Fig. 3) performed on the following day demonstrated opacification of the rectal ampulla only, without passage of contrast into the excluded sigmoid segment, confirming diversion and excluding proximal anastomotic dehiscence or obstruction.

Water-soluble contrast enema demonstrating opacification limited to the rectal ampulla, without contrast passage into the excluded sigmoid colon and without evidence of fistulous communication.

Despite transient clinical improvement, the patient again developed fecaluria and pneumaturia. A third CT scan on October 12, 2025, revealed a colovesical fistula, now clearly visible as a fistulous tract containing gas and contrast extending between the sigmoid colon and the bladder dome. The previously created stoma appeared no longer functional, and a stercoraceous pelvic collection persisted, with inflammatory infiltration of the perivesical fat. Multiloculated abscesses extended into the pelvic soft tissues and anterior abdominal wall, with residual intravesical gas and irregular thickening of the bladder wall (Fig. 4, Fig. 5).

Contrast-enhanced CT (arterial phase) showing a colovesical fistula with a gas- and contrast-containing tract between the sigmoid colon and bladder dome, associated with persistent stercoraceous pelvic collection, perivesical fat stranding, and multiloculated abscesses extending into the pelvic and anterior abdominal soft tissues.

Contrast-enhanced CT (portal phase) showing a colovesical fistula with a gas- and contrast-containing tract between the sigmoid colon and bladder dome, associated with a persistent stercoraceous pelvic collection. In the first image are shown the metallic clips of the previous surgery.

Conservative treatment was initiated, including intravenous antibiotics, urinary bladder decompression with a Foley catheter, and nutritional support. The patient’s symptoms progressively improved. A fourth CT scan performed on November 25, 2025, showed partial regression of the abscess cavities, reduction of perivesical inflammation, and no definite visualization of the fistulous tract. Postoperative findings were consistent with a new left-sided protective stoma and reparative thickening of the bladder wall. The patient remained asymptomatic and was scheduled for delayed definitive surgical closure (Fig. 6).

Follow-up CT demonstrating partial regression of abscess cavities, reduction of perivesical inflammation, and reparative thickening of the bladder wall; a new left-sided protective stoma is visible, with no definite visualization of the fistulous tract.

This case illustrates the potential for delayed, complex postoperative complications in elective colorectal surgery and emphasizes the importance of longitudinal, multimodal imaging for early detection, assessment of disease evolution, and guidance of individualized management strategies. It reinforces that even severe CVF with extensive soft tissue involvement can be managed safely with a stepwise approach, combining conservative therapy and timed surgical intervention (Table 1).

Discussion

Colovesical fistula is an uncommon but serious complication of diverticular disease, reported in approximately 2%-4% of patients with complicated diverticulitis and even more rarely as a postoperative event following elective sigmoid resection. In this case, the fistula was associated with a large stercoraceous pelvic collection and secondary extension into the subcutaneous and muscular planes—features infrequently documented in the literature. Cystoscopy was not performed in this case, as the diagnosis was considered sufficiently established based on CT findings and clinical presentation, and would not have altered initial management.

The pathogenesis of CVF typically involves chronic inflammation, micro-perforation, or anastomotic leakage, which may lead to progressive erosion between the colon and the bladder. Postoperative CVF, though less common than those arising from chronic diverticulitis or Crohn’s disease, presents a diagnostic challenge, particularly when clinical signs are nonspecific or overlap with expected postoperative findings.

CT with intravenous contrast remains the gold standard for diagnosis. Characteristic findings include intravesical gas, focal colonic wall thickening adjacent to the bladder, fat stranding, and direct visualization of the fistulous tract. In this case, CT not only demonstrated the tract clearly but also delineated the extent of inflammation and the associated multiloculated abscesses, providing key information for clinical decision-making. These findings are consistent with published data, which identify intravesical gas in the absence of instrumentation as highly suggestive of enterovesical communication, particularly in the postoperative setting or in the presence of infection-related complications [1,2,5].

Water-soluble contrast enema, although less sensitive, can be a valuable complementary tool when CT findings are inconclusive or when direct visualization of the tract is required. In our case, the water-soluble contrast enema did not demonstrate fistulous communication but confirmed effective diversion, with contrast limited to the rectal stump. This finding was important to exclude anastomotic leakage and to correlate with the subsequent CT-based diagnosis of colovesical fistula [3,6].

The differential diagnosis of pneumaturia and fecaluria includes fistulas secondary to colorectal carcinoma, inflammatory bowel disease (particularly Crohn’s disease), radiation cystitis, and bladder trauma. In postoperative patients, it is crucial to differentiate between expected postsurgical air and true fistulization. Persistent or progressive intravesical gas, especially when associated with systemic signs of infection or fecaluria, should prompt suspicion for CVF [4,7].

Management depends on the patient’s clinical stability and the extent of disease. Surgical repair is typically required for persistent fistulas or recurrent infections, but conservative management may be appropriate for clinically stable patients without systemic sepsis. In this case, the absence of septic deterioration and the favorable imaging evolution supported a delayed surgical approach. Conservative therapy—comprising antibiotics, bladder decompression, and close imaging follow-up—resulted in partial resolution of the fistulous tract and associated abscesses, aligning with recent evidence favoring personalized, stepwise management [8,9].

Serial CT imaging proved indispensable, not only for confirming the diagnosis but also for tracking disease progression and guiding the timing of therapy. The gradual regression of inflammatory findings without clinical deterioration justified the postponement of surgery, minimizing operative morbidity and optimizing patient recovery. CT examinations were performed using intravenous contrast in arterial and portal venous phases. The fistulous tract was best visualized on portal venous phase images with coronal and sagittal MPR reformations, which were crucial for its identification. The fistula was characterized by a combination of findings, including intravesical gas (in the absence of recent instrumentation), direct visualization of a tract containing air and contrast material, and tethering between the sigmoid colon and the bladder dome. Particular attention was paid to differentiating true fistulous gas from postcatheterization intravesical air.

To our knowledge, the combination of extensive pelvic abscesses with tracking into the anterior abdominal wall and the documented sequential evolution of postoperative CVF on serial CT imaging is rarely reported.

This case underscores the critical importance of longitudinal imaging in complex postoperative scenarios. The chronological documentation—from massive diverticulitis to fistula formation and subsequent regression—provides a unique educational insight into the natural course of postoperative CVF.

Conclusion

Colovesical fistula represents a rare yet severe complication of diverticular disease and colorectal surgery. This case highlights the pivotal role of multimodal imaging, particularly sequential contrast-enhanced CT complemented by contrast enema, early detection, characterization, and follow-up of fistulous disease. Familiarity with the evolving imaging appearances of CVF allows radiologists to make timely diagnoses, guide management strategies, and prevent progression to life-threatening septic complications.
