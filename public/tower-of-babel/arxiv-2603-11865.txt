Spin Chern phases and persistent spin texture in a quasi-2D SSH model
Hemant K Sharma, Saptarshi Mandal, Kush Saha

Abstract
We construct a quasi-two-dimensional Su-Schrieffer-Heeger model (SSH)-like model and uncover a rich set of topological phases with nontrivial spin textures in the presence of complex hopping and spin–orbit coupling. Despite its simple structure, the combined effect of complex hopping and spin–orbit interaction gives rise not only to the conventional quantum anomalous Hall insulating (QAHI) phase, but also to distinct combinations of spin Chern phases, namely quantum anomalous spin Hall insulating (QASHI) phase. Furthermore, we demonstrate that the bulk bands of this model can host persistent spin textures, whose formation and stability are governed by the relative strengths of nearest- and next-nearest-neighbor complex hopping. To elucidate the underlying mechanisms, we develop a low-energy continuum theory that captures the emergence of these topological phases and clarifies the origin of the persistent spin textures. Interestingly, the resulting spin textures closely resemble those typically observed in conventional semiconductor systems with topologically trivial band structures. However, in our case, they emerge within a nontrivial topological framework, enabled by carefully engineered hopping patterns that intertwine lattice geometry, complex hopping, and spin–orbit coupling

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{SOC}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{m,n}\Big[\psi^{\dagger}_{m,n}\,\Gamma_{0}\,\psi_{m,n}+\,\psi^{\dagger}_{m+1,n}\,\Gamma_{xy}\,\psi_{m,n}

\displaystyle H

\displaystyle=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Big[2\tau\sin k_{y}\,\tau_{z}\mathbb{I}_{2}+\tau^{+}M(\mathbf{k})+\tau^{-}M^{\dagger}(\mathbf{k})\Big],

Introducing complex hopping
only
along the SSH chains replaces

III
Distinct Topological Phases

Let us first chart out distinct topological phases for both in the absence and in the presence of different SOC and complex hopping terms. Wherever possible, we derive effective low energy model Hamiltonians for different choices of parameters and express Chern numbers explicitly in terms of model parameters.

In the absence of SOC (

\displaystyle h_{x}=v+2\delta\cos k_{y}+(w-2\delta\cos k_{y})\cos k_{x},

V
Summary and Discussion

In this work, we have introduced a two-dimensional quasi Su–Schrieffer–Heeger (SSH) model that supports distinct spin-resolved topological phases arising from the interplay of complex hopping and Rashba spin–orbit coupling. These phases are (i) the quantum anomalous spin Hall (QASH) phase, where only a single spin channel carries nontrivial topology with same or opposite chirality and (ii) the quantum anomalous Hall (QAH) phase, in which both spin sectors share the same Chern number. Wherever possible, we have derived approximate low-energy Dirac Hamiltonians to elucidate the microscopic origin of the distinct topological phases. Our analysis reveals how the interplay between complex hopping and spin–orbit coupling gives rise to nontrivial phases. Additionally, we discuss persistent spin textures embedded within a topological background, a feature that is typically absent in conventional topological models due to strong spin mixing induced by spin–orbit interactions. By constructing effective Hamiltonians near high-symmetry points of the Brillouin zone, we discuss the emergence of these nontrivial spin textures. Taken together, these results provide a unified framework that connects topological phases and the evolution of spin textures, thereby clarifying how complex hopping and Rashba-type spin–orbit interactions shape spin-resolved topology in two-dimensional systems.

The proposed lattice model can be realized using ultracold atoms in a two-dimensional optical lattice with Raman-assisted tunneling. A square optical lattice generated by two orthogonal standing-wave laser fields creates periodic potentials along the
x
and
y
directions. By introducing an additional superlattice potential along the
x
direction, each unit cell can be engineered to contain two inequivalent sites
A
and
B
, forming an SSH-type geometry
7
. The natural tunneling between these sites gives rise to the intracell hopping amplitude
v
.

Complex tunneling amplitudes can be engineered by applying a linear potential gradient that suppresses the natural hopping between neighboring sites and restoring it via Raman-assisted tunneling. In this scheme, pairs of Raman laser beams induce resonant tunneling between adjacent lattice sites while imprinting a controllable Peierls phase, resulting in an effective hopping amplitude
t_{\mathrm{eff}}=te^{i\phi}
, where
\phi
is determined by the momentum transfer of the Raman beams
5
;
35
. By tuning the Raman beam phases such that
\phi=\pi/2
, purely imaginary hopping amplitudes can be realized. In particular, Raman-assisted tunneling along the
x
direction can generate complex intercell hoppings of the form
i\eta_{1}\,b_{n,m}^{\dagger}a_{n+1,m}
and
i\eta_{2}\,a_{n,m}^{\dagger}b_{n+1,m}
, while an additional pair of Raman beams with momentum transfer along the
y
direction can produce the transverse hopping
i\tau\,c_{n,m}^{\dagger}c_{n,m+1}
. Such control over tunneling phases has been widely demonstrated in experiments realizing synthetic gauge fields and Hofstadter-type Hamiltonians in optical lattices
5
;
21
. The model can be further extended to include Rashba spin–orbit interaction (RSOI) using Raman-induced spin–momentum coupling. In this approach, two hyperfine states of the atoms serve as effective spin degrees of freedom, and Raman laser beams couple these states while imparting momentum to the atoms
31
;
18
. This mechanism generates synthetic spin–orbit coupling that mimics Rashba-type interactions in solid-state systems.

Appendix A
Effective Spin Hamiltonian

In this appendix we derive the low-energy effective Hamiltonian around the
\Gamma

(i) to show how the original four-band spin–orbit-coupled Hamiltonian reduces to an effective two-band spin Hamiltonian, and

(ii) to identify explicitly the momentum-independent and momentum-linear spin terms responsible for the unconventional spin texture discussed in the main text.

Throughout, we retain only the leading contributions relevant to the low-energy physics near
\Gamma
and
X

1. Momentum-space Hamiltonian

The full Hamiltonian in momentum space is written as

\displaystyle H=\sum_{\mathbf{k}}\Big[2\tau\sin k_{y}\,\tau_{z}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{2}+\tau^{+}\otimes M(\mathbf{k})+\tau^{-}\otimes M^{\dagger}(\mathbf{k})\Big].

(20)

Here
\tau_{i}
denote Pauli matrices acting in orbital (sublattice) space, while
\sigma_{i}
act in spin space. The Hamiltonian therefore lives in the tensor-product space of orbital and spin degrees of freedom.

The inter-orbital coupling matrix is

\displaystyle M(\mathbf{k})=A(\mathbf{k})\mathbb{I}_{2}+B(\mathbf{k})\sigma_{z}+D_{x}(\mathbf{k})\sigma_{x}+D_{y}(\mathbf{k})\sigma_{y}.

(21)

The coefficients have the following interpretation:

A(\mathbf{k})
spin-independent hopping,

B(\mathbf{k})
spin-dependent term along
\sigma_{z}
,

D_{x}(\mathbf{k})
and
D_{y}(\mathbf{k})
: spin–orbit coupling terms generating in-plane spin mixing.

Their explicit forms are

\displaystyle A(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=v+(w-i\eta_{1})e^{-ik_{x}}+(\gamma+i\eta_{2})e^{ik_{x}}

\displaystyle+2\delta\cos k_{y}(1-e^{-ik_{x}}),

(22)

\displaystyle B(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=i\zeta(1-e^{-ik_{x}}),

(23)

\displaystyle D_{x}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=2\alpha(\sin k_{y}+i\cos k_{y}e^{-ik_{x}}),

(24)

\displaystyle D_{y}(\mathbf{k})

\displaystyle=-i\alpha(1-e^{-ik_{x}})+2\alpha e^{-ik_{x}}\sin k_{y}.

(25)

The asymmetry between
\eta_{1}
and
\eta_{2}
will be shown to generate a finite spin term already at zero momentum. In the orbital basis the Hamiltonian takes the block form

\displaystyle H=\begin{pmatrix}H_{\alpha\alpha}&H_{\alpha\beta}\\
H_{\beta\alpha}&H_{\beta\beta}\end{pmatrix},

(26)

with

\displaystyle H_{\alpha\alpha}

\displaystyle=2\tau\sin k_{y}\,\mathbb{I}_{2},

(27)

\displaystyle H_{\beta\beta}

\displaystyle=-2\tau\sin k_{y}\,\mathbb{I}_{2},

(28)

and

\displaystyle H_{\alpha\beta}=A\mathbb{I}_{2}+D_{x}\sigma_{x}+D_{y}\sigma_{y}+B\sigma_{z},\qquad H_{\beta\alpha}=H_{\alpha\beta}^{\dagger}.

(29)

Introducing the vector

\displaystyle\vec{D}=(D_{x},D_{y},B),

(30)

the off-diagonal block can be written compactly as

\displaystyle H_{\alpha\beta}=A\mathbb{I}_{2}+\vec{D}\cdot\vec{\sigma}.

(31)

Product of the off-diagonal blocks:

The key quantity entering the Löwdin effective Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H_{\alpha\beta}H_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle=(A\mathbb{I}_{2}+\vec{D}\cdot\vec{\sigma})(A^{*}\mathbb{I}_{2}+\vec{D}^{\,*}\cdot\vec{\sigma}).

(32)

Expanding the product gives

\displaystyle H_{\alpha\beta}H_{\beta\alpha}

\displaystyle=|A|^{2}\mathbb{I}_{2}+A(\vec{D}^{\,*}\cdot\vec{\sigma})+A^{*}(\vec{D}\cdot\vec{\sigma})

\displaystyle+(\vec{D}\cdot\vec{\sigma})(\vec{D}^{\,*}\cdot\vec{\sigma}).

We now employ the Pauli-matrix identity

\displaystyle(\vec{a}\cdot\vec{\sigma})(\vec{b}\cdot\vec{\sigma})=(\vec{a}\cdot\vec{b})\mathbb{I}_{2}+i(\vec{a}\times\vec{b})\cdot\vec{\sigma},

(34)

which for
\vec{a}=\vec{D}
and
\vec{b}=\vec{D}^{\,*}
yields

\displaystyle(\vec{D}\cdot\vec{\sigma})(\vec{D}^{\,*}\cdot\vec{\sigma})=(\vec{D}\cdot\vec{D}^{\,*})\mathbb{I}_{2}+i(\vec{D}\times\vec{D}^{\,*})\cdot\vec{\sigma}.

(35)

Separation into scalar and spin parts :

Collecting terms proportional to the identity matrix gives

\displaystyle d_{0}=|A|^{2}+\vec{D}\cdot\vec{D}^{\,*}.

(36)

The spin-dependent contribution becomes

\displaystyle\vec{d}\cdot\vec{\sigma}=A\vec{D}^{\,*}\cdot\vec{\sigma}+A^{*}\vec{D}\cdot\vec{\sigma}+i(\vec{D}\times\vec{D}^{\,*})\cdot\vec{\sigma}.

(37)

Hence,

\displaystyle\vec{d}=A\vec{D}^{\,*}+A^{*}\vec{D}+i(\vec{D}\times\vec{D}^{\,*}).

(38)

Using

\displaystyle A\vec{D}^{\,*}+A^{*}\vec{D}=2\,\mathrm{Re}(A\vec{D}^{\,*}),

(39)

one obtains

\displaystyle\vec{d}=2\,\mathrm{Re}(A\vec{D}^{\,*})+i(\vec{D}\times\vec{D}^{\,*}).

(40)

For

BD_{x}^{*}-D_{x}B^{*}\\
D_{x}D_{y}^{*}-D_{y}D_{x}^{*}\end{pmatrix}.

(41)

Using

\displaystyle i(\vec{D}\times\vec{D}^{\,*})=\begin{pmatrix}-2\,\mathrm{Im}(D_{y}B^{*})\\
-2\,\mathrm{Im}(BD_{x}^{*})\\
-2\,\mathrm{Im}(D_{x}D_{y}^{*})\end{pmatrix}.

(42)

Combining all contributions gives

\displaystyle d_{x}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Re}(AD_{x}^{*})-2\mathrm{Im}(D_{y}B^{*}),

(43)

\displaystyle d_{y}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Re}(AD_{y}^{*})-2\mathrm{Im}(BD_{x}^{*}),

(44)

\displaystyle d_{z}

\displaystyle=2\mathrm{Re}(AB^{*})-2\mathrm{Im}(D_{x}D_{y}^{*}).

(45)

\displaystyle H_{\rm eff}=2\tau\sin k_{y}\,\mathbb{I}_{2}+\frac{1}{E+2\tau\sin k_{y}}H_{\alpha\beta}H_{\beta\alpha},

(46)

the effective spin field components become

\displaystyle d_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{E+2\tau\sin k_{y}}\Big[\mathrm{Re}(AD_{x}^{*})-\mathrm{Im}(D_{y}B^{*})\Big],

(47)

\displaystyle d_{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{E+2\tau\sin k_{y}}\Big[\mathrm{Re}(AD_{y}^{*})-\mathrm{Im}(BD_{x}^{*})\Big],

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{E+2\tau\sin k_{y}}\Big[\mathrm{Re}(AB^{*})-\mathrm{Im}(D_{x}D_{y}^{*})\Big].

(49)

\displaystyle H_{\rm eff}=d_{0}\sigma_{0}+d_{x}\sigma_{x}+d_{y}\sigma_{y}+d_{z}\sigma_{z},

where

acts as an

is therefore neglected. Hence

\displaystyle d_{x}=\frac{4\alpha}{E}\Big[(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})+a_{0}(q_{x}+q_{y})\Big].

(71)

(ii) Expansion of
d_{y}
:

Since
D_{y}
is already linear in momentum,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}(AD_{y}^{*})=a_{0}(\alpha q_{x}+2\alpha q_{y}).

(72)

The second term contributes

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Im}(BD_{x}^{*})=2\alpha\zeta q_{x}.

(73)

Therefore

\displaystyle d_{y}=\frac{2}{E}\Big[\alpha a_{0}q_{x}+2\alpha a_{0}q_{y}+2\alpha\zeta q_{x}\Big].

(74)

(iii) Expansion of
d_{z}
:

Since
B\propto q_{x}
,

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}(AB^{*})=-a_{0}\zeta q_{x}.

(75)

Furthermore,

\displaystyle-\mathrm{Im}(D_{x}D_{y}^{*})=4\alpha^{2}q_{y}.

(76)

Hence

\displaystyle d_{z}=\frac{2}{E}\Big[-a_{0}\zeta q_{x}+4\alpha^{2}q_{y}\Big].

(77)

Compact Form of the Low–Energy Hamiltonian at
\Gamma

Collecting all linear terms, the effective Hamiltonian near

\Gamma
takes the form

\displaystyle H_{\rm low}^{\Gamma}(q_{x},q_{y})

\displaystyle=(f_{1}^{\prime}q_{x}+f_{2}^{\prime}q_{y})\sigma_{y}+(f_{0}^{\prime}q_{y}+f_{3}q_{x})\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle\quad+\big[f_{0}(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})+f_{1}q_{x}+f_{2}q_{y}\big]\sigma_{x},

(78)

with the coefficients explicitly given by

f_{0}=\frac{4\alpha}{E},\quad f_{1}=\frac{4\alpha a_{0}}{E},\quad f_{2}=\frac{4\alpha a_{0}}{E},\quad f_{1}^{\prime}=\frac{2\alpha(a_{0}+2\zeta)}{E},

f_{2}^{\prime}=\frac{4\alpha a_{0}}{E},\quad f_{0}^{\prime}=\frac{8\alpha^{2}}{E},\quad f_{3}=-\frac{2a_{0}\zeta}{E}.

The term

\displaystyle f_{0}(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})\sigma_{x}

(79)

is momentum independent and survives exactly at the

\Gamma
point.

Appendix C
Low–Energy Expansion Around the
X=(\pi,0)
Point

In this section we derive the effective low–energy Hamiltonian
around the high-symmetry point

X=(\pi,0)
.
We introduce small deviations

\displaystyle k_{x}=\pi+q_{x},\qquad k_{y}=q_{y},\qquad|q_{x}|,|q_{y}|\ll 1,

(80)

and retain only terms up to linear order in momenta.

Using standard small-momentum expansions, the relevant Bloch
factors become

\displaystyle e^{-ik_{x}}

\displaystyle=-e^{-iq_{x}}\approx-1+iq_{x},

(81)

\displaystyle e^{ik_{x}}

\displaystyle=-e^{iq_{x}}\approx-1-iq_{x},

(82)

\displaystyle\sin k_{y}

\displaystyle\approx q_{y},

(83)

\displaystyle\cos k_{y}

\displaystyle\approx 1.

(84)

These relations determine the linearized form of the hopping
amplitudes near the
X
point.

Substituting the above expansions into the microscopic model,
the coefficient
A(\mathbf{k})
takes the form

\displaystyle A=a_{X}+i\Delta\eta+(\eta_{1}+\eta_{2})q_{x}+i\lambda q_{x},

(85)

where

\displaystyle a_{X}

\displaystyle=v-w-\gamma+4\delta,

(86)

\displaystyle\Delta\eta

\displaystyle=\eta_{1}-\eta_{2},

(87)

\displaystyle\lambda

\displaystyle=w-\gamma-2\delta.

(88)

Here
a_{X}
represents the effective static mass term at
X
,
while
\Delta\eta
controls the non-Hermitian imbalance.

The remaining coefficients become

\displaystyle B

\displaystyle=2i\zeta+\zeta q_{x},

(89)

\displaystyle D_{x}

\displaystyle=-2i\alpha+2\alpha(q_{y}-q_{x}),

(90)

\displaystyle D_{y}

\displaystyle=-2i\alpha-\alpha q_{x}-2\alpha q_{y}.

(91)

The constant imaginary parts originate from spin–orbit coupling,
whereas the linear terms encode momentum-dependent corrections.

Effective Low–Energy Hamiltonian

Projecting onto the low-energy subspace yields the effective
two-band Hamiltonian

\displaystyle H_{\mathrm{low}}^{X}=d_{x}\sigma_{x}+d_{y}\sigma_{y}+d_{z}\sigma_{z},

(92)

with coefficients

\displaystyle d_{x}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{E}\left[\mathrm{Re}(AD_{x}^{*})-\mathrm{Im}(D_{y}B^{*})\right],

(93)

\displaystyle d_{y}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{E}\left[\mathrm{Re}(AD_{y}^{*})-\mathrm{Im}(BD_{x}^{*})\right],

(94)

\displaystyle d_{z}

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{E}\left[\mathrm{Re}(AB^{*})-\mathrm{Im}(D_{x}D_{y}^{*})\right].

(95)

Below we summarize the resulting expressions after keeping only
linear terms in
(q_{x},q_{y})
.

Coefficient
d_{x}
:

Using

D_{x}^{*}=2i\alpha+2\alpha(q_{y}-q_{x})
,
we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}(AD_{x}^{*})

\displaystyle=-2\alpha\Delta\eta+2\alpha a_{X}(q_{y}-q_{x})-2\alpha\lambda q_{x},

(96)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(D_{y}B^{*})

\displaystyle=4\alpha\zeta q_{y}.

(97)

Hence

\displaystyle d_{x}=\frac{4\alpha}{E}\Big[-\Delta\eta+a_{X}(q_{y}-q_{x})-\lambda q_{x}-2\zeta q_{y}\Big].

(98)

Coefficient
d_{y}
:

Similarly, with

D_{y}^{*}=2i\alpha-\alpha q_{x}-2\alpha q_{y}
,
we find

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}(AD_{y}^{*})

\displaystyle=-2\alpha\Delta\eta-a_{X}\alpha q_{x}-2a_{X}\alpha q_{y}-2\alpha\lambda q_{x},

(99)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(BD_{x}^{*})

\displaystyle=4\alpha\zeta q_{y}-2\alpha\zeta q_{x}.

(100)

The resulting expression is

\displaystyle d_{y}=\frac{4\alpha}{E}\Big[-\Delta\eta-\frac{a_{X}}{2}q_{x}-a_{X}q_{y}-\lambda q_{x}+\zeta q_{x}-2\zeta q_{y}\Big].

(101)

Coefficient
d_{z}
:

For the
\sigma_{z}
component we obtain

\displaystyle\mathrm{Re}(AB^{*})

\displaystyle=2\zeta\Delta\eta+\zeta(a_{X}+2\lambda)q_{x},

(102)

\displaystyle\mathrm{Im}(D_{x}D_{y}^{*})

\displaystyle=-2\alpha^{2}q_{x}+8\alpha^{2}q_{y}.

(103)

Thus

\displaystyle d_{z}=\frac{4}{E}\Big[\zeta\Delta\eta+\frac{\zeta}{2}(a_{X}+2\lambda)q_{x}+\alpha^{2}q_{x}-4\alpha^{2}q_{y}\Big].

(104)

Compact Form of the Low–Energy Hamiltonian at
X

Collecting all constant and linear terms in
(q_{x},q_{y})
,
the effective Hamiltonian near the
X=(\pi,0)
point
can be written in compact form.

\displaystyle H_{\rm low}^{X}(q_{x},q_{y})

\displaystyle=\Big[r_{0}(\eta_{2}-\eta_{1})+s_{2}q_{x}+s_{3}q_{y}\Big]\sigma_{z}

\displaystyle\quad+\Big[r_{1}(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})+r_{2}q_{x}+r_{3}q_{y}\Big]\sigma_{x}

\displaystyle\quad+\Big[r_{1}^{\prime}(\eta_{1}-\eta_{2})+r_{2}^{\prime}q_{x}+r_{3}^{\prime}q_{y}\Big]\sigma_{y}.

(105)

Here the coefficients are given by

\displaystyle r_{0}=-\frac{4\zeta}{E},~~~~r_{1}=-\frac{4\alpha}{E},~~~~r_{1}^{\prime}=-\frac{4\alpha}{E},

\displaystyle r_{2}=-\frac{4\alpha}{E}(a_{X}+\lambda),r_{3}=\frac{4\alpha}{E}(a_{X}-2\zeta),

\displaystyle r_{2}^{\prime}=-\frac{4\alpha}{E}\left(\frac{a_{X}}{2}+\lambda-\zeta\right),r_{3}^{\prime}=-\frac{4\alpha}{E}(a_{X}+2\zeta),

\displaystyle s_{2}=\frac{4}{E}\left[\frac{\zeta}{2}(a_{X}+2\lambda)+\alpha^{2}\right],s_{3}=-\frac{16\alpha^{2}}{E}.

(106)
