Background:
Target Diagnostic’s Engineering Science (TDES) currently supports over 80 diagnostic systems,
all of which draw from a fixed budget that covers staff time and procurement. As the division
continues to add new diagnostics, demand on these limited resources increases, yet the budget
remains unchanged. Shot Responsible Individuals (RIs) have unrestricted freedom to select any
number or type of diagnostics for each experiment, often by copying previous shot
configurations with only minor modifications. This practice makes it easy to generate new shots
but does not encourage critical evaluation of diagnostic necessity. As a result, many diagnostics
are routinely included in a shot without a clear justification, and in practice, much of the data
collected is not analyzed. The ever-growing arsenal of diagnostics also increases the burden on
TDES personnel, requiring more staff for support, maintenance, and service. This environment
creates challenges in focusing resources on diagnostics that provide the greatest value to the
NIF mission and raises concerns about the continued justification for maintaining all existing
systems.
Problem Statement:
The static TDES budget cannot sustain the ongoing growth in demand for new diagnostic
systems. Shot RIs currently face no constraints on the number or type of diagnostics they
assign to each experiment, often defaulting to previous configurations without critically
assessing the necessity of each diagnostic. This results in the routine inclusion of diagnostics
whose data may not be analyzed or may no longer directly support the NIF mission.
Consequently, TDES is required to maintain and support an expanding set of diagnostics,
straining limited resources and risking inefficient use of staff and funding.
Need Statement:
There is a need for TDES to efficiently allocate limited resources by establishing a process that
requires shot RIs to critically evaluate and justify the inclusion of each diagnostic in their
experiments. This process should discourage the routine, unexamined inclusion of diagnostics,
support the identification of underutilized or obsolete systems, and ensure that only those
diagnostics providing the greatest value to the NIF mission are maintained and supported.
Ultimately, this approach must enable TDES to operate within budget constraints while
maximizing scientific impact and operational efficiency.
Picture of Success:

TDES resources are focused on supporting the highest-priority diagnostics, with experimenters
actively selecting only the most valuable and mission-critical diagnostics for each shot. As a
result, resource allocation aligns with budget constraints, operational efficiency improves, and
experimenters are empowered to make informed decisions that maximize scientific value.
Diagnostics that no longer contribute significant value are identified for reduced support or
retirement, ensuring the continued relevance and impact of TDES efforts on the NIF mission.

System Concepts:
Exploring alternative solution approaches is a core activity in the conceptual design phase,
fostering innovation and laying the groundwork for rigorous trade studies. By generating and
evaluating multiple system concepts—such as integration with AppMan, a standalone
application, or an AI-driven concierge—the project ensures that the final approach is both
feasible and optimal for delivering stakeholder value, addressing identified needs and
requirements in distinct ways.
The first concept: Integrated system that is built within the existing AppMan
This concept integrates diagnostic budget management directly into the existing SST, allowing
Shot RIs to select diagnostics while receiving immediate feedback on the cumulative diagnostic
value relative to their assigned budget. By leveraging the AppMan’s established data
infrastructure and workflows, the system ensures high data integrity and minimizes disruption
for users, as all budget tracking and approval processes are embedded within familiar
interfaces. When a proposed diagnostic set exceeds the allocated budget, the system prompts
for management review and approval, streamlining exception handling and maintaining clear
accountability. Real-time reporting and analytics are available to both users and management,
supporting informed decision-making and transparent oversight. This approach maximizes
adoption by building on existing tools, reduces implementation risk by avoiding redundant data
entry or synchronization, and delivers a seamless experience that aligns with stakeholder needs
for efficiency, accuracy, and ease of use.
The second concept: A standalone application that imports the information needed.
This concept envisions a dedicated, standalone application that manages diagnostic selection
and budget tracking independently from the existing Approval Manager (AppMan). The
application imports relevant shot and campaign data, automatically associates each shot with its
management-determined budget, and displays the value of each diagnostic based on pre-
calculated support cost and scientific impact. Shot Responsible Individuals (RIs) use the app to
select and adjust diagnostics, ensuring that their total value remains within the allocated budget
or, if necessary, requesting a management waiver for exceptions. The application’s interface is
designed for clarity and ease of use, providing real-time feedback on budget status and
supporting exportable reports for management review. While this approach offers flexibility and
the potential for customized analytics, it requires robust data synchronization with existing
systems and introduces a separate workflow that may pose adoption and integration challenges
for users accustomed to the AppMan environment.
The third concept: A system which uses AI to advise on the diagnostics that would best achieve
the primary goals of the campaign.
This concept leverages artificial intelligence to actively guide Shot Responsible Individuals (RIs)
in selecting diagnostics that best align with the scientific goals and budgetary constraints of
each shot. Upon receiving input about the primary objectives of the shot, the AI system analyzes
available diagnostics and recommends an optimal set that maximizes scientific value while
staying within the assigned budget. Users can review, accept, or modify these
recommendations, with the system dynamically updating the total value and ensuring
compliance with budget limits or facilitating management approval for exceptions. The AI
Concierge provides advanced analytics and scenario modeling to support decision-making,

aiming to enhance both the efficiency and effectiveness of diagnostic selection. While offering
significant potential for value delivery and innovation, this approach involves higher
implementation complexity, requires user trust in AI-driven recommendations, and depends on
seamless integration with existing data sources to maintain accuracy and reliability.

Concept Selection:
After evaluating all candidate concepts using a structured Pugh matrix and aligning with
stakeholder requirements and acceptance criteria, the integration of diagnostic budget
management into the existing AppMan emerges as the preferred solution. This approach
leverages the established data infrastructure and user workflows of the AppMan, ensuring high
data integrity and minimizing the risk of errors or inconsistencies that can arise from data
duplication or synchronization across multiple platforms. By embedding the new functionality
within a familiar environment, we maximize usability for Shot Responsible Individuals (RIs) and
campaign managers, reducing the learning curve and promoting rapid adoption. Compared to a
standalone application or an AI-driven concierge, the AppMan Integration concept significantly
lowers implementation risk and cost, as it builds upon proven, existing codebases and
processes rather than requiring the development and validation of entirely new systems.
Furthermore, this approach maintains flexibility for future enhancements, such as incorporating
advanced analytics or AI-based recommendations, should their value be demonstrated.
Ultimately, integrating with AppMan provides the optimal balance of cost-effectiveness, usability,
and data quality, while supporting a seamless transition for all stakeholders and ensuring the
system’s long-term scalability and adaptability.

Operational Scenario 1: Primary Function – Diagnostic Menu
In the primary operational scenario, the Shot RI initiates the shot setup process by using Shot
Planner to request DIM-specific diagnostics and assign their priorities, along with other
necessary parameters. DIM-based diagnostics, being critical to each shot, are prioritized and
their value costs are determined only after approval in Shot Planner and transfer to the
Application Manager (AppMan). Behind the scenes, Operations personnel (RSEs, TExS, and
Operations) have provided to the Diagnostic Menu owner with the time, effort, and resources
required to maintain, configure, and support each diagnostic, while the Diagnostic Responsible
Scientists (RSs) report the scientific impact of their respective diagnostics. The Diagnostic Menu
Owner compiles these inputs to establish the cost value for each diagnostic, which AppMan
uses to display individual and total costs as the Shot RI selects additional diagnostics during
configuration. If the total cost remains within the allowed budget, the system releases the shot

for subsequent steps; if the cost exceeds the budget, the Shot RI is prompted to seek
management approval or revise their selection until the total value cost is compliant. This
process ensures that both operational and scientific priorities are reflected in the diagnostic
selection, while maintaining adherence to budgetary constraints.

Operational Scenario 2: Maintenance Condition
During routine use of the Diagnostic Menu within AppMan, any stakeholder—be it a Shot RI,
Operations staff, or Diagnostic RS—who observes a malfunction, such as incorrect cost
calculations, scientific impact values, or system glitches, is empowered to report the issue to the
software division. Upon receiving a report, the software team investigates the bug, resolves the
underlying problem, and restores full system functionality. This responsive maintenance process
ensures the continued reliability and accuracy of the diagnostic budgeting system, supporting
uninterrupted shot planning and setup.

Operational Scenario 3: Periodic Update of Values and Budget
To maintain accuracy in diagnostic costs and scientific impact values, the Diagnostic Menu
Owner conducts periodic reviews in consultation with Operations and Diagnostic RSs.
Operations confirm or update the resource and effort data for each diagnostic, while Diagnostic
RSs verify or revise the scientific impact, subject to approval by scientific management. The
Diagnostic Menu Owner integrates these updates into the system, ensuring all new diagnostics
reflect the most current information. Once updates are complete, the system is released for use
by the Shot RI community, with changes affecting only new shots in AppMan or Shot Planner
that have not yet progressed to AppMan. This ongoing review process ensures that shot
planning is always based on up-to-date operational and scientific data, without disrupting shots
already approved.

Sequence Diagram 1:

When planning a new experiment, the person in charge (Shot RI) uses a special menu to
choose which diagnostic tools will be used to collect data. They pick the most important
diagnostics and set their priorities, making sure that the choices fit within the available budget.
Behind the scenes, other team members regularly update the menu with the latest information
about how much time, effort, and resources each diagnostic requires, as well as how valuable
each diagnostic is for the experiment. The system helps the planner see the total cost of their
selections and warns them if they go over budget, prompting them to get approval or adjust their
choices. This process ensures that both practical needs and scientific goals are balanced while
keeping spending under control.

Sequence Diagram 2:

If anyone using the diagnostic menu notices something isn’t working right—like a calculation
error or a technical glitch—they can easily report the problem to the software team. Once the
issue is reported, the software experts investigate what went wrong and fix it as quickly as
possible. After the fix, they let the person who reported the problem (and other users) know that
everything is working again. This quick reporting and repair process keeps the system reliable
and ensures that everyone can continue planning experiments without unnecessary delays or
mistakes.

Sequence Diagram 3:

To make sure the diagnostic menu always reflects the latest costs and scientific values, the
person managing the menu regularly checks in with both the operations team and the scientists
responsible for each diagnostic. Together, they review and update details like how much effort
each diagnostic takes and how important it is for research. Any changes are approved by
scientific managers before being added to the system. Once updates are complete, the new
information is made available for planning future experiments, while ongoing or already-
approved experiments continue without disruption. This routine updating means planners
always have the most accurate information, helping them make the best decisions for upcoming
work.

Quality Function Deployment:
Quality Function Deployment (QFD), or the “House of Quality,” is employed to systematically
translate stakeholder requirements into engineering characteristics. By mapping acceptance
criteria to design features and highlighting interrelationships, QFD prioritizes system attributes
that deliver maximum stakeholder value and ensures alignment between needs and technical
solutions. This methodical approach enhances the diagnostic menu’s ability to meet stakeholder
expectations efficiently.

The Quality Function Deployment (QFD), often referred to as the “House of Quality,” is a
powerful tool for identifying and prioritizing the engineering characteristics (the HOWs) needed
in a system. QFD begins by taking the acceptance criteria established from stakeholder input
and systematically translating them into specific engineering characteristics that define how the
system will fulfill those needs. Once these characteristics are established, the QFD matrix
enables the team to visualize not only the relationships between each acceptance criterion and
engineering characteristic (EC), but also the interrelationships among the ECs themselves.
For the Diagnostic Menu system, the analysis revealed that all relationships between criteria
and characteristics are either positive or neutral—there are no negative interactions. Notably,
EC2 (displaying the cost of selected diagnostics), EC4 (displaying the scientific impact), and
EC6 (calculating diagnostic value based on cost and impact) exhibit the strongest correlations.
This means that improvements in one of these areas tend to enhance the others as well,
highlighting a strong synergy: the more effectively the system displays cost and scientific
impact, the better it can calculate and present diagnostic value overall.

Reflection
This project is something I’d been loosely involved with for over a year, but honestly, there
wasn’t much momentum from me or anyone else to really move it forward. That changed when I
got a new manager, who pointed out that this was exactly the kind of project that would benefit
from a systems engineering approach. His reasoning made sense: we didn’t have a clear
solution in mind, and even the path to get there was uncertain. So, we needed to start by
defining what the end goal should look like, then work backwards to figure out what our
customers actually want, while also accounting for the system’s real-world constraints.
That all sounded reasonable, but it wasn’t until I really took the time to understand the systems
engineering process that it clicked for me. I learned how to translate vague stakeholder needs
into concrete acceptance criteria, and then identify the engineering requirements that would
actually make those criteria achievable. What stood out was the importance of verifying the links
between acceptance criteria and engineering requirements—making sure we understood how
each one influenced or depended on the other—so we could confidently define the true system
requirements.
I’d never experienced such a methodical and comprehensive way of developing system
requirements before. Now that I’ve actually worked through the process on this project, I have a
much deeper appreciation for it, and I can see how valuable it is for tackling complex or
ambiguous projects like this one.
