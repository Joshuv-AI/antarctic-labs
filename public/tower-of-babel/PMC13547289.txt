Effect of poultry manure and chemical fertilizers on soil chemical and biological properties in millet, sorghum, and maize
Authors: Sara Darabi, Gholamreza Heidari, Payman Salami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the effects of poultry manure and chemical fertilizer on soil chemical and biological properties in pearl millet (Pennisetum glaucum), sorghum (Sorghum bicolor), and maize (Zea mays) at the Research Farm of the University of Kurdistan, Iran, during 2022 and 2023. The experiment was conducted as a split-plot arrangement based on a randomized complete block design with three replications. The main plot factor included three crops (pearl millet, sorghum, and maize), while the subplot factor consisted of five fertilizer treatments, including control, poultry manure, chemical fertilizer, and combinations of poultry manure with urea and triple superphosphate at varying rates. The results showed that, among the fertilizer treatments, the highest soil organic carbon levels across all three crops were observed in the F2 treatment (poultry manure). Soil nitrate levels were higher under pearl millet (11.75 mg kg⁻1) and maize (11.23 mg kg⁻1) than under sorghum (10.53 mg kg⁻1). The highest soil potassium concentration (139.9 mg kg⁻1) was recorded in the full chemical fertilizer treatment (F5), whereas the lowest value (118 mg kg⁻1) was observed in the control treatment. Poultry manure significantly improved soil nutrient content, increased acid and alkaline phosphatase activities. The highest acid phosphatase activity (132.83 μg PNP g⁻1 h⁻1) was observed with poultry manure application, while the lowest activities were recorded in the control (79.45 μg PNP g⁻1 h⁻1) and chemical fertilizer (88.2 μg PNP g⁻1 h⁻1) treatments. Although poultry manure increased soil EC, its benefits for soil fertility and biological activity indicate that it may be suitable under controlled application rates and regular EC monitoring.

Introduction

Pearl millet (Pennisetum glaucum) is a traditional grain crop widely cultivated in dry and semi-arid tropical regions because of its rapid growth, drought tolerance, and efficient water use. As a C4 plant, pearl millet uses water more efficiently than C3 species, making it particularly suitable for cultivation in water-limited areas1. Globally, the cultivated area of pearl millet is approximately 35.50 million hectares, with much of it devoted to grain production2. Sorghum is nutritionally comparable to Zea mays (maize), but it generally contains a higher protein percentage. In addition, sorghum requires less water and produces more biomass than maize3. It can be used as green fodder, dry fodder, silage, or direct pasture for livestock. While maize typically requires around 770 mm of water annually to achieve high yields, sorghum uses approximately 25% less water because of its lower transpiration rate, making it a viable alternative in arid and semi-arid regions. Sorghum is also more resilient to environmental stress than many other C4 crops and adapts well to low-nutrient soils. It shows strong tolerance to abiotic stresses such as heat, drought, and salinity, and often performs better than other crops under stress conditions. Its well-developed root system improves water and nutrient uptake, resulting in higher dry matter yields than maize and other summer cereals3. These characteristics make sorghum a suitable crop for dryland areas and soils with limited water and nutrients3. Maize ranks among the world’s major cereal crops in terms of production. In 2020, the global cultivation area was approximately 250.5 million hectares, with production reaching about 1237.8 million tons2. Maize, along with wheat and rice, is one of the three primary cereal crops worldwide2. It is highly valued as a forage crop for green fodder and silage because of its high sugar and starch content and its relatively high yield per unit area, which provides economically favorable returns4.

Organic fertilizers, such as poultry manure, are widely used in agriculture to improve soil fertility. Poultry manure is a valuable organic resource rich in nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, offering ecological and economic benefits when applied properly. Its nutrient content is higher than that of many other animal manures, making it an effective fertilizer. However, improper application can reduce its efficiency and negatively affect soil health. To maximize economic yield, poultry manure should be applied according to crop nutrient requirements5. Beyond supplying essential nutrients, poultry manure offers several agronomic advantages, including the gradual release of nitrogen, which helps reduce nitrate leaching, and the slow release of calcium and potassium, which may help mitigate soil acidity. In addition, it increases soil organic matter and thereby improves the soil’s capacity to retain water and nutrients6. Given the limitations of both chemical and organic fertilizers, their combined application can improve overall fertilizer efficiency and contribute to better soil fertility, crop yield, and product quality7. Integrating non-chemical sources such as poultry manure with chemical fertilizers provides a balanced approach to meeting plant nutritional needs. This combination can optimize nutrient uptake and reduce reliance on chemical fertilizers alone, which may have adverse environmental effects. Because organic fertilizers release nutrients slowly, nutrient deficiencies may occur during critical growth stages, especially early in development, making integrated management strategies necessary8. The combined use of organic and chemical fertilizers is therefore considered an effective approach for producing high-quality fodder9. Moreover, such practices can reduce the environmental impact of fertilizer use, maintain nutritional balance, and enhance crop performance7. Muhamut et al.7 reported that nitrogen uptake efficiency and yield in crop plants were greater when an appropriate combination of chemical and organic fertilizers was applied than when either source was used alone in high amounts. They attributed this to the effective role of chemical fertilizers in supplying nutrients during early growth stages and organic fertilizers during later periods. In an experiment investigating the effect of different levels of chemical fertilizers alone and in combination with organic fertilizers on the growth, yield, and quality of corn grain over two consecutive years, the results showed that combined use of organic and chemical fertilizers significantly increased leaf area index, grain nitrogen percentage, and grain yield. In addition, this combined treatment increased the population of free nitrogen-fixing bacteria in the rhizosphere9. In another experiment conducted in southeastern Nigeria, four levels of chemical and organic fertilizers and their combined application on maize plants were evaluated, and the highest grain yield of 4.5 tons per hectare was obtained in the treatment combining 100 kg of urea fertilizer with five tons of organic fertilizer per hectare8. The aim of this experiment is to evaluate the response of three forage crops, pearl millet, sorghum, and maize, to the simultaneous application of animal manure and chemical fertilizers. This study also aims to elucidate the mechanistic effects of combined poultry manure and chemical fertilizer application on soil microbial enzyme activities and nutrient cycling processes in forage crops. Specifically, we hypothesize that integrated nutrient management enhances microbial-mediated nutrient transformations, leading to improved soil fertility and crop performance. By investigating changes in microbial community structure, enzyme activity profiles, and nutrient mineralization rates, this research seeks to advance understanding of the biological processes underpinning sustainable fertilization practices in nutrient-limited systems.

Materials and methods

The farm is situated 45 km east of Sanandaj, at 35.18° N latitude and 47.18° E longitude, with an elevation of 1866 m above sea level. The region receives an average annual rainfall of 350 mm, and its climate is classified as Mediterranean and semi-arid according to the Emberger classification method. The average annual maximum and minimum temperatures are 23 °C and 6.6 °C, respectively. Experimental design

The field experiment was conducted over two years at the research farm of the University of Kurdistan, located in Dehgolan Plain, Iran. The experiment was arranged as a split-plot based on a randomized complete block design with three replications. Crop species, including pearl millet (Nutrifid variety), sorghum (Speedfeed variety), and maize (SC 260), were assigned to the main plots. Fertilizer treatments were assigned to the subplots and included F1: control; F2: 4 t ha⁻1 poultry manure; F3: 3 t ha⁻1 poultry manure + 50 kg N ha⁻1 from urea + 25 kg P ha⁻1 from triple superphosphate; F4: 2 t ha⁻1 poultry manure + 100 kg N ha⁻1 from urea + 50 kg P ha⁻1 from triple superphosphate; and F5: complete chemical fertilizer consisting of 200 kg N ha⁻1 from urea + 100 kg P ha⁻1 from triple superphosphate. The field was planted on May 10 and harvested on October 19 in both years of the project.

The poultry manure used in this study was added to the soil in fresh form. This fertilizer contained 70% moisture and a carbon to nitrogen ratio of 8:12. Before planting, chicken manure was incorporated into the soil at a depth of 0–30 cm using a tiller, and nitrogen fertilizer was administered as a solution during the growth period. The experimental field dimensions were 60 m × 20 m, with main plots measuring 20 m × 5 m, and subplots measuring 5 m × 3 m. Each subplot contained five crop rows with row spacings as follows: Pearl millet at 8 cm, sorghum at 10 cm, and maize at 15 cm. The spacing between the main plots was 2 m, between subplots was 1 m, and the distance between adjacent irrigation strips was 60 cm.

An automatic drip irrigation system was employed, with a fixed irrigation cycle of seven days. The amount of water applied was determined based on soil moisture content, bringing each plot to field capacity. Water flow was measured using a volumetric meter installed on the main pipe (Eq. 1). Measurement of soil nitrate

To calculate the amount of soil nitrate, 50 g of soil sample were weighed and transferred to a 500 mL Erlenmeyer flask. Then, 250 mL of nitrate extracting solution was added, and the mixture was shaken for 10 min. Subsequently, 0.4 g of calcium hydroxide and 1 g of magnesium carbonate were added. Next, 25 mL of the clear soil extract was transferred to a ceramic crucible using a pipette, and the extract was evaporated in a bain-marie bath until completely dry. After drying, 2 mL of phenol disulfonic acid solution was added to the crucible and mixed thoroughly with the remaining extract for 10 min. Then, 35 mL of distilled water was added, dissolving the remaining extract by stirring. All dissolved materials, including the crucible washings, were transferred to a 100-mL volumetric flask. The final volume was adjusted to 100 mL with distilled water. The color intensity of the soil extract was then measured using a spectrophotometer at a wavelength of 420 nm. Finally, the absorption calibration chart using ultraviolet electromagnetic waves at a wavelength of 538 nm with at least 6 points was obtained using standard control samples and test samples14.

Measurement of soil potassium

To measure soil available potassium using the sodium tetraphenylborate extraction method, 0.5 g of soil passed through a 2 mm sieve was weighed and placed into digestion tubes of the digestion block device. Then, 3 mL of extraction solution containing 0.2 M sodium tetraphenylborate, 1.7 M sodium chloride, and 0.01 M EDTA was added. One minute after adding the extraction solution, 25 mL of a solution containing 0.5 M ammonium chloride and 0.50 M copper chloride was added to stop the potassium extraction process. The digestion tubes were then placed on the digestion block device for 30–45 min at 150 °C until complete precipitation occurred. Subsequently, the solution volume was increased to 50 mL, and after soil sedimentation, 20 mL of the supernatant was transferred to centrifuge tubes, with three drops of 6 N hydrochloric acid added. The samples were then centrifuged at 4700 rpm for 5 min. Finally, the clear supernatant was separated, and potassium content was measured using a flame photometer. Potassium stock standard solution 1000 mg/L: 1.9080 g of laboratory grade potassium chloride (KCL) was added to one liter of prepared ammonium acetate solution.

Standard solutions: To make a solution with a concentration of 100 mg/L, 100 ml of the stock standard solution was taken and made up to volume in a one-liter flask with the stock standard solution. In the next step, 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, 50 ml of the prepared solution were taken. They were made up to volume in 100-ml flasks with the extractant solution. These standard solutions contained 0, 10, 20, 30, 40, and 50 mg/L of potassium, respectively15.

Measurement of soil pH

First, the soil was passed through a 2-mm sieve. A portion of the soil was transferred into a beaker and saturated with distilled water. After 24 h to reach moisture equilibrium and re-adjustment of moisture to saturation, the acidity of the saturated soil was measured using a pH meter16.

Measurement of soil EC

The electrical conductivity of the saturated soil extract was measured using a conductivity meter (EC meter) at a temperature of 25 °C16.

Measurement of soil basal respiration

In order to measure basal respiration, 100 g of air-dried soil was weighed and transferred to 1-L plastic containers. By adding water, the soil moisture was increased to 70–80% of the field capacity, and this soil moisture was controlled by continuous weighing until the end of the experiment. The samples were incubated for 10 days at 25 °C before incubation, and then the carbon dioxide resulting from microbial respiration was collected in 0.5N sodium hydroxide (NaOH) and titrated with 0.25N HCl every 10 days for one month17.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SAS version 9.1. Prior to the combined analysis across the two years, the homogeneity of experimental error variances was tested using Bartlett’s test. Since the variances were homogeneous, the data were analyzed using a combined split-plot ANOVA model across years. In this model, year was considered as an environmental factor, crop species as the main-plot factor, and fertilizer treatment as the subplot factor. Replication was treated as a random effect within year. For the split-plot structure, the main-plot error was used to test the effect of crop species, whereas the subplot error was used to test the effect of fertilizer treatment and the interaction between crop species and fertilizer treatment. Treatment means were separated using the least significant difference (LSD) test at the 5% significance level only when the corresponding F-test was significant. Graphical representations of the data were prepared using Microsoft Excel.

Discussion

The results indicate that the application of organic fertilizers was associated with higher soil electrical conductivity (EC) and lower soil acidity. This pattern is consistent with previous reports showing that organic fertilizer application can increase EC while reducing acidity18. In the present study, the observed changes in EC and pH suggest that organic amendments may contribute to a more favorable soil chemical environment by modifying nutrient availability and buffering soil reactions, rather than implying direct effects on crop yield, biomass, or forage quality. However, the extent of these changes likely depends on the composition of the amendment, the initial soil condition, and the amount of organic matter incorporated into the soil. When sufficient organic matter is present, ions may be retained within the soil matrix and become available to plants as needed, whereas low organic matter content may limit this buffering capacity. Compared with fresh crop residues or animal manure, composted organic matter tends to decompose more slowly because it has already undergone substantial breakdown during composting19. Importantly, the significant crop × fertilizer interaction for EC indicates that the effect of fertilizer source was not uniform across forage crops. This difference may be related to crop-specific effects on the rhizosphere, including variation in root activity, ion uptake capacity, and sensitivity to salt accumulation under the prevailing soil conditions. Therefore, the observed interaction should be interpreted as a crop-dependent modification of soil EC under different fertilizer sources, not as evidence of differential crop productivity.

Different fertilizer treatments affected soil EC in distinct ways. In this study, treatments involving manure with nitrogen (N), manure with phosphorus (P), compost with nitrogen or phosphorus, and NPK chemical fertilizers showed different EC responses, with compost plus nitrogen producing the highest EC and NPK chemical fertilizers the lowest EC during the growing season. These differences appear to be influenced by soil conditions, water availability, and nutrient levels, which are known factors affecting EC20. Rather than indicating a universally superior amendment, these results suggest that the chemical behavior of each fertilizer source under the prevailing soil and moisture conditions determined the observed EC pattern. This interpretation is important because EC responses were treatment-specific and crop-dependent, indicating that the same fertilizer source may not produce identical soil chemical responses across forage species.

In the poultry manure treatment, higher soil nitrate levels were observed, which may reflect greater nitrogen accumulation in the soil. However, the available data do not by themselves indicate how much of this nitrate was taken up by plants or retained in the soil profile. Chemical fertilizers supplied nitrogen rapidly, but in high-pH Iranian soils nitrate may be more prone to leaching and therefore less persistent in the soil21. Plant nitrate uptake is influenced by multiple factors, including species characteristics, light intensity, soil type, temperature, soil moisture, harvest timing, and root access, which may explain variation among crops19. Accordingly, differences among crop–fertilizer combinations in soil nitrate should be viewed as reflecting both fertilizer-derived nitrogen supply and crop-specific effects on the rhizosphere, rather than as direct evidence of improved forage production.

A deficiency in organic matter causes phosphorus (P) to bind with soil colloids, reducing its availability to plants. Organic fertilizers, particularly poultry manure, may increase phosphorus availability by improving soil P absorption capacity and modifying soil chemical conditions. The use of compost can further increase soil phosphorus availability by altering colloid behavior through pH changes and by increasing soluble phosphorus levels20. Studies have also reported increased available phosphorus and exchangeable potassium in organic farming systems utilizing organic fertilizers21. The improved phosphorus availability associated with organic amendments can be attributed to mechanisms such as carbonic acid production during organic matter decomposition, formation of phospho-humic complexes, displacement of phosphates from adsorption sites, and reduced phosphate fixation on clay and iron or aluminum oxides22. These mechanisms provide a plausible explanation for the observed changes, although the present data mainly demonstrate shifts in soil P status rather than direct plant uptake measurements. Thus, the higher phosphorus availability observed under organic amendments should be interpreted as an improvement in soil nutrient status, while its consequences for plant P uptake or forage quality require direct crop measurements.

Organic manure also promotes potassium (K) availability by stimulating microbial activity, which can contribute to the dissolution of potassium-bearing minerals. Organic matter and its derivatives may form complexes with iron and aluminum, decreasing potassium fixation in the soil. Poultry manure, rich in microorganisms, can also release organic acids such as oxalic acid, which facilitate the release of potassium and other nutrients. Thus, the higher K availability observed in amended soils is consistent with enhanced mineral weathering and reduced fixation processes under organic inputs23. Additionally, alkaline phosphatase enzymes play a crucial role in mineralizing organic phosphorus; their activity increases when soil phosphorus levels are low or when precursors are abundant, especially in soils amended with poultry manure that supplies ample phosphorus, thus enhancing microbial activity and enzyme secretion24. However, because the study evaluated soil chemical and biological indicators rather than harvested biomass or forage nutritive traits, the observed increases in K and enzyme activity should not be extended to claims of improved crop performance.

Soil enzyme activity, particularly alkaline phosphatase, serves as an indicator of microbial activity and overall soil functioning, and it is often associated with soil organic matter content. In this study, the addition of organic fertilizers, including poultry manure, was associated with enhanced enzyme activity, which may reflect the combined effects of greater substrate supply, stimulation of microbial populations, and improved stabilization of enzymes on organic colloids, reducing their degradation24,25. These changes suggest that organic amendments can support enzymatic processes involved in nutrient cycling, particularly by contributing to the transformation and release of nutrients from organic pools. However, the data do not allow a direct quantification of how much of this activity translated into nutrient release. Organic amendments also increase organic carbon and nutrient availability, which can further support microbial biomass and enzymatic functions. The higher phosphatase activity observed in poultry manure treatments therefore provides evidence of intensified soil biochemical cycling, but not direct evidence of increased forage yield or quality.

The activity of phosphatases is influenced by the presence of easily degradable compounds such as cellulose, dissolved organic carbon, and microbial biomass, all of which can increase following organic fertilizer application24. Organic fertilizers may also reduce enzyme immobilization on clay minerals or humic substances, thereby helping maintain enzymatic activity26. Moreover, poultry manure can increase the soil organic phosphorus pool, providing substrate for phosphatase enzymes27. In this way, the observed phosphatase activity is consistent with changes in soil nutrient cycling processes, particularly those associated with phosphorus mineralization and availability. This crop–fertilizer response is therefore best explained through differences in soil substrate availability and microbial activity rather than through unmeasured differences in crop productivity.

Soil respiration, which reflects microbial activity, is stimulated by increased organic matter and nutrient inputs. The rate of gas exchange, including oxygen consumption and carbon dioxide release, depends on the quantity and quality of organic matter, microbial abundance, and soil properties. Elevated organic matter and nutrient levels can therefore support microbial activity and lead to higher basal respiration rates, which are commonly used as indicators of soil biological activity28. In the present study, the increased respiration observed under organic amendments is consistent with greater microbial substrate availability and intensified decomposition of added organic materials29. This suggests increased short-term microbial turnover and carbon mineralization in amended soils, although the results do not directly establish long-term carbon sequestration or stabilization outcomes. Similarly, increased soil respiration should be interpreted as a biological soil response to added organic substrates, not as direct evidence of improved crop growth or sustainable forage yield.

Overall, the results indicate that organic fertilizers influenced soil chemical, biological, and enzymatic properties by modifying nutrient availability, microbial activity, and soil biochemical processes. The findings show that these amendments altered soil functioning under the studied conditions and may have created a more favorable soil environment. While this study utilized significant rates of chemical fertilizers to meet crop demand, the integration of poultry manure significantly improved soil biological properties and nutrient availability. This integrated approach serves as a practical step toward sustainable nutrient management by enhancing fertilizer efficiency and reducing reliance on purely synthetic inputs, although comprehensive long-term sustainability requires further multi-dimensional assessment.

Conclusion

The results showed that fertilizer treatments had different effects on the measured soil chemical and biological properties. Poultry manure significantly increased soil nitrate availability, acid phosphatase activity, and basal respiration, whereas the highest soil potassium concentration was observed under the complete chemical fertilizer treatment. However, not all soil indicators responded uniformly to fertilization, and increases in EC under poultry manure indicate that its application should be carefully managed. Therefore, poultry manure may contribute to improved nutrient availability and microbial activity, but its long-term use should be accompanied by appropriate application rates, balanced nutrient management, and regular monitoring of soil salinity. Since chemical fertilizers were also used in this study, the findings should be interpreted within the context of integrated nutrient management rather than as evidence of fully sustainable fertilizer use. This approach may help reduce reliance on chemical inputs while maintaining soil fertility and minimizing potential environmental risks.
