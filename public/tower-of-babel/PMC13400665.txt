Interleukin-8–driven monocyte activation in primary sclerosing cholangitis: Insights from single-cell analysis and humanized immune system mice
Authors: Lander Heyerick, Zenzi De Vos, Aline Baekelandt, Kevin De Muynck, Rania Matthys, Lara Morren, Emine Gökce, Tim Meese, Malaïka Van Der Linden, Anne Hoorens, Jana Roels, Niels Vandamme, Hannah Stocks, Andy Wullaert, Filip Van Nieuwerburgh, Sander Lefere, Sarah Raevens, Anja Geerts, Xavier Verhelst, Hans Van Vlierberghe, Lindsey Devisscher
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background & AimsPrimary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) is an immune-mediated liver disease with an incompletely understood pathogenesis. Dysregulation of monocytes and macrophages has been implicated in disease development, although their functional relevance remains insufficiently understood. The main aim of this study was to identify peripheral myeloid cell populations involved in PSC pathogenesis and to assess the therapeutic potential of targeting associated inflammatory signalling pathways. Additionally, we sought to enhance translational relevance through the application of a humanized immune system (HIS) mouse model.MethodsPeripheral blood mononuclear cells from patients with PSC, patients with ulcerative colitis, and healthy controls were profiled using CITE-seq (cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes by sequencing). Inflammatory signalling via the IL-8:CXCR1/2 axis was further evaluated in both conventional and HIS mouse models of cholestatic liver disease.ResultsPatients with PSC exhibit a population of circulatory CXCL8+CD14+ monocytes with upregulated proinflammatory signalling. Serum concentrations of the proinflammatory cytokine IL-8, encoded by CXCL8, were increased in patients with PSC (p <0.001) and associated with poor prognosis. Patients with high IL-8 concentrations (≥27.8 pg/ml) at baseline had significantly worse transplant-free survival than those with lower IL-8 concentrations (log-rank hazard ratio 6.12, 95% CI 1.68-22.33). Intrahepatic IL-8+ macrophages were expanded during disease progression (p <0.05). Pharmacological Cxcr1 antagonism in conventional mouse models and antibody-mediated IL-8 neutralization in HIS mice reduced cholestatic liver injury.ConclusionsA circulating CXCL8+CD14+ monocyte subset and intrahepatic IL-8+ macrophages were identified as potential contributors to PSC pathogenesis. HIS mouse models could mark a significant advance in translational research on human immune responses. Targeting the IL-8:CXCR1/2 axis represents a promising therapeutic strategy that warrants further investigation.Impact and implicationsThe immune mechanisms underlying primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) remain incompletely understood, limiting the development of effective targeted therapies. We identified a circulating CXCL8+CD14+ monocyte subset and expanded intrahepatic IL-8+ macrophages associated with inflammatory activation in PSC, while elevated serum IL-8 concentrations identified patients with poorer transplant-free survival. Therapeutic inhibition of the IL-8:CXCR1/2 axis reduced cholestatic liver injury in both conventional and humanized immune system mouse models, supporting its relevance across experimental systems. These findings implicate IL-8-driven myeloid inflammation in PSC pathogenesis, identify IL-8 as a potential prognostic biomarker, and support further evaluation of IL-8-directed therapies in PSC.

Introduction

Primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC) is a chronic, heterogeneous cholestatic liver disease characterised by stricturing of the extra- and/or intrahepatic bile ducts with associated inflammation and biliary fibrosis, and is frequently complicated by progressive end-stage liver disease and/or hepatobiliary malignancy. Approximately two-thirds of patients with PSC have a clinically distinct form of concomitant inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), which most frequently resembles ulcerative colitis (UC). At present, no clinically approved pharmacological treatments can reduce liver disease progression in patients with PSC. In part, this can be attributed to the incompletely understood pathophysiology of the disease.1

PSC is considered an immune-mediated disease. However, a profound understanding of immune dysregulation and its potential to serve as a pharmacological target is lacking.2 In this regard, the single-cell era is rapidly revolutionizing our understanding of immune system (dys)functioning in several immune-mediated diseases, facilitating the development of new targeted treatments. Single-cell studies have identified PSC-specific hepatic monocyte-like macrophage clusters,3 highly inflammatory hepatic monocytes and macrophages,4 and an expanded hepatic CD4+ T-cell cluster with naïve-like characteristics and an increased propensity for Th17-associated effector differentiation.5 However, these results have not yet led to new therapeutic options. Importantly, alterations in circulating cell subsets are associated with disease prognosis, and identifying specific circulating immune signatures may provide key insights into pathophysiological mechanisms, supporting the identification of novel diagnostic and therapeutic targets.

To develop novel treatment strategies, it is essential to select the most appropriate model that is suited to the research objective, enabling accurate investigation of disease pathophysiology and therapeutic interventions. Classical pre-clinical mouse models of cholestatic liver disease have aided in the mechanistic understanding of pathogenetic concepts in PSC. However, considering the immune system, the mononuclear phagocyte system is substantially different across species2 and this hampers translation of results derived from these classical models. To study innate immune system-targeting treatments, the use of models incorporating parts of a functional human immune system is essential. To date, mouse models harbouring all human immune subsets are lacking and most research on humanized immune system (HIS) mice is concentrated in oncology, primarily targeting T-cell subsets, with limited studies addressing components of the human innate immune system. This results in a significant gap in translational immunology and hepatology, as dysregulated innate immune responses are central to the pathogenesis of numerous immune-mediated diseases.

In this study, we aimed to increase our understanding of the peripheral immune landscape of patients with PSC at single-cell resolution, with emphasis on myeloid cell populations, to identify potential novel diagnostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets. Furthermore, we wanted to advance the translational potential of pre-clinical hepato-immunology research by developing and employing HIS mice, thereby facilitating the translation of experimental findings into clinical practice.

Materials and methods

Participants

Patients with PSC, who were diagnosed according to established diagnostic guidelines of the European Association for the Study of the Liver6 and followed up at Ghent University Hospital (Ghent, Belgium), and healthy control participants, were included for study participation after written informed consent was obtained. Exclusion criteria were an ongoing active inflammatory and/or infectious episode of cholangitis and/or colitis, and a history of liver transplantation. Liver fibrosis was assessed according to established cut-off criteria for liver stiffness measurement,7 histological grading with Sirius Red staining, or by the clear clinical presence of cirrhosis. Paraffin-embedded liver samples were provided by the pathology department of Ghent University Hospital. The study was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Ghent University Hospital (Ethical Committee 2019/0611, Belgian registration number: B670201940078).

Human PBMC isolation

EDTA-anticoagulated whole blood from study participants was obtained via peripheral venipuncture and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated by Lymphoprep (Stemcell Technologies, #07801) density gradient medium and SepMate (Stemcell Technologies, #85450) isolation tubes according to manufacturer’s instructions. Then, the isolated mononuclear cells were cryopreserved at -80 °C in CryoStor CS10 (Stemcell Technologies, #07930) cell freezing medium using a slow-rate cell freezing container (CoolCell, Corning, CLS432002-1EA). Cells were transferred after overnight incubation to a liquid nitrogen tank for long-term storage.

Results

Single-cell transcriptome and receptor profiling of peripheral blood myeloid cells in PSC, UC and healthy controls

To characterize systemic immune profiles of patients with PSC, we performed cellular indexing of transcriptomes and epitopes by sequencing (CITE-Seq) on PBMCs from seven patients with PSC, of whom four had concomitant UC, and compared these profiles with four patients with UC and four healthy controls (Fig. 1A). Patient characteristics are provided in Table S1. After live-cell enrichment and quality control, 72,859 cells were obtained, of which 18,448 belonged to T-cell subsets, 8,134 to B-cell subsets, 6,065 to NK cell subsets, and 40,212 to myeloid cell subsets (Fig. 1B, Figs. S1 and S2). Reclustering the lymphoid cells resulted in 12 clusters (Fig. S3). Two B-cell subsets were identified and annotated based on canonical B-cell markers (CD19, CD79A, and CD79B). Memory B cells were characterized by CD27 and AIM2 expression, whereas naive B cells express IL4R and FCER2. Two NK cell populations were identified, corresponding to resting and proliferating NK cells. Both subsets express canonical NK cell markers (NKG7 and KLRD1) and lack expression of CD3D, distinguishing them from T-cell populations. The proliferating NK cell cluster was further defined by high expression of the proliferation marker MKI67. T cells were defined by expression of CD3D and further subdivided based on expression of CD4 and CD8. Regulatory T cells were identified by expression of FOXP3 and IL2RA. Mucosal-associated invariant T cells were characterized by expression of SLC4A10 and KLRB1. Naive T cells were distinguished from other T-cell subsets by expression of CCR7, SELL, and LEF1, and CD45RA. Within the CD8+ T-cell compartment, two cytotoxic subsets were annotated based on differential granzyme expression. In addition, one CD4+ T-cell subcluster exhibited high expression of ITGB1 and CD45RO, distinguishing it from the naive CD4+ T-cell population. A distinct γδ T cell cluster was identified, characterized by high expression of γδ T cell receptor genes TRDC and TRGC1. Finally, a small NK-like T cell cluster was identified, characterized by strong expression of killer lectin receptors (KLRC2, KLRC3, and KLRD1) and cytotoxic genes (CTSW, GZMK). For myeloid subsets, eight myeloid cell subpopulations (Fig. 1C) were distinguished based on the expression of canonical and differentially expressed marker genes, and on protein expression analysed via CITE-Seq antibodies. More specifically, a cluster of CD14+CD16+ monocytes, CD14dimCD16+ monocytes, CXCL8+ inflammatory CD14+ monocytes, IFN-responsive CD14+ monocytes, myeloid dendritic cells (DCs), PADI4+CD14+ monocytes, plasmacytoid DCs and platelet monocyte aggregates were identified (Fig. 1D,E). Classical CD14+CD16- monocytes comprised the majority of peripheral blood myeloid cells, while intermediary CD14+CD16+ and non-classical CD14dimCD16+ monocytes, myeloid DCs and plasmacytoid DCs were less abundant (Fig. S4A). Peripheral blood monocytes of patients with PSC display a pro-inflammatory state

In the CD14+CD16+ monocyte cluster, genes associated with a pro-inflammatory state were significantly upregulated in patients with PSC vs. controls, including UTS2, TULP2, multiple chemokines, TNF and TNFAIP3 (Fig. S5A,B and Supplementary file of all DEGs). Interestingly, the top upregulated genes in this cluster, namely UTS2 and TULP2, are involved in chemotaxis.8 These genes, together with other pro-inflammatory markers, were also significantly upregulated in patients with PSC compared to those with PSC/UC and UC (Fig. S5C,D and Supplementary file of all DEGs). In addition, in the CXCL8+ inflammatory CD14+ monocyte cluster (Fig. 2A), most DEGs were unique to the patient’s underlying disease (Fig. 2B-D and Fig. S6, and Supplementary file of all DEGs) and involved differences in proinflammatory gene expression, including TULP2, CXCL8, CCL3, JUN, and IL3RA. Expression of CXCL8 in this cluster was significantly upregulated in PSC (avg. log2FC 1.86, p <0.05) and UC (2.50, p <0.001) patients compared to control (Fig. 2E,F and Supplementary file of all DEGs). In CD14dimCD16+ monocytes, genes associated with inflammation and chemotaxis, such as TULP2, JUN, IL1B and TNF, were significantly upregulated in patients with PSC compared to those with PSC/UC and UC, although fewer pro-inflammatory markers were involved compared with the CD14+ clusters (Fig. S9A). The IFN-responsive monocyte cluster was characterized by interferon-signalling and viral responses, and pro-inflammatory markers. UTS2 and TULP2 and pro-inflammatory markers were also significantly upregulated in patients with PSC vs. healthy controls, and those with PSC/UC and UC (Fig. S9B). The PADI4+ inflammatory CD14+ monocyte cluster showed higher expression of genes involved in reactive oxygen species processes (CYP1B1, ITGAM, MGST1) without differences between groups, except for the pro-inflammatory markers which were again upregulated in patients with PSC. Two DC clusters were also identified, myeloid DCs and plasmacytoid DCs, which were implicated in antigen presentation and processing of nucleic acids. Myeloid DCs showed a similar pattern to inflammatory monocytes, with upregulation of inflammatory genes in patients with PSC, whereas plasmacytoid DCs showed no marked differences between patient groups (Supplementary file of all DEGs).

IL-8 is secreted in the systemic circulation and serves as a predictor of advanced disease and poor prognosis in patients with PSC

To validate previous reports demonstrating increased serum IL-8 concentrations in patients with PSC,9 we assessed if IL-8 could serve as a systemic biomarker. We analysed IL-8 serum profiles in PSC (+/- IBD) patients in comparison to age- and sex-matched healthy controls. 49 patients with PSC, of whom 28 had IBD, and 13 healthy controls, were analysed. Patient characteristics are described in Table S4. To determine ‘baseline’ expression, only participants without evidence of an active ongoing infection or an acute flare of IBD were included. The median (IQR) follow-up time after sampling was 36.5 (23.0-64.0) months.

The median serum IL-8 concentration was 17.9 pg/ml (IQR 10.2-34.5) and 13.6 pg/ml (IQR 8.0-27.4) in patients with PSC and PSC/IBD, respectively. In patients with PSC, IL-8 levels significantly correlated with alkaline phosphatase, alanine aminotransferase, (direct) bilirubin and the revised Mayo risk score (Table S5). Moreover, IL-8 concentrations were significantly higher in patients with advanced disease stage (F3-F4, median [IQR] 50.0 [17.9-98.0] pg/ml) compared to early disease stage (F0-F2, 21.9 [IQR 9.2-33.7] pg/ml; Fig. 4C). In addition, patients with PSC and high IL-8 concentrations (i.e. ≥ 27.8 pg/ml) at baseline had significantly worse transplant-free survival compared to patients with low IL-8 concentrations (log-rank hazard ratio 6.12, 95% CI 1.68-22.33) (Fig. 4D). The determined cut-off showed good discriminative potential for stratifying high- and low-risk patients with PSC (AUROC 0.84; 95% CI 0.69-0.98; Fig. 4E).

Expansion of hepatic IL-8+ macrophages in PSC

Overlapping regions within portal tracts1,8 were then analysed (Fig. 5A). Next, we wanted to investigate whether hepatic accumulation of IL-8+ macrophages is also present in cholestatic mouse models. However, since mice do not express CXCL8, we evaluated the expression of the murine homologues Cxcl1 and Cxcl2 in liver-isolated macrophages from common bile duct ligated (CBDL) and sham operated animals, using the dataset published in De Muynck et al..10 Mice were subjected to CBDL and sacrificed 2 weeks after induction of cholestatic liver injury. Hepatic monocyte-derived macrophages were isolated and processed for bulk RNA sequencing. The expression of Cxcl1 and Cxcl2 was indeed significantly upregulated in monocyte-derived CD11b+F4/80+ infiltrating macrophages isolated from the livers of CBDL mice compared to controls (Cxcl1 log2FC 2.09, p <0.01; Cxcl2 2.76, p <0.001; Fig. 5C).

Cxcr1 antagonism attenuates cholestatic liver injury

Previous reports show that IL-8 binds the downstream receptors C-X-C chemokine receptor type 1 (Cxcr1) and type 2 (Cxcr2) to exert its proinflammatory functions. Cell-cell communication analyses of our single-cell data show that CXCL8+ inflammatory monocytes from all patient groups actively interact with other circulatory myeloid cell types through CXCL8/IL-8 and its downstream receptors CXCR1 and CXCR2. Moreover, qualitative comparison of significant mean interaction scores across conditions revealed that CXCL8–CXCR2 signalling was consistently detected as an interaction between myeloid cell types and CXCL8+ inflammatory CD14+ monocytes across all conditions, whereas CXCL8–CXCR1 interactions occurred less frequently. Patients with PSC showed higher CXCL8-CXCR1 and CXCL8-CXCR2 mean interaction scores compared to healthy controls. To overcome this issue, we developed a humanized immune system mouse model in which human myeloid cells engraft. At sacrifice, human immune cell engraftment in the liver, spleen and peripheral blood was assessed (Figs S11-S14). In the liver, engraftment was significantly higher in normal chow-fed mice (median 78.4 [IQR 75.8-85.1]) compared to DDC-fed mice (IgG: 45.3 [IQR 41.1-54.2] and anti-IL-8: 43.8 [IQR 39.6-50.7]; Fig. 8B), without significant differences between genotypes. Immune cell subsets mainly consisted of monocytes/macrophages in the liver, and of B cells in the spleen and peripheral blood, without significant differences between genotypes or sex (Fig. 8C and Fig. S14).

IL-8 blockade in DDC-fed mice effectively neutralized serum IL-8 levels in our humanized mice (Fig. 8D). Blockade of IL-8 resulted in fewer inflammatory foci and diminished fibrosis deposition in humanized DDC-fed mice (Fig. 8E), and a trend towards less ductular reaction in SGM-3 mice (Fig. 8E). Liver expression of the proinflammatory CXCL8 and fibrosis-related Mmp12 gene was lower in Quad humanized DDC-fed anti-IL-8-treated mice compared to untreated DDC-fed Quad mice; however, this was not observed in SGM-3 mice (Fig. 8F). Male DDC-treated mice had significantly less fibrosis compared to their female counterparts after anti-IL-8 treatment (mean difference 2.78, 95% CI 0.41-5.14, p = 0.02, two-way ANOVA), and the same trends were observed in IgG-treated mice (2.37, 95% -0.027 to 4.77, p = 0.052, two-way ANOVA) (Fig. S15). Overall, CXCL8 expression demonstrated a statistically significant positive linear correlation with quantitative fibrosis measures (Spearman’s rho 0.62 (0.31-0.81), p = 0.0004).

Discussion

Immune-mediated diseases such as PSC are conditions in which excessive, dysregulated immune responses contribute to tissue damage. The vulnerability to develop an immune-mediated disease is dictated by a complex interplay of genetic and environmental factors.2,11 As a reflection of systemic immune dynamics, the peripheral blood represents a relevant compartment for investigating immune cell alterations in immune-mediated diseases. We performed CITE-Seq analysis on PBMCs to gain more insights into the dysregulated immune landscape that characterises PSC. These data demonstrate that patients with PSC are characterised by monocytes with an increased pro-inflammatory phenotype, consistent with the presence of a CXCL8+CD14+ monocyte cluster. Single-cell analyses did not show differences in frequencies of myeloid cell populations, potentially due to the limited number of samples analysed in single-cell experiments. However, flow cytometric analyses did show increased frequencies of IL-8+ monocytes in the blood of patients with PSC and PSC/UC. This phenotype resembles a previously described monocyte-like macrophage cluster identified in end-stage PSC livers, which also shows CXCL8 and IL1B upregulation.3 A bulk mRNA sequencing study identified a borderline significant increase in hepatic CXCL8 expression in patients with PSC at high risk of developing clinical events.12 However, this could not be replicated in another report comparing liver mRNA profiles from patients with advanced vs. early-stage PSC,13 highlighting the importance of single-cell resolution to identify changes in discrete cell populations.

Interleukins orchestrate a wide variety of processes such as immune system regulation, chemotaxis, metabolism, and tissue repair.14 Their dysregulated secretion initiates misdirected immune responses towards healthy cells, causing tissue damage and immune-mediated disease. IL-8 (encoded by the CXCL8 gene) is a proinflammatory interleukin produced by a plethora of cell types.[15], [16], [17], [18] During inflammation, expression of CXCL8 is upregulated by proinflammatory cytokines or pathogen-associated components through Toll-like receptor interactions.19 The transcription factor AP-1 (activator protein-1), encoded by JUN, and NF-κB subsequently mediate the upregulation of CXCL8 expression.20 While IL-8 is the most specific ligand for CXCR1, CXCR2 is bound by IL-8 and all other ELR+ CXC chemokines, including CXCL1-3 and CXCL5-7.[20], [21], [22], [23], [24], [25], [26] CXCR1/2 are found on a variety of cell types such as neutrophils, monocytes/macrophages, T cells and smooth muscle cells.[27], [28], [29] Via cell-cell communication analyses, we determined that the identified CXCL8+ monocyte cluster is indeed actively interacting with other myeloid cell types through CXCL8:CXCR1/2 binding.

IL-8 is a promising therapeutic target, since reports have shown associations between IL-8 and markers of disease severity and/or prognosis in chronic liver diseases.9,12,[30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37], [38] Here, we also demonstrate that peripheral blood IL-8 serum concentrations are increased in patients with PSC, and that high serum IL-8 serves as a marker of poor prognosis in these patients. In addition, we identified a clear association between hepatic macrophage accumulation during the progression from early to advanced-stage PSC and a concurrent increase in IL-8-expressing macrophages. These results are in line with findings from Zimmerman et al.39 who showed a direct correlation between hepatic CXCL8 mRNA expression and intrahepatic macrophage accumulation in livers from patients with end-stage primary biliary cholangitis.39 To further explore its therapeutic potential in the context of cholestatic liver disease, we employed antagonists of CXCR1 and/or CXCR2 in a mouse model of extrahepatic cholestasis and demonstrated attenuation of liver injury which was most pronounced after the selective CXCR1 antagonist reparixin. We postulate that the observed differential effects in the compounds used may be attributed to the significantly greater potency of reparixin in inhibiting CXCR1.40,41

Mice lack a direct homolog of human CXCL8 (IL-8). On the other hand, it was shown that murine CXCR1 is a functional receptor for human IL-8.42 We exploited these features to further pinpoint whether monocytic CXCL8:CXCR1/2 interactions are implicated in cholestatic liver disease. More precisely, a human immune system mouse model was developed consisting mainly of human monocytes/macrophages in the liver. Serum analyses confirmed that these mice secrete human IL-8 into the systemic circulation. Treatment with a monoclonal anti-IL-8 antibody effectively neutralized peripheral blood IL-8 and this was associated with attenuated cholestatic liver injury and reduced hepatic collagen accumulation, aligning with the results of the conventional cholestatic mouse model. While mice with the NSG-Quad genotype harbour an additional human CSF1 transgene, we did not observe major differences with respect to human immune cell engraftment and disease severity.

Altogether, these results show the implication of CXCL8:CXCR1/2 interactions in the pathogenesis of cholestatic liver disease and underscore the potential of blocking the CXCL8:CXCR1/2 axis as a therapeutic target in humanized models. Several antagonists and neutralizing antibodies targeting this axis have been evaluated in clinical trials for non-liver-related conditions, including asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. These studies demonstrated a favourable safety profile, with minimal adverse events and good tolerability,20,[43], [44], [45], [46] thereby supporting the translational relevance of these findings.

Although encouraging, several limitations warrant consideration. This study primarily investigated the role of IL-8 in myeloid cells within the context of cholestatic liver disease. However, the potential contribution of IL-8 derived from cholangiocytes should also be acknowledged. In cholangiocytes, IL-8 may facilitate the recruitment of immune cells to sites of hepatic injury. Nevertheless, the development and use of the HIS mouse model identified immune-cell derived IL-8 as a functional source of IL-8 activity, as evidenced by the attenuation of DDC-mediated cholestatic liver injury following antibody-mediated neutralization. Further research should aim to specifically assess the contribution of each of these cell types in cholestatic liver disease.

The methodologies utilized in this study are designed to enhance translational relevance within the field of hepato-immunology and aim to facilitate the continued exploration and investigation of therapeutic targets in immune-mediated liver diseases.
