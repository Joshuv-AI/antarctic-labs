 ABSTRACT

 Effective gas-gap calorimeter sizing and rating tools are required to design calorimeters that enable
well-defined operating conditions, high cooling powers, and accurate calorimetry. The present report
describes two tools, employing Microsoft Excel and MathWorks MATLAB, that enable the sizing and
rating of gas-gap calorimeters with a He-Ar binary gas mixture in the gap. The Excel tools are two
easy-to-use spreadsheets for calculating heat pipe operating temperature or He-Ar mole fractions, given the
required inputs. The MATLAB tool includes similar models with more-robust solution algorithms along
with sub-options for full vacuum, static gas-gap, and forced circulation in the gas-gap. The accuracy of
the developed tools was demonstrated by comparison with existing work in literature. The MATLAB tool
was used for a parametric study to determine the gas-gap thickness, coolant-gap thickness, coolant flow
rate, and coolant inlet temperatures for the testing of a 3/4-in. outer-diameter heat pipe up to powers of 10
kW and temperatures of 1,000°C. The effects of heat pipe and calorimeter inner-shell surface emissivities
were investigated, considering the inability to control or accurately measure surface emissivities in most
applications. It was found that controlling the He-Ar mole fractions provides a wide operating range
for gas-gap thicknesses around ∼ 0.042–0.090-in. (∼ 1.07–2.29 mm) for a surface emissivity range of
0.4–0.8 because conduction heat transfer is a significant fraction of the heat transfer across the gas-gap.
In addition, it was found that turbulent flow in the coolant gap is needed at high input powers to prevent
shell temperatures from approaching the boiling temperature of the water coolant. The parametric study
resulted in choosing stainless steel tubes with an outer diameter and thickness of 1 × 0.065 in. as the
inner shell, and a 1-1/2 × 0.156 in. as the outer shell of the calorimeter. Overall, this report presents the
necessary information for sizing and rating gas-gap calorimeters with He-Ar mixtures for the testing of
high-temperature heat pipes (∼ 500–1,000◦ C).

 Gas-Gap Calorimeter Sizing and Rating Tools
 for Heat Pipe Experimentation

 1. INTRODUCTION

 Gas-gap calorimeters are used in heat pipe experiments to obtain a variable thermal resistance between a
high-temperature heat pipe (400–1,200°C) and coolant fluid. This controllable thermal resistance enables
well-defined condenser conditions and the use of conventional coolants such as water to transfer heat to the
ultimate heat sink. The design of gas-gap calorimeters involves the proper sizing of parameters such as gas-gap
thickness, gas composition, and coolant flow rate based on the required heat pipe power throughput and
operating temperature. The Single Primary Heat Extraction and Removal Emulator (SPHERE) Facility at the
Idaho National Laboratory (INL) [2] requires the design of calorimeters for heat pipe testing that can enable
the desired operational conditions with minimal heat losses, while allowing high accuracy in calorimetry for
cooling-power calculations. This supports the facility’s purpose of addressing knowledge gaps in heat pipe
operational regimes and performance [3, 4]. Simple, robust, and user-friendly tools are thus required for the
sizing and rating of gas-gap calorimeters for heat pipe experimentation.
 A single-pass gas-gap calorimeter is composed of an inner and outer shell, as shown in Figure 1. The heat
pipe outer surface and inner shell serve as the boundaries of the gas-gap. A uniform gas-gap thickness can be
enforced by using spacers or a known-thickness wire wrapped around the heat pipe to prevent contact between
the heat pipe and the shell. Depending on the application, the gas gap could be sealed from the environment
with a different pressure than the ambient, or, as is the case for SPHERE, it could be open to the ambient if
the test article is placed in an environmental chamber.

 The radial heat transfer across the gas gap is primarily due to conduction and radiation for a static gas,
depending on the compositions and vacuum level. High vacuum in the gas gap results in only radiative heat
transfer that may not be sufficient for high heat pipe input powers. Controlling the radiation heat-transfer
efficiency is not possible in most cases because it depends on the surface temperatures and emissivities.

 1

However, the heat pipe operating temperature can be controlled at a given heating power by manipulating the
gas composition in the gap, such that the desired thermal resistance is obtained. The gas composition can also
be changed during startup to prevent the over-cooling of the heat pipe as temperatures rise to nominal levels.
The gas mixture usually includes helium due to its high thermal conductivity, mixed with an inert gas such as
argon [1, 5].
 The present report describes sizing and rating tools developed for gas-gap calorimeters for use in the
SPHERE facility at INL. Two tools with similar capabilities are developed employing Microsoft Excel and
MATLAB. The tools have the following two main functions: (1) finding heat pipe operating temperature
given the heat pipe and calorimeter geometries, power input, and gas compositions, and (2) finding He-Ar
composition given the heat pipe and calorimeter geometries, power input, and operating temperature.

 1.1. SPHERE Facility Description
 The present work is meant to enable sizing and rating of calorimeters for heat pipe testing at INL’s
SPHERE facility. SPHERE includes an environmental chamber designed to support experimental work under
a variety of conditions. The chamber is constructed from 1-in.-thick aluminum, with internal dimensions
of 2 ft tall, 3 ft wide, and 13 ft long. Aluminum was chosen to enable X-ray radiography through the two
thin-milled sections that are 5/16 in thick, enabling minimal attenuation. Eight 100 ISO-K flanges can be used
for instrumentation-signal and heate- power feedthroughs. The chamber has a primary door spanning its full
size for for loading and unloading test articles and three smaller doors on the opposite side for post-installation
access for instrumentation or inspection. The chamber can support vacuum up to 10−5 torr using an Agilent
DS 602 rotary-vane pump and an Agilent TwisTorr 305 FS turbo pump. The chamber can be back-filled with
inert gases such as nitrogen, argon, or helium to provide a controlled environment. Figure 2 showcase the
front and back views of the SPHERE chamber.

 The instrumentation used within the SPHERE system includes thermocouples, typically type-K or type-T,
resistance temperature detectors (RTDs) for temperature measurements, and strain gauges. Coolant flow-rate
measurements are conducted via an Adalet turbine flowmeter. The coolant used is water, supplied by a
chiller with an internal pump, ensuring stable and controlled cooling during experiments. The pressure of the
chamber is measured by a Granville-Phillips Micro-Ion ATM vacuum gauge.
 The heater control and data acquisition (DAQ) panels are National Instruments chassis and modules
integrated with LabView. Power is controlled via 6 Watlow Din-a-mites silicon controlled rectifier (SCR)
power controllers while the power drawn by the heaters is monitored by Ohio Semitronics PC5 watt transducers.
The cabinet operates with a 208 V power input and can supply up to 20 kW. The operator controls the system
using a LabVIEW VI interface, which can manage the system with either a power or a temperature setpoint
via proportional-integral-derivative (PID) controllers. PIDs enable varied temperature ramp rates during
startup and shutdown to prevent both localized dryout in the wick and large thermal stresses.

 The tools use a simple thermal-resistance network, as shown in Figure 4, where the heat pipe, gas gap,
shell, and coolant are modeled as lumped axially with uniform properties. The heat transfer from the heat pipe
to the inner shell wall is due to conduction and radiation heat transfer. Free convection is accommodated when
significant via an effective thermal conductivity, as described later in this report. Heat losses are neglected in
the calculations.

 A variety of assumptions are made to ensure the tools are fast and practical. These assumptions include:

 • Steady-state operation

 • Axially lumped heat pipe, gas-gap, shell, and coolant temperatures

 • Axially lumped fluid and solid properties, including surface emissivities

 • Coolant properties calculated at the mean temperature and pressure axially

 • Gas properties calculated at the average temperature radially, using the shell inner and heat pipe outer
 surface temperatures

 • Fully developed correlations are used for pressure drops and heat transfer

 • Nusselt-number correlation for flow in annulus with temperature boundary conditions

 • The effective heat transfer surface area represented by the active length of the heat pipe in the condenser

 • Minor losses neglected, and only friction pressure drop considered

 • Heat losses at the outer wall of the calorimeter and the adiabatic section neglected—i.e. the power
 input to the heat pipe equals the power output at the calorimeter

 2.5.3. Suboptions for MATLAB Code

 Three suboptions are available for the MATLAB code for vacuum (Suboption 1), static gas-gap (Suboption
 2), and flow in gas-gap (Suboption 3). The vacuum option simply takes the gas thermal conductivity as an
 input from the user. For high-vacuum the thermal conductivity, this could be set to a low number such as 10−6 ,
 which effectively neglects conduction. The static gas-gap and flow in gas-gap options consider conduction
 and convection, respectively. Suboption 3 for gas flow is not described explicitly in this report because current
 experimental capabilities do not include a gas loop that can accommodate such a parameter. Furthermore,
 turbulence needs to be induced for gas flow to be meaningful, otherwise the thermal resistance is comparable
 to conduction due to the small gas-gaps being considered.

 4.1. Gas-Gap Thickness
 Considerations in choosing a gas-gap thickness are mainly related to the operating temperature of the
heat pipe, the structural integrity of the calorimeter, and the ease of fabrication and instrumentation. From
operational experience, it is known that structural integrity can be seriously compromised if the inner shell
makes contact with the heat pipe at elevated temperatures. Therefore it is necessary to accommodate the
tolerances in standard tubing and fabrication processes in the design. This can be addressed by choosing
gas-gaps that are larger than 1 mm and using tubes with the necessary surface and axis straightness that keep
the gas-gaps within ±10% of their design values.
 Choosing the appropriate gas-gap thickness is important to achieve the desired operating-temperature
range. If a gas-gap thickness is smaller than required, the maximum operating temperature of the setup might
be lower than desired at a given power, even if the argon mole fraction is near 1. Essentially, it should be
possible to control the operating temperatures by manipulating the mole fractions to the extent sufficient for
the testing requirements. If high-vacuum is required to get to high temperatures, the input power at which that
temperature will be reached cannot be controlled due to pure radiation heat transfer.

4.1.1. Coupling Curves

 The start-up procedure must be considered because the heat pipe input power needs to be ramped up
gradually to the nominal operating range. During startup, the heat pipe follows the viscous and sonic-limit
curves as operating temperatures rise. The coupling curves in Figures 11 to 13 show these limit curves along
with the operating region of the heat pipe, shown as lines for different mole fractions as well as a for vacuum.
The limits were calculated for a reference heat pipe with evaporator, adiabatic, and condenser lengths of
1.0 m, 0.1 m, and 1.0 m, respectively. As the heat pipe operating temperature increases from near-ambient
conditions, the heat pipe will first follow the viscous and sonic-limit curves. Later, it will transition to the
normal operating range, where the heat pipe temperature is determined by the calorimeter-coupling curves. It
should be noted that these are ”steady-state” curves, representing where the heat pipe operating point will be
once the heating and cooling powers equalize.
 As can be seen from the coupling curves, a 1.52 mm gas-gap thickness is appropriate considering the
operating-temperature range at the different mole fractions, powers, and emissivities, spanning a large region
outside of the limit curves. This gas-gap thickness is also deemed appropriate considering the feasibility of

 4.2. Coolant-Gap Thickness and Flow Rate
 Coolant-gap thickness is another geometric parameter that must be determined for calorimeter design.
Because the coolant’s convection thermal resistance is considerably less than the thermal resistance of the gas
gap, coolant-channel thickness does not have a considerable impact on heat pipe operating temperatures under
most conditions. However, the coolant channel does have a significant impact on the shell temperatures, which
should be maintained at a temperature low enough to prevent boiling. Figure 14 shows shell temperatures at
different mass-flow rates and cooling powers for three different coolant-gap thicknesses. The sharp transition
in shell temperatures at around 0.09 kg/s coolant-flow rate is caused by the transition from laminar to turbulent
flow in the coolant gap. As can be seen, the coolant-gap thickness significantly affects shell temperatures,
particularly in the laminar range, whereas in the turbulent range, even the largest coolant gap is under the 80°C
threshold. This temperature threshold was chosen to have a sufficient buffer with the boiling temperature at

 24

ambient. However it should be considered that low coolant-flow rates result in better accuracy in calorimetry;
therefore, it may be desirable at lower powers to operate under the laminar regime such that the coolant-
temperature rise across the calorimeter is large compared to the thermocouple or RTD errors. It should
be noted that the results shown in Figure 14 assumed a constant calorimeter inlet pressure (of 4 bar ≈
60 psi) across the different mass-flow rates. The calculated pressure drops were low compared to this value
(∼ 0.1–2 kPa) because only friction pressure drop is considered in the calorimeter that is 1 m long. The
4 bar inlet pressure was determined as a nominal value based on the pump curves of the chiller. Based on
fabrication considerations, a coolant gap of 2.39 mm was deemed to be appropriate although 1.57 mm coolant
gap enables lower shell temperatures overall.

 5. CONCLUSIONS

 Gas-gap calorimeters are crucial for high-temperature heat pipe experimentation with water coolant
because they enable well-defined condenser conditions while allowing greater control over operating tempera-
tures compared to full-vacuum or ambient cooling setups. The present report described gas-gap calorimeter
sizing and rating tools using Microsoft Excel and MATLAB software using a simple thermal-resistance
network. Multiple options and sub-options are available to find operating temperatures or He-Ar compositions
given the required information. The accuracy of the developed tools was demonstrated against similar work
in literature. A parametric study was conducted to determine the gas-gap thickness, coolant-gap thickness,
coolant-flow rate and coolant-inlet temperature at different surface emissivities for a 3/4 in. (19.05 mm) OD
sodium heat pipe. Coupling curves were investigated to demonstrate the code’s use in determining testing
conditions. The findings of the analyses can be itemized as follows:

 • Standard tube sizes provide a sufficient variety of options for gas-gap calorimeters for heat pipes.

 • Controlling the mole fractions of the binary He-Ar mixture provides a wide heat pipe operating range
 for gas-gap thicknesses of ∼ 0.042–0.090 in. (∼ 1.07–2.29 mm) for a surface-emissivity range of
 0.4–0.8 for the heat pipe and inner shell.

 • The fraction of radiation heat transfer across the gas gap increases significantly with increasing argon
 fraction, from as low as ≈ 5% for an argon fraction of 0 to around 80% for an argon fraction of 1 for
 the geometries and emissivities considered.

 • Higher coolant-flow rates in the turbulent region are required at high input powers to keep inner-shell
 temperatures below 80◦ C.

 • Due to its influence on viscosity, coolant-inlet temperature affects the flow rate at which the coolant
 flow becomes turbulent and should be considered to keep inner-shell temperatures below 80◦ C.

 • The influence of coolant-flow rate on the heat pipe operating temperature is low unless the flow rate is
 increased past the laminar-to-turbulent transition. If the heat transfer across the gas gap is mainly due to
 conduction, the operating temperatures can change by around 50◦ C between laminar and turbulent flow.

 • Accurate estimation of surface emissivities are important to have accurate predictions of operating
 conditions, particularly for higher argon concentrations or operating temperatures where the fraction of
 heat transferred by radiation is high.

 • Even for large surface emissivities at the heat pipe and inner shell (≈ 0.9–1), full-vacuum operation for
 input powers larger that 5 kW will result in heat pipe temperatures above 900◦ C, indicating that having
 a He-Ar gas atmosphere is required for high input powers.

Based on the parametric study, a gas-gap thickness of 0.060 in. (1.52 mm), and a coolant gap thickness
if 0.094 in. (2.39 mm) were chosen for future tests in the SPHERE facility with 3/4 in. OD heat pipes.
Overall, this report contains all necessary information for the sizing or rating of gas-gap calorimeters for
high-temperature heat pipe testing.
