On the Counting Sequence of Z-convex Polyominoes
Luca Castelli Paolo Massazza

Abstract
The degree of convexity of a convex polyomino P is the smallest integer k such that any two cells of P can be joined by a monotone path inside P with at most k changes of direction. In this paper we present a set of formulas and equations that are the basis of a C++ program that allows you to compute the longest counting sequence known to date (with respect to the area) of convex polyominoes of degree of convexity at most 2.



1
Introduction

A polyomino is a geometrical figure consisting of a finite set of connected unitary squares (called cells) in the plane

\mathbb{Z}\times\mathbb{Z}
,
considered up to translations.
The problem of counting polyominoes with
n
cells (
i.e.
of area
n
) is probably one of the fundamental open problems in combinatorial geometry (see problem 37 in
[
10
]
). Recently, the problem has been solved up to

n\leq 70

[
2
]
.
Due to the difficulty of the problem, simpler classes of polyominoes have been introduced and studied. In particular, the class of convex polyominoes (polyominoes where the intersection with an infinite horizontal or vertical stripe is a finite segment) and some of its subclasses have been thoroughly investigated
[
3
,
4
,
15
,
6
]
.
Sometimes the generating function with respect to the area

\phi_{C}(x)=\sum_{n\geq 0}c_{n}x^{n}

of a class
C
of polyominoes is known, see for instance
[
14
]
.
This often allows one to get an estimate of the asymptotic growth of the number of polyominoes of area
n
in
C
using standard analytical methods.

When

\phi_{C}(x)

is not known (neither explicitly nor implicitly)
an algorithm for the exhaustive generation of
C
may produce
c_{n}
for small (but still significant) values of
n
. For instance, Constant Amortized Time (CAT) algorithms for generating several classes of polyominoes have been developed, where the exhaustive generation is done by semiperimeter
[
8
,
7
]
or by area
[
11
,
12
]
.

In this paper we consider the class
{\mathsf{ZConv}}
of Z-convex polyominoes. This class has been introduced in
[
9
]
and studied in
[
17
]
. It contains all convex polyominoes
P
with the property that any two cells of
P
can be joined by a path in
P
with at most two changes of direction.
Recently, a combinatorial approach
[
13
]
has been used to prove that Z-convex polyominoes can be counted with respect to the area in polynomial time. By slightly changing some of the combinatorial decompositions in
[
13
]
, we have obtained a new set of recurrence equations and formulas that can be implemented more efficiently. We developed a C++ program that allowed to compute
|{\mathsf{ZConv}}(n)|
up to
n=75
.

2
Basic Notions

Let
P
be a polyomino with an

r\times c

minimal bounding rectangle.
We number the columns (resp., rows) of
P
from left to right (resp., from bottom to top).
The first (resp., last) column of
P
is denoted by
\textsc{first}\,\!\!\left(P\right)

(resp.,
\textsc{last}\,\!\!\left(P\right)
).
Lastly, we indicate by
\text{A}\,\!\!\left(P\right)
the
area
of
P
, that is, the number of its cells.
We consider a
Z
-convex polyomino as the result of the concatenation of polyominoes belonging to well-known subclasses of convex polyominoes (stacks
{\mathsf{S}}
, rectangles
{\mathsf{T}}
, Ferrers diagrams
{\mathsf{F}}
, parallelograms
{\mathsf{C}}
).
We denote by
\text{low}\!\left(j\right)
(resp.,
\text{high}\!\left(j\right)
) the row index of the bottom cell (resp., top cell) of column
j
.
We introduce some binary relations on the set of columns of a convex polyomino, which play a special role in the decomposition of a Z-convex polyomino.

Definition 1

Let
i
and
j
be two columns of a convex polyomino
P
. We say that

•

i

includes

j
, denoted by

j\subseteq i
,
if and only if
\text{low}\!\left(i\right)\leq\text{low}\!\left(j\right)
and
\text{high}\!\left(i\right)\geq\text{high}\!\left(j\right)
,
see Fig.
1
(a);

•

i
and
j
are
overlapping
, denoted by

i\olap j
,
if and only if
\text{low}\!\left(j\right)<\text{low}\!\left(i\right)\leq\text{high}\!\left(j\right)<\text{high}\!\left(i\right)
or
\text{low}\!\left(i\right)<\text{low}\!\left(j\right)\leq\text{high}\!\left(i\right)<\text{high}\!\left(j\right)
, see Fig.
1
(b);

•

i
and
j
are
disjoint
, denoted by

i\asymp j
,
if and only if
\text{low}\!\left(i\right)>\text{high}\!\left(j\right)
or
\text{low}\!\left(j\right)>\text{high}\!\left(i\right)
, see Fig.
1
(c).

Figure 1:
An included column (a), two overlapping columns (b), two disjoint columns (c), a descending polyomino (d), an ascending polyomino (e) and a polyomino in
{\mathsf{LR}}
(f).

In the following, an index
∙
(resp.,
∘
) to the right of a class indicates that all polyominoes in the class have (resp., do not have) disjoint columns. Thus, for any class
{\mathsf{A}}
we have the partition

{\mathsf{A}}={\mathsf{A}}^{\bullet}\cup{\mathsf{A}}^{\circ}
.

We define the

center

\mathcal{C}(P)

of a convex polyomino
P
as the rightmost column
e
of
P
such that

c\subseteq e

for all columns
c
to the left of
e
. We also define the
pivot

\mathcal{P}(P)
of
P
as the leftmost column
j
of
P
such that

j\olap\mathcal{C}(P)

(possibly
\mathcal{P}(P)

does not exist, that is,
\mathcal{P}(P)=\epsilon
).
If
\mathcal{P}(P)\neq\epsilon

we say that
P
is
descending
(resp.,
ascending
) if

\text{low}\!\left(\mathcal{C}(P\right))>\text{low}\!\left(\mathcal{P}(P\right))

(resp.,

\text{low}\!\left(\mathcal{C}(P\right))<\text{low}\!\left(\mathcal{P}(P\right))
),
see Fig.
1
(d) (resp., (e)).
The set of descending (resp., ascending) convex polyominoes is indicated by
{\mathsf{DConv}}
(resp.,
{\mathsf{AConv}}
).
If

\mathcal{P}(P)=\epsilon

then
\mathcal{C}(P)
includes all columns of
P
. This means that

P
is in
{\mathsf{T}}\cup{\mathsf{F}}\cup{\mathsf{S}}\cup{\mathsf{LR}}

where
{\mathsf{LR}}
is the class containing all convex polyominoes that are the concatenation of two polyominoes,

P=P_{1}\cdot P_{2}
,
with

P_{1}\in{\mathsf{S}}\cup{\mathsf{F}}
,

P_{2}\in{\mathsf{T}}\cup{\mathsf{S}}\cup{\mathsf{F}}

and

\textsc{first}\,\!\!\left(P_{2}\right)\subsetneq\textsc{last}\,\!\!\left(P_{1}\right)
.
We indicate by

{\mathsf{DConv}}_{2}

(resp.,
{\mathsf{AConv}}_{2}
)
the set of descending (resp., ascending) polyominoes of degree of convexity 2.
Thus, one has

{\mathsf{ZConv}}={\mathsf{T}}\cup{\mathsf{S}}\cup{\mathsf{F}}\cup{\mathsf{LR}}\cup{\mathsf{AConv}}_{2}\cup{\mathsf{DConv}}_{2}

where the unions are disjoint. The counting problem for
{\mathsf{ZConv}}
is therefore reduced to computing

|{\mathsf{DConv}}_{2}(n)|

(by symmetry one has

|{\mathsf{DConv}}_{2}(n)|=|{\mathsf{AConv}}_{2}(n)|
), since the counting problem for
{\mathsf{T}}\cup{\mathsf{S}}\cup{\mathsf{F}}\cup{\mathsf{LR}}
is easily solved in polynomial time (see
[
13
, Sect. 4]
).
Lastly, we recall
[
5
, Thm. 1]
that
P\in{\mathsf{ZConv}}
if and only if for any two disjoint columns
i
and
j
of
P
there exists a column
k
, with

i<k<j
,
such that

i\subsetneq k

and

j\subsetneq k
.
This characterization is the basis of the combinatorial decompositions that lead to the formulas for computing

|{\mathsf{DConv}}_{2}(n)|
.

Polyominoes decomposition

We consider the
standard decomposition
of

P\in{\mathsf{DConv}}

as defined in
[

]
:

Definition 2

[standard decomposition]
A polyomino

P\in{\mathsf{DConv}}

is uniquely decomposed as

P=L\cdot F\cdot C\cdot R

for suitable polyominoes

L\in{\mathsf{S}}\cup{\mathsf{T}}\cup{\mathsf{F}}
,

F\in{\mathsf{F}}\cup{\mathsf{T}}\cup\{\epsilon\}
,

C\in{\mathsf{C}}\cup{\mathsf{T}}\cup{\mathsf{F}}
,
and

R\in{\mathsf{S}}\cup{\mathsf{T}}\cup{\mathsf{F}}\cup\{\epsilon\}

with

\textsc{last}\,\!\!\left(L\right)=\mathcal{C}(P)
,

\textsc{first}\,\!\!\left(C\right)=\mathcal{P}(P)

and

\text{low}\!\left(\textsc{last}\,\!\!\left(C\right)\right)<\text{low}\!\left(\textsc{first}\,\!\!\left(R\right)\right)
.

Note that any column
e
of
F
(if
F\neq\epsilon
) satisfies

\text{low}\!\left(\textsc{last}\,\!\!\left(L\right)\right)=\text{low}\!\left(e\right)

and that

\textsc{last}\,\!\!\left(F\right)\olap\textsc{first}\,\!\!\left(C\right)

or

\textsc{last}\,\!\!\left(F\right)\subsetneq\textsc{first}\,\!\!\left(C\right)
.
Furthermore,
if

one has

\textsc{first}\,\!\!\left(R\right)\subsetneq\textsc{last}\,\!\!\left(C\right)
.
We point out that by
[
5
, Thm. 1]
,
given

P\in{\mathsf{DConv}}_{2}

and its standard decomposition

P=L\cdot F\cdot C\cdot R
,
each column
c
of
P
to the right of
\textsc{last}\,\!\!\left(L\right)
satisfies the relation

c\olap\textsc{last}\,\!\!\left(L\right)

or

c\subsetneq\textsc{last}\,\!\!\left(L\right)
.

We indicate by
{\mathsf{LCR}}_{2}
(resp.,
{\mathsf{LC}}_{2}
,
{\mathsf{LFCR}}_{2}
,
{\mathsf{LFC}}_{2}
) the subset of
{\mathsf{DConv}}_{2}
containing polyominoes whose standard decomposition is

L\cdot C\cdot R

(resp.,
L\cdot C
,

L\cdot F\cdot C\cdot R
,
L\cdot F\cdot C
).
Polyominoes in
{\mathsf{DConv}}
will be recursively decomposed into polyominoes belonging to three particular subclasses, see Fig.
2
. The first one is the class
{\mathsf{Z}}_{2}
(slightly modified compared to that defined in

[

]
).

(
{\mathsf{Z}}_{2}
)

{\mathsf{Z}}_{2}
is the set of all
P
in
{\mathsf{LCR}}_{2}\cup{\mathsf{LC}}_{2}

such that: 1)
for all columns
l
to the left of
\mathcal{C}(P)
,
one has

\text{A}\,\!\!\left(l\right)<\text{A}\,\!\!\left(\mathcal{C}(P)\right)

and

;
2)

c\olap\mathcal{C}(P)

for all columns

of

(if

and are introduced to obtain simpler equations.

(

)

The set

\mbox{t-}{\mathsf{Z}}_{2}^{\bullet}

contains all

that can be written as

for suitable

,

,

E\in{\mathsf{S}}\cup{\mathsf{T}}\cup{\mathsf{F}}\cup\{\epsilon\}
,

such that:

\textsc{last}\,\!\!\left(L\right)=\mathcal{C}(P)
,

\textsc{first}\,\!\!\left(C\right)=\mathcal{P}(P)

and

\text{low}\!\left(\textsc{last}\,\!\!\left(C\right)\right)<\text{low}\!\left(\textsc{first}\,\!\!\left(E\right)\right)
(if

);

The set

that can be written as

,

and

E\in{\mathsf{S}}\cup{\mathsf{T}}\cup{\mathsf{F}}\cup\{\epsilon\}

such that:

•

\textsc{last}\,\!\!\left(L\right)=\mathcal{C}(P)
,

Computing

The first step is to refine the standard decomposition of

4
Implementation

We have developed a C++ program that exploits the formulas and the equations presented in the previous section to compute tables for

Z_{2}^{\bullet}(\mathbf{\alpha})
,

\mbox{t-}Z_{2}^{\bullet}(\mathbf{\alpha})

and

Z_{2}^{\circ}(\mathbf{\alpha})
.
More precisely, we used a dynamic hash table for

Z_{2}^{\bullet}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})

(resp.,
\mbox{t-}Z_{2}^{\bullet}(\mathbf{\alpha})
,

Z_{2}^{\circ}(\mathbf{\alpha})
),
with 64 bits keys encoding the eight parameters

n,h_{1},h_{2},h_{3},h_{4},\delta_{1},\delta_{2}

and
\delta_{3}
.
Each parameter takes up 8 bits,
hence the program only works for
n<256
. Furthermore, each table contains entries strictly greater than zero. The construction of the table exploits dynamic programming. This means that when you search for a key

\boldsymbol{\alpha}

in the table and you do not found it then

Z_{2}^{\bullet}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})=0
.
The size of the hash table for
n=65
is approximately
2^{32}
, compared to the size

2^{48}
of a table implemented via an 8-dimensional array (with
O(n^{8})
space complexity).
Each entry in a table is computed in polynomial time using one of the formulas in the previous section. The worst case is represented by formula (
3.1.1
), which leads to a time complexity of
O(n^{14})
(due to 14 nested summations). Actually, special care has been taken to identify necessary conditions on
\boldsymbol{\alpha}
for which

Z_{2}^{\bullet}(\boldsymbol{\alpha})\neq 0

(so avoiding unnecessary computations), as well as finding tight limits for summation indices.
This allowed to obtain the counting sequence of
{\mathsf{ZConv}}
up to
n=75
in a couple of days of computation. We point out that the sequence indicated in
[
13
]
turned out to be wrong due to a bug in the prototype program used to generate it. By using the new sequence, the conjecture for
|{\mathsf{ZConv}}(n)|

[
13
]
is corrected in

C\cdot\exp(\pi\sqrt{17n/6})/n^{3/2})
, for a suitable constant
C
.
Lastly, the sequence in Table 1 does not currently appear in OEIS
[
16
]
.
