Dynamic Wholesale Pricing under Censored-Demand Learning
Michalis Deligiannis, Marco Scarsini, Xavier Venel

Abstract
We study a finite-horizon dynamic wholesale-price contract between a manufacturer and a retailer, both of whom observe only sales, rather than the true demand. When the retailer stocks out, unmet demand is unobserved, so both parties update a common posterior over the demand distribution from sales data. Each period, the manufacturer sets the wholesale price, the retailer chooses an order quantity, and the public belief state is updated. We characterize MPE as functions of this public belief. Our main results are as follows: for Weibull demand, we extend the well-known scaling approach to this strategic learning setting, prove the existence of an equilibrium, and reduce computation to a standardized one-parameter recursion; for exponential demand, we show that the equilibrium is unique and computable via a simple backward recursion. Keywords: wholesale-price contracts; censored demand; Bayesian learning; Markov perfect equilibrium; dynamic Stackelberg game.



1
Introduction

In many retail and supply chain settings, firms observe sales rather than true demand.
When stockouts occur, unmet demand remains unobserved, resulting in censored data.
Frequent stockouts and short product life cycles further limit the value of historical data, leading firms to rely primarily on recent sales—data that imperfectly reflect underlying demand—for their operational decisions
(
Chuang and Kim, 2023
)
.

In this paper, we study a dynamic finite-horizon wholesale-price contract between a manufacturer and a retailer who face a random demand whose distribution depends on an unknown parameter.
The two agents update their beliefs about this parameter using censored demand observations.
The setting is motivated by two common features of contemporary practice.
First, in volatile markets, firms rely on short-term B2B agreements to maintain flexibility, which motivates modeling the relationship as a finite-horizon contract
(
Feng and Krishnan, 2022
,
Chestney, 2023
,
Floeck, 2023
)
.
Second, advances in digital infrastructure now allow manufacturers and retailers to share real-time
POS
(
POS
) data, so both parties observe the same sales information.
For example, Walmart’s Luminate platform provides manufacturers with granular, real-time sales visibility, which is routinely used to support forecasting and replenishment decisions
(
Souza, 2023
,
Silberstein, 2023
)
.
However, these systems record only realized sales and omit lost demand, meaning that both the manufacturer and the retailer learn from the same censored observations.

Although several studies have dealt with either supply chain contracts or censored demand, the interplay between contracts and learning from censored demand remains largely unexplored.
To address this gap, we model the interaction between the manufacturer and the retailer as an incomplete-information dynamic Stackelberg game in which, at each period, the manufacturer sets the wholesale price, the retailer responds by choosing an order quantity, and both parties update a shared belief about the unknown demand distribution.
This setting extends the classical “selling to the newsvendor” framework of
Lariviere and Porteus, 2001
by incorporating strategic dynamic learning from censored data.

Censored observations create analytical challenges because many standard demand families do not preserve conjugacy when only sales are observed.
To maintain tractability, we employ the newsvendor distribution family introduced by
Braden and Freimer, 1991
, which preserves conjugacy under Bayesian updating.
Using this framework, we adopt
MPE
(
MPE
) as the solution concept of our game.

Our main contributions are the following:

1.

Dynamic contracting with censored-demand learning.

We develop a dynamic Stackelberg game under a wholesale-price contract in which the manufacturer’s pricing and the retailer’s ordering decisions are jointly shaped by Bayesian learning from shared, censored data.

2.

Existence of an
MPE
.

For Weibull demand, we prove the existence of an
MPE
using a technique developed by
Lariviere and Porteus, 1999
in a non-strategic setting.
This technique scales the model so that the conjugate prior depends only on its shape hyperparameter and not on its rate hyperparameter, thereby reducing the dimensionality of the problem.
A byproduct of this technique is that, at equilibrium, the manufacturer’s wholesale price depends only on the initial belief and the number of uncensored demand observations.

3.

Uniqueness of the
MPE
.

When demand is exponentially distributed, the
MPE
is unique and can be computed by a simple backward recursion without solving an optimization problem in each period.

Section

Section

Section

Finally,

contains the proofs.

2
Literature Review

Our work relates to research on inventory management under censored demand and to dynamic wholesale-price contracts in supply chains.

A substantial literature in inventory management examines demand learning in settings where stockouts lead to censored sales. This line of research dates back to
Scarf, 1959
,
Scarf, 1960
, who shows that, when the demand is observable, the Bayesian dynamic program can be simplified through state-space reduction
(
Azoury, 1985
, see also)
.
Under censored demand, however, conjugacy fails for most standard distributions, and Bayesian updating becomes analytically difficult. To address this challenge,
Braden and Freimer, 1991
introduce the newsvendor family, which preserves conjugacy under censoring and enables tractable Bayesian inventory models in finite-horizon settings
(
Lariviere and Porteus, 1999
,
Bisi et al., 2011
,
Mersereau, 2015
,
Besbes et al., 2022
,
Chuang and Kim, 2023
,
Zhang et al., 2025
, see, e.g., )
.
Within this family,
Lariviere and Porteus, 1999
show that Weibull demand with a gamma prior admits a dimensionality-reduction result, which renders single-firm dynamic inventory problems tractable.
We build on this property and extend it from a single decision maker to a strategic two-tier supply chain.

Our paper builds on the “selling to the newsvendor” framework of
Lariviere and Porteus, 2001
, which studies wholesale-price contracts between a manufacturer and a retailer.
More broadly, supply chain contracting in this environment is reviewed by
Cachon, 2003
.

Several papers study multi-period wholesale pricing or repeated manufacturer–retailer interactions under a known demand distribution or exogenous information updates, and characterize subgame-perfect equilibria
(
Anand et al., 2008
,
Erhun et al., 2008
,
Martínez-de Albéniz and Simchi-Levi, 2013
,
Sheng et al., 2017
, e.g., )
.
Related work examines how exogenous forecast updates affect pricing and ordering decisions
(
Özer et al., 2007
, e.g., )
.
We refer to
Shen et al., 2019
for a broader review of supply chain contracting with information updating.
This stream of literature assumes that demand information is known or evolves exogenously, rather than being learned from censored demand observations.

Han, 2016
studies a two-period supplier–retailer Stackelberg newsvendor model with an ex ante constant wholesale price; learning under censored sales is made tractable via a binary Bernoulli–Beta demand model (myopic vs. forward-looking retailer), with a Weibull–Gamma extension treated numerically.

A related body of work studies dynamic contracting with information asymmetry about inventory and demand

(
Zhang et al., 2010
,
Lobel and Xiao, 2017
,
Bensoussan et al., 2025
, see, e.g., )
.

Kadiyala et al., 2020
study a long-term vendor-managed inventory arrangement in which the manufacturer observes the retailer’s
POS
and inventory data—which, as in our model, imply censored demand when stockouts occur—but the retailer also possesses additional private demand information; they propose a learn-and-screen mechanism that determines when the manufacturer should elicit this private information.
Recent work uses online learning and bandit methods to design dynamic price contracts and study regret
(
Cesa-Bianchi et al., 2023
,
Liu and Rong, 2024
,
Zhao et al., 2025
, see, e.g., )
.
Our model is a dynamic game with public incomplete information, where players have partial knowledge of the environment and update their beliefs through commonly observed outcomes.
For such games, two natural solution concepts exist: perfect Bayesian equilibrium
(
Cho and Kreps, 1987
,
Fudenberg and Tirole, 1991
)
and sequential equilibrium
(
Kreps and Wilson, 1982
)
.
Formally, a sequential equilibrium consists of a strategy profile and a belief system specified after every history, such
that strategies are sequentially rational, given beliefs, and beliefs are consistent with the strategy profile and Bayes’ rule,
whenever applicable.
These concepts may become cumbersome because strategies may in general depend on the whole history.
This issue can be overcome in models with evolving state variables, where attention can be restricted to Markov strategies, which depend only on the current state variables.
A standard solution concept for these games is
MPE

(
Maskin and Tirole, 1988a
,
Maskin and Tirole, 1988b
,
Maskin and Tirole, 1989
,
Maskin and Tirole, 2001
)
.
In games with incomplete information and a common belief, a standard approach is to take the common belief as the state variable.
See, for instance, games of experimentation
(
Keller et al., 2005
,
Marlats and Ménager, 2021
)
, reputation games
(
Board and Meyer-ter Vehn, 2013
)
, oligopoly with incomplete information
(
Bonatti et al., 2017
,
Cisternas, 2018
)
, or entry games with asymmetric information
(
Gryglewicz and Kolb, 2025
)
.

3
Problem Formulation

We consider a dynamic wholesale-price contract between a manufacturer
M

and a retailer
R
.
At time
0
, the two parties sign a finite-horizon contract that specifies the following:
In each period, the manufacturer first chooses a per-unit wholesale price.
After observing
this price, the retailer chooses an order quantity of a perishable good, to meet random customer demand.
The manufacturer has the obligation to produce and deliver the quantity ordered by the retailer.
The contract does not commit either party to future prices or quantities; rather, it defines the rules of interaction governing these decisions.
Inventory is not carried over across periods.
If the customers’ demand exceeds the order quantity in a given period,
unmet demand is not observed and not backlogged.
The retailer shares sales data with the manufacturer, so both parties observe only realized
sales rather than the full demand realization.

We formally define the environment of our pricing problem as follows:

Definition 3.1
.

The
environment
of our pricing problem is a tuple

\Psi\coloneqq(\Theta,\phi,(F(\,\cdot\,\mid\theta))_{\theta},p,c,T,W,Q),

(1)

where
\Theta
is an unknown parameter in
\mathbb{R}_{++}\coloneqq(0,\infty)
with common prior density
\phi
,

F(\,\cdot\,\mid\theta)
is the conditional demand distribution function given
\Theta=\theta
,

p
is the exogenous retail price,

c
is the manufacturer’s per-unit production cost,
T
is the time horizon,

W\subset\mathbb{R}_{++}
, with
[c,p]\subset W
, is the set of feasible wholesale prices, and
Q=\mathbb{R}_{+}\coloneqq[0,\infty)
is the set of feasible order quantities.

Throughout the paper we will assume
p>c
.
The environment
\Psi
is common knowledge to both agents.
Given this environment, we define a dynamic game describing the interaction between the manufacturer and the retailer.
The game unfolds as follows:
At the beginning, an unknown parameter
\Theta>0
is drawn from the distribution
\phi
.
In each period
t=1,\dots,T
, the manufacturer sets a wholesale price
w_{t}\in W
;
the retailer observes this price and orders a quantity
q_{t}\in Q
;
the manufacturer produces and delivers the ordered quantity
q_{t}
.

The demands
(D_{t})_{1\leq t\leq T}
are conditionally i.i.d. given
\Theta
.
The retailer sells
S_{t}=\min(q_{t},D_{t})
units at price
p
, with any unmet demand lost and unobserved.
Both parties observe the realized sales
s_{t}
, i.e., the realized demand
d_{t}
, if
d_{t}<q_{t}
, or the order quantity
q_{t}
, if
d_{t}\geq q_{t}
.

For
t\in\left\{2,\dots,T+1\right\}
, at the beginning of period
t
the
public history
is
h_{t}\coloneqq(w_{i},q_{i},s_{i})_{1\leq i\leq t-1}
, and
h_{1}\coloneqq\varnothing
.
We use the symbol
h_{t}^{+}
to indicate the history immediately after the manufacturer has chosen the wholesale price
w_{t}
at period
t
; that is,

h_{t}^{+}\coloneqq\left(h_{t},w_{t}\right)
.
We call
\mathcal{H}_{t}
the set of all histories
h_{t}
and
\mathcal{H}_{t}^{+}
the set of all histories
h_{t}^{+}
.
The manufacturer’s and retailer’s profits along history
h_{T+1}
are, respectively,

\Lambda^{\textup{M}}\left(h_{T+1}\right)\coloneqq\sum_{t=1}^{T}(w_{t}-c)q_{t}\quad\text{and}\quad\Lambda^{\textup{R}}\left(h_{T+1}\right)\coloneqq\sum_{t=1}^{T}p\,s_{t}-w_{t}q_{t}.

(2)

A
manufacturer’s strategy
is a sequence of maps
\sigma^{\textup{M}}\coloneqq\left(\sigma^{\textup{M}}_{t}\right)_{1\leq t\leq T}
such that
\sigma^{\textup{M}}_{t}\colon\mathcal{H}_{t}\to W
.
A
retailer’s strategy
is a sequence of maps
\sigma^{\textup{R}}\coloneqq\left(\sigma^{\textup{R}}_{t}\right)_{1\leq t\leq T}
such that
\sigma^{\textup{R}}_{t}\colon\mathcal{H}_{t}^{+}\to Q
.
We use the symbol
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
to indicate the strategy profile
\left(\sigma^{\textup{M}},\sigma^{\textup{R}}\right)
, and we call
\Sigma^{\textup{M}},\Sigma^{\textup{R}}
the set of manufacturer’s and retailer’s strategies, respectively.

The strategy profile
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
affects the distribution of sales, because of censoring.
With an abuse of notation we use the symbol
\Prob_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}
to indicate the probability on
\mathbb{R}_{++}\times\mathcal{H}_{T+1}
induced by
F,\phi
, and
\boldsymbol{\sigma}
, and we define

\begin{split}L^{\textup{M}}(\sigma^{\textup{M}},\sigma^{\textup{R}})&\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}_{++}\times\mathcal{H}_{T+1}}\Lambda^{\textup{M}}\left(h_{T+1}\right)\ \textup{d}\Prob_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\theta,h_{T+1}),\\
L^{\textup{R}}(\sigma^{\textup{M}},\sigma^{\textup{R}})&\coloneqq\int_{\mathbb{R}_{++}\times\mathcal{H}_{T+1}}\Lambda^{\textup{R}}\left(h_{T+1}\right)\ \textup{d}\Prob_{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}(\theta,h_{T+1}).\end{split}

(3)

Definition 3.2
.

The
dynamic wholesale-price game

\mathcal{G}_{\Psi}
induced by the environment
\Psi
is the tuple

\mathcal{G}_{\Psi}\coloneqq\left(\left(\textup{M},\textup{R}\right),\left(\Sigma^{\textup{M}},\Sigma^{\textup{R}}\right),\left(L^{\textup{M}},L^{\textup{R}}\right),\Psi\right).

(4)

In our model, all actions and sales observations are public; hence, after any public history
h_{t}
, both the manufacturer and the retailer have the same posterior belief about
\Theta
, which can be updated using Bayes’ rule.
For the sake of tractability, we assume a parametric demand family and a conjugate prior so that this common posterior admits a finite-dimensional
representation.

More specifically, following the Bayesian inventory literature
(
Lariviere and Porteus, 1999
,
Bisi et al., 2011
,
Mersereau, 2015
,
Besbes et al., 2022
,
Chuang and Kim, 2023

F(y\mid\theta)\coloneqq\Prob(D_{t}\leq y\mid\Theta=\theta)=1-e^{-\theta\ell(y)},\ \text{ for }y\geq 0,\ \theta>0,

(5)

where
\ell\colon[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)

\lim_{y\searrow 0}\ell(y)=0

\lim_{y\to\infty}\ell(y)=\infty

An example of a newsvendor distribution is the Weibull distribution, where
\ell(y)=y^{k}
, with

For
k=1

We assume a conjugate prior density

\phi(\theta\mid a,b)\coloneqq\frac{b^{a}\theta^{a-1}e^{-b\theta}}{\Gamma(a)}\ \text{ for }\theta>0,\ a>0,\ b>0,

The initial predictive distribution function of the demand, denoted by

6

G(y\mid a,b)\coloneqq\Prob(D_{t}\leq y)=1-\left(\frac{b}{b+\ell(y)}\right)^{a}\quad\text{ for }y\geq 0,\ a>0,\ b>0.

(7)

Moreover,
\overline{G}(\,\cdot\,\mid a,b)=1-G(\,\cdot\,\mid a,b)
denotes the survival function of the predictive demand.
The posterior distribution of
\Theta
and the predictive distribution of
D_{t}
are updated in a Bayesian manner that accounts for demand censoring

Braden and Freimer, 1991

.
Given the choice of the likelihood (
5
), and of the conjugate prior (

We start at time

a_{1}
and
b_{1}

For every history

\displaystyle a_{t+1}

\displaystyle=a_{t}+\mathds{1}_{\{{s_{t}}<q_{t}\}}=a_{1}+n_{t}

(8)

\displaystyle b_{t+1}

\displaystyle=b_{t}+\ell(s_{t})=b_{1}+\sum_{j=1}^{t}{\ell(s_{j})}\quad\text{for }t=1,\ldots,T-1,

(9)

where
\mathds{1}_{\{\,\cdot\,\}}
is the indicator function,

denotes the number of uncensored (exact-demand) observations up to period

is the realized sale at time

Under a newsvendor distribution, the posterior at period

Note that the sequences

a_{t}\in A\coloneqq\left\{a_{1}+n\colon n\in\mathbb{N}\right\}

b_{t}\in B\coloneqq[b_{1},\infty)

From now on, with an abuse of notation, we use the same symbol
\Psi
to indicate the environment together with the conditional demand distribution (
5

6
) (the symbol
\mathcal{G}_{\Psi}

We assume that the manufacturer’s decision in period

the retailer’s decision depends on

That is, a manufacturer’s Markov strategy is a sequence

We now follow the approach of

\Phi(a_{t},b_{t},q_{t},d_{t})=\left(a_{t}+\mathds{1}_{\{{s_{t}}<q_{t}\}},b_{t}+\ell(s_{t})\right),\text{ with }s_{t}=\min\{d_{t},q_{t}\}.

Definition 3.3
.

Fix initial hyperparameters
(a_{1},b_{1})

A pair of Markov strategies

(i)

(ii)

for every

\begin{split}\widehat{V}^{\textup{R}}_{t}(a,b,w)&=\max_{q\in Q}\left\{\Pi^{\textup{R}}(w,q\mid a,b)+\Expect\left[V^{\textup{R}}_{t+1}\bigl(\Phi(a,b,q,D)\bigr)\mid a,b\right]\right\},\\

(iii)

4
Existence of Equilibrium and Dimensionality Reduction

As discussed earlier, Weibull demand is common in the literature.
Building on earlier insights,
Lariviere and Porteus, 1999
show that a dimensionality-reduction technique can be used to analyze a single-agent newsvendor problem by representing beliefs through the shape hyperparameter
a
alone, thereby yielding a scalable dynamic program.
In this section, we extend this idea to our strategic setting.
We construct a standardized newsvendor model using dimensionality reduction, and show that its solution concept can be used to obtain an
MPE
of the original game.
This approach offers two advantages:

(i)

it simplifies the computation of the resulting
MPE
;

(ii)

it enables us to establish an existence result for the equilibrium.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first application of dimensionality reduction to prove the existence of an equilibrium in a strategic newsvendor context.
Moreover, solving
Eqs.

13
and
14
via backward induction is intractable because the state space grows rapidly.
The dimensionality reduction overcomes this challenge by leveraging the structural properties of the Weibull demand distribution.

From now on, we assume
a_{1}=\max(1,1/k)
.
Let
\tilde{Q}=Q=\mathbb{R}_{+}
.
If the conditional demand distribution
F
is Weibull and the prior
\phi
is conjugate with parameter
b=1
, then the predictive demand distribution function in
Eq.

7
has the
standardized
form:

G(y\mid a,1)=1-\left(\frac{1}{1+y^{k}}\right)^{a}\quad\text{for }y\geq 0,\ a\in A.

(16)

For
w\in W
,
\tilde{q}\in\tilde{Q}
,
a\in A
, the
retailer’s standardized per-period expected profit
is

\pi^{\textup{R}}(w,\tilde{q}\mid a)=(a-1)\left(p\Expect\left[\min(D,\tilde{q})\mid a,1\right]-w\tilde{q}\right),

(17)

and the
manufacturer’s standardized per-period profit
is

\pi^{\textup{M}}(w,\tilde{q}\mid a)=(a-1)\left(w-c\right)\tilde{q}.

(18)

We now define a solution concept for the environment
\Psi
, which will be called
SMPS

(
SMPS
).
A
manufacturer’s standardized Markov strategy
is a sequence
\left(w_{t}\right)_{1\leq t\leq T}
with
w_{t}\colon A\to W
.
A
retailer’s standardized Markov strategy
is a sequence
\left(\tilde{q}_{t}\right)_{1\leq t\leq T}
with
\tilde{q}_{t}\colon A\times W\to\tilde{Q}
.

The next definition requires three sequences of functions
\left(v^{\textup{R}}_{t},\widehat{v}^{\textup{R}}_{t},v^{\textup{M}}_{t}\right)_{t}
, with

,

\widehat{v}^{\textup{R}}_{t}\colon A\times W\to\mathbb{R}
, and

For the sake of convenience, given

Definition 4.1
.

Fix an initial hyperparameter
a_{1}
and a horizon

.
A pair of standardized Markov strategies
\left(w_{t}^{*},\tilde{q}_{t}^{*}\right)_{1\leq t\leq T}
is an
SMPS
(

) of
\Psi
if there exist three sequences of standardized value functions
\left(v^{\textup{R}}_{t},\widehat{v}^{\textup{R}}_{t},v^{\textup{M}}_{t}\right)_{1\leq t\leq T+1}
such that, for every

, the following conditions hold:

(i)

v^{\textup{R}}_{T+1}(a)=\widehat{v}^{\textup{R}}_{T+1}(a,w)=v^{\textup{M}}_{T+1}(a)=0
;

(ii)

for every
t\in\left\{1,\dots,T\right\}
,

\begin{split}\widehat{v}^{\textup{R}}_{t}(a,w)&=\max_{\tilde{q}\in\tilde{Q}}\left\{\pi^{\textup{R}}(w,\tilde{q}\mid a)+\Expectgen\left[v^{\textup{R}}_{t+1},\tilde{q}\mid a\right]\right\},\\
\tilde{q}_{t}^{*}(a,w)&\in\argmax_{\tilde{q}\in\tilde{Q}}\left\{\pi^{\textup{R}}(w,\tilde{q}\mid a)+\Expectgen\left[v^{\textup{R}}_{t+1},\tilde{q}\mid a\right]\right\};\end{split}

(20)

(iii)

for every
t\in\left\{1,\dots,T\right\}
,

\begin{split}v^{\textup{M}}_{t}(a)&=\max_{w\in W}\left\{\pi^{\textup{M}}(w,\tilde{q}^{*}_{t}(a,w)\mid a)+\Expectgen\left[v^{\textup{M}}_{t+1},\tilde{q}_{t}^{*}(a,w)\mid a\right]\right\},\\

(21)

(iv)

for every

v^{\textup{R}}_{t}(a)=\widehat{v}^{\textup{R}}_{t}(a,w_{t}^{*}(a)).

(22)

In the sequel, with an abuse of notation, we will use the symbol

\tilde{q}_{t}^{*}(a)\coloneqq\tilde{q}_{t}^{*}(a,w_{t}^{*}(a)),\ \text{ for all }t\leq T,\text{ and }a\in A.

6
Open Problems and Extensions

Research in operations management has examined decision-making under censored demand using Bayesian learning, but mostly in single-agent settings where only the retailer learns from sales data, leaving the manufacturer’s role largely unexplored.
This paper contributes to this stream of literature by analyzing a supply chain in which a manufacturer and a retailer jointly learn from shared
POS
data.
We view our model as a benchmark for studying dynamic contracting when learning is driven by stockout-censored sales.
Future research could explore alternative contract forms (such as buyback or revenue-sharing), environments with asymmetric information reflecting differences in data access or bargaining power, and models with inventory carryover, where unsold stock shapes subsequent learning.
The study of channel efficiency and information sharing is also valuable.
Extending the analysis to an infinite-horizon setting or alternative demand models may further clarify long-run dynamics and equilibrium behavior.

8
Berge’s Theorems

For the reader’s convenience, we briefly restate two key results from
(
Berge, 1959
, chapter VI, section 3)
, which we used to prove

Theorem

4.2
.
We state these results following the style of
Aliprantis and Border, 2006
.

Given a correspondence
\zeta
, the symbol
\Gr(\zeta)
denotes its graph.

Lemma 8.1

(
Aliprantis and Border, 2006
)
.

Let
\zeta\colon W\rightrightarrows\tilde{Q}
be an upper hemicontinuous correspondence between topological spaces with nonempty compact values, and let

U\colon\Gr(\zeta)\to\mathbb{R}

be an upper semicontinuous objective function.
Define the function
v\colon W\to\mathbb{R}
by

v(w)=\max_{\tilde{q}\in\zeta(w)}U(w,\tilde{q}).

Then the function
v
is upper semicontinuous.

Theorem 8.2

(
Aliprantis and Border, 2006
)
.

Let
\Xi\colon W\rightrightarrows\tilde{Q}
be a continuous correspondence between topological spaces with nonempty compact values, and let
U\colon\Gr(\Xi)\to\mathbb{R}

be a continuous objective function.
Define the
value function

v\colon W\to\mathbb{R}
by

v(w)=\max_{\tilde{q}\in\Xi(w)}U(w,\tilde{q}),

and the correspondence of maximizers
\zeta\colon W\rightrightarrows\tilde{Q}
by

\zeta(w)=\left\{\tilde{q}\in\Xi(w)\colon U(w,\tilde{q})=v(w)\right\}.

Then:

1.

The value function
v
is continuous.

2.

The “argmax” correspondence
\zeta
has nonempty compact values.

3.

If either
U
has a continuous extension to all of
W\times\tilde{Q}
, or if
\tilde{Q}
is Hausdorff, then the “argmax” correspondence
\zeta
is upper hemicontinuous.
