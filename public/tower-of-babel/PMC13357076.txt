The role of targeted therapy in the management of colorectal cancer liver metastases: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Ming Yan, Rixing Bai, Wenmao Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundColorectal cancer liver metastases (CRLM) are a leading cause of mortality in colorectal cancer (CRC) patients. Targeted therapy is widely used, but its precise impact on survival, tumor response, and resectability remains unclear. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the efficacy and safety of targeted therapy in CRLM patients through a meta-analysis.MethodsWe searched PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library up to July 2025. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and cohort studies evaluating targeted therapy in CRLM were included. Primary outcomes were overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS); secondary outcomes included objective response rate (ORR), disease control rate (DCR), conversion to resection, and adverse events (AEs). Meta-analyses were performed using random- or fixed-effects models. Risk of bias was assessed with Cochrane RoB 2.0 and Newcastle–Ottawa Scale; evidence certainty was evaluated using Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE).ResultsThirty-four studies involving 12,084 patients were included. Targeted therapy significantly improved OS [hazard ratio (HR) =0.82, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.76–0.89] and PFS (HR =0.74, 95% CI: 0.68–0.80). ORR and DCR were also higher [ORR, risk ratio (RR) =1.36, 95% CI: 1.20–1.54; DCR, RR =1.22, 95% CI: 1.10–1.35]. Conversion to resection increased (RR =1.45, 95% CI: 1.18–1.79), particularly in RAS wild-type, left-sided tumors. Grade ≥3 AEs were more frequent but manageable. Benefits were most pronounced in first-line and biomarker-selected populations.ConclusionsTargeted therapy improves survival, tumor response, and resectability in CRLM, especially in biomarker-selected subgroups. These findings support its integration into precision-based multidisciplinary management.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most commonly diagnosed malignancy and the second leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide (1,2). This manuscript focuses specifically on metastatic CRC (mCRC) with microsatellite stability (MSS) and proficient mismatch repair (pMMR), which represents the predominant molecular subtype in metastatic disease and is biologically distinct from microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) or mismatch repair-deficient (dMMR) tumors. MSS/pMMR mCRC exhibits different tumor biology, immune microenvironment characteristics, and therapeutic responsiveness compared with MSI-H/dMMR disease, and remains the primary setting in which targeted therapies such as anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) and anti-epidermal growth factor receptor (anti-EGFR) agents are used in combination with chemotherapy.

The liver is the most frequent site of distant metastasis, and nearly 50% of CRC patients eventually develop CRC liver metastases (CRLM) (3,4). The prognosis of CRLM remains poor, with a 5-year survival rate of less than 15% in patients receiving palliative treatment (5,6). Surgical resection offers the only potential cure; however, only 20–30% of patients are initially resectable, and recurrence rates after surgery remain high (7,8). Consequently, systemic therapy plays a pivotal role in the management of CRLM, both as a means of prolonging survival and as a potential bridge to resection in patients with initially unresectable disease (9,10).

Over the past two decades, advances in systemic therapy have reshaped the treatment landscape of CRLM. Conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy, particularly oxaliplatin- or irinotecan-based doublets, provides meaningful tumor control but limited survival benefit when used alone (11-13). The advent of molecularly targeted agents has significantly expanded therapeutic options. Monoclonal antibodies against EGFR and VEGF have demonstrated clinical benefit in mCRC, and their integration into combination regimens has been associated with improved tumor response, higher resection rates, and prolonged survival (14-17). Furthermore, molecular biomarkers such as RAS and BRAF mutations, as well as primary tumor sidedness, have emerged as critical determinants of therapeutic efficacy, underscoring the shift toward precision oncology in CRLM management (4-6,8,9).

Despite widespread adoption of targeted therapy, important uncertainties remain. Clinical trials and real-world studies have reported heterogeneous results regarding its impact on overall survival (OS), progression-free survival (PFS), response rates, and conversion to resection (10-12). Moreover, the toxicity profiles of these agents and their cost-effectiveness pose challenges in routine practice. While individual randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have provided valuable insights, their limited sample sizes and variations in patient selection, treatment regimens, and endpoints complicate the interpretation of results. Thus, there is a compelling need for a comprehensive synthesis of the available evidence to clarify the role of targeted therapy in CRLM.

In this context, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the efficacy and safety of targeted therapy in patients with CRLM. Specifically, we assessed its impact on survival outcomes, tumor response, and conversion to resectability, as well as treatment-related adverse events (AEs). By integrating evidence across diverse study designs and patient populations, this work seeks to refine patient selection, inform precision-based multidisciplinary strategies, and ultimately optimize outcomes for patients with CRLM. In particular, variability in resectability status among CRLM patients represents an important but often underreported source of heterogeneity that may influence treatment outcomes. We present this article in accordance with the PRISMA reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1-0418/rc).

Methods

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library from inception to July 2025. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms related to CRLMs and targeted therapy. Key words included “colorectal cancer”, “liver metastases”, “hepatic metastases”, “targeted therapy”, “EGFR inhibitor”, “VEGF inhibitor”, “bevacizumab”, “cetuximab”, “panitumumab”, “regorafenib”, and “systemic therapy.” Boolean operators (“AND”, “OR”) were applied to combine terms appropriately. No language restrictions were applied. Additionally, reference lists of relevant reviews and included studies were screened to identify additional eligible articles (18-20). The study protocol was not prospectively registered in the PROSPERO database, as the review had already progressed beyond the initial screening stage at the time when registration was considered. Nevertheless, all steps of the review were conducted following established methodological standards, including the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions, to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

The included studies encompassed heterogeneous patient populations with respect to liver metastasis resectability status. Specifically, patients could be classified as having initially resectable, potentially resectable (conversion candidates), or unresectable CRLMs, depending on tumor burden, anatomical considerations, and institutional multidisciplinary assessment. Given the lack of standardized definitions and inconsistent reporting of resectability criteria across studies, we did not apply strict stratification based on resectability status. Instead, variables such as line of therapy (first-line vs. later-line) and reported conversion to resection were used as surrogate indicators of treatment intent and disease stage.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

Meta-analyses were performed using Review Manager 5.4 and Stata 17.0. HRs were pooled for time-to-event outcomes (OS, PFS), while RRs were used for dichotomous outcomes (ORR, DCR, resection, AEs) (23-26).

Heterogeneity among studies was assessed using the Cochran’s Q test (P<0.10 considered significant) and quantified by the I2 statistic (24):

Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding one study at a time to assess the robustness of the results. Publication bias was evaluated visually with funnel plots and quantitatively using Egger’s regression test (P<0.05 indicating potential bias) (25).

Prespecified subgroup analyses were conducted according to line of therapy (first-line vs. later-line) and class of targeted agent (anti-VEGF, anti-EGFR, and multi-kinase inhibitors), as these variables reflect differences in disease burden, prior treatment exposure, biomarker-driven indications, mechanisms of action, and clinical use across distinct treatment settings.

Certainty of evidence

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach was used to rate the certainty of evidence for each outcome as high, moderate, low, or very low, considering risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias (27).

Results

Study selection

A total of 3,142 records were identified through electronic database searches and an additional 24 records through manual reference screening. After removing duplicates, 2,500 unique articles remained for title and abstract screening. Of these, 2,180 articles were excluded as irrelevant. The full texts of 320 articles were assessed for eligibility. Following detailed evaluation, 286 articles were excluded for the following reasons: (I) not specific to CRLMs (n=102); (II) insufficient data for quantitative synthesis (n=85); (III) duplicate or overlapping cohorts (n=61); and (IV) non-original articles such as reviews or conference abstracts (n=38) (28,29). Ultimately, 34 studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into the systematic review and meta-analysis. Study characteristics

The 34 included studies comprised 18 RCTs and 16 observational cohort studies, involving a total of 12,084 patients with CRLMs. The studies were published between 2005 and 2025 and were conducted across multiple regions, including Europe (n=15), Asia (n=10), North America (n=7), and multinational collaborations (n=2) (30). Sample size ranged from 78 to 1,492 participants. Median patient age ranged from 54 to 68 years, with a balanced sex distribution. Molecular profiling was reported in 28 studies: RAS mutation status was available in 24 studies, BRAF mutation status in 12 studies, and tumor sidedness in 15 studies.

Interventions included anti-VEGF therapy (bevacizumab, n=20 studies), anti-EGFR therapy (cetuximab or panitumumab, n=12 studies), and multi-kinase inhibitors (regorafenib or others, n=2 studies). Twenty-three studies evaluated targeted therapy in the first-line setting, while eleven studies assessed its role in later-line therapy.

Comparators included standard chemotherapy regimens such as FOLFOX, FOLFIRI, or XELOX, with or without targeted agents.

Outcomes: OS and PFS were reported in 28 and 26 studies, respectively; ORR and DCR in 25 and 19 studies; conversion to resection in 17 studies; and treatment-related AEs in 30 studies.

Most studies investigated anti-VEGF therapy (bevacizumab) or anti-EGFR therapy (cetuximab, panitumumab) in combination with standard chemotherapy regimens (FOLFOX, FOLFIRI, or XELOX). A smaller number of studies evaluated multi-kinase inhibitors (e.g., regorafenib) or newer agents in the refractory setting.

Molecular profiling was variably reported: KRAS mutation status was documented in 24 studies, BRAF mutation status in 12 studies, and tumor sidedness in 15 studies. These molecular features were frequently used to define subgroups or guide targeted therapy allocation, particularly in later-phase RCTs.

In terms of outcomes, OS and PFS were the most frequently reported endpoints (28 and 26 studies, respectively). Tumor response outcomes (ORR and DCR) were available in 25 and 19 studies, while conversion to resection was specifically evaluated in 17 studies, especially in trials such as CELIM and EPOC. Safety outcomes (any grade and grade ≥3 AEs) were reported in 30 studies, enabling pooled safety analyses (10,31-33).

Overall, the included studies represented a broad spectrum of clinical settings, ranging from first-line therapy in chemotherapy-naïve patients to salvage treatment in refractory disease, and covered both biomarker-selected and unselected populations, providing a comprehensive evidence base for meta-analysis (34).

In terms of surgical eligibility, the included studies represented a broad clinical spectrum, ranging from patients with initially unresectable disease undergoing systemic therapy with conversion intent, to those with potentially resectable disease receiving neoadjuvant treatment, and a smaller proportion with upfront resectable disease treated in the perioperative setting. However, explicit classification of resectability status was inconsistently reported across studies.

Survival outcomes

Twenty-eight studies involving 10,462 patients reported HRs for OS. Pooled analysis demonstrated that targeted therapy significantly improved OS compared with standard chemotherapy or best supportive care (HR =0.82, 95% CI: 0.76–0.89, P<0.001; I2=38%) (Figure 2) (35).

Subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent OS benefits across treatment lines; however, the magnitude of benefit differed between groups. Patients receiving first-line therapy experienced a greater survival advantage (HR =0.79, 95% CI: 0.72–0.87) compared with those treated in later-line settings (HR =0.87, 95% CI: 0.78–0.97), consistent with the more favorable tumor biology and higher treatment sensitivity observed in treatment-naïve populations.

Further stratification by targeted therapy class revealed heterogeneous treatment effects. Anti-EGFR therapies (cetuximab and panitumumab) were associated with more pronounced improvements in progression-free survival, particularly in patients with RAS wild-type and left-sided tumors. In contrast, anti-VEGF therapy (bevacizumab) demonstrated consistent but comparatively modest benefit across broader patient populations, irrespective of molecular status. Multi-kinase inhibitors such as regorafenib, which were primarily evaluated in later-line settings, showed limited survival benefit, reflecting their use in refractory disease.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis of 34 studies involving 12,084 patients provides a comprehensive evaluation of targeted therapy in CRLMs. Our findings demonstrate that targeted therapy significantly improves OS and PFS, enhances tumor response, and increases the likelihood of conversion to surgical resection, particularly in biomarker-selected subgroups (36-38). These results reinforce the central role of targeted therapy in multidisciplinary management and highlight the importance of precision oncology in optimizing outcomes for patients with mCRC and CRLM (39,40).

The pooled analysis showed robust survival benefits, with an HR of 0.82 for OS and 0.74 for PFS. The greatest benefits were observed in first-line treatment, RAS wild-type tumors, and left-sided primary tumors (41,42). Anti-EGFR therapies (cetuximab and panitumumab) inhibit the RAS-MAPK signaling pathway, thereby reducing cellular proliferation and promoting apoptosis. In contrast, anti-VEGF agents (bevacizumab) suppress tumor angiogenesis, decrease tumor perfusion, and modulate the tumor microenvironment, thereby enhancing the efficacy of chemotherapy (10,43,44). The differential response according to RAS mutation status and tumor sidedness highlights the molecular heterogeneity of mCRC and underscores the predictive value of biomarker-driven targeted therapy (11).

Importantly, targeted therapy represents a heterogeneous group of agents with distinct biological mechanisms and clinical roles. Anti-EGFR therapies are primarily effective in patients with RAS wild-type, left-sided tumors, whereas anti-VEGF therapies are applicable across a broader range of molecular subtypes, including RAS- or BRAF-mutant and right-sided tumors. While multi-kinase inhibitors like regorafenib are not primarily biomarker-driven for patient selection and are typically reserved for refractory mCRC in later-line settings, they are classified as targeted systemic therapies in treatment guidelines due to their anti-angiogenic and anti-proliferative activities. Their inclusion in this meta-analysis was intended to provide a comprehensive overview of targeted agents used across the full treatment continuum, including settings for refractory disease where options are limited. These differences may contribute to variability in treatment outcomes. Although pooling these agents provides an overall estimate of efficacy, subgroup analyses indicate that their benefits are context-dependent and should be interpreted accordingly, with regorafenib primarily representing later-line, non-biomarker-selected targeted therapy.

Targeted therapy significantly improved ORR and DCRs, leading to higher conversion-to-resection rates (RR =1.45) (36,45,46). Preoperative targeted therapy combined with chemotherapy can downstage initially unresectable liver metastases, particularly in patients with RAS wild-type, left-sided tumors, thereby increasing surgical eligibility (47,48). These findings emphasize the importance of stratifying patients according to resectability status and treatment setting. Notably, the observed overall benefit is largely driven by studies involving advanced or unresectable CRLM and may not be directly generalizable to patients undergoing perioperative treatment for resectable disease.

Although grade ≥3 AEs were more frequent in patients receiving targeted therapy, these toxicities were generally manageable (49,50). Anti-VEGF therapy was associated with hypertension, proteinuria, bleeding, and thromboembolic events, whereas anti-EGFR therapy commonly caused skin toxicity, hypomagnesemia, and infusion-related reactions. Multi-kinase inhibitors (e.g., regorafenib) demonstrated higher toxicity in refractory settings, necessitating careful dose adjustment and close monitoring (51).

Our findings further reinforce the pivotal role of molecular biomarkers and tumor sidedness in guiding targeted therapy. Patients with RAS wild-type tumors derive substantial benefit from anti-EGFR therapy, whereas those with RAS-mutant or right-sided tumors exhibit limited response (11,43). Tumor sidedness likely reflects underlying biological differences, including variations in EGFR pathway activation, microsatellite instability, immune infiltration, and microbiome composition (42,44). These observations highlight the need to integrate comprehensive molecular and anatomical profiling into routine clinical decision-making (52).

Importantly, the results of this meta-analysis should be interpreted in the context of the New EPOC trial, which demonstrated that adding cetuximab to perioperative chemotherapy in resectable CRLM resulted in significantly worse OS compared with chemotherapy alone. This finding contrasts with the established benefit of anti-EGFR therapy in advanced, unresectable mCRC. This discrepancy suggests that the efficacy of targeted therapy is highly context-dependent and varies according to disease stage, tumor biology, and treatment intent. In resectable disease, where the goal is curative resection and control of micrometastatic disease, anti-EGFR therapy may adversely influence tumor behavior or recurrence patterns. In contrast, in unresectable disease, it improves tumor response and facilitates conversion to resection.

Subgroup analyses further revealed clinically meaningful heterogeneity across treatment lines (53,54). Outcomes differed between patients receiving first-line therapy and those treated in later-line settings. Newly diagnosed patients with mCRC or CRLM are generally more treatment-sensitive and harbor fewer resistance mechanisms (55), whereas refractory patients often exhibit more aggressive disease biology and limited responsiveness. Although targeted therapy provided benefit in both groups, the magnitude of improvement was greater in the first-line setting. These findings support the paradigm that earlier integration of targeted therapy yields greater clinical benefit. Accordingly, pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution, as they may obscure differences between treatment-naïve and heavily pretreated populations. This underscores the importance of stratified analyses and more detailed reporting of treatment line in future studies.

This study has several limitations. First, the inclusion of studies across both resectable and unresectable settings may obscure context-specific effects of targeted therapy, as exemplified by the New EPOC trial. Second, resectability status—a key determinant of prognosis and treatment strategy—was not uniformly defined or reported. Consequently, patients with resectable, potentially resectable, and unresectable CRLM were analyzed together, introducing clinical heterogeneity and potentially affecting survival estimates. Although line of therapy and conversion to resection were used as surrogate indicators, these variables do not fully capture the complexity of surgical decision-making. Future studies should adopt standardized definitions of resectability and report outcomes stratified by surgical eligibility.

In addition, pooling first-line and later-line populations, as well as different classes of targeted therapy, may introduce further heterogeneity due to differences in prognosis, mechanisms of action, and clinical indications. Another limitation is the lack of prospective registration in the PROSPERO database, which may introduce reporting bias; however, PRISMA 2020 guidelines and Cochrane methodological standards were strictly followed to ensure methodological rigor.

Finally, variability and incomplete reporting of molecular profiles across included studies represent an important limitation. Not all studies provided comprehensive data on RAS mutation status, and fewer reported BRAF status or primary tumor sidedness. Earlier trials in particular often lacked systematic molecular stratification, limiting the availability of consistent subgroup data. This inconsistency may introduce residual confounding and reduce the robustness of biomarker-driven analyses, especially when interpreting differential treatment effects across molecular subgroups.

Conclusions

In conclusion, targeted therapy significantly improves survival, tumor response, and conversion to resection in CRLM, with the greatest benefit observed in RAS wild-type and left-sided tumors. These results support its integration into precision-based, multidisciplinary treatment strategies. Continued research into biomarker-guided approaches, novel combination regimens, and patient-centered outcomes will further refine therapy and optimize clinical benefit for patients with CRLM.
