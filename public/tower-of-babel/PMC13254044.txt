The challenge of cell death switching in inflammasome-targeted therapies for bacterial and viral infections
Authors: Yanfeng Li, Dongxiao Lv, Qingqing Xie, Yuanfu Qi, Xiaopeng Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inflammasome activation and pyroptosis are central to innate immune responses and play either protective or detrimental roles in host defense. Inhibiting these processes holds significant therapeutic promise for diseases exacerbated by inflammasome activation. However, clinically targeting this pathway remains challenging, as blocking pyroptosis may trigger alternative cell death pathways. In this mini-review, we summarize bacterial and viral infections in which inflammasome activation and pyroptosis are detrimental to host defense, explore the mechanisms underlying cell death switching, and discuss how effectively blocking multiple cell death pathways and potential interconversions represents a key consideration for current intervention strategies.

Introduction

Inflammasomes are signaling protein complexes that trigger immune responses by activating inflammatory caspases1. Inflammasome activation plays a critical role in responses to bacterial, viral, fungal, and other pathogenic infections or sterile inflammation2–5. The canonical inflammasome complex is composed of the sensor protein, the adapter ASC (also known as PYCARD), and the enzymatic effector protein caspase-16. The sensor proteins include NLR family members, such as NLRP1, NLRP3, NLRP6, NLRP9, NLRP12, and NLRC4 as well as non-NLR sensors like AIM2 (ALR), interferon-γ-inducible protein 16 (IFI16), and pyrin7. Following inflammasome activation, caspase-1 is activated and cleaves pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 for maturation, as well as gasdermin D (GSDMD) to generate the active N-terminal domain that forms membrane pores8,9. This membrane perforation facilitates the extracellular release of mature IL-1β and IL-18 and triggers pyroptosis10–13. In contrast, the non-canonical inflammasome pathway is initiated when cytosolic lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from bacteria that have escaped phagolysosomal degradation is detected. This sensing mechanism operates independently of TLR4 and instead relies on the inflammatory protease caspase-11/4/514,15. Subsequently, these caspases cleave GSDMD, which in turn initiates pyroptosis16–18.

Inflammasome activation and pyroptosis can play either beneficial or detrimental roles in host defense19. During the early stage of salmonellosis, inflammasome activation plays a protective role and contributes to bacterial clearance, and Nlrp3-deficient mice are significantly more susceptible to Salmonella infection20. Likewise, Caspase-1−/− and Caspase-11−/− mice exhibit an elevated bacterial burden in intestinal Salmonella infection models21. During the chronic phase of infection, suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome recognition and activation instead allows Salmonella to persist22. NLRP3 inflammasome activation is also critical for both pathogen surveillance and caspase-1/IL-1β-driven clearance of Staphylococcus aureus23,24. During influenza A virus (IAV) infection, NLRP3 initiates a beneficial inflammatory response that enhances innate and adaptive immunity to clear the infection25–27. However, as the disease progresses, excessive pyroptosis promotes the release of abundant inflammatory mediators, which can provoke a cytokine storm and lead to severe clinical pathologies, including chronic inflammation and tissue damage in multiple organs5. Thus, inhibiting inflammasome activation and pyroptosis holds significant promise for treating diseases exacerbated by this process. Several small-molecule NLRP3 inhibitors are currently in preclinical or early clinical evaluation. MCC950 (also known as CP-456, 773 or CRID3) was the first tool compound developed to specifically target the NLRP3 inflammasome28,29. Other NLRP3-inhibiting compounds have since been reported, including CY-0930, Tranilast31, oridonin32, Tetrahydroquinoline33, and OLT117734. Yet, distinct cell death pathways do not operate in isolation-they interact and can even switch from one form to another35,36. Therefore, effectively blocking multiple death pathways along with their potential conversions has become a key consideration for current intervention strategies. In this mini-review, we summarize infections in which the inflammasome-pyroptosis undermines host defense, explore the mechanisms underlying cell death switching, and discuss how synergistic inhibition may guide novel host-directed therapies.

Inflammasomes can mediate either protective or harmful inflammatory responses, depending on the pathogen and the infection context, as well as on factors, such as the timing of activation, the pathogen burden, and the tissue microenvironment. NLRP3 and AIM2

Bacterial activation of the NLRP3 and AIM2 inflammasomes

NLRP3, one of the most extensively studied inflammasome-forming NOD-like receptors (NLRs), exhibits broad tissue distribution and shows the highest levels in immune cells1. It detects a broad spectrum of stimuli, including pathogens, microbial toxins, nucleic acids, ATP, and crystalline substances, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of numerous infectious and inflammatory diseases37.

NLRP3 activation by both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria can undermine host defense through distinct mechanisms. One prominent mechanism involves bacterial pore-forming toxins or secretion systems that disrupt plasma membrane integrity, leading to K⁺ efflux, NLRP3 assembly, caspase-1 activation, and subsequent pyroptosis. This pathway is exploited by Mycobacterium tuberculosis (ESX-1 system)38, Bacillus cereus (haemolysin BL)39,40, Clostridium septicum (α-toxin)41, and Clostridium perfringens (lecithinase and perfringolysin O)42, all of which trigger excessive inflammasome-driven inflammation that exacerbates tissue damage, septic shock, or bacterial dissemination. Another detrimental mechanism involves the subversion of host metabolism. Staphylococcus aureus activates the NLRP3 inflammasome in macrophages via α-toxin, which drives host cell glycolysis and depletes cytoplasmic glucose. This glucose restriction reduces bacterial ATP levels and induces antibiotic tolerance, whereas NLRP3 inhibition or exogenous glucose restores antibiotic susceptibility43. Shigella degrades the host protein glomulin to activate NLRP3, inducing macrophage pyroptosis that facilitates bacterial escape and subsequent epithelial invasion44,45. Beyond these, NLRP3 activation also contributes to acute or chronic inflammatory diseases. In cigarette smoke-exposed lungs, Pseudomonas aeruginosa further potentiates this process through NLRP3 activation, impaired mitophagy, and p62 accumulation, thereby aggravating acute lung injury46. Cutibacterium acnes drives intervertebral disc degeneration via the TXNIP-NLRP3 axis47,48, while Escherichia coli K1 induces neuronal DNA double-strand breaks and permanent cellular senescence in meningitis through NLRP3/IL-18 signaling49.

AIM2 functions as an inflammasome pattern recognition receptor that is activated in response to cytosolic dsDNA50,51. A Francisella novicida mutant strain (XWK4) potently activates both AIM2 and NLRP3, driving robust cytokine production and pyroptosis that result in substantial cell death and tissue damage, as evidenced by the increased resistance of Asc−/− mice52. Overall, NLRP3 and AIM2 activation not only contribute to host defense but can also drive immunopathology, bacterial dissemination, and chronic inflammatory diseases through distinct molecular mechanisms.

Viral activation of the NLRP3 and AIM2 inflammasomes

Pyroptosis initially serves as a regulatory mechanism in antiviral immunity; however, excessive NLRP3 activation drives cytokine storms and hypercoagulability, as observed with SARS- CoV-253–56 and RVFV57. NLRP3-dependent pyroptosis also leads to neuroinflammation and neuronal injury by HIV-158, HSV-159, and SFTSV60. Furthermore, pyroptosis facilitates immunopathology and viral dissemination induced by MNV61, Coxsackievirus A16 and A1062, and AIM2-sensed MPXV63,64. Dysregulation drives cytokine storms and viral spread, making pyroptosis a key pathogenic mechanism in severe infections.

The NLRP6 and NLRC4 inflammasomes

NLRP6 (also known as NALP6 and PYPAF5) is highly expressed in the large and small intestine and is also present in the lungs, liver, kidneys, and brain of both humans and mice65. NLRP6 turnover is regulated in part at the transcriptional level, suggesting that, similar to NLRP3, its activation may be primed by an upstream signal1.

A detrimental role for NLRP6 in the host response to bacterial infection has been consistently observed in mice. During Staphylococcus aureus lung infection, NLRP6 suppresses neutrophil function (IFN-γ/ROS) and drives pyroptosis and necroptosis, thereby exacerbating inflammation and mortality66. In Listeria monocytogenes-infected macrophages, NLRP6 recognizes lipoteichoic acid (LTA) to activate caspase-1/11, promoting IL-18 maturation and worsening pathology. Accordingly, Nlrp6−/− mice exhibit reduced susceptibility to Listeria monocytogenes, which correlates with decreased bacterial burdens and diminished IL-18 production67. The Listeria monocytogenes virulence factor listeriolysin O (LLO) also promotes oligomerization of the inflammasome adapter ASC, driving this inflammatory pathology68.

NLRC4 (also known as CARD12, CLAN, CLAN1, and IPAF) was first identified through a search for genes with sequence similarity to caspase-169. Necroptosis is a pro-inflammatory form of regulated cell death mediated by receptor-interacting serine/threonine kinases RIPK1 and RIPK3 and executed by mixed lineage kinase domain-like protein (MLKL)70–72. Staphylococcus aureus activates NLRC4 to drive RIPK3-MLKL necroptosis and IL-18 production, suppressing neutrophil recruitment and impairing host defense73. Shigella triggers the NLRC4 inflammasome, leading to rapid breakdown of the phagocytic vesicle membrane, resulting in macrophage pyroptosis and bacterial multiplication44,74. Evidence also indicates that Yersinia can induce host pyroptosis via NLRC4 inflammasome activation75. In Nlrc4−/− mice infected with Pseudomonas aeruginosa, enhanced bacterial clearance and reduced lung injury improved survival via diminished NLRC4-driven IL-18 secretion, which attenuated excessive neutrophil recruitment and lung damage76. Compared with NLRP3 and AIM2, NLRC4 and NLRP6 have rarely been linked to the negative regulation of host defense against viral infections77–79.

GSDMD, GSDME, and NINJ1

GSDMD, a pore-forming protein, mediates lytic cell death upon inflammasome activation. Recent evidence underscores the plasticity among cell death pathways, indicating that pyroptosis is not exclusively dependent on inflammasome signaling12,80. Notably, GSDME expression can convert apoptotic signaling into lytic pyroptosis through caspase-3-dependent cleavage and subsequent plasma membrane pore formation81. Additionally, the membrane protein NINJ1 has been identified as a key regulator of plasma membrane rupture, and its structural basis has been extensively characterized in recent studies82,83.

Severe influenza A virus infections are characterized by excessive inflammation, pronounced cell death, and extensive epithelial damage, all of which contribute to lethal lung disease84–87. Studies demonstrate that GSDMD deficiency enhances resistance to influenza, as evidenced by reduced airway neutrophil infiltration, lower pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, decreased viral burden, and attenuated lung pathology88. Influenza A virus infection upregulates NINJ1 expression, which subsequently oligomerizes and mediates cell lysis in infected macrophages. In vivo ablation of NINJ1 ameliorates influenza A virus-induced viral pneumonia and reduces mortality89. Several other viruses also exploit GSDME-mediated pyroptosis to drive distinct pathologies, including HSV-2-induced neuroinflammation90, Zika virus-associated adverse fetal development91, and DHAV-1-triggered lethal cytokine storms92.

Although inhibiting inflammasome activation or subsequent pyroptosis can enhance host defense against certain bacteria and viruses (Table 1), accumulating evidence suggests that disrupting a single module rarely leads to a straightforward survival outcome; it instead shifts cell death execution toward alternative pathways35,36,93–98. Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of inflammasome components often alters the predominant cell death modality and reprograms the associated inflammatory profiles99. While blocking GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis may reduce acute immunopathology, upstream danger signaling can persist and be redirected through apoptotic, necroptotic, or other gasdermin-mediated lytic pathways. For example, Gsdmd-deficient macrophages with constitutive NLRP3 activation failed to undergo pyroptosis and secrete IL-1β upon brief exposure to NLRP3 activators; however, they release cytokines and undergo cell death under persistent activation, revealing that alternative GSDME-dependent cell death occurs in the absence of GSDMD93. Thus, tissue damage and inflammatory amplification may continue even when GSDMD-dependent lysis is suppressed100.

Crosstalk between multiple cell death pathways

The host has evolved a coordinated and flexible cell death system that involves extensive crosstalk between the effectors and initiators of multiple cell death pathways. During Salmonella infection, the loss of a single cell death pathway had only a minor impact on host defense; only the combined loss of multiple pathways led to a deficiency in bacterial control95. In fact, programmed cell death pathways do not operate in isolation but rather function as a coordinated and integrated system. These pathways are extensively interconnected and can flexibly compensate for one another, and the biological significance of this crosstalk is to ultimately eliminate cells (Fig. 1). The ASC platform as a central hub in cell death signaling

Apoptosis and necroptosis function as complementary pathways regulated by shared signaling adapters, kinases, and proteases36. Caspase-8 plays a critical role in this network, primarily mediating apoptosis activation and necroptosis inhibition101–106. In recent years, it has been recognized as a master molecular switch governing the cross-regulation of apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis107,108. Substantial evidence indicates that caspase-8 can facilitate a switch from apoptosis to pyroptosis by cleaving gasdermin family proteins, such as GSDMD109,110 and GSDMC111. In addition, caspase-3 can promote a similar switch by cleaving GSDME81,112.

Beyond its activation downstream of death receptors, caspase-8 is also engaged downstream of NLRP3, AIM2, and NLRC4 inflammasome following canonical stimulation in macrophages. In the absence of functional caspase-1 or GSDMD, the ASC platform can recruit and activate caspase-8, leading to a delayed apoptotic response93,113–115. Structural and biochemical evidence shows that ASC filaments can directly induce polymerization of the death effector domain (DED) in pro-caspase-8, providing a physical explanation for their interaction when classical pyroptosis is suppressed116. The balance between pyroptosis and apoptosis is influenced by cytosolic DNA levels, with caspase-8 acting as the apical caspase in AIM2- and NLRP3-dependent apoptotic pathways. In these pathways, pro-caspase-8 binds directly to the pyrin domain of ASC, integrating caspase-8 into the inflammasome complex117. NLRP3 and ASC can also assemble into a conserved, non-canonical platform that activates caspase-8 independently of the inflammasome to regulate apoptosis in epithelial cells118. Furthermore, while caspase-8 is recruited to the Naip5/NLRC4/ASC inflammasome in wild-type macrophages, its activation specifically requires inhibition of caspase-1 or GSDMD. Once activated within this complex, caspase-8 promotes a shift from pyroptosis to apoptosis, a mechanism that may counteract pathogens that suppress canonical pyroptotic components115,119.

PANoptosis is a regulated cell death pathway mediated by a multiprotein complex called the PANoptosome, which coordinates crosstalk among pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis3,86,120,121. Cells undergoing PANoptosis form a distinct punctum containing ZBP1, NLRP3, AIM2, RIPK1, RIPK3, ASC, caspase-1, or caspase-8, suggesting that the inflammasome may serve as a core structural component of the PANoptosome121. These complexes rely on the scaffold protein ASC to recruit and assemble their distinct molecular constituents86,122,123. Multiple mechanisms of NLRP3 activation during influenza A virus infection have been defined, including crosstalk with other cell death pathways via ZBP1, which forms the first recognized PANoptosome—the ZBP1-PANoptosome—in response to influenza A virus infection96. This crosstalk underscores the complex role of the inflammasome in infection, as it coordinates potent host defense while uncontrolled responses may drive unresolved inflammatory disease5. Collectively, these studies indicate that the ASC speck functions as a cell death switch platform to mediate alternative caspase activation (Fig. 1).

Mitochondria as a central hub in cell death modalities

As essential double-membrane organelles, mitochondria play a central role in cellular processes that extend far beyond ATP production, metabolism, and calcium homeostasis to include regulated cell death and innate immune responses124,125. This positions mitochondria as critical mediators of cell fate during the activation of death pathways126.

Recent studies have shown that certain gasdermins permeabilize intracellular membranes, particularly the mitochondrial outer and inner membranes (MOM and MIM), by interacting with cardiolipin127–129. Mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) are key drivers of necroptosis. They promote RIPK1 autophosphorylation, which facilitates the recruitment of RIPK3 and the assembly of the functional necrosome complex that executes cell death130. Furthermore, mtROS guides GSDMD to associate with mitochondrial membranes, a process involved in the switch from pyroptosis to necroptosis. GSDMD pore formation at mitochondrial membranes releases additional mtROS, creating a feed-forward loop that facilitates a switch to RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL-dependent necroptotic cell death131. In addition, GSDME has also been reported to permeabilize the mitochondrial membrane, leading to cytochrome c release and caspase-3 activation, thereby augmenting cell death132.

Mitochondria also integrate apoptotic and pyroptotic signaling. For instance, NLRP3 activators can inhibit mitochondrial ATP production and suppress cytochrome c release. This dual inhibition acts as a regulatory switch, simultaneously triggering NLRP3 activation while blocking apoptosis to coordinate the transition between these pathways133. During apoptosis, the macropores formed by BAX and BAK facilitate the release of mtDNA into the cytosol134. Once released, mtDNA serves as a key trigger for AIM2 inflammasome activation135,136. Similarly, mitochondrial permeabilization triggered by GSDMD and GSDME during pyroptosis and intrinsic apoptosis releases mtDNA, thereby amplifying and sustaining innate immune and inflammatory responses129,137. GSDMA preferentially targets mitochondria138, where it disrupts oxidative phosphorylation and promotes the release of mitochondrial proteins and mtDNA, driving inflammatory responses and accelerating pyroptosis128. Consequently, sustained inflammatory damage can persist even when pyroptosis is inhibited, as ongoing metabolic and redox stress may activate alternative or complementary cell death pathways (Fig. 1) Box 1.

Conclusions and outlook

The inflammasome is a crucial component of the innate immune system and is implicated in a broad spectrum of diseases, including autoimmune disorders, sepsis, metabolic syndromes, cancer, and neurodegenerative conditions. In the context of infection and immunity, pyroptosis acts as a double-edged sword: while it releases danger signals that trigger inflammatory responses to eliminate pathogen replication niches, it can also contribute to pathological tissue damage and excessive inflammation. An increasing number of pathways leading to GSDMD activation in different biological contexts have been identified, expanding pyroptosis from a host innate immune defense mechanism to a vital inflammatory cell death program involved in a wide range of physiological and pathological processes16,139–146. Substantial in vivo evidence indicates that drugs targeting pyroptosis can effectively inhibit pathogen replication and attenuate inflammatory responses over a short period in certain infection models; however, the potential for compensatory cell death pathways necessitates a broader therapeutic approach. Notably, regulated cell death is not a singular, terminal event but can undergo phenotypic switching. Investigating the crosstalk and switching between cell death pathways during infection will provide critical insights into host immune defense mechanisms. Such knowledge is essential for learning how to modulate these pathways and for understanding the clinical implications of such interventions.

Inflammasome research has now entered a phase of clinical translation, with foundational discoveries-particularly those concerning the NLRP3 inflammasome-progressing to human trials99,147. The NLRP3 inflammasome is embedded within a highly interconnected network of cell death pathways that exhibit substantial plasticity and compensatory crosstalk35,36. This complexity poses a significant challenge for conventional single-target inhibitors, which may only transiently suppress inflammation while allowing alternative lytic pathways to sustain tissue damage100. Given the extensive crosstalk and functional redundancy among pyroptosis, apoptosis, and necroptosis, targeting a single cell death pathway is often insufficient to prevent pathogen-induced tissue damage or immunopathology. Emerging evidence suggests several strategies for synergistic intervention. When GSDMD-dependent pyroptosis is suppressed, upstream inflammasome signaling persists and can switch to alternative lytic pathways93. In a silicosis mouse model, genetic ablation of either Gsdmd or Gsdme alone failed to protect mice from pulmonary inflammation and fibrosis, whereas Gsdmd−/−Gsdme−/− double knockout mice exhibited markedly attenuated pathology. Furthermore, only the combination of caspase-1, caspase-3, and caspase-8 inhibitors, but not a caspase-1 inhibitor alone, effectively alleviated silicosis94. In a mouse model of secondary hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (sHLH), combined genetic deletion of both GSDMD and GSDME, but not deletion of IL-1β/IL-18 or either gasdermin alone, significantly protected against disease lethality, demonstrating that concurrent blockade of multiple cell death executors is required to mitigate NLRP3 inflammasome-driven pathology98. Developing synergistic inhibition strategies to treat inflammation induced by pathogenic infections holds significant promise for managing inflammasome-driven diseases. Future studies should evaluate the safety and efficacy of these combination strategies in pathogen-driven inflammation, balancing antimicrobial defense with the need to curtail immunopathology.
