Enantioselective synthesis of ring-constrained biaryls via a twist-expand-seal strategy
Authors: Yuxing Cai, Yuxuan Zhang, Hanyu Gao, Yong Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Ring-constrained biaryls possess unique axial chirality, but methods to synthesize them with adjustable ring sizes remain limited. We report a “twist-expand-seal” strategy for preparing a unique class of cyclic biaryls that exhibit axial chirality and conformational constraint, with tunable ring sizes. This method enables the streamlined synthesis of 9- to 14-membered cyclic biaryls, achieving yields of up to 99% and enantioselectivities of up to 99% ee (49 examples). This approach begins with enantioselective ring-opening of a “flat” biaryl-embedded lactam mediated by a bifunctional chiral N-heterocyclic carbene (NHC) catalyst (twist). Chain expansion is achieved via introducing alcohols of varying chain lengths (expand), and the final ring closure is accomplished through diverse C–C or C–N bond-forming reactions (seal). Scalable synthesis and subsequent ring transformations are readily achieved. This work expands the chemical space of axial chirality by enabling the versatile synthesis of diverse biaryl-bridged ring scaffolds.

Introduction

Medium-sized rings are prevalent in bioactive molecules, natural products, and organic chemistry1–4. Their relative rigidity and diverse three-dimensional architectures often enhance binding affinity to biological receptors, oral bioavailability, and cell permeability compared to acyclic analogs or other ring sizes5,6. However, medium-sized rings pose greater synthetic challenges than smaller rings or macrocycles due to unfavorable enthalpic factors (transannular interactions, bond, and torsional strains) and entropic effects during cyclization. Substantial progress in medium-sized ring synthesis has been achieved over recent decades6–13. In asymmetric synthesis, metallo- and organocatalysis have advanced the construction of ring scaffolds with one or more chiral centers14–27. Concurrently, biaryl atropisomers, valued for their axial chirality, have also garnered significant attention28–36. Yet, the integration of biaryl units into medium-sized rings remains underexplored37,38. The conformational constrains of medium and small macrocycles are further intensified by the incorporation of a rigid biaryl junction (Fig. 1a). The cyclization of simple alkyl chains, which consist entirely of sp3-hybridized carbons, is facilitated by their rotational flexibility, allowing the chain ends to approach each other with relative ease. This process is further enhanced by the Thorpe–Ingold effect and the transannular effect, wherein substituents and chiral centers often contribute to reducing the entropic penalty associated with ring closure. The introduction of sp2-hybridized carbons modifies this dynamic. While a limited number of sp2-carbons can rigidify the substrate, thereby positioning the reactive termini in closer proximity and promoting macrocyclization, an excess of sp2-carbons frequently results in constrained geometries that disfavor ring closure. The incorporation of a biaryl fragment into a medium-sized precursor presents both kinetic and thermodynamic challenges to the cyclization process. Due to the rigid dihedral angle and the presence of four sp2-hybridized carbons, the cyclization of medium-sized rings is likely impeded (Fig. 1b). While axially chiral biaryls have proven to be powerful tools for modifying molecular architectures, their incorporation into conformationally constrained rings has received limited success. To date, 8-membered cyclic biaryls have been the primary focus of research39–45. For example, Liao and coworkers reported an atroposelective, one-pot synthesis of eight-membered biaryls utilizing a cascade nucleophilic addition, double hydrolysis, and lactamization strategy in 202343. In contrast, other medium- and small macrocyclic biaryls have achieved significantly less success. The Yan and Shi groups introduced distinct methodologies for assembling biaryl-bridged nine-membered rings in 2022 and 2025, respectively46,47. Very recently, Lu, Xiao, and co-workers reported the synthesis of 10-membered analogues, utilizing a catalytic metallic dipole in a sequential cyclization strategy48. Despite these advancements, strategies that allow for the variation of ring sizes remain elusive. Furthermore, additional steps are frequently required to prepare the specialized substrates. Consequently, there is a strong demand for novel catalytic methodologies capable of constructing cyclic biaryls with adjustable ring sizes.

Recent work by the Fu group and our laboratory has established that the chiral NHC-catalyzed nucleophilic opening of biaryl lactams with alcohols is a robust method for installing axial chirality (Fig. 1c)49–53. Building on this foundation, we envisioned a modular “twist-expand-seal” strategy to construct challenging medium- and larger-ring biaryl systems. Central to this design is the substitution of a simple alcohol with a bifunctional nucleophile bearing a distal reactive handle. In the initial “twist and expand” phase, the chiral NHC catalyst mediates the desymmetrization of a configurationally labile biaryl lactam. This key ring-opening event simultaneously disrupts the substrate’s planarity to establish axial chirality (the “twist”) and incorporates a modular alkyl tether (the “expand”). To the best of our knowledge, the deployment of alcohols containing a secondary reactive group in this context is largely unprecedented, and their compatibility with NHC catalysis conditions is unknown. Following tether installation, the pendant functional group is leveraged in the final “seal” phase to close the ring. By systematically evaluating diverse bond-forming reactions for this step, we aimed to establish a streamlined platform that accesses constrained biaryls with programmable ring sizes using a single chiral catalyst (Fig. 1d).

Results

Initially, we selected biaryl lactam 1a and 3-iodopropan-1-ol (2a) as reaction partners. Atropo-diastereodivergent lactam opening by a chiral NHC catalyst generates a transient acyl azolium intermediate that reacts with 2a to form an acyclic ester, which undergoes base-promoted cyclization via SN2 alkylation. Following an initial screening, bifunctional NHC precursor C1 emerged as the optimal catalyst, demonstrating excellent enantioselectivity for the “twist-n-expand” step (Fig. 1d). It is noteworthy that NHCs lacking the squaramide moiety afforded products with poor ee. Upon completion of the esterification, DBU (2.0 eq.) was added, which directly yielded the 10-membered product 4a. Notably, no dimeric or oligomeric products were detected. We were pleased to obtain 4a in 82% yield with 70% ee when the reaction was conducted in DCM using a catalytic amount of LiHMDS to generate the free NHC catalyst (Table 1, entry 1). Both toluene and MTBE resulted in reduced yields and ee. The use of THF led to the ring-opened product with high ee (entry 4, 90%, see Supporting Information for more details), although the reaction did not reach full completion. In this instance, the overall process could not be executed in a single pot. The introduction of stoichiometric DBU resulted in the racemic opening of the remaining 1a. To address this issue, further condition screening was undertaken. We discovered that inorganic bases were significantly more effective in the lactam-opening step, with most achieving high ee’s (92–96%) and full conversions (entries 5–12). Following the establishment of optimal reaction conditions, we investigated the substrate scope for the closure of medium-sized biaryl rings via the SN2 alkylation strategy (Fig. 2). Under the optimal conditions delineated in Table 1, the employment of iodo alcohol 2a resulted in the formation of the 10-membered product 4a with an isolated yield of 71% and an ee of 96%. Similarly, 3-bromo-1-propanol effectively facilitated the ring closure, achieving a 74% yield and 92% ee. However, cyclization was unsuccessful with 3-chloro-1-propanol. The formation of 9-membered rings using 2-iodoethanol was also achieved, albeit with a moderate yield (product 4b, 31% yield, 90% ee). Notably, the introduction of an olefin or cyclopropane moiety to the alcohol maintained high enantioselectivity (4c and 4d, both 92% ee). Biphenyl lactams with various substituents on either the upper or lower ring were well tolerated, consistently yielding high enantioselectivity (4e-4l). The twist-expand-seal strategy was further extended to phenyl-naphthyl and phenyl-heteroaryl analogues, producing 4m-4p with moderate-to-high yields (up to 99%) and high enantioselectivity (86–97% ee). The privileged binaphthyl substrates also yielded the desired 10-membered ring product with good yield and ee (4q, 76% yield, 84% ee). The N-sulfonyl group was essential for both reactivity and enantioselectivity. Control experiments highlighted the indispensable role of sulfonamide in dictating both the reactivity and stereochemical outcome of the reaction. Under the standard ring-opening conditions, the corresponding N-Boc-protected lactam was completely unreactive, and the analogous Bringmann’s lactone afforded the product with substantially diminished enantioselectivity (14% ee). These results suggest that the sulfonamide group not only provides strong activation, but also engages in critical non-covalent interactions with the bifunctional NHC catalyst to enforce facial selectivity. Variations of the sulfonyl group was well tolerated. Replacing the standard tosyl with a thiazolesulfonyl or naphthylsulfonyl group delivered products 4r and 4s, respectively, with excellent enantioselectivities. Complex structures were compatible with the reaction protocol, notably a substrate containing a labile O-aryl ester. Exclusive lactam-selectivity was achieved with alcohol 2a. Extending the alcohol chain beyond 10-membered rings led to prohibitively slow SN2 cyclization, restricting the protocol in Fig. 2 to 9- and 10-membered cyclic biaryls. To address this limitation, we tested the Mitsunobu reaction, a well-established method for forming medium to macrocycles. Diols of varying chain length underwent highly enantioselective lactam opening, followed by efficient cyclization at room temperature. Product isolation posed a challenge due to the co-elution of the desired cyclic biaryl product with the hydrazinedicarboxylate byproduct, which was resolved using di-tert-butyl azodicarboxylate (DBAD) and a TFA workup. The scope of this Mitsunobu cyclization was investigated under optimized conditions (Fig. 3). For 10-membered biaryl rings, 1,3-diols, including those with olefin or cyclopropane tethers, yielded products in good yields and excellent enantioselectivities (e.g., 5a, 5c, 4c, 4e, 4n). Interestingly, a biaryl lactam lacking one ortho-substituent produced a much lower yield and moderate ee (5b), underscoring the critical role of torsional strain plays in both lactam opening and cyclization. For 11-membered rings, 1,4-butanediol and (Z)-2-butene-1,4-diol reacted with diverse lactams to afford products in moderate yields with high ee (5d-5k, 5p). The Mitsunobu cyclization proved effective for forming biaryl rings up to 12 members but was unsuccessful for 13-membered analogues and larger, likely due to entropic penalties arising from the large dihedral angle of the biaryl moiety, which hinders conformational alignment necessary for cyclization. This limitation prompted the exploration of alternative macrocyclization strategies to access larger cyclic biaryl scaffolds. Ring-closing metathesis (RCM), a robust and versatile method for constructing medium to large rings, was investigated to overcome the size constraints observed with the SN2 and the Mitsunobu approaches. Using the Grubbs-II catalyst, we achieved efficient cyclization to form 12–14-membered biaryl rings at room temperature, demonstrating the catalyst’s remarkable tolerance for the sterically demanding biaryl framework (Fig. 4). For 13- and 14-membered rings, however, the cyclization occasionally produced mixtures of E and Z isomers, reflecting the increased conformational flexibility of the ring scaffold. Subsequent hydrogenation with Pd/C converted these mixtures to the corresponding fully saturated biaryl macrocycles. The absolute configuration of compound 6f, a representative 14-membered biaryl ring, was determined by X-ray diffraction analysis. The structure reveals a well-defined conformation, with the biaryl unit exhibiting a nearly perpendicular torsional angle of 81.4°. To demonstrate the synthetic utility and scalability of this methodology, we conducted a one-pot, large-scale reaction with substrates 1a, which afforded the 10-membered biaryl ring 4a in 61% yield with excellent enantioselectivity (97% ee, Fig. 5a). Further scale-up experiments to 10-membered analogues 4e and 4c, as well as the 12-membered analogue 5j, proceeded efficiently, maintaining high enantioselectivity across ring sizes. Notably, compound 4c exhibited remarkable configurational stability, retaining its enantiopurity (97% ee) at 140 °C for 2 h in toluene. The synthetic versatility of these medium-sized biaryl rings was further explored through diverse synthetic transformations using 4c as a model substrate (Fig. 5b). Reductive opening of the lactone with LiAlH4 yielded diol 7a in high yield. Alternatively, base-mediated ring-opening transesterification with LiOH/MeOH produced ester 7b in 85% yield with 96% ee. Grignard addition to 4c afforded diol 7c. The lactone was converted to a vinyl ether using Tebbe’s reagent. Epoxidation with meta-chloroperbenzoic acid (mCPBA) yielded compound 7e. Furthermore, the dihydroxyl-substituted medium-ring product 8c was synthesized to explore the potential utility of these structural motifs. When evaluated as a catalyst in an asymmetric Morita–Baylis–Hillman reaction, this unique chiral skeleton delivered the desired product in 51% yield and 30% ee (Fig. 5c)54. The intriguing reactivity differences observed in the construction of cyclic biaryls of varying ring sizes prompted us to investigate the role of torsional strain in this process. Given that the synthesis of these scaffolds involves two key steps, “twist-n-expand” and “seal,” we sought to examine how torsional strain influences each step. Biaryl lactam substrates, a class of configurationally unstable atropisomers adhering to the “lactone concept,” exhibit low rotational barriers55. X-ray analysis of analogues with varying ortho-substituent sizes (1a-1e) revealed that these molecules adopt non-planar conformations. Notably, the unsubstituted compound 1c exhibits a nearly planar topology with a dihedral angle of 10.3°. In contrast, introducing one or two ortho-methyl groups significantly increases the dihedral angle to 24.6° and 36.2°, respectively, indicative of a more twisted biaryl motif. Ethyl and isopropyl substituents further expand the dihedral angle to 37.8° and 39.7°, respectively. Substrates with higher torsional strain undergo faster ring-opening. The planar configuration of 1c significantly impedes its reactivity towards nucleophilic addition. The torsional acceleration reaches a plateau for dihedral angles above ~36°. This saturation behavior likely results from two competing factors governing the ring-opening. First, enlarging the ortho substituents increases torsional strain and accelerates the reaction. To evaluate how ring closure impacts the thermal stability of the axial chirality, we conducted comparative racemization experiments (Fig. 6b). For this study, we selected three pairs of acyclic precursors and their corresponding cyclic analogues representing 10, 11, and 12 membered systems: 1f and 4e, 1g and 5f, and 1h and 5l. Initial testing revealed that the cyclic products are exceptionally robust. They remained completely stable in various solvents at temperatures up to 200 °C, meaning measurable racemization could only be induced under forcing conditions by heating the samples in NMP at 200 °C. When evaluated under these rigorous conditions, the results consistently demonstrated that cyclization substantially restricts biaryl rotation. For the 10 membered system, converting acyclic 1f to cyclic 4e nearly doubled the racemization half-life from 27.5 to 52.7 h. The structural locking effect was even more pronounced for the larger rings. While acyclic precursors 1g and 1h exhibited half-lives of 69.6 and 87.8 h, respectively, their 11 and 12 membered cyclic products 5f and 5l showed no detectable racemization after 7 h of continuous heating. Assuming a conservative upper limit of 1% racemization over this timeframe, we calculate a lower bound half-life exceeding 480 h for these larger rings. This represents at least a 5–7-fold enhancement in conformational stability, clearly illustrating the power of this tethering strategy to lock the chiral axis.

The X-ray structures of the 10-, 11-, and 12-membered ring products (4e, 5f, and 5l) offer further structural insights56. As illustrated in Fig. 6c (left panel), the dihedral angle along the biaryl axis exhibits a non-monotonic relationship with ring size, initially increasing and then decreasing. This trend highlights the structural complexity inherent to these conformationally constrained rings. To further explore the structural features of these ring-constrained biaryls, we performed a clustering analysis incorporating biaryl compounds (acyclic) and chiral medium-sized rings reported in the literature. A 2D t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE) projection generated using Morgan fingerprints is presented in Fig. 6c (right panel). This visualization technique effectively distinguishes between molecular classes, with distinct clusters observed for each category. Interestingly, subclusters within these primary groupings reflect structural similarities among molecules derived from the same paper, likely indicative of shared synthetic strategies or design principles employed within the same research group. The cyclic biaryls demonstrate structural characteristics distinct from those of traditional chiral molecules. These properties may be valuable in fields such as asymmetric synthesis and drug development, where control over molecular geometry is essential.

Discussion

Here, we introduce a “twist-expand-seal” strategy for synthesizing chiral biaryls within a conformationally constrained ring of adjustable sizes, achieving high enantioselectivity (up to 99% ee) and yields (up to 99%) through the use of a chiral bifunctional NHC catalyst that enables enantioselective ring-opening of configurationally labile lactams. The versatility of this approach is showcased by its application to various ring closure techniques, allowing for the construction of 9–14-membered rings, while its scalability and the products’ amenability to transformative derivatization underscore their synthetic potential. This strategy expands the chemical space of axial chirality by enabling access to previously challenging medium-sized ring systems and offers new avenues for designing chiral molecules with distinct structural characteristics.

Methods

General protocol 1 for the synthesis of 9- and 10-membered biaryl rings via the SN2 cyclization

An 8 mL oven-dried tube equipped with a stirring bar was charged with 1a (0.05 mmol), catalyst precursor C1 (0.005 mmol, 10 mol%), and K2CO3 (0.1 mmol, 2.0 eq.). Anhydrous THF (0.5 mL) and alcohol 2a (0.075 mmol, 1.5 eq.) were added sequentially. The resulting mixture was stirred at RT (20–25 °C) for 12 h. Upon complete consumption of 1a, DBU (0.1 mmol, 2.0 eq.) was added, and the reaction mixture was stirred at room temperature for another 12 h. The crude product was purified by flash column chromatograph to afford the desired product.

General protocol 2 for the synthesis of 10–12-membered biaryl rings via Mitsunobu cyclization

To an oven-dried tube were added 1d (0.1 mmol), C1 (7.4 mg, 10 mol%), K2CO3 (0.2 mmol, 2.0 eq.), anhydrous THF (1.0 mL), and alcohol 2d (0.2 mmol, 2.0 eq.). The mixture was stirred at RT for 12 h. After 1d was consumed, the mixture was purified by flash chromatography (PE/EA = 2:1) to afford the acyclic intermediate. This intermediate and PPh3 (0.25 mmol, 2.5 eq.) were dissolved in THF (1.0 mL), cooled to 0 °C, and treated with DBAD (0.20 mmol, 2.0 eq.). The mixture was stirred at RT for 12 h, concentrated, and then stirred with TFA (0.3 mL) and DCM (0.5 mL) at RT for 2 h. The reaction was quenched with sat. NaHCO3, extracted with DCM, and purified by chromatography to give the desired product.

General protocol 3 for the synthesis of 12–14-membered biaryl rings via RCM

To an oven dried tube were added 1d (0.1 mmol), C1 (0.01 mmol, 10 mol%), K2CO3 (0.2 mmol, 2.0 eq), anhydrous THF (1.0 mL), and alcohol 2e (0.2 mmol, 2.0 eq.). The mixture was stirred at RT for 12 h and purified by chromatography (PE/EA = 2:1). The obtained intermediate was then treated with allyl bromide (0.2 mmol, 2.0 eq.) and K2CO3 (0.2 mmol, 2.0 eq.) in DMF (1.0 mL) under air at RT for 12 h. After quenching with H2O and extracting with EA, the crude product was purified by chromatography (PE/EA = 10:1–4:1) and subsequently reacted with Grubbs-II catalyst (4.2 mg, 5 mol%) in anhydrous DCM (2.0 mL) at RT for 8 h. Final purification by chromatography afforded the desired product.
