A cross-sectional survey of general practitioners’ influenza vaccination willingness and influencing factors based on the reasoned action approach
Authors: Yundi Ma, Yangni Li, Mingmin Chen, Jingjing Ren, Linlin Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGeneral practitioners (GPs) play a dual role in influenza prevention and control as both medical decision-makers and a high-risk population for infection. This study, based on the Reasoned Action Approach (RAA), aimed to explore GPs’ influenza vaccination willingness and its influencing factors. Data were collected via an online questionnaire from February 11 to March 20, 2025, using convenience sampling. The questionnaire covered general profiles, influenza vaccination in the last season, vaccination willingness, and a RAA-based scales (including dimensions of attitude, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control), and information-seeking behavior (active information collection, methods, and trusted sources). Among 341 questionnaires distributed, 330 valid ones were returned. The mean vaccination willingness score was 3.82 out of 5. Multiple regression analysis showed that vaccination willingness was positively associated with subjective norms (β = 0.367), attitudes (β = 0.291), vaccination in the last season (β = 0.124), and type of workplace (β = 0.111) (all p < .05). Given GPs’ exemplary role as health promoters, this study proposed multidimensional strategies like strengthening their health education, establishing social norm guidance mechanisms, and optimizing vaccine service accessibility. These aimed to enhance GPs’ vaccination willingness and their positive guidance on preventive behaviors in the population.

Introduction

Influenza, a highly contagious acute respiratory illness, poses a significant threat to global public health and imposes a substantial economic burden. It affects all age groups, with severe outcomes particularly common among high-risk populations such as the elderly, young children, pregnant women, individuals with chronic conditions, and healthcare workers. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), there are approximately 1 billion cases of seasonal influenza globally each year, resulting in 3 to 5 million severe cases and 290,000 to 650,000 deaths related to respiratory diseases. In China, influenza is estimated to cause 96,000 to 240,000 excess deaths from influenza-related respiratory diseases annually. Economically, the average outpatient cost per influenza patient is around 768 to 999.9 yuan, while the economic burden for hospitalized cases is approximately 9,832 yuan.1 Therefore, it is crucial to develop effective prevention and control strategies. Studies have confirmed that annual influenza vaccination effectively prevents the disease and severe complications, with high cost-effectiveness.2–5 To effectively control the spread of influenza, the WHO annually recommends vaccination for pregnant women at any stage of pregnancy, children aged 6 months to 5 y, the elderly (≥65 y), individuals with chronic diseases, and healthcare workers.6 China has also issued guidelines to strengthen influenza vaccination, ensure vaccine supply, and promote vaccination among key populations (pregnant women, infants, the elderly, and healthcare workers) to increase vaccination rates and reduce the occurrence of influenza outbreaks.1,7 However, multiple studies have shown that influenza vaccination rates in China remain at a low level.8,9

General practitioners (GPs) play a dual role in influenza prevention and control, as both influencers of public vaccine decisions and individuals at high risk of infection.10 Research indicates that GPs’ influenza vaccination not only reduces their own risk of contracting the influenza but also effectively interrupts the transmission chain of the virus to patients and their families, offering both preventive and health economic benefits.11,12 In China, influenza vaccines are non-immunization program vaccines, and the increase in vaccination rates relies on the willingness of the target population. As a demonstrative group, GPs’ vaccination willingness is a key prerequisite.13 Foreign studies, based on theoretical models such as the Knowledge, Beliefs, Attitudes, and Barriers model, and the Health Belief Model, have systematically explored GPs’ vaccination willingness or behavior and related influencing factors.12,14,15 The results show significant differences in vaccination rates among GPs across different studies, and their perception of influenza disease risk, trust in vaccine safety and efficacy, and information acquisition channels are all significantly related to vaccination willingness, indirectly influencing public behavior. In China, available research has described GPs’ influenza vaccination through cross-group coverage comparisons, or qualitative analyses among mixed community healthcare workers, leaving a gap for theory-based quantitative investigations into the associated factors of GPs’ personal vaccination willingness.16,17

The Reasoned Action Approach (RAA), an important extension of the Theory of Planned Behavior, is widely used to study the correlation between individuals’ beliefs about recommended preventive behaviors and their compliance with these behaviors.18 The theory emphasizes that the intention to perform a behavior is the immediate antecedent of the behavior itself and constructs an integrative model that includes attitudes, subjective norms, and perceived behavioral control (PBC).19 The RAA has a strong evidence base with a meta-analysis estimating that it explains 59% and 31% of the variance in health-related intentions and behavior, respectively.18 Despite this robust theoretical foundation, the RAA has not been applied to examine influenza vaccination willingness among Chinese GPs. This study addresses this gap by employing the RAA as the primary framework, to explore GPs’ influenza vaccination willingness and its influencing factors, with attitudes, subjective norms, and PBC as core constructs. Prior-season vaccination behavior and information-seeking behavior are examined as complementary correlates that extend the model’s explanatory scope, given their established associations with vaccination willingness.12,20 By quantifying the relative contributions of these variables, this study offers a more comprehensive explanatory model than previous descriptive or qualitative investigations, providing preliminary empirical evidence for the development of targeted intervention strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study was conducted from February 11 to March 20, 2025, via the “Wenjuanxing” online platform, a widely used Chinese online survey platform (see Supplementary Material for details), to explore GP’s vaccination willingness and its influencing factors. A convenience sampling method with snowball elements was employed. The survey link was distributed through researchers’ personal networks and social media channels (including WeChat, Weibo, and QQ), utilizing word-of-mouth among friends, colleagues, and professional contacts in China.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) licensed general practitioners currently engaged in general practice or family medicine; (2) registered practice scope in general practice or family medicine; and (3) willingness to participate. The exclusion criteria were: GPs not engaged in direct general practice clinical work (e.g., those working in administrative positions, preventive health departments, or other non-clinical roles).

The sample size was calculated based on 5 to 10 times the number of items, with an allowance for 10% to 20% invalid responses. Considering the requirements for exploratory factor analysis (EFA)(5 times the number of items) and confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) (a minimum of 200 cases), the minimum sample size was determined to be 330.21,22 To ensure methodological rigor and avoid sample bias, the participants were randomly divided into two independent subsamples. Subsample 1 (n = 130) was used for EFA to explore the factor structure, and subsample 2 (n = 200) was used for CFA to validate the model fit. The online questionnaire required complete responses before submission, leaving no missing data; surveys completed in under 5 minutes or of poor quality were excluded.

The survey began with a brief study introduction and informed consent information. Prior to accessing the main questionnaire, participants were presented with screening questions to confirm that they met the inclusion criteria. The informed consent statement indicated that participation was entirely voluntary, with no rewards or extra credits, and required clicking “Agree to Participate” for confirmation. The study adhered to principles of anonymity, privacy protection, and data confidentiality throughout.

Measures

Primary outcome

The primary outcome of this study was vaccination willingness in the next season, defined as GPs’ self-reported willingness to receive the influenza vaccine in the upcoming flu season. Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“Very Unwilling”) to 5 (“Very Willing”).

Associated factors

Demographic information

The study primarily collected personal information from participants, including gender, age, type of workplace, area, region, education level, work experience, presence of chronic diseases, and influenza vaccination in the last season (See Appendix 4 of the Supplementary Material for details.)

Vaccination factors based on the RAA model

This study, grounded in the RAA model and informed by prior research, designed a questionnaire to systematically assess factors influencing GPs’ vaccination willingness across three dimensions: attitude (11 items), subjective norms (5 items), and PBC (7 items). The questionnaire utilized a 5-point likert scale for data collection, ranging from 1 (“Strongly Disagree”) to 5 (“Strongly Agree”) (See Table 2 for details).

To ensure the quality and measurement validity of the questionnaire in this study, we conducted reliability and validity tests on the self – developed questionnaire. For reliability, the internal consistency reliability of the questionnaire was assessed by calculating the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient (CAC). Cronbach’s alpha values were interpreted following George and Mallery (2019): ≥0.9 indicates excellent, 0.8–0.9 good, 0.7–0.8 acceptable, 0.6–0.7 questionable, 0.5–0.6 poor, and <0.5 unacceptable.23 It is noteworthy that values exceeding 0.95 may suggest item redundancy due to insufficient variability. This questionnaire achieved an overall alpha of 0.937 (factors: 0.909–0.933), demonstrating good to excellent consistency without approaching the redundancy threshold (Table S7).

For validity, the structural validity of the questionnaire was verified using the EFA and the CFA on separate subsamples, respectively. The EFA results showed that the dimensions in the questionnaire could be well aggregated into the corresponding factors. The eigenvalues of the three factors were all greater than 1, the cumulative variance explained reached 72.064%, and the factor loadings of all items were above 0.5, preliminarily revealing the underlying factor structure of the questionnaire (see Tables S8, S9, and Figure S2 for details). The CFA results further confirmed the good fit of the three – factor model (X2/df = 1.374 < 3.000, RMSEA = 0.043 < 0.080, IFI, TLI, NFI and CFI all greater than 0.900), indicating that the structure of the questionnaire was consistent with the preset theoretical model and had good validity (see Appendix 2 for details)

Information seeking behavior and sources

To assess the information-seeking behaviors of GPs regarding influenza and vaccines, this study used three structured questions: (1) whether they actively seek information (single-choice question); (2) the specific channels through which they obtain information (multiple-choice question); and (3) the most trusted information channel (single-choice question).

Statistical analysis

Microsoft Excel 2019 (Microsoft Corporation, USA) was used for data tabulation, and SPSS software version 25.0 (IBM Corp., USA) was used for statistical analysis. The dependent variable was the influenza vaccination willingness score (range: 1–5). Independent variables included all sociodemographic characteristics, vaccination factors based on the RAA model, and proactive information-seeking behavior. Descriptive data were presented as frequency, percentage, mean, and standard deviation. Normality was assessed using skewness, kurtosis, and normal Q-Q plots; all continuous variables showed absolute skewness <1 and kurtosis <1, indicating approximate normality. Bivariate analyses were performed using independent samples t-tests, one-way analysis of variance with Tukey-HSD post-hoc tests, and Spearman’s rank correlation to examine associations between variables. Hierarchical multiple linear regression was subsequently employed to examine the independent effects of variables on vaccination willingness, modeled as Y = β0 + ΣβiXi + ε, where Y is the influenza vaccination willingness score, β0 is the intercept, βi are the regression coefficients, X are the independent variables, and ε is the error term. Three models were constructed using the enter method: Model 1 included sociodemographic characteristics, Model 2 added RAA constructs, and Model 3 further incorporated proactive information-seeking behavior. Bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations, bias-corrected and accelerated 95% CI) was applied to ensure robustness under non-normality. Regression assumptions were verified: residual normality was confirmed (histogram and normal P-P plot), no extreme outliers were present (standardized residuals within ±3.6), and multicollinearity was negligible (VIF < 3.2). Full bootstrap results were provided in Table S14. Sensitivity analysis using stepwise regression confirmed the primary findings (see Table S13).

Reliability was assessed by calculating the CAC for the overall questionnaire data. For validity evaluation, EFA was performed using SPSS 25.0 to initially construct the model, followed by using JASP with the diagonally weighted least squares (DWLS) estimator to validate and refine the measurement model.

Quality control

During questionnaire design, screening questions were included to ensure data collection only from the target population of active general practitioners. Before distribution, the survey went through several pretests to make sure the questionnaire was concise, standardized, scientifically rigorous, and logically structured. The system limited responses to once only to prevent duplicates. Three researchers extracted and processed the data separately, excluding invalid or incomplete questionnaires, with a fourth senior author consulted when disagreement occurred.

Sociodemographic and behavioral differences in RAA model among GPs

Significant differences were observed across the three RAA constructs by sociodemographic characteristics and proactive information seeking. Younger and less experienced GPs generally reported lower scores across these constructs. Attitude was significantly associated with age, work experience, and area (F = 9.961, 9.707, and 3.020, respectively), with those in the Central region also reporting lower scores. Subjective norms were associated with age, work experience, type of workplace, and area (F = 7.048, 5.821, 2.438, and 3.123, respectively), as well as prior-season influenza vaccination (t = 2.651), with lower scores among GPs working in general hospitals or the Central region, and those unvaccinated. PBC was associated with age, work experience, and type of workplace (F = 6.595, 4.746, and 3.407, respectively), as well as proactive information-seeking and prior-season vaccination (t = 4.224 and 2.001, respectively), with lower scores among non-seekers, those working in community health service stations, and those unvaccinated (see Tables S1–S4).

The results of the independent samples t-tests indicated that GPs vaccinated in the last season and those who actively sought information had higher vaccination willingness. The results of the one-way ANOVA indicated that GPs who were older and had longer working experience exhibit a higher vaccination willingness. Additionally, post hoc tests revealed that GPs in community health service centers had higher vaccination willingness than those in general hospitals (see Tables 4 and S5). Multivariate analysis of influenza vaccination willingness among GPs

Hierarchical multiple linear regression was conducted to examine associated factors of influenza vaccination willingness (Table 5). The demographic block (Model 1) accounted for 13.7% of variance (R2 = 0.137, adjusted R2 = 0.113, F = 5.636, P < .001). Discussion

This cross-sectional study demonstrated that Chinese GPs exhibited a moderate willingness to receive the influenza vaccination, aligning with a recent meta-analysis (53.6%).13 To our knowledge, this was the first study to systematically apply the RAA to this population, examining both individual cognitive and behavioral factors. Multiple regression analysis confirmed the predictive validity of the RAA and identified two key findings. First, subjective norms showed the strongest association with vaccination willingness, reflecting the profound influence of collectivism and organizational hierarchy on healthcare professionals’ behavior in the Chinese cultural context. Second, type of workplace was significantly associated with vaccination willingness, indicating that the organizational environment plays a pivotal role in vaccination decision-making. Additionally, attitude and past vaccination behavior were significantly associated with willingness. These findings extend the cross-cultural applicability of the RAA in occupational settings and provide preliminary empirical evidence for understanding GP vaccination behavior from both individual and structural perspectives. Furthermore, previous research has shown that sources of information on influenza vaccination are also associated with vaccination willingness.20,24 The following sections discuss the mechanisms underlying these factors to comprehensively elucidate the associated factors of vaccination behavior and inform the development of targeted interventions.

In the context of factors related to the RAA model, the study found that higher scores in attitude and subjective norms were associated with greater vaccination willingness among GPs. This finding aligned with the theory proposed by Ajzen, highlighting that attitude and subjective norms were key determinants of behavioral intention.25 Under the dimension of attitude, GPs’ high perception of their own risk of influenza infection, strong protective awareness for patients and family health, higher perceived efficacy and safety of the vaccine, and a strong sense of professional responsibility were all linked to higher vaccination willingness, which were consistent with previous studies.12,14,26 And some evidence suggested that health education interventions on influenza and the influenza vaccine for healthcare workers significantly increased their knowledge of influenza, as well as their vaccination behavior and willingness.27–29 These findings suggest that attitude formation is amenable to educational interventions targeting risk perception, influenza and vaccine knowledge, and professional identity. In the subjective norms dimension, the perceived social support and expectations from family, colleagues, and healthcare institutions significantly influenced GPs’ vaccination willingness. Multivariate analysis further revealed that subjective norms had a greater impact on vaccination willingness than other related factors. This may be attributed to Chinese collectivist culture, which significantly influences GPs’ vaccination willingness. Previous studies have noted that collectivist cultures emphasized the alignment of individual behavior with group interests, often leading individuals to prioritize group norms and others’ expectations.30 In the context of vaccination, GPs in China may be particularly influenced by the expectations and recommendations of family, colleagues, and their workplace. If family, colleagues, and the workplace collectively expect GPs to be vaccinated to ensure medical safety, or if there is a positive culture of vaccination among colleagues, GPs’ vaccination willingness may significantly increase. This cultural dynamic underscores the potential of norm-based interventions based on social influence. PBC – encompassing confidence in vaccination knowledge, the ability to vaccinate family members or persuade colleagues, and perceptions of cost and accessibility – showed no significant association with willingness. This lack of significance likely reflects that attitude and subjective norms explained the majority of the variance. As a result, despite a relatively high mean PBC score (3.58, out of 5), PBC had limited independent explanatory power in the model. However, this statistical finding does not imply that external barriers are unimportant in practice. Drawing on previous research, factors such as inconvenient vaccination timing, high costs, and lack of organizational support remain pertinent obstacles to immunization, which warrant attention despite their limited statistical contribution in this model.12,24,27

This study revealed that GPs vaccinated in the last season showed higher vaccination willingness, aligning with prior studies.20 This link may stem from habit formation and the boost from positive past experiences to self-efficacy and perceived social norms, as evidenced by their significantly higher PBC and subjective norm scores versus unvaccinated peers (Table S1). The vaccination rate in the study (42.12%) exceeds the 19.2% from a 2019 survey across ten Chinese provinces,31 possibly due to the COVID-19 pandemic enhancing awareness and supported by national policies. However, this rate is still lower than the proportion (66.06%) of GPs who expressed agreement with vaccination in principle, suggesting barriers in converting intention into action. This intention-behavior gap underscores the importance of interventions that address both motivational and volitional phases of behavior change.

This study found that the main sources of information for GPs included colleagues, continuing education courses or conferences, medical journals, and public media. Among these, medical journals were the most trusted source of information for GPs, followed by colleagues and continuing education courses or conferences, which was similar to previous studies.12,26 Previous research also indicated that the source of information affected GPs’ vaccination willingness. Official information sources like the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) could facilitate vaccination behavior, while information from social media might hinder it. This might be because public media controversies over vaccine safety can arouse GPs’ skepticism about vaccines.12,20,32 Workplace type emerged as an independent factor, indicating that organizational context moderates information receptivity and may serve as a channel for targeted messaging.

Implications for policy and practice

This study offers actionable implications for both health policy and clinical practice. At the policy level, first, financial incentives – such as subsidized or free vaccines – could reduce economic barriers, whereas institutional recognition systems (e.g., vaccination badges for healthcare teams) could leverage the strong influence of subjective norms identified in this study. Second, given the pivotal role of prior vaccination behavior, policy frameworks should support annual vaccination campaigns that reinforce habit formation. Third, structural facilitation, including extended vaccination hours, mobile units, and workplace-based delivery, remains essential to address the persistent intention-behavior gap.33–35 In clinical practice, fourth, healthcare institutions should adopt multi-faceted strategies tailored to their organizational settings – leveraging peer influence in community health centers and departmental briefings in general hospitals. Fifth, health education interventions should specifically address GPs’ risk perception, patient protection awareness, and vaccine safety concerns to strengthen positive attitudes. Finally, institutions should actively combat vaccine misinformation on social media while amplifying authoritative guidance from medical journals and professional associations.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the data in this study were collected at a single time point, making it difficult to establish causal relationships between vaccination willingness and influencing factors. Therefore, future studies should adopt cohort or experimental designs with longitudinal analyses to further verify causality. Second, convenience sampling via social media (WeChat, QQ) may have introduced selection bias, potentially overrepresenting digitally literate or pro-vaccination GPs while underrepresenting older or rural practitioners. Although the study sample spanned multiple regions and various levels of medical institutions across China, suggesting a degree of applicability within the Chinese healthcare context, caution should be exercised when generalizing these findings to broader GP populations or healthcare systems outside of China. Future studies should employ multi-center probability sampling and cross-cultural validation to strengthen external validity. Thirdly, while our Spearman’s rank correlation analysis found a significant relationship between attitudes and subjective norms, consistent with prior research, these constructs were often highly correlated in the RAA model, and it is challenging to disentangle the effects of personal and social factors on behavioral intentions.30 Future research could further explore the independent roles and interaction mechanisms of attitudes and subjective norms using longitudinal designs and causal inference methods to understand the formation of behavioral intentions better. Fourth, although we used a validated questionnaire, its reproducibility (e.g., test-retest reliability) was not assessed in this specific population of Chinese GPs. Future research should evaluate the questionnaire’s temporal stability in this target group. Finally, this study relied on self-reporting vaccination willingness and related factors, which may have produced report bias. However, we established the study sample criteria, provided participants with an explanation of the pertinent topics before they started to fill out the questionnaire, and utilized a validated questionnaire to mitigate bias.

Conclusions

This study, based on the RAA model, revealed that higher awareness of personal infection risk, vaccine efficacy and safety, recognition of public health responsibility, perceived positive impact of vaccination on patients, and vaccine recommendations and demonstrations from family members, colleagues, workplaces, and official health institutions all significantly enhanced GPs’ vaccination willingness. Additionally, vaccination behavior in the last season and type of workplace also positively influenced their vaccination willingness. Given GPs’ exemplary role as health promoters, these findings suggest potential directions for future interventions, including strengthening health education for GPs, establishing social norm guidance mechanisms, optimizing vaccine service accessibility, and implementing incentive policy packages to boost their vaccination willingness and positively influence public prevention behaviors.
