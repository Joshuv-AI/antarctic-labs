Preventable Dental Related Emergency Department Visits and Hospital Admissions: A Systematic Review of Economic Burden and Healthcare System Costs
Authors: U. M. G. Sanketha Francis, Jing Jie Lai, Ruvini M. Hettiarachchi, Leah Hobbs, Anura Ariyawardana, J. L. P. Chaminda, Haitham Tuffaha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo systematically evaluate the economic burden of preventable dental‐related emergency department visits (PDEDV) and preventable dental‐related hospital admissions (PDHA), identify reasons for these presentations, and synthesise recommendations to reduce avoidable utilisation.MethodsThis systematic review followed the PRISMA 2020 guidelines. A comprehensive multiple database search was conducted in PubMed, Embase, Cochrane Library, EBSCOHOST, and Web of Science for studies reporting direct and/or indirect costs of PDEDV and PDHA in any age group, published in English from 2000 to March 2026. Three calibrated reviewers screened and critically appraised the identified studies. Data including patient characteristics, estimated charges/costs, reasons for PDEDV and PDHA, and recommendations to minimise the avoidable burden were extracted. Charges/costs were inflation‐adjusted and standardised to 2024 US dollars.ResultsOf the 2600 total studies identified, 25 met the inclusion criteria. The majority were conducted in the United States and focused on direct medical costs. Uninsured individuals, public health insurance enrolees and residents of low‐income areas were more likely to experience PDEDV. Mean charges per any PDEDV ranged from $409.73 to $2740.76. Mean charges per any PDHA ranged from $5234.46 to $62 298.25, while mean hospital costs ranged from $350.42 to $22 375.53. Dental caries was the commonest cause for PDEDV. Odontogenic infections were the costliest to manage, with mean charges of $2740.76 per PDEDV and $62 298.25 per PDHA. PDEDV and PDHA were primarily driven by financial barriers, socio‐demographic disparities, limited access to routine dental care, and health workforce constraints. Proposed strategies focused on improving affordability and access to preventive dental care, enhancing oral health literacy, strengthening integration between medical and dental services, and expanding the dental workforce.ConclusionsPDEDV and PDHA, particularly those due to dental caries and odontogenic infections, impose a significant economic burden on healthcare systems, highlighting the need to reduce financial and access barriers to routine dental care for high‐risk groups and priority conditions.

Introduction

Preventable dental‐related emergency department visits (PDEDV) and Preventable Dental‐related hospital admissions (PDHA) refer to instances where individuals seek emergency or inpatient care for dental conditions that could have been avoided with timely and effective preventive care or early dental treatment [1, 2]. These presentations represent a considerable, yet frequently underestimated, burden on healthcare resources and patients [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]. A systematic review and meta‐analysis reported that a substantial proportion (52.3%–77.9%) of dental visits among individuals aged less than 25 years were PDEDV [6]. Despite showing a declining trend since 2014, the United States (US) recorded 1.8 million emergency department (ED) visits due to non‐traumatic dental conditions, costing $3.4 billion to treat in 2019 [4]. In Australia, around 87 400 PDHA were reported between 2022 and 2023, with an increasing trend over the past decade [1]. Dental caries is the most common cause of PDEDV and PDHA, followed by embedded or impacted teeth [2, 6]. In Western Australia, projections indicate a 43% increase in dental‐related hospital admissions among children aged 14 years or younger between 2000 and 2026 [7].

Many dental diseases can be prevented or effectively managed in primary care settings through timely access to routine assessments, preventive services, and early interventions [8]. However, if left untreated, preventable conditions such as dental caries, periodontal disease, tooth erosion, and impacted teeth may progress to complications requiring PDEDV, PDHA, extensive restorative treatment, or maxillofacial surgery [8]. These services are substantially more costly than primary dental care. For example, a US study reported that inpatient management of dental caries‐related conditions costs up to 10 times more than preventive care [9]. This underscores the broader economic implications and rising costs associated with PDEDV and PDHA [7].

The economic burden of preventable PDEDV and PDHA is multifaceted, encompassing direct medical costs, out‐of‐pocket expenses [10] and indirect costs such as productivity losses and long‐term health consequences [11]. In addition, these presentations place pressure on limited healthcare resources that could be better allocated to acute and life‐threatening conditions [6]. Overcrowding of EDs has also been associated with reduced quality of care and increased risks of medical errors, morbidity, and mortality [12].

Although systematic reviews and meta‐analyses have examined disease burden, epidemiology and utilisation patterns of PDEDV and PDHA, their economic burden has been insufficiently evaluated [2, 6]. Existing empirical studies report costs across diverse settings, time periods, currencies, and cost measures, limiting comparability [13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Despite the availability of empirical evidence from individual studies, there is a need to comprehensively synthesise evidence on costs, underlying reasons, and preventative strategies to inform policy, improve access to dental care, and reduce PDEDV and PDHA. Therefore, the aim of this review was to systematically evaluate the economic burden of PDEDV and PDHA, identify reported reasons for these presentations, and synthesise reported recommendations to reduce avoidable utilisation.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [21] (Table S7) and registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO ID: CRD42024613148) [22].

Search Strategy

Multiple databases (PubMed, Embase, Cochrane, EBSCOHOST and Web of Science) were searched using the keywords ‘Preventable Dental Conditions’, ‘Emergency Department’, ‘Hospitalisation’ and ‘Economic burden’ with appropriate MeSH terms and Boolean operators (‘AND’/‘OR’) where relevant. The search terms used to identify preventable hospital presentations were adapted from a previously published systematic review by Acharya et al. [2], with additional terms incorporated to enhance comprehensiveness while maintaining methodological consistency with existing literature. The detailed search terms and Population, Intervention, Comparator and Outcome (PICO) framework that guided the search are provided in Tables S1 and S2.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Studies were included if they were published in English and reported the economic burden of PDEDV and PDHA in any age group. Studies published between January 2000 and March 2026 were included to ensure relevance to contemporary healthcare systems, coding practices, and cost structures, as studies prior to 2000 are less comparable due to substantial changes in health service delivery and economic reporting.

Each ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 code used was assessed to identify preventable dental conditions using ‘potentially preventable hospitalisation codes’ published by the Australian Institute of Health and Welfare [23]. Studies that included both preventable and non‐preventable conditions were eligible if subgroup analyses allowed extraction of preventable cases or if additional case verification was performed through chart reviews.

Studies reporting direct costs, indirect costs or both were included. Direct medical costs refer to expenditures related to diagnosis, treatment, and management of a condition (e.g., hospital stays, physician services, medications and diagnostic tests) [24]. Direct non‐medical costs include expenses incurred as a consequence of receiving care (e.g., transport, accommodation, meals and childcare) [24]. These are typically borne out of pocket by patients and families. Indirect costs represent productivity losses due to illness, disability, premature death or caregiving responsibilities [24]. These costs arise because of the health conditions' impact on an individual's ability to work or perform normal activities. Cost perspectives included payer, provider, or societal.

Conference abstracts, case reports, letters, comments, editorials, reviews, conceptual or opinion papers were excluded. Studies focusing solely on traumatic dental injuries or conducted outside clinical settings (e.g., community‐based studies) were also excluded. Studies were further excluded if costs could not be isolated for preventable conditions or if only relative cost comparisons (without absolute cost estimates) were reported.

Study Screening and Selection

Search results were exported to Covidence, a web‐based software for screening studies [25]. After duplicate removal, a blinded, multi‐stage screening process was undertaken. Title and abstract screening were conducted independently by two calibrated reviewers (J.J.L. and U.M.G.S.F.), followed by full‐text review. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, with a third reviewer (R.M.H.) involved to reach consensus. The study selection process is presented in Figure 1 (PRISMA flow diagram).

PRISMA flow diagram.

Data Extraction and Data Analysis

Data were extracted independently by U.M.G.S.F. and J.J.L. into a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Extracted variables included study characteristics (authors, year, country, study design, duration), population characteristics, ICD codes and disease categories, cost perspectives (payer, provider or societal), cost type (direct or indirect), cost estimates (total or per‐episode and dispersion), currency and base year, and data sources. Reasons for PDEDV and PDHA and recommendations to reduce avoidable utilisation were extracted from the discussion sections of included studies.

Data were summarised descriptively using Microsoft Excel. To avoid introducing bias, costs and charges were not standardised or pooled but synthesised separately. Where terminology was used interchangeably, classification was guided by the data source and context. All charges and costs were converted to 2024 US dollars using the web‐based EPPI‐Centre cost conversion tool, applying country‐specific inflation indices and exchange rates to adjust costs to a common price year [26, 27]. Where the base year was not reported, the final year of the study period was assumed.

Results

The database search identified 2600 studies. After removal of duplicates, titles and abstracts of 2071 articles were screened to select 137 studies for full text review. Of these, most were excluded due to inclusion of non‐preventable dental conditions without subgroup analyses to isolate preventable cases, and/or because cost data were not reported as absolute estimates (e.g., presented only in relative or comparative terms), resulting in 25 studies meeting the inclusion criteria (Figure 1).

Study Characteristics

A summary of the included studies and their characteristics is presented in Table 1 and Table S3. Most studies (n = 22, 88%) were conducted in the United States. All included studies were retrospective observational in design, comprising five cohort studies, two cross‐sectional studies, one case series, one audit and one trend analysis (Table S3). ICD‐9 and ICD‐10 discharge diagnosis codes were the most commonly used methods for case identification (n = 18, 72%), with four studies additionally incorporating patient chart review for case verification (Table S4) [8, 17, 28, 29]. Dental caries, odontogenic infections (including dental abscesses and pulpal and periapical disorders), and gingival and periodontal diseases were among the most common indications for PDEDV and PDHA (Table S3). Most studies reported direct medical costs (hospital charges or costs) associated with PDEDV and PDHA, while only one Australian study reported indirect costs (Table S3) [30].

Patient Characteristics

Patients presenting with PDEDV and PDHA were predominantly from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds. Most individuals were uninsured or enrolled in public health insurance schemes such as Medicaid (Medicaid is a publicly funded US Health Insurance Program for eligible low‐income individuals and families, with coverage and eligibility varying by state) or Medicare (Medicare is a publicly funded national US Health Insurance Program primarily for aged 65 years or over and certain younger individuals with disabilities, with limited dental coverage) [13, 17, 18, 29, 31, 32, 33, 34]. Commonly affected groups included children, adolescents, and young adults, as well as individuals residing in low‐income households or dental health professional shortage areas [13, 14, 30]. Ethnic and geographic disparities were also reported in several studies [18, 35]. Notably, three studies observed that the majority of PDEDV occurred on weekdays, when preventive dental services are typically available [16, 33, 34].

Economic Burden of PDEDV

All 16 studies reporting charges for PDEDV were conducted in the United States. These studies reported ED charges from a payer perspective, including total charges (n = 14) and mean (n = 14) and/or median charges (n = 3) per PDEDV (Table 1 and Table S5). Total charges widely ranged from $0.24 million to $4391.6 million [34, 36], with lower estimates derived from small, localised datasets and higher estimates reflecting national‐level analyses, as well as indicating differences in study periods and disease conditions included (Table S5). Mean charges per any PDEDV ranged from $409.73 to $2740.76 [36, 37], while median charges ranged from $385.06 to $1231.06 [14, 29].

Table 2 presents PDEDV charges by disease category. Where multiple dental conditions were assessed, dental caries was the most common condition associated with PDEDV, followed by periapical abscesses (Table S3) [13, 29, 32, 37]. Across all age groups, mean charges for dental caries ranged from $569.03 to $645.60 [29, 33], while median charges ranged from $417.21 to $797.07 [14, 29]. One study reported that mean charges for dental caries were substantially higher in children ($998) compared with adults ($535), representing nearly a twofold difference [33]. Odontogenic infections, including periapical abscesses, were associated with the highest mean ED charges, ranging from $629.93 to $2740.76 per ED visit [29, 37].

Economic Burden of PDHA

The review included 16 studies reporting costs of PDHA. Of these, 13 were conducted in the United States, with one study each from Australia, New Zealand and China (Table S3) [19, 30, 40]. All studies reported costs from a payer perspective. Direct medical costs were presented using a range of indicators, including total hospitalisation charges (n = 8), total hospital costs (n = 4), mean charges (n = 9), mean costs (n = 6), median charges (n = 2), and median costs (n = 1) per PDHA (Table 1 and Table S6). Special Dental Benefit (SDB) revenue or cost claims and Medicaid allowed charges were used as proxy measures for hospital costs [19, 41].

Estimated costs of PDHA are summarised in Table S6. Total hospitalisation charges ranged from approximately $0.49 million to $7.45 billion [28, 34], whereas total hospitalisation costs ranged from approximately $97 924.95 to $1.59 million [17, 30]. This variation was attributable to differences in data aggregation, costing methods, study periods, countries and disease categories included (Table S6) [8, 34]. Mean charges per PDHA ranged from approximately $5234.46 to $62 298.25 [8, 20], while mean hospitalisation costs ranged from approximately $350.42 to $22 375.53 [17, 19]. Only one Australian study reported both direct and indirect costs, examining admissions for dental pain and sepsis requiring general anaesthesia [30]. The mean total cost per case was $1262.98, comprising direct costs of $484.39 and indirect costs of $777.78, with indirect costs approximately 1.6 times higher than direct costs (Table S6) [30].

Table 3 presents costs by disease category. Hospitalisation costs demonstrated a clear gradient by disease severity, with odontogenic infections incurring the highest mean charges (up to $62 298) [20], while dental caries ($9420.67–$28 712.95) [28, 35], pulpal conditions ($21 310.78–$23 561.25) [18, 35], and periodontal diseases ($22 081.17–$22 801.68) [18, 35] were associated with lower but still substantial costs. Overall, charges consistently exceeded cost‐based estimates by several‐fold.

Reasons for PDEDV and PDHA Discussed in Included Studies

Retrospective nature of all studies prevented establishment of causal relationships. However, probable reasons for PDEDV and PDHA discussed in the studies were summarised in Table 4. The included studies consistently identified multifactorial drivers of PDEDV and PDHA, broadly encompassing financial, socio‐demographic, health service and workforce‐related factors. Key contributors included financial barriers to routine dental care, such as high out‐of‐pocket costs, lack of dental insurance and limited public coverage, particularly for adults [8, 18, 30, 32, 34, 35]. Socio‐demographic disparities, including low socioeconomic status, poor oral health literacy and inequities affecting vulnerable populations (e.g., uninsured and minority groups), further exacerbated delayed care‐seeking [14, 19, 29, 32]. In addition, limited access to primary dental services, geographical barriers, and restricted provider availability contributed to reliance on EDs, which are often more accessible [13, 30, 32]. Workforce‐related issues, including low participation of dentists in publicly funded programmes and limited dental expertise within ED settings, also played a significant role [19, 29].

Recommendations to Reduce the PDEDV and PDHA Discussed in Included Studies

Recommendations discussed in the included studies are summarised in Table 4. The studies proposed a range of strategies targeting systemic, behavioural and service‐level gaps. Key recommendations included improving affordability and access to preventive dental care, such as expanding insurance coverage, subsidising care for low‐income populations, and integrating comprehensive dental benefits into public programmes [8, 13, 14, 33]. Enhancing oral health literacy and preventive behaviours, particularly among high‐risk groups and children, was also emphasised [13, 19, 32, 33, 34]. At the service level, studies recommended strengthening integration between medical and dental care, including establishing referral pathways, incorporating dental professionals within ED settings, and expanding community‐based services such as mobile and school dental programmes [13, 14, 16, 19, 28, 32]. Workforce‐focused strategies included increasing dentist participation in public insurance schemes, expanding the dental workforce (including mid‐level providers), and improving training for medical practitioners in managing dental conditions [8, 13, 14, 16, 29, 35].

Discussion

This study represents, to our knowledge, the first systematic review to comprehensively synthesise the economic burden, underlying drivers, and potential policy responses related to PDEDV and PDHA. By integrating evidence on both costs/charges and contributory factors across multiple health system contexts, this review provides a unified, policy‐relevant perspective on a fragmented body of literature. Importantly, it extends beyond prior descriptive work by linking economic burden with system‐level failures in access, prevention, and service integration, thereby offering actionable insights for healthcare planning and reform.

Several key insights emerge from these findings. PDEDV and PDHA impose a substantial and highly variable economic burden on healthcare systems, with wide ranges in both total and per‐episode costs and charges. Mean ED charges and hospitalisation costs were considerable, particularly for more severe conditions such as odontogenic infections, which consistently incurred the highest costs [8, 14, 17, 20, 29, 30, 34, 37, 42]. Given that dental caries, periodontal diseases and odontogenic infections, including periapical abscesses, are largely preventable and common, prevention has significant potential to substantially reduce this economic burden [14, 19, 28, 29, 35, 38, 40, 41].

The clear cost gradient by disease severity with advanced odontogenic infections driving disproportionately high hospital costs underscores the economic value of early intervention and prevention. Second, the observation that charges exceed true costs by several‐fold highlights systemic pricing distortions, particularly in systems reliant on payer‐based reimbursement models [20]. This has implications for interpreting economic burden and for designing equitable funding models. Third, the substantial variation across studies reflects differences in costing methodologies, aggregation levels and health system structures, indicating a lack of standardisation that limits comparability and policy translation.

From a policy perspective, these findings strongly support a shift from reactive, hospital‐based care to preventive, community‐based and value‐based dental care models [8, 14, 31, 33, 36, 37, 39, 41]. Investments in early diagnosis and management of dental conditions, particularly caries and pulpal disease, are likely to yield significant cost savings by avoiding progression to severe infections requiring hospitalisation [8, 13, 16, 17, 20, 34]. Furthermore, the evidence suggests that integrating oral health into broader health financing frameworks, including public insurance schemes, could reduce inefficient ED utilisation and improve system sustainability [14, 31, 36].

The review highlights that PDEDV and PDHA are driven by complex, interrelated determinants including financial barriers, socioeconomic disadvantages, limited access to primary dental care and workforce constraints [8, 31, 36, 37, 39, 40]. These findings underscore that preventable dental use of acute care reflects broader health system inequities rather than isolated clinical issues. High out‐of‐pocket costs and inadequate insurance coverage emerged as key drivers, particularly among disadvantaged populations, compounded by geographic maldistribution of the workforce and limited participation in publicly funded services [8, 14, 16, 19, 29, 31, 36]. The persistence of ED presentations during weekdays further indicates that barriers extend beyond availability to affordability, awareness and care‐seeking behaviours.

Policy responses must adopt a coordinated, system‐level approach. Expanding dental coverage and targeted subsidies is critical to improving equitable access [14, 33]. Strengthening integration between medical and dental services through embedded dental roles, referral pathways, and co‐located care can reduce inappropriate ED utilisation [13, 14, 16, 19, 32]. Prioritising prevention and oral health promotion, particularly among high‐risk groups, is essential to curb disease progression [8, 13, 19, 20, 30, 32, 33, 38, 39]. Workforce strategies, including greater engagement in public programmes and expanded roles for mid‐level providers (e.g., dental and oral health therapists), can address service gaps. In parallel, community‐based models such as mobile clinics, school‐based programmes, and outreach services offer scalable solutions for underserved populations. Collectively, these strategies support a shift towards equity‐focused, prevention‐oriented systems that integrate oral health within primary care and broader public health frameworks.

Several important limitations within the included studies must be acknowledged. First, the evidence base is heavily skewed towards the United States, with 88% of studies conducted in this setting. This limits the generalisability of findings to other healthcare systems, particularly those with different financing structures, levels of public coverage and service delivery models. Second, there is a lack of primary research from non‐US settings, with only a small number of studies from Australia, New Zealand and China. This represents a critical gap, particularly given the global relevance of oral health inequities and the need for context‐specific policy solutions. Third, most studies reported direct medical costs only, with very limited consideration of indirect costs such as lost productivity, caregiver burden and broader societal impacts. The single Australian study that included indirect costs demonstrated that these may exceed direct costs, suggesting that the overall economic burden is likely substantially underestimated. Fourth, the absence of standardised costing methodologies and reporting indicators makes cross‐study comparison challenging and limits the ability to synthesise pooled estimates. This heterogeneity reduces the precision of economic conclusions and complicates policy translation. Although several studies included paediatric populations, substantial heterogeneity in age definitions, settings and methodologies, along with the predominance of mixed‐age cohorts, limited meaningful comparison of cost patterns and unmet needs between children and adults. Finally, reliance on ICD‐based case identification introduces potential misclassification bias, particularly in settings where dental expertise within hospitals is limited. Coding inaccuracies, combined with variable use of chart review for validation, may affect data quality and the accurate identification of preventable cases.

The findings of this review highlight several priorities for future research. There is a clear need for high‐quality primary studies in diverse health system contexts, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries and publicly funded health systems such as Australia. Such research should aim to generate context‐specific evidence to inform local policy and resource allocation. Future studies should also incorporate comprehensive cost evaluations, including both direct and indirect costs, to better capture the true societal burden of PDEDV and PDHA. Standardisation of costing methodologies, including clear differentiation between costs and charges and consistent reporting of key indicators, is essential to improve comparability. Finally, improving data quality and case identification methods, including validation of ICD coding and incorporation of clinical verification, will enhance the reliability of future studies.

Conclusions

Overall, this review underscores that PDEDV and PDHA represent a preventable and inequitable burden on healthcare systems, and addressing this challenge requires coordinated, policy‐driven approaches that prioritise prevention, access, and integration of oral health within broader health systems.
