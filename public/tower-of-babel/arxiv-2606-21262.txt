ARCO: Adaptive Rubric with Co-Evolution for Multi-Step LLM-Based Agents
Zihang Tian, Jingsen Zhang, Rui Li, Xiaohe Bo, Yuanzi Li, Xu Chen

Abstract
Reinforcement learning for multi-step LLM agents often relies on scalar rewards that indicate success but cannot explain why a trajectory is good or bad. Rubric-based rewards improve interpretability through natural-language criteria, but existing methods score at the trajectory level and freeze the scorer behind a closed-source judge, leaving step-level credit assignment unresolved and the judge itself static. We propose ARCO ( A daptive R ubric CO -evolution), a rubric framework in which a same-scale model μ \mu shares a backbone with two heads: a generation head that produces per-step criteria, and a score head that predicts rubric-conditioned step-level rewards. A trajectory decomposition constraint ties the sum of step rewards to the terminal outcome, enabling credit assignment without step-level labels, while μ \mu and the policy π \pi are jointly updated on on-policy data so that the rubric content and the scoring function co-evolve at the parameter level. Across HotpotQA, 2WikiMultiHopQA, and MuSiQue with two open-source backbones, ARCO improves the best EM in every setting over strong outcome-, rubric-, and process-reward baselines, and analyses show that its rubrics are step-specific, robust to design choices, and useful for diagnosing agent behavior. Codes and data are available at https://github.com/zihangtian/ARCO .

Introduction

Reinforcement learning (RL) has become a standard approach for improving multi-step LLM-based agents on tasks such as multi-hop QA and tool use
37
;
15
;
14
. Most methods train with black-box scalar rewards—either trajectory-level outcome rewards or step-level process rewards—that indicate quality but cannot explain
why
a trajectory is good or bad
19
;
16
;
31
. This opacity invites reward hacking and limits the granularity of credit assignment across steps
4
.

Rubric-based rewards offer a more interpretable alternative by grounding evaluation in explicit natural-language criteria
7
;
8
;
22
;
17
. Yet existing rubric methods share two limitations. First, they all operate at the trajectory level: a single rubric scores the entire rollout, leaving the credit-assignment problem at individual steps untouched. Second, they rely on a frozen closed-source LLM (e.g., GPT-4) as the external judge—whether the rubric is static
7
, generated dynamically per trajectory
8
, or evolved through an on-policy buffer
22
, only the rubric
text
may change while the judge’s parameters never update.

Consider a multi-hop question:
In what year was the university where Sergei Tokarev was a professor founded?
A black-box reward assigns the same score to a trajectory that luckily guesses “1755” and one that methodically searches for Tokarev’s university before looking up its founding year. A static rubric might check “did the agent search?”—but once the agent learns to search, its errors shift to subtler modes: targeting the wrong entity, redundant retrieval, or premature answers. A frozen judge cannot adapt its scoring to these evolving failure modes, regardless of how the rubric text is updated.

We argue that an effective reward model for multi-step agents should be (i)
rubric-based
, providing interpretable criteria rather than opaque scalars; (ii)
step-level
, assigning per-action scores rather than a single trajectory score; and (iii)
co-evolutionary
, updating both the evaluation criteria and the scoring function alongside the policy at the parameter level. We propose
ARCO
(
A
daptive
R
ubric
CO
-evolution), a self-contained rubric model
\mu
that shares a backbone between a
rubric generation head
producing per-step natural-language criteria and a
score head
predicting rubric-conditioned step rewards. Both
\mu
and the policy
\pi
are updated jointly on on-policy data, so the evaluation criteria and scoring function co-evolve with the agent’s improving behavior—using the same-scale open-source model, with no external judge.

Our main contributions are:

•

We introduce
ARCO

step-level
rubric-based process reward framework in which both the natural-language rubric and the scoring function co-evolve with the policy at the parameter level, removing the dependence on a frozen closed-source judge.

Preliminary

A multi-step LLM-based agent solves a task
q\in\mathcal{Q}
by sequentially selecting actions
a_{t}\sim\pi(\cdot\mid h_{t})
given a trajectory prefix
h_{t}=(q,a_{1},o_{1},\ldots,a_{t-1},o_{t-1})
, producing a trajectory
\tau
with binary reward
R(\tau)\in\{0,1\}
. Rubric-based process supervision augments scalar scoring with natural-language criteria: at each step, a generator
\rho
produces evaluation criteria and a scorer
g
predicts a reward conditioned on them:

r_{t}=\rho(h_{t},a_{t}),\qquad s_{t}=g(h_{t},a_{t},r_{t}).

(1)

Existing methods differ in three design axes.
Rubric adaptation
: criteria may be static (fixed before training)
7
;
17
, dynamically generated per trajectory but with a frozen generator
8
, or evolving at the content level via on-policy buffer management
22
—yet in all cases the scoring function’s parameters remain frozen, and criteria are typically shared across all steps.
Scoring granularity
:
g
usually produces a single trajectory-level score
R(\tau)
rather than per-step scores
s_{t}

8
;
7
.
Judge model
: existing rubric-based methods rely on closed-source LLMs (e.g. GPT) as external judges, preventing the scorer from co-evolving with the policy.

ARCO addresses all three axes:
\rho
generates context-specific criteria
per step
using a same-scale model,
g
predicts
step-level
scores conditioned on those criteria, and both
\rho
and
g
are
co-evolved with
\pi
at the parameter level
on on-policy data throughout training.

3
ARCO

We consider a multi-step LLM-based agent solving instances
q\in\mathcal{Q}
. At step
t
, the policy
\pi
observes a trajectory prefix
h_{t}{=}(q,a_{1},o_{1},\ldots,a_{t-1},o_{t-1})
and selects an action
a_{t}{\sim}\pi(\cdot\mid h_{t})
. The environment returns observations for tool actions such as retrieval, and the trajectory
\tau
receives a binary outcome reward
R(\tau)\in\{0,1\}
after the agent finishes or reaches the horizon. ARCO turns this sparse signal into interpretable process supervision by attaching a natural-language rubric
r_{t}
and a scalar score
s_{t}
to every action. Unlike pipelines with a frozen rubric writer or judge, ARCO instantiates both rubric generation and scoring inside one trainable model
\mu
that co-evolves with the policy
\pi
.

3.1
Hierarchical Rubric Model

The rubric model
\mu
uses one causal LM backbone
f_{\theta}
with two lightweight heads, separating
what should be checked
from
how well the action satisfies those checks
. The hierarchy is motivated by the fact that step quality is structured and operation-dependent: in multi-hop QA, a search may fail by wrong-entity targeting, broad querying, or redundancy, while a finish may fail by missing support or wrong format. A flat scalar PRM must compress these heterogeneous failure modes into one implicit value; ARCO instead writes a local checklist for the current prefix-action pair and scores the action along that checklist, exposing the evaluator’s latent dimensions as readable criteria and narrowing the score head’s prediction problem to a small set of concrete checks.

Concretely, the LM head acts as the high-level rubric generator: given
(q,h_{t},a_{t})
, it generates an ordered rubric
r_{t}{=}(c_{t,1},\ldots,c_{t,K}){\sim}p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q,h_{t},a_{t})
, where each criterion names one aspect of action quality. Because criteria are generated after observing the trajectory prefix, the rubric can adapt across stages: early searches can be judged by entity targeting and evidence coverage, later searches by whether they resolve the remaining hop, and finish actions by answer support and format. The score head acts as the low-level evaluator: it reads the same state-action context together with the generated checklist and converts those semantic checks into numeric supervision. We feed the full scoring sequence
(q,h_{t},a_{t},r_{t})
through the shared backbone, obtain
\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{t}
by attention-mask mean pooling over non-padding hidden states, and apply the score head
g_{\phi}
to produce
K
criterion-level scores,

\mathbf{d}_{t}=g_{\phi}(\bar{\mathbf{h}}_{t})\in[-1,1]^{K},\qquad s_{t}=\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}d_{t,j}.

(2)

Sharing the backbone matters beyond parameter efficiency: with separate models, the writer and scorer would communicate only through generated text and the scorer would have to reinterpret criteria that may drift from its own representation. With shared parameters, the criteria are written in the same representation space used for scoring, and gradients from the decomposition objective shape the features used by both stages—so improvements to one stage transfer to the other through the shared backbone, while the rubric text itself is regularized by the KL term in
\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{rl}}
(Eq.
5
).

3.2
Co-Evolutionary Training

We perform a supervised warmup before RL: a closed-source LLM teacher (accessed via API) collects trajectories and step rubrics;
\pi
is supervised-fine-tuning (SFT) initialized on the trajectories, and
\mu
is initialized by a text-LM loss on the annotated criteria plus a mean-squared error (MSE) on projected criterion scores so that the trajectory-decomposition constraint holds at warmup (Appendix
B
). A frozen copy of
f_{\theta}
serves as
\mu_{\mathrm{ref}}
for KL regularization on rubric text.

Training alternates between on-policy rollouts and model updates. Each active step is annotated by the current
\mu
, producing
\{(r_{t},\mathbf{d}_{t},s_{t})\}_{t=1}^{T}
for the same behavior that updates
\pi
. After a short sparse phase using only
R(\tau)
, the policy is trained with reward-to-go advantages from rubric scores:

\mathcal{L}_{\pi}^{\mathrm{rl}}=-\frac{\alpha}{|\mathcal{B}|}\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{B}}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{V}_{\tau}}\bigl(G_{\tau,t}-\bar{G}_{\mathcal{B},b(\tau,t)}\bigr)\log p_{\pi}(a_{t}\mid h_{t})+\lambda_{\pi}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\pi},\qquad G_{\tau,t}=\sum_{k=t}^{T_{\tau}}s_{\tau,k},

(3)

where
\mathcal{V}_{\tau}
collects valid policy steps,
G_{\tau,t}
is the reward-to-go from step
t
inclusive,
\alpha
scales the dense reward, and
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KL}}^{\pi}
is a token-level KL between
\pi
and its frozen SFT reference
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
. Instead of a global mean, ARCO uses a position-bucketed baseline:
b(\tau,t)\in\{\texttt{search\_1},\ldots,\texttt{terminal}\}
buckets each step by search rank or as terminal (the trajectory’s last step, whether successful
Finish
or forced finish at budget exhaustion), and

\bar{G}_{\mathcal{B},b}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}_{b}|}\sum_{(\tau^{\prime},u)\in\mathcal{I}_{b}}G_{\tau^{\prime},u},\qquad\mathcal{I}_{b}=\{(\tau^{\prime},u):\tau^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B},\,u\in\mathcal{V}_{\tau^{\prime}},\,b(\tau^{\prime},u)=b\}.

(4)

The rubric model is updated on the same on-policy data using the terminal outcome as a trajectory-level constraint:

\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{\mathrm{rl}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{B}|}\!\sum_{\tau\in\mathcal{B}}\!\Bigl(\!\sum_{t=1}^{T}\!s_{t}-R(\tau)\Bigr)^{\!2}+\beta\,\mathbb{E}_{(h_{t},a_{t})}\!\Bigl[\sum_{i=1}^{|r_{t}|}\!\mathrm{KL}\bigl(p_{\theta}\,\|\,p_{\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}}\bigr)_{\!i}\Bigr],

(5)

where the KL is taken at each rubric token, and the shorthand
\mathrm{KL}(p_{\theta}\|p_{\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}})_{i}
stands for the per-token divergence
\mathrm{KL}\bigl(p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid r_{t}^{<i},h_{t},a_{t})\,\|\,p_{\theta_{\mathrm{ref}}}(\cdot\mid r_{t}^{<i},h_{t},a_{t})\bigr)
, with
i
indexing tokens of the flattened rubric sequence (not criteria). The decomposition term learns step scores without gold step labels; the KL term keeps generated rubrics close to coherent warmup language. Because
\pi
and
\mu
share rollouts, policy improvements expose new failure modes to the evaluator, and the evolving evaluator immediately reshapes the dense rewards used by the policy.

4
Experiments

We evaluate ARCO on three multi-hop QA benchmarks across two open-source backbone families. We organize the analysis around five research questions:

•

RQ1.
Does ARCO outperform existing ORM, rubric ORM, and PRM baselines?

•

RQ2.
Is each of ARCO’s design choices individually necessary?

•

RQ3.
Do ARCO’s rubrics actually point at the action they scored?

•

RQ4.
How sensitive is ARCO to the number of rubric criteria
K
?

•

RQ5.
How do rubric-model architecture and scale affect policy learning?

4.1
Experimental Setup

Datasets.

We evaluate on three multi-hop QA benchmarks of increasing reasoning complexity:
HotpotQA

35
(2-hop),
2WikiMultiHopQA

9
(multi-hop comparison/bridge), and
MuSiQue

26
(compositional, 2–4 hops). For each dataset we use
2{,}000
training and
500
evaluation examples and report Exact Match (EM) and token-level F1.

Agent environment.

Following prior work
1
;
25
;
36
;
14
, we adopt a unified multi-turn retrieval-augmented QA environment. At each step, the agent selects
Search[query]
(top-
1
passage from a SimCSE retriever
5
) or
Finish[answer]
. The search budget is
3
for HotpotQA and 2WikiMultiHopQA and
6
for MuSiQue; the environment returns binary EM as
R(\tau)\in\{0,1\}
. Full interaction protocol, including forced-finish handling and rubric scoring, is given in Algorithm
1
of Appendix
A
.

Implementation details.

We instantiate ARCO with two same-scale open-source backbones, Qwen3-4B-Instruct-2507
34
and Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct
6
(referred to hereafter as Qwen3-4B and Llama-3.2-3B); in each setting
\pi
and
\mu
share the same base model and are adapted with LoRA
11
. The rubric model uses a dual-head design: an LM head that auto-regressively generates
K{=}3
criteria per step, and a score head that maps the mean-pooled hidden states of
(q,h_{t},a_{t},r_{t})
to
K
scores in
[-1,1]
whose mean is the step reward. The supervised warmup stage uses GPT-annotated trajectories and per-step rubrics under a trajectory-level decomposition constraint. Subsequent RL training follows a sparse-to-dense schedule: a few sparse epochs train
\pi
with
R(\tau)
alone so that
\mu
catches up to fresh rollouts, after which
\pi
is trained with reward-to-go advantages from rubric scores using a position-bucketed baseline, while
\mu
is updated by a trajectory-decomposition MSE plus KL to its warmup reference. For each (method, dataset, backbone) cell we report EM/F1 at the best post-transition dev-EM checkpoint. Dense-stage wall-clock time is in Appendix
C
and full hyperparameters in Appendix
B
.

Baselines.

We compare ARCO against a comprehensive set of baselines organized by reward granularity. We first include a
Base Model
that uses zero-shot prompting without fine-tuning as a reference point, and then compare against two groups of trained baselines.
Group I (Outcome Reward Models, ORMs)
:
Search-R1

14
and
R1-Searcher

24
train with binary EM; three rubric-based ORMs score
at the trajectory level
with a frozen closed-source judge but differ in rubric source:
RaR

7
pre-generates a query-specific rubric and keeps it fixed;
CARMO

8
generates context-aware criteria at scoring time conditioned on the query and candidate trajectory;
RLER

22
maintains an on-policy rubric buffer with frozen judge parameters.
Group II (Process Reward Models, PRMs)
:
AgentPRM

31
estimates step values via temporal-difference plus generalized-advantage-estimation (TD+GAE) and is frozen during RL.
ARCO
(ours) generates per-step rubric criteria and predicts rubric-conditioned step scores with a same-scale open-source
\mu
that co-evolves with
\pi
.

6
Limitations

ARCO trains the rubric model with relatively lightweight objectives. The trajectory decomposition loss only ties the sum of step scores to the terminal outcome, and rubric text is shaped primarily by warmup imitation and KL regularization; richer process supervision or rubric-consistency losses could yield sharper per-step credit. The pace of
\pi
–
\mu
co-evolution is also set by fixed learning rates and regularization weights, leaving adaptive update ratios and drift-aware scheduling to future work. Finally, training both
\pi
and
\mu
and invoking
\mu
at every active step makes ARCO more expensive than single-model RL, and this overhead grows with horizon length; distillation, rubric caching, or asynchronous
\mu
updates are promising ways to reduce it. These limitations point in the same direction: tighter, more adaptive coupling between the policy and an interpretable evaluator, without giving up the cost-efficiency of an open-source same-scale design.

7
Conclusion

We presented ARCO, an adaptive rubric co-evolution framework for multi-step language agents with interpretable step-level process rewards. ARCO uses a hierarchical rubric model with a shared backbone to generate state-action-specific criteria and score actions under those criteria, while trajectory decomposition lets the evaluator and policy be jointly optimized from terminal outcomes. Because rubric generation, scoring, and policy improvement update the same backbone on the same on-policy rollouts, ARCO turns sparse outcome rewards into a dense, readable, step-aware signal without any external judge. Across multi-hop QA benchmarks and backbones, ARCO improves over outcome- and process-reward baselines, and analyses show that its rubrics are step-specific, robust to design choices, and useful for diagnosing agent behavior. We hope ARCO offers a foundation for adaptive, interpretable, process-aware reward modeling for language agents.

Appendix B
Warmup Stage Details

ARCO uses a supervised warmup before on-policy co-evolution so that both the policy and the rubric model start from a working initialization. We collect warmup records by rolling out an API policy on training questions in the same retrieval environment used for RL. For each question we sample multiple trajectories and log every action, observation, parse status, search budget, and terminal reward, then annotate the complete trajectory with a strong rubric model. HotpotQA and 2Wiki use a search horizon of three steps, MuSiQue uses six; the terminal reward is the task EM reward used by the main experiments.

Policy warmup.

The policy SFT dataset is built only from high-reward trajectories, filtered by a minimum reward threshold (
1.0
in our main runs) and converted into one supervised example per step. The input contains the question, the trajectory prefix before the current action, and the remaining search budget; when the budget is exhausted and the dataset supports forced finishing, the prompt switches to a finish-only template. The target is the recorded action string, e.g.,
Search[query]
or
Finish[answer]
, which initializes
\pi
to imitate successful tool-use traces rather than all sampled behavior.

Rubric warmup.

The rubric annotator sees the full sampled trajectory and returns, for each step,
K
natural-language criteria and
K
criterion scores. Scores are parsed, clipped to
[-1,1]
, padded or truncated to width
K
, and projected so that the summed step-score means satisfy the trajectory-level decomposition constraint,

\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{1}{K}\sum_{j=1}^{K}\hat{s}_{t,j}=R(\tau).

(6)

The projection preserves relative score preferences while making the warmup data compatible with the later decomposition loss. The trajectory-level prompt is shown in Appendix
E
. Before
\mu
SFT, records are filtered by the same constraint with a small tolerance, and steps with empty criteria are dropped.

The rubric model
\mu
is then trained with a dual objective: the LM head learns to generate the criterion JSON for each state-action pair, while the score head regresses the annotated criterion scores from the mean-pooled hidden representation of the full scoring sequence. The SFT loss is

\mathcal{L}_{\mu}^{\text{sft}}=\underbrace{-\sum_{t=1}^{T}\log p_{\theta}(\tilde{r}_{t}\mid q,h_{t},a_{t})}_{\text{rubric text loss}}+\lambda\underbrace{\sum_{t=1}^{T}\lVert\mathbf{d}_{t}-\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{t}\rVert_{2}^{2}}_{\text{criterion-score loss}},

(7)

where
\tilde{r}_{t}
is the annotated criterion list,
\hat{\mathbf{s}}_{t}\in[-1,1]^{K}
the projected score vector, and
\mathbf{d}_{t}
the score-head prediction. The default
K{=}3
; ablations reuse the same pipeline but either remove the rubric text target or replace it with a fixed rubric.

After warmup, the selected policy checkpoint initializes
\pi
, and the selected rubric checkpoint initializes both the evolving
\mu
and the frozen reference
\mu_{\mathrm{ref}}
used for KL regularization during RL. On MuSiQue, the best

These failures are informative: the rubrics are not nonsensical but describe plausible evaluation dimensions while omitting the contrastive details needed to identify the exact action. These cases suggest a concrete direction for improving rubric generation: step-level rubrics should be more action-discriminative, capturing the characteristic features of the intended action and contrasting it with nearby alternatives. For search steps, criteria should explicitly mention the target entity, relation, or missing evidence item that distinguishes the gold query from previous or alternative searches; for finish steps, they should clearly encode answer readiness and action type, stating that the step is judged as a final submission rather than another evidence-gathering action. Contrastive rubric training with same-trajectory negatives, or prompting the rubric generator to name the distinguishing entity or relation, may therefore improve step binding.
