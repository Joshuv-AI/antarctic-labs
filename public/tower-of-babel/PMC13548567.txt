Effect of age, sex, and BMI on serum olanzapine levels and their correlation with metabolic parameters and serum pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in schizophrenia patients
Authors: Wenfan Gao, Xian He, Yayun Xu, Feng Shan, Jun Liang, Qingrong Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveOlanzapine is an atypical antipsychotic drug which is effective in the treatment of schizophrenia (SCZ). Olanzapine exhibits significant pharmacokinetic variability and is associated with a higher propensity for metabolic abnormalities. Recent evidence indicates that the immune system may play a role in antipsychotic-induced metabolic abnormalities through antipsychotic-induced modifications of cytokine levels. This study examined the effect of age, sex, and BMI on serum olanzapine levels and their correlation with metabolic parameters and serum pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in SCZ patients.MethodsA cohort of 104 SCZ patients receiving olanzapine treatment was recruited retrospectively for the investigation. Based on gender, age, and body mass index (BMI), the patients were divided into two groups each. Following a 4-week olanzapine treatment, the serum olanzapine concentrations and concentration/dose (C/D) ratios were compared between the two groups. The associations between the age, sex, and BMI with the serum olanzapine concentrations and C/D ratios were examined. Additionally, the relationship between the metabolic parameters and serum pro-inflammatory cytokine levels with the serum olanzapine concentrations and C/D ratios was analyzed.ResultsFemale patients exhibited higher serum olanzapine concentrations (t = -3.523, P = 0.001) and C/D ratios (t = -2.816, P = 0.006) compared to male patients. Moreover, the gender exhibited a positive correlation with the serum olanzapine concentrations (r = 0.329, P = 0.001) and C/D ratios (r = 0.269, P = 0.006). The BMI exhibited a negative correlation with the serum olanzapine concentrations (r = -0.205, P = 0.037), but not the C/D ratios (r = -0.185, P = 0.060). Furthermore, no significant relationship was observed between the metabolic parameters with the serum olanzapine concentrations (P > 0.05). Additionally, the serum olanzapine concentrations were negatively correlated with the serum IL-1β (r = -0.530, P < 0.001), IL-6 (r = -0.446, P < 0.001), and TNF-α (r = -0.464, P < 0.001).ConclusionThe patients’ gender and BMI might affect the serum olanzapine concentrations. The serum olanzapine concentrations were negatively correlated with the serum IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α levels, but not with metabolic parameters during a short-term use of olanzapine in Chinese patients with SCZ.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12888-026-08322-4.Keywords: Schizophrenia, Olanzapine, Pro-inflammatory cytokine, Metabolic parameters, Gender, Age, Body mass index

Introduction

Schizophrenia (SCZ) is a debilitating neuropsychiatric disorder that affects over 21 million individuals globally [1, 2]. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, as of 2019, China had the highest number of SCZ cases, with 5.5 million individuals affected [3]. The latest epidemiological survey conducted in China indicates that the weighted lifetime prevalence of SCZ is 0.7% [4]. SCZ is characterized by a high prevalence, recurrence, and disability rate, imposing a significant burden on patients, their families, and society [5]. Second-generation antipsychotics (SGAs) are currently the cornerstone of SCZ treatment, offering several advantages over first-generation antipsychotics (FGAs) in addressing positive, negative, cognitive, and affective symptoms, as well as a reduced risk of extrapyramidal side effects [6]. Nevertheless, the propensity of SGAs to induce weight gain and disrupt glucose and lipid metabolism significantly constrains their clinical application and constitutes a major factor contributing to suboptimal medication adherence among patients [7, 8]. Simultaneously, these adverse reactions elevate the risk of metabolic syndrome, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease, thereby exacerbating the disease burden on patients [9, 10].

Olanzapine is among the most frequently utilized SGAs for the treatment of SCZ, and numerous clinical guidelines have relegated olanzapine to a second-line treatment option [11]. Olanzapine is notably associated with metabolic adverse effects, including substantial weight gain and disruptions in glucose and lipid metabolism [12]. There is sufficient evidence that the therapeutic response and metabolic dysfunction to olanzapine has a significant relationship with the drug’s steady-state plasma concentration [13–15]. Due to the significant inter-patient variability observed in blood concentrations of olanzapine [16], therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) of olanzapine levels may be beneficial for guiding treatment, enhancing therapeutic efficacy, and mitigating adverse effects. The Arbeitsgemeinschaft für Neuropsychopharmakologie und Pharmakopsychiatrie Consensus Guidelines categorize the recommendation for TDM of olanzapine as level 1, indicating a strong recommendation [17].

Emerging evidence suggests that key intrinsic and extrinsic factors influencing olanzapine pharmacokinetics include sex, age, body weight, smoking history, alcohol consumption, and concomitant medication use [18–21]. Nonetheless, previous studies have yielded inconsistent findings regarding these factors. Moreover, most research has been conducted in Western populations, with limited data available from Asian populations, particularly the Chinese population. For instance, research indicates that plasma concentrations of olanzapine are significantly higher in female patients compared to male patients in Caucasian [22]. Additionally, a study conducted in Switzerland reported a 27% increase in dose-adjusted olanzapine concentrations in patients over 60 years of age compared to younger individuals [23]. However, another study conducted in Germany found that age and sex did not affect the dose-corrected plasma levels [24]. The discrepancies in these findings may be attributable to variations in the racial composition of the study populations. Thus, the present study investigated the effect of age, sex, and BMI on serum olanzapine levels in Chinese patients with SCZ.

Weight gain and metabolic syndrome represent significant adverse effects associated with olanzapine therapy. Olanzapine is among the medications with a high likelihood of inducing weight gain and metabolic syndrome [25, 26]. In individuals diagnosed with SCZ, olanzapine may result in an average weight increase of 3.3 kg within the first eight weeks of treatment [27]. Aside from clozapine, olanzapine is linked to more severe metabolic adverse events compared to other SGAs [28]. It has been reported that olanzapine significantly elevates triglyceride levels after 8 and 12 weeks of administration [29]. Another study identified a significant elevation in total cholesterol and triglycerides associated with olanzapine, compared to placebo [30]. Preliminary evidence indicates a dose-response relationship between olanzapine plasma concentrations and metabolic outcomes [31]. However, there is a paucity of research examining the potential association between blood concentrations of olanzapine metabolites and metabolic outcomes, with existing studies involving a limited cohort of subjects [32]. Consequently, the current study explored the relationship between metabolic parameters and serum olanzapine concentrations, as well as C/D ratios.

Recent studies indicate that the immune system may partially contribute to metabolic abnormalities induced by antipsychotic medications, particularly through alterations in cytokine levels induced by these drugs [33]. Specifically, antipsychotic agents have been observed to modulate cytokine expression, ultimately downregulating neuroinflammatory signatures [34, 35]. An increase in inflammatory cytokines is associated with anorexigenic responses, whereas diminished inflammation has been independently linked to alterations in feeding behaviors and other metabolic parameters, potentially leading to obesity [36–38]. Evidence suggests that olanzapine reduces levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [39, 40]. To the best of our knowledge, no study has yet examined the relationship between serum olanzapine concentration and pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in patients with SCZ.

Given the substantial pharmacokinetic variability of olanzapine and its association with an increased risk of metabolic abnormalities, together with the involvement of immune system in the antipsychotic-induced metabolic abnormalities, this study aims to (1) investigate the influence of age, sex, and body mass index (BMI) on serum olanzapine levels; and (2) explore the relationship between metabolic parameters and serum pro-inflammatory cytokine levels with both serum olanzapine concentrations and C/D ratios in Chinese patients diagnosed with SCZ.

Methods

Subjects

In this retrospective study, a cohort of 104 patients clinically diagnosed with SCZ was recruited at Hefei Fourth People’s Hospital between December 2023 and October 2024. The inclusion criteria for participation in the study were as follows: (1) fulfillment of the diagnostic criteria for SCZ as specified in the International Classification of Mental and Behavioral Disorders (ICD-10); (2) absence of antipsychotic medication treatment in the 3 months preceding hospitalization; (3) age between 18 and 65 years; (4) Han Chinese ethnicity; (5) administration of olanzapine treatment for 4 weeks following hospitalization; (6) serum olanzapine concentrations and metabolic parameters were assessed following a 4-week treatment period; and (7) serum samples from patients were collected in the biological sample library of Hefei Fourth People’s Hospital. The study excluded participants based on the following criteria: (1) a history of substance abuse or dependence, including drugs, alcohol, or other psychoactive substances; (2) a history of serious suicide attempts; (3) the presence of significant physical illnesses, such as major cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases, hypertension, pulmonary diseases, thyroid disorders, diabetes, and epilepsy; and (4) pregnancy or lactation. The requirement for informed patient consent was waived by the research committee due to the retrospective nature of the study design. Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Hefei Fourth People’s Hospital (approval number: HFSY-IRB-YJ-KYXM-GWF (2024-027-001)). Clinical trial number: not applicable. The study was designed in strict accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines.

Demographic and clinical data collection

Patient medical records for individuals with SCZ were accessed via the doctor workstation system, and relevant information was collected, including: (1) demographic data such as age, sex, height, weight, and disease diagnosis; and (2) laboratory examination including serum olanzapine levels and parameters of biochemical laboratory tests, which comprised triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), and glucose levels.

Medication regimen

Upon admission, patients diagnosed with SCZ were initiated on olanzapine (Haosen Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., China) therapy, beginning at a dosage of 5 mg/day. This dosage was gradually titrated to a range of 10–20 mg/day, tailored to the individual patient’s clinical condition, and maintained for a duration of 4 weeks.

Detection of serum olanzapine concentrations

In this study, steady-state serum concentrations of olanzapine were assessed following 4 weeks of oral administration. Blood samples were collected from the participants via venipuncture between 6:00 and 7:00 a.m. after an overnight fast. The samples were drawn into dry tubes that contained no additives. Subsequently, the blood samples were subjected to centrifugation at 3,000 rpm for 5 min at a temperature of 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected and designated as the serum sample. The concentration of olanzapine in the serum was quantified using a US Waters-TQS UPLC-MS/MS mass spectrometer.

Measurement of serum cytokines

Seven serum cytokines, specifically IL-1β, IL-2, IL-6, IL-8, IL-12, TNF-α, and interferon-γ (IFN-γ), were measured using multiplex bead immunoassays (LXSAHM-10 and LXSAHM-27, R&D Systems for Antibody Detection, Shanghai Universal Biotech Co., Ltd.) in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocols. Standard curves were generated using the reference cytokine sample provided in the kit, which enabled the determination of cytokine concentrations in serum samples. All values were within the calibration curve range.

Sample calculation

The sample size for this study was determined using G*Power software version 3.1.9.6. For an independent t-test (one-tailed) with a medium effect size of 0.5, a significance level of 0.05, and a power of 0.80, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 53 for each group, for a total of 106 participants.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS software (Version 17.0; SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The Kolmogorov-Smirnov one-sample test was employed to assess the normality of data distributions. For variables exhibiting a normal distribution, comparisons between the two groups were performed using the independent samples t-test. Conversely, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied to compare variables that did not follow a normal distribution. Analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was performed to compare serum olanzapine concentrations and C/D ratios between different groups (sex, age subgroups, BMI subgroups), with age, sex, and BMI as mutually adjusted covariates. Correlation analyses were conducted using the Spearman correlation and Pearson correlation test. Partial correlation was used to explore the relationship between metabolic parameters/serum pro-inflammatory cytokine levels with serum olanzapine concentrations and C/D ratios while controlling for age, gender, and BMI. A two-tailed P-value of less than 0.05 was considered indicative of statistical significance.

Patients were further stratified by age using three commonly adopted cut-off values for sensitivity verification. Initially, participants were divided into those aged < 40 years (n = 58) and ≥ 40 years (n = 46). No significant intergroup differences were observed in serum olanzapine concentrations (t = 1.621, P = 0.108) or C/D ratios (t = 0.117, P = 0.907; Fig. 1C-D). After adjusting for sex and BMI, these differences remained non-significant for both olanzapine concentrations (F = 3.527, P = 0.063) and C/D ratios (F = 0.024, P = 0.877). Additional sensitivity analyses were performed using alternative age thresholds. Stratification by age < 35 years (n = 51) versus ≥ 35 years (n = 53) revealed no significant differences in serum olanzapine concentrations (t = 1.179, P = 0.241) or C/D ratios (t = 0.362, P = 0.718). Similarly, grouping according to age < 45 years (n = 66) and ≥ 45 years (n = 38) also showed no significant between-group differences in olanzapine concentrations (t = 0.479, P = 0.633) or C/D ratios (t = -0.798, P = 0.426). Multiple cut-off stratifications consistently confirmed that age grouping did not affect serum olanzapine levels or C/D ratios in the present cohort.

Based on BMI, patients were divided into two groups: BMI < 23 kg/m2 (n = 66) and BMI ≥ 23 kg/m2 (n = 38). No significant differences in serum olanzapine concentrations (t = 1.909, P = 0.059) and C/D ratios (t = 0.772, P = 0.442) were found between patients with BMI < 23 kg/m2 and patients ≥ 23 kg/m2 (Fig. 1E-F). After adjusting for age and sex, there were still no significant differences in serum olanzapine concentrations (F = 2.132, P = 0.147) or C/D ratios (F = 0.320, P = 0.573) between the two BMI subgroups.

In addition, smoking status was collected as part of the demographic data of the study population. Among the 104 enrolled schizophrenia patients, 9 cases (8.65%) were smokers, and 95 cases (91.35%) were non-smokers. Subgroup analysis was performed to compare serum olanzapine concentrations and C/D ratios between smokers and non-smokers. The results showed that there was no significant difference in serum olanzapine concentrations (t = 1.008, P = 0.316) and C/D ratios (t = 1.609, P = 0.111) between the two groups.

Discussion

The primary aim of this study was to investigate the influence of age, sex, and BMI on serum olanzapine levels and their association with metabolic parameters and serum pro-inflammatory cytokine levels in patients with SCZ. The analysis yielded five principal findings. Firstly, female patients demonstrated higher serum olanzapine concentrations and C/D ratios compared to their male counterparts. Secondly, gender was positively correlated with serum olanzapine concentrations and C/D ratios. Thirdly, a negative correlation was observed between BMI and serum olanzapine concentrations. Fourthly, no significant association was found between metabolic parameters and either serum olanzapine concentrations or C/D ratios. Fifthly, the serum olanzapine concentrations were negatively correlated with serum levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α.

Numerous studies have explored the influence of gender on serum olanzapine concentrations, consistently demonstrating that female patients exhibit higher serum olanzapine concentrations compared to their male counterparts [41–44]. Moreover, it has been established that male patients require higher doses of olanzapine than female patients to attain therapeutic serum concentrations [41]. In the current study, female patients consistently demonstrated elevated serum olanzapine concentrations and C/D ratios compared to their male counterparts. Correlational analysis revealed a positive association between gender and both serum olanzapine concentrations and C/D ratios. Although the precise mechanism underlying these observations remains unclear, we hypothesize that it may be attributable to several factors: (1) Olanzapine is primarily metabolized by the cytochrome P450 1A2 (CYP1A2) enzyme [45]. It is documented that drug metabolism via CYP1A2 isoenzymes occurs at a slower rate in women than in men [46]. (2) Women typically possess a lower lean body mass compared to men. Given that olanzapine exhibits a substantial volume of distribution, it may accumulate within adipose tissue [39]. (3) The extended elimination half-life and reduced plasma clearance observed in women may further elucidate our findings [40].

Age-related alterations in pharmacokinetics have been documented, as the processes of drug absorption, distribution, metabolism, and excretion undergo changes with advancing age. The primary mechanism underlying the increased dose-adjusted drug concentrations observed in the elderly population is likely attributable to a decrease in hepatic clearance, which may result from diminished intrinsic hepatic metabolic function, decreased liver volume, and/or reduced hepatic blood flow, potentially attributable to factors such as impaired cardiac output [47]. However, existing literature regarding the impact of age on serum olanzapine concentrations presents inconsistencies. Notably, it has been documented that for every 5-year increment in age beyond 40 years, dose-adjusted concentrations increased by 1.7% in males and 1.3% in females [48]. Another study identified an approximate 27% increase in the dose-adjusted concentration among patients over 60 years of age compared to younger individuals [49]. However, both our study and another investigation have demonstrated that the median concentration-to-dose (C/D) ratio of olanzapine in patients over 40 years of age does not significantly exceed that of patients under 40 years [50]. The discrepancies in these findings may be attributable to variations in racial demographics and sample sizes.

Physiological changes associated with obesity, including increased tissue volume and perfusion, altered blood protein concentrations, and changes in tissue composition, can significantly influence a drug’s volume of distribution [51, 52]. Consequently, higher drug doses may be required for obese patients to achieve therapeutic blood concentrations [53]. Overweight and obesity were classified using Asian-specific BMI thresholds, with overweight defined as a BMI of ≥ 23 kg/m² and obesity defined as a BMI of ≥ 27.5 kg/m² [54]. Accordingly, in the current study, participants were categorized into two groups: those with a BMI of < 23 kg/m² and those with a BMI of ≥ 23 kg/m². While no significant differences were observed in serum olanzapine concentrations and concentration-to-dose (C/D) ratios between patients with a BMI of less than 23 kg/m² and those with a BMI of 23 kg/m² or greater, a negative correlation was identified between BMI and serum olanzapine concentrations, as well as an almost significant correlation with C/D ratios (P = 0.060). These findings indicate a relationship between BMI and blood olanzapine concentrations, suggesting that dosage adjustments based on BMI may be necessary to achieve optimal therapeutic drug levels.

Numerous studies have investigated the correlations between serum or plasma concentrations of olanzapine and metabolic abnormalities [55–58]. It has been reported that olanzapine concentration is linked to alterations in weight, BMI, and LDL levels in drug-naive individuals, as well as to changes in glucose, triglyceride, and LDL levels in chronic patients who have not previously been exposed to olanzapine [12]. Moreover, elevated C/D ratios of olanzapine have been associated with increased LDL levels in patients with SCZ undergoing olanzapine treatment [57]. Furthermore, a ratio of olanzapine concentration to N-desmethyl-olanzapine concentration greater than 6.03 has been identified as a positive predictor of metabolic syndrome [58]. However, the current study did not find a significant association between metabolic parameters and serum olanzapine concentrations or C/D ratios. The discrepancy between the findings of this study and those of previous research may be attributed to the smaller cohort size and shorter treatment duration employed in our investigation. In the study conducted by Kang et al., the relationship between olanzapine concentration and metabolic dysfunction was examined following an 8-week course of olanzapine monotherapy [12]. In contrast, the current study implemented a 4-week treatment period to assess the the relationship between metabolic parameters and both serum olanzapine concentrations and C/D ratios in Chinese patients with SCZ. The timeframe was chosen in accordance with existing literature, which suggests that the majority of therapeutic responses to antipsychotic medications, especially in instances of first-episode psychosis, generally manifest within four weeks [59, 60], and early responses observed beyond this period are regarded as indicative of long-term outcomes [61].

Several studies suggest that antipsychotic drugs possess immunomodulatory properties, particularly exhibiting anti-inflammatory effects, which may contribute to their clinical efficacy [62]. Long-term administration of antipsychotics has predominantly been shown to enhance the expression of anti-inflammatory mediators while concurrently reducing pro-inflammatory markers in both the peripheral and central nervous systems [63, 64]. Consistent with these findings, the present study observed a negative correlation between serum olanzapine concentrations and serum levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. The modulation of cytokine systems by antipsychotics may have secondary effects on normal adiposity signaling by disrupting the release of inflammatory cytokines [33]. If inflammatory cytokine levels are suppressed, the ability to convey information regarding long-term energy reserves may be compromised. This disruption can hinder hypothalamic circuits from achieving energy homeostasis through anorexigenic responses, potentially resulting in the persistent accumulation of adiposity, as evidenced by weight gain and/or obesity [33]. Moreover, SGA exhibit more potent anti-inflammatory properties compared to first-generation antipsychotics (FGAs), which supports data trends consistently indicating that SGAs have a greater propensity to induce metabolic abnormalities than FGA treatments [63, 65]. In the current study, a significant correlation was identified between serum olanzapine concentrations and serum levels of IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α, although no such relationship was observed with metabolic parameters during short-term olanzapine administration in Chinese patients with SCZ. Considering the established link between serum olanzapine concentrations and an elevated risk of metabolic abnormalities, alongside the role of the immune system in olanzapine-induced metabolic disturbances, it is plausible to hypothesize that olanzapine may exert an earlier influence on pro-inflammatory cytokine levels than on metabolic parameters. Multicentric studies are essential to substantiate the association between metabolic parameters, serum pro-inflammatory cytokine levels, and serum olanzapine concentrations, and longitudinal studies are necessary to investigate the causal relationships involved.

This study is subject to certain limitations. Firstly, it is a single-center investigation with a relatively small sample size, which may introduce sampling bias. Secondly, N-desmethyl-olanzapine, a metabolite of olanzapine processed by CYP1A2, typically accounts for approximately 30% of the olanzapine concentration in the blood of patients undergoing chronic therapy with the drug [66]. However, this study did not include an assessment of serum levels of N-desmethyl-olanzapine. Thirdly, the investigation into the relationship between metabolic parameters and serum olanzapine concentrations was conducted only following a 4-week treatment period, with no assessment of this relationship after long-term treatment (8 weeks). Fourthly, only a small number of smokers were included in the present cohort (9 out of 104 patients), and the unbalanced distribution of smoking status reduced the statistical power to fully explore the influence of CYP1A2-mediated smoking induction on olanzapine pharmacokinetics.

In conclusion, the present study found that the patients’ gender and BMI might affect the serum olanzapine concentrations. Given the observed interindividual variability in serum olanzapine concentrations, it is suggested using TDM to monitor the serum olanzapine concentrations in addition to a thorough clinical follow-up. The study also found that the serum olanzapine concentrations were negatively correlated with the serum IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α levels, but not with metabolic parameters during a short-term use of olanzapine in Chinese patients with SCZ. Given that olanzapine-induced metabolic dysfunction is a significant concern for its clinical use, it is imperative to investigate the relationship between metabolic parameters and serum olanzapine concentrations following long-term treatment in a larger cohort of Chinese patients with SCZ.
