Age-related changes in saccade behavior
Authors: Hatice Eraslan Boz, Işıl Yağmur Tüfekci, Koray Koçoğlu, Müge Akkoyun, Merve Ekin, Gülden Akdal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSome studies have highlighted the impact of age on the saccade properties. This study aimed to investigate whether prosaccade and antisaccade metrics vary with age. The participant groups included 49 young adults, 25 middle-aged adults, and 31 older adults. Participants were instructed to look at the stimulus in the prosaccade task and to look in the opposite direction for 1000 milliseconds (ms) in the antisaccade task. Both prosaccade and antisaccade latencies were prolonged in older and middle-aged adults compared to younger adults. The rates of correct antisaccades were lower in older and middle-aged adults than in younger adults and lower in older adults than in middle-aged adults. Regression analyses showed that prosaccade latencies increased by more than 1 ms per year, and antisaccade latencies increased by more than 2 ms per year. These findings suggest a linear association between age and saccade latency throughout adulthood. In addition, antisaccade measures, particularly correct antisaccade rates, appeared to be more sensitive to age-related differences in oculomotor and inhibitory control than prosaccade measures were.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56741-w.Keywords: Aging, Prosaccade, Antisaccade, Saccades, Cognitive function, Eye movementsSubject terms: Neuroscience, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Eye movements are essential for exploring the environment and understanding our world. The eyes perform hundreds of movements daily1. Eye movements that focus on a stimulus in the visual field are called fixations, while quick and sudden shifts of gaze between stimuli are known as saccades. For example, when a sudden stimulus or movement occurs, people automatically look in that direction (prosaccade). The recognition of this sudden stimulus is important for guiding behavior. In environments with distracting stimuli, a person may need to intentionally look away from the distractor (antisaccade). Because this involves suppressing automatic responses, the inhibitory control mechanism becomes active. This is crucial for carrying out goal-oriented behaviors1,3. As prosaccades depend on stimuli, they rely on bottom-up processing to respond correctly to environmental changes. Antisaccades, which involve the suppression of reflexive gaze, are classified as voluntary saccades2. In the antisaccade task, participants are instructed to look in the opposite direction of the target stimulus. Because internal factors trigger antisaccades, they are associated with top-down mechanisms1,3. Intrinsic or extrinsic attentional processes trigger saccades. The appearance of an external stimulus activates a conscious attentional mechanism, shifting the focus from the current point to the new stimulus1. Prosaccades are linked to visual attention, whereas antisaccades demand greater cognitive effort3. Voluntarily preventing gaze from a stimulus when it appears requires inhibitory control, and gazing in the opposite direction of the stimulus also involves working memory. Prosaccade and antisaccade tasks are linked to fronto-parietal and frontal-striatal mechanisms3. Saccade planning, motor and sensory processes, and saccade initiation are regulated by the frontal eye field (FEF), supplementary eye field (SEF), dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), and superior colliculus (SC)4–7.

The aging process causes a gradual decline in oculomotor control, resulting in changes in saccadic characteristics8–11. Age affects voluntary eye movement control and top-down regulation3. Additionally, changes in saccade performance may reflect age-related cognitive changes that are not captured by traditional neuropsychological tests12,13. Therefore, studying age-related changes in saccade performance may have practical implications. Saccadic changes can be measured quickly, objectively, and noninvasively, potentially providing insights into age-related cognitive changes12. However, the impact of aging on prosaccade performance has been inconsistent findings in the literature. Some studies have reported longer prosaccade latencies in older individuals than in younger individuals14–18. However, one study found that prosaccade latency was not affected by age19. Older adults have been found to have increased antisaccade latency compared to younger adults18. Additionally, it has been shown that older adults make more errors and require more corrections than younger adults in antisaccades17,20. In another study, saccade performance was assessed by dividing elderly individuals aged 60–85 into 4-year age groups21. It has been reported that older individuals are slow to initiate saccades and make more errors. Furthermore, age significantly influenced the antisaccade task, whereas it did not influence the prosaccade task. Most of these studies overlook middle-aged individuals, especially those aged 40–59, focusing only on young and elderly groups10,12–15. This study examined age-related changes in saccadic performance among adults aged 18 years and older. Moreover, latency follows an asymmetrical U-shaped pattern from childhood to old age (age range 5–79)8. The study observed longer latencies in prosaccades and antisaccades during childhood, shorter latencies in adulthood, and again longer latencies in older adult subjects. A recent cross-sectional study of participants aged 5–93 years examined lifelong changes in prosaccades and antisaccades by treating age as a continuous variable10. The results indicated that prosaccade and antisaccade latencies were longer in children, and by age 15, these latencies reached adult levels. It was also been reported that prosaccade and antisaccade latencies gradually increase from the third to the ninth decade of life10. These studies offer valuable insights into the developmental changes in saccades8,10. A previous study reported that, in a large sample of 9- to 88-year-olds, prosaccade and antisaccade reaction times showed a curvilinear pattern up to age 27 and generally increased with age22. Considering the literature discussed above, we aimed to explore age-related changes in prosaccade and antisaccade properties, particularly the differences between adulthood and old age. Most previous studies have focused primarily on young and older adults, often excluding middle-aged people. In addition, previous lifespan studies, including those involving children and adolescents, have reported U-shaped developmental trajectories of saccade latency8,10,22. Because the present study focused exclusively on adults aged 18 years and older, we primarily expected gradual age-related increases in latency across adulthood rather than developmental U-shaped effects spanning the entire life span. Based on the literature, we a priori hypothesized that prosaccade latency would gradually increase with age (younger < middle-aged < older). Second, we expected antisaccade latency to increase and accuracy to decrease with age. Based on previous studies reporting a gradual increase in saccade latency from adulthood to old age, we examined exploratory linear and quadratic associations between age (treated as a continuous variable) and the saccade latency.

Methods

Participants

The individuals were enrolled at the Balance and Eye Movements Recording Laboratory of the Institute of Health Sciences, Department of Neurosciences, Dokuz Eylül University, Izmir, Türkiye, from 2020 to 2022. This study included 105 participants. They were divided into three groups based on the age categories proposed by Neugarten and Hagestead23: young adults aged 18–30 years, middle-aged adults aged 31–59 years, and older adults aged 60–79 years. Accordingly, the final analysis included 49 young adults, 25 middle-aged adults, and 31 older adults.

Participants had to be at least 18 years old, have no neurological or psychiatric conditions, and demonstrate normal cognitive performance according to the Turkish standardized neuropsychological criteria. They also needed to have normal or corrected vision. Each participant underwent a neurological evaluation at the Neurology Clinic of Dokuz Eylul University Hospital, including assessments of awareness, alertness, cranial nerves, motor system, reflexes, sensory system, coordination, and balance. Extensive neuropsychological assessments have been conducted in middle-aged and elderly adults to confirm cognitive health and identify cognitive impairments, including mild cognitive impairment and dementia. Furthermore, the likelihood of cognitive impairment in young adults is very low24. Participants who scored below the age- and education-adjusted cognitive norms were excluded. The Yesavage Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS) was used to assess depressive symptoms in older adults because it demonstrates better sensitivity and specificity in the elderly population25–27. Young and middle-aged adults completed the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI)28,29. Participants with scores exceeding the cut-off thresholds (GDS > 18; BDI > 9) were excluded because higher scores might adversely affect cognitive function30. Participants who did not meet the cognitive or affective criteria were excluded from the eye movement recordings.

Neuropsychological evaluations were administered to middle-aged and older participants, comprising the Mini Mental State Examination31,32, Digit Span, Trail Making Test33–35, Öktem Verbal Memory Processes Test36, Rey Complex Figure Test37–41, Fluency test encompassing semantic and phonemic components42, Wisconsin Card Sorting Test43–45, Stroop Test45,46, and Benton Face Recognition Test41,45,47,48. The means, standard deviations, and Bonferroni-corrected post hoc p-values for neuropsychological tests in middle-aged and older adults are shown in Supplementary Table 1.

All participants provided informed consent, both verbally and in writing. The study was approved by the Dokuz Eylul University Ethics Committee (protocol number: 2020/04–43). This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Eye movement recordings

Eye movements were recorded using an EyeLink 1000 Plus (SR Research, Mississauga, Ontario, Canada). The sampling rate was set to 1000 Hz. The screen resolution was 1920 × 1080 pixels. The refresh rate was 60 Hz, and the screen response time was four milliseconds (ms). Saccade tasks and stimuli were designed using the Experimental Builder within the EyeLink software package. Recordings were obtained from the right eye. Only the right eye was recorded, following the default setting of the EyeLink System to prevent interocular variability.

The participants sat in adjustable chairs in a comfortable position. The viewing distance was fixed at 52 cm from the screen. A height-adjustable head support was used to prevent head movements (SR Research, Mississauga, Ontario, Canada). The recordings were performed in a quiet, dimly lit room.

Prosaccade and antisaccade tasks

The visual angle of the target stimulus was 0.5º. The stimuli were light gray dots presented on a black background. Before each saccade task, a standard 9-point calibration and validation procedure was performed. Calibration involves capturing fixation samples at nine evenly spaced target points to map the raw saccade data to the gaze position. If the participants did not complete the calibration successfully, they were recalibrated. After calibration, validation was performed by presenting the same target points and measuring the difference between the fixation positions and targets. If the participant did not pass the validation, the calibration and validation processes were repeated.

After four practice trials, the participants performed the prosaccade (32 trials) and the antisaccade (64 trials) tasks. During practice, the instructions were displayed on a screen and read aloud by the experimenter. Participants who could not complete the practice trials were allowed to repeat them up to two times each. The 10º stimulus eccentricity was presented horizontally. The presentation of stimuli (right or left) was randomly assigned (Fig. 1).

The prosaccade paradigm was presented above, and the antisaccade paradigm was presented below. The red circle was not visible to participants and was intended solely to indicate the expected correct response for the task.

In each task, a fixation point (a white circle) was illuminated for 1000 ms, followed by a blank screen (GAP condition) for 200 ms, and the stimulus was displayed for 1000 ms. The trial ended if the participant made a correct saccade during each saccade task. If a correct response was not made within 1000 ms, the trial was terminated using an on-screen warning. The prosaccade task required participants to direct their gaze toward stimuli that appeared suddenly. In the antisaccade task, participants were instructed to make an eye movement to the location opposite that of a suddenly presented stimulus49,50.

After four practice trials, the prosaccade task consisted of 64 trials. In 32 trials, the stimulus eccentricity was 5°, whereas in the other 32, it was 10°. Additionally, following four practice trials in the antisaccade task, the stimulus eccentricity was 10°, with 64 trials in total. Because eye parameters such as latency and amplitude can be influenced by stimulus eccentricity in both tasks, this study only included the 32 trials with a 10° stimulus in the prosaccade task. The results of the 32 prosaccade trials at 5° eccentricity are presented in Supplementary Table 2.

Data analysis

Saccade responses were analyzed using Data Viewer software on EyeLink 1000 Plus. The correct saccade rate, latency, peak velocity, and amplitude were assessed in the prosaccade task. A saccade was considered correct if the participant gazed at the stimulus within 1000 ms. Latency refers to the time between the appearance of the stimulus and the initiation of the saccade. The peak velocity was defined as the maximum velocity attained between the onset and offset of the saccades. Latency was measured in milliseconds, and peak velocity was measured in degrees per second. The amplitude indicates the size of the saccade and is measured in degrees.

The rates of correct, anticipatory, express, corrected saccades, and uncorrected errors, as well as the latency, amplitude, and peak velocity, were calculated in the antisaccade task. A correct saccade in the antisaccade task was defined as the participant gazing in the opposite direction from the stimulus within 1000 ms. After the antisaccade stimulus appeared, a small-amplitude saccade occurred toward the stimulus within 80–100 ms. If a saccade occurred in the opposite direction from the stimulus, it was recorded as a corrected saccade. Small saccades to the right or left performed within 80 ms of stimulus onset were considered anticipatory. Express saccades were defined as small saccades performed between 80 and 130 ms after the stimulus presentation. The correct saccade rate was calculated as the percentage of trials in which the saccades were correct. The rates of anticipatory saccades, express saccades, and uncorrected errors were analyzed as percentages of the total number of trials for each participant.

Saccades were detected using the default EyeLink algorithm, which applies velocity (> 30°/s), acceleration (> 8000°/s²), and motion (> 0.1°) thresholds. Before classification, the software applied a 5-point moving average filter to reduce noise and automatically flagged and removed instances with blinks or missing pupil data. No additional offline filtering was applied beyond the exclusion criteria of this study. Saccades with a latency greater than 700 ms were excluded from the analysis because they may reflect factors such as delayed decision-making, loss of attention, or task disengagement19,51. Eye blinks and saccades with amplitudes of less than 3° were also excluded from the analysis because they might indicate microsaccades, fixational corrections, or noise51. For both prosaccades and antisaccades, the number and percentage of valid and invalid saccades across age groups are presented in Supplementary Table 3. Invalid saccades included trials with small amplitudes (< 3°) and long latencies (> 700 ms). In addition, blink-related signal loss, calculated as the ratio of blink duration to total trial duration, was also reported.

Statistical analysis

Eye-tracking data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, IBM Corporation), version 22.0, for Windows. Scores from neuropsychological assessments of middle-aged and older adults were compared using independent-sample t-tests (Supplementary Table 1). To investigate the multivariate differences between age groups, separate multivariate analyses of variance (MANOVAs) were performed for prosaccade and antisaccade measures. Box’s M test was used to test the homogeneity of covariance matrices. When significant multivariate effects were found, univariate ANOVAs were conducted with Bonferroni-adjusted α-levels. To control for multiple comparisons, Bonferroni-corrected significance criteria were applied independently to prosaccade (α = 0.0125) and antisaccade (α = 0.00625) results. Partial eta squared (η²ₚ) values were reported as the effect size measures for all ANOVAs. Cohen’s d effect sizes were calculated for each pairwise comparison. Hierarchical linear regressions were used to examine the effects of age on prosaccade and antisaccade latencies. Model 1 included the centered age (Age_c) as a linear predictor. Model 2 added a term (Age_c²) to assess potential non-linear (U-shaped) age effects. The dependent variable was the mean latency at the participant level. For each model, standardized beta coefficients (β), t-values, p-values, and changes in R² were noted.

A sensitivity analysis was performed using G*Power (version 3.1) for one-way ANOVA with three groups. With a total sample size of 105 participants, α = 0.05, and 1 − β = 0.80, the design was sufficiently powered to detect a minimum effect size of f = 0.31 (equivalent to η² ≈ 0.09). The study reported effect sizes corresponding to R² values of 0.16 and 0.23, which translate to f² values of 0.19 and 0.30 (f ≈ 0.43–0.55)22. Since the sensitivity analysis indicated a minimum detectable effect size of f = 0.31, the current study appears adequately powered to identify effects of a similar size.

Prosaccade

In line with the a priori hypothesis, a multivariate analysis of variance (MANOVA) using prosaccade measures as dependent variables revealed a multivariate effect of age group (Pillai’s Trace = 0.417, F(8, 200) = 6.583, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.208). The correct prosaccade rate was significant (F(2,103) = 3.541, p = 0.033, ηp² = 0.065); however, this finding did not remain significant after the Bonferroni correction (adjusted α = 0.0125). A statistically significant difference in prosaccade latency was found between age groups (F(2, 103) = 12.837, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.201). Both older (p < 0.001) and middle-aged adults (p = 0.009) had longer prosaccade latencies than young adults, whereas no significant difference in prosaccade latency was observed between middle-aged and older adults (p > 0.05; Fig. 2A). Prosaccade amplitude also varied across age groups (F(2,103) = 13.940, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.215), with older and middle-aged adults exhibiting smaller amplitudes than young adults (both p < 0.001) and no significant difference between middle-aged and older adults (p > 0.05). In addition, there was no statistically significant difference in peak velocity among the age groups (F(2,103) = 0.259, p = 0.772, ηp² = 0.005). Antisaccade

With a priori hypothesis, a MANOVA utilizing antisaccade metrics as dependent variables also revealed a significant multivariate effect of age group (Pillai’s Trace = 0.718, F(16,190) = 6.650, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.359). Univariate analyses showed age group differences in correct antisaccades, anticipatory saccades, uncorrected errors, latency, and amplitude (all p ≤ 0.001). The rates of correct saccades (F(2,103) = 29.186, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.366), anticipatory saccades (F(2,103) = 7.608, p = 0.001, ηp² = 0.131), and uncorrected errors (F(2,103) = 11.440, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.185), as well as latency (F(2,103) = 26.341, p < 0.001, ηp² = 0.343), and amplitude (F(2,103) = 15.743, p < 0.001,ηp² = 0.238) were found to differ significantly between age groups. All notable antisaccade effects remained significant after Bonferroni correction (α = 0.00625). Older adults had lower correct saccade rates than young (p < 0.001) and middle-aged adults (p = 0.002). Additionally, middle-aged adults exhibited lower correct saccade rates than young adults (p = 0.004) (Fig. 2B). Older adults showed higher rates of anticipatory saccades than young (p = 0.001) and middle-aged adults (p = 0.043). However, no significant difference was found in anticipatory saccade rates between young and middle-aged adults (p > 0.05). Older adults had higher rates of uncorrected errors than young (p < 0.001) and middle-aged adults (p = 0.003). However, no significant differences were observed between young and middle-aged adults in uncorrected error rates (p > 0.05). Prolonged antisaccade latency was observed in middle-aged (p < 0.001) and older adults compared to young adults (p < 0.001). However, no significant difference was found in latency between middle-aged and older adults (p > 0.05). Older adults (p < 0.001) and middle-aged adults had smaller saccade amplitudes than young adults (p = 0.011). However, no significant difference was found between older and middle-aged adults (p > 0.05). The express saccade rate (F(2,103) = 0.404, p = 0.669, ηp² = 0.008), corrected error rate (F(2,103) = 1.263, p = 0.287, ηp² = 0.024), and peak velocity were not found to differ significantly between age groups (F(2,103) = 0.084, p = 0.920, ηp² = 0.002). In contrast, express saccades, corrected errors, and peak velocity did not differ significantly between the groups (all p > 0.05). The participants’ antisaccade results are presented in Table 3.

Regression analyses of age-related changes in saccade latency

In line with the exploratory hypothesis, hierarchical regression models were used to assess the effect of age on prosaccade and antisaccade latency. For prosaccade latency, a two-step hierarchical approach was implemented, including centered age (Age_c) and its quadratic term (Age_c2). Simple linear regression revealed that centered age (Age_c) significantly predicted prosaccade latency (R² = 0.194; Adj. R² = 0.187; F(1,103) = 24.86, p < 0.001). Increased age was associated with longer prosaccade latency (β = 0.44, t = 4.98, p < 0.001). The quadratic term (Age_c²) did not significantly improve the model (Adj.R² = 0.180, R²=0.196, F(2,103) = 12.408, p = 0.696) (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Consistent with the exploratory hypothesis, a two-step hierarchical regression was also conducted for antisaccades using centered age and its quadratic term. The first model showed that age significantly predicted antisaccade latency (Adj. R² = 0.285; R² = 0.292; F(1, 102) = 42.020, p < 0.001). Increased age was associated with longer antisaccade latencies (β = 0.540, t = 6.482, p < 0.001). The quadratic model did not significantly improve the prediction (Adj. R² = 0.300, R² =0.314; F(2,101) = 23.110, p = 0.074). Correlation analysis between age and saccade parameters

Age was correlated with prosaccade latency (r = 0.441, p < 0.001), correct saccade rate (r = -0.243, p = 0.012), and amplitude (r = -0.423, p < 0.001). However, peak velocity did not correlate with age (p > 0.05). Moderately significant correlations were found between age and antisaccade parameters, including latency (r = 0.540, p < 0.001), correct saccade rate (r = -0.528, p < 0.001), anticipatory saccade (r = 0.298, p = 0.002), uncorrected error rate (r = 0.375, p < 0.001), and amplitude (r = -0.442, p < 0.001). Age was not significantly associated with the corrected saccade rate, express saccade rate, or peak velocity (p > 0.05). Discussion

Prosaccade

Partially aligned with our initial a priori hypothesis, prosaccade latency was significantly prolonged in both older and middle-aged adults compared to young adults, with a large effect size. Contrary to our a priori hypothesis, prosaccade latency did not differ significantly between middle-aged and older adults. Furthermore, both older and middle-aged adults exhibited significantly smaller prosaccade amplitudes than young adults, with a large effect size. The prosaccade amplitudes did not differ between the middle-aged and older groups. Other prosaccade parameters, including the correct saccade rate and peak velocity, remained consistent across different age groups.

Most studies have found age-related increases in prosaccade latency8,11,13–17,21,52. However, fewer studies have indicated that prosaccade latency remains consistent throughout adulthood18–20. Studies reporting no age effect argue that prosaccade latency mainly reflects sensorimotor responses that remain relatively intact throughout the lifespan18–20. Conversely, Pratt et al.15 suggested that increased latency indicates a general slowdown in saccade initiation in older adults. A study examining saccade changes across the lifespan in individuals aged 5–93 years found increased prosaccade latency at ages 23–33 and 52–58 years10. Furthermore, previous studies that treated age as a continuous variable and divided it into different subgroups (5–8, 9–11, 12–14, 15–17, 18–22, 23–29, 30–39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79 years) did not classify participants in the middle-aged group as a single category, unlike in our study8,10. This makes it difficult to compare prosaccade latencies in middle-aged participants. Consistent with our findings, younger adults have been reported to show larger saccade amplitudes than older adults8,15,18,20. In this study, the target eccentricity (amplitude) in the prosaccade task was set to 10º. Younger adults tended to land closer to the target, whereas older adults showed more undershooting.

Antisaccade

Partially aligned with our second predetermined hypothesis, antisaccade latency was significantly longer in middle-aged and older adults than in young adults, with large effect sizes. However, contrary to our a priori hypothesis, antisaccade latency did not differ significantly between middle-aged and older adults. In accordance with our a priori hypothesis, correct saccade rates decreased with age, with young adults showing the highest accuracy, older adults the lowest, and middle-aged adults performing at an intermediate level (medium to large effect sizes). Older adults exhibited higher rates of anticipatory saccades and uncorrected errors than young and middle-aged adults, with medium-to-large effect sizes. In contrast, young and middle-aged adults showed no significant differences in rates of correct saccades and uncorrected errors. Finally, both middle-aged and older adults demonstrated smaller antisaccade amplitudes than young adults, with large effect sizes. Antisaccade amplitudes were similar between middle-aged and older adults. Previous studies have also reported age-related increases in antisaccade latency17–19,52,53. Other reports have shown that antisaccade latency is similar in young and older people8,20. Unlike our findings, which showed age group differences in correct antisaccades, Crawford et al.19 reported no significant difference between young and older adults. Their sample included 16 young adults (18–30 years) and 15 older adults (50–77 years), and a narrower age range may partly explain why they did not observe significant age effects, unlike our findings. Similarly, studies have revealed that older adults make more uncorrected antisaccade errors than young adults9,18–21,54. Consistent with our findings, Klein et al.17 found that older adults exhibited more anticipatory saccades than younger adults. After learning the saccade rules, participants often made predictive saccades before the target stimulus appeared55. This rapid eye movement, known as an anticipatory saccade and not caused by visual input, is reported to be triggered by factors such as prediction or expectation56–58. The study by Klein et al.17 included young individuals aged 20–35 years and older individuals aged 53–73 years, but did not include those in the midlife range (36–52 years). Our study examined a wider age range, including young adults aged 18–30 years, middle-aged adults aged 31–59 years, and elderly adults aged 60–79 years. This trend was also observed in a previous study, which showed age-related decreases in antisaccade amplitude10. However, not all studies have considered amplitude as a variable18,20.

Linear relationship between saccade latency and age

In partial support of our exploratory hypothesis, age explained approximately 19% of the variance in prosaccade latency and 29% of the variance in antisaccade latency. Consistent with these findings, antisaccade performance was more sensitive to age than prosaccade performance10. Regression analyses suggested that prosaccade latency increased by approximately 1 ms per year, whereas antisaccade latency increased by more than 2 ms per year. Our exploratory hypothesis was partially supported, as regression analyses suggested a linear association between age and saccade latency. Some studies have suggested that saccade latency may follow a U-shaped trajectory. However, contrary to our exploratory hypothesis, our results did not confirm this8,10,22. The term “U-shaped trajectory” describes changes across the lifespan, from childhood to old age. In terms of latency, longer latencies in childhood, shorter latencies in early adulthood, and gradually increasing latency periods from the mid-twenties to early thirties exemplify these developmental U-shaped changes. We did not find a nonlinear relationship between saccade latency and age. Although our exploratory hypothesis was partly supported by the linear relationship, the lack of a nonlinear relationship suggests that it was not fully confirmed. The main reason for this is the exclusion of participants from childhood and adolescence, which prevented the examination of developmental differences in the study. Additionally, variations in the sample size and saccade paradigms used may have affected the results. Furthermore, the goal of this study was to compare saccade parameters across different age groups in accordance with the predefined hypotheses. We also aimed to investigate both linear and non-linear relationships in an exploratory manner. In summary, methodological differences have contributed to the variations observed between studies59,60.

The exploratory regression analysis suggested a significant linear association between age and prosaccade and antisaccade latency across adulthood. However, group comparisons provided only partial support for a progressive age-related increase in latency, as no significant differences were observed between middle-aged and older adults. Therefore, the evidence for continuous linear age-related slowing should be interpreted cautiously. Nevertheless, antisaccade measures appeared more sensitive to aging effects than prosaccade measures, particularly with respect to inhibitory control and working demands. This finding is consistent with that of a previous study61. This has been interpreted as possibly indicating an age-related decline in frontal function, with potentially longer latency in tasks with a high cognitive load and more complex instructions. Prosaccade latency also increases with age, possibly due to declines in efficiency and working memory capacity. Research has shown that in neurological disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease, changes in brain structure or function result in lower correct antisaccade performance, higher rates of uncorrected errors, and longer prosaccade latency3,48,49,62–65. Our exploratory findings suggest a linear association between age and saccade latency in healthy aging. However, contrary to previous studies, we did not observe a nonlinear relationship between short saccade latency in young adults and long saccade latency in older adults8,10. Furthermore, we found no differences in saccade latency across groups in middle-aged and older adults. Our explanatory analyses suggested a linear age-related trend in saccade latency; however, group comparisons provided only partial support for this pattern. Future research involving neurodegenerative or clinical populations may further clarify the potential utility of saccadic measures in investigating age-related cognitive changes.

One limitation of this study is that the number of middle-aged and older participants was smaller than that of young adults. The second limitation is the absence of neuropsychological assessments in the young group, although cognitive impairment is considered very unlikely. Our study focused on saccade changes only in the adult group. However, excluding participants from childhood and adolescence poses a third limitation, as their inclusion might have revealed developmental changes in the saccades. The final limitation was the relatively high rate of saccade exclusion. This partly reflects the high sensitivity of eye-tracking devices for detecting and classifying thousands of eye movements. Nevertheless, high exclusion rates may have influenced the estimates of saccade latency and potentially affected comparisons across age groups. For example, if exclusions occurred more frequently in older adults due to increased blinks, tracking loss, or unstable recordings, latency estimates may have been biased, or group differences may have been either overestimated or underestimated. Future studies with larger samples that include all age groups, from childhood to adulthood, and employ longitudinal designs are likely to yield more generalizable findings.
