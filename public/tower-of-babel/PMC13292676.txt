Probiotic spray on incubating chicken eggs accelerates the establishment of a healthy jejunal microbiota☆
Authors: Mihiprabha Rathnayake, Noor Ahmad Shaik, Shelly Popowich, Betty Chow-Lockerbie, Lisanework Ayalew, Suresh Tikoo, Mohamed Faizal Abdul-Careem, Susantha Gomis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Establishing beneficial microbiota in chickens earlier in life is advantageous for optimum production and health. This study was conducted to investigate the colonization of probiotics in the intestines of specific pathogen free chicken embryos using a coarse spray of probiotics on incubating eggs to accelerate the establishment of a healthy jejunal microbiota in neonatal chickens. Enterococcus faecalis were sprayed on incubating eggs on 12, 15 and/or 17 days of embryonation (DOE) to explore the optimum day(s) needed for colonization. Colonization was confirmed by direct culture and whole genome sequencing of intestinal contents of embryos. Colonization was best observed when probiotics were sprayed on two separate days - 15 and 17 DOE. Further, effects of coarse spray of E. faecalis, Ligilactobacillus salivarius, Lactobacillus plantarum, Bifidobacterium pullorum subsp. gallinarum, and Pediococcus acidilactici on incubating eggs individually or as a mixture in jejunal microbiota were assessed at 2, 10, 20, and 30 days post hatch (DPH) using 16S rRNA amplicon sequencing. Coarse spray application of L. salivarius, L. plantarum and the mixture of probiotics lead to significantly faster convergence trajectories towards a mature reference profile [represented by 30 DPH with no probiotics delivery (negative control)], compared to groups that received probiotics by the oral route. Beta diversity analysis showed the largest separation between groups at 2 DPH. Over 10, 20, and 30 DPH, all communities gradually converged, with the group that received the sprayed probiotic mixture retaining a unique profile for the longest period. The group that received the sprayed probiotic mixture consistently outperformed the negative control group and the group given oral probiotics on 2 DPH at both species and genus levels in terms of richness. This confirms the beneficial effect of a probiotic mixture spray compared to natural colonization or short-term oral application. In conclusion, spray application of probiotics as a coarse spray is an industry feasible technique and noninvasive method for introducing probiotics to chicken embryos leading to a rapid establishment of healthy jejunal microbial composition.

Introduction

The microbiota of the intestinal tract of chickens has emerged as a critical determinant of health and productivity. Intestinal microbiota influence nutrient absorption, immunomodulation, disease prevention and performance (Rychlik, 2020). However, microbial composition of the gut and functions in commercial chickens hatched and raised under strict biosecurity measures differ from chickens raised outdoors (J Scupham et al., 2008; Ocejo et al., 2019; Rychlik, 2020; Seidlerova et al., 2020). Although studies focused on cecal microbiota are common, microbial composition of jejunum in broilers raised in commercial barns and outdoors remain understudied. However, a significant transformation of microbiota in both cecum and jejunum in Cobb-500 broilers was observed when microbiota inoculum originating from chicken cecum was sprayed at hatch (Kayal et al., 2022). Biosecurity procedures in commercial broilers, while essential for preventing disease outbreaks and maintaining flock health, creates a barrier and prevents the transmission of beneficial microorganisms (Marcolla et al., 2023). Furthermore, newly hatched chicks may experience a period with limited access to feed and water, up to 24 hours as they are transported from the hatchery to the farm (Bergoug et al., 2013). Delayed access to feed reduces the potential of introduction of microflora and prevent colonization of the gastrointestinal tract with beneficial microorganisms (Siwek et al., 2018; Das et al., 2021). Hence alternative strategies are urgently needed to establish and maintain healthy intestinal microbiota in commercial poultry (Das et al., 2021).

Probiotics represent an alternative to disease control through various complementary mechanisms to enhance health and performance in poultry (Naeem and Bourassa, 2025). Probiotics competitively exclude pathogenic organisms by competing for binding sites preventing the colonization and proliferation of pathogenic bacteria, and produce antimicrobial compounds (Ahmed et al., 2023). Probiotics modulate immune responses of the intestinal epithelium and associated lymphoid tissues and enhance the structural and functional integrity of the intestinal epithelial barrier. This reduces the intestinal permeability and the translocation of pathogenic organisms and their toxins (Naeem and Bourassa, 2025). Probiotics enhance the production of short chain fatty acids. Collectively these mechanisms result in measurable improvements in growth performance, feed conversion efficiency, and overall health status.

The most common methods for delivering probiotics to commercial poultry have involved incorporation into feed or through drinking water. Oral delivery of probiotics however face challenges including variable consumption rates among individual birds, potential degradation of viability of probiotics during storage. Incorporation of probiotics into dry feed formulations also has similar challenges such as viability during processing, storage, and degradation in acidic gastric environment (Khomayezi and Adewole, 2022).

The efficacy of probiotics, however, depends on several factors including the bacterial species, the dose and frequency of administration, timing of introduction relative to critical development windows, and the degree of interaction between the probiotic strain and the existing microbial community within the host (Naeem and Bourassa, 2025). Recognizing the advantages of early probiotic colonization, researchers have increasingly investigated in ovo delivery methods where probiotics are administered directly into the developing embryo (Kpodo and Proszkowiec-Weglarz, 2023). Traditional in ovo delivery of probiotics involve injection of carefully measured volumes of probiotic suspension directly into the air cell or into the amniotic fluid using automated or semi-automated equipment (Ahmed et al., 2023). However, in ovo delivery of probiotics face practical and biological constraints that have limited its widespread commercial adoption (Tavaniello et al., 2023). Recent developments have identified spray based application of probiotics as a potentially transformative and non-invasive alternative to injection of probiotics (Villumsen et al., 2023). The concept of spray application involves applying a known concentration of a probiotic suspension directly onto the surface of incubating eggs, during the late incubation period.

Age has been identified as a main factor which influences the composition of the cecal microbiota (Ocejo et al., 2019). In a study using Ross 308 chickens from an intensive system and free-range slow growing SassoT451A chickens, showed an age associated community profile with a transient period during their development [29 days post-hatch (DPH) in broilers and 39 DPH in free-range chickens]. A similar observation was reported in another study where Ross hybrid broiler chickens at 3-7 DPH, 14-28 DPH, and 49 DPH had significant differences among cecal microbiota progressing towards maturity associated complexity as bird matured with age (Lu et al., 2003). However, age related studies on maturation of jejunal microbiota development remain limited. The optimum speed of microbiota development is important because it corelates with the natural resistance of chickens against infections such as Salmonella species and Clostridium perfringens (Hofacre et al., 1998; Varmuzova et al., 2016).

The jejunal microbiota involve in nutrient absorption, development of intestinal barrier function and pathogen exclusion in chickens. Despite the functional importance, effect of probiotic spray on hatching eggs on jejunal microbial composition has not been adequately addressed. This represents a significant gap in understanding development of jejunal microbial composition following administration of probiotics to hatching eggs. Further, jejunum undergoes critical development changes during 10-20 DPH and research demonstrated that jejunal microbiota shows distinct age dependant patterns in early life (Glendinning et al., 2019; Cordero et al., 2023). Hence, jejunum makes an ideal site for detecting accelerated microbiota maturation induced by early probiotic intervention through spray application on incubating specific pathogen free (SPF) eggs.

This study aimed to evaluate whether early probiotic intervention via coarse spray application on SPF chicken eggs during incubation accelerates jejunal microbiota maturation and establishes a beneficial bacterial community composition by 10-20 days of age, compared to the conventional 30-day maturation timeline observed in untreated control birds without probiotic intervention.

Materials and methods

Housing and maintenance of chickens

Animal experiments were approved by the Animal Research Ethics Board, University of Saskatchewan and adhered to the Canadian Council on Animal Care guidelines (protocol number 20160071). All methods were performed according to the guidelines and regulations of the Canadian Council on Animal Care. Euthanasia was performed by cervical dislocation by trained personnel. Fertile SPF hatching eggs were obtained (Canadian Food Inspection Agency, Nepean, ON, Canada) and were incubated in proper incubation conditions at the Animal Care Unit (ACU) at the Western College of Veterinary Medicine (WCVM), University of Saskatchewan, Canada. Briefly, from 0 to 5 DOE, eggs were maintained at 100 – 100.3°F with a relative humidity (RH) of 70%. Between 6 and 10 DOE, temperature was gradually reduced to 99.5 – 99.7°F, RH was held at 70% and damper settings were increased sequentially from 0 to 10,000 units to introduce controlled ventilation (Tona et al., 2022). In the incubator, damper setting regulated ventilation by controlling fresh air intake and exhaust, thereby maintaining appropriate oxygen availability, carbon dioxide removal, temperature stability, and humidity throughout embryonic development. From 11 DOE to 13 DOE, temperature gradually reduced from 99.2°F to 99°F while RH reduce from 55% to 45%. By 14 DOE, temperature was set to 98.8°F and 40% RH. Between 15 and 21 DOE, temperature remained stable at approximately 98.2-98.4°F and RH 40% until hatch.

After hatch, SPF birds were housed in the ACU, WCVM, University of Saskatchewan, Canada, with access to ad libitum feed (MasterFeeds Raised Without Antibiotics 20% poultry starter) and water. Each experimental group was housed in separate pen (n = 50/pen) in level 2 biosafety facility room with clean feeders and waterers provided in each pen. Personnel followed strict biosecurity protocols during daily feeding: clean boot covers, gloves and face masks were changed during entrance to each pen. Order of entry to each pen was organized to prevent cross contamination; personnel enter in the negative control pen as the first entry point. In the ACU, the air exchange was controlled using high efficiency particulate air (HEPA) filters for air exhaust, and the non-recirculated air is supplied at 15–20 air changes per hour. The air pressure differentials and strict sanitation were maintained in this isolation facility. SPF birds were raised at 32°C for the first 7 days of life and gradually reduced 2°C weekly until 21°C was achieved. Lighting (30–40 lux) was provided continuously until 2 DPH, thereafter lux and duration were decreased until 10–20 lx and 7 h of darkness were achieved. Pens were on concrete floor with clean sawdust shavings.

Source of probiotics

The E. faecalis isolate was obtained from the rectum of a healthy 32-week old broiler breeder chicken in Saskatchewan, 2017 (Karunarathna et al., 2022). Ligilactobacillus salivarius and Pediococcus acidilactici isolates were obtained from a commercial probiotic mixture (Biomin® PS SOL, Biomin GmbH, Industriestrasse 21, 3130 Herzogenburg, Austria). The isolate of Bifidobacterium pullorum subsp. gallinarum from a chicken was acquired from ATCC (33778). Lactobacillus plantarum was kindly provided by Dr. C. H. Song (Department of Animal Science and Biotechnology, Chungnam National University, Daejeon 34134, South Korea).

Preparation of probiotics for coarse spray on incubating SPF eggs

E. faecalis, L. salivarius, L. plantarum, B. pullorum subsp. gallinarum, and P. acidilactici were streaked on 5% Columbia sheep blood agar (Oxoid, Nepean, Ontario, Canada). E faecalis was incubated aerobically and L. salivarius, L. plantarum, B. pullorum subsp. gallinarum, and P. acidilactici were incubated anaerobically for 24 h at 37°C.

A single colony of E. faecalis was inoculated in 100 mL of Todd Hewitt broth and incubated aerobically at 37°C for 24 h on a shaker (150 rpm). Following incubation, a 1:100 dilution was made in Todd Hewitt broth. The dilution was then incubated 4 h at 37°C on a shaker (150 rpm) to bring the bacterial concentration to 1 × 109 colony forming units (CFU)/mL in logarithmically growing phase.

De Man–Rogosa–Sharpe (MRS) media was used to grow L. salivarius, L. plantarum, and P. acidilactici and reinforced clostridium media (RCM) was used to grow B. pullorum subsp. gallinarum. From L. salivarius, L. plantarum, B. pullorum subsp. gallinarum, and P. acidilactici culture plates, 10 µL were inoculated in 50 mL of respective media and incubated anaerobically for 24 h at 37°C. Following incubation 1:10 dilution was made in respective media. The dilution was incubated for 4 h at 37°C anaerobically to bring the bacterial concentration to 1 × 109 CFU/mL in logarithmically growing phase. The temperature of all bacterial broths was maintained at 10°C until spray application on incubating eggs.

Coarse spray of probiotics on incubating SPF eggs

Probiotic preparations containing logarithmically growing phase of respective bacterial species with a concentration of 1 × 109CFU/mL were filled in to a 500 mL handheld 360-degree spray bottle (Avantor Science CentralTM, VWR international Co, 2360 Argentia Road, Mississauga, ON, Canada) and maintained at 10°C on ice until use. Separate spray bottles were used for each probiotic and the probiotic mixture. A temperature gradient was maintained between the embryo (37°C) and bacterial broth (10°C) to facilitate bacterial migration into the embryo (Karunarathna et al., 2022). Incubating eggs were taken out from the incubator and probiotics were sprayed on the entire surface of egg for 30 s. Eggs were let dry at room temperature for 3 - 4 min and incubated eggs were placed into dedicated incubators for each bacterial isolate or probiotic group to minimize cross contamination amongst groups.

Experimental design

A. Determination of the number of coarse spray applications on eggshells required for optimum efficacy of migration of E. faecalis into the intestine of SPF embryos. To demonstrate migration of E. faecalis to the intestine of SPF embryos following a coarse spray, SPF eggs were set in to 7 groups (n = 15/group): (1) probiotic spray at 12,15, and 17 DOE; (2) probiotic spray at 12 and 15 DOE; (3) probiotic spray at 15 and 17 DOE; (4) probiotic spray at 12 DOE; (5) probiotic spray at 15 DOE; (6) probiotic spray at17 DOE; (7) no probiotics spray (negative control). Groups 1,2, and 4 were sprayed with E. faecalis at 12 DOE, groups 1,2,3, and 5 were sprayed with E. faecalis at 15 DOE, and groups 1,3, and 6 were sprayed with E. faecalis at 17 DOE as described above. The negative control group was not sprayed and kept int a separate incubator until sampling at 20 DOE. Separate egg incubators were assigned to each experimental group to prevent any potential cross contamination. Piping eggs were excluded during assessment of E. faecalis colonization. Sampling of intestines was conducted in class II biosafety cabinet using separate sterile gloves, scissors and forceps for each embryo. Intestines were collected and chopped with 750 µL phosphate buffered saline (PBS) at 20 DOE (n = 10). Samples of intestine were cultured directly on mEnterococcus agar (BD Difco, Mississauga, ON), the selective media for E. faecalis. The identification of isolated organisms was done using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time of flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF) at Prairie Diagnostic Services (PDS) Inc (52 Campus Drive, University of Saskatchewan, Canada). Whole genome sequencing was performed for the E. faecalis isolate used for the spray and E. faecalis isolated from the intestine of embryos at PDS Inc. Average nucleotide identity was used to confirm bacteria sprayed on eggs and recovered bacteria from the intestines of embryos were the same.

B. Coarse spray of individual or combination of probiotics on incubating eggs and its effects on the jejunal microbiota of chickens. A total of 720 SPF eggs were assigned randomly into 9 groups (n = 50/group). (1) E. faecalis spray (EF); (2) L. salivarius spray (LS); (3) L. plantarum spray (LP); (4) B. pullorum subsp. gallinarum spray (BP); (5) P. acidilactici spray (PA); (6) Probiotic mixture (E. faecalis, L. salivarius, L. plantarum, B. pullorum subsp. gallinarum and P. acidilactici) spray (Mix); (7) oral gavage of probiotic mixture for 2 DPH (O2); (8) oral gavage of probiotic mixture from 0 to 7 DPH (O7) and (9) no introduction of probiotics (negative control) (NC). Separate egg incubators were assigned to each experimental group to prevent any potential cross contamination. Groups 1-5 were sprayed with relevant probiotic bacterial broths at 15 and 17 DOE. Group 6 was sprayed with the probiotic mixture at 15 and 17 DOE. Group 7 was given a probiotic mixture (1 × 108 CFU/bird/day) orally once daily until 2 DPH and group 8 were given the probiotics mixture (1 × 108 CFU/bird/day) orally once daily for 0-7 DPH. Group 9 was the negative control. The jejunal contents were collected from eight birds (n = 8/group) at each time point (at 2 DPH, 10 DPH, 20 DPH and 30 DPH). Sterility was maintained by using separate sterile gloves, scissors, and forceps for each bird to prevent cross contamination. This procedure was conducted in a level 2 facility room using an air purifier close to the sample collection to minimize environment contamination. Samples were stored in −80°C until processing. DNA extraction

From each sample, DNA were extracted using MagmaxTM microbiome ultra nucleic acid isolation kit-automated extraction (Life Technologies Corp. Austin, TX 78744) following the manufacturer’s guidelines, using the Thermo ScientificTM KingFisherTM Flex Magnetic Particle Processor with 96 deep-well head. Addition to samples, ZymoBIOMICS Gut Microbiome Standard (catalog number D6331) was used as the internal control for 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing and extraction controls were used to confirm that no DNA contamination was introduced during the DNA extraction process. The quantity and purity of extracted DNA were measured by spectrophotometry. (Nanodrop 2000c Thermo ScientificTM). Extracted DNA were stored in −20°C. Minimum volume of 25 µL of 5 ng/µL DNA was prepared for 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing.

16S rRNA gene amplification

The 16S rRNA gene amplification and amplicon sequencing was conducted for all the samples including internal control (ZymoBIOMICS Gut Microbiome Standard) and extraction controls using nanopore sequencing at PDS Inc. The amplification of 16S rRNA gene was performed by PCR using universal primers 27F and 1492R which spans nearly the full length of the 16S rRNA gene, with an expected amplicon of ∼1400 bp. The master mix was prepared by mixing 12.5 μL of NEB Q5 Hot Start High-Fidelity 2 × Master mix (New England Biolabs Cat no. M0492L), 1.25 μL of each 10 μM 16S rRNA full length primers (27F −5′-AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG-3′ and 1492R - 5′-TACGGYTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3′) and 5 μL of nuclease-free water. For each PCR reaction, 5 μL of DNA template was added to 20 μL of the master mix. The PCR was carried out with the following cycling conditions: initial denaturation at 98°C for 30 sec, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 98°C for 10 sec, annealing at 55°C for 30 sec, extension at 72°C for 120 sec, and a final elongation step at 72°C for 2 min. The reaction was then held at 4°C.

Magnetic bead clean-up

Agencourt Ampure XP beads (Cat No. Beckman Coulter A63880) (25 μL) were added to each PCR product and mixed thoroughly. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 5 min and then placed on a magnetic rack to separate beads. After separation, the solution was discarded, without disturbing the pellet. Next, 200 μL of 70% ethanol was added to each sample without disturbing the pellet. The ethanol was then discarded by aspiration. The washing step was repeated one more time. The pellet was allowed to dry for 2 min and removed from the magnetic rack. The pellet was suspended in 50 μL of nuclease-free water and incubated at room temperature for 5 min, followed by magnetic separation. Once a clear separation was completed, the liquid was aspirated and transferred into a labeled tube or a 96-well PCR plate. The eluate was then quantified using the Qubit Invitrogen Qubit 4 Fluorometer (Qubit 1x dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermofisher, Cat no. Q33231)).

Library preparation

The sequencing library was prepared using the ligation sequencing amplicons protocol (Version: NBA_9170_v114_revL_15Sep2022). The cleaned-up PCR product was then normalised to 187.5 ng (200 fmol based on 1500 bp fragment size) and taken to end-preparation. The NEBNext Ultra II End repair/dA-tailing Module (NEB, Cat no. E7546) was used to add dA-tails to the ends of the amplicons, followed by magnetic bead clean up and elution with 12 μL of nuclease free water. Unique barcodes from the Native Barcoding Kit 96 V14 (ONT, Cat No. SQKNBD114.96) were ligated to the end-prepped amplicons using NEB Blunt/TA Ligase Master mix (NEB, Cat no. M0367). After barcoding, 48 samples with unique barcodes were pooled together and cleaned up using magnetic beads. Sequencing adapters were then ligated to the pooled samples using the NEBNext Quick Ligation Module (NEB, Cat no. E6056), followed by magnetic bead clean up. Finally, the resulting library was quantified using Invitrogen Qubit 4 Fluorometer with the (Qubit 1x dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Thermofisher, Cat no. Q33231)).

16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing

The prepared library was loaded onto a ONT R10.4.1 flow cell and sequenced on a GridION platform for a minimum duration of 48 h. Super-accurate base calling option in MinKNOW was used for base calling. Only reads with barcodes on both ends were used and barcodes were trimmed while sequencing using the trim barcodes option in MinKNOW software (Versions used: 23.04.5, 23.04.6, 23.07.5, 23.07.12, 23.11.3)

The microbial composition and relative abundance analysis

The microbial composition and relative abundance of intestinal microbial community were studied. Obtained fastq files were analyzed using epi2me-labs/wf-16s nextflow workflow (v1.2.0). Kraken2 pipeline was run using the setting read length 1400-1600, and Q-score <10. Further, the bracken length was set to 1000. The ncbi_16s_18s rRNA gene NCBI database within Kraken2 pipeline was used. The average number of reads per sample was 99,772 and was consistent within and among the groups. Any samples with read number less than 1000 were excluded. Relative abundances of species and genus levels were obtained matrices were prepared as each raw represented a sample and each column a microbial genus/ species for further microbial diversity analysis.

Alpha diversity analysis - genus and species level

Alpha diversity analysis was conducted to evaluate within-sample microbial richness and evenness in the jejunal contents of SPF chickens exposed to different probiotic treatments. All analyses were performed using python v3.10. The panda’s library (v2.0.3) was used for data manipulation and cleaning. Observed operational taxonomic units (observed richness), Chao1 index, Shannon index, and Simpson index were computed for each sample in genus and species levels using scikit-bio (v0.5.8). For genus and species level data, visualizations were generated using seaborn (v0.12.2) and matplotlib (v3.7.1). Boxplots and strip plots were employed to represent the distribution of diversity indices across groups.

Beta diversity analysis – genus and species level

To assess microbial community dissimilarity across treatment groups and developmental stages beta diversity analysis was conducted on genus and species level abundance profiles derived from jejunal microbiota samples collected at four time points: 2 DPH, 10 DPH, 20 DPH, and 30 DPH. Raw count matrices were pre-processed by transposing the data such that each raw represented a sample and each column a microbial genus. Samples belonging to treatment groups higher than 3 replicates were included to maintain statistical validity for ordination and permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) testing. The resulting data were normalized using a Hellinger transformation, a method that first converts raw counts into relative abundances per sample and then applies a square root transformation to down-weight highly dominant taxa.

Following normalization, Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrices were calculated using the beta diversity function from scikit-bio python library (v0.5.8). To visualize these multidimensional relationships, principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was performed using scikit-bio’s PCoA function. The first two principal coordinate axes (PCoA1 and PCoA2), which cumulatively captured the largest fraction of variance in community composition were extracted for plotting. Coordinates from the ordination were stored in a data frame alongside sample-specific treatment group labels for downstream plotting.

Visualization was performed using matplotlib (v3.7.1) and seaborn (v0.12.2). Each sample was displayed as a round point colored according to its treatment group. To visually emphasize group-level clustering, convex hulls were drawn around the coordinates of samples belonging to the same group using the “scipy.spatial” convex hull function. A consistent seaborn “set 2″ color palette was applied to ensure interpretability and uniform styling across all four time points. Axis labels included the percentage of variance explained by each PCoA axis, facilitating interpretation of microbial shifts overtime.

Longitudinal and trajectory analysis

Longitudinal analysis was conducted to evaluate how different probiotic treatments influenced jejunal microbial composition to converge towards the mature microbial composition at 30 DPH in NC community. Bray-Curtis dissimilarity was assessed in two complementary ways, the mean dissimilarity of each sample to all 30 DPH NC replicates, and the Euclidean distance between each group’s centroid and that of 30 DPH NC in PCoA ordination space.

Trajectory analysis quantified the direction and rate of microbial change by calculating daily convergence slopes (β_day) based on Bray-Curtis distances between each sample and the 30 DPH, NC community using the beta -diversity function of the scikit-bio package (python v0.5.8). Negative β_day values indicated faster convergence towards mature microbial composition, while positive values represented divergence over time.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism version 9.5.0 software (Dotmatics inc.) was used for data analysis. Phylum, family, genus, and species level relative microbiota diversities were analyzed using ordinary two-way ANOVA (P < 0.0001). The variation between different experimental groups at 95% confidence interval, was analyzed using Tukey’s multiple comparison. Statistical validation of community differences between groups was performed using PERMANOVA, implemented in scikit-bio. A total of 999 permutations were used to compute pseudo-F statistics and associated P-values. A P-value threshold of <0.05 was used to determine statistical significance. To assess intergroup differences in alpha diversity indices Kruskal-Wallis test was used as a non-parametric test suitable for ecological data with unequal variances and non-normal distributions. This was implemented via pingouin library (v0.5.3). When significant differences (P < 0.05) were detected, Dunn’s post hoc test was conducted using scikit-posthocs (v0.6.7), with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate correction applied to control for multiple comparisons. To evaluate difference in overall community composition between treatment groups, PERMANOVA (9999 permutations) was performed using scikit-bio. Both global and pairwise PERMANOVA tests were conducted for each timepoint to determine which treatments exhibited significantly different species level community structures. To verify that the observed differences were not due to within group variability PERMDISP was applied with 9999 permutations to assess multivariate homogeneity of dispersion.

Results

A. Determination of the number of coarse spray applications on eggshells required for optimum efficacy of migration of E. faecalis into the intestine of SPF embryos. E. faecalis was recovered from the intestines of SPF embryos at 20 DOE with recovery rate of 100% following coarse spray application at 12 and 15 DOE, 15 and 17 DOE or 12,15 and 17 DOE. Whole genome sequencing confirmed 100% average nucleotide identity between recovered and sprayed isolates. No E. faecalis was recovered from groups with coarse spray after a single application (12, 15 or 17 DOE). E. faecalis was not recovered from the control group did not receive probiotics. These results indicated that at least two coarse spray applications were needed for intestinal colonization of E. faecalis in SPF embryos (Table 1). At the species level, L. salivarius predominated in LS, O7 and BP groups and was significantly high LS and O7 relative to NC and O2 (P < 0.05).

The NC group was characterized by a higher abundance of [Clostridium]spiroforme. The detection of Clostridium spiroforme in the NC group was not accompanied by a clinical disease or intestinal pathology. Discussion

Commercial chickens raised under strict biosecurity conditions undergo intensely controlled environments designed to minimize entry of pathogenic microorganisms in the environment. Although biosecurity measures are essential for preventing diseases and maintain flock health, this management practice prevents the normal transmission of beneficial microorganisms (Marcolla et al., 2023). Hence, delivery of probiotics to chickens reared in industrial settings are beneficial. An industry feasible technique of coarse spray application of probiotics on incubating eggs was studied here to identify benefits following hatch. In this study, we first explored the frequency of probiotic E. faecalis spray applications required to achieve the goal of successful intestinal colonization of probiotic E. faecalis in chicken embryos. We found that a minimum of two coarse spray applications is needed to achieve this goal. In our study we did not observe colonization of E. faecalis when sprayed only once at 12, 15 or 17 DOE. We hypothesize that the cuticle of the eggshell likely interferes with introduction of probiotics through the eggshell hence it needs coarse spray application of probiotics more than once to introduce probiotics to embryos.

Intestinal colonization of E. faecalis was observed in 100% of the embryos when the probiotic spray was applied at 15 and 17 DOE. Notably, 100% of the embryos demonstrated colonization of E. faecalis when the spray was applied twice (12 and 15 DOE or 15 and 17 DOE) and when the spray applied three times (12, 15, and 17 DOE), confirmed by whole genome sequencing. We hypothesize that the first spray application compromises the egg cuticle, thus facilitating migration of probiotics upon second exposure. However, to evaluate this hypothesis further experiments examining integrity of eggshell cuticle following probiotic spray by scanning electron microscopy and functional permeability assays are warranted. In a previous study following spray application of E. faecium on incubating eggs of Ross 308 at 17 DOE, E. faecium was recovered from only 9% of embryos at 19 DOE. Moreover, spray application of L. animalis on incubating eggs at 18 DOE, L. animalis was not recovered from any embryo at 19 DOE (Thøfner et al., 2021). These observations are consistent with our observations since introduction of probiotics was not possible by one spray application on 12, 15 or 17 DOE. Thøfner et al. conducted PCR using strain specific primers to confirm colonization of probiotics. Furthermore, spray application of probiotics using a spray flask in this study was conducted using lyophilized E. faecium (1 × 108 CFU/egg) and L. animalis (1 × 107CFU/egg) following dissolving in isotonic saline. This study differs from our study regarding type of eggs used, spray frequency, sampling days, probiotics used and the method of spray. The authors proposed that the applied probiotics may have been transported to the embryo via blood vessels located along the inner surface of the egg shell (Thøfner et al., 2021). Further, previous studies discussed that the chorioallantoic membrane was involved in transferring probiotics into the circulatory system and the developing intestine (Gao et al., 2025). Another study used E faecium (50µL/egg) (1 × 1010 CFU/mL) as a spray application using a handheld pressure sprayer at 18 DOE maintained at 37°C to study the colonization of E faecium in Ross 308 chickens following hatch. They confirmed recovery of E faecium isolate by PCR from chicks at hatch (96%), on 7 (28%) and 21 (0%) DPH (Villumsen et al., 2023). Previous studies have demonstrated the ability of colonization of probiotics in the intestine of chickens following spray application of probiotics on incubating eggs, but our objective aimed to evaluate whether early probiotic intervention via spray application on broiler chicken eggs during incubation accelerates jejunal microbiota maturation and establishes a beneficial bacterial community composition by 10-20 days of age, compared to the conventional 30 day maturation timeline observed in untreated control birds without probiotic intervention. The jejunum was selected in our study to explore microbiota since the jejunum exhibits development of a dynamic microbial community during 10-20 DPH in contrast, cecal microbiota demonstrates age related maturation that extend beyond 10-20 day of age (Awad et al., 2016; Proszkowiec-Weglarz et al., 2022; Yin et al., 2023). Cecal microbiota assessment remains an important direction for future research to determine whether early jejunal microbial maturation translate into comparable acceleration of cecal microbial stabilization.

Furthermore, we wanted to evaluate the efficacy of delivering a mixture of five probiotics and individual probiotics as a coarse spray on incubating eggs reaching the goal of achieving microbiota of 30 DPH bird with no introduction of probiotics. To the best of our knowledge, our study is the first to study the effect of coarse spray application of probiotics on incubating eggs individually or as a mixture of probiotics on jejunal microbiota development of SPF birds using E. faecalis, L. salivarius, L. plantarum, B. pullorum subsp. gallinarum and P. acidilactici. A recent study has been conducted to study the effect of spray application of probiotics on the microbiota development on eggshells, chorioallantoic membrane, embryo intestine and hatchling intestine in Ross 308 chickens when probiotics were sprayed on 0, 3, 7, 10, 14, and 18 DOE using an atomizer. The probiotics used in this study were Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus and Lacticaseibacillus paracasei (Gao et al., 2025). This study investigated a significant modulation for the microbiota on eggshell, chorioallantoic membrane, embryo intestine and intestine of the hatchlings. However, this study did not optimize the probiotic dosage and application timing for effective use in commercial hatcheries. In our study, we evaluated the minimum number of spray applications needed for successful colonization of probiotics in the intestine of chicken embryos which is more convenient for the industry. Further, our study differs from this study as our study evaluated the jejunal microbial composition from hatchlings to mature chickens at 30 DPH, demonstrating early gut colonization of probiotics leading to rapid establishments of a healthy gut microbiota compared to broilers that did not receive probiotics at their embryonic stage.

We hypothesized that probiotic colonization in the intestine of chicken embryos would promote rapid establishment of a healthy gut microbial community after hatch. Supporting this hypothesis, probiotic mixture, L. salivarius, and L. plantarum sprayed groups developed a jejunal microbial composition enriched with beneficial bacteria by 10 and 20 DPH, whereas similar microbial profile was observed only by 30 DPH in the group which did not receive any probiotics (NC). This finding was further confirmed by Mean Bray Curtis dissimilarity to 30 DPH NC and Longitudinal convergence trajectory analysis. Pairwise slope difference confirmed that probiotic sprayed groups had significantly faster convergence trajectories than groups which received oral probiotic under SPF experimental conditions.

Embryos in the NC group had no exposure to probiotics, hence delayed establishment of beneficial bacteria in the intestine was observed compared to probiotics received groups. Similarly, the groups that received oral probiotics after hatch showed delayed establishment of beneficial microbial composition in the jejunum compared to probiotics sprayed groups. Even though there is a dearth of information on how colonization of probiotics in the intestine of chicken embryo led to rapid establishment of healthy gut microbiota, studies have shown rapid colonization of Lactobacillus by 7 days of age when probiotics (L. acidophilus, L. casei, E. faecium, B. bifidum) were administered orally with feed after hatch to commercial white leghorn layer hens. (Ballou et al., 2016; Fathima et al., 2022). Additionally, probiotic spray during the incubation compensates the natural microbiota development that normally occur in chickens reared in natural environments. The NC group had limited exposure to environmental bacteria before and after hatch. This might also be a reason for the observed beneficial bacterial colonization pattern in probiotic sprayed groups, probiotics orally given groups compared to NC.

We noted relatively high abundance of Citrobacter and Pseudocitobacter in the NC and LS groups at 2 DPH, which may potentially reflect environmental exposure during the early post hatch period since both genera are associated with environmental reservoirs. However, all nine groups were included in separate incubators and pens and they were provided with same incubation and husbandry conditions, suggesting that differences observed among groups reflect treatment associated microbial colonization rather than environmental contamination.

Further, hypothesizing probiotic mixture sprayed group has the most benefits for microbiota development in neonatal chickens, alpha and beta diversity analyses of other groups were compared with the Mix group. Alpha and beta diversity analysis revealed significant temporal and probiotic spray application dependant modulation of jejunal microbial composition in neonatal chicks at 2, 10, 20, and 30 DPH. At 2 DPH, all alpha diversity indices (observed richness, Chao 1, Shannon and Simpson) were significantly different among treatment groups (corrected P < 0.00002 for all metrices- Kruskal Wallis H test). Spray application of probiotics during incubation reduced the diversity and evenness, indicating limited genus representation and dominance of few beneficial taxa. This could be due to competition among strains, poor colonization, or mismatch with the host gut environment. Further, the introduction of set of probiotics could lead to manipulated colonization leading to selective enrichment resulting in reduced diversity and evenness at early age. Early colonizers can lead to an ecological monopolization (Kers et al., 2018). In contrast, NC showed higher Shannon and Simpson index suggesting a naturally balanced microbial composition. Beta diversity analysis at 2 DPH revealed Mix clustered differently and was significantly different from LS, NC, O2, LP and BP. However, by 10 DPH, microbial communities of Mix and LP shifted towards a more similar configuration, indicating early-stage convergence. On the other hand, alpha diversity indices showed no statistically significant differences at 10 DPH across groups, suggesting that by this stage, differences in overall genus level diversity across treatments had diminished. The duration of oral probiotic administration influences microbial diversity more effectively to shape a balanced gut microbiota. The beta diversity analysis at 20 DPH, showed broad overlap between the Mix group and other probiotic sprayed groups and O2 group, indicating substantial convergence of microbial profiles. By 30 DPH, compared to natural colonization, the group which was sprayed with the probiotic mixture exhibited significantly high genus level richness. Beta diversity results suggest that probiotics have a strong early effect, but over time, the natural environment in the gut takes over. To the best of our knowledge, jejunal microbial diversity analysis following spray application of probiotics on incubating eggs was not studied previously. However, cecal and ileal microbiota diversity in hatchlings were studied in Ross 308 chickens after spraying incubating eggs with L. rhamnosus and L. paracasei in a recent study. After probiotics were sprayed on hatching broiler chicken eggs on 0 3,7,10, 14 and 18 DOE, alpha diversity was not significantly different in the ileum and cecum of hatchlings in probiotics sprayed groups compared to control group. However, beta diversity was significantly different in the cecum and ileum in L. paracasei sprayed group compared to control group. (Gao et al., 2025). The microbial composition over time or trajectory analysis to study convergence towards healthy microbiota was not studied. Our study demonstrated, alpha and beta diversity analysis at 2, 10, 20, and 30 DPH. The alpha diversity was significantly different in jejunum at 2 DPH compared to negative control. Significant difference of richness was observed at 10 and 30 DPH while Shannon diversity index differed significantly at 20 DPH. Beta diversity analysis demonstrated a significant separation at 2 DPH followed by progressive overlap among groups at 10, 20 and 30 DPH indicting convergence towards a similar microbial community structure over time. Furthermore, longitudinal trajectory analysis revealed that the probiotic mixture sprayed group achieved a beneficial microbial composition at 10 DPH, whereas the negative control group required approximately 30 days to reach a comparable microbial state.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated probiotics can be colonized in the intestine of SPF chicken embryos successfully when sprayed twice during the incubation period. Further, spray application of probiotics during incubation significantly accelerate the establishment of a healthy microbial composition in the jejunum of SPF chickens. While this early intervention led to a reduction in microbial diversity and evenness immediately after hatch, it seems to promote a more directed and advantageous microbial community. This suggests that early microbial programming by colonization of embryo intestines with probiotics using spray technique can shape gut microbial development in a controlled and beneficial manner. However, SPF conditions may not fully reflect the microbial competition and environmental exposure present under commercial hatchery conditions. Hence, further research needs to be focused on boiler chickens under commercial hatchery conditions. Although the accelerated microbiota maturation pattern may have important implications for host health and disease resistance, additional studies incorporating functional performance, immunological and pathogen challenge outcomes are needed to determine their biological and translational significance under commercial production conditions.
