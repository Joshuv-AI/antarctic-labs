Public Health Indoor Air Surveillance for Respiratory Pathogens: From Pilot to Citywide Implementation
Authors: Samantha Smith, Hannah Barbian, Rachel J. Berkowitz, Sofiya Bobrovska, V. Eloesa McSorley, Alyse Kittner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Supplemental Digital Content is Available in the Text.

Introduction

Since the 2020 COVID-19 pandemic, the Chicago Department of Public Health (CDPH) has expanded its infectious disease surveillance capabilities to monitor respiratory pathogens, incorporating wastewater surveillance (WS) and genomic surveillance in addition to traditional case-based surveillance. However, beginning in late 2021, the increase in at-home COVID-19 self-testing, along with the absence of formal results reporting for these test results, led to fewer cases reported and fewer specimens available for genomic analysis.1,2 After the end of the Public Health Emergency in May 2023, COVID-19 clinical surveillance metrics continued, but with significant changes in data collection and reporting requirements.3 In part to address these gaps, public health has increasingly used environmental surveillance, applying molecular and genomic methods to monitor pathogen circulation.4,5 WS provides population-level data that can help overcome biases inherent in case-based surveillance systems, which are influenced by health care–seeking behaviors, testing availability, and other access-related factors.6 WS has been successfully applied to SARS-CoV-2 and other pathogens, including influenza, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), measles, and Mpox virus (MPXV), providing early warning signals and correlating with case-based surveillance.7–11

However, establishing and maintaining WS infrastructure is logistically complex and resource-intensive, as the process of identifying sampling sites with appropriate and safe access points, coordinating with specialized laboratories and wastewater utilities, and sustaining collection schedules with the technical personnel requires substantial ongoing coordination.11,12 Most (89%) of the WS sites are located at wastewater treatment plants, where diluted signals may result in missed localized outbreaks and limit the ability to trace sources to specific subpopulations or sites.13 Furthermore, the pathogen shedding dynamics, DNA/RNA degradation during transport and storage, and environmental factors such as rainfall and temperature introduce uncertainty in WS data interpretation.5,14 Finally, wastewater sampling may be established at facilities and support facility outbreak responses; however, it can be complicated by plumbing, require coordination with utilities, and necessitate tracer studies, appropriate sampling devices, and on-site feasibility assessments to ensure actionable data.15

To complement existing surveillance methods and address these limitations, CDPH implemented an indoor air surveillance (IAS) program. Air sampling captures pathogen-containing droplets and aerosols, enabling pathogen detection independent of individual testing behavior. In 2003, the Department of Homeland Security implemented outdoor air surveillance through the Biowatch program to detect aerosolized pathogens and enable local health officials to respond rapidly to potential bioterrorism threats.16 More recently, researchers have explored the use of indoor air sampling for public health surveillance, with proof-of-concept room-scale studies demonstrating that SARS-CoV-2 can be consistently detected.17 In real-world environments, detection of respiratory pathogens, including SARS-CoV-2, RSV, influenza, and adenovirus, by air samplers has been reported in hospitals, schools, daycares, nursing homes, shelters, coffee shops, and airports.18–23 Pathogen air surveillance has been shown to correlate with facility-level indicators, such as absenteeism at schools and site-specific known clinical cases.21,23 Similar correlations with population-level data, including national clinical trends and WS, have been demonstrated.20,24,25 Furthermore, air samples have been successfully used for genomic surveillance, and SARS-CoV-2 lineages detected in air samples show similar proportions of lineages from clinical and wastewater specimens taken during the same timeframe.24,25 Given the demonstrated effectiveness of IAS for pathogen detection and genomic monitoring, CDPH sought to establish a program across the city to enhance public health surveillance of airborne pathogens. Aggregate analysis of air samples from multiple locations enables facility-level or citywide surveillance, offering coverage that complements population-level WS data and can detect transmission patterns across different scales and settings.

Methods

Pilot Program

A pilot program (February–April 2023) was developed to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and effectiveness of implementing an IAS program at a citywide scale. Feasibility was assessed by adherence to sample collection protocols, ease of shipping logistics, and laboratory processing workflows. Acceptability focused on equipment placement and noise levels. Effectiveness was evaluated through data quality metrics such as detection sensitivity, genetic material recovery, and capability for whole genome sequencing (WGS). Completion of the pilot program informed the procedures and implementation of the citywide program.

Site Selection

Site selection for the IAS program considered several epidemiological and logistical factors (Table 1). Proposed sites were categorized into 3 types: emergency departments (EDs): hospital settings with a high probability for detection of airborne pathogens; congregate settings: correctional facilities, or group living environments for adults and youth; and community settings: public locations with general populations. Site categorization aligned with differing surveillance objectives; EDs capture disease trends from patients presenting with symptomatic illness, congregate settings monitor environments with potential for pathogen outbreaks, and community sites monitor pathogen circulation in the general public and provide broader demographic and geographic coverage. We included multiple sites in each category to aggregate citywide by site type. Site selection also prioritized geographic diversity across Chicago, with IAS coverage across all Healthy Chicago Zones (HCZ). The HCZ framework guides CDPH in promoting health and racial equity across 6 geographic zones in the city.26 We sought to place at least 1 air sampler in each zone to support citywide coverage and representation of diverse communities, including populations that, due to inequitable access to health care or testing resources, may be underrepresented by other surveillance methods. Locations already participating in CDPH's WS program were targeted to create paired surveillance sites, enabling comparison of detections and lineages across environmental sources. Alignment with clinical surveillance focused on selecting facilities, such as hospital EDs, where we anticipated, given data from syndromic surveillance, that there would be enough circulating viruses to capture sufficient genetic material to detect varying levels of pathogen circulation and to extract sufficient material for WGS.

Sampler placement at each site required adequate space, a stable surface, and proximity to an electrical outlet. Placements needed to avoid disrupting the daily operations of the site and be minimally susceptible to public tampering. Most importantly, sites needed staff available to coordinate cartridge exchanges and sample shipments. Engagement with site leadership and staff was essential, recognizing that consistent sample collection relies heavily on strong partnerships.

CDPH scheduled 30-minute video calls with representatives from prospective sites to provide an overview of the IAS program objectives and operations. After placement approval, an in-person visit was arranged to assess site layout and identify an appropriate location for placement. CDPH staff then arranged a deployment visit to set up the sampler and train site staff on cartridge exchange protocols, shipping procedures, and troubleshooting. Step-by-step written instructions were provided at the deployment and through electronic copies. CDPH points of contact were available for ongoing technical support and regularly communicated with all partners to ensure each site could confidently perform cartridge exchange and shipment.

Collection Methods

Two types of air samplers were used in the IAS program: the AerosolSense Sampler (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and AirPrep Cub Sampler (InnovaPrep). Site characteristics and operational needs determined which air sampler was used. AerosolSense samplers provided enhanced security due to their larger footprint, weight, and locking mechanisms, while AirPrep Cub samplers offered flexibility for areas requiring more discreet placement due to their smaller footprint and ability to change flow rate for quieter sampling. To test whether data from AerosolSense and AirPrep Cub samplers could be combined for aggregated analyses, 1 sampler of each type was placed approximately 1 m apart in an ED waiting room and hospital cafe. Samples were collected weekly for 17 weeks in the cafe and 23 weeks in the ED, excluding 3 ED weeks due to power loss in 1 sampler.

Samples were collected in continuous 7-day periods, and sampling periods known to be shorter than 6 days or longer than 8 days were excluded. Staff at each site were responsible for weekly sample changes on Mondays, unless logistical considerations required an alternative schedule. At the end of the sampling period, the used sample cartridge was removed and immediately replaced with a new cartridge using techniques to prevent contamination, including donning nitrile gloves. Stickers were provided to site staff containing a unique identifier consisting of a sample ID and QR code. During installation, new cartridges were labeled with the identifier stickers. Scanning the QR code directed on-site cartridge exchange staff to a secure, web-based REDCap survey that confirmed the exchange, included space for comments, and a drop-down menu for supply requests. After collection, samples were placed in biohazard bags, sealed within a secondary package, and shipped through a courier service to the laboratory within the same week. On receipt, samples were stored at 4°C until batch processing and analysis. Program compliance was defined as the number of weeks that partners performed the cartridge exchange and obtained a valid laboratory PCR result (recorded through a complete REDCap record) divided by the total number of weeks in the program. Reasons for excluded or missing samples included deviations from the sampling period, compromised sample integrity and quality control failures. These samples were not included in the analysis or reported to partners.

Laboratory Testing

Air samples were processed at the Regional Innovative Public Health Laboratory (RIPHL), a CDPH academic partner at Rush University Medical Center. AerosolSense samples were processed as previously described.25 AirPrep samples were processed using manufacturer-recommended protocols and AirPrep Tris Elution Buffer and elution kits (InnovaPrep), including concentrating eluate using the Concentrating Pipette and Ultra Mini tips (InnovaPrep). Tested pathogens included SARS-CoV-2, respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) A/B, influenza A, and influenza B. All laboratory procedures followed appropriate biosafety procedures with quality control measures, including processing an unsampled air cartridge as extraction blanks and timely sample processing (within 5 days of receipt). Quantitative PCR (qPCR) detection was performed using methods previously described.25 Quality control measures included extraction blanks, no template controls and internal human RNase P quantification to monitor sample integrity. Established Ct cutoffs distinguished low-level detections from background signal. Details on RNase P, positivity Ct cutoffs, and definitions of sample validity have been previously described.25

SARS-CoV-2–positive air samples underwent SARS-CoV-2 WGS to characterize the lineages present in the air samples and enable comparison with lineages detected in wastewater and clinical samples. Samples were included in sequencing analyses if they achieved more than 50% genome coverage, and the corresponding extraction blank for that sequencing run showed less than 50% coverage. Quality control measures and analytic pipelines, including lineage assignment, have been described in detail in previous publications.24,25

Data Management

Data management and reporting were handled through REDCap, a cloud-based, secure survey tool that served as the database for sample tracking and resulting.27 During cartridge installation, a digital installation record in REDCap was created by scanning the QR code for each sample, which captured the date, time, site location, and sample number. After laboratory testing, laboratory personnel uploaded qPCR results, including cycle threshold (Ct) values, qualitative detect/nondetect data, and any relevant laboratory processing notes to these records. Ct values reflect the number of amplification cycles required for a signal to be detected, with lower values indicating higher concentrations of target nucleic acid in the sample. Results were made available to partners in real time through a partner accessible REDCap dashboard as soon as they were uploaded. The dashboard displayed data from the past 120 days, including qualitative detect or nondetect results, and Ct values. Data were partitioned in REDCap so each site had access solely to its own data, with no access to data from other sites. WGS data were managed outside of REDCap due to complexity of the data. Bioinformaticians at RIPHL analyzed SARS-CoV-2 lineages and generated lineage proportion charts that were then integrated into reports. Monthly IAS reports included external reports for participating sites and an internal report for CDPH leadership. These reports included both citywide and site-specific data, allowing partners to track sampler-level disease trends within the broader city context.

Statistical Analysis

Percent positivity and mean Ct values were calculated for each pathogen and site type. Spearman correlation, linear regression, and 95% confidence intervals were calculated using GraphPad Prism software v.10.2.3. For positivity data, 95% confidence intervals were calculated using the hybrid Wilson/Brown method.

Results

The IAS program evolved from the pilot program with 5 samplers in 5 sites in February 2023 to its current deployment with 20 samplers in 17 sites (Figure 1A). The pilot program demonstrated successful implementation across 3 assessment criteria: feasibility, acceptability, and effectiveness. The program was feasible, as on-site staff consistently adhered to the sample collection protocol, and the laboratory successfully received, processed, and reported results in the established timeframes. Acceptability was supported by the absence of noise complaints and the identification of appropriate sampler placement at all sites with no concerns. Program effectiveness was evidenced by the detection of all targeted pathogens in collected air samples and the recovery of sufficient genetic material for WGS, which allowed identification of circulating viral strains and demonstrated that the program could generate meaningful surveillance data from IAS. Overall, the pilot confirmed the potential for broader implementation. Three of 5 sites continued participation while 2 discontinued due to reduced need for method development and limited on-site staffing capacity.

After the success of the pilot, the program expanded in 2023 with the addition of 6 samplers at 4 sites and 2 samplers at 1 site in 2024 (Figure 1A). Rapid scale-up occurred in 2025 with 12 additional samplers at 11 sites. Sites targeted during this expansion included EDs, dining areas in medical centers, and community sites with multiple locations (eg, libraries and mental health centers). As of September 2025, there were 5 samplers in 5 EDs, 3 samplers in 2 congregate sites, and 12 samplers in 12 community sites (Figure 1A).

Generally, site staff successfully conducted weekly cartridge exchanges and shipments. Over the course of the program, the mean compliance was 85% (SD = 0.15). Missed collections were primarily attributed to holidays, staffing shortages, and air sampler interruptions, such as being unintentionally turned off during the sampling period. Samplers performed reliably with minimal technical failures, and equipment-related failures were due to handling errors or public tampering. There were no reported issues with noise or disruption of daily operations. Shipping and courier issues were resolved by CDPH staff communicating with the service and sites. Site discontinuations were attributed to external factors rather than program dissatisfaction, indicating high acceptance and engagement across all site types. Laboratory capacity effectively scaled to increased sample volume, with no associated delays in processing or reporting.

Site Characteristics

Sites consisted of EDs, community settings, and congregate settings, covering all Chicago HCZ and representing both generally healthy populations and those at higher risk for respiratory viruses (Figure 1B). Since 2003, 21 sites have participated in the program, with participation durations ranging from 4 months to the full program period; at writing, 5 samplers at 4 sites were discontinued due to site staffing limitations (n = 2) or refined surveillance priorities (n = 3).

Different site categories supported distinct surveillance objectives, although all sites contributed to method development, pathogen trend analysis, and HCZ coverage (Table 2). Emergency departments, which exhibited higher levels of viral activity, provided additional data for understanding pathogen seasonality, tracking SARS-CoV-2 lineage circulation, and supporting specialized retrospective analyses. Certain congregate sites participated in WS surveillance, enabling intrafacility comparisons between WS and IAS.

ED placements included pediatric and adult hospitals, with placements in waiting rooms and hallways. Samples Processed

From February 2023 to September 2025, a total of 1246 air samples were tested. A total of 74% were positive for at least 1 respiratory virus, with mean 1.4 viruses detected in all air samples (SD = 1.2). SARS-CoV-2 was the most detected pathogen in 68% of air samples, followed by influenza A (37%), RSV (27%), and influenza B (11%). In total, 1.5%–2.1% of air samples submitted were excluded from analysis due to amplification in negative controls, incorrect sampling periods, specimens with no visible dust or material, or unusually high or undetectable RNase P Ct values, or other quality issues noted in received specimens. In total, 59% of air samples subjected to SARS-CoV-2 WGS yielded sufficient genome coverage for lineage proportion analysis.

Detection rates varied across site types (Figure 2A). ED placements generally showed the highest detection rates and lowest Ct values, while community settings had the lowest detection rates and the highest mean Ct values (Figure 2A, Supplemental Table 1, http://links.lww.com/JPHMP/B691).

Seasonal patterns in detection rates generally mirrored viral seasonality often seen in clinical surveillance, with RSV, influenza, and SARS-CoV-2 being detected more frequently in winter months (Figure 2B).

In a side-by-side analysis, Ct values produced from AerosolSense and AirPrep Cub samplers were strongly correlated (Spearman r = 0.79, 95% CI 0.72–0.84, P < .001) with comparable Ct values (linear regression Y = 0.9482 × X + 1.572; Supplemental Figure 1, http://links.lww.com/JPHMP/B690). Thus, results from both samplers were aggregated.

Recommendations

Based on program experience, a framework for establishing and sustaining effective IAS programs was developed (Figure 3). The planning and assessment phase defines surveillance goals and standardizes laboratory, cartridge exchange, and data management procedures, including pathogen panel selection, turnaround times, sampling schedules, and reporting. The second phase addresses site selection and characterization, including partner engagement and evaluation of facility characteristics. This includes heating, ventilation, and air conditioning systems, which may influence airflow and pathogen sampling and require input from building staff to assess accurately, along with foot traffic, and cartridge exchange capacity. The third phase covers sampler placement and staff training. Discussion

This IAS program demonstrated that respiratory pathogen surveillance through indoor air sampling is feasible and scalable for a local public health department. The broad geographic reach and range of site types provided valuable insights into pathogen circulation by capturing information across settings with varied indoor environments and populations. Assessing these settings individually generated site-specific insights, while aggregating data provided a more comprehensive picture of citywide disease trends.

A critical operational success was demonstrating that site staff could operate air samplers effectively with minimal training and that devices could be deployed in minutes without specialized personnel. This ease of use has important implications for program scalability, enabling broader and rapid implementation. Air samplers can be quickly positioned in a variety of facilities with minimal disruption, including high-risk environments or during special events. These capabilities could make IAS particularly useful for outbreak response and for detecting rare or emerging pathogens from diverse settings. IAS could also be deployed at transit hubs, such as airports or train stations, where early identification may prevent broader community transmission.18,20

The program evolved throughout implementation, incorporating improved laboratory methods, enhanced data reporting practices, and operational efficiencies. Notable programmatic changes included the addition of quantitative reporting alongside detection. Air surveillance can be adapted to monitor different pathogens. With program expansion, the pathogen panel was reduced from 8 to 4; the original panel included SARS-CoV-2, RSV A and B, influenza A, influenza B, human metapneumovirus, adenovirus, enterovirus, and Streptococcus pneumoniae. Driven by laboratory capacity constraints and partner feedback that the removed pathogens were less clinically informative, this change reduced the number of reported pathogens while maintaining consistent methods for the 4 remaining targets throughout the study period. These adaptations demonstrate the program's capacity for continuous improvement and responsiveness to public health needs.

The program's epidemiological findings are consistent with other studies, demonstrating that IAS can reliably detect SARS-CoV-2 and other pathogens across varied site types.18–25 The successful WGS of SARS-CoV-2 positive samples supports the use of IAS for monitoring viral lineages. Importantly, a previous study reported correlations between IAS, wastewater, and clinical data, supporting the potential of IAS as a complementary surveillance tool.24

IAS provides unique facility-level data, which, when combined with other surveillance data, can inform infection prevention measures such as masking, visitor restrictions, improved ventilation, or enhanced cleaning during periods of high transmission. When data from multiple sites are aggregated, IAS offers insights into pathogen circulation across a city or region. Aggregated site-level data contribute to understanding seasonal trends and the geographic distribution of respiratory viruses. This combination of site-specific and aggregated data strengthens overall surveillance capacity and highlights the value of IAS as a component of a comprehensive, multimethod surveillance system.

Although the program demonstrated feasibility as in the Methods section, several limitations must be considered when interpreting results and planning future implementations. First, the program lacked a comprehensive cost-effectiveness evaluation, limiting the ability to assess the relative value of IAS compared with other approaches. Cost comparisons across environmental surveillance methods are challenging due to factors such as economies of scale, variability in staffing requirements, infrastructure differences, and site-specific operational considerations. This is an opportunity for future study.

Several operational and structural factors constrained data interpretability and may have introduced biases. Site selection was often driven by an institution's willingness to participate rather than surveillance priorities. This approach, while beneficial for rapid program development, may have created a selection bias toward facilities with interest in surveillance and infection prevention. Interpretation of citywide patterns is also limited by the absence of defined demographics for individual sites. Detections may reflect transient presence rather than resident populations, further complicating identification of the contributing population. Uneven spatial distribution of sites and variation in building use may further influence observed patterns. Different categories of placements are represented unevenly across healthy Chicago zones, with 3 of the 6 zones having air samplers exclusively in community sites. Regional patterns may be influenced by this distribution, as respiratory pathogen detection can differ between site types due to differences in population characteristics. Expanding the representation of site types across regions is an area for improvement as the program expands. In addition, samples collected repeatedly from the same sites are not independent, which may lead to underestimation of variability and biased comparisons across sites. The relatively small number of participating sites limits generalizability, and expanding to include additional facilities would provide a broader and more representative picture of pathogen trends across the city.

Reliance on on-site staff, although feasible, led to occasional missed sample exchanges, shipments, and sampler failures due to absences, competing priorities, or improper handling. Informal feedback from staff indicated that these challenges were sometimes compounded by difficulties in scheduling sample collection around other duties, coordinating with multiple staff members, and managing unexpected absences. These operational disruptions led to gaps in data and highlighted the importance of robust procedures, training, and planning for absences. Incorporating staff feedback in future program development could further improve compliance and reduce operational disruptions. Documentation of sampling disruptions and exclusions relied on site annotations in REDCap records which were not always complete, limiting the ability to fully characterize compliance.

Interpretation of the program's data is limited by the lack of documentation of environmental and behavioral variables. Future research and program development should prioritize the collection of building- and behavior-related factors, such as ventilation and air filtration, which have been shown to influence pathogen detection.22 In addition, collecting data on foot traffic and room occupancy represents an important opportunity to enhance IAS. Incorporating these variables would allow for more meaningful comparisons of detection levels across sites, and implementing normalization techniques based on human contribution or occupancy could further improve the accuracy and interpretability of IAS data. The use of facility-level data in IAS raises privacy and ethical considerations. Ensuring privacy safeguards, transparent communication with participating sites, and clear protocols for data handling are essential to protect individual and community privacy.

Conclusion

The IAS program demonstrated that indoor air sampling is a valuable addition to respiratory pathogen surveillance systems for public health. While challenges remain around standardization and data interpretation, they represent opportunities for continued refinement and method development. The developed framework guides future programs to build upon those successes while addressing identified limitations. As infectious disease surveillance needs evolve, IAS offers unique capabilities that complement existing surveillance methods and enhance public health preparedness and response capacity.
