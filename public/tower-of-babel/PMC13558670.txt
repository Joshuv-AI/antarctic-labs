Evaluation of the sensitivity ocular and performance indicators for driver fatigue assessment in a driving simulator
Authors: Fatin Saffanah Didin, Sevty Auliani, Nana Rahdiana, Afif Hakim, Falahal Majid, Hardianto Iridiastadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFatigue is a significant risk factor contributing to accidents in the transportation sector, particularly in bus operations. Signs of fatigue can be identified using fatigue detection technologies. However, there is still ongoing debate regarding which parameter is most effective for detecting fatigue. This study aimed to identify the most sensitive parameters for fatigue detection. A total of 15 participants were included in this within-subject study, in which each participant completed both a baseline condition and a fatigue condition involving 24 h of wakefulness. The results of the study revealed that the percent miss of the Sustained Attention Test (SAT) is a parameter that effectively predicts fatigue. Additionally, the mean Psychomotor Vigilance Test (PVT) and minor lapses PVT demonstrated excellent accuracy in detecting fatigue. Twenty-four-hour sleep deprivation was associated with significantly increased levels of fatigue.Keywords: Fatigue detection, Ocular indicators, Psychomotor performance, Driving simulator, Commercial bus driverSubject terms: Risk factors, Occupational health

Introduction

Driver fatigue is a persistent and complex challenge within the bus transportation sector, with far-reaching implications for safety and operational performance, especially Bus driver vigilance1–4. Bus drivers are at elevated risk due to their demanding work environment, characterized by long working hours, irregular shifts, and tight schedules that contribute to both physical and mental exhaustion. This fatigue often leads to a decline in cognitive abilities, such as slower reaction times, reduced concentration, and impaired decision-making, all of which increase the risk of accidents1,2. Previous studies have consistently identified driver fatigue as one of the primary causes of road incidents involving buses, emphasizing the urgent need to address this issue comprehensively to protect drivers, passengers, and other road users. Therefore, the early detection of fatigue in bus drives is an essential step toward preventing accidents and enhancing road safety1,3.

Addressing driver fatigue requires a holistic approach that integrates improved work policies and innovative technologies to detect driver drowsiness5. Policies such as regulated shift patterns, mandatory rest breaks, and periodic health evaluations can help reduce fatigue among drivers6. Furthermore, advancements in fatigue detection systems, such as real-time monitoring tools that alert drivers when early signs of fatigue are detected, can serve as an effective preventive measure7. Collaboration among enterprises, management organizations in the public transport industry, transportation operators, and researchers is crucial to design and implement sustainable solutions that prioritize safety while maintaining the efficiency of bus operations8.

Various methods have been developed to detect driver fatigue particularly in transportation sector can generally be categorized into two main types6,9,10. First, physiological monitoring systems, which measure parameters such as heart rate variability, eye movement, and brain activity, are among the most reliable methods for detecting fatigue. Wearable devices equipped with sensors to track physical and biometric data have also gained popularity due to their portability and ease of use. Machine learning algorithms are increasingly integrated into these systems, allowing for more accurate predictions by analyzing complex real-time patterns5.

Second, behavioral-based fatigue detection technologies, which focus on identifying changes in driver performance and physical behavior. For instance, camera-based systems monitor eye closures, head movements, and facial expressions to detect signs of drowsiness3,7,11. Other methods also include vehicle-based technologies, such as lane departure warnings. The successful integration of these evolving technologies into workplace safety practices holds immense potential for reducing fatigue-related risks and improving overall safety outcomes12.

Video cameras have emerged as one of the most cost-effective and widely adopted tools for fatigue detection due to their ability to monitor and analyze driver behavior in real-time3. While non-intrusive, these systems provide valuable insights by capturing visual data such as eye movements, blink patterns, facial expressions, and head position key indicators of fatigue7,11,13,14. Advanced image processing techniques and machine learning algorithms further enhance the accuracy of these systems, allowing for timely detection and alerts5. While camera-based systems are physically non-intrusive, they can still introduce a degree of psychological intrusiveness from constant monitoring. Nonetheless, video cameras are highly effective at detecting early signs of drowsiness, making them a practical choice for industries seeking affordable and reliable fatigue management solutions12.

While technologies like video cameras offer significant advantages in detecting early signs of drowsiness15, their implementation often fails to address the specific challenges certain groups of drivers face, such as intercity and interprovincial bus drivers in Indonesia. These drivers operate under exceptionally demanding conditions that heighten their risk of fatigue. Legally, bus drivers are permitted to work up to eight hours per day16, with extensions to 12 h under specific circumstances. Moreover, in practice, many drivers frequently exceed these limits, sometimes driving for nearly 24 h continuously without sufficient rest, particularly on long-distance routes. This prolonged working time and inadequate rest opportunities pose a serious threat to road safety by significantly increasing the likelihood of fatigue-related accidents15. The weak enforcement of regulations on working hours further compounds the problem, emphasizing the urgent need for robust monitoring systems and better management of driver schedules to mitigate these risks.

Meanwhile, previous research has focused on general drivers, with limited attention given to bus drivers, who often face prolonged driving hours. While commonly used indicators such as reaction time, eye closure rate, and heart rate variability have been extensively studied, alternative parameters, such as the percent of miss (% miss) in the Sustained Attention Test (SAT), remain underexplored in the context of bus drivers17. The percentage of misses on SAT provides valuable insights into lapses in sustained attention, which are critical for understanding and addressing fatigue in high demand driving conditions.

Several previous studies have used performance indicators such as blink frequency, blink duration, mean SAT, median SAT, and % miss from the Sustained Attention Test (SAT), as well as mean PVT, major, and minor from the Psychomotor Vigilance Test (PVT), to examine fatigue and its impact on cognitive performance. These indicators have been shown to be effective in detecting how fatigue can affect attention and psychomotor abilities18. By utilizing these established measures, we can gain a deeper understanding of how fatigue influences human performance. This research highlights a critical gap, the urgent need for a tailored, real-time, and video-based fatigue detection system specifically optimized for bus driver’s unique operational environment. Given their paramount role in ensuring passenger safety and maintaining public transportation efficiency, such a system is essential. While shorter detection intervals may enable faster interventions, they risk misclassification due to transient behavioral variations, whereas longer intervals may delay critical alerts for bus drivers. To refine these technologies, achieving a balance between detection speed and reliability remains a central challenge in ensuring the practical application of video-based fatigue detection systems.

This paper aims to systematically compare the sensitivity of ocular (e.g., blink frequency and duration) and performance-based (e.g., SAT and PVT metrics) indicators for fatigue detection and identify optimal parameters and thresholds for accurately assessing and quantifying fatigue levels in bus drivers in a controlled simulator environment. Through simulator-based experiments, the study seeks to identify the factors that most significantly parameter and cut off value for the detection of driver fatigue to determine when these factors lead to potentially dangerous lapses in attention. The findings will contribute to road safety management and driver performance. On the other hand, the results provide practical recommendations for policy improvements, driver training, and fatigue management technology in the transportation sector. By addressing this critical issue, the study aims to enhance the overall safety of long-distance bus travel.

Methods

Participants

The participants in this study were fifteen licensed male drivers who volunteered to take part in a within-subject study. The drivers were between the ages of 20 and 40 years old and were classified as having either an intermediate or moderate morning chronotype. The statistical power analysis showed 0.767, indicating a moderate level of power, which is generally acceptable for exploratory research studies. The power analysis was conducted using a significance level of 0.05 and a medium effect size assumption based on Cohen’s criteria. Furthermore, all respondents had no reported sleep problems in the month leading up to the study, were in good health, and had no history of chronic illness. This study was conducted in accordance with ethical guidelines and received approval from the Research Ethics Committee of Bandung Institute of Technology (KEP/II/2024/X/M130924SA-KPTA). All participants provided written informed consent before participation, ensuring their voluntary involvement and understanding of the research objectives. The study protocol complied with applicable ethical guidelines, including those outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.

Procedure

Several gold-standard tools were employed for measurement, including an eye tracker for measuring ocular indicator (blink duration and blink frequency), the Sustained Attention Test (SAT) for tracking alertness, the Psychomotor Vigilance Task (PVT) for tracking vigilance, and the Karolinska Sleepiness Scale (KSS) questionnaire to assess participants’ levels of drowsiness during the driving simulation. The eye movement changes were monitored using an eye tracker (Gaze Point, Canada) that was installed inside the simulator cabin. Additionally, participants wore a smartwatch to ensure their sleep duration met the study’s requirements. The driving simulation was conducted using a Driving controller (Logitech G29, Swiss) and the Bus Simulator Indonesia 4.0 (Maleo, Indonesia), which simulated realistic bus driving conditions fit in Indonesian conditions.

Participants were asked to complete the informed consent form and attend simulator training one day before the experiment. During the training session, they also explained the experimental procedure and the instruments that would be used. As this study employed a counterbalanced within-subject design, each participant completed both the baseline and fatigue conditions on separate measurement days according to a predetermined schedule. In the baseline condition, participants were instructed to obtain sufficient sleep by going to bed at 21:00 and waking at 05:00. In the fatigue condition, participants were required to remain awake for 24 h prior to the experiment. The driving simulation under the baseline condition was conducted from 07:30 to 12:30, whereas under the fatigue condition it was conducted from 05:00 to 10:00. The simulation was conducted for one hour19 with a travel distance of 60 km from point A to point B. The weather condition in the simulator was set to clear weather with normal traffic density. On each measurement day, participants completed a series of assessments, including the KSS, VAS, SAT, and PVT, both before and after the driving simulation. During the simulation, an eye tracker and a camera were used to continuously monitor fatigue-related changes throughout the session (Fig. 1).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics V.25 to evaluate the relationships and predictive power of the variables under study. The independent variable in this study is sleeping condition and the dependent variable in this study was fatigue level, which was assessed using various ocular and performance-based parameters. This study employs repeated measures ANOVA in its experiment to analyze the within-subject differences across multiple conditions. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was utilized to compare paired samples, assessing differences between pre- and post-intervention measurements. Exploratory logistic regression analysis was performed to assess the association between each parameter and fatigue outcome, and odds ratios with 95% confidence intervals were estimated. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was subsequently used to evaluate the discriminatory ability of the significant parameters by calculating the area under the curve (AUC). The Youden Index was applied to determine the optimal fatigue cut-off point. Considering the limited sample size, these findings should be interpreted cautiously. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses. No adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied, as the analyses were exploratory in nature and intended to identify potential fatigue-related indicators.

Discussions

Fatigue can be identified through changes in physiological, psychological, self-reported scales, and performance aspects4,20. Several studies have shown that physiological indicators, such as eye movement, are highly effective in detecting fatigue. Blink duration and blink frequency are often considered effective in predicting fatigue, both at mild and severe levels7,14,21. This study aims to identify sensitive parameters for detecting fatigue as a basis for developing fatigue detection technology. Additionally, this study seeks to measure the level of driver fatigue after being awake for 24 h. The findings of this study produced two key results. First, meanpvt (cut-off = 0.309 ms), minor (3), and %miss (13%) was identified as the best parameters for detecting fatigue. Second, there is a significant effect of sleep conditions on the level of fatigue.

The instruments used in this study were the Sustained Attention Test (SAT) and the Psychomotor Vigilance Task (PVT). While both utilize reaction time as a core measurement principle, they tap into different cognitive domains. The PVT primarily measures simple vigilance and motor reflexes22. In contrast, the SAT incorporates a working memory and inhibitory control component, requiring subjects to maintain a representation of target stimuli while suppressing responses to non-targets23. This distinction is critical in fatigue research because executive functions and working memory are often the first to be impaired by mental fatigue, preceding the collapse of simple vigilant attention17. Therefore, the SAT offers greater ecological validity for complex tasks such as bus driving, where operators must process dynamic environmental information and manage memory intensive decision making17. The use of the SAT is highly suitable for tasks that require memory abilities and holds great potential for application in bus driving contexts.

The SAT parameter indicates that fatigue can be predicted based on the %miss value. This may be because %miss reflects attentional lapses more directly than conventional reaction time measures. In fatigue conditions, impaired performance may be expressed not only through slower responses, but also through failures to respond, particularly in tasks that require sustained attention and vigilance24. Research on the use of SAT parameters in the transportation sector remains limited. Most previous studies have relied on the PVT as the standard instrument for detecting fatigue14,21,25,26. Moreover, the PVT parameters demonstrate that minor lapses and mean reaction time exhibit high accuracy in detecting fatigue. In general, minor lapses and mean reaction time have long been recognized as reliable indicators for identifying fatigue14,21. The findings of this study further reinforce the notion that both parameters are highly suitable as primary indicators for detecting fatigue.

In contrast, ocular indicators showed opposite results. The findings suggest that neither blink duration nor blink frequency was an effective predictor of fatigue in this specific experimental context. Beyond technical constraints such as positioning and potential environmental noise, which may interfere with image processing, this ineffectiveness could be attributed to complex physiological mechanisms and high inter-individual variability. Theoretically, the relationship between blinking behavior and fatigue is not always linear. In this study, the prolonged driving simulator task likely induced “Staring Effect” among participants. As cognitive fatigue increased, drivers tended to stare fixedly at the simulated road to maintain lane tracking, which temporarily suppressed blink frequency and artificially altered normal ocular patterns, thereby masking expected cognitive fatigue signs26. Furthermore, the dissociation between ocular behavior and internal state where a subject may maintain open eyes while experiencing microsleep potentially limits the sensitivity of non-intrusive ocular measures compared to neurophysiological data27. To address these limitations, future studies should consider the use of higher-frequency eye tracker head-mounted devices to minimize external interference, control ambient lightning conditions, and account for individual physiological baselines to enhance detection accuracy.

Most current studies tend to focus on physiological aspects as the basis for fatigue detection technology, such as the use of driving monitoring systems (DMS) and cameras to monitor driver behavior and eye movement changes while driving. Physiological aspects are indeed highly effective in detecting fatigue. However, measurement results are often influenced by demographic factors, such as eyelid size and individual differences in blinking characteristics. The PVT and SAT-based approaches can mitigate the impact of these variabilities. There are no significant differences in physiological measurements among users of PVT and SAT. All workers can undergo testing without substantial disparities in results between participants.

Fatigue is a risk influenced by various factors, both work-related and non-work-related20,27. The findings of this study are expected to serve as a foundation for the development of performance-based fatigue detection technology, commonly referred to as work readiness technology. This technology enables the screening of drivers exposed to fatigue-inducing factors, particularly non-work-related factors such as sleep deprivation and poor sleep quality.

The results of this study indicate that extending sleep deprivation to 24 h contributes to increased levels of fatigue among participants. Previous studies have reported that sleep deprivation has a significant impact on driving performance4,9,28. The study stated that sleeping less than 12 h over two days can lead to a performance decline. Additionally, sleep deprivation has been found to have effects comparable to alcohol consumption of 0.05 to 0.10% BAC4. The use of a 24-hour wakefulness period in this experiment represents an extreme fatigue condition that is still plausible in high-risk transportation contexts.

Therefore, a minimum sleep duration of 7 h per day is essential before driving, especially in all transportation sectors. In addition to implementing fatigue detection technology, policies regarding sleep duration from companies should also be considered. Companies could introduce a fatigue declaration form as an assurance that drivers have adhered to the company’s sleep regulations. If sufficient funds are available, the use of smartwatches should be considered to objectively measure sleep duration and quality.

Direct fatigue measurement in the field is widely considered as the gold standard for validity. However, there is strong evidence of comparability between controlled laboratories and naturalistic environments. For instance, Mulhall et al.14 demonstrated that ocular parameters, such as blink duration and mean eye closure speed, are robust predictors of driving impairment, effectively detecting lane departures and microsleep during naturalistic driving study. Anderson et al.21 further established the reliability of these ocular metrics in controlled setting, validating that the subjective sleepiness correlates strongly with both objective performance lapses (PVT) and subjective sleepiness (KSS). Most importantly, the laboratory to field consistency is reinforced by Fors et al.29, who directly compared simulator and real road driving performance and found that physiological markers of sleepiness exhibit similar patterns and levels across both environments. These findings collectively suggest that laboratory-based frameworks serve as reliable proxies for fatigue mechanisms. This approach is particularly crucial in studies where field implementation poses safety risks, high logistics costs, or requires strict environmental control, such as monitoring the immediate and standardized impact of interventions.

This study has at least five limitations, first the small sample size may result in variability in the research findings. Second, because the ROC analysis in this study did not include bootstrap resampling or cross-validation to assess the stability of the AUC, the discriminative performance of these parameters should be interpreted with caution. Third, this study was conducted using a simulator in an open laboratory setting, which may have allowed unwanted stimuli to influence the measurement results. Fourth, the results from the blink test may also be influenced by the position of the camera/eye tracker, which could have affected the quality of eye data acquisition. Therefore, future studies are recommended to conduct simulations in a closed cabin to minimize external disturbances and combine with naturalistic driving study to ensure that the parameters used demonstrate consistent performance in measuring fatigue in real- world environments. The relatively small sample size in this study may limit the robustness and generalizability of the findings. The ROC analysis results should be interpreted with caution and further validated using larger sample sizes in future studies. Additionally, subsequent research should incorporate a larger sample size to increase the statistical power and enhance the generalizability of the findings. Fifth, the eye-tracking data quality may be improved in future studies using head-mounted eye trackers or closed-cabin simulator settings.

Conclusion

This study indicated that 24 h of sustained wakefulness significantly impairs drivers’ alertness, with performance-based metrics demonstrating high sensitivity within this experimental context. Specifically, the Sustained Attention Test (SAT) (%miss) and PVT minor lapses emerged as promising indicators for identifying fatigue related performance degradation. While these findings provide a preliminary technical foundation for “Fit for Duty” assessments and monitoring systems in Indonesia’s transportation sector, further research is required to validate these parameters across larger, more diverse driver populations and varying operation environments. Future studies should increase the sample size and employ cross-validation or bootstrap resampling to enhance the reliability of the ROC analysis. Additionally, future research should explore the integration of these performance metrics with real-time physiological sensors to enhance predictive reliability. By refining these objective thresholds, stakeholders such as the Ministry of Transportation can work towards data-driven safety policies, offering a more robust alternative to subjective fatigue reporting and potentially reducing accident risks in bus operations.
