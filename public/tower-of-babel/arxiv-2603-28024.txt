Nonlinear modulational instability of two-dimensional deep hydroelastic Stokes waves
Lizhe Wan, Jiaqi Yang

Abstract
. In this paper, we study the nonlinear modulational instability of two-dimensional hydroelastic Stokes waves in infinite depth. We first justify a focusing cubic nonlinear Schrödinger (NLS) approximation result for 2D deep hydroelastic wave system in the spirit of Ifrim-Tataru [ 22 ] . Then we exploit the instability mechanism of the cubic NLS to prove that the Stokes waves are nonlinearly unstable under long-wave perturbations.

35Q35, 76B15, 76E17

1.
Introduction

The hydroelastic wave problem concerns the interaction between elastic structures and hydrodynamic excitation, and arises in a wide range of applications, including biology, medical science, and ocean engineering (see
[
23
,
31
]
and the references therein).
Starting from Cosserat shell theory under Kirchhoff’s hypotheses, which accounts for both bending stresses and membrane-stretching tension, Toland
[
33
]
introduced a fully nonlinear elastic model for two-dimensional hydroelastic waves that possesses a clear Hamiltonian structure; see
[
32
]
for the membrane density considered and
[
4
]
for the existence of steady periodic traveling hydroelastic waves.
This model was later extended to three spatial dimensions by Plotnikov and Toland
[
29
]
.
Further developments in numerical simulation, experimentation, and applications of hydroelastic waves can be found in
[
25
,
28
]
.
For the Cauchy problem, we refer the reader to our recent work
[
34
]
and the references therein.

In this paper, we are concerned with the nonlinear modulational instability of two-dimensional deep pure hydroelastic waves.
The modulational instability, which is also known as the Benjamin–Feir instability or sideband instability, was discovered by Benjamin and Feir
[
5
]
in 1967, whereby a small long-wave perturbation of a small Stokes wave leads to exponential instability.
This is a phenomenon whereby deviations from a periodic waveform are reinforced by the nonlinearity, leading to the generation of spectral sidebands and the eventual breakup of the waveform into a train of pulses.
The modulational instability also exists in various dispersive equations. See Whitham
[
37
]
, Benney and Newell
[
6
]
, Lighthill
[
26
]
, Ostrovsky and Zakharov
[
39
]
, Jin, Liao, and Lin in
[
24
]
.

In the context of water waves, however, a rigorous proof of linear modulational instability (also referred to as spectral instability) for the full Euler equations has only been established relatively recently.
In the 1990s, Bridges and Mielke
[
11
]
proved spectral modulational instability for finite-depth water waves linearized around a small-amplitude Stokes wave.
More recently, significant progress has been made in the infinite-depth setting.
In a breakthrough result, Nguyen and Strauss
[
27
]
demonstrated spectral modulational instability under long-wave perturbations (i.e., perturbations with frequencies near zero).
Berti, Maspero, and Ventura
[
7
,
8
]
further advanced the field by characterizing all unstable modes in both finite and infinite depth regimes.
In addition, they proved in
[
9
]
that small-amplitude Stokes waves are linearly modulationally unstable at the critical Whitham–Benjamin depth.
More recently, in
[
10
]
, Berti, Maspero, and Ventura established the existence of the first isola of modulational instability in the deep-water case under longitudinal perturbations.
Building on the modulational approximation of the water wave system and the instability mechanism of the focusing cubic nonlinear Schrödinger equation, Chen and Su
[
13
]
established nonlinear instability of small-amplitude Stokes waves in infinite depth under long-wave perturbations.

Let us now introduce the mathematical formulation of two-dimensional hydroelastic waves, where the fluid domain at time
t
is denoted by
\Omega_{t}\subset\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}
and defined as the region below the graph of a function
\eta:\mathbb{R}_{t}\times\mathbb{T}_{x}\to\mathbb{R}
,

\Omega_{t}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}:y<\eta(t,x)\}.

Its free boundary, corresponding to the deformed elastic sheet, is given by

\Sigma_{t}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{T}\times\mathbb{R}:y=\eta(t,x)\}.

In the absence of gravity, the fluid motion is governed by the following system:

(1.1)

\begin{cases}\mathbf{u}_{t}+\mathbf{u}\cdot\nabla\mathbf{u}=-\nabla p&\text{in }\Omega_{t},\\
\operatorname{div}\mathbf{u}=0,\quad\operatorname{curl}\mathbf{u}=0&\text{in }\Omega_{t},\\
\eta_{t}=u_{2}-u_{1}\eta_{x}&\text{on }\Sigma_{t},\\
p=\sigma\mathbf{E}(\eta)&\text{on }\Sigma_{t},\\
\mathbf{u}(0,x)=\mathbf{u}_{0}(x)&\text{in }\Omega_{t}.\end{cases}

Here
\mathbf{u}=(u_{1},u_{2})\in\mathbb{R}^{2}
is the fluid velocity,
p
is the pressure,
\sigma
is the coefficient of flexural rigidity, and
\sigma\mathbf{E}(\eta)
represents the restoring force generated by the elastic sheet, expressed as a pressure jump across the interface, where

(1.2)

\mathbf{E}(\eta)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\eta_{x}^{2}}}\left[\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\eta_{x}^{2}}}\left(\frac{\eta_{xx}}{(1+\eta_{x}^{2})^{3/2}}\right)_{x}\right]_{x}+\frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\eta_{xx}}{(1+\eta_{x}^{2})^{3/2}}\right)^{3}.

See Toland
et al.
[
33
,
29
]
, Guyenne and Părău
[

or Groves
et al.

[

,
15
]
.

Using holomorphic coordinates, Yang (the second author) derived the two-dimensional deep hydroelastic wave equations in Section

[

The same coordinate framework was previously employed in Hunter-Ifrim-Tataru
[

and Ifrim-Tataru

to establish well-posedness for deep gravity waves and capillary water waves, respectively, and has since been applied to a broad range of other water wave problems (see

Although other formulations may be applicable, we utilize holomorphic coordinates to study the nonlinear modulational instability of the 2D hydroelastic Stokes waves in this paper.

Let
\mathbf{P}:=\frac{1}{2}(\mathbf{I}-iH)
be the holomorphic projection that selects the holomorphic portion of the complex-valued function, with

On the Fourier side,
{\mathbf{P}}

Introduce the holomorphic position variable

Main results

2.
Existence of the pure hydroelastic Stokes waves

In this section, we establish the existence of the pure hydroelastic Stokes waves, and obtain its first order asymptotic expansion.
We will first derive the Babenko equation that describes the profiles of the Stokes waves.
Then we will use the Crandall-Rabinowitz local bifurcation theorem to construct the solution of the Babenko equation.
Although it is possible to further apply the global bifurcation theorem to obtain refined properties of the Stokes waves, we will just focus on the nonlinear modulational instability of the Stokes waves, and will not proceed further.

2.1.
Derivation of the Babenko equation

First, we derive the Babenko equation following the approach in
[
30
]
.
We note that the Stokes waves can be characterized as a critical point of the total energy subject to the constraint of a fixed momentum.
The velocity
c
can be viewed as the corresponding Lagrange multiplier.
We will therefore use a variational approach to derive the real-valued Babenko equation satisfied by the Stokes waves profiles.

(W,Q)
are holomorphic functions.
For a holomorphic function, its real part equals the Hilbert transform of its imaginary part.
In the derivation of the Babenko equation, instead of writing a complex version of the equation using
(W,Q)
, we will just consider the real version of the Babenko equation using
(\Im W,\Im Q)
.

We define the Jacobian

J=|1+W_{\alpha}|^{2}=(1+|D|\Im W)^{2}+(\Im W_{\alpha})^{2}.

Here, the differential operator
|D|
is defined by

|D|f(\alpha)=\partial_{\alpha}Hf(\alpha).

An alternative definition of operator
|D|
is given via the Fourier transform

\widehat{|D|f}(\xi)=|\xi|\hat{f}(\xi).

The total energy of (
1.3
) is given by

\mathcal{E}=\frac{1}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}|D|\Im Q\cdot\Im Q\,d\alpha+\frac{\sigma}{2}\int_{0}^{2\pi}\frac{((1+|D|\Im W)\Im W_{\alpha\alpha}-\Im W_{\alpha}|D|\Im W_{\alpha})^{2}}{J^{\frac{5}{2}}}\,d\alpha,

where the first integral corresponds to the kinematic energy of the fluid, and the second integral corresponds to the nonlinear elastic energy of the surface.
The horizontal momentum associated to (
1.3
) is

\mathcal{P}=-\int_{0}^{2\pi}|D|\Im Q\Im W\,d\alpha.

Taking the functional derivatives of the energy
\mathcal{E}
and momentum
\mathcal{P}
with respect to
\Im Q,\Im W
, we get two equations.
The first equation is on the functional derivatives with respect to
\Im Q
,

\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}}{\delta\Im Q}=c\frac{\delta\mathcal{P}}{\delta\Im Q},

which can be simplified to

(2.1)

\Im Q=-c\Im W.

The second equation is on the functional derivative with respect to
\Im W
,

\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}}{\delta\Im W}=c\frac{\delta\mathcal{P}}{\delta\Im W}.

By direct computation,

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{E}}{\delta\Im W}=

\displaystyle\sigma\left[\frac{((1+|D|\Im W)\Im W_{\alpha\alpha}-\Im W_{\alpha}|D|\Im W_{\alpha})(1+|D|\Im W)}{J^{\frac{5}{2}}}\right]_{\alpha\alpha}

\displaystyle+\sigma|D|\left[\frac{((1+|D|\Im W)\Im W_{\alpha\alpha}-\Im W_{\alpha}|D|\Im W_{\alpha})\Im W_{\alpha}}{J^{\frac{5}{2}}}\right]_{\alpha}

\displaystyle+\sigma\left[\frac{((1+|D|\Im W)\Im W_{\alpha\alpha}-\Im W_{\alpha}|D|\Im W_{\alpha})|D|\Im W_{\alpha}}{J^{\frac{5}{2}}}\right]_{\alpha}

\displaystyle+\sigma|D|\left[\frac{((1+|D|\Im W)\Im W_{\alpha\alpha}-\Im W_{\alpha}|D|\Im W_{\alpha})\Im W_{\alpha\alpha}}{J^{\frac{5}{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{5}{2}\sigma\left[\frac{((1+|D|\Im W)\Im W_{\alpha\alpha}-\Im W_{\alpha}|D|\Im W_{\alpha})^{2}\Im W_{\alpha}}{J^{\frac{7}{2}}}\right]_{\alpha}

\displaystyle-\frac{5}{2}\sigma|D|\left[\frac{((1+|D|\Im W)\Im W_{\alpha\alpha}-\Im W_{\alpha}|D|\Im W_{\alpha})^{2}(1+|D|\Im W)}{J^{\frac{7}{2}}}\right],

\displaystyle\frac{\delta\mathcal{P}}{\delta\Im W}=

\displaystyle-|D|\Im Q,

which leads to the second equation

\displaystyle-c|D|\Im Q=

\displaystyle\sigma\left[\frac{((1+|D|\Im W)\Im W_{\alpha\alpha}-\Im W_{\alpha}|D|\Im W_{\alpha})(1+|D|\Im W)}{J^{\frac{5}{2}}}\right]_{\alpha\alpha}

\displaystyle+\sigma|D|\left[\frac{((1+|D|\Im W)\Im W_{\alpha\alpha}-\Im W_{\alpha}|D|\Im W_{\alpha})\Im W_{\alpha}}{J^{\frac{5}{2}}}\right]_{\alpha}

\displaystyle+\sigma\left[\frac{((1+|D|\Im W)\Im W_{\alpha\alpha}-\Im W_{\alpha}|D|\Im W_{\alpha})|D|\Im W_{\alpha}}{J^{\frac{5}{2}}}\right]_{\alpha}

\displaystyle+\sigma|D|\left[\frac{((1+|D|\Im W)\Im W_{\alpha\alpha}-\Im W_{\alpha}|D|\Im W_{\alpha})\Im W_{\alpha\alpha}}{J^{\frac{5}{2}}}\right]

\displaystyle+\frac{5}{2}\sigma\left[\frac{((1+|D|\Im W)\Im W_{\alpha\alpha}-\Im W_{\alpha}|D|\Im W_{\alpha})^{2}\Im W_{\alpha}}{J^{\frac{7}{2}}}\right]_{\alpha}

\displaystyle-\frac{5}{2}\sigma|D|\left[\frac{((1+|D|\Im W)\Im W_{\alpha\alpha}-\Im W_{\alpha}|D|\Im W_{\alpha})^{2}(1+|D|\Im W)}{J^{\frac{7}{2}}}\right].

Therefore by eliminating
\Im Q
in the second equation using (

solves the Babenko equation

(2.2)

G\left(\frac{c^{2}}{\sigma},U\right)=0,

where

\displaystyle G

\displaystyle\left(\lambda,U\right):=\lambda|D|U-\left[\frac{((1+|D|U)U_{\alpha\alpha}-U_{\alpha}|D|U_{\alpha})(1+|D|U)}{J^{\frac{5}{2}}}\right]_{\alpha\alpha}

\displaystyle-|D|\left[\frac{((1+|D|U)U_{\alpha\alpha}-U_{\alpha}|D|U_{\alpha})U_{\alpha}}{J^{\frac{5}{2}}}\right]_{\alpha}-\left[\frac{((1+|D|U)U_{\alpha\alpha}-U_{\alpha}|D|U_{\alpha})|D|U_{\alpha}}{J^{\frac{5}{2}}}\right]_{\alpha}

U=0

).
If one can obtain a non-trivial solution

2.2.
Local bifurcation of the Babenko equation

Next, we use the Crandall-Rabinowitz local bifurcation theorem to construct the non-trivial solution of the Babenko equation (
2.2
).
We will assume without loss of generality that
U
is an even function with mean value zero.

Since
U
is a trivial solution of (
2.2
),
G(\lambda,0)=0
for all
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}
.
Although the Babenko equation (
2.2
) looks complicated, it turns out that
\partial_{U}G[(\lambda,0)]V
is simple.

LV:=\partial_{U}G[(\lambda,0)]V=\lambda|D|V-\partial_{\alpha}^{4}V.

First, we show that
L
is a Fredholm operator of index zero.
We write
L=T+K
, where
T=-\partial_{\alpha}^{4}
and
K=\lambda|D|
.

T
is a fourth-order linear elliptic differential operator.
In the space of functions with mean value zero,
T
is a homeomorphism from
H^{s}_{e}(\mathbb{T})
to
H_{e}^{s-4}(\mathbb{T})
, where
H^{s}_{e}(\mathbb{T}):=\{u\in H^{s}(\mathbb{T}):\text{$u$ is even}\}
. Since the inclusion
H_{e}^{s-1}(\mathbb{T})\hookrightarrow H_{e}^{s-4}(\mathbb{T})
is compact, and
K
maps
H_{e}^{s}(\mathbb{T})\rightarrow H_{e}^{s-1}(\mathbb{T})
continuously,
K
is therefore a compact operator from
H_{e}^{s}(\mathbb{T})
to
H_{e}^{s-4}(\mathbb{T})
.
Hence, by Lemma
A.2
,
L=T+K
is a Fredholm operator with index zero.

Next, we consider the kernel of
L
.

\lambda|D|V-\partial_{\alpha}^{4}V=0\Leftrightarrow(\lambda|n|-|n|^{4})\hat{V}_{n}=0,\quad\forall n\in\mathbb{Z},\quad\hat{V}_{n}:=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{0}^{2\pi}V(\alpha)e^{-in\alpha}d\alpha.

If there exists
k\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\}
such that
\lambda=|k|^{3}
, then ker
L=\text{span}\{\cos(k\alpha)\}
.

Then, we verify the transversality condition.
Assuming that
\lambda_{0}=|k_{0}|^{3}
for some
k_{0}\in\mathbb{N}^{+}
, we choose
\xi_{0}=\cos k_{0}\alpha
, and compute

\partial_{\lambda,U}^{2}G[(\lambda_{0},0)](1,\xi_{0})=|D|\cos k_{0}\alpha=k_{0}\cos k_{0}\alpha.

Note that range
(L)=\{f\in H^{s-4}_{e}:\langle f,\cos k_{0}\alpha\rangle_{L^{2}}=0\}
.
Since

\langle k_{0}\cos k_{0}\alpha,\cos k_{0}\alpha\rangle_{L^{2}}=\int_{0}^{2\pi}k_{0}\cos^{2}(k_{0}\alpha)\,d\alpha=k_{0}\pi\neq 0,

we conclude that

\partial_{\lambda,U}^{2}G[(\lambda_{0},0)](1,\xi_{0})\notin\text{range}(L).

Therefore using Crandall-Rabinowitz bifurcation theorem, there exists a constant
\varepsilon_{0}
such that for
|\varepsilon|<\varepsilon_{0}
,

G(\Lambda(\varepsilon),\varepsilon\chi(\varepsilon))=0,\quad\text{where}\quad\Lambda(0)=k_{0},\quad\chi(0)=\cos(k_{0}\alpha).

\Lambda
and

are of class
C^{1}
, and

is of class
C^{0}
on
(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)
.

In the rest of this paper, for simplicity, we will simply consider
k_{0}=1
.
Moving back to
(W,Q)
variables in (
1.3
), this means that
(W_{ST},Q_{ST})
are frequency-localized at around
\xi_{0}=-1
.
We obtain the following result on the existence of the Stokes waves for two-dimensional deep hydroelastic waves.

In the following sections, we assume

for simplicity.
In addition, we will choose

so that the amplitude of the Stokes waves will be compatible with the Stokes of the cubic NLS equation (

3.
The NLS approximation of the hydroelastic waves

In this section, we prove the first main result of this paper, that the hydroelastic wave system (
1.3
) can be approximated by cubic focusing NLS equation on the cubic lifespan.

We begin with a heuristic discussion for the linearized hydroelastic waves around the zero solution.
Recall that the system is (
1.4
).

The dispersion relation for this linear evolution has two branches.
Let
\tau
be the Fourier variable in time and
\xi
be the Fourier variable in space.
Then
\tau
and
\xi
satisfy

\tau=\omega_{\pm}(\xi):=\pm|\xi|^{\frac{5}{2}},\quad\xi<0.

As discussed in
[
22
]
, the NLS approximation
applies to solutions which are localized on a single branch near a single frequency
\xi_{0}
.
In view of the frequency localization of the Stokes waves proved in Theorem
2.1
, we choose
\xi_{0}=-1
and the
-
sign.

Using the second-order Taylor expansion for
\omega_{-}(\xi)
near
\xi_{0}=-1
, we obtain the second-order approximation of the dispersion relation:

\tau=\omega_{0}(\xi):=-1+\frac{5}{2}(\xi+1)-\frac{15}{8}(\xi+1)^{2}.

The corresponding linear flow

(i\partial_{t}+\omega_{0}(D))y=0

can be recast as the linear Schrödinger flow

(3.1)

\left(i\partial_{t}+\frac{15}{8}\partial_{x}^{2}\right)u=0

via the transformation

y(t,x)=e^{-i(t+x)}u\left(t,x+\frac{5}{2}t\right).

Hence, for
y
as the solution of the linear flow,
(w,q)
can be approximated by
(w,q)\approx(y,y)
for solutions near frequency
\xi_{0}=-1
on the negative branch.

Since
\omega_{0}
is only a second-order approximation of
\omega_{-}
, if the frequencies of the solution are concentrated in an
\epsilon-
neighborhood of
\xi_{0}=-1
, we have the difference relation

|\omega_{0}-\omega_{+}|\lesssim\epsilon^{3}.

The linear evolution
e^{it\omega_{0}(D)}
is therefore a good approximation of
e^{it\omega_{-}(D)}
on the quartic time scale
|t|\ll\epsilon^{-3}
.
For
u_{0}=O(1)
,

|e^{it\omega_{0}(D)}u_{0}-e^{it\omega_{-}(D)}u_{0}|\lesssim\epsilon,\quad\text{on }|t|\lesssim\epsilon^{-2}.

Next, we take into account the nonlinear source terms of the hydroelastic waves.
Inspired by the result of the cubic lifespan of the hydroelastic waves in
[
38
]
, we consider the cubic NLS equation

(3.2)

\left(i\partial_{t}+\frac{15}{8}\partial_{x}^{2}\right)U=\lambda U|U|^{2},

for some real constant
\lambda
which will later shown to be
\frac{57}{512}
.
We rescale the functions,

(3.3)

U^{\epsilon}(t,x):=\epsilon U(\epsilon^{2}t,\epsilon x),\quad Y^{\epsilon}(t,x)=e^{-i(t+x)}U^{\epsilon}\left(t,x+\frac{5}{2}t\right).

Then the new rescaled variable
Y^{\epsilon}
solves the cubic nonlinear dispersive equation

(3.4)

(i\partial_{t}+\omega_{0}(D))Y^{\epsilon}=\lambda Y^{\epsilon}|Y^{\epsilon}|^{2}.

The first main result in this paper Theorem
1.1
gives the approximation result which shows that the cubic nonlinear dispersive equation (
3.4
) is a good approximation of the hydroelastic waves (
1.3
).
This section is devoted to the proof of this result, which is divided into three steps.
We begin with the cubic NLS equation (
3.2
).
In the first step, we truncate
U
in frequency and obtain the corresponding
\tilde{Y}^{\epsilon}
.
For the second step, we use the normal form analysis from
[
38
]
to construct a good approximate
solution to the hydroelastic waves which is close to
(\tilde{Y}^{\epsilon},\tilde{Y}^{\epsilon})
.
Finally, we show that the approximate solutions
(W^{\epsilon},Q^{\epsilon})
can be replaced by the exact solutions
(W,Q)
.

3.1.
The NLS truncation

In the first step, we perform the frequency truncation for the NLS equation (

The approach is the same as in Section 2 of
[

Let

Then, we have the following result from Proposition

[

Proposition 3.1

(
[
22
]
)

Let

be a solution of (

that we define above, we have

(1)

The uniform bounds

\|\tilde{U}\|_{H^{k}}\lesssim\epsilon^{s-k},\quad k\geq s.

(2)

The difference bounds

\|U-\tilde{U}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\epsilon^{s}.

(3)

The error estimates

\|\tilde{f}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\epsilon^{s+1}.

We further define
\tilde{Y}^{\epsilon}

The smallness of

above
guarantees that

is frequency localized in a small neighborhood of

We then have

By the above proposition,

In addition,

(i\partial_{t}+\omega_{0}(D))\tilde{Y}^{\epsilon}=\lambda\tilde{Y}^{\epsilon}|\tilde{Y}^{\epsilon}|^{2}+g^{\epsilon},

\|g^{\epsilon}\|_{L^{2}}\lesssim\epsilon^{s+\frac{7}{2}}

Later, we will just consider

34
]
, authors proved the above result with control norm
\mathcal{A}_{2}
replaced by the
L^{4}
based control norm

\mathcal{A}_{\sharp,\frac{7}{4}}:=\|(\mathbf{W},R)\|_{W^{2+\epsilon,4}\times W^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon,4}}.

However, the control norm
\mathcal{A}_{\sharp,\frac{7}{4}}
does not fit the analysis in Section
3.3
.

Note that in
[
34
]
, one of the key estimate that leads to the choice of the control norm
\mathcal{A}_{\sharp,\frac{7}{4}}
is the estimate for the frequency shift
a
:

\|a\|_{H^{\epsilon}}\lesssim\|R\|^{2}_{W^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon,4}}\lesssim{\mathcal{A}_{\sharp,\frac{7}{4}}^{2}}.

One can replace this estimate by

\|a\|_{W^{\epsilon,\infty}}\lesssim\|R\|^{2}_{W^{\frac{1}{2}+\epsilon,\infty}}\lesssim\mathcal{A}_{2}^{2}.

By using
\mathcal{A}_{2}
as the control norm and doing the rest estimates as in
[
34
]
, we can prove Theorem
B.2
.

Appendix C
Instability of cubic NLS equation

In this section, we recall a constructive proof from Appendix D of
[
13
]
on the instability of the Stokes wave of the focusing cubic NLS problem.

For the 1-D cubic focusing NLS equation (
4.1
), it has a Stokes wave solution

u(t,x)=e^{it}.

We consider the following perturbation of the Stokes waves

u(t,x)=e^{it}(1+w(t,x)),\quad x\in q\mathbb{T}

for some positive real number
q
.
Substituting this expression in (
4.1
), then according to the computation in Appendix D of
[
13
]
,
w
solves the equation

(C.1)

(i\partial_{t}+\partial_{x}^{2})w+2\Re w=0.

We choose
k_{0}\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}
such that

\left|\frac{k_{0}}{q}\right|\sqrt{2-\left|\frac{k_{0}}{q}\right|^{2}}=\tau,\quad\text{where }\tau=\sup_{k\in\mathbb{Z}}\Re\left|\frac{k}{q}\right|\sqrt{2-\left|\frac{k}{q}\right|^{2}}.

Given
0<\delta\ll 1
and
s^{\prime}>\frac{1}{2}
fixed, we consider the initial data

(C.2)

w_{0}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{q}}\left(\delta_{1}e^{i\frac{k_{0}}{q}x}+\delta_{2}e^{-i\frac{k_{0}}{q}x}+\eta_{1}e^{i\frac{x}{q}}+\eta_{2}e^{-i\frac{x}{q}}\right),

where
|\delta_{j}|=\frac{\delta}{2s^{\prime}}\ll 1
,
|\eta_{j}|\ll|\delta_{i}|
.
Then
w_{0}
satisfies the estimate

\|w_{0}\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}(q\mathbb{T})}\leq\frac{3}{2}\delta.

The result in
[
13
]
shows that the cubic NLS equation (
4.1
) is unstable near the Stokes wave solution
u(t,x)
in the sense that the perturbation
w(t,x)
does not remain small for all time.
More precisely, we have the following result.

Theorem C.1
.

Consider the equation (
C.1
) with initial data (
C.2
).
Then there exist
\mu
satisfying
|\delta|\ll\mu<1
and
T_{0}=\log\left(\frac{\mu}{\delta}\right)

such that

(C.3)

\|w(t)\|_{H^{s^{\prime}}(q\mathbb{T})}\lesssim\mu,\quad\forall t\in[0,T_{0}];\qquad\|w(T_{0})\|_{L^{2}(q\mathbb{T})}\geq\frac{\mu}{4}\gg\delta.
