Effect of the orexin receptor antagonist, suvorexant, on sleep architecture in the early postoperative period following cardiac surgery: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Karuna Wongtangman, Siddhartha reddy Janga, Omid Azimaraghi, Rafi Khandaker, Tanvi Khera, Jonathan Leff, Stephen J Forest, Aiman Suleiman, Christopher Tam, Stephen Spindel, Pamela N DeYoung, Atul Malhotra, Balachundhar Subramaniam, Matthias Eikermann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatients recovering from cardiac surgery in the intensive care unit (ICU) do not sleep well. Commonly used sedative-hypnotic medications can disrupt sleep architecture and increase the risk of delirium in critically ill patients after surgery. The orexin receptor antagonist suvorexant, improves sleep onset and duration in patients with chronic insomnia. We hypothesized that suvorexant improves sleep onset and duration while also reducing the incidence of delirium after cardiac surgery.MethodsThis multicentric, double-blind, randomized controlled trial was conducted at two university-based cardiac ICUs. One hundred adult patients were enrolled after admission to the ICU following cardiac surgery. Enrollment occurred between March 2020 and February 2025. Participants were randomized to receive either a once daily oral dose of suvorexant 20 mg or placebo. Treatment began on the first night after extubation and continued until hospital discharge or for a maximum of seven days, whichever occurred first. Sleep was recorded using an electroencephalography (EEG) monitor (SedLine, Masimo Corp., California, USA) on the first night after extubation and was scored blindly by an experienced registered polysomnographic technologist. The primary outcome was wakefulness after persistent sleep onset (WASO). Sleep onset was defined as the first 30-second epoch classified by rapid eye movement (REM) or non-REM stages 1, 2, 3 after lights off. Wakefulness was defined as an awake period of 30s or longer. Sleep questionnaires were administered and delirium screenings were conducted every morning until hospital discharge.ResultsOne hundred patients were randomized to receive suvorexant (n = 49) or placebo (n = 51). EEG analysis indicated that neither the median [inter-quartile range] nighttime WASO (200.7 [112.1, 328.4] minutes vs. 184.2 [80.4, 304.2] minutes; p = 0.33) nor total sleep time (224.0 [112.0, 379.0] minutes vs. 253.0 [68.0, 420.0] minutes; p = 0.92) differed significantly between the groups. There was no significant difference in rescue medication (melatonin, dexmedetomidine, benzodiazepine) utilization between the groups. Subjective sleep quality, incidence of delirium, and delirium-free days did not differ between the two groups.Trial registration numberClinical Trials Registry no. Background

More than 65% of the patients admitted to an intensive care unit (ICU) experience profoundly disrupted sleep due to a variety of factors including excessive noise, continuous light, frequent clinical interventions, pain, inflammation, hemodynamic instability, and psychoactive medications [1]. Sleep disruption in the ICU is characterized by severely fragmented sleep, reductions in total sleep time, and decreased rapid eye movement (REM) [2–4].

Sleep disruption impairs immune function and is associated with chronic fatigue, delirium in the ICU, prolonged duration of mechanical ventilation, longer ICU stays, and higher mortality [5–9].

Pharmacologic interventions targeting sleep promotion have been used for decades in post-operative ICU patients, but many hypnotics carry side effects that affect sleep quality and neurocognitive function [10, 11]. For example, there is evidence that Gamma-aminobutyric acidergic (GABAergic) sedatives disrupt sleep architecture [12–15]. Benzodiazepines improve sleep quality subjectively with less awakening and increased stage 2 non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep; however, slow wave sleep (SWS) and REM sleep are inhibited [12]. Dexmedetomidine can also improve sleep quality but may induce disturbed sleep architecture and hemodynamic instability [16–19].

Suvorexant, a dual orexin receptor antagonist, blocks orexin 1 & 2 receptors in the brain, which inhibits orexin-mediated wakefulness pathways in the hypothalamus [20]. The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approved suvorexant tablets for the treatment of insomnia characterized by difficulty with sleep onset and sleep maintenance in four strengths: 5, 10, 15, and 20 mg. In an outpatient setting, suvorexant has been shown to improve subjective total sleep time and reduce sleep latency, while promoting REM sleep [21]. Suvorexant is of interest as a novel agent to improve sleep and reduce delirium in hospitalized patients [22, 23]. No prior study has measured objective sleep quality in the ICU and the underlying mechanism by which improved sleep quality may translate into a reduced risk of delirium.

In this randomized controlled study, we prospectively examined the effects of suvorexant on sleep and delirium among adult patients recovering from cardiac surgery. We hypothesized that suvorexant compared to placebo decreases wakefulness after sleep onset (WASO) and increases total sleep time (TST) during the first night after extubation in the cardiac ICU.

Methods

This multicenter, double-blind, randomized placebo-controlled clinical trial was conducted at two tertiary referral hospitals in the United States: Montefiore Medical Center (Bronx, New York) and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (Boston, Massachusetts). The study protocol was approved by the local institutional review boards at both Albert Einstein College of Medicine/Montefiore Health System (2021–13369) and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (2019-P0000759). The trial protocol was published [24] and was registered prior to patient enrollment at clinicaltrials.gov (NCT04092894). This research was performed in accordance with ethical standards established in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and later amendments.

Study participants

Participants (age ≥ 60 years) admitted to the ICU after coronary artery bypass graft surgery with or without aortic and/or mitral valve replacement between March 2020 and February 2025 were eligible for enrollment. Patients with severe cardiovascular disorders (left ventricular ejection fraction < 30%), renal or liver failure, coma, signs and symptoms of delirium and agitation at baseline, psychiatric or neurological diseases, severe sleep apnea, morbid obesity, known hypersensitivity to study medications, or English language limitations were excluded (Supplement S1). Patient eligibility was assessed and informed consent was obtained preoperatively.

Intervention

After postoperative extubation, patients were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either suvorexant 20 mg or placebo. The random allocation sequence was generated using variable block sizes by an independent statistician not otherwise involved in the trial. The allocation sequence was provided directly to the research pharmacy to ensure allocation concealment. Study medications were prepared and dispensed according to the assigned randomization code. Identically sized study medication was dispensed by the research pharmacy to preserve blinding. Randomization code–specific emergency unblinding envelopes were sealed and maintained in the patient’s medical record for use only if clinically indicated. All members of the care and study team were blinded to the study assignment. The patients and caregivers were also blinded.

The study medication was administered orally between 21:00–22:00 after extubation until seven consecutive doses or until stop criteria (Supplement S3). Rescue medications for pain, agitation, sleeplessness including opioids, antipsychotics, sleep-promoting agents (melatonin), benzodiazepines, and non-benzodiazepine sedatives (dexmedetomidine) were recorded.

Outcomes

Wakefulness after persistent sleep onset (WASO) was selected a priori as the primary outcome to highlight sleep disruption. Sleep disruption is a clinically relevant sleep abnormality in critically ill patients after surgery and is best reflected by impaired sleep maintenance overnight [25].

WASO was defined as the cumulative duration (in minutes) of all wake epochs occurring after the onset of sleep, including recurrent awakenings, during the observation period between 22:00 and 06:00. Sleep onset was defined as the first 30-second epoch classified as rapid eye movement (REM) or non-REM stages 1, 2, 3 after 22:00. Persistent sleep was defined as one epoch (30 s) of sleep. The secondary outcomes were total sleep time (TST), subjective sleep quality, and delirium-free day.

Sleep and wakefulness in the ICU were measured by electroencephalography during the first night after extubation (22:00–06:00), representing the earliest standardized period in which patients were awake, extubated, and able to receive the study intervention. We used the SedLine Brain Function Monitor (Masimo Corp., Irvine, CA, USA), a portable 4-channel processed frontal EEG monitor. Although polysomnography remains the gold standard for sleep assessment, its use in the immediate postoperative cardiac ICU is challenging because patients do not tolerate the equipment well. The utility of the Sedline portable EEG monitor to measure different sleep stages, transitions, and arousals has been studied [26–28].

Manual sleep stage analysis and scoring was conducted by a board-certified polysomnography (PSG) technologist in accordance with the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) Manual for the Scoring of Sleep and Associated Events (Version 3) [29] (Supplement S4).

We used actigraphy in the first 6 patients enrolled in this study. An actigraphy watch (Actiwatch, Philips, Nevada, USA) was applied to the patients’ non-dominant wrist in the ICU and stayed connected until hospital discharge. We analyzed data from the first night during the overlapping periods of both EEG and actigraphy data.

Patient charts and nursing notes were systematically reviewed to identify and document delirium and adverse drug events. Adverse events were a priori defined as any undesirable experience associated with the use of a medical product in a patient. The most common side effects of suvorexant in clinical trials were somnolence, diarrhea, dry mouth, headache, dizziness and coughing. Abnormal dreams, hallucinations, and sleep paralysis are less common side effects of suvorexant [24]. Chart-based review using the combination of Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU) recordings by ICU staff and the analysis of free text nursing notes were used to detect delirium in the ICU. CAM-ICU [30] measurements were taken during every nursing shift (twice a day). The combination of CAM-ICU and free text analysis improves the detection of delirium [31].

Subjective sleep quality was assessed using the Richards-Campbell Sleep Questionnaire (RCSQ) [32] which was administered every morning until patients’ hospital discharge or up to seven days after surgery, whichever occurred first. The questionnaire was administered by trained study coordinators, who recorded patients’ responses. Patients rated five sleep domains (sleep depth, sleep latency, number of awakenings, return to sleep, and overall sleep quality) using a 100-mm visual analog scale. The total RCSQ score for each patient on each day was calculated as the mean of the five subcomponents [33]. Delirium and adverse events were recorded throughout the hospital stay. RCSQ was performed until hospital discharge or up to 7 days after surgery.

Statistical analyses

We considered a 30-minute difference in WASO, with a standard deviation (SD) of 45 min, as clinically meaningful as this threshold is commonly used to quantify clinically significant changes in sleep parameters [34, 35]. It is also referenced in clinical guidelines to distinguish normal from abnormal sleep maintenance [36]. A sample size of 50 subjects per group was needed to detect a difference in the primary outcome between participants receiving suvorexant and those receiving placebo with at least 90% power at a two-sided alpha error of 0.05.

Categorical data are presented as percentages, while continuous data are reported as the means (± SD) and medians (interquartile range (IQR)). Independent t-tests, Mann–Whitney U tests, and Chi-squared tests were used as appropriate. A repeated measures mixed linear model (xtmixed) was used to test for differences in the mean total RCSQ scores between the two groups on each day. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, Texas, USA).

Results

Out of 1957 patients who were evaluated for eligibility between March 2020 and February 2025, 716 patients met the eligibility criteria. One hundred patients were randomized to receive suvorexant (n = 49) or placebo (n = 51, Fig. 1). In sixteen patients, sufficient EEG recordings were unavailable. Accordingly, the primary analysis was conducted in a modified intention-to-treat population including all randomized patients with evaluable EEG recordings.

Consort diagram and Analytic Flowchart. Sixteen EEG recordings were excluded from the primary sleep analysis due to post-randomization technical failures, including corrupted data files and premature electrode removal by participants. Under the intention-to-treat principle, data from 83 patients were available for the RCSQ analysis, and all randomized patients were included in the delirium analysis

Enrolled patients received the study medication for a median (IQR) of 5 (3, 6) nights during the study period. The EEG recording was available in 78 patients (37 in suvorexant and 41 in placebo group) with a mean (SD) duration of 8.8 ± 3.2 h. 24% of the participants were female with the median age of 69 (63–73) years. Most patients were classified as ASA III–IV (96%), reflecting a population with substantial cardiometabolic comorbidity. Hypertension (76%) and diabetes (33%) were common chronic conditions; the prevalence of obstructive sleep apnea was low (≤ 6%). Body mass index was comparable between groups (P = 0.21).

The most frequent operations were coronary artery bypass graft (CABG) (59%), followed by valve replacement or combined procedures. Operative and intraoperative variables were similar between groups. Post-operative mechanical ventilation time, ICU and hospital length of stay, and overall opioid consumption were also comparable.

Rescue medication use did not differ between groups. No differences were observed in other adjunctive or sedative use during the study period (Table 1: Patient Characteristics of Study Cohort).

A significant difference was observed in the distribution of sleep stages. Stage 2 NREM sleep predominated in both groups, accounting for 69.3% (53.9, 80.5) of total sleep time in the suvorexant group versus 72.2% (42.1, 80.2) in the control group (p = 0.95). Actiwatch substudy

The 30-second epochs of actigraphy monitoring were matched with the simultaneous EEG monitoring. A total of 6,592 matched epochs from six patients were analyzed in the pilot study. Although the distribution demonstrated an inverse relationship between sleep depth and motor activity (coefficient − 1.09, 95% confidence interval − 1.51 to -0.66, p < 0.001, sFigure 3), activity was absent in 74.9% (2,486/3,324) of epochs classified as wakefulness on the EEG, emphasizing the lack of mobility after cardiac surgery.

Subjective sleep quality

RCSQ assessments were available for 83 patients with 41 (83.7%) in the suvorexant group and 42 (82.4%) in placebo. The RCSQ was completed a median (IQR) 4 (2, 5) times in both groups during the study period.

The median (IQR) total RCSQ score from the rating scale of five components during the first night after extubation was 34.7 (24.1, 45.4) in the suvorexant group and 33.3 (22.7, 43.8) in the control group (p = 0.85). Delirium

Seventy-nine patients (79%) were delirium-free during their hospital stays. There was no significant difference in delirium incidence (n (%); 10 (20%) in suvorexant group vs. 11 (22%) in control group; p = 0.89) nor in delirium-free days (median (IQR); 7 (6, 10) days in suvorexant group vs. 7 (6, 9) days in control group; p = 0.75).

Safety endpoints

Adverse event rates were similar between both groups (sTable1 supplementary document): Four patients reported adverse events of abnormal thoughts/hallucination, fatigue, or somnolence. One (2%) participant receiving suvorexant and one (2%) receiving placebo experienced somnolence and/or fatigue. Abnormal thoughts were reported in two patients (4%) in the suvorexant group.

Discussion

Among patients recovering in the intensive care unit following cardiac surgery with cardiopulmonary bypass, treatment with suvorexant did not affect wakefulness after sleep onset but may have resulted in deeper sleep.

Sleep promoting agents used in perioperative and ICU settings act through GABAergic, melatonergic, antihistaminergic, anticholinergic, and orexinergic mechanisms [37, 38]. Orexin neuropeptides promote long periods of wakefulness, whereas orexin receptor antagonists shorten the duration of wake episodes [39, 40]. Clinical studies in patients with chronic insomnia have shown that suvorexant improved the time to sleep onset, the duration of sleep, and wakefulness after sleep onset [41–45]; however, our study demonstrated no significant difference in TST or WASO.

Several perioperative and ICU-related factors may explain these findings. Cardiac surgery leads to severe pain, inflammation, electrolyte disturbance, low cardiac output, and bleeding, all of which can impair sleep. In addition, the ICU environment (e.g., light, noise, and frequent monitoring) further disrupts sleep [4, 46]. Opioids administered both intraoperatively and postoperatively can suppress REM and slow wave sleep, and increase fragmentation [47, 48], while GABAergic anesthetics such as propofol reduce REM and deep sleep [13]. Furthermore, on postoperative day 1, 55 (70.5%) patients received vasopressors (phenylephrine, norepinephrine, and/or vasopressin) and inotropic medications (dobutamine), important neurotransmitters found in our central nervous system. Exogenous catecholamines may influence sleep–wake regulation by increasing sympathetic activity and promoting cortical arousal [49, 50].

The 20 mg dose of suvorexant was chosen in this study since it is the highest FDA-approved clinical dose of suvorexant. Under the study conditions, this dose may have been insufficient to produce a measurable effect. Prior phase 3 insomnia trials of suvorexant suggest a dose-response relationship, with higher doses (30 mg and 40 mg) generally showing greater improvement, particularly for sleep maintenance measured by WASO [42]. In a small study of healthy young men, suvorexant 50 and 100 mg significantly decreased sleep latency, WASO, and increased TST, while 10 mg of suvorexant left sleep latency and total sleep time unaffected [43].

In our study, 19 (24.4%) patients had REM sleep during the first night, which is in accordance with a previous report of patients after open heart surgery. One small study using polysomnography followed up six patients after open heart surgery and found that five out of the six patients showed no evidence of REM sleep until the 4th postoperative night [51]. This delay in REM onset has been well-documented in surgical populations, where the combination of stress, medications, and physiological disruption leads to an altered sleep architecture.

There was no significant effect on delirium-free days and adverse events between the groups. A randomized controlled trial by Hatta et al. reported that suvorexant reduced delirium in hospitalized older adults [52], which motivated the present study to use both objective and subjective markers of sleep. Hatta’s cohort included mixed medical and acute care patients, with only about half of the patients admitted to the ICU with a broad range of admitting diagnoses. In contrast, our cohort consisted of extubated patients recovering from elective cardiac surgery in the ICU. These perioperative and critical illness–related factors are recognized contributors to delirium and may be less responsive to pharmacologic sleep promotion with suvorexant alone. Although the delirium incidence in our cohort was consistent with prior reports [52, 53], the study was not powered to detect differences in delirium outcomes.

RCSQ data was available for more than 80% of randomized patients, supporting the feasibility for the repeated collection of patient-reported sleep outcomes in an ICU setting. However, subjective sleep scores remained low in both groups. This likely reflects the influence of postoperative pain, ICU-related environmental disruption, nighttime care interruptions, and ongoing physiological stress on sleep quality.

Four-channel processed EEG monitoring with the SedLine monitor was used instead of full polysomnography. Polysomnography is difficult to perform in the immediate postoperative cardiac ICU since patients have limited tolerance for multiple sensors during the first night after open heart surgery. SedLine allows continuous overnight EEG recording with minimal disruption of routine care. However, SedLine does not include the electrooculography, electromyography, respiratory, or limb-movement channels used in polysomnography. Therefore, sleep architecture results are less accurate when compared to full polysomnographic measurements. Because the same EEG-based method and blinded scoring process was used in both groups, these limitations were more likely to affect the absolute sleep estimates rather than introducing a differential bias.

Our study has limitations. Twenty-two patients were removed from the primary analysis, leaving a total of 78 patients. The observed between-group difference in WASO was small and not directionally suggestive of a clinically meaningful benefit. Sleep scoring results using electroencephalography-based sleep scoring are less accurate compared with full polysomnographic results. In a substudy of six patients, we used accelerometry using an actigraphy wristwatch (Actiwatch, Philips, Nevada, USA) combined with EEG in an attempt to improve arousal detection [54]. However, actigraphy did not reveal meaningful information that could be used for sleep scoring since patients barely moved during the first night after cardiac surgery. Because actigraphy based sleep scoring algorithms rely on detecting movement, its use would have exposed our analysis to the risk of bias due to pain, and sedation, leading to an overestimation of sleep duration. Therefore, we stopped using actigraphy.

We quantified the EEG recordings using the AASM Manual for the Scoring of Sleep and Associated Events by an expert scorer in a gold standard reference sleep physiology laboratory [55]. A second expert scorer randomly and blindly audit epochs from the recordings and did not identify any discrepancies from our initial expert blinded scorer [56, 57]. The term atypical sleep is vaguely defined and thus difficult to quantify with any reliability [58, 59]. Some authors use “absence of spindles” or “absence of K complexes” as a criterion of atypical sleep which we quantified and used as a criterion of stage two sleep. Developing automated methods to rigorously and reproducibly quantify atypical sleep may improve future analyses.

Only nocturnal recordings during the first night after extubation were obtained. Because ICU patients often redistribute sleep across the 24-hour period, our study may not fully capture total sleep time or overall sleep patterns. The first night after surgery was chosen as the time when a treatment effect, if present, was most likely to be detected. Residual anesthetic effects, pain, and acute postoperative physiologic stress may also have influenced the observed results. Patients receiving suvorexant were observed to spend a greater percentage of their total sleep time in stage 3 NREM sleep. As a tertiary outcome, the significance of this remains uncertain but warrants further exploration.

In summary, this is the first study to our knowledge quantifying the effects of suvorexant on sleep characteristics after cardiac surgery. Suvorexant treatment after cardiac surgery did not affect wakefulness after sleep onset or total sleep time.

Take-home message

Among patients recovering in the intensive care unit who underwent cardiopulmonary bypass during cardiac surgery, the orexin receptor antagonist, suvorexant, at a dose of 20 mg did not affect wakefulness after sleep onset or post-operative delirium.
