Fidelity-informed neural pulse compilation of a continuous family of quantum gates with uncertainty-margin analysis
Arash Fath Lipaei 3,†, Ebrahim Khaleghian 1,†,∗, Selin Aslan 4, Gani Göral 5, Zidong Lin 6, and Özgür E. Müstecaplıoğlu 1,2 \orcid 0000-0002-9134-3951

Abstract
We develop a fidelity-informed neural pulse-compilation framework for a continuous family of single-qubit gates on a three-qubit liquid-state nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) processor. Instead of decomposing each target unitary into a sequence of calibrated basis gates, the method learns a direct map from the axis–angle parameters of an arbitrary U 2 ∈ SU ⁡ ( 2 ) U_{2}\in\mathrm{SU}(2) operation to a piecewise-constant radio-frequency control sequence that implements the desired transformation. Training is performed end-to-end through the time-ordered propagator of the driven Hamiltonian using global-phase-insensitive unitary fidelity as the learning signal. We show numerically that a single model generalizes across a continuous range of gate parameters and experimentally validate representative compiled pulses on a benchtop three-qubit NMR device. In addition, we analyze sensitivity to structured perturbations in Hamiltonian and control parameters by introducing a prescribed uncertainty set and performing a comparative risk-aware redesign based on right-tail Conditional Value-at-Risk (RU-CVaR). This stage produces pulse solutions with broader tolerance margins within the chosen uncertainty model. The results demonstrate continuous pulse-level gate synthesis in an experimentally accessible setting and illustrate a hardware-aware compilation strategy that can be extended to other quantum platforms. While the uncertainty model considered here is tailored to NMR, the neural compilation and risk-aware optimization framework are general and may be useful in architectures where calibration overhead, parameter drift, or control constraints make repeated per-gate optimization costly.

[

A common compilation strategy decomposes target operations into a discrete gate library followed by calibrated control routines
[

Direct pulse-level compilation provides an alternative by synthesizing a single control waveform that implements the full transformation. A key challenge is to construct a compiler that generalizes across a
continuous family

23

Given the axis–angle parameters of an arbitrary

Training is performed end-to-end through the physical propagator using global-phase-insensitive unitary fidelity. The resulting compiler maps gate parameters directly to implementable control sequences without labeled pulse data. Numerical results show generalization across unseen gates, and representative compiled pulses are verified experimentally on a benchtop NMR processor.

2
Physical model and control task

We consider a three-qubit liquid-state NMR system with a single global radio-frequency (RF) control channel. Each qubit corresponds to a nuclear spin-
1/2
, and the intrinsic dynamics are governed by Zeeman splittings and scalar (Ising-type) spin–spin couplings. In a rotating frame and under standard secular approximations, the time-dependent Hamiltonian is written as
[
24
]
:

H(t)=H_{0}+H_{c}(t),

(1)

where the drift Hamiltonian is

H_{0}=\pi\sum_{i=1}^{3}v_{i}\sigma_{z}^{(i)}+\pi\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}J_{ij}(\sigma_{x}^{(i)}\sigma_{x}^{(j)}+\sigma_{y}^{(i)}\sigma_{y}^{(j)}+\sigma_{z}^{(i)}\sigma_{z}^{(j)}).

(2)

Here
v_{i}
denote the effective Larmor frequencies (chemical shifts) and
J_{ij}
the scalar couplings between spins
i
and
j
.

Control is implemented through a single transverse RF field with fixed or time-dependent amplitude
A(t)
and phase
\phi(t)
,

H_{c}(t)=A(t)\cos\!\bigl(\phi(t)\bigr)H_{x}+A(t)\sin\!\bigl(\phi(t)\bigr)H_{y},

(3)

with collective spin operators
H_{x}=\sum_{i}\sigma_{x}^{(i)}
and
H_{y}=\sum_{i}\sigma_{y}^{(i)}
.

Time is discretized into
T
slices of duration
\Delta t
, yielding the time-ordered propagator

U(T)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\exp\!\bigl[-i\Delta t\,H(t)\bigr].

(4)

3
Target gates and fidelity metric

The control objective is to implement an arbitrary single-qubit operation on the first spin, while leaving the remaining qubits unchanged. A general single-qubit unitary is parameterized using an axis–angle representation
[
45
]
,

U_{2}(\gamma,\theta,\phi)=\cos\!\frac{\gamma}{2}\,I_{2}-i\sin\!\frac{\gamma}{2}\,\bigl(n_{x}\sigma_{x}+n_{y}\sigma_{y}+n_{z}\sigma_{z}\bigr),

(5)

where
\boldsymbol{n}=(\sin\theta\cos\phi,\sin\theta\sin\phi,\cos\theta)
defines the rotation axis.

The target operation on the full register is obtained by lifting,

U_{F}=U_{2}(\gamma,\theta,\phi)\otimes I_{2}\otimes I_{2}.

(6)

To quantify performance, we employ the global-phase-insensitive unitary fidelity

F(U_{F},U)=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\left|\mathrm{Tr}\!\left(U_{F}^{\dagger}U\right)\right|^{2},\qquad d=8,

(7)

which is the natural figure of merit for coherent gate synthesis in closed quantum systems.

4
Fidelity-informed neural pulse compiler

Rather than optimizing a separate control pulse for each target gate, we train a neural network to represent a
continuous pulse-level compiler
. The network takes as input the gate parameters
(\gamma,\theta,\phi)
, encoded via trigonometric features,

\boldsymbol{x}=(\cos\gamma,\sin\gamma,\cos\theta,\sin\theta,\cos\phi,\sin\phi),

(8)

and outputs a vector of controls.

Training is driven solely by the physical fidelity defined above, with loss

\mathcal{L}=1-F(U_{F},U(T)).

(9)

Importantly, no precomputed “optimal” pulses are required; the learning signal is obtained directly from the simulated time evolution of the controlled Hamiltonian
[
23
]
.

Gradients are propagated through the time-ordered product using automatic differentiation and the Fréchet derivative of the matrix exponential, following established optimal-control techniques. Gradient propagation through the time-ordered unitary evolution follows standard optimal-control techniques developed in the context of GRAPE
[
6
]
. Details of the gradient propagation through the time-ordered evolution and the associated Fréchet derivative are provided in appendix B. This fidelity-informed training ensures that the learned pulses are consistent with the underlying device physics rather than an abstract gate model.

An important practical aspect of this formulation is that the network is not trained to imitate a separate optimizer; instead, the physics model itself provides the supervision. The compiler therefore learns a map from gate parameters to controls directly through the device-consistent propagator. This makes the approach conceptually closer to differentiable pulse synthesis than to conventional supervised regression onto a pre-generated pulse library.

5
Prescribed-uncertainty analysis and risk-aware re-optimization

In addition to nominal pulse synthesis, we study how the learned controls behave when the Hamiltonian and control chain are perturbed away from the nominal model. The purpose of this stage is twofold: first, to quantify the sensitivity of the compiled pulses to relevant error channels; and second, to test whether a risk-aware training objective can enlarge the region over which high fidelity is maintained.

Importantly, the perturbations introduced here are treated as a
prescribed uncertainty set
for sensitivity and margin analysis. They should not be interpreted as an exact statistical reconstruction of the noise process on the experimental device. Rather, they provide a structured way to probe detuning, coupling mismatch, amplitude and phase distortions, and timing errors within controlled ranges.

For each target gate, multiple perturbation scenarios are sampled by modifying the Hamiltonian and control parameters within the ranges summarized in appendices A and C. For each scenario
s
, we evaluate the fidelity loss

\ell^{(s)}=1-F\!\left(U_{F},U^{(s)}(T)\right).

(10)

To compare nominal and uncertainty-aware design, we adopt a risk-averse aggregate objective based on right-tail Conditional Value-at-Risk (RU-CVaR), originally introduced in
[
25
]
and widely used in stochastic optimization
[
26
]
,

\rho_{\mathrm{RU\text{-}CVaR}}=t+\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\langle\max\{0,\ell^{(s)}-t\}\right\rangle,

(11)

where
t
is the
(1-\alpha)
quantile of the loss distribution. In the present paper, RU-CVaR is interpreted as a design principle that penalizes fragile pulse solutions by emphasizing adverse realizations within the chosen uncertainty set. It is therefore used to improve tolerance margins under prescribed perturbations rather than to claim guaranteed protection against the full experimental noise environment.

Additional smoothness and spectral regularization terms are included to keep the resulting controls compatible with finite-bandwidth arbitrary waveform generators.

6
Results

We now present the numerical and experimental results. The main numerical question is whether the proposed model can act as a continuous pulse-level compiler for unseen single-qubit gates in the nominal NMR setting. We then examine how those nominally trained pulses respond to prescribed perturbations and whether risk-aware re-optimization broadens their tolerance margins.

6.1
Generalization across a continuous family of gates in the nominal setting

We first evaluate whether the neural network trained under nominal dynamics generalizes beyond the discrete sample of target gates used during training. To this end, we sweep a dense grid over the continuous gate-parameter space
(\gamma,\theta,\phi)
, spanning rotations up to
\pi/2
along arbitrary axes, and compute the resulting unitary fidelity for each target.

The trained network achieves uniformly high fidelity across this continuous domain, with mean and median fidelities above
99\%
and only weak variation near the boundaries of the sampled region, see figure
1
. This indicates that the model has learned a smooth mapping from gate parameters to pulse sequences rather than simply memorizing a finite training set.

It is worth stressing that this result is more meaningful than a conventional interpolation test over a small discrete gate set. The target family is continuous, and the control landscape is highly nontrivial because the generated pulse must realize the desired transformation in the full three-spin Hilbert space while respecting the device Hamiltonian. Successful generalization therefore indicates that the model captures a structured relation between gate geometry and pulse design, rather than storing a lookup table of isolated examples.

We restrict the numerical study to rotations up to
\pi/2
in each angular coordinate in order to focus on a representative domain with dense and reliable coverage in both training and validation. This choice should be viewed as a controlled proof-of-principle region rather than a fundamental limitation of the framework. Extending the domain toward a broader portion of SU(2) would be a natural next step once the compilation landscape and training budget are enlarged accordingly.

These results support the central claim of the paper: a single neural model can function as a continuous pulse-level compiler for arbitrary single-qubit operations on the addressed qubit, avoiding repeated gate-by-gate pulse optimization or decomposition into a long discrete circuit. Additional statistics are provided in appendix D.

6.2
Sensitivity and uncertainty-margin analysis of nominal pulses

We next study how the nominally trained compiler behaves when the underlying model is perturbed. Here the objective is not to claim exact robustness under a calibrated experimental noise distribution, but to quantify sensitivity and identify tolerance margins with respect to relevant Hamiltonian and control errors. To this end, we apply the compiled pulses to dynamics containing structured perturbations in chemical shifts, scalar couplings, RF amplitude and phase, and timing.

The resulting fidelity sweeps show that nominal pulses are especially sensitive to some perturbation channels, most notably detuning, global amplitude scaling, and timing distortions, whereas other channels induce milder degradation. This is an informative result even without a direct real-noise claim: it reveals which imperfections most strongly shape the local control landscape around the nominal solution.

This pattern is physically plausible. Detuning and global amplitude or timing mismatches act coherently throughout the pulse and therefore accumulate as systematic control errors, whereas some of the other perturbations considered here produce milder distortions over the same sequence length. In this sense, the uncertainty sweeps do more than provide robustness plots: they help identify which hardware-level inaccuracies are most consequential for the learned compiler.

Accordingly, this subsection should be read as a
margin analysis
of the learned pulses. It provides the baseline against which risk-aware re-optimization can be compared and clarifies where nominally optimized solutions are fragile.

6.3
Risk-aware training and tolerance-margin enhancement

We then re-optimize the neural compiler under sampled perturbation scenarios using the RU-CVaR objective introduced above. In the present work, this stage is best interpreted as a comparative design study: we ask whether emphasizing adverse realizations within the prescribed uncertainty set yields pulse families whose fidelity degrades more gracefully away from the nominal model.

The answer is affirmative. Under the same perturbation sweeps used for the baseline study, the risk-aware controller maintains higher fidelities over wider parameter intervals for the most detrimental error channels. The improvement is particularly visible for detuning, amplitude scaling, and timing distortions, where the nominal solution can fail sharply while the risk-aware solution shows a flatter response. At the same time, nominal performance is only weakly affected.

This behavior matches the intended role of RU-CVaR. Because the training objective emphasizes the poor-performance tail of the scenario distribution, it suppresses solutions that rely on fine-tuned cancellations and favors controls whose fidelity remains high across a broader neighborhood of the nominal point. In geometric terms, the robust redesign seeks a wider plateau instead of the sharpest local optimum.

These results do not establish exact robustness to the full laboratory environment. Instead, they show that risk-aware training can enlarge tolerance margins and reduce fragility within the chosen uncertainty model. In this sense, RU-CVaR acts here as a practical mechanism for shaping the control landscape toward less sensitive solutions.

The extent of this tolerance enhancement depends on the choice of the risk level
\alpha
in the RU-CVaR objective. Smaller values of
\alpha
emphasize a narrower and more adverse tail of the scenario-loss distribution, and therefore favor more conservative pulse solutions. Larger values approach mean-risk optimization and place greater weight on overall nominal performance.

Our numerical results show the expected trade-off. Lower
\alpha
values generally broaden tolerance margins more strongly but slow convergence and may slightly reduce peak nominal fidelity. Higher
\alpha
values recover faster training and higher nominal fidelities while offering a smaller gain in adverse-case tolerance. Figure
2
summarizes this trade-off.

6.4
Overall interpretation of the numerical study

Taken together, the numerical results establish three points. First, the nominal compiler accurately realizes a continuous family of single-qubit gates with a single pulse per operation. Second, a prescribed-uncertainty analysis reveals which perturbation channels most strongly affect these compiled pulses. Third, risk-aware re-optimization based on RU-CVaR can produce less fragile control solutions and broader tolerance margins within that uncertainty set.

In the context of the present manuscript, these three points should be regarded as the main numerical message. The compiler solves the continuous-family pulse-synthesis problem in the nominal setting, and the risk-aware redesign improves tolerance properties in a controlled comparative study.

8
Conclusion

We have developed a fidelity-informed neural framework for pulse-level compilation of a continuous family of single-qubit gates in a three-qubit liquid-state NMR system. The central result is that a single model can map gate parameters directly to RF controls, generalize over unseen targets, and generate pulses that can be executed experimentally on a benchtop NMR processor. The main experimentally established contribution of the work is therefore a continuous NMR pulse compiler together with representative experimental validation.

The second contribution is the uncertainty analysis. By introducing a prescribed set of perturbations in Hamiltonian and control parameters, we examined the sensitivity of the nominal compiler and compared it with a risk-aware re-optimization strategy based on RU-CVaR. In the present paper, these results should be interpreted as evidence of broader tolerance margins and reduced fragility within the chosen uncertainty model, not as a complete robustness certification against the full laboratory noise environment.

More broadly, the manuscript suggests a useful division of roles between platform and method. NMR provides an experimentally accessible and well-controlled environment in which to demonstrate continuous-family neural pulse compilation. The underlying design principle, however, is not specific to NMR. Once combined with experimentally calibrated uncertainty models, the same risk-aware compilation strategy could be relevant to architectures in which control overhead and parameter drift are more severe and where robust pulse-level synthesis may offer a larger practical benefit.

In this sense, the present work should be viewed as a proof-of-principle study of hardware-aware continuous gate compilation with a secondary robustness layer. It shows that fidelity-informed neural learning can serve not only as a pulse synthesizer for a single target gate, but as a compiler for an entire gate family, while also enabling comparative exploration of less fragile control solutions. We expect these ideas to motivate further studies involving larger gate families, multi-qubit targets, broader experimental benchmarks, and transfer to platforms such as superconducting circuits and Rydberg systems.

Appendix B
Backpropagation through time-ordered evolution

This appendix collects the expressions required to compute gradients of the fidelity loss with respect to the controls.

Let

U(T)=\prod_{t=1}^{T}U_{t},\qquad U_{t}=\exp\!\bigl[-i\Delta t\,H(t)\bigr].

(16)

The derivative of the full propagator with respect to the phase at slice
t
is

\frac{\partial U}{\partial\phi_{t}}=U_{T:t+1}\frac{\partial U_{t}}{\partial\phi_{t}}U_{t-1:1},

(17)

where
U_{a:b}=U_{a}U_{a-1}\cdots U_{b}
for
a\geq b
.

The phase derivative of the Hamiltonian is

\frac{\partial H(t)}{\partial\phi_{t}}=A\bigl[-\sin\phi_{t}\,H_{x}+\cos\phi_{t}\,H_{y}\bigr].

(18)

We can also consider the derivative of the fidelity with respect to the amplitude, if we want to optimize that parameter as well. In the experimental part, we used a varying control amplitude, whereas in the theoretical part, we perform the robust redesign in a phase-only, constant-amplitude control class. This lower-dimensional ansatz is experimentally attractive and well established in NMR optimal control, but it is more restrictive than joint amplitude-and-phase modulation
[
48
,
49
]
. Since amplitude modulation can improve achievable pulse performance, robustness obtained in the fixed-amplitude setting should be interpreted as a conservative stress test under a hardware-constrained pulse family
[
50
,
51
,
52
,
53
]
.

Using the Fréchet derivative of the matrix exponential, one obtains

\frac{\partial U_{t}}{\partial\phi_{t}}=\int_{0}^{1}e^{(1-s)X_{t}}\bigl(-i\Delta t\,\partial_{\phi_{t}}H(t)\bigr)e^{sX_{t}}\,ds,\qquad X_{t}=-i\Delta tH(t).

(19)

Automatic differentiation frameworks evaluate the required Jacobian–vector products efficiently, enabling end-to-end optimization of the neural network parameters through the physical time evolution, yielding
\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial\phi(t)
which then backpropagates through the MLP via

\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\Theta}=\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\phi(t)}\,\frac{\partial{\phi(t)}}{\partial\Theta}.

(20)

In practice we optimize
\Theta
with AdamW using the hyperparameters in Table
3
. The convergence rate of this network is presented in figure
8
.

Appendix C
Risk-aware re-optimization under prescribed uncertainties

Previously, the network was trained only in the nominal setting. We now consider a second stage in which the pretrained model is re-optimized under sampled perturbation scenarios. The goal of this stage is not to certify exact experimental robustness, but to study whether a risk-aware objective can reduce fragility and enlarge tolerance margins within a prescribed uncertainty set.

C.1
Goal and Setup

We re-optimize the noiselessly pretrained network against sampled perturbation scenarios. The approach is
scenario-based risk minimization

[
25
,
44
,
13
,
26
]
: for each training example (target gate), we draw
S
uncertainty scenarios, propagate the system under each scenario, evaluate the unitary mismatch via fidelity, and optimize a risk-averse aggregate loss (RU-CVaR) computed over the scenario losses.

We retain the same three-qubit Hamiltonian and the phase-only control parameterization as in the nominal case: the network outputs
\boldsymbol{\phi}=(\phi_{1},\dots,\phi_{T})
while the nominal drive amplitude
A
is constant. In the uncertainty-aware stage, however, the
effective
amplitude, phase, and timing reaching the plant are perturbed according to the sampled scenarios defined below. Throughout this appendix, the resulting analysis should be understood as probing tolerance within a chosen uncertainty set rather than certifying exact behavior under a fully calibrated experimental noise model.

Targets and fidelity.

For angles
(\gamma,\theta,\varphi)
we form

U_{F}=U_{2}(\gamma,\theta,\varphi)\otimes I_{2}\otimes I_{2}

(21)

as before, and assess the (global-phase–insensitive) unitary fidelity

\displaystyle\mathcal{F}\!\left(U_{F},U^{(s)}(T)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d^{2}}\,\left|\mathrm{Tr}\!\left(U_{F}^{\dagger}U^{(s)}(T)\right)\right|^{2},\qquad d=8.

(22)

C.2
Uncertainty Model and Effective Dynamics

We model eight stochastic knobs split into (i)
Hamiltonian-parameter perturbations
and (ii)
control/timing perturbations
. Batches are indexed by
b\in\{1,\dots,B\}
, scenarios by
s\in\{1,\dots,S\}
, and slices by
t\in\{1,\dots,T\}
. These knobs define the prescribed uncertainty set used for the numerical sensitivity study.

Hamiltonian parameter noise (quasi-static per sequence).

Nominal coefficients are
(v_{1},v_{2},v_{3})
and
(J_{12},J_{13},J_{23})
. We sample relative, quasi-static perturbations

\displaystyle\delta v^{(b,s)}

\displaystyle\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{v}^{2}),\qquad\delta J^{(b,s)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{J}^{2}),

(23)

and set (componentwise)

\displaystyle v^{(b,s)}

\displaystyle=(1+\delta v^{(b,s)})v,

(24)

\displaystyle J^{(b,s)}

\displaystyle=(1+\delta J^{(b,s)})J.

(25)

Then

\displaystyle H_{0}^{(b,s)}

\displaystyle=\pi\sum_{i=1}^{3}v^{(b,s)}_{i}\,\sigma^{(i)}_{z}\;+\;\pi\!\!\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 3}\!J^{(b,s)}_{ij}\,\sigma^{(i)}_{z}\sigma^{(j)}_{z}.

(26)

Control/timing noise.

We draw:

amplitude bias:

\displaystyle\alpha_{g}^{(b,s)}\sim 1+\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{A,\text{bias}}^{2}),

(27)

amplitude jitter:

\displaystyle\alpha_{j}^{(b,s)}(t)\sim 1+\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{A,\text{jit}}^{2}),

(28)

phase offset:

\displaystyle\phi_{0}^{(b,s)}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\phi 0}^{2}),

(29)

phase jitter:

\displaystyle\phi_{j}^{(b,s)}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{\phi,\text{jit}}^{2}),

(30)

timing scale:

\displaystyle\beta_{dt}^{(b,s)}\sim 1+\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{dt}^{2}),

(31)

timing jitter:

\displaystyle\delta_{dt,\text{jit}}^{(b,s)}(t)\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_{dt,\text{jit}}^{2}).

(32)

Effective controls at slice
t
are

\displaystyle\phi^{(b,s)}_{\text{eff}}(t)

\displaystyle=\phi_{t}\;+\;\phi_{0}^{(b,s)}\;+\;\phi_{j}^{(b,s)}(t),

(33)

\displaystyle A^{(b,s)}_{\text{eff}}(t)

\displaystyle=A\ \alpha_{g}^{(b,s)}\ \alpha_{j}^{(b,s)}(t),

(34)

\displaystyle\Delta t^{(b,s)}(t)

\displaystyle=\Delta t\ \Big(\beta_{dt}^{(b,s)}\;+\;\delta_{dt,\text{jit}}^{(b,s)}(t)\Big),

(35)

and the control Hamiltonian is

\displaystyle H_{c}^{(b,s)}\!\big(\phi(t)\big)

\displaystyle=A^{(b,s)}_{\text{eff}}(t)\Big[\cos\!\big(\phi^{(b,s)}_{\text{eff}}(t)\big)\,H_{x}

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\quad+\sin\!\big(\phi^{(b,s)}_{\text{eff}}(t)\big)\,H_{y}\Big],

(36)

with
H_{x}=\sum_{i}\sigma^{(i)}_{x}
and
H_{y}=\sum_{i}\sigma^{(i)}_{y}
. The (time-ordered) propagator in scenario
(b,s)
is

\displaystyle U^{(b,s)}(\boldsymbol{\phi})

\displaystyle=\prod_{t=1}^{T}\exp\!\Big(-i\,\Delta t^{(b,s)}(t)\,\big[H_{0}^{(b,s)}+H_{c}^{(b,s)}(\phi_{t})\big]\Big).

(37)

C.3
Scenario Loss and Risk Aggregation: RU-CVaR

Define per-scenario physics losses (fidelity gaps)

\displaystyle\ell^{(b,s)}(\Theta)

\displaystyle=1-\mathcal{F}\!\left(U_{F}^{(b)},\,U^{(b,s)}(\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\Theta})\right),

(38)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\phi}_{\Theta}

\displaystyle=f_{\Theta}(x).

(39)

In implementation we aggregate risk at the
batch level
. Let the multiset of batch losses be

\mathcal{L}_{\text{batch}}\;=\;\big\{\,\ell^{(b,s)}\;:\;b=1,\dots,B,\ s=1,\dots,S\,\big\},\quad|\mathcal{L}_{\text{batch}}|=B\,S.

(40)

We use
RU-CVaR
—
Right-tail Unbalanced Conditional Value-at-Risk
. Let

t\;=\;\mathrm{Quantile}_{1-\alpha}\!\big(\mathcal{L}_{\text{batch}}\big)\quad\text{(treated with stop-gradient)},

(41)

then the batch risk is

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{RU-CVaR}}\!\big(\mathcal{L}_{\text{batch}}\big)

\displaystyle=t+\frac{1}{\alpha}\,\frac{1}{BS}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\max\!\big(0,\ \ell^{(b,s)}-t\big).

(42)

If
t
equals the exact Value-at-Risk, this equals classical CVaR (Expected Shortfall). Using a stop-gradient on
t
avoids unstable gradients through the quantile while still emphasizing the worst
\alpha
-tail. For comparison (also implemented),

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{mean}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{BS}\sum_{b,s}\ell^{(b,s)},

(43)

\displaystyle\rho_{\text{worst}}

\displaystyle=\max_{b,s}\,\ell^{(b,s)}.

(44)

C.4
Regularization

We optionally add smoothness and frequency-shaping priors on the control sequence:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\text{TV}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{T-1}\sum_{t=1}^{T-1}\big|\phi_{t+1}-\phi_{t}\big|,

(45)

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}_{\text{spec}}

\displaystyle=\Big\|\mathcal{F}_{\!t}(\boldsymbol{\phi})\Big\|_{2,\ \text{high-freq}}^{2},

(46)

where the norm is evaluated only on frequencies above a chosen cutoff fraction. The per-batch objective used in training is

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}(\Theta)

\displaystyle=\rho_{\text{RU-CVaR}}\!\big(\mathcal{L}_{\text{batch}}\big)\;+\;\lambda_{\text{TV}}\mathcal{R}_{\text{TV}}\;+\;\lambda_{\text{spec}}\mathcal{R}_{\text{spec}}.

(47)

C.5
Backpropagation Under Stochastic Dynamics

Gradients propagate through: MLP
\to
phases
\to
uncertain Hamiltonians
\to
per-slice matrix exponentials
\to
risk aggregator. For a fixed scenario
(b,s)
,

\displaystyle\frac{\partial U^{(b,s)}}{\partial\phi_{t}}

\displaystyle=U^{(b,s)}_{T:t+1}\,\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi_{t}}\exp\!\big(-i\Delta t^{(b,s)}(t)\,H^{(b,s)}(t)\big)\,U^{(b,s)}_{t-1:1},

(48)

with the Fréchet derivative of the matrix exponential as in the noiseless section, and

\displaystyle\frac{\partial H^{(b,s)}(t)}{\partial\phi_{t}}

\displaystyle=A^{(b,s)}_{\text{eff}}(t)\Big[-\sin\!\big(\phi^{(b,s)}_{\text{eff}}(t)\big)\,H_{x}+\cos\!\big(\phi^{(b,s)}_{\text{eff}}(t)\big)\,H_{y}\Big].

(49)

For RU-CVaR, only scenarios exceeding
t
contribute:

\frac{\partial\rho_{\text{RU-CVaR}}}{\partial\ell^{(b,s)}}\;=\;\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{\alpha\,B\,S},&\ell^{(b,s)}>t,\\[6.0pt]
0,&\ell^{(b,s)}\leq t.\end{cases}

(50)

Automatic differentiation handles Jacobian–vector products through the time-ordered products; the threshold
t
is treated as a constant (stop-gradient).

Appendix D
Uncertainty-margin results

This appendix collects the detailed perturbation sweeps for nominally trained and risk-aware controllers. Each panel shows the average fidelity over randomly sampled target gates while sweeping a single perturbation channel and keeping the remaining parameters fixed at their nominal values; see figure
10
. The purpose of these plots is to visualize sensitivity and tolerance margins within the prescribed uncertainty set.

The baseline controller, trained only in the nominal setting, achieves near-unit fidelity when the system is perfectly calibrated. Away from that point, however, some channels induce sharp degradation. In particular, global amplitude miscalibration, timing-scale errors, and strong detuning perturbations can substantially reduce fidelity, while static phase bias, coupling perturbations, and several jitter channels are comparatively less harmful. This establishes the nominal controller as high performing but locally fragile along selected directions in parameter space.

Risk-aware training using the RU-CVaR objective reshapes this behavior. For
\alpha=0.2
, the controller sacrifices a small amount of peak nominal fidelity but greatly broadens the region over which high fidelity is maintained for the most detrimental perturbation channels. The curves become noticeably flatter for amplitude and timing errors, and the sensitivity to detuning is reduced as well. This should be interpreted as a tolerance-margin gain within the chosen uncertainty set.

At
\alpha=0.5
, the controller achieves an attractive compromise between nominal performance and adverse-case tolerance. The fidelity remains high across most of the perturbation ranges considered, while the nominal value stays close to that of the baseline model. Training with
\alpha=0.8
moves further toward mean-risk optimization, preserving very high nominal fidelities while still outperforming the baseline for the most harmful perturbations.

Overall, the appendix figures make the same point as the main text: the nominal compiler solves the gate-synthesis task, whereas RU-CVaR re-optimization can reduce fragility and enlarge tolerance margins under prescribed perturbations. These results should not be read as a complete claim of experimental robustness, but rather as a structured comparative uncertainty study.
