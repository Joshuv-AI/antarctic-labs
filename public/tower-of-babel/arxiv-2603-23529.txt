Konkani LLM: Multi-Script Instruction Tuning and Evaluation for a Low-Resource Indian Language
Reuben Chagas Fernandes, Gaurang S. Patkar

Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) consistently under perform in low-resource linguistic contexts such as Konkani. This performance deficit stems from acute training data scarcity compounded by high script diversity across Devanagari, Romi and Kannada orthographies. To address this gap, we introduce Konkani-Instruct-100k, a comprehensive synthetic instruction-tuning dataset generated through Gemini 3. We establish rigorous baseline benchmarks by evaluating leading open-weights architectures including Llama 3.1, Qwen2.5 and Gemma 3 alongside proprietary closed-source models. Our primary contribution involves the development of Konkani LLM, a series of fine-tuned models optimized for regional nuances. Furthermore, we are developing the Multi-Script Konkani Benchmark to facilitate cross-script linguistic evaluation. In machine translation, Konkani LLM delivers consistent gains over the corresponding base models and is competitive with and in several settings surpasses proprietary baselines (Table 4 ).

1
Introduction

1.1
Background

Konkani is an Indo-Aryan language distinct to the Goa and Konkan regions of India. It presents substantial challenges for Natural Language Processing (NLP) due to its multiscript nature. The language uses Devanagari, Romi (Latin) and Kannada scripts officially. Konkani retains low-resource status despite its recognition as a scheduled language of India. High-quality parallel corpora remain scarce. Existing datasets suffer from fragmentation across these diverging scripts which impedes the training of unified language models.

1.2
The Gap: Language Contamination

Large language models (LLMs) such as Deepseek V3.2, Claude sonnet 4.6 and GPT-5.2 achieve strong multilingual performance but Konkani remains challenging. While some base models may have encountered Konkani during pre-training, they typically lack sufficient instruction-tuning data to reliably treat Konkani as a distinct target language.

1.3
Proposed Solution

We adopt a pipeline centered on Synthetic Data Generation and parameter-efficient fine-tuning to mitigate these issues. We used Gemini 3 to synthesize Konkani-Instruct-100k in the absence of native instruction-tuning data. The dataset balances Devanagari, Romi and Kannada scripts. We then fine-tune open-weight architectures, including Llama 3.1 8B, Gemma 3 4B, Gemma 3 12B, Gemma 3 27B, Qwen2.5 14B and Qwen2.5 1.5B on this synthetic corpus. Our goal is to show that compact models trained on linguistically controlled data can outperform general-purpose counterparts in script fidelity and grammatical accuracy for Konkani.

1.4
Contributions

This study delivers the following contributions:

Konkani-Instruct-100k: The first large-scale multiscript instruction-tuning dataset generated via distillation.

Konkani LLM: A suite of fine-tuned models optimized for Konkani.

Konkani-Bench: A 200-pair benchmark spanning Romi, Devanagari and Kannada scripts alongside English.

2
Related Work

Research on large language models for Indian languages has accelerated, yet coverage remains skewed towards high-resource languages such as Hindi and Tamil, with comparatively limited attention to Konkani. OpenHathi extends Llama 2 7B with an expanded tokenizer and bilingual training to support Hindi, English and Hinglish and reports performance comparable to GPT-3.5 on Hindi tasks in both Devanagari and Roman scripts
[
1
]
. Sarvam-1 targets ten Indic languages and reports strong results on benchmarks such as MMLU, ARC-Challenge, IndicGenBench and Flores translation and includes support for Konkani; however, it does not explicitly address Konkani in Roman (Romi) and Kannada scripts
[
2
]
. Tamil-LLaMA augments LLaMA 2 with additional Tamil tokens and instruction tuning on translated instruction datasets to improve monolingual Tamil understanding and generation, but the approach is not extended to minority Goan languages
[
3
]
. Existing Indic LLM efforts therefore provide strong baselines for Hindi and Tamil but leave a gap for low-resource languages like Konkani, especially in code-mixed, multi-script settings.

Synthetic instruction data has emerged as a standard strategy to compensate for limited supervised corpora and to boost downstream performance. Self-Instruct style pipelines use strong teacher models to generate diverse instructions and responses, followed by automatic filtering and curriculum design for student model fine-tuning
[
4
]
. CoT-Self-Instruct extends this paradigm by enforcing chain-of-thought planning before synthetic example generation and reports higher accuracy on reasoning tasks compared to vanilla Self-Instruct
[
5
]
. These methods indicate that synthetic data generation is a viable approach for building instruction-tuned models in settings where high-quality human-annotated data for Konkani is sparse or absent.

Work on script handling for Indian languages covers both transliteration and parallel data construction across scripts. Tamil-LLaMA relies on a standardized Tamil script and improved subword segmentation rather than explicit multi-script modeling, which limits transfer to languages like Konkani that routinely appear in Devanagari, Roman and Kannada scripts
[
3
]
. For Konkani specifically, existing NLP research focuses on foundational tasks such as POS tagging and sentiment analysis, often by leveraging transfer from resource-rich Marathi through parallel corpora and zero-shot models, not through dedicated multi-script LLMs. Recent efforts on Indic LLMs and resources highlight the scarcity of digital resources for Konkani and motivate dedicated multi-script evaluation and modeling
[
6
]
.

3
Konkani-Instruct-100k

3.1
Dataset Creation

3.1.1
Preliminary Experiments

Initial resource scarcity necessitated reliance on sparse open-source repositories. We utilized a 15,000-sample subset from Nagvekar
[
9
]
, consisting of Wikipedia-based edit/fix tasks rather than instruction-following pairs. We augmented these data with a Devanagari Alpaca dataset and employed Aksharamukha for transliteration into Romi and Kannada scripts to address script scarcity.

Pilot fine-tuning revealed limited generalization: models produced poorly formed sentences and struggled with open-ended queries. We attribute this to two factors. First, the closed-style nature of the training data did not promote conversational fluency. Second, automated transliteration introduced phonological errors that conflicted with dialectal nuances.

3.1.2
Synthetic Methodology

A synthetic generation pipeline addressed these structural weaknesses. Based on human evaluation, we selected Gemini 2.5 Pro and Gemini 3 Flash as the most reliable teacher models for Konkani. We also tested Grok 4.1, which performed competitively but tended to produce less appropriate lexical choices and occasionally generated NSFW content, making it unsuitable for data generation. We implemented a ”Tutor-Style” pedagogical framework rather than standard QA pairs. This approach teaches core linguistic mechanics via 18 foundational topics (Table
1
) to ensure basic competence in everyday Konkani.

3.1.3
Data Structure and Morphology

Morphological analysis tables constitute a distinguishing feature of this dataset. All 100% of samples provide the answer alongside a structural breakdown including part-of-speech tags and base-modifier morphology. Over 3,000 samples explicitly detail grammatical rules such as gender suffix modifications. Alternative phrasings for identical intents mitigate overfitting.

3.1.4
Prompting and Domain Diversity

Beyond these foundational lessons, we expand coverage by varying prompt domains (e.g., politics, science and technology) to increase contextual diversity. We employ a human-in-the-loop process in which the teacher model populates reusable instruction skeletons with synthetic entities, yielding prompts that better reflect natural variation.

3.1.5
Dataset Statistics

The final Konkani-Instruct-100k dataset comprises 105,982 samples, balanced across scripts to reduce orthographic bias. We additionally include a small set of English-to-English examples derived from the Sentence-Transformers Natural Questions dataset
[
8
]
as a regularizer to help preserve general-purpose reasoning.

4
Konkani Multi-Script Benchmark

We are developing a human-annotated benchmark for translation and transliteration, intended to stress-test (i) script fidelity and (ii) cross-script robustness for Konkani.

4.1
Task suite

The benchmark is built from 200 human-annotated items. For each item, we create parallel variants in Devanagari, Romi and Kannada and include an English translation to enable bilingual evaluation. We structure evaluation around two task families:

•

Translation:
Konkani-to-English.

•

Transliteration:
pairwise transliteration between Romi, Devanagari and Kannada (Romi–Devanagari, Romi–Kannada and Devanagari–Kannada; both directions).

4.2
Data splits and scripts

Each item is provided in Devanagari, Romi and Kannada along with an English translation. We recommend reporting results per-script and macro-averaged across scripts.

4.3
Evaluation protocol

We use automatic metrics for translation quality and transliteration accuracy and supplement them with human review for script fidelity and lexical appropriateness.

5
Methodology

We fine-tune open-weight base models on Konkani-Instruct-100k using parameter-efficient adaptation.

5.1
Data generation and filtering

We generate instruction–response pairs with a teacher model and apply automatic filters to reduce malformed or repetitive samples. We then balance scripts during sampling to avoid overfitting to a single orthography.

5.2
Training approach

We use Low-Rank Adaptation (LoRA) for parameter-efficient fine-tuning. We choose LoRA because it allows us to release lightweight adapter weights and support low-cost deployment by uploading adapters to hosted inference providers.

LoRA configuration.

We set rank
r=64
and scaling
\alpha=128
, apply LoRA to attention projections (
q_proj
,
k_proj
,
v_proj
,
o_proj
) and MLP projections (
gate_proj
,
up_proj
,
down_proj
), use dropout 0 and set bias adaptation to
none
. We enable gradient checkpointing for long-context training.

Optimization and checkpointing.

Unless otherwise stated, we fine-tune for 2 epochs with maximum sequence length 4096 and select checkpoints by validation loss. We train with per-device batch size 1 and gradient accumulation 32 (effective batch size 32), 8-bit AdamW, a cosine learning-rate schedule with 10% warmup and bfloat16 precision. We evaluate and checkpoint every 500 steps, keep the best checkpoint by validation loss and cap the number of saved checkpoints at 3.

7
Evaluation

7.1
Benchmark construction

We evaluate models on Konkani-Bench
[
10
]
, a Konkani multi-script benchmark constructed as follows: we first write human-authored Romi Konkani sentences, then (i) transliterate them into Devanagari and Kannada and (ii) translate them into English to create references.

7.2
Automatic metrics

For translation, we report BLEU
[
14
]
, chrF++
[
15
]
, and COMET (Table
4
). For transliteration, we report BLEU and chrF++ across script directions (Table
5
).

7.3
LLM-as-a-judge evaluation

We additionally evaluate outputs using an LLM-as-a-judge with a fixed rubric that scores helpfulness, script fidelity and absence of Marathi/Hindi contamination; unless otherwise stated, we use Gemini 3 Flash as the judge model (Tables
6
–
8
).

8
Limitations

Our dataset is largely synthetic and may encode teacher-model biases, stylistic artifacts, or factual inaccuracies. While script balancing reduces orthographic bias, it cannot fully address dialect diversity across Konkani communities. Additionally, our deployment constraints required using LoRA
[
13
]
with rank
r=64
to support serverless LoRA inference, which may underperform higher-rank adapters in some settings. Finally, due to time and budget constraints, we did not experiment with continued pre-training or full fine-tuning; we expect that these heavier training regimes would further improve performance beyond what we report here.

9
Conclusion

This work takes a step toward stronger multi-script support for Konkani by introducing Konkani-Instruct-100k and fine-tuned Konkani LLM variants. On reference-based translation evaluation (Table
4
), our best-performing fine-tuned model is
konkani-Qwen2.5-14B-Instruct
, achieving the highest COMET score among our models. For transliteration (Table
5
), our fine-tuned models substantially improve over their corresponding bases and show strong cross-script robustness across directions. Using LLM-as-a-judge evaluation (Tables
6
–
8
), Llama 8B performs best overall for Romi and Devanagari, while Qwen2.5 14B performs best for Kannada. We release models on Hugging Face at
huggingface.co/konkani
, and provide a public demo at
konkani.app
for easy try-out.

Konkani Text:
Khaunchem?

•
Translation:
Did you eat?

Common Lunchtime Phrases:

• Do you want to eat?
\rightarrow

Khatunk zai?

• What time is lunch?
\rightarrow

Jevnnacho vell kitem?
