Introduction

1.1.
The binary Goldbach Conjecture

The binary Goldbach Conjecture states that every even integer greater than
2
is the sum of two primes.
For an even integer
N>2
, let
D_{1,1}(N)
denote the number of solutions to

(1.1)

N=p+q

with primes
p

q

Hardy and Littlewood
[

proposed the more precise conjecture that, as
N\to\infty

(1.2)

D_{1,1}(N)\sim 2C(N)\frac{N}{\log^{2}N},

where

(1.3)

C(N)=\prod_{p>2}\left(1-\frac{1}{(p-1)^{2}}\right)\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p>2\\
p\mid N\end{subarray}}\frac{p-1}{p-2}.

The factor
2C(N)
is precisely the Hardy–Littlewood singular series associated with the binary Goldbach Conjecture,
written here in the form customary in sieve theory.

While (

q
,

1.3.
Conditional results

Chen’s theorem
(1+2)
relies on the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem
that if

R(y;q,a)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant y\\
p\equiv a\bmod q\end{subarray}}1-\frac{\pi(y)}{\varphi(q)},

then for any
A>0
, there exists
B=B(A)>0
such that

(1.11)

\sum_{q\leqslant x^{1/2}/\log^{B}x}\max_{y\leqslant x}\max_{(a,q)=1}|R(y;q,a)|\ll\frac{x}{\log^{A}x}.

Pan and Ding
[
29
]
(see also Pan
[
28
]
) generalized this result to a weighted variant.
By adopting this weighted form in place of the original estimate (
1.11
), they substantially simplified the proof of Chen’s theorem
[
7
,
8
]
. Now set

(1.12)

R(y;q,a,m)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}mp\leqslant y\\
mp\equiv a\bmod q\end{subarray}}1-\dfrac{\hbox{{\rm Li}}(y/m)}{\varphi(q)}.

The corresponding weighted mean-value theorem states that for any
A>0
, there exists
B=B(A)>0
satisfying

(1.13)

\sum_{q\leqslant x^{1/2}/\log^{B}x}\max_{y\leqslant x}\max_{(a,q)=1}\bigg|\sum_{m\leqslant M}f(m)R(y;q,a,m)\bigg|\ll\frac{x}{\log^{A}x},

where
f(m)
is a fairly general weight function and
M
is a large parameter. A key merit of this weighted approach is that it removes the complicated arguments used originally by Chen in his proof of the
(1+2)
theorem, which greatly streamlines the overall proof.

As is well known, the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture claims that the distribution level in the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem can be lifted to
1-\varepsilon
for any
\varepsilon>0
. We now formulate a weighted version of the Elliott–Halberstam conjecture with distribution level
\theta
, denoted by
\mathrm{WEH}(\theta)
, where
\theta\in(1/2,1)
characterizes the equidistribution quality of primes in arithmetic progressions. Roughly speaking, our weighted Elliott–Halberstam conjecture asserts that the above conclusion still holds when equipped with the weight functions introduced by Pan and Ding. We state the conjecture following the formulation in
[
30
, §8]
.

Conjecture WEH
(\theta)
.

Let
\theta\in(1/2,1)
be a constant. Suppose
f(m)\ll 1
and
\alpha\in(0,1]
. Let
r_{1}(y)
be a positive function depending on
x
such that

r_{1}(y)\ll x^{\alpha},\quad y\leqslant x.

Let
r_{2}(m)
be a positive function depending on
x
and
y
such that

mr_{2}(m)\ll x,\quad m\leqslant x^{\alpha},\quad y\leqslant x.

Let
R(y;q,a,m)
be as in (
1.12
).
Then for every
A>0
, there exists a constant
B=B(A)>0
such that

\sum_{q\leqslant x^{\theta}/\log^{B}x}\max_{y\leqslant x}\max_{(a,q)=1}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leqslant x^{1-\alpha}\\
(m,q)=1\end{subarray}}f(m)R(y;q,a,m)\bigg|\ll\frac{x}{\log^{A}x},

\sum_{q\leqslant x^{\theta}/\log^{B}x}\max_{y\leqslant x}\max_{(a,q)=1}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leqslant x^{1-\alpha}\\
(m,q)=1\end{subarray}}f(m)R(mr_{1}(y);q,a,m)\bigg|\ll\frac{x}{\log^{A}x},

and

\sum_{q\leqslant x^{\theta}/\log^{B}x}\max_{y\leqslant x}\max_{(a,q)=1}\bigg|\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m\leqslant x^{1-\alpha}\\
(m,q)=1\end{subarray}}f(m)R(mr_{2}(m);q,a,m)\bigg|\ll\frac{x}{\log^{A}x}.

We note that
{\rm WEH}(\theta)
is known to hold for
\theta\leqslant\frac{1}{2}
(see Lemma
3.1
).
We point out that the classical Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem cannot handle the error terms arising from the switching principle. In contrast, the Pan–Ding mean value theorem, is not only sufficient to cover those errors, but also encompasses the classical Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem itself. We can now state our conditional result.

Theorem 1.3
.

Assume
\mathrm{WEH}(0.999)
. Then Proposition
(1+1.4)
holds.
More precisely, we have

D_{1,1.4}(N)>0.105\,C(N)\frac{N}{\log^{2}N}

for every sufficiently large even
N
, where
C(N)
is as in (
1.3
).

Since Conjecture
\mathrm{WEH}(\theta)
applies to the twin-prime problem without modification,
it yields an analogous result of equivalent strength. The proof, which follows a parallel argument, is omitted in this paper
for brevity.

Theorem 1.4
.

Assume
\mathrm{WEH}(0.999)
. Then Proposition
(1-1.4)
holds. More precisely, we have

\pi_{1,1.4}(x)>0.052\,C\frac{x}{\log^{2}x}

for every sufficiently large
x
, where
C
is as in (
1.8
).

We remark that the exponent
1.4

Outline of the paper

Below we outline the structure of the paper.
In §

2.1.
Sieve functions and Buchstab’s function

The first lemma gives an asymptotic formula for the number of integers free of small prime factors,
together with some explicit bounds for Buchstab’s function
w(u)
. The asymptotic formula is a classical result
(see, e.g.,
[
14
, Lemma 12.1]
); the explicit expressions for
w(u)
in small ranges come from
[
25
, Lemma 20]
,
and the upper bound
w(u)\leqslant 0.561522
for
u\geqslant 3.5
is taken from
[
34
, Lemma 2.10]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let

\Psi(x,z)=|\{n\leqslant x:(n,Q(z))=1\}|,\quad Q(z)=\prod_{p<z}p.

Then for
1<u_{0}\leqslant u\leqslant 100
,

\Psi(x,x^{1/u})\sim u\,w(u)\,\frac{x}{\log x},

where
w(u)
is Buchstab’s function determined by the differential-difference equation

\begin{cases}w(u)=u^{-1},&1\leqslant u\leqslant 2,\\[5.69054pt]
(uw(u))^{\prime}=w(u-1),&u>2.\end{cases}

Explicitly,

w(u)=\begin{cases}\displaystyle\frac{1}{u},&1\leqslant u\leqslant 2,\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1+\log(u-1)}{u},&2\leqslant u\leqslant 3,\\[11.38109pt]
\displaystyle\frac{1+\log(u-1)}{u}+\frac{1}{u}\int_{2}^{u-1}\frac{\log(t-1)}{t}\,dt,&3\leqslant u\leqslant 4.\end{cases}

Moreover, for all
u\geqslant 3.5
one has
w(u)\leqslant 0.561522
.

The next lemma records the standard upper- and lower-bound sieve functions
F(s),f(s)
of the linear sieve. The formulas below are taken from
[
21
, (3.11) and (3.12)]
; they are valid for the Iwaniec–Rosser sieve with sifting parameter
\kappa=1
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
f
and
F
be the functions in the linear sieve.
For
F
, we have

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr s&1\leqslant s\leqslant 3&3\leqslant s\leqslant 5&5\leqslant s\leqslant 7\\
\hline\cr F(s)&F_{1}(s)&F_{2}(s)&F_{3}(s)\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

where

F_{1}(s)=\frac{2e^{\gamma}}{s},

F_{2}(s)=\frac{2e^{\gamma}}{s}\bigg(1+\int_{2}^{s-1}\frac{\log(t-1)}{t}\,dt\bigg),

and

F_{3}(s)=\frac{2e^{\gamma}}{s}\bigg(1+\int_{2}^{s-1}\frac{\log(t-1)}{t}\,dt+\int_{2}^{s-3}\frac{\log(t-1)}{t}\,dt\int_{t+2}^{s-1}\frac{1}{u}\log\frac{u-1}{t+1}\,du\bigg).

For
f
, we have

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr s&2\leqslant s\leqslant 4&4\leqslant s\leqslant 6\\
\hline\cr f(s)&f_{1}(s)&f_{2}(s)\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

where

f_{1}(s)=\frac{2e^{\gamma}}{s}\log(s-1),

f_{2}(s)=\frac{2e^{\gamma}}{s}\bigg(\log(s-1)+\int_{3}^{s-1}\frac{dt}{t}\int_{2}^{t-1}\frac{\log(u-1)}{u}\,du\bigg),

and
\gamma=0.577\ldots
is Euler’s constant.

Numerically one finds

F(7)\leqslant 1.000005,\quad f(6)\geqslant 0.999895,

so both
F(s)
and
f(s)
are extremely close to
1
for these arguments.
This explains why, in the above lemma, we only show

for
F
and to
s\leqslant 6
for
f

Computations involving
F
and
f
for larger

would require additional effort while offering only marginal
improvement to the final results.

2.2.
The linear sieve

In this subsection, we introduce the general setup of the linear sieve; later we will study two versions of the linear sieve theorem. In the proofs of Theorems
1.1
and
1.2
we shall employ the linear sieve with well-factorable coefficients; for Theorems
1.3
and
1.4
the standard linear sieve will suffice.

Now let
\mathscr{A}
be a finite sequence of integers and let
\mathscr{P}
be a set of primes. For
z\geqslant 2
we consider the sifting function

(2.1)

S(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{P},z)=|\{a\in\mathscr{A}:(a,P(z))=1\}|,

where

(2.2)

P(z)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<z\\
p\in\mathscr{P}\end{subarray}}p.

If
d
is a square‑free integer whose prime factors all belong to
\mathscr{P}
, we write
\mathscr{A}_{d}
for the set of elements of
\mathscr{A}
that are divisible by
d
. We assume an approximation of the form

(2.3)

|\mathscr{A}_{d}|=\frac{\omega(d)}{d}X+r(\mathscr{A},d),

where
X>1
is independent of
d
, and
\omega
is a multiplicative function satisfying

(2.4)

0\leqslant\omega(p)<p\quad\text{for }p\in\mathscr{P}.

We also define

(2.5)

V(z)=\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}p<z\\
p\in\mathscr{P}\end{subarray}}\left(1-\frac{\omega(p)}{p}\right),

and we assume that there exists an absolute constant
K>1
such that

(2.6)

\frac{V(z_{1})}{V(z_{2})}\leqslant\frac{\log z_{2}}{\log z_{1}}\left(1+\frac{K}{\log z_{1}}\right)

for
z_{2}\geqslant z_{1}\geqslant 2
. This inequality places us in the setting of the linear sieve.

2.3.
Standard linear sieve

First, we introduce the standard linear sieve
[

]
.

Lemma 2.3
.

We adopt the basic notations and assumptions of the linear sieve (

)–(

Assume further that

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}z_{1}\leqslant p<z_{2}\end{subarray}}\frac{\omega(p)}{p}=\log\frac{\log z_{2}}{\log z_{1}}+O\left(\frac{1}{\log z_{1}}\right),\quad z_{2}>z_{1}\geqslant 2.

Then, under these assumptions, the function

admits the upper and lower bounds

S(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{P},z)\leqslant XV(z)\left\{F(s)+O\left(\frac{1}{\log^{1/3}D}\right)\right\}+R_{D},

where

and finally, the functions

2.4.
The linear sieve with well‑factorable coefficients

In this subsection we present the well‑factorable form of the Rosser–Iwaniec sieve, which will replace the standard linear sieve in certain parts of the argument. We begin with the necessary definitions.

Let
k
be a positive integer. For any positive integer

(2.8)

\lambda(q)=0\quad\text{for all }q\geqslant Q,

(2.9)

|\lambda(q)|\leqslant\tau_{k}(q)\quad\text{for all }q\geqslant 1,

and if, for every decomposition

with
Q_{1},Q_{2}\geqslant 1
, there exist two functions
\lambda_{1}
and
\lambda_{2}
satisfying (
2.8
) and (

) with

replaced by
Q_{1}
and

respectively, such that

\lambda=\lambda_{1}*\lambda_{2},

where

denotes Dirichlet convolution.
The following lemma
[

, Lemma 2.1]
is useful.

Lemma 2.4
.

If
\lambda^{\prime}
is an arithmetical function of level
Q^{\prime}\leqslant Q
and of order

, then
\lambda*\lambda^{\prime}
is well factorable of level
QQ^{\prime}
and of order

.

Now we state
the well‑factorable version of the linear sieve
[

]
as follows.

Lemma 2.5
.

Let

(2.10)

0<\eta<1/8,\quad L=\exp(8\eta^{-3}),\quad 2\leqslant z\leqslant Q^{1/2}.

Then under the assumptions (
2.3
), (
2.4
) and (

), we have the inequalities

(2.11)

S(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{P},z)\leqslant XV(z)\left(F\left(\frac{\log Q}{\log z}\right)+E\right)+\sum_{1\leqslant l\leqslant L}\sum_{q\mid P(z)}\lambda_{l}^{+}(q)r(\mathscr{A},q),

and

(2.12)

S(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{P},z)\geqslant XV(z)\left(f\left(\frac{\log Q}{\log z}\right)+E\right)+\sum_{1\leqslant l\leqslant L}\sum_{q\mid P(z)}\lambda_{l}^{-}(q)r(\mathscr{A},q).

In these formulas,
\lambda_{q}^{+}
and
\lambda_{q}^{-}
are well-factorable coefficients of order

and of level

, and

3.
Equidistribution of primes

This section collects known results on the distribution of primes in arithmetic progressions.
The first one is a weighted version of the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem due to Pan and Ding
[
29
]
,
which implies Bombieri-Vinogradov’s theorem. In §
1
we have stated it in the form of
[
30
, §8]
.

Lemma 3.1
.

The weighted Elliott–Halberstam Conjecture
WEH
(\theta)
holds for every
\theta\leqslant 1/2
.

The next result, due to Bombieri–Friedlander–Iwaniec
[
1
, Theorem 10]
, provides a larger level of distribution for well‑factorable coefficients.

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
\lambda
be a well‑factorable function of order
k
and level
Q=x^{4/7-\varepsilon}
.
For any
\varepsilon>0
and any
A>0
, uniformly for
x\geqslant 3
and
|a|\leqslant\log^{A}x
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(q,a)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda(q)\left(\pi(x;q,a)-\frac{\hbox{{\rm Li}}(x)}{\varphi(q)}\right)\ll_{\varepsilon,k,A}\frac{x}{\log^{A}x},

where

\pi(x;q,a)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}p\leqslant x\\
n\equiv a\bmod q\end{subarray}}1.

The following result is due to Pascadi
[
31
, Theorem 1.2(ii)]
. We note, however, that the stated improvement applies only to the upper bound sieve, not the lower bound sieve.
In our proof, lower bound estimates for
S(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{P},z)
are required. The terms
that would necessitate upper bound estimates are those involving
S(\mathscr{A}_{p},\mathscr{P},z)
,
which cannot be obtained via Pascadi’s method. Consequently, this lemma is not applicable in our setting.

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
a\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},A,\varepsilon>0,x\geqslant 2
, and suppose that
Q\leqslant x^{3/5-\varepsilon}
,
and
(\lambda_{q})
are the well-factorable upper bound linear sieve weights of level
Q
. Then one has

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}q\leqslant Q\\
(q,a)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda_{q}\left(\pi(x;q,a)-\frac{\hbox{{\rm Li}}(x)}{\varphi(q)}\right)\ll_{\varepsilon,A,a}\frac{x}{\log^{A}x}.

1.1
and

[
34

, which is a corollary
that combines
[

, Corollary 2]
,

and

Lemma 3.4
.

Let
M\geqslant 1
,
N\geqslant 1
and
X:=MN
. Let
\{\alpha_{m}\}
and
\{\beta_{n}\}
be
two sequences of order
k
supported in
[M,2M]
and
[N,2N]
respectively. We also
suppose that for all constant
B>0
, the asymptotic formula

(3.1)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\equiv n_{0}\bmod{k}\\
(n,d)=1\end{subarray}}\beta_{n}=\frac{1}{\varphi(k)}\sum_{(n,dk)=1}\beta_{n}+O_{B,k}\!\left(\frac{N\tau_{k}(d)}{(\log 2N)^{B}}\right)

holds for
d\geqslant 1,k\geqslant 1
and
(k,n_{0})=1
. If
n
has a prime factor
p

with

, then
\beta_{n}=0
.
Then for any
A>0
and for any
\varepsilon>0
we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(q,a)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda(q)\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}mn\equiv a\bmod{q}\end{subarray}}\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(mn,q)=1\end{subarray}}\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}\bigg)\ll_{\varepsilon,A}\frac{X}{\log^{A}X}

uniformly for

\lambda(q)
is a well-factorable function of order
1
and of level

Q:=X^{\theta(\nu)-\varepsilon}
, where
\theta(\nu)
is given by

\begin{array}[]{|c|c|c|c|c|}\hline\cr\nu&\varepsilon\leqslant\nu\leqslant\frac{1}{15}&\frac{1}{15}\leqslant\nu\leqslant\frac{1}{10}&\frac{1}{10}\leqslant\nu\leqslant\frac{3}{14}&\frac{3}{14}\leqslant\nu\leqslant\frac{1}{4}\\
\hline\cr\theta(\nu)&\dfrac{6-5\nu}{10}&\dfrac{1}{2}+\nu&\dfrac{5-2\nu}{8}&\dfrac{3+2\nu}{6}\\
\hline\cr\nu&\frac{1}{4}\leqslant\nu\leqslant\frac{2}{7}&\frac{2}{7}\leqslant\nu\leqslant\frac{2}{5}&\frac{2}{5}\leqslant\nu\leqslant\frac{1}{2}&\frac{1}{2}\leqslant\nu\leqslant 1-\varepsilon\\
\hline\cr\theta(\nu)&\dfrac{2-\nu}{3}&\dfrac{2+\nu}{4}&1-\nu&\dfrac{1}{2}\\
\hline\cr\end{array}

The following lemma, sourced from
[

, was originally established for
investigations into the Twin Prime Conjecture. Wu
[

subsequently adapted this lemma to the
Goldbach Conjecture in 2004. This is the first result, which is valid uniformly for

|a|\leqslant X
and has the level of distribution

Lemma 3.5
.

Let
M\geqslant 1
,
N\geqslant 1
and
X:=MN
. Let
\{\alpha_{m}\}
and
\{\beta_{n}\}
be two sequences of order
k
supported in
[M,2M]
and
[N,2N]
respectively. We also suppose that for all
B
, the equality

(3.2)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\equiv n_{0}\bmod{k}\\
(n,d)=1\end{subarray}}\beta_{n}=\frac{1}{\varphi(k)}\sum_{(n,dk)=1}\beta_{n}+O_{B,k}\!\left(\frac{N\tau_{k}(d)}{(\log 2N)^{B}}\right)

holds for
d\geqslant 1,k\geqslant 1
and
(k,n_{0})=1
.
If
n
has a prime factor
p
with
p<\exp\bigl\{(\log\log n)^{2}\bigr\}
, then
\beta_{n}=0
.
Then for any
A>0
and for any
\varepsilon>0
we have

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(q,a)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda(q)\biggl(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}mn\equiv a\bmod{q}\end{subarray}}\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}-\frac{1}{\varphi(q)}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(mn,q)=1\end{subarray}}\alpha_{m}\beta_{n}\biggr)\ll_{\varepsilon,A}\frac{X}{\log^{A}X}

uniformly for
|a|\leqslant X

Here
\lambda(q)
is a well-factorable function of order
1
and of level
Q:=X^{5(1-\nu)/9-\varepsilon}
, where

\varepsilon\leqslant\nu:=\frac{\log N}{\log X}\leqslant\frac{1}{10}.

The lemma above is essential for establishing Proposition
(1+1.9)
. Historically, the constant in
Proposition
(1+2)
was successively refined from
0.67
to
0.81
by Chen.
Since a lower bound of at least
0.8429
is required for Proposition
(1+1.9)
,
this constant was far from being met in Chen’s time, so he could not have
proved Proposition
(1+1.9)
then.
More importantly, even in the period after Chen, without employing the aforementioned lemma,
the constant could at best reach
0.8285
as in Cai and Lu
[
6
]
.
This still falls short of the
0.8429
threshold. Thus, without this lemma,
subsequent advances
would likewise be insufficient to support the proof of Proposition
(1+1.9)
.

When we use the weighted inequality, some coefficients are merely almost well-factorable. So we need the following result due to
Fouvry–Grupp
[
13
, Theorem 2]
.

Lemma 3.6
.

Let
\lambda
be a well-factorable function of level
Q_{1}
and of order
k
,
\xi
an arithmetical function
satisfying the conditions

|\xi(q_{2})|\leqslant\log x

and

\xi(q_{2})=0

for

q_{2}>Q_{2}.

Let
\Lambda
be the von Mangoldt function. Then we have for any integer
a
, any
\varepsilon>0
and any
A>0
,

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(q_{1}q_{2},a)=1\end{subarray}}\lambda(q_{1})\xi(q_{2})\left(\pi(x;q_{1}q_{2},a)-\frac{\hbox{{\rm Li}}(x)}{\varphi(q_{1}q_{2})}\right)\ll_{a,\varepsilon,k,A}\frac{x}{\log^{A}x},

as long as one of the following four conditions is true:

(C.1)

Q_{2}\leqslant Q_{1},Q_{1}Q_{2}\leqslant x^{4/7-\varepsilon}
,

(C.2)

Q_{2}\geqslant Q_{1},Q_{1}Q_{2}^{6}\leqslant x^{2-\varepsilon}
,

(C.3)

\xi(q)=\Lambda(q),Q_{1}Q_{2}\leqslant x^{11/20-\varepsilon},Q_{2}\leqslant x^{1/3-\varepsilon}
.

4.
Weighted sieves

In this section, we develop weighted sieve methods to establish the theorems
stated in the introduction.
We adopt the standard notation of the linear sieve. Throughout the rest of the paper, the parameter
z
satisfies

(4.1)

\log z\asymp\log x,

and in both the Goldbach and the twin prime problems we set

(4.2)

\mathscr{P}=\{p:p>2\}.

We will also require information on the auxiliary sets

(4.3)

\mathscr{P}(q)=\{p\in\mathscr{P}:(p,q)=1\}.

4.1.
The Goldbach Conjecture (I)

In this subsection, we develop the weighted sieve method needed for Theorem
1.1

which deals with the Goldbach problem unconditionally.
Recall that
D_{1,a}(N)
denotes the number of solutions to (
1.6
), i.e.,

(4.4)

D_{1,a}(N)=|\{p\leqslant N:N-p=rq,\;r\leqslant q^{a-1}\}|,

where
r
is either a prime or
1
, and
p,q
are primes.
We now define the set

(4.5)

\mathscr{A}=\{N-p:p<(1-\varepsilon)N\}.

First, we establish the following fundamental weighted inequality.

Proposition 4.1
.

Let

1+3\varepsilon<a<2,\quad\tau=\dfrac{a-1}{a}-\varepsilon,\quad 0<\alpha<\tau<\frac{1}{2}.

If
N>\varepsilon^{\frac{1-a}{a\varepsilon}}
then

(4.6)

D_{1,a}(N)\geqslant\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{A}\\
P^{-}(n)\geqslant N^{\alpha}\end{subarray}}1-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{A}\\
P^{-}(n)\geqslant N^{\tau}\\
\Omega(n)\geqslant 2\end{subarray}}1+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{A}\\
P^{-}(n)\geqslant N^{\tau}\\
\Omega(n)\geqslant 3\end{subarray}}1-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{A}\\
P^{-}(n)\geqslant N^{\alpha}\\
\Omega(n)\geqslant 3\end{subarray}}1.

Moreover, the third term on the right vanishes when
a>1.5+3\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Fix an even integer
N>\varepsilon^{\frac{1-a}{a\varepsilon}}
and define the weight function

w(n)=\mathbf{1}_{P^{-}(n)\geqslant N^{\alpha}}-\mathbf{1}_{\Omega(n)=2}\mathbf{1}_{P^{-}(n)\geqslant N^{\tau}}+\mathbf{1}_{\Omega(n)\geqslant 3}\mathbf{1}_{P^{-}(n)\geqslant N^{\tau}}-\mathbf{1}_{\Omega(n)\geqslant 3}\mathbf{1}_{P^{-}(n)\geqslant N^{\alpha}}.

It suffices to show

(4.7)

D_{1,a}(n)\geqslant\sum\limits_{n\in\mathscr{A}}w(n).

Recall from (
4.5
) that if
n\in\mathscr{A}
, then
n>\varepsilon N
.
We examine
w(n)
according to the size of
P^{-}(n)
.

Case 1:
P^{-}(n)<N^{\alpha}
.
It is easy to see that
w(n)=0
regardless of the value of
\Omega(n)
.

Case 2:
N^{\alpha}\leqslant P^{-}(n)<N^{\tau}
.
In this case,
n
cannot be a prime. When
\Omega(n)=2
, we have
w(n)=1-0+0-0=1
; and when
\Omega(n)\geqslant 3
, we have
w(n)=1-0+0-1=0
.

Case 3:
P^{-}(n)\geqslant N^{\tau}
.
If
n
is a prime, then
w(n)=1-0+0-0=1
; if
\Omega(n)=2
, we have
w(n)=1-1+0-0=0
; and if
\Omega(n)\geqslant 3
, we have
w(n)=1-1+1-1=0
.

Therefore,
w(n)=1
if and only if
n
is prime or
n=rq
where
r
and
q
are primes satisfying

N^{\alpha}\leqslant r<N^{\tau},\quad q>\dfrac{\varepsilon N}{N^{\tau}}=\varepsilon N^{1-\tau}.

In the latter case we have

r\leqslant q^{a-1}
when
N>\varepsilon^{\frac{1-a}{a\varepsilon}}
, and (
4.7
) follows.
∎

Proposition 4.2
.

Let

1.5+3\varepsilon<a<2,\quad\tau=\dfrac{a-1}{a}-\varepsilon,\quad\frac{1}{21}<\kappa<\sigma\leqslant\frac{1}{3}.

Then we have

(4.8)

\begin{split}2D_{1,a}(N)\geqslant\ &2S_{1}(\kappa)-2S_{2}(\tau)-S_{3}(\kappa,\sigma)-2S_{4}(\sigma)\\
&-S_{5}(\kappa,\sigma)+S_{6}(\kappa,\sigma)+O(N^{1-\kappa}),\end{split}

where

S_{1}(\kappa):=S(\mathscr{A};\mathscr{P}(N),N^{\kappa}),

S_{2}(\kappa):=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\kappa}\leqslant p\leqslant N^{1/2}\\
(p,N)=1\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p};\mathscr{P}(N),p),

S_{3}(\kappa,\sigma):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\kappa}\leqslant p\leqslant N^{\sigma}\\
(p,N)=1\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p};\mathscr{P}(N),N^{\kappa}),

S_{4}(\sigma):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\sigma}\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant p_{2}\leqslant(N/p_{1})^{1/2}\\
(p_{1}p_{2},N)=1\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p_{1}p_{2}};\mathscr{P}(Np_{1}),p_{2}),

S_{5}(\kappa,\sigma):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\kappa}\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant N^{\sigma}\leqslant p_{2}\leqslant(N/p_{1})^{1/2}\\
(p_{1}p_{2},N)=1\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p_{1}p_{2}};\mathscr{P}(Np_{1}),p_{2}),

and

S_{6}(\kappa,\sigma):=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\kappa}\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant p_{2}\leqslant p_{3}\leqslant N^{\sigma}\\
(p_{1}p_{2}p_{3},N)=1\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}};\mathscr{P}(Np_{1}),p_{2}).

Proof.

When

1.5+3\varepsilon<a<2,\quad\tau=\dfrac{a-1}{a}-\varepsilon>\frac{1}{3},\quad\frac{1}{21}<\kappa\leqslant\frac{1}{3},

we derive by Proposition
4.1
the basic weighted inequality

(4.9)

\begin{split}D_{1,a}(N)\geqslant\ &\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{A}\\
P^{-}(n)\geqslant N^{\kappa}\end{subarray}}1-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{A}\\
P^{-}(n)\geqslant N^{\tau}\\
\Omega(n)\geqslant 2\end{subarray}}1-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{A}\\
P^{-}(n)\geqslant N^{\kappa}\\
\Omega(n)\geqslant 3\end{subarray}}1\\
\geqslant\ &S(\mathscr{A};\mathscr{P}(N),N^{\kappa})-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\tau}\leqslant p\leqslant N^{1/2}\\
(p,N)=1\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p};\mathscr{P}(N),p)\\
&-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\kappa}\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant p_{2}\leqslant(N/p_{1})^{1/2}\\
(p_{1}p_{2},N)=1\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p_{1}p_{2}};\mathscr{P}(Np_{1}),p_{2})+O(N^{1-\kappa})\\
=\ &S_{1}(\kappa)-S_{2}(\tau)-S_{4}(\kappa)+O(N^{1-\kappa}),\end{split}

where we have used the trivial bound

(4.10)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\kappa}\leqslant p\leqslant N^{1/2}\\
(p,N)=1\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p^{2}};\mathscr{P}(N),p)\ll N^{1-\kappa}

as well as the trivial bound

(4.11)

S(\mathscr{A}_{p};\mathscr{P}(N),p)\ll N^{1-\kappa}

which holds for
N^{\kappa}\leqslant p\leqslant N^{1/2}
.

Similarly, when
1/21<\sigma\leqslant 1/3
, we have

(4.12)

\begin{split}D_{1,a}(N)\geqslant\ &S(\mathscr{A};\mathscr{P}(N),N^{\sigma})-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\tau}\leqslant p\leqslant N^{1/2}\\
(p,N)=1\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p};\mathscr{P}(N),p)\\
&-\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\sigma}\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant p_{2}\leqslant(N/p_{1})^{1/2}\\
(p_{1}p_{2},N)=1\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p_{1}p_{2}};\mathscr{P}(Np_{1}),p_{2})+O(N^{1-\sigma})\\
=\ &S_{1}(\sigma)-S_{2}(\tau)-S_{4}(\sigma)+O(N^{1-\sigma}),\end{split}

where we have used the trivial bound

(4.13)

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\sigma}\leqslant p\leqslant N^{1/2}\\
(p,N)=1\end{subarray}}S\left(\mathscr{A}_{p^{2}};\mathscr{P}(N),p\right)\ll N^{1-\sigma}.

Noting that
\kappa<\sigma
, we have by (
4.9
) and (
4.12
) that

(4.14)

2D_{1,a}(N)\geqslant S_{1}(\kappa)+S_{1}(\sigma)-2S_{2}(\tau)-S_{4}(\kappa)-S_{4}(\sigma)+O(N^{1-\kappa}).

In the rest of this proof, bounds similar to (
4.10
), (
4.11
) and (
4.13
) will be applied
several times. Now
\kappa<\sigma
, we can rewrite

as

(4.15)

\begin{split}S_{4}(\kappa)=\ &\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\kappa}\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant p_{2}\leqslant(N/p_{1})^{1/2}\\

=\ &\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\kappa}\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant p_{2}\leqslant N^{\sigma}\\
(p_{1}p_{2},N)=1\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p_{1}p_{2}};\mathscr{P}(Np_{1}),p_{2})\\
&+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\kappa}\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant N^{\sigma}\leqslant p_{2}\leqslant(N/p_{1})^{1/2}\\
(p_{1}p_{2},N)=1\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p_{1}p_{2}};\mathscr{P}(Np_{1}),p_{2})\\
&+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\sigma}\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant p_{2}\leqslant(N/p_{1})^{1/2}\\
(p_{1}p_{2},N)=1\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p_{1}p_{2}};\mathscr{P}(Np_{1}),p_{2})+O(N^{1-\kappa})\\
=\ &\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\kappa}\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant p_{2}\leqslant N^{\sigma}\\
(p_{1}p_{2},N)=1\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p_{1}p_{2}};\mathscr{P}(Np_{1}),p_{2})+S_{5}(\kappa,\sigma)+S_{4}(\sigma)+O(N^{1-\kappa}).\end{split}

On the other hand, by Buchstab’s identity we can transform

into the form

(4.16)

\begin{split}S_{1}(\sigma)=\ &S(\mathscr{A};\mathscr{P}(N),N^{\sigma})\\

\begin{split}4D_{1,a}(N)\geqslant\ &3G_{1}+G_{2}-4G_{3}-G_{4}-G_{5}+G_{6}+G_{7}\\

In this subsection, we will present the weighted sieve method required for Theorem

Proposition 4.6
.

Let

1+3\varepsilon<a<2,\quad\tau=\dfrac{a-1}{a}-\varepsilon,\quad 0<\alpha<\tau<\frac{1}{2}.

If
N>\varepsilon^{\frac{1-a}{a\varepsilon}}
then

(4.74)

\pi_{1,a}(x)\geqslant\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{A}\\
P^{-}(n)\geqslant x^{\alpha}\end{subarray}}1-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{A}\\
P^{-}(n)\geqslant x^{\tau}\\
\Omega(n)\geqslant 2\end{subarray}}1+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{A}\\
P^{-}(n)\geqslant x^{\tau}\\
\Omega(n)\geqslant 3\end{subarray}}1-\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}n\in\mathscr{A}\\
P^{-}(n)\geqslant x^{\alpha}\\
\Omega(n)\geqslant 3\end{subarray}}1.

Moreover, the third term on the right vanishes when
a>1.5+3\varepsilon
.

Proof.

For
x>\varepsilon^{\frac{1-a}{a\varepsilon}}
we define the weight function

w(n,x)=\mathbf{1}_{P^{-}(n)\geqslant x^{\alpha}}-\mathbf{1}_{\Omega(n)=2}\mathbf{1}_{P^{-}(n)\geqslant x^{\tau}}+\mathbf{1}_{\Omega(n)\geqslant 3}\mathbf{1}_{P^{-}(n)\geqslant x^{\tau}}-\mathbf{1}_{\Omega(n)\geqslant 3}\mathbf{1}_{P^{-}(n)\geqslant x^{\alpha}}.

It suffices to show

(4.75)

\pi_{1,a}(x)\geqslant\sum\limits_{n\in\mathscr{A}}w(n,x).

Recall from (
4.73
) that if
n\in\mathscr{A}
, then
n>\varepsilon x
.
We examine
w(n,x)
according to the size of
P^{-}(n)
.

Case 1:
P^{-}(n)<x^{\alpha}
.
It is easy to see that
w(n,x)=0
regardless of the value of
\Omega(n)
.

Case 2:
x^{\alpha}\leqslant P^{-}(n)<x^{\tau}
.
In this case,
n
cannot be a prime. When
\Omega(n)=2
, we have
w(n,x)=1-0+0-0=1
; and when
\Omega(n)\geqslant 3
, we have
w(n,x)=1-0+0-1=0
.

Case 3:
P^{-}(n)\geqslant x^{\tau}
.
If
n
is a prime, then
w(n,x)=1-0+0-0=1
; if
\Omega(n)=2
, we have
w(n,x)=1-1+0-0=0
;
and if
\Omega(n)\geqslant 3
, we have
w(n,x)=1-1+1-1=0
.

Therefore,
w(n,x)=1
if and only if
n
is prime or
n=rq
where

and
q
are primes satisfying

x^{\alpha}\leqslant r<x^{\tau},\quad q>\dfrac{\varepsilon x}{x^{\tau}}=\varepsilon x^{1-\tau}.

In the latter case we have

r\leqslant q^{a-1}
when
x>\varepsilon^{\frac{1-a}{a\varepsilon}}
, and
(
4.75
) holds.
∎

8.
Further remarks

8.1.
What is the difference between Proposition
(1+a)
and Proposition
(1+2)
?

In the proof of our Proposition
(1+a)
, a term of the form

G:=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\tau}\leqslant p\leqslant N^{1/2}\\
(p,N)=1\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p};\mathscr{P}(N),p)

always appears.
A difficulty arises when the upper limit of the summation approaches
N^{1/2}
.
If we try to estimate an upper bound without using the switching principle, we obtain

(8.1)

\displaystyle G\leqslant

\displaystyle(1+O(E))\dfrac{2C(N)N}{\log N}\sum_{N^{\tau}\leqslant p\leqslant N^{1/2}}\dfrac{e^{-\gamma}}{\log p}\frac{F\bigg(\frac{\log(\sqrt{N}/p)}{\log p}\bigg)}{p-1}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle(1+O(E))\dfrac{2C(N)N}{e^{\gamma}\log^{2}N}\sum_{N^{\tau}\leqslant p\leqslant N^{1/2}}\dfrac{\log N}{p\log p}F\bigg(\frac{1/2-\log p/\log N}{\log p/\log N}\bigg)

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle(1+O(E))\dfrac{2C(N)N}{e^{\gamma}\log^{2}N}\int_{N^{\tau}}^{N^{1/2}}\dfrac{\log N}{t\log t}F\bigg(\frac{1/2-\log t/\log N}{\log t/\log N}\bigg)\dfrac{dt}{\log t}

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle(1+O(E))\dfrac{2C(N)N}{e^{\gamma}\log^{2}N}\int_{\tau}^{1/2}\dfrac{1}{u^{2}}F\bigg(\frac{1/2-u}{u}\bigg)du

\displaystyle\leqslant

\displaystyle g\dfrac{C(N)N}{\log^{2}N}(1+O(E)),

where

(8.2)

g:=\frac{2}{e^{\gamma}}\int_{\tau}^{1/2}\dfrac{1}{u^{2}}F\bigg(\frac{1/2-u}{u}\bigg)du=+\infty,

where
E
represents the corresponding error term.
This shows that when the summation range includes large primes, Chen’s switching principle plays an indispensable role; without it, we would not be able to make the upper bound of this term have the correct order of growth of its expected value.
In the other terms of Proposition
(1+2)
a more suitable choice of weights avoids this phenomenon; at least each term can be bounded above with the correct order of magnitude.
However, because the constants in those bounds are poor, avoiding the switching principle still fails to give a nontrivial lower bound for
D_{1,2}(N)
.

Thus, in the proof of Proposition
(1+2)
the switching principle is decisive, whereas it is not
necessary for Proposition
(1-2)
.
To this day we still have no proof of Proposition
(1+2)
that completely circumvents the switching principle.
Greaves
[
17
]
showed that when the level of distribution reaches
1/1.936
the switching principle can be avoided; this observation also underlies the work of Fouvry and Grupp
[
11
]
, who, after applying the Bombieri–Friedlander–Iwaniec
4/7
‑level
[
1
]
, managed to prove Proposition
(1-2)
without using the switching principle.

8.2.
When analytic tools advance, how do combinatorial tools keep pace with them?

Since Rényi
[
32
]
, the nearly eighty-year process of improving the level of distribution has deeply influenced the weighted sieve.
The step-by-step approach to the Goldbach Conjecture is inseparable from the synergy between analytic and combinatorial methods.
We explain what the weighted sieve method does, and why it is necessary to design different weighted sieves.

8.2.1.
The switching principle can only be used to estimate upper bounds

In the weighted sieve method, the fundamental reason why we employ multiple Buchstab iterations to obtain extremely complicated weights is that applying the switching principle yields only upper bounds, never lower bounds.

We illustrate this with one term from the proof:

(8.3)

G_{8}=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{3/11}\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant p_{2}\leqslant(N/p_{1})^{1/2}\\
(p_{1}p_{2},N)=1\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p_{1}p_{2}};\mathscr{P}(Np_{1}),p_{2}).

Because the exponent of the least prime factor satisfies
3/11>1/4
, the quantity
G_{8}
equals the number of primes
p\leqslant N
such that
N-p=p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}
with

(8.4)

N^{3/11}\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant p_{2}\leqslant(N/p_{1})^{1/2},\quad p_{3}\geqslant p_{2},\quad p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}\geqslant\varepsilon N,\quad(p_{1}p_{2}p_{3},N)=1.

Define the sets

(8.5)

\displaystyle\mathscr{M}

\displaystyle:=\{m\geqslant\varepsilon N:m=p_{1}p_{2},\ N^{3/11}\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant p_{2}\leqslant(N/p_{1})^{1/2},\ (p_{1}p_{2},N)=1\},

\displaystyle\mathscr{B}

\displaystyle:=\{b\leqslant(1-\varepsilon)N:b=N-mp,\ m\in\mathscr{M},\ p\leqslant N/m\}.

Clearly

G_{8}=S(\mathscr{B};\mathscr{P}(N),N^{1/2})+O(N^{1/2}).

In our paper we obtained an upper bound for
G_{8}
, whereas its lower bound is trivial:
G_{8}\geqslant 0
, because
f(s)=0
for
s\leqslant 2
.

In retrospect, in our proof of Proposition
(1+1.9)
we used a weighted inequality with twelve terms instead of the simpler Proposition
4.2
which states that

(8.6)

\begin{split}2D_{1,a}(N)\geqslant\ &2S_{1}(\kappa)-2S_{2}(\tau)-S_{3}(\kappa,\sigma)-2S_{4}(\sigma)\\
&-S_{5}(\kappa,\sigma)+S_{6}(\kappa,\sigma)+O(N^{1-\kappa})\end{split}

with

S_{6}(\kappa,\sigma)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{\kappa}\leqslant p_{1}\leqslant p_{2}\leqslant p_{3}\leqslant N^{\sigma}\\
(p_{1}p_{2}p_{3},N)=1\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p_{1}p_{2}p_{3}};\mathscr{P}(Np_{1}),p_{2}).

We made this choice since we were unable to derive a lower bound for
S_{6}
by switching principle.
If we estimated (
8.6
) directly, we would get a lower bound worse than the trivial one.

8.2.2.
At different scales of prime size, the level of prime distribution varies

Looking back at Lemmas
3.4
,
3.5
, and
3.6
, we see that the level of distribution varies depending on the range in which they are applied.
Consequently, when using the weighted sieve method, we often carefully select parameters to increase the level of distribution for those terms that are most sensitive to such an increase.
To illustrate this point, we present two different terms that exhibit different sensitivities as the level of distribution improves.

The first term is

G_{1}=S(\mathscr{A},\mathscr{P}(N),N^{4/53}).

In our proof we have

(8.7)

G_{1}\geqslant g_{1}\dfrac{C(N)N}{\log^{2}N}(1+O(E)),

where

(8.8)

g_{1}=\dfrac{53}{2}e^{-\gamma}f\bigg(\dfrac{53}{8}\bigg)\geqslant 14.87710,

and we have used

(8.9)

f\bigg(\dfrac{53}{8}\bigg)\geqslant f(6)\geqslant 0.999895,\quad e^{-\gamma}\geqslant 0.561459.

If we replace the Bombieri–Vinogradov theorem by the Elliott–Halberstam Conjecture, we obtain

(8.10)

G_{1}\geqslant g_{1}^{\prime}\dfrac{C(N)N}{\log^{2}N}(1+O(E)),

with

(8.11)

g_{1}^{\prime}=\dfrac{53}{2}e^{-\gamma}f\bigg(\dfrac{53}{4}-\varepsilon\bigg)\geqslant 14.87851,

where

f\bigg(\dfrac{53}{4}-\varepsilon\bigg)\geqslant 0.99999.

Thus the gain from the Elliott–Halberstam Conjecture in this term is tiny.

The second example is

(8.12)

G_{3}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}N^{9/19}\leqslant p\leqslant N^{1/2}\\
(p,N)=1\end{subarray}}S(\mathscr{A}_{p};\mathscr{P}(N),p),

In our proof we have

(8.13)

G_{3}\leqslant g_{3}\dfrac{C(N)N}{\log^{2}N}(1+O(E)),

where

(8.14)

g_{3}=8\log(10/9)\leqslant 0.84289.

If we instead use the weighted Elliott–Halberstam Conjecture, we obtain

(8.15)

G_{3}\leqslant g_{3}^{\prime}\dfrac{C(N)N}{\log^{2}N}(1+O(E)),

with

(8.16)

g_{3}^{\prime}=4\log(10/9)\leqslant 0.42145.

Here the benefit of the weighted Elliott–Halberstam Conjecture is substantial.

These two examples clearly show that the effect of improving the level of distribution is highly uneven across different sieve terms.
A careful design of the weighted sieve is therefore essential to exploit the available analytic information optimally.

8.2.3.
The calculus of variations cannot help us find the most suitable sieve function and parameters

The calculus of variations has achieved great success in mathematics and physics, providing a convenient framework for deriving the functional form that maximizes a given target parameter.
Yet, in the context of the weighted sieve method presented here, it cannot be applied—for two principal reasons.

First, the operations leading to weighted inequalities are inherently discrete, such as the Buchstab identity and the switching principle.
Moreover, the resulting expressions depend critically on the ranges of parameters.
For instance, in the setting of Proposition
4.5
, if the condition
\beta+\gamma+2\delta<1
is changed to
\beta+\gamma+2\delta>1
, the form of the weighted inequality changes dramatically.

Second, even after an appropriate range is chosen and the form of the weighted inequality is fixed, the calculus of variations remains impractical because the relevant auxiliary functions
F
,
f
, and
w
are defined by differential-difference equations.
The calculus of variations is naturally adapted to differential equations, not to difference equations.
