COMPASS-Hedge: Learning Safely Without Knowing the World


Abstract
Online learning algorithms often faces a fundamental trilemma: balancing regret guarantees between adversarial and stochastic settings and providing baseline safety against a fixed comparator. While existing methods excel in one or two of these regimes, they typically fail to unify all three without sacrificing optimal rates or requiring oracle access to problem-dependent parameters. In this work, we bridge this gap by introducing COMPASS-Hedge. Our algorithm is the first full-information method to simultaneously achieve: i) Minimax-optimal regret in adversarial environments ; ii) Instance-optimal, gap-dependent regret in stochastic environments; and -up to logarithmic factors- iii) 𝒪 ~ ​ ( 1 ) \tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1) regret relative to a designated baseline policy. Crucially, COMPASS-Hedge is parameter-free and requires no prior knowledge of the environment’s nature or the magnitude of the stochastic sub optimality gaps. Our approach hinges on a novel integration of adaptive pseudo-regret scaling and phase-based aggression, coupled with a comparator-aware mixing strategy. To the best of our knowledge, this provides the first “best-of-three-world” guarantee in the full-information setting, establishing that baseline safety does not have to come at the cost of worst-case robustness or stochastic efficiency.

Introduction

Online learning with expert advice provides a robust framework for sequential decision-making under uncertainty. In this setting, a learner must dynamically aggregate the opinions of a diverse pool of
A
experts. Lacking prior knowledge of which expert is most reliable—or even the nature of the environment itself—the learner’s success hinges on balancing the exploitation of historically top-performing strategies with the exploration of those that may excel in the future. This model is remarkably universal: whether navigating the fluctuations of a volatile stock market
[
15

or the risk-sensitive outcomes of clinical trials
[
50
]

(Regret)

Then, the classical desideratum is to ensure that

Historically, research has diverged into two distinct regimes. In adversarial version, where losses can be chosen maliciously, algorithms such as Hedge achieve minimax-optimal regret of order

The quest for a “universal” learner — one that performs optimally regardless of whether the environment is benign or malicious—has been a central theme in online learning research over the past decade, leading to the development of

Technical Bottleneck:
The Trade-off between Conservation and Aggression.

The fundamental difficulty in unifying these three “worlds” stems from a deep-seated
tension in the per-round update mechanism.
Baseline safety necessitates conservative mixing strategies that tightly control
deviation from a trusted comparator.
In contrast, achieving stochastic optimality requires selective and increasingly
aggressive updates that rapidly concentrate probability mass on the empirical leader
in order to exploit suboptimality gaps
\Delta_{a}
.
Moreover, state-of-the-art best-of-both-worlds methods lack explicit safeguards
for protecting a designated baseline under adversarial conditions.

A potential workaround could be to assume oracle access to environment-specific quantities,
such as suboptimality gaps or problem-dependent confidence bounds.
While such assumptions yield valid guarantees in principle,
they fundamentally alter the scope of the algorithm by embedding
environment-specific knowledge into its design.
Indeed, if tight upper bounds on stochastic instance-optimal rates were known
a priori, existing methods
[
43
]
could be adapted to satisfy safety constraints with minor
modifications.

However, this reliance on problem-dependent parameters breaks universality and renders
algorithms brittle in realistic settings where such quantities are unknown,
time-varying, or ill-defined. Hence, designing a single, parameter-free algorithm that reconciles these objectives remains a critical open challenge.

2

Thus, our scheme bypasses the impossibility result of

oblivious

This induces a self-correcting feedback loop: in benign environments, the learner

learning rate to recover
O(\sqrt{T})

a technique that may be of independent interest for other adaptive online learning problems where the scale of the regret is not known a priori.

2. Bridging the Expected–Pseudo Regret Gap.

A major theoretical hurdle in using empirical observations to drive the

i)
the

(the theoretical objective),
ii)

the
observed regret
(the quantity available to the algorithm),
and
iii)
the
pseudo-regret
(the quantity appearing in safety guarantees).
We rigorously control this discrepancy, denoted by
\Delta_{\mathrm{reg}}

providing a unified treatment across environments:

\Delta_{\mathrm{reg}}\;=\;\mathbb{E}\!\left[\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle c_{t},\mu^{c}-\mu\rangle\right]\;-\;\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle c_{t},\mu^{c}-\mu\rangle\right].

Leveraging modern concentration inequalities together with the structural result
of
1

\Delta_{\mathrm{reg}}=o(1)
in stochastic

\Delta_{\mathrm{reg}}=O(\sqrt{T})

To bridge these regimes, we distinguish between two adversarial models.
For
oblivious adversaries
—where losses are fixed in advance or generated
from a random instance selected at
t=0
—our proof techique follows directly.
For
adaptive adversaries
, we invoke Assumption 3.1, which limits the
adversary’s cumulative adaptivity.
This prevents the environment from “tricking” the doubling mechanism by
reacting too aggressively to the learner’s scaling, and establishes that the
empirical scheme remains a faithful proxy for the underlying safety constraints.

3. Horizon-Free Self-Scaling and Comparator Mixing.

To ensure that the algorithm is horizon-free,
COMPASS-Hedge
employs
a decaying step size (e.g.,
1/\sqrt{t}
) within the underlying Hedge framework.
We extend the phased-aggression logic by integrating it with a

comparator-aware mixing strategy
.
The key insight is that the mixture weight assigned to the baseline policy

\mu^{c}
is inversely proportional to the current “aggression budget.”

•

Initial Phase.

When the estimated regret against the baseline is large, the mixture
closely tracks
\mu^{c}
, providing immediate protection.

•

Adaptive Phase.

As the algorithm gains confidence and the self-scaling estimator increases,
the mixture relaxes, allowing the learner to achieve uniform optimality
from a Best-of-Both-Worlds perspective.

Just as in Follow-The-Regularized-Leader (FTRL), where the step size controls
the strength of regularization, our approach uses the doubling-trick estimator
as a
self-scaling regularizer
for the mixture.
This ensures that the algorithm only “breaks away” from the safety of the
baseline when the statistical evidence for a superior strategy is overwhelming.

Summary.
COMPASS-Hedge
unifies these objectives through a symbiotic architecture:
Pillar 3
establishes a horizon-free, comparator-aware mixing foundation, which is operationalized by
Pillar 1
’s parameter-free estimation of pseudo-regret. Finally,
Pillar 2
serves as the theoretical anchor; by rigorously bounding the expected-pseudo regret gap, it ensures our empirical scaling remains robust and universal across both stochastic and adversarial regimes.

Related Work and Motivating Scenarios

The regret properties of online learning have been extensively studied; for general overviews, see
29
,
47
.
The challenge of unifying robustness, adaptivity, and safety lies at the intersection of three mature yet largely disjoint research directions.
While recent BOBW-algorithms achieve stochastic instance-optimality alongside adversarial robustness—both in the full-information setting
[
42
]
and under bandit feedback
[
52
]
—the trajectory of this field has increasingly pivoted toward multi-armed bandit variants
[
37
, e.g.,]
. Conversely, the safety-oriented literature even in the full-information regime has remained more restrictive.

Bicriteria Measures and Baseline Safety.

A seminal precursor to our work is
23
, who investigated a bicriteria performance measure: simultaneously minimizing regret against the best expert while guaranteeing performance against the average of all experts (or a fixed weighting). They formalized the fundamental observation that for comparisons against a fixed baseline, the “gold standar” is
constant regret
(independent of
T
). However, while achieving zero regret against the average in isolation is trivial (via uniform weights), obtaining this simultaneously with worst-case guarantees is non-trivial.
23
addressed this via modified MWU (e.g., Prod) or conservative phased strategies in Exponential Weights—techniques later adapted by
43
for safety in bandit settings. However, a parameter-free unification that achieves "Best-of-Three-Worlds" guaranteesremained an open challenge in the full-information regime.

The Practical Imperative of Full Information.

Finally, we note that in many safety-critical domains,
full-information feedback is the natural modeling choice, as counterfactual costs
are observable post hoc, rendering bandit formulations unnecessarily restrictive.
For example, in
algorithmic finance
, all asset returns are publicly observed,
calling for strategies that exploit market structure without underperforming
benchmark indices
[
15
,
27
]
.
Similarly,
smart-grid load balancing
relies on known routing costs to optimize
efficiency while strictly adhering to stability constraints
[
48
]
.
Ultimately, these domains highlight the critical need for both theoretical foundations and practical solutions in principled safety-aware deployment.

Preliminaries and Problem Setup

Notation.

We use standard
O
-asymptotic notation.
For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
[n]:=\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
We denote by
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
the Euclidean inner product and by

\|\cdot\|
the Euclidean norm.
For
x\in\mathbb{R}
,
\lceil x\rceil
denotes the smallest integer greater than
or equal to
x
, and
e_{i}
denotes the
i

\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Finally, the
n
-dimensional probability simplex is defined as

\Delta_{n}\;:=\;\bigl\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\;:\;x_{i}\geq 0\ \forall i\in[n],\ \sum_{i=1}^{n}x_{i}=1\bigr\}.

We consider a full-information online learning setting with
A\geq 2
arms and a time horizon
T\geq 1
. Let
(\Omega,\mathcal{G},\mathbb{P})
be a probability space supporting the interaction. The game proceeds in discrete rounds
t=1,\dots,T
.

Interaction Protocol.

At the beginning of each round
t
, the learner selects a mixed action
\mu^{t}\in\Delta_{A}
, where
\Delta_{A}
denotes the probability simplex over
A
arms. Simultaneously, the environment (adversary) determines a loss vector
c^{t}=(c^{t}(1),\dots,c^{t}(A))\in[0,1]^{A}
. The learner then samples an action
a_{t}\sim\mu^{t}
and incurs loss
c^{t}(a_{t})
. Crucially, at the end of the round, the
entire
loss vector
c^{t}
is revealed to the learner (full-information feedback).

In addition to the standard framework, the player receives a
safe comparator strategy

\mu^{c}\in\Delta_{A}
as input. The objective is twofold: to compete with the best arm in hindsight (optimality), while ensuring performance effectively at least as good as
\mu^{c}
(safety).

Filtration and Adversarial Models.

To rigorously capture the information flow and the power of the adversary, we define the history and the associated filtration.
Let
H_{t-1}:=(\mu^{1},c^{1},\dots,\mu^{t-1},c^{t-1})
denote the history of interactions up to the beginning of round
t
. We view
H_{t-1}
as a random variable taking values in the history space
\mathsf{H}_{t-1}=(\Delta_{A}\times[0,1]^{A})^{t-1}
.
For any sample path
\omega\in\Omega
, we denote the realized history as
h_{t-1}:=H_{t-1}(\omega)
.

We define the natural filtration
\mathbb{F}=(\mathcal{F}_{t})_{t\geq 0}
where
\mathcal{F}_{t}:=\sigma(\mu^{1},c^{1},\dots,\mu^{t},c^{t})
represents the
\sigma
-algebra generated by all variables revealed up to the end of round
t
. Consequently,
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
encapsulates all information available to the learner before making the decision
\mu^{t}
.

This formalism allows us to distinguish between different environmental assumptions standard in the literature
[
11
,
14
]
:

•

Oblivious Adversary:
The loss sequence
c^{1:T}
is fixed in advance or depends only on extrinsic randomness, independent of the learner’s actions. Formally,
c^{t}
is independent of
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
.

•

Adaptive Adversary:
The adversary may choose
c^{t}
based on the realized history
h_{t-1}
. Here,
c^{t}
is
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
-measurable (or measurable with respect to
\mathcal{F}_{t-1}
augmented by internal randomization).

In this work, we consider a general setting that encompasses both, characterized by the
conditional mean loss
. We denote:

m_{t}(a\mid h):=\mathbb{E}[c^{t}(a)\mid H_{t-1}=h].

Throughout the paper,
\mathbb{E}[\cdot]
denotes the expectation with respect to the internal randomness of both the algorithm and the environment.

3.1
Performance Metrics

The primary objective in online learning is to minimize the difference between the learner’s cumulative loss and that of the best fixed strategy in hindsight. This gives rise to the standard notion of
Expected Regret
.

Definition 3.1

(Expected Regret)
.

The expected regret of an algorithm is defined as the expectation of the realized regret against the best arm in hindsight:

\mathcal{R}_{\text{exp}}(T)\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle c^{t},\mu^{t}\rangle-\min_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle c^{t},\mu\rangle\right].

This metric captures the algorithm’s performance relative to the optimal action for the
specific realization
of the loss sequence.
In our setting, however, competing with the best expert is not enough; we must also respect a specific safety constraint.

Definition 3.2

(Comparator Regret)
.

The regret compared to a specific fixed baseline strategy
\mu^{c}\in\Delta_{A}
is:

\mathcal{R}(\mu^{c})\triangleq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle c^{t},\mu^{t}-\mu^{c}\rangle\right].

Our goal is to design a single algorithm that simultaneously guarantees
\mathcal{R}(\mu^{c})\leq\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
(constant-like safety) while minimizing the worst-case regret against the best expert.

From Expected to Pseudo-Regret.

Directly analyzing
\mathcal{R}_{\text{exp}}
is often intractable due to the correlation between the algorithm’s choices and the identity of the hindsight optimizer. More concretely, the hindsight optimizer is also a random variable determined by each random history path.
To facilitate analysis, we utilize the notion of
Pseudo-Regret
, which compares the learner’s expected loss to the expected loss of any fixed comparator.

Definition 3.3

(Pseudo-Regret)
.

The worst-case pseudo-regret is defined as:

\mathcal{R}_{\text{pseudo}}(T)\triangleq\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\mathbb{E}\left[\langle c^{t},\mu^{t}-\mu\rangle\right].

Remark 3.1

(Discussion on the Gap.)
.

The distinction between
\mathcal{R}_{\text{exp}}
and
\mathcal{R}_{\text{pseudo}}
lies in the placement of the maximization (or minimization) operator relative to the expectation.

\mathcal{R}_{\text{exp}}
measures performance against the random empirical minimizer
(\mathbb{E}[\min\dots])
, whereas
\mathcal{R}_{\text{pseudo}}
measures performance against the minimizer of the expected losses
(\min\mathbb{E}[\dots])
.
By Jensen’s inequality or simple properties of the infimum,
\mathcal{R}_{\text{exp}}\geq\mathcal{R}_{\text{pseudo}}
, meaning pseudo-regret is generally a lower bound. However, as we demonstrate in Lemma
3.1
, the gap between these two metrics becomes asymptotically negligible
(o(T)
or even
o(1))
in an interesting spectrum of applications—specifically where the environment exhibits stochastic regularity or bounded martingale variance. Thus, optimizing for pseudo-regret serves as a valid proxy for the true objective.

3.2
Model Assumptions

3.2.1
Beyond Oblivious Adversaries

While standard Best-of-Both-Worlds (BOBW) approaches typically assume an oblivious adversary, our framework extends to
adaptive
environments. However, it is a known impossibility result that against a fully adaptive adversary—one with unlimited memory and sensitivity—policy regret is lower-bounded by
\Omega(T)

[
3
]
. To bypass this barrier, the literature typically imposes structural constraints: limiting the adversary’s memory
[
3
]
or smoothed adversaries
[
28
]
.

We introduce a third, distinct perspective: instead of limiting
what
the adversary remembers or
how concentrated
their randomness is, we limit the
magnitude
of their reaction.

Assumption 3.1

(Controlled Adaptivity)
.

For each
t\geq 1
and
a\in[A]
, assume there exists a deterministic nonnegative sequence
\{\varepsilon_{t}\}_{t\geq 1}
such that the conditional mean loss satisfies:

\sup_{h,h^{\prime}\in\mathsf{H}_{t-1}}\big|m_{t}(a\mid h)-m_{t}(a\mid h^{\prime})\big|\leq\varepsilon_{t}.

(
A1
)

We define the cumulative adaptivity for any interval
I
as
E_{I}:=\sum_{t\in I}\varepsilon_{t}
. We assume
E_{I}\leq C_{E}\sqrt{|I|}
for some constant
C_{E}>0
.

Remark 3.2
.

(
A1
) positions our model uniquely within the landscape of adaptive adversaries:

A1
vs. Memory-Bounded:
An
m
-memory bounded adversary
[
3
]
is constrained to depend only on the
m
most recent actions. While this limits the temporal scope of dependency, it allows for arbitrary discontinuities ("jumps") based on recent history. In contrast, our model allows for
infinite memory
(
\infty
-memory bounded), provided the aggregate "drift" or sensitivity to history remains within the budget
O(\sqrt{T})
.

A1
vs. Smoothed Analysis:
Recent works like
28
assume the adversary plays from distributions with bounded density (anti-concentration).
Our model does not require the adversary to be noisy or smooth; it allows for deterministic behavior (Dirac deltas), provided the deterministic choice is stable over histories.

Remark 3.3
.

Notably, our framework is not strictly stronger than these alternatives but rather
orthogonal
to them. For example, a switching-cost adversary satisfies the
m=1
memory constraint (local dependence), yet may violate our stability condition by inducing large instantaneous jumps in the loss landscape. As a result, neither set of assumptions subsumes the other.

In essence, (
A1
) restricts the environment from changing the “rules of the game” too aggressively in response to the learner’s past actions, thereby capturing a
drifting
—as opposed to vindictive—adversarial behavior. To facilitate concentration analysis, we also control the variance of the noise around the conditional mean.

3.2.2
Optimal Arm Identifiability

In the stochastic regime, the environment behaves probabilistically with a fixed underlying structure.

Assumption (Stoc.) 3.1

(i.i.d. Losses)
.

The loss vectors
c^{1},\dots,c^{T}
are independent and identically distributed.

Assumption (Stoc.) 3.2

(Unique Optimizer and Positive Gap)
.

There exists a
unique
optimal arm

a^{\star}\in\operatorname{argmin}_{a}\mathbb{E}[c^{t}(a)].

(
S2
)

Let
\Delta_{a}:=\mathbb{E}[c^{t}(a)-c^{t}(a^{\star})]
denote the suboptimality gap. Consequently, we assume
\Delta:=\min_{a\neq a^{\star}}\Delta_{a}>0.

Remark 3.4

(On Identifiability)
.

(
S2
) is a standard yet non-trivial requirement in gap-dependent analysis. It ensures a strictly positive margin
\Delta
, allowing the algorithm such as the UCB algorithm
[
5
]
. to settle on
a^{\star}
and achieve logarithmic instance-optimal regret
O(\frac{\log T}{\Delta})
.
The uniqueness of the optimal arm ensures an identifiable gap structure under which
pseudo-regret
faithfully tracks
expected regret
.

In contrast, when multiple arms are optimal,
42
show that the discrepancy between these metrics can remain asymptotically non-negligible (scaling as
\Omega(\sqrt{T})
) due to random fluctuations causing the empirical minimizer to oscillate among optimal arms. Consequently, addressing this regime would require techniques fundamentally different from the “regret self-estimation” framework developed here; thus, we highlight the relaxation of this assumption as a major open question for future research.

A key consequence of the above model assumptions is a uniform bound on the gap between
pseudo-regret and expected regret over arbitrary intervals.

Lemma 3.1

(Pseudo-regret versus expected regret)
.

Let
I\subseteq\{1,\dots,T\}
be any interval. Then,

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{pseudo}}(I)\;\leq\;\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exp}}(I)\;\leq\;\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{pseudo}}(I)+\mathbb{G}(I),

where
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{exp}}(I)
and
\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{pseudo}}(I)
denote,
respectively, the expected regret and the pseudo-regret accumulated over
I
.
Moreover, the remainder term
\mathbb{G}(I)
satisfies

\mathbb{G}(I)=\begin{cases}O\!\left(\sqrt{|I|}\right),\text{under (\hyperref@@ii[A1]{{\color[rgb]{0.2344,0.4688,0.707}A1}}\textendash adversarial regime)},\\[3.99994pt]
o(1),\text{under ({\color[rgb]{0.2344,0.4688,0.707}\hyperref@@ii[Stoc1]{{\color[rgb]{0.2344,0.4688,0.707}S1\&2}}}\textendash stochastic regime)}.\end{cases}

Remark 3.5
.

In words,
Lemma

3.1
bounds the gap between pseudo-regret and expected regret.
In the adversarial setting, the difference is controlled via moderate history dependence of conditional mean loss, and bounded variance of martingale noises. This leads to an
O(\sqrt{|I|})
gap between pseudo-regret and expected regret. In the stochastic setting, i.i.d. cost and unique optimal arm assumption imply that this gap vanishes asymptotically. This observation is the key technical turning point: it allows us to analyze
expected
regret while still using data-dependent stopping rules in the algorithm, even against adaptive adversaries.

4.1
Algorithm Description and Intuition

We now introduce
Compass-Hedge
(
Algorithm

1
), a meta-algorithm designed to safeguard a base learner (Hedge) using a conservative comparator
\mu^{c}
. The core intuition relies on a
hierarchical geometric regret estimation
that manages the trade-off between “safety” (adhering to
\mu^{c}
) and “optimality” (following the Hedge expert).

The algorithm operates on two time-scales:

1.

Stages (
s
):
The outer loop controls the global regret budget. Each stage
s
is associated with a pseudo-regret capacity
\widehat{R}_{s}
. If the Hedge expert accumulates regret exceeding this capacity, the stage ends, and we double the budget (
\widehat{R}_{s+1}\approx 2\widehat{R}_{s}
). This ensures the total number of stages is bounded by
O(\log T)
.

2.

Phases (
k[s]
):
Within a stage, time is divided into phases. In each phase, we fix a mixing coefficient
\alpha\in[0,1]
and play the mixture

\mu^{t}=\alpha\hat{\mu}^{t}+(1-\alpha)\mu^{c}

•

We start with small
\alpha
(mostly safe).

•

We only exit a phase (and increase
\alpha
) if we verify that the comparator
\mu^{c}
is significantly suboptimal (i.e., the Hedge expert has “earned" the right to be played more aggressively).

•

This “Phase-Budgeted" approach ensures that we never incur large regret against
\mu^{c}
unless we have already banked significant gains against the environment.

Intuitively,
COMPASS-Hedge
operates on a “probationary” principle: it anchors to a safe comparator and only shifts weight to an exploring no-regret learner when the comparator is empirically proven to be significantly suboptimal. This mechanism ensures
safety
: by requiring “proof of failure” from the safe arm before abandoning it, the algorithm caps potential losses, guaranteeing in each stage constant regret against the comparator (
lines 11,14
). While this ensures safety, calibrating this rejection threshold poses a dilemma:
identifying the precise magnitude of cumulative regret that distinguishes suboptimality from transient noise.
A static worst-case barrier of order
\Theta(\sqrt{T})
would suffice but would be overly conservative in benign (“beyond worst-case”) environments, effectively destroying instance-optimality. To overcome this, we deploy a
hierarchical doubling trick estimation
that allows the pseudo-regret budget to expand adaptively, scaling with the actual realized noise rather than the worst-case horizon (
line 24
) . Consequently, the algorithm automatically interpolates between adversarial robustness and stochastic instance-optimality without requiring prior knowledge of the regime.

Analysis and Proof Sketch

The proof of

Step 1: Bridging Realized and Expected Regret.

Our analysis begins by observing that the algorithm’s control logic (

) relies on

R_{\text{hedge}}
) and the comparator (
R_{\text{comp}}

Lemma

bridges this gap via standard martingale concentration arguments, ensuring that the stopping conditions triggered by realized losses validly translate to guarantees on the pseudo-regret.

Step 2: The Doubling Mechanism (Regret Control).

With the statistical validity established, we focus on the algorithmic structure. We ensure that our proxy for total regret does not grow uncontrollably. The stage-based doubling trick acts as a coarse regularizer.

Lemma 4.1

(Estimated Regret Bounds)
.

For every stage
s

\widehat{R}_{s}\leq\widehat{R}_{s+1}<2R_{\text{hedge}}
.

Implication:
This ensures that the number of stages remains logarithmic (
S\approx O(\log T)
), allowing us to sum per-stage regrets efficiently without incurring a linear penalty.

Step 3: The Safety Ratchet (Phase Dynamics).

This step constitutes the core safety mechanism. Within a phase
k
of any stage, the algorithm fixes the mixing weight
\alpha_{[k]}
. The following lemmas govern the behavior inside a phase:

Lemma 4.2

(Regret within a Normal Phase)
.

Consider a phase
k

\alpha_{[k]}<1

\mu^{c}
is bounded by
\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu^{c})\leq 2^{k-1}

Lemma 4.3

(Comparator Gain upon Phase Exit)

If the algorithm chooses to exit phase
k

\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu^{c})\leq-\,2^{k-1}.

Implication:
These two lemmas form a “ratche”: we only escalate risk (increase
\alpha

Crucially, by construction, the total number of phases cannot exceed
O(\log R_{\text{hedge}})
. This guarantees that the regret remains logarithmic even if the algorithm halts abruptly due to an unknown horizon.

Step 4: Aggregating to Stage Optimality.

Finally, by aggregating the phases within a stage
s

Lemma 4.4

For any fixed stage

Since the total regret against

Conclusion

We introduced
Compass-Hedge
, a single algorithm that reconciles three objectives that are usually treated separately:
baseline safety
,
worst-case robustness
, and
stochastic adaptivity
. Our guarantees show that one can simultaneously maintain
O(1)
regret relative to a trusted benchmark, achieve minimax-optimal
O(\sqrt{T})
adversarial regret, and obtain instance-dependent stochastic rates. Empirically, our financial experiments conceptually support this picture:
Compass-Hedge
exploits benign regimes to track strong experts, yet reliably falls back to the benchmark when conditions become volatile.

Future work.

A natural next step is to tighten the remaining polylogarithmic factors and to understand the fundamental “price of protection”: are the extra

More broadly, extending comparator-safe adaptivity beyond expert problems—to linear bandits, structured action spaces, and reinforcement learning—is a promising direction for building richer sequential decision multi-agent systems.

Appendix A
Extended Related Work and Motivating Scenarios

The literature on online learning is vast, spanning several decades of research in information theory, statistics, and computer science. We provide here a more detailed contextualization of
COMPASS-Hedge
within the broader landscape of sequential decision-making.

A.1
Exploitation vs. Safety Trade-offs.

Safe exploitation methods typically deviate from minimax play to target weak opponents, often using parameters to balance safety and aggression
[
17
,
39
]
. This approach forces a compromise: algorithms either lack theoretical exploitation guarantees
[
25
]
or risk linear regret against strong adversaries
[
17
,
39
]
.
Compass-Hedge
avoids these trade-offs by defining safety as achieving
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
regret against a baseline, ensuring robustness without sacrificing optimal adversarial rates.

A.2
Comparator Adaptivity in Full Information.

Safety, defined as ensuring low regret relative to a pre-specified baseline policy
\mu^{c}
, is a cornerstone of “safe AI.” In the full-information setting, it is established that algorithms can achieve constant regret against a specific comparator strategy while maintaining optimal worst-case regret guarantees. Notable examples include the Phased Aggression framework
[
23
]
and various adaptive strategies
[
30
,
32
,
35
,
46
]
. Similarly, parameter-free methods adapt to unknown comparators
[
44
,
16
,
45
]
. These works successfully resolve the “Best-of-Both-Worlds” challenge regarding safety and adversarial robustness. However, they typically do not account for the stochastic nature of the environment, potentially missing the opportunity for gap-dependent logarithmic regret. Our work extends this line of inquiry by unifying these objectives.

A.3
Hardness of Safe Bandit Learning.

Under bandit feedback, achieving constant regret against a deterministic comparator implies linear worst-case regret against other actions
[
36
]
. This result indicates that the
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
safety guarantees are impossible in the bandit setting without relaxing assumptions on the comparator, such as requiring it to be full-support
[
43
]
. Although recent advances in bandit convex optimization have explored comparator adaptivity
[
49
]
, they generally cannot replicate the safety guarantee under full information. By operating in the full-information regime,
Compass-Hedge
avoids these hardness results.

A.4
Conservative Bandits and Safe RL.

Our framework’s definition of safety is distinct from the paradigms often studied in conservative bandits
[
51
]
and conservative reinforcement learning (RL)
[
26
]
. In those contexts, algorithms are designed to satisfy a constraint—often defined as securing at least a
(1-\alpha)
-fraction of a baseline’s return. While this ensures performance relative to a baseline, it allows for linear regret
\mathcal{O}(\alpha T)
relative to the optimal policy in the worst case. Furthermore, in constrained settings such as Constrained MDPs
[
9
,
22
]
, achieving constant regret against a safe baseline is frequently unobtainable under adversarial environemnt
[
40
]
. In contrast,
Compass-Hedge
aims for constant
\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(1)
regret relative to the baseline while simultaneously minimizing regret against the best strategy in hindsight.

A.5
Foundations of Adversarial and Adaptive Learning

The adversarial online learning framework, pioneered by
38
and
24
, focuses on minimizing regret against an arbitrary sequence of losses. The Hedge and Weighted Majority algorithms established the minimax-optimal
O(\sqrt{T\log A})
rate, later generalized through the lens of Online Mirror Descent (OMD) and Follow-the-Regularized-Leader (FTRL)
[
47
,
29
]
.

Subsequent research sought to move beyond worst-case bounds.
First-order
bounds
[
7
]
scale with the loss of the best expert, while
second-order
(variance-dependent) bounds—achieved by algorithms like AdaHedge
[
18
]
and Squint
[
34
]
—exploit cases where experts exhibit low variance or large suboptimality gaps.
42
provided a landmark analysis showing that even vanilla Hedge with a
1/\sqrt{t}
learning rate can be instance-optimal in stochastic settings while remaining robust, though it lacks explicit safety guarantees relative to a specific baseline.

A.6
Adaptation via Doubling Tricks

The strategy of parameter adaptation via geometric restarts, commonly referred to as the “Doubling Trick,” is a foundational technique in online learning used to convert horizon-dependent algorithms into anytime strategies. The concept can be traced back to
6
in the context of adversarial bandits. In its most ubiquitous form, popularized by
14
for prediction with expert advice, the trick involves partitioning the time horizon into epochs of length
T_{i}=2^{i}
. At the start of each epoch, the base algorithm is restarted with the new horizon guess. This approach has been widely adapted across the bandit literature; for instance,
8
utilized a sparser doubling schedule with
T_{i}=2^{2^{i}}
to render the UCB-R algorithm anytime for stochastic environments. In the specific setting of delayed feedback,
31
employed this mechanism to adapt to the unknown total delay
D
by restarting the algorithm whenever the accumulated delay exceeded the current estimate.

However, while effective for minimax regret, the doubling trick is not without caveats. As analyzed by
10
, the “oblivious” nature of the trick—where the algorithm’s internal state is reset at every epoch—can be detrimental when preserving finer, instance-dependent guarantees. Specifically, in our safety-constrained setting, a standard doubling trick would periodically reset the negative regret accumulated against the comparator. This necessitates a more intricate, coupled schedule (as employed in
Compass
-Hedge) that adapts without discarding the statistical evidence required to maintain the
O(1)
safety bound.

A.7
The Best-of-Both-Worlds Paradigm

The Best-of-Both-Worlds (BOBW) literature seeks algorithms that perform optimally in both stochastic and adversarial environments without prior knowledge of the regime. While earlier work focused on full-information feedback, the field has recently prioritized the multi-armed bandit (MAB) setting
[
13
,
52
]
due to the challenge of exploration under uncertainty. However, the full-information setting remains technically distinct and practically vital. Modern BOBW methods often rely on adaptive regularization (e.g., Tsallis entropy) to interpolate between the two regimes
[
52
]
.
COMPASS-Hedge
contributes to this line by showing that the "third world" of safety can be integrated into this duality through an empirical doubling-trick on the aggression schedule.

A.8
Manipulation of Learning Agents

While our work focuses on designing learners that are robust to adversarial environments, a growing body of literature investigates the inverse problem: how strategic optimizers can exploit the predictable dynamics of learning agents
[
12
,
21
]
. This line of work demonstrates that non-myopic opponents can steer standard mean-based learners (such as MWU) toward outcomes that significantly favor the manipulator. It is characterized across repeated auctions
[
12
,
20
]
, contract design
[
33
]
, and Bayesian games
[
41
]
. Most recently, Assos et al.
[
4
]
demonstrated that while optimal manipulation strategies can be efficiently generated for zero-sum settings , the same task becomes computationally intractable when applied to general-sum game.

A.9
Detailed Motivating Scenarios for Full-Information Safety

We elaborate on why the full-information feedback model is the appropriate abstraction for many safety-critical applications, where counterfactual losses are observable post-hoc:

•

Clinical Decision Support and Titration:
In personalized medicine, clinicians use algorithms to suggest medication dosages. Once a patient is treated, their physiological markers (e.g., blood pressure, glucose levels) are recorded. Biomechanical models or historical patient data allow clinicians to estimate what the response
would have been
for alternative dosages within a local range (the counterfactuals). The algorithm must outperform the standard-of-care (
\mu^{c}
) if the patient responds predictably (stochastic), but must never exceed a risk threshold if the patient’s reaction is anomalous (adversarial)
[
50
]
.

•

Financial Hedging and Index Tracking:
In "Active Indexing," a fund manager aims to outperform a benchmark index (e.g., S&P 500) by weighting individual stocks or sector ETFs. At the end of each trading day, the exact returns for
all
available assets are known (full-information). The manager seeks an algorithm that exploits market inefficiencies when they appear (stochastic adaptivity) but guarantees that the portfolio’s tracking error relative to
\mu^{c}
remains near-constant during market crashes or manipulative "flash crashes"
[
15
]
.

•

Dynamic Resource Allocation in Smart Grids:
Balancing supply and demand in electrical grids requires selecting distribution paths. After the demand is realized, the costs (resistive losses, congestion prices) of all possible paths are observable. A safety-critical baseline
\mu^{c}
represents a legacy "stability-first" routing protocol. The learner must minimize energy waste while ensuring that it never performs significantly worse than the legacy protocol, which could otherwise lead to grid instability or blackouts during adversarial demand spikes
[
48
]
.

•

Autonomous Traffic and Logistics Routing:
Fleet management systems (e.g., delivery services) must choose routes for hundreds of vehicles. After a trip, GPS and sensor data from the entire network provide the travel times for all alternative routes. The "baseline"
\mu^{c}
is a conservative, high-capacity arterial route. The learner aims to find faster shortcuts (stochastic) but must revert to
\mu^{c}
to avoid systemic gridlock if traffic patterns become non-stationary or adversarial due to accidents or sensor failures.

•

Online Content Moderation and Policy Filtering:
Automated moderation systems must decide whether to flag content based on signals from multiple classifiers (the experts). When a human moderator audits a sample of the content, their label provides the ground truth for
all
classifiers simultaneously. The system’s baseline
\mu^{c}
is a strict "Safe Harbor" filter. The algorithm should adapt to new slang or evolving content (stochastic) but must guarantee safety relative to the baseline to comply with legal regulations during coordinated "trolling" attacks.

Appendix B
High-level Proof Sketch

Our Position and Contribution.

Our work unifies distinct strands of the online learning literature by presenting a
single full-information algorithm
that satisfies a “Best-of-Three-Worlds” guarantee. Specifically, it simultaneously achieves:
(i) minimax-optimal adversarial regret (up to logarithmic factors),
(ii) instance-optimal stochastic regret, and
(iii)
O(\log T)
regret with respect to an arbitrary safe comparator
\mu^{c}
.
To our knowledge, this is the first result to establish these three guarantees concurrently within a unified framework.

Algorithmic Construction: Beyond Fixed Budgets.

Our approach builds upon the
Phased Aggression with Mixing
framework introduced by
43
. While their work successfully combines comparator-oriented mixing with aggressive learning to achieve
\tilde{O}(\sqrt{T})
worst-case regret and constant comparator regret, it fundamentally relies on a
pre-specified pseudo-regret budget
. This requirement is prohibitive for achieving instance-optimality in stochastic settings, where the optimal regret rate (
O(\log T/\Delta)
) depends on unknown gap parameters and cannot be fixed a priori.

To overcome this limitation, we replace the fixed budget with a
data-dependent doubling trick
. Instead of receiving the pseudo-regret target as input, our algorithm estimates the budget dynamically by tracking the
observed
cumulative loss difference between the learner and the best arm. We initialize the estimated budget at a small constant (e.g.,
\widehat{R}_{1}=2
) and double it only when the empirical regret violates the current stage’s safety condition. This allows the algorithm to implicitly “learn” the complexity of the environment: keeping the budget small in easy (stochastic) instances while expanding it to
O(\sqrt{T})
in hard (adversarial) scenarios.

Analytical Challenge: Bridging the Gap to Expected Regret.

A core technical challenge in our analysis is bounding the gap between the
expected regret
and the
pseudo-regret
.
Recall that standard FTRL analyses typically bound the pseudo-regret:

{\mathcal{R}}_{T}^{\text{pseudo}}=\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle c^{t},\mu^{c}-\mu\rangle\right].

However, our doubling mechanism relies on the
observed
maximum regret, and our final goal is to bound the expected version of this observed regret:

{\mathcal{R}}_{T}^{\text{expected}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\langle c^{t},\mu^{c}-\mu\rangle\right].

The discrepancy arises because the algorithm makes decisions based on the realization of the maximum loss difference, not its expectation. To rigorously bound
\mathcal{R}_{T}
, we must control the concentration of the empirical losses around their means.

•

Stochastic Setting:
Following the techniques in
1
, we leverage the assumption of a unique optimal arm to show that the gap between the expected maximum and the maximum expectation vanishes asymptotically (i.e., the gap is
o(1)
). This allows our pseudo-regret bounds to translate directly to expected regret bounds.

•

Adversarial Setting:
Here, the gap cannot be neglected. We impose standard boundedness assumptions on the losses (Assumption
A1
) which allow us to apply concentration inequalities (e.g., Azuma-Hoeffding). We show that the deviation term is bounded by
O(\sqrt{T})
, which is absorbed into the minimax rate without degrading the overall guarantee.

By combining the adaptive budget scaling with these concentration arguments, we establish that the algorithm’s decisions—driven by observed losses—result in a strategy that satisfies the theoretical bounds for both regimes.

)
.

For every stage
s
, the pseudo-regret capacity satisfies:

\widehat{R}_{s}\leq\widehat{R}_{s+1}<2R_{\text{hedge}},

where
R_{\text{hedge}}
denotes the realized worst-case regret that triggered the stage update.

Proof.

•

Monotonicity (
\widehat{R}_{s}\leq\widehat{R}_{s+1}
):

The inequality holds by construction. In
Algorithm

1
, the budget is updated via
\widehat{R}_{s+1}\leftarrow\widehat{R}_{s}\cdot 2^{m}
for some integer
m\geq 1
when a reset occurs, or remains constant otherwise. Thus,
\widehat{R}_{s+1}\geq\widehat{R}_{s}
.

•

Tightness (
\widehat{R}_{s+1}<2R_{\text{hedge}}
):

A stage reset is triggered only when
R_{\text{hedge}}>\widehat{R}_{s}
. The algorithm finds the smallest integer
m\in\mathbb{N}_{\geq 1}
such that the new budget covers the realized regret:

2^{m}\widehat{R}_{s}\geq R_{\text{hedge}}.

The update rule sets
\widehat{R}_{s+1}=2^{m}\widehat{R}_{s}
. Due to the minimality of
m
, the exponent
m-1
would fail to cover the regret, implying:

2^{m-1}\widehat{R}_{s}<R_{\text{hedge}}\leq 2^{m}\widehat{R}_{s}=\widehat{R}_{s+1}.

(1)

To obtain the upper bound, we rearrange the left-hand side of (
1
):

\frac{1}{2}(2^{m}\widehat{R}_{s})<R_{\text{hedge}}\implies\frac{1}{2}\widehat{R}_{s+1}<R_{\text{hedge}}\implies\widehat{R}_{s+1}<2R_{\text{hedge}}.

Combining the lower and upper bounds, we conclude that
R_{\text{hedge}}\leq\widehat{R}_{s+1}<2R_{\text{hedge}}
.

∎

C.2
Relating Expected Regret to Pseudo-Regret

Proof Strategy.

The following proof bounds the gap between expected regret and pseudo-regret under both adversarial and stochastic settings.
In the adversarial setting, we rely on the assumption of controlled adaptivity (
3.1
) to apply Bernstein’s Inequality, achieving an
O(\sqrt{T})
gap.
In the stochastic setting, we rely on the unique optimal arm assumption to achieve an
o(1)
gap.
This lemma is essential to extend existing upper bounds on pseudo-regret found in the literature to the expected regret metric used in our phase-based analysis.

Lemma C.2

(Pseudo-Regret vs. Expected Regret; Appendix Version of Lemma
3.1
)
.

Consider any time interval
I
. Let the regret difference gap be defined as:

\Delta_{\text{gap}}(I):=\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\sum_{t\in I}\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu\rangle\right]-\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in I}\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu\rangle\right].

Furthermore, let
V_{I}\geq\sup_{a}\sum_{t\in I}\operatorname{Var}(c^{t}(a)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1})
denote the upper bound on the cumulative conditional variance, and let
E_{I}
denote the cumulative adaptivity error satisfying Assumption
A1
.
Then, the gap satisfies:

•

Adversarial Case:

\Delta_{\text{gap}}(I)\leq E_{I}+3\sqrt{V_{I}\log(2A)}+2\log(2A)
.

•

Stochastic Case:

\Delta_{\text{gap}}(I)\leq o(1)
.

Proof.

We analyze the two settings separately.

Part 1: Adversarial Setting.

We aim to upper bound the quantity:

\Delta_{\text{gap}}=\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\sum_{t\in I}\left\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu\right\rangle\right]-\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in I}\left\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu\right\rangle\right].

First, using the identity
\max_{\mu}\langle v,-\mu\rangle=-\min_{a}v(a)
, we rewrite the first term:

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\sum_{t\in I}\left\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu\right\rangle\right]=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in I}\left\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}\right\rangle\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\min_{a\in[A]}\sum_{t\in I}c^{t}(a)\right].

(2)

Step 1: Lower bounding the minimum loss.

Recall
m_{t}(a):=\mathbb{E}[c^{t}(a)\mid\mathcal{F}_{t-1}]
. For each arm
a\in[A]
, we decompose the realized cost:

\sum_{t\in I}c^{t}(a)=\sum_{t\in I}m_{t}(a)+\sum_{t\in I}\left(c^{t}(a)-m_{t}(a)\right)\geq\sum_{t\in I}m_{t}(a)-\left|\sum_{t\in I}\left(c^{t}(a)-m_{t}(a)\right)\right|.

By the property of the
L_{\infty}
norm, taking the minimum over
a\in[A]
yields:

\min_{a\in[A]}\sum_{t\in I}c^{t}(a)\geq\min_{a\in[A]}\sum_{t\in I}m_{t}(a)-\left\|\sum_{t\in I}\left(c^{t}-m_{t}\right)\right\|_{\infty}.

(3)

Substituting
Equation

3
back into
Equation

2
, we obtain:

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{\mu}\sum_{t\in I}\left\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu\right\rangle\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in I}\left\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}\right\rangle\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\min_{a}\sum_{t\in I}m_{t}(a)\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{t\in I}\left(c^{t}-m_{t}\right)\right\|_{\infty}\right].

(4)

Step 2: Analyzing the Expectation of the Estimator.

We expand the first term on the RHS of
Equation

4
:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in I}\left\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in I}\left\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu\right\rangle\right]+\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in I}\left\langle c^{t},\mu\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in I}\left\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu\right\rangle\right]+\min_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\sum_{t\in I}\left\langle\mathbb{E}\left[c^{t}\right],\mu\right\rangle.

Observing that
\min_{\mu}\sum\langle\mathbb{E}[c^{t}],\mu\rangle=\min_{a}\sum\mathbb{E}[c^{t}(a)]
, we substitute this back into
Equation

4
:

\displaystyle\Delta_{\text{gap}}\leq\underbrace{\left(\min_{a}\sum_{t\in I}\mathbb{E}\left[c^{t}(a)\right]-\mathbb{E}\left[\min_{a}\sum_{t\in I}m_{t}(a)\right]\right)}_{\text{(A) Adaptivity Term}}+\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{t\in I}\left(c^{t}-m_{t}\right)\right\|_{\infty}\right]}_{\text{(B) Concentration Term}}.

(5)

Step 3: Bounding Term (A) via Controlled Adaptivity.

Let
L_{t}(a):=\inf_{h}m_{t}(a\mid h)
and
U_{t}(a):=\sup_{h}m_{t}(a\mid h)
. By Assumption
A1
,
U_{t}(a)-L_{t}(a)\leq\varepsilon_{t}
.
Note that
\mathbb{E}_{h}\left[m_{t}(a\mid h)\right]\in\left[L_{t}(a),U_{t}(a)\right]
. Thus, for every history
h
:

m_{t}(a\mid h)\geq L_{t}(a)\geq\mathbb{E}_{h}\left[m_{t}(a\mid h)\right]-\left(U_{t}(a)-L_{t}(a)\right)\geq\mathbb{E}_{h}\left[m_{t}(a\mid h)\right]-\varepsilon_{t},

where
\mathbb{E}_{h}[\cdot]
denotes expectation when
h=H_{t-1}
is drawn from its induced distribution. Summing over
t\in I
and taking the minimum over
a
:

\min_{a\in[A]}\sum_{t\in I}m_{t}(a\mid h)\geq\min_{a\in[A]}\sum_{t\in I}\mathbb{E}_{h}\left[m_{t}(a\mid h)\right]-E_{I}.

Taking expectation over history
h
(and noting
\mathbb{E}_{h}[m_{t}(a|h)]=\mathbb{E}[c^{t}(a)]
) gives:

\mathbb{E}\left[\min_{a}\sum_{t\in I}m_{t}(a)\right]\geq\min_{a}\sum_{t\in I}\mathbb{E}\left[c^{t}(a)\right]-E_{I}.

Rearranging implies Term (A)
\leq E_{I}
.

Step 4: Bounding Term (B) via Bernstein Inequality.

Since
c^{t}(a)\in[0,1]
, the centered variables
\xi_{t}(a)=c^{t}(a)-m_{t}(a)
form a martingale difference sequence with
|\xi_{t}(a)|\leq 1
and conditional variance sum bounded by
V_{I}
(or trivially
|I|
).
By Bernstein’s Inequality and a union bound over
A
actions:

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\sum_{t\in I}(c^{t}-m_{t})\right\|_{\infty}\geq x\right)\leq 2A\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2(V_{I}+x/3)}\right).

Let
x_{0}=\sqrt{2V_{I}\log(2A)+\frac{1}{9}(\log 2A)^{2}}+\frac{1}{3}\log(2A)
. Using the layer-cake representation:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{t\in I}(c^{t}-m_{t})\right\|_{\infty}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\sum_{t\in I}(c^{t}-m_{t})\right\|_{\infty}\geq x\right)dx\leq x_{0}+\int_{x_{0}}^{\infty}2A\exp\left(-\frac{x^{2}}{2(V_{I}+x/3)}\right)dx.

Evaluating the tail integral results in a bounded constant. Specifically, we obtain:

\mathbb{E}\left[\left\|\sum_{t\in I}\left(c^{t}-m_{t}\right)\right\|_{\infty}\right]\leq x_{0}+\sqrt{\frac{V_{I}}{2\log 2A}}+\frac{2}{3}.

Expanding
x_{0}
and simplifying terms (using
\sqrt{a+b}\leq\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}
):

(B)

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2V_{I}\log 2A}+\frac{\log 2A}{3}+\frac{\log 2A}{3}+\sqrt{\frac{V_{I}}{2\log 2A}}+\frac{2}{3}

\displaystyle\leq 3\sqrt{V_{I}\log(2A)}+2\log(2A).

Combining terms (A) and (B) yields the final bound.

Part 2: Stochastic Setting.

The simplex is convex and compact. Moreover, its diameter is finite. Therefore, Assumption 1 of
1
holds. According to Theorem 2 in
1
, under assumptions
S1
and
S2
(unique optimal arm), the expected fluctuations of the empirical maximizer vanish:

\mathbb{E}\left[\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\sum_{t\in I}\left\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu\right\rangle\right]-\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t\in I}\left\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu\right\rangle\right]\leq o(1).

∎

C.3
Analysis of a Normal Phase

Proof Strategy.

This proof establishes the regret bounds within a "normal" phase
k
of stage
s
—defined as a phase where the mixing parameter satisfies
\alpha^{k}<1
(i.e., we have not yet fully committed to the aggressive strategy).
The core idea is to decompose the phase-level regret into two components:

(i)

a "Hedge-driven" component bounded by the aggressive estimator;

(ii)

a "Comparator-driven" component bounded by the safety budget.

We leverage the phase exiting condition to bound the comparator component in terms of the estimated budget
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{R}_{s}]
. Crucially, we then link this budget back to the expected regret using
Lemma

4.1
(Appendix Version-
Lemma

C.1
) and bridge the gap between observed and expected regret via
Lemma

3.1
(Appendix Version-
Lemma

C.2
).

Lemma C.3

(Regret within a Normal Phase; Appendix Version of Lemma
4.2
)
.

Consider a phase
k
within stage
s
where the mixing parameter satisfies
\alpha^{k}<1
. The expected regret accumulated during this phase satisfies:

•

Against the Comparator:

\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu^{c})\leq 2^{k-1}
.

•

Against any
\mu
(Adversarial):

\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu)\leq 4\sqrt{T\log A}+O(\sqrt{T})
.

•

Against any
\mu
(Stochastic):

\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu)\leq\frac{16\log A+100}{\Delta}+o(1)
.

Proof.

For simplicity of notation, let
k
denote the current phase index
k(s)
and let
\mathcal{T}_{k}=\{\operatorname{start}_{k},\dots,\operatorname{start}_{k+1}-1\}
be the set of time steps in this phase.
Since
\widehat{R}_{s}
is fixed throughout the stage, the mixing parameter
\alpha^{k}=(2^{k}-1)/\widehat{R}_{s}
remains constant for all
t\in\mathcal{T}_{k}
.

The expected regret relative to an arbitrary strategy
\mu\in\Delta_{A}
is decomposed as follows:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle c^{t},\alpha^{k}\hat{\mu}^{t}+(1-\alpha^{k})\mu^{c}\right\rangle-\left\langle c^{t},\mu\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\alpha^{k}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu\right\rangle\right]}_{\text{(I) Aggressive Component}}+\underbrace{(1-\alpha^{k})\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle c^{t},\mu^{c}-\mu\right\rangle\right]}_{\text{(II) Conservative Component}}.

(6)

Bounding Term (II): The Safety Check.

Let
\bar{t}=\operatorname{start}_{k+1}-1
be the last time step of the phase (the exit time). The phase continues as long as the cumulative comparator regret does not violate the budget. Therefore, strictly before the exit time (up to
\bar{t}-1
), the condition holds:

\sum_{t=\operatorname{start}_{k}}^{\bar{t}-1}\langle c^{t},\mu^{c}-\mu\rangle\leq 2\widehat{R}_{s}.

Including the final time step
\bar{t}
, and using Hölder’s inequality (
\|c^{t}\|_{\infty}\leq 1,\|\mu^{c}-\mu\|_{1}\leq 2
), the total realized regret is bounded by:

\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}\langle c^{t},\mu^{c}-\mu\rangle\leq\widehat{R}_{s}+\langle c^{\bar{t}},\mu^{c}-\mu\rangle\leq 2\widehat{R}_{s}+2.

Substituting this into Term (II):

\text{(II)}\leq(1-\alpha^{k})(\mathbb{E}[2\widehat{R}_{s}]+2).

Combining and Bridging to Expected Regret.

We now bound the total regret
\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu)
. For stage
s=1
, Term (I) is bounded by
\alpha^{k}\mathbb{E}[R_{\text{hedge}}]
. For stage
s\geq 2
, if it is not the first phase of a new stage, then
Term (I) is bounded by
\alpha^{k}\mathbb{E}[R_{\text{hedge}}]
. Otherwise, Term (I) is bounded by
\alpha^{k}[\mathbb{E}[R_{\text{hedge}}]+1]
, this is because:
\mathbb{E}[\langle c^{\text{start}_{1}},\hat{\mu}^{\text{start}_{1}}-\mu\rangle]\leq 1
, then we initialize
\hat{\mu}^{\text{start}_{2}}=1/A
and continue the phases.
Applying
Lemma

C.1
(Equation
1
), we know that
\mathbb{E}[\widehat{R}_{s}]<2\mathbb{E}[R_{\text{hedge}}]
. Thus:

\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu)\leq\alpha^{k}(\mathbb{E}[R_{\text{hedge}}]+1)+(1-\alpha^{k})(4\mathbb{E}[R_{\text{hedge}}]+2)\leq 4\mathbb{E}[R_{\text{hedge}}]+2.

We now relate
\mathbb{E}[R_{\text{hedge}}]
to the pseudo-regret using
Lemma

C.2
:

1.

Adversarial Setting:

Assumption
A1
ensures the gap is
O(\sqrt{T})
. Using Proposition 1 of
42
:

\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu\rangle\right]\leq\sqrt{T\log A}

and consequently we have that

\mathbb{E}[R_{\text{hedge}}]\leq\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu\rangle\right]+O(\sqrt{T})\leq\sqrt{T\log A}+O(\sqrt{T}).

Substituting back:
\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu)\leq 4\sqrt{T\log A}+O(\sqrt{T})
.

2.

Stochastic Setting:

Under Assumptions
S1
–
S2
, the pseudo-expected gap is
o(1)
. Using the stochastic Hedge bound (Theorem 2 of
42
):

\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu\rangle\right]\leq\frac{4\log A+25}{\Delta}

and consequently we have that

\mathbb{E}[R_{\text{hedge}}]\leq\frac{4\log A+25}{\Delta}+o(1).

Substituting back:
\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu)\leq\frac{16\log A+100}{\Delta}+O(1)
.

Comparator Regret (
\mu=\mu^{c}
).

Finally, we evaluate the regret against the comparator
\mu^{c}
. In this case, the second term in
Equation

6
vanishes because
\langle\mu^{c}-\mu^{c}\rangle=0
. We are left with only the aggressive component.
Recall that the phase condition implies
\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu\rangle\leq\widehat{R}_{s}
(otherwise we would have triggered a stage reset, not just a phase exit, or the budget bounds the loss).

\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu^{c})=\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha^{k}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{k}}\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu^{c}\rangle\right]\leq\mathbb{E}\left[\alpha^{k}\widehat{R}_{s}\right].

Substituting
\alpha^{k}=\frac{2^{k-1}}{\widehat{R}_{s}}
:

\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu^{c})\leq\frac{2^{k-1}}{\widehat{R}_{s}}\cdot\widehat{R}_{s}=2^{k-1}.

This concludes the proof.
∎

C.4
Analysis of Phase Exit Conditions

Proof Strategy.

This lemma formalizes the “safety valve" mechanism: we only exit a phase when the comparator proves to be significantly suboptimal relative to the best fixed action. Intuitively, if the comparator is performing poorly, measuring regret
against
it yields a negative value (a gain).

The proof proceeds by decomposing the regret against the comparator into two parts:

(i)

the regret of the aggressive learner
\hat{\mu}^{t}
, which is bounded by the budget
\widehat{R}_{s}
;

(ii)

the performance gap of the comparator itself, which, by the exit condition, is bounded by
-2\widehat{R}_{s}
.

Combining these shows that the net regret is negative, specifically
-2^{k-1}
. This negative term is crucial for the overall analysis, as it cancels out the positive regret accumulated in normal phases, ensuring the total comparator regret remains
O(1)
.

Lemma C.4

(Comparator Gain upon Phase Exit; Appendix Version of Lemma
4.3
)
.

If the algorithm chooses to exit phase
k
(triggering a stronger Hedge mix), it implies that the mixture has significantly outperformed the comparator. Specifically:

\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu^{c})\leq-\,2^{k-1}.

Proof.

For simplicity, we denote the current phase index
k(s)
simply as
k
.
The proof relies on the specific condition that triggers the phase exit.

Step 1: The Exit Condition.

At the final time step
t=\operatorname{start}_{k+1}-1
, the algorithm exits because the cumulative comparator regret exceeds the threshold:

\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\sum_{j=\operatorname{start}_{k}}^{\operatorname{start}_{k+1}-1}\langle c^{j},\mu^{c}-\mu\rangle>2\widehat{R}_{s}.

Let
\mu^{\star}
be the maximizer of this sum. The condition implies:

\sum_{j=\operatorname{start}_{k}}^{\operatorname{start}_{k+1}-1}\langle c^{j},\mu^{c}-\mu^{\star}\rangle>2\widehat{R}_{s}.

Multiplying by
-1
reverses the inequality, yielding a bound on the comparator’s suboptimality:

\sum_{j=\operatorname{start}_{k}}^{\operatorname{start}_{k+1}-1}\langle c^{j},\mu^{\star}-\mu^{c}\rangle<-2\widehat{R}_{s}.

(7)

Step 2: Regret Decomposition.

We now analyze the expected regret accumulated during the phase. Recall that our strategy is
\mu^{t}=\alpha^{k}\hat{\mu}^{t}+(1-\alpha^{k})\mu^{c}
.

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu^{c})

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=\operatorname{start}_{k}}^{\operatorname{start}_{k+1}-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle c^{t},\mu^{t}-\mu^{c}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=\operatorname{start}_{k}}^{\operatorname{start}_{k+1}-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle c^{t},\left(\alpha^{k}\hat{\mu}^{t}+(1-\alpha^{k})\mu^{c}\right)-\mu^{c}\right\rangle\right]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=\operatorname{start}_{k}}^{\operatorname{start}_{k+1}-1}\alpha^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu^{c}\right\rangle\right].

We introduce the maximizer
\mu^{\star}
by adding and subtracting it inside the inner product:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu^{c})

\displaystyle=\alpha^{k}\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=\operatorname{start}_{k}}^{\operatorname{start}_{k+1}-1}\left(\left\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu^{\star}\right\rangle+\left\langle c^{t},\mu^{\star}-\mu^{c}\right\rangle\right)\right]

\displaystyle=\alpha^{k}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=\operatorname{start}_{k}}^{\operatorname{start}_{k+1}-1}\left\langle c^{t},\hat{\mu}^{t}-\mu^{\star}\right\rangle\right]}_{\text{(I) Aggressive Regret}}+\alpha^{k}\underbrace{\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{t=\operatorname{start}_{k}}^{\operatorname{start}_{k+1}-1}\left\langle c^{t},\mu^{\star}-\mu^{c}\right\rangle\right]}_{\text{(II) Comparator Gap}}.

Step 3: Final Bound.

We bound the two terms:

•

Term (I):
By definition, the regret of the aggressive learner
\hat{\mu}^{t}
is bounded by the stage budget
\widehat{R}_{s}
(otherwise the
stage
would have reset, not just the phase). Thus,
\text{(I)}\leq\widehat{R}_{s}
.

•

Term (II):
Using the exit condition derived in
Equation

7
, we have
\text{(II)}<-2\widehat{R}_{s}
.

Substituting these back:

\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu^{c})\leq\alpha^{k}\left(\widehat{R}_{s}-2\widehat{R}_{s}\right)=-\alpha^{k}\widehat{R}_{s}.

Finally, utilizing the definition of the mixing rate
\alpha^{k}=\frac{2^{k-1}}{\widehat{R}_{s}}
:

\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu^{c})\leq-\frac{2^{k-1}}{\widehat{R}_{s}}\cdot\widehat{R}_{s}=-2^{k-1}.

This completes the proof.
∎

C.5
Per-Stage Regret Analysis

Proof Strategy.

This proof establishes the regret bounds within a single stage
s
. The core idea is to decompose the stage-level regret based on whether the algorithm fully transitions to the aggressive strategy. We distinguish two cases:

(i)

Conservative Regime (
\alpha^{k(s)}<1
):
The stage concludes while still mixing with the comparator. In this case, the regret is bounded primarily by the phase decomposition logic.

(ii)

Saturation Regime (
\alpha^{k(s)}=1
):
The mixing weight saturates, and the algorithm plays pure Hedge in the final phase. Here, the total regret is the sum of the regret accumulated during the mixing phases plus the standard Hedge regret in the final phase.

We leverage
Lemma

C.1
to bound the number of phases and
Lemma

C.4
(Phase Exiting Conditions) to handle the telescoping sums of the mixing weights.

Lemma C.5

(Per-Stage Regret Bounds)
.

For any fixed stage
s
:

•

Comparator Regret:

\mathcal{R}^{s}(\mu^{c})=O(1)
in both adversarial and stochastic settings.

•

Adversarial Worst-Case:

\max_{\mu}\mathcal{R}^{s}(\mu)=O(\sqrt{T\log A}\log T)
.

•

Stochastic Worst-Case:

\max_{\mu}\mathcal{R}^{s}(\mu)=O(\frac{\log A}{\Delta}\log T)
.

Proof.

Let
K(s)
(abbreviated as
K
) be the total number of phases in stage
s
. The regret accumulated in stage
s
against any strategy
\mu\in\Delta_{A}
is the sum of regrets across all
K
phases. Using the definition of our strategy
\mu^{t}=\alpha^{k}\hat{\mu}^{t}+(1-\alpha^{k})\mu^{c}
, we write:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{s}(\mu)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\sum_{t=\operatorname{start}_{k}}^{\operatorname{start}_{k+1}-1}\mathbb{E}\left[\left\langle c^{t},\alpha^{k}\hat{\mu}^{t}+(1-\alpha^{k})\mu^{c}-\mu\right\rangle\right].

We analyze the bounds by considering two mutually exclusive cases regarding the mixing coefficient
\alpha^{k}
.

Case 1: Partial Mixing (
\alpha^{k}<1
for all phases
k\leq K
).

In this scenario, the algorithm always mixes the Hedge strategy with the comparator.
First, we upper bound the number of phases
K
. Since
\alpha^{K}<1
, by definition
\frac{2^{K-1}}{\widehat{R}_{s}}<1
, which implies
2^{K-1}<\widehat{R}_{s}
.
Using
Lemma

4.1
, we know
\widehat{R}_{s}<2R_{\text{hedge}}
. Since
R_{\text{hedge}}\leq T
(trivial linear regret bound), we have:

K<1+\log_{2}\widehat{R}_{s}<2+\log_{2}R_{\text{hedge}}\leq 2+\log_{2}T.

Thus, the number of phases is logarithmic in
T
.

Subcase 1.1: Worst-Case Regret (
\max_{\mu}\mathcal{R}^{s}(\mu)
).

We sum the regret bounds of each "Normal Phase" (since phases
1\dots K
were entered normally).

•

Adversarial:
By
Lemma

C.3
, each phase contributes at most
O(\sqrt{T\log A})
. Summing over
K=O(\log T)
phases:

\max_{\mu}\mathcal{R}^{s}(\mu)\leq(2+\log_{2}T)\cdot O(\sqrt{T\log A})=O(\sqrt{T\log A}\log T).

•

Stochastic:
By
Lemma

C.3
, each phase contributes
O(\frac{\log A}{\Delta})
. Summing over
K
:

\max_{\mu}\mathcal{R}^{s}(\mu)\leq(2+\log_{2}T)\cdot O\left(\frac{\log A}{\Delta}\right)=O\left(\frac{\log A}{\Delta}\log T\right).

Subcase 1.2: Comparator Regret (
\mathcal{R}^{s}(\mu^{c})
).

We exploit the phase exit condition. For all phases
k<K
, the algorithm exited because the comparator was performing poorly, yielding a gain
\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu^{c})\leq-2^{k-1}
(
Lemma

4.3
). The final phase
K
has not been completed (or is the current one), and its regret is bounded by the budget definition
\alpha^{K}\widehat{R}_{s}=2^{K-1}
.
Summing these up:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{s}(\mu^{c})

\displaystyle=\underbrace{\mathcal{R}^{K}(\mu^{c})}_{\text{Current Phase}}+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\underbrace{\mathcal{R}^{k}(\mu^{c})}_{\text{Exited Phases}}\leq 2^{K-1}+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}\left(-2^{k-1}\right)=2^{K-1}-(2^{K-1}-1)=1.

Case 2: Full Hedge (
\alpha^{K}=1
).

Here, the algorithm reaches the final phase
K
where it plays the Hedge strategy purely (
\alpha^{K}=1
).
This implies the budget constraint was saturated:
1=\min\{1,\frac{2^{K-1}}{\widehat{R}_{s}}\}\implies\widehat{R}_{s}\leq 2^{K-1}
.

Subcase 2.1: Worst-Case Regret (
\max_{\mu}\mathcal{R}^{s}(\mu)
).

The regret is the sum of the “Prefix" phases (
1
to
K-1
) and the “Final" phase
K
.
The prefix phases contribute
O(\log T\times\text{Rate})
exactly as in Case 1.
The final phase is a standard Hedge instance initialized uniformly. By
42
:

•

Adversarial:
\leq\sqrt{T\log A}
.

•

Stochastic:
\leq O(\frac{\log A}{\Delta})
.

Adding the prefix (logarithmic factor) dominates the final phase term, preserving the overall
O(\log T)
bounds stated in the Lemma.

Subcase 2.2: Comparator Regret (
\mathcal{R}^{s}(\mu^{c})
).

The exited phases
k<K
still contribute negative regret
\leq-2^{k-1}
.
For the final phase
K
, since we play Hedge (
\alpha^{K}=1
), the regret is bounded by the stage budget
\widehat{R}_{s}
(by design of the doubling trick, the stage ends if regret exceeds this).
Using
\widehat{R}_{s}\leq 2^{K-1}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{R}^{s}(\mu^{c})\leq\widehat{R}_{s}+\sum_{k=1}^{K-1}(-2^{k-1})\leq 2^{K-1}-(2^{K-1}-1)=1.

In both cases and settings, the comparator regret is
O(1)
, and the worst-case regret scales with
\log T
.
∎

C.6
Analysis of
Compass-Hedge
: Proof of Main Theorem

Proof Strategy.

The following proof establishes the upper bounds for the (worst-case) pseudo-regret and comparator regret incurred over
T
rounds in both stochastic and adversarial environments.
Recall that
Algorithm

1
partitions the horizon
T
into
S
stages. The analysis proceeds in two steps:

(i)

we first bound the total number of stages
S
;

(ii)

we then utilize the per-stage regret bounds derived in the auxiliary lemmas.

Summing these bounds over all stages yields the final regret guarantees.

Theorem C.1

(Regret Guarantees (Appendix Version of Theorem
4.1
))
.

Let
\mu^{c}\in\Delta_{A}
be an arbitrary comparator policy. For any horizon
T
,
Algorithm

1
guarantees the following bounds:

•

Adversarial Setting.

Under Assumption
A1
:

\mathcal{R}(\mu^{c})\leq O(\log T),\quad\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{pseudo}}(T)\leq O\!\left(\sqrt{T\log A}\,\log^{2}T\right).

•

Stochastic Setting.

Under Assumptions
S1
–
S2
:

\mathcal{R}(\mu^{c})\leq O(\log T),\quad\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{pseudo}}(T)\leq O\!\left(\frac{\log A\,\log^{2}T}{\Delta}\right).

Proof.

Algorithm

1
divides the time horizon
T
into
S
stages.
We proceed by first bounding the number of stages and then summing the regret across them.

Step 1: Bounding the number of stages (
S
).

The stage index
s
increments only when the cumulative regret condition is violated:

\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\sum_{j=\text{start}}^{t}\langle c^{j},\hat{\mu}^{j}-\mu\rangle>\hat{R}_{s}.

Since losses are bounded
c^{t}(a)\in[0,1]
, the cumulative regret cannot exceed the total horizon
T
. Thus, we trivially have:

\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\sum_{j=\text{start}}^{t}\langle c^{j},\hat{\mu}^{j}-\mu\rangle\leq T.

By construction, the pseudo-regret budget
\hat{R}_{s}
doubles after each stage reset (specifically
\hat{R}_{s+1}\geq 2\hat{R}_{s}
). Consequently, the maximum number of stages
S
is bounded by the number of times
\hat{R}_{1}
can be doubled before exceeding
T
.
Let
S_{max}
be the smallest integer such that
2^{S_{max}-1}\geq T
. It follows that:

S\leq\lceil\log_{2}T\rceil+1=O(\log T).

Step 2: Summing the regret.

We now aggregate the regret accumulated in each stage. Invoking
Lemma

4.4
, the regret bounds within any single stage
s
are:

•

In the Adversarial Setting:
\mathcal{R}^{s}(\mu^{c})\leq O(1)\quad\text{and}\quad\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\mathcal{R}^{s}(\mu)\leq O(\sqrt{T\log A}\log T).

•

In the Stochastic Setting:
\mathcal{R}^{s}(\mu^{c})\leq O(1)\quad\text{and}\quad\max_{\mu\in\Delta_{A}}\mathcal{R}^{s}(\mu)\leq O\left(\frac{\log A\cdot\log T}{\Delta}\right).

Since there are at most
S=O(\log T)
stages, the total regret over
T
rounds is obtained by summing these per-stage bounds.
For the comparator regret in both settings:

\mathcal{R}(\mu^{c})=\sum_{s=1}^{S}\mathcal{R}^{s}(\mu^{c})\leq S\cdot O(1)=O(\log T).

For the worst-case pseudo-regret in the adversarial setting:

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{pseudo}}(T)\leq\sum_{s=1}^{S}O(\sqrt{T\log A}\log T)=O(\sqrt{T\log A}\log^{2}T).

Similarly, for the stochastic setting:

\mathcal{R}_{\mathrm{pseudo}}(T)\leq\sum_{s=1}^{S}O\left(\frac{\log A\cdot\log T}{\Delta}\right)=O\left(\frac{\log A\cdot\log^{2}T}{\Delta}\right).

This concludes the proof.
∎

Extended Experimental Evaluation

In this section, we provide a detailed breakdown of the experimental setup, data preprocessing steps, and additional qualitative analyses that substantiate the findings presented in Section

. Our goal is to ensure full reproducibility and to offer deeper insights into the algorithm’s behavior under distinct market regimes.

D.1
Dataset and Preprocessing

Data Source and Universe.

We utilize daily Adjusted Closing Prices for all constituents of the S&P 500 index, sourced from the Center for Research in Security Prices (CRSP) via WRDS. The dataset spans the 10-year period from
January 1, 2015, to December 31, 2024
, totaling
T=2,516
trading days.
This universe was chosen because it represents a highly liquid, efficiency-driven market that nevertheless exhibits heavy tails, non-stationarity, and sudden regime shifts—an ideal testing ground for robust online learning.

Handling Survivorship Bias (The "Dead Arm" Problem).

A critical aspect of realistic financial simulation is handling assets that are delisted during the simulation period. In a bandit setting, a delisted stock acts as an arm that effectively "dies" or yields worst-case returns.
To rigorously test the adversarial robustness of
Compass-Hedge
, we
do not
exclude delisted stocks. Instead, if a stock
i
is delisted at time
t_{dead}
, for all
t>t_{dead}
we set its return
r_{t,i}=-100\%
(effectively a total loss). This simulates a "trap" in the environment where an arm suddenly turns catastrophic.

Mapping Returns to Losses.

Standard Hedge-type algorithms require losses bounded in
[0,1]
. We employ a dynamic, robust sigmoidal transformation to map returns to losses while preserving ordinal rankings.
Let
r_{t,i}
be the return of asset
i
at day
t
. We compute the cross-sectional median
m_{t}
and the interquartile range
\text{IQR}_{t}
. The loss
\ell_{t,i}
is defined as:

\ell_{t,i}=\frac{1}{1+\exp\left(-\frac{r_{t,i}-m_{t}}{\gamma\cdot\text{IQR}_{t}}\right)}

where
\gamma
is a scaling factor (set to
\gamma=1
).

•

Intuition:
This transformation centers market "noise" around
0.5
. Assets outperforming map to losses
<0.5
, while underperformers map to
>0.5
.

•

Robustness:
Using the median and IQR prevents extreme outliers from collapsing gradient information during high-volatility crashes.

N=500
