What induces plane structures in complete graph drawings?
Alexandra Weinberger, Ji Zeng

Abstract
This paper considers the task of connecting points on a piece of paper by drawing a curve between each pair of them. Under mild assumptions, we prove that many pairwise disjoint curves are unavoidable if either of the following rules is obeyed: any two adjacent curves do not cross, or any two non-adjacent curves cross at most once. Here, two curves are called adjacent if they share an endpoint. On the other hand, we demonstrate how to draw all curves such that any two adjacent curves cross exactly once, any two non-adjacent curves cross at least once and at most twice, and thus no two curves are disjoint. Furthermore, we analyze the emergence of disjoint curves without these mild assumptions, and characterize the plane structures in complete graph drawings guaranteed by each of the rules above.

Introduction

In the plane, a
complete graph drawing
is a collection of points and curves connecting each pair of these points. The points are called vertices and the curves are called edges of this drawing. More precisely, an edge is the image of a piecewise smooth map from a closed interval on the real line to the plane which starts and ends at its corresponding pair of vertices. In this paper, we make
mild assumptions
for complete graph drawings: we assume no edge contains vertices other than its endpoints; we assume no edge self-intersects, that is, its defining map must be injective; we assume edges cross properly whenever they intersect, in particular, edges do not merely touch. It is easy to imagine that a complete graph drawing can be tangled like a mass of spaghetti noodles. Our main result asserts that the breaking point of “tangledness” in complete graph drawings is the logical disjunction of the two rules below. Here, two edges are called adjacent if they share an endpoint.

\displaystyle\text{Adjacent-simple: any two adjacent edges do not cross;}

\displaystyle\text{Separate-simple: any two non-adjacent edges cross at most once.}

Theorem 1
.

For any positive integer
m
, there exists
n
such that every complete graph drawing on
n
vertices that is either adjacent-simple or separate-simple must contain
m
pairwise disjoint edges. On the other hand, there exist complete graph drawings on arbitrarily many vertices in which any two adjacent edges cross exactly once, and any two non-adjacent edges cross at least once and at most twice.

In general, a
graph
in the abstract sense consists of a vertex set and an edge set, where each edge is a vertex pair (unordered). A
drawing
of a graph is a representation of its vertices as distinct points in the plane, and its edges as curves connecting the corresponding pair of vertices. We make the same mild assumptions for all drawings of graphs. A drawing is said to be
plane
if it has no crossings. Planarity has been a main theme in the development of graph theory featured by famous theorems such as Euler’s formula and the four color theorem. A drawing is said to be
simple
if it is adjacent-simple and separate-simple simultaneously. Rafla
[
31
]
conjectured that every simple complete graph drawing on at least three vertices must contain a plane Hamiltonian cycle, that is, a cycle that visits every vertex exactly once. Motivated by this conjecture or not, unavoidable plane structures in simple drawings are extensively studied, see our incomplete list

We shall state the two cases of Theorem

as theorems on their own. We call a graph

a

of graph drawings if there exists an integer

such that any drawing in

of at least

is called a sub-drawing of another drawing

if the vertex set and edge set of

are subsets of those of

respectively. A

is a connected graph that contains exactly one cycle which is a triangle, and all other vertices only form edges with two fixed vertices on this triangle. A

is a connected graph without cycles that contains a central path, and all other vertices only form edges with vertices on this path. An

in a graph is a vertex that does not form any edges.

Theorem 2
.

The plane structures guaranteed in adjacent-simple complete graph drawings are exactly squids with extra isolated vertices, and unions of disjoint caterpillars.

Theorem 3
.

The plane structures guaranteed in separate-simple complete graph drawings are exactly unions of disjoint edges and isolated vertices.

One may ask to what extent our main result still holds if the mild assumptions are dropped. For example, when we connect points on a piece of paper, the curves drawn by a pen could touch or run through any points, then what would be the earliest cause of disjointness in this scenario? To model an edge in a drawing without mild assumptions, we use the image
\text{Im}(f)
of a function

from the unit interval
[0,1]
to the plane
\mathbb{R}^{2}
. We also define self-intersection of

as
\Delta(f)=\{p:p=f(x)=f(y)\penalty\ \text{for $x\neq y$}\}
. A family of functions

is called

if all the pre-images

are unions of finitely many sub-intervals of

. Although mathematically two curves can intersect infinitely many times without coincide along any segment, it is not physically possible to draw non-stroke-like curves on a piece of paper using a pen. We can conclude the following corollary of Theorem

be distinct points in the plane,

(A)

\forall

Proposition 5

We shall prove Theorem

of a drawing (not necessarily plane) refers to a connected component of the complement of this drawing inside the plane.

Lemma 8
.

In any separate-simple drawing of a triangle
abc
union an edge

, such that the vertices

are in the same cell of

, the edge

does not cross one of

Proof.

Two cells of the triangle
abc
is said to be adjacent if their boundaries coincide along a segment of an edge. We can assign black and white colors to the cells of
abc
such that adjacent cells have different colors. Such a “chessboard” coloring can be argued with standard graph theory knowledge, see e.g. Section 4.6 of
[
11
]
(especially Exercise 24), or we may simply apply Proposition 2 in
[

]
. Now, as we travel along the trajectory of the edge

, the cell color of our location changes exactly when we encounter a crossing between

and edges of

are in the same cell of

, the number of such crossings must be even. Hence

cannot cross all three of

by separate-simplicity. We remark that multiple edges are allowed to cross at the same point, if
de
passes through a crossing point between

Proof of Theorem

Note that a union of disjoint edges is also a union of disjoint caterpillars. The first part of this theorem follows from combining Theorem

For the second part of this theorem, we describe a construction for

. Fix a circle with radius one centered at a point

Proof of Corollary
4

By abuse of notation, we shall call each
p_{i}
a vertex and each

(or its image) an edge. We shall perform local modifications to the given configuration of vertices and edges, and obtain a drawing with mild assumptions that is either adjacent-simple or separate-simple. We make sure not to introduce any new pair of disjoint edges, so that Theorem

Condition (A) means any two adjacent edges have exactly one intersection counted via pre-images. In particular, an edge

First, if there is a touching point

Next, we remove coincidences between edges along segments. For any maximal segment

Then, we resolve crossing points that are self-intersections by a standard redraw technique, see Figure

Although our mild assumption does not forbid multiple segments crossing at the same point, we can assume such a scenario does not happen after slight perturbations, which makes it more intuitive to resolve self-crossings. At the end, we obtain an adjacent-simple complete graph drawing satisfying the mild assumptions as wanted.

Condition (S) means any two non-adjacent edges have at most one intersection counted via pre-images, and that no such intersection is a touching point. Under this condition, an edge

Since Condition (S) forbids non-adjacent edges to touch, the removal of touchings will not make two non-adjacent edges cross more than once. At the end, we obtain a separate-simple complete graph drawing satisfying the mild assumptions as wanted.

Finally, we verify the existence of non-sandwiched segments. Let

5
Discussion

It is natural to ask for a characterization of the plane structures guaranteed in simple complete graph drawings. To address this question, we can follow an approach similar to that of Theorem
2
. We call a simple complete graph drawing
convex
if its vertices can be ordered as
v_{1},v_{2},\dots,v_{n}
such that
v_{i},v_{j},v_{k},v_{\ell}
induce exactly the crossing
v_{i}v_{k}
with
v_{j}v_{\ell}
for all
i<j<k<\ell
, and we call it
twisted
if its vertices can be ordered such that
v_{i},v_{j},v_{k},v_{\ell}
induce exactly the crossing
v_{i}v_{\ell}
with
v_{j}v_{k}
for all
i<j<k<\ell
. Pach, Solymosi, and Tóth
[
24
]
have shown every sufficiently large simple complete graph drawing contains a sub-drawing on arbitrarily many vertices that is either convex or twisted. On the other hand, it is known both convex drawings and twisted drawings exist and can be arbitrarily large
[
20
]
. Therefore, an abstract graph
G
is a plane structure guaranteed in simple complete graph drawings if and only if
G
is
stack
and
queue
. Here,
G
being
stack
(
queue
respectively) means its vertices can be ordered such that
v_{i}v_{k}
and
v_{j}v_{\ell}
(
v_{i}v_{\ell}
and
v_{j}v_{k}
respectively) are not edges of
G
simultaneously for all
i<j<k<\ell
. The names of these properties come from their relationships with the data structures stack and queue. We refer our readers to
[
12
,
23
,
30
]
and the references therein for information on related studies. It is known that stack graphs have nice characterizations
[
40
]
and can be recognized in linear time, but the task of recognizing queue graphs is NP-complete
[
21
]
. It seems to be an open problem whether graphs that are both stack and queue can be recognized efficiently, but that is beyond the scope of this paper.

In Corollary
4
, we cannot further relax Condition (A) to allow adjacent edges to touch, nor relax Condition (S) to allow non-adjacent edges to touch. Otherwise, we pick vertices
p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{n}
on a circle, and draw internally disjoint curves
h_{ij}
(
j\neq i
) from
p_{i}
to the center, then define edge
c_{ij}
as the concatenation of
h_{ij}
and
h_{ji}
. In such way, any two edges intersect exactly once in their interiors. If we choose the order for
h_{ij}
to emanate from each vertex
p_{i}
to be the same order as
p_{i}
sees other vertices, then any two adjacent edges touch. If we choose the reverse order for each
p_{i}
, then any two adjacent edges cross; in fact, this is a degenerate case of the construction in the proof of Theorem
1
by choosing
r_{1}=\dots=r_{d}=0
. We cannot further relax the equation in Condition (A) to
|\text{Im}(c_{ij})\cap\text{Im}(c_{k\ell})|=1
, because we need to forbid edges from passing through their own endpoints. Otherwise, we can make thin loops emanating from vertices like open-ended half-edges and include them as edge parts, which gives us more flexibility to create crossings, see Figure
14
for a construction in which adjacent edges do not cross or touch but no two edges are disjoint. This construction can be extended to arbitrarily many vertices. In contrast, perhaps interestingly, edges can pass through endpoints in Condition (S) for Corollary
4
.

After all, Corollary
4
may not capture the exact cause of disjoint curves in the process of connecting points on paper. For example, we did not study the scenario that forbids non-adjacent curves to touch but allows adjacent curves to touch. In fact, we did not consider whether a rule like “at least 64% pairs of non-adjacent edges cross less than twice” would force a pair of disjoint edges in complete graph drawings. We leave these obscure and interesting questions drifting in this river called time.
