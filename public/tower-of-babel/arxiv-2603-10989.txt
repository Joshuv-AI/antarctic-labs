Causal Survival Analysis in Platform Trials with Non-Concurrent Controls
Antonio D’Alessandro, Samrachana Adhikari, and Michele Santacatterina Division of Biostatistics, Department of Population Health, New York University School of Medicine, New York, NY, 10016

Abstract
Platform trials allow treatment arms to enter and exit over time while maintaining a shared control arm, yielding concurrent and non-concurrent controls (NCC). Pooling NCC is often motivated as a strategy to improve statistical efficiency, but it is unclear which estimand is targeted, what assumptions justify identification and estimation, and when precision gains are achievable; these questions are further complicated by time-to-event/survival data. Motivated by the Adaptive COVID-19 Treatment Trial (ACTT) platform trial with time to recovery as the primary endpoint, we develop an estimand-first causal survival framework targeting the treatment-specific counterfactual survival curve in the concurrent population and the corresponding functionals including the concurrent restricted mean survival time (RMST). We give nonparametric identification results and formalize conditions that justify pooling using NCC. We study covariate-adjusted outcome-regression (OR) and doubly robust (DR) estimators for the concurrent RMST, comparing concurrent-only versions to pooled-control versions. Pooling improves precision for OR estimators only when the pooling assumption holds and parametric hazard models are correctly specified; otherwise, pooling can induce bias. Moreover, in certain settings, pooling NCC yields no efficiency gain for the DR estimator. Overall, the most robust route to improve precision is to target concurrent causal survival estimands and use a covariate-adjusted DR estimation that uses only concurrent controls. An ACTT application corroborates these results.



Keywords:
Causal survival estimands; Doubly robust estimation; Non-concurrent controls; Platform trials; Restricted mean survival time

1
Introduction

A platform trial is an adaptive multi-arm experimental design that allows for the simultaneous evaluation of multiple treatments for a single disease
(
1
)
. Traditional randomized controlled trials (RCTs) feature parallel arms which remain fixed by design through the duration of the experiment, while platform designs allow different treatment arms to enter and exit at distinct times, while also maintaining a shared control arm through the end of the study. This shared control arm is composed of
concurrent controls
, subjects who entered the trial concurrent with the treatment of interest and could have been randomized to the treatment of interest or control arm, and
non-concurrent controls
(NCC), subjects who entered the trial when the treatment of interest was unavailable and had zero probability of being assigned to the treatment of interest. This design has seen widespread use during the COVID-19 pandemic
(
13
)
. For example, the Adaptive COVID-19 Treatment Trial (ACTT)
(
20
)
evaluated multiple regimens across stages, with remdesivir serving as a shared arm across ACTT-1 and ACTT-2.

Time-to-event endpoints are common in platform trials and require methods that accommodate censoring. Although analyses often rely on the Cox proportional hazards model
(
9
)
, the hazard ratio targets an instantaneous risk-set contrast that may not correspond to a clinically meaningful causal effect on survival time
(
15
;
36
)
. This motivates complementary estimands such as the restricted mean survival time (RMST), which summarizes average survival up to a clinically relevant horizon and yields interpretable contrasts between treatment arms
(
32
)
.

While platform trials like the ACTT offer increased flexibility, they also create an apparent opportunity to increase precision by pooling earlier-stage control patients with concurrent controls for a given treatment comparison. However, it is not generally clear when such pooling is valid, what causal quantity is targeted, or how pooling should be implemented for time-to-event endpoints in the presence of censoring. This raises three
practical
questions:
1) how should causal survival estimands be defined when controls are partly nonconcurrent; 2) under what assumptions are these estimands identifiable and estimable; and 3) when, if at all, does incorporating NCC improve precision for the concurrent treatment comparison?
Answering these questions requires thinking carefully about how calendar time can enter the data-generating process in a platform trial. When the distribution of baseline covariates change over the enrollment period, naïvely pooling NCC and concurrent controls can bias estimated treatment effects; this is commonly referred to as “time drift” or “temporal drift”. Proposed solutions to deal with this bias include test-then-pool procedures
(
37
)
, regression adjustment
(
35
)
, propensity weighting
(
5
)
, and robust borrowing approaches
(
19
;
38
)
.

While these approaches enable the use of NCC, the causal target is often implicit and the estimand can be tied closely to a chosen modeling strategy (e.g., a particular regression coefficient or hazard ratio) rather than stated upfront as a clinically meaningful causal contrast and research question. This emphasis on specifying the scientific question first is central to the estimand-first framework advocated by the FDA
(
10
)
and the ICH E9(R1) guidance
(
18
)
. Recent reviews note that, despite substantial methodological development, there remains a need for a unified estimand-first framework for platform trials that clearly links causal questions, identifying assumptions, and estimation strategies, including for time-to-event endpoints
(
8
;
23
)
.

Motivated by the ACTT platform trial with time to recovery as the primary endpoint, this paper addresses the practical questions raised above using an estimand-first framework. We give nonparametric identification results, formalize conditions for pooling NCC, and study covariate-adjusted outcome-regression and doubly robust estimators. Pooling NCC improves precision for outcome-regression estimators only under valid pooling assumptions and correct parametric hazard specification; otherwise it can induce bias. When treatment availability is deterministic in entry time, pooling NCC gives no efficiency gain for the doubly robust estimator. Overall, the most robust strategy is to target concurrent estimands and use covariate-adjusted doubly robust estimation with concurrent controls only. An ACTT application supports these findings.

Additional Related Work

Platform trials extend traditional parallel-arm designs through features such as shared control arms and adaptive treatment entry and removal
(
2
;
6
)
. Methodological development continues from both Bayesian and frequentist perspectives
(
25
;
11
)
, with recent reviews documenting the diversity of design characteristics and analytic strategies
(
24
)
. A central statistical question concerns how best to leverage shared or pooled control information. Borrowing of control data, including from external sources, has a long history
(
37
)
, and its potential efficiency gains in platform settings have been studied in
17
and
34
; see
30
for a review.

For time-to-event endpoints, both frequentist and Bayesian approaches exploit shared controls. Frequentist proposals build on multi-arm multi-stage survival designs using standard time-to-event methods under proportional hazards working models
(
33
)
. Bayesian approaches include adaptive platform designs such as MIDAS
(
40
)
and commensurate or MAP-style borrowing strategies for censored outcomes
(
31
)
. Applications include REMAP-CAP
(
28
)
and the HEALEY ALS Platform Trial
(
14
)
. Across these strands, inference is typically framed in terms of model-based parameters (e.g., hazard ratios or borrowing parameters), underscoring the importance of clearly specifying the causal estimand. We focus on estimating causal survival contrasts in platform trials using the restricted mean survival time (RMST), which offers interpretational advantages over hazard ratios
(
12
)
; see
22
for applied illustrations. Our estimators rely on semiparametric efficiency theory and double robustness
(
21
;
16
)
, building on weighted estimating-equation approaches originally developed in
29
.

Time-to-event quantities

In this section we introduce statistical quantities used in the analysis of time-to-event outcomes. Using the previously established notation, fix
\tilde{a}\in\{1,\ldots,\mathcal{A}\}

3.2
Causal model and associated directed acyclic graph

To formalize entry time, covariates, treatment assignment, and treatment availability in a platform trial, we adopt the following structural causal model
(
26
)
,

\displaystyle E=f_{E}(U_{E}),\quad W=f_{W}(E,U_{W}),\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad\quad

(1)

\displaystyle\quad V=(V_{0},\ldots V_{\mathcal{A}}),\quad V_{a}=f_{V_{a}}(E,U_{V_{a}})\quad a\in\{0,\ldots,\mathcal{A}\},\quad

\displaystyle A=f_{A}(W,V,U_{A}),\quad T=f_{T}(A,W,E,U_{T}),\quad C=f_{C}(A,W,E,U_{C}).

Fix
\tilde{a}\in{1,\ldots,\mathcal{A}}
and consider the comparison of treatment
\tilde{a}
versus shared control (
A=0
) within the concurrent population
{V_{\tilde{a}}=1}
, assuming the control arm is always available (
V_{0}\equiv 1
).
The model allows variables to depend on entry time
E
, accommodating temporal drift. Treatment assignment
A
may depend on baseline covariates
W
(e.g., stratified randomization) but depends on
E
only through treatment availability
V
, so subjects are randomized only among arms available at entry, with fixed randomization probabilities over time.
Event and censoring times
(T,C)
depend on realized treatment
A
and entry time
E
, but not directly on availability
V
, and no direct causal relationship between
T
and
C
is imposed. Availability

depends on calendar time but not on individual covariates
W
. Baseline covariates
W
may vary with entry time, while

does not depend on

, allowing population composition to evolve over time.
Functional forms for
f_{E},f_{W},f_{T},
and

are unrestricted, whereas treatment assignment and availability are known by design and may be deterministic or stochastic in entry time. Exogenous variables
U_{E},U_{W},U_{T},U_{V_{a}},U_{C}
represent unmeasured factors, and

encodes the randomization mechanism.

Definition and identification of the concurrent treatment-specific counterfactual survival curve

Throughout this paper, we employ counterfactual notation
(
27
)
. For each

a\in\{0,\tilde{a}\}
, let
T(a,K)
denote the event time that would be observed under the joint intervention
setting treatment assignment to
A=a
and eliminating censoring by setting the censoring time to
C=K

with probability one. For notational simplicity, we write
T(a)\equiv T(a,K)
throughout. For a cutoff denoted by
\tau\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
the target estimand of interest is the area under the treatment specific survival curve among those patients in the concurrent population:

We focus on the comparison of the active treatment
\tilde{a}
versus control within the

concurrent population
for
\tilde{a}
, i.e., among those with
V_{\tilde{a}}=1
.

Definition 4.1

(Concurrent treatment-specific counterfactual survival curve)
.

For
a\in\{0,\tilde{a}\}
and
t\in\{0,1,\ldots,K\}
, the concurrent treatment-specific
counterfactual survival curve is

\theta(a,t)=S_{a}^{\ast}(t):=\mathsf{P}\{T(a)>t\mid V_{\tilde{a}}=1\}.

The curve
\theta(a,t)
is defined in terms of counterfactual outcomes
T(a)
and is therefore not, in general,
a functional of the observed-data distribution without additional assumptions. In particular, for any given
subject we observe at most one of the potential event times
\{T(0),T(\tilde{a})\}
, so the joint distribution of
counterfactual outcomes is not identified from the observed data alone. Nonparametric identification provides
conditions under which
S_{a}^{\ast}(t)
can nonetheless be expressed uniquely as a functional of the observed-data
distribution
\mathsf{P}
, without imposing parametric restrictions on outcome or censoring models

(
26
)
. Such an observed-data representation motivates estimators and frequentist inference.
To discuss identification of
\theta(a,t)
, we introduce the following assumptions:

A1
Exchangeability.

For each
a\in\{0,\tilde{a}\}
,

T(a)\perp A\ \big|\ (W,E,V_{\tilde{a}}=1).

Assumption
A1
states that within the concurrent population and conditional on
(W,E)
, treatment is as-if randomized so there is no residual confounding of
A
and
T(a)
; this is fundamentally untestable because it involves counterfactual outcomes, however we expect it to hold by design under proper randomization.

A2
Consistency.

If
A=a
, then the latent event time satisfies
T=T(a)
for
a\in\{0,\tilde{a}\}
.

Assumption
A2
states that for subjects who in fact receive arm
a
, the observed event time equals the corresponding potential event time
T(a)
; this is a definitional/measurement assumption (not empirically testable in the counterfactual sense) and can fail only via treatment-version ambiguity or interference.

A3
Random censoring.

For each
a\in\{0,\tilde{a}\}
,

T(a)\perp C\ \big|\ (A,E,W,V_{\tilde{a}}=1).

Given
(A,E,W)
in the concurrent population, censoring carries no additional information about
T(a)
beyond these variables; this is untestable without additional structure.

A4
Positivity of shared control assignment.

For all
(e,w)
such that
\mathsf{P}(E=e,W=w)>0
,

\mathsf{P}(A=0\mid E=e,W=w)>0.

Every
(E,W)
stratum that occurs in the data has nonzero probability of being randomized to control, preventing empty control strata; this is checkable from the design and the realized data (violations appear as zero counts/zero estimated probabilities). This assumption should hold by design.
Note that, because the shared control arm is available throughout the platform trial (
V_{0}\equiv 1
), the positivity condition for control assignment does not depend on treatment availability; consequently, we state Assumption
A4
unconditionally in
(E,W)
(and it therefore holds, in particular, within the concurrent population
\{V_{\tilde{a}}=1\}
).

A5
Probability of censoring.

For all
t\in\{0,\ldots,K-1\}
,

\mathsf{P}(C=t\mid C\geq t,\ A=a,\ E=e,\ W=w,\ V_{\tilde{a}}=1)<1\quad\text{for each }a\in\{0,\tilde{a}\}.

Among those still under follow-up, censoring is not deterministic at any time point within any
(A,E,W)
stratum in the concurrent population; this assumption can be checked empirically (violations appear as
\mathsf{P}(C=t\mid C\geq t,\cdot)=1
in some strata/time points).

A6
Positivity among concurrent units.

For all
(e,w)
such that
\mathsf{P}(E=e,W=w,V_{\tilde{a}}=1)>0
,

\mathsf{P}(A=\tilde{a}\mid E=e,W=w,V_{\tilde{a}}=1)>0.

Within the concurrent population
\{V_{\tilde{a}}=1\}
, every
(E,W)
stratum that occurs has nonzero probability of assignment to
\tilde{a}
, ensuring overlap for the
\tilde{a}
versus control comparison; this is primarily a design/implementation condition (checkable from the randomization scheme), though finite-sample data can still exhibit practical violations (e.g., zero counts in some strata).

A7
Pooling concurrent and nonconcurrent controls for the hazard.

For each
m\in\{1,\ldots,K\}
and all
(e,w)
such that
V_{\tilde{a}}=1
and

\mathsf{P}(I_{m}=1,A=0,E=e,W=w)>0
,

h(m,0,\tilde{a},e,w)=h(m,0,e,w),

where

\displaystyle h(m,0,e,w)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}(T=m\mid T\geq m,\;C\geq m,\;A=0,\;E=e,\;W=w)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}(L_{m}=1\mid I_{m}=1,\;A=0,\;E=e,\;W=w).

Assumption
A7
states that, after conditioning on
(E,W)
(and being at risk at
m
), the control-arm event hazard is the same in the concurrent subset and in the pooled (concurrent plus nonconcurrent) controls, so pooled control data can be used to learn the concurrent control hazard. Section
10
in the Supplementary Material provides additional details on Assumption
A7
, including its role in identification, conditions under which it is plausible, and how it interacts with parametric model specification.

Finally, the identification of the causal parameter given in Definition
4.1
requires the following preliminary results,

Proposition 1

(Product representation)
.

Under assumptions
A1
-
A4
the distribution functions for survival and censoring admit the following product representation:

S(t\mid A=a,V_{\tilde{a}}=1,E=e,W=w)=\prod_{m=1}^{t}\{1-h(m,a,\tilde{a},e,w)\},\\

Using these propositions it is possible to link the concurrent treatment-specific counterfactual survival curve to the observed data distribution,

(Nonparametric identification of
\theta(a,t)
)
.

Fix
\tilde{a}\in\{1,\ldots,\mathcal{A}\}
and let

,

,

,

,

,

Remark 1

(Pooling controls for the hazard)
.

The representation in Theorem
1
identifies
\theta(0,t)
using the control hazard

h(m,0,\tilde{a},E,W)
evaluated in the concurrent population. If, in addition, Assumption
A7
holds,
then
h(m,0,\tilde{a},E,W)=h(m,0,E,W)
for

, and one may equivalently write

\displaystyle\theta(0,t)=\mathsf{E}\!\left[\prod_{m=1}^{t}\{1-h(m,0,E,W)\}\ \big|\ V_{\tilde{a}}=1\right]

4.1
Restricted mean survival time

The restricted mean survival time (RMST) is a novel alternative measure of time-to-event quantities in survival analysis. The RMST represents the area under the survival curve from time 0 to a given cut-off, in this work denoted
\tau
. The interpretation is straight forward, the RMST is the average time-to-event during the time period between 0 and
\tau

(
12
)
. Contrasts such as the difference in restricted mean survival time thus represent the difference in the average time-to-event between two specified groups such as treatment vs. control or new treatment vs. standard of care. In addition to the easy interpretation, the RMST and related contrasts remain valid even when the proportional hazards assumption does not hold
(
32
)
, a common but often tenuous assumption in medical studies.
Given the advantages of the RMST (and its contrasts), the causal estimand of interest in this work will be the difference in restricted mean survival time among the population of concurrent control subjects.
The identification result in Theorem
1
allows functions of the treatment-specific survival curve, like the RMST to be similarly identified,

Definition 4.2

(Difference in restricted mean survival time (
\mathsf{dRMST}
))
.

Using Theorem
1
, for each
a\in\{0,\ldots,\mathcal{A}\}
and
\tau\in\{1,\ldots,K\}

\displaystyle\mathsf{dRMST}(a,\tau)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}[\min(T(a),\tau)-\min(T(0),\tau)\mid V_{\tilde{a}}=1]

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau-1}S^{*}_{a}(t\mid V_{\tilde{a}}=1)-S^{*}_{0}(t\mid V_{\tilde{a}}=1)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau-1}\theta(1,t)-\theta(0,t)

\displaystyle=\sum_{t=1}^{\tau-1}\Big\{\mathsf{E}\Big[\prod_{m=1}^{t}1-h(m,1,1,e,w)\mid V_{\tilde{a}}=1\Big]-\mathsf{E}\Big[\prod_{m=1}^{t}1-h(m,0,1,e,w)\mid V_{\tilde{a}}=1\Big]\Big\}.

In the following section we will discuss different estimators for
\mathsf{dRMST}
based on outcome regression and influence functions.

4.2
Other survival contrasts

In this paper we focus on difference in restricted mean survival time, which as shown in Definition
4.2
, is a linear function of
\theta(a,t)
, however other contrasts may be constructed
(
39
)
. For example, following the analysis conducted in the ACTT study, one may consider the recovery ratio
\theta(0,t)/\theta(1,t)
. Additionally, other end points common in survival analysis such as: the survival ratio
\theta(1,t)/\theta(0,t)
, risk difference
\{1-\theta(1,t)\}-\{1-\theta(0,t)\}
or risk-ratio
\{1-\theta(1,t)\}/\{1-\theta(0,t)\}
may be calculated depending on the scientific question of interest.

5
Estimation

We will begin our discussion of estimation by introducing outcome regression (OR) estimators to help build intuition. However, outcome regression estimators are subject to model misspecification, i.e. if the functional form of the models are incorrect bias will be introduced. To address this limitation we then introduce doubly robust (DR) estimators. We assume
V_{\tilde{a}}=\mathds{1}(E>t)
for some discrete time
t
such that the treatment was only available for subjects who entered the trial after time
t
.

5.1
Estimators based on parametric outcome regression

Using Theorem
1
an outcome regression based estimator using only concurrent control subjects can be defined as,

\displaystyle\widehat{\mathsf{dRMST}}_{OR}^{oc}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau-1}\widehat{\textbf{S}}_{1}(i,t)\times\mathds{1}(V_{\tilde{a},i}=1)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}(V_{\tilde{a},i}=1)}-\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau-1}\widehat{\textbf{S}}_{0}(i,t)\times\mathds{1}(V_{\tilde{a},i}=1)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathds{1}(V_{\tilde{a},i}=1)}.

Alternatively, employing Theorem
1
and Remark
1

5.2
Doubly robust estimators

So called doubly robust (DR) estimators provided consistent treatment effect estimates by combining outcome regression and inverse probability weights. DR estimators are preferable to outcome regression as the functional form of the estimator will guarantee consistency even when a subset of the regression models used to generate the estimates are misspecified. In this work we construct DR estimators, following

Influence functions are fundamental object in mathematical statistics and allow one to construct estimators with many favorable properties. In addition to the doubly robust feature described previously, estimators constructed using influence functions are asymptotically normal, often achieve fast rates of convergence and allow researchers to integrate machine learning into estimation while still admitting traditional inference (point estimation, confidence interval construction and hypothesis testing).

5.4
On efficiency

When estimators are based on parametric hazard regression, pooling can improve precision for the
concurrent control estimand only when Assumption
A7
ensures that the pooled and concurrent control hazards coincide (so pooling does not change the target) and the fitted hazard models are correctly
specified (or otherwise converge to the true concurrent hazards). In this regime, the pooled likelihood uses a
superset of the control risk-set contributions at each time point, increasing Fisher information for the hazard
parameters and thereby reducing the hazard-estimation component of the plug-in estimator’s asymptotic
variance through the delta-method mapping from the model parameters to the targeted causal parameter; see Q4 in Section
10
of the Supplement. Outside this regime, pooling should be viewed as a bias–variance tradeoff relative to the concurrent estimand rather than
as an efficiency improvement. These patterns are reflected in the simulation results (Figure
1
and Figure
4
in Section
6
).
The bottom row of Figure
2
(Section
6
) shows that the DR estimator using all controls can improve efficiency when
V_{\tilde{a}}
is stochastic given
E
. We now give a heuristic intuition for why this can occur. Any efficiency gains from pooling in the DR
setting arise through a different mechanism than in parametric plug-in regression. Under Assumption
A7
and when the availability indicator
V_{\tilde{a}}
is stochastic given
X=(E,W)
(so that
0<\mathsf{P}(V_{\tilde{a}}=1\mid X)<1
on a set of positive
probability), nonconcurrent controls overlap with concurrent units at the same covariate values. In this
overlap regime, pooled controls contribute valid information about the concurrent control nuisance functions
(e.g., the control hazard and censoring hazard given
X
), and the EIF for the pooled estimator effectively
replaces the noisy indicator weight
\mathbbm{1}\{V_{\tilde{a}}=1\}
by its conditional mean

\mathsf{P}(V_{\tilde{a}}=1\mid X)
(a Rao–Blackwellization of the selection weight). This reduces the variance of
the influence-function correction term and can yield a strictly smaller asymptotic variance when nuisance
functions are consistently estimated. By contrast, if
V_{\tilde{a}}
is deterministic in
E
, the
V_{\tilde{a}}=0
controls are off-support for the concurrent covariate distribution and cannot improve efficiency in the nonparametric sense; any apparent gains then reflect additional modeling restrictions rather than information
borrowed at the same
X
.

9
Conclusion

In this work, we identified the treatment-specific survival curve,
\theta(a,\tau)
, in platform trials with time-to-event outcomes and extend these results to functionals of
\theta(a,\tau)
, such as the
\mathsf{dRMST}
. We stated the required assumptions, clarify the rationale and challenges of pooling concurrent and non-concurrent controls, and develop estimators for
\mathsf{dRMST}
, whose properties we studied through simulations and a real-data application. We argue that the assumptions required to pool the full shared control arm for estimating causal survival effects based on
\theta(a,\tau)
, are subtle and often unrealistic. Simulations show that pooling concurrent and non-concurrent controls can induce substantial bias under model misspecification and invalidate inference. We therefore recommend covariate-adjusted doubly robust estimators targeting only concurrent controls, with efficiency gains sought through richer baseline prognostic adjustment rather than pooling all controls. Finally, based on the results of this manuscript, we provide practical guidelines in Section
16
of the Supplementary Materials.

\displaystyle h(m,0,e,w)

\displaystyle=\mathsf{P}(L_{m}=1\mid I_{m}=1,\ A=0,\ E=e,\ W=w)

), so
h(m,0,e,w)=h(m,0,\tilde{a},e,w)
holds automatically and
A7
is not needed to identify the concurrent control hazard from the observed data; in this case, “pooling” is purely an estimation move, as it justifies fitting/estimating
h(m,0,e,w)
using all control observations (including nonconcurrent ones) to gain precision.

10.2
Q2: When is Assumption
A7
plausible?

Assumption
A7
is a restriction on the
true
distribution
\mathsf{P}_{0}
stating that,
among control-arm participants who are event-free and uncensored through time
m
(i.e.,
I_{m}=1
), the event
hazard at time
m
does not depend on whether treatment
\tilde{a}
was available at entry, once we condition
on
(E,W)
:
h(m,0,e,w)=h(m,0,\tilde{a},e,w)
for all
e\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
and in its equivalent form
L_{m}\perp V_{\tilde{a}}\ \big|\ (I_{m}=1,\ A=0,\ E,\ W).

Thus, beyond the deterministic case
V_{\tilde{a}}=v_{\tilde{a}}(E)
where the assumption is redundant,
A7
is
plausible precisely when
V_{\tilde{a}}
carries no residual information about the control event process after
conditioning on the measured covariates
W
and entry time
E
.

An important setting in which Assumption
A7
may be implausible is when there exist unmeasured
calendar-time or site-level factors that jointly influence treatment availability and control outcomes. Let
U
denote generic unmeasured calendar-time or site-level features (e.g., changes in standard of care, diagnostic intensity, background risk, or enrollment practices) that may affect the
event process. If
U
is associated with
V_{\tilde{a}}
within levels of
(E,W)
and also prognostic for the
event hazard within the control risk set, then
A7
will generally fail:

U\not\!\perp V_{\tilde{a}}\mid(E,W)\quad\text{and}\quad L_{m}\not\!\perp U\mid(I_{m}=1,A=0,E,W)\ \Longrightarrow\ L_{m}\not\!\perp V_{\tilde{a}}\mid(I_{m}=1,A=0,E,W).

Since Assumption
A7
is equivalent to

L_{m}\perp V_{\tilde{a}}\mid(I_{m}=1,A=0,E,W)
, the above implication suggests that there exist
(m,e,w)
(with

e\in\mathcal{E}_{1}
and
\mathsf{P}(I_{m}=1,A=0,E=e,W=w)>0
) such that

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}(L_{m}=1\mid I_{m}=1,\ A=0,\ V_{\tilde{a}}=1,\ E=e,\ W=w)

\displaystyle\neq

\displaystyle\mathsf{P}(L_{m}=1\mid I_{m}=1,\ A=0,\ V_{\tilde{a}}=0,\ E=e,\ W=w),

and consequently, in the original hazard notation,

h(m,0,e,w)\neq h(m,0,\tilde{a},e,w).

Conversely,
A7
becomes more plausible when the observed covariates
W
(possibly together with
E
) adequately
adjust for the drivers of both availability and baseline prognosis, so that any remaining association between

V_{\tilde{a}}
and the control event hazard is negligible.

Q3: How does assumption

Well-specified model.

10.4
Q4: How does Assumption
A7
relate to the efficiency of parametric plug-in estimators? Formalizing the intuition that pooling data increases precision.

This subsection formalizes a common intuition: when the goal is the concurrent control survival curve

\theta_{\mathrm{conc}}(0,t;\mathsf{P}_{0})
, borrowing nonconcurrent controls can improve precision because it uses
more information to estimate the same control-arm hazard, provided that pooling does not change the target.
As shown in the preceding discussion, Assumption
A7
is precisely the condition under which
pooling controls is compatible with the concurrent hazard target. We focus here on parametric plug-in
estimators based on fitted discrete-time hazards (e.g., logistic regression at each time point).

Setup

Fix
t\in\{1,\ldots,\tau\}
and consider the concurrent control survival functional

\displaystyle\theta_{\mathrm{conc}}(0,t;\mathsf{P}_{0})

\displaystyle=\mathsf{E}_{\mathsf{P}_{0}}\!\left[\prod_{m=1}^{t}\{1-h_{\mathrm{conc}}(m,E,W)\}\ \big|\ V_{\tilde{a}}=1\right],

\displaystyle h_{\mathrm{conc}}(m,e,w)

\displaystyle:=\mathsf{P}(L_{m}=1\mid I_{m}=1,A=0,V_{\tilde{a}}=1,E=e,W=w).

Assume a parametric hazard model
\{h_{\beta_{m}}(m,e,w):\beta_{m}\in\mathbb{R}^{p_{m}}\}
for the control hazard at each
m
,
with design vector
\phi_{m}(e,w)
(e.g., a logistic regression with
\operatorname{logit}h_{\beta_{m}}(m,e,w)=\beta_{m}^{\top}\phi_{m}(e,w)
).

When pooling can increase precision for the concurrent estimand

Assume:
(i) Assumption
A7
holds so that
h_{\mathrm{conc}}(m,e,w)=h_{\mathrm{pool}}(m,e,w)
on the concurrent domain;
(ii) the model is well-specified, i.e. for each
m
there exists
\beta_{m}^{\star}
such that

h_{\beta_{m}^{\star}}(m,e,w)=h_{\mathrm{conc}}(m,e,w)
on the relevant support;
(iii) standard regularity conditions for M-estimators hold.

Consider two estimators of
\beta_{m}^{\star}
:
a
concurrent-only
estimator and a
pooled-controls
estimator, defined as solutions to weighted score equations

0=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{m,i}^{(k)}\,\varphi_{m}(Z_{i};\beta_{m}),\qquad k\in\{\mathrm{conc},\mathrm{pool}\},

where
\varphi_{m}(Z;\beta_{m})
is the per-observation score-like contribution for time
m
(for logistic regression,

\varphi_{m}(Z;\beta_{m})=I_{m}\mathbf{1}\{A=0\}\,\phi_{m}(E,W)\{L_{m}-h_{\beta_{m}}(m,E,W)\}
),
and the weights are

\omega_{m}^{\mathrm{conc}}:=\mathbf{1}\{V_{\tilde{a}}=1\},\qquad\omega_{m}^{\mathrm{pool}}:=1.

(Equivalently,
\omega_{m}^{\mathrm{conc}}
restricts to concurrent controls while
\omega_{m}^{\mathrm{pool}}
uses all controls.)

Let

A_{m}^{(k)}:=\mathsf{E}\!\left[\omega_{m}^{(k)}\,\nabla_{\beta_{m}}\varphi_{m}(Z;\beta_{m}^{\star})\right],\qquad B_{m}^{(k)}:=\Var\!\left(\omega_{m}^{(k)}\,\varphi_{m}(Z;\beta_{m}^{\star})\right).

Then the influence-function expansions of the two estimators are

\sqrt{n}\big(\hat{\beta}_{m}^{(k)}-\beta_{m}^{\star}\big)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\psi_{\beta_{m}}^{(k)}(Z_{i})+o_{p}(1),\qquad\psi_{\beta_{m}}^{(k)}(Z):=-(A_{m}^{(k)})^{-1}\,\omega_{m}^{(k)}\,\varphi_{m}(Z;\beta_{m}^{\star}),

and hence

\sqrt{n}\big(\hat{\beta}_{m}^{(k)}-\beta_{m}^{\star}\big)\ \overset{d}{\to}\ \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\ (A_{m}^{(k)})^{-1}B_{m}^{(k)}(A_{m}^{(k)})^{-T}\right).

Under correct specification and canonical MLE-type scores, this reduces to the familiar information form

(A_{m}^{(k)})^{-1}
(since
B_{m}^{(k)}=A_{m}^{(k)}
), and moreover

A_{m}^{\mathrm{pool}}\succeq A_{m}^{\mathrm{conc}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad(A_{m}^{\mathrm{pool}})^{-1}\preceq(A_{m}^{\mathrm{conc}})^{-1},

because
\omega_{m}^{\mathrm{pool}}\geq\omega_{m}^{\mathrm{conc}}
pointwise and the remaining terms in
A_{m}^{(k)}

are positive semidefinite. Thus, pooling yields a weak efficiency improvement for estimating each
\beta_{m}^{\star}
.

Propagation to the concurrent survival curve

Define the plug-in estimator

\hat{\theta}^{(k)}(0,t):=\frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{V_{\tilde{a},i}=1\}\sum_{t=1}^{\tau-1}\prod_{m=1}^{t}\{1-h_{\hat{\beta}_{m}^{(k)}}(m,E_{i},W_{i})\}}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf{1}\{V_{\tilde{a},i}=1\}},\qquad k\in\{\mathrm{conc},\mathrm{pool}\}.

Because
\theta_{\mathrm{conc}}(0,t;\mathsf{P}_{0})
is a smooth functional of
(\beta_{1}^{\star},\ldots,\beta_{t}^{\star})
,
a first-order expansion yields the influence function

\psi_{t}^{(k)}(Z)=\psi_{t}^{\mathrm{mean}}(Z)+\sum_{m=1}^{t}\left\{\nabla_{\beta_{m}}\theta_{\mathrm{conc}}(0,t;\mathsf{P}_{0})\right\}^{\top}\psi_{\beta_{m}}^{(k)}(Z),

where
\psi_{t}^{\mathrm{mean}}(Z)
is the empirical-mean component from averaging over
\{V_{\tilde{a}}=1\}

(identical for the two estimators), and the second term captures the additional variability from estimating the hazards.
Consequently,

\Var\!\big(\psi_{t}^{\mathrm{pool}}(Z)\big)=\Var\!\big(\psi_{t}^{\mathrm{mean}}(Z)\big)+\Var\!\left(\sum_{m=1}^{t}g_{m,t}^{\top}\psi_{\beta_{m}}^{\mathrm{pool}}(Z)\right)+2\,\Cov\!\left(\psi_{t}^{\mathrm{mean}}(Z),\sum_{m=1}^{t}g_{m,t}^{\top}\psi_{\beta_{m}}^{\mathrm{pool}}(Z)\right),

with
g_{m,t}:=\nabla_{\beta_{m}}\theta_{\mathrm{conc}}(0,t;\mathsf{P}_{0})
, and the analogous expression holds for

\Var(\psi_{t}^{\mathrm{conc}}(Z))
.
Since
\omega_{m}^{\mathrm{pool}}\geq\omega_{m}^{\mathrm{conc}}
pointwise, the pooled estimating equation uses a
superset of the control-arm risk-set observations at time
m
relative to the concurrent-only fit. Under the
canonical likelihood case (so
B_{m}^{(k)}=A_{m}^{(k)}
), this yields

A_{m}^{\mathrm{pool}}\succeq A_{m}^{\mathrm{conc}}
and hence

(A_{m}^{\mathrm{pool}})^{-1}\preceq(A_{m}^{\mathrm{conc}})^{-1}.

Because
\psi_{\beta_{m}}^{(k)}(Z)=-(A_{m}^{(k)})^{-1}\omega_{m}^{(k)}\varphi_{m}(Z;\beta_{m}^{\star})
, the above implies that
for any fixed vector
g
(in particular
g=g_{m,t}
),

\Var\!\big(g^{\top}\psi_{\beta_{m}}^{\mathrm{pool}}(Z)\big)\ \leq\ \Var\!\big(g^{\top}\psi_{\beta_{m}}^{\mathrm{conc}}(Z)\big),

so pooling weakly reduces the variance contribution propagated through estimation of
\beta_{m}^{\star}
.

For the concurrent survival curve, the hazard-estimation component is

\Var\!\left(\sum_{m=1}^{t}g_{m,t}^{\top}\psi_{\beta_{m}}^{(k)}(Z)\right)
, and pooling therefore weakly reduces this
component (holding fixed the mean-term
\psi_{t}^{\mathrm{mean}}
, which is identical for
k\in\{\mathrm{conc},\mathrm{pool}\}
).
We do
not
claim a general PSD ordering for the
full
asymptotic variance

\Var\!\big(\psi_{t}^{(k)}(Z)\big)
because it also includes the cross-covariance term

2\,\Cov\!\left(\psi_{t}^{\mathrm{mean}}(Z),\sum_{m=1}^{t}g_{m,t}^{\top}\psi_{\beta_{m}}^{(k)}(Z)\right)
, whose sign
need not be preserved by pooling. Nonetheless, under (i)–(iii), pooling improves precision through the
hazard-estimation pathway by making the
\hat{\beta}_{m}
’s (and hence the propagated term) asymptotically more precise.

Effective sample size heuristic

If the time-
m
risk-set contributions are comparable across concurrent and nonconcurrent controls after conditioning on
(E,W)
,
then
A_{m}^{(k)}
scales approximately with the expected number of control risk-set observations used at time
m
:

A_{m}^{\mathrm{pool}}\approx c_{m}^{\mathrm{pool}}\Sigma_{m},\qquad A_{m}^{\mathrm{conc}}\approx c_{m}^{\mathrm{conc}}\Sigma_{m},

where

c_{m}^{\mathrm{pool}}=\mathsf{E}_{\mathsf{P}_{0}}[I_{m}\mathbf{1}\{A=0\}]

and

c_{m}^{\mathrm{conc}}=\mathsf{E}_{\mathsf{P}_{0}}[I_{m}\mathbf{1}\{A=0,V_{\tilde{a}}=1\}].

Thus, at a back-of-the-envelope level,

\Var(\hat{\beta}_{m}^{\mathrm{pool}})\ \text{is smaller than}\ \Var(\hat{\beta}_{m}^{\mathrm{conc}})\ \text{by roughly the factor}\ c_{m}^{\mathrm{pool}}/c_{m}^{\mathrm{conc}},

and this variance reduction propagates to
\hat{\theta}(0,t)
through the gradients
\{\nabla_{\beta_{m}}\theta\}_{m\leq t}
.

What can go wrong without A7 and/or correct specification

If A7 fails, then
h_{\mathrm{pool}}(m,e,w)\neq h_{\mathrm{conc}}(m,e,w)
for some
(m,e,w)
, so a pooled
plug-in estimator is generally consistent for a
different
survival curve than the concurrent estimand.
In that case pooling trades bias for variance relative to
\theta_{\mathrm{conc}}(0,t;\mathsf{P}_{0})
and should
not be justified as an efficiency gain for the concurrent target.
If the parametric hazard model is misspecified, then
\hat{\beta}_{m}
converges to a pseudo-true limit

\beta_{m}^{\dagger}
; pooling may still reduce the asymptotic variance around that pseudo-true limit because it
increases information, but it does not guarantee smaller mean squared error for the concurrent estimand.

Take-away

Pooling can deliver genuine efficiency gains for the concurrent estimand when (i) A7 ensures that pooling does not change the target hazard, and (ii) the hazard model is correctly specified (or, more generally, the fitted hazards converge to the true concurrent hazards). Outside this regime, improvements in precision should be interpreted through a bias–variance lens rather than as a pure efficiency gain.

Practical considerations

What estimands should we target?
Recent regulatory guidance
(
10
,
18
)
advocates first determining the causal estimand of interest through scientific discussion with subject matter experts before positing a statistical model for the outcome of interest. Only after the estimand and its necessary components (target population, outcome variable, etc.) are established do researchers proceed with selecting an optimal estimation strategy. The organization of this work and the target causal estimand follow the recommendations of
(
10
)
.

Integrating the shared control arm.
Following the discussion in Section
10
we advise against pooling concurrent and nonconcurrent controls. Combining control subjects requires a sequence of related assumptions which are not testable (random censoring) and are likely to be untenable in real world applications (correct model specification and hazard/censoring function stability over time). Simple numerical studies like those described in Section
6
show how pooling controls when Assumption
A7
does not hold can invalidate the statistical analysis.

Precision via stronger baseline predictors.
Recent work suggests that incorporating stronger predictor variables can improve efficiency in estimating treatment effects
(
7
)
. In this article we propose this approach as a means to improve efficiency in place of integrating the complete study population of control subjects. Evidence for this alternative can be found in the real world data application Section
8
, specifically the improvement in efficiency obtained by
OR_oc
and
DR_oc
despite the fact that these estimators only incorporate concurrent control units.

What estimators should we use?
Assuming Model
1
, to estimate
\mathsf{dRMST}
we suggest employing the doubly robust estimator using only the concurrent control population denoted
DR_oc
. Since the estimator
DR_oc
integrates only concurrent controls the previously mentioned difficulties associated with utilizing the entire shared control arm are not a factor in estimation. Researchers do not need to be concerned with the many challenges associated with pooling controls discussed in Section
10
, and any estimation bias which may come as a result. Additionally, the estimator maintains the desirable properties discussed in Section
5.2
, i.e. valid statistical inference and additional safeguards against model misspecification compared to outcome regression based techniques.

Sample size and power calculations.
Theory and results from the numerical studies in this work suggest an efficiency gain when including non-concurrent control subjects into the data analysis with outcome regression estimators. Although these results appear promising, we advise conducting sample size and power calculations as if only the concurrent control units were available. During the analysis of trial data, if researchers desire efficiency gains, non-concurrent control data may be included as described earlier, but with the understanding that the outcome model must be correctly defined, otherwise treatment effect estimates may be biased and the estimated
95\%
confidence intervals invalid.

: it asserts that, within the control-arm risk set at time
m
, the censoring hazard does not depend on whether treatment
\tilde{a}
was available at entry once we condition on
(E,W)
, so that nonconcurrent controls can be pooled to estimate
g(m,0,\tilde{a},e,w)
using the marginal control censoring hazard
g(m,0,e,w)
. In contrast to Assumption
A7
, Remark
2
is not required for identification of the concurrent treatment-specific survival curve (in either the deterministic or stochastic availability regimes), because identification relies on the concurrent censoring mechanism
g(m,0,\tilde{a},e,w)
and does not require replacing it by its pooled counterpart. However, Remark
2
follows as a consequence of Assumption
A7
and is needed for the doubly robust estimator that pools all controls, since its efficient influence function uses the pooled censoring survival
G(\cdot\mid A=0,X)
in the denominators of the control-arm augmentation terms; without Remark
2
, those denominators correspond to a different censoring mechanism than the concurrent one, so pooling censoring can induce bias even when the concurrent estimand remains identified (more details in Section
5.2
).

To determine the superiority of Remdesivir plus Baricitinib compared with Remdesivir alone in reducing the time to recovery in patients hospitalized adults with Covid-19.

Variable

Time to recovery between baseline and 28 days where recovery is defined as the first day, during the 28 days after enrollment, on which a patient attained category 1, 2, or 3 on an eight-category ordinal scale.

Remdesivir plus Baricitinib vs Remdesivir.

Population

Patients hospitalized with COVID-19.

Difference in the mean change in time to recovery between treatments.

Post randomization events are not considered.
