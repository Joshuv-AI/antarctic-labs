Routine Blood Biomarkers and Lung Function in Asthma-COPD Overlap versus Asthma-Only Among Hospitalized Asthma Patients: A Retrospective Cross-Sectional Study
Authors: Mei Xie, Xiaofei Jin, Hui Li, Lei Tian, Shaojin Wang, Yating Zhang, Ting Wang, Shuni Wang, Hongyi Wang, Xiaoyan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare routine blood biomarkers, lung function, and medication-associated biomarker patterns between hospitalized asthma patients classified as having asthma-COPD overlap (ACO) and those with asthma-only.MethodsThis retrospective cross-sectional analysis included 505 hospitalized asthma patients admitted between January 2020 and December 2024, comprising 190 with ACO and 315 with asthma-only. Disease history, pulmonary function parameters, complete blood cell counts, differential percentages, immunoglobulin E, and medication exposure were extracted from electronic medical records and analyzed using complete cases. Multivariable logistic regression, multivariable linear regression, Spearman correlation, and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analyses were used to evaluate associations and discriminatory performance. Medication analyses were exploratory because treatment was not randomized.ResultsCompared with asthma-only patients, patients with ACO were older, had a lower prevalence of allergy history, used inhaled corticosteroids, long-acting beta2-agonists, and long-acting muscarinic antagonists more frequently, and had worse airflow limitation and lower diffusing capacity. Higher neutrophil percentage (adjusted odds ratio: 2.15, 95% confidence interval: 1.62–2.85, p<0.001) and lower lymphocyte percentage (adjusted odds ratio: 0.51, 95% confidence interval: 0.39–0.67, p<0.001) were independently associated with ACO after Bonferroni correction. In ACO patients, inhaled corticosteroid and long-acting beta2-agonist exposure was nominally associated with higher neutrophil-related and lower lymphocyte-related measures, but these observational findings should not be interpreted as treatment effects. FEV1/FVC showed the best discrimination for ACO (area under the curve [AUC]: 0.696), whereas neutrophil percentage showed modest complementary discrimination (AUC: 0.596).ConclusionWithin this hospitalized asthma cohort, ACO was associated with older age, more severe airflow limitation, impaired diffusing capacity, and a more neutrophil-dominant systemic inflammatory profile than asthma-only. Routine blood biomarkers, particularly neutrophil and lymphocyte percentages, may complement pulmonary function parameters in identifying ACO, but their standalone diagnostic performance was modest. Medication-associated biomarker differences were exploratory and require prospective validation.Keywords: asthma-COPD overlap, asthma-only, neutrophils, routine blood biomarkers, lung function, cross-sectional study

Introduction

Asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) have traditionally been regarded as distinct obstructive airway diseases with different etiologies, inflammatory profiles, and therapeutic approaches. In practice, however, some patients present with clinical and functional features of both conditions, often described as asthma-COPD overlap (ACO). ACO is best viewed as a heterogeneous overlap phenotype or spectrum rather than a single uniformly defined disease entity, and current discussions increasingly emphasize treatable traits alongside diagnostic labels.1,2 Patients with overlapping asthma and COPD features often have higher symptom burden, more frequent exacerbations, impaired quality of life, accelerated lung-function decline, and greater healthcare utilization than patients with asthma-only or COPD-only in many cohorts.2–5 Therefore, better characterization of ACO within asthma populations remains clinically relevant, while claims of disease specificity must be made cautiously.

Because ACO definitions vary across guidelines and studies, differences observed between ACO and asthma-only populations may reflect a mixture of asthma-related traits, COPD-like fixed airflow obstruction, exposure history, and age-related lung-function impairment. International proposals, including GEMA/GESEPOC-based criteria and consensus discussions, commonly combine persistent airflow limitation, asthma features, exposure history, bronchodilator reversibility, atopy, and eosinophilic inflammation, but no single definition has become universally accepted.6–9 This uncertainty is important when interpreting biomarker studies and motivated our use of an explicit operational definition in the present analysis.

Despite its clinical significance, the reliable identification of ACO in routine practice remains a formidable challenge, primarily due to the lack of a single, definitive diagnostic biomarker.1,10 For instance, a bronchodilator response of ≥280 mL was moderately predictive of ACO, yet significant overlap exists among the groups.11 The search for more specific biomarkers has led to the investigation of various plasma proteins, such as CXCL9 and MCP-3, which show promise in discriminating ACO from pure asthma or COPD, particularly in specific age subgroups.12 Nevertheless, the reliance on such specialized and often costly assays limits their widespread application in everyday clinical settings, particularly in primary care and resource-limited environments, creating a significant gap between advanced clinical research and practical, accessible diagnostics.

This creates a pressing need to identify and validate diagnostic tools that are both effective and readily accessible. In this context, the potential value of routine, low-cost, and universally available laboratory investigations, such as the complete blood count (CBC), has been comparatively underexplored.13 Notably, components of the CBC are already embedded in the assessment of obstructive lung diseases. Blood eosinophil count has firmly established itself as a prognostic biomarker and a predictor of response to inhaled corticosteroids in both asthma and COPD.14,15 The neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio itself has been identified as a risk factor for acute exacerbations in patients with COPD.16 These findings underscore those simple hematological parameters available in routine labs may offer a pragmatic, cost-effective means to aid in the identification of ACO in diverse clinical settings.

Based on this background and the need for accessible tools, this study was designed as a retrospective cross-sectional analysis to compare routinely available clinical biomarkers and pulmonary function parameters between hospitalized asthma patients classified as having ACO and those with asthma-only. We also explored medication-associated biomarker patterns, while recognizing that the observational design does not support causal inference about treatment effects.

Methods and Materials

Study Design and Participant Selection

This retrospective cross-sectional analysis was conducted at the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University. The study protocol was approved by the hospital’s Institutional Review Board (KYLL-2025-1724), which waived the requirement for informed consent because only routinely collected medical-record data were analyzed retrospectively. Patient confidentiality was protected through anonymized data extraction and analysis, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. We initially screened all patients with a diagnosis of asthma who were admitted to the hospital between January 2020 and December 2024. The primary objective was to compare clinical characteristics, pulmonary function, and hematological biomarkers between patients with asthma-only and those with ACO. The patient inclusion and exclusion process is detailed in Figure 1. A total of 6741 asthma admissions were initially identified from the hospital’s electronic medical record system. After screening, 505 complete eligible records were included in the final analysis. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) incomplete key clinical data, most commonly because the record was from a non-hospitalized patient or lacked pulmonary function tests or blood cell counts; (2) severe systemic diseases (eg, heart failure, renal failure, autoimmune diseases) that could substantially affect immune function or biomarker levels; and (3) age below 18 years A complete-case approach was used; no statistical imputation was performed for excluded records. Ultimately, 190 patients (37.6%) were classified as having ACO, and 315 patients (62.4%) comprised the asthma-only group.

Data Collection and Clinical Characteristics

Comprehensive clinical data were extracted from the electronic medical records for all included patients. The extracted variables included: (1) demographics and baseline characteristics, including age, sex, weight, height, and body mass index (BMI); smoking history was recorded as a binary variable (yes/no), and exposure to indoor or outdoor air pollution was abstracted from documented long-term occupational dust/fume exposure, biomass or coal-smoke exposure, or chronic heavy ambient exposure in the respiratory history when available; (2) disease-specific characteristics, including asthma type (cough variant asthma or other), allergy history, acute exacerbation severity, and duration of asthma; (3) pulmonary function parameters measured according to American Thoracic Society/European Respiratory Society standards, including VC%, FVC%, FEV1%, FEV1/FVC, PEF%, TLC%, and DLCO%; (4) hematological biomarkers and immunoglobulin E, including complete blood count with differential and serum total IgE, measured during admission before initiation of specific in-hospital treatments whenever available; and (5) pharmacological treatments before or during hospitalization, including inhaled corticosteroids, long-acting beta2-agonists, long-acting muscarinic antagonists, leukotriene receptor antagonists, theophylline, systemic corticosteroids, and other bronchodilators. Medication exposure was analyzed as a binary yes/no variable for each category.

Diagnosis of Asthma and ACO

The primary outcome was ACO versus asthma-only. The operational ACO definition was adapted from GEMA/GESEPOC-based criteria and international consensus proposals, because these frameworks combine persistent airflow limitation with asthma features, exposure history, bronchodilator response, atopy, and eosinophilic inflammation.6–9,17 A patient was classified as having ACO if they fulfilled three or more major criteria and at least one minor criterion. Major criteria were: (1) post-bronchodilator FEV1/FVC <0.70 in patients aged 40 years or older; (2) smoking history of at least 10 pack-years or documented equivalent indoor/outdoor pollution exposure; and (3) asthma onset before 40 years of age or a marked bronchodilator response, defined as an increase in FEV1 of at least 400 mL after bronchodilator administration. Minor criteria were: (1) atopy history; (2) bronchodilator response with an increase in FEV1 of at least 200 mL and at least 12% from baseline on two or more separate visits; and (3) peripheral blood eosinophil count of at least 300 cells/uL. Patients with asthma who did not meet this composite definition were classified as asthma-only. Because ACO remains heterogeneously defined, we also performed a sensitivity analysis restricted to ACO patients aged at least 40 years with FEV1/FVC <0.70. The severity of acute exacerbations at admission was categorized as mild, moderate, or severe based on symptoms, signs, and oxygen saturation according to hospital practice derived from Global Initiative for Asthma guidance.18

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1) in the original analysis workflow. Continuous variables were assessed for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and Q-Q plots. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent-samples t-tests or Welch’s t-tests when variances were unequal. Non-normally distributed data are presented as median with interquartile range and compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using Pearson’s Chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Associations between hematological biomarkers and ACO status were evaluated using separate multivariable logistic regression models for each biomarker to reduce overfitting from simultaneously including highly correlated blood-cell measures. Adjusted models included age, sex, asthma type, acute exacerbation severity, smoking history, allergy history, BMI, FEV1%, FEV1/FVC, PEF%, IgE level, and disease duration. Spearman correlation analyses were conducted separately in the ACO and asthma-only groups. Multivariable linear regression was used to evaluate associations between each blood cell parameter and FEV1%, FEV1/FVC, or PEF%, with the same covariate set except where the pulmonary-function measure was the outcome. Medication-associated biomarker comparisons were exploratory and used Wilcoxon rank-sum tests within each disease group for treated versus untreated patients in each medication category. ROC curves were used to evaluate discrimination of ACO from asthma-only. Bonferroni correction was applied within pre-specified families of comparisons: 11 hematological biomarkers in logistic models (threshold p<0.05/11=0.0045), 77 biomarker-pulmonary function correlations within each disease group (11 biomarkers x 7 pulmonary function indices; threshold p<0.00065), 11 biomarkers for each multivariable linear-regression outcome (threshold p<0.0045), and 11 biomarkers for each medication category within each disease group (threshold p<0.0045). ROC analyses were descriptive, and AUCs were interpreted cautiously.

Results

Baseline Characteristics and Pulmonary Function Differences

This study ultimately included 505 asthma patients from the General Hospital of Ningxia Medical University between 2020 and 2024 for final analysis, among whom 190 (37.6%) were diagnosed with asthma-chronic obstructive pulmonary disease overlap syndrome and 315 (62.4%) had asthma-only. The patient inclusion flowchart is detailed in Figure 1. The baseline characteristics of the two groups are shown in Table 1. The median age of patients in the ACO group was significantly higher than that in the asthma-only group (53 years vs 47 years, p < 0.001), and the proportion of patients aged ≥60 years was higher in the ACO group (26.8% vs 14.6%, p < 0.001). There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of sex, weight, height, body mass index, and smoking history. However, in terms of clinical features, the proportion of patients with a history of allergies in the ACO group was significantly lower than that in the asthma-only group (18.9% vs 75.2%, p < 0.001). Regarding the severity of acute exacerbations, the proportion of patients with mild exacerbations was higher in the ACO group (40.5% vs 19.0%), while the proportion of moderate exacerbations was lower (22.6% vs 54.0%), with the difference being statistically significant (p < 0.001). In terms of medication use, the proportions of ACO group patients using inhaled corticosteroids, long-acting β2-agonists, long-acting muscarinic antagonists, and triple therapy were all significantly higher than those in the asthma-only group.

Pulmonary function assessment results (Table 2) revealed fundamental differences between ACO and asthma-only. Compared to the asthma-only group, key pulmonary function parameters in the ACO group, including the percentage of predicted forced expiratory volume in 1 second, the ratio of FEV1 to forced vital capacity, and the percentage of predicted peak expiratory flow, were all significantly reduced (FEV1%: 78% vs 87%, p < 0.001; FEV1/FVC: 64% vs 72%, p < 0.001; PEF%: 74% vs 90%, p < 0.001). Furthermore, the diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide was also significantly lower in the ACO group (96% vs 107%, p < 0.001). However, there were no statistically significant differences between the two groups in the percentage of predicted vital capacity, forced vital capacity, and total lung capacity. These data clearly indicate that ACO patients have more severe airflow limitations and impaired gas exchange.

Differences in Hematological Biomarkers and Their Associations with ACO and Pulmonary Function

We analyzed a range of blood biomarkers to explore their role in differentiating ACO from asthma-only. As shown in Table 3, ACO patients had lower eosinophil count (0.22 vs 0.35, p=0.015) and eosinophil percentage (0.04 vs 0.05, p=0.003) than asthma-only patients. Conversely, neutrophil count (4.17 vs 3.81, p=0.001) and neutrophil percentage (0.60 vs 0.55, p<0.001) were higher in ACO patients. Basophil count and lymphocyte percentage also differed between groups. In separate multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for age, sex, asthma type, acute severity, smoking, allergy, BMI, FEV1%, FEV1/FVC, PEF%, IgE, and duration, higher neutrophil percentage (adjusted OR: 2.15, 95% CI: 1.62–2.85, p<0.001) and lower lymphocyte percentage (adjusted OR: 0.51, 95% CI: 0.39–0.67, p<0.001) remained independently associated with ACO after Bonferroni correction (Figure 2a). Significant associations after correction were also observed for white blood cell count, basophil count, and neutrophil count. Spearman correlation analysis showed that, in the ACO group, FEV1%, FEV1/FVC, and PEF% tended to correlate positively with lymphocyte parameters and negatively with neutrophil parameters, whereas patterns were weaker or different in asthma-only (Figure 2b). After Bonferroni correction across 77 tests, the most robust correlations involved DLCO%, including monocyte percentage, basophil percentage, monocyte count, and basophil count in asthma-only. Multivariable linear regression did not identify significant associations between the blood-cell parameters and FEV1%, FEV1/FVC, or PEF% after Bonferroni correction (Figure 2c–e). In the sensitivity analysis restricted to ACO patients aged at least 40 years with FEV1/FVC <0.70 (n=117), the main biomarker pattern was unchanged: neutrophil percentage remained higher (median 0.602 vs 0.550, p<0.001) and lymphocyte percentage remained lower (median 0.269 vs 0.306, p<0.001) than in asthma-only patients.

Medication-Associated Biomarker Patterns and the Discriminative Value of Biomarkers for ACO

We explored medication-associated biomarker patterns for six commonly used medication categories. Because treatment was not randomized and was recorded as binary exposure, these analyses should be interpreted as associations rather than treatment responses. Figure 3 summarizes standardized median differences between treated and untreated patients within ACO and asthma-only groups. In ACO patients, inhaled corticosteroid exposure was nominally associated with lower lymphocyte count (median 2.22 in untreated vs 1.94 in treated, p=0.037), higher neutrophil percentage (57% vs 61%, p=0.034), and lower lymphocyte percentage (31% vs 26%, p=0.012) (eTable 1). Long-acting beta2-agonist exposure showed a similar nominal association with lower lymphocyte count (p=0.047) (eTable 2). In contrast, long-acting muscarinic antagonist treatment was not associated with any significant changes in blood cell parameters in either patient group (eTable 3). After Bonferroni correction within medication-specific biomarker families, fewer associations remained, including higher WBC count among ACO patients receiving leukotriene receptor antagonists (p=0.004). In asthma-only patients, theophylline exposure was associated with lower eosinophil count and eosinophil percentage after correction, and leukotriene receptor antagonist and systemic corticosteroid comparisons showed selected corrected associations in MXD or BASO measures (eTables 4–6). Finally, ROC analysis showed that pulmonary function parameters, especially FEV1/FVC, had the highest discriminatory performance for ACO (AUC: 0.696). Among hematological biomarkers, neutrophil percentage showed only modest discrimination (AUC: 0.596), supporting a complementary rather than standalone diagnostic role (Figure 4).

Discussion

This retrospective cross-sectional study compared clinical, functional, and inflammatory profiles between hospitalized asthma patients classified as having ACO and those with asthma-only. Patients with ACO were older, had less frequent allergy history, more severe airflow limitation, lower diffusing capacity, and a more neutrophil-dominant systemic inflammatory profile. These findings support the concept that ACO within an asthma cohort is associated with COPD-like functional impairment and non-type 2 inflammatory features.19,20 However, because ACO remains variably defined and no COPD-only comparator was included, our results should be interpreted as differences between ACO and asthma-only rather than proof that ACO is a fully distinct disease entity or that the observed biomarker profile is specific to ACO.

The demographic and clinical distinctions we observed align with the conceptual framework of ACO as an overlap phenotype bridging asthma and COPD.2 Older age, lower allergic predisposition, lower FEV1/FVC, and reduced DLCO may reflect COPD-like traits superimposed on an asthma background.21,22 The higher use of inhaled corticosteroids, long-acting beta2-agonists, long-acting muscarinic antagonists, and triple therapy in the ACO group likely reflects clinical complexity and perceived disease severity in real-world practice.23,24 Importantly, without a COPD-only comparison group, we cannot determine whether these functional and inflammatory differences are ACO-specific or represent COPD-like characteristics within a hospitalized asthma population.

A key finding was the higher neutrophil percentage and lower lymphocyte and eosinophil measures in ACO compared with asthma-only. This pattern is biologically plausible because neutrophil-mediated inflammation and innate immune activation are central features of COPD pathophysiology and may be present in overlap phenotypes.25–27 The lower eosinophil count in ACO deserves careful interpretation. ACO is often associated clinically with asthma-like or type 2 traits, but our asthma-only comparator may have been enriched for allergic/eosinophilic asthma, whereas the ACO definition and hospitalized setting may have selected patients with more COPD-like neutrophilic biology. Prior corticosteroid exposure and differences in exacerbation phenotype may also have influenced blood eosinophil levels.28 Thus, the eosinophil finding should not be read as contradicting type 2 involvement in all ACO patients, but as describing the inflammatory pattern observed in this specific cohort.

The diagnostic performance of neutrophil percentage was modest, with an AUC of 0.596. This level of discrimination is insufficient for neutrophil percentage to be used as a standalone diagnostic biomarker. Instead, it may serve as a simple complementary marker alongside spirometry, exposure history, bronchodilator response, and other clinical features. FEV1/FVC showed better discrimination (AUC: 0.696), emphasizing that pulmonary function remains central to identifying ACO-like fixed airflow limitation. The practical value of routine blood biomarkers may therefore be greatest in low-cost phenotyping and risk stratification rather than definitive diagnosis.13

The medication-associated biomarker analysis warrants particular caution. Inhaled corticosteroids and long-acting beta2-agonists were more frequently used in ACO patients and were nominally associated with higher neutrophil percentage and lower lymphocyte measures, but the retrospective design cannot distinguish medication effects from confounding by indication.24 Patients receiving these therapies may have had more severe disease, more fixed obstruction, more frequent exacerbations, or different baseline inflammatory profiles. Therefore, these results should be considered exploratory signals that generate hypotheses for prospective studies rather than evidence that inhaled corticosteroids or long-acting beta2-agonists increase neutrophilic inflammation in ACO.

This study has several strengths, including a relatively large hospitalized asthma cohort and a comparative analysis covering demographics, lung function, routine blood biomarkers, and medication patterns. Several limitations must also be acknowledged. First, the retrospective cross-sectional design precludes causal inference about biomarker changes, treatment effects, or disease progression. Second, this was a single-center study, which may limit generalizability. Third, the ACO definition was operational and based on available medical-record data; although we performed a stricter airflow-limitation sensitivity analysis, alternative ACO definitions could produce different classifications. Fourth, no COPD-only comparator group was available, limiting conclusions about whether observed profiles are specific to ACO or reflect COPD-like traits. Fifth, pollution exposure was not quantitatively measured and depended on documentation in the medical record. Sixth, treatment analyses are susceptible to confounding by indication. Seventh, although separate biomarker models were used to reduce overfitting, the number of covariates was substantial, no formal a priori power calculation was performed, and residual confounding remains possible. Finally, airway-specific inflammatory measures such as sputum cell counts, fractional exhaled nitric oxide, imaging-derived emphysema burden, or proteomic markers were unavailable.

The clinical implications of these findings are therefore measured. The profile of ACO patients in this cohort supports comprehensive assessment that combines spirometry, diffusion capacity, exposure history, allergy/eosinophilic features, and routine systemic inflammatory markers. Blood neutrophil percentage is inexpensive and widely available, and may help identify a neutrophil-dominant subgroup among asthma patients with COPD-like features, but it should not replace established clinical and functional assessment.13,16 Observed medication-associated biomarker patterns further suggest that reassessment of inflammatory phenotype may be useful in patients with persistent symptoms or airflow limitation despite inhaled therapy; however, treatment decisions should continue to rely on guideline-based clinical judgment until prospective evidence is available.23,29

Future research should validate these findings in larger, multi-center prospective cohorts that include asthma-only, ACO, and COPD-only comparator groups. Longitudinal studies are needed to determine whether baseline neutrophilia predicts exacerbations, lung-function decline, or differential response to pharmacotherapy. Studies using standardized ACO definitions, quantitative exposure assessment, imaging, sputum inflammatory profiles, fractional exhaled nitric oxide, and multi-omics approaches may clarify whether the neutrophil-dominant pattern represents a stable treatable trait. Randomized or carefully designed prospective treatment studies are required before medication-associated biomarker patterns can be translated into individualized therapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

In this hospitalized asthma cohort, patients classified as having ACO differed from asthma-only patients in age, airflow limitation, diffusing capacity, and systemic inflammatory profile. Higher neutrophil percentage and lower lymphocyte percentage were independently associated with ACO and persisted in a stricter airflow-limitation sensitivity analysis. However, the discriminatory performance of neutrophil percentage was modest and should be regarded as complementary to pulmonary function rather than diagnostic on its own. Medication-associated biomarker findings were exploratory and should not be interpreted causally. These results support cautious use of routine blood biomarkers as part of broader clinical phenotyping of patients with overlapping asthma and COPD features.
