Construction and validation of a predictive nomogram for 28-day mortality in critically ill patients with toxic encephalopathy
Authors: Mingzhu Wang, Chengchao Peng, Zeguang Ye, Min Lu, Lehao Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundToxic encephalopathy (TE) is a serious neurological complication among critically ill patients and is associated with considerable disability and mortality. Early risk prediction is essential for timely intervention and improved outcomes. However, evidence on prognostic modeling in TE remains limited. The primary objective of this study was to develop and validate a prognostic nomogram for predicting 28-day mortality in critically ill patients diagnosed with TE and to evaluate its clinical utility.MethodsAdult patients diagnosed with TE were retrospectively selected from the MIMIC-IV database based on relevant International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes. Baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory variables were extracted, and candidate predictors were selected through univariate logistic regression analyses based on clinical relevance and statistical significance. Subsequently, a multivariable logistic regression model was developed and presented as a nomogram. The value of the predictive model was evaluated using the area under the ROC curve (AUC), calibration curve, and decision curve analysis (DCA). Its potential clinical applicability was further assessed with a clinical impact curve (CIC).ResultsA total of 3039 patients with TE were included, among whom 526 (17.3%) died within 28 days. The cohort was randomly divided into training and validation cohorts at a 7:3 ratio. The final model incorporated age (OR = 1.022, 95% CI: 1.013–1.032), heart rate (OR = 1.007, 95% CI: 1.001–1.013), hemoglobin (OR = 0.904, 95% CI: 0.852–0.958), albumin (OR = 0.677, 95% CI: 0.546–0.836), PaO2 (OR = 0.998, 95% CI: 0.996–0.999), SOFA score (OR = 1.094, 95% CI: 1.043–1.148), APACHE II score (OR = 1.038, 95% CI: 1.011–1.066), and acute kidney injury (OR = 1.520, 95% CI: 1.132–2.501). The nomogram exhibited acceptable discriminative ability, with AUC values of 0.750 (95% CI: 0.725–0.776) and 0.757 (95% CI: 0.715–0.799) in the training and validation cohorts, respectively, along with satisfactory calibration. Furthermore, decision curve analysis (DCA) and clinical impact curve (CIC) results supported its clinical utility for individualized risk stratification.ConclusionUtilizing the MIMIC-IV database, we constructed and validated a prognostic nomogram to predict 28-day mortality in critically ill patients with TE. Introduction

Toxic encephalopathy (TE) refers to a heterogeneous group of acute and chronic brain dysfunction syndromes caused by exposure to exogenous or endogenous neurotoxic substances, including drugs, heavy metals, industrial chemicals, or metabolic derangements [1–3]. Clinically, TE presents with diverse neurological symptoms, such as seizures, muscle weakness, lethargy, and coma [4, 5]. In severe cases, these symptoms may progress to irreversible cognitive impairment or death if not recognized and managed promptly [6].

The prognosis of TE is influenced by multiple factors, including exposure severity and duration, the timeliness and efficacy of interventions, as well as individual physiological and genetic predispositions. Although therapeutic advances have improved outcomes to some extent, TE remains associated with considerable disability and mortality [7]. In intensive care unit (ICU) populations, Sun et al. reported a 28-day mortality of 21.9% among TE patients, further highlighting the substantial risk of early death in this group [1]. Neuroimaging abnormalities in toxic or metabolic encephalopathy often provide valuable insights into disease onset and prognosis [8]. However, the clinical presentation of TE is highly variable and nonspecific, which complicates early diagnosis and prognostic evaluation [9].

Accurate early risk stratification is therefore crucial to guide clinical decision-making in patients with TE. Early identification of patients at high risk of short-term mortality may allow for more aggressive interventions, closer monitoring, and rational allocation of critical care resources. Conventional prognostic approaches rely on clinical judgment, neurological examination, and general severity scoring systems [10, 11]. However, these methods may not fully capture the specific pathophysiological processes of TE and often lack precision in predicting individual outcomes.

With the growth of large-scale clinical datasets and advances in statistical and machine learning techniques, predictive models have emerged as valuable tools to support evidence-based clinical decisions [12]. Prognostic tools have been developed and validated for sepsis, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and traumatic brain injury (TBI), demonstrating their potential to improve individualized outcome prediction [13, 14]. Yet, few studies have focused on TE, and robust tools for short-term prognostication in this population remain scarce.

Based on this gap, the present study aimed to construct and validate an early warning model to predict 28-day mortality in patients with TE. By integrating demographic, clinical, and laboratory parameters, we sought to provide a reliable and practical tool to assist clinicians in early risk stratification and individualized treatment planning.

Materials and methods

Sources of data

Data were sourced from the publicly accessible MIMIC-IV 3.1 database, curated by the MIT Computational Physiology Laboratory (https://physionet.org/content/mimiciv/3.1), which encompasses comprehensive records of all admissions to the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2022. Following completion of the required training initiative course, the authors obtained access permission (Certificate No. 61774601). The study was reported in accordance with the TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis) statement [15].

Study population

The study enrolled adult patients with a diagnosis of TE, as defined by ICD-9 code 349.82 or ICD-10 codes G92, G92.8, and G92.9. The distribution of TE-related ICD codes is shown in Supplementary Table S1. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) age < 18 years; (2) non-first ICU admission; and (3) ICU length of stay < 24 h. The primary outcome was 28-day mortality after ICU admission.

Data collection

Patient data were obtained from the MIMIC-IV database and comprised the following categories: (1) demographic and clinical characteristics, including age, gender, weight, heart rate (HR), respiratory rate (RR), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), body temperature, and comorbidities such as acute kidney injury (AKI), diabetes, hyperlipidemia (HLP), heart failure (HF), coronary heart disease (CHD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) and sepsis; (2) laboratory parameters, including hemoglobin, platelet count, white blood cell (WBC), albumin, glucose, lactate, partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2), pH, partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2), international normalized ratio (INR), prothrombin time (PT), activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), creatinine (Cr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN); (3) illness severity scores, including Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) and Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score; (4) length of ICU stay (LOS ICU). All variables were collected within the first 24 h following ICU admission. For laboratory and physiological measurements with multiple records during this period, the first available value within the first 24 h was used for analysis.

Missing data handling

Although demographic information is generally well documented in the MIMIC-IV database, missing data are common for laboratory and physiological variables. It would be highly inappropriate to exclude patients with incomplete variable data, as this would introduce significant bias into the study. Given the absence of a universally accepted threshold for missing values, we prespecified to exclude variables with more than 30% missing data. Missing values were assumed to be missing at random and were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations. Details regarding the missingness of candidate variables are provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Sample size

As this was a retrospective study based on the MIMIC-IV database, all eligible patients meeting the inclusion and exclusion criteria were included, and no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. To assess the adequacy of the sample size for model development and the potential risk of overfitting, the events per variable (EPV) was calculated as the number of outcome events in the training cohort divided by the number of predictors retained in the final multivariable model. In the training cohort, 386 patients died within 28 days. The final multivariable model included eight predictors, resulting in an EPV of 48.25. This value was well above the commonly recommended minimum threshold of 10 events per variable, suggesting that the number of outcome events was sufficient for model development and that the risk of overfitting due to sparse events was relatively low.

Statistical analysis

The final patient cohort was split into training and validation sets in a 7:3 ratio. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median [interquartile range] and compared using the Student’s t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. Categorical variables are expressed as numbers (percentages) and analyzed with the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were used to identify risk factors for 28-day mortality and to construct a predictive nomogram. The model was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves to assess discriminative ability, calibration curve to examine agreement between observed and predicted outcomes, and decision curve analysis (DCA) to quantify clinical utility [16]. Clinical impact curves (CIC) were generated to illustrate the number of high-risk individuals and true-positive patients across various probability thresholds.

Data were analyzed using SPSS 27.0 and R language (version 4.1.3). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Characteristics of patients

Initially, we identified 6196 TE patients who had ICU admission. After applying the exclusion criteria, including age under 18 years (n = 0), non-first ICU admission (n = 2,686), and ICU length of stay < 24 h (n = 471), a total of 3157 patients were excluded. The final cohort consisted of 3039 eligible patients (Fig. 1), with a median age of 70 years, including 1344 (44.2%) women and 1695 (55.8%) men. Baseline characteristics are detailed in Table 1. Baseline characteristics were generally comparable between the training and validation cohorts. However, significant differences were observed in the proportions of T2DM, T1DM, and HF. A total of 526 patients (17.3%) died within 28 days of ICU admission.

Development of nomogram

Univariate logistic regression identified 24 variables significantly associated with 28-day mortality, including age, HR, SBP, DBP, RR, hemoglobin, platelet, WBC, albumin, lactate, pH, PaO2, INR, PT, APTT, Cr, BUN, SOFA score, GCS, APACHE II, hypertension, AKI, HF, and sepsis (Table 2). Multivariable logistic regression analysis further identified eight independent predictors of 28-day mortality in patients with TE, which were used to develop the nomogram: age (OR: 1.022, 1.013–1.032), HR (OR: 1.007, 1.001–1.013), hemoglobin (OR: 0.904, 0.852–0.958), albumin (OR: 0.677, 0.546–0.836), PaO2 (OR: 0.998, 0.996–0.999), SOFA score (OR: 1.094, 1.043–1.148), APACHE II (OR: 1.038, 1.011–1.066), and AKI (OR: 1.520, 1.132–2.501) (Table 3; Fig. 2).

Validation of the nomogram

The nomogram was validated using 1000 bootstrap samples. The ROC curve analysis demonstrated acceptable discriminative ability for 28-day mortality in TE patients, achieving an AUC of 0.750 (95% CI: 0.725–0.776) in the training cohort (Fig. 3A) and 0.757 (95% CI: 0.715–0.799) in the validation cohort (Fig. 3B), exceeding 0.7 in both cases. Calibration curves, which plot observed against predicted probabilities (Fig. 4), showed generally satisfactory agreement between predicted and observed probabilities.

Clinical application

DCA evaluates the clinical value of a diagnostic tool by calculating the net benefit at various threshold probabilities. As shown in Fig. 5A and B, the nomogram provided a higher net benefit than both the treat-all and treat-none strategies across threshold probabilities of 0.01–0.56 in the training cohort and 0.01–0.65 in the validation cohort. Moreover, the clinical impact curve (CIC) demonstrated that the number of patients classified as high-risk closely matched the observed 28-day mortality within these thresholds (Fig. 6A and B). These findings suggest that the prediction model has potential clinical applicability. CIC analyses in both the training and validation cohorts further confirmed the model’s effectiveness in identifying patients at risk.

Discussion

Toxic encephalopathy represents a clinically heterogeneous syndrome with a broad spectrum of aetiologies and variable short-term prognosis. Despite sometimes reversible aetiologies, critically ill patients with TE continue to experience substantial morbidity and mortality, placing a heavy burden on families and health systems [7]. Early recognition of neurologic dysfunction is associated with improved survival and an increased likelihood of awakening, thereby altering the clinical trajectory of brain dysfunction. Thy et al. demonstrated that in patients with severe sepsis-associated encephalopathy, the persistence of systemic insults during the early ICU phase was associated with increased mortality and decreased chances of awakening [17]. Currently, there is no unified or specific standard for the early identification of high-risk TE patients in the ICU. Our study addresses this gap by developing a parsimonious nomogram that integrates readily available demographic, physiologic, and laboratory indicators to predict 28-day mortality, and demonstrates acceptable discriminative performance in both training and validation cohorts derived from the MIMIC-IV database.

In this retrospective study, we analyzed 3,039 ICU patients with TE. Using demographic characteristics, laboratory parameters, critical illness scores, and complications of these patients, we performed univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses to identify independent risk factors. Based on these factors, a nomogram was developed to predict 28-day mortality in critically ill TE patients. The final model included eight significant predictors: age, heart rate, hemoglobin, albumin, PaO2, SOFA score, APACHE II score, and AKI. This nomogram demonstrated acceptable predictive accuracy, achieving an AUC greater than 0.7 in both the training and validation cohorts. Following validation using multiple approaches, the model exhibited good calibration, discriminative ability, and clinical applicability. These predictors should not be interpreted as TE-specific biomarkers. Instead, they likely reflect the combined burden of general critical illness, impaired oxygen delivery, nutritional and inflammatory status, and multi-organ dysfunction in critically ill patients with TE.

Our analysis confirms that older age is associated with an elevated risk of 28-day mortality among critically ill patients with TE, a finding consistent with previous research in general critical care and acute encephalopathy populations. A cohort study of 619 brain-injured ICU patients demonstrated that age ≥ 60 years was an independent predictor of mortality and unfavorable outcomes [18]. Physiologically, aging is associated with diminished neuronal plasticity and repair capacity, which renders the brain more susceptible to irreversible damage under toxic or metabolic stress [19]. In addition, elderly patients often present with multiple comorbidities, such as cardiovascular disease, renal dysfunction, and malnutrition, which compromise systemic oxygen delivery, inflammatory regulation, and immune response, thereby exacerbating cerebral injury. Furthermore, tolerance to standard ICU interventions, including sedation, mechanical ventilation, and fluid management, is generally reduced in older patients, increasing the risk of treatment-related complications. Prior studies have consistently reported age as an independent risk factor for short-term mortality in sepsis-associated encephalopathy and critically ill brain-injured patients, as well as in COVID-19–related encephalopathy [20].

Similarly, elevated heart rate was identified as an independent risk factor for 28-day mortality in this population. This finding is consistent with evidence from a large MIMIC-III-based cohort study showing that prolonged elevated heart rate was significantly associated with decreased 90-day survival among acutely ill patients [21]. In those with sepsis or metabolic encephalopathy, tachycardia often accompanies neuroinflammation, autonomic dysregulation, and sympathetic overactivation [22]. In patients with toxic encephalopathy, neurotoxic insults can impair central autonomic control, leading to persistent elevation of heart rate. While this compensatory response may initially support cardiac output and tissue perfusion, sustained tachycardia can increase myocardial oxygen consumption, exacerbate hemodynamic instability, and worsen organ dysfunction, thereby contributing to higher mortality [23].

The prognostic significance of biochemical markers is also evident in our results. Statistically significant differences in hemoglobin and albumin levels were observed between the deceased and surviving groups, indicating that lower values are associated with increased 28-day mortality. This observation aligns with recent ICU studies. Rabi et al. reported in a retrospective study of 300 ICU patients that hypoalbuminemia was independently associated with higher overall and 28-day mortality even after adjustment for age, comorbidities, and disease severity [24]. Similarly, among patients requiring continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT), those with serum albumin ≤ 2.4 g/dL had significantly worse outcomes (90-day mortality) than those with albumin ≥ 3.5 g/dL. A recent cohort study further substantiates the prognostic value of hemoglobin levels in this vulnerable population. The study demonstrated that among patients receiving venoarterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (VA-ECMO) support following cardiotomy, lower hemoglobin levels on the first day of ECMO were independently associated with significantly increased in-hospital mortality, with each unit decrease in hemoglobin corresponding to an approximately 19% elevation in the adjusted odds of death [25]. Anaemia reduces systemic oxygen delivery and may potentiate cerebral hypoxia in patients whose neurocognitive reserve is already compromised by toxic/metabolic insults; observational data consistently link lower hemoglobin with worse in-hospital outcomes and ventilator liberation failure in critical care cohorts [26]. Hypoalbuminemia, in turn, is a well-recognized marker of malnutrition, systemic inflammation, and capillary leak; low albumin has been independently associated with higher ICU and hospital mortality in multiple settings, and may aggravate cerebral edema or reflect a host phenotype less able to tolerate multi-organ stressors [27]. These findings suggest that incorporation of these routinely available indicators into the nomogram may help clinicians identify TE patients with a higher overall risk burden and prioritize closer surveillance and earlier reassessment.

Impaired oxygenation also emerged as a critical prognostic factor. Our study demonstrated that lower PaO2 was significantly associated with increased 28-day mortality in patients with TE. Hypoxemia likely exacerbates cerebral injury by reducing oxygen delivery, promoting anaerobic metabolism, and impairing mitochondrial function, thereby accelerating neuronal death. It may also indicate systemic organ dysfunction, such as respiratory failure or circulatory instability, further contributing to poor outcomes. Furthermore, recent studies have demonstrated a U-shaped relationship between oxygen saturation and mortality in patients with acute brain injury, emphasizing that both hypoxemia and hyperoxemia are associated with increased risk [28, 29]. Clinically, early identification and management of hypoxemia—through supplemental oxygen, mechanical ventilation, or ECMO—may help reduce secondary injury and improve survival. The strong association between AKI and mortality in our cohort is consistent with a growing body of evidence that kidney dysfunction substantially increases short-term risk in critically ill populations [30–32]. In-hospital mortality approaches 27% in critically ill patients with AKI, and rises to 45–60% when accompanied by other organ dysfunctions such as pneumonia, acute heart failure, or sepsis [33, 34]. Our results further underscore the importance of evaluating and managing renal insufficiency in critically ill patients with TE to improve their prognosis. AKI is not only a marker of disease severity but also a contributor to systemic complications through mechanisms such as fluid overload, accumulation of uremic toxins, and impaired acid–base balance [35]. In addition, AKI is closely linked to the activation of systemic inflammation and oxidative stress, which may aggravate neuronal injury and contribute to encephalopathy progression [35, 36]. Accumulation of nitrogenous waste products due to impaired renal clearance and ongoing metabolic production may lead to uremic toxin–mediated endothelial injury, neurotoxicity, and cognitive dysfunction [37]. AKI may mediate worse outcomes via accumulation of neurotoxic metabolites, deranged fluid balance and acid-base milieu, immune dysfunction, and increased need for renal replacement therapy — mechanisms particularly harmful in the context of TE, where cerebral homeostasis is fragile [38]. Therefore, early identification and intervention for AKI in TE patients may mitigate secondary organ dysfunction, reduce the burden of systemic inflammation, and ultimately improve short-term outcomes.

Our predictive nomogram achieved AUC values exceeding 0.7 in both cohorts, indicating acceptable discriminative performance. To our knowledge, few prediction models have been developed to estimate 28-day mortality in critically ill patients with TE. By using the large MIMIC-IV database, our study provides a practical nomogram that may help address this gap in early risk stratification. By integrating demographic, physiological, and laboratory indicators, the model enables clinicians to identify high-risk patients early and implement targeted management strategies, which may support individualized care and clinical decision-making [39].

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as the analysis was based on a single database, the findings may not be fully generalizable to other populations, particularly given differences in toxin exposures, case mix, and healthcare resources. Second, due to the retrospective observational design, this study was subject to inherent biases, including potential selection bias, information bias, and unmeasured confounding. Therefore, this study could only identify associations between predictors and 28-day mortality, and causal relationships could not be established. Third, reliance on ICD coding for case identification introduces a risk of misclassification, as toxic encephalopathy is a heterogeneous condition with variable etiologies that may carry distinct prognoses. Fourth, certain disease-specific biomarkers and diagnostic modalities, such as neuroimaging findings, EEG patterns, or toxin measurements, were unavailable and thus not included in the model. Fifth, this study focused only on 28-day mortality, and long-term outcomes such as 90-day mortality, functional recovery, neurological sequelae, and quality of life were not evaluated. Finally, although internal validation was conducted, external validation in independent cohorts and prospective clinical settings is required before the model can be applied in practice. In addition, the calibration curves suggested some degree of miscalibration, with overestimation at lower predicted risks and underestimation at higher predicted risks. Therefore, the predicted probabilities should be interpreted cautiously, particularly among patients at the extremes of predicted risk.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed and validated a nomogram model to predict 28-day mortality in critically ill patients with toxic encephalopathy using the large MIMIC-IV database. The model incorporates age, heart rate, hemoglobin, albumin, PaO2, SOFA score, APACHE II score, and acute kidney injury. It showed acceptable discrimination and generally satisfactory calibration, reflecting the multifactorial determinants of early mortality in this population. Its application can assist clinicians in identifying high-risk patients with toxic encephalopathy, optimizing monitoring intensity, and guiding timely interventions, thereby potentially improving patient outcomes.
