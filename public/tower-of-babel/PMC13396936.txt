Characterization of preferentially expressed antigen in melanoma (PRAME) expression in pigmented actinic keratoses and pigmented squamous cell carcinomas in situ
Authors: Tyler D. Coolman, Megan V. Ha, Bethany R. Rohr
Published: 2026

To the Editor: Preferentially expressed antigen in melanoma (PRAME) is a tumor-associated antigen that is used as a diagnostic tool for melanoma.1 Commercially available gene expression profile tests (GEP) assess PRAME expression in both formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue and noninvasive sampling (ie DermTech) to help distinguish benign from malignant pigmented lesions.2,3 DermTech uses a tape stripping technique on pigmented lesions in vivo to detect RNA genomic markers LINC00518 and PRAME, guiding decision to biopsy.2,3 Studies have demonstrated that PRAME may be expressed in nonmelanocytic neoplasms and may show incidental positivity in junctional melanocytes on sun-damaged skin.4,5 This raises the concern that GEP testing may lead to over-biopsy of clinically pigmented nonmelanocytic lesions, as evidenced by studies that demonstrate false positive GEP testing in nonmelanocytic lesions.5 We sought to characterize the immunohistochemical expression of PRAME in lesional keratinocytes and melanocytes of pigmented actinic keratoses (pAK) and pigmented squamous cell carcinomas in situ (pSCCIS) to determine whether this could contribute to false positive GEP testing for these lesions.

We retrospectively identified 52 cases of pAK and 52 cases of pSCCIS at University Hospitals Cleveland Medical Center. Cases were submitted as pigmented lesions clinically and confirmed microscopically. Sections were taken from formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded and stained with commercially available PRAME antibody (Rabbit Monoclonal Antibody EPR20330; Roche). Similar to prior studies,4 we assessed PRAME expression quantitively (0%, 1% to 24%, 25% to 49%, >50% of cells) (Fig 1). The incidental staining of dermo-epidermal junction (DEJ) melanocytes with PRAME was assessed qualitatively (weak or strong expression). Only nuclear staining was considered specific and scored as positive. Cytoplasmic staining was not included in PRAME assessment. In cases where PRAME expression using red chromagen were equivocal due to significant background staining, PRAME was repeated with brown chromagen and re-evaluated by a blinded reviewer. Overall, 22% (11/52) of pAKs and 8% (6/52) of pSCCISs exhibited any degree of PRAME expression in lesional keratinocytes (Table I). All of these cases exhibited a low proportion of PRAME expression: 1% to 24%. Fifty percent (26/52) of pAKs showed PRAME staining of DEJ melanocytes, comprised of 13% (7/52) with weak and 37% (19/52) with strong staining. Our findings demonstrate low-level PRAME staining in keratinocytes of pAK and pSCCIS. No cases showed strong nuclear keratinocyte staining, and no cases met the conventional ≥50% cutoff for PRAME positivity. This parallels findings by Elsenjohn et al,4 who noted that among 10 cases of well-differentiated SCC, 20% showed PRAME expression. Expression of PRAME by DEJ melanocytes was found in half of pAK cases and a third of pSCCIS cases, supporting prior findings that PRAME-positive junctional melanocytes may be associated with sun-damaged skin.1,4 We hypothesize that low-level PRAME expression in pAK and pSCCIS keratinocytes and/or lesional melanocytes could be a potential mechanism contributing to positive GEP testing in clinically pigmented nonmelanocytic lesions.5 Limitations of our study include intrarater variability of a single dermatopathologist in grading. Additionally, PRAME staining had to be repeated in 22% of cases, raising concern for the original assay robustness.
