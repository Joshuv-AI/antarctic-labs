The association between preoperative serum transferrin and occult metastasis in early T classification oral squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Li Tan, Tian-cheng Jiang, Zhao-jian Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the potential of preoperative serum transferrin levels as a biomarker for predicting occult metastasis in patients with clinically node-negative (cN0), early T-classification (T1-T2) oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC).MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 386 eligible patients with cN0 early-stage OSCC who underwent primary tumor resection and elective neck dissection at the Second Xiangya Hospital, Changsha, China. Preoperative serum transferrin levels were measured, and their association with occult metastasis (determined by postoperative pathology) was analyzed. Statistical analyses included univariate and multivariable logistic regression, restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis for nonlinear relationships, subgroup analyses, and predictive performance metrics (AUC, calibration, decision curve analysis).ResultsOccult metastasis was identified in 104 patients (26.9%). Serum transferrin levels did not differ significantly between patients with and without metastasis (2.16 ± 0.37 vs. 2.14 ± 0.35 g/L, P = 0.693). On multivariable analysis, transferrin was not associated with occult metastasis (P > 0.05), whereas T classification and poorly differentiated histology emerged as significant predictors. RCS analysis confirmed a linear, non-significant relationship. None of the 27 subgroups showed significant differences. Adding transferrin to the baseline model did not improve AUC (unchanged at 0.637), net benefit, or calibration.ConclusionsPreoperative serum transferrin level was not associated with occult metastasis in early-stage, cN0 OSCC. Tumor-related factors, specifically differentiation grade and T classification, remained the primary predictors of metastatic risk. Serum transferrin does not appear to be a useful independent biomarker for preoperative metastasis risk stratification in this patient population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08895-2.Keywords: Oral squamous cell carcinoma, Oral cancer, Occult metastasis, Neck metastasis, Serum transferrin, Biomarkers

Introduction

The management of early T-classification (T1-T2) oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) presents a significant clinical challenge. This is largely due to the high incidence of occult metastases, which occur in approximately 20–40% of cases despite negative clinical neck examination [1, 2]. The unique anatomical characteristics of the oral cavity, particularly the tongue’s rich bilateral lymphatic drainage system, contribute to distinct metastatic patterns that differ from those in other parts of the body [3]. This specialized lymphatic architecture facilitates early and often unpredictable nodal spread, underscoring the need for predictive tools in OSCC.

Preoperative imaging techniques (contrast-enhanced CT, MRI, and PET-CT) show limited sensitivity for detecting micro-metastases in early-stage OSCC, with false-negative rates of 20–30% [4]. Several key histopathological risk factors for lymph node metastasis, such as lymphovascular invasion and depth of tumor invasion, are generally confirmed only through postoperative pathology [5]. Since these critical data points are unavailable preoperatively, they cannot inform surgical decision-making at the time of planning. For OSCC patients with occult nodal metastases, clinical mismanagement (e.g., observation instead of elective neck dissection) can lead to disease recurrence and significantly reduced survival [6–8]. Conversely, overtreatment in patients without occult metastases, through unnecessary elective neck dissection, may result in impaired quality of life [9]. This clinical dilemma underscores the need to identify biomarkers that, in combination with clinical data, aim to improve the preoperative assessment of metastatic risk in OSCC.

In this context, serum biomarkers offer a promising, minimally invasive avenue for preoperatively stratifying the risk of occult metastasis in early-stage OSCC [10, 11]. Among candidate molecules, transferrin, a liver-synthesized iron‑transport protein [12] that is routinely measured at low cost in clinical practice for nutritional status [13], may serve as a potential serum biomarker in OSCC.

Several lines of evidence support the rationale for investigating serum transferrin in early‑stage OSCC. First, direct biological evidence has established that circulating transferrin can actively promote cancer metastasis. Cheng et al. demonstrated that transferrin binds to the TCRα constant domain, disrupts TCR‑CD3 signaling, suppresses T cell activation, and drives liver metastasis in mouse models [14]—providing proof‑of‑concept that transferrin is not merely a nutritional marker but a functional promoter of metastatic progression. Second, several OSCC‑specific observations show clear involvement of the transferrin system in this disease. Mumtaz et al. reported that transferrin‑related proteins, including its receptor TFRC, are among the most upregulated proteins in OSCC tissues [15], and Si et al. identified a TANs‑TF‑TFRC axis that promotes OSCC cell migration and proliferation [16]. Additionally, salivary transferrin levels are elevated in OSCC patients and correlate with tumor size and stage, indicating local dysregulation [17, 18]. Mechanistically, it has been established that blood proteins can enter saliva via passive diffusion through the blood–saliva barrier and via leakage through the gingival crevicular epithelium [19]. Furthermore, a separate study has shown that transferrin itself can be transported from blood to saliva through similar routes [20]. It is therefore plausible that serum transferrin levels may influence salivary transferrin levels, raising the possibility that the two are correlated to some extent, although this hypothesis requires direct validation in OSCC patients. Together, these findings raise the question of whether preoperative serum transferrin level—a routinely available, low‑cost parameter—could reflect metastatic risk in early‑stage OSCC, warranting evaluation as a potential preoperative predictor. Accordingly, this study is designed as an exploratory, hypothesis-screening analysis to assess this association.

Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to elucidate the relationship between preoperative serum transferrin levels and the presence of occult metastases in patients with early-stage (T1-T2), clinically node-negative (cN0) OSCC. We hypothesize that preoperative serum transferrin levels differ significantly between patients with (pN+) and without (pN0) pathologically confirmed nodal metastasis following elective neck dissection. By quantifying this association, we aim to determine whether serum transferrin can serve as a valuable independent or complementary biomarker. The ultimate goal is to assess the value of serum transferrin, either independently or in conjunction with existing clinical parameters, to improve preoperative risk assessment for occult metastasis. Given the retrospective and observational nature of this study, we aim to evaluate this association without inferring causality. Identifying such a biomarker could aid clinical decision-making by identifying which cN0 patients are most likely to benefit from elective neck dissection and which can be safely reassured.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of the Second Xiangya Hospital, Central South University (No. LYF20250221) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2008). Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study. A total of 386 patients with clinically early-stage (T1 or T2) OSCC who were clinically node-negative (cN0) were included. All patients underwent primary tumor resection along with elective neck dissection between October 2022 and October 2025. Ethics approval was obtained in February 2025 (No. LYF20250221), and data were retrospectively collected from electronic medical records thereafter. The data lock date was set as October 2025, corresponding to the end of the inclusion period.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histologically confirmed primary OSCC; (2) preoperative clinical staging of cN0, determined by physical examination and imaging (ultrasound, CT, or MRI); (3) scheduled to undergo primary tumor resection plus elective neck dissection; and (4) age ≥ 18 years. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a history of radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or surgery in the head and neck region (excluding diagnostic biopsy); (2) a history of other malignancies; (3) conditions that could influence inflammatory or iron metabolism markers, such as acute or chronic infections, chronic inflammatory diseases, autoimmune disorders, hematological diseases, or severe hepatic dysfunction; (4) recent use (within 1 month) of corticosteroids, NSAIDs, immunosuppressants, anticoagulants, or antiplatelet agents; and (5) Patients with a documented diagnosis of HPV-positive oropharyngeal carcinoma, based on clinical records. This selection process resulted in a final analytical cohort of 386 patients with clinically early-stage (T1 or T2), node-negative (cN0) OSCC. Tumor staging was performed according to the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 7th Edition.

Data collection

All primary analyses were based exclusively on preoperatively available variables. Postoperative variables were collected only for a secondary explanatory purpose. Primary Outcome: Occult metastasis status, determined by postoperative histopathological examination of neck dissection specimens, and classified as pN + or pN0. All neck dissection specimens were processed according to the routine histopathological protocol of our institution (visible lymph node retrieval, step‑sectioning at 2‑mm intervals if no nodes grossly identifiable, paraffin embedding, 4 μm sectioning, and H&E staining). The slides were examined by two board‑certified pathologists specializing in head and neck pathology as part of routine clinical diagnosis; the original pathology reports were signed after consensus review. For this retrospective study, outcome classification (pN + vs. pN0) was extracted directly from these reports. This standardized dual‑review process ensured consistent and reliable lymph node evaluation. Primary Predictor: Preoperative serum transferrin level (g/L). Serum transferrin data were obtained from routine preoperative clinical testing extracted from the hospital’s electronic medical record system (EMRS). Blood samples were collected upon hospital admission, and surgery was performed within one week thereafter following completion of all necessary preoperative examinations. Transferrin levels were determined from a single blood sample collected at admission. Patients were instructed to fast for at least 8 h before blood collection in accordance with standard preoperative protocols. Individuals with clinical evidence of acute infection or inflammation at the time of blood draw (fever > 38 °C or white blood cell count > 11.0 × 10⁹/L) were excluded as specified in the exclusion criteria. Transferrin concentrations were determined by immunoturbidimetry using a Beckman Coulter Immage 800 analyzer with the Beckman Coulter HPT reagent (catalog number RET 446480; each kit contains 4 mL of reagent A and 4 mL of reagent B). All reagents and consumables were provided by the original manufacturer. The assay was conducted strictly following the standard operating procedure: serum samples were analyzed at a detection wavelength of 340 nm and a reaction temperature of 37 °C, with the instrument automatically performing sample dilution and result reading. Daily calibration and quality control were carried out to ensure accuracy. Routine internal quality control (commercial controls with known transferrin concentrations) was performed daily, and all quality metrics met the laboratory’s acceptance criteria. All steps complied with the instrument and reagent instructions, ensuring the reliability and reproducibility of the results. Secondary Variables: Histopathological features: perineural/lymphovascular invasion, and tumor differentiation (well, moderate, poor). Demographic and clinical data: age, gender, smoking history, alcohol consumption, betel nut use, tumor site, clinical T stage, marital status, diabetes, hypertension and cardiovascular disease.

Statistical analysis

The main analyses were based on a preoperative prediction model using only preoperatively available variables. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.0) and SPSS (version 26.0). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using independent samples t-tests. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, and group differences were assessed using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify factors associated with occult metastasis. To address the study objectives, two multivariable logistic regression models were constructed: ໿ Model 1 (Preoperative prediction model): This model aimed to evaluate the predictive value of serum transferrin using only variables available before surgery. Covariates included age, sex, smoking history, alcohol consumption, betel nut chewing, hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease, primary tumor site, and T classification. Marital status was excluded due to its limited clinical relevance and to avoid overfitting. ໿ Model 2 (Postoperative explanatory model): This model assessed whether serum transferrin remained independently associated with occult metastasis after adjusting for established pathological risk factors. In addition to the preoperative variables in Model 1, postoperative variables—differentiation grade and perineural/lymphovascular invasion—were included. Results were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The selection of covariates was guided by established literature demonstrating their association with OSCC prognosis or lymph node metastasis [21–23], ensuring that the models accounted for relevant clinical and pathological factors while maintaining clinical interpretability. To evaluate the incremental predictive value of serum transferrin beyond conventional clinical factors, we assessed the discriminative ability, calibration, and clinical utility of the models. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated for both Model 1 (without transferrin) and the extended model (Model 1 plus transferrin). Calibration curves were generated to examine the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes, using 10 bins. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to quantify the net benefit of each model across a range of clinically reasonable threshold probabilities (0.01–0.50). All predictive performance metrics were reported according to the TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis) statement. To assess the robustness of our findings and address concerns regarding potential overfitting, we performed a sensitivity analysis using a more parsimonious set of covariates. In this analysis, we retained only the core clinical variables (age, sex, primary tumor site, T classification, and serum transferrin) and removed lifestyle factors (smoking, alcohol, betel nut) and comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease). Sample size adequacy for logistic regression was confirmed using the events‑per‑variable rule (≥ 10 events per variable). The main preoperative logistic regression model (10 covariates, 104 events) yielded an EPV of 10.4, and the sensitivity logistic regression model (5 core covariates, 104 events) yielded an EPV of 20.8, both meeting the recommended threshold. To examine the potential nonlinear relationship between serum transferrin levels and occult metastasis risk, we performed restricted cubic spline (RCS) analysis with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the transferrin distribution. Nonlinearity was tested using a likelihood ratio test. Both unadjusted and adjusted RCS analyses were conducted, with the adjusted model including the same preoperative covariates as in the main multivariable logistic regression. Subgroup analyses were performed to evaluate the consistency of the association between transferrin levels and occult metastasis across various demographic, clinical, and pathological subgroups. Violin plots and line plots with 95% CIs were used to visualize the distribution and mean differences of transferrin levels between pN0 and pN+ groups within each subgroup. For subgroup analyses, a Bonferroni‑corrected significance level of α = 0.05/27 ≈ 0.00185 was considered (based on 27 subgroup comparisons). Independent samples t-tests were applied to compare transferrin levels between groups in each subgroup. A two-tailed P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses, except where otherwise specified.

Demographic analysis revealed no significant differences between the two groups in terms of age distribution, sex, marital status, smoking history, alcohol consumption, betel nut chewing, hypertension, diabetes, or cardiovascular disease (all P > 0.05). The majority of patients were male and married (Table 1).

Significant disparities were observed in several key clinicopathological parameters. Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses of occult metastasis in OSCC

Model 2 (postoperative model, presented in Supplementary Table S1) additionally adjusted for postoperative pathological factors—differentiation grade and Perineural/lymphovascular invasion—along with all preoperative covariates. In this model, poorly differentiated histology emerged as a strong independent predictor of occult metastasis (vs. moderate differentiation, OR = 2.744, P = 0.010), and T1 classification retained its protective effect compared to T2 tumors (vs. T2, OR = 0.470, P = 0.006). Perineural/lymphovascular invasion did not maintain statistical significance after multivariable adjustment (P = 0.133), and serum transferrin remained non‑significant (P = 0.823). Detailed confidence intervals are presented in Table 2 and Supplementary Table S1. These findings indicate that tumor differentiation grade and T classification are independent predictors of occult metastasis in early‑stage OSCC, whereas preoperative serum transferrin levels do not provide predictive value beyond established clinical and pathological factors.

A sensitivity analysis using a more parsimonious set of covariates (excluding lifestyle factors—smoking, alcohol, betel nut—and comorbidities—hypertension, diabetes, cardiovascular disease) yielded consistent results. In this analysis, serum transferrin remained non‑significant in the preoperative model (OR = 1.055, P = 0.875) (Supplementary Figure S1) and in the postoperative model (OR = 0.953, P = 0.890) (Supplementary Figure S2). T classification and poorly differentiated histology remained significant predictors, with effect sizes similar to the primary analysis (Supplementary Figure S1 and S2).

RCS analysis was performed with four knots placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the transferrin distribution. Subgroup analysis of serum transferrin levels and occult metastasis in OSCC

After applying Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons (corrected α = 0.00185), none of the 27 subgroup analyses showed a statistically significant difference in serum transferrin levels between pN0 and pN+ patients; detailed results for all subgroups, including violin plots and line plots, are provided in Supplementary Figure S3.

Across demographic subgroups stratified by age (median 54 years), sex, smoking history, alcohol consumption, and betel nut chewing, serum transferrin levels were comparable between pN0 and pN+ patients, with all P values exceeding 0.05. Similarly, no significant differences were observed in subgroups defined by comorbidities including hypertension, diabetes, and cardiovascular disease.

Analysis of tumor characteristics, including primary tumor site, differentiation grade, perineural/lymphovascular invasion status, and T classification, also revealed no statistically significant associations with serum transferrin levels. The consistency of these null findings across all examined subgroups further supports the conclusion that preoperative serum transferrin is not associated with occult metastasis in early-stage OSCC.

Predictive performance of models for occult metastasis

To evaluate the incremental predictive value of serum transferrin beyond conventional clinical factors, we compared the discriminative ability, calibration, and clinical utility of a baseline preoperative model (without transferrin) and an extended model (baseline plus transferrin). As shown in Supplementary Figure S4A (ROC curves), the baseline model achieved an AUC of 0.637, and adding transferrin yielded an identical AUC of 0.637 (ΔAUC = 0.000), indicating no improvement in discrimination. Decision curve analysis (Supplementary Figure S4B) demonstrated that the net benefit curves of the two models were nearly identical across all threshold probabilities (0.01–0.50). Below a threshold of 0.2, both models closely overlapped with the “treat all” strategy. Between thresholds of 0.2 and 0.5, the two models gradually diverged and remained above the “treat all” curve; net benefit remained positive throughout. Adding transferrin did not provide any meaningful incremental clinical utility. The calibration curves (Supplementary Figure S4C) showed systematic deviation from the ideal diagonal: the baseline model exhibited no significant under‑ or over‑prediction across the risk spectrum, while the extended model showed over‑prediction at a predicted risk of approximately 0.54. Adding transferrin did not improve calibration. Consistent with the multivariate regression results (OR = 1.027, 95% CI 0.527–2.002, P = 0.938), these predictive performance metrics collectively confirm that serum transferrin does not improve preoperative risk stratification for occult lymph node metastasis in OSCC.

Discussion

Our study conducted a comprehensive, multi-modal analysis to evaluate the association between preoperative serum transferrin levels and occult metastasis in patients with clinically cN0, early T-classification (T1–T2) OSCC. Contrary to our initial hypothesis, we found no significant association between serum transferrin and occult metastasis. This finding was consistent across univariate, multivariable, RCS, extensive subgroup analyses, and predictive performance metrics (AUC unchanged, no improvement in net benefit or calibration), suggesting that circulating transferrin does not serve as a reliable independent biomarker for preoperative metastatic risk stratification in this specific clinical context.

Transferrin is essential for cellular proliferation, and previous in vitro studies have demonstrated that it promotes malignant growth [24]. In certain malignancies, serum transferrin has also shown clinical utility; for example, in patients with colorectal liver metastases, preoperative serum transferrin independently predicts poor overall survival [25]. However, the relationship between circulating transferrin and cancer progression varies across tumor types. One study reported that serum transferrin was significantly decreased in patients with gastrointestinal cancers compared with healthy controls, whereas no such difference was observed in other malignancies [26]. This heterogeneity underscores the necessity of cancer‑specific validation for candidate biomarkers. In the context of early‑stage OSCC, our negative finding provides a compelling example: an association observed in other malignancies does not necessarily translate to OSCC.

The consistent null associations across various subgroups, including those stratified by age, gender, lifestyle factors, comorbidities, and most tumor characteristics, further strengthen the conclusion that serum transferrin is not a major modulator of the risk of occult metastasis in early-stage OSCC. In contrast, our study reaffirmed the prognostic value of established tumor-related factors. Poor differentiation and T2 classification emerged as independent predictors of occult metastasis, consistent with prior literature [27, 28]. These factors, however, are typically confirmed postoperatively [29], underscoring the ongoing need for reliable preoperative biomarkers to guide surgical decision-making.

A key scientific insight from this study is the compartmentalization of transferrin as a biomarker in OSCC. Based on the established physiology that blood proteins, including transferrin, can enter saliva [19, 20], we initially hypothesized that serum transferrin levels might correlate with salivary transferrin and thereby reflect local tumor activity. Previous reports indeed showed that salivary transferrin is elevated in OSCC patients and correlates with tumor size and stage, indicating local dysregulation [17, 18]. Furthermore, recent mechanistic studies have demonstrated that transferrin can actively promote metastasis in other cancer contexts (e.g., by binding to TCRα and suppressing T cell activation) [14]. Nevertheless, our study demonstrates that serum transferrin does not predict occult metastasis. This contrast suggests that, in OSCC, local (salivary) and systemic (serum) transferrin do not necessarily have the same predictive performance, and that the pro‑metastatic functions of transferrin may be confined to the local tumor microenvironment rather than reflected by circulating levels. This discrepancy in biomarker levels between saliva and serum is supported by recent studies. For instance, one study reported significantly decreased lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels in the saliva of OSCC patients compared to controls, while serum LDH levels remained unchanged [30]. Similarly, other researchers have observed that Neutrophil Extracellular Traps (NETs) biomarkers were significantly decreased in OSCC patients versus controls, whereas serum NETs biomarkers were elevated [31]. More broadly, our findings add to the growing evidence that biomarker performance is often cancer‑type‑ and context‑specific: a marker that works in one malignancy or for one clinical application may not generalize to another. This underscores the necessity of rigorous, disease‑specific validation – a principle that our study exemplifies.

By rigorously demonstrating that serum transferrin is not a useful predictor in this setting, our study suggests that future research should focus on alternative biomarkers. We propose three specific directions that warrant immediate investigation: (i) local microenvironment markers – salivary transferrin itself, or other tumor‑associated proteins in saliva or tissue; (ii) functional iron metrics – transferrin saturation, ferritin, or soluble transferrin receptor, which may reflect iron availability and tumor metabolism more accurately than total transferrin [32]; and (iii) composite biomarker panels that integrate inflammatory, metabolic, and invasion‑related signals. These directions align with the growing emphasis on precision medicine and the need for non‑invasive, preoperative risk stratification tools. As recently highlighted by Noor et al., demonstrating the boundaries of a biomarker’s utility is itself an important scientific contribution – it refines hypotheses, prevents resource waste, and accelerates discovery [33]. Applied to serum transferrin, this principle offers an indirect clinical guidance: routine testing for occult metastasis prediction can be reasonably omitted, sparing unnecessary use of clinical resources without affecting patient management.

Our study has several limitations. First, its retrospective, single-center design may limit generalizability [34]; therefore, validation in multicenter cohorts is needed to confirm the applicability of our findings. Second, although we adjusted for numerous confounders, residual confounding cannot be excluded (e.g., nutritional or inflammatory markers). Third, the measurement of total transferrin does not capture its functional isoforms or iron-loading status [35], highlighting the need for future studies to incorporate additional markers such as transferrin saturation and ferritin to fully elucidate the role of iron homeostasis in OSCC progression. Fourth, due to the retrospective nature of this study, depth of invasion (DOI) was not uniformly recorded in pathology reports across the study period, reflecting routine clinical practice. Although this is a limitation, DOI is a postoperative parameter whereas our study focused exclusively on preoperative predictors; thus its absence does not materially affect our main findings. Even considering its postoperative predictive value, in early OSCC, T classification based on tumor dimension already largely overlaps with DOI in predicting metastatic risk. Salama et al. found that tumor thickness (closely related to T size) and DOI are near‑perfectly correlated (r = 0.984), with only 6% discordance, and both perform identically in predicting nodal metastasis and survival [36]. Therefore, we believe that T classification alone may capture most of the predictive information that DOI would provide, and the lack of DOI does not undermine our conclusion. Nevertheless, future prospective studies with standardized assessment of these pathological features are warranted. Fifth, serum transferrin was measured only once at admission (within one week before surgery). A single measurement does not capture potential temporal fluctuations or inter‑individual variability, which is inherent to our retrospective design and may have attenuated weak associations. Sixth, because we included only patients who underwent elective neck dissection, the cohort may not represent the broader population of clinically node‑negative OSCC patients, particularly those managed by observation or sentinel node biopsy. This selection was necessary to establish occult metastasis status (pN + vs. pN0) via the pathological gold standard, but it limits the external validity and generalizability of our findings. Seventh, although this study included nearly 400 participants, providing a relatively substantial sample size, it may still have been underpowered to detect very subtle effect differences when performing subgroup analyses. Future prospective multicenter studies with standardized laboratory assessments are warranted to validate our findings and further explore the potential role of iron metabolism in OSCC.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that preoperative serum transferrin level is not associated with occult metastasis in early-stage cN0 OSCC. Tumor differentiation grade and T stage remain the cornerstone predictors of metastatic risk.
