A Case of Wide Complex Tachycardia With Right Bundle Branch Block—Electrocardiographic Features to Distinguish Supraventricular Tachycardia With Aberrancy Versus Ventricular Tachycardia
Authors: Mon Ei Ei Moe, Germaine Loo, Daniel Chong, Keong Chi Ching
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWide complex tachycardia with right bundle branch block and right axis deviation in young age, fascicular ventricular tachycardia will be the top differential diagnosis to be considered. AV nodal blocking agents like verapamil, diltiazem, and amiodarone are not resolving the WCT with RBBB tachycardia in this case. Not all fascicular VTs are verapamil sensitive unless the verapamil sensitive re‐entry pathway is involved.Keywords: electrocardiographic features, fascicular ventricular tachycardia, supraventricular tachycardiac with aberrancy

A 41‐year‐old female with no prior cardiac history presented with pneumonia and tachycardia. Electrocardiogram (ECG) showed a heart rate of 100 beats per minute, with a slightly widened QRS duration of 130 ms, typical right bundle branch block (RBBB) morphology and right axis deviation (Figure 1). Notable ECG features include a short ventriculo‐atrial (VA) interval (< 70 ms), 1:1 VA conduction, and retrograde P‐waves in the inferior leads. Several spontaneous premature ventricular contractions (PVC) were noted, which did not reset or terminate the tachycardia. P wave morphology appears similar during tachycardia and with the PVC (Figure 2). However, these ECG features cannot reliably distinguish between supraventricular tachycardia (SVT with aberrancy) and ventricular tachycardia (VT), specifically fascicular VT.

The main differential diagnosis for a wide complex regular tachycardia with typical RBBB pattern includes:

Fascicular VT. In this case, given the right axis deviation with only a slightly widened QRS duration of 130 ms, left anterior fascicle VT is the top differential.

Supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) with RBBB aberrancy. Any type of SVT with RBBB aberrancy—atrioventricular nodal re‐entrant tachycardia, atrioventricular re‐entrant tachycardia, or atrial tachycardia with aberrancy are all possible differentials.

Other VTs originating from the left ventricle, such as mitral annulus VT or papillary muscle VT, are reasonable differentials. However, these are less likely as the ECG would show an atypical RBBB pattern, regular wide complex tachycardia, normally with a wider QRS duration. Of note, a patient presenting with an undifferentiated regular wide complex tachycardia should be assumed and managed as VT until otherwise proven.

Decision was made to proceed with intravenous adenosine challenge for diagnostic indications. This unmasked classical ECG hallmarks of VT, with VA dissociation, fusion beats, and capture beats (Figure 3). Thereafter, antiarrhythmics (intravenous verapamil, amiodarone, lidocaine) were given to convert the tachycardia, but with no avail. The next day, this spontaneous ECG tracing was noted (Figure 4), showing sinus beats with intermittent fusion, capture, and isolated ventricular beats. These are also typical ECG features consistent with VT. Transthoracic echocardiogram performed revealed mildly impaired left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 45%. Given the incessant nature of the VT with mildly reduced LVEF, the patient underwent electrophysiology study (EPS) and catheter ablation. This revealed a short HV interval of 13 milliseconds during tachycardia with 1:1 VA conduction, with inability to entrain the tachycardia during ventricular pacing. These EPS features are a hallmark of a proximal focal fascicular VT. Electroanatomical mapping using Advisor HD grid catheter confirmed a focal tachycardia origination from the left anterior fascicle. This was successfully terminated with radiofrequency ablation. Post‐ablation ECG returned to sinus rhythm with a normal axis and narrow QRS (Figure S1). However, at 3 months follow‐up, it was noted that the same VT had recurred, although no longer incessant. The patient underwent a repeat EPS and catheter ablation, and the VT was localized to a slightly more proximal ablation spot with good final ablation result.

This case demonstrates classic ECG features of a left anterior fascicular VT. Fascicular/purkinje related VT can occur in patients with or without structural heart disease. They can be classified into monomorphic or polymorphic purkinje related VT [1]. The common monomorphic purkinje related VT subtypes are: verapamil sensitive fascicular VT, bundle branch re‐entry VT and focal fascicular VT [2]. Although fascicular related VT is most commonly a verapamil‐sensitive, macro‐re‐entrant arrhythmia, our patient had a verapamil‐insensitive, focal left anterior fascicular VT. This variant is characterized by abnormal focal automaticity arising from the purkinje system. Although classically described as sensitive to lidocaine and beta blockers, this was not so for our patient [2]. ECG features are similar between the types of fascicular VT but differences in the QRS duration and cardiac axis may help to localize the fascicle involved and its proximity in the conduction system [3]. There are various distinguishing factors between the types of fascicular VT noted during electrophysiology study. Key electrophysiology study findings of a focal fascicular VT include—inability to entrain the tachycardia using overdrive pacing, short HV interval during tachycardia compared to a normal HV during sinus rhythm and termination of tachycardia during ablation of the earliest pre‐systolic purkinje potential [1]. Catheter ablation is the definitive treatment for focal fascicular VT. However, the success rate for focal fascicular VT ablation is lower as compared to other types of fascicular VT—with a reported recurrence rate of 3 out of 7 and 3 out of 11 by Tsutsui et al. [3]. It must be emphasized that additional care is required to avoid severe conduction disturbance during fascicular VT ablation.

In summary, we present a case of focal left anterior fascicular VT with characteristic ECG features of fascicular VT. Close evaluation and recognition of the characteristic ECG hallmarks are key for the diagnosis of fascicular VT.
