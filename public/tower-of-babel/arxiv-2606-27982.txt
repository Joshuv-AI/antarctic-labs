PRONE : Petrov-Galerkin Operator Learning Unifies DMD, SINDy & Koopmanism
Matthew J. Colbrook, April Herwig, J. Nathan Kutz, [0.5em] Department of Applied Mathematics, Theoretical Physics, University of Cambridge Cambridge CB3 0WA, UK

Abstract
Data-driven dynamics often asks how to linearize a nonlinear system. We ask instead: which observables should be advanced, and where should their futures live? This leads to Petrov Regression of Nonlinear Evolution (PRONE), a Petrov–Galerkin regression framework based on Ψ ⁡ ( 𝐗 ) ​ K ≈ Φ ⁡ ( 𝐘 ) , \Psi(\mathbf{X})K\approx\Phi(\mathbf{Y}), with distinct trial and test dictionaries. In this form, DMD, EDMD, SINDy, Koopman regression, sparse regression, and low-rank regression become variants of one construction: different dictionaries, weights, and constraints. We keep the linear algebra of Koopman learning, but drop the artificial requirement that a finite model map a dictionary into itself. With this asymmetry, eigenmodes are no longer the right objects. Instead, we use singular modes, which identify the observable combinations captured by the data, their projected futures, and the strength of the coupling between the two spaces. We identify the limiting projected operator and prove L 2 L^{2} convergence of the resulting nonlinear predictor. We give examples from chaotic maps, the double gyre, a pitching-airfoil wake, and Lorenz–63, where PRONE outperforms DeepONets, Fourier neural operators, and reservoir computers with considerably fewer parameters. These examples show the same message: lift once, regress once, and let the singular structure reveal statistics, transport, prediction, and dimension.

Introduction

We consider a discrete-time dynamical system

\mathbf{x}^{k+1}=F(\mathbf{x}^{k}),\qquad F:\Omega\to\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Our aim is to study its evolution from trajectory data. The obstacle is that
F
is typically nonlinear: a pendulum, a chemical reaction, a chaotic attractor, or a fluid wake may obey a simple law, but not one that is linear in the measured variables.

There is an old and powerful way around this difficulty. Instead of looking
only at the state
\mathbf{x}
, look at functions of the state. These functions are
called observables. The temperature at a point, the energy of an oscillator, a
Fourier coefficient, a polynomial feature, or a sensor reading are all
observables. A nonlinear map on states induces a linear map on observables:

\phi\mapsto\phi\circ F.

This is the Koopman point of view
[
17
]
. The price is that the
linear operator is infinite-dimensional. The art of data-driven Koopman
computation is to choose a useful finite part of it.

Most numerical methods make this finite part square. One chooses a dictionary
of observables, advances the data, projects back to the same dictionary, and
obtains a matrix. Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD), Extended Dynamic Mode
Decomposition (EDMD), kernel variants, and many related algorithms fit naturally
into this pattern
[
23
,
26
,
28
,
29
,
18
]
.
The resulting matrix is then often analysed through its eigenvalues and
eigenvectors.

This paper begins by changing one line of this story. The observables whose
future we want to predict need not be the observables in which we represent that
future. That is, the right finite section of Koopman is not necessarily square.

Suppose we have snapshot pairs

\mathbf{X}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf{x}_{1}^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
\mathbf{x}_{M}^{\top}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\mathbf{Y}=\begin{pmatrix}F(\mathbf{x}_{1})^{\top}\\
\vdots\\
F(\mathbf{x}_{M})^{\top}\end{pmatrix}.

Choose two finite families of observables:

\Phi=\{\phi_{j}\}_{j=1}^{N_{2}},\qquad\Psi=\{\psi_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N_{1}}.

The family
\Phi
contains the quantities whose values at the next time we want
to learn. The family
\Psi
contains the quantities of the present state from
which we shall learn them. The computation is simply

\Psi(\mathbf{X})K\approx\Phi(\mathbf{Y}).

(1)

Thus each column of
K
is an ordinary least-squares fit. It approximates one
future observable
\phi_{j}\circ F
by a linear combination of the present
observables in
\Psi
. This is the basic object of the paper. We call the resulting framework Petrov Regression of Nonlinear Evolution (PRONE). The construction is sketched in
Figure

1
: lift through two
dictionaries, regress once, and read the input–output structure through the
singular modes.

Several familiar methods appear at once. If
\Psi=\Phi=\mathrm{id}
, then
(
1
) is DMD
[
23
,
26
]
. If

\Psi=\Phi
is a nonlinear dictionary, it is EDMD
[
28
,
29
]
.
If
\Phi=\mathrm{id}
and
\Psi
is a nonlinear library, then

K^{\top}\Psi(\mathbf{x})

is a nonlinear approximation of
F(\mathbf{x})
obtained by a linear regression, the
unregularized least-squares core of SINDy
[
5
]
. Sparse, kernel,
structure-preserving, and verified Koopman variants arise by changing the
dictionaries, the weights, the regularization, or the constraints

[
4
,
7
,
11
,
8
]
.

The rectangular viewpoint also changes the modal analysis. Eigenvectors are
the right objects for a square map from a space to itself. But
(
1
) maps one finite observable space into another. In
general there need be no eigenvectors to compute. The natural objects are
singular vectors. Right singular modes are combinations of the observables in

\Phi
whose futures are most strongly captured by the chosen trial space.
Left singular modes are the corresponding projected futures in the span of

\Psi
. The singular values measure the strength of these pairings.

The distinction is more than cosmetic. Eigenanalysis asks for coordinates that
reproduce themselves up to multiplication. Singular analysis asks which
observable combinations are seen, retained, amplified, or lost by the dynamics
and by the chosen dictionary. This is an input-output question, and it remains
meaningful when the finite Koopman section is rectangular, rank-deficient, or
deliberately asymmetric.

The contributions of the paper are as follows.

•

We formulate DMD, EDMD, SINDy, and Koopman-based regression as instances of one
Petrov–Galerkin least-squares problem between observable spaces. The square
case is only one possibility; the rectangular case is the main freedom.

•

We show how PRONE gives nonlinear predictors from linear maps in lifted
coordinates. In the important case
\Phi=\mathrm{id}
, the learned map

K^{\top}\Psi(\mathbf{x})
is a nonlinear approximation of the dynamics
F(\mathbf{x})
.

•

We identify the limiting operator. Although the notation is Koopman-theoretic,
the learned predictor also exposes the adjoint, transfer-operator side of the
story. Under quadrature and density assumptions, the finite-data regressions
converge to the corresponding projected operator, and the resulting predictors
converge in
L^{2}
.

•

We develop a singular-mode analysis for rectangular Petrov–Galerkin sections.
The right modes identify observables whose evolution is captured by the chosen
space; the left modes identify their projected futures; the singular values
quantify the strength of these pairings.

•

We demonstrate the framework on maps, flows, chaotic attractors, and fluid data.
The examples show that the same
regression captures the prediction horizon and invariant statistics of a
chaotic map, coherent transport in the double gyre, the statistical geometry of
Lorenz–63, and an effective rank for a high-dimensional airfoil wake. Moreover the time-series prediction outperforms popular operator methods, while using two orders of magnitude less parameters.

2
Petrov–Galerkin Learning of Dynamical Systems

We now pass from dynamics to matrices. From snapshot pairs we form two lifted data matrices, using dictionaries that may differ, and compute the best linear map between them in least squares. This map is a Petrov–Galerkin section of the evolution between observable spaces; it need not be square. We then identify the limiting operator, prove convergence of the associated predictor, and discuss the two regularizations most important in computation: sparsity and low rank.

2.1
A Linear Framework for Nonlinear Problems

Wishing to perform only a simple linear-regression-based learning method but still having the desire to capture nonlinear effects, we nonlinearly embed
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
into observable spaces:

\Psi(\mathbf{X})=\begin{pmatrix}\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{1})&\ldots&\psi_{N_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{1})\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\psi_{1}(\mathbf{x}_{M})&\ldots&\psi_{N_{1}}(\mathbf{x}_{M})\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N_{1}}

and

\Phi(\mathbf{Y})=\begin{pmatrix}\phi_{1}(F(\mathbf{x}_{1}))&\ldots&\phi_{N_{2}}(F(\mathbf{x}_{1}))\\
\vdots&\ddots&\vdots\\
\phi_{1}(F(\mathbf{x}_{M}))&\ldots&\phi_{N_{2}}(F(\mathbf{x}_{M}))\end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N_{2}}.

The functions
\psi_{i},\phi_{j}\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
are real-valued observables, and the collections
\Psi
and
\Phi
are dictionaries. The inner product is the real Lebesgue
L^{2}
inner product
\langle f,g\rangle=\int_{\Omega}fg\,\mathrm{d}m
. In some cases we may use a different measure
\rho
; these cases will be clearly distincted as
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R};\rho)
.
The Petrov–Galerkin freedom is that these dictionaries need not coincide: in general
N_{1}\neq N_{2}
. After lifting the data, the nonlinear learning problem becomes the linear regression

\min_{K\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}}}\big\|\sqrt{W}\Psi(\mathbf{X})K-\sqrt{W}\Phi(\mathbf{Y})\big\|_{F}^{2}

(2)

where
W\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times M}
is a positive diagonal weight matrix. This problem is solved by taking

K=\Psi(\mathbf{X})^{\dagger}\Phi(\mathbf{Y})=\left(\Psi(\mathbf{X})^{\top}\,W\,\Psi(\mathbf{X})\right)^{-1}\Psi(\mathbf{X})^{\top}\,W\,\Phi(\mathbf{Y})

(3)

where
\cdot^{\dagger}
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse.

Here is the operator behind the regression. For an observable
g
, the Koopman operator acts as

\mathcal{K}:L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}),\quad g\mapsto\mathcal{K}g=g\circ F.

On a suitable
L^{2}
space this is a linear operator; boundedness requires the usual assumptions on
F
and the reference measure
[
20
]
. The regression (
2
) is a Petrov–Galerkin approximation of this operator. For instance, suppose that the samples
\mathbf{x}_{m}
are drawn independently from the reference measure
m
, and take
W=M^{-1}I
. Then
Eq.

2
is the Monte Carlo discretization of

\min_{K\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}}}\sum_{i=1}^{N_{2}}\int\Big|\big(\sum_{j=1}^{N_{1}}K_{ji}\,\psi_{j}(\mathbf{x})\,\big)-[\mathcal{K}\phi_{i}](\mathbf{x})\Big|^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

(4)

Thus each Koopman-advanced observable
\mathcal{K}\phi_{i}
is approximated in the trial space
{\mathrm{span}}\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N_{1}}\}
, and the regression solves all
N_{2}
approximation problems simultaneously.

The choice of dictionaries makes the construction flexible.

Example 2.1
.

Let
\Psi=\Phi=id
be the identity on a bounded subset
\Omega\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(so that
id\in L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
). Then
Eq.

3
reduces to
K=\mathbf{X}^{\dagger}\mathbf{Y}
, the matrix yielded by Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD)
[
23
]
.

Example 2.2
.

Alternatively, consider that
\Psi
is again some arbitrary dictionary but
\Phi
is still the identity. One then recovers (an unregularized version of) Sparse Identification of Nonlinear Dynamics (SINDy)
[
5
]
. We mention in
Section

2.4
some forms of regularization of
Eq.

2
which encompass SINDy as well.

Example 2.3
.

Further still, if
\Psi=\Phi
is an arbitrary dictionary then one recovers the Extended Dynamic Mode Decomposition (EDMD)
[
28
]
. In this context the Petrov–Galerkin method reduces to a Riesz–Galerkin one. The rectangular viewpoint is more revealing: even when the dictionaries contain the same type of observables but have different sizes, for instance
\Phi\subset\Psi
, the singular structure exposes information that is invisible in the square formulation.

The case
\Phi=\mathrm{id}
is especially important, for then the regression learns the dynamics themselves. If
\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x})=x_{i}
, then
\mathcal{K}\phi_{i}(\mathbf{x})=\phi_{i}(F(\mathbf{x}))=[F(\mathbf{x})]i
, and
Eq.

4
becomes

\min_{K\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times d}}\int\Big\|\big(\sum_{j=1}^{N_{1}}K_{j}\,\psi_{j}(\mathbf{x})\,\big)-F(\mathbf{x})\Big\|_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}^{2}\mathrm{d}\mathbf{x}.

where
K_{j}
is the vector-valued
j
th row of
K
. Thus
K^{\top}
maps lifted observables back to state space, giving the optimal linear reconstruction of
F
from the nonlinear embedding
\Psi:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}}
. This is the essential architecture of Petrov Regression of Nonlinear Evolution (PRONE): lift nonlinearly, regress linearly, and use rank reduction to retain the dynamically relevant directions; see

2.2
Extracting Dynamically Relevant Modes

First consider the EDMD case
\Psi=\Phi
, where the regression produces a square finite section of the Koopman operator
\mathcal{K}
. In this setting spectral language is natural: Koopman eigenfunctions organize the asymptotic growth, decay, and oscillation of observables. If

g=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathbf{g}_{j}\varrho_{j},\quad\mathcal{K}\varrho_{j}=\lambda_{j}\varrho_{j},\quad\text{then}\quad g(x^{1})=g(F(x^{0}))=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\lambda_{j}\mathbf{g}_{j}\varrho_{j}(x^{0}).

The eigenvalues record growth, decay, and oscillation. For nonnormal finite sections, however, eigenvalues alone are not enough; pseudospectra control finite-time growth and transient amplification
[
25
, page 25]
. This operator-theoretic picture motivates EDMD: eigenfunctions, and approximate eigenfunctions, identify observables that remain coherent under the dynamics. Thus an observable
g
is coherent at scale
\epsilon
if

\|g\circ F-\lambda g\|=\mathcal{O}(\epsilon),

with
\lambda
describing the leading growth, decay, or oscillation over time intervals before the accumulated defect dominates.

The top spectral information is the most useful in practice. Eigenvalues and pseudoeigenvalues of largest modulus, together with their associated modes, give a coarse description of the dynamics through observables that decay slowly, oscillate persistently, or exhibit finite-time amplification. This interpretation must be used with care: a finite EDMD matrix
K
need not inherit the spectrum of the Koopman operator
\mathcal{K}
, and this possible mismatch motivates residual and verified variants such as ResDMD
[
7
]
. Even so, the eigenvectors
v^{j}
of
K
and their associated
eigenmodes

\sum_{\ell}v^{j}_{\ell}\psi_{\ell}
remain useful diagnostic objects in computations; see
Section

4
.

When
\Psi\neq\Phi
, this eigenmode picture breaks. The learned map
K
is now rectangular, or at least acts between distinct observable spaces, so eigenvectors are no longer the right objects. The corresponding structure is singular: an input observable in
{\mathrm{span}}\Phi
is paired with its best-resolved output in
{\mathrm{span}}\Psi
. Given a truncated singular value decomposition

K=S\Lambda^{1/2}Z^{\top},\quad S\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times r},\quad\Lambda^{1/2}\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},\quad Z\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}\times r},\quad r\leq\min\{N_{1},N_{2}\},

we define the
singular modes

\varphi_{k}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{1}}S_{\ell k}\psi_{\ell}\quad\text{and}\quad\zeta_{k}=\sum_{\iota=1}^{N_{2}}Z_{\iota k}\phi_{\iota},\quad 1\leq k\leq r.

They are evaluated on data by
\Psi(\mathbf{X})S
and
\Phi(\mathbf{X})Z
, respectively. Their interpretation is developed in
Section

, and their computation is summarized in

2.3
Convergence

We now connect the regression matrix
K
to its limiting operator and prove convergence of the resulting nonlinear predictor in the appropriate topology. Define the analysis and synthesis maps

\begin{split}\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}:L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})\to\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}},&\quad\quad\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}g=\begin{pmatrix}\langle\psi_{1},g\rangle\\
\langle\psi_{2},g\rangle\\
\vdots\\
\langle\psi_{N_{1}},g\rangle\end{pmatrix},\\
\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}^{*}:\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}}\to L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}),&\quad\quad\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}^{*}c=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{1}}c_{\ell}\psi_{\ell}\end{split}

with analogous definitions for
\Phi
. If
\Psi
is orthonormal, then
\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}
is the orthogonal projection onto
{\mathrm{span}}\{\psi_{1},\ldots,\psi_{N_{1}}\}
. For a general linearly independent dictionary, the same projection is

\Pi_{\Psi}=\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}^{*}\,(\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}^{*})^{-1}\,\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}:L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})\to L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})

(5)

(and analogously for
\Phi
).
Since
Eq.

4
is approximated from data by quadrature, the snapshots should sample the reference measure through a convergent quadrature rule: Monte Carlo in high dimension, and higher-order rules such as Gauss–Legendre when the dimension permits. We also assume that the trial data matrix
\Psi(\mathbf{X})\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times N_{1}}
has full column rank for all sufficiently large
M
; with positive diagonal weights this is equivalent to full column rank of
W^{1/2}\Psi(\mathbf{X})
. This is the finite-data counterpart of
L^{2}
-linear independence of the dictionary functions
\psi_{j}
, and in computation it is enforced by an SVD, with unresolved or nearly dependent directions discarded.

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\Psi
and
\Phi
be dictionaries whose elements are linearly independent in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
. Suppose that the snapshots
\{\mathbf{x}_{m}\}_{m=1}^{M}
and weights
W
define a convergent quadrature rule for the relevant inner products. Let
K
be the least-squares solution of
Eq.

2
, and set

G_{\Psi}=\Psi(\mathbf{X})^{\top}W\Psi(\mathbf{X}),\quad G_{\Phi}=\Phi(\mathbf{X})^{\top}W\Phi(\mathbf{X}),\quad A=\Psi(\mathbf{X})^{\top}W\Phi(\mathbf{Y}).

(6)

Then

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}^{*}K

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\Psi}\mathcal{K}\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}^{*},

(7)

\displaystyle\lim_{M\to\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}^{*}G_{\Psi}^{-1}AG_{\Phi}^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}

\displaystyle=\Pi_{\Psi}\mathcal{K}\Pi_{\Phi}.

(8)

Corollary 2.1
.

Assume the conditions of
Proposition

2.1
are satisfied. Let
\Psi=\Psi_{N}
be a sequence of dictionaries which span a dense subset of
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
in the limit
N\to\infty
. Let
\Phi=id
be the coordinate dictionary. The corresponding PRONE predictor is

F_{N,M}(\mathbf{x})=K^{\top}\Psi_{N}(\mathbf{x}).

Then

\lim_{N\to\infty}\lim_{M\to\infty}\big\|F-F_{N,M}\big\|_{L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R}^{d})}=0.

Remark 2.1
.

This result clarifies what data-based predictors such as PRONE, SINDy
[
5
]
, Exact DMD
[
26
]
, and kernel EDMD
[
29
]
actually learn. Although the construction can be written in Koopman language, the fitted predictor is tied to the adjoint action: it represents the transfer operator, adjoint to Koopman, projected through the chosen trial and test dictionaries.

Example 2.4

(Pointwise convergence in
Corollary

2.1
is not guaranteed)
.

Let
\Omega=[0,1)
be the one-dimensional torus and let

Thus

(Smoothness can restore pointwise convergence)
.

as

for every

.

.

We first notice

\lim_{M\to\infty}G_{\Psi}\ =\ \left(\langle\psi_{i},\psi_{j}\rangle\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq N_{1}}\ =\ \mathcal{P}_{\Psi}\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}^{*}.

Analogous statement holds for

\lim_{M\to\infty}A\ =\ \big(\langle\psi_{i},\mathcal{K}\phi_{j}\rangle\big)_{\scriptscriptstyle{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq N_{1}\\

Since the dictionary functions are linearly independent, the limiting Gram matrices are nonsingular, and hence matrix inversion is continuous along the sequence for all sufficiently large

\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}^{*}\lim_{M\to\infty}K=\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}^{*}\lim_{M\to\infty}G_{\Psi}^{-1}A=\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}^{*}(\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}^{*})^{-1}\mathcal{P}_{\Psi}\mathcal{K}\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}^{*},

which, using

\Pi_{\Psi}\mathcal{K}\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}^{*}

Hence

\begin{split}\lim_{M\to\infty}F_{N,M}&=\lim_{M\to\infty}K^{\top}\Psi=\lim_{M\to\infty}K^{\top}G_{\Psi}^{1/2}G_{\Psi}^{-1/2}\Psi\\
&\quad\quad=\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}\mathcal{K}^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{\Psi}}^{*}(\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{\Psi}}\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{\Psi}}^{*})^{-1}\widetilde{\Psi}=\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}\mathcal{K}^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{\Psi}}^{*}\widetilde{\Psi}.\end{split}

Let
e_{j}(x)=[x]_{j}
be the
j
th component function. Now

\left[\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}\mathcal{K}^{*}\mathcal{P}_{\widetilde{\Psi}}^{*}\widetilde{\Psi}(\mathbf{x})\right]_{j}=\ \Big\langle e_{j},\mathcal{K}^{*}\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{1}}\widetilde{\psi}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})\widetilde{\psi}_{\ell}\Big\rangle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{1}}\left\langle e_{j},\mathcal{K}^{*}\widetilde{\psi}_{\ell}\right\rangle\widetilde{\psi}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x}).

Since
\widetilde{\Psi}
is orthonormal we have

\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{1}}\left\langle e_{j},\mathcal{K}^{*}\widetilde{\psi}_{\ell}\right\rangle\widetilde{\psi}_{\ell}(\mathbf{x})=\left(\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{1}}\left\langle\mathcal{K}e_{j},\widetilde{\psi}_{\ell}\right\rangle\widetilde{\psi}_{\ell}\right)(\mathbf{x})=\left(\Pi_{\widetilde{\Psi}}\mathcal{K}e_{j}\right)(\mathbf{x})=\left[\left(\Pi_{\widetilde{\Psi}}F\right)(\mathbf{x})\right]_{j}

Noting that
\Pi_{\widetilde{\Psi}}=\Pi_{\Psi}
and since
\Psi=\Psi_{N}
spans a dense subset of
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
in the limit
N\to\infty
, the projected coordinate functions converge in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
to the coordinate functions of
F
. This proves the claimed convergence of the learned map.
∎

2.4
Regularization

The least-squares solution of
Eq.

2
is explicit, but applications often call for more structure. Sparsity is the simplest example. One may replace
Eq.

2
by

\min_{K\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times N_{2}}}\big\|W^{1/2}\big(\Psi(\mathbf{X})K-\Phi(\mathbf{Y})\big)\big\|_{F}^{2}+\lambda\|K\|_{1}

penalizes the number and size of active dictionary couplings, with
\lambda\geq 0
setting the sparsity level. This objective generally loses the closed-form pseudoinverse solution, but it can be solved by standard sparse-regression algorithms
[
1
]
. When
\Phi=id
and
\Psi
is a nonlinear library, this is the discrete-time, sparsity-promoting form of the SINDy architecture: a small set of nonlinear observables is selected to predict the next state
[
5
]
.

Low rank gives a second regularization. It is useful when the dictionary is redundant, or nearly so, and several observables carry almost the same information. Let

W^{1/2}\Psi(\mathbf{X})\approx U\Sigma V^{\top}

be a truncated SVD, with
U\in\mathbb{R}^{M\times r}
,
\Sigma\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r}
, and
V\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{1}\times r}
. Then the least-squares solution is approximated by

K\approx V\Sigma^{\dagger}U^{\top}W^{1/2}\Phi(\mathbf{Y}).

In the square case
\Psi=\Phi
, one may work with the compressed matrix

\widetilde{K}=V^{\top}KV=\Sigma^{\dagger}U^{\top}W^{1/2}\Phi(\mathbf{Y})V\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times r},

whose eigenvectors lift back to the original dictionary coordinates by multiplication with
V
. This is the computational core of Exact DMD: the dominant spectral information is obtained from an
r\times r
matrix rather than the full dictionary matrix. For
\Psi\neq\Phi
, the same compression gives

\widetilde{K}=V^{\top}K=\Sigma^{\dagger}U^{\top}W^{1/2}\Phi(\mathbf{Y})\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times N_{2}}.

Since
V
is an isometry on the retained trial subspace, the singular values and right singular vectors of
K
are obtained from
\widetilde{K}
, while the left singular vectors are lifted by
V
. Thus the rectangular Petrov–Galerkin singular modes can be computed from the smaller regression, as summarized in
Algorithm

2
.

A square Koopman matrix invites eigenvectors. A rectangular Petrov–Galerkin matrix does not, and this is a feature rather than a defect. This section studies the singular modes of
\Pi_{\Psi}\mathcal{K}\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}^{*}
produced in
Algorithm

2
. Given an SVD
K=S\Lambda^{1/2}Z^{\top}

\varphi_{k}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{1}}S_{\ell k}\psi_{\ell}\quad\text{and}\quad\zeta_{j}=\sum_{\iota=1}^{N_{2}}Z_{\iota j}\phi_{\iota},\quad 1\leq k,j\leq r.

\Psi(\mathbf{X})S
and
\Phi(\mathbf{X})Z
, respectively.

{\mathrm{span}}\Phi

{\mathrm{span}}\Psi

\mathcal{K}\mathcal{K}^{*}
and hence by folding, compression, and preimages. This distinction is particularly important
when
\Phi
consists only of coordinate functions, as in PRONE (
Algorithm

1
), rather
than a full dictionary of nonlinear observables.

3.1
Right Singular Modes

The right singular modes are eigenvectors/eigenfunctions of the operator

\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}\mathcal{K}^{*}\Pi_{\Psi}\mathcal{K}\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}^{*}\quad\text{resp.}\quad\Pi_{\Phi}\mathcal{K}^{*}\Pi_{\Psi}\mathcal{K}\Pi_{\Phi}^{*}.

Taking
\Psi=\Psi_{N_{1}}
to be a sequence of dictionaries which span a dense subset of
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
in the limit
N_{1}\to\infty
, we have

\lim_{N_{1}\to\infty}\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}\mathcal{K}^{*}\Pi_{\Psi_{N_{1}}}\mathcal{K}\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}^{*}=\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}\mathcal{K}^{*}\mathcal{K}\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{N_{2}\times N_{2}}

(9)

in operator norm (since
\Phi
is finite). Thus, in the rich-trial-space limit, the right modes are governed by
\mathcal{K}^{*}\mathcal{K}
. We will make use of the following fact for Koopman operators (see, e.g.,
[
12
]
). Let
F_{\sharp}m
be the pushforward of the standard Lebesgue measure
m
on
\Omega
(i.e.,
F_{\sharp}m(A)=m(F^{-1}(A))
where
F^{-1}(A)
is the preimage).

Lemma 3.1
.

Suppose that
F_{\sharp}m
is absolutely continuous with respect to
m
, and let
\rho=\frac{dF_{\sharp}m}{dm}
be essentially bounded. Then

(\mathcal{K}^{*}\mathcal{K}g)(x)=g(x)\rho(x).

The proof of this lemma is immediate from the inner product representation

\langle f,\mathcal{K}^{*}\mathcal{K}g\rangle=\langle\mathcal{K}f,\mathcal{K}g\rangle=\int f\circ F\cdot g\circ F\,\mathrm{d}m=\int f\cdot g\,\mathrm{d}F_{\sharp}m=\int f\cdot g\cdot\rho\,\mathrm{d}m=\langle f,g\rho\rangle.

Combining
Lemma

3.1
with
Eq.

9
, the limiting right-mode problem is governed by

\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}\mathcal{K}^{*}\mathcal{K}\mathcal{P}_{\Phi}^{*}=\left(\langle\phi_{i},\phi_{j}\rangle_{L^{2}(\rho)}\right)_{1\leq i,j\leq N_{2}}.

The eigenvectors
Z_{:,k}
therefore satisfy the Rayleigh principle

\lambda_{k+1}=\max\Big\{\|\phi\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}\ \Big|\ \phi=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{2}}[z]_{\ell}\,\phi_{\ell},\;\ z^{\top}z=1,\;\ z\perp Z_{:,j},\;\ \forall\,1\leq j\leq k\Big\}.

(10)

In particular, when
\Phi
is orthonormal, then

\lambda_{k+1}=\max\left\{\frac{\|\zeta\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}}{\|\zeta\|_{L^{2}(m)}^{2}}\ \Big|\ \zeta\in{\mathrm{span}}\,\Phi,\;\ \zeta\perp\zeta_{j}\;\ \forall\,1\leq j\leq k\right\}.

From this we conclude that the right singular modes
\zeta_{k}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{N_{2}}Z_{\ell,k}\phi_{\ell}
are preciseley the mutually orthogonal observables in
{\mathrm{span}}\Phi
with maximal future
L^{2}
mass.

Arnold’s cat map gives a clean illustration. Let
F:\mathbb{T}^{2}\to\mathbb{T}^{2}
be

F\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}2x+y\mod 1\\
x+y\mod 1\end{pmatrix},

a classically studied map on the torus. Since
F
is measure-preserving,
\rho=1
, and the Rayleigh quotient in
Eq.

10
is identically one in the full-space limit. Finite sections can nevertheless lose modes. For the real Fourier block

C_{n,m}(x,y)=\cos(2\pi(nx+my)),\quad S_{n,m}(x,y)=\sin(2\pi(nx+my)),

the Koopman action is

\mathcal{K}C_{n,m}=C_{2n+m,n+m},\quad\mathcal{K}S_{n,m}=S_{2n+m,n+m}.

Thus, if
\Phi
contains all sine–cosine blocks with
|n|,|m|\leq N
and
\Psi
contains all blocks with
|n|,|m|\leq 3N
, every Koopman image of a test block is retained and all singular values are one. A square truncation need not have this property: blocks whose images leave the truncated box are projected away, producing zero singular values. This is the practical advantage of the Petrov–Galerkin view: a rectangular trial space can preserve the dynamically generated content that a square EDMD section discards.

Equation

10
is especially revealing when
\Phi=id
, the prediction setting of PRONE (
Algorithm

1
). In this case the columns of
Z
rotate the coordinate observables into directions that best capture future states: they maximize variance with respect to the pushforward density
\rho
. More precisely, if the coordinate functions are centered, then for
z\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,

\Var_{\rho}\Big(\sum_{\ell=1}^{d}[z]_{\ell}\,[\cdot]_{\ell}\Big)=\|z^{\top}\cdot\,\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}^{2}=\|z^{\top}F(\cdot)\|_{L^{2}(m)}^{2}.

(11)

As a concrete example, take
F
to be the inverse Arnold cat map and parameterize the torus by
[-1/2,1/2]^{2}_{\mathrm{per}}
. Let
\Phi=\sqrt{12}\,id
, so that the coordinate functions are normalized, and choose an orthonormal family
\Psi\subset L^{2}([-1/2,1/2]^{2}_{\mathrm{per}};\mathbb{R})
for which
\Pi_{\Psi}F^{-1}=F^{-1}
. The singular modes selected by
Algorithm

2
are shown in
Fig.

; their analytic computation is given in
Appendix

A
.

3.2
Left Singular Modes

The left singular modes admit a dual interpretation. Since
\varphi_{j}=\Pi_{\Psi}\mathcal{K}\zeta_{j}
, they are the Koopman-evolved right singular modes, resolved in the trial space
{\mathrm{span}}\Psi
. To isolate the limiting structure, consider the fully projected operator
\Pi_{\Psi}\mathcal{K}\Pi_{\Phi}
. If
\Psi
is fixed and
\Phi=\Phi_{N_{2}}
becomes dense in
L^{2}(\Omega;\mathbb{R})
as
N_{2}\to\infty
, then the left singular modes are governed by
\Pi_{\Psi}\mathcal{K}\mathcal{K}^{*}\Pi_{\Psi}
. This operator has an explicit form for nonsingular, finite-to-one
C^{1}
maps with nonzero Jacobian determinant almost everywhere. In that case

(\mathcal{K}^{*}h)(\mathbf{y})=\sum_{\mathbf{x}\in F^{-1}(\mathbf{y})}\frac{h(\mathbf{x})}{|\det DF(\mathbf{x})|},

and therefore

(\mathcal{K}\mathcal{K}^{*}h)(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{\widetilde{\mathbf{x}}\,:\,F(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})=F(\mathbf{x})}\frac{h(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})}{|\det DF(\widetilde{\mathbf{x}})|}.

Thus the left singular modes are the observables in
{\mathrm{span}}\Psi
whose weighted sums over preimage fibers have maximal
L^{2}
energy. Values on points that are mapped to the same future state add constructively or cancel, while the Jacobian weights emphasize regions whose volume is compressed by the map. If
F
is injective, this reduces to

(\mathcal{K}\mathcal{K}^{*}h)(\mathbf{x})=\frac{h(\mathbf{x})}{|\det DF(\mathbf{x})|},

so the left modes concentrate on directions resolved by
\Psi
where the inverse-volume weight is large. The conceptual duality of this multiplication operator with
\mathcal{K}^{*}\mathcal{K}
(cf.
Lemma

3.1
) should be emphasized:
\mathcal{K}^{*}\mathcal{K}
measures future mass, while
\mathcal{K}\mathcal{K}^{*}
measures how evolved observables fold back geometrically through the preimage.

Returning to Arnold’s cat map, the Koopman operator is orthogonal on the real
L^{2}
space. The right singular modes for the map are therefore the left singular modes for the inverse map, which is precisely the relationship illustrated in
Fig.

2
.

4
Examples

We test the method on four problems of increasing difficulty: a chaotic cubic map, the periodically driven double gyre, the Lorenz–63 system, and a pitching-airfoil wake. Each example stresses a different aspect of the framework. For the cubic map, pointwise prediction gives way to invariant statistics. For the double gyre, singular modes reveal coherent transport. For Lorenz–63, nonlinear lifting improves finite-time prediction while preserving the statistical shape of the attractor. For the airfoil data, the singular spectrum gives a practical rank.

Conclusion

We have framed a broad class of data-driven dynamics methods as Petrov–Galerkin regressions between observable spaces. The key change—allowing the two observable spaces to differ—puts DMD,
EDMD, SINDy, and Koopman regression into the same Petrov–Galerkin framework.
The regression

\Psi(\mathbf{X})K\approx\Phi(\mathbf{Y})

is a
finite section of the Koopman action between two chosen observable spaces; when

\Phi=id
, it is also a nonlinear predictor of the state obtained by
reevaluating the lift at each step. In the large-data limit the fitted maps
converge to the corresponding projected operator, and the predictor converges
in
L^{2}
as the trial space is enriched.

The rectangular viewpoint also changes what should be plotted and interpreted.
Eigenvectors belong to square maps from a space to itself. A Petrov–Galerkin
section between different observable spaces asks a different question: which
observable combinations are carried by the dynamics into the chosen trial
space, and with what strength? Singular modes answer this question directly.
The right modes identify the inputs that are best resolved after evolution; the
left modes are their projected futures; the singular values measure the
coupling. In this sense the singular value decomposition is not merely a
compression device. It is the modal decomposition appropriate to finite,
asymmetric Koopman computation.

The examples show the range of this interpretation. For the chaotic cubic map,
trajectory agreement ends at the Lyapunov horizon, but the learned map still
captures invariant statistics. For the double gyre, singular modes recover
coherent transport structures. For Lorenz–63, nonlinear lifting gives a compact
predictor that preserves the geometry and dominant statistics of the attractor.
For the pitching-airfoil wake, the singular spectrum supplies a practical rank
selection rule and the leading modes resolve coherent flow structures. The
same least-squares object and singular modes therefore supports prediction, statistics, transport
diagnostics, and reduced modelling.

The message is simple. Do not force the data-driven Koopman problem to be
square. Choose the observables whose future matters, choose the space in which
that future can be represented, fit the Petrov–Galerkin map, and read its
singular structure. This is PRONE: a rectangular, regression-based view
of nonlinear dynamics in which forecasting and interpretation are two sides of
the same matrix.
