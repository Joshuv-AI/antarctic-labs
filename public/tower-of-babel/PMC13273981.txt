The impact of bronchoscopy on the safety of percutaneous tracheostomy: authors’ reply to commentary
Authors: Akiva Nachshon, Avishai Shapiro, Smadar Goldfarb, Natalia Kuzmina, Marc Romain, Asaf Schwartz, Avraham Abutbul, Ido Vilchik, Michael Beil, Peter V. van Heerden, the Tracheostomy Study Group
Published: 2026

We thank Qin H. and Zhang W. [1] for their interest in our study and for their thoughtful comments regarding the role of bronchoscopy during percutaneous dilational tracheostomy (PCT). Their remarks provide an opportunity to further clarify several methodological and clinical aspects of our work.

Our study [2] was designed to evaluate whether routine bronchoscopy use during PCT improves clinically meaningful outcomes in mechanically ventilated ICU patients. The principal finding was that routine bronchoscopy did not reduce major complications, mortality, or length of hospitalization, while significantly prolonging both preparation and procedural duration.

Regarding physiologic effects during the procedure, we agree that bronchoscopy may influence gas exchange and may theoretically contribute to hypercapnia, hypoxemia, or elevated intracranial pressure. However, despite these theoretical concerns, no significant difference in desaturation events was observed between groups in our cohort. As part of routine preparation in both groups, we routinely performed oral suctioning around the endotracheal tube as well as deep endotracheal suctioning prior to initiation of the procedure. Patients were also preoxygenated and maintained on 100% oxygen throughout the procedure, and the relatively short duration of most procedures may have further limited transient desaturation events. Secretion clearance through bronchoscopy may indeed be beneficial in selected patients, although this was not specifically evaluated as a predefined endpoint in the current study.

The commentators also asked about the use of direct laryngoscopy in the non-bronchoscopy group. This technique was described in the Methods section of the published manuscript and is routinely used in our institution to facilitate controlled withdrawal of the endotracheal tube above the puncture site prior to tracheal puncture and cannulation. The technique is technically straightforward, familiar to airway operators, and avoids the additional staffing, equipment utilization, maintenance, and disinfection costs associated with bronchoscopy.

With respect to coagulation status, only patients with clinically significant uncontrolled coagulopathy associated with active bleeding risk were excluded. Mild or corrected coagulation abnormalities, which are common among critically ill ICU patients, were not exclusion criteria and were managed according to standard ICU practice.

We agree with the authors that procedure duration may depend on multiple factors including operator experience, workflow organization, institutional practice, and team familiarity. Indeed, one of the motivations for prospectively defining procedural timing in our study was the heterogeneity among prior publications regarding how procedure duration was measured. To address this limitation, our protocol predefined two separate intervals: preparation time (sedation to skin incision) and procedural time itself (skin incision to cuff inflation). Using these standardized prospective definitions, both intervals were significantly longer in the bronchoscopy group.

Concerning the three deaths occurring within 24 h, only one was considered procedure-related, and mortality rates were identical between groups. Importantly, no evidence suggested that the absence of bronchoscopy contributed to this outcome. Similarly, baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were well balanced between groups, making clinically meaningful confounding by measured baseline variables unlikely.

The authors also questioned the subgroup findings according to operator experience. We would like to emphasize that the final published analysis demonstrated no significant differences in complication distribution between experienced physicians and trainees. The subgroup analyses regarding procedural duration demonstrated statistically significant but clinically modest differences, while overall complication rates remained comparable.

We agree that bronchoscopy remains a valuable adjunct in selected clinical scenarios, particularly in patients with difficult anatomy or when additional intratracheal visualization is considered necessary. However, the question addressed by our trial was whether routine use of bronchoscopy improves clinically meaningful outcomes in all patients undergoing PCT.

Recently published multicenter randomized data have similarly reported no reduction in major complications with routine bronchoscopic guidance during percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy, despite measurable physiologic effects associated with bronchoscopy use [3].

As appropriately noted by Qin and Zhang, this was a single-center study, and multicenter trials with longer follow-up would further strengthen the evidence base. Nevertheless, given the prospective randomized design and the relatively large cohort compared with previous studies in this field, we believe the present study contributes important data to an area where high-quality comparative evidence remains limited.

We thank the authors again for their constructive comments and for contributing to this important clinical discussion.
