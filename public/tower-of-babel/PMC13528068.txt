Making every contact count for adult patients in healthcare settings: a scoping review of implementation contexts, methods, and levels of evidence
Authors: Diane Geindreau, Lou Martineau, Aymery Constant, Alexis Descatha, Celine Schnebelen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionIn the context of the global shift towards primary prevention and behavioural change interventions for non-communicable diseases, this scoping review aimed to map the existing scientific literature on the Making Every Contact Count (MECC) programme implemented in healthcare settings for adult patients and identify remaining knowledge gaps.MethodsPubMed, Scopus, and Web of Science were searched for indexed articles published between 2010 and January 2026 using combinations of the terms “Making Every Contact Count”, NOT “third and social economy sector”, and NOT “voluntary and community sector”. Studies were included if they investigated MECC implementation in healthcare settings for adult patients. Two reviewers independently extracted and appraised the data. A visual evidence mapping synthesis was then developed.ResultsOf the 304 references identified, 22 articles published between 2013 and 2025 were included. Studies were conducted in the United Kingdom and Ireland across diverse healthcare delivery sectors. Investigated dimensions included MECC training, implementation, and delivery from the perspectives of healthcare professionals, patients, and students, as well as document analyses. Outcomes mainly related to attitudes, perceptions, and implementation determinants. The methodological and reporting quality of the included studies was heterogeneous. Identified gaps concerned working conditions, the applicability of MECC across different implementation contexts, the intensity, duration, and content of MECC delivery, and its cost-effectiveness, particularly regarding specific behavioural risk factors.ConclusionsInterest in MECC is increasing. This scoping review synthesised the existing literature, mapped key evidence gaps, and highlighted the limited evidence currently available regarding the effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of MECC, particularly across different clinical and national implementation contexts. The findings also suggest that evidence supporting Screening, Brief Intervention and Referral to Treatment approaches cannot be directly extrapolated to MECC. The protocol was registered in PROSPERO under the number CRD420251044644.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14907-2.

Background

Behavioural risk factors such as smoking, physical inactivity, harmful alcohol consumption and an unhealthy diet increase the risk of non-communicable diseases (NCDs). Despite some encouraging decreases, NCDs still pose a significant threat to human health [1]. Therefore, screening for behavioural risk factors and supporting individuals in changing their habits is crucial for NCD prevention. Although such screening is highly acceptable to healthcare users [2, 3], missed opportunities to detect and provide brief interventions for behavioural risk factors are common, particularly in hospital settings [4–6]. Because healthcare services involve frequent interactions with large and diverse patient populations, healthcare settings represent valuable opportunities to deliver systematic screening and brief interventions targeting behavioural risk factors and reducing the social gradient in health [7].

The Making Every Contact Count (MECC) initiative was developed in the United Kingdom in the early 2010s as part of a broader public health strategy led by the National Health Service (NHS) and public health agencies to integrate prevention into routine healthcare delivery. MECC was designed in response to the growing burden of chronic non-communicable diseases associated with behavioural risk factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, physical inactivity, poor diet, and mental health difficulties. The programme is based on the premise that routine interactions between patients and healthcare professionals represent valuable opportunities to initiate brief behaviour change interventions and promote healthier lifestyles [8, 9].

MECC applies evidence-based behaviour change approaches, including brief interventions, motivational communication techniques, active listening, and signposting to appropriate support services. It aims to support healthcare professionals in integrating preventive conversations into everyday care delivery rather than limiting prevention to dedicated public health consultations [10]. National implementation resources, evaluation frameworks, and e-learning training programmes have subsequently been developed to support the dissemination and operationalisation of MECC across healthcare organisations [11]. Since its implementation in the UK and subsequent adaptation in Ireland, MECC has been the subject of several scientific publications, including two literature reviews [12, 13].

The review published by Parchment et al. in 2021 focused on the Wessex MECC/ Healthy Conversation Style (HCS) approach [13]. The aim was to synthesize evidence relating to MECC and HCS in a narrative way. The review outlined a high level of MECC acceptability but also revealed a knowledge gap, primarily due to a lack of patient-focused research and robust impact evaluations. Batten et al.‘s rapid review aimed to explore trainees’ training preferences, as well as the barriers to and facilitators of MECC training and delivery [12]. They found consistent evidence that training HCPs to deliver the MECC increased their confidence and willingness to do so. Although these reviews offer valuable insights into MECC, there is currently no comprehensive synthesis of the implementation contexts, study designs, investigated populations, outcomes, and levels of evidence relating to MECC in healthcare settings for adult patients. In particular, the current literature remains fragmented regarding the distinction between MECC as a broader opportunistic prevention strategy and the evidence-based behaviour change techniques that may support its implementation, such as SBIRT or HCS. More precisely, we propose a scoping review adopting a context-focused implementation perspective centred on healthcare settings in which healthcare professionals deliver patient care. Therefore, this review aimed to address the following research questions: how is MECC implemented across different healthcare settings? What does the existing literature report regarding the populations investigated, study design, quality of evidence, and outcomes used to evaluate its implementation and effectiveness? What gaps remain in the current knowledge regarding MECC implementation for adult patients in healthcare settings?

Method

This scoping review aim was to map existing and missing knowledge about MECC by identifying the contexts of implementation, types of studies conducted, main outcome and population of interest and quality of the evidence. It was conducted in accordance with guidelines of the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses Extension for Systematic Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) [14]. This scoping review involved four steps: (1) inclusion, (2) extraction, (3) quality assessment, and (4) a visual synthesis.

Eligible records were peer-reviewed publications explicitly addressing MECC in their titles or abstracts. More precisely, the search strategy and eligibility criteria were designed according to the Population, Intervention, Context, Outcomes, and Design (PICOD) framework described in Table 1. Searches of electronic databases were conducted in May 2025 and updated in January 2026. Records published from 2010 onwards and in any language were eligible for inclusion, as 2010 corresponded to the national implementation of MECC in the UK.

Examples of search terms and keywords used in PubMed included “Making Every Contact Count” NOT “third and social economy sector” and “voluntary and community sector”. Search strings were adapted for each database according to its indexing system and search functionalities. The full search strategies for all databases are provided in Additional File 1. Existing reviews on MECC and the reference lists of included studies were consulted during the development of the search strategy to help identify key references and refine the search algorithm. Several combinations of search terms were piloted and compared across databases to assess their relevance and specificity. The NOT operator was introduced to reduce retrieval of literature related to community and voluntary sector contexts falling outside the scope of this review, while preserving references relevant to healthcare settings.

Two important limitations should be acknowledged regarding the study selection process. First, the search strategy was intentionally restricted to references explicitly mentioning MECC. MECC is conceptually related to Screening, Brief Intervention and Referral to Treatment (SBIRT), Behaviour Change Techniques (BCTs), and Healthy Conversation Skills (HCS), as these approaches may support its implementation. Previous literature has sometimes used these concepts alongside or in close association with MECC [13]. Consequently, some relevant studies describing interventions closely related to MECC may not have been identified if they used alternative terminology such as SBIRT, BCTs, or HCS. However, the aim of this review was to describe the nature and extent of the evidence specifically related to MECC as a holistic and opportunistic primary prevention programme, as defined in the Delphi study [10], rather than focusing on one of its individual components.

Second, although MECC can be implemented across a wide range of contexts, including social care and community environments, this review focused specifically on healthcare settings involving healthcare professionals delivering patient care. This decision was motivated by the potential contribution of primary prevention interventions within healthcare services, particularly in a global context where healthcare systems face increasing pressure to contain costs [16, 17]. Furthermore, this choice was consistent with an implementation science perspective, which assumes that an intervention shown to be effective in one setting cannot automatically be considered effective in another.

All identified citations were uploaded to the Covidence software. Duplicates were first identified and removed automatically using the Covidence duplicate detection function and subsequently checked manually by the reviewers. During title and abstract screening, all records were assessed independently by two authors, against the inclusion criteria outlined previously. Any discrepancies between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. The same approach was taken for full-text reference screening.

For the extraction step, the following variables were extracted: first author, year of publication, country of origin, setting, population and sample size, data collection method, outcomes assessed and key findings.

Due to the heterogeneity of the included study designs, various quality appraisal tools were used whenever feasible. This constituted a deviation from the protocol, as diverse quality assessment checklists were considered more appropriate to accommodate the methodological diversity of the included studies than the Navigation Guide Systematic Review Methodology [18], which had initially been planned. The following tools were therefore used :

Critical Appraisal Skills Programme (CASP) Checklist: For Descriptive/Cross-Sectional Studies [19].CASP Checklist for Qualitative Research [20].Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) [21].Joanna Briggs Institute Critical Appraisal Checklist For Quasi-Experimental Studies [22]. The purpose of this assessment was not to exclude studies or grade the certainty of the evidence, but rather to identify common strengths and limitations in the methodology of the literature. Similarly, to the inclusion step, all records were assessed by two co-authors.

Data were synthesised descriptively through an evidence mapping approach focusing on implementation contexts, intervention components, investigated populations, study designs, outcomes, and levels of evidence. Knowledge gaps were identified based on underrepresented implementation contexts, insufficiently explored outcome dimensions, and limited methodological diversity across the included studies. The PICOD framework and implementation science perspective guided the identification and interpretation of these knowledge gaps. These elements were visually synthesized in a map showing the state of knowledge on MECC deployed in healthcare settings for adults and its unexplored aspects.

The protocol was registered in PROSPERO under the number CRD42025104464.

Quality appraisal

The overall level of quality was heterogeneous (see Table 3). The appraisal highlighted both methodological limitations and limitations in the reporting quality of the included studies. While the qualitative design was generally considered relevant to the aims of the studies (except for one [35]), the choice of method/design [23, 27, 28, 32, 34, 35, 43, 44], and the researcher’s role in formulating the research questions [23, 27, 28, 32, 34, 35, 43] were often insufficiently discussed or reported. Contributions to existing knowledge and the transferability of findings to other healthcare contexts were also often insufficiently discussed [31, 32, 34, 35]. The data analysis process was not always described in sufficient detail [27, 28, 32, 35], and the derivation of themes from the data was sometimes unclear [32]. Recruitment strategies [35, 43], ethical considerations [35] and data collection method [32] also revealed some isolated reporting or methodological limitations. For observational quantitative studies, sample sizes tended to be small with no power calculation, increasing the risk that the results were due to chance [29, 30, 37]. All references in this category provided limited detail regarding analytical procedures [29, 30, 33, 37]. Finally, transferability appeared limited because key contextual factors were insufficiently described [29, 30, 37]. For interventional studies, there were no control groups [24, 25] and the outcome measurements were either inconsistent or not repeated before and after the intervention [24, 25]. Among the mixed-methods studies, one reference was rated as poor, primarily due to the inadequate integration of qualitative and quantitative components [36].

Evidence mapping and identification of knowledge gaps

In the final step, a figure was developed that mapped the existing knowledge and identified gaps for further investigation. This figure outlines the diversity of implementation contexts explored at the meso level (i.e. care delivery sectors), in contrast to the limited diversity of national contexts explored at the macro level and the limited reporting of clinical care configurations and patient pathways at the micro level. The included references present studies that took place in these contexts and examined various aspects of the MECC programme (Interventions). The first investigated aspect was the MECC training, for HCPs [23, 24, 32, 35, 36, 40] and students [25, 41, 42]. There was also a document analysis of training procedures [27]. In general, training was associated with more positive attitudes and knowledge regarding MECC, though it did not always influence delivery. Low motivation was identified as a barrier. The second investigated aspect was implementation (adoption, launch, piloting), based on HCPs’ perspectives [35, 37, 39, 43] or documents analysis [26, 27]. MECC was perceived as low cost and easy to implement, especially given its alignment with roles and values. However, barriers remained, mainly relating to context, resources, and individual characteristics. Third investigated aspect was the analysis of delivery practices with references examining how HCPs deliver MECC to patients [29–31, 33, 34, 43, 44] and exploring patients’ views and reactions to MECC delivery [28, 38]. It revealed a high number of missed opportunities, primarily due to time constraints and staffing issues, while enablers included supportive environments and positive attitudes and perceptions. Patients found MECC acceptable because of its person-centered approach. Overall, the literature predominantly explored perceptions and experiences related to MECC, with limited use of objective or longitudinal outcome measures. Knowledge gaps were identified according to implementation contexts, investigated intervention dimensions, outcomes explored, and methodological characteristics of the included studies.

Discussion

This scoping review of the scientific literature on the MECC implemented in healthcare setting for adult patients reveals a growing interest in opportunistic primary prevention in recent years. The review outlines key knowledge on MECC training, implementation and delivery from a variety of perspectives. However, the methodological quality of the included studies was heterogeneous, although the overall level of quality was considered moderate. Most studies focused on individual perceptions and declarative data.

This scoping review builds on the existing body of knowledge on MECC [12, 13]. It also highlights two important observations. First, although implementation contexts appeared heterogeneous at the meso level (i.e. care delivery sectors), the included studies rarely reported detailed information at the micro level of implementation (i.e. specific clinical care configurations and patient pathways). In particular, organisational characteristics and patient admission modalities (e.g. outpatient consultations, inpatient admissions, day hospital care, emergency care, or intra- versus extra-hospital care) were seldom described in sufficient detail. As a result, the review provides limited insight into how specific clinical care configurations may facilitate or constrain MECC implementation. Yet such organisational characteristics are likely to influence opportunities for brief interventions, time availability, continuity of care, and the relevance of MECC across specialties and healthcare pathways.

Furthermore, the included studies were conducted only in the UK and Ireland, limiting the geographical diversity of national implementation contexts. This may restrict the applicability of the findings to other healthcare systems and national contexts, particularly given the importance of context in implementation science [45, 46]. In implementation science, applicability refers to the extent to which an intervention can realistically be implemented within a particular context [47]. Greater diversity in both clinical and national implementation contexts would therefore be valuable to better understand the core and discretionary components of MECC and their potential effects across settings.

Secondly, the outcomes explored in the included studies also appeared relatively homogeneous. Most studies focused primarily on the attitudes and perceptions of MECC recipients and providers. As highlighted in the UCFIR framework [15], such outcomes remain important because they reflect how healthcare professionals and organizations adapt to an intervention in real-world settings. However, this strong focus on perceptions and self-reported experiences also highlights important gaps in the current evidence base. The literature on MECC relies almost exclusively on declarative data derived from interviews or questionnaires, with very limited use of objective or routinely collected indicators. As a result, important dimensions of MECC implementation and effectiveness remain insufficiently explored, including its long-term impact on health behaviours, working conditions, professional recognition, or organizational outcomes. In addition, none of the included studies quantified MECC delivery in terms of frequency, duration, or intervention content, and no cost-effectiveness analyses were identified. Although approaches such as SBIRT have demonstrated effectiveness across several behavioural risk factors and healthcare contexts, these findings cannot automatically be extrapolated to MECC because of its broader and more holistic approach to behaviour change [8, 9, 48]. Consequently, the current literature does not yet allow robust conclusions regarding the overall effectiveness, efficiency, or cost-effectiveness of MECC, nor its specific effects on individual behavioural risk factors.

In conclusion, this overview has highlighted the growing interest in opportunistic primary prevention, particularly the MECC programme. However, based on Fig. 2, ‘Identified Gaps’, further investigations are required to improve the training, implementation, and delivery of MECC, as well as to strengthen both the applicability of the intervention across healthcare contexts and the transferability of its effects. Priority areas for future research include: (1) cost-effectiveness analyses; (2) long-term evaluation of behavioural impacts for both patients and healthcare professionals; and (3) objective assessment of the frequency, duration, and content of MECC delivery across different clinical care settings. Such investigations should rely on robust study designs and high-quality methodologies using objective and routinely collected indicators. They should also focus on clearly identified behavioural risk factors in order to better isolate the specific contribution of MECC to behaviour change and evaluate its effectiveness within particular clinical and organisational contexts. Strengthening this evidence base would support future recommendations regarding the adaptation and implementation of MECC across diverse healthcare settings and national contexts.
