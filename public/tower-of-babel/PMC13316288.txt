A diagnostic nomogram for tuberculous pleural effusion: Methodological considerations
Authors: Arman Abroumand Gholami, Jasur Rizaev, Mukhtor Jumaev, Ozimbay Jabbarov, Dildora Saidova
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We read with considerable interest the recent article by Ha et al. entitled “Development of a diagnostic nomogram for tuberculous pleural effusion” published in the Journal of Clinical Tuberculosis and Other Mycobacterial Diseases [1]. The authors should be commended for addressing an important and persistent diagnostic challenge in pleural tuberculosis. Given the limited sensitivity of microbiological confirmation in tuberculous pleural effusion (TPE), the development of a practical prediction model based on routinely available laboratory parameters represents a clinically meaningful effort. The proposed nomogram integrating pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA), serum ADA, and pleural fluid protein demonstrated excellent discriminatory performance, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve approaching 0.98. Such findings are promising and support the growing recognition that multivariable approaches may outperform reliance on a single biomarker alone. Nevertheless, several issues warrant further discussion before broad clinical implementation can be recommended.

First, the issue of external validity remains central. The nomogram was derived from a retrospective single-center cohort, and although internal validation was performed, model performance may differ substantially across epidemiologic settings. The diagnostic spectrum of pleural tuberculosis varies according to tuberculosis prevalence, host immune status, HIV coinfection rates, and the prevalence of competing causes of lymphocytic exudative pleural effusions [2]. Prediction models developed in highly selected cohorts frequently demonstrate reduced accuracy when applied to independent populations. Therefore, prospective multicenter external validation across geographically diverse settings would be essential to establish reproducibility and calibration of the proposed nomogram.

Second, although ADA is an established and widely used marker for TPE, incorporation of both pleural and serum ADA into the model raises questions regarding incremental clinical utility over conventional ADA-based diagnostic strategies alone. In many high-burden settings, pleural ADA already performs well as a standalone rule-in test. It would therefore be valuable to know whether the nomogram provides statistically and clinically meaningful improvement compared with pleural ADA alone, particularly through analyses such as net reclassification improvement, integrated discrimination improvement, or head-to-head comparison of diagnostic accuracy [3]. Demonstrating superiority over existing simplified approaches would strengthen the rationale for adopting a more complex model.

Third, the applicability of the nomogram in real-world heterogeneous pleural disease populations requires further clarification. Diagnostic studies of TPE should ideally include a broad range of competing etiologies, particularly malignant pleural effusion, parapneumonic effusion, connective tissue disease–associated pleuritis, and non-tuberculous mycobacterial disease, all of which may share overlapping biochemical characteristics [4]. The extent to which such conditions were represented within the derivation cohort may substantially influence apparent model performance. Additional subgroup analyses evaluating performance in immunocompromised individuals, elderly patients, or patients with indeterminate ADA ranges would also enhance clinical interpretability.

Fourth, although the nomogram demonstrated excellent discrimination, calibration and clinical implementation deserve equal emphasis. Highly discriminative models may still perform sub optimally if predicted probabilities are poorly calibrated across different prevalence settings. Because positive and negative predictive values are prevalence-dependent, clinicians in low-burden regions may interpret the model differently from those practicing in endemic areas [5]. Future studies evaluating clinical impact, decision thresholds, and implementation feasibility in resource-limited settings would therefore be valuable.

Finally, simplicity remains an important consideration for bedside diagnostic tools. One of the strengths of the present model is its reliance on routinely obtainable laboratory variables. However, translating a statistical nomogram into everyday clinical use may require simplified scoring systems, web-based calculators, or integration into electronic clinical workflows. Prospective implementation studies would help determine whether the model meaningfully improves diagnostic decision-making, reduces invasive procedures, or accelerates treatment initiation.

In summary, Ha and colleagues have provided an important contribution toward improving the diagnosis of tuberculous pleural effusion using accessible laboratory parameters. Their findings represent a promising step toward individualized diagnostic prediction in pleural tuberculosis. We believe that future external validation, comparative performance analyses against conventional ADA-based approaches, and prospective implementation studies will be critical to defining the ultimate clinical role of this nomogram. We thank the authors and editors for advancing research in this neglected yet clinically significant form of extrapulmonary tuberculosis.
