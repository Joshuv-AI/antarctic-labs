A short-term randomized controlled trial of bilaminar techniques using tunnel versus envelope flap for root coverage in multiple mandibular anterior gingival recessions with thin gingival phenotype
Authors: Nana Liu, Qiang Chen, Ying Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo compare clinical, aesthetic, and patient-reported outcomes of tunnel technique (TUN) versus envelope flap technique (ENV), both combined with connective tissue graft (CTG), for the treatment of multiple mandibular anterior gingival recession defects in patients with thin gingival phenotype.MethodsFifty-nine patients contributing 103 recession sites in teeth 33–43 were enrolled. Randomisation was performed at the patient level, and all eligible sites within the same patient received the same intervention. Thirty-four patients (59 sites) received TUN + CTG and 25 patients (44 sites) received ENV + CTG. Clinical outcomes were analysed at the site level with patient-level clustering accounted for using mixed-effects models. Root coverage percentage (RC%), complete root coverage (CRC), keratinized tissue width (KTW), gingival thickness (GT), Root Esthetic Score (RES), and patient-reported outcomes were assessed at 3 and 6 months.ResultsAt 6 months, mean RC% in the TUN group (79.18 ± 31.49%) was significantly higher than in the ENV group (57.93 ± 37.24%; adjusted mean difference 19.8%, 95% CI 6.9–32.7; P = 0.003). CRC was achieved in 37/59 sites (62.7%) in TUN and 14/44 sites (31.8%) in ENV (adjusted OR 3.36, 95% CI 1.48–7.61; P = 0.004). Both groups showed significant gains in KTW and GT, while RES scores and patient satisfaction were consistently higher in the TUN group.ConclusionBoth techniques effectively improved recession defects and soft tissue phenotype in multiple mandibular anterior gingival recessions. The tunnel technique provided superior root coverage, aesthetic integration, and patient satisfaction over 6 months while maintaining a comparable operative time and favourable safety profile.

Introduction

Gingival recession is a prevalent periodontal condition characterized by the apical migration of the gingival margin, resulting in root surface exposure [1]. In the mandibular anterior region, recession defects present unique challenges due to the anatomical constraints, including thin alveolar bone, reduced keratinized tissue width, and prominent root prominences [2]. The aesthetic and hypersensitivity concerns associated with mandibular anterior recession significantly impact patients’ quality of life, making effective treatment approaches essential [3].

The classification of gingival biotype has evolved significantly, with thin biotype characterized by gingival thickness ≤ 1.0 mm, delicate tissue texture, and increased susceptibility to recession [4]. Patients with thin biotype present particular treatment challenges, as the limited tissue thickness may compromise graft survival and long-term stability [5]. The Cairo classification system, which has largely replaced the Miller classification, categorizes recession defects based on the presence of interproximal attachment loss and provides more accurate prognostic indicators for root coverage procedures [6].

Bilaminar techniques, combining a vascularized pedicle flap with a connective tissue graft (CTG), have emerged as the gold standard for root coverage procedures [7]. The CTG provides additional tissue volume, enhances vascular supply, and improves long-term stability of the gingival margin [8]. Among contemporary approaches for multiple recession defects, the tunnel technique (TUN) and envelope flap technique (ENV) represent two distinct philosophies in flap design and tissue management [9, 10].

The tunnel technique, pioneered by Allen and subsequently refined by Zuhr and colleagues, involves creating a supraperiosteal tunnel without releasing incisions at the papillae, thereby preserving blood supply and minimizing tissue trauma [11, 12]. This approach theoretically offers several advantages, including reduced surgical morbidity, better vascular integrity, and enhanced aesthetic outcomes due to papilla preservation [13]. The technique has been successfully applied to treat multiple adjacent recession defects and has shown promising results in anatomically demanding sites [14].

In contrast, the envelope flap technique involves an intrasulcular access with split-thickness pouch preparation to accommodate the CTG without vertical releasing incisions [8, 15]. This approach provides direct access for graft placement while maintaining marginal tissue continuity, but concerns remain regarding flap stability and tension-free coronal positioning in thin periodontal phenotype cases with limited vestibular depth.

Despite the widespread use of both techniques, comparative evidence specifically addressing their efficacy in patients with multiple mandibular anterior recession defects and thin gingival phenotype remains limited. Most existing studies have focused on maxillary teeth, isolated recessions, or mixed phenotypes without explicit clustering of multiple sites within the same patient. This gap in knowledge necessitates targeted investigations to guide clinical decision-making for this challenging patient population.

The present study was designed to compare the clinical efficacy, aesthetic outcomes, and patient-reported outcomes of tunnel versus envelope techniques combined with CTG for treating gingival recession in patients with thin gingival biotype in the lower anterior teeth over a 6-month observation period.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was conducted as a prospective, parallel-group, randomized controlled clinical trial comparing two surgical techniques for root coverage. The study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee, and all participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and followed the CONSORT 2025 statement for reporting randomized trials [16].

Study population

Patients were recruited at Suzhou Stomatological Hospital. The inclusion criteria were: (1) age ≥ 18 years; (2) presence of at least one and up to three adjacent Miller Class I, II, or III (Cairo RT1 or RT2) gingival recession defects in the mandibular anterior region (central incisors, lateral incisors, or canines, teeth 33–43); (3) baseline gingival thickness (GT) ≤ 1.0 mm at the affected site, classified as thin gingival phenotype; and (4) good compliance with oral hygiene instructions and follow-up. Exclusion criteria included: (1) untreated periodontitis, caries, or endodontic lesions at the target teeth; (2) previous periodontal surgery at the target site; (3) systemic diseases affecting wound healing, such as uncontrolled diabetes mellitus; and (4) current smoking.

Patients underwent comprehensive periodontal examination and radiographic evaluation. Detailed medical and dental histories were obtained, and oral hygiene instruction was provided. All patients completed a full-mouth supragingival and subgingival scaling at least 4 weeks prior to surgical intervention to ensure optimal periodontal health.

Treatment allocation

Randomisation was performed at the patient level using a computer-generated allocation sequence prepared by a research assistant not involved in treatment or outcome assessment. Allocation was concealed in sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes that were opened immediately before surgery. All eligible recession sites within the same patient were assigned to the same intervention to avoid within-patient contamination of surgical care. Thirty-four patients contributing 59 sites were allocated to the tunnel group, and 25 patients contributing 44 sites were allocated to the envelope group. All surgeries were performed by a single experienced periodontist to minimize operator variability.

Surgical procedures

Connective tissue graft harvesting

Following preparation and measurement of the recipient site to determine the required graft dimensions, the connective tissue graft (CTG) was harvested from the palatal premolar region. A de-epithelialized free gingival graft approach was used, as previously described for periodontal plastic surgery graft harvesting [17]. After local anesthesia with 2% lidocaine containing 1:100,000 epinephrine, a free gingival graft was obtained from the palate and the epithelial layer was carefully removed extra-orally using a sharp scalpel blade to obtain the connective tissue portion. Hemostasis at the donor site was achieved according to standard protocols, and the graft was stored in sterile saline before trimming to fit the recipient bed. Final graft thickness was maintained at approximately 1.0–1.5 mm, extending 2–3 mm beyond the recession margins to ensure adequate coverage.

Recipient site preparation - tunnel technique (TUN)

The tunnel technique was performed according to the microsurgical principles described by Zuhr and colleagues [11, 12]. After achieving adequate anesthesia, root surface preparation was performed using Gracey curettes to remove plaque-retentive deposits and smooth the root surface without aggressive root planing. Microsurgical instruments were employed throughout the procedure to maximize precision and minimize tissue trauma.

An intrasulcular incision was made at each recession site without extending into the papillae. Using a microsurgical blade and tunneling instruments, a split-full-split thickness dissection was performed. Initially, a full-thickness flap was elevated in the first 2–3 mm beyond the recession defect to maintain flap thickness and stability. Subsequently, a partial-thickness dissection was extended apically and laterally beyond the mucogingival junction to ensure adequate flap mobility. The dissection was carefully extended to create a continuous tunnel connecting adjacent recession sites when multiple teeth were involved, while meticulously preserving the integrity of the interdental papillae.

The CTG was carefully introduced into the tunnel using microsurgical forceps and positioned at the level of the cemento-enamel junction (CEJ). The graft was secured in position with stabilizing sutures anchored to the periosteum apical to the recession defects. The coronal portion of the tunnel flap was then advanced and suspended to the desired position using sling sutures or horizontal mattress sutures passed through the interproximal areas, achieving tension-free coronal advancement. Care was taken to position the gingival margin 1 mm coronal to the cemento-enamel junction (CEJ).Additional interrupted sutures were placed as needed to ensure complete wound closure and intimate contact between the flap and the underlying graft.

Recipient site preparation - envelope flap technique (ENV)

For the envelope flap approach, an intrasulcular incision was made along the involved recession defect(s), and a split-thickness pouch was elevated apically and laterally to create an envelope-type recipient bed for the CTG, following the principles used in bilaminar treatment of multiple recession defects [8, 15]. The dissection was extended at least 3–4 mm beyond the mucogingival junction to allow adequate flap mobility and tension-free coronal advancement, while avoiding vertical releasing incisions.

The root surface was prepared as described above. The trimmed CTG was then inserted into the envelope pouch, positioned to cover the exposed root surface, and stabilized with periosteal anchoring sutures placed apically. The CTG was positioned to cover the exposed root surface at the level of the cemento-enamel junction (CEJ) and typically extended 2–3 mm beyond the recession margins in all directions.The envelope flap was then advanced coronally over the graft and secured using interrupted sutures or horizontal mattress sutures, positioning the gingival margin 1 mm coronal to the CEJ. Tension-free closure was verified, and additional sutures were placed as necessary to ensure intimate contact between tissues.

Postoperative care

All patients received standardized postoperative instructions and care protocols. Patients were prescribed: (1) systemic antibiotics (amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily for 7 days, or alternative antibiotics for patients with penicillin allergy); (2) analgesics as needed (ibuprofen 400 mg three times daily); (3) chlorhexidine gluconate 0.12% mouth rinse three times for 2 weeks. Patients were instructed to avoid mechanical tooth brushing in the surgical area for 4 weeks and to maintain a soft diet for the first 2 weeks postoperatively. Gentle rinsing with prescribed mouth rinse was encouraged. Sutures were removed 14 days postoperatively. At each follow-up visit, professional prophylaxis was performed, and oral hygiene reinforcement was provided. Patients were instructed to avoid mechanical tooth brushing in the surgical area for 4 weeks. For non-surgical sites, tooth brushing was resumed 2 weeks postoperatively. After 4 weeks, patients were instructed to use an ultra-soft toothbrush on the surgical site using the modified Bass technique.

Clinical parameter assessment

Measurements were recorded at the mid-buccal aspect of each treated site using a UNC-15 periodontal probe to the nearest 0.5 mm. The primary outcome measure was the percentage of root coverage (RC%) at 6 months postoperatively, calculated as: RC% = [(Baseline RD - Postoperative RD) / Baseline RD] × 100%. Complete root coverage (CRC) was defined as 100% root coverage with postoperative RD of 0 mm. Secondary outcome measures included gains in KTW and GT, calculated as the difference between baseline and postoperative values.

Aesthetic and patient-reported outcome assessment

Root Esthetic Score (RES)

Two independent periodontists, who were not involved in the study and were blinded to treatment allocation, evaluated aesthetic outcomes using standardized clinical photographs taken at baseline, 3 months, and 6 months. The Root Esthetic Score (RES) proposed by Cairo et al. was used to assess the aesthetic outcome on a 0–10 scale [19]. The evaluation included gingival margin position, marginal contour, soft tissue texture, mucogingival junction alignment, gingival color, and scar tissue appearance. Photographs were obtained under standardized lighting, magnification, and patient positioning, and the mean of the two evaluators was used as the final RES value. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC).

Patient-Reported Outcomes (PROMs)

Patients completed visual analog scale (VAS) assessments at baseline and follow-up visits to evaluate: (1) Root sensitivity: patients indicated their level of tooth sensitivity on a 10 cm horizontal line (0 = no sensitivity, 10 = extreme sensitivity) in response to cold stimulus (ice water); (2) Overall satisfaction: at follow-up visits, patients rated their overall satisfaction with the treatment outcome on a 10 cm VAS (0 = completely dissatisfied, 10 = completely satisfied). Additional qualitative feedback was collected through structured interviews at the 6-month visit.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (version 26.0, IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Sample size was calculated for the primary endpoint (RC% at 6 months) using the formula n = 2(Z1-α/2 + Z1-β)^2σ^2/Δ^2. Based on pilot observations, a clinically relevant between-group difference (Δ) of 15%, a standard deviation (σ) of 22%, two-sided α = 0.05, and 80% power yielded 34 sites per group. Because some patients contributed more than one site, this estimate was inflated by a design effect of 1.06 derived from an average cluster size of 1.7 sites per patient and an intracluster correlation coefficient of 0.08, resulting in a minimum requirement of 36 sites per group.

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD), and categorical variables as frequency and percentage. Site-level outcomes were analysed with linear mixed-effects models including treatment group as a fixed effect and patient as a random intercept to account for within-patient clustering. Baseline values of the corresponding outcome and clinically relevant baseline imbalances were entered as covariates when appropriate. Normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical baseline variables were compared using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test, and complete root coverage (CRC) at 6 months was analysed using a generalized estimating equation model with patient-level clustering.

To address baseline imbalance, Miller classification and tooth brushing technique were prespecified covariates in adjusted analyses of the primary endpoint, and age and baseline gingival thickness were explored in sensitivity analyses. Results are reported with effect estimates, 95% confidence intervals, and two-sided P values. Inter-rater reliability for RES scores was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

The tunnel group demonstrated significantly thinner baseline gingival thickness (0.72 ± 0.29 mm vs. 0.88 ± 0.35 mm, P = 0.012) and shallower probing depth (0.92 ± 0.44 mm vs. 1.14 ± 0.47 mm, P = 0.018) compared to the envelope group. Baseline imbalance was observed for recession classification distribution and tooth brushing technique. The envelope group included a greater proportion of more advanced recession defects, whereas the tunnel group included a higher proportion of patients reporting the Bass brushing technique. These variables were therefore retained as covariates in the adjusted analyses.

Surgical outcomes

All surgeries were completed as planned, and all patients tolerated the procedures well. No significant difference in operative duration was observed between the two groups (P = 0.453). Mean surgery time was 96.61 ± 22.54 min for the tunnel technique and 92.73 ± 28.09 min for the envelope technique. No adverse events such as excessive bleeding, graft exposure, suppuration, or postoperative infection were recorded during the observation period.

At 6 months, the mean RC% in the tunnel group was 79.18 ± 31.49%, significantly higher than 57.93 ± 37.24% in the envelope group (adjusted mean difference 19.8%, 95% CI 6.9–32.7; P = 0.003). Residual recession depth was significantly lower in the tunnel group (0.48 ± 0.74 mm vs. 1.22 ± 1.13 mm, P < 0.001). The RES scores continued to demonstrate significant aesthetic superiority for the tunnel technique at 6 months (9.27 ± 1.27 vs. 7.43 ± 2.16, P < 0.001). Patient satisfaction remained significantly higher in the tunnel group (9.42 ± 1.49 vs. 8.20 ± 2.09, P = 0.001).

Complete root coverage was achieved in 37 of 59 sites (62.7%) in the tunnel group and 14 of 44 sites (31.8%) in the envelope group. In the clustered binary model, treatment with the tunnel technique was associated with a significantly greater likelihood of CRC (adjusted OR 3.36, 95% CI 1.48–7.61; P = 0.004).

Discussion

This short-term randomized controlled trial compared the clinical efficacy and aesthetic outcomes of tunnel technique versus envelope flap technique combined with connective tissue graft for treating multiple mandibular anterior gingival recession defects in patients with thin gingival phenotype. The results demonstrate that while both bilaminar approaches effectively improved recession coverage and increased soft tissue thickness, the tunnel technique showed superior performance in terms of root coverage, complete root coverage, aesthetic integration, and patient satisfaction.

The primary finding of this study—that the tunnel technique achieved significantly higher mean root coverage and complete root coverage at 6 months—aligns with previous evidence indicating favorable performance of tunnel-based approaches in localized and multiple recession defects [9]. The persistence of this difference after adjustment for baseline gingival thickness, recession classification, brushing technique, and patient-level clustering strengthens the clinical relevance of the observed treatment effect.

The enhanced performance of the tunnel technique in our study can be attributed to several biomechanical and biological advantages. Preservation of the interdental papillae and tension-controlled coronal advancement help maintain vascular integrity and reduce mechanical stress on the flap-graft complex [20, 21]. These factors likely contributed to the superior healing and tissue maturation observed in the tunnel group. In addition, the supraperiosteal dissection plane of the tunnel approach provides a protected recipient bed for CTG incorporation while limiting visible scar formation.

The baseline imbalance in gingival thickness between groups—with the tunnel group starting from significantly thinner tissue—makes the present findings particularly noteworthy. Thin gingival phenotype is commonly regarded as a less favorable substrate for root coverage because of reduced tissue volume and greater susceptibility to postoperative shrinkage [5]. Nevertheless, the tunnel group achieved superior clinical outcomes, suggesting that preservation of tissue vascularity and flap stability may partly offset the biological disadvantage associated with thin phenotype.

Our study documented significant gains in keratinized tissue width in both groups, with the tunnel technique showing a greater increase at 3 months. Although this difference diminished by 6 months, the numerical trend favored the tunnel group. These gains are clinically relevant because sufficient keratinized tissue facilitates plaque control and improves tolerance to mechanical cleaning in the mandibular anterior region, where vestibular depth and muscle pull may complicate oral hygiene [22, 23].

Both techniques achieved comparable gains in gingival thickness, effectively converting thin sites to a more favorable phenotype. This finding confirms that bilaminar procedures with CTG successfully address the soft-tissue deficit characteristic of thin phenotype recession defects [24, 25]. The comparable thickness gains between groups suggest that graft dimension and integration are major determinants of tissue thickening, whereas flap design may play a larger role in coronal stabilization and surface esthetics.

The aesthetic evaluation in our study employed blinded independent assessors, standardized photography, and a validated Root Esthetic Score system [19, 26]. The tunnel technique demonstrated consistently superior RES scores at both 3 and 6 months. Preservation of papillary architecture, reduced scar visibility, and more uniform coronal positioning of the gingival margin are plausible explanations for this aesthetic advantage.

Patient-reported outcomes also favored the tunnel group. This is clinically meaningful because esthetic concern and dentin hypersensitivity are major reasons patients seek treatment for buccal recession defects [3, 27]. The higher satisfaction scores in the tunnel group are consistent with the stronger professional RES ratings and greater root coverage obtained in that group, supporting the view that clinician-rated esthetic success and patient-perceived benefit are closely related [26, 28].

The comparable surgical duration between techniques suggests that, when performed by an experienced operator, the tunnel approach can be delivered without a clinically important increase in treatment time. This finding is relevant because reluctance to use tunnel techniques is often linked to perceived technical complexity rather than measured operative inefficiency.

Several limitations warrant discussion. First, the follow-up period was limited to 6 months, and longer observation is needed to determine stability of the achieved coverage. Second, although randomisation was performed at the patient level and the statistical models accounted for clustering of multiple sites within the same patient, the unequal distribution of recession classes and brushing technique may still have introduced residual confounding. Third, this was a single-center study performed by one experienced surgeon, which supports procedural consistency but may limit external generalizability.

The envelope group’s lower performance relative to the tunnel group may be attributable to the anatomical constraints of the mandibular anterior region, particularly in thin phenotype sites with shallow vestibular depth and pronounced root prominence. Under these conditions, the ability to obtain stable, tension-free coronal positioning may be more critical than simple access for graft placement.

Future studies should incorporate longer follow-up, multicenter recruitment, and predefined multilevel analyses to further clarify the interaction between site anatomy, phenotype, and surgical technique in multiple recession defects. Digital three-dimensional monitoring and patient-centered outcomes should also be integrated to evaluate the stability and esthetic perception of treatment over time.

From a clinical perspective, these findings support the tunnel technique as a preferred option for treating multiple mandibular anterior gingival recession defects in thin phenotype patients when adequate microsurgical expertise is available. The technique combined superior root coverage and esthetic integration with similar operative time and an uncomplicated healing profile in this cohort.

In conclusion, this randomized controlled trial demonstrated that the tunnel technique combined with connective tissue graft achieved significantly better root coverage, complete coverage rate, aesthetic outcomes, and patient satisfaction than the envelope flap technique for multiple mandibular anterior gingival recession defects in thin gingival phenotype patients. Both techniques successfully increased soft tissue dimensions, but the tunnel approach provided a more favorable overall short-term clinical profile.
