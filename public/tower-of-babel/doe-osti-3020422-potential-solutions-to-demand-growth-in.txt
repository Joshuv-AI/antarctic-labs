Summary

• NREL helped evaluate demand flexibility technical potential for the Virginia
  Department of Energy through the U.S. Department of Energy State Energy Office
  Direct Technical Assistance Program
• The following report presents NREL’s work and is split into two parts:
  • A literature review covering projected load growth in Virginia and virtual power plants (VPPs)
  • A technical analysis that estimates demand flexibility technical potential in Virginia across
 common sectors and end-uses
• The literature review and analysis show that the potential for demand flexibility in
  Virginia is substantial, however, further analysis could extend the assumptions
  and data used in this report to develop an estimate of economic and/or achievable
  potential

 High-Level Takeaways

• Data centers account for ~26% of Virginia’s total electricity demand, a figure that is expected to grow by 2030. With proper
 enabling policy and program design, demand flexibility and VPPs could help to meet Virginia’s load growth.1
• In practice, VPPs have lowered peak demand and provided customers with more affordable devices in exchange for
 enrollment.2 Flexible energy use at data centers also has the potential for beneficial peak shaving and load shifting to
 support the grid.3
• Challenges of demand flexibility and VPPs include uncertainty in predicted vs. actual load when a VPP is called,
 communication failures impacting DER response, determining the ideal number of demand response events to keep customers
 engaged, and compliance with enabling policy.4
• Our estimates for demand flexibility potential in Virginia are substantial and well-above Virginia’s currently assessed
 demand response capacity, e.g., the upper end of estimated current summer peaking capacity is >4,300 MW.
• There is significant potential for both summer and winter seasons, and projections to 2030 suggest growing opportunities
 for demand flexibility.
• Even while there is large potential, our estimates are subject to uncertainty, especially due to assumptions around data
 center flexibility and possible variation within industrial end-uses not captured in the analysis.
• A natural next step to this analysis would be evaluation of economic and/or achievable demand flexibility potential based on
 participation rates, incentives, etc.

 Glossary of Terms

 • Demand flexibility: the ability to reduce or shift electricity consumption to mitigate demands on the grid or
 lower customer bills. This can take place through strategies such as time-varying rates, event-based
 demand response, or virtual power plants.
 • Distributed energy resource (DER): “Small demand flexibility/responsive load, generation or storage
 assets, often behind-the-meter and connected to lower-voltage distribution grids.”1 VPPs are made up of
 groups of DERs.
 • Virtual power plant (VPP): “aggregations of distributed energy resources (DERs) that balance electrical
 loads and provide utility-scale and utility-grade grid services like a traditional power plant.”2 VPPs are
 overseen by a centralized controller and are sometimes referred to by other names, such as distributed
 power plants (DPPs).
 • Demand response (DR): The practice of curtailing electricity consumption in response to peak demand
 signals from grid operators. Often, behavioral changes are incentivized through time-varying retail
 electricity rates. Responses include turning down HVAC systems or rescheduling industrial production line
 operations.

1. K. Horowitz et al., “An Overview of Distributed Energy Resource (DER) Interconnection: Current Practices and Emerging Solutions,” NREL/TP-6A20-72102, 2019.
2. Jennifer Downing et al., “Pathways to Commercial Liftoff: Virtual Power Plants,” U.S. Department of Energy, 2023; Brittany Speetles et al., “Virtual Power Plants
and Energy Justice,” NREL, 2024

 Glossary of Terms (Cont.)

 • Responsive load DERs: Sometimes termed demand flexibility DERs, responsive load DERs “include
 flexible, controllable resources such as smart thermostats and smart water heaters.”1
 • DER management system (DERMS): “Hardware and software that can aggregate, monitor, control and
 forecast/predict DER operations. DERMS are often managed by distribution network system operators
 and used to support and expand DER integration. A VPP uses a DERMS to control DERs collectively.”1
 • Microgrids: “An interconnected group of DERs and loads—typically including a storage component—
 that can interact with the electricity grid or form a separate grid in “islanded mode.” VPPs are not
 typically designed to be islandable or to prioritize providing resilience benefits to end users.” 1
 • Non-wires alternatives (NWA): Investments in DERs and other technologies intended to displace
 transmission or distribution system upgrades or other investments. VPPs can be a type of non-wires
 alternative, but the term also encompasses other strategies such as energy efficiency, demand
 response, microgrids, etc.

1. Brittany Speetles et al., “Virtual Power Plants and Energy Justice,” NREL, 2024

Background

 What Is Demand Flexibility?
 • Demand flexibility is the ability to reduce or shift electricity consumption to mitigate demands on the grid
 or lower customer bills.
 • Demand flexibility is an umbrella term, and includes tools such as:
 • Price-based demand response, including time-varying rates
 • Behavior-based demand response
 • Virtual power plants (VPPs)

Source: Paul Mathew and Lino Sanchez, “Demand Flexibility for Laboratories,” Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, 2022 [Technical Memo].

 What Are Virtual Power Plants?
 • Defined as “aggregations of distributed energy resources (DERs) that balance electrical loads and provide
 utility-scale and utility-grade grid services like a traditional power plant.”1
 • DERs are “small demand flexibility/responsive load, generation or storage assets, often connected to
 lower-voltage distribution grid or behind-the-meter.”2
 • Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (FERC) Order 2222 lets DERs participate in wholesale markets.
 • Demand flexibility use cases include load shifting, peak shaving, and fast-response grid services.

Sonali Razdan et al., “Pathways to Commercial Liftoff: Virtual Power Plants (2025 Update),” U.S. Department of Energy, 2025
2. K. Horowitz et al., “An Overview of Distributed Energy Resource (DER) Interconnection: Current Practices and Emerging Solutions,” NREL/TP-6A20-72102, 2019.

 Demand Flexibility Use Cases

 • Load shifting: “Altering the demand profile to meet certain performance criteria, usually involving a
 reduction in on-peak demand and a load increase at a different time.”1
 • Water heaters with tanks, electric vehicles, thermostat control

 • Peak shaving: Displacing power consumption by a predetermined amount for a specific time.2
 • Air conditioners, heat pumps

 • Local resiliency: Providing the ability to continue using electricity during and in response to major
 disruptions.3
 • Battery storage, storage + solar

 • Frequency response: Injecting energy into (or absorbing energy from) a power system at the second to
 subsecond scale with quick and/or continuous response to maintain appropriate frequency levels.4
 • Resistance water heaters, commercial and industrial fast response load, storage, solar plus storage, heavy industry
 equipment (i.e., aluminum smelter)

 Massachusetts
 • Utility-run, bring your own device program
 • Up to $1,500/year for event participation

Demand Flexibility Technical
 Potential Analysis

High-Level Analysis Overview

• Objective: Estimate technical potential for state-wide demand flexibility in 2025 and 2030.
• High-level approach:
  • Identify sectors & end-uses for demand flexibility analysis
  • Analyze system conditions in Virginia and define on-peak (response) windows
  • Define summer and winter demand response seasons based on Virginia precedent
  • Develop baseline load profiles by end-use and sector based on top days of system stress by season
  • Define flexibility assumptions by end-use and sector
  • Define generalized flexibility models for pure shift, pure shed, and shed/shift
  • Apply flexibility assumptions to baseline load profiles using generalized flexibility models
  • Estimate current total demand response capacity in Virginia
  • Compare estimated flexibility potential to existing demand response programs.

 Develop Baseline Load Profiles & Apply Flexibility Models

 • We use forecasted hourly load from dsgrid1,2 to construct representative diurnal load profiles for
 different end-uses by sector
 • Process:
 1. Cambium load forecasts for Virginia3 are used to identify the top 15 days of peak load within summer and
 winter seasons in 2025 and 2030.
 2. Cross-reference the top 15 days by season and model year with forecasted end-use load data and construct
 average diurnal (24-hour) load profiles for each end-use (or combination of end-uses) based on load forecasts
 for these matched days.4

 • We then apply flexibility models to each representative diurnal load profile, which allows us to
 estimate the average peak capacity reduction and average energy savings per-event; to estimate
 average per-season energy savings by end-use, we assume 15 demand response events per season
 and therefore multiply the per-event average energy savings by 15

Analysis Takeaways

• This analysis focuses on using assumptions about demand flexibility to estimate technical potential
  across different sectors and end-uses.
• Estimated technical potential is substantial; e.g., for Summer 2025, the estimated peak capacity reduction
  is between 3,630 and 4,340 MW based on our assumptions and degree of data center flexibility
• Technical potential estimates are subject to uncertainty, especially due to assumptions around data
  center flexibility—which comprises a large share of the estimated technical potential—and possible variation
  within industrial end-uses not captured here
• The technical potential for 2030 is higher than for 2025 for both summer and winter seasons, which may
  suggest growing opportunities for demand flexibility.
• Planned Dominion VPP pilot (up to 450 MW) is still only ~12% of our assessed difference between
  technical potential and current VPP capacity.
• Technical potential is not equal to economic or achievable potential, which should be evaluated
  separately and depends on factors such as participation rates, program costs, etc.

Analysis Caveats and Limitations

• The analysis is based on high-level assumptions about flexibility potential, demand response event
  timing and duration, etc. More complex modeling and analysis could draw on real-world response and
  participation data, consider higher-resolution and broader-scoped response optimization, or incorporate costs
• A large share of the technical potential (average peak power reduction) is from data center flexibility;
  however, this estimate is highly sensitive to assumptions about willingness to participate, how many events
  are called per year, and their duration
• We focus on load reduction within time periods of high system stress; however, there may be other
  hours outside of these periods where it is possible to achieve further load reductions
• Industry is modeled in aggregate with an overall flexibility factor applied, which does not capture
  differences in flexibility between different industrial sectors and end-uses (this caveat also applies to data
  centers)
• Estimates for 2030 should be interpreted with a high degree of caution as adoption rates, evolving load
  forecasts, etc., are all uncertain into the future
