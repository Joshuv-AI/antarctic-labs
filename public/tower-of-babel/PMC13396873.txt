Influence of Ethnic Diversity on Service Delivery Outcomes in Kenyan Public Healthcare
Authors: Collince Odiwuor Ogolla, Caroline Sitienei Koech, Josephine Opondo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHealthcare service delivery considers ethnic diversity as an essential factor in its operations. There's however, limited empirical evidence from Kenyan public hospitals.ObjectiveThe study aimed to determine how ethnic diversity affects service delivery results, which included staff performance, teamwork, and patient satisfaction.MethodsAn analytical cross‐sectional study was conducted, including 182 healthcare workers and 210 outpatients. Blau's Index (1 − Σpi2) was used to measure ethnic diversity, divided into three groups: low ethnic diversity at 0.3 level or below, moderate ethnic diversity between 0.3 and 0.6 level, and high ethnic diversity above 0.6 level. The researchers obtained data through three instruments: the Team Climate Inventory and the Individual Work Performance Questionnaire and a 5‐point patient satisfaction scale. Multivariable regression analysis was used to examine results while controlling for demographic and professional variables.ResultsThe average ethnic diversity index across departments showed high diversity with a value of 0.64 ± 0.12. The relationship between ethnic diversity and staff performance showed a positive connection with a correlation coefficient of 0.35 (p < 0.001) and a 95% confidence interval of 0.22 to 0.47. The research results showed that ethnic diversity accounted for 18.9% of staff performance variations (Adjusted R 2 = 0.189, p < 0.001). Public healthcare facilities experience improved service delivery results when they adopt ethnic diversity as a positive factor.ConclusionThe positive effects of diversity management require the effective implementation of diversity management initiatives.Keywords: Blau index, ethnic diversity, healthcare performance, Kenya, teamwork

Introduction

Workforce diversity has become an important feature of modern healthcare systems, particularly in multicultural environments where healthcare institutions serve linguistically, culturally, and socioeconomically diverse populations. Among the dimensions of workplace diversity, ethnic diversity has attracted increasing scholarly attention due to its influence on communication, teamwork, and healthcare service delivery outcomes [1, 2]. In healthcare settings, where service delivery depends heavily on interprofessional collaboration and patient interaction, ethnic diversity may enhance innovation, adaptability, and responsiveness to patient needs, but may also generate communication barriers and interpersonal tension when organizational integration mechanisms are weak [3].

Recent diversity research emphasizes the conditional nature of diversity outcomes. The “diversity‐as‐resource” perspective views ethnic diversity as a strategic organizational asset that improves creativity, problem‐solving, and service responsiveness through varied perspectives and experiences. Conversely, the “diversity‐as‐process‐loss” perspective argues that diversity may reduce cohesion and coordination due to differences in language, values, and communication styles [4]. Contemporary studies further suggest that the impact of diversity depends on contextual factors such as leadership quality, communication systems, and inclusive organizational practices [5].

Despite growing literature on workforce diversity, several gaps remain. Most existing studies originate from high‐income countries, limiting their applicability to healthcare systems in low‐ and middle‐income countries [6]. In addition, many studies rely on perceptual assessments of diversity rather than objective measures such as Blau's Index, and often examine isolated outcomes instead of multidimensional service delivery indicators, including staff performance, teamwork efficiency, and patient satisfaction [1]. Theoretical integration within diversity research also remains limited, particularly regarding how organizational context shapes diversity outcomes. These gaps are particularly relevant in Kenya, where public healthcare institutions operate within ethnically diverse environments and under policy frameworks that emphasize equity and inclusiveness in public service delivery [7, 8]. However, empirical evidence linking ethnic diversity to healthcare service delivery outcomes in Kenyan public hospitals remains limited.

This study, therefore, examines the influence of ethnic diversity on service delivery outcomes in Kenyan healthcare context. Ethnic diversity is operationalized using Blau's Index, while service delivery outcomes are conceptualized through staff performance, teamwork efficiency, and patient satisfaction. The study is grounded in Social Identity Theory, the Resource‐Based View (RBV), and the Job Demands–Resources (JD‐R) model, which collectively explain how ethnic diversity may function either as a strategic organizational resource or a source of interpersonal and coordination challenges depending on organizational conditions [9].

The study further introduces inclusive organizational practices as a moderating factor in the diversity–service delivery relationship. Inclusive leadership, equitable communication systems, and diversity‐supportive organizational climates may reduce intergroup bias and strengthen collaboration within ethnically diverse teams [5]. By integrating contextual, methodological, and theoretical perspectives, this study contributes to workforce diversity literature by providing evidence from a Sub‐Saharan African public healthcare setting, applying Blau's Index as an objective measure of ethnic diversity, and advancing understanding of how organizational inclusion shapes healthcare service delivery outcomes.

Theoretical Framework and Hypothesis Development

Social Identity Theory (SIT)

Social Identity Theory (SIT) explains how individuals categorize themselves and others into social groups based on characteristics such as ethnicity, language, and culture [10]. In ethnically diverse healthcare teams, these categorizations may influence communication, trust, and collaboration through in‐group and out‐group dynamics. In healthcare settings characterized by high interdependence, such processes may either strengthen complementary collaboration or generate relational fragmentation if diversity is poorly managed. However, inclusive leadership and diversity‐supportive organizational practices can reduce intergroup bias and improve teamwork effectiveness. SIT therefore provides a useful framework for explaining how ethnic diversity influences interpersonal relations and service delivery outcomes in public healthcare institutions.

Resource‐Based View (RBV)

The Resource‐Based View conceptualizes organizational resources as sources of competitive advantage when they are valuable, rare, and effectively utilized [9]. Within this perspective, ethnic diversity represents a strategic organizational resource that enhances creativity, adaptability, cultural competence, and problem‐solving capacity. In healthcare organizations, ethnically diverse teams may improve clinical decision‐making and responsiveness to diverse patient needs. However, the performance benefits of diversity depend on the organization's ability to effectively integrate and manage heterogeneous teams.

Job Demands–Resources (JD‐R) Model

The Job Demands–Resources (JD‐R) model explains how workplace factors function either as job demands or job resources [11]. In healthcare settings, ethnic diversity may function as a resource by enhancing knowledge sharing and innovation, or as a demand when differences in communication styles and cultural expectations create interpersonal strain. The direction of these effects depends largely on organizational context, particularly the presence of inclusive leadership, equitable communication systems, and diversity‐supportive practices. The JD‐R model, therefore, highlights the conditional nature of diversity outcomes in healthcare organizations [12].

Conceptualization of Study Variables

In this study, ethnic diversity refers to the degree of heterogeneity within healthcare teams and is operationalized using Blau's Index, an objective measure of group diversity widely applied in organizational research [13]. Service delivery outcomes are conceptualized as a multidimensional construct comprising staff performance, teamwork efficiency, and patient satisfaction [14]. Inclusive organizational practices, including equitable leadership and diversity‐sensitive communication systems, are conceptualized as moderating factors influencing the relationship between ethnic diversity and service delivery outcomes.

Hypothesis Development

Based on the preceding theoretical arguments and empirical evidence, the following hypotheses are proposed regarding the relationship between ethnic diversity and service delivery outcomes in public healthcare institutions.

Ethnic Diversity and Staff Performance

Ethnic diversity may improve staff performance by enhancing problem‐solving capacity, innovation, and responsiveness to patient needs through diverse perspectives and experiences [9]. However, SIT suggests that unmanaged diversity may also reduce trust and cohesion through in‐group and out‐group dynamics. Empirical studies indicate that inclusive organizational environments strengthen the positive effects of diversity on employee performance [4].

Ethnic diversity is positively associated with staff performance in public healthcare teams.

Ethnic Diversity and Teamwork Efficiency

Teamwork efficiency in healthcare depends on effective coordination, communication, and mutual trust. Ethnic diversity may strengthen teamwork through complementary knowledge and broader perspectives, but may also create communication barriers and interpersonal tension if poorly managed [1, 15]. The JD‐R model suggests that organizational support systems determine whether diversity enhances or constrains collaborative functioning.

Ethnic diversity is positively associated with teamwork efficiency in healthcare teams.

Ethnic Diversity and Patient Satisfaction

Ethnically diverse healthcare teams may improve patient satisfaction by enhancing cultural competence, communication responsiveness, and patient‐centered care delivery [2]. However, ineffective coordination within diverse teams may negatively affect consistency in service delivery and patient experience. From the RBV perspective, workforce diversity enhances organizational capability in multicultural healthcare environments.

Ethnic diversity is positively associated with patient satisfaction in public hospitals.

Moderating Role of Inclusive Organizational Practices

Inclusive organizational practices such as equitable leadership, diversity‐sensitive communication systems, and supportive workplace policies may strengthen the positive effects of ethnic diversity while reducing interpersonal conflict and fragmentation [16]. Both SIT and the JD‐R model suggest that inclusive environments reduce intergroup bias and transform diversity into a productive organizational resource. Empirical evidence further supports the moderating role of inclusion in diversity–performance relationships [4].

Inclusive organizational practices positively moderate the relationship between ethnic diversity and service delivery outcomes, such that the relationship is stronger when inclusive practices are high.

Theoretical Integration

This study integrates Social Identity Theory, the Resource‐Based View, and the Job Demands–Resources model to provide a multidimensional explanation of how ethnic diversity influences healthcare service delivery outcomes. SIT explains interpersonal and group‐based dynamics, RBV emphasizes the strategic value of workforce heterogeneity, and the JD‐R model explains the organizational conditions under which diversity functions either as an asset or a strain. Together, these theories provide a coherent analytical framework for understanding the influence of ethnic diversity on staff performance, teamwork efficiency, and patient satisfaction in Kenyan public hospitals.

The primary objective of this study is to examine the influence of ethnic diversity on service delivery outcomes in a Kenyan public healthcare setting. The study further seeks to evaluate the relationship between ethnic diversity and staff performance, teamwork efficiency, and patient satisfaction, as well as the moderating role of inclusive organizational practices in these relationships. By addressing contextual, methodological, and theoretical gaps in the literature, the study contributes to workforce diversity scholarship in three important ways. First, it provides context‐specific evidence from a Sub‐Saharan African public healthcare institution. Second, it strengthens methodological rigor through the application of Blau's Index as an objective measure of ethnic diversity. Third, it advances theoretical understanding by integrating Social Identity Theory, the Resource‐Based View, and the Job Demands–Resources model within a unified framework for explaining diversity outcomes in healthcare organizations.

Methods

Study Design

This study adopted a quantitative cross‐sectional analytical design to examine the influence of ethnic diversity on service delivery outcomes. The design was considered appropriate because it enabled the study to assess the relationships between ethnic diversity and multidimensional service delivery outcomes, including staff performance, teamwork efficiency, and patient satisfaction, at a single point in time without manipulation of variables. The study further examined the moderating role of inclusive organizational practices within these relationships. A quantitative approach was selected to facilitate hypothesis testing and statistical analysis of measurable variables grounded in Social Identity Theory, the Resource‐Based View (RBV), and the Job Demands–Resources (JD‐R) model. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines to enhance reporting quality and scientific credibility; CONSORT guidelines were not applicable because the study was non‐experimental and did not involve randomized intervention procedures.

Study Area and Setting

The study was conducted in a Kenyan tertiary hospital. The hospital has a staff of more than 600 spread across various departments such as clinical services, nursing, laboratory, pharmacy, and administration. The ethnic composition of the workforce at the is diverse, just as in the entire country and it is also an indication of Kenya's wider cultural heterogeneity.

Study Population

The target population for this study consisted of both healthcare workers and adult outpatients. The healthcare worker population included medical and non‐medical staff involved in healthcare service delivery, including doctors, nurses, clinical officers, pharmacists, laboratory personnel, administrative staff, support staff, and hospital management. According to official hospital human resource records, the hospital had a total workforce population of 300 employees. The patient population comprised adult outpatients aged 18 years and above who accessed healthcare services during the study period. These populations were considered appropriate for assessing the relationship between ethnic diversity and healthcare service delivery outcomes, including staff performance, teamwork efficiency, and patient satisfaction.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria for healthcare workers consisted of full‐time employees who had worked at the for at least 6 months prior to the study to ensure adequate familiarity with the organizational environment. For patients, only adult outpatients aged 18 years and above who had completed consultation or treatment during the study period were included. The exclusion criteria included temporary employees, interns, locum staff, and healthcare workers with less than 6 months of service at the hospital. Patients who were unable to communicate effectively due to severe medical conditions or cognitive impairment were also excluded from the study.

Sample Size and Sampling Procedure

The sample size for healthcare workers (n = 182) was determined using Yamane's formula for finite populations at a 95% confidence level and 5% margin of error. Yamane's formula was preferred over generalized table‐based approaches such as Sekaran and Bougie because it provides a population‐specific statistical estimation suitable for finite institutional populations with known population sizes. The calculated sample was considered adequate to achieve acceptable statistical power for detecting significant relationships among the study variables. A total of 200 healthcare workers were approached, of whom 182 participated (response rate = 91.0%). For the patient satisfaction survey, 230 outpatients were approached and 210 participated (response rate = 91.3%).

Healthcare workers were selected using stratified random sampling based on professional category and departmental affiliation, including doctors, nurses, pharmacists, laboratory personnel, administrative staff, support staff, and hospital management. Proportional allocation was applied to ensure that the number of participants selected from each stratum reflected its relative size within the overall workforce population. Simple random sampling was then used within each stratum to minimize selection bias and enhance representativeness. For outpatient participants, systematic random sampling was applied, whereby every fifth eligible patient exiting the consultation room was invited to participate until the required sample size was attained.

Data Collection Instruments

Data collection was done with the help of structured self‐administered questionnaires for the staff and interviewer‐administered questionnaires for staff and interviewer‐administered questionnaires for patients. The staff questionnaire comprised sections on: Socio‐demographic characteristics (age, gender, profession, ethnicity, years of service), Ethnic diversity index – constructed using Blau's diversity index, based on the proportion of ethnic representation in each department, Teamwork and performance metrics adapted from the Team Climate Inventory [17] and the Individual Work Performance Questionnaire [18, 19]. Ethnic diversity was calculated using Blau's Index (B = 1 − Σpi2), where pi represents the proportion of individuals belonging to the ith ethnic group within a department. Diversity levels were categorized as: Low (< 0.30), Moderate (0.30–0.60), and High (> 0.60). The patient questionnaire assessed service satisfaction using a 5‐point Likert scale across dimensions of communication, timeliness, empathy, and overall satisfaction. The Team Climate Inventory and Individual Work Performance Questionnaire used standardized Likert‐scale procedures for scoring, which showed that higher scores represented greater teamwork and performance abilities.

Validity and Reliability of Instruments

In order to guarantee the content validity, it was subjected to an expert review of three professionals from the healthcare management and social sciences. A pilot study with 20 staff members and 15 patients was conducted to test the clarity and consistency. The reliability was measured using Cronbach's alpha, and the coefficients were 0.82 for staff scales and 0.85 for patient satisfaction, which indicates that the internal consistency was acceptable.

Data Collection Procedure

Once ethical and administrative approvals were secured, the participants were approached during the working hours and were informed about the objectives of the study. The participants had consented prior to the administration of the questionnaires. The patient satisfaction survey was conducted at the outpatient exit area, and data were collected in July 2025 over a 4‐week period. Potential confounders, including department workload and patient case mix, were considered but could not be fully controlled due to data limitations.

Data Management and Analysis

Statistical analyses were pre‐specified and conducted using two‐sided tests with a significance level set at α = 0.05. The study presents continuous variables through means combined with standard deviation (SD) measurement, and it shows categorical variables through their respective frequencies and percentages. The study presents effect sizes through three measurements, which include correlation coefficients (r), regression coefficients (β), and adjusted R 2 values together with 95% confidence intervals (CI). The research employed Pearson correlation analysis, independent t‐tests, one‐way ANOVA, and multiple linear regression analyses according to their applicable situations. The research team conducted all analyses through IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), which they used according to SAMPL reporting guidelines. The study examined three regression assumptions through testing procedures, which included multicollinearity assessment using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF < 2) and Q–Q plots for residual normality testing and residual scatterplots for homoscedasticity evaluation.

Ethical Considerations

The study was conducted in strict observance of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Ethnic Diversity and Patient Satisfaction

Patient satisfaction was assessed using a 5‐point Likert scale on 210 outpatients who interacted with hospital staff. Results indicated that departments with higher ethnic diversity had significantly higher patient satisfaction scores (mean = 4.36 ± 0.64) compared to those with lower diversity (mean = 3.98 ± 0.73; p = 0.021).

Discussion

This study examined the influence of ethnic diversity on service delivery outcomes in Kenyan public healthcare, focusing on staff performance, teamwork efficiency, and patient satisfaction. The findings provide strong empirical support that ethnic diversity influences service delivery through both direct effects and context‐dependent mechanisms, particularly shaped by inclusive organizational practices. Overall, the results indicate that ethnic diversity functions as both a strategic resource and a potential source of operational complexity, depending on how it is managed within healthcare teams.

Ethnic Diversity and Staff Performance

The findings of this study demonstrated that ethnic diversity significantly and positively influences staff performance, thereby supporting Hypothesis 1. This suggests that ethnically diverse teams contribute to improved adaptability, creativity, and overall job effectiveness in healthcare settings. The integration of diverse cultural perspectives and experiential knowledge appears to enhance problem‐solving capacity and responsiveness to patient needs, which are critical in complex service environments such as public hospitals.

These findings are consistent with prior studies, which report that workforce diversity enhances organizational performance and innovation [4, 20]. Similarly, Obeng and Atan [5], found that diversity improves employee productivity when supported by appropriate management practices. The results, therefore, reinforce the growing empirical consensus that diversity, when effectively harnessed, contributes positively to performance outcomes.

From a theoretical perspective, the findings provide strong support for the Resource‐Based View (RBV), which posits that heterogeneous human capital constitutes a valuable organizational resource that enhances performance [8]. In this study, ethnic diversity functioned as a source of cognitive variety and adaptability, thereby strengthening staff effectiveness. At the same time, the findings also align with Social Identity Theory (SIT), which suggests that diversity may introduce in‐group and out‐group dynamics that influence interpersonal relationships [21]. The presence of perceived bias among a minority of respondents indicates that while diversity enhances performance, it may simultaneously generate relational challenges if not effectively managed.

Ethnic Diversity and Teamwork Efficiency

The findings revealed that ethnic diversity has a dual effect on teamwork efficiency, partially supporting Hypothesis 2. While ethnically diverse teams demonstrated higher levels of creativity and broader knowledge integration, they also reported increased levels of interpersonal conflict. This suggests that diversity enhances team capability but may simultaneously introduce coordination challenges.

This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that diversity improves team innovation but may reduce cohesion in the absence of effective management [16, 17]. Similarly, reported that diversity can create communication barriers that negatively affect teamwork processes. The coexistence of improved creativity and increased conflict observed in this study highlights the complexity of diversity dynamics in healthcare teams.

The findings strongly support the Job Demands–Resources (JD‐R) model, which explains that diversity can function either as a job resource that enhances collaboration and innovation or as a job demand that increases interpersonal strain [11]. In this context, ethnic diversity simultaneously generated beneficial and constraining effects on teamwork efficiency. The findings also reinforce Social Identity Theory, which suggests that subgroup formation based on ethnic identity may influence trust and cooperation within teams [10].

Within the Kenyan public healthcare context, these findings may also be influenced by the socio‐cultural and linguistic diversity that characterizes the workforce. Differences in language use, cultural communication styles, and historically embedded ethnic identities may intensify coordination challenges in team‐based clinical environments. In high‐pressure hospital settings, where rapid decision‐making and clear communication are critical, such differences may increase the likelihood of misunderstandings and interpersonal tension. This context‐specific dynamic helps explain why diversity, while enhancing cognitive resources, may simultaneously constrain teamwork efficiency when not supported by structured integration mechanisms.

Ethnic Diversity and Patient Satisfaction

The findings further demonstrated that ethnic diversity significantly improves patient satisfaction, thereby supporting Hypothesis 3. Departments with higher ethnic diversity recorded higher patient satisfaction scores, particularly in relation to communication, empathy, and cultural responsiveness. This suggests that ethnically diverse healthcare teams are better equipped to meet the needs of heterogeneous patient populations.

These findings are consistent with previous studies showing that culturally diverse healthcare teams enhance patient‐centered care and service quality [2]. Similarly, Ziegler et al. [6] found that diversity improves patient trust and satisfaction in multicultural healthcare environments [6]. The results, therefore, provide empirical support for the argument that diversity enhances healthcare responsiveness and inclusivity.

From a theoretical standpoint, the findings support the Resource‐based view by demonstrating that diversity enhances organizational capability in delivering culturally sensitive care. Diverse teams are more likely to possess linguistic and cultural competencies that improve communication and patient experience, which are critical determinants of satisfaction in healthcare settings.

Moderating Role of Inclusive Organizational Practices

The findings also indicate that inclusive organizational practices strengthen the relationship between ethnic diversity and service delivery outcomes, thereby supporting Hypothesis 4. This suggests that diversity yields more positive outcomes when supported by inclusive leadership, equitable policies, and a supportive organizational culture.

This finding is consistent with prior research demonstrating that inclusion moderates diversity–performance relationships [22]. It also strongly supports the JD‐R model, which emphasizes that organizational resources determine whether diversity functions as a benefit or a burden [12]. In this study, inclusive practices appear to transform ethnic diversity from a potential source of interpersonal strain into a productive organizational resource.

Furthermore, the findings reinforce Social Identity Theory by suggesting that inclusive environments reduce intergroup bias and promote cohesion among diverse teams [21]. This highlights the critical role of organizational context in shaping the outcomes of diversity in healthcare settings.

Interpretation of Mixed Findings

While the overall findings indicate positive effects of ethnic diversity, the study also identified increased levels of conflict in highly diverse teams. This highlights the complexity and conditional nature of diversity effects. The results suggest that diversity alone does not guarantee improved outcomes; rather, its effectiveness depends on the presence of integration mechanisms and supportive organizational structures.

These findings are consistent with prior studies indicating that unmanaged diversity may lead to inefficiencies and interpersonal conflict [3, 4]. The results therefore reinforce the argument that diversity must be actively managed to yield positive outcomes, particularly in resource‐constrained healthcare environments such as those in Kenya. Furthermore, the observed increase in interpersonal conflict may reflect broader socio‐cultural dynamics within Kenya, where ethnic identity remains a salient social marker influencing trust, communication, and group affiliation. In organizational settings such as public hospitals, these underlying dynamics may manifest in subtle forms of in‐group preference or communication barriers, particularly in the absence of strong institutional mechanisms for inclusion. This underscores the importance of contextualizing diversity outcomes within the socio‐political and cultural realities of the Kenyan healthcare system.

Theoretical Contribution

This study makes important theoretical contributions by integrating Social Identity Theory, the Resource‐Based View, and the Job Demands–Resources model to explain the relationship between ethnic diversity and service delivery outcomes. While SIT explains interpersonal and group dynamics, RBV highlights the strategic value of diversity, and the JD‐R model emphasizes the contextual conditions under which diversity enhances or constrains performance.

The findings extend these frameworks by demonstrating that ethnic diversity in healthcare simultaneously generates both performance‐enhancing and performance‐constraining effects and that these effects are highly context‐dependent. The findings extend these theoretical frameworks by demonstrating that ethnic diversity in healthcare settings within resource‐constrained environments simultaneously operates as both a performance‐enhancing resource and a performance‐constraining demand. Specifically, this study advances the Job Demands–Resources model by providing empirical evidence that diversity can concurrently generate cognitive benefits and interpersonal strain within the same organizational context. Additionally, by situating these dynamics within the Kenyan public healthcare system, the study contributes context‐specific insights to the predominantly Western‐centric diversity literature, highlighting how socio‐cultural and institutional factors shape diversity–performance relationships. This reinforces the need for contextually grounded applications of diversity theories in healthcare systems within Sub‐Saharan Africa.

Managerial Implications

The findings of this study have important and actionable implications for hospital administrators and policymakers seeking to improve service delivery outcomes in public healthcare systems. To effectively harness the benefits of ethnic diversity, healthcare institutions should adopt structured diversity management frameworks embedded within human resource systems. This includes integrating diversity objectives into recruitment protocols, performance appraisal systems, and organizational key performance indicators (KPIs), as well as conducting regular diversity climate assessments to monitor inclusion, equity, and staff perceptions. Embedding such frameworks ensures that diversity management moves beyond symbolic initiatives to measurable and institutionalized practice.

To address the dual effects of diversity on teamwork, hospital administrators should invest in intercultural communication training and conflict management programs aimed at reducing misunderstandings and strengthening team cohesion. The development of shared team goals and identity frameworks that transcend ethnic boundaries is essential in promoting collaboration and minimizing subgroup formation. In addition, hospitals should institutionalize structured conflict resolution mechanisms, including formal mediation processes, team debriefing sessions, and clear escalation protocols for addressing interpersonal disputes. Embedding these mechanisms within routine team operations can help transform diversity‐related tensions into opportunities for learning and improved collaboration.

In relation to patient satisfaction, the findings suggest the need for integrating cultural competence into healthcare delivery systems. This can be achieved through continuous staff training on culturally sensitive communication, language support services, and patient‐centered care approaches that recognize diverse cultural expectations. Additionally, hospitals should implement structured feedback mechanisms to capture patient experiences across different demographic groups and use this information to improve service quality. To operationalize culturally responsive care, healthcare facilities should adopt established cultural competence frameworks, such as continuous staff training models that integrate awareness, knowledge, and skills development. These programs should be reinforced through periodic evaluations, patient feedback audits, and the inclusion of cultural competence indicators in service quality assessments.

At the organizational level, the study highlights the importance of institutionalizing inclusive practices through leadership development, equitable human resource policies, and transparent decision‐making structures. Regular diversity climate assessments should be conducted to monitor inclusion levels and identify areas requiring intervention. These strategies will ensure that ethnic diversity is effectively leveraged as a strategic asset to enhance service delivery outcomes. Furthermore, leadership accountability is critical in ensuring the effectiveness of diversity initiatives. Hospital management should incorporate inclusion‐related performance metrics into leadership evaluation systems, ensuring that managers are directly responsible for fostering inclusive work environments and equitable team practices. At the policy level, there is a need for the development of national guidelines on diversity management within healthcare systems in Kenya. Policymakers should integrate diversity indicators into health system performance evaluations and promote equitable workforce distribution across regions. Such measures will strengthen the capacity of public healthcare institutions to respond effectively to the needs of diverse populations while improving overall service delivery outcomes.

Limitations and Recommendations for Future Research

This study provides important empirical insights into the relationship between ethnic diversity and service delivery outcomes in a Kenyan public healthcare setting; however, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross‐sectional design limits the ability to establish causal relationships between ethnic diversity and service delivery outcomes. While statistically significant associations were identified, the directionality of these relationships cannot be conclusively determined. Future studies should employ longitudinal or panel designs to capture temporal dynamics and better assess causality. Second, the study is based on data from a single institution, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings in Kenya or Sub‐Saharan Africa. Institutional cultures, management practices, and patient demographics may differ across facilities. Future research should therefore incorporate multi‐site studies across diverse healthcare levels (county, national, and private hospitals) to enhance external validity. Third, the study relied partly on self‐reported measures of staff performance and teamwork, which may be subject to social desirability bias and common method bias. Although validated instruments were used, respondents may have overestimated performance outcomes. Future studies should mitigate this limitation by triangulating self‐reported data with objective indicators, such as administrative performance records, patient wait times, clinical outcomes, or supervisor evaluations. Fourth, selection bias may have influenced the findings. The exclusion of temporary staff, interns, and locum workers may limit the representativeness of workforce diversity, particularly in public hospitals where such personnel often constitute a significant proportion of the workforce. Additionally, the exclusion of non‐communicative or critically ill patients may have led to an overrepresentation of relatively stable outpatient experiences, potentially inflating patient satisfaction scores. Future research should consider inclusive sampling strategies or alternative data collection methods (e.g., proxy reporting or caregiver‐assisted responses) to capture a broader patient population. Fifth, while this study quantitatively assessed ethnic diversity using Blau's Index, it did not fully capture the qualitative dimensions of diversity, such as perceived inclusion, interpersonal trust, or experiences of discrimination. These factors are critical in explaining how diversity translates into performance outcomes. Future research should adopt mixed‐methods approaches, integrating qualitative interviews or focus groups to provide deeper insights into the mechanisms underlying diversity effects. Finally, although the study examined key dimensions of service delivery outcomes, it did not include other important indicators such as clinical quality measures, patient safety outcomes, or cost‐efficiency metrics. Expanding the outcome framework in future studies would provide a more comprehensive assessment of healthcare performance.

Practical Implications and Recommendations

The findings of this study provide important practical implications for healthcare administrators and policymakers seeking to improve service delivery outcomes in ethnically diverse healthcare settings. Because ethnic diversity was positively associated with staff performance and patient satisfaction, healthcare institutions should strengthen inclusive recruitment, equitable workforce management, and diversity‐sensitive leadership practices. Hospital human resource departments should integrate diversity and inclusion indicators into recruitment systems, staff appraisal processes, and leadership evaluations to institutionalize diversity management within organizational practice. The study also found that while ethnic diversity enhanced creativity and knowledge integration, it was associated with increased interpersonal conflict within teams. To address these challenges, hospital administrators should implement intercultural communication and conflict‐management training programs, establish structured team communication protocols, and strengthen formal conflict‐resolution mechanisms to improve teamwork efficiency and collaboration. In addition, the positive association between ethnic diversity and patient satisfaction highlights the need for culturally responsive healthcare delivery. Healthcare institutions should provide continuous training on cultural competence and patient‐centered communication while strengthening patient feedback systems to identify and address service delivery gaps across diverse patient populations. The findings further demonstrated that inclusive organizational practices strengthened the positive effects of ethnic diversity on service delivery outcomes. Consequently, hospital leadership should promote transparent decision‐making, equitable promotion practices, and inclusive workplace policies that reduce perceptions of bias and strengthen organizational cohesion. At the policy level, national and county health authorities should develop diversity and inclusion guidelines for public healthcare institutions and integrate workforce diversity indicators into healthcare quality assessment frameworks. Such measures may strengthen collaboration, improve patient‐centered care, and enhance service delivery outcomes within diverse healthcare environments.

Conclusion

This study examined the association between ethnic diversity and service delivery outcomes within a Kenyan public healthcare setting. The findings indicated that ethnic diversity was positively associated with staff performance and patient satisfaction, while also being associated with increased interpersonal conflict within some teams. The study further found that inclusive organizational practices strengthened the positive association between ethnic diversity and service delivery outcomes. These findings suggest that the relationship between ethnic diversity and healthcare service delivery is influenced by organizational context and workplace practices. The findings provide empirical support for the relevance of the Resource‐Based View, Social Identity Theory, and the Job Demands–Resources model in explaining diversity dynamics within healthcare organizations. However, the results should be interpreted within the limitations of a cross‐sectional study conducted in a single public healthcare institution and, therefore, should not be generalized beyond similar contexts without caution. From a practical perspective, the findings highlight the importance of inclusive leadership practices, intercultural communication, and equitable organizational policies in managing ethnically diverse healthcare teams. These implications are presented as context‐specific recommendations based on the study findings rather than universally generalizable conclusions. Overall, this study contributes empirical evidence to the limited literature on ethnic diversity and healthcare service delivery within Sub‐Saharan African public healthcare settings. Future research should employ longitudinal, multi‐site, and mixed‐methods designs to further examine the relationship between ethnic diversity and service delivery outcomes across different healthcare contexts.
