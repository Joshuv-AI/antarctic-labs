Comparative Efficacy of Herbal Products on Metabolic Parameters in Metabolic-Dysfunction Associated Liver Disease: A Systematic Review and Network Meta-Analysis
Authors: Revathi Thirumushi Sairaj, Alejandro Chen Liang, Yeison Cruz Castillo, Mariela Denise Fermin Madera, Jose Medina, Tatiana Alexandra Moreno Díaz, Gabriela Flores Monar, Supritha Chintamaneni, Camila Sanchez Cruz, Rashmi Advani, Sushovan Guha, Ernesto Calderón-Martinez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimsMetabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is closely linked to dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, and impaired glucose metabolism. Herbal products are widely used in MASLD, but their comparative metabolic effects and certainty of evidence remain unclear. We conducted a systematic review and network meta-analysis to evaluate herbal products on metabolic outcomes in MASLD.MethodsFollowing Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines and International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews registration (CRD420251236813), we searched PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, and Cochrane through September 2025 for randomized trials of herbal products in adults with MASLD. Primary outcomes were triglycerides (TG), fasting blood glucose (FBG), and Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR); secondary outcomes included cholesterol fractions, hemoglobin A1c, and body mass index. Random-effects network meta-analysis estimated mean differences (MDs) (95% confidence interval); certainty was assessed using CINeMA (Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis).ResultsA total of 123 trials were included (n = 8595). For TG, significant reductions vs placebo occurred with Korean red ginseng (MD: –99.00), naringenin (MD: –92.50), and artichoke leaf (MD: –52.60; all P < .01); surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) favored naringenin and Korean red ginseng (both 0.96). For FBG, green tea (MD: –16.98; P = .03), hesperidin + flaxseed (MD: –14.44), and garlic (MD: –13.51) were top-ranked (SUCRA: 0.90, 0.89, 0.87). For HOMA-IR, sumac (MD: –2.32; P < .01), green tea (MD: –1.81), and berberine + tocotrienols + green coffee (MD: –1.80) showed greatest improvements (SUCRA: 0.94 for sumac). Artichoke leaf, curcumin, and green tea improved total cholesterol, low density lipoprotein-cholesterol, and body mass index; no intervention reduced hemoglobin A1c. CINeMA showed high confidence only for artichoke leaf on total cholesterol; moderate for select TG, high density lipoprotein-cholesterol, and HOMA-IR outcomes; remaining were low or very low.ConclusionSelected herbal products, particularly artichoke leaf, curcumin, green tea, flaxseed, and garlic may offer modest, domain-specific metabolic improvements in MASLD, though confidence was low for most outcomes. These therapies may serve as adjuncts, not substitutes for approved treatments. Larger randomized controlled trials with standardized formulations, long-term safety data, and liver-specific outcomes are needed.Keywords: MASLD, Herbal Therapies, Network Meta-Analysis, Metabolic Parameters, CINeMA

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction is a central component in metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Most patients present with insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, visceral adiposity, and chronic low-grade inflammation, and these abnormalities tend to progress together with liver disease.1 MASLD affects approximately 30% of the global population, making it one of the most common chronic liver conditions worldwide.2 Among these individuals, up to 40% to 70% have some degree of insulin resistance, 50% to 80% have dyslipidemia, and nearly half meet criteria for metabolic syndrome.3 Although MASLD is defined by hepatic steatosis, its clinical impact extends far beyond the liver, with cardiovascular disease being the leading cause of death in MASLD, exceeding morbidity from cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma combined.4

Because of this strong metabolic foundation, improving body weight, glycemic control, and lipid parameters remains critical to clinical management. Lifestyle change remains the primary recommendation, but it is difficult to maintain over time, and its metabolic benefits vary between patients.5 Newer medications, such as GLP-1 receptor agonists and thyroid hormone receptor-β agonists, offer promising benefits for steatosis and fibrosis, yet their metabolic effects vary, and issues related to cost and availability can limit broader use.6,7 These challenges have driven interest in complementary strategies that target metabolic pathways through alternative mechanisms. Herbal and plant-derived compounds are among the most studied of these additional options. Some have been associated with reductions in hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), fasting glucose, and low density lipoprotein-cholesterol (LDL-C) in selected trials, while others have shown improvements in triglycerides (TG) and insulin resistance: however, the evidence is inconsistent, with wide variation in study designs, formulations, doses, and which metabolic outcomes are reported.8, 9, 10 This makes it difficult to determine which interventions offer meaningful metabolic benefits for MASLD.

Despite growing interest in herbal and plant-derived therapies for MASLD, it remains unclear whether these interventions provide broad metabolic benefit or exert more selective, domain-specific effects. Prior meta-analyses have largely focused on individual compounds or single outcomes, limiting comparative interpretation across metabolic domains and obscuring the certainty of the evidence.8, 9, 10 Moreover, the increasing use of ranking-based approaches without formal assessment of confidence may overstate the clinical relevance of statistically significant but uncertain findings. To address these gaps, we conducted a comprehensive systematic review and network meta-analysis (NMA) of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating single-compound herbal products and derivatives in adults with MASLD, focusing on key metabolic outcomes and explicitly assessing the certainty of evidence using the CINeMA (Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis) framework.

Materials and Methods

The present systematic review followed the recommendations and criteria established by the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses reporting guidelines.11 The protocol was pre-registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; registration number CRD420251236813).12

Eligibility Criteria

This systematic review included RCTs published from database inception to September 2025, enrolling participants with MASLD, as defined by imaging, biopsy, or legacy nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) criteria, including a clinical profile consistent with NAFLD characterized by elevated liver enzyme levels in the absence of other liver diseases, in accordance with American Gastroenterological Association guidance.13 Because most included trials predated MASLD nomenclature, studies using legacy NAFLD/nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) terminology were included when populations demonstrated hepatic steatosis with metabolic features compatible with current MASLD criteria. Throughout this manuscript, the term MASLD is used to refer to the disease spectrum previously described as NAFLD and NASH, acknowledging that trials used legacy terminology.

Studies were excluded if they involved animals, participants with significant alcohol intake, other chronic liver diseases, major nonmetabolic comorbidities, nonoriginal research, duplicate data, insufficient detail, or missing information. Interventions of interest were single-compound herbal therapies or any herbal derivative compared with placebo, no treatment, lifestyle modifications, or standard treatment. Any study involving approved traditional Chinese medicines or formulations combining multiple herbal products (more than 4 active ingredients) was excluded to maintain consistency and isolate the effect of individual herbal agents. Trials using active pharmacological medications as comparators were not included, and no NMA node was created for these interventions. In cases where multiple overlapping reports were identified from the same population, both studies were retained if each contributed unique variables; however, data from the less comprehensive report were excluded to avoid double-counting while preserving the most informative dataset. Primary outcomes were Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance (HOMA-IR), TG, and FBS. Secondary outcomes included other metabolic and anthropometric measures (total cholesterol [TC], LDL-C, high density lipoprotein-cholesterol [HDL-C], HbA1c, and body mass index [BMI]). Only studies reporting at least one primary predefined outcome were eligible for inclusion.

Searching Methods

A systematic search was initially conducted in September 2025 on PubMed, Web of Science, EMBASE, and Cochrane, using the following terms, but not limited to: “Non-alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease,” “Fatty Liver,” “Steatohepatitis,” “MAFLD,” “NAFLD,” “NASH,” “Silymarin,” “Milk,” “Curcumin,” “Turmeric,” “Herbal,” “Phytomedicine,” “medicinal plant,” among others. All detailed search strategies can be found in the supplementary material (Supplementary Tables 1-4).

Selection of Studies

All references were exported to Rayyan (Rayyan Systems Inc, Cambridge, MA, USA), and duplicates were removed.14 Two authors independently (R.T.S. and Y.C.C.) completed the eligibility assessment, first by title and abstract analysis, followed by full-text assessment. In disagreements between reviewers, a third reviewer (E.C.M.) helped reach a consensus.

Data Extraction

Two independent reviewers extracted the data (T.A.M.D. and A.C.L.), and disagreements were resolved by consensus. Extracted data included sample sizes, intervention type, comparator, duration, and measured outcomes. Conventional methods were used for data extraction, complemented by specialized tools such as WebPlotDigitizer (Automeris, Austin, TX, USA) for digitizing data from graphs, Cochrane Calculator for statistical conversions, and StatsToDo for advanced calculations.14, 15, 16, 17 Extracted outcomes comprised TC (mg/dL), LDL-C (mg/dL), HDL-C (mg/dL), TG (mg/dL), BMI (kg/m2), FBS (mg/dL), HOMA-IR (%), and HbA1c (%). Additional data extracted included subgroup characteristics, such as country of study, sample size, intervention duration, and risk-of-bias levels.

Assessment of Risk of Bias in Included Studies

To assess the quality of the studies included in the systematic review, we applied the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2.0 tool, in accordance with Cochrane guidelines for RCTs.18 Two independent reviewers (A.C.L.) and (J.M.) evaluated the risk of bias in each study, considering the specific criteria and guidelines provided by the respective tools. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion with a third, blind reviewer (E.C.M.).

Statistical Analysis

A meta-analysis was performed using R version 4.0 or later.19 The pooled effect of the outcomes was examined using a random-effects meta-analysis (DerSimonian-Laird approach).20 Whenever the number of studies reporting an outcome of interest was insufficient, only a qualitative analysis of the results was performed. Effect sizes were expressed as change from baseline mean differences (MDs), since all variables were measured using the same units. All estimates are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The I2 statistic assessed heterogeneity, and the following cutoff values used for interpretation: <25, 25 to 50, and >50% were considered small, medium, and large heterogeneity, respectively, and in the context of low heterogeneity, extensive sensitivity analyses are generally not recommended.21 Network geometry and rankings were visualized using network plots, ranking probabilities expressed as P-scores or surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA), and forest plots showing multiple outcomes. For all outcomes, sensitivity analyses of low risk-of-bias studies and studies that implemented the intervention for at least 3 months were conducted to determine the influence of these variables on the overall effect.22 Egger's regression test examined publication bias when 10 or more reports with the same outcome were available.23 Results were presented using consistent analytical approaches and tabular formats across outcomes wherever possible, including random-effects network meta-analyses with MDs, league tables, and SUCRA rankings assessments. Node-splitting analyses were conducted when both direct and indirect evidence were available within the network. When comparisons were informed exclusively by direct evidence or by indirect evidence alone, node-splitting was not feasible, and these results were reported narratively in the text. Further statistical details are provided in Supplementary Methods.

Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis Assessment of Outcomes

Two reviewers (Y.C.C. and A.C.L.) independently assessed confidence in the evidence for each outcome using the CINeMA framework. CINeMA evaluates 6 domains: within-study bias, across-study bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence. The assessment was conducted following the methodological standards described by Nikolakopoulou et al24, 25, 26, and all judgments were performed using the CINeMA web application. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached.

Results

Study Selection

In the initial phase of our research, we identified 14,037 potential articles across 4 databases. After 3664 articles were removed, we conducted screening based on title and abstract, leading to the exclusion of 10,166 articles. Fifty-two of the 207 articles sought for retrieval were not retrieved, leaving 155 articles for eligibility assessment. Following eligibility assessment, 2 studies were excluded due to duplication, 4 due to different languages, 7 due to wrong population, 9 for wrong outcome, and 10 more for wrong study design. Finally, 123 studies were included in this review. The selection process is summarized in our Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow chart (Figure 1).

PRISMA flow diagram of study selection. The diagram summarizes the identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and inclusion of randomized controlled trials evaluating herbal products in MASLD. PRISMA, Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses.

Characteristics of Included Studies

A total of 123 RCTs involving 8595 participants with MASLD were included, representing a broad global distribution. Iran contributed the largest proportion of studies (70.2%), reflecting its leading role in clinical research on herbal and nutraceutical therapies for MASLD. Additional contributions originated from China (12.9%) and Italy (8.9%), with smaller yet meaningful representation from Pakistan (3.2%), South Korea (2.4%), Malaysia (1.6%), Japan (1.6%), India (1.6%), and the United States (1.6%). Single eligible trials (each contributing 0.8% of the total) were identified from Spain, Denmark, Poland, Germany, Romania, Egypt, and Thailand. In this dataset (n = 265 intervention arms), the 6 most frequently studied active interventions were silymarin (24 arms, 9.1%), followed by curcumin (13 arms, 4.9%). Resveratrol and green tea were each evaluated in 6 trials (each contributing 2.3%), while berberine and Nigella sativa were each evaluated in 5 arms (each contributing 1.9%). Together, these represent the most tested non-placebo treatments in the dataset. We have summarized these findings in Table 1 and Supplementary Table 53. Full references for all included studies are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Risk of Bias

Risk of bias was assessed using the Risk of Bias 2 tool across all 123 trials.18 Overall, most outcomes (>75–85%) were judged at low risk of bias, with a smaller proportion (≈12–20%) rated as having some concerns and only 1% to 3% classified as high risk. Detailed, outcome-specific assessments are provided in Supplementary Tables 5–12.

Meta-Analysis

Primary Outcomes

The NMAs for metabolic outcomes were based on extensive networks, including a wide range of herbal products and derivatives. All models used random-effects MDs with placebo as the reference treatment (Figure 2A–H). The TG network comprised 85 studies and 53 interventions; the FBS network included 63 studies and 40 interventions; and the HOMA network consisted of 39 studies, 29 interventions, and 27 designs (Figure 3A–H). Triglycerides

Multiple herbal interventions were associated with statistically significant reductions in TG levels compared with placebo (Figure 2A). The largest effect estimates were observed for Korean red ginseng (MD: −99.00, 95% CI: −155.97 to −42.03, P < .01) and naringenin (MD: −92.50, 95% CI: −133.93 to −51.07, P < .01), followed by artichoke leaf (MD: −52.60, 95% CI: −75.57 to −29.63, P < .01). Flaxseed and the garlic–silymarin combination were also associated with statistically significant reductions, whereas several additional interventions demonstrated smaller or inconsistent effects. Overall heterogeneity was moderate to high (I2 = 55.9%), with the global Q statistic indicating significant variability across the network (Q = 86.14, df = 38; P < .0001). Decomposition analyses showed that heterogeneity arose predominantly within designs, driven mainly by placebo comparisons involving silymarin, pomegranate, curcumin + piperine, sumac, and berberine. The between-design component was small and statistically nonsignificant (Q = 1.18, df = 4; P = .88), and this remained unchanged under the full design-by-treatment interaction model (P = .91), supporting overall network coherence (Supplementary Table 13). Node-split analyses did not identify meaningful discrepancies between direct and indirect estimates across evaluated contrasts, with all decomposed comparisons showing nonsignificant differences (Supplementary Table 14). Most high-ranking interventions were supported by low to moderate CINeMA confidence, limiting the reliability of these rankings.

SUCRA rankings suggested higher probabilities of benefit for naringenin (0.96), Korean red ginseng (0.96), garlic + silymarin (0.88), saffron (0.84), and artichoke leaf (0.83) (Figure 4A; Table 2). The complete set of pairwise network estimates and their direct and indirect decompositions are presented in the league table (Supplementary Table 15). Visual inspection of the funnel plot demonstrated asymmetry, and Egger’s test was statistically significant (P < .01), suggesting the presence of small-study effects or publication bias within the TG network (Supplementary Figure 2A).

Fasting blood glucose

For FBS, only a subset of interventions demonstrated statistically significant improvements relative to placebo (Figure 2B). Green tea (MD: −16.98, 95% CI: −32.65 to −1.31, P = .03), garlic (MD: −13.51, 95% CI: −25.08 to −1.94, P = .02), and the hesperidin–flaxseed combination (MD: −14.44, 95% CI: −26.07 to −2.81, P = .01) showed the largest point estimates, while curcumin was associated with a smaller but statistically significant reduction (MD: −5.01, 95% CI: −9.92 to −0.09, P = .046). Most other interventions demonstrated numerical decreases that did not reach statistical significance. Overall heterogeneity was substantial (I2 = 73.9%; Q = 99.68, df = 26; P < .01) and arose predominantly from within-design variability, particularly in placebo comparisons involving silymarin, curcumin, turmeric, and green tea. The between-design component was small and nonsignificant (P = .66), and consistency was maintained under the design-by-treatment interaction model (P = .85). Node-split analyses demonstrated good agreement between direct and indirect estimates across evaluated contrasts (Supplementary Tables 16–17). SUCRA rankings suggested higher probabilities of benefit for green tea (0.90), hesperidin + flaxseed (0.89), garlic (0.87), chicory (0.73), and Rosa damascena (0.71; Table 2). The full set of pairwise network estimates, including placebo comparisons, is provided in the league table (Supplementary Table 18). Funnel plot visual inspection and Egger’s test did not suggest relevant small-study effects (Supplementary Figure 2B). CINeMA assessments were predominantly low or very low, indicating limited confidence in ranking stability.

Homeostatic model assessment

For insulin resistance assessed by HOMA, a limited subset of interventions demonstrated statistically significant reductions compared with placebo (Figure 2C). Sumac showed the largest and most precise effect estimate (MD: −2.32, 95% CI: −3.62 to −1.02, P < .01), followed by green tea (MD: −1.81, 95% CI: −3.32 to −0.30, P = .02) and the combination of berberine, tocotrienols, and green coffee (MD: −1.80, 95% CI: −3.28 to −0.32, P = .02). Other interventions demonstrated nonsignificant trends toward lower HOMA values. Between-study heterogeneity was very high (I2 = 82.6%), and the global Q statistic indicated substantial variability across the network (Q = 80.51, df = 14; P < .0001). Decomposition analyses showed that most heterogeneity originated within designs, particularly in placebo comparisons involving silymarin, green tea, and curcumin. In the base model, between-design inconsistency was statistically significant (Q = 10.34, df = 2; P = .006); however, after incorporating a full design-by-treatment interaction structure, the between-design component was no longer significant (P = .83), suggesting that design-level differences adequately accounted for the observed inconsistency (Supplementary Table 19). Node-split analyses for key contrasts demonstrated good agreement between direct and indirect estimates, with all differences centered around zero (P ≥ .52 for all; Supplementary Table 20). SUCRA rankings suggested higher probabilities of benefit for sumac (0.94), green tea (0.87), and berberine + tocotrienols + green coffee (0.87), followed by cinnamon (0.68) and Zataria multiflora Boiss (0.65), whereas the lowest rankings were observed for Magnolia officinalis (0.06), pomegranate (0.20), and resveratrol (0.23; Table 2). A detailed league table of pairwise HOMA comparisons, including decomposition of direct and indirect contributions, is provided in Supplementary Table 21. Funnel plot inspection showed no relevant asymmetry, and Egger’s test did not suggest important small-study effects for HOMA (P = .50; Supplementary Figure 2C). Despite high SUCRA rankings, CINeMA confidence was moderate to low for most comparisons.

Secondary Outcomes

Total cholesterol

For TC, several interventions were associated with statistically significant reductions compared with placebo (Figure 2D). The largest and most precise effect was observed with artichoke leaf (MD: −42.59, 95% CI: −65.35 to −19.84, P < .001), followed by curcumin (MD: −27.02, 95% CI: −38.84 to −15.20, P < .001) and green tea (MD: −23.55, 95% CI: −40.41 to −6.70, P = .006). Additional significant reductions were observed with naringenin, QuYuHuaTanTongLuo Decoction, and silymarin, whereas most other interventions did not differ significantly from placebo. Between-study heterogeneity was high (I2 = 78.4%), and the global Q statistic indicated substantial variability across the network (Q = 157.6, df = 34; P < .001). Decomposition analyses demonstrated that heterogeneity arose predominantly from within-design variability, particularly in placebo comparisons involving silymarin and curcumin, while the between-design inconsistency component was small and statistically non-significant, supporting overall network coherence (Supplementary Table 22). SUCRA rankings identified artichoke leaf as having the highest probability of being the most effective intervention for TC reduction (SUCRA 0.94; Table 2). Complete league tables of pairwise comparisons are provided in (Supplementary Table 23). Funnel plot inspection and Egger’s test did not indicate relevant small-study effects (Supplementary Figure 2D). In contrast, artichoke leaf was supported by high CINeMA confidence, while other rankings were based on lower-certainty evidence.

LDL

For LDL cholesterol, several interventions demonstrated statistically significant reductions compared with placebo (Figure 2E). The largest effect was observed with artichoke leaf (MD: −34.42, 95% CI: −52.59 to −16.25, P < .001), followed by curcumin (MD: −24.13, 95% CI: −34.26 to −14.01, P < .001) and silymarin (MD: −11.56, 95% CI: −19.36 to −3.75, P = .004). A significant reduction was also observed with QuYuHuaTanTongLuo Decoction, whereas no other interventions differed significantly from placebo. Between-study heterogeneity was high (I2 = 82.4%), with the global Q statistic indicating substantial variability across studies (Q = 192.7, df = 34; P < .001). Decomposition analyses showed that heterogeneity was driven predominantly by within-design variability, particularly in placebo comparisons involving silymarin and curcumin, while between-design inconsistency was minimal and statistically nonsignificant, supporting the coherence of the network (Supplementary Table 24). SUCRA rankings indicated that artichoke leaf had the highest probability of being the most effective intervention for LDL reduction (SUCRA 0.94) (Table 2). Full league tables of pairwise network estimates and consistency assessments are provided in Supplementary Table 25. Funnel plot inspection did not suggest relevant small-study effects (Supplementary Figure 2E). CINeMA ratings were largely low, suggesting that ranking results should be interpreted cautiously.

HDL

For HDL cholesterol, only a small number of interventions were associated with statistically significant increases compared with placebo (Figure 2F). Green cardamom (MD: 8.00, 95% CI: 0.79–15.21, P = .02), green tea (MD: 7.45, 95% CI: 2.41–12.49, P = .004), and pomegranate (MD: 4.43, 95% CI: 0.18–8.68, P = .041) demonstrated modest improvements, whereas most other interventions showed no significant differences. Between-study heterogeneity was very high (I2 = 86.1%), with the global Q statistic indicating substantial variability across studies (Q = 229.8, df = 32; P < .001). Decomposition analyses indicated that heterogeneity was driven almost entirely by within-design variability, particularly in placebo comparisons involving curcumin and green tea. Although some between-design inconsistency was observed, this was expected given the diversity of interventions and study designs included in the network (Supplementary Table 26). SUCRA rankings suggested that QuYuHuaTanTongLuo Decoction had the highest probability of being the most effective intervention for increasing HDL (SUCRA 0.84) (Table 2); however, effect estimates were generally small and imprecise. Complete league tables of pairwise comparisons and funnel plot assessments are provided in Supplementary Table 27 and Supplementary Figure 2F. Most comparisons were supported by low or very low CINeMA confidence, limiting interpretability of rankings.

Hemoglobin A1c

The NMA for HbA1c included 16 studies evaluating 14 interventions. No intervention demonstrated a statistically significant reduction in HbA1c compared with placebo (Figure 2G). Between-study heterogeneity was high (I2 = 81%), with the global Q statistic indicating significant variability across studies (Q = 15.8, df = 3; P = .001). Decomposition analyses showed that heterogeneity arose exclusively from within-design variability, with no evidence of between-design inconsistency, supporting overall network coherence (Supplementary Table 28). Although SUCRA rankings suggested that garlic had the highest probability of being the most effective intervention (SUCRA 0.78), effect estimates were imprecise and did not reach statistical significance. Complete league tables of pairwise comparisons and funnel plot assessments, including Egger’s test, are provided in Supplementary Table 29 and Supplementary Figure 2G. All comparisons were supported by low or very low CINeMA confidence, reinforcing the lack of robust effects.

Body mass index

For BMI, several interventions were associated with statistically significant but modest reductions compared with placebo (Figure 2H). The largest decrease was observed with chicory (MD: −2.46, 95% CI: −3.61 to −1.32, P < .01), followed by green tea (MD: −1.67, 95% CI: −2.49 to −0.85, P < .01), orange (MD: −1.64, 95% CI: −3.21 to −0.07, P = .04), and pomegranate. Most other interventions demonstrated numerical reductions that did not reach statistical significance. Between-study heterogeneity was moderate (I2 = 64.2%), with the global Q statistic indicating significant variability across studies (Q = 100.5, df = 36; P < .001). Decomposition analyses suggested that heterogeneity was driven primarily by within-design variability, particularly in placebo comparisons involving silymarin and curcumin, while between-design inconsistency was present but limited (Supplementary Table 30). SUCRA rankings indicated that chicory had the highest probability of being the most effective intervention for BMI reduction (SUCRA 0.93; Table 2); however, effect sizes were modest and varied across interventions. Complete league tables and funnel plot assessments are provided in Supplementary Table 31 and Supplementary Figure 2H. CINeMA confidence was predominantly low, indicating uncertainty in the observed ranking hierarchy.

Subgroup Analysis

We conducted predefined subgroup analyses restricted to studies at low risk of bias with intervention durations ≥3 months. This subgroup included 82 studies for TC, 78 for LDL, 75 for HDL, 79 for BMI, and 16 for HbA1c, 34 studies for FBS, 40 for TG, and 25 for HOMA (Supplementary Figure 1A–H).

For FBS, significant reductions vs placebo were observed with curcumin (MD: −8.83, 95% CI: −16.67 to −0.99; P = .03), green tea (MD: −17.21, 95% CI: −33.18 to −1.24; P = .03), and hesperidin + flaxseed (MD: −14.44, 95% CI: −26.61 to −2.27; P = .02), whereas all other interventions demonstrated non-significant effects (Supplementary Figure 1A). Heterogeneity remains high (I2 = 75.6%). For TG, significant reductions persisted for garlic + silymarin (MD: −73.15, 95% CI: −113.61 to −32.70; P < .01), flaxseed (MD: −54.03, 95% CI: −92.37 to −15.69; P < .01), curcumin (MD: −32.88, 95% CI: −55.80 to −9.96; P < .01), green tea, hesperidin, hesperidin + flaxseed, and silymarin (Supplementary Figure 1B). Heterogeneity remains high (I2 = 50.4%). For HOMA, significant improvements were observed with silymarin (MD: −1.29, 95% CI: −2.13 to −0.45; P < .01), green tea (MD: −1.74, 95% CI: −3.09 to −0.38; P = .01), and curcumin (MD: −0.93, 95% CI: −1.78 to −0.08; P = .03), while remaining interventions demonstrated no statistically significant effects (Supplementary Figure 1C). Heterogeneity remains moderate (I2 = 66.7%).

For TC, significant reductions vs placebo persisted for artichoke leaf (MD: −42.59, 95% CI: −65.35 to −19.83; P < .01), curcumin (MD: −27.02, 95% CI: −38.84 to −15.20; P < .01), green tea (MD: −23.55, 95% CI: −40.40 to −6.69; P < .01), naringenin, and QuYuHuaTanTongLuo Decoction, among others (Supplementary Figure 1D). Heterogeneity remained high (I2 = 78.4%). For LDL, significant reductions were maintained for artichoke leaf (MD: −34.42, 95% CI: −52.59 to −16.25; P < .01), curcumin (MD: −24.13, 95% CI: −34.26 to −14.00; P < .01), silymarin (MD: −11.56, 95% CI: −19.36 to −3.75; P < .01) (Supplementary Figure 1E). Heterogeneity remained high (I2 = 82.4%). For HDL, significant increases vs placebo were seen for green cardamom (MD: 8.00, 95% CI: 0.79–15.21; P = .03), green tea (MD: 7.45, 95% CI: 2.41–12.49; P < .01), pomegranate (MD: 4.43, 95% CI: 0.18–8.67; P = .04) (Supplementary Figure 1F). Heterogeneity remained very high (I2 = 86.1%). For BMI, significant reductions remained for chicory (MD: −2.46, 95% CI: −3.60 to −1.31; P < .01), green tea (MD: −1.67, 95% CI: −2.49 to −0.85; P < .01), and pomegranate (MD: −1.40, 95% CI: −2.35 to −0.45; P < .01), whereas other interventions showed minimal or nonsignificant changes (Supplementary Figure 1G). Heterogeneity remained moderate (I2 = 64.2%). For HbA1c, no interventions showed statistically significant reductions after restriction (Supplementary Figure 1H). Heterogeneity remained high (I2 = 81%). SUCRA values for all subgroup analyses are presented in Supplementary Table 36, full league tables of pairwise comparisons in Supplementary Tables 37-44, and funnel plots in Supplementary Figure 2A–H.

Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis Assessment

According to the CINeMA evaluation, confidence in the evidence varied substantially across outcomes.24, 25, 26 High-confidence evidence was limited exclusively to artichoke leaf for TC, while moderate-certainty evidence was observed for HDL-C (silymarin comparisons and ajwain seeds), TG (artichoke leaf, flaxseed, Magnolia officinalis, and naringenin), and HOMA-IR (sumac). All other outcomes, including LDL-C, HbA1c, FBS, and BMI, showed uniformly low or very low confidence, largely due to imprecision, incoherence, and methodological limitations, highlighting the need for more robust and adequately powered randomized trials. Full domain-level CINeMA assessments for each comparison are provided in Supplementary Tables 45–52.

Discussion

In this systematic review and NMA of 123 randomized trials including 8595 adults with MASLD, we evaluated the comparative metabolic effects of single-compound herbal therapies and their derivatives. Rather than demonstrating uniform metabolic improvement across all cardiometabolic features of MASLD, our findings show that herbal interventions exert domain-specific effects on lipid, glycemic, insulin resistance, and anthropometric outcomes. This pattern provides a unifying framework for interpreting the substantial heterogeneity observed across prior trials and meta-analyses. However, this heterogeneity is largely driven by differences in formulation, dose, bioavailability, and treatment duration across studies, which limits the interpretability of pooled effect estimates. These characteristics are now summarized in Supplementary Table 53. Specifically, the observed effects differed by metabolic domain, with more consistent signals for lipid-related endpoints and insulin resistance, whereas the effects on long-term glycemic control and anthropometric measures were more variable.

Lipid metabolism showed the most consistent associations across the metabolic domains evaluated in this meta-analysis. Dyslipidemia is a clinically relevant feature of MASLD and occurs in the context of hepatic lipid accumulation driven by increased free fatty acid flux, enhanced de novo lipogenesis, and impaired fatty acid oxidation.149, 150, 151 Within this metabolic context, several herbal interventions were associated with reductions in TG and cholesterol, with artichoke leaf demonstrating the most robust lipid-modulating effects. Artichoke leaf supplementation has been associated with improvements in liver enzymes and hepatic steatosis in individual studies, although these outcomes were not systematically evaluated in the present analysis.121,152, 153 These lipid effects have been attributed to polyphenolic constituents such as luteolin and chlorogenic acid, which are linked to enhanced bile acid secretion and suppression of hepatic cholesterol synthesis.154,155 Importantly, our CINeMA assessment identified high-confidence evidence for artichoke leaf exclusively with respect to TC, expanding the strength of the findings and the understanding of the intervention effects. Beyond this, moderate-certainty evidence for triglyceride reduction was observed for a limited number of additional agents, including flaxseed and naringenin, whereas confidence in effects on other lipid outcomes, particularly LDL-C, was low and characterized by substantial heterogeneity. Notably, several highly ranked interventions were supported by low-certainty evidence. This highlights a key limitation of ranking-based metrics in NMA: SUCRA reflects relative probability of effect but does not incorporate the certainty or robustness of the underlying evidence. Therefore, rankings should not be interpreted as clinical recommendations and must be considered in conjunction with CINeMA assessments.

Additionally, glycemic outcomes followed an outcome-specific pattern across analyses. Improvements were most consistently observed for measures of insulin resistance, with sumac demonstrating the most reproducible association with improved HOMA-IR. In contrast, effects on fasting blood glucose varied across interventions, and no herbal intervention was associated with a clinically meaningful reduction in HbA1c, a finding that persisted in predefined subgroup analyses restricted to studies with low risk of bias and treatment durations of at least 3 months, indicating that longer exposure did not alter this finding. From a clinical perspective, these results indicate that short-term improvements in insulin resistance or fasting glucose should not be extrapolated to durable glycemic benefit in MASLD. This pattern is consistent with MASLD pathophysiology, in which hepatic insulin resistance is a central abnormality even in normoglycemic individuals, and improvements in hepatic insulin sensitivity can occur relatively quickly.156 In contrast, HbA1c integrates average glycemia over approximately 2 to 3 months and shows limited responsiveness in populations with normal or mildly elevated baseline HbA1c due to physiological floor effects.157,158 However, stratification by diabetes status was not feasible in the present analysis because most trials did not report diabetes-specific outcomes and included limited numbers of participants with diabetes.

Current MASLD guidelines prioritize modification of metabolic risk factors, including dyslipidemia, insulin resistance, glycemic control, and body weight, as central therapeutic targets and do not recommend herbal or nutraceutical agents for MASLD outside clinical trials due to limited and heterogeneous evidence.159,160 While several herbal interventions showed statistically significant effects on metabolic outcomes, the overall clinical relevance was heterogeneous across endpoints. Effect sizes were generally modest for glycemic control and lipid parameters, whereas more pronounced reductions were observed only in selected triglyceride-lowering interventions. These findings support the interpretation that herbal products may exert adjunctive metabolic effects rather than function as standalone disease-modifying therapies. Accordingly, SUCRA rankings should be interpreted in conjunction with absolute effect sizes and certainty of evidence, as higher probabilistic ranking does not necessarily imply clinically meaningful benefit. To address this limitation, a structured summary of clinical relevance integrating effect size, certainty, and interpretation is provided in Supplementary Table 54, which contextualizes statistical findings within clinically meaningful thresholds and comparative pharmacological benchmarks. Consistent with these recommendations, the present meta-analysis identifies domain-specific metabolic effects rather than global therapeutic benefit. High-confidence evidence supports a cholesterol-lowering effect of artichoke leaf, and moderate-certainty evidence supports improvement in insulin resistance with sumac, whereas effects on other metabolic outcomes remain uncertain. These findings reinforce that ranking-based efficacy signals must be interpreted within a certainty-informed framework and should not be conflated with clinical recommendation. Accordingly, herbal products should not be considered substitutes for established MASLD therapies and may, at best, serve as adjunctive approaches for specific metabolic outcomes until confirmed by large, well-designed randomized trials.

Safety Considerations, Drug–Herb Interactions, and Regulatory Challenges

Although herbal therapies showed potential metabolic benefits, safety remains a key concern. Herb-induced liver injury has been increasingly reported and may present with hepatocellular or cholestatic patterns, particularly in patients with underlying liver disease. Proposed mechanisms include direct hepatotoxicity, oxidative stress, and immune-mediated injury.27, 28, 29

Clinically relevant drug–herb interactions are also important in MASLD, especially in patients receiving statins, antidiabetics, anticoagulants, or immunosuppressants. Many herbal compounds can modulate CYP450 enzymes and drug transporters, potentially altering drug exposure and increasing risks such as hypoglycemia, bleeding, or statin-related toxicity.27,30 In addition, variability in herbal composition, lack of standardization, and potential contamination with heavy metals, pesticides, or undeclared pharmaceuticals further limit the interpretation of safety.29,31 Regulatory oversight also remains inconsistent across countries, as many products are marketed as dietary supplements without strict premarketing evaluation.32

Overall, while these therapies may be considered adjunctive options, their use should be interpreted cautiously in clinical practice, particularly in patients with polypharmacy or chronic liver disease.

Strengths and Limitations

This systematic review and NMA provides a comprehensive comparative assessment of herbal products on metabolic parameters in MASLD, restricted to RCTs, with risk of bias assessment and predefined subgroup analyses enhancing internal validity. The network framework allowed integration of direct and indirect evidence and probabilistic ranking of interventions.

However, several limitations should be considered. Substantial within-design heterogeneity reflects variability in formulations, doses, cointerventions, and follow-up duration. Many interventions were evaluated in small trials, resulting in imprecision and unstable rankings. The predominance of single-country studies, particularly from Iran, may limit generalizability, and funnel plot asymmetry in the triglyceride network suggests possible small-study effects. Regional differences in baseline metabolic risk parameters, dietary composition, and familiarity of herbal supplementation across cultures may influence responses to treatment. As a result of this, the efficacy observed with certain herbal interventions may not be directly transferable to the broader category of population. Additionally, most included studies used legacy NAFLD/NASH definitions established before adoption of MASLD nomenclature. Nevertheless, most study populations demonstrated metabolic characteristics compatible with current MASLD criteria. Importantly, liver-specific outcomes (eg, aminotransferases, fibrosis, imaging, or histology) were inconsistently reported and could not be analyzed, limiting conclusions regarding disease modification. Future studies should prioritize adequately powered, multicenter trials using standardized formulations, incorporating clinically relevant liver-specific endpoints across geographically diverse populations to determine whether metabolic effects translate into hepatic benefit and improve generalizability.

Conclusion

In this systematic review and NMA, a limited number of herbal products, including artichoke leaf, curcumin, green tea, silymarin, flaxseed, and select garlic-based preparations, demonstrated modest, domain-specific improvements in metabolic parameters in patients with MASLD. These effects were most evident for TG, LDL-C, FBS, BMI, and selected HOMA IR outcomes, whereas no intervention showed consistent benefit for HbA1c. CINeMA identified high-confidence evidence only for artichoke leaf with respect to TC, with moderate confidence limited to selected outcomes across TG, HDL-C, and HOMA IR. Most remaining outcomes were supported by low-certainty evidence. Overall, these findings suggest that certain herbal derivatives may provide adjunctive metabolic benefit when used alongside lifestyle modification and established MASLD management, but they should not be considered primary therapies or substitutes for approved treatments. Further large-scale, long-duration RCTs using standardized formulations, dose-finding strategies, purity verification, long-term safety evaluation, and prespecified liver-specific outcomes are needed before clinical integration Figure 3.
