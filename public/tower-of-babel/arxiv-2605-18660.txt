Evaluating Multi-turn Human-AI Interaction
Shi Ding, Sijian Tan

Abstract
Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as collaborative assistants, yet dominant NLP evaluation practices remain centered on aggregate metrics such as accuracy and fluency. These approaches often overlook behaviors that are critical in human-facing settings (e.g., consistency across multiple turns and iterative refinement). In this paper, we examine limitations of current NLP evaluation practices and introduce TCR, a structured framework for evaluating human–AI interaction using educational LLM assistants as an illustrative example. TCR emphasizes dimensions such as transparency, consistency, and refinement. We further present structured evaluation prompts and illustrative interaction examples demonstrating how structured evaluation can complement aggregate metrics and LLM-as-a-judge approaches. Our work highlights the need for more human-centered evaluation practices for interactive LLM systems.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly adopted in human-facing settings, yet their evaluation remains largely governed by general-purpose NLP benchmarks. While these benchmarks support scalable and standardized comparison, they often provide limited insight into interactive behaviors that emerge in real-world use
11
. This paper examines gaps in current LLM evaluation practices and introduces
TCR (Transparency, Consistency, and Refinement)
, a structured framework for analyzing model behaviors in multi-turn human–AI interaction. Using educational settings as an illustrative example, we discuss how structured evaluation may reveal limitations often overlooked by dominant NLP evaluation approaches and outline implications for the design and assessment of interactive LLM systems. We take the position that aggregate-centric evaluation may overlook critical behaviors in multi-turn interaction, highlighting the need for more interpretable behavioral evaluation. We make two contributions in this position paper. First, we identify gaps between dominant NLP evaluation practices and real-world interactive AI systems. Second, we introduce TCR, a structured framework that supports interaction-level evaluation beyond aggregate correctness metrics for interactive LLM systems.

TCR Evaluation Framework

3.1
Framework Overview

We introduce TCR as a lightweight framework for evaluating model behaviors in multi-turn human-facing AI systems. Rather than focusing only on traditional aggregate correctness or fluency, TCR emphasizes how models support users through iterative interaction, contextual guidance, and refinement across turns. 4
Implications for Model Evaluation

Our analysis highlights key limitations in dominant model evaluation practices, particularly the common assumptions around correctness, fluency, and automated judgment in the evaluation of human-facing LLM systems. We distill two implications that clarify why current evaluation practices remain insufficient for interactive language models.

4.1
Implication 1: Limitations in LLM-as-a-Judge Systems

LLM-as-a-judge frameworks offer scalability and efficiency for automated evaluation. However, our analysis identifies limitations in their applicability to human-facing evaluation settings. Evaluating interaction quality often requires sensitivity to context, user goals, and iterative interaction patterns that cannot be reliably inferred through fluency- and similarity-based evaluation alone. In our illustrative example, LLM judges may prioritize technically correct responses while overlooking contextual misalignment, inconsistent behavior, or ineffective refinement support. These patterns suggest that LLM-as-a-Judge Systems provide only a partial view of interaction quality and highlight the need for hybrid evaluation approaches that combine automated auditing with structured human-centered assessment.

4.2
Implication 2: Evaluating Multi-Turn Interaction

Current evaluation approaches often collapse multi-turn interaction into aggregate correctness or preference signals, masking behaviors that emerge through refinement, clarification, and contextual adaptation over time. While these approaches support scalable comparison, they provide limited insight into how interactions evolve through dialogue, revision, and iterative refinement. These limitations highlight the sociotechnical nature of evaluation, which is shaped by human values, institutional expectations, and contextual interaction. As illustrated in our educational example, structure evaluation through structured evaluation prompts helps surface assumptions that are often hidden by aggregate evaluation practices and makes model behavior more transparent in interactive contexts.

Together, these implications suggest that future model evaluation methods should incorporate contextual evaluation, multi-turn consistency analysis, and hybrid human–AI assessment approaches.

5
Limitations and Future Work

While TCR is intentionally scoped in this work, future work may further validate and extend the framework through large-scale empirical studies and more accessible evaluation practices for diverse users and interaction contexts.
