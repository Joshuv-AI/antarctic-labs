Matrigel/serum-free, high-fidelity patient-derived tumor-like cell clusters as an in vitro platform for large-scale compound screening and chemoresistance prediction in oral cancer

ABSTRACT

Background

Oral cancer represents a significant global health burden, with over 300,000 new cases annually. More than 90% of these are oral squamous cell carcinomas (OSCC), malignancies of epithelial origin that demonstrate a rising incidence, particularly in developing countries [1, 2]. OSCC is characterized by a high mortality rate, with five-year survival rates remaining below 50% for patients with locally advanced disease, highlighting the critical need for improved therapeutic strategies [3, 4].

For advanced OSCC, radical resection may result in facial defects and functional disorders, which severely affect the swallowing, chewing and speech functions of patients, and reduce their quality of life (Additional file 1: Fig. S1A-C). Neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) is widely employed to reduce tumor burden, achieve downstaging, minimize the scope of surgical resection, and preserve vital anatomical structures [5, 6]. The chemotherapy TPF regimen (docetaxel, cisplatin, fluorouracil), endorsed by the NCCN guidelines, serves as a cornerstone of NAC for OSCC patients [7].

Existing research confirms that some patients with a favorable response can obtain survival benefits from NAC [8]. Despite its potential, the current selection of NAC regimens remains largely empirical, guided by standardized protocols rather than personalized precision medicine. This challenge is exacerbated by the profound intertumoral heterogeneity intrinsic to OSCC, which arises from site-specific stromal diversity, variable epithelial-microenvironment crosstalk, and unique exposures of the oral cavity to carcinogens [9]. Consequently, patient responses to NAC are highly unpredictable: some experience clinical remission, while others endure significant toxicity or outright disease progression. Faithfully modeling this heterogeneity is therefore essential to deciphering tumor biology and optimizing therapeutic efficacy [10, 11].

To tackle these limitations, there is an urgent need for personalized medicine approaches centered on functional drug sensitivity testing. A strategy capable of identifying NAC-responsive patients, predicting efficacy, and tailoring regimens could spare non-responders from unnecessary toxicity, minimize invasive surgeries, improve functional and aesthetic outcomes, and ultimately enhance the quality of life. Realizing this paradigm shift requires the development of robust preclinical models that accurately capture tumor complexity and directly inform clinical decision-making, thereby enabling dynamic, evidence-based treatment personalization for maximal patient benefit.

Despite these advances, prevailing preclinical models for drug testing—including patient-derived cells (PDCs), xenografts (PDXs), and organoids (PDOs)—are hampered by notable shortcomings [12, 13]. PDCs fail to recapitulate the spatial architecture and stromal interactions of the native tumor microenvironment [14, 15]. PDXs are constrained by prolonged establishment timelines, low engraftment efficiency, and high costs [16]. While promising, PDOs often lack native immune components and suffer from inter-laboratory variability due to a lack of standardization in culture protocols [17–19]. These limitations are reflected in clinical translation: most drug response consistency rates in oral cancer trials fall below 80% [20], underscoring an imperative need for humanized models that can faithfully recapitulate tumor biology.

To overcome these barriers, we developed patient-derived tumor-like cell clusters (PTCs) through a three-dimensional (3D) suspension culture system. Generated from minimal tumor specimens (including solid tumors and effusions), the PTC platform operates independently of variable extracellular matrices or serum. Previously validated across diverse cancer types (e.g., gastric, colorectal, breast, lung and sarcoma), PTCs consistently preserve the morphology, pathology, and genetic landscape of primary tumors and have demonstrated ≥ 90% clinical consistency in predicting responses to both chemotherapy and immunotherapy [21–23].

Here, we deploy the PTC platform to resolve precision medicine challenges in oral cancer by targeting three pivotal gaps. First, we systematically benchmark the biological fidelity of oral cancer-derived PTCs against primary patient tumors to validate their capacity to model disease heterogeneity. Second, we correlate in vitro PTCs drug sensitivity profiles with clinical objective response rates (ORR) to NAC, establishing a robust framework for guiding regimen selection. Third, we perform transcriptomic analysis to identify aberrantly expressed genes linked to tumor progression and drug resistance, thereby uncovering potential therapeutic targets for next-generation therapies. By directly integrating a sophisticated preclinical model with clinical outcomes, this study provides a transformative platform for advancing precision medicine in oral oncology.

Methods

Study design and registration

This is an observational trial, aiming to compare the in vivo clinical efficacy with in vitro drug sensitivity results, and investigate the application value and guiding significance of PTC technology for the NAC treatment of patients with advanced oral cancer. The study was carried out at Peking University School of Stomatology and was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University School of Stomatology (PKUSSIRB-202388078). The protocol was in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice. The study obtained informed consent from patients for the collection of tissue samples and medical records.

Participants

Inclusion criteria included adults between 18 and 75 years old; patients with locally advanced OSCC, clinical stage III or higher, cT3-4 or N + , without distant metastasis; patients with an initially resectable lesion determined by the investigator; patient with at least 1 measurable lesion (according to RECIST 1.1); ECOG 0-2 and eligible for further chemotherapy; expected life span longer than 6 months. Exclusion criteria were as follows: patients with previous tumor-related treatment, including chemotherapy, radiotherapy or immunotherapy; patients with previous diagnosis of other malignant tumors; patients who participated in other clinical trials within 6 months; patients who received significant surgery unrelated to oral cancer within four weeks or did not achieve complete recovery from such a procedure; pregnant or breastfeeding women; significant comorbidities that obstruct the therapy regimen; severe hepatic and renal dysfunction; cognitive deficits, psychiatric disorders, poor compliance; allergy to identified pharmaceutical components. The PTC cell viability (tumor killing efficiency) was tested by technicians, and the clinical efficacy was independently assessed by clinicians based on enhanced CT results and pathological sections. The technicians were not aware of the clinical efficacy of patients and the clinicians could not know the results of PTCs-based drug sensitivity testing in advance.

The chemotherapy protocol of patients employed an empirical TPF regimen (cisplatin 60-75 mg/m2 on day 1, docetaxel 60-75 mg/m2 on day 1, and fluorouracil 600-750 mg/m2 on days 1 to 5), with medication dosages modified based on the patient’s response to the treatment. NAC was administered every 3 weeks for two cycles. When the cancer advanced or significant toxic side effects manifested during the chemotherapy, the clinicians would modify the treatment strategy based on the current state.

Sample collection and preservation

The sampling process of tumor tissues should be completed in the sterile room to avoid microbial contamination. The sampling sites with rich blood vessels should be preferentially selected while areas with necrosis, fibrosis, and those burned by the electrocautery should be avoided. Generally, the weight of the specimens should be no less than 10 mg.

If necessary, the excised tumor tissue should be washed in normal saline to remove impurities and then be stored in the sample preservation solution (FBS, HEPES, and HBSS) within 30 min.

Sample pre-treatment and tissue digestion

Before culture, the tumor tissues should be washed at least 5 times with sterile PBS supplemented with antibiotics, and the necrotic areas and adipose tissues in the sample should be removed as much as possible. In particular, for potentially contaminated tissue samples, they should be repeatedly washed with sterile PBS until the washed liquid is clear and free of solid impurities. If there are severely contaminated parts, they should be cut off and discarded.

The tumor tissues were cut into small pieces and digested with a sample releasing agent (collagenase I/II/III/IV) at 37°C for 1–2 h, with gentle pipetting performed every 15 min to facilitate mixing. After that, cell culture media should be added to terminate the digestion, and the resulting cell suspension should be collected by filtering through a 100 μm filter.

Cell self-assembly culture and quality control

Single cells should be plated at a density of 105/cm2 in an ultra-low attachment microplate (Corning, Cat No. 3471) and cultivated in the incubator (37°C, 5% CO2). The culture media should be changed every 2-3 days during the growth phase if necessary.

PTCs should present as nearly circular cell clusters with a diameter of 100–300 μm, characterized by regular or nearly regular shapes, relatively uniform texture, clear boundaries, and a semi-transparent appearance. The average yield of PTCs should exceed 200, adequate for single-agent drug screening, with 30–50 PTCs plated per well in three technical replicates for both experimental and NC groups. No bacterial or fungal contamination should be observed in cells under an optical microscope during cultivation.

Steps of drug sensitivity testing

First, primary cells derived from tumor tissues were cultured to enable stable growth and formation of PTCs in advance of any drug intervention. The fully established PTCs were then harvested by centrifugation at 300 g for 5 min and resuspended in fresh cell culture medium. Subsequently, 100 μL of cell suspension containing 30–50 intact PTCs was inoculated into a 96-well culture plate (Corning, Cat No. 3474). Finally, different drugs were added to the corresponding assay wells, with three technical replicates prepared for each experimental group.

The inoculated culture plate should be placed in the incubator (37°C, 5% CO2) for continued culture for 7 days. Images of PTCs in each well should be obtained through a Nikon Ti-U microscope system. Photos should be taken under a 10× objective lens of the microscope, and one representative photo should be taken for each well.

Cell block embedding

Special centrifuge tubes were heated in a metal bath at 85 °C for no less than 15 min until the embedding matrix was fully liquefied and flowed freely within the tube, after which the tubes were tilted slightly. The tube cap was unscrewed, and pre-fixed cell suspension was added into the liquefied matrix. The mixture was quickly homogenized by repeated pipetting.

Samples were immediately centrifuged at 1500 rpm for 5 min with the transparent end of the centrifuge tube placed downward. After centrifugation, the tubes were maintained at 4 °C for more than 10 min or at room temperature for over 20 min to achieve complete matrix solidification. Both end caps and the sealing film were removed, and the cell-containing matrix was gently pushed out. The matrix was cut into 3-mm-thick segments and placed into embedding cassettes with the cell-enriched basal layer facing downward (CITOTEST, Cat No. 31050102 W). Subsequent dehydration and embedding were performed following routine protocols for tissue blocks.

Histochemistry

Sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated through xylene (2 × 10 min) and graded ethanol (75%, 85%, 95%, and absolute ethanol , 5 min each), followed by washing with water (3 × 3 min). Nuclei were stained with hematoxylin for 10 min, rinsed with water, and then blue-developed in water for 30–60 min, with microscopic monitoring to achieve optimal nuclear staining. Counterstaining was performed with eosin for 1 min, followed by washing with water (3 × 3 min). Dehydration was carried out through graded ethanol (75%, 85%, 95%, and absolute ethanol , 2 min each), and clearing was achieved with xylene (2 × 5 min). Sections were mounted with neutral resin, and coverslips were gently applied to avoid air bubbles. Mounted slides were air-dried in a fume hood for at least 30 min to allow xylene evaporation.

Immunofluorescence

Sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated to water, and washed with water. Antigen retrieval was performed using a pressure cooker in retrieval buffer (boiling, 3 min after valve lifting), followed by cooling under running water and washing. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 15 min at room temperature. After washing, sections were circled with a hydrophobic pen and incubated with primary antibody overnight at 4°C. Following 3 × 3 min washes with PBS, sections were incubated with secondary antibody (diluted 1:5000 in 4% BSA) for 60 min at room temperature. Diaminobenzidine was applied for visualization, and the reaction was monitored microscopically and stopped by immersion in water. Sections were counterstained with hematoxylin for 10 min, rinsed in water, and blue-developed. Dehydration was carried out through graded ethanol (75%, 85%, 95%, and absolute ethanol , 2 min each), followed by clearing in xylene ( 2 × 5 min). Finally, sections were mounted with neutral resin and cover slipped, and the slides were air-dried in a fume hood for at least 30 min to allow xylene evaporation.

Flow cytometry sorting analysis

PTC samples were filtered to retain cell clusters and deplete single cells. Add 2 ml of cell dissociation solution (Versene containing 1 mg/ml collagenases I/II/III/IV and 1 mg/ml hyaluronidase), then transfer the mixture to a 35 mm dish and digest with horizontal shaking in an incubator, gently pipetting every 10 min and observing under a microscope until most PTCs are dissociated into single cells. After digestion, dilute with PBS or add medium to terminate the reaction, wash with PBS, and centrifuge at 400 g for 5 min. Resuspend the cells in staining buffer (or PBS with 2% FBS), divide into groups, add Human TruStain FcX™ (5 µl per 100 µl volume), mix, and block at room temperature for 5–10 min (do not wash before adding antibodies); set a NC without antibodies. Add the prepared antibody mixtures and incubate on ice in the dark for 1 h. Centrifuge at 400 g for 5 min, discard the supernatant, resuspend in 1 ml staining buffer, centrifuge again at 400 g for 5 min, discard the supernatant, and resuspend in approximately 300 µl staining buffer for later use.

Nucleic acid extraction and library preparation

For DNA extraction, samples were washed twice with PBS, and total DNA was extracted using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Cat No. 69504), during which protein impurities were digested with proteinase K and subsequently removed through purification steps. For RNA extraction, each sample was resuspended in 500 µL of Trizol, mixed with 100 µL of chloroform, and centrifuged at 12,000 g for 15 min at 4°C. The aqueous phase was transferred to a new tube for precipitation, and the RNA pellet was washed with 200 µL of isopropanol and 1 mL of pre-cooled 70% ethanol to obtain total RNA.

For library preparation and sequencing, DNA-targeted sequencing libraries were generated using the NEBNext Ultra II DNA Library Preparation Kit (NEB, Cat No. E7645L) with a panel of 712 cancer-related genes, involving enzymatic fragmentation and purification steps. RNA libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra II RNA Library Preparation Kit (NEB, Cat No. E7770L). Libraries were quantified using the Illumina sequencing Library qPCR Quantification Kit (Illumina, Cat No. SY-930-1010), followed by paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) on the Illumina HiSeq X Ten platform.

Cell transfection and CRISPR mediated gene activation

Per 96-well, 75 ng of pEF1a-dCas9-VPR-EGFP plasmid,50 ng of pU6-sgRNA-mCherry plasmid with a specific 20-nt target sequence (binding to MMP13 or It26), and 0.125 µL of P3000 reagent (Invitrogen, Cat No. L3000015) were first mixed and then added to 12.5 µL of Opti-MEM (Gibco, Cat No. 31985070). Separately, 0.375 µL of Lipofectamine 3000 and 12.5 µL of Opti-MEM were combined, followed by mixing with the above plasmid mixture.

After incubation at room temperature for 10–15 min, this mixture was added to the OSCC cell line SCC15 (grown to 50%–70% confluency). At 8–12 h post-transfection, fresh medium was added to minimize cell damage. At 24–48 h post-transfection, candidate drugs were administered to assess drug sensitivity. Subsequently, cell proliferation and cytotoxicity were evaluated using the CCK-8 assay kit (HARVEYBIO, Cat No. CT0049006).

Cell migration assay

Cell migratory potential was evaluated using 6-well PET Transwell permeable inserts with an 8 μm pore size (Beyotime, Cat No. FTW010). Briefly, SCC15 cells and its stable transfectants were resuspended in serum-free DMEM medium and seeded into the upper Transwell chamber, while the lower chamber was filled with complete DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS to serve as a chemoattractant.

After incubation for 48 h at 37 °C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO₂, non-migrated cells remaining on the upper membrane surface were gently wiped off with a sterile cotton swab. The migrated cells attached to the lower membrane surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and subsequently stained with crystal violet staining solution (Beyotime, Cat No. C0121). Microscopic images of migrated cells were captured at 4× magnification using the high-content imaging system (Molecular Devices Shanghai, ImageXpress® HCS.ai).

Wound healing assay

SCC15 cells and its stable transfectants were trypsinized and uniformly seeded into 6-well plates pre-installed with wound healing attachment films (Beyotime, Cat No. FAM106). Cells were cultured overnight to reach nearly full confluence before the experiment.

The pre-placed attachment films were then manually removed to generate a consistent cell-free wound gap. After film removal, residual cell debris and floating cells were carefully washed away with serum-free DMEM medium. Cell migration into the wound area was dynamically photographed at 0 h and 24 h time points using the abovementioned high-content imaging system.

Deliver siRNA into cell lines and PTCs

For cell line transfection, adherent cells were seeded and cultured to 80% confluency after 24-48 h. Two separate tubes were prepared: Tube 1 contained 150 μL Opti-MEM medium with 1 μL siRNA (25 pmol), and Tube 2 contained 150 μL Opti-MEM medium with 9 μL Lipofectamine® RNAiMAX Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat No. 13778030). The two mixtures were combined, incubated for 5 min, and then added to the plates.

For PTCs electroporation, single-cell suspensions were prepared from digested oral cancer specimens, centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was discarded. The cell pellets were resuspended in electroporation buffer (MaxCyte, Cat No. EPB-1) and adjusted to a density of 5 × 10⁷ to 1 × 10⁸ cells/mL. Synthesized siRNAs were mixed with the cell suspensions to a final concentration of 5–10 μM, and electroporated using the ExPERT ATx® electroporation system (MaxCyte, Cat No. E-ATx) with Optimization 4 program. After electroporation, the cuvettes were incubated at 37 °C for 20–30 min, and the cells were seeded into culture plates.

Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS, R, Graphpad Prism or Excel. P values were corrected for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg procedure to calculate the false discovery rate. For continuous variables, groups were compared using two-sample Wilcoxon’s test (also known as Mann-Whitney test) or two-sample t test. Fisher’s exact test was used to test the independence of two categorical variables. The correlation between a continuous variable and an ordinal categorical variable was calculated using Spearman’s correlation. All continuous cell viability measurements were normalized to the respective mean and standard deviation.

Results

Optimization of a tailored medium for PTCs generation from oral cancer tissues

To establish a robust protocol for generating PTCs from oral cancer tissues, we systematically analyzed a panel of growth factors and inhibitors. This led to the development of a tailored, serum-free, and Matrigel-independent culture medium (Additional file 1: Table S1). A key advantage of this optimized medium is its capacity to not only enable the sustained proliferation of primary oral cancer cells but also to support the concomitant survival and expansion of associated stromal and immune cell populations, thereby preserving critical tumor microenvironmental components.

Generally, the establishment of PTCs relied on the inherent self-organizing capacity of dissociated primary cells. These cells spontaneously assembled into 3D clusters, with initial aggregation observed within 12 h and progression to stable, maximally-sized PTCs within 48–72 h (Fig. 1A). Our tailored medium significantly enhanced the efficiency of cluster formation compared to standard DMEM control conditions (Fig. 1B).

From 2023 onwards, a cumulative total of 165 tumor specimens were obtained from the Peking University School and Hospital of Stomatology for this study (Additional file 2). The cohort included tumors from diverse sites in oral and maxillofacial region, including the tongue, gingiva, salivary gland, buccal mucosa, floor of the mouth, and others (Fig. 1C).

Histopathological classification identified 138 cases as squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and 27 as adenocarcinoma (AC) (Fig. 1D). The protocol demonstrated high reliability, with an overall culture success rate of 97.6% (161/165) (Fig. 1E). Of the four failed PTC cultures, failure showed no obvious correlation with cancer site or pathological type and presented a random distribution. Three failed cases (one tongue SCC puncture, one salivary gland AC puncture, one maxillary sinus SCC biopsy) contained extremely few or almost no viable tumor cells, and one buccal SCC surgical resection sample was discarded due to bacterial contamination. All failures originated from pre-experimental specimen quality issues and were theoretically preventable. Notably, puncture specimens yielded a lower PTC culture success rate (3/5) than biopsy (62/63) and surgical resection samples (96/97).

Among the vast majority of specimens, including 96 postoperative specimens (surgical resection) and 65 preoperative specimens (biopsy or puncture), the average number of PTCs per culture was 1500–3000 (Fig. 1F). This yield provides sufficient material for panel screening of 10–30 drugs—with 30–50 PTCs seeded per well and 3 technical replicates per condition—a scale that readily covers the full spectrum of conventional chemotherapeutic agents used for oral cancer. Importantly, this standardized and efficient workflow ensures the rapid generation of scalable PTC cultures that accurately retain tumor heterogeneity, laying a robust foundation for subsequent drug sensitivity profiling and biomarker discovery (Additional file 1: Fig. S2A and S2B).

Multidimensional validation confirms PTCs as high-fidelity model for oral cancer

Comprehensive multi-omics and histological analysis confirmed that PTCs faithfully recapitulate the complex features of their parental tumors. Histopathological examination revealed that PTCs preserve the distinct morphology of their original tumors: those derived from SCC (SCC-7333, SCC-8397) exhibited characteristic polygonal cells with prominent intercellular bridges, while those from AC (AC-6469, AC-8358) maintained cytoplasmic mucin vacuoles (Fig. 2A). The proportion of diverse cell types within the PTCs was also comparable to that of the original tumor tissue (Additional file 1: Table S2).

This phenotypic fidelity was further corroborated at the protein level by immunohistochemical (IHC) profiling. For example, SCC-7333 PTCs perfectly mirrored the parental tumor’s negative expression of P53 and positive expression of other key markers (Additional file 1: Fig. S3A and S3B). Similarly, AC-8358 PTCs recapitulated the original tumor’s P53-/CD31-/TUBB3- immunophenotype (Additional file 1: Fig. S3C and S3D).

Moreover, genomic analysis demonstrated a high degree of concordance between PTCs and their parental tumors. Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) profiling was displayed in top-ranked gene lists for both SCC and AC subtypes. A cohort of seven SCC cases (SCC-6344, SCC-6399, SCC-6933, SCC-8329, SCC-6988, SCC-2101, SCC-2268) exhibited high concordance rates, ranging from 97.5% (39/40) to 100% (40/40) (Fig. 2B, left panel). Specifically, three AC cases (AC-2269, AC-3100, AC-8358) showed a consistency rate of 100% (40/40), 100% (40/40), and 92.5% (37/40), respectively (Fig. 2B, right panel). These findings confirm that PTCs robustly preserve the genetic landscape of their source tumors.

This genomic fidelity further extended to the transcriptomic and chromosomal levels. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis revealed a gene expression similarity of over 94% across representative cases, including six SCC samples (SCC-7973, SCC-8329, SCC-6988, SCC-6344, SCC-6399, SCC-2101) and three AC samples (AC-2269, AC-8358, AC-2396) (Fig. 2C). Additionally, copy number variation (CNV) patterns were highly conserved in six SCC samples (SCC-2101, SCC-2268, SCC-2330, SCC-6344, SCC-6988, SCC-8329) and four AC (AC-0110, AC-1003, AC-1057, AC-8358) samples (Fig. 2D).

Crucially, PTCs also retained the cellular heterogeneity and immune microenvironment of the original tumors. Immunofluorescence (IF) staining confirmed the presence of a diverse immune infiltrate (CD45+ /CD3+ /CD68+ /CD56+ /CD20+) within the dominant tumor cell population (PanCK+) (Fig. 2E). Flow cytometric quantification of paired parental tumors and PTCs identified a broad spectrum of stromal and immune subsets, including epithelial cells, fibroblasts, T cells, macrophages, B cells, dendritic cells, endothelial cells, natural killer cells, etc. (Additional file 1: Figs. S4A–S4B and Table S2). This analysis captured significant inter-tumor heterogeneity, with samples ranging from immune-rich microenvironments (e.g., SCC-0845 and SCC-1281, > 30% immune cells) to those dominated by epithelial and stromal elements with minimal immune infiltration (e.g., SCC-1404, < 3% immune cells). Moreover, we analyzed fusion transcripts and identified that the MYB-NFIB fusion is specifically present in PTCs of adenoid cystic carcinoma (a subtype of AC). This finding demonstrates that PTCs can accurately recapitulate the key gene fusion characteristics of the original tumors (Fig. 2F).

Collectively, these multi-dimensional data establish PTCs as a robust, true-to-origin model that accurately preserves the histological, genomic, transcriptomic, and microenvironmental heterogeneity of parental oral cancer.

PTCs-based assay for oral cancer drug efficacy evaluation and personalized therapy guidance

To evaluate therapeutic efficacy, we developed an imaging-based metric aligned with RECIST 1.1 criteria, quantifying drug response by measuring changes in the total cluster area of PTCs before and after drug exposure. Drug concentrations used in this study were consistent with previously established protocols [21]. Clusters smaller than 40 μm in diameter were excluded from analysis to focus on viable tumor masses.

Furthermore, we leveraged this platform to evaluate both single agents and multi-drug combinations, with the goal of informing evidence-based optimization of drug regimens. A cohort comprising 49 SCC and 4 AC clinical samples was tested to systematically profile drug sensitivity patterns and identify treatment preferences. Of note, the count of effective drug regimens varied among tumor samples harvested from different oral subsites, suggesting a potential spatial heterogeneity in intrinsic drug sensitivity (Fig. 3C). Intriguingly, multi-drug combinations (including double- and triple-drug regimens) consistently outperformed single-agent therapies (Fig. 3D)—a finding that aligns with clinical guidelines advocating for combinatorial chemotherapy in relevant settings. Taken together, these findings validate that the PTCs-based assay can effectively capture clinically relevant inter-patient heterogeneity in drug response.

For refractory cases, we implemented a high-throughput screening platform capable of testing 48 drugs (across 3 concentrations × 3 replicates) within two weeks. Using a multi-well chip, we conducted 432 parallel PTC assays from a 1.0 g sample (AC-1835), plating an average of 30–50 PTCs per well (Fig. 3E). The assay demonstrated exceptional reproducibility, as evidenced by strong correlations between technical replicates (Pearson’s r = 0.98, 0.98, and 0.97; all P < 0.001) (Fig. 3F, G and H). This scalable approach enables rapid prioritization of treatment regimens, offering a crucial tool for patients with multi-line therapy resistance. By directly linking in vitro PTCs responses to clinical outcomes, this platform establishes a robust and reproducible framework for guiding personalized therapy selection in oral cancer, ultimately aiming to improve treatment efficacy and patient survival.

PTCs platform demonstrates high clinical concordance in predicting NAC response for OSCC

To validate the clinical utility of the PTC platform, we carried out a prospective trial (ChiCTR2300075543). The study design is summarized as: patients first underwent an initial biopsy and CT scan (designated as time point 1, T1); subsequently, they received two cycles of NAC with the TPF regimen (docetaxel, cisplatin, fluorouracil). Following NAC, patients proceeded to surgical resection, before which a follow-up CT scan (designated as time point 2, T2) was performed to assess changes in lesion size (Fig. 4A). Notably, the biopsy samples obtained at T1 were cultured to generate PTCs, which were then used for parallel TPF sensitivity testing. The in vivo clinical responses (based on RECIST 1.1 criteria) were then compared to the in vitro PTC results to determine concordance. Besides, the pathological responses were also concurrently evaluated according to the grading method [24]. From 42 enrolled patients with stage III/IV OSCC, PTCs were successfully generated from 41 pre-treatment biopsies (97.6% success rate). Ultimately, a total of 38 patients were included in the final analysis, with 3 excluded due to loss to follow-up or protocol deviations (Fig. 4B). Notably, the PTC testing process was conducted independently and did not interfere with clinical decision-making.

Clinical outcomes for the 38 patients included 2 cases of complete response (CR), 16 partial response (PR), 16 stable disease (SD), and 4 progressive disease (PD) (Fig. 4C, and Additional file 1: Table S4).

Overall, the PTC platform demonstrated remarkable predictive accuracy. Using a viability cutoff of PA < 0.7, it correctly identified 100% of CR cases (2/2), 93.8% of PR (15/16), 87.5% of SD (14/16), and 75% of PD (3/4), yielding an overall clinical concordance rate of 89.5% (34/38) (Fig. 4D and E). Cell viability data enabled clear stratification of CR/PR versus SD/PD groups, suggesting its potential value for therapeutic response prediction (Fig. 4F). Furthermore, the ability of the PTC drug testing assay to distinguish clinically sensitive and resistant patients was also evaluated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis. The areas under the ROC curve (AUC) for distinguishing the CR/PR group from the SD group and PD group were 0.9427 and 0.9028, showing high discriminatory efficiency (Fig. 4G and H). In addition, this platform achieved accurate prediction for Grade 3 pathological complete response (AUC = 0.8839) and favorable predictive performance for Grade 1 response (AUC = 0.6966) (Additional file 1: Fig. S5A–C and Table S5).

These quantitative findings are further corroborated by specific clinical examples. Two representative cases underscore this high concordance. The first involved a 60-year-old male (SCC-1120) with tongue cancer who achieved CR by RECIST 1.1 criteria following two cycles of TPF chemotherapy (Fig. 5A), with contrast-enhanced CT demonstrating complete tumor regression and reduction of lymph nodes to non-pathological size (Fig. 5B, left and middle panel). Pathological assessment of surgical specimens revealed post-treatment changes including mucosal inflammation, local keratinization, and foreign body giant cells in the left tongue, alongside one metastatic lymph node in zone III of the left neck (Fig. 5B, right panel). Specifically, ex vivo drug sensitivity testing of PTCs derived from this patient showed 44% tumor killing efficiency (Fig. 5C), aligning with the clinical CR.

Beyond predicting patient responses to standard chemotherapy regimens, the PTC platform also provides evidence for individualized regimen selection. Although the TPF regimen generally yielded superior efficacy compared with the TP doublet (docetaxel plus cisplatin), a subset of patients (7/31, ~23%) achieved a better response to the simpler TP regimen (Fig. 6A). As excessive multi-drug combination regimens tend to increase systemic toxicity and adverse reactions; consistent with previous clinical findings, our results further suggest that this PTC platform can accurately screen eligible candidates for treatment de-escalation, thereby reducing unnecessary drug exposure and minimizing chemotherapy-related toxicity [25].

MMP13 identified as a mediator of chemotherapy resistance in OSCC

Transcriptomic profiling of PTCs from patients with distinct clinical response (CR, PR, SD, and PD) revealed 8 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), including 4 upregulated and 4 downregulated candidates (Fig. 6B and Additional file 1: Table S6). Based on both the significant overexpression of matrix metallopeptidase 13 (MMP13) and its previously reported association with tumor progression, we suggested that MMP13 could serve as a predictive risk marker in OSCC [26, 27]. Correlation analysis in a subset of 8 patients (4 classified as drug-resistant/SD and 4 as drug-sensitive/PR) confirmed a strong positive association (r = 0.90, P < 0.01) between MMP13 expression levels and treatment resistance (Fig. 6C).

To functionally validate the role of MMP13, we used CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) to overexpress MMP13 in the established OSCC cell line SCC15 [28]. Following sequential lentiviral transduction, SCC15 cells were transfected with dCas9-VPR plasmids along with either MMP13-targeting sgRNA or negative control sgRNA, yielding the stable cell lines CRISPRa‑MMP13 and CRISPRa‑ctrl. Compared with CRISPRa‑ctrl cells with low MMP13 expression, CRISPRa‑MMP13 cells displayed 1.04–1.58-fold higher proliferative ability upon cisplatin treatment (Fig. 6D), 1.30–1.48-fold under fluorouracil (Fig. 6E), and 1.47–1.84-fold under docetaxel monotherapy (Fig. 6F). Notably, CRISPRa‑MMP13 cells also exhibited a 1.02–1.17-fold increase in basal proliferation even without drug administration (Fig. 6D–F). These findings indicating that MMP13 may promote rapid cell division and confer both basal and therapy-induced proliferative advantage.

Mechanistically, MMP13 upregulation was linked to activation of epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway genes, corroborating earlier reports of a GOLM1–MMP13–EMT axis in cervical cancer [29]. This suggests that MMP13 may also drive chemoresistance through EMT-mediated mechanisms in oral cancer. To verify this hypothesis, we conducted wound-healing and Transwell assays in CRISPRa-MMP13 and SCC15 cell line (NC). The wound closure rate of CRISPRa-MMP13 cells was elevated 1.70-fold (Fig. 6G, H), while cell migratory ability increased 1.1-fold (Fig. 6I, J), confirming that MMP13 overexpression facilitates EMT progression.

Furthermore, we validated the biological function of MMP13 through gene knockdown, and siRNA-mediated silencing of MMP13 significantly reduced chemoresistance in CRISPRa-MMP13 cells to 0.6–0.7 fold of the original level. (Fig. 7A; Additional file 1: Table S8). Consistently, electroporation of siRNA in primary PTCs also efficiently downregulated endogenous MMP13 expression and restored chemosensitivity to some extent (Fig. 7B and C). These results support that MMP13 represents a viable therapeutic target to reverse chemoresistance in OSCC.

In addition, we also identified that other top-ranked DEGs were associated with drug resistance (Additional file 1: Fig. S6A-D). Consistent with previous literature: IRX1, a novel biomarker, is overexpressed in high-grade astrocytoma [30]; positive expression of LOXL2 is correlated with shorter overall survival and poorer clinicopathological parameters [31]; and higher expression of FAM111B has been linked to cancer progression and unfavourable prognosis [32]. Discussion

Head and neck cancer, which includes oral cancer, ranks as the sixth most common malignancy worldwide [33, 34]. However, conventional drug treatment strategies—largely based on generalized clinical experience—frequently yield suboptimal outcomes for patients with OSCC. To address this challenge, we developed PTCs as a transformative preclinical platform tailored for oral cancer research and therapy personalization. Unlike traditional models such as PDC (lacking architectural context), PDX (hampered by prolonged timelines and high costs), or other matrix-dependent PDO systems [35, 36], PTCs retain critical tumor heterogeneity and stromal-immune interactions while requiring minimal tissue input.

Through optimization of 3D culture conditions, we established a robust protocol that achieved a success rate exceeding 97% for oral cancer PTC generation. The high fidelity of PTCs to original patient tumors was rigorously confirmed through multi-level validation encompassing histopathological, genomic, and immune microenvironmental analysis. In a prospective clinical study involving OSCC patients undergoing neoadjuvant TPF chemotherapy, drug sensitivity assay using PTCs demonstrated approximately 90% concordance with clinical ORR—surpassing the predictive performance of existing preclinical models [37, 38]. These results highlight the potential of PTCs to inform individualized treatment strategies and expedite anti-cancer drug discovery.

Moreover, we investigated the association of baseline characteristics with the clinical response. Results showed that the response was not associated with gender, age, anatomic site, T stage, N stage or clinical stage (Additional file 1: Table S7). Therefore, it is necessary to explore biomarkers for predicting the efficacy of NAC in oral cancer.

Furthermore, high-fidelity PTCs were employed to identify DEGs across samples with varying drug sensitivities. This approach not only offers novel insights into investigating the mechanisms of drug resistance but also provides a valuable reference for the discovery of biomarkers in oral cancer. Transcriptomic profiling identified MMP13 overexpression as a promising predictive biomarker, implicating its role in promoting tumor proliferation and chemoresistance. Additionally, PTCs-derived cell lines offer a renewable resource for biobanking, supporting future basic and translational studies. As the first framework to apply PTCs for evaluating chemotherapy response in OSCC, this system effectively bridges tumor biology with clinical outcomes and advances three fundamental pillars of precision oncology: elucidating mechanisms of disease progression and drug resistance, enabling high-throughput therapeutic screening, and facilitating biomarker-driven treatment personalization.

Besides, the cost-effectiveness (no Matrigel/serum), high-throughput screening capability (> 100 drugs per cycle), short time window (< 2 weeks), and strong clinical consistency (typically ≥ 90%) not only solidify PTCs as a trustworthy tool for chemotherapeutic sensitivity assessment but also render them well-suited for resolving immunotherapy challenges, particularly the variable efficacy of checkpoint inhibitors (e.g., PD-1/PD-L1 blockers) that demands reliable predictive stratification [39, 40]. Future investigations exploring PTCs in immune microenvironment modeling or MMP13-targeted therapeutic development may substantially enhance treatment precision, mitigate therapy-related morbidity, and ultimately improve survival for oral cancer patients.

Limitations of the study

Although this study underscores the clinical promise of PTCs in oral cancer, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the relatively modest cohort size (n = 42) restricts robust assessment of factors that may influence drug sensitivity—such as genetic background or comorbidities. Second, using single biopsy samples raises concerns about tumor heterogeneity and sampling bias; multi-region sampling or some modeling to account for this would improve confidence in predictive accuracy. Third, the long-term stability of PTCs across serial passages and cross-laboratory reproducibility remained incompletely clarified. Further validation of histological, genomic and immune consistency across passages and independent laboratories, together with optimized cryopreservation protocols, will be pursued in future work. Moreover, the high-throughput drug screening and multi-drug combination analyses are interesting but lack in-depth mechanistic explanation and clinical correlation beyond the TPF regimen. Additional pathway enrichment analysis and further validation using PTCs or clinical outcome data are needed to strengthen the translational relevance of these findings. Although MMP13 overexpression was correlated with chemotherapy resistance (r = 0.90), its precise functional role in mediating treatment failure remains to be established, warranting further mechanistic investigation to infer causality. Finally, as a single-institution study conducted within the Chinese health care framework, broader validation across diverse ethnic populations and clinical settings will be essential to confirm generalizability. Addressing these limitations through multi-center collaborations, larger patient cohorts, optimized sampling strategies, systematic pathway analysis, independent PTC validation, and integrated multi-omics approaches will be crucial to fully realize the platform’s potential in global precision oncology.

Conclusions

This study establishes PTCs as a transformative 3D ex vivo model that addresses critical unmet needs in OSCC precision therapy. Distinguished by three key strengths, the PTC platform first demonstrates exceptional accuracy—evidenced by an 89.5% concordance rate with clinical NAC outcomes in a prospective trial, far exceeding the < 80% consistency of conventional preclinical models. Second, its standardization is ensured by a Matrigel-independent, serum-free culture protocol that achieves a > 97% success rate across 165 diverse oral cancer specimens, eliminating inter-laboratory variability common in organoid systems and enabling reliable replication. Third, the platform’s efficiency is highlighted by its short time period: it supports large-scale screening of >100 compounds within two weeks and generates clinically actionable data from minimal tissue samples (≥10 mg), a timeline compatible with the urgent decision-making needs of advanced OSCC management.

Beyond its technical merits, the PTC model holds profound clinical significance for patient-centered outcomes. By accurately predicting responses to the standard TPF NAC regimen, it empowers clinicians to select effective therapies for individual patients—directly reducing the risk of administering ineffective treatment that would otherwise lead to unnecessary tumor progression or toxicity. For responders, this targeted approach facilitates maximal tumor downstaging before surgery, which is critical for minimizing surgical resection areas in the oral and maxillofacial region. In turn, reduced resection translates to tangible benefits for patients: preservation of facial aesthetic integrity (a key determinant of quality of life in head and neck cancer) and maintenance of essential physiological functions such as chewing, swallowing, and speech—outcomes that conventional empirical treatment strategies often fail to prioritize.

Collectively, the PTC platform bridges preclinical research and clinical practice, not only advancing precision oncology for OSCC but also setting a benchmark for patient-centric therapeutic development in head and neck malignancies. Its combination of accuracy, standardization, and efficiency positions it as a valuable tool for optimizing personalized treatment regimens and ultimately improving patient prognosis.
