Molecular phylogenetics of Kashmir catfish (Glyptothorax kashmirensis): Genetic identity, conservation units, and basin connectivity
Authors: Nagina Gilani, Richard Alan Nichols, Muhammad Rafique, Shamim Akhter, Shakeel Ahmad, Ahmad Shoaib, Huma Qureshi, Tauseef Anwar, Hesham Hassanien, Nozima Toshova, Ibraheem M. Almalki, Asqar Quvatov, Maryam Alenize, Árpád Székely
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryThe Kashmir catfish, Glyptothorax kashmirensis, is a rheophilic sisorid endemic to the western Himalayas and listed as Critically Endangered because of population decline associated with habitat degradation and river impoundment. To clarify its genetic identity and population status, we analyzed three mitochondrial loci, cytochrome oxidase I (COI), cytochrome b (Cyt-b), and 16S rRNA, from Glyptothorax specimens collected in the Jhelum and Poonch rivers of Azad Jammu and Kashmir, Pakistan, and compared them with GenBank records of related taxa. Maximum likelihood analysis of a 668-bp concatenated alignment recovered the Jhelum and Poonch specimens assigned to G. kashmirensis as a well-supported monophyletic clade with very low mitochondrial divergence. Their closest affinity to Girardia garhwali suggests recent evolutionary separation or historical basin connectivity between the Indus and Ganges systems. These findings support genetic identification and conservation planning for G. kashmirensis.

Introduction

Freshwater fishes are among the most threatened vertebrate groups globally, and river regulation by dams with associated flow modification is a major driver of their decline. Habitat fragmentation, altered flow regimes, disruption of sediment transport, and loss of longitudinal connectivity can rapidly affect rheophilic species that depend on fast-flowing habitats. These threats are especially severe in montane river systems, where localized endemics often have narrow habitat requirements and restricted dispersal ability.

The Kashmir catfish, Glyptothorax kashmirensis Hora,1 is a hill-stream catfish of the family Sisoridae associated with swift-flowing Himalayan streams and rivers and is currently listed as Critically Endangered by the International Union for Conservation of Nature.2 The species is particularly vulnerable to the ecological effects of river impoundment and flow alteration in the Jhelum and Poonch rivers. Such changes may reduce habitat suitability by replacing riffle-dominated stretches with slower, pooled sections and by interrupting connectivity among habitat patches.

Although G. kashmirensis was originally described from the Kashmir Valley, India,1 subsequent records have extended its reported range to additional Himalayan tributaries linked to the Indus system and, in some databases, to upper Ganges drainage localities.3,4,5 However, much of this distributional information is based on morphological species identification, and the species remains poorly represented in molecular datasets. This approach has limited robust evaluation of its taxonomic status, its distinction from different morphologically similar congeners, and the extent of genetic differentiation among populations occurring in different impacted river systems.

Mitochondrial loci have long been used in fish systematics, DNA barcoding, and preliminary conservation genetics. In Glyptothorax, previous work has shown the utility of mitochondrial markers in regions such as COI, Cyt-b, and 16S rRNA for species identification and phylogenetic reconstruction.6,7,8 The use of multiple mitochondrial loci can provide stronger resolution than single-marker analyses, particularly in taxa that are morphologically conservative or difficult to distinguish on external characters alone.9,10,11

The present study uses three mitochondrial loci, COI, Cyt-b, and 16S rRNA, to assess the genetic identity and phylogenetic placement of G. kashmirensis specimens collected from the Jhelum and Poonch rivers in Azad Jammu and Kashmir, Pakistan. Specifically, our objectives were to evaluate the utility of these loci for genetic identification of G. kashmirensis, examine whether the Jhelum and Poonch samples show evidence of mitochondrial differentiation, and place these samples within a broader phylogenetic context using comparative sequence data from related taxa. Given the conservation status of the species, these data are intended as a mitochondrial baseline rather than a complete population-genetic assessment.

Results

Sequence recovery and taxonomic assignment

High-quality mitochondrial sequence data were obtained for the analyzed specimens across the three target loci, COI, Cyt-b, and 16S rRNA. After trimming of low-quality ends and exclusion of ambiguously aligned positions, the retained sequences were suitable for comparative phylogenetic analysis. The newly generated G. kashmirensis 16S rRNA sequences were deposited in GenBank under accession numbers PZ170497, PZ170525, PZ170536, and PZ170593, corresponding to voucher specimens PMNH-GK1, PMNH-GK2, PMNH-GK3, and PMNH-GK4, respectively (Table 1). Comparative sequences of related Glyptothorax taxa and outgroups were downloaded from GenBank and incorporated into the phylogenetic analysis. BLASTn comparison of the edited sequences against GenBank confirmed close affinity to congeneric Glyptothorax taxa and supported the generic assignment of all newly sequenced material. Concatenated alignment characteristics

The final concatenated sequence matrix used for phylogenetic analysis comprised 668 bp of edited sequence derived from COI, Cyt-b, and 16S rRNA. This reduced length reflects retention of only high-quality, alignable, and overlapping regions across all included taxa, rather than the full raw amplicon lengths generated during PCR. Thus, the final phylogenetic matrix represents the conservative shared alignment used for robust interspecific comparison.12

Phylogenetic relationships

Maximum likelihood analysis of the concatenated mitochondrial dataset recovered the sampled G. kashmirensis individuals from the Jhelum and Poonch rivers as a well-supported monophyletic clade (Figure 1). Within this clade, mitochondrial differentiation was minimal, and no subclade corresponding exclusively to the river of origin was recovered. The G. kashmirensis clade was recovered as sister to Girardia garhwali, with approximately 0.5% sequence divergence across the concatenated alignment. This was the closest relationship observed among the comparative taxa included in the present analysis (Figure 1). Other included reference taxa were generally resolved as distinct lineages, although the degree of support varied depending on taxon sampling and availability of homologous sequence data in GenBank (Table 1; Figure 1). The overall topology supports the distinct placement of G. kashmirensis relative to sympatric congeners examined in this study.13,14,15,16

Jhelum-Poonch comparison

The mitochondrial sequences of G. kashmirensis from the Jhelum and Poonch rivers displayed extremely low divergence, with pairwise differentiation below 0.5% in the concatenated dataset. On the basis of these mitochondrial markers, the sampled material is most appropriately interpreted as representing a single genetic unit within one species, rather than two strongly differentiated river-specific mitochondrial lineages. However, because only four G. kashmirensis individuals were included and all markers are maternally inherited, this conclusion should be regarded as preliminary and limited to mitochondrial evidence.

Interpretation of the phylogenetic signal

The phylogenetic pattern observed here provides two principal inferences. First, the Jhelum and Poonch samples identified morphologically as G. kashmirensis are genetically coherent and distinct from the other Glyptothorax taxa examined. Second, their closest mitochondrial affinity to G. garhwali suggests comparatively recent divergence or historical connectivity between ancestral lineages associated with western Himalayan drainages. These inferences are supported by the topology of the ML tree and the low observed divergence values. Still, they should not be taken as evidence of contemporary gene flow or recent demographic connectivity, which would require nuclear marker and population genetic analyses.

Discussion

Utility of mitochondrial loci for genetic identification

The present study demonstrates that a multi-locus mitochondrial approach based on COI, Cyt-b, and 16S rRNA is effective for the preliminary genetic identification and phylogenetic placement of Glyptothorax kashmirensis. The concordant recovery of morphologically assigned G. kashmirensis individuals as a distinct monophyletic lineage in the concatenated ML analysis (Figure 1) supports the usefulness of these loci in a genus where external morphology can be conservative and taxonomic discrimination may be difficult. This result is consistent with text of previous work showing that combinations of mitochondrial markers provide stronger discriminatory power than single-locus barcoding alone in fishes and other vertebrates.7,9,10,11

Among the three loci analyzed here, COI contributes species-level identification value, Cyt-b offers complementary phylogenetic resolution because of its comparatively rapid substitution rate, and 16S rRNA adds stability at somewhat deeper phylogenetic levels. Although mitochondrial markers alone cannot resolve all taxonomic questions, they are especially useful as a first molecular framework for poorly characterized threatened taxa.17,18,19,20 For G. kashmirensis, which has historically been represented mainly by morphological records and sparse genetic data, the new sequences reported here provide an important reference baseline (Table 1).

Taxonomic identity and distinctiveness of Glyptothorax kashmirensis

A major outcome of the present study is the confirmation that the sampled Jhelum and Poonch individuals assigned morphologically to G. kashmirensis form a coherent mitochondrial lineage distinct from other Glyptothorax species included in the analysis (Figure 1). This supports the continued recognition of G. kashmirensis as a valid taxonomic entity rather than a misidentified local population of another congener. Given the historical uncertainty associated with distributional records and the limited molecular representation of the species in public databases, this result has direct systematic value.

At the same time, the close mitochondrial relationship between G. kashmirensis and G. garhwali deserves careful interpretation. Their low divergence in the present dataset may reflect recent evolutionary separation, incomplete lineage sorting, or historical drainage connectivity. However, the available evidence is not sufficient to synonymize the taxa or to collapse them into a single operational unit. Taxonomic decisions at that level would require denser geographic sampling and inclusion of informative nuclear markers, ideally supplemented by renewed comparative morphological study.

Relationship between the Jhelum and Poonch populations

The very low mitochondrial divergence observed between the Jhelum and Poonch samples suggests that the populations examined here are best interpreted as a single genetic unit within one species. This inference is intentionally conservative because mitochondrial homogeneity alone cannot demonstrate contemporary panmixia or unrestricted dispersal. Mitochondrial homogeneity can arise under several scenarios, including recent separation, retention of ancestral haplotypes, or low but historically sufficient female-mediated connectivity. It does not, by itself, demonstrate panmixia, unrestricted dispersal, or demographic interchange at present.21,22,23

This distinction is important in a conservation context. Panmixia is properly tested using nuclear markers and population-genetic approaches that can assess allele frequencies, Hardy-Weinberg expectations, and contemporary or recent gene flow. Because the present dataset includes only a small number of individuals and only mitochondrial loci, the current evidence should be interpreted as indicating no strong mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) structuring in the sampled material, rather than as proving the absence of population subdivision. Future studies should therefore extend sampling across the known range of G. kashmirensis and employ nuclear loci or genome-wide markers to evaluate whether the Jhelum and Poonch populations remain genetically connected or are becoming differentiated under ongoing fragmentation pressure.24,25,26

Historical basin connectivity and sister relationship with Glyptothorax garhwali

The sister relationship recovered between G. kashmirensis and G. garhwali is one of the most biogeographically informative outcomes of this study. Their close mitochondrial affinity suggests that historical connections among western Himalayan drainages may have shaped the current distribution of lineages now occupying different basin systems. River capture, drainage rearrangement, and glacially influenced hydrological shifts are well-established drivers of faunal exchange and diversification in mountain freshwater biotas.27,28 In this context, the observed affinity between taxa associated with the Indus-linked and Ganges-linked systems is plausible and consistent with a history of basin reorganization.29,30,31

Nevertheless, this interpretation should be framed as a historical biogeographic hypothesis rather than a demonstrated mechanism. The present study did not estimate divergence times, reconstruct ancestral areas, or test alternative drainage-history models. A stronger test of the river-capture hypothesis would require broader taxon sampling, dated phylogenetic analysis, and denser genomic data across relevant Himalayan drainages.32,33,34,35

Conservation implications

The conservation implications of these findings are substantial even at this preliminary stage. First, the study provides molecular support for recognizing G. kashmirensis as a distinct lineage of conservation concern. Second, the lack of strong mitochondrial differentiation between the Jhelum and Poonch samples suggests that management should prioritize maintaining or restoring connectivity before prolonged isolation erodes within-species variation. Third, the species’ apparent restriction to fast-flowing hillstream habitat makes it especially vulnerable to dam construction, altered flow regimes, sediment retention, and habitat homogenization, all of which reduce the availability of riffle-dominated microhabitats typical of rheophilic catfishes.36,37,38

Conservation practice should therefore extend beyond species recognition to habitat-focused intervention. Protection of remaining suitable reaches, environmental flow maintenance, reduction of local habitat degradation, and preservation of longitudinal connectivity are immediate priorities. Where hydrological fragmentation is already severe, carefully assessed mitigation options such as fish passage structures, managed translocation, or assisted gene flow may merit consideration. Such interventions should aim not merely to preserve census numbers, but to retain within-population genetic variation and thus the evolutionary potential of the species.39,40,41

Conservation units and future research priorities

Although the present mitochondrial results support the interpretation that the sampled Jhelum and Poonch material belongs to a single genetic unit, they do not provide a sufficient basis for formal delineation of management units or evolutionarily significant units (ESUs).25,42 Widely used conservation-genetic frameworks generally require evidence from multiple independent data sources, often including nuclear differentiation, demographic independence, ecological distinctiveness, and, in some definitions, adaptive variation.43,44,45 Accordingly, the current findings should be viewed as a first approximation relevant to conservation planning, not as a final statement on unit delimitation. The present study was designed as a preliminary mitochondrial phylogenetic analysis and was not intended to provide a full population-genetic assessment. Therefore, analyses such as haplotype networks, gene-flow estimation, Hardy-Weinberg testing, demographic reconstruction, and formal ESU inference were beyond the scope of the available dataset.

Future research should address at least five priorities: (1) broader geographic sampling across the range of G. kashmirensis; (2) nuclear marker or genome-wide assessment of population structure and connectivity; (3) denser comparative sampling of closely related Glyptothorax taxa, especially G. garhwali; (4) integration of morphology, ecology, and genomics in taxonomic reassessment; and (5) repeated temporal monitoring to determine whether fragmentation is already generating measurable genetic subdivision. Such work would allow more defensible inference regarding conservation units and provide a stronger evidence base for long-term management.

This study provides the mitochondrial phylogenetic assessment focused on the Critically Endangered Kashmir catfish, G. kashmirensis. Using three mitochondrial loci—COI, Cyt-b, and 16S rRNA—we confirmed that the sampled Jhelum and Poonch individuals identified morphologically as G. kashmirensis form a distinct and well-supported monophyletic lineage. The species shows a close mitochondrial relationship to G. garhwali, consistent with recent evolutionary separation and possible historical connectivity between Himalayan River basins. The very low mitochondrial divergence between the Jhelum and Poonch samples indicates that, based on the present dataset, these populations represent a single genetic unit within one species. However, this conclusion remains preliminary because it is based on a small sample size and maternally inherited markers only. Accordingly, the results should be used as a baseline for conservation genetics rather than as a definitive assessment of population structure. Given the threatened status of the species and the ongoing pressures imposed by habitat fragmentation and river regulation, immediate conservation attention is warranted. Broader genomic sampling, nuclear-marker-based population surveys, and long-term habitat and genetic monitoring will be essential for evidence-based conservation planning and for refining conservation-unit boundaries across the range of G. kashmirensis.

Limitations of the study

This study provides a mitochondrial baseline for the genetic identification of G. kashmirensis, but several limitations should be considered. The analysis was based on a small number of G. kashmirensis specimens from only the Jhelum and Poonch rivers, which limits inference across the species’ full geographic range. The phylogenetic dataset was also restricted to mitochondrial loci and a conservative 668-bp concatenated alignment; therefore, the results reflect maternally inherited variation only and cannot fully resolve nuclear genetic structure, contemporary gene flow, demographic history, or formal conservation-unit boundaries. The close affinity between G. kashmirensis and G. garhwali should be interpreted as a biogeographic hypothesis rather than definitive evidence of recent connectivity. Broader sampling and nuclear or genome-wide markers are needed to refine conservation planning.

Experimental model and study participant details

Fish specimens were collected from the Jhelum and Poonch rivers, two tributaries of the Indus River system in Azad Jammu and Kashmir, Pakistan. Fish collection was conducted under appropriate institutional permission, and no protected-area-specific collection permit was required for the sampled localities. Sampling localities were geo-referenced at Poonch (33°42′14.94″ N, 73°57′12.92″ E) and Jhelum (34°10′24.28″ N, 73°47′5.07″ E). Method details

DNA extraction

Muscle tissue was dissected from ethanol-preserved specimens, and genomic DNA was extracted using the PCRBIO Rapid Extract Kit (PCR Biosystems, UK) following the manufacturer’s protocol with minor optimization for fish tissue. Tissue samples of approximately 2.5–6 mg were incubated in extraction buffer and protease solution, followed by heat treatment to facilitate lysis and inactivate proteases. DNA extracts were stored at −20 °C until PCR amplification.

PCR amplification and sequencing

Three mitochondrial loci were targeted: COI, Cyt-b, and 16S rRNA. Published primer pairs were used for each marker,6 and related references. PCR reactions were performed in 50-μL volumes using 2× PCRBIO Taq Mix Red master mix containing Taq DNA polymerase, dNTPs, MgCl2, and reaction buffer (PCR Biosystems, UK), locus-specific forward and reverse primers, template DNA, and PCR-grade water. Thermal cycling conditions were adjusted according to the published methods for each primer pair.

For the Glyptothorax specimens analyzed in this study, the expected amplified regions were approximately 650 bp for COI, approximately 1,140 bp for Cyt-b, and approximately 550 bp for 16S rRNA. These values refer to expected PCR products rather than the final edited alignment used in phylogenetic analysis. PCR products were purified using the GeneJET PCR Purification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) following the manufacturer’s instructions, and sequenced bidirectionally by Eurofins Genomics (Germany). Chromatograms were inspected manually using FinchTV v1.4.0 (Geospiza Inc.; https://digitalworldbiology.com/FinchTV) to confirm base calls and identify ambiguous sites. Problematic sites were checked against reverse reads and, where necessary, by repeat amplification and sequencing.

Sequence assembly, alignment, and GenBank submission

Forward and reverse sequences were edited and assembled to produce consensus sequences. Sequences were aligned using MAFFT v7 and inspected manually.52 For phylogenetic analysis, only high-quality overlapping regions that could be aligned confidently across samples and reference taxa were retained. Newly generated G. kashmirensis 16S rRNA sequences were deposited in GenBank under accession numbers PZ170497, PZ170525, PZ170536, and PZ170593.

Phylogenetic analysis

The best-fitting nucleotide substitution model for the concatenated alignment was selected in jModelTest v2.1.10 using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC),47 the software is available from the jModelTest2 repository (https://github.com/ddarriba/jmodeltest2). Phylogenetic reconstruction was performed using the Maximum Likelihood (ML) method in MEGA7.48 Bootstrap support values were estimated from 1,000 pseudoreplicates. Glaridoglanis andersonii was used as the outgroup based on prior phylogenetic studies of sisorid catfishes. The resulting tree was visualized and annotated in iTOL v5.53

Quantification and statistical analysis

The final concatenated sequence matrix used for phylogenetic analysis comprised 668 bp of edited sequence derived from COI, Cyt-b, and 16S rRNA. This reduced length reflects retention of only high-quality, alignable, and overlapping regions across all included taxa, rather than the full raw amplicon lengths generated during PCR. Maximum Likelihood analysis was performed using the selected nucleotide substitution model, and nodal support was assessed using 1,000 bootstrap pseudoreplicates. Bootstrap values above 70% are reported on the phylogenetic tree. Pairwise mitochondrial divergence was interpreted as percentage sequence divergence across the concatenated alignment. No pooled quantitative experimental data, error bars, or asterisk-coded statistical significance tests are presented in the figures.
