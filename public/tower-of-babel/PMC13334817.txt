A scoping review of real-world data sources for retrospective oncology analysis in Japan
Authors: D. Ng, H. Pittell, I. Nasu, M. Araki, Q. Zhang, E. Tajima, A. Sujenthiran, B. Adamson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCancer remains the leading cause of mortality in Japan, creating significant demand for high-quality real-world evidence to support clinical research and decision making. This study aims to provide a comprehensive overview of oncology studies using real-world data (RWD) in Japan, assessing the suitability of these data sources for oncology research.MethodsSearches were conducted in PubMed, Ichushi-Web, and Google Scholar for studies published from 1 January 2020 to 28 February 2025. Eligibility criteria focused on retrospective observational studies utilizing readily available RWD databases. The availability of information on patient demographics, clinical characteristics, genomic information, treatment patterns, and clinical outcomes, as well as data source limitations reported by authors, was extracted and analyzed.ResultsOf 484 studies screened, 121 met the eligibility criteria. The majority utilized administrative claims data (52%), followed by registries (44%) and electronic health record (EHR)-derived data (6%). Limitations varied by data source. Claims data lacked clinical detail, resulting in challenges in accurate study cohort identification and outcomes research; registries contained deep genomic information but had high clinical data missingness and restricted cohorts; EHR-derived data provided detailed clinical insights but were less established in scale.ConclusionThis scoping review highlights the strengths and limitations of Japanese RWD sources, revealing the frequent use of claims data in oncology research despite their limited clinical granularity. Future research should increasingly leverage clinically rich and longitudinal data sources, particularly EHR-derived datasets, to enable more precise cohort identification and capture meaningful clinical outcomes, better supporting clinical and research needs in Japanese oncology.

Introduction

Cancer has been the leading cause of mortality in Japan for more than 40 years, representing a significant public health burden.1 In recent years, accelerated research and development of anticancer therapeutics, such as molecular targeted therapies indicated for specific genetic mutations and immune checkpoint inhibitors for specific cancer types or biomarker-defined subgroups, has driven the demand for high-quality evidence to expedite patient access to effective treatments.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have traditionally served as the gold standard for generating evidence in cancer treatment.2 However, most conventional RCTs employ restrictive inclusion and exclusion criteria based on factors such as age, comorbidities, and performance status, resulting in the exclusion of substantial portions of the patient population encountered in routine clinical practice. Consequently, RCT populations do not reflect the diversity and complexity of real-world populations of patients with cancer, limiting the external validity and generalizability of trial findings in clinical practice.3 With only ∼7% of patients with cancer participating in clinical trials,3 and the inclusion of Japanese patients in global trials remaining limited,4 the need for methodologies for generating holistic evidence to inform real-world clinical practice is imminent.

To address the aforementioned gap, real-world data (RWD), defined as data relating to patient health status and/or the delivery of health care routinely collected from a variety of sources, and real-world evidence (RWE), defined as the evidence derived from analysis of RWD,5 are increasingly being utilized as a complement to evidence generated from RCTs. Globally, with various guidelines and frameworks being developed to guide the selection of fit-for-purpose RWD and design of methodologically robust research,6, 7, 8 RWE has become indispensable in supporting decision making across clinical practice, drug development, regulatory approval, health care policy, and patient access.9,10

Amidst these developments, it is crucial to acknowledge that the ability to generate and utilize RWE for informing significant decisions concerning patient access to safe and effective treatments relies heavily on the availability and maturity of RWD within the region. In Japan, the Pharmaceuticals and Medical Devices Agency’s published notifications that guide the use of real-world databases and registry data in supporting postmarketing safety studies and new drug applications, respectively,11, 12, 13 as well as various academia-led registries and local initiatives, have bolstered the use of RWD locally.5 However, there remain widely acknowledged challenges in the selection and practical application of fit-for-purpose RWD, particularly in the regulatory setting, where data quality, reliability, and relevance are paramount.14, 15, 16, 17 Notably, there is a growing number of real-world databases available for generating evidence on drug effectiveness and safety in Japan,18 yet the majority of RWD use in Japan is reported to be centered around administrative claims-based data.19 In the field of oncology research, generating meaningful RWE requires information on real-world outcomes such as disease progression and response; detailed diagnosis-related information such as genetic mutations, clinical and pathological stage, histology, and evidence of metastasis; and critical clinical characteristics that may influence treatment decisions and prognosis, such as the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group Performance Status (ECOG PS) score, menopausal status, and smoking history. Administrative claims-based RWD do not contain the majority of such information, and previously reported reviews of the literature did not adequately elucidate the current state of RWD availability and utilization specific to the field of oncology in Japan.

As such, this study aimed to provide an up-to-date overview of oncology studies conducted utilizing existing, readily available RWD sources in Japan and to assess the strengths and limitations of these databases available in Japan for oncology research.

Methods

Search strategy and eligibility criteria

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across multiple databases and information sources to identify relevant studies on RWD utilization in oncology research in Japan. The primary databases searched were PubMed, Ichushi-Web, and Google Scholar. The search criteria for each database are provided in Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100727. The databases were last searched on 21 March 2025. To capture recent developments and emerging research, a targeted review of gray literature was conducted, focusing on studies presented at the most recent academic and clinical congresses at the time this review was conducted. Specifically, we reviewed abstracts and presentations from the 62th Annual Meeting of Japanese Society of Clinical Oncology (JSCO 2024), the 16th Asian Conference on Pharmacoepidemiology (ACPE 2024), and the 22nd Japanese Society of Medical Oncology Annual Meeting (JSMO 2025). This multi-database approach, combined with gray literature review, ensured comprehensive coverage of both published literature and recent conference presentations in the field of RWE in Japanese oncology research.

This scoping review included studies conducted on patient populations in Japan with a focus on oncology. We included noninterventional, retrospective studies utilizing RWD from existing databases, including commercially available datasets and academia-led registries. Studies with data sources involving study-specific intentional data capture from medical institutions, such as retrospective chart reviews and prospective observational studies, were excluded. Additionally, qualitative studies examining clinical outcomes assessment, patient-reported outcomes, interviews, or surveys were considered irrelevant to this study and therefore excluded. The literature search was conducted to identify studies published between 1 January 2020 and 28 February 2025. Studies published in English or Japanese were included to ensure comprehensive coverage of relevant literature from both international and Japanese sources. This review was limited to open access articles. Apart from studies identified from the gray literature review, studies were required to have abstracts and full manuscripts accessible in the public domain to be included in this review. This approach ensured that the review captured relevant RWE studies from the Japanese oncology setting while maintaining methodological rigor and accessibility standards.

Data extraction and synthesis

Title and abstract screening was conducted using a technology-enabled approach, employing a large language model (LLM; OpenAI API GPT-4o) using prompts engineered to assess the eligibility of studies according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria described above. To ensure the accuracy of the LLM-enabled screening, a human reviewer conducted a quality check of LLM screening results for all articles to make a final determination of eligibility. Reasons for misalignment were identified in the process. Duplicate articles and those meeting the above exclusion criteria were excluded at this stage. Initial plans to deploy an LLM for full-text screening and data extraction were revisited based on the performance of the model during the title and abstract screening stage; full-text screening was therefore conducted independently by a human reviewer, and articles that fulfilled the eligibility criteria were included for data extraction review. Data extraction was conducted by one reviewer using a standardized form. No processes were implemented to obtain or confirm data from study investigators, as this review was restricted to information reported in the published literature.

We extracted information on the availability of the following categories of variables in databases used in each study: patient demographics, clinical features (cancer stage, histology), genomic information, treatment patterns, outcomes (mortality, progression, response), safety, and health care resource utilization (HCRU) and/or medical costs. All available data points compatible with these categories were extracted from each study. Additional information extracted included study design, data source type, cancer types studied, author affiliations, publication language, and database limitations as reported by study authors. No assumptions were made about missing or unclear information. Risk of bias assessment was not carried out for individual studies, as this review focused on characterizing data sources rather than evaluating study quality or treatment effects. No effect measures were calculated as this review was descriptive in nature, focusing on characterizing data sources and study characteristics rather than synthesizing treatment effects. Studies were grouped by data source type for descriptive synthesis. No statistical synthesis or meta-analysis was carried out. Results were tabulated and presented descriptively using frequencies and percentages. We followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist.20

We summarized the number and percentages of studies by database type [administrative claims data, electronic health record (EHR)-derived data, registry data, and others; some studies utilized more than one type of database], author affiliations (academia, industry, and government), tumor type(s) studied, and key study endpoints. We extracted a summary of the study limitations and database limitations reported for each database that was used in two or more oncology studies between 2020 and 2025.

Results

Among 484 studies screened, 121 studies met the eligibility criteria after the full-text screening process and were included for data extraction in this scoping review of readily available RWD databases used in oncology research in Japan. Further details on the process of study identification, screening, and final inclusion are provided in the PRISMA flow diagram (Figure 1). After removing duplicates, 470 records remained, of which 122 (26%) were in Japanese and 348 (74%) in English. Title and abstract screening with the LLM had ∼77% concordance with human screening and all disagreements were resolved. Of 63 (52%) studies that reported specific limitations about the Japan oncology data source used, key themes were identified by data source category. Among the 43 studies using administrative claims data, 93% reported limitations related to insufficient diagnostic and clinical information, including confirmed cancer diagnosis, cancer stage, histology, and ECOG PS. Additionally, 56% of studies using administrative claims data noted limited availability of treatment details beyond prescription claims, such as drug dosage, medication administration, and oral treatment adherence, while 44% of these studies reported insufficient clinical outcomes information, including disease progression, treatment response, and mortality events occurring outside the hospital. On the contrary, among 21 studies using registry data, inadequacy or missingness of patient clinical characteristics, treatment history, and outcomes were reported in 76%, 62%, and 38% of studies, respectively. Insufficient data capture was linked to the inability to follow patients through their patient journey beyond the scope of the registry, and data missingness was primarily attributed to the voluntary nature of these registries, which resulted in heterogeneity in data entry quality by participating sites. Of the two studies using EHR-derived data that reported data source-specific limitations, the lack of clinical characteristics and outcomes information were primarily due to reliance on structured data sources from within the EHR. Detailed clinical data critical for oncology research can often be found in unstructured data in the EHR, but the RWD sources used in these two studies lacked sufficient clinical depth, owing to cited challenges faced with accurate data extraction for unstructured free-text or scanned documents within the EHR. Discussion

This is the first known scoping review focused on oncology studies utilizing readily available RWD sources in Japan, with findings reported in accordance with the PRISMA-ScR checklist (Supplementary Table S3, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmorw.2026.100727). Prior reports have outlined the range of real-world databases and RWD collection initiatives that can support epidemiologic and health outcomes research in Japan across all therapeutic areas.5,15,18,19 Similar to trends observed in these prior reviews on pan-therapeutic area RWE research, the three main categories of databases used in oncology studies identified in this review were administrative claims-based data, registry data, and EHR-derived databases. The characteristics and limitations reported by authors for each database category were largely aligned with those of prior reports5,10,143; in the context of oncology RWE generation, this review identified a distinct set of strengths and limitations pertaining to the fitness-for-purpose of various data sources in enabling oncology research.

Core RWD sources for retrospective oncology analysis in Japan

Administrative claims-based data

Claims-based data in Japan were vital for capturing a broad range of health care interactions, including inpatient and outpatient visits, procedures, and pharmaceutical prescriptions. These datasets were characterized by their relatively large sample sizes, offering a high-level snapshot of HCRU and prescription patterns across therapeutic areas. Although claims data were often used effectively in tracking drug prescription patterns and providing insights into health care delivery systems in the context of a single hospital or insurer, the claims databases were inherently limited by their lack of clinical detail, as they did not capture information on clinically confirmed diagnoses, disease severity, outcomes, or specific clinical parameters such as genetic mutations, which are crucial for identifying populations of interest and assessing treatment effectiveness in the context of oncology. Additionally, potential misclassification and inaccuracies due to coding errors or missing data were often taken into consideration in the studies. A common limitation reported in several studies included in this review was the risk of misclassification bias, as the identification of specific study cohorts defined by pathologic and molecular profiles (e.g. breast cancer molecular subtypes) could only be defined using surrogate variables (e.g. prescription history of anti-HER2 therapy, CDK4/6 inhibitors, etc.).26,27,30,34,57,83 The utility of administrative claims data was primarily in the description of prescription patterns and HCRU among a broad population of patients with cancer identified by diagnosis codes.

Registry data

The review included a wide variety of oncology registry databases in Japan. Nationwide and prefectural hospital-based cancer registries captured a wide range of incident cancer cases; other national registries such as C-CAT focused on a specific subset of patients with cancer who receive comprehensive genomic profiling; and registries led by clinical and academic societies such as the Japanese Society of Obstetrics and Gynecology Tumor Registry included patients with a specific tumor type. Although the magnitude and case coverage of these registries differed depending on the predefined purpose and nature of the organization, the depth and accuracy of available clinical and genomic variables sometimes rendered these datasets more suitable as data sources for the observational oncology research reviewed, compared with administrative claims databases. However, although population-based cancer registry data were recognized as a standard for assessing cancer epidemiology and burden,144 challenges in tracking treatments and outcomes beyond the predefined scope of variables and participating institutions were also recognized as common limitations in studies included in this review. Additionally, high levels of missingness were reported across the majority of studies using registry data in this review; some studies alluded to challenges in data entry at each participating institution and limited governance on the accuracy and completeness of information registered.85,88 As such, availability of the longitudinal capture of patient journey and the representation of patient profiles and treatment patterns were critically interpreted while considering the specific characteristics of the registry used in each research study.

EHR-derived data

EHR-derived databases provided a unique opportunity to conduct real-world oncology studies in Japan. EHR-derived data were classified in two main categories: databases that include information primarily from structured data sources within the EHR (i.e. diagnosis codes, laboratory test results, medication orders), such as the Tokushukai Real-World Data, and databases that curate information comprehensively from both structured and unstructured data sources within the EHR and peripheral department systems (e.g. laboratory information management systems), such as the Japan Flatiron Health Research Database. The former ensured access to laboratory test results that were not available in most administrative claims-based databases, while the latter provided deep clinical insights into variables that are critical for oncology research, such as clinical and pathological stage, histology, ECOG PS, biomarker test results, and clinical outcomes such as real-world progression and response. With the variability of information documentation practices within EHR systems within and across medical institutions, and in turn, the effort required to process such data, challenges in curating certain variables such as adverse events and prognostic factors, metastatic site, tumor markers, prognostic scores based on laboratory data, comorbidities, and complications were cited.135 Additionally, it has been acknowledged that with the complexities of curating EHR-derived RWD, time and resources are required for data curation and growth of these datasets to be adequately representative of the study population.9 Despite these challenges, with a growing number of EHR-derived RWD databases emerging in Japan,18 researchers can now leverage real-world datasets derived from a mix of structured and unstructured EHR data sources to conduct comparative effectiveness research on a biomarker- and cancer stage-defined subpopulation of patients with cancer in Japan.

Current and future directions of Japanese RWE in oncology

Given the profile of existing real-world databases that are readily available for use in research in Japan, it is not surprising that claims-based databases were the most commonly used data source, despite the widely acknowledged limitations of claims-based data, particularly in the field of oncology. Outside of Japan, other reviews of RWD utilization in global oncology research have demonstrated varying trends. In a recent review on RWE studies in solid tumors conducted globally between 2020 and 2022, the proportion of studies using EHR-derived data far outweigh that of studies using claims-based data (55% compared with 5%).145 In a review of databases used in clinical oncology research that were submitted to the American Society of Clinical Oncology Publications,146 it was found that 49% of studies used the Medicare claims-based database, while 40% used Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) registry data, and ∼25% utilized these in combination with another database, such as EHR-derived RWD. Although the composition of studies and definitions of data sources included in each of these reviews varied, and the important acknowledgment that the availability of RWD sources in each country impacts the volume of research conducted using these data sources, the trend toward leveraging EHR-derived data for oncology RWE generation is clear.

Differences in countries’ health care, technology, and insurance systems remain deeply rooted factors that impact the ease of access and curation of RWD. In Japan, the universal national health insurance system mandates standardized formatting for administrative claims submissions by health care institutions. However, challenges persist in the EHR infrastructure, characterized by site-specific customizations and siloed, on-premise systems. Consequently, administrative claims data represents the most accessible and least complex source for RWD curation within the Japanese health care landscape. However, with the emergence of EHR-derived real-world databases in Japan, the maturation of the RWD landscape presents significant opportunities for researchers and pharmaceutical sponsors, allowing for the generation of reliable oncology RWE to inform drug development strategies, clinician assessments, and regulatory decision making. The global discourse on the development of high-quality RWD for research continues, with discussions on scale, granularity, harmonization, and data quality in general. It is therefore crucial for database curators to develop robust frameworks to ensure data quality, tailored to the respective markets in which they operate.136

Limitations

This review has several limitations. Firstly, the review was limited to open access publications from three databases (Pubmed, Ichushi-Web, and Google Scholar) and may have missed other relevant studies; a targeted review of local congresses mitigated this limitation. Secondly, risk of bias assessment was not conducted, limiting our ability to comment on study quality. Thirdly, LLM screening achieved only 77% concordance with human screening, but studies were included in this review based on human assessment, thereby mitigating the risk of LLM-induced systematic bias in study selection. Fourthly, this review was intentionally limited to studies that utilized existing real-world databases in Japan, given the time-sensitive nature of oncology evidentiary needs that differentiate the value of readily available databases compared with conducting intentional data capture via observational studies. As such, other prominent RWD collection initiatives and prospective observational studies such as SCRUM-Japan were not included in this review.147 Additionally, as this study was focused on oncology RWE research, other databases that are more commonly used in non-oncology therapeutic areas, such as the Medical Information Database Network,148 were not identified via the search strings used. Finally, the included studies varied in their methodological approaches and reporting standards, thus we were not able to guarantee the exhaustive capture of all strengths and limitations of real-world databases in the context of oncology research.

Conclusion

Amidst the evolving landscape of evidence generation in the field of oncology, where RWE is increasingly recognized as a valuable complement to the traditional gold standard of RCTs, understanding the availability of fit-for-purpose RWD sources in Japan is paramount in the furtherance of oncology research and improving patient access to effective treatments in Japan. This review was therefore a first step in elucidating the strengths and limitations of readily available RWD sources to support oncology research in Japan. Recent initiatives to improve data quality, integration, and access are likely to further enhance the value of Japanese RWD for oncology research. Although challenges remain, particularly regarding data completeness, linkage capabilities, and standardization, the growing body of published studies demonstrates the significant potential of these data sources for advancing oncology care in Japan and contributing to global knowledge in cancer research. Future research should focus on developing standardized data quality metrics, improving interoperability between data sources, and providing guidance for researchers on selecting appropriate data sources and designing methodologically sound research to answer important clinical questions in oncology.
