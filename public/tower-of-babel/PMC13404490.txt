Introduction

Falls among older adults constitute a major global public health concern with escalating burden.1 The Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 reported approximately 45.7 million new falls globally, representing a 182% increase since 1990.1 The WHO estimates that 684 000 fatal falls occur annually, with adults aged 60 years and older experiencing the greatest proportion of these deaths.2 For community-dwelling adults aged 65 years and older, between 28% and 35% experience at least one fall each year, rising to 42% for those aged 70 years and above.3 4 Recent temporal trend analysis across 59 countries projects continued increases in fall-related mortality through 2040 without enhanced prevention efforts.5

The consequences of falls extend beyond immediate physical injury. Hip fractures carry a 1-year mortality rate of approximately 20–30%, with survivors often experiencing permanent functional decline.6 Fear of falling affects 20–85% of older adults who have experienced a fall, leading to activity restriction, social isolation and increased fall risk through deconditioning.7 The psychological sequelae including anxiety and loss of confidence frequently precipitate a downward trajectory toward frailty and institutionalisation.3

The economic burden is substantial and continues to escalate. Updated estimates indicate that healthcare spending attributable to non-fatal older adult falls in the USA reached US$80 billion in 2021, significantly higher than previous estimates.8 Similar cost escalation patterns are observed globally, with projections suggesting the economic burden will continue to rise as populations age.9 10

Home-based fall detection and monitoring technologies have emerged as promising interventions to reduce fall-related morbidity and mortality. These technologies have evolved considerably, encompassing personal emergency response systems, wearable sensors using accelerometers and gyroscopes, ambient sensor systems, camera-based detection platforms and artificial intelligence-driven predictive analytics.11 12 A recent scoping review identified motion sensors, imaging systems, environmental sensors and robotics as the primary technological categories, with machine learning algorithms increasingly integrated into detection systems.13 However, a systematic review of digital technologies for fall detection in older adults noted that most studies were based on synthetic rather than real-world data, limiting generalisability.14

Despite technological advancement, the cost-effectiveness evidence remains poorly synthesised. While systematic reviews have examined the clinical effectiveness of fall detection devices and the cost-effectiveness of fall prevention exercise programmes,10 no comprehensive review has specifically examined the economic value of fall detection and monitoring technologies. A scoping review of telehealth in ageing care found inconsistent evidence regarding cost benefits, with 42 reviews reporting positive findings and 20 reporting unclear outcomes.15 This fragmented evidence base impedes informed decision-making by healthcare policymakers, technology assessment bodies and clinicians seeking to advise patients about technology adoption.

The primary objective of this planned systematic review is to determine whether home-based fall detection and monitoring technologies represent cost-effective interventions for community-dwelling older adults compared with usual care, no intervention or alternative fall detection strategies. While fall detection technologies respond to falls after occurrence, they reduce healthcare costs through rapid emergency response that minimises injury severity, prevention of prolonged immobility (‘long lies’) that compound morbidity and identification of high-risk individuals who may benefit from targeted prevention interventions. Cost-effectiveness will be assessed through examination of incremental cost-effectiveness ratios, cost per quality-adjusted life year gained, cost per fall prevented and other relevant economic outcomes reported in identified studies.

Secondary objectives include characterising the range of technologies evaluated in economic studies; examining methodological approaches employed in cost-effectiveness analyses including analytical perspectives, time horizons and uncertainty handling; identifying key cost drivers and health outcomes measured; exploring heterogeneity across settings, populations and technology types; and highlighting evidence gaps to inform future research priorities and health technology assessment requirements.

This review focuses specifically on technologies designed for fall detection, monitoring and emergency response. While fall prevention interventions (such as exercise programmes, home modifications or medication reviews) represent important complementary strategies, they are outside the scope of this review unless integrated with detection/monitoring technologies. Technologies that combine fall detection with predictive analytics or risk monitoring will be included, as the detection component remains central to their function and cost-effectiveness. The distinction is important: fall prevention aims to reduce fall incidence before events occur, while fall detection responds to falls after occurrence by enabling rapid emergency response, minimising injury severity through timely intervention and identifying high-risk patterns that may inform subsequent prevention efforts.

The review question follows the Population, Intervention, Comparator, Outcomes, and Study design (PICOS) framework as recommended for economic evaluation reviews.16 The population comprises community-dwelling older adults aged 60 years or older. Interventions include any home-based technology designed to detect falls, monitor fall risk or facilitate emergency response. Comparators include usual care, no intervention or alternative fall detection strategies. Outcomes encompass all economic measures including costs, cost-effectiveness ratios and budget impact. Eligible study designs include full and partial economic evaluations conducted alongside trials or using modelling approaches.

Methods

Study design and registration

This protocol was reported following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Protocols (PRISMA-P) statement.17 The completed review will be reported according to PRISMA 2020 guidelines with adaptations recommended for systematic reviews of economic evaluations.18 19 The protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (registration number CRD420261433029) prior to commencing the search to enhance transparency and reduce reporting bias. Methodological guidance has been drawn from the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and the Campbell and Cochrane Economics Methods Group recommendations for incorporating economic evidence.20 Registration was completed during the peer-review process for this protocol and prior to commencing the review; the PROSPERO record (CRD420261433029) has since been updated through a dated amendment so that the registry entry and this manuscript are concordant with respect to the review team, the bibliographic and economic databases to be searched and the risk-of-bias instruments to be applied (the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool for economic evaluations conducted alongside randomised trials and the Assessment of the Validation Status of Health-Economic decision models (AdViSHE) tool for model-based evaluations). The registry additionally lists team members who will contribute to study screening and data extraction during conduct of the review.

Information sources and search strategy

A comprehensive search will be conducted across multiple databases following established guidance for systematic reviews of economic evaluations.21 Primary bibliographic databases include MEDLINE via PubMed, Embase via Elsevier, Cochrane Library including CENTRAL, CINAHL via EBSCO and Web of Science Core Collection. Economic databases include EconLit, the NHS Economic Evaluation Database (archived content through 2015), Health Technology Assessment Database and the Tufts Cost-Effectiveness Analysis Registry.22 Grey literature sources include ClinicalTrials.gov, health technology assessment agency websites including National Institute for Health and Care Excellence (NICE), Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health (CADTH), and Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ), and conference proceedings from International Society for Pharmacoeconomics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) and related meetings. Reference lists of included studies and relevant systematic reviews will be hand-searched, with forward citation searching via Google Scholar.

The search strategy combines controlled vocabulary (Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms, Emtree) with free-text terms addressing population, intervention and economic outcomes, following the approach used in recent high-quality systematic reviews of fall prevention interventions.10 23 No methodological filters will be applied to maximise sensitivity. The search will cover database inception to the date of search execution, with no language restrictions. Non-English articles will be translated using professional translation services where necessary.

The following example search string will be used for PubMed/MEDLINE:

This strategy will be adapted for each database according to specific syntax and indexing conventions. The complete, line-by-line search strategies for all databases, registers and websites, including all filters and limits, are provided in online supplemental file 1.

Study selection

Study selection will be conducted using Covidence systematic review software (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia), which enables independent duplicate screening with automated conflict detection and maintains a complete audit trail of decisions. The selection process will proceed in two stages following Cochrane guidance.20 First, two reviewers will independently screen titles and abstracts against eligibility criteria. Second, full texts of potentially eligible records will be retrieved and assessed independently. Exclusion reasons will be documented hierarchically. Disagreements will be resolved through discussion or consultation with a third reviewer. The process will be piloted on a sample of 50 records to ensure consistent criteria application and to refine screening instructions. Data extraction

Data will be extracted using a standardised form developed in Covidence and piloted on five included studies. Extracted information will include study identification (authors, year, country, funding source and conflicts of interest); study design (economic evaluation type, trial-based or model-based, analytical perspective, time horizon and discount rates); population characteristics (sample size, age distribution, sex, fall history, comorbidities and functional status); intervention details (technology type, manufacturer, specifications, monitoring arrangements, implementation duration and adherence); comparator characteristics; and economic data (cost categories and sources, currency, price year, resource utilisation, effectiveness measures, Incremental Cost-Effectiveness Ratios (ICERs), Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs), sensitivity analysis results and probability of cost-effectiveness at relevant thresholds). Two reviewers will independently extract data, with discrepancies resolved through consensus. Study authors will be contacted for missing or unclear data, with a maximum of three contact attempts over 4 weeks. Intervention and comparator details will be documented using the Template for Intervention Description and Replication checklist to ensure comprehensive and consistent characterisation. Because Covidence is optimised for reviews of effectiveness, data items that its forms do not accommodate well, including economic quality appraisal and risk-of-bias assessment, will be managed in a bespoke Microsoft Excel workbook.

Quality assessment

Methodological quality will be assessed using validated instruments appropriate for economic evaluations. The Consolidated Health Economic Evaluation Reporting Standards (CHEERS) 2022 checklist will evaluate reporting completeness across its 28 items.24 The Drummond checklist will assess methodological quality across 10 domains including clear question formulation, comprehensive alternatives description, effectiveness evidence, appropriate identification of costs and consequences, accurate measurement and credible valuation, adjustment for differential timing, incremental analysis and uncertainty characterisation.22 For model-based evaluations, the Phillips checklist will additionally assess model structure justification, data sources, validation approaches and parameter uncertainty handling.25 Quality assessment will be performed independently by two reviewers, with results presented in tabular format and summarised narratively. Studies will not be excluded based on quality, but assessment results will inform evidence interpretation and Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) certainty ratings.

Risk of bias assessment

For economic evaluations conducted alongside randomised trials, risk of bias in the underlying clinical data will be assessed using the Cochrane RoB 2 tool across domains of randomisation process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement and selection of reported results.26 For model-based evaluations, assessment will focus on structural assumptions, evidence sources for model parameters, validation approaches and potential conflicts of interest, guided by the AdViSHE tool.27 Additional considerations include sponsorship bias from industry funding, publication bias, and selective outcome reporting. Risk of bias assessment will be conducted independently by two reviewers, with results presented using Cochrane-style traffic light plots and weighted summary (barometer) plots generated using the robvis visualisation tool where appropriate.

Data synthesis

Narrative synthesis will provide structured summaries organised by technology type, population characteristics and methodological approach, following the Synthesis Without Meta-analysis (SWiM) reporting guideline.28 Tabular presentations will display study characteristics, quality assessment results and cost-effectiveness findings in standardised formats. Costs will be converted to a common currency (reported in US dollars, using the most up-to-date price year for which conversion indices are available at the time of synthesis) using purchasing power parities and inflated to a common price year using country-specific healthcare cost indices. Quantitative meta-analysis of ICERs or net monetary benefit may be undertaken where at least three sufficiently homogeneous studies are identified, using random effects models with appropriate measures of uncertainty including confidence and prediction intervals. Heterogeneity will be quantified using I² statistics and explored through prespecified subgroup analyses examining technology type, study design, analytical perspective, time horizon, population risk profile and healthcare system context. Population risk profile will be operationalised from study-reported baseline fall risk (eg, prior fall history, fracture risk or frailty status), and healthcare system context will be classified by country, the analytical perspective adopted and the predominant financing model (eg, tax-funded, social insurance or private insurance) as reported in each study. Sensitivity analyses will examine robustness to inclusion of studies with high risk of bias and alternative approaches to currency conversion. Certainty of evidence will be assessed using GRADE adapted for economic evidence (defined GRADE Economic Evidence Framework).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design of this protocol. The research question and outcome measures were informed by published literature on fall-related healthcare burden and technology adoption barriers among older adults. We plan to disseminate the completed systematic review findings through open-access publication and will explore opportunities to present results in formats accessible to older adults and their caregivers through collaboration with patient advocacy organisations.

Ethics and dissemination

Ethical approval is not required for this systematic review, as it will analyse only previously published, aggregate data and will not involve primary data collection from, or direct contact with, human participants. Any amendments to this protocol will be documented in the PROSPERO record, with the date of, and reason for, each amendment. The completed review will be disseminated through peer-reviewed open-access publication and presentation at relevant scientific conferences. To support uptake by healthcare policymakers, health technology assessment bodies, clinicians and technology developers, key findings will additionally be summarised in plain-language formats developed, where feasible, in collaboration with patient and caregiver advocacy organisations.

Discussion

This protocol establishes the methodological framework for what will be the most comprehensive systematic review to date of cost-effectiveness evidence for home-based fall detection and monitoring technologies in community-dwelling older adults. The planned review addresses a critical evidence gap at the intersection of geriatric care, health technology assessment and healthcare economics that has become increasingly urgent as populations age globally and fall-related healthcare expenditure continues to escalate.5 8

The anticipated findings will have significant implications for multiple stakeholder groups. Healthcare policymakers and commissioners will gain access to synthesised evidence to inform resource allocation decisions regarding fall detection technologies within community care pathways. Given that falls represent one of the largest preventable sources of healthcare expenditure in older populations, with costs now exceeding US$80 billion annually in the USA alone, evidence regarding the cost-effectiveness of detection technologies could substantially influence healthcare budgets and service configurations. Health technology assessment bodies including NICE, CADTH and similar organisations will benefit from a comprehensive evidence base to support technology appraisals and clinical guideline development, as current guidance on fall prevention often lacks specific recommendations regarding detection technologies due to evidence gaps.

Clinicians and allied health professionals will be better positioned to advise patients and families about the value proposition of fall detection technologies within comprehensive fall prevention strategies. The World Guidelines for Falls Prevention and Management emphasise the importance of multifactorial interventions, yet the role of detection technologies within such programmes remains inadequately characterised from an economic perspective. The planned review will provide evidence to support shared decision-making between healthcare providers and older adults regarding technology adoption. Technology developers and manufacturers will gain understanding of the evidence requirements for demonstrating cost-effectiveness, informing future study design and value propositions that address the information needs of healthcare purchasers and reimbursement authorities.

Several methodological considerations and anticipated challenges warrant discussion at the protocol stage. First, the heterogeneity of fall detection technologies presents significant challenges for evidence synthesis. Technologies range from simple personal emergency response systems requiring user activation to sophisticated artificial intelligence platforms capable of predictive analytics and automatic detection.12 13 The planned review will address this through structured categorisation of technologies and prespecified subgroup analyses, though substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity may preclude quantitative meta-analysis for some comparisons.

Second, healthcare system context differences across countries may limit the transferability of cost-effectiveness findings. Costs, resource utilisation patterns, reimbursement mechanisms and willingness-to-pay thresholds vary considerably across health systems.22 Economic evaluations, which may be diverse or heterogenous, when conducted in one setting, may not directly translate to another, and the planned review will explicitly address transferability through stratified analysis by healthcare system type and geographical region, with clear documentation of the contexts in which included studies were conducted.

Third, the rapid pace of technological evolution means that some studies may evaluate technologies that have been superseded or significantly updated or obsoleted. A recent literature review noted that fall detection research has increased substantially over the past decade, with continuous improvements in sensor accuracy, algorithm sophistication and integration with healthcare systems.12 The planned review will carefully document the technological characteristics of evaluated interventions and discuss the applicability of findings to contemporary technologies, acknowledging that cost-effectiveness conclusions may require updating as technologies evolve.

Fourth, the quality and completeness of economic evaluation reporting in this field may constrain the depth of analysis possible. Previous assessments of economic evaluation reporting have identified substantial deficiencies in adherence to reporting guidelines (defined reporting quality assessment). The planned use of the CHEERS 2022 checklist will enable systematic documentation of reporting quality and identification of information gaps that may affect the reliability of synthesised findings.24 Additionally, publication bias may affect the evidence base if studies demonstrating unfavourable cost-effectiveness findings are less likely to be published or indexed in searched databases.

Despite these anticipated challenges, this protocol establishes a rigorous foundation for addressing the evidence gap. The comprehensive search strategy spanning multiple bibliographic and economic databases, supplemented by grey literature searching, will maximise the likelihood of identifying all relevant evidence. The use of Covidence software will ensure transparent and auditable study selection with complete documentation of screening decisions. Standardised data extraction with independent duplicate processes will minimise errors and bias in data capture. The application of validated quality assessment tools including CHEERS 2022 and the Drummond checklist will enable systematic evaluation of methodological rigour. Appropriate synthesis methods following SWiM guidance will integrate evidence while transparently acknowledging heterogeneity and uncertainty.

In summary, this protocol presents the methodological framework for a systematic review that will provide comprehensive, high-quality evidence regarding the cost-effectiveness of home-based fall detection and monitoring technologies for community-dwelling older adults. By addressing a critical knowledge gap at the intersection of geriatric care and health technology assessment, the planned review will inform healthcare policy decisions, support clinical practice guidelines, guide technology development priorities and ultimately contribute to better outcomes for older adults at risk of falls. The protocol ensures transparency through prospective registration and establishes the scientific rigour necessary to produce reliable findings that can meaningfully influence healthcare decision-making in this important and rapidly evolving field.
