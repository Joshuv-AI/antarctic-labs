Metabolic characteristics of poultry eggs from five species: insights from metabolomics, network analysis, and machine learning☆
Authors: Hongqiang Li, Xiangmin Zhao, Junyang Li, Jing Liang, Xiaoming Li, Fuwei Li, Defu Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPoultry eggs are recognized as a rich source of essential nutrients for humans. To optimize production, develop high-value-added products, and better understand interspecies differences, we investigated the metabolic profiles of chicken, duck, goose, quail, and pigeon eggs (yolk and albumen (egg white)). Using non-targeted metabolomics integrated with multivariate analyses and machine learning-based biomarker screening, we identified 519 and 312 significantly different metabolites in the yolk and white of eggs from the aforementioned species, respectively. These metabolites included lipids and lipid-like molecules, organoheterocyclic compounds, benzenoids, etc. The co-occurrence network showed that the egg yolks had higher modularity than the egg whites. ABC transporters and amino acid metabolism-related pathways were the main enriched pathways, reflecting species-adaptive strategies. Moreover, feature selection assisted by machine learning identified 9 candidate biomarkers with potential discriminatory value for poultry egg species classification, although further targeted validation using independent external cohorts is required. This study provides insights into the metabolomics profiles of poultry eggs and their industrial applications.Keywords: Poultry eggs, Metabolomics, Co-occurrence network, Machine learning, Candidate biomarkers

Introduction

Poultry eggs are rich in various nutrients such as proteins, lipids, vitamins, and minerals, making them a crucial component of the human diet. They play a significant role in the global food supply as well as human nutrition and health (Sun et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2022). Common poultry eggs are sourced from chicken, duck, goose, quail, and pigeon, and the species cover Anseriformes, Galliformes, and Columbiformes, which are taxonomically and industrially representative (Sun et al., 2017). The nutritional composition, physicochemical properties, and sensory characteristics of eggs vary significantly among species (Kumbar et al., 2021; Liu et al., 2018; Yang C et al., 2023), as a result of multiple biological and production-related factors, including genotype, breeder age, laying stage, rearing system, and dietary intervention. For instance, genotype and laying stage affect egg traits, chemical composition, fatty acid profiles, and hatchability in breeder geese (Aslan et al., 2026), while breeder age influences egg composition, yolk mineral deposition, embryo development, and hatchability in Pekin ducks (Onbasilar et al., 2014). In laying hens, cage system, hybrid line, and age also affect egg weight, shell characteristics, albumen (egg white) index, and Haugh unit (Onbasilar et al., 2018). In addition, dietary natural additives can alter eggshell quality and yolk oxidative stability (Deniz et al., 2025). These findings indicate that egg composition is species-dependent and shaped by multiple biological and production-related factors, highlighting the need for systematic approaches to characterize interspecies differences at the molecular level.

Previous research on poultry eggs has primarily focused on the quantitative analysis of nutritional components, the establishment of quality evaluation indicators, and the impact of processing techniques on egg quality (Tan et al., 2023; Yang C et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025). However, these studies have mainly targeted known components and therefore provide limited information on the global metabolic characteristics and underlying biochemical differences among eggs from different poultry species. Compared with conventional compositional analysis, metabolomics provides a comprehensive and dynamic approach for characterizing global metabolite variations in biological systems, thereby offering a powerful tool for elucidating physiological status, metabolic regulation, and biochemical differences among biological samples (Zhang et al., 2023). In recent years, metabolomics has been widely applied in the field of food science (Kefale et al., 2023; Li H et al., 2025a). It can also be employed in egg research to comprehensively analyze metabolite profiles and characteristic metabolites of eggs from different sources (such as processing methods, feed types, and varieties), as well as to identify the potential metabolic pathways and regulatory mechanisms involved (Goto et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2023). Furthermore, comparative metabolomic analysis of eggs from different sources can help screen for specific biomarkers. These biomarkers can serve as molecular tags to distinguish different eggs and provide a scientific basis for quality control, traceability, and breed identification of eggs (Tian et al., 2024; Yang W et al., 2023; Zhang R et al., 2025).

Although metabolomics has recently been applied to egg research, current studies remain limited in three aspects. First, most studies have compared only a few egg types or focused on single-species differences caused by diet, breed, or processing rather than systematically comparing multiple common poultry species (Liu et al., 2018; Wu et al., 2023; Zhang R et al., 2025). Second, many studies have focused on specific metabolites, such as volatile compounds, fatty acids, or amino acids (Dermane et al., 2024; Yang C et al., 2023; Yang et al., 2025), and have failed to elucidate the complexity of the metabolome in egg yolk and egg white at the metabolic pathways and network level. Third, metabolomic datasets are typically characterized by high dimensionality, complex correlations, and nonlinear relationships among metabolites, which makes it difficult to identify robust biomarkers using conventional univariate statistical methods (Liebal et al., 2020; Santana et al., 2024). In this context, machine learning (ML) provides an effective data-driven strategy for biomarker screening because it can handle high-dimensional datasets, evaluate the relative importance of multiple variables, capture complex interactions among metabolites, and reduce redundant features (Hu et al., 2023; Qiu et al., 2024). By integrating metabolomics with ML, it is therefore possible to improve the efficiency, accuracy, and robustness of biomarker selection and to identify discriminative metabolite panels for egg species classification. Taken together, these limitations highlight the need for a systematic metabolomic comparison of egg yolk and egg white from different poultry species, combined with ML-based feature selection, to better understand interspecies metabolic characteristics and identify candidate biomarkers for egg species discrimination.

To address these gaps, the present study performed untargeted metabolomics using LC-MS/MS to profile whole eggs (yolk and white) from five common poultry species (chicken, duck, goose, quail, and pigeon). The objectives were to systematically characterize interspecies metabolomic differences among poultry eggs, compare the metabolic characteristics of egg yolk and white at the metabolic pathways and network level, and identify candidate species-discriminative biomarkers using multiple ML algorithms. The findings of this study will provide a new theoretical basis and technical support for the advancement of the egg industry, the improvement of egg quality, and the development of high-value-added products.

Materials and methods

Preparation of samples

As illustrated in our previous study (Li H et al., 2025b), fresh chicken, duck, goose, pigeon, and quail eggs were collected from a poultry farm in Jiangsu Province, China, within 24 h after being laid (Fig. 1A). Detailed information about the egg samples is shown in Table S1. Eggs with atypical shapes, deformed or broken shells, or visible biological defects were discarded based on the previous study (Kumbar et al., 2021). From the remaining high-quality samples, 48 egg samples from each species were randomly divided into 8 groups (n = 6 eggs/group). Then, the egg yolks were separated from the egg whites, ensuring sufficient mixing of egg yolk/white within each group while maintaining separation between groups. The specific preparation method of the egg samples was referred to previous studies (Kumbar et al., 2021; Yang C et al., 2023). Subsequently, the collected yolks/whites were placed in lyophilization tubes and stored at −80°C for further analysis. The egg yolks of chicken, duck, goose, pigeon, and quail were referred to as EY, DEY, GEY, PEY, and QEY, respectively, whereas the egg whites were called EW, DEW, GEW, PEW, and QEW, respectively.

The geographical origin and species information of the egg samples (A); The proportion of metabolites identified in egg yolk (B) and egg white (C) chemical classification.

Sample extraction

The egg yolk/white samples were treated as described by Yang W et al. (2023). Briefly, 100 mg (± 1%) of samples were mixed with beads and extraction solution [500 μL; MeOH: ACN: H2O; 2:2:1 (v/v)]. The extraction solution contains deuterated internal standards. The solution was then vortexed for 30 s, homogenized (35 Hz,4 min; JXFSTPRP-24, Shanghai Jingxin Technology Co., Ltd.), and sonicated for 5 min in a 4°C water bath thrice. Then the samples were incubated for 1 h at −40°C to precipitate proteins and centrifuged at 12000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C to collect supernatant, which was transferred to a fresh glass vial for analysis. The quality control (QC) sample was prepared by mixing an equal aliquot of the sample’s supernatant.

LC-MS/MS analysis

This analysis was performed via a UHPLC system (Vanquish, Thermo Fisher Scientific) with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 μm) coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer (Orbitrap MS, Thermo). The mobile phase comprised (A) ammonium acetate (25 mmol/L) + ammonia hydroxide in water (pH = 9.75; 25 mmol/L) and (B) acetonitrile. The auto-sampler temperature was 4°C, and the injection volume was 2 μL. To acquire MS/MS spectra on information-dependent acquisition (IDA) mode, the Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer was used with acquisition software (Xcalibur, Thermo). In this mode, the acquisition software continuously evaluates the full scan MS spectrum. The ESI source conditions were set as follows: sheath gas flow rate = 50 Arb, Aux gas flow rate = 15 Arb, capillary temperature = 320°C, full MS resolution = 60000, MS/MS resolution = 15000, collision energy = SNCE 20/30/40, spray voltage = 3.8 kV (positive) or −3.4 kV (negative).

Data preprocessing and annotation

The raw LC-MS/MS data were converted to the mzXML format using the ProteoWizard (version 3.0.6150) (Li H et al., 2025a). The data were processed using the “xcms” package (version 3.8.2) in the R platform, including peak detection, extraction, alignment, and integration. In the QC samples, peaks with relative standard deviations of > 30% were removed. For metabolite identification, the cutoff value was set as 0.3, based on an in-house database built using available commercial standard compounds and existing public databases including HMDB (https://hmdb.ca), MoNA (https://mona.fiehnlab.ucdavis.edu/), and METLIN (https://metlin.scripps.edu). The missing value was imputed by half the minimal peak value in the metabolomics dataset (Li H et al., 2025a).

Construction of Ml models and screening of candidate biomarkers

Data preprocessing was performed using the Python scikit-learn library. Briefly, features with >50% missing values were excluded, and remaining missing values were imputed using the K-nearest neighbors (KNN) algorithm to ensure data completeness. All features were standardized using z-score to enable comparability, thus improving the stability and efficiency of model training.

ML algorithms were implemented for model training using the scikit-learn and XGBoost libraries. In total, 5 algorithms were employed, including Gaussian naive Bayes (GNB), logistic regression (LR), random forest (RF), support vector machine (SVM), and XGBoost. For each model, 100 training iterations were performed, and the dataset was split into 80% training and 20% testing sets.

Recursive Feature Elimination with Cross-Validation (RFECV) was employed to identify optimal biomarkers, using 5-fold cross-validation with a step size of 1. Initially, a model was trained on all candidate features. Then, the feature importance was calculated, and those with lower importance were iteratively eliminated to prioritize those most predictive of the target variable. Cross-validation in each RFECV iteration ensured robust and generalizable feature selection, minimizing overfitting while maximizing biological relevance. Following feature selection, models were retrained using the identified candidate biomarkers to validate their predictive performance and reliability on independent data.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were performed on eight biological replicates. Principal component analysis (PCA) and partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) were performed using the OmicStudio tools at https://www.omicstudio.cn/tool. The "vegan" package in the R platform was employed for variance partitioning analysis (VPA). Spearman correlation analysis was conducted for the data of each group, and the edges with r !≥ 0.75 & p ≤ 0.001 were selected as effective connections. Furthermore, the “igraph” package of the R platform was used for calculating the network parameters, whereas the co-occurrence network graph was drawn, and modules were authenticated using the “Louvain” method.

Differential metabolites were evaluated based on the variable influence in projection (VIP) values obtained from the PLS-DA model, as well as the p-value from the one-way ANOVA with multiple testing correction implemented via the false discovery rate (FDR). Metabolites with VIP > 1 and p < 0.05 were considered significantly different. Moreover, GraphPad Prism software (version 9.5.0) was employed to display the differential metabolites in a bar chart. The heatmap analysis was performed using the heatmap R package to represent the differences in the metabolite content among the egg yolk/white samples. Furthermore, the Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG, www.genome.jp/kegg) pathway database was employed to identify the metabolic pathways related to the compounds.

Results and discussion

Metabolic phenotype analyses

The metabolic characteristics of non-volatile metabolites in different poultry eggs were identified using the non-targeted UHPLC-MS/MS technology. In total, 930 metabolites were identified from different species of egg yolks in both positive ion (564 metabolites) and negative ion modes (366 metabolites) (Fig. 1B). These metabolites can be classified into 18 categories, with most metabolites being lipids and lipid-like molecules, followed by organoheterocyclic compounds and organic acids and derivatives. For egg whites, 633 metabolites were identified in positive (414 metabolites) and negative ion modes (219 metabolites), which were then classified into 18 types based on their properties (Fig. 1C). Among these, lipids and lipid-like molecules were the highest number across all species, followed by organoheterocyclic compounds and benzenoids.

The relative content of metabolites in poultry eggs of different species varied considerably between species (Fig. 2). The lipids and lipid-like molecules and amino acids and peptides had higher relative contents in the egg yolks of all samples, followed by organoheterocyclic compounds and fatty acids (Fig. 2A). Particularly, the number of amino acids and peptides (46 metabolites) was lower in egg yolk samples, but their relative content was second only to lipids and lipid-like molecules. Except for fatty acids and amino acids and peptides, the sum of intensities of all metabolites in the other major class showed significant differences (p < 0.05) between species (Fig. 2C). For egg whites, fatty acids had the highest relative content in all samples, followed by organoheterocyclic compounds and carbohydrates (Fig. 2B). Interestingly, fatty acids (42 metabolites) were found in lower quantities in the egg white samples, but had the highest relative content. In addition, the relative content of metabolites varied more in egg whites of different species than in yolks. Except for organoheterocyclic compounds and organic nitrogen compounds, the sum of the intensities of all metabolites within the other major class revealed significant variations (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2D).

Stacked bar chart of the relative contents of different metabolite classes in egg yolk (A) and egg white (B) samples; Relative contents of the major metabolite classes in egg yolk (C) and egg white (D) samples.

For specific assessment of metabolite variability between and within groups, multivariate data analyses were carried out (Fig. 3). PCA can identify the similarities and differences between metabolites in the sample and has been used to study the metabolic characteristics of different poultry eggs (Wu et al., 2023; Zhang R et al., 2025). The PCA results of metabolites in the eggs of five species revealed that different egg yolks and whites had species-dominated distribution characteristics (Fig. 3A). Moreover, compared with egg whites, the metabolic characteristics of different egg yolks were more similar. Further, the VPA indicated that the association of poultry species with the differences in egg metabolomics is much greater than that of egg white/yolk types (Fig. 3B), indicating that the egg metabolomic characteristics are mainly driven by the specificity of poultry species. This might be related to the differences in genetic background and metabolic pathways (such as lipid synthesis and amino acid metabolism) among different species (Rehault-Godbert et al., 2019; Zhang H et al., 2025).

Multivariate statistical analysis of identified metabolites. PCA score plots of egg samples (A); VPA between egg species and egg types (B); PCA score plots of egg yolk (C) and egg white (F) samples; The PLS-DA score of egg yolk (D) and egg white (G) samples; Hypothesis testing of the PLS-DA model of egg yolk (E) and egg white (H) samples.

Based on the above results, PCA of the metabolites in different egg yolks and whites was performed, respectively. The results revealed that the QC samples formed a distinct cluster, indicating that the analytical equipment had good repeatability and stability (Fig. 3C and F). Furthermore, PLS-DA of the identified metabolites revealed that the yolk and white of the five species eggs were located in different regions of the model and did not intersect, validating that the PLS-DA model could correctly distinguish the five types of poultry eggs (Fig. 3D and G). Cross-validation indicated that the model did not overfit (all blue Q2 points were lower than the original red R2 point from left to right, and the intercept of the Q2 regression line was < 0) (Fig. 3E and H). Overall, whether the egg yolk or the white, the metabolic characteristics of quail and pigeon eggs were similar, and those of the chicken, goose, and duck eggs matched each other, which is related to their phylogenetic proximity (Rehault-Godbert et al., 2019; Sun et al., 2017). This result also supports that the characteristics of egg metabolomics are mainly driven by the specificity of poultry species (Fig. 3B).

Metabolite network co-occurrence

A co-occurrence network correlation was calculated for the sample metabolite profiles (Fig. 4). Since these data are not compositional, a network was established using significantly positive/negative Spearman correlations to evaluate the metabolic interactions and dependencies of egg yolk and white metabolites (Lax et al., 2019). The co-occurrence network of the yolk metabolites was dominated by modules 1, 2, 3, and 4 (Fig. 4A, modularity = 0.67). Module 1 contained a broader range of metabolites, including lipids and lipid-like molecules, organoheterocyclic compounds, as well as organic acids and derivatives. Glycerophosphocholines (GPCs), exemplified by PC(18:1(9Z)/18:1(9Z)), PC(18:1(6Z)/18:1(6Z)), and PC(18:1(9E)/18:1(9E)), emerged as central nodes in Module 1, functioning not only as energy reservoirs and membrane structural components but also as antioxidants and lipid homeostasis regulators (Olgun et al., 2022). Module 2 primarily comprised lipids and lipid-like molecules, along with the organoheterocyclic compounds. Key lipid metabolism intermediates, such as lysophosphatidylcholine(22:5) (LPC(22:5)) and fulvestrant 9-sulfone, exhibited elevated connectivity within Module 2, underscoring their regulatory roles. Metabolites of Module 3 were mainly distributed in organoheterocyclic compounds as well as organic acids and derivatives, while those of Module 4 mainly included lipids and lipid-like molecules. Pantothenic acid, a pivotal node in Module 3, acted as a metabolic hub in egg yolk by facilitating efficient nutrient utilization (e.g., lipids, proteins, and carbohydrates) through coenzyme A-mediated reactions (Wang Y et al., 2023). These biochemical pathways are critical for poultry embryo development.

Metabolite co-occurrence network of egg yolk (A) and egg white (B) samples.

For egg whites, the co-occurrence network was dominated by modules 1, 2, 3, 4, and 13 (Fig. 4B, modularity = 0.37). Modules 1 and 4 primarily comprised lipids and lipid-like molecules, and Modules 3 and 13 mainly consisted of organoheterocyclic compounds as well as lipids and lipid-like molecules. Module 4 exhibited the highest connectivity, containing 94 nodes with an average degree >110, suggesting it serves as the central hub of the metabolic network. Phospholipid (20:3(5Z,8Z,11Z)/18:2(9Z,12Z)) (Pl (20:3(5Z,8Z,11Z)/18:2(9Z,12Z))), a key node in Module 4, plays a critical role in membrane dynamic balance (Bruns et al., 2002). Module 2 was predominantly composed of fatty acids and organoheterocyclic compounds, with lactosylceramide(d18:1/16:0) (LacCer (d18:1/16:0)) emerging as a pivotal regulator of antimicrobial and immunometabolic functions (Salihovic et al., 2024). The results showed that the modularity of egg yolks was higher than that of egg whites, which was also confirmed in the subsequent co-occurrence networks of different egg yolks/egg whites (Fig. S1 and S2). The high egg yolk metabolic network modularity indicated that it has highly organized functional divisions, highlighting its resilience to metabolic disturbances. The egg white metabolic network had a relatively low modularity, which may be related to its functional diversity (such as antimicrobial, antioxidant, anti-cancer, and immunomodulatory activities) (Giansanti et al., 2015; Rehault-Godbert et al., 2019). For more decentralized network connections, its metabolites should be widely involved in dynamic interactions.

The co-occurrence networks of different egg yolk/white metabolites were further constructed (Fig. S1 and S2, Table S2). For egg yolk, EY had the most nodes and edges, the highest network density, the shortest mean distance, and the highest average number of neighbors; however, the modularity and clustering coefficients were the lowest (Fig. S1A, Table S2). For QEY, the number of edges, the average number of neighbors, and the network density were the lowest, and the mean distance was the longest, but the clustering coefficient and modularity were the highest (Fig. S1B, Table S2). The PEY, GEY, and DEY network attribute parameters were between EY and QEY (Fig. S1C, D, and E, Table S2). Overall, EY indicated the most complex and connected metabolic network, with frequent interactions between metabolites and high metabolic conversion efficiency. Low modularity suggests that the metabolic network is more inclined to global integration rather than local modular functions. The QEY’s metabolic network was sparse, with fewer connections between metabolites; however, a clear modular structure (modularity = 0.88) was observed. This reflects QEY’s metabolic specificity, indicating that its metabolic activity may be concentrated in some specific functional modules rather than global integration. Whereas the metabolic networks of PEY, GEY, and DEY showed a certain balance.

For egg whites, PEW indicated the shortest mean distance, the highest number of edges, network density, and average number of neighbors, but less modularity (Fig. S2A, Table S2). These data indicate that the PEW metabolic network promotes global integration rather than modular functionality. The QEW had the highest modularity, indicating a clear modular structure in its metabolic network, which may reflect its metabolic specificity (Fig. S2B, Table S2). Furthermore, EW had the highest clustering coefficient, highlighting the presence of more local clusters in its metabolic network, which might be associated with the high coordination of metabolism in egg whites (Fig. S2C, Table S2). The GEW also showed a high clustering coefficient; however, its mean distance was long, indicating moderate connectivity of its metabolic network (Fig. S2D, Table S2). The DEW had the least number of edges, the lowest network density, the longest mean distance, and the lowest average number of neighbors (Fig. S2E, Table S2). This indicates a sparse metabolic network with fewer connections between metabolites and poor connectivity, suggesting that DEW’s metabolic activity may be simpler or focused on specific functions.

Differential abundance analyses

To investigate the differences in metabolite abundance in poultry egg samples, one-way ANOVA (p < 0.05) and PLS-DA (VIP > 1) were carried out to filter out the metabolites derived from the raw data. The positive and negative ion modes screened 519 differential metabolites from 930 egg yolk metabolites (Fig. 5A, Table S3). The main differential metabolites in egg yolks included lipids and lipid-like molecules, organoheterocyclic compounds, as well as organic acids and derivatives. Of 633 metabolites in egg white, 312 were selected for metabolomics in both positive and negative ion modes (Fig. 5A, Table S4). These metabolites were then classified as lipids and lipid-like molecules, organoheterocyclic compounds, benzenoids, organic acids and derivatives, as well as organic nitrogen compounds, etc.

The abundance analysis of differential metabolites. Classification of differential metabolites (A); Heatmap of hierarchical clustering analysis of egg yolk (B) and egg white (C) samples.

Subsequently, the different metabolites of yolk and white of different poultry eggs were analyzed by cluster analysis. The overall expression patterns between different groups were visually observed (Fig. 5B and C), which revealed that each species can express the same metabolites in similar values, and the repeatability of each sample is good. This validated that the data is reliable and logical. The metabolites in the same cluster have similar expression patterns and may have similar functions or participate in the same metabolic process or cell pathway together. In the egg yolks, the differential metabolites of DEY and GEY showed more obvious differences. The differential metabolites of QEY, PEY, and EY showed a relatively obvious state (Fig. 5B). In the egg whites, the differential metabolites of PEW and QEW showed an extremely obvious state, while those of EW and GEW showed a relatively obvious state (Fig. 5C).

Metabolic pathway analysis

The pathway annotation and enrichment analysis of the metabolites with significant differences were performed to assess the signal transduction pathways and the metabolite accumulation characteristics of poultry eggs of different species. The KEGG pathway analysis identified 35 significantly enriched metabolic pathways in egg yolks (p < 0.05), including amino acid metabolism (6), digestive system (5), lipid metabolism (4), carbohydrate metabolism (3), global and overview maps (3), and metabolism of other amino acids (3), etc. The top 20 of these metabolic pathways involved in the significantly different metabolite profiles are shown in Fig. 6A and include ABC transporters (ko02010), histidine metabolism (ko00340), protein digestion and absorption (ko04974), C5-branched dibasic acid metabolism (ko00660), and arginine biosynthesis (ko00220), etc. For egg white, 36 significantly enriched metabolic pathways were determined (p < 0.05), including amino acid metabolism (6), digestive system (4), global and overview maps (4), and metabolism of cofactors and vitamins (3), etc. Fig. 6B shows the top 20 pathways with active involvement of significantly differential metabolites, such as ABC transporters (ko02010); pyrimidine metabolism (ko00240); protein digestion and absorption (ko04974); and valine, leucine, and isoleucine biosynthesis (ko00290), etc. These data provide novel annotation information for understanding the biological functions of egg yolks and whites from different poultry species. Subsequently, interaction networks were established to illustrate the molecular relationships between the differential metabolites in egg yolk and white, as well as their significantly enriched associated terms (Fig. 6C and D).

KEGG pathway enrichment plot of differential metabolites of egg yolk (A) and egg white (B) samples; Interaction networks of differentially regulated metabolites with significantly enriched terms (p < 0.05) of egg yolk (C) and egg white (D) samples.

KEGG metabolic pathway analysis revealed that differential metabolites in the egg yolk and white were significantly enriched in metabolic pathways, including membrane transport, amino acid metabolism, digestive system, and translation. This was close to the previous research results (Wu et al., 2023). Furthermore, each key pathway was mostly shared by more than one metabolite. ABC transporters, as a type of transmembrane transporter proteins widely present in organisms, play a significant role in material transport, metabolic regulation, and drug resistance (Thomas and Tampe, 2020). Here, it was observed that ABC transporters had the highest number of differential metabolites enriched in egg yolks and egg whites, with the most significant value. This observation aligns with species-specific physiological adaptations in poultry. As altricial birds, pigeons require parental feeding after hatching. This necessitates the egg white functioning not only as a nutrient reservoir, but also as a continuous delivery system for small molecules (e.g., nucleosides and amino acids) to the embryos via ABC transporters, thereby supporting prolonged developmental demands (Sun et al., 2019; Zhang R et al., 2025). In contrast, precocial birds such as chickens, ducks, geese, and quails exhibit rapid embryonic development. ABC transporters in their yolk may more efficiently transport stored lipids and protein precursors to the embryos, meeting the demand for short-term rapid growth (Sun et al., 2017). Notably, although quails are precocial birds, they have a short reproductive cycle and high embryonic metabolic rate. The co-enrichment of ABC transporters in both egg white and yolk in this study reflects the need for rapid transport of nutrients, ensuring embryos complete development over a short timeframe. Additionally, previous studies have shown that ABC transporters regulate the transport and distribution of nutrients in egg yolk and white, affecting egg production traits and shell color (Wang et al., 2024). Therefore, the high number of differential metabolites enriched in ABC transporters in our study may be closely related to the species-specific physiological needs and reproductive capacity of poultry.

Amino acid metabolic pathways were most enriched in both egg yolks and egg whites. This indicates the differences in physiological functions and metabolism among different poultry eggs and provides a basis to evaluate how these differences affect their flavor characteristics. Previous studies have demonstrated that amino acid metabolic pathways are closely related to the growth, reproduction, and immunity of poultry (Li et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2024). For example, the arginine and proline metabolic pathway, which was significantly enriched in egg white in our study, has been reported to regulate egg weight (Yan et al., 2023). Amino acid metabolism in quail may be closely related to the synthesis and regulation of reproductive hormones, given their high embryonic metabolic rate and short reproductive cycle (Lan et al., 2021). Rapidly growing chickens likely exhibit heightened amino acid metabolic activity to satisfy protein synthesis demands for somatic development. Furthermore, amino acid metabolism plays a critical role in yolk lipoprotein synthesis. Given the high yolk percentage in goose eggs, the observed enrichment of amino acid pathways in this species may reflect enhanced protein storage capacity, providing essential nutrients for embryonic development of precocial birds (Li W et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2017).

Besides, amino acids have been identified to regulate the formation of egg flavors (Yang W et al., 2023), have various nutritional and physiological functions, and influence food taste. Amino acids are precursors that create flavor compounds via chemical processes like the Maillard reaction, lipid oxidation, and Strecker degradation (Li et al., 2023). Quail eggs have higher nucleotide and amino acid contents, due to which the flavor of their eggs is better than that of chicken and duck (Wu et al., 2023). Yang W et al. (2023) identified candidate biomarkers for the dietary quality of eggs laid by indigenous chicken and high-yield hens through metabolomics. They revealed that different metabolites in the egg yolks of both breeds were significantly enriched in two amino acid metabolism-related pathways, which were associated with the formation of egg flavor. In addition, the aminoacyl-tRNA biosynthesis pathway is crucial for reacting to danger signals and regulating immunity against viral infections (Lee et al., 2018). The protein digestion and absorption pathway has been found to regulate the hydrolysis and transport of proteins, peptides, and amino acids (Yi et al., 2022).

Screening candidate biomarkers of different poultry eggs through Ml

Recently, ML has gained prominence for large-scale multi-omics data analysis, enabling the identification of microbial or metabolomics signatures relevant to food science, diseases, and environmental conditions (Asnicar et al., 2024; Hu et al., 2023). The integration of multiple ML algorithms [e.g., SVM, RF, LR, and classification and regression tree (CART)] can help researchers identify reliable biomarkers from complex biological datasets (Qiu et al., 2024). Therefore, in this study, the ML model was employed to elucidate the differences between the metabolites in eggs from different poultry species. After preprocessing egg yolk and white metabolomics data, five ML models (GNB, LR, RF, SVM, and XGBoost) were constructed. The prediction accuracy of each ML model is presented in Fig. 7A. Among these five models, the accuracy of LR (yolk: 0.896, white: 0.885) and RF (yolk: 0.944, white: 0.904) was the highest, while the values of other ML models were less than 0.85. Therefore, LR and RF were selected as reference models for subsequent marker screening. Using RFECV, feature selection was performed on metabolomics data of egg yolk and white based on LR and RF models. The models were then reconstructed using the selected features before evaluating their prediction accuracy (Fig. 7B). The models’ accuracies were improved after feature selection, indicating that the selected features effectively represented the metabolic profiles of the different egg yolks and whites. Based on the above analyses, LR identified 10 egg yolk and 9 egg white features, while RF screened 7 egg yolk and 153 egg white features (Table S5).

Screening of candidate biomarkers using ML. Model accuracy before RFECV (A); Model accuracy after RFECV (B); Venn diagram of candidate biomarkers of egg yolk (C) and egg white (D) samples; Heatmap of hierarchical clustering analysis of 9 candidate biomarkers (E).

To minimize false positives, the features identified by both models were intersected (Fig. 7C and D), which yielded 3 metabolites in egg yolk, including 1,3-Dimethyl-6-(propylamino)-2,4(1H,3H)-pyrimidinedione, 1-[4-Nitro-2-(trifluoromethyl)phenyl]piperazine, and benserazide. Furthermore, 6 metabolites were identified in egg white, including cytidine, wedelolactone, lysophosphatidylcholine(20:4(5Z,8Z,11Z,14Z)) (lysoPC(20:4(5Z,8Z,11Z,14Z))), sphingomyelin(d18:1/22:0) (SM(d18:1/22:0)), 2-thiophenecarboxamide, 3-[(aminocarbonyl)amino]−5-[4-(4-morpholinylmethyl)phenyl], and 2-methylguanosine. These 9 features were identified as significantly differential metabolites in differential abundance analysis (Fig. 5). This overlap highlights the accuracy and efficiency of ML-based screening, which has higher predictive power and lower error rates (Hu et al., 2023). Moreover, the distribution patterns of these screened features across different poultry eggs were evaluated, revealing clear discrimination among samples (Fig. 7E) and significant differences across poultry egg types (p < 0.05; Fig. S3). Although none of the 9 features is exclusive to a single species, their relative enrichment patterns support species-oriented classification rather than serving as universal markers. Consistent with this view, previous metabolomics studies have reported that pigeon eggs exhibit higher abundances of specific lipids, nucleosides, and nucleotides rather than the presence of unique compounds (Zhang R et al., 2025). Similarly, biomarkers used to distinguish duck egg weight are defined mainly by differences in arginine and proline concentrations (Yan et al., 2023). However, these features should be interpreted as candidate biomarkers rather than validated biomarkers. First, the current study was based on semi-quantitative untargeted metabolomics. Second, several features were annotated at MSI level 2 and were not confirmed with authentic standards. Third, the classification models were evaluated using repeated held-out test sets from the same experimental dataset, and no truly independent external validation cohort was available. Therefore, these candidate biomarkers provide a useful starting point for egg species authentication but require targeted LC-MS/MS validation and testing across independent farms, regions, breeds, diets, and production systems.

Among the candidate biomarkers, cytidine and 2-methylguanosine were associated with nucleoside metabolism and may reflect species-related differences in egg white small-molecule profiles (Leng et al., 2023; Wang C et al., 2023). However, their interpretation should be restricted to their observed abundance patterns and pathway context. Features putatively annotated as benserazide, wedelolactone, and SM(d18:1/22:0) were treated with particular caution because they were assigned at MSI level 2. Without confirmation using authentic standards, these annotations may represent isomers, structurally related compounds, or database-matching artefacts (Blazenovic et al., 2018; Contrepois et al., 2020). Therefore, no pharmacological or physiological conclusions were drawn from these candidate biomarkers. Future targeted analyses are required to verify their identities and determine whether they are true egg-derived metabolites.

Overall, compared with previous metabolomics studies on poultry eggs, this work offers a more detailed characterization of inter-species differences by integrating untargeted metabolomics with multivariate statistical analyses and ML–based feature selection to identify candidate biomarkers of egg type (Sun et al., 2017; Wu et al., 2023). Particularly, all eggs analyzed in this study were collected from a single geographic region (Jiangsu Province, China) under broadly comparable semi-intensive production conditions, with efforts made to standardize housing, lighting, feeding, and sampling at species-specific peak laying periods. While this design reduces major environmental heterogeneity, it does not eliminate the influence of non-genetic factors. Various studies have demonstrated that feed composition, production system (e.g., cage, barn, or free-range), age, and broader environmental conditions can substantially alter egg metabolite and nutrient profiles, including amino acids, lipids, fatty acids, and volatile compounds (Adams et al., 2025; Goto et al., 2019; Gyawali and Goto, 2025; Rodríguez-Hernández et al., 2024; Wu et al., 2023). For instance, interactions between breed and diet influence egg yolk and egg white metabolites such as fatty acids and amino acids (Goto et al., 2019; Gyawali and Goto, 2025), rearing systems and pasture access affect yolk fatty acid composition and carotenoid content (Rodríguez-Hernández et al., 2024), and regional variation in forage, soil, and climate leads to geographically distinct nutrient densities even within similar pasture-based systems (Adams et al., 2025). Moreover, genetic background within a species (breed or strain) also shapes metabolite and flavour profiles (Goto et al., 2019; Yang W et al., 2023). Therefore, independent validation of the identified candidate biomarkers in eggs from different housing conditions, and across diverse climates, diets, and breeds, will be essential to establish broadly applicable biomarker panels and to disentangle genetic from environmental contributions more rigorously.

In particular, the ML models developed in this study were based on a high-dimensional metabolomics dataset with a relatively limited sample size, a setting that inherently increases the risk of overfitting and optimistic performance estimates, as widely recognized in omics-based ML applications (Labory et al., 2024; Liebal et al., 2020; Vabalas et al., 2019). Although we used 5-fold cross-validation within RFECV and repeated random train–test splits to mitigate this risk and to reduce the feature space, RFECV cannot fully remove feature-selection bias, and model performance may still be overestimated (Grissa et al., 2016; Labory et al., 2024; Vabalas et al., 2019). Furthermore, no independent external validation cohort was available, so the generalizability of both the classifiers and the selected biomarkers beyond this dataset remains uncertain, a critical step recommended for ML-based metabolomics diagnostics (Liebal et al., 2020; Santana et al., 2024). Finally, our semi-quantitative untargeted data and reliance on a single imputation and scaling strategy may influence model stability, and alternative preprocessing or validation schemes (e.g., nested cross-validation, external cohorts) could refine and challenge the robustness of the proposed biomarker panel in future work (Galal et al., 2022; Su et al., 2022).

Conclusions

This study established a foundational metabolic atlas for poultry eggs from five different species (chicken, duck, goose, quail, and pigeon) and revealed substantial species-specific differences in the yolk and white metabolomes. Furthermore, the enrichment of ABC transporters and amino acid metabolism-related pathways indicates the biological mechanisms driving nutrient allocation and species adaptation. Co-occurrence network analysis revealed that yolks had higher modularity, suggesting more complex metabolic interactions than egg whites. Moreover, this investigation synergized metabolomics with ML to bridge the gaps in multi-species egg analysis. The identification of 9 candidate biomarkers (3 yolk, 6 white) provided a molecular basis for distinguishing eggs at the species level and offered a reference for the authenticity of poultry eggs. However, although five common types of poultry eggs were studied in this research, the sample size was relatively limited, and the differences in the metabolic characteristics of poultry eggs under different regions and different breeding conditions were not comprehensively analyzed. Therefore, samples of poultry eggs from different sources should be increased for an in-depth assessment of the diversity and complexity of poultry egg metabolism.
