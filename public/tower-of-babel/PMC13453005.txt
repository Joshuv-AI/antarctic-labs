Delayed access to COVID-19 vaccination among migrants in Spain during 2021: an electronic health records-based study
Authors: Jhosy Canedo, Sandra Campos, Sophie Campman, Carmen Olmedo, Olivier Núñez, Susana Monge
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn Spain, COVID-19 vaccination started on 27 December 2020, prioritizing people in long-term care facilities (LTCF), the elderly, and healthcare and essential workers. Different barriers to vaccination might have decreased or delayed uptake for migrant populations. We assessed the time to first COVID-19 vaccination by country of birth.MethodsWe studied ≥12-year-olds registered within the Spanish healthcare administrative database between 27 December 2020 and 31 December 2021, not living in LTCF, with information on country of birth, and at least one COVID-19 vaccine dose. Using Weibull parametric survival models, we compared time to first vaccination between Spanish-born individuals and those born in: Western countries (WC), Eastern Europe (EE), North Africa and the Middle East (NAME), Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), South-East Asia (SEA), Central America and the Caribbean (CAC), or South America (SA); adjusting for sex, age, priority vaccination group and region.ResultsWe included 26,720,450 individuals (Spanish-born: 86.1%; WC: 3.5%; EE: 0.4%; SSA: 0.6%; NAME: 2.1%; SEA: 0.6%; CAC: 1.4%; SA: 5.3%). Hazard Ratio for first vaccination was lower for all migrants’ groups, WC: 0.68, EE: 0.45, SSA: 0.49, NAME: 0.56, SEA: 0.64, CAC: 0.65 and SA: 0.74, and median time to vaccination (days) was higher, WC: 164, EE: 185, SSA: 181, NAME: 174, SEA: 167, CAC: 166 and SA: 160, compared to 147 in Spanish-born.ConclusionsMigrants of any origin living in Spain accessed COVID-19 vaccination later than Spanish-born individuals. These findings highlight existing barriers increasing migrants’ vulnerability during the pandemic and call for additional efforts to ensure equitable access.Keywords: COVID-19, Vaccination, Migrant population, Health inequalities, Access to healthcare

Introduction

Migrant populations are often underserved for healthcare and preventive interventions, posing them at an increased risk of vaccine-preventable diseases, among other adverse health events (Halliday et al., 2025). Increasing evidence suggests that migrant populations in Europe may experience barriers to vaccination (Crawshaw et al., 2022; Cacciani et al., 2023; Grupo de Trabajo de la Ponencia de Programa y Registro de Vacunaciones, 2026). An evidence-informed framework (Crawshaw et al., 2022) has been used to define and categorize the determinants of such barriers, including access, awareness, affordability (both direct and indirect costs, rigid scheduling), acceptance and activation. Access barriers play a major role, and include language and literacy limitations, communication challenges, digital exclusion, fear of charges, fear of immigration status checks, and service-related issues such as missing guidelines and limited knowledge among health-care providers around catch-up vaccination among this population (Grupo de Trabajo de la Ponencia de Programa y Registro de Vacunaciones, 2026). Acceptance barriers are also relevant, including concerns about vaccine safety and side-effects, cultural, religious and social factors, low perceived risk of disease, lack of proactive physician recommendation, and feelings of distrust, alienation, and disempowerment (Crawshaw et al., 2022; Morgan et al., 2023).

Vaccine coverage has been found to be lower among migrant populations for measles-mumps-rubella- varicella (MMRV) in Germany (Halliday et al., 2025; Jablonka et al., 2016), diphtheria-tetanus-polio (DT-IPV) in Germany, France, Switzerland and the UK (Mansor-Lefebvre et al., 2020; Jablonka et al., 2016), Hemophilus influenzae type b (Hib) in Germany and France (Mansor-Lefebvre et al., 2020), human papillomavirus (HPV) in Denmark and Norway (Fernández de Casadevante et al., 2016; Amdisen et al., 2018; Bjerke et al., 2021; Hertzum-Larsen et al., 2020) and influenza in Spain and France (Jiménez-García et al., 2008; Freund et al., 2011), among others. Specific sociodemographic factors, such as geographical origin, recent migration (Fernández de Casadevante et al., 2016; Freund et al., 2011), low vaccination coverage in countries of origin (Carreras-Abad et al., 2022), socioeconomic differences (Hertzum-Larsen et al., 2020), and language difficulties (Mansor-Lefebvre et al., 2020) were the most significant factors associated with under-vaccination.

The COVID-19 pandemic exposed severe health inequities among migrant populations, highlighting the urgent need to adopt a socioeconomic and intercultural framework in the pandemic response (Crawshaw et al., 2022). Several studies have shown that migrants were disproportionately affected by the COVID-19 pandemic (Jaljaa et al., 2022; Mazzalai et al., 2023; Lassale et al., 2020; Pérez-Muto et al., 2024; Campos and Monge, 2024). Migrant populations are often overrepresented among essential workers, in low-income or customer-facing occupations, employed under precarious contracts or as self-employed workers, and tend to live in overcrowded and multigenerational households, further exacerbating their vulnerability (Mazzalai et al., 2023; Lassale et al., 2020). Some migrant groups were found to experience higher COVID-19 mortality (Mazzalai et al., 2023; Vanthomme et al., 2021), despite the widely accepted healthy migrant effect (Aldridge et al., 2018; Razum et al., 2000).

One key element that allowed a drastic decrease in COVID-19 morbidity and mortality was the availability of highly effective vaccines less than one year after the declaration of the pandemic (European Medicines Agency, 2026). In Spain, COVID-19 vaccination started on 27 December 2020 which, due to vaccine scarcity during the early phases of the campaign (Crawshaw et al., 2022; Matlin et al., 2022), was prioritized for those at the highest risk, starting with individuals residing in long-term care facilities (LTCF), the elderly, and healthcare and other essential workers, followed by decreasing age groups. By the end of the 2021 vaccination campaign, 79% of the Spanish population had received at least one COVID-19 vaccine dose and 75% had completed the primary vaccination schedule (Ministerio de Sanidad (España), 2021).

Despite migrants’ higher vulnerability to COVID-19, little attention was paid to vaccination access among migrants, with migration status not being collected in COVID-19 vaccination registries or other available data sources to be able to monitor success in reaching these population groups nor tailor vaccination campaigns in real time. A few studies have analyzed access to COVID-19 vaccines by migrant populations in Italy (Cacciani et al., 2023), the Netherlands (Campman et al., 2023; Campman et al., 2023), the United States (Nguyen et al., 2022), England (Gaughan et al., 2023), Denmark (Gram et al., 2023) and Sweden (Spetz et al., 2022).

In Spain, no such study has been conducted nationally. Due to the unique feature of Spain as a host country for many migrants from Latin America (Instituto Nacional de Estadística (España) [Internet], 2021), with smaller language and cultural distance with Spanish native population, and the context of a national health system with universal free-of-charge coverage, including irregular migrants, barriers to COVID-19 vaccination may have been different from other settings. Evidence from Catalonia (Roel et al., 2022) suggests that coverage with the first dose (up to June 2021) was lower among non-Spanish nationals, at 60% for those from Western Europe and the Americas and below 50% for those from Africa, Asia, Oceania and Eastern Europe, compared with 86% among Spanish-born. Moreover, it is possible that migrants experienced delayed access to vaccines, even if eventually vaccinated, which in the pandemic context could have placed them at an increased risk of COVID-19.

The aim of this study was to assess differences by country of birth in time to first COVID-19 vaccination within the first year of the vaccination roll-out in Spain (between 27 December 2020 and 31 December 2021). Moreover, differences by country of birth were analyzed separately for males and females and across different age groups in order to detect gender and age-specific differences in access to vaccines by country of birth.

Materials and methods

Data sources

We used data from the Spanish COVID-19 Vaccination Registry (REGVACU), a database registering every COVID-19 vaccine dose administered by all public and private healthcare providers. In order to be vaccinated by a public healthcare provider and registered in REGVACU, individuals first needed to be registered for healthcare within the National Health Coverage database (TSI, by its Spanish acronym). Registration in TSI is free of charge for anyone registered as living in Spain, which is accessible to irregular migrants by showing proof of settlement (house rental contract, household bills in their name, or equivalent documents). Temporary registration of non-residents, including undocumented migrants, was also possible during the COVID-19 vaccination roll-out to grant universal access, though these were inconsistently notified to the central TSI database and might not have been captured in our study. Because there is no fee or prospective validation of registration, removals from the TSI are infrequent, resulting in more people being actively registered than actually living in Spain. For this reason, TSI cannot be validly used as denominator for epidemiological studies, nor can it be used to identify individuals living in Spain who were never vaccinated.

REGVACU includes individual identifiers, pseudonymized using a HASH algorithm (Agencia Española de Protección de Datos, 2019), along with basic demographic information, date of vaccination and target group, as defined by health authorities during the phases of vaccine prioritization, among others. The first dose of a COVID-19 vaccine received by each individual was extracted and cross-matched with the TSI which contains similar pseudonymized identifiers. TSI data included sociodemographic information on every person who was registered to receive healthcare in Spain during the study period, including data on country of birth.

Study design and participants

We retrospectively constructed an open cohort of the non-pediatric general population in Spain during the first year of the vaccination roll-out, between 27 December 2020 and 31 December 2021. The study population included any individual ≥12 years old as of 27 December 2020, registered in TSI at any point between 27 December 2020 and 31 December 2021, and who had at least one COVID-19 vaccine dose registered in REGVACU up to the date of data extraction for this study on 31 July 2022 (in order to capture in the study population individuals with very delayed first-dose vaccine administration). Individuals who, according to REGVACU, were first vaccinated while living in LTCFs were excluded, since they accessed vaccination considerably earlier than people of equivalent age groups living in the community and this outlier group could influence our estimates. Moreover, determinants of vaccination may differ between the LTCF and community settings.

A time-to-event study was performed with the administration of a first COVID-19 vaccine dose (of any type) as the event of interest. Individuals were enrolled on the latest among 27 December 2020 or the date of first registration in TSI, and were followed up until the earliest among first COVID-19 vaccine dose, death, de-registration from the TSI, or study administrative censoring on 31 December 2021. Time to vaccination was calculated from the start of the vaccination campaign on 27 December 2020. Individuals who had not received their first COVID-19 vaccine dose by 31 December 2021, or who died or de-registered in TSI before accessing vaccination, were censored as unvaccinated on the corresponding date.

Variables and definitions

Sociodemographic variables were extracted from the TSI, including date and country of birth. Country of birth was categorized into eight geographic regions: Spain (reference region), Western Europe and other Western countries (WC), Eastern Europe (EE), Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), North Africa and the Middle East (NAME), Southeast Asia and other Oceanian countries (SEA), Central America and the Caribbean (CAC) and South America (SA). The countries included in each region can be found in the Supplementary Material Table S1.

Autonomous region of residence according to the TSI (19 in Spain) or, if unavailable, region of vaccination according to REGVACU, was included to account for geographic variation in vaccine roll-out across Spain. Other covariates were extracted from REGVACU or, when unavailable, from TSI, including sex (male, female) and age, which was categorized into six groups: 12–17, 18–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79 and ≥80 years; with cut-offs aligned with the official vaccination roll-out cohorts defined by health authorities during phases of COVID-19 vaccines prioritization. Additionally, the vaccination target group was categorized into three groups: severe comorbidities, healthcare and other essential workers (HC&OEW), or general population.

Individuals with missing data on country of birth, sex, vaccination target group and/or autonomous region were excluded from further analyses. However, due to the low completeness of the country of birth variable, as sensitivity analyses, those with missing country of birth were alternatively considered as Spanish-born or analyzed as a separate category. As a third sensitivity analysis, the analysis was restricted to general population, i.e., dropping those vaccinated due to severe comorbidities or belonging to the HC&OEW group.

Statistical analysis

Kaplan-Meier was used to estimate the crude cumulative time to first COVID-19 vaccination by region of birth, using calendar time as the underlying time-scale. To compare the time to first vaccination by region of birth, using the Spanish-born as the reference, the event-time ratio (ETR) and its 95% confidence interval (95% CI) were estimated using an Accelerated Failure Time Weibull survival model adjusted by sex, age, population group, and autonomous region. Hazard ratios (HR) were derived from these estimates and the model shape using the standard relationship between the ETR and the HR in a Weibull model (Carroll, 2003). To assess this same effect but in the scale of absolute time to vaccination (in days), standardized cumulative incidence and median time (with interquartile range) to first COVID-19 vaccine were derived from the previous model for each region of birth, using as standard population the distribution of sex, age, population group and autonomous region in the full dataset.

Finally, the previous analyses were stratified by age group (12–17, 18–49, 50–59, 60–69, 70–79, 80 years and over) and sex, in order to assess variations in the effect of region of birth on time to first COVID-19 vaccination across these strata.

This study was approved by the research ethics committee at the Instituto de Salud Carlos III (CEI PI 02_2024). Informed consent was not required because this study is based on national population registries. The analysis was conducted using RStudio v.4.2.1.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics

Out of 44,510,140 individuals ≥12 years of age as of 27 December 2020 registered in TSI between that date and 31 December 2021, 37,602,182 (84.5%) fulfilled the study inclusion criteria. Among them, 26,720,450 (71.1% of 37,602,182) had a known country of birth and were included in the main analysis (Fig. 1). Of these, 23,008,018 (86.1%) were born in Spain; 925,064 (3.5%) in Western Europe and other Western countries; 103,664 (0.4%) in Eastern Europe; 148,791 (0.6%) in Sub-Saharan Africa; 573,028 (2.1%) in North Africa and the Middle East; 167,428 (0.6%) in South-East Asia; 373,524 (1.4%) in Central America and the Caribbean; and 1420,933 (5.3%) in South America. A total of 26,511,878 (99.2%) were vaccinated with at least one dose by December 31, 2021, while the remaining 0.8% received it at a later time. The proportion of vaccinated individuals within the study period was higher among the Spanish-born (99.5%) compared to the non-Spanish-born (range 95.2% to 98.4%) (Table 1).

The distribution by sex varied across regions of birth. People born in Eastern Europe, Central America and the Caribbean and South-America had a higher proportion of females compared with the Spanish-born population. In contrast, the proportion of males was higher among people born in North Africa and the Middle East and Sub-Saharan Africa. Overall, individuals born in non-European countries had a significantly younger age profile than those born in Spain, with a much lower proportion of people aged 60 years and over.

Differences in time to first COVID-19 vaccine dose, overall

Individuals born outside Spain, in any region, showed significantly lower cumulative probability of receiving the first COVID-19 vaccine dose in an unadjusted Kaplan-Meier estimation (Fig. 2). This crude difference became lower when accounting for sex, age, population group, and autonomous region, via standardization (Fig. 3). The Spanish-born population was vaccinated at a median (interquartile range) of 147 (106 – 196) days after the start of the vaccination roll-out on 27 December 2020 (Table 2). In contrast, individuals from Eastern Europe and Sub-Saharan Africa accessed vaccination at a median of 38 and 34 days later, respectively. Other groups showed smaller delays, being minimal for people born in South America, vaccinated a median of 13 days later than the Spanish-born population.

On the relative scale (adjusted Event Time Ratios and Hazard Ratios) these delays translated into 26% and 23% longer time to first COVID-19 vaccination, respectively, in people born in Eastern Europe and Sub-Saharan Africa vs. the Spanish-born (ETR: 1.26 and 1.23), and a 55% and 51% lower instantaneous probability of receiving the COVID-19 vaccine, respectively (HR: 0.45 and 0.49) (Table 2). On the opposite side, differences were minimal for people born in South America, who experienced 9% longer time (ETR: 1.09) and 26% lower instantaneous probability of receiving the vaccine (HR: 0.74) compared with the Spanish-born population.

Differences in time to first COVID-19 vaccine dose, by age and sex

In a crude Kaplan-Meier analysis, differences across regions of birth were more evident among women than among men (Fig. 4) but, after accounting for age, population group, and autonomous region, the direction and magnitude of the differences for the non-Spanish-born population were mostly similar for males and females (Table 3, standardized curves can be found in the Supplementary Material Figure S1). Across all groups, males accessed vaccination later than females. For example, median time to vaccination among the Spanish-born population was 155 days for males but 139 days for females.

Sensitivity analyses

Classifying individuals with an unknown country of birth (n = 10,881,732) as born in Spain or, alternatively, as a separate “unknown” category in the model, provided virtually identical results (Supplementary material, Table S4). The unknown group was vaccinated a median of 2 days later than Spanish-born population, corresponding to a 2% longer time to first COVID-19 vaccination and a 5% lower instantaneous probability of receiving the vaccine (ETR: 1.02; HR: 0.95), showing that they were probably missing at random, including a majority of Spanish-born. Finally, the sensitivity analysis restricting to the general population confirmed the robustness of the findings, with minimal variations in HRs and no changes in ETR (Supplementary material, Table S4).

Discussion

This study identified a consistent delay in receiving the first COVID-19 vaccine dose among individuals aged 12 years and over born outside Spain, highlighting existing barriers for migrant populations during the COVID-19 vaccination roll-out. The greatest delay was observed among people born in Eastern Europe and Sub-Saharan Africa, who accessed COVID-19 vaccination >30 days after the Spanish-born, while the difference was smallest for people born in South America (13 days).

While the magnitude of differences in time to vaccination does not seem big, the impact of such delay needs to take into consideration the risk of COVID-19 infection during such a time gap. Besides, it became more evident that migrant groups were disadvantaged when looking at the ratios, showing up to 55% lower probability of receiving the COVID-19 vaccine at any given time for Eastern Europe compared to the Spanish-born population. The number of days of delayed vaccination puts this lower probability of vaccination in the context of the pandemic´s rapid vaccine roll-out.

Throughout 2021, the Spanish National Health System, together with the autonomous regions, implemented a universal and inclusive strategy for the COVID-19 vaccination roll-out, aimed at achieving high coverage (Limia et al., 2021). The main guideline was to guarantee free access to the vaccine for anyone residing in Spain, regardless of whether they were registered for public healthcare, and temporary registrations were performed to provide access to vaccination. Migrants, including irregular migrants, were included in the vaccination campaign strategies as a socioeconomically vulnerable population, though they were not explicitly prioritized (Limia et al., 2020). Operational mechanisms included relaxing documentation requirements for temporary registration in healthcare (e.g. accepting passports or municipal registration certificates), setting up registration points at primary care centers (CAPs), collaborating strategically with non-governmental organizations (NGOs) to identify and mobilize underserved populations, such as those living in temporary settlements or reception centers, and including information in multiple languages, such as English, Chinese, Arabic, and Romanian [(Consejería de Sanidad de la Comunidad de Madrid, 2021; Vasco, 2021; Associació d’Ajuda Mútua d’Immigrants a Catalunya (AMIC), 2021; Ayuntamiento de Barberà del Vallès, 2021; Canarias, 2021)]. Although this approach facilitated high vaccination coverage and reduced bureaucratic barriers, our findings indicate that it was insufficient to fully eliminate disparities in timely vaccination uptake.

Observed inequalities might be related not only to remaining structural or administrative barriers, but to socioeconomic conditions, length of residence in Spain, documentation status, occupational composition and employment characteristics, higher mobility, digital literacy, differences in healthcare utilization, vaccine hesitancy, mistrust in the public healthcare system and/or differential prior SARS-CoV-2 infection, among other possible factors. Unfortunately, information on these variables was not available and could not be used to further investigate the mechanisms underlying the vaccination delay in our study, which was limited to signaling such inequalities. Differential underregistration among migrant populations for healthcare might both signal structural barriers and preclude comparable assessment of vaccination across groups. Additional research is needed to understand the drivers of and barriers to vaccination in the different migrant groups. Such information will be crucial to inform future vaccine roll-outs to address current challenges, expanding beyond pandemic scenarios into re-emerging infections and routine immunizations.

Disparities in time to vaccination were heterogeneous across migrant groups, reflecting their specificities depending on their prior health status, socioeconomic status, education, employment, living conditions, administrative status, ethnic and cultural background, migration drivers, and language skills, among others (Gaughan et al., 2023; Spetz et al., 2022; Dolby et al., 2022; Perry et al., 2021). The relatively shorter delays among South American migrants in our study support the hypothesis that lower linguistic and cultural barriers in this group, together with possibly a higher proportion of individuals with legal residence or Spanish nationality, might have greatly impacted access to COVID-19 vaccination in the pandemic context. Among the groups with the longest time to vaccination, the low vaccine uptake among Eastern Europeans is noteworthy, with a gap that increased with age. Interestingly, effects were largely similar in males and females indicating no major interaction between gender and geographical origin, despite both being determinants of vaccination uptake (Spetz et al., 2022; Perry et al., 2021; Tessier et al., 2022).

There is a limited number of studies addressing delays in the administration of the first dose of the COVID-19 vaccine in other settings. A number of studies have found lower COVID-19 vaccine uptake among adults from ethnic minorities in the first months of the vaccination roll-out in the United Kingdom (Dolby et al., 2022; Perry et al., 2021; Tessier et al., 2022) and the United States of America (USA) (Nguyen et al., 2022), with between 2- and 7-times higher risk of remaining unvaccinated. In both settings, the groups most at risk were people identified as Black/African/Caribbean ethnicity. In the study conducted in the USA, this was attributed both to vaccine hesitancy and to barriers to accessing vaccination (Nguyen et al., 2022). Other factors influencing vaccination in these studies were religion, living in deprived areas, belonging to low socioeconomic groups, having lower educational attainment and not having English as a first language, among others.

Outside English-speaking countries, studies have also found around 3-and-a-half-times higher risk of not being vaccinated for migrants in the initial COVID-19 vaccination roll-out in Sweden in working-age adults (Spetz et al., 2022) and 6-times higher risk in older adults (Spetz et al., 2022) . This risk was similar for first-dose or second-dose vaccination and was slightly reduced when accounting for other socioeconomic variables, highlighting the multi-dimensional nature of barriers to vaccination. In Italy (Cacciani et al., 2023), migrants were 55% less likely to receive the COVID-19 vaccine at any given time adjusted for age and the deprivation index. Finally, studies in Denmark (Gram et al., 2023) found 5 times higher risk of non-vaccination for migrants, especially of non-Western origin, and in the Netherlands, studies in ethnic minority groups including African Surinamese, Ghanaian, Turkish and Moroccan found lower intent to vaccinate (Campman et al., 2023) and lower vaccine uptake (Campman et al., 2023) . Results across Europe highlight the relevance of assessing migration inequalities specifically for each context, as the characteristics of the migrant populations and the determinants of vulnerability differ across countries. Despite this, there is a widespread observation of low vaccination uptake among migrants, that is even more striking considering that this population has been disproportionately affected by the COVID-19 pandemic in many countries (Jaljaa et al., 2022; Mazzalai et al., 2023; Lassale et al., 2020; Pérez-Muto et al., 2024; Campos and Monge, 2024; Vanthomme et al., 2021) .

A key strength of our study is the use of nationally representative data, making this the first analysis of COVID-19 vaccine first-dose uptake for migrants in Spain. Furthermore, our estimation of absolute differences, in addition to relative effects, provides direct estimates of the potential impact of delayed vaccination in these groups and examining these effects by age and sex provides a more nuanced understanding of the heterogeneity within the migrant population.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. Firstly, as in any study aiming to capture migrant populations, the definition of migrant is highly imperfect. Migration is a construct formed of many different realities unlikely to be captured by a single variable. We chose to classify our population by region of birth due to the accessibility and completeness of country of birth information, though there is high potential for misclassification, especially since we studied large geographical regions that preclude analyzing intra-regional differences. Moreover, information on migrants’ legal status was unavailable, preventing us from assessing the potential influence of administrative circumstances on access to vaccination and from determining the extent to which our study based on administrative databases managed to include irregular migrants. The large proportion of individuals with missing country of birth could have further compromised the representativity of the study. However, sensitivity analyses with alternative management of missing data resulted in similar conclusions. Finally, since residents of long-term care facilities were excluded, our results need to be interpreted as time to vaccination among people living in the community.

Conclusion

Overall, the findings suggest that universal health coverage alone is insufficient to ensure equitable and timely vaccination for all population groups in a pandemic context. They also highlight the heterogeneity across migrant groups in their uptake of COVID-19 vaccination during the initial roll-out. While further research is needed to understand the drivers of vaccination in different migrant populations, our results underscore the need for targeted, culturally adapted public health strategies for vaccine roll-out. These insights are also probably more broadly applicable to current vaccination programmes, supporting the need to strengthen such programmes and implement tailored, innovative strategies to reach these underserved population groups.
