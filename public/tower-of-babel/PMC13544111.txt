Spatial heterogeneity of Rift Valley fever burden in Kenya: a 10-year One Health zDALY evaluation
Authors: Lilian Owino, Bryan O Nyawanda, Samuel Kahariri, Penelope Vounatsou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRift Valley fever (RVF) is an endemic zoonotic disease in Kenya, with recurring outbreaks that affect both humans and livestock. Despite its impact, the full burden of RVF remains poorly quantified. This study presents the first integrated and spatially disaggregated estimates of RVF burden in Kenya using a One Health approach.MethodsWe quantified the national and subnational burden of RVF in Kenya from 2014 to 2024 using the zoonotic disability-adjusted life years (zDALYs) framework, which combines human health loss (disability-adjusted life years [DALYs]) and livestock-related losses (measured as animal loss equivalents [ALE]). Estimates were based on reported RVF cases, livestock mortality, abortions, and treatment costs. Monte Carlo simulations were used to generate 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs).ResultsThe burden of RVF (zDALY per 1000 persons) varied across the counties, with the highest burden observed in northern Kenya. The highest burden was recorded in Marsabit (24.67, 95% UI: 20.22–29.17), Baringo (12.23, 95% UI: 8.05–17.22), Wajir (9.31, 95% UI: 6.08–12.4), Embu (9.07, 95% UI: 6.58–11.63), and Tana River (6.23, 95% UI: 4.23–8.48). The counties with the lowest burden of RVF per 1000 inhabitants were observed in Kiambu (0.02; 95% UI: 0.00–0.07), Kisii (0.01; 95% UI: 0.00–0.07), Meru (0.00; 95% UI: 0.00–0.04), Samburu (0.00; 95% UI: 0.00–0.34), Tharaka Nithi (0.00; 95% UI: 0.00–0.00), and Uasin Gishu counties (0.00; 95% UI: 0.00–0.00). DALY and ALE differed across the counties with Marsabit found to have the highest animal burden (ALE) and Nairobi to have the highest human burden (DALYs).ConclusionThis study quantitatively demonstrates the substantial burden of RVF in Kenya across human and livestock populations. The findings can guide targeted risk assessments, preparedness planning, and cost-effective One Health strategies for future RVF outbreaks.Keywords: Rift Valley fever, Rift Valley fever virus, Zoonotic disability-adjusted life years, Burden of disease, Livestock economic losses, One Health

Introduction

Rift Valley fever (RVF) is a zoonotic, climate-sensitive arboviral disease caused by the Rift Valley fever virus (RVFV), with profound public health and economic implications globally. RVF was originally identified in Kenya in 1931 [1] and is now endemic in multiple African countries and the Arabian Peninsula [2,3]. In 2000, the disease was reported for the first time outside Africa, with outbreaks occurring in Saudi Arabia and Yemen [4]. More recently, RVFV has been detected in animal populations and mosquito vectors in parts of the Middle East, including Iran and Turkey [5]. The geographic spread of RVFV, coupled with increasing ecological vulnerability driven by climate change, underscores the risk of RVF outbreaks in new regions that lack prior exposure to the virus and may have limited capacity to respond [6].

Historically, RVF epidemics have followed a roughly 10-year cyclic pattern, often triggered by periods of excessive rainfall or flooding that favor mosquito population booms [7,8]. Inter-epidemic transmission may occur every 3–5 years, resulting in sporadic livestock losses and ongoing low-level viral circulation in ruminants [9]. Since the first isolation of the virus, Kenya has experienced several major outbreaks, necessitating coordinated interventions such as animal movement restrictions, vaccination, and enhanced surveillance [10].

RVFV is transmitted primarily through the bites of infected Aedes and Culex mosquitoes, and can also be transmitted to humans through direct contact with infected animal fluids or tissues [2,3]. In animals, particularly sheep, goats, and cattle, RVFV infection results in abortion during pregnancy and high mortality in newborns. Pregnant ewes often experience nearly 100% abortion rates, and lamb mortality may exceed 90% [11]. These losses disrupt herd demographics, reduce yields of meat, milk, and breeding stock, and lead to cascading effects on household food security and income. In humans, RVF infections range from asymptomatic or mild febrile illness to severe manifestations including hepatitis, retinitis, encephalitis, and hemorrhagic fever. Case fatality rates among hospitalized patients may reach 30%–40% [3]. High-risk populations include livestock herders, abattoir workers, veterinarians, and those involved in animal slaughter and birthing. Despite the potential severity of human cases, awareness of RVF remains low in many endemic settings, contributing to delayed diagnosis and elevated morbidity during outbreaks.

The socioeconomic impacts of RVF are multifaceted. Outbreaks may trigger local markets disruptions, national or regional trade restrictions, and high veterinary and public health costs [12]. During the 2006–2007 outbreak in Kenya, economic losses were estimated at approximately United States dollar (USD) 9.3 million [13], primarily due to livestock deaths, abortions, trade bans, treatment costs, and reduced productivity. More recent outbreaks in Kenya have similarly led to temporary trade suspensions and vaccination campaigns, although their full economic impact is less systematically documented [14].

In Kenya, the 2006/2007 outbreak resulted in an estimated 3.4 disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) lost per 1000 people, which is approximately 1% of total DALYs [15]. The most affected area, Garissa County, reported 8.0 DALYs per 1000 inhabitants, with male mortality from RVF roughly twice that of females. Direct out-of-pocket treatment expenses during the outbreak averaged USD 120 per case. Although these figures highlight the substantial burden of RVF, updated DALY estimates for more recent outbreaks are lacking, reflecting the gaps in surveillance and standardized data collection. Recent modeling work by Frings et al. [16] demonstrated the utility of high-resolution spatial approaches for mapping the burden of disease across Kenya and identified persistent gaps in subnational data availability and disease-specific burden estimation, particularly for neglected zoonoses like RVF.

Despite the evident impact of RVF, quantifying its full burden remains methodologically challenging. Traditional assessments of zoonotic disease burden have focused primarily on human health outcomes, using DALYs, which integrate years of life lost (YLL) due to premature mortality and years lived with disability (YLD) due to illness [17]. While DALYs provide a standard public health metric for prioritizing interventions, they overlook losses incurred in the animal sector and broader economic consequences. To address this limitation, the zoonotic disability-adjusted life years (zDALYs) framework was developed, extending the DALY concept to incorporate animal losses (e.g., lost animal life years, productivity loss) and the costs of public health and veterinary interventions [18,19]. One Health frameworks further advocate for joint surveillance and assessment across human, animal, and environmental systems to better capture the integrated burden of zoonoses like RVF [20,21]. Nevertheless, few studies have operationalized these frameworks quantitatively, particularly in endemic African settings.

This study aims to quantify the burden of RVF in Kenya through an integrated epidemiological, economic, and health assessment. Specifically, we evaluate livestock losses, human DALYs, and associated economic costs at both national and regional levels, drawing on historical outbreak data and emerging surveillance systems. This work represents the first effort to assess the geographic variation in zDALY burden across Kenya and is intended to inform targeted risk assessments, preparedness planning, and cost-effective strategies for future RVF outbreaks.

Methods

Study area

Kenya is located on the eastern coast of Africa (Fig. 1). The country had an estimated population of 52.4 million in 2024 and is growing at approximately 2% annually [23]. The population is unevenly distributed, with high concentrations in urban centers and the fertile highlands, and lower densities across the arid and semi-arid regions in the north and east. Agriculture is the largest contributor to the economy, accounting for 16.8% of gross domestic product (GDP) in 2024—the highest share of any sector [24,25]. Livestock keeping is predominantly small-scale, with most farmers maintaining 1–20 livestock [26,27].

Data sources

This is a retrospective burden-of-disease analysis based on routinely collected surveillance data. A priori sample size calculation was not performed; instead, all eligible RVF cases recorded in the human surveillance system (District Health Information Software version 2, DHIS2) and the livestock surveillance system (Department of Veterinary Services, DVS) across all 47 counties of Kenya during the study period (2014–2024) were included in the analysis. The precision of county-level burden estimates therefore depends on the number of recorded cases per county, which varies substantially and is reflected in the 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs) reported throughout.

Data on laboratory-confirmed and clinically diagnosed RVF cases, as well as livestock deaths during the study period, were obtained from the Directorate of Veterinary Services (DVS). Livestock market prices for the years 2021–2024 were sourced from the Ministry of Agriculture and Livestock Development [28]. In counties where price data were unavailable, average prices from neighboring counties were used as proxies. Gross National Income (GNI) per capita for 2014–2023 was retrieved from the World Bank database [23]. The abortion rates due to RVF by species were obtained from Hassan et al. [29] and used to estimate the non-monetary impact of animal losses at the county level. Livestock prices were converted to USD using the May 2025 exchange rate of 1 USD = 130 Kenyan shillings (KES) [30]. Livestock population figures were derived from the 2019 Kenya Population and Housing Census [31].

Records of human RVF cases in Kenya were extracted from the DHIS2 system for the period 2014–2024. Life expectancy data were sourced from the World Health Organization Global Health Observatory Data Repository [32]. In cases where data such as the number of deaths due to RVF or the median age at death were unavailable, estimates were derived from existing literature [29]. To estimate human mortality, we applied an RVF-specific case fatality rate of 23% to the number of reported cases [29]. Data triangulation was conducted using multiple secondary sources, and missing values were imputed using parameter-based estimation methods.

Data analysis

To estimate the burden of RVF in both humans and animals, we applied the zDALYs framework. This approach integrates the DALY metric, which quantifies disease burden in human populations, with the animal loss equivalents (ALE) method, which captures livestock-related losses in non-monetary time units. The combined metric facilitates a unified evaluation of RVF burden across species.

DALYs are calculated as the sum of YLL due to premature mortality and YLD caused by morbidity. ALE, on the other hand, reflects livestock losses, such as animal deaths, abortions, and treatment costs, converted into time equivalents by dividing their monetary value by Kenya's GNI per capita. This conversion enables a direct comparison between the human and animal components of disease burden. Conceptually, ALE expresses livestock economic losses as the number of equivalent human productive years that those losses represent, using Kenya's GNI per capita as the value of one average human productive year. Because both DALYs and ALE are therefore ultimately expressed in the same time-equivalent units (years), they can be summed additively to produce the integrated zDALY metric, following the framework proposed by Torgerson et al. [21] and applied in subsequent zoonotic burden studies [33,34].

YLL was calculated as the product of the number of human deaths due to RVF and the corresponding life expectancy at the time of death. YLD was estimated by multiplying the number of non-fatal human RVF cases by the disability weight and average duration of illness. For this analysis, a disability weight of 0.133 (range: 0.088–0.190) for dengue fever sourced from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2015, as RVF does not have a defined disability weight and dengue fever has similar clinical epidemiology [35]. It was assumed that all recorded cases reflected individuals with moderate to severe symptoms who sought healthcare. ALE was calculated by estimating the monetary value of livestock losses, including treatment costs, the value of dead animals, and the value of abortions. This total was divided by the GNI per capita [23]. The full mathematical formulations of YLD, YLL, and ALE, together with the complete variable definition list, are provided in Supplementary material 1.

To account for uncertainty in the estimates, we conducted Monte Carlo simulations with 1000 iterations per county, allowing parameters to vary within their distributions. Binomial distributions were used to model abortion and death outcomes in both humans and animals, while normal distributions were applied to continuous variables, such as livestock prices, disability weights, duration of illness, and age at death. At each of the 1000 county-level iterations, each parameter was drawn 1000 times from its distribution, and one value randomly selected for the final estimation. The median value was reported as the estimate, while the 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles were used to construct the 95% UIs. All burden estimates were reported per 1000 population, based on the 2019 Kenya Population and Housing Census [31].

All the analysis and figures were developed using R version 4.3.2 [22].

Ethical consideration

In this study, we analyzed secondary health facility data reported through the DHIS2 system, obtained with permission from the Ministry of Health, Kenya. Ethics approval was waived, as the data were aggregated at the district level and contained no individual-level identifiers. Animal health data were acquired from the Directorate of Veterinary Services with the necessary authorization. Additional secondary data used in the analysis were sourced from publicly available official datasets and peer-reviewed publications and therefore did not require ethical approval.

Results

During the study period, RVF cases in livestock and humans were recorded in 45 counties out of the 47 counties between 2014 and 2024. Only Tharaka-Nithi and Uasin Gishu counties reported no RVF cases throughout the study period. In total, there were 7756 reported cases and 2914 deaths in livestock. The distribution of RVF cases was highly variable, with some counties experiencing only one or two reported cases over the entire period, while others, such as Marsabit, Baringo, and Embu, recorded over 900 combined human and animal cases.

Discussion

This study represents the first comprehensive effort to quantify the burden of RVF in Kenya by integrating epidemiological, economic, and health metrics into a unified zDALY framework. By combining health impact (DALYs) and economic losses (ALE) from livestock, we reveal spatial heterogeneity in RVF burden between 2014 and 2024. It is also the first study to assess the spatial variation in zDALY burden across Kenya, providing granular insights into how the disease differentially affects counties. The results indicate that Marsabit, Baringo, and Wajir counties have the highest burden of RVF in Kenya. The highest overall burden was recorded in Marsabit County, predominantly driven by livestock losses. This aligns with earlier evidence that pastoralist communities in arid and semi-arid zones are disproportionately affected by RVF due to both ecological and socioeconomic vulnerabilities. For example, Somalia experienced a 25% decline in GDP between 1998 and 2002 due to livestock trade restrictions [36]. Other countries, such as Tanzania and Saudi Arabia, experienced GDP losses of 2% and approximately USD 75–90 million, respectively, during comparable outbreaks [37].

These findings confirm and extend earlier studies that identified the northern and eastern parts of Kenya as RVF hotspots and high-risk areas [29,38,39]. These regions are semi-arid and prone to flooding during rainy seasons. Historical observations of RVF outbreaks show it follows a cyclical outbreak pattern every 5–6 years, often triggered by excessive rainfall or flooding that facilitates mosquito breeding in Kenya [40]. Orinde [15] quantified human DALYs from the 2006/2007 outbreak in Garissa at 8.0 DALYs per 1000 people, providing standardized estimates comparable to other diseases. However, by integrating animal health data into the zDALY metric, this study provides a broader and more representative assessment of the total burden in a One Health context.

The integrated zDALY framework reveals burden patterns that would be missed by either DALY-only or ALE-only analyses. While Marsabit and Wajir, in the arid north and northeast, have historically been recognized as RVF hotspots, our results identify additional hotspots in other regions of Kenya. Embu (Mount Kenya region) emerges as a predominantly livestock-driven hotspot, with very high ALE contributions to total zDALY burden (96% ALE) deviating from previously positive association of low altitude with RVF activity [41]. In contrast, Nairobi, Nandi, Kitui, Homa Bay, Isiolo, Bomet, Elgeyo Marakwet, Kericho, Laikipia, and Kiambu emerge as predominantly human-driven hotspots, where the burden is almost entirely captured by DALYs (≥97% DALY in the 10 counties). Baringo, in the central Rift Valley, is unique in showing simultaneously high human and livestock burdens, with DALYs (2738) and ALE (4075) both ranking among the top three values nationally for their respective components. These patterns reflect distinct underlying drivers: high livestock densities and pastoralist livelihoods in Marsabit, Wajir, and Baringo; mixed smallholder livestock systems in Embu; and concentrations of reported human cases, potentially reflecting healthcare-seeking behavior, surveillance intensity, or genuine transmission, in Nairobi, Nandi, Kitui, and Turkana. The ability of the zDALY framework to identify these complementary burden profiles illustrates its added value over single-sector metrics for guiding One Health surveillance and intervention priorities.

RVF outbreaks in Kenya are strongly associated with arid and semi-arid lowland ecological zones, where seasonal flooding creates suitable breeding habitat for the principal RVFV vectors. Aedes mcintoshi and Culex pipiens have been molecularly detected as RVFV-positive during outbreak and inter-epidemic periods in Kenya [42], and increases in Aedes and Culex abundance have been temporally associated with RVF outbreak onset. Soil type, particularly the impermeable black cotton soils characteristic of dambo depressions has been positively associated with RVF outbreak risk, as these soils retain water and produce the temporary pools required for vector emergence [40,42].

Moreover, counties with known flood-prone landscapes such as Baringo exhibited both high human health burden and significant livestock losses. Our results also emphasize the role of surveillance since counties such as Baringo and Marsabit, with active community reporting mechanisms, may appear to have higher burdens due to improved detection. Sixty-five percent (65%) of farmers in Baringo reported outbreaks to authorities, often using mobile phones, a behavior possibly shaped by past exposure to outbreaks and awareness campaigns [43]. This was further highlighted in modeling work by Frings et al. [16], which demonstrated the utility of high-resolution spatial approaches in mapping the burden of disease across Kenya and identified persistent gaps in subnational data availability and disease-specific burden estimation, particularly for neglected zoonoses like RVF.

Several Kenyan counties share the ecological features—arid or semi-arid lowlands, yet did not emerge as RVF hotspots in our analysis. For example, Mandera, Garissa, Samburu, and Kajiado have ecological profiles broadly comparable to Marsabit and Wajir [44] but recorded substantially lower zDALY burdens in zDALYs per 1000 persons (Mandera: 2.16, 95% UI: 1.69–2.63; Garissa: 0.90, 95% UI: 0.50–1.44; Samburu: 0.00, 95% UI: 0.00–0.34; and Kajiado 0.50, 95% UI: 0.18–1.00; compared with Marsabit at 24.67, 95% UI: 20.22–29.17 and Wajir at 9.31, 95% UI: 6.08–12.40)). Several non-mutually exclusive explanations may account for this pattern. First, RVF case detection depends critically on surveillance intensity and laboratory capacity, both of which vary across counties; under-reporting in counties with weaker surveillance infrastructure cannot be excluded. Second, vaccination coverage of livestock differs between counties, and historical mass vaccination campaigns in some regions may have suppressed transmission. Third, livestock movement patterns and pastoralist routes may concentrate exposure in some counties more than others despite similar local ecology. Fourth, finer-scale ecological heterogeneity—not captured at county level—may explain the divergence. Disentangling these explanations is beyond the scope of the present study but represents an important direction for future work.

RVF burden is shaped not only by environmental factors but also by gendered occupational roles and social practices. Men are more likely to engage in herding, slaughtering, and handling of sick livestock, while women are often exposed during milking, water collection, or food preparation [2]. Despite this, sex-disaggregated burden estimates remain scarce [12]. A recent review noted that pregnant women and informal workers may face additional risks, but are underrepresented in existing burden frameworks [45]. Future assessments should include sex- and age-disaggregated data to better reflect differential exposure and vulnerability across populations.

The burden of RVF in livestock-dominant counties such as Marsabit and Wajir was heavily driven by ALE, underscoring the substantial economic cost of livestock mortality and abortions. In contrast, urban counties such as Nairobi showed a higher human burden (DALYs), reflecting more severe disease outcomes, better reporting of human cases, or possible importation of cases through livestock markets. Nairobi, the capital city of Kenya, hosts the country's best health facilities, including two of the four level six referral hospitals, and critical cases requiring specialized care are often referred there. Additionally, livestock slaughtered in Nairobi are sourced from other counties. For example, the Kiamaiko market and slaughterhouse source goats and sheep from northern Kenya, Somalia, and Ethiopia [46], regions with a high RVF burden. The variation in RVF burden across counties further highlights the influence of animal movement and transhumance in sustaining the spread of the disease.

This study is among the first to operationalize the zDALY framework for RVF in Kenya. Previous applications of zDALYs have been limited to other zoonoses, such as cystic echinococcosis, Taenia solium, rabies control, leptospirosis, anthrax, and brucellosis, and have mostly relied on previously published literature [33,34,[47], [48], [49]]. Our integration of livestock loss estimates, public health surveillance data, and socioeconomic valuation provides a reproducible and scalable approach for assessing other endemic zoonotic diseases. The use of Monte Carlo simulations adds robustness to uncertainty quantification and supports evidence-based policymaking.

Zero recorded RVF cases were strongly linked to existing surveillance system limitations. In Uasin Gishu and Tharaka Nithi, no official government record reported RVF cases on humans or livestock during the study period. At the same time, there is no evidence of active investigation to determine whether these counties are truly free of RVF. Seroprevalence studies have revealed possible RVF circulation in areas with no official reports, supporting the need to strengthen surveillance systems to enable real-time outbreak detection and mitigation [50]. In Uganda, re-emergence of RVF was detected after 40 years, attributed to improvements in reporting system [51].

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, previous studies have reported disproportional burden by age, sex, pregnancy status, or socioeconomic status. However, these data were unavailable, limiting the granularity of burden estimates. Second, RVF does not have a disease-specific disability weight in the Global Burden of Disease catalogue [35]. We therefore used the dengue fever disability weight (0.133) as a proxy, as RVF and dengue are both arboviral mosquito-borne infections with overlapping early clinical features (fever, myalgia, headache, and generalized malaise) and most cases are asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic [52,53]. Previous RVF burden studies have used the same proxy in the absence of a dedicated value [15]. Importantly, we did not treat 0.133 as a fixed point estimate: in the Monte Carlo simulation the disability weight was sampled from a normal distribution centered on the Global Burden of Disease 2015 dengue point estimate (0.133) and spanning the published uncertainty range (0.088–0.190), so the uncertainty arising from this choice is propagated into the 95% UIs reported throughout. We nevertheless acknowledge that severe RVF presentations (e.g. hemorrhagic, encephalitic, retinitis) may warrant higher disability weights; the development of a disease-specific RVF weight from clinical cohort data would further strengthen future burden estimates.

Third, underreporting, especially in the earlier years of the study period, may have influenced burden estimates, although we mitigated this through data triangulation and simulation modeling. Fourth, livestock ALE estimates relied on average livestock prices and assumed abortion rates, which may not fully reflect county-level market variation or price fluctuations during outbreaks. A further limitation concerns case ascertainment. Our analysis combined laboratory-confirmed and clinically diagnosed cases drawn from routine surveillance: livestock data from the DVS and human data from the DHIS2 system. RVF shares non-specific febrile features with malaria, dengue, leptospirosis, and other tropical illnesses, and clinical diagnosis without real-time PCR or immunoglobulin M serological confirmation may misclassify cases of these other conditions as RVF, particularly during co-circulation. The most plausible direction of this bias is overestimation of RVF cases, and our burden estimates should therefore be interpreted as plausible upper bounds. Strengthening laboratory confirmation in routine RVF surveillance, through improved diagnostic capacity at sub-national level and standardized confirmation protocols—would substantially reduce diagnostic uncertainty in future burden assessments.

Our findings underscore the value of zDALYs as a standardized metric for prioritizing One Health interventions, particularly in regions with concurrent human and animal disease burden. In Kenya, counties such as Marsabit, Wajir, and Baringo should be considered priority zones for RVF vaccination, mosquito control, and enhancement of early warning systems. Moreover, the results highlight the need for joint surveillance systems linking veterinary and public health sectors, to capture the full epidemiological footprint of RVF and inform outbreak preparedness planning.
