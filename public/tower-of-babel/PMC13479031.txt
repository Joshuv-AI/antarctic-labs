From Control to Connection: Reflections on Implementing Safe Steps in Acute Mental Health Units
Authors: Alison Taylor, Matthew Flowers, Marie Hutchinson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis paper presents a reflective account of the implementation of the Safe Steps for De‐escalation practice model over a 12‐month period across three acute mental health units. It describes the implementation process of the model, designed to reduce restrictive practices in acute mental health settings. The Safe Steps model is a mental health nurse‐designed and led approach for person‐centred, structured and relational de‐escalation. It positions therapeutic engagement, emotional attunement, and psychological safety as central components of effective responding, particularly during high‐stress situations. The model offers a practical and adaptable resource for diverse acute care environments. This paper examines the implementation process and lessons learned from a multi‐site evaluation of the Safe Steps model in a number of acute mental health units. The evaluation was guided by the complexity model of Skivington et al. (2021), which emphasises multifaceted real‐world approaches to evaluation. Drawing on reflective accounts from the research and implementation leads, using practice‐informed insights, the paper explores the opportunities, challenges and lessons learned in embedding a relational de‐escalation model into everyday clinical practice. Particular attention is given to capability building, scenario‐based learning, and team‐based support strategies that facilitated implementation. The findings highlight the importance of leadership, emotional safety, and inclusion of the consumer voice in sustaining reductions in coercive practices. The paper offers practical insights for clinicians and researchers seeking to implement relational and trauma‐informed approaches in acute mental health services.Keywords: coercion, de‐escalation, mental health services, psychiatric nursing, reflective practice

Background

Mental health nurses (MHNs) working at the frontline of acute inpatient care report emotional and ethical strain from managing behavioural escalation. Research and policy consistently emphasise the need for approaches that balance safety with compassion and dignity (Muir‐Cochrane et al. 2018; Price et al. 2018). Even so, the rates of restrictive practices such as seclusion, physical restraint and chemical sedation remain a concern (AIHW 2024b). While often framed as necessary for safety in high‐risk situations (Wilson, Rouse, et al. 2017), these practices significantly limit personal freedom. They are distressing for consumers who report feelings of fear and shame (Hawsawi et al. 2020). MHNs describe restrictive practice use as ethically troubling, sometimes referring to it as a ‘necessary evil’ (Wilson, Rouse, et al. 2017, 504) used reluctantly when no safer alternative is available (Power et al. 2020).

The continued high rates of involuntary admission and the use of coercive practices are partly embedded in the legacy of institutional mental health care, which historically prioritised containment over connection. The clinical and ethical consequences of coercion are now widely acknowledged (Muir‐Cochrane et al. 2018). Yet sustained change at the point of care remains difficult to achieve, especially in fast‐paced, emotionally complex institutional environments, where clinical decision making is heavily influenced by risk and team dynamics (Isobel and Edwards 2017; Power et al. 2020).

Despite decades of reform, including policy mandates, service level initiatives, and regulatory oversight, Australia, the location of the Safe Steps initiative, still reports high levels of involuntary treatment: In 2022–2023, around 46% of acute mental health hospital admissions and 16%–20% of community mental health contacts were involuntary (AIHW 2025). These patterns align with international concerns regarding the persistence of, and harms associated with, coercive practices in mental health settings (Chieze et al. 2019). For many, especially Australian First Nations peoples, culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) communities, and individuals with a history of trauma, such practices can reinforce disempowerment, evoke past harms, and further erode trust in services (AIHW 2024a; Brophy et al. 2016; The Healing Foundation 2023).

De‐escalation is increasingly recognised as a key strategy to reduce the reliance on restrictive practices (Price et al. 2018). The NICE Guideline NG10 (2015; reviewed in 2024) highlights the role of emotional regulation, timely relational connection, and therapeutic communication in reducing interpersonal distress and the use of restrictive practices. However, embedding these practices consistently is challenging. Inconsistent training, competing time pressures, unclear role expectations and safety concerns (Muskett 2014; Power et al. 2020; Wilson, Hutchinson, and Hurley 2017) undermine staff confidence. These challenges can lead to reactive, rather than proactive responses, and are further compounded by organisational culture and systemic factors that influence practice autonomy and consistency (Isobel, Wilson, Gill, Schelling, and Howe 2021; Price, Papastavrou Brooks, et al. 2024; Wilson et al. 2024).

Although de‐escalation is widely promoted as a first‐line strategy for managing conflict, enhancing safety and minimising the use of restrictive or coercive practices in mental health, there is little robust evidence to support its effectiveness (Lee et al. 2025; Price, Papastavrou Brooks, et al. 2024). Systematic evaluations of de‐escalation interventions in mental health settings report a high risk of bias (Lee et al. 2025; Price, Papastavrou Brooks, et al. 2024). Highlighting this limitation, a recent systematic review identified six moderate‐quality studies that provided outcome data on conflict rates (Price, Papastavrou Brooks, et al. 2024). Although four of these moderate‐quality studies claimed favourable results, only one reported sufficient data to establish a significant effect, with a further two reporting increased rates of assault and aggression. Most study designs relied on small, cross‐sectional samples at single sites. Another review found that only two out of 38 studies used active controls (Price et al. 2015). Furthermore, confounding factors were often underreported or uncontrolled, and outcomes were typically measured over short durations. Available evidence on de‐escalation training primarily evaluates outcomes in terms of staff knowledge, confidence, or performance on the day. Although training programmes are assumed to change practice, the 2019 RAND Europe report found no reduction in violent incidents following such training (Leach et al. 2019). Importantly, the content and strategies of de‐escalation training remain poorly described, typically limited to broad statements of intent (Duncan et al. 2023; Miller et al. 2025). Techniques taught vary considerably; only eight of 38 studies in one review reported to include specific interpersonal strategies (Price et al. 2015). Overall, the mechanisms and components of de‐escalation, particularly as applied in practice, remain poorly defined.

A recent scoping review similarly found that, while emotional intelligence (EI) competencies such as self‐awareness, self‐regulation and empathy are often referenced in the literature, they are rarely explicitly measured or systematically embedded into training (Taylor et al. 2026). There remains a lack of structured, ready‐to‐implement models specifically designed for acute mental health settings (Price, Papastavrou Brooks, et al. 2024). Where models such as Six Core Strategies and Safewards have demonstrated reductions in conflict events and decreased containment rates (Daguman et al. 2024), there is variability in the reported procedures (see Lee et al. 2025). Moreover, these widely adopted models do not provide sufficient guidance on de‐escalation strategies beyond universal statements about enhancing interpersonal style, communication or self‐regulation. In addition, broad, generic approaches often overlook the emotional labour and relational complexity of frontline mental health nursing. This deficit is a key limitation in both implementation research and training design (Price, Armitage, et al. 2024; Price, Papastavrou Brooks, et al. 2024).

The emerging body of evidence on Safe Steps for De‐escalation indicates that structured therapeutic responses based on relational competencies, such as those highlighted by Daguman et al. (2025a), are associated with reduced distress and lower use of seclusion and restraint, even within institutional constraints. Despite increasing interest in interventions aimed at reducing restrictive practices, the implementation activities underpinning these approaches are often poorly described, limiting opportunities for replication, adaptation and critical reflection in practice (Baker et al. 2021).

This paper addressees this gap providing a detailed reflective account of the implementation of the Safe Steps model (Safe Steps) within acute mental health units. The reflections examine the opportunities, challenges and lessons arising from implementation to inform future clinical practice and research. First, Safe Steps and the study details are outlined in the following section.

A Model for Relational De‐Escalation

Introduced in 2018 in a regional mental health unit in New South Wales (NSW), Australia, the Safe Steps was designed in response to MHNs requests for a de‐escalation approach specifically suited to the relational and practical demands of acute mental health nursing. These requests emerged during a period of rising acuity and complexity of inpatient presentations and an increased use of seclusion and restraint (NSW Health MNCLHD 2020). Consequently, we sought to develop a model that would respond to policy and practice imperatives to reduce restrictive practices (NMHCCF 2021; NSW Health 2017, 2020). Our intention was for Safe Steps to align with international and national priorities for safer, recovery‐focused mental health care, and that it reflected human rights models (United Nations 2006; World Health Organization and the United Nations [represented by the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights] 2023) and nursing literature advocating systemic reform in response to the harms associated with restrictive practices (Cusack et al. 2018; Sweeney et al. 2016).

Rather than adding more theoretical content to an already crowded clinical environment, we developed Safe Steps by drawing on relational, trauma‐informed and emotionally intelligent theories and translating these principles into a structured, implementable model. Safe Steps consists of three interrelated components: the relational de‐escalation model itself, training in the approach and its underpinning principles, and structured restrictive practice review processes intended to support reflection and implementation. This approach responded to a gap in the literature, where de‐escalation training has often lacked a strong theoretical foundation, adaptation and practical application (Price, Armitage, et al. 2024).

Safe Steps centres on strengthening therapeutic connection as a pathway to emotional and physical safety. Through offering training, a structured model, and in‐practice support, we aimed to build clinical decision‐making capacity through early recognition of distress, validation of emotional experiences, and collaborative negotiation of expectations. This focus on emotions in clinical practice aligns with evidence that they play a critical role in clinical judgement and that emotional intelligence skills are essential for safe and ethical decision‐making (Kozlowski et al. 2017). Through the Safe Steps approach, we aimed to support MHNs to act with empathy and confidence, particularly during high‐stress situations. We intended to offer a structured stepwise approach that is intended to help MHNs recognise early warning signs of emotional distress and interpersonal conflict, to maintain emotional regulation, and respond with deliberate therapeutic intent.

Additionally, our objective in developing Safe Steps was to anchor responses in values‐based care and emotional awareness, supporting consumer dignity and promoting empathic, therapeutic engagement, an interactional process that strengthens the nurse‐consumer relationship and improves care outcomes (Wu 2021). We aimed to create an approach that would support MHNs to act with therapeutic intent under pressure, while positioning relational safety as an essential, practical and measurable component of inpatient care. Within this paper, relational safety is understood as the creation of emotional and psychological conditions in which consumers and staff can engage without fear, coercion, or escalation. It is supported through emotionally attuned interactions, consistent therapeutic communication, and staff capacity for self‐regulation and empathy in high‐pressure situations. Initial implementation and controlled trials suggest that this approach could contribute to reductions in restrictive practices in acute mental health settings.

Evidence to Date

Safe Steps has been evaluated as a complex intervention in a number of acute mental health units over a period of time. A synopsis of evaluation outcomes related to restrictive practice use and nurse and consumer perspectives and experiences is presented here to provide context for the implementation reflections.

The first evaluation was a single‐centre before‐and‐after study within an adult acute mental health inpatient unit. This study examined the association between therapeutic responses and reductions in restrictive practice use and situational aggression (Daguman et al. 2025a). A feature analysis of 917 contemporaneous nurse‐recorded de‐escalation events identified the drivers associated with seclusion and physical restraint (Daguman et al. 2025b). The study also evaluated the implementation of strengths‐based restrictive practice review meetings as part of the Safe Steps model (Daguman et al. 2025c).

Building on this work, the second study evaluated Safe Steps across three implementation sites using a prospective mixed concurrent control design with three matched control units. Outcomes were assessed over a 12‐month implementation period and compared with a 12‐month baseline for both intervention and control sites. Based on legally mandated reports of seclusion and physical restraints, the initial implementation study reported a 63% reduction in seclusion rates (IRR = 0.37, 95% CI [0.24, 0.57], p < 0.001) and a 24% insignificant reduction in the use of physical restraint compared to baseline (IRR = 0.76, 95% CI [0.53, 1.09], p = 0.14; Blinded 2025). Results included a 78% statistically significant lower rate of total restrictive practice events (seclusion, physical restraint and as‐needed intramuscular psychotropics) at the 12‐month point (IRR = 0.22, 95% CI [0.09, 0.55], p < 0.001; Blinded 2025). In comparison with control units, weighted modelling estimated that overall restrictive practice events at the intervention sites during the one year of implementation were 35% lower (IRR = 0.65, 95% CI [0.60, 0.69], p < 0.001; Blinded 2025). At a frequency level, this finding included a lower (61%) use of administration of intramuscular antipsychotics (excluding Zuclopenthixol acetate), and a 59% lower use of intramuscular benzodiazepine compared to control sites.

Empirical findings on nurses' experiences of Safe Steps suggest it was experienced as a relational, adaptive and collective practice, shaped by staffing pressures and leader support (Daguman, Yoxall, et al. 2026). Consumers recounted how clarity calmed and confusion harmed; how the use of coercion and control cut deep; and how connection with MHNs, no matter how small, was treatment in itself. The meaning‐making that arose from these connections far outweighed their experience of medication as treatment (Daguman, Owen, et al. 2026).

Completing the cycle of evaluation, this paper provides a reflective account of the implementation process from the perspective of the lead researcher, with particular attention to context, adaptation, and the relational work involved in embedding a complex intervention into acute mental health nursing practice.

Method

Employing the updated Medical Research Council (MRC) framework for developing and evaluating complex interventions (Skivington et al. 2021), this paper focuses upon the implementation phase of the model. It provides a practice‐informed description and reflexive account of the implementation, gives attention to implementation of the intervention, the local context, staff engagement and implications for sustainability. Addressing how Safe Steps was introduced, adapted, supported and sustained in practice, and the lessons learned from the perspective of the implementation and research leads.

The implementation record and reflections are drawn from the first author's role as implementation lead, which involved coordinating implementation activities across the participating sites, including delivery of education, scenario‐based learning, and ongoing engagement with champions and the clinical teams. Reflections were captured during these activities and compiled upon completion. A reflexive interpretive approach was used to organise and coalesce the reflections, guided by the broader complexity model. Care was taken to critically consider assumptions, the relationship‐based implementation process and interpretations of experience. An ongoing cycle of critical reflexivity was used to examine the implementation process and the emerging interpretation.

Implementing Safe Steps in Context

The intervention was implemented in phases, beginning with capability building and progressing to full integration into routine care (Figure 1), aligning with established implementation models (Fixsen 2005). It included team‐based debriefing and feedback, which are recognised as essential implementation drivers that support practitioner development, relational practice, and emotional resilience (Hammervold et al. 2019). These structures were intended to help translate policy goals into everyday nursing practice by supporting MHNs with the emotional and relational skills needed to manage real‐world challenges in mental health nursing.

Safe Steps implementation process.

Safe Steps was implemented through a phased, MHN‐led process designed to build capability, shift unit culture and embed relational practice sustainably and measurably. While the model's structure provides consistency, its delivery was intended to be flexible and responsive to the unique needs and context of each acute mental health unit.

The implementation process drew on adult learning theory, participatory leadership principles, and the core concepts of emotional intelligence and trauma‐informed care. Knowles et al. (2014) emphasised that adult learning programmes should be grounded in andragogy, recognising learners' need for autonomy, relevance and practical engagement. Within the implementation, this principle was operationalised by supporting mental health nurses' autonomy in the learning process: online resources were offered as flexible, supplementary materials to be engaged with at their own pace, with no tracking of attendance or completion. Taylor and Kroth (2009) further highlighted the importance of experience‐based, learner‐centred environments, reflected in our use of reflective practice sessions that enabled nurses to actively shape their own learning. Leadership in nursing is similarly understood to develop through education, practice and empowerment, with an emphasis on relational skills and emotional intelligence (Curtis et al. 2011). Consistent with this, Te Pou o te Whakaaro (2018) promoted inclusive, values‐based leadership, which we incorporated by positioning mental health nurses as change agents who could influence how new models were embedded in practice. This approach aligns with implementation science literature, which stresses the importance of practitioners as active participants in shaping and sustaining interventions (Fixsen 2005).

Implementation Phase 1: Engagement and Assessment

Implementation began with site selection and multi‐level engagement, including executive leaders, clinical directors, frontline MHNs, peer workers and educators. In developing our approach, we aimed to follow Curtis et al. (2011) in emphasising early and inclusive involvement across roles, to foster shared ownership, strengthen relationships, and avoid perceptions of top‐down change.

Staff were encouraged to express concerns, frustrations and hopes for change. These early discussions not only surfaced the emotional labour of working in high‐pressure environments but also acted as an informal assessment of nurses' readiness for relational practice. The emotional toll on staff working in such environments, including fear, moral distress, and balancing safety with consumer‐centred care, is acknowledged in current literature (Muir‐Cochrane et al. 2018).

Implementation Phase 2: Capacity Building

The Safe Steps training was delivered across four progressive levels and intended to support MHNs to develop skills through staged, reflective learning (Benner 1984; Knowles et al. 2014). To support diverse teams, a comprehensive online training package complemented face‐to‐face training, creating a shared foundation of knowledge. Face‐to‐face sessions then focused on skill application, scenario practice, and collaborative problem solving.

To meet the diverse needs of busy acute inpatient teams, the Safe Steps training included a comprehensive online training package to provide flexible, self‐paced learning that complements face‐to‐face education. The online program introduces foundational concepts such as cultural safety, EI, relational practice and trauma–informed care in a format that is accessible across shifts, roles and locations. Interactive elements, including video demonstrations, reflective practice activities and practical scenarios, were provided to help staff engage with the material in ways that were both meaningful and relevant to their context.

Staff were encouraged to complete the online modules education before the first face‐to‐face education session ensuring all participants began with a shared understanding of the model's core theories and principles. This foundation was intended to allow the education session to focus more deeply on skill development, clinical application and team‐based integration.

Training across all formats was grounded in emotional safety and incorporated multiple learning modes to ensure engagement and retention. Knowles et al. (2014) emphasised the importance of psychologically safe environments and learner‐driven methods in adult education. Te Pou o te Whakaaro (2018) reinforced this by highlighting inclusive, values‐based leadership that supports an emotionally attuned practice environment. Neuroscience further supports this approach, demonstrating that emotional states significantly influence cognitive processing, attention and memory (Immordino‐Yang and Damasio 2007).

Level 1: Foundation‐ Responding to Early Signs of Distress

Level 1 introduces the four steps of relational de‐escalation and emotional safety. It focuses on helping MHNs recognise and respond to the early signs of agitation, such as pacing, restlessness, or irritability, before behaviour escalates. This level aligns with the early stages of the Safe Steps Continuum of Situational Aggression, which describes aggression as a staged process that progresses from calm to more overt forms of escalation, each carrying an underlying message and guiding an appropriate clinical response (see Daguman et al. 2025a). MHNs were supported to respond in ways that preserve dignity, prevent escalation and maintain a therapeutic connection.

It again builds on the four steps and the previous levels to focus on managing high‐risk behaviour while maintaining emotional regulation, connection and professional integrity.

Reflections on Implementation

The implementation of Safe Steps provided insights into the opportunities, challenges and lessons associated with embedding relational approaches in acute mental health care. These reflections are drawn from the experiences of the implementation lead and embedded lead researcher involved in the development and implementation of the model.

Opportunities: Supporting Relational Practice in Acute Settings

A key opportunity was the positioning of mental health nurses as active contributors to practice change. Safe Steps' emphasis on emotional intelligence, therapeutic engagement and structured relational responses provided a shared language for care, which appeared to support consistency across teams.

The phased implementation process also created opportunities for capability building through reflective learning, scenario‐based practice and team discussion. These approaches supported nurses to translate abstract concepts such as empathy and emotional regulation into practical strategies that could be applied during escalating situations. In addition, embedding relational principles into everyday tools such as data logs, visual prompts and reflective meetings contributed to the visibility and normalisation of relational practice.

Challenges: Navigating Complexity in Real‐World Practice

Despite these opportunities, several challenges were encountered during implementation. Acute mental health environments are characterised by high acuity, competing demands and workforce pressures, which can limit the time and capacity for engagement with new models of care. Variability in staff experience, confidence and readiness for relational approaches also influenced the consistency of implementation across units.

A further challenge was the emotional labour inherent in relational practice. Supporting staff to remain emotionally regulated and therapeutically engaged during high‐stress interactions required ongoing reinforcement, leadership support and opportunities for reflection. In addition, while tools such as data logs and dashboards were introduced to support implementation, their consistent use across sites varied, highlighting the complexity of embedding new practices into routine care.

Lessons Learned: Informing Future Implementation

Several key lessons can be drawn from this process. First, embedding relational practice requires more than training alone; it depends on ongoing leadership, reinforcement and integration into everyday systems and processes. Second, creating space for reflective practice is critical in supporting staff to manage the emotional demands of care and sustain relational approaches over time. Third, the implementation highlighted the importance of clearly defining and operationalising key concepts, such as relational safety, to support shared understanding and application in practice. Finally, while the inclusion of consumer perspectives was recognised as essential, peer workers contributed lived experience expertise through training resources, reflective discussions and broader implementation activities. Future implementation may benefit from more structured and sustained involvement of people with lived experience throughout development, restrictive practice review and reflective processes.

These reflections provide a basis for interpreting the implementation of Safe Steps in relation to broader policy and practice considerations.

Discussion

The following discussion interprets these implementation reflections in relation to current policy and practice.

The intention of Safe Steps was to reframe de‐escalation as a therapeutic and relational process rather than a behaviour management task. This approach was intended to align with current policy directions in mental health (NSW Health 2020; World Health Organization and the United Nations [represented by the Office of the United Nations High Commissioner for Human Rights] 2023) and reflect a broader move toward trauma‐informed, human rights‐based care. At the same time, it remains grounded in the realities of acute mental health practice, where nurses are often required to hold the tension between maintaining safety and sustaining therapeutic engagement (Gerace and Muir‐Cochrane 2019; Muir‐Cochrane et al. 2018).

Safe Steps differs from other contemporary de‐escalation interventions in its origins. Safewards (Bowers 2014), the Six Core Strategies (Huckshorn 2004, 2005), and the EDITION intervention (Price, Papastavrou Brooks, et al. 2024) were developed through combinations of research, policy, service‐level reform and co‐produced approaches involving clinicians and people with lived experience. In contrast, Safe Steps emerged directly from the experiences, knowledge and collaboration of frontline MHNs working within acute inpatient settings. The model emphasises the emotional experience of clinicians and provides structured support for emotional intelligence and reflective decision‐making. By focusing on their emotional experiences, the model was intended to acknowledge the emotional labour of nursing. Its phased implementation, with flexible training levels, was intended to be adaptable for use in varied workforce needs and service contexts. Rather than relying solely on policy mandates, we sought to cultivate a therapeutic culture through everyday interpersonal routines, clinical supervision and team‐based learning through Safe Steps.

Another important feature of Safe Steps is its emphasis on creating safety for clinicians. When mental health nurses are supported to process their own emotional dysregulation and are empowered in their roles, they may be better placed to provide therapeutic care for consumers in crisis (Foster et al. 2024; Stevenson and Taylor 2020). This relational interdependence between staff wellbeing and consumer outcomes appears central to the model. Recent research further supports this, showing that staff emotional states, ward culture and relational safety strongly influence the outcomes of de‐escalation (Price, Papastavrou Brooks, et al. 2024).

Taken together, these reflections indicate that Safe Steps could not only be considered a practical intervention, but also a potential shift in how de‐escalation is understood and enacted. It may help bridge the aspirations of policy with the lived experience of mental health nurses, highlighting that safety and therapeutic engagement are interdependent processes sustained through relationships.

These findings suggest that sustaining Safe Steps over time requires attention to several key areas. Leadership continuity is needed to keep priorities, policies and resources aligned with relational care goals, and to address systemic pressures, such as coercive practices and workforce fatigue. These values can be supported by fostering a culture of emotional safety, compassion and therapeutic engagement through recruitment, supervision and performance processes (Cusack et al. 2018; Sweeney et al. 2018). We also suggest that tools such as a huddle debrief for post‐incident review, may complement the restrictive practices reviews used to support the Safe Steps implementation by providing a more immediate and intimate opportunity for reflection after incidents. They can help build team awareness, strengthen psychological safety and encourage shared learning. The inclusion of the consumer voice through debriefs, feedback and co‐produced resources is also important, ensuring the model continues to develop in partnership with those most directly affected.

Conclusion

Safe Steps is a practice‐informed intervention intended to equip MHNs to respond to distress with compassion, emotional regulation and therapeutic consistency. Its design balances structure with flexibility and prioritises the lived experiences of both consumers and MHNs. Through translating relational principles into an implementable model, Safe Steps may support safer and more responsive inpatient mental health care. Grounded in emotionally intelligent, trauma‐informed, recovery‐oriented and culturally aware practice, it suggests that care can be both humane and clinically robust.

Future research is needed to evaluate the model's impact on other forms of restrictive practices and its adaptability across diverse settings. For now, Safe Steps may offer a meaningful contribution to informing implementation of relational and trauma‐informed approaches in acute mental health settings.

Relevance for Clinical Practice

Although de‐escalation is widely promoted in acute mental health settings, clinicians are often given limited guidance on how relational and trauma‐informed principles can be applied during escalating situations. This reflective paper describes the implementation of the Safe Steps model, which translates these principles into a practical approach for MHNs. This model is intended to support the early recognition of distress, emotional regulation, and therapeutic engagement, while outlining strategies that may assist services to embed consistent relational responses within everyday care.
