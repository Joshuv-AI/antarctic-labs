Beyond protected characteristics: rethinking AI fairness through the lens of structural and social determinants of health
Authors: Karol Basta, Uzma Zahid, Mahi Hardalupas, Dijana Vilic
Published: 2026

Fairness refers to the equitable distribution of healthcare resources, opportunities and outcomes, such that no group is systematically disadvantaged.1 While artificial intelligence (AI) systems could support fairer care, growing evidence shows that they often amplify existing inequalities by embedding them within digital systems. These risks arise across the AI lifecycle, from who is represented in data and how conditions are recorded to design choices, evaluation practices and uneven real-world implementation.2 Recent reviews have mapped these issues, synthesising definitions of fairness, sources of bias and both technical and governance-based mitigation approaches.3 However, despite this progress, we argue prevailing approaches to AI fairness remain too narrow to meaningfully address healthcare inequalities.

Current approaches to AI fairness

In the AI literature, fairness is largely framed in technical terms with two common approaches. One approach, group fairness, compares performance across predefined subgroups (eg, based on sex or age). Another approach, individual fairness, instead assesses whether people with similar relevant characteristics experience similar outcomes, regardless of group membership. However, due to challenges around how similarity is defined and operationalised in individual fairness comparisons, group fairness dominates in practice.4

In group fairness, the choice of subgroups and how they are defined is crucial, as these fairness metrics only reveal inequalities between the categories selected. In practice, these categories are drawn from protected characteristics, which originate in antidiscrimination law such as the UK Equality Act 2010, to safeguard individuals from unfair treatment within institutional settings. For example, a scoping review of 467 clinical AI fairness studies showed the majority focused on group-level comparisons across race/ethnicity, sex/gender and age.5 Similarly, a review of electronic health record-based AI models found that all 20 studies assessing bias detection and mitigation were limited to group fairness using a narrow set of attributes based on singular protected characteristics.6

Beyond protected characteristics

Protected characteristics are an essential legal safeguard against discrimination. However, used alone to evaluate fairness in sociotechnical systems such as AI, they risk reducing fairness to legal compliance while diverting attention from deeper social, economic and institutional drivers of inequality.

Research shows that health is shaped less by individual attributes than by the conditions in which people are born, live, work and age. Evidence from the WHO Commission on Social Determinants of Health and large reviews demonstrates that healthcare inequalities reflect overlapping forms of social and institutional exclusion, such as poverty, insecure housing, migration status and communication barriers. Furthermore, the impact of individual attributes captured by protected characteristics on health outcomes is shaped by unequal exposure to social, economic and institutional conditions. An intersectional approach is therefore needed to understand how these factors combine, rather than operate in isolation, to produce unequal health outcomes.7 The relative importance of specific determinants can also vary by context. The same determinant may compound disadvantage in one setting but be mitigated by protective factors such as strong social networks, accessible local services or effective outreach in another.1 8

Health inequalities therefore are layered, intersecting and context-specific. Evaluating AI fairness primarily through singular protected characteristics risks creating a veneer of equity that leaves structural drivers unaddressed and those facing the greatest barriers to care invisible. For example, a hypertension risk model may perform similarly across ethnic groups yet still miss those most likely to remain undiagnosed if deprivation or multimorbidity are not considered as relevant subgroups.9

Bridging the gap

AI fairness approaches should scrutinise how inequalities are produced in real health systems and design for equity from the outset, rather than treating equity as a final compliance check. This begins by defining the equity problem: identifying who experiences delayed care, poorer outcomes, exclusion or reduced visibility within relevant pathways, why these inequalities occur and what actions are needed to reduce them. A data audit can then assess which protected characteristics, social determinants, contextual factors and outcome measures are available. Determinants shaping unfair outcomes may themselves be absent, poorly recorded or ethically sensitive, making them difficult to incorporate into fairness assessments. Making these data gaps visible is therefore an important first step, and further research is needed on when such determinants require direct measurement or can be assessed through linked or contextual evidence. Relevant subgroups should be selected for model evaluation, deployment review and postdeployment monitoring according to the pathway and inequality being addressed, rather than defaulting to available demographic categories. This enables assessment of whether the system is reducing, reproducing or worsening inequalities over time and whether safeguards remain effective.

Existing tools offer practical ways to support this. Health Inequalities Impact Assessments, for example, could be adapted for AI, providing a structured way to anticipate who might benefit or be left behind before deployment. Work by the Ada Lovelace Institute on algorithmic impact assessments for large-scale national medical imaging initiatives illustrates how structured approaches can be applied in practice.10

Patient and public involvement and engagement (PPIE) is a critical component. Although increasingly recognised in principle, most AI projects involve patients late and often in tokenistic roles. Evidence suggests that embedding PPIE upstream shifts focus on accessibility, communication and context, issues frequently overlooked by technical teams.11 Public contributors, particularly from communities affected by the inequality being addressed, should help define the equity problem, identify relevant social and structural determinants, challenge assumptions about proxy variables, advise on acceptable data use, shape deployment safeguards and interpret postdeployment evidence of benefit or harm. This helps give patients and communities meaningful influence over the purpose, design, deployment and governance of AI systems, reducing the risk that PPIE becomes symbolic while technical teams retain control over the most consequential decisions.

Advancing fairness in healthcare AI requires looking beyond protected characteristics to the social and structural determinants shaping data and outcomes. By treating inequity as the core problem and involving patients and the public early, AI can help close rather than widen existing health gaps.
