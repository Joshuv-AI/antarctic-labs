Unifying the Hoover and Gini indices: Analytical, bias, and computational aspects
Roberto Vila 1 , Helton Saulo 1,2 and Felipe Quintino 1 1 Department of Statistics, University of Brasilia, Brasilia, Brazil 2 Department of Economics, Federal University of Pelotas, Pelotas, Brazil

Abstract
We propose a new family of inequality indices that bridges the Hoover index and the Gini coefficient. The measure is defined as the normalized expected absolute value of a convex combination of deviations from the mean and pairwise differences, providing a continuous interpolation between these two classical indices. We establish key theoretical properties, including scale invariance, boundedness, continuity, and compliance with the Pigou–Dalton transfer principle. Analytical representations are derived, allowing explicit evaluation under gamma distributions and leading to closed-form expressions involving incomplete gamma functions. From a statistical perspective, we study the plug-in estimator, obtaining a general expression for its expectation and explicit formulas for its bias under gamma populations. Simulation results indicate good finite-sample performance, with decreasing bias and mean squared error as the sample size increases. An empirical application to GDP per capita data illustrates the practical usefulness of the proposed index as a flexible tool for inequality analysis.



Keywords.
Gamma distribution, Hoover index estimator, Gini coefficient estimator, biased estimator.

Introduction

Measures of economic inequality play a central role in economics, statistics, and the social sciences. Quantifying disparities in income and wealth distributions is essential for understanding economic development, social welfare, and the effects of public policy. Among the most widely used measures of inequality are the Hoover index
(
Hoover, 1936
)
, also known as the Robin Hood index, and the Gini coefficient
(
Gini, 1936
)
. These classical indices have been extensively studied and applied in both theoretical and empirical contexts.

Let
X\geqslant 0
be a random variable representing income with finite mean
\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]>0
. Let
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
be independent copies of
X
. The Hoover index and the Gini coefficient are respectively defined as

H=\frac{1}{2\mu}\,\mathbb{E}|X-\mu|,\quad G=\frac{1}{2\mu}\,\mathbb{E}|X_{1}-X_{2}|.

The Hoover index measures inequality through the mean absolute deviation from the mean and admits a direct redistributive interpretation, representing the proportion of total income that would need to be reallocated to achieve equality. In contrast, the Gini coefficient is based on pairwise differences between individuals and is closely related to the Lorenz curve
(
Lorenz, 1905
)
, providing a global measure of dispersion across the entire distribution.

Although both indices quantify inequality, they emphasize different aspects of dispersion. The Hoover index focuses on deviations from the mean, while the Gini coefficient captures interpersonal differences. As a consequence, these measures may lead to different conclusions in empirical applications, particularly in distributions with high skewness or extreme values
(
Cowell and Flachaire, 2007
)
.

In this paper, we introduce a new family of inequality indices that continuously bridges these two classical measures. For
\lambda\in[0,1]
, we define

I_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{2\mu}\,\mathbb{E}\left|(1-\lambda)(X_{1}-\mu)+\lambda(X_{1}-X_{2})\right|.

(1)

By construction,
I_{0}=H
and
I_{1}=G
, so that the proposed family provides a continuous interpolation between the Hoover and Gini indices. For intermediate values of
\lambda
, the index combines dispersion around the mean with pairwise differences, yielding a flexible framework for inequality measurement.

The proposed index offers several advantages. First, it allows for a unified treatment of two classical measures within a single parametric family. Second, it provides a tool for sensitivity analysis with respect to the concept of inequality itself, enabling researchers to assess how conclusions depend on the relative importance assigned to mean deviations versus interpersonal differences. Third, the index admits tractable analytical representations that facilitate both theoretical analysis and statistical inference.

The contributions of this paper are threefold. First, we establish the main theoretical properties of the proposed index, including scale invariance, boundedness, continuity in
\lambda
, and compliance with the Pigou–Dalton transfer principle. Second, we derive analytical representations that allow explicit evaluation of the index for gamma distributions in terms of incomplete gamma functions. Third, we study the statistical properties of the natural plug-in estimator, deriving a general expression for its expectation based on Laplace transform techniques and exponential tilting, and obtaining explicit formulas for its bias under gamma populations.

The remainder of the paper is organized as follows.
In Section
2
we introduce the proposed index and study its main theoretical properties.
In Section
3
we present an auxiliary lemma based on exponential tilting and Laplace transform techniques that will be used in subsequent derivations.
In Section
4
we derive general expressions for the expected value of the plug-in estimator of the index for non-negative populations.
In Section
5
we specialize these results to gamma distributions and obtain an explicit expression for the bias of the estimator.
In Section
6
we report results from a Monte Carlo simulation study. Section
7
presents an empirical application. Finally, Section
8
concludes the paper.

2
Properties of the index

In this section, for the new index
I_{\lambda}
defined in (
1
), we present a general integral form and a closed-form expression for gamma families.
Additionally, we list several properties, such as boundary cases, non-negativity, range, an upper bound index, scale invariance, and continuity in
\lambda
.

Proposition 2.1
.

The index
I_{\lambda}
of a non-negative, non-degenerate random variable
X
with finite mean
\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu>0
can be expressed as

\displaystyle I_{\lambda}=1-{1\over\mu}\int_{0}^{\infty}\overline{F}_{X}(t^{-})\overline{F}_{\lambda X_{2}+(1-\lambda)\mu}(t^{-})\,{\rm d}t={1\over\mu}\int_{0}^{\infty}F_{X}(t^{-})\overline{F}_{\lambda X_{2}+(1-\lambda)\mu}(t^{-})\,{\rm d}t,

where
F_{X}
denotes the distribution function of
X
,

\overline{F}_{X}(x)=1-F_{X}(x)
, and
F_{X}(x^{-})=\lim_{u\uparrow x}F_{X}(u)=\mathbb{P}(X<x)
.

Proof.

Since

(1-\lambda)(X_{1}-\mu)+\lambda(X_{1}-X_{2})=X_{1}-\big[\lambda X_{2}+(1-\lambda)\mu\big],

and using the identity

\displaystyle|y_{1}-y_{2}|=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\mathbf{1}_{\{y_{1}\geqslant t\}}+\mathbf{1}_{\{y_{2}\geqslant t\}}-2\,\mathbf{1}_{\{y_{1}\geqslant t\}}\mathbf{1}_{\{y_{2}\geqslant t\}}\right){\rm d}t,\quad y_{1},y_{2}\geqslant 0,

(2)

together with Tonelli’s theorem and the independence of
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
, we obtain

\displaystyle I_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{2\mu}\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\overline{F}_{X}(t^{-})+\overline{F}_{\lambda X_{2}+(1-\lambda)\mu}(t^{-})-2\,\overline{F}_{X}(t^{-})\,\overline{F}_{\lambda X_{2}+(1-\lambda)\mu}(t^{-})\right]{\rm d}t.

Finally, using the well-known identity

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[X]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\overline{F}_{X}(x^{-})\,{\rm d}x,\quad X\geqslant 0,

(3)

the result follows.
∎

Proposition 2.2
.

The index
I_{\lambda}
, with
\lambda\in(0,1]
, of a gamma random variable
X\sim\text{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)

is given by

I_{\lambda}=\frac{(1-\lambda)^{\alpha}\alpha^{\alpha-1}\exp\{-(1-\lambda)\alpha\}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}+\lambda\,\frac{\Gamma(\alpha,(1-\lambda)\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}-{1\over\alpha}\int_{(1-\lambda)\alpha}^{\infty}{\Gamma(\alpha,t)\over\Gamma(\alpha)}\,{\Gamma\left(\alpha,{t-(1-\lambda)\alpha\over\lambda}\right)\over\Gamma(\alpha)}\,{\rm d}t,

where
\Gamma(s,x)=\int_{x}^{\infty}t^{s-1}\exp\{-t\}{\rm d}t
is the upper incomplete gamma function and
\Gamma(s)=\lim_{x\to 0^{+}}\Gamma(s,x)
is the (complete) gamma function.

Proof.

Since
X\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)
, we have
\overline{F}_{X}(x)=\Gamma(\alpha,\beta x)/\Gamma(\alpha)
,

\displaystyle\overline{F}_{\lambda X_{2}+(1-\lambda)\mu}(t)=\begin{cases}1,&t\leqslant(1-\lambda)\mu,\\
\overline{F}_{X}\left({t-(1-\lambda)\mu\over\lambda}\right),&t>(1-\lambda)\mu,\end{cases}

and

\mu=\mathbb{E}[X]={\alpha}/{\beta}
.
Applying Proposition
2.1
and the scale property of the gamma distribution, we have

\displaystyle I_{\lambda}=1-{\beta\over\alpha}\left[\int_{0}^{(1-\lambda)\alpha/\beta}{\Gamma(\alpha,\beta t)\over\Gamma(\alpha)}\,{\rm d}t+\int_{(1-\lambda)\alpha/\beta}^{\infty}{\Gamma(\alpha,\beta t)\over\Gamma(\alpha)}\,{\Gamma\left(\alpha,{\beta t-(1-\lambda)\alpha\over\lambda}\right)\over\Gamma(\alpha)}\,{\rm d}t\right].

Finally, applying the change of variables
t\mapsto\beta t
together with the identity

\int_{0}^{(1-\lambda)\alpha}\frac{\Gamma(\alpha,t)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\,{\rm d}t=\alpha-\frac{(1-\lambda)^{\alpha}\alpha^{\alpha}\exp\{-(1-\lambda)\alpha\}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}-\alpha\lambda\,\frac{\Gamma(\alpha,(1-\lambda)\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)},

the proof follows.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

Note that
I_{\lambda}
is scale invariant and therefore does not depend on the rate parameter
\beta
(see Proposition
2.2
).

Remark 2.4
.

Since

\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\mathbf{1}_{\{t>(1-\lambda)\alpha\}}\,\frac{\Gamma\left(\alpha,\frac{t-(1-\lambda)\alpha}{\lambda}\right)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}=\begin{cases}1,&t=\alpha,\\
0,&t\neq\alpha,\end{cases}

it follows from Proposition
2.2
that

H=\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}I_{\lambda}=\frac{\alpha^{\alpha-1}\exp\{-\alpha\}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}.

The above result was recently established as Proposition 2.4 in
Vila and Saulo, 2026
.

Remark 2.5
.

Letting
\lambda\to 1
in Proposition
2.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle G=\lim_{\lambda\to 1}I_{\lambda}=1-{1\over\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}{\Gamma^{2}(\alpha,t)\over\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}\,{\rm d}t=\frac{\Gamma\left(\alpha+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)}.

(4)

The above result is well documented in the literature; see, for instance,
Baydil et. al, 2025
.

Remark 2.6
.

Using the triangle inequality,

\left|(1-\lambda)(X-\mu)+\lambda(X_{1}-X_{2})\right|\leqslant(1-\lambda)|X-\mu|+\lambda|X_{1}-X_{2}|.

Taking expectations yields

\displaystyle I_{\lambda}\leqslant(1-\lambda)H+\lambda G\equiv J_{\lambda}.

(5)

The
J_{\lambda}
in the right-hand side of inequality (
5
) is much easier to manipulate mathematically than
I_{\lambda}
defined in (
1
). A natural question, then, is why not use the right-hand side as the index, since it also bridges the Hoover and Gini indices.
In Section
6
, we will show that the bias of the natural estimator of
I_{\lambda}
has a smaller bias than the natural estimator of
J_{\lambda}
.

Remark 2.7
.

The index
I_{\lambda}
satisfies the following properties:

1.

Note that
I_{0}=H
and
I_{1}=G.

Thus, the proposed family smoothly connects the Hoover and Gini measures.

2.

Since
H\leqslant G
, the previous inequality (
5
) implies that the index remains bounded
between zero and the Gini coefficient. That is,

0\leqslant I_{\lambda}\leqslant G\leqslant 1.

Moreover,
if
X=c
almost surely for some constant
c
, then

I_{\lambda}=0.

3.

For any constant
a>0
, let
Y=aX
. Then

I_{\lambda}(Y)=I_{\lambda}(X).

Hence, the index depends only on relative income differences. Furthermore,
if
Y=X+c
with
c>0
, then

I_{\lambda}(Y)=[{\mu}/({\mu+c})]I_{\lambda}(X).

4.

The function
\lambda\mapsto I_{\lambda}
is continuous on
[0,1]
.

5.

Since
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are exchangeable, replacing them does not change the
value of
I_{\lambda}
. In other words, the index depends only on the distribution of
X
.

6.

Since

\mathbb{E}|Y|=2\textrm{Cov}(Y,\mathbf{1}_{\{Y>0\}})

for any centered variable
Y
, with
Y=(1-\lambda)(X_{1}-\mu)+\lambda(X_{1}-X_{2})
, we obtaing

I_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{\mu}\,\mathrm{Cov}\left((1-\lambda)(X_{1}-\mu)+\lambda(X_{1}-X_{2}),\mathbf{1}_{\{(1-\lambda)(X_{1}-\mu)+\lambda(X_{1}-X_{2})>0\}}\right).

7.

If
X^{\prime}
denotes the income distribution after the transfer, then

I_{\lambda}(X^{\prime})\leqslant I_{\lambda}(X)
(Pigou–Dalton transfer principle).

3
An auxiliary lemma

The following auxiliary lemma will be instrumental in deriving the results of Section
4
.

Lemma 3.1
.

If
W
,
Y
, and
Z
are independent, non-degenerate, nonnegative random variables, then for

a,b,c\geqslant 0
and
z>0
,

\mathbb{E}\left[\left|aW+bY-cZ\right|\exp\{-z(W+Y+Z)\}\right]\\[5.69046pt]
=\mathscr{L}_{W}(z)\mathscr{L}_{Y}(z)\mathscr{L}_{Z}(z)\left\{a\mathbb{E}[W_{z}]+b\mathbb{E}[Y_{z}]+c\mathbb{E}[Z_{z}]\right\}\boldsymbol{G}(aW_{z}+bY_{z},cZ_{z}),

where
\mathscr{L}_{W}(z)=\mathbb{E}[\exp\{-zW\}]
is the Laplace transform of
W
, and for independent random variables
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{G}(Y_{1},Y_{2})=\frac{\mathbb{E}\lvert Y_{1}-Y_{2}\rvert}{\mathbb{E}[Y_{1}]+\mathbb{E}[Y_{2}]}

(6)

denotes the
normalized mean absolute difference
.
In the particular case where
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
are independent copies of a random variable
Y
,
the quantity
\boldsymbol{G}(Y_{1},Y_{2})
reduces to the Gini coefficient
G=G(Y)
of
Y
.

Furthermore, for each
z>0
,
W_{z}
denotes the exponentially tilted (or Esscher-transformed)
version of
W

(
Butler, 2007
)
, whose cumulative distribution function (CDF) is given by

\displaystyle F_{W_{z}}(t)=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[\mathds{1}_{\{0\leqslant W\leqslant t\}}\exp\{-zW\}\right]}{\mathscr{L}_{W}(z)},\quad t\geqslant 0.

(7)

In the above, we assume that the Laplace transforms and expectations involved exist and are finite.

Proof.

Since
W
,
Y
, and
Z
are independent nonnegative random variables, for any
Borel-measurable function
g:(0,\infty)^{3}\to\mathbb{R}
it follows from the
definition of the exponentially tilted distribution that

\mathbb{E}[g(W_{z},Y_{z},Z_{z})]=\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[g(W,Y,Z)\exp\{-z(W+Y+Z)\}\right]}{\mathscr{L}_{W}(z)\mathscr{L}_{Y}(z)\mathscr{L}_{Z}(z)}.

Hence,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|aW+bY-cZ\right|\exp\{-z(W+Y+Z)\}\right]=\mathscr{L}_{W}(z)\mathscr{L}_{Y}(z)\mathscr{L}_{Z}(z)\,\mathbb{E}\left|aW_{z}+bY_{z}-cZ_{z}\right|.

Since
aW_{z}+bY_{z}
and
cZ_{z}
are independent, the result follows directly from the definition of the normalized mean absolute difference between
aW_{z}+bY_{z}
and
cZ_{z}
.
∎

4
Expectation of the estimator
\widehat{I}_{\lambda}

In this section we derive simple expressions for the expected value of the estimator
\widehat{I}_{\lambda}
for general non-negative-support populations (Theorem
4.2
). This result allows us to derive the estimator’s bias in the gamma case (see Corollary
5.2
in Section
5
).

Let
X_{1},\dots,X_{n}
be a sample from the distribution of
X
with sample mean

\overline{X}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}.

The plug-in estimator of
I_{\lambda}
is obtained by replacing
\mu
with

\overline{X}
and the expectation with the corresponding sample average. Thus, for
\lambda\in[0,1]
,

\displaystyle\widehat{I}_{\lambda}=\frac{1}{2n(n-1)\overline{X}}\sum_{1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant n}|(1-\lambda)(X_{i}-\overline{X})+\lambda(X_{i}-X_{j})|\,\mathds{1}_{\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}>0\}}.

(8)

For
\lambda=0
, this estimator reduces to the Hoover estimator
(
Vila and Saulo, 2026
)

\widehat{I}_{0}\equiv\widehat{H}=\frac{1}{2n\overline{X}}\sum_{i=1}^{n}|X_{i}-\overline{X}|\,\mathds{1}_{\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}>0\}},

(9)

while for
\lambda=1
it becomes the upward-adjusted Gini estimator
(
Deltas, 2003
)

\widehat{I}_{1}\equiv\widehat{G}=\frac{1}{n(n-1)\overline{X}}\sum_{1\leqslant i<j\leqslant n}|X_{i}-X_{j}|\,\mathds{1}_{\{\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}>0\}}.

(10)

Remark 4.1
.

Using the triangle inequality,

|(1-\lambda)(X_{i}-\overline{X})+\lambda(X_{i}-X_{j})|\leqslant(1-\lambda)|X_{i}-\overline{X}|+\lambda|X_{i}-X_{j}|.

Summing over all
i,j
such that
1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant n
, and then multiplying by
\big[2n(n-1)\,\overline{X}\big]^{-1}
, yields

\displaystyle\widehat{I}_{\lambda}\leqslant(1-\lambda)\widehat{H}+\lambda\widehat{G}\equiv\widehat{J}_{\lambda}.

(11)

Theorem 4.2
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
be independent copies of a non-negative and non-degenerate random variable
X
with finite mean
\mathbb{E}[X]=\mu>0
. The following holds:

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{I}_{\lambda}\right]=n\left(1+{\lambda-1\over n}\right)\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathscr{L}_{X}^{n}(z)\mathbb{E}[X_{z}]\boldsymbol{G}\left({1-\lambda\over n}\sum_{k=3}^{n}(X_{k})_{z}+\left[\lambda+{1-\lambda\over n}\right](X_{2})_{z},\left[1-{1-\lambda\over n}\right](X_{1})_{z}\right){\rm d}z,

where
\mathscr{L}_{X}(z)=\mathbb{E}\left[\exp\{-zX\}\right]
denotes the Laplace transform of
X
, and
\boldsymbol{G}(Y_{1},Y_{2})
is the normalized mean absolute difference between
Y_{1}
and
Y_{2}
stated in (
6
). For each
z>0
,
F_{X_{z}}
is the CDF of the exponentially tilted random variable
X_{z}
defined in (
7
).
Moreover, throughout the above derivations, it is implicitly assumed that all Lebesgue–Stieltjes and improper integrals involved are well defined.

Proof.

Using the well-known identity

x\int_{0}^{\infty}\exp\{-zx\}\,{\rm d}z=1,\,x>0,

with
x=\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2(n-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}\sum_{1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant n}|(1-\lambda)(X_{i}-\overline{X})+\lambda(X_{i}-X_{j})|\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2(n-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}\sum_{1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant n}\left|{(1-\lambda)\over{n}}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq i,j\end{subarray}}^{n}X_{k}-{\left[1-{(1-\lambda)\over n}\right]}X_{i}+{\left[\lambda+{(1-\lambda)\over n}\right]}X_{j}\right|\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(n-1)}\mskip-3.0mu\mskip-3.0mu\sum_{1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant n}\mskip-3.0mu\mskip-3.0mu\mathbb{E}\left[\int_{0}^{\infty}\left|{(1-\lambda)\over n}\mskip-3.0mu\mskip-3.0mu\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq i,j\end{subarray}}^{n}\mskip-3.0muX_{k}\mskip-3.0mu-\left[1-{(1-\lambda)\over n}\right]X_{i}\mskip-3.0mu+\left[\lambda+{(1-\lambda)\over n}\right]X_{j}\right|\exp\left\{-z\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq i,j\end{subarray}}^{n}X_{k}\mskip-3.0mu+X_{i}\mskip-3.0mu+X_{j}\right)\right\}{\rm d}z\right]

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2(n-1)}\mskip-3.0mu\mskip-3.0mu\sum_{1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant n}\mskip-3.0mu\int_{0}^{\infty}\mskip-3.0mu\mskip-3.0mu\mathbb{E}\mskip-3.0mu\left[\left|{(1-\lambda)\over n}\mskip-3.0mu\mskip-3.0mu\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq i,j\end{subarray}}^{n}X_{k}\mskip-3.0mu-\left[1-{(1-\lambda)\over n}\right]X_{i}\mskip-3.0mu+\left[\lambda+{(1-\lambda)\over n}\right]X_{j}\right|\exp\left\{-z\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq i,j\end{subarray}}^{n}X_{k}\mskip-3.0mu+X_{i}\mskip-3.0mu+X_{j}\right)\right\}\right]{\rm d}z,

(12)

where in the last equality Tonelli’s theorem was used.

Since
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are independent and identically distributed (i.i.d.), it is clear that, for each
i=1,\ldots,n
, the variables
\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq i,j\end{subarray}}^{n}X_{k}
,
X_{i}
and
X_{j}
are independent, and

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}k=1\\
k\neq i,j\end{subarray}}^{n}X_{k}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{k=3}^{n}X_{k},

where
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}
denotes equality in distribution. Therefore, from (
4
) we have

\mathbb{E}\left[\frac{1}{2(n-1)\sum_{i=1}^{n}X_{i}}\sum_{1\leqslant i\neq j\leqslant n}|(1-\lambda)(X_{i}-\overline{X})+\lambda(X_{i}-X_{j})|\right]\\[5.69046pt]
=\frac{n}{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{E}\left[\left|{(1-\lambda)\over n}\sum_{k=3}^{n}X_{k}-\left[1-{(1-\lambda)\over n}\right]X_{1}+\left[\lambda+{(1-\lambda)\over n}\right]X_{2}\right|\exp\left\{-z\left(\sum_{k=3}^{n}X_{k}+X_{1}+X_{2}\right)\right\}\right]{\rm d}z.

(13)

By using Lemma
3.1
with

\displaystyle W=\sum_{k=3}^{n}X_{k},\quad Y=X_{1},\quad Z=X_{2},\quad a={(1-\lambda)\over n},\quad b=1-{(1-\lambda)\over n},\quad c=\lambda+{(1-\lambda)\over n},

(14)

we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left|{(1-\lambda)\over n}\sum_{k=3}^{n}X_{k}-\left[1-{(1-\lambda)\over n}\right]X_{1}+\left[\lambda+{(1-\lambda)\over n}\right]X_{2}\right|\exp\left\{-z\left(\sum_{k=3}^{n}X_{k}+X_{1}+X_{2}\right)\right\}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathscr{L}_{X}^{n}(z)\left\{{(1-\lambda)\over n}\,\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=3}^{n}X_{k}\right)_{z}\right]+(\lambda+1)\mathbb{E}[X_{z}]\right\}

\displaystyle\times\boldsymbol{G}\left({(1-\lambda)\over n}\left(\sum_{k=3}^{n}X_{k}\right)_{z}+\left[\lambda+{(1-\lambda)\over n}\right](X_{2})_{z},\left[{1-{(1-\lambda)\over n}}\right](X_{1})_{z}\right).

(15)

Since
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are i.i.d. with the same distribution as

, we have

\displaystyle\left(\sum_{k=3}^{n}X_{k}\right)_{z}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle d}}{{=}}\sum_{k=3}^{n}(X_{k})_{z},\quad\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\sum_{k=3}^{n}X_{k}\right)_{z}\right]=(n-2)\mathbb{E}[X_{z}].

(16)

Finally, the proof follows by combining (

), (
4
), and (

), along with the definition (

) of
\widehat{I}_{\lambda}
.

Corollary 4.3
.

Under the assumptions of Theorem
4.2
, it holds that

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{I}_{\lambda}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathscr{L}_{X}^{n}(z)\left\{(n-1+\lambda)\mathbb{E}\left[X_{z}\right]-\int_{0}^{\infty}\overline{F}_{{(1-\lambda)}(\sum_{k=2}^{n}X_{k})_{z}+n\lambda(X_{2})_{z}}(t^{-})\overline{F}_{(n-1+\lambda)X_{z}}\left(t^{-}\right){\rm d}t\right\}{\rm d}z.

Proof.

For simplicity in the proof of this result, we adopt the notation for
W
,
Y
,
Z
,
a
,
b
, and
c
introduced in (
14
).

Using the identity (
2
) together with the fact that
aW_{z}+bY_{z}
and
cZ_{z}
remain independent non–negative random variables, Tonelli’s theorem yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|aW_{z}+bY_{z}-cZ_{z}\right|=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left[\overline{F}_{aW_{z}+bY_{z}}(t^{-})+\overline{F}_{cZ_{z}}\left(t^{-}\right)-2\,\overline{F}_{aW_{z}+bY_{z}}(t^{-})\overline{F}_{cZ_{z}}\left(t^{-}\right)\right]{\rm d}t.

Using
the well known representation (
3
),
the previous identity can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left|aW_{z}+bY_{z}-cZ_{z}\right|=a\mathbb{E}[W_{z}]+c\mathbb{E}[Y_{z}]+b\mathbb{E}[Z_{z}]-2\int_{0}^{\infty}\overline{F}_{aW_{z}+bY_{z}}(t^{-})\overline{F}_{cZ_{z}}\left(t^{-}\right){\rm d}t.

Hence,

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{G}(aW_{z}+bY_{z},cZ_{z})={a\mathbb{E}[W_{z}]+c\mathbb{E}[Y_{z}]+b\mathbb{E}[Z_{z}]-2\int_{0}^{\infty}\overline{F}_{aW_{z}+bY_{z}}(t^{-})\overline{F}_{cZ_{z}}\left(t^{-}\right){\rm d}t\over a\mathbb{E}[W_{z}]+c\mathbb{E}[Y_{z}]+b\mathbb{E}[Z_{z}]}.

(17)

Combining Theorem
4.2
with (
17
) completes the proof.
∎

Remark 4.4
.

It is important to note that, depending on the distribution specified for
X
, the Laplace transform in Theorem
4.2
and Corollary
4.3
may constrain the domain over which the improper integral is well-defined.

Remark 4.5
.

When
\lambda\to 0
, Corollary
4.3
yields

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{H}\right]=\lim_{\lambda\to 0}\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{I}_{\lambda}\right]=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathscr{L}_{X}^{n}(z)\left\{(n-1)\mathbb{E}[X_{z}]-\int_{0}^{\infty}\overline{F}_{(\sum_{k=2}^{n}X_{k})_{z}}(t^{-})\overline{F}_{(n-1)X_{z}}(t^{-})\,{\rm d}t\right\}{\rm d}z.

An equivalent formula was recently obtained in Theorem 4.1 of
Vila and Saulo, 2026
.

Remark 4.6
.

Setting
\lambda\to 1
in Theorem
4.2
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{G}\right]=\lim_{\lambda\to 1}\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{I}_{\lambda}\right]=n\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathscr{L}_{X}^{n}(z)\mathbb{E}[X_{z}]\boldsymbol{G}((X_{2})_{z},(X_{1})_{z}){\rm d}z.

Since
(X_{1})_{z}
and
(X_{2})_{z}
are i.i.d., because
X_{1}
and
X_{2}
are i.i.d. copies of
X
, the normalized mean absolute difference
\boldsymbol{G}((X_{2})_{z},(X_{1})_{z})
reduces to the Gini coefficient
G(X_{z})
. Therefore, the above identity can be written as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{G}\right]=n\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathscr{L}_{X}^{n}(z)\mathbb{E}[X_{z}]G(X_{z})\,{\rm d}z.

This representation has recently appeared in Theorem 3.2 of
Vila and Saulo, 2025
.

Remark 4.7
.

As an application of Corollary
4.3
, in the next section we derive a simple expression for
\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{I}_{\lambda}\right]
in the case of gamma distributions (including the Exponential, Erlang, Chi-square, and Scaled Gamma distributions). The gamma distribution is a natural choice due to its flexibility and the fact that its tilted versions, sums, and differences lead to distributions that are well known in the literature.

5
Bias of the estimator
\widehat{I}_{\lambda}
under gamma populations

Let
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
be i.i.d. random variables with

X\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta),

where
\alpha>0
is the shape parameter and
\beta>0
is the rate parameter.
The Laplace transform of
X
is given by

\mathscr{L}_{X}(z)=\left(\frac{\beta}{\beta+z}\right)^{\alpha},\quad z>-\beta.

Under exponential tilting with parameter
z>0
, it is well known that

\displaystyle X_{z}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta+z).

(18)

Unless otherwise specified, in the remainder of this section we assume that
\lambda\in[0,1)
.

From (
18
) and the scale property of the gamma distribution, we obtain

(n-1+\lambda)X_{z}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}\left(\alpha,{\beta+z\over n-1+\lambda}\right).

Moreover, noting that

\sum_{k=2}^{n}X_{k}=\sum_{k=3}^{n}X_{k}+X_{2},

we obtain

\displaystyle(1-\lambda)\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}X_{k}\right)_{z}+n\lambda(X_{2})_{z}=(1-\lambda)\left(\sum_{k=3}^{n}X_{k}\right)_{z}+[1+(n-1)\lambda](X_{2})_{z}.

(19)

Since
X_{1},X_{2},\ldots
are independent,

\left(\sum_{k=3}^{n}X_{k}\right)_{z}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}((n-2)\alpha,\beta+z),\quad(X_{2})_{z}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta+z).

Hence,

\displaystyle(1-\lambda)\left(\sum_{k=3}^{n}X_{k}\right)_{z}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}\left((n-2)\alpha,{\beta+z\over 1-\lambda}\right),\quad[1+(n-1)\lambda](X_{2})_{z}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}\left(\alpha,{\beta+z\over 1+(n-1)\lambda}\right).

(20)

Definition 5.1
.

Let
X_{1},X_{2}
be independent random variables such that

X_{i}\sim\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha_{i},\beta_{i}),\ \alpha_{i}>0,\;\beta_{i}>0,\;i=1,2,

where
\alpha_{i}
and
\beta_{i}
denote the shape and rate parameters, respectively.
Then the random variable

S=X_{1}+X_{2}

is said to follow a
generalized hypoexponential distribution
(GHypo)
(
Moschopoulos, 1985
)
, and we write

S\sim\textrm{GHypo}(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{2}).

Its PDF is given by

f_{\text{GHypo}(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{2})}(t)=\frac{\beta_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\beta_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}}{\Gamma(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2})}\ t^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}-1}\exp\{-\beta_{2}t\}\,{}_{1}F_{1}\mskip-3.0mu\left(\alpha_{1};\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2};(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})t\right),\quad t>0,

where
{}_{1}F_{1}
denotes the confluent hypergeometric function of Kummer. Its corresponding CDF is denoted by

F_{\text{GHypo}(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{2})}
and can be obtained by using the identity

\int_{0}^{t}x^{\nu-1}\exp\{-\mu x\}\,{}_{1}F_{1}(a;\nu;cx)\,{\rm d}x=\frac{t^{\nu}}{\nu}\,\exp\{-\mu t\}\,{}_{1}F_{1}\mskip-3.0mu\left(a;\nu+1;(c-\mu)t\right),\quad\nu>0,

yielding

\displaystyle F_{\mathrm{GHypo}(\alpha_{1},\beta_{1},\alpha_{2},\beta_{2})}(t)=\frac{\beta_{1}^{\alpha_{1}}\beta_{2}^{\alpha_{2}}}{\Gamma(\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+1)}\,t^{\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}}\exp\{-\beta_{2}t\}\,{}_{1}F_{1}\mskip-3.0mu\left(\alpha_{1};\alpha_{1}+\alpha_{2}+1;(\beta_{2}-\beta_{1})t\right),\quad t>0.

(21)

Combining Definition
5.1
with (
19
) and (
20
), we obtain

\displaystyle(1-\lambda)\left(\sum_{k=2}^{n}X_{k}\right)_{z}+n\lambda(X_{2})_{z}\sim\text{GHypo}\left((n-2)\alpha,{\beta+z\over 1-\lambda},\alpha,{\beta+z\over 1+(n-1)\lambda}\right).

Applying Corollary
4.3
, for
\lambda\in[0,1)
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\widehat{I}_{\lambda}\right]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\beta}{\beta+z}\right)^{n\alpha}\left\{{(n-1+\lambda)\alpha\over\beta+z}-\int_{0}^{\infty}\overline{F}_{\text{GHypo}\left((n-2)\alpha,{\beta+z\over 1-\lambda},\alpha,{\beta+z\over 1+(n-1)\lambda}\right)}(t)\,{\Gamma\left(\alpha,{(\beta+z)t\over n-1+\lambda}\right)\over\Gamma(\alpha)}\,{\rm d}t\right\}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{\beta}{\beta+z}\right)^{n\alpha}\left\{{(n-1+\lambda)\alpha\over\beta+z}-\int_{0}^{\infty}\overline{F}_{\text{GHypo}\left((n-2)\alpha,{1\over 1-\lambda},\alpha,{1\over 1+(n-1)\lambda}\right)}((\beta+z)t)\,{\Gamma\left(\alpha,{(\beta+z)t\over n-1+\lambda}\right)\over\Gamma(\alpha)}\,{\rm d}t\right\}{\rm d}z

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\{{\left(1+{\lambda-1\over n}\right)\alpha}-{1\over n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\overline{F}_{\text{GHypo}\left((n-2)\alpha,{1\over 1-\lambda},\alpha,{1\over 1+(n-1)\lambda}\right)}(t)\,{\Gamma\left(\alpha,{t\over n-1+\lambda}\right)\over\Gamma(\alpha)}\,{\rm d}t\right\},

(22)

where in the second equality we used the scale property of the GHypo distribution, and in the third equality the change of variable
t\mapsto(\beta+z)t
was performed.

Remark 5.1
.

Because the estimator
\widehat{I}_{\lambda}
is scale invariant, its expectation in Item (
5
) does not depend on the rate parameter
\beta
.

Therefore, by combining Proposition
2.2
and (
5
), we have:

Corollary 5.2
.

For
n\geqslant 2
and
\lambda\in(0,1)
, the bias of
\widehat{I}_{\lambda}
relative to
I_{\lambda}
, denoted by
\text{Bias}(\widehat{I}_{\lambda},I_{\lambda})
, is given by

\text{Bias}(\widehat{I}_{\lambda},I_{\lambda})=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\{{\left(1+{\lambda-1\over n}\right)\alpha}-{1\over n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\overline{F}_{\text{GHypo}\left((n-2)\alpha,{1\over 1-\lambda},\alpha,{1\over 1+(n-1)\lambda}\right)}(t)\,{\Gamma\left(\alpha,{t\over n-1+\lambda}\right)\over\Gamma(\alpha)}\,{\rm d}t\right\}\\[5.69046pt]
-\left\{\frac{(1-\lambda)^{\alpha}\alpha^{\alpha-1}\exp\{-(1-\lambda)\alpha\}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}+\lambda\,\frac{\Gamma(\alpha,(1-\lambda)\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}-{1\over\alpha}\int_{(1-\lambda)\alpha}^{\infty}{\Gamma(\alpha,t)\over\Gamma(\alpha)}\,{\Gamma\left(\alpha,{t-(1-\lambda)\alpha\over\lambda}\right)\over\Gamma(\alpha)}\,{\rm d}t\right\},

or, equivalently, by using (
21
),

\text{Bias}(\widehat{I}_{\lambda},I_{\lambda})=\frac{\left(\frac{1}{1-\lambda}\right)^{(n-2)\alpha}\left[\frac{1}{1+(n-1)\lambda}\right]^{\alpha}}{n\Gamma((n-1)\alpha+1)\Gamma(\alpha+1)}\\[5.69046pt]
\times\int_{0}^{\infty}t^{(n-1)\alpha}\exp\left\{-\frac{t}{1+(n-1)\lambda}\right\}\Gamma\left(\alpha,{t\over n-1+\lambda}\right)\,{}_{1}F_{1}\mskip-3.0mu\left((n-2)\alpha;(n-1)\alpha+1;-\frac{n\lambda t}{(1-\lambda)(1+(n-1)\lambda)}\right){\rm d}t\\[5.69046pt]
-\left\{\frac{(1-\lambda)^{\alpha}\alpha^{\alpha-1}\exp\{-(1-\lambda)\alpha\}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}+\lambda\,\frac{\Gamma(\alpha,(1-\lambda)\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}-{1\over\alpha}\int_{(1-\lambda)\alpha}^{\infty}{\Gamma(\alpha,t)\over\Gamma(\alpha)}\,{\Gamma\left(\alpha,{t-(1-\lambda)\alpha\over\lambda}\right)\over\Gamma(\alpha)}\,{\rm d}t\right\}.

Remark 5.3
.

Since

\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}F_{\text{GHypo}\left((n-2)\alpha,{1\over 1-\lambda},\alpha,{1\over 1+(n-1)\lambda}\right)}(t)=F_{\mathrm{GHypo}\big((n-2)\alpha,1,\alpha,1\big)}(t)=F_{\mathrm{Gamma}((n-1)\alpha,1)}(t),

combining Corollary
5.2
with Remark
2.4
yields

\text{Bias}(\widehat{H},H)=\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\text{Bias}(\widehat{I}_{\lambda},I_{\lambda})\\[5.69046pt]
=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\{\left(1-\frac{1}{n}\right)\alpha-\frac{1}{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma\left((n-1)\alpha,t\right)}{\Gamma((n-1)\alpha)}\,\frac{\Gamma\left(\alpha,\frac{t}{n-1}\right)}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\,\mathrm{d}t\right\}-\frac{\alpha^{\alpha-1}\exp\{-\alpha\}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}.

Note that the above identity recently appeared in Corollary 4.5 of
Vila and Saulo, 2026
.

Remark 5.4
.

Since

\lim_{\lambda\to 1^{-}}F_{\text{GHypo}\left((n-2)\alpha,\frac{1}{1-\lambda},\alpha,\frac{1}{1+(n-1)\lambda}\right)}(t)=F_{{\rm Gamma}(\alpha,{1\over n})}(t),

combining Corollary
5.2
with Remark
2.5
gives

\displaystyle\text{Bias}(\widehat{G},G)=\lim_{\lambda\to 1^{-}}\text{Bias}(\widehat{I}_{\lambda},I_{\lambda})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\alpha}\left\{\alpha-{1\over n}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma^{2}\left(\alpha,\frac{t}{n}\right)}{\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}\,{\rm d}t\right\}-\frac{\Gamma\left(\alpha+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)}

\displaystyle=1-{1\over\alpha}\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{\Gamma^{2}\left(\alpha,t\right)}{\Gamma^{2}(\alpha)}\,{\rm d}t-\frac{\Gamma\left(\alpha+\tfrac{1}{2}\right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)}=0,

where the third equality follows from the change of variables
t\mapsto t/n
,
and the last equality follows from identity (
4
).

The above result on the unbiasedness of the Gini coefficient estimator
\widehat{G}
for gamma populations is well established in the literature; see, for example,
Baydil et. al, 2025
;
Vila and Saulo, 2025
.

6
Monte Carlo simulation study

In this section, we assess the finite-sample performance of the plug-in estimator
\widehat{I}_{\lambda}
defined in Equation (
8
) by means of a Monte Carlo simulation study. The analysis is conducted under gamma populations. Let
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
be a random sample from a gamma distribution with shape parameter
\alpha>0
and rate parameter
\beta>0
. Since the proposed index
I_{\lambda}
is scale invariant, its value does not depend on
\beta
. Accordingly, without loss of generality, we set
\beta=1
throughout the simulation study and vary only the shape parameter
\alpha
, the interpolation parameter
\lambda
, and the sample size
n
. This choice allows us to isolate the effects of distributional asymmetry and sample size on the estimator’s performance.

6.1
Evaluating the Bias and MSE of estimates

The simulation design considers the values

\alpha\in\{0.5,\,1,\,2,\,5,\,10\},\quad\lambda\in\{0.25,\,0.50,\,0.75\},\quad n\in\{10,\,20,\,40,\,80,\,120\}.

These settings cover a broad range of relevant scenarios. In particular, smaller values of
\alpha
correspond to more skewed gamma distributions, whereas larger values of
\alpha
produce less dispersed and more symmetric populations. Likewise, the selected values of
\lambda
represent distinct points in the interior of the family bridging the Hoover and Gini measures. For each combination of
(\alpha,\lambda,n)
, we generate
R=1000
independent Monte Carlo samples.

For each replication, a sample
X_{1},\ldots,X_{n}
is generated from the
\mathrm{Gamma}(\alpha,1)
distribution, and the estimator
\widehat{I}_{\lambda}
is computed according to Equation (
8
). The corresponding population value
I_{\lambda}
is evaluated numerically using the integral representation derived for gamma distributions in Proposition
2.2
. For each scenario, the true value
I_{\lambda}
is computed once and used as a benchmark across all replications.

The performance of the estimator is evaluated using the empirical bias, mean squared error (MSE), and variance. Specifically, we compute

\operatorname{Bias}_{MC}(\widehat{I}_{\lambda})=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bigl(\widehat{I}_{\lambda}^{(r)}-I_{\lambda}\bigr),

\operatorname{MSE}_{MC}(\widehat{I}_{\lambda})=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bigl(\widehat{I}_{\lambda}^{(r)}-I_{\lambda}\bigr)^{2},

and

\operatorname{Var}_{MC}(\widehat{I}_{\lambda})=\frac{1}{R-1}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\bigl(\widehat{I}_{\lambda}^{(r)}-\overline{\widehat{I}_{\lambda}}\bigr)^{2},

where
\overline{\widehat{I}_{\lambda}}=({1}/{R})\sum_{r=1}^{R}\widehat{I}_{\lambda}^{(r)}
denotes the Monte Carlo average of the estimator.
These measures allow us to separately assess the systematic error (bias), the overall accuracy (MSE), and the dispersion of the estimator (variance).

The numerical implementation was carried out in
R
. The integral expressions required to evaluate
I_{\lambda}
were computed numerically using adaptive quadrature methods. For each parameter configuration, the Monte Carlo summaries were obtained from
R=1000
independent replications. The purpose of this study is threefold. First, it allows us to quantify the magnitude and direction of the finite-sample bias of
\widehat{I}_{\lambda}
under different levels of skewness and sample size. Second, it evaluates the overall accuracy of the estimator through the MSE. Third, the empirical variance provides insight into the variability of the estimator across repeated samples.

The Monte Carlo results, reported in Table 1, indicate that the estimator exhibits the expected improvement as the sample size increases. In particular, both the MSE and the variance decrease systematically with
n
, indicating increased precision. The bias also tends to decrease in magnitude for moderate and large samples. Moreover, the results suggest that the estimator’s performance depends on the shape parameter
\alpha
, with more skewed distributions (small
\alpha
) generally leading to larger bias and variability. This behavior is consistent with the increased difficulty of estimating inequality measures in highly asymmetric populations.

Empirical application

To illustrate the practical usefulness of the proposed family of inequality measures, we consider the distribution of GDP per capita across countries in the Americas using data from the World Bank. The variable of interest is GDP per capita measured at purchasing power parity (PPP), expressed in constant 2021 international dollars; see
https://ourworldindata.org/grapher/gdp-per-capita-worldbank

8
Concluding remarks

We proposed a new family of inequality indices that bridges the Hoover index and the Gini coefficient through a continuous interpolation parameter. The index provides a unified framework that captures both deviations from the mean and pairwise differences, offering greater flexibility in inequality measurement. We established its main theoretical properties and derived analytical representations that enable explicit evaluation under gamma distributions. From a statistical perspective, we analyzed the plug-in estimator and obtained expressions for its expectation and bias. The Monte Carlo results show that the estimator performs well in finite samples, with improved accuracy as the sample size increases. The empirical application illustrates the practical relevance of the proposed index, highlighting its ability to provide a nuanced assessment of inequality and to serve as a complement to classical measures.
