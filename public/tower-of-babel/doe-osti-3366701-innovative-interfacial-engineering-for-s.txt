3. Executive Summary
The discovery of perovskite solar cells (PSCs) based on metal-halide-perovskite (MHP) thin-film
light-absorbers in 2009,1 and further groundbreaking developments during 2012-14,2-8 sparked
worldwide excitement in this photovoltaic (PV) technology. This has resulted in a rapid rise in the
power conversion efficiency (PCE) of PSCs, from 3.8% since their invention in 2009,1 to the
current certified record PCE of 26.7% for single-junction PV and 34.6% for tandem PV in 2024.9
What makes PSCs so promising is that they are processed at near-room temperatures, employ
potentially scalable processes, and they use very small amounts of earth-abundant materials.10-12
Thus, the estimated energy-payback time and greenhouse-gas emission for PSCs is expected to be
as low as ~0.09 years and ~13.35 g CO2 eq./KWh, respectively, relative to ~1.3 years and ~29.67
g CO2 eq./KWh for comparable state-of-art Si PV.13 Also, PSCs offer a significant opportunity to
simplify and diversify the global supply chain for their manufacturing. Thus, PSCs have the
potential for achieving the levelized cost of energy (LCOE) SETO goals of 0.02 to 0.05 $/kWh.
However, there are several technical challenges in the path towards widespread commercialization
of PSCs. The chief among them are durability/reliability and scalability. This project set out to
address those challenges.
The overarching objective of this project was to increase significantly the mechanical adhesion
toughness of the key interfaces in PSCs, thereby improving the durability and reliability of PSCs
and minimodules, while simultaneously enhancing their PCE. This is achieved by incorporating
rationally-chosen self-assembled monolayers (SAMs) at key PSC interfaces. Inherently scalable
methods for the deposition of MHP light-absorber thin films and SAMs are employed to ensure a
viable path towards manufacturability. Furthermore, detailed understanding of mechanisms of
PSCs and minimodules degradation, and SAMs-induced mitigation, is provided.
As part of the project accomplishments, >60% increase in the adhesion toughness of the key PSC
interfaces over their SAMs-free control counterparts was achieved. Also, PCEs >20% and >19%
in rigid and flexible, respectively, single-junction PSCs (~1 cm2 area) were achieved. It was shown
that <20% PCE loss in flexible PSCs with SAMs can be achieved after bend testing. It was
demonstrated that T80 (80% retention of initial PCE) of >5,000 hours in rigid PSCs (~1 cm2 area)
can be achieved. Comprehensive understanding of the degradation mechanisms in PSCs was
obtained. These strategies were adopted towards developing minimodules. PCE of >21% was
obtained in an 6-cm2 area minimodule, with T91 of 1,000 hours.

5. Background
Tremendous progress has been made over the past decade in enhancing the power-conversion
efficiency (PCE), operational-stability, and scalability of PSCs) based on MHP thin-film light
absorbers.12,14,15 However, progress in mechanical reliability, which is also critically important for
the overall durability of PSCs, has been limited.16-22 While there have been reports on enhancing
adhesion toughness of interfaces in PSCs,16,19,23-33 it was demonstrated that there is a direct
correlation between mechanical reliability, and both PCE and operational-stability in planar n-i-p
rigid PSCs.34 The premise behind that effort was that stronger mechanical bonding between the
various interfaces in a PSC can reduce the propensity for flaw nucleation (pores, cracks) during
operation, and enhance the resistance to propagation of any such flaws at those interfaces, thereby
preventing delamination.34,35 This was accomplished by significantly increasing the adhesion
toughness of the interface between the SnO2 electron-transport layer (ETL) and the MHP thin film
using a iodine-terminated silane-based self-assembled monolayer (SAM) — so-called ‘molecular
glue.’34
Using this approach, in this project simultaneous enhancement in the PCE, operational-stability,
and cyclic-fatigue life was achieved in planar n-i-p flexible PSCs on plastic substrates.36 Here,
both interfaces, ETL/MHP and hole-transport layer (HTL)/MHP, were reinforced. The former was
achieved by using the SAM,34 and the latter by using an in-situ-grown low-dimensional (LD) MHP
at the HTL/MHP interface.37
While it is generally known that the use of mesoscopic ETL can improve the adhesion toughness
in rigid n-i-p PSCs,26 in this project it was shown that the use of mesoscopic TiO2 (m-TiO2) atop
the compact TiO2 (c-TiO2) ETL, together with the silane SAM, results in simultaneous
enhancement in mechanical reliability, operational-stability, and PCE in rigid mesoscopic n-i-p
PSCs.35
In comparison to n-i-p architecture, p-i-n PSCs show comparable efficiencies but are typically
easier to fabricate, have improved stability, and are better suited for tandem photovoltaic (PV)
applications. However, the important issue of mechanical adhesion of interfaces in p-i-n PSCs has
not been addressed adequately. In this project we were able to achieve a 2.6-fold increase in the
interfacial adhesion toughness via judicious selection of carbazole-based SAM. This resulted in
PSCs simultaneous enhancement in mechanical reliability, operational-stability, and PCE in rigid
p-i-n PSCs.38

6. Project Objectives
The overarching objective of the project was to increase significantly the mechanical adhesion
toughness of the key interfaces in PSCs, thereby improving the stability and reliability of PSCs
and minimodules, while simultaneously enhancing their PCE. This is achieved by incorporating
rationally-chosen SAMs at key PSC interfaces. Inherently scalable methods for the deposition of
MHP light-absorber thin films and SAMs are employed to ensure a viable path towards
manufacturability. Furthermore, detailed understanding of mechanisms of PSCs and minimodules
degradation, and SAMs-induced mitigation, is provided.
The expected outcomes for Budget Period 1 are: (1) >60% increase in the adhesion toughness of
the key PSC interfaces over their SAMs-free control counterparts; (2) PCE >23% and >21% in
rigid and flexible, respectively, single-junction PSCs (~1 cm2 area); (3) <20% PCE loss in flexible

Subtask 1.4. Interfacial Toughness Evaluation
The well-established ‘sandwich’ double-cantilever beam (DCB) method was used to measure the
interfacial toughness of ETL/MHP interface and MHP/HTL interface.
The DCB specimens for ETL/MHP interface were prepared with the following structure: glass/
compact TiO2/mesoporous TiO2/SAM-1/MHP/PMMA/Epoxy/glass. The dimension of the glass
substrate used is 37.5 x 12.5 x 1 mm3. The compact TiO2 (~ 50 nm thickness) was deposited by
spin coating a titanium diisopropoxide bis(acetylacetonate) solution. Mesoporous TiO 2 (~ 200 nm
thickness) was spin-coated at 2,000 rpm for 30 s using diluted TiO2 paste. The coated film was
sintered at 470 °C for 30 min. The prepared glass/compact-TiO2/mesoporous-TiO2 substrate was
immersed in an aqueous 20 mM titanium(IV) chloride solution, followed by sintering at 470 °C
for 30 min. The glass/compact TiO2/mesoporous TiO 2 substrates were then coated by 3-iodopropyl
trimethoxysilane SAM (referred to as SAM-1). The substrates were immersed in a 5 mM solution
of SAM-1 in isopropanol/DI water mixture (v/v 95/5) for 1 h. Subsequently, the substrates were
dried under flowing dry N2 and annealed at 100 °C for 5 min. They were further rinsed with
isopropanol several times before drying under N2 again. The MHP thin films (~500 nm thickness)
of target composition (CsPbI3)0.04{(FAPbI3)0.9(MAPbBr3)0.1}0.96 with 10 mol% excess PbI2 were
deposited using an established solution method. Here, a 1.3M precursor solution in n,n-
dimethylformamide (DMF)/dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) mixture (v/v 4/1) was spin-coated on the
substrates at 6,000 rpm for 30 s. At the 20th second of spinning, 200 μL of anti-solvent
chlorobenzene was dripped at the center. Subsequently, the as-deposited films were annealed at
100 °C for 50 min. The PMMA layer (~ 800 nm thickness) was deposited onto MHP layer for
protection by spin-coating the PMMA solution (8 wt% in chlorobenzene) at 3,000 rpm for 60s.
Subsequently, a thin layer of epoxy (~2 µm thickness) was applied onto the PMMA layer to ‘glue’
another cleaned glass substrate on top.
The DCB specimens for MHP/HTL interface were prepared with the following structure: glass/
3D-MHP/SAM-2 or LD-MHP/Spiro-OMeTAD/Ag/Epoxy/glass. The dimension of the glass
substrate used is 37.5 x 12.5 x 1 mm3. The deposition of 3D-MHP followed the aforementioned
procedure. The 1,1,2,2-tetrakis(4-bromophenyl)ethylene (referred to as SAM-2) solution (0.5
mg/ml in chlorobenzene) was spin-coated on 3D-MHP surface at 3,000 rpm for 30 s. For the
deposition of LD-MHP capping layer, 3-chlorobenzylamine iodide (3-CBAI) solution (3 mg/ml in
2-propanol) was spin-coated on 3D-MHP surface at 3,000 rpm for 30 s, followed by an annealing
step at 100 °C for 2 min. The Spiro-OMeTAD solution was prepared by dissolving 60 mg of Spiro-
OMeTAD with additives in 700 μL of chlorobenzene. The additives were 15.5 μL of Li-TFSI
solution (520 mg/mL in acetonitrile), 12.5 μL of FK 209 Co(III)TFSI salt (375 mg/mL in
acetonitrile), and 22.5 μL of 4-tert-butylpyridine. The Spiro-OMeTAD HTL (~ 300 nm thickness)
was prepared by spin coating the solution at 3,000 rpm for 30 s. A thin Ag layer (~ 20 nm thickness)
was thermally evaporated onto Spiro-OMeTAD HTL for protection. Subsequently, a thin layer of
epoxy (~2 µm thickness) was applied onto the PMMA layer to ‘glue’ another cleaned glass
substrate on top.
Ten specimens were measured for each condition using DCB delamination methods, where
averages and standard deviations of GC values were reported. SEM and XPS were used to
characterize the fractured DCB specimens to determine the fracture pathway.

Task 2.0. Fabrication of Rigid PSCs and Performance Evaluation
Subtask 2.1: Small-Area Rigid PSCs
Small-area rigid PSCs (~ 0.1 cm2 active area) were fabricated with the following structure:
glass/fluorine doped tin oxide (FTO)/compact TiO 2/mesoporous TiO2/MHP/Spiro-
OMeTAD/Gold. FTO substrates were cleaned in the mixed solution of deionized water, ethanol
and acetone with 1:1:1 volume ratio by ultra-sonication for 30 min. The deposition of compact
TiO2, mesoporous TiO2, SAM-1, SAM-2, LD-MHP, and Spiro-OMeTAD followed the
aforementioned procedure. A 1.6 M MHP precursor solution was prepared by dissolving
synthesized FAPbI3 single crystal with methylammonium chloride (MACl) additive in
DMF/DMSO mixture (v/v 4/1). The 3D-MHP layer was prepared by spin coating the precursor
solution on as-prepared FTO/compact TiO2/mesoporous TiO2 substrates under controlled
temperature and humidity at 5,000 rpm for 20 s, where 1 mL of diethyl ether was dripped on the
rotating substrate. Subsequently, the MHP layer was annealed at 150 °C for 15 min. An 80 nm
thickness of gold electrode was deposited on the Spiro-MeOTAD HTL layer by thermal
evaporation.

Subtask 2.3: Operational Stability of Expanded Small-Area Rigid PSCs
The unencapsulated as-fabricated expanded small-area rigid PSCs (~1 cm2) with SAM-1 and LD-
MHP incorporated at interfaces were loaded in a sealed chamber with a transparent quartz cover.

Subtask 5.1: Rigid Minimodules
Rigid minimodules (~10 cm2) are fabricated, and their PV performance is evaluated. Blade coated
devices were built using a well-established “racetrack” architecture that has been scaled up for 5x5
cm2 substrates and that was developed to allow for packaging and outdoor testing. Devices were
built using the following structure: glass/ITO/SAMs/MHP/C60/BCP/Ag. ITO on glass was
sourced from Colorado Concept Coatings (CCC). The dimension of the substrates used is
50x50x1.18 mm3. For both single-pixel devices and modules, substrate TCO was patterned using
532 nm nanosecond pulsed laser. After etching ITO, the substrates were cleaned with a sonicating
toothbrush and detergent, then in sonicating baths of deionized water, acetone, and isopropanol.
Each substrate was individually sprayed with N 2 to dry isopropanol while pushing any leftover
debris into a single corner. The substrates were then UV-ozone cleaned for 15 minutes immediately
prior to SAMs deposition. SAMs were spin coated in inert nitrogen atmosphere at 3000 rpm for
30 s using 1.5 mM I-2PACz or 2 mM MeO-2PACz in anhydrous ethanol. The SAMs films were
subsequently annealed at 100 degrees Celsius for 10 minutes each. The SAMs coated ITO
substrates were stored in foil-covered air-tight containers for up to one day before MHP coating.
MHP films were coated in nitrogen atmosphere using a blade coater with nitrogen air knife quench.
Substrates were sandwiched between leading and trailing substrates, and tape was applied to the
leading edge of each SAMs-coated substrate to hold them down against lifting force from air knife.
Doctor blade gap was set to approximately 100um for the MHP films in this study, or 1280 mm
above platen for 1.18mm thick CCC ITO/glass. The ink volume dispensed was 20uL of 1.6M
Cs0.13FA0.87Pb(I0.95Br0.05)3 in 4:1 (v:v) DMF:NMP solvent. Researcher varied feed rate and N 2
quench flow rate and quench delay time. The optimal settings in this study were found to be 7
mmps feed rate, 6 scfm for 6” air knife, 10 mm air knife height, and 30 s delay between coating

                                           Page 42 of 54

and air knife quench. Nitrogen air knife was passed over wet MHP film 4 times during quench to
imitate the film passing under four subsequent air knives on a manufacturing line. With each pass,
color changing from clear/yellow to transparent orange-brown indicated intermediate film
formation. MHP films were then annealed at 100-110 °C using a hot plate in inert nitrogen
atmosphere. Films were then transported to an inert atmosphere glovebox in another building for
photoluminescence mapping. Films were pumped using green LEDs, and a camera with RG9 and
RG780 filters took images of the MHP photoluminescence. 20 nm of C 60 and 6 nm of BCP were
then thermally evaporated onto the MHP films. LiF passivation layer was not used for these
devices. For single-pixel devices, SAMs/MHP/C60/BCP films were etched using a razor blade in
inert nitrogen atmosphere prior to Ag evaporation. For modules, SAMs/MHP/C60/BCP films were
etched using a 355 nm nanosecond pulsed laser from SpectraPhysics in inert nitrogen atmosphere
prior to Ag evaporation. SAMs/MHP/C60/BCP laser etching parameters were varied to help find
the best setting. P2 etched patterns were generally 140um in width. For single-pixel devices, Ag
back contact was patterned using a shadow mask during thermal evaporation. For modules, back
contact was etched using a 355nm nanosecond pulsed laser from SpectraPhysics in inert nitrogen
atmosphere after thermal evaporation. Laser etching settings were determined by applying test P3
scribes to single pixel devices made during the same blade coating batch. Since all tested P3 scribe
settings did not shunt out the pixels. The modules were then P3 etched using 2.5 uJ per pulse, with
87% overlap assuming 25 mm diameter beam spot (25 mmps feed rate at 10 kHz pulse frequency).
Following P3 etch, modules were electrically isolated by laser etching down to the glass. The same
laser scribing parameters were used to isolation etch, but with 11.5 uJ per pulse. Single-pixel
devices were measured in inert nitrogen atmosphere using a G2V LED solar simulator. Modules
were measured in air using a Xenon lamp Oriel solar simulator in a superstrate configuration.

We conducted module fabrication using iodine-terminated 2PACz {2-(3,6-diiodo-9H-carbazol-9-
yl)ethyl}phosphonic acid (I-2PACz) SAM molecule in p-i-n PSCs. As we showed early, the use
of I-2PACz makes the ITO surface more polar and polarizable for better wettability and the iodine
termination provides a favorable dipole. More importantly, I-2PACz SAM provides stronger
mechanical adhesion between ITO and perovskite film thereby reducing microscopic damage to
the PSCs during their operation and improving the operational stability. Thus, we tested I-2PACz
SAM as the hole transport layer (HTL) to prepare perovskite minimodules. For this test, we used
an FA-rich perovskite composition with a small fraction of Cs and MA mixtures. For this
perovskite composition, we further tried to optimize the solvent mixture in the perovskite ink
solution by using 2ME in the solvent mixture. A typical perovskite film coated on a 50 mm x 50
mm substrate is shown in Fig. 55, left panel. Finally, we processed the perovskite film into a
minimodule device, using the device stack of ITO glass/I-2PACz/perovskite/C60/ALD-SnO2/Au
(Fig. 55, right panel). With optimization of the coating condition and module fabrication process,
we obtained a module aperture PCE of 21.02% (aperture area 6 cm2), with a per-cell Jsc of
5.67 mA cm-2, per-cell Voc of 4.79 V and FF of 77.40% (Fig. 56). The slightly smaller aperture
area is due to the room we intentionally leave for making electrical contacts in addition to PIB
(Fig. 55, right panel). Note that our minimodule fabrication normally leads to >20% PCE
(milestone 3.2 and EOP-B).

Subtask 5.2: Operational Stability of Minimodules
Optimized rigid minimodules (~10 cm2) are tested to evaluate their long-term operational stability.
It is worth noting that Figure 57 shows the PSM lifetime is T91 = 1000 hours, corresponding to
about T80  2200 hours. This test was conducted at about 55°C. According to our recent study 39
at a cell level, aging an elevated temperature often shows accelerated degradation than room-
temperature aging test. An accelerated factor of about 4 is expected when aging at ~50°C versus
25°C. Thus, our obtained T80 of 2200 hours at 55°C would correspond to about 9000 hours at
room temperature, surpassing our target in Milestone 3.3 and EOP-C.

Task 6.0: Basic Understanding of Minimodules Degradation
Tested minimodules at various stages of degradation are characterized to elucidate the degradation
mechanisms. This serves as a guide for future degradation-mitigation efforts. Although our
understanding of minimodule degradation is still preliminary, our recent study 40 shows that
increasing the interfacial toughness by modulating the charge transport layers could increase the
operational stability at the minimodule level. Thus, we suspect that delamination associated with
the weak adhesion among device layers also contributes to the minimodule level degradation, and
is consistent with our cell level studies from this project. In addition, we also suspect that

encapsulation materials/processes and/or UV illumination are also important to module level
stability as hinted from our comparison between Figs. 45 and 46.

8. Significant Accomplishments and Conclusions

The most significant accomplishment of this study is the clear demonstration of the role of
increased interfacial adhesion toughness in n-i-p and p-i-n rigid and flexible PSCs in enhancing
their efficiency, durability, and reliability. It was also demonstrated, to some extent, that this
approach could be scaled up to minimodule level.
The EOP goals are listed as follows:
1. >23% PCE and >5000 h T80 life for expanded small-area (~1 cm2) rigid PSCs with SAMs. This
goal was achieved.
2. >21% PCE, <20% loss of cell PCE after bending to critical a radius, and >4000 h T80 life for
expanded small-area (~1 cm2) flexible cells with SAMs. While the PCE goal was achieved for
~0.1 cm2 flexible PSCs, it was not achieved for ~1 cm2 flexible PSCs. The <20% loss in cell PCE
was achieved. However, due to equipment issues, the flexible PSCs could not be tested for
operational-stability. That work is ongoing.
3. Achieve >20% PCE in rigid minimodules (~10 cm 2 area) based on best-in-class rigid PSCs with
SAMs. Achieve >5000 h T80 life in rigid minimodules. These goals are partially achieved, where
>20% minimodules with 6 cm2 area were fabricated, and it showed operational-stability T91 of
1000 h.

9. Path Forward
While this project was highly successful, upscaling remains a challenge. For example, Milestone
1.6 and 2.2 pertaining to upscaling from 0.1 cm2 to 1.0 cm2 were only partially achieved. We did
identify the causes, which are primarily related to transparent conducting oxide (TCO) sheet
resistance that increases the device series resistance and decreases the fill factor (FF). We have
shown that minimodules with a FF of 0.77 can be achieved, which means the TCO resistivity can
be managed in large devices through serial interconnection. Therefore, a possible path towards
addresses these issues could be better design of cell width of the serially-connected modules to
limit resistive losses in the TCO. Thus, next R&D steps could entail using such approaches for
successful upscaling of future PSCs.
This project further reinforced the connection between improved mechanical adhesion of
interfaces and the enhanced operational-stability of PSCs. In future R&D other innovative
approaches for improving the interfacial adhesion could be explored, for achieving further
improvements in not only operational-stability but also efficiency.
