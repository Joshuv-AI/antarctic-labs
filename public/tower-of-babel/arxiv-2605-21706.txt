Latent-space Attacks for Refusal Evasion in Language Models
Giorgio PirasUniversity of Cagliari giorgio.piras@unica.it, Raffaele MuraUniversity of Cagliari raffaele.mura@unica.it, Fabio BrauUniversity of Cagliari fabio.brau@unica.it Maura PintorUniversity of Cagliari maura.pintor@unica.it Luca OnetoUniversity of Genova luca.oneto@unige.it Fabio RoliUniversity of Genova fabio.roli@unige.it Battista BiggioUniversity of Cagliari battista.biggio@unica.it

Abstract
Safety-aligned language models are trained to refuse harmful requests, yet refusal behavior can be suppressed by steering their internal representations. Existing methods do so by ablating a refusal direction from model activations, aiming to remove refusal from the model’s residual stream. Despite their empirical success, these methods lack a principled account of the latent-space transformation they induce and why it suppresses refusal. In this work, we recast refusal suppression as a latent-space evasion attack against linear probes trained to separate refused from answered prompts. Under this view, prior work’s difference-in-means direction naturally defines such a probe, and its ablation is exactly a projection onto its decision boundary, i.e., a minimum-confidence evasion attack. This perspective not only explains the empirical success of prior work but also admits a key limitation: evasion stops at the decision boundary, motivating the need to push representations further into the compliant region, i.e., where the model answers. We leverage this by proposing a Controlled Latent-space Evasion attack that projects representations past the boundary with an optimized confidence. We achieve state-of-the-art attack success rate across 15 instruction-tuned, multimodal, and reasoning models, outperforming existing refusal-ablation baselines and specialized jailbreak attacks.

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are trained on broad web corpora that inevitably include harmful and unsafe content. This makes safety alignment a central requirement, as it allows models to
refuse
harmful requests
[
1
,
2
]
.
However, a growing literature shows that refusal behavior can be manipulated. First, jailbreak attacks demonstrated that carefully crafted input prompts can bypass refusal
[
3
,
4
]
.
More recently, work at the intersection of interpretability and alignment has shown that steering interventions on internal model representations can suppress refusal and enable harmful generations.
In particular,
Arditi et al. 2024
show that refusal behavior can be suppressed by a particular steering intervention, named
ablation
, leveraging a direction computed as the difference-in-means (DiM) between activations of harmful and harmless prompts. Ablating this direction consists of projecting each activation onto the hyperplane orthogonal to the DiM. The motivation is intuitive: if refusal is encoded in a single direction, writing it out of the residual stream should prevent the model from refusing.
Follow-up work refined this view by selecting directions from orthonormal bases
[
6
]
, optimizing them via string losses
[
7
]
, or extracting multiple directions
[
8
]

However, existing work has primarily focused on refining
which
directions to ablate, while implicitly assuming that ablation itself is sufficient to suppress refusal. This lack of principled analysis leaves unanswered
why
ablation is the right intervention, whether it is optimal, and how it can be systematically improved.

This work addresses this gap by providing a principled recasting of refusal suppression as a
latent-space evasion attack
against linear probes. Under this view, suppressing refusal amounts to modifying internal representations so that a linear probe trained to separate harmful from harmless prompts is fooled into classifying a harmful input as harmless.
This reframing explains why DiM ablation works. DiM direction naturally corresponds to the weight vector of a probe, and its ablation is exactly the orthogonal projection of activations onto the probe’s decision boundary. This coincides with the DeepFool
minimum-confidence
attack against a binary classifier
[
9
]
.
Furthermore, this exposes a central limitation of prior work, which projects activations onto the boundary without optimizing the probe’s confidence across layers. Since minimum-confidence evasion is, by construction, the weakest perturbation that flips a linear decision, pushing representations past the boundary with explicit confidence control can yield stronger refusal suppression.

Figure 1
:
1^{\rm st}
PC of prompt activation across layers:
CLE
variants confidently shift test prompts into the harmless, compliant region, while DiM leaves activation distribution nearly unchanged.

Building on this, we propose
Controlled Latent-space Evasion
(
CLE
), a refusal-suppression mechanism built on a set of linear probes trained at each layer to separate harmful from harmless representations.
CLE
perturbs activations to evade these probes with a controlled, optimized confidence margin. Since our formulation recasts the goal from erasing refusal (i.e., ablating all activations) to evading the probe, we instantiate
CLE
in two variants that differ in how the intervention is applied:
CLE-P
(
projective
) reprojects every token activation, similar to prior work, yet past the probe’s boundary by enforcing the target confidence on each projected activation;
CLE-A
(
additive
) instead, computes the perturbation
once
on the post-instruction token and adds it uniformly to all activations. This tests whether the continuous reprojection is necessary once the goal is evasion. We find that this single fixed perturbation suffices to sustain evasion throughout generation and yields stronger refusal suppression than per-token reprojection.

Fig. 1
visualizes the overall contrast: the minimum-confidence intervention of DiM’s minimum-confidence ablation barely shifts harmful representations out of the refusal region, whereas
CLE-P
and
CLE-A
project them confidently into the compliant region occupied by harmless prompts.
We present our latent-evasion formulation and recasting of prior work in
Sect. 2
, then describe our
CLE-P
and
CLE-A
methods in
Sect. 3
. We evaluate our approach in
Sect. 4
across 15 instruction-tuned, reasoning, and multimodal models, achieving state-of-the-art attack success, demonstrating the effectiveness of controlled latent-space evasion and observing its effect from a mechanistic viewpoint.

2
Refusal Suppression as a Latent-space Evasion Attack

We recast the problem of suppressing refusal in a safety-aligned model as a
latent-space evasion attack
against linear probes defined over internal representations.
Sect. 2.1
formalizes refusal suppression in terms of an abstract oracle that decides whether the model refuses a prompt and motivates surrogating it with a probe over internal activations.
Sect. 2.2
shows how this reduces to a latent-space evasion, and serves to both recast prior work in
Sect. 2.2.1
, and later introduce our approach in
Sect. 3
.

2.1
Refusal Suppression

We consider an autoregressive language model
f
composed of
L
layers
f^{(1)},\ldots,f^{(L)}
, each operating on sequences of
d
-dimensional token representations, and a last layer
f^{(L+1)}
providing the next-token scores. We use bold symbols to denote sequences of arbitrary length, i.e.,
\boldsymbol{x}\in\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{d}\bigr)^{*}
is a sequence in
\mathbb{R}^{d}
, with
\boldsymbol{x}[i]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
its
i
-th entry and
\boldsymbol{x}[-1]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
its last entry. We reserve the non-bold symbol for the last-token slice,
x\coloneqq\boldsymbol{x}[-1]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. The activation at the output of the
l
-th layer for input
\boldsymbol{t}
is then

\boldsymbol{h}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})\;\coloneqq\;\big(\,f^{(l)}\circ\cdots\circ f^{(1)}\,\big)(\boldsymbol{t})\;\in\;\bigl(\mathbb{R}^{d}\bigr)^{*}
,
and we denote the
post-instruction
(last-token) representation by
h_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})\coloneqq\boldsymbol{h}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})[-1]\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.

Refusal Oracle.

For a prompt
\boldsymbol{t}
, we assume the existence of an
oracle

\mathcal{Y}
that provides a binary outcome
\mathcal{Y}(f,\boldsymbol{t})
, which is
0
if and only if the model
f
fulfills the request in
\boldsymbol{t}
(i.e., does not refuse to fulfill the request, or does not produce degenerated completion). The oracle is intentionally left abstract: it may be instantiated as an LLM-as-a-judge evaluating the autoregressive completion of
f
on
\boldsymbol{t}
,
\mathcal{Y}(f,\boldsymbol{t})\coloneqq\mathcal{J}\!\big(\boldsymbol{t},\,\boldsymbol{o}(\boldsymbol{t})\big)
with
\boldsymbol{o}(\boldsymbol{t})
obtained by iterating
\boldsymbol{o}[j+1]=\texttt{next}(f(\boldsymbol{t}\oplus\boldsymbol{o}[:j]))
(where
next
is any selection of the next token of the autoregressive-based generation) or as any other refusal detector. This abstraction keeps the formulation independent of the specific evaluation protocol.

Refusal-Suppression Problem.
Let
\mathcal{D}
be a distribution of prompts, including both
harmless
and
harmful prompts
, on which the safety-aligned model
f
is trained to refuse. The attacker’s goal is to construct a new, perturbed model
\Psi f
minimizing the following problem:

\min_{{\Psi}}\quad\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{t}\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\mathcal{Y}({\Psi}f,\boldsymbol{t})\right]

(1)

where
{\Psi}
is any transformation applied to either model weights (e.g., with fine-tuning) or by intervening on activations (e.g., through
steering
). In our work, we focus on the latter case by defining steering operators
\Psi
that modify the residual stream of
f
at runtime while leaving the weights unchanged; this family encompasses prior refusal-ablation methods
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
. Importantly, solving
Eq. 1
via direct optimization of
\mathcal{Y}
is impractical for two reasons: (i) (
Black-box Oracle
) when
\mathcal{Y}
is instantiated by an external LLM-as-a-judge, gradients with respect to the model’s parameters or activations are simply unavailable (ii) (
Computational Cost
) even assuming a local oracle the autoregressive structure of
\mathcal{Y}
requires backpropagation through repeated next-token sampling, which is computationally prohibitive. We therefore replace
\mathcal{Y}
by a tractable, gradient-friendly surrogate.

Oracle Surrogate.
Observe that the oracle’s
\mathcal{Y}
verdict on
(f,\boldsymbol{t})
is determined by the activations of the inner layers
\boldsymbol{h}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})
for any prompt
\boldsymbol{t}
, since these deterministically provide the autoregressive completion (more details in
App. C
). This justifies replacing the expensive or intractable oracle
\mathcal{Y}
by a surrogate function
\phi\colon\prod_{l=1}^{L}(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}\;\to\;\mathbb{R}
.
Practically, we can reduce to an ensemble
\phi
of per-layer
linear probes
each applied on the
post-instruction token
defined as follows:

{\phi}(\boldsymbol{z}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{z}_{L})\coloneqq\sum_{l=1}^{L}\phi_{l}(\boldsymbol{z}_{l})\quad\text{where}\quad\phi_{l}(\boldsymbol{z}_{l})\;:=\;w_{l}^{\!\top}z_{l}+b_{l},\quad\boldsymbol{z}_{l}\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)^{*},

(2)

where
z_{l}
is a short notation for
\boldsymbol{z}_{l}[-1]
. Crucially, this provides a tractable behavioral proxy for
\mathcal{Y}
: the layer-wise probes approximate refusal decisions from labeled post-instruction residual-stream activations. This choice is motivated by evidence that refusal is linearly represented across layers and models
[
5
,
10
]
, while the construction of the labels and probes is detailed in
App. C
.
For each layer
l
, the probe
\phi_{l}
outputs a refusal logit for the input activation: positive predicts refusal, negative predicts compliance. Finally, observe that the hyperplane
\mathcal{H}_{l}=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:w_{l}^{\top}x+b_{l}=0\}
separates the two regions in latent space, where the weight vectors
w_{l}
and biases
b_{l}
define per-layer decision boundaries. Henceforth, we assume that
w_{l}
has unit norm.

2.2
Latent-space Refusal Evasion

We restrict to transformations
\Psi
defined by steering interventions on the residual stream, in line with prior work on activation steering for refusal suppression. Hence, we parameterize
\Psi
with
L
maps
\Psi_{i}\colon\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
inserted between layer blocks as follows:

(\Psi f):=f^{(L+1)}\circ\Psi_{L}\circ f^{(L)}\circ\cdots\circ\Psi_{1}\circ f^{(1)},

(3)

where each
\Psi_{i}
is applied token-wise, i.e., uniformly across token positions.
Without loss of generality, let us consider the problem of minimizing
\mathcal{Y}(\Psi f,\boldsymbol{t})
on a given input prompt
\boldsymbol{t}
, thereby assuming a prompt-dependent steering, which results in the existence of prompt-dependent latent-perturbations
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}
such that the steered activation
\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}_{i}(\boldsymbol{t})
can be written in terms of an additive perturbation
\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})=f^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}_{l-1}(\boldsymbol{t}))+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})
for each
\boldsymbol{t}
. Problem
1
reduces to the following minimization problem:

\min_{\boldsymbol{\delta}_{1},\ldots,\boldsymbol{\delta}_{L}}\,\mathcal{Y}(\Psi f,\boldsymbol{t})\quad\text{s.t.}\quad\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{t})=\boldsymbol{t},\quad\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})=f^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}_{l-1}(\boldsymbol{t}))+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t}),\quad l\leq L

(4)

which corresponds to a
latent-space evasion problem
.

Solving the Refusal Evasion Problem.

A sub-optimal solution to
Eq. 4
is given by a descending-gradient-based strategy leveraging the surrogate
\phi
to estimate the gradient of
\mathcal{Y}
on
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{l}
, as follows:

\frac{\partial\phi}{\partial\boldsymbol{\delta}_{l}}\;=\;\underbrace{\left.\frac{\partial\phi_{l}}{\partial\boldsymbol{z}_{l}}\right|_{\boldsymbol{z}_{l}=\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})}}_{\text{direct}}\;+\;\underbrace{\sum_{i>l}\frac{\partial\phi_{i}}{\partial\boldsymbol{z}_{i}}\;\frac{\partial f^{(i)}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}_{i-1}}\cdots\frac{\partial f^{(l+1)}}{\partial\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}_{l}}}_{\text{indirect (through subsequent layers)}}.

(5)

where, for each layer
l
, the gradient decomposes into a direct
l-
th contribution and an indirect one propagated through the subsequent layers. To avoid backpropagating through model layers, we approximate the full gradient by its direct component, thereby defining a truncated gradient
\hat{g}_{l}=\nabla_{\boldsymbol{z}_{l}}{\phi_{l}}(\boldsymbol{h}_{l})
. Note that, when
\phi_{l}
is instantiated as the linear probe of
Eq. 2
, this reduces to
\hat{g}_{l}=[0,\ldots,0,w_{l}]
, which is constant, i.e., independent of the input prompt
\boldsymbol{t}
. Following this truncated direction, the sub-optimal solution yields a layer-wise activation update of the form:

\boldsymbol{\delta}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})=\left[0,\ldots,0,-\alpha_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})\,w_{l}\right]\in\left(\mathbb{R}^{d}\right)^{*},

(6)

which particularly applies to the post-instruction token, and where
\alpha_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})\in\mathbb{R}
is the step size.
In conclusion,
Eq. 6
does not merely define a generic update; it rather defines an evasion step against the probe
\phi_{l}
, moving the activation in the direction that increases the probe’s compliance confidence.

2.2.1
Recasting Prior Work

We show that the proposed latent-space evasion attack formulation encompasses prior work on ablation-based refusal suppression
[
5
,
6
,
7
,
8
]
, showing how the ablation corresponds to a minimum-confidence evasion attack in latent space.
Let us first observe that an interesting choice of the step size is given by
\alpha_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})=\phi_{l}(\boldsymbol{h}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t}))
, which produces the update

\tilde{h}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})=h_{l}-\phi_{l}(\boldsymbol{h}_{l})\nabla_{z_{l}}\phi_{l}(\boldsymbol{h}_{l}),

(7)

that coincides with the
\ell_{2}
projection of
h_{l}
into the hyperplane
\mathcal{H}_{l}
, the classification boundary of
\phi_{l}
. Indeed, because
\phi_{l}
is linear,
\nabla_{z_{l}}\phi_{l}(\boldsymbol{h}_{l})
is constant, of unitary norm, and normal to
\mathcal{H}_{l}
; hence, moving along the gradient with step
\phi_{l}(\boldsymbol{h}_{l})
traces the shortest path from
h_{l}
to
\mathcal{H}_{l}
(refer to
[
9
]
for the linear case).

Difference in Means (DiM)
[
5
]
defines a refusal direction
r
computed as the difference-in-means between harmful (
\mathcal{D}_{\tt hf}
) and harmless (
\mathcal{D}_{\tt hl}
) activations at a given layer
l^{*}
. This corresponds to considering the same probe on all layers,
\phi_{l}(\boldsymbol{z}_{l})=\hat{r}^{\top}z_{l}
, with
\hat{r}
the normalized version of
r=\mu_{l^{*}}-\nu_{l^{*}}
, where
\mu_{l^{*}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\tt hf}}h_{l^{*}}(\boldsymbol{t})
and
\nu_{l^{*}}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}_{\mathcal{D}_{\tt hl}}h_{l^{*}}(\boldsymbol{t})
are the centroids of harmful and harmless representations.
The truncated gradient reduces to
\hat{g}_{l}=\hat{r}
for all layers
1\leq l\leq L
, from which the update in
Eq. 7
simplifies to
\tilde{h}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})=h_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})-(h_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})^{\top}\hat{r})\hat{r}
, which strictly coincides with the ablation introduced in the method and, thus, represents a
minimum-confidence evasion
attack.

Refusal Direction Optimization (RDO)
[
6
]
extends the DiM-based ablation by refining the choice of the refusal direction
r
and by also considering an
additive
intervention
h\mapsto h-\alpha\,\hat{r}
(initially proposed in
[
11
]
), which represents a special case of
Eq. 6
, under a shared probe
\phi_{l}(\boldsymbol{z}_{l})=\hat{r}^{\top}z_{l}
, where the perturbation has a constant step size
\alpha_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})\equiv\alpha
independent of
\boldsymbol{t}
.

Promotion Steering (PS)
[
7
]
proposes a joint update of the form
h\mapsto x-\hat{r}\hat{r}^{\top}x+r
. Instantiating the shared probe
\phi_{l}(\boldsymbol{z}_{l})=\hat{r}^{\top}z_{l}
, this update can be rewritten as
h\mapsto h-\bigl(\hat{r}^{\top}h-\|r\|\bigr)\hat{r}
, which fits the general form of
Eq. 6
with prompt-dependent step size
\alpha_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})=\phi_{l}(\boldsymbol{h}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t}))-\|r\|
. Within our framework, this corresponds to a
non-evasive
intervention, as the step size is strictly smaller than the projection step
\phi_{l}(\boldsymbol{h}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t}))
, meaning that the steered activation falls short of the decision boundary
\mathcal{H}_{l}
remaining in the refusal region at distance
\|r\|
from the boundary itself.

Multiple Directions (MD)
[
8
]
, finally, differs from the other approaches in that the update of
Eq. 7
is applied through multiple linear probes
\phi_{l}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{z}_{l})=w^{(i)\top}z_{l}
for
i\leq K
, where the directions
\{w^{(i)}\}
are obtained from the neurons of a self-organizing map (SOM). Using the notation of the paper, the per-direction ablation
\Pi_{w^{(i)}}
—i.e.,
Eq. 7
with
\alpha_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})=\phi_{l}^{(i)}(\boldsymbol{h}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t}))
—is applied
K
times (one for each probe), yielding the shared intervention
\Psi=\Pi_{w^{(1)}}\circ\cdots\circ\Pi_{w^{(K)}}
.

3
Controlled Latent-space Evasion Attacks

In
Sect. 2
, we recast refusal suppression as a latent-space evasion attack against linear probes. We show that the widely known refusal ablation corresponds to the minimum-confidence update of
Eq. 7
, which moves the activation onto the decision boundary
\mathcal{H}_{l}
and represents the weakest evading perturbation. We leverage this observation here to design a stronger attack. We formalize controlled-confidence evasion in
Sect. 3.1
, then describe two variants—
CLE-P
and
CLE-A
—that differ in how the perturbation is applied across token activations in
Sect. 3.2
.

3.1
From Minimum-Confidence to Controlled Evasion

Figure 2
:
Minimum vs. Controlled evasion in the PCA-rendered latent space of
LLaMA2-7B
.

The minimum-confidence update of
Eq. 7
places every steered activation exactly on
\mathcal{H}_{l}
—the region of maximum uncertainty—which empirically leads to inconsistent evasion across layers, as shown in
Fig. 1
. Moving activations past
\mathcal{H}_{l}
, into the compliant half-space, requires replacing the fixed step size with one that encodes an explicit evasion margin.
Fig. 2
provides a graphical interpretation of this idea.
Practically, we introduce a per-layer margin
m_{l}\geq 0
that controls how far past
\mathcal{H}_{l}
each activation is projected, with
m_{l}=0
reducing to a boundary projection. Notably, each margin
m_{l}
is a direct measure of the classification confidence of a linear probe (refer to
App. E
for more details). We refer to this family of evasion attacks as
Controlled Latent-space Evasion
, and operate the resulting confidence control through the choice of the step size.

The Choice of the Step Size.
Leveraging latent-space probes as surrogates, the analysis of
Sect. 2.1
reduces arbitrary layer-wise perturbations
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{l}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}
from
Eq. 4
to the one-dimensional
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})=[0,\ldots,0,-\alpha_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})w_{l}]
aligned with the probe’s truncated gradient (
Eq. 6
). With this formulation, controlling evasion confidence amounts to choosing the step
\alpha_{l}
that we parameterize as:

\alpha_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})\;=\;\lambda_{l}\cdot\,\bigl(\phi_{l}(f^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}_{l-1}))+m_{l}\bigr),\qquad\lambda_{l}\in\{0,1\},\quad m_{l}\geq 0,

(8)

where
\lambda_{l}
selects whether layer
l
is actively steered.
In practice, if
\lambda_{l}=0
, the layer is left untouched; if
\lambda_{l}=1
, it is actively steered with
m_{l}
. This allows us to avoid steering on layers where the intervention would be practically useless or would destroy model coherence (see
Sect. D.2
for more details).
Plugging
Eq. 8
into
Eq. 4
collapses the optimization problem of
Eq. 1
to the search of
2L
scalars and yields:

\displaystyle\min_{\{\lambda_{l},m_{l}\}_{l}}\;\;\;\;

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\boldsymbol{t}\sim\mathcal{D}}\!\bigl[\,\mathcal{Y}(\Psi f,\,\boldsymbol{t})\bigr]

(9a)

s.t.

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}_{0}(\boldsymbol{t})=\boldsymbol{t},\;\;\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})=f^{(l)}\!\bigl(\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}_{l-1}(\boldsymbol{t})\bigr)+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{l},\;\;\forall l,

(9b)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\delta}_{l}=[0,\ldots,0,-\alpha_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})w_{l}],\;\;\alpha_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})=\lambda_{l}\bigl(\phi_{l}(f^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}_{l-1}))+m_{l}\bigr),

(9c)

\displaystyle\lambda_{l}\in\{0,1\},\;\;m_{l}\geq 0.

(9d)

The use of latent-space probes as surrogates reduces the search space from tensors
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{l}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}
to a set parameterized by a binary layer mask
\{\lambda_{l}\}
, selecting where evasion matters, and a non-negative margin vector
\{m_{l}\}
, controlling evasion confidence. In turn, although
\mathcal{Y}
is black-box and non-differentiable, the resulting minimization problem is amenable to gradient-free black-box optimization on a held-out validation set
\mathcal{D}_{\rm val}
. We solve

via Bayesian Optimization (BO)

3.2
Instantiating Controlled Evasion:
CLE-P
and
CLE-A

We instantiate
CLE
in two variants.
CLE-P
follows prior refusal-ablation methods by reprojecting each token activation, but enforces a target confidence beyond the probe boundary.
CLE-A
instead tests whether continuous reprojection is necessary: it computes the evasion perturbation once on the post-instruction token and reuses it across activations.

Algorithm 1
reports the full procedure, by taking as input the aligned model
f
, a training
\mathcal{D}_{\tt train}
and a validation set
\mathcal{D}_{\rm val}
, a validation oracle
\mathcal{Y}_{\rm val}
, a mode flag (
CLE-P
or
CLE-A
), and by returning a steered model
\Psi f
with optimized layer-wise interventions.
For every layer
l\leq L
, we fit the linear probe of
Eq. 2
on post-instruction activations from
\mathcal{D}_{\tt train}
, using linear SVMs with normalized weights (Line
5
), which define the decision boundaries.

CLE-P
(Projective) and
CLE-A
(Additive) variants.

In Line
7
, we define the
CLE-P
intervention as
\Psi_{l}(h)=h-\lambda_{l}(\phi_{l}(h)+m_{l})w_{l}
, applied uniformly to every activation
h
in both inputs and generated tokens. Geometrically, when
\lambda_{l}=1
, the update is the Euclidean projection of
h
onto the (shifted) hyperplane
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{l}\coloneqq\mathcal{H}_{l}-m_{l}w_{l}
parallel to
\mathcal{H}_{l}
at distance
m_{l}
inside the compliant half-space; when
\lambda_{l}=0

\Psi_{l}
collapses to the identity.

thus generalizes the boundary projection of prior refusal-ablation methods, where each activation is individually projected to
\tilde{\mathcal{H}}_{l}
.
From Line
8
to Line
10
we define
CLE-A
, which produces a perturbation that is
universal
across decoding tokens. Concretely, the projection is computed
once
at the post-instruction token
h_{l}=\boldsymbol{h}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})[-1]
, yielding a perturbation
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})[i]=-\lambda_{l}(\phi_{l}(h_{l}(\boldsymbol{t}))+m_{l})w_{l}
for all the generated tokens
i
. The layer-wise intervention, however, is then defined as
\Psi_{l}(h)=h+\boldsymbol{\delta}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})
, where the same
\boldsymbol{\delta}_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})
is added to every token activation without any further projection. The resulting perturbation is therefore sufficient to sustain evasion without continuous reprojection of the residual stream, as shown in
Sect. 4
.

parameterized by the
2
scalars

5
Related Work

Our work studies the limits of LLM safety alignment from a mechanistic perspective. It differs from prompt-level jailbreaks such as GCG
[
3
]
and SAA
[
4
]
, which optimize input-space suffixes for each harmful prompt. Instead, we recast activation steering as latent-space evasion and intervene directly on internal representations; for completeness, we compare against these jailbreak attacks and consistently outperform them. Closest to our work are refusal-direction ablation methods, already discussed throughout the work.
Arditi et al. 2024
first showed that refusal can be suppressed by ablating a DiM direction from the residual stream, following also the hypothesis from
Park et al. 2024
. Subsequent work refined this direction by modeling refusal through cone-like orthogonal bases
[
6
]
, optimizing steering directions with string-based losses
[
7
]
, or extracting multiple directions with Self-Organizing Maps
[
8
]
, in line with recent evidence that concepts may have richer geometric structure
[
19
,
20
,
21
]
. We reframe these methods as latent-space evasion attacks and show that our controlled variants,
CLE-P
and
CLE-A
, outperform them. Operationally,
CLE-P
extends ablation by replacing boundary projection with controlled-confidence evasion, while
CLE-A
relates to activation-addition methods. Prior additive approaches either add a prompt-independent DiM shift
[
5
]
or contrastive activation vectors
[
22
,
11
]
. In contrast,
CLE-A
computes a prompt-dependent perturbation on the post-instruction token that evades the probe with controlled confidence and then sustains this evasion without reprojection.

6
Conclusions, Limitations and Future Work

We recast refusal suppression in safety-aligned language models as a latent-space evasion attack against linear probes separating refused from compliant representations. Under this view, prior refusal-ablation methods reduce to minimum-confidence evasion, which is, by construction, the weakest perturbation flipping a linear decision. Based on this, we proposed
Controlled Latent-space Evasion
(
CLE
), which pushes representations past the boundary with an optimized confidence margin, and instantiated it in two variants:
CLE-P
, reprojecting every token activation during generation, and
CLE-A
, computing the perturbation once on the post-instruction token and applying it universally. Empirically,
CLE-A
surpasses
CLE-P
, indicating that continuous reprojection is suboptimal, and attains state-of-the-art attack success across
15
instruction-tuned, multimodal, and reasoning models, outperforming all refusal-ablation baselines and per-prompt jailbreak.
One
limitation
of the work is the use of BO used to select layers and margins. Future work can be oriented to parameterize
\lambda_{l},m_{l}
as differentiable variables and fold them into the gradient-based formulation of
Eq. 4
, replacing BO with direct gradient-based optimization. Finally, the
CLE
approach relies on the linear separability of refused and answered requests in latent space, which consistently holds across all models we tested, enabling reliable evasion. Therefore, alignment procedures that actively discourage linear separability could, in principle, reduce the attack effectiveness. Future work could focus on mitigating such a family of attacks by acting on the separability property at the latent space, e.g., using alignment procedures that actively discourage it by distributing refusal representations across non-trivial manifolds.

Appendix A
Ethical and Societal Impact

This work exposes a weakness of current safety-aligned language models: refusal behavior can be suppressed by targeted latent-space interventions. Similar to prior work on jailbreaks and refusal ablation, this research is dual-use and could be misused to weaken model safeguards, especially in white-box settings where internal activations can be modified. At the same time, studying this failure mode is important for understanding how refusal is represented inside language models and why current alignment mechanisms remain vulnerable. Our contribution is intended to support defensive research by providing a principled account of refusal suppression as latent-space evasion, clarifying the limitations of existing ablation methods, and motivating stronger safety mechanisms that are less dependent on linearly separable refusal features. We therefore believe that the scientific and safety benefits of disclosing these results outweigh the associated risks.

Responsible Release.

To support reproducibility while limiting misuse, we will release the code and experimental details needed to verify the main results. The release will prioritize analysis and evaluation of the proposed method, rather than packaging it as a tool for bypassing safeguards. We will not release the harmful model completions generated during evaluation. This choice preserves the reproducibility of the scientific claims while avoiding unnecessary dissemination of harmful outputs.

Bayesian Optimization Algorithm

The Bayesian optimizer searches for the active layer window and target margins that maximize ASR on the
HarmBench
validation set. We outline the procedure in
Algorithm 2
. The search is parameterized by a contiguous layer window
\{s,\ldots,e-1\}
, inducing the binary layer-selection variables
\lambda_{l}=\mathbf{1}\{s\leq l<e\}
. This reduces the dimensionality of the search space while still allowing the optimizer to select any intervention region in the model.

The first stage uses
70\%
of the trial budget to search over a layer window
(s,e)
and a shared margin
m
. Each candidate configuration is evaluated through the
TRIAL
function, which instantiates the steered model
\Psi_{\{\lambda_{l}\},\{m_{l}\}}f
using Algorithm
1
, generates completions on
\mathcal{D}_{\rm val}
, and computes ASR with the validation oracle
\mathcal{Y}_{\rm val}
. The sampler is initialized with random trials and then updated with the observed
(s,e,m)
and ASR values, following a Tree-Parzen Estimator (TPE) strategy.

After this coarse search, the remaining
30\%
of the budget is used to locally refine the margins on the best selected layer window. The window is kept fixed, and each active layer margin is sampled from the local grid around the best shared margin, i.e., one grid step below, equal to, or one grid step above it. This gives a lightweight per-layer adjustment without expanding the full BO search to all layer-wise margins. The best observed configuration is finally returned as
\{\lambda_{l}^{\star}\}_{l=1}^{L},\{m_{l}^{\star}\}_{l=1}^{L}
.

The cost of the procedure is dominated by
TRIAL

\mathcal{D}_{\rm val}
. In our experiments, we use
T=500
trials for most models, increasing it to
700
for models with more than
32
layers, which we found sufficient for the reduced search space induced by the contiguous layer-window parameterization.

Appendix E
Margin and Confidence

We show here that the margin
m_{l}
introduced in
Eq. 8
admits a direct interpretation as the classification confidence of the linear probe under a logistic model. Consider a linear probe
\phi(x)=w^{\top}x+b
with
\|w\|=1
. Under the standard logistic link, the predicted probability of refusal is

\mathbb{P}(y=1\mid x)\propto\sigma(\phi(x))=\frac{1}{1+e^{-\phi(x)}},

(10)

where
\sigma
denotes the sigmoid function. The minimum-confidence update of
Eq. 7
, which projects
x
onto the decision boundary
\mathcal{H}
, yields a steered activation
\tilde{x}=x-\phi(x)\,w
satisfying
\phi(\tilde{x})=0
, and hence
P(y=1\mid\tilde{x})=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
: the point of maximum uncertainty.

To obtain a steered activation with a prescribed compliance confidence
\gamma\in(0,1)
, we require
P(y=1\mid\tilde{x})=1-\gamma
, i.e., the probe predicts refusal with probability
1-\gamma
(equivalently, compliance with probability
\gamma
). Writing
\tilde{x}=x-\alpha(x)\,w
and imposing this constraint gives

\frac{1}{1+e^{-\phi(x-\alpha(x)\,w)}}=1-\gamma.

(11)

Since
\phi
is linear with
\|w\|=1
, we have
\phi(x-\alpha(x)\,w)=\phi(x)-\alpha(x)
, from which

\phi(x)-\alpha(x)=\log\!\left(\frac{1-\gamma}{\gamma}\right)=-\log\!\left(\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\right),

(12)

yielding the step size

\alpha(x)=\phi(x)+\log\!\left(\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\right).

(13)

Comparing with our parameterization
\alpha_{l}(\boldsymbol{t})=\lambda_{l}(\phi_{l}(f^{(l)}(\boldsymbol{\tilde{h}}_{l}))+m_{l})
from
Eq. 8
, with
\lambda_{l}=1
, we see that the margin
m_{l}
corresponds exactly to the log-odds of the target compliance confidence:

m_{l}=\log\!\left(\frac{\gamma}{1-\gamma}\right).

(14)

Setting
m_{l}=0
recovers
\gamma=\nicefrac{{1}}{{2}}
, i.e., the boundary projection of
[
5
]
and the DeepFool attack
[
9
]
on linear classifiers. Positive margins push the steered activation deeper into the compliant half-space with increasing confidence: for instance,
m_{l}\approx 2.94
corresponds to
\gamma=0.95
, while
m_{l}\approx 4.60
yields
\gamma=0.99
. This provides a principled interpretation of the margin search in
Eq. 9
: optimizing
m_{l}
via Bayesian Optimization is equivalent to selecting, per layer, the probe compliance confidence that maximizes attack success.

In this section, we provide additional details on the mechanistic analysis reported in
Sect. 4
. We focus on two complementary views of the latent-space effect induced by
CLE
. First, we inspect how the intervention moves the post-instruction representation across layers. Second, we analyze whether the induced shift is preserved during autoregressive generation.

Layer-wise prompt representations.

Fig. 8
reports ridgeline plots of post-instruction activations across layers. For each model and layer, we collect the last-token residual-stream activations of harmful and harmless prompts used to train the probes, together with the activations obtained on
HarmBench
test prompts under DiM ablation,
CLE-P
, and
CLE-A
. We then project the activations of each layer onto the first principal component, computed from the reference harmful/harmless activations at that layer. The sign of the component is chosen so that harmless representations lie on the positive side of the axis. Each ridge shows, for a fixed layer, where the harmful test prompts are placed relative to the harmful (refusal) and harmless (compliance) training distributions.

These plots provide a direct latent-space view of the difference between minimum-confidence ablation and controlled evasion. DiM often leaves the harmful test distribution close to its original refusal region, or only weakly moves it toward the decision boundary. This is consistent with our formulation: DiM ablation corresponds to a boundary projection and does not explicitly optimize for a positive compliance margin. In contrast, both
CLE-P
and
CLE-A
shift the same prompts toward the harmless side of the representation space, often making their distribution overlap with the harmless training prompts. This confirms that the optimized margins do not merely remove refusal evidence, but push representations into the compliant region. The effect is especially visible in intermediate and late layers, where harmful and harmless representations are more separated. Early layers are instead often less informative, with overlapping harmful/harmless distributions, which explains why the BO search tends not to select them.

Generated-token trajectories.

Fig. 9
complements the layer-wise prompt analysis by tracking the latent trajectory during generation. For each harmful prompt of the
HarmBench
dataset, we generate completions under DiM,
CLE-P
, and
CLE-A
. At each generated token position, we record the last-layer activation and evaluate it with the corresponding linear probe. We convert the probe score into compliance confidence as
1-\sigma(\phi_{l}(h))
, so that values above the decision threshold indicate movement toward the compliant side of the probe. The curves report the average compliance confidence across prompts at each generated token position, while the horizontal reference lines indicate the average confidence of harmful and harmless probe-training prompts.

The token-level plots show that
CLE-A
preserves a compliant trajectory throughout generation, despite applying only a fixed perturbation computed at the post-instruction token. This is important because
CLE-A
does not reproject each newly generated activation onto the shifted probe boundary. Nevertheless, its generated-token activations remain consistently above the decision threshold and close to the harmless reference region. In several models,
CLE-A
matches or exceeds
CLE-P
, suggesting that continuous reprojection is not required once the initial latent trajectory has been moved into the compliant region.

By contrast, DiM generally remains close to the harmful region or oscillates around low compliance-confidence values. This supports the interpretation that minimum-confidence ablation is too weak: it may reduce local refusal evidence, but it does not reliably induce a stable, compliant trajectory during decoding. Overall, the ridgeline and token-generation analyses support the same conclusion from two perspectives: controlled evasion changes where the prompt representation lies in latent space, and this shift is then propagated through generation, especially for the additive
CLE-A
variant.

Appendix G
Additional Ablation Analysis

Fig. 10
and
Fig. 11
ablate the main components of
CLE-P
and
CLE-A
. Starting from the full method introduced in
Sect. 3
, we remove one design choice at a time: we replace the SVM probes with DiM probes, remove layer selection by setting
\lambda_{l}\equiv 1
(i.e. intervening on all layers), remove confidence control by setting
m_{l}\equiv 0
, and finally remove both layer selection and confidence control by setting
\lambda_{l}\equiv 1
and
m_{l}\equiv 0
. This allows us to test whether the performance gain comes from the probe quality, the choice of intervention layers, the positive evasion margin, or their combination.

The results show the same trend for both
CLE-P
and
CLE-A
. Using DiM instead of SVM probes reduces ASR, confirming that a more accurate latent surrogate gives a stronger evasion direction. Setting
\lambda_{l}\equiv 1
is also suboptimal: even with optimized margins, intervening on all layers can perturb layers where refusal information is weak or where steering compromise the generation. Setting
m_{l}\equiv 0
is weaker as well, because it results on moving activations to the probe boundary, recovering a minimum-confidence evasion attack rather than pushing them into the compliant region. The weakest variant is generally the one with both
\lambda_{l}\equiv 1
and
m_{l}\equiv 0
, which corresponds to all-layer boundary projection.

Overall, these ablations show that the gains of
CLE-P
and
CLE-A
are not explained by a single component. Strong refusal suppression requires the combination of accurate probes, selective layer intervention, and positive margin control.

Appendix I
Computing

For models up to 15B parameters, we ran all experiments on a single NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU with 48GB of memory. For larger models, we used a single NVIDIA H100 GPU with 94GB of memory. On average, the Bayesian Optimization search required approximately 6 hours on one GPU when evaluated on the
HarmBench
validation set. The remaining steps of the pipeline were substantially cheaper. Training the linear probes, instantiating the intervention, and generating completions with the steered model required only a few minutes beyond standard generation time. In particular, once the optimized layer window and margin were selected, inference with
CLE-P
and
CLE-A
ran at the model’s usual generation cost, since the intervention only modifies activations during the forward pass and does not require additional optimization at test time.
