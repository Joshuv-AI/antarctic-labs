Screening and diagnosis tools for sarcopenia: an overview of systematic reviews
Authors: Jingfeng Zou, Liping Wang, Shaotian Li, Jiajia Ran, Xin Yang, Chunhui Tian, Yiting Liu, Wen Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis overview of reviews aims to evaluate the validity of systematic reviews (SRs) and meta-analyses on screening and diagnosis tools for sarcopenia. The methodological quality and the quality of the evidence (QOE) were assessed following the AMSTAR-2 tool and the GRADE approach, respectively. Four SRs were evaluated on SARC-F, while 2 focused on its modified versions, employing both qualitative and quantitative analyses. The 4 SRs analyzed 98 overlapping reports, comprising 51 unique index publications and 26 duplicates. The “% overlaps” for this overview was 50.98%, meanwhile the covered area (CA) was 48.04%, and the corrected CA (CCA) was 30.72%, indicating a very high overlap. When SARC-F was used, results from four records showed a pooled sensitivity ranging from 14 to 77% and pooled specificity from 63 to 94% (QOE: very low to moderate). When its modified versions were used, results from two records showed a pooled sensitivity ranging from 19 to 57% and pooled specificity from 79 to 92% (QOE: low to moderate). AMSTAR-2 assessments revealed that only 10.7% critical domains were unmet, demonstrating high methodological rigor and strong confidence in the findings. Compared to SARC-F, its modified versions show improved performance and can be a more desirable screening tool for sarcopenia. Introduction

Sarcopenia is a gradual and widespread decline in skeletal muscle function typically observed in the elderly population and is marked by decreased in muscle strength, muscle mass, and muscle quality [1]. It was first introduced into consensus definitions in 2010 and classified as an independent disease in 2016, but there is still no standardized diagnostic criterion for sarcopenia. The prevalence of sarcopenia has ranged from 10 to 27% in recent studies, depending on different diagnostic criteria and threshold values used [2]. As far as we know, sarcopenia is commonly identified through the utilization of dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) and bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA). To diagnose sarcopenia, evaluations of muscle strength, muscle mass, and/or muscle quality are necessary, and the severity of sarcopenia is indicated by physical performance. Sarcopenia is linked to adverse outcomes such as morbidity, functional decline, mortality, and considerable economic costs, underscoring its public health significance [3, 4]. However, these assessments require allocated time and skilled staff, adding to societal burdens. As the global population age, it is crucial to early identify sarcopenia in older adults, especially in high-risk populations.

As the number of screening and diagnostic tools for sarcopenia has increased, researchers have conducted systematic reviews and meta-analyses of the diagnostic accuracy of different assessment tools, but evidence quality remains inconsistent, and findings vary [5]. Therefore, it is crucial to give consideration to the screening and diagnosis instruments designed for sarcopenia. Among these tools, strength, assistance with walking, rising from a chair, climbing stairs, and falls (SARC-F) questionnaire was introduced into the guidelines in recent years [6, 7]; its modified version also has shown promising utility. However, many systematic evaluations may exist for the same clinical question, and decision-makers may face obstacles in making decisions based on the vast amount of evidence from systematic evaluations. This study comprehensively evaluated existing systematic reviews (SRs) and meta-analysis on sarcopenia diagnostic accuracy to clarify the quality of the evidence, identify the potential risk of bias, and offer clinicians reliable guidance for decision-making.

An overview of reviews can provide a comprehensive and systematic evaluation of SRs and meta-analyses available for a particular research question, forming a comprehensive overview of the specific research question or existing findings, and offering the possibility of understanding the strength of the body of evidence and the potential risk of bias [8]. It can synthesize the diagnostic accuracy of assessment tools, compare the differences between the results of different studies of the same assessment tool, and clarify the level of evidence and potential risk of bias of different systematic evaluations and meta-analyses. This study applied the AMSTAR-2 (A Measurement Tool to Assess systematic Reviews-version 2) methodological requirements and the GRADE (Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation) approach to ensure methodological rigor [9, 10]. The study protocol has been registered on the PROSPERO platform (ID: CRD42024542506). This study was reported based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement and an extension to the PRISMA statement for Reporting Literature Searches in Systematic Reviews (PRISMA-S) [11, 12].

Material and methods

Literature inclusion and exclusion

In this overview, we evaluated different screening and diagnosis tools for sarcopenia in comparison with guideline-recommended diagnostic criteria. Therefore, the diagnostic validity and sensitivity were assessed across various screening and diagnosis tools in patients with sarcopenia.

Inclusion criteria: Studies were included if they: (i) reported at least one sarcopenia screening instrument and (ii) examined diagnostic accuracy against a widely accepted diagnostic criterion of sarcopenia, such as European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People 2019 (EWGSOP2), EWGSOP, Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS), International Working Group on Sarcopenia (IWGS), the Foundation for the National Institutes of Health (FNIH), and The Society of Sarcopenia, Cachexia and Wasting Disorders (SCWD).

Exclusion criteria: Studies were excluded if they: (i) consisted of case series, comments, letters, protocol, meeting reports, and animal and cell culture studies and (ii) were published in languages other than English.

Literature search strategies

In December 2024, the computerized review databases were performed in PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews. The timeframe for the search was database creation to December 2024, and the type of study was restricted to systematic review and meta-analysis. Literature screening and data extraction

Study selection: (1) Initial screening: Two independent reviewers (Z. JF and W. LP) conducted the searches. They screened the titles and abstracts of all studies identified through the search strategy against the pre-determined inclusion and exclusion criteria. This initial screening excluded irrelevant studies based on the information provided in the title and abstract. (2) Full-text screening: Full-text articles of potentially eligible studies were retrieved for further assessment. The same two reviewers (Z. JF and W. LP) independently evaluated these full texts against the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Reasons for excluding studies at this stage were recorded. (3) Final selection: Any discrepancies between the two reviewers regarding study inclusion was resolved through third independent reviewer (L. ST).

The following data were extracted: study reference/country, year of publication, included studies and sample size, methodological quality assessment, male (n %), age (years, mean ± SD), setting for study population, diagnostic criteria of sarcopenia, prevalence of sarcopenia, screening and diagnosis tools (e.g., SARC-F and its modified version), sensitivity, specificity, positive likelihood ratio (PLR), negative likelihood ratio (NLR), diagnostic odds ratio (DOR), a summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve for these tools, and conclusions. The primary authors were contacted if additional information was needed for the systematic review.

Methods of data analysis and summary of the evidence

The duplication of index publications included in the overview was analyzed using % overlap (% overlap = duplication of index publications/r), covered area (CA = N/r*c), and corrected covered area (CCA = N − r/[(r*c) − r]), where “N” denotes the total number of included publications (including duplicates), “r” represents the number of index publications, and “c” indicates the number of reviews included. A higher degree of overlap was observed with an increasing number of publications. Overlaps are required to be reported in rigorously conducted overviews, with the CCA method offering a comprehensive approach. A CCA value lower than 5 indicates a slight overlap, whereas values greater than or equal to 15 reflect a very high overlap [13]. And the pooled sensitivity and pooled specificity with or without 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were summarized as stated in each SR and meta-analysis. Other measures of diagnostic accuracy such as PLR, NLR, and DOR were reported when available.

Assessment of methodological quality and evidence quality

Since there is no specificity quality assessment tool for diagnostic SRs and meta-analysis, the methodological quality of evidence was appraised following the AMSTAR-2 tool, and the quality of evidence was appraised following the GRADE approach [9, 10]. Two review authors (Z. JF, W. LP) independently completed the above evaluations. If necessary, a third review author (L. ST) took part in and resolved disagreements through discussion.

Results

Literature screening process and description of the studies

A total of 46 references are identified through an electronic search and the flowchart following PRISMA 2020 is reported in Fig. 1. To avoid the inclusion of duplicate records, data were extracted from 24 SRs and meta-analyses. Following a full-text review, 8 SRs and meta-analysis were finally included, focusing on the screening and diagnosis tools for sarcopenia, such as the SARC-F questionnaire and its modified versions [14–17], muscle ultrasound [18, 19], and blood biomarkers [20, 21]. Due to publication type, study population, and study design, 16 records in the overview were also excluded.

Summary of findings

The prevalence of sarcopenia, as assessed by SARC-F, varied from 2.8 to 85.0%, while its modified versions varied from 2.42 to 53.7%. The pooled sensitivity for SARC-F and its modified versions were 14–77%, and 19–57%, respectively. The pooled specificity for SARC-F and its modified versions were 63–94%, 79–92%, respectively. As shown in Table 3, according to the GRADE approach, the evidence quality was assessed as very low to moderate (1 SR rated very low, 2 rated low, and 1 rated moderate). Although the screening sensitivity performance of SARC-F and its modified versions was poor, the specificity was consistently high; compared to SARC-F, its modified versions could be an effective tool for sarcopenia diagnosis. In addition, SARC-F and its modified versions frequently suggested as the primary screening test tool are shown in Table 4.

Discussions

This overview of reviews aimed to provide an overall summary of the accuracy of screening and diagnosis tools for sarcopenia, particularly with a focus on SARC-F and its modified versions. To our knowledge, this is the first overview of the topic area to date. We collated 4 SRs and meta-analysis and plotted a table of SARC-F and its modified versions based on the available evidence. Due to different populations, settings, type of specimens, or other variables summarized, it provided an opportunity to gain valuable insights into the performance of these tools, contributing critical evidence for guideline development and adaptation.

In this overview, the results of 4 records were published between 2018 and 2023. These records included 98 overlapping reports, based on 51 individual index publications and 26 duplicates, along with “% overlaps” of 50.98%, CA 48.04%, and CCA 30.72%, indicating a very high overlap. When SARC-F was used, results from four records showed a pooled sensitivity ranging from 14 to 77% and pooled specificity from 63 to 94% (QOE: very low to moderate, due to the serious risk of bias, indirectness, inconsistency, publication bias). When its modified versions were used, results from two records showed a pooled sensitivity ranging from 19 to 57% and pooled specificity from 79 to 92% (QOE: low to moderate, due to the serious risk of bias). Only 10.7% of AMSTAR-2 critical domains were unmet, demonstrating a high quality of methodological assessment with a high confidence.

Sarcopenia risk was assessed by employing the SARC-F tool, which evaluates strength, walking assistance, chair rising, stair climbing, and falls. A SARC-F score of 4 or higher indicates the presence of sarcopenia. Consensus guidelines, such as EWGSOP2 and AWGS2019, recommend it as a formal and effective screening tool for sarcopenia [6, 7]. However, while SARC-F demonstrates moderate-high specificity, its low-moderate sensitivity raises concerns about its utility as a standalone screening tool for sarcopenia, particularly when applied to older inpatients using EWGSOP2 diagnostic standards. Despite its widespread use, SARC-F may not fully address clinical needs. Recent studies suggest that refinements to the tool could enhance its sensitivity and specificity, enabling the detection of more sarcopenia cases. Further research is essential to improve its diagnostic performance, especially in high-risk populations.

Modified versions of SARC-F (shown in Table 4). According to a recent study on meta-analysis, the SARC-CalF demonstrated sensitivity ranging from 45.9 to 57.2% and specificity ranging from 87.7 to 91.3%, indicating low to moderate sensitivity and high specificity [16]. Another meta-analysis of subgroups analysis indicated that the SARC-F modified edition, comprising SARC-F, SARC-CalF, SARC-F + TC, SARCF-CalF + TC, SARC-F + E, and SARC-F + EBM, demonstrated a pooled sensitivity of 49% and specificity of 92% [17]. Incorporating additional anthropometric measurements, such as SARC-CalF + AC, the diagnostic effectiveness of SARC-F can be significantly enhanced. According to previous findings, SARC-CalF + AC, which demonstrated the highest overall accuracy in diagnosis, appears to be the most effective screening method for identifying old individuals with sarcopenia living in the community [22, 23]. Moreover, SARC-CalF + MAC can also enhance the effectiveness of SARC-CalF in identifying community dwellers with sarcopenia by improving the sensitivity to detect sarcopenic obesity [24]. Additionally, the Otassha Study, which is a cohort study utilizing SARC-F, developed a simple and efficient screening instrument to identify sarcopenia in 683 older community dwellers. The findings indicated that it possessed superior diagnostic efficacy in detecting sarcopenia compared to the SARC-F questionnaire [25].

Ishii test and its score. The Ishii test was created in 2014, and its score was based on age, hand grip strength, and CC, which was suggested for sarcopenia screening by EWGSOP2 [6]. A recent meta-analysis of 4 original studies reported that the Ishii score had moderate sensitivity (79%) and high specificity (85%) [17]. The Ishii test has been demonstrated as having better overall screening capabilities for sarcopenia in old adults residing in nursing homes compared to the CC, SARC-F, and SARC-CalF [26]. Furthermore, combining Ishii with SARC-F, an effective and straightforward method, has the potential for screening sarcopenia in acute hospital care. This approach significantly enhances diagnostic accuracy, as it offers higher levels of sensitivity and specificity [27].

Mini Sarcopenia Risk Assessment (MSRA) questionnaire. It can be categorized into two tests, MSRA-5 and MSRA-7, which assess age, history of hospitalization, level of activity, dietary items (either one or three sub-items), and weight. A recent meta-analysis of 5 original studies reported that the MSRA had high sensitivity (82%) and moderate specificity (62%). The results of subgroup analysis for MSRA-5 and MRSA-7 were generally in line with the study [17]. At present, it is impossible to ascertain whether MSRA-5 or MSRA-7 is a superior tool for evaluating the risk of sarcopenia based on the findings [28, 29]. Nevertheless, the utilization of both SARC-F and MSRA can significantly enhance the precision in identifying sarcopenia, especially when combined with MSRA-7 instead of MSRA-5 [30].

Muscle ultrasound. It is low in cost and easily accessible and can be used to examine the muscles in the lower limbs, with muscle thickness (MT) being the most commonly assessed factor, followed by the measurement of cross-sectional area (CSA). This method shows promise for diagnosing sarcopenia, as supported by a systematic review of six studies that identified rectus femoris ultrasound as effective across various clinical populations [18]. A recent meta-analysis study of 17 studies involving 2143 participants reported low to moderate diagnostic accuracy for MT, CSA, and echo intensity (EI), with the AUC (area under the curve) values of summary receiver operating characteristic ranging from 0.43 to 0.83 [19]. The utilization of both CSA (indicators of muscle quantity) and EI (indicators of muscle quality) in the biceps brachii or rectus femoris muscles could potentially enhance the accuracy of diagnostic tests for sarcopenia, surpassing the effectiveness of using CSA or EI individually. Despite certain studies showing statistically significant results, there was considerable variation in the approaches employed. Furthermore, it is crucial to highlight the necessity of standardizing ultrasound protocols and elevate reported sensitivities in order to effectively and consistently utilize ultrasound in a clinical setting.

Blood biomarkers. Sarcopenia screening or diagnosis could potentially utilize biomarkers. The main source of serum creatinine (Cr) is the degradation of creatine phosphate during the metabolism of skeletal muscles. Consequently, people experiencing a reduction in muscle mass might exhibit diminished levels of creatinine. Furthermore, aspartate aminotransferase (AST), which is a prevalent liver enzyme, is linked to reduced muscle mass. A comprehensive analysis conducted a meta-analysis on 32 studies involving 23,840 individuals, evaluating a collective of 30 biomarkers. Among these biomarkers, only the creatinine-to-cystatin C (Cr/CysC) ratio and the AST/alanine aminotransferase (AST/ALT) ratio showed superior diagnostic accuracy for sarcopenia compared to the remaining 28 blood biomarkers (e.g., insulin and circulating cell-free mitochondrial DNA) [20]. Nonetheless, based on the combined sensitivity and specificity, it appears that none of these biomarkers is ideal for the screening or diagnosis of sarcopenia. The most studied indices—the Cr/CysC ratio and Cr × eGFRcys index—showed comparable diagnostic accuracy performance and may serve as surrogate markers for sarcopenia [21], though further validation is essential.

Based on the synthesized evidence, the performance characteristics of sarcopenia screening and diagnostic tools, including sensitivity, specificity, clinical settings, advantages, and disadvantages, are summarized in Table 5. Current evidence supports the combined use of measurement tools such as SARC-CalF + AC/MAC, SARC-F + Ishii score, and SARC-F + MSRA across different settings to improve diagnostic accuracy. Future priorities include direct comparisons of SARC-F versions in specific populations, validation of modified scores in diverse ethnic groups, cost-effectiveness analyses, and optimizing tool combinations for enhanced diagnostic precision. Artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning represent promising non-invasive approaches for sarcopenia screening and diagnosis [31]. Models like the SARC-CXR score use machine learning to assess muscle characteristics, while advanced systems such as DSnet-v1 integrate transcriptomic data with muscle mass, quantity, and performance metrics [32, 33]. Despite their potential, these technologies require extensive clinical validation to confirm their reliability and applicability.

Conclusions

This overview consolidates the existing evidence about screening and diagnosis tools for sarcopenia. As determined by GRADE assessment, the level of certainty (evidence) for the individual studies is variable (very low to moderate). It was concluded that the SARC-F and its modified versions are frequently recommended as the primary screening test, although numerous alternative tools have also been proposed. Current evidence supports combining tools, such as SARC-CalF + AC/MAC, SARC-F + Ishii score, and SARC-F + MRSA, to suit specific contexts. There is an urgent need to develop a quality assessment tool and standardized framework for systematic evaluation of diagnostic accuracy tests in the future.
