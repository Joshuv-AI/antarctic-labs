Pediatric Migraine Disability Assessment (PedMIDAS): translation into Turkish and cross-cultural adaptation
Authors: Nisa Nur Ayhanci, Zeynep Parlak Özer, Fırat Can, Şeyma Sönmez Şahin, Elif Yüksel Karatoprak, Hıdır Apak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Pediatric Migraine Disability Assessment (PedMIDAS) questionnaire is widely used to evaluate migraine-related disability in children and adolescents. The aim of this study was to assess the validity and test–retest reliability of the Turkish version of the PedMIDAS.MethodsThe PedMIDAS was translated into Turkish using a forward-backward translation procedure. A total of 60 children and adolescents diagnosed with migraine participated in this cross-sectional study. Data collection was conducted in two sessions separated by 15 days. During the first session, sociodemographic information and migraine characteristics, including attack frequency, duration, and pain severity assessed by the visual analogue scale (VAS), were recorded. Participants also completed the Turkish PedMIDAS and the Child Self-Report form of the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory™ 4.0 (PedsQL™) to evaluate convergent validity. Fifteen days later, the participants completed the Turkish PedMIDAS again to assess test–retest reliability.ResultsThe Turkish version of the PedMIDAS demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (α = 0.74) and strong test–retest reliability (r = 0.92). PedMIDAS scores were significantly associated with migraine clinical characteristics and quality-of-life measures.ConclusionThe Turkish version of the PedMIDAS is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing migraine-related disability in children and adolescents.Graphical Abstract Keywords: PedMIDAS, Migraine, Disability, Pediatric, Validation

Introduction

Migraine is a multifactorial neurovascular syndrome characterized by moderate to severe headache attacks, typically unilateral and/or throbbing, accompanied by nausea, vomiting, photophobia and phonophobia, occurring in genetically susceptible individuals and often triggered by environmental factors. Migraine is a common disorder associated with significant disability in the pediatric population and affects approximately 11% of children and adolescents [1, 2]. The estimated prevalence of migraine among schoolchildren (aged 6–18) in Türkiye is between 5.7% and 10.3% [3, 4]. Pediatric migraine has a significant negative effect on daily academic functioning, peer relationships, school and family life, and quality of life [5–7]. It is also suggested that pediatric migraine increases the likelihood of developing psychological disorders, including anxiety disorders and depression [8].

Several studies have aimed to better understand and measure headache burden [9]. Among the current indicators, the measurement of functional impact is suitable for assessing the concrete consequences of a disease on an individual’s life. This model clearly distinguishes the symptoms of the disease from its potential consequences, particularly in terms of activity limitations. When applied to the field of headache, the model means distinguishing between measuring the characteristics of the pain and assessing its impact on the individual’s life. In fact, two people with seemingly similar headaches may be affected in very different ways in their daily activities [10].

This perspective led to the creation of a specific tool – the Migraine Disability Assessment Scale (MIDAS) – to assess the severity of disability caused by headaches [11]. MIDAS is not particularly suitable for children and adolescents, as it focuses primarily on occupational activities. This questionnaire was later adapted for children and adolescents as the Pediatric Migraine Disability Assessment Scale (PedMIDAS) [12, 13]. Unlike MIDAS, which has been translated into Turkish and validated [14], the validity and reliability study of PedMIDAS in Turkish has not yet been conducted. PedMIDAS has been used in several studies, translated into many languages, and is currently accepted as the reference standard for assessing the functional impact of headaches.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to perform the formal translation, cross-cultural adaptation, and psychometric validation of the PedMIDAS into the Turkish language for use among children and adolescents.

Materials and methods

Translation of the PedMIDAS into Turkish

The PedMIDAS scale was used with permission from Cincinnati Children’s Hospital Medical Center, which retains the copyright of the instrument. The cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric evaluation processes were conducted in accordance with the recommendations of the COSMIN (Consensus-based Standards for the Selection of Health Measurement Instruments) guidelines and the cross-cultural adaptation procedures was conducted in accordance with the recommendations of Guillemin and Beaton [15–17]. Four independent forward translations were produced by three translators whose native language was Turkish and who were proficient in English, and by one translator whose native language was English and who was proficient in Turkish. The translations were synthesized by a bilingual team to produce a single preliminary version. This version was then subjected to two independent back-translations by translators who were blinded to the purpose of PedMIDAS. The back-translators were native English speakers who were also proficient in Turkish. The forward translations, the synthesized version, the two back-translations, and the original version were reviewed by a bilingual multidisciplinary committee to ensure semantic, idiomatic, experiential, and conceptual equivalence. Based on this evaluation, the Turkish version of PedMIDAS was developed. The pre-final version was subsequently pre-tested on a pilot group of 15 children through live online video interviews (via Zoom) to assess clarity and comprehensibility.

Study design and participants

This cross-sectional study included 60 children and adolescents with migraine from two pediatric neurology clinics in Türkiye (Göztepe Professor Dr Suleyman Yalcin City Hospital and Mardin Training and Research Hospital).

The study excluded children who started or changed prophylactic treatment after the first-meeting questionnaire, as well as those with hemiplegic, basilar-type, or retinal migraine, migraine complications [18], medication overuse headache, or concomitant chronic diseases. All included patients reported migraine as their main symptom during assessment.

To test the validity and reliability of Turkish-PedMIDAS, it would be necessary to include at least 5 patients preferably 10 patients for each item (total of 30–60 participants) [19]. We decided to include at least 60 participants. Seventy individuals were initially included in the study. Ten participants were excluded following a data quality assessment process because their questionnaires contained substantial inconsistencies that could not be resolved during researcher-guided interviews. Further examination indicated that these inconsistencies were primarily related to difficulties in retrospectively recalling migraine-related disability (n = 4), unresolved discrepancies between child and parent reports (n = 6), and uncertainty regarding whether functional limitations were attributable to migraine or to concurrent acute illnesses, such as sinusitis, upper respiratory tract infections, and other temporary health conditions. As these issues limited the validity and interpretability of the PedMIDAS responses, the affected cases were excluded from the psychometric analyses.

The study was conducted in two sessions, approximately 15 days apart. At meeting 1, sociodemographic data, migraine clinical findings including attack frequency, duration and severity according to the visual analogue scale, Turkish-PedMIDAS and Child Self-Report of the Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory™ 4.0 (PedsQL™) were collected. For test–retest reliability, the Turkish-PedMIDAS was administered again to each child 15 days after (Meeting 2) the initial assessment. Prior to this readministration, participants and their parents were queried to ensure that their headache patterns and treatment regimens had remained strictly stable. The participants and their parents reported no major clinical changes during this 15-day interval. During these meetings the researcher administered the entire questionnaire by reading it aloud to the child to ensure that the child understood the items and response ratings [20, 21]. If any question was not understood, it was read again. The researcher-guided format was not intended to explain questionnaire items or influence participant responses. Rather, it was adopted to evaluate the consistency and credibility of responses, identify potential recall uncertainty and parent-child discrepancies, and detect functional impairments that could not be reliably attributed to migraine because of acute health conditions. All interviews were conducted by the researcher via video call on the Zoom platform.

The data was collected in December 2025-February 2026 (test/retest).

3.Turkish version of Pediatric Migraine Disability Assessment (Turkish-PedMIDAS)

Turkish version of Pediatric Migraine Disability Assessment (Turkish-PedMIDAS)

Migraine-related headache attacks may cause a child to miss school, experience a decline in school performance or homework performance, or face limitations in social activities. The PedMIDAS was developed to determine this level of disability. PedMIDAS was developed for use in pediatric populations between 4 and 18 years of age [13]. In the PedMIDAS scale, items are scored based on the number of days affected, and the total score is calculated by summing the number of days reported for each item. The score is “0–10” = low or no disability, “11–30” = mild disability, “31–50” = moderate disability, “above 50” = severe disability [12]. Because the PedMIDAS evaluates headache-related disability over the previous three months, the possibility of recall bias among children and adolescents was considered. To minimize this risk, the questionnaire was administered through standardized, researcher-guided online interviews rather than unassisted self-completed forms. Each item was read aloud at an appropriate pace, allowing the participants sufficient time to accurately recall their experiences. In addition, parents were present and allowed to assist younger children in recalling specific objective events, such as missed school days, during the specified period.

4.Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory™ 4.0 (PedsQL)

Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory™ 4.0 (PedsQL)

The PedsQL was introduced by Varni et al. to assess the health-related quality of life in pediatric populations between 2 and 18 years of age [23]. It examines the areas of physical health, emotional functioning and social functioning, which are characteristics of the state of health as defined by the World Health Organization. In addition, school functioning is also examined. Scoring is conducted in three areas. Firstly, the scale total score is calculated; secondly, the physical health total score is calculated; and thirdly, the psychosocial health total score is calculated, which consists of the item scores assessing emotional, social, and school functioning [24]. The scale consists of 23 items and is designed for application in large populations, such as including school and hospital settings, among both healthy and clinically ill children and adolescents. The score that can be obtained on the scale ranges from 0 to 100. If the answer to the question is marked as ‘never’, 100 points are awarded; if marked as ‘rarely’, 75 points; if marked as ‘sometimes’, 50 points; if marked as ‘often’, 25 points; and if marked as ‘almost always’, 0 points are awarded. Higher total scores on the scale reflect better perceived health-related quality of life [24]. The Cronbach’s alpha of the scale was 0.93 [23]. The validity and reliability studies of the scale for the 8–12 and 13–18 age groups in Turkish was conducted by Çakın Memik et al. [25, 26]. The Turkish version of the scale was used in the study.

Statistical analysis

All data were analysed using the SPSS 25.0 statistical package program. Descriptive analysis included frequencies, percentages, means, and standard deviations (SD). Kurtosis and skewness values were examined to determine whether the variables in the study showed a normal distribution. Skewness and kurtosis were within acceptable limits (-1,5 to 1.5) [27], indicating an approximately normal distribution and supporting the use of parametric analyses.

The psychometric evaluation included assessment of internal consistency, test–retest reliability, and construct validity in line with COSMIN recommendations for health-related patient-reported outcome measures [17]. The internal consistency of the Turkish PedMIDAS was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha. To assess test/retest reliability, the Turkish-PedMIDAS scores (each Turkish-PedMIDAS question and total score) in each assessment were compared using paired t test. The relationships between numerical variables (attack frequency, duration, severity, and PedsQL) were assessed using Pearson’s correlation analysis for normally distributed variables and Spearman’s correlation analysis for variables that were not normally distributed. The correlations between Turkish-PedMIDAS scores in the two evaluations were calculated with the Spearman correlation analysis. A correlation coefficient of 0.1-0.2 was considered poor, 0.3‐0.5 fair, 0.6‐0.7 moderate, and 0.8–0.9 very strong, and 1.0 perfect [28]. In all analyses, p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

The findings suggest that the Turkish PedMIDAS is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing migraine-related disability in children with migraine. In addition, significant positive correlations were observed between PedMIDAS scores and key clinical characteristics of migraine, including attack frequency, attack duration, and pain severity measured by the VAS. These findings indicate that increased migraine severity is associated with greater functional disability in pediatric patients. Furthermore, the negative correlation between PedMIDAS scores and quality of life (PedsQL) suggests that higher levels of migraine-related disability are associated with poorer quality of life. Taken together, these findings support the construct validity of the Turkish PedMIDAS and highlight its usefulness for evaluating the impact of migraine on daily functioning in pediatric populations.

The Cronbach’s alpha for the Turkish-PedMIDAS was 0.74. Since values above 0.70 are generally considered acceptable for internal consistency [29], this result indicates that the Turkish-PedMIDAS has good reliability. This value is close to the Cronbach’s alpha reported for the original US PedMIDAS (0.78) [13] and the French version of PedMIDAS (0.76) [30] and aligns well with the Brazilian Portuguese version (0.84) [21], although it is slightly lower than the exceptionally high internal consistency reported for the Arabic version (0.94) [20]. These subtle cross-cultural variations in internal consistency may stem from demographic diversity or language-specific structural nuances, yet they universally confirm that the PedMIDAS maintains robust reliability across different languages.

Furthermore, the Turkish-PedMIDAS demonstrated excellent temporal stability with a strong test-retest correlation (r = 0.92) between the two assessments conducted 15 days apart. This high reproducibility is consistent with the findings of the original study (r = 0.77) [13], the French validation (r = 0.85), the Brazilian version (r > 0.80) [21] and the Arabic version (intra-class correlation coefficient = 0.99) [20], demonstrating that the instrument provides highly stable disability scores over time, regardless of the cultural setting.

Regarding construct validity, the Turkish-PedMIDAS scores showed a moderate significant positive correlation with pain severity measured by VAS (r = 0.45, p < 0.01) and attack frequency (r = 0.34, p < 0.01), and a weak significant positive correlation with attack duration (r = 0.29, p < 0.05). Additionally, a weak significant negative correlation was found with PedsQL scores (r = -0.27, p < 0.05). These results mirror the patterns observed in the original validation study by Hershey et al. [13] and are strongly supported by the Arabic [20] and French [30] versions, which likewise reported that higher migraine disability scores are directly linked to higher clinical severity (VAS and frequency) and significantly poorer health-related quality of life. Collectively, these cross-cultural alignments reinforce the global validity of the PedMIDAS and confirm that the Turkish version is a highly robust tool for capturing the functional impact of pediatric migraines.

In our psychometric evaluation, Q6 (assessing days with > 50% disability during non-school activities) demonstrated corrected item-total correlation (0.18). According to standard biostatistical guidelines, correlation coefficients between 0.1 and 0.2 are classified as poor [28]. Psychometric reporting for this specific item varies across international literature; while some adaptations reported higher correlations (e.g., 0.49 in the Brazilian Portuguese version), others omitted individual item-total correlations (e.g., French and Arabic versions) [20, 21, 30]. This lower correlation in the Turkish cohort can be attributed to the unstructured nature of leisure activities compared to the strictly monitored school environment, making it difficult for children to quantify a precise 50% functional decline during play. Additionally, pediatric migraineurs frequently adopt an ‘all-or-nothing’ coping strategy—either completely withdrawing from activities or participating fully despite the pain—which deflates the variance of partial disability items. Despite its lower correlation, Q6 was retained in the Turkish-PedMIDAS to preserve the original scale structure and ensure cross-cultural comparability.

A limitation of this study is the administration format, as the Turkish-PedMIDAS was administered through researcher-guided Zoom interviews rather than as a fully independent self-report questionnaire. This approach was intentionally adopted to identify potential recall difficulties associated with the three-month retrospective assessment, ensure consistent interpretation of questionnaire items, and improve data reliability. However, the psychometric properties reported in this study should be interpreted within the context of the administration format used. In routine clinical practice, when children and adolescents complete the questionnaire independently, recall-related difficulties and response inconsistencies may occur, which could influence questionnaire performance. As PedMIDAS relies on retrospective self-report, the accurate recall and attribution of migraine-related disability may present challenges in some pediatric patients, particularly in the presence of comorbid conditions or other health-related events occurring during the reference period. Therefore, careful clarification of migraine-specific disability during questionnaire administration may support more accurate reporting and interpretation of disability scores.

Furthermore, our cohort was heavily weighted toward pediatric patients experiencing severe migraine with high-frequency attacks. Because this study was conducted within a tertiary care training and research hospital, the sample inherently reflects referral patterns to specialized clinics, where patients with higher disease burden and disability are represented. Consequently, the psychometric properties and cut-off performance reported here may have limited applicability to milder pediatric migraine populations typically managed in primary or secondary care settings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the Turkish-PedMIDAS is a valid and reliable instrument for assessing migraine-related disability in children and adolescents. Evaluating functional disability is an essential component of migraine assessment, as it reflects the impact of the disease on daily life beyond headache characteristics alone. The significant positive associations observed between PedMIDAS scores and attack frequency, attack duration, and attack severity, together with the inverse association with quality of life, provide further evidence supporting its construct validity. Although the questionnaire does not replace a comprehensive clinical evaluation, it offers a practical and reliable tool for assessing and longitudinally monitoring migraine-related disability, disease burden, and treatment outcomes in Turkish-speaking pediatric populations.
