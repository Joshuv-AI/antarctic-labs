Kinetic models of opinion-driven epidemic dynamics modulated by graphons
Andrea Bondesan, Jacopo Borsotti, Mattia Fontana

Abstract
. We introduce kinetic models to simulate epidemic spread while accounting for individuals’ opinions on protective behaviors. Opinion exchanges occur on a social network represented by a graphon, leading to scenarios with or without opinion leaders. We prove convergence to equilibrium in the strong L 1 L^{1} norm via relative entropy methods and in homogeneous Sobolev spaces H ˙ − s \dot{H}^{-s} , s ∈ ( 1 2 , 1 ) s\in\big(\frac{1}{2},1\big) , using Fourier-based techniques. We then design a structure-preserving scheme for the coupled opinion-epidemiological system, highlighting graphon effects: opinion leaders supporting protective behaviors limit disease spread, whereas influenceable individuals may shift toward opposing views, worsening epidemics. Finally, we introduce a time-dependent quantity, analogous to the reproduction number, whose oscillations can generate epidemic waves without explicit external forcing. The MATLAB code implementing our algorithms is made publicly available.



Keywords:
Kinetic equations; Mathematical epidemiology; Opinion dynamics; Social networks; Time-dependent reproduction number.

AMS Subject Classification
: 35Q84; 82B21; 91D10; 94A17.

1.
Introduction

The origins of mathematical epidemic modeling based on compartmental approaches trace back to the pioneering work of Kermack and McKendrick
[
40
]
. Since that time, several models, usually described by systems of Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs), have been developed to simulate complex biological dynamics
[
30
,
42
,
51
]
and to account for collective behaviors
[
14
,
21
,
54
]
. While some efforts were aimed at reproducing the spread of the epidemic as precisely as possible by considering, for example, secondary infections
[
38
]
, disease severity
[
12
]
, different timescales
[
37
]
, and age-dependent host mortality
[
3
]
, starting from the COVID-19 pandemic it became clear that human behaviors should be incorporated in such models to effectively predict the outcome of the epidemics. Indeed, the presence of an underlying society is what distinguishes the spread of epidemics among humans from the ones occurring among other animal species, as observed in farms
[
44
]
and national parks
[
13
]
.

The nowadays globalized society can be considered at three different levels: anyone can share their opinion through social networks, allowing them to reach people all over the world and amplifying their impact on the society
[
52
]
; citizens are physically connected with each other thanks to transportation and infrastructures
[
15
]
; governments implement strategies to slow down the spread of the epidemics, like quarantines
[
46
]
and vaccination campaigns
[
41
]
. While mobility and policy interventions have been extensively studied, with numerous contributions in the literature, the interplay between opinion dynamics and epidemic spreading remains less explored. For instance, from a graph-theoretical perspective, a considerable number of models has been developed to consider the movement of the citizens and the connections between them (see, e.g.,
[
18
,
27
,
35
,
36
]
), while optimal control strategies have been developed to organize effective quarantines and vaccination campaigns (see, e.g.,
[
1
,
5
,
6
]
). On the other hand, fewer works have been dedicated to linking opinion dynamics with epidemics (see, e.g.,
[
2
,
9
,
20
,
22
,
25
,
62
]
) and several questions remain open due to the complexity of such models, notably the relaxation to equilibrium of their solutions and the quantification of the convergence rates.

In this work, we focus on the impact that individuals’ opinions have on the spread of the epidemic. Indeed, opinions influence whether a person will actively try to limit the transmission, for example by wearing face masks
[
39
]
, or, conversely, will disregard the risks of infection, potentially endorsing conspiracy theories
[
60
]
. In general, individuals with an opinion in favor of a protective behavior are less likely to contract the disease and, in case this happens, to spread it. Obviously, since opinions evolve over time, one has to model interactions among citizens to account for these dynamics
[
26
,
59
,
58
]
. In addition, we shall also assume here that opinion exchanges occur on an underlying graphon
[
31
]
, which is interpreted as the continuous extension of a dense graph and can be used to describe connections on a social network. It is also important to consider the presence of opinion leaders, namely highly influential people (such as politicians and influencers) that are capable of altering citizens’ opinions while leaving their own unaffected. Interestingly, from a mathematical point of view, one of the novelties of our approach is that, thanks to appropriate choices of the graphon, we are able to characterize both normal citizens and opinion leaders using a single model, instead of considering two separate classes of individuals like in
[
8
,
23
]
. This feature highlights in particular the influence that a social network can have on the formation of opinions inside a population.

In order to mimic opinion dynamics within a society composed of a large number of individuals, we rely on Toscani’s seminal kinetic model
[
59
]
, which has been further expanded over more recent years (see, e.g.,
[
8
,
26
,
58
]
). Kinetic models describe opinion formation through binary interactions between agents and it is typically assumed that an individual’s opinion evolves according to two mechanisms: compromise dynamics, where agents vary their opinions through interactions with others, and self-thinking dynamics. The main assumption behind these kinetic models is that when two agents interact, only small variations of their opinions can occur. Considering this quasi-invariant regime of opinions, Toscani
[
59
]
showed that their dynamics can be described by Fokker–Planck-type equations, whose great mathematical advantage relies on the possibility of explicitly computing their equilibria, hence offering a clear description of key phenomena like consensus formation and opinion polarization
[
8
]
. These equilibria are crucial to understand how extreme opinions emerge within a society and preventing their formation is clearly important in epidemiological contexts, where polarized views can have harmful consequences. For example, in
[
62
]
it is showed that opinion polarization against protective behaviors during an epidemic can lead to a significant increase in the number of infections, highlighting the real-world impact of opinion dynamics.

In this paper, following
[
2
,
9
,
62
]
, we introduce a kinetic SIR-type model where, for the reasons previously described, the opinion of each individual plays a central role in determining the evolution of the epidemic. The operator modeling contagions and recoveries takes into account such opinions and leads to a generalization of the classical SIR model, allowing us to recover the latter after suitable integrations. Alongside this operator, we consider a collisional kinetic operator describing the evolution of opinions inside each compartment of individuals (susceptible, infected, and recovered) by taking into account an underlying graphon. Indeed, opinion dynamics could in general vary from one compartment to another since, for instance, infected individuals could be characterized by different self-thinking processes than susceptible ones. Our model is not fully kinetic like the one introduced in
[
45
]
, as we combine moment operators resembling ODE-type interactions with kinetic ones, in order to simulate complex phenomena. Finally, inspired by
[
58
]
, we also introduce a full kinetic model to measure the population’s degree of concern regarding the severity of the disease spread. The idea is to track the popularity of each product related to the epidemic: news, videos, and social posts, that individuals tend to share whenever the latter are aligned with their own opinion. Note that the evolution of the products’ popularity depends on the opinion dynamics, but the latter evolve independently of the former.

Aside from the novelty of the proposed model, from a mathematical viewpoint the main contributions of this work are twofold. Firstly, we prove that solutions to our kinetic SIR-type model converge to equilibrium in the standard
L^{1}
norm, making use of relative entropy techniques
[
4
,
8
,
29
]
to determine the relaxation rate. We also demonstrate a result on the convergence to equilibrium for solutions to the kinetic equation modeling the popularity of products, in the homogeneous Sobolev spaces
\dot{H}^{-s}
,
s\in\big(\frac{1}{2},1\big)

[
45
,
57
]
. Secondly, we develop two-dimensional (in phase space) structure-preserving schemes to simulate both models. These numerical algorithms, which we have made publicly available, build upon the seminal work of Pareschi and Zanella
[
50
]
and further extend more recent structure-preserving schemes (see, e.g.,
[
9
,
43
,
45
,
47
,
62
]
). The main novelty lies in the fact that our schemes are specifically designed to simulate compartmental models involving both kinetic and non-kinetic operators, and multiple distribution functions depending on more than one kinetic variable.

Similarly, from the point of view of applications we also emphasize the two main contributions of our work. On one side, we show how to measure the propensity to interact of each individual by looking at their connections on the graphon. This allows us to demonstrate that opinion polarization is more likely to occur among individuals with a low propensity to interact, while those inclined to interact tend to reach a consensus, highlighting the importance of social interactions for preventing the creation of dangerous extreme opinions. This aligns in particular with the results obtained in
[
8
]
, where the first and second authors studied the effects that the level of social activity of the agents has on opinion formation. On the other side, the numerical simulation allow us to highlight the effects of the graphon: opinion leaders in favor of protective behaviors help limit disease spread, whereas easily influenceable individuals may shift toward opinions opposing protective behaviors, potentially leading to more severe epidemics. Moreover, we introduce a time-dependent quantity, resembling the classical basic reproduction number, which presents oscillations that can lead to different epidemic waves. Importantly, these different waves are not related to an explicit external forcing (like in previous works; see, e.g.,
[
9
]
), but naturally arise from the complex structure of our model.

1.1.
Outline of this work

The article is organized as follows. In Section
2
we introduce our kinetic SIR system. In particular, concerning the modeling of opinion dynamics, starting from a Boltzmann description of the microscopic interactions, we derive the corresponding Fokker–Planck approximation in a quasi-invariant regime of the parameters. At the microscopic scale, the binary exchanges between agents depend on the social network through an interaction function that quantifies how strongly each individual can influence the others. At the macroscopic scale, instead, a graphon characterizes the tendency of agents to compare themselves with one another. In this section we also show why our model represents a generalization of the classical SIR equations, and we illustrate how to deduce the latter by suitable integration of the kinetic system. We also compute the (local) equilibria of the model. Finally, we prove several analytical properties of its solutions, including nonnegativity,
L^{p}
-regularity, and uniqueness.

In Section
3
we then introduce a simplification of the original model by replacing the previously defined interaction function and the graphon with a single quantity that incorporates all their information at the microscopic scale: the individuals’ propensity to interact. After computing the equilibria of this new model, we investigate the connection between the propensity to interact and the phenomena of opinion polarization and consensus formation. At last, we prove that solutions to the simplified kinetic compartmental model converge to equilibrium in the strong
L^{1}
distance and we also determine the relaxation rate.

We proceed in Section
4
by introducing a Fokker–Planck model, coupled with the original kinetic SIR system, that measures the population’s level of concern regarding the disease spread. We calculate the equilibria of this model and we analyze their relation with the ones of the compartmental system. We then exhibit the main analytical properties of its solutions (nonnegativity, regularity, and uniqueness) and we prove that they converge to equilibrium in the homogeneous Sobolev
\dot{H}^{-s}
distance, for any
s\in\big(\frac{1}{2},1\big)
.

Section
5
is dedicated to the numerical simulations. We start by introducing our structure-preserving scheme and by describing in details the algorithm. We then carry out several tests to reproduce the trends to equilibrium of the models demonstrated in the previous sections. We also analyze how the underlying graphon influences the evolution of the epidemic, both in presence and absence of opinion leaders. Moreover, we show the possible presence of different epidemic waves.

We conclude this work in Section
6
. As a last thing, we provide a link to the MATLAB code implementing our algorithms.

2.
A kinetic SIR model on graphons

We consider a large population of agents subdivided in the following epidemiologically relevant compartments: susceptible (
S
) agents are the ones that can contract the disease; infectious agents (
I
) are responsible for the spread of the disease; removed (
R
) agents are healed and can no longer spread the disease. Let us define the set of these three compartments as
\mathcal{C}=\{S,I,R\}
. We associate to each agent a characteristic trait
w\in\mathcal{I}=[-1,1]
which represents their opinion about the importance of protecting themselves and the others from the spread of the disease. In particular,
w=\pm 1
correspond to the two opposite believes, namely agents with opinion
w=-1
absolutely do not believe in the necessity of protections, whereas agents with opinion
w=1
are in complete agreement with a protective behavior. We suppose that the frequency of interactions agent–agent leading to variations in their opinion depends on an underlying social network, modeled by a graphon
[
10
,
11
,
26
,
31
]
, which is a measurable function
\mathcal{B}\colon[0,1]^{2}\to[0,+\infty)
satisfying
\mathcal{B}\in L^{1}([0,1]^{2})
. Therefore, we assume that each agent is further characterized by a static position
x\in\Omega=[0,1]
on the graphon. Note that naturally these social interactions are distinct from the ones leading to the spread of the disease.

In the kinetic approach, the evolution of agents within each compartment is described statistically via the use of distribution functions
f_{J}=f_{J}(t,x,w)
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, depending on time
t\geq 0
, position on the graphon
x\in\Omega
, and opinion
w\in\mathcal{I}
. This means that the quantity
f_{J}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w
counts the number of agents in the compartment
J\in\mathcal{C}
that at time
t
possess a position on the graphon in
[x,x+\mathrm{d}x]
and an opinion in
[w,w+\mathrm{d}w]
. The number of agents in each compartment and their average opinion are then respectively defined as

(1)

\rho_{J}(t)=\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}f_{J}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w,\qquad m_{J}(t)=\frac{1}{\rho_{J}(t)}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}wf_{J}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w.

Without loss of generality, we may assume that
\displaystyle\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\rho_{J}(0)=1
. We will be also interested in studying the evolution of the total average opinion of the population, given by

(2)

m(t)=\frac{1}{\displaystyle\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\rho_{J}(t)}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}wf_{J}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w.

The time evolution of the functions
f_{J}
is then obtained by coupling the compartmental epidemiological description with the kinetic evolution of the social variable
[
9
,
24
,
62
]
. Denoting with
\mathbf{f}=(f_{J})_{J\in\mathcal{C}}
the vector distribution function of the three compartments, their evolution is described by the multi-species kinetic model

(3)

\partial_{t}\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})+\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}),\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\ x\in\Omega,\ w\in\mathcal{I},

where the SIR-like operator
\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})=(E_{J}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}))_{J\in\mathcal{C}}
models the evolution of the epidemic by prescribing suitable exchanges between compartments, while the kinetic-type operator
\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})=(Q_{J}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}))_{J\in\mathcal{C}}
models the evolution of opinions inside the population. Moreover, the parameter
\tau>0
translates the fact that the opinion variations may happen at a different timescale than the disease spread. This means in particular that the proposed model is multiscale, since it interfaces the dynamics of the epidemic with that of the opinion. Let us now detail the explicit form of the operators
E
and
Q
.

2.1.
Effects of individuals’ opinions on epidemic spread

Following
[
9
]
, we model the compartmental exchanges through the integral SIR-like operators

(4)

\begin{split}E_{S}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})(t,x,w)&=-f_{S}(t,x,w)\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\beta_{T}(w,w_{*})f_{I}(t,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*},\\[5.69054pt]
E_{I}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})(t,x,w)&=f_{S}(t,x,w)\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\beta_{T}(w,w_{*})f_{I}(t,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*}-\gamma f_{I}(t,x,w),\\[11.38109pt]
E_{R}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})(t,x,w)&=\gamma f_{I}(t,x,w),\end{split}

defined for any
t\geq 0
,
x\in\Omega
, and
w\in\mathcal{I}
. Here, the recovery rate
\gamma>0
is such that
1/\gamma
gives the mean infectious period of the disease
[
23
]
, while
\beta_{T}(w,w_{*})
is a nonnegative decreasing (with respect to each variable taken individually) function that models the transition rate of agents between the compartments
S
and
I
, giving a measure of the impact of protective behaviors on the interactions between susceptible and infectious individuals. One possible choice is given by

(5)

\beta_{T}(w,w_{*})=\beta(1-w)^{\alpha}(1-w_{*})^{\alpha},\qquad w,w_{*}\in\mathcal{I},

for some parameters
\beta>0
(characterizing the baseline transmission rate of the epidemics) and
\alpha\geq 0
. In particular, we have assumed that the local incidence rate governing the transmission dynamics explicitly depends on the agents opinion
w
, meaning that the more protective an individual’s behavior is, the more likely they are to get vaccinated and the less likely they are to contract the disease. As a consequence of this modeling choice, the evolution of the disease is fully dependent on the agents’ opinion about the use of protective behaviors.

2.2.
Kinetic modeling of opinion dynamics

The opinion formation process is based on two mechanisms:

•

compromise dynamics, i.e., individuals tend to settle their differences of opinion;

•

opinion fluctuation, i.e., every interaction between individuals is associated with a variation in opinions due to self-thinking.

Consider an agent belonging to a compartment
J\in\mathcal{C}
and characterized by a position on the graphon and an opinion given by the couple
(x,w)\in\Omega\times\mathcal{I}
. When they interact with an agent from a compartment
J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
and having kinetic traits
(y,w_{*})\in\Omega\times\mathcal{I}
, their opinions vary according to the following microscopic interaction
[
59
]
:

(6)

\displaystyle w^{\prime}

\displaystyle=w+\lambda G(w,w_{*})P(x,y)(w_{*}-w)+D(w)\eta_{J},

\displaystyle w_{*}^{\prime}

\displaystyle=w_{*}+\lambda G(w_{*},w)P(y,x)(w-w_{*})+D(w_{*})\eta_{J^{\prime}}.

Here, the parameter
\lambda\in(0,1)
characterizes the strength of the (deterministic) exchange of opinions. The symmetric function
G(w,w_{*})\in[0,1]
describes how strongly the opinion
w_{*}
affects the agent with opinion
w
, therefore it is natural to assume that
G(w,w_{*})=\bar{G}(|w-w_{*}|)
with
\bar{G}
being a decreasing function that satisfies
\bar{G}(0)=1
. The function
P(x,y)\in[0,1]
ensures that the interactions depend on the connectivity of each agent and can be interpreted in different ways. For example, one can assume that the opinion exchanges (
6
) depends on the quality of the relationship between the two agents: the better this is, the more strongly they influence each other. Moreover, one might assume that highly connected individuals are less influenced by each interaction than poorly connected ones. In Section
3
we will analyze the function
P
in more details. The opinion fluctuations are then modeled by centered random variables
\eta_{J}
and
\eta_{J^{\prime}}
, which depend on each agent’s compartment and possess finite variance of respective values
\sigma_{J}^{2}>0
and
\sigma_{J^{\prime}}^{2}>0
. In particular, if both
G\equiv 1
and
P\equiv 1
, the constant
\lambda
would measure the propensity to move toward the average societal opinion (compromise dynamics), while the variances
\sigma_{J}^{2}
and
\sigma_{J^{\prime}}^{2}
would measure the degree of opinion spreading due to diffusion processes (self-thinking). Finally, the function
D(w)\in[0,1]
encodes the relevance of such diffusion; for example, agents with an indifferent opinion (
w\simeq 0
) diffuse the most (
D(0)=1
), while those with an extreme opinion (
w\simeq\pm 1
) are less influenced by external factors (
D(\pm 1)=0
).

Denoting with
\left\langle X\right\rangle=\mathbb{E}(X)
the expected value of some random variable
X
, simple computations show that

\begin{split}\left<w^{\prime}+w_{*}^{\prime}\right>&=w+w_{*}+G(w,w_{*})\lambda(w_{*}-w)(P(x,y)-P(y,x))+D(w)\left<\eta_{J}\right>+D(w_{*})\left<\eta_{J^{\prime}}\right>\\[5.69054pt]
&=w+w_{*}+G(w,w_{*})\lambda(w_{*}-w)(P(x,y)-P(y,x)).\end{split}

In particular, when the function
P
is symmetric, namely
P(x,y)=P(y,x)
, then
\left<w^{\prime}+w_{*}^{\prime}\right>=w+w_{*}
and thus the average opinion is preserved by the microscopic opinion interactions (
6
).

Following
[
59
]
we will usually consider

(7)

D(w)=\sqrt{1-w^{2}},\qquad w\in\mathcal{I},

which ensures that the post-interaction opinions
w^{\prime}
and
w_{*}^{\prime}
stay in the interval
\mathcal{I}
. However, several other choices for the self-diffusion function
D
are possible
[
47
,
59
]
.

If we neglect the compartmental exchanges due to the operator
\mathbf{E}
, employing standard methods of kinetic theory
[
48
]
and resorting to the derivation of the classical linear Boltzmann equation for elastic rarefied gases
[
16
]
, one can show that the temporal variation of the vector distribution function
\mathbf{f}
depends on a sequence of elementary binary interactions of type (
6
). In our case, given any smooth function
\varphi=\varphi(x,w)
(defining the observable quantities of the system), we assume that the evolution of each
f_{J}
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, is described by the integral equation

(8)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}&\varphi(x,w)f_{J}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\
&=\frac{1}{\tau}\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\int_{\Omega^{2}\times\mathcal{I}^{2}}\mathcal{B}(x,y)\left<\varphi(x,w^{\prime})-\varphi(x,w)\right>f_{J}(t,x,w)f_{J^{\prime}}(t,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w\mathrm{d}w_{*},\end{split}

where the kernel
\mathcal{B}\colon\Omega^{2}\to[0,+\infty)
is the given graphon, which can be thought of as the continuous equivalent of an adjacency matrix. In particular, the choice
\varphi\equiv 1
in (
8
) leads to the conservation of mass in each compartment, i.e., the total number of agents inside each class (and therefore also inside the whole population) does not change over time.

2.3.
Derivation of a mean-field description

Starting from the collisional-like operators defined through the weak formulation (
8
), we shall now perform a quasi-invariant limit procedure to derive a reduced model of Fokker–Planck-type, defining the operator
\mathbf{Q}
in system (
3
). Such a simplified mean-field setting will in fact allow us to compute its kinetic equilibria explicitly, and study the evolution of the associated macroscopic quantities (
1
) and (
2
).

Fixed
0<\epsilon\ll 1
, we introduce the scalings

(9)

\lambda\mapsto\epsilon\lambda,\qquad\sigma_{J}^{2}\mapsto\epsilon\sigma_{J}^{2},\qquad\tau\mapsto\epsilon\tau,

which imply that the interaction (
6
) produces only an extremely small variation of the opinion, while the frequency of interactions is increased accordingly. Next, we perform a Taylor expansion of the term encoding the binary interactions in (
8
), namely

\varphi(x,w^{\prime})-\varphi(x,w)=\partial_{w}\varphi(x,w)(w^{\prime}-w)+\frac{1}{2}\partial^{2}_{w}\varphi(x,w)(w^{\prime}-w)^{2}+\frac{1}{6}\partial^{3}_{w}\varphi(x,\tilde{w})(w^{\prime}-w)^{3},

where
\tilde{w}\in(\min\{w,w^{\prime}\},\max\{w,w^{\prime}\})
. Hence, we can rewrite (
8
) as

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}&\varphi(x,w)f_{J}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\[5.69054pt]
&=\frac{1}{\epsilon\tau}\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\int_{\Omega^{2}\times\mathcal{I}^{2}}\mathcal{B}(x,y)\partial_{w}\varphi(x,w)\left<w^{\prime}-w\right>f_{J}(t,x,w)f_{J^{\prime}}(t,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w\mathrm{d}w_{*}\\[5.69054pt]
&\ +\frac{1}{2\epsilon\tau}\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\int_{\Omega^{2}\times\mathcal{I}^{2}}\mathcal{B}(x,y)\partial^{2}_{w}\varphi(x,w)\left<(w^{\prime}-w)^{2}\right>f_{J}(t,x,w)f_{J^{\prime}}(t,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w\mathrm{d}w_{*}\\[5.69054pt]
&\ +\frac{1}{\epsilon\tau}R_{\varphi}(f_{J}),\end{split}

where
R_{\varphi}(f_{J})=\frac{1}{6}\displaystyle\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\int_{\Omega^{2}\times\mathcal{I}^{2}}\mathcal{B}(x,y)\partial^{3}_{w}\varphi(x,\tilde{w})\left<(w^{\prime}-w)^{3}\right>f_{J}(t,x,w)f_{J^{\prime}}(t,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w\mathrm{d}w_{*}
. We then substitute
w^{\prime}=w+G(w,w_{*})P(x,y)\lambda(w_{*}-w)+D(w)\eta_{J}
and compute the mean of all random variables, recalling that
\left<\eta_{J}\right>=0
. By now taking the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, under the hypothesis that
\left<|\eta_{J}|^{3}\right><+\infty
, we can check that
R_{\varphi}(f_{J})/\epsilon\to 0

[
19
,
59
]
. Integrating back by parts in order to get rid of the derivatives of
\varphi(x,w)
and assuming that the boundary terms vanish, we formally recover a Fokker–Planck-type equation
[
19
,
49
,
59
]
describing the evolution of each
f_{J}
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
,

(10)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}f_{J}&=\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\lambda\partial_{w}\left(\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{K}[f_{J^{\prime}}]f_{J}\right)+\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}\partial^{2}_{w}\left(D^{2}(w)\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{H}[f_{J^{\prime}}]f_{J}\right)\right)\\[5.69054pt]
&=\frac{1}{\tau}Q_{J}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\ x\in\Omega,\ w\in\mathcal{I},\end{split}

where we have defined, for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
, the nonlocal operators

(11)

\mathcal{K}[f_{J}](t,x,w)=\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{B}(x,y)P(x,y)G(w,w_{*})(w-w_{*})f_{J}(t,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;x\in\Omega,\;w\in\mathcal{I},

and

(12)

\mathcal{H}[f_{J}](t,x)=\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{B}(x,y)f_{J}(t,y,w)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;x\in\Omega.

Obviously, the functionals
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{H}
must be well defined (i.e., must be finite) for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
. Therefore, we require that the following integrability conditions

(13)

\mathcal{B}(x,\cdot)f_{J}(t,\cdot,\cdot)\in L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I}),\qquad J\in\mathcal{C},

hold for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
x\in\Omega
. Moreover, system (
10
) is completed with the no-flux boundary conditions

(14)

\begin{cases}\left.D^{2}(w)\displaystyle\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{H}[f_{J^{\prime}}](t,x)f_{J}(t,x,w)\right|_{w=\pm 1}=0,\\[17.07164pt]
\left.\lambda\displaystyle\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{K}[f_{J^{\prime}}](t,x,w)f_{J}(t,x,w)+\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}\partial_{w}\left(D^{2}(w)\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{H}[f_{J^{\prime}}](t,x)f_{J}(t,x,w)\right)\right|_{w=\pm 1}=0,\end{cases}

holding for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
x\in\Omega
, where the first relation is also linked to the property
D(\pm 1)=0
. Finally, notice that the parameters
\lambda
and
\sigma_{J}^{2}
(measuring the strength of compromise and self-thinking processes) appear in equation (
10
) as coefficients of the drift and diffusion terms, respectively. As a consequence, small values of
\nu_{J}=\sigma_{J}^{2}/\lambda
correspond to compromise-dominated opinion dynamics, while large values of
\nu_{J}
characterize self-thinking-dominated opinion dynamics.

As mentioned, the effects of opinion exchanges on the evolution of the relevant macroscopic quantities (
1
) and (
2
) can be inferred from the three Fokker–Planck equations (
10
). The operators
Q_{J}
preserve the mass of each compartment due to the second boundary condition of (
14
), hence the number of agents in each class varies only according to the operator
\mathbf{E}
of (
3
). On the other hand, the evolution of the mean compartmental opinions depend on both operators, since
\mathbf{Q}
describes how individuals’ opinion changes, while
\mathbf{E}
can cause a variation of opinion within each compartment by removing agents from it or adding new ones from other classes. Indeed, from the boundary conditions (
14
) it follows that, for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
,

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}&wf_{J}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w=\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}wE_{J}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w-\frac{\lambda}{\tau}\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{K}[f_{J^{\prime}}]f_{J}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\[5.69054pt]
&=\ \frac{1}{\tau}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}wE_{J}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\[5.69054pt]
&\quad-\frac{\lambda}{\tau}\sum_{J^{\prime}\neq J}\int_{\Omega^{2}\times\mathcal{I}^{2}}\mathcal{B}(x,y)P(x,y)(w-w_{*})f_{J}(t,x,w)f_{J^{\prime}}(t,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w\mathrm{d}w_{*},\end{split}

where
\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}wE_{J}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w
describes the opinion variations due to the epidemiological operator. Summing up the above equations and assuming that the graphon
\mathcal{B}
and the interaction function
P
are symmetric, we also deduce the conservation of the total average opinion
m
, since all contributions depending on
\mathbf{E}
cancel out (by definition (
4
) and in analogy with the classical SIR system, it holds that
\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}E_{J}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})\equiv 0
, also implying the conservation of the total mass).

Remark 1
.

Analogous computations to the ones performed to determine the evolution of the average opinion show that, for any fixed
x\in\Omega
,
\partial_{t}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{H}[f_{J}](t,x)=0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Therefore, it is enough to require that the integrability conditions (
13
) hold only for the initial distributions
f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}(x,w)=f_{J}(t=0,x,w)
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
. This conservation property of the functional
\mathcal{H}
will be crucial for the analysis conducted in Subsection
2.6
.

2.4.
Macroscopic SIR systems

In the following, any solution
\mathbf{f}=(f_{J})_{J\in\mathcal{C}}
of the kinetic SIR system (
3
) satisfying
\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})=0
will be termed
local equilibrium
of the kinetic operator and denoted by
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{eq}}=(f_{J}^{\mathrm{eq}})_{J\in\mathcal{C}}
. If we suppose, for the moment, that the opinion exchanges happen on a much faster timescale than that of the epidemic spread, which amounts to consider a regime
\tau\ll 1
, one expects
\mathbf{f}
to quickly converge toward
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{eq}}
. In this asymptotics, if we choose
\alpha=0
in the function
\beta_{T}
defined by (
5
), when the kinetic model evolves around a local equilibrium it can be approximated by a closed macroscopic SIR system prescribing the temporal evolution of the densities
\bm{\rho}=(\rho_{J})_{J\in\mathcal{C}}
. Actually, we can say more: integrating system (
3
) over
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
, since the kinetic operators
Q_{J}
are mass-preserving, one easily checks that the densities
\rho_{J}
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, solve the classical SIR equations

(15)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\rho_{S}=-\beta\rho_{S}\rho_{I},\\[5.69054pt]
&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\rho_{I}=\beta\rho_{S}\rho_{I}-\gamma\rho_{I},\\[5.69054pt]
&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\rho_{R}=\gamma\rho_{I},\end{aligned}\right.

and we can thus infer several analytical properties on the quantities
(\rho_{J})_{J\in\mathcal{C}}
. For this, let us introduce the basic reproduction number
\mathcal{R}_{0}=\beta/\gamma

[
61
]
, which can be interpreted biologically as the expected number of cases produced by one infective agent in a large population where all other individuals are susceptible to the infection. First of all, there exists a unique equilibrium point
\bm{\rho}^{\infty}=(\rho_{J}^{\infty})_{J\in\mathcal{C}}
depending only on the given initial conditions
\rho_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}=\rho_{J}(0)
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, which is globally asymptotically stable. In particular,
\rho_{S}^{\infty}>0
,
\rho_{I}^{\infty}=0
, and
\rho_{R}^{\infty}=1-\rho_{S}^{\infty}
, where
\rho_{S}^{\infty}
is the unique solution in the interval
(0,1/\rho_{R}(0))
of the equation

(16)

\log\frac{\rho_{S}^{\infty}}{\rho_{S}^{\mathrm{in}}}+\mathcal{R}_{0}(\rho_{S}^{\mathrm{in}}+\rho_{I}^{\mathrm{in}}-\rho_{S}^{\infty})=0.

Moreover,
\rho_{S}
and
\rho_{R}
are respectively a decreasing and an increasing function of time, which imply the upper and lower bounds
\rho_{S}^{\infty}\leq\rho_{S}(t)\leq\rho_{S}^{\mathrm{in}}
and
\rho_{R}^{\mathrm{in}}\leq\rho_{R}(t)\leq\rho_{R}^{\infty}
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. At last, the behavior of
\rho_{I}
depends on the parameter
\mathcal{R}_{0}
, so that whenever
\mathcal{R}_{0}<1
one sees that
0\leq\rho_{I}(t)\leq\rho_{I}^{\mathrm{in}}
is a decreasing function of time converging exponentially to zero at a rate at least
\mathcal{O}(\exp(-\gamma(1-\mathcal{R}_{0})t))
, while for
\mathcal{R}_{0}>1
the density initially increases up to a maximum value
\rho_{I}^{\max}
, given by

(17)

\rho_{I}^{\max}=-\frac{1}{\mathcal{R}_{0}}(\log\mathcal{R}_{0}+\log\rho_{S}^{\mathrm{in}}+1)+\rho_{S}^{\mathrm{in}}+\rho_{I}^{\mathrm{in}},

before decaying once again to zero exponentially fast, which readily implies the bounds
0\leq\rho_{I}(t)\leq\rho_{I}^{\max}<1
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

On the other hand, if we set
\alpha=1
in (
5
), integrating system (
3
) in
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
, we obtain a generalization of the classical SIR system (
15
) having the form

(18)

\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\rho_{S}=-\beta(1-m_{S})(1-m_{I})\rho_{S}\rho_{I},\\[5.69054pt]
&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\rho_{I}=\beta(1-m_{S})(1-m_{I})\rho_{S}\rho_{I}-\gamma\rho_{I},\\[5.69054pt]
&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\rho_{R}=\gamma\rho_{I},\end{aligned}\right.

which is not closed since it does not contain any information on the evolution of the means
m_{S}
and
m_{I}
, but it highlights the influence of the agents’ opinion on the epidemic spread. We can therefore introduce the time-dependent quantity, hereafter named
effective reproduction number
,

(19)

\mathcal{R}_{\textnormal{eff}}(t)=\frac{\beta}{\gamma}(1-m_{S}(t))(1-m_{I}(t))\rho_{S}(t),

which, from a mathematical point of view, has the same effect as the basic reproduction number, although biologically they are not equivalent.

2.5.
Local equilibria

In order to understand the long-time behavior of our kinetic SIR system, we first have to analyze the equilibria of the Fokker–Planck equation (
10
). For the sake of simplicity, we fix
J\in\mathcal{C}
and focus our attention only on one of the Fokker–Planck equations (
10
), with an initial condition of unit mass. Moreover, we assume that
G\equiv 1
and
D(w)=\sqrt{1-w^{2}}
. Let us define, for
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
x\in\Omega
, the quantities

(20)

\begin{split}\rho_{\mathcal{B}}(t,x)&=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{B}(x,y)f_{J}(t,y,w)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w,\\[11.38109pt]
\rho_{\mathcal{B},P}(t,x)&=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{B}(x,y)P(x,y)f_{J}(t,y,w)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w,\\[11.38109pt]
m_{\mathcal{B},P}(t,x)&=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{B}(x,y)P(x,y)wf_{J}(t,y,w)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w.\end{split}

Recall in particular from Remark
1
that
\rho_{\mathcal{B}}
and
\rho_{\mathcal{B},P}
are conserved over time. The steady state
B_{J}=B_{J}(x,w)
of (
10
) must satisfy, for any
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
,

(21)

\lambda\left(\rho_{\mathcal{B},P}(x)w-m_{\mathcal{B},P}^{\infty}(x)\right)B_{J}(x,w)+\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}\partial_{w}\left((1-w^{2})\rho_{\mathcal{B}}(x)B_{J}(x,w)\right)=0,

where
m_{\mathcal{B},P}^{\infty}
denotes the quantity
m_{\mathcal{B},P}
computed from the limit distribution
B_{J}
. Therefore, after developing the derivative with respect to
w
, it is easy to see that the Fokker–Planck model relaxes toward beta distributions (with respect to
w
) of the form

(22)

B_{J}(x,w)=c_{J}(x)(1+w)^{-1+\frac{1}{\nu_{J}\rho_{\mathcal{B}}(x)}(\rho_{\mathcal{B},P}(x)+m_{\mathcal{B},P}^{\infty}(x))}(1-w)^{-1+\frac{1}{\nu_{J}\rho_{\mathcal{B}}(x)}(\rho_{\mathcal{B},P}(x)-m_{\mathcal{B},P}^{\infty}(x))},\quad x\in\Omega,\;w\in\mathcal{I},

where
c_{J}(x)>0
is a suitable normalization function guaranteeing that
\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}B_{J}(x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w=1
.

Unfortunately, the structure of the equilibrium states (
22
) cannot be easily interpreted. For this reason, in Section
3
we will show how to characterize them in a more explicit way by considering a slight simplification of the Fokker–Planck operators, which will allow us to analyze the long-time behavior of the full kinetic SIR model.

2.6.
Analytical properties

We conclude this section with an investigation of the main analytical properties of the kinetic SIR model (
3
), where the Fokker–Planck operators
Q_{J}
have been defined in (
10
). Throughout this study, in order to justify our calculations we will tacitly assume that the integrability condition (
13
) holds (so that the operators
\mathcal{K}
and
\mathcal{H}
are well defined) and that the functions
G
and
D
are regular enough.

We begin by showing that solutions to (
3
) starting from a nonnegative initial datum remain nonnegative over time. We restrict this analysis to the simplified case
\alpha=0
in (
5
), which will be considered again in Subsection
3.4
. The idea is to separately study the epidemic operator
\mathbf{E}
and the kinetic one
\mathbf{Q}
. In particular, in order to deal with the operators
Q_{J}
we need the following preliminary result (which will be also useful to recover the uniqueness of solutions) concerning an
L^{1}
comparison principle for solutions to the Fokker–Planck equation (
10
).

Lemma 1
.

Let
(f_{J})_{J\in\mathcal{C}}
be a solution to the Fokker–Planck system (
10
)
satisfying the no-flux boundary conditions (
14
), and posed on
\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega\times\mathcal{I}
with initial conditions
f_{J}(0,x,w)=f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}(x,w)
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
. If
f^{\mathrm{in}}_{J}\in L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})
, then the
L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})
norm of
f_{J}
is non-increasing for
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Proof.

Consider, for some parameter
\varepsilon>0
, a regularized increasing approximation of the sign function
\textrm{sign}_{\varepsilon}(u)
,
u\in\mathbb{R}
. We introduce a regularization
|f_{J}|_{\varepsilon}(t,x,w)
of
|f_{J}|(t,x,w)
by defining it as the primitive of
\textrm{sign}_{\varepsilon}(f_{J})(t,x,w)
, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
. Multiplying by
\textrm{sign}_{\varepsilon}(f_{J})
both sides of equation (
10
), integrating with respect to
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
and applying integration by parts together with the no-flux boundary conditions (
14
), we can successively compute

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}|f_{J}|_{\varepsilon}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}w=&-\lambda\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\textrm{sign}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(f_{J})f_{J}\partial_{w}f_{J}\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{K}[f_{J^{\prime}}]\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\
&\quad-\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\textrm{sign}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(f_{J})\partial_{w}f_{J}\partial_{w}\left(D^{2}(w)f_{J}\right)\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{H}[f_{J^{\prime}}]\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\[5.69054pt]
=&-\lambda\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\textrm{sign}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(f_{J})f_{J}\partial_{w}f_{J}\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{K}[f_{J^{\prime}}]\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\
&\quad-\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\textrm{sign}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(f_{J})f_{J}\partial_{w}f_{J}\partial_{w}D^{2}(w)\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{H}[f_{J^{\prime}}]\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\
&\qquad-\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\textrm{sign}_{\varepsilon}^{\prime}(f_{J})\left(\partial_{w}f_{J}\right)^{2}D^{2}(w)\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{H}[f_{J^{\prime}}]\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w.\end{split}

Noticing that

\textrm{sign}^{\prime}_{\varepsilon}(f_{J})f_{J}\partial_{w}f_{J}=\partial_{w}(\textrm{sign}_{\varepsilon}(f_{J})f_{J}-|f_{J}|_{\varepsilon}),

we can integrate back by parts the first two integrals and observe that they vanish as
\varepsilon\to 0
, since by construction
\textrm{sign}_{\varepsilon}(f_{J})f_{J}-|f_{J}|_{\varepsilon}\underset{\varepsilon\to 0}{\longrightarrow}0
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
. The thesis follows from the fact that the last integral is nonnegative.
∎

We are now able to prove the following property of nonnegativity-preservation.

Proposition 1

(Nonnegativity)
.

Let
\mathbf{f}
be a solution to the SIR kinetic model (
3
) with Fokker–Planck operator
\mathbf{Q}
, defined componentwise by (
10
) and satisfying the no-flux boundary conditions (
14
). Assume moreover that
\alpha=0
in (
5
). Consider for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
an initial distribution
f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}\in L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})
such that
f^{\mathrm{in}}_{J}(x,w)\geq 0
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
. Then,
f_{J}(t,x,w)\geq 0
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
,
w\in\mathcal{I}
, and for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

Proof.

Define the negative part of each distribution
f_{J}
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, via the regularization introduced in the proof of Lemma
1
, namely

f_{J,\varepsilon}^{-}(t,x,w)=\frac{1}{2}(|f_{J}|_{\varepsilon}(t,x,w)-f_{J}(t,x,w)),\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;x\in\Omega,\;w\in\mathcal{I}.

Multiplying the first equation of kinetic SIR model (
3
) on both sides by
\textrm{sign}_{\varepsilon}(f_{S})
and integrating in
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
, from Lemma
1
and since
\alpha=0
we initially deduce that

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}|f_{S,\varepsilon}|(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w&\leq-\beta\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}|f_{S,\varepsilon}|\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}f_{I}(t,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*}\\[5.69054pt]
&=-\beta\rho_{I}(t)\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}|f_{S,\varepsilon}|\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w,\end{split}

where we have used the fact that the densities
(\rho_{J})_{J\in\mathcal{C}}
solve the macroscopic SIR system (
15
). Therefore

\begin{split}2\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}f_{S,\varepsilon}^{-}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}w&=\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}|f_{S}|_{\varepsilon}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}w-\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}f_{S}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}w\\[8.53581pt]
&\leq-\beta\rho_{I}(t)\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}|f_{S,\varepsilon}|\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w+\beta\rho_{S}(t)\rho_{I}(t)\\[5.69054pt]
&=\beta\rho_{I}(t)\left(\rho_{S}(t)-\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}|f_{S,\varepsilon}|\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\right),\end{split}

and taking the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, the nonnegativity of
f_{S}
follows because
\rho_{S}(t)\leq\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}|f_{S}|(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w
.
Next, using that
f_{S}
is nonnegative,

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}|f_{I,\varepsilon}|(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\leq\beta\rho_{I}(t)\rho_{S}(t)-\gamma\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}|f_{I,\varepsilon}|\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w,\end{split}

which implies that

\begin{split}2\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}f_{I,\varepsilon}^{-}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}w\leq\gamma\left(\rho_{I}(t)-\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}|f_{I,\varepsilon}|\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\right),\end{split}

and taking again limit the
\varepsilon\to 0
, the nonnegativity of
f_{I}
follows similarly. Finally, the nonnegativity of
f_{R}
is trivial since
f_{I}
is nonnegative.
∎

Remark 2
.

If a solution
\mathbf{f}
to the SIR kinetic model (
3
) is nonnegative, then the mass of each compartment is always bounded between 0 and 1. Indeed, the nonnegativity implies that
\rho_{J}(t)\geq 0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
J\in\mathcal{C}
, from which follows that
\rho_{J}(t)\leq 1
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
J\in\mathcal{C}
since we have conservation of the total mass.

Assuming the nonnegativity of solutions, their
L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})
regularity directly follows from the conservation of mass. Indeed, suppose that
f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}\in L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})
for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
, and that they are nonnegative. Since from Proposition
1
we know that
f_{J}(t,x,w)\geq 0
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
,
w\in\mathcal{I}
, and for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, then
||f_{J}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}=\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}f_{J}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Therefore summing up the three equations of system (
3
) and integrating with respect to
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
, we get that for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}

\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}||f_{J}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}||f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})},

because the contributions of the operator
\mathbf{E}
cancel out and the Fokker–Planck operators
Q_{J}
are mass-preserving. In conclusion,
f_{J}\in L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})
for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
and any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

One possible strategy to prove the
L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})
regularity of solutions for any
q\in[2,\infty)
consists in rewriting the diffusion term of the Fokker–Planck equation (
10
) in such a way that it exhibits a more manageable self-adjoint structure, and the temporal evolution of
||f_{J}||_{L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}
can be dealt with using suitable integration by parts. The price to pay is to impose additional no-flux boundary conditions ensuring enough regularity at the boundaries. The hypothesis that
q\geq 2
will be needed to handle derivatives of the form
\partial_{w}f_{J}^{q-1}
. However, since the set
\Omega\times\mathcal{I}
is bounded, if
f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}\in L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, for some
q\in(1,2)
, then
f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}\in L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, and we can at least obtain the
L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})
regularity of solutions. We will have to assume that
\beta_{T}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{I}^{2})
, but we point out that this hypothesis is not restrictive and the modeling choice (
5
) satisfies it for any
\alpha\geq 0
.

Proposition 2

(Regularity)
.

Let
q\in[2,+\infty)
and let
\mathbf{f}
be a solution to the SIR kinetic system (
3
). Suppose that the
f_{J}
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, are nonnegative. Assume that
\beta_{T}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{I}^{2})
and
\mathcal{H}[f_{J}]\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega)
for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
. Furthermore, we impose that for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
the additional no-flux boundary conditions

(23)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\left.D^{2}(w)f_{J}^{q-1}(t,x,w)\partial_{w}f_{J}(t,x,w)\right|_{w=\pm 1}=0,\\[5.69054pt]
&\left.f_{J}(t,x,w)\right|_{w=\pm 1}=0,\end{aligned}\right.

hold for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
x\in\Omega
. If
f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}\in L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, then
f_{J}\in L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. More precisely, we have the following estimate

||f_{J}(t,\cdot,\cdot)||_{L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}\leq 3^{\frac{1}{q}}\max_{J\in\mathcal{C}}||f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}||_{L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}\exp(\eta_{q}t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

where the constants
\eta_{q}>0
are equi-bounded with respect to
q
.

Proof.

Fix
q\in[2,\infty)
and multiply each equation of system (
3
) by
f_{J}^{q-1}
, with respect to the corresponding index
J\in\mathcal{C}
. After integrating in
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
we initially obtain

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{q}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}||f_{S}||^{q}_{L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}=-\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}f_{S}^{q}\left(\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\beta_{T}(w,w_{*})f_{I}(t,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*}\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\
&\hskip 256.0748pt+\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}Q_{S}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})f_{S}^{q-1}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w,\\[5.69054pt]
&\frac{1}{q}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}||f_{I}||^{q}_{L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}=\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}f_{S}f_{I}^{q-1}\left(\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\beta_{T}(w,w_{*})f_{I}(t,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*}\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w-\gamma||f_{I}||^{q}_{L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}\\
&\hskip 256.0748pt+\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}Q_{I}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})f_{I}^{q-1}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w,\\[5.69054pt]
&\frac{1}{q}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}||f_{R}||^{q}_{L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}=\gamma\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}f_{I}f_{R}^{q-1}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w+\frac{1}{\tau}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}Q_{R}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})f_{R}^{q-1}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w.\end{aligned}\right.

The idea is to control (up to a constant) each term on the right-hand side of the above equations with the quantities
\|f_{J}\|_{L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}^{q}
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
and then conclude by applying the Grönwall’s Lemma.

Let us start from the epidemiological operators. We first derive the straightforward control

\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}f_{S}^{q}(t,x,w)\left(\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\beta_{T}(w,w_{*})f_{I}(t,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*}\right)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\leq||\beta_{T}||_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{I}^{2})}||f_{I}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}||f_{S}||^{q}_{L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})},

where
||f_{I}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}=\rho_{I}\in[0,1]
. Using Hölder’s and Young’s inequalities we then infer the bound

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}f_{S}(t,x,w)f_{I}^{q-1}(t,x,w)\bigg(\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}&\beta_{T}(w,w_{*})f_{I}(t,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*}\bigg)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\[5.69054pt]
&\leq||\beta_{T}||_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{I}^{2})}||f_{I}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}\left(\frac{1}{q}||f_{S}||^{q}_{L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}+\frac{q-1}{q}||f_{I}||^{q}_{L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}\right).\end{split}

Finally, Hölder’s inequality also implies that

\gamma\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}f_{I}(t,x,w)f_{R}^{q-1}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\leq\gamma\left(\frac{1}{q}||f_{I}||^{q}_{L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}+\frac{q-1}{q}||f_{R}||^{q}_{L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}\right).

Moving now to the Fokker–Planck operators, we observe that

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}Q_{J}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})f_{J}^{q-1}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w=&\overbrace{\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\partial_{w}\left(D^{2}(w)\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{H}[f_{J^{\prime}}]\partial_{w}f_{J}\right)f_{J}^{q-1}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w}^{\mathcal{T}_{1}}\\[5.69054pt]
&\quad+\underbrace{\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\partial_{w}\left(\left(\lambda\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{K}[f_{J^{\prime}}]+\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}\partial_{w}D^{2}(w)\right)f_{J}\right)f_{J}^{q-1}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}.\end{split}

Integrating by parts
\mathcal{T}_{1}
and using the first of the boundary conditions (
23
) allows to show that this term is nonpositive, since

\mathcal{T}_{1}=-\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}(q-1)\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}D^{2}(w)\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{H}[f_{J^{\prime}}]\left(\partial_{w}f_{J}\right)^{2}f_{J}^{q-2}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\leq 0.

The second term
\mathcal{T}_{2}
can be treated in two different ways. At first we perform an integration by parts and use the boundary conditions (
23
) to get

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}_{2}&=-\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\left(\lambda\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{K}[f_{J^{\prime}}]+\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}\partial_{w}D^{2}(w)\right)f_{J}\partial_{w}f_{J}^{q-1}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\[5.69054pt]
&=-(q-1)\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\left(\lambda\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{K}[f_{J^{\prime}}]+\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}\partial_{w}D^{2}(w)\right)f_{J}^{q-1}\partial_{w}f_{J}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w.\end{split}

Secondly, we compute the derivative with respect to
w
to obtain

\begin{split}\mathcal{T}_{2}=&\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\left(\lambda\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{K}[f_{J^{\prime}}]+\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}\partial_{w}D^{2}(w)\right)f_{J}^{q-1}\partial_{w}f_{J}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\[5.69054pt]
&\qquad\qquad+\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\partial_{w}\left(\lambda\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{K}[f_{J^{\prime}}]+\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}\partial_{w}D^{2}(w)\right)f_{J}^{q}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w.\end{split}

Therefore we can split
\mathcal{T}_{2}
as
\mathcal{T}_{2}=\frac{1}{q}\mathcal{T}_{2}+\frac{q-1}{q}\mathcal{T}_{2}
to finally write

\mathcal{T}_{2}=\frac{q-1}{q}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\partial_{w}\left(\lambda\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{K}[f_{J^{\prime}}]+\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}\partial_{w}D^{2}(w)\right)f_{J}^{q}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w.

Hence, expanding the derivative with respect to
w
of the operator
\mathcal{K}
, a direct estimate shows that (recall Remark
1
)

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}Q_{J}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})f_{J}^{q-1}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w&\leq\frac{q-1}{q}\bigg(\lambda\left(2\left|\left|\partial_{w}G\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{I}^{2})}||\mathcal{H}[f_{J}]||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega)}+||\mathcal{H}[f_{J}]||_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+}\times\Omega)}\right)\\
&\hskip 170.71652pt+\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}\left|\left|\partial_{w}^{2}D^{2}\right|\right|_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{I})}\bigg)||f_{J}||^{q}_{L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})},\end{split}

for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
. In conclusion,

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}||f_{J}||_{L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}^{q}\leq C\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}||f_{J}||_{L^{q}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}^{q},

with a constant
C=C(q,\gamma,\lambda,\sigma_{J}^{2},||\beta_{T}||_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{I}^{2})},||\mathcal{H}[f_{J}]||_{L^{\infty}([0,\infty)\times\Omega)},||\partial_{w}G||_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{I}^{2})},||\partial_{w}^{2}D^{2}(w)||_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{I})})
, and the sought regularity estimate follows from an application of Grönwall’s Lemma.
∎

.

The additional no-flux boundary conditions (

)) can diverge at

The constants

are equi-bounded by some

.

We conclude this section by proving a uniqueness result. The main difficulty lies in the fact that we would like to linearize the Fokker–Planck equations (
10
), so that the difference of two solutions is still a solution.

Proposition 3

(Uniqueness)
.

Let
\mathbf{f}
and
\mathbf{g}
be two solutions of the SIR kinetic system (
3
), with
\beta_{T}\in L^{\infty}(\mathcal{I}^{2})
. Suppose that
f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}},g_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}\in L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
. Moreover, assume that for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
the equality
\mathcal{K}[f_{J}](t,x,w)=\mathcal{K}[g_{J}](t,x,w)
holds for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and for a.e.
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
. If
f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}(x,w)=g_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}(x,w)
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, for a.e
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
, then
f_{J}(t,x,w)=g_{J}(t,x,w)
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and for a.e.
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
.

Proof.

Since for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and for a.e
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
we have
\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{K}[f_{J}](t,x,w)=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{K}[g_{J}](t,x,w)
and
\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{H}[f_{J}](t,x)=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{H}[g_{J}](t,x)
(recall Remark
1
), the differences
f_{J}-g_{J}
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, of two solutions to the Fokker–Planck equation (
10
) solve the following system:

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}(f_{S}-g_{S})&=-\bigg(f_{S}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\beta_{T}(w,w_{*})f_{I}(t,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*}-g_{S}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\beta_{T}(w,w_{*})g_{I}(t,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*}\bigg)\\
&\hskip 312.9803pt+\frac{1}{\tau}Q_{S}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{g}),\\
\partial_{t}(f_{I}-g_{I})&=\bigg(f_{S}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\beta_{T}(w,w_{*})f_{I}(t,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*}-g_{S}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\beta_{T}(w,w_{*})g_{I}(t,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*}\bigg)\\
&\hskip 256.0748pt-\gamma(f_{I}-g_{I})+\frac{1}{\tau}Q_{I}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{g}),\\
\partial_{t}(f_{R}-g_{R})&=\gamma(f_{I}-g_{I})+\frac{1}{\tau}Q_{R}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}-\mathbf{g}).\end{aligned}\right.

Now, the idea is to recover an
L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})
comparison principle for the differences
f_{J}-g_{J}
to get rid of the Fokker–Planck operators. This is done by introducing an increasing regularized approximation of the sign function
\textrm{sign}_{\varepsilon}(u)
,
u\in\mathbb{R}
, and defining through it a regularization
|f_{J}-g_{J}|_{\varepsilon}
of
|f_{J}-g_{J}|
for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
. Multiplying each equation in the previous system by the corresponding
\textrm{sign}_{\varepsilon}(f_{J}-g_{J})
, integrating with respect to
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
, and taking the limit
\varepsilon\to 0
, we easily recover the estimates (see Lemma
1
)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}||f_{S}-g_{S}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}\leq||\beta_{T}||_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{I}^{2})}\left(||f_{I}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}||f_{S}-g_{S}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}+||g_{S}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}||f_{I}-g_{I}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}\right),\\[11.38109pt]
&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}||f_{I}-g_{I}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}\leq||\beta_{T}||_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{I}^{2})}\left(||f_{I}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}||f_{S}-g_{S}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}+||g_{S}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}||f_{I}-g_{I}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}\right)\\
&\hskip 369.88582pt+\gamma||f_{I}-g_{I}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})},\\
&\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}||f_{R}-g_{R}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}\leq\gamma||f_{I}-g_{I}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})},\end{aligned}\right.

and we apply Grönwall’s inequality to conclude that

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}||f_{J}-g_{J}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}\leq C\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}||f_{J}-g_{J}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})},

where
C=C(\gamma,||\beta_{T}||_{L^{\infty}(\mathcal{I}^{2})},||f_{I}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})},||g_{S}||_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})})
. Since the initial distributions coincide, the above estimate implies the uniqueness of solutions.
∎

Finally, we provide a sufficient condition for equalities
\mathcal{K}[f_{J}](t,x,w)=\mathcal{K}[g_{J}](t,x,w)
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
to hold for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and for a.e.
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mathbf{f}
and
\mathbf{g}
be two solutions of the SIR kinetic system (
3
) such that
f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}(x,w)=g_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}(x,w)
for a.e.
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
. If the functionals
\mathcal{K}[f_{J}](t,x,w)
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, are analytical, then

\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{K}[f_{J}](t,x,w)\equiv\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{K}[g_{J}](t,x,w)

for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and for a.e.
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
.

Proof.

Following
[
8
, Lemma 2]
, since the initial distributions coincide, the idea is to show that

\partial_{t}^{n}\left.\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\big(\mathcal{K}[f_{J}](t,x,w)-\mathcal{K}[g_{J}](t,x,w)\big)\right|_{t=0}=0,

for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
, and for a.e.
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
. For
n=1
it holds that

\begin{split}\partial_{t}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}&\big(\mathcal{K}[f_{J}]-\mathcal{K}[g_{J}]\big)\bigg|_{t=0}=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{B}(x,y)P(x,y)G(w,w_{*})(w-w_{*})\partial_{t}(f_{J}-g_{J})(t,y,w_{*})\big|_{t=0}\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*}\\[5.69054pt]
&=\frac{\lambda}{\tau}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\mathcal{B}(x,y)P(x,y)G(w,w_{*})(w-w_{*})\\
&\hskip 56.9055pt\times\partial_{w_{*}}\left(\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{K}[f_{J^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}}](y,w_{*})\;f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}(y,w_{*})-\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{K}[g_{J^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}}](y,w_{*})\;g_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}(y,w_{*})\right)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*}\\[5.69054pt]

&\hskip 56.9055pt\times\partial_{w_{*}}^{2}\left(D^{2}(w_{*})\left(\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{H}[f_{J^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}}](y)\;f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}(y,w_{*})-\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{H}[g_{J^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}}](y)\;g_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}(y,w_{*})\right)\right)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*}\\[5.69054pt]
&=0,\end{split}

for a.e.
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
, since the initial distributions coincide. The calculations for higher values of

are similar.
∎

3.
A simplification of the model

As already observed in Subsection
2.4
, the general structures of the graphon
\mathcal{B}
and of the interaction function
P
do not allow to deduce a meaningful expression for the (local) equilibria of system (
3
). The goal of this section is to derive a simplified version of our model which admits meaningful equilibria, and to study their properties. The main idea consists in introducing a new quantity, the agents’ propensity to interact, which incorporates both effects of
\mathcal{B}
and
P
.

3.1.
An analysis of the agents’ propensity to interact

The propensity to interact of each agent depends on two different factors: the graphon
\mathcal{B}
indicates how often an agent with position
x\in\Omega
interacts with the other agents; the interaction function
P
tells us how much the same agent is influenced by each of these interactions. For any
x\in\Omega
, we can therefore define the propensity to interact
p=p(x)
as

p(x)=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{B}(x,z)P(x,z)\mathrm{d}z,

which is well defined as soon as

\mathcal{B}(x,\cdot)\in L^{1}(\Omega),

for any
x\in\Omega
. Note that this hypothesis is directly related with the integrability condition (
13
). In order to highlight the importance of the function
p:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we analyze the following example (another one is reported in Appendix
A
, and both of them will be fundamental for the numerical simulations that we will carry out in Section
5
).

Consider the symmetric (note that this assumption is nor unrealistic, nor necessary in order to define
p
) graphon

(24)

\mathcal{B}(x,y)=(xy)^{-\xi},\quad\xi\in(0,1),

usually associated with the context of scale-free networks
[
10
,
11
]
, i.e., simple graphs whose degree distribution possesses fat tails. Moreover, assume that
[
26
]

(25)

P(x,y)=\left(1+\frac{d_{\mathrm{in}}(x)}{d_{\mathrm{in}}(y)}\right)^{-\chi},\quad\chi>0,

where the in-degree
d_{\mathrm{in}}(x)
of
x\in\Omega
is defined as

(26)

d_{\mathrm{in}}(x)=\int_{\Omega}\mathcal{B}(x,z)\mathrm{d}z.

Note that, with this particular choice (
25
) of
P
, the interactions depend on the connectivity of each agent: the more connected an agent is the less they are influenced by the others, while agents with a lower connectivity are more affected. In particular,

•

d_{\mathrm{in}}(x)\gg d_{\mathrm{in}}(y)
implies that, on average with respect to the random variables
\eta_{J}
, the agent with the highest degree keeps their opinion;

•

d_{\mathrm{in}}(x)\simeq d_{\mathrm{in}}(y)
implies that agents with a similar number of incoming connections
are the ones that can most influence each other;

•

d_{\mathrm{in}}(x)\ll d_{\mathrm{in}}(y)
implies that the less influential agents tend to adopt the opinion of the more connected ones.

) and the interaction function (

The propensity to interact
p
explicitly reads

(27)

p(x)=x^{\xi(\chi-1)}\int_{\Omega}z^{-\xi}(x^{\xi}+z^{\xi})^{-\chi}\mathrm{d}z,

hence two situations must be distinguished:

•

\chi<1
implies that
p(x)
decreases as
x
increases. Moreover
p\to+\infty
as
x\to 0
;

•

\chi>1
implies that
p(x)
can show different behaviors as
x
varies, depending on the relationship between the parameters
\xi
and
\chi
. Note that in this case, if
\xi(1+\chi)<1
then
p(0)=0
(in particular, it is well-defined even if

is not).

The chosen unbounded graphon (

) can be used to model different phenomena. Agents in position
x\simeq 0
could actually be interpreted as opinion leaders (e.g., the media or highly influential individuals) since they have an extremely high number of connections
[

. In this case, it is reasonable to assume that their propensity to interact is almost equal to zero since they are willing to influence the others but they do not want to be influenced. Hence, a reasonable choice of
\chi>1
and of
\xi<(1+\chi)^{-1}
is such that
p
attains its maximum value in the interior of
\Omega
. This means that the agents who are the most inclined to interact have an intermediate number of connections. Indeed, highly connected individuals (i.e., the opinion leaders, corresponding to
\mathcal{B}\to+\infty
) do not want to be influenced by the others (
P\simeq 0
), while poorly connected individuals do not engage in a relevant number of interactions (
\mathcal{B}\simeq 0
) even if they are strongly influenced by them (
P\simeq 1
). On the other hand, if no opinion leaders are considered, it is reasonable to assume
\chi<1
. In this case, only normal agents are taken into account, and as their connectivity decreases, their propensity to interact decreases accordingly. In Figure

.

The choice (

t>0
, one must have
f_{J}\sim y^{-\varsigma}
at
y=0
with
\varsigma+\xi>1
, meaning that the number of agents increases as the connectivity increases. This is clearly unrealistic since highly connected individuals should be a minority. Moreover, recall that (see Remark
1
) it suffices to require that
\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}y^{-\xi}f_{J}(0,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*}<+\infty
for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
.

3.2.
Derivation of a simplified model

Instead of considering both the graphon
\mathcal{B}
and the interaction function
P
, we can derive a simpler model depending solely on the propensity to interact
p
, since it incorporates information from both quantities. The idea is to remove the graphon
\mathcal{B}
from (
8
) (and the subsequent calculations) and to replace the microscopic interactions (
6
) with

\displaystyle\begin{aligned} w^{\prime}&=w+G(w,w_{*})\tilde{P}(x)\tilde{P}(y)\lambda(w_{*}-w)+D(w)\eta_{J},\\[5.69054pt]
w_{*}^{\prime}&=w_{*}+G(w_{*},w)\tilde{P}(y)\tilde{P}(x)\lambda(w-w_{*})+D(w_{*})\eta_{J^{\prime}},\end{aligned}

where we have defined
\tilde{P}(x)=g(p(x))
, based on an increasing function
g:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to[0,1]
such that
g(0)=0
. For example, one can consider

(28)

g(p(x))=\frac{p(x)}{a+p(x)},

for some
a>0
. Note that this simplification is reasonable only if there are no opinion leaders, since the latter would be incapable of influencing other agents (indeed, according to Subsection
3.1
, opinion leaders would satisfy
\tilde{P}\simeq 0
).

Following Subsections
2.2
and
2.3
, we can derive the following Fokker–Planck equation to describe the evolution of
f_{J}
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
,

(29)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}f_{J}&=\frac{1}{\tau}\left(\lambda\partial_{w}\left(\tilde{P}(x)\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\tilde{\mathcal{K}}[f_{J^{\prime}}]f_{J}\right)+\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}\partial^{2}_{w}\left(D^{2}(w)f_{J}\right)\right)\\[5.69054pt]
&=\frac{1}{\tau}\tilde{Q}_{J}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\ x\in\Omega,\ w\in\mathcal{I},\end{split}

where we have defined, for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
,

(30)

\tilde{\mathcal{K}}[f_{J}](t,x,w)=\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\tilde{P}(y)G(w,w_{*})(w-w_{*})f_{J}(t,y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;x\in\Omega,\;w\in\mathcal{I}.

Once again, we have to complete the Fokker–Planck equation (
29
) with no-flux boundary conditions of the form

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\left.D^{2}(w)f_{J}(t,x,w)\right|_{w=\pm 1}=0,\\[5.69054pt]
&\left.\lambda\tilde{P}(x)\displaystyle\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\tilde{\mathcal{K}}[f_{J^{\prime}}](t,x,w)f_{J}(t,x,w)+\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}\partial_{w}\left(D^{2}(w)f_{J}(t,x,w)\right)\right|_{w=\pm 1}=0,\end{aligned}\right.

holding for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
x\in\Omega
. Finally, notice that model (
29
) preserves the average opinion of the population over time, because obviously
\tilde{P}(x)\tilde{P}(y)=\tilde{P}(y)\tilde{P}(x)
.

By defining
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})=(\tilde{Q}_{J}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}))_{J\in\mathcal{C}}
componentwise with the reduced operators (
29
), the simplified version of the full SIR kinetic system (
3
) now reads

(31)

\partial_{t}\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})+\frac{1}{\tau}\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\ x\in\Omega,\ w\in\mathcal{I},

for which all analytical properties derived in Subsection
2.6
clearly still hold.

3.3.
Equilibria, opinion polarization, and consensus formation

Following and further expanding Subsection
2.5
, we can compute steady state of the Fokker–Planck equation (
29
) assuming
G\equiv 1
and
D(w)=\sqrt{1-w^{2}}
. In order to do so, let us define, for
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, the quantities

(32)

\begin{split}\rho_{\tilde{P}}(t)&=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\tilde{P}(x)f_{J}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w,\\[11.38109pt]
m_{\tilde{P}}(t)&=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\tilde{P}(x)wf_{J}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w,\end{split}

and point out that only
\rho_{\tilde{P}}
is conserved over time. The steady states
B_{J}=B_{J}(x,w)
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, of equations (
29
) must satisfy, for any
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
,

(33)

\lambda\tilde{P}(x)\left(\rho_{\tilde{P}}w-m_{\tilde{P}}^{\infty}\right)B_{J}(x,w)+\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}\partial_{w}\left((1-w^{2})B_{J}(x,w)\right)=0,

where
m_{\tilde{P}}^{\infty}
denotes the quantity
m_{\tilde{P}}
computed from the limit distribution
B_{J}
. Therefore, after developing the derivative with respect to
w
, it is easy to see that the Fokker–Planck model relaxes toward the beta distributions

(34)

B_{J}(x,w)=c_{J}(x)(1+w)^{-1+\frac{\tilde{P}(x)}{\nu_{J}}(\rho_{\tilde{P}}+m_{\tilde{P}}^{\infty})}(1-w)^{-1+\frac{\tilde{P}(x)}{\nu_{J}}(\rho_{\tilde{P}}-m_{\tilde{P}}^{\infty})},\quad x\in\Omega,\;w\in\mathcal{I},

for all
J\in\mathcal{C}
, where each
c_{J}(x)>0
is a suitable normalization function such that
\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}B_{J}(x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w=1
(assuming that
\tilde{Q}_{J}
is initially applied to distributions
f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
of unitary mass). This function is uniquely determined by the fact that the operators
\tilde{Q}_{J}
are mass-preserving and that the agents’ position on the graphon does not change over time, thus
c_{J}
can be determined from the initial conditions. In particular, it is easy to check that

c_{J}(x)=\frac{2^{1-\frac{\tilde{P}(x)}{\nu_{J}}\rho_{\tilde{P}}}}{B\left(\frac{\tilde{P}(x)}{\nu_{J}}(\rho_{\tilde{P}}+m_{\tilde{P}}^{\infty}),\frac{\tilde{P}(x)}{\nu_{J}}(\rho_{\tilde{P}}-m_{\tilde{P}}^{\infty})\right)}\int_{\mathcal{I}}f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}(x,w)\mathrm{d}w,\quad x\in\Omega,

where
B
denotes the standard beta function. Note that the equilibrium (
34
) is well defined as long as
|m_{\tilde{P}}^{\infty}|<\rho_{\tilde{P}}
, i.e., as long as not all agents have opinion
w=1
or
w=-1
. This is a reasonable assumption since the latter cases would correspond to a trivial equilibrium given by a Dirac delta distribution.

The equilibria (
34
) can describe two different scenarios:

(1)

None of the exponents of the distribution are negative, therefore
B_{J}(x,\pm 1)=0
and, fixed
x
, the maximum of
B_{J}
is obtained for
w\in(-1,1)
. This situation corresponds to a consensus formation
[
26
]
due to the compromise process of the binary interactions (
6
), where a full consensus would be associated with Dirac delta distributions.

(2)

At least one of the exponents is negative, hence the distribution diverges. This situation corresponds to a polarization of opinions
[
62
]
and is commonly related to a self-thinking process stronger than the compromise propensity.

Clearly, the formation of (partial) consensus is preferable over opinion polarization since one would like to avoid the formation of extreme opinions. For example, in
[
62
]
it is shown that extreme opinions regarding a disease can trigger an increasing spread of infection in the society. Since the quantity
\tilde{P}(x)
appears at the exponents of (
34
), at equilibrium, agents that are inclined to interact (
\tilde{P}(x)\simeq 1
) can be characterized by consensus formation, while at the same time, agents that are not inclined to interact (
\tilde{P}(x)\ll 1
) experience opinion polarization. This is related to the fact that compromise dynamics are always stronger among connected individuals. Moreover, since
|m_{\tilde{P}}^{\infty}|<\rho_{\tilde{P}}\leq 1
, one easily checks that

\min\left\{\rho_{\tilde{P}}-m_{\tilde{P}}^{\infty},\rho_{\tilde{P}}+m_{\tilde{P}}^{\infty}\right\}\in(0,1],

and since
\tilde{P}(x)\in(0,1]
, we need small values of
\nu_{J}
in order to obtain consensus formation. However, as illustrated in Figure
3
,
\nu_{J}<1
is not a sufficient condition to prevent opinion polarization. Recall that
\nu_{J}<1
means that the compromise dynamics are stronger than the self-thinking ones, and we see again the importance of the compromise processes for consensus formation. Finally, since
\nu_{J}
can vary with
J\in\mathcal{C}
, some agents with position
x
can be characterized by consensus formation if they belong to a certain compartment, while by opinion polarization if they belong to another compartment. This interesting dynamics highlights the influence of the environment on self-thinking. Indeed, if we assume that infected agents gain a clearer idea of how the disease develops, one could take
\sigma_{R}^{2}\leq\sigma_{I}^{2}\leq\sigma_{S}^{2}
, implying that opinion polarization is more likely to occur among susceptible agents.

Remark 6
.

These results align with the analysis performed in
[
8
]
, where the first and the second author studied a kinetic model for opinion dynamics driven by the social activity of individuals. There it was shown that socially inactive agents tend to be characterized by opinion polarization, while socially active ones are more prone to reach a consensus, highlighting the importance of social interactions to prevent the formation of extreme opinions.

Consider now the simplified SIR kinetic model (
31
). We call
local equilibrium

f_{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}=f_{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,x,w)
any distribution that reached the steady state (
34
) of the Fokker–Planck equation (
29
). These local equilibrium distributions
f_{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, have the structure

(35)

c_{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,x)\geq 0

\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}f_{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w=\rho_{J}(t)
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Notice that in general
c_{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,x)\not\equiv\rho_{J}(t)c_{J}(x)
since the local incidence rate (

w
. This implies that, for example, some positions on the graphon could increase the chances of contracting the disease because the evolution of the opinion depends on such positions. Hence, unlike
c_{J}
, the function
c_{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}
cannot in general be derived from the initial conditions and its evolution over time can be complex. Going further, the
global equilibrium

f_{J}^{\infty}=f_{J}^{\infty}(x,w)
of system (
31
) must satisfy
\rho_{I}^{\infty}=0
, and it is thus given by

(36)

\begin{split}f_{S}^{\infty}(x,w)=\frac{c_{S}^{\infty}(x)}{c_{S}(x)}B_{S}(x,w),\quad f_{I}^{\infty}(x,w)=0,\quad f_{R}^{\infty}(x,w)=\frac{c_{R}^{\infty}(x)}{c_{R}(x)}B_{R}(x,w),\quad x\in\Omega,\;w\in\mathcal{I},\end{split}

where each
c_{J}^{\infty}(x)
denotes the quantity
c_{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,x)
computed at the corresponding final distribution. Notice that a particular situation occurs when we suppose that the opinion exchanges are much faster than the evolution of the epidemic, which happens when
\tau\ll 1
. In this case, one expects the distribution functions
f_{J}
to quickly reach the corresponding steady state (
35
).

3.4.
Trends to equilibrium

We conclude this section with an analysis of the asymptotic behavior of solutions to the compartmental kinetic model (
31
) assuming
\alpha=0
in (
5
),
G\equiv 1
, and
D(w)=\sqrt{1-w^{2}}
. Recall that (see Subsection
2.4
) with this choice we can derive the macroscopic SIR-type system (
15
). Note also that the local equilibria (
35
) become in this case

(37)

f_{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,x,w)=\rho_{J}(t)B_{J}(x,w),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;x\in\Omega,\;w\in\mathcal{I},

meaning that
c_{J}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,x)=\rho_{J}(t)c_{J}(x)
, since the local incidence rate (
5
) is now independent on
w
. Similarly, the global equilibria simplify to

(38)

\begin{split}f_{S}^{\infty}(x,w)=\rho_{S}^{\infty}B_{S}(x,w),\quad f_{I}^{\infty}(x,w)=0,\quad f_{R}^{\infty}(x,w)=\rho_{R}^{\infty}B_{R}(x,w),\quad x\in\Omega,\;w\in\mathcal{I}.\end{split}

Heuristically, one expects that any solution
\mathbf{f}
to this version of system (
31
) would approach over time a local equilibrium
\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{eq}}
given by (
37
), which cancels out the kinetic operator
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})
and whose moments
\bm{\rho}
are solutions to the macroscopic SIR-type system (
15
). These moments should then converge to the unique globally asymptotically stable equilibrium
\bm{\rho}^{\infty}
of the SIR equations, meaning that we would eventually observe a longtime relaxation of the form
\mathbf{f}\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathbf{f}^{\infty}
.

In order to make this argument rigorous, we proceed as in
[
4
,
8
]
by studying the temporal evolution of the relative entropy functional between two distributions
g=g(t,x,w)
and
h=h(t,x,w)
, defined as

(39)

H(g|h)(t)=\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}g(t,x,w)\log\frac{g(t,x,w)}{h(t,x,w)}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

proving that it exhibits a decay to zero as time approaches infinity, and concluding with the decay of the
L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})
norm via Csiszár–Kullback–Pinsker inequality. We shall develop these considerations in three successive steps, starting to analyze the relaxation of
f_{S}
toward
f_{S}^{\infty}
, proceeding with the study of the long-time behavior of
f_{I}
toward zero, and finally deducing the convergence of
f_{R}
toward
f_{R}^{\infty}
thanks to mass conservation. For this last step, we will need to assume that for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
it holds
\sigma_{J}^{2}=\sigma^{2}
for some
\sigma^{2}>0
. Additionally, we shall only consider initial distributions
f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, that are independent of
x
, since in this case the quantity
m_{\tilde{P}}
(
32
) is conserved over time (recall that
\rho_{\tilde{P}}
is always conserved over time), as proven in the following lemma.

Lemma 3
.

Suppose that
G\equiv 1
and that the initial distributions
f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, are independent of
x
, namely
f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}(x,w)=f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}(w)
for all
J\in\mathcal{C}
. If the weighted average opinion
m_{\tilde{P}}
defined by (
32
) is analytical, then it is conserved over time.

Proof.

If we show that for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
,

\frac{\mathrm{d}^{n}}{\mathrm{d}t^{n}}\left.m_{\tilde{P}}\right|_{t=0}=0,

then the result will follow from the assumption that
m_{\tilde{P}}
is analytical. For
n=1
we have

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left.m_{\tilde{P}}\right|_{t=0}&=-\left.\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\tilde{P}^{2}(x)\lambda\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\tilde{\mathcal{K}}[f_{J^{\prime}}](t,x,w)f_{J}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\right|_{t=0}\\[5.69054pt]
&=-\left.\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\lambda\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\tilde{P}^{2}(x)\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\tilde{P}(y)(w-w_{*})f_{J^{\prime}}(t,y,w_{*})f_{J}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\right|_{t=0}\\[5.69054pt]
&=-\lambda\int_{\Omega^{2}}\tilde{P}^{2}(x)\tilde{P}(y)\underbrace{\left(\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\int_{\mathcal{I}^{2}}(w-w_{*})f_{J^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}}(w_{*})f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}(w)\mathrm{d}w_{*}\mathrm{d}w\right)}_{=0}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\\
&=0,\end{split}

using the hypothesis on the initial distributions. Note that the epidemiological operator
\mathbf{E}
in (
31
) can be neglected since its contributions cancel out by summing over
J\in\mathcal{C}
. The strategy to prove that also the higher order derivatives cancel out follows the same lines, and relies on exhibiting the sum of symmetric integrals. As an example, we show what happens in the case
n=2
. Due to the Leibniz product rule, the second derivative is a sum of several terms. However, if for example we fix
J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}
and we differentiate
f_{J}
for all
J\in\mathcal{C}
, then the resulting sum is equal to zero. Indeed,

\begin{split}-\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}&\left.\lambda\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\tilde{P}^{2}(x)\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\tilde{P}(y)(w-w_{*})f_{J^{\prime}}(t,y,w_{*})\partial_{t}f_{J}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\right|_{t=0}\\[5.69054pt]
&=\left.\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\lambda^{2}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\tilde{P}^{2}(x)\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\tilde{P}(y)f_{J^{\prime}}(t,y,w_{*})\tilde{P}(x)\sum_{J^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\tilde{\mathcal{K}}[f_{J^{\prime\prime}}](t,x,w)f_{J}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\right|_{t=0}\\[5.69054pt]
&=\lambda^{2}\int_{\Omega^{3}\times\mathcal{I}}\tilde{P}^{3}(x)\tilde{P}(y)\tilde{P}(\bar{x})\underbrace{\left(\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\sum_{J^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\int_{\mathcal{I}^{2}}(w-\bar{w})f_{J^{\prime\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}}(\bar{w})f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}(w)\mathrm{d}\bar{w}\mathrm{d}w\right)}_{=0}f_{J^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}}(w_{*})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}\bar{x}\mathrm{d}w_{*}\\
&=0.\end{split}

Clearly the computations are similar when fixing
J
instead of
J^{\prime}
. Therefore, the result is proved for
n=2
, and by iterating these computations one gets the desired result for all
n\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
. Indeed, the key of the proof is that there are always two paired distributions such that their sums cancel out, while the remaining distributions (only
f_{J^{\prime}}^{\mathrm{in}}
in the previous case, but for larger
n
the number will grow) can be separated from the others.
∎

Remark 7
.

If the initial distributions are not independent of the position on the graphon
x
, then Lemma
3
is not true since the function
\tilde{P}
could give more weight to some opinions than to others. The additional hypothesis on the initial distributions ensures that no opinion is privileged at time
t=0
, and the previous lemma shows that this property is in fact preserved over time.

Step 1 – Relaxation of
f_{S}
.
Let us fix
g=f_{S}
and
h=f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}
in (
39
). Then, the time derivative of their relative entropy
H(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})
explicitly reads

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}H(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})=\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\left(1+\log\frac{f_{S}}{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}}\right)\partial_{t}f_{S}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w-\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\frac{f_{S}}{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}}\partial_{t}f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w.

Now, using the explicit form of
f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}
given by (
37
), we see that the second integral reduces to

\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\frac{f_{S}(t,x,w)}{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,x,w)}\partial_{t}f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w=\frac{\partial_{t}\rho_{S}}{\rho_{S}}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}f_{S}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w=\partial_{t}\rho_{S},

thanks to the definition of
\rho_{S}
. Using the first equation of the macroscopic SIR system (
15
), we thus conclude that

\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\frac{f_{S}(t,x,w)}{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,x,w)}\partial_{t}f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w=-\beta\rho_{S}\rho_{I}.

Going back now to the first integral, since
f_{S}
solves the SIR kinetic system (
31
) and the transition rate
\beta_{T}
does not depend on
w
anymore (we set
\alpha=0
in (
5
)), we can rewrite it as

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}&\left(1+\log\frac{f_{S}(t,x,w)}{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,x,w)}\right)\partial_{t}f_{S}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\[11.38109pt]
&=\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\left(1+\log\frac{f_{S}}{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}}\right)E_{S}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w+\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\left(1+\log\frac{f_{S}}{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}}\right)\tilde{Q}_{S}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\[11.38109pt]
&=-\beta\rho_{S}\rho_{I}-\beta\rho_{I}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}f_{S}\log\frac{f_{S}}{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w-I_{H}(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}),\\[17.07164pt]
&=-\beta\rho_{S}\rho_{I}-\beta\rho_{I}H(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})-I_{H}(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}),\end{split}

where
I_{H}(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})\geq 0
denotes the entropy production functional

I_{H}(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})(t)=4\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}(1-w^{2})f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,x,w)\left(\partial_{w}\sqrt{\frac{f_{S}(t,x,w)}{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,x,w)}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Combining the computations of the two integral terms, we infer the initial estimate

(40)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}H(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})\leq-I_{H}(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}),

since the term depending on
H(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})\geq 0
can be neglected.

From the computations performed in
[
28
]
one can then prove the inequality

(41)

D_{H}^{2}(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})\leq\frac{1}{2}I_{H}(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}),

linking the entropy production
I_{H}
with the Hellinger distance between the two distributions
f_{S}
and
f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}
, given by

D_{H}(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})(t)=\left(\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\left(\sqrt{f_{S}}-\sqrt{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

In particular, its temporal evolution reads

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}D_{H}^{2}(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})=\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}}{f_{S}}}\right)\partial_{t}f_{S}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w+\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{f_{S}}{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}}}\right)\partial_{t}f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w.

Proceeding in a similar way as with the study of
H(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})
, we can see the the second integral is nonpositive since

\begin{split}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{f_{S}(t,x,w)}{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,x,w)}}\right)\partial_{t}f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w&=\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\left(f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}-\sqrt{f_{S}f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}}\right)\frac{\partial_{t}f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}}{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\[5.69054pt]
&=-\beta\rho_{S}\rho_{I}+\beta\rho_{I}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\sqrt{f_{S}f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\[5.69054pt]
&\leq-\beta\rho_{S}\rho_{I}+\beta\rho_{I}\underbrace{\left(\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}f_{S}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}_{\sqrt{\rho_{S}}}\ \underbrace{\left(\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\right)^{\frac{1}{2}}}_{\sqrt{\rho_{S}}}\\
&=0\end{split}

where we have replaced
\partial_{t}\rho_{S}=-\beta\rho_{S}\rho_{I}
and used Cauchy–Schwarz inequality to bound the integral of
\sqrt{f_{S}f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}}
. Using the first equation of the simplified SIR kinetic system (
31
) to replace
\partial_{t}f_{S}
inside the first integral and adapting the computations performed in
[
28
]
, which can be proved to hold (in particular the weighted Chernoff inequality from
[
28
, Theorem 3.3]
) for two general distributions having the same density, we can then infer the estimate

(42)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}D_{H}^{2}(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})\leq&\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}}{f_{S}}}\right)\partial_{t}f_{S}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\[5.69054pt]
=&\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}}{f_{S}}}\right)E_{S}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w+\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}}{f_{S}}}\right)\tilde{Q}_{S}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\[5.69054pt]
=&-\beta\rho_{S}\rho_{I}+\beta\rho_{I}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\sqrt{f_{S}f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w+\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\left(1-\sqrt{\frac{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}}{f_{S}}}\right)\tilde{Q}_{S}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\\[11.38109pt]
\leq&-I_{D}(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}),\end{split}

where we have used once again Cauchy–Schwarz inequality as in the previous estimate to prove that the first two contributions cancel out, and we have introduced the functional
I_{D}(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})\geq 0
defining the entropy production of the squared Hellinger distance, which is given by

I_{D}(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})(t)=8\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}(1-w^{2})f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,x,w)\left(\partial_{w}\sqrt[4]{\frac{f_{S}(t,x,w)}{f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,x,w)}}\right)^{2}\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Now, integrating equation (
40
) over
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, we infer that
I_{H}(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})
belongs to
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
since
H(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})\geq 0
and thus

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}I_{H}(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})\mathrm{d}t\leq H(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})(0)<+\infty,

Therefore, from the inequality (
41
) relating the squared Hellinger distance with the entropy production
I_{H}
and from the entropy-entropy production estimate (
42
) on
D_{H}^{2}
, we respectively deduce that the Hellinger distance is integrable in time and non-increasing. This allows to conclude that
D_{H}^{2}(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})=\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.7}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}(1/t)
when
t\to+\infty
, recovering the following
L^{1}
estimate for the convergence of
f_{S}
toward the local equilibrium
f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}
:

\left\|f_{S}-f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}\leq 2\sqrt{\rho_{S}^{\mathrm{in}}}D(f_{S}|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}})=\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.7}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}(1/\sqrt{t}),\quad t\to+\infty,

which is obtained from the equality
|a-b|=|\sqrt{a}-\sqrt{b}|(\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b})
and from two successive applications of Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, also recalling that
\rho_{S}\leq\rho_{S}^{\mathrm{in}}
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

At last, we can study the relaxation of the local equilibrium
f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}
toward the global one
f_{S}^{\infty}
via simple computations of their relative
L^{1}
distance. We obtain

\left\|f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}-f_{S}^{\infty}\right\|_{L^{1}({\Omega\times\mathcal{I}})}=\big|\rho_{S}-\rho_{S}^{\infty}\big|\underbrace{\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}B_{S}(x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w}_{=1}\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0,

using the fact that
\rho_{S}
converges toward
\rho_{S}^{\infty}
. Therefore, one finally deduces the long-time relaxation to equilibrium
\left\|f_{S}-f_{S}^{\infty}\right\|_{L^{1}({\Omega\times\mathcal{I}})}\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
.

Step 2 – Relaxation of
f_{I}
.
The study of the long-time behavior of
f_{I}
is very simple, since
\rho_{I}^{\infty}=0
and we would thus want to prove that
f_{I}
relaxes to zero in the
L^{1}({\Omega\times\mathcal{I}})
norm as
t\to+\infty
. But since the SIR kinetic system (
31
) preserves the mass and the positivity of solutions, one has that
\left\|f_{I}\right\|_{L^{1}({\Omega\times\mathcal{I}})}=\rho_{I}
which converges to the unique disease-free equilibrium of the corresponding macroscopic SIR system (
15
). We thus easily conclude that
\left\|f_{I}\right\|_{L^{1}({\Omega\times\mathcal{I}})}\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
.

Step 3 – Relaxation of
f_{R}
.
It remains to show that also the distribution
f_{R}
of recovered individuals converges toward its corresponding global equilibrium state
f_{R}^{\infty}
, which we shall prove by exploiting the fact that the SIR kinetic model preserves the total mass of the population. Specifically, we introduce the distribution
f(t,x,w)=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}f_{J}(t,x,w)
of the whole population. Summing the equations of system (
31
), since
\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}E_{J}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})\equiv 0
and thanks to the particular structure of the operators
\tilde{Q}_{J}
with parameters independent of
J\in\mathcal{C}
(recall that we assumed
\sigma_{J}^{2}=\sigma^{2}
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
), one finds that the total distribution
f
solves the Fokker–Planck equation

\partial_{t}f=\frac{1}{\tau}\lambda\partial_{w}\left((\rho_{\tilde{P}}w-m_{\tilde{P}})f\right)+\frac{\sigma^{2}}{2}\partial_{w}^{2}\left((1-w^{2})f\right),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;x\in\Omega,\;w\in\mathcal{I}.

In particular, thanks to mass conservation ensuring that
\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\rho_{J}=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\rho_{J}^{\infty}=1
for all
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, one expects
f
to converge toward the global equilibrium state
f^{\infty}(x,w)=B(x,w)
defined by (
34
) (recall once again that
\sigma_{J}^{2}=\sigma^{2}
for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
, implying that
\nu_{J}=\nu
for some
\nu>0
; similarly, for any
x\in\Omega
we have that
c_{J}(x)=c(x)
for some normalization function
c(x)>0
). Following the exact same computations performed in Step 1 to prove the convergence of
f_{S}
toward
f_{S}^{\mathrm{eq}}
(except that now we do not have to deal with the epidemiological operators), one can show that
\left\|f-f^{\infty}\right\|_{L^{1}({\Omega\times\mathcal{I}})}\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
.

Therefore, using the definition of
f
and the fact that
f^{\infty}(x,w)=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\rho_{J}^{\infty}B_{J}(x,w)
for any
x\in\Omega
and
w\in\mathcal{I}
, we easily deduce the long-time relaxation of
f_{R}
toward
f_{R}^{\infty}
as

\begin{split}\left\|f_{R}-f_{R}^{\infty}\right\|_{L^{1}({\Omega\times\mathcal{I}})}&=\left\|f-f_{S}-f_{I}-(f^{\infty}-f_{S}^{\infty})\right\|_{L^{1}({\Omega\times\mathcal{I}})}\\[11.38109pt]
&\leq\underbrace{\left\|f_{S}-f_{S}^{\infty}\right\|_{L^{1}({\Omega\times\mathcal{I}})}}_{\underset{\textrm{from Step 1}}{\underset{t\longrightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0}}+\underbrace{\left\|f_{I}\right\|_{L^{1}({\Omega\times\mathcal{I}})}}_{\underset{\textrm{from Step 2}}{\underset{t\longrightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0}}+\underbrace{\left\|f-f^{\infty}\right\|_{L^{1}({\Omega\times\mathcal{I}})}}_{\underset{\textrm{from Step 3}}{\underset{t\longrightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0}}\underset{t\longrightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.\end{split}

We are therefore ready to prove the following theorem.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathbf{f}
be a solution to the simplified SIR kinetic model (
31
), starting from a nonnegative initial datum that is independent of the position on the graphon, namely
f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}=f_{J}^{\mathrm{in}}(w)
for all
J\in\mathcal{C}
. Assume that
G\equiv 1
,
D(w)=\sqrt{1-w^{2}}
, and set
\alpha=0
in (
5
). Moreover, suppose that
\sigma_{J}^{2}=\sigma^{2}
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, for some
\sigma^{2}>0
. Then, for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
,

\left\|f_{J}-f_{J}^{\infty}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}=\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.7}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}(1/\sqrt{t}),\quad t\to+\infty,

where the global equilibria
f_{J}^{\infty}
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, are given by (
38
).

Proof.

We have already proved that
\left\|f_{J}-f_{J}^{\infty}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}\underset{t\longrightarrow+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
. It remains to demonstrate the rates of convergence. The rate for
f_{S}
was already deduced in Step 1. Concerning
f_{I}
, since
\rho_{I}
tends to
0
exponentially fast for
t
large enough,
\left\|f_{I}-f_{I}^{\infty}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}=\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.7}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}(\exp(-\eta t))
as
t\to+\infty
for some
\eta>0
. As shown in
[
28
]
,
\left\|f-f^{\infty}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}=\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.7}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}(1/\sqrt{t})
when
t\to+\infty
, hence also
\left\|f_{R}-f_{R}^{\infty}\right\|_{L^{1}(\Omega\times\mathcal{I})}=\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.7}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}(1/\sqrt{t})
when
t\to+\infty
, and the thesis follows.
∎

4.
Perception of the disease’s dangerousness

Until now, we have analyzed how the agents’ opinion about the willingness to adopt or not a protective behavior influences the evolution of an epidemic. In this section we want to investigate how seriously the disease is perceived by the population. Even if this dynamic does not directly affect the evolution of the epidemic, it would be interesting to determine the relationship between the global equilibria derived in Subsection
3.3
and the population’s opinion about the disease itself. Our goal is to fix a value
\hat{w}\in[-1,1]
, representing a hypothesized danger level of the disease, and understand how any products related to this hypothesis spread inside the population. Products may be any piece of information reaching the agents: news, videos, advertisements, and social posts, that individuals possibly share depending on how much their current opinion is aligned with them. Agents with an opinion
w\geq\hat{w}
will contribute to the spread of these products, since they consider the disease at least as dangerous as
\hat{w}
. On the other hand, agents with opinion
w<\hat{w}
consider the disease less dangerous than
\hat{w}
, so they will not contribute to spread these products. For example, by fixing
\hat{w}=0
we could measure the popularity of the ideas that judge the disease to be dangerous. We will assume that the evolution of these products depends on the propensity to interact
p
of each agent, even if we do not necessarily have to consider the simplified version of our model (recall that
p
can in fact be defined even for the original model).

4.1.
Kinetic modeling of popularity spread

We quantify the popularity of a product by means of a variable
v\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, whose evolution depends on the interaction with the opinion
w
and the propensity to interact
p
of the agents that the product reaches. Inspired by
[
58
]
, we consider the following microscopic update rule for
v
:

(43)

v^{\prime}=v-\mu v+\theta p(x)\mathbb{I}_{[\hat{w},1]}(w)+D_{p}(v)\omega,

where
\mu\in(0,1)
represents the natural decay rate of the product’s popularity when it is not shared by an agent,
\theta>0
measures the incidence of
p
on the popularity,
\mathbb{I}_{A}
denotes the indicator function of the set
A\subseteq\mathcal{I}
, and
\omega
is a random variable with zero mean and finite variance
\zeta^{2}>0
, modeling a stochastic fluctuation of the popularity that is modulated by a popularity-dependent strength
D_{p}(v)\geq 0
. According to (
43
), the increase in popularity depends on whether an individual decides to share the product, which happens when their opinion about the disease’s dangerousness is equal or larger than the hypothesized danger level
\hat{w}
. In such a case, the increase in popularity is proportional to the individual’s propensity to interact. In order to guarantee that
v^{\prime}\geq 0
it suffices to impose that
D_{p}(v)\omega\geq(\mu-1)v
, which is satisfied as soon as
\omega\geq\mu-1
and
D_{p}(v)\leq v
. Note that this latter inequality implies
D_{p}(0)=0
. We may assume

(44)

D_{p}(v)=v,\quad v\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

since, as we shall see, with this particular choice the equilibria are coherent with empirical evidence. Notice however that other choices are possible
[
58
]
. Obviously we also have to assume that
p
is bounded, although this is not a restrictive assumption since, if needed, it suffices to consider a modified version
p_{\Lambda}
of
p
such that for any
x\in\Omega
,
p_{\Lambda}(x)=\min\{p(x),\Lambda\}
for some
\Lambda\gg 1
.

Given a distribution function
h=h(t,v)
, depending on time
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and popularity
v\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, and such that
h(t,v)\mathrm{d}v
represents the number of products with popularity in
[v,v+\mathrm{d}v]
at time
t
, the evolution of
h
should depend on the opinion distribution inside the population, namely we have to consider the coupling

(45)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\partial_{t}\mathbf{f}=\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})+\frac{1}{\tau}\mathbf{Q}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f}),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;x\in\Omega,\;w\in\mathcal{I},\\[5.69054pt]
&\partial_{t}h=\frac{1}{\tau_{p}}Q_{p}(h,\mathbf{f}),\qquad\qquad\ t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;v\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\end{aligned}\right.

where
\tau_{p}>0
represents a possible additional timescale at which the popularity of products evolve. Note that in (
45
) the collisional operator
\mathbf{Q}
can be replaced by its simplified version
\tilde{\mathbf{Q}}
introduced in Section
3
. On the other hand, the collisional operator
Q_{p}
can be derived as follows. Proceeding as in Subsection
2.2
, the second equation from system (
45
) reads in weak form

(46)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\psi(v)h(t,v)\mathrm{d}v=\frac{1}{\tau_{p}}\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}\times\mathbb{R}_{+}}\left<\psi(v^{\prime})-\psi(v)\right>h(t,v)f_{J}(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w\mathrm{d}v,

where
\psi=\psi(v)
is a smooth test function. Note that the choice
\psi(v)=1
implies the conservation of mass for the distribution
h
, hence we can assume that
\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}h(t,v)\mathrm{d}v=1
. Introducing the scaling

(47)

\mu\mapsto\epsilon\mu,\qquad\theta\mapsto\epsilon\theta,\qquad\zeta^{2}\mapsto\epsilon\zeta^{2},\qquad\tau_{p}\mapsto\epsilon\tau_{p},

which depends on a small parameter
0<\epsilon\ll 1
, we can follow the computations of Subsection
2.3
to formally derive, in the limit
\epsilon\to 0
, a Fokker–Planck-type equation describing the evolution of the distribution
h
, namely

(48)

\begin{split}\partial_{t}h&=\frac{1}{\tau_{p}}\left(\partial_{v}\big(\left(\mu v-\theta\mathcal{F}[f]\right)h\big)+\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2}\partial^{2}_{v}\left(D_{p}^{2}(v)h\right)\right)\\[5.69054pt]
&=\frac{1}{\tau_{p}}Q_{p}(h,\mathbf{f}),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;v\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\end{split}

where we have defined the nonlocal quantity

\mathcal{F}[f](t)=\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}p(x)\mathbb{I}_{[\hat{w},1]}(w)f(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

with
f(t,x,w)=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}f_{J}(t,x,w)
. Model (
48
) is then completed with the following no-flux boundary conditions, holding for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
:

(49)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\left.D_{p}^{2}(v)h(t,v)\right|_{v=0}=0,\\[5.69054pt]
&\left.(\mu v-\theta\mathcal{F}[f](t))h(t,v)+\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2}\partial_{v}\left(D_{p}^{2}(v)h(t,v)\right)\right|_{v=0}=0,\\[5.69054pt]
&\displaystyle\lim_{v\to+\infty}v^{\kappa-1}D_{p}^{2}(v)h(t,v)=0,\\[5.69054pt]
&\displaystyle\lim_{v\to+\infty}v^{\kappa}\left((\mu v-\theta\mathcal{F}[f](t))h(t,v)+\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2}\partial_{v}\left(D_{p}^{2}(v)h(t,v)\right)\right)=0,\end{aligned}\right.

where
\kappa\geq 1
must be sufficiently large so that the distribution
h
has finite moments of interest. Note that the first boundary condition is related to
D_{p}(0)=0
. It is important to notice that the evolution of
h
depends only on the opinion operator
\mathbf{Q}
. Moreover, one can bound
\mathcal{F}[f]
as

(50)

0\leq\mathcal{F}[f](t)\leq\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}p(x)f(t,x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w,

for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, where the last integral is constant since the position on the graphon
x
does not vary over time (similarly to the total weighted density
\rho_{\tilde{P}}
defined by (
32
)).

Regarding the macroscopic moments, we introduce the average popularity

(51)

m_{p}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}vh(t,v)\mathrm{d}v,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

whose evolution is obtained by taking
\psi(v)=v
in (
46
), to get

(52)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}m_{p}=-\frac{\mu}{\tau_{p}}m_{p}+\frac{\theta}{\tau_{p}}\mathcal{F}[f],\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

Similarly, if we set
D_{p}(v)=v
and we assume that
\kappa\geq 2
, from (
48
) it follows that the energy of the distribution
h

(53)

e_{p}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}v^{2}h(t,v)\mathrm{d}v,\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

evolves according to

(54)

\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}e_{p}=\frac{\zeta^{2}-2\mu}{\tau_{p}}e_{p}+\frac{2\theta}{\tau_{p}}\mathcal{F}[f]m_{p},\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}.

These considerations will be useful later on.

4.2.
Local and global equilibria

Let us set
D_{p}(v)=v
and assume that
\mathcal{F}[f](t)>0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Then, the
local equilibrium

h^{\mathrm{eq}}
of the Fokker–Planck equation (
48
) is the (unique) solution of unitary mass to

(55)

\left(\mu v-\theta\mathcal{F}[f](t)\right)h(t,v)+\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2}\partial_{v}\left(D_{p}^{2}(v)h(t,v)\right)=0,

for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, which is given by the inverse gamma distribution

(56)

h^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,v)=\frac{\left(2\theta\mathcal{F}[f](t)\right)^{1+\frac{2\mu}{\zeta^{2}}}}{\zeta^{2+\frac{4\mu}{\zeta^{2}}}\Gamma\left(1+\frac{2\mu}{\zeta^{2}}\right)}\;v^{-2\left(1+\frac{\mu}{\zeta^{2}}\right)}\exp\left(-\frac{2\theta\mathcal{F}[f](t)}{\zeta^{2}}\frac{1}{v}\right),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;v\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

where
\Gamma
denotes the gamma function.
Note that
\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}h^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,v)\mathrm{d}v=1
.
Assuming now that
f
reached a global equilibrium
f^{\infty}
and defining

\mathcal{F}^{\infty}=\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}p(x)\mathbb{I}_{[\hat{w},1]}(w)f^{\infty}(x,w)\mathrm{d}x\mathrm{d}w,

the
global equilibrium
of equation (
48
) reads

(57)

h^{\infty}(v)=\frac{\left(2\theta\mathcal{F}^{\infty}\right)^{1+\frac{2\mu}{\zeta^{2}}}}{\zeta^{2+\frac{4\mu}{\zeta^{2}}}\Gamma\left(1+\frac{2\mu}{\zeta^{2}}\right)}\;v^{-2\left(1+\frac{\mu}{\zeta^{2}}\right)}\exp\left(-\frac{2\theta\mathcal{F}^{\infty}}{\zeta^{2}}\frac{1}{v}\right),\quad v\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

which has also unit mass. We point out that
h^{\infty}
is admissible as long as
\mathcal{F}^{\infty}>0
, i.e., as long as
\hat{w}<1
, otherwise it would not have a finite mass. Notice also that such global equilibrium is a fat-tailed inverse gamma distribution, since
h^{\infty}(v)\sim v^{-2(1+\mu/\zeta^{2})}
when
v\to+\infty
. More precisely, this distribution exhibits a Pareto tail
[
33
]
which indicates that products reaching very high popularity levels may be rare in general but not that improbable. The mean of the distribution
h^{\infty}
is
m_{p}^{\infty}=\theta\mathcal{F}^{\infty}/\mu
, in accordance with equation (
52
) (indeed,
h^{\infty}
satisfies the boundary conditions (
49
) for
\kappa=1
, which corresponds to having a finite mean). Concerning the variance of
h^{\infty}
, due to its behavior when
v\to+\infty
, it is finite if and only if
\zeta^{2}<2\mu
, which implies that the global equilibrium (
57
) satisfies the boundary conditions (
49
) for
\kappa=2
. Note that this is coherent with equation (
54
), which in turn tells us that in this case the energy of
h^{\infty}
is given by
e_{p}^{\infty}=2\theta^{2}{\mathcal{F}^{\infty}}^{2}/(\mu(2\mu-\zeta^{2}))
. The relevance of the distribution
h^{\infty}
is supported by empirical evidence on the diffusion of products (in non-epidemiological contexts). For instance, in
[
53
]
the statistical distribution of the popularity of scientific articles, measured in terms of their citations, features a power law-type tail with Pareto exponent close to 3. Moreover, in
[
55
]
it is shown that the popularity of movies in the United States, measured in terms of their box office gross income, exhibits completely analogous statistical properties. Figure
4
shows different graphs of the distribution
h^{\infty}
defined by (
57
), for fixed values of the parameters
\mu
,
\zeta
, and
\theta
(corresponding to a finite variance) and increasing values of the quantity
\mathcal{F}^{\infty}
, which corresponds to the amount of popularity pumped into the system by social network users sharing the products. In particular, from the asymptotic analysis conducted in Section
3
we infer that
\mathcal{F}^{\infty}
is well defined and can be computed explicitly at least in the case when
\mathbf{f}
evolves according to the simplified SIR kinetic model (
31
) and the transition rate
\beta_{T}
is constant, since in this case we proved the rigorous convergence of
\mathbf{f}
toward
\mathbf{f}^{\infty}
(for which we provided the explicit expression (
38
)) and thus also that of
f=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}f_{J}
toward
f^{\infty}=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}f_{J}^{\infty}
. The lowest values of
\mathcal{F}^{\infty}
(and hence of the average popularity) correspond to the occurrence of opinion polarization only at
w=-1
, like shown in Figure
3
(B). In fact, in this case the agents tend to have opinions
w
lower than some
\hat{w}\in(-1,1)
and the function
p
tends to be small, since we showed that high values of
p
facilitate consensus formation (see Subsection
3.3
). These two factors contribute to reduce both values of
\mathcal{F}[f](t)
and
\mathcal{F}^{\infty}
. On the other hand, the hightest values of
\mathcal{F}^{\infty}
do not necessarily correspond to the occurrence of opinion polarization at
w=1
. Indeed, even if in the latter situation there are many agents willing to share the products, their influence on other individuals could be very low since, once again, opinion polarization is facilitated by small values of the function
p
. Therefore, these two effects might compensate each other and prevent
\mathcal{F}^{\infty}
to reach its supremum. Such considerations suggest that it is generally better to avoid the formation of extreme opinions. In particular, the popularity of products considering the disease as dangerous is favored by a population of agents that willing to adopt a protective behavior, but are also inclined to interact with each other.

) for different values of

. We have set
\mu=1.5
,

, and
\theta=1
, corresponding to an equilibrium

h^{\infty}
with finite variance.

Finally, we remark that it is possible to replace the function
\mathbb{I}_{[\hat{w},1]}(w)
in (

) with its opposite
\mathbb{I}_{[-1,\hat{w}]}(w)
. In this case, one could measure how not seriously the disease is perceived by the population and we could determine analogous results to the ones obtained in this section.

4.3.
Analytical properties

Following the strategies presented in Subsection
2.6
, it is easy to prove that the Fokker–Planck equation (
48
) preserves the nonnegativity of the initial datum and, since it is mass preserving, also its
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
regularity. Moreover, given the function
\mathcal{F}[f]
, it is possible to demonstrate the uniqueness of its solution. Concerning the
L^{q}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
regularity,
q\geq 2
, the following proposition holds.

Proposition 4

(Regularity)
.

Let
q\in[2,+\infty)
and let
h
be a solution to the Fokker–Planck equation (
48
). Assume that
\partial_{v}^{2}D_{p}^{2}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
and that the following additional no-flux boundary conditions

(58)

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} &\left.D_{p}^{2}(v)\partial_{v}h(t,v)h^{q-1}(t,v)\right|_{v=0}=0,\\[5.69054pt]
&\left.h(t,v)\right|_{v=0}=0,\\[5.69054pt]
&\displaystyle\lim_{v\to+\infty}D_{p}^{2}(v)\partial_{v}h(t,v)h^{q-1}(t,v),\\[5.69054pt]
&\displaystyle\lim_{v\to+\infty}h^{q}(t,v)(v+\partial_{v}D_{p}^{2}(v))=0,\end{aligned}\right.

hold for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and
v\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. If
h^{\mathrm{in}}\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, then
h(t,\cdot)\in L^{q}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. More precisely,

\left\|h(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\leq\left\|h^{\mathrm{in}}\right\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\exp(\eta_{q}t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

where
\eta_{q}=\frac{q-1}{q}\left(\mu+\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2}\left\|\partial_{v}^{2}D_{p}^{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\right)
.

Proposition
4
can be proved in the exact same way as Proposition
2
. Notice that the choice
D_{p}(v)=v
satisfies the request
\partial_{v}^{2}D_{p}^{2}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Recall that, if
D_{p}(v)=v
and
\mathcal{F}[f]\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{F}^{\infty}>0
, the stationary solution
h^{\infty}
to (
48
) is given by (
57
). Due to the presence of the exponential function, such equilibrium always satisfies the two no-flux boundary conditions (
58
) at
v=0
. Moreover, a simple computation shows that, since
h^{\infty}(v)\sim v^{-2(1+\mu/\zeta^{2})}
when
v\to+\infty
, it also always satisfies the other two no-flux boundary conditions at
v\to+\infty
. Therefore, there are no restrictions on the parameters governing the popularity spread in order to derive these regularity properties for the global equilibrium.

The constants
\eta_{q}
are equi-bounded by
\eta_{\infty}=\mu+\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2}\left\|\partial_{v}^{2}D_{p}^{2}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}
, for any
q\in[2,+\infty)
. Therefore, in the limit
q\to+\infty
we infer the regularity
h(t,\cdot)\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
, specifically

\left\|h(t,\cdot)\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\leq\left\|h^{\mathrm{in}}\right\|_{L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\exp(\eta_{\infty}t),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},

provided that
h^{\mathrm{in}}\in L^{\infty}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.

4.4.
Asymptotic analysis

Assuming that
D_{p}(v)=v
, we shall rigorously analyze the trend to equilibrium of the solution
h
to (
48
) in homogeneous Sobolev spaces
\dot{H}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, with
s\in\big(\frac{1}{2},1\big)
. These are defined through the corresponding norm

(59)

\left\|g\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}=\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{-2s}|\hat{g}(\xi)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi\right)^{\frac{1}{2}},

where
\hat{g}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{C}
denotes the Fourier transform of some function
g:\mathbb{R}_{+}\to\mathbb{R}_{+}
. Clearly, no probability distribution
g
belongs to such space because
\hat{g}(0)=1
,
\hat{g}
is continuous, and
\int_{(-\varepsilon,\varepsilon)}|\xi|^{-2s}\mathrm{d}\xi=+\infty
for any
s\in\big(\frac{1}{2},1\big)
and any
\varepsilon>0
. For this reason, instead of investigating the convergence of
h
toward
h^{\infty}
, we will study the convergence of the difference
h-h^{\infty}
toward
0
. We start with the following useful result.

Lemma 4
.

Consider a random variable
X
distributed as
g
, with a finite mean
\mathbb{E}(X)
. Then, the Fourier transform
\hat{g}
of
g
can be written as
\hat{g}(\xi)=1-i\xi\mathbb{E}(X)+R(\xi)
for any
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
, where:

•

if
\mathbb{E}(X^{2})<+\infty
, then
|R(\xi)|=\mathcal{O}(|\xi|^{2})
;

•

if
g(v)=\frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}v^{-1-\alpha}\exp(-\beta/v)
for some constants
\alpha>1
and
\beta>0
, then
|R(\xi)|=\mathcal{O}(|\xi|^{\alpha})
.

Proof.

Let
X
be a random variable with probability density function
g
over
\mathbb{R}_{+}
, then

(60)

\hat{g}(\xi)=\mathbb{E}(\exp(-i\xi X))=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}g(v)\exp(-i\xi v)\mathrm{d}v.

The following Taylor expansion with integral error term for
\exp(-i\xi X)
holds:

(61)

\exp(-i\xi X)=1-i\xi X-\xi^{2}X^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\varsigma)\exp(-i\xi X\varsigma)\mathrm{d}\varsigma.

Therefore

(62)

\hat{g}(\xi)=\mathbb{E}(\exp(-i\xi X))=\mathbb{E}(1-i\xi X)+R(\xi)=1-i\xi\mathbb{E}(X)+R(\xi),

where

(63)

R(\xi)=\mathbb{E}(\exp(-i\xi X)-1+i\xi X)=-\xi^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\varsigma)\mathbb{E}(X^{2}\exp(-i\xi X\varsigma))\mathrm{d}\varsigma

denotes the remainder term. If
\mathbb{E}(X^{2})<+\infty
then

(64)

|R(\xi)|\leq|\xi|^{2}\int_{0}^{1}(1-\varsigma)\mathbb{E}(X^{2})\mathrm{d}\varsigma=\frac{\mathbb{E}(X^{2})}{2}|\xi|^{2}.

On the other hand, if
g(v)=\frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}v^{-1-\alpha}\exp(-\beta/v)
, with
\alpha>1
and
\beta>0
, then for
\xi>0

(65)

\begin{split}|R(\xi)|&\ \ \leq\int_{0}^{+\infty}|\exp(-i\xi v)-1+i\xi v|\frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}v^{-1-\alpha}\mathrm{d}v\\[5.69054pt]
&\overset{\varsigma=\xi v}{=}\xi^{\alpha}\int_{0}^{+\infty}|\exp(-i\varsigma)-1+i\varsigma|\frac{\beta^{\alpha}}{\Gamma(\alpha)}\varsigma^{-1-\alpha}\mathrm{d}\varsigma\\[11.38109pt]
&\ \ \leq C(\alpha,\beta)|\xi|^{\alpha},\end{split}

where
C(\alpha,\beta)>0
because
\exp(-i\varsigma)-1+i\varsigma=-\frac{\varsigma^{2}}{2}+\mathchoice{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{{\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}}}{\scalebox{.7}{$\scriptscriptstyle\mathcal{O}$}}(\varsigma^{3})
for
\varsigma\to 0
. The calculations for
\xi<0
are similar and the thesis follows.
∎

Remark 8
.

The estimates of Lemma
4
are interesting in the limit
|\xi|\to 0
. Indeed, we trivially have

(66)

\begin{split}|R(\xi)|\leq\mathbb{E}(2+|\xi|X)=2+|\xi|\mathbb{E}(X),\end{split}

which is stronger than the previous estimates for sufficiently large
|\xi|
.

Assume that the initial distribution
h^{\mathrm{in}}(v)=h(0,v)
has finite second moment. Lemma
4
implies that, in the limit
|\xi|\to 0
,

(67)

\hat{h}(0,\xi)-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(0,\xi)=\mathcal{O}(|\xi|).

Note that this equality holds even if the local equilibrium does not have a finite energy. Therefore, as
|\xi|\to 0
,

(68)

|\xi|^{-2s}|\hat{h}(0,\xi)-\hat{h}^{\infty}(0,\xi)|^{2}\leq C|\xi|^{-2s}|\xi|^{2}

for some
C>0
, hence
\left\|h(0,\xi)-h^{\infty}(0,\xi)\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}<+\infty
for all
s\in\big(\frac{1}{2},1\big)
. Obviously, the same is true if one of the two distributions is replaced with the local equilibrium
h^{\mathrm{eq}}
defined by (
56
). We are now ready to prove a convergence result in the spaces
\dot{H}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
,
s\in\big(\frac{1}{2},1\big)
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
D_{p}(v)=v
and
h
be a solution to the Fokker–Planck equation (
48
), starting from a nonnegative initial datum
h^{\mathrm{in}}
with finite second moment. Assume that
\mathcal{F}[f](t)>0
for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
\mathcal{F}[f]\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{F}^{\infty}>0
and
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{F}[f]\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
. Then, for any
s\in\big(\frac{1}{2},1\big)
, the difference
h-h^{\infty}
converges to
0
in the homogeneous Sobolev space
\dot{H}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
.

Proof.

Since

\left\|h-h^{\infty}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\leq\left\|h-h^{\mathrm{eq}}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}+\left\|h^{\mathrm{eq}}-h^{\infty}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})},

we divide the proof into two parts, similarly to what we have done in Subsection
3.4
. Firstly, we will demonstrate that
\left\|h-h^{\mathrm{eq}}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
, and then we will prove that
\left\|h^{\mathrm{eq}}-h^{\infty}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
.

Step 1 – Convergence to the local equilibrium
{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}
.
We follow the strategy adopted in
[
45
]
. Since the right hand side of the Fokker–Planck equation (
48
) vanishes when evaluated at the local equilibrium
h^{\mathrm{eq}}
, we have

(69)

\tau_{p}\partial_{t}(h-h^{\mathrm{eq}})=-\tau_{p}\partial_{t}h^{\mathrm{eq}}+\partial_{v}\left(\left(\mu v-\theta\mathcal{F}[f]\right)(h-h^{\mathrm{eq}}\right)+\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2}\partial^{2}_{v}\left(v^{2}(h-h^{\mathrm{eq}})\right).

The Fourier transformed version of (
69
) is
[
57
]

\begin{split}\tau_{p}\partial_{t}(\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}})=\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2}\xi^{2}\partial_{\xi}^{2}(\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}})-\mu\xi\partial_{\xi}(\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}})-i\theta\mathcal{F}[f]\xi(\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}})-\tau_{p}\partial_{t}\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}},\end{split}

Let
\hat{h}(t,\xi)-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,\xi)=a(t,\xi)+ib(t,\xi)
and
\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,\xi)=\tilde{a}(t,\xi)+i\tilde{b}(t,\xi)
, then

\begin{split}\tau_{p}\partial_{t}a=&\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2}\xi^{2}\partial_{\xi}^{2}a-\mu\xi\partial_{\xi}a+\theta\mathcal{F}[f]\xi b-\tau_{p}\partial_{t}\tilde{a},\\
\tau_{p}\partial_{t}b=&\frac{\zeta^{2}}{2}\xi^{2}\partial_{\xi}^{2}b-\mu\xi\partial_{\xi}b-\theta\mathcal{F}[f]\xi a-\tau_{p}\partial_{t}\tilde{b}.\end{split}

If we multiply the first equation by
2a(t,\xi)
and the second one by
2b(t,\xi)
, summing up we get

(70)

\begin{split}\tau_{p}\partial_{t}\left|\hat{h}(t,\xi)-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,\xi)\right|^{2}&=\zeta^{2}\xi^{2}(a\partial^{2}_{\xi}a+b\partial^{2}_{\xi}b)-\mu\xi\partial_{\xi}\left|\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|^{2}-2a\tau_{p}\partial_{t}\tilde{a}-2b\tau_{p}\partial_{t}\tilde{b}\\[11.38109pt]
&\leq\zeta^{2}\xi^{2}(a\partial^{2}_{\xi}a+b\partial^{2}_{\xi}b)-\mu\xi\partial_{\xi}\left|\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|^{2}+2\tau_{p}\left|\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|\left|\partial_{t}\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|.\end{split}

Multiplying by
|\xi|^{-2s}
,
\frac{1}{2}<s<1
, and integrating over
\mathbb{R}
with respect to
\xi
, we obtain the evolution equation for
\|h-h^{\mathrm{eq}}\|_{\dot{H}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}^{2}
, which reads

(71)

\begin{split}\tau_{p}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{-2s}|\hat{h}(t,\xi)-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,\xi)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi&\leq\underbrace{\zeta^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{2-2s}\left(a\partial^{2}_{\xi}a+b\partial^{2}_{\xi}b\right)\mathrm{d}\xi-\mu\int_{\mathbb{R}}\xi|\xi|^{-2s}\partial_{\xi}\left|\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi}_{\mathcal{T}_{1}}\\[5.69054pt]
&\hskip 142.26378pt+\underbrace{2\tau_{p}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|\left|\partial_{t}\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|\mathrm{d}\xi}_{\mathcal{T}_{2}}.\end{split}

Let us start from
\mathcal{T}_{1}
. Suitable repeated integrations by parts (and the behavior of the Fourier transform for large
|\xi|
) imply that
[
45
,
56
,
57
]

(72)

\mathcal{T}_{1}=(1-2s)(\zeta^{2}(1-s)+\mu)\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi-\zeta^{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{2-2s}\left(\left|\partial_{\xi}a\right|^{2}+\left|\partial_{\xi}b\right|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}\xi.

Then, for any
R>0
,

(73)

0\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{-2s}\big(\xi\partial_{\xi}a(t,\xi)-Ra(t,\xi)\big)^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi=\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{2-2s}|\partial_{\xi}a|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi+\left(R^{2}+R(1-2s)\right)\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{-2s}a^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi,

where we have integrated by parts to deal with the mixed term of the square. In particular, we find

(74)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{2-2s}|\partial_{\xi}a(t,\xi)|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi\geq(R(2s-1)-R^{2})\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{-2s}a^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi,

and an analogous inequality holds for
b(t,\xi)
. By choosing
R
in the optimal way, namely
R=\frac{2s-1}{2}
, we thus obtain

(75)

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{2-2s}\left(\left|\partial_{\xi}a(t,\xi)\right|^{2}+\left|\partial_{\xi}b(t,\xi)\right|^{2}\right)\mathrm{d}\xi\geq\frac{(2s-1)^{2}}{4}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi.

Combining (
72
) and (
75
), we infer that
\mathcal{T}_{1}
is controlled by

(76)

\mathcal{T}_{1}\leq-\underbrace{(2s-1)\left(\zeta^{2}\frac{3-2s}{4}+\mu\right)}_{C_{0}>0}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi.

Concerning
\mathcal{T}_{2}
, by the definition of the Fourier transform and the properties of the inverse Gamma distributions
[
45
, Section 5(a)]
(see also
[
32
]
) we have

(77)

|\partial_{t}\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,\xi)|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\partial_{t}h^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,v)\exp(-i\xi v)\mathrm{d}v\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|\partial_{t}h^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,v)|\mathrm{d}v\leq C_{1}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{F}[f](t)\right|,

for some suitable constant
C_{1}>0
. Since both
h
and
h^{\mathrm{eq}}
are probability densities, then
\hat{h}(t,0)=\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,0)=1
and there exists a constant
C_{2}>0
such that the following Taylor expansion with Lagrange remainder can be bounded as

(78)

|\hat{h}(t,\xi)-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,\xi)|=|\partial_{\xi}\hat{h}(t,\xi_{1})-\partial_{\xi}\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,\xi_{2})||\xi|\leq(m_{p}(t)+m_{p}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t))|\xi|\leq C_{2}|\xi|,

where
\xi_{1},\xi_{2}\in(\min\{0,\xi\},\max\{0,\xi\})
and we have defined
\displaystyle m_{p}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t)=\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}vh^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,v)\mathrm{d}v
. Indeed,

(79)

|\partial_{\xi}\hat{h}(t,\xi)|=\left|-i\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}vh(t,v)\exp(-i\xi v)\mathrm{d}v\right|\leq\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}vh(t,v)\mathrm{d}v=m_{p}(t),

so that (
52
) and (
50
) hold (clearly, a similar inequality holds also for
\partial_{\xi}\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,\xi)
). Combining (
77
) and (
78
) we then get

|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|\left|\partial_{t}\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|\leq C_{1}C_{2}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{F}[f]\right||\xi|^{1-2s},

which in turn implies that for any
R>0

\int_{|\xi|\leq R}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}(t,\xi)-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,\xi)\right|\left|\partial_{t}\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,\xi)\right|\mathrm{d}\xi\leq C_{1}C_{2}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{F}[f]\right|\frac{R^{2-2s}}{1-q},

hence Cauchy–Schwartz inequality allows to infer that

\begin{split}\int_{|\xi|>R}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}(t,\xi)-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,\xi)\right|\left|\partial_{t}\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,\xi)\right|\mathrm{d}\xi&\leq\sqrt{\int_{|\xi|>R}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi}\;\sqrt{\int_{|\xi|>R}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\partial_{t}\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi}\\[5.69054pt]
&\leq C_{1}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{F}[f]\right|\sqrt{\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi}\;\sqrt{\int_{|\xi|>R}|\xi|^{-2s}\mathrm{d}\xi}\\[5.69054pt]
&=C_{1}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{F}[f]\right|\frac{1}{R^{q-\frac{1}{2}}}\sqrt{\frac{2}{2s-1}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi}.\end{split}

Combining these two inequalities and choosing
R
in the optimal way, the term
\mathcal{T}_{2}
can be finally bounded as

(80)

\mathcal{T}_{2}\leq\tau_{p}C_{3}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{F}[f]\right|\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi\right)^{\frac{2-2s}{3-2s}},

where
C_{3}=C_{3}(s,C_{1},C_{2})>0
. At last, combining (
71
), (
76
), and (
80
) we get

(81)

\begin{split}\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}(t,\xi)-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,\xi)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi\leq-\frac{C_{0}}{\tau_{p}}\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi+C_{3}\left|\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{F}[f]\right|\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi\right)^{\frac{2-2s}{3-2s}}.\end{split}

Since
\frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{F}[f]\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
, it is easy to demonstrate that
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}(t,\xi)-\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,\xi)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
, implying the convergence of
h
toward
h^{\mathrm{eq}}
with respect to the
\dot{H}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
norm, for
s\in\big(\frac{1}{2},1\big)
.

Step 2 – Convergence to the global equilibrium
h^{\infty}
.
For this step, notice that the dominate convergence theorem implies that
\left\|h^{\mathrm{eq}}-h^{\infty}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
. Indeed, for a sufficiently large
t
,

(82)

\displaystyle h^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,v)\leq g(v)=\begin{cases}2\frac{\left(2\theta\mathcal{F}^{\infty}\right)^{1+\frac{2\mu}{\zeta^{2}}}}{\zeta^{2-\frac{4\mu}{\zeta^{2}}}\Gamma\left(1+\frac{2\mu}{\zeta^{2}}\right)}v^{-2\left(1+\frac{\mu}{\zeta^{2}}\right)}\exp\left(-\frac{\theta\mathcal{F}^{\infty}}{\zeta^{2}}\frac{1}{v}\right),\quad&0<v<1,\\
\\
2\frac{\left(2\theta\mathcal{F}^{\infty}\right)^{1+\frac{2\mu}{\zeta^{2}}}}{\zeta^{2-\frac{4\mu}{\zeta^{2}}}\Gamma\left(1+\frac{2\mu}{\zeta^{2}}\right)}v^{-2\left(1+\frac{\mu}{\zeta^{2}}\right)},&\quad v\geq 1,\end{cases}

with
g\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, and obviously
h^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,v)\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}h^{\infty}(v)
pointwise in
v\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
.

For a fixed
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
, we have that

(83)

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,\xi)-\hat{h}^{\infty}(\xi)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi=\underbrace{\int_{|\xi|>\varepsilon}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}-\hat{h}^{\infty}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi}_{\mathcal{T}_{3}}+\underbrace{\int_{|\xi|\leq\varepsilon}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}-\hat{h}^{\infty}\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi}_{\mathcal{T}_{4}},

and we can study the two integrals separately. Let us start with
\mathcal{T}_{3}
. For all
\xi\in\mathbb{R}
we have

(84)

\left|\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,\xi)-\hat{h}^{\infty}(\xi)\right|=\left|\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\big(h^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,v)-h^{\infty}(v)\big)\exp(-i\xi v)\mathrm{d}v\right|\leq\left\|h^{\mathrm{eq}}-h^{\infty}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}.

Therefore

(85)

\sup_{\xi\in\mathbb{R}}\left|\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,\xi)-\hat{h}^{\infty}(\xi)\right|^{2}\leq\left\|h^{\mathrm{eq}}-h^{\infty}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}^{2},

which implies that

(86)

\mathcal{T}_{3}\leq\left\|h^{\mathrm{eq}}-h^{\infty}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}^{2}\int_{|\xi|>\varepsilon}|\xi|^{-2s}\mathrm{d}\xi=\frac{2}{2s-1}\left\|h^{\mathrm{eq}}-h^{\infty}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}^{2}\varepsilon^{1-2s}.

Consider now the integral
\mathcal{T}_{4}
. Substituting
\hat{h}
with
\hat{h}^{\textnormal{eq}}
in (
67
) leads directly to

(87)

\mathcal{T}_{4}\leq\frac{2C}{3-2s}\varepsilon^{3-2s},

for some
C>0
which does not depend on
t
since the first moment is bounded (recall (
52
) and (
50
)) and, in the case of an infinite second moment, with the notation of Lemma
4
,
\alpha
is constant and
\beta
is bounded because
\mathcal{F}[f]\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}\mathcal{F}^{\infty}
(hence the estimate for
|R(\xi)|
is uniform in time).

In conclusion,

(88)

\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}|\xi|^{-2s}\left|\hat{h}^{\mathrm{eq}}(t,\xi)-\hat{h}^{\infty}(\xi)\right|^{2}\mathrm{d}\xi\leq\frac{2}{2s-1}\left\|h^{\mathrm{eq}}-h^{\infty}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}^{2}\varepsilon^{1-2s}+\frac{2C}{3-2s}\varepsilon^{3-2s}.

Setting, for example,
\varepsilon=\varepsilon(t)=\left\|h^{\mathrm{eq}}-h^{\infty}\right\|_{L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}
we obtain that
\left\|h^{\mathrm{eq}}-h^{\infty}\right\|_{\dot{H}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
.
∎

Remark 9
.

The calculations of Theorem
2
could be repeated to prove the convergence of
h
to equilibrium, even when initially
h^{\mathrm{in}}
does not have a finite energy. However, in this case one would also have to repeat the calculations of Lemma
4
to demonstrate that
h(0,v)-h^{\mathrm{eq}}(0,v)\in\dot{H}^{-s}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
, which held true in the proof of Theorem
2
thanks precisely to the hypothesis that the energy of the initial distribution was finite.

Remark 10
.

Assuming the exponential relaxation of
\mathcal{F}[f]
toward
\mathcal{F}^{\infty}>0
, it is possible to recover the convergence of
h
to the global equilibrium
h^{\infty}
in
L^{1}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
. Indeed, it would be enough to adapt the computations from the proof of
[
8
, Theorem 2]
. The only additional difficulty would arise from the fact that here our (local and global) equilibria are given by inverse gamma distributions, while in the cited paper the equilibria were described by beta distributions. These lead to the appearance in the estimates of integral terms involving the
\log
moment of the solution to the Fokker–Planck equation, which could be controlled by applying Hölder’s inequality and by exploiting the regularity of the solution itself. In the present setting, the inverse gamma distributions would instead produce more singular moments of the form
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\frac{1}{v}h(t,v)\mathrm{d}v
, and the regularity of
h
could not be used to bound them since obviously
\frac{1}{v}
does not belong to any
L^{q}(\mathbb{R}_{+})
,
q\geq 1
. The idea in this case would thus be to reduce the singularity via an integration by parts, recovering integrals of the form
\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}_{+}}\log v\partial_{v}h(t,v)\mathrm{d}v
that one could estimate once again thanks to Hölder’s inequality, provided that
\partial_{v}h
is regular enough. Such regularity would follow from an adaptation of Proposition
4
to study the evolution of the norms
\|\partial_{v}h\|_{L^{q}(\mathbb{R}_{+})}
,
q\geq 2
, under suitable additional boundary conditions on the solution
h
.

We spend some final words on the particular case of
\mathcal{F}^{\infty}=0
. Since (
52
) implies that
m_{p}\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0
, then
h(t,v)
converges (in the sense of distributions) toward the Dirac delta
\delta(v)
because the support of
h
is always contained in
\mathbb{R}_{+}
(even if
D_{p}(v)\neq v
). Indeed, for any
\varepsilon>0

(89)

\int_{\varepsilon}^{+\infty}h(t,v)\mathrm{d}v\leq\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_{\varepsilon}^{+\infty}vh(t,v)\mathrm{d}v\leq\frac{m_{p}}{\varepsilon}\underset{t\to+\infty}{\longrightarrow}0.

This result is not surprising. Indeed,
\mathcal{F}^{\infty}=0
is equivalent to
\hat{w}=1
, which implies that only a negligible part of the population will contribute to the spread of the products, hence their popularity can only decrease. Interestingly, the variance of
h
converges to zero (i.e., to the variance of
\delta(v)
) if and only if
\zeta^{2}<2\mu
(see (
54
)). Indeed, if
\zeta^{2}\geq 2\mu
the diffusion process is very strong and a relevant fraction of the mass of
h
remains scattered over
\mathbb{R}_{+}
for any time
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
. This represents a nice example of the fact that the convergence in the sense of distributions does not imply the convergence of the moments.

5.
Numerical simulations

We conclude the analysis of our model with several numerical simulations to illustrate the complete behaviors of system (
45
). We start by presenting the numerical algorithm used in the simulations in Subsections
5.1
and
5.2
, while we will carry out a series of tests in the following subsections. We will mainly describe the structure-preserving numerical scheme for the kinetic SIR-type system (
3
) since it represents a generalization of the numerical scheme employed in the simulations of the Fokker–Planck model (
48
). However, we will explain how the two schemes interact with each other. Concerning the numerical tests, we will start by analyzing the trends to equilibrium of the simplified model (
31
), before moving to the analysis of epidemic dynamics considering the original model (
3
). Throughout this last section we will always assume
D(w)=\sqrt{1-w^{2}}
and
D_{p}(v)=v
, for which we derived explicit expressions for the equilibria of the models.

5.1.
Numerical algorithm for the kinetic SIR system

The approximation of the kinetic SIR system (
3
) is obtained through a splitting strategy to deal separately with the Fokker–Planck opinion formation process and with the epidemiological operators. Specifically, the opinion step is advanced using a semi-implicit, structure-preserving scheme
[
50
]
, whereas the epidemiological step is integrated using a classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method.

In the following, we will focus on the Chang–Cooper-type scheme
[
17
]
adopted for the Fokker–Planck step. This method is structure-preserving, meaning that it preserves at the numerical level the structural properties of the model, by conserving mass, maintaining the solution nonnegative and capturing its correct long-time behavior.

Domain discretization.
Given
N_{x}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
points in the graphon domain
\Omega=[0,1]
, the (uniform) spatial grid is given by

x_{i}=i\Delta x,\qquad i\in\{0,\ldots,N_{x}\},\qquad\Delta x=\frac{1}{N_{x}},

with
x_{N_{x}}=1
, while given
N_{w}\in\mathbb{N}^{*}
points in the opinion domain
\mathcal{I}=[-1,1]
, the (uniform) opinion grid reads

w_{j}=-1+j\Delta w,\qquad j\in\{0,\ldots,N_{w}\},\qquad\Delta w=\frac{2}{N_{w}},

with
w_{N_{w}}
= 1. Finally, the time variable is discretized as

t^{n}=n\Delta t,\qquad n\in\{0,\ldots,N_{t}\},

where
\Delta t>0
is an adaptive time step chosen to satisfy the stability requirements of the scheme (which will be presented later on) and we denote with
T>0
the final simulation time, which may vary between the different tests.

At each time
t^{n}
, the discrete approximation of each distribution function
f_{J}
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
, is represented by the values
(f_{J})_{i,j}^{n}\simeq f_{J}(t^{n},x_{i},w_{j})
, forming a two-dimensional array for each compartment. The indices
i
and
j
run over all spatial and opinion grid points, respectively.

Chang–Cooper reconstruction of the
w
-flux.
We formally rewrite the Fokker–Planck operator
\mathbf{Q}
defined by (
10
) in gradient flow form as

Q_{J}(\mathbf{f},\mathbf{f})(t,x,w)=-\partial_{w}\mathcal{J}_{J}(t,x,w),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;x\in\Omega,\;w\in\mathcal{I},

for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
, where the the
J
-th microscopic flux in the opinion variable is given by

\displaystyle\mathcal{J}_{J}(t,x,w)

\displaystyle=-\lambda\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{K}[f_{J^{\prime}}](t,x,w)f_{J}(t,x,w)+\sigma_{J}^{2}w\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{H}[f_{J^{\prime}}](t,x)f_{J}(t,x,w)

\displaystyle\hskip 99.58464pt-\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}(1-w^{2})\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{H}[f_{J^{\prime}}](t,x)\partial_{w}f_{J}(t,x,w),\quad t\in\mathbb{R}_{+},\;x\in\Omega,\;w\in\mathcal{I}.

This structure allows for a Chang–Cooper-type discretization in
w
. For this, let us define for any
t\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
,
x\in\Omega
, and
w\in\mathcal{I}
, the drift and diffusion terms

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{J}^{\mathrm{drift}}(t,x,w)

\displaystyle=-\lambda\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{K}[f_{J^{\prime}}](t,x,w)+\sigma_{J}^{2}w\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{H}[f_{J^{\prime}}](t,x),

\displaystyle\mathcal{T}_{J}^{\mathrm{diff}}(t,x,w)

\displaystyle=\frac{\sigma_{J}^{2}}{2}(1-w^{2})\sum_{J^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}}\mathcal{H}[f_{J^{\prime}}](t,x),

whose cell-averaged discretizations
(\mathcal{T}_{J}^{\mathrm{drift}})_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}
and
(\mathcal{T}_{J}^{\mathrm{diff}})_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}
are given by

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}_{J}^{\mathrm{drift}})_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta w}\int_{w_{j}}^{w_{j+1}}\mathcal{T}_{J}^{\mathrm{drift}}(t^{n},x_{i},w)\,\mathrm{d}w,

\displaystyle(\mathcal{T}_{J}^{\mathrm{diff}})_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{\Delta w}\int_{w_{j}}^{w_{j+1}}\mathcal{T}_{J}^{\mathrm{diff}}(t^{n},x_{i},w)\,\mathrm{d}w.

Then, at each fixed position
x_{i}
on the graphon and time
t^{n}
, the Chang–Cooper numerical flux in the
w
-direction is written as

(\mathcal{F}_{J})_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}=(\mathcal{T}_{J}^{\mathrm{drift}})_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}(\bar{f}_{J})_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}-(\mathcal{T}_{J}^{\mathrm{diff}})_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}\frac{(\bar{f}_{J})_{i,j+1}^{n}-(\bar{f}_{J})_{i,j}^{n}}{\Delta w},

where the central weighted averages
(\bar{f}_{J})_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}
is defined by

(\bar{f}_{J})_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}=(1-\delta_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n})(f_{J})_{i,j+1}^{n}+\delta_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}(f_{J})_{i,j}^{n},

with weights
[
50
]

\delta_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}=\frac{1}{\lambda_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}}+\frac{1}{1-\exp\big(\lambda_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}\big)},

and

\lambda_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}=\int_{w_{j}}^{w_{j+1}}\frac{(\mathcal{T}_{J}^{\mathrm{drift}})_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}+\partial_{w}(\mathcal{T}_{J}^{\mathrm{diff}})_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}}{(\mathcal{T}_{J}^{\mathrm{drift}})_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}}\mathrm{d}w.

Mass conservation is ensured by the no-flux boundary conditions imposed at the boundaries of the opinion domain
[
50
]
.

Discretization in time.
The drift–diffusion operator described above is integrated with a semi-implicit backward Euler step. For each fixed position
x_{i}
on the graphon, the update in the opinion variable reads

\frac{(f_{J})_{i,j}^{n+1}-(f_{J})_{i,j}^{n}}{\Delta t}=\frac{1}{\Delta w}\left((\mathcal{F}_{J})_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n+1}-(\mathcal{F}_{J})_{i,j-\frac{1}{2}}^{n+1}\right).

This semi-implicit discretization leads, at each time step and for each position
x_{i}
, to a tridiagonal linear system in the
w
-index for
(f_{J})_{i,j}^{n+1}
, which can be written in matrix form as

A[(\mathbf{f}_{J})_{i}^{n}](\mathbf{f}_{J})_{i}^{n+1}=(\mathbf{f}_{J})_{i}^{n},

where
(\mathbf{f}_{J})_{i}^{n}=((f_{J})_{i,0}^{n},\ldots,(f_{J})_{i,N_{w}}^{n})
is the vector of unknowns at time
t^{n}
and
A[(\mathbf{f}_{J})_{i}^{n}]
is the tridiagonal matrix whose entries depend on the drift–diffusion terms and on the Chang–Cooper weights introduced above.

A key property of this semi-implicit scheme is that, under a suitable restriction on the time step, nonnegativity of the solution is preserved, namely from
(\mathbf{f}_{J})_{i}^{n}\geq 0
it follows that
(\mathbf{f}_{J})_{i}^{n+1}\geq 0
. More precisely, nonnegativity is ensured as soon as the CFL (Courant–Friederichs–Lewy) condition

(90)

\Delta t<\frac{\Delta w}{2C^{n}}\qquad\textnormal{with}\qquad C^{n}=\max_{\underset{j\in\{0,\dots,N_{w}\}}{\underset{i\in\{0,\dots,N_{x}\}}{J\in\mathcal{C}}}}\left|(\mathcal{T}_{J}^{\mathrm{drift}})_{i,j+\frac{1}{2}}^{n}\right|

is satisfied
[
50
]
.

Operator splitting for the full kinetic SIR system.
To efficiently advance system (
3
) in time, we employ an operator splitting technique during which, for any fixed position
x_{i}
on the graphon, opinion and epidemic dynamics are solved separately over each time interval
[t^{n},t^{n+1}]
. We adopt a simple first-order splitting scheme composed of the following two steps.

Firstly, in the opinion formation step, for each compartment
J\in\mathcal{C}
we solve

\partial_{t}f_{J}^{*}=\frac{1}{\tau}Q_{J}(\mathbf{f}^{*},\mathbf{f}^{*})

using the semi-implicit structure-preserving Chang–Cooper scheme described beforehand. Note that here
f_{J}^{*}
is a two-dimensional array whose components correspond to a pair
(x_{i},w_{j})
. Similarly
\mathbf{f}^{*}
denotes a three-dimensional array incorporating all values of the two-dimensional arrays
f_{J}^{*}
,
J\in\mathcal{C}
. The result of this step is then

f_{J}^{n,*}=\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\Delta t}(\mathbf{f}^{n},\mathbf{f}^{n}),

for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
. Secondly, in the epidemiological step, we consider the updated opinion distributions
\mathbf{f}^{*}
as initial datum to solve the SIR-type system

\displaystyle\left\{\begin{aligned} \partial_{t}f_{S}^{**}&=-f_{S}^{**}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\beta_{T}(w,w_{*})f_{I}^{**}(y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*},\\[5.69054pt]
\partial_{t}f_{I}^{**}&=f_{S}^{**}\int_{\Omega\times\mathcal{I}}\beta_{T}(w,w_{*})f_{I}^{**}(y,w_{*})\mathrm{d}y\mathrm{d}w_{*}-\gamma f_{I}^{**},\\[11.38109pt]
\partial_{t}f_{R}^{**}&=\gamma f_{I}^{**},\end{aligned}\right.

using a classical fourth-order Runge–Kutta method. The outcome of this step is

(91)

f_{J}^{n,**}=\mathcal{E}_{J}^{\Delta t}(\mathbf{f}^{n,*},\mathbf{f}^{n,*}),

for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
. In conclusion, the overall update of the unknowns over one time step is given by

(92)

f_{J}^{n+1}=\mathcal{E}_{J}^{\Delta t}\left(\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\Delta t}(\mathbf{f}^{n},\mathbf{f}^{n}),\mathcal{Q}_{J}^{\Delta t}(\mathbf{f}^{n},\mathbf{f}^{n})\right),

for any
J\in\mathcal{C}
.

In all our simulations, the opinion domain
\mathcal{I}
is discretized using a uniform grid of
N_{w}=101
points, while
\Omega
is discretized with
N_{x}=21
evenly spaced nodes. Moreover, unless otherwise specified, the weights of the Chang–Cooper-type scheme are computed using a Newton–Cotes sixth-order accurate quadrature formula.

5.2.
Numerical algorithm for the popularity model

As the popularity of a product is quantified by the variable
v\in\mathbb{R}_{+}
and since the steady state of the kinetic equation (
48
) is represented by an inverse gamma distribution with fat tails, in the numerical approximation the domain of
v
must be truncated in an appropriate manner. In more details, we discretize the popularity interval
[0,L]
using a uniform grid

v_{k}=k\Delta v,\qquad k\in\{0,\dots,N_{v}\},\qquad\Delta v=\frac{L}{N_{v}},

where the right boundary
L
and the number of nodes
N_{v}
are selected adaptively by balancing the retention of the inverse gamma tail-mass and a sufficient resolution of its peak, namely we impose a tail criterion based on the incomplete gamma function, choosing
L
so that the residual density beyond
L
is below a prescribed tolerance
\varepsilon_{\mathrm{tail}}
. In addition, we prescribe a target mesh size
\Delta v_{\mathrm{target}}
to resolve the peak of the equilibria with a fixed number of grid points. The final pair
(N_{v},L)
is obtained by enforcing

\Delta v\leq\Delta v_{\mathrm{target}},\qquad N_{v}\in[N_{\min},N_{\max}],\qquad L\geq L_{\min},

where
N_{\min}
and
N_{\max}
are user-prescribed bounds on the admissible grid size and
L_{\min}
is a minimum admissible truncation ensuring that the peak region is included.

The time variable is then discretized as

t^{m}=m\Delta t_{p},\qquad m\in\{0,\ldots,N_{t_{p}}\},

where
\Delta t_{p}>0
is an adaptive time step chosen to satisfy the CFL condition detailed below (which ensures the stability of the scheme for the popularity) and in general
\Delta t_{p}\neq\Delta t
. We highlight however that the final simulation time
T
does instead coincide with that used to run the structure-preserving scheme for the kinetic SIR system. This is natural, since the evolution of the popularity depends on how the nonlocal quantity
\mathcal{F}[f](t)
evolves through
f=\sum_{J\in\mathcal{C}}f_{J}
. More precisely, time integration of system (
45
) involves two distinct CFL constraints. The SIR Fokker–Planck solver is run on a time grid determined by the time step
\Delta t
computed from the CFL stability condition (
90
), which provides values of the functional
\mathcal{F}[f](t)
only at the corresponding output instants. The popularity equation is then advanced with an independent time step
\Delta t_{p}
dictated by the stability condition of the structure-preserving scheme in the variable
v
, which reads

\Delta t_{p}<\frac{\Delta v}{2C^{m}}\qquad\textnormal{with}\qquad C^{m}=\max_{k\in\{0,\dots,N_{v}\}}\left|\left(\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{drift}}\right)_{k+\frac{1}{2}}^{m}\right|,

where now

\left(\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{drift}}\right)_{k+\frac{1}{2}}^{m}=\frac{1}{\Delta v}\int_{v_{k}}^{v_{k+1}}\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{drift}}(t^{m},v)\mathrm{d}v,

and

\mathcal{T}^{\mathrm{drift}}(t,v)=-(\mu+\zeta^{2})v+\theta\mathcal{F}[f](t).

Since typically the popularity time step
\Delta t_{p}
is much smaller than the kinetic SIR time step
\Delta t
, the quantity
\mathcal{F}[f](t)

must be evaluated at intermediate times to determine the evolution of the popularity. We therefore reconstruct
\mathcal{F}[f](t)
by piecewise-constant interpolation on the kinetic SIR output grid, namely we take
\mathcal{F}[f](t)=\mathcal{F}[f](t^{n})
for
t\in[t^{n},t^{n+1})
.

6.
Conclusions

In this work we introduced kinetic SIR-type models to simulate the spread of an epidemic while explicitly accounting for the opinions that individuals hold regarding the adoption of protective behaviors. Opinion exchanges were mediated by a social network, represented by an underlying graphon
[
26
]
. In particular, each agent occupied a position on the graphon, which determined with which other individuals he was more prone to interact and by who he was more strongly influenced (these two properties were independent of each other). We also introduced a Fokker–Planck-type equation to simulate the spread of the idea of the dangerousness of the disease as the epidemic evolves.

Our kinetic epidemiological model is a generalization of the classical macroscopic SIR system, and we showed how to derive the latter starting from the former. We also introduced the propensity to interact of each agent as a combination of the above discussed properties depending on the position on the graphon. The importance of this new quantity is twofold. Firstly, it allowed us to distinguish between the presence and the absence of opinion leaders (such as politicians and influencers), highlighting the influence that a social network has on the dynamics occurring inside a population. This is an important novelty of our work since in previous articles (see
[
8
,
9
]
) they had to be introduced separately from the rest of the population due to mathematical complexities. Secondly, the propensity to interact allowed us to to derive a simplification of the original model, which admits explicit global equilibria. These equilibria showed that agents prone to interact tend to be characterized by opinion polarization, while socially active ones can be characterized by consensus formation, highlighting the importance of social interactions to prevent the formation of extreme opinions, in line with the results presented in
[
8
]
by the first and second author. Moreover, we demonstrated strong
L^{1}
-convergence to equilibrium results toward these equilibria, also providing an explicit rate of convergence through relative entropy methods
[
4
,
8
]
.

The spread of the popularity of the idea of the dangerousness of the disease did not influence the evolution of the epidemics, however it depended on the opinion of the individuals. This Fokker–Planck-type model admitted explicit equilibria, which showed that the population tends to consider the disease dangerous (respectively, not dangerous) not only if there are many agents with an opinion in favor of (respectively, against) a protective behavior, but they must be also prone to interact in order to diffuse their opinion among other agents. Importantly, we were able to demonstrate a convergence to equilibrium result in the homogeneous Sobolev space
\dot{H}^{-s}
,
s\in\big(\frac{1}{2},1\big)
. Moreover, an additional converge to equilibrium result in
L^{1}
was derived under a suitable additional assumption, which was proven to be realistic by the results of the numerical simulations.

To investigate the complete behaviors of the proposed models, we performed several numerical simulations based on a novel structure-preserving scheme
[
50
]
. In particular, the numerical scheme for the SIR model combines a Chang–Cooper type discretization for the Fokker–Planck opinion dynamics with a Runge–Kutta integration of the epidemiological operators, preserving key structural properties such as nonnegativity and mass conservation. Firstly, the numerical simulations reproduced the trends to equilibrium previously mentioned. Secondly, they allowed us to illustrate how the interplay between opinion dynamics and the graphon-mediated interactions affects the epidemic evolution, both in presence and absence of opinion leaders in favor or a protective behavior. For example, the presence of the latter reduced considerably the epidemic spread. Moreover, we introduced a time-dependent quantity, named effective reproduction number, resembling the classical basic reproduction number, whose temporal evolution may exhibit an oscillatory behavior, reflecting in epidemic waves. Notably, these waves naturally arose from our model, without the need of introducing an explicit external forcing (see, e.g.,
[
9
]
).

Throughout this paper, we considered the possibility that
\tau\ll 1
(recall that it represents the fact that the opinion variations happen at a different timescale than the disease diffusion), meaning that the opinion reaches an equilibrium very quickly and that the epidemic evolves according to such opinion distribution. However, it is reasonable to assume that the agents do not immediately have a precise opinion regarding the disease. Hence, a future development might be assuming
\tau\in\mathcal{O}(1)
and considering an SIRS-type model such that the rate of loss of complete immunity is governed by a small parameter
0<\varepsilon\ll 1
(see, e.g.,
[
12
,
37
]
). With this choice, one would introduce an additional slow timescale (with times of the order
\mathcal{O}(1/\varepsilon)
) describing the period between two subsequent waves of the epidemic. In particular, during the fist wave of the epidemic (which happens on the fast timescale) the opinion evolves at the same speed of the epidemic, while it reaches an equilibrium when the macroscopic system enters in the slow timescale (since the opinion evolves always on the fast timescale). Hence, the subsequent waves of the epidemic will be affected by such equilibrium of the opinion. Obviously, this analysis must be mainly carried out with numerical simulations.
