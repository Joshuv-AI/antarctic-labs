Localization of a quantum particle in a classical one-component plasma. Fluctuation-induced random potential and the Coulomb logarithm
Yury A. Budkov

Abstract
We develop a microscopic theory of disorder-induced localization for a quantum particle moving in a fully ionized classical one-component plasma, within the static-fluctuation approximation. The random potential acting on the particle originates from equilibrium thermal fluctuations of the ionic charge density, described within the random phase approximation (RPA). The resulting potential correlation function exhibits an unscreened 1 / r 1/r tail at large distances, leading to a logarithmic divergence of the integrated disorder strength. Using the Feynman path-integral representation of the retarded Green’s function and performing the Gaussian average over the fluctuations exactly, we obtain closed-form expressions for the length scale ℓ ⁡ ( k ) \ell(k) that characterizes the exponential decay of the disorder-averaged Green’s function, with Planck’s constant fully restored. In the weak-disorder (high-energy) regime, ℓ ⁡ ( k ) = ℏ 4 ​ k 2 / [ m 2 ​ k B ​ T ​ q 0 2 ​ ln ⁡ ( κ ​ L ) ] \ell(k)=\hbar^{4}k^{2}/[m^{2}k_{B}Tq_{0}^{2}\ln(\kappa L)] ; in the strong-disorder (low-energy) limit, ℓ = 4 ​ 2 3 3 ​ ( ℏ 4 / [ m 2 ​ k B ​ T ​ q 0 2 ​ ln ⁡ ( κ ​ L ) ] ) 1 / 3 \ell=\frac{4\sqrt[3]{2}}{3}\big(\hbar^{4}/[m^{2}k_{B}Tq_{0}^{2}\ln(\kappa L)]\big)^{1/3} . Both limits contain the Coulomb logarithm ln ⁡ ( κ ​ L ) \ln(\kappa L) , providing a direct link between quantum localization and classical plasma kinetic theory. We also discuss the limitations of the static-disorder approximation and the role of dynamic screening in real plasmas.



I
Introduction

The phenomenon of localization – the disorder-induced exponential decay of single-particle wave functions – has been a cornerstone of condensed matter physics since its discovery
Anderson et al. 1958
. The localization length
\ell
, which measures the spatial extent of an eigenstate, plays a central role in understanding the metal–insulator transition and low-temperature transport in disordered systems
Lifshits, Gredeskul, and Pastur 1988
;
Kramer and MacKinnon 1993
;
Lee and Ramakrishnan 1985
. While the bulk of theoretical and experimental effort has concentrated on short-range impurity potentials, there exists a broad class of systems – electrolyte solutions, ionic liquids, and fully ionized plasmas – where the dominant interactions are Coulombic. In these systems, screening effects give rise to a finite Debye length
\lambda_{D}=\kappa^{-1}
, but as is well known from classical plasma physics, the potential fluctuations themselves are not fully screened: their correlation function retains a long-range
1/r
tail. The interplay between such long-range correlations and quantum interference is largely unexplored, despite its potential relevance for transport anomalies in dense plasmas and for the physics of electrons in ionic liquids.

The experimental and theoretical progress in understanding localization has been summarized in several authoritative reviews.
Kramer and MacKinnon
Kramer and MacKinnon 1993
provided a comprehensive survey of both weak and strong localization phenomena, including the scaling theory of the Anderson transition, quantum interference effects in disordered metals, and the role of magnetic fields.
The weak-localization regime was thoroughly examined by Bergmann
Bergman 1984
, who presented extensive experimental evidence for logarithmic corrections to the conductivity in thin metallic films and for the Aharonov–Bohm-like magnetoresistance oscillations.
Lee and Ramakrishnan
Lee and Ramakrishnan 1985
reviewed the diagrammatic techniques used to treat interacting and non-interacting disordered electrons, emphasizing the deep connection between weak localization and electron–electron interactions.
A particularly comprehensive analysis of the role of statistical correlations in random potentials was given by Izrailev, Krokhin, and Makarov
Izrailev, Krokhin, and Makarov 2012
, who developed a unified framework for one-dimensional systems with short- and long-range correlated disorder.
Their work demonstrated that specific long-range correlations can give rise to effective mobility edges, and it provided explicit expressions for the localization length in terms of the power spectrum of the random potential.

More recently, the study of localization phenomena in Coulombic systems has gained momentum.
Sous and Grant
Sous and Grant 2019
investigated the role of quenched randomness and localization in ultracold neutral plasmas, highlighting the importance of long-range interactions in disordered many-body settings.
Vojta, Epperlein, and Schreiber
Vojta et al. 1998
studied the quantum Coulomb glass and the interplay between Anderson localization and electron–electron interactions, emphasizing that long-range Coulomb correlations can significantly modify localization properties.
These works underscore the need for a fundamental understanding of how the long-range character of the Coulomb interaction influences quantum localization.

A powerful tool for studying non-perturbative localization effects is the functional-integral approach developed by Efimov
Efimov 2015
.
In Efimov’s formalism, the averaged retarded Green’s function of a quantum particle in a Gaussian random potential is expressed as a path integral over trajectories, and the length scale governing its exponential decay is extracted from the asymptotic behaviour at large distances.
The method is particularly well-suited for our purpose because it reduces the localization problem to the evaluation of a single functional integral whose integrand depends solely on the pair correlation function of the random potential.

In this work we combine the statistical mechanics of a classical one-component plasma with Efimov’s path-integral formalism to construct a first-principles theory of disorder-induced localization in a Coulombic environment.
The random potential acting on a test quantum particle is taken to be the instantaneous electrostatic potential produced by the equilibrium thermal fluctuations of the ionic charge density, which are described within the random phase approximation (RPA).
This route yields a Gaussian random field whose correlation function
K(r)=k_{B}Tq_{0}^{2}(1-e^{-\kappa r})/r
displays the characteristic ionic atmosphere at short distances and a bare Coulomb tail at large distances.
The exact Gaussian average of the Green’s function is then performed, and the resulting path integral is evaluated in the eikonal (straight-line) approximation.
We obtain closed-form expressions for the length scale characterizing the exponential decay of the averaged Green’s function in the weak-disorder (high-energy) and strong-disorder (low-energy) limits, both of which contain the celebrated Coulomb logarithm
\ln(\kappa L)
.
This logarithmic factor, well known from the classical kinetic theory of plasmas
Pitaevskii and Lifshitz 2012
, provides a direct link between quantum localization and the classical transport properties of Coulomb systems.

We emphasize that the present theory is developed within the static-fluctuation approximation: the random potential is treated as a static Gaussian field with prescribed correlations.
This approach captures the essential physics of disorder-induced exponential decay of the single-particle Green’s function, but it does not constitute a proof of Anderson localization in the sense of the scaling theory of localization.
In particular, we do not investigate whether the obtained decay length coincides with the localization length defined from the conductance or from the sensitivity of energy levels to boundary conditions, as required for a complete description of the Anderson transition
Kramer and MacKinnon 1993
.
Such questions lie beyond the scope of the present work, which focuses on the influence of the long-range Coulomb fluctuations on the coherent propagation of a quantum particle.

II
Preliminaries: Efimov’s path-integral formalism

Before applying the method to a plasma, we recall the general framework developed by Efimov
Efimov 2015
for a nonrelativistic quantum particle in a static random potential.
The system is described by the Hamiltonian

H=\frac{\mathbf{p}^{2}}{2m}+W(\mathbf{x}),

(1)

where
W(\mathbf{x})
is a random field with prescribed statistical properties.
We are interested in the retarded Green’s function at energy
E=k^{2}/2m
,

G_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|W)=\left\langle\mathbf{x}\left|\frac{1}{\frac{\mathbf{p}^{2}}{2m}+W(\mathbf{x})-\frac{k^{2}}{2m}+i0}\right|\mathbf{x}^{\prime}\right\rangle.

(2)

The standard Feynman path-integral representation reads

G_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|W)=i\int_{0}^{\infty}dt\,\exp\!\Big[-it\Big(-\frac{\nabla^{2}}{2m}+W(\mathbf{x})-\frac{k^{2}}{2m}-i0\Big)\Big]\delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}).

(3)

To make the expression suitable for averaging over disorder, Efimov introduced the dimensionless variable
s=tk/rm
and scaled the fluctuating part of the path.
After some transformations, the Green’s function takes the compact form
Efimov 2015

G_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|W)=B\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{s^{3/2}}\,\exp\!\Big[\frac{i}{2}kr\Big(s+\frac{1}{s}\Big)\Big]\int\frac{\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\xi}}{C}\,\exp\!\Big[\frac{i}{2}\int_{0}^{r}d\tau\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{2}(\tau)\Big]\\
\times\exp\!\Big[-i\frac{ms}{k}\int_{0}^{r}d\tau\,W\big(\mathbf{x}(\tau)\big)\Big],

(4)

where
\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
,
r=|\mathbf{r}|
,
\mathbf{n}=\mathbf{r}/r
, and the “classical” path is given by

\mathbf{x}(\tau)=\mathbf{n}\tau+\mathbf{x}^{\prime}-\sqrt{\frac{s}{k}}\,\boldsymbol{\xi}(\tau),\qquad 0\leq\tau\leq r,\qquad\boldsymbol{\xi}(0)=\boldsymbol{\xi}(r)=0.

(5)

Here
B
and
C
are normalization constants whose precise values drop out of the final expressions.
The dimensionless parameter
s
plays the role of a scaled inverse mass; the auxiliary field
\boldsymbol{\xi}(\tau)
describes quantum fluctuations around the straight-line trajectory connecting
\mathbf{x}^{\prime}
and
\mathbf{x}
.

If the random potential
W
is Gaussian with zero mean and pair correlation function

K(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime})=\langle W(\mathbf{r})W(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})\rangle,

(6)

the average of the interaction exponential
\exp[-i\frac{ms}{k}\int W]
is performed exactly and yields the following path integral:

\langle G_{k}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}^{\prime}|W)\rangle_{W}=B\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{s^{3/2}}\,e^{\frac{i}{2}kr(s+1/s)}\int\frac{\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\xi}}{C}\,\exp\!\Big[\frac{i}{2}\int_{0}^{r}d\tau\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{2}(\tau)\Big]\\
\times\exp\!\Big[-\frac{m^{2}s^{2}}{2k^{2}}\int_{0}^{r}\!\!d\tau\int_{0}^{r}\!\!d\tau^{\prime}\,K\big(\mathbf{n}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})-\sqrt{\tfrac{s}{k}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}(\tau)-\boldsymbol{\xi}(\tau^{\prime}))\big)\Big].

(7)

The obtained length characterizes the exponential decay of the disorder-averaged Green function within the static Gaussian approximation.
It is defined by the asymptotic decay of the averaged Green’s function at large distances:

\frac{1}{\ell(k)}=\Gamma(k)=-\lim_{r\to\infty}\frac{1}{r}\,\mathrm{Re}\ln\langle G_{k}(r)\rangle_{W}.

(8)

Thus the problem reduces to calculating the functional integral (
7
) for
r\to\infty
and extracting the exponential decay rate.

In the following sections we take
W
to be the potential created by thermal fluctuations of the ionic charge density in a classical one-component plasma, whose Gaussian statistics follows from the RPA.
This allows us to evaluate (
7
) within the eikonal approximation and obtain explicit expressions for
\ell(k)
.

III
Model of the plasma

We consider a one-component plasma (OCP) consisting of mobile ions of charge
q
and a uniform neutralizing background of electrons that ensures overall electroneutrality.
The ions are treated as classical point particles with the average number density
n_{0}
; the background is static and does not contribute to the fluctuating potential.
The plasma is assumed to be ideal, and the medium is vacuum (
\varepsilon=1
).

A quantum test particle (e.g., an electron) of charge
q_{0}
interacts with the instantaneous electrostatic potential created by the fluctuating ion density:

W(\mathbf{r})=q_{0}\int d^{3}r^{\prime}\,\frac{q\,\delta\rho(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|},

(9)

where
\delta\rho(\mathbf{r})=c(\mathbf{r})-n_{0}
is the fluctuation of the ion concentration away from its mean value, and
c(\mathbf{r})
is the local ion density.
The charge density fluctuation is
\delta\rho_{c}(\mathbf{r})=q\,\delta\rho(\mathbf{r})
with
\langle\delta\rho_{c}(\mathbf{r})\rangle=0
.

IV
Correlation function of the random potential

To obtain the statistics of
W(\mathbf{r})
, we consider the effective fluctuation Hamiltonian of the classical one-component ion plasma
Borue and Erukhimovich 1988
;
Budkov and Kalikin 2024
.
Expanding the ideal-gas free energy to second order in the density fluctuation
\delta\rho(\mathbf{r})=c(\mathbf{r})-n_{0}
, measured in units of
k_{B}T
, yields

\beta\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{id}}\simeq\frac{1}{2}\int d^{3}r\,\frac{\bigl(\delta\rho(\mathbf{r})\bigr)^{2}}{n_{0}}.

The Coulomb energy is

\beta U_{\mathrm{C}}=\frac{\beta}{2}\int d^{3}r\,d^{3}r^{\prime}\,\frac{q^{2}\,\delta\rho(\mathbf{r})\delta\rho(\mathbf{r}^{\prime})}{|\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{r}^{\prime}|}.

Introducing the Fourier representation, the total action decouples into independent modes,

S=\frac{1}{2}\int\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,\biggl(\frac{1}{n_{0}}+\frac{4\pi\beta q^{2}}{k^{2}}\biggr)\bigl|\delta\rho(\mathbf{k})\bigr|^{2}.

(10)

Hence
\delta\rho(\mathbf{k})
is a Gaussian field with variance

\langle|\delta\rho(\mathbf{k})|^{2}\rangle=\frac{1}{\dfrac{1}{n_{0}}+\dfrac{4\pi\beta q^{2}}{k^{2}}}=\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{4\pi q^{2}}\,\frac{\kappa^{2}k^{2}}{k^{2}+\kappa^{2}},

(11)

where the inverse Debye screening length is
\kappa^{2}=4\pi n_{0}q^{2}/(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)
.
Equation (
11
) is the standard RPA result for the charge structure factor of a one-component plasma
Barrat and Hansen 2003
.

From (
9
) the Fourier transform of the potential is
W(\mathbf{k})=(4\pi q_{0}/k^{2})\,q\,\delta\rho(\mathbf{k})
.
Consequently
W
is also a Gaussian random field with zero mean and pair correlator

\langle W(\mathbf{k})W(-\mathbf{k})\rangle=\Bigl(\frac{4\pi q_{0}q}{k^{2}}\Bigr)^{\!2}\langle|\delta\rho(\mathbf{k})|^{2}\rangle=4\pi q_{0}^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\,\frac{\kappa^{2}}{k^{2}(k^{2}+\kappa^{2})}.

(12)

Fourier inversion gives the coordinate‑space correlation function

K(r)\equiv\langle W(\mathbf{r})W(0)\rangle=4\pi q_{0}^{2}k_{\mathrm{B}}T\!\int\!\frac{d^{3}k}{(2\pi)^{3}}\,e^{i\mathbf{k}\cdot\mathbf{r}}\Bigl(\frac{1}{k^{2}}-\frac{1}{k^{2}+\kappa^{2}}\Bigr)=k_{\mathrm{B}}T\,q_{0}^{2}\,\frac{1-e^{-\kappa r}}{r}.

(13)

The limits of (
13
) reveal a clear physical separation of scales:

K(r)\simeq k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}\kappa,\qquad r\ll\kappa^{-1}\quad\text{(constant, ionic atmosphere)},

(14)

K(r)\simeq\frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}Tq_{0}^{2}}{r},\qquad r\gg\kappa^{-1}\quad\text{(bare Coulomb tail)}.

(15)

Thus, the potential fluctuations retain the long‑range
1/r
tail characteristic of three‑dimensional Coulomb systems.

V
Eikonal approximation

Thus averaging over the concentration fluctuations in the RPA, we obtain for the disorder-averaged Green’s function:

G_{k}(r)=B\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{ds}{s^{3/2}}\,e^{\frac{i}{2}kr(s+1/s)}\int\frac{\mathcal{D}\boldsymbol{\xi}}{C}\,\exp\!\Big[\frac{i}{2}\int_{0}^{r}d\tau\,\dot{\boldsymbol{\xi}}^{2}(\tau)\Big]\\
\times\exp\!\Big[-\frac{m^{2}s^{2}}{2k^{2}}\int_{0}^{r}\!\!d\tau\int_{0}^{r}\!\!d\tau^{\prime}\,K\big(\mathbf{n}(\tau-\tau^{\prime})-\sqrt{\tfrac{s}{k}}(\boldsymbol{\xi}(\tau)-\boldsymbol{\xi}(\tau^{\prime}))\big)\Big].

(16)

This is the exact starting point for the localization analysis.

For
r\to\infty
the dominant paths are essentially classical straight lines.
We therefore discard the fluctuations
\boldsymbol{\xi}(\tau)
and set
\boldsymbol{\xi}=0
.
The double integral then factorizes as

\int_{0}^{r}d\tau\int_{0}^{r}d\tau^{\prime}\,K(\mathbf{n}(\tau-\tau^{\prime}))\approx 2r\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,K(u),\qquad r\to\infty.

Using Eq. (
13
) we define

G=m^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,K(u)=m^{2}k_{B}Tq_{0}^{2}\int_{0}^{\infty}du\,\frac{1-e^{-\kappa u}}{u}.

(17)

The integral is logarithmically divergent at the upper limit because
K(u)\sim 1/u
for
u\to\infty
.
Physically, the divergence is cut off by a large-distance scale
L
, which may be the mean free path, the system size, or the phase-coherence length.
Introducing an upper cutoff
L
and evaluating the integral for
\kappa L\gg 1
,

I(L)=\int_{0}^{L}du\,\frac{1-e^{-\kappa u}}{u}=\ln(\kappa L)+\gamma+\mathcal{O}(e^{-\kappa L}),

with
\gamma\approx 0.5772
the Euler–Mascheroni constant.
Hence, to logarithmic accuracy,

G=m^{2}k_{B}Tq_{0}^{2}\,\ln(\kappa L).

(18)

The quantity
\ln(\kappa L)
resembles the Coulomb logarithm familiar from classical plasma kinetic theory
Pitaevskii and Lifshitz 2012
; however, its physical origin is different.
In kinetic theory the logarithm arises from the Coulomb scattering cross section, regularized by Debye screening at large impact parameters and by the classical distance of closest approach at small ones.
Here, in contrast, it originates from the long‑range
1/r
tail of the potential correlator and must be cut off by a large‑distance scale
L
unrelated to the short‑range divergence of the Rutherford cross section.

Within the same straight-line approximation, the functional integral over
\boldsymbol{\xi}
in (
16
) yields a factor
\exp[rA(s/k)]
with

A(s/k)=-\frac{G}{k^{2}}s^{2}.

(19)

The inverse length scale
\Gamma(k)=1/\ell(k)
is extracted from the
r\to\infty
asymptotics of
G_{k}(r)
via a saddle-point evaluation of the
s
-integral:

\Gamma(k)=-\operatorname{Re}\Phi(s_{c}),\qquad\Phi(s)=\frac{ik}{2}\Bigl(s+\frac{1}{s}\Bigr)+A(s/k).

In the weak-disorder (high-energy) limit
k\gg\kappa
, the saddle point lies at
s_{c}=1
, leading to

\Gamma(k)=\frac{G}{k^{2}}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad\ell(k)=\frac{k^{2}}{G}=\frac{k^{2}}{m^{2}k_{B}Tq_{0}^{2}\,\ln(\kappa L)}.

(20)

In the Gaussian (CGS) system we have:

\ell(k)=\frac{\hbar^{4}k^{2}}{m^{2}k_{B}T\,q_{0}^{2}\,\ln(\kappa L)}.

(21)

VI
Low-energy limit: strong localization

When
k\ll\kappa
, the term
k^{2}
in
\Phi(s)
becomes negligible.
It is convenient to introduce
v=s/k
, so that

\Phi\approx\frac{i}{2v}+A(v),\qquad A(v)=-Gv^{2}.

(22)

The saddle-point equation
\partial\Phi/\partial v=0
reads

-\frac{i}{2v^{2}}-2Gv=0\quad\Longrightarrow\quad v^{3}=-\frac{i}{4G}.

Solving this cubic equation, one finds the relevant saddle point
v_{c}
(with negative real part of
\Phi
) to be

v_{c}=(4G)^{-1/3}e^{-i\pi/6}=\frac{\sqrt{3}-i}{2}(4G)^{-1/3}.

Substituting
v_{c}
into (
22
) gives

\Phi(v_{c})=-\frac{3}{2}\,4^{-2/3}G^{1/3},

from which the inverse length scale follows:

\Gamma(k)=-\operatorname{Re}\Phi(v_{c})=\frac{3}{2}\,4^{-2/3}G^{1/3}=\frac{3}{4\sqrt[3]{2}}\,G^{1/3}.

(23)

Consequently, the length scale in the low-energy limit is

\ell(k)=\frac{1}{\Gamma(k)}=\frac{4\sqrt[3]{2}}{3}\,G^{-1/3}=\frac{4\sqrt[3]{2}}{3}\Bigl[m^{2}k_{B}Tq_{0}^{2}\,\ln(\kappa L)\Bigr]^{-1/3}.

(24)

In the Gaussian system of units we have:

\ell=\frac{4\sqrt[3]{2}}{3}\left(\frac{\hbar^{4}}{m^{2}k_{B}T\,q_{0}^{2}\,\ln(\kappa L)}\right)^{\!1/3}.

(25)

This result demonstrates that the length scale becomes independent of the particle momentum
k
and is controlled entirely by the disorder strength
G
.
The latter is consistent with the results obtained by Efimov in general
Efimov 2015
.

It is important to note that for
k\ll\kappa
the eikonal approximation is not strictly controlled, as the de Broglie wavelength of the particle exceeds the correlation length of the random potential.
Nevertheless, the scaling
\ell\sim G^{-1/3}
obtained in Eq. (
24
) is robust.
Dimensional analysis alone forces any characteristic length to be proportional to
G^{-1/3}
, because
G
is the only remaining dimensional parameter in the limit
k\to 0
.
The numerical prefactor
\frac{4\sqrt[3]{2}}{3}
derived from the saddle-point calculation may be modified by quantum fluctuations of the paths, but the functional dependence
\ell\propto[m^{2}k_{B}Tq_{0}^{2}\ln(\kappa L)]^{-1/3}
is expected to be correct and provides a reliable estimate of the length scale in the strong-disorder regime.

VII
Results and Discussion

The expressions obtained for the length scale
\ell(k)
in a classical one-component plasma offer a unifying perspective on quantum transport in a fluctuating Coulomb environment.
At high energies,
k\gg\kappa
, the particle experiences weak localization, and
\ell(k)
grows quadratically with momentum, as
\ell(k)=k^{2}/[m^{2}k_{B}Tq_{0}^{2}\ln(\kappa L)]
.
This scaling coincides with the behaviour expected from elementary transport theory once the inverse of this length scale is identified with the inverse transport mean free path.
Comparison with the Born approximation for scattering on a screened Coulomb potential shows that the same Coulomb logarithm
\ln(\kappa L)
emerges naturally.
The logarithm originates from the unscreened
1/r
tail of the potential correlator, which causes a logarithmic divergence of the integrated disorder strength and must be regularized by a large-distance cutoff
L
, such as the mean free path or the system size.

It is instructive to compare our results with the general framework for one-dimensional disordered systems developed by Izrailev, Krokhin, and Makarov
Izrailev, Krokhin, and Makarov 2012
.
In their work, the inverse localization length for a continuous random potential is expressed through the power spectrum
\mathcal{K}(k)
of the potential at twice the wave number
k

(
Izrailev, Krokhin, and Makarov 2012
, Eq. (2.84))
.
Our expression (
20
) has a similar structure, but with the crucial difference that the power spectrum in our case is determined self-consistently by the Coulomb interactions within the plasma and exhibits a
1/k^{2}
singularity at small wave numbers.
This singularity is regulated by the Debye screening, which introduces the infrared cutoff
L
.
As a result, instead of the sharp mobility edges that can be engineered by specific long-range correlations in the models of Ref.
Izrailev, Krokhin, and Makarov 2012
, we obtain a smooth, logarithmically weak energy dependence of the length scale.
This reflects the fact that the long-range character of the Coulomb potential, even when screened, leaves an indelible trace on the localization properties.

At low energies,
k\ll\kappa
, the length scale becomes independent of momentum and saturates at a value
\ell=\frac{4\sqrt[3]{2}}{3}[m^{2}k_{B}Tq_{0}^{2}\ln(\kappa L)]^{-1/3}
.
Here the eikonal approximation yields a strong-disorder regime in which
\ell
is determined solely by the disorder strength
G
and consequently by the temperature and the cutoff scale.
The cubic-root dependence weakens the logarithmic sensitivity to
L
, but does not eliminate it entirely; the Coulomb logarithm remains a fingerprint of the long-range character of the underlying ionic fluctuations.

A noteworthy feature is the temperature dependence: at fixed particle momentum,
\ell
decreases with increasing temperature, roughly as
1/\left(T\ln(\text{const}/\sqrt{T})\right)
in the high-energy limit and as
T^{-1/3}(\ln T)^{-1/3}
in the low-energy limit.
This behaviour reflects that thermal ion fluctuations amplify the amplitude of the fluctuating potential, thereby enhancing the effective disorder seen by the quantum particle.

The necessity of introducing the infrared cutoff
L
highlights a fundamental property of three-dimensional Coulomb systems: without such a regularization the fluctuations of the potential are too long-ranged to allow a finite length scale for the exponential decay.
If the plasma is treated quantum-mechanically, the
1/r
tail of the potential correlator survives, but the theory itself provides natural physical mechanisms for regularizing the large-distance cutoff
L
.
Quantum transport in the plasma introduces intrinsic length scales – the electron mean free path or the phase-coherence length – any of which can serve as the effective infrared cutoff
L_{\mathrm{eff}}
.
In the low‑energy (strong‑disorder) regime, where the electron de Broglie wavelength is large, the ionic screening cloud is fully developed and the natural cutoff is of the order of the Debye length
\lambda_{D}=\kappa^{-1}
; consequently the Coulomb logarithm
\ln(\kappa L_{\mathrm{eff}})
becomes a constant of order unity.
For high‑energy electrons, on the other hand, the finite flight time limits the effective interaction range, and the relevant length scale is set by the distance the electron travels during one ion plasma period,
L_{\mathrm{eff}}\sim v/\omega_{pi}
, where
\omega_{pi}
is the ion plasma frequency.
In this case the logarithm grows with electron velocity, reflecting the incompletely screened
1/r
tail.
Thus, even within the static description, the phenomenological parameter
L
can be replaced by a physically motivated scale that depends on the electron kinematics and plasma properties.
A fully self-consistent determination of
L_{\mathrm{eff}}
requires the inclusion of dynamic screening and remains an important goal for future work.
Our results therefore lay the groundwork for such studies and for a quantitative description of localization phenomena in ionic liquids, dense astrophysical plasmas, and other Coulomb-disordered systems.

It is important to bear in mind that a fundamental limitation of the present theory is that Efimov’s path‑integral formalism is inherently designed for static random potentials.
In a real plasma, however, the ionic density fluctuations that generate the random potential are intrinsically dynamic, exhibiting collective modes at the ion plasma frequency and thermal motion with characteristic velocity
v_{i}
.
For a fast test particle (
v\gg v_{i}
) the quasi‑static approximation may be justified, but for slow particles or when dynamic screening effects become important, a full time‑dependent treatment is required.
Extending Efimov’s framework to include dynamic fluctuations is a formidable theoretical challenge: one would need to handle a time‑dependent random field in the path integral and perform the disorder average with a frequency‑dependent kernel, which goes beyond the static Gaussian average used here. This problem is addressed in Part II
Budkov 2026a
, where the static approximation is relaxed and a full dynamic theory of the disorder‑induced decay is developed for a classical one‑component plasma.
A possible route is to couple the particle motion to the fluctuating electrostatic field via the fluctuation–dissipation theorem, but a rigorous formulation of such an approach within the functional‑integral representation of the Green’s function remains an open problem.
Despite this limitation, the static results obtained in this work provide a crucial benchmark and an important starting point for future theories that will incorporate dynamic screening, non‑Gaussian corrections, and self‑consistent determinations of the infrared cutoff.

It is also worth commenting on the role of the background electrons that are not explicitly included in the present model.
In a real plasma, electron-density fluctuations provide an additional source of disorder with a typical frequency scale on the order of the electron plasma frequency
\omega_{pe}
.
For a slow test particle these fluctuations act as a rapidly varying dynamical noise that, instead of enhancing localization, tends to destroy the phase coherence indispensable for quantum interference.
The competition between the quasi-static ionic disorder and the phase-breaking electron collisions can be characterized by the phase-coherence length
L_{\phi}
.
In a dilute plasma the electron-electron collision frequency
\nu_{ee}
is low, so that
L_{\phi}\sim v/\nu_{ee}
can substantially exceed the length scale
\ell
obtained from the ionic background.
In this regime the static picture advocated here is expected to give a quantitatively reliable description.
In contrast, in dense plasmas
L_{\phi}
may become comparable to or even shorter than
\ell
, leading to a suppression of weak and strong localization.
The present results are therefore most directly applicable to dilute, high-temperature plasmas, such as the solar corona, and to suprathermal particles whose velocity is large compared with the typical ion acoustic speed. VIII
Conclusion

We have presented a first-principles theory of disorder-induced localization for a quantum particle in a classical one-component plasma within the static-fluctuation approximation.
By combining the RPA description of ionic fluctuations with the path-integral formalism for the averaged Green’s function, we have derived analytic expressions for the length scale that characterizes the exponential decay of the disorder-averaged Green’s function in the static Gaussian disorder.
These expressions explicitly contain the Coulomb logarithm, linking quantum localization to classical plasma kinetic theory.
The present results provide a quantitative starting point for studying transport anomalies in Coulombic disordered systems, such as doped semiconductors, ionic liquids, and dense astrophysical plasmas, and they open the way to future studies incorporating dynamic screening and self-consistent determinations of the infrared cutoff.
