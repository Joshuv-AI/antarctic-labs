[99mTc]Tc-FAPI-04 SPECT/CT for initial staging of gastrointestinal malignancies: a prospective comparison with contrast-enhanced CT
Authors: Yuqing Du, Bo Li, Xinyu Wu, Xiali Li, Yafei Wang, Yongju Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis single-center prospective pilot study evaluated [99mTc]Tc-FAPI-04 SPECT/CT for initial staging in patients with newly diagnosed gastrointestinal (GI) malignancies and compared it directly with contrast-enhanced CT (ceCT) to assess its potential complementary staging value. Thirteen patients with suspected or histopathologically confirmed GI malignancies were prospectively enrolled between March and June 2025. All patients underwent both [99mTc]Tc-FAPI-04 SPECT/CT and ceCT examinations. Using histopathology or composite clinical/imaging follow-up as the reference standard, the detection capabilities of both modalities for primary tumors, lymph node metastases, and distant metastases were compared, and the impact of FAPI imaging on clinical treatment decisions was recorded. Both modalities achieved 100% detection rates for primary tumors. For metastatic lesions, [99mTc]Tc-FAPI-04 SPECT/CT showed higher sensitivity than ceCT at the lesion level (45.9% vs. 36.5%, P = 0.039). SPECT/CT detected 10 lesions missed by ceCT, predominantly in the peritoneum and mesentery (including regional lymph nodes), while ceCT identified 2 lesions missed by FAPI imaging (1 hepatic metastasis and 1 regional lymph node). In the pre-specified subgroup analysis of lymph node and tumor deposit metastases, FAPI SPECT/CT showed higher detection sensitivity than with ceCT (50.0% vs. 37.9%, P = 0.021). FAPI imaging identified occult peritoneal metastases missed by ceCT in one illustrative case, leading to upstaging and subsequent modification of the treatment strategy. Preliminary results indicate that [99mTc]Tc-FAPI-04 SPECT/CT may provide complementary information for the initial staging of GI malignancies, particularly for occult peritoneal disease that may be difficult to detect on ceCT. Introduction

According to GLOBOCAN 2022, approximately 4.91 million new cases of digestive system cancers were diagnosed worldwide, accounting for 24.6% of all incident cancers1. Asia contributes to 60.5% of the global burden of new gastrointestinal (GI) cancer cases. The situation in China is particularly prominent: it accounts for over one-third of global gastric cancer cases, and the incidence of colorectal cancer is rapidly increasing2. Current international guidelines, including those of the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), recommend contrast-enhanced CT (ceCT) as the standard first-line modality for preoperative staging3,4. However, ceCT has well-recognized limitations in detecting lymph node metastases and peritoneal dissemination5,6. For patients with locally advanced gastric cancer in whom ceCT cannot definitively exclude peritoneal metastasis, guidelines recommend diagnostic laparoscopy—an invasive procedure that carries additional procedural risk and cost. Accurate initial staging is a prerequisite for optimal treatment planning, and there remains an unmet need for more sensitive staging modalities.

Fibroblast activation protein (FAP) is a serine protease with dual dipeptidyl peptidase and endopeptidase activities7. It is virtually absent in healthy human tissues but is overexpressed on cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) in 90% of malignant epithelial tumors8,9. Recent studies have demonstrated the excellent performance of [68Ga]Ga- or [18F]F-labeled FAPI PET/CT in the staging of various GI tumors. Koerber et al. reported that [68Ga]Ga-FAPI PET/CT altered the oncologic or radiation oncology management in 81% of 22 patients with lower GI tract tumors, facilitating more precise TNM staging10. FAPI imaging has demonstrated superiority over ceCT for the detection of peritoneal and hepatic metastases, and in some cases has outperformed [18F]F-FDG PET/CT11–13.

Nevertheless, the high cost and limited availability of PET/CT restrict its widespread application in regions with constrained healthcare resources. In contrast, SPECT/CT offers the advantages of lower cost and higher institutional availability, and [99mTc]Tc-labeled FAPI has the potential for broader clinical application. Preliminary studies have explored the diagnostic value of [99mTc]Tc-FAPI in digestive system tumors14,15, but direct comparative data with ceCT for the initial staging of GI malignancies remain limited. Therefore, this study prospectively evaluated the clinical value of [99mTc]Tc-FAPI-04 SPECT/CT for staging of patients with newly diagnosed GI malignancies and compared it directly with ceCT to investigate its incremental clinical value.

Materials and methods

Patients

This prospective study was conducted at Henan Provincial People’s Hospital between March and June 2025 and enrolled patients with newly diagnosed GI malignancies. The enrollment flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. Inclusion criteria were: (1) clinical suspicion or histopathological confirmation of a GI malignancy; (2) requirement for ceCT or completion of ceCT within the preceding week; and (3) provision of voluntary written informed consent. Exclusion criteria were: (1) a final pathological diagnosis of a non-GI primary tumor; (2) receipt of any antineoplastic therapy (including radiotherapy, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, or immunotherapy) within one month prior to imaging; (3) pregnancy, lactation, or planned pregnancy in the near future; and (4) inability to complete the nuclear medicine scan due to claustrophobia, severe psychiatric illness, or inability to remain supine for more than 30 min. The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Henan Provincial People’s Hospital (approval no. 2024-91) and registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (registration no. ChiCTR2400087183; first posted 22/07/2024; https://www.chictr.org.cn/showproj.html?proj=236253). All participants provided written informed consent. The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles set forth in the Declaration of Helsinki and all applicable clinical research ethics guidelines.

Flowchart of patient enrollment.

[99mTc]Tc-FAPI-04 scintigraphy and review

The radiopharmaceutical [99mTc]Tc-FAPI-04 was prepared according to a previously described protocol16. The radiochemical purity, as determined by radio-thin-layer chromatography or high-performance liquid chromatography, was found to be not less than 95%. Planar whole-body (WB) images and SPECT/CT data acquisition were performed 60–90 min after the intravenous injection of 555–740 MBq (15–20 mCi) of [99mTc]Tc-FAPI-04, using a Symbia T16 SPECT/CT scanner (Siemens Symbia Intevo, Erlangen, Germany) that combines a dual-headed gamma camera with a 16-slice CT system. WB planar scintigraphy (anterior and posterior) was acquired using a low-energy high-resolution (LEHR) collimator with a matrix of 256 × 1024 and a scan speed of 10 cm/min. Imaging parameters for SPECT were: 6 degrees angular resolution and 30 s per step with a 256 × 256 matrix. The low-dose CT scan parameters were: 130 kV and 25 reference mAs modulation. CT data were reconstructed at a 5-mm slice thickness using B31s medium smooth kernels (Siemens Healthineers). SPECT components were calibrated monthly using a 57Co source (Calibrated Sensitivity Source) for quantitative imaging. SPECT data were reconstructed using Flash3D with scatter and attenuation corrections, with 4 iterations and 8 subsets, followed by Gaussian post-processing filtering. The quantitative uptake maps were reconstructed using xSPECT Quant (Siemens, Germany), which corrected attenuation and scatter and provided standardized calibrations for absolute quantification. Images were interpreted independently by two senior nuclear medicine physicians and one junior nuclear medicine physician, all blinded to clinical information; discrepancies were resolved by consensus. After exclusion of physiological uptake, trauma, infection, or inflammation, abnormally elevated FAPI uptake was defined as positive. The tumor-to-background ratio (TBR) was defined as the lesion SUVmax divided by the SUVmax of a background region of interest placed in the erector spinae muscle at the level of the liver.

ceCT imaging and review

All ceCT images were reviewed independently by two senior radiologists blinded to clinical information and FAPI imaging results; discrepancies were resolved by consensus. The diagnostic criteria for metastatic lymph nodes were defined as: round or oval shape (long-to-short axis ratio < 2), short-axis diameter > 6 mm, or an average CT value ≥ 80 HU in the venous phase17,18. The presence of other distant metastases, such as in the liver or peritoneum, was comprehensively evaluated based on typical clinical and radiologic features.

Reference standard and follow‑up

Histopathological examination of biopsy or surgical resection specimens served as the reference standard; pathological confirmation of the primary tumor was required for all enrolled patients. For suspected metastatic lesions for which biopsy was not available, follow-up imaging data obtained after SPECT/CT were required. Malignancy was defined by: (a) typical malignant features confirmed by multimodal imaging; (b) significant lesion progression on follow-up imaging (e.g., a marked increase in lesion size); or (c) significant tumor response following antineoplastic therapy. All suspicious lesions were followed for 3 to 6 months.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since tracer uptake parameters (SUVmax and TBR) did not follow a normal distribution, they were expressed as median and interquartile range (IQR); comparisons between independent groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. The correlation between FAPI uptake parameters and tumor differentiation grade was assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (ρ). Differences in tracer uptake across primary tumor sites were evaluated using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. The diagnostic sensitivities of [99mTc]Tc-FAPI-04 SPECT/CT and ceCT at both the patient and lesion levels were compared using the exact McNemar’s test. All P values are two-sided; P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Comparisons of diagnostic performance between [99mTc]Tc-FAPI-04 SPECT/CT and ceCT at the lesion and patient levels, together with the lymph node and tumor deposit metastasis subgroup analysis, were pre-specified. Semi-quantitative uptake analyses and hepatic metastasis subgroup analysis were exploratory. Case-level management impact was assessed descriptively.

Results

Patient characteristics and primary tumor evaluation

A total of 13 patients (10 males and 3 females) were included in this study, with a median age of 59 years (IQR 56–66 years). Ten patients underwent surgical treatment, and 3 received systemic chemotherapy or targeted therapy. Final diagnoses included gastric cancer (n = 6), colon cancer (n = 4), rectal cancer (n = 2), and esophagogastric junction cancer (n = 1) (Table 1).

Both FAPI SPECT/CT and ceCT achieved a 100% detection rate for primary tumors. Semi-quantitative analysis revealed that the median SUVmax and TBR for primary tumors were 7.02 (IQR 4.33–9.09) and 4.27 (IQR 3.33–5.98), respectively. There was no significant correlation between FAPI uptake parameters and tumor differentiation grade (Spearman ρ = 0.07, P = 0.82), nor were there significant differences among various primary tumor sites (Kruskal–Wallis test, P = 0.475). One patient with signet-ring cell carcinoma (SRCC) (Case 12) exhibited an exceptionally high uptake in the primary tumor (SUVmax = 24.23), far exceeding the median level of adenocarcinoma patients.

Diagnostic performance and metastatic distribution

Based on the reference standard, 10 out of the 13 patients were confirmed to have metastases. Patient-level analysis revealed one false positive for both [99mTc]Tc-FAPI-04 SPECT/CT and ceCT. The diagnostic sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and accuracy (Ac) for FAPI SPECT/CT were 90.0% (9/10), 66.7% (2/3), 90.0% (9/10), 66.7% (2/3), and 84.6% (11/13), respectively, which were higher than or equal to those of ceCT at 70.0% (7/10), 66.7% (2/3), 87.5% (7/8), 40.0% (2/5), and 69.2% (9/13) (Table 2). The difference in sensitivity between the two modalities at the patient level did not reach statistical significance (exact McNemar’s test, P = 0.500).

Among the 10 patients with metastatic disease, a total of 85 reference-standard-confirmed metastatic lesions were identified. At the lesion level, FAPI SPECT/CT detected 39 lesions with a sensitivity of 45.9% and a PPV of 90.7%, whereas ceCT detected 31 lesions with a sensitivity of 36.5% and a PPV of 83.8%; the difference in sensitivity was statistically significant (P = 0.039). SPECT/CT detected 10 lesions missed by ceCT, predominantly located in the peritoneum and mesentery (including regional lymph nodes) (Fig. 2), and led to upstaging of one patient (Case 8) from M0/M1a to M1c, prompting the surgeon to extend the resection margins. Conversely, ceCT identified only 2 additional lesions missed by SPECT/CT (1 hepatic metastasis and 1 regional lymph node).

In the pre-specified stratified analysis, the overall lesion-level difference was predominantly attributable to the lymph node and tumor deposit metastasis subgroup (n = 66): SPECT/CT detected 33 metastatic lesions (sensitivity 50.0%) (Fig. 3) versus 25 for ceCT (sensitivity 37.9%), a difference that was statistically significant (P = 0.021). SPECT/CT identified 9 additional lesions missed by ceCT, while ceCT detected only 1 additional lesion. Lesions exclusively detected by FAPI imaging were predominantly located in the mesentery; although small in size on CT, all demonstrated substantial tracer uptake.

Semi-quantitative analysis showed that primary tumor uptake parameters were significantly higher than those of metastatic lesions (median SUVmax: 7.02 vs. 2.61, P < 0.001; median TBR: 4.27 vs. 2.52, P < 0.001; Fig. 4). Considerable heterogeneity in FAPI uptake was observed among metastatic lesions, particularly in hepatic metastases. Although the median TBR of hepatic metastases was 3.45, there was considerable variability (range: 2.62–4.00). For example, the 14 hepatic metastases in Case 1 showed only mildly abnormal uptake (SUVmax 1.70–2.60), whereas the 2 oligometastatic hepatic lesions in Case 5 (Fig. 5) demonstrated intense uptake (SUVmax 4.72–5.45).

Discussion

Accurate tumor staging is essential for the formulation of optimal treatment strategies. ceCT, as the current standard-of-care modality, has recognized limitations in detecting occult metastases, while [68Ga]Ga- or [18F]F-labeled FAPI PET/CT, which has the potential to address this limitation, is hampered by its high cost and limited availability. From a radiopharmaceutical perspective, [99mTc]Tc-labeled FAPI tracers have practical advantages, including lower cost, generator-based availability, potential kit-based preparation, and compatibility with widely available SPECT/CT systems. Previous studies have reported high radiochemical purity and acceptable biodistribution of [99mTc]Tc-labeled FAPI compounds, supporting their clinical translation as accessible FAPI-based imaging agents15,19. To explore an alternative that combines high diagnostic performance with cost-effectiveness, this study prospectively evaluated the diagnostic performance of [99mTc]Tc-FAPI-04 SPECT/CT.

Both FAPI imaging and ceCT achieved a 100% detection rate for primary tumors, consistent with previous studies on [99mTc]Tc-FAPI14,16,20. At the patient level, FAPI SPECT/CT demonstrated higher sensitivity than ceCT (90.0% vs. 70.0%, P = 0.500). Among the 85 reference-standard-confirmed metastatic lesions, FAPI SPECT/CT showed significantly higher sensitivity than ceCT (45.9% vs. 36.5%, P = 0.039). Although FAPI SPECT/CT demonstrated statistically higher lesion-level sensitivity than ceCT, the absolute sensitivity of both modalities was modest, indicating that the clinical relevance of this difference should be interpreted cautiously. In this pilot cohort, FAPI imaging provided additional information in selected cases, including one illustrative case in which the findings were relevant to clinical management. These findings support the possibility that FAPI SPECT/CT may serve as a supplementary tool to ceCT when occult metastatic disease is suspected, particularly for peritoneal involvement and small metastatic lesions, and as a practical FAPI-based option when PET/CT is unavailable.

In the lymph node and tumor deposit subgroup analysis, FAPI SPECT/CT demonstrated statistically significantly higher detection sensitivity than ceCT (50.0% vs. 37.9%, P = 0.021). This is primarily attributable to the higher TBR in the peritoneal and mesenteric regions, which may partly compensate for the lower spatial resolution of SPECT/CT and facilitate detection of early lesions before morphological changes become apparent on CT. This finding is consistent with the results of Sun et al.15, who similarly reported that [99mTc]Tc-FAPI-04 SPECT/CT outperformed ceCT in detecting lymph node metastases in postoperative patients with colorectal cancer (90.0% vs. 65.0%, P = 0.042). However, it must be acknowledged that the sensitivity of SPECT/CT in this cohort (50.0%) was lower than the level reported by Zhang et al. (71.0%)20, but slightly higher than the result reported by Zhou et al.14 for [99mTc]Tc-DP-FAPI (32.8%). This phenomenon is not unique to SPECT/CT: even [68Ga]Ga-FAPI PET/CT, which has a higher spatial resolution (~ 4 to 6 mm), faces the same challenge in detecting small lymph nodes. A meta-analysis by Ruan et al.21 reported a pooled sensitivity of only 64.0% for PET/CT in detecting lymph node metastases; data reported by Lin et al.22 were even lower (19.2%), with 91% of missed lesions measuring less than 5 mm in diameter. The intrinsic spatial resolution of SPECT/CT (~ 10 mm) is inferior to that of PET/CT, rendering sub-centimeter micrometastases susceptible to partial volume effects (PVE). Detection of regional lymph nodes adjacent to the primary tumor is also frequently compromised by spillover from high primary tumor radioactivity, contributing to the modest lesion-level sensitivity observed in this cohort.

ceCT has limited sensitivity for detecting peritoneal metastases from GI malignancies23. In this study, FAPI imaging identified one occult peritoneal metastatic lesion missed by ceCT (Case 8), thereby informing the clinical management plan in this illustrative case. Compared with lymph node metastases, peritoneal metastases are generally associated with more active stromal reactions24, with relatively abundant FAP expression. Previous studies have similarly confirmed that FAPI uptake in peritoneal metastases is generally higher than in lymph node metastases10. The high TBR achieved by FAPI imaging therefore enables detection of small peritoneal metastases before significant anatomical changes have occurred25,26. Zhou et al.27 found that FAPI imaging outperformed ceCT in evaluating peritoneal metastases in sensitivity (97.06% vs. 64.71%), specificity (95.65% vs. 78.26%), and AUC (0.964 vs. 0.715, P < 0.001), altering treatment plans in 29.82% of patients. Hu et al.13 indicated that FAPI imaging has the potential to reduce the need for invasive laparoscopic staging by 84%. Although the present study had a limited sample size, Case 8 still demonstrates the practical value of [99mTc]Tc-FAPI-04 SPECT/CT in identifying occult peritoneal metastases.

Most studies suggest that FAPI imaging outperforms ceCT and even [18F]F-FDG PET/CT for the detection of hepatic metastases, enabling detection of lesions smaller than 1 cm in diameter11,16. Although [99mTc]Tc-FAPI-04 SPECT/CT successfully identified the 2 hepatic metastases in Case 5 in this study, the multiple hepatic metastases in Case 1 were missed by both modalities. The ceCT miss in Case 1 was attributable to the absence of the typical rapid wash-in and wash-out enhancement pattern in most lesions, making them difficult to differentiate from benign lesions. The SPECT/CT miss may indicate that these lesions had low CAF recruitment density or insufficient hypoxia, resulting in limited FAP expression28,29. By contrast, the successful detection of Case 5 lesions may be related to their more robust internal stromal reaction. Similar inconsistencies exist in the literature (Table 3). Jia et al.16 reported that the sensitivity of [99mTc]Tc-HFAPi for hepatic metastases (88.2%) was lower than that of ceCT (100%), whereas the results of Sun et al.15 and Zhang et al.20 were the opposite, with SPECT/CT hepatic metastasis sensitivities of 94.7% (ceCT 78.9%) and 85% (FDG-PET 50%), respectively; Fu et al.12 reported a patient-level hepatic metastasis sensitivity of 100% for [68Ga]Ga-FAPI PET/CT, also superior to ceCT (80%). Therefore, when hepatic metastases have atypical vascular supply and low stromal biological activity, a single imaging modality may miss them, and comprehensive assessment incorporating clinical follow-up is required.

The heterogeneous composition of primary GI tumor types may also have influenced tracer uptake and detection rates, as FAP expression and stromal density can vary among tumor subtypes. In our exploratory analysis, however, SUVmax and TBR of primary tumors did not differ significantly by primary site. The imaging heterogeneity observed among hepatic metastases in this study reflects tumor heterogeneity in FAP expression. Such stromal variability arising from the tumor microenvironment similarly influenced lesion detection at other anatomical sites. Regarding pulmonary metastases, some studies have reported superior sensitivity of FAPI imaging over [18F]F-FDG30; however, the pulmonary metastases of Case 1 in this study were FAPI-negative, consistent with observations reported by Xiong et al.31, suggesting that pulmonary metastases may exhibit significant stromal heterogeneity resulting in insufficient FAP target expression. Regional lymph node assessment faces analogous challenges: from a tumor biology perspective, CAF recruitment and activation in some early micrometastatic lymph nodes may be insufficient to generate detectable tracer accumulation, thereby causing false-negative results. In relation to [18F]F-FDG PET/CT, which remains an established molecular imaging modality for GI malignancies, previous comparative studies have reported higher uptake or detection rates with FAPI imaging in selected settings, particularly for peritoneal metastases and tumors with relatively low FDG avidity20,30. One patient with SRCC (Case 12) in this cohort exhibited a primary tumor SUVmax as high as 24.23. Previous studies have shown that this subtype is prone to false-negative results on [18F]F-FDG PET/CT due to low GLUT-1 expression levels32. This finding is consistent with the unique diagnostic advantage of FAPI imaging for such tumors, as reported by Chen et al.33.

Furthermore, since FAP expression is not tumor-specific34, chronic inflammation and tissue remodeling can similarly activate fibroblasts and lead to elevated non-specific tracer uptake. In this cohort, FAPI-avid non-malignant findings included reactive lymph node hyperplasia in the draining region, chronic appendicitis, and Hashimoto’s thyroiditis (Cases 5 and 6). Illustratively, in Case 11, FAPI imaging demonstrated mildly abnormal tracer concentration in a small lymph node adjacent to the primary tumor (SUVmax = 3.59), while concurrent ceCT identified two regional lymph nodes meeting the predefined metastasis criteria (one oval with short-axis diameter > 6 mm; one round with short-axis diameter > 6 mm and venous phase CT value reaching 86 HU); both were ultimately confirmed as reactive hyperplasia on histopathology. Additionally, high background uptake in the uterus and ovaries of premenopausal women may obscure true local signals (e.g., Case 4), requiring careful differentiation during imaging assessment35.

This study has several limitations. First, this was a single-center prospective pilot study with a relatively small sample size and limited statistical power; further validation in larger populations is required. In addition, because multiple lesions could arise from the same patient, lesion-level analyses may be influenced by within-patient clustering and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. Second, the enrolled cohort comprised a heterogeneous mix of tumor types, and the limited number of patients per tumor type precluded in-depth subgroup analysis. Third, not all metastatic lesions were histopathologically confirmed due to ethical and practical constraints, and a composite reference standard including 3- to 6-month imaging follow-up was used for some lesions. Follow-up after systemic chemotherapy demonstrated typical treatment responses: hepatic, pulmonary, and lymph node metastases in Cases 1 and 10 showed significant size reduction, and the degree of enhancement of the suspicious hepatic lesion in Case 5 decreased, supporting their classification as malignant. Nevertheless, this approach may introduce potential bias and influence diagnostic performance estimates, particularly for small lesions, and the minimum 3-month follow-up may be relatively short for some GI malignancies. Fourth, SUVmax and TBR derived from quantitative SPECT are less standardized than PET metrics and may be affected by technical and ROI-related factors; therefore, these semi-quantitative results should be interpreted cautiously. In addition, FAP immunohistochemistry was not performed, precluding direct correlation between quantitative uptake values and target expression levels. Fifth, only a conventional single-phase SPECT/CT acquisition protocol was employed; for some small or equivocal lesions, future studies may consider introducing dual-phase delayed imaging to further improve lesion discrimination36.

Conclusion

In this single-center prospective pilot study, [99mTc]Tc-FAPI-04 SPECT/CT showed preliminary diagnostic performance for initial staging of GI malignancies and provided additional information in selected cases, particularly for occult peritoneal metastases that are difficult to identify on ceCT. Despite the need for vigilance regarding non-specific tracer uptake and the limitations of detecting very small lesions, the low cost, high availability and no fasting requirement of this modality may support its use as a complementary tool to ceCT and as a practical FAPI-based imaging option when PET/CT is not readily available. Larger prospective studies are warranted to validate these preliminary findings and to clarify the clinical role of this approach.
