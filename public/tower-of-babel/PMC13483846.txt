Taming the contaminants: a quality improvement initiative to minimize blood culture contamination and enhance patient care in a resource-limited setting
Authors: Maria Khan, Syed Naveed Ullah Shah, Saba Khan, Asma Qudrat, Attiya Hameed Khan, Haider Zaman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBlood culture contamination (BCC) in hospitals negatively impacts patient outcomes. Theobjective of this study is to reduce the mean BCC rate at the Peshawar Institute of Cardiology-MTI from a baseline to below the international benchmark of 3%. This will be achieved through a prospective interventional quality improvement project utilizing the Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycle, specifically focusing on “hardened” aseptic protocols, “Master Trainer” mentorship, and the institutionalization of BCC as a Key Performance Indicator (KPI).MethodsThis interventional study at our tertiary care setup in Peshawar, Pakistan, from December 2024 to December 2025, included all blood cultures. The study period was divided into a 6-month pre-intervention baseline and a 7-month intervention period with monthly BCC monitoring. Interventions included regular training, didactic sessions, and re-competencies. Laboratory personnel monitored BCC rates, reported findings, and provided feedback. BCC rate calculation followed pre-set criteria specific to our institution.ResultsAcross all hospital departments, we received 646 blood cultures, with 285 collected during the pre-intervention phase and 361 during the intervention. The mean BCC rate significantly decreased from 12.9% in the pre-intervention period to 6.09% by the end of the intervention. Notably, the majority of blood cultures originated from inpatient units (83.06%), and 62.7% of all contaminations were reported in male patients.ConclusionA simple intervention effectively lowered BCC in our setting, particularly addressing inpatient factors. However, without observational audits, pinpointing specific causes remains challenging. Future research should focus on targeted strategies for sustained improvements.

Introduction

Blood culture, a cornerstone of clinical microbiology, remains the gold standard for diagnosing bacteremia and guiding antimicrobial therapy, particularly in critical conditions like sepsis and infective endocarditis [1, 2]. International guidelines emphasize proper blood collection techniques, typically involving multiple sets (aerobic and anaerobic bottles) obtained via peripheral venipuncture before initiating antibiotics [3, 4]. These guidelines aim to optimize pathogen detection and minimize contamination [5].

However, blood culture contamination (BCC), the introduction of non-pathogenic organisms (often skin flora like coagulase-negative staphylococci and Corynebacterium spp.) into the sample, persists as a significant challenge [1]. Alarmingly, false positives due to contamination can reach up to 50%, leading to increased hospital costs, compromised laboratory efficiency, and adverse patient outcomes [6]. The consequences extend beyond resource utilization, potentially triggering unnecessary diagnostic procedures, imaging, medications, prolonged hospital stays, and even inappropriate hospital admissions. This, in turn, fuels antibiotic overuse, contributing to the rise of multidrug-resistant infections and ultimately undermining the reliability of blood culture results for informed treatment decisions [7]. Studies have shown that contaminated blood cultures can extend a patient’s hospital stay by an average of two days [8].

Recommendations from the American Society for Microbiology (ASM) and the Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) set a benchmark of no more than a 3% overall BCC rate [9]. While complete elimination may be unattainable, maintaining rates within this threshold is crucial. Unfortunately, many healthcare facilities, especially high-volume centers, frequently report rates exceeding this target. This is often attributed to inconsistent collection practices, inadequate staff training, and the lack of routine procedural audits to ensure adherence to aseptic techniques [3]. In the modern era of blood culturing, contamination predominantly occurs during the collection process, with the patient’s skin or the hub/cannula of indwelling catheters being the primary sources (if catheters are used for sampling). Monitoring and reporting BCC rates are recognized as essential laboratory quality practices [10]. Reducing BCC is a paramount quality improvement goal for hospitals striving to enhance clinical outcomes and curtail avoidable healthcare expenditures. Contamination not only compromises diagnostic accuracy but also represents a substantial and preventable financial burden. Estimates suggest that a single contaminated blood culture can cost between $1,000 and $8,700, primarily due to the cascade of unnecessary treatments and prolonged care [11]. Recognizing this significant impact, our study was designed to evaluate the cost and efficiency implications of blood culture contamination and to implement and assess the effectiveness of strategies aimed at reducing its occurrence within our clinical setting.

Methodology

The study was conducted at Peshawar Institute of Cardiology-MTI, encompassing the outpatient department, emergency department, clinical units, and intensive care unit (ICU). This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board, (Protocol No. IRC/25/188). All procedures and experiments involving human participants were performed in strict accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations, including the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective analysis of existing laboratory culture data, the requirement for individual patient informed consent was waived by the IRB. Measures were implemented to ensure data confidentiality, with collected information utilized solely for clinical research purposes and access restricted to the study team. Patient identities were anonymized through numerical coding. The study was structured as a prospective interventional quality improvement project following the Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) cycle. During the Plan phase, a baseline assessment and root cause analysis (RCA) were conducted, identifying that the 12.9% contamination rate was largely driven by inadequate skin antisepsis contact time and high staff turnover. In the Do phase, we implemented a multi-pronged intervention involving targeted retraining and the appointment of “Master Trainers.” This was followed by the Study phase, where monthly data monitoring revealed a reduction in contamination to 6.09%. Finally, in the Act phase, the findings led to the institutionalization of blood culture contamination (BCC) as a Key Performance Indicator (KPI) to ensure long-term sustainability and behavioral adherence.

An initial baseline period (December 2024 to May 2025) involved the retrospective collection of data pertaining to all in-house blood culture samples, adhering to the institution’s established protocol. This six-month period facilitated a retrospective quality improvement and cost-efficiency analysis. The blood culture contamination rate was established as a key performance indicator for the Microbiology laboratory, subject to monthly monitoring and feedback to nursing leadership and phlebotomy staff.

The study population comprised patients across all age groups presenting with clinical features suggestive of bacteremia. Exclusion criteria included prior commencement of antibiotic therapy to mitigate potential confounding effects on culture results. Furthermore, the study focused on multiple sets of blood cultures obtained via peripheral venipuncture, excluding samples from central lines to ensure a homogeneous blood culture population for reliable contamination rate determination. Blood specimens were collected from both inpatient (IPD) and outpatient (OPD) departments following standard operating procedures delineated in the institutional laboratory manual. Each collection event was documented using a pre-designed checklist to ensure protocol adherence. Venous blood samples were obtained prior to antibiotic administration to optimize pathogen recovery. All personnel involved adhered to strict aseptic and infection control protocols, including rigorous hand hygiene and the use of sterile gloves. The venipuncture site was disinfected with 70% isopropyl alcohol, applied in a concentric motion from the center outward, and allowed a minimum 30-second air-drying period. While 70% isopropyl alcohol was utilized as the primary antiseptic agent due to regional resource constraints, we emphasize that the subsequent application of chlorhexidine gluconate is clinically preferred to provide sustained residual antimicrobial activity, which further mitigates the risk of contamination from skin commensals during difficult or prolonged venipuncture. In the absence of chlorhexidine, our intervention focused on “hardening” the alcohol-only protocol by mandating a 30–60 s friction scrub and a full air-drying period. Post-disinfection palpation of the site was prohibited to minimize contamination risk. A target blood volume of 20 mL per set (10 mL per bottle; 40 mL for two sets) was collected and aseptically transferred into sterile blood culture bottles. Institutional protocol mandated recollection for samples not meeting this volume criterion, when clinically feasible.

Blood samples were inoculated into BACT/ALERT FA Plus aerobic (containing complex medium and adsorbent polymeric beads) and BacT/ALERT Paed (PF Plus) bottles for a maximum incubation period of five days on the BACT/ALERT 3D system (bioMérieux). Positive bacterial cultures underwent identification and antibiotic susceptibility testing using VITEK 2® COMPACT (bioMérieux, France). Prior to inoculation, the culture bottle’s rubber septum was disinfected with 70% isopropyl alcohol. Inoculated bottles were promptly transported to the microbiology laboratory for processing. Isolates from positive cultures were subjected to standard microbiological techniques for identification, subcultured onto blood agar and MacConkey agar until growth, assessed for potential contamination, and underwent antimicrobial susceptibility testing.

Blood culture contamination was defined as the isolation of a commensal organism from one set of multiple sets collected within a 24-hour period. Monthly contamination rates were calculated using the formula: (Number of blood culture sets with growth of skin commensals without the same organism in other sets collected within 24 h / Total number of all eligible blood culture sets collected) x 100. Eligible blood culture sets were defined as at least two sets collected from an adult patient within 24 h. A culture was classified as contaminated based on the isolation of known skin flora (e.g., Bacillus spp., coagulase-negative Staphylococci, Corynebacterium, Micrococcus, and alpha-hemolytic Streptococci) and clinical evaluation deeming the isolate non-pathogenic. Positive blood cultures were categorized as either true bacteremia (confirmed bloodstream infection) or blood culture contamination (false positive). The unit cost of a single blood culture at our institution was PKR.2000. Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 22 (IBM Corp., USA). Descriptive statistics were employed to analyze demographic data and causative microbial organisms. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages.

Interventions

Pre-intervention data were analyzed by department to ascertain their respective contributions to blood culture volume and contamination rates. A root cause analysis (RCA) identified deviations from the standard operating procedure (SOP) for blood culture collection by nursing staff and phlebotomists, particularly under conditions of overcrowding and high patient turnover. A structured Root cause analysis was performed following the baseline period to identify the primary drivers of the 12.9% mean contamination rate. The analysis utilized a thematic approach to categorize deviations in Personnel, Process, and Environment. Key findings included a significant ‘knowledge-to-action’ gap, where phlebotomy staff were aware of the SOP but failed to observe the mandatory 30–60 second friction scrub and air-drying time due to high-volume patient surges, Table 1. Furthermore, the lack of stratified reporting by department meant that high-burden areas were not previously receiving targeted feedback. These insights directly informed our ‘Master Trainer’ strategy and the subsequent inclusion of BCC rates in staff performance appraisals.

To address these findings, we conducted two primary training phases in June and September 2025, utilizing a “Master Trainer” model to provide continuous bedside mentorship. Each phlebotomy staff member attended at least one core didactic session and one practical workshop, with sessions typically lasting 60 to 90 min. Teaching materials included the institutional SOP manual, standardized checklists for collection, and instructional material distributed to the phlebotomy team. Training emphasized a “hardened” 30–60 s mechanical friction scrub, sterile technique, avoidance of re-palpation post-disinfection, and correct bottle and equipment preparation Following the sessions, every staff member had to pass independent competency assessments conducted by two Master Trainers to ensure practical adherence. Ongoing education and material distribution to the phlebotomy team were also implemented. These initiatives, aligned with institutional continuous improvement policies, comprised workshops and lectures to reinforce adherence to the current protocol. Experienced phlebotomy staff with historically low BCC rates were designated as master trainers to retrain all phlebotomy personnel. All staff underwent retraining on blood culture collection as per the defined SOP, followed by independent competency assessments by two master trainers.

Discussion

This study compellingly demonstrates the effectiveness of a focused and sustained team-based approach in addressing the persistent challenge of blood culture contamination (BCC) within a high patient turnover hospital unit. Originating from a cardiology-specialized hospital situated within a lower-middle-income country, operating under inherent resource constraints, the findings underscore the feasibility of addressing this critical issue even in challenging settings. The pre-intervention data unequivocally highlighted a significant and ongoing problem of BCC, which not only compromised the accuracy of diagnostic procedures but also imposed a substantial and preventable economic burden on the institution. Notably, there is a relative scarcity of research specifically assessing the prevalence and the patterns of antimicrobial susceptibility of bloodstream infections within our particular geographic region, making our findings all the more pertinent to the local healthcare context.

The scientific literature widely acknowledges that the primary etiological factor contributing to BCC is the occurrence of breaches in aseptic technique, particularly during the critical stages of venipuncture and the essential pre-collection skin antisepsis [12]. While it is well-established that having adequately trained personnel is a key determinant in achieving lower BCC rates, suggesting that fluctuations in staff turnover alone may not fully account for observed increases, a confluence of other factors has been implicated in contributing to this persistent problem. These factors include advancements in the sensitivity of detection technologies, such as sophisticated automated blood culture systems capable of identifying even very low concentrations of bacterial organisms, an increasing reliance on the use of various intravascular devices for patient care, and modifications in phlebotomy techniques primarily aimed at minimizing the risk of needlestick injuries to healthcare workers [13].

Prior to the implementation of our targeted intervention strategies, the blood culture contamination rates observed within our institution frequently exceeded the established threshold of 3% as recommended by the College of American Pathologists (CAP), with peak monthly values reaching alarmingly high levels of over 16% [13]. Similarly, the average BCC rate during the pre-intervention period stood at 12.9%, significantly surpassing the more stringent threshold of less than 3% as proposed by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI). These findings resonate with those of a retrospective study conducted in Nigeria, which reported a BCC rate of 10.4% over a one-year period, thereby underscoring the widespread and persistent challenge of false-positive blood culture results within the field of clinical microbiology [14]. Furthermore, the observations made by Lalezari et al. [15], indicating that up to 50% of positive blood culture results could be attributed to contaminants, highlight the substantial burden that BCC places on both diagnostic reliability and the efficient allocation of valuable healthcare resources. Post-intervention fluctuations (1.3%–10.2%) stemmed from a “decay of adherence” during high-volume surges and staff turnover, which we mitigated by institutionalizing BCC as a mandatory Key Performance Indicator (KPI).

In the present study, the microorganisms most frequently isolated as contaminants were those commonly recognized as constituents of the normal skin flora. Despite typically being non-pathogenic in most clinical contexts, their inadvertent presence in blood cultures can lead to significant diagnostic confusion, the unnecessary prescription of antimicrobial therapy, and potentially prolonged durations of hospital stays for affected patients [4]. The most frequently identified contaminants included Bacillus species, coagulase-negative Staphylococci (CoNS), Corynebacterium, Micrococcus, and alpha-hemolytic Streptococci, a pattern that exhibits notable similarities to the findings reported in a study conducted in Japan [16]. The phenomenon of contamination typically occurs when commensal bacteria residing on the patient’s skin or the skin of healthcare personnel are inadvertently introduced into the sterile culture medium during the process of blood collection [17]. Notably, in our study, Staphylococcus epidermidis emerged as the most frequently encountered contaminant, accounting for a significant 72.2% of all identified contaminants, a finding that aligns with observations from a study conducted in Ireland, where S. epidermidis was also identified as the primary causative agent of BCC [18]. While CoNS have been consistently reported in the literature as the leading cause of BCC, comprising up to 84.3% in earlier studies [19, 20], our current findings demonstrate a nearly comparable distribution. It is of paramount importance to accurately distinguish BCC involving CoNS from true positive cultures, as S. epidermidis, while often a contaminant, possesses the potential to behave as an opportunistic pathogen, particularly in the context of indwelling medical devices.

Conversely, organisms such as Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Candida albicans, and members of the Enterobacteriaceae family are generally accepted as true pathogens when isolated from blood cultures [21]. While the presence of multiple organisms in a single culture bottle often suggests contamination, polymicrobial bacteremia can occur in 6% to 21% of confirmed bloodstream infections, particularly in immunocompromised or critically ill patients [22]. In our study, polymicrobial bacteremia was reported in 5% of cases. Therefore, clinical judgment, taking into careful consideration the specific clinical context of each patient and the results obtained from multiple sets of blood cultures, is essential to distinguish true infection from contamination. The finding of a single positive culture amidst several others collected simultaneously often serves as a strong indicator of contamination rather than true bacteremia. It is also important to note that certain high-risk patient groups, such as those with central venous catheters or chronic conditions, may be more susceptible to BCC, although our study did not specifically analyze these factors.

An important consideration in blood culture practice is sample volume. Guidelines suggest that larger volumes enhance the sensitivity of blood cultures; however, the literature lacks randomized controlled trials definitively supporting this claim [23]. While precise volume measurement was not feasible in our study, and anaerobic bottles were frequently not utilized, obtaining multiple culture sets can be particularly challenging in pediatric populations. In this context, Haimi-Cohen et al. [24] demonstrated that clinical parameters, combined with a time to positivity of ≤ 15 h, had a positive predictive value of 84% for true coagulase-negative Staphylococci infections in children. Furthermore, the National Nosocomial Infection Surveillance (NNIS) system often relies on the treating clinician’s diagnosis of bloodstream infection to differentiate true bacteremia from contamination involving common contaminants [25].

The financial implications of BCC are significant, although often underrecognized in our region. A local estimate from our study indicated a cost of approximately PKR 110,000 over four quarters, underscoring the economic imperative for strict adherence to sterile collection practices. The financial impact of blood culture contamination has been documented in numerous studies [26, 27]. Bates et al. [28] found that contaminant results, compared with true-negative results, were independently associated with increased subsequent laboratory charges (20% increase) and intravenous antibiotic charges (39% increase). In a study involving 9,959 blood cultures in children, Thuler et al. [29] reported that 26% of children with false-positive cultures initially evaluated as outpatients were subsequently admitted to the hospital based on these misleading results.

Post-intervention data in our study revealed a substantial reduction in contamination rates to 1.3%, demonstrating the effectiveness of focused training and procedural standardization. These findings are consistent with outcomes from similar quality improvement initiatives [30]. A study from India reported a contamination rate of 2.4% in blood cultures from both IPD and OPD settings [6]. Interventions targeting skin disinfection techniques, proper preparation of culture bottles, and direct venipuncture have been shown to be effective in reducing contamination rates [31]. A strategic approach to further reduce BCC involves targeting high-burden areas, such as internal medicine and intensive care units, where contamination rates tend to be higher. Factors contributing to contamination include blood collection via indwelling lines, inadequate skin disinfection, and improper technique, especially when performed by inadequately trained staff. Though achieving zero contamination is unlikely, several evidence-based measures can substantially reduce rates. A common issue is insufficient contact time for antiseptics during skin preparation; healthcare providers often do not allow the recommended 90 to 120 s for adequate disinfection. In addition to reporting rates regularly to infection prevention and antibiotic stewardship teams, specialized reporting of rates stratified by patient care locations and collection staff, were undertaken to better target improvement efforts. Evidence supports that peripheral venipuncture is associated with significantly lower contamination rates compared to collection via intravenous catheters, as confirmed by a meta-analysis [32] and a recent investigation [33]. The successful reduction to 1.3% post-intervention demonstrates the effectiveness of targeted training and procedural reinforcement [30], suggesting that a target contamination rate of 1% is achievable when best practices are followed [3]. The ways to address the problem of blood culture contamination included proper skin antisepsis with alcoholic solution [1], blood culture bottle disinfection, hand hygiene, dedicated phlebotomy teams, and education on proper technique and direct venipuncture practices [31, 34]. Nurses should be educated and committed to adhering to sterile and aseptic procedures during blood culture collection [35]. The laboratory director should regularly review blood culture contamination rates, as tracking the contamination rate and providing feedback to units and persons drawing blood cultures is one method that has been shown to reduce contamination rates. Furthermore, to ensure better compliance among phlebotomists and nursing staff, BCC was introduced as one of their key performance indicators based on which they were appraised.

It is challenging to maintain specimen quality to international standards while offering the best possible patient care in health setups with limited resources. A strategic approach to quality control measures and ongoing monitoring are needed to achieve this goal. Even with limited resources, adherence to these standards improves the accuracy of research and diagnosis. There were few limitations in our study, the authors intend to conduct a multi-center study in the future, which would incorporate a wider understanding of specimen contamination on the overall care of patients and the health setup. We didn’t study the sensitivity pattern of the isolated pathogens from the contaminated cultures. Furthermore, a more targeted study is needed in this regard to assess the precise intervention which has the most impact in reducing sample contamination rates.

Conclusion

In conclusion, blood culture contamination is a preventable challenge that significantly impacts patient safety and healthcare costs. By utilizing a structured Plan-Do-Study-Act (PDSA) framework and a systematic thematic root cause analysis, this study demonstrated that a multi-pronged intervention can reduce contamination rates from a baseline of 12.9% to a low of 1.3%. While regional resource constraints necessitated an adaptive approach to skin antisepsis, the significant improvement confirms that ‘hardening’ aseptic protocols through friction-scrub techniques and ‘Master Trainer’ oversight is clinically effective. To address the sustainability challenges posed by high staff turnover, we have institutionalized contamination rates as Key Performance Indicators (KPIs). This ensures that rigorous adherence to standardized collection protocols remains a permanent priority for hospital leadership and regulatory bodies. Moving forward, integrating clinical context with laboratory findings and exploring real-time guidance technologies will be essential for maintaining these quality gains and optimizing resource allocation in resource-limited settings.
