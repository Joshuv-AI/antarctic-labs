The impact of calcium supplementation on hypertension management in postmenopausal women with hypocalcemia
Authors: Ahmed Farouk Alaarag, Mohamed Naseem, Haidy Khattab, Reham Younis, Ahmed Hamam, Hazem K Shalaby, Mahmoud Abd Elkhalek Abou-Omar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension poses a significant health risk for postmenopausal women, with incidence rates notably higher than for men of the same age group. The decline in estrogen levels leads to several physiological changes, such as hypocalcemia, which may contribute to the development of hypertension. This study investigates the effects of calcium supplementation on blood pressure control in postmenopausal women with hypocalcemia.MethodsWe prospectively included 300 postmenopausal women diagnosed with both hypocalcemia and hypertension. The participants received calcium carbonate (1,000 mg/day) in two daily doses, in addition to their standard antihypertensive treatment. Blood pressure measurements and serum calcium levels were recorded at baseline and after 3 months. Patients were divided into two groups according to serum calcium level at 3 months: those with normalized calcium levels and those who continued hypocalcemia.ResultsWomen with persistent hypocalcemia at follow-up were younger at the age of menopause (mean age 48.65 years) compared to those who achieved normocalcemia (mean age 49.44 years), P = 0.017. Those who had normocalcemia had a significant SBP, DBP, and more patients achieved target blood pressure ( 0.017, 0.029 & 0.012), respectively.ConclusionsUse of calcium supplements to correct hypocalcemia in postmenopausal hypertensive women may help reduce blood pressure and achieve the target blood pressure.Keywords: Post menopause, Hypertension, Hypocalcemia, Target blood pressure

Introduction

Postmenopausal women suffer from increased morbidity and mortality due to Hypertension [1]. Hypertension is more common among postmenopausal women compared to men of the same age group. The risk of hypertension doubles after menopause. In the United States, 75% of women over the age of 60 are hypertensive [2].

Many factors may contribute to the development of hypertension in postmenopausal women, including alternations in the estrogen-to-androgen balance leading to increased androgen levels, activation of the renin-angiotensin aldoserone system (RAAS), sympathetic nervous system (SNA), and endothelin-1 (ET-1), as well as, inflammation, a rise in vasoconstrictor eicosanoids, decreased calcium levels and psychological factors like anxiety and depression, may also play significant roles in the pathogenesis of postmenopausal hypertension [3].

One of the main factors that controls vascular smooth muscle contraction and relaxation is calcium, making hypocalcemia a potential contributor to the development of hypertension [4]. Postmenopausal women are particularly vulnerable to hypocalcemia due to decreased estrogen levels, which impair calcium absorption [5].

Hypocalcemia can trigger the release of parathyroid hormone (PTH), which increases intracellular calcium in vascular smooth muscle, resulting in vasoconstriction and increased blood pressure. Additionally, insufficient calcium levels can impair the regulation of vascular tone, leading to hypertension [6].

Calcium supplementation may therefore play a role in managing hypertension in this group of population group, as it helps restore calcium homeostasis. By restoring calcium balance, supplementation addresses one of the underlying mechanisms contributing to hypertension in Postmenopausal women, offering a potential intervention for better blood pressure control [6].

However, the findings are inconsistent. While some research supports the role of calcium supplementation in reducing blood pressure, particularly in postmenopausal women, other research yields inconclusive outcomes [7].

The present study aims to investigate the effects of calcium supplementation on managing hypertension in postmenopausal women who have hypocalcemia.

All patients received calcium supplementation in the form of calcium carbonate (1,000 mg per day) divided into two daily doses. Standard antihypertensive therapy was maintained and adjusted by treating physicians, according to the current practice guidelines [10].

Follow-up assessments were conducted at 3 months to monitor changes in serum calcium levels. Based on the follow-up measurements, patients were divided into two groups: Group 1 (patients with normalized calcium levels) and Group 2 (patients who persisted with hypocalcemia).

Office blood pressure readings were taken with the patient seated after resting for at least 5 min in a calm environment. The measurements were conducted three times using mercury sphygmomanometers, with 1–2-minute intervals between each reading. The systolic and diastolic blood pressures were averaged from the last two readings, and all readings were standardized according to the ESC 2024 guidelines [10].

Blood pressure measurements were recorded at baseline and follow-up visits. At follow-up visits, we considered achieving blood pressure goals in patients who achieved systolic blood pressure less than 130 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure less than 80 mmHg.

Statistical analysis

The data normality was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For normally distributed data, the mean ± standard deviation (SD) was used to present the descriptive statistics, while the interquartile range (IQR) and median were used to present the non-normally distributed parameters.

For inferential analyses, for continuous parameters, the Independent Samples t-test was utilized for comparison, while we utilized the Mann–Whitney U test for the non-normally distributed continuous variables, and the categorical parameters were tested using the Chi-square test.

Linear multivariate regression analyses were conducted to identify predictors of persistently elevated (SBP) and (DBP) after 3 months of follow-up (continuous outcomes). For persistent hypertension (categorical outcomes), binary logistic regression analyses were performed. Both univariate and multivariate analyses were carried out to identify potential predictors and adjust for confounding variables.

To assess changes over time and between groups, repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) within the General Linear Model (GLM) framework was applied. Time (baseline vs. 3-month follow-up) served as the within-subject factor, and group (Group 1 vs. Group 2) as the between-subject factor. The main effects of time and group, along with the time × group interaction, were evaluated. The assumption of sphericity was assessed using Mauchly’s test, with Greenhouse–Geisser corrections applied when violated. In the presence of a significant interaction effect, pairwise comparisons were conducted using the Bonferroni adjustment.

Effect sizes were expressed as partial eta squared (η²), with thresholds conventionally interpreted as: small effect (0.01 to < 0.06), medium effect (≥ 0.06 to < 0.14), and large effect (≥ 0.14). The limits of statistical significance were set at P-value < 0.05; IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 23.0, was used to perform that analysis.

The power of sample size was calculated by the G Power software (Franz Faul, University of Kiel, Germany, version 3.1.9.4) for different variables. The power value was set at 0.85 with an alpha error of 0.05 and a medium effect size.

Results

A total of 300 postmenopausal women with coexisting hypocalcemia and hypertension who completed the 3-month follow-up and were compliant with the prescribed treatment were included in the analysis. All participants received calcium carbonate supplementation (1,000 mg/day administered in two divided doses) in addition to their standard antihypertensive regimen. Based on serum calcium status at follow-up, participants were stratified into two groups: 157 (52.33%) who achieved normocalcemia (Group 1) and 143 (47.67%) who remained hypocalcemic (Group 2).

Regarding age, the mean age of the participants was 53.36 years, and there was no statistically significant difference between those who achieved normocalcemia and those who did not at the end of the study. (Table 1)

Patients in group 1 had a statistically significantly higher mean age at menopause (49.4 ± 2.8 vs. 48.7 ± 2.9 years, p = 0.017) and higher baseline adjusted serum calcium levels (8.18 ± 0.21 vs. 7.76 ± 0.18 mg/dL, p < 0.001). In contrast, there were no statistically significant differences (P > 0.05) between the two groups with respect to postmenopausal duration, body mass index, presence of diabetes, baseline systolic and diastolic blood pressure, LDL-C, triglyceride levels, or antihypertensive therapy. (Table 1)

At the 3-month follow-up, participants in group 1 demonstrated a statistically significant lower systolic blood pressure (146.5 ± 10.9 vs. 150.0 ± 12.0 mmHg, P = 0.008) and diastolic blood pressure (88.4 ± 8.6 vs. 90.5 ± 8.0 mmHg, P = 0.029) compared with those in group 2. In addition, the participants in group 1 achieved a statistically significantly higher proportion reaching the target blood pressure (27.4% vs. 15.4%, P 0.012). No statistically significant differences (P > 0.05) were observed between groups in LDL-C or triglyceride levels (Table 1).

In linear multivariate regression analyses (Tables 2 and 3), baseline SBP and DBP emerged as strong independent predictors of their respective values at 3 months. Furthermore, DBP at 3 months was independently correlated with SBP at 3 months; at the same time, SBP at 3 months was independently correlated with DBP at 3 months.

Persistent hypocalcemia was significantly associated with higher 3-month blood pressure. Specifically, for SBP, univariate analysis yielded (B = 3.521, p = 0.008), and multivariate analysis confirmed an independent effect (B = 3.385, p = 0.027). Similarly, for DBP, persistent hypocalcemia was associated with higher values in both univariate (B = 2.112, p = 0.029) and multivariate analyses (B = 2.814, p = 0.014).

Adjusted calcium at 3 months showed a significant negative association with both SBP and DBP in univariate analyses (p = 0.018 and 0.027, respectively). However, in the multivariate model, the association was no longer significant (P 0.981, 0.819, respectively). (Tables 3 and 4)

Additionally, postmenopausal duration was significantly negatively associated with DBP in univariate analysis (p = 0.019).In multivariate logistic regression analyses, it was no longer significant (P 0.974). (Table 4).

For SBP, a significant time effect was observed (F = 963.08, p < 0.001) with a large effect size (partial η² = 0.764). Both groups exhibited clinically meaningful reductions, with a greater mean decrease in Group 1 (− 16.08 mmHg) compared with Group 2 (- −12.06 mmHg). The intergroup main effect was not significant (F = 1.46, p = 0.228; partial η² = 0.005), suggesting no overall difference between groups when averaged across time. However, the time × group interaction was statistically significant (F = 19.67, p < 0.001) with a medium effect size (partial η² = 0.062), indicating that the magnitude of SBP reduction over time differed between the two groups.

Similarly, DBP showed a significant time effect (F = 996.79, p < 0.001) with a large effect size (partial η² = 0.770). Group 1 demonstrated a larger mean reduction (− 11.15 mmHg) compared with Group 2 (− 8.23 mmHg). The intergroup effect was not statistically significant (F = 0.58, p = 0.447; partial η² = 0.002). In contrast, the time × group interaction was significant (F = 22.53, p < 0.001) with a medium effect size (partial η² = 0.070), indicating differential improvements in DBP between groups over time.

Overall, these findings indicate that while both groups experienced reductions in SBP and DBP over the 3-month period, Group 1 achieved a greater and more significant reduction in blood pressure as evidenced by the significant interaction effects with medium effect sizes.

Discussion

Women experience two sex-specific physiological phases in their lives that increase their hypertension risk. These two phases are pregnancy and menopause [12].

Women before menopause have lower blood pressure than age-matched men. After menopause, the prevalence of hypertension increases in women; however, men still have a higher incidence of being hypertensive, but women have a less efficient response to antihypertensive medication with age compared to men [13]. The risk of hypertension doubles after menopause, 41% of postmenopausal women develop hypertension [3].

Postmenopausal women are at increased risk of hypocalcemia because of increased calcium demands, reduced intestinal calcium absorption, and increased urinary calcium excretion. Estrogen deficiency after menopause is thought to play a central role in these alterations in calcium homeostasis [14]., Many studies highlighted that hypocalcemia and fluctuations in serum calcium levels were strongly associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes, highlighting potential contribution of disrupted calcium homeostasis to cardiovascular risk [15].

We aimed from this prospective study to examine the effect of oral calcium supplementation on the control of blood pressure of hypocalcemic postmenopausal women, and we found that achieving normocalcemia may improve their response to anti-hypertensive medications, helping to better reduce both systolic and diastolic blood pressure and increase the number of those achieved target blood pressure goals.

The mean SBP and DBP were lower at the end of the study in those with normocalcemia than those with persistent hypocalcemia by 3.52 mmHg in SBP and 2.11 mmHg in DBP. Even this minimal reduction in blood pressure in different clinical trials was associated with reduced mortality from coronary artery and cerebrovascular disease [6].

At the end of the follow-up period, those who reached the normocalceamic state had a higher incidence of achieving blood pressure goals (25.4%) versus (15.4%) in those who remained hypocalceamic.

These results match the findings of Hofmeyr et al., who found that in non-pregnant women with previous pre-eclampsia, calcium supplements led to a significant reduction in their blood pressure [16].

As well as low calcium intake of less than (< 696 mg/day) showed an increased mortality.

from cardiovascular diseases was observed in Caucasian women aged 55–69 years who were included in The Iowa Women Health Study [17]. Matching with the previous results, the study performed with Wang et al., who found that women over 45 years with low calcium intake had a higher rate of hypertension [18].

However, there are some trials like WHI (Women Health Initiative) that showed the use of calcium and or vitamin D did not reduce blood pressure nor reduce the risk of developing hypertension over 7 years of follow-up [19]. Despite these conflicting findings of different clinical studies, the pathophysiological mechanism of hypertension in postmenopausal women can be reversed by calcium. This conflict may be explained by the possible blood pressure-lowering effect of calcium, which is achieved when women reach normocalcemic levels, not due to calcium supplementation itself, the routine non-indicated use of calcium supplements, or large single doses may be attributed to this conflict.

The Current guidelines strongly recommend adequate calcium intake in postmenopausal women. Supplemental calcium should be reserved for women with specific clinical indications. Also, routine use of calcium in women who do not have a clinical indication for calcium supplements is not recommended and may lead to worse clinical outcomes. Excessive total calcium intake (> 1,500 mg/day), from either dietary or supplemental sources, has been associated with increased all-cause mortality [20].

Therefore, in the current study, all participants received 1000 mg calcium carbonate supplementation administered in two divided doses.

The divided-dose regimen used in our study may have minimized the acute hemodynamic effects associated with large single-dose calcium supplementation on blood pressure, and to avoid overdose of calcium. In support of this hypothesis, Billington et al. demonstrated that acute calcium supplementation may alter short-term blood pressure hemodynamics in normotensive postmenopausal women. In their randomized double-blind crossover trial, administration of a single 1000 mg dose of calcium citrate attenuated elevated SBP compared with placebo [21].

The pathophysiological mechanisms of hypertension post menopause are mostly dependent on increased peripheral vascular resistance due to increased sympathetic tone, activation of the renin angiotensin aldosterone system (RAAS), and endothelial dysfunction resulting in vascular smooth muscle proliferation and increased vascular tone [3–22].

During menopause, the progressive reduction in estrogen and a marked increase in follicle-stimulating hormone and luteinizing hormone affect vascular tone by multiple pathways. Estrogen is known to improve nitric oxide bioavailability and inhibit kinase-mediated vascular smooth muscle contraction. At the same time, estrogen enhances vagal tone and maintains autonomic balance, and studies in postmenopausal women decreased baroreceptor activity and increased dominance of sympathetic activity, and finally increased RAAS activity [3–23]. Improving calcium homeostasis and achieving hyocalceamia in postmenopausal women can reverse all these mechanisms, and some studies found that this effect is more evident in women with lower calcium levels [15, 24].

Conclusions

Use of calcium supplementation to achieve normocalcemia in postmenopausal women with hypertension may improve their response to antihypertensive medication and help in achieving target blood pressure goals, which will help to reduce their future cardiovascular risk. We recommend large multi-centers studies with a larger number of patients to validate our results.

Limitations

The relatively small number of patients, the Singel center study, the short follow-up period, and the lack of information on the vitamin D status in participants are the main limitations of this study.
