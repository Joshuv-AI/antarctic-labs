UV irradiation combined with high voltage electrostatic field alleviates surface discoloration in fresh-cut purple yam by regulating enzymatic browning
Authors: Jieni Liu, Leilei Wu, Fan Yinyin, Hongqian Hu, Yu Qiao, Qing Sun, Lihui Zhang, Dahui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFresh-cut purple yam is highly susceptible to enzymatic browning during storage, primarily driven by peroxidase (POD), while superoxide dismutase (SOD) serves as an oxidative-stress marker. This study investigated the effects of ultraviolet-C (UV-C), high-voltage electrostatic field (HVEF), and their combination (UV-HVEF) on browning inhibition and quality preservation. The combined treatment (UV-C at 68 W for 120 min followed by HVEF at 30 kV for 8 min) exhibited the strongest synergistic effect, achieving the highest L* value, total phenolics, flavonoids, and anthocyanins, along with the lowest browning degree after 8 days of storage. UV-HVEF induced tryptophan exposure and α-helix loss in these enzymes, leading to inactivation, while downregulating POD, phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), cinnamate-4-hydroxylase (C4H) expression and preserving p-coumaric acid at 54.56% higher than the control. Correlation analysis confirmed brightness positively correlated with phenolics and negatively with enzyme activities. Scanning electron microscopy revealed HVEF damaged starch granules, while UV-HVEF caused irregular morphology. Overall, UV-HVEF is an effective non-thermal strategy balancing enzyme inactivation with quality maintenance.Keywords: Fresh-cut purple yam, Ultraviolet, High-voltage electrostatic field, Browning

Introduction

Fresh-cut products have gained substantial popularity in modern markets due to their convenience and fresh-like quality. However, the mechanical processing involved in cutting induces physiological stress and tissue damage, leading to rapid quality deterioration. Among these issues, surface discoloration, primarily caused by enzymatic browning, is a major concern that severely limits shelf-life and consumer acceptance. Yam (Dioscorea spp.), a nutritious tuber rich in polyphenols and starches, is highly susceptible to browning after cutting. This not only affects its visual appeal but also signifies losses in nutritional and commercial value. Therefore, developing effective strategies to inhibit browning is crucial for preserving the quality of fresh-cut yam.

In yam, enzymatic browning is primarily driven by peroxidase (POD) and polyphenol oxidase (PPO), while superoxide dismutase (SOD) serves as a key oxidative-stress marker involved in reactive oxygen species (ROS) regulation during postharvest stress (Sui, Meng, Dong, Fan, & Wang, 2023). The phenylpropanoid pathway plays a pivotal role as it governs the biosynthesis of phenolic compounds, providing the substrates for browning reactions (Zhao et al., 2021). Key enzymes such as phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), cinnamate-4-hydroxylase (C4H), and 4-coumarate-CoA ligase (4CL) regulate the flux of phenolics (Cheng et al., 2024). Thus, an effective anti-browning treatment must not only inhibit the activity of browning enzymes but also modulate the phenylpropanoid pathway to reduce substrate availability.

Traditional methods to control browning, such as chemical inhibitors (e.g., sulfites, ascorbic acid, and calcium salts), are increasingly scrutinized due to concerns over residual toxicity, environmental impact, and potential alterations to sensory properties. Consequently, there is a growing interest in developing non-thermal, chemical-free, and environmentally friendly physical technologies (Teng, Zhang, & Mujumdar, 2023). Among these, ultraviolet (UV) irradiation, particularly UV-C (200–280 nm), has shown promise, which can inactivate enzymes and alter secondary metabolism without generating excessive heat or residues (Han, Zhen, Chen, & Fu, 2021). Xin et al. (2024) reported that UV-C irradiation can effectively suppress the activity of PPO and POD in fresh-cut lettuce slices, thereby delaying the browning process. Similarly, high voltage electrostatic field (HVEF) treatment, a novel non-thermal technology, can influence enzyme conformation and microbial activity through its induced electric effects, offering another residue-free preservation alternative (Kao, Tu, Sridhar, & Tsai, 2019; Wang, Zhang, & Rui, 2025). Similarly, studies on fresh-cut cucumbers have demonstrated that HVEF treatment effectively inhibits the activities of PPO and POD, thereby delaying quality deterioration (Shi, Zhang, Mujumdar, & Lei, 2026). The combined UV-HVEF treatment offers distinct advantages over other integrated non-thermal techniques. It is a residue-free physical method without chemical additives, unlike edible coatings. In contrast to cold plasma or ozone treatments, which require gas-feeding systems and may generate undesirable by-products, UV-HVEF operates in ambient air, simplifying industrial scaling. Furthermore, the two technologies act through complementary mechanisms: UV-C induces direct photochemical protein damage, while HVEF generates reactive oxygen species and ozone via corona discharge, enabling synergistic effects on enzyme inactivation and phenolic metabolism. Although individual applications have been explored for various fresh-cut commodities, their combined effect on POD-mediated browning in yam remains largely uninvestigated. This synergy may reduce individual treatment intensities and provide a multi-targeted approach to browning control.

Therefore, the objective of the present study was to investigate the efficacy of combined UV-C irradiation and HVEF treatment in mitigating surface discoloration in fresh-cut yam. A systematic investigation was conducted by applying UV-C (38–100 W) and HVEF (10–30 kV) at different exposure durations to determine the optimal treatment conditions. The conformation and morphological changes of POD and SOD in UV-C and HVEF treatment, the inactivation mechanism of POD and SOD were assessed. Furthermore, the storage quality parameters (color, anthocyanin content, and visual appearance), enzymatic activities (POD and SOD) and phenylpropanoid pathway metabolites (phenylalanine, p-coumaric acid, total phenolics) during storage were monitored. Additionally, gene expression levels of key oxidative enzymes and critical enzymes in the phenylpropanoid pathway were analyzed to provide molecular-level insights into the regulatory mechanisms. This comprehensive approach aims to establish a scientific foundation for developing effective non-thermal processing strategies to extend the shelf life of fresh-cut yam products.

Materials and methods

Materials

Horseradish peroxidase (POD) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) were sourced from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. and Aladdin Reagent Co., Ltd. (both located in Shanghai, China), respectively. Folin's phenol was supplied by Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China), and rutin was acquired from Shanghai Runjie Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). All other reagents were procured from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China).

Fresh purple yams harvested from Badong County (Hubei Province, China) were selected based on uniform size and maturity. Prior to experimentation, the yams were stored at 4 ± 1 °C. Subsequently, they were washed, peeled, and cut into 5 mm thick slices using a microtome (Model 801, TERUISI, China) for further experimental procedures.

HVEF and UV-C treatment for POD and SOD

A high-voltage electrostatic field–static magnetic field combination system was designed and fabricated by INDUC Scientific Co., Ltd. (Jiangsu, China). The electric field was generated using two electrode plate pairs: a grounded lower plate enclosing the device and an upper barbed plate (19.9 × 19.9 cm) positioned at the center of the chamber. The upper barbed electrode consisted of multiple stainless steel needles (tip curvature radius approximately 0.5 mm, needle-to-needle spacing 2 cm) arranged in a grid pattern, connected to the positive DC high-voltage output (anode), while the lower stainless steel plate served as the cathode. A DC voltage generator (maximum 30 kV) supplied positive high voltage to the upper plate, which discharged toward the lower plate to establish electric fields. Samples placed 10 cm below the barbed plate were subjected to electric field intensities ranging from 0 to 3 kV/cm. A schematic diagram of the electrode configuration is provided in Fig. S1. During HVEF operation, a stable corona discharge was observed at the needle tips at voltages above the corona onset voltage (approximately 3 kV for the 10 cm electrode gap). No sparking or arcing occurred under the experimental conditions, as the applied voltage remained below the air breakdown threshold. The corona current was monitored using a microammeter connected in series with the ground electrode. The relative humidity in the chamber was maintained at 55 ± 5% and monitored throughout the experiments using a hygrometer, as humidity can affect corona discharge characteristics and ozone generation. The chamber temperature was maintained at 25 ± 1 °C.

A centrifuge tube containing 10 mL of enzyme solution (POD 150 U/mg or SOD 150 U/mg) was placed inside the electrostatic field chamber. Both POD and SOD solutions were treated for 0–14 min under electric fields generated at 10, 20, and 30 kV, corresponding to a fixed distance of 10 cm from the barbed electrode plate. All HVEF treatments were performed in triplicate.

The POD and SOD solutions were irradiated using a UV-C light device (ZW-38D17-W-410×, λ = 254 nm) at three nominal power levels (38 W, 68 W, and 100 W), with the lamp maintained at a constant distance of 0.5 cm from the sample surface. The irradiance at the sample surface was calibrated using a high-precision radiometer (TES-1339 P, Shanghai Chugong Industrial Co., Ltd.) and was recorded as 37 μmol·m−2·s−1 at the operating distance. The spectral output was centered at 254 nm with a half-peak bandwidth of approximately 10 nm, as specified by the manufacturer, and the lamp was calibrated prior to use following the standard protocol for UV-C dosimetry. For each treatment, 10 mL of enzyme solution (3 mm liquid depth) was placed in a transparent polypropylene centrifuge tube (transmittance ≥90% at 254 nm). The tubes were positioned directly beneath the lamp with the liquid surface perpendicular to the incident light to ensure uniform exposure. To minimize positional variation, the same geometric configuration was maintained across all experiments. During irradiation, the sample temperature was monitored using a thermocouple probe and maintained at 25 ± 1 °C by placing the samples in a temperature-controlled chamber with forced air circulation, ensuring that the treatment remained non-thermal. A thermal control group (lamp off but identical temperature conditions) was included to confirm that the observed effects were attributable to UV-C irradiation rather than heating. UV-C irradiation treatments were conducted in triplicate at 25 °C over durations of up to 140 min. No significant sample dehydration was observed during treatment.

Determination of POD and SOD activity

POD activity and SOD activity were assayed spectrophotometrically using commercial kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing China).

Enzyme inactivation mechanism

HVEF, UV irradiation and UV irradiation combined with HVEF (UV-HVEF)

Based on the inactivation efficacy of POD and SOD under different conditions, the treatment parameters for HVEF, UV irradiation, and their combination (UV-HVEF) were optimized and applied as follows.

For HVEF treatment alone, the enzyme solutions (POD and SOD) were subjected to an electrostatic field generated at 30 kV for 8 min, with samples placed 10 cm below the barbed electrode plate.

For UV-C irradiation alone, the enzyme solutions were exposed to UV-C light at 68 W for 120 min, with the lamp positioned 0.5 cm from the sample.

For the combined UV-HVEF treatment, the enzyme solutions were first treated with UV-C irradiation (68 W, 120 min) followed immediately by HVEF exposure (30 kV, 8 min). All treatments were carried out in triplicate at 25 °C.

Untreated controls (CK, kept at 25 °C for the same durations), buffer-only controls (phosphate buffer without enzyme), and heating controls (enzyme solutions incubated at 60 °C for 30 min) were included for comparison.

Ultraviolet–visible spectrum measurement

UV–Vis absorption spectra of untreated and treated (HVEF, UV-C, and UV-HVEF) POD and SOD solutions were recorded at 25 °C using a UV-2600 spectrophotometer (Shimadzu Corporation, Japan) across the wavelength range of 220–700 nm. A phosphate buffer (0.1 mM, pH 6.0) was used as the blank for baseline correction. After treatment, all enzyme solutions were adjusted to a uniform protein concentration of 0.1 mg/mL to ensure comparability of spectral data. All spectra were corrected for light scattering by subtracting the baseline spectrum obtained from the corresponding blank buffer and applying a smoothing algorithm where necessary.

Determination of intrinsic fluorescence spectrum

Changes in the intrinsic fluorescence of POD and SOD after each treatment (HVEF, UV, and UV-HVEF) were measured on a fluorescence spectrophotometer (Agilent Cary Eclipse, Agilent Technologies Inc., USA). The excitation and emission slit widths were both set to 10 nm, with excitation wavelengths scanned from 320 to 400 nm and corresponding emission spectra collected. All enzyme solutions were normalized to the same protein concentration (0.1 mg/mL) after treatment. Fluorescence spectra were corrected for buffer background and scattering artifacts by subtracting the spectrum of the corresponding blank buffer.

Measurement of circular dichroism spectrum

Circular dichroism (CD) spectra of POD and SOD solutions (0.01 mg/mL) were acquired before and after treatment (HVEF, UV, and UV-HVEF) using a JASCO CD spectrometer (Japan). All samples were diluted to a final concentration of 0.01 mg/mL in phosphate buffer (0.05 mM, pH 6.0) after treatment. Measurements were performed in a 1 cm path length quartz cell with a bandwidth of 0.2 nm. A scanning speed of 50 nm/min was applied in the far-UV region (200–260 nm). A secondary structure content was estimated from the CD data using the CDNN 2.1 software, with deconvolution fitting quality assessed by normalized root mean square deviation (NRMSD <0.1).

Fresh-cut purple yam processing

Based on the optimized parameters established above, fresh-cut purple yam slices (5 mm thickness) were subjected to UV irradiation, HVEF, and combined UV-HVEF treatments. The treated samples were then stored at 4 °C and analyzed at 0, 2, 4, 6, and 8 days of storage to evaluate the treatment effects. All treatments and analyses were performed in triplicate.

Determination of color

The surface color of fresh-cut yam slices was measured using an automatic colorimeter (NH310, Guangdong San'enshi Technology Co., Ltd., China) equipped with a D65 illuminant and a 10° standard observer. The instrument was calibrated against a standard white plate before each measurement session. Color values were recorded in the CIE L*a*b* color space, where L* represents lightness (0 = black, 100 = white), a* indicates redness (positive) or greenness (negative), and b* indicates yellowness (positive) or blueness (negative). For each slice, three random positions on the surface were measured, and the average values were calculated for each replicate. The browning degree was evaluated based on the L* value, as a lower L* value indicates greater surface darkening due to enzymatic browning. All measurements were performed at room temperature (25 ± 1 °C) on yam slices immediately after removal from storage.

Determination of anthocyanin content

Anthocyanin content of yam slices was determined using the differential pH method, following the procedures established by Lee, Durst, Wrolstad, and Collaborators (2005) with modifications from Santos, Llavata, Augusto, and Cárcel (2025). Briefly, aliquots of the extracted sample were mixed with KCl buffer (0.025 M, pH 1.0). Absorbance was then measured at 520 nm (λmax for anthocyanins) and 700 nm using a microplate reader.

Determination of total phenolic content

The total phenolic content (TPC) in yam slices was determined using the Folin-Ciocalteu method with slight modifications (Shen, Chen, Li, Cui, & Lin, 2023). Briefly, 0.5 mL of yam extract was mixed with 1 mL of Folin-Ciocalteu reagent in a 15 mL centrifuge tube and allowed to stand for 5 min. Then, 3 mL of sodium carbonate solution (75 g/L) and 10 mL of distilled water were added. The mixture was incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 60 min, followed by centrifugation at 10,000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min. The absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 760 nm using a spectrophotometer. TPC was quantified based on a gallic acid standard curve and expressed as micrograms of gallic acid equivalents per 100 g of fresh weight (μg GAE/100 g).

Determination of total flavonoid content

Total flavonoid content (TFC) was assessed according to a published procedure (Shen et al., 2023) with minor adjustments. In detail, 2 mL of yam extract was mixed with 0.3 mL of sodium nitrite solution (5%) and incubated at 25 °C for 6 min. Subsequently, 0.3 mL of aluminum nitrate solution (10%) was added, and the mixture was maintained at 25 °C for another 15 min. After the addition of distilled water to bring the final volume to 10 mL, the absorbance was recorded at 510 nm. TFC was calculated using a rutin standard curve and expressed as micrograms of rutin equivalents per 100 g of fresh weight (μg RE/100 g).

Determination of POD and SOD activity

The activities of POD and SOD were determined following the assay procedures described in Section 2.3.

Determination of phenylalanine, p-coumaric acid

The contents of phenylalanine and p-coumaric acid in fresh-cut yam were analyzed following a previously reported method (Cheng et al., 2024) with minor modifications. Separation was carried out using a Waters e2695 high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) (Shimadzu LC-2030C 3D PLUS) system equipped with an Agilent ZORBAX SB-C18 column (4.6 × 250 mm, 5 μm). All analyses were performed in triplicate.

For phenylalanine analysis, 1.0 g of frozen yam sample (−80 °C) was homogenized with 5.0 mL of 15% methanol, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22-μm nylon membrane and transferred into a 1.0 mL light-protected vial for HPLC injection. The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile and water under the following gradient program: 0–5 min, 2% acetonitrile; 5–15 min, 2%–15%; 15–20 min, 15%; 20–25 min, 15%–2%. The column temperature was maintained at 25 °C, the injection volume was 10 μL, the flow rate was 1.0 mL/min, and detection was performed at 212 nm. Quantification was conducted using the external standard method.

For the quantification of p-coumaric acid, 1.0 g of frozen yam sample (−80 °C) was extracted with 5.0 mL of methanol and centrifuged at 12,000 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was passed through a 0.22 μm nylon filter and collected in a 1.0 mL light-protected vial. HPLC analysis was performed using a gradient elution program with acetonitrile (mobile phase A) and 0.2% phosphoric acid (mobile phase B), as follows: 0–8 min, 10%–20% A; 8–40 min, 20%–30% A. The column temperature was maintained at 25 °C, the injection volume was 10 μL, the flow rate was 1.0 mL/min, and detection was carried out at 310 nm. Quantification was achieved using the external standard method.

Primer design and qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from fresh-cut yam using a AFTMag Quick Complex Plant RNA Extraction Kit (Wuhan ABclonal Technology, Hubei, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) was performed using 2× Universal SYBR Green Fast qPCR Mix (RM21203, ABclonal) on a Archimed X4 qPCR detection system. Each experiment included three biological replicates and three technical replicates. Gene expression levels were analyzed by the 2 − ΔΔCT method, with samples at day 0 serving as the calibrator and Ubiquitin10 used as the internal reference gene (Wei et al., 2023). The primers used in qRT-PCR are listed in Table S1, some of which were designed according to previous research (Cheng et al., 2024).

Microstructure

The microstructure of yam slices was observed using a scanning electron microscope (JSM-6390LV, JEOL Ltd., Japan). The yam was cut to 3 × 3 × 2 mm, and fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C for 12 h, rinsed with phosphate buffer, and dehydrated in a graded ethanol series (30–100%). After critical point drying and gold sputter-coating, the specimens were examined at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 22.0 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality and homogeneity of variance were verified prior to analysis. Given the factorial design with repeated measurements over storage time, a two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with repeated measures was employed to evaluate the main effects of treatment, storage time, and their interaction. When significant differences were detected, Duncan's multiple range test was used for post-hoc comparisons at a significance level of P < 0.05. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent replicates. In addition to P-values, effect estimates (mean differences) and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for the post-hoc comparisons to better assess the magnitude and biological significance of treatment effects.

Results and discussions

Peroxidase (POD) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity

Peroxidase (POD) and superoxide dismutase (SOD) are pivotal enzymes in the browning and quality deterioration of fruits and vegetables. POD catalyzes the oxidation of phenolic compounds leading to pigment formation, while SOD plays a crucial role in the antioxidant system by scavenging superoxide radicals. As shown in Fig. 1, both POD and SOD activities decreased significantly with increasing HVEF voltage and treatment duration. The lowest enzyme activities were observed after 8 min of treatment at 30 kV, with POD and SOD residual activities reduced to 34.07% and 43.33% of the control group, respectively. The inactivation of both enzymes under HVEF can be attributed to structural modifications, including irreversible oxidative damage and potential reversible conformational changes, induced by reactive species (Zhang, Law, & Guo, 2022). Amino acid residues in these enzymes are susceptible to oxidation by hydroxyl radicals, superoxide anions, and other active oxygen species generated during HVEF exposure (Valdez-Miranda et al., 2024). For POD, oxidation of susceptible amino acid residues, particularly those at or near the heme active site, can disrupt the native conformation, while the heme cofactor itself is vulnerable to oxidative degradation. The four disulfide bridges that stabilize the HRP structure may also be affected by radical-mediated cleavage. Similarly, SOD activity loss may stem from the oxidative degradation of its metal cofactor center and the disruption of its essential histidine residues. The synergistic effects of ion bombardment and free radical attack during HVEF treatment likely accelerate these structural alterations. The synergistic effects of ion bombardment and free radical attack during HVEF treatment likely accelerate these structural alterations (Shi et al., 2026). Notably, low-intensity HVEF treatments transiently enhanced both POD and SOD activities. This phenomenon may be explained by two mechanisms: mild membrane disruption facilitating enzyme release, and the activation of cellular defense responses that upregulate antioxidant enzymes under sublethal stress (Zhu et al., 2024). However, extended exposure to intense HVEF ultimately overwhelms these compensatory mechanisms. Based on the comprehensive inactivation efficacy, the treatment condition of 30 kV for 8 min was selected for subsequent experiments.

The effects of different treatments on residual enzyme activity.

The inactivation of POD and SOD by UV-C irradiation exhibited a clear dependence on both treatment time and power intensity. As illustrated in Fig. 1, prolonged exposure and higher UV-C power led to a progressive reduction in the activities of both enzymes, with the most pronounced inactivation observed at 68 W after 120 min of treatment. This observation aligns with the findings of Sampedro and Fan (2014), who reported superior efficacy of UV-C over visible light in inactivating polyphenol oxidase, attributing the effect to its higher photon energy. The primary mechanism underlying enzyme inactivation by UV-C is believed to be direct photochemical damage. The high-energy UV-C photons are absorbed by aromatic amino acid residues (such as tryptophan and tyrosine) and peptide bonds within the enzyme's structure, leading to photo-oxidation and subsequent disruption of the protein's three-dimensional conformation (Lante, Tinello, & Nicoletto, 2016). This structural alteration can result in the loss of catalytic activity and the possible formation of protein aggregates. Furthermore, UV-C irradiation can indirectly promote enzyme inactivation via the generation of ROS, which inflict oxidative damage on amino acid side chains and critical functional groups at the enzyme's active site (Pehlivan Karakas, Sahin, Ucar Turker, & Verma, 2022). It is noteworthy, however, that under low-dose UV-C conditions (e.g., 38 W, short duration), a transient activation or increase in SOD activity was occasionally detected. This phenomenon may be attributed to a mild oxidative stress response mounted by the plant tissue, which stimulates the synthesis or activation of antioxidant enzymes as a defensive mechanism (Teoh, Lasekan, Adzahan, & Hashim, 2016). Nonetheless, extended or intense UV-C exposure ultimately overwhelms this compensatory response, leading to significant and irreversible enzyme inactivation. Consequently, the treatment condition of 68 W for 120 min was selected for subsequent experiments as it achieved the most effective suppression of POD and SOD activities.

Exploration of the blunting mechanism of POD and SOD

UV–vis spectrum analysis

UV–vis spectral analysis revealed distinct structural perturbations in both POD and SOD following HVEF, UV, and combined treatments. As shown in Fig. 2 (a-b), the characteristic Soret absorption of POD at 402 nm, originating from the heme cofactor, maintained its position but exhibited progressively diminished intensity with increasing treatment duration across all three treatment conditions. This decrease in Soret band intensity without peak shift suggests possible oxidative modifications to the porphyrin environment or alterations in the planar geometry of the heme group, as previously reported for heme proteins under oxidative stress (Guo et al., 2022), although direct chemical evidence for heme oxidation (e.g., mass spectrometric analysis of porphyrin derivatives) was not obtained in the present study. Concurrently, the visible absorption peak of SOD at 550 nm, corresponding to the charge-transfer transition in the copper‑zinc cluster at the active site, demonstrated a parallel reduction in absorbance. This decrease implies the disruption of the metal coordination geometry, potentially through oxidation of the essential histidine residues or displacement of the metal ions within the catalytic center (Lin et al., 2013). The spectral changes observed in both enzymes can be attributed to the differential yet complementary mechanisms of the applied treatments. UV-C irradiation primarily induces direct photochemical damage through absorption by aromatic amino acids and peptide bonds, leading to electron excitation and subsequent oxidative reactions (Teng et al., 2023). In contrast, HVEF generates reactive oxygen species through corona discharge and ion bombardment, promoting oxidation of susceptible amino acid side chains (Zhang et al., 2022). The combined UV-HVEF treatment exhibited the most pronounced spectral alterations, suggesting a synergistic effect where UV-C-induced photosensitization enhances the enzyme's vulnerability to subsequent electrochemical oxidation. This combination likely accelerates the degradation of both the heme environment in POD and the metal cluster in SOD through simultaneous photochemical and electrochemical pathways. Notably, the absence of peak shifts in both enzymes' spectra suggests that the treatments did not induce major alterations in the primary coordination spheres, but rather caused gradual degradation of the chromophores through cumulative oxidative damage. This interpretation aligns with previous studies reporting that non-thermal technologies can induce progressive structural modifications in enzymes without complete unfolding. The correlation between spectral changes and enzymatic activity loss further supports that the functional deterioration stems from subtle but critical alterations in the active site microenvironments of both POD and SOD.

The effect of different treatments on POD and SOD spatial conformation.

Fluorescence spectroscopy analysis

Intrinsic fluorescence spectroscopy provides valuable insights into conformational dynamics of enzymes by monitoring changes in the microenvironment surrounding tryptophan residues. As shown in Fig. 2 (c-d), the fluorescence emission spectrum of POD excited at 293 nm exhibited maximum intensity at 320 nm. Following treatments with HVEF, UV, and their combination, the fluorescence intensity increased progressively with treatment duration, without significant spectral shifts. This enhancement suggests structural unfolding that exposes buried tryptophan residues to a more hydrophilic environment (Wang et al., 2017). The absence of peak shifts indicates that while the tertiary structure was perturbed, no major conformational reorganization occurred that would alter the polarity around the chromophores (Pellicer & Gómez-López, 2017).

Similar fluorescence patterns were observed for SOD, though with varying degrees of response to different treatments. The combined UV-HVEF treatment produced the most pronounced fluorescence enhancement in both enzymes, indicating synergistic structural disruption. This effect can be attributed to complementary mechanisms: UV-C directly excites aromatic residues leading to photo-oxidation, while HVEF generates reactive species that promote oxidative modification of amino acid side chains (Guo et al., 2022; Lante et al., 2016; Yang et al., 2024). The fluorescence changes correlate well with the enzymatic activity loss, suggesting that structural unfolding and active site disruption represent key inactivation pathways (Wang et al., 2021). The observed fluorescence enhancement may be explained by two interconnected mechanisms (Han, Cheng, & Sun, 2019). The unfolding of enzyme structures exposes previously buried tryptophan residues to the solvent environment. In addition, the increased distance between tryptophan residues and heme groups in HRP reduces resonance energy transfer, diminishing the quenching effect. This interpretation is supported by previous research demonstrating that HVEF treatments can induce tryptophan hydroxylation, which further enhances fluorescence through extended conjugation. These structural alterations ultimately compromise enzyme functionality by disrupting the precise spatial arrangement required for catalytic activity.

Circular dichroism analysis

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy provides direct evidence of changes in protein secondary structure. As shown in Fig. 3, the CD spectra of native POD and SOD exhibited characteristic negative peaks around 204 nm and 227 nm, corresponding to their α-helical content. Following HVEF, UV-C, or combined UV-HVEF treatment, the mean residue ellipticity of these peaks progressively decreased, indicating a gradual loss of ordered secondary structure. Quantitative deconvolution of the CD spectra revealed that HVEF and UV-HVEF treatments caused a marked reduction in α-helical content in both enzymes (e.g., POD α-helix decreased from 76.2% to 18.1% and 23.9% under HVEF and UV-HVEF, respectively), accompanied by substantial increases in β-sheet, β-turn, and random coil proportions for POD, while SOD exhibited a decrease in α-helix (from 15.6% to 6.8% and 8.2%, respectively) with a concurrent increase in β-sheet, but with β-turn and random coil remaining relatively stable. In contrast, UV treatment alone induced only moderate secondary structure changes in both enzymes (α-helix remained above 60% for POD and above 12% for SOD). These structural transitions suggest a shift from ordered to more disordered conformations, likely resulting from the disruption of hydrogen bonds and other non-covalent interactions that maintain native folding.

Changes in the secondary structure of POD and SOD after different treatments.

A similar trend was observed for SOD, where treatment-induced alterations in its CD profile reflected conformational destabilization. The combined UV-HVEF treatment exerted the most pronounced effect on both enzymes, indicating a synergistic action that enhances structural disruption. This can be attributed to the complementary mechanisms of photo-oxidation and radical-mediated oxidation, which collectively perturb the delicate balance of forces stabilizing secondary structure (Han et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2022). The loss of α-helical content correlates with the decline in enzymatic activity, supporting the conclusion that structural unfolding and active-site distortion are key factors in the inactivation of POD and SOD under these non-thermal treatments. While the deconvolution fitting quality was carefully assessed, HT voltage and MRE values were not recorded during original data acquisition, as this study focused on relative rather than absolute secondary structure comparisons. Nevertheless, these parameters are essential for CD reproducibility and quantitative rigor, reflecting sample transparency and intrinsic protein ellipticity, respectively. This limitation will be addressed in future work through comprehensive reporting of HT voltage, MRE calculations, and deconvolution quality metrics to facilitate cross-study comparability and more precise structural analysis.

Effects of different treatments on the color and anthocyanin content in fresh-cut purple yams

The appearance, color, and anthocyanin content of fresh-cut purple yam slices after 8 days of storage under different treatments (UV, HVEF, and UV-HVEF) are presented in Fig. 4. The untreated control (CK) showed severe browning (Fig. 4a), while UV-treated samples exhibited obvious browning, likely because UV irradiation alone, though capable of inactivating surface microorganisms and some oxidative enzymes, may not sufficiently penetrate the tissue or may cause initial oxidative stress that accelerates browning if the dose is sublethal (Guan et al., 2016). In contrast, HVEF-treated samples displayed only slight browning at the edges, and the UV-HVEF combination resulted in no significant browning. This enhanced effect can be attributed to the synergistic inactivation of PPO and POD by the combined treatment, which suppress the oxidative browning cascade (Huang et al., 2017; Q. Wang, Zhang, & Sun, 2026). Although enzymatic browning and physicochemical quality were the main focuses of this work, microbial growth (e.g., total aerobic bacteria, yeasts, molds, and coliforms) is also critical for quality and safety of refrigerated fresh-cut products. Future studies should include comprehensive microbial analyses to evaluate the practical shelf-life and safety implications of UV-C and HVEF treatments.

Effect of different treatments on appearance (a), color (b-d), anthocyanin (e), TPC (f) and TFC (g) in fresh-cut yam during storage.

As shown in Fig. 4b, the L* value (brightness) of fresh-cut yam decreased during storage. Compared with the CK group, UV treatment did not significantly affect the L* value after 6 days, whereas HVEF and UV-HVEF treatments significantly retarded the decline in L*. The a* value exhibited a similar decreasing trend across all treatments, with UV-HVEF-treated yams maintaining higher a* values than the control (Fig. 4c). The b* value, which reflects the degree of yellowing, was significantly (P < 0.05) suppressed by all treatments (Fig. 4d). Among the treatments, UV-HVEF preserved the highest L* and lowest b* values. These results indicate that UV-HVEF effectively inhibit the enzymatic browning responsible for color deterioration, possibly through the disruption of cell membranes and the inactivation of tyrosinase and PPO, as previously observed in UV and HVEF-treated brown Agaricus bisporus and fresh-cut taro (Lei et al., 2018; Zhu et al., 2025). The free oxygen radicals generated during HVEF treatment also possess mild bleaching ability, which may further contribute to color maintenance.

Anthocyanin content decreased progressively during storage (Fig. 4e). After 8 days, the anthocyanin contents of CK, UV, HVEF, and UV-HVEF samples were 1.36 ± 0.06, 1.44 ± 0.04, 1.56 ± 0.04, and 1.73 ± 0.05 mg/100 g, respectively. The higher retention in HVEF and especially UV-HVEF samples suggests that these treatments reduce oxidative degradation of anthocyanins. This protection likely arises from the inactivation of anthocyanin-degrading enzymes (such as polyphenol oxidase) and the scavenging of reactive oxygen species, thereby stabilizing these pigments under oxidative stress (Ferreira et al., 2021).

Effects of different treatments on the TPC and TFC in fresh-cut purple yams

Phenolic compounds are crucial secondary metabolites that help preserve food quality by counteracting free radical activity in fruits and vegetables. As shown in Fig. 4f, the total phenolic content (TPC) in fresh-cut yam slices decreased by 19.47–61.23% after 8 days of storage. Among all treatments, the UV-HVEF sample exhibited the highest retention of phenolic substances, followed by HVEF and UV alone. This enhanced retention, despite downregulated PAL and C4H expression, indicates that the preservation of phenolic compounds is primarily due to reduced oxidative degradation and the release of bound phenolic compounds from cellular compartments, rather than enhanced de novo biosynthesis (Guo, Zhang, Mujumdar, & Rui, 2025). Specifically, UV irradiation alone may induce mild oxidative stress, stimulating phenolic biosynthesis to a certain extent, but its effect is limited due to possible photo-degradation of some phenolics (Rutigliano et al., 2025). HVEF treatment, on the other hand, can increase extracellular adenosine triphosphate content and accelerate sugar utilization, thereby promoting the synthesis of phenolic compounds in yam (Zhang et al., 2025). More importantly, the etching effect of reactive species generated during HVEF and UV-HVEF treatments causes controlled degradation of cell membranes, increasing surface area and releasing bound phenolic compounds, which contributes to the higher extractable phenolic content. The combined UV-HVEF treatment synergistically enhances these effects, leading to the highest phenolic retention. Similar observations have been reported by He et al. (2024).

Flavonoids, which possess a flexible phenolic aldehyde structure, exhibit anti-mutagenic, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties. Preserving these compounds is essential for maintaining chemical stability in processed foods (Kamiloglu, Tomas, Ozdal, & Capanoglu, 2021). The TFC determination in Fig. 4g showed a significant decrease after 8 days of storage compared to fresh yam samples. Relative to fresh samples (day 0), the TFC of CK, UV, HVEF, and UV-HVEF samples decreased by 51.09%, 44.48%, 43.23%, and 29.15%, respectively. The superior retention in treated samples, especially UV-HVEF, can be attributed to reduced oxidative degradation (due to POD inactivation) and the release of membrane-bound flavonoids, rather than enhanced biosynthesis (Lei et al., 2018; Valdez-Miranda et al., 2024). Some flavonoids and phenolic compounds are initially bound to cell membranes; the high-energy intensity of HVEF and UV-HVEF treatments liberates these bound flavonoids, making them more extractable and detectable (Zhu et al., 2024). The inactivation of POD by UV-HVEF further minimizes the oxidative consumption of these released flavonoids (Shen et al., 2023). In contrast, UV treatment alone, while still effective, showed less flavonoid retention than HVEF or UV-HVEF, likely because its energy transfer is less efficient in disrupting membrane-bound flavonoid complexes.

Effects of different treatments on the POD and SOD activity in fresh-cut purple yams

SOD is an important antioxidant enzyme in plants that prevents cell senescence induced by oxidative stress. As shown in Fig. 5a, SOD activity in all samples increased during the first part of storage and then decreased over the 8-day period. However, compared with the CK group, samples treated with HVEF and UV-HVEF exhibited lower SOD activity throughout storage. This reduction can be attributed to the ability of HVEF and its combination with UV to directly inactivate the enzyme via reactive species generated during treatment, as well as to alleviate the overall oxidative burden on the tissue (Lante et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2026). In contrast, UV treatment alone showed a less pronounced effect, possibly because its limited penetration depth and dose-dependent oxidative stress may transiently stimulate SOD expression before eventual inactivation (Guan et al., 2016).

Effect of different treatments on SOD (a), POD (b) activity, phenylalanine (c) and p-coumaric acid (d) in fresh-cut yam during storage.

Peroxidase (POD) can utilize hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) generated during respiratory metabolism to catalyze the oxidation of phenolic compounds, producing quinones that polymerize into brown pigments, thereby contributing to enzymatic browning and loss of nutritional value in stored yams (Zhou et al., 2024). Fig. 5b shows that compared with the CK, both UV and HVEF treatments reduced POD activity during storage, with the UV-HVEF combination achieving the most effective suppression. This enhanced effect likely stems from the synergistic action of UV oxidation and HVEF-induced radical attack, which together alter enzyme conformation and reduce the reaction rate between POD and its substrates (Rutigliano et al., 2025; Valdez-Miranda et al., 2024). Furthermore, it has been reported that increased POD activity is associated with oxidative stress and membrane damage (Xin et al., 2024). Therefore, the lower POD activity observed in HVEF and UV-HVEF treated samples reflects reduced oxidative stress and better membrane integrity, which is consistent with the relative conductivity results and agrees with previous findings on HVEF-treated pomegranate fruit (Shi et al., 2026).

Effects of different treatments on the phenylalanine and p-coumaric acid in fresh-cut purple yams

As depicted in Fig. 5(c-d), the phenylalanine content of all samples decreased with prolonged storage time. Notably, on day 8, the UV, HVEF, and UV-HVEF groups exhibited phenylalanine contents that were 2.13 ± 0.60, 3.42 ± 0.55, and 4.56 ± 0.28 mg/kg higher, respectively, and p-coumaric acid contents that were 23.96%, 41.09%, and 54.56% higher, respectively, than those in the CK samples. This preservation of phenylalanine and p-coumaric acid under UV-HVEF treatment, despite the downregulation of PAL and C4H gene expression (as shown in Fig. 6b–c), suggests that the accumulation of these metabolites is primarily due to reduced oxidative degradation and the release of bound phenolic compounds from cellular compartments, rather than enhanced de novo biosynthesis (Wang et al., 2026). The combined UV-HVEF treatment showed the most pronounced effect, indicating a synergistic protection of phenolic acid pools. Consequently, the elevated levels of phenylalanine and p-coumaric acid provide substrates for residual POD activity. However, since POD is largely inactivated by the treatments, the oxidation of these phenolics is minimized, thereby suppressing browning during storage (He et al., 2024). The present data indicate that UV-HVEF affects the early segment of the phenylpropanoid pathway, namely the conversion of phenylalanine to p-coumaric acid. However, complete elucidation of its regulatory effects on downstream branches involving intermediates such as caffeic acid, ferulic acid, and chlorogenic acid will require more comprehensive metabolomic profiling in future studies.

Effect of different treatment on gene expression levels of POD (a), PAL (b) and C4H (c) in fresh-cut yam during storage.

Effects of different treatments on the gene expression levels of oxidant enzymes and phenylpropanoid pathway connected enzymes in fresh-cut purple yams

The gene expression profiles of POD, PAL, and C4H in fresh-cut yam during storage are shown in Fig. 6. As presented in Fig. 6a, POD gene expression in both treated and control groups increased sharply at the early stage of storage and then declined. Notably, HVEF and UV-HVEF treatments maintained significantly lower (P < 0.05) POD expression levels compared with the CK group throughout storage. This suppression can be attributed to the direct inactivation of the enzyme and the reduction of oxidative stress by these treatments, which alleviates the need for transcriptional upregulation of POD as a defense response (Zhang, Yang, Li, Chiang, & Fu, 2021). In contrast, UV treatment alone showed a weaker effect, possibly due to its limited penetration or dose-dependent stress that transiently induces POD expression (Guan et al., 2023).

Fig. 6b shows that UV, HVEF, and UV-HVEF treatments significantly (P < 0.05) suppressed PAL gene expression during storage. PAL expression increased progressively in all samples, but the UV-HVEF treated group accumulated PAL transcripts at a consistently lower level than the control. PAL encodes phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, the key enzyme in the phenylpropanoid pathway (Bai et al., 2022). The observed suppression indicates that these non-thermal treatments reduce the abiotic stress burden on yam tissues, thereby downregulating the expression of stress-responsive genes (Guo, Zhao, Ma, Zhang, & Wang, 2021). The combined UV-HVEF treatment exhibited the strongest inhibitory effect, suggesting a synergistic reduction of stress signals.

Similarly, as shown in Fig. 6c, C4H gene expression increased in all groups during storage. HVEF and UV-HVEF treatments significantly (P < 0.05) inhibited its expression, maintaining lower levels than the control throughout the storage period. C4H (cinnamate-4-hydroxylase) is another key enzyme in phenylpropanoid metabolism, and its downregulation further confirms that HVEF and UV-HVEF treatments alleviate the stress-induced activation of the entire biosynthetic pathway leading to phenolic compounds (Cheng et al., 2024). The lower expression of PAL and C4H in treated samples correlates well with the reduced oxidative stress and membrane damage, which in turn limits the stress-induced activation of the phenylpropanoid pathway, consistent with the preservation of phenolic compounds through reduced degradation rather than enhanced synthesis.

Effects of different treatments on the microstructure in fresh-cut purple yams

The microstructure of fresh-cut purple yam slices subjected to different treatments was examined using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). As shown in Fig. 7a, the control (CK) sample exhibited intact and uniformly distributed starch granules, indicating a well-preserved cellular structure. In contrast, HVEF treatment caused noticeable damage to the starch granules, as indicated by the red circles in the figure, suggesting that the reactive species (e.g., ozone, free radicals) and ion bombardment generated during HVEF exposure physically etched the granule surface (Cao et al., 2026). UV treatment alone did not induce significant alterations in starch granule morphology, implying that UV-C irradiation primarily affects surface microorganisms and soluble enzymes rather than the compact starch structure (Ferreira et al., 2021). Interestingly, the combined UV-HVEF treatment led to pronounced morphological changes, including irregular granule shapes and heterogeneous size distribution. This synergistic effect may arise from UV-C-induced weakening of the granule surface, which renders it more susceptible to subsequent electrochemical etching by HVEF-generated reactive species. These structural modifications, particularly the disruption of starch granules by HVEF and UV-HVEF, could facilitate water evaporation and potentially influence textural properties during storage (Guo et al., 2025).

The microstructure (a), PCA (b), correlation analysis (c) and suggested mechanism (d) in fresh-cut yam slices after treatments.

Multivariate statistical analysis of browning-related characteristics in fresh-cut yam slices

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to evaluate sample variations. As shown in Fig. 7b, PC1 and PC2 accounted for 57.8% and 24.5% of the total variance, respectively, with a cumulative contribution of 82.3%, indicating that the two principal components effectively captured the characteristic differences among sample groups. CK and treated samples clustered in distinct regions during storage, reflecting significant differences in browning-related characteristics.

To analyze the link among browning system-related characteristics, correlation analysis was conducted to further identify characteristics associated with quality maintenance and browning after treatments. As shown in Fig. 7c, L* of fresh-cut yam slice was positively correlated with TFC, TPC, and phenylalanine, but negatively correlated with SOD, POD, gene expression levels of POD, PAL, and C4H. This correlation analysis suggests that higher brightness (L*) in treated yams is associated with the preservation of phenolic compounds and flavonoids, which are linked to antioxidant capacity and browning inhibition. Conversely, the negative correlations indicate that higher activities of SOD and POD, along with upregulated expression of key genes in the phenylpropanoid pathway (POD, PAL, C4H), are associated with darker color and more severe browning. Therefore, effective treatments such as HVEF and UV-HVEF likely maintain yam quality by suppressing oxidative enzyme activities and their gene expression, while preserving higher levels of total phenolics, flavonoids, and phenylalanine, thereby reducing browning during storage.

Suggested mechanism

Fig. 7d illustrates the proposed mechanism of HVEF, UV-C, and combined UV-HVEF treatments in regulating the browning inhibition and quality preservation of fresh-cut purple yam during storage. HVEF generates ozone (O3) and ROS including hydroxyl radicals (•OH), superoxide anions (O2−•), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) (Shi et al., 2026). These reactive species attack the active sites of POD and SOD, leading to chemical modification of amino acid residues and disruption of essential cofactors (heme in POD and copper‑zinc cluster in SOD). UV-C irradiation complements this mechanism by inducing direct photochemical damage to aromatic amino acid residues (Ferreira et al., 2021). The combined UV-HVEF treatment exhibits a synergistic effect: UV-C-induced photosensitization enhances the enzyme's vulnerability to subsequent electrochemical oxidation by HVEF-generated ROS, accelerating irreversible inactivation (Lei et al., 2018; Valdez-Miranda et al., 2024). Notably, low-intensity treatment may cause transient enzyme activation as a protective response, but prolonged exposure overwhelms this compensatory mechanism.

Furthermore, the preservation of phenolic compounds, flavonoids, phenylalanine, and p-coumaric acid under UV-HVEF treatment, despite the downregulation of PAL and C4H expression, suggests that the accumulation of these metabolites is primarily due to reduced oxidative degradation and the release of bound phenolic compounds from cellular compartments, rather than enhanced de novo biosynthesis (Ferreira et al., 2021; Shi et al., 2026). The etching effect of reactive particles on cell membranes releases bound phenolic compounds, further increasing their extractable content, while the inactivation of POD minimizes the oxidative consumption of these phenolics. The combined UV-HVEF treatment shows the strongest effect due to synergistic oxidative stress and enzyme inactivation. The downregulation of stress-responsive genes (POD, PAL, and C4H) further supports the conclusion that UV-HVEF alleviates oxidative stress, thereby reducing the need for transcriptional activation of defense-related pathways. While the existing data demonstrate consistent associations between these treatments, enzyme inactivation, phenolic preservation, and browning inhibition, the causal relationships remain to be fully established (Sampedro & Fan, 2014; Sui et al., 2023). Future investigations employing ROS scavengers, inhibitors of ozone-mediated oxidation, targeted gene silencing, and electron paramagnetic resonance or fluorescent probe-based ROS detection are necessary to confirm the involvement of oxidative stress and phenylpropanoid pathway regulation in the observed preservation effects, as direct ROS quantification was not carried out in the present study despite strong spectroscopic evidence of oxidative modifications.

Conclusions

This study systematically investigated the effects of UV-C, HVEF, and their combination (UV-HVEF) on browning inhibition and quality preservation of fresh-cut purple yam. Among all treatments, UV-HVEF exhibited the strongest synergistic effect, as reflected by the highest L values, the lowest browning degree, and the best retention of anthocyanins after 8 days of storage. Mechanistically, UV-HVEF treatment induced significant conformational disruptions in POD and SOD, including protein unfolding and a transition from α-helix to β-sheet, which collectively contributed to enzyme inactivation. Notably, while UV-HVEF elevated the levels of total phenolics, flavonoids, phenylalanine, and p-coumaric acid, it concurrently downregulated the expression of POD, PAL, and C4H, suggesting that phenolic preservation was primarily achieved through reduced oxidative degradation and release of bound forms, rather than de novo biosynthesis. Correlation analysis further confirmed that L* values were positively correlated with phenolic content and negatively correlated with enzyme activities and their corresponding gene expression levels. In addition, UV-HVEF maintained membrane integrity and alleviated oxidative stress, as indicated by lower relative electrolyte leakage and reduced ROS accumulation. Unlike UV or HVEF alone, the combined treatment consistently outperformed across all evaluated parameters. While the present study did not assess microbiological quality or conduct sensory evaluation, future investigations should incorporate these aspects to more comprehensively evaluate the practical shelf-life and consumer acceptability of UV-HVEF treated fresh-cut yam. Overall, UV-HVEF represents a promising non-thermal preservation strategy that effectively inactivates browning-related enzymes via multi-level structural disruption, preserves phenolic antioxidants, suppresses stress-responsive gene expression, and maintains superior color and nutritional quality, offering a sustainable alternative for postharvest preservation of root vegetables.
