Potential
Biodiversity Footprint Reductions from Intended
Shifts to Plant-Based Meals

Hanzhong Zheng, Koen J. J. Kuipers, Selwyn Hoeks, Mark A. J. Huijbregts, Nicole Neufingerl, Elizabeth H. Zandstra, Sarah Sim

Abstract

Adopting plant-based diets can mitigate
biodiversity loss by reducing
the demand for land required for feed and livestock. Yet, most assessments
rely on hypothetical dietary scenarios that potentially overestimate
biodiversity gains. We conducted a large-scale consumer survey across
three countries (NLD, GBR, and USA) and estimated the biodiversity
footprints of current and intended future diets using the loss of
mean species abundance (MSA-loss) and the loss of global species richness
(RSR-loss) as indicators. Over half of the respondents expressed no
intention to reduce meat or dairy consumption. Among those intending
to replace meat with plant-based alternatives, biodiversity footprints
based on MSA-loss associated with the consumption of meat and plant-based
alternatives in main meals would decrease by up to 34% on average.
When considering all respondents, the average reduction in MSA-loss
from meat and meat alternatives in the three countries would be 13%.
We found similar results for current and intended future consumption
of dairy and their plant-based alternatives. RSR-loss showed the same
patterns as MSA-loss, although dairy alternatives would contribute
a large share of the total biodiversity footprint based on RSR-loss
in the USA. Our findings show that intended dietary shifts may not
match hypothetical dietary scenarios and that achieving sustainable
dietary shifts to reduce biodiversity loss remains a considerable
challenge.

Introduction

The
global food system accounts for 33% of global anthropogenic
greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, 40% of land use, and 70% of freshwater
consumption.
−

 Land use for crops and pasture is a major driver of biodiversity
loss. The amount of cropland and pasture
required for global food production is determined by population size,
agricultural productivity, supply chain efficiencies and dietary patterns.
,
 Indeed, Kastner et al. found that dietary changes from cereals to
meat and dairy in rapidly developing regions like Eastern Asia, drive
land demand for food production more than population growth. These dietary changes run counter to those recommended
for biodiversity loss mitigation. For instance, the EAT-Lancet Commission
has proposed that a key priority for wealthier countries is to substantially
reduce meat and dairy consumption.

Previous studies comparing different dietary patterns (e.g., vegan,
vegetarian, meat-eater) have shown the biodiversity impact of a vegan
diet is on average 65% lower compared to a diet that contains high
levels of animal-based products, highlighting the lower biodiversity
impact of plant-based diets. Adopting
plant-forward diets, which emphasize a
substantial reduction in animal product consumption while increasing
plant-based food consumption, has also been shown to reduce a range
of other environmental impacts, such as GHG emissions and water use.
−

 Traditional (e.g., tofu and tempeh) and novel (e.g., meat alternatives
that mimic the taste and texture of animal meat) plant-based meat
alternatives are becoming increasingly popular among those seeking
to adopt more plant-based ways of eating.
−

Insights
into the environmental benefits of dietary shifts have
mostly been obtained through assessment of dietary scenarios focused
on assumption-driven dietary changes or modifications needed to achieve
national dietary guidelines.
,
 Such modeled scenarios
may not accurately reflect real-world individual intentions to shift
diets. For example, the extent to which meat-eaters may shift toward
increased consumption of plant-based alternatives and the corresponding
biodiversity impacts has not yet been investigated. Hence, there is a need for comprehensive analyses of individuals’
intention to engage in dietary behavior change to provide realistic
data for environmental modeling of dietary transitions. Quantifying
potential biodiversity loss mitigation from such dietary change is
essential for developing targeted interventions that encourage more
sustainable consumption patterns and population-wide dietary shifts.

Here, we advance the present knowledge by using individual dietary
change intention survey data with biodiversity footprint assessment,
providing a novel perspective on the effects of intended dietary transitions.
To this end, we conducted a survey across The Netherlands (NLD), United
Kingdom (GBR), and United States (USA) to quantify the current and
intended near future (i.e., within the next six months) intake of
meat (e.g., beef, pork, and poultry) and meat alternatives (e.g.,
vegetables, tofu, and plant-based meats that mimic the taste and texture
of animal meat) during the main meal of the day, as well as current
and intended near future intake of dairy (e.g., milk, yoghurt, and
cheese) and dairy alternatives (e.g., soymilk, plant-based yoghurt,
and plant-based cheese) during the day. We used the Food and Agriculture
Biomass Input-Output (FABIO) model to
trace both primary and processed agricultural commodity supply chains
and quantify biodiversity footprints per kilogram of food commodity
consumed in each country. FABIO was selected because it provides high
level of agricultural commodity detail and because it considers physical
flows that avoid the price distortions present in monetary input-output
databases. Biodiversity footprints were
assessed using two complementary indicators: mean species abundance
loss (MSA-loss) to evaluate impacts on local ecosystem intactness, and relative species richness loss (RSR-loss)
as a supplementary indicator for global species extinction footprints. We then combined the survey data and biodiversity
footprints per food commodity to quantify the respondents’
yearly total biodiversity footprint of current and intended near future
intake of meat and alternatives as well as of current and intended
near future intake of dairy and dairy alternatives. This study goes
beyond hypothetical dietary scenarios by quantifying biodiversity
footprints of individual dietary preferences and intended changes.

Methods

An online survey was
conducted in the NLD, GBR and USA to collect
data on respondents’ current and intended near future intake
of meat and meat alternatives during the main meal of the day as well
as current and intended near future intake of dairy and dairy alternatives
during the day (survey is provided in Text Section S1). We used FABIO combined with biodiversity indicators (i.e.,
MSA-loss and RSR-loss, see RSR-loss results in SI) to quantify the land-use related biodiversity footprint
per kg of food commodity consumed in each country. These values were
applied to survey-based individual dietary intake data to estimate
annual biodiversity footprints related to the current and intended
near future intake of meat and meat alternatives, as well as the current
and intended near future intake of dairy and dairy alternatives. We
then quantified the change in biodiversity footprints as the difference
between current and intended future intake of meat and meat alternatives
or dairy and dairy alternatives. Finally, we explored variations in
the change in biodiversity footprints across the three countries,
as well as demographic differences within each country using multivariate
analysis.

Survey

The online survey was conducted in November
2023 across 2436 respondents in The Netherlands, 2079 in the GBR and
2058 in the USA. Recruitment was guided by soft quotas to obtain broadly
comparable samples across countries, with approximately equal distributions
across gender (male and female), three age groups (18–33, 34–49,
50–65 years), education level (low and high), and food environment
for urban/rural (see Text Section S2). Respondents were asked about
current and intended near future intake of meat (e.g., beef, pork,
and poultry) and meat alternatives (i.e., plant-based meat alternatives
that mimic the taste and texture of meat, tofu or tempeh, pulses,
nuts or seeds, vegetables, cheese or dairy items, eggs, fish or seafood)
during the main meal of the day. In addition, respondents were asked
about current and intended near future intake of dairy (i.e., milk
and dairy drinks, yoghurt/quark, cheese, ice cream, dairy desserts)
and dairy alternatives (e.g., plant-based alternatives for milk and
dairy drinks, yoghurt/quark, cheese, ice cream, dairy desserts) (see Table S1 for a list of products covered in survey).
Respondents were recruited from an online consumer panel through a
market research agency (Essensor B.V.). Panel members had previously
consented to participate in survey research and were invited to complete
the survey via a web-based interface. The estimated time required
to complete the survey was about 15 min.

Respondents were classified
into four dietary groups based on self-reported dietary restrictions
toward the consumption of animal-based foods: (1) meat-eater, i.e.,
no restriction toward meat consumption, (2) pescatarian, i.e., excluding
meat from the diet but not excluding fish, (3) vegetarian, i.e., excluding
meat and fish from the diet, but not excluding dairy and eggs, and
(4) vegan, i.e., excluding meat, fish, dairy and eggs from the diet.
Meat-eating respondents were asked questions about their consumption,
reduction and replacement of both meat and meat alternatives as well
as dairy and dairy alternatives, while pescatarian and vegetarian
respondents were asked questions related to dairy only. If respondents
were identified as vegan, they did not proceed with dietary related
questions. Their survey responses were not in scope of our analysis
(detailed in the following section)
since the purpose of the survey was to elicit information on respondents’
intention to shift from meat intake to meat alternatives as well as
dairy intake to dairy alternatives. The survey collected information
on respondents’ (1) current consumption (meat and meat alternatives
and dairy and dairy alternatives), (2) stages of changewhether
they intend to or are already reducing their meat or dairy consumption
(respondents were classified into three stages of dietary change:
no intention to reduce consumption, intention to reduce consumption
within the next six months, and having already initiated reductions;
see Text Section S3), (3) dietary approach
for reducing meat/dairy, (4) barriers to reducing meat/dairy.

Annual Intake (kg per Food Product per Person per
Year)

To evaluate respondents’ current meat and meat
alternatives consumption, we assessed the frequency and the type of
meat that was consumed during the main meal of the day over the past
month. The main meal was defined as the biggest meal of the day, often
the warm meal, consumed for lunch or dinner. In the survey, we provided
six pictures to enable respondents to best estimate their meat portion
size (i.e., the amount of meat consumed per main meal): very small
(21 g), small (36 g), medium-small (75 g), medium-large (113 g), large
(172 g) and very large (277 g).

To investigate respondents’ approaches to (further) reduce
meat intake, the survey assessed whether, and to what extent, respondents
intend to reduce the consumption of meat during their main meal, and
whether, and to what extent, they intend to replace it with meat alternatives
in the near future. A similar framework was applied to dairy and dairy
alternatives.

To quantify respondents’ current and intended
future food
consumption, we converted the reported frequencies for meat and meat
alternatives, and for dairy and dairy alternatives into the yearly
total number of portions per individual (see Text Section S4). For meat, the corresponding mass (kg per person
per year) was estimated for each respondent by multiplying the yearly
total number of portions with the respondents’ reported habitual
portion size for meat. For meat alternatives, dairy and dairy alternatives,
the corresponding mass (kg per person per year) was estimated by multiplying
the yearly total number of portions with a standard portion size for
each product category (see Table S1) and
a correction factor. The standard portion sizes were derived based
on reference portion sizes provided by national health institutes
or dietetic authorities in the three countries.
−

 The aim of
the correction factor was to account for variations in portion sizes
across individuals. We assumed that respondents who reported a relatively
big portion size for meat, would also replace meat with a relatively
big portion of an alternative product and vice versa, and that they
would also consume relatively bigger portions of dairy and dairy alternatives.
Therefore, for each respondent, an individual correction factor was
calculated as the ratio of the respondents’ habitual reported
portion size of meat and the standard portion size for meat (i.e.,
100 g, see Table S1).

Survey respondents
were excluded if: (1) they selected “Other”
for gender (n = 12), because our demographic analysis
compared female and male groups; (2) they did not complete the questions
required for classification into dietary groups (n = 430); (3) their answers produced estimates of daily food intake
that were deemed contradictory (e.g., respondents classified themselves
as meat-eaters, but indicated meat portions per year = 0 and meat
alternatives portion per year = 0, n = 21). A total
of 463 respondents were excluded, resulting in a final sample size
of 6110.

Biodiversity Footprints of Individual Meat, Dairy and Alternative
Intake

We used FABIO 2020 to quantify the biodiversity footprints
of both current and near future diets. We first estimate the global
land use footprints per food commodity in the NLD, GBR and USA (i.e.,
considering worldwide land use corresponding to the production of
food commodities consumed in NLD, GBR and USA). FABIO is a balanced
and physical unit multiregional input-output database, covering 123
agriculture commodities, to which all food products listed in our
survey could be mapped. Based on FAOSTAT Commodity Balance Sheets,
FABIO links production, processing, and trade flows to final consumption,
enabling land-use based biodiversity footprints to be traced along
global food supply chains. Feed use is represented through the allocation
of crop commodities to livestock sectors according to their feed requirements. FABIO land use footprints describe harvested
areas. Since the physical area of agricultural land, rather than the
area of harvested land drives biodiversity impacts, we converted the
area of harvested land per food commodity into physical land, based
on crop-and region-specific conversion ratios obtained from the Spatial
Production Allocation Model for year 2010.

To quantify biodiversity footprints, we linked the physical
land use footprints to an impact indicator for decline in ecosystem
intactness per unit of land use derived from the GLOBIO4 model, which
quantifies the loss in mean species abundance (MSA), distinguishing the biodiversity impacts on plants and vertebrates.
We calculated land-use related biodiversity impacts on local ecosystem
intactness responses as the average of plant and vertebrate MSA responses,
following Schipper et al. As a complementary
indicator, we also quantified biodiversity footprints using an indicator
of the relative loss in the total number of species of vertebrates
and plants (RSR-loss), to capture impacts on global species richness. We only consider the biodiversity footprint
of land-based food production (excluding fish farming). The biodiversity
footprints of each commodity consumed in the NLD, GBR and USA was
formulated as follows

Multivariate Analysis

We applied an Extreme Gradient
Boosting (XGBoost) machine learning algorithm to explore the extent
to which shifts in biodiversity footprints due to dietary change differ
between sociodemographic groups (based on age, gender, education,
food environment (urban/rural)) and dietary behavior (portion size,
annual number of portions consumed). XGBoost is a scalable and regularized
tree-based ensemble learning method that is particularly well-suited
for capturing nonlinear relationships and variable interactions. XGBoost is widely used in classification and
regression tasks for its high predictive accuracy, efficiency, and
ability to handle mixed-type variables and missing data. For tuning the hyperparameters we utilized the caret package (version 6.0–90) in combination with xgboost (version 1.7.11.1) in R (version
4.1.2).

First, an XGBoost classification analysis was used to
identify which sociodemographic variables contribute most to predicting
the probability of intention to shift diet. For each sociodemographic
variable (e.g., age), we compared the respective groups (e.g., 18–33,
34–49, and 50–65 age group) to determine which are most
inclined toward dietary change. Variable importance was quantified
by the gain in the accuracy of the model predictions.

Second,
an XGBoost regression analysis was applied to data from
the group of respondents intending to reduce their meat and dairy
consumption in the near future to quantify the potential average change
in biodiversity footprints per sociodemographic group. Variable importance
was quantified by the gain in the root-mean-square error (RMSE) when
excluding the variable.

Estimates
of biodiversity footprints based on the average current
annual intakes of meat and meat alternatives (during main meal), dairy,
and dairy alternatives per country are shown in . The per capita absolute value results for
each country indicate that the USA exhibits the highest biodiversity
footprint, with a mean MSA-loss•ha of 0.126 per person for
meat and meat alternatives, which is 2.6 and 3.5 times higher compared
to the NLD and GBR, respectively. This difference can be attributed
to the higher yearly consumption of animal meat products such as beef
(on average 40% more than NLD and GBR) and lamb (30% more than NLD
and GBR). Meat products significantly contribute (more than 90%) to
the total biodiversity footprint of meat and meat alternatives, with
beef being the dominant contributor among all meat products (b). Biodiversity
footprints were distributed across both domestic production and international
supply chains (a). For the USA, a large share of impacts originated from domestic
production, whereas footprints associated with consumption in the
NLD and GBR were more strongly linked to imports. Results based on
RSR-loss were broadly consistent with those obtained using MSA-loss,
with meat products accounting for the majority of biodiversity footprints
across all countries (Figure S1b). However,
the sourcing countries identified using RSR-loss indicated a greater
contribution from biodiversity-rich regions such as Brazil (Figure S1a,c). Biodiversity footprints of dairy
consumption are highest in the USA as well (c). This overall pattern was also observed
for RSR-loss, although dairy alternatives contributed a large share
of the total biodiversity footprint in the USA (Figure S1d).

To better understand the variation in the current
biodiversity
footprint across the three countries, we observed that respondents
who reported larger portion size also reported higher meat/dairy consumption
and consequently higher biodiversity footprints. On average, USA respondents
reported larger portion sizes of meat and alternatives (1.81 ±
0.85 times the standard portion size) compared to NLD (1.24 ±
0.71 times the standard portion size) and GBR (1.47 ± 0.72 times
the standard portion size) (Figure S2),
which explains the higher biodiversity footprint associated with meat
and meat alternatives consumption observed in the USA in addition
to the larger share of meat in their total intake of meat and meat
alternatives. Respondents with the highest current consumption of
meat, and therefore also the highest biodiversity footprints, are
least likely to intend to reduce their meat intake in the future (Figure S2). In contrast, respondents who indicated
that they had already reduced their meat consumption showed a substantially
lower contribution, reflecting the potential of meat reduction behaviors
to mitigate biodiversity loss. Those who indicated intention to reduce
their meat intake in the near future demonstrated a variable contribution
to their country’s total biodiversity footprint.

Potential Biodiversity
Loss Mitigation from Adopting Meat Alternatives
and Dairy Alternatives

illustrates the potential biodiversity loss mitigation
of shifting from meat to meat alternatives, as well as shifting from
dairy to dairy alternatives, among respondents who indicated an intention
to reduce meat or dairy consumption within the next six months. For
respondents who intend to reduce meat consumption and replace it with
meat alternatives, we calculate that there would be a consistent decrease
in the biodiversity footprints of meat and meat alternatives across
all countries: 0.012 MSA-loss·ha for the GBR, 0.016 MSA-loss·ha
for the NLD, and 0.039 MSA-loss·ha for the USA (). This would represent an
average of 34.3% reduction when comparing biodiversity footprints
of current and intended future intake of meat and meat alternatives
(Figure S3). The difference in reduction
potential can be explained in that, although respondents in the GBR
and NLD intend to consume meat alternatives more frequently within
the next six months, the average absolute meat consumption reduction
is higher in the USA due to the larger portion sizes. The geographic
distribution of biodiversity footprint reductions differed across
countries. In the USA, most avoided impacts would be associated with
domestic production, whereas reductions linked to dietary changes
in NLD would be distributed across a broader set of international
sourcing countries (Figure S5a).

Similarly, the potential biodiversity loss mitigation
from adopting
dairy alternatives demonstrate consistent reductions among respondents
intending to reduce dairy consumption: 0.027 MSA-loss·ha for
the NLD, 0.029 MSA-loss·ha for the GBR, and 0.058 MSA-loss·ha
for the USA, equivalent to about a 50% reduction between biodiversity
footprints of current and intended future intake of dairy and dairy
alternatives ( and Figure S3). Among the various dairy
products, milk and desserts contribute most to reducing the biodiversity
footprint when replaced by dairy alternatives. The main sourcing countries
of dairy products also exhibited biodiversity footprint reductions,
similar to those observed for meat products (Figure S5b). RSR-loss yielded comparable results for the NLD and GBR,
however, biodiversity footprints associated with dairy alternatives
would be increase in the USA, indicating that the biodiversity impacts
of dietary substitutions depend on the specific alternative products
consumed (Figure S4, Figure S6f, Figure S7b).

Across the three countries, respondents reported similar
intentions
to reduce portions of beef and poultry, with the exception of the
GBR, where more respondents indicated a desire to reduce poultry.
However, given that beef consumption has the highest biodiversity
footprint intensity (i.e., biodiversity footprint per kilogram of
beef) compared to other meat products, replacing poultry consumption
offers limited potential for reducing the biodiversity footprint compared
to replacing beef consumption.

Assessing the Effect of
Demographics and Current Dietary Behavior
on Potential Biodiversity Footprints

We first examined the
probability of intention to reduce meat or dairy intake based on demographic
and current dietary behavior variables (). Current meat consumption (meat portions
per year) was the most important predictor of the probability to reduce
meat intake and replace it with meat alternatives (a). A similar pattern was observed
for dairy, where current dairy consumption best predicted the probability
of shifting to dairy alternatives (b). Respondents who regularly consume meat
have a lower intention to shift to meat alternatives. Similarly, respondents
who consume large portion sizes are less likely to intend to shift
their diet from meat or dairy to meat alternatives or dairy alternatives
compared to respondents who consume small portion sizes. Respondents
that received higher education are more likely to shift their consumption
from meat to meat alternatives compared to respondents that did not
receive higher education, especially in NLD. However, this effect
was less obvious for dairy products. Additionally, younger respondents
(18–33) were more likely to shift their consumption to both
meat alternatives and dairy alternatives compared to older respondents
(50–65). Moreover, people living in urban areas showed a higher
preference to reduce meat consumption compared to those in rural areas.

We further assessed the magnitude of potential
biodiversity footprint
reductions (i.e., Delta MSA-loss, %) among respondents intending to
reduce their meat or dairy intake (). Again, current consumption portions (meat/dairy
portions per year) and portion size remained the most significant
predictors of biodiversity footprint change. This means that respondents
who consumed a higher number of meat portions per year as well as
larger portion sizes, would generally exhibit smaller reductions in
biodiversity footprint with their intended dietary changes. Respondents
who had already reduced their meat consumption would show greater
potential for biodiversity footprint reductions compared to those
who expressed an intention to decrease their meat intake in the future.
Female respondents demonstrated a higher reduction potential than
males for meat products, while smaller differences were observed for
dairy products. Age had a more pronounced influence on the reduction
in biodiversity footprint for dairy products than for meat products.
The XGBoost models based on RSR-loss showed similar patterns to those
based on MSA-loss (Figure S8 and Figure S9).

Discussion

This study examined the
biodiversity footprint associated with
current and near future consumption of animal meat and meat alternatives
(during main meals), dairy and dairy alternatives across respondents
from the NLD, GBR and USA. Our findings demonstrate that diet-related
biodiversity footprints vary substantially both within and between
countries, reflecting the diversity in dietary habits and preferences
among a large number of respondents. More than half of the respondents
expressed no intention to reduce their consumption of animal meat
and dairy products, suggesting that effective promotion of plant-based
alternatives to meat and dairy remains a considerable challenge. Among
those who did express intention to reduce meat or dairy intake, we
found a large variability in potential biodiversity footprint reductions
in the near future.

Around 40% of respondents in the NLD, GBR,
and USA intend to reduce
their meat or dairy intake, highlighting a significant mismatch between
assumption-driven dietary scenarios and survey-based data. This emphasizes
the need for more realistic approaches that take actual consumer behavior
when estimating biodiversity benefits from dietary transitions than
currently adopted in existing scenario studies.
,−

 For instance, Kozicka et al. have quantified the potential climate and biodiversity impacts by
assuming reductions of 10%, 25%, 50% and 90% in animal product consumption,
replacing them with plant-based alternatives. These studies likely
overestimate the potential for dietary change because they do not
account for the substantial proportion of people who have no intention
to reduce their meat and dairy intake. By assuming widespread adoption
of plant-based diets, these models risk underestimating the challenges
associated with promoting dietary shift on a large scale.

In
addition, our results demonstrate that among respondents who
intend to reduce meat or dairy consumption, the biodiversity footprint
associated with their intended consumption patterns would significantly
lower than that of their current intake, indicating the potential
for considerable biodiversity loss mitigation. We also found that
portion sizes play an important role in the biodiversity footprint
of food consumption. This finding aligns with previous research emphasizing
the role of portion size in shaping dietary impact. For instance,
a study in the United Kingdom found that reducing the size of meat
portions contributed the most to the overall decline in meat consumption,
followed by other behavioral change strategies such as fewer meat-eating
days. Given that we identified the number
of annual meat (main meals) and dairy portions and portion size as
key predictors of dietary shift probability and biodiversity footprint
changes, our findings suggest that initiatives focusing on portion
size reduction could be an effective strategy to lower the overall
biodiversity footprint, particularly for respondents less inclined
to adopt meat alternatives.

Moreover, our results reinforce
the substantial contribution of
animal products, particularly beef and lamb, to biodiversity loss,
aligning with existing literature.
−

 By disaggregating broad meat categories into specific
meat products, we captured significant variability in the respondents’
intent to decrease consumption of different types of meat products.
For example, respondents were more likely to reduce beef consumption
than poultry, revealing a level of granularity overlooked in prior
research that focused on a general meat category. This enables a more accurate estimation of biodiversity
footprint reductions associated with different dietary transitions.
Interestingly, the pattern of reduction observed in individual food
consumption aligns with broader dietary trends identified in previous
studies. For example, our results show
that the NLD consumes a higher proportion of plant-based foods compared
to the GBR and USA, and younger people are more likely to have the
intention to reduce meat and dairy intake than older generations.
We found notable difference between intentions to shift from dairy
to dairy alternatives. For example, few intended to replace cheese
with plant-based cheese, possibly due to lower market availability
and consumer adoption. This highlights the importance of targeting
specific products within meat or dairy categories to maximize the
biodiversity benefits through dietary shift.
,

The biodiversity footprint intensity of many meat alternatives
is lower than that of meat products, indicating that replacing certain
animal-based products with alternatives could generate biodiversity
benefits. Such benefits are particularly relevant in the context of
national commitments to halt biodiversity loss, as in the NLD, GBR,
and USA.
−

 However, this is not always the case across all alternative
products. While plant-based alternatives generally reduced biodiversity
footprints, the RSR-loss results showed that replacing dairy products
with dairy alternatives was associated with an increase in biodiversity
footprints in the USA. This was largely driven by plant-based desserts
and plant-based ice cream containing coconut-derived ingredients,
which were linked to sourcing regions such as Philippines, where RSR-loss
characterization factors are relatively high, reflecting high species
richness and endemism. These findings suggest that biodiversity outcomes
depend not only on the replacement of animal-based products, but also
on the composition and sourcing of the alternative products themselves.
Shifting from animal-based to more plant-based diets could make a
meaningful contribution toward achieving national biodiversity targets.
However, intentions to reduce animal meat consumption vary substantially
among individuals and across countries, a factor for consideration
for the development of effective dietary policy frameworks. Survey
responses on barriers to reducing meat and dairy products further
highlight why these intentions may not readily translate into action.
In particular, the most frequently reported barriers were the enjoyment
of meat taste (∼72%) and texture (∼62%), whereas only
about 40% of respondents associated reducing meat consumption with
being “good for the planet”. Similarly, for dairy products,
only 29% of respondents made such a link. These findings suggest that
while sensory preferences remain strong, awareness of the environmental
impacts of meat and dairy consumption remains limited.

One promising
approach to promoting dietary shift is to implement
population-level interventions that increase awareness of the link
between animal product consumption and biodiversity loss. For example,
integrating biodiversity considerations into national food-based dietary
guidelines would offer a concrete step toward environmental objectives. In addition, guidelines could specify recommended
portion sizes for animal products or suggest plant-based alternatives,
potentially helping people make more sustainable choices.
,
 However, their effectiveness would depend on consumers’ willingness
to adjust their current eating habits, which remains limited as our
findings suggest. Beyond dietary guidelines, broader intervention
strategies targeting education, public communication, and restructuring
of food environmentsfor instance, through enhanced access
to plant-based alternatives and portion size adjustmentscan
promote dietary shifts. Recent evidence has shown that interventions
combining education and communication-based strategies tend to be
the most effective in promoting sustainable diet transitions, highlighting
the need for coordinated, multilevel action to translate individual
intentions into large-scale biodiversity benefits.

Although our analysis offers insights into the biodiversity
loss
mitigation of realistic shifts in consumer diets, it is subject to
several limitations and model uncertainties. First, our study assessed
the potential reduction in biodiversity footprint based on self-reported
near future dietary intentions obtained from survey responses. Intentions
are an important component of many behavior change models,

 and previous research has shown that stronger intentions are associated

 and a higher willingness to pay for plant-based alternatives. However, intentions do not necessarily translate
into actual behavior change,
,
 because dietary behavior
is often habitual and influenced by taste preferences, availability
of alternatives and affordability. Consequently, the estimated biodiversity
footprint reductions should be regarded as an upper-bound estimate
of what could be achieved if these intentions result in sustained
dietary change. To better account for variability in dietary change,
we distinguished respondents into different stages of dietary change,
including those who had already reduced meat or dairy consumption,
those who had not yet but plan to reduce meat or dairy consumption
within the next six months and those who do not intend to change their
diet. Focusing on those who have reduced or express an intention to
reduce meat or dairy consumption provides a more realistic basis for
estimating the potential biodiversity footprint reductions associated
with dietary change compared to hypothetical dietary scenarios. Nevertheless,
the actual magnitude of biodiversity footprint reductions will ultimately
depend on the extent to which intended dietary changes are adopted
and maintained over time. Second, our focus was limited to meat and
meat alternatives consumed during the main meal, as well as dairy
and dairy alternatives consumed during the day. This does not capture
the entire spectrum of dietary intake, indicating that contributions
from other food groups (e.g., grains, beverages) were not included.
Although meat products contribute the most to biodiversity footprints,
this scope limits our ability to assess total dietary biodiversity
impacts. In addition, seafood (including both aquaculture and wild-caught
fish) was not included in our analysis due to the lack of data and
methods to quantify biodiversity footprints of seafood consumption, even though survey respondents indicated potential
shifts in that direction. As a result, the biodiversity footprints
related to reducing and replacing meat with seafood were not assessed.
Third, the self-reported dietary intakes differ from national FAO
food consumption statistics. FAO estimates are derived from food balance
sheets that account for domestic production and trade and represent
annual food availability at the population level, whereas our survey
captured participants’ typical daily consumption of meat, dairy,
and alternative products in their main meal (which may under- or overestimate
annual average consumption). However, previous studies comparing dietary
surveys with FAO statistics have found that FAO estimates can substantially
exceed reported consumption of meat and milk. This discrepancy may arise because FAO food balance sheets do not
fully account for losses during processing, retail, and final consumption
and may include food not intended for human consumption such as pet
food. Consumption surveys therefore provide
valuable information on individual dietary intake and intended dietary
shifts that cannot be captured by food balance sheets. Fourth, biodiversity
footprint estimates are subject to uncertainties related to the representation
of agricultural management practices and livestock production systems.
Empirical research has demonstrated that biodiversity responses vary
across pasture management intensities, yet current footprint methods do not adequately distinguish management
systems, such as extensive rangeland versus intensive livestock production. As a result, footprint estimates do not fully
capture the variability in biodiversity impacts associated with different
production practices. To reduce uncertainty in biodiversity footprints,
we therefore recommend the development of footprint methods that distinguish
agricultural management and livestock production intensity as a promising
avenue for future research. Another uncertainty concerns the allocation
of inputs in multioutput processes. Previous research has shown that
the choice between mass- and value-based allocation can substantially
affect footprint estimates for livestock products, indicating that
our results may be sensitive to the selected allocation method. We used the FABIO physical input-output table,
which applies mass-based allocation to assign inputs in multioutput
processes. Biophysical allocation is the most commonly used allocation
approach and is recommended by life cycle assessment guidelines for
livestock production,
,
 because value-based allocation
depends on market prices, which vary across regions, production systems,
and over time.

 Finally, our analysis focused
exclusively on the NLD, GBR, and USA. While these countries may be
representative of Western dietary patterns, they do not reflect the
dietary habits of many other regions, such as Global South and East
Asia, where both consumption patterns and biodiversity footprints
may differ substantially.

In summary, substituting high biodiversity
impact food products
with plant-based alternatives offers a promising near-future opportunity
to accelerate biodiversity loss mitigation. However, across all survey
respondents in the NLD, GBR, and USA who expressed an intention to
reduce meat consumption, the estimated future dietary changes would
lead to an average biodiversity footprint reduction of only 13% when
compared to their current meat and meat alternative intake. Relying
solely on consumer motivation is therefore unlikely to be sufficient
to achieve substantial biodiversity loss mitigation via dietary change.
This suggests that realizing biodiversity footprint reductions through
dietary shift remains a considerable challenge. It is essential to
consider actual eating behaviors and perceived barriers to change
to enable effective dietary transitions.
