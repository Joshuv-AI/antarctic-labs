Beyond Chamfer Distance: Granular Order-aware Evaluation Metric For Online Mapping
Chouaib Bencheikh Lehocine, Adam Lilja, Junsheng Fu, Lars Hammarstrand

Abstract
Online map estimation is a crucial component of autonomous driving systems that reduces the reliance on costly high-definition maps. State-of-the-art (SOTA) methods commonly predict map elements as ordered sequences of points that form polylines and polygons. The evaluation of these methods relies predominantly on mean average precision (mAP) based on thresholded Chamfer distance (CD) . This framework lacks sensitivity to point ordering and provides limited granularity in assessing geometric quality, making it difficult to distinguish which methods truly excel over others. In this work, we address these limitations on two fronts. For the single-instance similarity measure , we introduce sequence optimal sub-pattern assignment (SOSPA) , an order-aware metric that enables fine-grained evaluation of individual geometries while satisfying all metric axioms. For the multi-instance evaluation framework , we propose polyline localisation and detection (PLD) , a soft metric that jointly captures detection quality and geometric accuracy, replacing the hard thresholding of mAP with a principled soft assignment. Through evaluations on nuScenes, we demonstrate that PLD effectively ranks SOTA online mapping methods (MapTRv2, StreamMapNet, MapTracker) while providing a decomposed error analysis. This analysis identifies detection capability as the dominant bottleneck in current methods, revealing a performance trend that mAP fails to capture. Code for evaluation using our metrics will be released.

Introduction

Autonomous vehicles require accurate and up-to-date information about the surrounding road geometry to plan safe trajectories.

Online map estimation (OME)
methods aim to eliminate the reliance on costly pre-built
high-definition (HD)
maps by predicting map elements using on-board sensors in real time.
The performance of downstream planning and control modules is, in turn, directly dependent on these maps.
Therefore, the evaluation metrics of
OME

State-of-the-art (SOTA)

,

,

,

,
3

Their evaluation rests on two pillars: (i) a
similarity measure
between individual predicted and ground truth geometries, and (ii) a
multi-instance framework
that aggregates element-wise similarities into an overall score.
The prevailing evaluation approach employs
Chamfer distance (CD)
for (i) and
average precision (AP)
for (ii), with
mean average precision (mAP)
representing the mean score across predicted classes
[
7
,
12
,
9
,
10
,
23
,

.
This

-

First,
CD
operates on unordered point sets, making it ill-suited as a similarity measure for polylines where point ordering encodes geometric structure. Beyond this,
CD
measures the joint proximity of full instances, resulting in coarse matching granularity and increased susceptibility to outliers. Additionally, it violates the triangle inequality, rendering it non
-
metric. The metric axioms (non-negativity, identity, symmetry, and triangle inequality) ensure that an evaluation measure behaves consistently and intuitively, preventing unexpected outcomes such as two identical geometries being rated as dissimilar, or an indirect comparison path yielding a lower cost than a direct one. Some of the limitations of
CD
can be mitigated by adopting

, as was done in
[

,
21
]
, which is order-aware and constitutes a proper metric.
However, as a supremum-based measure,

5.2
).

Second,
AP
converts the similarity measure into hard

/

discards positional accuracy. Thus, two predictions classified as
TPs
at the same threshold contribute equally regardless of their geometric accuracy. Furthermore, the thresholds commonly used are permissive relative to the requirements of downstream autonomous driving applications. For instance, even the tightest threshold of
0.5
m implies that a prediction that is
0.5
m offset can still receive a perfect
AP
score, even though such geometric inaccuracy would critically impact planning and control.
An adequate
OME

AP
itself does not satisfy the metric axioms, making it a non-principled comparison framework.

To address these shortcomings of existing
OME

similarity measures
and
multi-instance framework
, we draw inspiration from the

family of metrics from the

[

,
17
,
22
]
, together with the string matching metric

[

,
13
]
. For the

, we introduce

[

]
from unordered point sets to sequences of points.

is an assignment-based metric that optimally matches individual points between two sets and penalizes unmatched points with a tunable cutoff cost

. This cutoff prevents the metric from being dominated by outliers and admits a direct physical interpretation (e.g., maximum tolerable lateral offset).
SOSPA
inherits these properties while additionally enforcing that matched pairs respect the sequential ordering of points, a key structural property of polylines that
GOSPA
and
CD
both ignore.
Additionally, we show that the assignment in

and, provide a bounded normalization in

[

]
.

For the

3
Order-aware polyline similarity metric:
SOSPA

To improve the evaluation of
OME
methods, we address the limitations of existing similarity measures by introducing
SOSPA
, an order-aware similarity metric for finite point sequences, i.e., polylines. We treat open polylines (Section
3.1
) and polygons (Section
3.2
) separately and address normalization and computation.

3.1
Sequence optimal sub-pattern assignment

We begin by defining
\mathbf{x}=(x_{1},\ldots,x_{|\mathbf{x}|})
and

\mathbf{y}=(y_{1},\ldots,y_{|\mathbf{y}|})
to be finite sequences of points in

\mathbb{R}^{N}
.
An assignment set between
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
is any subset
\theta\subseteq\{1,\ldots,|\mathbf{x}|\}\times\{1,\ldots,|\mathbf{y}|\}

satisfying the uniqueness constraint that each index appears at most once. The collection of all valid assignment sets between
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
is denoted by
\Gamma_{|\mathbf{x}|,|\mathbf{y}|}
.
An assignment set
\theta=\{(i_{k},j_{k})\}_{k=1}^{|\theta|}\in\Gamma_{|\mathbf{x}|,|\mathbf{y}|}
is said to be an
ordered
assignment set if its index pairs respect a strictly increasing order. That is,
i_{1}<i_{2}<\cdots<i_{|\theta|}
, and
j_{1}<j_{2}<\cdots<j_{|\theta|}
.
The set of all ordered assignment sets between
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
is denoted by
\Gamma^{\textrm{o}}_{|\mathbf{x}|,|\mathbf{y}|}
.

Definition 1
.

Given a metric
d(.,.)
in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
, a scalar
c>0
, and a scalar
p
where
1\leq p<\infty
,

\displaystyle d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\Bigg(\min_{\theta\in\Gamma^{\textrm{o}}_{|\mathbf{x}|,|\mathbf{y}|}}\sum_{(i,j)\in\theta}d(x_{i},y_{j})^{p}+\frac{c^{p}}{2}(|\mathbf{x}|+|\mathbf{y}|-2|\theta|)\Bigg)^{1/p}.

(1)

From the definition, it follows immediately that
SOSPA
satisfies identity, non-negativity, and symmetry. The proof for the triangle inequality can be found in Appendix
B
.

The main difference between
SOSPA
and
GOSPA

[
17
]
with (
\alpha=2
) is that
SOSPA
is defined over sequences instead of sets, thus constraining the matches to be ordered
(i.e.,
\theta\in\Gamma^{\textrm{o}}
).
The cutoff distance
c
in (
1
) defines the maximum allowable error for pairs to be regarded as matched points, as well as the penalty for missed and false detections given by
c^{p}/2
.
The exponent
p\geq 1
determines the sensitivity of the metric to localization errors, where a higher
p
results in higher penalization of large deviations among matched pairs. The use of a constant penalty
c^{p}/2
per unmatched point ensures the robustness of the metric to outliers, since they cannot excessively influence the final score. Furthermore, while the overall matching must respect the sequential order of points in both sequences, the formulation allows individual points in each sequence to be skipped (i.e., left unmatched) for
c^{p}/2
per skipped point, providing flexibility in handling sequences of unequal length, or locally poor correspondence.

Computing
SOSPA
.
The ordering constraint in (
1
) makes the underlying optimization problem for
SOSPA
fundamentally different from the bipartite matching problem in
GOSPA
, which can be efficiently solved using the Hungarian algorithm.
To find an efficient solution to (
1
), we propose to cast it as a minimum-cost trace problem from the
GED
literature
[
20
,
15
]
.
GED
measures the minimum cost of transforming a sequence
\mathbf{x}
into
\mathbf{y}
through (i) substitutions
x_{i}\!\rightarrow\!y_{j}
at cost
\gamma(x_{i}\!\rightarrow\!y_{j})\geq 0
, (ii) deletions
x_{i}\!\rightarrow\!\Lambda
at cost
\gamma(x_{i}\!\rightarrow\!\Lambda)\geq 0
, and (iii) insertions
\Lambda\!\rightarrow\!y_{j}
at cost
\gamma(\Lambda\!\rightarrow\!y_{j})\geq 0
, where
\Lambda
is the null element. A
trace
is a set
T
of index pairs that respects sequence ordering, i.e., an ordered assignment set. Its cost is

\displaystyle C(T)

\displaystyle=\sum_{(i,j)\in T}\gamma(x_{i}\rightarrow y_{j})+\sum_{i\notin\pi_{1}(T)}\gamma(x_{i}\rightarrow\Lambda)+\sum_{j\notin\pi_{2}(T)}\gamma(\Lambda\rightarrow y_{j}),

(2)

where
\pi_{1}(T)=\{i:(i,j)\in T\}
and
\pi_{2}(T)=\{j:(i,j)\in T\}
are index projections of
T
.
We now show that
SOSPA
is equivalent to a minimum cost trace of
GED
with specific cost functions.

Lemma 1
.

d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})^{p}
equals the minimum trace cost of
GED
between
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
with cost functions

\displaystyle\gamma(x_{i}\rightarrow y_{j})

\displaystyle=d(x_{i},y_{j})^{p},\quad\gamma(x_{i}\rightarrow\Lambda)=\gamma(\Lambda\rightarrow y_{j})=\frac{c^{p}}{2}.

(3)

Proof.

Substituting (
3
) into (
2
) and noting that traces are ordered assignment sets, the minimum trace cost,
\min_{T}C(T)
, coincides with (
1
) raised to the power
p
.
∎

By Lemma
1
,
SOSPA
can be computed via
dynamic programming (DP)
using the
WF
algorithm
[
20
]
(equivalently, Needleman-Wunsch algorithm
[
16
]
) in
\mathcal{O}(|\mathbf{x}|\cdot|\mathbf{y}|)
time.

Remark 1
.

When
\gamma(\cdot\rightarrow\cdot)
is a metric,
GED
coincides with the minimum trace cost
\min_{T}C(T)

[
20
]
and is a metric. The costs in (
3
) satisfy these conditions for
p=1
(since
d(\cdot,\cdot)^{p}
is a metric when
p=1
), providing an alternative proof of the triangle inequality for
SOSPA
(see Appendix for the direct proof covering all
p\geq 1
). Given
\gamma(\cdot\rightarrow\cdot)
is not a metric, the minimum-cost trace problem remains well-defined for any non-negative cost function, and the
WF
algorithm solves it regardless. Hence, the
WF
algorithm correctly computes
SOSPA
for all
p\geq 1
.

Normalization of
SOSPA
.

To facilitate the use of
SOSPA
as a polyline matching metric, we normalize
d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}
while preserving its metric property. A natural normalization is
d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}/\big(\tfrac{c^{p}}{2}(|\mathbf{x}|+|\mathbf{y}|)\big)^{1/p}
. However, this does not satisfy the triangle inequality in general. Instead, following the approach used to normalize
GED
in
[
8
]
, we define

\bar{d}^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}
as follows.

Definition 2
.

Let
d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}
be as defined in (
1
). Then

\displaystyle\bar{d}^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\frac{2\cdot d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}{\big(\frac{c^{p}}{2}(|\mathbf{x}|+|\mathbf{y}|)\big)^{1/p}+d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})}\in[0,1].

(4)

For
p=1
,
\bar{d}^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}
satisfies the triangle inequality as shown in Appendix
B.2
, and is thus a metric (identity and symmetry follow from
d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}
).

3.2
SOSPA
for polygons

Since the starting point of a polygon (i.e., closed polyline) can be changed without altering its geometry, computing
SOSPA
for polygons (i.e., closed polylines) requires inspecting all cyclic shift combinations of the polygons. The same difficulty arises for cyclic
FD

[
19
]
and cyclic
GED

[
13
]
.
Therefore, in the following, we define
SOSPA
over cyclic sequences and establish a Lemma that enables solving it using an efficient exact algorithm that leverages cyclic shift properties.

Let
\langle k\rangle_{n}
denote a cyclic shift operation following

\displaystyle\langle k\rangle_{n}=((k-1)\mod n)+1,\quad k\in\mathbb{Z},\;n\in\mathbb{N}^{+}.

(5)

Then, define the cyclically shifted version of the sequence
\mathbf{x}
as

\displaystyle\mathcal{S}_{s}(\mathbf{x})=\big(x_{\langle 1+s\rangle_{|\mathbf{x}|}},x_{\langle 2+s\rangle_{|\mathbf{x}|}},\ldots,x_{\langle|\mathbf{x}|+s\rangle_{|\mathbf{x}|}}\big),\quad s\in\mathbb{Z}.

(6)

We can now readily denote the set of all cyclic shifts of a finite sequence as
[\mathbf{x}]=\{\mathcal{S}_{l}(\mathbf{x}):0\leq l\leq|\mathbf{x}|-1\}
, and this will be referred to as a cyclic sequence.

Definition 3
.

Given that two finite cyclic sequences
[\mathbf{x}]
and
[\mathbf{y}]
, then
SOSPA
is defined as

\displaystyle d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}([\mathbf{x}],[\mathbf{y}])=\min_{\begin{subarray}{c}s_{x}\in\{0,\ldots,|\mathbf{x}|-1\}\\
s_{y}\in\{0,\ldots,|\mathbf{y}|-1\}\end{subarray}}d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}\big(\mathcal{S}_{s_{x}}(\mathbf{x}),\mathcal{S}_{s_{y}}(\mathbf{y})\big).

(7)

A straightforward evaluation of (
7
) requires
|\mathbf{x}||\mathbf{y}|
computations of
SOSPA
.
Following a key result for cyclic
GED

[
13
, Lemma 3.1]
, we show that optimizing over the cyclic shifts of one sequence while fixing the other suffices.

Lemma 2
.

For any two finite cyclic sequences
[\mathbf{x}]
and
[\mathbf{y}]
,
we have

\displaystyle d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}([\mathbf{x}],[\mathbf{y}])

\displaystyle=d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}(\mathbf{x},[\mathbf{y}])=\min_{s_{y}\in\{0,\ldots,|\mathbf{y}|-1\}}d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}\big(\mathbf{x},\mathcal{S}_{s_{y}}(\mathbf{y})\big).

(8)

Proof.

The proof follows a similar argument to one outlined in
[
13
, Lemma 3.1]
. See Appendix
D
for details.
∎

Using the result above, and the established relation between
GED
and
SOSPA
in Lemma
1
, the cyclic
SOSPA
can be computed
efficiently using Maes algorithm
[
13
]
in
\mathcal{O}(|\mathbf{x}||\mathbf{y}|\log|\mathbf{y}|)
time, and faster cyclic
GED
algorithms such as
[
14
]
directly apply. We note that the original reference on cyclic
GED

[
13
]
does not establish whether cyclic
GED
constitutes a metric (the same is true for cyclic
FD

[
19
]
). As cyclic
SOSPA
shares a similar structural formulation with cyclic
GED
, we likewise do not claim it to be a metric, as we believe it does not satisfy the triangle inequality in general.

4
Multi-instance evaluation metric:
PLD

To score
OME
methods, we need a multi-instance evaluation measure. While
SOSPA
can be combined with
AP
, this combination still inherits
AP
rigid matching strategy and discards the geometric accuracy. Therefore, in this section, we introduce
PLD
, a soft multi-instance metric that jointly evaluates detection and geometric accuracy.

4.1
Online mapping outputs as multi-Bernoulli RFS

For each road element class, an
OME
method outputs
\mathbf{X}=\big\{(r_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i})\big\}_{i=1}^{|\mathbf{X}|},
where
r_{i}\in[0,1]
is the confidence score of the
i
-th instance and
\mathbf{x}_{i}
is a polyline (i.e., a finite sequence of points in
\mathbb{R}^{N}
) describing its estimated geometry. The output
\mathbf{X}
parametrizes a
MB

RFS
distribution over sequences, where each component
(r_{i},\mathbf{x}_{i})
constitutes an independent Bernoulli process with existence probability
r_{i}
and a degenerate single-object density concentrated at the polyline
\mathbf{x}_{i}
. Ground truth instances take the same form with
r_{i}=1
for all
i
. This probabilistic interpretation of
OME
outputs promotes the use of
P-GOSPA

[
22
]
, a metric designed for comparing
MB
distributions, as a multi-instance evaluation framework. Accordingly, we define
PLD
as the instantiation of
P-GOSPA
that uses
\bar{d}^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}
as the base single-instance distance, and adopt it as our multi-instance evaluation metric.

4.2
Polyline localisation and detection error metric

Definition 4
.

For sets of polylines
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
,
c>0
and
1\leq p<\infty
,

\displaystyle d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle=\Bigg[\min_{\theta\in\Gamma}\Bigg(\sum_{(i,j)\in\theta}\left[\min(r_{\mathbf{x}_{i}},r_{\mathbf{y}_{j}})\,\bar{d}^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})^{p}+\frac{1}{2}\left|r_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}-r_{\mathbf{y}_{j}}\right|\right]

\displaystyle\qquad+\frac{1}{2}\bigg(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\forall j,(i,j)\notin\theta\end{subarray}}r_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j:\forall i,(i,j)\notin\theta\end{subarray}}r_{\mathbf{y}_{j}}\bigg)\Bigg)\Bigg]^{\frac{1}{p}}.

(9)

where
\bar{d}^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}
is defined in (
4
), and where
\theta\in\Gamma
is an assignment set between
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
.

This is
P-GOSPA

[
22
, Proposition. 2]
with
\alpha=2
,
c=1
, and
\bar{d}^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}
as the base distance. Since
\bar{d}^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}
is a metric for
p=1
, so is
PLD
by
[
22
, Proposition. 2]
. The minimization is a 2D assignment problem, solvable in polynomial time by the Hungarian algorithm.

Looking at (
4
), the role of confidence scores becomes clear. Unmatched instances incur a penalty proportional to their confidence. A high-confidence miss or false alarm is penalized more than an uncertain one, reflecting the intuition that confident errors are more consequential. For matched pairs, the cost combines a confidence-weighted geometric term with a confidence-mismatch term
\tfrac{1}{2}|r_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}-r_{\mathbf{y}_{j}}|
, which penalizes disagreement between predicted and ground truth existence probabilities. Consequently, a good match requires not only geometric accuracy but also well-calibrated confidence. This makes
PLD
a comprehensive measure for evaluating probabilistic map instances as produced by online mapping methods. Although ground-truth carries
r_{j}=1
, (
4
) is fully general and supports comparing two stochastic outputs (e.g. for measuring temporal consistency between consecutive map predictions).

PLD
decomposition into interpretable error terms.
Letting
\theta^{\star}
denote the optimal assignment in (
4
), define

\displaystyle e^{(c,p)}_{\text{loc.}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle=\!\!\sum_{(i,j)\in\theta^{\star}}\!\!\min(r_{\mathbf{x}_{i}},r_{\mathbf{y}_{j}})\,\bar{d}^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})^{p},

(10)

\displaystyle e_{\text{det.}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle=\!\!\sum_{(i,j)\in\theta^{\star}}\!\!\tfrac{1}{2}|r_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}-r_{\mathbf{y}_{j}}|+\tfrac{1}{2}\Big(\!\sum_{i:(i,j)\notin\theta^{\star}}\!\!r_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}+\!\sum_{j:(i,j)\notin\theta^{\star}}\!\!r_{\mathbf{y}_{j}}\Big),

(11)

so that
d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=[e^{(c,p)}_{\text{loc.}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})+e_{\text{det.}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})]^{1/p}
. The term
e^{(c,p)}_{\text{loc.}}
is a probability-weighted aggregation of geometric error over matched pairs. The quantity
e_{\text{det.}}
pools detection-related penalties: confidence mismatch on matched pairs, missed instances, and false alarms. Including confidence mismatch within the detection term is justified by the fact that a matched instance with confidence
r
behaves as a
(1-r)
-weighted false detection. The constant
\tfrac{1}{2}
on detection penalties keeps the metric robust to outliers.

Normalization of
PLD
.

To facilitate comparability across categories and baselines, we apply the same normalization as used for
SOSPA
. That is,

\displaystyle\bar{d}^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=\frac{2\,d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})}{\big(\tfrac{1}{2}(R_{\mathbf{X}}+R_{\mathbf{Y}})\big)^{1/p}+d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})}\in[0,1],

(12)

where
R_{\mathbf{X}}=\sum_{i}r_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}
and
R_{\mathbf{Y}}=\sum_{j}r_{\mathbf{y}_{j}}
. The normalized
PLD
is positive and symmetric. Moreover, for the case
p=1
, it does satisfy the triangle inequality as shown in Appendix
B.2
and is therefore a metric. Beyond satisfying the triangle inequality, the case
p=1
enables a particularly convenient use of the decomposition terms. Specifically, (
4
) simplifies to
d^{(c,1)}_{\text{PLD}}=e^{(c,1)}_{\text{loc.}}+e_{\text{det.}}
, and the decomposition extends naturally to the normalized form with

\displaystyle\bar{e}^{(c,1)}_{\text{loc.}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle=\frac{2\,e^{(c,1)}_{\text{loc.}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})}{\tfrac{1}{2}(R_{\mathbf{X}}+R_{\mathbf{Y}})+d^{(c,1)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})},\quad\bar{e}_{\text{det.}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=\frac{2\,e_{\text{det.}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})}{\tfrac{1}{2}(R_{\mathbf{X}}+R_{\mathbf{Y}})+d^{(c,1)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})},

(13)

giving
\bar{d}^{(c,1)}_{\text{PLD}}=\bar{e}^{(c,1)}_{\text{loc.}}+\bar{e}_{\text{det.}}
. We use
p=1
throughout the experiments.

1
) for polylines and (

6
Conclusions

To improve the understanding of
OME
methods,
we introduced
SOSPA
, an order-aware polyline similarity metric, and
PLD
, a soft multi-instance evaluation metric, as alternatives to
CD
-based
mAP
for evaluating
OME
methods. Both satisfy metric axioms, remain computationally tractable, and jointly capture detection and positional accuracy.
Benchmarking three
SOTA
methods on nuScenes shows that
PLD
preserves relative performance rankings while its error decomposition reveals that detection error dominates across all methods, a finding that
mAP
fails to capture.

Limitations and future work.
Our framework focuses on geometry, and instance-level topological structure (e.g. which lanes connect to which) is not captured. One promising direction for future work is therefore to investigate topology-aware extensions of
SOSPA
and
PLD

[
5
,
21
]
. Additionally, while we focus on nuScenes for its popularity, evaluating on additional datasets and on other methods would further test the generality of the framework.

Finally, we hope that an order-aware, decomposable, metric-based evaluation framework will support continued progress in online mapping for safer autonomous driving.

Appendix B
Proof of the triangle inequlaity for
SOSPA

For an ordered assignment set
\theta\in\Gamma^{\textrm{o}}_{|\mathbf{x}|,|\mathbf{y}|}
and finite sequneces
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{y}
, define the assignment cost

\displaystyle C(\theta,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\left(\sum_{(i,j)\in\theta}d(x_{i},y_{j})^{p}+\frac{c^{p}}{2}(|\mathbf{x}|+|\mathbf{y}|-2|\theta|)\right)^{1/p}

(14)

so that

\displaystyle d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\min_{\theta\in\Gamma^{\textrm{o}}_{|\mathbf{x}|,|\mathbf{y}|}}C(\theta,\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})

(15)

B.1
Proof of triangle inequlaity for non-normalized
SOSPA

Theorem 1
.

The non-normalized
SOSPA
defined in (
1
)
satisfies the triangle inequality

\displaystyle d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})+d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})

(16)

To show Theorem
1
, we begin by stating and proving several lemmas.

Let
\theta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}\in\Gamma^{\textrm{o}}_{|\mathbf{x}|,|\mathbf{z}|}
and
\theta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}\in\Gamma^{\textrm{o}}_{|\mathbf{z}|,|\mathbf{y}|}
be ordered assignment sets. Then we define the composed set
\theta_{\textrm{c}}
as

\displaystyle\theta_{\textrm{c}}\triangleq\theta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}\circ\theta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}=\{(i,m):

\displaystyle(i,j)\in\theta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}\text{ and }(j,m)\in\theta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle\qquad\text{ for some }j\}

(17)

Moreover, we define

\displaystyle J_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}

\displaystyle=\{j:(i,j)\in\theta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}\text{ for some }i\}

\displaystyle J_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}

\displaystyle=\{j:(j,m)\in\theta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}\text{ for some }m\}

\displaystyle J_{\textrm{c}}

\displaystyle=J_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}\cap J_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}

(18)

Observe that
J_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}
and
J_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}
represents indices in
\mathbf{z}
matched to
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{y}
, respectively.

Lemma 3
.

The composed set
\theta_{c}
defined in (
B.1
) is an ordered assignment set between
\mathbf{x}
amd
\mathbf{y}
, i.e.,
\theta_{c}\in\Gamma^{\textrm{o}}_{|\mathbf{x}|,|\mathbf{y}|}
.

Proof.

Suppose
(i_{1},m_{1}),(i_{2},m_{2})\in\theta_{c}
with
i_{1}<i_{2}
. By the definition of
\theta_{c}
, there exist indices
j_{1},j_{2}
such that:

\displaystyle(i_{1},j_{1})\in\theta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}},\quad(j_{1},m_{1})\in\theta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}},

\displaystyle(i_{2},j_{2})\in\theta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}},\quad(j_{2},m_{2})\in\theta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}.

(19)

Since
\theta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}\in\Gamma^{\textrm{o}}_{|\mathbf{x}|,|\mathbf{z}|}
is an ordered set and
i_{1}<i_{2}
, the ordering constraint implies
j_{1}<j_{2}
.

Since
\theta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}\in\Gamma^{\textrm{o}}_{|\mathbf{z}|,|\mathbf{y}|}
is an ordered set and
j_{1}<j_{2}
, the ordering constraint implies
m_{1}<m_{2}
.

Therefore, whenever
i_{1}<i_{2}
for pairs in
\theta_{c}
, we have
m_{1}<m_{2}
, which means
\theta_{c}
satisfies the ordering constraint. Hence,
\theta_{c}\in\Gamma^{\textrm{o}}_{|\mathbf{x}|,|\mathbf{y}|}
.
∎

Lemma 4
.

Let
\theta_{\textrm{c}}
be as defined in (
B.1
) then it follows that

\displaystyle|\theta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}|+|\theta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}|\leq|\mathbf{z}|+|\theta_{c}|

(20)

Proof.

Given
J_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}
,
J_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}
, and
J_{\textrm{c}}
as defined in (
18
), using the inclusion-exclusion principle we have

\displaystyle|J_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}\cup J_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}|

\displaystyle=|J_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}|+|J_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}|-|J_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}\cap J_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}|

\displaystyle=|\theta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}|+|\theta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}|-|\theta_{c}|

(21)

Combining this with the fact that
|J_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}\cup J_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}|\leq|\mathbf{z}|
we establish (
20
) and the lemma follows.
∎

Lemma 5
.

Let
\mathbf{x}
,
\mathbf{y}
,
\mathbf{z}
be finite sequences. There exists a valid ordered alignment
\theta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}\in\Gamma^{\textrm{o}}_{|\mathbf{x}|,|\mathbf{y}|}
such that the alignment cost (
14
) satisfies

\displaystyle C(\theta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})+d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})

(22)

Proof.

Given
J_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}
,
J_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}
, and
J_{\textrm{c}}
as defined in (

), let

, then let us define two sequnces
\mathbf{u},\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{n+2}
as

\displaystyle(u_{k},v_{k})=

\displaystyle\begin{cases}\big(d(x_{\sigma(j)},z_{j}),\;d(z_{j},y_{\pi(j)})\big),&k\leftrightarrow j\in J_{c},\\
\big(d(x_{\sigma(j)},z_{j}),\;0\big),&k\leftrightarrow j\in J_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}\setminus J_{c},\\
\big(0,\;d(z_{l},y_{\pi(l)})\big),&k\leftrightarrow l\in J_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}\setminus J_{c},\\
\big(g_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}^{1/p},\;0\big)\text{ or }\big(0,\;g_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}^{1/p}\big),&k=n{+}1,\,n{+}2,\end{cases}

(23)

where
g_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}=\tfrac{c^{p}}{2}(|\mathbf{x}|{+}|\mathbf{z}|{-}2|\theta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}|)
and
g_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}=\tfrac{c^{p}}{2}(|\mathbf{z}|{+}|\mathbf{y}|{-}2|\theta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}|)
, and
\sigma
,
\pi
are ordered mappings on
\{1,2,\ldots,|\mathbf{x}|\}
, and
\{1,2,\ldots,|\mathbf{y}|\}
, respectively. They satisfy

\displaystyle\{(\sigma(j),\pi(j)):j\in J_{\textrm{c}}\}=\theta_{\textrm{c}}

(24)

By construction, and using (
14
), we have that

\displaystyle C(\theta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})+C(\theta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})=\|\mathbf{u}\|_{p}+\|\mathbf{v}\|_{p}\geq\|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\|_{p}.

(25)

where the inequality is obtained by applying, Minkowski’s inequality (
1\leq p
).
Expanding the terms, we arrive at

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{u}+\mathbf{v}\|_{p}^{p}

\displaystyle=\sum_{j\in J_{\textrm{c}}}\big(d(x_{\sigma(j)},z_{j})+d(z_{j},y_{\pi(j)}))^{p}+\underbrace{A}_{\geq\,0}+g_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}+g_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}

(26)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{(i,j)\in\theta_{\textrm{c}}}d(x_{i},y_{j})^{p}+\frac{c^{p}}{2}(|\mathbf{x}|+|\mathbf{y}|-2|\theta_{\textrm{c}}|)+c^{p}(|\mathbf{z}|+|\theta_{\textrm{c}}|-|\theta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}|-|\theta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}|)

(27)

\displaystyle\geq\sum_{(i,j)\in\theta_{\textrm{c}}}d(x_{i},y_{j})^{p}+\frac{c^{p}}{2}(|\mathbf{x}|+|\mathbf{y}|-2|\theta_{\textrm{c}}|)

(28)

where (
27
) follows by dropping
A\geq 0
and applying the triangle inequality on the base metric
d
. The last inequality holds by applying the result of Lemma
4
.

Taking the
p
-th root in (
28
), and combining with (
25
), we obtain

\displaystyle C(\theta_{c},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\leq C(\theta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})+C(\theta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})

(29)

for any valid ordered assignments
\theta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}
and
\theta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}
. Choosing the optimal assignments, i.e.,
C(\theta_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z}}^{\star},\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})=d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{z})
and
C(\theta_{\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y}}^{\star},\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})=d^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}(\mathbf{z},\mathbf{y})
, we establish (
22
), since
\theta_{\textrm{c}}\in\Gamma^{\textrm{o}}_{\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}}
is a valid ordered assignment by Lemma
3
.
∎

Now the result of Theorem

follows immediately by the result of Lemma
3
and (

).

B.2
Proof of the triangle inequality for
SOSPA
normalization

The proof of triangle inequality for
\bar{d}^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}
is similar to that of
\bar{d}^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}
. We therefore demonstrate only the proof for this latter.

Appendix C
Proof of the triangle inequality for
PLD
normalization (
p=1
)

We prove that
\bar{d}^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}
is a metric for
p=1
. The proof follows the normalization framework of Li and Liu
[
8
]
. We proceed in three steps: (i) we define a
PLD
similarity measure analogous to the generalized Levenshtein similarity (GLS); (ii) we establish four key properties of this similarity (Theorem
2
); and (iii) we use these properties to prove that the normalized distance
\bar{d}^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}
satisfies the triangle inequality and is therefore a metric (Theorem
3
).

Definition 5

(
PLD
Similarity)
.

For
p=1
, we define
PLD
similarity between two sets of polylines
\mathbf{X}
and
\mathbf{Y}
is

\displaystyle S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=\frac{\frac{1}{2}\bigl(R_{\mathbf{X}}+R_{\mathbf{Y}}\bigr)-d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})}{2}.

(30)

Theorem 2

(GLS properties for DAP)
.

Let
p=1
. For any three sets of polylines
\mathbf{X}
,
\mathbf{Y}
,
\mathbf{Z}
, the GLS from Definition
5
satisfies:

(i)

S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})=\dfrac{1}{2}\,R_{\mathbf{X}}
,

(ii)

S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{X})
,

(iii)

0\leq S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\leq\min\bigl\{S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}),\;S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Y})\bigr\}=\dfrac{1}{2}\min\{R_{\mathbf{X}},R_{\mathbf{Y}}\}
,

(iv)

S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Y})+S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Z})\geq S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})+S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})
.

These are the direct analogues of properties (1)–(4) in Theorem 1 of
[
8
]
, with
\lambda=1/2
and
|\cdot|
replaced by
R_{(\cdot)}
.

Proof.

Before we show the properties we first establish few basic results.

Lemma 6
.

For
p\geq 1
, the
PLD
metric satisfies:

\displaystyle d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\leq\left(\frac{1}{2}(R_{\mathbf{X}}+R_{\mathbf{Y}})\right)^{1/p}

(31)

Proof.

The bound is obtained by using the empty assignment
\theta=\emptyset
in Definition
4
. With no matched pairs, all elements are unassigned, and the bound (
31
) follows.
∎

Lemma 7
.

For
p\geq 1
, the
PLD
metric satisfies:

\displaystyle d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\geq\left(\frac{1}{2}\left|R_{\mathbf{X}}-R_{\mathbf{Y}}\right|\right)^{1/p}

(32)

Proof.

Recall Definition
4
. Since
\bar{d}^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}(\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{y}_{j})\geq 0
and
\min(r_{\mathbf{x}_{i}},r_{\mathbf{y}_{j}})\geq 0
, we can lower bound by ignoring the non-negative localization terms

\displaystyle d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})^{p}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\min_{\theta\in\Gamma}\Bigg(\sum_{(i,j)\in\theta}\left|r_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}-r_{\mathbf{y}_{j}}\right|+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}i:\forall j,(i,j)\notin\theta\end{subarray}}r_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}+\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}j:\forall i,(i,j)\notin\theta\end{subarray}}r_{\mathbf{y}_{j}}\Bigg)

(33)

Recalling
R_{\mathbf{X}}=\sum_{i}r_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}
and
R_{\mathbf{Y}}=\sum_{j}r_{\mathbf{y}_{j}}
. Using the identity
|r_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}-r_{\mathbf{y}_{j}}|=r_{\mathbf{x}_{i}}+r_{\mathbf{y}_{j}}-2\min(r_{\mathbf{x}_{i}},r_{\mathbf{y}_{j}})
, (
33
) simplifies to

\displaystyle d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})^{p}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\min_{\theta\in\Gamma}\Bigg(R_{\mathbf{X}}+R_{\mathbf{Y}}-2\sum_{(i,j)\in\theta}\min(r_{\mathbf{x}_{i}},r_{\mathbf{y}_{j}})\Bigg)

The term
\sum_{(i,j)\in\theta}\min(r_{\mathbf{x}_{i}},r_{\mathbf{y}_{j}})
is maximized when we match elements optimally, but we can only match up to
\min(R_{\mathbf{X}},R_{\mathbf{Y}})
total probability. Therefore,

\displaystyle d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})^{p}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{2}\left(R_{\mathbf{X}}+R_{\mathbf{Y}}-2\min(R_{\mathbf{X}},R_{\mathbf{Y}})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left|R_{\mathbf{X}}-R_{\mathbf{Y}}\right|

(34)

Taking the
p
-th root of both sides yields (
32
).
∎

Now we can readily show the properties of
PLD
similarity.

Property (i).

Follows directly from
d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X})=0
.

Property (ii).

Follows from
d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{X})
.

Property (iii).

Lower bound.

From Lemma
6
, it follows that
S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\geq 0
.

Upper bound.

By Lemma
7
with
p=1
,
d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\geq\tfrac{1}{2}|R_{\mathbf{X}}-R_{\mathbf{Y}}|
. Substituting:

\displaystyle S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})

\displaystyle\leq\frac{\frac{1}{2}(R_{\mathbf{X}}+R_{\mathbf{Y}})-\frac{1}{2}|R_{\mathbf{X}}-R_{\mathbf{Y}}|}{2}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\min\{R_{\mathbf{X}},R_{\mathbf{Y}}\}.

(35)

By property (i), we obtain
S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})\leq\min\{S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}),S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Y})\}
.

Property (iv).

By the triangle inequality for
d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}

\displaystyle d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})+d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})\geq d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Z}).

(36)

Expanding using (
30
) and reshuffling, we reach

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\,R_{\mathbf{Y}}+S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Z})

\displaystyle\geq S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})+S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Z})

(37)

Substituting
S^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{Y},\mathbf{Y})=\tfrac{1}{2}R_{\mathbf{Y}}
(from property (i)), yields property (iv).
∎

Theorem 3

(Normalized
PLD
is a metric)
.

Let
p=1
. The normalized
PLD

\displaystyle\bar{d}^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})=\frac{2\,d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})}{\frac{1}{2}(R_{\mathbf{X}}+R_{\mathbf{Y}})+d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{Y})}

(38)

is a metric valued in
[0,1]
.

Proof.

Identity and symmetry follow directly from the corresponding properties of
d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}
. As for the triangle inequality, the proof follows the same argument as in
[
8
, Theorem 2]
.The four properties established in Theorem
2
mirror the result in
[
8
, Theorem 1]
, where for string edit distance with uniform deletion/insertion cost
\lambda
, the GLS is defined as
\mathrm{GLS}(X,Y)=\tfrac{\lambda(|X|+|Y|)-d(X,Y)}{2}
. In our setting,
\lambda=1/2
, and
R_{\mathbf{X}}
,
R_{\mathbf{Y}}
play the role of the string lengths
|X|
and
Y
, respectively.

Note that
d^{(c,p)}_{\text{PLD}}
is itself a metric for
p=1
, and its base single-instance metric
\bar{d}^{(c,p)}_{\text{SOSPA}}
(Definition
2
) is a metric as established in the main document. This ensures all prerequisites are met for the argument in
[
8
, Theorem 2]
to hold.
∎
