DynoJEPP: Joint Estimation, Prediction and Planning in Dynamic Environments
Mikolaj Kliniewski *, Jesse Morris, Yiduo Wang, Ian R. Manchester and Viorela Ila

Abstract
DynoJEPP is a factor-graph–based framework that jointly formulates and simultaneously optimizes estimation, prediction, and planning in dynamic environments. In conventional factor-graph-based approaches that jointly formulate estimation, prediction, and planning, information from prediction and planning feeds back into state estimation, yielding corrupted estimates, undesired behaviors, and unsafe plans. To address this, DynoJEPP introduces a novel directed factor that enforces directional information flow within the factor graph, preventing prediction and planning from corrupting state estimation. We evaluate the impact of directed factors on inter-module interactions during navigation in both static and dynamic environments. Our results demonstrate that these factors are critical for safe operation, as without them, the robot collides in the majority of experiments. Building on this, we further introduce Cooperative DynoJEPP, which enables the ego robot to incorporate cooperative object behavior into its prediction and trajectory planning.



I
Introduction

Real-world robotic systems must operate in the presence of dynamic objects, where complex and unpredictable motions pose a fundamental challenge to safe and reliable autonomy.

In static environments, recent approaches formulate both robot state estimation and path planning as instances of trajectory optimization
[
24
,
25
]
.
These problems can be solved within a unified factor-graph framework, reducing redundancy across traditionally separated modules
[
25
]
.
However, real-world environments are inherently dynamic, where understanding and predicting the motion of surrounding objects, together with the static scene structure, becomes essential for reliable navigation
[
31
,
28
,
12
,
10
,
15
]
.
While many solutions jointly formulate estimation and planning in static environments, their extension to dynamic scenes remains limited. As shown in Table
I
, existing approaches typically either assume future object trajectories are known
a priori

[
15
]
or rely on purely reactive avoidance strategies
[
1
]
, restricting their applicability in realistic dynamic environments.
Moreover, simultaneously solving estimation and planning can cause the planning component to adversely influence state estimates themselves, leading to overly optimistic, and ultimately unsafe plans.

:
A robot navigating a simulated warehouse environment with dynamic obstacles. The ego robot is black, while dynamic obstacles are red. The framework simultaneously estimates the ego pose and dynamic object motions, predicts the trajectories of moving obstacles, and plans a safe local path.

To address the challenges of operating in dynamic environments while properly managing simultaneous optimization, we propose
DynoJEPP
, a unified framework for joint estimation, prediction and planning in dynamic environments.

Robust estimation in dynamic environments is achieved by leveraging recent advances in Dynamic SLAM
[
21
,
23
,
14
,
5
]
that simultaneously localize the robot and map the scene while estimating the
\mathrm{SE}(3)
motions of dynamic objects.

Due to the large number of state variables and variety of representations used in Dynamic SLAM
[
21
]
, extracting the specific quantities required for prediction and planning is often non-trivial.
DynoJEPP addresses this by extending Dynamic SLAM into a single unified factor graph formulation that jointly estimates the robot pose and object motions, predicts future object states, and plans safe robot actions.
An example of the DynoJEPP framework operating in a simulation environment is shown in Fig.
1
.

To prevent planning and prediction from degrading estimation results in DynoJEPP,
we further propose a novel
directed factor
that
facilitates directional information flow between factor graph components.
We use this factor to propagate our estimation results directly into prediction and planning, whilst keeping estimation isolated from their influence.
To validate the directed factor’s necessity and show the functionalities of our framework,
we assess DynoJEPP on a variety of challenging scenarios in simulation.
Our experiment results demonstrate that DynoJEPP is able to consistently navigate safely through dynamic environments.
By comparing our method with and without directed factors, we clearly show the necessity of controlling the influence of prediction, planning and estimation on each other to ensure successful operations in dynamic environments.

To this end, the contributions of our work are as follows:

•

DynoJEPP
, a framework that jointly formulates and simultaneously optimizes robot and object state estimation, trajectory prediction and local planning in dynamic environments. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first factor-graph-based framework that jointly estimates dynamic object motion, predicts their evolution, and incorporates these predictions into robot control actions.

•

A novel
directed factor
that specifies the influence direction in a factor graph, allowing information flow among estimation, prediction and planning to be controlled.

•

An extension of our formulation parameterizing the level of dynamic object cooperation within the simultaneous optimization, enabled via directed factors.

III
Preliminaries

III-A

Notation

Our formulation defines robot poses, velocities and accelerations as:

\mathcal{X}=\{\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{X}}_{k}\}_{{k\in\mathcal{K}}},\;\mathcal{V}=\{\prescript{X_{k}}{}{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}},\;\mathcal{A}=\{\prescript{X_{k}}{}{\mathbf{a}}_{k}\}_{k\in\mathcal{K}}

where
\{W\}
is the world frame and
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{X}}_{k}\in\mathrm{SE}(3)
is the robot’s body-fixed frame.

\mathcal{K}
is the set of all time-steps.
As the time-step of the velocity and acceleration is always identical to that of their reference frames, we simplify their notations to
\prescript{X_{k}}{}{\mathbf{v}}_{k}\equiv\prescript{X}{}{\mathbf{v}}_{k},\prescript{X_{k}}{}{\mathbf{a}}_{k}\equiv\prescript{X}{}{\mathbf{a}}_{k}
.

In a dynamic scene,
an object pose at time-step
k
in frame
\{W\}
is denoted as
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{L}}_{k}\in\mathrm{SE}(3)
and is associated with a body-fixed reference frame
\{L_{k}\}
.
A 3D point is denoted as
\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{m}}^{i}
, where
i

denotes the point index.

We denote the rigid-body motion of an object between arbitrary time-steps
A
and
B
as
\prescript{W}{A}{\mathbf{H}}_{B}\in\mathrm{SE}(3)
following the Dynamic SLAM works
[
11
,
23
]
:

\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{L}}_{B}=\prescript{W}{A}{\mathbf{H}}_{B}\>\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{L}}_{A}\text{.}

(1)

III-B

Factor Graph Optimization

A factor graph represents a function over a set of variables
\bm{\theta}
as a product of factors where each factor
\phi_{i}
encodes a relationship over a subset of variables expressing how those variables are coupled, e.g. through measurements, costs, or constraints and is written in exponential residual form:

\phi_{i}(\theta_{i})\propto\exp\!\left(-\frac{1}{2}\,\|r_{i}(\cdot)\|_{\Sigma_{i}}^{2}\right),

(2)

where
r_{i}(\cdot)
is a residual function and
\Sigma_{i}
is a positive definite weighting matrix.
Maximum a posteriori (MAP) inference corresponds to minimizing the total objective:

\bm{\theta}^{\star}=\arg\min_{\bm{\theta}}\sum_{i}\|r_{i}(\cdot)\|_{\Sigma_{i}}^{2}.

(3)

This nonlinear least–squares problem is solved iteratively by linearizing the residuals
around the current estimate of
\bm{\theta}
. Denoting the stacked residual vector by
\mathbf{r}

and its Jacobian by
\mathbf{J}
, the increment
\bm{\delta}
satisfies:

\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{J}\,\bm{\delta}=-\,\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{r}.

(4)

IV
Directed Factor

Future robot plans should not influence estimates of current or past states, as these are determined entirely by existing measurements.
However, existing frameworks that simultaneously solve estimation and planning
[
24
,
25
,
15
]
allow information to propagate bidirectionally between past and future states.
In this section, we demonstrate that by allowing this, the planning will degrade the estimation results.

In Fig.
2
, we construct a static 2D toy example highlighting the degradation effect and its resolution by the directed factor.
The robot moves from
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{X}}_{k-1}
to
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{X}}_{k}
and plans two poses ahead using a constant velocity model as shown in Fig.
2
(a).
For estimation,
the robot measures a static map point
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{m}}^{0}
at time-steps
k-1
and
k
, seen in the corresponding factor graph Fig.
2
(b).
In Fig.
2
(c, d) a similar setup considers the static obstacle in the plan through the use of static obstacle factors which encourage collision-free trajectories.

In the initial setup (Fig.
2
(a, b)), the estimation correctly reflects all measurements, but the robot plans into unsafe regions.
By taking into account the static obstacle (Fig.
2
(c, d)), the planned trajectory becomes collision free; however, estimated states and their uncertainties are erroneously influenced by additional constraints added by factors involved in planning.
The goal is therefore to create a single factor-graph that retains the accurate estimation from Fig.
2
(a) while also planning collision-free trajectories, as in Fig.
2
(c).

To achieve this, we propose a directed factor that enforces unidirectional information flow, as illustrated in Fig.
2
(f).
The directed factor connects
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{X}}_{k}
and
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{X}}_{k+1}
, creating a directional connection between estimation and planning.
Past variables
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{m}}^{0},\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{X}}_{k-1}
and
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{X}}_{k}
become independent of planning,
while planning remains dependent on the current Maximum a Posterior (MAP) estimate of
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{X}}_{k}
.
The estimated and planned trajectory from this new factor graph is visualized in Fig.
2
(e).
Compared with Fig.
2
(a), this new factor graph yields equivalent estimation results and uncertainties while planning around the obstacle.

Fig. 3
:
Sparsity pattern of the factor graph in Fig.
2
(f). Estimation-related entries are green, and planning-related entries are orange. Solid red entries are zeroed by the directed factor, while red-striped entries are partially affected but remain non-zero.

The directed factor is realized via
Jacobian manipulation
.
By zeroing the Jacobian with respect to the source variable, we ensure it remains unaffected by the factor’s gradient.
As illustrated in Fig.
3
, this modification removes the non-zero entries that would otherwise form off-diagonal cross terms in
\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{J}
.
Based on Eq. (
4
), the resulting normal equation for the factor graph in Fig.
2
(f) becomes:

\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{J}\bm{\delta}\!=\!\begin{bmatrix}{\color[rgb]{0,0.6211,0.4492}\blacksquare}\mathbf{m}^{0},\mathbf{X}_{k-1},\mathbf{X}_{k}\\[3.00003pt]
{\color[rgb]{0,0.6211,0.4492}\blacksquare}\mathbf{m}^{0},\mathbf{X}_{k-1},\mathbf{X}_{k}\\[3.00003pt]
{\color[rgb]{0,0.6211,0.4492}\blacksquare}\mathbf{m}^{0},\mathbf{X}_{k-1},\mathbf{X}_{k}\\[3.00003pt]
{\color[rgb]{0.8281,0.5547,0}\blacksquare}\mathbf{X}_{k+1},\mathbf{X}_{k+2}\\[3.00003pt]
{\color[rgb]{0.8281,0.5547,0}\blacksquare}\mathbf{X}_{k+1},\mathbf{X}_{k+2}\end{bmatrix}\!=\!\begin{bmatrix}{\color[rgb]{0,0.6211,0.4492}\blacksquare}\mathbf{r}_{5},\mathbf{r}_{6}\\[3.00003pt]
{\color[rgb]{0,0.6211,0.4492}\blacksquare}\mathbf{r}_{1},\mathbf{r}_{2},\mathbf{r}_{5}\\[3.00003pt]
{\color[rgb]{0,0.6211,0.4492}\blacksquare}\mathbf{r}_{2},\mathbf{r}_{6}\\[3.00003pt]
{\color[rgb]{0.8281,0.5547,0}\blacksquare}\mathbf{r}_{3},\mathbf{r}_{4},\mathbf{r}_{7}\\[3.00003pt]
{\color[rgb]{0.8281,0.5547,0}\blacksquare}\mathbf{r}_{4},\mathbf{r}_{8}\end{bmatrix}\!=\!-\mathbf{J}^{\top}\mathbf{r}\text{.}

(5)

The absence of cross terms reflects the structural decoupling between estimation and planning. Importantly, the RHS of Eq. (
5
) shows that the update for
\mathbf{X}_{k+1}
still depends on the current linearized value of
\mathbf{X}_{k}
through the residual
\mathbf{r}_{3}
, despite the zeroed cross terms.
Because cross terms are removed, uncertainty does not propagate from the directed variable. For the local planner, this is not restrictive, as the robot remains well localized relative to nearby objects through recent measurements.
In practice, global uncertainty is accounted for by a separate module that provides the objective for the local goals.
Furthermore, since estimation, prediction and planning are distinct cost objectives, this decoupling ensures that weights or covariances in one module do not influence the other.
In large dynamic graphs, this prevents the growing estimation sub-graph from shifting the overall objective balance.
By isolating these cross-module interactions, the framework maintains consistent behavior without frequent retuning.

V
DynoJEPP

DynoJEPP processes sensor measurements to generate a static-dynamic map estimate, predict object motions, and plan local trajectories and control actions.
Prediction and planning are scene-aware and are optimized simultaneously with the estimation as new data is received.

V-A

Jointly Formulated Estimation, Prediction & Planning

DynoJEPP solves the optimization problem represented by the factor graph shown in Fig.
4
, which integrates estimation, prediction and planning.
The estimation component resembles a typical SLAM factor graph, where
\phi
in Eq. (
2
) represents measurement models and
\theta
in Eq. (
3
) denotes estimated variables.
Our formulation additionally incorporates variables representing object motion predictions, planned robot states, and control commands, with factors modeling the costs for desired object and ego trajectories.

Given the joint formulation, our formulation critically depends on the proposed directed factors (highlighted by the red arrow in Fig.
4
) to enforce a hierarchical coupling: estimation remains independent of prediction and planning, prediction depends on estimation, and planning is informed by both.
This architecture acts as a hybrid of two classical models: directed acyclic graphs for causal dependencies between modules, and undirected graphs for the internal structure of each component
[
17
]
. By ensuring information only flows downstream, this structure prevents prediction and planning objectives from altering the state estimate.

V-B

State Estimation

To accurately estimate for the dynamic scene, our method builds upon the Hybrid Dynamic SLAM formulation proposed in
[
22
]
.
This approach estimates the robot pose
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{X}}_{k}
and the per-frame motion
\prescript{W}{e}{\mathbf{H}}_{k}
of each object.
The static structure and per-object map are additionally estimated for and refined over multiple time-steps.
Rather than directly estimating the object pose at each time-step
k
,
this formulation estimates the motion
\prescript{W}{e}{\mathbf{H}}_{k}
which maps any fixed frame on the object from time-step
e
to
k
following Eq. (
1
).
The object pose
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{L}}_{k}
can be recovered for any
k
given an initial frame
\{L_{e}\}
. All factors in the estimation component follow
[
22
]
, and are omitted here due to space constraints, slight modifications are detailed below.

This work implicitly models all objects as spheres, therefore their pose is considered
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{L}}_{e}=\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{C}}_{e}
where
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{C}}_{e}
is the object’s Center of Mass (CoM) at
e
.
Thus the estimated motion will track the object’s CoM in the world frame for each
k
as
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{C}}_{e}
is a constant for each object:

\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{C}}_{k}=\prescript{W}{e}{\mathbf{H}}_{k}\>\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{C}}_{e}\>.

(6)

In practice,
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{C}}_{e}
can be approximated from accumulated reconstructions, learning-based methods, or known models.

We denote all residuals that depend on the object pose with
\mathbf{r}(\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{C}}_{k})
.
This indicates that they are connected to the motion variable
\prescript{W}{e}{\mathbf{H}}_{k}
and include the constant
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{C}}_{e}
such that the full pose can be recovered via Eq. (
6
).

At each step
k
, observations of both the static and dynamic environment are received and integrated using
point measurement
and
hybrid motion factors
.
Point measurement factors link the robot pose to tracked static points
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{m}}
, while each hybrid motion factor connects observations of tracked dynamic points to the rigid-body motion model
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{H}}
and the corresponding robot pose.
The
odometry factor
links consecutive robot poses through a measured relative transformation, obtained from visual odometry or, when available, from the IMU.

Finally, the
object smoothing factor
imposes a constant motion model between pairs of motion variables, promoting locally consistent and physically plausible trajectories:

\mathbf{r}_{\delta H}=\log\Bigg[\left(\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{C}}_{k-2}^{-1}\>\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{C}}_{k-1}\right)^{-1}\left(\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{C}}_{k-1}^{-1}\>\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{C}}_{k}\right)\Bigg]^{\vee}\text{.}

(7)

As described in
[
22
]
this factor is conceptually similar to constant velocity model but encoded in
\mathrm{SE}(3)
.

V-C

Object Motion Prediction

The prediction module in Fig.
4
utilizes the estimated
\mathrm{SE}(3)
motions of dynamic objects from Section
V-B
, to forecast future trajectories over a horizon of
N
steps.

Since our method builds upon the Dynamic SLAM formulation proposed in
[
23
,
22
]
, the estimated object motions are inherently smooth and require no additional preprocessing
[
16
]
, enabling future motions
\mathcal{H}_{k+1:k+N}
to be predicted using only the two most recent object motion estimations.
This is implemented via the
object smoothing factor
Eq. (
7
), which enforces a constant velocity motion model and maintains temporal consistency.
In order to prevent future predictions from influencing past estimates, we use directed factors whenever estimation variables
\prescript{W}{e}{\mathbf{H}}_{k-1}
or
\prescript{W}{e}{\mathbf{H}}_{k}
are involved.

V-D

Local Planning in Action Space

The planning component of our framework (Fig.
4
) employs a factor-graph-based MPC to optimize a local trajectory over a horizon of
N
steps.
The optimized variables are future robot poses
\mathcal{X}_{k+1:k+N}
, velocities
\mathcal{V}_{k+1:k+N}
, and accelerations
\mathcal{A}_{k:k+N-1}
.
While the estimation and prediction modules operate in
\mathrm{SE}(3)
, control in our experiments is executed in
\mathrm{SE}(2)
under a planar motion assumption.
The formulation can naturally extend to
\mathrm{SE}(3)
.
All factors that involve the planned robot pose follow the mapping:
\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{X}}_{k}\in SE(3)\mapsto\mathbf{x}_{k}=(x_{k},y_{k},\theta_{k})\in\mathrm{SE}(2)
.

We follow the work of Bazzana
et al
.
[
4
]
and model our robot as a unicycle controlled in translational and angular velocities such that
\mathbf{v}_{k}=[v_{k},\omega_{k}]

using the kinematic model
\mathbf{x}_{k+1}=f(\mathbf{x}_{k},\mathbf{v}_{k})
:

\begin{split}x_{k+1}&=x_{k}+v_{k}\Delta t\cos\left(\theta_{k}+{\omega_{k}\Delta t}/2\right)\\
y_{k+1}&=y_{k}+v_{k}\Delta t\sin\left(\theta_{k}+{\omega_{k}\Delta t}/2\right)\\
\theta_{k+1}&=\theta_{k}+\omega_{k}\Delta t\end{split}

(8)

which propagates the current
\mathrm{SE}(2)
pose
\mathbf{x}_{k}
to the next pose
\mathbf{x}_{k+1}
over an integration interval
\Delta t
.

To inform future velocities and accelerations in the MPC, the robot requires information about its current state;
thus, the known current velocity
\mathbf{v}_{k}
and previously applied acceleration
\mathbf{a}_{k-1}
are fixed via a
prior factor
.
Furthermore, all future-related factors connected to known or estimated variables, i.e.
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{X}}_{k}
,
\mathbf{v}_{k}
,
\mathbf{a}_{k-1}
are directed to prevent planned states from influencing the estimation.

A
motion model factor
models the kinematics in Eq. (
8
) to ensure consistency with the robot’s dynamics, relating acceleration to velocity and pose updates:

\mathbf{r}_{\text{dyn}}=\begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{X}_{k+1}^{-1}\>f(\mathbf{X}_{k},\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{v}}_{k+1})\\
\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{v}}_{k+1}-(\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{v}}_{k}+\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{a}}_{k}\Delta t)\end{bmatrix}\text{.}

(9)

We utilize
limit factors

[
15
]
to keep velocities
\mathcal{V}_{k+1:k+N}
and accelerations
\mathcal{A}_{k:k+N-1}
within desired bounds.
Unary
cost factors

\mathbf{r}_{c}=\mathbf{a}_{k}
penalize non-zero accelerations to encourage constant velocity,
while
constant acceleration factors

\mathbf{r}_{ca}=a_{k}-a_{k-1}
smooth control inputs.
Finally, a
goal factor

r_{\text{goal}}=\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{X}}_{\text{goal}}^{-1}\>\prescript{}{}{\mathbf{X}}_{k+N}
is applied to the
(k+N)^{\text{th}}
pose to guide the robot toward a local goal.
This mission objective can be replaced or augmented by other factors attached to the final or all planned poses, depending on the desired task.

V-E

Static Scene-Aware Prediction & Planning

To incorporate static scene awareness, the framework interfaces with a static scene map via a Euclidean Signed Distance Field (ESDF)
[
27
]
. This information is integrated into the formulation through
static obstacle factors
:

\mathbf{r}_{\text{sdf}}=\begin{cases}d_{\text{os}}-\text{esdf}(\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{P}}_{k}),&\text{esdf}(\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{P}}_{k})<d_{\text{os}}\\
0,&\text{otherwise}\end{cases}\text{,}

(10)

where the evaluated pose
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{P}}_{k}\in\{\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{C}}_{k},\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{X}}_{k}\}
corresponds to either a predicted object pose or a planned robot pose. Notably,
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{C}}_{k}
is a function of the predicted motion
\prescript{W}{e}{\mathbf{H}}_{k}
following Eq. (
6
).
The function
\text{esdf}(\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{P}}_{k})
looks up the distance value in the static map using coordinates given by the translational component of
\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{P}}_{k}
, while
d_{\text{os}}
accounts for the respective robot or object radius plus a safety offset.

In prediction, these factors relax the constant-motion assumption to yield more realistic behaviors near obstacles, while
object smoothing factors
ensure the resulting motions remain smooth.
In planning, they provide the necessary information for collision-free navigation.

Since the ESDF map used by these factors is precomputed, state estimation is supported by low-frequency global localization factors, to maintain alignment with the static environment. In practice, this can be achieved by online mapping
[
29
]
.

V-F

Dynamic Scene-Aware Prediction & Planning

To incorporate dynamic scene awareness, the formulation links predicted object motions with planned robot states via directed
dynamic obstacle factors
, which penalize proximity to moving objects:

\displaystyle\epsilon

\displaystyle=d_{\text{ros}}-\text{range}(\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{X}}_{k},\>\prescript{W}{}{\mathbf{C}}_{k})\text{,}

where
d_{\text{ros}}
is the sum of the robot and object radii plus a safety offset.
While object radii can be obtained from dynamic mapping

These factors are directed to ensure the robot plan adapts to predictions without affecting the predicted object motions.

Cooperative DynoJEPP (C-DynoJEPP) extends the DynoJEPP framework by incorporating cooperative behaviors of predicted dynamic objects into the simultaneous optimization, enabling less conservative local plans.
Cooperative behavior assumes mutual influence: the ego robot reacts to the object’s motion, while the dynamic object is expected to react to the robot’s actions.
We model the interaction between the robot’s future plan and the object’s predicted motion using the
dynamic obstacle factors
in Eq. (
11
). As shown by the ’C’ factors in Fig.
4
, these are now directed from planning to prediction to explicitly model the influence of the robot’s intended path on the object prediction.

C-DynoJEPP introduces a directed cycle between prediction and planning, enabling mutual influence while maintaining estimation independent.
Standard undirected factors are insufficient here, as their uniform weighting eliminates the flexibility to independently tune prediction and planning costs.
Alternatively, replicating this simultaneous influence with a decoupled architecture is inefficient, requiring alternating between modules and fixing intermediate solutions.
The degree of cooperation is governed by the ‘C’ factor weights.
While these remain constant throughout the execution in this work, updating them online would allow shifting between conservative and assertive behavior of agents.

VI
Experiments

This section evaluates the impact of directed factors within the DynoJEPP and C-DynoJEPP frameworks.
Rather than comparing to external baselines, we analyze how these factors regulate inter-module information flow
to prevent estimation degradation and enable safe planning.
We compare three configurations:
Undirected
, allowing free bidirectional information propagation between modules;

Directed
, which regulates flow via directed factors; and
Decoupled
, a baseline where estimation is optimized independently, followed by simultaneous prediction and planning.
The factor graph structure (Fig.
4
) and covariance parameters remain identical across all configurations for fairness of comparison.

Experiments are conducted in a simulated warehouse using Gazebo (Fig.

The ego robot is a differential drive equipped with an Intel RealSense D435 RGB-D camera, with Gaussian noise (
\sigma=$0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{m}$
) added to per-pixel depth measurements.
Objects are only detected when they appear in the camera field of view.
The static environment is mapped offline using SLAM Toolbox
[
19
]
and converted to a 2D ESDF. Low-frequency GPS-like pose measurements are provided to maintain map alignment.

A global reference path for the ego robot is generated via the Nav2 stack
[
20
]
using Dijkstra’s algorithm, designed to pass near static obstacles to elicit local avoidance.
The local planner uses a horizon of
N=30
with
\Delta t=$0.1\text{\,}\mathrm{s}$
.
In all dynamic scenarios, moving obstacles are differential drive robots using the Nav2 MPPI controller.
While these agents follow predefined trajectories in Section

Overall, directed factors are critical for robust navigation in both static and dynamic scenarios.

VII
Conclusion and Future Work

This paper introduces
DynoJEPP
, a factor-graph-based framework for simultaneous estimation,
\mathrm{SE}(3)
motion prediction, and local planning in dynamic environments.
We proposed a novel
directed factor
to enforce causal dependencies, effectively enabling control over the information flow among architecture components.
Experimental validation in dynamic scenarios confirms that controlling this information flow is critical for successful navigation, allowing the framework to safely avoid both static and dynamic obstacles.
Our analysis indicates that while estimation does not inherently benefit from joint optimization, prediction and planning can benefit from it, particularly when modeling interactive dynamic object behavior, as in the
C-DynoJEPP
extension.
Future research will focus on developing an efficient, incremental, and multi-threaded implementation of DynoJEPP to achieve real-time performance. This involves leveraging incremental dynamic SLAM
[
22
]
, online mapping
[
32
]
, and benchmarking against industry-standard navigation frameworks such as Nav2
[
20
]
.
