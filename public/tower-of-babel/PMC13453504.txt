Clinical insights into syncope and presyncope during graded exercise testing: A 40,715-test experience
Authors: Keti Mamillo, Ibolya Csécs, Maria Irene Barillas Lara, Andrés Garzona-Navas, Amanda R Bonikowske, Thomas G Allison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSyncope and presyncope during graded exercise testing (GXT) are uncommon but clinically important events that may reflect hemodynamic, autonomic, conductionrelated, or arrhythmic mechanisms. We reviewed all clinically significant syncope and presyncope occurring during non-imaging GXTs performed at Mayo Clinic between January 2006 and December 2010. All clinically significant events underwent detailed chart review incorporating exercise findings, clinical documentation, and longitudinal follow-up to systematically adjudicate the most likely mechanism. Among 40,715 GXTs performed in 33,885 unique patients, eight episodes of syncope (0.20 per 1000 tests) and five episodes of hemodynamically significant presyncope (0.12 per 1000 tests) required test termination. Five additional patients with transient lightheadedness without exercise-induced hypotension or clinically significant arrhythmia and five mechanical treadmill events without hemodynamic compromise were excluded from the primary analysis. Only two events were attributed to a documented arrhythmic mechanism. Most episodes were adjudicated as hemodynamic, autonomic/neurocardiogenic, or indeterminate. Syncope occurred predominantly in younger women and was generally associated with sinus rhythm and hypotensive or autonomic mechanisms, whereas presyncope occurred predominantly in older men with underlying cardiovascular disease, atrial arrhythmias, bradycardia, or chronotropic incompetence. Clinically significant syncope and presyncope during supervised GXT were exceedingly uncommon and were rarely associated with a documented arrhythmic mechanism. These findings support the safety of supervised exercise testing in appropriately monitored settings and improve understanding of the mechanisms underlying these uncommon exercise laboratory events.Keywords: Syncope, Presyncope, Exercise testing, Cardiopulmonary exercise testing, Hemodynamic instability, Treadmill exercise testing

Graded exercise testing (GXT) is widely used to evaluate exercise capacity, myocardial ischemia, and cardiac arrhythmias and is generally considered safe [1]. Syncope and presyncope during supervised GXT may reflect hypotension, autonomic dysfunction, conduction abnormalities, or clinically significant arrhythmias [3]. Contemporary large-scale data describing these events remain limited [2], [4]. We therefore reviewed all clinically significant syncope and presyncope during guideline-directed GXT to characterize their presentation, exercise findings, and outcomes.

We reviewed the Mayo Clinic Integrated Stress Center database for all non-imaging graded exercise tests (GXTs) performed between January 2006 and December 2010. All clinically significant events underwent chart review, including exercise findings, clinical documentation, and longitudinal follow-up, to adjudicate the most likely mechanism. Events were classified as hemodynamic, autonomic/neurocardiogenic, documented arrhythmic, or indeterminate when no single non-arrhythmic mechanism could be confidently assigned despite the absence of a documented arrhythmic mechanism.

Hemodynamically significant presyncope was defined as lightheadedness or near-syncope associated with symptomatic hypotension requiring test termination or clinical intervention. Patients with transient lightheadedness without hypotension or clinically significant arrhythmia and those with mechanical treadmill events without hemodynamic compromise were excluded from the primary analysis. The study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Review Board.

During the study period, 40,715 GXTs were performed in 33,885 unique patients, including 16,306 tests (40.0%) in individuals with known cardiovascular disease. The most common referral indications were evaluation of suspected coronary artery disease, follow-up of known coronary artery disease, and assessment of cardiac arrhythmias. Hemodynamically significant events were exceptionally rare. Eight episodes of syncope occurred (0.20 per 1000 tests; 0.23 per 1000 patients), and five episodes of hemodynamically significant presyncope (0.12 per 1000 tests; 0.15 per 1000 patients) required test termination. Five additional patients experienced transient lightheadedness without exercise-induced hypotension or clinically significant arrhythmia, and five mechanical treadmill events occurred without hemodynamic compromise; these events were excluded from the primary analysis (Fig. 1).

Study population and clinical characteristics of patients with clinically significant syncope and presyncope during graded exercise testing.

Detailed chart review identified a documented arrhythmic mechanism in two patients. The remaining events were adjudicated as hemodynamic, autonomic/neurocardiogenic, or indeterminate (Fig. 1).

Distinct clinical patterns were observed following detailed chart review. Syncope occurred predominantly in younger women (19–43 years) and was most often associated with sinus rhythm and hypotension or autonomic mechanisms. In contrast, presyncope occurred predominantly in older men (57–86 years), more frequently in the setting of underlying cardiovascular disease, atrial arrhythmias, bradycardia, or chronotropic incompetence. No deaths or significant musculoskeletal injuries were directly attributable to exercise testing.

Clinically significant syncope and presyncope during supervised GXT were exceedingly uncommon and rarely associated with a documented arrhythmic mechanism. Systematic chart review demonstrated that most events were more consistent with hemodynamic or autonomic mechanisms, emphasizing the importance of integrating exercise findings with subsequent clinical evaluation rather than assuming malignant ventricular arrhythmia. Although limited by its retrospective single-center design and small number of events, our findings further support the safety of supervised exercise testing in appropriately monitored settings [4], [5] and provide insight into the mechanisms underlying these events.
