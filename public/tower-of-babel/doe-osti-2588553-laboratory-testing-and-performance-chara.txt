Executive Summary
The high efficiency dehumidification system (HEDS) is a unique air handling unit (AHU) design
with two water coils developed by Conservant Systems. Connected in series and employing a
counter-flow design, the HEDS technology can sensibly reheat the airstream after it is cooled
and dehumidified, thereby mitigating (or sometimes eliminating) the need for conventional
sensible reheat with additional energy from hot water, electric resistance or steam coils. Another
unique design feature is the size of the HEDS primary cooling coil. While typical AHU cooling
coils have a 6-row geometry, the HEDS cooling coil has a deeper 10-row geometry, which
enables its discharge air dew point to achieve 55°F or below despite the chilled water supply
temperature near or even exceeding 50°F. The NREL laboratory testing explored this capability
confirming the HEDS technology was able to maintain a 55°F discharge air dew point with a
50°F chilled water supply temperature despite inlet air conditions exceeding 70°F dew point. The
ability to meet acceptable discharge air dew points with warmer chilled water supply
temperatures improves both operational efficiency and system resilience against chilled water
plant operational or control issues.

Figure ES-1 shows how the two water coils operate in series. The air-side and water-side
performance reported here are from the laboratory test results of the HEDS technology
conducted at the National Renewable Energy Laboratory (NREL). The operation of a HEDS unit
is a two-stage process. First, the entering air is cooled and dehumidified in the primary cooling
coil. Because of its deep 10-row geometry and low airflow velocity (typically less than 350 feet
per minute for mixed air systems and 200 feet per minute for DOAS units), the chilled water
temperature leaving the cooling coil is significantly warmer than a conventional AHU coil. For
the second stage of the HEDS unit, this warm water enters the cooling recovery coil and provides
sensible reheat to the airstream. As shown in Figure ES-1, a three-way valve provides a bypass
around the cooling recovery coil to control the amount of reheat provided, which is controlled
based on a target supply air dry-bulb temperature.

This configuration results in performance benefits by improving moisture removal in mild to
humid climates. It also mitigates condensation in the supply ductwork, grills and conditioned
spaces by reheating the discharge air away from being near full saturation (100% relative
humidity) before it leaves the HEDS unit.

Although the HEDS technology does not eliminate the need for reheat at terminal units (if they
exist in the air distribution system), it provides some decoupling of the latent and sensible
cooling. It can yield significant energy savings in warm-humid and hot-humid climates by
reducing (or sometimes eliminating) the sensible reheat required at the AHU through
conventional hot water, electric resistance or steam coils. For mildly humid climates, its energy
savings magnitude will be smaller, yet it may enable the HVAC design to eliminate the need for
a separate reheat coil in the AHU and even reheat coils in the terminal units.

In 2016, the Environmental Security Technology Certification Program (ESTCP) conducted two
HEDS field demonstrations, one at Fort Liberty in Fayetteville, North Carolina, and the second
at Tinker Air Force Base in Oklahoma City, Oklahoma. While both demonstrations provided
valuable insight into the HEDS design integration and its impacts on resiliency, operations and
maintenance, both demonstrations experienced chiller plant control issues unrelated to the HEDS
technology. Those issues prevented a definitive quantification of how the HEDS technology
impacted space thermal comfort and energy savings.

As a result, ESTCP is conducting another HEDS field demonstration at the Naval Exchange
building on Naval Base Guam. In addition, ESTCP is coordinating with another HEDS field
demonstration at the Timken Museum of Art in San Diego, California, funded by the U.S.
Department of Energy. Both field demonstrations have properly operating chilled water systems,
so they will provide ESTCP the field data needed to quantify the HEDS impact on space thermal
comfort and energy savings.

In parallel with these two field demonstrations, ESTCP has also funded the laboratory testing
and energy model characterization of a HEDS unit at the Thermal Test Facility located at NREL
in Golden, Colorado. This report summarizes the 59 steady-state test results that form a
comprehensive performance map of the HEDS technology including its reheat capability
described above. The breadth of outdoor air conditions and flow rates over which the HEDS
technology was tested and characterized encompasses the unit 1) operating as a dedicated
outdoor air system or mixed air system and 2) controlled with constant air volume or variable air
volume fan control. In other words, these 59 steady-state tests provide a comprehensive
performance map for all operating and control variations of the HEDS technology.

After the performance map of the 59 steady-state results was complete, NREL researchers
trained a black-box model of the HEDS technology. This model can be used to simulate HEDS
within a building energy modeling program such as EnergyPlus™. Instead of U.S. Department
of Defense (DOD) stakeholders (i.e., design engineers and energy managers) having to
approximate the HEDS impact for their unique facility, HVAC design and climate based on the
field demonstration results, they are now empowered to quantify the space thermal comfort and
energy saving impacts more accurately within EnergyPlus™ or a similar energy modeling
program. This model characterizes the HEDS performance when operating as a dedicated

outdoor air system or mixed air system, and with constant air volume or variable air volume fan
control.

The HEDS technology can be paired with a more advanced sequence of operation which adjusts
the discharge air dry-bulb and dew point temperatures based on the space temperature and dew
point trends. The laboratory testing and report here did not evaluate the advanced sequence of
operation. This controls capability will be covered in more detail with the ESTCP report of the
HEDS field demonstration at Naval Base Guam.

The process of laboratory characterization of an emerging technology and subsequent training of
a model from those data does not replace the valuable insights gleaned from a field
demonstration. Rather, these activities complement one another. Field demonstrations capture
the design integration, potential resiliency benefits and impacts to operations and maintenance –
these elements cannot be simulated in a building energy modeling environment. In addition, the
measurement and verification (M&V) data from field demonstrations is critical to validating the
black-box model trained from laboratory testing data.

This report begins with a description of the laboratory setup and the design of the HEDS unit
under test. Then we review the design of experiments for the 59 steady-state tests. Finally, we
discuss the test results and how the HEDS black-box model was trained by correlating the unit’s
sensible heat ratio to its latent part load ratio, chilled water supply temperature and airflow rate
fraction. The NREL test results in both imperial and metric units can be found in the shared
spreadsheet here. All testing was conducted at NREL’s Thermal Test Facility in Golden
Colorado at 5,850 feet elevation. The test results were converted to Sea Level performance and
are provided in the shared spreadsheet as well. The following report details the psychrometric
approach to convert the test results from NREL’s elevation to Sea Level.

For the next step in the HEDS project for ESTCP, NREL will validate this HEDS model using
the M&V data from the two HEDS field demonstrations at Naval Base Guam and the Timken
Museum of Art. The final HEDS report for ESTCP will include the M&V results from the Naval
Base Guam and Timken Museum demonstration, the validation of the HEDS black-box model
using the M&V data, and examples of how to simulate the HEDS technology in a prototypical
DOD Barracks facility, across several climates, using the EnergyPlus modeling environment.

1 Introduction
The following report summarizes the laboratory characterization and model training of a unique
air handling unit (AHU) technology called the high efficiency dehumidification system (HEDS).
The technology was developed to provide multiple benefits including HVAC energy savings,
improved ventilation without compromising humidity control and addressing biological growth
by preventing condensation on HVAC surfaces. Its design uses two water coils in series to cool
and dehumidify the airstream in the first coil and then sensibly reheat the airstream in the second
coil without needing additional energy input. Leveraging the chilled water (CHW) to provide
sensible reheat can mitigate (or sometimes eliminate) the need for reheat from conventional hot
water or electric resistance coils. Figure 1 shows the general layout of the HEDS technology.

The first coil, called the cooling coil, cools and dehumidifies the air down to a desired dew point
setpoint. The second coil, called the cooling recovery coil (CRC), uses the warm water leaving
the cooling coil to sensibly reheat the airstream to a desired dry-bulb setpoint. Combined, these
coils partially decouple the sensible cooling from latent cooling delivered to the airstream. The
result is a unique AHU design that can achieve a lower sensible heat ratio (SHR), which is
desirable in humid climates because the supply air dew point is sufficiently low to maintain
proper humidity levels in the space while providing more space neutral (less cool) dry-bulb
temperatures to avoid overcooling. In other words, achieving lower SHR operation enables U.S.
Department of Defense (DOD) operations teams to achieve more desirable space dry-bulb
temperatures and relative humidity conditions that promote better thermal comfort and defend
against moisture issues such as mold. Conventional AHU designs achieve lower SHR operation
using additional energy providing sensible reheat with a hot water coil served by a boiler or an
electric resistance coil. Instead, the HEDS technology can provide the reheat mostly (or entirely)
using the cooling recovery coil (CRC). As an additional benefit, the discharge air leaving the
HEDS unit is not saturated, which significantly reduces the risk of condensation and associated
mold growth in the supply ductwork, grills and occupied spaces.

1.1 Technology Description
The HEDS technology is a two-stage process. Entering air is first cooled and dehumidified
through a deep, 10-row CHW coil called the cooling coil. Compared to a conventional AHU
with a 6-row coil, the deeper cooling coil in the HEDS unit can remove more energy from the
airstream, resulting in significantly warmer water temperatures leaving the coil. This warmer
water is then supplied to the CRC. As the sub-cooled air leaving the cooling coil passes through
the CRC, the warmer water reheats the airstream. The final supply air is at a relative humidity
sufficiently below full saturation (100% relative humidity) which reduces opportunities for
condensation that can occur on supply ductwork and grills. Additionally, the chilled water return
is partially cooled while the air is reheated, which reduces the cooling load on the chilled water
plant by an amount equal to the sensible reheat the CRC provided to the supply air.

If less sensible reheat is needed for temperature control, a three-way valve can bypass water flow
around the CRC. Figure 2 provides a similar schematic to the one in Figure 1 but includes a more
detailed description of the laboratory tests at the National Renewable Energy Laboratory
(NREL). The reported measurements show how the hot-humid air is cooled and dehumidified
down to a 52.9°F dew point and then reheated toward a space-neutral dry-bulb temperature of
69.2°F. The partial cooling of the chilled water from 70.9°F when it exits the cooling coil to
65.7°F when leaving the CRC reduces the cooling load on the chilled water plant by 19%.

The HEDS operation comprises two stages:

• In Stage 1, the cooling coil cools and dehumidifies the inlet air. Figure 2 indicates a 53°F
 dew point setpoint. This is controlling the CHW flow via a two-way valve on the chilled
 water return (CHWR) line. In a real building application, the dew point setpoint can vary and
 is established by the building engineer depending on the latent cooling demands of the space.
 Common office space applications typically target a supply air 55°F dew point setpoint for
 conditioned spaces. Yet the optimal supply air dew point setpoint for a building depends on
 its climate, envelope performance and internal loads. For a space that requires more stringent
 humidity control such as a laboratory or hospital environment, a supply air 50°F dew point
 setpoint for the conditioned spaces may be more appropriate. More advanced control
 sequences can vary the dew point setpoint based on the time of day and the actual loads
 needing to be served. For example, raising the dew point setpoint during unoccupied times
 can save energy and reduce operating costs with the HEDS unit. The HEDS technology can
 come paired with an advanced control sequence that modulates the supply air dew point, as
 well as dry-bulb, based on the space’s temperature and dew point. While this report does not
 evaluate this control strategy, it will be analyzed in the final HEDS report for this ESTCP
 project that includes the M&V analysis at the Naval Base Guam demonstration.
• In Stage 2, the CRC sensibly reheats the air up to the dry-bulb temperature setpoint. A three-
 way valve shown in Figure 2 adjusts the CHW flow rate through the CRC versus a bypass to
 control the amount of reheat. For all the NREL tests, the bypass was closed so that the
 maximum reheat from the CRC could be measured. As shown by the test results in Figure 2,
 the CRC reheats the air’s dry-bulb temperature from 55.2°F toward a space neutral
 temperature of 69.2°F. Because the CRC only provides sensible reheat, the air’s dew point
 remains constant at 52.9°F. The supply air relative humidity is 56%, well below full
 saturation which addresses condensation in the supply ductwork and grills – comparatively

 3

 supply air relative humidity off a typical cooling coil will be 95% or higher, which increases
 the risk of condensation.
A conventional AHU chilled water coil is typically designed for an air face velocity of 550 feet
per minute. The HEDS cooling coil is typically designed for face velocities between 300 and 350
feet per minute in a mixed air system and approximately 200 feet per minute for a dedicated
outdoor air system (DOAS) or make-up air system. The final face velocities for each project are
dependent on the desired conditions and any physical space limitations imposed by the
constraints of the existing mechanical rooms.
The deeper coil geometry and lower face velocity of the HEDS cooling coil has several benefits:

• It can provide significantly more moisture removal (latent cooling). This is especially
 important when HEDS is configured as a DOAS. It is also important when the HEDS is a
 mixed air system with a large outdoor air fraction.
• The exiting CHW temperature from the HEDS cooling coil is much warmer than a
 conventional AHU. It can reach temperatures exceeding 70°F as shown in Figure 2. This
 warmer water enables the CRC to sensibly reheat the airstream. This reduces, and in certain
 cases eliminates, the reheat needed at constant air volume (CAV) or variable air volume
 (VAV) terminal boxes. The result is chilled water plan energy savings and potentially
 eliminating the need for additional reheat energy from hot-water coils or electric resistance
 coils during the cooling months.
• The cooling coil provides a closer approach temperature between the discharge air dew point
 temperature and the coil’s entering CHW temperature. Consequently, warmer CHW supply
 temperatures from the chilled water plant can be used to achieve the desired dew point
 setpoint. For example, in the Figure 2 test results, a HEDS supply dew point of 52.9°F was
 achieved with a chilled water supply (CHWS) temperature of 44°F. Comparatively, for a
 conventional AHU cooling coil, the same 44°F CHWS temperature would typically only
 achieve a 55°F or warmer dew point setpoint. The primary benefit here is enabling HVAC
 design engineers and operators to use a warmer CHWS temperature from their chilled water
 plant. This improves the chiller’s energy efficiency by reducing the compressor lift while still
 maintaining proper space temperature and humidity control. A secondary benefit for DOD
 operations teams is improved resiliency such that they can still maintain proper building
 humidity levels despite operational issues with their chilled water plants. This benefit was
 shown at the HEDS field demonstration at Fort Liberty (Chu and Duncan 2018), where a
 thermally comfortable space dew point was maintained in the dining facility’s kitchen despite
 the CHWS temperature sometimes exceeding 60°F because of pre-existing chilled water
 plant capacity and control issues.
The HEDS technology can save energy in the following ways, ordered by their energy savings
magnitude, from largest to smallest:

• Reducing reheat energy. Compared to a conventional AHU with a single cooling coil, the
 HEDS unit can achieve a lower operating SHR. SHR is calculated by dividing the sensible
 cooling rate of the unit by its total cooling rate. SHR is a common metric used by HVAC
 design engineers to quantify how much sensible cooling an HVAC design will deliver
 relative to its latent cooling. For humid climates, achieving a lower SHR operation will
 reduce or eliminate the need for additional reheat energy. The reduced need for reheat can

 4

 result in downsizing the CAV or VAV terminal reheat coils downstream of the HEDS unit.
 In some cases, depending on the building type and climate, the reheat provided by the HEDS
 CRC can eliminate the need for additional terminal unit reheat entirely.
 Yet the amount of reheat the HEDS CRC can provide is more limited than a conventional
 hot-water or electric resistance reheat coil. This limitation is driven by the entering air dry-
 bulb temperature and the entering chilled water supply temperature. The cooler the entering
 air temperature and the warmer the chilled water supply temperature, the less the HEDS can
 recover energy to reheat the air. For example, during cool-humid ambient conditions where
 the entering air dry-bulb temperature is less than 70°F, the maximum discharge dry-bulb
 temperature the HEDS can achieve will be near 60°F. Comparatively, a conventional hot-
 water or electric resistance coil will be able to reheat the discharge air to whatever that coil
 was designed for such as a space neutral temperature of 70°F. Therefore, design and
 operating engineers should model the HEDS technology in a BEM like EnergyPlus to
 analyze where its reheat capability may be limited during their climate’s cool-humid hours or
 at desired warmer chilled water supply setpoints.
• Chiller cooling load reduction. For a conventional AHU, the cooling load on the chilled
 water plant is equal to the coil’s cooling load. Yet for HEDS, the cooling load on the chilled
 water plant is calculated as the cooling coil load minus the reheat provided by the CRC.
 Consequently, the chilled water plant realizes a load reduction equal to the sensible reheat
 from the CRC.
• Operate at warmer, more comfortable space temperature setpoints. In humid climates,
 when conventional AHUs are not providing proper moisture control, a common building
 operator strategy is to turn down the thermostat temperature (dry-bulb) setpoint. Whether the
 operator understands the physics behind this strategy or not, ultimately, they are driving the
 AHU to provide more latent cooling relative to sensible cooling—hence a lower SHR
 operation. The additional latent cooling comes from a larger airflow rate of supply air and/or,
 depending on how the AHU is controlled (or overridden), lower supply air dry-bulb and dew
 point temperatures by driving more chilled water through the cooling coil.

 For example, NREL found that the two-way valve on the baseline AHU at Naval Base Guam
 was overridden to a fixed position to drive the CAV system to a lower supply air dry-bulb
 and dew point temperature below its originally intended setpoint. Because the baseline AHU
 did not have a reheat coil to separately control sensible versus latent cooling, the building
 operator had to resort to this “override” strategy to drive the space’s humidity levels down in
 response to occupant comfort complaints. Yet this action also drove the space dry-bulb
 temperature down to uncomfortable levels, in some office spaces below 69°F, for the
 building occupants who resorted to wearing more clothing. By partially decoupling the
 sensible from latent cooling, the HEDS unit can help building operators achieve a lower
 space dew point without needing to rely on lowering the thermostat setpoint or manually
 overriding the AHU control. In the case of many legacy AHU systems throughout DOD
 facilities in humid climates that do not have reheat coils, the HEDS technology provides
 building operators an extra control level - the capability to separately control space dry-bulb
 and dew points while saving energy.

 5

• Reduced chilled water pump energy. Because of its significantly larger and deeper cooling
 coil, HEDS maintains a larger temperature difference between its CHWS and CHWR
 temperatures throughout the year. A conventional AHU typically maintains a 10°F
 temperature difference across its cooling coil. In the case of the existing AHU cooling coils
 for the Guam demonstration, NREL found them operating at a 5°F temperature differential.
 Yet HEDS can operate at a temperature difference of 15°F to 25°F as evident by the test
 results in Section 4.1 below. With a larger temperature difference, the chilled water pump
 needs to flow less water for a given cooling load, resulting in pump energy savings.

• Reduction or elimination of “low delta T syndrome”. HEDS can provide improved
 operation to a new or existing chilled water plant by mitigating (or sometimes eliminating)
 what is called “low delta-T” syndrome. This condition refers to having a low temperature
 differential between the CHWS and CHWR temperatures across the AHU cooling coil(s).
 Conventional AHUs are typically designed for a 10°F temperature rise, such as from 44°F
 CHWS to 54°F CHWR. Yet the actual operation – from improper control sequences,
 undersized cooling coils or chilled water supply setpoint overrides - can result in a much
 lower temperature difference. It is common to see temperature differentials down to 5°F as in
 the case of the CHW plant that NREL has been monitoring for a year at the NEX building on
 Naval Base Guam. From talking to the facility operator, the driver here was to achieve a
 lower supply air dew point off the existing AHU cooling coil to provide a drier space. This
 resulted in excessive flow rate by the chilled water pumps, as well as nonoptimal operation of
 the chillers, which were designed for a typical 10°F temperature increase across the coils.
 Given that HEDS normally operates with a 15°F to 25°F temperature differential, it can
 mitigate or eliminate the “low delta-T” syndrome that can be a common chilled water plant
 operating issue. Yet the fraction of HEDS system size relative to all the other AHUs on the
 same chilled water loop needs to be large enough to impact the chilled water plant in an
 appreciable way. For example, at the Naval Base Guam field demonstration, the HEDS unit
 was less than 10% of the overall AHU cooling coils, so the HEDS impact on “low delta-T”
 syndrome could not be measured.

• Increased chiller plant and distribution system capacity. By reducing or eliminating the
 “low delta-T” syndrome (previous bullet), the warmer chilled water return can slightly
 increase a chiller’s cooling capacity. To quantify this, the DOD stakeholder would need to
 work with the chiller provider. Additionally, the larger temperature differential increases the
 chilled water distribution system (piping and pump) capacity. As a result, for new
 construction, the chilled water distribution system could be decreased in size. For existing
 buildings, additional chiller capacity could be added if needed with less impact on the chilled
 water distribution system.

While not tested as part of the laboratory analysis summarized in this report, the HEDS
technology can be packaged with advanced controls that adjust its multiple setpoints based on
the building’s thermal response. By continuously monitoring the space and return air dry-bulb
temperature and dew point, as well as the supply static pressure, the HEDS control can adjust the
following to maximize thermal comfort and energy savings.
 o Reset of the supply air dry-bulb temperature setpoint.
 o Reset of the supply air dew point temperature setpoint.
 o Reset of the static pressure setpoint.

 6

If the HEDS units are an appreciable fraction of the overall all chiller and boiler plant load, the
HEDS control can integrate with the larger building automation system (BAS) to adjust the
following setpoints.
 o Reset of the chilled water supply temperature setpoint.
 o Reset of the chilled water pumping system differential pressure setpoint.
 o Reset of the heating hot water supply temperature setpoint.
 o Reset of the heating hot water pumping system differential pressure setpoint.
 o Thermal flywheel, or “coasting cycle” to shut down the cooling and heating plants
 at the end of the day (or on a mild day), relying on the building’s thermal mass to
 maintain comfortable space conditions.
 o Overnight continuous pressurization and dehumidification to assist in humidity
 control for buildings typically in moderate to humid climates.
These control changes occur on a minutely basis and are smoothed, avoiding larger step-wise
impacts, to provide a consistent thermal environment. The Naval Base Guam and Timken
Museum M&V results that will be summarized in the final HEDS report will provide more detail
about the HEDS advanced control capabilities.

1.2 Field Demonstrations
In 2018, HEDS was demonstrated at two DOD bases (Chu and Duncan 2018). A CAV HEDS
unit served the kitchen at a dining facility at Fort Liberty in Fayetteville, North Carolina. Then a
VAV HEDS unit served an office block of an administration building at Tinker Air Force Base
(AFB) in Oklahoma City, Oklahoma.

Although both field demonstrations showed positive energy savings because of enhanced AHU
dehumidification and improved space moisture (dew point) control, definitively quantifying the
impacts thermal comfort and energy savings was not possible for three reasons:

• The chillers at both locations had previously existing operational issues, which prevented
 them from reaching full cooling capacity to meet their design CHWS temperature setpoints.
 The CHW plant at Tinker AFB was limited to 60% of its rated capacity because of chiller
 fouling and plugged fill on the cooling towers. As a result, the chiller at times was only able
 to supply a chilled water temperature of 62°F (16.7°C). Similarly, the Fort Liberty chiller
 was operating at 50% of its rated capacity because of compressor staging control issues.
• Prior to the HEDS installation, the existing AHUs were not measured to provide a complete
 baseline performance. Although the 2018 ESTCP report states this M&V approach was used
 primarily to quantify the energy impact of reheat, it neglects the impact of the HEDS
 technology on fan power. Consequently, by not comparing the fan power of the HEDS unit
 to the existing AHU, the overall energy impact of HEDS was not accounted for. HEDS uses
 a deeper, 10-row dehumidification coil and has a second, 6-row, reheat coil. However, air
 velocities through the HEDS coils are less than 300 feet per minute (fpm), much lower than a
 typical AHU designed for 550 fpm. Without directly monitoring the existing AHU and then
 the HEDS fan power, it is uncertain if the HEDS would have saved fan energy. In particular,
 the CAV system at Fort Liberty, with its constant fan speed, may have realized a fan energy
 increase with the HEDS unit having to overcome a potentially larger air-side pressure drop.
 The M&V plan for the ESTCP demonstration at Naval Base Guam includes the static

 7

 pressure drop across the fan and fan power measurement for both the baseline (existing)
 AHUs and HEDS units.
• The space dry-bulb and dew point temperatures during the HEDS operation were not
 extensively measured throughout the M&V period. Moreover, the space dry-bulb and dew
 point temperatures were not measured during the baseline AHU operation. As a result, the
 HEDS impact on thermal comfort and potentially improved dew point control could not be
 compared against the baseline AHUs. The Naval Base Guam M&V is measuring space dry-
 bulb and dew point conditions throughout the building area served by the baseline AHUs and
 HEDS unit.

1.3 Objective of Laboratory Testing
Beyond capturing design integration insights and impacts to O&M, field demonstrations of
emerging HVAC technologies provide critical empirical data on their performance under real-
world conditions. Yet field demonstrations have the following limitations that can be addressed
with laboratory testing:

• Scaling across DOD facilities. Field demonstrations of emerging HVAC technologies are
 expensive. For budget reasons, usually only one or two can be funded. Yet the DOD has
 more than 300,000 facilities with unique space conditioning and ventilation needs. After
 factoring in the different climate types, the test space increases exponentially. A field

 10

 demonstration provides critical information regarding energy, design integration, and O&M
 impacts. Yet it only captures a single snapshot of an emerging technology—one building in a
 single climate with a specific HVAC design (e.g., mixed air or DOAS). Extrapolating the
 results of a field demonstration requires DOD stakeholders to make assumptions about how
 an emerging HVAC technology will perform in their unique building application, climate,
 and modes of operation. For example, in the case of the HEDS field demonstrations, both
 Fort Liberty and Tinker AFB pilot projects were mixed air systems (Chu and Duncan 2018).
 Large assumptions are necessary to infer how the HEDS will perform in the Guam
 demonstration when operated as a DOAS. One uncertainty here would be whether the HEDS
 unit could achieve its dew point setpoint during Guam’s extreme outdoor dew point
 conditions, given the chilled water supply temperature from the chiller plant is fixed at 45°F.
 Laboratory testing of HVAC technologies can reduce this uncertainty by informing a
 comprehensive performance map across multiple dimensions including outdoor
 psychrometric conditions (e.g., dry-bulb, dew point), designs (e.g., mixed air or DOAS), and
 operating conditions (e.g., CHWS temperature, HEDS supply dew point setpoint). The
 laboratory data can be summarized in performance tables to be referenced directly by HVAC
 design engineers for answering key questions such as what supply air dew point can HEDS
 achieve given a CHWS temperature and Guam’s 0.4% ASHRAE dew point design condition.
 In addition, a performance map can train a data-driven model (gray-box or black-box) of the
 emerging HVAC technology, and the model can be used in a BEM environment such as
 EnergyPlus. Here the performance of HEDS (or other HVAC technologies) can be simulated
 to quantify the space thermal comfort and energy impacts across a wide range of DOD
 building types, climates, and HVAC designs. Instead of extrapolating the quantified impacts
 of a single HEDS field demonstration to a different building type and climate, modeling the
 HEDS technology within EnergyPlus provides a significantly more accurate quantification of
 its impacts.
• M&V accuracy limitations. Advances in cost-effective sensor technologies have helped
 improve the accuracy of M&V in field demonstrations. Yet laboratory grade instrumentation
 will always achieve better measurement accuracy. For example, airflow rates are extremely
 difficult to measure and are at least ±15%, and can be above ±30%, in the field.
 Comparatively, airflow rate measurements in the laboratory are ±2% using nozzle diffusers.
 With the advent of piezo-rings on fan inlet nozzles, the accuracy of real-time airflow rate
 monitoring in the field has significantly improved. Yet the accuracy of laboratory airflow rate
 measurement is still better in the laboratory environment.
 The airflow rate measurement is critical for quantifying HVAC performance to calculate
 thermal power (e.g., total cooling, sensible cooling, latent cooling). Given the limitation of
 airflow measurement accuracy in the field, the uncertainty of important HVAC performance
 metrics is heavily impacted. This is exacerbated further when the accuracy of the several
 measurements is involved in a thermal calculation such as total cooling that requires airflow
 rate, dry-bulb and relative humidity.
• Field operational issues. Compared to laboratory environments, field demonstrations have
 significantly higher risk of unforeseen operational issues that can impact the HVAC
 technology under test. For example, the HEDS pilot projects at Fort Liberty and Tinker AFB
 both experienced pre-existing chiller operational issues that resulted in the design CHW
 supply temperature not being satisfied and also impacting HEDS performance. In a

 11

 controlled testing environment such as a laboratory, the HVAC technology under test is
 quantified across a broad set of operational conditions without the risk of unforeseen
 operational issues.
The inevitable risks of operational issues and uncertainty with field demonstrations are
significantly mitigated by laboratory testing. However, testing an emerging HVAC technology
solely in the laboratory ignores critical insights like design integration, the potential inclusion of
advanced control algorithms, installation hurdles, and O&M impacts. Therefore, field
demonstrations and laboratory testing do not replace but rather complement one another.
Because of certain laboratory constraints, some HEDS unit designs are unable to be measured in
the laboratory. For example, HEDS can be designed to condition more than 100,000 cfm.
However, NREL’s laboratory has a maximum airflow rate of 5,500 cfm as summarized in
Appendix D. Therefore, we used normalization techniques to ensure a trained black-box model
from a smaller HEDS unit can accurately estimate the performance of larger HEDS units. The
model normalizes the measured SHR to the latent part load ratio (PLR) of the HEDS cooling
coil. Section 5.1 below describes in more detail the normalization approach used.

1.4 Objective of EnergyPlus Modeling
After the Fort Liberty and Tinker AFB field demonstrations, the Oak Ridge National Laboratory
developed a simulation to estimate energy savings impacts. They looked to approximate the
HEDS performance with a conventional AHU model already built into the EnergyPlus
environment (Bhandari and Buckberry 2018). The simulation study was unable to accurately
model the HEDS two-coil technology because they said the field data were insufficient to
validate the model.

Recognizing the lack of sufficient field data and their approximated model, the Oak Ridge
National Laboratory study concluded that “a detailed calibrated study or side-by-side comparison
of HEDS with a conventional AHU system would provide more accurate energy savings
potential” (Bhandari and Buckberry 2018). This conclusion led ESTCP to work with NREL to
develop a laboratory test plan in parallel with the Naval Base Guam field demonstration.

As discussed in Section 1.3, the main outcome from the laboratory testing is training a black-box
model of the HEDS unit based on the performance map. The model can estimate the HEDS
performance within a BEM environment such as EnergyPlus (Woods et al. 2023). The
performance map is comprehensive, capturing the relevant outdoor air conditions (dry-bulb, dew
point), HVAC designs (mixed air, DOAS) and operational setpoints (CHWS temperature, supply
air dry-bulb, supply air dew point). Using this black-box model, a whole building simulation
program, like EnergyPlus, can capture the complex air-side and water-side interactions between
a HEDS unit and the chilled water plant.

 12

Having an explicit HEDS model in the EnergyPlus environment will allow DOD stakeholders to
evaluate the HEDS technology via whole building simulation and apply the findings to other
unique facilities in different climates. These simulations can provide accurate energy
performance estimates to a proposed HVAC design, such as whether additional reheat is needed.
In addition, if it is, what type of reheat would be best and where—at the HEDS location or
downstream in terminal boxes? An accurate HEDS model can also provide insights toward
satisfying more complex operational sequences such as those in ASHRAE Guideline 36
(ASHRAE 2021).

2.3 Measurement and Instrumentation Plan
The sensors used for air-side, water-side, and electrical measurements are listed in Table 3. This
section briefly describes how the measurements were used to calculate the thermal performance
of the various HEDS components.

The air-side thermal power (sensible, latent, and total) of the cooling coil and CRC were
calculated using the measured air mass flow rate and psychrometric measurements before and
after each coil. ASME 1 flow nozzles were used in the inlet and outlet laboratory airstreams to
measure the air mass flow rates through the HEDS unit. Comparison of the inlet and outlet air
mass flow rate within 2% ensures leakage in the experimental setup is minimized. NREL’s
psychrometric air monitoring stations at the HEDS inlet and exit (supply) used in-duct static,
flow mixers to adequately mix the air temperature and humidity prior to the measurement
locations. A small sample of air is drawn from dozens of points across the duct cross-section to
ensure a representative, average, bulk air measurement is made. Given the close spacing between
the cooling coil and CRC, the dry-bulb and dew point were not measured in between the coils
because of nonuniform conditions immediately downstream of the cooling coil. Instead, the air
dry-bulb temperature at the cooling coil exit (and the CRC inlet) were assumed to be at saturated
conditions and therefore equal to the HEDS exiting supply air dew point temperature.

The sensible reheat from the CRC was determined from water-side measurements of flow rate,
inlet temperature, and outlet temperature. The water-side thermal power of the cooling coil and
CRC was calculated using the measurements from the electromagnetic water flow and immersed
water temperature probes.

Electrical power to the HEDS fan was measured using Acuvim true-RMS 2 power meters. Three
split-core current transducers monitored the current on each power phase to the HEDS unit. The
UV lights on HEDS were not installed and operated, therefore the power measurement solely
represents energy to the VFD controlling the supply fan motor.

The air-side total thermal power was compared to the water-side thermal power to calculate an
energy balance, which is provided for each of the 59 steady-state tests. These can be seen in
Appendix B through Appendix C. The energy balance accounts for the measured differences in
calculating the thermal power using the air side versus the water side of the system. All 59
steady-state test results had an energy balance within ±5% except for 5 of the tests.

3 Experimental Plan
The following subsections summarize the HEDS control sequence used for the 59 steady-state
tests, as well as the design of experiments for the air-side and water-side testing.

3.1 Control Sequence
The HEDS unit and NREL’s laboratory were controlled using the four setpoints shown in Table
4. The following list provides more detail on each setpoint:

• Airflow rate. The VFD serving the HEDS supply fan was varied to achieve each test’s
 airflow rate. Because NREL is located at a 5,850-ft (1,783-m) elevation with a nominal
 atmospheric pressure of 11.9 psi (82.0 kPa), the HEDS airflow rate was controlled to
 standard volumetric flow rate conditions. By doing so, the airflow rates represent the
 operation of the HEDS unit as if it were situated at sea level. Using NREL’s TTF laboratory
 supply fan, conditioned air was provided to the HEDS inlet while maintaining 0 inches of
 water column static pressure relative to the ambient air. NREL’s laboratory exhaust fans
 downstream of the HEDS were operated to maintain a 0 inch water column differential static
 pressure across the HEDS inlet and outlet plenums. Controlling both laboratory fans this way
 ensured the HEDS supply fan motor consumed the precise amount of electric power required
 to only overcome its own ISP. In other words, the HEDS supply fan operated against zero
 external static pressure for every test.
 The HEDS laboratory testing took place in December 2023 at NREL located in Golden,
 Colorado. Because of the extremely dry air conditions experienced in Colorado during the
 winter, the maximum dew point the laboratory was able to provide to a test article was
 limited to slightly more than 80°F (26.7°C). Also, the HEDS unit was sized to condition
 4,000 SCFM when operated as a mixed air system, but the laboratory was unable to meet the
 highest inlet air dew points at these flow rates. Consequently, the experimental plan was
 modified such that 3,200 SCFM was the highest mass flow rate tested, as shown in Table 4.
• Chilled water supply temperature. The experimental plan characterized the HEDS unit at
 three CHWS temperatures. The first setpoint at 44°F (6.7°C) represented typical CHWS
 temperatures provided from a conventional chilled water plant. The next setpoint at 40°F
 (4.4°C) captured colder CHWS temperatures for facilities requiring lower space dew points.
 The last CHWS temperature at 50°F (10.0°C) represented CHWS reset strategies such as
 those in ASHRAE Guideline 36 (ASHRAE 2021). This warmer CHWS 50°F setpoint also
 may represent the HEDS performance during times when the chilled water plant may not be
 operating per the original design as was seen during the Fort Liberty and Tinker AFB field
 demonstrations (Chu and Duncan 2018).
• Supply air dew point temperature. To meet a supply air dew point setpoint, the HEDS
 controller varied the CHW flow into the cooling coil using a two-way, pressure independent
 control valve. If the HEDS unit measures the supply air dew point higher than the dew point
 setpoint, the valve opens further to allow more CHW into the coil, which provides more
 dehumidification. Three supply air dew point setpoints were tested. The first was a 55°F
 (12.8°C) setpoint, which represents typical design practice for both CAV and VAV
 applications. Two lower setpoints of 50°F (10°C) and 53°F (11.7°C) capture more aggressive
 dehumidification operation for facilities that may have more stringent space requirements

 21

 such as laboratories, hospitals, and museums. This setpoint may also apply to older DOD
 facilities in humid climates that struggle to meet indoor space dew point setpoints to mitigate
 mold growth.
• Supply air dry-bulb temperature. To meet the supply air dry-bulb temperature setpoint, the
 HEDS controller modulates a three-way bypass valve that controls the amount of CHW flow
 into the CRC. For all 59 steady-state laboratory tests, the HEDS controller was commanded
 to provide an excessively warm supply air dry-bulb temperature. Doing so forced the
 controller to close the three-way bypass valve so all the CHW entered the CRC. Because no
 water bypassed the CRC, the HEDS unit was operated to provide the maximum amount of
 sensible reheat from the CRC for each test. This can be seen in the results summary tables of
 Appendix B through Appendix C. Depending on the entering water temperature to the CRC,
 the exiting reheated supply air temperature varies for each of the tests. In a real building
 application, there are times when the HEDS unit is unable to reheat the air to the desired dry-
 bulb temperature setpoint. This typically occurs when the entering dry-bulb air temperature is
 not hot enough. An example would be a cool-humid day of 75°F dry-bulb temperature and
 70°F dew point temperature. Under these conditions, additional reheat likely would be
 needed to achieve the desired dry-bulb temperature setpoint.

3.3 Converting Air-Side Results from NREL Elevation to Sea Level
NREL’s TTF laboratory is in Golden, Colorado, at 5,865 ft (1,783 m) above sea level. Testing at
altitude presents a challenge because equipment must operate in a lower air density environment.
Because the laboratory’s atmospheric air pressure is 11.9 psi (82.0 kPa), the air side inlet test
conditions needed to be converted after the tests were performed to determine the sea level
equivalent psychrometric conditions. The following steps explain how we made that adjustment:

• The HEDS entering air, dew-point-depression condition, represented on a psychrometric
 chart in Figure 12, is kept constant between NREL and sea level elevations. The dew point-
 depression is the difference between the dew point and dry-bulb temperature. This ensured
 the same sensible cooling of the air was performed by the HEDS cooling coil prior to
 reaching a saturated air state. In the following example, the dew point depression equals the
 dry-bulb (89.0°F) minus the dew point (78.0°F) yielding 11.0°F.
• Because the HEDS unit controls the water flow to the cooling coil to satisfy a leaving supply
 air dew point setpoint, that supply air dew point value is kept identical for both NREL and
 sea level elevations. In this example, the cooling coil dehumidified the air to provide a supply
 air dew point temperature of 54.9°F.
• The latent enthalpy quantity, represented by the Δh_cooling coil_saturation_curve, is kept
 constant between NREL and sea level elevations. This ensures both elevations experience the
 same amount of latent cooling as the HEDS cooling coil dehumidifies the saturated air. The

 27

 Δh_cooling coil_saturation_curve is determined from the experimental test results at NREL’s
 elevation. As shown in Figure 12, the Δh_cooling coil_saturation_curve equals 21.6
 Btu/lbm 4 from the saturated entering air condition to the exiting supply air dew point
 temperature.
• By constraining the exiting supply air dew point condition and the latent cooling load
 provided by Δh_cooling coil_saturation_curve, an equivalent entering saturated air dew point
 temperature at sea level can be iteratively calculated.
• Finally, by knowing the entering air dew point and dew point depression, the entering air
 dry-bulb is calculated at sea level. The dew point depression will be the same at altitude and
 sea level, but the dry-bulb temperature will differ.

highlights the three metrics that are kept constant between the NREL and sea level elevations. As
summarized in the previous steps, these three metrics include the HEDS supply air dew point,
entering air dew point-depression, and the Δh_cooling coil_saturation_curve.

4 Test Results
Based on the experimental plans from Section 3, the following subsections summarize the air-
side, water-side, and electric power results from the 59 steady-state tests.

4.1 Air-Side Test Results
For each steady-state test, numerous thermodynamic performance metrics were calculated. Both
air-side and water-side measurements resulted in an energy balance agreement within ±5% for
all but five of the tests. Because of the better accuracy of the water-side instrumentation, the
water-side performance metrics are reported when the same metrics are available from both the
air-side and water-side calculations. For example, the total cooling load of the cooling coil was
determined based on measurements of the chilled water inlet/outlet temperatures and flow rates.
Yet, to calculate the sensible air cooling load, this performance metric must be calculated using
air-side measurements. The performance metrics and values from which the calculations are
derived include the following:

• Total cooling: cooling coil. Calculated from the CHWS temperature, CHWR temperature of
 the cooling coil, and the water-side flow rate.
• Total cooling: HEDS. Calculated from the CHWS temperature into the cooling coil, CHWR
 temperature exiting the CRC, and the water-side flow rate. This metric accounts for the
 CRC’s sensible reheat of the air before exiting the HEDS unit.
• Sensible cooling: cooling coil. Calculated by subtracting the latent cooling value of the
 HEDS from the total cooling of the cooling coil.
• Sensible reheat: CRC. Calculated from the CHWS temperature, CHWR temperature of the
 CRC, and the water-side flow rate. This value was determined using the more accurate water-
 side measurements because the CRC provides sensible heating only to the airstream.
• Sensible cooling: HEDS. Calculated from the air-side inlet dry-bulb temperature and supply
 dry-bulb temperature across the HEDS unit and the airflow rate.
• CHW cooling Load % Reduction. Calculated by dividing the sensible reheat from the CRC
 by the total cooling load of the cooling coil. This equals the reduced CHW load on a chilled
 water plant.
• Latent cooling: HEDS. Calculated from the difference between the air-side inlet and outlet
 dew point temperatures and the airflow rate. This is equal to the latent cooling of the cooling
 coil.
• SHR: cooling coil. The SHR of the cooling coil is calculated by dividing the sensible cooling
 load of the cooling coil by the total cooling load of the cooling coil.
• SHR: HEDS. SHR of the entire HEDS unit is calculated by dividing the sensible cooling of
 the HEDS unit by the total cooling of the HEDS. This metric accounts for the sensible reheat
 provided by the CRC.
Summary tables of the measurements and calculated performance metrics can be found in
Appendix B and Appendix C. The test results at NREL and Sea Level elevation in both imperial
and metric units can also be found in the shared spreadsheet here.

Figure 13 maps two test results as measured at NREL’s altitude, one from the “Cool Humid” test
group and another from the “DOAS 1” test group. In both cases, the sensible reheat from the
CRC reduced the SHR of the total HEDS performance by approximately 0.10. These two tests

 30

show a significant difference in the maximum available reheat from the CRC. Because the
“DOAS 1” test group has a hotter inlet air dry-bulb temperature of 89.0°F (31.7°C), the CHWR

CRC can only warm the supply air dry-bulb temperature to 62.8°F (17.1°C). Depending on the

top of what the CRC can provide may be necessary to bring the supply air dry-bulb temperate
closer to space neutral conditions.

the CC to still achieve a 53.1°F dew point despite a warm 49.9°F CHWS. This example speaks to
the resilience capability of the larger, deeper CC to achieve sufficiently low supply dew points

4.2 Water-Side Test Results
Compared to conventional AHUs that typically operate with a 10°F CHW temperature
differential across the cooling coil, the HEDS unit achieves significantly larger temperature
differentials for most of the tests. Figure 16 plots the CHW temperature rise across the HEDS
unit versus the latent PLR of the cooling coil. Using the laboratory data, the delta-T here is
calculated as the difference between the CC inlet and CRC outlet temperatures – meaning this
delta-T includes the CRC operating to provide its maximum amount of reheat.

As discussed in Section 1.1, maintaining a larger CHW differential temperature across the HEDS
unit provides two benefits. First, the CHW pump can throttle to a lower flow rate while
providing the same cooling load. Second, it addresses a common chilled water plant operational
issue called “low delta-T syndrome,” where the CHW temperature differential across an AHU
coil can operate as low as 5°F—resulting in suboptimal chiller performance relative to how it
was designed to operate. For example, the two existing AHUs at the Naval Exchange field
demonstration in Guam had their two-way valves overridden to a fixed position that operated the
cooling coils at a 5°F delta-T.

Figure 17 shows the CHW delta-T across the CC which approached 35°F for some of the larger
latent PLR tests. Compared to the conventional 10°F, the increased delta-T directly correlates to
the design and operational CHW flowrates. For new construction, the over 3x delta-T of the
HEDS CC can result in a significant downsizing of the CHW distribution pump and piping.

5 Performance Characterization
NREL used the 59 steady-state test results to train a model of the HEDS unit to capture its
performance as both a DOAS and mixed air system. The model also captures the HEDS unit
operated with a CAV and VAV fan control strategy. This model can be used by a building
energy modeling (BEM) program like EnergyPlus to quantify the impact of the HEDS
technology on a specific building design and climate. The following subsections summarize the
HEDS model training and NREL’s next steps in model validation using the HEDS field
demonstrations at Naval Base Guam and the Timken Museum of Art. See Table 1 above for a
summary of the HEDS design specifications comparing the HEDS unit tested at NREL and the
field demonstrations in Guam and the Timken Museum.

5.1 Model Training
The 59 steady-state test results were used to train a model of the HEDS unit capturing its
performance as a DOAS and mixed air system, as well as with CAV and VAV supply fan
control. After evaluating several model structures, NREL found the best fit by correlating the
HEDS SHR versus two independent variables, latent PLR and coil face velocity. These variables
are calculated as follows:

• SHR. The model’s dependent variable is calculated by dividing the sensible cooling of the
 HEDS unit by its total cooling (sensible plus latent). The impacts of both water coils are
 included in this calculation. In other words, the sensible reheat of the CRC is factored in.
• Latent PLR. The first independent variable is calculated by dividing the operating latent
 load required to meet a desired supply air dew point by the design latent cooling load of the
 cooling coil (not including the reheat impact of the CRC). The cooling coil’s design latent
 cooling is calculated from the design airflow rate, the entering air at the 0.4% ASHRAE
 dehumidification design condition, and the desired HEDS discharge air dew point setpoint. In
 the case of the HEDS unit tested at NREL, the design latent cooling load was 159.3 MBTUH
 (46.7 kW). This design latent cooling load can be found on the mechanical schedule.
• Coil face velocity. The second independent variable is the airflow face velocity across the
 cooling coil in meters per second at sea level conditions using standard volumetric flow
 conditions.
Figure 21 provides a visual of the correlation between SHR and latent PLR for the test results
segmented by the airflow rate (scfm). Figure 22 provides the same visual as Figure 21 yet
segments the airflow by its coil face air velocity in meters per second. Looking at the data trends
in these Figures, for a constant latent PLR, the SHR of the HEDS unit decreases with lower
airflows (coil face velocities). This captures the cooling coil’s ability to provide better
dehumidification at slower air velocities across the coil. Another trend captured by the HEDS
model is the lower SHR operation when the cooling coil operates at a larger latent PLR.

This model of the HEDS technology can be used for any size HEDS unit regardless of its design
cooling load and airflow rate because both independent variables are normalized. More
specifically, the latent PLR is normalized to the design latent cooling load of the HEDS unit. The
airflow rate is normalized by dividing it by the coil face area to obtain the coil face velocity.

5.2 Model Validation
The next step for the HEDS project will be to use the M&V data from field demonstrations to
validate the HEDS model trained from the 59 steady-state laboratory test results. There are two
HEDS field demonstrations that will be used:

• Naval Base Guam. Funded by the ESTCP, a 13,000 cfm HEDS unit will serve a retail space
 within the Naval Exchange serving the clothing sales floor and back-of-house office spaces.
 The unit will operate as a mixed air, VAV system during occupied times. During the
 unoccupied nighttime, the unit will switch over to DOAS, CAV operation to target
 pressurizing parts of the Naval Exchange building when the other AHUs serving adjacent
 spaces are off. Compared to conventional AHUs, the significantly larger cooling coil on the
 HEDS unit makes it capable, when designed as such, to operate as both a mixed air and
 DOAS system in the same installation.
• Timken Museum of Art. Funded by the U.S. Department of Energy High Impact
 Technology (DoE-HIT) program, this field demonstration is in San Diego, California. The
 HEDS unit is sized for 12,500 cfm as a mixed air system. During the M&V period, the
 HEDS unit alternates weekly between VAV and CAV operation. Since there was no existing
 AHU for this site to baseline, the HEDS unit itself is used as a baseline unit by turning off its
 energy recovery capability (100% bypassing the CRC) for certain weeks of the M&V period.

 40

For a more detailed description of these field demonstrations, including a summary of the design
of the HEDS units, see Section 1.2 and Table 1 above. The M&V efforts at both Naval Base
Guam and Timken sites will be provided by NREL.
The M&V data from each field demonstration will be processed to capture the correlation
between the HEDS unit SHR relative to its latent PLR and coil face velocity (and possibly
CHWS temperature). These correlations will be compared to the HEDS model trained from the
NREL laboratory test data. Standard statistical metrics according to ASHRAE Guideline 14
(ASHRAE 2023) such as the root-mean squared error and coefficient of variation of the root-
mean squared error will be used to quantify the accuracy of the HEDS model relative to the field
data.
If these statistical metrics show the trained HEDS model does not accurately reflect the HEDS
performance in both field demonstrations, NREL will reevaluate the HEDS model approach.
NREL’s goal will be to establish a single, generalized HEDS model that accurately reflects the
performance of the HEDS unit for use in BEM software. This model will capture the HEDS
technology operated as a DOAS and mixed air system, as well as with CAV or VAV supply fan
control.

5.3 Modeling in EnergyPlus
The primary outcome of the HEDS laboratory testing was to collect sufficient data to inform a
performance map of the system. This test data has been used to develop a HEDS model that will
later be validated using M&V data from two field demonstrations. The newly developed model,
specific to the HEDS technology, will be used in whole building simulation within the
EnergyPlus BEM environment. The model has been generalized and normalized to capture the
HEDS operation as a DOAS and mixed air system, as well as operated with CAV or VAV fan
control. The model can be used for any size HEDS unit since its independent variables are
normalized as summarized in Section 5.1 above.

It is currently unclear if the accuracy will be compromised by using a single, generalized model
of the HEDS technology to capture its various design architectures (DOAS or mixed air, CAV or
VAV) and system size. NREL’s next step in the project is to quantify the model accuracy using
statistic metrics compared against the M&V data from two HEDS field demonstrations. The
reason to develop a model for use in a BEM program like EnergyPlus is that it enables DoD
stakeholders (design engineers, building operations teams) to simulate the HEDS technology in
any building type or climate. Rather than simple extrapolation of percent energy savings from a
couple field demonstrations, EnergyPlus modeling provides significantly more accurate
quantification of how the HEDS technology would impact a building’s annual energy usage and
thermal comfort.

More specifically, the HEDS model will give design engineers and other DOD stakeholders the
ability to model the HEDS technology for their building’s unique design, unique operation and
climatic impacts. In other words, the BEM simulation output—along with the critical insights
(design integration, installation, and O&M impacts) provided by the field demonstrations—
reduces the number of assumptions stakeholders need to make regarding how a HEDS unit will
perform for their distinct project, empowering them to make better data-driven decisions.

 41

A BEM environment like EnergyPlus will capture the four ways the HEDS technology can save
energy:
• Reducing reheat energy,
• Reducing the chiller cooling load,
• Operating at a warmer space temperature setpoint and
• Reducing the chilled water pump energy.
Also, depending on the chiller type (e.g., scroll, centrifugal) and chilled water plant design, the
larger chilled water differential operation of the HEDS unit may save additional energy by
eliminating the “low delta-T syndrome,” which can be common in DOD facilities.

See Section 1.1 for a detailed description of how the HEDS technology provides each of these
energy savings. The magnitude of each of these energy saving pathways will change based on
the climate, the building design, and HVAC system design. For example, an older office building
in a mildly humid climate, such as the mid-Atlantic region, with large internal sensible loads
from computer equipment and older fluorescent lights may not need much reheat to meet its dry-
bulb and dew point setpoints. The HEDS model in EnergyPlus would show some marginal
chiller cooling load and chilled water pump energy savings while the other two savings pathways
(reheat energy and operating at a warmer space temperature setpoint) might be minimal.

Another example that would showcase significant HEDS energy savings would be a DOD
barracks with a high occupancy density in a hot-humid climate, which requires significantly
more annual latent versus sensible cooling. In this example, the HEDS unit operates as a mixed
air system with VAV terminal units downstream. Because of more ventilation requirements in a
high occupancy, dormitory style space, many of the VAV units should not turn down past 30%
to abide by ASHRAE Standard 62.1 (ASHRAE 2022). There would also be high latent cooling
loads from the occupants as well as the shower loads. Simulating this HVAC architecture within
a BEM environment like EnergyPlus would quantify all four energy savings pathways,
especially the reheat energy savings that is exacerbated by the limited turndown of the VAV
boxes.

Although these two examples would showcase very different energy savings of the HEDS
technology, they both demonstrate the importance of being able to explicitly model the HEDS
technology withing a BEM environment like EnergyPlus. HVAC design engineers and other
DOD stakeholders would have explicit data showing the thermal comfort and energy impacts of
the HEDS technology based on the unique characteristics of their building and HVAC design,
operation and climate.

There is a final benefit of using EnergyPlus modeling. The HEDS technology can come
packaged with advanced controls that continuously adjust the control setpoints of the HEDS unit
itself, as well as setpoints on the chilled water and hot water plants. The setpoint resets on the
HEDS unit include (1) supply air dry-bulb setpoint, (2) supply air dew point setpoint and (3) air
static pressure setpoint. If the HEDS unit (or units) represent most or all of the cooling load of a
chilled water plant, then the potential resets here are (1) CHWS temperature setpoint and (2)
CHW pumping system differential pressure setpoint. The hot water plant resets that the HEDS
can adjust are the (1) heating hot water supply temperature setpoint and (2) heating hot water
pumping system differential pressure setpoint. Lastly, the HEDS advanced control has some

 42

capability to leverage the thermal mass (flywheel) of the building to avoid cooling or reheat
needed later in the day. Given that EnergyPlus explicitly models the buildings thermal mass and
all these setpoints across the HVAC system, it can emulate the HEDS reset strategies to capture
their impact on energy savings and thermal comfort. In summary, the HEDS model simulated in
EnergyPlus captures all of its impacts, including the hardware benefits (like larger CHW coils
and heat reclaim from its CRC), as well as its advanced control strategies.

the airflow rate designate the run number NREL used and can be ignored. For example, the “c”
after the airflow rate indicates NREL used the 3rd run for a test condition. NREL sometimes

Some of the metrics in these Tables refer to notes 1 through 5 which are defined just below.

• Note 1. The HEDS designation here captures the unit’s net total cooling and sensible cooling
 by subtracting the CRC reheat from the cooling provided by the CC.
• Note 2. Sensible Reheat - CRC is the reheat provided to the airstream from the CRC. This is
 the same load that cools the CHW back down after it leaves the cooling coil. For all 59
 steady-state tests, all the CHW that flowed through the cooling coil also flowed through the

 measure the maximum sensible reheat possible. In a real building application, the CHW flow

 on a chilled water plant.
• Note 4. Part Load Ratio (PLR) is calculated by dividing the Total Cooling - CC by the

Appendix C. Test Results at Sea Level
The following tables are the test results at Sea Level in imperial (IP) units. These test results, as
well as the test results at NREL elevation and metric (SI) units, can be downloaded from the
following spreadsheet. Each table captures a test group (e.g., AHRI_920 A, Cool Humid).

The rationale for each entering air condition along with the HEDS operational setpoints are
summarized in Sections 3.2 and 3.3. For clarification in the nomenclature used here, the “cooling
coil” results draw a control volume around the cooling coil (CC). For example, the “Total
Cooling - CC” captures the total cooling (sensible and latent) provided by the cooling coil. The
“CRC” focuses on the sensible reheat from the cooling recovery coil (CRC). The metrics with
“HEDS” capture the combined impact of the CC and CRC, drawing a control volume around the
entire unit and focusing on the difference between the entering and discharge air conditions.

Each test has a unique label based on its entering air (EA) conditions, chilled water (CHW)
supply temperature, and HEDS operational setpoints. The decoding of the unique label structure
using the test “HEDS_40_55_95_71p8_1600a” translates to a 40°F chilled water supply
temperature, 55°F HEDS supply dew point setpoint, 95°F entering air dry-bulb temperature,
71.8°F entering air dew point temperature, and 1,600 cfm supply airflow rate. The letters after
the airflow rate designate the run number NREL used and can be ignored. For example, the “c”
after the airflow rate indicates NREL used the 3rd run for a test condition. NREL sometimes
conducted multiple runs to achieve a target energy balance and mass balance.

These air-side setpoints are based on NREL elevation. Using the NREL elevation to sea level
conversion methodology from Section 3.3, the entering air dry-bulb and dew point temperature
are transformed to sea level.

Some of the metrics in these Tables refer to notes 1 through 5 which are defined just below.

• Note 1. The HEDS designation here captures the unit’s net total cooling and sensible cooling
 by subtracting the CRC reheat from the cooling provided by the CC.
• Note 2. Sensible Reheat - CRC is the reheat provided to the airstream from the CRC. This is
 the same load that cools the CHW back down after it leaves the cooling coil. For all 59
 steady-state tests, all the CHW that flowed through the cooling coil also flowed through the
 CRC. In other words, none of the CHW bypassed the CRC. NREL’s rationale here was to
 measure the maximum sensible reheat possible. In a real building application, the CHW flow
 rate to the CRC would be modulated to achieve a HEDS supply air dry-bulb setpoint.
• Note 3. CHW Cooling Load % Reduction is calculated by dividing the Sensible Reheat -
 CRC by the Total Cooling - CC. This metric captures the CRC’s reduction to the CHW load
 on a chilled water plant.
• Note 4. Part Load Ratio (PLR) is calculated by dividing the Total Cooling - CC by the
 cooling coil’s rated total cooling capacity of 240.7 MBH (70.5 kW).
• Note 5. Part load ratio (PLR) is calculated by dividing the Latent Cooling - HEDS by the
 cooling coil’s rated latent cooling capacity of 159.3 MBH (46.7 kW).

Appendix D. NREL Thermal Technology Facility
Testing Capabilities and Measurement Accuracies
The following specifications summarize the laboratory testing capabilities and accuracies of the
NREL Thermal Technology Facility.

Airstreams

•   Dry-bulb range: 20°F up to 300°F (-7°C up to 149°C)
           o Accuracy: ±0.9°F (±0.4°C) absolute, ±0.2°F (0.1°C) delta
 o Slew rate: >20°F/min (>11°C/min) typical
           o Setpoint control (dynamic/static): ±1.0°F/0.5°F (±0.5°C/0.3°C)
• Dew point range: Subfreezing up to 85°F (29.4°C)
           o Accuracy: ±0.36°F (0.2°C) absolute, 0.2°F (0.1°C) delta
 o Slew rate: >9°F/min (5°C/min) typical
           o Setpoint control (dynamic/static): ±1.0°F/0.5°F (±0.5°C/0.3°C)
• Airflow: Four airstreams with 10 to 5,500 standard cubic feet per minute (2.60 standard cubic
 meters per second) for each stream
 o Accuracy: ±1% up to 500 standard cubic feet per minute, ±2% up to 5,500
 standard cubic meters per second
 o Slew rate: Up to 10,000 standard cubic feet per minute
 o Setpoint control (dynamic/static): ±5.0%/0.5%

Supply glycol streams

 • Hot water from a boiler: 1.5 MMBTU (440 kW) up to 220°F (104°C)
 • Chilled water from a chiller: 20 rated tons (70 kW) at 44°F chilled water supply (6.7°C).
 The entire chilled water supply range is from 15°F up to 86°F (-10°C up to 30°C). For the
 HEDS testing, the chiller was able to provide up to 16 tons at 40°F (4.4°C).

Natural gas supply (and fume exhaust)

 • 200 MBTUH (117 kW) higher heating value
