Gradient Flow Polarizes Softmax Outputs towards Low-Entropy Solutions
Aditya Varre, Mark Rofin, Nicolas Flammarion

Abstract
Understanding the intricate non-convex training dynamics of softmax-based models is crucial for explaining the empirical success of transformers. In this article, we analyze the gradient flow dynamics of the value-softmax model, defined as ℒ ⁡ ( 𝐕 ​ σ ​ ( 𝐚 ) ) \mathcal{L}(\mathbf{V}\sigma(\mathbf{a})) , where 𝐕 \mathbf{V} and 𝐚 \mathbf{a} are a learnable value matrix and attention vector, respectively. As the matrix times softmax vector parameterization constitutes the core building block of self-attention, our analysis provides direct insight into transformer’s training dynamics. We reveal that gradient flow on this structure inherently drives the optimization toward solutions characterized by low-entropy outputs. We demonstrate the universality of this polarizing effect across various objectives, including logistic and square loss. Furthermore, we discuss the practical implications of these theoretical results, offering a formal mechanism for empirical phenomena such as attention sinks and massive activations .

Introduction

Despite the widespread adoption of large language models (LLMs), our understanding of the internal mechanisms by which they process information remains limited. In particular, the self-attention mechanism and the representations it induces play a central role in model behavior, yet many aspects of their functioning remain in the early stages of understanding. Recent work has begun to shed light on this opacity by identifying recurring learned structures within transformer architectures, such as induction heads
(
18
)
and attention sinks
(
32
)
. These components have attracted significant attention as crucial building blocks underlying complex model capabilities.

Building on these efforts, identified components are often examined in isolation to assess how architectural design choices, training algorithms and hyperparameters, as well as properties of the training data, contribute to their emergence. A theoretical understanding of these factors is a necessary step toward improving the robustness, efficiency, and safety of large language models.
In this work, we pursue this objective by studying a broader phenomenon that underlies many of these components:
low-entropy (sparse) attention patterns
, where the attention distribution concentrates most of its mass on a small number of tokens, and by asking whether this sparsification is solely enforced by the task.

As a motivating example, we consider
attention sinks

(
32
)
: sparse attention patterns characterized by attention mass that is highly concentrated on one token, often the first token in the sequence. A common hypothesis is that such low-entropy patterns are useful because concentrating mass on a small set of tokens induces a systematic bias in the attention output; however, the need for such bias is not always clear from task semantics or sequence structure. Moreover, some softmax-free attention types do not form attention sinks (Figure
1
), pointing to the role of architecture in modulating their emergence.
This motivates us to ask the following question:

Why does attention become sparse in the first place, and does low-entropy attention reflect a functional requirement of the task or an implicit preference induced by optimization and parameterization?

We investigate this question by isolating the minimal computation underlying a single attention head. The output of a head can be written as a value matrix multiplied by a softmax attention distribution, i.e.,
\mathbf{V}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{a})
. In this form, an attention sink corresponds to
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{a})
becoming low-entropy (near one-hot), concentrating mass on a single token. We therefore study a simplified
value–softmax
model
\mathbf{V}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{a})
, where both
\mathbf{V}
and
\bm{a}
are trainable, and analyze whether gradient-based training exhibits an
implicit bias toward sparsification
even when many dense solutions exist. Concretely, we consider several losses with a fixed target vector, analyze gradient-flow dynamics, and complement the theory with empirical evidence.

Our contributions are:

•

We study the value-softmax model with logistic loss and show that among many possible solutions, the gradient flow is biased towards the sparse solution and the output of the softmax, i.e.,
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{a})
converges to a
one-hot
vector. This sparsification is attributed to the polarizing dynamics of the gradient flow.

•

We extend these polarizing dynamics to the case of regression and attribute the sparsification to the speed of convergence. We further discuss how alternative nonlinearities and normalization schemes modulate (or prevent) this polarization.

•

We connect these results to transformer attention, arguing that attention sinks and massive activations can emerge as a consequence of the implicit bias induced by the softmax parameterization, helping explain sink formation.

•

We provide empirical evidence on how normalization, both its presence and absence can impact the proportion of attention sinks specifically in the formation of induction heads. We also discuss the pitfalls of such sparsification bias, where a dispropotionate burden of decision making is attributed to a single token.

Polarization and population dynamics.

From a technical perspective, the dynamical system analysis of our value-softmax model shares significant structural similarities with replicator dynamics in evolutionary game theory

Problem Setup

Notations.

For
n\in\mathbb{N}
, let
[n]:=\{0,1,\ldots,n{-}1\}
. Bold letters denote quantities of interest which includes time-dependent variables and their limiting values. For a vector
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
x_{i}
denotes its
i^{th}
coordinate. For a matrix
X\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}
,
X_{i}
denotes its
i^{th}
column. The notation
\|\cdot\|
denotes the Euclidean norm for vectors and the Frobenius norm for matrices. For a vector
a\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
\mathrm{diag}(a)
denotes the diagonal matrix with entries given by
a
. The all-ones vector is denoted by
\mathbf{1}
and its dimension is inferred from the context. For vectors
u,v\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
u\odot v
denotes the Hadamard (elementwise) product.

The softmax function
\mathrm{\bm{\sigma}}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
is defined by

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}(a)_{i}=\frac{\exp(a_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{p}\exp(a_{j})}.

We first present a single self-attention block and then simplify it to a model we call the value–softmax model.

Self attention.

Let
X\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}
be a sequence of input token embeddings and let
Q,K,V
denote the query, key, and value matrices of the attention block. The updated representation after the attention block is given by

\displaystyle X_{+}=\bm{\sigma}\left(XQK^{\top}X^{\top}\right)XV^{\top}.

Let
z
denote the embedding of the last input token. Its updated embedding is

\displaystyle z_{+}=VX^{\top}\bm{\sigma}\left(XKQ^{\top}z\right)=\bar{V}^{\top}\bm{\sigma}\left(\bar{a}\right),

where we define
\bar{V}=XV^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times d}
and
\bar{a}=XKQ^{\top}z\in\mathbb{R}^{T}
.
Such an update has the following structure: a trainable matrix
\bar{V}
multiplied by softmax of a vector
\bar{a}
. The embedding

\displaystyle z_{+}=\sum_{i}v_{i}\sigma(a)_{i}

can be represented in multiple ways. For example, the scores
\sigma_{i}^{\prime}s
may be uniform, in which case the vectors
v_{i}^{\prime}
average to
z_{+}
; alternatively, the scores may be sparse(e.g., one-hot) in which case a single vector
v_{i}
is selected to be
z_{+}
.

We would like to understand which of these regimes the optimization procedure tends to favor. Note that in the above derivation we ignore the contribution of the value matrix
V
to the updates of the other tokens and assume independence of the
z_{+}
.
While these assumptions do not hold in general, they motivate a simplified model, defined next, that provides insight into the behavior induced by the softmax parameterization.

A value-softmax model.

Let
\mathbf{V}\in\mathbb{R}^{p\times p}
and
\bm{a}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
denote the trainable value matrix and score vector, respectively. Let
\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbf{V},\bm{a}\right)
be a loss function that depends only on the product
\bm{\beta}=\mathbf{V}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{a})
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbf{V},\bm{a}\right)=\ell\left(\mathbf{V}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{a})\right)=\ell\left(\bm{\beta}\right).

(1)

The loss does not depend on
\bm{a}
and
\mathbf{V}
separately but only through the combination
\mathbf{V}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{a})
. This model is a stylized simplification of the self-attention block.
Re-parameterized models are a popular setting to gain insight into the training dynamics of non-convex optimization problem
(
14
)
.
For convenience, we denote
\bm{s}=\bm{\sigma}(\bm{a})
.

Gradient flow.

We study the dynamics of the continuous time limit of gradient descent, the gradient flow. Although this analysis does not capture discrete-time effects, adaptive step sizes, or stochasticity, it nevertheless captures the principal behavior of first-order optimization and provides useful insight into the training process.

The gradient flow for the parameters
\mathbf{V}
and
\bm{a}
is given by the differential equations

\displaystyle\textup{{d}}{\mathbf{V}}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\mathbf{V}}\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbf{V},\bm{a}\right)\textup{{d}}{t},\,\,\textup{{d}}{\bm{a}}=-\nabla_{\bm{a}}\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbf{V},\bm{a}\right)\textup{{d}}{t}.

(2)

We exploit the reparameterization
\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbf{V},\bm{a}\right)=\ell\left(\mathbf{V}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{a})\right)
to obtain explicit expressions for the gradients. Using the chain rule on
\bm{\beta}=\mathbf{V}\bm{\sigma}\left(\bm{a}\right)
, the gradient flow can be written as

\displaystyle\textup{{d}}{\mathbf{V}}

\displaystyle=-\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\ell(\bm{\beta})\bm{s}^{\top}\textup{{d}}{t},

(3)

\displaystyle\textup{{d}}{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle=-\left[\mathrm{diag}\left(\bm{s}\right)-\bm{s}\bm{s}^{\top}\right]\mathbf{V}^{\top}\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\ell(\bm{\beta}).

(4)

The Jacobian of the softmax
\mathrm{diag}\left(\bm{s}\right)-\bm{s}\bm{s}^{\top}
plays a central role in shaping the dynamics of
\bm{a}
.

3
Polarizing effect of softmax

In this section, we present the main results for the value–softmax model. We begin with the logistic loss and then discuss regression losses as well as other nonlinearities and normalization functions.

3.1
Logistic loss

We consider a binary classification setting, mirroring standard transformer training where the final representation is optimized with cross-entropy (i.e., logistic loss in the binary case).
To isolate the effect of the value–softmax parameterization, we consider a stylized teacher setting in which the desired decision rule is given by a fixed linear classifier
\bm{\beta}_{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
.
The attention block produces
\bm{\beta}
, and the logistic loss encourages a large positive margin
\left\langle{\bm{\beta}_{*}},{\bm{\beta}}\right\rangle
, i.e., agreement with the classifier induced by
\bm{\beta}_{*}
.

This setting leads to the following formulation of the loss as a function of
\mathbf{V},\bm{a}
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbf{V},\bm{a}\right)=\log\left(1+\exp\left\langle{\bm{\beta}_{*}},{\,-\mathbf{V}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{a})}\right\rangle\,\right),

(5)

The gradient flow dynamics are given by

\displaystyle\textup{{d}}{\mathbf{V}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma(\bm{\beta})\,\bm{\beta}_{*}\bm{s}^{\top}\textup{{d}}{t},

\displaystyle\textup{{d}}{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma(\bm{\beta})\,\left[\mathrm{diag}\left(\bm{s}\right)-\bm{s}\bm{s}^{\top}\right]\mathbf{V}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{*}\textup{{d}}{t}.

where
\gamma(\bm{\beta})=\left(1+\exp\left\langle{\bm{\beta}_{*}},{\bm{\beta}}\right\rangle\right)^{-1}
.
Letting
\bm{u}=\mathbf{V}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{*}
, the dynamics reduced to a couples system over two vectors
\bm{a},\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{p}

\displaystyle\overset{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}{\bm{u}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma(\bm{\beta})\,\bigl\|{\bm{\beta}_{*}}\bigr\|^{2}\bm{s},

(6)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma(\bm{\beta})\,\left[\mathrm{diag}\left(\bm{s}\right)-\bm{s}\bm{s}^{\top}\right]\bm{u}.

(7)

Intuition from replicator dynamics.

Inspecting the gradient-flow equations, the evolution of the scores is driven by the centered signal

\displaystyle\overset{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}{\bm{a}}\sim\left(\bm{u}-\left\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{s}}\right\rangle\mathbf{1}\right),\quad\overset{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}{\bm{u}}\sim\left(\bm{u}-\left\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{s}}\right\rangle\mathbf{1}\right),

where we omit scaling factors and normalization terms for clarity.

Since
\bm{s}=\bm{\sigma}(\bm{a})
lies on the simplex,
\langle\bm{u},\bm{s}\rangle
is the
\bm{s}
-weighted average of the coordinates of
\bm{u}
. Thus, the term
\bm{u}-\langle\bm{u},\bm{s}\rangle\mathbf{1}
measures each coordinate’s deviation from the average.
This is reminiscent of replicator dynamics
(
27
)
: coordinates with above-average “fitness” (larger
\bm{u}_{i}
) are amplified relative to those below average, polarizing the mass towards the best-performing coordinates. Motivated by this analogy, we next analyze how softmax normalization induces such polarization and sparsity in
\bm{s}
.

Repulsion between the coordinates.

To formalize the polarizing effect, we first show that the dynamics induce repulsion between the coordinates of the attention scores
\bm{s}
and the value projections
\bm{u}
. We begin by stating an assumption on the initialization.

Assumption 3.1
.

The parameters
\mathbf{V},\bm{a}
are initialized such that
\bm{a}(0)=0
and
\bm{u}_{0}(0)>\bm{u}_{1}(0)>\ldots>\bm{u}_{p-1}(0)
for the projection
\bm{u}=\mathbf{V}^{\top}\bm{\beta}_{*}
.

This assumption is mild. The condition
\bm{a}(0)=0
implies
\bm{s}(0)
is uniform, which is consistent with standard practice. Without loss of generality, we assume the coordinates of
\bm{u}(0)
are ordered by re-indexing. The strict ordering holds almost surely under generic random initialization and can be relaxed if needed. We now show that gradient flow preserves this ordering and induces repulsion between coordinates.

Theorem 3.2
.

Consider gradient flow on the loss (
5
) under the initialization in
3.1
. Then, for all
t>0
,

(a)

Order preservation.
The order of both the projections
\bm{u}_{i}(t)
and attention scores
\bm{s}_{i}(t)
is preserved

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{0}(t)

\displaystyle>\bm{u}_{1}(t)>\ldots>\bm{u}_{p-1}(t),

\displaystyle\bm{s}_{0}(t)

\displaystyle>\bm{s}_{1}(t)>\ldots>\bm{s}_{p-1}(t).

(b)

Repulsion.
Pairwise gaps between value projections grow over time: for any
i\neq j
and any
t_{+}>t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\left(\bm{u}_{i}(t_{+})-\bm{u}_{j}(t_{+})\right)>\left(\bm{u}_{i}(t)-\bm{u}_{j}(t)\right).

Theorem

3.2
first establishes a
no-crossing
property: the coordinate ordering is preserved along the trajectory. To prove this, we introduce the Lyapunov potential
\Phi_{ij}=-\left(e^{-{\bm{a}}_{i}}-e^{-{\bm{a}}_{j}}\right)\left(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j}\right)
for
i\neq j\in[p]
. The derivative of potential is positive,
\textup{{d}}{\Phi_{ij}}>0
, hence the relative ordering between the scores and projections is preserved. In particular,
\Phi_{ij}(t)>0
, for all
t>0
, hence the co-ordinates never cross and the initial order remains conserved.
Second, the gradient flow exhibits repulsion among the coordinates of
\bm{u}
: for
i<j
, we have
\textup{{d}}{(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j})}>0
, driven by the preserved ordering of the attention scores.
Together, these properties highlight the polarizing nature of the gradient field in the value–softmax model.

using logistic loss. The plot shows the evolution of attention scores

Sparsity of the attention scores.

Having established the polarizing nature of the dynamics, we now quantify its strength and characterize the resulting limiting behavior.

Theorem 3.3
.

Consider gradient flow for the loss (
5
) under the initialization in
3.1
. Let
\delta=\min_{i<j}\left(\bm{u}_{i}(0)-\bm{u}_{j}(0)\right)
. Then we have

(a)

Vanishing loss.

\lim_{t\to\infty}L(\bm{\beta}(t))=0
.

(b)

Decay of non-maximal scores.
For any
j\neq 0
and any
t>0
,

\frac{\bm{s}_{j}(t)}{\bm{s}_{0}(t)}\leqslant\frac{1}{1+\nicefrac{{\delta}}{{p}}\int\limits_{0}^{t}\gamma(\bm{\beta}(s))ds}.

(c)

One-hot limit.

The polarization coefficient
\int_{0}^{t}\gamma(\bm{\beta}(s))ds=\Theta(\log t)
, and consequently the attention scores converge to a one-hot vector, i.e,

\lim_{t\to\infty}\bm{s}_{0}(t)=1,\quad\text{and}\quad\lim_{t\to\infty}\bm{s}_{j}(t)=0,\quad\text{for }j\neq 0.

To prove the theorem, we track the ratio
\bm{s}_{j}(t)/\bm{s}_{0}(t)
and show that it decays at a rate controlled by the integral
\int_{0}^{t}\gamma(\bm{\beta}(s))\,ds
.
We then prove that this integral diverges as
t\to\infty
, implying that
\bm{s}(t)
converges to a one-hot vector.
The softmax parameterization implicitly biases the dynamics toward low-entropy (peaked) attention.
Finally, note that,

\displaystyle\bm{\beta}(t)=\sum_{j}\bm{s}_{j}(t)\bm{v}_{j}(t).

Thus, in the limit,
\bm{\beta}(t)
becomes supported on a single column of
\mathbf{V}(t)
: among the many possible decompositions of
\bm{\beta}
as a convex combination of
\{\bm{v}_{j}\}
, gradient flow selects an extremal (one-hot) representation. This provides a theoretical explanation for the empirically observed tendency of softmax attention to become sparse.

Comparison with the dynamics of linear networks.

When
\sigma
is the identity, the model reduces to a linear network, whose gradient-flow dynamics have been extensively studied in connection with implicit regularization of gradient-based methods. In particular, it is known that the solution converges to a rank-one factorization of
\mathbf{V}
(see, e.g.,
(
12
)
).
In our setting, a similar phenomenon occurs: under the softmax parameterization,
\mathbf{V}(t)
also becomes approximately rank one (see Appendix
B.1
). The crucial difference, however, is that the attention scores
\bm{s}(t)
converge to a one-hot vector. This behavior does not arise in linear networks, and it forces the dominant right singular direction of
\mathbf{V}
to align with a coordinate axis (i.e., to be one-hot) rather than a generic direction.

The polarization we observe is not caused by the softmax nonlinearity in isolation, but by the
joint
training of the scores
\bm{a}
and the value matrix
\mathbf{V}
. Their co-adaptation drives the dynamics toward low-entropy (peaked) attention patterns.

Finally, it is important not to conflate this effect with sparse regression: the sparsity here is in the attention scores
\bm{s}
, not in the entries of the value matrix
\mathbf{V}
or final predictor
\bm{\beta}
.

3.2
Extensions of the result

We now explore how far our analysis extends beyond the logistic-loss setting. In particular, we vary the loss and the attention map to identify when polarization persists and when it fails.

Regression.

We now consider a regression setting in which the target vector
\bm{\beta}_{*}
is to be fitted. The loss is

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}\left(\mathbf{V},\bm{a}\right)=\frac{1}{2}\bigl\|{\bm{\beta}_{*}-\mathbf{V}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{a})}\bigr\|^{2}.

(8)

The associated gradient flow dynamics are

\displaystyle\textup{{d}}{\mathbf{V}}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{\beta}_{*}-\mathbf{V}\bm{s}\right)\bm{s}^{\top}\textup{{d}}{t},

\displaystyle\textup{{d}}{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle=\left[\mathrm{diag}\left(\bm{s}\right)-\bm{s}\bm{s}^{\top}\right]\mathbf{V}^{\top}\left(\bm{\beta}_{*}-\mathbf{V}\bm{s}\right)\textup{{d}}{t}.

We make the following initialization assumption.

Assumption 3.4
.

The parameters
\mathbf{V},\bm{a}
are initialized such that
\mathbf{V}(0)=0
and the attention scores satisfy
\bm{s}_{0}(0)>\bm{s}_{1}(0)>\ldots>\bm{s}_{p-1}(0)
.

Initializing the linear predictor to zero is standard in analyses of gradient flow for linear regression and simplifies the resulting dynamics. We assume the attention scores are strictly ordered at initialization to avoid ties and break symmetry. Without loss of generality, we label the coordinates so that the ordering is decreasing. Under this initialization,
\mathbf{V}(t)
remains rank one, which allows us to reduce the dynamics to a coupled system similar to
Equation

6
, as stated next.

Lemma 3.5
.

Consider gradient flow for the loss (
8
) under
3.4
. Then:

(a)

Rank-one structure.

There exists
\bm{u}(t)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}
such that
\mathbf{V}=\frac{\bm{\beta}_{*}}{\|\bm{\beta}_{*}\|^{2}}\bm{u}^{\top}
.

(b)

Reduced dynamics.
The pair
\bm{a},\bm{u}
satisfies

\displaystyle\overset{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}{\bm{u}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma(\bm{\beta})\,\bigl\|{\bm{\beta}_{*}}\bigr\|^{2}\bm{s},

(9)

\displaystyle\overset{\mathchoice{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\displaystyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\textstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\scriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}{\mathbin{\vbox{\hbox{\scalebox{.4}{$\scriptscriptstyle\bullet$}}}}}}{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle=-\gamma(\bm{\beta})\,\left[\mathrm{diag}\left(\bm{s}\right)-\bm{s}\bm{s}^{\top}\right]\bm{u},

(10)

where
\gamma(\bm{\beta})=1-\frac{\left\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{s}}\right\rangle}{\bigl\|{\bm{\beta}_{*}}\bigr\|^{2}}
.

Using the same techniques as in the logistic-loss case, we can derive an analogue of
Theorem

3.2
for the regression dynamics.
A key difference is that the cumulative polarization strength is finite:
\int_{0}^{\infty}\gamma(\bm{\beta}(s))\textup{{d}}{s}=\Theta(1)
(see Appendix for the result).
Consequently, polarization is only partial and
\bm{s}(t)
does not converge to a one-hot vector in general. More broadly, sparsification is controlled by the integral of the gradient magnitude: the slower the gradient decays, the stronger the induced sparsity in the attention scores.
This suggests that ill-conditioned regression problems (e.g.,
\|\bm{\beta}_{*}-X\mathbf{V}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{a})\|^{2}
for a matrix
X
) may exhibit stronger sparsification, since ill-conditioning slows down convergence. We empirically verify this relationship in Figures

.

KL divergence.

A natural alternative is the KL divergence between a target distribution
p_{*}
and a predicted distribution
\bm{\beta}
, e.g.,
\ell(\bm{\beta})=-\langle p_{*},\log\bm{\beta}\rangle
, where the logarithm is applied elementwise. The resulting gradient-flow dynamics are analogous to the regression case, and we again observe polarization, albeit with diminishing strength compared to the case of classification (Figure
10
).

Other non-linearities and normalization functions.

We first consider replacing softmax with elementwise nonlinearities such as the sigmoid activation
\sigma_{sig}(a)_{i}=\nicefrac{{1}}{{1+\exp(-a_{i})}}
or the Relu. For both the regression and logistic losses, we do not observe the same polarization behavior under sigmoid (and similarly under ReLU), see Figure
13
for reference. Intuitively, the key mechanism behind polarization in our analysis is the mean-centering term that appears in the softmax Jacobian; elementwise nonlinearities such as sigmoid or ReLU do not induce an analogous “fitness minus average fitness” interaction across coordinates.

We next consider alternative
normalization
maps, which retain this interaction structure. Let
f
be a monotonically increasing function and define the normalization
\bm{\sigma}_{f}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
by

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}_{f}(a)_{i}=\frac{f(a_{i})}{\sum_{j=1}^{p}f(a_{j})}.

Writing
\bm{s}_{f}\coloneqq\bm{\sigma}_{f}(\bm{a})
, the gradient flow dynamics for the logistic loss take the form

\displaystyle\textup{{d}}{\bm{u}}

\displaystyle=\gamma(\bm{\beta})\,\|\bm{\beta}_{*}\|^{2}\bm{s}_{f}\,\textup{{d}}{t},

\displaystyle\textup{{d}}{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle=\gamma(\bm{\beta})\,\frac{f^{\prime}(\bm{a})}{\sum_{j=1}^{p}f(\bm{a}_{j})}\odot(\bm{u}-\left\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{s}_{f}}\right\rangle\mathbf{1})\,\textup{{d}}{t},

This retains the replicator-type structure, and an analogue of
Theorem

3.2
can be established.
The polarization strength now depends on
f
and
f^{\prime}
. For instance, with
f(x)=x
(which does not enforce positivity), the polarization is typically insufficient to yield one-hot scores, where as with
f(x)=x^{2}
the dynamics can drive
\bm{s}_{f}
toward a one-hot limit, see Figure
13
provides empirical evidence for these behaviors.

Limitations.

A key limitation of our analysis is that, for the losses considered above, the gradient
\nabla\ell(\bm{\beta})
is aligned with the target direction
\bm{\beta}_{*}
(in the regression case, this relies on
3.4
).
This alignment substantially simplifies the dynamics and enables the reduction to the two-vector system in
\bm{a}
and
\bm{u}
.
In practice, such an alignment need not hold, and the present analysis may therefore not apply directly. Nevertheless, our experiments indicate that the polarization effect persists even without exact alignment; see
Figure

3
as alignment breaks in the large condition number case.

4.2
Imbalance in token influence

A second major implication of our results is the emergence of tokens that exert a disproportionate influence on the model’s output. As predicted by Theorem
3.3
, when attention weights converge to a one-hot vector, the output effectively ignores all context except for the single ’max-logit’ token. Consequently, the model’s predictions become highly sensitive to perturbations of that specific token.

We construct a simple classification dataset in the following way:
Consider a vocabulary
V
and partition
V
into
k

of equal size. An input and its corresponding label are

, where
x_{i}
is a sequence of
n
tokens sampled from
V^{(K)}
and labeled
k
. Note that as the sets are disjoint any one of the input tokens unambiguously predicts the label, thus any form of attention map allows to solve the classification problem.

Due to the low-entropy bias of softmax, however, we expect that the attention weights will concentrate on few tokens, effectively ignoring the others. We test this by examining the behavior of the classifier in adversarial out-of-distribution setting where we are allowed to change one token in the input with the goal of flipping the classifier’s prediction. The success rates of this experiment are presented in Figure

5
Conclusion

This work isolates an implicit optimization bias in the value–softmax parameterization
\mathbf{V}\bm{\sigma}(\bm{a})
: under gradient flow, jointly training the scores and values polarizes the attention distribution toward low-entropy solutions, even when many higher-entropy decompositions represent the same predictor. In a stylized classification setting, we prove that
\bm{\sigma}(\bm{a})
converges to a one-hot vector, selecting an extremal representation. We extend the analysis to regression and related objectives, clarifying how convergence speed and activation choices modulate polarization.
Finally, we connect this dynamics to transformer behavior and identify its connection with attention sinks and associated pathologies. We support the theory with experiments showing how attention type impacts sparsity in models trained for induction and classification, as well as for pretrained LLMs.

C.1
Remarks on the linear regression case

Note that the dynamics of
\bm{a}
and
\bm{u}
are similar to the dynamics in the classification case with logistic loss. The only difference is the scaling factor
\left(1-\left\langle{\bm{\beta}_{*}},{\bm{\beta}}\right\rangle/\bigl\|{\bm{\beta}_{*}}\bigr\|^{2}\right)
which depends on the current loss. Theorem
3.2
implies the no-crossing property of the projections and attention scores in this case as well in the case of linear regression.

Now we will turn to the strength of the polarization, i.e.,
\int\gamma(s)ds.
Following the same approach as in Theorem
3.3
, we can establish the following result regarding the sparsity of the attention scores in the case of linear regression. From now on, assume that
\bigl\|{\bm{\beta}_{*}}\bigr\|=1
for brevity. From the Lemma
D.2
, we have that,

\displaystyle\frac{d\ell(\bm{\beta})}{dt}

\displaystyle\leqslant-\frac{1}{p}\bigl\|{\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\ell}\bigr\|^{2}.

Using this in the context of linear regression, we get,

\displaystyle\ell(\bm{\beta})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}_{*}\|^{2},

\displaystyle\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\ell

\displaystyle=(\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}_{*}),

\displaystyle\frac{d\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}_{*}\|^{2}}{dt}

\displaystyle\leqslant-\frac{1}{p}\|\bm{\beta}-\bm{\beta}_{*}\|^{2}.

Lemma D.2
.

Convergence.

Consider the gradient flow dynamics given by the equations (
12
) over the loss function
\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{V},\bm{a})
,

\displaystyle\frac{d\,\ell(\bm{\beta})}{dt}

\displaystyle\leqslant-\frac{1}{d}\bigl\|{\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\ell}\bigr\|^{2}

Proof.

With
\bm{r}=-\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\ell
, the time derivatives of the parameters are given by,

\displaystyle\frac{d\mathbf{V}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\bm{r}\bm{s}^{\top},

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{s}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathrm{diag}\left(\bm{s}\right)-\bm{s}\bm{s}^{\top}\right)^{2}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\bm{r}.

Considering the evolution of
\bm{\beta}=\mathbf{V}\bm{s}
, we have,

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{\beta}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\frac{d\mathbf{V}\bm{s}}{dt}=\frac{d\mathbf{V}}{dt}\bm{s}+\mathbf{V}\frac{d\bm{s}}{dt},

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{r}\bm{s}^{\top}\right)\bm{s}+\mathbf{V}\left(\mathrm{diag}\left(\bm{s}\right)-\bm{s}\bm{s}^{\top}\right)^{2}\mathbf{V}^{\top}\bm{r},

\displaystyle=\left[\bigl\|{\bm{s}}\bigr\|^{2}\mathrm{I}+\mathbf{V}\left(\mathrm{diag}\left(\bm{s}\right)-\bm{s}\bm{s}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{V}^{\top}\right]\bm{r}.

Let us denote the matrix in the brackets as
\mathbf{M}(\bm{s},\mathbf{V})
, i.e.,

\displaystyle\mathbf{M}(\bm{s},\mathbf{V})=\bigl\|{\bm{s}}\bigr\|^{2}\mathrm{I}+\mathbf{V}\left(\mathrm{diag}\left(\bm{s}\right)-\bm{s}\bm{s}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{V}^{\top}.

Using this notation, we can write the evolution of
\bm{\beta}
as follows:

\displaystyle\frac{d\bm{\beta}}{dt}

\displaystyle=\mathbf{M}(\bm{s},\mathbf{V})\bm{r}=-\mathbf{M}(\bm{s},\mathbf{V})\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\ell.

Considering the time derivative of the loss function
\ell(\bm{\beta})
, we have,

\displaystyle\frac{d\ell(\bm{\beta})}{dt}

\displaystyle=\left\langle{\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\ell},{\frac{d\bm{\beta}}{dt}}\right\rangle=-\left\langle{\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\ell\,\,},{\,\,\mathbf{M}(\bm{s},\mathbf{V})\,\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\ell}\right\rangle.

Now we will show that the matrix
\mathbf{M}(\bm{s},\mathbf{V})
is positive definite. First,

\displaystyle\bigl\|{\bm{s}}\bigr\|^{2}\geqslant\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bm{s}_{i}^{2}\geqslant\frac{1}{d}\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bm{s}_{i}\right)^{2}=\frac{1}{d}.

The next term is positive semi-definite since for any vector
\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we have,

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{\top}\mathbf{V}\left(\mathrm{diag}\left(\bm{s}\right)-\bm{s}\bm{s}^{\top}\right)\mathbf{V}^{\top}\bm{u}

\displaystyle=\left(\mathbf{V}^{\top}\bm{u}\right)^{\top}\left(\mathrm{diag}\left(\bm{s}\right)-\bm{s}\bm{s}^{\top}\right)\left(\mathbf{V}^{\top}\bm{u}\right),

\displaystyle=\mu^{\top}\left(\mathrm{diag}\left(\bm{s}\right)-\bm{s}\bm{s}^{\top}\right)\mu,\quad\text{ where }\mu=\mathbf{V}^{\top}\bm{u},

\displaystyle=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bm{s}_{i}\mu_{i}^{2}-\left(\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bm{s}_{i}\mu_{i}\right)^{2}\geqslant 0,

where the last inequality follows from the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality. Hence we have,

\displaystyle\bm{u}^{\top}\mathbf{M}(\bm{s},\mathbf{V})\bm{u}\geqslant\frac{1}{d}\bigl\|{\bm{u}}\bigr\|^{2},\quad\forall\,\bm{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

This shows that the matrix
\mathbf{M}(\bm{s},\mathbf{V})
is positive definite. Using the positive definiteness of
\mathbf{M}(\bm{s},\mathbf{V})
, we have,

\displaystyle\frac{d\ell(\bm{\beta})}{dt}

\displaystyle\leqslant-\frac{1}{d}\bigl\|{\nabla_{\bm{\beta}}\ell}\bigr\|^{2}\leqslant 0.

This shows that the loss function
\ell(\bm{\beta})
is non-increasing along the gradient flow dynamics of
\mathbf{V}
and
\bm{s}
.
∎

\displaystyle=\mathbf{V}\frac{\partial\bm{s}}{\partial\bm{a}}=\mathbf{V}\left(\mathrm{diag}\left(\bm{s}\right)-\bm{s}\bm{s}^{\top}\right).

(29)

It is needed to be clarified that the jacobian
\frac{\partial\bm{\beta}}{\partial\mathbf{V}}
is a matrix of size
d\times d^{2}
and it is constructed in the following way:

\displaystyle\frac{\partial\bm{\beta}}{\partial\mathbf{V}}=\begin{bmatrix}\frac{\partial\bm{\beta}}{\partial\bm{v}_{1}}&\frac{\partial\bm{\beta}}{\partial\bm{v}_{2}}&\ldots&\frac{\partial\bm{\beta}}{\partial\bm{v}_{d}}\end{bmatrix}=\bm{s}^{\top}\otimes\mathrm{I}_{d}.

where each row is
\frac{\partial\bm{\beta}_{i}}{\partial\mathrm{vec}{(\mathbf{V})}}
.

D.2
Normalization

Here, we will consider the case of general normalization functions and establish that the no-crossing property holds for the logistic loss as well as the regression loss function . In particular,
consider a general monotonically increasing differentiable function
f
, and define the normalization
\bm{\sigma}_{f}:\mathbb{R}^{p}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}
for any
a
in
\mathbb{R}^{p}
as

\displaystyle\bm{\sigma}_{f}(a)_{i}=\frac{f(a_{i})}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}f(a_{j})}.

For the logistic loss function, the gradient flow dynamics is given by

\displaystyle\textup{{d}}{\bm{u}}

\displaystyle=\gamma(\bm{\beta})\,\|\bm{\beta}_{*}\|^{2}\bm{s}_{f}\,\textup{{d}}{t},

(30)

\displaystyle\textup{{d}}{\bm{a}}

\displaystyle=\gamma(\bm{\beta})\,\frac{f^{\prime}(\bm{a})}{\sum\limits_{j=1}^{p}f(\bm{a}_{j})}\odot(\bm{u}-\left\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{\sigma}_{f}}\right\rangle\mathbf{1})\,\textup{{d}}{t}.

(31)

Lemma D.4
.

Consider the gradient flow dynamics given by the equation Eq. (
30
) over the logistic loss function
\ell(\bm{\beta})
. For the initialization given by the Assumption
3.1
, the following no-crossing property holds for all time
t\geqslant 0
,

\displaystyle\bm{u}_{0}(t)>\bm{u}_{1}(t)>\bm{u}_{2}(t)\ldots>\bm{u}_{p-1}(t),

\displaystyle{\bm{a}}_{0}(t)\geqslant{\bm{a}}_{1}(t)\geqslant{\bm{a}}_{2}(t)\ldots\geqslant{\bm{a}}_{p-1}(t).

Proof.

Let
\mathbf{F}=\sum_{j}f(\bm{a}_{j})
and let

G(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\frac{1}{f^{\prime}(v)}dv.

Using this to we rewrite the co-ordinate wise dynamics as

\displaystyle d\bm{u}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{\mathbf{F}}f({\bm{a}}_{i})dt,

\displaystyle d{\bm{a}}_{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{\mathbf{F}}f^{\prime}({\bm{a}}_{i})\left(\bm{u}_{i}-\left\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{s}_{f}}\right\rangle\right)dt,

\displaystyle\frac{d{\bm{a}}_{i}}{f^{\prime}({\bm{a}}_{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{\mathbf{F}}\left(\bm{u}_{i}-\left\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{\sigma}_{f}}\right\rangle\right)dt.

\displaystyle d{G(a_{i})}

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{\mathbf{F}}\left(\bm{u}_{i}-\left\langle{\bm{u}},{\bm{\sigma}_{f}}\right\rangle\right)dt,

For any
i,j
, taking the differences,

\displaystyle d\left(G({\bm{a}}_{i})-G({\bm{a}}_{j})\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{\mathbf{F}}\left(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j}\right)dt.

\displaystyle d\left(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{\mathbf{F}}\left(f({\bm{a}}_{i})-f({\bm{a}}_{j})\right)dt.

We can take a potential function
\Phi_{ij}=\left(G({\bm{a}}_{i})-G({\bm{a}}_{j})\right)\left(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j}\right)
, using this we have,

\displaystyle d\Phi_{ij}

\displaystyle=\left(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j}\right)d\left(G({\bm{a}}_{i})-G({\bm{a}}_{j})\right)+\left(G({\bm{a}}_{i})-G({\bm{a}}_{j})\right)d\left(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j}\right),

\displaystyle=\frac{\gamma}{\mathbf{F}}\left[\left(\bm{u}_{i}-\bm{u}_{j}\right)^{2}+\left(G({\bm{a}}_{i})-G({\bm{a}}_{j})\right)\left(f({\bm{a}}_{i})-f({\bm{a}}_{j})\right)\right]dt.

Note that
f
increases monotonically, hence
f^{\prime}(a)>0
everywhere, which implies
1/f^{\prime}(a)>0
everywhere and G also increases monotonically. We assume that it does not hit
0
in finite time, so the term
\left(G({\bm{a}}_{i})-G({\bm{a}}_{j})\right)\left(f({\bm{a}}_{i})-f({\bm{a}}_{j})\right)
is always non-negative. This implies that
d\Phi_{ij}\geqslant 0
, ensuring that the potential function
\Phi_{ij}
does not decrease over time. Consequently, the no-crossing property holds for all time
t\geqslant 0
.
∎

Metrics.

We discard all runs that did not achieve induction accuracy above

a sink head
if it’s sink score is higher than 0.9.
The computation can be expressed via the following pseudocode:

A = attention_matrix # [layer, head, sample, query_seq_len, key_seq_len]

# index 0 is removed because it’s BOS.
# indices -1 and -2 are removed because prediction is not random.
bos_score[L, H] = A[L, H, :, 1 : -2, 0]
total_weight[L, H] = A[L, H, :, 1 : -2, :].sum(axis=-1)

# clipping affects attention types without positivity. doesn’t change is_sink.
sink_score[L, H] = (bos_score[L, H] / total_weight[L, H]).mean().clip(0, 1)
is_sink[L, H] = (sink_score[L, H] > 0.9)

We only plot the results from the second layer, as all heads in the first layer typically attend to the previous token.

We calculate the out-of-distribution flip rate, presented in Figure
6

X1, X2, Y1, Y2 = pick_samples_with_different_labels()

A1 = attention_matrix(X1) # [layer, head, query_seq_len, key_seq_len]
A2 = attention_matrix(X2)

# obtaining the max-logit tokens excluding CLS itself
ranking_1[L, H] = A1[L, H,-1,:-1].argsort(reverse=True)
ranking_2[L, H] = A2[L, H,-1,:-1].argsort(reverse=True)

# max-logit token from label 2 + others from label 1
adversarial_1[L, H] = [ranking_2[L, H][0]] + ranking_1[L, H][1:]

# max-logit token from label 1 + others from label 2
adversarial_2[L, H] = [ranking_1[L, H][0]] + ranking_2[L, H][1:]

prediction_adv_1[L, H] = model(adversarial_1[L, H])
prediction_adv_2[L, H] = model(adversarial_2[L, H])

# flipped if swapping one token changes the prediction
flipped_1[L, H] = (prediction_adv_1[L, H] == Y2)
flipped_2[L, H] = (prediction_adv_2[L, H] == Y1)
flip_rate[L, H] = (flip_1 + flip_2) / 2
