Healthcare workers’ knowledge and acceptance of the malaria vaccine in the Nanoro Health District of Burkina Faso: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Jean-Vincent Lamien, Bérenger Kaboré, Karim Derra, Zanma Michaël John Compaoré, Guétawendé Job Wilfried Nassa, Aimé Parfait Korou Yaro, Bapouguini Raymond Gbangou, Raffaella Ravinetto, Halidou Tinto, Carine Dochez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn February 2024, Burkina Faso introduced the malaria vaccine RTS,S/AS01 into its Expanded Programme on Immunisation (EPI). For vaccination to be successful, an important criterion is its acceptance by the population. Healthcare workers (HCWs) are among the primary sources of information for caregivers when deciding whether to vaccinate their children or not. This study aimed to assess HCWs’ knowledge and acceptance of the new malaria vaccine (MV) in the Nanoro Health District and to identify factors associated with MV acceptance within this group.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional study among HCWs from April to May 2025 in Nanoro (Burkina Faso), using an online questionnaire. All HCWs employed in the public or private health sectors of the Nanoro Health District at the time of data collection were eligible to participate, upon voluntary informed consent. The vaccine acceptance rate was expressed as the proportion of HCWs willing to vaccinate their children out of the total number of participants. Logistic regression was employed for both bivariate and multivariate analyses to test associations.ResultsTwo hundred fifty-seven (257) HCWs out of 340 eligible HCWs participated in the survey. The vaccine acceptance rate was 98.8%. Insufficient information about the vaccine was the primary reason for hesitancy. Twenty-two percent (22.6%) of the HCWs were unaware of the type of vaccine used in the district and had no knowledge of the vaccination schedule. More than half (58.0%) stated that they did not receive any training before the vaccine rollout. Willingness to recommend the vaccine and positive attitudes were associated with HCWs’ willingness to vaccinate their children, although independent effects could not be confirmed. No links were found between vaccine acceptance and sociodemographic characteristics.ConclusionsMost HCWs were willing to vaccinate their children against malaria. However, few were hesitant due to insufficient information about the vaccine and more than one-fifth had limited knowledge about this specific vaccine. Training all HCWs on the MV (whether involved in EPI or not) and strengthening communication about it are essential to address concerns around the vaccine, and ensure sustained uptake in the community, thereby improving national vaccination coverage.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12936-026-06006-8.Keywords: Malaria, Vaccine, Vaccination, Acceptance, Hesitancy, Confidence, Healthcare workers, Nanoro, Burkina Faso, Africa

Background

Malaria remains a significant public health challenge globally, with an estimated 282 million cases and 610,000 deaths reported in 2024, as per the latest World Health Organization (WHO) report [1]. In the same year, Africa bore 94% of the world’s malaria cases and accounted for 95% of malaria-related deaths [1]. Four species of the Plasmodium protozoan parasite are responsible for human malaria infections (P. falciparum, P. vivax, P. ovale, P. malariae). P. falciparum is the deadliest species, accounting for more than 90% of all malaria-attributable cases and deaths [2].

In Burkina Faso, malaria is the primary cause of medical consultations (24.1%), hospitalizations (38.3%) and the fourth cause of death within healthcare facilities (5.2%), after prematurity, neonatal infection and neonatal distress [3]. The most vulnerable populations include children under five years of age and pregnant women. In 2025, the country documented 7,349,425 malaria cases, 26.7% of which concerned children under five years. The incidence rates of malaria were reported as 302.3 per 1,000 inhabitants for the general population and 465.6 per 1000 children under five years old [3].

Several tools and interventions have been deployed during the last decades, including prevention, vector control, diagnosis and case management [2, 4, 5]. Pre-existing prevention tools include chemoprophylaxis, preventive chemotherapy and measures against mosquito bites, such as mosquito nets and mosquito repellents. Vector control strategies include the use of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), indoor residual spraying (IRS) and larval source management. The failure of these measures to control or eliminate the disease in many countries has led to the development of malaria vaccines (MVs). Two MVs, RTS,S/AS01 and R21/Matrix M, were approved by the WHO in October 2021 and December 2023, respectively, for widespread use in endemic countries [6]. These pre-erythrocytic vaccines cannot interrupt malaria transmission. They are intended to prevent P. falciparum infection and the resulting illness and death in children aged 5 to 17 months for RTS,S, and in children aged 5 to 36 months for R21 [2].

The government of Burkina Faso introduced the RTS,S/AS01 malaria vaccine on February 5, 2024, as part of its Expanded Programme on Immunisation (EPI), alongside existing preventive measures [7]. The vaccine’s introduction in Burkina Faso follows that of Cameroon and of countries that participated in the WHO Malaria Vaccine Implementation Programme (MVIP): Ghana, Kenya, and Malawi [8, 9]. In a phase 3 clinical trial, this vaccine demonstrated 39% efficacy against clinical malaria and 29% efficacy against severe malaria in children aged 5 to 17 months at the time of the first vaccination, over a median follow-up period of 46 months, after receiving four doses [6, 10]. In Burkina Faso, children should receive four doses of the vaccine, according to the following EPI schedule: the first dose at 5 months, the second at 6 months, the third at 7 months and the fourth at 15 months after birth. From February 2024 to August 2025, vaccinations with RTS,S were rolled out in 27 priority health districts out of the country's 70 districts. On August 18, 2025, the country scaled the vaccination up to the national level with R21 vaccine, following the same vaccination schedule as RTS,S. The 27 priority health districts continued with RTS,S until the end of 2025 before switching to R21, while the new 43 districts have started directly with R21. At the time of this study, the malaria vaccination programme was still in its early implementation phase, and most eligible children had not yet reached 15 months of age. Consequently, children who were up to date with the vaccination schedule had generally received the first three doses and were awaiting the fourth dose scheduled at 15 months of age.

Despite the country's efforts to ensure high vaccination coverage, the vaccine uptake was relatively modest during the first phase of the rollout with RTS,S. Indeed, the Ministry of Health’s 2024 Statistical Yearbook revealed an overall coverage of 70.2% for the first dose, 59.0% for the second dose, and 47.8% for the third dose in the 27 health districts, compared with the national targets set at 91, 87, and 84% for the first, second, and third doses respectively [11].

The Nanoro health district is one of the 27 districts where the initial rollout of the RTS,S vaccine took place. The vaccination coverage in this area follows a similar trend as at the national level, with coverage rates of 67.1, 57.8, and 48.1% for the first, second and third doses respectively [11]. Several studies conducted prior to the MV rollout have emphasized challenges such as inadequate community engagement, inefficient delivery of child immunization services, poor vaccine availability and insufficient funding, all of which could hinder successful implementation in Sub-Saharan Africa [12–14].

Although vaccination is recognized as one of the most cost-effective interventions for preventing infectious diseases, one of the biggest challenges to its implementation is vaccine hesitancy, which has been classified by the WHO in 2019 as one of the ten main global health threats [15]. However, to the best of our knowledge, there is no quantitative evidence regarding MV hesitancy in Burkina Faso. The only study that analysed community perceptions of MVs in the country was a qualitative study conducted in 2014, far before the vaccine was introduced in the EPI [16]. Many participants were favourable towards a potential future MV and hoped to see it distributed as soon as possible. However, given the low malaria vaccination coverage following months of vaccine roll-out, there is an urgent need to reassess the acceptance of the vaccine in the country, especially in a post-COVID-19 context when vaccine hesitancy has increased worldwide [17].

This study aims to evaluate the knowledge and acceptance of the MV among healthcare workers (HCWs) in the Nanoro Health District. Numerous studies have shown that HCWs play a vital role in building public confidence in vaccines and addressing the concerns of patients and caregivers [18, 19]. Understanding their perceptions, attitudes and behaviours regarding the MV in Nanoro will provide insights into their potential influence on vaccine uptake in the community. This analysis is important for informing policies to improve the vaccine uptake among the population, especially given the current vaccination scale-up across the country since August 2025.

Methods

The methodology was inspired by two studies conducted in Oman and Indonesia, which assessed the acceptance of the COVID-19 vaccine among HCWs, along with a third study conducted in Indonesia that evaluated the knowledge, attitude and acceptance of the human papillomavirus vaccine and vaccination among university students [20–22].

Study design and setting

Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional study between April and May 2025 to assess the knowledge and acceptance of the MV among HCWs in the Nanoro Health District of Burkina Faso.

The Nanoro Health District is a rural district located 85 km from Ouagadougou (the capital city) in the Centre-West Region of the country and is one of the 27 districts where the initial phase of the MV rollout took place. It covers an area of 1302 km2, with an estimated population of 193,777 in 2024, distributed across 61 villages [23]. The population is predominantly young, with the 0–15 years age group accounting for 50.6%. The local health system, led by the District Management Team, consists of the formal sector which includes public and private health facilities, as well as the informal sector comprising traditional healers and informal drug sellers. The public sector includes health and medical centres at the first line, one district hospital at the second line, and the Clinical Research Unit of Nanoro (CRUN). The private sector includes the CMA Saint-Camille de Nanoro and two pharmacies. CRUN is one of the research units in Africa that has conducted clinical trials for both the RTS,S/AS01 and R21/Matrix-M MVs [10, 24]. The district has approximately 340 HCWs. Malaria vaccination is delivered through the EPI services in public health facilities by the itinerant community health and hygiene officers and through active research and referral of zero-dose children coupled with seasonal malaria chemoprevention campaigns.

Study population

Study population

All HCWs who were employed in the public or private health sector of the Nanoro Health District at the time of data collection were considered eligible for this survey. Only HCWs who consented to respond to the questionnaire participated in the survey. We considered the following individuals as HCWs in our study: doctors, pharmacists, nurses, midwives, health officers, laboratory technicians, pharmacists’ assistants and itinerant community health and hygiene officers.

The minimum sample size was calculated to be 180 participants, based on a total population of 340 HCWs, an assumed acceptance rate of 50%, a 5% margin of error, and a 95% confidence interval. To account for potential non-response, the sample size was increased by 10%, resulting in a final target sample size of 200 participants.

Data collection

We collected data using an online questionnaire developed in English with Microsoft Forms and translated into French, one of the two official working languages of Burkina Faso. The questionnaire comprised 32 items and required approximately 10 to 15 min to complete. It was designed to assess the knowledge and acceptance of the MV among HCWs and to capture their perceptions and attitudes about the vaccine.

The questionnaire included four sections: (1) Sociodemographic information; (2) Knowledge and perceptions of malaria vaccination; (3) Attitudes and behaviours toward malaria vaccination; (4) Trust in government and opinions on the impact of malaria vaccination.

The invitation to participate was disseminated through WhatsApp groups and mailing lists of health facilities, with a link to the questionnaire that allowed participants to decide freely whether to take part or not to the survey. The questionnaire was not sent individually to HCWs. The invitation clearly stated that responses would remain anonymous and would not be shared with supervisors or the District Management Team.

Prior to data collection, the questionnaire was pre-tested with 16 HCWs from Burkina Faso who were not working in the Nanoro Health District and would therefore not participate in the study.

Descriptive statistics

Descriptive statistics

The sociodemographic variables were summarized as categorical variables. Frequencies and percentages were used to describe the sociodemographic characteristics of the study population. The outcome of interest was HCWs’ willingness to have their own children vaccinated. All HCWs were asked whether they would vaccinate their own child against malaria, regardless of whether they had a child, and all respondents were included in the analysis of the primary outcome. The acceptance rate of the MV among HCWs was expressed as the proportion of HCWs who were willing to vaccinate their children out of the total number of participants. Sociodemographic data were disaggregated based on HCWs’ willingness to vaccinate their children (Yes, No, Not sure) and the results were presented in frequency tables. Participants’ responses were categorized as binary (True/False; Yes/No) or on a Likert scale. Likert scale questions related to “knowledge” were categorized as binary (True/False), while those pertaining to “attitudes” and “trust in government” were converted into a three-point scale (Agree, Neutral, Disagree) and responses were classified as positive or negative attitude based on the nature of each question. The responses were further disaggregated according to HCWs’ willingness to recommend the vaccine and presented in frequency tables.

Analytical statistics

Analytical statistics

Logistic regression was employed for both bivariate and multivariate analyses to test associations. The analysis aimed to identify sociodemographic factors as well as knowledge, attitudes and perceptions and trust-in-government factors associated with vaccine acceptance. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant for assessing the association between variables. Knowledge and attitude scores were computed by assigning a value of one or zero, depending on the nature of the question. For binary responses, the correct answer was scored as 1. In the binary analysis, a median score of 6 served as the cut-off point for MV knowledge. For attitudes and perceptions, the median scores were converted to a 100% scale. Scale variables related to attitudes and perceptions were scored as one or zero, depending on the response. Odds ratios were calculated for bivariate and multivariate analyses. Friedman’s test was used to rank the sources of information on the MV for caregivers and HCWs. This test is a non-parametric method suitable for comparing ranked data from related samples; allowing us to assess differences in perceived importance without assuming normality [25]. Data were analysed using R software version 4.4.1 and Excel 365 was used to generate the figures.

Results

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study population

Sociodemographic characteristics of the study population

Out of the 340 HCWs employed in the Nanoro Health District at the time of data collection, a total of 257 participated in this study, resulting in a response rate of 75.6% (Table 1). Among the participants, 59.1% (n = 152) were aged between 30 and 39 years, 50.6% (n = 130) were female, and 29.6% (n = 76) had no children. More than half of the HCWs (61.9%; n = 159) had not attained a university education. Most respondents (80.9%; n = 208) were clinicians, 75.5% (n = 194) had less than 10 years of professional experience, 59.5% (n = 153) were working in a public health facility, and 43.2% (n = 111) were employed in a Primary Healthcare (PHC) facility.

Sociodemographic characteristics of healthcare workers and their willingness to vaccinate their own children against malaria (N = 257)

Malaria vaccine acceptance

Malaria vaccine acceptance

Two hundred fifty-four (254) respondents stated that they were willing to vaccinate their own children and the children of family members or friends against malaria, leading to a significant MV acceptance rate of 98.8% (95% CI 0.97–0.99) (Table 1). Of the HCWs willing to vaccinate their own children, 76.0% (193/254) were below 39 years of age, 50.4% (128/254) were female, and 29.5% (75/254) had nod children. Sixty-two percent (62.6%; 159/254) had not attained a university education level. Additionally, 81.9% (208/254) of them were clinicians, 75.6% (192/254) had less than 10 years of professional experience, 59.8% (152/254) were working in a public health facility, and 43.3% (110/254) were employed in a PHC facility.

No HCWs stated that they would not vaccinate their own children. However, three HCWs (1.2%; 95% CI 0.002–0.034) were hesitant (answering “Not Sure”). All of them were over 30 years old, had attained university-level education, and were non-clinicians. Of the three HCWs, one had no children, two were female and two had less than 10 years of work experience.

Healthcare workers’ willingness to recommend the malaria vaccine

Healthcare workers’ willingness to recommend the malaria vaccine

Almost all HCWs (98.8%, 254/257) said they would recommend the MV to caregivers. Three participants (3/257) were hesitant about recommending the vaccine, with two (0.8%) reporting uncertainty (“Not sure”) and one (0.4%) expressing unwillingness (“No”). These were the same individuals who were also hesitant to allow vaccination of their own children. All three cited a common reason for their hesitation: “I don’t have enough information about the malaria vaccine”.

Knowledge of the malaria vaccine

Knowledge of the malaria vaccine

Almost all HCWs (99.6%; n = 256) were aware of the introduction of a MV in the country’s EPI and 97.3% (n = 250) were aware of its introduction in Nanoro (Table 2). However, 22.6% (n = 58) of HCWs were unable to identify the type of malaria vaccine introduced in the district. Among these participants, 45 (77.6%) were affiliated with the Clinical Research Unit of Nanoro (CRUN). In addition, 22.2% (n = 57) of HCWs did not know the recommended malaria vaccination schedule. More than half of the HCWs (58.0%; n = 149) stated that they did not receive any training before the introduction of the vaccine in the district. Two hundred forty-six participants (95.7%; n = 246) agreed that malaria vaccination can protect children aged 5 to 17 months from being infected by P. falciparum and 89.1% (n = 229) stated that malaria vaccination can prevent children aged 5 to 17 months from developing severe malaria. Ninety-one participants (35.4%) declared that malaria vaccination is the only way to protect children aged 5 to 17 months from being infected by P. falciparum. However, 5.4% (n = 14) believed that malaria vaccination would not yield any benefit for children aged 5 to 17 months.

None of the three hesitant HCWs received training on malaria vaccination, and one of them was not aware of the introduction of the MV in the Nanoro Health District (Table 2).

Attitudes and perceptions towards malaria vaccine and trust in the government

Attitudes and perceptions towards malaria vaccine and trust in the government

Seventeen percent of respondents (17.5%; n = 45) expressed concerns about the safety of the MV, 14.0% (n = 36) worried about potential serious side effects, and 3.9% (n = 10) believed that other malaria prevention measures are much better than the MV (Table 2).

Among the three HCWs who expressed hesitancy, two raised concerns regarding the general safety of the MV, and two voiced apprehensions about potential serious side effects.

Two hundred thirty-eight (92.6%; n = 238) participants stated that they trusted the government regarding the planning and introduction of the MV. Eighty-six percent (86.4%; n = 222) found that the government provides transparent and up-to-date information on the MV development and its introduction in the EPI and 78.2% (n = 201) declared that the government provides adequate training for HCWs on the MV and vaccination (Table 2).

Healthcare workers’ views on caregivers’ reactions to the malaria vaccine

Healthcare workers’ views on caregivers’ reactions to the malaria vaccine

Of the 257 HCWs, 54.5% (n = 140) reported encountering caregivers who were hesitant to vaccinate their children against malaria, while 40.5% (n = 104) stated that they did not encounter hesitant caregivers (Fig. 1). Most participants (90.3%; n = 232) expressed confidence in addressing hesitancy towards the MV.

Proportion of healthcare workers who encountered hesitant caregivers regarding malaria vaccination, N = 257.

To the question of why some caregivers are uncertain or unwilling to vaccinate their children against malaria, HCWs identified misinformation as the primary reason (199/257) followed by fear of new vaccines (178/257), concerns about vaccine safety (97/257), doubts regarding vaccine efficacy (76/257) and the necessity of using other preventive measures (28/257) in addition to vaccination to prevent the disease (Fig. 2).

Sources of information and communication about the vaccine

Sources of information and communication about the vaccine

Healthcare workers’ opinions on the reasons for caregivers' hesitancy towards the malaria vaccine, N = 257

HCWs’ primary source of information on the MV was the workplace (Friedman’s test; mean rank: 2.84), followed by radio, television and newspapers (mean rank: 3.37), colleagues (mean rank: 3.74), and social media (mean rank: 4.04). The Ministry of Health (MOH) website was ranked fifth as a source of information for the HCWs (mean rank: 4.29).

The respondents reported that television and radio were the primary sources of information for the public regarding malaria vaccination (Friedman’s test; mean rank: 3.56), followed by direct counselling (mean rank: 3.68). Facebook and WhatsApp were ranked 4th (mean rank: 4.70) and 5th (mean rank: 5.09) respectively as information sources for the public in the Nanoro Health District.

For communication to the public regarding the MV, HCWs indicated that information on the benefits of the vaccine should be the main message to deliver (240/257). Communication related to disease prevention measures and the vaccine's effectiveness in preventing future malaria infections were identified as the second (211/257) and third (208/257) key messages to disseminate, respectively (Fig. 3).

Predictors of malaria vaccine acceptance

Predictors of malaria vaccine acceptance

To determine the factors associated with vaccine acceptance among HCWs, we hypothesized that there would be no differences in HCWs’ willingness to vaccinate their own children based on age, gender, religion, education level, work experience, occupation, type and level of health facility, recommendation of the MV to caregivers, knowledge of the MV and trust in the government.

In the bivariate analysis, HCWs who recommended the MV to caregivers were significantly more likely to be willing to vaccinate their own children (OR = 875.60; 95% CI 102.86–∞) (Table 3). HCWs with a positive attitude towards the MV were also more likely to ensure that their children were vaccinated (OR = 26.62; 95% CI 3.98–∞). However, these variables were not included in the multivariate model due to quasi-complete separation which makes the interpretability difficult and the reliability questionable.

Additional comments from study participants

Additional comments from study participants

Of the 257 HCWs who completed the questionnaire, 45 (17.5%) provided additional written comments. These open-ended responses highlighted key barriers to MV implementation and offered practical suggestions for improvement. Several respondents (n = 10) cited inadequate communication and limited community engagement during the vaccine rollout. Fifteen (15) HCWs emphasized the need for improved training of HCWs on malaria vaccination. Some participants proposed expanding the target population for vaccination to include additional age groups and vulnerable populations, such as older children and pregnant women.

Discussion

Malaria vaccine acceptance rate

Malaria vaccine acceptance rate

Our study showed a very high acceptance rate (98.8%) of the MV among HCWs in the Nanoro Health District. This very high acceptance of the vaccine may be linked to the participation of the CRUN to the phase 3 clinical trials for the two WHO-approved MVs [10, 24]. Indeed, the proximity of HCWs with the CRUN may have fostered a relationship of trust between them and the research institute, positively influencing their attitudes towards the vaccine uptake. Furthermore, more than half (56.8%) of the participants in our study worked at CRUN, making them likely to be familiar with the vaccine and, consequently, to have a positive attitude towards it. A COVID-19 vaccine acceptance study conducted in Indonesia has demonstrated that HCWs who were aware of the clinical trial of the COVID-19 vaccine in the country were more likely to accept it [22]. Conducting a MV acceptance study in other districts of the country where vaccine trials are not underway, and comparing the results with our findings, could provide more in-depth information on HCWs’ attitudes towards the MV in Nanoro and across the country.

The acceptance rate in our study also suggests a good understanding of the disease among HCWs in the Nanoro Health District where, as in the rest of the country, malaria is the leading cause of consultations and hospitalizations [3, 26]. Indeed, the Health Belief Model suggests that individuals' perceptions of disease severity and the perceived benefits of preventive measures are important factors influencing their response to health interventions [27]. HCWs in Nanoro, who are familiar with malaria, are likely to be aware of the harmful effects malaria can have on children's lives and would therefore be inclined to accept a vaccine as a tool of preventing the disease. Although our study was conducted at the district level and was limited to HCWs, it aligns with the findings of a 2025 national study conducted in Cameroon, which reported a MV acceptance rate of 91% [28]. Conducting a similar study at the national level in Burkina Faso would provide a broader understanding of public attitudes toward the vaccine across diverse population groups.

Reasons for malaria vaccine hesitancy

Reasons for malaria vaccine hesitancy

In our study, three HCWs (1.2%) were hesitant about vaccinating their own children and recommending the MV to caregivers. All of them mentioned insufficient information about the vaccine as the primary reason for their hesitancy. These findings are consistent with those of a systematic review conducted in 2018, which highlighted the lack of information as a major barrier to MV acceptance [12]. Similarly, another study in Dar es Salaam, Tanzania outlined the lack of information as the primary cause of vaccine hesitancy among caregivers [29].

The information gap reported in our study by hesitant respondents may be related to the inadequate training on the MV, especially for HCWs who are not directly involved in malaria prevention and clinical care. Indeed, none of the hesitant HCWs were clinicians and had received training on the vaccine since its introduction in the district. Vaccine-related training efforts in the district usually target health practitioners involved in EPI services. Improving training efforts for all HCWs, including non-clinicians, could significantly enhance vaccine knowledge and facilitate informed decision-making regarding malaria vaccination. Indeed, every HCW, regardless of their role, encounters patients who may require information about vaccination at some level. In our study, more than half of the participants (54.5%) reported encountering caregivers who were hesitant to vaccinate their children against malaria and almost 10% (n = 25) expressed lack of confidence in addressing hesitancy towards the MV. Therefore, it appears essential to equip all HCWs with the necessary tools through adequate training to help them to address concerns of hesitant families.

According to the respondents, misinformation, fear of new vaccines and concerns about the safety of vaccines were the primary factors contributing to caregivers’ hesitancy. Ten years prior to the rollout of the vaccine in the country, a qualitative study conducted in the districts of Kaya and Houndé had outlined concerns about side effects, rumours and misinformation within the communities as potential obstacles to the rollout of a future MV [16]. The “3 Cs (Complacency, Confidence and Convenience)” model of vaccine hesitancy identifies “confidence” as a key determinant of vaccine uptake. This includes trust in the safety and effectiveness of vaccines and the motivations of policymakers who recommend the vaccine [30]. Allowing the general population access to accurate information and reinforcing trust through targeted communication initiatives may therefore be essential strategies for improving MV acceptance. Furthermore, more research is needed to explore reasons for people’s hesitancy towards the MV at country level, especially considering that COVID-19 may have weakened trust in vaccine [17, 31].

Knowledge of the malaria vaccine

Knowledge of the malaria vaccine

Respondents showed sufficient awareness of the MV, in this study as nearly all HCWs (99.6%) knew that the vaccine was rolled out in the country and most of them (97.3%) were aware of its introduction in the Nanoro Health District. However, even though most of them agreed with its protection and prevention capacity, 22.6% HCWs were unaware of the type of vaccine that was rolled out and 22.2% did not know the vaccination schedule. This limited knowledge reinforces the issue of inadequate training of HCWs. Notably, over half of the participants (58.0%) stated that they did not receive any training before the introduction of the vaccine. The determinant role of knowledge and awareness about vaccines in shaping the attitudes of HCWs towards vaccines has been proven in several studies [32, 33]. HCWs are data-driven professionals who need convincing evidence about a health intervention before utilizing it or recommending it to their patients. A study conducted in the UK found that nurses with higher knowledge scores were more inclined to recommend the influenza vaccine to their patients and expressed greater willingness to do so in the future [34]. Therefore, it seems essential to strengthen district-level training strategies around the MV, especially since this is a new intervention. Training should not only cover the technical aspects of the vaccine but should also provide HCWs with effective communication skills, including tools to understand the risk/benefit of MV in the target population, enabling them to inform individuals and families in a caring, informed and confident manner when it comes to making decisions about vaccination.

Predictors of malaria vaccine acceptance

Predictors of malaria vaccine acceptance

In our study, bivariate analysis showed that HCWs who recommended the vaccine to caregivers, as well as those who had a positive attitude toward the vaccine, were more likely to vaccinate their own children. However, due to quasi-complete separation, these two variables could not be included in the multivariate model. This is a known statistical problem in logistic regression when one predictor perfectly predicts the outcome, resulting in unreliable or infinite estimates. We are therefore unable to draw conclusions about the independent effects of these variables after controlling for other factors. Nevertheless, their exclusion should not be interpreted as evidence of irrelevance, but rather as a limitation of the sample size and data distribution. A wider sample would help to address the quasi-complete separation issue.

Sociodemographic characteristics such as age, gender, having children, religion, education level, work experience, occupation, type and level of health facility did not predict willingness to accept the MV. The results of the logistic regression for these variables are characterized by wide confidence intervals, reflecting variability and possible limitations of the sample size. A larger sample size may have provided statistically significant results. However, our findings are similar to those reported by Andreas et al., who did not find any association between sociodemographic factors such as the age and gender of respondents and MV acceptance in a nationwide study of a larger sample [28]. In Nigeria, a cross-sectional study of hospital pharmacists concluded that age, gender, and education level did not affect MV acceptance [35].

Strengths and limitations of the study

Strengths and limitations of the study

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first MV acceptance study in Burkina Faso after MV introduction in the EPI. With a high response rate (75.6%; 257/340), it provides relevant insight into vaccine acceptance rate among HCWs in the Nanoro Health District. The questionnaire was self-administered and anonymous, minimizing the risk of socially desirable answers.

However, our study has several limitations that should be considered. First, while using a self-administered questionnaire reduces the risk of socially desirable answers, it also creates risks of self-reporting bias, hindering the accuracy of responses. Second, the questionnaire was shared via WhatsApp groups and mailing lists, presuming that all HCWs would have a smartphone or a computer to access it; however, some lack of smartphones and unstable internet connectivity could have created a selection bias. Finally, the findings should be interpreted with caution and are not generalisable to the national level. The study was conducted in a single health district (Nanoro), which is not representative of all health districts in Burkina Faso. In addition, Nanoro has been involved in MV clinical trials, which may have increased HCWs’ awareness and acceptance of the vaccine compared with other settings. Furthermore, this study focused exclusively on HCWs and did not assess the perceptions or acceptance of the MV among caregivers or the general population. Therefore, the findings cannot be extrapolated beyond the HCW population.

Recommendations and implications of the findings

Recommendations and implications of the findings

Although limited to a single district and a single point in time, the findings of this study provide useful insights that may inform the design of strategies to strengthen MV acceptance and uptake in similar settings in Burkina Faso.

First, health authorities at district and central levels should strengthen training (including periodic refresher training) on MV for all HCWs, whether directly involved in malaria care or the EPI or not. This seems particularly important in rural settings like Nanoro, where HCWs are expected to provide vaccine-related information among the community.

Second, the MOH and related stakeholders, such as the Permanent Secretary for Malaria Elimination (SP/Palu) and the EPI Direction, should strengthen and expand communication strategies on MV, while adapting approaches to local contexts and evidence from different regions of the country. Mass media, particularly radio and television, may be effective channels in rural areas, while social media platforms could play a larger role in urban settings. Communication should be culturally adapted, delivered in national languages, informed by community perspectives and supported by community engagement approaches, for addressing the knowledge, concerns and (scientific) literacy of the targeted communities. Messages should emphasize on the benefits of the MV, possible side effects and how to tackle them, and on the integration with the other malaria prevention measures and EPI vaccines. Both training and communication measures will require adequate financial and material support from the MOH.

Third, further research is needed to assess the acceptance of the MV at both local and national levels, and how it changes over time after the implementation of reinforcement strategies. Future studies should also explore barriers and facilitators to vaccination among the general population, other specific groups such as caregivers, and in other districts, to better inform national scale-up and policy decisions.

Conclusion

The development of MVs has been a major achievement, with key-leadership and key-contributions from sub-Sahara African research centres, including in Burkina Faso. The next step of integrating the MV in EPI requires attention for the non-medical determinants of vaccination, such as vaccine hesitancy and acceptance among HCWs and the community. Our findings provide insights that may help inform strategies to strengthen HCWs’ knowledge and acceptance of MVs as part of broader efforts to support their integration into the EPI in Burkina Faso and similar settings. Further research on MV acceptance among other population groups and at the national level is needed to guide implementation and policy decisions.
