Exploring cartilage development and disease models: applications of cartilage organoids
Authors: Chenhao Xu, Qiu Dong, Pengling Zhao, Yuting Lei, Vincent Kam Wai Wong, Manseng Tam, Zhenyan Li, Zhengang Zha, Jinzhu Pang, Huan-Tian Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cartilage is an avascular and aneural connective tissue vital for joint function, yet its limited intrinsic regenerative capacity poses significant challenges for treating injuries and degenerative diseases such as osteoarthritis (OA). Conventional models—including animal studies, cell lines, and primary chondrocyte cultures—exhibit considerable limitations in accurately replicating the native cartilage microenvironment, species-specific relevance, and differentiation potential. Recently, cartilage organoids have emerged as transformative tools that closely emulate the architecture, cellular heterogeneity, and extracellular matrix (ECM) properties of native cartilage, thereby offering a physiologically relevant platform for investigation. This review systematically examines the mechanisms governing chondrogenesis, including chondrocyte differentiation and matrix synthesis, as well as the construction of cartilage disease models. We focus on recent advances in cartilage organoid research, covering cultivation techniques, structural and functional characteristics, and diverse characterization methodologies. Furthermore, we highlight their applications in simulating pathological features of cartilage diseases, drug screening, toxicity testing, and gene therapy exploration. Current technological limitations—such as challenges in vascularization and mechanical strength—are discussed, together with future directions for developing multifunctional organoids and associated ethical and clinical considerations. This review aims to provide insights into the translational potential of cartilage organoids in regenerative medicine and disease modeling.

Background

Cartilage is a vital connective tissue in joints, airways, and the ear, providing structural support and flexibility essential for skeletal function, owing to its unique composition and biomechanical properties [1]. However, its avascular, alymphatic, and aneural nature severely limits intrinsic repair capacity following injury [2]. This inherent limitation presents a significant clinical challenge, compounded by rising cartilage injury incidence driven by an aging population and increasing obesity rates [3]. Notably, approximately 60% of patients with knee cartilage injuries present high-grade defects involving over half of the cartilage depth [4]. A comprehensive understanding of cartilage development, governed by complex intrinsic and extrinsic interactions, is therefore fundamental for diagnosing and treating pathologies like OA, where cartilage degradation leads to functional impairment. Research progress, however, is hindered by the tissue’s developmental complexity. Traditional animal models and two-dimensional (2D) cell cultures inadequately replicate the native cartilage microenvironment. Recent biotechnological advances offer promising alternatives through cartilage organoids—three-dimensional (3D) constructs that better simulate the architecture and function of natural cartilage tissue (Table 1) [5–8]. These organoids represent powerful tools for investigating cartilage biology and disease pathology, holding considerable potential to advance our understanding of developmental mechanisms and the pathogenesis of degenerative cartilage diseases. Comparison of different cartilage models

Organoids are complex 3D microtissues derived from stem or organ-specific progenitor cells under in vitro conditions, recapitulating key structural and functional aspects of their source organs. As transformative tools, they model human development, disease mechanisms, drug screening, and regenerative therapies, earning the designation of “Method of the Year” by the New England Journal of Medicine in 2019 [9]. In the musculoskeletal field, cartilage organoids—derived from bone marrow mesenchymal stromal cells (BM-MSCs), chondrocytes, or induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs)—mimic native tissue characteristics through physiological oxygen gradients, cell–matrix interactions, and ECM deposition [10]. Nonetheless, cartilage organoid research remains nascent compared to more established systems, confronting challenges in vascularization, mechanical strength, and functional maturation [11]. Recent innovations, such as silk fibroin hydrogels for ECM biomimicry and 3D bioprinting with stem cell-laden bioinks, have advanced organoid engineering. These models facilitate studies on disease mechanisms like OA, high-throughput drug screening, and personalized regenerative strategies, providing new platforms to investigate chondrogenic signaling pathways (e.g., BMP, Wnt) and enhance cartilage repair therapies [12, 13].

In this review, we define a cartilage organoid as a 3D microtissue derived from stem or progenitor cells (e.g., iPSCs, BM-MSCs) that develops through intrinsic self-organization and differentiation programs [5, 7, 14]. By recapitulating key aspects of developmental chondrogenesis, these organoids exhibit native-like hierarchical architecture, cellular heterogeneity, and a functional extracellular matrix (ECM). Unlike simple physical aggregates (spheroids) or exogenous scaffold-driven constructs, true cartilage organoids maintain phenotypic stability without spontaneous hypertrophy, while displaying the metabolic and mechanical responsiveness characteristic of native hyaline cartilage. Consequently, this human-mimetic platform holds significant promise for studying disease [6, 8, 15]. Conceptually, this strict definition distinguishes pure cartilage organoids from osteochondral models and organ-on-chip platforms, recognizing the latter systems as advanced integrative applications rather than standalone organoids (Table 2) [7, 8, 16–19]. This review systematically introduces the history, development and recent progress of cartilage organoids, as well as explores state-of-the-art methodologies and translational applications of cartilage organoids, offering fresh perspectives on their roles in regenerative medicine and disease modeling.

Development of cartilage organoids

Origin and development of organoids

Organoids are 3D cellular constructs that recapitulate the structural and functional features of native organs [20]. The field’s foundation spans over a century: H.V. Wilson’s 1907 observation that dissociated sponge cells could reaggregate and self-organize [21] was followed by studies that gradually underscored the pivotal role of cell–matrix interactions by the 1980s [22]. The isolation of pluripotent stem cells (PSCs) in 1981 [23] and the advent of induced pluripotent stem cell (iPSC) technology in 2006 [24] provided the essential cellular source for modern organoid construction. A seminal breakthrough occurred in 2009, when single Lgr5 + intestinal stem cells yielded crypt–villus structures in vitro [25], launching a “golden era” that rapidly produced diverse organoid models—including hepatic, cerebral, skeletal, and skin tissues [26]. Collectively, these milestones established the conceptual and technical groundwork for cartilage organoid research.

Rise of skeletal organoids

Although the field of bone and cartilage organoids started later than other types of organoids, it has achieved rapid and remarkable milestone results. In 2017, early foundational research by Iodachescu A et al. demonstrated the feasibility of constructing bone-like models using femoral periosteal cells within a fibrin gel; these models were capable of simulating the chemical composition and structure of mature bone over time [27]. Subsequent studies, continuing through 2021, successfully generated skeletal organoids from various cell sources, including BM-MSCs, iPSCs, and human periosteum-derived cells (hPDCs).

The year 2023 marked a period of significant breakthroughs. A notable study by Ouyang et al. reported the development of large-scale, scaffold-free cartilage tissue organoids with significant regenerative potential, providing high-quality transplantable tissue for joint repair [28]. Concurrently, Yang et al. employed another innovative approach to generate self-assembled osteochondral tissue organoids featuring integrated cartilage and bone layers, which exhibited excellent mechanical properties and biocompatibility [19]. By 2024, the field had adopted advanced biofabrication techniques. Wang et al. utilized composite bioinks and 3D bioprinting to construct large-scale, self-mineralizing bone tissue units with favorable viability and differentiation potential.

Recent progression in cartilage organoids research

In recent years, substantial advancements have been achieved in the field of cartilage organoid research, encompassing various application domains. Notably, several key application fields can be summarized.

Within the domain of biomimetic tissue construction and implant development, cartilage organoids have been employed as “living building blocks” with distinct functionalities to fabricate biomimetic implants characterized by region-specific structures. While pure cartilage organoids are invaluable for studying isolated chondrogenesis, regenerating the entire joint interface necessitates an advancement toward osteochondral models. Hall et al. successfully engineered composite tissues that exhibit both osseous and cartilaginous properties in vivo by integrating human-derived cartilage organoids at different developmental stages into multilayered configurations [18]. Similarly, Yang et al. utilized customized microgel materials to direct the self-assembly of MSCs into organoids with biphasic cartilage-bone structures in vivo, thereby achieving spatial regulation of cartilage and bone regeneration. Their approach was further substantiated by demonstrating repair efficacy in large animal models [19].

In the context of disease modeling and mechanistic research, cartilage organoids serve as a crucial platform for investigating the pathogenesis of conditions such as OA. Cartilage organoids are capable of recapitulating OA pathophysiology with high fidelity. By exposing MSCs to inflammatory cues during chondrogenesis, researchers have engineered organoids that exhibit hallmark OA features, including chondrocyte hypertrophy, matrix mineralization, and mechanical stiffening [29]. Such models enable high-throughput screening of therapeutics. This is exemplified by devices like HiT-MACE, which rapidly evaluates organoid responses to physiological and supra-physiological loads, identifying, for instance, TGF-β1 pathway activation under physiological conditions [30]. Additionally, organoids derived from OA patient chondrocytes and cultured in viscoelastic hydrogels provide a platform to study disease-specific matrix remodeling and test candidate drugs [31]. van Hoolwerff et al. employed a patient-specific iPSC-derived cartilage organoid model and discovered that a mutation in the FN1 gene results in reduced chondrogenic capacity and contributes to a catabolic state by impairing the binding affinity of fibronectin to type II collagen (Col II), thereby elucidating a key molecular pathway of the disease [32]. Similarly, De Giuseppe et al. utilized a 3D organoid co-culture model to elucidate the pivotal role of integrin α11β1 in the invasion of cartilage by arthritic fibroblast-like synoviocytes, offering evidence for its potential as a therapeutic target [33]. Collectively, these studies demonstrate a common principle: cartilage organoids provide a highly tractable platform to elucidate complex molecular crosstalk (e.g., integrin and FN1 pathways) and evaluate targeted interventions that are difficult to accurately capture in traditional 2D cultures or animal models.

In the realm of drug screening and the identification of therapeutic targets, high-throughput screening models utilizing organoids have demonstrated significant potential. Wei et al. employed hPSCs with integrated fluorescent reporter genes to develop an organoid system capable of real-time monitoring of chondrogenesis and hypertrophy processes. By screening over 2000 drugs, they identified that targeting the α2-adrenergic receptor/SLPI signaling axis can effectively enhance hyaline cartilage regeneration while inhibiting hypertrophy. This discovery offers a novel therapeutic strategy for cartilage repair [34].

In the field of biomaterials and microenvironment regulation, innovative hydrogel materials have been developed to optimize the culture and functionality of organoids. Therapeutically, cartilage organoids show considerable promise in regenerative medicine. hPSC-derived cartilaginous organoids have been shown to bridge critical-size bone defects in mice, while self-assembling MSC organoids can regenerate hyaline-like cartilage in vivo without requiring extrinsic factors [35, 36]. Emerging strategies, such as employing silk fibroin hydrogels loaded with organoid precursors, further enhance chondrogenesis and cartilage repair, underscoring the translational potential of these systems [37]. Shen et al. introduced silk fibroin-DNA-based hydrogel microspheres as scaffolds for cartilage organoid precursors, which markedly enhanced the proliferation, adhesion, and chondrogenic differentiation of MSCs, thereby significantly improving cartilage repair in vivo [37]. Similarly, Yang et al. engineered a hyperdynamic hydrogel capable of adapting to fluctuations in cell volume. This hydrogel induced metabolic reprogramming and histone lactoacylation by mimicking the hypoxic microenvironment characteristic of developmental processes, thereby substantially augmenting the generation capacity and reparative efficacy of cartilage organoids [38].

Mechanisms of chondrogenesis

Introduction to chondrogenic differentiation

Chondrogenesis is the first and fundamental process of cartilage organoid construction, by which cartilage is formed from MSCs, crucial for embryonic skeletal development and adult cartilage homeostasis. This multi-step differentiation, from initial mesenchymal condensation to mature chondrocyte formation, is orchestrated by a complex and highly coordinated network of signaling pathways, cytokines, and transcription factors [41]. The precise regulation of these molecular cues ensures proper skeletal formation, while their dysregulation can lead to congenital disorders like campomelic dysplasia or degenerative diseases such as OA. Key players in this process include the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) superfamily, the SOX trio of transcription factors, and a suite of other modulating pathways including Hedgehog, Wnt, FGF, and Hippo signaling, all of which integrate to direct the chondrogenic fate of MSCs (Fig. 2) [42].Fig. 2Overview of transcription factors and growth factors in the regulation of chondrogenesis and cartilage homeostasis

The central role of the TGF-β superfamily

The TGF-β superfamily, encompassing TGF-βs and bone morphogenetic proteins (BMPs), acts as a primary conductor of chondrogenesis. TGF-β signaling plays a stage-specific role, stabilizing the master transcription factor SOX9 through both canonical Smad and non-canonical p38 MAPK pathways, thereby promoting the expression of essential cartilage matrix genes like type II collagen (Col2a1) and aggrecan (Acan). It is vital for maintaining articular cartilage homeostasis postnatally, and its dysregulation is a feature of OA. In contrast, BMP signaling is a potent and consistent inducer of chondrogenesis from the earliest stages. BMP2, for instance, upregulates Sox9 expression by promoting chromatin remodeling at its promoter and by alleviating epigenetic repression, such as through the microRNA Let-7a which targets the repressor Chd4. Genetic studies, including those on mice with disrupted BMP signaling components like TAK1, confirm the indispensable role of BMPs, as their loss leads to severe chondrodysplasia with markedly reduced SOX9 levels, underscoring their non-redundant function in driving cartilage formation [43].

Key transcription factors: the SOX trio

The execution of the chondrogenic program is fundamentally dependent on the SOX family of transcription factors, with SOX9, SOX5, and SOX6 forming a critical coregulatory triad. SOX9 is the master regulator, indispensable for committing MSCs to the chondrogenic lineage. It binds to enhancer regions of cartilage-specific genes, often in collaboration with its partners. While its expression declines in the hypertrophic zone, recent evidence confirms its necessity in the early phases of hypertrophy, where it interacts with AP-1 proteins to direct the process. The vital importance of SOX9 is highlighted by human haploinsufficiency, which causes campomelic dysplasia, a severe skeletal malformation syndrome. SOX5 and SOX6, while lacking transactivation domains themselves, form dimers that bind DNA adjacent to SOX9, dramatically enhancing its transcriptional efficiency. The profound skeletal defects in Sox5/Sox6double-knockout mice, which completely lack cartilage despite normal SOX9 expression, demonstrate their essential and cooperative role with SOX9 in activating the full chondrogenic gene program [44].

Ancillary signaling pathways: fine-tuning chondrogenesis

Beyond the core TGF-β/BMP and SOX trio axis, several other signaling pathways provide critical fine-tuning and contextual regulation for chondrogenesis. The Hedgehog pathway, particularly Sonic Hedgehog (Shh), is pivotal for chondrogenic patterning, inducing SOX9 expression and guiding the formation of specific cartilage structures like the trachea [45]. Furthermore, Indian Hedgehog (Ihh) from prehypertrophic chondrocytes forms a critical feedback loop with parathyroid hormone-related protein (PTHrP) to precisely control the timing of chondrocyte hypertrophy, with SOX9 and GLI transcription factors often cooperating on target genes [46]. Conversely, canonical Wnt/β-catenin signaling generally antagonizes chondrogenesis; it can recruit DNA methyltransferases to the Sox9 promoter, leading to its epigenetic silencing, though this can be counteracted by fibroblast growth factor (FGF) signaling [47]. FGF signaling itself, primarily through FGFR3, acts as a negative regulator of chondrocyte proliferation, and its balanced interaction with BMP signaling is crucial for normal growth plate dynamics [48]. Finally, the Hippo pathway, which senses mechanical cues and cell density, influences chondrogenesis through its effectors YAP/TAZ, which repress Sox9 transcription when hyperactivated, thereby linking the physical microenvironment to the transcriptional control of cartilage development [49].

Integrated regulatory layers: from epigenetics to non-coding RNAs

The precise control of chondrogenesis is achieved through the integration of multiple regulatory layers that operate on the SOX9-centered network. At the epigenetic level, the chromatin state of the Sox9 locus is dynamically regulated by enzymes such as the histone demethylase KDM4B, which activates transcription, and repressors like Chd4 [50]. Post-translationally, SOX9 protein activity is modulated by phosphorylation (e.g., by PKA), which enhances its nuclear import and transactivation potential, as well as by methylation, acetylation, and ubiquitination, which fine-tune its stability, interactions, and transcriptional output. Superimposed on this is a sophisticated network of non-coding RNAs. MicroRNAs (miRNAs) like miR-145 and miR-495 directly target SOX9 mRNA to inhibit chondrogenesis, while SOX9 itself can promote the expression of chondroprotective miRNAs like miR-140, creating stabilizing feedback loops [51]. Furthermore, long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) such as ROCR function as upstream regulators that promote SOX9 expression and are essential for successful differentiation [52]. In conclusion, the chondrogenic differentiation of MSCs is not governed by a linear pathway but by a complex, interconnected regulatory circuit. This circuit integrates master transcription factors like the SOX trio with upstream signals from the TGF-β superfamily and ancillary pathways, all fine-tuned by epigenetic mechanisms, PTMs, and non-coding RNAs. A holistic understanding of this intricate network is paramount for advancing skeletal biology and developing effective strategies for cartilage regeneration.

Construction of cartilage organoids

Progenitor cells for cartilage organoids construction

The construction of cartilage organoids begins with the critical step of selecting appropriate chondroprogenitor cells, which determines the developmental potential, phenotypic stability, and eventual functionality of the resulting tissue [53]. Diverse stem cell sources are utilized, including MSCs, iPSCs, embryonic stem cells (ESCs), and neural crest stem cells (NCSCs). Each cell type offers distinct advantages: MSCs, particularly those derived from bone marrow (BM-MSCs) or adipose tissue (ADMSCs), are widely used due to their relative accessibility, robust proliferative capacity, and well-established chondrogenic differentiation protocols [54]. For instance, Cho et al. utilized ADMSCs to form self-assembling organoid-tissue modules (organoid-TMs) that demonstrated significant cartilage regeneration in animal models without the need for exogenous scaffolds [55]. Similarly, Sun et al. employed synovial MSCs organoids to target cellular senescence and promote cartilage repair, highlighting the utility of adult stem cells in generating functional cartilage units [56].

iPSCs represent a particularly versatile and ethically non-contentious source, capable of unlimited expansion and differentiation into any cell type, including chondrocytes [57]. Their potential for generating patient-specific tissues makes them highly attractive for personalized medicine and disease modeling. Lamandé et al. detailed a method directing iPSC-derived sclerotome to chondroprogenitors in 3D pellet culture, which could further differentiate into articular chondrocytes or hypertrophic chondrocytes that transition to osteoblasts, effectively modeling endochondral bone formation. This capacity to recapitulate developmental pathways is crucial for creating organoids that mirror the complexity of native skeletal tissues [58]. Abe et al. provided a groundbreaking demonstration of the therapeutic potential of iPSC-derived cartilage organoids, showing that allogeneic transplants could survive, integrate, and remodel within primate knee chondral defects for at least 4months without eliciting an immune reaction. Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis in their study further revealed that the transplanted organoids acquired expression of PRG4, a critical lubrication protein for joint function, underscoring their capacity to emulate in vivo cartilage maturation processes [15].

Among the various progenitor populations, LGR5-expressing embryonic joint chondroprogenitors are considered ideal due to their inherent potential to differentiate into functional chondrocytes and synthesize a cartilage-specific ECM rich in Col II and ACAN [7]. To enhance chondrogenic commitment, cells are often preconditioned with key morphogens (e.g., TGF-β3, BMP-2) that are central to the chondrogenic regulatory network (see Section 3). The choice of cell source is also influenced by the intended application. For modeling genetic skeletal disorders, iPSCs derived from patients offer an unparalleled platform, as demonstrated by Lamandé et al., who identified gene expression signatures at key developmental stages to model cartilage and bone disorders [58]. For regenerative applications, the scalability and robustness of the cell source are paramount. Crispim et al. developed a method for the rapid mass production of bovine cartilage organoids via a new suspension expansion protocol, leading to massive cell proliferation and high viability, which is essential for scaling up tissue engineering approaches [14]. Furthermore, the concept of using developmentally engineered building blocks is exemplified by Hall et al., who used distinct cartilaginous organoids derived from human periosteum-derived cells or iPSCs as living building blocks to create patterned osteochondral implants with zone-specific functionality upon in vivo implantation [18]. This approach highlights the importance of selecting and pre-programming seed cells to achieve complex tissue architectures.

Scaffolds for cartilage organoids construction

The physical and biochemical microenvironment for cartilage organoids is provided by scaffolds, which are pivotal for supporting their 3D structure, guiding cell behavior, and facilitating the deposition and organization of a functional ECM. Technological advancements in scaffold design and biofabrication have greatly enhanced the utility and physiological relevance of organoids [59]. Hydrogels are the predominant scaffold material due to their high water content, biocompatibility, and ECM-mimetic properties, which allow for efficient nutrient diffusion and cell–matrix interactions. Gelatin methacryloyl (GelMA) is widely used for its tunable mechanical stiffness, cell-adhesive motifs, and capacity for photopolymerization, allowing precise 3D shaping and patterning. Chen et al. utilized GelMA complexes to support single BM-MSC-derived cartilage organoids, leveraging its properties to study gradient heterogeneous osteochondral regeneration guided by the native vascular microenvironment [60].

Similarly, silk fibroin (SF)-based hydrogels offer superior biocompatibility, biodegradability, and structural stability, effectively mimicking the fibrous network of native cartilage ECM [61]. Zhou et al. pioneered the development of dual-network DNA-silk fibroin (SF) hydrogels with controllable surface rigidity. They discovered that the supramolecular networks formed through DNA base-pairing induced the development of β-sheet structures in SF, and the resulting hydrogels’ surface rigidity could be tuned by varying DNA content [62]. Intriguingly, hydrogels with moderate surface rigidity were most effective in facilitating the migration of BM-MSCs and their chondrogenic differentiation, primarily by upregulating the Wnt and TGF-β signaling pathways. Building on this, Shen et al. further advanced this platform by creating RGD-modified SF-DNA hydrogel microspheres (RSD-MS) via a microfluidic system. These microspheres served as an ideal 3D scaffold for constructing cartilage organoid precursors (COPs), enhancing BM-MSC proliferation, adhesion, and chondrogenesis through integrin-mediated adhesion pathways and glycosaminoglycan biosynthesis, and significantly boosted cartilage repair in vivo [63].

Innovations in material science have introduced bioactive components to enhance chondrogenesis and suppress undesirable differentiation pathways. As is mentioned earlier, Crispim et al. incorporated milk basic protein (MBP) into SF hydrogels, which was shown to promote chondrocyte proliferation and suppress hypertrophic differentiation, as evidenced by upregulated SOX9 and COL2A1 expression alongside reduced RUNX2 and COL10A1 levels. The mechanical and viscoelastic properties of scaffolds are also critically important [14]. While elastic alginate hydrogels constrained the growth and fusion of cartilage organoids, viscoelastic hydrogels allowed for their fusion into a homogeneous neocartilage tissue rich in Col II and glycosaminoglycans. This highlights how scaffold mechanics can dictate tissue assembly and maturation. Gao et al. developed a novel HAMA-SBMA hydrogel with adhesive, pro-chondrogenic, and anti-inflammatory properties, which, when combined with cartilage organoids (CCO), exhibited excellent repair efficacy in both rat and sheep models of cartilage defects by activating Frizzled-related protein (Frzb) and enhancing integrin ɑ5β1 expression [64]. A critical synthesis of these advancements highlights a core principle in scaffold design: fine-tuning the mechanical and biochemical properties of hydrogels—such as matching dynamic viscoelasticity and moderate surface rigidity—is a fundamental determinant for activating pro-chondrogenic signaling pathways (e.g., Wnt, TGF-β), preventing hypertrophic drift, and maintaining a stable hyaline phenotype.

Beyond hydrogels, other sophisticated scaffold platforms are employed. Decellularized cartilage matrix (DCM) or cartilage-derived matrix (CDM) scaffolds represent an advanced platform that retains native ECM composition and bioactivity [65]. Rowland et al. engineered anatomically shaped osteochondral constructs from CDM and further enhanced them by regulating remodeling via scaffold-mediated lentiviral delivery of an inducible interleukin-1 receptor antagonist (IL-1Ra) transgene. This protected the constructs from inflammation-mediated degradation and supported robust bone and cartilage tissue formation even in the presence of IL-1, showcasing the potential of “smart” scaffolds that actively modulate the microenvironment [66]. Moving beyond pure cartilage constructs, sophisticated scaffolds are also instrumental in engineering multi-tissue osteochondral models. Furthermore, automated 3D bioassembly systems combine chondrogenic microtissues with thermoplastic polymer scaffolds. Hall et al. combined melt electrowritten (MEW) polycaprolactone meshes with cartilaginous microspheroids to create biohybrid sheets. The MEW meshes were tailored with pores specifically sized to capture the microspheroids, and upon implantation, these sheets mineralized and formed bone and bone marrow, demonstrating the power of integrating microfabrication techniques with living cellular aggregates [18]. These sophisticated scaffolds, whether based on hydrogels, decellularized matrices, or synthetic polymers, enable precise control over the organoid microenvironment, facilitating investigations into critical processes like mechanotransduction and providing a foundation for manufacturing complex tissue implants.

Influencing factors of cartilage organoids construction

The successful construction, maturation, and eventual functionality of cartilage organoids are influenced by a multitude of interdependent factors, encompassing advanced biofabrication technologies, the composition of the cellular and biomaterial building blocks, and meticulously controlled cultivation conditions that aim to recapitulate the native cartilage niche [67]. Biofabrication technologies, such as 3D bioprinting and automated bioassembly, have revolutionized the field by enabling precise spatial control over cell distribution and scaffold architecture, which is essential for creating complex, heterogeneous tissues like osteochondral units [68]. Extrusion-based 3D bioprinting layers cell-laden hydrogels (e.g., GelMA mixed with MSCs) to form complex geometries. Recent advancements explore the use of novel bioinks that enhance functionality. Sun et al. developed a simple and efficient strategy for preparing cell-spheroid-based bioink (Sph-bioink) using a poly(N-isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAAm)-based porous hydrogel. This “all-in-one” platform served both as a tool for high-yield fabrication of ADMSCs spheroids and as a thermo-responsive bioprinting medium, enabling the construction of custom-shaped mature cartilage [56]. Dey et al. formulated bioinspired, cell-specific bioinks to recreate intricate morphogen gradients in a 3D bioprinted human osteochondral microphysiological system (MPS), which recapitulated native-like tissue organization and cartilage-bone crosstalk under physiomimetic conditions [69].

Automated systems further optimize this process by inserting pre-differentiated chondrogenic microtissues into scaffolds with high placement efficiency. Burdis et al. employed a modular biofabrication strategy, spatially localizing phenotypically distinct cartilage microtissues within an instructive 3D printed polymer framework [70]. They found that combining less cellular microtissues resulted in superior fusion and more hyaline-like cartilage, and that temporally exposing microtissues to TGF-β accelerated their volumetric growth and fusion capacity. This approach successfully engineered osteochondral grafts that supported defect stabilization and cartilage restoration in a caprine model. The cultivation conditions are equally critical and are meticulously tailored to mimic the dynamic native cartilage environment. Engineering a hypoxic environment (1–5% O₂) is a key strategy, as it induces a glycolytic metabolism in chondroprogenitors, which promotes chondrogenesis [71]. Yang et al. provided a mechanistic insight into this process by engineering a cell-adaptable supramolecular hydrogel that facilitated the formation of large multicellular cartilaginous organoids. The adapted matrix fostered a hypoxic environment, inducing a metabolic shift towards glycolysis and increasing lactate accumulation. This, in turn, enhanced histone lysine lactylation (specifically on H3K18), which promoted chondrogenesis by improving the accessibility of chondrogenic genes. Inhibition of histone lactylation disrupted these processes, confirming the critical link between matrix biophysics, metabolism, and epigenetic regulation in cartilage development [38].

Dynamic culture systems, including bioreactors that apply cyclic mechanical loading, enhance ECM maturation by activating mechanotransduction pathways [72]. For instance, physiological loads are known to upregulate TGF-β1 signaling, promoting anabolic activity. Damerau et al. developed a microphysiological system (MPS) to precisely study the impact of shear stress on cartilage health. They demonstrated that physiological shear forces could reduce TNF-α-mediated cartilage damage in a 3D chondrogenic model, while also validating the restorative effects of a JAK inhibitor under such conditions, highlighting the importance of incorporating physiologically relevant mechanical cues in organoid culture [73]. Co-culture models have also been widely employed to simulate critical interactions among different joint tissues. For instance, Ertl et al. developed an organ-on-a-chip-based three-dimensional tissue co-culture model, in which synovial organoids and cartilage organoids were co-cultured to mimic their bidirectional signaling communication. This model provides deeper insights into tissue-level interaction mechanisms in arthritis disease models [74].

Beyond hypoxia and biochemical cues, the mechanical microenvironment is a pivotal regulator of cartilage organoid development, governing chondrogenic commitment, matrix maturation, and phenotypic maintenance. Mechanical forces act as epigenetic rheostats that profoundly shape organoid fate [75]. For instance, dynamic compression with physiological magnitudes (e.g., 0.5–10% strain) activates the TGF-β and Wnt/β-catenin cascades, enhancing glycosaminoglycan deposition and Type II collagen network organization, which mirrors the anabolic response of native articular cartilage [76]. Conversely, hyperphysiological or static loads trigger a catabolic shift characterized by elevated MMP-13 activity and hypertrophic markers such as COL10A1, driving a degenerative, osteoarthritis-like phenotype within the organoid [77]. At the molecular level, the ion channel Piezo1 functions as a critical mechanosensor, where transient activation promotes chondrocyte proliferation, yet its chronic overstimulation induces ferroptosis and senescence via calcium influx and miR-155-5p expression, compromising organoid viability [78]. Similarly, the Hippo-YAP/TAZ axis serves as a central transduction hub: nuclear translocation of YAP/TAZ under appropriate matrix stiffness drives Sox9 expression and chondrogenesis, whereas their cytoplasmic retention under stiff or aging-mimetic matrices inhibits differentiation [79, 80]. The viscoelastic properties of the scaffold further refine this mechanical signaling, with fast-relaxing hydrogels better dissipating applied stress to reduce nuclear YAP overload, thereby maintaining a stable hyaline phenotype and preventing hypertrophic drift [81]. Therefore, recapitulating the physiologically dynamic, zone-specific mechanical landscape is not merely supportive but is a fundamental instructional parameter for engineering functional and phenotypically stable cartilage organoids.

Together, these findings underscore a unifying concept in organoid cultivation: merely providing a 3D scaffold is insufficient; explicitly recapitulating the native physicochemical niche—specifically through hypoxic metabolic reprogramming and physiological mechanical loading—is fundamentally required for the robust maturation and functional maintenance of cartilage organoids.

Despite these advances, scaling up production presents significant challenges, including ensuring batch-to-batch consistency, maintaining viability in large constructs, and optimizing the integration of bioactive components [61]. Future directions will likely combine multi-omics analyses to tailor culture conditions precisely and integrate four-dimensional (4D) bioprinting—3D printing with time-dependent material properties—to dynamically mimic the evolving process of cartilage development and maturation, thereby creating even more sophisticated and functionally robust cartilage organoids [82].

Structural and functional characteristics of cartilage organoids

The physiological relevance of cartilage organoids is unequivocally demonstrated by their structural and functional attributes, which can be rigorously characterized using a suite of advanced techniques [83]. Structurally, they comprise chondrocytes embedded within a 3D ECM that is rich in key components such as Col II, aggrecan, and glycosaminoglycans (GAGs), which confer the tissue’s characteristic tensile strength and compressive resistance. The collagen fibers within organoids are organized hierarchically, similar to native tissue, a feature that can be visualized using advanced imaging techniques like second harmonic generation (SHG) microscopy [84]. Wu et al. engineered a multi-gradient cartilage organoid by controlling the orientation of collagen fibers and establishing a concentration gradient of chondroitin sulfate. This resulted in an organoid with a surface layer containing parallel-aligned fibers and flattened cells that specifically secreted Col II and superficial zone protein (SZP), crucial for lubrication and durability, thereby recapitulating the zonal organization of articular cartilage [85].

The pericellular matrix (PCM) is also faithfully recapitulated in advanced organoid models, with type VI collagen and hyaluronic acid forming a protective niche that regulates mechanotransduction and chondrocyte metabolism. Crispim et al. confirmed that their bovine cartilage organoids were composed of Col II and VI, GAGs, and Sox9-positive cells, embedded in both pericellular and interterritorial matrices, similarly to native hyaline cartilage [14]. Functionally, these organoids replicate critical biological processes. They actively secrete cartilage-specific ECM and exhibit metabolic activity comparable to in vivo chondrocytes. For example, organoids derived from human articular chondroprogenitor cells (hACPCs) have been shown to produce a uniform ECM with GAG levels and collagen organization resembling fetal cartilage. They also respond dynamically to mechanical stimuli: physiological cyclic loading upregulates anabolic genes via pathways like TGF-β, while supra-physiological stress induces catabolic responses, mirroring the homeostatic balance and injury responses of native tissue [86].

A significant utility of organoids is their ability to recapitulate disease-relevant phenotypes, providing powerful platforms for pathophysiological studies and drug discovery. When exposed to inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β or TNF-α, they develop OA-like characteristics, including increased MMP-13 activity, collagen degradation, and GAG loss [87]. Dönges et al. engineered human osteoarthritic cartilage organoids by imposing specific inflammatory challenges during chondrogenesis. These organoids recapitulated key OA traits such as chondrocyte hypertrophy, matrix mineralization, catabolism, and mechanical stiffening. Using this model, they identified that IL-1Ra treatment reduced the production of the transcription factor C/EBPβ, and inhibition of C/EBPβ-activating kinases could revert the degradative processes [29]. Furthermore, organoids derived directly from OA patient chondrocytes retain disease-specific traits, such as altered matrix turnover and elevated expression of hypertrophic markers like COL10A1 [88]. Xie et al. utilized cartilage organoids to demonstrate that anticitrullinated SR-A peptide antibody (anti-CSP), a novel biomarker identified for rheumatoid arthritis (RA), was capable of provoking inflammation in these organoids and exacerbating disease progression in experimental arthritis, thereby validating its pathogenic role [89].

The comprehensive characterization of these structural and functional attributes is achieved through a suite of advanced analytical techniques. Imaging methods like compositional MRI (e.g., T2 mapping, dGEMRIC) and photon-counting detector CT (PCD-CT) are used to non-invasively quantify biochemical changes in ECM composition. At the molecular level, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) reveals heterogeneous cell populations and differentiation trajectories within organoids [90].

Collectively, these sophisticated characterization methods validate that cartilage organoids are robust, physiologically relevant models that faithfully emulate native cartilage biology, providing an Applications of cartilage organoids in disease research

Simulation of pathological features

Building upon their ability to recapitulate native cartilage architecture and function (Section 4.4), cartilage organoids have proven exceptionally valuable for modeling disease pathophysiology, with osteoarthritis (OA) being a primary focus. The utility of these models extends beyond simulating isolated chondropathy; they enable the deconstruction of complex pathological processes, such as the dysregulation of key signaling pathways (e.g., Wnt/β-catenin, TGF-β) that drive aberrant chondrocyte differentiation and hypertrophy [98].

A significant advancement is the development of complex “mini-joint” or co-culture systems. By integrating cartilage organoids with other joint-relevant cell types (e.g., fibroblast-like synoviocytes, subchondral bone cells), these models more faithfully mimic the dynamic multicellular crosstalk that underpins joint disease progression in vivo [74]. The platform’s versatility allows for the precise manipulation of the microenvironment—through controlled application of inflammatory cytokines, mechanical overload, or altered biochemical gradients—to dissect the individual and synergistic contributions of these insults to cartilage breakdown.

Drug screening and toxicity testing

The physiological relevance of cartilage organoids positions them as superior platforms for high-throughput drug screening and toxicity assessment, addressing key limitations of conventional 2D cultures and animal models. A significant advantage is their ability to model complex chondrocyte-ECM interactions, which are critical for determining drug response [99]. This enables the discrimination of compounds that not only stimulate cartilage regeneration but also minimize off-target effects such as chondrocyte hypertrophy and fibrocartilage formation—common pitfalls in current repair strategies [100]. For instance, the integration of fluorescent reporters into organoids allows for real-time monitoring of chondrogenesis and hypertrophy during compound screening [101].

Technologically, the field is advancing towards automation and miniaturization. Automated bioreactors and microfluidic systems now enable the parallel culture and analysis of numerous organoids, permitting the rapid interrogation of extensive compound libraries. Furthermore, patient-derived organoids enable personalized drug screening, assessing how individual genetic backgrounds influence drug response and potential toxicity [102]. This scalability directly translates to an accelerated drug discovery pipeline while adhering to the “3Rs” principles (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement) by diminishing the reliance on animal testing [103]. Overall, cartilage organoids are transitioning from a basic research tool to an integral component of a more predictive and ethical preclinical framework for cartilage disorder therapeutics.

Exploration of gene therapy strategies

The exploration of gene therapy strategies using cartilage organoids is a burgeoning area with significant potential for treating cartilage-related diseases. Gene therapy aims to address underlying genetic causes, offering a targeted approach compared to conventional symptomatic treatments [7]. Cartilage organoids provide an ideal platform for testing gene editing techniques, most notably CRISPR/Cas9, to correct disease-associated mutations or enhance the expression of protective genes [53]. Recent studies demonstrate the feasibility of using gene therapy in cartilage organoids to induce chondrogenesis and inhibit pathological processes like inflammation and matrix degeneration [104]. Delivering specific genes or gene-editing tools directly into organoids allows researchers to assess their effects on cartilage formation and maintenance in a controlled, human-relevant system. This approach not only evaluates the efficacy of gene therapy strategies but also provides valuable insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying cartilage development and disease [105]. Moreover, patient-derived cartilage organoids facilitate the investigation of personalized treatment strategies. Tailoring gene therapy to an individual’s genetic makeup holds promise for optimizing therapeutic outcomes and minimizing off-target effects or adverse reactions [106]. This personalized approach is particularly crucial for complex diseases like OA, where multiple genetic and environmental factors contribute to disease progression. The integration of gene therapy with cartilage organoid models represents a promising frontier in regenerative medicine, potentially revolutionizing treatment paradigms and improving patient outcomes for a range of cartilage-related disorders [107]. Cartilage organoid-on-chip

The integration of cartilage organoids with organ-on-a-chip (OoC) technology has given rise to the cartilage organoid-on-chip (COoC) platform. This innovative approach leverages microfluidic systems to exert precise spatiotemporal control over the organoid microenvironment, thereby addressing key limitations of traditional static 3D cultures. The COoC platform represents a significant advancement, enabling the emulation of dynamic physiological and pathological processes within joint tissues that are impossible to capture in conventional dish-based systems.

The microfluidic advantage in cartilage modelling

The core value of the COoC platform lies in its ability to use microfluidic technology to accurately replicate the dynamic microenvironment of articular cartilage. A primary application is the precise application of mechanical stimulation. For instance, one established model utilizes a deformable polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) membrane. By pressurizing the underlying chamber, the membrane exerts vertical compression on an upper chamber containing chondrocytes embedded in a hydrogel matrix. This system has demonstrated that hyperphysiological compression can directly induce an OA phenotype in the organoids, characterized by the upregulation of catabolic markers like MMP-13 and the downregulation of anabolic genes [77]. Beyond compression, microfluidic systems can also generate fluid shear stress, another critical mechanical cue. Research using a dedicated microphysiological system (MPS) has shown that physiological levels of shear stress can even mitigate cytokine-induced cartilage damage, highlighting the role of mechanical forces in maintaining tissue homeostasis [108].

Furthermore, microfluidic chips are exceptionally suited for modeling the critical cellular crosstalk between cartilage and adjacent tissues within a joint. Unlike simple co-cultures, chip systems can spatially pattern different cell types—such as chondrocytes, fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS), and even sensory neurons—within interconnected microchambers, allowing for controlled, soluble factor-mediated communication. This design enables the study of how synovial inflammation drives cartilage degradation in RA or how sensory nerve innervation influences chondrocyte metabolism [109]. The platform’s capacity for integrating multiple tissue types paves the way for constructing a comprehensive “joint-on-a-chip” model that recapitulates the complex interactions of the entire osteochondral unit [110].

Key technological components of a COoC system

The construction of a functional COoC platform relies on several key technological components. The pedestal material is most commonly PDMS due to its excellent optical transparency for real-time imaging, gas permeability for maintaining CO₂ levels, and elasticity for applying mechanical strain. However, its hydrophobicity and potential for absorbing small molecules are notable drawbacks. To counter this, surface modification strategies, such as coating with hyaluronic acid (HA) or other biocompatible polymers, are employed to improve cell adhesion and reduce nonspecific adsorption [111]. Alternative materials like poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) and glass are also used, particularly for their superior biochemical inertness, though they lack the inherent deformability of PDMS [112].

Within the chip, the hydrogel scaffold serves as the 3D niche for the cartilage organoid. Its role extends beyond simple structural support; the hydrogel’s permeability allows for the creation of anisotropic nutrient and biochemical gradients, which are crucial for patterning tissue organization. For example, a gradient-generating microfluidic device has been used to steer stem cell differentiation into chondrogenic and osteogenic lineages on a single hydrogel slab, creating an integrated osteochondral interface [113]. The incorporation of biosensors is an emerging frontier that promises to transform COoC platforms from mere culture systems into analytical tools. While still underutilized in cartilage-specific models, integrated electrochemical sensors in other organ chips demonstrate the potential for real-time monitoring of metabolites like glucose and lactate, or specific enzymatic activities such as alkaline phosphatase (ALP) [114]. The future integration of such sensors into COoC systems would enable continuous, non-destructive monitoring of organoid health and metabolic status during disease modeling or drug testing.

Applications and future integration

The COoC platform finds immediate application in disease modeling and drug screening. Its ability to apply controlled mechanical loads and orchestrate multi-tissue interactions makes it ideal for studying the initiation and progression of OA and RA. For example, chips that co-culture cartilage and synovium can be used to screen for drugs that effectively block the pro-inflammatory crosstalk between FLS and chondrocytes [115]. The platform’s compatibility with high-resolution, live-cell imaging also allows for the direct observation of cellular processes like monocyte extravasation into a joint-mimicking chamber, a key event in inflammatory arthritis [116].

Looking forward, the greatest potential of COoC lies in its integration into multi-organ-chip systems. While a standalone cartilage chip is powerful, its physiological relevance is amplified when connected to other organ modules, such as liver and kidney, to simulate systemic drug metabolism and toxicity. Although a fully vascularized bone/cartilage module linked to other organs has not yet been widely reported, the technological foundation exists [110]. The convergence of COoC with patient-specific iPSCs, advanced biosensing, and automated systems is poised to create a new generation of highly predictive, personalized ex vivo models. These models will not only deepen our understanding of cartilage pathology but also dramatically accelerate the development and safety testing of novel therapeutics for joint diseases.

Challenges and future perspectives

Despite substantial advances, translating regenerated cartilage from bench to bedside remains constrained by several interrelated bottlenecks: inadequate mechanical maturation, a lack of standardization and reproducibility, vascularization and nutrient supply limitations, long-term hypertrophic drift, a widening gap between in vitro performance and clinical expectations, and imprecise control over the biochemical and biophysical microenvironment. These challenges collectively restrict the ability of current cartilage organoids to fully recapitulate the load-bearing function, phenotypic stability, and regulatory readiness required for reliable clinical application.

Inadequate mechanical maturation and load-bearing function

The ultimate clinical goal of cartilage organoid technology is to generate grafts capable of withstanding the complex, multi-directional forces experienced in articulating joints. However, even the most advanced cartilage organoids currently exhibit compressive moduli that are at least an order of magnitude lower than those of native hyaline cartilage. While scaffolds derived from decellularized cartilage ECM have shown promise in promoting chondrogenesis, their mechanical strength generally remains insufficient for load-bearing applications in vivo [115]. Mechanically tunable hydrogels—spanning stiffness and viscoelasticity—have emerged as powerful tools to recapitulate tissue-specific ECM mechanics and overcome the batch variability and static properties of conventional culture systems [59]. Yet the precise adjustment of matrix mechanics to simultaneously support chondrogenesis and mechanical robustness remains a major engineering challenge. Recent work further demonstrates that construct maturation critically governs mechanoresponsiveness: immature grafts may not tolerate mechanical stimulation, and dynamic compression bioreactors may serve not only to support cartilage engineering but also to predict in vivo graft performance [117]. The limited collagen fiber alignment and inadequate zonal organization in current organoids further compound this shortfall. Without a hierarchical ECM architecture—characterized by arcade-like collagen orientation and depth-dependent proteoglycan distribution—engineered cartilage lacks the anisotropic mechanical behavior essential for durable joint function.

Lack of reproducibility and standardization

A fundamental obstacle to both research progress and clinical translation is the absence of standardized protocols across laboratories. Inconsistent seeding densities, growth factor concentrations, oxygen tensions, and culture durations produce organoids of widely divergent composition and function, impeding inter-study comparisons. The inherent batch-to-batch variability arising from different MSC donors and iPSC lines further amplifies heterogeneity. During stepwise directed differentiation or long-term expansion, genetic and epigenetic drift can shift the balance between chondrogenic commitment and hypertrophic divergence, producing organoids with unpredictable matrix quality [118]. Despite the growing recognition that standardization is essential for generating reproducible and regulatory-grade results, standardized quality control frameworks remain in early developmental stages even for more established organoid systems such as cerebral cortical organoids [119]. Key issues include the absence of universally accepted quality metrics—histological scoring rubrics, biochemical composition thresholds, mechanical performance benchmarks—and the lack of consensus on critical process parameters such as media formulation, feeding schedules, and passaging criteria [118]. Consequently, the field is not yet positioned to produce the consistent, scalable, and regulatory-compliant cartilage organoids needed for large animal validation or human trials.

Vascularization and nutrient supply limitations

Nutrient and oxygen delivery poses a paradox for cartilage organoids. Native articular cartilage functions as a metabolically quiescent, avascular tissue; yet organoids cultured beyond a critical diameter of approximately 300 μm inevitably develop central necrosis owing to the diffusion limit of oxygen and nutrients [38, 120, 121]. The hypoxic cores that form in large organoids not only compromise viability but also create an artificial biochemical gradient that can confound experimental interpretations and skew drug screening readouts. When cartilage organoids are used as building blocks for osteochondral composites, the lack of an integrated vascular network undermines their capacity to integrate with host subchondral bone and sustain long-term graft viability upon implantation. Significant efforts have been devoted to engineering perfusable blood vessel networks in organoid systems, with the inclusion of perfused vascular compartments markedly enhancing physiological relevance [122]. However, current vascularization strategies face an unresolved tension: pre-vascularization promotes endochondral ossification and loss of the hyaline phenotype, while avascular culture limits size and in vivo integration. Balancing these opposing requirements without triggering undesirable lineage switching remains a formidable biological challenge that demands novel bioengineering and developmental biology-driven approaches.

Long-term phenotypic stability and prevention of hypertrophy

Although initial 2D expansion of chondrocytes or progenitors is often necessary to generate sufficient cell numbers, this process predisposes cells to dedifferentiation and irreversible loss of the chondrogenic phenotype [98]. Even after successful 3D chondrogenesis, transplanted organoids often undergo hypertrophic differentiation in vivo, as evidenced by elevated COL10A1 expression, progressive matrix calcification, and eventual transition toward endochondral bone rather than stable articular cartilage. Notably, co-delivery of chondrogenic morphogens (e.g., NGF and BMP-2) through thermosensitive hydrogels can enhance matrix production but may simultaneously prime the tissue toward hypertrophy, underscoring the signaling duality that complicates therapeutic design [123]. The pro-inflammatory environment of the osteoarthritic joint—rich in IL-1β, TNF-α, and activated matrix metalloproteinases—further accelerates hypertrophic drift, matrix degradation, and the loss of the lubricating superficial zone protein (SZP/lubricin). Substrate stiffness has been shown to modulate hypertrophic chondrocyte behavior, with soft substrates promoting phenotype reversion and cartilage-specific gene expression via YAP-mediated mechanotransduction, whereas stiff matrices upregulate osteogenic and hypertrophic markers [124]. This highlights the importance of mechanical microenvironment engineering in maintaining phenotypic stability. Moreover, replicative senescence, accumulated oxidative damage, and epigenetic erosion during extended culture alter the responsiveness of organoids to anabolic and catabolic cues, leading to a progressive decline in matrix synthesis and repair capacity. Addressing these challenges requires a paradigm shift from protocols that merely initiate chondrogenesis to those that actively suppress hypertrophy and maintain a stable hyaline phenotype over clinically relevant timescales.

Gap between current in vitro systems and clinical translation

Multiple obstacles separate laboratory-scale cartilage organoid production from regulatory-grade manufacture. Establishing cGMP-compliant processes for cell expansion, differentiation, quality assurance, and cryopreservation requires infrastructure, expertise, and financial investment that is not typically available in academic settings. Furthermore, several translational challenges currently hinder the clinical implementation of cutting-edge cartilage repair research findings, including scalability of manufacturing, cost-effectiveness, and the need for standardized regulatory pathways [125]. Although allogeneic iPSC-derived cartilage organoids have demonstrated low immunogenicity and successful engraftment in primate models [15], concerns regarding residual pluripotent cells, genomic integrity following prolonged culture and gene editing, and potential tumorigenicity remain unresolved safety considerations. Small animal models (mouse, rat, rabbit) have thin articular cartilage and fundamentally different joint biomechanics compared with humans, leading to an “animal-to-human efficacy gap” that limits the predictive value of preclinical studies. Cell-based therapies for cartilage repair, including autologous chondrocyte implantation and allogeneic stem cell treatments, show great promise but face challenges due to high costs and the diverse regulatory landscapes that govern their clinical implementation [126]. At present, no dedicated regulatory guidelines exist for cartilage organoid products, and the absence of consensus on clinically meaningful endpoints, validated potency assays, and standardized trial designs creates substantial uncertainty for developers seeking to advance these technologies through the translational pipeline.

Challenges in controlled delivery of biochemical and biophysical signals

The ability to orchestrate growth factor presentation, mechanical loading, and oxygen tension in a coordinated, spatiotemporally controlled manner is a prerequisite for engineering cartilage organoids that mature predictably and function reliably in vivo. However, sustained and localized growth factor delivery remains technically challenging: rapid burst release can lead to local toxicity, ectopic tissue formation, or premature signal exhaustion, while insufficient or mistimed release may fail to initiate or maintain the desired chondrogenic program [127]. In contrast, cartilage lacuna-biomimetic hydrogel microsphere systems endowed with integrated biological signals—incorporating TGF-β3, IGF-1, and PDGF-BB—have recently demonstrated the feasibility of robust, sequential growth factor delivery for endogenous articular cartilage regeneration, providing a strategic pathway for optimizing delivery kinetics and prolonged microenvironmental remodeling [128]. These findings point to the promise of integrating multiphasic delivery systems with mechanically dynamic hydrogels to recapitulate native developmental sequences. However, the synergistic coupling of biomechanical and biochemical cues in a multi-factorial organoid culture platform remains an underexplored frontier.

Inter-patient variability in therapeutic response

Considerable inter-patient variability influences outcomes of cartilage organoid-based therapies. Differences in donor age, genetic background, OA disease stage and subtype, systemic comorbidities, and concomitant medication use all shape the regenerative capacity of implanted organoids and the host tissue’s response. This heterogeneity confounds the development of standardized, off-the-shelf therapeutic protocols and underscores the need to incorporate personalized design principles into organoid manufacturing workflows. The variability in therapeutic response is intimately linked to the reproducibility challenges addressed in Section 7.2, as well as to the economic feasibility of patient-specific manufacturing approaches—highlighting the intrinsic complexity of balancing standardization with individualization in the evolving landscape of cartilage regenerative medicine.

Collectively, these limitations are not isolated barriers but deeply interconnected constraints that must be addressed in an integrative manner. The mechanical, vascular, phenotypic, and manufacturing challenges described above all converge on a central theme: current cartilage organoids remain too immature, too variable, and too poorly integrated with host biology to serve as reliable clinical implants. Overcoming these bottlenecks demand close interdisciplinary collaboration spanning developmental biology, biomaterials science, microsystems engineering, and regulatory science. Advances in single-cell multi-omics and AI-driven quality control tools are poised to substantially accelerate progress by providing deeper insights into organoid heterogeneity and maturation processes, but the sustained investment of resources into standardization frameworks, predictive preclinical models, and cGMP-compatible manufacturing platforms will ultimately determine whether cartilage organoids fulfill their long-standing translational promise.

Development of multifunctional cartilage organoids

To address the multifaceted technological bottlenecks discussed in Sections 7.1 through 7.7, the development of multifunctional cartilage organoids represents a crucial strategic paradigm. These next-generation organoids can be engineered to more closely mimic the structure and function of native cartilage, providing more accurate models for studying biology and disease. Recent advancements highlight the potential of using various stem cells, particularly MSCs and iPSCs, to generate organoids capable of robust chondrogenic differentiation and production of a cartilage-like ECM [7]. Integrating advanced bioactive materials, such as silk fibroin or bioactive glass, can enhance the mechanical properties of these constructs while simultaneously promoting cell viability and function [129]. Moreover, the incorporation of signaling molecules and growth factors can be finely tuned—potentially using spatially controlled release systems—to create a dynamic microenvironment that supports cartilage tissue maturation and long-term maintenance [130]. This multifaceted approach not only improves the regenerative capacity of organoids but also provides a sophisticated platform for exploring disease mechanisms and testing therapeutic interventions in a controlled, human-relevant setting. The ultimate goal is to translate these advanced organoid technologies into clinical applications, potentially revolutionizing the treatment of cartilage-related disorders.

Ethical and clinical application considerations

The development and prospective clinical application of advanced cartilage regeneration technologies, including cartilage organoids, present multifaceted ethical considerations that must be carefully addressed. As researchers explore these innovative approaches, it is crucial to proactively tackle ethical concerns surrounding their future clinical use. Key priorities include ensuring informed consent, safeguarding patient safety, and vigilantly monitoring for potential unintended long-term consequences. It is imperative that new treatments do not inadvertently exacerbate existing health disparities or introduce novel risks [131]. Furthermore, the increasing integration of enabling technologies like artificial intelligence (AI) in organoid research and personalized medicine raises additional ethical questions regarding data privacy, algorithmic bias, and the potential dehumanization of patient care [132]. As the field progresses towards personalized medicine, actively engaging diverse stakeholders—including patients, clinicians, and ethicists—in discussions about the societal implications of these advancements is essential. Establishing robust ethical frameworks and clear guidelines will be critical for the responsible translation of research innovations into clinical practice, ensuring that patient rights are safeguarded and equitable access to new therapies is promoted. Ultimately, the successful and responsible implementation of these technologies depends not only on scientific and technical prowess but also on a steadfast commitment to ethical principles that prioritize patient welfare and the broader benefit to society.

Conclusion

Cartilage organoids represent sophisticated 3D models that closely replicate the structural and functional characteristics of native cartilage, providing invaluable insights into cartilage development, disease mechanisms, and regenerative medicine strategies. Significant advances in their construction have been achieved through the utilization of diverse stem cell sources, the development of novel scaffolds (e.g., silk fibroin, decellularized matrix), and the adoption of innovative biofabrication techniques like 3D bioprinting, which have collectively expanded their utility. However, major challenges persist. These include limited vascularization, an incomplete replication of the full spectrum of native cartilage’s mechanical properties, and difficulties in precisely controlling stem cell differentiation trajectories, all of which currently restrict the ability of organoids to fully emulate the complexity of native cartilage.

Despite these limitations, cartilage organoids have proven exceptionally useful, particularly in modeling OA. They enable detailed studies of matrix degradation and inflammatory processes, and facilitate high-throughput drug screening and gene therapy research. Looking forward, integrating cutting-edge technologies is crucial for the next leap forward. For instance, advanced 3D bioprinting with sophisticated bioactive inks promises enhanced structural fidelity and regional complexity. Microfluidic systems and organ-on-chip platforms will enable the study of dynamic interactions, such as simulating chondro-synovial crosstalk to recreate joint-like microenvironments. Moreover, advances in multi-omics and single-cell analyses will provide deeper insights into organoid heterogeneity and maturation processes. Addressing ethical considerations and standardizing cultivation protocols are equally vital for the successful translation of these sophisticated models into clinical applications.

In conclusion, cartilage organoids represent a powerful intersection of developmental biology, tissue engineering, and clinical translation. With ongoing innovation in biomaterials, bioprinting technologies, and interdisciplinary collaboration, these models hold immense promise to revolutionize our understanding of cartilage diseases and the development of regenerative therapies, potentially transforming clinical outcomes for patients suffering from cartilage-related disorders.
