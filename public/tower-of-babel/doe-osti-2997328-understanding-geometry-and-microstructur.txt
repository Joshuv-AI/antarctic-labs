ABSTRACT:
The mechanical properties derived from simple tensile tests are uniquely important in the design
and qualification of load bearing metallic components. The tensile properties in standards for
wrought metallic materials are generally accessible for design and simulation engineers, but this
is not the case for additively manufactured metals. This work investigates the influence of tensile
specimen geometry on the mechanical properties of laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) additively
manufactured Ti-6Al-4V, particularly in relation to properties measured from specimens excised
from an exemplar part. A comprehensive analysis was conducted across various specimen
geometries, considering factors such as thermal history, cross-sectional area, shape, and surface
roughness. Key findings reveal that sample size, particularly cross-sectional area, significantly
affects reported tensile properties, with ASTM E8 flat and round specimens exhibiting differences
in elastic modulus (13%), yield strength (17%), ultimate tensile strength (UTS) (14%), and
elongation to failure (11%). Additionally, surface roughness was found to have a limited impact
on ASTM round geometries, but it becomes critical in thin-walled and small high-throughput
samples. The ASTM flat samples closely matched the properties of samples excised from the
exemplar part, underscoring the necessity of testing witness samples that accurately represent end-
use conditions. Furthermore, mechanical properties of samples built within the stitch zone of the
exemplar part demonstrated reduced ductility, highlighting the importance of laser alignment and
qualification in multi-laser additive manufacturing processes. Overall, this study emphasizes the
need for careful consideration of specimen geometry and testing conditions to ensure accurate
reporting of mechanical properties in LPBF Ti-6Al-4V.

INTRODUCTION AND EXECUTIVE SUMMARY OF RESULTS:
Additive manufacturing (AM) enables the design and production of geometrically complex
components that can accelerate production timelines and enable superior component performance
simultaneously. However, metal AM is a thermally complex manufacturing process where both
material is being altered, melting powder to solid material, and the component is being built, layer-
by-layer. This intertwined relationship results in microstructural variation in as-built parts, making
it difficult and important to evaluate mechanical properties that are representative of the final
components being built for applications with mechanical loading. Typically, in the AM workflow,
mechanical test “witness” coupons are built at the same time as the relevant parts and then are test
independently of the part and are taken to represent how all the material in the part will perform
[1]. Choosing the geometry, surface finish, build location, gauge height, etc. are all important
considerations when designing “witness” coupons to best represent the part performance.

Additionally, the thermal history of a part can be very different from the “witness” coupon due to
thermal mass differences, cross-sectional area, or geometric complexity. Oftentimes, heat
treatments are done to normalize these effects [2]. The disconnect between property evaluation
and part performance is a significant gap in the engineering workflow of AM.

Mechanical properties are used to simulate part performance, via finite element analysis (FEA), to
ensure reliability in applications and properties are also used to set factors of safety in component
design. Therefore, it is critical to appropriately determine properties that will best represent that
component of interest. One of the most used tests for determining mechanical properties of
materials used in the AM process are static, tensile tests [3], [4], [5] . Tensile tests are relatively
simple to conduct using standard geometries, the results are commonly reported in material
standards and are used to measure properties such as elastic modulus, yield strength, ultimate
tensile strength, and elongation to failure, as shown in Figure 1. Although tensile tests do provide
important information, there are additional tests that provide even more depth about material
mechanical performance, such as fatigue [6], compression [7], high strain rate [8], and multiaxial
testing [9], however these tests are outside the scope of the current investigation.

Figure 1. A generic engineering stress versus engineering strain plot from a quasi-static tensile
test. Important values that are commonly reported from a stress v. strain curve are identified. The
elastic modulus is the slope of the curve within the elastic region. Elongation to failure in this
instance is defined as the strain at a stress that is 10% lower than the UTS.

The material of interest in the current work is Ti–6Al–4V, which is an α+β titanium alloy that
exhibits both high strength and relatively good ductility because of its dual phase nature [10]. The
alloy is commonly used in commercial aviation, aerospace, and the medical industries because of

the combination of mechanical performance and low density. At Sandia National Laboratories, Ti-
6Al-4V is used in multiple laser powder bed fusion (LPBF) AM machines across both sites. The
AM process parameters and heat treatment were co-developed with our production agency partners
at KCNSC for one of the mutual LPBF machines. Building on the previous knowledge gained
from the partnership with KCNSC, in this work we aim to focus on the geometric effects of
samples on reported mechanical properties.

The current study has evaluated different “witness” tensile test coupon geometries and related
those properties to samples that are excised directly from a large, thin-walled exemplar part
manufactured via LPBF AM with Ti-6Al-4V grade 23 alloy powder. It has been found that sample
size is an important factor when determining the properties that best represent the properties
directly measured from an exemplar part. The properties measured from the thin-walled exemplar
part were best represented by testing “witness” coupons that were similarly thin-walled as
compared to round samples with larger cross-sectional geometries. Additionally, the smaller
geometry samples, both flat ASTM E8 and non-standard high-throughput geometries develop at
SNL [4], [11], are more impacted by surface roughness effects, which resulted in lower reported
yield and ultimate tensile strength compared to the larger cylindrical ASTM E8 samples. Finally,
the larger exemplar part was built in an LPBF AM machine that uses two lasers to increase the rate
of production, this study found a clear drop in mechanical properties for samples that were built in
regions were the working area of the lasers overlapped, commonly referred to as the stitch zone,
and further exploration should be completed to better characterize the effects of the stitch zone on
part level performance.

DETAILED DESCRIPTION OF RESEARCH AND DEVELOPMENT AND
METHODOLOGY:
The samples evaluated in this study were manufactured on the Nikon SLM Solution SLM280
Production Series V3 LPBF AM machine located in building 979 on the SNL-CA site. Spherical
Ti-6Al-4V grade 23 powder was from the supplier AP&C with a particle size distribution of 20-
63 µm. The tensile samples, listed in Table 1 and shown in Figure 2, were built concurrently on a
single Ti-6Al-4V build plate, as shown in Figure 3. The parameters for all sample on the build
used a layer height of 60 µm, a laser power of 350 W, and a laser speed of 1000 mm/s. After the
build was completed, the ASTM flat tensile samples with the tab (Figure 2b) were removed from
the build plate, twelve of the samples were kept in the as-built condition. The rest of the build plate
with all other samples and the exemplar part attached, in addition to the remaining 12 ASTM flat
with tab samples, were heat treated in a vacuum furnace at 800 °C for 2 h. The ramp rate was 6.25
°C/min., the build was furnace cooled and removed at 100 °C. All samples were wire electrical
discharge machined (wEDM) from the build plate after the heat treatment. All sample were test
with as-built surface roughness, except for the edges of the samples excised from the exemplar
part.

The exemplar part was modeled to represent a large, thin-walled housing with complex wall
angles. This type of part would require a large blank of wrought material, creating a large amount
of waste material with subtractive machining. The part could also be traditionally made in multiple
components, but that would compromise performance and add to the part count number in a large
assembly while adding to assembly time. This type of housing is an ideal part to use AM for except
for the fact that thin walls are susceptible to distortion during the AM process. Mitigation
techniques, such adding corrugation to stiffen the walls is an option for real-part use cases but was
not explored here. For this study, flat walls, accepting that they would be distorted, were built with
the end goal of cutting samples out of the walls. Tensile specimens with the same dimensions as
the ASTM flat samples (Figure 2a), were cut in the vertical orientation from each of the walls, as
shown in Figure 4, using wEDM. A total of 37 samples were cut from the exemplar part.

Figure 4. Process of excising tensile specimens from the exemplar part. The digital cut plan in
this figure shows horizontal specimens that were not used in the study, instead all samples were
cut in the vertical orientation.

Mechanical testing of the ASTM flat samples, with and without the tab, were completed on an
MTS 810 load frame with a 100 kN load cell. Strain measurements were made with both a non-
contact laser-based strain gauge and a clip-on strain gauge, both were 25.4 mm long. Samples were
loaded at a strain rate of 0.001 s-1. Data was collected at a rate of 100 Hz. The ASTM round samples
were test on an MTS 370 load frame, with everything else being the same as the flat samples. The
high-throughput samples were subjected to tensile testing at a strain rate of 0.001 mm/s until
failure. This testing was performed using a 3A Stephens load frame (model number: 204m, serial
number: 783), equipped with both a 67 kN load cell (model number: SWP20-25k-B000, serial
number: 126430A) and a 22 kN load cell (model number: 1210AF-5K-B, serial number:
1174144A). High-throughput grips were employed to securely hold the samples during testing.
To facilitate digital image correlation (DIC), for strain measurements, the specimens were
speckled along the gauge length surface. The front face of each sample was imaged using a Point
Gray camera with a resolution of 4.1 million pixels, capturing images at a rate of one per second.
For the W=1.2 mm samples the gauge length was approximately 4.5 mm and for the W=2.5mm
samples the gauge length was 10.2 mm.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION:
Effect of Sample Size using the High Throughput Geometry
Two of the four high-throughput sample geometries that were manufactured were evaluated to
determine the effect of size on mechanical properties. The W=1.2 mm sample and the W=2.5 mm
samples exhibited different tensile behavior, as shown in Figure 5. The yield strength and ultimate
tensile strength were 9% and 8% different, respectively. The largest difference was the value of
the elastic modulus, which was 21% different between the two sample geometries. The elongation
to failure was only 2% different between the samples, 13.8% and 14.1% average strain to failure
for the 1.2 mm and 2.5 mm samples, respectively. To better evaluate the role of the geometry on
the properties, the stress data was calculated using the designed cross-sectional area for both sizes,
referred to as the effective properties. Measuring linear dimensions using mechanical tools such
as calipers and micrometers always overestimates the dimension because these tools measure from
the peak of the surface roughness on one side to the peak of the surface roughness on the other.
The nominal CAD dimensions do not suffer this problem, but also do not account for potential
over/under print, leading to potential confounding with the printer dimensional tolerance. The
CAD dimensions however better connect the design intent with the reality of the print, making it
a useful metric for design purposes.

When comparing effective properties, the yield and ultimate tensile strengths begin to converge,
now only 3% and 2% differences, respectively. However, the average elastic modulus difference
remains relatively large at 15% difference, 103.2 GPa for the 1.2 mm sample and 120.1 GPa for
the 2.5 mm samples, using the effective properties. The large difference in the modulus indicates
the geometry has an effect on the intrinsic properties of the material, while the strength differences
better indicate the effect of surface roughness or reduction of load bearing area.

Figure 5. Engineering stress vs. engineering strain data for the high-throughput 1.2 mm and 2.5
mm samples exhibiting clear variation in both yield and ultimate tensile strength. Effective
properties, begin to converge, but still convey a size-effect indicating that geometry is driving the
material properties beyond microstructural influences.

Surface roughness can have a significant impact on the evaluate properties of a sample, due to the
stress concentrations that the rough features can cause resulting in reduction of strength and lower

and cross-sectional area surpasses 0.001 µm-1.

Effect of Heat Treatment and Thermal Isolation/Build Height
The 800 °C, 2-hour heat treatment, as expected, has a significant impact on the material properties
of the LPBF Ti-6Al-4V material, as shown in Figure 7. The yield and ultimate tensile strengths of
the as-built samples are over 100 MPa greater than the heat-treated samples. With the increased
strength is a trade off in elongation to failure, where the heat treated with tab samples on average
didn’t fail until 13.2 ± 0.2 %, while the samples heat-treated without the tab failed at 10.1 ± 2.0 %,
and the as-built sample with a tab failed at 5.5 ±0.2 %. The reduction in ductility between as-built
and heat-treated samples was an expected result, however the difference in the spread of the two
heat-treated geometries was an unexpected result. The tab at the bottom of the samples was
partially intended to constrict the thermal pathway for cooling the sample during manufacturing
thus retaining more heat in the sample and converting the brittle α phase to the ductile β phase in
situ. A much more thorough examination of the samples, that was not done in the current iteration
of the study, would be required to verify this effect. Other potential causes for the variation in
ductility would be build plate effect, potentially stochastic porosity was more prevalent in the
regions where the samples without a tab were built and finally, the 10 mm height difference may
be enough to change the thermal history of the two samples by changing the laser on time in the
layers where the gauge section was being built. In our previous work with KCNSC in developing
Ti-6Al-4V parameters, we observed an effect of an overcrowded build plate that resulted in worse
mechanical properties in samples when a significant portion of the build area was being melted.
Further investigation is required to rule for or against each of these potential causes, but it is a
noteworthy finding.

Effect of Specimen Shape and Surface Roughness
Although the ASTM E8 flat and round tension specimens were generated from the same standard
their resulting properties were significantly different for the heat-treated LPBF Ti-6Al-4V with as-
built surface roughness, as shown in Figure 8. The elastic modulus is 13% different between the
sample geometries, while the yield strength is 17% different, the UTS is 14% different, and the
elongation to failure is 11% different. This across the board >10% difference in properties is
indicative of both intrinsic material change as a function of the geometry and again the role of
surface roughness on samples with smaller cross-sectional areas, as shown in Figure 6. The ASTM
E8 flat and high-throughput W=1.2 mm samples have close agreement in properties, as shown in
Figure 8b, showcasing the high-throughput geometries ability to replicate the properties derived
via an ASTM standard geometry. Notably, the W=1.2 effective properties, and although not shown
the W=2.5 mm sample, are similar to the round ASTM E8 geometry. This highlights that effective
properties can map to sample geometries that are minimally affected by surface roughness.

Figure 8. (a) Engineering stress vs strain curves for the ASTM E8 flat with tab and ASTM E8
round specimens, where the round specimens were significantly stronger than the flat geometry
and (b) comparison of the data in (a) to the 1.2 mm high-throughput geometry as measured and
effective properties. The W=1.2 mm sample in the as-measured was similar to the ASTM 38 flat
geometry and the effective properties were similarly close to the round geometry.

from the previous study were machined from cylinders that were directly built. Figure 9 reveals

Tensile Results from Excised Specimens
The exemplar part was designed and manufactured with 1.2 mm wall thickness, identical to the
thickness of the ASTM E8 flat and W=1.2 mm high-throughput geometry. The ASTM E8
geometry was excised from each of the eight unique walls in the part, as shown in Figure 10.
Samples from the walls were relatively consistent for walsl that were not built across the stitch
zone on the AM machine. The average yield strength and UTS from specimens excised from walls
I-VI compared to the ASTM E8 flat samples with a tab was 854 MPa vs 809 MPa and 916 MPa
vs. 875 MPa, respectively. The elastic modulus for the two sample sets were 104 GPa (excised)
and 101 GPa, while the elongation to failure for the non-excised samples was 13.2% compared to
10.3% for excised samples. The ASTM E8 flat specimen with the tab best mapped to the excised
parts although they were not exactly the same. The increased strength in the excised samples could
be from a variety of factors including that the heat treatment for a large part needs to be for a longer
time to alter and homogenize the microstructure to the more ductile β phase, additionally the
exemplar parts walls were geometrically distorted and may have retained the residual stress from
the manufacturing process.

only three samples were test from walls (c) IV-VI, and four samples were test from walls (d) VII

A notable result from the evaluation of excised samples was the effect of the stitch zone on material
properties, namely elongation to failure, as shown in Figure 11. For both walls, the samples that
were cut right at the interface between the two lasers was picked to be test – these samples all had
significantly lower ductility due to the stitch zone defects. This finding is an area that is ripe for
further exploration as there is more desire for large scale LPBF parts that are built on machines
with more than two lasers.

ANTICIPATED OUTCOMES AND IMPACTS:
The study on the effects of tensile specimen geometry on the mechanical properties of LPBF Ti-
6Al-4V provides significant insights into the complexities of AM and the critical importance of
accurately measuring mechanical properties. As AM technologies continue to evolve and gain
traction in the NNSA, understanding the nuances of how specimen geometry influences material
behavior becomes essential for ensuring the reliability and performance of AM components. One
of the primary impacts of this study is its contribution to the ongoing discourse regarding
mechanical testing size effects in AM. Traditional mechanical testing methodologies often rely on
standardized specimen geometries, such as those defined by ASTM E8. However, the unique
characteristics of AM processes, including thermal gradients, layer-by-layer construction, and
inherent microstructural variations, necessitate ability to test alternative geometries. By
systematically investigating a range of specimen geometries, this study highlights the need for
tailored testing approaches that account for the specific conditions under which AM parts are
produced.

The findings of the study reveal that sample size plays a crucial role in determining the reported
tensile properties of LPBF Ti-6Al-4V. The observed differences in between ASTM E8 flat and
round specimens underscore the sensitivity of mechanical properties to geometric variations. This
insight is particularly relevant for engineers and researchers involved in the design and
qualification of AM components, as it emphasizes the necessity of selecting appropriate specimen
geometries that accurately reflect the intended application and service conditions. The current
study sheds light on the influence of surface roughness on mechanical properties. While surface

roughness was found to have a limited effect on ASTM round geometries, it became a critical
factor in thin-walled and small high-throughput samples. This finding is significant, as it suggests
that surface roughness can markedly affect the performance of AM components, particularly in
applications where mechanical integrity is paramount. Engineers must therefore consider surface
roughness as a design parameter, ensuring that the manufacturing process is optimized to achieve
the desired surface characteristics without compromising mechanical performance.

The alignment of ASTM flat samples with the properties of flat samples excised from the exemplar
part further emphasizes the importance of representative testing. The study indicates that testing
witness samples that closely mimic the end-use conditions can lead to more accurate assessments
of mechanical properties. By advocating for the use of representative samples, the study
encourages a shift towards more realistic testing methodologies that can better predict the behavior
of AM parts in real-world applications. Another noteworthy outcome of the study is the
observation regarding the mechanical properties of samples built within the stitch zone of the AM
part. The reduced ductility observed in these samples highlights the potential challenges associated
with laser alignment and qualification in production AM machines. As AM technology matures,
ensuring consistent and high-quality production processes becomes increasingly important. The
findings of this study serve as a reminder that even minor variations in the manufacturing process
can lead to significant differences in material performance. This underscores the need for rigorous
quality control measures and process monitoring to mitigate the risks associated with stitch zones
and other critical areas in AM components. The evaluation of the stitch zones in multi-laser LPBF
systems should be further studied to ensure reliable part performance.

In addition to its technical contributions, the study also has broader implications for the AM
community. As the demand for AM parts continues to grow, there is an increasing need for
standardized testing protocols that can accommodate the unique characteristics of AM materials.
The insights gained from this research can inform the development of new testing standards that
better reflect the realities of AM processes, ultimately leading to more reliable and consistent
material property data. This, in turn, can facilitate the adoption of AM technologies across various
interest groups within the NNSA and DOE, as stakeholders gain confidence in the performance
and reliability of AM components.

In conclusion, the study on the effects of tensile specimen geometry on the mechanical properties
of LPBF Ti-6Al-4V has far-reaching implications for the field of additive manufacturing. By
elucidating the impact of specimen geometry, surface roughness, and manufacturing conditions on
mechanical properties, the research provides valuable insights that can inform the design, testing,
and qualification of AM components. As the AM landscape continues to evolve, the findings of
this study will play a crucial role in shaping future research and development efforts, ultimately
contributing to the advancement of reliable and high-performance AM technologies.

CONCLUSION:
This study explored the effects of tensile specimen geometry on reported mechanical properties
for LPBF Ti-6Al-4V and how those properties relate to the properties measured from specimens
excised from an exemplar part. By testing a wide range of specimen geometries with varying
thermal history, cross-sectional areas, cross-sectional shape, and surface roughness conditions a
complete picture of the factors influencing reported properties was revealed. The primary
findings for this study include:
 ▪ The sample size is an important factor when reporting tensile static material properties
 for LPBF Ti-6Al-4V that was manufactured in this study. ASTM E8 flat and round
 specimens resulted an elastic modulus that was 13% different between the sample
 geometries, while the yield strength was 17% different, the UTS was 14% different, and
 the elongation to failure was 11% different.
 ▪ Surface roughness has a limited effect in ASTM round geometries, whereas in thin-
 walled samples and small-high throughput samples surface roughness is very important
 to consider.
 ▪ The ASTM flat samples were most aligned with the flat samples excised from the
 exemplar part, indicating the importance of testing witness samples that are
 representative of the end use case.
 ▪ The mechanical properties for sample built within the stitch zone of AM part have
 noticeably reduced ductility, and signify the importance of laser alignment and
 qualification for production AM machines.
