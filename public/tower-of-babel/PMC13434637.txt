Cu-Catalyzed dynamic kinetic asymmetric allylation enabled enantioselective assembly of sterically congested triaryl tertiary alcohols
Authors: Jiyao Han, Fangjia Fu, Dongliang Zhang, Pei Tang, Fen-Er Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Chiral α,β,β-triaryl tertiary alcohols represent significant structural motifs that find extensive applications in pharmaceuticals and bioactive molecules. Their distinctiveness lies in their unique three-dimensional architectures, which are conducive to interactions with biological targets, thus making them particularly worthy of attention. However, the stereocontrolled assembly of adjacent chiral centers within these sterically congested scaffolds has remained a formidable challenge. Herein, we present a Cu-catalyzed dynamic kinetic asymmetric allylation of racemic α,β,β-triarylethanones, providing efficient access to a diverse range of chiral α,β,β-triaryl tertiary alcohols with robust enantioselectivity and diastereoselectivity. When combined with subsequent transformations, this method establishes a highly flexible platform for the synthesis of antitubercular agent (S,S)-TBAJ-587 and bedaquiline stereoisomers. Mechanistic investigations, including control experiments, density functional theory calculations, and interaction region indicator analyses, indicate that the steric characteristics of the ligand and weak intermolecular interactions synergistically lower the reaction energy barrier and enhance both reaction activity and stereocontrol.

Introduction

Stereochemically defined aryl alcohols are key structural motifs in many clinically approved pharmaceuticals and drug candidates1–5. Their significance arises from two principal factors: (1) The aryl and alcohol functionalities exert synergistic effects: the aryl moiety facilitates spatial anchoring and hydrophobic interactions, while the hydroxyl group enhances binding affinity through strong intermolecular interactions, particularly hydrogen bonding, with biological targets; and (2) the presence of stereocenters increases molecular three-dimensionality, which is correlated with improved physicochemical and pharmacokinetic profiles, thereby enhancing the likelihood of clinical success6–8. Within this context, α,β,β-triaryl tertiary alcohols bearing two adjacent stereocenters represent critical components in bioactive molecules. Prominent examples include the antitubercular agents (R,S)-bedaquiline ((R,S)-BDQ) and (S,S)-TBAJ-587, the potential antitumor agent flavagline, and the anti-hepatocellular carcinoma oxindole phosphonate (Fig. 1a)9–12. Furthermore, due to the hydroxyl group as a synthetic functional handle, these compounds serve as highly valuable synthons in the synthesis of other important chiral molecules13–17. The diastereoselective addition to α-chiral ketones represents a widely employed approach (Fig. 1b left)18–23. A range of organometallic nucleophiles, such as those based on zinc, magnesium, and lithium, have been successfully applied in these transformations. However, the synthetic accessibility of α-chiral ketones remains limited, and the reactions typically require low-temperature conditions and highly basic nucleophiles, thereby restricting the compatibility with various functional groups and substrates19. In addition, achieving precise diastereocontrol is inherently challenging, often leading to unpredictable stereochemical outcomes20. As an alternative strategy, the use of alkanes or alkenes as precursors to generate carbanion intermediates has been explored for stereoselective ketone functionalization24–32. For example, the Zhang group has developed efficient bimetallic catalytic systems enabling the asymmetric addition of diarylmethane derivatives to ketones30,31. Despite the significant advances demonstrated by these methods, limitations persist regarding substrate scope and the applicability of nucleophilic reagents. Moreover, the requirement for stoichiometric additives compromises the overall sustainability and practicality of these processes.

Dynamic kinetic resolution (DKR), a strategy that enables the conversion of both enantiomers of a racemic ketone into a single chiral product, represents an elegant approach for the synthesis of chiral alcohols bearing vicinal stereocenters33–37. Since the pioneering work by Noyori33, various DKR-based asymmetric hydrogenation (AH) methodologies have been developed for the preparation of α,β-chiral secondary alcohols38–43. However, the application of DKR to the synthesis of α,β-chiral tertiary alcohols remains limited, indicating a significant need for further advancement (Fig. 1b, right)44–50. In 2017, the Johnson group reported an enantioconvergent arylation and alkynylation of α-keto esters, affording a range of α,β-chiral tertiary alcohols (Fig. 1c, I)45,46. Subsequently, a nickel-catalyzed dynamic kinetic asymmetric transformation of β-halo-α-keto esters was disclosed47. Notably, these approaches are restricted to α-keto ester substrates. Only recently has the Shi group achieved dynamic kinetic asymmetric arylation and allylation of simple ketones through nickel- and copper-catalyzed protocols, respectively (Fig. 1c, II)48,49. Despite these advances, the dynamic kinetic asymmetric addition to racemic α,β,β-triarylethanones for the enantioconvergent synthesis of chiral α,β,β-triaryl tertiary alcohols remains a formidable challenge. Several factors have hindered progress in this transformation19,51–54: (1) The increased steric hindrance associated with these substrates results in substantially reduced reactivity; (2) The undesired retro-aldol reaction of α,β,β-triaryl tertiary alcohols is likely driven by the highly strained steric environment; (3) Achieving precise control over both enantio- and diastereoselectivity by differentiating between three structurally similar aryl groups presents a significant challenge.

Recently, our group has developed a rhodium-catalyzed DKR-AH protocol to efficiently access chiral α,β,β-triaryl secondary alcohols from racemic α,β,β-triarylethanones21. Building upon this advancement, we hypothesized that racemic α,β,β-triarylethanones might also serve as viable precursors in a catalytically enabled dynamic kinetic asymmetric allylation, thereby facilitating the synthesis of enantioenriched α,β,β-triaryl tertiary alcohols through an appropriately designed catalytic system. Herein, we present a copper-catalyzed dynamic kinetic asymmetric allylation protocol for racemic α,β,β-triarylethanones (Fig. 1d). This method enables the synthesis of sterically hindered α,β,β-triaryl tertiary alcohols bearing adjacent stereocenters in good yield with excellent enantioselectivity and diastereoselectivity. The resulting products contain flexible functional handles, such as hydroxyl and allyl moieties, that allow further structural diversification through subsequent transformations. Furthermore, the synthetic utility of this approach is demonstrated through the asymmetric total synthesis of the antitubercular agent (S,S)-TBAJ-587 and the bedaquiline stereoisomers. Control experiments and density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal that the steric hindrance of the ligand and weak interactions (such as π–π and C–H···π interaction) between the catalyst and substrate play crucial roles in determining reactivity and stereocontrol.

Results

Reaction optimization

We began our investigation using racemic 2-(6-bromo-2-methoxy-3-quinolinyl)-1-(1-naphthalenyl)-2-phenylethanone (1a) and allylboronic acid pinacol ester (2a) as model substrates, which share the core structural framework of the BDQ family55,56. A variety of readily available ligands were screened using Cu(CH3CN)4PF6 (10 mol%) as the precatalyst, NaOtBu (50 mol%) as the base in THF (0.1 M) at 60 °C for 24 h (Fig. 2). Ferrocenyl-based and N-heterocyclic carbene ligands L1-L3 exhibited poor reactivity despite their success in catalytic asymmetric allylation and arylation of simple ketones48,49. The reaction with bidentate phosphine ligands L4 − L7 delivered the desired product anti-3a in 19 − 31% yield. Unfortunately, the highest enantiomeric excess (ee) value observed was only 21% with 1.8:1 diastereomeric ratio (dr). Switching to BOX, PHOX, and MOP ligands L8 − L10, the reactivities and stereoselectivities were not significantly improved. To our delight, the BINOL-derived phosphoramidite ligand L11 afforded a 63% yield and 64% ee, albeit with low diastereoselectivity. This outcome highlighted the promising potential of this ligand class for further exploration. With other types of phosphoramidites, such as TADDOL-, SPINOL-, and H8 − BINOL-derived ligands L12-L17, the reaction proceeded with moderate yields but lower enantioselectivities. These results focused our search on ligands with the BINOL framework. A wide range of BINOL-derived phosphoramidite ligands were synthesized and evaluated (Supplementary Table 1). The reactivity and stereoselectivity of this transformation were found to be highly sensitive to the steric environment of the phosphoramidite ligands57,58. Specifically, replacing the phenyl groups in L11 with bulkier 1-naphthyl groups (L18a) dramatically enhanced the stereoselectivity, increasing the ee from 64% to 90% and the dr from 3:1 to > 20:1. This clear contrast between L11 and L18a indicates that greater steric hindrance on the chiral amine moiety generally benefits both enantio- and diastereoselectivity. This principle was further supported by the performance of other ligands: L18b, featuring 3,3′-dimethyl groups, afforded a slightly higher enantioselectivity of 93% ee. Conversely, L18c, with minimal 3,3′-substituents, led to a slight decrease (88% ee). With the optimal ligand L18b identified, we proceeded to optimize other reaction conditions. An initial evaluation of copper sources (CuBr₂, Cu(OAc)₂, CuOAc, CuTC, etc., Table 1, entries 1–4 and Supplementary Table 2) identified CuTC as the most effective, providing product anti-3a in 78% conversion with 93% ee and > 20:1 dr. Subsequent investigation revealed a strong dependence on base stoichiometry. Increasing the loading of NaOtBu from 30 mol% to 100 mol% severely diminished the yield and slightly eroded enantioselectivity (Table 1, entries 4–7 and Supplementary Table 3). Notably, the formation of a byproduct, 3-benzyl-6-bromo-2-methoxyquinoline, was observed at 100 mol% base loading, which we attribute to a base-promoted retro-aldol reaction of the sterically strained product (Supplementary Fig. 1)51. Further screening of temperature, bases, and solvents (Table 1, entries 8–11 and Supplementary Table 3–4) established the optimal protocol: L18b as the ligand, CuTC as the precatalyst, NaOtBu as the base in THF (0.1 M) at 40 °C for 24 h (Table 1, entry 9). Control experiments underscored the ligand’s critical role, as no 3a was detected in its absence (Table 1, entry 12). A reaction conducted without base afforded anti-3a in 44% conversion with 91% ee and > 20:1 dr, while unreacted starting material was recovered in 41% yield with 64% ee (Table 1, entry 13). Substrate scope

With the optimized reaction conditions, we explored the scope of α-1-naphthyl-α,β,β-triarylethanones using allylboronate 2a as the nucleophilic reagent. As shown in Fig. 3, a series of electron-withdrawing groups (EWGs) and electron-donating groups (EDGs), as well as substituents with varying steric hindrance at different positions on the aromatic ring, were well tolerated, yielding the desired products (anti-3a–anti-3v) in good yields with excellent enantio- and diastereoselectivities (up to 84% yield, 99% ee, and > 20:1 dr). For example, substituents including EDGs ( − OMe, −Me) or EWGs ( − Cl, −Br, −F) at different positions of the quinoline unit were well compatible, providing the corresponding products (anti-3a–anti-3g) in 63%–74% yields with 93–99% ee and > 20:1 dr. This protocol also tolerated the substitution of quinoline with other aryl frameworks, such as 2-naphthyl, indolyl, and pyridyl, delivering the desired products anti-3h–anti-3l in 69–74% yields and 91–96% ee. Among them, the diastereoselectivity significantly decreased with substrates 1j and 1 l, demonstrating the substantial influence of the steric environment at the substrate’s β-position on diastereoselective control. No potential detrimental influence of the coordinating nitrogen or oxygen atom on the reaction outcome was observed30. We further investigated the influence of steric and electronic properties of substituents in benzene and naphthalene. Specifically, substrates bearing EWGs ( − Cl, −CF3, −F) or EDGs ( − Me, −OMe) at different position of the phenyl unit provided their corresponding products (anti-3m–anti-3r) in good yields (64–73%) with excellent enantio- (83–97%) and diastereoselectivities ( > 20:1). Substrate 1 s bearing a bulky substituent ( − tBu) at the para position on the benzene ring resulted in a moderate enantioselectivity (50% ee) with 61% yield and > 20:1 dr. A decrease in enantioselectivity was observed as the steric bulk at the para position of the benzene ring increased (anti-3b vs. anti-3r vs. anti-3s). This trend is likely attributable to the increased steric hindrance of the substituents, which impede effective chiral recognition between the catalyst and the substrate59–61. The reactivity of substrates featuring −OMe or –Me at the para position of α-naphthyl was well demonstrated, which could smoothly undergo dynamic kinetic asymmetric allylation, yielding products in good yields with excellent enantio- and diastereoselectivities (anti-3t and anti-3u). A substrate with benzo[d][1,3]dioxole at the α-position was also suitable for the reaction, affording the desired product 3 v in 84% yield, along with moderate enantioselectivity (40% ee for anti-3v, 33% ee for syn-3v) and diastereoselectivity (6:1 dr). However, substrates 1w and 1x (where the naphthyl or phenyl group of the substrate is replaced by an alkyl group, respectively) were unable to undergo this transformation under standard conditions.

Having successfully achieved dynamic kinetic asymmetric allylation using α-1-naphthyl-α,β,β-triarylethanones, we then examined the possibility of employing α-1-phenyl-α,β,β-triarylethanones. When the reaction was performed using 1 y as the substrate with L18b as the ligand, the product 3 y was obtained with over 98% conversion but only 16% ee (for syn-3y) and 1:2 dr (Supplementary Table 5). After fine-tuning the reaction parameters with L16 as the ligand, substrate 1 y successfully underwent the transformation to afford the syn-3y (42% yield, 92% ee) and anti-3y (41% yield, 48% ee) in a 1:1 dr (Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 5–8). Under the optimized conditions, a range of α-phenyl-α,β,β-triarylethanones were evaluated, furnishing the corresponding products syn-3z, syn-3aa and syn-3v (up to 42% yield, up to 92% ee) and products anti-3z, anti-3aa and anti-3v (up to 41% yield, up to 66% ee), all with a consistent 1:1 dr. Substrates bearing 2-naphthyl and pyridine groups also delivered comparable outcomes, providing syn-3ab (42% yield, 90% ee) and syn-3ac (42% yield, 90% ee), along with their anti-counterparts anti-3ab (39% yield, 43% ee) and anti-3ac (42% yield, 34% ee). The reaction proved compatible with the replacement of the quinolyl moiety by indolyl and pyridyl groups, yielding products syn-3ad (30% yield, 82% ee) and anti-3ad (54% yield, 20% ee), as well as syn-3ae (41% yield, 45% ee) and anti-3ae (40% yield, 20% ee). However, when substituted allylboronates such as crotyl, 3-methoxyallyl, and 2-methylallylboronate were employed as nucleophiles, only trace amounts of the desired products were detected, likely due to steric hindrance. It is worth noting that the diastereomers of 3z, 3ac, and 3ad could not be separated by column chromatography. Their structures and enantiomeric excesses were unambiguously confirmed through oxidation of the alkenyl group followed by reductive amination (see Supplementary Discussion). This methodology also provides a concise route to access biologically relevant molecules. For instance, compound 3ac serves as a key intermediate in the synthesis of TBAJ-587. Synthetic applications

The development of this asymmetric allylation offers an elegant method for preparing a range of enantioenriched α,β,β-triaryl tertiary alcohols, which serve as the fundamental structural skeleton for many drugs and bioactive natural products. To examine the synthetic utility, we first performed a gram-scale enantioconvergent allylation of 1b, yielding the desired product anti-3b in 68% yield with 92% ee and > 20:1 dr (Fig. 5a). The allyl moiety of anti-3b can undergo a variety of downstream transformations without any loss of enantio- and diastereoselectivity62–64. For example, Ru-catalyzed olefin metathesis of anti-3b with (Z)-but-2-ene-1,4-diol afforded 4a in 78% yield. Moreover, anti-3b could be converted to compound 4b in high yield via a Pd-catalyzed reduction reaction. The borylation of anti-3b followed by oxidation also proceeded smoothly, delivering product 4c in 73% yield. Furthermore, this strategy was conveniently applied to prepare the stereoisomers of the anti-tuberculosis drug (–)-bedaquiline65–67. As shown in Fig. 5b, the copper-catalyzed asymmetric allylation of 1a can be smoothly conducted to deliver (S,S)-3a and (R,R)-3a with good yields and excellent enantio- and diastereoselectivities using (S,S,S)-L18b and (R,R,R)-L18b as ligands, respectively. Then, oxidative cleavage followed by reductive amination of (S,S)-3a and (R,R)-3a directly afforded optically pure (S,S)-BDQ (94% ee) and (R,R)-BDQ (92% ee). Different stereochemical configurations of a molecule often exhibit distinct biologically activities due to the difference of three-dimensional architectures68–71. The preparation of biological active molecules to access their isomers is of great importance for exploring structure−activity relationships71. For a notable example, (S,S)-TBAJ-587, currently in phase I clinical trials, has been identified as a novel anti-tuberculosis agent, which exhibited high bacterial potency, excellent pharmacokinetic profile, and minimal toxicity10. Using 1ac as the substrate and (R)-L16 as the ligand, the reaction was efficiently performed on a gram scale, affording a 1:1 diastereomeric mixture of syn-3ac and anti-3ac. Subsequent functional group transformations, as described above, furnished (S,S)-TBAJ-587 and (R,S)-TBAJ-587 in 32% yield with 90% ee and 32% yield with 34% ee, respectively. Mechanistic studies

To gain insight into the DKR process, several control experiments were performed. First, (R)-1a, (S)-1a, and racemic 1a were used for the asymmetric allylation reaction with (R,R,R)-L18b as the ligand, respectively (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 2−3). Both enantiomers and the racemate yielded product anti-3a in similar enantio- and diastereoselectivity with the same relative and absolute configuration. These results confirm an excellent catalyst-controlled process. When using (S)-1a, the enantioselectivity were slightly decreased to 87% ee with > 20:1 dr. This result indicates that (R)-1a is matched with (R,R,R)-L18b and that the amount of the mismatched substrate might slightly affect the reactivity and enantiocontrol in this transformation (Table 1, entry13). Next, control experiments were also performed to investigate potential racemization pathways (Supplementary Fig. 4). As shown in Fig. 6b, the reactions with CuTC and (S,S,S)-L18b did not promote the racemization process of (S)-1a. Conversely, racemization of (S)-1a was observed in the presence of base. These results suggest that the ketone racemization may occur via a base-deprotonating enolate-formation pathway.Fig. 6Control experiments.a Asymmetric allylation with chiral and racemic ketone substrates. b The exploration of the racemization process c Non-linear effect study. d The influence of ligand chirality.

The relationship between the ee value of the ligand and the enantio- and diastereomeric composition of the product was also examined. As shown in Fig. 6c, a linear correlation between the enantiopurity of the catalyst and product 3a suggests the presence of a mononuclear chiral catalyst in the enantiodetermining transition states. Interestingly, the diastereoselectivity did not exhibit significant changes with the variations of the ligand’s enantiopurity (Supplementary Table 9). To identify this phenomenon, we employed ligand L18a with different configurations for the reaction of 1a (Supplementary Fig. 5). As shown in Fig. 6d, the diastereoselectivity did not show an obvious change, which proved that the excellent diastereoselectivity primarily benefits from the increased steric hindrance on the chiral amine portion of the ligand. Based on our observations and previous reports49, we propose a catalytic cycle in which the nucleophilic addition of the allyl copper species to ketones is both the rate-determining and stereodetermining step (Supplementary Fig. 6).

To gain further mechanistic insights into the Cu-catalyzed dynamic kinetic asymmetric allylation enabled by a phosphoramidite ligand, DFT calculations were performed on the key transition states using 1a and 2a as model substrates (see Supplementary Discussion and Source Data). As illustrated in Fig. 7, the transition state (TS) TS1 for the reaction of (S)-1a to (S,S)-3a exhibits the lowest activation free energy of 23.4 kcal/mol. The barriers for the competing pathways TS2 [(S)-1a to (S,R)-3a], TS3 [(R)-1a to (R,R)-3a] and TS4 [(R)-1a to (R,S)-3a] are 25.3, 24.2, and 27.0 kcal/mol, respectively, all higher than that of TS1 (Supplementary Fig. 7). In contrast, DFT calculations show that the TS5 for the substrate (S)-1w has a high energy barrier of 29.7 kcal/mol. This relatively high barrier is considered to be unfavorable for the reaction to proceed smoothly, which is consistent with the experimentally observed inertness of this substrate under the reaction conditions (Supplementary Fig. 8 and Fig. 9). The nucleophilic attack of the allyl copper species to (S)-1a (the matched ketone substrate) occurs by a polar Felkin–Anh (PFA) model. The PFA model is based on transition-state stabilization via hyperconjugative interactions of the forming bond with the carbon–carbon bond connecting the quinolyl group72. Furthermore, the interaction region indicator (IRI) analysis was employed to visualize the secondary interactions between the substrates and the catalytic species in the TS73. The reactivity and stereoselectivity are governed by the steric hindrance of the ligand and the weak interactions between the catalytic species and the substrate. Specifically, in TS1, the phenyl of (S)-1a and the 1-naphthyl of (S,S,S)-L18b adopt a parallel displacement arrangement, which promotes a π–π stacking interaction (the bright green regions pointed by the green arrow, Fig. 7). Concurrently, the C–H bond of the 1-naphthyl in (S)-1a points toward the plane of the 1-naphthyl in (S,S,S)-L18b, providing additional stabilization through the C–H···π interaction (the light green regions pointed by the gray arrow). In TS2, the phenyl of (S)-1a rotates away from the 1-naphthyl of (S,S,S)-L18b, resulting in the loss of π–π stacking, remaining only a weaker C–H···π interaction between substrate and ligand. Moreover, there is a weak steric effect (red-green mixed region pointed by the red arrow) between the carbonyl oxygen of (S)-1a and C–H bond of the 1-naphthyl of (S,S,S)-L18b, which leads to the increase of reaction barrier. In TS3 and TS4, the significant steric hindrance between the naphthyl and phenyl groups of (R)-1a (the red region pointed by the red arrow) reduces the available space for the allyl copper species to react with (R)-1a. Moreover, the geometry permits only weak C–H···π interactions (small green region pointed by the gray arrow) between the 1-naphthyl of (R)-1a and (S,S,S)-L18b. Those factors contribute to less stable transition states and higher barriers.

Comparing the 1-naphthyl of (S)-1a with the alkyl of (S)-1w, TS5 shows significant steric hindrance (red-green mixed region pointed by the red arrow) among the alkyl side chain, the carbonyl group, and the phenyl ring (Supplementary Fig. 9). This steric hindrance prevents favorable π–π stacking between the phenyl of (S)-1w and the 1-naphthyl of (S,S,S)-L18b, allowing only a weak C–H···π interaction with the alkyl chain and thus raising the barrier. Therefore, the cooperative π–π stacking and C–H···π interactions between the substrate’s phenyl group and the ligand’s naphthalene ring promote the formation of (S,S)-3a, whereas steric hindrance disfavors the reaction.

Discussion

In summary, we have developed a copper-catalyzed dynamic kinetic asymmetric allylation of racemic α,β,β-triarylethanones, affording a series of sterically congested chiral α,β,β-triaryl tertiary alcohols containing two contiguous stereocenters with excellent enantioselectivity (up to 99%) and diastereoselectivity (up to > 20:1). The practicality of this catalytic system was demonstrated through a gram-scale reaction and downstream transformations, including the synthesis of the antitubercular agent (S,S)-TBAJ-587 and bedaquiline stereoisomers. Combined control experiments, DFT calculations, and IRI analyses reveal that the steric profile of the ligand, together with weak intermolecular interactions, such as π–π stacking and C–H···π contacts between the substrate and catalyst species, collectively lower the reaction energy barrier and enhance both catalytic activity and stereocontrol. This work offers an efficient strategy for constructing bioactive molecules featuring chiral α,β,β-triaryl tertiary alcohol motifs, which hold considerable relevance in medicinal chemistry. We anticipate that these findings will advance the field of dynamic kinetic asymmetric transformations toward the synthesis of high-value molecular architectures.

Methods

Typical procedure for dynamic kinetic asymmetric allylation of ketones

An oven-dried vial was charged with CuTC (1.9 mg, 0.01 mmol, 0.1 equiv), NaOtBu (2.9 mg, 0.03 mmol, 0.3 equiv) and Ligand (0.011 mmol, 0.11 equiv). The vial was connected to an argon-vacuum line, evacuated and back filled with argon. THF (0.5 mL) was added, and the mixture was stirred for 20 min at r.t. Substrate 2 (0.3 mmol, 3.0 equiv) was added to vial. The solution was stirred for 20 min and a solution of 1 (0.1 mmol, 1.0 equiv) in THF (0.5 mL) was added. The solution was stirred at the corresponding temperature for 24 h, filtration through a short celite. The solvent was evaporated in vacuo, and the crude reaction mixture was purified by column chromatography on silica gel to give the corresponding product.
