Diverse Sampling in Diffusion Models with Marginal Preserving Particle Guidance
Gal Vinograd, Idan Achituve, Ethan Fetaya

Abstract
We present EDDY ( E xact-marginal D iversification via D ivergence-free d Y namics), a guidance mechanism for diffusion and flow matching models that promotes diversity among samples generated while maintaining quality. EDDY exploits symmetries of the Fokker–Planck equation, using drift perturbations that change particle trajectories while preserving the evolving marginal distribution. We instantiate this principle through kernel-based anti-symmetric pairwise matrix fields, constructed from the repulsive directions. The resulting divergence-free dynamics promote diversity at the joint particle level while preserving each particle’s marginal distribution without any additional training. As computing the guidance can be computationally expensive in cases such as text-to-image generation with perceptual embeddings, we propose practical approximations as an effective and efficient solution. Experiments on synthetic distributions and text-to-image generation show that EDDY improves diversity while maintaining strong distributional fidelity compared to common baselines.

Introduction

Diffusion and flow matching models
[
2
,
14
,
28
,
29
,
45
,
47
]

have become a prominent class of generative models, enabling high-quality sampling
across multiple modalities, such as images

[
8
,
28
,
42
,
46
]
,
molecules
[
5
,
15
,
49
,
20
]
,
and texts

[
4
,
26
,
31
,
44
]
.
These models can be viewed as transporting a simple prior distribution to a complex
target distribution through time-dependent stochastic or deterministic dynamics.
One advantage of this formulation is that generation can be modified at
inference time through guidance
[
13
,
42
]
:
the drift can be adjusted to favor a condition, satisfy a constraint, or steer
samples toward desired properties. However, when multiple samples are drawn for
the same condition, the resulting set may contain similar outputs.

This issue has motivated a growing body of work on diverse sampling

[
24
,
32
,
43
]
. In many applications,
one does not want a single output, but rather a small set of samples that explores
different plausible modes of the conditional distribution. Naively drawing
independent samples is often inefficient, partly because guidance improves sample
fidelity at the expense of diversity
[
13
]
. Recent
methods addressed this issue by introducing interactions between samples, for example,
by optimizing initial noise latents
[
10
,
21
]

or adding repulsive particle potentials during sampling

[
6
,
18
,
33
]
. These approaches
can improve diversity but can suffer from severe drawbacks. Mainly, we found that
adding repulsive terms during sampling creates noticeable artifacts, while
optimizing the noise latents had a limited effect on diversity (see examples in
Fig.
1
).

In this work, we ask whether particle-based diversity can be introduced while
preserving the target marginal distribution of each sample in a
training-free

manner. Our starting point is the observation that the Fokker–Planck equation
that governs the probability evolution over time admits nontrivial symmetries:
certain drift perturbations can change particle trajectories without changing the
evolving marginal density. In particular, perturbations constructed from anti-symmetric
matrix fields through a Stein-type operator yield zero additional contribution
to the Fokker–Planck equation. We use this symmetry to construct a particle
guidance mechanism in which each particle interacts with the others through a
kernel-induced repulsive direction. For each pair of particles, we form an
anti-symmetric matrix from the difference of two outer products involving the
repulsive kernel direction. Averaging these pairwise contributions gives a guidance
field that promotes diversity at the joint level while preserving the single-particle
marginal distribution.

For practical text-to-image generation, we often wish to define similarity not
directly in pixel or latent coordinates, but in a perceptual feature space
such as DINO or CLIP embeddings. In this setting, computing the exact kernel
can be prohibitively expensive due to second order derivatives. We therefore use
finite-difference and Hutchinson trace estimation to approximate the expensive-to-compute
elements needed to form the marginal-preserving drift perturbation. The exact
theoretical guarantee no longer holds under these approximations, but the construction
continues to be guided by it: the resulting drift remains a finite-sample
estimate of a provably marginal-preserving field, and we find empirically that
it inherits the robustness of the exact method, maintaining high sample
quality where unstructured repulsion baselines visibly degrade.

To summarize, in this paper, we make the following novel contributions: (1) We
identify symmetries of the Fokker–Planck equation and prove that they can be used
as a mechanism for particle guidance that preserves the underlying marginal distribution.
(2) We introduce a kernel-based construction of anti-symmetric pairwise
matrices that induces repulsive interactions between particles while remaining
compatible with these symmetries. (3) We develop practical approximations that
allow the method to operate with feature-space kernels, making it suitable for
text-to-image generation and other high-dimensional settings where semantic
similarity measures are needed. (4) We evaluate the method on synthetic distributions
and text-to-image generation, showing that it improves sample diversity while
maintaining stronger distributional fidelity than leading baselines.

Background

Throughout the paper, state variables such as
x_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
and functions
such as
\mu_{t}
and
p_{t}
carry implicit argument
x_{t}
unless stated
otherwise; and
\nabla
(
\divergence
) denotes the gradient (resp. divergence)
with respect to
x_{t}
.

2.1
Diffusion Models and Flow Matching

Modern generative modeling transports a simple prior
p_{0}=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
to
a target distribution
p_{1}
over the unit interval
t\in[0,1]
through continuous-time
dynamics. DDPM
[
14
,
47
]
fixes a Gaussian
noising process with prior
x_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and noise schedule

\beta_{t}
. Sampling
x_{1}\sim p_{1}
is done by integrating the time-reversal
SDE
[
3
]
,

dx_{t}=\left[\tfrac{1}{2}\beta_{t}\,x_{t}+\beta_{t}\,\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})\right]dt+\sqrt{\beta_{t}}\,dW_{t},

(1)

forward in
t
from
x_{0}\!\sim\!p_{0}
to
x_{1}\!\sim\!p_{1}
, where
W_{t}

is the Wiener process. Optimal-transport flow matching (OT-FM)
[
2
,
28
,
29
]

instead sample by integrating the following deterministic ODE:

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}x_{t}=\mathbb{E}[x_{1}-x_{0}\mid x_{t}].

(2)

Both frameworks therefore fall under a common Itô SDE

dx_{t}=\mu_{t}(x_{t})\,dt+\sigma_{t}(x_{t})\,dW_{t},

(3)

with drift
\mu:\mathbb{R}^{d}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
and volatility
\sigma:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}
. Diffusion takes
\sigma_{t}>0
and absorbs the score into
the drift as in (
1
), while OT-FM has a degenerate

\sigma\equiv 0
deterministic flow and learns
\mu_{t}
directly. For simplicity,
we assume that if a condition is given (e.g. a prompt) then it is already incorporated
into the drift.

The score
\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})
is available in both methods. The output of
diffusion models is the score (up to a known scaling factor). In flow matching
it can be inferred from
\mathbb{E}[x_{1}-x_{0}\mid x_{t}]
through Tweedie’s
formula
[
9
]
:

\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})=\frac{t\mathbb{E}[x_{1}-x_{0}\mid x_{t}]-x_{t}}{1-t}.

(4)

The complete derivation can be found in Appendix
E
.

2.2
Fokker–Planck Equation and its Symmetries

The state
x_{t}
in (
3
) is a random variable, and its marginal
density
p_{t}
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
evolves deterministically under the Fokker-Planck equation
(FPE),

\frac{\partial}{\partial t}p_{t}\;=\;-\divergence(p_{t}\,\mu_{t})\;+\;\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{2}\,\Delta p_{t},

(5)

so each pair
(\mu,\sigma)
induces a path of marginals

\{p_{t}\}_{t\in[0,1]}
connecting prior
p_{0}
to target
p_{1}
. This mapping
is not one-to-one: many different combinations of
(\mu,\sigma)
can produce
the identical evolution of
p_{t}
. These are the
symmetries
of the
FPE: changes to
(\mu,\sigma)
that leave the density evolution, and hence
the marginal at every time
t
, invariant, while reshaping how individual
particles move.

Definition 1

(Matrix Stein operator)
.

For a matrix field
F:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
,
the Stein operator
[
48
]
over
F
is defined as

\mathcal{A}_{p}(F)\;=\;\frac{1}{p}\nabla\left(pF\right)\;=\;\divergence F\;+\;F\,\nabla\log p,

(6)

where
\divergence F
is the row-wise divergence:

(\divergence F)_{i}=\sum_{j}\frac{\partial}{\partial x_{j}}F_{ij}
.

Claim 1

(Symmetry of the FPE)
.

Let
A:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
be any smooth anti-symmetric matrix field (i.e.,

A^{\top}=-A
), that can depend on
t
. Then the guidance
\psi_{t}=\mathcal{A}_{p_{t}}(A)
yields the same Fokker–Planck equation (
5
) and hence the same
marginal
p_{t}
for all
t
.
[
16
,
41
]

Proof.

Substituting the guided drift
\tilde{\mu}_{t}=\mu_{t}+\psi_{t}
into
(
5
), the additional contribution is
\divergence(p_{t}\,\mathcal{A}_{p}(A))=\divergence(p_{t}\nabla(p_{t}A)/p_{t})=\divergence(\divergence(A\,p_{t}))

where the inner divergence acts on a matrix and the outer divergence acts on
a vector. For any anti-symmetric
A
, this double divergence vanishes identically
by the symmetry of mixed partial derivatives (
\tfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{i}\partial x_{j}}(A_{ij}p_{t})+\tfrac{\partial^{2}}{\partial x_{j}\partial x_{i}}(A_{ji}p_{t})=0

since
A_{ji}=-A_{ij}
).
∎

Remark 1
.

The operator
\mathcal{A}_{p}
in (
6
) is exactly the classical Stein
operator
f\mapsto\divergence(f)+\langle f,\nabla\log p\rangle
, but acting
on each row of the matrix
F
independently.

For completeness, we note that an additional symmetries holds, for example, with
volatility: adding noise
\epsilon
with drift correction

\mathcal{A}_{p}(\epsilon\epsilon^{\top}/2)
also preserves
p_{t}
. Both can be written compactly
as
\tilde{\mu}_{t}=\mu_{t}+\mathcal{A}_{p}(A+\epsilon\epsilon^{\top}/2)
for anti-symmetric
matrix
A
and positive semi-definite matrix
\epsilon\epsilon^{\top}/2
.

3
Related Work

Diverse Conditional Sampling in Diffusion Models.

Guidance is a powerful mechanism to gain sampling control in diffusion and
flow models. Early methods in this area of study are classifier guidance
[
8
]

and classifier-free guidance (CFG)
[
13
]
, both trade-off
diversity for fidelity. Several studies suggested methods to improve diversity
while maintaining high fidelity in CFG. We list here several prominant
examples. Inspired by the forward process in diffusion models,
CADS

[
43
]

injects random noise into the conditioning signal. This increases sample diversity
while maintaining strong image quality; however, the noisy conditioning leads to
a noticeable decline in prompt alignment.
[
24
]

proposed limiting the guidance to an interval of sampling steps in the middle.
In
[
23
]
an adaptive guidance scale is proposed based on
the posterior likelihood of the current time step and the condition prior.

[
32
]
suggested to create better diversity by pushing away
samples from a given reference set based on the approximate denoised values.

Diverse Sampling in Diffusion Models using Particles.
In the literature,
several approaches proposed diverse sampling in diffusion and flow models
based on interactions between particles. Both in
[
21
]
and
[
10
]

optimization of the initial noise samples was proposed. In

[
21
]
, the InfoNCE loss
[
36
]
was used
based on the approximate denoised values of the particles, while in
[
10
]

an objective composed of quality, diversity and noise regularization was
proposed. Other approaches intervene in the sampling process.

[
38
]
suggested sub-sampling at each flow step a subset of
particles using quadratic integer programming that promotes both high quality
and high diversity. Other approaches attempt to modify the SDE during sampling.

[
22
]
shift particles whose approximate denoised values
fall inside balls centered at the elements of some reference set. More related
to our proposed approach are
PG

[
6
]
, DiverseFlow

[
33
]
, and SPARKE
[
18
]
, all of which
modify the SDE during sampling using kernels.
PG

[
6
]

adds a fixed potential function based on similarity kernels, DiverseFlow
[
33
]

uses determinantal point processes (DPP) as a repulsion force on the
approximate denoised values of the particles, and SPARKE
[
18
]
,
which is designed to operate on semantic similar prompts simultaneously, adds a
repulsion term based on the RKE score
[
17
]
. Unlike

EDDY
, all of these approaches modify the marginal distribution of particles,
hence scarifying fidelity or quality for diversity. We note that
PG
discussed
the possibility of preserving the marginal distribution of particles by learning
the potential function, but they did not follow this path in their
experimental evaluations. Lastly, recently
[
30
]
suggested
to use importance weighting for correcting the bias introduced by adding a
repulsion term between the particles; however, this method requires learning
an additional model to track the importance weight unlike
EDDY
which is training-free.

4
Method

Throughout this section, time
t
is implicit unless stated otherwise. That
includes
p=p_{t}
,
x^{i}=x^{i}_{t}
, and derived quantities that depend on

t
. The
marginal

p_{t}(x^{i}_{t})
describes the distribution of a
single particle at time
t
. When sampling a batch of
n
particles jointly,
their collective behavior is governed by a
joint
distribution
{p}_{t}(x^{1}_{t},\ldots,x^{n}_{t})
. Our goal is to find a drift that (i) preserves

p_{t}
for every particle individually, and (ii) shapes the joint distribution
so that particles repel each other, increasing diversity.

4.1
Marginal Preserving Drift

Using the previously discussed FPE symmetries, we will prove
a general method to use them to change the joint distribution while preserving
the marginals.

Claim 2

(Marginal preservation)
.

Let
X_{t}=\{x^{i}_{t}\}_{i=1}^{n}
be
n
particles, each individually evolving by drift

\mu_{t}
under probability path
p_{t}
which is always nonzero. If for each

i
, we add to the drift
\mathcal{A}_{p_{t}}(A^{(i)}_{t}(X_{t}))
(w.r.t the marginal probability

p_{t}
of particle
i
) for some matrix
A^{(i)}_{t}
that is anti-symmetric
and Lipschitz continuous in
X_{t}
, then for every particle
i
and every time

t
, the marginal density of
x^{i}_{t}
equals
p_{t}
. The joint distribution
need not be preserved.

If the effect on each particle was due to external forces, this would be
trivial due to claim
1
, however, this requires a more
delicate consideration due to the interdependent interactions between
particles.

Proof sketch
.

Fix a particle
i
and let
p_{t}(x^{i}_{t})
denote the target marginal and
\tilde{p}_{t}(x^{i}_{t})
the marginal under the modified dynamics. Additionally, denote
the neighbors of particle
i
by
x^{-i}_{t}:=\{x^{j}_{t}\}_{j\neq i}
.

We compare the
true
dynamics to an auxiliary
frozen
system in which

x^{-i}_{t}=x^{-i}_{t_{0}}
is kept fixed for all
t\geq t_{0}
for some
t_{0}
. The particle
x^{i}_{t}
evolves with drift

\mu_{t}(x^{i}_{t})+\psi^{i}_{t}(X_{t})
, where
\psi^{i}_{t}(X_{t}):=\mathcal{A}_{p_{t}}(A^{(i)}_{t}(X_{t}))
(where
X_{t}
varies between systems) and the Stein operator is taken
w.r.t. the marginal
p_{t}(x^{i}_{t})
. Since
A^{(i)}_{t}(\cdot,x_{t_{0}}^{-i})

is anti-symmetric, Claim
1
implies that for the
frozen system this perturbation preserves the marginal
p_{t}(x^{i}_{t})
for
any fixed
x^{-i}_{t_{0}}
.

We now compare the infinitesimal evolution of expectations under the true
and frozen dynamics. Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{d}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
be a smooth
test function depending only on
x^{i}_{t}
. Since the marginal evolution of

x^{i}_{t}
depends directly on derivatives with respect to
x^{i}_{t}
, the time
variation of
x^{-i}_{t}
affects it only at second order, so the true and
frozen dynamics induce identical first-order evolution of all test function
expectations
\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}[f(x_{t})|X_{t_{0}}]
, and hence the same time
derivative
\frac{d}{dt}p_{t}(x^{i}_{t})
. Finally, since the change to the drift
does not hold the symmetric form we describe in Claim 1 w.r.t the joint distribution,
it will not in general be preserved.
∎

A more detailed proof is available in Appendix
A
.

4.2
Constructing
EDDY-RBF

We first instantiate Claim
2
into an exact
algorithm with the RBF kernel. We will, in the next subsection, generalize the
construction to arbitrary similarity kernels, but this requires approximations
to scale to high dimensions.

For each particle
x^{i}
and each neighbor
x^{j}\neq x^{i}
, we form the anti-symmetric
matrix
A^{(ij)}
as follows:

A^{(ij)}\;=\;r^{(ij)}\otimes v^{(j)}\;-\;v^{(j)}\otimes r^{(ij)},

(7)

where
\otimes
is the outer product of vectors,
v^{(j)}
is a vector that depends
on neighbor
j
, and
r^{(ij)}=-\nabla k(x^{i},x^{j})
is the repulsive direction induced
by a similarity kernel
k
. A simple intuition behind this construction is that

r^{(ij)}\otimes v^{(j)}
is a rank-one matrix whose range is the desired direction
r^{(ij)}
,
and we turn it into an anti-symmetric matrix by subtracting its transpose. The
reason why
v^{(j)}
depends on neighbour
j
is to avoid additional costly terms in
the divergence term, and to incorporate additional information regarding
neighbour
j
. In our experiments, we found that
v^{(j)}=\mu^{(j)}
(the drift) works
well for OT-FM as well as the synthetic experiment, while
v^{(j)}=\sigma\,\nabla\log p(x^{j})
works well for SDXL where
\sigma=\sigma_{t}
is the current noise
in the schedule.

To account for the contribution of all particles on
x^{i}
we average the contribution
over all neighbors, which yields the per-particle guidance:

\psi^{i}_{\textsc{EDDY}{}}\;=\;\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\mathcal{A}_{p}(A^{(ij)}).

(8)

This quantity preserves every particle’s marginal by Claim
2
.

By Definition
1
,
\mathcal{A}_{p}(A^{(ij)})=\divergence A^{(ij)}+A^{(ij)}\,s^{(i)}

where
s^{(i)}=\nabla\log p(x^{i})
. The score
s^{(i)}
is available either directly for
diffusion or via Tweedie’s formula using Eq.
4
for flow matching,
reducing the problem to evaluating the divergence:

\divergence A^{(ij)}\;=\;(\divergence r^{(ij)})\,v^{(j)}\;+\;\cancel{(\nabla v^{(j)})\,r^{(ij)}}\;-\;\cancel{(\divergence v^{(j)})\,r^{(ij)}}\;-\;(\nabla r^{(ij)})\,v^{(j)}.

(9)

Where, the terms in Eq.
9
disappear because
v^{(j)}
does not depend
on
x^{i}
, so its gradient and divergence with respect to
x^{i}
are zero.
The remaining terms are pure kernel quantities,

\divergence r^{(ij)}=-\Delta k(x^{i},x^{j})
and

(\nabla r^{(ij)})\,v^{(j)}=-\nabla^{2}k(x^{i},x^{j})\,v^{(j)}
, both
do not
require
backpropagation through the generative model. These combine into
\divergence A^{(ij)}=K^{(ij)}\,v^{(j)}
, where
K^{(ij)}=\nabla^{2}k(x^{i},x^{j})-\Delta k(x^{i},x^{j})\,I_{d}
is the
divergence-free matrix kernel
[
34
,
35
]
,
classically used to represent flow and electromagnetic fields. Eq. (
8
)
thus simplifies to:

\psi^{i}_{\textsc{EDDY}{}}\;=\;\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}\bigl(A^{(ij)}\,s^{(i)}\;+\;K^{(ij)}\,v^{(j)}\bigr).

(10)

For the RBF kernel
k(x^{i},x^{j})=\exp\bigl(-\|x^{i}-x^{j}\|^{2}/\gamma\bigr)

with bandwidth
\gamma
, all the kernel derivatives admit closed forms,
resulting in an easy to calculate formula (see Appendix
B
)
that we name
EDDY-RBF
.

To gain further insight about
\psi^{i}_{\textsc{EDDY}{}}
, we analyze the transport field
in Eq.
10
when
\gamma
is small and independent of
d

and show that in high dimensions while keeping bandwidth independent of
d
it
is well-approximated by the divergence-free transport term alone, resulting in:

\psi^{i}_{\textsc{EDDY}{}}\;\approx\;\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}K^{(ij)}\,v^{(j)}.

(11)

This dominance of the divergence-free kernel term is what motivated the name

EDDY
: the resulting transport is eddy-like, locally divergence-free. See
appendix
C
for the proof.

4.3
Constructing
EDDY

EDDY-RBF
assumes the kernel derivatives are given in closed form. In our text-to-image
experiments (Section
5.2
) we use an RBF kernel applied to DINOv2
features, so the second-order derivatives in Eq. (
10
) are no
longer known analytically. Moreover, to exactly compute the Laplacian element
of
EDDY
would require
\mathcal{O}(d)
backpropagations, which is
impractical for high-dimensional problems.

We therefore drop the closed-form assumption and approximate both second-order
quantities. We use approximations that require only fast kernel evaluations and
first-order kernel gradients (as available from CLIP or DINO). We note, however,
that the marginal is no longer preserved exactly due to the approximations
used. Nevertheless, as demonstrated in our experiments,
EDDY
still
outperforms competing approaches.

For the Hessian–vector product, we use a central difference with two backward
passes,

\nabla^{2}k(x^{i},x^{j})\,v^{(j)}\;\approx\;\frac{\nabla k(x^{i}_{t}+\epsilon v^{(j)},x^{j}_{t})-\nabla k(x^{i}_{t}-\epsilon v^{(j)},x^{j}_{t})}{2\epsilon}.

(12)

For the Laplacian, we use a forward-only Hutchinson trace estimator with

m=25
Rademacher probes

r_{1},\ldots,r_{m}\overset{\mathrm{iid}}{\sim}\mathrm{Rad}(\{\pm 1\}^{d})
,
requiring
2m+1
forward passes,

\Delta k(x^{i},x^{j})\;\approx\;\frac{1}{m}\sum_{\ell=1}^{m}\frac{k(x^{i}_{t}+\epsilon r_{\ell},x^{j}_{t})-2k(x^{i},x^{j})+k(x^{i}_{t}-\epsilon r_{\ell},x^{j}_{t})}{\epsilon^{2}}.

(13)

Empirically, the guidance has its largest effect in the early, high-noise
portion of the trajectory, so we apply
\psi^{i}_{\textsc{EDDY}{}}
only for the first fraction

r_{\textup{stop}}=0.2
of sampling steps. As a result, we cut the overhead from
applying
EDDY
fivefold with negligible loss in output quality. Algorithm
1

in the appendix summarizes the full procedure. In addition, Table
4

in the appendix reports the average sampling time for
EDDY
compared to the

I.I.D
baseline and the PG baseline with guidance applied to the the first fraction

0.2
of sampling steps similarly to
EDDY
.

5
Experiments

We first show the effectiveness of our method on synthetic low-dimensional
model, where we can verify using standard statistical tests that the marginal distribution
is preserved. Next, we show the effectiveness of our method on modern Text-to-Image
generation models.

5.1
2D Gaussian Mixture Model

Experimental Setting.
We evaluate
EDDY-RBF
on a 2-dimensional data
model for which we can compute the diffusion process analytically.
Specifically, we use VP-DDPM
[
47
]
to transport a batch
of
5
particles from the prior
p_{0}=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{2})
to target
p_{1}
an equal
mixture of five Gaussians with unit variance, where the
\ell^{\text{th}}

Gaussian is centered at

5\cdot\bigl(\sin(2\pi\ell/5),\cos(2\pi\ell/5)\bigr)
.

Evaluation metrics.
We test the diversity and fidelity of our approach.
We measure diversity by the expected mode coverage: how many unique modes are a
nearest neighbor for some particle. The baseline coverage for independent (non-interacting)
particles can be calculated directly as

5\bigl[1-(1-\tfrac{1}{5})^{5}\bigr]\approx 3.36
. We test fidelity by several
statistical tests. We define two test statistics, using the distance of each sample
to the nearest Gaussian center and its angle around it. We then perform
several two-sample tests between
EDDY-RBF
and
I.I.D
samples. We use Kolmogorov-Smirnoff,
Mann–Whitney and Welch t-test. We obtain independent sampling from the marginal
distribution by choosing a single particle from each batch.

Experimental results.
Samples from
EDDY-RBF
pass all statistical tests
with p-values significantly higher then
0.05
(exact numbers in table
1

in the appendix). We can see in figure
4
(left) that we indeed
increase diversity as we increase
EDDY
’s guidance coefficient
w_{g}
. In
figure
4
(center) we show the CDF of the distance and angle to the
nearest center for
I.I.D
and
EDDY-RBF

Limitations

Despite its strong theoretical grounding and improved diversity–fidelity
trade-off compared to competing methods, our approach has several limitations.

First, it incurs a higher computational cost. When comparing
EDDY
to
I.I.D

and
PG
on FLUX,
EDDY
introduces an approximate
25\%
runtime overhead (see
the appendix for detailed results). Second, for non-RBF kernels in high dimensions
or when the similarity is defined on the embeddings of pre-trained networks,
the exact kernel is expensive to compute, and we can only compute an approximation.

In addition, empirically, we observe occasional blurriness in images generated
with FLUX.1-dev. Such artifacts are a known issue specific to this model,
suggesting that the observed blur may reflect a mode of diversity that remains
consistent with the underlying data distribution. Notably, we do not observe
similar blurriness when using SDXL; however, overall image quality is lower in
that case, likely due to the weaker base model.

7
Conclusion

We introduced EDDY, a training-free particle guidance method
for improving diversity in diffusion and flow-matching models while preserving
each sample’s marginal distribution. EDDY is built on the symmetries of the
Fokker-Planck equation by using anti-symmetric drift perturbations that alter
joint particle dynamics without changing the single-particle marginal. We instantiated
this idea through kernel-based pairwise interactions, producing divergence-free
transport fields that introduce a repulsive force between particles. On synthetic
data, EDDY-RBF increases mode coverage while maintaining statistical
consistency with the target marginal. For text-to-image generation, we
extended EDDY to perceptual feature-space kernels using finite-difference and
Hutchinson approximations. Experiments with modern flow matching and diffusion
models show that EDDY improves the diversity-fidelity trade-off compared to other
baselines, with fewer artifacts at matched diversity levels. These results suggest
that marginal-preserving particle interactions provide a principled path toward
diverse conditional generation. In terms of societal impact, this paper aims to
improves diversity in generative outputs which can add to their usefulness. As
such it inherits the positive and negative impact of the underlying generative
models.

Proof of Claim 2

Setup

Consider the modified (
true
) dynamics

dx^{i}_{t}=\bigl[\mu_{t}(x^{i}_{t})+\psi^{i}_{t}(x^{i}_{t},x^{-i}_{t})\bigr]\,dt+\sigma_{t}\,dW^{i}_{t},

(14)

where
\psi^{i}_{t}(x^{i}_{t},x^{-i}_{t}):=\mathcal{A}_{p_{t}}\!\bigl(A^{(i)}_{t}(x^{i}_{t},x^{-i}_{t})\bigr)
, with the Stein operator taken with respect to
the marginal
p_{t}(x^{i}_{t})
. Here
x^{-i}_{t}:=\{x^{j}_{t}\}_{j\neq i}
denotes
all particles other than the
i
-th. We assume
\psi^{i}_{t}
is Lipschitz in
all its arguments, uniformly in
t
. Let
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})

be a test function depending only on
x^{i}_{t}
.

Denote by
\tilde{p}_{t}(x^{i})
the marginal density of particle
i
under the
modified dynamics. We wish to show
\tilde{p}_{t}(x^{i})=p_{t}(x^{i})
for all

t\in[0,1]
.

Frozen reference system and coupling

Fix
t_{0}\in[0,1)
and a realization
X_{t_{0}}=\{x^{j}_{t_{0}}\}_{j=1}^{n}
.
Define the
frozen dynamics
as the system in which, starting from
X_{t_{0}}
,
the neighbors
x^{-i}
are held fixed at
x^{-i}_{t_{0}}
for all
t\geq t_{0}
,
while particle
i
evolves via

dx^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{t}=\bigl[\mu_{t}(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{t})+\psi^{i}_{t}(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{t},\,x^{-i}_{t_{0}})\bigr]\,dt+\sigma_{t}\,dW^{i}_{t}.

(15)

Since
A^{(i)}_{t}(\,\cdot\,,x^{-i}_{t_{0}})
is anti-symmetric in
x^{i}
for
any fixed
x^{-i}_{t_{0}}
, Claim 1 applies directly: the frozen dynamics
preserves the marginal
p_{t}(x^{i}_{t})
. This holds for
every
choice
of the frozen values
x^{-i}_{t_{0}}
.

Coupling construction.

To compare the true and frozen dynamics pathwise, we construct both systems on
the
same
probability space, driven by the
same
Brownian motion
W^{i}
. Concretely, we couple the true particle
x^{i}_{s}
(Eq.
14
)
and the frozen particle
x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{s}
(Eq.
15
) by feeding
both SDEs the identical realization of
W^{i}
, starting from the same initial
condition
x^{i}_{t_{0}}=x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{t_{0}}
. Under this coupling, the
diffusion terms
\sigma_{t}\,dW^{i}_{t}
cancel exactly when we subtract the
two SDEs, so the difference process
e_{s}:=x^{i}_{s}-x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{s}

satisfies a
deterministic
integral equation (given the realization of
W^{i}
and the neighbor trajectories), enabling the Grönwall comparison in Step 3
below. Note that the coupling is used solely as an analysis device to bound
the difference of expectations; it does not change the marginal law of either system.

Step 1: Integral form (true dynamics)

Applying Itô’s formula to
f(x^{i}_{t_{0}+\Delta t})
on the interval
[t_{0},\,t_{0}+\Delta t]
and taking the conditional expectation given
X_{t_{0}}
, the
stochastic integral has zero mean, yielding

\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{true}}\!\bigl[f(x^{i}_{t_{0}+\Delta t})\mid X_{t_{0}}\bigr]=f(x^{i}_{t_{0}})+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta t}\!G^{\mathrm{true}}(s)\,ds\;\middle|\;X_{t_{0}}\right],

(16)

where

G^{\mathrm{true}}(s):=\bigl[\mu_{t}(x^{i}_{s})+\psi^{i}_{t}(x^{i}_{s},x^{-i}_{s})\bigr]\cdot\nabla f(x^{i}_{s})+\tfrac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{2}\,\Delta f(x^{i}_{s}).

Step 2: Integral form (frozen dynamics)

Similarly, for the frozen system (with
x^{-i}_{s}\equiv x^{-i}_{t_{0}}
):

\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{fr}}\!\bigl[f(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{t_{0}+\Delta t})\mid X_{t_{0}}\bigr]=f(x^{i}_{t_{0}})+\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta t}\!G^{\mathrm{fr}}(s)\,ds\;\middle|\;X_{t_{0}}\right],

(17)

where

G^{\mathrm{fr}}(s):=\bigl[\mu_{t}(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{s})+\psi^{i}_{t}(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{s},\,x^{-i}_{t_{0}})\bigr]\cdot\nabla f(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{s})+\tfrac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{2}\,\Delta f(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{s}).

Step 3: Comparison

At
s=t_{0}
both systems share the same initial state
X_{t_{0}}
, so

G^{\mathrm{true}}(t_{0})=G^{\mathrm{fr}}(t_{0})
. For
s\in(t_{0},\,t_{0}+\Delta t]
the two integrands differ for two reasons.

(a) Neighbor displacement.

In the true system the neighbors evolve:
\mathbb{E}[\|x^{-i}_{s}-x^{-i}_{t_{0}}\|\,|X_{t_{0}}]=\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{s-t_{0}})
by standard SDE moment estimates.
In the frozen system
x^{-i}_{s}\equiv x^{-i}_{t_{0}}
, so the expected discrepancy
is
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{s-t_{0}})
.

(b) Particle-
i
trajectory divergence.

Under the coupling constructed above, both
x^{i}_{s}
(true) and
x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{s}
(frozen) start from the same
x^{i}_{t_{0}}
and are driven by the
same

realization of
W^{i}
, but with different drifts. Because the shared Brownian
motion cancels upon subtraction, the error process is governed by a deterministic
integral equation that we bound via a Grönwall argument.

Grönwall argument.
Write
e_{s}:=x^{i}_{s}-x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{s}

for the error process. Subtracting the two SDEs and using the fact that both
are driven by the same
W^{i}
:

de_{s}=\Bigl[\bigl(\mu_{t}(x^{i}_{s})-\mu_{t}(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{s})\bigr)+\bigl(\psi^{i}_{t}(x^{i}_{s},x^{-i}_{s})-\psi^{i}_{t}(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{s},x^{-i}_{t_{0}})\bigr)\Bigr]\,ds.

(18)

We split the
\psi^{i}
difference by adding and subtracting
\psi^{i}_{t}(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{s},x^{-i}_{s})
:

\psi^{i}_{t}(x^{i}_{s},x^{-i}_{s})-\psi^{i}_{t}(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{s},x^{-i}_{t_{0}})=\underbrace{\psi^{i}_{t}(x^{i}_{s},x^{-i}_{s})-\psi^{i}_{t}(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{s},x^{-i}_{s})}_{\text{(I): sensitivity to }x^{i}}+\underbrace{\psi^{i}_{t}(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{s},x^{-i}_{s})-\psi^{i}_{t}(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{s},x^{-i}_{t_{0}})}_{\text{(II): sensitivity to }x^{-i}}.

(19)

By the Lipschitz assumption on
\psi^{i}_{t}
and
\mu_{t}
(with constants
L_{\psi}

and
L_{\mu}
, respectively):

•

Term (I) plus the
\mu
difference:

\bigl\|\mu_{t}(x^{i}_{s})-\mu_{t}(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{s})+\text{(I)}\bigr\|\leq(L_{\mu}+L_{\psi})\,\|e_{s}\|
.

•

Term (II):

\|\text{(II)}\|\leq L_{\psi}\,\|x^{-i}_{s}-x^{-i}_{t_{0}}\|
.

Taking norms and using
e_{t_{0}}=0
:

\|e_{s}\|\;\leq\;\int_{t_{0}}^{s}\bigl[(L_{\mu}+L_{\psi})\,\|e_{r}\|+L_{\psi}\,\|x^{-i}_{r}-x^{-i}_{t_{0}}\|\bigr]\,dr.

(20)

By standard SDE moment estimates for the neighbor particles (whose drifts and
volatilities are bounded on compact time intervals),

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|x^{-i}_{r}-x^{-i}_{t_{0}}\|\mid X_{t_{0}}\bigr]\leq C_{1}\sqrt{r-t_{0}}

(21)

for some constant
C_{1}>0
. Setting
\varphi(s):=\mathbb{E}[\|e_{s}\|\mid X_{t_{0}}]
and
L:=L_{\mu}+L_{\psi}
, we obtain the integral inequality

\varphi(s)\;\leq\;\int_{t_{0}}^{s}\bigl[L\,\varphi(r)+L_{\psi}C_{1}\,\sqrt{r-t_{0}}\bigr]\,dr,\qquad\varphi(t_{0})=0.

(22)

This has the standard form

\varphi(s)\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{s}[L\,\varphi(r)+h(r)]\,dr
with forcing

h(r)=L_{\psi}C_{1}\sqrt{r-t_{0}}
. Grönwall’s inequality (with forcing; see,
e.g.,
[
37
]
) gives

\varphi(s)\;\leq\;\int_{t_{0}}^{s}h(r)\,e^{L(s-r)}\,dr\;=\;L_{\psi}C_{1}\int_{t_{0}}^{s}\sqrt{r-t_{0}}\,e^{L(s-r)}\,dr.

(23)

For
s-t_{0}\leq\Delta t
small, the exponential satisfies

e^{L(s-r)}\leq e^{L\Delta t}=1+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)
, so

\varphi(s)\;\leq\;L_{\psi}C_{1}\bigl(1+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)\bigr)\int_{t_{0}}^{s}\sqrt{r-t_{0}}\,dr\;=\;\frac{2L_{\psi}C_{1}}{3}\bigl(1+\mathcal{O}(\Delta t)\bigr)\,(s-t_{0})^{3/2}.

(24)

Therefore

\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|x^{i}_{s}-x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{s}\|\mid X_{t_{0}}\bigr]=\mathcal{O}\bigl((s-t_{0})^{3/2}\bigr).

(25)

The intuition is that the only source driving the two particle-
i
trajectories
apart is the neighbor displacement, which grows at rate
\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{s-t_{0}})
. This acts as a forcing term in the error ODE (
18
), so the error
itself is one order smaller, i.e.
\mathcal{O}((s-t_{0})^{3/2})
, after
integration.

Combining both sources.
Since
G
depends on the particle position
and the neighbor positions through Lipschitz functions, and since
f
and its derivatives
are bounded (
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}
),

\bigl|G^{\mathrm{true}}(s)-G^{\mathrm{fr}}(s)\bigr|\;\leq\;L_{1}\,\|x^{-i}_{s}-x^{-i}_{t_{0}}\|+L_{2}\,\|x^{i}_{s}-x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{s}\|

(26)

for constants
L_{1},L_{2}
depending on the Lipschitz constants of
\psi^{i}
,
\mu
,
and the supremum norms of
\nabla f
,
\nabla^{2}f
. Taking expectations and
integrating:

\mathbb{E}\!\left[\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta t}\bigl|G^{\mathrm{true}}(s)-G^{\mathrm{fr}}(s)\bigr|\,ds\;\middle|\;X_{t_{0}}\right]\leq\int_{t_{0}}^{t_{0}+\Delta t}\bigl[L_{1}\cdot\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{s-t_{0}})+L_{2}\cdot\mathcal{O}((s-t_{0})^{3/2})\bigr]\,ds=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3/2}).

(27)

Therefore

\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{true}}\!\bigl[f(x^{i}_{t_{0}+\Delta t})\mid X_{t_{0}}\bigr]-\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{fr}}\!\bigl[f(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{t_{0}+\Delta t})\mid X_{t_{0}}\bigr]=\mathcal{O}(\Delta t^{3/2}).

(28)

Step 4: Infinitesimal generator identity

Dividing by
\Delta t
and sending
\Delta t\to 0
:

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{true}}\!\bigl[f(x^{i}_{t})\mid X_{t_{0}}\bigr]\bigg|_{t=t_{0}}=\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{fr}}\!\bigl[f(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{t})\mid X_{t_{0}}\bigr]\bigg|_{t=t_{0}}.

(29)

This holds for every realization
X_{t_{0}}
and every test function
f\in C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Step 5: Marginal evolution by integration

We now derive the evolution of the unconditional marginal
\tilde{p}_{t}(x^{i})
.
Fix
t_{0}
and suppose, as an inductive hypothesis, that

\tilde{p}_{t_{0}}(x^{i})=p_{t_{0}}(x^{i})
.

The unconditional rate of change of
\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{true}}[f(x^{i}_{t})]

is obtained by integrating the conditional rate over
X_{t_{0}}
drawn from the

joint
distribution
\tilde{\pi}_{t_{0}}(x^{i},x^{-i})
under the modified dynamics:

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{true}}[f(x^{i}_{t})]\bigg|_{t=t_{0}}=\int\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{true}}\!\bigl[f(x^{i}_{t})\mid X_{t_{0}}\bigr]\bigg|_{t=t_{0}}\,d\tilde{\pi}_{t_{0}}(X_{t_{0}}).

(30)

By Step 4, we may replace the true conditional rate with the frozen one:

=\int\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{fr}}\!\bigl[f(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{t})\mid X_{t_{0}}\bigr]\bigg|_{t=t_{0}}\,d\tilde{\pi}_{t_{0}}(X_{t_{0}}).

(31)

Now, the frozen generator for particle
i
, given fixed neighbors

x^{-i}_{t_{0}}
, is the generator of a one-particle SDE with drift
\mu_{t}+\psi^{i}_{t}(\cdot,x^{-i}_{t_{0}})
. By Claim 1, this generator produces the same marginal
evolution as the unperturbed drift
\mu_{t}
for
any
fixed value of
x^{-i}_{t_{0}}
. That is, the frozen generator acting on the marginal of
x^{i}
gives

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{fr}}\!\bigl[f(x^{i,\mathrm{fr}}_{t})\mid x^{i}_{t_{0}},\,x^{-i}_{t_{0}}\bigr]\bigg|_{t=t_{0}}=\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{unpert}}_{t_{0}}f(x^{i}_{t_{0}}),

(32)

where
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{unpert}}_{t_{0}}
is the generator of the
unperturbed dynamics, depending only on
x^{i}_{t_{0}}
. Crucially, the right-hand
side does not depend on
x^{-i}_{t_{0}}
.

Therefore, integrating over the joint:

\frac{d}{dt}\mathbb{E}^{\mathrm{true}}[f(x^{i}_{t})]\bigg|_{t=t_{0}}=\int\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{unpert}}_{t_{0}}f(x^{i}_{t_{0}})\,d\tilde{\pi}_{t_{0}}(X_{t_{0}})=\int\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{unpert}}_{t_{0}}f(x^{i})\,\tilde{p}_{t_{0}}(x^{i})\,dx^{i},

(33)

where the second equality follows because
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{unpert}}_{t_{0}}f(x^{i})
depends only on
x^{i}
, so integrating out
x^{-i}
from the joint
\tilde{\pi}_{t_{0}}

yields the marginal
\tilde{p}_{t_{0}}(x^{i})
.

By the inductive hypothesis
\tilde{p}_{t_{0}}=p_{t_{0}}
, this equals

=\int\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{unpert}}_{t_{0}}f(x^{i})\,p_{t_{0}}(x^{i})\,dx^{i},

(34)

which is exactly the rate of change of
\mathbb{E}[f(x^{i}_{t})]
under the unperturbed
dynamics at
t_{0}
. In weak form,
\tilde{p}_{t}
and
p_{t}
satisfy the same
Fokker–Planck equation:

\partial_{t}p=-\nabla\cdot(\mu_{t}\,p)+\tfrac{\sigma_{t}^{2}}{2}\,\Delta p.

(35)

Step 6: Conclusion by continuous induction

At
t=0
the marginals agree:
\tilde{p}_{0}(x^{i})=p_{0}(x^{i})=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
by assumption. Steps 4–5 show that whenever
\tilde{p}_{t_{0}}=p_{t_{0}}
,
both marginals satisfy the same FPE at
t_{0}
with the same initial datum. Under
standard regularity (the drift
\mu_{t}
is locally Lipschitz and has at most
linear growth, and
\sigma_{t}
is bounded), the FPE has a unique weak solution.
By uniqueness:

\tilde{p}_{t}(x^{i})=p_{t}(x^{i})\quad\text{for all}\;t\in[0,1].

(36)

Since the perturbation
\psi^{i}_{t}
depends on
x^{-i}_{t}
and does not
take the anti-symmetric form of Claim 1 with respect to the
joint
distribution,
the joint
\tilde{\pi}_{t}(x^{1},\dotsc,x^{n})
is not preserved in general. ∎

Appendix C
EDDY-RBF
Theoretical Analysis

C.1
High-Dimensional Asymptotics

First, we will show that the divergence-free kernel dominates
EDDY-RBF
in high
dimensions:

\psi^{i}_{\textsc{EDDY}{}}\;\approx\;\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{j\neq i}K^{(ij)}\,v^{(j)}.

(
11
revisited)

We assume that: (1) the vector
v^{(j)}
and score
s^{(i)}
scale by the square root
of the dimension:
\|v^{(j)}\|,\|s^{(i)}\|=\Theta\bigl(\sqrt{d}\bigr)
for every

i,j\leq n
; (2) the bandwidth is constant w.r.t. the dimension:

\gamma=\mathcal{O}(1)
; and (3)
\|\delta^{(ij)}\|=\mathcal{O}(1)
for every contributing
pair
(i,j)
.

Assumption (1) comes from the fact that we apply
EDDY
when
t
is small, so

p_{t}\approx\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
, leading to
s^{(i)}_{t}\approx-x^{i}
thus the
squared norm
\|x^{i}\|^{2}
is distributed like chi-squared with
d

degrees of freedom
\chi_{d}^{2}
, thus
\mathbb{E}\bigl[\|x^{i}\|\bigr]\approx\sqrt{d}
with negligible variance as
d
grows.

Assumption (2) can easily be assumed, as
\gamma
is a user-specified value.

Assumption (3) is without loss of generality: since
k(x^{i},x^{j})=\exp\bigl(-\|\delta^{(ij)}\|^{2}/\gamma\bigr)
decays exponentially in
\|\delta^{(ij)}\|^{2}/\gamma
, any pair
with
\|\delta^{(ij)}\|\gg\gamma=\mathcal{O}(1)
contributes an exponentially small
factor and hence negligible in the sum (
10
); the guidance is
therefore dominated by close pairs satisfying
\|\delta^{(ij)}\|=\mathcal{O}(1)
.

We claim that

\|A^{(ij)}s^{(i)}\|/\|K^{(ij)}v^{(j)}\|\to\mathcal{O}\bigl(1/\sqrt{d}\bigr)\quad\text{
as }\quad d\to\infty,

(43)

and indeed from one hand
\|A^{(ij)}s^{(i)}\|=\mathcal{O}(k(x^{i},x^{j})\,d)
since, using

r^{(ij)}=\tfrac{2k(x^{i},x^{j})}{\gamma}\delta^{(ij)}
(Section
B
),

A^{(ij)}\,s^{(i)}\;=\;\frac{2k(x^{i},x^{j})}{\gamma}\bigl[\langle\delta^{(ij)},s^{(i)}\rangle\,v^{(j)}-\langle v^{(j)},s^{(i)}\rangle\,\delta^{(ij)}\bigr],

(44)

and Cauchy–Schwarz bound each term in the brackets by
\|\delta^{(ij)}\|\cdot\|s^{(i)}\|\cdot\|v^{(j)}\|=\mathcal{O}(d)
; and from the other hand

\|K^{(ij)}v^{(j)}\|=\Theta\bigl(k(x^{i},x^{j})\,d^{3/2}\bigr)
: from (
40
),
the divergence-free component is

K^{(ij)}\,v^{(j)}\;=\;\frac{2k(x^{i},x^{j})}{\gamma}\Bigl[\Bigl(d-1-\frac{2}{\gamma}\|\delta^{(ij)}\|^{2}\Bigr)v^{(j)}\;+\;\frac{2}{\gamma}\langle\delta^{(ij)},v^{(j)}\rangle\,\delta^{(ij)}\Bigr],

(45)

and
v^{(j)}
-coefficient is
\Theta(d)
(notice that

\frac{2}{\gamma}\|\delta^{(ij)}\|=\mathcal{O}(1)
), so when multiplying with

v^{(j)}=\Theta(\sqrt{d})
we get the desired result, thus claim
43

follows.

Appendix E
Score Derivation via Tweedie’s formula in Flow Matching

First, let us state Tweedie’s formula
[
9
]
: given clean
x

and estimation
\hat{x}=x+\epsilon
with independent noise
\epsilon\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\sigma^{2}I_{d})
it follows that
\mathbb{E}[x\mid\hat{x}]=\hat{x}+\sigma^{2}\,\nabla\log p(\hat{x})
.

In training, a flow matching model is trained to predict
x_{1}-x_{0}

given
x_{t}=tx_{1}+(1-t)x_{0}
for noise

x_{0}\sim p_{0}=\mathcal{N}(0,I_{d})
and data point
x_{1}\sim p_{1}
. Thus,
given a state
x_{t}
the model will estimate
\mathbb{E}[x_{1}-x_{0}\mid x_{t}]

for some unknown
x_{1}
.

From one hand, using Tweedie’s formula over "clean"
tx_{1}
and estimation
x_{t}

with independent noise
(1-t)x_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}\bigl(0,(1-t)^{2}I_{d}\bigr)
, we
get

\mathbb{E}[tx_{1}\mid x_{t}]=x_{t}+(1-t)^{2}\,\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})

(46)

From the other hand, it easily follows from the
x_{t}
’s definition that
tx_{1}=t(1-t)(x_{1}-x_{0})+tx_{t}
, so by taking expectation conditioned on

x_{t}
we get

\mathbb{E}[tx_{1}\mid x_{t}]=t(1-t)\,\mathbb{E}[x_{1}-x_{0}\mid x_{t}]+tx_{t}

(47)

Putting it all together

\nabla\log p_{t}(x_{t})=\frac{t\mathbb{E}[x_{1}-x_{0}\mid x_{t}]-x_{t}}{1-t}

(48)
