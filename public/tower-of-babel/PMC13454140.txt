Brain-inspired spatial intelligence for embodied agents
Authors: Shouwei Ruan, Liyuan Wang, Caixin Kang, Qihui Zhu, Songming Liu, Xingxing Wei, Hang Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpatial cognition enables adaptive goal-directed behavior through structured internal models of space. Robust biological systems consolidate spatial knowledge into three interconnected forms: landmarks for salient cues, route knowledge for movement trajectories, and survey knowledge for map-like representations. While recent advances in multi-modal large language models (MLLMs) have enabled visual-language reasoning in embodied agents, these efforts lack structured spatial memory and instead operate reactively, limiting their generalization and adaptability in complex real-world environments. Here we present Brain-inspired Spatial Cognition for Navigation (BSC-Nav), a unified framework for constructing and leveraging structured spatial memory in embodied agents. BSC-Nav builds allocentric cognitive maps from egocentric trajectories and contextual cues, and dynamically retrieves spatial knowledge aligned with semantic goals. Integrated with powerful MLLMs, BSC-Nav achieves state-of-the-art efficacy and efficiency across diverse embodied navigation tasks (e.g., improving success weighted by path length from 17.6% to 44.9% in instance-level navigation and from 42.7% to 53.1% in zero-shot long-horizon instruction-following), while also supporting versatile embodied behaviors in the real physical world. These results highlight a scalable path toward general-purpose spatial intelligence.Subject terms: Computer science, Cognitive neuroscience

Introduction

Spatial cognition, the ability to acquire, organize, exploit, and update knowledge about external space, is fundamental to both human beings and artificial intelligence (AI)1,2. It underlies not only sensorimotor skills such as navigation and manipulation but also supports higher-level cognitive functions, including abstraction, planning, and reasoning. In humans, generalizable spatial representations support interpretation of sensory input, anticipation of future events, and flexible adaptation to changing environments, from brewing coffee in a familiar kitchen to navigating an unfamiliar city3–5. Given its broad relevance in embodied interaction with the real physical world, spatial cognition has emerged as a central theme across disciplines, driving advances in robotics6, urban simulation7, and planetary-scale modeling8, while increasingly recognized as a foundational component of artificial general intelligence (AGI)3,9,10.

Despite the rapid progress in multi-modal large language models (MLLMs) and growing efforts to equip embodied agents with visual-language reasoning11–13, current artificial systems remain fundamentally limited in large-scale spatial cognition14, especially in tasks requiring long-horizon navigation and mobile manipulation. A central bottleneck lies in the lack of structured spatial memory15–17, a mechanism for persistently encoding, organizing, and retrieving spatial knowledge about the environment. Most existing methods, whether based on end-to-end reinforcement learning18–20 or modular pipelines with powerful MLLMs21,22, process observations in a reactive and stateless manner. Without durable internal models of external space, agents struggle to consolidate coherent spatial representations or perform reasoning beyond immediate stimuli, resulting in fragmented knowledge, short-sighted planning, and poor generalization. Addressing these limitations requires a paradigm shift from reactive processing to memory-centric spatial cognition, which supports persistent representations and compositional reasoning over time and space.

Compared to artificial systems, biological spatial cognition offers a robust and compelling template. Decades of neuroscience research revealed that organisms consolidate spatial knowledge into three distinct yet interconnected forms16,17 (Fig. 1a): landmarks, which encode stable associations of salient environmental cues to support localization and contextual understanding23,24; route knowledge, which captures egocentric movement trajectories between landmarks for habitual navigation and path integration25; and survey knowledge, which integrates multiple routes into allocentric, map-like representations that support flexible inference, shortcut discovery, and detour planning26. These spatial representations are accessed and coordinated via working memory27, especially visual-spatial working memory28,29, enabling adaptive retrieval, composition, and generalization based on task demands and environmental familiarity.

Motivated by these biological principles, we introduce Brain-inspired Spatial Cognition for Navigation (BSC-Nav), a unified framework that instantiates cognitive spatial intelligence in embodied agents via structured spatial memory (Fig. 1b). BSC-Nav explicitly constructs spatial knowledge via two synergistic branches: a landmark memory module, which encodes durable associations between salient environmental cues and 3D spatial locations to instantiate landmarks; and a cognitive map module, which accumulates egocentric observations along movement trajectories as route knowledge and then organizes the voxelized trajectories into allocentric, map-like representations as survey knowledge. Compared to previous embodied navigation frameworks confined to localized modeling with a single granularity, such as purely dense grids30,31 or sparse topologies32,33, the hierarchical spatial architecture of BSC-Nav achieves the decoupling of spatial knowledge while incorporating targeted adaptive update mechanisms. This allows spatial knowledge to continuously evolve as the agent explores external environments, overcoming the limitations of singular, static spatial representations.

BSC-Nav further incorporates a working memory module that dynamically retrieves and combines spatial representations from landmark memory and the cognitive map, thereby aligning semantic goals with grounded spatial actions. Each component interfaces seamlessly with large-scale foundation models: visual foundation models like DINOv234,35 provide perceptual grounding of environmental cues, while MLLMs like GPT-4V21 guide high-level semantic interpretation and goal-conditioned reasoning. Unlike previous end-to-end action-planning navigation frameworks11,30,36, which are highly susceptible to data distribution shifts between training and testing environments, BSC-Nav leverages the explicit retrieval mechanism of its working memory to achieve a complete decoupling of high-level semantic goal planning from low-level physical motion control, thereby paving the way toward zero-shot, general-purpose embodied navigation.

By integrating MLLM-based embodied agents with structured spatial memory, BSC-Nav achieves robust spatial cognition capabilities, supporting long-horizon reasoning, experience reuse, and flexible transitions between local and global policies. BSC-Nav achieves state-of-the-art performance across a broad spectrum of navigation tasks, including object-goal, open-vocabulary, and instance-level navigation, while also demonstrating strong spatial generalization in instruction following, embodied question answering, and mobile manipulation. These results position BSC-Nav as a scalable path that complements MLLMs’ vision-language reasoning with cognitive spatial intelligence, enabling more capable, adaptable, and cognitively informed AI operating in the real physical world.

Results

We begin by presenting the BSC-Nav framework, which instantiates brain-inspired structured spatial memory in embodied agents. We then systematically evaluate its performance across both simulated and real-world scenarios, highlighting: (i) universal navigation capabilities in foundational tasks; (ii) higher-level spatially aware skills in instruction-driven visual-language navigation and active embodied question answering; and (iii) real-world efficacy in navigation and mobile manipulation.

Construction and exploitation of structured spatial memory in embodied agents

Structured spatial memory is essential for guiding goal-directed behavior in complex real-world environments3–5, yet remains largely absent from current embodied agents. Drawing inspiration from the robust spatial representations in biological systems, namely, landmarks23,24, route knowledge25, and survey knowledge26, BSC-Nav implements a modular architecture that explicitly constructs and exploits analogous memory structures to achieve spatial cognition capabilities (Fig. 1b, detailed in Methods).

BSC-Nav comprises two branches that are continuously updated to accumulate and organize spatial knowledge during environment exploration. First, a landmark memory module encodes salient environmental cues as associative triplets comprising spatial coordinates, semantic categories, and contextual descriptions, consolidating landmarks of the surroundings. This design yields abstract and sparse representations that prioritize salient instances, enabling efficient retrieval and forming a flexible, interpretable scaffold of the external space (detailed in Methods). Meanwhile, a cognitive map module accumulates egocentric observations along movement trajectories as route knowledge, which are then voxelized and organized into persistent allocentric representations as survey knowledge (detailed in Methods). Motivated by the broad idea in free-energy37,38 and predictive-processing39,40 accounts, that biological systems update internal representations in response to mismatches between observation and expectation, we implement a surprise-driven update strategy that selectively integrates novel or unexpected spatial observations. Here, this mechanism refers to a heuristic computational design, rather than a strict implementation of variational free-energy optimization41.

To exploit structured spatial memory during task execution, BSC-Nav further incorporates a working memory module that adaptively retrieves landmarks, route knowledge, and survey knowledge for goal localization and trajectory planning (detailed in Methods). Analogous to visual-spatial working memory in biological systems28,29, this module dynamically composes spatial representations based on task demands and environmental familiarity.

We propose a hierarchical retrieval strategy guided by the complexity of the goal. For simple targets, the agent leverages MLLMs22,42 in a text-only form to reason over semantic associations and contextual cues within the landmark memory (Fig. 1c). For more complex or ambiguous instructions, BSC-Nav activates association-enhanced retrieval over the cognitive map. In this process, MLLMs enrich the initial goal descriptions by inferring object-specific attributes and scene-level priors, transforming vague commands into semantically detailed representations. These enriched descriptions are then rendered into imagined visual prototypes using a text-to-image diffusion model43. The generated images are encoded and matched against dense visual features stored in the cognitive map to localize candidate target regions. This hierarchical retrieval strategy allows BSC-Nav to access complementary forms of spatial memory in response to target complexities (category-level and instance-level) and modalities (text and image), supporting precise localization for universal goal-directed navigation (Fig. 2).

For image goals, the cognitive map is queried directly using extracted visual features. Working memory retrieval often produces multiple candidate coordinates, each associated with a confidence score and relative distance. In scenarios with multiple valid targets (e.g., category-level navigation), confidence scores alone may not reliably indicate correctness. Rather than greedily selecting the candidate with the highest confidence, BSC-Nav employs a composite ranking strategy that integrates distance- and confidence-based scores to determine an efficient, adaptive exploration sequence. Candidate targets are then sequentially visited and verified using online visual-semantic observations parsed by the MLLM until the goal is confirmed. A more detailed illustration of candidate generation, ranking, and verification is provided in Supplementary Fig. 1. Low-level movement policies are generated using heuristic planning along this sequence. During execution, MLLMs continuously parse spatial-semantic context from observations, verify landmark proximity, and confirm goal arrival, enabling affordance-aware action generation. Upon reaching the appropriate target locations, agents can invoke additional skills, such as grasping objects or answering questions, to accomplish more complex, task-driven objectives (Fig. 1c).

Universal navigation across modalities and granularities

Through brain-inspired spatial cognition that incorporates the three types of spatial knowledge, BSC-Nav demonstrates strong generalization across diverse navigation tasks and consistently outperforms recent strong baselines in both success rate and navigation efficiency.

We conduct systematic evaluations across 8195 episodes in 62 indoor scenes from physically reconstructed environments (MP3D44 and HM3D45 datasets) in the Habitat simulator46, covering four representative navigation tasks (Fig. 3): Object-Goal Navigation (OGN)18,47,48, Open-Vocabulary Object Navigation (OVON)49, Text-Instance Navigation (TIN)50, and Image-Instance Navigation (IIN)51. In each episode, agents initialized at random positions execute discrete actions (forward 25 cm, turn left 30∘, turn right 30∘, and stop)52. Following standard protocols52, navigation succeeds only when agents execute a stop within 1.0 m of goals. Evaluation metrics include the Success Rate (SR)53, defined as the fraction of completed episodes for efficacy; and the Success weighted by Path Length (SPL)53, which scales the binary success by the ratio of the shortest path distance to the agent’s actual trajectory length for efficiency (detailed in Sec. 4.2). We compare BSC-Nav with end-to-end methods (PixNav11, DAgRL49, PSL50) and modular methods that instantiate only analogous landmark memory (VLFM13, MOD-IIN54, UniGoal33, GOAT36). Figure 3a summarizes the quantitative benchmark results for category-level and instance-level navigation, while Fig. 3b presents a representative example of navigation trajectories and target verification.

As shown in Fig. 3a, BSC-Nav demonstrates superior spatial generalization in category-level navigation (OGN and OVON). For common targets in OGN tasks, BSC-Nav achieves 78.5% SR on HM3D (6 categories) and 56.5% on MP3D (20 categories with larger areas), surpassing the state-of-the-art method UniGoal by 24.0% and 15.5%, respectively. Unlike UniGoal, which organizes only landmark memory with abstract goals and scene graphs, BSC-Nav leverages structured spatial memory and thus obtains significant performance gains. In the more challenging OVON tasks involving 79 everyday objects (e.g., “kitchen lower cabinet”), BSC-Nav maintains 40.2 and 38.9% SR on MP3D’s unseen and seen validation sets, outperforming the supervised method DAgRL in zero-shot settings. The results of instance-level navigation (TIN and IIN) further validate BSC-Nav’s robust multi-modal generalization. In TIN tasks, it nearly doubles the SR compared to UniGoal and VLFM. In IIN tasks, direct matching of target images with cognitive map features yields 71.4% SR, outperforming UniGoal by 11.4%.

Notably, BSC-Nav achieves significantly higher navigation efficiency across all tasks compared to baseline methods, as measured by SPL scores. This improvement stems from our distance- and confidence-based scoring strategy within the working memory module, which enables the agent to plan efficient exploration sequences by examining only a small number of candidate locations (Supplementary Fig. 3). Interestingly, OGN and IIN generally reach higher SR and SPL with fewer explored candidates than OVON and TIN (Supplementary Fig. 3). We attribute this difference to target ambiguity: OGN and IIN typically provide stronger semantic or visual grounding, whereas OVON and especially TIN require broader open-vocabulary reasoning or finer-grained instance disambiguation, making candidate verification more challenging. Besides, performance differences across target categories likely reflect both visual saliency and contextual regularity. Large and visually distinctive objects such as beds are often easier to localize and verify because they occupy more stable and semantically predictable regions, whereas smaller or less distinctive targets can introduce greater ambiguity during retrieval and stopping decisions. A representative visualization example is presented in Fig. 3b, including top-down trajectories, first-person observations, and MLLM-assisted target verification. We provide additional examples across various navigation tasks in Supplementary Movie 1 and Supplementary Fig. 4. Furthermore, detailed numerical results for all four navigation tasks are provided in Supplementary Tables 1–3.

We evaluate BSC-Nav on a representative task termed Long-horizon Instruction-based Navigation (LIN)55,56, which requires agents to understand and execute complex instructions containing multiple intermediate goals and spatial constraints. For example, an instruction such as “Go through the glass door, pass between the sofa and the coffee table, walk to the refrigerator, then turn right and stop at the staircase entrance” demands nuanced vision-language reasoning and spatial understanding. To address this, we equip BSC-Nav with GPT-o357, a powerful MLLM with advanced reasoning capabilities. GPT-o3 decomposes complex instructions into spatially grounded subgoals (termed waypoints, such as “glass door”, “refrigerator”, and “staircase entrance”) by language instruction and initial visual observation of the agent. This hierarchical planning strategy transforms instruction-following into sequential goal-directed navigation steps, enabling BSC-Nav to reliably reach each waypoint and ultimately the final destination.

We evaluate BSC-Nav on the VLN-CE R2R benchmark56, which includes 1,000 human-annotated long-horizon instructions from MP3D. As shown in Fig. 4a, BSC-Nav achieves 38.5% SR in zero-shot settings, only 8.5% below Uni-Navid12, the state-of-the-art vision-language-action model trained with extensive task-specific supervision. Notably, BSC-Nav attains 53.1% SPL, significantly outperforming all baseline methods in navigation efficiency. We attribute this result to the complementary strengths of structured spatial memory and MLLM-based reasoning: GPT-o3 decomposes complex instructions into semantically grounded subgoals, while BSC-Nav reuses the landmark memory and cognitive map to localize and reach these waypoints efficiently across long trajectories. Compared with purely reactive or heavily supervised baselines, this memory-centric design allows the agent to better preserve spatial consistency and reduce unnecessary exploration, which is especially important in zero-shot long-horizon instruction following. Meanwhile, the remaining gap in SR relative to the best supervised method suggests that fully resolving complex linguistic ambiguity and multi-step spatial dependencies remains challenging without instruction-level task-specific training. Representative navigation trajectories are visualized in Fig. 4c, including simplified waypoint descriptions and visual prototypes generated by working memory retrieval. Additional examples are provided in Supplementary Movie 2. The full quantitative comparison is provided in Supplementary Table 4.

The value of spatial cognition extends beyond navigation to spatial reasoning and scene understanding. Here we consider a representative task termed Active Embodied Question Answering (A-EQA)58–60, which requires agents to answer spatially grounded questions through active exploration of the environment. Unlike static visual question answering, A-EQA demands spatial understanding, exploratory planning, and dynamic observation synthesis, all well aligned with BSC-Nav’s strengths. To address each question, BSC-Nav first parses target waypoints (instances or regions relevant to the query) and then conducts goal-directed exploration. Upon reaching the appropriate locations, GPT-4o42 integrates local observations with the original question to generate spatially grounded answers. We evaluate BSC-Nav on the A-EQA subset of the OpenEQA benchmark60, which includes 184 questions that span seven categories. We compare BSC-Nav with three representative baselines: a blind LLM that answers without visual observations; a question-agnostic frontier exploration strategy60; and Explore-EQA61, an active exploration method without structured spatial memory. The overall performance is measured by LLM-Match60, which computes semantic similarity between the generated and reference answers using LLMs (detailed in Methods).

As shown in Fig. 4b, BSC-Nav achieves the highest LLM-Match score of 54.6, significantly outperforming all baselines. We attribute this advantage to the fact that A-EQA requires not only local visual understanding but also active exploration guided by reusable spatial memory. In contrast to blind LLMs or question-agnostic exploration, BSC-Nav can parse target-relevant waypoints, conduct goal-directed exploration, and accumulate spatial evidence before answering. The category-wise breakdown in Fig. 4b further shows particularly strong improvements in tasks that require fine-grained spatial localization and object-state awareness, where structured spatial memory is especially beneficial for efficient and accurate information gathering. The remaining gap to human performance underscores that embodied spatial reasoning still requires stronger integration of commonsense knowledge, causal inference, and abstraction from sparse observations. A representative example in Fig. 4d exhibits how BSC-Nav combines goal-directed exploration with structured spatial memory to address diverse spatial reasoning problems. The detailed LLM-Match scores across all question categories are reported in Supplementary Table 5.

Real-world navigation and mobile manipulation

To demonstrate BSC-Nav’s generalization beyond simulation and its broad applicability in downstream tasks, we deploy it on a custom-built mobile robotic platform within physical indoor environments. This evaluation demonstrates how structured spatial memory enables reliable long-range navigation and integrated manipulation in the real physical world. The mobile platform (Fig. 5a) features a compact mechanical design equipped with high-precision motion control, as well as modular sensory and actuation capabilities. We conduct a total of 75 navigation episodes in a two-story indoor space (around 200 m2, Fig. 5b), covering three types of goal-directed tasks: Object-Goal Navigation (OGN), Text-Instance Navigation (TIN), and Image-Instance Navigation (IIN), with five distinct targets per task (Fig. 5c). BSC-Nav demonstrates robust spatial generalization in all real-world tasks. As shown in Fig. 5d, it achieves at least 3 out of 5 successful trials per target (SR is defined as reaching within 1.0 m), with IIN performing best, reaching 100% SR on 4 out of 5 targets. Both OGN and TIN reach 100% SR on two targets and exceed 66.7% SR across all cases. Even in failure cases, BSC-Nav reliably localizes to semantically plausible regions, demonstrating strong spatial awareness. The distance-to-goal distribution in Fig. 5e further confirms the precise stop location, with the final distance below 2.5 m in all cases and tight clustering between targets, particularly for IIN. Navigation efficiency is reflected in a mean velocity of 0.76 m/s with low variance (Fig. 5f), indicating stable and efficient movement. To further demonstrate the advantages of the BSC-Nav architecture in real-world deployments, we conduct an ablation study on the Text Instance Navigation (TIN) task, comparing the full BSC-Nav against a landmark-memory-only variant (i.e., excluding the cognitive map). The configuration of this variant serves a dual critical purpose: (i) it acts as a proxy baseline for existing modular methods that model solely landmark-level spatial knowledge without incorporating dense visual-spatial representations; and (ii) it provides a controlled setting to rigorously analyze the specific contributions of different memory modules in physical environments. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 5c, the landmark-only baseline achieved an overall navigation success rate of merely 52%, substantially lower than that of the full BSC-Nav (78%). This pronounced performance gap arises because the baseline, stripped of the dense visual-spatial features provided by the cognitive map, struggles immensely with text-instance targets that necessitate fine-grained visual disambiguation. Constrained exclusively to category-level semantic matching, the baseline method is forced to resort to inefficient sequential exploration across a series of candidate waypoints. Supplementary Fig. 5d visualizes this candidate region exploration process for the landmark-only variant, and the detailed quantitative raw data are provided in Supplementary Table 9.

Beyond navigation, BSC-Nav’s structured spatial memory supports robust mobile manipulation guided by natural language. Although prior embodied manipulation systems62,63 have shown promise in static, small-scale environments, they often lack the spatial generalization necessary for large-scale deployment. BSC-Nav overcomes this limitation by seamlessly integrating long-horizon navigation with goal-conditioned manipulation. In our demonstrations, natural language instructions are interpreted by GPT-464 to generate waypoint-action sequences. Upon reaching each waypoint, the agent executes a predefined manipulation primitive, such as grasp, place, and pour. Representative examples in Figure 6 show both single-step and multi-step tasks. Notably, the “make breakfast” task (Fig. 6c) involves identifying and interacting with three spatially distributed, open-vocabulary objects, emphasizing the critical role of structured spatial memory in long-horizon reasoning and reliable object grounding. Discussion

This work demonstrates that organizing embodied navigation around high-level principles inspired by biological spatial cognition can be effective in practice. By constructing a structured spatial memory comprising computational counterparts of landmarks, route knowledge, and survey knowledge, BSC-Nav improves navigation performance and extends the spatial capabilities that complement the perceptual and reasoning strengths of modern MLLMs.

Conventional embodied agents often rely on reinforcement learning or imitation learning, acquiring task-specific policies through extensive trial-and-error or teleoperated demonstrations. Here, “embodied” refers to agents that perceive and act in closed-loop interaction with physical or simulated environments, rather than explicitly modeling morphology-dependent cognition in the stronger sense emphasized in embodiment theory65. While effective in controlled settings, prior methods remain fundamentally reactive, responding to immediate stimuli without persistent or reusable spatial knowledge. BSC-Nav addresses this limitation by integrating structured spatial memory with foundation models, enabling a transition from observation-driven pattern matching to multi-level spatial reasoning. BSC-Nav exhibits strong performance across navigation tasks of varying modalities and granularities, especially in open-vocabulary and instance-level settings. These results demonstrate how memory-centric spatial representations enable such agents to decouple planning from immediate perception, reuse prior experience across tasks, and translate high-level goals into concrete actions, which we view as important components of cognitive spatial intelligence. Importantly, BSC-Nav is not purely retrieval-based but also supports active exploration by ranking candidate targets, selectively visiting promising locations, and verifying goals through ongoing interaction with the environment.

BSC-Nav provides a computational framework that operationalizes long-standing theories of spatial knowledge in biological systems16,17. While neuroscience has proposed that spatial cognition builds upon interconnected representations of landmarks, route knowledge, and survey knowledge, most experimental evidence has been behavioral or correlational. BSC-Nav demonstrates how these elements can be synergistically implemented to support long-horizon spatial understanding, planning, and reasoning. The cognitive map module further incorporates a surprise-driven update strategy inspired by the free-energy principle37,38 of representation updating, encouraging stronger incorporation of observations that are less consistent with existing memory. Furthermore, recent findings suggest that sequential route learning drives the formation of allocentric cognitive maps in the hippocampus-entorhinal circuits66, a process with similar ideas to BSC-Nav’s trajectory voxelization. These parallels reveal a convergent computational basis shared between cognitive science and AI.

BSC-Nav is particularly suited to goal-directed embodied operation in partially observable environments, where agents must link semantic goals to persistent spatial structure, revisit previously seen regions, disambiguate candidate targets, and coordinate navigation with downstream interaction. This makes the framework directly relevant not only to service and assistive robotics, but also to embodied question answering and other long-horizon tasks that require accumulated, queryable, and updateable spatial experience. Extending BSC-Nav across environmental scales, however, poses distinct challenges rather than a single scaling problem. In compact environments, the key issue is representational economy, namely avoiding redundant memory growth while maintaining efficient retrieval. In larger or more open-ended environments, the main bottlenecks shift to memory capacity, map consistency, and retrieval efficiency. These observations highlight scalability as an important next consideration for structured spatial memory beyond the benchmark settings studied here.

As embodied AI systems advance in perceptual and motor capabilities, it becomes increasingly important to evaluate not only whether agents can act successfully in physical environments but also how language and spatial cognition interact to support such behavior. In cognitive science, growing evidence suggests that language is not merely a detached symbolic interface but can itself serve as a source of grounding and a mode of action, while spatial knowledge and map-like representations are in turn deeply entangled with language experience67–69. Our results are broadly consistent with this perspective. In BSC-Nav, linguistic goals become most effective when they can be anchored to structured spatial memory, whereas spatial memory becomes more flexible when it can be queried, abstracted, and verified through language-guided reasoning. This interplay suggests that human-like spatial intelligence may depend not only on building internal models of space, but also on coupling them tightly with linguistic representations that support abstraction, disambiguation, and goal specification. From this viewpoint, a future embodied Turing test for spatial cognition70 may need to assess not only navigation accuracy, but also whether agents can construct reusable spatial representations, reason from sparse observations, and translate natural-language goals into grounded spatial plans in ways that remain robust across tasks and environments.

Despite its effectiveness, the present work still has several limitations. First, although BSC-Nav is organized around high-level principles inspired by biological spatial cognition, it is not intended as a cognitively faithful model of human cognition. In particular, the language and visual reasoning components are implemented with modern MLLMs, which are used primarily as engineering modules for semantic grounding, visual interpretation, and high-level reasoning, rather than as biologically or developmentally plausible models of human language processing. Recent psycholinguistic work has likewise emphasized that strong linguistic generalizations learned by neural language models do not by themselves establish cognitively plausible mechanisms, especially for large-scale models trained far beyond human-like data conditions71. From this perspective, BSC-Nav shows that structured spatial memory can provide an effective computational interface between perception, language, and action, rather than offering a mechanistic account of how these functions are jointly realized in the brain. Second, while the framework has clear conceptual implications for memory-centric embodied agents, its theoretical contribution remains primarily architectural and computational. More formal analyses of when and why structured spatial memory improves generalization, efficiency, and language-guided behavior remain important directions for future work.

Several future directions emerge from this work. Extending BSC-Nav to dynamic and unstructured environments will require spatial memory that can be continually revised as scenes evolve over time. Scaling to larger environments will in turn demand more efficient memory organization, including hierarchical abstraction, compact storage, and retrieval with bounded latency. Richer embodied settings may further benefit from integrating additional sensory streams, such as depth, audio, or tactile signals, together with uncertainty-aware updating. Beyond single-agent navigation, future systems should also support collaborative and multi-agent spatial reasoning, where memory can be shared, communicated, or jointly refined. More broadly, an important longer-term direction is to explore architectures in which memory, perception, and language are developed under stronger constraints of embodiment, experience, and computational efficiency. Progress along these directions may help narrow the gap between high-performing engineering systems and more cognitively grounded models of spatial intelligence.

Methods

The BSC-Nav framework

This section describes the implementation details of BSC-Nav, including the three synergistic modules (Fig. 1b) and the pipeline of constructing and leveraging structured spatial memory (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Observation space and pre-processing

The observation space of BSC-Nav is defined as Ot={It,Dt,Pt}, comprising the RGB image It∈RH×W×3, the depth image Dt∈Rw×h and the pose of the agent Pt=(Xt,Yt,ϕta)∈R3 at time t, where Xt and Yt denote the agent’s 2D position in the world coordinate system (defaulting to the Cartesian coordinate centered at the SLAM72 initialization point), ϕt is the angle of yaw, and H and W are image width and height, respectively. For simplicity, we omit the time subscript t in subsequent descriptions. RGB images serve as input for both salient object detection and visual feature extraction, while depth images and pose information enable projection from pixel coordinates to the world coordinate system.

In a simulation environment, the agent receives RGB images at a resolution of 680 × 680 pixels with the depth image aligned to the same frame, and its pose is obtained directly from the simulator’s ground-truth odometry. In real-world deployment, BSC-Nav acquires 848 × 480 RGB-D observations at 30 FPS from an Intel RealSense D435i depth camera mounted at 1.5 m above ground level. The agent’s pose is estimated in real time via 32-beam LiDAR-based SLAM. To mitigate sensing artifacts such as depth holes and edge discontinuities, the raw depth stream in the real-world is processed through a cascaded filtering pipeline, comprising threshold filtering (valid range [0.3, 8.0] m), spatio-temporal smoothing, and hole-filling filters.

In Mcog, we avoid redundantly storing all visual features from new observations, which would lead to information overload and inefficient retrieval. We also forgo conventional fusion methods, such as grid averaging or distance-based weighting31,74, which often introduce bias in visual feature representation. Instead, inspired by biological learning and memory, we maintain dynamic buffers for each voxel and introduce a surprise-based update strategy. Free-energy37,38 and predictive-processing39,40 accounts suggest that biological systems update internal models in response to mismatches between observation and expectation. Motivated by this broad perspective, BSC-Nav updates its cognitive map according to the degree of deviation between new observations and existing memory representations within a given spatial region, quantified as a heuristic surprise score. This score is used as an engineering criterion for selective memory updating and is not intended as a formal variational free-energy objective41. where Nn(v)={v′∈Z3:∥v−v′∥∞≤n} denotes the n-hop Chebyshev neighborhood around voxel v, FNn(v)=⋃v′∈Nn(v)Fv′ represents the union of all feature buffers in the neighborhood, and D(⋅,⋅) is the distance metric (e.g., cosine distance). We set n = 1 as each voxel’s surprise is computed against features stored in its 33 − 1 = 26 adjacent voxels (1-hop Chebyshev neighborhood). When S(fnew,v)>τ (the surprise threshold), fnew is added to buffer Fv. Since D is instantiated as the cosine distance between L2-normalized DINOv2 features, the computed surprise score S is strictly bounded within [0, 1]. We select τ = 0.5 as the natural midpoint of this spectrum, which ensures that a new feature is incorporated only when its average divergence from existing neighborhood representations outweighs its similarity. Adopting a lower threshold would admit near-redundant features and needlessly inflate memory without enhancing representational diversity. Conversely, a higher threshold risks discarding observations from moderately novel viewpoints, ultimately compromising the multi-perspective coverage of the cognitive map.

If ∣Fv∣=B, the feature with the highest cosine similarity to fnew (i.e., the most redundant entry) within the buffer is replaced to maintain memory efficiency. In addition, a periodic deduplication step is applied: features within each voxel whose pairwise cosine similarity exceeds 0.95 are grouped via union-find clustering and merged by averaging, thereby compacting redundant encodings accumulated from overlapping viewpoints over time. This surprise-based update strategy achieves two key advantages: (i) it enhances the robustness of spatial knowledge by selectively caching diverse features across viewpoints and timepoints in adapting to dynamic environments; and (ii) it maintains memory storage and retrieval efficiency by avoiding redundant encoding of stable environmental elements.

Working memory

The working memory module is responsible for the hierarchical retrieval and strategic reorganization of spatial knowledge from both the landmark memory Mlandmark and the cognitive map Mcog, enabling BSC-Nav to address navigation tasks of varying modalities and granularities (Fig. 2b). Unlike the parallel construction and passive updating of the two memory branches, the working memory is activated only upon receiving navigation instructions. It employs a hierarchical retrieval strategy guided by instruction complexity. For simple and concrete goals, it prioritizes the fast retrieval of landmark memory. For fine-grained or image-based instructions, it further engages the cognitive map for precise visual-spatial localization (Fig. 2c).

The structured knowledge base of landmark memory, including landmark categories, confidence scores, and contextual descriptions, provides a strong foundation for MLLMs to reason about target locations. Unlike direct rule matching, this approach enables context-aware reasoning and can infer the location of unrecorded targets based on spatial associations. For example, even if a “toaster” is not explicitly recorded, the system can infer its location by leveraging co-located landmarks such as “stove” and “kitchen island”. Specifically, we design retrieval prompts (see prompt templates in Supplementary Sec. A) that guide a text-only GPT-4 to integrate confidence scores and descriptive semantics to generate a set of candidate coordinates {θcandi}i=1K from Mlandmark.

To bridge the modality gap between textual instructions and visual representations, we perform association-enhanced retrieval over the cognitive map. It first employs text-only GPT-4o to refine goal descriptions, enriching them with detailed texture and spatial context information. Low-level navigation policy generation

Low-level navigation policies are typically derived from deterministic path planning conditioned on environmental constraints and high-level spatial goals52. BSC-Nav employs a hierarchical navigation strategy: the exploration sequence of each candidate coordinate serves as a high-level policy, while heuristic algorithms are used to generate actionable low-level policies toward each candidate coordinate. In simulation environments, we employ the greedy shortest path algorithm provided by the Habitat simulator46, which operates directly on 3D mesh representations of the scene to compute optimal action sequences within a discrete action space. For real-world deployment, we implement a two-tier planning architecture. Global planning is carried out using the A* algorithm77 on occupancy grid maps constructed via LiDAR-based SLAM, yielding globally optimal paths. Local planning leverages the Timed Elastic Band (TEB) algorithm78,79, which dynamically adjusts trajectories to avoid obstacles while maintaining efficiency. The TEB planner outputs continuous velocity commands to control the robot chassis, ensuring smooth and precise motion execution.

Goal verification and affordance

Upon arriving at a candidate coordinate, BSC-Nav verifies whether the navigation target is present at the location. The robot first performs a 360∘ rotational scan to capture a sequence of RGB images. Cosine similarities are then computed between the CLIP visual embeddings of these images and the goal’s text or visual embeddings to identify the viewing angle with the highest semantic alignment. The selected image is subsequently fed into GPT-4o for precise target verification. Beyond confirming target presence, GPT-4o is also prompted to generate a sequence of affordance-based actions to guide the robot in fine-tuning its pose, adjusting its relative position, and orientation for optimal proximity and visibility. This ensures favorable initial conditions for downstream manipulation tasks.

Hardware stack

We implement BSC-Nav on a physical robotic platform for real-world deployment, comprising five core components: locomotion chassis, industrial computer, SLAM suite, robotic arm, and vision sensors.

The locomotion system employs the Agilex Ranger-mini 3.0 platform, chosen for its balance between cost and payload capacity. It features Ackermann steering with zero-radius turning capability, enabling agile maneuvering in complex environments. The SLAM suite integrates a 32-beam LiDAR, IMU sensor, and depth-compensation camera to support collision detection and real-world localization. For manipulation capabilities, we equipped the platform with a Franka Emika Research 3 robotic arm. Two Intel RealSense D435i cameras serve as primary vision sensors (one mounted at 1.5 m above ground level and another at the manipulator’s end effector), providing 848 × 480 RGB-D imagery with an 87∘ field of view. To mitigate depth-sensing artifacts including holes and edge discontinuities, we apply spatio-temporal and hole-filling filters, constraining the effective sensing range to 0.3-8.0 m. An industrial-grade computer equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU handles all real-time processing, including BSC-Nav, SLAM computation, and manipulator control, ensuring end-to-end system responsiveness.

Simulation environments

Before task execution, BSC-Nav requires environmental perception to construct preliminary landmark memory and cognitive map representations. For simulation environments, we implement a frontier-based52 autonomous exploration strategy for spatial memory construction: at each time step, the system generates a height map through depth projection to identify boundaries between explored and unexplored regions. On traversable boundaries, the system selects the nearest frontier point to the current position as the next exploration target, utilizing Habitat’s scene-mesh-based greedy navigator for low-level action planning. Upon reaching each frontier point, the agent performs a 360-degree rotation to comprehensively perceive the surrounding environment. This exploration process continues until a predetermined iteration limit is reached, which is dynamically adjusted based on scene scale. The iteration count is defined as half of the traversable area.

During task execution, both landmark memory and cognitive map maintain continuous updates. Parameter configurations are as follows: for landmark memory, the detector confidence threshold is set to 0.55, a standard operating point for YOLO-World on indoor datasets that balances precision against recall for salient object retention, with a spatial overlap distance of 1.0 m, chosen to match the navigation success radius so that landmarks within one success zone are merged as a single instance.

For the cognitive map, we set a voxel resolution of δ = 0.1 m3, a grid dimension of G = 1000, and a buffer capacity of B = 10 per voxel. The grid configuration yields a 100 × 100 m2 spatial coverage that exceeds the largest indoor scenes in HM3D and MP3D (typically < 80 m span), while Δ = 0.1 m affords object-scale spatial resolution. The per-voxel buffer capacity B = 10 empirically balances feature diversity against memory footprint: with 1024-dimensional features, the worst-case working set remains within the 24 GB VRAM budget of our deployment GPU.

For each navigation episode, the system first retrieves candidate target locations through the working memory module, with a maximum of K = 3 candidates for landmark memory retrieval and Q = 3 for cognitive map retrieval. Our empirical analysis shows that the majority of successful episodes locate the target at the first explored candidate, and beyond K, Q = 3 the cumulative success rate plateaus while path efficiency (SPL) steadily declines (Supplementary Fig. 3b,c), indicating diminishing returns in efficacy at the cost of exploration efficiency. We employ Stable Diffusion 3.5-Medium for cognitive map retrieval, generating three images per batch to mitigate stochasticity in visual imagination. The system subsequently navigates to each candidate location following the planned exploration sequence, performing target verification upon arrival at each candidate. Task success is determined when at least one candidate passes verification. If all candidates fail verification, the task is deemed unsuccessful. In simulation environments, the agent operates in a discrete action space comprising forward motion of 0.25 m and left/right turns of 30∘, with a maximum of 5,000 steps per episode and a success distance threshold of 1.0 m. The effective depth sensing range is [0.1, 10.0] m. These parameter settings follow the choices made by prior works during benchmark evaluations.

Real-world deployment

For real-world deployment, the initial phase of spatial memory construction relies on manual teleoperation, guided by two primary considerations. Firstly, strict safety constraints necessitate human oversight; unlike simulated environments, autonomous exploration (e.g., frontier-based methods) often struggles to navigate complex physical hazards safely in unconstrained settings. Given our physical environment features stairs, numerous glass doors and walls, and frequent human activity, manual guidance mitigates significant risks to both the robot and its payload. Secondly, employing manual exploration isolates our core contribution, i.e., the structured organization and retrieval of spatial memory, from the inherent failure rates of underlying exploration algorithms. During this pre-collection phase, an operator guides the robot through the environment at a walking pace, while the perception pipeline records RGB-D observations and builds the cognitive map and landmark memory in real time. During the subsequent task execution phase, BSC-Nav operates autonomously. Since a simulator mesh does not bound the physical environment, several adaptations are required relative to the simulation configuration as follows.

The robot relies on LiDAR-based SLAM for real-time localization within a pre-built 2D occupancy grid map. At the start of each experiment, we must manually specify the robot’s initial pose by providing approximate pixel coordinates (x, y, θ) on the occupancy grid map. These pixel coordinates are converted to metric map coordinates via the map’s resolution and origin parameters and published to the SLAM system as the initial pose estimate.

Several parameter adjustments are adopted to accommodate the physical sensing conditions. The detection confidence threshold is lowered to 0.4 to compensate for increased visual noise, motion blur, and lighting variability in uncontrolled environments, and the predefined landmark category set is reduced from 21 to 16. The voxel grid is configured with Δ = 0.2 m and G = 2000. The robot executes continuous velocity commands at a nominal linear speed of 0.2 m/s and an angular speed of 0.3 rad/s. A goal is considered reached when the agent’s position remains within a 0.5 m threshold for a stability duration of 5.0 s, with a per-goal navigation timeout of 300 s. The comprehensive hyperparameter configurations are provided in Supplementary Tables 12 and 13.

We identify several failure modes observed during physical deployment: (i) Impassable regions. The local planner inflates obstacles on the occupancy grid by a safety margin to prevent collisions. In narrow passages or cluttered areas, the inflated costmap from the SLAM system may mark the entire passageway as non-traversable, causing the robot to become stuck or to abort the navigation goal due to a timeout. In such cases, the system proceeds to the next candidate location. If no candidates remain reachable, the episode is recorded as a failure. (ii) Calibration drift. Inaccurate initial pose calibration or accumulated SLAM drift over long trajectories can cause a systematic offset between the robot’s estimated position and the true coordinates stored in spatial memory. This misalignment degrades both candidate retrieval accuracy and goal verification, as the robot may arrive at a location displaced from the intended memory coordinate. (iii) Dynamic environmental disturbances. Large-scale environmental changes between memory construction and task execution, such as rearranged furniture, newly placed obstacles, or dense pedestrian traffic, can invalidate stored spatial memory and obstruct planned paths. When a navigation goal is persistently unreachable due to dynamic obstacles (e.g., sustained pedestrian flow blocking a corridor), the system times out, and the episode requires manual intervention to re-initialize the robot’s position and re-attempt the task.

Computational footprint

We present the computational requirements and real-time performance of BSC-Nav in both simulated and physical deployments. The minimum hardware requirement is a GPU with at least 24 GB of VRAM, primarily allocated for the real-time inference of DINOv2, YOLO-World, and Stable Diffusion 3.5 Medium, alongside 16 GB of system RAM dedicated to 3D coordinate projection and voxelized memory operations. Such resource configurations are readily satisfied by current-generation embodied computing platforms.

In simulated environments (primarily Habitat-sim), the system executes approximately 10 spatial memory update cycles per second during exploration. Scene-scale exploration typically requires 5 to 10 minutes, depending on the navigable area. BSC-Nav operates at approximately 10 Hz during task execution in the Habitat simulator. LLMs and MLLMs (GPT-4, GPT-4o, and o3-mini) are invoked exclusively for high-level planning tasks, such as candidate waypoint selection, target association augmentation, and instruction decomposition. These API-based calls are executed asynchronously at a low frequency, ensuring they neither exhaust local computational resources nor impede real-time low-level control due to network latency.

In our physical deployment, BSC-Nav employs a decoupled high- and low-frequency system architecture to ensure real-time responsiveness. High-frequency low-level control ( > 30 Hz), encompassing chassis motion control, obstacle avoidance, and real-time localization via LiDAR-based SLAM, operates directly on the robot’s edge computing unit. Low-to-medium-frequency perception and spatial memory updates ( ~ 10 Hz), including visual feature extraction (DINOv2 and YOLO-World), coordinate projection, and cognitive map updates, are offloaded to an industrial host PC (Fig. 5a). During task execution, the robot navigates at an average speed of 0.76 m/s, governed by heuristic path planning and real-time motion control (Fig. 5f). Real-time (1 × speed) navigation demonstrations on the physical robot are provided in Supplementary Movies 3–7.

Foundation model specifications

BSC-Nav seamlessly integrates multiple foundation models across its perception, reasoning, and generation stages. For visual feature extraction within the cognitive map module, we employ DINOv2 with registers (ViT-L/14)34,35, a self-supervised vision transformer that yields 1,024-dimensional patch-level features. For open-vocabulary object detection in the landmark memory module, we utilize YOLO-World-X (YOLOv8 Extra-Large, WorldV2)73 with a confidence threshold of 0.55 across 20 predefined object categories. For text-image semantic matching during target verification, we adopt CLIP (ViT-H/14, pre-trained with MetaCLIP FullCC)83. For visual imagination in association-augmented retrieval, we leverage Stable Diffusion 3.5 Medium43, generating images at a 512 × 512 resolution using 28 inference steps, a guidance scale of 7.0, and a maximum sequence length of 512 tokens. To drastically reduce GPU memory consumption, the diffusion model is quantized to 4-bit NormalFloat (NF4) and evaluated with bfloat16 precision, with CPU offloading.

For high-level semantic reasoning and planning, BSC-Nav employs three OpenAI models accessed via API, all utilizing default inference parameters (temperature = 1.0, top-p = 1.0): GPT-4 (gpt-4-0613) for landmark memory retrieval and spatial reasoning; GPT-4o (gpt-4o-2024-11-20)42 for scene understanding, target verification, and description augmentation; and o3-mini57 for complex instruction decomposition in long-horizon navigation tasks. As these are closed-source proprietary models, their precise architectural details, parameter counts, and training data compositions remain undisclosed by OpenAI. To strictly facilitate reproducibility, we report the exact model version identifiers above, and all prompt templates are provided in Supplementary Sec. A and in our code repository.

Baseline methods and comparison

End-to-end navigation methods leverage deep neural networks to directly map egocentric observations to action sequences, implicitly encoding spatial-geometric priors and semantic knowledge within network parameters. While they streamline the decision-making process, they must simultaneously learn spatial memory tracking and action planning, necessitating extensive trajectory data or expert demonstrations for effective training. For instance, PixNav 11 performs greedy navigation by predicting optimal actions toward salient pixels in the current view. DAgRL 49 and PSL 50 integrate pre-trained vision-language encoders with historical action embeddings or contrastive learning to align visual observations with textual goals. Despite their strong performance in simulated environments, these methods are highly susceptible to data distribution shifts between training and testing environments, severely impairing their generalization to real-world physical deployments. Recent vision-language-action models 12,84,85 attempt to address these limitations by scaling model capacity and diversifying training trajectories. Uni-NavId exemplifies this trend, achieving robust performance in parsing complex multi-modal instructions. However, these large-scale models incur significant computational overhead, restrict action generation frequency, and fundamentally lack mechanisms for persistent, structured spatial memory integration. Unlike these frameworks, BSC-Nav leverages the explicit retrieval mechanism of its working memory to achieve a complete decoupling of high-level semantic goal planning from low-level physical motion control, thereby paving the way toward genuine zero-shot, general-purpose embodied navigation.

Modular and map-based navigation methods represent a growing class of approaches that construct explicit spatial representations to support long-horizon planning and mitigate sim-to-real gaps. The necessity of internal spatial representation has been recognized since early computational models, such as the RNN-based dynamic forward models of Tani 86, which achieved sequential planning and symbol grounding without explicit global coordinates. However, limited by the perceptual technologies of their era, such models could not scale to semantically rich, large-scale scenarios. Driven by advances in foundation models, recent methods have increasingly adopted explicit memory structures. For example, GOAT36 and MOD-IIN54 project closed-set semantics or visual features onto top-down maps, while VLFM13 and UniGoal33 construct probabilistic frontier maps or abstract graph-based representations. More recently, GridMM30 builds a top-down dense grid memory map that aggregates fine-grained visual features via cross-modal attention (essentially modeling localized route knowledge), whereas ETPNav32 dynamically evolves sparse topological nodes in continuous environments (leaning towards a reasoning-capable landmark representation).

While these methods advance beyond purely reactive processing, they predominantly suffer from incomplete spatial memory by relying on a single-granularity representation, either purely dense grids or purely sparse topologies. Furthermore, they heavily depend on task-specifically trained cross-modal Transformers or policies for memory attention and planning, which inherently constrain their generalization to unseen environments. BSC-Nav fundamentally distinguishes itself in both representational granularity and retrieval paradigms. It operationalizes a brain-inspired tripartite decomposition, preventing the semantic blurring typical of monolithic integration. Moreover, by introducing a transparent working memory interface equipped with visual imagination and MLLM-based logical reasoning, BSC-Nav replaces black-box policy attention with explicit, zero-shot inference. This allows the agent to flexibly align complex semantic intents with accumulated spatial structures without relying on heavy instruction-level fine-tuning, achieving robust spatial cognition across tasks and environments.

The repository is organized into two branches: the sim-branch contains simulation implementations, benchmark configurations, and evaluation scripts; the phy-branch includes low-level control interfaces adapted for our robotic platform and BSC-Nav deployment scripts.
