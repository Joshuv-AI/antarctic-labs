Lower-neck involvement and post–concurrent chemoradiotherapy response predict distant metastasis in head and neck cancer
Authors: Dongchan Kim, Donghyun Kim, Wontaek Kim, Jiho Nam, Dahl Park, Yongkan Ki, Jihyeon Joo, Kyeonghyo Lee, Sangil Pak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate whether lower-neck involvement and post–concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) response predict distant metastasis (DM) in patients with head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) treated with definitive intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT)–based CCRT.Materials and MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 210 patients with non-metastatic HNSCC treated with definitive IMRT-based CCRT between 2009 and 2020. Lower-neck involvement was defined as radiologic involvement of inferior cervical lymph nodes. Post-treatment response was categorized as complete response (CR) versus non-CR. Logistic and Cox regression models were used to identify predictors of DM and survival outcomes.ResultsAt a median follow-up of 59.8 months, DM developed in 51 patients (24.3%). In multivariable logistic analysis, lower-neck involvement (odds ratio [OR], 2.32; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.18 to 4.54; p = 0.014) and non-CR (OR, 2.57; 95% CI, 1.31 to 5.05; p = 0.006) independently predicted DM. In Cox analyses, lower-neck involvement was associated with inferior distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) (hazard ratio [HR], 2.07; 95% CI, 1.15 to 3.75; p = 0.016) and progression-free survival (PFS) (HR, 1.93; 95% CI, 1.19 to 3.14; p = 0.008), whereas non-CR predicted worse DMFS (HR, 2.71; 95% CI, 1.49 to 4.95; p = 0.001), PFS (HR, 3.77; 95% CI, 2.25 to 6.33; p < 0.001), and overall survival (HR, 5.08; 95% CI, 1.94 to 13.34; p < 0.001).ConclusionLower-neck involvement and post-CCRT response independently predict DM after definitive CCRT for HNSCC and may improve risk stratification for systemic failure.

Introduction

Head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) remains a major cause of cancer-related morbidity and mortality worldwide. For locally advanced disease, definitive concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) is the standard treatment based on randomized trials demonstrating improved outcomes compared with radiotherapy (RT) alone [1-3]. With the widespread adoption of intensity-modulated radiotherapy (IMRT), locoregional control has improved substantially. Despite advances in multimodality treatment and IMRT, distant metastasis (DM) remains a leading cause of treatment failure in HNSCC [4,5].

To reduce systemic dissemination, induction chemotherapy (IC) was explored, and docetaxel-based regimens improved response rates in randomized trials [6,7]. However, subsequent studies have not consistently demonstrated a durable survival benefit or a clear reduction in DM with IC prior to CCRT [8,9]. Accordingly, evaluation of patients treated with definitive IMRT-based CCRT without IC provides a clearer framework for assessing intrinsic risk factors for DM.

Cervical lymph node (LN) involvement is a well-established risk factor for DM. Both advanced nodal stage and the anatomic distribution of metastatic nodes have been associated with increased systemic risk [10-14]. In particular, involvement of the lower neck (level IV, level Vb, and supraclavicular regions) may reflect biologically advanced regional spread. Given the proximity of these nodal stations to major lymphatic and venous drainage pathways, lower-neck involvement may serve as a clinically identifiable marker of systemic access rather than simply indicating nodal stage.

In addition, HNSCC demonstrates preferential metastatic involvement of specific organs, most commonly the lung, followed by bone and liver [7,9]. However, the clinical implications of site-specific distribution in homogeneous IMRT-treated cohorts remain unclear. Furthermore, early post-treatment response may reflect intrinsic tumor biology and could be linked to subsequent systemic dissemination.

Accordingly, we aimed to evaluate DM in a homogeneous cohort of patients with HNSCC treated with definitive IMRT-based CCRT without IC. Specifically, we examined the association between lower-neck LN involvement and the development of DM, characterized patterns of metastatic dissemination including organ-specific and multi-site involvement within the cohort, and assessed the prognostic significance of early post-treatment response as a marker of systemic risk.

Materials and Methods

1. Study design and patient population

This retrospective cohort study included patients with HNSCC who underwent definitive CCRT at a university-affiliated tertiary medical center (institution name withheld for peer review) between January 2009 and December 2020. A total of 210 consecutive patients meeting the eligibility criteria were included in the final analysis.

Patients were eligible if they had histologically confirmed squamous cell carcinoma, no evidence of DM (M0) on pre-treatment computed tomography (CT)/magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT), received definitive IMRT-based CCRT delivering 66–70 Gy in conventional fractions, and had availability of at least 6 months of clinical follow-up. Patients were excluded if they underwent adjuvant RT following upfront surgery, or salvage RT for recurrent disease, evidence of DM before initiation of CCRT, non-squamous histology (e.g., adenocarcinoma, salivary gland carcinoma, sinonasal malignancies), or failure to complete planned definitive CCRT.

2. Imaging evaluation and staging

All patients underwent baseline staging with neck CT or MRI and chest CT, with PET-CT performed when clinically indicated. Tumor staging was reassigned according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system [15]. Nasopharyngeal carcinoma was staged according to the nasopharynx-specific system. For all other subsites, including oropharyngeal cancer, the non–p16-based staging system was uniformly applied regardless of biomarker status to maintain consistency across the cohort. Human papillomavirus (HPV; p16) status was assessed when available; however, due to incomplete data, it was not incorporated into the multivariable analysis.

Follow-up evaluations were generally performed every 3–6 months during the first 2 years and at longer intervals thereafter. Imaging studies, including neck CT or MRI and chest CT, were performed at regular intervals or when clinically indicated. PET-CT was not routinely performed but was used selectively based on clinical suspicion.

3. Clinical and treatment characteristics

All patients received definitive CCRT. RT was delivered primarily using IMRT, predominantly with Tomotherapy systems; a small proportion of earlier cases were treated with conventional step-and-shoot IMRT. The prescribed RT regimen consisted of daily fractions of 2.0–2.2 Gy, administered five days per week, to a total dose of 66–70 Gy. Target volume delineation followed standard institutional and international guidelines and included the gross tumor volume, clinical target volume, and planning target volume. Elective nodal regions and anatomically contiguous areas at risk were treated with 54–60 Gy, depending on clinical stage, radiologic extent, and anatomical considerations. CCRT consisted of cisplatin-based regimens administered during the RT course. Most patients received weekly cisplatin at a dose of 40 mg/m², while a minority were treated with high-dose cisplatin (100 mg/m²) administered every three weeks. Only chemotherapy delivered during the definitive CCRT course was considered in the analysis; systemic therapies administered after disease progression for salvage or palliative purposes were not included in the analysis. For clinical variable definition, cervical LN involvement was assessed based on pretreatment imaging. Radiologic nodal involvement was determined by size and morphologic criteria on CT/MRI and/or focal fluorodeoxyglucose uptake on PET-CT, as interpreted by dedicated head and neck radiologists. The lower neck was anatomically defined as level IV, level Vb, and supraclavicular LN regions. Lower-neck involvement was considered present when pretreatment CT, MRI, or PET-CT demonstrated radiologically abnormal lymphadenopathy or definite nodal tumor infiltration in these regions. This definition was applied irrespective of overall N stage.

4. Treatment-related time factor

To assess the impact of treatment duration on outcomes, overall treatment time (OTT) was evaluated. OTT was defined as the total elapsed time from the start to the completion of RT. Based on radiobiological evidence indicating accelerated tumor repopulation after approximately 4 weeks and an increased risk of locoregional control deterioration with treatment durations exceeding 7–8 weeks (49–56 days) [16,17], an OTT >8 weeks (56 days) was classified as prolonged.

5. Response assessment

Treatment response was evaluated using CT, MRI, or PET-CT performed after completion of RT. The timing of the first response assessment was defined as the interval between the first post-treatment simulation CT and the earliest subsequent imaging study, regardless of imaging modality. In cases where multiple simulation CT scans were performed, the first simulation CT was used as the reference time point.

For analytic purposes, response was dichotomized into complete response (CR) versus non-CR. CR was defined as the absence of radiologic evidence of residual disease at the primary site and regional lymph nodes on post-treatment imaging; all other responses were classified as non-CR. Post-treatment response was treated as an early clinical marker for subsequent systemic failure in multivariable survival analyses.

6. Follow-up and outcome definitions

Patients were followed every 1–3 months during the first two years and every 6–12 months thereafter. Follow-up evaluations included neck CT or MRI and chest CT. Abdominal imaging (CT or ultrasonography) and PET-CT were performed when clinically indicated or when DM was suspected. DM was defined as radiologic or pathologic evidence of metastatic disease beyond the locoregional extent of the primary tumor and regional lymphatics [16]. Distant metastasis-free survival (DMFS) was defined as the time from initiation of RT to the first occurrence of DM. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from RT initiation to locoregional recurrence, DM, or death from any cause. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from RT initiation to death from any cause. Site-specific DM incidence was defined as the occurrence of metastatic involvement at each organ site at any time during follow-up, regardless of whether multiple sites were involved. Local and regional recurrences were assessed independently; thus, patients could contribute more than one recurrence category. Locoregional recurrence was defined as recurrence involving both the primary site and regional LNs, either simultaneously or sequentially, during follow-up.

7. Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables were summarized as medians with ranges. Univariable logistic regression analyses were performed to explore factors associated with DM as a binary outcome. Variables deemed clinically relevant or showing p < 0.10 in univariable analyses were entered into multivariable logistic regression models. To minimize overfitting, a multivariable model including a limited number of clinically relevant variables was constructed. Given the potential collinearity between N stage and lower-neck involvement, an exploratory model incorporating N stage was additionally evaluated to assess their independent contributions. Odds ratios (ORs) and hazard ratios (HRs) were reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Site-specific DM incidence was analyzed within the overall cohort based on whether metastatic involvement at each organ site occurred at any time during follow-up. Patients with metastases at multiple sites were allowed to contribute to more than one organ-specific category. DMFS, PFS, and OS were estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared using the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression models were constructed to evaluate independent prognostic factors for time-to-event outcomes, incorporating clinically relevant covariates. Only patients with complete data for variables included in each model were analyzed. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using Python 3.10 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics and treatment parameters are summarized in Table 1. The median age was 63 years (range, 20 to 82 years), and 89.5% of patients were male. The most common primary sites were nasopharynx (27.6%), oropharynx (24.8%), and larynx (24.8%). Advanced-stage disease was prevalent: 37.6% of patients had T3–4 tumors, 49.5% had N2–3 disease, and 50.0% were classified as AJCC stage IVA–IVB. Lower-neck LN involvement was present in 39.5% of patients. Weekly cisplatin (40 mg/m²) was administered to 81.9% of patients, whereas 18.1% received high-dose cisplatin every 3 weeks. A cumulative cisplatin dose ≥200 mg/m² was achieved in 93.8% of patients. The median OTT was 51 days (range, 41 to 73 days), and OTT >8 weeks occurred in 24.3% of patients. Following CCRT, 126 patients (60.0%) achieved a CR, whereas 84 (40.0%) had a non-CR. The median time to first response assessment after CCRT was 19.9 weeks (range, 5.9 to 93.4 weeks). The median follow-up duration was 59.8 months. During follow-up, local recurrence occurred in 30 patients (14.3%), regional recurrence in 34 (16.2%), and DM in 51 (24.3%). To identify baseline factors associated with overall DM occurrence, univariable logistic regression analysis (Table 2) showed that N stage (N2–3 vs. N0–1; OR, 2.85; 95% CI, 1.46 to 5.57; p = 0.002), lower-neck LN involvement (OR, 2.86; 95% CI, 1.49 to 5.46; p = 0.001), and post-CCRT response (non-CR vs. CR; OR, 3.10; 95% CI, 1.62 to 5.95; p < 0.001) were significantly associated with increased odds of DM, whereas OTT >8 weeks showed a borderline association (OR, 1.84; 95% CI, 0.92 to 3.68; p = 0.086). In the multivariable model, only lower-neck LN involvement (OR, 2.32; 95% CI, 1.18 to 4.54; p = 0.014) and post-CCRT response (OR, 2.57; 95% CI, 1.31 to 5.05; p = 0.006) remained independently associated with DM.

Overall, the lung was the most common site of DM (27.7% in LN+ vs. 14.2% in LN−), followed by bone (12.0% vs. 2.4%) and liver (9.6% vs. 3.9%), whereas brain and other sites were relatively uncommon (Table 3). As shown in Table 3, DM occurred in 16.5% of patients without lower-neck involvement (LN–) and 36.1% of those with lower-neck involvement (LN+) (OR, 2.86; 95% CI, 1.49 to 5.47; p = 0.002). Lung metastasis developed in 14.2% of LN– patients and 27.7% of LN+ patients (OR, 2.32; 95% CI, 1.16 to 4.62; p = 0.020), and bone metastasis in 2.4% and 12.0%, respectively (OR, 5.66; 95% CI, 1.50 to 21.20; p = 0.007). Differences in liver metastasis (3.9% vs. 9.6%, p = 0.141), brain metastasis (1.6% vs. 1.2%, p > 0.99), pleural or mediastinal metastasis (1.6% vs. 6.0%, p = 0.116), distant LN metastasis (2.4% vs. 7.2%, p = 0.159), and other sites (0.8% vs. 3.6%, p = 0.303) were not statistically significant. Multi-site metastasis occurred more frequently in lower neck LN+ patients (18.1% vs. 6.3%; OR, 3.28; 95% CI, 1.32 to 8.17; p = 0.012).

Kaplan-Meier analysis demonstrated significantly inferior DMFS and PFS in patients with lower-neck involvement compared with those without (both log-rank p < 0.001), whereas the difference in OS did not reach statistical significance (log-rank p = 0.068) (Fig. 1).

In univariable Cox regression analyses (Table 4), lower-neck LN involvement and post-CCRT non-CR status were significantly associated with inferior DMFS and PFS, while post-CCRT non-CR status was also strongly associated with worse OS. T stage (T3–4 vs. T1–2) was associated with inferior PFS but did not show a significant association with DMFS or OS. In multivariable Cox regression analysis incorporating post-CCRT response (Table 4), lower-neck LN involvement independently predicted inferior DMFS (HR, 2.07; 95% CI, 1.15 to 3.75; p = 0.016) and PFS (HR, 1.93; 95% CI, 1.19 to 3.14; p = 0.008), but not OS (HR, 1.64; 95% CI, 0.70 to 3.81; p = 0.253). In contrast, non-CR status was strongly associated with worse outcomes across all endpoints, including DMFS (HR, 2.71; 95% CI, 1.49 to 4.95; p = 0.001), PFS (HR, 3.77; 95% CI, 2.25 to 6.33; p < 0.001), and OS (HR, 5.08; 95% CI, 1.94 to 13.34; p < 0.001). OTT >8 weeks was not significantly associated with DMFS, PFS, or OS.

Discussion and Conclusion

In this study, we evaluated DM in a homogeneous cohort of patients with HNSCC treated with definitive IMRT-based CCRT without IC. Three principal findings emerged. First, DM remained a major mode of failure in the contemporary IMRT era. Second, lower-neck LN involvement was independently associated with increased odds of DM and inferior DMFS and PFS. Third, non-CR after CCRT was strongly associated with inferior DMFS, PFS, and OS. Notably, when post-CCRT response was incorporated into survival models, the association between lower-neck involvement and OS was attenuated (Table 4).

Despite favorable overall outcomes, with a 5-year OS of 88.5% (Fig. 1), DM occurred in 24.3% of patients (Table 1) and was consistently associated with inferior survival endpoints. These findings are consistent with prior IMRT-era data demonstrating that improvements in locoregional control have not eliminated systemic relapse as a major determinant of long-term outcome [9,10]. Favorable OS in this cohort may be partly explained by improved locoregional control with IMRT and the potential for effective salvage treatment. In addition, cohort characteristics and follow-up structure, including patient selection and censoring, may have contributed to the relatively high observed survival rates. Five-year OS also varied across subsites, with relatively favorable outcomes in nasopharyngeal carcinoma (93.1%) and oropharyngeal cancer (92.3%) compared to hypopharyngeal cancer (78.0%). These differences likely reflect distinct tumor biology, including greater radiosensitivity and more favorable treatment response, particularly in HPV-associated oropharyngeal cancer.

Lower-neck LN involvement emerged as a robust marker of systemic risk. In multivariable analyses, inferior nodal extension remained significantly associated with DM and adverse time-to-event outcomes (Table 4). When N stage was included in the model, the magnitude of association decreased. This finding suggests partial biological overlap between overall nodal burden and inferior nodal extension, consistent with prior studies linking nodal extent and distribution to systemic dissemination [11-14]. To further explore this relationship, we performed analyses stratified by N stage. In N-stage–stratified analyses, lower-neck involvement showed a consistent direction of association with adverse outcomes across subgroups. The effect was more pronounced in patients with N2–3 disease, in whom lower-neck involvement remained significantly associated with both DM (OR, 2.81; 95% CI, 1.15 to 6.85; p = 0.024) and PFS (HR, 2.59; 95% CI, 1.31 to 5.12; p = 0.006). In contrast, in patients with N0–1 disease, a similar trend was observed, although statistical significance was not reached for either DM (OR, 1.33; 95% CI, 0.38 to 4.63; p = 0.650) or PFS (HR, 2.02; 95% CI, 0.90 to 4.50; p = 0.087), likely due to the limited number of events. These findings support that inferior nodal extension may represent a distinct marker of systemic dissemination risk, rather than merely reflecting overall nodal burden.

Anatomically, level IV, Vb, and supraclavicular LNs are closely connected to major lymphatic and venous drainage pathways. Thus, involvement of these regions may reflect more advanced regional disease with increased potential for systemic dissemination, as suggested in prior studies. This interpretation is consistent with prior studies suggesting that inferior nodal extension is associated with an increased risk of systemic dissemination [13,14].

Regarding metastatic distribution, the lung was the most frequent site of DM, followed by bone and liver (Table 3), consistent with previous reports [7-10]. Although the incidence of lung and bone metastases was higher in patients with lower-neck involvement, the overall distribution pattern—lung predominance followed by bone and liver—remained consistent across groups. This indicates that lower-neck involvement influences the probability of developing DM, rather than determining the metastatic organ site [18].

Post-CCRT response showed the strongest association with survival outcomes (Table 4). Patients with non-CR had substantially higher risks of DM and death. Notably, when post-treatment response was included in the survival model, the association between lower-neck involvement and OS was attenuated. This finding suggests that early treatment response may partly mediate the impact of inferior nodal extension on survival.

Lower-neck involvement and post-CCRT response represent two complementary aspects of disease biology at different time points. Lower-neck involvement reflects baseline systemic risk at diagnosis, whereas post-CCRT response reflects the realized treatment effect and residual disease after therapy. In this framework, lower-neck involvement identifies patients with an inherent predisposition to systemic dissemination, while non-CR indicates persistent or treatment-resistant disease. Together, these variables provide a more comprehensive assessment of systemic failure risk by capturing both baseline risk and treatment response. From a practical standpoint, both lower-neck LN involvement and early post-CCRT response are readily assessable in routine care. Patients exhibiting both inferior nodal extension and non-CR may represent a clinically identifiable subgroup with particularly elevated systemic risk. Such patients could be considered candidates for closer surveillance or future trials evaluating intensified systemic strategies.

Our study has several limitations. This study was retrospective and conducted at a single institution.

Primary tumor subsites were heterogeneous, and both the biological behavior and baseline risk of DM are known to vary across subsites, which may influence the clinical interpretation of lower-neck involvement. DM rates varied across primary tumor subsites in our cohort, with hypopharyngeal cancer showing higher rates and oropharyngeal cancer lower rates, consistent with known biological differences among HNSCC subsites. However, these subsite-specific differences did not account for our main findings, as the association between lower-neck involvement and DM remained consistent across analyses. In addition, HPV and Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) status were incomplete, potentially affecting subsite-specific outcomes. Due to incomplete biomarker data, p16/HPV status was not included in the multivariable analysis. While biomarker distributions (p16 in oropharyngeal cancer and EBV in nasopharyngeal carcinoma) were descriptively assessed, no further subsite-specific analyses were performed due to limited sample sizes. The limited number of DM events restricted detailed subgroup analyses.

In sensitivity analyses excluding nasopharyngeal carcinoma and oropharyngeal cancer, the association between lower-neck involvement and adverse time-to-event outcomes remained generally consistent. Lower-neck involvement remained associated with worse DMFS (HR, 2.24; 95% CI, 0.97 to 5.19; p = 0.060) and significantly worse PFS (HR, 2.39; 95% CI, 1.18 to 4.87; p = 0.016). Similar patterns were observed after excluding nasopharyngeal carcinoma and p16-positive oropharyngeal cancer, with lower-neck involvement remaining associated with worse DMFS (HR, 2.10; 95% CI, 0.98 to 4.54; p = 0.058) and PFS (HR, 2.16; 95% CI, 1.14 to 4.10; p = 0.018). These findings suggest that the observed effect may not be solely explained by subsite-specific biological differences. Furthermore, although p16 status is an established prognostic factor in oropharyngeal carcinoma, non–p16-based staging was applied in the present study due to incomplete biomarker data and the retrospective nature of the cohort. This approach allowed for consistent staging across the entire study population and avoided substantial case exclusion. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that this may limit subsite-specific interpretation and should be considered when generalizing the results.

Several additional nodal factors, such as bilateral involvement, extranodal extension, and nodal size, are known to be associated with metastatic risk and were not individually evaluated in this study due to the limited number of events. However, lower-neck involvement often reflects more extensive nodal disease and may serve as a simple clinical marker capturing multiple high-risk nodal features. The consistent associations observed in our analyses suggest that lower-neck involvement itself carries additional prognostic information beyond these factors.

Additionally, systemic therapies administered after recurrence were not standardized and may have influenced OS. Although OTT >8 weeks occurred in nearly one-quarter of patients, this likely reflected calendar-day calculation and institutional non-treatment days rather than treatment-related toxicity. Nevertheless, the consistency of associations across logistic and time-to-event models supports the internal validity of the findings.

In an IMRT-treated cohort of patients with HNSCC, DM remained a major determinant of long-term outcome. Lower-neck LN involvement and non-CR after CCRT independently identified patients at increased risk of systemic dissemination. These clinically accessible factors may improve risk stratification.
