Determining rock damage strength with acoustic emission monitoring technique in terms of hurst exponent
Authors: Tong Jiang, Siyuan Liu, Zhenxing Yang, Rongchao Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn complex deep engineering environments, progressive rock mass failures, such as slabbing failure and deep fracturing, often pose significant threats to engineering safety, and the rock damage strength (σcd) is a critical threshold parameter to characterize the progressive failure process of rock masses. Thus, accurately identifying the σcd is one of the key tasks for achieving the early warning and short-term forecasting for geological disaster. Based on acoustic emission (AE) monitoring data, this paper proposed an overlapping sliding window improved rescaled range (R/S) analysis method to dynamically calculate the Hurst exponent (H) of AE count rate and AE energy rate time series. aiming to characterize the statistical characteristic changes during the evolution of rock damage. In addition, the uniaxial compression test was conducted on granite, sandstone, and basalt under five different strain rate conditions, and the evolution of the H-value during loading was analyzed. Subsequently, the σcd was determined by combining the physical mechanism of H variation. Finally, the value of the σcd obtained in terms of the Hurst exponent was verified with that determined by the crack volume strain (CVS) method. The results indicate that, under quasi-static loading conditions, the H-value in the AE count rate and AE energy rate sequences generally initially declines, subsequently rises, and finally reaches a peak during the whole damage stage. The smallest difference between H-based σcd and CVS-based σcd will be obtained when the Hurst exponent is represented by AE energy rate compared with AE count rate, AE cumulative count, and AE cumulative energy. The methodology proposed in this paper exhibits good applicability for hard rock under low strain rate, providing new insight into quantitative identification of rock mass instability and engineering disaster early warning.Keywords: Acoustic emission, Hurst exponent, Rock damage strength, Sliding window method, Strain rate, Crack volume strainSubject terms: Engineering, Natural hazards, Solid Earth sciences

Introduction

In recent years, with infrastructure construction and resource development continuously advancing towards greater depths, larger scales, longer spans, and more hazardous conditions1–3, a large number of projects, such as mountain tunnels, high slopes, deep-buried caverns, pumped storage power stations, and deep mines, have emerged. Compared with shallow engineering rock masses, deep rock masses are exposed to a long-term environment with coupled effects from multiple factors including high geostress, complex structural planes, strong unloading disturbances, and groundwater. The mechanical behaviors of deep rock masses frequently exhibit significant non-linearity and abruptness, leading to frequent disasters such as rock mass instability, landslides, and rock bursts4–6. These disasters are often characterized by high suddenness, extensive destruction, and severe consequences, which not only threatens the construction and operational safety but also significantly increases the total life cycle cost of the projects. Therefore, accurately identifying the σcd, which is the critical threshold marking the transition from stable deformation to accelerated fracture, has become a key scientific issue for early warning of deep geotechnical engineering disasters7–9.

At the microscopic scale, rock damage undergoes a gradual evolutionary process from the closure of primary fractures, the initiation of new cracks, stable expansion, the crack interconnection to macroscopic failure10,11. During this process, the initiation and expansion of microcracks release energy in the form of elastic waves. The AE technology provides an effective means to reveal the entire process of rock failure, from microscopic damage accumulation to macroscopic instability, by capturing the microcrack signals in real time12–16. Thus, identifying rock damage strength based on AE information is considered a crucial technique to achieve proactive early warning of rock mass instability.

Currently, the determination of rock damage strength depends primarily on mechanical testing and multi-parameter monitoring methods. The Crack Volumetric Strain (CVS) method, based on strain measurement, determines the damage threshold with respect to the inflection point where volumetric strain transitions from consolidation to expansion within stress-strain curves. Due to its clear physical significance, the CVS method is widely used in experimental research17–19. Meanwhile, the criteria based on AE activity have also received considerable attention, and many researchers often indicate the stage of damage evolution through sudden changes in parameters such as AE event rate, energy release, and b-value variation20–22. However, under practical engineering or complex experimental conditions, AE signals are susceptible to environmental noise, sensor arrangement, and rock mass heterogeneity23–25. The response of different parameters to the damage stage is not consistent, Moreover, traditional AE parameter methods (event rate, b-value) are sensitive to noise and lithology, lacking a statistically robust criterion for damage threshold identification.

The Hurst exponent is a statistical indicator used to characterize long-range dependence and memory effects in time series, first proposed by Hurst in the study of chronological sequences26. Additionally, the classical calendared range (R/S) analysis method has been widely applied in geological disaster monitoring fields such as landslide displacement, earthquakes, and microseismic sequences, demonstrating good recognition capabilities for phased evolution and trend changes27,28. Compared with conventional AE parameters, the Hurst exponent offers a noise-robust, scale-invariant statistical measure suitable for capturing the evolving correlation structure of fracture-induced AE sequences. In recent years, a limited number of studies have begun to explore the application of the Hurst exponent within rock mechanics to analyze the statistical characteristics of AE sequences during rock crack. However, existing research focuses on post-hoc static analysis of complete AE sequences, the traditional R/S method suffers from two major limitations:(I) static analysis of the entire AE sequence, which cannot capture dynamic changes during damage evolution; and (Ⅱ) discontinuous interval division (non-overlapping subsequences), which reduces time resolution and requires large data volumes. These issues make it difficult to adapt to the non-stationary characteristics of AE signals during rock failure29,30.

From the perspective of rock failure mechanisms, at the initial stages of axial loading, primary cracks within the rock gradually close, and the AE activity exhibits weak correlation with Hurst exponent or even anti-persistence characteristics, with a possible transient decrease in the Hurst exponent. As stress continues to increase, new cracks begin to initiate and expand steadily, and the AE events gradually show stronger correlation in time and space, leading to a gradual increase in the H with a peak near the damage strength. When cracks rapidly converge and form a through-going fracture surface, the AE activity transitions to highly sporadic and intermittent releases, with rapid lose of the persistence of the time series, and the H subsequently decreases. This evolutionary characteristic of "initial decrease, subsequent increase, and then instability" is physically consistent with the process of crack evolution from independent evolution to synergistic instability in classical rock progressive crack theory31,32.

To address the limitations of traditional R/S methods, such as static analysis and discontinuous interval division, this paper proposes an improved R/S analysis method that employs a sliding window processing technique to estimate the dynamic Hurst exponent in acoustic emission (AE) time series. By designing overlapping sliding subsequences, this method significantly enhances time resolution while ensuring statistical stability, thereby enabling real-time characterization of the Hurst exponent evolution trend. Subsequently, the evolution characteristics of the Hurst exponent and the changes in AE activity are combined with the physical mechanism of H variation to determine the σcd. Finally, the σcd obtained by the Hurst exponent is validated against the results derived from the crack volume strain (CVS) method. This study may provide a new theoretical basis and technical means for interpretable early warning of rock mass instability.

Hurst exponent for AE parameters

Traditional hurst exponent

The Hurst exponent, first introduced by Hurst when investigating the long-term dependency characteristics of the Nile River hydrological series, is frequently used to characterize the statistical correlation of time series across different temporal scales. Unlike traditional analyses based on mean, variance, or frequency domain characteristics, the Hurst exponent, grounded in fractal statistics and stochastic processes, can judge the long-term memory and evolutionary trend of time series. Consequently, it has been widely applied in the analysis of financial markets, geophysical processes, and material fracture behavior33,34.

The signals generated during rock loading constitute essentially the non-stationary time series whose statistical characteristics vary with crack propagation. The Hurst exponent provides an effective tool for characterizing the temporal correlation structure of such sequences.

The Hurst effect is quantified with the parameter of H in three cases35:

If H=0.5, the sequence approximates a memoryless random walk, with negligible correlation between successive increments and near-random fluctuation in the whole.

If 0.5<H<1, the sequence demonstrates the persistence behaviour denoting that the consistency between the future trend and the prevailing variation.

If H < 0.5, the sequence exhibits anti-persistence which means that the future trend may reverse the current one. In general, whenever H ≠ 0.5, the time series data can be described as the fractional Brownian motion.

During rock loading, the generation of AE events directly corresponds to the initiation, propagation, and interaction of internal cracks. The temporal distribution characteristics of AE events produced at different fracture evolution stages differ greatly from each other. During fissure closure and local slipping stages, the AE events generally occur separately with irregular temporal intervals, exhibiting an overall lack of discernible temporal correlation structure. As new cracks emerge gradually and expand stably, the AE events begin to exhibit a phased concentration in time with a certain degree of clustering behavior. When the crack numbers increase and the interaction intensifies, the AE events exhibit more obvious continuity and structural characteristics in the time series. The Hurst exponent, by quantifying precisely this structural evolution within the time series of AE events, can indirectly reflect the transition of the rock’s internal fracture system from dispersed to coordinated evolution. Therefore, the Hurst exponent serves as a crucial bridge linking the statistical characteristics of AE to the micro-damage mechanisms within the rock.

Traditional Hurst exponent methods possess advantages such as relatively straightforward computation and lower demands on data distribution patterns, making them particularly suitable for AE data analysis with sufficient sample size and strong signal stationarity36,37. However, this method exhibits notable limitations in practical applications. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the classical R/S method often divides the time series into M non-overlapping subsequences. That is, for a given sub-sequence length of n, each data point contributes only once to the calculation of (R/S)^n. When an intuitive analysis of the trend of a certain time series is required, more data is needed to calculate the Hurst exponent. Once the data volume drops to the 'hundreds of points’ level, the number of usable scales becomes very limited. At slow loading rates, the AE signal data is abundant. However, when the loading rate increases from 10⁻5/s to 10⁻3/s, the collected AE signal data diminishes to merely one per cent of the original volume, leading to a significant decrease in the predictive effectiveness of the calculated Hurst exponent for rock damage strength, which makes it impossible to form a reliable threshold criterion. Therefore, it is necessary to improve this method by introducing window moving processing method to enhance the time resolution of the Hurst exponent for damage evolution during the loading process.

It is noted that the Hurst exponent is a statistical parameter, and its physical significance should be interpreted with consideration of specific loading conditions, lithology, and crack evolution stages, rather than serving as a single threshold for directly determining rock failure states. The key to the effectiveness of the Hurst exponent in rock damage research lies in its reasonable integration with the stress-strain evolution process.

Experimental apparatus and specimen preparation

To investigate the statistical characteristics of AE in rock damage evolution under different rock types and loading rates, this study selected three representative hard rocks, namely, granite, sandstone, and basalt. These materials are commonly encountered in engineering practice and exhibit significant differences in physical and mechanical properties, which is appropriate to verify the suitability of the method proposed in this paper. Five strain rates (10⁻5/s, 5×10⁻5/s, 10⁻4/s, 5×10⁻4/s, and 10⁻3/s) were set to cover the typical quasi-static loading conditions. These five rates span from very slow to relatively fast (but still quasi-static) loading, allowing systematic evaluation of the proposed method’s performance under different deformation rates commonly used in hard rock testing. By combining the real-time AE monitoring with dynamic analysis of Hurst exponent, a method was established to identify the damage strength based on statistical features. As shown in Fig. 3(a) and Fig. 3(b), the MTS815 rock mechanics testing system was used to load the rock specimens, and the 32-channel Micro-II AE system developed by the American PAC Company was adopted for AE monitoring. The AE system has a threshold of 40 dB, with AE probe of NANO-30 and frequency range of 0–750 kHz. Drill a rock core from a complete rock block, cut and polish the rock core section, and prepare it into a cylindrical specimen with a diameter of 50mm and a height of 100mm according to the method recommended by the International Society for Rock Mechanics and Engineering, as seen in Fig. 3(c). Measure the mass of each sample using an electronic scale with an accuracy of 0.01g, then measure the diameter of the sample using a vernier caliper to calculate the density of each sample, and finally calculate the average value for the same type of sample. Under the same strain rate conditions, the evolution characteristics of the AE Hurst exponent during uniaxial compression of rocks are primarily controlled by the evolution pattern of internal cracks. Taking a strain rate of 5×10-5/s as representative, a comparative analysis was conducted on the variation patterns of the Hurst exponents for both the count rate and energy rate of AE throughout the loading process for granite, sandstone, and basalt specimens.

During the initial loading stage, primary cracks, pores, and grain contact interfaces within the rock undergo progressive closure. AE events primarily originate from crack closure and local friction slipping. At this stage, AE activity exhibits scattered distribution with weak temporal correlation between events. Both the count rate and energy rate Hurst exponents remain at relatively low level, accompanied by moderate fluctuations.

As axial stress increases gradually, the crack closure process is completed in essence, stress gradually concentrates on weak areas within the rock, and new micro-cracks begin to initiate and expand stably. Meanwhile, the AE events gradually shift from a dispersed distribution to a local concentration, exhibiting distinct clustering characteristics in the time series, and the Hurst exponent shows an overall upward trend. During this stage, the Hurst exponent curve often displays multiple local peaks, reflecting synergistic activities at different scales during the crack propagation process.

Based on these evolutionary characteristics, this paper proposed a method for determining the rock damage strength using the Hurst exponent of AE. Under the same strain rate condition, a local significant peak emerging during the crack synergistic propagation stage is identified from the stress evolution curve of the Hurst exponent in terms of AE count rate or energy rate. The stress level corresponding to this peak is defined as the rock damage strength point, denoted as σcd. The σcd point occurs at the critical transition stage where the crack propagation pattern shifts from stable expansion to synergistic evolution, and its physical significance corresponds to the critical state where the internal damage mechanism within the rock undergoes a qualitative transformation.

Specimens of different rock types exhibit distinct patterns in Hurst exponent evolution. For low-porosity hard rocks such as granite and basalt, the crack initiation locations are relatively concentrated, and the propagation paths exhibit good consistency. The Hurst exponent curves are generally smooth, with clear peak characteristics during the damage stage. In contrast, due to the high porosity and internal defect development in sandstone, the crack closure coincides with the propagation, and the Hurst exponent curves vary greatly. Nevertheless, the prominent peaks still correspond to stages where the degree of synergistic crack propagation is significantly enhanced.

It is noted that Figs. 4-6 also demonstrate the Hurst exponents of cumulative count and cumulative energy. However, cumulative parameters inherently represent the integration of historical events, and the smoothing effect of their time series obscures transient characteristics during damage evolution. Comparative analysis shows that the Hurst exponent curve of the cumulative parameter changes relatively smoothly, and is insensitive to the phased response of crack propagation, making it difficult to clearly identify the critical point of damage synergy. Thus, this paper focused on the Hurst exponent based on ‘rate’ parameters (count rate and energy rate).

Physical mechanisms for different evolutionary stages of hurst exponent

The dynamic Hurst exponent obtained by the overlapping sliding window processing method reveals the evolution of statistical characteristics in AE sequences during rock damage processes. The systematic analysis of experimental data on granite, sandstone, and basalt under multiple strain rates indicates that the Hurst exponent exhibits complex characteristics of multi-peak fluctuations rather than a simple single-peak pattern of "initial decline, subsequent rise, and reach a peak". Its evolution law is closely related to the synergy, intermittent expansion, and heterogeneity of internal cracks in rocks. The physical mechanisms are as follows.

Phase I: Initial compaction and micro-crack adjustment (temporary decline in H)

Phenomenon: During the initial loading stage, primary cracks, pores, and particle contact interfaces within the rock gradually close under axial compression. AE activity in this stage primarily originates from crack closure, inter-particle micro-sliding, and local stress redistribution. These activities occur at scattered locations, with small scale, and are significantly influenced by rock heterogeneity.

Physical mechanism: This stage corresponds to the gradual closure of primary rock cracks and inter-particle frictional sliding. Due to high discreteness and randomness exhibited by such AE events over time, a stable time-related structure has not yet formed between events. Consequently, the Hurst exponent is generally at a low level, accompanied by a certain degree of disorderly fluctuation. For rocks with high porosity and complex internal cracks (e.g., sandstone), Hurst exponent during this stage fluctuates considerably, reflecting the complex microscopic pattern of crack closure and local expansion.

Phase II: Stable expansion and damage accumulation (large H fluctuation and multiple peaks)

Phenomenon: As axial stress further increases, the closure of primary cracks is basically completed. Stress gradually concentrates on the weak areas inside the rock, and new micro-cracks begin to initiate and expand stably. Unlike the phenomenon during the crack closure stage, the AE events originate predominantly from the tensile expansion and shear slip at crack tips, exhibiting spatial concentration.

Physical mechanism: The crack propagation process exhibits distinct intermittent characteristics. In localized stress concentration areas, cracks undergo a series of propagation events within a short period of time, followed by a relatively stable stage. This cyclical pattern of 'expansion-stagnation-re-expansion’ gradually leads to the clustering of AE events in the time series.

Statistically, this clustering behaviour can enhance the temporal correlations between events, and the Hurst exponent exhibits an overall upward trend, along with significant crack propagation activities and multiple local peaks. It should be noted that these local peaks do not correspond to singular damage states, but reflect the synergistic crack propagation at different spatial scales.

Phase III: Damage synergy and instability precursor (principal H peak and subsequent rapid decline)

Phenomenon: As the loading approaches the macroscopic rock failure, the number of cracks increases significantly, the spacing between cracks continuously diminishes, and the interaction effect gradually improves. At this time, the crack propagation is no longer an isolated local event, but begins to form a synergistic evolution characteristic within a certain spatial range, with local crack networks gradually developing.

Physical mechanism: The main peak corresponds to a critical state where crack interactions markedly intensify and localized zones form. During this stage, the AE events exhibit higher continuity and correlation in their time series. The Hurst exponent reaches a maximum peak throughout the entire loading process. This peak reflects a transition in the crack system from local stable propagation to one dominated by crack interaction and synergistic evolution, which is an important indicator of a qualitative change in the degree of internal damage in the rock.

It should be emphasized that, under most experimental conditions in this study, the Hurst exponent in stage III did not exhibit a significant sudden drop, but instead gradually declined after reaching its peak. This phenomenon indicates that the physical significance of the Hurst exponent lies not in a fixed threshold, but in the degree of crack synergy corresponding to its peak value. This peak should be understood as a statistical representation of a significant change in the evolutionary state of the internal crack network within the rock, rather than a direct indicator of macroscopic failure.

The heterogeneity of rock masses, particularly the distribution of pores and defects, constitutes another critical controlling factor. For hard rocks with high strength and low porosity, such as granite and basalt, the internal structure is relatively uniform, and the crack propagation pathways tend to be parallel to each other. As a result, the evolution law of the Hurst exponent is relatively clear, with a distinct main peak. However, for rocks with high porosity and strong heterogeneity, such as sandstone, which have abundant primary micro-cracks, pores, and weak cementation surfaces, the simultaneous presence of multiple mechanisms during loading, such as compaction, sliding, and tension, leads to higher intermittency and spatial randomness in the occurrence of AE events. This complex mechanism is reflected in the Hurst exponent, which manifests as great fluctuations in the curve throughout the loading process, particularly with prominent multiple peaks. Consequently, the critical state of damage synergy (main peak) may be obscured by frequent fluctuations or manifest as multiple pseudo-peaks, thereby increasing the difficulty and error margin in identifying the damage threshold.

In summary, the dynamic evolution of the Hurst exponent is essentially a statistical representation of the "non-uniform and intermittent" expansion process of internal cracks in rocks. Multiple local peaks at the curve correspond to synergistic jumps or clustering stages at different scales during crack propagation, while the prominent main peak (Hpeak) physically marks the critical transition point where the crack network shifts from independent, stable propagation to intense mutual interaction, forming local crack zones, which provides a damage warning perspective of statistical dynamics. The results of this study indicate that this method has optimal applicability and warning accuracy under low strain rate and high strength hard rock. For high strain rate conditions or highly heterogeneous rock masses, such as porous sandstone, the Hurst exponent criterion is recommended as part of a comprehensive warning system combined with other methods such as microseismic monitoring and deformation measurement, to enhance the reliability of rock mass instability warning in complex environments.

Causes for differences in hurst exponent evolution under different loading rates

Observing the variation of AE Hurst exponent in sandstone and basalt under different strain rates indicates that, during uniaxial compression, the Hurst exponent of sandstone exhibits characteristics of numerous mutation inflection points. In addition, the sandstone has a high porosity, implying the presence of numerous defects such as primary cracks and pores, and will undergoes more complex micro-structural changes during loading. While some micro-fractures and pores experience the closure and compaction, the defects located at weakly cemented or grain-contact interfaces may enter states of local shear slipping and tensile expansion. This competitive mechanism of "coexistence of closure and expansion" can make the occurrence of AE events more intermittent and irregular. The sandstone contains more voids, leading to unstable changes in the Hurst exponent during compression, which manifests as more fluctuations in the Hurst exponent. Comparing Figs. 7 and 8 reveals that the distance between points B and C is relatively greater in sandstone, with the Hurst exponent reaching its peak earlier. Sandstone has a lower uniaxial compressive strength than other hard rocks, and its internal structure is relatively poorer, leading to faster progression into the damage stage and final failure. This also reasonably explains why its peak time is earlier than that of other hard rocks and the B-C spacing is larger.

Experimental results indicate that, as the loading strain rate increases, the evolution pattern of the AE Hurst exponent undergoes more significant changes compared to low strain rate conditions. The primary reason lies in the compression effect of the crack propagation process on the time scale.

Under low strain rates, the stress loading process is relatively slow to allow sufficient time for the initiation, propagation, and interaction of rock cracks. The cracking evolution process exhibits good separability along the time axis. The AE events can gradually accumulate over a longer time scale, and the statistical characteristics of the sequence remain relatively stable, enabling the Hurst exponent to clearly reflect the evolution of crack synergy.

When the loading rate increases, external loading can be rapidly transferred into the rock interior in a short period of time, and the closure of preexisting cracks coincides with extension of new cracks. New cracks enter the accelerated propagation stage or even the breakthrough stages before achieving sufficient stable propagation. The crack evolution process is significantly compressed, with the AE events concentrated within a short period, resulting in a strong non-stationary characteristic in the time series. Under these conditions, the Hurst exponent curve exhibits larger fluctuations and more frequent local peaks, making the identification of the main peak more challenging.

Therefore, the altered evolution pattern of the Hurst exponent under high strain rate conditions does not indicate a failure of the method, but rather directly reflects changes in the crack evolution dynamics. This results also suggest that the damage strength identification method based on the Hurst exponent is more suitable for quasi-static loading conditions where the crack propagation process is fully developed.

Determination and error analysis of rock damage thresholds

During uniaxial compression, rocks exhibit several stress thresholds including the crack closure threshold, crack initiation threshold, crack damage threshold, and peak threshold. According to the above analysis, the crack initiation threshold and crack damage threshold are crucial for understanding the initiation and progression of rock cracking and failure. The crack initiation threshold marks the starting point for the initial development of internal cracks within rock, also termed the rock’s crack initiation stress point. The damage threshold indicates the stress level at which cracks within rock commence rapid growth and propagation, also defined as the rock’s damage strength point. The crack volume strain (CVS) method possesses clear physical significance and straightforward operational procedures, and was widely adopted both domestically and internationally as the most commonly used technique for determining rock stress thresholds. The crack volume strain calculated by the CVS method can intuitively reflect the closure and development processes of internal cracks in rocks. Fig. 9 illustrates a typical stress-strain curve by the CVS method and a schematic of how to determine the damage strength point, with the horizontal axis representing the axial strain. During the uniaxial compression, the rock volumetric strain undergoes several stages of initial compaction, elastic deformation, stable crack growth, and unstable crack growth. The crack volume strain curve remains stable during the elastic stage. When the rock enters the damage stage, the volumetric strain exhibits an inflection point, indicating a change from compressive deformation to dilatation. The stress corresponding to this inflection point is the damage strength σcd (marked as point B in Figs.4-8).

The evolution pattern of the Hurst exponent is significantly regulated by both the loading strain rate and rock heterogeneity, and its mechanism is directly related to the fracture dynamics process within the rock. Under low strain rate (≤10-4/s) loading conditions, stress is applied slowly, allowing the stages of crack closure, stable propagation, and interaction to be fully performed in a sequential way. The AE events exhibit relatively uniform temporal distribution, with strong long-range correlation between their sequences (persistence), which enables moving-window R/S analysis to reliably capture the evolving trend of the Hurst exponent. At this stage, the curve is relatively smooth, featuring a prominent main peak (Hpeak) that correlates well with the σcd determined by the CVS method, resulting in high early warning accuracy. Under high strain rate (≥10-3/s) loading conditions, the rate of external force work is extremely fast, and thus various stages of microcrack evolution are highly compressed or even overlap partly in time. The inertial effects of crack propagation become prominent, and the fracture process exhibits sudden and clustered characteristics, leading to drastic mutations in energy and count in the AE sequence within a short period of time. This pronounced non-nationality causes extreme fluctuations in statistical quantities within local time windows, increasing the noise and multi-peak phenomena in Hurst exponent curves. The long-time scale nationality assumption for the traditional R/S method is weakened, making it difficult to identify the main peak and significantly increasing early warning errors.

By comparing the σcd derived from granite, sandstone, and basalt using the Hurst exponent of AE energy rate, smaller errors will yield when employing the CVS method with Hurst exponent. Sandstone has a higher internal porosity and lower strength, so the plastic characteristics exhibited by the uniaxial compression stress-strain curve are more pronounced, leading to relatively larger errors. This heightened error becomes particularly remarkable when determining rock damage thresholds using the Hurst exponent under high strain rate. Comparisons across different strain rates reveal that the relative error increases with strain rates, reaching the maximum at 10-3/s. Thus, the Hurst exponent is feasible to determine the damage threshold of high-strength hard rocks with strain rates below 10-4/s, with errors less than 10%. For hard rocks with strain rates over 10-4/s, as well as other soft rocks, the Hurst exponent may not determine the σcd.

Conclusions

A method for calculating the Hurst exponent of AE based on overlapping moving window R/S analysis was proposed. Compared with traditional processing methods, this approach can stably characterize the statistical features of time series even under conditions of limited AE data, making it suitable for dynamic analysis of non-stationary signals during rock failure process.

A method for calculating the Hurst exponent of AE based on overlapping moving window R/S analysis was proposed. Compared with traditional processing methods, this approach can stably characterize the statistical features of time series even under conditions of limited AE data, making it suitable for dynamic analysis of non-stationary signals during rock failure process.

“Under uniaxial compression, the Hurst exponent of AE count rate and energy rate exhibits an overall trend of initial decline, subsequent rise, and a final peak during the damage stage, which physically aligns with the classical progressive failure theory.” Compared to cumulative parameters, the Hurst exponent calculated from rate-based parameters exhibits high sensitivity to rock damage evolution.

Among the four AE parameters (AE count rate, AE energy rate, AE cumulative count and AE cumulative energy), the σcd determined by H in AE energy rate demonstrates minimal error compared with that determined by the crack volume strain (CVS) method under low strain rates (≤1×10-4/s). As the loading rate increases further, the error gradually increases. Due to its higher porosity and lower strength, the proposed method has limited applicability for sandstone. However, it is well suited for high-strength hard rocks (e.g., granite and basalt). Therefore, for hard rock engineering applications under quasi-static loading conditions with strain rates ≤1×10⁻4/s, the Hurst exponent based on AE energy rate is recommended as a reliable indicator for early warning of rock damage.

Future research should integrate the Hurst exponent evolution with multi-source monitoring data to improve its short-term predictive capability in the pre-failure stage of rock masses. In engineering applications, by comprehensively analyzing the Hurst exponent method with microseismic, deformation, and stress monitoring results, it is expected to provide a more reliable quantitative basis for rock mass instability.
