Genomic analysis reveals close genetic similarity between ESBL and other β-lactamase-producing E. coli isolates from humans and dogs, suggesting potential for inter-species transmission
Authors: Charles E Whitehead-Tillery, Samantha E Waite, Gabriel A E Durand-Piña, Elizabeth K Green, Julia A Bell, Lixin Zhang, Linda S Mansfield
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtended-spectrum β-lactamase-(ESBL)-producing Enterobacteriaceae are emerging in hospital and community settings as important causes of urinary tract infections. These plasmid-mediated enzymes have been identified in human and dog hosts, with blaCTX−M variants being the most prevalent ESBLs worldwide. Our objective was to identify horizontal gene transfer (HGT) events amongst human and dog-derived ESBL-producing bacteria by examining genetic relatedness of plasmid and bacterial whole genome sequences (WGS) associated with ESBLs and other β-lactamase genes. By understanding genetic relatedness, we aimed to provide insight into transmission dynamics of ESBLs and antibiotic resistance among humans and dogs in community-acquired settings.ResultsOf 149 plasmids collected from humans (n = 125) and dogs (n = 24), 111 (74.5%) carried class A ESBL genes with blaCTX−M−14 (31.6%) predominating in human-derived plasmids and blaCTX−M−1 in dog-derived plasmids (29.6%). In addition, ESBLs and other β-lactamase genes, including blaTEM−1,were also identified in both populations. pMLST showed that IncF, IncI1, and IncN plasmids were the main groups contributing to the dissemination of ESBLs amongst human and dog populations. Neighbor-joining analysis revealed clustering of human and dog-derived plasmids carrying similar ESBL genes as well as other antibiotic-resistant genes. The maximum-likelihood tree revealed a high predominance of ST131 carried by E. coli serotypes O25:H4 in humans but not dogs. Virulence gene analysis revealed that ESBL-producing bacteria were not limited to UPEC.ConclusionsThe presence of conserved ESBLs, other β-lactamase genes and E. coli clones in both humans and dogs highlights widespread circulation of shared resistance elements. These findings support the need for broader One Health surveillance, particularly involving companion animals, to better track and mitigate ARG spread in community settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12864-026-13015-z.Keywords: Extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) producing Enterobacteriaceae, Escherichia coli, Antibiotic resistance, Humans and dogs, Uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC), Horizontal gene transfer (HGT), Comparative genomics, Community-acquired

Background

Antibiotic resistance has become a serious public health threat due to the overuse of antibiotics. This problem has challenged healthcare systems by contributing to the emergence of antibiotic-resistant (AR) pathogens, resulting in high morbidity and mortality rates [1]. Among these resistant bacteria, Gram-negative, extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing Enterobacteriaceae are major contributors to increased antibiotic-resistant (AR) infections. These organisms have been the main causes of urinary tract infections (UTIs), bloodstream infections, and pneumonia in both hospital and community settings [2]. In the U.S. alone, it is estimated that ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae are responsible for about 198,000 infections annually, resulting in roughly 9,000 deaths [1].

β-lactam resistance among Gram-negative organisms is mediated by the production of β-lactamase enzymes, which hydrolyze β-lactam antibiotics [3]. β-lactamases are usually classified using the Ambler molecular classification system, which is based on amino acid sequence identity, dividing β-lactamases into four classes (A, B, C, and D) [4]. ESBLs represent a subset of β-lactamases primarily within Ambler class A, that hydrolyze β-lactam antibiotics, including penicillins, cephalosporins, and monobactams, but are also inhibited by clavulanic acid [5–7]. Among class A ESBLs, CTX-M enzymes have emerged as the most prevalent worldwide and are frequently associated with plasmid-mediated dissemination in Escherichia coli [8].

Historically, ESBL-producing infections were confined to hospital settings, with TEM- and SHV-derived ESBL variants predominating in Klebsiella pneumoniae during the late 20th century. However, since the late 1990s, CTX-M enzymes have contributed to a global shift with E. coli emerging as the dominant ESBL-producing species in both hospital and community settings, particularly across South America and Europe [9]. This transition of hospital-associated pathogens to community settings further reflects the expanding nature of ESBL dissemination.

Horizontal gene transfer (HGT) plays a significant role in ESBL dissemination across bacterial strains and species occurring through transformation, transduction, and conjugation [10]. Among these mechanisms, conjugation is considered the primary driver of ESBL spread, as it allows bacteria to transfer ESBL genes through conjugative plasmids [2, 11]. Plasmid-mediated ESBL transfer is often associated with specific incompatibility (Inc) groups, which are classified by replicon type based on their ability or inability to stably coexist with other plasmids in the same bacterial host [12]. Common Inc groups carrying ESBLs include IncF, IncN, IncI1, IncHI2, IncL/M, IncA/C, IncK, and IncU [2]. Among these, IncI1 and IncF are widely recognized as major vehicles for the carriage and global spread of ESBLs in humans and animals [13–15]. In addition to conjugation, mobile genetic elements (MGEs) such as insertion sequences (ISs), transposons, and integrons further facilitate the mobilization of ESBLs and antibiotic-resistant genes [16]. Notably, insertion sequence ISEcp1 has been reported to mobilize CTX-M enzymes from the chromosomes of Kluyvera spp. into E. coli plasmids further promoting global dissemination [16].

Although ESBL-producing infections were historically hospital-associated, increasing prevalence in community settings suggests dissemination across interconnected reservoirs, including humans, livestock, companion animals, wildlife, and the environment [17–20]. Transmission of ESBL-producing bacteria from livestock to humans through food consumption has been reported and has already emerged as a significant public health concern, highlighting potential transmission pathways that contribute to ESBL dissemination across human and animal populations [21]. Many of these antibiotic-resistant pathogens are enteric organisms that reside in the human gut microbiota [1]. Similar ESBL-producing organisms have also been identified in companion animals such as dogs, where extensive antimicrobial use and close human-animal contact may facilitate their persistence in shared community reservoirs, including households, livestock, wildlife, and soil [22–26].

ESBL-producing E. coli have increasingly been reported in dogs worldwide. Global meta-analysis estimates the global prevalence of ESBL-producing E. coli in dogs to be approximately 6.87%, although estimates varied by region with reported rates exceeding 15% in Africa [25]. Molecular studies demonstrated that CTX-M enzymes predominate in dog isolates, with blaCTX−M−15 reported as the most common variant. Among ESBL-positive isolates, CTX-M enzymes accounted for approximately 41.4% of isolates reported in healthy animals and 13.3% reported in both hospital and community settings [25]. Moreover, clonal lineages that have been globally disseminated and considered high risk include ST131, ST410, and ST648, which has also been identified in humans and dogs. Furthermore, these clonal lineages are often associated with plasmids classified as IncF and IncI1 carrying ESBLs and other ARGs [23, 24]. The detection of globally disseminated ESBL-associated lineages in dogs emphasizes the importance of dogs as a potential reservoir of clinically relevant ESBL-producing E. coli.

Given the interconnectedness between companion animals, humans, and other community reservoirs, dogs may serve as potential disseminators of ESBL-producing E. coli in community settings [20, 27]. Dogs interact extensively with environmental and animal reservoirs that harbor ESBL-producing bacteria, which may facilitate acquisition and introduction of resistant strains into household settings [28–30]. Close contact between humans and dogs may subsequently enable dissemination within the human gut microbiota through HGT [27]. Despite growing recognition of ESBL dissemination across multiple community reservoirs, it remains unclear whether ESBL plasmids in dogs share similar genetic features with those found in humans. In particular, the extent to which shared plasmid replicon types and MGEs contribute to cross-host transmission between humans and dogs remains incompletely understood.

To investigate dissemination dynamics of ESBL-producing E. coli between humans and dogs, we performed a comprehensive genomic analysis integrating long-read Nanopore sequence data with publicly available plasmids and whole-genome sequences. Nanopore-derived sequences were used as reference queries for BLAST-based identification and curation of β-lactamase-producing plasmids from the NCBI database. This study focused on characterizing the distribution and diversity of ESBL genes, including their family classification and variants, while also documenting other β-lactamase genes present in human- and dog-derived isolates. We further evaluated plasmid incompatibility groups, conserved mobile genetic elements, sequence types and performed phylogenetic analyses at both the plasmid and whole-genome levels to assess genetic relatedness across hosts. We hypothesized that (1) circulating ESBL-producing bacteria are disseminated between humans and dogs and (2) IncI1-associated class A ESBLs flanked by ISEcp1 family transposases would be the most prevalent among human- and dog-derived ESBL plasmids.

By integrating plasmid characterization with phylogenetic analysis and comparative genomic approaches, this study aims to clarify which shared plasmid features and bacterial lineages could potentially contribute to cross-host dissemination of β-lactam resistance. Understanding these genetic relationships has important implications for antimicrobial resistance surveillance and One Health strategies aimed at mitigating ESBL spread in community settings.

Materials and methods

Rationale and study design

Dissemination of ESBLs is often plasmid-mediated, with members of Enterobacteriaceae being primarily responsible for the spread worldwide [31–33]. Within this family, ESBL-producing E. coli represent one of the most clinically significant and widely distributed species in both humans and companion animals [22, 34]. Although ESBL-producing E. coli have been identified in both human and dog populations, the extent to which clinically relevant ESBL plasmid backbones are shared across these reservoirs within community settings remains unclear [24, 35].

To address this question, we designed a comparative genomic study to evaluate whether ESBL-producing E. coli plasmids derived from human isolates exhibit genetic similarity to those identified in dogs. We hypothesized that if dogs act as reservoirs and contribute to ESBL dissemination, plasmids from human and dog isolates would share conserved ESBL genes, other β-lactamase genes, plasmid incompatibility groups, MGEs, and chromosomal sequence types.

Complete plasmid sequences were generated from ESBL-producing E. coli isolates obtained from human patients [36]. These human-derived plasmids were used as reference queries to curate a human plasmid collection exhibiting sequence similarity based on defined query coverage and nucleotide identity thresholds. In parallel, ESBL-carrying plasmids from dog isolates were independently identified from the NCBI database using host-specific and ESBL annotation filters.

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) data was obtained exclusively from publicly available NCBI datasets for comparative analysis of chromosomal features in E. coli isolates when available. The resulting human and dog plasmid and WGS datasets were analyzed using a standard comparative genomic framework.

Comparative analyses were performed to evaluate ESBL gene content, other β-lactamase genes, and other ARGs. We also assessed plasmid replicon types, MGEs, sequence types (STs), virulence-associated genes, serotypes, and phylogenetic clustering patterns. Genetic similarity in plasmid sequence composition, replicon type, and ESBL genes was interpreted as shared potential dissemination patterns consistent with plasmid-mediated mobility across host species. Phylogenetic analyses of available chromosomal sequences were conducted to assess whether isolates clustered by host origin or genetic relatedness and to examine shared clonal lineages and strain typing, providing insight into cross-reservoir dissemination within a One Health framework (Fig. 1).

Bacterial strains, isolation, and culture methods

A subset of the 1,658 E. coli isolates originally collected between 2006 and 2008 from human patients at the Gachon University Gil Medical Center, South Korea, was obtained from a previously characterized study [36]. The isolates included in this study were previously confirmed to be ESBL-producing in the original investigation. The 2006–2008 collection period refers only to the human clinical isolates used for long-read Nanopore sequencing. These isolates were used as reference sequences for BLAST-based identification and curation of related plasmids from publicly available NCBI datasets, which included sequences from different geographic locations. Therefore, comparative genomic analyses were based on genetic relatedness rather than temporal alignment.

The original study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the hospital (GIRBA-2212). Of the 1,658 isolates, 94 were ESBL-producing isolates from different body sites (76 urinary tract, 17 blood, and 1 rectal swab), while 1,564 were ESBL-negative isolates (707 urinary, 373 blood, and 484 rectal swabs) [36]. E. coli species were identified using the VITEK system (bioMérieux) for urine and blood samples [36]. For rectal samples, E. coli isolates were identified by culture using UriSelect media (Bio-Rad) [36]. The microdilution method was used to confirm that urinary isolates were ESBL-positive, and results were interpreted based on the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) criteria [36, 37]. In this study, ESBL-producing isolates were classified according to the phenotypic testing interpreted according to CLSI guidelines, as described in the Graham study.

From the 94 urinary ESBL-producing isolates reported in the Graham study, 16 E. coli isolates were selected and plated on Luria agar plates aerobically at 37 °C with cefotaxime (4 µg/ml) to reconfirm resistance to third-generation cephalosporins prior to sequencing rather than redefining ESBL status. These isolates were subsequently subjected to long-read sequencing to characterize ESBL-associated plasmids. β-lactamase genes were identified through genomic analysis.

Plasmid DNA extraction and nanopore sequencing

Plasmid DNA was extracted from ESBL-producing E. coli samples using the Qiagen Plasmid Midi Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Qiagen, Catalogue. No. 12143). Samples were submitted to the Michigan State University Genomics Core (https://rtsf.natsci.msu.edu/genomics/) for Nanopore library preparation and sequencing. Libraries were prepared using the Oxford Nanopore Rapid Barcoding Sequencing Kit (SQK-RBK004) following the manufacturer’s recommendations (Oxford Nanopore Technologies, UK). Barcoded samples (up to 12 per run) were loaded onto a Nanopore FLO-MIN106 (vR9.4.1) flow cell, and sequencing was performed on a GridION sequencer.

Nanopore Guppy software was used for processing raw signals, demultiplexing, and base calling. Reads with a Q score ≥ 7.0 were considered pass reads and used for further quality checking and assembly. An average of 0.77 Gb was obtained per sample. The resulting FASTQ data were imported into the CLC Genomics Workbench (v21.0) (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) for further quality check and assembly using its De Novo Assemble Long Reads plug-in module. The assembly was based on the open-source tool Raven [38]. The default workflow and settings were used, optimized for the assembly quality of long, error-prone reads, such as those typically produced by single-molecule sequencing technologies. Two iterations of contig polishing were executed, creating a partial order alignment (POA) of the reads against the contigs and improving contig quality by rapid consensus calling.

Analysis of Nanopore sequences was performed using the online genomic annotation tool RAST Server (v3.35.5) and verified using the Bacterial and Viral Bioinformatic Resource Center (BV-BRC) (v3.57.14) [39] (Table 1). The presence of ESBL, which harbor blaCTX−M or other β-lactamase genes in Nanopore-derived plasmid sequences was verified using multiple bioinformatic tools, including RAST Server [39], BV-BRC [40], the Center for Genomic Epidemiology (CGE) ResFinder (v4.7.2) [41], and Comparative Antibiotic Resistance Database Resistance Gene Identifier (CARD RGI) (v4.0.1) [42]. Identified ESBL and β-lactamase alleles are listed in Additional File 1.

Retrieval and curation of public plasmid and genome sequences

Nanopore-derived plasmid sequences from human isolates in the Graham study were used as reference queries for BLAST searches against the NCBI database [43]. The Nanopore sequences were included within the set of plasmid genomes retrieved from the NCBI database (accessed August 2022-July 2023). Plasmids with a query coverage greater than 65% were selected as an indicator of alignment length relative to the reference sequence. Additional inclusion criteria required that (1) the sequence entry contained an associated BioProject with whole-genome sequencing data and (2) plasmid sequences were identified as ESBL- or β-lactamase-carrying and associated with members of the Enterobacteriaceae family. Plasmid sequences retrieved from the NCBI database were not restricted by sequencing platform. Instead, sequences were selected based on assembly completeness, regardless of the sequencing technology used.

For dog-derived plasmids, no Nanopore reference sequences were available for comparison. Therefore, the NCBI database was queried using a keyword search “dog plasmids,” and results were filtered to identify plasmids classified as ESBL- or β-lactamase-carrying and associated with Enterobacteriaceae family members. The same inclusion criteria described above were applied to dog-derived plasmids.

In total 99 whole-genome sequences were retrieved, including 92 from human sources (Additional File 4) and 7 from dogs (Additional File 5). To investigate the distribution and regional enrichment of ESBLs and other β-lactamases among humans and dogs, we retrieved 149 ESBL-carrying plasmid sequences from the NCBI database: 125 from humans (Additional File 2) and 24 from dogs (Additional File 3), along with associated sampling location metadata.

Genomic analysis

Several bioinformatic tools were used to analyze genetic features in both plasmid and whole-genome sequences in the collection (Fig. 1). Consistent with the study design, these analyses were conducted to identify shared genomic features between human- and dog-derived isolates that may indicate potential plasmid-mediated dissemination of ESBL genes across hosts.

For all human- and dog-derived ESBL-carrying plasmids, genome annotations were performed using the BV-BRC (v3.57.14) to annotate genes located on both plasmid and chromosomal sequences (https://www.bv-brc.org/) [40]. To investigate potential associations between insertion sequences and ESBL genes, we conducted NCBI BLAST analyses to identify mobile genetic elements flanking individual ESBL genes. Plasmids were also visualized using SnapGene (v8.2) to assess structural features.

The CARD RGI program (v4.0.1) was used to identify ESBLs, other β-lactamase genes, and other antibiotic resistance genes from annotation results (https://card.mcmaster.ca/analyze/rgi) [42]. Plasmid Multi-Locus Sequence Typing (pMLST) was performed on plasmid sequences to examine the plasmid replicon type group for each sequence (https://pubmlst.org/organisms/plasmid-mlst) [44]. Multi-Locus Sequence Typing (MLST) was performed on the chromosome sequences retrieved from the database using the Achtman scheme to determine the sequence type (https://pubmlst.org/multilocus-sequence-typing) [45].

ESBL-producing bacteria are associated with UTIs [46, 47]. Therefore, we screened for virulence genes associated with uropathogenic E. coli (UPEC) using the CGE Virulence Finder 2.0 program (v2.0.5) on human and dog-derived ESBL-producing E. coli (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/VirulenceFinder/) [48]. In addition, in silico serotyping was performed using the CGE Serotype Finder 2.0 program (v2.0.1) to identify O and H antigen profiles in E. coli isolates from both hosts and determine whether shared serotypes were present (https://cge.food.dtu.dk/services/SerotypeFinder/) [48, 49].

Statistical analysis

To assess whether the distribution of Ambler molecular classes (A, B, C, and D) differed between human- and dog-derived plasmids, a chi-squared test with Monte Carlo simulation was performed using 10,000 permutations. Due to the presence of expected cell counts < 5 in a 2 × 4 contingency table, a simulation-based approach was used to provide a robust estimate of the p-value. The analysis was performed in RStudio (v3.6.0) (http://www.rstudio.com/) using the chisq.test function with simulate.p.value = TRUE and a two-tailed significance level of α = 0.05 was applied for all comparisons.

Phylogenetic analysis of ESBL plasmids and bacterial strains

Human- and dog-derived ESBL-producing plasmid sequences retrieved from the NCBI database were aligned using the Multiple Alignment using Fast Fourier Transform (MAFFT) program (v7.520) with the FFT-NS-2 strategy, which employs a two-step progressive alignment based on an initial guide tree constructed from pairwise distances (https://mafft.cbrc.jp/alignment/software/) [50]. To investigate the phylogenetic relationships between human- and dog-derived plasmids, a Neighbor-Joining (NJ) tree was constructed using the Tamura-Nei genetic distance model in Geneious Tree Builder (v2026.0.2) (https://www.geneious.com/#), with 1,000 bootstrap replicates to assess branch support and clustering patterns. Plasmid trees were then visualized using the Interactive Tree of Life (iTOL) program (v7.5) (https://itol.embl.de/) [51].

For chromosomal phylogenies, whole-genome sequences underwent core genome alignment using the Roary pangenome pipeline (v3.13.0) with the -e and -mafft parameters for soft core genome definition and MAFFT-based alignment [52]. A core gene FASTA alignment file was generated and used to construct a maximum-likelihood (ML) phylogeny with RAxML (v8.2.12) [53], using the GTRGAMMA model (-m GTRGAMMA), a random seed (-p 12345), bootstrap resampling (-x 67890), and 1,000 replicates. The resulting chromosomal trees were also visualized in iTOL. These analyses were conducted to evaluate the evolutionary relationships of ESBL plasmids and E. coli strains from both hosts, providing insight into potential horizontal gene transfer (HGT) and cross-species dissemination events. In parallel, MLST, serotyping, virulence factor identification (UPEC-associated genes), and ESBL gene profiling were performed on the same genomes set to assess clonal relatedness between human- and dog-derived E. coli strains.

Results

Distribution of human and dog-derived ESBL and β-lactamase resistance by class and type

Plasmid sequences were annotated using BV-BRC to identify genes associated with conjugative plasmids. Following annotation, the CARD RGI was employed to identify ESBL genes and other β-lactamase genes, which were categorized by Ambler class (A, B, C, and D) and by gene family.

We hypothesized that class A ESBL genes would be the most prevalent among both human- and dog-derived plasmids. Our findings revealed that among human-derived plasmids (n = 125), class A β-lactamases (blaCTX−M, blaTEM, blaSHV) were the most prevalent (74.4%; n = 93), followed by class C (blaCMY) (17.6%; n = 22), class D (blaOXA) (13.6%; n = 17), and class B (blaNDM) (5.6%; n = 7) (Fig. 2A). Similarly, among dog-derived plasmids (n = 24), class A β-lactamases accounted for the majority (70.8%; n = 18), followed by class C (20.8%; n = 5) and class D (8.3%; n = 2) (Fig. 2F). No class B β-lactamases were identified in dog-derived plasmids. A chi-squared test with Monte Carlo simulation (10,000 permutations) revealed no statistically significant difference in β-lactamase class distribution between human and dog plasmids (p = 0.8411).

Subsequent analyses pertained exclusively to human-derived ESBL plasmids (n = 125). Percentages within each Ambler class represent the frequency of β-lactamase genes detected within that class, as some plasmids contained multiple β-lactamase genes. Within class A β-lactamases (n = 101 genes), ESBL genes were primarily represented by blaCTX−M alleles, with blaCTX−M−14 (31.7%; n = 32) and blaCTX−M−15 (21.8%; n = 22) being the most frequently detected. Other class A β-lactamase genes were also identified, including blaTEM−1 (31.7%; n = 32) (Fig. 2B). Within class B β-lactamases (n = 7 genes), all genes detected were blaNDM−5 (100%; n = 7) (Fig. 2C). Within class C β-lactamases (n = 21 genes), AmpC β-lactamase genes blaCMY−2 (57.1%; n = 12) and blaDHA−1 (19.0%; n = 4) predominated in this class (Fig. 2D). Finally, within class D β-lactamases (n = 17 genes), plasmids were predominantly encoded by blaOXA−1 (88.2%; n = 15) and blaOXA−4 (11.8%; n = 2) β-lactamase genes (Fig. 2E).

In contrast, this analysis refers specifically to dog-derived ESBL plasmids (n = 24). Again, percentages within each Ambler class represent the frequency of β-lactamase genes detected within that class. Within class A β-lactamases (n = 27 genes), ESBL genes were again primarily represented by blaCTX−M alleles, with blaCTX−M−1 (29.6%; n = 8) and blaCTX−M−55 (14.8%; n = 4) being the most frequently detected. Additional class A β-lactamase genes were also identified, including blaTEM−1 (25.9%; n = 7) (Fig. 2G). Notably, no class B β-lactamase genes were identified. Within class C β-lactamases (n = 5 genes), we detected AmpC β-lactamase genes blaCMY−2 (100%; n = 5) (Fig. 2H). Finally, within class D β-lactamases (n = 2 genes), plasmids were encoded by blaOXA−1 (50%; n = 1) and blaOXA−48 (50%; n = 1) β-lactamase genes (Fig. 2I).

Geographic distribution of β-lactamase Ambler classes in human- and dog-derived plasmids

Among human-derived sequences, plasmids encoding class A β-lactamases exhibited the widest geographical spread, encompassing isolates from six continents—North America, South America, Europe, Asia, Africa, and Australia—with China, South Korea, and the USA as the most represented countries. In contrast, dog-derived class A β-lactamase plasmids were largely limited to China, Switzerland, France, Kenya, the United Kingdom, and Israel. Human-derived class B β-lactamase plasmids were predominantly isolated from countries in Asia, Europe, and North America, particularly Bangladesh, China, Italy, and the USA. Plasmids encoding class C β-lactamases in humans primarily originated from Asia and Europe, with notable representation from Taiwan, South Korea, and Switzerland. Meanwhile, plasmids encoding class C β-lactamases from dogs were found in European and North American countries, including Denmark, the United Kingdom, and the USA. Lastly, human-derived class D β-lactamase plasmids were recovered from a broad set of regions across Asia, Europe, Africa, and North America, including China, South Korea, Sweden, Bangladesh, Mali, and the USA. In comparison, dog-derived plasmids encoding class D β-lactamases were geographically restricted to Asia and Africa, represented solely by Kenya and Thailand. Overall, these results indicate that human-derived plasmids carrying class A β-lactamase genes, including CTX-M genes, in our dataset were represented across all six continents, whereas dog-derived plasmids were more geographically restricted (Fig. 3).

Analysis of clade A in neighbor-joining tree

Clade A (lime green) consisted of plasmids from the genera Escherichia, Klebsiella, Salmonella, and Shigella, with high bootstrap support for the clade (bootstrap values > 95%). Within Clade A, the tree bifurcated into two subclades: A1 (light pink, n = 5) and A2 (hot pink, n = 22 human, n = 1 dog). Within Clade A1, most plasmids harbored class A β-lactamases, including ESBLs blaCTX−M−14, blaCTX−M−15, and blaCTX−M−65, as well as the β-lactamase gene blaTEM−1, primarily associated with the IncN replicon group. In addition, plasmids within Clade A1 carried resistance genes for other ARGs, including sulfonamides, aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, macrolides, quinolones, and trimethoprim. Within Clade A2, the plasmids harbored ESBLs from the CTX-M family and other β-lactamase genes such as blaNDM−5, blaDHA−1, and blaOXA. Again, these plasmids carried resistance genes for other classes of antibiotics similar to Clade A1; however, Clade A2 also contained chloramphenicol resistance genes. Lastly, Clade A2 appeared to consist mainly of IncF plasmids, except for four. One IncF dog-derived plasmid carrying the blaTEM−1 β-lactamase gene clustered with human-derived plasmids within Clade A. Additionally, the blaTEM−1 β-lactamase gene, and macrolide resistance identified in this dog plasmid was also identified in human plasmids classified as IncN. Overall, Clade A1 was differentiated from Clade A2 by the specific replicon groups represented, as well as by the diversity of Ambler classes present. While both clades contained plasmids from multiple replicon groups, some of the same ESBL genes such as blaCTX−M−14 and blaCTX−M−15 were present in both, regardless of replicon type. This overlap suggests that the dissemination of ESBL genes may be occurring between IncN and IncF plasmids. Notably, IncN plasmids were associated exclusively with class A β-lactamases including ESBLs in the CTX-M family, whereas IncF plasmids carried a broader range of β-lactamases belonging to all 4 Ambler classes. This pattern suggests a narrower role for IncN plasmids in disseminating blaCTX−Ms however, IncF plasmids appear to contribute to a broader role in disseminating ESBLs as well as additional β-lactamases. In addition to ESBLs, plasmids in both clades carried other ARGs, indicating that dissemination extends beyond β-lactam resistance.

Within Clade B, the tree multifurcated into four subclades: B1 (grey, n = 2 human), B2 (maroon, n = 4 dog), B3 (blue, n = 13 human), and B4 (red, n = 20 human, n = 5 dog). The IncI1 plasmid harbored the class A ESBL gene blaCTX−M−65, whereas the IncA/C plasmid harbored class B and class D β-lactamase genes (blaNDM−5, and blaOXA−1). Clade B2 contained four dog-derived IncF plasmids that harbored class A ESBL genes (blaCTX−M−55, and blaCTX−M−65) as well as the β-lactamase gene blaTEM−1, but no β-lactamases belonging to Ambler classes (B-D). Three of the four dog-derived plasmids also carried sulfonamide, macrolide, tetracycline, aminoglycoside, and trimethoprim resistance genes. Clade B3 contained only human-derived IncF plasmids with only the blaCTX−M−15 ESBL gene being identified. Among both human- and dog-derived E. coli genomes, the predominant ESBL genes identified in the maximum-likelihood tree were blaCTX−M−14, and blaCTX−M−15, as well as β-lactamase genes blaTEM−1, and blaCMY−2 (Fig. 8). Discussion

Extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL)-producing bacteria contribute to a significant portion of antibiotic-resistant infections globally, posing a serious challenge to public health [1]. These enzymes are predominantly carried by members of the Enterobacteriaceae family and confer resistance to β-lactam antibiotics. The spread of ESBLs has led to increased mortality rates, prolonged hospital stays, and higher healthcare costs [54]. Furthermore, the spread of ESBLs is largely facilitated by horizontal gene transfer (HGT), enabling their dissemination not only in hospital settings but also in community-acquired infections, further complicating efforts to control their prevalence [55]. This study aimed to determine whether ESBL genes exhibited patterns consistent with cross-host dissemination between human- and dog-derived ESBL plasmids via HGT in community-acquired settings.

Our study revealed that plasmids from both human- and dog-derived bacterial isolates harbored class A β-lactamases, with blaCTX−M ESBL genes being the most frequently detected. These genes were identified in plasmids associated with members of the Enterobacteriaceae family, including E. coli, Salmonella enterica, K. pneumoniae, Citrobacter spp., and Shigella spp. This finding is consistent with previous reports describing the widespread distribution of ESBLs, specifically blaCTX−M genes, in the United States [56]. Furthermore, a chi-squared test revealed no statistically significant difference in β-lactamase class distribution between human- and dog-derived plasmids. This suggests that the observed class frequencies reflect shared plasmid dynamics rather than host-specific patterns. These findings support the widespread distribution of class A ESBL genes, particularly blaCTX−M alleles, across the plasmid sequences derived from both human and dog isolates. IS5 elements were observed flanking ESBL genes such as blaCTX−M−1, and blaCTX−M−55, as well as other β-lactamase genes including blaTEM−1, and blaCMY. These findings suggest that IS5 elements could play a potential role in the shared carriage and dissemination of ESBLs and other β-lactamases between humans and dogs. Neighbor-joining tree analysis further showed that plasmids from human- and dog-derived isolates clustered together in multiple clades, indicating that they share genetic similarity. IncF, IncI1, and IncN were the most commonly detected replicon types in human- and dog-derived plasmids carrying ESBLs and other β-lactamase genes, highlighting the potential these plasmids may serve in disseminating these genes. Additionally, ARGs were also detected in IncF and IncI1 plasmids including genes conferring resistance to sulfonamides, aminoglycosides, tetracyclines, and macrolides, suggesting that these plasmids may disseminate ARGs beyond β-lactamases.

ST131, ST167, ST10, and ST410 predominated among the E. coli strains analyzed, with ST131 and ST167 detected exclusively among human isolates, whereas ST10 and ST410 were shared between human and dog isolates, suggesting potential clonal overlap across hosts. Virulence gene analysis in ML trees revealed that UPEC, in addition to other E. coli pathotypes, may contribute to the dissemination of ESBL and other β-lactamase-producing plasmids. O25:H4 and O101:H9 were the most frequently detected serotypes in human- and dog-derived E. coli strains, and we observed antigenic variation within shared sequence types, suggesting the potential for new variants to arise during cross-host dissemination events.

Epidemiological studies report that blaCTX−M genes have increased in prevalence since the 1990s, so we expected to observe a similar trend in both human and dog populations [57]. In our human dataset, blaCTX−M−14 and blaCTX−M−15 accounted for at least 50% of the class A ESBL distribution. In the dog population, we observed a high prevalence of blaCTX−M−1 and blaCTX−M−55 genes, which aligns with findings from a previous study [58]. We also expected to observe blaTEM genes in both human and dog populations, given that this β-lactamase gene family was reported to be predominant during the 1980s and 1990s [59]. Consistent with prior genomic characterization studies, our results showed that the blaTEM−1 gene was widely distributed in both populations [60, 61].

There are considerations to take into account for this analysis. Public databases like the NCBI constantly get updated with newly submitted sequences, therefore this analysis represents what was available during time the database was accessed. It is also important to note that because plasmid sequences were retrieved from the NCBI database, the observed distribution of β-lactamase genes in each Ambler class may reflect sampling bias in where genomes have been sequenced, rather than true global distribution. While the number of sequences from Europe and Asia likely reflects sampling density in public databases rather than true epidemiological dominance, the overlapping geographic representation of human- and dog-derived plasmids across regions underscores the value of a One Health perspective. Therefore, integrating human and veterinary surveillance would allow for a more complete understanding of the global distribution and dissemination of ESBLs. These patterns may also be influenced by the prevalence of these genes in the geographical regions in which the sequences were originally sampled. For our plasmid collection, we obtained samples from regions across the world; however, the regional distribution was skewed toward class A β-lactamases, which are the most prevalent worldwide [57]. Furthermore, due to the small sample size for dog-derived ESBL plasmids (n = 24) compared to human-derived plasmids (n = 125), there was less apparent regional variation among dog isolates. This imbalance is likely due to the underrepresentation of companion animal plasmid sequences in public databases such as NCBI, rather than a lack of diversity in the dog population. Additionally, the small sample size of dog isolates may have contributed to the lack of statistical significance observed for the distribution of Ambler classes. However, the presence of classes A, C, and D β-lactamases across multiple regions among dog-derived plasmids suggests that, we were still able to obtain a diverse collection, despite the small sample size. The uneven sampling also introduces bias that may affect the generalizability of patterns observed in dog-derived plasmids, particularly in terms of regional variation, plasmid diversity, and mobile genetic element composition. Additionally, some plasmid sequences for human and dog-derived isolates originated from the same studies, potentially reducing observed diversity within the dataset. Furthermore, incomplete metadata limited our ability to distinguish isolates that were community-associated or hospital-associated, which could obscure patterns of ESBL dissemination in community settings. Thus, while this work represents an important first step, for future studies, filtering out samples isolated from hospitals would improve specificity. Yet it may reduce sample size, making it difficult to detect significant patterns and accurately assess dissemination dynamics. One should also consider shared environmental or food sources that may also contribute to the relatedness observed among human- and dog-derived isolates. Therefore, broader One Health analyses that include food, environmental, and livestock reservoirs are needed to better understand the transmission dynamics underlying the relatedness of human and dog isolates.

We identified identical mobile genetic elements upstream of ESBL genes in both human and dog-derived plasmids, underscoring their potential role in the horizontal gene transfer (HGT) of ESBLs across species. Mobile genetic elements, including insertion sequences (ISs), transposons, and integrons, are known contributors to ESBL dissemination, particularly for blaCTX−M variants [62]. Our results showed that human and dog-derived plasmids encoding β-lactamase genes such as blaTEM−1 were associated with Tn3 elements [56, 63]. These findings align with other genetic analyses that have identified Tn3 elements in other members of the Enterobacteriaceae family, including Salmonella Typhimurium and Klebsiella quasipneumoniae [64, 65].

The blaCTX−M−1 gene was predominantly carried by dog-derived ESBL plasmids surrounded by IS5 elements, consistent with prior studies isolating blaCTX−M−1 from dog fecal samples [58, 66]. One human-derived ESBL-producing plasmid (accession no.: KJ484629) also harbored a blaCTX−M−1 flanked by an IS5 element [67], suggesting that IS5 could facilitate cross-host mobilization of blaCTX−M−1 genes via its transposase ins5A [68]. IS5 elements were also found upstream of blaCMY−2, an AmpC β-lactamase gene, in both human- and dog-derived plasmids, underscoring their broad role in mobilizing ESBL and AmpC genes across hosts [69].

Plasmids carrying blaCTX−M−55 ESBL genes differed in their genetic context. IS5 elements were detected in dog-derived plasmids, whereas IS26 and ISEcp1 were identified in human-derived plasmids. This difference suggests that blaCTX−M−55 genes may be disseminated through distinct sources. While our dataset does not directly examine these reservoirs, prior studies have implicated food animals (e.g., cattle, chickens, ducks) as important contributors [58, 70]. This is supported by molecular characterization of blaCTX−M−55 genes in E. coli, Salmonella, and Shigella, showing associations with IS26 and ISEcp1 mobile genetic elements [70–72].

Overall, our findings highlight the distinct roles of MGEs in ESBL dissemination. The predominance of IS5 elements in dog-derived plasmids and their association with both ESBL and AmpC genes underscore the potential for these elements to mediate resistance across hosts. Taken together, these findings are consistent with IS5 elements contributing to the mobilization of blaCTX−M−1, whereas blaCTX−M−55 genes may be disseminated to both humans and dogs from external reservoirs, such as food animals or the environment. Future studies should investigate the ecological and community-level factors influencing the distribution of IS5, IS26, and ISEcp1 elements to better understand the mechanisms driving ESBL dissemination and spillover in community settings.

Class A ESBLs were predominantly carried by IncI1 and IncF plasmids, mainly derived from E. coli strains. Other members of the Enterobacteriaceae family, including E. coli, Klebsiella, Salmonella, Enterobacter, and Shigella spp., were also found to harbor these plasmids. Many studies have demonstrated that Gram-negative organisms, particularly those carrying blaCTX−M’s genes, are responsible for the spread of ESBLs [16, 56]. Within clades, we identified plasmids from human- and dog-derived isolates harboring ESBL genes blaCTX−M−14 and blaCTX−M−15, and β-lactamase genes blaTEM−1, highlighting patterns consistent with cross-host dissemination of these genes. Furthermore, we detected multiple ARGs within ESBL plasmids from both hosts, suggesting possible dissemination of resistance beyond β-lactams.

Based on pMLST, IncI1 plasmids harbored ESBL genes blaCTX−M−1, and blaCTX−M−14 which was mainly carried by E. coli strains, consistent with previous studies characterizing IncI1 plasmids from humans and dogs [73]. In contrast, other studies have reported that IncI1 plasmids carrying blaCTX−M−1, and blaCTX−M−14 in strains of Salmonella strains derived from food-producing animals [74–77]. This finding highlights the possibility that human- and dog-derived ESBL-producing strains may share plasmid backbones similar to those described in food-producing animal sources suggesting a potential external reservoir. However, our dataset cannot directly confirm acquisition from animals. Compared with IncF and IncN plasmids, only 14% of IncI1 plasmids carried other ARGs, suggesting that dissemination of non-ESBL ARGs is less frequent in IncI1 plasmids. By contrast, IncF plasmids frequently carried ESBL genes blaCTX−M−14, blaCTX−M−15, and blaCTX−M−1, as well as the β-lactamase gene blaTEM−1. These plasmids were identified in both human- and dog-derived isolates and across multiple members of Enterobacteriaceae, indicating that IncF plasmids may contribute to the dissemination of β-lactamase genes including ESBLs across bacterial hosts. Prior studies have reported that IncF plasmids in humans often carry blaCTX−M−15 genes [78], which is consistent with our findings. The detection of additional β-lactamase genes including blaTEM−1, blaCTX−M−14, and blaCTX−M−1 on IncF plasmids in both human- and dog-derived isolates further suggests that these resistance genes may circulate across host populations.

Among these dominant lineages, ST131 and ST410 are recognized globally as high-risk clones responsible for the dissemination of blaCTX−M−15 genes worldwide [79, 80]. ST131, particularly serotype O25:H4, is globally recognized as a high-risk clone associated with ESBL production, increased virulence, persistent colonization, and the capacity to acquire carbapenemase genes such as blaNDM and blaOXA−48 [35, 81, 82]. This lineage is often multidrug-resistant, including resistance to fluoroquinolones, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, and aminoglycosides, and is strongly associated with community-acquired infections such as UTIs, pneumonia, intra-abdominal infections, and bloodstream infections [35]. ST131, particularly serotype O25:H4, has been shown to be commonly associated with IncF plasmids carrying blaCTX−M−15 [35]. Since ST131 belongs to phylogroup B2, it is also known to harbor more virulence factor-encoding genes that contribute to extraintestinal infections [81].

Although ST131 was confined to human isolates in this study, previous studies have reported ST131 carriage in companion animal dogs indicating that this lineage can circulate across host species [83, 84]. The absence of ST131 among dog-derived isolates in our dataset may reflect the small number of dog isolates analyzed or sampling bias associated with sequences that were publicly available. On another note, different ecological niches and transmission dynamics may also influence the distribution of specific E. coli lineages and which ones can thrive in each host. Despite the absence of ST131 in dogs in this dataset, the detection of shared ST10 and ST410 lineages underscores the potential for companion animals to harbor or exchange closely related ESBL-producing E. coli clones. Collectively, the presence of shared and host-specific lineages supports the possibility of cross-host dissemination and highlights the need for integrated One Health surveillance of high-risk ESBL-producing E. coli [85].

Belas et al. [24] reported shared multidrug-resistant E. coli lineages ST131 and ST648 in community-acquired UTIs from unrelated humans and companion animals in Portugal [25]. While our study differs in using publicly available plasmid datasets, it builds upon that work by offering a genome-based comparative analysis of plasmid replicon types (IncF, IncI1) and MGEs (IS5, Tn3), consistent with clonal sharing and plasmid-mediated HGT across hosts. Unlike Belas et al. [24], who primarily used phenotypic methods for characterizing resistance, virulence genes, and MLST, there was no examination of plasmid backbones or MGEs responsible for ESBL dissemination. ST131 and ST410 have also been detected in humans, domestic animals, and environmental isolates [85]. While shared STs suggest a common ancestor, we did not examine clonal complex relationships to determine whether these isolates are truly clonally related, so direct relatedness remains uncertain. Antigenic variation observed within shared STs suggests adaptive diversification of O-antigens that may enhance immune evasion or phage resistance [86, 87]. Virulence gene analysis further indicated that, in addition to UPEC, other E. coli pathotypes such as enteroaggregative E. coli (EAEC) and enterotoxigenic E. coli (ETEC) may also contribute to ESBL dissemination [88].

Conclusions

Overall, our study provides evidence that HGT facilitates the dissemination of β-lactamase genes, including ESBLs as well as other ARGs between human and dog populations, with mobile genetic elements such as IS5 frequently observed flanking resistance genes, consistent with a mobilization role. The identification of shared STs and plasmid groups highlights the potential for cross-species dissemination of ESBLs and ARGs at both the plasmid and clonal levels. These findings emphasize the need to consider surveillance of dogs, as well as human reservoirs—especially in community-acquired settings. By understanding the dynamics of cross-species dissemination, we can help mitigate the spread of ESBLs within community settings.

Although reliance on a public database of available plasmid sequences provided valuable insights, we acknowledge that sampling biases related to temporal, geographic, and strain-specific prevalence may have influenced our results. Therefore, future studies using geographically diverse, longitudinal samples would strengthen our understanding of ESBL dissemination patterns in dogs and humans. There also needs to be expanded efforts to improve sequencing and data sharing for veterinary and companion animal sources, which are critical for accurately assessing regional and genetic variation in plasmid-mediated resistance. Thus, future research should explore ecological and community factors that drive the dissemination of MGEs and the roles that other community sources play in the spread of ESBLs. Our findings build upon prior studies such as Belas et al. [24] by advancing the understanding of how shared plasmid structures, MGEs, and STs contribute to cross-species ESBL dissemination, underscoring the importance of integrating molecular and genomic surveillance to better understand cross-species AR dynamics. By identifying the mechanisms underlying ESBL gene dissemination among both hosts, we can identify strategies to mitigate the spread of antibiotic resistance at the community level.
