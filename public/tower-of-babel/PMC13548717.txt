Induced of Giardia lamblia Infection on metabolic and hepatic alterations
Authors: R. N. Hamoo, A. H. Suaan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The present study investigated the metabolic and hepatic effects of experimental Giardia lamblia infection in male rats over a four-week post-infection period. Results demonstrated that Giardia lamblia infection significantly disrupted glucose homeostasis, as evidenced by a progressive and time-dependent elevation in serum glucose levels. Infected rats exhibited markedly higher glucose concentrations compared with controls, reaching a peak of 472.40 ± 200.86 mg/dL at four weeks post-infection, indicating impaired intestinal absorption and dysregulated glucose metabolism.Concurrently, serum insulin levels were significantly reduced in infected rats, with the highest post-infection value (2.543 ± 0.5431) remaining lower than the control group. Assessment of insulin resistance using the HOMA-IR index revealed a notable increase in infected groups (59.49 ± 61.73), accompanied by a significant decline in insulin sensitivity (0.345 ± 0.132). These findings suggest that Giardia lamblia infection promotes insulin resistance and disrupts systemic glucose regulation, likely through inflammatory mechanisms and alterations in gut epithelial function levels increased progressively over the four-week period, reaching 180.00 ± 75.54 U/L and 492.45 ± 389.57 U/L, respectively, reflecting hepatocellular stress or damage induced by systemic inflammatory responses associated with intestinal infection. In contrast, serum albumin concentrations were consistently higher in infected rats compared with controls (up to 4.48 ± 0.303 g/dL), though these changes did not reach statistical significance, potentially indicating adaptive hepatic protein synthesis in response to infection.Overall, these results demonstrate that Giardia lamblia infection exerts profound systemic metabolic and hepatic effects, including hyperglycemia, hypoinsulinemia, increased insulin resistance, and elevated liver enzymes. The findings highlight the broader physiological impact of intestinal parasitic infections and underscore the need to consider their metabolic consequences in both experimental models and clinical settings.

Introduction

Giardia lamblia is a flagellated intestinal protozoan parasite that infects humans and animals worldwide, including Iraq [1, 2]. Infection occurs primarily through the ingestion of food or water contaminated with the cysts of the parasite, leading to Giardia lambliasis [3, 4]. The parasite colonizes the upper part of the small intestine, where it adheres to the intestinal mucosa and causes a range of gastrointestinal disturbances. These disturbances are mainly characterized by malabsorption of disaccharides, fats, and fat-soluble vitamins, which negatively affects metabolic processes and energy production [5, 6].

Giardia lamblia infection has both direct and indirect effects on the host. The parasite primarily infects the small intestine, particularly in children, leading to malnutrition and, in severe cases, growth retardation, especially in developing countries [7, 8]. One of the most common routes of infection is the ingestion of cyst-contaminated water, as the parasite is frequently found in lakes, ponds, marshes, and water streams [9]. Although, the relationship between Giardia lamblia infection and diabetes mellitus is not considered a direct causal one, several studies suggest that Giardia lambliasis may influence glucose and insulin metabolism, as well as alter albumin levels in the body. These effects are attributed to the parasite-induced damage to intestinal epithelial cells and disruption of nutrient absorption mechanisms [10]. Accumulating evidence indicates that Giardia lamblia infection, particularly chronic infection, affects the host immune system and the intestinal environment (gut microbiome). Such alterations may subsequently lead to changes in glucose and lipid metabolism [11, 12]. Furthermore, Giardia lamblia infection has been shown to modify the expression of genes associated with glucose metabolism and cellular metabolic homeostasis in intestinal epithelial cells [1].

Malabsorption of sodium and glucose has been associated with a reduction in the number of villous epithelial cells in the intestine and increased intestinal barrier permeability, which represents a key pathogenic mechanism underlying diarrhea in Giardia lambliasis [13]. Moreover, certain intestinal parasites, including Giardia lamblia lamblia, induce the production of reactive oxygen species, which damage cellular components such as proteins, lipids, and nucleic acids. This oxidative stress triggers immune responses involving further generation of free radicals [14].

Although Giardia spp. primarily infect the small intestine, increasing evidence suggests that giardiasis may also exert systemic effects beyond the gastrointestinal tract, including hepatic and metabolic alterations. Experimental studies demonstrated that Giardia infection can induce oxidative stress and inflammatory responses that contribute to liver dysfunction and tissue injury. Recent animal studies reported elevated oxidative stress markers and hepatic biochemical disturbances associated with giardiasis, indicating a possible link between intestinal infection and liver pathology. Furthermore, oxidative stress has been recognized as an important mechanism in parasite-induced hepatic injury through the imbalance between reactive oxygen species production and antioxidant defenses [15].

Accordingly, the current study aims to evaluate the effect of Giardia lamblia on blood sugar and insulin levels and its relationship to insulin resistance and sensitivity experimentally in rats.

Microscopic examination

A microscopic examination was carried out to confirm the presence of the parasite in stool sample, by using the saline sedimentation method, as described by Ritchie [16] and examined using a light microscope with 100x magnification.

Parasite isolation and preparation of parasite dose

In order to isolate Giardia lamblia cysts were isolated from stool samples, the samples were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 5 min multiple times. Parasites were identified microscopically using Giemsa stain and examined under a light microscope. To Prepared dose, cysts were counted at 20 µL stool sample solution was placed on a slide, and dose of 150 cysts per animal was used [17].

Molecular examination

Conventional PCR was used for the molecular detection of Giardia lamblia lamblia. DNA was extracted from human stool samples using a DNA extraction kit (Geneaid), and the extracted DNA was stored at − 20 °C. Specific primers for Giardia lamblia lamblia were prepared by Macrogen for amplification of the target DNA.

Laboratory animals

Study were used Swiss albino rats, aged 6–8 weeks and weighing between 175–200 g. The rats were orally inoculated with Giardia lamblia cysts using a gavage tube, with dose containing approximately 150 cysts. Blood samples were taken from heart of rats and collected in test tubes containing gel without anticoagulant at 2, 3, and 4 weeks P.I (post infection). The blood sample tubes were centrifuged at a speed of 3000 xg for 15 min. The serum was subsequently preserved at −20 °C for biochemical examination [18, 19].

Laboratory examination

Measurement of glucose in serum

Quantitative determination of serum glucose by the enzymatic colorimetric method. Kit from (Giesse-Italy) was used, and the procedure was automatically performed by the chemistry analyzer device (Smart-120, USA).

Measurement of insulin in serum

Rat Insulin (INS) ELISA Kit (shidealtech-China. Item No.: ADL-ELRT00483). The double antibody sandwich method was used.

Homeostatic model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA-IR)

Insulin resistance was assessed by the HOMA-IR calculated after fasting rats for 12 h as:HOMA-IR=Fasting insulin (µU/ml) × Fasting glucose (mmol/l)/22.5. (Er et al., [20, 21]), Higher HOMA-IR values are indicative of insulin resistance.

The hormonal parameters in experimental rats were evaluated using specific ELISA kits. These kits were used to measure serum levels of insulin, thyroxine (T4), triiodothyronine (T3), and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) to assess the physiological and metabolic changes associated with the experimental condition.

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software. Differences among groups were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (One-Way ANOVA).

Discussion

The present study demonstrated that experimental Giardia lamblia infection significantly alters glucose metabolism in male rats. Serum glucose levels showed a progressive and time-dependent increase following infection, reaching a peak of 353.2 ± 14.53 mg/dL at four weeks post-infection (Table 8). These findings are consistent with previous reports indicating that Giardia lamblia infection can induce hyperglycemia by disrupting normal intestinal function and nutrient absorption [11, 12, 23]. Furthermore, the observed decrease in serum insulin levels (Table 3) suggests that infection may impair pancreatic β-cell function or enhance insulin clearance, contributing to relative insulin deficiency. The highest insulin levels recorded at four weeks post-infection remained significantly lower than those of the control group, indicating a disruption in insulin homeostasis. These findings align with prior studies demonstrating that Giardia lamblia infection can modulate host insulin secretion, potentially through inflammatory cytokine-mediated β-cell dysfunction [1, 7, 8]. In the current study, infected groups exhibited significantly higher insulin resistance values compared with the control group, reaching 5.243 ± 0.90 at 4 weeks post-infection. Concurrently, alterations in insulin sensitivity were observed, indicating disruption of normal metabolic regulation. These findings may reflect the systemic metabolic consequences associated with Giardia infection, including intestinal dysfunction, altered nutrient absorption, and host metabolic stress. Statistical analysis using one-way ANOVA confirmed highly significant differences among the experimental groups, with large effect sizes (η2 = 0.80), supporting the substantial influence of infection on metabolic parameters.. These changes indicate that Giardia lamblia infection promotes insulin resistance, likely via systemic and local inflammatory responses as well as alterations in gut microbiota composition. Chronic Giardia lamblia infection has been shown to induce a pro-inflammatory environment in the intestine, which can impair insulin signaling pathways in peripheral tissues, thereby increasing insulin resistance [11, 12, 24].

The progressive nature of these metabolic disturbances suggests a cumulative effect of prolonged infection. Early disruptions in glucose absorption, coupled with inflammatory-mediated insulin dysregulation, may collectively contribute to the observed hyperglycemia and reduced insulin sensitivity. Such findings highlight the systemic metabolic consequences of intestinal parasitic infections beyond local gastrointestinal pathology [25] Overall, the present findings support the hypothesis that Giardia infection may exert systemic metabolic effects extending beyond intestinal pathology, potentially contributing to impaired glucose regulation and insulin resistance. Additional mechanistic investigations involving inflammatory cytokines, oxidative stress biomarkers, gut microbiota profiling, and pancreatic histopathology are necessary to better elucidate the pathophysiological basis of these alterations.

The observed increase in transaminase levels may reflect direct or indirect hepatic effects induced by the parasite. Although Giardia lamblia primarily colonizes the small intestine, systemic effects mediated by inflammatory responses, oxidative stress, and gut-liver axis interactions can result in liver enzyme elevation. Similar findings were reported by Solaymani-Mohammadi [7, 8] and Klimczak et al. [11, 12, 26] who observed that intestinal parasitic infections could induce oxidative and inflammatory-mediated hepatocellular injury. The gradual increase over time suggests that prolonged infection exacerbates hepatic stress, possibly due to chronic intestinal inflammation, malabsorption of nutrients, and systemic immune activation.

Furthermore, the present study revealed an increase in serum albumin concentrations in infected rats compared with controls (Table 1). Albumin levels were consistently higher across all post-infection intervals, reaching 4.48 ± 0.303 g/dL at two weeks post-infection. Although these differences did not reach statistical significance (p-values 0.065–0.08), the trend suggests that Giardia lamblia infection may alter protein metabolism or induce compensatory hepatic protein synthesis. Previous studies have shown that parasitic infections giardiasis may be associated with systemic inflammatory and metabolic alterations in experimentally infected animals. Further studies are needed to clarify the underlying mechanisms [23, 24]. metabolic disturbances through multiple interconnected mechanisms, including intestinal inflammation, oxidative stress, and disruption of the gut–liver axis. Persistent adhesion of trophozoites to the intestinal epithelium can compromise epithelial barrier integrity and impair nutrient absorption, particularly glucose transport, thereby contributing to alterations in glucose homeostasis and insulin signaling pathways. Moreover, infection-associated inflammatory responses and oxidative stress may play a role in hepatocellular dysfunction and elevation of liver enzyme activities, indicating that the pathological impact of Giardia lamblia is not limited to the gastrointestinal tract.

Additionally, the observed metabolic alterations may be consistent with previously reported inflammatory and physiological responses associated with prolonged Giardia infection. However, no microbiota or hepatic functional analyses were performed in the present study, and therefore these mechanisms remain speculative. Likewise, the non-significant increase in albumin levels observed in infected rats should be interpreted cautiously, as factors such as dehydration secondary to diarrhea could not be excluded [7, 8, 11, 12, 27]. Further studies are recommended to investigate inflammatory markers, oxidative stress parameters, and gut microbiome composition in experimentally infected animals to better clarify the underlying mechanisms associated with Giardia infection and its metabolic effects.

Conclusion

These findings suggest that Giardia lamblia infection may be associated with systemic metabolic effects beyond the intestinal tract. These, results indicate possible broader metabolic alterations associated with intestinal parasitic infection, which require further investigation to clarify the underlying mechanisms.
