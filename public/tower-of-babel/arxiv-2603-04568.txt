Mask-aware inference with State-Space Models
Ignasi Mas, Ramon Morros, Javier Ruiz-Hidalgo, Ivan Huerta

Abstract
Many real-world computer vision tasks, such as depth completion, must handle inputs with arbitrarily shaped regions of missing or invalid data. For Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs), Partial Convolutions solved this by a mask-aware re-normalization conditioned only on valid pixels. Recently, State Space Models (SSMs) like Mamba have emerged, offering high performance with linear complexity. However, these architectures lack an inherent mechanism for handling such arbitrarily shaped invalid data at inference time. To bridge this gap, we introduce Partial Vision Mamba (PVM), a novel architectural component that ports the principles of partial operations to the Mamba backbone. We also define a series of rules to design architectures using PVM. We show the efficacy and generalizability of our approach in the tasks of depth completion, image inpainting, and classification with invalid data.



1
Introduction

Many real-world vision pipelines do not receive complete, valid images due to sensor errors, application constraints, or other problems (
e.g
.
, sparse LiDAR or anonymized regions). Concurrently, State Space Models (SSMs), particularly Mamba
[
13
]
and its vision variants (like Vision Mamba
[
57
]
or VMamba
[
29
]
), are rapidly becoming a dominant architecture in computer vision. They have proven to be a strong alternative to Transformers
[
42
]
by achieving state-of-the-art or competitive performance while maintaining linear complexity, making them ideal for high-resolution tasks and efficiency-critical applications.

Standard architectures (such as CNNs or Mambas) are designed for fully valid inputs. They treat all data as valid, including "invalid" values (often filled with placeholders,
e.g
.
, zeros). This corrupts feature extraction and alters hidden states, leading to failures. Partial Convolutions (PConvs)
[
28
]
solved this for CNNs: the authors modified standard Convolutional layers to be mask-aware, meaning they consider which pixels are invalid. PConvs consist of a mechanism for making outputs agnostic to the placeholder values of the invalid data.

While the principle of partial operations is well-established for CNNs, its adaptation to sequential, patch-based architectures like Mamba is non-trivial. To bridge this gap, we introduce Partial Vision Mamba (PVM), the first mask-aware, Mamba-based architecture designed for inference on arbitrarily shaped invalid inputs. PVM is a novel architectural component that, inspired by PConvs
[
28
]
, adapts the solution for Mamba state-space models to ensure that only valid data influence the model’s output. Along with PVM, we establish a mask-aware framework that outlines a set of properties dictating how architectures must be adapted to incorporate PVM, thus enabling capable solutions for inference on any form of invalid input data. Although Masked Image Modeling (MIM) methods, such as MAE
[
16
]
and MAP
[
51
]
, do exist, they are pre-training strategies rather than mask-aware architectures like PConvs
[
28
]
and their final model expects a complete image during inference and is not inherently mask-aware. Notably, adapting mask-aware logic to SSM mechanisms is non-trivial, and requires a specific token-processing design that we propose to prevent invalid data propagation, otherwise a single token corrupted by invalid data would contaminate the entire image.

We prove the utility of our novel PVM block and our mask-aware framework by designing adapted architectures for 3 different tasks: depth completion, image inpainting and image classification. This proves the generalizability of the approach in 3 problems of different nature (generative and discriminative), that also belong to different domains (the first works entirely on depth data while the rest are RGB tasks). In the Depth Completion case, as there are no unguided methods based on VM, we define 2 architectures leveraging VM and PVM equivalently. For the remaining tasks we design solutions by modifying standard architectures and compare the improvement. We expect PVM-based solutions to improve the original VM-based ones in the presence of invalid data.

Our contributions are summarized as:

•

We design a novel component, PVM, that allows processing inputs containing invalid data within the Vision Mamba architecture.

•

We formalize a mask-aware framework that allows the design of solutions using PVM for arbitrarily shaped invalid input data tasks.

•

We prove the generalizability of our approach by demonstrating its improvements in 3 distinct tasks: depth completion, image inpainting and image classification with invalid data.

2.2
Architectures for mask-aware inference

Inference on partially invalid data requires mask-aware architectures. For CNNs, early approaches (such as
[
26
]
) pioneered mask-aware operations to efficiently process sparse data like LiDAR. Building upon this, Partial Convolutions
[
28

solved this with a mask-based re-normalization step, conditioning the output only on valid pixels. Soft alternatives include Gated Convolutions
[

[

[
42

[
23
]
) and Diffusion models
[
36

also exist, but are outside the scope of our work, which focuses on Mamba-based models that are computationally much less complex. We explicitly differentiate from Masked Image Modeling (MIM)
[

41

51

as its final models are mask-unaware at inference (MIM is a pre-training strategy).

Critically, an equivalent, explicit architectural solution for the Mamba backbone does not exist. Current Mamba models lack a built-in mechanism to dynamically ignore arbitrarily shaped invalid inputs at inference, preventing the propagation of invalid tokens. Our work is the first to bridge this gap by proposing a mask-aware formulation for State Space Models.

3.1
Mask-aware processing

Our framework for handling invalid data defines the input as a tuple
(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{m})
, where
\mathbf{x}
is the data tensor and
\mathbf{m}
is its corresponding boolean validity mask. Let
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{C\times H\times W}
be the input feature map, which contains both valid pixel data and placeholder values (
e.g
.
, zeros) for invalid regions. The mask
\mathbf{m}\in\{0,1\}^{H\times W}
is a binary tensor where each spatial location
(i,j)
is defined as:

\mathbf{m}_{i,j}=\begin{cases}1&\text{if the data at }\mathbf{x}_{:,i,j}\text{ is valid}\\
0&\text{if the data at }\mathbf{x}_{:,i,j}\text{ is invalid}\end{cases}

(1)

Both tensors,
\mathbf{x}
and
\mathbf{m}
, are passed to the network. The main principle of our methodology is to use
\mathbf{m}
to ensure that all operations on
\mathbf{x}
are conditioned only on valid data.

A crucial aspect of our mask-aware framework is that the boolean validity mask
\mathbf{m}
is not static; it is dynamically updated as data flows through the network, ensuring that the validity of each new feature is correctly tracked. The update rules depend on the specific operation.

•

Element-wise Operations
: For operations like element-wise addition or multiplication between two tensors,
(\mathbf{x}_{1},\mathbf{m}_{1})
and
(\mathbf{x}_{2},\mathbf{m}_{2})
, the resulting feature’s validity is the logical intersection (AND) of the input masks. An output location
(i,j)
is valid only if the inputs at that location were both valid. This mechanism allows valid features to be retained even when paired with invalid values, ensuring that only placeholder-agnostic information propagates.

•

Feature concatenation
: When different feature maps are concatenated along the channel axis, their corresponding validity mask is also the logical intersection of the input masks as in Element-wise operations.

•

Feature reshaping
: When feature maps are reshaped (
e.g
.
flattened), the validity mask is reshaped accordingly.

•

Receptive Field Operations
: For standard, mask-unaware layers that aggregate information (like convolutions or fully-connected layers), an output feature is marked as invalid if it was computed using at least one invalid input. In contrast, when the partial, mask-aware, analogous version is used, an output feature is marked as valid if it was computed using at least one valid input. Some examples:

–

Convolutions
: A standard 2D Convolution output is invalid if any input in its receptive field is invalid (morphological erosion
[
15
]
). A PConv
[
28
]
output is valid if at least one input is valid (morphological dilation
[
15
]
).

–

Fully-Connected Layers
: A standard FC layer’s entire output is invalid if any input feature is invalid (
i.e
.
,
sum(m_{in})<count(m_{in})
). A mask-aware equivalent produces a valid output if at least one input feature is valid (
sum(m_{in})>0
).

–

Pooling Layers
: Pooling operations update the mask
\mathbf{m}
identically to their convolutional counterparts. A standard pool (
e.g
.
average pooling) requires all features in the receptive field to be valid, while a partial pool (
e.g
.
partial average pooling, where the average would be computed over input valid features) requires only one valid pixel.

–

Sequence Modeling
: In operations like SSMs, the sequence history acts as the receptive field. Assuming that different types of scanning mechanisms are applied, a standard SSM’s output is corrupted if any input token is invalid. A partial mechanism
[
23
]
requires at least one valid token to produce a valid output.

These properties are the design principles of our mask-aware framework. They can be used to create a mask-aware solution to any task, allowing the application of SSM to problems with partially invalid input data.

3.2
Partial Vision Mamba

Figure 1
:
The VM backbone (left) is unable to handle any invalidity (

.

The Partial Vision Mamba (PVM) is our novel architectural block designed to replace standard Mamba layers for processing inputs with any form of invalidity.

The VM backbone
[
57
]
is composed of a Patch embedding step (typically a linear layer), with the goal of describing each patch in the image by a one-dimensional feature vector (the SSM input tokens), followed by the SSM.
Fig.

1
illustrates the phenomenon of using VM when the input contains irregularly shaped invalid data, with the patch split resulting into three type of patches: (i) when all the data contained is valid (
e.g
.
, only foreground in the image, marked as green in
Fig.

1
), (ii) when only invalid data is contained (
e.g
.
, hole, marked as red), (iii) when we find valid data but also some invalid pixels (
e.g
.
, patches placed at the border between foreground and hole, marked as yellow). Two types of invalidity appear: inner-patch, where partially valid patches result in corrupted, invalid tokens (grey in
Fig.

1
), and inter-patch (where invalid tokens are processed normally by the SSM along with valid tokens, resulting in a completely invalid output sequence). Thus, the standard processing results in an invalid sequence.

The PVM block is designed to avoid both issues. The inner-patch invalidity is solved by modifying the Patch embedding module to make it mask-aware. This implies the use of a Partial Patch projection. We replace the Linear layer that performs the projection by a Partial Linear layer, which consists of a regular Linear layer preceded by a mean padding on the invalid positions. This trick allows using the same mechanism as in the PConvs approach
[
28
]
. Hence, the resulting tokens from partially valid input patches are now considered valid, and only valid and invalid tokens are present at this point. This operation results in two sequences: a sequence of feature tokens
\mathbf{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{L\times D}
, and a corresponding token-level validity mask
\mathbf{m}_{\text{token}}\in\{0,1\}^{L}
, where
L
is the number of tokens and
D
is their dimension. To avoid the inter-patch invalidity that would arise during the Mamba SSM processing, we explicitly replace the invalid token by some representation that the SSM should learn to identify (called masked tokens, purple in
Fig.

1
). We use a learned masked token (inspired by BERT
[
7
]
). We explicitly remark that this PVM design provides a structural guarantee of insensitivity to invalid placeholder values, which become mathematically irrelevant to the valid output stream. This was empirically verified during development.

3.3
PVM Block Functionality

The PVM blocks alone cannot guarantee the proper processing of invalid data. Some additional requirements are necessary: the input data must contain at least one unit of valid data, and the validity of the data received by the PVM block must be identified by the validity mask. The layers preceding the PVM block must handle these requirements. For instance, a Convolutional layer preceding a PVM block should be replaced by its equivalent PConv, which is able to handle the invalid data at the input and update the validity mask accordingly. Note that, although the PVM block cleans out all the input invalid data, the presence of skip connections around a given block can re-corrupt the resulting feature maps after element-wise operations or concatenation (
Section

3.1
). The PVM block’s role is determined by its position in the network: when used with a residual connection (
x^{\prime}=x+PVM(x)
, where
x
are the input and
x^{\prime}
the output features), the PVM block can update the full feature map, but only features in the input valid positions will be used after the merge (the rest must be discarded), thus producing a still partially valid feature mask and requiring the input validity mask
m_{in}
to be retained. When used without a residual connection, the PVM block ingests a sparse sequence and outputs a dense, fully-valid sequence. Often, a VM backbone module is formed by
N
residual VM blocks. When this happens with PVM blocks, the first
N-1
act as partially valid feature extractors and the last one as a valid feature completion module.

4
Experimental results

We validate the effectiveness and generalizability of our Partial Vision Mamba (PVM) across the three distinct tasks mentioned previously: depth completion, image inpainting, and image classification on masked inputs. For each task, we detail the specific datasets, evaluation metrics, and architectural baselines used to isolate and quantify the contribution of our mask-aware framework, and present results. We remark that the goal of these experiments is not to provide state-of-the-art methods, but to prove PVM impact over regular VM, allowing its usage for further architecture designs.

4.1
Depth Completion

We use the KITTI-3D Depth Completion benchmark
[
12
]
. This task is an ideal real-world testbed as the input LiDAR is inherently sparse, providing a natural, irregular validity mask (
\mathbf{m_{i,j}}=0
for missing pixels) without the need for synthetic occlusions. We use the official dataset split and evaluate in the developer’s test set, where the Ground Truth is available.

We evaluate PVM-DC using only the sparse depth input (RGB-unguided). We compare it to its mask-unaware equivalent, VM-DC. We configure both models equally, with 6 RSSB/RPSSB blocks in the DFE, with 2 VMM/PVMM blocks each. We use a pixel-wise Charbonnier Loss on the valid regions of the output, like
[
32
]
. We report the root mean square error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) on the valid pixels of the ground truth.

5
Discussion

Our results demonstrate that the PVM block, along with its careful integration into an architecture by following the design principles outlined in
Section

3.1
, is an effective and generalizable architectural mask-aware solution for handling invalid data, with no constraints on the mask shape. Across all tasks, we observe that our mask-aware "Partial" models significantly outperform their mask-unaware counterparts. This confirms that although a standard Mamba model possesses some ability to handle partially invalid inputs in a mask-unaware manner, it remains suboptimal. By architecturally enforcing mask-awareness, our PVM-based models become able to achieve higher performance and more robust solutions.

While we have proposed solutions for three distinct tasks, the design principles are broadly applicable. We believe the results of PVM are promising and can be intuitively applied to many other scenarios where modern, efficient SSMs must interface with sparse, occluded, or otherwise incomplete real-world data.

6
Conclusions

In this work, we introduced Partial Vision Mamba (PVM), the first Mamba-based architectural component designed to perform robust inference on inputs with arbitrarily shaped invalid data. We also formalized a set of validity properties that serve as a framework for designing mask-aware models using PVM. We validated our contributions across three diverse tasks, demonstrating that: (i) Our PVM-DC model benefits from the mask-aware design and surpasses (23%) its non-partial counterpart. (ii) For Image Inpainting, we demonstrate improvement by using a Mamba-based architecture and using our PVM novel component. (iii) On Image Classification with invalid data, the use of PVM yields a remarkable improvement.

For future work, given PVM’s demonstrated generalizability, we aim to explore more tasks within our mask-aware framework. We will specifically target other depth-related tasks, such as depth enhancement, by developing optimal PVM-based models, following the mask-aware principles studied in this work. We also plan to investigate core PVM improvements, including novel mask-aware scanning strategies and positional encodings.
