Climate change-related behaviors among hospital nurses in South Korea: a latent profile analysis
Authors: Dukhyun Back, Kihye Han, Hyang Baek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare systems contribute substantially to global carbon emissions. As the largest healthcare workforce, nurses play a critical role in climate change mitigation. Understanding their behavioral patterns is essential for developing targeted interventions to promote environmental engagement. This study aimed to identify latent profiles of climate change mitigation behaviors among Korean hospital nurses and examine their associations with climate extreme experiences, information exposure, efficacy beliefs, and work-related factors.MethodsThis cross-sectional study utilized survey data from 861 hospital nurses in South Korea, derived from the “Nurses’ accOuntability To Implement Climate and Environmental justice” project. Latent profile analysis was performed using eight behavioral items from the Korean version of the Climate, Health, and Nursing Tool. Differences among the identified profiles were examined using analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Games–Howell post hoc tests and chi-square tests.ResultsThree distinct profiles emerged: low engagement (8.5%), moderate engagement (45.3%), and high engagement (46.2%). The use of less gasoline (e.g., transportation) was a key distinguishing factor between the low and moderate engagement groups. Nurses in the high engagement group reported more frequent experiences with extreme climate events and higher levels of self-efficacy and collective efficacy. Paradoxically, greater exposure to climate-related information was associated with lower engagement. Furthermore, inadequate staffing was identified as a significant structural barrier negatively associated with pro-environmental behaviors.ConclusionThe findings underscore the importance of tailored strategies that account for nurses’ levels of engagement. Notably, the paradoxical association between greater climate-related information exposure and lower engagement suggests that information alone is insufficient to drive behavioral change. Accordingly, interventions should move beyond increasing information exposure to emphasize actionable guidance, including positively framed messages that highlight the environmental impact of professional nursing practices. To foster sustained engagement and efficacy, healthcare organizations should address structural barriers by ensuring adequate staffing and implementing supportive systems for environmentally sustainable practices.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Climate change represents one of the most pressing global challenges, with rising temperatures, extreme weather events, and environmental degradation posing severe threats to public health [1]. The urgent need to slow the progression of these impacts has made climate change mitigation behaviors—those that reduce greenhouse gas emissions and environmental footprints—a priority across all sectors. Although healthcare systems are essential for protecting human health, they paradoxically contribute substantially to global carbon emissions. Healthcare-related greenhouse gas emissions are categorized into direct emissions from healthcare facilities (Scope 1), indirect emissions from purchased energy (Scope 2), and other indirect emissions throughout the healthcare supply chain (Scope 3) [2]. A recent study reported that the carbon footprint of healthcare systems accounts for an average of 4.9% of national greenhouse gas emissions and reaches 9.8% in the United States [3]. Within the healthcare sector, hospitals represent one of the largest sources of emissions across countries, accounting for an average of 28.6% of the healthcare carbon footprint across 36 countries [3]. In this context, nurses, as the largest workforce in hospitals [4], play a significant role in climate change mitigation through both their personal behaviors as citizens and their professional roles within hospitals. Given the substantial environmental impact of hospitals, it is crucial to understand the climate change mitigation behaviors of hospital nurses.

Background

Nurses’ climate actions are often shaped by the perceived relevance of climate risks to their personal and local contexts [5]. Extreme climate experiences, such as direct exposure to heatwaves, heavy precipitation, droughts, and flooding, are becoming increasingly severe [6], thereby driving climate change mitigation behaviors. Additionally, exposure to climate-related information through media (e.g., TV and social media) can promote environmental behaviors [7]. However, excessive information exposure may induce feelings of helplessness or fatigue, potentially leading to information avoidance and behavioral inaction, a phenomenon that warrants attention [8].

Perceptions, particularly efficacy, can drive pro-environmental behaviors [9]. Self-efficacy—the belief that “I can make a difference”—is essential for everyday actions, such as conserving energy, reducing waste, and minimizing resource consumption. Collective efficacy—the belief that “we, as a group, can drive change”—also plays a crucial role in motivating pro-environmental actions in the workplace. Among professional groups such as nurses, collective efficacy reflects the belief that group efforts can improve patient and community health, thus motivating nurses to adopt sustainable practices in their personal and professional lives [10].

Work-related factors can either promote or restrict climate change mitigation behaviors. Among these factors, structural constraints, such as inadequate resources, heavy workloads, and time pressures, may limit the translation of experiences and efficacy into actual actions [5]. These constraints are particularly pronounced in healthcare settings, where patient care priorities may conflict with environmental considerations [11]. Consequently, even motivated nurses may struggle to engage in pro-environmental behaviors consistently.

Examining the subgroup characteristics based on hospital nurses’ climate change mitigation behaviors can provide important foundational data for developing effective and tailored interventions and policies. Importantly, climate change mitigation behavior is not confined to the workplace but also includes everyday practices, such as energy conservation, waste reduction, and sustainable consumption at home and in daily life. Given that nurses’ professional and personal roles are closely intertwined, understanding their climate change mitigation behaviors requires consideration of both hospital and everyday-life settings. However, existing research has primarily focused on the general population, with fewer studies specifically exploring behavioral patterns and contextual factors among hospital nurses [12, 13]. Although previous studies, including one based on the NOTICE project dataset [14], have primarily adopted variable-centered approaches, these methods may overlook heterogeneity in climate change mitigation behaviors among nurses. In contrast, latent profile analysis (LPA), a person-centered approach, can identify distinct subgroups of nurses based on shared climate change mitigation behavior patterns [13]. Accordingly, this study applied LPA to examine hospital nurses’ climate change mitigation behaviors.

Aims

This study aimed to identify latent profiles of climate change mitigation behaviors among Korean hospital nurses and to examine how these profiles are associated with climate extreme experiences, climate change-related information exposure, climate change-related efficacy, and work-related factors.

Methods

Design

This study employed a cross-sectional design utilizing survey data from the Nurses’ accOuntability To Implement Climate and Environmental justice (NOTICE) project [14]. This project was initiated to explore Korean nurses’ perceptions of and engagement in climate change and environmental justice, along with related influencing factors.

Data sources and sampling

Detailed information on the sampling methods and data collection procedures of the NOTICE project can be found in a previous study [14]. Data were collected via an online survey conducted between March and April 2024. Participants were recruited through notices and links distributed via social media platforms and online nursing communities. The inclusion criterion was that participants must be registered nurses currently employed in nursing. From the total sample (n = 1,022), 120 non-hospital nurses and 41 hospital nurses with missing data on key climate change mitigation behavior variables were excluded. The final analytic sample comprised 861 hospital nurses (84.2%), exceeding the sample size of approximately 500 generally recommended for LPA [15].

Measures

Climate change mitigation behaviors

Climate change mitigation behaviors were evaluated across two domains: behaviors at home (4 items) and behaviors at work (4 items). These behaviors were measured using the Korean version of the Climate, Health, and Nursing Tool (CHANT), which was translated, culturally adapted, and psychometrically validated in 2022 [16, 17]. Permission to use both the original CHANT instrument and its Korean version was obtained by email from the original developer (January 15, 2024) and the researcher who translated the instrument into Korean (January 26, 2024), respectively. The items consist of eight climate change mitigation behaviors, implemented across domestic and occupational settings: (1) conserve energy at home (e.g., use energy-efficient appliances, maintain proper temperature, turn off lights and appliances when not in use), (2) use less gasoline at home (e.g., use fuel-efficient vehicles, reduce unnecessary travel, bike/walk), (3) reduce waste at home (e.g., buy less, reuse more, recycle more), (4) choose foods that require fewer resources at home (e.g., eat local/seasonal foods, reduce animal products, reduce packaging), (5) conserve energy at work (e.g., turn off lights and devices when not in use), (6) use less gasoline for commuting (e.g., bike, walk, carpool, use public transportation), (7) reduce waste at work (e.g., reduce plastic, paper, linen, clinical supplies), and (8) ask leaders to reduce greenhouse gases at work (e.g., changes in policies, products, processes). Each item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always). In the original instrument, Cronbach’s α was 0.79 for the home domain and 0.69 for the work domain; in this study, Cronbach’s α was 0.67 for the home domain and 0.62 for the work domain [17]. The domains align with established frameworks for climate change mitigation behaviors [18, 19], supporting the validity of the items [20].

Climate extreme experiences

The level of climate extreme experiences was assessed using the following question: “How often have you experienced the following climate-related weather events in your area?” [17]. The listed events included: (1) extreme heat, (2) heavy precipitation, (3) droughts, (4) flooding, (5) hurricanes and storm surges, and (6) wildfires. Possible answers were: 1 = “never,” 2 = “rarely,” 3 = “sometimes,” 4 = “frequently,” and 5 = “very frequently.” The scores were averaged, with higher scores indicating more frequent climate extreme experiences (Cronbach’s α = .75).

Climate change-related information exposure

The level of exposure to climate change-related information was assessed using the following question: “How often do you hear or read about climate change from the following sources?” [14, 21]. The sources included: (1) TV news and other TV programs, (2) printed and online newspapers, (3) radio news and other radio programs, (4) Instagram, (5) Facebook, (6) Twitter, (7) YouTube, (8) books and magazines, (9) scientific articles/journals/blogs, and (10) family, friends, and colleagues. The frequency of exposure was measured on a 9-point scale ranging from 0 (never) to 8 (more than 10 times per day). The responses were averaged, with higher scores indicating more frequent exposure to climate change-related information (Cronbach’s α = 0.93).

Climate change-related efficacy

We utilized items from the Media Exposure, Climate Anxiety and Mental Health (MECAMH) project to evaluate climate change-related efficacy [21]. The translation and cultural adaptation procedures for the Korean version have been reported elsewhere [14]. Climate change-related efficacy was evaluated in two domains: self-efficacy (5 items, Cronbach’s α = 0.78) and collective efficacy (5 items, Cronbach’s α = 0.82) [14, 21]. Participants responded using a 5-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree). Domain scores were calculated by averaging the item responses, with higher scores indicating higher levels of perceived efficacy.

Participant characteristics

Personal and work-related characteristics were assessed, including age, gender, marital status, having children, educational attainment, position, hospital size, and work schedule (regular daytime vs. rotating shifts or fixed night shifts). Additionally, participants were asked to rate perceived staffing adequacy using a single item: “In the past month, did you feel that your department was adequately staffed?” The item was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (never) to 5 (always).

Data analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) to summarize the participants’ sociodemographic and study-related characteristics. Means and standard deviations were calculated for continuous variables, whereas frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical variables.

To identify subgroups of climate change mitigation behaviors among hospital nurses in Korea, an exploratory LPA was conducted using Mplus version 8.0 [22, 23]. LPA is a statistical technique that identifies unobserved subgroups within a population based on patterns in observed variables, facilitating the classification of individuals into distinct profiles. In this study, 8 items (4 items for behaviors at home and 4 items for behaviors at work) were used as profile indicators. Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices, including the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), and adjusted Bayesian Information Criterion (aBIC), with lower values indicating a better fit [24]. To compare models with different numbers of classes, the Lo-Mendell-Rubin adjusted likelihood ratio test (LMR-LRT) and the bootstrapped likelihood ratio test (BLRT) were used [22, 25]. A significant LMR-LRT or BLRT result (p < .05) suggests that the model with k classes fits better than the model with k–1 classes [26]. Entropy was also assessed to evaluate classification accuracy, with values closer to 1 indicating better classification accuracy and values ≥ 0.80 considered acceptable [27]. In addition, no absolute minimum class size criterion exists for LPA, and class size should instead be evaluated in conjunction with model fit indices and the substantive interpretability of the identified profiles [27].

Following profile identification, differences across the latent profiles in climate extreme experiences, climate change-related information exposure, climate change-related efficacy, and work-related factors were assessed using analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Games-Howell post-hoc tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. A p-value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Ethical considerations

The NOTICE project was initiated with the approval of the study protocol from the Institutional Review Board of Chung-Ang University (IRB No. 1041078-20240129-HR-015, approved on March 27, 2024). Participation in the project was voluntary, and participants could withdraw at any time. Personal data were kept confidential. Because the present study falls within the original scope of this approved protocol, no separate IRB approval was required.

Model selection

Several model fit indices were compared for models with different numbers of profiles (Table 2). Although AIC, BIC, and aBIC values were slightly higher in the 3-profile model compared to the 4-profile model, the overall model fit was similar. The 4-profile model resulted in two small groups (7.2% and 8.1%). These small profiles did not provide substantially distinct patterns, and the improvement in model fit was marginal relative to the increased model complexity. Therefore, the three-profile model was selected as the most appropriate representation of heterogeneity in climate change mitigation behaviors among hospital nurses. Additionally, the entropy value (0.809) exceeded the generally accepted threshold of 0.80, indicating acceptable classification quality.

Individual and work-related characteristics across latent profiles

Nurses in the high engagement group reported more frequent climate extreme experiences than those in the low and moderate engagement groups (F = 10.94, p < .001) (Table 3). In contrast, exposure to climate change-related information decreased progressively across profiles, from the low to the moderate to the high engagement group (F = 32.06, p < .001). Efficacy measures also showed statistically significant differences across the profiles. Both self-efficacy (F = 91.40, p < .001) and collective efficacy (F = 110.73, p < .001) were highest among nurses in the high engagement group, followed by those in the moderate and low engagement groups, in a step-wise pattern. Regarding work-related factors, nurses in the low engagement group perceived staffing adequacy as significantly lower than that in the moderate and high engagement groups (F = 5.84, p = .003). Furthermore, nurses in the high engagement group were more likely to work in smaller hospitals (χ² = 22.56, p < .001). Although differences in work schedules were not statistically significant (χ² = 3.53, p = .171), the proportion of nurses working rotating shifts or fixed night shifts was higher in the low engagement group (84.93%) than in the other groups (74.94–75.32%).

Discussion

This study identified three distinct latent profiles of climate change mitigation behaviors among hospital nurses—low, moderate, and high engagement—which differed according to climate extreme experience, information exposure, efficacy, and work-related factors. The findings suggest that hospital nurses’ engagement in climate change mitigation behaviors is shaped by both individual and organizational factors, underscoring the importance of developing strategies to enhance hospital nurses’ engagement in pro-environmental actions.

We identified three distinct subgroups of hospital nurses in Korea based on their climate change mitigation behaviors (low, moderate, and high engagement). Similarly, a previous study identified low, moderate, and high engagement groups in the general population using similar behavioral measures [12], reporting 17.5% in the high engagement group and 22.6% in the low engagement group. In contrast, our study found a larger proportion of high engagement (46.2%) and a smaller proportion of low engagement (8.5%). This higher level of engagement among hospital nurses may be attributed to their greater health consciousness, higher educational attainment, and stronger altruistic motivations [28]. The three groups in our study were primarily differentiated by their level of engagement in climate change mitigation behaviors. Individuals who engage in one type of climate-friendly behavior tend to engage in other behaviors as well. Notably, transportation-related behaviors, such as reducing gasoline use at home and for commuting, were key features that differentiated the low and moderate engagement groups. Unlike other environmental behaviors, which can often be implemented through minor adjustments or momentary decisions, transportation-related behaviors demand fundamental changes to daily routines, time management, and convenience [29]. Therefore, gasoline reduction behaviors may represent one factor that helps distinguish between engagement levels. In contrast, energy conservation behaviors did not clearly differentiate between engagement groups. Notably, even within the low engagement group, energy conservation behaviors at home and at work were performed at a relatively higher level (3.25 and 3.11, respectively) compared with other mitigation behaviors. This pattern may reflect the nature of energy conservation as a low-cost and low-barrier behavior (e.g., turning off unused lights), requiring minimal effort or disruption to daily routines. Such behaviors can therefore be performed even by individuals with low engagement in climate change mitigation, as they demand little motivation or commitment compared to higher-cost behaviors such as reducing gasoline use [30].

Our study revealed a complex and somewhat paradoxical relationship between climate change mitigation behaviors and both experiences of extreme weather events and exposure to climate change-related information. Nurses who had more frequent climate extreme experiences were more likely to engage in mitigation behaviors; however, greater exposure to climate change-related information via media was associated with lower engagement. This paradox may arise because passive consumption of information through media does not always translate into tangible behavioral changes [31]. Moreover, media coverage of climate change often emphasizes the severity of the crisis without providing clear, actionable steps for individuals, potentially leading to helplessness and psychological reactance [32]. These findings underscore the need for approaches that go beyond simply increasing information exposure. Instead, interventions should focus on providing actionable, practical guidance. In addition, messages should be framed around hope and positive change, rather than fear or guilt, to foster long-term behavioral engagement [33].

Consistent with previous studies [7, 9], our results demonstrated that hospital nurses with greater confidence in addressing environmental issues—both individually and collectively—were more likely to engage in climate change mitigation behaviors. Therefore, environmental education and awareness campaigns should prioritize building confidence in individuals’ capacity to effect change, rather than merely highlighting the severity of the climate crisis. For example, collective efficacy can be strengthened through campaigns that emphasize a professional identity, such as “Green Nurses,” which would encourage collective responsibility for climate action. Enhanced collective efficacy may in turn improve individual self-efficacy [34]. Additionally, self-efficacy can be improved through programs that equip nurses with practical skills and knowledge for implementing climate-friendly practices.

Our findings also revealed that inadequate staffing hindered pro-environmental engagement, with a higher proportion of nurses in the high engagement group working in smaller hospitals. Although there is a scarcity of literature directly linking nurse work characteristics to environmental behavior, the statistically significant association suggests that organizational factors, such as differences in workload and patient acuity, may influence nurses’ ability to engage in environmental practices. Smaller hospitals may have less severe workloads [35], thereby providing more opportunities for pro-environmental engagement. Furthermore, although not statistically significant, nurses in the low engagement group more frequently worked rotating shifts or fixed night shifts. This finding suggests that organizational stressors, including staff shortages and high workload, may contribute to nurse fatigue and stress, reducing the time and energy available for non-clinical priorities, such as environmental stewardship. This is particularly true in healthcare settings where patient safety and quality of care are prioritized above other concerns [11]. To promote hospital nurses’ engagement in climate change mitigation, it is essential to address these structural barriers. Organizational initiatives should consider evaluating staffing adequacy for both patient care and environmental practices. Moreover, the installation of automated eco-friendly systems, such as pedal or sensor faucets, energy-efficient lighting, and waste segregation mechanisms, can reduce nurses’ workload while promoting sustainability. Further support may also be needed for nurses who worked rotating shifts or fixed night shifts to ensure that they have sufficient time to engage in eco-friendly activities.

Limitations

The limitations of this study should be considered when interpreting the results. The use of self-reported measures for assessing climate change mitigation behaviors may introduce social desirability bias and thus may not accurately reflect actual behaviors. Convenience sampling through social media channels and online nursing communities could have introduced selection bias, as participants with greater access to technology or stronger interest in environmental issues may have been overrepresented, thus limiting the diversity of the sample. The cross-sectional design precludes any causal inferences regarding the relationships among the study variables. Moreover, the sample was limited to hospital nurses in Korea, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other healthcare settings or countries with different cultural contexts.

Conclusions

This study identified three subgroups of hospital nurses in Korea based on their climate change mitigation behaviors, offering valuable insights for the development of tailored strategies to enhance pro-environmental behaviors. Interventions should consider both individual and organizational factors to effectively promote engagement in climate change mitigation behaviors. Specifically, targeted commuting-focused interventions, such as public transportation subsidies and improved bicycle facilities, may help nurses in the low engagement group transition to moderate engagement. Notably, the paradoxical association between greater climate-related information exposure and lower engagement suggests that information alone is insufficient to drive behavioral change. Accordingly, interventions should move beyond increasing information exposure to emphasize actionable guidance, including positively framed messages that highlight the environmental impact of professional nursing practices. Given this, experiential and job-embedded programs—such as simulation training linking extreme weather events to patient safety—may be more effective than conventional information campaigns. To foster sustained engagement, healthcare organizations should address structural barriers by ensuring adequate staffing and implementing supportive systems for environmentally sustainable practices, including integration of environmental activities into workload planning. Future research should utilize objective assessments or multi-source data to minimize self-reported bias. Expanding the sample to include nurses from diverse healthcare settings and countries would also enhance the generalizability of the findings.
