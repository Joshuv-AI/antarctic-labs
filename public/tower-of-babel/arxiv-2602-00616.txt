Inference-Only Prompt Projection for Safe Text-to-Image Generation with TV Guarantees
Minhyuk Lee, Hyekyung Yoon, Myungjoo Kang

Abstract
Text-to-Image (T2I) diffusion models enable high-quality open-ended synthesis, but their real-world deployment demands safeguards that suppress unsafe generations without degrading benign prompt–image alignment. We formalize this tension through a total variation (TV) lens: once the reference conditional distribution is fixed, any nontrivial reduction in unsafe generations necessarily incurs TV deviation from the reference, yielding a principled Safety-Prompt Alignment Trade-off (SPAT). Guided by this view, we propose an inference-only prompt projection framework that selectively intervenes on high-risk prompts via a surrogate objective with verification, mapping them into a tolerance-controlled safe set while leaving benign prompts effectively unchanged, without retraining or fine-tuning the generator. Across four datasets and three diffusion backbones, our approach achieves 16.7–60.0% relative reductions in inappropriate percentage (IP) versus strong model-level alignment baselines, while preserving benign prompt–image alignment on COCO near the unaligned reference. Introduction

Recent Text-to-Image (T2I) diffusion models have improved photorealism and prompt-following, enabling broad content-creation use
(
31
;
27
)
. However, open ended prompting also raises their deployment risks: users can elicit harmful or policy violating imagery, as documented by risk taxonomies and system level assessments of deployed models
(
3
;
25
)
. Industry-standard keyword safeguards (e.g., blocklists) are widely deployed but can be effortlessly circumvented under open-ended prompting, motivating safeguards that generalize beyond fixed lexicons. We therefore seek
safety alignment
: suppressing
unsafe generations
while preserving
benign prompt–image alignment
.

Most existing approaches pursue safety alignment via model intervention, including latent/model level editing, concept removal, and alignment-oriented training
(
32
;
10
;
11
;
22
;
21
)
. While these methods can suppress unsafe generations, they often perturb the underlying generative distribution. A key difficulty is that model-level changes act globally, so updates that suppress unsafe generations can also reshape behaviors needed for benign prompt–image alignment. As a result, stronger intervention can degrade benign prompt–image alignment even when no safety action is necessary. AlignGuard
(
21
)
reports this effect, where more aggressive alignment coincides with degraded benign prompt–image alignment on COCO
(
20
)
. This recurring degradation suggests a distributional constraint behind the safety–prompt alignment tension, motivating our TV analysis.

We analyze this phenomenon from a distributional perspective. Fix a trained
reference
model with conditional image law
G^{*}(\cdot\mid c)
for each prompt
c
. In a total variation (TV) framework, any nontrivial reduction in the likelihood of unsafe generations for some prompts necessarily induces TV deviation from
G^{*}(\cdot\mid c)
, yielding a TV Safety–Prompt Alignment Trade-off (SPAT) that lower-bounds the deviation required for safety gains. This distributional shift acts on the same reference conditionals that govern benign prompt–image alignment, explaining why model level intervention can coincide with degraded benign behavior.

Building on this insight, we avoid modifying the T2I generator and instead operate in prompt space. We propose an inference-only
prompt projection
framework that selectively intervenes on prompts likely to trigger unsafe generations, mapping them into a safety bounded set under a user specified safety tolerance, while leaving benign prompts essentially unchanged in practice. Conceptually, prompt projection approximates a constrained mapping into a safety bounded set. Since exact projection is intractable, we approximate it with a two-stage inference-time framework: an LLM-based surrogate proposes and ranks candidate prompts, and a safeguard VLM
(
19
)
verifies image-level safety for the final selection. By localizing intervention to the prompt space and keeping the base generator fixed, the framework targets unsafe generations without the direct model perturbations that often degrade benign prompt–image alignment.

Empirically, across four datasets and three diffusion backbones, prompt projection consistently suppresses unsafe generations while preserving benign prompt–image alignment close to the unaligned reference. By contrast, stronger intervention-based baselines typically yield safety improvements while incurring a nontrivial loss in benign prompt–image alignment.

Our contributions are as follows:

•

We formalize prompt-conditioned safety alignment in TV and derive a
SPAT bound
that lower-bounds the TV deviation from a fixed reference needed to suppress unsafe generations.

•

We propose
inference-only prompt projection
that maps high-risk prompts into a safety-bounded set under a user-specified tolerance threshold
\tau
, while leaving already-safe prompts effectively unchanged in practice.

•

We operationalize projection via a
two-stage inference-time cascade
: an LLM-based surrogate proposes and ranks candidates, and a safeguard VLM performs image-level verification.

•

Across four datasets and three diffusion backbones, we obtain
16.7–60.0%
relative reductions in inappropriate percentage (IP) versus strong model-level alignment baselines, while preserving benign prompt–image alignment close to the unaligned reference.

Related Works

Input/Prompt Filtering and Rewriting.

Deployed systems often moderate text inputs via keyword blocklists
(
1
;
24
)
or learned prompt detectors (e.g., LLM guards)
(
15
)
. Recent work also intervenes on prompts or textual embeddings without changing the diffusion backbone. LatentGuard
(
22
)
learns an auxiliary latent space above the text encoder for contrastive harmfulness detection. PromptGuard
(
43
)
optimizes a universal safety soft prompt that acts as an implicit system prompt in embedding space. POSI
(
38
)
and VALOR
(
44
)
revise unsafe prompts, and VALOR further couples revision with a rewrite–generate–verify loop. In contrast, GuardT2I
(
40
)
does not rewrite prompts. It uses a conditional LLM to translate text-encoder latents back into plain text to support moderation under adversarial prompt obfuscation. However, prior prompt-side methods typically lack an explicit
projection
notion, namely a constrained, near-identity intervention that leaves already-safe prompts effectively unchanged. They also rarely describe the prompt-conditioned distributional shift induced by the intervention.

Inference-time Guidance and Latent Control.

Another line intervenes at inference time
inside
the generation process to suppress unsafe content. SLD
(
32
)
and UCE
(
11
)
modulate cross-attention or latent representations to down-weight harmful concepts. SAFREE
(
42
)
identifies toxic trigger tokens and dynamically adapts diffusion timesteps to suppress their influence during generation. PNO
(
26
)
performs gradient-based updates on text embeddings and diffusion noise trajectories. These methods can be effective, but they often rely on fixed taxonomies or concept lists and can broadly perturb the denoising process, which may cause meaning drift on benign or borderline prompts. Because they operate on denoising trajectories rather than prompt space, it is also harder to state or diagnose when the intervention is
minimal
relative to the original prompt-conditioned behavior.

Model Editing and Alignment.

Model-level approaches modify the generator to enforce safety. ESD-u
(
10
)
edits pretrained weights to remove prohibited concepts, and AlignGuard
(
21
)
inserts category-specific safety experts into the backbone. While structural, these approaches often require substantial per-category optimization and remain tied to fixed taxonomies. They also risk over-alignment, where edits can hurt faithful rendering of benign intent even without any modification to the user prompt. In contrast, our method is
inference-only
and operates in prompt space via a
projection
view under a user-specified tolerance threshold
\tau
. We provide a prompt-conditioned TV analysis that formalizes the unavoidable distributional shift needed to suppress unsafe mass under a fixed reference, and we connect this to a
\tau
-controlled projected-reference target via projection kernels.

3
TV-Based Safety–Alignment Theory for Prompt-Conditioned Generation

3.1
Preliminaries and notation

Prompts
c\in\mathcal{C}
are drawn from
\mu
and images lie in
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
. A generator induces conditionals
G(\cdot\mid c)\in\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
. We fix a reference model
G^{*}
. We measure deviation from the reference in TV:

\mathrm{TV}(\nu,\nu^{\prime})\coloneqq\sup_{A\in\mathcal{F}}|\nu(A)-\nu^{\prime}(A)|.

(1)

Let
U:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
be a continuous unsafety score function and define the prompt-wise and population unsafety as

\displaystyle u(G\!\mid\!c)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{x\sim G(\cdot\mid c)}[U(x)],

(2)

\displaystyle\mathcal{U}(G)

\displaystyle:=\mathbb{E}_{c\sim\mu}[u(G\!\mid\!c)].

(3)

The reference unsafety
\mathcal{U}(G^{*})
is denoted by
\mathcal{U}^{*}
.

3.2
Safety–Prompt Alignment Trade-off in TV (SPAT)

We quantify average prompt-wise deviation from the reference by

\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{TV}}(G):=\mathbb{E}_{c\sim\mu}\!\left[\mathrm{TV}\big(G(\cdot\mid c),\,G^{*}(\cdot\mid c)\big)\right].

(4)

Intuitively,
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{TV}}(G)
aggregates, over prompts, the TV deviation between the deployed conditionals and the reference conditionals. This alignment functional will enter the safety bound through the fact that TV controls deviations of bounded unsafety functionals.

Theorem 3.1

(Safety–prompt alignment trade-off (SPAT))
.

For any conditional generator
G
and any prompt
c\in\mathcal{C}
,

u(G\!\mid\!c)\ \geq\ u(G^{*}\!\mid\!c)\;-\;\mathrm{TV}\!\big(G(\cdot\mid c),\,G^{*}(\cdot\mid c)\big).

(5)

Consequently,

\mathcal{U}(G)+\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{TV}}(G)\ \geq\ \mathcal{U}^{*},

(6)

equivalently,
\mathcal{U}(G)\geq\mathcal{U}^{*}-\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{TV}}(G)
.

Proof.

See Appendix.
A.3
.

∎

Equation (
6
) follows by averaging (
5
) over
c\sim\mu
. Theorem
3.1
implies a floor: if
\mathcal{A}_{\mathrm{TV}}(G)
is small, then
\mathcal{U}(G)
cannot fall far below
\mathcal{U}^{*}
. Safety gains beyond this level therefore require larger departures from the reference conditionals, potentially reducing fidelity for matched prompts or prompt faithfulness, which motivates selective prompt level interventions rather than a global shift away from
G^{*}
.

3.3
Existence of
\tau
-safe minimal edit projection

Fix a tolerance
\tau\in[0,1]
and define the
\tau
-safe prompt set under the reference model,

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}:=\{c\in\mathcal{C}:u(G^{*}\mid c)\leq\tau\}.

(7)

Our goal is to map an original prompt
c
to a nearby
\tau
-safe prompt
c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}
so that querying the fixed reference model with
c^{\prime}
yields controlled prompt-wise unsafety. To formalize
minimal
edits, we endow
\mathcal{C}
with an embedding-induced angular distance, motivated by the widespread use of dot product/cosine similarity in retrieval and representation learning
(
18
;
29
;
13
;
16
)
. Let
\phi:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be a sentence embedding with
\phi(c)\neq 0
, and define

d(c,c^{\prime}):=\arccos\!\Big(\tfrac{\langle\phi(c),\phi(c^{\prime})\rangle}{\|\phi(c)\|_{2}\|\phi(c^{\prime})\|_{2}}\Big)\in[0,\pi].

(8)

Given
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}
, define the projection set

\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(c):=\argmin_{c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}}d(c,c^{\prime}).

(9)

If
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(c)
is nonempty, any selection
\pi_{\tau}(c)\in\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(c)
yields a minimal-edit
\tau
-safe prompt under
d
. Equivalently, (
9
) is a constrained edit problem that seeks the closest
\tau
-safe prompt (with respect to
d
). In general,
d
is a pseudometric and minimizers need not be unique (formal details are given in Appendix
A.4
). Our method provides a practical approximation of this projection.

Assumption 3.2

(Well-posedness of
\tau
-safe projection)
.

Fix
\tau\in[0,1]
and set
\Gamma(c)\!:=\!G^{*}(\cdot\!\mid\!c)
. At a high level, assume:

(i)

\Gamma
is weakly continuous in
c
under
d
, meaning that small prompt edits do not cause abrupt changes in the reference conditional distribution.

(ii)

\{\phi(c)/\|\phi(c)\|_{2}:\,c\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}\}
is nonempty and closed in
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, so minimizing sequences admit limit points within the feasible set.

Sufficient technical conditions are given in Appendix.
A.2
.

Theorem 3.3

(Existence of a measurable
\tau
-safe projection)
.

Under Assumption
3.2
,
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(c)\neq\emptyset
for
\mu
-a.e.
c
.
Moreover, there exists a
set-valued measurable correspondence

\pi_{\tau}\!:\!\mathcal{C}\rightrightarrows\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}

with nonempty values such that for
\mu
-a.e.
c
,

\displaystyle\emptyset\!\neq\!\pi_{\tau}(c)\!\subseteq\!\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(c),\;\;d(c_{1},c_{2})\!=\!0\;\forall\,c_{1},c_{2}\!\in\!\pi_{\tau}(c).

(10)

Proof.

See Appendix.
A.4
.

∎

Theorem
3.3
is primarily an enabling result: it guarantees (a.e.) existence and measurability, but does not assert uniqueness. Establishing uniqueness would typically require substantially stronger structural assumptions on the prompt space and the
\tau
-safe set, which we do not impose. Accordingly, it is more natural to treat the projection as the set-valued correspondence
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(c)
rather than as a single canonical map. In the next subsection, we formalize this non-uniqueness via a Markov kernel projection, i.e., a conditional distribution over
\tau
-safe prompts. Operationally, the kernel view provides a measurable way to allocate mass over multiple equally close safe prompts (rather than committing to an arbitrary tie-breaking rule), which is convenient for downstream distributional analysis.

3.4
Projection kernels and a
\tau
-controlled target

To model set-valued and stochastic prompt projections, we represent projection by a Markov kernel
\Pi_{\tau}(c,\cdot)
over
\tau
-safe prompts, which in turn induces the projected reference target
G^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\tau,\Pi_{\tau}}
. Formally,
\Pi_{\tau}:\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau})\to[0,1]
. We call
\Pi_{\tau}
a
\tau
-nearest projection kernel
if

\displaystyle\Pi_{\tau}(c,\cdot)=\delta_{c}(\cdot)\ \ \forall c\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau},

(11)

\displaystyle\Pi_{\tau}(c,\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(c))=1\ \text{for }\mu\text{-a.e. }c\in\mathcal{C}.

(12)

for all
c\in\mathcal{C}
and all
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau})
. Eq. (
11
) enforces
no intervention
on already
\tau
-safe prompts, while Eq. (
12
) concentrates all mass on the nearest
\tau
-safe set
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(c)
. Consequently, reapplying
\Pi_{\tau}
has no effect (idempotence). For brevity, we refer to a
\tau
-nearest projection kernel simply as a
projection kernel
.

Under standard regularity conditions (Assumption
3.2
) and Theorem
3.3
, such
\Pi_{\tau}
can be constructed. Details are given in Appendix
A.5
.
Given any conditional generator
G
, define the kernel-projected conditionals

\displaystyle\widetilde{G}_{\tau,\Pi_{\tau}}(\cdot\mid c):=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}}G(\cdot\mid c^{\prime})\,\Pi_{\tau}(c,dc^{\prime}),

(13)

\displaystyle G^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\tau,\Pi_{\tau}}(\cdot\mid c):=\int_{\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}}G^{*}(\cdot\mid c^{\prime})\,\Pi_{\tau}(c,dc^{\prime}).

(14)

Here
G^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\tau,\Pi_{\tau}}
is the
projected reference
: it describes the reference conditional laws under the rewrite distribution rather than the original prompt. We measure alignment to this target by the kernelized TV functional

\mathcal{A}^{\Pi_{\tau}}_{\mathrm{TV}}(G)\!:=\!\mathbb{E}_{c\sim\mu}\!\!\left[\mathrm{TV}\big(\widetilde{G}_{\tau,\Pi_{\tau}}(\cdot\mid c),G^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\tau,\Pi_{\tau}}(\cdot\mid c)\big)\right].

(15)

The next result shows that SPAT carries over to this projected-conditioning setting and yields a tolerance-controlled reference floor.

Theorem 3.4

(Kernelized SPAT and a
\tau
-controlled floor)
.

Fix
\tau\in[0,1]
and let
\Pi_{\tau}
be any projection kernel satisfying (
11
) and (
12
). Then for any conditional generator
G
,

\mathcal{U}\!\big(\widetilde{G}_{\tau,\Pi_{\tau}}\big)+\mathcal{A}^{\Pi_{\tau}}_{\mathrm{TV}}(G)\ \geq\ \mathcal{U}\!\big(G^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\tau,\Pi_{\tau}}\big),

(16)

and moreover

\mathcal{U}\!\big(G^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\tau,\Pi_{\tau}}\big)\ \leq\ \tau.

(17)

Proof.

See Appendix.
A.6
.
∎

Theorem
3.4
captures the key rationale for projecting at the level of the
reference
conditionals. By restricting the projection kernel to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}
, the projected reference
G^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\tau,\Pi_{\tau}}
becomes a
\tau
-controlled target, i.e.,
\mathcal{U}(G^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\tau,\Pi_{\tau}})\leq\tau
by construction. Moreover, SPAT persists under this projected conditioning: if a deployed generator remains aligned with
G^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\tau,\Pi_{\tau}}
in the kernelized TV sense, then its population unsafety cannot deviate substantially from the target level. Practically, this suggests a selective intervention: prompts that already satisfy the tolerance can, in principle, be passed through with little or no modification, while projection is applied mainly when the prompt-wise unsafety exceeds
\tau
. This selectivity aims to reduce unnecessary distributional shifts and may help preserve fidelity on benign prompts.

The theory defines
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}
using the ideal unsafety
u(G^{*}\mid c)
, but in practice this quantity is not directly observable, so the support condition (
11
) can only be enforced via operational checks. We therefore treat
\Pi_{\tau}
as an implicit, procedure-induced kernel over
\tau
-safe prompts and quantify how well the induced support constraint is realized via acceptance into an operational
\tau
-safe set and semantic-drift diagnostics (§.
5.5
). With this viewpoint, it suffices to realize the projected reference
G^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\tau,\Pi_{\tau}}
as a practical
\tau
-controlled target. The next section describes our instantiation of the projection mechanism and the resulting empirical trade-offs.

Method

4.1
Inference-only projected reference sampling

We propose an
inference-only
prompt projection pipeline that realizes sampling from the projected reference target

G^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\tau,\Pi_{\tau}}
in (
14
)
without training
.
To avoid reliance on fixed taxonomies or keyword blocklists, we leverage a pretrained LLM as a general-purpose safety reasoner and instantiate the projection kernel
\Pi_{\tau}(c,\cdot)
at test time: given a prompt
c
, the LLM produces a finite set of minimal edit rewrites together with an induced sampling distribution, which we treat as
\Pi_{\tau}(c,\cdot)

(
5
;
37
;
7
;
12
)
.
We then sample from
G^{\mathrm{ref}}_{\tau,\Pi_{\tau}}(\cdot\mid c)
via rewrite
\to

c^{\prime}\sim\Pi_{\tau}(c,\cdot)
and generate

\hat{x}\sim G^{*}(\cdot\mid c^{\prime})
.
This implements the selective-intervention principle from §
3.4
, leaving already
\tau
-safe prompts unchanged and projecting only when needed.

Since prompt-wise risk under
G^{*}
is costly to evaluate, we enforce
\tau
-control with a two-stage cascade
(
6
)
.
Stage-1 ranks candidate rewrites using a prompt-only score
\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{LLM}}(c^{\prime})\in[0,1]
from a binary query (A=
Safe
, B=

) and the next token logit gap (see §
4.3
).
It is used solely for routing/search, not to estimate

. Stage-2 verifies the output with a safeguard VLM
(

)
by scoring
\hat{x}
(with
\hat{x}\sim G^{*}(\cdot\mid c^{\prime})
) under the same A/B protocol and accepting if
\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{VLM}}(\hat{x})\leq\tau
, else resampling up to
R

Because the procedure-induced kernel
\Pi_{\tau}
only approximately meets the ideal support restriction to
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}
, we justify this cascade by reporting Stage-1/Stage-2 score agreement (Pearson/Spearman) and the speedup over VLM-only checks across Stable Diffusion backbones (Appendix

4.2
Prompt projection via soft-constrained local search

Let
d(\cdot,\cdot)
be the embedding-induced prompt metric (
8
). Direct metric projection
onto
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}
is infeasible because membership depends on prompt-conditional risk, and per-candidate
sample-based verification is prohibitively costly.
We therefore approximate the constrained objective in (
9
) by a Lagrangian style penalty relaxation, using a hinge penalty as a conservative soft constraint, and rank a finite candidate set with an operational unsafety statistic
\widehat{u}
:

\displaystyle J_{\tau}(c;c^{\prime}):=

\displaystyle d(c,c^{\prime})+\alpha\big[\widehat{u}(c^{\prime})-\tau\big]_{+},

(18)

\displaystyle[z]_{+}

\displaystyle:=\max\{z,0\},

(19)

where
\alpha>0
is a penalty weight. The objective form is agnostic to how
\widehat{u}
is computed: Stage-1 uses the
prompt-only statistic
\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{LLM}}(c^{\prime})
for ranking, while Stage-2 draws
\hat{x}\sim G^{*}(\cdot\mid c^{\star})

and applies the safeguard VLM to obtain
\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{VLM}}(\hat{x})
for final acceptance
(Section
4.3
).

We instantiate

using MiniLM-L6 sentence embeddings
(
36
)
: letting
\phi(\cdot)
be the embedding map and

\tilde{\phi}(c)=\phi(c)/\|\phi(c)\|_{2}
,

d(c,c^{\prime}):=\arccos\!\big(\tilde{\phi}(c)^{\top}\tilde{\phi}(c^{\prime})\big)\in[0,\pi].

(20)

Starting from
c_{0}=c
, local search iterates: sample
N
rewrites
\{c_{t}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}
conditioned on
c_{t}
, evaluate

J_{\tau}(c;\cdot)
using
\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{LLM}}(\cdot)
over
\{c_{t}\}\cup\{c_{t}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}

c_{t+1}\in\mathrm{argmin}_{c^{\prime}\in\{c_{t}\}\cup\{c_{t}^{(n)}\}_{n=1}^{N}}\;J_{\tau}(c;c^{\prime}).

(21)

We optionally stop early when
\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{LLM}}(c_{t+1})\leq\tau
. To discourage unintended semantic drift, we
provide edit-preserving instructions (preserve key entities, composition, background, and style; edit only unsafe cues;
enforce fully clothed characters). These instructions are heuristic guidance rather than formal invariants.

4.3
Two-stage unsafety scoring and verification

Both stages use the same A/B multiple-choice protocol to extract an unsafety statistic from next token log-probabilities,
avoiding free-form generation. We use neutral labels (A/B) instead of Yes/No to reduce acquiescence and label/order
sensitivity
(
34
;
4
)
. Since the score is
protocol-dependent
, we keep the
elicitation template, token surface forms, and logit-to-score mapping identical across Stage-1 and Stage-2 (details in
Appendix
B
). Stage-1 guides local-search routing, while Stage-2 acts as the final safety gate.

Concretely, let
\mathcal{T}_{A}
and
\mathcal{T}_{B}
be small, fixed sets of token IDs for A=SAFE and B=UNSAFE
(Appendix
B
). From next-token probabilities, we aggregate mass over each set in log space to obtain

p_{A}
and
p_{B}
, set
\Delta:=\log p_{B}-\log p_{A}
, and define

\displaystyle\widehat{u}:=\sigma(\Delta),\qquad\sigma(\Delta):=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-\Delta)}\in[0,1].

(22)

Stage-2 applies this protocol with a safeguard VLM to a realized sample
\hat{x}\sim G^{*}(\cdot\mid c^{\prime})
, yielding

\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{VLM}}(\hat{x})
, which we use as an image-based
verification
statistic for acceptance
(§
4.1
). Stage-1 applies the same protocol with a prompt-only LLM to
c^{\prime}
to obtain

\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{LLM}}(c^{\prime})
, used
only
for candidate ranking and early stopping inside the local-search surrogate
objective (Eqs. (
18
)–(
21
)), not for acceptance.

For efficiency, we request only the top-
K
next-token log-probabilities (
K=20
). If no token in

\mathcal{T}_{A}\cup\mathcal{T}_{B}
appears in the returned top-
K
, we output
\widehat{u}=0.5
(abstain), which incurs a
large hinge penalty when
\tau
is small and thus discourages such candidates. We share a single tolerance
\tau
across
stages and fix the template, token sets, and mapping. We empirically justify the cascade (correlation, threshold agreement,
and runtime) in Appendix
B.2
.

4.4
Scope and limitations

Our method implements projected-reference sampling via finite search and makes no global-optimality claim for metric

5.2
Main results and analysis

Table

Cross-dataset safety generalization (IP).

Our method achieves the lowest inappropriate percentage not only on the in-domain CoProV2 benchmark but also on both OOD
prompt sets. On SD1.5, IP drops to 0.04/0.06/0.04 on CoProV2/I2P/UD, outperforming strong baselines that are competitive
in-domain yet transfer less reliably across prompt distributions (e.g., LatentGuard: 0.06 on CoProV2 vs. 0.16 on I2P).
This trend is consistent on SD2.1 and SDXL, indicating that the gains are not driven by CoProV2-specific phrasing or
concept coverage.

Robustness across diffusion backbones.

Safety gains persist under backbone changes without re-training the diffusion model. While unaligned models exhibit
substantial unsafe rates (e.g., SDXL: 0.49/0.31/0.47 on CoProV2/I2P/UD), our method reduces IP to 0.03/0.04/0.02 on SDXL
and similarly improves SD2.1 (0.05/0.06/0.04), suggesting the alignment transfers across SD variants rather than
overfitting to a specific checkpoint.

Utility on benign prompts (FID/CLIP on COCO).

On benign COCO captions, our method preserves image quality and text–image alignment close to the no-alignment reference
across all backbones (e.g., SD1.5: 32.46/33.36 vs. 32.34/33.42 in FID/CLIP), while substantially reducing IP on unsafe
prompts. In contrast, methods based on aggressive suppression or embedding/noise manipulation can incur clear utility
degradation, with notably worse FID and/or lower CLIP (e.g., UCE and PNO on SD1.5), consistent with semantic drift even
under benign inputs.

Guard-style pipelines and metric distortion.

Guard-style methods can exhibit strong CLIP yet degraded FID because utility is effectively evaluated on a prompt-filtered
subset. On COCO, LatentGuard and GuardT2I abort generation for 13.53% and 32.87% of captions, respectively, due to
prompt level screening (hence largely model-agnostic, independent of the diffusion backbone). On the retained prompts,
the generated images can remain comparable to the reference model, which helps preserve CLIP. However, the high abort
rate induces a selection bias in the samples used for FID, inflating the reported values and reducing comparability to
methods that generate for all prompts.

Overall, our method offers a favorable safety–utility frontier: it consistently achieves the lowest IP across datasets
and backbones while keeping benign-prompt utility near the original models. Compared to model-editing approaches, it
avoids distribution shift on safe prompts. Compared to guard pipelines, it reduces unsafe content without relying on
frequent refusals that can confound utility metrics.

5.5
Empirical validation of SPAT and projection diagnostics

We report two diagnostics: (i) SPAT-style safety–fidelity trade-offs across datasets/backbones, and (ii) projection-kernel behavior of our inference-time rewriting (near-identity on safe prompts and rapid stabilization).

In other words, safety improvements on unsafe benchmarks are accompanied by measurable distributional shift even on benign prompts, as reflected by the reference-closeness proxy. 6
Conclusion

We formalize SPAT in TV lens, showing that reducing unsafe generations under a fixed-reference target necessarily induces TV deviation and can impact benign prompt–image alignment.
Building on this, we propose inference-only prompt projection that selectively intervenes on high-risk prompts and maps them into a
\tau
-controlled safe set without retraining or fine-tuning the generator.
Across four datasets and three diffusion backbones, we achieve 16.7–60.0% relative reductions in IP versus strong model-level alignment baselines while staying near the unaligned reference on COCO. Future work includes tightening the operational–ideal
\tau
link, reducing semantic drift, and online attacks.

Impact Statement

Our inference-only prompt projection retrofits safety onto existing Text-to-Image generators without retraining by selectively rewriting high-risk prompts while largely preserving benign ones. Residual harms arise from imperfect evaluators and rewriting—false negatives/positives, unequal error rates across user groups, and misuse or misconfiguration of tolerance
\tau
. We recommend deployment only within a layered safety stack with calibrated
\tau
and evaluators, continuous monitoring and bias audits on diverse prompts, transparent failure reporting, and human review for high-stakes use.

Appendix A
Technical details for TV-based projection theory

This appendix formalizes the measure-theoretic and topological ingredients used in Section
3
. We provide (i) total variation bounds for bounded functionals, (ii) existence and measurable selection for
\tau
-safe minimal-edit projections under the angular distance, and (iii) kernelized SPAT.

A.1
Basic objects: spaces, probability measures, and kernels

Let
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
be a measurable space. We write
\mathcal{P}(\mathcal{X})
for the set of probability measures on
(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
.

Markov kernels.

A Markov kernel from
(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{C}))
to
(\mathcal{Y},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Y}))
is a map
K:\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Y})\to[0,1]
such that (i) for each
c\in\mathcal{C}
,
A\mapsto K(c,A)
is a probability measure on
\mathcal{Y}
, and (ii) for each
A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Y})
,
c\mapsto K(c,A)
is
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{C})
-measurable. We often write
K(\cdot\mid c)
for the measure
A\mapsto K(c,A)
.

Composition of kernels.

If
K
is a kernel from
\mathcal{C}
to
\mathcal{Y}
and
L
is a kernel from
\mathcal{Y}
to
\mathcal{Z}
, their composition
L\circ K
is the kernel from
\mathcal{C}
to
\mathcal{Z}
defined by

(L\circ K)(c,A):=\int_{\mathcal{Y}}L(y,A)\,K(c,dy),\qquad A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{Z}).

(23)

Standard measurability of (
23
) follows from kernel calculus
(
17
)
.

Dirac (deterministic) kernels.

For a measurable map
T:\mathcal{C}\to\mathcal{Y}
, the associated deterministic kernel is
K_{T}(c,\cdot):=\delta_{T(c)}(\cdot)
. It is a Markov kernel whenever
T
is measurable.

A.2
Total variation: definitions and inequalities

For probability measures
\nu,\nu^{\prime}
on
(\Omega,\mathcal{F})
, define

\mathrm{TV}(\nu,\nu^{\prime}):=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{F}}|\nu(A)-\nu^{\prime}(A)|.

(24)

Lemma A.1

(TV controls expectation gaps for bounded functionals)
.

Let
f:\Omega\to[0,1]
be measurable. Then

\big|\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{\prime}}[f]\big|\leq\mathrm{TV}(\nu,\nu^{\prime}).

(25)

Proof.

Let
A_{t}:=\{x:\ f(x)\geq t\}\in\mathcal{F}
. By distribution-function identity for
f\in[0,1]

(
9
)
,

\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f]=\int_{0}^{1}\nu(A_{t})\,dt\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{\prime}}[f]=\int_{0}^{1}\nu^{\prime}(A_{t})\,dt.

(26)

Hence

\displaystyle|\mathbb{E}_{\nu}[f]-\mathbb{E}_{\nu^{\prime}}[f]|\leq\int_{0}^{1}|\nu(A_{t})-\nu^{\prime}(A_{t})|\,dt\leq\sup_{A\in\mathcal{F}}|\nu(A)-\nu^{\prime}(A)|=\mathrm{TV}(\nu,\nu^{\prime}).

(27)

∎

A.4
\tau
-safe projection: formal assumptions and consequences

We restate the projection objects. Fix
\tau\in[0,1]
and define

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}:=\{c\in\mathcal{C}:\ u(G^{*}\mid c)\leq\tau\}
.
Let
\phi:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
be an embedding with
\phi(c)\neq 0
for all
c
, and define

\bar{\phi}(c):=\frac{\phi(c)}{\|\phi(c)\|_{2}}\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}.

(29)

The angular distance is

d(c,c^{\prime}):=\arccos\big(\langle\bar{\phi}(c),\bar{\phi}(c^{\prime})\rangle\big)\in[0,\pi],

(30)

and the projection correspondence is

\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(c):=\argmin_{c^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}}d(c,c^{\prime}).

(31)

Remark (metric vs. pseudometric).

If
\bar{\phi}
is not injective, then
d
is a pseudometric on
\mathcal{C}
: it may satisfy

d(c,c^{\prime})=0
for distinct prompts
c\neq c^{\prime}
.
This causes no inconsistency for our development.
All arguments use
d
only as a measurable, bounded cost, and we will explicitly allow the projection
output to be
set-valued
within a
d
-zero fiber.
Concretely,
d(c,c^{\prime})=0
is equivalent to
\bar{\phi}(c)=\bar{\phi}(c^{\prime})
, so the
d
-zero classes coincide
with the fibers of the normalized embedding map
\bar{\phi}
.

Assumption A.2

(Regularity for
\tau
-safe projection)
.

Fix
\tau\in[0,1]
and define
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}:=\{c\in\mathcal{C}:u(G^{*}\mid c)\leq\tau\}
. Assume:

(i)

(\mathcal{C},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{C}))
is standard Borel. The map
\bar{\phi}:\mathcal{C}\to\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
is Borel measurable (equivalently,
(c,c^{\prime})\mapsto d(c,c^{\prime})
is Borel measurable).

(ii)

(\mathcal{X},\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{X}))
is Polish with its Borel
\sigma
-field,
U:\mathcal{X}\to[0,1]
is bounded and continuous, and the reference law map
\Gamma(c):=G^{*}(\cdot\mid c)
is weakly continuous under
d
, i.e.,
d(c_{n},c)\to 0
implies
\Gamma(c_{n})\Rightarrow\Gamma(c)
.

(iii)

\bar{\phi}(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau})
is closed in
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}
is nonempty.

Lemma A.3

(Continuity of the reference risk map)
.

Under Assumption
A.2
, the map

c\mapsto u(G^{*}\mid c)=\int U(x)\,G^{*}(dx\mid c)
is continuous (with respect to
d
).

Proof.

Let
d(c_{n},c)\to 0
. By Assumption
A.2
(ii),

G^{*}(\cdot\mid c_{n})\Rightarrow G^{*}(\cdot\mid c)
.
Since
U
is bounded and continuous, the portmanteau theorem yields

\int U(x)\,G^{*}(dx\mid c_{n})\ \to\ \int U(x)\,G^{*}(dx\mid c).

∎

Lemma A.4

(Closedness of the
\tau
-safe set)
.

Under Assumption
A.2
,
\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}
is
d
-closed.

Proof.

By Lemma
A.3
,
c\mapsto u(G^{*}\mid c)
is continuous. Hence

\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}
is a closed sublevel set.
∎

Lemma A.5

(Compactness induced by angular embeddings)
.

Under Assumption
A.2
(iii),
(\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau},d)
is compact (in the pseudometric sense).

Proof.

Let
F:=\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}
and let
(c_{n})\subset F
.
Set
y_{n}:=\bar{\phi}(c_{n})\in\bar{\phi}(F)\subseteq\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
By Assumption
A.2
(iii),
\bar{\phi}(F)
is closed in the compact space
\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,
hence compact. Thus
(y_{n})
has a convergent subsequence
y_{n_{k}}\to y\in\bar{\phi}(F)
.
Since
y\in\bar{\phi}(F)
, there exists
c^{\star}\in F
with
\bar{\phi}(c^{\star})=y
. Then

d(c_{n_{k}},c^{\star})=\arccos(\langle y_{n_{k}},y\rangle)\ \to\ \arccos(\langle y,y\rangle)=0,

so
(c_{n_{k}})
has a
d
-convergent subsequence in
F
. Hence
(F,d)
is (sequentially) compact.
∎

Lemma A.6

(Nearest-point attainment under angular distance)
.

Under Assumption
A.2
,
\mathcal{P}_{\tau}(c)\neq\emptyset
for every
c\in\mathcal{C}
.

Proof.

Fix
c\in\mathcal{C}
and let
F:=\mathcal{C}_{\mathrm{safe},\tau}
.
By Lemma
A.5
,
(F,d)
is compact. The map
c^{\prime}\mapsto d(c,c^{\prime})
is continuous in
c^{\prime}
,
so it attains its minimum over

Appendix B
Two-stage unsafety scoring: protocol, calibration, and VLM–LLM correlation

This appendix documents the two-stage unsafety pipeline used in §
4.1
–§
4.3
and reports an empirical relationship between
the Stage-1 LLM score
P
(prompt-only) and the Stage-2 VLM score
Q
(image).
Our intent is
not
to substitute the VLM with the LLM: Stage-1 is a low-cost
routing/ranking
signal for candidate
rewrites during projection search, while Stage-2 is the
authoritative
verifier that gates acceptance of realized samples.
Accordingly, any mismatch between
P
and
Q
affects only compute/utility (which candidates are tried first and when local
search terminates), not the final safety decision, because acceptance is determined solely by
Q\leq\tau
with resampling budget
R
.

B.1
Shared deterministic A/B logprob protocol

Both stages use the same A/B multiple-choice protocol to avoid free-form generation and to reduce known response biases
associated with Yes/No formats. Let
\mathcal{T}_{A}
and
\mathcal{T}_{B}
be small, fixed sets of
surface forms

corresponding to the labels
A
(SAFE) and
B
(UNSAFE), including minor variants induced by whitespace,
parentheses, and newlines (Tab.
4
). We convert each surface form to token IDs using the model tokenizer
and aggregate next-token probability mass over the resulting IDs. Given next-token probabilities, we aggregate the mass over
each set (in log space for numerical stability) to obtain

\displaystyle p_{A}:=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{A}}p(t\mid\cdot),\qquad p_{B}:=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{B}}p(t\mid\cdot),\qquad\Delta:=\log p_{B}-\log p_{A},

(41)

and define the protocol-dependent unsafety statistic

\displaystyle\widehat{u}:=\sigma(\Delta)\in[0,1],\qquad\sigma(z)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-z)}.

(42)

Stage-1 applies this rule to the prompt
c^{\prime}
alone, producing
P=\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{LLM}}(c^{\prime})
; Stage-2 applies the same rule
to the realized image
\hat{x}\sim G^{*}(\cdot\mid c^{\prime})
, producing
Q=\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{VLM}}(\hat{x})
.
For efficiency, we request only top-
K
next-token log-probabilities (we use
K=20
). If no token in

\mathcal{T}_{A}\cup\mathcal{T}_{B}
appears in the returned top-
K
, we output
\widehat{u}=0.5
(abstain), which is penalized
under small
\tau
by the hinge in the local-search objective (Eq.
18
–
19
), discouraging such candidates without making
any hard claim about their safety.

B.2
VLM–LLM relationship and why a cascade is appropriate

The cascade is motivated by an asymmetry in both cost and authority: computing the Stage-2 score
Q
requires image generation and VLM inference, whereas the Stage-1 score
P
is prompt-only and can be evaluated cheaply for many candidates. We therefore separate roles by design: Stage-1 is used only to route compute (ranking and early stopping inside local search), while Stage-2 remains the sole acceptance gate, i.e., the method never accepts based on
P
and accepts only if
Q\leq\tau
after sampling an image, with resampling up to
R
attempts. We report prompt level end-to-end wall-clock time under our proposed projection-and-verification pipeline, evaluated in two variants: a VLM-only variant that uses Stage-2 scoring throughout search, and the proposed cascade that uses prompt-only scoring for Stage-1 routing while reserving the VLM for Stage-2 verification. Under this metric (one final realized image per input prompt, including local search, image generation, and all safety-scoring overhead), the mean time per prompt drops from 1.93/1.82/2.51 sec (SD1.5/SD2.1/SDXL; VLM-only) to 0.38/0.40/0.58 sec (SD1.5/SD2.1/SDXL; cascade), a
4.3\times
–
5.1\times
speedup while preserving the same acceptance rule
Q\leq\tau
.

To justify routing quality at the operational tolerance
\tau=0.05
, we additionally report an enrichment diagnostic that matches the algorithmic use of
P
and is computed as a
measurement study
of the two scoring signals (not after applying our projection method). Specifically, we compute
P=\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{LLM}}(c)
directly on the original prompt
c
, and we compute
Q=\widehat{u}_{\mathrm{VLM}}(\hat{x})
by first realizing an image
\hat{x}\sim G^{*}(\cdot\mid c)
from the same original prompt using Stable Diffusion v1.5 as the reference backbone (i.e.,
G^{*}=\mathrm{SD}\text{-}\mathrm{v1.5}
in this diagnostic) and then scoring the realized image under the shared A/B protocol (Appendix
B.1
). We sort the
n
paired samples by
P
in ascending order, define
k=\lceil fn\rceil
for a forwarded fraction
f\in(0,1]
, and let
\mathrm{Top}_{f}(P)
be the first
k
samples in this sorted list (the lowest-
P

\Pr(Q\leq\tau\mid\mathrm{Top}_{f}(P))
and compare it to the baseline pass probability
\Pr(Q\leq\tau)
without routing. On

training split (indices in Appendix

49\%
–

D.4
Ablation on A/B Label Token Ordering

Motivation.

As described in §
4.3
, our unsafety statistic
\widehat{u}
is extracted via an A/B multiple-choice
protocol from next-token log-probabilities, using neutral labels to mitigate known response biases such as acquiescence and
label/order sensitivity
(
34
;
4
)
. Since
\widehat{u}
is inherently

protocol-dependent
(template, token sets, and the probability-to-score mapping), we verify that our reported
safety–utility trade-offs are not an artifact of a particular A/B ordering.

Setup.

We repeat the full SD1.5 evaluation while keeping the pipeline, prompts, tolerance
\tau
, and all hyperparameters fixed, and
changing
only
the A/B semantic assignment. In the default configuration, A=SAFE and B=UNSAFE with token sets

(\mathcal{T}_{A},\mathcal{T}_{B})
. In the ablation, we
swap the token sets across labels
(A=UNSAFE, B=SAFE) in
both

Stage-1 (prompt-only LLM) and Stage-2 (VLM verifier), but we keep the elicitation template, top-
K
log-probability extraction,
and the scoring rule unchanged—in particular, we still compute
\Delta=\log p_{B}-\log p_{A}
and
\widehat{u}=\sigma(\Delta)
as
in Eq. (
22
). The reported numbers correspond to a single-seed run.

Results.

Table
8
shows that reversing the A/B ordering yields very similar outcomes across safety (IP) and benign
utility (COCO FID/CLIP). IP shifts are within
0.01
across CoProV2/I2P/UD (I2P:
0.06\!\rightarrow\!0.05
, UD:

0.04\!\rightarrow\!0.05
, CoProV2 unchanged at
0.04
), and COCO utility remains close (FID:

32.46\!\rightarrow\!32.87
, CLIP:
33.36\!\rightarrow\!33.25
).

Takeaway.

This ablation suggests that our conclusions are not driven by a fragile dependence on label ordering: swapping the A/B
assignment in
both
stages produces nearly unchanged safety–utility behavior. Together with fixing the template, token
surface forms, and mapping across Stage-1/Stage-2 (§
4.3
), these results support A/B elicitation as a
stable operational interface for two-stage routing and verification.

D.5
Ablation on the Choice of Prompt-Embedding Model

We ablate the choice of the prompt-embedding model used to define the prompt-space distance in our projection objective.
Specifically, we compare three off-the-shelf Hugging Face checkpoints:

sentence-transformers/all-MiniLM-L6-v2
(default),

sentence-transformers/all-mpnet-base-v2
,

and
intfloat/e5-small
.

For all three, we compute distances as the angular metric on
\ell_{2}
-normalized embeddings, i.e.,

d(c,c^{\prime})=\arccos\!\left(\left\langle\frac{\phi(c)}{\|\phi(c)\|_{2}},\frac{\phi(c^{\prime})}{\|\phi(c^{\prime})\|_{2}}\right\rangle\right)
.
For a controlled comparison, we fix SD 1.5 as the diffusion backbone and keep
all
other components and hyperparameters
(including
\tau
, surrogate/verification settings, local-search budgets, and prompts) at the baseline default configuration;
we do not retune any parameter per embedding model.
We use a single random seed throughout.
On COCO, we apply the same projection pipeline and report FID against the ground-truth COCO reference images under the same evaluation protocol.

9
summarizes the results on CoProV2, I2P, UD (IP; lower is better) and COCO (FID/CLIP).

On COCO, FID changes are small (slightly improved for MPNet/E5 in this setting), while CLIP remains in a similar range with minor variations.

Qualitative Results: Generated Image Examples and Failure Analysis

E.1
Additional qualitative results

Figures
9
,
10
, and
11
present a qualitative comparison of images generated from the CoProV2 dataset using different diffusion model variants, including SD 1.5, SD 2.1, and SDXL. Figures
12
,
13
, and
14
provide corresponding qualitative results on the COCO dataset. In all figures, the first row shows images generated from the original dataset, grouped by category and without any alignment mechanism. The second, third, and fourth rows correspond to AlignGuard, LatentGuard, and GuardT2I, respectively, while the final row presents the results produced by our method.

As illustrated in Figure
9
, when using the SD 1.5 model, AlignGuard successfully generates safe images; however, the resulting outputs often exhibit semantic deviations from the original prompts. For example, in the violence category, AlignGuard produces images of notebooks, while in the self-harm category, it generates overly simplified, cartoon-like illustrations. Similar behavior is observed with the SD 2.1 model (Figure
10
), where AlignGuard produces simplified images for both the sexual and self-harm categories. In contrast, when applied to the SDXL model (Figure
11
), AlignGuard fails to consistently enforce safety constraints, generating unsafe images in the violence, shocking, and illegal categories. On the COCO dataset, AlignGuard largely preserves the semantic content of the generated images; nevertheless, noticeable stylistic simplification is observed in several cases, particularly for SD 2.1 (samples 5 and 6) and SDXL (samples 5 and 6).

In parallel, guard-based models appear to effectively detect unsafe content; however, they suffer from a high rate of false positives. As discussed in Paragraph
5.2
, these models tend to overzealously flag benign content as unsafe on the COCO dataset. Consequently, both LatentGuard and GuardT2I frequently generate black images for COCO samples, reflecting excessive suppression rather than precise safety control.

In contrast, images generated by our method consistently preserve the semantic meaning of the original prompts while effectively eliminating unsafe content across all diffusion model variants and categories. Moreover, as shown in Table
3(a)
, our method maintains semantic fidelity on the COCO dataset, producing outputs that closely align with the original inputs.

Appendix F
Dataset Source and Acquisition

CoProV2 (SD1.5).

We use the CoProV2 dataset released for Stable Diffusion v1.5.
We obtain the full release (including paired images) from HuggingFace:

https://huggingface.co/datasets/Visualignment/CoProv2-SD15
.
For prompt level bookkeeping (e.g., categories and prompt lists), we follow the
public CSV files provided by the SafetyDPO repository:

https://github.com/Visualignment/SafetyDPO
(see
data/CoProv2_train.csv
and
data/CoProv2_test.csv
).

For fitting/calibration that requires unsafe prompt–image pairs, we extract
the
unsafe
side of CoProV2 training pairs (unsafe prompt and its paired unsafe image),
and uniformly subsample 1,000 pairs without replacement using a fixed RNG seed (seed=42).
We ensure this fitting subset is disjoint from all evaluation splits, and we store the
selected example indices/IDs for exact reproducibility.

I2P.

We use the Inappropriate Image Prompts (I2P) benchmark as a prompt-only evaluation set.
We download the prompt list from HuggingFace:

https://huggingface.co/datasets/AIML-TUDA/i2p
.
Since I2P provides prompts (not images), all images used for evaluation are generated
by the corresponding reference Text-to-Image model under our standardized inference settings.

UD (Unsafe Diffusion prompts).

We use harmful prompt sets from
Unsafe Diffusion
and download the official release from Zenodo:

https://zenodo.org/records/8255664
(file:
Unsafe Prompts&Images Dataset.zip
).
We use the prompt CSVs contained in this release as the UD prompt source for evaluation.

COCO-3k (benign prompts).

To benchmark benign-prompt utility, we start from the HuggingFace dataset

https://huggingface.co/datasets/sayakpaul/coco-30-val-2014
,
which contains 30k (image, caption) pairs derived from COCO 2014 validation.
We construct a 3k subset by shuffling with a fixed seed (seed=42) and selecting
the first 3,000 examples (uniform without replacement). We store the selected
indices/IDs to make the COCO-3k subset exactly reproducible.

Implementation note (sampling).

In practice, we implement subsampling via a deterministic shuffle-and-select routine
(e.g., using HuggingFace
datasets

shuffle(seed=42)
followed by
select
),
and we persist the resulting index list alongside our code release.

Safety handling.

These datasets may contain harmful prompts and/or unsafe images. We restrict access to the raw files
to research personnel and use them solely for benchmarking and safety research purposes.
