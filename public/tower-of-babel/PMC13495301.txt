A systematic review and meta-analysis of fatty acid composition in polycystic ovary syndrome: perspectives from blood and follicular fluid
Authors: Reza Zarezadeh, Amir Fattahi, Kobra Hamdi, Mohammad Nouri, Masoud Darabi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & aimsA comprehensive assessment of fatty acids (FAs) in polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) has yet to be carried out. The current study aimed to synthesize existing evidence on the FA composition of blood and follicular fluid (FF) in women with PCOS compared to non-PCOS controls.MethodsFour electronic databases were systematically searched until February 2025 using standard keywords. Risk of bias and evidence certainty were assessed using the ROBINS-I framework and the GRADE system, respectively. Random effects models were employed to calculate pooled standardized mean differences (SMDs) with respective 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Publication bias was inspected using funnel plots, Begg’s test, and Egger’s test.ResultsTwelve studies qualified for inclusion in the meta-analysis. Overall effect sizes for several prominent FAs, including palmitoleic acid (SMD [95% CI] = 0.389 [0.121, 0.657], p = 0.004, I2 = 25.2%), oleic acid (SMD [95% CI] = 0.811 [0.107, 1.515], p = 0.024, I2 = 90.0%), linoleic acid (SMD [95% CI] = 0.542 [0.349, 0.736], p < 0.001, I2 = 0%), and arachidonic acid (SMD [95% CI] = 0.252 [0.053, 0.451], p = 0.013, I2 = 0%), indicated an increase in the PCOS group. The direction and significance of the results partly persisted following subgroup analyses based on sample type and lipid fraction, as well as sensitivity analyses.ConclusionThe findings of this study suggest that FA profile may be altered in women with PCOS, primarily characterized by potential elevations in levels of key mono- and n-6 polyunsaturated FAs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13048-026-02151-5.Keywords: Polycystic Ovary Syndrome, Fatty Acids, Blood, Follicular Fluid, Meta-Analysis

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a prevalent endocrinopathy, affecting around 5–18% of reproductively active women globally, with variations often attributed to the criteria used for diagnosis, and accounting for the chief cause of anovulatory infertility. Characterized by hyperandrogenism, ovulation loss or irregularity, and polycystic ovarian appearance on ultrasound, PCOS is a multifaceted disorder that brings about complicated endocrine, reproductive, and metabolic implications. Aside from its influence on fertility, PCOS is intimately connected to various metabolic disturbances, including obesity, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and metabolic syndrome. Furthermore, it raises the probability of progressing type 2 diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular diseases, and cancer, jeopardizing the health status of affected women in the long term [1]. Although the pathophysiology of PCOS is yet to be completely disclosed, accumulating evidence suggests that metabolic dysfunction is implicated in its development and clinical manifestation.

Dyslipidemia affects approximately 40–75% of women with PCOS, depending on body mass index (BMI), ethnicity, and other factors, making it a common metabolic disruption in these patients [2]. It has been extensively documented that PCOS is associated with aberrations in plasma levels of well-known lipid metabolism markers, including triglycerides, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [3]. Beyond the conventional lipid profile, lipidomic evidence revealed extensive alterations in numerous circulating lipid metabolites such as acylcarnitines, glycerides, phospholipids, and their derivatives in women with PCOS [4–6]. As a fluid that encompasses the oocyte and can be reflective of both systemic and localized metabolic composition, follicular fluid (FF) also undergoes substantial changes with regard to lipids, some of which were associated with oocyte developmental competence [7, 8].

Fatty acids (FAs) are rich sources of metabolic energy, indispensable constituents of membrane lipids, and precursors of bioactive lipid mediators, playing prominent roles in metabolic and reproductive functions [9]. As one of the significant structural units of lipids, FAs may have a direct impact on the metabolism of lipid molecules and thereby participate in the dysregulation of lipid metabolism [10, 11]. Importantly, specific FAs are involved in the processes that are linked to the pathophysiology of PCOS, including insulin resistance [12], oxidative stress [13, 14], inflammation [15, 16], and steroidogenesis [17, 18]. Furthermore, FA profile is correlated with granulosa cell function [19, 20], oocyte quality [21, 22], and embryo development [23]. Hence, understanding the FA composition may provide deeper insights into the metabolic and reproductive disturbances observed in PCOS and offer new avenues for diagnostic or therapeutic interventions.

Although the FA composition of various body compartments, particularly blood and FF, in women with PCOS has come into the spotlight over the past few years, the findings have failed to reach a consensus, possibly due to methodological differences among the studies. To date, no comprehensive synthesis has systematically assessed existing evidence about the effect of PCOS on body FA profile. Therefore, the current systematic review and meta-analysis was conceived and conducted to critically evaluate the available literature on blood and intrafollicular FA composition in women with PCOS compared to non-PCOS controls.

Methodology

Protocol and registration

The current systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in compliance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 statement [24]. The protocol failed to be registered in the International Database of Prospectively Registered Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO), as there was only the feasibility for registering “intervention reviews” in the time period between study conceptualization and data extraction.

Search approach

A systematic search was carried out in four databases, including PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, and Cochrane Library, from their inception to February 2025. Customizing to the features of each database, search strategies were constructed by combining the terms as follows: Polycystic Ovary Syndrome, Fatty Acids, Lauric Acid, Myristic Acid, Palmitic Acid, Palmitoleic Acid, Stearic Acid, Oleic Acid, Linoleic Acid, Linolenic Acid, Arachidic Acid, Dihomo-Gamma-Linolenic Acid, Arachidonic Acid, Eicosapentaenoic Acid, Behenic Acid, Docosapentaenoic Acid, Docosahexaenoic Acid, Lignoceric Acid, and Nervonic Acid. Relevant synonymous and abbreviated terms were also used to expand the search. Search strategies are presented in Supplementary Table S1. Except for refining search results for publications in the English language, no other limitation was imposed. Furthermore, the reference lists of recovered reports and related review articles were manually checked to identify possibly omitted eligible studies.

Eligibility criteria

Following the removal of duplicates, studies were screened and selected in a stepwise procedure through the exclusion of ineligible reports according to details obtained from the title and abstract, followed by the careful full-text inspection of the remaining studies, which were regarded as eligible in the first stage of screening.

According to PICOS (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome, Study design), studies qualified inclusion if they satisfied the following criteria: (1) recruiting women with PCOS diagnosed based on National Institutes of Health (NIH), Rotterdam, or Androgen Excess Society criteria; (2) enrolling women without PCOS as controls; (3) quantifying either absolute or relative amounts of individual FAs in blood-derived specimens or FF; and (4) having a cohort, case-control, or cross-sectional study design. Research containing information on at least one individual FA was included, whereas no ceiling on the number of reported FAs was considered. Moreover, no restriction was applied in the process of study selection based on the quantification technique of FAs, e.g., gas chromatography (GC), gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), liquid chromatography-mass spectrometry (LC-MS), and liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS), or lipid fraction of FAs, e.g., total, phospholipid, and free FAs.

Studies were excluded from the analysis if any of the following conditions was the case: (1) diagnosing PCOS on the basis of arbitrary criteria rather than well-established criteria, e.g., NIH, Rotterdam, etc., (2) lacking non-PCOS control group, (3) providing either no data or non-extractable data on levels of individual FAs, (5) not being an original research article with an analytical observational design, including books, reviews, meta-analyses, practical guidelines, editorial comments, letters to the editor, hypotheses, conference abstracts, case reports, theoretical studies, experimental research on cell culture or animal models, and clinical trials, (6) investigating different biological samples but not blood-derived specimens or FF, and (7) analyzing short-chain FAs originated from gut bacterial flora.

Data retrieval

Two authors reviewed and elicited the qualifying studies and corresponding data in an independent and cross-over fashion, respectively. The information retrieved from any of the eligible publications comprised the first author, publication year, country, features of the study population (i.e., study groups, sample size, age, BMI), lipid fraction FAs belonging to, quantification method of FAs, reported unit of FAs, featured FAs with respective means and standard deviations (SDs), and narrative synopsis of results. In the case of non-numerical or graphical presentation of data, which consequently prevented their direct transcription, the investigators were requested to provide further information. If no response was received from the researchers, data were obtained from graphs using the PlotDigitizer software (PlotDigitizer, version 3.1.6, plotdigitizer.com).

Quality assessment

The methodological rigor of identified publications was appraised by the use of the Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies of Interventions (ROBINS-I) framework [25], which offers the possibility to estimate the bias in seven distinct domains, as follows: bias due to confounding, bias in selection of participants, bias in classification of intervention, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing data, bias in outcome measurement, and bias due to selective reporting. Based on the ROBINS-I tool, the overall risk of bias for each study falls into one of the low, moderate, serious, or critical categories. If the collective risk of bias was determined to be serious or critical for a certain study, it was considered to be biased as a whole. The robvis software (Risk-of-bias VISualization, riskofbias.info, Bristol, UK) was utilized to graphically depict domain-specific and overall risk of bias for the included studies [26]. Possible publication bias was examined by visually inspecting funnel plot asymmetry and conducting Begg’s and Egger’s tests. If evidence of publication bias was noticed, the reason behind it was scrutinized using the trim-and-fill analysis. Two authors individually assessed each study, and consensus was achieved by discussing or checking with a third author when controversy arose.

The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) system was used to weigh the evidence quality of each outcome. In this regard, results certainty was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low according to the score of selected studies across five domains, including risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and other considerations, e.g., publication bias.

Data synthesis and meta-analysis

Only FAs for which data were available from at least three independent studies were included in the meta-analysis to ensure sufficient statistical power and robustness. Statistical analyses were carried out using the Comprehensive Meta-Analysis (CMA) version 3 (Biostat, Englewood, NJ, USA). The standardized mean differences (SMDs) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to measure individual effect sizes. Random effects models were used to calculate pooled estimates. Between-study heterogeneity was determined using the Cochrane’s Q test, I-squared (I2), and tau-squared (τ2) statistics. A significant p-value for the Cochran’s Q test or an I2 value higher than 75% was considered as high statistical heterogeneity. To address the potential effect of confounding factors on the association between PCOS and FA composition, subgroup analyses were conducted when adequate data were available. The robustness of the results was examined by sensitivity analyses through the successive exclusion of each study at a time. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

To ensure uniformity in statistical analysis, the mean and SD were estimated based on median, range, and sample size wherever necessary [27]. In addition, if the standard error of the mean (SEM) or CI was reported, SD was calculated by the SD = SEM × √n and SD = (CI/3.92) × √n formulas, respectively. Furthermore, to avoid violating the assumption of independence between data points in meta-analysis, wherever two values were reported for a certain outcome, one of them was selected to enter into the main analysis.

Results

Study selection

A total of 2242 potentially relevant records were retrieved as a result of the initial database search: 496 from PubMed, 820 from Scopus, 789 from Web of Science, and 137 from Cochrane. After omitting duplicates, 1066 reports were excluded according to the data extracted from the title and abstract. The remaining 122 articles underwent full-text screening, of which ultimately 12 studies were found to qualify for inclusion in the review [28, 39]. Study characteristics

The major characteristics of the identified publications are summarized in Table 1. The included studies were published between 2014 and 2023 and mostly conducted in China [28, 30, 32, 33, 35–39], with one study from each of the United Kingdom [31], Poland [29], and Germany [34]. The overall number of women included in the review was 1472, consisting of 842 PCOS patients and 630 non-PCOS controls. The diagnosis of PCOS was implemented based on the Rotterdam criteria in all investigations. In seven out of 12 articles, the control group was selected from healthy women without evidence of androgen excess and ovulatory dysfunction, and FAs were quantified in plasma or serum [28–34]. The remaining five studies recruited infertile women seeking in vitro fertilization (IVF), in which individuals with tubal obstruction and male infertility accounted for controls, and FF was examined for FAs [35–39]. Concerning the lipid fraction harboring FAs, the majority of reports focused on the profile of the free (non-esterified) fraction [30, 33, 34, 36, 37, 39], with phospholipid [28, 29, 32] and total [31, 35] fractions coming in subsequent places in order. Additionally, one study analyzed the favored FA in both free and total fractions [38]. In most eligible articles, the method of choice for FA measurement was GC-MS [30, 32, 33, 35, 36, 39]. This was followed by GC in three [28, 29, 31], LC-MS/MS in two [34, 37], and LC-MS in one [38] investigation. Seven out of 12 studies presented absolute concentrations of FAs [30, 33, 34, 36–39], while the remainder used a relative unit for reporting FA values [28, 29, 31, 32, 35]. Five publications provided a relatively comprehensive profile of FAs, comprising more than 10 distinct FAs [29, 31, 32, 35, 37], whereas the remaining studies included only a subset of key FAs [28, 30, 33, 34, 36, 38, 39].

Quality assessment

Scrutinizing the rigor of the included studies revealed that half of them were of a moderate overall risk of bias, and the other half were of serious aggregate quality (Fig. 1A). Bias due to confounding contributed the most to upgrading the overall risk of bias in the selected studies. Specifically, four reports were rated serious in terms of confounder bias owing to neglecting the confounding effect of BMI [29, 32, 34, 38], while the remainder were deemed to be of moderate confounder bias because of omitting other potential confounding factors such as dietary intake, genetic context, physical activity, etc., which were not controlled in any of the included articles and might be unlikely to be a major source of bias due to relative homogeneity of the populations with regard to geographical and cultural backgrounds. The overall risk of bias was also graded as serious in two out of 12 studies due to strong evidence of reporting bias arising from the incomplete provision of FA data in the study groups [30, 33]. Moreover, one publication showed a moderate level of data missingness [34] (Fig. 2B).

Meta-analysis

Twenty-one individual FAs and three FA groups were found to be eligible for meta-analysis. There were significant differences in palmitoleic acid (C16:1n-7; SMD [95% CI] = 0.389 [0.121, 0.657], p = 0.004), oleic acid (C18:1n-9; SMD [95% CI] = 0.811 [0.107, 1.515], p = 0.024), linoleic acid (C18:2n-6; SMD [95% CI] = 0.542 [0.349, 0.736], p < 0.001), dihomo-γ-linolenic acid (DGLA, C20:3n-6; SMD [95% CI] = 0.774 [0.367, 1.180], p < 0.001), and arachidonic acid (C20:4n-6; SMD [95% CI] = 0.252 [0.053, 0.451], p = 0.013), all being higher in PCOS patients compared to control subjects (Fig. 3A-E). On the contrary, erucic acid (C22:1n-9) statistically differed between two groups, with lower levels in women with PCOS (SMD [95% CI] = -0.877 [-1.553, -0.201], p = 0.011; Fig. 3F). Heterogeneity across studies was low for palmitoleic acid (I2 = 25.2%) and insignificant for linoleic acid (I2 = 0%), DGLA (I2 = 9.9%), and arachidonic acid (I2 = 0%). However, high and moderate levels of inter-study variability were identified in the case of oleic acid (I2 = 90.0%) and erucic acid (I2 = 66.0%), respectively. No other significant difference was noted with respect to the rest of the individual FAs, as well as FA groups, in women with PCOS compared to their non-PCOS counterparts (p > 0.05). Details of the synthesized results are provided in Supplementary Table S2.

Subgroup analysis

Sample type

According to subgroup analyses by sample type, plasma or serum from women with PCOS showed significantly elevated levels of linoleic acid (SMD [95% CI] = 0.529 [0.267, 0.791], p < 0.001, I2 = 0), along with statistically reduced values of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, C22:6n-3; SMD [95% CI] = -0.521 [-0.865, -0.177], p = 0.003, I2 = 50.2%), compared to controls (Fig. 4A, B). In addition, FF from the PCOS group contained significantly higher levels of oleic acid (SMD [95% CI] = 1.065 [0.209, 1.920], p = 0.015, I2 = 91.4%) and arachidonic acid (SMD [95% CI] = 0.274 [0.025, 0.522], p = 0.031, I2 = 3.6%) than that of the control group (Fig. 4C, D). Intrafollicular values of palmitoleic acid (SMD [95% CI] = 0.481 [-0.033, 0.995], p = 0.067, I2 = 65.1%) and DHA (SMD [95% CI] = 0.422 [-0.025, 0.869], p = 0.064, I2 = 55.4%) also tended to be different between women with and without PCOS, but they did not reach statistical significance (Fig. 4E, F). Notably, the pooled estimates for DHA significantly differed between plasma or serum and FF subgroups (p = 0.001). There was no further statistical difference in FA composition between PCOS and non-PCOS cases across subgroups of sample type (p > 0.05; Supplementary Table S3).

Lipid fraction

Subgroup analyses by lipid fraction indicated that free linoleic acid was significantly different, undergoing an increase in women with PCOS compared to controls (SMD [95% CI] = 0.483 [0.201, 0.764], p = 0.001; Fig. 4G). Conversely, a statistical decrease in phospholipid levels of eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA, C20:5n-3; SMD [95% CI] = -0.637 [-1.030, -0.244], p = 0.001) and docosapentaenoic acid (DPA, C22:5n-3; SMD [95% CI] = -0.427 [-0.682, -0.172], p = 0.001) was observed in the PCOS group by comparison with the control group (Fig. 4H, I). The content of DHA in the phospholipid fraction also tended to decline in PCOS patients, but the difference failed to reach statistical significance (SMD [95% CI] = -0.487 [-1.005, 0.030], p = 0.065; Fig. 4J). Between-study heterogeneity was low for linoleic acid (I2 = 27.6%) in the free FA subgroup and insignificant for DPA (I2 = 23.5%) and moderate for EPA (I2 = 53.9%) and DHA (I2 = 72.6%) in the phospholipid subclass. No other notable difference in values of FAs was found between cases with and without PCOS among distinct subgroups of lipid fraction (p > 0.05; Supplementary Table S4).

Sensitivity analysis

Based on leave-one-out sensitivity analyses, the pooled estimates for palmitoleic acid, linoleic acid, and DGLA remained stable and robust, irrespective of the omission of any single study (Fig. 5A-C). Nevertheless, in the case of oleic acid, excluding each of the reports by Szczuko et al. [29], Lai et al. [37], and Ma et al. [35] resulted in the marginal deviation of the overall effect size from the statistical significance (Fig. 5D). Similarly, the pooled estimate for arachidonic acid demonstrated trivial and modest instability against the removal of studies by Szczuko et al. [29] and Lai et al. [37], respectively (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, omitting each of the articles by Zhang et al. [32] and Szczuko et al. [29] negated the statistical significance of the overall effect size for erucic acid (Fig. 5F). By contrast, the pooled estimates for α-linolenic acid (C18:3n-3), behenic acid (C22:0), and total polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) substantially altered following the exclusion of the studies by Lu et al. [28], Szczuko et al. [29], and Tian et al. [30], respectively (Fig. 5G-I).

Publication bias

Although some extent of asymmetry was visually observed in funnel plots corresponding to most outcomes, relevant statistical tests did not consistently identify significant evidence of publication bias (Table 3). In the case of arachidic acid (C20:0), eicosenoic acid (C20:1n-9), and behenic acid, either Begg’s test or Egger’s test indicated statistical significance, whereas the other remained non-significant, suggesting a modest probability of publication bias for these FAs. However, given the small number of reports per outcome, ranging from 3 to 8, the power of these tools for detecting publication bias is limited, and their results should be interpreted cautiously.

Discussion

Changes in the profile of FAs exert a considerable influence on various biological processes and cell function [40] and are intimately connected to a wide range of pathological conditions such as cardiovascular diseases, diabetes mellitus, and metabolic syndrome [41]. There is a mounting body of research investigating levels of FAs in women with PCOS. However, findings remained inconsistent due to the existence of significant heterogeneity in various aspects, like the types of FAs analyzed, body compartments in which FAs were examined, the results obtained, etc., precluding drawing firm conclusions about the impact of PCOS on FA composition. In light of these inconsistencies, this systematic review and meta-analysis was conceived to comprehensively synthesize existing evidence and thereby elucidate the relationship between PCOS and FA profile.

As a consequence of analyzing 12 individual research articles, our work found that the FA composition of major body fluids underwent substantial alterations in PCOS patients. Specifically, PCOS was associated with an elevation in levels of multiple key FAs, including palmitoleic acid, linoleic acid, DGLA, oleic acid, and arachidonic acid, although the trends with regard to the latter two FAs were not fully robust according to sensitivity analyses. Subgroup analyses also demonstrated that plasma or serum from women with PCOS was enriched with linoleic acid and depleted of DHA, while increasing values of oleic acid and arachidonic acid in FF. From the standpoint of the lipid fraction, the PCOS group revealed higher levels of linoleic acid in the free FA fraction and, conversely, lower levels of EPA and DPA in the phospholipid fraction. To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first systematic review and meta-analysis assessing alterations in the FA profile of the main biological fluids in women with PCOS.

Oleic acid, followed by palmitoleic acid, accounts for the largest proportion of monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) in the human body [42]. Stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD1) is the key enzyme that catalyzes the conversion of saturated fatty acids (SFAs) into MUFAs. Notably, SCD1 has been reported to be upregulated in PCOS adipose tissue [43], which may potentially promote the production and release of MUFAs from adipose tissue. Concurrently, the de novo biosynthesis of palmitoleic acid and oleic acid has been found to rise in abdominal subcutaneous adipose stem cells from PCOS cases compared to those from controls during adipogenesis as a result of active chromatin rearrangement and subsequent adipogenic gene upregulation [44]. Moreover, pregnant women with PCOS demonstrated higher levels of oleic acid and desaturation activity in their abdominal subcutaneous adipose tissue than their non-PCOS counterparts [45]. The findings of a metabolomic analysis of serum also unraveled that palmitoleic acid, as well as its and oleic acid’s amide derivatives, are elevated in the PCOS group compared to the control [46]. These observations concur with those of our study, in which women with PCOS displayed markedly higher overall values of palmitoleic acid and oleic acid than control subjects. Importantly, serum levels of palmitoleic acid and oleic acid were shown to be robustly correlated with indices of insulin resistance [30], which, taken together with our results, suggests that increased amounts of primary MUFAs might play a prominent role in the development of insulin resistance and metabolic dysfunction in PCOS.

Aside from the overall increase, our findings also indicated that FF from PCOS patients contains elevated levels of oleic acid, with a tendency toward augmented values of palmitoleic acid. These observations are in keeping with those of a previously published study [47], corroborating the intrafollicular enrichment of oleic acid in particular, but also palmitoleic acid, in women with PCOS. Several lines of evidence suggest that excessive levels of these FAs are linked to various pathophysiological processes within PCOS follicles. For instance, palmitoleic acid was found to be present in higher amounts in FF from PCOS women with ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome compared to normoresponder PCOS cases [48], implying a possible contribution of increased palmitoleic acid to exaggerated ovarian response to gonadotropins in PCOS. It has also been reported that the exposure of ovarian granulosa cells to aberrantly high concentrations of oleic acid could stimulate inflammatory response and disrupt fatty acid β-oxidation in these cells, leading to impairment in oocyte development [37]. In support of this, embryo fragmentation was shown to have a negative correlation with intrafollicular values of oleic acid in women with PCOS [39].

Linoleic acid, DGLA, and arachidonic acid are predominant n-6 PUFAs in the human body [42]. As an essential FA, linoleic acid is solely obtainable from dietary sources and acts as a precursor of more elongated n-6 FAs [49]. Several studies have reported that circulating levels of linoleic acid are significantly elevated in the PCOS group in comparison to the control one [46, 50, 51]. Triglyceride species harboring linoleic acid and arachidonic acid were also found to be higher in pregnant women with PCOS than in their non-PCOS counterparts [52]. Moreover, metabolomic profiling of vaginal fluids from PCOS cases revealed enrichment in metabolites related to linoleic acid and arachidonic acid metabolism in the affected cohort [53]. In agreement with these findings, our results demonstrated a marked increase in overall values of linoleic acid, DGLA, and arachidonic acid in women with PCOS, suggesting a higher total content of n-6 PUFAs in this group. Importantly, it has been shown that plasma levels of n-6 PUFAs are positively correlated with circulating values of androgens [54]. In addition, DGLA has direct associations with indicators of insulin dysfunction in PCOS patients [30, 32], highlighting the involvement of n-6 PUFAs in the pathophysiology of PCOS. In support of this, excessive dietary consumption of linoleic acid was reported to induce hallmarks of PCOS, including hyperandrogenism, impaired ovulation, and polycystic ovaries, as well as to bring about systemic inflammation, compromised antioxidant potential, and ovarian degeneration in mice [55].

According to our findings, linoleic acid was more abundant in plasma or serum from women with PCOS, with no such increase in FF. On the contrary, arachidonic acid exhibited an opposite pattern, being elevated in FF but not in circulating specimens, implying preferential transportation and enrichment of arachidonic acid within the follicular milieu. Consistently, a lipidomic analysis indicated an increment in FF levels of arachidonic acid in PCOS cases, which was even more pronounced in those with insulin resistance [56]. It has been found that exposure to high concentrations of arachidonic acid gives rise to oxidative stress, secretory impairment, and mitochondrial dysfunction in ovarian granulosa cells, resulting in their apoptosis [35]. Furthermore, arachidonic acid excess may lead to the overproduction of inflammatory lipid mediators, such as prostaglandins (PGs), within ovarian follicles, thereby compromising oocyte developmental competence. Interestingly, it has been evident that PCOS is associated with an elevation of various PGs, including PGE2, PGF2α, and PGJ2, in ovarian tissue and FF, which in turn are negatively correlated with embryo quality [36, 38, 57].

EPA, DPA, and DHA constitute the largest portion of the human body’s n-3 PUFA pool [58], primarily obtained from the diet and, to a lesser extent, biosynthesized from α-linolenic acid [59]. Adipose tissue from women with PCOS has been shown to contain lower amounts of α-linolenic acid, EPA, and total n-3 PUFAs, with no concomitant difference in DHA compared to non-PCOS participants [45]. By contrast, intrafollicular EPA was reported to increase in PCOS patients [7]. Although our results revealed no difference between the PCOS and control groups with respect to overall values of any n-3 PUFAs, women with PCOS demonstrated a significant reduction in circulating DHA and, conversely, a trend toward elevation in FF DHA. Moreover, we noted that levels of EPA and DPA decreased, and those of DHA tended to decline in the phospholipid fraction of PCOS cases. In partial agreement, attenuation in EPA- and DHA-containing lipid classes, such as ether lipids and plasmalogens, was found to be associated with exacerbated insulin resistance and androgen excess [60, 61], as well as with greater susceptibility to pregnancy loss [62] in women with PCOS. The findings of numerous meta-analyses have also indicated that the supplementation of PCOS patients with long-chain n-3 PUFAs notably improved metabolic profile and inflammation status in the affected women, as evidenced by a decrease in insulin resistance, triglycerides, total cholesterol, and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, along with an increase in adiponectin [63–65]. These observations, in some sense, support our results that systemic depletion of elongated n-3 PUFAs is implicated in the pathophysiology of PCOS.

The observed alterations in FA composition across body fluids in women with PCOS may have meaningful translational implications for both diagnostics and therapeutics. From the diagnostic viewpoint, these findings support the concept of FA signatures as complementary metabolic biomarkers for PCOS, potentially improving phenotypic classification beyond current clinical and endocrine criteria. Therapeutically, our results provide a mechanistic rationale for targeted dietary or pharmacological modulation of FA metabolism in PCOS patients. Moreover, FA profiling in blood-derived samples or FF could serve as a tool to monitor treatment response or optimize assisted reproductive strategies.

The current study possesses some notable strengths. A comprehensive search strategy was implemented across four mainstream databases, ensuring broad coverage of peer-reviewed literature. The review adhered to PRISMA guidelines, and study selection, data extraction, and risk of bias assessment were performed independently by two reviewers to minimize potential bias. Certainty of evidence across outcomes was evaluated using the well-established GRADE approach. In addition, robust statistical methods were applied, including random effects meta-analysis, sensitivity analyses, and subgroup analyses, as well as assessments for publication bias using funnel plots, Begg’s test, and Egger’s test. To the best of our knowledge, the current study is the first to thoroughly synthesize available data on FA composition in women with PCOS and likely includes the largest sample sizes for each corresponding outcome in this population.

This systematic review and meta-analysis also has limitations that merit consideration. First of all, the number of studies reporting each of the individual FAs in PCOS patients was low and, specifically, fewer than 10, which not only lowered the statistical power of the meta-analyses but also precluded conducting meta-regression analyses and conclusively assessing publication bias. Secondly, due to most included articles being from China, the ethnic and geographical diversity of the study subjects was low, restricting the generalizability of the pooled estimates to the broader population of women with PCOS. Furthermore, a substantial proportion of the study participants were women undergoing ovarian hyperstimulation, which may further limit the applicability of the findings to the general PCOS population not receiving gonadotropins. Thirdly, not all studies stratified the subjects into subgroups based on PCOS phenotypes, e.g., reproductive, metabolic, and indeterminate PCOS, or crucial confounding factors like BMI, insulin resistance status, diet, physical activity, medication use, etc., presenting an obstacle to the implementation of relevant subgroup analyses and, consequently, obscuring the effect of these covariates on the overall effect sizes and respective heterogeneity values. Fourthly, significant between-study heterogeneity was observed in the case of some findings, which failed to be explained through subgroup analyses, suggesting that there might be additional unmeasured factors contributing to high variability. Ultimately, gray literature and publications written in languages other than English were excluded from the study, not allowing to refute the possibility of publication bias.

Further methodologically rigorous research is warranted to broaden the understanding of PCOS-induced alterations in FA profile. Achieving this purpose necessitates including larger and more diverse cohorts, establishing the diagnosis of PCOS based on valid assessments, using matched control groups with transparent inclusion and exclusion criteria, and quantifying a full spectrum of FAs in different body compartments ranging from adipose tissue to follicles. Moreover, key confounding factors, including dietary intake, BMI, genetic background, insulin resistance, inflammation markers, menstrual status, physical activity, and medication use, need to be systematically assessed and controlled for. Given the metabolic complexity of PCOS, future investigations would benefit from an integrative approach combining biochemical assays, nutritional evaluations, and metabolomic tools to explore causal relationships and identify potential biomarkers or therapeutic targets. Longitudinal studies tracking FA changes in relation to PCOS phenotypes and treatment responses are particularly needed to inform clinical practice and personalized dietary or pharmacological interventions.

Conclusion

The results of this meta-analysis suggest that FA composition may undergo potential alterations in women with PCOS, with patterns indicating higher levels of key MUFAs and n-6 PUFAs, including palmitoleic acid and linoleic acid in particular, but also oleic acid and arachidonic acid, although the findings regarding the latter two FAs should be approached with caution due to variable stability depending on study inclusion. In addition, specific variation patterns emerged across body fluids in the PCOS group, with increased linoleic acid and decreased DHA in plasma or serum, alongside intrafollicular enrichment of oleic acid and arachidonic acid. Differences in terms of FA profile were also evident among lipid fractions, among which a reduction in the EPA and DPA content of phospholipids from PCOS patients was prominent.

Altogether, these results support the idea of dysregulated FA metabolism in PCOS, especially characterized by elevated pro-inflammatory lipid species and attenuated anti-inflammatory n-3 PUFAs. Such alterations may have implications for metabolic and reproductive dysfunctions observed in PCOS and can pave the way for developing novel biomarkers and targeted therapeutic interventions. Future research should focus on clarifying the causal relationships and clinical relevance of these FA disturbances, taking into account biological sample type and lipid subclass.
