Diet change reveals asymmetric response in gene expression and microbial composition across the digestive tract of two closely related herbivores
Authors: Danny P Nielsen, Matthew L Holding, Robert E del Carlo, Kathryn M Everson, Kaitlin Ochsenrider, W Brian Simison, Jim Henderson, M Denise Dearing, Jack P Hayes, Steven A Frese, Lora A Richards, Bradley S Ferguson, Jennifer S Forbey, Marjorie D Matocq
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding what shapes variation in organisms’ capacity to utilize novel resources is essential to predicting how species will respond to environmental change. For herbivores, exposure to toxic phytochemicals in novel plants may limit persistence in new habitats. We investigated the behavioral, physiological, genetic, and microbial consequences of diet switching in two closely related species of rodent herbivores that each consume differentially toxic plants in their native habitat, and that maintain different dietary strategies (i.e., relative dietary specialist versus relative generalist).ResultsIn reciprocal laboratory feeding trials, we exposed wild-caught woodrats (genus Neotoma) to toxins characteristic of either familiar or novel plant secondary compounds. We measured changes in food and water intake, locomotor activity, gut microbial composition, and gene expression across the digestive tract following feeding trials. The dietary generalist responded minimally, but the specialist responded strongly when exposed to the novel diet. This response included behavioral and genetic components including increased water intake, reduction in locomotor activity, increased differential expression of detoxification genes, and a greater shift in gut microbial composition.ConclusionsThe dietary specialist exhibited a strong response to diet switching that corresponded with ecologically relevant shifts in behavior and physiology that would have negative fitness consequences. Although the dietary specialist had a strong genetic and microbial response to novel plant secondary compounds, this response would likely be insufficient to overcome the immediate challenge of exposure to novel dietary toxins in the wild. Our results underscore the link between feeding strategy and the capacity to shift to novel dietary resources in response to environmental change.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12915-026-02658-9.Keywords: RNAseq, Detoxification, Microbiome, Neotoma, Herbivory

Main text

Background

Organisms respond to environmental change through distributional shifts, behavioral changes, or in situ adaptation, often requiring a shift in resource use [1, 2]. Species’ success in exploiting new resources as ranges shift is likely constrained by both genotypic and phenotypic mechanisms, as well as the degree of individual variation and plasticity in these responses [3, 4]. For herbivores, persistence in novel habitats may be limited by their ability to maximize intake of available food plants while minimizing exposure to novel phytochemicals [5, 6]. Mammalian herbivores have several strategies for minimizing their exposure to toxic phytochemicals. Behavioral responses such as diet mixing or switching [7, 8] can support range shifts if plant composition in the new habitat is diverse or includes plant species that have limited toxicity. When avoidance of toxic phytochemicals is not an option, some mammalian herbivores have a remarkable ability to biotransform toxic phytochemicals through the expression of detoxification enzymes in the host and from their associated gut microbiome [9, 10]. These detoxification enzymes regulate absorption, metabolism, distribution, and excretion of phytochemicals to increase intake of toxic plants [11]. While our empirical understanding of the role of these mechanisms in non-model species is limited, we are now able to interrogate the integrated response of behavior, physiology, host gene expression, and gut microbiome composition in response to dietary challenges faced by wild populations. Understanding how species’ traits interact with changing biotic and abiotic conditions is central to predicting organismal response to climate change [12, 13].

Detoxification can occur via the combined activity of host enzymes and the gut microbiome throughout the digestive tract and associated organs. The pre-gastric stomach (hereafter referred to as foregut) in some animals is morphologically and physiologically distinct. The foregut hosts a diverse microbial community and is the first major site of microbial and host enzymatic activity [14, 15]. After passing through the foregut, the absorption of nutrients, toxic phytochemicals and their metabolites, and water from food occurs in the small intestine. Here, the absorption of toxic phytochemicals is regulated by host proteins [16] such as efflux transporters belonging to the ATP-binding cassette (Abc) family [17]. The transport of digested and metabolized nutritional and toxic phytochemicals continues across the membranes of the hindgut. Herbivorous rodents have a specialized, enlarged caecum, which hosts a large community of microbes that primarily contribute to cellulose fermentation [14] and provides another potential site for interactions between microbial and host enzymatic interactions with phytochemicals. Outside the digestive tract, the liver is a primary site of detoxification of absorbed toxins through the enzymatic activity of oxidation (phase I via cytochrome P450 (Cyp) genes), conjugation (phase II via glutathione S-transferases (Gst), UDP-glucuronosyltransferases (Ugt), and sulfotransferases (Sult) enzymes), and transporter genes (phase III such as ATP-binding cassette transporters (Abc)) [17]. In addition, the transport of toxins and their metabolites across the lumen is regulated by Abc genes in the liver. Beyond studies in model organisms, we lack a broad understanding of the inducibility of detoxification mechanisms in hosts and microbes across the digestive tract relative to the liver in response to natural dietary challenges faced by wild herbivores.

Woodrats in the genus Neotoma have served as a model system for understanding the role of host gene expression and the gut microbiome in facilitating consumption of phytochemically well-defended plants [9, 15, 18, 19]. Members of this genus consume a variety of potentially toxic plants including Quercus spp. [20] (oak), Larrea tridentata [9] (creosote bush), Juniperus spp. [21] (juniper) and Opuntia spp. [22] (cactus). Previous studies have described the role of cytochrome P450s in dietary specialization in Neotoma [11, 12], and have characterized the expression of a variety of biotransformation enzymes in the liver when exposed to potentially toxic phytochemicals [23]. However, in many traditional model systems (i.e., mouse and human), extrahepatic expression of these detoxification enzymes is known to occur along the entire digestive tract [24–26]. Yet to date, little is known about the expression of these detoxification genes throughout the gut of wild mammalian herbivores. In addition to their own detoxification enzymes, woodrats also rely on their gut microbiome for nutrient acquisition and detoxification [9, 22, 27]. Unlike many traditional model rodent systems, woodrats have two sites in their digestive tract that harbor microbes: a pre-gastric stomach (foregut) and an enlarged caecum in the hindgut, both of which are considered to be adaptations to metabolizing challenging herbivore diets [28]. A comprehensive view of detoxification capacity requires examination of both gene expression and microbial community composition at various locations along the digestive tract.

Here, we investigated the ability of two species of woodrats to respond to novel plant secondary compounds during an experimental diet shift. The desert woodrat (Neotoma lepida) and Bryant’s woodrat (N. bryanti) are closely related species that come into contact and hybridize at a sharp habitat boundary in the western Mojave Desert [29, 30] (Fig. 1). Across their respective ranges, both species are considered dietary generalists, but individual populations exhibit a range of specialist to generalist feeding strategies [31]. In this study area, N. lepida exhibits a relatively specialized dietary strategy with a diet dominated by Prunus fasciculata (containing cyanogenic glycosides) in summer (~ 65%) and even in spring (~ 54%) when more plant diversity is available [32–34]. Neotoma bryanti in this area exhibits a more generalist feeding strategy by consuming a variety of plants, with their most common diet plant, Frangula californica (containing anthraquinones), making up 35% of the summer diet and 22% of the spring diet [34]. Each of these plants is largely co-distributed with each woodrat species on either side of the sharp habitat transition separating them (Fig. 1), yet given a choice between both plants in experimental trials, each woodrat species prefers their natively consumed plant (i.e., N. bryanti- Frangula and N. lepida- Prunus) [32, 34]. Experimentally switching the diet of each species from their native (preferred, familiar) to the novel (non-preferred, unfamiliar) diet allows us to explore the potential consequences of dispersal across the habitat boundary, which in this system would facilitate hybridization. We hypothesized that the relative specialist (N. lepida) would exhibit a stronger response to diet switching than the relative generalist (N. bryanti), and that the specialist would exhibit more immediate negative consequences. To evaluate this hypothesis, we quantified multiple axes of response to exposure to novel phytochemicals including changes in food and water intake, voluntary running, resting metabolic rate, and gene expression and microbial community composition across the gut. Our work comprehensively assesses gene expression and microbiome composition across the gut of these small mammals, helping to link feeding strategy to the capacity to shift to novel dietary resources.

Results

Diet switching elicits asymmetric behavioral responses

We quantified changes in several ecologically relevant behavioral responses during feeding trials with the relative specialist (N. lepida) on their preferred diet (Prunus, n = 7; hereafter specialist-P) and non-preferred diet (Frangula, n = 7; hereafter specialist-NP) and compared their response to that of the relative generalist (N. bryanti) on their preferred diet (Frangula, n = 7; hereafter generalist-P) and non-preferred diet (Prunus, n = 8; hereafter generalist-NP; Fig. 1, Additional file 1: Table S1) [35]. Consistent with the hypothesis that dietary specialization reduces the initial capacity to shift diet, we found that the specialist-NP group exhibited some of the strongest changes in behavioral responses to diet switching. The specialist-NP was the only group to significantly increase water intake (n = 7, emmean = 0.195, SE = 0.023, z ratio = 8.320, p < 0.0001; Fig. 1B). For water intake, we identified significant effects of diet (p < 0.001), species × diet interaction (p < 0.001), and diet × sex interaction whereby males increased water intake less than females (p < 0.0001; Additional File 1: Table S2). Minutes per day of wheel running decreased in both the specialist-P (n = 7, emmean = − 0.725, SE = 0.289, z ratio = − 2.513, p = 0.01), and the specialist-NP groups (n = 7, emmean = − 1.145, SE = 0.290, z ratio = − 3.943, P < 0.0001; Fig. 1B), and we found significant effects of species (p = 0.013) and sex (p = 0.026; Additional File 1: Table S2). Daily food consumption increased in both the specialist-P (n = 7, emmean = 0.016, SE = 0.007, z ratio = 2.288, p = 0.022) and generalist-NP groups (n = 8, emmean = 0.015, SE = 0.007, z ratio = 2.194, p = 0.028; Fig. 1B), and there were significant effects of diet (p = 0.041), sex (p = 0.050), and species × sex interaction (p = 0.007; Additional File 1: Table S2). We did not detect significant differences in other behavioral responses or in resting metabolic rate at the end of the trial (Additional file 1: Figs. S1–S3 and Table S2). On average, the specialist-NP group could only sustain themselves on an average dose of less than 60% phytochemical extract before sharply increasing water intake (emmean = 56.3, SE = 6.10, z ratio = − 7.163, p < 0.0001; Fig. 1B).

Detoxification genes are expressed in the liver and throughout the gut

To understand where key detoxification genes are expressed, we measured gene expression in the liver and across multiple locations of the digestive tract (foregut, small intestine, and caecum, Figs. 2 and 3). Gene expression was most strongly associated with gut compartment, regardless of species or diet treatment (Fig. 2B). Oxidation enzymes (cytochrome P450s and flavin-containing monooxygenases (Fmo)) generally had higher expression across all treatments than conjugation or transporter genes (Fig. 2C). Of the eight cytochrome P450 subfamilies classified as evolutionary labile due to frequent tandem gene duplication and associated copy number variation [36] (Cyps 2a, 2b, 2c, 2d, 2j, 3a, 4[abx], and 4F), Cyp2a and Cyp3a were highly expressed in the liver, while the other six subfamilies were expressed more evenly across all tissues and locations except the foregut (Fig. 2C; Additional file 2: Figs. Asymmetric gene expression between relative specialist and generalist

We used principal components analysis to visualize variation in woodrat gene expression across the gut and liver and among treatment groups. Gene expression in the foregut was the most similar among treatment groups while the small intestine and liver exhibited strong differences between species (Fig. 3A). However, when on the same diet treatment (i.e., Frangula), the specialist-NP group had elevated expression of Cyps 2a and 2d in the liver relative to the generalist-P group (Fig. 2C), but lower expression of Cyps (2b, 2c, 3a) as well as Gst and Ugt2b.

Caecum gene expression exhibited the most striking differentiation among treatment groups, where the first principal component separated the composition of genes expressed in the caecum in the specialist-NP group from all other treatment groups. This differentiation was associated with upregulation of a sulfotransferase (Sult2b1-1), as well as the genes Apoa-IV and Fads3 (Fig. 3A). We also detected over 900 differentially expressed genes in the caecum of the specialist, thirty of which were detoxification related (Fig. 4A). Enrichment analysis revealed that these genes could be classified into 217 overrepresented functional terms and 20 underrepresented terms, including those related to xenobiotic transport and immune function (Additional file 3: Table 1). We then further reduced the number of functional terms using GO Slim functional groupings and retained 35 GO Slim categories (Additional file 3: Table 2)—the top two categories were response to stimulus (51 genes) and immune system process (44 genes).

We next evaluated expression of genes that are known to be related to detoxification. Of the thirty differentially expressed detoxification genes in the caecum of the specialist (Fig. 4B; Additional file 4), five were upregulated (i.e., more highly expressed on the non-preferred diet) and are classified as either phase I or phase II genes, while the other 25 genes were downregulated and belonged to all three phases of detoxification (Fig. 4A; Additional file 4). In contrast to the specialist, only twenty genes were differentially expressed in the caecum of the generalist, only two of which were detoxification-related, and these were downregulated (Fig. 4A; Additional file 4). We did not detect any differentially abundant genes in the liver of either species, and only three non-detoxification genes were downregulated in the small intestine of the generalist (Additional file 4).

Microbiome response differs between relative specialist and generalist

We did not detect differences among treatment groups in microbial richness or Faith’s phylogenetic diversity in either the foregut or caecum (Additional file 1: Fig. S4). We used principal components analysis to summarize variation in microbiome composition across the woodrat gut and across treatment groups. Globally, microbial community composition differed between the foregut and caecum, with more diversity in the former (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). However, microbial composition differed little among treatment groups within the two gut compartments (Fig. 3B).

To test the prediction that the microbiome of the specialist would exhibit a greater response than that of the generalist, we quantified differential abundance of microbes across diet treatments within each species, and the foregut and caecum, independently. We detected one ASV belonging to the Lactobacillus family that was upregulated in the foregut of the specialist, and one ASV belonging to Clostridia_UCG-014 family was downregulated in the foregut of the generalist (Fig. 4B). In the caecum, we detected ten differentially abundant microbial ASVs in the specialist and none in the generalist (Fig. 4B). Among the differentially abundant microbes in the caecum of the specialist were Desulfovibrionaceae, Lactobacillus, Lachnospiraceae, Oscillospiraceae, Clostridia, and Bifidobacteriaeae that increased on the non-preferred diet, and ASVs belonging to Lachnospiraceae and Oscillospiraceae decreased (Fig. 4B).

Discussion

Consistent with our hypothesis, the relative specialist, N. lepida, exhibited greater response to diet switching than the relative generalist, N. bryanti. These differences in response included ecologically relevant behaviors such as increased water consumption and lower voluntary locomotion, as well as changes in gene expression and microbial community composition. The greater magnitude of genetic and microbial response in the specialist demonstrates they have a high capacity for inducible response to dietary change, yet, this strong response was still accompanied by negative consequences in ecologically relevant behaviors.

Asymmetry in behavioral responses to diet switching

The metabolic and energetic consequences of detoxification can indirectly be measured through behavioral shifts in food consumption, water use and voluntary locomotion. We found strong asymmetry between the species in these responses to diet change. Both species were able to consume diets that contained the equivalent of 100% of the phytochemicals contained in the dominant plant they each consume in the wild. The generalist, N. bryanti, was also able to consume a diet containing the 100% equivalent of the unfamiliar Prunus and even consumed more of this novel diet than the one containing its natively consumed plant. In contrast, the relative specialist, N. lepida, could only, on average, consume 60% of the unfamiliar Frangula diet suggesting that this local specialist did not have the capacity to immediately meet the challenge of the novel phytochemicals in this plant. We do note that there was important variation among N. lepida individuals in their capacity to consume the novel diet with two males being able to consume the 100% level diet. However, even these individuals suffered reductions in locomotory ability (see below).

Water demand significantly increased only in the relative specialist, N. lepida, when fed the novel diet. Plant secondary compounds can alter water use by mammalian herbivores and increased water consumption may indicate exposure to toxins with which an individual is not familiar or adapted to detoxify [37]. If increased water intake helps individuals overcome the immediate physiological impact of high toxin load or the diuretic effects of phytochemicals [37, 38], this potential strategy would not be available to woodrats living in arid environments. We also found that female N. lepida exhibited greater increases in water demand than males. These results suggest that females may be more sensitive than males to exposure to novel toxins, which may have particularly important implications for individual fitness and population dynamics in new habitats.

The specialist N. lepida also exhibited reduced voluntary locomotion. This happened on both experimental diets, but the effect size was greatest on the novel diet, which is consistent with previous findings of reduced energy availability associated with ingestion of novel toxins [39]. Reduction in locomotory capacity would have multiple negative consequences in the wild including reduced ability to escape predators, find mates, and disperse. Overall, individuals in the wild experiencing increased water demand, decreased locomotory capacity, or both, would be expected to have lower fitness which could limit population persistence in the face of changing food plant availability.

Tissue-, species-, and diet-specific expression of detoxification genes

The liver is the primary site of detoxification in vertebrates including woodrats [18, 40] and liver gene expression differed greatly between the species studied here. The non-preferred diet induced higher expression of Cyps 2a and 2d, and lower expression of several other detoxification genes (Cyps 2b, 2c, 3a, and Ugt2b) in the liver of the specialist compared to the generalist on the same diet (i.e., its preferred diet). While this may be partly explained by dose effects (i.e., on average, the generalist consumed a higher dose of phytochemicals in the trials), our finding of higher expression of Cyp2a in the specialist is consistent with a recent finding that Cyp2a shows signs of positive selection in creosote feeding N. lepida [19], suggesting this particular cytochrome P450 gene subfamily may play an important role in response to dietary toxins. While the liver is the primary site of detoxification via host expression of detoxification enzymes, we also detected detoxification gene expression across all gut compartments in both species of woodrats. Each species of woodrat exhibited a strong, species-specific signal of gene expression in every gut compartment, which suggests that regardless of diet, these species have evolved distinct detoxification capacity.

Foregut exhibits no host gene response and minimal microbiome response to diet shift

Host gene expression in the foregut suggested a limited response relative to other gut compartments, and we detected no differentially expressed genes between diet treatments in the specialist or generalist. However, metabolism of plant compounds in the foregut may largely be the result of microbial activity rather than host enzymatic activity [15, 41], and this activity will influence the passage of plant phytochemicals into distal portions of the digestive tract. We detected one differentially abundant microbial ASV each in the foregut of the generalist and specialist. The ASV that decreased in abundance in the foregut of the generalist belonged to Clostridia_UCG-14, a genus considered beneficial [42]. In the foregut of N. lepida, a Lactobacillus ASV increased when exposed to the non-preferred diet. Lactobacilli are known to contribute to overall host health [43], and distinct strains have formed symbioses across vertebrate hosts [44]. In woodrats, lactobacilli in the foregut have been shown to degrade oxalates—plant toxins that mammalian enzymes cannot act upon [27]. We hypothesize that dietary perturbations may disrupt this important host-microbe symbiosis, as seen in other taxa [45]. Future work on the role of these lactobacilli in woodrat detoxification would benefit from functional and culturing studies to identify the different strains and better test this hypothesis.

Small intestine exhibits strong species differences in gene expression

Our sampling of the small intestine was confined to the proximal portion of this tissue for characterization of host gene expression, and we did not sample the microbial community, potentially restricting our ability to identify more complex responses within this portion of the GI tract. We found strong species effects in differential gene expression in the small intestine, as well as expression of detoxification genes. In particular, the expression of transporter enzymes in the Abcb subfamily we identified in the small intestine may modulate the absorption of ingested toxins [46]. In addition, expression of Abcb subfamily in the small intestine may play a role in functional interactions between host and gut microbial enzymes via enterohepatic recycling [47]. Enterohepatic recycling involves absorption of chemicals from the intestinal lining into the blood stream that are then transported to the liver, secreted into bile, then cycled back to the intestines [47]. This process can influence the bioavailability of a compound, amplify its effects, and lengthen systemic exposure to toxic compounds [47]. Many enzymes mediate this recycling process, including the Abcb subfamily of ATP-binding cassette transporter genes [48]. Despite these interactions between the intestinal tract and the liver, studies of detoxification gene expression in wild mammalian herbivores have primarily focused on response in the liver [19]. By including gene expression and microbial profiles across the gut, as well as the liver, our study provides novel findings that set a foundation for further study of the integrative nature of detoxification in wild mammalian herbivores.

Caecum exhibits strong host gene response and shifts in microbiome

In addition to being a critical location of water and nutrient absorption, the caecum harbors a rich microbiome which has been implicated in metabolism of phytochemicals as well as regulation of host gene expression [22, 45]. We found high levels of expression of host detoxification genes in the caecum, particularly those involved in phase I and III pathways. Within the caecum of the specialist, we found a striking response with over 900 differentially expressed genes between diet treatments. These genes were related to xenobiotic metabolism, and when further generalized using GO Slim, response to stimulus was the number one category (this parent term includes detoxification and xenobiotic metabolism pathways), in addition to oxidoreductase activity as well as transporter and transferase activity, indicating a coordinated response to ingested toxins. Immune system processes were also highly represented, as well as DNA repair and replication, suggesting a multi-system response to stress and injury after consuming a non-preferred diet plant. Among the genes for which we had a priori predictions for their role in detoxification, thirty were differentially expressed in the caecum. Of these, most phase I genes were downregulated, and most phase II genes were upregulated in the caecum of the relative specialist. Oxidation (phase I) enzymes are more energy efficient than phase II enzymes, are not limited by the availability of endogenous substrates, and may be used more by specialist herbivores than generalists [21, 49]. However, the relative specialist we studied was limited in its ability to express more energy efficient phase I enzymes when challenged with a non-preferred diet—possibly the result of inhibition by anthraquinones in Frangula [50], or the lack of enzymes specifically adapted to the compounds in this unfamiliar diet plant.

The relative specialist also shifted expression of three sulfotransferase (phase II) enzymes that mediate conjugation of toxins making them water-soluble and available for excretion [51]. When the specialist consumed the non-preferred diet, Sult1b1 and Sult2b1 were upregulated, while Sult5a1 was downregulated (Additional file 4). Though not differentially expressed in the caecum between diets, a different member of the Sult family, Sult2a, was highly expressed by the generalist (Fig. 3A). Interestingly, then, both species express Sults when consuming plant toxins, but they deploy different copies of the same gene family, a pattern of differential expression of distinct gene copies that may be linked to neofunctionalization of duplicated detoxification genes and spatial variation in plant toxin resistance [52]. Other genes not often considered to be directly related to detoxification may also play a role in response to dietary changes and may elicit important behavioral response. One gene upregulated in the specialist on the non-preferred diet was an apolipoprotein, Apoa4, which is known to be involved in lipid metabolism and stimulation of satiation [53]. Apoa4 may be involved in a response to dietary toxins by inducing a satiety effect to reduce ingestion of unfamiliar diet items. Our understanding of the potential role of apolipoproteins in toxin metabolism is expanding [54] including discoveries that these loci are involved in response to rattlesnake venom [55] and may be suppressed in the liver in response to certain toxic plants [56]. Overall, many loci involved in metabolism, like the apolipoproteins, may also be involved in detoxification, but studies of natural systems are required to augment our understanding of their ecological role.

Our measure of woodrat caecum gene expression is a combination of direct response to toxins present in the experimental diets but also a response to the metabolic activity of microbial symbionts. The caecal microbiome of the relative specialist, N. lepida, exhibited a stronger response to diet switching than the relative generalist that had no differentially abundant microbes. The caecum of woodrats is a microbially rich environment where bacterial enzymatic activity can produce short-chain fatty acids, metabolize xenobiotic compounds, and regulate host gene expression in both the intestine and liver via enterohepatic cycling [47, 57, 58]. But the impacts of gut microbial activity to host health are complex. The bacterial family Desulfovibrionaceae increased in the caeca of the specialist when on its non-preferred diet. Increases of genera in this sulfate-reducing microbial family have been associated with damage to the intestinal lining, resulting in the absorption of toxic compounds, and additional damage to the liver [59]. The increased abundance of this microbial family may indicate a cost incurred by the relative specialist when consuming the non-preferred diet. However, not all differentially abundant microbes were indicative of negative health impacts. In the caeca of the specialist, we found increased abundance in response to the non-preferred diet in taxa known to produce butyrate [58, 60] (i.e., Lachnospiraceae and Oscillospiraceae), one of several short chain fatty acids (SCFAs) that, among many health benefits, has immunoregulatory and anti-inflammatory properties. Future studies incorporating metagenomic sequencing would help to further elucidate microbial gene expression in response to changes in plant toxins.

Our results suggest that the gut microbial response to changes in dietary plant compounds is complex and that both potentially beneficial and harmful bacterial lineages are involved in this plastic response to diet [61]. Given what is currently known, primarily from model organisms, and the striking patterns we observed in our study, the caecum is a promising location for continued interrogations of host gene–gut microbe interactions [62], especially in wild mammalian herbivores. Future studies could expand on this work by incorporating field-based investigations with both increased experimental times and samples sizes.

Conclusions

For mammalian herbivores, plant phytochemicals are an important source of environmental selection that influences what individuals consume and the habitats they occupy [6, 51]. Neotoma lepida and N. bryanti are estimated to have diverged ~ 1.6 million years ago [29], long enough for both species to have accumulated different ecological adaptations, including those related to diet and interactions with their microbiome. Previous studies of the woodrat microbiome found that composition was influenced more by ancestry than diet, suggesting limitations to the plasticity of the gut microbiome [32, 63]. In our study, Neotoma lepida and N. bryanti had distinct behavioral and physiological responses to diet shifts. However, the magnitude of these responses was asymmetric, with the dietary specialist at our site, N. lepida, exhibiting environmentally unattainable water demands, and decreases in voluntary locomotion in response to toxins from novel phytochemicals. In the wild, the magnitude of dietary change imposed in this experiment would likely not be immediately sustainable for most individual N. lepida in this area. In contrast, the generalist, N. bryanti, exhibited relatively minimal response in behavior, physiology, gene expression and microbial composition to a novel phytotoxin suggesting that these individuals are able to quickly acclimate to this magnitude of dietary change with their standing detoxification capacity. While our experiment only measured short-term response to novel toxins, these results indicate that the specialist would have more difficulty overcoming the initial challenge of establishment in a new habitat than the generalist. Identifying the fitness consequences of these short-term results will require intensive field-studies that follow the fate of individuals in the wild.

Environmental change threatens biodiversity on multiple axes, including changes in resource availability [64]. To persist through these changes, species will be forced to utilize novel habitats and resources [65]. In response to environmental change, generalists have been found to outperform specialists [66–68], although warm-adapted specialists may have an advantage over generalists in some circumstances [69]. This difference in specialist versus generalist strategies in woodrats varies among populations [31] and even extends to the level of the individual [70]. This natural variation among individuals will ultimately determine the capacity of local populations to persist in the face of environmental change.

Methods

Field collection and transport

We collected N. bryanti and N. lepida from populations near to but not at the site of active hybridization between the two species [29] in order to avoid the inclusion of admixed individuals in this study. We selected sites where N. bryanti overlapped with Frangula californica (California coffeeberry) and N. lepida overlapped with Prunus fasciculata (desert almond; S.I. Table S1). We live-trapped woodrats and retained only adult males and non-pregnant/lactating females. Individuals were transported to the University of Nevada, Reno and transferred to individual cages and maintained on ad libitum water and a high fiber rabbit chow (Teklad formula 2031). Temperature in the housing room was kept at 23 °C and lighting was maintained on a 15:9-h light:dark cycle. During experiments, woodrats were fed the same diet in powder form to prevent caching. All animal collections and procedures were reviewed and approved by the University of Nevada Reno Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee, the California Department of Fish and Wildlife, and were consistent with the guidelines developed by the American Society of Mammalogists [71].

Feeding trials and resting metabolic rate

All individuals were acclimated to the lab for a minimum of 3 weeks, including a required quarantine period, prior to feeding trials. During feeding experiments, woodrats were placed in shoebox cages outfitted with a running wheel, food hopper, water bottle, and a wire floor to allow feces and urine to be collected. Food and water consumption and woodrat body mass were measured each day of the trial. Food and water were reapportioned as needed and recorded. We also recorded running wheel activity using custom designed Raspberry Pi monitors and calculated daily max speed (m/s), total time spent running (minutes/day), and total number of rotations per day.

Experimental diets were prepared via methanol extraction of dry leaves of P. fasciculata and F. californica. After soaking overnight, extracts were dried using a Genevac EZ-2 (SP Scientific), followed by overnight high-vacuum to remove residual solvent. For each plant we divided the mass (in grams) of dried extract by the mass of starting material to determine the amount of extract to incorporate into rabbit chow for each desired dosage. Extract of P. fasciculata yielded a mass of 20% of dry plant material, and F. fasciculata yielded 15% mass. Extract was thoroughly mixed with powdered rabbit chow so that woodrats could not parse chow from experimental compounds.

We have previously established that at the hybrid zone N. lepida prefers Prunus fasciculata and N. bryanti prefers Frangula californica, and that these plants are among the most nutritious available at this site [34]. In this study, our aim was to determine the response of individuals to plant toxins (i.e., extract) while holding nutrition constant (i.e., chow). We therefore placed individuals from both species into a preferred (i.e., N. lepida on Prunus or N. bryanti on Frangula) or non-preferred diet treatment (i.e., N. lepida on Frangula or N. bryanti on Prunus). We standardized doses based on the percent of plant compounds in dried material for each plant (see above). Thus, a 100% dose is the maximum amount of extract an animal would be exposed to in the wild if they only consumed that plant. Experimental diets ranged from 0% dose (no plant extract, only lab chow) to 100%. Woodrats were maintained at a particular dose for 2 days to allow liver enzyme induction, and then for 3 days on the maximum dose determined for each treatment group. The first trial diets were adjusted as follows: 0%, 1%, 5%, 10%, then increased in 10% intervals until reaching 100%. We conducted this first trial to determine the tolerance of each treatment group. In subsequent trials, dose was increased in 2-day intervals as follows: 0%, 5%, 10%, 20%, 30%, 50%, 70%, 100%. Individuals were allowed to proceed through the entire trial to 100% dose unless we observed one of three possible early indicators that an individual was having a strong physiological response to toxin exposure and should be removed from the feeding trial for measurement of response to toxin. An early endpoint (prior to 100% dose) was indicated by (1) significant reduction in daily food consumption; (2) greater than 10% loss in body weight; or (3) significant increase in daily water intake. All individuals who were removed from the experimental trials prior to reaching 100% dose were N. lepida on the non-preferred diet and in every case due to dramatic increase in daily water intake part way through the trial. On the last day of an individual’s diet trial, food was removed 4 h prior to metabolic measurements and euthanasia. Resting metabolic rate was measured at 23 °C; each metabolic chamber received dry, filtered air at 800 ml min−1, and control chambers without woodrats were treated identically except they did not contain animals (see Additional file 1 for more details).

Tissue sample collection

Immediately following metabolic measurements, woodrats were euthanized by over inhalation of isoflurane. Tissue from the foregut, small intestine, caecum, and liver were rapidly harvested, rinsed with sterile saline to reduce bacterial contamination, minced on an ice-cooled tray, and immediately placed in a cryovial in liquid nitrogen and then transferred to − 80° C storage. Contents from the foregut and caecum were also collected for microbiome analysis. All contents and tissues were handled with sterile tools, and all tools were changed in between different tissue types and across individuals to minimize risk of cross contamination.

RNA isolation, sequencing, and analysis

To characterize liver gene expression, we isolated RNA from tissue using QIAzol (Qiagen) extraction method following manufacturer protocols, and RNA quality was measured using an Agilent RNA Bioanalyzer for which all samples had RIN scores > 7. We sent liver RNA for library preparation and sequencing to QB3-Berkeley Genomics core labs (Berkeley, CA). rRNA was depleted from samples with a Ribo-Zero Plus rRNA Depletion kit (Illumina 20,037,135). Both total RNA and depleted RNA quality was assessed on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. Libraries were prepared using the KAPA RNA Hyper Prep kit (Roche KK8540). Truncated universal stub adapters were ligated to cDNA fragments, which were then extended via PCR using unique dual indexing primers into full length Illumina adapters. Library quality was checked on an AATI Fragment Analyzer. Library molarity was measured via quantitative PCR with the KAPA Library Quantification Kit (Roche KK4824) on a BioRad CFX Connect thermal cycler. Libraries were then pooled by molarity and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 S4 flowcell for 2 × 150 cycles, targeting at least 25 M reads per sample. Fastq files were generated and demultiplexed using Illumina bcl2fastq2 v2.20 and default settings.

Subsequently, to characterize gene expression from tissues along the digestive tract, we isolated RNA from the foregut, small intestine, and caecum following the steps listed above. We sent 1 µg of RNA to Novogene (Sacramento, CA) for library preparation and sequencing. Libraries were made using NEBNext Ultra Directional RNA library prep kit for Illumina (cat# E7420S, New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, mRNA was enriched using oligo(dT) beads followed by two rounds of purification and fragmented randomly by adding fragmentation buffer. The first strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamers primer, after which a custom second-strand synthesis buffer (Illumina), dNTPs, RNase H and DNA polymerase I were added to generate the second-strand (ds cDNA). After a series of terminal repair, poly-adenylation, and sequencing adaptor ligation, the double-stranded cDNA library was completed following size selection and PCR enrichment. The resulting 250–350 bp insert libraries were quantified using a Qubit 2.0 fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and quantitative PCR. Size distribution was analyzed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA). Qualified libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq Platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA) using a paired-end 150 run (2 × 150 bases). 30 M raw reads were generated from each library.

We used TrimGalore (Babraham Bioinformatics) to trim raw RNAseq reads and remove Illumina adapters. We also removed bases with phred quality scores below 20 and removed any read pairs shorter than 20 bp in length. Prior to alignment, we annotated the genomes of both species using a combination of Augustus [72], Apollo [73], GeneMark [74], and subsequent manual annotation. As mappability bias could affect our ability to compare expression between species, and to obtain the most comprehensive and comparable transcriptome dataset, we aligned all reads to the genomes available for both species. We aligned trimmed and quality filtered RNAseq reads to the contig-level N. bryanti genome [75] using HISAT2 [76] and converted alignments to sorted and indexed bam files using samtools [77]. We used the un-conc option in HISAT2 to save reads that did not align to the N. bryanti reference genome and then aligned these reads to the N. lepida contig-level genome [75]. Alignments and genome annotation files for each species were then used to count reads mapped to exon features using HTSeq v.2.0.1 [78].

16S sequencing and analysis

Immediately after euthanasia, the foregut and caecum of each woodrat was removed, and contents were sampled with either a sterile spatula or cotton swab, placed in a cryovial, and flash frozen in liquid nitrogen. Samples were stored at – 80 °C until being shipped on ice to Jonah Ventures (Boulder, CO). DNA was extracted from ~ 0.25 g per sample using the DNeasy Powersoil HTP 96 kit. Part of the mitochondrial 16S rRNA gene was amplified from each DNA sample using the following primers: 515 F GTGYCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA, 806R GGACTACNVGGGTWTCTAAT [79]. Sequences were demultiplexed using Golay barcodes in Qiime v1.9.1 [80]. Pooled libraries were sequenced on Illumina MiSeq (San Diego, CA).

Demultiplexed 16S reads (fastq) were processed using QIIME2 version 2021.8.0 [81]. We removed the forward and reverse primers using cutadapt [82]. Sequences were denoised using DADA2 [83], with forward and reverse reads truncated at 231 and 230, respectively. We used the Naive Bayes classifier to assign taxonomy using the Greengenes database [84] version 13_8 and specific to 515F/806R primers used. To estimate Faith’s phylogenetic diversity, we constructed a phylogeny by first aligning sequences with the alignment mafft command, then alignment mask, and finally phylogeny fasttree. The phylogeny was rooted using the midpoint-root command.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted in R version 4.2.0 [85]. We first averaged the percent dose at toxin response (Fig. 1B) for each treatment group and performed analysis of variance using the aov function in R to test for difference among treatment groups.

We measured body mass, and quantified mass-adjusted during-trial change in water and food intake, and wheel running, to test whether individuals showed significant increases or decreases in these measures between the initial 0% dose and the maximum dose by the end of an individual’s trial. We performed generalized linear mixed models in the glmmTMB [86] package to test the three-way interactive effects of species × diet × sex on change between the beginning (0% dose) and end (max dose) of trials for the following behavioral responses: daily water intake, daily food intake, daily time spend running on wheel (in minutes), daily max speed (meters/second), and daily total wheel rotations. We also included experimental trial number as a random effect. We then used emmmeans package to test the null hypothesis that the change in value for each variable, and within each of the four treatment groups, was equal to 0 (i.e., did not change). We performed these same analyses to test for differences among treatment groups in maximum dose, and for emmeans tested the null hypothesis that the value was equal to 100. We visualized these results with boxplots using ggplot2 [87]. We also tested for differences in metabolic rate using analysis of covariance on log-transformed metabolic rate and adjusted for log body mass.

Gene expression

We first explored gene expression profiles across treatment groups for all manually annotated detoxification gene subfamilies by combining N. bryanti and N. lepida aligned counts across subfamilies (i.e., all Cyp2b copies). Resulting composite counts were then plotted to show the total read counts of gene subfamilies across species, diet treatments, and tissues.

As patterns were generally consistent between both N. bryanti and N. lepida aligned data, we used only N. bryanti-aligned reads for the remainder of the analyses. This also allowed for differentiation in gene variants expressed among treatment groups. HTseq count data from the foregut, small intestine, caecum, and liver were imported into the DESeq2 package [88].

All liver samples were sequenced at one time, and all other gut compartment samples were sequenced subsequently, but gene expression within each gut compartment was analyzed separately to identify differential expression among treatment groups. To visualize patterns of gene expression across the four treatment groups and within each tissue type, we plotted square-root transformed counts of gene subfamilies (i.e., Cyp2a, Cyp2b). We then performed a variance stabilizing transformation with the vst function to estimate dispersion and performed a principal components analysis on the resulting data using the prcomp function with scale and center set to true. For each tissue, we conducted a principle components analysis (PCA) and plotted the first two axes using the fviz_pca_biplot function in the factoextra [89] package. We used limma voom (package limma [90]) to identify differentially expressed genes between diet treatments within each tissue or gut compartment and for each species separately, and included sex and trial as effects. We treated the preferred diet for each species as the reference condition and the non-preferred diet as the treatment. We used an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and log2 fold change of > 2 as a threshold for detecting differentially expressed genes and plotted these results as a volcano plot using ggplot2.

To understand the function of the over 900 genes differentially expressed in the caecum of N. lepida on the non-preferred diet, we performed enrichment analysis using OmicsBox version 3.4.5. The coding sequence fasta for the N. bryanti genome was translated into a protein sequence fasta file using the transeq function of EMBOSS version 6.6.0.0 [91]. We then performed BLASTP and InterProScan on the subsequent translated sequences. The results were then imported into OmicsBox and GO mapping and GO annotation were performed, followed by enrichment analysis using Fisher’s exact test, and using the 900 DE genes as the test set. We included Biological Process, Molecular Function, and Cellular Component GO categories. The subsequent table of terms was then reduced to the most specific terms using an FDR threshold of 0.01. To further categorize these functional GO terms into fewer, and more general terms, we converted the terms into GOSlim categories using the goSlim function of the R package GSEABase [92] version 1.64, and using the mouse GOSLim subset.

Microbial composition

We imported QIIME2 artifacts including the feature count table, phylogenetic tree, taxonomy table, and sample metadata into R as a phyloseq object using the qza_to_phyloseq function of the qiime2R package [93]. ASVs representing mitochondria or chloroplast were filtered out and we removed singleton ASVs. We estimated Faith’s phylogenetic diversity and calculated microbial richness in both the foregut and caecum using the estimate_pd function of the btools package [94]. We plotted diversity using ggplot2 and performed Wilcox tests for differences between diet treatments within N. lepida and N. bryanti using the geom_signif function in the ggpubr package [95].

To remain consistent with our gene expression analysis, we used principal components analysis (PCA) to characterize differences in microbial composition in the foregut and caecum between N. lepida and N. bryanti and between P. fasciculata and F. californica diet treatments. We agglomerated the data to the family level using the tax_glom function of phyloseq. We then performed PCA followed by variance stabilizing transformation to transform read counts and plotted the results using the fviz_pca_biplot function as with gene counts (see above).

We used limma voom to identify microbial ASVs that were differentially abundant between diet treatments within N. lepida and N. bryanti individually and used sex and trial as covariates (see above). As with differential gene expression, we treated the preferred diet for each species as the reference condition and the non-preferred diet as the treatment. An adjusted p-value of < 0.05 and log2 fold change of > 2 was used to define ASVs that were significantly differentially abundant between treatments, and results were visualized as a volcano plot created using ggplot2.
