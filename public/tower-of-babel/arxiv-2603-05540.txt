Attention Meets Reachability: Structural Equivalence and Efficiency in Grammar-Constrained LLM Decoding
Faruk Alpay Department of Computer Engineering, Bahçeşehir University, Istanbul, Turkey faruk.alpay@bahcesehir.edu.tr Bilge Senturk Department of Industrial Engineering, Bahçeşehir University, Istanbul, Turkey bilge.senturk@bahcesehir.edu.tr

Abstract
Grammar-constrained decoding (GCD) enforces that an autoregressive language model generates outputs within a formal language specified by a context-free grammar (CFG). We formalize GCD as a coupling between a Transformer-style next-token distribution and a reachability oracle over a pushdown system compiled from a CFG, and develop a unified theoretical framework addressing both the complexity structure of the constraint engine and the probabilistic fidelity of the constrained generator. We prove an oracle invariance theorem: language-equivalent grammars induce identical next-token admissibility sets for every prefix (hence identical logit masks), yet can yield provably different compiled state spaces and online ambiguity costs. We give exact state-count blowups for the canonical a n ​ b n a^{n}b^{n} language under redundant nonterminal delegation, and introduce a left-to-right structural ambiguity cost (SAC) that measures incremental packed-parse-forest growth per token. For two equivalent Σ ∗ \Sigma^{*} grammars, we prove that SAC is O ⁡ ( 1 ) O(1) per token under right-recursion but Θ ⁡ ( t 2 ) \Theta(t^{2}) per token (and Θ ⁡ ( n 3 ) \Theta(n^{3}) cumulatively) under concatenation, via tight bounds on packed parse forest density. Beyond asymptotic complexity, we establish three further theoretical contributions. First, we elevate SAC into engine-independent lower bounds : any sound, retrieval-efficient, parse-preserving online masking engine must incur Ω ⁡ ( t 2 ) \Omega(t^{2}) work per token (and Ω ⁡ ( n 3 ) \Omega(n^{3}) cumulatively) on a specific constant-size CFG family; this parallels classical complexity results connecting CFG parsing to matrix multiplication [ 34 , 18 ] . Second, we define decoding-cost equivalence classes of grammars and prove the existence of minimal-SAC representatives within bounded rewrite families, clarifying what canonical low-SAC normal forms can and cannot mean. Third, we formalize grammar-conditioned autoregressive processes : the true conditional sampler p ( ⋅ ∣ τ ( y ) ∈ L ) p(\cdot\mid\tau(y)\in L) is characterized by a Doob h h -transform, and we prove sharp distortion bounds for hard-masked decoding in terms of survival-probability spread among admissible next tokens. Finally, we integrate these results with Transformer and Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architectures, derive decoding-time latency envelopes in terms of vocabulary size, active state sets, and beam width, and extend SAC into a measurement-calibrated predictive performance model that supports empirical validation and automated grammar optimization.

Introduction

Autoregressive large language models (LLMs) define distributions of the form

p_{\theta}(y_{1:T})=\prod_{t=1}^{T}p_{\theta}(y_{t}\mid y_{<t}),

(1)

where
y_{t}\in\mathcal{V}
is a vocabulary token and
\theta
are model parameters.
In many deployments, however, the desired output is not arbitrary text but a structured
artifact (e.g., SQL, JSON, or a programming language fragment).
Grammar-constrained decoding (GCD) addresses this by restricting generation to tokens
that keep the current prefix
completable
under a formal language specification,
typically a CFG, rejecting inadmissible tokens on the fly

[
28
,
2
]
.
The same conceptual interface underlies popular open-source toolchains for structured
generation
[

,
13
,
8

36
,
15

.

This paper addresses a tension at the core of GCD: language equivalence is semantic
(two grammars that generate the same strings are interchangeable from the user’s
perspective), but constrained decoding executes a
specific
compiled recognizer
whose runtime depends on grammar structure.
Our central claim is that even when two CFGs generate exactly the same language,
they can induce radically different internal search spaces for a left-to-right decoding
engine.
This discrepancy is not semantic; it is purely structural, and it manifests through
pushdown reachability.

2
Decoding as Pushdown Reachability

We fix (i) a CFG over an abstract terminal alphabet, (ii) a tokenizer mapping from
model tokens to terminal strings, and (iii) an explicit compilation of the CFG to a
pushdown automaton whose reachable configuration set determines admissible next tokens.

2.1
Languages, prefixes, and tokenization

Definition 1

(CFG)
.

A context-free grammar is a tuple
G=(N,\Sigma,P,S)
where
N
is a finite set of
nonterminals,
\Sigma
is a finite set of terminals,
P\subseteq N\times(N\cup\Sigma)^{*}
is a finite set of productions written
A\to\alpha
, and
S\in N
is the
start symbol.
The language generated by
G
is denoted
\mathcal{L}(G)\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
.

Definition 2

(Prefix closure and one-step extension)
.

For any language
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
, define its prefix closure

\operatorname{Pref}(L)\coloneqq\{u\in\Sigma^{*}\;:\;\exists v\in\Sigma^{*}\text{ such that }uv\in L\}.

Define the
next-terminal set
after prefix
u
as

\operatorname{Next}_{L}(u)\coloneqq\{a\in\Sigma\;:\;ua\in\operatorname{Pref}(L)\}.

Define the end signal
\langle\mathrm{eos}\rangle
as admissible after
u
iff
u\in L
.

Definition 3

(Tokenizer homomorphism)
.

Let
\mathcal{V}
be the LLM vocabulary.
A tokenizer homomorphism is a map
\tau:\mathcal{V}\to\Sigma^{*}
.
For a token sequence
y_{1:T}\in\mathcal{V}^{T}
, define its realized terminal string as

\tau(y_{1:T})\coloneqq\tau(y_{1})\tau(y_{2})\cdots\tau(y_{T})\in\Sigma^{*},

where juxtaposition denotes concatenation in
\Sigma^{*}
.
We set
\tau(\langle\mathrm{eos}\rangle)=\varepsilon
.

The constraint “the model output is in
\mathcal{L}(G)
” is interpreted as

\tau(y_{1:T})\in\mathcal{L}(G)
.

2.2
Pushdown automata and CFG compilation

Definition 4

(Nondeterministic PDA)
.

A nondeterministic pushdown automaton (NPDA) is a tuple

\mathcal{A}=(Q,\Sigma,\Gamma,\delta,q_{0},\bot,F),

where
Q
is a finite set of control states,
\Gamma
is a finite stack alphabet with
bottom marker
\bot
,
q_{0}\in Q
is the initial state,
F\subseteq Q
is the set of
accepting states, and

\delta:Q\times(\Sigma\cup\{\varepsilon\})\times\Gamma\to\mathcal{P}(Q\times\Gamma^{*})

is the transition function.
A configuration is a triple
(q,u,\gamma)
where
q\in Q
,
u\in\Sigma^{*}
is the
unread input, and
\gamma\in\Gamma^{*}
is the stack contents with
\bot
at the bottom.
Acceptance is by final state with empty input and stack exactly
\bot
.

Definition 5

(Recursive-transition-network compilation)
.

Given
G=(N,\Sigma,P,S)
, define
\mathcal{A}_{G}
as follows.
For each nonterminal
A\in N
, create control states
q_{A}^{\mathrm{in}}
and

q_{A}^{\mathrm{out}}
.
For each production
p=(A\to X_{1}\cdots X_{m})\in P
and each dot position

i\in\{0,1,\dots,m\}
, create a control state
q_{p,i}
.
Let

Q\coloneqq\{q_{\mathrm{start}}\}\cup\{q_{A}^{\mathrm{in}},q_{A}^{\mathrm{out}}:A\in N\}\cup\{q_{p,i}:p\in P,\,i\}.

Let the stack alphabet be
\Gamma\coloneqq\{\bot\}\cup\{q_{p,i}:p\in P,\,i\}
.
Define transitions:

•

Start:
\delta(q_{\mathrm{start}},\varepsilon,\bot)\ni(q_{S}^{\mathrm{in}},\bot)
.

•

Choice of production: for each production
p=(A\to\cdots)
,

\delta(q_{A}^{\mathrm{in}},\varepsilon,\gamma)\ni(q_{p,0},\gamma)
for all
\gamma\in\Gamma
.

•

Exit:
\delta(q_{p,m},\varepsilon,\gamma)\ni(q_{A}^{\mathrm{out}},\gamma)
.

•

Terminals: if
X_{i}\in\Sigma
, then

\delta(q_{p,i-1},X_{i},\gamma)\ni(q_{p,i},\gamma)
.

•

Nonterminal calls: if
X_{i}=B\in N
, then

\delta(q_{p,i-1},\varepsilon,\gamma)\ni(q_{B}^{\mathrm{in}},q_{p,i}\gamma)
and, for each
return address
r=q_{p,i}
,
\delta(q_{B}^{\mathrm{out}},\varepsilon,r)\ni(r,\varepsilon)
.

Set
q_{0}=q_{\mathrm{start}}
and
F=\{q_{S}^{\mathrm{out}}\}
.
We call
Q
the
engine control-state space
.

Proposition 1

(Correctness of compilation)
.

For every CFG
G
, the compiled NPDA
\mathcal{A}_{G}
accepts exactly
\mathcal{L}(G)
.

Proof.

Each nonterminal invocation pushes a return address and transitions into the callee’s
entry state; completing a nonterminal pops the return address and resumes at the
caller’s dot-position state.
Terminal transitions consume exactly the required terminal; nondeterminism models
production choice.
An accepting run corresponds to a leftmost derivation tree and vice versa.
A detailed proof follows by adapting the classic CFG
\to
PDA conversion in

[
16
, Ch. 6]
to the dot-position control-state
representation.
∎

2.3
Engine semantics for left-to-right masking

Definition 6

(Reachability and liveness)
.

Fix a compiled NPDA
\mathcal{A}_{G}
.
For a terminal prefix
u\in\Sigma^{*}
, define
\operatorname{Reach}_{G}(u)
as the set of
configurations reachable from the initial configuration after consuming exactly
u
,
allowing any number of
\varepsilon
-moves before, between, and after terminal moves.
A configuration
c
is
live
if there exists some
v\in\Sigma^{*}
such that

c
reaches an accepting configuration after consuming
v
.
Let
\operatorname{CoReach}_{G}(u)\subseteq\operatorname{Reach}_{G}(u)
be the subset of reachable configurations
that are live.

Definition 7

(Admissible next vocabulary tokens)
.

Given prefix
u\in\Sigma^{*}
, define the admissible token set

\Omega_{G}(u)\coloneqq\{v\in\mathcal{V}\;:\;\operatorname{CoReach}_{G}(u\,\tau(v))\neq\emptyset\}.

Remark 1

(Reachability as the computational bottleneck)
.

Computing
\operatorname{CoReach}_{G}
is a reachability question in a pushdown system, tightly
connected to CFL reachability and interprocedural analysis

[
27
,
1
,
4
]
.
Modern GCD implementations reduce this cost via offline preprocessing, memoization,
deterministic pushdown compilation, and careful tokenizer alignment

2
,
23
,
6
,
33
,

]

3
Structural Equivalence, Masked Decoding, and Probability Constraints

3.1
Oracle invariance under language equivalence

Definition 8

(Language equivalence)
.

Two CFGs
G
and
G^{\prime}
are (strongly) equivalent if
\mathcal{L}(G)=\mathcal{L}(G^{\prime})
.

Theorem 1

(Oracle invariance)
.

If
\mathcal{L}(G)=\mathcal{L}(G^{\prime})
, then for every terminal prefix
u\in\Sigma^{*}
,

\Omega_{G}(u)=\Omega_{G^{\prime}}(u).

Consequently, any decoding procedure that uses only the pair

(p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid y_{<t}),\,\Omega_{G}(\tau(y_{<t})))
is
behaviorally
invariant
under replacing
G
by
G^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Fix
u\in\Sigma^{*}
and
v\in\mathcal{V}
.
By definition,
v\in\Omega_{G}(u)
iff
\operatorname{CoReach}_{G}(u\tau(v))\neq\emptyset
.
By correctness of compilation (Proposition
1
),

\operatorname{CoReach}_{G}(w)\neq\emptyset
iff
w\in\operatorname{Pref}(\mathcal{L}(G))
(i.e.,
w
is completable).
Thus

v\in\Omega_{G}(u)\ \Longleftrightarrow\ u\tau(v)\in\operatorname{Pref}(\mathcal{L}(G)).

Since
\mathcal{L}(G)=\mathcal{L}(G^{\prime})
implies
\operatorname{Pref}(\mathcal{L}(G))=\operatorname{Pref}(\mathcal{L}(G^{\prime}))
, the condition
is identical for
G^{\prime}
.
∎

3.2
Masked decoding as a constrained stochastic process

Let
\ell_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{V}|}
denote the model logits at step
t
, with

p_{\theta}(v\mid y_{<t})=\frac{\exp(\ell_{t}(v))}{\sum_{w\in\mathcal{V}}\exp(\ell_{t}(w))}.

Definition 9

(Hard mask operator)
.

Given
A\subseteq\mathcal{V}
, define
m_{A}\in(\mathbb{R}\cup\{-\infty\})^{|\mathcal{V}|}
by

m_{A}(v)\coloneqq\begin{cases}0,&v\in A,\\
-\infty,&v\notin A.\end{cases}

Define the masked next-token distribution

q_{\theta,A}(v\mid y_{<t})\coloneqq\mathrm{softmax}(\ell_{t}+m_{A})(v)=\frac{\exp(\ell_{t}(v))\mathbf{1}[v\in A]}{\sum_{w\in A}\exp(\ell_{t}(w))}.

In GCD,
A=\Omega_{G}(\tau(y_{<t}))
.
By Theorem
1
, this depends only on the language, not on the grammar
presentation.

Proposition 2

(Soundness of hard-masked decoding)
.

Let
q_{\theta}^{G}
denote autoregressive sampling where at each step

y_{t}\sim q_{\theta,\Omega_{G}(\tau(y_{<t}))}(\cdot\mid y_{<t})
.
Then every generated sample
y_{1:T}
terminated by
\langle\mathrm{eos}\rangle
satisfies

\tau(y_{1:T})\in\mathcal{L}(G)
.

Proof.

At each step, the sampled token keeps the realized prefix in
\operatorname{Pref}(\mathcal{L}(G))
.
Moreover,
\langle\mathrm{eos}\rangle
is admissible only when the current realized prefix is in
\mathcal{L}(G)
.
Therefore termination implies membership.
∎

Remark 2

(Masking is not the true conditional distribution)
.

Sampling with hard masks yields a distribution supported on
\mathcal{L}(G)
, but it is not
in general equal to conditioning the base model on
\tau(y)\in\mathcal{L}(G)
.
Exact conditioning requires weighting by the model’s probability of
eventual
successful completion
from each candidate next token.
We characterize this discrepancy precisely via a Doob
h
-transform in
Section
8
.

Lemma 1

(Separation from global conditioning)
.

There exist a base autoregressive model
p_{\theta}
and a language
L
such that the
masked process
q_{\theta}^{L}
differs from the globally conditioned distribution

p_{\theta}(\cdot\mid\tau(y)\in L)
.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma=\{a,b\}
,
L=\{a,\,ba\}
, and
\mathcal{V}=\{a,b,\langle\mathrm{eos}\rangle\}
with

\tau(a)=a
,
\tau(b)=b
,
\tau(\langle\mathrm{eos}\rangle)=\varepsilon
.
Set

p_{\theta}(a\mid\varepsilon)=0.6,\quad p_{\theta}(b\mid\varepsilon)=0.4,\quad p_{\theta}(\langle\mathrm{eos}\rangle\mid\varepsilon)=0,

p_{\theta}(\langle\mathrm{eos}\rangle\mid a)=0.1,\quad p_{\theta}(a\mid a)=0.9,\quad p_{\theta}(b\mid a)=0,

p_{\theta}(a\mid b)=0.01,\quad p_{\theta}(b\mid b)=0.99,\quad p_{\theta}(\langle\mathrm{eos}\rangle\mid b)=0.

Under the masked process,
q(b\mid\varepsilon)=0.4
(both tokens are admissible; mask
unchanged at step 1).
Under global conditioning, the total acceptance mass is

p_{\theta}(\text{accept via }a)=0.6\cdot 0.1=0.06,\qquad p_{\theta}(\text{accept via }ba)=0.4\cdot 0.01\cdot 1=0.004,

so
p_{\theta}(b\mid\tau(y)\in L)=0.004/(0.06+0.004)\approx 0.0625\neq 0.4
.
∎

4
Formal State-Space Blowup from Nonterminal Delegation

4.1
Static control-state counts

Consider the canonical language
L=\{a^{n}b^{n}:n\geq 0\}
and grammars:

\displaystyle G_{1}

\displaystyle:S\to aSb\mid\varepsilon

\displaystyle G_{2}

\displaystyle:S\to aAb\mid\varepsilon,\quad A\to aAb\mid\varepsilon.

Both satisfy
\mathcal{L}(G_{1})=\mathcal{L}(G_{2})=L
.

Definition 10

(Grammar size functional)
.

For a CFG
G=(N,\Sigma,P,S)
, define the right-hand-side length of

p=(A\to\alpha)
as
|\alpha|
.
Define the compilation control-state count

\kappa(G)\coloneqq 1+2|N|+\sum_{p\in P}(|\mathrm{rhs}(p)|+1),

matching the construction in Definition
5
.

Lemma 2

(Exact compiled control-state count)
.

For every CFG
G
, the compiled NPDA
\mathcal{A}_{G}
has exactly
\kappa(G)
control
states.

Proof.

By Definition
5
,
Q
contains:
(i) one start state; (ii) two states per nonterminal (entry/exit); and (iii) for every
production
p
with RHS length
m
, a dot-position chain of size
m+1
.
Summing yields
\kappa(G)
.
∎

Lemma 3

(State-space blowup for
G_{2}
vs.
G_{1}
)
.

Under the compilation of Definition
5
,

\kappa(G_{1})=8,\qquad\kappa(G_{2})=15.

In particular,
\kappa(G_{2})>\kappa(G_{1})
and the compiled control-state space
inflates by a factor
15/8
.

Proof.

For
G_{1}
:
|N|=1
, productions
S\to aSb
(RHS length
3
) and
S\to\varepsilon
(length
0
):

\kappa(G_{1})=1+2\cdot 1+((3+1)+(0+1))=8.

For
G_{2}
:
|N|=2
, four productions (two of length
3
, two of length
0
):

\kappa(G_{2})=1+2\cdot 2+2\cdot(3+1)+2\cdot(0+1)=15.

∎

4.2
Online consequences for masking engines

Definition 11

(Bitset-style active-set engine)
.

An active-set engine represents the set of live control states as a bitset over
Q
,
and updates the bitset by applying precomputed adjacency lists for
\varepsilon
-moves and
terminal moves.

Proposition 3

(Lower bound induced by control-state inflation)
.

Fix any bitset-style active-set engine and any grammar
G
.
If the engine performs any operation that scans the entire bitset universe once per
decoding step (e.g., to compute
\varepsilon
-closure, normalize, or garbage-collect inactive
states), then the per-step overhead is
\Omega(\kappa(G))
.
Consequently, replacing
G_{1}
by
G_{2}
increases that overhead by a multiplicative
factor of at least
15/8
.

Proof.

Scanning a bitset of length
\kappa(G)
costs
\Omega(\kappa(G))
in the RAM model.
The multiplicative factor follows from Lemma
3
.
∎

Remark 3
.

This is an engine-level statement about a common representation strategy, not a
universal lower bound over all implementations.
It isolates a checkable invariant: redundant nonterminal structure enlarges the compiled
state universe, increases memory footprint, and typically worsens cache locality, even
when language acceptance is unchanged.

Structural Ambiguity Cost and Parse Forest Density

5.1
Two equivalent grammars for
\Sigma^{*}

Let
\Sigma=\{a,b\}
.
Define:

\displaystyle G_{3}

\displaystyle:S\to aS\mid bS\mid\varepsilon,

\displaystyle G_{4}

\displaystyle:S_{0}\to S\mid\varepsilon,\quad S\to SS\mid a\mid b.

Lemma 4

(Language equivalence)
.

\mathcal{L}(G_{3})=\mathcal{L}(G_{4})=\Sigma^{*}
.

Proof.

G_{3}
generates any string by repeatedly emitting
a
or
b
before terminating with
the
\varepsilon
case; thus
\mathcal{L}(G_{3})=\Sigma^{*}
.
In
G_{4}
,
S
generates all nonempty strings via
S\to a
,
S\to b
, and closure under

S\to SS
, while
S_{0}
additionally permits the empty string.
Thus
\mathcal{L}(G_{4})=\Sigma^{*}
.
∎

Remark 4
.

The use of the auxiliary start symbol
S_{0}
in
G_{4}
avoids spurious
\varepsilon
-expansion
ambiguity that arises when
\varepsilon
is directly permitted by the recursive nonterminal

S
(which would yield infinitely many derivations for the empty string).

5.2
Parse trees and Catalan ambiguity

Definition 12

(Parse-tree count)
.

For a grammar
G
and string
w\in\Sigma^{*}
, let
A_{G}(w)\coloneqq|\mathrm{PT}_{G}(w)|

denote the number of distinct parse trees deriving
w
from the start symbol.

Lemma 5

(Catalan parse explosion for
G_{4}
)
.

For any nonempty
w\in\{a,b\}^{n}
with
n\geq 1
,

A_{G_{4}}(w)=C_{n-1},

where
C_{k}
is the
k
-th Catalan number, and
A_{G_{4}}(w)
is therefore exponential
in
n
.

Proof.

In
G_{4}
, every terminal is produced by a leaf rule (
S\to a
or
S\to b
).
Thus any parse tree for a string of length
n\geq 1
is a full binary tree with
n

leaves, whose internal nodes are applications of
S\to SS
.
The leaf labels are forced by
w
.
Therefore
A_{G_{4}}(w)
equals the number of full binary trees with
n
leaves,
which is
C_{n-1}
.
∎

5.3
Packed-forest density

We use half-open spans
[i,j)
on positions
0,1,\dots,n
.

Definition 13

(Packed-forest split count)
.

In the natural SPPF-style representation for
G_{4}

[
29
,
17
]
,
a
symbol node

S[i,j]
exists for each span derivable by
S
, and a

packed node
records each distinct split
S[i,j]\Rightarrow S[i,k]\,S[k,j]

with
i<k<j
.

Lemma 6

(Tight packed-forest size for
G_{4}
)
.

Fix any nonempty
w\in\{a,b\}^{n}
with
n\geq 2
.
The number of symbol nodes for
S
is
\binom{n+1}{2}=\Theta(n^{2})
, and the total
number of packed nodes is

P_{n}=\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq n}(j-i-1)=\binom{n+1}{3}=\Theta(n^{3}).

Proof.

A symbol node
S[i,j]
exists for every
0\leq i<j\leq n
because
S
derives any
nonempty substring, giving
\binom{n+1}{2}
symbol nodes.
For a fixed span
[i,j)
of length
L\geq 2
, rule
S\to SS
can split at any

k\in(i,j)
, yielding
L-1=(j-i-1)
packed nodes.
Summing over all spans,

P_{n}=\sum_{0\leq i<j\leq n}(j-i-1).

The triple
(i,k,j)
with
0\leq i<k<j\leq n
is in bijection with a 3-element subset
of
\{0,\dots,n\}
, so
P_{n}=\binom{n+1}{3}
.
∎

5.4
Structural ambiguity cost

Definition 14

(Structural ambiguity cost (SAC))
.

Fix a grammar
G
and an online strategy maintaining a packed structure
F_{t}
for the
length-
t
prefix of
w
.
Define the per-step increment

\Delta_{G}(t;w)\coloneqq|F_{t}(w)|-|F_{t-1}(w)|,

and the worst-case structural ambiguity cost

\mathrm{SAC}_{G}(t)\coloneqq\max_{w\in\Sigma^{t}}\Delta_{G}(t;w).

Theorem 2

(SAC separation:
G_{3}
vs.
G_{4}
)
.

For
G_{4}
,
\mathrm{SAC}_{G_{4}}(t)=\Theta(t^{2})
.
For
G_{3}
, there exists a deterministic online strategy achieving

\mathrm{SAC}_{G_{3}}(t)=O(1)
.

Proof.

For
G_{4}
, when the
t
-th symbol arrives, all new spans
[i,t)
for
0\leq i<t
become
available.
Each span
[i,t)
of length
t-i\geq 2
admits
(t-i-1)
new binary splits.
The total number of newly introduced packed alternatives is

\sum_{i=0}^{t-2}(t-i-1)=\sum_{m=1}^{t-1}m=\frac{t(t-1)}{2}=\Omega(t^{2}).

Lemma
6
provides a matching
O(t^{2})
upper bound on new packed
nodes at step
t
, so
\mathrm{SAC}_{G_{4}}(t)=\Theta(t^{2})
.

For
G_{3}
, the grammar is right-linear (hence regular) and unambiguous; an online
strategy need only track the current unambiguous state, which is constant-sized.
Thus
\mathrm{SAC}_{G_{3}}(t)=O(1)
.
∎

Corollary 1

(Cumulative online cost)
.

For
G_{4}
, cumulative packed-structure growth over a length-
n
string is
\Theta(n^{3})
.
For
G_{3}
, cumulative cost is
O(n)
.

Proof.

Summing
\Theta(t^{2})
over
t=1,\dots,n
yields
\Theta(n^{3})
; Lemma
6

provides the matching upper bound.
For
G_{3}
, summing
O(1)
gives
O(n)
.
∎

Remark 5

(A stress test for engines, not for the language)
.

For
\Sigma^{*}
, the ideal admissibility oracle is trivial: all tokens are always
admissible and
\langle\mathrm{eos}\rangle
is always admissible.
Nevertheless, when the grammar engine is treated as a black-box CFG executor, internal
ambiguity can force it to maintain large packed structures merely to rediscover this
trivial oracle.
SAC quantifies that overhead.

6
Engine-Independent Lower Bounds for Parse-Preserving Masking

This section elevates the SAC analysis into an engine-independent lower bound by
formalizing a broad class of engines that are simultaneously (i) sound for masking and
(ii) parse-preserving in a retrieval-efficient sense.

6.1
A parse-preserving masking model

Definition 15

(Online masking engine)
.

An online CFG masking engine for grammar
G
is an algorithm that, after reading each
prefix
u\in\Sigma^{*}
, outputs
\Omega_{G}(u)
and updates an internal state
\sigma(u)

used for subsequent steps.

Definition 16

(Parse-preserving, retrieval-efficient engine)
.

Fix a grammar
G
and a prefix
u
.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{G}(u)
denote the set of all partial parse structures consistent with

u
(i.e., all packed split alternatives that can appear in some parse forest for some
completion of
u
).
An online engine is
parse-preserving
if
\sigma(u)
determines

\mathcal{P}_{G}(u)
exactly (there exists a deterministic extractor recovering

\mathcal{P}_{G}(u)
from
\sigma(u)
).
It is
retrieval-efficient
if the extractor enumerates all elements of

\mathcal{P}_{G}(u)
in
O(|\mathcal{P}_{G}(u)|)
time.
This is the standard output-sensitive retrieval posture used in generalized parsing

[
29
,
17
]
and the retrieval-efficient parsing model studied
in
[
18
]
.

Remark 6

(Why retrieval efficiency is principled)
.

Without a retrieval requirement, an engine could vacuously “preserve all parses” by
storing the pair
(G,u)
and recomputing parse information on demand.
Retrieval efficiency rules out this degenerate loophole by requiring that the maintained
state genuinely encodes the parse set in an output-sensitive way.

6.2
The universal
\Omega(t^{2})
per-token lower bound

Theorem 3

(Engine-independent SAC lower bound)
.

There exists a constant-size CFG
G^{\star}
and a family of prefixes of length
t
such
that any online masking engine that is both sound and parse-preserving retrieval-efficient
for
G^{\star}
must incur
\Omega(t^{2})
per-token update work in the worst case.
The cumulative work over a length-
n
input is therefore
\Omega(n^{3})
.

Proof.

Take
G^{\star}=G_{4}
.
Consider any length-
t
prefix
u
over
\{a,b\}
.
By Lemma
6
, the set
\mathcal{P}_{G^{\star}}(u)
contains all
triples
(i,k,j)
with
0\leq i<k<j\leq t
, with
\binom{t+1}{3}
elements cumulatively.
By the argument of Theorem
2
, the number of triples
newly
introduced
at step
t
is
\binom{t}{2}=\Omega(t^{2})
.

For a retrieval-efficient parse-preserving engine,
\sigma(u_{1:t})
must permit
enumeration of all elements of
\mathcal{P}_{G^{\star}}(u_{1:t})
in linear output time.
Encoding
\Omega(t^{2})
newly distinct objects requires
\Omega(t^{2})
elementary memory
writes in the RAM model; otherwise the extractor cannot enumerate them in
O(|\mathcal{P}|)

time.
Thus the per-token update cost is
\Omega(t^{2})
, and summing gives
\Omega(n^{3})
.
∎

Remark 7

(Relation to classical parsing complexity)
.

Theorem
3
is unconditional within the specified semantic interface
(soundness + retrieval-efficient parse preservation).
It complements the classical results of Valiant
[
34
]
and
Lee
[
18
]
, which relate CFG parsing complexity to Boolean matrix
multiplication via algorithmic reductions.
Our argument is output-structure driven—grounded in the growth of the packed split
set—rather than a reduction from another problem.

7
Decoding-Cost Equivalence Classes and Canonical Low-SAC Forms

Oracle invariance (Theorem
1
) shows that language equivalence preserves
the admissible token oracle.
In this section we ask a finer question: when are two equivalent grammars also

equivalent from the decoder’s cost perspective
?

7.1
Intrinsic SAC

Definition 17

(Intrinsic parse-preserving SAC)
.

Fix a grammar
G
.
Let
\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pp}}(G)
be the class of sound, retrieval-efficient
parse-preserving online masking engines for
G
.
Define the
intrinsic
SAC as

\mathrm{SAC}^{\star}_{G}(t)\coloneqq\inf_{E\in\mathcal{E}_{\mathrm{pp}}(G)}\;\;\max_{w\in\Sigma^{t}}\Delta^{E}_{G}(t;w),

where
\Delta^{E}_{G}(t;w)
is the engine-induced per-step growth of its maintained parse
representation (measured in primitive parse objects).

Remark 8
.

The infimum captures the best possible asymptotic behavior among engines that preserve
all parses retrieval-efficiently.
It abstracts away implementation details while remaining meaningfully constrained by
Theorem
3
.

7.2
Cost-equivalence relations

Definition 18

(Cost equivalence)
.

For grammars
G
and
H
over a shared terminal alphabet, define:

•

Language equivalence
:
G\equiv_{L}H
iff
\mathcal{L}(G)=\mathcal{L}(H)
.

•

SAC equivalence
:
G\equiv_{\mathrm{SAC}}H
iff there exist constants

c_{1},c_{2}>0
and
t_{0}
such that for all
t\geq t_{0}
,

c_{1}\,\mathrm{SAC}^{\star}_{G}(t)\leq\mathrm{SAC}^{\star}_{H}(t)\leq c_{2}\,\mathrm{SAC}^{\star}_{G}(t).

•

Decoding-cost equivalence
:
G\equiv_{\mathrm{dec}}H
iff

G\equiv_{L}H
and
G\equiv_{\mathrm{SAC}}H
.

Remark 9

(Interpretation)
.

G\equiv_{L}H
implies identical admissible token sets (Theorem
1
).

G\equiv_{\mathrm{dec}}H
strengthens this: the
best possible

parse-preserving online cost is asymptotically the same, so the grammars are equivalent
not just semantically but from a decoder-cost perspective.

7.3
Canonical low-SAC representatives under bounded rewrites

Full minimization of pushdown models is NP-hard even in restricted settings

[
11
]
, so any canonical minimal-SAC claim must be carefully scoped.

Definition 19

(Bounded rewrite family)
.

Fix a finite set of local grammar rewrites
\mathcal{R}
, each mapping a grammar to a
language-equivalent grammar.
For integer
k\geq 0
, let
\mathsf{Reach}_{\mathcal{R},k}(G)
be the finite set of
grammars reachable from
G
by applying at most
k
rewrites from
\mathcal{R}
.

Theorem 4

(Existence of minimal-SAC representatives)
.

Fix
\mathcal{R},k
, and a grammar
G
such that every rewrite in
\mathcal{R}

preserves
\mathcal{L}(G)
.
Then there exists
G_{\min}\in\mathsf{Reach}_{\mathcal{R},k}(G)
such that

\mathrm{SAC}^{\star}_{G_{\min}}(t)\leq\mathrm{SAC}^{\star}_{H}(t)\quad\text{for all }H\in\mathsf{Reach}_{\mathcal{R},k}(G)\text{ and all }t.

Moreover, there exists such a
G_{\min}
that is also minimal for any finite
tie-breaker functional (e.g., grammar size) over
\mathsf{Reach}_{\mathcal{R},k}(G)
.

Proof.

\mathsf{Reach}_{\mathcal{R},k}(G)
is finite since
\mathcal{R}
is finite and
k

is bounded.
The pointwise partial order induced by
\mathrm{SAC}^{\star}
over this finite set has at
least one minimal element.
Applying any finite tie-breaker selects a canonical representative.
∎

Remark 10

(Scope)
.

Theorem
4
is an existence result: a canonical low-SAC object is
mathematically well-defined once one fixes a bounded transformation family.
It does not assert computability of
G_{\min}
in general, nor that unrestricted global
minimization is tractable.

8
Grammar-Conditioned Autoregressive Processes

We now move beyond parsing mechanics and treat constrained decoding as a stochastic
process interacting with a formal language constraint.
This develops the theoretical foundations that quantify the distortion introduced by
hard masking relative to true conditional sampling.

8.1
True conditioning via a Doob
h
-transform

Fix a base autoregressive model
p(\cdot)
over token sequences terminated by
\langle\mathrm{eos}\rangle
.
Let
L\subseteq\Sigma^{*}
be the target language with acceptance event

E\coloneqq\{\tau(y_{1:T})\in L\text{ and }y_{T}=\langle\mathrm{eos}\rangle\}.

Definition 20

(Survival function)
.

For any prefix token sequence
y_{<t}
, define the
survival probability

h(y_{<t})\coloneqq\Pr_{p}(E\mid y_{<t}).

Theorem 5

(Doob
h
-transform for conditional decoding)
.

Assume
h(y_{<t})>0
.
Then the true conditional next-token distribution satisfies

p_{E}(v\mid y_{<t})\coloneqq\Pr_{p}(y_{t}=v\mid y_{<t},E)=p(v\mid y_{<t})\cdot\frac{h(y_{<t}v)}{h(y_{<t})}.

Proof.

By Bayes’ rule and the tower property,

\Pr(y_{t}=v\mid y_{<t},E)=\frac{\Pr(y_{t}=v\mid y_{<t})\Pr(E\mid y_{<t}v)}{h(y_{<t})}=p(v\mid y_{<t})\frac{h(y_{<t}v)}{h(y_{<t})}.

This is the discrete-time analogue of Doob’s
h
-transform for Markov chains

[
7
,
20
]
.
∎

Definition 21

(Hard-masked process)
.

Let
A(y_{<t})\coloneqq\Omega_{G}(\tau(y_{<t}))
be the admissible token set.
Define the hard-masked next-token distribution

q(v\mid y_{<t})\coloneqq\frac{p(v\mid y_{<t})\,\mathbf{1}[v\in A(y_{<t})]}{\sum_{w\in A(y_{<t})}p(w\mid y_{<t})}.

8.2
Exact characterization and distortion bounds

Since
h(y_{<t}v)=0
whenever
v\notin A(y_{<t})
, both
p_{E}
and
q
have support
contained in
A(y_{<t})
when
h(y_{<t})>0
.
The distributions differ in how they weight admissible branches.

Theorem 6

(When hard masking equals true conditioning)
.

For a fixed prefix
y_{<t}
with
h(y_{<t})>0
, the following are equivalent:

1.

q(\cdot\mid y_{<t})=p_{E}(\cdot\mid y_{<t})
,

2.

h(y_{<t}v)
is constant over all admissible
v\in A(y_{<t})
.

Proof.

Restricting to
A=A(y_{<t})
:

p_{E}(v\mid y_{<t})\propto p(v\mid y_{<t})\,h(y_{<t}v),\qquad q(v\mid y_{<t})\propto p(v\mid y_{<t}).

These coincide iff the multiplicative factor
h(y_{<t}v)
is constant over
A
.
∎

Definition 22

(Survival spread)
.

For prefix
y_{<t}
with admissible set
A=A(y_{<t})
, define

h_{\min}(y_{<t})\coloneqq\min_{v\in A}h(y_{<t}v),\qquad h_{\max}(y_{<t})\coloneqq\max_{v\in A}h(y_{<t}v),

and spread ratio
\Gamma(y_{<t})\coloneqq h_{\max}(y_{<t})/h_{\min}(y_{<t})

(with
\Gamma=\infty
if
h_{\min}=0
).

Proposition 4

(One-step KL bound)
.

Assume
0<h_{\min}(y_{<t})\leq h_{\max}(y_{<t})<\infty
.
Then

\operatorname{KL}\!\big(q(\cdot\mid y_{<t})\,\|\,p_{E}(\cdot\mid y_{<t})\big)\;\leq\;\log\Gamma(y_{<t}).

Proof.

Write
A=A(y_{<t})
,
p_{v}=p(v\mid y_{<t})
,
h_{v}=h(y_{<t}v)
,

Z=\sum_{w\in A}p_{w}
,
H=\sum_{w\in A}p_{w}h_{w}
.
Then
q_{v}=p_{v}/Z
and
p^{E}_{v}=p_{v}h_{v}/H
, giving

\operatorname{KL}(q\|p_{E})=\mathbb{E}_{q}\!\left[\log\frac{q_{v}}{p^{E}_{v}}\right]=\log\frac{H}{Z}-\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log h_{v}].

Since
h_{\min}\leq h_{v}\leq h_{\max}
, we have
\log(H/Z)\leq\log h_{\max}
and

-\mathbb{E}_{q}[\log h_{v}]\leq-\log h_{\min}
, so

\operatorname{KL}(q\|p_{E})\leq\log h_{\max}-\log h_{\min}=\log\Gamma(y_{<t})
.
∎

Corollary 2

(One-step total-variation bound)
.

Under the assumptions of Proposition
4
,

\operatorname{TV}\!\big(q(\cdot\mid y_{<t}),\,p_{E}(\cdot\mid y_{<t})\big)\;\leq\;\sqrt{\tfrac{1}{2}\log\Gamma(y_{<t})}.

Proof.

Apply Pinsker’s inequality
\operatorname{TV}(P,Q)\leq\sqrt{\operatorname{KL}(P\|Q)/2}
to
Proposition
4
.
∎

Remark 11

(Interpretation)
.

Hard masking approximates true conditional sampling when survival probabilities from
admissible next tokens are nearly equal (
\Gamma(y_{<t})\approx 1
).
This formalizes the intuition that hard masking ignores “ease of completion” effects
and quantifies distortion purely in terms of completion-probability variability.
The spread
\Gamma
is large when different admissible tokens lead to vastly different
probabilities of eventual acceptance, and small when the constraint is nearly uniform
over admissible branches.

Neural Integration: Attention and Mixture-of-Experts Routing

We express the algebra by which a reachability oracle modulates logits in a Transformer
and extend the formulation to MoE routing probabilities.

9.1
Transformer logits under grammar-modulated admissibility

Let a causal Transformer
[
35
]
compute a hidden state

h_{t}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
from prefix
y_{<t}
(with KV-caching assumed).
The unmasked logits are

The masked logits are

\ell_{t}^{(G)}\coloneqq\ell_{t}+m_{A_{t}},

(2)

q_{\theta}^{G}(y_{t}=v\mid y_{<t})=\mathrm{softmax}(\ell_{t}^{(G)})(v)=\frac{\exp(\ell_{t}(v))\mathbf{1}[v\in A_{t}]}{\sum_{w\in A_{t}}\exp(\ell_{t}(w))}.

(3)

This is the algebraic logit modulation performed by GCD engines

[

,
2
,
23
,
6
,

9.2
MoE routing under grammar state

Consider a Transformer with MoE feed-forward blocks

[

,
10
]
.
At layer
\ell

over

Standard routing computes

A grammar-aware MoE can incorporate

9.3
Latency envelopes

Let
V\coloneqq|\mathcal{V}|
(vocabulary size),
B

t
the current step,

\bar{\ell}\coloneqq\mathbb{E}[|\tau(y_{t})|]

M_{t}
the live-configuration representation size per hypothesis, and

Definition 23

(Abstract engine-step primitives)
.

Let
\mathrm{Update}_{G}(M_{t},\bar{\ell})
be the cost to update the grammar engine state
after consuming one token, and
\mathrm{Enumerate}_{G}(M_{t})
be the cost to enumerate

Proposition 5

(Per-step complexity: dense-vocabulary masking)
.

If the model computes dense logits over all
V
tokens for each of
B

O\!\big(B\cdot\big(T_{\mathrm{model}}(t)+\mathrm{Update}_{G}(M_{t},\bar{\ell})+\mathrm{Enumerate}_{G}(M_{t})+V\big)\big),

Per hypothesis: compute logits (
T_{\mathrm{model}}(t)
), update and enumerate mask
(
\mathrm{Update}_{G}+\mathrm{Enumerate}_{G}
), then scan a

B
.
∎

Proposition 6

(Per-step complexity: sparse admissible-set scoring)

If the inference stack scores only admissible tokens (e.g., via trie-restricted

O\!\big(B\cdot\big(T_{\mathrm{model}}(t)+\mathrm{Update}_{G}(M_{t},\bar{\ell})+\mathrm{Enumerate}_{G}(M_{t})+K_{t}\big)\big).

Corollary 3

(SAC-induced masking bottleneck)

For

9.4
Grammar-conditioned logits with sound masking

Definition 24

(Grammar-conditioned logits)

(Soundness under grammar-conditioned logits)

Sampling from

.

The support of

.

From SAC to Runtime Prediction, Grammar Optimization, and Hybrid Control

This section extends the SAC analysis into (i) an empirically calibratable performance
model, (ii) an equivalence-preserving grammar optimizer, and (iii) deeper
symbolic–neural coupling via instrumented engines.

10.1
Instrumented engines and SAC proxies

Production engines do not expose
|F_{t}(w)|
directly; they expose engine-native
structures (Earley items, LR items, trie nodes, persistent-stack nodes, etc.).
LLGuidance uses an Earley-based recognizer combined with token-trie traversal

[
15
]

representations
[
6
]
.
We introduce a general instrumentation interface to unify these.

Definition 25

(Instrumented decoding engine)
.

Fix grammar
G

\tau

An instrumented decoding engine is a triple
E=(\mathrm{Step},\mathrm{Mask},\mathrm{Ctr})

where at each step

•

\mathrm{Step}
advances engine state for each hypothesis;

•

\mathrm{Mask}
returns
\Omega_{G}(\tau(y_{<t}))
;

•

\mathrm{Ctr}
returns a vector of nonneg. counters recording symbolic work
(items created, edges traversed, trie nodes visited, stack nodes touched, etc.).

Definition 26

(SAC proxy)

Let
c_{t}(E;w)\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}

A scalar

S_{t}^{E}(w)\coloneqq\langle\alpha,c_{t}(E;w)\rangle

for some fixed
\alpha\in\mathbb{R}_{\geq 0}^{d}

symbolic work.

Lemma 7

(Proxy lower-bounds SAC under split completeness)

Consider
G_{4}
with rule
S\to SS
.
Assume engine

is
split-complete

[i,t)
of length
\geq 2

in its maintained structure, it represents each split point

with

Then there exists
\beta>0
such that for all

\max_{w\in\Sigma^{t}}S_{t}^{E}(w)\;\geq\;\beta\cdot\mathrm{SAC}_{G_{4}}(t).

Proof.

At step

, split completeness forces the engine to account for each newly introduced
split alternative for each new span ending at
t
.
By Theorem
2
, this is
\Omega(t^{2})
in the worst case.
Any proxy assigning positive weight to such alternatives yields a linear lower bound
on that count up to a constant.
∎

10.2

Proposition 8

(Beam amplification of symbolic work)
.

Assuming beam hypotheses do not share grammar engine state,

S^{E}_{t,\mathrm{total}}\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{B}S^{E}_{t}(w^{(i)})\;=\;\Theta(B\cdot\overline{S}_{t}),

where
\overline{S}_{t}
is the average proxy per hypothesis.
Under the affine model,
T_{\mathrm{mask,total}}(t)\approx a\cdot\Theta(B\cdot\overline{S}_{t})+b^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Counters are generated by disjoint engine updates; the proxy is additive across
independent hypotheses.
Substituting into the affine model absorbs constant terms into
b^{\prime}
.
∎

Remark 14

(DPDA compilation and batch efficiency)
.

When nondeterminism forces persistent-stack branching,
S_{t}^{E}
can grow with beam even
at fixed prefix length.
Pre
3
targets this by compiling LR(1) structures to a DPDA and precomputing
prefix-conditioned edges to reduce runtime path exploration
[
5
]
.
Within our framework this corresponds to reducing active configuration fanout and
shrinking variance in
S_{t}^{E}
across steps.

10.3
Automated grammar optimization

Oracle invariance (Theorem
1
) permits a compiler searching over
equivalent grammars to minimize an engine-dependent cost model.

Definition 29

(Grammar optimization problem)
.

Fix engine family
E
, tokenizer
\tau
, and workload
\mathcal{D}
.
Define the feasible set
\mathcal{G}(G)\coloneqq\{G^{\prime}:\mathcal{L}(G^{\prime})=\mathcal{L}(G)\}
and
objective

\min_{G^{\prime}\in\mathcal{G}(G)}\;\;\mathbb{E}_{w\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\mathrm{Cost}_{E}(G^{\prime},\tau;w)\Big],

where
\mathrm{Cost}_{E}
can combine static terms (e.g.,
\kappa(G^{\prime})
), dynamic terms
(expected SAC proxy), and tokenizer alignment overhead.

Remark 15

(Tokenizer alignment)
.

DOMINO highlights that improper alignment between subword vocabularies and grammar
terminals can distort model distributions and incur “bridge” handling overhead

[
2
]
.
Park et al. formalize lexer-state-dependent token-to-terminal mapping to avoid
inefficient combinations at runtime
[
23
]
.
Thus misalignment cost is a first-class component of
\mathrm{Cost}_{E}
.

Definition 30

(Inlining rewrite)
.

Let
A\in N
have productions
A\to\gamma_{1}\mid\cdots\mid\gamma_{m}
.
An
inlining
rewrite replaces a production
B\to\alpha A\beta
by the set

\{B\to\alpha\gamma_{i}\beta:i=1,\dots,m\}
, leaving other productions unchanged.

Lemma 8

(Inlining preserves the language)
.

Assume
A
is not the start symbol and the inlining rewrite introduces no new
nonterminals.
Then the rewritten grammar
G^{\prime}
satisfies
\mathcal{L}(G^{\prime})=\mathcal{L}(G)
.

Proof.

Each derivation in
G
applying
B\to\alpha A\beta
followed by
A\Rightarrow^{*}\gamma_{i}

maps to a derivation in
G^{\prime}
using
B\to\alpha\gamma_{i}\beta
; thus

\mathcal{L}(G)\subseteq\mathcal{L}(G^{\prime})
.
The reverse containment is symmetric.
∎

Remark 16

(Equality saturation for grammar optimization)
.

Even with local rewrites, the search space of equivalent grammars is large.
Equality saturation with e-graphs
[
32
,
37
]
offers a
principled approach: represent grammar fragments in an e-graph, apply
equivalence-preserving rewrites (inlining, delegation elimination, recursion
normalization), attach cost analyses estimating
\kappa
, SAC proxy surrogates, and
tokenizer alignment, then extract the minimum-cost equivalent grammar.
Hardness results (NP-completeness of VPA minimization
[
11
]
) motivate
heuristic or class-restricted optimization rather than global optimality.

12
Conclusion

We formalized grammar-constrained decoding as a coupling between a neural next-token
model and a pushdown reachability oracle derived from a CFG.
Our contributions are as follows.

Oracle invariance
(Theorem
1
): language-equivalent grammars
induce identical admissible token sets, hence identical logit masks, for every prefix.
This invariance coexists with provable differences in engine state-space size and online
update cost.

State-space blowup
(Lemma
3
): for the
a^{n}b^{n}
language, we
gave exact control-state counts under a concrete PDA compilation and showed a
15/8

blowup factor from redundant nonterminal delegation.

SAC bounds
(Theorem
2
, Corollary
1
):
concatenation grammars incur
\Theta(t^{2})
incremental packed-structure growth and

\Theta(n^{3})
cumulative cost; right-recursive grammars admit
O(1)
and
O(n)
,
respectively.

Engine-independent lower bounds
(Theorem
3
): any sound,
retrieval-efficient, parse-preserving masking engine must incur
\Omega(t^{2})
per-token
work on
G_{4}
, making the SAC lower bound unconditional within this model.

Decoding-cost equivalence classes
(Theorem
4
): minimal-SAC
grammar representatives exist within any bounded-rewrite family, providing a
mathematically precise notion of canonical low-SAC normal forms.

Grammar-conditioned processes
(Theorem
5
,
Proposition
4
, Corollary
2
): the true conditional
sampler is characterized by a Doob
h
-transform, and hard masking incurs a one-step
KL distortion bounded by
\log\Gamma(y_{<t})
, where
\Gamma
measures
survival-probability spread among admissible next tokens.

Neural integration and performance modeling
: we derived latency envelopes
for Transformer and MoE architectures, showed that SAC-induced bottlenecks affect
masking-critical-path time under beam search, and connected these results to
instrumentation-based predictive models and automated grammar optimization via
equivalence-preserving rewrites and equality saturation

[
12
,
5
,
32
,
37
]
.

Together, these results establish a rigorous theoretical foundation for grammar
refactoring as a latency optimization problem with well-defined semantic invariants and
measurable cost surrogates.
