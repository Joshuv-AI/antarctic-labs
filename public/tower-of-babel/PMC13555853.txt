Integrative analysis of transcriptome and chromatin accessibility reveals promoter–proximal regulation and identifies candidate ABC transporters associated with cold stress responses in maize
Authors: Wenbin Tang, Yuchen Feng, Meicheng Ke, Feng Yu, Guoqing Dong, Xing Huang, Huanhuan Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLow–temperature stress is a formidable environmental constraint that severely limits the growth and productivity of maize (Zea mays L.), particularly during the highly vulnerable early seedling stage. While cold tolerance is a critical agronomic objective, the integrated transcriptional and epigenetic regulatory mechanisms that govern this trait remain largely elusive. Characterizing these coordinated molecular networks is fundamental to the genetic enhancement of cold resilience in maize.MethodsUsing two maize inbred lines contrasting in chilling response (ZHB12 tolerant, B73 sensitive), we performed integrative time‑course RNA‑seq and ATAC‑seq to thoroughly and systematically characterize the precise dynamic interplay between gene expression and chromatin accessibility under cold stress conditions at the seedling stage.ResultsPhysiological assessments confirmed that ZHB12 possesses superior cold tolerance, manifested by significantly attenuated electrolyte leakage and reduced foliar damage compared to B73. Transcriptomic profiling revealed a massive, time–dependent divergence in gene expression between the two genotypes, with a major regulatory transition identified at 24 h of cold exposure. Functional enrichment analysis demonstrated that ZHB12 preferentially activates a robust defense repertoire, including Photosystem II electron transport, diterpenoid biosynthesis, and ATP biosynthetic pathways. Notably, multiple ATP–binding cassette (ABC) transporter genes were coordinately upregulated under chilling, suggesting their potential involvement in cellular homeostasis. ATAC–seq analysis indicated that cold stress is associated with chromatin remodeling in ZHB12, with increased accessibility observed in proximal promoter regions. Integrative analysis identified a core set of dual–responsive genes, in which increased promoter accessibility coincided with transcriptional upregulation. These genes were predominantly enriched in transporter activity and transcriptional regulation, suggesting potential epigenetic link to the superior stress response of ZHB12.ConclusionOur findings reveal extensive transcriptional and chromatin accessibility changes in ZHB12 under cold stress. The observed associations between promoter accessibility and gene activation, particularly in genes involved in transport processes, highlight candidate regulators potentially contributing to cold tolerance. This study provides a molecular framework and identifies high-value candidate genes that may inform future efforts in breeding cold-tolerant maize, pending functional validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09328-6.

Introduction

Maize (Zea mays L.) is one of the world’s most essential staple crops, playing a pivotal role in global food security, livestock feed, and industrial applications [1]. Despite its global importance, the tropical region of maize renders it inherently susceptible to low–temperature stress. Chilling temperatures (< 12 °C) during germination, seedling establishment, and reproductive stages significantly inhibit development–inducing leaf chlorosis, impeding root growth, reducing photosynthetic efficiency, and limiting dry matter accumulation–ultimately leading to severe yield losses. Under the trajectory of ongoing climate change, the increased frequency of extreme weather events, coupled with the expansion of maize cultivation into higher latitudes, has further elevated the risks associated with chilling stress [2, 3]. Extensive research has demonstrated that chilling stress impairs maize physiology through multiple interconnected mechanisms, including destabilization of the cell membrane structure due to altered lipid fluidity, induction of oxidative damage through reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, disruption of hormonal balance (particularly in abscisic acid [ABA] and ethylene [ET] signaling), and inhibition of osmoregulatory capacity [4, 5]. Notably, peroxidase (POD) has been recognized as a key biomarker for evaluating cold tolerance in maize radicles [6]. Consequently, deciphering the molecular mechanisms underlying cold resilience and identifying effective genetic resources are crucial for developing stress–tolerant maize varieties.

Recent studies have successfully cloned several key cold–tolerance genes in maize, revealing a complex regulatory landscape. For instance, the bZIP transcription factor bZIP68 acts as a negative regulator by repressing DREB1 expression; its stability and DNA binding are further enhanced by MPK8–mediated phosphorylation [7]. Conversely, natural variation in the HSF21 promoter enhances maize cold tolerance by disrupting bZIP68 binding, thereby elevating HSF21 expression and activating lipid metabolism genes (e.g., GPATs, FADs) to maintain membrane integrity [8]. Similarly, a 15–amino acid deletion in the A–type response regulator ZmRR1 (ZmRR1Del) stabilizes the protein through MPK8–mediated phosphorylation at Ser–78, subsequently activating downstream DREB1/CBF genes and CesA cellulose synthases [9]. Other mechanisms involve epigenetic and structural variations: a 328–bp transposon insertion in the TIP4;3 promoter suppresses its expression via DNA methylation, reducing ROS accumulation [10], while the bHLH transcription factor COOL1 negatively regulates tolerance until stabilized by CPK17–mediated phosphorylation [3]. Furthermore, ZmICE1 fine–tune the cold responses by activating ZmDREB1 genes while repressing Glu/Asn biosynthesis genes (ZmASs) to, a process modulated by promoter variations that affect ZmMYB39 [11, 12].

While these individual genetic components provide valuable insights, plant adaptation to cold stress involves a systemic cascade of alterations across chromosomal structure, transcriptional activity, and metabolic pathways. The rapid maturation of genomic and multi–omics technologies has provided powerful platforms for deciphering these complex adaptation mechanisms. Because gene expression is spatiotemporally regulated and highly dependent on chromatin accessibility–with approximately 90% of cis–regulatory elements enriched in accessible chromatin regions (ACRs)–the integration of ATAC–seq [13] and RNA–seq offers a multi–dimensional view of gene regulation. For example, variations in the tomato WRKY34 promoter modulate chromatin accessibility to affect GATA element binding [14], and tissue–specific accessibility of ERF5 regulates cold responses in maize [15]. Furthermore, integrated GWAS, transcriptomic, and metabolomic analyses have identified critical pathways involving carotenoid metabolism, ABC transporters, and MAPK signaling as pillars of cold adaptation [16–19].

Despite these advances, the integrated landscape of chromatin accessibility and transcriptional rewriting during maize cold adaptation remains poorly understood. In this study, we employed an integrated RNA–seq and ATAC–seq approach to systematically investigate the molecular basis of cold tolerance. By performing parallel comparative profiling of cold–tolerant genotype ZHB12 versus cold–sensitive B73 under both control and stress conditions, we identified key regulatory loci and construct the molecular networks associated with chilling resilience.

Materials and methods

Plant materials, growth conditions, and cold stress treatment

The maize (Zea mays L.) inbred lines B73 and ZHB12 were utilized in this study. Among them, B73 is an inbred line with a reference genome, while ZHB12 is a selectively bred inbred line with excellent comprehensive agronomic traits. All plant materials were preserved and provided by our laboratory. Seeds were surface–sterilized and germinated in pots containing a mixture of nutrient soil and vermiculite (2:1, v/v). Plants were cultivated in controlled environment growth chambers under a 14/10 h light/dark photoperiod, a photosynthetic photon flux density of 100 µmol.m⁻².s⁻¹, and 60% relative humidity. At the three–leaf stage, uniformly grown seedlings were divided into two groups for temperature treatment: the control group was maintained at 25 °C, while the treatment group was subjected to cold stress at 4 °C for a specified duration. For transcriptomic analysis, seedling leaves were harvested at 0, 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 72 h post–cold treatment. For ATAC–seq, leaf samples were collected at 0 h (control) and 24 h after the onset of cold stress.

Phenotypic evaluation

Following the treatment period, phenotypic responses to cold stress were systematically assessed. Leaf damage was quantified by calculating the percentage of Leaf Damage Area (LDA) exhibiting chlorosis or necrosis on the leaf using digital image analysis with ImageJ software. Concurrently, electrolyte leakage, a key indicator of membrane integrity, was measured. Leaf discs were harvested and immersed in deionized water, and the initial electrical conductivity (EC1) of the solution was measured. The samples were then boiled to release all electrolytes, and the final conductivity (EC2) was measured after cooling. The relative electrolyte leakage was calculated as (EC1/EC2) × 100%.

Transcriptome sequencing (RNA–seq) analysis

Total RNA was extracted from the leaf tissues of both B73 and ZHB12 seedlings, including those from the control group and cold–treated group, with three biological replicates per condition. RNA integrity was verified using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. The cDNA libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina platform (NovaSeq 6000) to generate paired–end reads. The raw sequencing reads in fastq format were processed to remove adapters and low–quality bases by fastp (v1.1.0) [20]. Clean reads were then aligned to the reference maize genome (B73 RefGen_v5) using HISAT2 [21]. Gene expression levels were quantified using FeatureCounts. Differentially expression genes (DEGs) analysis between groups was performed using DESeq2 [22], with genes satisfying a |log2(FoldChange)| ≥ 1 and an adjusted p–value < 0.05 considered significantly differentially expressed.

Assay for Transposase–Accessible Chromatin sequencing (ATAC–seq)

Nuclei were isolated from fresh leaf tissue under normal and cold–treated conditions. The transposase reaction was performed using the Illumina Tagment DNA TDE1 Enzyme and Buffer Kits to fragment accessible genomic regions. The resulting libraries were purified, amplified, and sequenced on an Illumina platform. After quality control of the raw reads, they were aligned to the reference genome using Bowtie2 [23]. Peaks representing open chromatin regions were called using MACS2 and annotated with the closest genes using ChIPseeker. Differential chromatin accessibility analysis was conducted to identify regions that became significantly more or less accessible upon cold stress. Based on peak–derived signals, significant differential analysis was conducted between comparison groups. By comparing the ATAC–seq signal intensities of peaks between two sample groups, regions with significantly different signal intensities were identified, namely, differentially accessible regions (DARs). The specific analytical approach is outlined as: firstly, peaks from each group were merged to create a comprehensive peak set. Subsequently, for each peak within this combined set, read coverage was extracted according to its genomic location. Then, differential accessibility analysis between groups was conducted using DESeq2 software, with filtering thresholds set as |log2FoldChange| ≥ 2, p–value ≤ 0.01, and adjusted p–value (p–adjust) ≤ 0.01, to identify DARs between the two sample groups. Based on this method, DAR detection was performed for samples across groups, leading to the identification of group–specific DARs, encompassing gain DARs (regions exhibiting increased accessibility) and loss DARs (regions showing decreased accessibility).

Gene ontology and pathway enrichment analysis

To interpret the biological significance of the identified candidate genes from the above analyses, Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis was performed for terms in biological process, molecular function, and cellular component. Additionally, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was conducted. Both analyses were carried out using g: Profiler (https://biit.cs.ut.ee/gprofiler/gost), with terms/pathways having an adjusted p–value < 0.05 for statistical significance.

Quantitative Real–Time (RT) PCR

Total RNA isolated for transcriptome analysis was also subjected to qRT–PCR. After DNase treatment (RNase–free DNase, Invitrogen) and reverse transcription with recombinant M–MLV reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen), qRT–PCR was performed using gene–specific primers and 2× iTaq™ Universal SYBR Green Supermix (BioRad) in a 20 µL reaction. The thermal cycling protocol included an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 58 °C for 10 s, and 72 °C for 20 s. The maize Actin1 gene (ZmActin1) served as the internal reference for data normalization, and relative expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Results

ZHB12 exhibits superior cold tolerance compared to inbred line B73

To elucidate the regulatory mechanism underlying the maize cold stress response, we selected two contrasting genotypes: the cold–sensitive inbred line B73 and the cold–tolerant inbred line ZHB12. Seedling were grown to the three–leaf stage and then subjected to either a 4℃ chilling treatment or maintained at ambient room temperature as control. Our results indicated that cold stress significantly inhibited the growth of both genotypes. After 4 days of chilling, both lines exhibited pronounced phenotypic alterations, including leaf chlorosis, rolling, and wilting (Fig. 1A). However, the cold–sensitive line B73 sustained more severe damage than the tolerant line ZHB12. Specifically, the injured length of second leaf was significantly greater in B73 than in ZHB12 (p < 0.001; Fig. 1B), a disparity that persisted even after a 24–hour recovery period (p < 0.01) (Fig. 1C). Furthermore, the central leaf whorls showed significant morphological differences between the two varieties (p < 0.05) (Fig. 1D). To quantify the degree of membrane damage, we measured electrolyte leakage (EL) over time in first leaf. While both genotypes showed a gradual increase in EL under cold stress, B73 exhibited significantly higher leakage rates than ZHB12 at every time point starting from 12 h post–treatment (Fig. 1E). This trend was similarly observed in the second leaf, where B73 experienced a more pronounced increase in ion leakage compared to ZHB12 (Fig. 1F). These physiological and phenotypic data collected confirmed the superior cold tolerance of ZHB12 over B73.

Transcriptomic profiling reveals global gene expression divergence between B73 and ZHB12

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying maize seedling cold tolerance, we performed comprehensive RNA–sequencing on B73 and ZHB12 across a detailed cold–stress time course. The sequencing yielded a total of 504 GB of clean data, exhibiting high technical quality with Q20 and Q30 scores exceeding 97.74% and 93.98%, respectively (Table S1). An average mapping rate of ≥ 89.66% to the reference genome further confirmed the high reliability of the dataset (Table S1). Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was employed to evaluate the transcriptomic variability across genotypes and treatments based on gene expression matrix (Table S2). PC1 and PC2 accounted for 28.8% and 22.2% of the total variance, respectively. Samples within each biological group clustered closely, demonstrating high experimental reproducibility (Fig. 1A). Notably, the transcriptome of B73 and ZHB12 showed clear separation even at 0 h (control) stage, suggesting inherent baseline differences in their gene expression profiles. As the duration of cold stress increased, the PCA trajectories of B73 and ZHB12 diverged significantly, reflecting distinct transcriptional responses to chilling between the two genotypes. When evaluated relative to control conditions (0 h), a large number of DEGs were identified within B73 and ZHB12 across the time course (Fig. S1A). A vast majority of these cold-responsive genes were genotype-specific at each individual time point (Fig. S1B). Concurrently, GO enrichment analysis highlighted highly divergent, genotype-specific functional terms in response to chilling (Fig. S1C), further emphasizing the distinct molecular mechanisms deployed by B73 and ZHB12 to cope with low-temperature stress.

To precisely pin down the specific genetic drivers underlying these variations, we systematically characterized the cross-genotype DEGs between ZHB12 and B73 across a comprehensive time point (0, 1, 3, 6, 12, 24, 36, 48, and 72 h). Integrated volcano plots illustrate the distribution of these DEGs, with orange and blue dots representing genes significantly up– or down– regulated in the cold–tolerant ZHB12 relative to the sensitive B73, respectively (Fig. 1B). Under control conditions (0 h), we identified 4,202 DEGs (2,256 up– and 1,946 down– regulated), indicating inherent baseline differences between the two genotypes. As cold stress progressed, the number of DEGs fluctuated dynamically: early responses at 1, 3, and 6 h remained relatively stable, whereas a substantial expansion in transcriptomic divergence occurred at 12 h (4,581 DEGs) and reached a distinct peak at 24 h post–treatment with 6,763 DEGs (3,105 up– and 3,658 down–regulated). During the late stress stages (36–72 h), the magnitude of differential expression narrowed but remained significant, with 5,219 DEGs identified at the final 72 h time point (Table S3). These results highlight a massive and time–dependent transcriptional reprogramming in ZHB12 that likely underpins its superior chilling resilience.

Functional enrichment and expression profiling identify key regulatory modules for cold resilience

To uncover the biological functions driving the transcriptomic divergence between B73 and ZHB12, we performed a comprehensive GO enrichment analysis on the identified DEGs (Fig. 2C). Under control conditions (0 h), significant enrichment was observed in lipid metabolism, sulfur compound catabolism, diterpenoid biosynthesis, defense response and alcohol metabolism, reflecting inherent physiological differences between the two genotypes. Upon cold exposure, the temporal progression of DEGs revealed a highly dynamic and coordinated transcriptomic landscape (Fig. 2C). In the early stage (1–6 h), ZHB12 prioritized rapid stress perception and cellular priming for antioxidant defence. This was evidenced by the significant enrichment of diterpenoid biosynthesis, glutathione metabolism, and photosynthetic electron transport in PSII. The mid–stage (12–24 h) marked a critical regulatory transition where the transcriptomic response reached its peak complexity. Notably, at 24 h of cold exposure, the ATP biosynthetic process exhibited its maximum statistical significance (indicated by the deep red color). This represents a decisive biological shift toward establishing systemic energy homeostasis to fuel downstream defense machinery. This was accompanied by a surge in transmembrane transport and heme transport, facilitating the mobilization of protective solutes. During the late stage (36–72 h), the enrichment patterns shifted toward sustained physiological maintenance. Pathways related to respiratory electron transport chains and alcohol metabolism remained prominent, while the ATP biosynthetic process showed continued, albeit secondary, enrichment at 72 h. This temporal progression from early signaling to a 24 h–centered energy peak, followed by late–stage metabolic stabilization, defines the superior adaptive strategy of ZHB12 in maintaining cellular homeostasis under prolonged cold stress.

Notably, the “transmembrane transport” pathway was consistently enriched across multiple time points, encompassing 614 DEGs that were further partitioned into 6 distinct clusters (Fig. 3). Within this pathway, several ABC transporters (e.g., Zm00001eb178670, Zm00001eb277060, Zm00001eb301970, and Zm00001eb332210) were prominently involved. These genes, grouped in Cluster5 and 6, manifested significantly higher induction levels in the tolerant line ZHB12, particularly during the late stages of cold treatment in both lines, suggesting their potential involvement in maize cold stress response. Furthermore, twenty-five genes associated with Photosystem II (PSII) exhibited divergent expression patterns between B73 and ZHB12; specifically, the majority of these genes showed an earlier transcriptional response in ZHB12 (Fig. 4A). A substantial proportion of DEGs in this category maintained high expression levels in ZHB12 during the middle and late stages of stress, whereas they were down regulated or lowly expressed in B73 (Fig. 4C). Additionally, 174 genes identified in defense response pathway exhibited marked expression differences between the two lines (Fig. 4D), which may contribute to differences in stress-responsive profiles between B73 and ZHB12.

Genome–wide chromatin accessibility dynamics in B73 and ZHB12 under cold stress

To investigate changes in chromatin accessibility in cold–tolerant and cold–sensitive materials under low–temperature stress, we performed a systematic analysis of chromatin accessibility in leaves of B73 and ZHB12 under normal condition (0 h) and after 24 h of cold exposure. Comparison of chromatin accessibility signal intensity and chromosomal distribution revealed that open chromatin regions were predominantly enriched around transcription start sites (TSSs), with additional enrichment observed within gene bodies. Notably, ZHB12 exhibited the strongest enrichment of open chromatin regions after 24 h of cold treatment (Fig. 5A; Fig. S2). To further assess the reliability of the identified open chromatin regions, we evaluated peak reproducibility between biological replicates of B73 and ZHB12 under both 0 h and 24 h conditions. In B73 leaves, 234,920 and 268,392 peaks were identified under 0 h and 24 h conditions, respectively, whereas 179,296 and 154,208 peaks were identified in ZHB12 at the corresponding time points (Fig. 5B). Overall, a greater number of peaks were detected in B73 than in ZHB12. The majority of peaks showed significant overlap between replicates, indicating high biological reproducibility and the robustness of the chromatin accessibility data. Further genomic annotation of reproducibly overlapping peaks revealed distinct changes in chromatin accessibility distribution following cold treatment (Fig. 5C). In B73, open chromatin regions were predominantly located in distal intergenic regions, with their proportion increasing from 78.50% under control conditions to 83.68% after cold treatment. In contrast, the proportion of distal intergenic peaks in ZHB12 decreased from 76.73% under control conditions to 71.03% after 24 h of cold exposure. Meanwhile, the proportion of peaks located in promoter regions, particularly within 1 kb upstream of TSSs, markedly increased in ZHB12 after cold treatment, reaching 12.89%. Conversely, in B73, the proportion of promoter–associated peaks, especially within the promoter 1 kb region, decreased from 7.95% to 4.70% following cold treatment. These results indicate that low–temperature stress preferentially promotes chromatin opening at proximal promoter regions in the cold–tolerant material.

Differential chromatin accessibility between ZHB12 and B73 under cold stress

To comprehensively map the epigenetic variations and compare the chromatin remodeling landscapes across different treatment periods within each line, we systematically evaluated the distribution and functional profiles of DARs at 0 h and 24 h for both genotypes (Fig. S3). Spatial genomic quantification showed that cold stress triggered a pronounced divergence in the numbers of promoter–proximal and distal intergenic ‘Gain’ and ‘Loss’ DARs between the lines (Fig. S3A). This was accompanied by a corresponding shift in the numbers of associated downstream target genes (Fig. S3B). Furthermore, GO enrichment analysis revealed that genes associated with Gain DARs were enriched in distinct, genotype-specific biological pathways upon cold challenge (Fig. S3C), providing a higher–resolution view of how individual genotypes adjust their accessible chromatin landscape over different treatment periods.

Building upon these intra–genotypic epigenetic foundations, we next sought to pinpoint the exact locations where chromatin accessibility diverges directly between the two lines under thermal challenge. To achieve this, we systematically cross–characterized the specific genomic distributions of differentially accessible regions (DARs) between ZHB12 and B73 under low–temperature stress at both 0 h and 24 h of cold treatment (Fig. 1D) Using B73 as the reference, ZHB12 at 0 h exhibited predominantly loss DARs, which predominated over gain DARs. These loss DARs were mainly located in distal intergenic regions, whereas less than 10% of peaks were found in promoter regions. Specifically, 80.33% of gain DARs and 87.13% of loss DARs were mapped to distal intergenic regions at 0 h. After 24 h of cold treatment, loss DARs in ZHB12 relative to B73 remained the dominant category and continued to be enriched in distal regions. However, in proximal promoter regions, ZHB12 displayed a substantial number of gain DARs, exceeding the corresponding loss DARs. At this time point, 47.81% of gain DARs were located in distal intergenic regions, whereas over 43% of gain DARs were located in promoter regions. In contrast, loss DARs at 24 h were still primarily distal, with 88.93% of peaks in distal intergenic regions and fewer than 8% in promoter regions (Fig. S4). Because B73 and ZHB12 are highly polymorphic inbred lines, mapping ZHB12 reads directly to the B73 reference genome can inherently introduce technical mapping bias. To systematically evaluate and rule out potential false positives (i.e., artifacts) arising from such cross-line sequence divergence, we performed a temporal intersection analysis tracking both the physical genomic coordinates of the DARs and their associated downstream target genes (Fig. S5). We reasoned that if these Loss DARs were merely artifacts of unmappable genomic deletions or structural variations in ZHB12, they would remain statically unmappable across all time points. However, our data revealed high condition-specificity among the physical DAR regions (Fig. S5A). Most decisively, we identified a prominent “Loss-to-Gain” chromatin state transition among nearly 900 downstream target genes; these loci appeared less accessible at 0 h but were successfully mapped and exhibited significantly enriched accessibility upon 24 h of cold exposure (Fig. S5B). Collectively, these multi-dimensional validations confirm that the observed alterations represent authentic, highly dynamic epigenetic remodeling rather than genomic structural variations. Taken together, these results demonstrate that low-temperature stress selectively intensifies chromatin accessibility in the proximal regulatory regions of ZHB12, whereas distal intergenic regions predominantly exhibit a stress-induced loss of accessibility.

Gene Ontology analysis of genes associated with proximal gain DARs in ZHB12 under 24 h cold treatment revealed significant enrichment of multiple biological processes (Fig. 1E). Regulatory processes, including regulation of metabolic process, regulation of nucleobase–containing compound, regulation of RNA biosynthetic process, regulation of DNA – templated transcription, and regulation of macromolecule biosynthetic process, were enriched. Stress response–related processes, such as response to abiotic stimulus, response to light stimulus, response to nutrient levels, response to lipid, and response to hormones, were also enriched. Additionally, signaling pathways, protein dephosphorylation, and hormone–mediated signaling pathways were significantly enriched. These results suggest that cold–induced chromatin opening in proximal regions may contribute to the transcriptional regulation of genes involved in stress responses, signaling, and metabolic regulation in ZHB12.

Enhanced coupling between promoter–proximal chromatin accessibility and gene expression in ZHB12 under cold stress

To investigate the relationship between promoter–proximal chromatin accessibility and gene expression divergence between ZHB12 and B73 under cold stress, we examined the overlap between differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and proximal differentially accessible regions (DARs) at 0 h and 24 h of cold treatment (Fig. 6A). At 0 h, 1,035 genes overlapped between DEGs and proximal DARs, whereas 3,167 DEGs and 5,772 proximal DARs were unique to each dataset. In contrast, after 24 h of cold treatment, the number of overlapping genes markedly increased to 2,293, accompanied by a substantial expansion of proximal DARs. These results suggest a stronger association between promoter–proximal chromatin accessibility and transcriptional regulation in ZHB12 than in B73 under cold stress. To further assess the concordance between changes in chromatin accessibility and gene expression, DEGs were classified according to the direction of expression change and the corresponding gain or loss of proximal DARs (Fig. 6B). At 0 h, downregulated genes associated with loss DARs mainly exhibited regulatory patterns. However, after 24 h of cold treatment, the number of genes showing concordant changes in promoter accessibility and gene expression increased markedly, with both DEG up+ gain DARs and DEG down+ loss DARs representing the predominant categories. These findings suggest that cold–induced chromatin remodeling at promoter regions contributes directly to transcriptional activation or repression of cold–responsive genes in ZHB12.

Given the strong concordance between promoter–proximal chromatin opening and transcriptional activation observed after 24 h of cold treatment, we focused on genes classified as DEGs up with gain DARs in ZHB12. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of this gene set revealed significant enrichment of multiple molecular functions, including transmembrane transporter activity, transporter activity, DNA binding, and protein binding (Fig. 6C). Notably, these enriched GO terms were largely consistent with those identified from the DEGs enrichment analysis. In addition, several biological processes associated with transcriptional regulation, such as regulation of metabolic process, regulation of nucleobase–containing compound, regulation of RNA biosynthetic process, regulation of DNA–templated transcription, and regulation of macromolecule biosynthetic process, were significantly overrepresented (Fig. S6). Together, these results highlight the central roles of transcriptional regulation and transport–related functions in cold stress tolerance, and suggest that cold–induced promoter accessibility contributes to the coordinated activation of regulatory and transport–associated genes in ZHB12.

In addition to the GO enrichment results, KEGG pathway analysis of genes categorized as upregulated with gain of accessibility DARs revealed a significant enrichment of transporter–related pathways. Notably, four members of the ATP–binding cassette (ABC) transporter family were identified within this specifically enriched category. To validate the biological relevance of chromatin accessibility profiles, we mapped the ATAC–seq peak distribution across candidate genes loci. Under 24 h of cold treatment, a robust and distinct enrichment of ATAC–seq signals was observed within the proximal promoter regions in ZHB12. This epigenetic signature was highly congruent with both the quantitative increase in chromatin accessibility and the observed transcriptional induction (Fig. 6D). Discussion

Low–temperature stress is a major environmental constraint limiting maize productivity, particularly during early seedling establishment when the photosynthetic apparatus and membrane systems are highly vulnerable to chilling injury. As a typical thermotolerant crop, maize lacks the robust cold–acclimation capacity found in temperate cereals, necessitating a precise and rapid molecular response to survive sudden thermal shifts [24]. In the present study, we demonstrated that the inbred line ZHB12 possesses a superior capacity for cold adaptation compared to B73, as evidenced by reduced electrolyte leakage and alleviated leaf damage (Fig. 1). These physiological hallmarks indicate that ZHB12 effectively maintains membrane stability and cellular integrity under thermal stress. At the molecular level, this resilience is underpinned by the sustained activation of photosynthesis–and energy–related pathways, specifically those associated with Photosystem I. Given that PSII is a primary target of chilling–induced photoinhibition [25], the ability of ZHB12 to preserve PSII functionality represents a critical metabolic determinant of its superior cold hardiness, ensuring the energy flux required for active defense mechanisms.

Our transcriptomic analyses further revealed that this resilience is driven by a highly dynamic, staged transcriptional reprogramming, with the 24–hour mark serving as a pivotal regulatory transition (Fig. 1). The early–stage response (1–6 h) in ZHB12 was defined by the enrichment of antioxidant systems and secondary metabolism–such as glutathione metabolism and diterpenoid biosynthesis–which likely serve to mitigate initial oxidative bursts and prime stress signaling [26]. As stress progressed into the mid–stage (12–24 h), we observed a pronounced shift toward energy–intensive processes, including ATP biosynthesis and transmembrane transport (Fig. 1). This transition suggests that ZHB12 successfully establishes a new metabolic equilibrium to sustain prolonged defense responses. The consistent enrichment of transmembrane transport pathways across the entire time course highlights their fundamental necessity for cold adaptation. In particular, the robust induction of multiple ABC transporter genes in ZHB12 suggests an enhanced capacity for the redistribution of phytohormones, lipids, and secondary metabolites. Such transport–mediated processes are vital for maintaining cellular homeostasis through detoxification and membrane stabilization [27], suggesting that efficient molecular trafficking is a hallmark of ZHB12’s adaptive strategy.

Beyond downstream transcriptional output, our ATAC–seq results provide a high–resolution view of the epigenetic architecture underlying these responses. Chromatin accessibility profiling revealed that ZHB12 undergoes substantial targeted remodeling under cold stress, characterized by an influx of accessibility toward proximal promoter regions at the expense of distal intergenic regions (Fig. 1). This redistribution suggests a strategic shift toward a more efficient transcriptional configuration. Dynamic chromatin remodeling is a central pillar of plant stress biology, as “open” chromatin states lower the physical barriers for transcription factor binding, enabling rapid gene activation [28, 29]. Similar patterns of stress–induced promoter opening have been observed in Arabidopsis and rice, where they facilitate transcriptional plasticity under adverse conditions [30–32]. Conversely, the predominance of distal accessible regions in the sensitive line B73 may represent a less “primed” or more disorganized regulatory state, potentially explaining its sluggish transcriptional response and subsequent chilling sensitivity. It is worth noting that while our two-time-point ATAC–seq strategy at 0 h and 24 h successfully captured the sustained chromatin architecture supporting the peak of transcriptomic divergence, current epigenetic paradigms suggest that immediate–early chromatin opening often precedes transcription. Future high–resolution temporal ATAC–seq profiling across the early hours of cold exposure (e.g., 1 to 12 h) will be invaluable to dissect the transient, pioneering priming events that initiate this downstream regulatory cascade. Moreover, because the B73 RefGen_v5 genome was used as the common reference for both B73 and ZHB12, some degree of reference-mapping bias may exist, particularly in structurally divergent genomic regions. Therefore, part of the observed reduction in accessible chromatin regions in ZHB12 may reflect sequence divergence or structural variation rather than true biological accessibility differences. Nevertheless, the consistent enrichment of accessible regions near promoter loci together with their association with transcriptional responses suggests that the major trends described here likely reflect biologically meaningful chromatin remodeling events.

Critically, the integration of RNA–seq and ATAC–seq datasets identified a strong epigenetic–transcriptional coupling in ZHB12, particularly following 24 h of cold exposure (Fig. 1). The marked expansion of genes exhibiting both gain–of–accessibility and up regulated expression suggests that chromatin remodeling is closely associated with transcriptional activation during cold stress. These dual–responsive genes were significantly enriched in transporter activity and transcriptional regulation, reinforcing the functional importance of these modules. Notably, the consistent behavior of specific ABC transporter genes across ATAC–seq, RNA–seq, and qRT–PCR validation (Fig. 1) supports the potential involvement of these ABC transporter genes in the coordinated cold stress response of ZHB12 [33]. This multilayered control is consistent with the established framework of Gene Regulatory Networks (GRNs), wherein environmental adaptation is governed by the seamless integration of epigenetic landscapes and transcriptional signals [34].

Taken together, our findings support a model in which ZHB12 achieves superior cold tolerance through the synchronized regulation of its genome and transcriptome. The preferential opening of proximal promoter regions, coupled with the induction of key functional modules such as ABC transporters and ATP metabolism, enables a robust and sustained response to chilling. These results not only deepen our understanding of the maize cold stress response but also identify specific regulatory elements and candidate genes that serve as high–value targets for molecular breeding and genomic engineering aimed at improving maize resilience in temperate climates.
