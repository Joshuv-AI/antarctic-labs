Desmoglein 2 is associated with dihydroartemisinin resistance in HeLa cervical cancer cells: a potential link to the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway
Authors: Yanan Liu, Shaojuan Wang, Xiaolu Xia, Tong Zhang, Xiaohang Chen, Fengxiang Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe primary challenges in the clinical treatment of cervical cancer include metastasis, recurrence, proliferation, and drug resistance. DSG2 is a transmembrane desmosomal cadherin, has been implicated in various cancers, but its specific role in cervical cancer remains unclear.Recently, Dihydroartemisinin (DHA) has emerged as a promising antitumor drug. In this study, we aimed to investigate the impact of DSG2 on cervical cancer cells treated with DHA. Cellular assays demonstrated that DHA effectively inhibited the proliferation, migration, and invasion of HeLa cells. Increasing concentrations of DHA prolonged its effects and reduced HeLa cell proliferation. RNA-seq analysis revealed that DHA induced differential gene expression involved in cell migration, protein digestion and absorption, cytokine receptor interaction, the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, and the Rap1 signaling pathway. DHA downregulated the expression of PI3K and AKT, while upregulating P53, ATM, cleaved caspase-3, and H2AX expression. Overexpression of DSG2 in HeLa cells increased resistance to DHA inhibition, whereas knockdown of DSG2 enhanced the inhibitory effects of DHA. This study highlights that DSG2 modulates the cytotoxicity of DHA, which is potentially associated with the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, suggesting DSG2 as a candidate biomarker and a potential target for further investigation in cervical cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05425-y.Keywords: Cervical cancer, DSG2, Dihydroartemisinin, Cell proliferation, PI3K/AKT signaling pathway

Introduction

According to the 2020 WHO Cancer Research Institute report, the number of newly diagnosed cases of cervical cancer in China reached 110,000, with 60,000 deaths, ranking 6th in incidence and 7th in mortality among women [1]. Dihydroartemisinin (DHA), as the primary metabolite and derivative of artemisinin, has demonstrated significant efficacy in anti-malarial treatment and has attracted considerable attention in recent years for its potential anti-tumor properties [2]. The mechanisms underlying DHA’s anti-tumor effects are intricate and varied, exerting toxic effects on tumor cells through multiple pathways. Research suggests that DHA impacts cancer cells by inducing cell cycle arrest at the G0/G1 or G2/M phase and activating apoptotic factors, ultimately leading to cell apoptosis [3–6]. The cytotoxicity of DHA is linked to its ability to induce DNA damage in various cell types, selectively promoting anti-tumor effects [7, 8]. Previous studies have demonstrated that artesunate, an artemisinin derivative, effectively inhibits the proliferation of breast cancer MCF-7 and MDA-MB231 cells by impacting genomic stability and triggering a DNA damage response. In recent years, in vitro and in vivo studies have shown DHA’s potential in reducing the incidence and progression of cervical cancer [6, 9–12]. Studies show that dihydroartemisinin can trigger cell death in cervical cancer cells by regulating cell cycle mechanisms, increasing RKIP levels, and decreasing bcl-2 levels, as well as enhancing sensitivity to radiation [9, 13] .

The expression of DSG2 has been associated with poor prognosis in cervical cancer and is implicated in the development of early cervical cancer [14, 15]. Research has highlighted the importance of the desmosomal cadherin family, to which DSG2 belongs, in tumor migration, invasion, and drug resistance [16–18]. After staining ovarian cancer sections for DSG2, it was found that certain cases of primary chemotherapy resistance exhibited heightened immunohistochemical signals for DSG2 19. Recent research has underscored the substantial interplay between DSG2 and c-MYC in modulating ADAM17 expression, a pivotal factor in the advancement of cervical cancer. This interaction implies a potential mechanism through which DSG2 may augment the proliferative and invasive capacities of cervical cancer cells [20]. Studies have shown that DSG2 may play a role in modulating the MAPK signaling pathway, a crucial regulator of cellular responses to environmental stimuli [21].Multiple studies suggests that DSG2 influences the malignant behavior of cervical cancer cells through various pathways, potentially contributing to there aggressiveness and metastatic potential [16, 22, 23]. While bioinformatics analysis and some studies have indicated that DHA may suppress tumor metastasis by inhibiting the HIF-1α/VEGF and PI3K/AKT/mTOR angiogenic pathways [24–26], the potential association between DSG2 and the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway remains poorly understood.

Limited reports exist on the expression of DSG2 and its clinicopathologic relationship to cervical cancer. Therefore, this study had two aims; (1) explore the role of DHA in HeLa cell proliferation and injury, (2) In the context of utilizing DHA as an inducer of HeLa cell damage, the study investigates the impact of DSG2 on the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway and on cell damage. This research direction is significant as it helps elucidate the molecular underpinnings of how DSG2 influences cellular responses.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

The human cervical cancer cell line HeLa (official name: HeLa; species: Homo sapiens; sex: female; tissue of origin: cervical adenocarcinoma; Research Resource Identifier, RRID: CVCL_0030) was purchased from the Cell Bank of the Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China) 6 months prior to experimental initiation to ensure cell viability and stability. Cell line authenticity was validated by short tandem repeat (STR) profiling (Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Co., Ltd. STR report No. PC-H2023121201), showing 98.5% concordance with the reference STR profile. To prevent mycoplasma contamination during cell culture, Plasmocin™ prophylactic (Invivogen, Lot: ant-mpp-39–03 A) was added to the complete medium (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium, DMEM, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum). Three experimental groups were established: control group (HeLa-EV)(Wild-type HeLa cells); DSG2 overexpression group (HeLa-DSG2); DSG2 knockout group (HeLa-KO). CRISPR/Cas9 system implementation: two sgRNAs targeting DSG2 (sgRNA1: 5´-CGCCTGGATCACCGCCCCCG-3´; sgRNA2: 5´-CTCCCTCCCGAAGAGCCACG-3´) were designed via the GPP Web Portal. The lentiCRISPRv2-sgRNA cloning primers were devised in accordance with the sgRNA, and the plasmid was extracted subsequent to vector connection. 293T cells (Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences) were cultured to logarithmic phase, and the medium containing 25 µM Chloroquine diphosphate was used for virus packaging. The transfection reagent and plasmid were mixed and incubated for a specified duration. Meanwhile, target cells were cultured and Hela cells were infected with virus solution. Post-transfection, positive cells were selected by puromycin medium and expanded. The PCR products were enzymatically digested, and the outcomes were detected by agar gel electrophoresis. Finally, the overexpression and knockout of DSG2 were detected by Western blot. The CRISPR/Cas9 experimental protocol was performed as previously described [27]. Frozen cells were rapidly thawed in a 37 °C water bath, resuspended in complete medium (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium, DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Cell pellets were seeded into T25 flasks and maintained at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ humidified incubator. Culture medium was replaced every 48 h or when cell density reached 80% confluence. Cells were dissociated with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Beyotime Biotechnology, Inc.) at 90% confluence, neutralized with complete medium, and subcultured at a 1:3 ratio. For DHA experiments, cells at 90% confluence were treated with an appropriate concentration DHA (Sigma-Aldrich). The time and concentration varied from experiment to experiment.

Expression of and survival curve for DSG2 in cervical cancer

Using the GEPIA database (version GEPIA2 2019), DSG2 gene expression was analyzed in different types of female cancers, using log2(TPM + 1) normalization, |Log2foldchange| ≥ 1, and a p-value cutoff of 0.01. The log-rank test was used to assess overall survival (OS) based on DSG2 expression in cervical cancer. The survival plots included the Cox proportional hazard ratio and a 95% confidence interval. Both cutoff-high and cutoff-low parameters were set at 50%. The Human Protein Atlas database (Version 22.0) was searched for immunohistochemical staining intensity of DSG2 in cervical cancer tissues, yielding 11 immunohistochemical staining maps.

Cell counting by crystal violet assay

When cells reached 90% confluence, they were treated with serum-free medium containing gradient concentrations of DHA (0–200 µg/mL). Following a 48-hour incubation, cells were stained with crystal violet. After solubilizing the stain in acetic acid, absorbance at 550 nm was measured using a microplate reader.

Inhibition of cell proliferation as judged by the CCK-8 assay

HeLa cells were seeded into 96-well plates, to which was added different concentrations of DHA (0–200 µg/mL). After incubation at 37℃ for 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h, 10 µL of CCK-8 (Beyotime Biotechnology, Inc.) was added to each well. The absorbance value (A) of each well at 450 nm was read with a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). Results of three replicates were averaged. Inhibition rate of cell proliferation: (%) = (A control group - A experimental group)/ (A control group -A blank group) ×100%.

Cell cycle measurement by flow cytometry

HeLa cells were divided into an experimental group and a control group. Cells in the experimental group were incubated with DHA at a concentration of 50 µg/mL at 37 °C for 48 h, while cells in the control group were incubated without DHA under the same conditions. After the incubation, they were collected and fixed overnight in 1 mL of 70% ethanol kept at -20℃. Subsequently, the cells were resuspended in 400 µL of a PI working solution (Beyotime Biotechnology, Inc.) for measurement of the cell cycle by flow cytometry (Becton-Dickinson) after 30 min of dark staining. ModFit LT (version 5.0.9) was used for data analysis.

Cell migration measured by scratch assay

HeLa cells were cultured in 6-well plates and treated with DHA at the experimental concentration of 50 µg/mL (experimental group), which was determined as the IC50 by the CCK-8 assay, or 0 µg/mL (control group) for 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h. After forming a confluent monolayer, a micropipette tip was utilized to create a wound. The width of the wound was measured at 0, 24, 48, and 72 h following cell culture.

Cell invasive capacity as judged by the Transwell assay

Matrigel (Sigma-Aldrich) was thoroughly mixed with DMEM at a volume ratio of 1:4, then each well was coated evenly with 50 µL of the mixture on the upper surface of the bottom membrane of the Transwell chamber (Corning Costar, Inc.). The number of cells inoculated in each well was 1 × 104. Medium-containing serum was added to the lower chamber and incubated for 48 h. The number of invasive cells were counted using a 10 × 10 magnification microscope.

mRNA expression verified by RT-qPCR

Cells were categorized into two groups: a control group with no DHA treatment (0 µg/mL), consisting of HeLa-KO, HeLa-EV, and HeLa-DSG2 cells; and an experimental group treated with 50 µg/mL DHA, comprising HeLa-KO-DHA, HeLa-EV-DHA, and HeLa-DSG2-DHA cells. Upon observation of enlarged cell morphology with indistinct edges, the cells were harvested. Subsequent extraction of RNA and protein was conducted for each group, thereby laying the groundwork for further experimental procedures.

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol® reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Inc.). Next, RT-qPCR was performed using the miScript SYBR®-Green PCR kit (Qiagen GmbH, Inc.). Thermo-cycling conditions for RT-qPCR were; 95℃ for 2 min, 95℃ for 20 s, 58℃ for 30 s, and 72℃ for 30 s, with a total of 40 cycles. Based on the target gene mRNA sequence in the Pubmed database, Sangon Biotech (Shanghai) Co. Ltd. was commissioned to design and synthesize primers. GAPDH was used as the experimental reference. The relative expression levels of target genes were calculated using 2−△△Ct. Primer designs are shown in Table 1.

Protein expression verified by western blot

Protein extracts were prepared with lysis buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The proteins (20 µg per lane) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and then transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with Non-Fat Powdered Milk (Sangon Biotech, Inc.) and immunoblotted with primary antibodies directed against; AKT, p-AKT, PI3K, p-PI3K, P53, p-P53, cleaved caspase-3, CHK2, p-CHK2, H2AX, γ-H2AX, DSG2 and β-actin at a dilution of 1:1,000 overnight at 4 °C. Secondary antibodies (1:5,000) were peroxidase-conjugated goat anti-rabbit or anti-mouse IgG and incubated with membranes for 1 h at 37 °C. CHK2, p-CHK2, H2AX, and γ-H2AX were re-detected on the same membrane using the stripping buffer (Beyotime Biotechnology). Protein bands were detected with a multifunctional imaging system (AZURE Biosystems), enhanced with chemiluminescence substrates. ImageJ (version 1.6) and GraphPad Prism (version 5.0) were used to measure the gray values and to quantify data.

RNA-seq and bioinformatic analysis

Transcriptome sequencing was performed by Novogene using the Illumina High-throughput sequencing platform (HiSeq 4000). FastQC (version 3.0) was used to control the quality of the raw data. In order to clarify the function of DSG2 in HeLa cells, genes with significantly different expression levels for the three comparisons (HeLa-EV-DHA vs. HeLa-EV, HeLa-EV-DHA vs. HeLa-DSG2-DHA, and HeLa-EV-DHA vs. HeLa-KO-DHA) were screened (Padj < 0.01, |log2foldchange| >1). A Venn diagram was used to demonstrate the intersection of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) for the three comparisons. Pearson’s correlation coefficient was used to analyze the relationships among DSG2 concentration and DEGs within the intersection. To explore the biological function of the DSG2, GO and KEGG enrichment analysis were carried out via R-package “Clusterprofil”. DEGs were further assessed by GO functional enrichment analysis. Raw data have been deposited in NCBI SRA (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/sra) under the accession number: SRP392594.

Statistical analysis

All experimental studies were carried out in three biological replicates. SPSS software (version 19.0) was used to perform all statistical analyses. Data are presented as means ± standard deviation for three independent experiments. Unpaired Student’s t-test was used to compare mean values of two groups. One-way ANOVA was used to compare multiple groups. Homogeneity of variance test, LSD (equal variances), and Tamhane’s T2 (unequal variances) tests were used for post-hoc pairwise comparisons. The p-value less than 0.05 was regarded as statistically significant.

Results

The up-regulation of DSG2 gene expression in cervical cancer tissues is directly related to a poor prognosis for cervical cancer patients

Through bioinformatics analysis of publicly available datasets, DSG2 expression levels retrieved from the GEPIA database showed a significant increase in various female tumors (Fig. 1A). The OS for cervical cancer patients with high DSG2 mRNA levels was significantly lower than that of patients with low DSG2 expression levels. The hazard ratio of 1.8 suggests that DSG2 is a significant risk factor for cervical cancer and is associated with an unfavorable prognosis (Fig. 1B). Immunohistochemical staining data derived from the Human Protein Atlas database (via database-based tissue expression analysis) showed that DSG2 was positively expressed in cervical cancer tissues, with high (5/11) and medium (3/11) expression levels observed (Fig. 1C).

DSG2 influences the inhibitory effect of DHA on the proliferation of HeLa cells

Before commencing the in vitro experiments, our first step was to use Western blot assays to evaluate the expression levels of DSG2 in three cell groups and the potential effects of the drug on the DSG2 gene. Our data revealed that DSG2 protein levels were decreased in the HeLa-KO group but elevated in the HeLa-DSG2 group. DHA had no effect on the DSG2 levels (Fig. 2).

Relative expression of DSG2 in HeLa-EV, HeLa-DSG2, and HeLa-KO cells assessed by western-blot analysis

The inhibitory effect of DHA on cell proliferation was examined by crystal violet assay. When the DHA concentration was 0 µg/mL, Crystal Violet staining demonstrated that the density of HeLa-DSG2 cells was significantly greater than HeLa-EV and HeLa-KO cells (Fig. 3A). When treated with DHA, cell proliferation was significantly reduced for HeLa-KO cells compared to HeLa-EV cells, while HeLa-DSG2 cell proliferation was considerably increased compared to HeLa-EV cells. DHA significantly inhibited proliferation of HeLa cells. For DHA concentrations ranging from 0 to 200 µg/mL, the inhibitory effect on cell proliferation gradually increased (Fig. 3A).

Evaluation of cell proliferation inhibition. A Crystal violet staining showing the inhibitory effects on cell proliferation after 48 h of treatment with DHA. (B-D) Time-dependent inhibition of cell proliferation quantified by CCK-8 assay at designated intervals: B 24 h, C 48 h, and D 72 h

The CCK-8 assay was employed to determine the half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of DHA in HeLa cells and evaluate the impact of DHA on cell proliferation across HeLa cell lines with differential expression of DSG2. This analysis also included assessment of both concentration- and time-dependent effects to characterize DHA’s proliferative inhibitory profile under varying DSG2 expression conditions. The results of CCK-8 showed that all concentrations of DHA could significantly inhibit the proliferation of HeLa-EV cells, and the effect was proportional to the drug concentration. The inhibition rate of cell proliferation after 48 h of DHA treatment was higher than that after 24 h, and the IC50 of DHA at 48 h was about 50 µg/mL (Fig. 3B-C). Therefore, 50 µg/mL was chosen as the DHA concentration for the experimental group. Using cck8 experiments we also found that no significant difference in DHA inhibitory effect was observed for the three HeLa cell lines at 24 h (Fig. 3B). At 48 h, inhibited proliferation inhibition was significantly decreased for HeLa-DSG2 cells, and notably increased for HeLa-KO cells compared to HeLa-EV cells (Fig. 3C, ). At 72 h, inhibitory rates among the cell population were more evident (Fig. 3D).

DSG2 affects the G2/M phase arrest of HeLa cells treated with DHA

After treatment with 50 µg/mL DHA for 48 h, the percentage of cells G0/G1 HeLa-EV-DHA cells were reduced compared to HeLa-EV cells. Hela-EV-DHA, HeLa-KO-DHA, and HeLa-DSG2-DHA cells were all arrested at G2/M. There was no significant difference in the proportion of cells in the G2/M phase between HeLa-DSG2-DHA cells and HeLa-EV-DHA cells, whereas the proportion of cells in the G2/M phase was increased in HeLa-KO-DHA cells (Fig. 4).

DSG2 affects migration and invasion by HeLa cells treated with DHA

The results of the scratch and Transwell invasion test showed that DHA significantly inhibited cell migration and invasion by the three cell lines. Both at 24 h and 48 h, the migration rate of HeLa-EV cells were significantly greater than that of Hela-EV-DHA cells (Fig. 5A-B). Compared to HeLa-EV-DHA, the migration rate of HeLa-KO-DHA cells were decreased significantly, while the migration rate of HeLa-DSG2-DHA cells were not different at 24 h, but did increase at 48 h (Fig. 5C-D). Compared to Hela-EV-DHA cells, the number of invasive cells were reduced for HeLa-KO-DHA cells and increased for HeLa-DSG2-DHA cells. DSG2 overexpression enhanced the number of invasive Hela cells in the absence of DHA treatment (Fig. 6). Migration and invasion results may be partially influenced by altered proliferation, but the consistent phenotypic differences support the conclusion that DSG2 attenuates DHA response.

Discussion

Research indicates that DHA can effectively inhibit the proliferation of cervical cancer cells in a time-and dose-dependent manner [28]. This inhibitory effect is believed to be linked to the increased production of reactive oxygen species caused by the peroxide bridge structure of DHA. This, in turn, affects gene stability, leads to double-strand DNA breaks, and influences DNA transcription. Previous studies [29] found that artemisinin and its derivatives target Topoisomerase 1, impacting DNA transcription.DNA topoisomerases 1 and 2 play crucial roles in maintaining DNA topology and carrying out essential genomic functions such as DNA replication and transcription [30]. In our study, bioinformatics analysis revealed that DHA affects key processes in HeLa cells, including translation, DNA replication, and transcription. Notably, the p53 pathway, vital for cell survival, was significantly enriched. DHA was found to enhance the expression of early DNA damage biomarkers and important DNA repair genes, such as H2AX and CHK2, along with an increase in p53 expression. Several studies have yielded similar results to ours, indicating that when DNA double-strand breaks occur, the ATM protein becomes phosphorylated, leading to downstream phosphorylation of molecules such as CHK2, ultimately resulting in cell cycle arrest at the G2/M phase [31, 32]. Phosphorylated ATM inhibits the cell cycle by activating the p53 pathway and downstream Caspase-3, affecting cell migration, invasion, and promoting apoptosis [33]. Iintegrating bioinformatics analysis, experimental validation, and literature review, these findings support that DHA exerts its cytotoxic effects in HeLa cells by triggering a DNA damage response and inhibiting cell growth, potentially involving the ATM/p53 pathway.

Our research, utilizing database searches of TCGA and Human Protein Atlas, revealed a positive correlation between DSG2 expression in cervical cancer tissues and poor prognosis, suggesting it as a potential risk factor for cervical cancer. Through literature review and bioinformatics analysis of public databases, we found DSG2 dysregulation is implicated in multiple cancer types [14, 20, 22, 23]. Our in vitro experiments further confirmed that DSG2 enhances cervical cancer cell proliferation and migration, accelerating tumor progression—findings that are consistent with both database-derived insights and prior literature. Despite a lack of research on the interaction between DSG2 and drugs, our experiments demonstrated that DHA does not alter the expression of DSG2 in HeLa cells. Upon literature review, we found that in Jonathan’s study, camptothecin, a topoisomerase poison, elicited a DNA damage response in cells. Gene chip analysis detected no notable alterations in the abundance of DSG2 mRNA in camptothecin-treated HeLa cells [34], aligning with our findings. Therefore, we employed DHA as an inducer of cellular damage in our experiments, observing its inhibitory effects on the proliferation, invasion, and migration of HeLa cells. Overexpression of DSG2 partially reversed the damaging effects of DHA, while knockdown of DSG2 enhanced these effects. To delve into the signaling pathways of DSG2, we investigated its role in cellular damage induced by DHA. Bioinformatics analysis unveiled a strong correlation between the differential expression of DSG2 and genes such as CPA4, LYPD6B, and TMC5. Literature review indicated that these genes serve not only as cancer biomarkers but are also closely linked to the activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway [35–38]. This pathway and its associated genes stood out prominently in our RNA-seq analysis of differentially expressed genes. Therefore, we have identified the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway as a key focus for our ongoing research. Our experiments revealed that DHA reduces the phosphorylation levels of PI3K and AKT in HeLa cells, leading to the inhibition of cell proliferation and exhibiting anticancer effects. Overexpression of DSG2 promotes the phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT, partially counteracts the effect of DHA on PI3K phosphorylation levels. Additionally, the expression of ATM and H2AX, linked to DNA damage, decreased. These results suggest that DSG2 is potentially associated with the PI3K/AKT pathway and may attenuate the DNA damage response induced by DHA. In a study by Wang et al. [39] it was observed that dephosphorylation and inactivation of PI3K/AKT enhanced the tumor suppressor function of the ATM. Our experimental findings align with this, as we noted that in DSG2-knockdown cells, the PI3K/AKT pathway was suppressed, leading to increased ATM activity and reduced cell proliferation. Reduction in AKT expression inhibits the entry of phosphorylated substrate proteins into the nucleus, activating p53 [40]. In our study, DSG2-deficient HeLa cells exhibited decreased AKT expression but increased levels of P53 and activated caspase-3. This process plays a crucial role in regulating cell proliferation and programmed cell death [32]. Activation of the P53 signaling pathway boosts H2AX expression, enhancing the DNA damage response and forming a positive feedback loop to inhibit tumor cell proliferation [41]. The findings are consistent with our results, indicating that treatment with DHA activated the DNA damage response in HeLa cells, leading to increased expression of H2AX and ATM. Furthermore, the inhibitory effect of DHA on HeLa cells was augmented by activating the P53 signaling pathway through DSG2 knockdown.

The role of DSG2 in the PI3K/AKT pathway remains partially understood, despite its varied functions observed in different tumor types. The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is a classic oncogenic pathway. Although our study did not explore its primary molecular mechanisms, we aim to investigate whether differential expression of DSG2 can inhibit cancer development. Studies have shown that DSG2 can directly interact with PI3K in intestinal epithelial cells, and overexpression of DSG2 activates the PI3K/AKT pathway in skin cancer [42, 43]. Studies have shown that the loss of DSG2 can trigger the activation of the PI3K/AKT and EGFR/AKT signaling pathways, promoting cell invasion and migration in gastric cancer cells, as well as stimulating inflammatory responses in intestinal epithelial cells [22, 42]. In contrast, reduced DSG2 expression in cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma did not impact Erk1/2 or AKT phosphorylation [44]. However, inhibition of DSG2 in cervical cancer cells decreased the expression of p-MEK and p-ERK proteins, thereby hindering the proliferation, migration, and invasion capabilities of cervical cancer cells [21]. These findings align with our research in cervical cancer, where we observed that DSG2 influenced the MAPK pathway, the PI3K/AKT pathway, and other pathways. While discrepancies exist in previously published data, Our results indicate that in cervical cancer cells, DSG2 is associated with activation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, which in turn correlates with reduced p53 activity and enhanced cell survival, thereby promoting DHA resistance. From our study, we also found that DSG2 may be related to the mechanism of cancer drug-resistance. Although our in vitro experiments demonstrate a significant correlation, Whether DSG2 acts as a direct upstream regulator of the PI3K/AKT pathway remains unclear, and further validation using Co-IP or pharmacological inhibitor assays is required due to the complexity of signaling networks. Nevertheless, our RNA-seq data reveal that the PI3K/AKT pathway is significantly enriched in the DSG2 differential expression groups by KEGG analysis, providing strong transcriptomic evidence supporting this hypothesis. The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, which regulates multiple downstream target proteins, is widely recognized as a key driver of carcinogenesis and drug resistance in various cancers. Notably, DSG2 not only modulates the PI3K/AKT pathway but also interacts with multiple other tumor growth-related pathways [45]. DSG2 has been shown to modulate intercellular adhesion, promote cell migration, and interact with complex signaling network [46, 47]. Structurally, DSG2 is a major component of desmosomes, and studies have shown that the molecular penetration barrier formed by DSG2 connecting cells to each other confers resistance to therapeutic drugs [19].

Limitations of the current study and suggestions for subsequent research: (1) Although RNA‑seq and KEGG enrichment indicated a potential association between DSG2 and the PI3K/AKT pathway, these analyses only show correlation, not direct mechanism. Our focus was on validating the PI3K/AKT pathway using Western blot experiments to assess differences in PI3K, AKT, P53, and cleaved caspase-3 protein levels. However, due to time constraints, we did not investigate cyclin-related proteins, which represents a limitation of this study. There is an association between DSG2 expression and PI3K/AKT activation, but the exact mechanism—whether direct or indirect—requires further investigation. Future studies could employ pharmacological inhibition assays or co-immunoprecipitation experiments to validate direct protein-protein interactions. (2) While DSG2 was found to be enriched in multiple pathways, we chose to validate its effects specifically in the PI3K/AKT pathway. It is worth noting that the effects of DSG2 on signaling pathways were complex. Therefore,, for future research, we plan to delve deeper into exploring the relationships among upstream and downstream targets of DSG2 by investigating other significantly enriched pathways. This comprehensive approach will provide a more thorough understanding of the role of DSG2 in cellular signaling and its implications in cancer progression. (3) Our experimental focus was on the HeLa cell line, and we did not utilize other cervical cancer cell lines such as SiHa and CaKi. However, data analysis from the GEO database revealed that the expression levels of DSG2 in HeLa cells closely resemble those in CaSki cells, with only minor variations compared to SiHa cells. closely resemble those in CaSki cells, with only minor variations compared to SiHa cells [48–50]. Synthesizing the information above, we have chosen the HeLa cell line as our experimental model to explore the biological characteristics of cervical cancer. Although the present study was primarily conducted in the HeLa cell line, we validated the prognostic value of DSG2 using TCGA‑CESC clinical data and confirmed its protein expression via the Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database. These findings indicate that our results carry clinical relevance and are not merely restricted to a cell‑line‑specific phenomenon. While our choice of the HeLa cell line was based on its wide utility in cervical cancer research, we recognize the importance of incorporating other cervical cancer cell lines to fully grasp the complexity of the disease. Thus, our current research serves as an initial exploration in this field, with future endeavors aimed at encompassing a broader spectrum of cell lines to enhance our understanding. (4) To enrich the scope of our research, we intend to integrate 3D cell culture technology, which better mimics the in vivo tumor microenvironment, offering more reliable data for drug screening and efficacy evaluation. Additionally, by establishing animal models, we aim to validate our findings in a physiological context, enhancing the clinical relevance of our research. Literature has documented an upsurge in DSG2 expression from low-grade to high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesions in the cervix [51]. Our subsequent experiments seek to bridge the gap between basic research and clinical practice by collecting clinical samples from varying degrees of cervical inflammation and cancer. Several limitations should be acknowledged in the present study. First, although RNA-seq analysis indicates that DSG2 may modulate drug resistance via the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, rescue experiments using specific pharmacological inhibitors (e.g., LY294002) were not performed to provide definitive mechanistic proof. Second, all functional assays were conducted exclusively in the HeLa cervical cancer cell line. To strengthen our findings, we integrated multi-center clinical data from TCGA, GTEx, and Human Protein Atlas (HPA) databases, which improved the reliability and translational potential of our conclusions. Further investigations will be required to validate these observations in additional cervical cancer cell lines and in vivo models. We believe that through these multidimensional research approaches, we can acquire more comprehensive data, laying a robust scientific groundwork for the diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of cervical cancer. Our research not only contributes to advancing the understanding of cervical cancer within the scientific community but also holds promise for the development of more effective treatment modalities for patients.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study revealed that DHA effectively inhibits the proliferation of HeLa cells and induces DNA damage. Additionally, DSG2 was found to enhance HeLa cell proliferation, likely through modulation of the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, which correlates with reduced p53 and caspase‑3 activity, thereby being associated with resistance to DHA‑induced damage in HeLa cells.
