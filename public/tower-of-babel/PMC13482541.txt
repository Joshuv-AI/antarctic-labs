Impact of weight loss and reduction of ultra-processed foods on liver fat content in MASLD: A randomized controlled trial
Authors: Simona Bo, Angelo Armandi, Gabriele Castelnuovo, Ilaria Goitre, Fabio Bioletto, Marta Guariglia, Nuria Perez Diaz Del Campo, Eleonora Dileo, Valentina Ponzo, Francesca Saba, Chiara Rosso, Gian Paolo Caviglia, Elisabetta Bugianesi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & AimsDietary interventions are a cornerstone of management for metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). However, it remains unclear whether specific dietary patterns confer benefits beyond weight loss. We aimed to compare the effects of different dietary strategies on liver fat and metabolic health in patients with MASLD.MethodsWe conducted a 6-month, three-arm, parallel-group randomized controlled trial including patients with MASLD and overweight or obesity who were naïve to structured nutritional counselling. Participants were randomized to Mediterranean diet (MeD), low-carbohydrate, high-protein diet (LCHO), or standard nutritional recommendations. Liver fat content was assessed by controlled attenuation parameter (CAP). Secondary outcomes included changes in anthropometric and metabolic parameters, body composition, and ultra-processed food (UPF) consumption. Structural equation modelling was used to investigate pathways linking dietary allocation, weight loss, and UPF intake with changes in CAP.ResultsOf 173 enrolled participants, 148 (85.5%) completed the study. CAP decreased significantly within the LCHO and control groups (Δ −19.5 [−48.8 to 8.0], p = 0.004 and −11.0 [−32.0 to 12.0], p <0.001), with no significant between-group differences. All study arms achieved significant improvements in body weight, BMI, adiposity measures, metabolic profile, and dietary quality, including reductions in UPF intake. Structural equation modelling analyses showed no direct or total effect of dietary pattern on changes in CAP. In contrast, reductions in BMI and UPF consumption independently and significantly predicted CAP improvements: β = 14.34 (95% CI 10.55-18.13), and β = 0.64 (95% CI 0.25-1.03).ConclusionsIn patients with MASLD, weight loss and reductions in UPF intake, rather than diet type, were the main drivers of improvements in liver fat and metabolic health. These findings support a patient-centered dietary approach in MASLD management.Clinical trial numberNCT073043336.Impact and implicationsThis randomized controlled trial shows that weight loss and reductions in ultra-processed food intake, rather than the specific dietary pattern, are the primary factors associated with improvements in liver fat and metabolic health in MASLD. These findings challenge the emphasis on prescriptive dietary models and support a more flexible, patient-centered approach focused on sustainable weight reduction and improved dietary quality. Prioritizing reduction of ultra-processed foods may represent a practical and scalable strategy to enhance adherence and maximize long-term clinical benefit in routine care.Keywords: lifestyle intervention, steatotic liver disease, vibration-controlled transient elastography, bioelectrical impedence analysis, metabolic syndrome

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most prevalent chronic liver disease worldwide and represents the hepatic manifestation of systemic metabolic dysfunction.1 It is characterized by excessive hepatic fat accumulation in the context of obesity, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and the metabolic syndrome, and is associated with increased liver-related and cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Lifestyle modification remains the cornerstone of MASLD management, with particular emphasis on dietary interventions and weight reduction.

Current evidence indicates that a 5–10% reduction in body weight is associated with meaningful histological improvement, including regression of steatosis, resolution of steatohepatitis, and, at higher degrees of weight loss, regression of fibrosis.2 While caloric restriction is broadly effective,3 growing evidence suggests that dietary composition, beyond energy deficit alone, may influence MASLD outcomes.4 The Mediterranean diet (MeD), characterized by a high intake of plant-based foods, extra-virgin olive oil, and moderate consumption of fish and dairy products, has been associated with improvements in hepatic steatosis and cardiometabolic risk factors.5,6 Its favorable antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and metabolic profile has led to its endorsement by international guidelines for the management of MASLD.7,8 Low-carbohydrate dietary approaches, including low-carbohydrate (LCHO) diets, have also been reported to improve liver fat content and metabolic parameters, primarily through weight loss and improvements in insulin sensitivity.9 Nonetheless, comparative studies have yielded heterogeneous results, with evidence supporting either modest advantages, non-inferiority, or no clear differences between dietary strategies when matched for energy restriction.5,10,11

More recently, attention has shifted towards the role of dietary quality and food processing. Diets characterized by high consumption of ultra-processed foods (UPF) – typically rich in added sugars, refined carbohydrates, saturated fats, and food additives – have been consistently associated with increased risk of MASLD and progression to more severe liver outcomes. Proposed mechanisms include excess energy intake, promotion of hepatic de novo lipogenesis, insulin resistance, visceral adiposity, chronic inflammation, and gut dysbiosis.[12], [13], [14] Observational studies have shown that higher UPF intake is independently associated with both incident MASLD and disease progression.15 However, interventional evidence addressing the impact of reducing UPF consumption on hepatic steatosis remains scarce.

Therefore, the primary aim of the present study was to compare the effects of three dietary strategies – MeD, LCHO diet, and standard nutritional recommendations – on liver fat content assessed non-invasively by controlled attenuation parameter (CAP) in patients with MASLD who were naïve to structured nutritional counselling. Secondary objectives included evaluating within- and between-group changes in anthropometric measures, metabolic profile, body composition, and changes in UPF consumption to improvements in hepatic steatosis.

Materials and methods

Trial design

This was a 6-month, three-arm parallel group, randomized controlled trial. Eligible participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1:1 ratio to receive a MeD, LCHO diet, or standard nutritional recommendations (control group). Owing to the nature of the intervention, the study was open-label. However, all personnel involved in data collection and statistical analyses were blinded to group allocation.

Study population

Individuals with confirmed MASLD were screened for eligibility. MASLD was diagnosed based on the presence of hepatic steatosis in association with at least one cardiometabolic risk factor and in the absence of alternative causes of liver disease, in accordance with the most recent EASL–EASD–EASO Clinical Practice Guidelines.7 Hepatic steatosis was defined by a CAP value ≥248 dB/m assessed by vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE).

Screening and enrollment were conducted at the Liver Outpatient Clinics of the “Città della Salute e della Scienza” University Hospital (Turin, Italy) between February 2022 and September 2024.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: age ≥18 years; BMI ≥25 and <35 kg/m2; confirmed diagnosis of MASLD; and no previous exposure to structured nutritional counselling for MASLD.

Exclusion criteria included: advanced liver disease, defined by radiological or histological evidence of cirrhosis or liver stiffness measurement >15 kPa; other causes of liver disease (including viral, autoimmune, cholestatic, genetic, alcoholic, or drug-induced liver disease); medical conditions requiring specific dietary prescriptions; history of significant alcohol consumption, defined as ≥140 g/week in women and ≥210 g/week in men (or ≥20 g/day in women and ≥30 g/day in men); decompensated diabetes mellitus (HbA1c >9.5%); current use of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists; pharmacological treatments potentially interfering with study outcomes or inducing hepatic steatosis (e.g. corticosteroids, methotrexate, valproic acid, estrogen–progestin compounds); pregnancy or breastfeeding; inability to provide written informed consent; and life expectancy <1 year.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was changes in CAP after 6 months of dietary intervention, both within and between study arms. Secondary outcomes included: changes in anthropometric measures, body composition, dietary intake and quality, metabolic parameters, and UPF consumption.

Interventions

All participants received standardized verbal and written nutritional information by three trained dieticians during a 90-minute individual counselling session. Specifically, patients were given information on the importance of a healthy lifestyle and recommendations to abstain from alcohol consumption, and reduce fructose and UPF intake. The educational session included practical tips about lowering fat and salt intake, reducing beverages, packaged snacks and food with added sugars, choosing unprocessed/minimally processed foods, and strategies for out-of-home eating and healthy food shopping, portion control, recommended frequency of food consumption, identification of common dietary mistakes, and how to include physical movement into habitual activities. Participants were advised to perform at least 150 minutes of moderate physical activity per week, including approximately 20 minutes of brisk walking per day (11 to 13 on the Borg Rating of Perceived Exertion scale).16

Participants in the control group did not receive personalized dietary prescriptions beyond this standardized counselling.

In addition, participants randomized to the MeD and LCHO arms received personalized dietary plans, including both verbal and written recommendations, with total energy intake tailored to individual estimated requirements. The prescribed macronutrient distributions were as follows:

-MeD: 45–60% carbohydrates, 15% protein, 25-35% fats;

MeD: 45–60% carbohydrates, 15% protein, 25-35% fats;

-LCHO: 35–40% carbohydrates, 30% protein, 30–35% fats.

LCHO: 35–40% carbohydrates, 30% protein, 30–35% fats.

Participants in both MeD and LCHO arms were encouraged to consume plant-based foods, use olive oil as the main culinary fat, and limit intake of red and processed meats and added sugars. In the MeD arm, moderate consumption of fish, poultry, and dairy products was specifically encouraged. Recommended cereal portions were larger in the MeD arm compared with the LCHO arm. Detailed dietary recommendations are summarized in Table S1.

The use of nutritional supplements was prohibited throughout the study. Participants were instructed to maintain stable pharmacological treatments, without changes in type or dosage, for the duration of the trial.

Metabolic equivalents were obtained using IPAQ by multiplying the reported minutes per week of activity at each intensity by a standardized metabolic equivalent value assigned to that activity category.

Weight and height were measured with the participants wearing light clothes and no shoes, respectively, by a mechanical-column scale (SECA model 711, Hamburg, Germany) and a Stadiometer (SECA 220 measuring rod, Hamburg, Germany). Waist and neck circumferences were assessed by a plastic-tape measure at the umbilicus level or under the cricoid cartilage, respectively.

Single-frequency bioelectrical impedance (BIA 101, Akern, Florence Italy) was used to assess body composition, after voiding and at least 8 h-fasting, with patients lightly dressed, without heavy physical activity during the preceding 12 hours. Resistance (R) and reactance (Xc) were normalized for subject's height (R/h and Xc/h, Ohm/m) to eliminate conductor length effect, and the bioelectrical impedance vector analysis (BIVA) software was employed. Phase angle, total water (as percentage of body weight) extra-cellular water and intracellular water (both as a percentage of total water), as well as fat mass index (FMI: fat mass/height2) and muscle mass index (muscle mass/height2) were measured.

Indirect calorimetry (Q-NRG Metabolic Monitor, Cosmed, Rome, Italy) was used to assess resting metabolic rate, by measuring the inspired and expired concentrations of oxygen and carbon dioxide, which reflect nutrient metabolism. The canopy was placed over the face and carefully checked to prevent air leakage. The exams were performed in a quiet room with a temperature kept at 23–25 °C, with patients awake, motionless, and silent, in a supine position, after at least 12 h fasting.

Arterial blood pressure was measured from the left arm, in a sitting position, after at least 10 min of rest, with a mercury sphygmomanometer with appropriate cuff sizes (ERKA Perfect-Aneroid, Germany). Two measurements were taken by trained personnel; the values reported were the means of the two measurements.

The following indices were calculated: the HOMA-IR (homeostasis model assessment of insulin resistance), using the formula fasting glucose (mmol/L) × fasting insulin (μU/ml)/22.5, and the FIB-4 index.

Liver stiffness measurement and CAP were assessed by VCTE using a FibroScan device (Echosens, Paris, France), using the M or XL probe as appropriate, with measurements considered reliable when at least 10 valid acquisitions were obtained and the interquartile range/median ratio was <30%.

Follow-up and adherence monitoring

Participants from all study arms were evaluated at 1, 3, and 6 months after enrollment. At each visit, adherence to dietary recommendations and physical activity advice was assessed, and the occurrence of adverse events was recorded. In addition, a structured telephone interview was conducted between the fourth and fifth month to reinforce adherence and monitor potential adverse events. Participants recorded all foods and beverages consumed over two consecutive weekdays and one weekend day. Adherence to the MeD was assessed by the Medi-Lite score. All baseline and 6-month assessments were included in the present analyses.

Ethics

Participants provided written informed consent to participate. The present study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval by the Ethics Committee of A.O.U. Città della Salute e della Scienza di Torino (CEI/492/2021, 9/11/2021). The trial was registered at www.clinicaltrials.gov under the identifier NCT073043336.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for the primary outcome (relative change in liver steatosis, estimated by CAP) was determined a priori under a one-way ANOVA framework. The calculation was informed by effect estimates from Holmer et al.,17 assuming relative reductions in CAP of 30% in both the LCHO and MeD groups and 15% in the control group, and a common within-group standard deviation of 20%. Under these assumptions, considering equal allocation across the three groups (1:1:1), a two-sided α of 0.05, and 80% power, a minimum of 81 participants (27 per group) completing the study was required. However, to ensure adequate precision and stability of estimates in the investigation of the pathways linking dietary assignments and changes in steatosis progression – including the simultaneous estimation of approximately 15 structural paths in a structural equation model (SEM) framework – we targeted a total sample of 150 participants (50 per group) completing the study. Allowing for approximately 15% dropout, the study was planned to enroll approximately 175 participants.

Randomization: Sequence generation and allocation concealment

Participants were randomly allocated to one of the treatment arms (1:1:1) using a covariate-adaptive biased-coin randomization protocol, following the algorithm described by Frane.18 The randomization software was implemented by an independent statistical expert not involved in patients’ enrollment, using STATA 17 (StataCorp, College Station, Texas, USA). The allocation of the first enrolled patient was completely random. Afterwards, at each assignment, the algorithm computed the prospective marginal imbalance across key prespecified covariates (age, sex, and BMI) for assigning the participant to each of the three treatment arms. The arm that minimized the post-allocation imbalance was favored with an allocation probability of p = 0.67, while the other two arms were disfavored with an allocation probability of p = 0.165 each. No additional blocking or fixed strata were used beyond the adaptive algorithm. Allocation concealment was maintained by revealing the assignment only after eligibility confirmation and baseline data entry. The trial investigators responsible for patient enrollment had no access to past nor future allocations, thus preventing the possibility to predict or influence the assignment of participants. At least one author had access to all of the data and can vouch for the integrity of the data analyses.

Statistical analysis

The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to test normality of the data. Within-group differences were assessed respectively by t-test for paired data or Wilcoxon matched-pairs test. Between-group differences were tested by ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test, as appropriate. Changes in the variables (deltas) were calculated as: final value minus baseline value of the variables. Most delta values were not-normally distributed. Nonparametric tests were used for all comparisons of delta values as a conservative approach, given that the delta values were often normally distributed in one group but not in the others (Statistica, ver. 7.0; StatSoft Inc., Tulsa, OK, USA).

Dietary data were analyzed using Win Food Pro 3 software (Medimatica, Colonnella, Italy), and mean daily nutrient intakes were calculated.19 Each food item was classified according to the NOVA classification20 into the following categories according to the extent of food processing: 1) fresh or minimally processed foods (e.g. fruits and vegetables, meat and fish); 2) processed culinary ingredients (e.g. honey, butter); 3) processed foods with salt, sugar, or oil, i.e. foods from class I or class II that undergo further industrial manipulations to make them more tasty and better preserved (e.g. canned or bottled vegetables and legumes, canned fish); 4) UPF (e.g. carbonated drinks, processed meat, sweets, salty snacks, etc.).

To estimate total consumption of UPF, the amount consumed daily (g/day) of each food from the group 4 of the NOVA classification was summed. The mean percentage of UPF with respect to the assumed total daily energy was calculated as the sum of the mean daily caloric value of each consumed item from group 4 divided by the mean total daily caloric intake.

To investigate the pathways linking dietary assignments and changes in steatosis progression, we estimated a SEM with delta CAP as the outcome measure. Dietary assignments were modeled by two dummy variables coding the LCHO and MeD groups vs. the control group. Mediators were the change in BMI (delta BMI) and the change in daily intake of UPFs (delta UPF expressed as % energy), with delta UPF also specified as a potential predictor of delta BMI to allow for a sequential mediation pathway. Baseline covariates entered as exogenous variables were sex, age, baseline BMI, baseline UPF intake, and baseline CAP. Structural equation modelling was conducted using the lavaan package (version 0.6-20) in R (version 4.5.1). A mixed-effects model with repeated CAP measurements at baseline and 6 months was also fitted to assess the consistency of the findings across different analytical approaches.

Anthropometric measures and body composition

All three study arms achieved significant within-group reductions in body weight, BMI, waist circumference, and neck circumference after 6 months of intervention (Table 2). The magnitude of these changes did not differ significantly between groups.

BIA revealed significant reductions in FMI and increases in total body water across all groups, without significant between-group differences. No significant changes were observed in muscle mass index, phase angle, or intracellular and extracellular water distribution.

Physical activity and energy expenditure

Self-reported physical activity, assessed by IPAQ, increased significantly within all study arms over the intervention period, with no significant between-group differences. Sitting time decreased modestly in all groups.

Resting energy expenditure, measured by indirect calorimetry, showed a small but statistically significant reduction in the LCHO group, whereas no significant changes were observed in the MeD or control groups. Between-group differences were not significant.

Metabolic and biochemical parameters

Changes in metabolic and biochemical parameters are reported in Table 2. In the control group, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase and gamma glutamyltransferase decreased significantly over the intervention period. In the LCHO group, fasting insulin levels and HOMA-IR improved significantly, whereas no significant changes were observed in the MeD group. Lipid profiles showed modest changes across groups, with no consistent between-group differences.

High-sensitivity C-reactive protein decreased significantly in the control group only. No significant between-group differences were observed for any biochemical parameter.

Dietary intake and dietary quality

All study arms reported significant reductions in total energy intake over the 6-month intervention period (Table 2). Intake of saturated fatty acids decreased significantly in all groups, whereas changes in total fat, carbohydrate, and fiber intake were modest and broadly comparable between groups.

Adherence to MeD, assessed by the Medi-Lite score, increased significantly in the LCHO and control groups, while remaining stable in the MeD group, reflecting the higher baseline adherence in this arm. Among all participants included in the final analytical sample, 45 (30.4%; 14, 13, and 18 in the LCHO, MeD, and control arms, respectively) met the criteria for potential under-reporting (Supplementary Methods).

UPF consumption decreased significantly in all three study arms, both in absolute terms (g/day) and as a percentage of total daily energy intake. No significant between-group differences were observed in the magnitude of UPF reduction.

Path analysis

The SEM results are summarized in Fig. 2 and Table S3. Baseline CAP was strongly and inversely associated with subsequent changes in CAP (β = −0.29, 95% CI −0.44 to −0.15), indicating greater reductions in liver fat among participants with higher baseline steatosis.

Path analysis of factors associated with changes in hepatic steatosis.

Structural equation model illustrating the direct and indirect pathways linking dietary assignment, changes in BMI, changes in UPF intake, and changes in CAP. Standardised estimates and corresponding 95% CIs are reported for each path. CAP, controlled attenuation parameter; LCHO, low-carbohydrate; MeD, Mediterranean diet; UPF, ultra-processed food.

Neither assignment to the MeD nor to the LCHO diet showed a statistically significant direct or total effect on changes in CAP when compared with the control group. Estimated direct effects were 10.47 dB/m (95% CI −2.87 to 23.81) for the LCHO group and 10.78 dB/m (95% CI −3.53 to 25.10) for the MeD group. Corresponding total effects were 2.47 dB/m (95% CI −13.01 to 17.95) and 11.99 dB/m (95% CI −5.05 to 29.02), respectively.

In contrast, reductions in BMI and UPF intake were independently and significantly associated with improvements in CAP. Greater reductions in BMI were associated with larger decreases in CAP (β = 14.34, 95% CI 10.55 to 18.13), and greater reductions in UPF intake were also associated with larger improvements in CAP (β = 0.64, 95% CI 0.25 to 1.03).

Baseline UPF intake was strongly associated with subsequent reductions in UPF consumption (β = −0.71, 95% CI −0.88 to −0.55), indicating that participants with higher baseline UPF intake achieved greater dietary improvements. No significant sequential mediation effects through UPF reduction followed by BMI change were observed.

Of note, in a mixed-effects model with repeated CAP measurements at baseline and 6 months, the associations between dietary assignment and CAP change over time were not statistically significant, confirming the findings of the main analyses (time × LCHO: p = 0.39; time × MeD: p = 0.13).

Sensitivity analyses replacing BMI with the BIA-derived FMI yielded largely comparable findings. Notably, unlike baseline BMI, baseline FMI significantly predicted its own reduction during follow-up and exhibited a significant total effect on changes in CAP (Fig. S1; Table S4).

We then performed a sensitivity analysis after excluding participants with potential under-reporting (n = 45) (Fig. S2; Table S5). The results were largely consistent with those of the original SEM, as changes in BMI and UPF intake remained significantly associated with reductions in CAP: β = 13.26 (95% CI 8.45 to 17.86) and 0.54 (95% CI 0.05 to 1.03), respectively.

Discussion

In this randomized controlled trial including patients with MASLD who were naïve to structured nutritional counselling, no single dietary strategy demonstrated clear superiority in reducing hepatic steatosis over a 6-month period. Regardless of dietary assignment, all study arms achieved clinically meaningful improvements in anthropometric measures, dietary quality, and metabolic parameters. Importantly, reductions in BMI and ultra-processed food intake emerged as the strongest independent predictors of improvements in liver fat content, whereas dietary composition per se did not exert a significant direct effect.

The positive association between reductions in BMI and improvements in CAP observed in our study reinforces the central role of weight loss in the management of MASLD. The magnitude of CAP reduction was proportional to the degree of weight loss, consistent with evidence demonstrating that even modest weight reductions can lead to significant improvements in hepatic steatosis.21 A seminal study by Vilar-Gomez et al., conducted in 293 patients with biopsy-proven MASLD undergoing 52-week lifestyle intervention, showed that weight loss of at least 5% led to histological improvement of steatosis. Higher weight loss could lead to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis resolution and improvement in fibrosis (7% and 10% weight loss, respectively).22

CAP is a widely available and cost-effective surrogate marker of hepatic fat content23 that correlates well with histological steatosis and with longitudinal changes, particularly for changes exceeding 38 dB/m.24 Changes in CAP exceeding 15% have been associated with clinically meaningful reductions in liver fat assessed by proton density fat fraction, supporting its use as an outcome measure in lifestyle intervention trials.25

Short-term hypocaloric (e.g. 1,000 kcal/daily) diet26 or gluten-free diets27 have been associated with CAP improvements in MASLD, supporting its longitudinal use for assessing hepatic response to lifestyle treatment. In longer and more structured dietary intervention trials, CAP decreased significantly from baseline and showed a progressive decline over the course of the intervention, with greater improvements observed in participants with higher baseline CAP values.28 In particular, the close connection between weight loss and CAP decrease was supported in a 6-month lifestyle intervention based only on nutritional advice.29

In line with previous reports,30 participants with higher baseline CAP values experienced greater reductions over the 6-month intervention period, reflecting a greater potential for improvement among individuals with more pronounced steatosis at enrollment.

Neither MeD nor LCHO diet demonstrated superiority over standard nutritional recommendations in terms of CAP reduction at 6 months.

SEM further confirmed the absence of both direct and total effects of dietary assignment on changes in liver fat. These findings suggest that, over a relatively short timeframe, the magnitude of caloric reduction and associated weight loss may play a more prominent role than specific macronutrient distribution in determining short-term improvements in hepatic steatosis.

Importantly, baseline adherence to the Mediterranean dietary pattern was already relatively high in participants allocated to the MeD arm, with minimal improvement observed during the intervention (Table 2). This limited room for dietary modification may have attenuated the potential to detect incremental benefits attributable to further optimization of the MeD pattern. This potential ceiling effect should be considered when interpreting the lack of apparent superiority of the MeD intervention.

The lack of superiority of LCHO or MeD over standard dietary advice when energy deficits are similar is consistent with prior evidence.5,6 Moreover, standardized lifestyle counseling across all groups likely reduced inter-arm differentiation, while heterogeneous metabolic responses and the multifactorial pathophysiology of MASLD may have further influenced individual trajectories. These findings suggest that when baseline adherence is already moderate-to-high and caloric restriction is achieved across intervention arms, additional short-term improvements in hepatic steatosis may be modest.

An exploratory finding of this study was the association between reductions in UPF intake and improvements in hepatic steatosis, even after adjustment for weight loss. All study arms achieved significant reductions in UPF consumption, reflecting the effectiveness of the standardized dietary counselling delivered to participants. Individuals with higher baseline UPF intake exhibited greater reductions over time, suggesting considerable potential for dietary improvement among those with less favorable initial dietary habits. The consumption of UPFs has increased globally and now represents a substantial component of Western dietary patterns.31 A recent meta-analysis of 17 observational studies supported the association between high UPF intake and incident liver disease, including MASLD.33 Longitudinal studies suggest that reducing UPF consumption may be accompanied by improvements in MASLD biomarkers and measurable decreases in intrahepatic fat content, particularly within the context of whole-food–based patterns, in a dose-response manner.14,15,32

In the present trial, changes in UPF intake were not randomized and may reflect overall adherence to the dietary intervention rather than an isolated effect of UPF reduction per se. The study was not specifically designed to isolate the independent effect of UPF reduction on liver steatosis, and participants across all arms were encouraged to reduce UPF intake. In addition, dietary exposure was assessed using self-reported records, which may introduce measurement error and residual confounding. Therefore, the observed association between UPF reduction and CAP improvement should be interpreted cautiously and considered hypothesis-generating rather than indicative of causality. Nevertheless, these findings support the concept that dietary quality, beyond caloric quantity alone, may represent a relevant dimension in MASLD management and warrants further investigation in trials specifically designed to test this hypothesis.

This study has several strengths. First, it is a randomized controlled trial specifically designed to compare different dietary strategies in patients with MASLD who were naïve to structured nutritional counselling, a population that closely reflects real-world clinical practice. Second, the intervention combined detailed dietary assessment with comprehensive anthropometric, metabolic, and non-invasive liver evaluations, allowing an integrated analysis of changes in hepatic steatosis and cardiometabolic health. Third, the assessment of UPF intake using the NOVA classification represents a novel aspect, providing insights into dietary quality beyond macronutrient composition. Finally, the application of structural equation modelling enabled exploration of direct and indirect pathways linking dietary allocation, weight change, UPF intake, and liver fat reduction, offering mechanistic insights not captured by conventional analyses.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. Physical activity was assessed using self-reported questionnaires rather than objective measures, which may have introduced reporting bias. Dietary intake was based on food diaries, which are inherently subject to under-reporting and recall bias. Although potential under-reporters were identified, they were retained in the analyses to avoid potential selection bias; therefore, some degree of residual misreporting of energy intake cannot be excluded. In addition, the NOVA classification has several limitations that should be considered, including insufficient consideration of nutritional quality, a reliance on evidence derived primarily from observational studies, and a lack of specificity and clarity in food definitions.33 The follow-up period was limited to 6 months, precluding evaluation of long-term adherence and the sustainability of dietary changes. Liver fat was assessed using CAP rather than magnetic resonance imaging-proton density fat fraction or liver histology, which, although validated and widely used, may limit comparability with trials employing more sensitive imaging techniques. A further limitation concerns the nature of the control arm. Participants from all study arms received nutritional counselling and repeated clinical assessments, which may have independently promoted lifestyle changes. While this approach enhances ecological validity and aligns with ethical standards of care, it may have attenuated between-group differences and limited the magnitude of detectable intervention effects. Finally, despite the comprehensive assessment, the possibility of residual confounding by unmeasured factors cannot be excluded.

In this randomized controlled trial of patients with MASLD, structured nutritional and lifestyle counselling, rather than a specific dietary pattern, resulted in significant improvements in liver fat content. Reductions in body weight and UPF consumption emerged as the strongest and independent predictors of steatosis regression, underscoring the central role of both energy balance and dietary quality. These findings support a pragmatic, patient-centered approach to dietary management in MASLD, prioritizing sustainable weight loss and reduction of UPF over rigid adherence to a specific dietary model.
