Development of single-chain C1q affinity chromatography–mass spectrometry for the glycoform-resolved characterization of low-affinity immunoglobulin G interactions
Authors: Steffen Lippold, Kelvin Chang, Mirjam Dürkoop, Jan Terje Andersen, Feng Yang, Tilman Schlothauer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe interaction between complement factor C1q and the Fc region of immunoglobulin G (IgG) antibodies is modulated by the conserved IgG glycosylation site at Asn 297 and is a critical determinant of complement-dependent cytotoxicity. However, characterizing this weak, monovalent interaction is challenging, as standard binding assays fail to resolve the inherent heterogeneity of antibody proteoforms without specific engineering or enrichment. Here, we report the development of a single-chain C1q affinity chromatography–mass spectrometry (scC1q AC–MS) method to resolve IgG glycoforms. A major hurdle in analyzing low-affinity interactions is nonspecific binding (NSB) to the stationary phase. Through systematic screening of volatile salts, we identified ammonium hydrogencarbonate as a suitable mobile phase, significantly reducing NSB compared to standard ammonium acetate or formate solvents and previously established nonvolatile buffers. The optimized method successfully reproduced the established affinity hierarchy of human IgG subclasses (IgG3 > IgG1 ≫ IgG2 >IgG4). Furthermore, high-resolution MS detection enabled the separation of individual glycoforms, confirming that high-mannose glycans significantly reduce C1q affinity, whereas galactosylation provides a modest enhancement. The novelty of our developed method lies in advancing scC1q AC into an MS-compatible platform, which eliminates the need for laborious glycoengineering by providing a direct, simultaneous binding readout of individual glycoforms within heterogeneous antibody proteoform mixtures. By enabling the unbiased comparison of intrinsic antibody affinities independent of glycosylation variability, scC1q AC-MS provides a powerful new tool for the development and characterization of IgG antibodies and establishes a blueprint for the chromatographic analysis of weak protein interactions.

Introduction

The characterization of protein–protein interactions is fundamental to understanding the biological function and therapeutic efficacy of antibodies.1 Low-affinity protein–protein interactions (KD > 10 µM) are particularly challenging to assess with commonly used binding assays, such as surface plasmon resonance (SPR), due to limitations involving fast kinetics, solubility, and mass transport.2,3 Affinity chromatography (AC) is a powerful technique for studying low-affinity interactions.4 A major advantage of AC is its direct compatibility with online mass spectrometry (MS), which provides proteoform resolution and, hence, can unravel the inherent antibody heterogeneity.5 However, developing AC–MS methods for low-affinity interactions depends highly on mitigating nonspecific binding (NSB); secondary interactions with the stationary phase may lead to significant peak broadening or shifted retention times, potentially masking the subtle differences in specific affinity and compromising assay performance.6 Consequently, a systematic understanding of the factors impacting NSB is required for successful AC–MS method development.

The interaction between complement component 1q (C1q) and the crystallizable fragment (Fc) of immunoglobulin G (IgG) is the critical first step in the activation of the classical complement pathway, making it crucial for the assessment of antibody effector functions.7,8 For therapeutic monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that rely on complement-dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) for their mechanism of action, such as anti-CD20 or anti-CD38 IgG1-based therapies, precise tools to assess C1q binding are needed for the identification of critical quality attributes.9,10 C1q interaction is characterized by a low monovalent IgG affinity, with dissociation constants (KD) typically residing in the high-micromolar to low-millimolar range.11 While the functional relevance of this pathway in vivo is driven by multivalent avidity,12 the relevance of studying the monovalent C1q–IgG interaction is recognized and provides a simplified binding readout for comparing IgG subclasses or variants without the confounding effects of cooperativity.13,14 Previous studies have utilized bio-layer interferometry, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA), and SPR to rank the C1q-binding potential of human IgG subclasses, Fc engineering, and various modifications, such as Fc glycosylation.7,15–18 However, the characterization of such weak, transient interactions remains technically demanding. Conventional biosensors often require specialized, high-density surfaces to generate sufficient signal-to-noise ratios, which can introduce mass‑transport artifacts and complicate the resolution of subtle differences in heterogeneous samples.19 AC using single-chain human C1q (scC1q) has been previously reported as an alternative; however, the current assay only provides an UV readout (non-MS‑compatible mobile phases), and the selected conditions exhibited a high degree of NSB.4 To further advance the AC platform toward a robust proteoform-resolved assay, compatibility with MS and the mitigation of NSB are required.

In this study, we report the development of a novel AC–MS method for the analysis of monovalent scC1q–IgG interactions. We performed a systematic optimization of the mobile phase composition to maximize specific scC1q binding while simultaneously mitigating NSB. This optimization involved a comprehensive comparison of volatile buffer systems, specifically ammonium acetate, ammonium formate, and ammonium hydrogencarbonate, across a range of ionic strengths and pH values. To ensure the broad applicability of the platform, we utilized a panel of eight mAbs with varying propensities for scC1q binding and NSB. The method performance was further assessed for an IgG1 mAb with respect to linearity, recovery, and injection repeatability. Finally, the developed assay was applied to study the interaction of the four human IgG subclasses (1–4) in a glycoform-resolved manner, demonstrating the ability of the platform to resolve subtle differences in affinity within heterogeneous antibody proteoform mixtures.

Materials & methods

Antibodies

Eight recombinant mAbs were utilized in this study. The panel included three IgG1 variants (different complementarity-determining regions (CDRs)) to assess a range of molecular scaffolds: IgG1-A (human IGHG1 × 03 heavy chain; human IGLC2 × 01 lambda light chain), IgG1-B (human IGHG1 × 03; murine IGLC1 × 01 lambda light chain), and IgG1-C (human IGHG1 × 03; murine IGKC *01 kappa light chain). To evaluate the impact of heavy-chain subclass on binding affinity, three additional antibodies were generated using the IgG1-C scaffold (murine kappa light chain) by substituting the heavy-chain constant regions: IgG2-C (IGHG2 × 01), IgG3-C (IGHG3 × 01), and IgG4-C (IGHG4 × 01). Of note, murine light chains for IgG-B and IgG-C were used as examples of challenging antibodies with higher propensities for NSB, while keeping the human heavy chain to maintain relevance for C1q interactions. Finally, Fc effector-silent variants featuring the PGLALA amino acid substitutions (Pro329Gly, Leu234Ala, and Leu235Ala) were produced for IgG1-B and IgG1-C to serve as negative controls for specific C1q binding. All antibody sequence information is provided in the Supplemental Sequences File. The intact mass profiles, with assignment of the most abundant glycoforms, are presented in Figure S1. Of note, because the inherent mAb glycoforms were simultaneously resolved via the online MS readout described below, no preparative glycoengineering of the mAbs was required for this study.

ScC1q affinity column preparation

The scC1q affinity column was prepared as described previously.4 Briefly, the scC1q construct (Figure S2), featuring a C-terminal AviTag, was expressed in HEK293 cells. The purified scC1q was in-vitro biotinylated using a BirA biotin–protein ligase kit (Avidity) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Excess biotin was subsequently removed by buffer exchange using 3 kDa molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) centrifugal filters (Merck Millipore). For immobilization, 1.5 mg of the biotinylated scC1q was loaded onto Streptavidin Sepharose High Performance beads (Cytiva). The functionalized resin was packed into a Tricorn column housing (5 mm × 25 mm, Cytiva) to a final bed volume of 0.5 mL. A blank column was prepared in an identical manner, using the same packing material and housing dimensions, but without the immobilization of scC1q to serve as a reference for NSB.

ScC1q AC–MS

ScC1q AC–MS was performed on a Vanquish Horizon UHPLC (Thermo Scientific) coupled to a Q Exactive UHMR Orbitrap (Thermo Scientific). For initial mobile-phase screening, UV absorbance was monitored at 280 nm. Prior to analysis, samples were buffer-exchanged into ultrapure water using 10 kDa MWCO filters and adjusted to a final concentration of 1 mg/mL. A 5 µL (5 µg) injection was used for all runs. The flow rate was maintained at 0.25 mL/min with a column temperature of 25°C. For MS detection, the flow was split post-UV detection at a 1:100 ratio, delivering a final flow of approximately 2.5 µL/min to a Nanospray Flex ion source (Thermo Scientific).

Mobile phase A consisted of MS-grade water, and mobile phase B consisted of a 150 mM volatile ammonium salt (ammonium acetate, ammonium formate, or ammonium hydrogencarbonate). The pH of the mobile phases was monitored before and after each sequence to ensure stability within ±0.1 pH units. For pH adjustments to 7.4, 150 mM ammonium hydroxide was used to increase the pH of the ammonium acetate and formate solvents, while 150 mM ammonium formate was used to titrate the pH of ammonium hydrogencarbonate. Screening sequences utilized IgG1-A as a bracketing control to monitor potential shifts in retention time or peak shape. The optimized AC method utilized an initial isocratic hold at 50 mM ammonium salt for 5 min, followed by a linear salt gradient to 150 mM over 15 min. The column was then re-equilibrated to initial conditions for 10 min. The final optimized AC–MS conditions utilized 150 mM ammonium hydrogencarbonate (unadjusted pH 7.8) as mobile phase B.

MS spectra were acquired in a positive ion mode (2 kV capillary voltage) over an m/z range of 2,000–25,000. The mass resolution was set to 25,000 (at m/z 400). The ion transfer tube temperature was set to 250°C. No additional gas flow in the source was applied. To facilitate effective desolvation of the antibody proteoforms, in-source trapping was applied at −100 V (automatic gain control fixed at 106). The maximum ion injection time was set to 100 ms. MS data analysis, including spectral deconvolution and mass assignment, was performed using Byosphere (v5.7, Protein Metrics). UV-based peak areas and retention times were extracted using FreeStyle (v1.8, Thermo Scientific). Extracted ion chromatograms (XIC) of inherent glycoforms of the tested mAbs were generated using the theoretical m/z values of the three most abundant charge states in a 100 ppm window.

Results

The choice of volatile salt is the most critical factor for scC1q AC–MS

The optimization of the mobile phase for scC1q AC–MS focused on maximizing specific scC1q–antibody interactions while minimizing NSB to the sepharose–streptavidin matrix. A panel of eight mAbs, including the human IgG subclasses (IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, and IgG4), variants with different CDR regions (A, B, and C), and Fc amino‑acid substitutions (PGLALA variant abolishing C1q binding), was used for the screening. Notably, both the IgG-B and IgG-C antibody scaffolds feature a murine light chain. The inclusion of the IgG-B/C scaffolds, which feature a murine kappa light chain, served as a critical benchmark for method development, as chimeric or non-humanized variable domains are frequently associated with increased NSB and challenging biophysical profiles.20 The primary variables investigated were the type of MS-compatible ammonium salt, ionic strength, and pH (Figure 1). The choice of volatile ammonium salt was the most significant factor influencing NSB on the blank (non-functionalized) column (Figure 1(A)). At an initial concentration of 50 mM, ammonium hydrogencarbonate yielded the lowest NSB across all tested mAbs. However, the IgG-C scaffold proved to be the most prone to NSB, exhibiting significant interaction with the blank column in the presence of ammonium acetate and ammonium formate (Figure 1(A)).

Adjusting the mobile phase to pH 7.4 reduced the NSB for both ammonium acetate and formate, but ammonium hydrogencarbonate remained the superior buffer, maintaining the lowest overall NSB (Figure S3–S8). Furthermore, specific binding to the scC1q column was marginally enhanced at pH 7.4 across all conditions. Notably, under certain parameters, specifically using ammonium acetate or formate, IgG1-C and IgG1-C PGLALA exhibited higher NSB on the blank column than on the scC1q-functionalized column.

As expected, decreasing the ionic strength generally correlated with increased specific binding affinity for scC1q, though it also introduced greater variability in NSB across the sample set (Figure 1(B), Figure S3–S8). The final binding affinity ranking was established as follows: IgG1-B PGLALA = IgG1-C, PGLALA < IgG4-C < IgG2-C < IgG1-A = IgG1-B < IgG1-C < IgG3-C. Based on these findings, 50 mM ammonium hydrogencarbonate (unadjusted pH) was selected as the most suitable mobile phase for scC1q AC–MS.

Assessment of key method performance characteristics of scC1q AC–MS

The analytical performance of the scC1q AC–MS method was evaluated using IgG1-C, representing the most challenging scaffold regarding NSB, to assess linearity, recovery, injection repeatability, MS response, and specificity (Figure 2). The method demonstrated excellent linearity, with UV peak areas showing a proportional response across mass loads ranging from 2.5 µg to 7.5 µg (50% to 150% of the 5 µg target injection amount, Figure 2(A)). Furthermore, retention time remained stable across this load range, indicating no column overloading (Figure 2(A)). Quantitative recovery based on the peak area was consistently high, ranging from 94% to 108% (Table S1). Injection repeatability was assessed through six consecutive injections, yielding consistent UV chromatographic profiles and a stable retention time of 5.3 ± 0.1 min (Figure 2(B)). The repeatability of XIC retention times based on the standard deviation (n = 6) was assessed for the major glycoforms and ranged from ±0.2 min for the most abundant glycoform (G0F/G0F) to ±0.3 min for low‑abundant glycoforms (e.g., G1F/G2F) (Table S2). The broader deviations for XICs (MS trace) compared to the UV profiles are expected due to the generally broad peaks in AC and differences in signal-to-noise. In addition, consistency of the chromatographic profiles was demonstrated for independently prepared scC1q affinity columns utilizing bracketing injections of IgG1-A and IgG1-B PGLALA as a negative control (Figure S9). Of note, while the reproducibility of the presented in-house packed affinity columns is currently assessed qualitatively to fulfill the primary objective of ranking low-affinity protein–protein interactions, future column development efforts should integrate quantitative performance metrics when focusing on the optimization of chromatographic efficiency. The electrospray current trace was used to monitor the mobile phase composition reaching the MS source, confirming the linearity of the gradient transition from 50 mM to 150 mM ammonium hydrogencarbonate (Figure 2(C)). Chromatographic data indicated that the majority of IgG1-C eluted under the subsequent isocratic conditions. A representative native MS signal displayed a charge‑state envelope centered from 26+ to 32+, providing sufficient resolution to resolve the major glycoforms of IgG1-C (Figure 2(D)). The specificity of the method was demonstrated by the elution order of IgG-C subclasses 1–4 and a PGLALA variant of IgG1-C (Figure 2(E)). In line with prior knowledge of the IgG–C1q interaction, the elution order was IgG1-C PGLALA < IgG4-C < IgG2-C < IgG1-C < IgG3-C.21 Collectively, these data confirm that scC1q AC–MS yields reproducible retention times and high-quality MS spectra suitable for structural characterization. Finally, based on the specificity of the scC1q AC–MS, it is expected that the differences in retention time provide meaningful functional insights with respect to complement activation.

Resolving the relative binding of mAb glycoforms using scC1q AC–MS

Finally, the capability of C1q AC–MS to separate individual glycoforms was evaluated across the four IgG-C subclasses (Figure 3). ScC1q AC–MS revealed partial separation of IgG variants, enabling a direct comparison of glycoform-specific binding behavior. IgG3-C exhibited the strongest affinity, eluting within the linear salt gradient with the highest retention time, followed by IgG1-C. In contrast, the weaker binders, IgG2-C and IgG4-C, eluted during the initial isocratic phase. Within the isocratic phase, IgG2-C demonstrated stronger binding than IgG4-C, evidenced by a slightly later retention time and increased peak width compared to the sharp, early-eluting peaks of IgG4-C.

Glycosylation patterns also influenced binding behavior, though this effect was subclass-dependent. For the strong binders (IgG1-C and IgG3-C), increasing levels of galactosylation correlated with a slight shift toward higher retention times (Figure 3, Figure S10). However, the shift of galactosylated glycoforms was not observed for the weaker binders (IgG2-C and IgG4-C). Conversely, high‑mannose species (M5/M5) consistently eluted earlier than complex-type glycoforms across all four subclasses, indicating reduced C1q affinity. Additionally, the presence of a single O-glycan (H1N1S2) on the IgG3-C G0F/G0F variant was assessed, but resulted in no observable retention shift compared to the G0F/G0F glycoform without O-glycan (Figure 3(C)).

Discussion

Importance of mobile–phase selection for low-affinity interaction studies by AC–MS

AC offers distinct advantages for studying weak affinity interactions (KD in the µM range), such as the monovalent scC1q–IgG interaction. While SPR has been successfully qualified for determining IgG–C1q binding affinities, it inherently provides an averaged binding constant for the heterogeneous antibody population.17 In contrast, AC–MS possesses the unique capability to resolve the binding behaviors of specific proteoforms that are otherwise averaged out in standard SPR assays. However, the transition from bulk binding assays to high-resolution AC–MS relies critically on mobile‑phase optimization to maximize specific retention while suppressing NSB. The challenge of NSB in C1q AC–UV has been recently reported.4 While the work by Marshall et al. established the feasibility of the scC1q AC format, the study used nonvolatile buffers with no option for online MS-hyphenation and was limited by a significant degree of NSB with the column matrix, which challenges the ability to rank antibody binding due to vastly different retention profiles of antibody variants on the blank column. Our study advances this methodology by systematically screening volatile, MS-compatible mobile‑phase conditions to specifically mitigate this background.

We identified that the choice of ammonium salt is the most impactful factor in reducing NSB. While previous AC–MS studies often successfully utilized ammonium acetate (e.g., 30 mM for FcɣRIIIb AC–MS), our data indicate that these conditions result in high background for certain antibody scaffolds (e.g., IgG-C).5 Instead, ammonium hydrogencarbonate demonstrated superior performance, yielding the lowest NSB across all tested variants, independent of the mobile phase pH. The minor impact of pH agrees with a previous study from Roumenina et al. that showed broad pH stability of the IgG1–C1q interaction.7 However, for other biologically relevant interaction partners of C1q, such as C-reactive protein, the optimization of pH may be more important to consider. Additionally, ionic strength was confirmed as a key modulator. As expected, decreasing ionic strength (30–40 mM) generally increased specific binding, but can inadvertently amplify NSB depending on the antibody scaffold. We observed variability in NSB, where the IgG-B/C scaffolds were highly prone to NSB, while IgG-A showed consistently low NSB, underscoring the importance of using a diverse antibody panel and negative controls (PGLALA) during method development. Notably, an analysis of sequence-based attributes (Supplemental Sequences File) revealed that standard 1D sequence-derived metrics, such as theoretical pI and global hydrophobicity (GRAVY) score, failed to predict nonspecific column interactions. Scaffolds with identical theoretical pI values (e.g., IgG1-A and IgG2-C) exhibited drastically different retention profiles on the blank column, and global hydrophobicity did not correlate with NSB. This indicates that NSB in low-affinity scC1q AC–MS is primarily driven by localized spatial patches rather than bulk sequence properties. Consequently, this highlights the importance of mobile‑phase optimization for AC–MS to broadly mitigate NSB. The successful mitigation of NSB for the IgG-B/C scaffolds demonstrates the broad applicability of the developed scC1q AC–MS platform, ensuring robust characterization even for therapeutic candidates with challenging developability profiles or non-optimized variable domains.22

Finally, while the selected conditions were determined to be suitable for scC1q AC–MS, additional parameters may be considered for future adaptations of weak–affinity interactions. For example, decreased column temperature and an increased ligand density on the affinity column (up to 6 mg/mL) are expected to enhance retention in AC. In addition, while ammonium hydrogencarbonate provides the nearest buffering capacity at neutral pH compared to the other tested volatile ammonium salts,23,24 more recently introduced MS-compatible salts with more suitable buffering ranges would be interesting to test for applicability in AC–MS.25 In summary, our mobile‑phase optimization effectively overcomes the limitations of significant NSB reported in the sC1q AC–UV method by Marshall et al., while greatly advancing the analytical power of the platform through the added capability for online MS-hyphenation.

Insights from glycoform-resolved binding readout

Fc-linked N-glycosylation at Asn297 is a well-established modulator of IgG effector functions, including C1q binding and subsequent complement activation.1,10,15,26 Our scC1q AC–MS analysis revealed a modest enhancement in binding affinity for galactosylated glycoforms within the IgG1 and IgG3 subclasses, evidenced by a shift to slightly increased retention times. This observation aligns with previous studies, showing that terminal galactose residues increase C1q binding affinity for IgG1 and IgG3, which translates to increased efficacies.15,16,27 In contrast, high-mannose glycoforms (M5/M5) exhibited the most significant reduction in retention time, eluting consistently earlier than complex glycoforms across all four IgG subclasses. This finding corroborates earlier studies which demonstrated that high-mannose species reduce C1q affinity and hence CDC activity.15,16,28 Furthermore, the high resolution of the AC–MS platform enabled the unique assessment of O-glycosylation within the extended hinge region of IgG3, which showed no discernible impact on retention time. Traditionally, defining glycosylation-related CQAs is a highly laborious and time-consuming process. The classic workflow requires enzymatic remodeling, preparative fractionation, or selective enrichment of specific glycoforms, followed by extensive structural verification. These isolated variants are then subjected to conventional bulk binding or cell-based assays, which frequently suffer from limited precision, avidity artifacts, and high assay variability.29 In contrast, our scC1q AC–MS platform unifies functional separation and structural identification into a streamlined workflow, circumventing the need for preparative fractionation.

The applicability of scC1q AC–MS extends beyond the characterization of glycosylation variants; it is expected to serve as a robust tool for the direct comparison of different mAb scaffolds and other post-translational modifications (PTMs) or their combinations (proteoforms). Standard bulk assays (e.g., SPR or ELISA) yield a single binding value that represents a weighted average of the entire heterogeneous population. For example, if two mAb batches differ in their glycosylation profiles (e.g., one has higher high-mannose content), a bulk measurement might falsely suggest a difference in backbone affinity. scC1q AC–MS eliminates this bias by allowing the extraction and comparison of identical glycoforms (e.g., comparing G0F/G0F directly to M5/M5) across different samples. We successfully demonstrated this capability across the four human IgG subclasses (IgG1–IgG4), demonstrating the broad applicability of the method. By isolating specific glycoforms from the XIC, we could perform a direct relative comparison of the mAb backbones themselves, which was not affected by the variability of their glycosylation.

It is important to note that our study focused on establishing MS-compatible conditions and used a qualitative assessment by ranking the retention time shifts. While AC can theoretically yield quantitative thermodynamic parameters (e.g., KD), the present setup prioritized MS-compatible method development and the relative ranking of variants. Future work could extend this workflow to derive quantitative binding constants from the elution profiles. Moreover, analysis of relevant stress panels may further expand the identification of CQAs, impacting complement activation and attribute the impact to specific PTMs or proteoforms.

Relevance of characterizing monovalent IgG–C1q interaction

The physiological activation of the complement cascade is driven by on-target IgG oligomerization up to hexamers and the multivalent binding to the C1 complex.13,30 While oligomerization is crucial for high-avidity binding and potent CDC, the fundamental thermodynamic driver remains the intrinsic, monovalent affinity between a single C1q globular head and a single IgG Fc domain. Variations in this intrinsic affinity directly influence the threshold for complement activation.11,14

To rigorously assess the monovalent affinity, we utilized a recombinant scC1q ligand encompassing the globular head domains hC1q A, B, and C (Figure S2). The selection of scC1q over native C1q was driven by the need for analytical standardization. Native C1q is a structurally complex, hexameric assembly involving non-covalent interactions, making it prone to potential batch-to-batch heterogeneity and stability issues. By employing the engineered scC1q construct, we ensured a homogeneous, molecularly defined stationary phase. This eliminates ligand-derived variability, ensuring that the observed differences in retention time are attributable solely to the antibody variants. We successfully replicated the established hierarchy of human IgG subclass binding to C1q (IgG3 > IgG1 >IgG2 >IgG4).21 This substantiates that scC1q AC–MS provides a precise tool for comparative profiling of low-affinity interactions, capable of dissecting how specific structural modifications in the Fc (such as IgG subclass or glycoform) fine-tune the C1q affinity of the antibody monomer.

In the future, other relevant scC1q AC–MS applications for the screening of Fc-engineered IgGs and other more complex or higher-affinity mAb formats, such as IgM or anti-C1q autoantibodies, may provide novel immunological insights.31–33 However, applying the method to stronger binders or IgG complexes (such as target-bound IgG hexamers) would require significant adaptation of the elution strategy. For instance, Marshall et al. found that IgG1 Fc-engineered to form hexamers spontaneously in solution (E345R, E430G, S440Y amino‑acid substitutions) require high salt concentrations (e.g., 500 mM NaCl) for elution in the wash step of scC1q AC–UV.4 Similarly, adapting the mobile phase to include low or high pH elution strategies may be necessary to disrupt the multivalent bonds of immunoglobulin complexes to allow for MS-compatible conditions.

Ultimately, our work provides broader insights into mobile-phase selection for weak biological interactions. By validating ammonium hydrogencarbonate as a superior solvent that preserves weak binding while enabling MS detection, we established a framework that can be translated to other challenging low-affinity protein interactions.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we introduce scC1q AC–MS as a novel analytical tool to extend and simplify the functional and structural characterization of IgG proteoforms and their ability to bind human scC1q in the monomeric state. We report, for the first time, a robust MS-compatible AC method specifically optimized for such weak biological interactions. In contrast to bulk binding assays, this approach provides separation of individual proteoforms based on a functionally relevant interaction, thereby reducing ambiguities in defining meaningful CQAs related to C1q binding and CDC. As we demonstrated the suitability of the assay for all human IgG heavy‑chain subclasses and different IgG scaffolds, we expect this method to be broadly applicable throughout therapeutic development and fundamental immunological research. We believe that our developed method streamlines the development of Fc-engineered IgG mAbs and serves as an important blueprint for analyzing low-affinity protein interactions and identifying CQAs by AC–MS.
