Training Infinitely Deep and Wide Transformers
Raphaël Barboni, Takashi Furuya, Maarten V. de Hoop, Gabriel Peyré

Abstract
Transformers have become the dominant architecture in modern machine learning, yet the theoretical understanding of their training dynamics remains limited, especially for deep architectures with many layers and attention heads. This paper develops a rigorous mathematical framework for analyzing gradient-based training of transformers in the mean-field regime, where both the depth (number of layers) and width (number of attention heads) tend to infinity. In fact, we extend the mean-field training theory for ResNets to the transformer setting. While ResNet training can be understood as controlling a neural ordinary differential equation (ODE), transformer training corresponds to controlling a neural partial differential equation (PDE), due to the coupling of multiple token distributions through the attention mechanism. Our mean-field model features two types of measure representations: token distributions evolving through layers (represented as measures over the token space), and attention parameters at each layer (represented as measures over the attention parameter space). We establish well-posedness of the forward pass through infinitely deep transformers, characterizing token evolution via flow maps that satisfy ODEs in function spaces. Using adjoint sensitivity analysis, we derive an explicit formula for the conditional Wasserstein gradient of the training risk, involving adjoint variables governed by backward ODEs. We prove the existence and uniqueness of gradient flow curves in the conditional Wasserstein metric space, establishing a rigorous foundation for gradient-based transformer training. A key technical contribution is providing necessary and sufficient conditions for injectivity of the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) for attention mechanisms: we show that NTK injectivity is equivalent to linear independence of log-sum-exp functions modulo affine functions, a condition satisfied by diverse token distributions, including discrete distributions, uniform distributions, and Gaussian mixtures. Under this NTK injectivity assumption, we prove that gradient flow converges to global minima when the initial loss is sufficiently small, eliminating spurious local minima from the optimization landscape.



1
Introduction

Transformers
[
44
]
have emerged as the dominant architecture in modern machine learning, powering breakthroughs in natural language processing, computer vision, and beyond. Despite their empirical success, the theoretical understanding of transformer training dynamics remains limited, particularly for deep architectures with many layers and large numbers of attention heads. Understanding when and why gradient-based optimization converges for transformers is essential for developing principled design choices and training procedures.

1.1
Previous Works

Infinite Width: Mean Field over Parameters

The analysis of infinitely wide neural networks depends on the scaling normalization of the weights, leading to distinct mathematical representations.
The Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) regime
[
28
]
arises when weights scale as
1/\sqrt{\text{width}}
. In this lazy training regime, the network remains close to initialization throughout optimization, and training dynamics reduce to kernel regression in a reproducing kernel Hilbert space (RKHS). The kernel is fixed by the architecture and initialization, and the problem becomes linear in the RKHS. This Euclidean structure is mathematically simpler and enables global convergence guarantees
[
20
,
3
]
, but precludes genuine feature learning since the network’s representational capacity is frozen at initialization.
The mean-field regime corresponds to a different scaling of
1/\text{width}
, where network parameters are represented as a probability distribution over the parameter space
[
34
,
39
,
14
]
. Unlike the Euclidean NTK setting, this mean-field formulation is inherently non-Euclidean: training dynamics evolve on the Wasserstein manifold of probability measures and are governed by continuity equations or Wasserstein gradient flows. This richer regime allows for feature learning, as parameters can move substantially during training. Global convergence results have been established for shallow mean-field networks under appropriate regularity conditions
[
14
,
34
]
.
In the present paper, we adopt the mean-field representation to model transformers with infinitely many attention heads: each layer contains a probability distribution over attention parameters (query, key, value matrices). For our convergence analysis, although the model is formulated in the mean-field framework, we also leverage tools from NTK theory by studying the Neural Tangent Kernel of the attention mechanism and establishing conditions for its injectivity.

Infinite Depth: Neural ODE Models

Understanding training dynamics of deep neural networks has been a central challenge in deep learning theory. Initial results on training deep networks
[
20
,
3
]
established convergence guarantees in overparameterized regimes, typically under conditions analogous to the NTK regime where the network remains close to initialization.
The introduction of residual connections
[
26
]
was crucial for training very deep networks in practice. Theoretically, residual networks admit an interpretation as discretizations of continuous dynamical systems. Chen
et al
.
[
13
]
introduced Neural ODEs, viewing ResNets in the limit of infinitely many layers as ordinary differential equations on the feature space. The continuous-time perspective depends on the scaling: with residual weights scaling as
1/\text{depth}
, one obtains a neural ODE, while
1/\sqrt{\text{depth}}
scaling leads to a neural SDE
[
33
]
.
Training dynamics in the neural ODE regime, where residual blocks are themselves mean-field MLPs, have been rigorously analyzed by
[
10
]
. They established well-posedness of the gradient flow via the theory of curves of maximal slope in the conditional Wasserstein metric, and proved local convergence (when the initial loss is sufficiently small) under a Polyak-Łojasiewicz condition. In our paper, we adopt the same framework for infinitely deep transformers, with a crucial distinction: the data (tokens) are also represented in the mean-field limit. Unlike ResNets that operate on finite-dimensional feature vectors via a neural ODE, transformers in our formulation process token distributions through a neural PDE that couples the evolution of multiple probability measures.

Infinite Number of Tokens: Mean Field over Tokens and Neural PDE Frameworks

Dealing with very large numbers of tokens is highly relevant for modern transformer applications: long-context language models process thousands of tokens, vision transformers handle thousands of image patches.
In the encoder (non-causal) setting, a natural approach is to represent the token distribution as a probability measure over the token space. This perspective was pioneered by
[
40
]
, who formulated the evolution of tokens through self-attention layers as a non-linear PDE on the space of probability measures. We refer to this approach as “mean field over tokens”. In the present work, it is crucial to note that we employ mean field in a dual sense: both over tokens (infinitely many tokens) and over parameters (infinitely many attention heads).
Building on this mean-field token framework,
[
24
]
analyzed the emergence of clustering behavior in the PDE governing token evolution, proving that attention mechanisms drive tokens toward discrete clusters.
From an expressivity perspective,
[
21
]
established that deep transformers are universal approximators for in-context mappings from probability measures over tokens to output tokens.

Optimal Transport and Wasserstein Gradient Flows

Flows over measures provide a unifying framework for understanding both training dynamics and transformer computations. In mean-field neural networks, Wasserstein gradient flows
[
29
,
4
]
describe the training dynamics: gradient descent on parameters induces a gradient flow on the measure representing the parameter distribution
[
14
]
. For transformers, token evolution through layers can be formulated as continuity equations with velocity fields determined by attention
[
40
,
24
]
, though these are not generally Wasserstein flows.
For deep architectures, the layer structure must be preserved during training: parameters at different depths should not mix. This motivates conditional optimal transport
[
10
]
. We adopt this framework for transformers, with the additional complexity that tokens themselves evolve as measures, requiring a neural PDE formulation.

Training of Transformers

Most existing results on transformer training focus on in-context learning, a behavior first demonstrated experimentally in
[
23
]
. Subsequent works have shown that transformers are expressive enough to exhibit this phenomenon
[
2
]
, that shallow but optimal transformers already possess this capability
[
32
]
, and, in more restricted settings, that the training dynamics converge to globally optimal parameters
[
1
]
.
The works most closely related to ours are
[
46
]
and its refinement in
[
43
]
, which establish convergence to a global minimum for the training of a single attention layer under an NTK-type parameter rescaling (equivalently, sufficiently small initialization loss). This line of work is further extended in
[
38
,
22
]
, which study deep transformers by incorporating skip connections.
Our work adopts a similar NTK-based perspective but goes beyond these results by accommodating an arbitrarily large (possibly infinite) number of residual blocks, as well as an arbitrary number of tokens.

1.2
Our Contributions

This paper extends the mean-field training theory of
[
10
]
from ResNets to transformers. While ResNet training corresponds to controlling a neural ODE (an ordinary differential equation on the token space), transformer training corresponds to controlling a neural PDE (a partial differential equation coupling the evolution of multiple token distributions). Our mean-field model represents transformers as both infinitely deep (parameterized by a depth variable) and infinitely wide (parameterized by a probability distribution of attention parameters), with the key distinction that there are two types of measure representations: tokens evolving through layers, and attention parameters at each layer.

Injectivity of the Neural Tangent Kernel for Attention (

A key technical challenge is ensuring the NTK remains positive definite throughout training. In the section on analysis for injective NTK of attention, we establish necessary and sufficient conditions for injectivity of the NTK map for attention mechanisms. The main proposition (

35

Training mean-field models of Transformers

We introduce in this section mean-field models of infinitely deep and arbitrarily wide Transformers (
sections

2.1
and
2.2
), show how the associated training dynamics can be interpreted as a gradient flow on the space of parameter distributions (
section

2.3
) and give conditions for convergence of this gradient flow (
section

2.4
).
A detailed study of our convergence assumptions will be the subject of

.

2.1
Mean-field Attention layers

We consider in this paper Attention layers where a supplementary bias parameter is added to the
query matrix
and where the
key matrix
is fixed to
K=\mathrm{Id}
.
Given a distribution
\mu
of tokens and an input token
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we hence consider Attention maps defined by:

\displaystyle\varphi_{\theta}[\mu](x)\coloneqq\frac{\int e^{\left<Qx+q,y\right>}Vy\,d\mu(y)}{\int e^{\left<Qx+q,y\right>}\,\text{d}\mu(y)}.

(1)

In the above equation, the Attention layer is parameterized by the triplet
\theta=(Q,q,V)
, living in the parameter set

\Theta\coloneqq\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
.

Remark 1
.

We introduce two modifications w.r.t. standard parameterizations of Attention layers:

•

The fact that the matrix
K
is fixed is introduced in this section mainly for technical reasons.
The main purpose is to ensure appropriate regularity and integrability of the Attention w.r.t. its parameters (see
lemmas

4
,
5
and
6
)
for the gradient flow dynamics to be well-defined.
Note that this simply amounts to performing a change of variables and to replacing
(Q,q)
by
(K^{\top}Q,K^{\top}q)
.
In particular, it does not affect the expressivity of Attention.

•

In contrast, the supplementary bias parameter
q
is precisely introduced to enhance the expressivity of Attention layers.
In particular, it breaks some invariance by symmetry in
eq.

1
.
The parameter
q
thus plays a key role in proving the positivity of the tangent kernel in
section

3
.

In the following, we consider mean-field models of multi-head Attention layers of infinite width, which are parameterized by the distribution of their parameters.
For
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
we define:

\displaystyle\Phi_{\rho}[\mu](x):=\int_{\Theta}\varphi_{\theta}[\mu](x)\,\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta).

(2)

We recover the case of multi-head Attention layers of finite width by considering an empirical distribution of parameters.
Given a finite number
H\geq 1
of heads and a family of parameters
(\theta_{h})_{1\leq h\leq H}\in\Theta^{H}
, considering
\hat{\rho}:=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h}\delta_{\theta_{h}}
, we have:

\Phi_{\hat{\rho}}[\mu](x)=\frac{1}{H}\sum_{h}\varphi_{\theta_{h}}[\mu](x).

2.2
Mean-field models of Transformers

In this work, we consider infinitely deep Transformers.
These consist of a continuum of multi-head Attention layers, each parameterized by a measure
\rho(.|s)
for each depth parameter
s\in[0,1]
.

For a discrete distribution of tokens
\mu(s=0):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\delta_{x_{i}(s=0)}
at depth
s=0
, the set of tokens
x_{i}(s)
, encoded as a measure
\mu(s):=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\delta_{x_{i}(s)}
, evolves according to a set of coupled ODEs:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathrm{d}x_{i}}{\mathrm{d}s}(s)=\Phi_{\rho(.|s)}[\mu(s)](x_{i}(s)).

(3)

This is extended to an arbitrary (possibly infinite) number of tokens by considering the PDE over the space of tokens:

\partial_{s}\mu(s)+\text{div}(\mu(s)\Phi_{\rho(.|s)}[\mu(s)])=0.

(4)

Note that in the following, these continuity equations are to be understood in the weak or
distributional
sense, that is, for every test function
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
it holds:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathcal{X}}\varphi(1)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(1)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(0)+\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathcal{X}}\left(\partial_{s}\varphi(s)+\nabla\varphi(s)\cdot\Phi_{\rho(.|s)}[\mu(s)]\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

In particular, when restricted to discrete measures, one recovers the system of ODEs in
eq.

3
.
Once this PDE has been solved and
\mu(s)
has been computed, one can define the flow-map
\Lambda_{\rho}[\mu(0)](s)
(which depends only on
\mu(0)
) induced by the Transformer, which is the mapping advecting
\mu(0)
to
\mu(s)
.
Formally, we denote by
\Lambda_{\rho}[\mu(s=0)](s,x):=x(s)
the mapping obtained by integrating the ODE

\displaystyle\forall s\in[0,1],\quad\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}s}(s)=\Phi_{\rho(.|s)}[\mu(s)](x(s)),\quad\text{with}\quad x(0)=x.

(5)

This implies that
\mu(s)=\Lambda_{\rho}[\mu(0)](s)_{\#}\mu(0)
.
Note that this mapping
\Lambda_{\rho}[\mu]
can be defined on the whole space, although for evaluation of the training risk, it is only required to compute it on the support of the input token distribution
\mu(0)
as well as on some input token
x(0)
.

Deep residual Transformers as flow-maps

Since the output of a deep Transformer can be seen as the output of a flow-map, in the following, we view Attention layers as mappings acting on the space of continuous functions.
Given parameters
\theta\in\Theta
, a compact subset
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
and a compactly supported probability measure
\mu
with
\Supp(\mu)\subset S
, we define for every continuous map
\Lambda\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(S,\mathbb{R}^{d})
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\theta}[\mu](\Lambda):x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}\mapsto\varphi_{\theta}[\Lambda_{\#}\mu](\Lambda(x))\,.

(6)

In this way,
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda)
is the mapping of the Attention layer after a push-forward of both token distribution
\mu
and input token
x
by
\Lambda
.
Similarly, for a parameter distribution
\rho\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
, one can define:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{\rho}[\mu](\Lambda)\coloneqq\int_{\Theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}[\mu](\Lambda)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta)\,,

(7)

which is the mapping of a multi-head Attention layer after a push-forward of both token distribution
\mu
and input token
x
by
\Lambda
.
In particular,
eq.

7
is to be understood as a Bochner integral while the definitions of
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
and
\mathcal{A}_{\rho}
are justified in the proof of
lemma

4
.
In the following, since the token distribution
\mu
will be explicit from the context, we use the shorthand notations
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}_{\theta}[\mu]
and
\mathcal{A}_{\rho}\coloneqq\mathcal{A}_{\rho}[\mu]
.

Then, instead of seeing Transformers as controlling the token distribution
\mu
through the continuity equation
eq.

4
, one can see Transformers as controlling the flow-map
\Lambda=\Lambda_{\rho}[\mu_{0}]\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,1]\times S,\mathbb{R}^{d})
through the (Banach-space-valued) ODE:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\Lambda(s)=\mathcal{A}_{\rho(.|s)}(\Lambda(s)),\quad\text{with}\quad\Lambda(0)=\mathrm{Id}.

(8)

Note that, due to the absence of regularity assumption on the parameterization
\{\rho(.|s)\}_{s\in[0,1]}
, this equation is to be understood in the sense of Carathéodory solutions:

\displaystyle\forall s\in[0,1],\quad\Lambda(s)=\mathrm{Id}+\int_{0}^{s}\mathcal{A}_{\rho(.|r)}(\Lambda(r))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r.

Remark 2
.

The idea of studying evolution PDEs of the form
eq.

4
on the space of densities through the lens of ODEs on the space of flows is a standard idea which can be traced back to the work of Arnold on Euler’s equation
[
7
]
.
There are at least two advantages of this approach:

•

From a conceptual perspective, it provides a convenient mathematical framework for studying the training of deep Transformers.
This framework indeed extends the one developed for the study of deep ResNets or NODE models
[
10
,
31
,
19
,
27
]
.
However, whereas NODEs usually process data points through independent ODEs, the particularity of Transformers is to process tokens through coupled ODEs (
eq.

3
).
This results in the study of non-local PDEs on the space of token distributions (
eq.

4
) whose perturbations are hard to study due to the lack of a proper differentiable structure.
In contrast, we study here deep Transformers seen as parameterized ODEs on the Banach space
\mathcal{C}^{0}(S,\mathbb{R}^{d})
(
eq.

8
).
As a consequence, and as opposed to other works
[
43
,
22
,
38
]
, our results do not depend on the number of tokens used for training.

•

From a more practical perspective, such a reparameterization of Attention layers provides them with enhanced regularity.
It is for example known that the Lipschitz constant of Attention layers has an exponential dependence on the radius of the support of the input token distribution
[
11
,
24
]
.
In contrast, the Lipschitz constant of
\mathcal{A}
behaves here quadratically w.r.t.
\Lambda\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(S,\mathbb{R}^{d})
(see
lemma

4
).

Well-posedness of the forward process

We study here the well-posedness of the mean-field models of Transformers, i.e., existence and uniqueness of solutions to

4

For the latter Banach-space-valued ODE to be well-posed, a minimal working assumption is that the associated velocity field satisfies adequate measurability and integrability conditions (see

In the case of the parameterized map

Note that every parameterization
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
corresponds to a (

[

The following result shows that solutions to

In the following, we denote by
\mathcal{C}^{0}_{\mathrm{co}}([0,1],\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
the set of co-compactly supported paths of probability measures, i.e., the set of continuous paths

(Existence and uniqueness of the forward process)
.

2.3
Training mean-field Transformers with Conditional Wasserstein gradient flows

We are interested here in the dynamics of gradient-based algorithms for the training of deep Transformer architectures. In the following, we consider a given (finite) dataset
\{(\mu^{(j)},x^{j})\}_{1\leq j\leq N}\in(\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}
constituted by
N
pairs of token distributions
\mu^{(j)}
and input tokens
x^{j}
.
For each index
j\in\{1,...,N\}
we will consider a loss
\ell^{j}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
on the space of tokens. Then, for a parameterization
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
, we define the training risk as:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\rho)\coloneqq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\ell^{j}(x^{j}(1)),

(13)

where
(x^{j}(s))_{s\in[0,1]}
and
(\mu^{(j)}(s))_{s\in[0,1]}
are, respectively, the solutions of the forward process
eq.

5
and
eq.

4
, with respective initializations,
x^{j}(0)=x^{j}
and
\mu^{(j)}(0)=\mu^{(j)}
.

Equivalently, the training risk is given for
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\rho)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\ell^{j}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(1,x^{j})),

(14)

where, in the above equation and in the rest of this paper, we use the shorthand
\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}=\Lambda_{\rho}[\mu^{(j)}]
to denote the flow-maps given by
proposition

1
and solution to the ODE:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s)=\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\rho(.|s)}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s)),

with
\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\rho(.|s)}=\mathcal{A}_{\rho(.|s)}[\mu^{(j)}]
defined by
eq.

7
.
We will also consider, for each index
j\in\{1,...,N\}
, a compact subset
S^{j}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
such that
\Supp(\mu^{(j)})\cup\{x^{j}\}\subset S^{j}
.
In particular,
\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,1]\times S^{j},\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Remark 3
.

For concreteness, one can consider in the following that the losses
\ell^{j}
are of the form
\ell^{j}:x\mapsto\frac{1}{2}\|x-y^{j}\|^{2}
for some target tokens
y^{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
In this case, the training risk associated with some parameterization
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
reads:

\displaystyle\mathcal{L}(\rho)=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\|x^{j}(1)-y^{j}\right\|^{2}=\frac{1}{2N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\|\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(1,x^{j})-y^{j}\right\|^{2}.

2.3.1
Conditional Optimal Transport geometry

To study the dynamics of gradient-based methods for the minimization of the risk
\mathcal{L}
in the training of Transformers, we follow the approach of
[
10
]
.
Using tools from the theory of gradient flows in metric spaces
[
4
]
, it was shown in
[
10
]
that gradient flows for the training of mean-field models of residual architectures can be modeled by metric gradient flows on the space of parameterizations when provided with a
Conditional Optimal Transport (COT)
metric.
Such a COT metric on
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
corresponds to a layer-wise
\mathcal{W}_{2}
-distance, which enforces the marginal constraint defining
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
to be conserved throughout training.
For
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
we define:

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})\coloneqq\left(\int_{0}^{1}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho(.|s),\rho^{\prime}(.|s))^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)^{1/2}

(15)

Then
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
provides
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
with a complete metric space structure (see e.g.
[
10
, Sec. 2]
).

Proposition 2
.

The distance
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
defines a complete metric space structure on
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
.

Of particular interest here is that on
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
provided with the metric
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
, absolutely continuous curves enjoy dynamical properties that are similar to those of the classical Wasserstein space.
That is, a celebrated result from
[
4
]
is that absolutely continuous curves in
\mathcal{W}_{2}(\Theta)
are characterized by solutions of continuity equations. Here, for an interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
, a narrowly continuous curve
(\rho_{t})_{t\in I}
in
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
is absolutely continuous w.r.t.
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
if and only if it is a solution to a continuity equation of the form:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}+\mathrm{div}_{\theta}(\rho_{t}v_{t})=0\quad\text{on $I\times[0,1]\times\Theta$},

(16)

where
v:I\times[0,1]\times\Theta\to\Theta
is some Borel velocity field s.t.
\|v_{t}\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{t})}\in L^{1}(I)
(see e.g.
[
10
, Sec. 2]
but also
[
36
]
). This property informally provides
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
with a differential structure that will motivate our definition of gradient flow curves in
definition

2
. Precisely, gradient flow curves for the risk
\mathcal{L}
are solutions to the above continuity equation for a velocity field
v_{t}
that is the solution to some variational problem (
theorem

2
).

2.3.2
First-order perturbation of the risk and adjoint equations

We study here first-order perturbations of the training risk
\mathcal{L}
defined in
eq.

13
along perturbations of the parameterization of the form
eq.

16
.
Such a perturbation analysis is permitted by the regularity of the Attention map, which we detail in
sections

A.1
and
A.2
.
We start by stating a result on the first-order perturbations of the mean-field Transformers flow-maps, which we prove in
section

A.3
.

Proposition 3
.

Let
(\rho_{t})_{t\in(0,1)}
be some absolutely continuous curve in
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
with velocity field
(v_{t})_{t\in(0,1)}
.
For some initial token distribution
\mu(0)\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\Supp(\mu(0))\subset S
, denote by
\Lambda_{t}=\Lambda_{\rho_{t}}[\mu(0)]\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,1]\times S,\mathbb{R}^{d})
the flow-map obtained in
proposition

1
.
Then the map
t\mapsto\Lambda_{t}
is absolutely continuous in
\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,1]\times S,\mathbb{R}^{d})
and, for a.e.
t\in(0,1)
, its (Fréchet) differential
\delta\Lambda_{t}\coloneqq\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\Lambda_{t}
is the solution of the Cauchy problem:

\displaystyle\delta\Lambda_{t}(s,x)=

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{s}\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\rho(.|r)}(\Lambda_{t}(r))\cdot\delta\Lambda_{t}(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r+\int_{0}^{s}\int_{\Theta}\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda_{t}(r))\cdot v_{t}(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta,r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r,

where
\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\rho(.|r)}(\Lambda)
and
\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda)
are defined in
lemma

4
and
lemma

5
respectively.

To define perturbations of the risk, we will not, in fact, directly rely on the above linear ODEs but rather on
adjoint equations
, which can be seen as their dual, defined backward in time.
The associated
adjoint variables
live in the dual of the space of solutions to the forward equation
eq.

8
, i.e., the space of finite,
d
-dimensional vector measures
\mathcal{M}^{d}(S)=\mathcal{C}^{0}(S,\mathbb{R}^{d})^{*}
.
These can be seen as limits of the quantities computed by the backpropagation algorithm when the depth of the Transformers goes to infinity. Numerical resolution of the adjoint equations is at the core of the adjoint method developed for the training of NODE models
[
13
]
.

Definition 1

(Adjoint equations)
.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
be some parameter distribution and, for index
j\in\{1,...,N\}
, let
\ell^{j}
be the loss function in
eq.

13
.
The
adjoint variable
associated to input token distribution
\mu^{(j)}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and input token
x^{j}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the measure-valued function
\mathbf{m}^{j}_{\rho}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,1],\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{j}))
solution of the (linear) Cauchy problem:

\displaystyle\forall s\in[0,1],\quad\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{\rho}(s)=\nabla\ell^{j}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(1,x^{j}))\,\delta_{x^{j}}+\int_{s}^{1}\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\rho(.|r)}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(r))^{*}\cdot\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{\rho}(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\,,

(17)

where
\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\rho(.|r)}(\Lambda)
is defined in
lemma

4
.

Note that the above adjoint equations are well-posed linear ODEs on the space of finite vector measures. The following result expresses the first-order perturbation of the training risk as a function of the solutions
\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}
(resp.
\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{\rho}
) to the forward (resp. backward) ODEs.

Theorem 1
.

Let
(\rho_{t})_{t\in(0,1)}
be some absolutely continuous curve in
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
with velocity field
(v_{t})_{t\in(0,1)}
.
Then the map
t\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\rho_{t})
is absolutely continuous and for a.e.
t\in(0,1)
we have:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{L}(\rho_{t})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\Theta}\left<\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{\rho_{t}}(s),\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda_{\rho_{t}}^{j}(s))\cdot v_{t}(s)\right>\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(\theta,s)\,.

(18)

Moreover, for
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
, defining

\displaystyle\left|\nabla\mathcal{L}\right|(\rho)\coloneqq\left(\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\Theta}\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s))^{*}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{\rho}(s)\right\|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta,s)\right)^{1/2}\,,

(19)

then
\left|\nabla\mathcal{L}\right|
is a (strong) upper-gradient for
\mathcal{L}
in the sense of
[
4
, Def. 1.2.1]
.

Proof.

We use the shorthand
\Lambda^{j}_{t}\coloneqq\Lambda^{j}_{\rho_{t}}
,
\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{t}\coloneqq\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{\rho_{t}}
and
\delta\Lambda^{j}_{t}\coloneqq\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\Lambda^{j}_{t}
for
t\in(0,1)
.
By
proposition

3
(and assuming sufficient regularity on the functionals
\ell^{j}
), we have that the map
t\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\rho_{t})
is absolutely continuous and for a.e.
t\in(0,1)
it holds:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{L}(\rho_{t})=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left<\nabla\ell^{j}(\Lambda^{j}_{t}(1,x^{j})),\delta\Lambda^{j}_{t}(1,x^{j})\right>=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left<\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{t}(1),\delta\Lambda^{j}_{t}(1)\right>

Then using the definition of
\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{t}
(
eq.

17
) and
\delta\Lambda^{j}_{t}
(
eq.

8
) we have for every index
j\in\{1,...,N\}
:

\displaystyle\left<\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{t}(1),\delta\Lambda^{j}_{t}(1)\right>={}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left<\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{t}(s),\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\rho_{t}(.|s)}(\Lambda^{j}_{t}(s))\cdot\delta\Lambda^{j}_{t}(s)\right>\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle+\int_{0}^{1}\left<\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{t}(s),\int_{\Theta}\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}_{t}(s))\cdot v_{t}(s,\theta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(\theta|s)\right>\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle-\int_{0}^{1}\left<\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\rho_{t}(.|s)}(\Lambda^{j}_{t}(s))^{*}\cdot\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{t}(s),\delta\Lambda^{j}_{t}(s)\right>\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle={}

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\left<\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{t}(s),\int_{\Theta}\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}_{t}(s))\cdot v_{t}(s,\theta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(\theta|s)\right>\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\,.

But then by properties of the Bochner integral (
[
18
, Thm. 6]
), for a.e.
s\in[0,1]
it holds:

\displaystyle\left<\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{t}(s),\int_{\Theta}\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}_{t}(s))\cdot v_{t}(s,\theta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(\theta|s)\right>=\int_{\Theta}\left<\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{t}(s),\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}_{t}(s))\cdot v_{t}(s,\theta)\right>\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(\theta|s)\,.

Putting these last two equations together gives the desired result for
\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{L}(\rho_{t})
.
The fact that
\left|\nabla\mathcal{L}\right|
is an upper-gradient then follows by applying the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality.
∎

Example 1

(Finite number of tokens)
.

Let us study in more detail the case of a finite number of tokens, that is, when for every index
j\in\{1,...,N\}
we have an empirical token distribution
\mu^{(j)}=\frac{1}{n^{j}}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{j}}\delta_{x^{j}_{i}}
for some
n^{j}\geq 1
.
We denote by
x^{j}_{0}\coloneqq x^{j}
the input token.
We can thus study the action of the Transformer on the compact subsets
S^{j}=\left\{x^{j}_{0},x^{j}_{1},...,x^{j}_{n^{j}}\right\}
and, for simplicity, assume all tokens are distinct, i.e.,
\#S^{j}=n^{j}+1
.

Let us fix a parameterization
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
.
With the above notations, the forward process through the Transformer reads:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}x^{j}_{i}(s)=\Phi_{\rho(.|s)}[\mu^{(j)}(s)](x^{j}_{i}(s))=\int_{\Theta}\frac{\sum_{l=1}^{n^{j}}e^{\left<Qx^{j}_{i}(s)+q,x^{j}_{l}(s)\right>}x^{j}_{l}(s)}{\sum_{l=1}^{n^{j}}e^{\left<Qx^{j}_{i}(s)+q,x^{j}_{l}(s)\right>}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta|s),\quad\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq j\leq N,\\
0\leq i\leq n^{j}.\end{subarray}

Here
\mu^{(j)}(s)=\frac{1}{n^{j}}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{j}}\delta_{x^{j}_{i}(s)}
.
By construction, the flow-map
\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}
defined in
proposition

1
satisfies
\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s,x^{j}_{i})=x^{j}_{i}(s)
.
In this setting, the adjoint variable
\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{\rho}
can be identified for every
s\in[0,1]
with a family of vectors
(\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{i}(s))_{0\leq i\leq n^{j}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times(n^{j}+1)}
.

Also, it is interesting to look at the expression for the gradient
|\nabla\mathcal{L}|
in the case of a finite number of tokens.
We will be interested in
section

2.4
in the lower-bound
|\nabla_{V}\mathcal{L}|
(
eq.

25
).
It reads here:
Denoting the attention weights by

p^{j}_{i,l}(s,\theta)\coloneqq\frac{e^{\left<Qx^{j}_{i}(s)+q,x^{j}_{l}(s)\right>}}{\sum_{r=1}^{n^{j}}e^{\left<Qx^{j}_{i}(s)+q,x^{j}_{r}(s)\right>}},

we obtain

\displaystyle|\nabla_{V}\mathcal{L}|^{2}(\rho)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]\times\Theta}\Big\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{D}_{V}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}(s))^{*}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}(s)\Big\|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta,s)

\displaystyle=\int_{[0,1]\times\Theta}\Big\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}0\leq i\leq n^{j}\\
1\leq l\leq n^{j}\end{subarray}}p^{j}_{i,l}(s,\theta)\,\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{i}(s)x^{j}_{l}(s)^{\top}\Big\|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta,s).

It thus appears that the above expression is a quadratic function of the adjoint variables
(\mathbf{m}^{(j)})_{1\leq j\leq N}
.
In
section

2.4
we will analyze the conditioning of the associated quadratic form to deduce convergence guarantees for the training of Transformers with gradient flow.

2.3.3
Gradient flow equation and well-posedness

We now define a notion of gradient flow for the minimization of the training risk
\mathcal{L}
.
We show in
theorem

2
that it corresponds to a metric gradient flow w.r.t. the COT metric
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
on
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
and state the existence and uniqueness of gradient flow curves in
theorem

3
.

Definition 2
.

Let
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
be some initial parameter distribution.
We say a locally absolutely continuous curve
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[0,+\infty)}
is a gradient flow for the risk
\mathcal{L}
starting from
\rho_{0}
if
\lim_{t\to 0}\rho_{t}=\rho_{0}
and it is a solution to the continuity equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}-\mathrm{div}_{\theta}(\rho_{t}\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}])=0\quad\text{on $(0,+\infty)\times[0,1]\times\Theta$},

(20)

where, for
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
, the velocity field
\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho]
is defined by:

\displaystyle\forall(s,\theta)\in[0,1]\times\Theta,\quad\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho](s,\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda_{\rho}^{j}(s))^{*}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{\rho}(s)\ \in\Theta.

(21)

To study the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the gradient flow
eq.

20
, we rely on the theory of gradient flows in metric spaces, applied here to the case of the metric space
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
provided with the Conditional Optimal Transport metric
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
.
Although there is no smooth differentiable structure on this space, the gradient flow curve can instead be characterized as a solution to a variational problem
(see e.g.
[
4
]
but also
[
5
]
or
[
42
]
).
The following result (proved in
section

A.4
) states that such a notion of
curve of maximal slope
is equivalent to the notion of gradient flow defined in
definition

2
.

Theorem 2
.

Let
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
be some parameter distribution.
Then a locally absolutely continuous curve
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[0,+\infty)}
is a gradient flow for the risk
\mathcal{L}
starting from
\rho_{0}
in the sense of
definition

2
if and only if it is a
curve of maximal slope
for
\mathcal{L}
, i.e., it satisfies:

1.

the map
t\in[0,+\infty)\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\rho_{t})
is non-increasing,

2.

for a.e.
t\in[0,+\infty)
the following
Evolution Dissipation Inequality
holds:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{L}(\rho_{t})\leq-\frac{1}{2}\left(\left|\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\rho_{t}\right|^{2}+\left|\nabla\mathcal{L}\right|^{2}(\rho_{t})\right),

(EDI)

with
\left|\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\rho_{t}\right|
the
metric derivative
of the curve
(\rho_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
\left|\nabla\mathcal{L}\right|
the upper-gradient defined in
eq.

19
.

We prove in
section

A.5
the following result, stating the existence and uniqueness of gradient flow curves for the training of mean-field models of Transformers.

Theorem 3
.

Let
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
be some initial parameter distribution such that
\Supp(\rho_{0})\subset[0,1]\times\left\{(V,Q,q)\in\Theta,\,\|V\|\leq R\right\}
for some
R\geq 0
.
Then there exists a unique gradient flow
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[0,+\infty)}
of the risk
\mathcal{L}
starting from
\rho_{0}
in the sense of
definition

2
.

2.4
Convergence of gradient flow curves

We turn to the convergence analysis of the previously defined gradient flow dynamics for the training of mean-field Transformers.
We explain how the geometry of the risk landscape is related to the conditioning of the
Neural Tangent Kernels (NTK)
of Attention layers.
In particular, we present a local convergence result in
theorem

4
.
In
section

3
, we will study in more detail the conditioning of such NTKs and give examples of parameter initializations for which our convergence conditions are satisfied.

Concretely, we will show here that the training risk
\mathcal{L}
satisfies a
Polyak-Łojasiewicz inequality
around well-chosen initializations of the parameters.

Definition 3

(Polyak-Łojasiewicz inequality)
.

Let
R,m\geq 0
be constants.
The training risk
\mathcal{L}
satisfies the
(R,m)
-P-Ł inequality around some parameterization
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
when it holds:

\displaystyle\forall\rho\in B(\rho_{0},R),\quad\left|\nabla\mathcal{L}\right|^{2}(\rho)\geq m\mathcal{L}(\rho),

with
\left|\nabla\mathcal{L}\right|(\rho)
the upper-gradient defined in
eq.

19
.

The P-Ł inequality is a property that has been shown to often hold for the risk associated with the training of overparameterized neural network architectures
[
30
]
.
Classical mathematical results can then be applied and give quantitative guarantees for the convergence of gradient flow
[
25
,
17
,
12
]
.

2.4.1
Neural tangent kernel and convergence under positivity assumption

As it appears in
eq.

19
, the square gradient of the risk is a quadratic function of the adjoint variables
\mathbf{m}^{(j)}
.
We explain here how good conditioning of the associated quadratic form, through the
Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK)
, implies convergence guarantees for gradient flow.

Definition 4

(Neural Tangent Kernel)
.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
be some parameter distribution.
We define the Neural Tangent Kernel (NTK) of
\rho
at time
s\in[0,1]
as the quadratic forms
\mathbb{K}[\rho](s)
on
\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{1})\times...\times\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{N})
given by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}[\rho](s)(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{m})\coloneqq\int_{\Theta}\left\|\sum_{1\leq j\leq N}\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{j}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s))^{*}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\right\|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta|s)

(22)

for all
\mathbf{m}=(\mathbf{m}^{1},...,\mathbf{m}^{N})\in\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{1})\times...\times\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{N})
.

Moreover, we denote by
\lambda_{\min}
its smallest eigenvalue w.r.t. the mixed
L^{2}
-
\mathrm{TV}
-norm, i.e.,

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}[\rho](s))\coloneqq\min_{\mathbf{m}}\mathbb{K}[\rho](s)(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{m}),

(23)

where the minimum is taken w.r.t.
\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{1})\times...\times\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{N})
such that

\displaystyle\|\mathbf{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}-\mathrm{TV}}\coloneqq\sum_{1\leq j\leq N}\|\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\|^{2}\leq 1.

The following result shows how a good conditioning of the NTK of Attention layers implies the local P-Ł inequality of
definition

3
for the training of Transformers.
As a consequence,
corollary

1
states that gradient flow converges at a linear rate when the NTKs of the Attention layers stay well-conditioned.
While hard to ensure a priori before training, such an assumption could be checked numerically during training.

Proposition 4
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\geq 0
.
Then there exists a constant
C=C(\mathcal{E})>0
s.t. for any parameter distribution
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
with
\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho)\leq\mathcal{E}
it holds

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho]\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)}\geq CN^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}[\rho](s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathcal{L}(\rho).

Proof.

First observe that, thanks to the forward ODE
eq.

8
and the linear growth of
\mathcal{A}
w.r.t.
\theta
and
\Lambda
, we have a constant
C_{1}=C_{1}(\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho))
such that it holds:

\forall j\in\left\{1,...,N\right\},\,\forall s\in[0,1],\quad\left\|\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s)\right\|\leq C_{1}.

Similarly, using the quadratic growth of
\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}
w.r.t.
\theta
and
\Lambda
, we can provide an estimate on the solutions of the backward ODE
eq.

17
of the form:

\forall j\in\left\{1,...,N\right\},\,\forall s\in[0,1],\quad\left\|\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{\rho}(s)\right\|\geq C_{2}^{-1}\left\|\nabla\ell^{j}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(1,x^{j}))\right\|,

for some constant
C_{2}=C_{2}(\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho))
.
Then by construction of the gradient field
\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho]
in
eq.

21
and of the tangent kernel in
eq.

22
we have:

\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho]\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)}=\frac{1}{N^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\mathbb{K}[\rho](s)(\mathbf{m}(s),\mathbf{m}(s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,

where
\mathbf{m}(s)=(\mathbf{m}^{1}(s),...,\mathbf{m}^{N}(s))\in\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{1})\times...\times\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{N})
.
Thus, using the previous estimate gives:

\displaystyle\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho]\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)}

\displaystyle\geq\frac{1}{N^{2}}\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}[\rho](s))\|\mathbf{m}(s)\|^{2}_{L^{2}-\mathrm{TV}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

\displaystyle\geq C_{2}^{-1}N^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}[\rho](s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)\left(N^{-1}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\left\|\nabla\ell^{j}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(1,x^{j}))\right\|^{2}\right)

\displaystyle\geq C_{2}^{-1}N^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}[\rho](s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathcal{L}(\rho),

where in the last inequality we used that the loss
\ell
satisfies a P-Ł-inequality.
∎

Corollary 1
.

Let
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
be some initial parameter distribution and let
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[0,+\infty)}
be a gradient flow curve of the risk
\mathcal{L}
starting from
\rho_{0}
.
Assume that, for every
t\geq 0
, it holds
\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho)\leq\mathcal{E}
for some
\mathcal{E}\geq 0
and there exists some
\lambda>0
s.t.

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}[\rho_{t}](s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\geq\lambda>0.

(24)

Then
\rho_{t}\xrightarrow{t\to+\infty}\rho_{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
and it holds

\displaystyle\forall t\geq 0,\quad\mathcal{L}(\rho_{t})\leq e^{-CN^{-1}\lambda t}\mathcal{L}(\rho_{0}),

for some constant
C=C(\mathcal{E})>0
.

Proof.

By the assumptions in the statement and by applying
proposition

4
, it holds for any
t\geq 0
the P-Ł-inequality:

\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}]\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{t})}\geq CN^{-1}\left(\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}[\rho_{t}](s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right)\mathcal{L}(\rho_{t}),

for some constant
C=C(\mathcal{E})
.
Since
\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}]\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{t})}
is an upper-gradient for
\mathcal{L}
, the result follows from classical results on convergence of gradient flow curves in metric spaces under a P-Ł assumption (see e.g.
[
17
,
25
]
).
∎

We conclude this section with a remark linking the positivity of the NTK to the expressivity of Attention layers.

Remark 4
.

Note that we study here the conditioning of the risk landscape by looking at the conditioning of the NTK operator
\mathbb{K}
.
In particular,
proposition

4
is meaningful only when the NTK is (strictly) positive, as a consequence of which we show the risk satisfies a Polyak-Łojasiewicz inequality.

Alternatively, such a positivity assumption on the NTK can be seen, in a dual sense, as an expressivity assumption on Attention layers.
Indeed, considering a parameter distribution
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
and
s\in[0,1]
, the assumption

\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}[\rho](s))>0

holds if and only if the space of functions

\displaystyle\Bigl\{(x^{j})_{j=1}^{N}\in S^{1}\times...\times S^{N}\mapsto\left(\int_{\Theta}\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}^{j}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s))(x^{j})\cdot v(\theta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta|s)\right)_{j=1}^{N}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{N}\colon\;v\in L^{2}(\rho(.|s))\Bigr\}

is dense in
\mathcal{C}^{0}(S^{1},\mathbb{R}^{d})\times...\times\mathcal{C}^{0}(S^{N},\mathbb{R}^{d})
.
This can be proven by following the same argument as in
[
35
, Prop. 1]
.

However, a fundamental advantage of
proposition

4
over a study of the expressivity properties of Attention layers is the possibility to compute (at least numerically) the minimal eigenvalue
\lambda_{\min}
.
This leads us to obtain quantitative convergence rates in
corollary

1
, when assuming a uniform lower-bound on this minimal eigenvalue.

2.4.2
Lower-bound on the NTK and local convergence

Of particular interest in the following will be a lower-bound on the upper-gradient, which is provided by only considering the gradient w.r.t. the linear variable
V
.
Indeed, for any parameter distribution
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
it follows from
eq.

19
that:

\displaystyle\left|\nabla\mathcal{L}\right|(\rho)\geq\left(\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\Theta}\left\|\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{D}_{V}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda_{\rho}^{j}(s))^{*}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{\rho}(s)\right\|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta,s)\right)^{1/2}\eqqcolon\left|\nabla_{V}\mathcal{L}\right|(\rho)\,.

(25)

Recall that
\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j})
is linear w.r.t. the parameter
V
, namely

\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j})(x)=\varphi_{\theta}[\Lambda^{j}_{\#}\mu^{(j)}](\Lambda^{j}(x))=V\cdot\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j})(x),

where for
\Lambda^{j}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(S^{j},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and parameter
\theta=(V,Q,q)\in\Theta
we defined:

\displaystyle\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j})(x)\coloneqq\frac{\int e^{\left<Q\Lambda^{j}(x)+q,\Lambda^{j}(y)\right>}\Lambda^{j}(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu^{(j)}(y)}{\int e^{\left<Q\Lambda^{j}(x)+q,\Lambda^{j}(y)\right>}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu^{(j)}(y)}.

(26)

In particular, with those notations:

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{D}_{V}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j})^{*}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{S^{j}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}(x)\otimes\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j})(x).

We denote by
\mathbb{K}^{1}
the kernel associated with the quadratic form in
eq.

25
. By construction, we have
\mathbb{K}\geq\mathbb{K}^{1}
, where
\mathbb{K}
was the quadratic form defined in
definition

4
.

Definition 5
.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
be some parameter distribution.
For time
s\in[0,1]
we define the quadratic forms
\mathbb{K}^{1}[\rho](s)
for all
\mathbf{m}=(\mathbf{m}^{1},...,\mathbf{m}^{N})\in\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{1})\times...\times\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{N})
by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}^{1}[\rho](s)(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{m})\coloneqq\int_{\Theta}\left\|\sum_{1\leq j\leq N}\mathrm{D}_{V}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}^{j}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s))^{*}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\right\|^{2}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta|s).

(27)

Equivalently, we have:

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}^{1}[\rho](s)(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{m})=\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\left<\mathbf{m}^{(i)},K^{1}_{i,j}[\rho](s)\star\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\right>,

where
\star
is the convolution operator and for indices
i,j\in\left\{1,...,N\right\}
the kernel function
K^{1}_{i,j}[\rho](s)\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(S^{i}\times S^{j},\mathbb{R}^{d\times d})
is given by:

\displaystyle\forall(x,y)\in S^{i}\times S^{j},\quad K^{1}_{i,j}[\rho](s)(x,y)\coloneqq\int_{\Theta}\mathcal{M}^{i}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{i}(s))(x)\otimes\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}(s))(y)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta|s).

As in
definition

4
, we denote by
\lambda_{\min}
the smallest eigenvalue w.r.t. the mixed
L^{2}
-
\mathrm{TV}
-norm, i.e.

\displaystyle\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}^{1}[\rho](s))\coloneqq\min_{\mathbf{m}}\mathbb{K}^{1}[\rho](s)(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{m}),

(28)

where the minimum is taken w.r.t.
\mathbf{m}\in\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{1})\times...\times\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{N})
s.t.
\|\mathbf{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}-\mathrm{TV}}\leq 1
.

Example 2

(Finite number of tokens)
.

Of particular interest is the case of a finite number of tokens, that is, when the token distributions are empirical distributions of the form
\mu^{(j)}=\frac{1}{n^{j}}\sum_{i=1}^{n^{j}}\delta_{x^{j}_{i}}
.
In this case, the adjoint variables can be identified as families of vectors
\mathbf{m}^{(j)}=(\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{i})_{0\leq i\leq n^{j}}\in(\mathbb{R}^{d})^{n^{j}+1}
(assuming all tokens are distinct) and we have:

\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{D}_{V}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s))^{*}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}=\sum_{j=1}^{N}\sum_{i=0}^{n^{j}}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}_{i}(s)\otimes\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s))(x^{j}_{i}).

The kernel matrix
\mathbb{K}^{1}
thus corresponds to a finite-dimensional matrix of size
n\times n
, where
n=\sum_{j=1}^{N}(n^{j}+1)
is the total number of tokens used for training.
Its entries are defined at time
s\in[0,1]
, for indices
j,j^{\prime}\in\{1,...,N\}
,
i\in\{0,...,n^{j}\}
and
i^{\prime}\in\{0,...,n^{j^{\prime}}\}
by:

\displaystyle\mathbb{K}^{1}_{(j,i),(j^{\prime},i^{\prime})}[\rho](s)\coloneqq{}

\displaystyle\int_{\Theta}\left<\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s))(x^{j}_{i}),\mathcal{M}^{j^{\prime}}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j^{\prime}}_{\rho}(s))(x^{j^{\prime}}_{i^{\prime}})\right>\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta|s).

The following result shows that, provided
\mathbb{K}^{1}
is strictly positive at initialization and the risk at initialization is sufficiently small, gradient flow converges towards a global minimizer of the risk with a linear convergence rate.
In particular, the convergence rate is explicit w.r.t. the conditioning of
\mathbb{K}^{1}
and the sample size
N
.
In contrast with
corollary

1
, the positivity of
\mathbb{K}^{1}
only needs to be checked here at initialization.

Theorem 4
.

Let
\mathcal{E}\geq 0
.
Then there exists a constant
C=C(\mathcal{E})>0
such that, for any parameter distribution
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
satisfying
\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho_{0})\leq\mathcal{E}
and

\mathcal{L}(\rho_{0})<CN^{-3}\lambda_{0}^{3},\quad\text{with}\quad\lambda_{0}\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}^{1}[\rho_{0}](s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,

any gradient flow curve
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[0,+\infty)}
starting from
\rho_{0}
satisfies:

\rho_{t}\xrightarrow{t\to+\infty}\rho_{\infty}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta),\quad\text{and}\quad\mathcal{L}(\rho_{t})\leq\mathcal{L}(\rho_{0})\exp(-CN^{-1}\lambda_{0}t),\,\forall t\geq 0.

Proof.

Consider
C_{1}=C(2\mathcal{E}+2)
the constant given by
lemma

1
and let
R=\min(1,\frac{\lambda_{0}}{2C_{1}N})
.
Then by
lemma

1
, for every
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
with
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\rho_{0})\leq R
it holds:

\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}^{1}[\rho](s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\geq\lambda_{0}/2,

and, hence, by
proposition

4
:

\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho]\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)}\geq C_{2}N^{-1}\lambda_{0}\mathcal{L}(\rho),

for some constant
C_{2}=C_{2}(\mathcal{E})>0
.
The result then follows from
[
17
, Thm. 1.4]
.
∎

Lemma 1
.

For a parameter distribution
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
, let
\mathbb{K}^{1}
be defined by
eq.

27
.
Then the map
\rho\mapsto\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}^{1}[\rho](s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s
is locally Lipschitz.
Precisely, for parameter distributions
\rho,\tilde{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
and corresponding kernel matrices, we have:

\displaystyle\left|\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}^{1}[\rho](s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s-\int_{0}^{1}\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}^{1}[\tilde{\rho}](s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s\right|\leq CN\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\tilde{\rho}),

where
C=C(\mathcal{E})
is a constant depending on
\mathcal{E}=\max(\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho),\mathcal{E}_{2}(\tilde{\rho}))
.

Proof.

Consider the map
\mathcal{M}^{j}:\Theta\times\mathcal{C}^{0}(S^{j},\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{C}^{0}(S^{j},\mathbb{R}^{d})
defined in
eq.

26
.
Observe that, proceeding as the proof of
lemmas

4
and
5
we can find a constant
C\geq 0
s.t. for every index
j\in\left\{1,...,N\right\}
, every
\Lambda\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(S^{j},\mathbb{R}^{d})
and every
\theta\in\Theta
it holds:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda)\right\|\leq C(1+\|\Lambda\|^{2})(1+\|\theta\|)\quad\text{and}\quad\left\|\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda)\right\|\leq C(1+\|\Lambda\|^{3}).

(29)

We can use the above estimates to bound the quantity
\left\|K^{1}_{i,j}[\rho](s)-K^{1}_{i,j}[\tilde{\rho}](s)\right\|
.
Indeed, fixing indices
i,j\in\left\{1,...,N\right\}
, it follows from the linear growth of
\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{M}^{j}
w.r.t.
\theta
in
eq.

29
and
lemma

7
that for every
(s,\theta)\in[0,1]\times\Theta
and every
x\in S^{j}
, we have:

\left\|\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s))(x)-\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}_{\tilde{\rho}}(s))(x)\right\|\leq C\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\tilde{\rho}),

for some constant
C=C(\mathcal{E})
. Taking an outer product, we have analogously for every
(x,y)\in S^{i}\times S^{j}
:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{M}^{i}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{i}_{\rho}(s))(x)\otimes\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s))(y)-\mathcal{M}^{i}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{i}_{\tilde{\rho}}(s))(x)\otimes\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}_{\tilde{\rho}}(s))(y)\right\|

\displaystyle\hskip 200.0003pt\leq C\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\tilde{\rho}),

for some constant
C=C(\mathcal{E})
.
Then using the boundedness of
\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{M}
w.r.t.
\theta
in
eq.

29
, we have for every
s\in[0,1]
, every
\theta,\tilde{\theta}\in\Theta
and every
x\in S^{i}
:

\left\|\mathcal{M}^{i}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{i}_{\rho}(s))(x)-\mathcal{M}^{i}_{\tilde{\theta}}(\Lambda^{i}_{\rho}(s))(x)\right\|\leq C\|\theta-\tilde{\theta}\|,

for some constant
C=C(\mathcal{E})
.
Taking an outer product, we have analogously for every
(x,y)\in S^{i}\times S^{j}
:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{M}^{i}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{i}_{\rho}(s))(x)\otimes\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s))(y)-\mathcal{M}^{i}_{\tilde{\theta}}(\Lambda^{i}_{\rho}(s))(x)\otimes\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\tilde{\theta}}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s))(y)\right\|

\displaystyle\hskip 200.0003pt\leq C\|\theta-\tilde{\theta}\|,

for some constant
C=C(\mathcal{E})
.

At time
s\in[0,1]
, consider an optimal coupling
\gamma\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(\Theta\times\Theta)
between
\rho(.|s)
and
\tilde{\rho}(.|s)
.
Then, using the definition of
\mathbb{K}^{1}_{i,j}[\rho](s)
in
definition

5
and the previous estimates, we obtain for every
(x,y)\in S^{i}\times S^{j}
:

\displaystyle\left\|K^{1}_{i,j}[\rho](s)(x,y)-K^{1}_{i,j}[\tilde{\rho}](s)(x,y)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\int_{\Theta}\Bigl\|\mathcal{M}^{i}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{i}_{\rho}(s))(x)\otimes\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s))(y)-\mathcal{M}^{i}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{i}_{\tilde{\rho}}(s))(x)\otimes\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}_{\tilde{\rho}}(s))(y)\Bigr\|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\tilde{\rho}(\theta|s)

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{\Theta\times\Theta}\Bigl\|\mathcal{M}^{i}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{i}_{\rho}(s))(x)\otimes\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s))(y)-\mathcal{M}^{i}_{\tilde{\theta}}(\Lambda^{i}_{\rho}(s))(x)\otimes\mathcal{M}^{j}_{\tilde{\theta}}(\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s))(y)\Bigr\|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\gamma(\theta,\tilde{\theta})

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})+\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho(.|s),\tilde{\rho}(.|s))\right),

for some constant
C=C(\mathcal{E})
.
Let
\Delta K_{i,j}(s)\coloneqq K^{1}_{i,j}[\rho](s)-K^{1}_{i,j}[\tilde{\rho}](s)
.
Hence, for
\mathbf{m}=(\mathbf{m}^{(1)},...,\mathbf{m}^{(N)})\in\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{1})\times...\times\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{N})
we obtain:

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{K}^{1}[\rho](s)(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{m})-\mathbb{K}^{1}[\tilde{\rho}](s)(\mathbf{m},\mathbf{m})\right|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\left|\left<\mathbf{m}^{(i)},\Delta K_{i,j}(s)\star\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\right>\right|

\displaystyle\leq C\left(\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})+\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho(.|s),\tilde{\rho}(.|s))\right)\sum_{1\leq i,j\leq N}\|\mathbf{m}^{(i)}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}\|\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\|_{\mathrm{TV}}

\displaystyle\leq CN\left(\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})+\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho(.|s),\tilde{\rho}(.|s))\right)\|\mathbf{m}\|^{2}_{L^{2}-\mathrm{TV}},

implying

\left|\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}^{1}[\rho](s))-\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}^{1}[\tilde{\rho}](s))\right|\leq CN\left(\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\tilde{\rho})+\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho(.|s),\tilde{\rho}(.|s))\right)

for some constant
C=C(\mathcal{E})
. The result then follows by integrating over
s\in[0,1]
.
∎

3
Conditioning analysis of the NTK of Attention

In the previous section, we studied the training dynamics of deep Transformers with gradient flow and linked the geometry of the risk landscape with the conditioning of the tangent kernels of Attention layers.
Concretely, we showed in
corollary

1
that if the NTKs of Attention layers stay well-conditioned during training, then the gradient flow converges towards a global minimizer of the training risk at a linear rate.
We also showed a local convergence result in
theorem

4
, assuming the NTKs associated with the
V
-derivatives of Attention layers are positive at initialization. In this section, we study the conditioning of the NTK of Attention layers and explain to what extent our convergence assumptions are satisfied for generic token distributions.
Precisely,
proposition

8
characterizes the positivity of the Attention’s NTK in terms of linear independence of log-sum-exp (cumulant generating) functions of the token distributions.
In
section

3.3
we then discuss this property for various examples of token distributions.
Importantly, in
proposition

9
and
proposition

10
, we show that it is always almost surely satisfied for empirical token distributions.

3.1
NTK positivity via Attention feature map injectivity

In
definitions

4
and
5
, we framed the conditioning of the Attention’s NTK in terms of the positivity of the associated quadratic form, as quantified by the smallest eigenvalue,
\lambda_{\min}
. In this section, we will focus on an equivalent (but more qualitative) characterization of positivity in terms of the injectivity of some linear map.

Proposition 5
.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
be some parameter distribution.
For
s\in[0,1]
let
\mathbb{K}[\rho](s)
and
\mathbb{K}^{1}[\rho](s)
be defined by
definition

4
and
definition

5
respectively.
Then for every
s\in[0,1]
, we have
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}[\rho](s))>0
if and only if the map

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{j})\ni(\mathbf{m}^{(j)})\mapsto\left[\Theta\ni\theta\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{S^{j}}\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\varphi_{\theta}[\Lambda^{j}(s)_{\#}\mu^{(j)}](\Lambda^{j}(s,x))^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}(x)\right]\in L^{2}(\rho(.|s))

(30)

is injective. Similarly, we have
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}^{1}[\rho](s))>0
if and only if the map

\displaystyle\prod_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{j})\ni(\mathbf{m}^{(j)})\mapsto\left[\Theta\ni\theta\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int_{S^{j}}\mathrm{D}_{V}\varphi_{\theta}[\Lambda^{j}(s)_{\#}\mu^{j}](\Lambda^{j}(s,x))^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}(x)\right]\in L^{2}(\rho(.|s))

(31)

is injective.
In particular, in the case
\Supp(\rho)\subset[0,1]\times\{(V,Q,q)\in\Theta\colon V=0\}
, then
\Lambda^{j}_{\rho}(s)=\mathrm{Id}
for every index
j\in\{1,...,N\}
and the above conditions do not depend on
s\in[0,1]
.

Proof.

We only provide the proof for
\mathbb{K}
since the proof for
\mathbb{K}^{1}
is similar.
It directly follows from
eq.

22
that
\lambda_{\min}(\mathbb{K}[\rho](s))>0
is equivalent to the injectivity of the map:

\prod_{j=1}^{N}\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{j})\ni(\mathbf{m}^{(j)})\mapsto\left[\Theta\ni\theta\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{N}\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}(s))^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\right]\in L^{2}(\rho(.|s)).

Recalling the definition of the map
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
in
eq.

6
, for every index
j\in\{1,...,N\}
and every
x\in S^{j}
:

\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}(s))(x)=\varphi_{\theta}[\Lambda^{j}(s)_{\#}\mu^{j}](\Lambda^{j}(s,x)),

where
\varphi_{\theta}
is the Attention layer as defined in
eq.

1
.
Then taking derivatives w.r.t.
\theta
, it follows that for
\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\in\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{j})
:

\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}^{j}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{j}(s))^{*}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}=\int_{S^{j}}\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\varphi_{\theta}[\Lambda^{j}(s)_{\#}\mu^{(j)}](\Lambda^{j}(s,x))^{*}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mathbf{m}^{(j)}(x),

which concludes the proof.
∎

In what follows, we fix some parameter distribution
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
and study the injectivity of the maps in

30
and
31
.
We will assume that this parameter distribution is of the product form
\rho=\mathrm{Leb}([0,1])\otimes\bar{\rho}
for some
\bar{\rho}\in\mathcal{P}(\Theta)
.
In this setting, we will, without loss of generality, assume that the parameter space is

and we will check the pointwise injectivity of the maps in

and

3.2
Necessary and sufficient condition for injective NTK

In what follows, we assume for simplicity that
\Lambda^{j}(s)=\mathrm{Id}

We define the cumulant generating functions by

and cumulant generating functions for fixed angle

Informally,

\langle q,y\rangle
and
\tilde{q}\langle e,y\rangle

and thus provide smooth approximations of their maxima.
Furthermore, we define the set of affine functions by

\mathrm{Affine}:=\{\mathbb{R}^{d}\ni q\mapsto\langle a,q\rangle+b\ :\ a\in\mathbb{R}^{d},b\in\mathbb{R}\}.

We define the following notion:

Definition 6

We say that

\{g_{\mu^{(j)}}\}_{j=1}^{N}

\sum_{j=1}^{N}C_{j}g_{\mu^{(j)}}\in\mathrm{Affine}\ \Rightarrow\ C_{j}=0\quad\forall j=1,\dots,N.

(32)

We say that

satisfies the strong independence property
if there exists

such that

C_{0}\mathrm{id}+\sum_{j=1}^{N}C_{j}g_{\mu^{(j)},e}=0\ \Rightarrow\ C_{j}=0\quad\forall j=0,\dots,N.

(33)

We immediately observe that the following holds.

Proposition 7
.

The strong independence property implies the weak independence property.

We have the following proposition.

Proposition 8
.

If
\{\mu^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{N}
satisfies the weak independence property, then, the map

(\mathbf{m}^{(1)},...,\mathbf{m}^{(N)})\in\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{1})\times...\times\mathcal{M}^{d}(S^{N})\mapsto\left[\Theta\ni\theta\mapsto\sum_{j=1}^{N}\int\mathrm{D}_{V}\varphi_{\theta}[\mu^{(j)}](x)^{*}d\mathbf{m}^{(j)}\right]

\bigcap_{j=1}^{N}S^{j}\text{ has at least two distinct points.}

Lemma 2
.

Let
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, and let
G,H:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}
be continuous functions.
Assume that

1.

K:=\Supp(\mu)
is compact, and

2.

H>0
on
K
.

Then

\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{\displaystyle{\int H(y)e^{sG(y)}G(y)\,d\mu(y)}}{\displaystyle{\int H(y)e^{sG(y)}\,d\mu(y)}}=\max_{y\in\Supp(\mu)}G(y).

Proof.

Set

M:=\max_{y\in K}G(y),\qquad K=\Supp(\mu).

We define

D_{s}:=\int H(y)e^{sG(y)}\,d\mu(y),\qquad N_{s}:=\int H(y)e^{sG(y)}G(y)\,d\mu(y).

We will prove that

\frac{N_{s}}{D_{s}}\longrightarrow M\qquad(s\to\infty).

First, since
G(y)\leq M
on
K
, we have

N_{s}=\int H(y)e^{sG(y)}G(y)\,d\mu(y)\leq M\int H(y)e^{sG(y)}\,d\mu(y)=MD_{s}.

Hence,

\frac{N_{s}}{D_{s}}\leq M\qquad\text{for all }s.

It remains to prove the lower bound. Fix
\varepsilon>0
and consider

U_{\varepsilon}:=\{y\in K:\ G(y)>M-\varepsilon\}.

Since
G
is continuous and attains its maximum
M
on compact set
K
, we have

\mu(U_{\varepsilon})>0.

Moreover, since
H
is continuous and strictly positive on the compact set
K
,
there exists

m_{H}:=\min_{y\in K}H(y)>0.

For the definitions, we obtain

MD_{s}-N_{s}=\int H(y)e^{sG(y)}(M-G(y))\,d\mu(y).

Since
M-G(y)\geq 0
, we estimate

MD_{s}-N_{s}=\int_{K}H(y)e^{sG(y)}(M-G(y))\,d\mu(y)\leq\varepsilon\int_{U_{\varepsilon}}H(y)e^{sG(y)}\,d\mu(y)+C\int_{K\setminus U_{\varepsilon}}H(y)e^{sG(y)}\,d\mu(y),

where

C:=\max_{y\in K}(M-G(y))<\infty.

Dividing by
D_{s}
gives

M-\frac{N_{s}}{D_{s}}\leq\varepsilon+C\,\frac{\int_{K\setminus U_{\varepsilon}}H(y)e^{sG(y)}\,d\mu(y)}{\int_{K}H(y)e^{sG(y)}\,d\mu(y)}.

But then it suffices to show that, as
s\to\infty
,

\frac{\int_{K\setminus U_{\varepsilon}}H(y)e^{sG(y)}\,d\mu(y)}{\int_{K}H(y)e^{sG(y)}\,d\mu(y)}\longrightarrow 0.

For
y\in K\setminus U_{\varepsilon}
, we have
G(y)\leq M-\varepsilon
, so that

\int_{K\setminus U_{\varepsilon}}H(y)e^{sG(y)}\,d\mu(y)\leq e^{s(M-\varepsilon)}\int_{K}H(y)\,d\mu(y).

Suppose we choose
0<\varepsilon^{\prime}<\varepsilon
. Then the set,

U_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}:=\{y\in K:\ G(y)>M-\varepsilon^{\prime}\},

also satisfies
\mu(U_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})>0
, and on
U_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}
we have

G(y)>M-\varepsilon^{\prime}
. Therefore,

\int_{K}H(y)e^{sG(y)}\,d\mu(y)\geq m_{H}e^{s(M-\varepsilon^{\prime})}\mu(U_{\varepsilon^{\prime}}).

Combining the last two estimates, we obtain

\frac{\int_{K\setminus U_{\varepsilon}}H(y)e^{sG(y)}\,d\mu(y)}{\int_{K}H(y)e^{sG(y)}\,d\mu(y)}\leq\frac{\int_{K}H\,d\mu}{m_{H}\,\mu(U_{\varepsilon^{\prime}})}e^{-s(\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime})}.

Since
\varepsilon-\varepsilon^{\prime}>0
, the right-hand side tends to
0
as

s\to\infty
. Hence,

\limsup_{s\to\infty}\left(M-\frac{N_{s}}{D_{s}}\right)\leq\varepsilon.

Because
\varepsilon>0
was arbitrary, we get

\liminf_{s\to\infty}\frac{N_{s}}{D_{s}}\geq M.

Together with the already proven upper bound,
\frac{N_{s}}{D_{s}}\leq M
, this implies

\lim_{s\to\infty}\frac{N_{s}}{D_{s}}=M.

This completes the proof.
∎

If

Lemma 3
.

We assume that

C_{0}q+\sum_{j=1}^{N}C_{j}\log\left(a+\sum_{r=1}^{m_{j}}a_{r}^{(j)}\exp{(-qy_{r}^{(j)})}\right)=0\quad\text{for all $q>0$},

where
C_{j}\in\mathbb{R}
, and
a,a_{r}^{(j)}>0
, and
y^{(j)}_{r}>0
satisfying that for each
j\in[N]

y^{(j)}_{p}\neq y^{(j)}_{q}\quad\text{ for all }p\neq q

and

\alpha_{j}\neq\alpha_{i}\quad\text{ for all }j\neq i,

where
\alpha_{j}=\min\{y^{(j)}_{r}:r=1,...,m_{j}\}
. Then we have,

C_{j}=0\quad\text{ for all }j=0,1,2,...,N.

Proof.

Without loss of generality, we can assume that
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j\in[N]}
is in increasing order, i.e.,

0<\alpha_{1}<\alpha_{2}<\alpha_{3}<\cdots<\alpha_{N}.

The assumption implies that, by the Taylor expansion of

,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=C_{0}q+\sum_{j=1}^{N}C_{j}\log\left(a+\sum_{r=1}^{m_{j}}a_{r}^{(j)}\exp{(-qy_{r}^{(j)})}\right)

\displaystyle=C_{0}q+\sum_{j=1}^{N}C_{j}\log a+\sum_{j=1}^{N}C_{j}\sum_{r=1}^{m_{j}}\frac{a_{r}^{(j)}}{a}\exp{(-qy_{r}^{(j)})}+O(\exp{(-2q\alpha_{1})})

\displaystyle=C_{0}q+\sum_{j=1}^{N}C_{j}\log a+C_{1}\frac{a_{r^{*}}^{(1)}}{a}\exp{(-q\alpha_{1})}+C_{1}\sum_{r\neq r^{*}}\frac{a_{r}^{(1)}}{a}\exp{(-qy_{r}^{(1)})}+o(\exp{(-q\alpha_{1})})

\displaystyle=C_{0}q+\sum_{j=1}^{N}C_{j}\log a+C_{1}\frac{a_{r^{*}}^{(1)}}{a}\exp{(-q\alpha_{1})}+o(\exp{(-q\alpha_{1})}),

because
\alpha_{1}=\min_{r}y_{r}^{(1)}
. We also have denoted by
r^{*}:=\mathrm{argmin}\{y^{(1)}_{r}:r=1,...,m_{1}\}
.
Multiplying by

and taking

Taking the limit as

Multiplying by
\exp{(q\alpha_{1})}
and taking the limit as

In a similar way,

\displaystyle 0

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{N}C_{j}\log\left(a+\sum_{r=1}^{m_{j}}a_{r}^{(j)}\exp{(-qy_{r}^{(j)})}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{j=2}^{N}C_{j}\sum_{r=1}^{m_{j}}\frac{a_{r}^{(j)}}{a}\exp{(-qy_{r}^{(j)})}+\mathcal{O}(\exp{(-2q\alpha_{2})})

\displaystyle=C_{2}\frac{a_{r^{**}}^{(2)}}{a}\exp{(-q\alpha_{2})}+o(\exp{(-q\alpha_{2})}).

3.3.2
Examples of measures with densities

We explore examples of measure with densities. We first prove the following lemma:

Proposition 11
.

Let
N\in\mathbb{N}
and let
f_{1},\dots,f_{N}
be defined on a neighborhood of
0
with the following expansions

f_{j}(q)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\alpha_{k}\,s_{j}^{\,k}\,q^{2k},\qquad j=1,\dots,N,

where
\alpha_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
and
s_{j}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. Assume that

•

\alpha_{k}\neq 0
for
k=1,\dots,N
;

•

s_{i}\neq s_{j}
if
i\neq j
.

Then the family

\{\operatorname{Id},f_{1},\dots,f_{N}\}

is linearly independent.

Proof.

Suppose that

c_{0}q+\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}f_{j}(q)=0.

Since each
f_{j}
is even, the left-hand side is even, hence
c_{0}=0
. Differentiating
2k
times at
0
for
k=1,\dots,N
gives

0=\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}f_{j}^{(2k)}(0)=(2k)!\,\alpha_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}s_{j}^{\,k}.

Because
\alpha_{k}\neq 0
, we have

\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}s_{j}^{\,k}=0,\qquad k=1,\dots,N.

Let
V=(s_{j}^{\,k})_{1\leq k,j\leq N}
, which is a Vandermonde matrix. Since the
s_{j}
are pairwise distinct,
V
is invertible; hence,
c_{j}=0
for all
j\in[N]
.
∎

Example 3

(Uniform distributions)
.

Let
0<a_{1}<\dots<a_{N}
and let
\mu^{(j)}
be the law of
X_{j}\sim\operatorname{Unif}([-a_{j},a_{j}]^{d})
. For any
e\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
,

\{\operatorname{Id},g_{\mu^{(1)},e},\dots,g_{\mu^{(N)},e}\}

is linearly independent. Thus,
\{\mu^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{N}
satisfies the strong independence property.

Proof.

Let
e=(e_{1},\dots,e_{d})\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. We have

g_{\mu^{(j)},e}(q)=\log\left[\int e^{q\langle e,y\rangle}d\mu^{(j)}\right]=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\log\left(\frac{\sinh(a_{j}e_{i}q)}{a_{j}e_{i}q}\right).

We observe that, in one dimension, the cumulant generating function,

g_{\operatorname{Unif}([-a,a])}(q)=\log\left(\frac{\sinh(aq)}{aq}\right),

admits an even power series expansion

g_{\operatorname{Unif}([-a,a])}(q)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\gamma_{k}\,a^{2k}q^{2k},

with real coefficients
\gamma_{k}\neq 0
for all
k
. Using this fact, we see that

g_{\mu^{(j)},e}(q)=\sum_{i=1}^{d}\sum_{k\geq 1}\gamma_{k}(a_{j}e_{i})^{2k}q^{2k}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\gamma_{k}\Bigl(a^{2k}_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{d}e_{i}^{2k}\Bigr)q^{2k}.

Thus
g_{\mu^{(j)},e}
has the form

g_{\mu^{(j)},e}(q)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\gamma_{k}\,s_{j}^{\,k}\,q^{2k},\qquad s_{j}:=a^{2k}_{j}\sum_{i=1}^{d}e_{i}^{2k},

where
s_{j}\neq 0
are pairwise distinct. By applying
proposition

11
, we obtain the claim.
∎

Example 4

(Symmetric multivariate Laplace)
.

Let
\mu^{(j)}
be the law
X_{j}\sim\operatorname{Lap}(0,\Sigma_{j})
,
j=1,\dots,N
, where
\Sigma_{j}
are positive semidefinite
d\times d
matrices. Fix
e\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and set

s_{j}:=\tfrac{1}{2}e^{\top}\Sigma_{j}e.

Assume that
s_{1},\dots,s_{N}
are pairwise distinct and nonzero. Then,

\{\operatorname{Id},g_{\mu^{(1)},e},\dots,g_{\mu^{(N)},e}\}

is linearly independent. Thus,
\{\mu^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{N}
satisfies the strong independence property.

Proof.

Let
\Sigma
be a positive semidefinite
d\times d
matrix.
A centered symmetric multivariate Laplace distribution

\operatorname{Lap}(0,\Sigma)
can be defined via its characteristic function

\varphi_{X}(t):=\mathbb{E}_{X\sim\operatorname{Lap}(0,\Sigma)}\bigl[e^{i\langle t,X\rangle}\bigr]=\frac{1}{1+\tfrac{1}{2}t^{\top}\Sigma t},\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

Let
X\sim\operatorname{Lap}(0,\Sigma)
with law
\mu
, and fix
e\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
. Then the one-dimensional projection

Y:=\langle e,X\rangle

has characteristic function

\varphi_{Y}(q)=\mathbb{E}_{Y}\bigl[e^{iqY}\bigr]=\varphi_{X}(qe)=\frac{1}{1+\tfrac{1}{2}q^{2}e^{\top}\Sigma e},\qquad q\in\mathbb{R}.

Thus the moment generating function is given by

M_{Y}(q)=\varphi_{Y}(-iq)=\mathbb{E}_{Y}\bigl[e^{qY}\bigr]=\frac{1}{1-\tfrac{1}{2}q^{2}e^{\top}\Sigma e}.

This implies that

g_{\mu,e}(q)=-\log\Bigl(1-sq^{2}\Bigr),\qquad s:=\tfrac{1}{2}e^{\top}\Sigma e.

Expanding the logarithm yields

g_{\mu,e}(q)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{k}s^{k}q^{2k}.

Thus, we have for each
j

g_{\mu^{(j)},e}(q)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\frac{1}{k}s_{j}^{\,k}q^{2k},

where
s_{1},\dots,s_{N}
are pairwise distinct by assumption. Hence, by
proposition

11
, we obtain the claim.
∎

We now show that linear independence of the cumulant functions is stable under convolution with centered Gaussian measures.

Proposition 12

(Stability under centered Gaussian convolution)
.

Let
\mu^{(1)},\dots,\mu^{(N)}
be probability measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
and fix
e\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
.
Suppose that for each
j

g_{\mu^{(j)},e}(q)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\alpha_{k}s_{j}^{\,k}q^{2k},

where
\alpha_{k}\neq 0
and
s_{1},\dots,s_{N}
are pairwise distinct and nonzero.
Let
\nu^{(j)}=\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{j})
be centered Gaussian measures on
\mathbb{R}^{d}
with covariances
\Sigma_{j}
and define

\widetilde{\mu}^{(j)}:=\mu^{(j)}*\nu^{(j)}.

Then

\{\operatorname{Id},g_{\widetilde{\mu}^{(1)},e},\dots,g_{\widetilde{\mu}^{(N)},e}\}

is linearly independent. Thus,
\{\widetilde{\mu}^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{N}
satisfies the strong independence property.

Proof.

By the property of the convolution, we have

g_{\widetilde{\mu}^{(j)},e}(q)=g_{\mu^{(j)},e}(q)+\frac{1}{2}\,(e^{\top}\Sigma_{j}e)\,q^{2}.

Suppose that

c_{0}\,q+\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}g_{\widetilde{\mu}^{(j)},e}(q)=0.

As the functions
g_{\widetilde{\mu}^{(j)},e}
are even,
we see that
c_{0}=0
. For
k\geq 2
, the quadratic terms vanish, so that

0=\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}g_{\mu^{(j)},e}^{(2k)}(0)=(2k)!\,\alpha_{k}\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}s_{j}^{\,k},\qquad k=2,\dots,N+1.

Since
\alpha_{k}\neq 0
for
k=2,\dots,N+1
, we obtain

\sum_{j=1}^{N}c_{j}s_{j}^{\,k}=0,\qquad k=2,\dots,N+1.

This is again a Vandermonde system in the
s_{j}
(just with powers starting at
2
), and thus
c_{j}=0
for all
j
.
∎

Example 5

(Two-component multivariate Gaussian mixtures)
.

Fix
e\in\mathbb{S}^{d-1}
and let
a_{1},\dots,a_{N}>0
be pairwise distinct.
Let

\mu^{(j)}:=\frac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{N}(a_{j}e,\Sigma_{j})+\frac{1}{2}\,\mathcal{N}(-a_{j}e,\Sigma_{j}),\qquad j=1,\dots,N,

where each
\Sigma_{j}
is a symmetric positive semidefinite covariance matrix. Then

\{\operatorname{Id},g_{\mu^{(1)},e},\dots,g_{\mu^{(N)},e}\}

is linearly independent. Thus,
\{\mu^{(j)}\}_{j=1}^{N}
satisfies the strong independence property.

Proof.

We can view
\mu^{(j)}
as the convolution of the symmetric two-point measure,

\nu^{(j)}:=\tfrac{1}{2}(\delta_{a_{j}e}+\delta_{-a_{j}e})

\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{j})

\mu^{(j)}=\nu^{(j)}*\mathcal{N}(0,\Sigma_{j}).

For the discrete measure
\nu^{(j)}

\langle e,X\rangle
takes values

\pm a_{j}
with equal probability, so its moment generating function is

\mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{q\langle e,X\rangle}\bigr]=\frac{1}{2}\bigl(e^{qa_{j}}+e^{-qa_{j}}\bigr)=\cosh(a_{j}q),

and, hence,

g_{\nu^{(j)},e}(q)=\log\cosh(a_{j}q).

Expanding around
q=0
yields

g_{\nu^{(j)},e}(q)=\sum_{k\geq 1}\beta_{k}a_{j}^{2k}q^{2k}=\sum_{k\geq 1}\beta_{k}s_{j}^{k}q^{2k},\quad\text{ where }s_{j}=a_{j}^{2},

where
\beta_{k}\in\mathbb{R}
are nonzero for all
k
. Thus the family
\{g_{\nu^{(1)},e},\dots,g_{\nu^{(N)},e}\}
satisfies the assumptions
of Lemma
12
with
s_{j}:=a_{j}^{2}
.
Consequently,

\{\operatorname{Id},g_{\mu^{(1)},e},\dots,g_{\mu^{(N)},e}\}

is linearly independent.
∎

4
Conclusion

This paper provides a mathematical foundation for gradient-based training of transformers in the joint infinite-depth and infinite-width limit. We show that, unlike ResNets whose training follows a neural ODE on features, transformers are governed by a neural PDE that couples token distributions through attention, a structure arising from the simultaneous mean-field limits over tokens and heads. Using tools from optimal transport and Wasserstein gradient flows, we establish well-posedness of both the forward dynamics and training evolution, and characterize NTK injectivity for attention via linear independence of log-sum-exp functions. Although our convergence result is local, it applies to realistic deep, multi-head softmax attention on continuous token distributions and reveals an optimization landscape without spurious local minima when initialization is sufficiently close to optimal.
Several directions remain for future work. Our analysis is restricted to encoder-style (non-causal) attention; extending the framework to decoder architectures with causal masking poses substantial technical challenges, since the triangular structure of causal attention breaks the symmetries used in our proofs. In addition, practical transformers typically scale the embedding dimension with the number of heads. Capturing this coupled scaling within a mean-field limit remains an open problem, as our current formulation keeps the head dimension fixed while letting the number of heads grow to infinity.

Regularity of

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\rho_{0}(.|r)}(\Lambda_{0}(r)+ut\Gamma_{t}(r))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{C}^{0}(S,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\mathcal{C}^{0}(S,\mathbb{R}^{d}))

\displaystyle b_{t}(r)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\Theta}\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta+utv(r,\theta)}(\Lambda_{t}(r))\cdot v(r,\theta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta|r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}u\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(S,\mathbb{R}^{d})\,.

Let us further define:

\displaystyle A_{0}(r)

\displaystyle\coloneqq D_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\rho_{0}(.|r)}(\Lambda_{0}(r))\in\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{C}^{0}(S,\mathbb{R}^{d}),\mathcal{C}^{0}(S,\mathbb{R}^{d}))\,,

\displaystyle b_{0}(r)

\displaystyle\coloneqq\int_{\Theta}\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda_{0}(r))\cdot v(r,\theta)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta|r)\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(S,\mathbb{R}^{d})\,.

such that
\Gamma_{0}
is by definition solution of the Cauchy problem
\Gamma_{0}(s)=\int_{0}^{s}\left(A_{0}(r)\cdot\Gamma_{0}(r)+b_{0}(r)\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r
.
Then for every
s\in[0,1]
it holds:

\displaystyle\left\|\Gamma_{t}(s)-\Gamma_{0}(s)\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{s}\left\|A_{t}(r)\cdot\Gamma_{t}(r)-A_{0}(r)\cdot\Gamma_{0}(r)\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\quad+\int_{0}^{s}\left\|b_{t}(r)-b_{0}(r)\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle\leq\int_{0}^{s}\Bigl(\left\|A_{t}(r)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{C}^{0},\mathcal{C}^{0})}\left\|\Gamma_{t}(r)-\Gamma_{0}(r)\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}

\displaystyle\quad+\left\|A_{t}(r)-A_{0}(r)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{C}^{0},\mathcal{C}^{0})}\left\|\Gamma_{0}(r)\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}+\left\|b_{t}(r)-b_{0}(r)\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}\Bigr)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r,

leading, by an application of Grönwall’s lemma, to:

\displaystyle\left\|\Gamma_{t}(s)-\Gamma_{0}(s)\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}

\displaystyle\leq e^{\int_{0}^{1}\left\|A_{t}(r)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{C}^{0},\mathcal{C}^{0})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}

\displaystyle\quad\times\int_{0}^{1}\left(\left\|A_{t}(r)-A_{0}(r)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{C}^{0},\mathcal{C}^{0})}+\left\|b_{t}(r)-b_{0}(r)\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r.

Now, by
lemma

7
we have
\left\|\Gamma_{t}(r)\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}\leq\|v\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}
for every
t\neq 0
and every
r\in[0,1]
.
Also, the maps
\Lambda\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(S,\mathbb{R}^{d})\mapsto\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\rho}(\Lambda)
and
(\theta,\Lambda)\mapsto\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda)
are continuous.
Thus for a.e.
r\in[0,1]
it holds
A_{t}(r)\to A_{0}(r)
and
b_{t}(r)\to b_{0}(r)
and hence by application of Lebesgue’s theorem:

\displaystyle\sup_{s\in[0,1]}\left\|\Gamma_{t}-\Gamma_{0}\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}\leq e^{\int_{0}^{1}\left\|A_{t}(r)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{C}^{0},\mathcal{C}^{0})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\int_{0}^{1}\left(\left\|A_{t}(r)-A_{0}(r)\right\|_{\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{C}^{0},\mathcal{C}^{0})}+\left\|b_{t}(r)-b_{0}(r)\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\xrightarrow[t\to 0]{}0\,,

which concludes the proof.
∎

A.5
Well-posedness of the gradient flow equation

This section is devoted to the proof of
theorem

3
, that is well-posedness of the gradient flow equation for the training of mean-field models of Transformers as presented in
section

2
.
We will rely on classical results from the theory of gradient flows in metric spaces (see
[
4
]
and
[
42
]
) showing that gradient flow curves can be obtained as limits of proximal schemes.
Historically, such a notion of a
Minimizing Movements
scheme dates back to De Giorgi
[
15
]
.

To apply this methodology to the case of the training risk
\mathcal{L}
of mean-field Transformers a few regularity assumptions need to be satisfied (see
[
4
, Section 2.1]
).
For this purpose we first consider a modification of the Attention architecture.
For parameters
(Q,q,V)\in\Theta
we consider Attention layers of the form:

\displaystyle\varphi_{\theta}[\mu](x)=\Pi(V)\cdot\frac{\int e^{\left<Qx+q,y\right>}y\,d\mu(y)}{\int e^{\left<Qx+q,y\right>}\,\text{d}\mu(y)},

(45)

where
\Pi:\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
is any smooth bounded function.

Note that all the regularity results on the Attention mapping detailed in
sections

A.1
and
A.2
carry over to the case of this modified Attention.
In particular,
theorems

2
and
1
still hold.
However, thanks to the use of the function
\Pi
, bounding the parameters
V
, we obtain a better dependence of the derivatives of the Attention map w.r.t. its parameters.
Namely, considering a compact set
S\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
\Supp(\mu)\subset S
and
\Lambda\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(S,\mathbb{R}^{d})
with
\|\Lambda\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}\leq R
, we have that there exists a constant
C=C(S,R)\geq 0
such that it holds for any
\theta\in\Theta
:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\|\theta\|),

(46)

\displaystyle\left\|\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\Lambda,\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\|\theta\|^{2}),

\displaystyle\left\|\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\theta,\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C,

\displaystyle\left\|\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\Lambda,\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq C(1+\|\theta\|).

These growth estimates are crucial to obtain existence (
theorem

5
) and uniqueness (
theorem

6
) of gradient flow curves for general initial parameter distributions.
In turn, we are able to extend the result for the case of a normal Attention (i.e.,
\Pi=\mathrm{Id}
) when the initial parameter distribution has a
V
-marginal which is compactly supported (
theorem

7
).

A.5.1
Existence of gradient flow curves

We start by stating the existence of gradient flow curves for the minimization of the risk in
eq.

13
.
These are obtained as the limit of a
Minimizing Movements
scheme
[
15
]
.

Theorem 5
.

Let the Attention map be defined by
eq.

45
and let the training risk
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by
eq.

13
.
For any initialization
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
there exists a gradient flow curve
(\rho_{t})_{t\geq 0}
starting from
\rho_{0}
.

Proof.

The result will follow from the successive application of
[
4
, Thm. 2.2.3 and Thm. 2.3.3]
.
Let us denote by
\tau
the topology of narrow convergence on
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)

(i.e., the weak-* topology in duality with
\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,1]\times\Theta)
).
In particular,
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
-bounded sets are
\tau
-sequentially compact.
In order to prove the result we need to show that the risk
\mathcal{L}
and its upper gradient
\left|\nabla\mathcal{L}\right|
are
\tau
-lower-semicontinuous on
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
-bounded subsets.

Part 5:

\mathcal{L}
is
\tau
-continuous on
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
-bounded subsets.

Let
(\rho_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
-bounded sequence in
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
s.t.
\rho_{n}\xrightarrow{\tau}\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
.
For an input token distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
, let us write
\Lambda_{n}=\Lambda_{\rho_{n}}[\mu]
.
Using Ascoli’s theorem, we will proceed by showing that
\Lambda_{n}\to\Lambda=\Lambda_{\rho}[\mu]
in
\mathcal{C}([0,1]\times S,\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any compact set s.t.
\Supp(\mu)\subset S
.

From the
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
-boundedness it follows that the sequence
(\Lambda_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is bounded.
Using the linear growth of
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
w.r.t. its parameters we get that there exists a constant
C\geq 0
s.t. for any
n\geq 0
and any
s_{1}\leq s_{2}\in[0,1]
:

\displaystyle\left\|\Lambda_{n}(s_{1})-\Lambda_{n}(s_{2})\right\|\leq\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}\int_{\Theta}\left\|\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda_{n}(r))\right\|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta|r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r\leq\int_{s_{1}}^{s_{2}}\int_{\Theta}C(1+\|\theta\|)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta|r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r.

Then, using the fact that the sequence
(\rho_{n})_{n\geq 0}
has uniformly integrable first-order moments, we get that for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
k\geq 0
s.t.

\displaystyle\left\|\Lambda_{n}(s_{1})-\Lambda_{n}(s_{2})\right\|\leq\varepsilon+C(1+k)|s_{2}-s_{1}|.

Thus, the curves
(\Lambda_{n})_{n\geq 0}
are equicontinuous and, by Arzelà–Ascoli’s theorem, we have (up to a subsequence) that
\Lambda_{n}\to\bar{\Lambda}\in\mathcal{C}([0,1]\times S,\mathbb{R}^{d})
.

Let us show that
\bar{\Lambda}=\Lambda=\Lambda_{\rho}[\mu]
.
For
n\geq 0
,
s\in[0,1]
and
x\in S
we have:

\displaystyle\Lambda_{n}(s,x)=x

\displaystyle+\int_{[0,1]\times\Theta}\mathbbm{1}_{r\leq s}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda_{n}(r))(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(r,\theta)

\displaystyle=x

\displaystyle+\int_{[0,1]\times\Theta}\mathbbm{1}_{r\leq s}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\bar{\Lambda}(r))(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(r,\theta)

\displaystyle+\int_{[0,1]\times\Theta}\mathbbm{1}_{r\leq s}\left(\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda_{n}(r))-\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\bar{\Lambda}(r))\right)(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(r,\theta)

(L1)

\displaystyle+\int_{[0,1]\times\Theta}\mathbbm{1}_{r\leq s}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\bar{\Lambda}(r))(x)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(\rho_{n}-\rho)(r,\theta).

(L2)

Using that the sequence
(\rho_{n})_{n\geq 0}
has uniformly integrable first-order moments and that
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda_{n}(r))(x)\to\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\bar{\Lambda}(r))(x)
uniformly on compact subsets, we get that
eq.

L1
vanishes.
Approximating the function
(r,\theta)\mapsto\mathbbm{1}_{r\leq s}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\bar{\Lambda}(r))(x)
on a compact set by a continuous and bounded function, we similarly show that
eq.

L2
vanishes.
Thus taking the limit
n\to+\infty
we have shown for every
s\in[0,1]
:

\displaystyle\bar{\Lambda}(s)=\mathrm{Id}

\displaystyle+\int_{[0,1]\times\Theta}\mathbbm{1}_{r\leq s}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\bar{\Lambda}(r))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(r,\theta),

and hence
\bar{\Lambda}=\Lambda=\Lambda_{\rho}[\mu]
.

Part 6:
The adjoint variables
\mathbf{m}
are
\tau
-continuous on
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
-bounded subsets.

Let
(\rho_{n})_{n\geq 0}
be a
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
-bounded sequence in
\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
s.t.
\rho_{n}\xrightarrow{\tau}\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
.
For an input token distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and an input token
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, let us write
\Lambda_{n}=\Lambda_{\rho_{n}}[\mu]
and
\mathbf{m}_{n}=\mathbf{m}_{\rho_{n}}[\mu,x]
for the associated adjoint variable.
Similarly as we have shown
\Lambda_{n}\to\Lambda=\Lambda_{\rho}[\mu]
in
\mathcal{C}([0,1]\times S,\mathbb{R}^{d})
for any compact set s.t.
\Supp(\mu)\subset S
, we prove
\mathbf{m}_{n}\to\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{m}_{\rho}[\mu,x]\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,1],\mathcal{M}(S)^{d})

Indeed, as before, from the
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
-boundedness it follows that the sequence
(\mathbf{m}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
is bounded (in total variation).
Then, using the linear growth of
\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
w.r.t.
\theta
in
eq.

46
and using that the sequence
(\rho_{n})_{n\geq 0}
has uniformly integrable first-order moments we get that, for every
\varepsilon>0
, there exists
C\geq 0
such that for any
n\geq 0
and any

\displaystyle\left\|\mathbf{m}_{n}(s_{1})-\mathbf{m}_{n}(s_{2})\right\|\leq\varepsilon+C|s_{2}-s_{1}|.

Thus, the curves
(\mathbf{m}_{n})_{n\geq 0}
are equicontinuous and, by Arzelà–Ascoli’s theorem, we have (up to a subsequence) that
\mathbf{m}_{n}\to\bar{\mathbf{m}}\in\mathcal{C}([0,1],\mathcal{M}(S)^{d})

\mathcal{M}(S)^{d}

We will show that
\bar{\mathbf{m}}=\mathbf{m}=\mathbf{m}_{\rho}[\mu,x]
is the adjoint variable associated to
\rho
,
\mu
and
x
.
To this end, considering a test function
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(S,\mathbb{R}^{d})

s\in[0,1]
:

\displaystyle\left<\bar{\mathbf{m}}(s),\Gamma\right>=\left<\bar{\mathbf{m}}(1),\Gamma\right>+\int_{s}^{1}\int_{\Theta}\left<\bar{\mathbf{m}}(r),\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda(r))\cdot\Gamma\right>\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta|r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r.

Indeed, for
n\geq 0
, we have by the definition of
\mathbf{m}_{n}
(
eq.

17

\displaystyle\left<\mathbf{m}_{n}(s),\Gamma\right>=\left<\mathbf{m}_{n}(1),\Gamma\right>

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{1}\int_{\Theta}\left<\mathbf{m}_{n}(r),\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda_{n}(r))\cdot\Gamma\right>\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(\theta|r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle=\left<\mathbf{m}_{n}(1),\Gamma\right>

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{1}\int_{\Theta}\left<\bar{\mathbf{m}}(r),\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda(r))\cdot\Gamma\right>\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta|r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{1}\int_{\Theta}\left<\mathbf{m}_{n}(r),\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda(r))\cdot\Gamma\right>\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}(\rho_{n}-\rho)(\theta|r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

(L3)

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{1}\int_{\Theta}\left<\mathbf{m}_{n}(r),\left(\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda_{n}(r))-\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda(r))\right)\cdot\Gamma\right>\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{n}(\theta|r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

(L4)

\displaystyle+\int_{s}^{1}\int_{\Theta}\left<\mathbf{m}_{n}(r)-\bar{\mathbf{m}}(r),\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda(r))\cdot\Gamma\right>\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta|r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r,

and to prove the result it suffices to show that

,

As before, using that the sequence
(\rho_{n})_{n\geq 0}
has uniformly integrable first-order moments and that
\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
has linear growth w.r.t.
\theta
(

46
), we can restrict the integrand to
\theta
in a compact set

.
First, to show that

L5
vanishes, observe that the integrand is bounded and vanishes for every

.
For

L4
,
observe that the map

\Lambda,\theta

\mathbf{m}_{n}(r)

Finally, to show that

L3

(r,\theta)\mapsto\left<\mathbf{m}_{n}(r),\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda(r))\cdot\Gamma\right>

vanishes since
\rho_{n}\to\rho
narrowly.

Part 7:

|\nabla\mathcal{L}|
is
\tau
-continuous on
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
-bounded subsets.

Finally, we show that the upper-gradient
|\nabla\mathcal{L}|
defined in

is
\tau
-continuous on
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
-bounded subsets.
For every index

, for the token distribution
\mu^{i}
and the input token

, with the same notation as previously, we have shown that

\displaystyle\lim_{n\to\infty}\|\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho_{n}]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{n})}

\displaystyle=\|\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho]\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)},

Part 8:

Applying

.

.

Let
\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)

Then
\left|\nabla\mathcal{L}\right|(\rho)
is the

of the risk
\mathcal{L}
at
\rho

\displaystyle\left|\nabla\mathcal{L}\right|(\rho)=\limsup_{\rho^{\prime}\to\rho}\frac{\left(\mathcal{L}(\rho)-\mathcal{L}(\rho^{\prime})\right)_{+}}{\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})}.

Proof.

First note that, by definition of
\left|\nabla\mathcal{L}\right|(\rho)
in

19

\nabla\mathcal{L}(\rho)
in

, we have:

\left|\nabla\mathcal{L}\right|(\rho)=\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\rho)\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}.

Let us first prove one side of

47
by considering
\rho_{t}\coloneqq(\mathrm{Id}+t(0,\nabla\mathcal{L}(\rho)))_{\#}\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
, for
t\in\mathbb{R}
.
Then, using
proposition

3
and performing the same calculations as in the proof of
theorem

1
, we have that the map
t\mapsto\mathcal{L}(\rho_{t})
is differentiable at
t=0
and

\mathcal{L}(\rho_{t})=\mathcal{L}(\rho)+t\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\rho)\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho)}+o(t).

Also by construction,
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\rho_{t})\leq t\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\rho)\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}
, and hence

\limsup_{\rho^{\prime}\to\rho}\frac{\left(\mathcal{L}(\rho)-\mathcal{L}(\rho^{\prime})\right)_{+}}{\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})}\geq\limsup_{t\to 0}\frac{\left(\mathcal{L}(\rho)-\mathcal{L}(\rho_{t})\right)_{+}}{\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\rho_{t})}\geq\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\rho)\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}.

For the converse inequality, let us denote by
\left|\partial\mathcal{L}\right|(\rho)
the right-hand side of
eq.

47
.
Then, for
\varepsilon>0
, using the definition of
\left|\partial\mathcal{L}\right|(\rho)
and the
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
-continuity of the map
\rho\mapsto\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\rho)\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}
(in the proof of
theorem

5
, we showed it is narrowly continuous on
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
-bounded subsets, this implies
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}
-continuity), we can find
\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
s.t.

\frac{(\mathcal{L}(\rho)-\mathcal{L}(\rho^{\prime}))_{+}}{\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})}\geq\left|\partial\mathcal{L}\right|(\rho)-\varepsilon

and for every
\rho^{\prime\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
with
\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime\prime})\leq\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})
it holds:

\left|\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\rho)\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}-\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\rho^{\prime\prime})\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho^{\prime\prime})}\right|\leq\varepsilon.

Considering a constant speed geodesic
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[0,1]}
between
\rho=\rho_{0}
and
\rho^{\prime}=\rho_{1}
, we have by
theorem

1
:

\mathcal{L}(\rho^{\prime})\geq\mathcal{L}(\rho)-\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})\int_{0}^{1}\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\rho_{t})\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{t})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t\geq\mathcal{L}(\rho)-\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})\left(\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\rho)\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}+\varepsilon\right).

By construction of
\rho^{\prime}
, this shows
\left|\partial\mathcal{L}\right|(\rho)\leq\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}(\rho)\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho)}+2\varepsilon
.
∎

A.5.2
Uniqueness of gradient flow curves

We now prove the uniqueness of the gradient flow curves, whose existence was previously shown.

Theorem 6
.

Let the Attention map be defined by
eq.

45
and let the training risk
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by
eq.

13
.
For any initialization
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
, the gradient flow curve
(\rho_{t})_{t\geq 0}
starting from
\rho_{0}
is unique.

Proof.

Let
(\rho_{t})_{t\geq 0}
and
(\rho_{t}^{\prime})_{t\geq 0}
be two gradient flow curves starting from
\rho_{0}
in the sense of
definition

2
.
Then using
[
10
, Lem. 2.3]
, we have for (a.e.)
t>0
:

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})^{2}=2\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\Theta\times\Theta}\left<\theta-\theta^{\prime},\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}](s,\theta)-\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}^{\prime}](s,\theta^{\prime})\right>\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\gamma(\theta,\theta^{\prime}|s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s,

where
\gamma\in\Gamma^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{o}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})
is any optimal (conditional) coupling between
\rho_{t}
and
\rho_{t}^{\prime}
.
Considering a time horizon
T\geq 0
and defining
\mathcal{E}\coloneqq\sup_{t\in[0,T]}\left\{\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho_{t})+\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho_{t}^{\prime})\right\}
, we have by
lemma

10
that there exists a constant
C=C(\mathcal{E})
s.t. it holds for
t\in[0,T]
,
s\in[0,1]
and
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
:

\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}](s,\theta)-\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}^{\prime}](s,\theta^{\prime})\right\|\leq C\left(\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|+(1+\|\theta\|)\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})\right).

Combining this with the previous equation, using Cauchy-Schwarz and a Grönwall argument gives for every
t\in[0,T]
:

\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho_{t},\rho_{t}^{\prime})\leq e^{Ct}\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho_{0},\rho_{0}^{\prime})=0,

which leads to the desired result.
∎

The above result relies on the two following lemmas, showing local Lipschitz regularity of the adjoint variables and of the gradient field w.r.t. the parameterization.

Lemma 9
.

Fix an input token distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and an input token
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
Let
S
be some compact set s.t.
\Supp(\mu)\cup\{x\}\subset S
.
Then the associated adjoint-variable map:

\displaystyle\rho\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)\mapsto\mathbf{m}_{\rho}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,1],\mathcal{M}(S)^{d})

is locally Lipschitz w.r.t.
\rho
.

Proof.

Consider an input token distribution
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and an input token
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
as in the statement.
Let
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
be two parameter distributions and define
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho)+\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho^{\prime})
.
Denote respectively by
\Lambda
,
\Lambda^{\prime}
,
\mathbf{m}
and
\mathbf{m}^{\prime}
the flow maps and adjoint variables associated with the parameterizations
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
.
Recall that, by definition, those respectively satisfy

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\Lambda(s)&=&\mathcal{A}_{\rho(.|s)}(\Lambda(s))\\
\Lambda(0)&=&\mathrm{Id}\end{array}\right.,\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\Lambda^{\prime}(s)&=&\mathcal{A}_{\rho^{\prime}(.|s)}(\Lambda^{\prime}(s))\\
\Lambda^{\prime}(0)&=&\mathrm{Id}\end{array}\right.,

and

\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\mathbf{m}(s)&=&-\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\rho(.|s)}(\Lambda(s))^{*}\mathbf{m}(s)\\
\mathbf{m}(1)&=&\nabla\ell(\Lambda(1,x))\,\delta_{x}\end{array}\right.,\quad\left\{\begin{array}[]{rcl}\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}\mathbf{m}^{\prime}(s)&=&-\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\rho^{\prime}(.|s)}(\Lambda^{\prime}(s))^{*}\mathbf{m}^{\prime}(s)\\
\mathbf{m}^{\prime}(1)&=&\nabla\ell(\Lambda^{\prime}(1,x))\,\delta_{x}\end{array}\right.,

with
\ell
the loss associated to the problem.

Using the smoothness of
\ell
and the local Lipschitz regularity of the flow map w.r.t. the parameterization (
lemma

7
), we already have that there exists a constant
C=C(\mathcal{E})
s.t.

\left\|\mathbf{m}(1)-\mathbf{m}^{\prime}(1)\right\|\leq C\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime}).

Then, for (a.e.)
s\in[0,1]
, set

\displaystyle\textup{I}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\|\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\rho(.|s)}(\Lambda(s))\right\|,

\displaystyle\textup{II}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\|\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\rho^{\prime}(.|s)}(\Lambda(s))-\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\rho(.|s)}(\Lambda(s))\right\|,

\displaystyle\textup{III}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\|\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\rho^{\prime}(.|s)}(\Lambda^{\prime}(s))-\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\rho^{\prime}(.|s)}(\Lambda(s))\right\|.

We have

\displaystyle\left\|\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}(\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{m}^{\prime})(s)\right\|

\displaystyle\leq\textup{I}\left\|(\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{m}^{\prime})(s)\right\|+\left(\textup{II}+\textup{III}\right)\left\|\mathbf{m}^{\prime}(s)\right\|.

We will thus proceed by estimating
I
,
II
and
III
.

For
I
, using the linear growth of
\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
w.r.t.
\theta
in
eq.

46
, we directly obtain

\textup{I}\leq\int_{\Theta}\left\|\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda(s))\right\|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho(\theta|s)\leq C\left(1+\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho(.|s))\right)^{1/2},

for some constant
C=C(\mathcal{E})
.

For
II
, consider an optimal coupling
\gamma\in\Gamma(\rho(.|s),\rho^{\prime}(.|s))
between
\rho(.|s)
and
\rho^{\prime}(.|s)
.
Then by the definition of
\mathcal{A}_{\rho}
and properties of the Bochner integral, we have:

\textup{II}\,\leq\int_{\Theta\times\Theta}\left\|\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda(s))-\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}(\Lambda(s))\right\|\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\gamma(\theta,\theta^{\prime}).

But then, thanks to the linear growth of
\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\Lambda,\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda)
in
lemma

4
, we see that we can find a constant
C=C(\mathcal{E})
s.t. it holds, for every
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
,

\left\|\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda(s))-\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}(\Lambda(s))\right\|\leq C\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\|(1+\|\theta\|+\|\theta^{\prime}\|).

Integrating w.r.t.
\gamma
and using Cauchy-Schwarz then gives:

\textup{II}\,\leq C\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho(.|s),\rho^{\prime}(.|s))\left(1+\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho(.|s))+\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho^{\prime}(.|s))\right)^{1/2}.

For
III
, observe in
eq.

46
that
\mathrm{D}^{2}_{\Lambda,\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda)
has quadratic growth w.r.t.
\theta
.
Hence, there exists a constant
C=C(\mathcal{E})
s.t. for every
\theta\in\Theta

\left\|\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{\prime}(s))-\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda(s))\right\|\leq C\left\|\Lambda(s)-\Lambda^{\prime}(s)\right\|\,(1+\|\theta\|^{2}).

Integrating this w.r.t.
\rho^{\prime}(.|s)
and using the local Lipschitz regularity of the flow map (
lemma

7
) then gives

\textup{III}\,\leq C\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})\left(1+\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho^{\prime}(.|s))\right).

Using the previous estimates on
I
,
II
and
III
gives:

\left\|\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}(\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{m}^{\prime})(s)\right\|\leq C\left(\begin{array}[]{c}(1+\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho(.|s)))^{1/2}\left\|(\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{m}^{\prime})(s)\right\|\\[5.0pt]
+\left(1+\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho(.|s))+\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho^{\prime}(.|s))\right)^{1/2}\mathcal{W}_{2}(\rho(.|s),\rho^{\prime}(.|s))\\[5.0pt]
+(1+\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho^{\prime}(.|s)))\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})\end{array}\right).

for some constant
C=C(\mathcal{E})
.
Hence we obtain the desired result using the initial condition on
\mathbf{m}(1)
and
\mathbf{m}^{\prime}(1)
as well as a Grönwall argument.
∎

Lemma 10
.

Let
\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho]
be the velocity field defined in
eq.

21
.
Then, for
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
, there exists a constant
C=C(\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho)+\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho^{\prime}))
s.t. for

and every

it holds:

Proof.

Consider
\rho,\rho^{\prime}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
and
\mathcal{E}=\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho)+\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho^{\prime})
.
We have for

and

:

\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho](s,\theta)-\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho^{\prime}](s,\theta^{\prime})\right\|\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\left\|\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}^{i}_{\theta}(\Lambda^{i}_{\rho}(s))^{*}\mathbf{m}^{i}_{\rho}(s)-\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}^{i}_{\theta^{\prime}}(\Lambda^{i}_{\rho^{\prime}}(s))^{*}\mathbf{m}^{i}_{\rho^{\prime}}(s)\right\|.

For this reason we now focus on a single index
i\in\left\{1,...,N\right\}
and drop the dependence w.r.t.
i
.
We also denote by
\Lambda
,
\Lambda^{\prime}
,
\mathbf{m}
and
\mathbf{m}^{\prime}
the flow-maps and adjoint variables associated to
\rho
and
\rho^{\prime}
respectively.
With this notation we obtain, for
s\in[0,1]
and every
\theta,\theta^{\prime}\in\Theta
,

\displaystyle\left\|\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda(s))^{*}\mathbf{m}(s)-\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}(\Lambda^{\prime}(s))^{*}\mathbf{m}^{\prime}(s)\right\|\leq\textup{I}+\left(\textup{II}+\textup{III}\right)\left\|\mathbf{m}^{\prime}(s)\right\|,

where

\displaystyle\textup{I}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\|\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda(s))\right\|\left\|(\mathbf{m}-\mathbf{m}^{\prime})(s)\right\|,

\displaystyle\textup{II}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\|\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}(\Lambda(s))-\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda(s))\right\|,

\displaystyle\textup{III}

\displaystyle\coloneqq\left\|\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}(\Lambda^{\prime}(s))-\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}(\Lambda(s))\right\|.

and we proceed by estimating
I
,
II
and
III
.

For
I
, using the boundedness of
\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}
w.r.t.
\theta

For

(

46
) as well as local Lipschitz regularity of the flow (

7

\textup{III}\leq C(1+\|\theta^{\prime}\|)\|\Lambda(s)-\Lambda^{\prime}(s)\|\leq C(1+\|\theta^{\prime}\|)\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime}).

,

and

\displaystyle\left\|\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}(\Lambda(s))^{*}\mathbf{m}(s)-\mathrm{D}_{\theta}\mathcal{A}_{\theta^{\prime}}(\Lambda^{\prime}(s))^{*}\mathbf{m}^{\prime}(s)\right\|

\displaystyle\hskip 60.00009pt\leq C\left((1+\|\theta^{\prime}\|)\mathcal{W}_{2}^{\mathrm{COT}}(\rho,\rho^{\prime})+\left\|\theta-\theta^{\prime}\right\|\right),

for some constant
C=C(\mathcal{E})
.

∎

A.5.3
Extension to the case of unbounded parameters

We justify here the existence and uniqueness of gradient flow curves for the minimization of the training risk in the case of Attention layers of the form
eq.

1
.
In contrast with the modified Attention defined in
eq.

45
, we thus get rid of the function
\Pi
previously used to bound the parameter
V
.
This is achievable at the price of considering an initial parameter distribution whose marginal w.r.t.
V
is compactly supported.

Theorem 7
.

Let Attention layers be defined by
eq.

1
and let the training risk
\mathcal{L}:\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)\to\mathbb{R}
be defined by
eq.

13
.
Let
\rho_{0}\in\mathcal{P}^{\mathrm{Leb}}_{2}([0,1]\times\Theta)
be some initial parameter distribution such that
\Supp(\rho_{0})\subset[0,1]\times\left\{(V,Q,q)\in\Theta,\|V\|\leq R_{0}\right\}
for some
R_{0}\geq 0
.
Then there exists a unique gradient flow curve
(\rho_{t})_{t\geq 0}
starting from
\rho_{0}
in the sense of
definition

2
.

The proof of
theorem

7
relies on two intermediary results.
The first one shows that, for any time
T\geq 0
, we can provide estimates on the size of the support of the
V
-marginal of gradient flow curves.

Lemma 11
.

Consider the modified Attention of
eq.

45
with
\Pi
s.t.
\|\Pi(V)\|\leq\|V\|
for every
V\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
(note that this in particular encompasses the normal Attention of
eq.

1
).
Let
\rho_{0}
be as in
theorem

7
and consider
T\geq 0
.
Then there exists
\mathcal{E}_{T},R_{T}\geq 0
such that, if
(\rho_{t})_{t\geq 0}
is a gradient flow curve starting from
\rho_{0}
in the sense of
definition

2
, it holds

\displaystyle\forall t\in[0,T],\quad\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho_{t})\leq\mathcal{E}_{T},\quad\text{and}\quad\Supp(\rho_{t})\subset[0,1]\times\left\{(V,Q,q)\in\Theta,\|V\|\leq R_{T}\right\}.

Proof.

Let us start by proving the estimate on the second-order moment.
This is actually a general property of gradient flows of bounded functionals that the parameters cannot blow up in finite time.
Using the gradient flow equation
eq.

20
we have for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
:

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho_{t})

\displaystyle=-2\int_{[0,1]\times\Theta}\left<\theta,\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}](s,\theta)\right>\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\rho_{t}(s,\theta).

Thus

\displaystyle\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho_{t})\leq 2\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho_{t})}\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}]\right\|_{L^{2}(\rho_{t})}

and using a Grönwall argument and Cauchy-Schwarz gives for
t\in[0,T]
:

\displaystyle\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho_{t})\leq\left(\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho_{0})}+\left(T\int_{0}^{T}\left\|\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t^{\prime}}]\right\|^{2}_{L^{2}(\rho_{t^{\prime}})}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t^{\prime}\right)^{1/2}\right)^{2}\leq\left(\sqrt{\mathcal{E}_{2}(\rho_{0})}+\sqrt{T\mathcal{L}(\rho_{0})}\right)^{2}\eqqcolon\mathcal{E}_{T}.

(48)

It remains to estimate the support of the
V
-marginal of
\rho_{t}
.

The gradient field has linear growth and is locally Lipschitz in
\theta
.

The Cauchy problem is

\displaystyle\dot{X}_{t}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\
-\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}](X_{t})\end{pmatrix}

Here
t\in[0,T]
and
X_{0}(s,\theta)=(s,\theta)
.
It is then a consequence of classical results in the representation of solutions to continuity equations (see e.g.
[
6
]
) that, for every
t\in[0,T]
, we have

\rho_{t}=(X_{t})_{\#}\rho_{0}
.
Let us denote by
V_{t}(s,\theta)
the
V
-component of
X_{t}(s,\theta)
.
Then, taking the
V
-component in the above Cauchy problem, we have for a.e.
t\in[0,T]
:

\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}V_{t}(s,\theta)=-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{D}_{V}\mathcal{A}_{X_{t}(s,\theta)}^{i}(\Lambda^{i}_{\rho_{t}}(s))^{*}\mathbf{m}^{i}_{\rho_{t}}(s).

In particular, leveraging the linearity of
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
w.r.t. the parameter
V
and its boundedness w.r.t. the parameters
(Q,q)
we have:

\left\|\frac{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}}{\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}t}V_{t}(s,\theta)\right\|\leq\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\|\Lambda^{i}_{\rho_{t}}(s)\|\|\mathbf{m}^{i}_{\rho_{t}}(s)\|.

Using the forward ODE
eq.

11
and the backward ODE and considering that
\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
has quadratic growth w.r.t.
\theta
and linear growth w.r.t.
\Lambda
we have:

\forall i\in\{1,...,N\},\quad\forall(s,t)\in[0,1]\times[0,T],\quad\left\|\Lambda^{i}_{\rho_{t}}(s)\right\|\leq C_{0}e^{C_{0}\mathcal{E}(\rho_{t})}\leq C_{0}e^{C_{0}\mathcal{E}_{T}},

for some universal constant
C_{0}\geq 0
.
Similarly, using the backward ODE
eq.

17
and considering that
\mathrm{D}_{\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{\theta}
has quadratic growth w.r.t.
\theta
and
\Lambda
we obtain:

\forall i\in\{1,...,N\},\quad\forall(s,t)\in[0,1]\times[0,T],\quad\left\|\mathbf{m}^{i}_{\rho_{t}}(s)\right\|\leq C_{1}e^{C_{1}e^{C_{1}\mathcal{E}_{T}}},

for some universal constant
C_{1}\geq 0
.
Thus, if
\theta=(V,Q,q)\in\Theta
is such that
\|V\|\leq R_{0}
, then using the two previous estimates as well as
eq.

48
, we have for any
t\in[0,T]
:

\displaystyle\|V_{t}(s,\theta)\|\leq R_{T}\coloneqq R_{0}+TC_{2}(T),

(49)

where
C_{2}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
is some continuous function.
∎

We can now proceed to the proof of
theorem

.

Proof of
theorem

.

Let
T\geq 0
.
We prove existence and uniqueness of solutions to the gradient flow equation up to time

.

Part 9:
Uniqueness

From
lemma

11
, we can find a

such that, if

.

Denote by
\tilde{\phi}
the modified Attention map of

45
with bounding function

s.t.

for

.
Similarly, denote by

the associated mean-field Attention in

2
, by

the associated risk in

13
and

.
Then by construction, we have for every

and a.e.

and hence

. Also
\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{L}}[\rho_{t}]=\nabla\mathcal{L}[\rho_{t}]
on
[0,1]\times\left\{(V,Q,q)\in\Theta,\|V\|\leq R_{T}\right\}
, hence
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a solution to the continuity equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{t}\rho_{t}-\nabla_{\theta}\cdot\left(\rho_{t}\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\rho_{t})\right)=0,\quad\text{on $[0,T]\times[0,1]\times\Theta$},

i.e., the curve
(\rho_{t})_{t\in[0,T]}
is a gradient flow of
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
in the sense of
definition

2
.
The same arguments show that it is also the case for
(\rho_{t}^{\prime})_{t\in[0,T]}
.
Hence, by
theorem

6
we obtain
\rho_{t}=\rho_{t}^{\prime}
for every
t\in[0,T]
.

Part 10:
Existence

Using the existence result in the case of the modified Attention (
theorem

5
), we have that there exists a solution
(\tilde{\rho}_{t})_{t\in[0,+\infty)}
to the gradient flow equation for the modified risk
\tilde{\mathcal{L}}
starting from
\rho_{0}
.

For
T^{\prime}\geq 0
, let
R_{T^{\prime}}
be defined by
eq.

49
.
In particular, by
lemma

11
, we have that
\Supp(\tilde{\rho}_{t})\subset[0,1]\times\left\{(V,Q,q)\in\Theta,\|V\|\leq R_{t}\right\}
for every
t\geq 0
.
Using the continuity of the map
T^{\prime}\mapsto R_{T^{\prime}}
, let us fix some
T^{\prime}>T
s.t.
R_{T^{\prime}}<R_{T}+1
.
Then by a similar argument as before, we have for
t\in[0,T^{\prime}]
that
\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{L}}[\tilde{\rho}_{t}]=\nabla\mathcal{L}[\tilde{\rho}_{t}]
on
\Supp(\tilde{\rho}_{t})
.
Hence
\tilde{\rho}_{t}
is a solution to the continuity equation:

\partial_{t}\tilde{\rho}_{t}-\nabla_{\theta}\cdot\left(\tilde{\rho}_{t}\nabla\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\tilde{\rho}_{t})\right)=0,\quad\text{on $[0,T^{\prime}]\times[0,1]\times\Theta$},

and is hence a gradient flow for the risk
\mathcal{L}
up to time
T^{\prime}
.
∎

Appendix B
Transport equations with non-local velocities

We investigate the well-posedness of transport equations with non-local velocity fields.
Precisely, given an initial distribution
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and a time-dependent velocity field
V_{s}:\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{R}^{d})\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
, we investigate the existence and uniqueness of solutions to the transport equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\mu(s)+\mathrm{div}(\mu(s)V_{s}[\mu(s)])=0,\quad\text{with $\mu(0)=\mu_{0}$.}

(50)

We will consider the following set of assumptions on the time-dependent velocity
V
:

Assumption 1
.

1.

For every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
and
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
the map
s\mapsto V_{s}[\mu](x)\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
is measurable.

2.

There exists a function
C_{0}\in L^{1}([0,1])
s.t. for every
s\in[0,1]
it holds for every radius
R>0
and every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
supported in
B(0,R)
:

\displaystyle\left\|V_{s}[\mu]\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}\leq C_{0}(s)(1+R).

3.

For every radius
R>0
there exist functions
L_{R},M_{R}\in L^{1}([0,1])
s.t. for every
\mu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
supported in
B(0,R)
it holds:

\displaystyle\sup_{x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}}\|V_{s}[\mu](x)-V_{s}[\mu](y)\|\leq L_{R}(s)\|x-y\|

and for every
\mu,\nu\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
supported on
B(0,R)
:

\displaystyle\|V_{s}[\mu]-V_{s}[\nu]\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}(B(0,R))}\leq M_{R}(s)\mathcal{W}_{1}(\mu,\nu).

Under those assumptions, one can show the existence and uniqueness of the transport equation with velocity-field
V
.
The following result is a time-dependent generalization of the result in
[
37
, Thm. 2.3]
.
Recall that
\mathcal{C}^{0}_{\mathrm{co}}([0,1],\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
denotes the set of continuous paths of co-compactly supported probability measures.

Theorem 8
.

Assume
V
satisfies
1
and
\mu_{0}\in\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
. Then there exists a unique
\mu\in\mathcal{C}^{0}_{\mathrm{co}}([0,1],\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
with
\mu(0)=\mu_{0}
that is a solution (in the sense of distribution) to the transport equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\mu(s)+\mathrm{div}(\mu(s)V_{s}[\mu(s)])=0,\quad\text{over $(0,1)\times\mathbb{R}^{d}$}.

(51)

Moreover, if
\Lambda:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is the flow-map solution of the Cauchy problem:

\displaystyle\Lambda(s,x)=x+\int_{0}^{s}V_{r}[\mu(r)](\Lambda(r,x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r,\quad\forall s\in[0,1],x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}

(52)

then we have for every
s\in[0,1]
,
\mu(s)=\Lambda(s)_{\#}\mu_{0}
.

Proof.

The proof can be done following the lines of
[
37
, Thm. 2.3]
or
[
24
, Thm. 6.6]
and adapting the arguments to handle time-dependent velocity fields.
We propose here a different argument based on the well-posedness of the flow-map equation.
In the rest of the proof, we consider a radius
R>0
s.t.
\Supp(\mu_{0})\subset B(0,R)
.

Part 11:
Existence and uniqueness of the flow-map

For
s\in[0,1]
, consider the time-dependent mapping
\mathcal{A}_{s}:\mathcal{C}^{0}(B(0,R),\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{C}^{0}(B(0,R),\mathbb{R}^{d})
defined for
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(B(0,R),\mathbb{R}^{d})
by:

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}_{s}(\Gamma)(x)\coloneqq V_{s}[\Gamma_{\#}\mu_{0}](\Gamma(x)).

We first show existence and uniqueness of a solution
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,1]\times B(0,R),\mathbb{R}^{d})
to the Cauchy problem:

\displaystyle\forall s\in[0,1],\quad\Gamma(s)=\mathrm{Id}+\int_{0}^{s}\mathcal{A}_{r}(\Gamma(r))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r.

(53)

Indeed, for
s\in[0,1]
, the mapping
\mathcal{A}_{s}:\mathcal{C}^{0}(B(0,R),\mathbb{R}^{d})\to\mathcal{C}^{0}(B(0,R),\mathbb{R}^{d})
is well-defined since, for
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(B(0,R),\mathbb{R}^{d})
,
\Gamma_{\#}\mu_{0}
has compact support and hence
\mathcal{A}_{s}(\Gamma)\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(B(0,R),\mathbb{R}^{d})
by
1
.
Also
\mathcal{A}_{s}
is locally Lipschitz, as if
\Gamma,\Gamma^{\prime}\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(B(0,R),\mathbb{R}^{d})
are such that
\|\Gamma\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}},\|\Gamma^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}\leq N
then
\Supp(\Gamma_{\#}\mu_{0})\subset B(0,N)
and for
x\in B(0,R)
:

\displaystyle\left\|\mathcal{A}_{s}(\Gamma)(x)-\mathcal{A}_{s}(\Gamma^{\prime})(x)\right\|=

\displaystyle\left\|V_{s}[\Gamma_{\#}\mu_{0}](\Gamma(x))-V_{s}[\Gamma^{\prime}_{\#}\mu_{0}](\Gamma^{\prime}(x))\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left\|V_{s}[\Gamma_{\#}\mu_{0}](\Gamma(x))-V_{s}[\Gamma_{\#}\mu_{0}](\Gamma^{\prime}(x))\right\|+\left\|V_{s}[\Gamma_{\#}\mu_{0}](\Gamma^{\prime}(x))-V_{s}[\Gamma^{\prime}_{\#}\mu_{0}](\Gamma^{\prime}(x))\right\|

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle L_{N}(s)\|\Gamma-\Gamma^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}+M_{N}(s)\mathcal{W}_{1}(\Gamma_{\#}\mu_{0},\Gamma^{\prime}_{\#}\mu_{0})

\displaystyle\leq

\displaystyle\left(L_{N}(s)+M_{N}(s)\right)\|\Gamma-\Gamma^{\prime}\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}},

where we used
1
.
The local Lipschitz constant
L_{N}
and
M_{N}
being integrable w.r.t.
s\in[0,1]
, we have that
\mathcal{A}
satisfies the Carathéodory and the
L^{1}
-Lipschitz conditions in
2
.
Moreover, if
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(B(0,R),\mathbb{R}^{d})
, then
\Supp(\Gamma(s)_{\#}\mu_{0})\subset B(0,\|\Gamma(s)\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}})
and hence using
1
:

\left\|\mathcal{A}_{s}(\Gamma)\right\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}\leq C_{0}(s)\left(1+\|\Gamma(s)\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}\right).

Hence
\mathcal{A}
also satisfies the
L^{1}
-linear growth condition of
2
.
Thus, by
theorem

9
, there exists a unique solution
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{0}([0,1]\times B(0,R),\mathbb{R}^{d})
to the Cauchy problem
eq.

53
.
Moreover, an application of Grönwall’s lemma gives for every
s\in[0,1]
:

\displaystyle\|\Gamma(s)\|_{\mathcal{C}^{0}}\leq R+(1+R)(e^{\int_{0}^{s}C_{0}(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}-1).

Part 12:
Existence and uniqueness of solutions

Observe that the above defined flow-map
\Gamma\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(B(0,R),\mathbb{R}^{d})
gives a solution of the transport equation
eq.

51
. Indeed, for
s\in[0,1]
, defining
\mu(s)\coloneqq\Gamma(s)_{\#}\mu_{0}
, we have
\mu\in\mathcal{C}^{0}_{\mathrm{co}}([0,1],\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
and for any test function
\varphi\in\mathcal{C}^{\infty}_{c}([0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d})
and any
x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
:

\displaystyle\varphi(1,\Gamma(1,x))-\varphi(0,x)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{1}\left(\partial_{s}\varphi(s,\Gamma(s,x))+\nabla\varphi(s,\Gamma(s,x))\cdot V_{s}[\mu(s)](\Gamma(s,x))\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

and integrating w.r.t.
\mu_{0}
:

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(1)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(1)-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\varphi(0)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(0)=\int_{0}^{1}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\left(\partial_{s}\varphi(s)+\nabla\varphi(s)\cdot V_{s}[\mu(s)]\right)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}\mu(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

which is the definition of (weak) solutions to
eq.

51
.

Conversely, if
\mu\in\mathcal{C}^{0}_{\mathrm{co}}([0,1],\mathcal{P}_{c}(\mathbb{R}^{d}))
is a solution to
eq.

51
, then the time-dependent velocity field
v:(s,x)\in[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to V_{s}[\mu(s)](x)
is Lipschitz with an integrable Lipschitz constant. Hence by Cauchy-Lipschitz theorem, the flow-map
\Lambda:[0,1]\times\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
is uniquely defined and continuous and since
\mu
is a solution to the continuity equation:

\displaystyle\partial_{s}\mu(s)+\mathrm{div}(\mu(s)v(s))=0.

It follows from classical results in the representation of solutions to the transport equation that
\mu(s)=\Lambda(s)_{\#}\mu_{0}
(see
[
4
, Prop. 8.18]
).
Hence, when restricted to
B(0,R)
,
\Lambda\in\mathcal{C}^{0}(B(0,R),\mathbb{R}^{d})
and by definition for every
x\in B(0,R)
and every
s\in[0,1]
:

\displaystyle\Lambda(s,x)=x+\int_{0}^{s}V_{r}[\Lambda(r)_{\#}\mu_{0}](\Lambda(r,x))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r

i.e.,
\Lambda
is a solution to
eq.

53
, which by uniqueness implies
\Lambda=\Gamma
on
B(0,R)
.
∎

Appendix C
Theory of ODEs in Banach spaces

We work extensively in this paper with weak solutions to
Ordinary Differential Equations (ODEs)
in separable Banach spaces.
The theory of such
Carathéodory
differential equations can be found in
[
9
,
47
,
16
]
.
The following conditions and well-posedness results are taken from
[
9
]
.

Assumption 2
.

Consider a separable Banach space
\mathcal{X}
, an interval
I\subset\mathbb{R}
and a map
f:I\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
.

•

We say
f
satisfies the
Carathéodory conditions
if

1.

for every
x\in\mathcal{X}
the map
t\in I\mapsto f(t,x)\in\mathcal{X}
is measurable,

1
1

1

Since we assume
\mathcal{X}
is separable, we do not need to distinguish between weak measurability and strong measurability of Banach-space-valued functions. This is a consequence of Pettis’ measurability theorem
[
18
, Chap. II]

2.

for a.e.
t\in I
, the map
x\in\mathcal{X}\mapsto f(t,x)\in\mathcal{X}
is continuous.

•

We say
f
is
locally
L^{1}
-Lipschitz
if for every bounded subset
\mathcal{V}\subset\mathcal{X}
there exists a function
L_{\mathcal{V}}\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I)
such that for a.e.
t\in I
it holds:

\displaystyle\forall x,y\in\mathcal{V},\quad\left\|f(t,x)-f(t,y)\right\|\leq L_{\mathcal{V}}(t)\|x-y\|.

•

We say
f
has
L^{1}
-linear growth if there exists a function
C\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I)
such that for a.e.
t\in I
it holds:

\displaystyle\forall x\in\mathcal{X},\quad\|f(t,x)\|\leq C(t)(1+\|x\|).

Theorem 9

(Carathéodory theorem
[
9
, Thm. 2.4]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a separable Banach space and
I\subset\mathbb{R}
be an interval.
Assume
f:I\times\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{X}
satisfies the Carathéodory conditions, is locally
L^{1}
-Lipschitz and has
L^{1}
-linear growth (
2
).
Then for any
x_{0}\in\mathcal{X}
, there exists a unique solution
x\in\mathcal{C}^{0}_{\mathrm{loc}}(I,\mathcal{X})
to the Cauchy problem:

\displaystyle\forall t\in I,\quad x(t)=x_{0}+\int_{0}^{t}f(s,x(s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.

(54)

In particular, the map
I\ni t\mapsto x(t)\in\mathcal{X}
is locally absolutely continuous.

We also extensively use Grönwall’s lemma to provide a priori estimates on solutions.

Lemma 12

(Grönwall’s lemma)
.

Let
T\geq 0
and let
u\in\mathcal{C}^{0}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,T),\mathbb{R}_{+})
be such that it holds:

\displaystyle\forall t\in[0,T),\quad u(t)\leq u(0)+\int_{0}^{t}(a(s)u(s)+b(s))\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s

for some
a,b\in L^{1}_{\mathrm{loc}}([0,T),\mathbb{R}_{+})
with
a
non-negative.
Then for all
t\in[0,T)
it holds:

\displaystyle u(t)\leq u(0)e^{\int_{0}^{t}a(s)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s}+\int_{0}^{t}b(s)e^{\int_{s}^{t}a(r)\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}r}\mathop{}\!\mathrm{d}s.
