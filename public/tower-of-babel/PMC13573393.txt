Potential evaluation of SULT1A3 as an early diagnostic marker for nasopharyngeal carcinoma: a study based on serum proteomics screening and ELISA validation
Authors: Zhifang Lin, Wei Song, Wenhai Liang, Jian Liu, Ruiwei Gan, Yueying Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundNasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) represents a highly prevalent and aggressive malignancy endemic to Southeast Asia. Early and accurate diagnosis is critical to improving survival outcomes; however, the absence of robust, stage-specific biomarkers remains a key obstacle to clinical implementation of early screening strategies.MethodsWe performed untargeted serum proteomic profiling using mass spectrometry in 15 treatment-naïve early-stage NPC patients and 15 VCA-IgA-positive healthy controls. Bioinformatics analyses were conducted to identify differentially expressed proteins (DEPs). Machine learning (random forest combined with recursive feature elimination) was employed to prioritize candidate biomarkers, which were subsequently verified using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) in independent sample cohorts.ResultsIn total, 1,428 serum proteins were identified, among which 1,410 were reliably quantified. We observed 31 upregulated and 189 downregulated proteins in NPC patients relative to controls. Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant associations: LTA4H (leukotriene A4 hydrolase) levels correlated with serum cell infiltration (r = 0.383, p = 0.032) and CD8 + T-cell abundance (r = 0.408, p = 0.021); both SULT1A3 (sulfotransferase family 1 A member 3) and FGL1 (fibrinogen-like protein 1) levels were positively associated with M1 macrophage infiltration (r = 0.510, p = 0.003 and r = 0.430, p = 0.015, respectively).In a preliminary validation cohort (n = 80), ELISA yielded AUC values of 0.631 (95% CI: 0.515–0.736, p = 0.04) for LTA4H, 0.787 (95% CI: 0.681–0.871, p < 0.001) for SULT1A3, and 0.688 (95% CI: 0.575–0.787, p = 0.002) for FGL1. In large-scale independent validation, SULT1A3 achieved an AUC of 0.826 (95% CI: 0.766–0.876; sensitivity = 78.89%, specificity = 75.47%) in cohort 1 (n = 196) and 0.796 (95% CI: 0.723–0.857; sensitivity = 76.67%, specificity = 76.67%) in cohort 2 (n = 150).ConclusionsThrough an integrated workflow combining proteomic screening, machine learning prioritization, and multi-stage ELISA validation, we identified SULT1A3 as a candidate serum-based biomarker for early detection of NPC. Preliminary findings suggest that SULT1A3 may have potential utility in clinical screening, though further validation in independent, multi‑center cohorts is required.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12885-026-16428-x.

Introduction

Nasopharyngeal Carcinoma (NPC) is the most prevalent malignant neoplasm of the head and neck region, exhibiting distinct epidemiological characteristics, including significant geographical and ethnic variations as well as familial predisposition. In China, the highest incidence rates are concentrated in the Pearl River Delta and Xijiang River Basin; specifically, Sihui District of Zhaoqing City, Guangdong Province, reports the highest national incidence rates, with 30.96 and 15.45 cases per 100,000 population for males and females, respectively. The disease shows a marked male predominance, with a male-to-female ratio of approximately 3.4:1, and primarily affects individuals aged 30–60 years, particularly those between 40 and 59 years old [1, 2]. While early-stage patients have a favorable 5-year survival rate of up to 90%, this rate declines significantly to 60% in advanced-stage cases [3]. The insidious onset of early symptoms often leads to delayed diagnosis, with approximately 70% of patients presenting at advanced stages; this has limited substantial improvements in the overall 5-year survival rate in recent years. Current diagnostic and therapeutic strategies, including imaging examinations, biopsies, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, are still constrained by the accuracy and timeliness of early detection. Therefore, the identification of novel biomarkers is imperative to enhance early diagnostic efficacy, facilitate timely intervention, and ultimately improve patient prognosis.

NPC represents a malignant epithelial tumor arising from the nasopharyngeal mucosa, characterized by a multifactorial etiology that encompasses genetic predisposition, environmental exposures, and viral agents, with Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) infection constituting a pivotal pathogenic factor [4]. Epidemiological evidence demonstrates a near‑universal association between EBV infection and NPC, wherein the virus establishes persistent latency predominantly within B lymphocytes. Serological detection of EBV-specific antibodies—most notably viral capsid antigen IgA (VCA-IgA) and early antigen IgA (EA-IgA), has been epidemiologically linked to NPC risk stratification [5, 6], rendering EBV serology the most widely implemented screening modality for NPC surveillance. Nonetheless, the limited positive predictive value (PPV) of this approach underscores its restricted utility for early‑stage disease detection [7–9].

This study focuses on the critical challenge of early NPC detection, underscored by the observation that only 15.8% of newly diagnosed cases were identified at an early stage in our institution between 2019 and 2020. Current screening methods, especially those relying on EBV antibody detection, demonstrate suboptimal sensitivity and low positive predictive value. These limitations not only cause unnecessary psychological distress in healthy individuals but also lead to the inefficient allocation of healthcare resources. Consequently, improving the sensitivity and positive predictive value of early NPC screening is of utmost clinical significance. We propose an innovative methodological framework that integrates state-of-the-art mass spectrometry and proteomic technologies for the systematic identification of potential serum biomarkers in NPC [10, 11]. Our approach incorporates the “Blood+” detection platform, which synergistically combines Field Asymmetric Ion Mobility Spectrometry (FAIMS) with advanced machine learning algorithms, thereby enhancing both detection sensitivity and analytical reliability for large-scale sample processing [12–16]. The research methodology was structured into three sequential phases: (1) initial biomarker discovery using high-throughput small-sample screening techniques, (2) preliminary validation via small-scale ELISA analysis, and (3) comprehensive confirmation through large-scale ELISA validation. This systematic investigation is expected to provide robust data to support NPC screening initiatives with the potential to improve early detection accuracy, reduce false-positive rates associated with EBV antibody testing, alleviate patient psychological distress, and optimize clinical outcomes.

Results

Proteomic analysis of nasopharyngeal carcinoma patients

The comprehensive proteomic analysis workflow involved meticulous examination of serum samples obtained from patients with NPC and healthy controls. This workflow integrated sample preparation techniques with liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) and ion mobility separation. The entire pipeline encompassed several critical stages, including raw data conversion, precise peptide and protein identification, label-free quantification, and downstream bioinformatic analysis.

A total of 1,428 serum proteins were successfully identified across all samples, reflecting extensive coverage of the circulating proteome. Of these identified proteins, 1,410 were quantifiable, demonstrating the robustness and integrity of the data acquisition and processing workflow (Fig. 1a). Strict quality control measures were applied throughout the proteomic analysis to ensure data reliability. The Pearson correlation coefficients (PCC) for quality control samples ranged consistently from 0.80 to 0.94, indicating excellent reproducibility and consistency of the LC-MS/MS measurements (Fig. 1b). To investigate the distribution of the protein intensity values and differences among various samples, the protein intensity values for each sample were extracted. Sample means at the same level further indicated good sample quality and stable and reliable results (Fig. 1c).

Identification of nasopharyngeal carcinoma biomarkers based on serum proteomic profiles

To identify DEPs, fold change (FC) was calculated as the ratio of the mean relative quantitative values between the NPC patient group and the control group. A t-test was then performed on these relative quantitative values to assess statistical significance, and the corresponding p-values were calculated. Differentially expressed proteins were defined by FC > 1.5 or < 0.67 and FDR‑adjusted q < 0.05 (Benjamini‑Hochberg correction). This comprehensive analysis identified 31 significantly upregulated and 189 significantly downregulated proteins (Fig. 2a).

Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was performed for both Gene Ontology (GO) and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses to identify biological processes and pathways perturbed in nasopharyngeal carcinoma. GO enrichment analysis revealed processes such as positive regulation of exocytosis, zymogen activation, RNA processing, and regulation of the MAPK cascade (Fig. 2b). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis identified several enriched pathways, including platelet activation, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, tight junctions, and phagosome formation and function (Fig. 2c) [17].

Based on these enrichment results, which highlighted immune‑related and inflammatory pathways (e.g., platelet activation, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, and phagosome function), combined with the following five criteria—(1) relatively high fold change, (2) high statistical significance (P ≤ 0.01), (3) detectability in serum, (4) reported associations with tumor biology in the literature, and (5) high‑quality MS/MS spectral evidence (FDR < 1%). Their differential expression values were as follows: LTA4H (FC = 1.75, q = 0.014), SULT1A3 (FC = 2.17, q = 0.019), and FGL1 (FC = 2.63, q = 0.014) (Table 1). These three proteins have been implicated in tumor immune evasion or metastasis in other cancer types, suggesting their potential relevance to NPC pathogenesis, although direct evidence in NPC remains to be established.

To further investigate the interactions among DEPs, proteins with a fold change > 1.5 were queried against the STRING protein interaction network database using their database IDs or protein sequences. Interactions with a confidence score > 0.7 were retained. The resulting differential protein interaction network was visualized using the R package visNetwork. The top 50 proteins with the strongest interactions were selected to construct the final protein interaction network (Fig. 2d).

These analyses revealed a complex signalling network involving key pathways such as platelet activation, neutrophil extracellular trap formation, tight junctions, and phagosome formation and function. Notably, the platelet activation pathway has been reported to impacts tumor development and progression. This pathway operates through a vicious cycle in which tumor cells stimulate platelet aggregation, promoting platelet activation and thrombosis, while concurrently enhancing platelet production. These preliminary findings provide initial insights into NPC pathogenesis and highlight several pathways that may warrant further investigation. Intervening at key nodes within these pathways could represent potential therapeutic strategies, but these hypotheses require validation in functional studies and larger independent cohorts.

Characterization of nasopharyngeal carcinoma immune features based on serum proteomic profiles

To further characterize the immune profile associated with NPC, the CIBERSORT method was applied to estimate the relative proportions of the 22 major immune cell types from blood proteomic features for each subject, including both NPC patients and controls (Fig. 3a). Preliminary cluster analysis based on these estimated proportions did not reveal marked overall differences in immune cell composition between the NPC and control groups (Fig. 3b). This finding may reflect a limitation of serum samples in fully recapitulating the immune cell dynamics observed in tissue. Based on the proteomic data, three proteins significantly upregulated in NPC patients-LTA4H, SULT1A3, and FGL1-were selected for Spearman’s correlation analysis with the estimated immune cell proportions. This analysis identified specific, statistically significant correlations: LTA4H expression was correlated with serum cells (r = 0.383, p = 0.032) and CD8 + T cells (r = 0.408, p = 0.021); SULT1A3 expression was correlated with M1 macrophages (r = 0.510, p = 0.003); and FGL1 expression was also correlated with M1 macrophages (r = 0.430, p = 0.015) (Fig. 3c).

These findings reveal specific, statistically significant associations between the three key upregulated proteins (LTA4H, SULT1A3, and FGL1) and the types and proportions of certain immune cells. Although no marked global differences were observed in the overall peripheral blood immune cell composition, the activity or abundance of particular immune cell types appears to be altered in the context of NPC. This suggests that the immune dysregulation accompanying NPC is targeted and nuanced, rather than reflecting a generalized systemic shift. These correlations implicate LTA4H, SULT1A3, and FGL1 in the complex interplay between tumor cells and the immune system. Given their association with specific immune cell profiles, these proteins may serve as potential biomarkers for identifying patients with NPC. However, because serum proteomics may not fully capture tissue-level immune infiltration, these correlation findings should be interpreted with caution and considered hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

ELISA validation of potential nasopharyngeal carcinoma biomarkers

Proteomic analysis identified LTA4H, SULT1A3, and FGL1 as candidate biomarkers for NPC. We initially validated these three proteins using ELISA in a small‑scale cohort comprising 40 NPC patients and 40 VCA‑IgA‑positive healthy individuals (total n = 80). Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to evaluate their diagnostic efficacy. As shown in Fig. 4a, the area under the ROC curve (AUC) values for LTA4H, SULT1A3, and FGL1 were 0.631 (95% CI: 0.515–0.736), 0.787 (95% CI: 0.681–0.871), and 0.688 (95% CI: 0.575–0.787), respectively. VCA-IgA alone achieved an AUC of 0.793 (95% CI: 0.688–0.876). Detailed performance metrics including sensitivity, specificity, Youden index, and optimal cutoff values are summarized in Table 2.

To assess whether combining multiple markers could improve diagnostic accuracy, we constructed two logistic regression models in the same cohort: a three-differential-protein panel (LTA4H + SULT1A3 + FGL1) and a four-indicator panel (three differential proteins + VCA-IgA). The combined panels were evaluated using ROC analysis (Fig. 4a). The three-differential-protein panel yielded an AUC of 0.790 (95% CI: 0.684–0.873), with a sensitivity of 77.50% and a specificity of 67.50% at the optimal cutoff (> 0.4200). DeLong’s test showed that this AUC was not significantly higher than that of SULT1A3 alone (0.787, P = 0.6924). The four-indicator panel achieved an AUC of 0.853 (95% CI: 0.756–0.922), with a sensitivity of 67.50% and a specificity of 92.50% at the optimal cutoff (> 0.6001).

We further analyzed the diagnostic performance of these markers specifically in early‑stage NPC patients (stage I/II according to AJCC/UICC 9th edition). In the early‑stage subgroup (n = 10 NPC patients vs. 40 controls, total n = 50), the AUC values for LTA4H, SULT1A3, and FGL1 were 0.609 (95% CI: 0.460–0.744), 0.840 (95% CI: 0.709–0.928), and 0.733 (95% CI: 0.588–0.848), respectively. VCA-IgA alone achieved an AUC of 0.930 (95% CI: 0.821–0.983), with a sensitivity of 80.00% and a specificity of 95.00% at the optimal cutoff (≤ 6.05). The three-differential-protein panel yielded an AUC of 0.850 (95% CI: 0.721–0.935), with a sensitivity of 90.00% and a specificity of 67.50% at the optimal cutoff (> 0.1238). The four‑indicator panel achieved an excellent AUC of 0.985 (95% CI: 0.902–1.000), with 100.00% sensitivity and 92.50% specificity at the optimal cutoff (> 0.1449; Table 2; Fig. 4b).

Based on its promising and stable performance across both the full cohort and the early-stage subgroup, SULT1A3 was selected for further validation in larger, independent cohorts. Cohort 1 included 90 NPC patients and 106 VCA‑IgA‑positive healthy individuals (total n = 196). Cohort 2 included the same 90 NPC patients and 60 VCA‑IgA/EA‑IgA double‑negative healthy controls (total n = 150). In Cohort 1 (NPC vs. VCA‑IgA‑positive), SULT1A3 achieved an AUC of 0.826 (95% CI: 0.766–0.876), with a sensitivity of 78.89% and a specificity of 75.47% at the optimal cutoff; VCA-IgA showed an AUC of 0.657 (95% CI: 0.579–0.729). In Cohort 2 (NPC vs. double‑negative healthy controls), SULT1A3 achieved an AUC of 0.796 (95% CI: 0.723–0.857) with sensitivity 76.67% and specificity 76.67%; VCA-IgA gave an AUC of 0.823 (95% CI: 0.743–0.887). Early-stage sub-analyses in the large cohorts also supported the utility of SULT1A3 (Table 2; Fig. 5).

Discussion

NPC is a highly prevalent malignant tumor of the head and neck region in southern China and Southeast Asia. Zhaoqing City, located in Guangdong Province, exhibits a markedly elevated incidence rate of approximately 15–30 cases per 100,000 population-substantially exceeding the national average of 3–5 per 100,000. The pathogenesis of NPC has been linked to EBV infection, environmental exposures, and genetic susceptibility; however, the precise underlying mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated. Owing to the absence of conspicuous early symptoms, approximately 70% of patients present with advanced-stage disease at initial diagnosis, thereby limiting meaningful improvement in the overall 5‑year survival rate in recent years. Therefore, early detection coupled with timely therapeutic intervention is crucial for optimizing patient outcomes.

Currently, the clinical diagnosis of NPC relies predominantly on EBV-related serological markers (e.g., VCA-IgA and EA-IgA), imaging modalities (MRI/CT), and pathological biopsy. Although these established approaches contribute substantially to case identification, each is associated with inherent limitations. First, the positive predictive value of serological markers remains suboptimal; benign conditions such as chronic rhinitis may yield false‑positive results, thereby imposing unnecessary psychological burden on individuals and generating inefficient utilization of healthcare resources. Second, imaging techniques exhibit insufficient sensitivity for detecting early-stage or small -volume lesions, whereas biopsy-being an invasive procedure-is unsuitable for broad population-based screening. Third, there is a conspicuous lack of specific biomarkers tailored for high-risk populations residing in endemic regions such as Zhaoqing. Consequently, there exists an urgent unmet clinical need for novel early diagnostic biomarkers characterized by both high sensitivity and high specificity.

Our study directly compared the diagnostic performance of SULT1A3 with VCA-IgA, the most widely used serological marker for NPC screening in endemic regions. In the small-scale overall cohort (n = 80), SULT1A3 achieved an AUC of 0.787 (95% CI: 0.681–0.871), which was comparable to that of VCA-IgA (AUC = 0.793, 95% CI: 0.688–0.876). However, in the larger Cohort 1 (n = 196), SULT1A3 (AUC = 0.826, 95% CI: 0.766–0.876) outperformed VCA-IgA (AUC = 0.657, 95% CI: 0.579–0.729). Notably, the specificity of VCA-IgA reached 100% in several cohorts (small overall, Cohort 1, Cohort 2), but its sensitivity remained consistently low (57–65%), limiting its utility for early detection. In contrast, SULT1A3 showed moderate to good sensitivity (76–85%) across cohorts, making it more suitable for screening applications where missing cases are undesirable.

Regarding early-stage NPC diagnosis, SULT1A3 demonstrated robust performance. In the early-stage subgroup of the small cohort (10 NPC vs. 40 controls), SULT1A3 yielded an AUC of 0.840 (95% CI: 0.709–0.928) with a sensitivity of 90.0% at a cutoff of > 0.515. In the large cohorts, SULT1A3 maintained AUCs of 0.817 (Cohort 1 early) and 0.778 (Cohort 2 early), with sensitivities of approximately 79% at similar cutoffs. These values compare favorably with previously reported performances of VCA-IgA in early-stage NPC, which typically show AUCs around 0.70–0.75 and sensitivity below 70% [18, 19]. Moreover, the four-indicator panel (three differential proteins plus VCA-IgA) achieved an excellent AUC of 0.985 in the early-stage small cohort, suggesting that combining SULT1A3 with VCA-IgA may further improve early detection, although this finding requires validation in larger early-stage samples.

In addition to evaluating individual markers, we constructed two logistic regression-based combined panels to explore whether combining multiple biomarkers could improve diagnostic accuracy. The three-differential-protein panel (LTA4H + SULT1A3 + FGL1) yielded an AUC of 0.790 in the overall preliminary cohort (n = 80), which was not significantly higher than that of SULT1A3 alone (AUC = 0.787, P = 0.6924, DeLong’s test). This lack of improvement may be attributed to the relatively small sample size and the wide confidence intervals of the odds ratios for LTA4H and FGL1 observed in the logistic regression model, suggesting unstable estimates for these two proteins. Consequently, we did not pursue this three-protein panel in the large-scale validation cohorts.

In contrast, the four-indicator panel (three differential proteins plus VCA-IgA) showed promising performance, particularly in the early-stage subgroup of the small cohort (n = 50), where it achieved an excellent AUC of 0.985 (95% CI: 0.902–1.000) with 100% sensitivity and 92.5% specificity. This suggests that adding VCA-IgA to the three-protein panel may synergistically enhance early detection. However, this finding should be interpreted with caution due to the very small number of early-stage NPC patients (n = 10). Moreover, the four-indicator panel was not validated in independent early-stage cohorts, and its performance in the overall cohort (AUC = 0.853) was only modestly better than that of SULT1A3 alone (AUC = 0.787). Therefore, while the four-indicator panel holds potential for early NPC diagnosis, further validation in larger, multi-center early-stage cohorts is required before any clinical application can be considered.

In the context of existing literature, our findings position SULT1A3 as a complementary rather than a replacement biomarker for EBV serology. Its high sensitivity in early-stage disease addresses a key shortcoming of VCA-IgA alone, supporting SULT1A3 as a practical and cost-effective serum biomarker for NPC screening, especially in high-risk populations. A potential two-step algorithm could involve initial VCA-IgA testing followed by SULT1A3 measurement in VCA-IgA-positive individuals; those positive for both would be referred for nasopharyngoscopy. This strategy could reduce unnecessary invasive procedures while maintaining high detection rates for early NPC. Combining multiple markers (e.g., the four-indicator panel) may offer marginal improvements in specific settings such as early-stage disease, but the added value and generalizability need to be confirmed in future studies. Prospective studies with larger early-stage cohorts and standardized cutoffs are warranted to validate these preliminary observations.

Recent studies have identified promising transcriptomic biomarkers for NPC. Wang et al. reported DTL with AUC values exceeding 0.900 across multiple Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) datasets [20], while Wang et al. identified RCN1 with an AUC of 0.953 and developed hybrid machine learning models (e.g., Stepglm[both]-RF) that achieved AUCs approaching 1.000 in external validation [21]. Compared with these tissue-derived transcriptomic markers, our serum protein marker SULT1A3 offers several potential advantages. First, serum sampling is minimally invasive and more feasible for population-based screening than tissue biopsy. Second, protein markers may provide real-time insights into systemic biology, including tumor–immune interactions, thereby complementing tissue transcriptomics. Third, our study specifically addresses the clinically relevant scenario of discriminating NPC from VCA-IgA-positive individuals (i.e., the high-risk population), whereas most transcriptomic studies compare tumor tissue against normal tissue. Nevertheless, the diagnostic performance of SULT1A3 (AUC 0.796–0.826) remains below that of the multi-gene ML models reported by Wang et al. (2025). This suggests that combining SULT1A3 with other markers-such as EBV serology, RCN1, or DTL-may further improve diagnostic accuracy. Future studies should therefore explore multi-analyte panels that integrate serum proteins with circulating tumor DNA and EBV markers.

Blood serves as an ideal biological specimen due to its minimally invasive collection and ease of access. In this study, we employed a “Blood+” proteomics platform that integrates FAIMS with machine learning-based marker screening. FAIMS effectively reduces sample complexity by deflecting interfering ions, thereby increasing the detection depth from approximately 600 to over 1000 proteins—representing an improvement of more than 50%—while maintaining good quantitative reproducibility. This approach enabled the identification of three upregulated proteins: LTA4H, SULT1A3, and FGL1.

LTA4H, SULT1A3, and FGL1 have been reported to play significant roles in various diseases, including cancer, infectious diseases, and bone and joint disorders.

SULT1A3, a sulfotransferase involved in monoamine neurotransmitter metabolism, has been reported to be upregulated in liver cancer tissues, where its expression level correlates with the invasive and migratory abilities of cancer cells [22]. However, its role in NPC has not been experimentally validated. Our finding of elevated SULT1A3 levels in the serum of NPC patients, along with its correlation with M1 macrophages, suggests a potential role in tumor-associated immune regulation, though direct mechanistic evidence remains lacking.

LTA4H, a key enzyme in the arachidonic acid pathway, is responsible for converting leukotriene A4 to the pro-inflammatory mediator leukotriene B4 (LTB4). Beyond its classical role in inflammation, LTA4H has been implicated in inflammation-related cancers. LTA4H inhibitors (e.g., bestatin) have shown potential in alleviating inflammation and suppressing carcinogenesis [23], and LTA4H/BLT1 signaling is upregulated in chronic skin inflammation and squamous cell carcinoma [24]. Nevertheless, whether LTA4H directly contributes to NPC pathogenesis remains to be determined [25].

FGL1, a liver-secreted protein, has recently emerged as a ligand for the immune checkpoint receptor LAG3, mediating tumor immune escape [26–28]. While FGL1 is abnormally expressed in various malignancies, its expression and function are highly heterogeneous across cancer types. In our study, FGL1 was upregulated in the serum of NPC patients and correlated with M1 macrophages, suggesting it may participate in NPC-related immune regulation.

Taken together, the biological interpretations of LTA4H, SULT1A3, and FGL1 presented herein remain hypothesis‑generating. Functional assays—including knockdown/overexpression experiments in NPC cell lines and xenograft models—are required to establish causal relationships. Such studies are planned in our laboratory, with ongoing sample collection to support these efforts.

Limitations: Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged.

First, although NPC samples were collected from two institutions (Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center and Zhaoqing First People’s Hospital), both are located in Guangdong Province and the controls came from a single site. A multi-center external validation study is currently being planned

Second, the sample size of the discovery cohort (n = 30) and the early-stage subgroup in the small cohort (n = 10) are relatively small, which may lead to overestimation of performance due to small sample bias. Nevertheless, the subsequent validation cohorts (total n = 346) provide robustness.

Third, the optimal cutoff values for SULT1A3 varied across cohorts (ranging from 0.455 to 0.515), and its specificity was only moderate (62–76% in most cohorts). Standardization of cutoffs and further validation are required before clinical implementation. The four-indicator panel, although highly accurate in the small early cohort (AUC 0.985), was not validated in independent early-stage cohorts due to limited sample size.

Fourth, the correlation between serum protein levels and tissue immune infiltration should be interpreted with caution, as peripheral blood may not fully reflect the tumor microenvironment.

Fifth, direct comparison with other emerging biomarkers for early NPC (e.g., EBV DNA, methylation markers) was not performed, and head-to-head studies are needed.

Sixth, the mechanistic role of SULT1A3 in NPC pathogenesis remains speculative, as functional assays were beyond the scope of this diagnostic study. In addition, the positive predictive value of SULT1A3 in a true screening setting (low prevalence) remains to be determined

Given the lack of external validation from an independent multi‑center cohort, the findings of this study should be considered preliminary. While SULT1A3 shows promise as a serum biomarker for early NPC detection, its clinical utility and generalizability await confirmation in larger, externally validated studies.

Materials and methods

Study population

For the proteomics screening cohort, serum samples were collected from 15 untreated, pathologically confirmed early stage (I/II) NPC patients. Early-stage nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) was defined as stage I or II according to the AJCC/UICC TNM staging system (9th edition, effective January 2025, Stage I: T1 N0 M0 ,Stage II: T1 N1 M0; T2 N0-1 M0) [29]. The control group consisted of 15 healthy individuals who tested positive for VCA-IgA antibodies. For the preliminary ELISA validation cohort, 40 NPC patients and 40 VCA-IgA-positive healthy individuals were enrolled. Given the significant regional differences in NPC prevalence and the potential influence of environmental or genetic factors on disease development and protein profiles, we minimized potential confounding factors by ensuring that participants in the screening and preliminary validation cohorts were as similar as possible across key demographic and lifestyle characteristics, including age, sex, place of residence, native origin, and smoking history.

A large-scale ELISA validation cohort was designed to comprehensively assess the specificity and generalizability of the candidate biomarkers. This cohort included 90 clinically and pathologically confirmed NPC patients. Two distinct healthy control groups were included: 106 VCA-IgA-positive individuals (high‑risk population) and 60 VCA-IgA/EA-IgA double-negative individuals (truly healthy controls). A summary of demographic and clinical characteristics for all cohorts is provided in Table 3.

Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Protein enrichment, digestion, and separation

For protein extraction, samples were stored at -80 °C and centrifuged at 4 °C at 12,000 × g for 10 min to remove cellular debris. The supernatant was then transferred to a new centrifuge tube. High-abundance proteins were depleted using the Pierce™ Top 14 Abundant Protein Depletion Spin Column Kit (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Total protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit.

Equal amounts of protein from each sample were used for enzymatic digestion. Sample volumes were adjusted to consistency with lysis buffer. Dithiothreitol (DTT) was added to a final concentration of 5 mM, and protein reduction was carried out at 56 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, iodoacetamide (IAA) was added to a final concentration of 11 mM, and alkylation proceeded at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. The alkylated samples were transferred to ultrafiltration tubes and centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 20 min at room temperature. Buffer exchange was performed three times with 8 M urea, followed by three washes with replacement buffer. Trypsin was then added at a proteinase-to-protein ratio of 1:50 (m/m), and digestion was carried out overnight. Peptides were recovered by centrifugation at 12,000 × g for 10 min at room temperature, followed by an additional recovery wash with ddH2O. The combined peptide solutions were retained for further analysis.

Mass spectrometry analysis

Tryptic peptides were dissolved in solvent A (0.1% formic acid, 2% acetonitrile in water) and directly loaded onto a custom-made reversed-phase analytical column (25-cm length, 100 μm i.d.). The mobile phase consisted of solvents A and solvent B (0.1% formic acid, 90% acetonitrile in water). Peptide separation was carried out on an EASY-nLC 1200 UPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at a constant flow rate of 500 nl/min using the following gradient: 0–16 min, 7%-20%B; 16–24 min, 20%-32%B; 24–27 min, 32%-80%B; 27–30 min, 80%B.

The separated peptides were analyzed on an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer equipped with a nanoelectrospray ion source. The electrospray voltage was set to 2300 V. High-Field Asymmetric-waveform Ion Mobility Spectrometry (FAIMS) compensation voltages (CV) were et at -45 V and − 70 V.

Both precursor and fragments ions were analyzed in the Orbitrap mass analyzer. Full MS scan were acquired at a resolving power of 30,000 over the m/z range 390–810. MS/MS scans were acquired with a fixed first mass of m/z 200 at a resolving power of 30,000. Higher-energy Collisional Dissociation (HCD) was performed at normalized collision energies (NCE) of 25%, 30%, and 35%. The automatic gain control (AGC) target was set at 3 × 106, with the maximum injection time set to Auto.

Database processing

Data-Independent Acquisition (DIA) data were processed using Spectronaut (v.17). Tandem mass spectra were searched against the H. sapiens_9606_SP_20230103. fasta database (20,389 entries), concatenated with a reverse decoy database. Trypsin/P was specified as the cleavage enzyme, allowing up to two missed cleavages. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a fixed modification; acetylation of protein N-terminals and oxidation of methionine were set as variable modifications. A stringent false discovery rate (FDR) of less than 1% was applied at the protein, peptide, and Peptide Spectrum Match (PSM) levels.The mass spectrometry proteomics data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifier PXD077783. Data normalization: After database search, raw protein intensities were normalized to correct for sample and batch effects, generating Normalized intensity (I). To obtain relative quantitative values (R) for each protein across samples, a centralization step was performed using the formula: Rij = Iij / Mean(Ij), where *i* denotes sample and *j* denotes protein.

Machine learning–based biomarker selection

Thirty samples (15 NPC, 15 controls) with 220 protein features were included in the analysis. Features with > 50% missing values across samples were removed. For the remaining missing values, we assumed they were below the limit of detection and imputed them using the observed minimum intensity for each protein across all samples. Features with zero variance were filtered out, and highly correlated features (Pearson correlation coefficient > 0.8) were removed to reduce redundancy, resulting in a final set of 159 features. The dataset was split into training and test sets using stratified sampling. A support vector machine (SVM) classifier with a linear kernel was used to build a binary classification model. Features were ranked by their F‑statistic and P‑value from univariate analysis of variance (ANOVA). Incremental feature selection (IFS) combined with 10‑fold cross‑validation was applied to identify the optimal feature subset (biomarker panel). Model performance was assessed using accuracy (ACC) and area under the ROC curve (AUC). The optimal feature subset was selected based on the highest ACC achieved during cross‑validation. The final model was validated on the independent test set using confusion matrix analysis.

Immune cell deconvolution and correlation analysis

The relative proportions of 22 immune cell subtypes were estimated from the serum proteomic data using the CIBERSORT algorithm (v1.0) with the LM22 signature matrix (R package CIBERSORT). Proteins mapping to the same gene symbol were averaged and merged; the normalized protein intensity values were log2-transformed and provided as input. CIBERSORT was run with 1,000 permutations to obtain p-values for each estimated proportion.For each of the three candidate proteins (LTA4H, SULT1A3, FGL1), Spearman’s rank correlation was calculated between its log₂‑transformed expression intensity and the estimated proportion of each immune cell type across all samples (NPC patients and controls). Two‑tailed p-values were computed for each correlation.To account for multiple testing (3 proteins × 22 immune cell types = 66 tests), the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied. Correlations with an FDR‑adjusted q < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Correlation results are displayed as a heatmap (Fig. 3c), with correlation coefficients and corresponding p-values indicated.

ELISA analysis

ELISA was performed to validate the expression levels of LTA4H, SULT1A3, and FGL1 using commercial kits (Ruixin Biotechnology, China; catalog numbers: RX100059H for LTA4H, RX102052 for SULT1A3, RX103104H for FGL1). All procedures were carried out strictly according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Briefly, serum samples were thawed at room temperature and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 20 min to remove any cryoprecipitate. For each assay, six concentrations of calibrators (provided with the kits), a positive control, a negative control, and a blank control were included in duplicate. Then, 50 µL of each calibrator, control, or sample was added to the appropriate wells of the pre-coated microplates, followed by 100 µL of HRP-conjugated detection antibody. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for 60 min, then washed five times with 1× wash buffer (with a 30-second soak per wash). Subsequently, 100 µL of mixed substrate (A + B, 1:1) was added and incubated at 37 °C for 15 min in the dark. The reaction was stopped by adding 50 µL of stop solution, and the optical density (OD) was measured at 450 nm using a Tecan SUNRISE microplate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). All samples and controls were measured in duplicate, and the mean OD was used for analysis. Standard curves were generated using a four-parameter logistic (4-PL) fit based on the six calibrator concentrations.

Assay precision: According to the manufacturer’s certificates, the intra-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) for all three kits were < 10%, and the inter-assay CVs were < 15%. The detection ranges were 6.25–200 pg/mL for LTA4H, 46.875–1500 pg/mL for FGL1, and 0.3125–10 ng/mL for SULT1A3.

Blinding: To avoid observer bias, all serum samples were re-coded by an independent researcher before being handed over to the laboratory technician. The technician who performed the ELISA measurements was completely blinded to the group assignment (NPC patients vs. controls). The blinding code was only revealed after all OD values had been recorded and the initial data analysis had been completed. No missing values were present in the ELISA dataset, as all samples were successfully measured in duplicate and generated valid OD readings within the detection range of the standard curves. Therefore, no imputation was performed for the ELISA data.

Combined diagnostic analysis

To evaluate the added value of combining multiple markers, binary logistic regression (Enter method) was performed using MedCalc (version 23.0.1). The dependent variable was NPC status (1 = patient, 0 = control). Two logistic regression models were constructed: a **three‑protein panel** (independent variables: ELISA‑measured OD values of LTA4H, SULT1A3, and FGL1) and a **four‑indicator panel** (independent variables: the three proteins plus VCA‑IgA). Predicted probabilities were saved from each regression model and used as combined diagnostic indices. ROC curve analysis was then performed on the predicted probabilities to obtain the AUC, 95% confidence interval, Youden index, and corresponding sensitivity and specificity for each combined panel. DeLong’s test was used to compare the AUCs between the combined panels and each individual marker. All analyses were conducted in the preliminary validation cohort (n = 80).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using MedCalc (version 23.0.1). Continuous variables were compared using t-test or Mann-Whitney U test, and categorical variables using chi-square test. ROC curve analysis was used to calculate AUC, 95% CI, sensitivity, and specificity. Logistic regression was applied to adjust for age and gender and to construct the combined diagnostic model. AUC comparisons were performed using DeLong’s test. A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Adjustment for age (preliminary validation cohort, n = 80) and for sex (large-scale cohort 2, n = 150) was performed using multivariate logistic regression when evaluating the candidate protein SULT1A3.

Conclusions

In this study, we describe a strategy that integrates machine learning with serum proteomic analysis for biomarker discovery in early NPC. Proteomic analysis identified three candidate biomarkers—LTA4H, SULT1A3, and FGL1. Among these, SULT1A3 was further evaluated by ELISA in independent cohorts, providing preliminary evidence for its potential as a serum biomarker for early NPC. Characterization of immune features in early NPC offered initial insights into possible molecular mechanisms associated with the disease. These exploratory findings suggest potential directions for future diagnostic research but do not yet support clinical application. Several limitations should be emphasized: the relatively small discovery cohort, the lack of external validation in multi‑center settings, the absence of direct comparison with established biomarkers such as EBV‑DNA, and the lack of functional mechanistic studies. Therefore, the results should be considered hypothesis‑generating. Future studies with larger, multi‑center cohorts and comprehensive analytical validation are required to determine whether SULT1A3 or other identified proteins have clinical utility.
