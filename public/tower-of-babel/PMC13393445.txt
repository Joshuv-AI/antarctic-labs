Artificial Intelligence-Driven Multimodal Prediction of 10-Year Incident Glaucoma Integrating Genetic and Deep Learning–Derived Imaging Features
Authors: Fengze Wu, Xiaoyi Raymond Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeGlaucoma is the leading cause of irreversible blindness worldwide. It often remains asymptomatic until advanced stages. Hence, accurate prediction of glaucoma is crucial for timely intervention to prevent vision loss.DesignPopulation-based prospective cohort study.SubjectsThe UK Biobank participants had available color fundus photographs (CFPs), genetic data, and ocular measurements and were free of glaucoma at baseline. The primary analytic cohort for strictly defined incident primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) comprised 340 cases and 9374 controls; the secondary broadly defined POAG cohort comprised 1241 cases and 34 216 controls.MethodsWe developed an interpretable, multimodal machine learning (ML) framework to predict 10-year incident POAG. The framework integrated 5 feature domains: deep learning (DL)–derived CFP features, ocular measurements, polygenic risk scores (PRS), physical and lifestyle factors, and electronic health records. Deep learning–derived imaging features included a CFP glaucoma score and automated vertical cup-to-disc ratio estimation. We benchmarked ten ML algorithms to identify the optimal model. We applied SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) for model interpretability and feature contribution.Main Outcome MeasuresThe primary outcome was incident POAG defined by International Classification of Diseases 10 code H40.1. A secondary broad POAG phenotype incorporated H40.1, H40.0, and H40.9, while excluding other glaucoma subtypes.ResultsIn the strictly defined POAG analysis, XGBoost achieved the strongest overall performance for 10-year incident glaucoma prediction, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.927 (95% confidence interval, 0.895–0.959), high sensitivity (0.685) at a fixed specificity of 0.95, and excellent calibration (Brier score, 0.024). The corresponding XGBoost model for broadly defined POAG showed modestly lower discrimination while maintaining similarly strong calibration. Incremental modeling demonstrated that both PRS and DL-derived imaging features provided complementary predictive value beyond traditional clinical factors. SHapley Additive exPlanations analysis identified the CFP DL score, age, PRS, and intraocular pressure as the most influential predictors. Glaucoma is the leading cause of irreversible blindness,1 affecting approximately 80 million people in 20202 and projected to exceed 110 million by 2040.3 As many as half of glaucoma cases in the United States and up to 90% in some developing regions worldwide remain undiagnosed.4 Primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG), the most common type of glaucoma, accounts for most cases in North America and Europe. Because POAG is often asymptomatic until advanced stages5 and vision loss from glaucoma is irreversible, early prediction of glaucoma is crucial for vision preservation.1,6,7

Current clinical evaluation of glaucoma relies on tonometry, ophthalmoscopy, perimetry, gonioscopy, and pachymetry to diagnose and monitor glaucoma.8, 9, 10, 11 These tests remain essential for clinical assessment, and ocular measurements derived from these examinations, such as intraocular pressure and corneal-related parameters, provide important predictive information. However, in routine care, these measurements are often obtained after clinical suspicion of glaucoma has already arisen.12,13 Integrating ocular measurements with features from other modalities may therefore support earlier risk stratification before overt structural or functional damage becomes clinically apparent.

Genetic susceptibility plays a substantial role in POAG, and genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have identified many genetic loci associated with glaucoma.14,15 Polygenic risk scores (PRS) built from these findings aggregate inherited susceptibility into individual-level risk estimates.16,17 Recent studies have demonstrated the integration of PRS can improve prediction accuracy of diseases such as coronary artery disease,18 Alzheimer disease,19 and glaucoma.20 Because POAG is hereditary and has shown a strong genetic component, incorporating PRS into predictive models represents a promising approach.

In parallel, recent advances in retinal imaging have highlighted the growing importance of fundus image analysis in ophthalmic disease. Color fundus photographs (CFPs) enable visualization of key structures affected by glaucoma including the optic nerve head, retinal vasculature, and neuroretinal rim. Deep learning (DL) models trained on fundus images have demonstrated high accuracy in identifying glaucomatous changes.21,22 Building on these advances, our study integrates PRS and DL-derived fundus imaging features to enhance the prediction of incident glaucoma.

The UK Biobank (UKB) provides a unique opportunity to develop and validate multimodal risk prediction models for glaucoma. The UKB offers large-scale prospective data that include genetics, retinal imaging, ocular measures, physical and lifestyle factors, and longitudinal electronic health records (EHR).23,24 Machine learning (ML) models are well suited to handle these high-dimensional, nonlinear relationships while maintaining robustness and scalability. Such approaches have shown strong performance in precision medicine applications across cardiovascular, renal, neurologic, and ophthalmic diseases.9,25, 26, 27 Importantly, advances in explainable artificial intelligence (AI), including SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP),28 now enable transparent interpretation of complex models, facilitating insight into biological mechanisms.

Leveraging these rich resources, we developed an AI-driven multimodal framework to predict 10-year incident POAG. By integrating PRS and DL-derived imaging features with traditional clinical and systemic features and systematically comparing multiple ML algorithms, we aimed to develop an accurate, well-calibrated, and interpretable model for incident POAG prediction.

Methods

Study Population and Cohort Selection

Our study utilized data from UKB, a large-scale, ongoing prospective cohort study designed to investigate the genetic, environmental, and lifestyle determinants of a wide range of diseases.29,30 Between 2006 and 2010, 502 269 participants aged 40-69 years were recruited across the United Kingdom. The data set consists of demographic data, health data, ocular data, genetic data, and multimodal imaging scans. Color fundus photographs were available for approximately 67 000 participants, captured using the TOPCON 3D OCT 1000 Mk2, with a resolution of 2048 × 1536 pixels. Our research adheres to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. The UKB received approval from the North West Multi-centre Research Ethics Committee, and informed consent was obtained from all participants. Our access to the UKB data was approved under application number 23424, in compliance with their Access Procedures and Ethics. All data we obtained were fully deidentified.

The primary outcome was 10-year incident POAG. In the strictly defined POAG analysis, cases were defined as participants with International Classification of Diseases 10 (ICD-10) records of H40.1. To evaluate the robustness of alternative EHR-based phenotype definitions, we also performed a secondary broadly defined POAG analysis, which was defined as participants with a first recorded diagnosis of H40.1, H40.0, or H40.9. H40.1 was used to capture POAG, whereas H40.0 and H40.9 were included to account for glaucoma suspect and unspecified glaucoma diagnoses that may represent clinically suspected or likely open-angle glaucoma. For participants with >1 eligible code among H40.0, H40.1, and H40.9, the earliest diagnosis date was used as the incident glaucoma date. For both the strict and broad phenotype definitions, participants with ICD-10 codes H40.2–H40.8 were excluded from both case groups to avoid inclusion of angle-closure glaucoma, secondary glaucoma, or other non-POAG glaucoma subtypes. Incident cases were required to occur between 1 and 10 years after baseline assessment to reduce potential misclassification from delayed EHR capture of pre-existing disease. Follow-up time was calculated from the baseline assessment date to the earliest of glaucoma diagnosis, death, loss to follow-up, or the hospital inpatient data censoring date of March 31, 2023. Controls were defined as participants with no documented history of glaucoma based on ICD-10 codes H40.X or H42.X and no self-reported glaucoma history. After applying these phenotype definitions, 2232 strictly defined POAG cases, 6888 broadly defined POAG cases, and 480 069 controls were identified.

We applied a sequential data exclusion process to ensure that all included participants had complete key multimodal predictors. Color fundus photographs from all available UKB assessment instances were included. For incident cases, only CFPs acquired before the first eligible glaucoma diagnosis date were included to ensure that imaging features preceded disease onset. Color fundus photographs were excluded if the optic disc area could not be detected by the automated detection pipeline.31,32 Also, CFPs with extreme brightness values, defined as mean brightness <15 or >140, were excluded for limited visualization of retinal vessels and anatomical structures. After applying the image availability and image quality-control, 392 strict POAG cases, 1357 broad POAG cases, and 77 474 eligible controls remained.

We then excluded participants with missing value of age, sex, ethnicity, or PRS. We also excluded those with a history of ocular surgery or glaucoma-related procedures, including refractive laser eye surgery, surgical treatment for glaucoma or elevated intraocular pressure, and laser procedures for glaucoma management, because these procedures may alter ocular anatomy and physiology. After these exclusions, 340 strictly defined POAG cases, 1241 broadly defined POAG cases, and 72 534 eligible controls remained. To approximate the reported glaucoma prevalence of 3.5%33 and avoiding extreme class imbalance, controls were randomly sampled at a 27:1 control-to-case ratio. This resulted in 340 cases with 9374 controls for the strict POAG cohort, and 1241 cases with 34 216 controls for the broad POAG cohort.

Demographic, Physical, Lifestyle, and Ocular Features

We selected demographic, physical, lifestyle, and ocular features that are relevant with glaucoma risk and overall systemic health. Demographic features including age, sex, and ethnicity were established factors that influence the risk of glaucoma.34 Because prior studies have reported associations between cardiovascular or respiratory function and glaucoma,35,36 physical measures, including body mass index, blood pressure, heart rate, and pulmonary capacity variables (forced vital capacity and peak expiratory flow), were incorporated as indicators of systemic health.

Lifestyle features were included to capture socioeconomic status and health-related factors. These features included educational qualifications and average total household income as proxies for socioeconomic status, smoking status, and alcohol drinker status to reflect behavioral risk factors, summed metabolic equivalent minutes per week as a measure of habitual physical activity, and sleep duration as an indicator of circadian and metabolic health. We additionally included tea intake and coffee intake, given prior evidence suggesting potential associations between caffeine consumption, intraocular pressure, and glaucoma-related outcomes.37

Eye measures (corneal compensated intraocular pressure [IOPcc] values, visual acuity, corneal hysteresis, and corneal resistance factor) were included as direct physiological correlates of ocular biomechanics and optic nerve susceptibility. We chose IOPcc in this study as it accounts for corneal measurements and reduces interindividual measurement bias.38 Visual acuity provided a measure of functional vision, while corneal hysteresis and corneal resistance factor reflected elastic properties of the cornea.39 All features were obtained directly from the UKB baseline assessments.

EHRs

Electronic health record data were obtained from the UKB “Date of first in-patient diagnosis – ICD-10” field. Each retained ICD-10 code was included as a separate binary feature, with a value of 1 indicating that the diagnosis was recorded on or before the baseline assessment date and 0 indicating absence of that diagnosis at baseline. Glaucoma-related ICD-10 codes (H40.X and H42.X) were excluded to prevent information leakage. In addition, ICD-10 codes with zero occurrences in the study cohort were removed. After filtering, 3406 ICD-10 codes were retained as EHR features in the strict-phenotype analysis and 5335 in the broad-phenotype analysis.

Genetic Features

Polygenic risk scores were included as the genetic predictor in our models. To construct a POAG PRS, we first performed a meta-analysis combining GWAS summary statistics from the International Glaucoma Genetics Consortium (n = 192 702, excluding UKB individuals)14 with our own POAG GWAS summary statistics derived from UKB individuals who did not participate in the CFP imaging study (n = 401 410; 15 661 cases and 385 749 controls). In the UKB GWAS, POAG cases were defined using a combination of self-reported glaucoma (UKB data fields 6148, 20002) and ICD-10 or ICD-9 diagnosis codes for glaucoma (UKB data fields 131186, 131188, 41202, 41204, 41076, 41078, 41270), excluding secondary glaucoma resulting from eye trauma, inflammation, other eye disorders, drug-induced effects, and other glaucoma.40 This approach for glaucoma case identification based on self-reports and ICD codes has been validated in previous studies.40, 41, 42 Controls were defined as participants without glaucoma or self-reported eye problems. From these meta-analysis summary statistics, we then generated a POAG PRS43 using the SBayesRC44 method, which integrates functional genomic annotations with genome-wide single nucleotide polymorphism data. This approach leveraged 96 functional annotations, including coding variants, regulatory elements, chromatin states, and transcription factor binding sites, across >7 million single nucleotide polymorphisms.

DL–Derived Imaging Features

We extracted 2 DL-derived features from CFPs: vertical cup-to-disc ratio (VCDR),31 and a CFP glaucoma score.32 Vertical cup-to-disc ratio is a well-established structural biomarker of glaucomatous damage, reflecting neuroretinal rim thinning. To estimate VCDR, we developed an automated pipeline based on the YOLOv745 architecture to detect the optic disc and cup and compute their ratio.31 This model was trained on 10 public datasets and fine-tuned on the Retinal Fundus Glaucoma Challenge46 data set. We obtained the CFP glaucoma score32 from an ensemble of Swin8 Transformer models trained on optic nerve head–centered crops from 17 public fundus datasets. Each model was initialized from the Swin-Base-Patch4-Window12-384 architecture pretrained on ImageNet-22K and modified with an additional fully connected layer to produce a single logit indicating glaucoma risk. Input images were resized to 384 × 384 pixels and augmented with random horizontal flipping, rotation, color jittering, and Gaussian blur to improve robustness. The final glaucoma probability score for each participant was computed as the average prediction across all ensemble classifiers.

Multimodal ML Model Development and Validation

The all-features multimodal model integrated 7 feature domains: demographic features, physical measures, lifestyle factors, ocular measurements, EHR-derived ICD-10 diagnosis features, PRS, and DL-derived CFP imaging features, including the CFP glaucoma score and VCDR. We developed and evaluated 10 ML models: logistic regression,47 k-nearest neighbors (KNN),48 support vector machine,49 multilayer perceptron,50 TabNet,51 decision tree (DT),52 random forest,53 CatBoost,54 LightGBM,55 and XGBoost.56 The selected models represent a systematic comparison across linear, distance-based, tree-based, and DL approaches for tabular data. CatBoost, LightGBM, and XGBoost were trained without missing-value imputation, as these tree-based boosting algorithms natively handle missing data. For all other models, missing values were imputed using KNN imputation. For distance-based models (KNN, support vector machine, and multilayer perceptron), features were additionally standardized to ensure comparable scales. To address class imbalance, imbalance-aware settings were applied where supported. For example, the scale_pos_weight parameter in XGBoost and class weighting in TabNet were set to the ratio of controls to cases within the training data. Hyperparameters were optimized using nested fivefold cross-validation with Optuna57 within the training folds. We applied SHAP28,58 to enhance interpretability and identify key contributors to model predictions.

Model training and testing were performed under a fivefold cross-validation at the participant level. In each run, the data set was split into training + validation (80%) and testing (20%) sets. Both eyes from the same participant were always assigned to the same fold to prevent data leakage. Analyses and visualizations were performed in Python (v3.12) with Optuna (v4.3), LightGBM (v4.6.0), XGBoost (v2.1), scikit-learn (v1.6), SHAP (v0.47), and PyTorch (v2.6).

Evaluation Metrics

Model performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination and calibration. Discrimination was primarily assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), which served as the principal performance metric. In addition, we reported accuracy and sensitivity at a fixed specificity of 95%, a threshold that is widely used and considered informative for glaucoma screening.59,60 Decision curve analysis plots were used to evaluate the net benefit of the models at different thresholds. Model calibration was evaluated by the Brier score, the expected calibration error (ECE), and reliability plots, to assess agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. All evaluations were performed at the participant level. For individuals with images from both eyes, a prediction was derived by taking the maximum predicted probability across the left and right eyes. For single-fold test evaluation, we compared the AUC of the best-performing model with competing methods using DeLong test to assess the statistical significance of differences in AUCs (2-sided P < 0.05). Area under the receiver operating characteristic curves are reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), computed using the delong_test function from the MLstatkit Python library. For fivefold test evaluation, performance metrics are summarized as mean ± standard deviation across folds.

Results

Population Characteristics

Among participants included in the primary strict-phenotype cohort, 340 of 9714 individuals developed POAG over 10 years of follow-up, compared with 1241 of 35 457 individuals in the secondary broad-phenotype cohort (Table 1). In the strict-phenotype analysis, participants who developed POAG were older at baseline than controls (median age, 65.1 vs. 59.3 years), had a higher proportion of males (53.82% vs. 46.02%), and had a higher representation of non-European ancestry (14.71% vs. 7.42%). Cases also had higher PRS values (162.18 vs. 161.49), higher DL-derived glaucoma scores (0.81 vs. 0.42), larger VCDR (0.48 vs. 0.38), higher IOPcc (19.6 vs. 15.6 mmHg), and lower corneal hysteresis (9.7 vs. 10.6 mmHg) than controls. In the broad-phenotype analysis, similar but generally attenuated differences were observed, including higher DL-derived glaucoma scores (0.69 vs. 0.41), larger VCDR (0.46 vs. 0.38), higher IOPcc (19.1 vs. 15.5 mmHg), and lower corneal hysteresis (9.8 vs. 10.7 mmHg). Systemic cardiovascular and respiratory measures showed modest differences, whereas lifestyle and socioeconomic characteristics were broadly comparable between cases and controls across both phenotype definitions. The missing percentage of each characteristic is shown in Supplementary Table 1 (available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

Model Performance Comparison

We compared the predictive performance of 10 ML models for 10-year incident glaucoma prediction using the strictly defined POAG phenotype in a representative cross-validation fold (Table 2; Fig 1). Predictive performance across the remaining folds and the averaged results are presented in Supplementary Tables 2 and 3 and Supplementary Figure 1 (available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Overall discriminative performance varied substantially across algorithms, with tree-based ensemble models consistently outperforming linear, distance-based, and neural network approaches. Among all models, XGBoost achieved the highest discrimination (AUC, 0.927; 95% CI, 0.895–0.959), followed closely by DT (AUC, 0.915), LightGBM (AUC, 0.913), random forest (AUC, 0.909), and TabNet (AUC, 0.901). Using DeLong test with XGBoost as the reference, no statistically significant differences in AUC were observed for DT and TabNet, whereas several nonensemble models demonstrated significantly lower discrimination.

At a fixed specificity of 0.95, ensemble tree models also showed superior sensitivity and positive predictive value (PPV). XGBoost yielded the highest sensitivity (0.685) and a high accuracy (0.939). In contrast, linear and kernel-based models, including logistic regression, support vector machine, and KNN, exhibited substantially lower accuracy and sensitivity at the same specificity. At the same specificity threshold, XGBoost also achieved a PPV of 0.333 and negative predictive value (NPV) of 0.988, indicating that participants predicted as low risk were unlikely to develop incident POAG during follow-up. Random forest showed similar performance, with PPV of 0.336 and NPV of 0.9876, whereas other models generally had lower PPV. Decision curve analysis further demonstrated that XGBoost and LightGBM provided the greatest net benefit across the full range of clinically relevant threshold probabilities (Fig. 1B).

XGBoost and LightGBM showed the most favorable calibration, with the lowest Brier scores ≤0.025 and minimal ECE ≤0.01, indicating close agreement between predicted and observed risks. Random forest and DT models demonstrated comparable, though slightly inferior calibration, whereas TabNet exhibited substantially poorer calibration. Consistently, reliability plots confirmed that XGBoost and LightGBM achieved the closest alignment with the ideal calibration curve among all evaluated models (Fig. 1C).

In the secondary analysis using the broadly defined POAG phenotype, XGBoost again ranked as the best-performing model, with LightGBM showing nearly identical performance. Across fivefolds, XGBoost achieved a mean AUC of 0.864, sensitivity of 0.519 at 95% specificity, PPV of 0.267, NPV of 0.982, and favorable calibration (Brier score, 0.028; ECE, 0.010). Compared with the strictly defined POAG analysis, discrimination and sensitivity were modestly lower, but the overall model ranking remained consistent, with gradient-boosted tree models outperforming linear, distance-based, and neural network approaches (Supplementary Fig. 2; Supplementary Tables 4 and 5, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

Taken together, these results indicate that XGBoost provides the best performance with a favorable balance of discrimination, sensitivity at high specificity, clinical net benefit, and calibration for incident POAG prediction and was therefore selected for all subsequent analyses.

SHAP–Based Model Interpretation and Feature Selection

We applied SHAP to the all-features XGBoost model to interpret the contribution of individual predictors to incident POAG risk in the primary strictly defined POAG analysis. Global feature importance (Fig 3A) ranked by mean absolute SHAP values revealed that the DL-derived CFP glaucoma score was the most influential predictor, substantially exceeding all other features. Age and PRS ranked next, followed by established ocular risk factors including IOPcc, VCDR, and corneal hysteresis. The SHAP beeswarm plot further illustrated the directionality and heterogeneity of feature effects at the individual level (Fig 3B). Higher CFP DL scores, older age, higher PRS, elevated IOPcc, and larger VCDR values were consistently associated with positive SHAP values, corresponding to increased predicted POAG risk. In contrast, higher corneal hysteresis and pulmonary function measures (forced vital capacity and peak expiratory flow) showed negative SHAP values, consistent with their known protective association. Several systemic and lifestyle variables, including blood pressure and physical activity, contributed more modestly to risk prediction, reflecting secondary or indirect associations. Electronic health record-derived diagnosis features contributed less than the leading imaging, genetic, demographic, and ocular predictors. We examined SHAP dependence plots for the top 9 SHAP-ranked features (Fig 4). The CFP DL score exhibited a strong monotonic association with predicted POAG risk, with a marked inflection point at approximately 0.65, beyond which SHAP values increased sharply, indicating a rapid escalation in predicted risk. Age showed a near-linear relationship with POAG risk across the observed range, consistent with increasing susceptibility to optic nerve damage at older ages (>60 years old). Similarly, PRS demonstrated a sigmoidal pattern, with disproportionately higher SHAP values observed beyond a narrow PRS range. Among ocular risk factors, IOPcc displayed a clear threshold, with SHAP values transitioning from neutral or negative to strongly positive at approximately 20 mmHg, while VCDR showed a stepwise increase in risk contribution around values of 0.48. In contrast, corneal hysteresis exhibited an inverse association with POAG risk, with higher values (>10 mmHg) associated with negative SHAP values, reflecting its protective role. Systemic physiological measures, such as forced vital capacity, showed weaker but structured associations with predicted risk. Lower pulmonary function measures were associated with higher SHAP values, whereas higher diastolic blood pressure was linked to reduced predicted risk, suggesting potential vascular and systemic contributions to POAG susceptibility.

Discussion

In this study, we developed and validated a multimodal ML framework for predicting 10-year incident POAG using demographic, ocular, systemic, genetic, and DL-derived imaging features. Among 10 commonly used ML algorithms, XGBoost demonstrated the best overall performance, achieving superior discrimination and calibration. By integrating DL-derived imaging features and PRS with traditional clinical variables, the final model achieved stronger predictive accuracy while maintaining reliable calibration. Importantly, SHAP-based model interpretation revealed biologically plausible, nonlinear risk relationships and enabled feature selection without substantial loss of performance.

Previous AI-based glaucoma studies have primarily focused on disease detection, reporting high AUCs ranging from 0.92 to 0.97.9,22,46 However, predicting future glaucoma onset is a more challenging task than detecting existing disease, because clinically apparent structural or functional damage may not yet be present at baseline. Our work extends this literature by shifting the focus to long-term incident glaucoma prediction using prospective cohort data from UKB. Given the substantial class imbalance in incident glaucoma prediction, we emphasized discrimination and sensitivity at high specificity rather than overall accuracy. Using this multimodal approach, the final model achieved an AUC of 0.927 and a sensitivity of 0.685 at 95% specificity, supporting its potential value for risk stratification.

A key finding of this study is the complementary value of PRS and DL-derived imaging features. Although the absolute difference in median PRS between cases and controls was modest, PRS contributed complementary information in the multivariable model beyond demographic, ocular, and imaging-derived features. Polygenic risk scores remained among the top SHAP-ranked features and improved sensitivity and PPV at 95% specificity. This suggests that the predictive value of PRS is better interpreted in combination with other risk factors rather than solely by univariate group differences, highlighting the contribution of inherited genetic susceptibility. Incorporation of DL-derived imaging features led to an even larger performance gain. These results suggest that imaging features capture structural or textural signatures of optic nerve changes that may precede disease manifestation.

Beyond predictive accuracy, interpretability is critical for clinical translation. Using SHAP values, we demonstrated that model predictions were driven by clinically and biologically meaningful features. Color fundus photograph DL scores emerged as the most influential feature, followed by age, PRS, and established ocular risk factors such as IOPcc and VCDR. SHapley Additive exPlanations dependence plots revealed clear nonlinear and threshold-dependent effects that align with known POAG risk patterns. A clinically relevant finding of this study is that a single CFP-derived DL score captured the majority of predictive information traditionally attributed to multiple structural and ocular measurements. Using SHAP-guided feature selection, we showed that a model incorporating only 4 features (CFP DL score, age, PRS, and IOPcc) achieved discrimination statistically indistinguishable from the all-features model, while demonstrating higher sensitivity and comparable calibration. Notably, the CFP DL score consistently ranked above VCDR, suggesting that the DL model implicitly encodes optic nerve head. This simplification substantially lowers implementation barriers, as CFP acquisition is rapid and widely available, whereas reliable VCDR estimation often depends on segmentation accuracy or clinician expertise. The ability to achieve high predictive performance without explicit VCDR measurement enhances the feasibility of deploying automated glaucoma risk prediction in potential screening settings.

The use of both strictly and broadly defined POAG phenotypes provides an assessment of robustness across EHR-based outcome definitions. As expected, the broadly defined POAG analysis showed modestly lower discrimination than the strict H40.1-based analysis, likely reflecting greater phenotype heterogeneity from inclusion of glaucoma suspect and unspecified glaucoma codes. Nevertheless, the overall findings were consistent: XGBoost remained the best-performing model, multimodal integration improved prediction, and imaging-derived features contributed the largest incremental gain. This consistency supports the robustness of the framework and suggests that the model captures generalizable glaucoma risk signals rather than patterns specific to a single diagnostic code definition.

Our study has several limitations. First, the model was evaluated using internal fivefold cross-validation, and external validation in an independent cohort was not performed. At present, identifying external datasets with comparable genetic, imaging, ocular, clinical, and EHR features is challenging. Future studies should assess model generalizability across independent cohorts, health care systems, imaging devices, and more diverse populations before clinical implementation. Second, the UKB cohort is predominantly composed of individuals of European ancestry, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Nevertheless, the multimodal AI framework developed in this study is broadly applicable and could be evaluated in other population-based cohorts with similar data modalities. Third, outcome and control definitions relied on EHR and self-reported data. Although participants with glaucoma-related ICD-10 codes H40.X or H42.X and self-reported glaucoma were excluded from the control group, UKB participants were not systematically examined for glaucoma at baseline. Therefore, some controls may have had undiagnosed prevalent glaucoma, which could lead to control misclassification and cause DL-derived imaging features to partly capture existing but undiagnosed glaucomatous structural changes rather than only preclinical risk signatures. Fourth, while SHAP improves model interpretability, it describes associations rather than causal relationships, and further studies are needed to confirm causal relevance. Finally, CFP image quality in UKB varies substantially, which may have influenced the performance of imaging-derived features.

Clinical implementation of this multimodal framework remains a future goal, and several practical considerations must be addressed before translation to routine care. The current model integrates multiple data domains, which may not be simultaneously available in all screening settings, particularly genetic information. Although XGBoost can natively accommodate missing values, predictions generated with incomplete modalities would likely have reduced performance. A potential future strategy could involve staged risk stratification, using readily available information such as age, basic clinical variables, and CFPs for initial assessment, with PRS and detailed ocular measurements incorporated when available to refine risk estimates. Importantly, we do not propose that individuals classified as higher risk should be treated solely on the basis of model output. Given the low prevalence of incident glaucoma and the long prediction horizon, false positives are expected even at high specificity. Therefore, the predicted risk score generated by our multimodal framework should be interpreted in conjunction with established structural and functional clinical assessments for glaucoma, rather than as a stand-alone basis for clinical decision-making.

In conclusion, we present a multimodal, interpretable ML framework for 10-year POAG risk prediction that integrates clinical, genetic, and DL-derived imaging features. The tree-based ensemble model XGBoost achieved the best balance of discrimination, calibration, and interpretability. SHapley Additive exPlanations-based analyses revealed meaningful nonlinear risk relationships and enabled efficient feature selection without compromising performance. Together, these findings support the potential of multimodal ML to advance personalized risk stratification and early detection strategies for POAG.
