2-switch: transition and stability on forests and pseudoforests
Victor N. Schvöllner i, Adrián Pastine i, Daniel A. Jaume

Abstract
Given any two forests (pseudoforests) with the same degree sequence, we show that one can be transformed into the other by a sequence of 2-switches in such a way that all the intermediate graphs of the transformation are forests (pseudoforests). We also prove that the 2-switch operation perturbs minimally some well-known integer parameters in families of graphs with the same degree sequence. Then, we apply these results to conclude that the studied parameters have the interval property on those families.



Keywords:

2-switch, degree sequence, realization graph, tree, forest, pseudoforest, unicyclic, stability, interval property.

1
Introduction

Let
G
be a graph. We use
V(G)
and
E(G)
to refer to the vertex set and the edge set of
G
, respectively. We denote the order of
G
by
|G|
and the size of
G
by
\lVert G\rVert
. The degree sequence of a graph
G
with
V(G)=\{1,\ldots,n\}
is denoted by
d=d(G)=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})
, where
d_{v}
is the degree of the vertex
v
in
G
. Let
a,b,c
and
d
be four distinct vertices of
G
such that
ab,cd\in E
and
ac,bd\notin E
. The process of deleting the edges
ab
and
cd
from
G
and adding
ac
and
bd
to
G
is referred to as a
2-switch
on
G
. This is a classical operation, see
[
3
,
7
]
. If
G^{\prime}
is the graph obtained from
G
by a 2-switch, it is straightforward to check that
d(G)=d(G^{\prime})
. In other words, this operation preserves the degree sequence. A key fact about 2-switch is the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

If
G
and
H
are two graphs with the same degree sequence, then there exists a 2-switch sequence transforming
G
into
H
.

Theorem

1.1
appears throughout the literature, although its earliest reference appears most likely in
[
3
]
. A similar result is known for bipartite graphs with a given bipartite degree sequence.
This result follows from a proof of a version of the Havel-Hakimi Theorem (
[
14
]
,
[
15
]
) for bipartite graphs (see
[
20
]
).

The
realization graph
of a degree sequence
d
is the graph
{\cal G}(d)
whose vertices are the
graphs with degree sequence
d
, with two graphs
G,H
forming an edge if
G
and
H
are obtained from
one another by a
2
-switch (see
[
23
]
). A direct consequence of
Theorem

1.1
is that
\mathcal{G}(d)
is connected. In recent years, the problem of determining whether a given induced subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(d)
is connected or not has been extensively studied. In this direction, many advances have already been made. Since
Theorem

1.1
assures the existence of a path between
G
and
H
in
\mathcal{G}(d)
, a natural question to ask is how short that path can be. This is studied in
[
2
]
and more recently in
[
9
]
. Another natural question is whether there exists a version of
Theorem

1.1
constrained to connected graphs. This was studied by Taylor in
[
18
]
and answered in the positive.

Theorem 1.2

(
[
18
]
)
.

Let
\mathcal{K}(d)
be the subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(d)
induced by connected graphs. Then,
\mathcal{K}(d)
is connected.

The article by Taylor includes similar results for multigraphs, and asks whether a result similar to
Theorem

1.2
can be obtained if we change the restricting property.

By
\mathcal{F}(d)
,
\mathcal{U}(d)
and
\mathcal{P}(d)
, we denote the subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(d)
induced, respectively, by forests, unicyclic graphs and pseudoforests (i.e., graphs whose components are trees or unicyclic graphs). Notice that if
V(\mathcal{G}(d))
contains a tree, then every connected graph in
V(\mathcal{G}(d))
must be a tree. In such a case,
\mathcal{F}(d)
is connected by
Theorem

1.2
. Similarly, if
V(\mathcal{G}(d))
is a unicyclic graph, then every connected graph in
V(\mathcal{G}(d))
must be unicyclic, and hence
\mathcal{U}(d)
is connected by
Theorem

1.2
. The most important feature of a connected induced subgraph
\mathcal{H}
of
\mathcal{G}(d)
is that it ensures the possibility of transforming, via 2-switches, a graph
G\in V(\mathcal{H})
into another graph
G^{\prime}\in V(\mathcal{H})
in such a way that every intermediate graph in the transition also belongs to
V(\mathcal{H})
. This article is essentially about the connectedness of
\mathcal{F}(d)
and
\mathcal{P}(d)
(first part), and how this topological property is related to the values attained by integer parameters on
V(\mathcal{G}(d))
(second part).

The first part of our work is organized as follow. In
Section

2
we characterize those 2-switches that preserve tree or forest structure. Then, in
Section

3
, we show that
\mathcal{F}(d)
is connected for all
d
, giving an algorithm to compute the transforming 2-switch sequence. Furthermore, in the same section, we establish an upper bound for the distance in
\mathcal{F}(d)
between two forests, in terms of the their sizes. In
Sections

4
and
5
we characterize those 2-switches that preserve, respectively, unicyclic and pseudoforest structure. Then, in
Section

6
, we prove that
\mathcal{P}(d)
is connected. Finally, in
Section

7
, we show that the subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(d)
induced by bipartite (non-bipartite) graphs is not connected in general.

One of the most studied problems in the literature is, given a graph parameter (clique number, domination number, matching number, etc.), finding the minimum and maximum values for the parameter on
V(\mathcal{G}(d))
(see
[
6
,
11
,
12
,
19
,
21
,
22
]
). Another interesting problem is deciding which values between the minimum and the maximum can be realized by a graph in
V(\mathcal{G}(d))
(see
[
16
,
17
]
).
For instance, in
[
6
]
the authors study the matching number of trees with a given degree sequence, and of bipartite graphs with a given bipartite degree sequence. The authors find minimum and maximum values for the matching number in these families, and then show that all the other intermediate values are also attained.
To establish the same property for bipartite degree sequences, they show that a 2-switch alters the matching number by at most
1
, and use the version of
Theorem

1.1
for bipartite degree sequences. In the case of trees, the authors of
[
6
]
constructed the tree realizing each value between the minimum and the maximum, although they could have applied
Theorem

1.2
.

In the second part of this article, consisting only of
Section

8
, we apply our results from the first part to show that a plethora of integer parameters
are stable under 2-switch, i.e., the 2-switch perturbs them by at most 1, and have the interval property, which means that they attain every intermediate value between maximum and minimum.
Section

8
is organized as follow. In
Sections

8.1
and
8.2
we study, respectively, matching number and independence number, obtaining results also on related parameters like edge-covering number, rank, nullity, vertex-covering number and clique number. Next, we study the domination number in
Section

8.3
and the number of connected components in
Section

8.4
.
Section

8.5
is about path-covering number, zero forcing number and Z-Grundy domination number. Finally, in
Section

8.6
, we analyze the chromatic number.

2
T-Switch and F-Switch

We denote a 2-switch operation by the
2\times 2
matrix
{{a\ b}\choose{c\ d}}
, where the rows represent the edges to be removed and the columns represent the edges to be added. If
\tau={{a\ b}\choose{c\ d}}
is a 2-switch on
G
, then
\tau(G)
denotes the transformed graph

G-ab-cd+ac+bd.

In order to prove that
\mathcal{F}(d)
is connected, we need to characterize those 2-switches over a forest that preserve the forest structure. A first step in doing so is characterizing those preserving the tree structure. A 2-switch
\tau
over a tree
T
is said to be a
t-switch
if
\tau(T)
is a tree. It is easy to check that a 2-switch
\tau={{a\ b}\choose{c\ d}}
on
T
is a t-switch if and only if
T
contains the path
ab\ldots cd
or
ba\ldots dc
.

Note that any two forests with the same degree sequence have the same number of connected components.
In particular, if
d
is the degree sequence of a tree, then every member of
V(\mathcal{F}(d))
is also a tree.

A 2-switch
\tau
on a forest
F
is said to be an
f-switch
if
\tau(F)
is a forest. Using t-switch, we can easily characterize when a 2-switch on a forest is an f-switch. In fact, being
\tau
a 2-switch between two (disjoint) edges
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
of a forest
F
, we have the following:

1.

if
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are in the same component
T
of
F
, then
\tau
is an f-switch on
F
if and only if is a t-switch on
T
;

2.

if
e_{1}
and
e_{2}
are in different components of
F
, then
\tau
is an f-switch on
F
.

3
Forests

Let
G,H\in V(\mathcal{G}(d))
. Obviously,
G
and
H
have the same set of leaves. A leaf
\ell
is said to be
trimmable
in
G
and
H
if
\ell v\in E(G)\cap E(H)
, for some
v
. We denote the set of trimmable leaves of
G
and
H
by
\Lambda(G,H)
, or just
\Lambda
when
G
and
H
are clear from the context.
Recall that in this section we show that
\mathcal{F}(d)
is connected. The next two lemmas are preliminary steps to this result, which will use the simple idea of consecutive deletions of trimmable leaves.

Lemma 3.1
.

Let
F,F^{\prime}\in V(\mathcal{F}(d))
.
Suppose that
\theta=(\tau_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
is an f-switch sequence transforming
F-\Lambda
into
F^{\prime}-\Lambda
. Then,
\theta
is an f-switch sequence transforming
F
into
F^{\prime}
.

Proof.

Let
F_{0}=F
and
F_{i}=\tau_{i}(F_{i-1})
. As none of the vertices in
\Lambda
is involved in any of the f-switches,
F_{i}-\Lambda=\tau_{i}(F_{i-1}-\Lambda)
for all
i
. Hence
\theta
is a sequence of 2-switches transforming
F
into
F^{\prime}
. Since
F_{i}-\Lambda
is obtained from
F_{i}
by removing vertices of degree
1
,
F_{i}-\Lambda
has the same
cycles as
F_{i}
. Thus, as every
F_{i}-\Lambda
is a forest, every
F_{i}
is a forest as well. Therefore,
\theta
is a sequence of f-switches transforming
F
into
F^{\prime}
.
∎

Lemma 3.2
.

Let
F,F^{\prime}\in V(\mathcal{F}(d))
. If
\Lambda(F,F^{\prime})=\varnothing
, then there exists an f-switch
\tau
over
F
such that
\Lambda(\tau(F),F^{\prime})\neq\varnothing
.

Proof.

Assume
\Lambda(F,F^{\prime})=\varnothing
.
We split the proof in two cases: 1) there is a leaf whose neighbor in
F^{\prime}
has degree
\geq 2
; 2) every vertex has degree 1.

(1).

Let
\ell
be a leaf such that its neighbor
u
in
F^{\prime}
has degree at least
2
, and let
v
be the neighbor of
\ell
in
F
. If
\ell
and
u
are in different components of
F
, let
w
be a neighbor of
u
in
F
. If
\ell
and
u
are in the same component of
F
, consider the path
\ell v\ldots u
in
F
. As
d_{u}\geq 2
, there is a neighbor
w
of
u
that is not in
\ell v\ldots u
.
In either case,
\tau={{\ell\ v}\choose{u\ w}}
is an f-switch on
F
such that
\ell\in\Lambda(\tau(F),F^{\prime})
.

(2).

Let
\ell
be any leaf, let
v
and
u
be the neighbors of
\ell
in
F
and
F^{\prime}
respectively,
and let
w
be the neighbor of
u
in
F
.
Then,
\tau={{\ell\ v}\choose{u\ w}}
is an f-switch on
F
such that
\ell\in\Lambda(\tau(F),F^{\prime})
.

∎

The next result states that given two forests with the same degree sequence, there is a sequence of f-switches transforming one into the other.
Before proceeding with the proof, we need to note two things. First, it is sufficient to prove it for forests without isolated vertices, because they do no not participate in any 2-switch. Secondly, it is easy to check that the result holds for forests of order
n\leq 4
. We are now ready to proceed.

Theorem 3.3
.

\mathcal{F}(d)
is connected.

Proof.

Let
F,F^{\prime}\in V(\mathcal{F}(d))
and suppose that
F
and
F^{\prime}
have no isolated vertices. We use induction on
n=|F|
. If
n\leq 4
, the statement is true. Hence, let
n>4
, and suppose that every pair of forests of order
<n
with the same degree sequence can be transformed into each other by a sequence of f-switches. We have two cases: 1)
\Lambda(F,F^{\prime})\neq\varnothing
; 2)
\Lambda(F,F^{\prime})=\varnothing
.

(1).

Consider
F-\Lambda
and
F^{\prime}-\Lambda
. These are two forests of order
n^{\prime}<n
, with the same degree sequence
d^{\prime}
. So, inductive hypothesis applies to
F-\Lambda
and
F^{\prime}-\Lambda
: there exists an f-switch sequence
\theta
transforming
F-\Lambda
into
F^{\prime}-\Lambda
. Hence, by
Section

3
,
\theta
is an f-switch sequence from
F
to
F^{\prime}
.

(2).

By
Section

3
, this case can be reduced to case (1).

∎

The proof of
Theorem

3.3
contains a procedure that returns a transforming f-switch sequence. We make this algorithm explicit, see (
3
).

Transition algorithm

INPUT: two forests
F,F^{\prime}\in\mathcal{F}(d)
.

1.

Let
r=0
and
\Lambda=\Lambda(F,F^{\prime})
.

2.

While
F\neq F^{\prime}
:

(a)

If
\Lambda=\varnothing
:

i.

Let
r=r+1
.

ii.

If every vertex in
F^{\prime}
has degree
1
choose a leaf
\ell\in V(F^{\prime})
. Else choose a leaf

\ell\in V(F^{\prime})
such that its neighbor
u
in
F^{\prime}
has degree at least
2
.

iii.

Find the f-switch
\tau
such that
\ell
is trimmable between
\tau(F)
and
F^{\prime}
(such f-switch exists by
Section

3
).

iv.

Let
\tau_{r}=\tau
,
F=\tau(F)
, and
\Lambda=\Lambda(F,F^{\prime})
.

(b)

Let
F=F-\Lambda
and
F^{\prime}=F^{\prime}-\Lambda
.

3.

RETURN
(\tau_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
.

Note that in each step of (
3
) either
F=F^{\prime}
or at least one leaf is removed (equivalently, one edge is removed). Thus, (
3
) runs at most
|E(F^{\prime})-E(F)|
times. This number can be improved by
1
. First, we need a technical lemma.

Lemma 3.4
.

If
G,H\in V(\mathcal{G}(d))
, then
|E(G)-E(H)|\neq 1
.

Proof.

We proceed by contradiction. Assume that
|E(G)-E(H)|=1
. Clearly,
d(G)=d(H)
implies
\lVert G\rVert=\lVert H\rVert
. Thus,
|E(H)-E(G)|=1
. Let
ab\in E(G)-E(H)
. As
d(G)=d(H)
,
there must be an edge incident to
a
and an edge incident to
b
in
E(H)-E(G)
. But, as
|E(H)-E(G)|=1
, this can only be possible if
ab\in E(H)
, contradicting the fact that
ab
is the only edge in
E(G)-E(H)
.
∎

Suppose now that
(\tau_{i})_{i=1}^{r}
is the f-switch sequence obtained as output of (
3
) applied to
F
and
F^{\prime}
. Let
F_{0}=F
and
F_{i}=\tau_{i}(F_{i-1})
for
1\leq i\leq r
.
Notice that for each
i
,
|E(F^{\prime})\cap E(F_{i})|\geq|E(F^{\prime})\cap E(F_{i-1})|+1
. Thus,

\sum_{i=1}^{r-1}\left(|E(F^{\prime})\cap E(F_{i})|-|E(F^{\prime})\cap E(F_{i-1})|\right)\geq r.

Since this last sum is telescoping we have

|E(F^{\prime})\cap E(F_{r-1})|-|E(F^{\prime})\cap E(F)|\geq r-1.

On the other hand,
Section

3
implies

\displaystyle|E(F^{\prime})\cap E(F_{r})|-|E(F^{\prime})\cap E(F_{r-1})|=|E(F^{\prime})|-|E(F^{\prime})\cap E(F_{r-1})|\geq 2.

Thus,

\displaystyle|E(F^{\prime})\cap E(F_{r})|-|E(F^{\prime})\cap E(F)|\geq r+1.

But
E(F_{r})=E(F^{\prime})
, and
|E(F^{\prime})|-|E(F^{\prime})\cap E(F)|=|E(F^{\prime})-E(F)|
.
Therefore

\displaystyle r\leq|E(F^{\prime})-E(F)|-1.

Hence, (
3
) runs at most
|E(F^{\prime})-E(F)|-1
times. We have just established the other main result of this section.

Theorem 3.5
.

The distance between
F
and
F^{\prime}
in
\mathcal{F}(d)
is at most

|E(F^{\prime})-E(F)|-1.

Proof.

It follows from
Theorem

3.3
and the the previous discussion.
∎

It is important to note that even though the bound in
Theorem

3.5
seems to be worse than the result in
[
2
]
, the sequence of 2-switches that
they construct is not necessarily a sequence of f-switches.

4
u-switch

Let
G
be a graph.
By
\operatorname{Cyc}(G)
we denote the subgraph of
G
induced by all the vertices that belongs to some cycle of
G
. Now, define

\operatorname{For}(G)=G-E(\operatorname{Cyc}(G)),

which is a spanning forest for
G
whose components are trees attached to vertices of
\operatorname{Cyc}(G)
.
Clearly,
E(G)=E(\operatorname{Cyc}(G))\dot{\cup}E(\operatorname{For}(G))
.

A 2-switch
\tau
on a unicyclic graph
U
is said to be a
u-switch
if
\tau(U)
is also unicyclic.

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
U
be a unicyclic graph and let
e\in E(\operatorname{Cyc}(U))
. If
\tau
is a t-switch on
U-e
between the edges
ab,cd
of
\operatorname{For}(U)
, then
e\notin\tau(U-e)
.

Proof.

Let
C=\operatorname{Cyc}(U)
be the cycle of
U
. Notice that
C-e
is a subgraph of both
U-e
and
\tau(U-e)
, because none of the edges in
C-e
are involved in
\tau
. If
e\in\tau(U-e)
, then
C\subset\tau(U-e)
(the symbol
\subset
denotes the subgraph relation). Hence,
\tau
is not a t-switch on
U-e
.
∎

Lemma 4.2
.

Let
U
be a unicyclic graph and let
ab\in E(\operatorname{Cyc}(U))
,
cd\in E(\operatorname{For}(U))
. If
\tau
is a 2-switch between
ab
and
cd
, then there exists an edge
e\in\operatorname{Cyc}(U)
such that
e\neq ab
and
e\notin\tau(U-e)
.

Proof.

Let
\tau={{a\ b}\choose{c\ d}}
.
Since
cd\in E(\operatorname{For}(U))
, at least one of the vertices
c,d
does not belong to
\operatorname{Cyc}(U)
. Assume without loss of generality that
d\not\in\operatorname{Cyc}(U)
. Let
v\neq a
be a neighbor of
b
in
\operatorname{Cyc}(U)
, and let
e=bv
. Notice that
v\neq d
because
d\not\in\operatorname{Cyc}(U)
. Therefore,

e\not\in\tau(U-e)=U-e-ab-cd+ac+bd.

∎

The following observation will be used many times. Let
\tau={{a\ b}\choose{c\ d}}
be a 2-switch on a graph
G
and let
e\in E(G)-\{ab,\,cd\}
. If
e\notin\tau(G-e)
, then
\tau(G-e)+e=\tau(G)
. In particular, if
\tau(G-e)
is a tree, then
\tau(G)
is unicyclic. Next, we characterize the u-switches.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
\tau
be a 2-switch between two disjoint edges
e_{1},e_{2}
of a unicyclic graph
U
. Then, the following statements hold:

1.

If
e_{1},e_{2}\in E(\operatorname{For}(U))
, then
\tau
is a u-switch over
U
if and only if
\tau
is a t-switch over
U-e
, for all
e\in E(\operatorname{Cyc}(U))
.

2.

If
e_{1}\in E(\operatorname{Cyc}(U))
and
e_{2}\in E(\operatorname{For}(U))
, then
\tau
is a u-switch over
U
.

3.

If
e_{1},e_{2}\in E(\operatorname{Cyc}(U))
, then
\tau
is a u-switch over
U
if and only if
\tau(\operatorname{Cyc}(U))\approx\operatorname{Cyc}(U)
.

Proof.

(1).

(
\Leftarrow
) By
Section

4
,
e\notin\tau(U-e)
. Since
\tau(U-e)
is a tree,
\tau(U-e)+e=\tau(U)
is a unicyclic graph.

(
\Rightarrow
) If
e_{1},e_{2}\in E(\operatorname{For}(U))
,
e\in E(\operatorname{Cyc}(U))
and
\tau
is not a t-switch in
U-e
, then
\tau
disconnects
U
.

(2).

By
Section

4
, there exists
e\in E(\operatorname{Cyc}(U))-e_{1}
such that
e\notin\tau(U-e)
. Clearly,
U-e
is a tree. If
\tau(U-e)
is a tree, then
\tau(U-e)+e=\tau(U)
is a unicyclic graph. Otherwise,
\tau(U-e)
has two components: a unicyclic graph
U^{\prime}
and a tree
T
. Since
e
links
U^{\prime}
to
T
, we have that
\tau(U-e)+e=\tau(U)
is a unicyclic graph.

(3).

Let
C
be a cycle. If
|C|=3
, we cannot apply any 2-switch on
C
. If
|C|\in\{4,5\}
, then
\tau(C)\approx C
(the symbol
\approx
denotes the isomorphism relation), for every 2-switch
\tau
on
C
. If
|C|\geq 6
, then either
\tau(C)\approx C
or
\tau(C)
is the union of two disjoint cycles.

(
\Rightarrow
) If
\tau(\operatorname{Cyc}(U))\not\approx\operatorname{Cyc}(U)
, then
\tau
disconnects
U
.

(
\Leftarrow
) If
\tau(\operatorname{Cyc}(U))\approx\operatorname{Cyc}(U)
, then
\tau(U)
is obviously unicyclic.

∎

Recall that, if
V(\mathcal{F}(d))
contains a tree, then all members of
V(\mathcal{F}(d))
are trees as well. In contrast, if
V(\mathcal{P}(d))
contains a unicyclic graph, then the rest of pseudoforests in
V(\mathcal{P}(d))
are not necessarily all unicyclic.

5
p-switch

A 2-switch
\tau
on a pseudoforest
G
is said to be a
p-switch
if
\tau(G)
is a pseudoforest. Notice that t-switches, f-switches and u-switches are clearly particular cases of p-switches.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
G
be a pseudoforest with two components,
F
and
U
, where
F
is a forest and
U
is a unicyclic graph. We have the following:

1.

every 2-switch on
F
is a p-switch;

2.

let
\tau={{a\ b}\choose{c\ d}}
be a 2-switch on
G
. If
ab\in E(F)
and
cd\in E(\operatorname{For}(U))
, then
\tau
is a p-switch on
G
.

Proof.

(1).

It is straightforward to see that every 2-switch on

Stability and interval property

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a connected induced subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(d)
and let
\xi:V(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
. We say that
\xi
is
stable
(under
2
-switch) in
\mathcal{X}
if, for each
G\in V(\mathcal{X})
, we have

\left|\xi(\tau(G))-\xi(G)\right|\leq 1,

for every 2-switch
\tau
on
G
such that
\tau(G)\in V(\mathcal{X})
. Clearly, if
\xi
is stable in
\mathcal{G}(d)
, then it is stable in every connected induced subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(d)
. When
\mathcal{X}=\mathcal{G}(d)
, we simply say that
\xi
is stable.
Let
G,H\in V(\mathcal{X})
. Notice that if
\xi
is stable in
\mathcal{X}
, then

dist_{\mathcal{X}}(G,H)\geq|\xi(G)-\xi(H)|,

where
dist_{\mathcal{X}}(*,*)
is the usual path-metric in
{\mathcal{X}}
. This inequality gives an interesting way to estimate
dist_{\mathcal{X}}(G,H)
(hard to determine in general) by choosing a suitable stable parameter easier to compute on
G
and
H
.

We say that
\xi
has the

in
\mathcal{X}
if
\xi(V(\mathcal{X}))=I\cap\mathbb{Z}
, for some interval

. In other words, if
\xi_{\max}
and
\xi_{\min}
are the maximum and minimum values attained by
\xi
, then
\xi
has the interval property in
\mathcal{X}
if, for every integer
k
in the interval
[\xi_{\min},\xi_{\max}]
, there is a
G\in V(\mathcal{X})
such that
\xi(G)=k
. One could think of this property as a discrete analog of the Intermediate Value Theorem from elementary Calculus.

The next theorem shows that stability implies interval property. This idea was used in
[
6
]

to prove the interval property for the matching number on the family of bipartite graphs with a given bipartite degree sequence, but here we formalize it in order to apply it to many parameters.

Theorem 8.1
.

Let
\mathcal{X}
be a connected induced subgraph of
\mathcal{G}(d)
and let
\xi:V(\mathcal{X})\rightarrow\mathbb{Z}
. If
\xi
is stable in
\mathcal{X}
, then
\xi
has the interval property in
\mathcal{X}
.

Proof.

Consider two graphs

such that
\xi(G_{1})
and
\xi(G_{2})
are, respectively, the minimum and the maximum value for
\xi
. As
\mathcal{X}
is connected, there exists a 2-switch sequence
(\tau_{i})
transforming
G_{1}
into
G_{2}
, such that every intermediate graph of the transformation is also a vertex of
\mathcal{X}
. Since each
\tau_{i}
perturbs
\xi
at most by 1, every integer value in the interval
[\xi(G_{1}),\xi(G_{2})]
must be attained in some graph of the transition. Thus,

\mathcal{X}
.

Lemma 8.4
.

8.2
Independence number and related parameters

An independent set of a graph
G
is a set of vertices in
G
, no two of which are adjacent. A maximum independent set in
G
is an independent set of
G
with the largest possible cardinality. This cardinality is called the independence number of
G
, and it is denoted by
\alpha(G)
.

Theorem 8.10
.

The independence number is stable.

Proof.

Let
I
be a maximum independent set in a graph
G
,
\tau={{a\ b}\choose{c\ d}}
be a 2-switch on
G
and let
V_{\tau}=\{a,b,c,d\}
. Notice that
|I\cap V_{\tau}|\leq 2
.

If
\left|I\cap V_{\tau}\right|\leq 1
, then
\alpha(\tau(G))\geq|I|=\alpha(G)
, because
I
is an independent set in
\tau(G)
. We can easily conclude the same when
I\cap V_{\tau}=\{a,d\}
or
I\cap V_{\tau}=\{b,c\}
.

If
I\cap V_{\tau}=\{a,c\}
, notice that
I
is not an independent set in
\tau(G)
, since
ac\in E(\tau(G))
. Thus,
I-a
is an independent set in
\tau(G)
, and so
\alpha(\tau(G))\geq|I-a|=\alpha(G)-1
. The same argument holds if
I\cap V_{\tau}=\{b,d\}
.

In any case,
\alpha(\tau(G))\geq\alpha(G)-1
. Thus,
\alpha
is stable by
Section

8
.
∎

A vertex-cover of a graph
G
is a set of vertices
U\subset V(G)
such that each edge of
G
is incident to at least one vertex of the set
U
. A minimum vertex-cover of
G
is a vertex cover of
G
of minimum size. The
vertex-covering number
of
G
, denoted by
\nu(G)
, is the size of a minimum vertex-cover of
G
.

Corollary 8.11
.

The vertex-covering number is stable.

Proof.

It follows immediately from the well-known fact that

\nu(G)=n-\alpha(G).

∎

Let
G
be a graph. A clique is a subset of
V(G)
that induced a complete subgraph of
G
. The
clique number
of
G
, denoted by
\omega(G)
, is the maximum size of a clique in
G
. Recall that
\omega(G)=\alpha(\overline{G})
.

Theorem 8.12
.

The clique number is stable.

Proof.

If
G
is a graph and
d=d(G)
, let
\overline{d}
be the degree sequence of the complement
\overline{G}
of
G
. Assume that
\omega
is not stable in
\mathcal{G}(d)
. Then, there exist a graph
H\in V(\mathcal{G}(d))
and a 2-switch
\tau={{a\ b}\choose{c\ d}}
on
H
such that
\omega(\tau(H))-\omega(H)\geq 2
. Hence,
\alpha(\overline{\tau(H)})-\alpha(\overline{H})\geq 2
. Now, observe that
\overline{\tau(H)}=\tau^{-1}(\overline{H})
, where
\tau^{-1}={{a\ c}\choose{b\ d}}
. But, this means that

\alpha(\tau^{-1}(\overline{H}))-\alpha(\overline{H})\geq 2,

which contradicts the stability of
\alpha
in
\mathcal{G}(\overline{d})
(
Theorem

8.10
).
∎

Theorem 8.13
.

Independence number, vertex-covering number and clique number have the interval property.

and
8.12
, and

8.2
and

.

8.3
Domination number

A dominating set of a graph
G
is a set
D
of vertices such that every vertex of
G
not in
D
is adjacent to at least one element of
D
. Under this condition, we say that
D
dominates (or covers) a vertex
v
, if
v
is adjacent to some vertex of
D
or if
v\in D
. A minimum dominating set is a dominating set of minimum size. The domination number of
G
, denoted by
\gamma(G)
, is the size of a minimum dominating set of
G
.

Theorem 8.14
.

The domination number is stable.

Proof.

Let
G
be a graph of order
\geq 4
,
D
a minimum dominating set on
G
, and
\tau={{a\ b}\choose{c\ d}}
a 2-switch on
G
.

If
D
is a dominating set in
\tau(G)
, then
\gamma(\tau(G))\leq|D|=\gamma(G)\leq\gamma(G)+1
.

Assume
D
is not a dominating set in
\tau(G)
. As the edges incident to vertices not in
\{a,b,c,d\}
in
G
and in
\tau(G)
are the same,
D
dominates every vertex in
V(G)-\{a,b,c,d\}
. Hence at least one vertex in
\{a,b,c,d\}
is not dominated by
D
in
\tau(G)
. Without loss of generality, assume that such a vertex is
a
and consider its neighbors in
G
and
\tau(G)
. Since the only edge incident to
a
in
G
that is not in
\tau(G)
is
ab
,
b
must be in
D
. Therefore,
d
is dominated in
\tau(G)
by
b
. Moreover,
D\cup a
is a dominating set in
\tau(G)
, because
c
is dominated by
a
in
\tau(G)
. Thus,
\gamma(\tau(G))\leq|D\cup a|=\gamma(G)+1
.

As in either case
\gamma(G)\leq\gamma(G)+1
,
Section

8
implies that
\gamma
is stable.
∎

Theorem 8.15
.

The domination number has the interval property.

Proof.

It follows from
Theorem

8.14
and
Section

8
.
∎

8.4
Number of connected components

Previously, we said that two forests with the same degree sequence
d
must have the same number of connected components. We can rephrase this by saying that
\kappa
is constant on
V(\mathcal{F}(d))
, and therefore, it (trivially) has the interval property in
\mathcal{F}(d)
. We will prove that the same occurs in
\mathcal{G}(d)
.

Theorem 8.16
.

The number of connected components is stable.

Proof.

Let
G
be a graph,
\tau={{a\ b}\choose{c\ d}}
a 2-switch on
G
and
G^{\prime}=G-ab-cd
. Clearly,
\kappa(G^{\prime})\leq\kappa(G)+2
, as deleting an edge increases the number of components by at most 1. If
\kappa(G^{\prime})\leq\kappa(G)+1
, then

\kappa(\tau(G))=\kappa(G^{\prime}+ac+bd)\leq\kappa(G)+1,

as adding edges cannot increase the number of components. Assume
\kappa(G^{\prime})=\kappa(G)+2
. If
a
and
c
are in different components of
G^{\prime}
, then

\kappa(G^{\prime}+ac)=\kappa(G^{\prime})-1\leq\kappa(G)+1,

which implies
\kappa(\tau(G))\leq\kappa(G)+1
. Suppose
a
and
c
are in the same component
H
containing
G^{\prime}
. Then, neither
b
nor
d
are in
H
, because
\kappa(G^{\prime})=\kappa(G)+2
. Furthermore,
b
and
d
must be in different components, since otherwise

\kappa(G)=\kappa(G^{\prime}+ab+cd)=\kappa(G^{\prime})-1.

Hence,

\kappa(G^{\prime}+bd)=\kappa(G^{\prime})-1\leq\kappa(G)+1,

implying that
\kappa(\tau(G))\leq\kappa(G)+1
. Therefore,
\kappa
is stable by
Section

8
.
∎

Theorem 8.17
.

The number of connected components has the interval property.

Proof.

It follows from
Theorem

8.16
and
Section

8
.
∎

8.5
Path-covering number and related parameters

Let
G
be a graph. Two paths in
G
that do not share vertices, are said to be vertex-disjoint. A path covering of
G
is a set of vertex-disjoint paths of
G
containing all the vertices of
G
. The path-covering number of
G
, denoted by
\pi(G)
, is the minimum number of paths in a path-covering of
G
. A minimum path-covering in
G
is a path-covering in
G
of size
\pi(G)
. We can look at a path-covering
\Phi
of
G
as a spanning forest of
G
, whose components are just the paths of
\Phi
.

Theorem 8.18
.

The path-covering number is stable.

Proof.

Let
G
be a graph and
\tau={{a\ b}\choose{c\ d}}
a 2-switch over
G
. Suppose that
\Phi
is a minimum path-covering in
G
. Then,
\kappa(\Phi)=\pi(G)
. There are three cases:

1.

ab
and
cd
are both edges of the forest
\Phi
.

2.

ab
and
cd
are not edges of the forest
\Phi
.

3.

exactly one of
ab
and
cd
is an edge of the forest
\Phi
.

(1).

If
\tau(\Phi)
is a forest, then
\tau(\Phi)
is a path-covering of
\tau(G)
. Hence,
\pi(\tau(G))\leq\pi(G)
. If
\tau(\Phi)
is not a forest, then the vertices
a,b,c,
and
d
are all in the same path
P
of
\Phi
. This path breaks in a cycle
C
and a path after applying
\tau
. If
e
is an edge of
C
, then
\tau(\Phi)-e
is a path-covering of
\tau(G)
. Hence,
\pi(\tau(G))\leq\pi(G)+1
.

(2).

As
\tau
has no effect on
\Phi
, this is still a path-covering in
\tau(G)
. Hence,
\pi(\tau(G))\leq\pi(G)
.

(3).

Assume that
ab\in E(\Phi)
and
cd\notin E(\Phi)
. Note that
\tau(\Phi)-ab
is a path-covering of
\tau(G)
. Hence,
\pi(\tau(G))\leq\pi(G)+1
.

Therefore,
\pi(\tau(G))\leq\pi(G)+1
, and so
\pi
is stable by
Section

8
.
∎

Let
G
be a graph. A subset
S
of initially infected vertices of a graph G is called forcing set if we can infect the entire graph by iteratively applying the following process. At each step, any infected vertex which has a unique uninfected neighbor, infects this neighbor. The
zero-forcing number
of
G
, which is denoted by
Z(G)
, is the minimum size of a forcing set in
G
. It is well-known that for all graphs
G
,
\pi(G)\leq Z(G)
, and that for trees the path-covering number agrees with the zero-forcing number (see
[
1
]
, Proposition 4.2). Therefore, we have the following.

Corollary 8.19
.

The zero-forcing number is stable in
\mathcal{F}(d)
.

Proof.

It follows from the previous discussion and
Theorem

8.18
.
∎

Let
G
be a graph without isolated vertices. A sequence
(v_{1},\dots,v_{k})
, where
v_{i}\in V(G)
, is called a Z-sequence if, for each
i
,

N_{i}-\bigcup_{j=1}^{i-1}\left(N_{j}\cup\{v_{j}\}\right)\neq\varnothing,

where
N_{i}
is the (open) neighborhood of
v_{i}
in
G
. The
Z-Grundy domination number

\gamma_{gr}^{Z}(G)
of the graph

is the length of a longest Z-sequence. In
[
5
]
the following Theorem was proved.

Theorem 8.20

(
[
5
]
, Theorem 2.2)
.

If
G
i a graph without isolated vertices, then

\gamma_{gr}^{Z}(G)+Z(G)=|G|.

Corollary 8.21
.

Let
d
be a degree sequence of a forest without isolated vertices. Then, the Z-Grundy domination number is stable in
\mathcal{F}(d)
.

Proof.

It follows from
Theorem

8.20
and
Section

8.5
.
∎

Theorem 8.22
.

The path-covering number has the interval property. The zero-forcing number and the Z-Grundy domination number have the interval property in

8.18

8.5

8.6
Chromatic number

A coloring of
G
is a function
f:V(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
such that
xy\in E(G)
implies
f(x)\neq f(y)
, for every
xy\in E(G)
. In this context,
f(V(G))
is called the set of colors of
f
. If
|f(V(G))|=k
, we say that
f
is a
k
-coloring of
G
. The chromatic number of a graph
G
, denoted with
\chi(G)
, is the smallest value of
k
for which there is a
k
-coloring of
G
.

Theorem 8.23
.

The chromatic number is stable.

Proof.

Let
\tau={{a\ b}\choose{c\ d}}
be a 2-switch over a graph
G
which has a
k
-coloring
f
, with
k=\chi(G)
.
Notice that if
f
is not a coloring of
\tau(G)
, then either
f(a)=f(c)
or
f(b)=f(d)
. Assume without loss of generality that
f(V(G))=\{1,\ldots,k\}
. Define
\varphi:V(G)\rightarrow\mathbb{N}
as

\varphi(v)=\left\{\begin{array}[]{ll}f(v),&\text{if }v\not\in\{c,d\},\\
k+1,&\text{otherwise.}\end{array}\right.

Then
\varphi(a)\neq\varphi(c)
and
\varphi(b)\neq\varphi(d)
, which implies that
\varphi
is a coloring of
\tau(G)
.
Furthermore,
|\varphi(V(G))|=k+1
.
Therefore,
\chi(\tau(G))\leq\chi(G)+1
, and so
\tau
is stable by
Section

8
.
∎

Theorem 8.24
.

The chromatic number has the interval property.

Proof.

It follows from
Theorem

8.23
and
Section

8
.
∎
