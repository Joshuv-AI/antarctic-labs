Feasibility and performance evaluation of a robotic steerable endoscopic submucosal dissection knife (with video)
Authors: Sang Hyun Kim, Chanwoo Kim, JaeYoun Kim, Woocheol Shin, Hyuk Soon Choi, Han Jo Jeon, Jae Min Lee, Eun Sun Kim, Bora Keum, Yoon Tae Jeen, Daehie Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) requires high precision and dexterity and poses significant challenges for novice operators. Robotic steerable ESD knives have been developed to improve tip control and reduce the technical difficulties associated with conventional instruments. This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility, efficiency, and safety of a novel robotic-steerable ESD knife using simulated tissue models and to compare procedural outcomes between novice and expert endoscopists. Two experts and two novice endoscopists performed standardized ESD procedures using synthetic gastric tissue models. The experiments compared two modalities, robotic knife-assisted ESD (RESD) and conventional knife–assisted ESD (CESD). Each operator performed five procedures for each method. Performance metrics included total procedure time, submucosal dissection speed, dissection accuracy, and incidence of procedural complications. Twenty RESD and CESD procedures were analyzed. The RESD group demonstrated a significantly shorter total procedure time (30.1 ± 13.1 min) compared with CESD (42.3 ± 17.4 min; P < 0.05). Submucosal dissection speed was also significantly higher in RESD (48.3 ± 7.8 mm²/min) than in CESD (20.2 ± 7.4 mm²/min; P < 0.05). Furthermore, dissection accuracy was markedly higher with RESD than with CESD (91.1 ± 4.9% vs. 75.1 ± 14.1%, P < 0.05). The steerable robotic ESD knife facilitated a more controlled and precise submucosal dissection, particularly aiding novice endoscopists in overcoming technical challenges. These findings indicate that robotic knife–assisted ESD may enhance procedural performance, with pronounced benefits for less-experienced operators.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-53173-4.

Introduction

Endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) is an established therapeutic modality for early gastric cancer and precancerous lesions that offers the benefits of en bloc resection and organ preservation1,2. However, ESD is technically demanding and requires advanced expertise and meticulous submucosal dissection skills, resulting in a steep learning curve3,4. Consequently, novice endoscopists often encounter substantial challenges in achieving procedural proficiency5,6.

These difficulties stem from the need to maintain an appropriate dissection plane and the limited maneuverability of conventional ESD knives. Consequently, procedures performed by less experienced operators are often prolonged, with a higher incidence of blind dissection and an increased risk of muscular injury or perforation7. To overcome these limitations, numerous device innovations have been proposed. Various traction techniques such as clip-in-line methods8, the S-O clip (Zeon Medical, Tokyo, Japan)9, elastic bands, and more recently, systems such as the ProdiGi Traction Wire system (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA)10 and TracMotion (Fujifilm, Tokyo, Japan)11 have been developed to improve the exposure of the submucosal layer. Despite these advances, relatively little attention has been directed toward enhancing the maneuverability of the ESD knife. Even when traction devices provide improved visualization, precise submucosal dissection with conventional knives requires a high degree of technical skill. The ability to adjust the dissection plane freely and intuitively remains dependent on operator experience12. These considerations underscore the need for next-generation ESD knives that can overcome the limitations of conventional instruments. A device that enables intuitive multidirectional control of the knife tip could facilitate a more precise dissection, reduce reliance on operator experience, and enhance the safety, efficiency, and accessibility of ESD. In this context, this study developed a novel robotic-steerable ESD knife with a precise tip-angulation capability designed to enhance maneuverability and enable more stable submucosal dissection. The device can be used in combination with an endoscope-mounted traction unit developed in parallel, allowing simultaneous stable tissue traction and precise dissection.

This study aimed to evaluate the feasibility and efficacy of this integrated therapeutic endoscopy platform. Using a synthetic gastric tissue model, the performance of novice and expert endoscopists was compared to determine whether steering functionality could reduce technical barriers and improve dissection quality.

Materials and methods

Steerable ESD knife platform

The system consists of four main components: a robotic steerable ESD knife, an endoscope-mounted traction unit, controllers, and a driver unit (Fig. 1a). Similar to the conventional knife, the steerable ESD knife was inserted through the accessory channel of the endoscope for submucosal dissection. The steerable knife is based on a two-degree-of-freedom (DOF) structure in which each DOF provides independent motion through a rearranged joint configuration. Actuation was achieved using a tendon–sheath mechanism comprising four tendons and their corresponding sheaths. The knife was connected to both the electrosurgical generator (VIO 200D; ERBE Elektromedizin GmbH, Tübingen, Germany) and the driver unit. The steerable ESD knife incorporated a tip-angulation mechanism (± 80 °) that enabled active bidirectional control during submucosal dissection (Fig. 1b). Tip movement was regulated using an endoscope-mounted controller that allowed real-time adjustment of the dissection direction. The endoscope-mounted traction unit comprised external forceps attached to the distal tip of the endoscope in a cap-like configuration via a connecting wire (Fig. 1b). When a dissection flap was created, it was grasped using a traction unit to expose the submucosal layer. Once the submucosal plane was adequately visualized, the steerable knife allowed precise bidirectional dissection parallel to the submucosal layer. Using the controller, both the direction and amplitude of tip angulation can be finely adjusted. When a misalignment occurs between the dissection plane and the knife tip, the system enables vertical adjustment of the cutting level to maintain an optimal bidirectional dissection trajectory.

Experiment protocol

This randomized crossover experimental study was conducted using synthetic gastric tissue models to compare robotic steerable knife–assisted ESD (RESD) to conventional ESD (CESD). Before performing RESD, each operator received a 10-minute orientation that included a hands-on tutorial on the system’s control interface and basic articulation maneuvers. Four endoscopists participated in the study: two experts who had performed more than 200 ESD procedures and two novices who had initiated ESD training but had completed fewer than 30 procedures. Informed consent was obtained from all experts involved in the study. A commercially available synthetic gastric tissue (VTT; Kotobuki, Tokyo, Japan) was mounted on an automatic endoscopic simulator (EndoCubot; EndoRobotics, Seoul, Korea) at the lesser curvature of the antrum. A 30 × 25 mm oval lesion was drawn on the tissue surface, and ESD was performed within the marked area. Each endoscopist alternated between CESD and RESD, performing five procedures with each technique, for 40 procedures. A transparent cap (FM-EC0002; Finemedix, Seoul, South Korea) was attached to the distal end of a standard endoscope (GIF-HQ290; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). Submucosal dissection was performed using a dual knife (KD-650 L; Olympus, Tokyo, Japan), following a standardized sequence of lesion marking, submucosal injection, circumferential incision, and submucosal dissection. In RESD, the incision was initiated along the marked lesion using a steerable knife tip. Conventional submucosal dissection is performed until the mucosa is sufficiently mobile for grasping. An endoscope-mounted traction device was used to grasp and lift the mucosal flap, thereby exposing the submucosal plane (Fig. 2). The operator manipulated the steerable knife using an endoscope-mounted controller, adjusting both the direction and amplitude of the tip angulation to align the knife parallel to the dissection plane (Fig. 2). When the knife tip and dissection plane were not aligned, the cutting level was vertically adjusted to optimize bidirectional dissection. The steerable knife was connected to the same electrosurgical unit with settings identical to those used for CESD. The resected VTT models were retrieved after each procedure for subsequent evaluation. This study did not involve human patients, human-derived tissues, or animal subjects. Therefore, approval from an institutional review board or ethics committee was not required. Written informed consent was obtained from all participating physicians prior to study participation.

Outcome measures

A comparative technical analysis was conducted between the conventional and robotic surgery groups. The evaluated parameters included the resected specimen area, total ESD procedure time, dissection speed, dissection accuracy, and occurrence of perforations. The resected specimen area was calculated using the formula for an ellipse, based on the longest and shortest diameters measured immediately after resection: Area = π × (long axis/2) × (short axis/2). Total procedure time was defined as the interval between lesion marking and specimen retrieval. Dissection accuracy was assessed by evaluating the adequacy of the dissection depth. The resected VTT bed was inspected immediately after the resection to determine the depth of dissection. The VTT is a commercially available multilayered konjac-based training material designed to simulate the structure and mechanical properties of the human stomach. It consists of three layers: mucosa, submucosa, and muscle13. Each layer was made of polymeric materials with distinct stiffness and texture to reproduce realistic endoscopic tactile feedback during incision and dissection. The submucosal layer allows injection and elevation, enabling a realistic simulation of ESD procedures. After each procedure, the ESD defect was scanned using a three-dimensional (3D) scanner (EinScan HX; SHINING 3D, China) to quantify the dissection depth. The specimen was placed on a reflective plate to minimize glare and scanned manually in laser mode from multiple angles. The point cloud data were aligned, merged, and reconstructed into a 3D mesh (STL format) using EXScan Pro software (SHINING 3D, China). The resection depth and surface morphology were analyzed using MATLAB (MathWorks, USA). A reference plane was defined based on the surrounding intact mucosal surface, and the relative depth of the resected region was calculated with respect to this plane. The reconstructed surface was visualized as a color-coded depth map from which the main depressed region was segmented to determine the mean and maximum resection depths. (Fig. 3). An adequate dissection zone was defined as a submucosal layer between 25% and 75% of the total thickness. The proportion of dissected areas within this zone represents dissection accuracy. Subgroup analysis was also performed to compare novice and expert operators. Using both robotic and conventional techniques, ESD procedure time, dissection speed, and dissection accuracy were analyzed according to the operator’s experience.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using the R software (version 3.5.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables are summarized as counts and percentages. The distribution of continuous data was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. For paired comparisons, normally distributed variables were analyzed using a paired t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were evaluated using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Paired binary outcomes were compared using McNemar’s test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05, which was considered statistically significant.

Results

Technical outcomes of RESD vs. CESD

Forty ESD procedures were performed on synthetic gastric tissue models, 20 using RESD and 20 using the CESD (Fig. 4) (Video 1). Table 1 summarizes the comparative outcomes between the two groups. There was no significant difference in specimen size between RESD and CESD (558.8 ± 94.1 mm² vs. 570.2 ± 102.8 mm², P = 0.53), and complete resection was achieved in all cases. The mean procedure time was significantly shorter in the RESD group compared with the CESD group (5.7 ± 1.4 min vs. 11.0 ± 4.6 min, P < 0.05). Correspondingly, dissection speed was higher in the RESD group (186.7 ± 62.1 mm²/min) than in the CESD group (109.7 ± 22.3 mm²/min, P < 0.05). Three-dimensional scanning of the resection beds demonstrated that dissection accuracy, defined as the proportion of dissected area within 25–75% of the submucosal layer, was significantly higher in the RESD group than in the CESD group (91.1 ± 4.9% vs. 75.1 ± 14.1%, P < 0.05) (Table 1).

Subgroup analysis

To compare the effect of the robotic knife according to the participants’ experience level, the performance was analyzed using the novel platform. Both dissection speed and accuracy were compared between the conventional and robotic groups. For dissection speed, a significant increase was observed in both groups: in the expert group, it increased from 133.4 ± 13.7 mm²/min to 211.1 ± 56.0 mm²/min (P < 0.05), and in the novice group, from 74.2 ± 16.6 mm²/min to 162.3 ± 60.4 mm²/min (P < 0.05) (Fig. 5a). Regarding dissection accuracy, a modest improvement was noted in the expert group (from 87.7 ± 4.6% to 94.0 ± 4.6%, P < 0.05), whereas the novice group demonstrated a marked enhancement (from 66.2 ± 7.0% to 90.4 ± 5.5%, P < 0.05) (Fig. 5b). Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2.

Discussion

This study developed a novel robotic-steerable ESD knife platform and evaluated its performance using a synthetic gastric tissue model. Artificial lesions > 3 cm in length were created to compare the efficacy of ESD performed using a robotic knife versus a conventional knife. This system enables intuitive and dynamic control of the knife tip, allowing operators to maintain a stable dissection plane and perform consistent and precise submucosal dissection. Compared to the conventional group, the robotic group demonstrated significantly shorter procedure times, faster dissection speeds, and superior dissection accuracy.

ESD offers clear technical advantages for the resection of gastrointestinal neoplasms; however, its widespread adoption has been limited by procedural complexity and complication concerns14,15. Perforation remains the most serious complication of ESD, with reported rates ranging from 1.2% to 5.2% in the stomach and from 4.8% to 5.7% in the colon16,17. Most previous efforts to reduce procedural complications and improve dissection efficiency have focused on traction-based techniques8. Methods such as clip-with-line, S-O clip, and traction wire facilitate exposure of the submucosal layer but do not fundamentally enhance the maneuverability of the knife itself. Consequently, even with improved visualization, precise submucosal dissection relies heavily on the technical skill of the operator. This study demonstrated that improving the controllability of endoscopic resection instruments, rather than simply enhancing traction, provides a more direct and effective means of increasing procedural efficiency and safety.

The key technical advantage of a steerable ESD knife is its active knife tip angulation mechanism, which enables the operator to finely adjust the dissection direction in real-time during the procedure. Conventional ESD knives have a fixed tip geometry, which requires the endoscope to be twisted or rotated to change the dissection direction. This maneuver is technically demanding, particularly within the confined visual and working spaces of the gastrointestinal tract18,19. In contrast, a robotic knife allows precise adjustment of the tip direction through an endoscope-mounted controller while maintaining a fixed endoscopic view, thereby facilitating efficient and accurate submucosal dissection. This enhanced controllability results in shorter overall procedure times and increased dissection speeds in the present study.

This study quantitatively assessed dissection accuracy for the first time using 3D scanning technology. Maintaining a consistent dissection depth is essential to achieve complete curative resection without causing muscle injury or perforation. Therefore, the ability to sustain an appropriate and stable dissection plane is a key indicator of procedural quality in ESD. An adequate dissection zone was defined as the submucosal layer between 25% and 75% of its total thickness, and the proportion of the dissected area was calculated within this range. This proportion was significantly higher in the RESD group than in the CESD group. In conventional ESD, frequent changes in the visual field make it difficult to maintain an appropriate dissection depth under varying conditions20,21. However, in this experiment, the combined system of the steerable knife and endoscope-mounted traction device maintained a stable dissection plane under various conditions, enabling precise and stable submucosal dissection parallel to the target layer.

From a training perspective, the marked improvement observed in the novice group was particularly noteworthy. Although both the dissection speed and accuracy improved in the expert group when the robotic system was used, the relative gain was much greater among the novices. This finding suggests that the steerable knife can shorten the ESD learning curve, by reducing the dependence on complex manual skills such as endoscopic torque control and wrist rotation3.

However, this study has several limitations. First, it was conducted in a laboratory setting using synthetic gastric tissue, which does not fully replicate the complex conditions encountered in clinical practice. Accordingly, two key considerations should be addressed: the applicability of the system across various gastrointestinal organs in actual clinical practice and the management of potential adverse events. Although synthetic models cannot fully reproduce real clinical environments, the fundamental mechanism of traction-assisted exposure and controlled dissection is preserved. Therefore, the system is expected to provide comparable benefits in clinical practice and may assist operators, particularly novices, in achieving more stable and consistent dissection. The applicability of this technique to ESD in the esophagus and colorectum should also be considered. These procedures are inherently more challenging due to thinner submucosal layers, narrower luminal spaces, and limited maneuverability, all of which increase the risk of perforation. In this context, the device’s active tip angulation may provide potential advantages by enabling maintenance of a stable dissection plane, thereby reducing unintended injury to the muscularis propria. Furthermore, its ability to provide both lateral and vertical motion control may facilitate more precise and controlled dissection, even in anatomically constrained environments such as the esophagus and colon. However, in the esophagus and colorectum, the narrow luminal space may limit the full range of lateral motion of the knife, potentially resulting in reduced dissection speed. Further validation through preclinical and clinical studies is required before direct clinical application. Regarding adverse events, the system is fully compatible with the standard endoscopic working channel, allowing immediate exchange to conventional instruments for hemostasis or defect closure. As such, complications such as bleeding or perforation can be promptly managed using established techniques without procedural delay. Second, this study has limitations related to its study design. The number of procedures performed was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. In addition, a potential learning effect may have influenced the overall results, as operator performance can improve with repeated procedures. This effect may be more pronounced in novice operators. To minimize this bias, a crossover design was adopted in which each operator alternated between conventional and robotic ESD. Nevertheless, the influence of cumulative procedural experience cannot be completely eliminated. Therefore, additional large-scale studies, including those using animal models or human subjects, are warranted to validate the reproducibility and safety of the proposed system. Third, ergonomic parameters such as the NASA-TLX score were not employed to quantitatively assess the operator’s physical and mental workloads; future studies should incorporate such quantitative ergonomic evaluations. This study developed an in-house endoscopic knife fabricated using 3D printing with brass, with an estimated cost of approximately $2,000 for the driver unit and $250 for the disposable knife and associated components. While the current cost structure and technical challenges related to large-scale manufacturing may pose barriers to commercialization, these findings underscore the need for further technological refinement and cost optimization strategies. Importantly, the detachable design enables reuse of the driver unit while replacing only the disposable knife, offering a potentially cost-efficient model for clinical application. Although the use of clinical-grade materials may increase production costs, standardization and mass production are expected to improve overall cost-effectiveness over time.

In summary, the robotic-steerable ESD knife developed in this study enabled intuitive, precise, and efficient submucosal dissection, demonstrating superior performance in terms of procedural speed and dissection accuracy compared with conventional ESD in synthetic gastric tissue models. The system offers significant advantages to novice users, indicating its strong potential for use in both ESD training and clinical practice. When integrated with a traction device, a robotic-steerable knife platform may serve as a next-generation robot-assisted ESD system that further enhances procedural safety, efficiency, and accessibility.
