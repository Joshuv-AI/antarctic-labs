 Executive Summary
The motivation for this project was to develop a high-pressure 100+ kW fuel cell stack that can
be used in a modular fashion to power HD applications up to 1 MW. One of the issues that was
identified during fuel cell system integration was the need for extra space for heat rejection
systems(radiators) as well as additional fans and associated energy consumption to compensate
for lower operating temperatures of fuel cells compared to internal combustion (IC) engines. To
maximize the heat rejection capability of existing water-based coolant systems that are
prevalent on IC engine-based systems, fuel cell stack design was developed to withstand and
operate at IC engine coolant temperatures or higher, which in turn helps to reduce the radiator
size requirement.

The work presented in this study was to develop a stack design with optimized bipolar plate
fluid flow channel geometry as well as special membrane electrode assembly (MEA) to maintain
performance and durability at >100degC operating temperatures, and 250kPa pressure like
currently available commercial fuel cells. The original goal of the project was to demonstrate a
100kW fuel cell with capability to meet performance and durability of current fuel cells at
temperature >100degC with an air compressor that is designed to meet the air flow
requirement of stack as well as cost and efficiency. Additional goals were to establish advanced
manufacturing methods to scale this technology and do a detailed cost analysis based on slow
ramp up production numbers. We were unable to get to the original goal due to project funding
end and equipment issues. But the work progressed the effort to develop MEAs and stack
design that help fuel cell to operate at temperatures >100degC.

DOE has reported a more challenging performance target of 2.5 kW/gPGM power (1.07 A/cm2
current density) at 0.7V after 25,000 hour-equivalent accelerated durability test
(https://millionmilefuelcelltruck.org/) after this project proposal was awarded. Stack
performance done as part of this project does not meet that target at the end of test but it does
at the beginning of life. Two commercially available materials, a catalyst and an ionomer, have
already been identified and are expected to bring the stack performance closer to the target
beyond this program. Also, a higher temperature of 110 °C is possible as demonstrated by
subscale accelerated stress tests but verifications were limited due to the current high power
test station limitations.

 Project Objective
The objective of this project is to develop a new modular polymer electrolyte membrane fuel
cell (PEMFC) stack with a power rating of 100 kW to power heavy duty applications. The stack
will be designed to run at high pressure and tolerate high temperatures during peak power
excursions (> 100 °C). A key metric is the stack cost of $80/kW - 1 at 100,000 units per year
volume. To achieve this objective a study on advanced manufacturing methods to reduce
production costs will be a key outcome.

 Background
PEM fuel cell power trains are an attractive option to provide zero emission solutions for the
Heavy-Duty (HD) market without sacrificing payload and up-time. However, for commercial
viability, fuel cell systems need to reduce costs, increase continuous rated power output and
operating temperatures. Cummins has 100 years of history in the HD space and has been
successful in aggregating engine designs over multiple different markets to increase sales
volumes and cover development costs. This project seeks to develop a high-pressure 100+ kW
fuel cell stack and system that can be used in a modular fashion to power HD applications up to
1 MW.

Stack components will be developed and prototyped in the US enhancing the maturity of the US
supply chain and encouraging investment. Cummins Accelera (CA) will lead the stack design,
using a bipolar plate incorporating micro-channels for efficient high-pressure operation,
designed for volume manufacturing. Plate manufacturing capabilities were initially developed in
partnership with Dana. However, the manufacturer may be revised due to the recent change in
stack design platform. Channel geometry will be determined from modeling by Cummins R&T
Advanced Fuel Cell team at Cummins Technical Center (CTC) to optimize performance and life at
high pressure and temperature. The membrane and MEA will be developed in partnership with
W. L. Gore who will provide a membrane with optimal trade-off between performance,
temperature, cost, and durability. Argonne National Laboratory (ANL) will help to define the
best available MEA architecture by modeling load cycles and flowing down requirements from
the system and stack down to the MEA. In parallel, a state-of-the-art electric air compressor or
e-Turbo will be developed by Cummins Engine Components (CEC formerly Cummins Turbo
Technologies) with best-in-class efficiency and cost to provide the air pressure maximizing
power and operating temperature of the stack.

 Project results and discussion
M1: Cell active area definition:
Significant events took place during 2024, which led to a recent strategic change in the
commercialization of hydrogen technologies at Cummins. These events include a global and
slower uptake in hydrogen technologies and a less than expected benefit of the Clean
Hydrogen Production Tax Credit established under Section 45V of the US tax code. As a result,
the proposed active area fuel cell platform (908 cm2) was discontinued in favor of a current
industry standard active area (350 to 500 cm2) that is still able to reach a stack size of 100 kW
and operate at temperatures above 100 °C and low relative humidities. But the progression of
the project has been summarized below so as not to loose the learnings along the project
duration.

6.1 Task - Cell Active Area Sizing:
The bipolar plate is one of the many repeating units that house the individual CCM (or MEA)
and Gas diffusion layer (GDL) assemblies. With reactant gases, hydrogen and air present in the
active area, the cell will produce a voltage difference. Stacking the cells and utilizing the
bipolarity of the cell assembly allows for a series connection and a linear multiple for the total
voltage output. The size of the active area dictates the total current that the fuel cell will
output, at a given current density. The power output of the stack is the product of the voltage
and current and is therefore directly related to the cell count and active area.

The DC voltage output of a fuel cell will vary with current draw and can reduce as much as 40%
from low load to high load. For a fuel cell to be electrically integrated into a hybrid powertrain, a
DC-to-DC converter is required to regulate the fuel cell output voltage to closely match the
battery and system voltage. With the constraints imposed by the DC-DC system, as well as the
parasitic values to continuously operate the stack we can size the overall bipolar plate.
Additional considerations include the largest power node that can be packaged into a single
stack fuel cell package. The detail on how to determine the maximum allowable cell count is
considered in the next portion of the analytics but must be practically considered at this point
both for manufacturability and to ensure cell-to-cell distribution is fluidically attainable without
excessive spatial requirements. The DC-DC constraint suggests that a 1000 cm2 active area will

                                                          Page 15 of 102

best cope with the voltage limits of a unidirectional DCDC system allowing it to be the largest
single stack fuel cell solution.

In addition to performance, stack cost was also analyzed to understand the economics of the
active area vs. cell count trade-offs to enable the best chances for FC adoption. The results
show that the 1000cm2 is the most cost-effective path forward for the size of the fuel cell
engine Cummins is currently designing for. This needs to be revisited with both the bipolar plate
and MEA vendors to validate their expected pricing as annual volumes increase. The active area
under consideration is preliminary and subject to change based on supplier input and iterative
product development phase until the end of Q2 of the project.

6.2 Task – Develop Product Requirements: Bipolar Plates:
Initially, to study the characteristics of the BPP and the design of flow channel geometry, the
flow field was modeled, including channel geometry, to gain insight and improved
understanding of optimal operation. Future heavy-duty products should be aimed at improving
the technology to suit various platforms and customer requirements where the stack will be
operated under unique conditions. Moreover, to implement design changes on production
tooling, require extensive costs and lengthy lead times, so a smaller platform that enables us to
rapidly test design changes in cost effective way helped us to be more confident and effective in
long-term product change on future bipolar plate platforms. Therefore, a smaller platform that
has about 82cm2 active area was considered and modeled using a CFD tool to develop a
graphite bipolar plate that can be used to mimic current and future detailed design features
and use as a test platform to improve our products. This small bipolar plate was used to
expedite the development of the future large platforms, additionally, can we used in MEA
performance and durability test.
The 82 series design retains header widths, land-channel ratio, and most 2-D dimensions. The
height was modified to produce similar pressure drops. The mass flow rate was used for the
inlet boundary condition and the pressure was used for the outlet boundary condition. The
working fluid at the anode was hydrogen and the fluid at the cathode was air.

Subtask 1.2: Stack Development (M1-M17)
The bipolar plate has several functional requirements within the fuel cell stack. In addition to
providing the foundation for the electrical architecture and being mechanically robust, it must

                                                   Page 24 of 102

facilitate and manage the fluidic transport of reactants and product water in the active area
along with coolant transport through intermediary channels. To ensure adequate reactant
distribution through the individual unit cell, as well as the unit cells spatially located over the
length of a stack assembly, fluidic models were developed to analyze several sections of the
bipolar plate and stack sections.

The three fluidic areas of interest are the inlet manifolds, the flow fields, and the outlet
manifolds. The manifold geometry is part of the bipolar plate design and is constructed by
stacking multiple cell assemblies together, resulting in a stack-long cavity. When designed
appropriately, the manifolds allow for a near equal amount of reactant to be directed from the
common plenums into the flow fields of the individual bipolar plates. Having a near equal
amount of reactant span the surface of the CCM supports a more even current distribution and
helps to prevent local reactant starvation.

Subsequently, the bipolar plate must be able to manage the product water formation and
provide advection by supporting the working pressures, temperatures, and channel velocities.
This will help to extend, or avoid, the concentration polarization voltage losses that a cell at
high loads may experience.

Both effects can cause accelerated aging effects within the catalyst layers and membrane,
which will result in rapid voltage losses. As a result, this section of the report details the flow
field optimization for the reactant and coolant channels to complement the high-
performance targets and improving robustness.

Reactant flow field optimization
The reactant flow field channels were developed with two key requirements. Firstly, the GDL
must be evenly supported, with no unsupported lengths spanning more than a single channel
width. This feature prevents excessive GDL sag into the main flow field or distribution
sections of the bipolar plate. Excessive GDL sag results in unnecessary pressure drop within
the bipolar plate and can cause flow bias within the active area. Additionally, having a more
uniformly supported GDL ensures a more homogeneous MPL/CCM interface, which may
improve performance by a lower contact resistance for electron transport.

Secondly, the channel-to-channel reactant distribution must be as spatially even as possible
at the intended power node. Having a near-equal reaction distribution enables a more

                                        Page 27 of 102

uniform local current density and subsequently helps to avoid local low voltages or cell
reversal.

Figure 4 shows an example of the anode channel distribution curves for the previous- and
next-generation graphite flow field design approaches. The horizontal axis spans from the first
channel, closest to the anode manifold inlet, to the last channel, furthest from the anode
manifold inlet. The vertical axis shows the normalized mass flow along all the channels and is
normalized to the bulk excess stoichiometric reactant flow rate. A value of 1.0 means that the
bulk flowrate is perfectly achieved respective of the channel requirement, while a value larger
or smaller signifies a deviation from the targeted value. The previous design has a
stoichiometric variance of ±20 %, while the newly designed and optimized flow field has a

Coolant flow field optimization
The entire perimeter was utilized to align scaling laws with practical fluidic limitations for fuel,
air, and coolant handling in mobility applications. As a result, the coolant manifolds were
located at the top-left and bottom-right manifolds shown in Figure 8.

 Figure 8 - Example CAD rendering of the bipolar plate.

With the coolant ports located in the tangent direction to the cathode ports, instinctually it
would seem that without optimization the temperature contours may result in local cold and
hot spots that do not coincide with the water concentration increase, and subsequently the

                                           Page 30 of 102

increased advection requirements. Having a thermal gradient that does not monotonically
increase in a similar manner as the product water concentration gradient may lead to local
thermodynamic conditions that are favorable to promote condensation. 3D CFD models were
developed to develop the cross-flow regime and optimize the thermal gradients of the
coolant to be more congruent with the water production direction.

 Figure 9 – Conceptual coolant flow arrangement with temperature callouts. The coolant flow
 path is shown in red, while the cathode flow path is shown in green. The graph at the top
 shows the variation in water content in the air flow from the inlet to the outlet port.

This optimization task can be explained by regarding the six temperature callouts on Figure 9
and assuming the water constituent monotonically increases from left-to-right, with the
absence of a dependency from the top to the bottom of the active area. This assumption
coincides with the cathode flow field design objectives of a uniform air distribution and
progressive water accumulation from inlet to outlet port. On that basis, there are two main
goals to reach the desired thermal gradients. First, a minimal temperature difference should
be achieved between the vertical points with T1 = T4, T2 = T5, and T3 = T6 respectively. Second,
temperatures should follow the sequence: T3,6 > T2,5 > T1,4.

With the above two goals in mind, it becomes clear that the conceptual case, with no
optimization, has large temperature uniformity maldistributions. Although the temperatures
at T1, T2, and T3 would follow a monotonic increase, by the time the coolant reaches T 4 the
temperature would increase substantially. Therefore, the first objective would not be
achieved, with only a partial fulfillment of the second objective.

Figure 10 shows the baseline and the optimized coolant flow field channel results. After
several iterations of detailed flow field optimization, the right contour was developed while
remaining within a 70 kPa pressure differential through the coolant flow field with the use of
a 50:50 water-ethylene glycol mixture for the medium. By comparing the thermal lines that
correspond to various y-direction locations, we can see that the optimal design converges
towards the right-half of the bipolar plate. This is where the advection requirements are the
highest and thus the thermal uniformity should be maximized. With the aforementioned
temperatures and subsequently water constituent goals in mind, the optimized temperature
contours fall more closely to the ideal scenario.

Manifold analytical analysis
The manifold geometries shown in Figure 1 were developed with two primary functional
requirements. Firstly, the plate geometry must have the mechanical strength to withstand the
operational pressure at the targeted temperature. Secondly, the manifolds must fluidically
support the stack reactants and coolant supply and exhaust while maintaining adequate cell-
to-cell distribution along the length of the stack.

The preliminary manifold wall thickness dimensions were developed by reviewing manifold
pressure testing conducted on existing graphite composite bipolar plates. Due to the highly
form-dependent and anisotropic strength characteristics of the formed graphite, test samples

                                                         Page 32 of 102

were designed to represent the formed geometry to experimentally develop and validate the
required strength for the maximum allowable working pressure.

Reactant distribution models were developed for a “U” shaped configuration, where the inlet
and outlet stack ports are located on the same end of the stack (as opposed to a Z type flow
arrangement). The manifolds were then sized to allow for a stoichiometric molar flow tolerance
variation of no greater than 10 % along the length of the stack. The stoichiometric nominal for
the excess air ratio was set to 2.0, and an anode nominal excess fuel ratio of 1.4 was used. The
study was conducted for each of the aspect ratios and channel geometries developed for the
active areas.

Active Area Optimization
The active area was further refined in this quarter to include the above analysis in addition to
bipolar plate and MEA manufacturing process optimization studies. Based on the output of
these studies, the proposed active area is reduced from 1000 cm2 to 900 cm2.

The bipolar plates used in this project will be made of a graphite composite material, for its
resistance to corrosion and the ability to operate at the targeted temperature and pressure.

In a prior section, the active area of the fuel cell was set in the range of 750 to 1000 cm 2, which
is larger than what Cummins FCHT has worked with in its commercialized fuel cells. Meanwhile,
the new 100 kW fuel cell stack will operate at higher temperatures and pressures than

Channel size limits
The design of the bipolar plates will also be constrained by the manufacturing capabilities in
channel sizing. Cummins discussed with DANA to understand the expected limits, which are
both imposed by current mainstream machining capabilities and by the physical characteristics
of the compression molding process.

Amongst the parameters shown in Figure 2, the most important parameters are the landing
widths (L4) and the channel depth (h). The top of the landing in the plate is the bottom of the
channels in the tool, so the parameter L4 is the minimal feature that can be machined into the
tool. DANA has advised that, as a general guideline, L4 should adhere to a minimal size of 0.5
mm for computerized numerical control (CNC) machining. Smaller sizes can possibly be
achieved with electrical discharge machining (EDM), but the finishes will have to be less ideal.
Another constraint is that the depth of the channels (h) should be less than the landing width
L4. The higher the aspect ratio (h/L4), the narrower the gap, and the more difficulty for
material to be “squeezed” into the landing area.

These design guidelines are an input to the bipolar plate design. In addition, Cummins will
coordinate with DANA to perform trial tooling by preparing a small-sized mold with smallest
channel sizes expected on the plate design. The trial will define the lower limits of channel
geometries for plate design considerations.

Manifold geometry for pressure containment
Nominally, the stack will be designed to operate at 2.5 bara, which drives both the dimensional
requirements for flow distribution along with the structural requirements for mechanical
strength and safety. Finite-element analysis (FEA) can be a useful tool for predicting material
behavior and failure mode. A FEA simulation of manifold design has been completed by
Cummins CTC. However, because the composite graphite material is highly anisotropic, and the
mechanical properties are a function of the part thickness and the parameters during
fabrication, the design was investigated with experimental validation, guided by FEA
predictions. In parallel with FEA simulation, a manifold pressure study was conducted to
understand the mechanical integrity of the manifold against the targeted operating pressure.

A manifold coupon (Figure 12) was developed for numerical and experimental study of
manifold pressure behavior. This coupon was prepared by DANA, using the same material and
processing conditions as would be for the actual bipolar plates. Cummins FCHT will performed
pressure tests to determine the maximum pressure that this design can sustain. Where time

Forming capability
The composite bipolar plates for this stack will be manufactured by compression molding. Key
to any bipolar plate design is the ability to manufacture the plate in a manner conducive to
high-volume production. These relate to capital intensity required for manufacture and bipolar
plate function respectively.

                                         Page 35 of 102

DANA, Cummins’ bipolar plate supplier, has been manufacturing bipolar plates for Cummins
FCHT with 500 cm2 active area. For the new plates with larger active area, DANA has confirmed
that the size can fit into existing machinery, but the forming pressure might have to be reduced
due to increased area. The reduction in the forming pressure can potentially cause problems
such as incomplete forming, non-uniform thickness, or poor mechanical strengths. To further
understand the impact of this potential problem, DANA undertook a series of experiments to
find the relation between production parameters of compression force and bipolar plate-
thickness variation.

Individual production plates were produced with two different methods and measured for
thickness at several locations on the surface of the plate. Method A represents the current
process parameters for production parts except the forming pressure. Method B was proposed
as an alternative method to achieve even better thickness uniformity. The thickness variation
was determined by calculating the maximum range of the thickness measurements and
normalizing it to the thickness variation target. The compression force was normalized to the
maximum compression pressure achievable. The resultant measurements are shown in Figure
1, below. This experiment was replicated twice, with the second experiment showing similar
results. As can be seen in Figure 1, for both production methods, the thickness variation
decreases with increase of compression force. However, with Method A at a forming pressure
as low as 20% of benchmark, the thickness variation is still within target. With Method B, the
thickness variation was further reduced by approximately half.

Subtask 1.213: Stack Manifold and Hardware Design (M5-M11)
A design concept for the stack hardware architecture was completed. It includes endplates,
insulators, busbars, thread rods and their insulators, seals/gaskets hardware (screws, nuts,
spring discs, etc), and manufacturing assembly fixtures/jigs (alignment bars, manifolds,
mounting plates). The end plates and thread rods are used for fastening bipolar plates and
MEA assemblies, maintaining contact pressure, and providing mechanical structure support
for the fuel cell assembly. Insulators mainly provide electrical isolation between busbars and
endplates. Alignment bars keep all bipolar plate assemblies straight during assembly and
subsequent compression. Manifolds provide an interface for fluids circulating between pipes
and stack ports and are used for precheck and post check diagnostic tests. The following bill
of materials (BOM, table 2) summarizes stack hardware parts. Two design options are
proposed for the wet components: a traditional design with separate end plate assembly and
bus bar separated by an insulator, and a non-traditional end plate assembly also fulfilling
busbar functions (table 1). Both end plates are similarly designed for each design option.

Finite element analysis (FEA) was used to assess the maximum deflection of the end plate
assembly subject to a compression load and support the determination of the component
thickness. The analysis was initially carried out with a geometry that included large openings
for fluid connections and two of the four corners being cut-off that mirrors the bipolar plate
design (lower material usage preferred, possible manufacturing and assembly benefits). The
maximum deflection on the side of the end plate assembly with corners still present was >90
microns. In comparison, on the side of the end plate assembly without corners, the maximum
deflection was >115 microns. These deflections were considered too large and were minimized
by keeping the corners of the end plate assembly and by decreasing the size of the fluid
connection openings to avoid structure weakening. For that revised design, the FEA analysis
revealed smaller maximum deflections of 70 and 30 microns for a revised end plate assembly
corner design and smaller fluid connection openings combined with the revised end plate
assembly corner design, respectively. FEA analysis of the non-traditional end plate assembly
design has not yet been completed.

Subtask 2.2: 1kW Short Stack Performance Test (M17-M18)

A test plan was established for the 10 kW short stack that focuses on performance
(polarization curve) obtained by varying all operating parameters. Specifically, three
incomplete factorial designs were adopted (figure 2). The test plan was slightly modified and
completed with a single cell of 120 cm2 active area while waiting for the completion of the 10
kW assembly. Adaptations considered test station and cell design (liquid cooled cell)
characteristics: the inlet temperature was controlled and inlet temperatures were set by
reducing the planned value by 10 or 15 (only for 110 °C) °C. A temperature of 100 °C at the
outlet was achieved at 2 A/cm2 with a 95 °C inlet temperature. This data set provided an early
indication of the results expected with the 10 kW stack.

A stack model that will serve as the building block for a system model was developed using
AVL’s commercial software to analyze single cells and eventually 10 kW stack data. The
schematic of the model topology is shown in figure 5. It consists of a stack component which is
fed air and hydrogen through mass flow elements (Mass Flow Boundary: MFB) to the cathode
and anode side respectively. The back pressure for both anode and cathode is applied through
State Boundary (SB) elements. The stack is also allowed to exchange heat with the coolant flow
elements represented by the liquid flow pipe components in the model. The model has the
flexibility to add the desired number of cells in the stack and balance of plant components such
as an air compressor and humidifier. In addition, the model can simulate transient conditions
that are more representative of duty cycle operation.

                                               Page 76 of 102

 Figure 5. AVL model topology.

Representative physical phenomena included in the model are:

 • Oxygen reduction and hydrogen oxidation kinetics at the cathode and anode catalyst
 layers respectively
 • Multicomponent gas phase transport in the cathode flow channels, gas diffusion layer,
 and catalyst layer
 • Multicomponent gas phase transport in the anode flow channels, gas diffusion layer,
 and catalyst layer
 • Proton transport through membrane and membrane hydration/dehydration rates
 corresponding to local humidity in the catalyst layer
 • Conductive heat transfer through the membrane-gas diffusion layer-bipolar plate layers
 and convective heat transfer to coolant fluid

The model consists of many tunable parameters as well as material property inputs that need
to be estimated from component level characterization data. This step is especially important
for high operation temperatures targeted in this project considering relatively little information
is available. For example, membrane protonic conductivity as a function of temperature and
relative humidity was used as an input based on available characterization data. Tunable
parameters related to oxygen reduction kinetics were estimated by calibrating the model
response to single cell experimental data. The outcome of the model calibration process is
shown in figure 6. It is noted that the low current density feature described above is not
currently captured by the reduced order model, which will require additional tuning.
Additionally, at high current densities the lower limiting current for 50 and 60 °C cases are also

likely related to water management, which is consistent with the low current density feature.
Tunable parameters related to water management will be reexamined to improve model

Subtask 2.1: 1kW Short Stack Build and Non-Functional Test (M17)

During the short stack assembly, it was noted that the compression was uneven over both
inactive and active areas using a pressure sensitive film. This observation was ascribed to end
plates, which was verified by assembling bipolar plates, GDLs, and a polyethylene naphthalate
sheet to mimic the CCM in a MEA press, which resulted in an acceptably uniform compression.
A straight edge and a feeler gauge were used to assess the flatness of the end plates, which
showed deviations from ~0.1 to 0.6 mm. These measurements are significant considering that
finite element analysis showed end plate deflections during compression of ~0.07 to 0.14 mm.

Finite element analyses are ongoing to verify earlier end plate deflection estimates.
Additionally, shims and hard stop blocks are currently being used to improve compression
uniformity. These approaches require multiple iterations. Initial results indicated improvements
in compression evenness. Other approaches that will be considered include reinforcement bars
and thicker end plates.

 Significant accomplishments & Conclusion
The significant accomplishment of this project was to demonstrate that subscale fuel cell stack
can be run at 110°C without compromising durability by demonstrating degradation rate of <10
mV/1000hr at 0.4A/cm2. Also, the project demonstrated high efficiency 0.75V at 0.3A/cm2 and
cell peak power of 0.8W/cm2. Additionally, this study also highlights that there are material
choices available for catalyst to bring performance of the stack to DOE end of life target of 2.5
kW/gPGM power (1.07 A/cm2 current density) at 0.7 V after 25,000 hour-equivalent accelerated
durability test, even though in this study the metric was achieved only for beginning of life. This
work also showcases that precious metal loading on the catalyst layer can be brought down to
<0.3mg without impact to performance or durability even at higher temperatures.

This study also explored levers in manifold design and channel geometry to achieve optimum
distribution of reactant gases and water vapor handling along with coolant routing for even heat
distribution, considering manufacturability of these designs. The study helped
verification/validation along with calibration of analytical models to get closer to experimental
data on the performance prediction.

It is to be noted that original plan to test a 100kW stack was not accomplished due to testing
equipment availability during the timeframe and design and material of the bipolar plate was
changed from the initial agreed design, towards end of the project due to part availability and
market shift. This helped to build a short stack with 20cells with all the planned MEA properties
intact but change affected the bipolar plate material and design.

Additionally, it has also been noted that cost of the stack can be brought down by choice of
manufacturing methods, reduction of precious metal loading in catalyst and choosing the right
active area size for the stack to gain advantage of volume rather than targeting specific market.

 Path forward
It would be ideal to continue this work by running stack for >1000hrs to validate the
degradation rate of the stack with final MEA. It has been observed from field trials that it takes
few 100hrs to really get degradation rates steady.
Getting the MEA into production ready state is big task to ensure consistency and quality. This
means manufacturing technology and methods need to be studied to bring newer catalyst
materials and coating methods into mainstream manufacturing faster and ability to adapt to
market demands. Along with seal improvement, another critical area for maximizing the
performance of MEA is cell compression. We utilized shims and hard stops to attain an even
compression; exploring more methods will be critical to consistent performance and
degradation.
Additionally, identifying seal material that is compatible with manufacturing methods and
bipolar plate design is important. One of challenges was to find suppliers that had the seal
material and dispensing technology that could withstand 100degC temperatures and highly
acidic environment inside the stack.
It is also important to continue to evaluate ways to reduce the cost of the stack while improving
the durability. For this reason, system design plays a critical role as stack is only as good as the
inputs. It is important to size the system correctly and develop balance of plant components as
these components adds up to 50% of the cost of the system.
