Introduction

The registration of clinical data and the associated use of healthcare resources are the subject of ongoing political debate, often focused on reducing documentation time and introducing minimum standards.1 At the same time, data-driven technologies such as electronic health records (EHRs) are central to ambitions for a data-informed healthcare system.2 These technologies typically require health professionals to align their documentation practices with administrative objectives, prioritising data accuracy and completeness to meet the informational needs of managers, policymakers and researchers.3,5 This creates tension between clinical relevance and administrative compliance, where the latter frequently takes precedence.6

In healthcare systems, high-quality data are considered to be accurate, complete, reliable, timely and accessible across disciplines. These qualities are essential for assessing treatment outcomes, improving clinical processes, supporting evidence-based practice and building a more efficient and cohesive healthcare system.7 8 By contrast, poor data quality can result in medication errors, complications, inadequate follow-up care and prolonged hospitalisation.9 To optimise data quality in healthcare, a comprehensive overview of existing knowledge is needed to bridge the gap between technical data strategies and the complex realities of clinical practice. Understanding how individual, organisational, technological and contextual factors shape data generation and use is essential for advancing meaningful, high-quality data across healthcare systems.

Although data-informed healthcare strategies emphasise technological and structural solutions,10 11 they often overlook how cultural norms, hierarchies and everyday work routines shape data generation and use.12 This misalignment between technical systems and clinical realities may undermine even the most sophisticated initiatives. Health professionals play a key role in ensuring data quality; yet the complexities of clinical data practices remain poorly understood. To advance healthcare, it is essential to identify the real-world barriers and enablers of high-quality data and to shift the focus from generating more data to ensuring better, contextually meaningful data.

A preliminary search of Open Science, PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar, the Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews and Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Evidence Synthesis did not present current or underway systematic reviews or scoping reviews regarding documentation practice. Previous reviews have focused on specific documentation domains, such as perioperative nursing13 and nutritional care in primary healthcare14 and rehabilitation,15 but none have systematically examined promoting and hindering factors for data quality in EHRs across hospital-based health professions.

This scoping review aims to map existing knowledge and highlight knowledge gaps related to promoting and hindering factors for data quality in EHRs among health professionals, as described in peer-reviewed publications concerned with monodisciplinary and interdisciplinary health professional data practices in hospital settings.

Review question

The review question is developed in accordance with the JBI established Population, Concept and Context framework for scoping reviews.16

What factors at the individual, organisational, technological and contextual levels promote and hinder data quality in electronic health records among health professionals in hospital settings?

Methods and analysis

Study design and protocol registration

The scoping review follows the JBI methodology17 and will be presented in accordance with the checklist for Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analysis for scoping reviews (PRISMA-ScR).18 The protocol is registered in the Open Science Framework. The research team, specialised in nursing and science and technology studies, will identify, collect and map relevant sources of evidence.

The review is planned to start in April 2026 and is expected to be completed by September 2026.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Population

This review will include, but not be limited to, health professionals as defined by the International Standard Classification of Occupations (ISCO),19 encompassing physicians, nurses, midwives, physiotherapists, occupational therapists, dieticians and nutritionists. In the Danish hospital context, medical secretaries, classified under Administrative and Specialized Secretaries in ISCO, play a key role in documentation by transcribing physicians’ dictations and independently performing registration and record-keeping tasks that support both clinical and administrative processes. Medical scribes, who assist in real-time clinical documentation, are also included within the review’s scope.

Concept

In this review, data quality is defined according to the six core dimensions of the Digital Health Data Quality Dimension and Outcome (DQ-DO) framework by Syed et al20: accuracy, completeness, consistency, contextual validity, currency and accessibility. These dimensions provide a comprehensive lens for assessing the usability and reliability of clinical data. They will guide the mapping of factors promoting or hindering data quality.

Beyond these dimensions, data quality is also shaped by administrative priorities and governance structures.21 22 Documentation is often required to serve organisational, managerial and secondary purposes such as standardisation, accountability and research. While such aims can improve comparability and reuse of data, they may also reduce clinical relevance and add to the burden of documentation.6 23 Thus, in this review, data quality is understood as a concept situated at the intersection of clinical usefulness and administrative priorities, where both technical characteristics and organisational conditions determine the extent to which health data can support safe, effective and meaningful care.

Context

To include studies from different healthcare systems with comparable technological and organisational characteristics, this review will consider hospital environments where EHRs are implemented and routinely used by health professionals. The focus is on hospital contexts with established digital infrastructures supporting clinical documentation and data use. Peer-reviewed studies published from the year 2000 onward will be included to ensure relevance to contemporary EHR systems

Search strategy

In accordance with JBI methodology,16 the inclusion of grey literature was considered. However, due to the lack of standardised indexing or accessibility of grey literature, only peer-reviewed sources will be included. This decision aims to ensure methodological consistency and transparency.

A three-step strategy will be developed in collaboration with experienced librarians from academia and hospital settings. First, an initial limited search of Medline (PubMed), Scopus, Web of Science, Google Scholar and CINAHL (EBSCO) will be undertaken to identify materials on the topic. An example of the preliminary search strategy is provided in online supplemental appendix 1. Second, the text words contained in the titles and abstracts of relevant literature, and the index terms used to describe the literature will be used to develop a full search strategy. The search strategy, including all identified keywords and index terms, will be adapted for each included database source. Third, the reference list of all included sources of evidence will be screened for additional studies.

The searches will be limited to publications from 2000 onwards. Materials published in English and Scandinavian languages will be considered in accordance with the research team’s language competencies.

Study/source of evidence selection

All citations identified through the search will be imported into Covidence (Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) for study management. Duplicates will be automatically identified and removed within the software.

The selection process will be conducted by two independent reviewers in two stages: first, title and abstract screening against the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Second, full-text review of all potentially relevant sources to confirm eligibility.

Any disagreements between the reviewers at any stage will be resolved through discussion or, if necessary, consultation with a third reviewer or the wider author team. Reasons for exclusion at the full-text stage will be recorded. The entire selection process will be documented in Covidence, which will also generate the PRISMA-ScR flow diagram for reporting the number of records identified, screened and included in the final review.

Data extraction

Two independent reviewers will extract and chart data using the JBI Extraction Form,16 including study characteristics, data quality dimensions and identified promoting or hindering factors. Charted data will be mapped across dimensions, professions and contexts and presented in tables and graphical formats. A narrative summary will contextualise patterns, relationships and knowledge gaps.

A draft data extraction form is provided (see online supplemental appendix 2). The extraction tool will be pilot tested by the reviewers on a small number of included sources of evidence to refine the data extraction process. Disagreements between reviewers will be resolved through discussion or, if necessary, with input from a third reviewer. Where appropriate, authors of included papers may be contacted to clarify or provide missing data.

Consistent with established methodological guidance for scoping reviews,16 a formal critical appraisal of included studies will not be conducted. The aim of this scoping review is to map the extent, range and nature of the available evidence on factors influencing data quality in EHRs, rather than to assess the quality or effectiveness of individual studies. Therefore, critical appraisal is not considered necessary for addressing the review objective.

Data analysis and presentation

The extracted data will be charted and presented in tabular form to provide an overview of the evidence in relation to the review objective and research question. Tables will summarise study characteristics (eg, author, year, country, setting, study design, professional group) and key findings related to data quality in EHRs.

The data will be mapped to illustrate how promoting and hindering factors are distributed across health professional groups and hospital contexts, and how these factors relate to the six dimensions of data quality. Where appropriate, graphical representations (eg, frequency charts, concept maps or evidence maps) will be used to visualise how factors are distributed across these dimensions and to highlight patterns, relationships and knowledge gaps.

A narrative summary will accompany graphical representations and explain how the findings relate to the review objective and research question, with particular emphasis on identifying recurring themes, variations across contexts and areas requiring further research.
