Peri-procedural recognition of a calcified cerebral embolus following mechanical thrombectomy: A case report
Authors: Monami Dai, Takeshi Ogura, Shigehiro Ohmori
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Calcified cerebral embolism (CCE), an uncommon but increasingly recognized cause of ischemic stroke, is often associated with technical challenges during mechanical thrombectomy (MT). The case reported here is of an octogenarian woman who presented with acute aphasia and scattered bilateral microinfarcts without large vessel occlusion, followed by recurrent aphasia 1 week later due to acute left internal carotid artery occlusion requiring MT. Despite multiple device passes, only partial reperfusion (modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction 2a) was achieved, and post-procedural computed tomography revealed a newly appeared, well-circumscribed, hyperdense lesion consistent with a calcified cerebral embolus that had not been detected on prior imaging. This case highlights the importance of recognizing CCE as a potential cause of incomplete reperfusion and procedural difficulty during MT, as well as the diagnostic value of thin-section computed tomography for detecting calcified emboli. Increased awareness of CCE may facilitate a more accurate diagnosis, guide procedural strategies, and improve etiological evaluation in embolic stroke.

Introduction

Calcified cerebral embolism (CCE) is an uncommon, but likely underdiagnosed, cause of acute ischemic stroke. Although traditionally considered rare, recent retrospective analyses have reported CCE in up to 2%-3% of acute stroke computed tomography (CT) scans, suggesting that its true prevalence is underestimated [1]. The clinical and radiological spectrum of CCE is broad, ranging from transient ischemic events to large territorial infarctions, and its identification frequently relies on careful interpretation of non-contrast CT images [2,3].

The widespread use of mechanical thrombectomy (MT) has brought increasing attention to CCE because calcified materials pose distinct mechanical challenges [4,5]. Calcified emboli are rigid, poorly deformable, and prone to fragmentation, which limits device engagement and contributes to lower reperfusion rates than non-calcified thrombi [6]. Several multicenter, cohort studies have demonstrated that MT in CCE is associated with modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction (mTICI) ≥ 2b in only 50%-60% of cases and functional independence in approximately one-quarter of patients [4,5]. These observations emphasize the clinical importance of accurate recognition and procedural planning.

Radiologically, thin-section CT is the most reliable modality for identifying calcified emboli, which are typically observed as small, round, and high-attenuation foci [7]. Conventional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) sequences may fail to depict calcified components, reinforcing the importance of CT as the primary method for early detection [3]. Distinguishing CCE from contrast staining or hemorrhagic transformation is essential because the therapeutic considerations differ substantially.

Here, a case is reported in which a calcified cerebral embolus became newly apparent immediately after MT. The absence of calcification on pre-procedural imaging raises the possibility of peri-procedural embolic fragmentation or retention of material during thrombectomy. This case highlights the technical limitations of the current endovascular devices for treating calcified emboli and underscores the value of thin-section CT for peri-procedural assessment. In addition, the identification of aortic and valvular calcifications provides insight into potential embolic sources relevant to secondary prevention.

The aim of this report is to contribute to the growing understanding of CCE by describing its peri-procedural recognition and discussing diagnostic and technical considerations in the context of the existing literature.

Case presentation

An octogenarian woman with hypertension and arrhythmia, who was previously independent in activities of daily living, presented with aphasia of unknown onset. She had a prior history of tachyarrhythmia noted at another hospital; however, no antithrombotic therapy had been prescribed. Upon admission, her Glasgow Coma Scale score was E3V4M4. Brain MRI revealed scattered bilateral microinfarcts without large vessel occlusion (Fig. 1). Electroencephalography revealed epileptic discharges, and the patient was diagnosed with symptomatic epilepsy secondary to a cerebral infarction. Dual-antiplatelet therapy was initiated after clinical improvement. At this stage, differential diagnoses for the newly identified, hyperdense lesion included intracranial hemorrhage, contrast staining, and non-calcified thrombus. Intracranial hemorrhage was considered less likely because the lesion was well-circumscribed and showed no surrounding edema or mass effect. Contrast staining was also unlikely given the distinct, sharply-demarcated hyperdensity and its persistence on follow-up imaging. In addition, the high attenuation on non-contrast CT was more consistent with calcific material than with soft thrombus. Based on these imaging characteristics, the lesion was interpreted as a calcified cerebral embolus. Following the procedure, the patient was managed conservatively with antiplatelet therapy continuation. Given the presence of calcifications in the aortic valve and ascending aorta, the embolic source was considered to be of a calcific origin, and medical management was continued with a focus on secondary stroke prevention. Following treatment, the patient’s neurological deficits persisted, with severe aphasia and hemiparesis. At discharge, the patient was wheelchair-dependent, and the modified Rankin Scale score was 4. No further neurological deterioration or recurrent ischemic events were observed during the hospitalization.

Discussion

Recognition and imaging considerations

CCE is uncommon, yet likely to be underrecognized; in a large retrospective series, it accounted for up to 2.7% of acute stroke CT scans [1]. Recognition is frequently delayed because acute-phase findings are subtle and clinical presentations are heterogeneous [2]. On non-contrast CT, CCE typically appears as small, well-circumscribed, hyperattenuating foci; thin-section reconstructions (≤1-2 mm) and region-of-interest measurements can increase diagnostic confidence [7]. Although MRI contributes to the etiological workup, conventional T1/T2 sequences may miss calcific foci, supporting the primacy of CT for early CCE evaluation [3] Particular attention should be paid to differentiating the calcified emboli from contrast staining or hemorrhagic transformation because their therapeutic implications differ greatly.

Peri-procedural recognition and case-specific relevance

The novelty of this case lies in the peri-procedural recognition of a calcified embolus that first appeared immediately after an MT. Baseline CT showed no discrete calcification, and a hyperattenuating focus emerged only post-MT. Although this could theoretically reflect a newly-dislodged calcified fragment during thrombectomy, the absence of pre-procedural CT limits definitive temporal attribution. It is also plausible that calcified material was pre-existing but unrecognized because of the lack of thin-section pre-procedural imaging. Nevertheless, the post-MT appearance supports the interpretation that a calcified embolus was retained or fragmented during retrieval attempts, which aligns with the known mechanical resistance of such lesions [6].

Endovascular management for calcified emboli

In dedicated CCE cohorts, MT achieved mTICI ≥2b in ∼50%-60% and 90-d modified Rankin scale 0-2 in ∼25%-30%, inferior to expectations for non-calcified thrombi [4,5]. This disparity aligns with the mechanical rigidity and fragmentation tendency of calcified thrombi, which limit device engagement and effective retrieval. Aspiration-only techniques often prove inadequate, whereas a stent retriever with adjunctive aspiration or stent-mediated wedging may enhance engagement but carries a risk of vessel injury [8,9]. In this case, repeated retrieval attempts with partial reperfusion followed by new calcified foci on CT suggested incomplete removal and possible distal migration, underscoring the technical limitations of current devices against highly mineralized substrates. Lesion size and density of calcified cerebral emboli are correlated to vascular location, suggesting heterogeneity in embolus characteristics [10]. Lower recanalization rates and greater procedural difficulties during thrombectomy exist for such emboli [11]. A recent in vitro study has reinforced this interpretation, showing that a combination of stent retriever, distal aspiration, and proximal flow arrest achieves the most effective retrieval of calcified analogs, highlighting the importance of multimodal techniques for treating rigid emboli [12].

Etiologic evaluation and embolic source

Simultaneous or recurrent involvement of multiple vascular territories raises the suspicion of a proximal embolic source, particularly in calcified valvular or aortic lesions [13,14]. Transthoracic echocardiography and chest CT demonstrated calcification of the aortic valve and ascending aorta, which could represent an embolic source. As transthoracic imaging may under-detect mobile or ulcerated aortic plaques, comprehensive aortic assessment (eg, transesophageal echocardiography or contrast-enhanced CT) is warranted when CCE is suspected. The recognition of a systemic calcific embolic source has direct implications for secondary prevention and risk stratification.

Clinical implications and management strategies

CCE carries a recurrence risk of up to 41% and may cause persistent steno-occlusion if the calcified material remains unretrieved [1,15]. The current case exemplifies the therapeutic challenges of CCE—limited device efficacy, procedural difficulty, and the risk of peri-procedural hemorrhage. Three practical lessons emerged:1.Pre-procedural high-resolution CT should be considered for suspected embolic stroke to identify potential calcified components.2.During MT, early recognition of device resistance or poor engagement should prompt strategy modification, favoring stent-assisted or reconstructive approaches over repeated traction attempts.3.When calcified debris is suspected after the procedure, thin-section CT with Hounsfield unit quantification can confirm retention and guide further management.

Limitations

This report was limited by the absence of pre-procedural, thin-section CT scans and lack of retrieved embolic specimens for histological confirmation. However, this highlights the need for procedural awareness of calcified emboli as a potential cause of incomplete reperfusion and post-thrombectomy complications. Future studies should aim to characterize imaging predictors, refine the device design, and develop pre-operative risk stratification tools for calcified embolic stroke.

Conclusion

This case reports the peri-procedural appearance of a calcified cerebral embolus following mechanical thrombectomy, underscoring the diagnostic value of thin-section CT in detecting calcified material that may influence procedural outcomes. Recognition of CCE is clinically relevant because calcified emboli may contribute to incomplete reperfusion and procedural difficulty. Further accumulation of cases may help refine imaging strategies and device selection for the management of calcified embolic stroke. Future studies should focus on developing thrombectomy devices specifically optimized for rigid or highly mineralized emboli and establishing standardized imaging criteria to better predict technical difficulty and clinical prognosis.
