Effect of spraying distance and arc current on the corrosion behavior of arc-sprayed Zn–15Al coatings for rolling stock components
Authors: D. Yutthakamthon, C. Sukhonkhet, P. Wangjina, B. Moonsri, H. Koiprasert, P. Sheppard, W. Pongsaksawad, C. Banjongprasert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Arc sprayed Zn15Al coatings with different spraying parameters: arc current and spraying distance were investigated. The corrosion behavior of the coatings was studied using an electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), cyclic corrosion testing (CCT-4), and coastal railway exposure test. Corroded surfaces were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The corrosion product formed on the coating was analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) and scanning electron microscopy. From the potentiodynamic polarization test, the corrosion rate of the coating, using arc current of 100 A and a spraying distance of 100 mm, was 0.012 mm/year. The corrosion rate increased with increasing arc current and spraying distance. The corrosion mechanism of Zn15Al coating started with selective dissolution of Zn-rich phase leaving behind the passivated Al-rich phase and followed by stable corrosion product formation. From the surfaces analysis after the corrosion test, Simonkolleite (Zn5(OH)8Cl2•H2O) was found on the coating surfaces. Similarly, the samples exposed on the coastal railway line revealed Zn-rich phase dissolution and Al-rich phase passivation, but without protective corrosion product formations. Based on the corrosion rates, the results from the coastal railway exposure agreed well with electrochemical impedance measurement and cyclic corrosion test. Suggested spraying parameters for onsite repair was 100 A arc current and 100 mm spraying distance to achieve the optimal corrosion resistance of Zn15Al coating.

Introduction

The railway industry is important for transportation and plays a significant role in national economy1,2. Steel and cast iron have attributed up to 2/3 of the total material consumptions in the railway industry3. It is widely known that corrosion leads to a premature failure of steel railway components when exposed to environments. Additionally, the protective coatings for steel in railway structures exposed to atmospheric environments still require essential innovation to prolong the service life and reduce the repair cost3–6. Many researchers have been searching for maintenance corrosion protection methods to improve mechanical and corrosion resistant properties7,8. Hot-dip galvanizing process has been applied in several industries as a surface treatment technique for the cathodic protection of steel. However, it is difficult for onsite applications in the railway maintenance aspect. Other coating processes for sacrificial coating is focused on thermal spray techniques because it is possible to facilitate onsite coating9.

Thermal spray coating is a type of coating technique which deposits heated or molten materials on the surface of components for corrosion protection in various applications8,10,11. The raw materials in this process can be powders or wires, which are applicable for a variety of materials and compositions12,13. Many studies have been carried out by thermal spray coating for corrosion protection. Featured sub-categories of thermal spray coating include plasma spray, combustion flame spray, and arc spray processes. Flame temperature of plasma spray can be as high as 16,000 °C, whereas those of arc spray is about 5000–6000 °C and those of flame spray is the lowest in the range of 3000 °C. The higher the flame temperature, the greater adhesion strength of coating is produced14.

Coating material selection is crucial for corrosion protection of steel. Zinc (Zn) and aluminum (Al) alloys were normally employed in cathodic protection as sacrificial anodes to prevent corrosion of steel structures. Because the standard half-cell reduction potentials of Zn, Al, and Zn-Al alloys are lower than that of steel and can be oxidized more easily, they become anode when galvanically connected to steel.

Thermal sprayed Zn-Al coating can be deposited by plasma spray, flame spray, or arc spray processes. Atmospheric plasma spray (APS) Zn coating on steel showed excellent anti-corrosion properties after exposure to NaCl solution for 3200 h 15. Long term test had been studied to evaluate the anti-corrosion property of Zn-Al coating by Katayama et al.16. Thermal spray coating of Zn, Al, and Zn30Al by gas wire flame spraying had been exposed in Choshi, Chiba for 33 years. The result of pure Zn and pure Al coating with 100 μm thick were as protective as 50-micron Zn30Al alloy coatings. The best protective property was observed on Zn30Al coatings which depended on the thicker layer of corrosion products as a result of selective corrosion reaction of Zn-rich phase16. Zn-Al alloy also has higher mechanical properties and better corrosion resistance than those of pure Zn. Due to better adhesion of coating, arc spraying technique is expected to provide more durable Zn-Al coating than flame spray technique. Several recent studies on Zn-Al coating are geared towards arc spray process17–24. Arc thermal spraying Zn-Al coating provided a dense lamellar microstructure and relatively low porosity and could be an adequate alternative for large steel structures17,18. Gulec et al.24 studied the effects of Al addition in Zn coatings by twin wire electric arc (TWEA) spraying technique exposed to 2000-h salt-spray test. The results showed that Zn15Al sprayed at 200 A revealed the best corrosion resistance. In the Zn-Al coating by arc spray process, Zn provides cathodic protection while Al improves erosion resistance24–26. The formation of metastable β-ZnAl in Zn-Al coating enhanced corrosion protection because the β-phase promotes the formation of stable corrosion product layer19. Microstructure and corrosion behavior of Zn-xAl coating by arc spraying were studied by Hu et al.20. The result indicated that Zn15Al coating could provide long-term protection from self-sealing mechanism within the corrosion product layer. Moreover, Zn15Al coating showed highest corrosion rate (Zn15Al > Zn50Al > Zn30Al) because Al-rich phase improves the compact microstructure of coating20,22,27. The corrosion products of arc sprayed Zn-Al coatings could block porosity and cracks of the coatings by the formation of simonkolleite (Zn5(OH)8Cl2·H2O) phase in the present of chloride21,26–29.

The properties of the coating such as thickness and hardness depend on arc spray processing parameters30. Corrosion behaviors of Zn-Al coating by arc spraying have been investigated in several aspects such as varying zinc: aluminum ratio, corrosion test duration, atomizing gas, arc current, etc.9,20,24,27,31. However, during onsite coating, another important parameter is the spraying distance in order to control consistent quality of coating. Though very important, the corrosion behavior of the coatings from different arc currents and spraying distance in the process has not been extensively studied. Therefore, this research was focused on the comparison of Zn-Al coatings produced by arc thermal spray at different spraying distances for 100 A and 200 A arc currents. The corrosion behavior was studied by potentiodynamic polarization and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy as well as 1000-h accelerated testing by cyclic corrosion test (CCT-4). Moreover, the coatings were exposed at underfloor area of an in-service railway coach on a coastal line in Thailand. The purpose of this work was to optimize spraying parameter for onsite repair job of underfloor railway steel structure.

Experimental method

Materials and methods

Zn-Al alloy coating was deposited on a low carbon steel substrate (SS400) by arc spraying. The Zn15Al alloy (85 wt.% Zn and 15 wt.% Al) was used as raw materials for feedstock wire with a diameter of 1.6 mm (Sulzer Metco-Smart Arc 9000). Before spraying, substrates were grit-blasted using compressed air with alumina (Al2O3 of 24 mesh size) to create profiles on the surface of substrates and improve mechanical interlocking. The surface roughness (Ra) of the substrate was 9-11 µm (Mitutoyo SJ-301). The arc spraying parameters were as follows: spray voltage 30 V, air pressure 4 bar, arc current 100 A and 200 A, spraying distance 100 mm, 150 mm, 200 mm, and 250 mm, as coded and listed in Table 1. Electrochemical studies

The coated samples were characterized using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and potentiodynamic polarization tests in a three-electrode system, where Zn15Al coating was the working electrode, platinum was the counter electrode and silver-silver chloride (Ag/AgCl) was the reference electrode. The area of the working electrode was 100 mm2. Electrochemical studies of the coating were carried out in 3.5 wt.% NaCl solution. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy studies were performed at open circuit potential (OCP) for 3600 s to stabilize the potential with a potentiostat. The impedance data was collected over the frequency ranging from 100 kHz to 0.01 Hz with an amplitude of 10 mV. Potentiodynamic polarization studies were performed at a scan rate of 1.0 mV/s from − 0.5 V to + 0.3 V vs. OCP on a AUTOLAB PGSTAT320N. Cyclic corrosion test

Cyclic corrosion testing was conducted in accordance with the Nissan Engineering Standard—Methods of Compound Corrosion Test (NES M 0158: CCT-4), an accelerated corrosion testing standard widely used for materials in the automotive industry. In this study, the corrosion protection performance of coatings for railway applications was investigated. Therefore, the CCT-4 test cycle, which is commonly used for evaluating coated materials in automotive applications, was selected.

The cyclic corrosion test was performed under CCT-4 conditions, as summarized in Table 3. The test consisted of alternating dry and wet conditions in a corrosive environment using a 5.0 wt.% NaCl solution. Each test cycle had a total duration of 24 h and 10 min. During the dry stage, the chamber temperature was maintained at 60 °C. In the wet stage, a humid atmosphere with a relative humidity of 95% at 60 °C was applied. Coastal railway exposure test

An exposure test was carried out on an in-service railway coach in Thailand. The tested railway line operates from Baan Laem station in Samut Sakhon province to Mae Klong station in Samut Songkhram province. Arc spray-coated samples were mounted under the stairs, which represented the underfloor condition as shown in Fig. 1 The specimens were facing groundward. Exposed samples included AS1010 in comparison with AS1020 in order to determine the spraying distance window in the actual onsite maintenance. Polyvinyl tape was wrapped around the edges and the back of the sample to confine the exposure area to 50 × 50 mm2. Samples were collected for analysis after 6-month and 12-month exposure. The appearance of the corroded surface was examined. Corrosion products were removed by a chemical agent according to ASTM G132. Cleaning periods for each cycle were 3 s and 5 s for 6-month and 12-month exposure times, respectively until corrosion products were completely removed33. Surface morphology was analyzed by a measuring microscope and a laser confocal microscope.Fig. 1Exposure test under the stairs of an in-service train for 12 months (a) Sample installation area on the train and (b) Samples facing groundward under the stairs.

Characterization of the corroded surfaces and corrosion products

The corroded surfaces and corrosion products of Zn15Al coatings were characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS), using JSM-IT300 and JSM-IT800 microscope operating at 1, 5, and 15 kV. The surface roughness after corrosion testing was measured by a laser confocal microscope. In addition, phases on the coating and corrosion products were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a SmartLab X-Ray Diffractometer, Rigaku using Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54060 Å), 2θ = 10 − 90 degrees with a step size of 0.01°. Furthermore, X’Pert High Score Plus was used to analyze phases with the JCPDS database.

Results and discussion

Electrochemical studies

Electrochemical properties of Zn15Al coatings were studied in 3.5 wt.% NaCl solution. The potentiodynamic polarization curves of the Zn15Al alloy coatings are shown in Fig. 2. The cathodic reaction was controlled by activation process, which shifted left when spraying distance increased. The anodic reaction exhibited two passivation regions. The first passivation of AS1010 was the narrowest and became wider as the spraying arc current and distance increased. The second passivation region occurred at similar potential values. Overall anodic polarization curve shifted right as the spraying arc current, and distance increased. Results of polarization curves defined corrosion potential (Ecorr) and corrosion current density (Icorr) of the Zn15Al coated steel. Corrosion potential indicates the equilibrium potential of a metal surface. Corrosion potential of sacrificial coating material is required to be more anodic than the materials being protected. Corrosion current density is related to the corrosion rate by Eq. (1). Next, the effect of arc current was considered. From polarization curve comparisons between arc spray coating at 100 A and 200 A, it was noticed that AS1010 coating had higher Ecorr and lower corrosion rate than AS2010 coating. The Ecorr values of AS1010 coating and AS2010 coating were − 1.26 and − 1.30 V vs Ag/AgCl and corrosion rates were 0.012 and 0.018 mm/year, respectively. Moreover, AS1020 coating also had higher Ecorr, but similar corrosion rate compared to AS2020 coating. Therefore, arc current evidently affects the Ecorr of Zn15Al coating. To determine the effect of arc current on corrosion rate, the results were compared to literature data17,34. At 150 mm spraying distance, corrosion rate of Zn15Al coating at 100 A (28 V) and 200 A (31 V) were 0.0688 mm/year17 and 0.1533 mm/year34, respectively. While 100 A (30 V) 150 mm coating in this research yielded corrosion rate of 0.015 mm/year. Based on reported data, our results indicate that higher arc current leads to an increase in corrosion rate at spraying distance between 100 and 150 mm.

The electrochemical impedance plots of different coatings tested in 3.5 wt.% NaCl are shown in Fig. 3. The Nyquist plot consisted of two capacitive loops (Fig. 3a and b). The first semi-circle loop appeared at high frequency range. This high frequency resistance is associated with the corrosion product or oxide layer on the coating. Meanwhile, a second incomplete semi-circle loop is related to the charge transfer resistance attributed by coating dissolution. The size of the high-frequency loop indicates the texture, porosity, and uniformity of corrosion product. The diameter of the low-frequency loop indicates better corrosion resistance when the diameter increases9,20. The Bode plots in Fig. 3c–f show the impedance and phase shift parameters. The impedance at high frequency is dominated by corrosion product resistance, while the low-frequency impedance characterizes the dissolution of Zn15Al coating. Two peaks of phase angle appeared at near 0.1 Hz, corresponding to selective dissolution of Zn, and 100 Hz, representing deposition of corrosion product9.Fig. 3EIS results (a–b) Nyquist (c–d) Bode modulus plot and (e–f) Bode phase plot of Zn15Al coating tested in 3.5 wt.% NaCl solution.

Corrosion resistance depends on several factors related to coating properties which are influenced by coating parameters, for example oxide film16, porosity and defects9, a Zn-rich fcc β-phase19, and bonding strength between splats35. Based on the prior work of this research, Kaewpradit et al.18 reported porosity in Zn15Al coating by arc sprayed. Increasing spraying distance resulted in a higher porosity fraction and reduces the hardness of coatings18. From the corrosion test, the increase in corrosion rate with increasing spraying distance could be caused by higher porosity. Accordingly, the AS1010 coating from this research is the optimal parameter that exhibits the lowest corrosion rate. Nevertheless, the spraying distance exceeded 200 mm at 200 A, the corrosion rate of coatings increases. Consequently, the Zn15Al coating exhibited the highest corrosion resistance at spraying distances below 200 mm under arc currents of 100 A and 200 A. This shows that higher arc current and spraying distance reduce the performance of corrosion protection9,19,34. The electrochemical parameters after fitting of EIS data show no significant difference and the trend of corrosion resistance of the coatings is related to the polarization result. Accordingly, coatings at a spaying distance of 100 mm exhibits high corrosion resistance.

The corrosion products of coatings after cyclic corrosion test were identified by x-ray diffraction (XRD). The XRD patterns are shown in Fig. 9. The spectrum of XRD peaks indicated the intensity of Zinc (Zn) Aluminum (Al), and simonkolleite (Zn5(OH)8C12•H2O). Corrosion behavior from coastal railway exposure test

Retrieved samples had exhaust emission dirt covering the surface. The dirt was carefully removed by acetone. The appearances after dirt cleaning are illustrated in Fig. 10 Thin scattered white rust was presented in some areas on both samples. No red rust was detected. The SEM images in Fig. 11 at 500 × magnification revealed a rough surface with preferential attack and cracks on the coating surface. The elemental distribution results from the EDS map indicated the main composition of oxygen, zinc, iron, and sulfur. Both spraying distances resulted in similar corrosion product contents. Oxygen was the major content in the zinc-rich area (a–b in Fig. 11), which indicated that the corrosion product consisted of zinc and oxygen. The short-term corrosion revealed low resistance corrosion product formation and high charge transfer resistance of Zn-dissolution without stable protective corrosion product layer. After 1000-h in cyclic corrosion testing chamber, corrosion product evolved from porous to dense morphology after around 500-h exposure. Laboratory test results confirmed the greatest corrosion resistance coating with low corrosion rate was obtained from 100 A current and 100 mm spraying distance, which was in agreement with the lower weight loss results from underfloor exposure test on a coastal railway line.

Conclusion

Electrochemical, accelerated corrosion, and coastal railway exposure studies were carried out. The main findings are concluded as follow:Arc current and spraying distance of arc thermal sprayed process affect the corrosion behavior of Zn15Al coating. Anti-corrosion ability was reduced at higher arc current and spraying distance.The electrochemical studies: electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and polarization tests showed that at spraying distance of 100–150 mm, a higher arc current resulted in a higher corrosion rate.Cyclic corrosion test revealed dense simonkolleite (Zn5(OH)8C12•H2O) corrosion product as exposure time increased. However, there was no protective corrosion product formed under the short-term electrochemical test and one-year exposure test on the underfloor area of railway coach in C3 environment.Optimal coating parameter for Zn15Al arc spray coating is 100 A arc current and 100 mm spraying distance based on laboratory and one-year field test results.
