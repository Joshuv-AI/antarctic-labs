Finetuning-Free Diffusion Model with Adaptive Constraint Guidance for Inorganic Crystal Structure Generation
Auguste de Lambilly, Vladimir Baturin, David Portehault, Guillaume Lambard, Nataliya Sokolovska, Florence d’Alché-Buc, Jean-Claude Crivello

Abstract
The discovery of inorganic crystal structures with targeted properties is a significant challenge in materials science. Generative models, especially state-of-the-art diffusion models, offer the promise of modeling complex data distributions and proposing novel, realistic samples. However, current generative AI models still struggle to produce diverse, original, and reliable structures of experimentally achievable materials suitable for high-stakes applications. In this work, we propose a generative machine learning framework based on diffusion models with adaptive constraint guidance, which enables the incorporation of user-defined physical and chemical constraints during the generation process. This approach is designed to be practical and interpretable for human experts, allowing transparent decision-making and expert-driven exploration. To ensure the robustness and validity of the generated candidates, we introduce a multi-step validation pipeline that combines graph neural network estimators trained to achieve DFT-level accuracy and convex hull analysis for assessing thermodynamic stability. Our approach has been tested and validated on several classical examples of inorganic families of compounds, as case studies. As a consequence, these preliminary results demonstrate our framework’s ability to generate thermodynamically plausible crystal structures that satisfy targeted geometric constraints across diverse inorganic chemical systems.

Introduction

Identifying and employing new functional materials has become critical to address pressing environmental (abundance, sustainability) and geopolitical challenges
5
.
The discovery of new inorganic solids relies on both empirical and computational approaches.
Empirical methods, combining chemical design rules with trial-and-error experiments, remain time- and resource-intensive.
In contrast, computational methods—particularly first-principles approaches such as Density Functional Theory (DFT)—have enabled a deeper understanding of matter and, in some cases, prediction of atomic-scale ordering, albeit at high computational cost
4
.

Over the past decade, data-driven science and machine learning (ML) have emerged as powerful paradigms for accelerating materials discovery, notably by guiding experiments
1
;
44
;
15
;
6
.
Generative models, including Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)
29
;
18
and more recently diffusion models
13
;
7
, have enabled the direct generation of novel structures rather than the screening of large candidate spaces
39
.
In parallel, Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) incorporate physical laws into the learning process, ensuring consistency with fundamental principles
31
.
Meanwhile, Graph Neural Networks (GNNs) and Machine Learning Interatomic Potentials (MLIP) have become standard tools for efficient and accurate property prediction, leveraging the relational structure of crystalline materials without requiring expensive DFT calculations
32
.

Despite these promising opportunities, a critical gap has emerged:
data alone cannot replace domain expertise and critical thinking in materials science.
This became evident with large-scale models developed by major technology companies
48
;
45
announcing impressive claims, such as Google DeepMind’s GNoME predicting 2.2 million new crystal structures
26
.
Subsequent critical analyses revealed fundamental flaws: many predicted structures were merely combinatorial constructs of already known compounds, without proper experimental verification
8
.
Subsequent analyses also raised concerns about the claims of 41 new inorganic compounds reported by the autonomous laboratory for the accelerated synthesis of novel materials (A-Lab), as several structures were found to be mischaracterized or already known in the literature
40
;
20
.
These issues highlight that computational predictions, without chemical intuition and physical validation, can lead to physically implausible or trivial ”discoveries”.

The purpose of this paper is to integrate classical diffusion models with critical human guidance grounded in chemistry,
building on our prior work on diffusion-based models
39
and on the state-of-the-art generative model MatterGen
52
.
By introducing an additional module of chemical constraints (such as minimum interatomic distances or local chemical environments), we allow chemists, leveraging their physical intuition, to generate novel, functional, crystallographic structures that (i) satisfy a pre-defined set of constraints and (ii) are (meta)stable, meaning their energy lies below a pre-defined threshold above the convex hull.
Rather than generating millions of unverified candidates, our approach emphasizes quality over quantity, ensuring that the generated structures respect fundamental chemical principles and domain-specific knowledge from the outset.
We focus on constraints whose satisfiability can be estimated through differentiable functions, such as neural networks.
Adding new constraints should then be straightforward and should not require additional fine-tuning.

To this end, our approach relies on a foundation model: an unconstrained generative model for inorganic materials that has been pre-trained to generate stable and diverse crystal structures across the periodic table.
Here, we employ MatterGen
52
, a robust diffusion-based model for inorganic materials.
MatterGen has been pre-trained to generate stable structures by focusing on compounds within 0.10 eV/atom of the convex hull, ensuring diversity in crystal structures and supporting flexible property conditioning.
While MatterGen currently handles unit cells with up to 20 atoms only, this limitation is outweighed in our setting by its maturity, optimization, and demonstrated performance, making it a strong backbone for our approach.

At sampling time, we introduce a training-free guidance mechanism
49
, compatible with any off-the-shelf guidance function based on the Universal Guidance for Diffusion Models
3
.
This mechanism enforces user-defined physical and chemical constraints, as detailed in methodology Section 2.
Building on this foundation, Section 3 of this paper presents our finetuning-free generative framework for materials, demonstrated through case studies of inorganic crystal structures generated under user-defined geometrical constraints such as interatomic distances and coordination numbers.
These constraints are expressed and evaluated through the analysis of their statistical distributions and energy profiles, leveraging GNN-MLIPs such as GRACE
23
as high-performing energy estimators.

2
Finetuning-free guidance in diffusion models

2.1
Context of the score-based methods

The large family of generative models either exploits an iterative denoising process (as in diffusion models), a latent-variable formulation trained via variational inference (as in variational autoencoders), or an explicit likelihood-based construction, such as normalizing flows and autoregressive models.
In this work, we use a diffusion model that demonstrated impressive performance in generation of crystal cells, based on the MatterGen model
52
.
The unit cell is defined by a triplet
(X,A,L)
, where
X
denotes the fractional atomic coordinates,
A
the atomic species, and
L
the lattice basis (see SI
A
for more details).
We describe below the diffusion process used in the model.
If not explicitly stated otherwise, we use the notation
z_{t}
for the sample
z
drawn from distribution at time
t
, meaning that it is a general case.

Figure 1:
Diffusion model for material generation based on two processes, the training and the sampling. The green arrow represents the
\theta
parameters estimated in the training. The red circle pinpoints where the guidance is applied, with the condition
C
.

Using the representation of a crystal structure described in SI
A
, a diffusion model for compound generation is an algorithm that is trained to recover the observations in the backward process by denoising samples corrupted by the forward procedure.
The forward process
z_{t}|z_{t-1}\sim\mathcal{N}(a_{t}z_{t-1},b_{t}^{2}{\bf I})
is denoted
q
. We will only consider normal distribution here, but other forward process are also used.
The scalar
a_{t}
scales the mean of the transition
z_{t}|z_{t-1}
, while
b_{t}
controls the noise variance, jointly governing the gradual noising process in the forward diffusion.
The specificities of the forward noising process are described in the SI
B.1
.
We assume that
z_{t}|z_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{t}z_{0};\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bf I})
, the complete proof can be found in the SI
B.2
.

On Figure
1
, we illustrate the diffusion process adapted to crystal structure generation.
The green arrow represents the
\theta
parameters of the model that have been learned.
The score of the diffusion model that is used to generate samples is defined as

s_{\theta}(z_{t})\approx\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(z_{t}),

where
\theta
parameters are the weights and biases learned in the training procedure.
This score is directly involved into the de-noising process:

z_{t-1}|z_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\dfrac{z_{t}+b_{t}^{2}s_{\theta}(z_{t})}{a_{t}},\dfrac{{\sigma}_{t-1}^{2}}{{\sigma}_{t}^{2}}b_{t}^{2}\right).

(1)

The model relies on the assumption that an observation cannot be distinguished from a Gaussian noise after
T
noising steps.
After the training procedure, the generative process starts from a Gaussian noise and successively denoises a sample, in our case, a structure, for a pre-defined number of timesteps.

2.2
Guidance for diffusion models

The primary objective of generative models in materials science has traditionally been the generation of stable materials.
However, in practice, researchers are often interested in specific materials that have to satisfy some well-defined properties and be experimentally reachable, even if they do not correspond to the most stable structures.
For example, the synthesis of functional materials often involves strict constraints: the coordination number of metal centers can dictate optical properties, interatomic distances must be finely tuned to enable some catalytic mechanisms or provide mechanical properties, and the band gap must be adjusted to meet the requirements of optoelectronic applications.
Incorporating mechanisms to control the generation process within such constrains is therefore essential to make these models more suitable to address actual properties.
In the present work, we use guidance for this purpose.

Guidance diffusion models aim to modify the reverse diffusion process so that a certain property
C
is satisfied.
From a Bayesian point of view, the score function is modified to incorporate the condition
C
, yielding:

\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(z_{t},C)=\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(z_{t})+\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(C|z_{t}).

The score is approximated by the score network
\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(z_{t})
, and an estimate of
\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(C|z_{t})
has to be approximated.
Different methods have been explored to compute this approximation, and details about guidance for diffusion models are provided in SI
C
.

2.2.1
Universal Guidance

MatterGen proposed a classifier-free guidance (CFG) trained on set of selected stable compounds.
The CFG allows guiding the backward diffusion process toward a desired result, such as a stable structure, without requiring an additional classifier layer (more details in the SI
C.2
).
The main issue with the CFG method is that one needs to retrain the model for each specific new set of conditions.
Otherwise the model is out-of-distribution, and can yield irrelevant results, especially if the conditions are far from those used for training.
To overcome this issue, the Universal Guidance method
3
(the notations introduced in SI
B
) uses an off-the-shelf loss function
\ell
.
Although in the original report on this method,
3

only the Variance Preserving (VP) case was considered,
here we need both VP and Variance Exploding (VE), so that we consider the general case.
To integrate both VE and FP, we propose a different formulation of the approach:

\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(C|z_{t})\approx-\nabla_{z_{t}}\ell(C,f(z_{t})).

Indeed, the negative log-likelihood is a measure of the model’s performance
widely used in machine learning.
We use here the cross-entropy loss
\ell
that measures how strongly the condition
C
is violated by
z_{t}
;

f
is an objective function that evaluates a specific property of
z_{t}
and compares it to the condition
C
.
The estimation of the property is, however, challenging for noisy samples, especially if
f
is a black-box machine learning
model trained on clean samples only.
The Universal Guidance method replaces
z_{t}
by
\hat{z}_{0|t}
, the estimation of
z_{0}
at timestep
t
, which is the predicted final sample:

\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(C|z_{t})\approx-\nabla_{z_{t}}\ell(C,f(\hat{z}_{0|t})).

Equation (
4
) (from SI
B
) for
\hat{z}_{0|t}
is the general case of the reverse diffusion process; we recall that
{\sigma}_{0}=0
:
\hat{z}_{0|t}=\dfrac{z_{t}+{\sigma}_{t}^{2}s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)}{\alpha_{t}}
.
This gives the
Forward Universal Guidance update
:

\hat{s}_{\theta}(z_{t},t)=s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)-\nabla_{z_{t}}\ell\left(C,f\left(\dfrac{z_{t}+{\sigma}_{t}^{2}s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)}{\alpha_{t}}\right)\right).

(2)

Here,
f
is a user-defined differentiable function that encodes task-specific properties of the material system, such as the volume per atom, atom coordination number, bond distance, or other relevant structural or chemical descriptors.
This differentiability ensures that the gradient
\nabla_{z_{t}}\ell
in the guidance update can be computed effectively.

Unfortunately, the
3
approach alone did not give reasonable results in the tests.
Therefore, the authors performed a second guidance, the
Backward Universal Guidance
, in order to strengthen the constraint:

\hat{s}_{\theta}(z_{t},t)=s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)-\dfrac{\alpha_{t}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\nabla_{z_{0}}\ell\left(C,f\left(\hat{z}_{0|t}\right)\right).

(3)

Equation
3
is similar to the training-free guidance
49
.
Indeed, it can be seen as a minimization problem of
\hat{z}_{0|t}
as the initial state in the clean sample space, where
\Delta
is the variable on which the optimization is performed and represents a small perturbation on the material in:

\displaystyle\Delta z_{0}

\displaystyle=\arg\min_{\Delta}\ell(C,f(\hat{z}_{0|t}+\Delta))

\displaystyle\hat{z}_{0|t}+\Delta z_{0}

\displaystyle=\dfrac{z_{t}+{\sigma}_{t}^{2}\left(s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)+\dfrac{\alpha_{t}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\Delta z_{0}\right)}{\alpha_{t}}.

In this perspective, one step of Backward Universal Guidance (
3
) is equivalent to perform one step of gradient descent with a constant step size equal to
1
in the optimization problem (starting from
\Delta=0
).

Finally, the last ingredient of the Universal Guidance framework is the
self-recurrence
loop.
This loop consists of re-noising the sample according to the forward corruption process right after the denoising step.
This is repeated
k
times.
The final estimation of
z_{t-1}
is then chosen to continue the diffusion process.
This allows a better exploration of the manifold of noisy samples at timestep
t
.
All these elements result in the
Universal Guidance algorithm
drafted as Algorithm

, which is based on the methods introduced in
49
and

Universal Guidance.

The coefficients
g_{s},k_{s}
in Algorithm
1
are
guidance strengths

that allow for more flexibility in the guidance.
Their utility is detailed in Section
2.2.2
.

We
propose here to derive
a more formal analysis of the tradeoff behind the above mentioned algorithm, beyond the analysis originally performed
3
.
By defining
(z_{t}^{i})_{i=0}^{k}
and
(z_{t-1}^{i})_{i=0}^{k}
and by using both the forward and the backward diffusion processes, the updated equations are:

\displaystyle z_{t-1}^{i}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{a_{t}}\left(z_{t}^{i}+b_{t}^{2}s_{\theta}(z_{t}^{i})\right)+\dfrac{\sigma_{t-1}}{\sigma_{t}}b_{t}\cdot\varepsilon_{t}^{i}

\displaystyle z_{t}^{i+1}

\displaystyle=a_{t}\cdot z_{t-1}^{i}+b_{t}\cdot\epsilon_{t}^{i+1}

where the noises
\epsilon_{t}^{i},\varepsilon_{t}^{i}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)
are mutually independent. Overall the new reverse update becomes:

\displaystyle z_{t-1}^{k}=\dfrac{1}{a_{t}}\left(z_{t}^{i}+b_{t}^{2}\sum_{i=0}^{k}s_{\theta}(z_{t}^{i})\right)+\dfrac{\sigma_{t-1}}{\sigma_{t}}b_{t}\sum_{i=0}^{k}\varepsilon_{t}^{i}+\dfrac{b_{t}}{a_{t}}\sum_{i=1}^{k}\epsilon_{t}^{i}.

Therefore, by renaming
\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k}\varepsilon_{t}^{i}=\sqrt{k+1}\cdot\tilde{\varepsilon}_{t}
and
\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{k}\epsilon_{t}^{i}=\sqrt{k}\cdot\tilde{\epsilon}_{t}
, these updates can be rewritten:

\displaystyle z_{t-1}^{k}=\dfrac{1}{a_{t}}\left(z_{t}^{0}+\left(\sqrt{k+1}b_{t}\right)^{2}\frac{1}{k+1}\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k}s_{\theta}(z_{t}^{i})\right)+\dfrac{\sigma_{t-1}}{\sigma_{t}}\left(\sqrt{k+1}b_{t}\right)\tilde{\varepsilon}_{t}+\dfrac{1}{a_{t}}\sqrt{\dfrac{k}{k+1}}\left(\sqrt{k+1}b_{t}\right)\tilde{\epsilon}_{t}.

This
writing highlights
that when
k
increases,
\frac{1}{k+1}\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{k}s_{\theta}(z_{t}^{i})
gets closer to the average score in the neighborhood of the noisy sample
z_{t}
, while the variance, becoming proportional to
\left(\sqrt{k+1}\cdot b_{t}\right)
, increases.
Therefore, a tradeoff should be found for a good approximation of the average score but also a variance kept low.

2.2.2
Guidance strength

The guidance strength coefficients
g_{s},k_{s}
are used to control precisely the strength of the guidance during the diffusion process. Overall, three different cases are possible:
(i) increasing functions, (ii) decreasing functions, (iii) constant functions.
E.g., in case where the estimator
\hat{z}_{0|t}
is quite noisy in the first steps, we can fix
g_{s}(t)=k_{s}(t)=0
for
t/T>0.9
.
As explained in
49
, each of these cases is adapted to specific conditions.
To reduce the computational complexity,
it is also possible to guide only at certain timesteps, for example:
g_{s}(t)=k_{s}(t)=0
if
t\not\equiv 0~[M]
where
M
is the desired periodicity of the guidance.
This reduces the guidance strength but improves the running time of the
algorithm by reducing the number of operations, so, the overall generation is accelerated.

2.2.3
Gradient Normalization

We faced an issue when using off-the-shelf loss function: the gradient of the guidance function and its derivative was not of the same order as the score.
This often leads to misguidance, like in a classical gradient descent.
To face this, especially with potentially new losses added afterward without any control of the units used for it, we decided to implement a slight modification of the guidance score updates by normalizing the gradient.
This method is inspired by the normalized gradient descent method as explained in
54
.

\displaystyle g_{s}(t)=\dfrac{\gamma_{t}}{\|\nabla\ell(C,z_{t})\|};\quad k_{s}(t)=\dfrac{\kappa_{t}}{\|\nabla\ell(C,z_{t})\|}

The coefficient
\gamma_{t},\kappa_{t}
with this modification plays a central role, they allow to control how strong the guidance should be. Usually they are kept constant, but it is possible to have a broader control over it.
In a more general way, Spider-SFO
10
adds an upper bound for the coefficient :

g_{s}(t)=\min\left(\dfrac{\gamma_{t}}{\|\nabla\ell(C,z_{t})\|},\eta_{t}\right)

All these choice are made to simplify the theoretical smoothness condition needed for the loss function.
However, due to the difficulties to guarantee all these conditions for our guidance function, we chose to hold on constant values for
\gamma_{t},\kappa_{t}
and test experimentally the best one.
In order to achieve theoretical guarantee, an additional study would be needed.

3
Case studies of crystal structures generated under constraints

Having introduced the general guidance procedure above, we now turn to its main application in this work, namely inorganic crystal structure generation.
We show how physically motivated constraints can be incorporated into diffusion sampling in order to control the generation of crystallographic cells.
Although the discovery of new materials is not the primary objective here, we demonstrate that guidance can shift the statistical distributions of generated structures relative to non-guided sampling of the foundation model, while the generated structures are still checked for chemical consistency in the postprocessing and analysis steps described below.

We illustrate the methodology on several solid-state chemical systems taken as case studies.
For each case, structure generation is guided by specific geometric constraints related to the targeted structural features.
A chemical system here simply refers to the set of crystalline materials formed from a given combination of chemical elements, and the corresponding guidance loss is defined according to the imposed constraints
C
.

The effect of guidance is assessed by comparing the statistical distributions obtained with guided and non-guided generation on batches of 1000 generated crystal structures.
The non-guided generation is obtained using MatterGen
chemical\_system
checkpoint, i.e. the fine-tuned model trained on chemical systems with a maximum of 20 atoms per unit cell and energies less than 0.1 eV/atom above the convex hull
27
.
Our diffusion-guidance implementation built on top of MatterGen is available at
https://github.com/Inirius/mattergenbis
; additional details on the model, checkpoints, and code availability are provided in the SI
D
.
For each case study, the guidance strengths
k
and
g
were chosen by testing a few candidate values so as to preserve reasonable structures while still producing a measurable effect on the targeted descriptors.
This led us to retain relatively low values rather than enforcing the guidance constraints too strictly.

After sampling, the generated data are analyzed along two complementary directions. First, in order to assess the direct effect of guidance on the sampling procedure itself, descriptor distributions for the guided and non-guided generated structures are computed from the raw generated data, i.e. before any postprocessing.
These distributions are represented by histograms or kernel-smoothed curves and compared with the corresponding reference distributions.
The efficiency of guidance with respect to the targeted constraint is further quantified through a one-sided
p
-value from a two-proportion
z
-test comparing the fractions of generated structures that satisfy the constraint in the guided and non-guided samples.
Small
p
-values provide evidence against the null hypothesis that guidance does not increase the constraint-satisfaction rate, suggesting that guidance may have a positive effect; values below
0.05
are taken as statistically significant.

Second, both the guided and non-guided structure sets are processed through the same postprocessing pipeline, described in detail in SI
E
.
Among other steps, this pipeline includes symmetrization, deduplication, and the removal of structures that are chemically unreasonable from the outset.
The latter screening is performed using interatomic-distance criteria and, more importantly, by estimating the height above the energy convex hull defined by a combined dataset from the Alexandria
35
, OQMD
19
, and Materials Project
16
databases.
In the following, we refer to these known structures as the “references”.
They provide a consistent comparison set for the generated distributions, but should not be interpreted as an exact reconstruction of the MatterGen training set, since the latter involves atomic-number and energy cutoffs.

Energies are evaluated with the GRACE model
23
, using the “GRACE-2L-OMAT-large-ft-AM” checkpoint
23
, which proved to be of accuracy comparable to DFT for the systems considered here.
We note that energy estimations are performed without crystal relaxation in order to directly assess the quality of generation and to preserve user-imposed geometric constraints, such as the target volume in our first case study.
The postprocessed data are then used to assess the relationship between constraint satisfaction and physical plausibility, measured here through the height above the convex hull, by means of Pareto fronts (see the first example below for definitions and details).

Technical details of the proposed guidance functions for materials generation, including environment-dependent losses and equality constraints, are provided in SI
D
.
There, we also show that multiple guidance objectives can be combined by treating
C
constraints as a multidimensional objective.
More precisely, given losses
(\ell_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
associated with properties computed by functions

The following case studies range from unary to quaternary systems, progressively illustrating the scope of the proposed guidance strategy and how increasingly complex forms of structural control can be achieved.

4
Conclusions

In this work, we have demonstrated that a foundational diffusion model for the generation of crystal structures, already trained on structures with energies on or close to the convex hull, can be equipped with an adaptive guidance mechanism that enforces explicit physico-chemical constraints during generation, without any re-training.
By relying on differentiable cost functions, in particular those based on local environments, we show on several inorganic case studies (from unary to quaternary systems) that sampling can be concentrated around targeted atomic volumes, coordination numbers and nature of the near neighbors, while preserving thermodynamic plausibility as assessed by GNN/MLIP-based estimators.
In the present work, our goal was to prove that chemical and geometrical constraints can be imposed in diffusion algorithms using Universal Guidance, which we have implemented on top of the MatterGen code (currently limited to unit cells with up to 20 atoms), in order to force the statistical distributions toward structural regimes that differ from those present in the reference datasets.
This framework paves the way for a more directed exploration of materials space, where the statistical distributions of crystallographic cells can be oriented toward regions featuring local environments and densities compatible with desired physico-chemical properties, thereby providing a flexible tool for the assisted design of functional materials.

A
Material representation

Except the case of quasi-crystals, a defect-free crystalline solid
is described by a unit cell that contains atoms and that repeats in all directions following symmetry operations.
This unit cell is represented by a triplet:
U=(X,A,L)
, defined as follows:

•

Fractional atomic coordinate :
X=(x^{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in[0,1[^{3\times n}

•

Atomic species :
A=(a^{i})_{i=1}^{n}\in\mathbb{A}^{n}
where
\mathbb{A}
is the list of all the atoms species

•

Lattice :
L=(a,b,c)\in\mathbb{R}^{3\times 3}

The lattice is here composed of the three basis vectors of the unit cell also called
primitive translation vectors
.
Then, the material is the repetition of the unit cell in all directions:

M(U)=\{(L(x^{i}+m),a^{i})~|~i\in[\![1,n]\!],m\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}\}

More formally, in this work, what we will call here a solid corresponds to a Bravais lattice, and we use term lattice to denote the primitive vectors of this lattice.

However, for a given solid, the unit cell is not unique. Let us denote
\sim
the equivalent relation between two unit cells that describe the same solid. The solid satisfies several invariances:

•

Permutation
:
\forall\sigma\in\mathfrak{S}_{n},M(U)=M(\sigma(X),\sigma(A),L)

•

Translation
:
\forall z\in\mathbb{R}^{3},M(U)\sim M(X+z[1_{3}],A,L)

•

Rotation
:
\forall O\in O_{3}(\mathbb{R}),M(U)\sim M(X,A,OL)

•

Periodic cell choice
:
\forall C\in\mathbb{Z}^{3\times 3}
triangular such that
\det C=1,M(U)=M(C^{-1}X,A,LC)

•

Supercell
:
\forall(k_{i})_{i=1}^{3}\in\mathbb{N^{*}}^{3},C=diag(k_{i})_{i=1}^{3},

M(U)=M\left(\displaystyle\bigoplus_{i\in\prod_{l=1}^{3}[\![0,k_{l}-1]\!]}C^{-1}(X+i),\bigoplus_{i=1}^{\det C}A,LC\right)

where
\bigoplus
is the concatenation operator.

B
Notation of classical diffusion process

B.1
Forward Diffusion Process

The forward diffusion process is the noising/corruption process. Here, for the solid, we assume that this process is independent, meaning it can be decomposed with separate noising processes for each atom position, atom type and for the lattice:

q(M_{t}|M_{t-1})=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{n}q(x_{t}^{i}|x_{t-1}^{i})q(a_{t}^{i}|a_{t-1}^{i})\right)q(L_{t}|L_{t-1})

However, this independence hypothesis could be discussed, as the Cartesian coordinates of the atoms depend on both the fractional coordinate and the lattice. In
46
for example, the Cartesian coordinates are chosen instead of the fractional ones, but this is not suited for this framework because the lattice also evolves, so atom coordinates could go outside the unit cell.

We then describe independently each forward diffusion process.

B.1.1
Position noising

The positions are translation invariant, and the diffusion process should stay within
[0,1)
, so a wrapped normal distribution is used. More specifically. It behaves like a Gaussian distribution around each equivalent position that is wrapped afterward:

q(x_{t}\mid x_{t-1})\propto\sum_{k\in\mathbb{Z}^{3}}\exp\left(-\frac{\|x_{t}-x_{t-1}+k\|^{2}}{2(\sigma_{t}^{2}-\sigma_{t-1}^{2})}\right)

B.1.2
Atom type noising

The atom type is in a discrete set
\mathcal{A}
. Therefore, the noising process uses a discrete denoising diffusion probabilistic model (D3PM). It is a Markov process with an absorbing state
[MASK]
corresponding to the absence of an atom at this coordinate:

q(a_{t}\mid a_{t-1})=\text{Cat}(a_{t-1}Q_{t})

\text{where }\forall x,y\in\mathcal{A}\cup\{[MASK]\},[Q_{t}]_{xy}=q(a_{t}=y\mid a_{t-1}=x)

Cat is defined as :
X\sim\mathrm{Cat}(p),\quad p\in\Delta^{d-1},
where
\Delta^{d-1}=\{\,p\in\mathbb{R}^{d}:p_{i}\geq 0,\displaystyle\sum_{i=1}^{d}p_{i}=1\,\}

is the probability simplex. The distribution
\mathrm{Cat}(p)
is supported on the
canonical one-hot vectors
\{e_{1},\dots,e_{d}\}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
with

\Pr[X=e_{i}]=p_{i},\quad i\in[\![1,d]\!]

Note that the probability of change could depend on the timestep.

B.1.3
Lattice noising

The lattice is rotation invariant, therefore, instead of noise
L_{t}
directly, a rotation matrix
O\in SO_{3}(\mathbb{R})
is fixed to decompose :
L_{t}=O\hat{L}_{t}
, with
\hat{L}_{t}
symmetric. The noising process is therefore:

q(L_{t}\mid L_{t-1})=q(\hat{L}_{t}\mid\hat{L}_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{t}\hat{L}_{t-1};\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bf I})

Unfortunately, the simple Gaussian distribution tends to give overly flat lattices. Therefore, to avoid such noisy cells, a tendency toward a cubic lattice is added:

q(\hat{L}_{t}|\hat{L}_{t-1})=\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{t}\hat{L}_{t-1}+(1-\alpha_{t})\sqrt[3]{nc}~I_{3}~;~\sigma_{t}^{2}(n\nu)^{\frac{2}{3}}~I_{3})

Where
C
is the inverse of the desired density and
\nu
is a noise constant.

B.2
Reverse diffusion Process

The reverse diffusion process is the denoising part of the diffusion model. In this section, we consider a general case of a Gaussian forward diffusion process:
z_{t}|z_{t-1}\sim\mathcal{N}(a_{t}z_{t-1},b_{t}^{2}{\bf I})
. In most of the paper, a special case where
a_{t}^{2}+b^{2}_{t}=1
is considered, which corresponds to the variance-preserving case. However, we also need the formula for the variance exploding case (
a_{t}=1,~b_{t}^{2}=\sigma_{t}^{2}-\sigma_{t-1}^{2}
) We can compute
z_{t}|z_{0}
:

\displaystyle z_{t}

\displaystyle=a_{t}z_{t-1}+b_{t}\epsilon_{t}\quad\text{where }\epsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{\bf I})

\displaystyle=a_{t}a_{t-1}z_{t-2}+(a_{t}b_{t-1}\epsilon_{t-1}+b_{t}\epsilon_{t})

\displaystyle=\quad...

\displaystyle=\left(\prod_{i=1}^{t}a_{i}\right)z_{0}+\sum_{i=1}^{t}b_{i}\left(\prod_{j=i+1}^{t}a_{j}\right)\epsilon_{i}.

Therefore, due to the mutual independence of
(\epsilon_{i})_{i=1}^{t}
, we have:

z_{t}|z_{0}\sim\displaystyle\mathcal{N}\left(\left(\prod_{i=1}^{t}a_{i}\right)z_{0};\left(\sum_{i=1}^{t}b_{i}^{2}\prod_{j=i+1}^{t}a_{j}^{2}\right){\bf I}\right).

For the sake of simplicity, we note:
z_{t}|z_{0}\sim\mathcal{N}(\alpha_{t}z_{0};\sigma_{t}^{2}{\bf I})
. With this notation, we get for
z_{t}|z_{t-1}
, by reversing the formula:

z_{t}|z_{t-1}\sim\mathcal{N}\left(\dfrac{\alpha_{t}}{\alpha_{t-1}}z_{t-1};\left(\sigma_{t}^{2}-\sigma_{t-1}^{2}\dfrac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{\alpha_{t-1}^{2}}\right){\bf I}\right).

So this gives the formulas:

\begin{cases}a_{t}&=\dfrac{\alpha_{t}}{\alpha_{t-1}}\\
b_{t}^{2}&=\sigma_{t}^{2}-\sigma_{t-1}^{2}\dfrac{\alpha_{t}^{2}}{\alpha_{t-1}^{2}}.\end{cases}

Unfortunately, in general this formula is untractable. However, conditioning over
z_{0}
makes it tractable:

\displaystyle\log q(z_{t-1}|z_{t},z_{0})

\displaystyle=\log q(z_{t}|z_{t-1},z_{0})+\log q(z_{t-1}|z_{0})-\log q(z_{t}|z_{0})

\displaystyle=\log q(z_{t}|z_{t-1})-\dfrac{\|z_{t-1}-\alpha_{t-1}z_{0}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t-1}^{2}}+\dfrac{\|z_{t}-\alpha_{t}z_{0}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}+K_{1}\quad\text{(Markov property)}

\displaystyle=-\frac{\|z_{t}-a_{t}z_{t-1}\|^{2}}{2b_{t}^{2}}-\dfrac{\|z_{t-1}-\alpha_{t-1}z_{0}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t-1}^{2}}+\dfrac{\|z_{t}-\alpha_{t}z_{0}\|^{2}}{2\sigma_{t}^{2}}+K_{2}

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{\sigma_{t}^{2}/\sigma_{t-1}^{2}}{b_{t}^{2}}z_{t-1}^{2}-\left\langle\frac{a_{t}}{b_{t}^{2}}z_{t}+\dfrac{\alpha_{t-1}}{\sigma_{t-1}^{2}}z_{0}\bigg|z_{t-1}\right\rangle\right)+K_{3}(z_{t},z_{0}).

However,
z_{0}
is not known before the end of the process, so it should be replaced by

z_{0}=\dfrac{z_{t}-\sigma_{t}\varepsilon_{t}}{\alpha_{t}}
, which gives:

\displaystyle\log q(z_{t-1}|z_{t},z_{0})

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{2}\left(\dfrac{\sigma_{t}^{2}/\sigma_{t-1}^{2}}{b_{t}^{2}}z_{t-1}^{2}-\dfrac{1}{a_{t}}\left\langle\frac{\sigma_{t}^{2}/\sigma_{t-1}^{2}}{b_{t}^{2}}z_{t}-\dfrac{\sigma_{t}}{\sigma_{t-1}^{2}}\varepsilon_{t}\bigg|z_{t-1}\right\rangle\right)+K_{3}(z_{t},z_{0})

\displaystyle=-\dfrac{1}{2}\cdot\dfrac{\sigma_{t}^{2}/\sigma_{t-1}^{2}}{b_{t}^{2}}\left(z_{t-1}^{2}-\dfrac{1}{a_{t}}\left\langle z_{t}-\dfrac{b_{t}^{2}}{\sigma_{t}}\varepsilon_{t}|z_{t-1}\right\rangle\right)+K_{4}(z_{t},z_{0}).

Now, it is clear that unless
\varepsilon_{t}
is known, this distribution remains untractable. This is the reason why a neural network is trained to predict
\varepsilon_{t}
from
z_{t}
and
t
. This neural network is named the
noise network
, and is noted
\varepsilon_{\theta}:(z_{t},t)\mapsto\varepsilon_{\theta}(z_{t},t)
, parametrized with
\theta
.

Another approach
37
uses the
score
, which is defined as :

\displaystyle\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(z_{t})

\displaystyle=\nabla_{z_{t}}\mathbb{E}_{q(z_{0})}[\log q(z_{t}|z_{0})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(z_{0})}[\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(z_{t}|z_{0})]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(z_{0})}\left[-\dfrac{z_{t}-\alpha_{t}z_{0}}{\sigma_{t}^{2}}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{q(z_{0})}\left[-\dfrac{\varepsilon_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}\right].

Therefore, the score network
s_{\theta}:(z_{t},t)\mapsto s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)
, in charge of approximating the score, is related to the noise network with:

s_{\theta}(z_{t},t)\approx\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(z_{t})=\mathbb{E}_{q(z_{0})}\left[-\dfrac{\varepsilon_{t}}{\sigma_{t}}\right]\approx\mathbb{E}_{q(z_{0})}\left[-\dfrac{\varepsilon_{\theta}(z_{t},t)}{\sigma_{t}}\right]=-\dfrac{\varepsilon_{\theta}(z_{t},t)}{\sigma_{t}}

Depending on the structure of the object to generate, this neural network can have different architecture.

The parameters
\alpha_{t},\sigma_{t}
can have different profiles. We describe two kinds: Variance Preserving (VP) and Variance Exploding (VE). But first, let us describe more specifically the generative model used here based on Stochastic Differential Equations (SDE).

B.3
Predictor-Corrector Sampling

The predictor-corrector sampler was introduced in
38
to correct errors in the evolution of the discretized reverse-time SDE, so it is an amelioration of the simple denoising process that only uses the reverse diffusion formula
4
. It is composed of a corrector and a predictor. The corrector uses a Monte-Carlo Markov Chain (MCMC) score-based approach by correcting the marginal distribution of the estimated sample given by the predictor, which comes from the reverse diffusion process SDE formula (
4
).

The overall algorithm is the following:

Input:

N
: number of denoising steps (discretization steps for the reverse-time SDE)

n_{c}
: number of corrector steps

for

i=N

to

1

do

z_{i-1}\leftarrow\text{Predictor}(z_{i})

for

j=1

to

n_{c}

do

z_{i}\leftarrow\text{Corrector}(z_{i})

return

z_{0}

Algorithm 3

Predictor–Corrector Sampling.

The predictor step

The predictor function can be any sampling method that computes
z_{i-1}|z_{i}
, either random or deterministic. In our case, this computation uses the reverse diffusion equation
4
, which is random to enable more diversity in the generated samples. However, a deterministic method could be useful when reproducibility is needed.

The corrector step

As for the predictor step, several algorithms can be used in the corrector step. In the MatterGen code, Langevin steps are used as corrector step:

x_{t-1}=x_{t}+\delta\cdot s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)+\sqrt{2\cdot\delta}\cdot\varepsilon_{t},

where
\varepsilon_{t}\sim\mathcal{N}(0,{\bf I})
, and the step size
\delta
is defined as follows:

\delta=\min(2\alpha_{t}\left(r\frac{\|\varepsilon_{t}\|}{\|s_{\theta}(x_{t},t)\|}\right)^{2},\bar{\delta}),

with
\bar{\delta}
being the maximum step size (
1
here), and
r
, the signal-to-noise ratio (
0.2
here).

This algorithm is the Corrector algorithm (VP SDE) from
38
. It serves as a stochastic refinement step, repeatedly pulling samples closer to high-density regions of the data distribution, thus compensating for discretization errors made by the predictor and improving sample quality.

B.4
Architecture of the model

Having described more precisely each part of the diffusion models for material generation, we will focus more precisely on the one we will use: MatterGen.

MatterGen exploits an SE(3)-equivariant graph neural network based on the GemNet architecture
11
to predict scores for the lattice, atom positions, and atom types during the denoising process. The model adapts GemNet-dT,
the non-conservative force prediction variant of GemNet, which efficiently captures directional, two-body, and three-body interactions to ensure SO(3)-equivariance.
MatterGen employs four message-passing layers, a 7Å cutoff radius for neighbor list construction, and sets hidden node and edge feature dimensions to 512.
Ideally, this cutoff radius should be dependent on the atom type, however we chose to keep it fixed so that the results can be compared. It is trained to predict Cartesian coordinate scores that are equivariant to rotation and permutation, invariant to translation, and subsequently transforms these scores into fractional coordinates.

C
Guidance for diffusion models

In this section, we describe the main ways to guide a diffusion process.

C.1
Classifier guidance

This guidance introduced in
9
is based on a classifier
f_{\phi}
that is trained to classify the noisy sample
z_{t}
with respect to the property
C
.
The term ”classifier” is employed here because usually the property
C
is a class label for
z_{t}
. In this context:

\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(c|z_{t})\approx\nabla_{z_{t}}\log f_{\phi}(C,z_{t},t)

The weakness of this method is that it needs an accurate and efficient classifier that obtains decent accuracy for noisy samples at each timestep.
It is therefore generally impossible to use off-the-shelf classifier network, as those are trained on existing stable materials, so for a noisy sample the classification error increases. With a bad classification, the guidance becomes erratic.

C.2
Classifier-free guidance

To address the issue of designing a classifier, in
14
the authors came up with a way to avoid its use. Using the Bayes rules:

\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(c|z_{t})=\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(z_{t}|c)-\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(z_{t})

The model is therefore trained as a conditional score model with the condition
C
as an input so that:
s_{\theta}(z_{t},t,c)=\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(z_{t}|c)
.
Therefore, with an empty condition, we retrieve the unconditional score model :
s_{\theta}(z_{t},t,\emptyset)=\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(z_{t})
.
However, if this approximation is used directly, the term
\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(z_{t})
vanishes and only the conditional model remains.
Therefore, the guidance scale
\gamma
is used to control a trade-off
between the conditional and unconditional models:

\displaystyle\nabla_{z_{t}}\log q(z_{t},c)

\displaystyle=s_{\theta}(z_{t},t,\emptyset)+\gamma(s_{\theta}(z_{t},t,c)-s_{\theta}(z_{t},t,\emptyset))

\displaystyle=(1-\gamma)s_{\theta}(z_{t},t,\emptyset)+\gamma s_{\theta}(z_{t},t,c)

\gamma=0
corresponds to the unconditional model, and
\gamma=1
to the conditional model.
The innovation of the classifier-free guidance is to use
\gamma>1
, which leads to significantly better alignment with the conditioning signal, but at the cost of a lack of diversity.

C.3
Training-Free Guidance

The most recent improvement in guidance methods for diffusion models that do not require retraining is the unified Training-Free Guidance (TFG)
49
.
This work proposes a unified framework that brings together various training-free guidance techniques used in diffusion models.
Overall the method is almost exactly the same as the Universal Guidance, the only difference lies in the guidance function. In order to avoid local issues with its gradient, this method replaces
f
with a time-dependent smooth function
\tilde{f}_{t}
instead of:

\tilde{f}_{t}(x)=\mathbb{E}_{\delta\sim\mathcal{N}(0,I)}[f(x+\gamma\sigma_{t}\delta)]

Called
Implicit Dynamic
, the expectation is approximated using a Monte Carlo method. However, reducing the number of Monte Carlo samples does not hurt performance much, suggesting that it is not the precise estimation that matters here, but the noise-driven dynamics.

We choose not to use this additional method, since, as shown in the analysis of the self-recurrence loop, the score is already averaged with a random dynamics in this self-recurrence loop by averaging the score over all the
z_{t}^{i}
.

D
Details of the developed guidance functions for solid generation

We have developed some guidance functions for solid generation.
Simple guidance functions like a target cell volume, choice of the chemical environment (nature and number of first neighbors environment) have been implemented.
The results of the tests on selected case-studies are presented in the main text.
The guidance functions should be differentiable to be compatible with our algorithm.
Because we used
pytorch
, the gradient is automatically calculated with
torch.autograd
as long as the function is rightly implemented.
The parameters on which the derivative is computed should have
requires_grad = True
, and every operations should be done so that the
grad_fn
is kept all along.
This last requirement is easy to satisfy when using machine learning predictor, only minor adaptations are needed.
However for already existing functions written with
numpy
, the code must be entirely rewritten using
pytorch
in order to be used efficiently as a guidance function.

To support material generation within our framework, we have developed several dedicated guidance functions.
These functions address specific objectives, such as controlling local atomic environments, ensuring material stability, and handling multi-objective optimization.
Our diffusion-guidance implementation built on top of MatterGen is available at:

https://github.com/Inirius/mattergenbis
.

D.1
Environment

The
environment loss
computes the average number of atoms of type
B
in the neighborhood of the atoms of type
A
, both atoms can be of the same nature, chemical element, so
B
can be equal to
A
, under a certain distance and compare it to a predefined objective.
Such condition will be written
A-B:n
, where
n
is the desired objective.

More intuitively, the property that is targeted is the number of atoms in the first coordination shell of a given chemical-type.
The loss used between the computed number and the objective
C
can be
l1,l2
or
huber
.
We recall that the
huber
loss is
\ell_{\delta}(a)=\begin{cases}\frac{1}{2}a^{2}&\text{for }|a|\leq\delta,\\
\delta\cdot\left(|a|-\frac{1}{2}\delta\right)&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}
with the parameter
\delta>0
, which controls the switch between the quadratic behavior and the linear behavior.

\ell(C,f(\hat{z}_{0|t}))=\begin{cases}\|c-f(\hat{z}_{0|t})\|_{1}\quad\text{for }l1\\
\|c-f(\hat{z}_{0|t})\|_{2}\quad\text{for }l2\\
\ell_{\delta}(c-f(\hat{z}_{0|t}))\quad\text{for }huber\text{ with }\delta=1.5\end{cases}

The goal of this guidance function is to enforce one specific environment around type
A
atoms.

Figure 8:
Schematic representation of the atoms smearing.

Technically speaking, it computes a differentiable species-pair value
f(\hat{z}_{0|t})=[A,B]
under periodic boundary conditions (PBC):

[A,B]=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{A}_{A}|}\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{A}}\sum_{j\in\mathcal{A}_{B}}g(d_{ij}),

Where
\mathcal{A}_{X}
is the set of atoms of type
X
,
d_{ij}
is the distance between the atoms
i
and
j
,
g(d)
is either:

•

Gaussian:
\exp\left(-\left(\frac{d}{\sigma}\right)^{2}\right)

•

Soft-cutoff:
\sigma\left(\alpha\cdot(R_{\text{cut}}-d)\right)

Where
\sigma
is the sigmoid function :
\sigma(x)=\dfrac{1}{1+e^{-x}}
, and
\alpha
a sharpness parameter for the sigmoid.
We chose to use the soft-cutoff function to handle more precisely the cutoff.

This smoothing method enables to have a differentiable function that corresponds to the desired property of counting the number of atoms of type
B
in vicinity of the type
A
atoms.

Noting that this loss guide toward the average neighborhood, it is possible to get undesired results due to averaging.
For example, when guiding toward
4
neighbors, one material could get half of type
A
atoms with
2
neighbors, and half with
6
.
To face this issue, we came up with a more general loss.
By considering the (discrete) distribution of the neighborhood of type
A
atoms, it can be compared to a desired distribution that can control the fraction of each desired environment.
The loss is here a divergence between the two distributions.

However, because the number of atoms is not directly computed but rather approximated smoothly, the choice of the divergence is important.
Indeed, the absolute continuity of the desired distribution with respect to the computed one is needed for the KL divergence.
So it could not be used.
Therefore, divergences like Wasserstein metric or Jensen-Shannon divergence should rather be used.

D.2
Energy above hull

The convex hull is the lower envelope of formation energies over composition.
For guidance, we directly use the energy above the convex hull (i.e., the deviation from the hull) as the guidance function:

\ell(C,f(\hat{z}_{0|t}))=E_{hull}(\hat{z}_{0|t})

Indeed, the lower the energy, the more stable the solid is.
The exact energy being really costly to compute, we use an off-the-shelf estimator from Microsoft: MatterSim
47
.
Some adaptations were needed due to incompatibilities with the dependencies and also because it was designed for
numpy
.
The energy on the convex hull is subtracted according to the composition of
\hat{z}_{0|t}
by using the known databases.
The databases used were the classical DFT database used by the community: Alexandria
35
, Materials Project
16
and OQMD
19
.

D.3
Inequality and more general constraints

Our framework until this point was mainly focused on equality constraints, with
\ell
being a difference measure between the objective
C
and the estimated property
f(\hat{z}_{0|t}
, either a distance or a divergence.
However, for inequality constraints, the objective
C
is no more a point, in the same space as
f(\hat{z}_{0|t}
but rather a set of desired points.
Therefore, the best solution would be to use the distance between a point
y
and a set
\mathcal{C}
defined like this :

\ell(\mathcal{C},y)=\min_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\ell(C,y)

With this broader definition, the objective can be a set of objective
\mathcal{C}
.

D.4
Combining multiple guidance objectives

One might have multiple desired structural features for the solids to generate.
We came up with a simple way to handle this, which is working with
C
as a multi-dimension objective.
More precisely,
C
has
d
dimension where
d
is the number of desired properties.
Then, assuming that
(\ell_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
are the losses for each of these properties, and
f=(f_{i})_{i=1}^{d}
the function that computes the desired properties, the final loss needs to be real-valued.
So they need to be combined. We then defined:

\ell(C,f(\hat{z}_{0|t})=\|(\ell_{i}(c_{i},f_{i}(\hat{z}_{0|t})\|_{1}

The choice of the norm
\|\cdot\|_{1}
is arbitrary, other norms might be used for combining the losses.

E
Postprocess pipeline

To assess the quality of generated structures we developed a postprocess workflow as shown in the Fig.
9
.

Figure 9:
Postprocessing workflow for generated samples.

After parsing the data into the in-house data format (1), we perform a symmetrization procedure (2) using the
spglib
package
41
.
This step is necessary because the generated structures frequently appear with
C_{1}
symmetry, although they are in fact only slightly distorted variants of structures with higher symmetry.
We then screen the structures for unphysically short interatomic distances (3). Subsequently, duplicate entries are removed by means of a fingerprint-based similarity analysis (4), where the fingerprint is constructed from radial distribution functions
42
.

To place the results in the context of existing knowledge, the generated structures are compared against reference databases.
In the present study, we use the Alexandria
35
, OQMD
19
, and Materials Project
16
databases.
The corresponding procedure (5) consists of sequentially querying these three databases and retaining only those structures that have not been encountered previously.
This filtering is performed using the same similarity-analysis approach as in step (4). Prior to deduplication, we optionally exclude entries lying more than 0.5 eV/atom above the convex hull, based on the energies reported in the databases themselves.
This is motivated by our primary interest in assessing how newly generated low-energy candidate structures are positioned relative to the reference convex hull.
Finally, we perform joint energy estimation and calculate the energies above the reference-based convex-hull (7).
