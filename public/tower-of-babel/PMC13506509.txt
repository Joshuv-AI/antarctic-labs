Tumor regression pattern and distribution of residual tumor cells in potential candidates for local excision of rectal cancer: a prospective cohort study
Authors: A Gheller, D B Basílio, J S Córes, C C P Coelho, L de Araújo, A C Melo, G I B de Meloe Patriarca da Silva Neiva, D da Motta Girardi, A R Aiza, J B de Sousa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUnderstanding the patterns of regression and distribution of residual tumor cells (RTCs) is crucial for selecting appropriate candidates for local excision of ypT0-2 rectal cancer.MethodsPatients with extraperitoneal T3/T4 N0/N + rectal adenocarcinoma (< 10 cm) treated with radiotherapy (5 × 5 Gy) followed by six cycles of CAPOX chemotherapy were prospectively analyzed. The tumor regression pattern was classified as solid or fragmented, and microscopic intramural spread (MIS) was measured. A novel RTC distribution model was used: type I (luminal), type II (invasive front), type III (concentric), and type IV (random).ResultsA total of 40 patients were included (median age, 66 years; 57.5% male). Of these, 19 (47.5%) had ypT0-2 tumors: 5 ypT0, 3 ypT1, and 11 ypT2. There was no lymph node involvement, and only two (10.5%) showed a fragmented pattern; both ypT2. MIS was present in four cases (21.0%); all ypT2. The greatest MIS extension was 8 mm. All three ypT1 cases had RTCs in the mucosa (100%). Among 11 ypT2 cases, RTCs were found in the mucosa in 10 (90.9%) and in the submucosa in 9 (81.8%). RTC distribution was type I in 16 cases (84.2%). Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) tumor regression grades 1–2, RTC type I distribution, absence of MIS, and pathologic complete response were significantly associated with the occurrence of ypT0-2 (p < 0.05).ConclusionsIn this study, ypT0-2 cases were characterized by a predominantly solid regression pattern, absence of MIS, and no lymph node involvement. These findings may contribute to defining resection margins and guiding the selection of surgical techniques.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10151-026-03373-x.Keywords: Rectal neoplasms, Chemoradiotherapy, Neoadjuvant therapy, Neoplasm, Residual

Introduction

Treatment for locally advanced extraperitoneal rectal cancer typically involves neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (CRT) followed by total mesorectal excision (TME). While this combined approach offers excellent locoregional control, TME is associated with substantial morbidity, non-negligible mortality, and high rates of anorectal, urinary, and genital dysfunctions, with most patients requiring temporary or permanent ostomies [1–6].

To mitigate these drawbacks, interest in organ preservation strategies has progressively increased. One such approach is the watch-and-wait strategy, pioneered by Habr-Gama et al. [7], which offers selected patients achieving a clinical complete response (cCR) the possibility of avoiding immediate surgery and instead undergoing close surveillance. Several studies have shown excellent survival and quality-of-life benefits with this strategy [8–11].

Local excision (LE) represents another organ-preserving alternative to TME. CRT followed by LE has yielded favorable oncologic results, with local recurrence rates of less than 8%, while enabling rectal preservation [12–14]. Recently, the phase III TAUTEM randomized clinical trial, which included patients with T2-T3ab N0 tumors of the middle and lower third of the rectum, reported no statistically significant differences between upfront TME and CRT combined with LE in terms of local recurrence, disease-free survival, or overall survival [15].

However, a major challenge with LE is effectively selecting suitable patients. Because LE does not allow for assessment of mesorectal lymph node involvement, estimating this risk may be helpful in the selection of potential LE candidates. The likelihood of mesorectal lymph node involvement after CRT has been associated with pathologic T stage (ypT). Specifically, the probability of lymph node involvement is up to 12% for ypT0-1 tumors, reaching 20% for ypT2 tumors [16, 17].

Rectal tumors undergoing CRT have also been shown to regress as a cohesive cluster of tumor cells (solid pattern) or as small, fragmented clusters separated by fibrotic tissue (fragmented pattern) [18]. Additionally, tumor cells have been observed beyond the boundaries of the gross residual tumor, known as microscopic intramural spread (MIS) [19].

Current histopathologic analysis of TME specimens does not routinely assess regression patterns or the presence of MIS [20]. In this regard, we hypothesized that these features, specifically a solid regression pattern and absence of MIS, may help identify candidates suitable for LE. To obtain this information, we developed a novel anatomopathologic examination protocol. Therefore, the primary aim of this prospective study was to describe the patterns of regression and distribution of residual tumor cells (RTCs), as well as the presence or absence of MIS, in patients with ypT0-2 rectal cancer, who are potential candidates for LE. A secondary objective was to investigate variables associated with the occurrence of ypT0-2 tumors.

Materials and methods

The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Institute of Strategic Health Care Management of the Federal District – Hospital de Base of the Federal District, Brazil. All procedures involving human participants were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the institutional research committee and with the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments or comparable ethical standards.

A prospective cohort study was conducted on consecutive patients with rectal cancer who were treated from January 2022 to June 2023 at the Hospital de Base of the Federal District. Eligible participants were all patients aged > 18 years with a diagnosis of extraperitoneal T3/T4 N0/N + adenocarcinoma of the middle and lower third of the rectum, located < 10 cm from the anal verge as determined by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), who received neoadjuvant CRT.

Exclusion criteria were synchronous colorectal tumors, noncolorectal cancer, stage IV disease, rectal cancer associated with inflammatory bowel disease or familial adenomatous polyposis, palliative resection, previous exposure to radiotherapy or chemotherapy, loss of expression of DNA repair enzymes, and achievement of a cCR. A cCR was defined as the absence of identifiable tumor on digital rectal examination, rectoscopy, and MRI.

Baseline assessment and staging consisted of a thorough proctoscopic examination, colonoscopy, dedicated pelvic MRI for rectal cancer, abdominal computed tomography, and measurement of carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) levels. Restaging was conducted between 1 and 6 weeks before surgery, at the end of chemotherapy. It involved a thorough proctoscopic examination, flexible rectosigmoidoscopy, and pelvic MRI. Both the initial staging and restaging data obtained from pelvic MRI scans adhered to the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) Guidelines for Rectal Cancer [21]. Additionally, restaging MRI provided information on downsizing > 50% (at least 50% reduction in the largest tumor diameter) and downstaging (stage regression).

Neoadjuvant CRT involved short-course radiotherapy (5 × 5 Gy) followed by six cycles of CAPOX chemotherapy. The CAPOX regimen consisted of capecitabine (1000 mg/m2 orally twice daily on days 1–14) and oxaliplatin (130 mg/m2 intravenously on day 1), with a chemotherapy-free interval between days 15–21 [22]. Patients who achieved a cCR were offered a watch-and-wait strategy. For those undergoing surgery, the procedure was performed between 2 and 8 weeks after the completion of chemotherapy. Surgical intervention consisted of TME, with or without sphincter preservation.

Anatomopathologic examination

The detailed protocol has been described elsewhere [23]. Macroscopic preparation of surgical specimens was conducted by the first author, a colorectal surgeon. Although pathology residents participated in specimen preparation as part of their training, they were not responsible for determining the analyses or final reports. Briefly, all specimens underwent gross examination and photographic documentation prior to formalin fixation (Fig. 1 a,b). TME quality was assessed according to Nagtegaal et al. [24] The specimens were fixed in 4% formalin for at least 48 h and then marked from the superior, inferior, and lateral margins of the residual tumor to a distance of 4 cm in each direction (Fig. 1 c,d). In the absence of tissue within 4 cm, the entire segment was analyzed. Subsequently, the area including either the margins at the 4-cm level or the entire segment was divided into 1 × 1 cm full-thickness squares (Fig. 1 e,f). Each sample was examined microscopically according to the College of American Pathologists guidelines [20].

Tumor regression was graded on the basis of Mandard’s grading system [25]. The rectal wall and mesorectum were examined for residual disease. If residual disease was identified, each paraffin block from each patient was analyzed for the presence of RTCs and their location was reported (mucosa, submucosa, muscularis propria, and subserosa). An RTC distribution model, previously used in esophageal cancer [26], was adapted for the analysis of RTC distribution across all layers of the rectal wall, as follows: type I—regression toward the lumen, with a higher concentration of tumor cells in the mucosa and submucosa; type II—regression toward the invasive front, with a higher concentration of tumor cells in the muscularis propria and subserosa; type III—concentric regression, with a higher concentration of tumor cells in the submucosa and muscularis propria; and type IV—random regression, with a comparable concentration of tumor cells distributed across all layers.

The pattern of tumor regression was categorized as either fragmented or solid, and with or without MIS. The fragmented pattern was characterized by tumor cell clusters separated by at least 1 mm of fibrotic or normal tissue. The solid pattern was characterized by a single tumor cell cluster surrounded by fibrotic or normal tissue (Fig. 2 a,b). Patients who achieved a pathologic complete response (pCR) were considered to have a solid regression pattern. MIS was identified when tumor cells were present at least 1 mm beneath normal-appearing mucosa, extending beyond the boundaries of the gross residual lesion in any direction [27]. MIS was measured when observed. All histopathologic analyses were performed by a single pathologist specializing in the gastrointestinal tract.

Symbols: (*) muscularis propria; (+) submucosa with fibrosis; (X) mucosa. Residual tumor cells are delineated by a dashed line

In the investigation of factors associated with the occurrence of ypT0-2 tumors, cases achieving a pCR and those with RTC type I distribution pattern were grouped together for analysis.

Statistical analysis

The number of eligible cases at our center during the study period determined the sample size. All analyses were performed using SAS, version 9.4. Data are expressed as frequency (n) and percentage (%) or median (range), as appropriate. Poisson regression with robust variance was used to determine demographic and clinicopathologic factors associated with ypT0-2 tumors. Both bivariate and multivariate analyses were performed. Initially, simple Poisson models were adjusted for each variable. Variables with p values < 0.05 were then included in the multivariate Poisson model. A stepwise procedure was employed to adjust these variables, and only those with p values < 0.05 were retained in the final model. Prevalence ratio (PR) and 95% CI were derived from these models. p values < 0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

Results

From a cohort of 45 consecutive patients with rectal cancer undergoing CRT during the study period, 5 were excluded: 4 achieved a cCR and were offered a watch-and-wait strategy, and 1 due to loss of expression of DNA repair enzymes (MLH1 and PMS2). Therefore, our study sample consisted of 40 patients.

The time from radiotherapy to surgery ranged from 20 to 35 weeks (median, 28 weeks). For 32 patients, this interval was 30 weeks or less. Regarding surgical procedures, 32 patients underwent low anterior resection (including one intersphincteric resection and one total pelvic exenteration), and 8 patients underwent abdominoperineal resection (1 with combined sacrectomy). Lymphovascular invasion was found in 15 cases, and perineural invasion in 10. On anatomopathologic examination, all specimens demonstrated a complete and intact mesorectum. A positive radial margin was identified in one case, and a positive distal margin in one case.

MIS extended from 1 to 18 mm (median, 4 mm). Seven solid pattern cases showed MIS (1–3 mm). Of 17 fragmented pattern cases with MIS, 4 had MIS of 4–8 mm, and 13 had MIS > 10 mm. Overall, 14 cases (35.0%) had MIS > 10 mm; all were fragmented.

A pCR was achieved by five patients. For the 35 patients without a pCR, RTCs were located in the mucosa in 29 (82.8%), submucosa in 31 (88.5%), muscularis propria in 30 (85.7%), and subserosa in 20 (57.1%). Regarding ypT categories, all three ypT1 cases showed RTCs in the mucosa (100%). Of 11 ypT2 cases, 10 (90.9%) had RTCs in the mucosa and 9 (81.8%) in the submucosa. Of 20 ypT3 cases, 15 (75.0%) had RTCs in the mucosa, 18 (90.0%) in the submucosa, and 19 (95.0%) in the muscularis propria. The single patient with ypT4 disease (100%) had RTCs in the mucosa, submucosa, muscularis propria, and subserosa.

Of 19 patients, 14 (73.6%) had stage III disease on initial clinical staging. On post-CRT restaging, 11 cases (57.9%) were classified as stage 0 (pCR) or I. A total of 17 patients (89.5%) had a tumor size < 3 cm, with downsizing > 50% in 16 (84.2%). MRI tumor regression grade (TRG) was 3–4 in 11 cases (57.9%).

There was no anatomopathologic lymph node involvement. Only two cases (10.5%) showed a fragmented pattern, both of which were ypT2 (Fig. 2 c,d). MIS was present in four cases (21.0%); all were ypT2, and the greatest MIS extension was 8 mm.

None of the eight ypT0-1 cases exhibited a fragmented regression pattern or MIS. However, for the 11 ypT2 cases, 2 (10.5%) demonstrated a fragmented pattern and MIS was identified in 4 cases (21.0%).

The RTC distribution pattern was type I (including pCR) in 16 ypT0-2 cases (84.2%), and type II, III, or IV in 3 (15.8%). Pathologic TRG was 1–2 in 12 cases (63.2%). A total of 16 patients (84.2%) had low or no (pCR) tumor budding (84.2%). Lymphovascular invasion was identified in one case (5.3%).

Variables associated with the occurrence of ypT0-2 tumors

In bivariate analysis, the following variables were associated with a higher prevalence of ypT0-2 tumors: initial clinical stage II disease, stage 0 (pCR) or stage I disease on post-CRT restaging, MRI TRG 1–2, tumor size < 3 cm on restaging MRI, downsizing > 50%, solid regression pattern, RTC type I (including pCR) distribution pattern, absence of MIS, no lymphovascular invasion, pathologic TRG 1–2, and low or no (pCR) tumor budding.

After adjusting the multivariate Poisson model, only MRI TRG 1–2, RTC type I (including pCR) distribution pattern, absence of MIS, and low or no (pCR) tumor budding remained significantly associated with ypT0-2 tumors (p < 0.05) (Table 3).

Discussion

While minimally invasive surgical techniques and neoadjuvant CRT regimens have improved in recent years, the anatomopathologic examination still needs refinement. Aspects such as tumor regression pattern, RTC distribution, and MIS are crucial for selecting appropriate candidates, defining resection margins, and individualizing follow-up strategies. Analyzing the entire rectum and TME specimen is essential to identifying these pathologic aspects. Therefore, using a recently published comprehensive anatomopathologic examination protocol [23], this prospective cohort study described regression-distribution patterns and the presence of MIS in ypT0-2 cases, which are currently considered suitable for LE. We believe this knowledge can enhance the oncological safety of patient selection. Additionally, we identified criteria associated with the occurrence of ypT0-2 tumors.

In rectal cancer, a solid regression pattern is ideal for LE because it allows for a precise correlation between macroscopic and microscopic analysis. This pattern presents as a single tumor cell cluster, regressing toward the lumen without MIS. Conversely, fragmented regression, often associated with more advanced ypT stages, presence of MIS, and poorer prognosis [18, 28–30], increases the risk of RTCs after LE. In these cases, resection margins should be calculated based on the gross boundaries of the lesion before neoadjuvant therapy, rather than using the residual lesion after therapy, which would render LE unfeasible. Interestingly, most local recurrences after neoadjuvant therapy followed by LE occur in cases with tumor-free surgical margins and no lymph node involvement [27, 31]. This highlights that understanding what has been resected does not always equate to knowing what has been left behind, a phenomenon potentially explained by the regression pattern and the presence of MIS.

Our study supports the premises discussed. Of 19 ypT0-2 cases, 17 (89.5%) showed a solid regression pattern and 15 (78.9%) had no MIS. The absence of MIS was associated with ypT0-2 tumors in both bivariate and multivariate analyses. Patients with ypT0-2 tumors had a maximum MIS extension of 8 mm. The two cases with fragmented regression and four cases with MIS were found exclusively in patients with ypT2 tumors. This suggests that, to avoid fragmented regression or MIS, ypT2 cases should be excluded. However, if LE is considered for ypT2 cases, the minimum resection margin should be greater than 8 mm.

Perez et al. [27], analyzing a cohort of patients with rectal cancer treated with LE, identified a solid regression pattern in 63% of cases and absence of MIS in 47%, with a maximum MIS extension of 5.6 mm. Our data show some differences compared with theirs, which can be attributed to important variations between the studies. Specifically, the study by Perez et al. [27] included patients with ypT3 tumors (representing 20% of their sample) and excluded patients with a pCR. In contrast, our study excluded ypT3 cases, and 5 of the 19 patients with ypT0-2 tumors achieved a pCR.

Understanding the distribution of RTCs is also crucial. Defining their location and concentration through a distribution pattern can help identify optimal biopsy sites and areas where imaging resolution needs to be enhanced. For instance, staging a patient as ypT1 implies that RTCs have reached the submucosa, but not necessarily the mucosa. Similarly, in ypT2 cases, RTCs are found in the muscularis propria, but not necessarily in the mucosa or submucosa. In our study, all three patients staged as ypT1 had RTCs in the mucosa. Among the 11 ypT2 cases, RTCs were present in the mucosa in 10 (90.9%) and in the submucosa in 9 (81.8%). Our results differ from those published by Duldulao et al. [32], who reported the presence of RTCs in the mucosa in only 33% of ypT1 cases, while for ypT2 cases, they identified RTCs in the mucosa in 15% and in the submucosa in 56%. The discrepancy between the results may be attributed to the use of different neoadjuvant CRT regimens, as well as to variations in the time intervals between the initiation of neoadjuvant therapy and surgery.

Type I was the most common RTC distribution pattern, observed in 84.2% of potential candidates for LE. This finding was expected, since our study population consisted primarily of patients with early-stage tumors, where RTCs are typically located in the mucosa and submucosa. It is important to note that the inclusion of cases achieving a pCR within the type I distribution pattern further contributed to its higher prevalence. We found no published studies on RTC distribution patterns for comparison with our data.

Our investigation into factors associated with ypT0-2 tumors identified five preoperative variables that are easily identifiable in clinical practice: initial clinical stage II—earlier-stage cases are associated with improved response to neoadjuvant therapy, and stage II, specifically, indicates no lymph node involvement, supporting LE; stage 0 (pCR) or stage I on post-CRT restaging and MRI TRG 1–2—these conditions indicate an excellent response to neoadjuvant therapy and correlate strongly with the occurrence of ypT0-2 tumors; tumor size < 3 cm on restaging MRI—smaller tumors on restaging are associated with earlier-stage cases and are one of the criteria for LE indication; and downsizing > 50%—this reflects a good response to neoadjuvant therapy and correlates with less advanced tumors [15, 33–35].

Bivariate analysis also identified anatomopathologic factors associated with the occurrence of ypT0-2 tumors: solid regression pattern and absence of MIS—these factors are consistent with cases suitable for LE, as previously published [18, 28–30]; RTC type I distribution—this pattern is expected in ypT0-2 cases, as both indicate tumors limited to the most superficial layers of the rectal wall; pathologic TRG 1–2—this indicates an excellent response to neoadjuvant therapy, consistent with cases amenable to LE [36]; and low or no tumor budding and no lymphovascular invasion—these are current criteria for LE indication [16].

Our study has limitations. Its real-world setting made it challenging to accurately control for variables such as the timing of restaging, completion of all cycles of consolidation chemotherapy, and timing of surgery. Additionally, the sample size was small, particularly for potential candidates for LE. Also, for the analysis of factors associated with the occurrence of ypT0-2 tumors, ypT0 (pCR) cases were grouped into cases with a solid regression pattern. We acknowledge that no RTCs are present in pCR cases and, therefore, no regression pattern can be directly assessed. However, this approach was adopted owing to the limited sample size and the characteristics of homogeneous regression and more favorable prognosis shared by pCR cases and cases with a solid regression pattern compared with fragmented regression patterns. Another limitation is that, although this study addressed LE, no LE procedures were performed. However, our primary objective was to identify potential LE candidates based on tumor regression-distribution patterns in ypT0-2 cases, rather than to demonstrate LE feasibility or outcomes. Also important, this study was designed with a focus on anatomopathologic rather than clinical end points (e.g., local recurrence, survival, or follow-up), which may limit its clinical applicability. The neoadjuvant regimen, similar to that of the RAPIDO trial [28], could also be seen as a limitation owing to its association with higher local recurrence rates. However, the decision to use the RAPIDO regimen in this study was made 1 year before the data on higher locoregional recurrence were published. At the time, in addition to its presumed oncologic safety, the regimen offered practical advantages, including a shorter treatment duration and reduced radiotherapy resource utilization. At the same time, we believe it is relevant to emphasize that this CRT regimen allowed us to use the watch-and-wait strategy in four cases (with no tumor regrowth to date) and could potentially enable LE in another 19 patients. This means that nearly half of the patients could potentially benefit from rectum-sparing strategies. Strengths of our study include its prospective design, a comprehensive anatomopathologic examination protocol, and examinations conducted by a dedicated gastrointestinal pathologist, which enhance the reliability of our findings. The study also provides valuable insights into the patterns of regression and distribution of RTCs in patients with tumors amenable to LE, a topic not well documented in the literature.

Conclusions

In this study, ypT0-2 cases were characterized by a predominantly solid regression pattern and absence of MIS. No lymph node involvement was observed in this subgroup. Resection margins should be greater than 8 mm, a practice already routinely implemented. Furthermore, the criteria associated with ypT0-2 cases align with previous research.
