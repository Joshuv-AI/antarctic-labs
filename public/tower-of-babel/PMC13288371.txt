Association Between Geomagnetic Activity and Unsuccessful Out‐of‐Hospital Cardiac Arrest
Authors: Jone Vencloviene, Asta Krikscionaitiene, Linas Darginavicius, Deimante Baksevice, Egle Vaitkaitiene, Dinas Vaitkaitis, Paulius Dobozinskas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOut‐of‐hospital cardiac arrests (OHCAs) represent a significant global health challenge, with a survival rate <10%. Recent research has demonstrated that geomagnetic activity (GMA) can disrupt the circadian rhythm. Therefore, GMA may affect patient outcomes after an OHCA event wherein resuscitation is attempted. This study involved a retrospective analysis of the Emergency Medical Services (EMS) patient call records and clinical data collected from the Kaunas (Lithuania) EMS digital databases from 1 January 2016 to 31 December 2021. Multivariate logistic regression was used to analyze the association between GMA and the risk of absence of return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) on the scene, adjusting for potential confounders. Among the 1,507 patients evaluated, 66.6% were male and 47.2% were aged <70 years. A shockable rhythm (SHR) was identified in 436 patients (28.9%), while only 19.9% of patients in the unsuccessful resuscitation group had an SHR. In patients without SHR, a lower rate of ROSC was observed during the period from the second and subsequent days of a geomagnetic storm (GS) to 2 days after the end of the GS. During these days and on days with higher GMA, SHR was associated with a higher probability of achieving ROSC. The impact of SHR on the ROSC was statistically non‐significant in the days of GS onset. The association between the risk of absence of ROSC at the scene and higher GMA levels or different GS periods was modified by SHR, with a stronger effect modification observed in males and in patients with a presumed cardiac cause.

Introduction

In recent decades, studies have reported a statistical association between geomagnetic activity (GMA) and cardiovascular variables, including heart rate (Papailiou et al., 2023; Wanliss et al., 2018), heart rate variability (Alabdulgader et al., 2018; Dimitrova et al., 2013; Zilli Vieira et al., 2022), and arterial blood pressure (Azcárate et al., 2016; Dimitrova et al., 2004; Wang et al., 2021). Moreover, increased GMA, especially geomagnetic storms (GSs), reportedly increases the risk of adverse cardiovascular events, including acute myocardial infarction (Cornelissen et al., 2002; Shaposhnikov et al., 2014; Vencloviene et al., 2020), stroke (Feigin et al., 2014; Shaposhnikov et al., 2014; Vencloviene et al., 2021), sudden cardiac death (Stoupel et al., 2002), and cardiovascular mortality (Caswell et al., 2016; Zilli Vieira et al., 2019). Previous studies have found that geomagnetic field (GMF) variation may act as a “zeitgeber” (i.e., any external or environmental cue that entrains or synchronizes an organism's biological rhythms) (Cremer‐Bartels et al., 1984; Liboff, 2014), similar to light and temperature (Martel et al., 2023). In the past decade, an increasing number of animal and human studies have found that GMA disrupts the circadian rhythm (Chai et al., 2023; Krylov, 2017; Martel et al., 2023; McCraty et al., 2017; Tracy et al., 2022). The onset of melatonin production is commonly used as the gold‐standard for the circadian system (St Hilaire & Lockley, 2022). Elevated GMA has been linked to lower nocturnal melatonin concentration and reduced overnight melatonin secretion (Burch et al., 2008; Weydahl et al., 2001).

Out‐of‐hospital cardiac arrests (OHCAs) represent a significant global health challenge, with a survival rate of <10% (Darginavicius et al., 2022; Gräsner et al., 2026). As a critical medical emergency and a substantial public health issue, it necessitates the identification of risk factors. This includes understanding the influence of short‐term environmental changes on OHCA incidence and outcomes. As the circadian system controls various physiological parameters and disruptions in the circadian rhythm affect cardiovascular parameters, thereby increasing cardiovascular risk (Chellappa et al., 2019; Fishbein et al., 2021; Tripathi et al., 2020), there is a possibility that GMA affects the risk of OHCA and even post‐OHCA outcomes when resuscitation is attempted.

This study aimed to assess the risk of non‐successful resuscitation at the scene with GMA and to examine the shockable rhythms as a potential effect modifier for the impact of GMA on the risk of not achieving successful resuscitation at the scene by using Emergency Medical Services digital databases from 1 January 2016 to 31 December 2021. To date, the associations of OHCA events with GMA has not been analyzed. Our results could help to detect the specific GMA conditions linked to the risk of an OHCA event, informing better outcomes after resuscitation.

Methods

Study Design

We conducted a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected data from Kaunas Emergency Medical Services (EMS) attended OHCA cases in which resuscitation was attempted from 1 January 2016 to 31 December 2021, excluding the period from 1 January 2020, to 30 March 2020, when data were unavailable because the EMS transitioned to a new electronic database system provider. EMS dispatcher and patient care records were collected from the Kaunas EMS digital databases. Call records and clinical data from on‐scene EMS teams were reviewed and analyzed. We used data from 1,507 patients, among whom 1,044 were males and 711 were aged <70 years.

The primary outcome of this study was risk of unsuccessful resuscitation, defined as failure to achieve return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) at the scene in OHCA patients treated during the study period. We assessed the association between this outcome and various levels of GMA, utilizing the presence of a shockable rhythm (SHR) as a potential effect modifier for the association.

(ROSC) at the scene is defined as the restoration of a palpable pulse and measurable blood pressure sustained long enough for further stabilization or hospital transport. ROSC at the scene is a critical indicator of initial resuscitation success and has been associated with improved survival rates and neurological outcomes (Wnent et al., 2015).

An initial SHR (ventricular fibrillation or pulseless ventricular tachycardia) is a known independent predictor of OHCA survival, and it is treated only by defibrillation (Darginavicius et al., 2022; Gräsner et al., 2026; Søholm et al., 2015).

Analyses were performed for males and females, for patients with and without a presumed cardiac cause, and for patients aged <70 years and ≥70 years. Furthermore, we also analyzed the impact of GMA on the characteristics of patients that were resuscitated.

Ethical Approval

Ethical approval for this study was granted by the Lithuanian University of Health Sciences on 13 July 2020 (approval no. BE‐2‐270). Informed consent was not required. Additionally, the study was registered in the ClinicalTrials.gov database under the identifier NCT04784117, with the Unique Protocol ID LITOHCA, covering the period from 2016 to 2021.

Geomagnetic and Other Environmental Data

As a measure of GMA, we used the geomagnetic planetary 3‐hr index Kp obtained from the website of GFZ German Research Center for Geosciences (https://www‐app3.gfz‐potsdam.de/kp_index/Kp_ap_Ap_SN_F107_since_1932.txt). The planetary 3‐hr‐range index Kp is the mean standardised K‐index from 13 geomagnetic observatories between 44 and 60° northern or southern geomagnetic latitude. The K‐index quantifies disturbances in the horizontal component of Earth's magnetic field with an integer in the range of 0–9 (https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/products/planetary‐k‐index). We used the definition of GS according to the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration scale (https://www.swpc.noaa.gov/noaa‐scales‐explanation): a 3‐hourly Kp of 5 or more. The day of GS was defined if 3‐hourly Kp ≥ 5 had occurred during 24 hr of local time. If 3‐hourly Kp was expressed in a scale of thirds, then GS occurred when 3‐hourly Kp ≥ 4.67. In the analysis, the sum of over a day of the 3‐hr Kp index (Kpsum) on the day of OHCA (lag 0), on the previous day (lag 1), on the second day before OHCA (lag 2), and the mean value of the Kpsum of the above days (a lag of 0–2 days) were used. The variables of Kpsum were used as continuous and categorized by quartiles. In addition, we analyzed the impact of the first day of GSs (days of GS onset), the second and subsequent days of GSs (if the GS lasted more than 1 day), and the first and second day after the GS. If the interval between two consecutive GS lasted 1 day, such period of sequential GS was defined as one GS.

To eliminate the effect of other environmental conditions on the OHCA outcomes, we used data of daily air temperature and relative humidity obtained from the Kaunas Meteorological Station, as well as daily mean fine particulate matter (defined as particles that are ≤2.5 microns in diameter [PM2.5]) concentrations from the freely available webpage of the Environmental Protection Agency (https://aaa.lrv.lt/lt/veiklos‐sritys/oras/oro‐kokybes‐statistika‐ir‐duomenys/).

Statistical Analysis

The associations between two categorical variables (patients characteristics, ROSC, SHR, and GMA variables) were assessed using the chi‐square (χ2) test. The association between GMA and the presence of SHR during resuscitation was evaluated using multivariate logistic regression. In the model, the covariates were included: years and the month as categorical predictors, as well as the daily air temperature and relative humidity, age, sex, witnessed arrest, etiology of cardiac arrest, and time of EMS departure to the scene.

Multivariate logistic regression was employed to analyze the associations between GMA (exposure variables) and the risk of not achieving ROSC on the scene (outcome). We created models separately for patients with and without SHR and created regression models with the interaction term between SHR and GMA variables. In the models, the following covariates were included: years and the month as categorical predictors, and the daily PM2.5 concentration, air temperature, relative humidity, age, sex, SHR in the models with interaction, presumed cardiac cause, and witnessed arrest. As GMA variables, we used the Kpsum values and assessed the risk of absence of ROSC during different time intervals around GSs, as compared with other days. We assessed how SHR modifies the associations between GS/GMA levels and the absence of ROSC.

In the logistic regression models, to assess the impact of GMA, we evaluated the adjusted odds ratios (ORs) at the relevant 95% CIs and p‐values. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 20 software (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Discussion

This study is the first to report an association between GMA and the risk of non‐successful resuscitation at the scene. When a GS occurs, the second and subsequent days of GS and the days immediately following the storm (up to 2 days later) are associated with a significantly higher chance that patients who suffer an OHCA without an SHR will not regain a pulse on the scene (i.e., no ROSC). We found that elevated GMA levels and the occurrence of a GS influenced the effect of an SHR on the risk of no ROSC at the scene. Age and sex may also modify the association between GMA and the risk of non‐successful resuscitation. These results were obtained adjusting for time trend, seasonality, weather variables, and patients' characteristics. Apart from this, on days of higher GMA levels and 1–2 days after, we found a higher rate of OHCA patients with presumed cardiac cause.

Days with higher GMA levels were associated with a higher probability of achieving ROSC in patients with SHR, except on the first day of a GS. On the first day of a GS event, no statistically significant protective impact of SHR on the risk of death at the scene was observed. However, SHR could be considered “strongly protective” during the period involving the second and subsequent days of a GS to 2 days after the end of the GS. These results may be attributed to the different physical conditions during and after a GS, which affect the human physiology in a variety of ways.

According to previous studies, diurnal geomagnetic variation acts as secondary zeitgeber for the circadian rhythm, in addition to the primary night‐day light cycle (Krylov, 2017; Martel et al., 2023). Thus, GSs or substorms disrupt the circadian rhythm, thereby affecting the central and autonomic nervous systems (ANS) (McCraty et al., 2017; Zenchenko et al., 2025; Zilli Vieira et al., 2022). The results of previous experimental studies have suggested that geomagnetic variations in the ultra‐low frequency (ULF) (<5 Hz) range serve as a rhythm sensor for humans. In healthy individuals, the synchronization between the heart rate and R‐wave parameters and GMF variation in the frequency range of 0.5–3.0 mHz was observed (Zenchenko et al., 2014, 2024), and the human heart‐rate synchronization with variations in the GMF in the 1–5‐mHz range during GS was detected (Zenchenko et al., 2025). Thus, it has been hypothesized that geomagnetic variations in the ULF range serve as a rhythm sensor for healthy individuals (Zenchenko et al., 2025).

Enhanced solar wind velocity, large pressure variability, and intense magnetospheric particle injections before and during GS generate geomagnetic pulsations in the ULF range. Some of these pulsations may explain the impact of space weather conditions on human health. Pc1 pulsation is comprised of continuous ultra‐low‐frequency waves with a frequency range of 0.2–5 Hz, that is, in the human heart rhythm range. Pc5 pulsations occur in the morning sector of the magnetosphere and lie within a frequency range of approximately 2–7 mHz. ULF pulsations indicated that the properties of ULF activity vary over time during a GS. For Pc5 and daytime Pc1–Pc2 (0.2–5 Hz, which overlap normal heartbeat frequencies), during the onset of a GS, these pulsations tend to be broadband (with lots of chaotic noise) (Engebretson et al., 2008; Posch et al., 2003), potentially interfering with the ability of defibrillation to “reset” an SHR. During the GS recovery phase, as the magnetosphere recovered to a quiet state, Pc5 pulsations become mainly narrowband in the dawn–noon (Posch et al., 2003). This may even enhance synchronization between the shock of a defibrillator and the electrical cycle of the heart, thus restoring ROSC more effectively.

The occurrence of Pc1–Pc2 and Pc5 waves increases during the GS recovery phase (Engebretson et al., 2008; Posch et al., 2003). We observed a higher rate of ROSC at the scene on the day after the GS onset in patients with an SHR. The strongest “protective effect” of an SHR was observed after the first day of GS. This suggests that an increase in the narrowband frequency GMF pulsations in Pc1 and Pc5 positively influenced the work of defibrillators during the GS recovery phase. In addition, the synchronization of human heart rhythm with GMF variation in the Pc5 frequency range mainly manifested during the GS recovery phase (Zenchenko et al., 2025). In addition, GMA has been shown to influence the autonomic nervous system, particularly heart rate variability, which reflects sympathovagal balance (Alabdulgader et al., 2018; Dimitrova et al., 2013; Zilli Vieira et al., 2022). Different studies report increased parasympathetic activity or altered ANS balance during periods of elevated geomagnetic activity. Reduced excessive sympathetic tone may stabilize myocardial electrophysiology, lowering the likelihood of the degeneration of shockable rhythms into non‐shockable rhythms (asystole/PEA), thereby maintaining a window for successful defibrillation. However, this is only one possible explanation. The answer is not clear and needs further research.

According to our results, the period comprising the second and subsequent days of a GS to 2 days after the end of a GS was associated with a higher risk of the absence of ROSC on the scene in patients without an SHR, especially in females and patients with presumed cardiac cause. According to results of numerous studies, individuals with cardiac diseases have poorer health characteristics in response to GMA levels (Chai et al., 2023), including lower melatonin levels, reduced heart rate variability (HRV), ANS imbalance, and disruption in circadian rhythm. Patients with coronary artery disease, hypertension, and metabolic disorders have low melatonin levels and reduced HRV (Dominguez‐Rodriguez et al., 2010; Sammito et al., 2024). Females generally exhibit stronger daily melatonin fluctuations (Gunn et al., 2016) and different autonomic patterns and metabolic disorders (Sammito et al., 2024), which may amplify storm‐related rhythm disturbances (and blunt defibrillation effectiveness) in the days after a storm. It is possible that a higher intensity of Pc1 and Pc5 pulsations had a negative impact on patients with presumed cardiac cause and females due to the lower ability of the ANS to adapt to external challenges, such as via the circadian rhythm, making it more susceptible to geomagnetic perturbations.

Differences in the associations between GMA levels and the ROSC at the scene between males and females were observed. During days with higher GMA levels after a GS, the additional protective impact of SHR was observed only in male patients. This may be explained by differences in circadian variation and melatonin concentration dynamics between males and females (Cain et al., 2010; Gunn et al., 2016), as well as sex differences in the HRV parameters throughout the day (Walton et al., 2022) and differences in the intrinsic circadian period, which is shorter in females (Duffy et al., 2011). Differences in outcomes after OHCA by sex have also been observed in previous studies, with males having been found to be more likely to experience OHCA, but also more likely to survive such events (Darginavicius et al., 2022; Gräsner et al., 2020).

In the present study, days with higher GMA (Kpsum > median) were associated with a higher proportion of OHCAs attributed to a primary cardiac event. In cardiovascular diseases, melatonin production is also significantly lower (Dominguez‐Rodriguez et al., 2010). Higher GMA levels are associated with lower total overnight melatonin levels (Burch et al., 2008). The impact of waned melatonin production may provoke an OHCA event in some individuals.

Limitations

The study is based on retrospective observational single‐center data from the Kaunas EMS. Consequently, the findings may not be generalizable to other regions or populations. Unmeasured confounders (e.g., individual comorbidities, medication use, lifestyle factors, and socioeconomic status) are likely to influence the survival of patients with OHCA or may be effect modifiers. Other environmental conditions, such as individual exposure to PM2.5 or other air pollutants may have adverse health effects or may also act as effect modifiers, for example, PM2.5 due to particle radioactivity. Because the patients and some environmental factors may have modified the associations between GMA and ROSC, the sample size is another potential limitation of this work. In addition, the study period of 6 years (2016–2021) may not fully reflect long‐term trends or variations in GMA and its effects. GMA follows approximately 11‐year solar cycles; thus, a longer study period may reveal different patterns. In addition, we did not consider the origin of the GSs and the impact of other extreme space weather events, such as solar proton events and high‐speed solar winds, which may also have an impact on human health.

Conclusions

We found that the impact of higher GMA levels and different GS phases on the risk of no ROSC at the scene was modified by the presence of SHR. The presence of a shockable rhythm (SHR) was associated with a higher probability of achieving ROSC during days of elevated GMA and during the period comprising the second and subsequent days of a GS to 2 days after the end of the GS. GMA levels appeared to exert a stronger effect in male patients and in patients with a presumed cardiac cause. These results suggest that GMA may be considered a background environmental factor influencing the success of resuscitation efforts in OHCA, which could be valuable for the organisation of EMS systems, particularly for quality improvement initiatives and the temporal analysis of resuscitation outcomes. These insights encourage further research into the physiological mechanisms underlying the influence of GMA on cardiac function and resuscitation response. If confirmed in larger, multicentre studies, space weather forecasting could become a novel tool for EMS to anticipate days with a higher risk of OHCA.
