Electrical, thermal, optical, and magnetic properties of ZTO nanocomposites in a PVA matrix: functional correlations with non-thermal structural modifications
Authors: M M Saadeldin, Sabreen J Salim, K A Abdelghany
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractZTO/PVA nanocomposite sheets were successfully fabricated using precipitation and sonochemical routes for ZnO and TiO2 nanoparticle synthesis, respectively, followed by solution casting into the PVA matrix. This process ensured uniform dispersion of ZTO nanoparticles within the polymer matrix and was completed without post-mixing thermal calcination, representing an energy-efficient and novel synthesis route. XRD analysis confirmed the incorporation of nanoparticles and revealed an anatase-to-rutile phase transformation of TiO2 after composite formation. XPS spectra confirmed the presence of Zn, O, and Ti LMM signals, indicating the presence of oxide components within the composite structure. Optical analysis showed a significant reduction in the band gap after ZTO incorporation. The indirect band gap decreased from 3.03 eV for pure PVA to 2.93, 2.72, and 2.5 eV for 2, 5, and 8 wt% ZTO/PVA, respectively, while the direct band gap decreased from 4.59 to 2.72 eV at higher filler loading. Photoluminescence results showed a slight red shift for 2 and 5 wt% ZTO/PVA due to interfacial defect states, followed by a blue shift at 8 wt% associated with near-band-edge emission and phase transformation effects. AFM analysis revealed a significant increase in surface roughness from 9.46 to 131.97 nm as ZTO content increased, indicating nanoparticle aggregation and surface heterogeneity. Thermal analysis demonstrated improved thermal stability and multi-step degradation behaviour. Dielectric measurements showed high permittivity at low frequencies due to interfacial polarisation, with non-Debye relaxation behaviour confirmed by Havriliak–Negami and Cole–Cole analysis. Magnetic measurements indicated predominantly non-magnetic behaviour with a slight enhancement after ZTO incorporation. These findings demonstrate that the addition of ZTO effectively tailors the structural, optical, thermal, dielectric, and magnetic properties of PVA nanocomposites, making them promising for multifunctional dielectric and optoelectronic applications.Keywords: ZTO/PVA nanocomposites, Havriliak–Negami (HN) model, Cole–Cole plot, Optical properties, Thermal stability, Rutile stabilization

Introduction

Polymer-based nanocomposites incorporating multifunctional metal oxides have emerged as promising materials for advanced optoelectronic, energy storage, sensing, and flexible electronic applications due to their tunable structural and functional properties. Among various polymer matrices, poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) is widely utilized owing to its excellent film-forming ability, optical transparency, chemical stability, and strong interaction capability with inorganic fillers through hydrogen bonding and coordination effects [1–3]. Titanium dioxide (TiO2) is one of the most extensively studied oxide materials because of its outstanding optical, dielectric, and photocatalytic characteristics. Its physical properties are strongly dependent on its crystalline phase, where anatase and rutile represent the most technologically relevant polymorphs. Anatase is generally metastable and exhibits superior surface-related activity, whereas rutile is thermodynamically stable and often preferred for electronic and dielectric applications [4–6]. Conventionally, the phase transition from Anatase to rutile requires high-temperature calcination, typically above 600–700 °C, which severely restricts the integration of TiO2 into polymer matrices and increases energy consumption [7–9].

To overcome these limitations, recent research has focused on low-temperature strategies for phase engineering of TiO2 through compositional modification, interfacial stress, defect generation, and heterostructure formation. In this context, zinc-based oxides have received considerable attention due to their ability to modify the local lattice environment of TiO2 and alter its phase stability. The incorporation of ZnO into TiO2-containing systems has been reported to induce lattice distortion, oxygen vacancies, and interfacial strain, which can significantly influence crystallization behavior even at reduced processing temperatures [10–12].

Several studies have reported ZnO- or TiO2-filled polymer nanocomposites with enhanced optical, electrical, and dielectric properties. For instance, TiO2/PVA nanocomposites have been shown to exhibit improved optical band gap and nonlinear optical behavior due to the strong interaction between TiO2 nanoparticles and the polymer matrix. Similarly, ZnO nanoparticles incorporated into PVA films have demonstrated reduced optical band gap and enhanced optical conductivity. Furthermore, hybrid TiO2/ZnO-based polymer nanocomposites have shown synergistic improvements in optical absorption and dielectric performance resulting from the interaction between the two oxide nanofillers within the polymer network [13, 14].

In our previous work, the phase stability of TiO2 in ZTO/PVA nanocomposites was systematically investigated, demonstrating the critical role of zinc–titanium oxide interactions in stabilizing specific crystalline phases within a polymeric environment. Building upon these findings, the present study advances this concept by exploring a ZTO/PVA nanocomposite system in which ZnO and TiO2 coexist within the PVA matrix. The polymer–oxide interface plays a decisive role in determining the structural evolution of nanocomposites. Strong hydrogen bonding between PVA chains and oxide surfaces, coupled with nanoscale confinement effects, can impose internal stress and modify surface energy, thereby influencing phase stability [15–17]. In recent years, several studies have investigated oxide–polymer nanocomposites based on TiO2 or ZnO for improving electrical, optical, and dielectric properties. However, most reported systems rely primarily on thermally induced structural modifications achieved through high-temperature annealing processes. Such approaches not only increase energy consumption but also limit the compatibility of oxide nanostructures with temperature-sensitive polymer matrices [18]. Moreover, the correlation between structural phase evolution and multifunctional physical properties in oxide–polymer hybrid systems remains insufficiently understood, particularly when phase transitions occur without external thermal treatment. Therefore, developing alternative low-energy routes for controlling phase stability and exploring their impact on electrical, thermal, optical, and magnetic properties remains a significant challenge in the field of polymer-based nanocomposites [19].

Accordingly, the main objectives of this study are the following:

i.to synthesize ZTO/PVA nanocomposites through a low-energy processing route,

to synthesize ZTO/PVA nanocomposites through a low-energy processing route,

Chemicals

Zinc acetate dihydrate (Zn(CH3COO)2·2H2O, 99.5% purity, LANXESS AG, Germany), aqueous ammonia solution (NH4OH, 34%, El Naser Chemical Co., Egypt), polyvinyl alcohol (PVA (CH2CH)n), and titanium dioxide (TiO2) were used without further purification.

Experimental

Preparation of ZnO nanoparticles

Zinc oxide (ZnO) nanoparticles were prepared using a precipitation route. Zinc acetate dihydrate was selected as the zinc source, while ammonium hydroxide solution (33%) served as the precipitating agent. Initially, an aqueous zinc precursor solution with a concentration of 0.2 M was obtained by dissolving the required amount of zinc acetate in 200 mL of double-deionized water. The solution was exposed to ultrasonic irradiation using a Hielscher UP400S ultrasonic processor operating at 400 W and 24 kHz, with an amplitude of 80% and a duty cycle of 0.8, for 10 min at 60 °C. During ultrasonication, ammonium hydroxide was added dropwise until complete precipitation occurred, leading to the formation of a white Zn(OH)2 precipitate. The precipitate was separated and repeatedly washed three times with double-deionized water to remove any residual ionic species, followed by air drying at ambient conditions for 24 h. Preparation of TiO2 nanoparticles using the sonochemical method

Titanium dioxide (TiO2) nanoparticles were synthesized through a sonochemical process. In a typical synthesis, 0.5 g of TiO2 powder was dispersed in 100 mL of a 5 M sodium hydroxide (NaOH) aqueous solution to form a homogeneous suspension. The mixture was subjected to high-intensity ultrasonic irradiation at room temperature for 4 h using an ultrasonic processor operated at 90% amplitude and a duty cycle of 0.9, while continuous magnetic stirring was maintained. After completion of the sonication process, the resulting precipitate was collected and thoroughly washed three times with double-deionized water to remove excess alkali residues. The cleaned material was then air-dried for 24 h and calcined at 500 °C for 2 h in a muffle furnace to improve crystallinity and stabilize the TiO2 phase.

The PVA pure and ZTO/PVA nanocomposite preparation

Polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) in granular white form was used as the polymer matrix. A PVA solution was prepared by dissolving 2 g of PVA in 100 mL of distilled water under continuous stirring. The solution was heated to 80 °C until a clear and homogeneous solution was obtained.

Four different samples were prepared: pure PVA and PVA-based nanocomposites containing 2%, 5%, and 8% (w/v) of a ZnO and TiO2 nanoparticle mixture with a 1:1 weight ratio, labeled as 2% ZTO/PVA, 5% ZTO/PVA, and 8% ZTO/PVA, respectively.

For nanocomposite preparation, the required amount of nanoparticles was gradually introduced into the PVA solution, followed by heating at 90–110 °C under continuous stirring for 30–45 min. To ensure uniform dispersion of the nanoparticles within the polymer matrix, the mixtures were further treated using ultrasonic irradiation with a Hielscher UP400S processor (400 W, 24 kHz, 80% amplitude, 0.8 duty cycle) for up to 30 min at 60 °C. The homogeneous suspensions were cast into glass Petri dishes and allowed to dry at room temperature, resulting in flexible, free-standing nanocomposite films with a sheet-like morphology. Table 1. Characterization of PVA and ZTO/PVA nanocomposite

The phase identification of pure PVA and ZTO/PVA nanocomposites was carried out using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Shimadzu XRD-6000, Shimadzu, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 0.15406 nm). The elemental composition and chemical states of the prepared samples were analyzed using X-ray Photoelectron Spectroscopy (XPS) with a K-alpha spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) equipped with a monochromatic Al Kα source.

The surface morphology and compositional analyses were investigated using Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) with a Shimadzu Wet-SPM9600 Scanning Probe Microscope (Shimadzu, Japan) at the Microanalytical Center. The optical properties of pure PVA and ZTO/PVA nanocomposites were studied using UV–Vis spectroscopy. The absorption spectra were recorded using a Shimadzu UV-1800 double-beam spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan) in the wavelength range of 190–1100 nm. Photoluminescence (PL) spectra of the ZTO/PVA nanocomposites were measured using a Jasco FP-6500 spectrofluorometer (Jasco, Japan) equipped with a 150 W xenon arc lamp as the excitation source, with an excitation wavelength of 350 nm. The thermal stability of the ZTO/PVA nanocomposites was investigated using a DTG-60H thermogravimetric analyzer (Shimadzu, Japan), which records weight variations of the samples under controlled heating conditions. The electrical and dielectric properties of the ZTO/PVA nanocomposites (ZTO content: 0–8 wt%), including dielectric relaxation and electrical conductivity, were measured using a broadband dielectric spectrometer (BDS) equipped with a high-resolution Alpha analyzer and an active sample head (Novocontrol Concept 40 GmbH, Germany). The measurements were conducted over a frequency range from 0.1 Hz to 20 MHz at room temperature. Magnetic measurements were carried out using a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM, Lakeshore 7410, USA).

Urbach energy

In order to determine the width of the defect states formed inside the nanocomposite bandgap, we employ the Urbach model [28]. The Urbach energy (Eu) values provide insight into the degree of structural disorder and defect density within the electronic band structure. The Urbach energy (Eu) increases with the dopant concentration, indicating enhanced disorder and the formation of localized states near the band edges [29, 30] as shown in Fig. 7. The variation in band gap (Eg) and Urbach energy (Eu) is a critical analysis used to interpret structural disorder, crystalline quality, and the impact of defects (such as vacancies, doping, or stress) on optical properties. A common finding is an inverse relationship, where a narrowing of the band gap (decrease in Eg) corresponds to a widening of the Urbach tail (increase in EU), signaling increased structural disorder.

Photoluminescence (PL) spectra

Photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy is are plots of light intensity versus energy (hv), showing how materials emit light after absorbing photons, revealing electronic structure, defects, and composites in non-destructive way crucial for semiconductors and nanomaterials. In this study, PL spectra were recorded using a spectrofluorometer (Jasco FP-6500, Japan) equipped with a 150 W xenon arc lamp as the excitation source, with the excitation wavelength set at 350 nm. The obtained PL spectra of pure PVA and ZTO/PVA nanocomposites are presented in Fig. 8. For pure PVA, the emission peak at ~ 416 nm is primarily associated with intrinsic n → π* transitions originating from the non-bonding electrons of hydroxyl and carbonyl groups. Upon the incorporation of 2% and 5% ZTO, this peak shifts slightly to 418–420 nm, indicating a red shift. This behavior is attributed to the interfacial interactions between the polymer chains and the nanoparticles, along with the generation of additional defect or interface states that effectively narrow the bandgap [32]. Interestingly, at 8% ZTO loading, the emission undergoes a pronounced blue-shift to ~ 382 nm. This shift is mainly related to the anatase-to-rutile phase transformation in TiO2, which modifies the electronic band structure and facilitates charge carrier separation. As a result, the PL response becomes dominated by near-band-edge emission resembling that of ZnO, while radiative recombination via defect-related states is significantly suppressed. Therefore, the systematic red-shift observed at lower ZTO concentrations, followed by a strong blue-shift at higher loading, reflects the combined effects of interfacial interactions, defect state evolution, and phase transformation of TiO2 [33]. Additionally, PL spectra recorded under 325 nm excitation (Fig. 7) exhibit multiple emission bands in both the UV and visible regions. Deconvolution of the PVA spectrum using Gaussian fitting reveals peaks centered at 418.7 and 473.3 nm; the emission around 418 nm can be attributed to electronic transitions associated with carbonyl-related groups and intrinsic defects within the PVA polymer structure. The broader emission band located at ~ 473 nm is related to localized states and oxygen-related defect centers within the polymer matrix [34]. When the deconvolution of the 8% ZTO/PVA spectrum using Gaussian fitting reveals peaks centered at 351, 390, 414, and 468 nm. The bands in the visible region can be ascribed to deep-level defects within the oxide lattice, such as zinc and titanium vacancies, oxygen vacancies (V₀), oxygen interstitials (Oᵢ), single ionized oxygen vacancies, and zinc interstitials (Znᵢ). In particular, the emission at ~ 418 nm is generally attributed to the recombination of deeply trapped electrons in oxygen vacancies with photoinduced holes. The emission near 468 nm has also been reported in ZnO-based systems and remains a subject of ongoing debate in the literature [35]. PL analysis confirms that pure PVA exhibits weak emission, whereas the intensity gradually increases with increasing ZTO content [36], reaching its maximum at 8%. This enhancement is directly linked to defect-mediated energy states and improved radiative recombination efficiency [14]. Such behavior highlights the promising potential of ZTO/PVA nanocomposites for advanced optoelectronic and photocatalytic applications.

Thermal properties

Thermal properties were investigated using a DTG-60H, this technique is widely used to evaluate thermal stability and degradation mechanisms of polymer-based nanocomposites. Thermogravimetric (TGA) and differential thermal analyses (DTA) were employed to investigate the thermal stability, decomposition behavior, and phase evolution of ZTO/PVA nanocomposites with different filler loadings [37]. Figure 9a–c present the TGA/DTA curves of ZTO/PVA nanocomposites containing 2%, 5%, and 8% ZTO, highlighting the progressive influence of inorganic fillers on the thermal degradation behavior of the PVA matrix. The comparative analysis indicates that increasing ZTO content leads to enhanced thermal stability, which can be attributed to stronger interfacial interactions between the polymer chains and the ZTO fillers. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of the 8% ZTO/PVA composite Fig. 9d reveals a multi-step weight-loss profile associated with successive thermal events of the polymeric matrix and the embedded inorganic phases. The initial weight loss below ~ 155 °C is attributed to the removal of physically adsorbed moisture and weakly bound hydroxyl groups present in the PVA matrix and at the filler–polymer interfaces. The second and most pronounced weight loss observed between 155 and 340 °C corresponds to the thermal decomposition of organic moieties within the PVA backbone, including dehydration and side-chain elimination processes [38]. A subsequent weight loss in the range of 340–450 °C is associated with further degradation of the PVA main chains, accompanied by partial carbonization and enhanced interfacial interactions between the polymer matrix and the ZTO fillers. The fourth degradation stage, occurring between 450 and 600 °C, is related to the oxidation and decomposition of the remaining carbonaceous residues, while the presence of inorganic fillers results in a delayed and broadened decomposition behavior, indicating improved thermal stability of the composite system [39, 40].

Beyond 600 °C, only a minor weight loss is observed, and the residual mass remains nearly constant up to 800 °C, confirming the formation of thermally stable inorganic phases. In previously reported TiO2-based hybrid systems [41], a weight-loss event at elevated temperatures has been associated with amorphous-to-anatase or anatase-to-rutile phase transformation. Although TGA does not directly provide crystallographic evidence of phase transitions, the high thermal stability observed in the present system suggests the stabilization of the rutile phase of TiO2 at elevated temperatures [42]. Accordingly, the anatase-to-rutile phase transformation is inferred from complementary XRD analysis rather than directly from TGA measurements. The appearance of characteristic rutile reflections in the XRD patterns indicates that the phase transition likely occurred at lower temperatures, facilitated by Zn incorporation, nitrogen-related defects, and the polymer-assisted synthesis route, which collectively reduce the activation energy required for phase transformation [43].

Figure 9e presents the DTA curves of 2%, 5%, and 8% ZTO/PVA composites, revealing distinct thermal events associated with the polymer matrix and inorganic fillers. A weak endothermic feature observed below ~ 150 °C is attributed to the removal of physically adsorbed moisture and residual hydroxyl groups within the PVA matrix. A pronounced exothermic region appearing between ~ 250 and 400 °C corresponds to the thermal decomposition of PVA chains, including dehydration, side-group elimination, and backbone scission. The shift and broadening of this region with increasing ZTO content indicate enhanced interfacial interactions between the polymer and ZTO fillers, which retard polymer degradation. At higher temperatures (~ 400–600 °C), additional exothermic features are observed, particularly for higher filler loadings, which are associated with carbonization and oxidation of residual organic species. The gradual stabilization of the DTA signal beyond ~ 600 °C reflects the dominance of thermally stable inorganic phases. Although DTA does not directly confirm crystallographic phase transitions, the exothermic behavior in this temperature range is consistent with the anatase-to-rutile transformation of TiO2, as supported by XRD analysis, suggesting that Zn incorporation and defect states facilitate the phase transition at reduced temperatures [44].

In the intermediate frequency range (102–105 Hz), the permittivity reveals a step-like dispersion, a signature of interfacial Maxwell–Wagner–Sillars (MWS) polarization typically associated with heterogeneous and multiphase dielectric systems. Such interfacial polarization arises from the contrast in conductivity between the polymer matrix and the ZTO nanoparticles, leading to space-charge accumulation at the ZnO, TiO2, and PVA interfaces. According to the MWS model, grains and grain boundaries coexist within the dielectric structure; grain boundaries act as highly resistive regions that dominate the response at low frequencies, while dipolar relaxation becomes more prominent at higher frequencies [48].

At even higher frequencies (> 105 Hz), ε′ becomes nearly frequency-independent. This plateau indicates that all polarization mechanisms—interfacial, dipolar, ionic, and electronic—fail to follow the rapid oscillations of the applied electric field, resulting in suppression of dielectric fluctuations. In this regime, the intrinsic structure and composition of each sample govern the ε′ values rather than frequency-dependent effects. The reduction in permittivity at high frequencies further reflects the inability of dipoles and hopping charge carriers to reorient fast enough, causing ε′ to collapse into a narrow plateau.

The addition of ZTO nanoparticles leads to an increase in the dielectric permittivity for the 2% and 5% ZTO/PVA composites compared with pure PVA. This enhancement can be attributed to the interfacial (Maxwell–Wagner–Sillars) polarization arising from the accumulation of charges at the interfaces between the polymer matrix and the nanoparticles due to the difference in their electrical conductivities. However, when the filler content increases to 8%, the permittivity decreases slightly. This behavior may be associated with nanoparticle agglomeration and the nano-confinement effect, a similar reduction in dielectric parameters due to nano-confinement induced by nanoparticle incorporation has also been reported in PVA-based polymer blends containing ferrite nanoparticles [47], where interactions between the nanoparticle surface and the hydroxyl (–OH) groups of PVA restrict the segmental mobility of polymer chains, thereby limiting dipolar polarization [49–51].

To better interpret the dielectric performance, XRD-derived microstructural parameters were analyzed. Crystallite size calculations at the ZTO/PVA peak (2θ ≈ 36°) yield average values of 17.99 nm (2% ZTO/PVA), 19.73 nm (5% ZTO/PVA), and 17.81 nm (8% ZTO/PVA). The corresponding dislocation densities (δ ≈ 1/D2) are ~ 0.00309, 0.00257, and 0.00315 nm−2, respectively. The 5% ZTO/PVA sample thus exhibits the largest crystallite size and the lowest dislocation density, although its microstrain values indicate localized lattice distortion, which provides additional interfacial polarization sites [46]. This combination relatively large and well-ordered crystallites, along with localized strain and an optimized distribution of grain boundaries, creates the most effective interfacial network for charge accumulation. Consequently, the 5% ZTO/PVA composition exhibits the highest dielectric permittivity ε′ across the measured frequency range. In contrast, the 8% ZTO/PVA sample, despite having a high dislocation density and strong lattice distortion, suffers from excessive structural disorder and possible nanoparticle agglomeration, which suppresses interfacial polarization efficiency. The 2% ZTO/PVA sample, with fewer structural defects, offers fewer polarization centres. Thus, the superior dielectric response of the 5% ZTO/PVA composite arises from its balanced microstructure, optimal defect concentration, and enhanced Maxwell–Wagner–Sillars interfacial polarization [52]. To provide an overall understanding of the obtained results, a correlation between the structural, optical, and dielectric properties can be established. The AFM results further support this behavior, showing a significant increase in surface roughness and grain development with increasing ZTO content, which enhances the interfacial area and facilitates charge trapping at the polymer–nanoparticle interfaces. In addition, the optical analysis reveals a progressive reduction in the optical band gap together with an increase in Urbach energy with increasing ZTO concentration, indicating the formation of localized states and structural disorder within the polymer matrix. These localized states can act as polarization centers and contribute to charge carrier trapping, thereby influencing the dielectric response. Therefore, the improved dielectric performance of the 5% ZTO/PVA composite arises from a balanced microstructure characterized by optimized crystallite size, moderate structural disorder, and enhanced interfacial polarization, while excessive disorder and nanoparticle agglomeration at higher loading (8%) reduce the efficiency of polarization.

The dielectric loss (ε″) and loss tangent (tan δ) spectra of pure PVA and ZTO/PVA nanocomposites (Fig. 10b, c) exhibit clear relaxation peaks in the intermediate frequency region, attributed to segmental motion of PVA chains and interfacial polarization due to ZTO incorporation. The relaxation time (τ = 1/2πfₘ) decreases with increasing ZTO content, indicating enhanced charge mobility and faster dipolar response [53].

The relaxation behavior deviates from ideal Debye behavior and is well described by the Havriliak–Negami model (Eq. 11), confirming a distribution of relaxation times due to structural heterogeneity (Fig. 10d). The dielectric strength (Δε) increases with ZTO loading, reflecting enhanced interfacial polarization (Maxwell–Wagner effect), while the rise in σ₀ suggests improved charge transport. The relaxation frequency remains around ~ 105 Hz, indicating preserved relaxation mechanisms. Variations in B and G further support non-Debye behavior. The electric modules of ZTO/PVA nanocomposite

The electric modulus formalism was employed to further investigate the relaxation dynamics of pure PVA and ZTO/PVA nanocomposites. The real (M′) and imaginary (M″) parts of the electric modulus were derived from the complex permittivity using the relation M(ω) = 1/ε*(ω) = M′ + iM″. As illustrated in Fig. 11a, the low values of M′ observed at low frequencies indicate an effective suppression of electrode polarization effects. With increasing frequency, M′ gradually increases and approaches a steady-state plateau, reflecting the transition from long-range charge mobility to localized relaxation dynamics. The M″ spectra and the HN fitting of 8% ZTO/PVA, shown in Fig. 11b and c, exhibit pronounced relaxation peaks in the intermediate frequency region, analogous to those observed in ε″ and tanδ spectra [54]. These relaxation features are attributed to the α-relaxation process associated with the segmental motion of PVA polymer chains, influenced by interfacial polarization arising from the presence of ZTO nanoparticles. The modulus relaxation time (τm) was evaluated using the relation τm = 1/(2πfm), where fm corresponds to the frequency at which the M″ peak occurs. A systematic decrease in τM with increasing ZTO content was observed, indicating enhanced charge carrier mobility and faster relaxation dynamics due to improved interfacial interactions within the nanocomposite system. The broad and asymmetric nature of the M″ peaks further confirms a non-Debye type relaxation behavior, consistent with the Havriliak–Negami model, as shown in Fig. 11c; the parameters of fitting are shown in Table 4 [55].

The Nyquist plots of pure PVA and ZTO/PVA composites with different filler concentrations are shown in Fig. 12a. In the Nyquist plot, the observed semicircle represents the bulk relaxation process, arising from the parallel combination of resistance and capacitance in the polymer matrix. It reflects charge transport through the grains and interfacial regions, governed by bulk resistance and interfacial polarization. The incorporation of conductive nanoparticles forms efficient pathways for charge carriers, reducing charge transfer resistance and enhancing carrier mobility. Interactions between the nanoparticle surfaces and polymer chains further promote interfacial polarization and hopping conduction between localized states, improving the overall electrical conductivity of the composite [56–58].

Figure 12b shows the variation of AC conductivity sigma (σ′) with frequency for pure PVA and ZTO/PVA composites. Although the referenced study investigated in reference [59], the general frequency-dependent AC conductivity behavior is consistent with that observed for the ZTO/PVA composites in the present work. In both systems, low conductivity at low frequencies is attributed to polarization effects and interfacial charge accumulation, while an increase in conductivity at higher frequencies is observed due to the reduction of polarization effects. This behavior can be explained by hopping conduction mechanisms between localized states, which are commonly reported in polymer-based composites containing ceramic fillers, independent of the presence of metallic additives [60].

AC conductivity increases with higher frequencies because the rapid oscillation of the electric field enhances the hopping of charge carriers between localized states, where higher frequencies enable carriers to overcome energy barriers more frequently. In other words, At higher frequencies, electrons or ions (such as oxygen vacancies) gain energy to jump between localized sites more frequently, enhancing mobility. On the other hand, the alternating current (AC) conductivity decreases at low frequencies in the dielectric spectra because the dominant mechanism shifts from free conduction/hopping at high frequencies to dependence on space charge polarization and direct current (DC) conductivity. At low frequencies, the charges do not have sufficient energy for continuous hopping, and increasing the frequency inhibits this transition.

VSM analysis of pure PVA and ZTO/PVA nanocomposites:

The pure PVA sample in Fig. 13a shows a very modest magnetic response, which is in line with insulating polymers' intrinsic non-magnetic nature. It has an extremely low squareness ratio (~ 0.0053), an almost insignificant remanent magnetization (Mr = 2.57 × 10−2 emu/g), and a very modest saturation magnetization (Ms ≈ 0.0478 emu/g). The absence of any stable magnetic ordering is further confirmed by the low coercivity (Hc = 72.4 G), which suggests that the measured signal is primarily caused by trace impurities or small surface-related contributions within the polymer matrix [61, 62]. In comparison to pure PVA, the 8% ZTO/PVA sample in Fig. 13b shows a somewhat increased magnetic response. The sample exhibits a significant increase in coercivity (Hc = 472.9 G), a bigger remanent magnetization (Mr ≈ 1.08 × 10−3 emu/g), and a higher saturation magnetization (Ms ≈ 0.0493 emu/g). These values show the presence of weak paramagnetic contributions brought about by adding ZnO and TiO2 to the PVA matrix, even if they are still modest. It is noteworthy that the diamagnetic background of the PVA matrix continues to dominate the total magnetic response. Rather than the creation of any real magnetic phase, the observed boost is explained by defect-related electronic states, such as oxygen vacancies, and interfacial interactions between the oxides and the polymer. The addition of 8% TiO2 and ZnO causes a modest increase in the magnetic response without producing ferromagnetic ordering, supporting the conclusion that surface- and defect-related effects dominate the observed behavior. Nevertheless, both samples remain non-magnetic in nature [63, 64].

Conclusion

ZTO/PVA nanocomposites were successfully fabricated via a solution casting method using ZnO and TiO2 nanoparticles synthesized by precipitation and sonochemical routes. XRD analysis confirmed the incorporation of nanoparticles into the PVA matrix and revealed an anatase-to-rutile phase transformation of TiO2 after composite formation. XPS spectra detected Zn, O, and Ti LMM signals, confirming the presence of oxide components within the composite structure. Optical analysis showed a significant reduction in the band gap after ZTO incorporation. The indirect band gap decreased from 3.03 eV for pure PVA to 2.93, 2.72, and 2.5 eV for 2, 5, and 8 wt% ZTO/PVA, respectively, while the direct band gap decreased from 4.59 to 2.72 eV at higher filler content. Photoluminescence (PL) analysis revealed that pure PVA exhibited an emission peak at ~ 416 nm, which slightly red-shifted to 418–420 nm for 2 and 5 wt% ZTO/PVA, indicating the formation of interfacial defect states. At 8 wt% ZTO, a noticeable blue shift to ~ 382 nm was observed, associated with the anatase-to-rutile phase transformation of TiO2 and near-band-edge emission. Gaussian deconvolution showed emission bands at 418.7 and 473.3 nm for pure PVA, while 8 wt% ZTO/PVA exhibited peaks at 351, 390, 414, and 468 nm, related to defect states such as oxygen vacancies and zinc interstitials. AFM analysis revealed notable changes in surface morphology with increasing ZTO loading, where the surface roughness (Ra) increased from 9.46 nm for 2 wt% ZTO/PVA to 131.97 nm for 8 wt% ZTO/PVA, indicating increased nanoparticle aggregation and surface heterogeneity. Thermal analysis revealed a five-step weight-loss process, indicating modified degradation behavior and improved thermal stability of the nanocomposites. Dielectric measurements showed high dielectric permittivity at low frequencies that decreased with increasing frequency due to interfacial polarization. The 8 wt% ZTO/PVA sample exhibited lower dielectric permittivity than the 5 wt% composite, and dielectric relaxation analysis using the Havriliak–Negami and Cole–Cole models confirmed non-Debye relaxation behavior. Magnetic measurements indicated predominantly non-magnetic behavior with a slight enhancement after ZTO incorporation. These results demonstrate that ZTO addition effectively tailors the structural, optical, thermal, dielectric, and magnetic properties of PVA nanocomposites, highlighting their potential for multifunctional dielectric applications.
