DPEP1 promotes pulmonary fibrosis by regulating arachidonic acid metabolism
Authors: Guiyu Zhou, Jie He, Jiaqing Jiang, Zhenglin Chen, Yaju Deng, Yu Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough dipeptidase 1 (DPEP1) is known to be involved in endothelial function and metabolic regulation, its contribution to idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) has not been established. This study aimed to investigate the role of DPEP1 in the pathogenesis of IPF and to explore its potential mechanism involving arachidonic acid (ARA) metabolism and endothelial inflammatory responses.MethodsPublic transcriptomic datasets from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, including bulk RNA sequencing and single-cell RNA sequencing datasets, were analyzed to evaluate DPEP1 expression and cellular localization in IPF. Correlations between DPEP1 expression and pulmonary function parameters were assessed. Serum samples from IPF patients and healthy controls were collected to validate DPEP1 and ARA levels. A bleomycin (BLM)-induced pulmonary fibrosis mouse model was established to investigate the in vivo role of DPEP1. Histological staining, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA), reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR), immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, and Western blotting were performed to evaluate fibrosis, inflammatory responses, and signaling pathway alterations. The therapeutic effects of the DPEP1 inhibitor cilastatin sodium were also examined.ResultsDPEP1 expression was significantly elevated in lung tissues and pulmonary vascular endothelial cells of IPF patients and negatively correlated with lung function indices, including forced expiratory volume in the first second percent predicted (FEV1%), diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide percent predicted (DLCO%), and forced vital capacity percent predicted (FVC%). Single-cell RNA sequencing identified endothelial cells as the primary source of DPEP1 expression in IPF lungs. Functional enrichment analysis suggested that DPEP1 was closely associated with fatty acid metabolism, particularly ARA metabolism. Serum ARA levels were significantly decreased in IPF patients and negatively correlated with DPEP1 expression. In the BLM-induced mouse model, DPEP1 expression was markedly increased, whereas ARA levels were reduced. Pharmacological inhibition of DPEP1 alleviated pulmonary fibrosis, decreased inflammatory cytokine expression, restored ARA, PGE2, and cAMP levels, and suppressed activation of the TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling pathway.ConclusionsDPEP1 promotes pulmonary fibrosis by regulating ARA metabolism and endothelial inflammatory responses. Targeting DPEP1 may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for the treatment of IPF.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Dipeptidase 1 (DPEP1) is upregulated in idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) lung tissues and pulmonary vascular endothelial cells, and correlates negatively with forced expiratory volume in the first second as a percentage of predicted(FEV1%), diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide as a percentage of predicted(DLCO%), and forced vital capacity as a percentage of predicted(FVC%).

• DPEP1 drives pulmonary fibrosis by dysregulating arachidonic acid metabolism and promoting endothelial inflammatory responses.

• Pharmacological inhibition of DPEP1 with cilastatin sodium alleviates bleomycin-induced fibrosis and restores arachidonic acid/PGE2/cAMP levels in vivo.

What is known and what is new? (two paragraphs)

• IPF is a progressive fibrotic lung disease with limited therapeutic options. Arachidonic acid metabolic dysregulation and endothelial dysfunction have been implicated in IPF pathogenesis, but the upstream regulators linking them remain unclear.

• This study identifies DPEP1 as a novel key regulator in IPF. We demonstrate that DPEP1 is predominantly expressed in pulmonary vascular endothelial cells of IPF patients, promotes fibrosis via the arachidonic acid metabolic–endothelial inflammation axis, and that targeting DPEP1 with cilastatin sodium mitigates disease phenotypes in a bleomycin-induced mouse model.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• DPEP1 represents a promising novel therapeutic target for IPF. Future drug development should explore specific DPEP1 inhibitors, and clinical studies are warranted to validate DPEP1 as a diagnostic/prognostic biomarker in IPF patients.

Introduction

Idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis (IPF) represents a progressive and chronically fatal form of interstitial lung disease (ILD) with an obscure etiology, constituting roughly one-third of the total clinical cases diagnosed with ILD (1), with its characterization defined by an extremely poor prognosis (2,3). The median survival time post-diagnosis is typically documented as 2 to 5 years (4). This condition is defined by persistent damage to the alveolar epithelium, a buildup of aging alveolar epithelial cells (AECs), and the abnormal accumulation of myofibroblasts and extracellular matrix stimulated by profibrotic factors (5). Such destructive processes are compounded by shifts in the lung microbiome and a breakdown in host defense systems, which precipitates severe tissue remodeling, compromised gas exchange, shortness of breath, reduced physical capacity, and a diminished quality of life (6). This sequence of pathological events eventually culminates in respiratory failure. At present, clinical management relies on two approved antifibrotic medications: nintedanib and pirfenidone (7-9). However, disease progression is only delayed and mortality rates are not reduced by these drugs, and adverse reactions are experienced by approximately 50% of patients due to drug intolerance (10,11). Therefore, the understanding of IPF pathophysiology is urgently required to be deepened, and new therapeutic strategies aimed at improving patient outcomes must be explored.

First discovered within the renal brush border epithelium, dipeptidase 1 (DPEP1) is a glycosylphosphatidylinositol-anchored membrane protein (12). DPEP1 participates in a variety of physiological functions, such as modulating neutrophil adhesion, contributing to leukotriene (LT) metabolism, and breaking down metabolic substrates (12). This protein also degrades the β-lactam ring of thienamycin antibiotics and is recognized as a specific target for the enzyme inhibitor cilastatin (13). Beyond its dipeptidase activity, DPEP1 binds to neutrophils in response to lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation, subsequently driving the inflammatory process (14). Furthermore, DPEP1 is also expressed in pulmonary microvascular endothelial cells and has been associated with acute lung injury, fatty acid β-oxidation, and ferroptosis (15). Nevertheless, the association between DPEP1 and pulmonary function, alongside the precise involvement of DPEP1 in the pathogenesis of IPF, is yet to be fully elucidated.

Utilizing public data from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database, this study assessed DPEP1 expression within the lung tissue and pulmonary vascular endothelial cells of IPF patients, while concurrently exploring its role in a bleomycin (BLM)-induced model of pulmonary fibrosis. The results demonstrate that DPEP1 levels associate with pulmonary function in individuals with IPF and suggest that this protein drives endothelial cell secretion of pro-inflammatory factors. Furthermore, DPEP1 modulates the endothelial inflammatory response during BLM exposure by regulating the pathways of arachidonic acid (ARA) metabolism. In summary, it is suggested by these results that a promising therapeutic strategy for IPF may be provided by targeting DPEP1. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2026-1666/rc).

Methods

Dataset processing

The analytical framework of this research is depicted in Figure 1. By querying the GEO repository (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/), a total of 8 distinct datasets were retrieved, which included 5 specific tissue-level datasets (GSE47460-GPL14550, GSE47460-GPL6480, GSE53845, GSE10667, GSE150910) (16-19) were derived from lung tissue samples; and 2 single-cell sequencing datasets (GSE135893 and GSE233844) (20,21) were obtained from lung tissue specimens, and one dataset (GSE38958) (22) was obtained from blood, with the subsequent normalization of the matrix datasets executed via the “Affy” package within the R software environment.

Workflow diagram. GEO, Gene Expression Omnibus; GO, Gene Ontology; IPF, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis; KEGG, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes.

IPF patients and healthy individuals

Study subjects

The study cohort comprised thirty patients diagnosed with IPF at the Department of Respiratory and Critical Care Medicine alongside twenty-five healthy controls from the Health Management Center at The First Affiliated Hospital of Chengdu Medical College between 2020 and 2024. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Chengdu Medical College (approval No. 2020CYFYIRB-BA-101-01), and all participants provided written informed consent.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The enrollment criteria for patients with IPF required: (I) a confirmed diagnosis of IPF established in accordance with the 2019 consensus guidelines issued by the American Thoracic Society, European Respiratory Society, Japanese Respiratory Society, and Latin American Thoracic Society; (II) age >18 years; (III) ILDs of other known causes, such as environmental exposure (dust, mold), drug toxicity, autoimmune diseases, etc., were excluded; (IV) communication through spoken or written language could be performed by the patients, and the capability to complete auxiliary examinations related to the trial was possessed. The intensity of IPF was assessed using a multidimensional index and staging system for IPF, known as the Gender-Age-Physiology (GAP) index (23). Exclusion criteria for IPF: (I) alternative categories of ILD—including connective tissue disease-associated ILD (CTD-ILD), nonspecific interstitial pneumonia (NSIP), hypersensitivity pneumonitis (HP), and sarcoidosis—were systematically excluded; (II) concomitant severe systemic diseases: such as uncontrolled cardiovascular disease, liver and kidney function failure, etc.; (III) pregnant or lactating women; (IV) inability to cooperate with examinations or follow-up.

Inclusion criteria for healthy controls: (I) no history of respiratory system diseases or chronic lung diseases; (II) age and gender were matched with IPF patients.

Determination of DPEP1-interacting proteins

A protein-protein interaction (PPI) network was constructed utilizing the STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/cgi/input.pl). To validate these predicted interactions between DPEP1 and its candidate target genes, cross-referencing was performed against the GENEMANIA database (http://genemania.org/search/homo-sapiens), which consolidates data from 100 publicly available gene expression repositories.

Functional analysis

Functional enrichment analysis, including Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways for the identified DPEP1-interacting partners, was executed via the gene set enrichment method within the R package “clusterProfiler” (v.3.18.1). To evaluate cellular immune composition, the CIBERSORT bioinformatics platform (http://cibersort.stanford.edu/) was utilized, leveraging its validated deconvolution algorithm and signature matrix of 22 distinct leukocyte subtypes to quantify immune cell infiltration scores within the RNA profiles of IPF patients. With the median value of DPEP1 expression level used as the cutoff value, 103 samples in the GSE150910 dataset were divided into a DPEP1 low-expression group and a DPEP1 high-expression group, and the differences in the scores of 22 immune cell subtypes between the two groups were analyzed.

Single-cell sequencing

Single-cell RNA sequencing transcriptomic analysis of the pulmonary datasets GSE135893 and GSE233844 was executed leveraging the R toolkit “Seurat”. Initial data filtration and quality controls restricted retention to cellular profiles exhibiting a mitochondrial gene fraction below 20% and an intracellular feature RNA count between 200 and 5,000. Following data normalization via the NormalizeData module, dimensionality reduction was initialized through principal component analysis (PCA) using the RunPCA function. The leading 50 principal components were subsequently utilized to perform Louvain clustering and uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) visualizations, applying a clustering resolution of 0.6. Distinct cell clustering populations were annotated by evaluating differential cluster markers extracted with the FindAllMarkers algorithm against reference signatures from the CellMarker registry and corresponding source literature. Targeted mapping of specific transcript distributions and intensity variations across cell types was generated through Seurat’s built-in DotPlot, VlnPlot, and FeaturePlot utilities.

Validation of DPEP1 as a diagnostic biomarker for IPF

To verify the capability of DPEP1 to serve as a diagnostic marker for IPF, transcriptomic information from the public cohorts GSE47460-GPL14550, GSE47460-GPL6480, GSE53845, GSE10667, and GSE150910 was analyzed alongside institutional clinical specimens. The diagnostic capacity of this candidate biomarker was determined by constructing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and quantifying the corresponding area under the curve (AUC) values. Statistical thresholds for significance were measured utilizing two-tailed testing, with a P value below 0.05 establishing statistical significance.

Hydroxyproline assay

Pulmonary hydroxyproline concentrations were quantified utilizing a commercial hydroxyproline colorimetric assay kit (Cat. No. BC0255, Solarbio, Beijing, China) in strict accordance with the protocol provided by the manufacturer. Final values were calculated and normalized against total protein content, with the results expressed as micrograms (µg) of hydroxyproline per milligram (mg) of protein.

Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome staining

Following deparaffinization and rehydration procedures, harvested lung sections underwent H&E staining (Cat. No. G1120, Solarbio) alongside Masson’s trichrome staining (Cat. No. G1346, Solarbio) in strict compliance with the respective manufacturer protocols. Structural alterations and histopathological variations across the tissue samples were subsequently evaluated and captured via light microscopy.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Commercial ELISA kits were used to detect DPEP1 (CSB-MP007123HU, CUSABIO, Wuhan, China), ARA (CSB-E14102m, CUSABIO), PGE2 (CSB-E07966m, CUSABIO), and cAMP (E-EL-0056, Elabscience, Wuhan, China) in lung tissues. The specific operations were as follows: The diluted capture antibody was immobilized onto a 96-well microplate by incubating overnight at room temperature. Following appropriate washing steps, non-specific binding sites were blocked by adding 300 µL of reagent diluent to each well for 1 hour. Subsequently, prepared samples and standard series diluted in the same reagent matrix were introduced into the wells and incubated for a duration of 2 hours; after washing, 100 µL of detection antibody was added and incubated for 2 hours; subsequently, 100 µL of streptavidin-horseradish peroxidase (HRP) conjugate was introduced into each well and allowed to incubate for 20 minutes. This was followed by the addition of 100 µL of substrate solution, which was incubated for an additional 20 minutes to develop the colorimetric signal. To terminate the enzymatic reaction, 50 µL of stop solution was dispensed into all wells. Optical density values were then immediately quantified at a detection wavelength of 450 nm using a standard microplate reader.

Immunofluorescence

Paraffin-embedded lung tissue sections underwent initial deparaffinization and rehydration procedures, followed by tissue permeabilization using 0.3% Triton X-100 (Cat. No. KGA1508-10, KeyGEN Bio, Nanjing, China). Non-specific binding sites were subsequently blocked with 5% goat serum for 30 minutes. The sections were then incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with a 1:200 dilution of rabbit anti-DPEP1 primary antibody (Cat. No. 12222-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) and anti-CD31 antibody (Cat. No. AF3628, R&D Systems, Minnesota, USA). Following a 1-hour incubation with the corresponding secondary antibodies at room temperature, the slides were rinsed with PBS, and immunofluorescence signals were captured using a confocal fluorescence microscope.

Western blot

Total cellular protein extracts were isolated utilizing RIPA lysis buffer (Cat. No. KGB5203-100, KeyGEN Bio) supplemented with a cocktail of protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Cat. No. KGB5303-100, KeyGEN Bio). Following quantification of protein concentrations with a commercial bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit (Cat. No. BL521A, Beyotime, Shanghai, China), samples were prepared for separation by boiling in sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) loading buffer (Cat. No. FD002, Fude Biological, Hangzhou, China). Resolving proteins were subsequently transferred onto polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Cat. No. IPVH00010, Merck, Germany). The membranes were blocked in 5% non-fat milk for 1 hour at room temperature, followed by an overnight incubation at 4 ℃ with specific primary antibodies. Western blot detection was conducted under standardized conditions; after incubation with appropriate secondary antibodies, protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent (Cat. No. BL520B, Beyotime, Hangzhou, China). The primary antibody panel utilized in this research comprised: glyceraldehyde 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH; Cat. No. 2118 S, CST, Massachusetts, USA), collagen I (Cat. No. 14695-1-AP, Proteintech), Fibronectin (Cat. No. ET1702-25, Huabio, Hangzhou, China), DPEP1 (Cat. No. 12222-1-AP, Proteintech), COX2 (Cat. No. EPR12012, Abcam), TGF-β1 (Cat. No. EPR21143, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), p-Smad3 (Cat. No. AF8315, Affinity, Jiangsu, China), Smad3 (Cat. No. AF6362, Affinity), and the secondary antibody utilized was a goat anti-rabbit IgG conjugate (Cat. No. 7074 S, CST, USA).

Immunohistochemistry

Immunohistochemical analysis was conducted on paraffin-embedded lung tissue sections. Antigen retrieval was performed by exposing the sections to a citrate-ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) buffer at 95 or 155 ℃ for 10 minutes, followed by gradual cooling to room temperature. To minimize non-specific background staining, tissues were incubated with a 5% goat serum blocking solution. Subsequently, sections were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with an optimized dilution of rabbit anti-DPEP1 primary antibody (Cat. No. 12222-1-AP, Proteintech). Following treatment with an appropriate secondary antibody at room temperature, immunoreactivity and tissue morphology were evaluated and visualized via light microscopy, and diaminobenzidine color developing solution (Cat. No. GK500705, Gene Create Bio, Wuhan, China) was added dropwise for color development, the reaction was terminated in a timely manner, and coverslipping was performed after dehydration. Analysis of the immunohistochemical results was performed using Fiji Image software.

Reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was isolated from harvested cellular and tissue specimens using TRIzol reagent, with subsequent complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis executed in accordance with the protocol provided with the HiScript III RT SuperMix kit (Cat. No. R323-01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). RT-qPCR amplifications were performed utilizing the 2× ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Cat. No. Q711-02, Vazyme). Gene-specific primers were synthesized by GenScript (USA), with the respective nucleotide sequences detailed in table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/jtd-2026-1666-1.xlsx and Table S1​. GAPDH served as the internal reference gene for normalization.

Animals

In the bleomycin-induced pulmonary fibrosis experiments, male wild-type C57BL/6J mice (body weight 20–25 g, 6–8 weeks of age) were purchased from Guangdong Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. [License No. SCXK (Yue) 2022-0063] and used in this study. Normal saline was administered to the control group via intratracheal nebulization using a microsyringe, while BLM solution was injected intratracheally into the experimental group at a dose of 3 mg/kg according to body weight, and the inhibitor group was treated with tail vein injection of the DPEP1 inhibitor cilastatin sodium (24) (Cat. No. HY-A0166A, MCE, State of New Jersey, USA) (10 mg/kg, three times a week). The general status, activity, coat appearance, and food and water intake of the mice were observed daily after intervention. On day 21 post-intervention, the mice were humanely euthanized. Euthanasia was performed in accordance with the acceptable methods defined in the 2013 Guidelines for Euthanasia approved by the American Veterinary Medical Association (AVMA). Animals were euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of ketamine/xylazine at a dose of 100/10 mg/kg body weight, followed by a secondary method, cervical dislocation. At the experimental endpoint, following intraperitoneal euthanasia, lung tissues were collected for subsequent analyses. The left lung samples were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for immunohistochemistry and related studies, while the right lung was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for subsequent Western blot and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) experiments. All animal experiments were performed under a project license [No. 2025-IACUC-C (003)] granted by the Ethics Committee for Medical Experimental Animals, The Second Affiliated Hospital of Guangxi Medical University, in compliance with the Guiding Opinions on the Treatment of Laboratory Animals issued by the Ministry of Science and Technology of the People’s Republic of China, the national standard GB/T 35892-2018 Laboratory Animal—Guideline for Welfare and Ethical Review guidelines for the care and use of animals.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons between two distinct groups were evaluated using Student’s t-test. All quantitative data are expressed as the mean ± SD, with a P value <0.05 considered statistically significant. For univariate correlation analyses, Pearson’s or Spearman’s correlation coefficients were applied based on the distribution and nature of the dataset. The levels of statistical significance are designated by asterisks within the figures as follows: *, P<0.05; **, P<0.01; ***, P<0.001; and ****, P<0.0001.

Results

Expression of DPEP1 in IPF

Based on tissue expression data from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database, DPEP1 is predominantly expressed in the human kidney, gastrointestinal tract, and pulmonary tissues (Figure S1, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/gene/?term=DPEP1). To investigate the specific expression profile of DPEP1 in IPF, six distinct datasets were retrieved and analyzed from the GEO database. The compiled transcriptomic results demonstrated that DPEP1 expression levels were significantly elevated in the lung tissues and blood of patients with IPF compared to healthy controls (Figure 2).

Expression of DPEP1 mRNA of lung tissue and blood in patients with IPF. AE, acute exacerbation; IPF, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis.

DPEP1-interacting proteins

Thirteen protein-coding genes interacting with DPEP1 were discovered utilizing the STRING database: ADAMDEC1, ABR, DPEP3, XPNPEP2, DPEP2, CNDP2, CTSZ, ABTB1, OGDHL, DNASE1L2, BSG, CA9, and DERPC (Figure S2A). According to the GENEMANIA database, the two most significant co-expressed genes of DPEP1 are GGT5 and GGT1 (Figure S2B). A total of 31 genes (from STRING database and GENEMANIA database) interact with DPEP1.

Correlation between DPEP1 expression level and lung function

Analysis of the GSE47460-GPL14550 dataset revealed that elevated DPEP1 expression levels significantly and negatively correlated with predicted forced expiratory volume in the first second percent predicted (FEV1%), diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide percent predicted (DLCO%), and forced vital capacity percent predicted (FVC%) metrics (Table 1). This exact negative correlation pattern with predicted FEV1%, DLCO%, and FVC% was consistently mirrored within the GSE47460-GPL6480 cohort (Table 1). Furthermore, evaluation of the GSE38958 dataset confirmed a significant inverse relationship between DPEP1 transcription levels and both predicted DLCO% and FVC% values (Table 1). Validating these bioinformatic insights within our independent institutional cohort, we observed a matching, statistically significant negative correlation between clinical DPEP1 expression and all three primary lung function parameters: predicted FEV1%, DLCO%, and FVC% (Table 1).

Functional analysis

It was shown by GO and KEGG analysis that DPEP1 and the 31 interacting genes have significant associations with the metabolic process of fatty acid derivatives, the catabolic process of fatty acid derivatives, the metabolic process of modified amino acids, and ARA metabolism (Figure S3). Stratifying the patient cohort by expression levels revealed that the neutrophil immunity score of the DPEP1 high-expression group was significantly elevated compared to that of the DPEP1 low-expression group (P<0.05; Figure S4).

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

To comprehensively evaluate cell-specific transcriptomic profiles, scRNA-seq analysis was performed on two independent human pulmonary datasets: GSE135893, comprising 12 IPF patients and 10 healthy controls, and GSE233844, consisting of 25 IPF patients and 13 healthy controls. It is shown in Figure S5 that endothelial cells are the primary and most abundant cell type expressing DPEP1. In addition, DPEP1 levels in the endothelial cells of IPF patients were significantly increased (Figure S5).

Identification and validation of DPEP1 diagnostic characteristic biomarker

The expression of DPEP1 as a diagnostic characteristic biomarker was determined by us in the GEO datasets, and it was indicated by the ROC curves of DPEP1 that the mRNA expression of DPEP1 possesses good diagnostic value in the GEO datasets. The AUC value of DPEP1 in GSE47460-GPL14550 was 0.7765, the AUC value of DPEP1 in GSE47460-GPL6480 was 0.8297, the AUC value of DPEP1 in GSE53845 was 0.8125, the AUC value of DPEP1 in GSE10667 was 0.9130, the AUC value of DPEP1 in GSE150910 was 0.9053, and the AUC value of DPEP1 in the clinical samples included in this study was 0.6933. These results indicate that good diagnostic efficacy is possessed by DPEP1, and it can be used for the diagnosis of IPF (Figure 3).

Results of ARA determination

Thirty patients with confirmed IPF and 25 control patients were included in this study. In the IPF cohort, 25 cases were receiving antifibrotic therapy (15 cases used nintedanib, 10 cases used pirfenidone), while 5 cases did not receive antifibrotic therapy. The baseline characteristics of the patients included in this study are summarized in Table 2. Our analysis revealed no significant differences between the IPF and control groups regarding baseline demographics, including age, sex, body mass index (BMI), smoking history, or any evaluated comorbidities. Notably, we observed that the baseline concentration of ARA in the IPF cohort was significantly lower than that recorded in the control group (78.87±14.74 vs. 96.74±11.48 mg/L, respectively; Table 2). Furthermore, when patients were stratified according to the GAP staging system, ARA levels in all IPF subgroups (GAP stages I, II, and III) remained significantly lower than those in controls (all P<0.05). However, no statistically significant differences were observed among the three GAP stages [analysis of variance (ANOVA) P=0.15; Figure S6A].

Serum DPEP1 levels

Our analysis revealed that DPEP1 expression levels were significantly elevated in the IPF group compared to the control group (47.67±10.46 vs. 40.65±7.85 pg/mL). When patients were stratified by GAP stage, DPEP1 levels in GAP stage I did not significantly differ from controls. However, DPEP1 levels in GAP stage III were markedly higher than those in both GAP stages I and II (P<0.01), suggesting a potential association between DPEP1 and IPF disease severity (Figure S6B). Although one-way ANOVA across the three stages did not reach statistical significance (P=0.10), this may be attributable to the limited sample size, particularly the GAP stage III subgroup (n=3).

Correlation between ARA and serum DPEP1

A correlation between ARA and lung function in IPF patients has been found in previous studies (25). It was also suggested by the results of bioinformatics analysis that DPEP1 is involved in regulating ARA metabolism. Therefore, the correlation between serum DPEP1 and ARA was detected by us, and it was suggested by the results that a negative correlation exists between ARA and DPEP1 (cor =−0.395, P=0.03; Figure S6C); in addition, the DLCO% serves as one of the most reliable and powerful predictors of survival and disease progression in patients with IPF (26). Therefore, the relation between serum DPEP1 level in IPF and predicted DLCO% was analyzed, and a trend toward a negative correlation was observed (cor =−0.341, P=0.07; Figure S6D).

DPEP1 is upregulated in pulmonary vascular endothelial cells of BLM mice

To validate our bioinformatic findings in vivo, we evaluated lung tissue sections from mice subjected to BLM administration. Consistent with the computational predictions, histopathological and molecular assessments revealed that the expression levels of the core fibrotic markers fibronectin and collagen I were significantly elevated in the lung tissues of the BLM-treated mice at day 21 post-induction, while the RNA level and protein level of DPEP1 were significantly increased (Figure 4A-4C). It was shown by immunohistochemistry results that compared with the control group, the immunohistochemical staining of DPEP1 in BLM-induced mouse lung tissues was enhanced (Figure 4D-4F). To further localize the expression of DPEP1 in lung tissues, immunofluorescence co-localization of DPEP1 and vascular endothelial cells (CD31) was performed on BLM-treated mouse lung tissues, and it was shown by the results that DPEP1-positive staining was increased in vascular endothelial cells located in the fibrotic areas (Figure 4G); conversely, serum metabolomic analysis revealed that ARA levels were significantly decreased in mice 21 days post-BLM treatment (Figure 4H). Taken together, these findings demonstrate that DPEP1 expression is systematically upregulated at both the transcriptomic and proteomic levels within the vascular endothelial cells of fibrotic lung tissues, which is consistent with the single-cell sequencing results. Treating BLM-induced mice with the DPEP1 inhibitor cilastatin sodium significantly mitigated pulmonary fibrosis compared with the BLM group (Figure 5A,5B), and the hydroxyproline content in lung tissues was correspondingly reduced (Figure 5C). It was found by further detection of inflammatory factor expression in lung tissues that the mRNA levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 in the lung tissues of fibrotic mice could be reduced by inhibiting DPEP1 (Figure 5D). In addition, the expression of COX-2 in the lung tissues of pulmonary fibrosis mice could be upregulated by inhibiting DPEP1, while the expressions of TGF-β1 and phospho-Smad3 were downregulated (Figure 5E-5H); meanwhile, the levels of ARA, PGE2, and cAMP in lung tissues also recovered (Figure 5I-5K). It is indicated by the above results that the pathogenesis of IPF may be mediated by DPEP1 in pulmonary capillary endothelial cells through the regulation of the ARA metabolism pathway.

Discussion

IPF is a persistent, life-threatening lung disease whose pathological and radiological features are manifested as typical interstitial pneumonia. Our bioinformatic investigation revealed a marked elevation of DPEP1 gene expression within the lung tissues of individuals with IPF. Furthermore, single-cell RNA sequencing identified pulmonary vascular endothelial cells as the primary site of DPEP1 expression. And a negative correlation was exhibited between DPEP1 and the lung function of IPF patients. In addition, our in vitro assays validated that DPEP1 was upregulated in both the serum of individuals with IPF and mouse models of BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis. Crucially, administration of a DPEP1 inhibitor mitigated the severity of fibrotic disease in these mice. In this study, a BLM-induced fibrosis model was established by us through mice to investigate the effect of DPEP1 on pulmonary fibrosis. It is confirmed for the first time in this study that BLM-induced pulmonary fibrosis can be promoted by DPEP1, and it is suggested by bioinformatics analysis and clinical samples that the profibrotic effect of DPEP1 may be related to pathways associated with lipid metabolism and ARA metabolism, which may offer a novel clinical strategy for both the diagnosis and therapeutic management of patients with IPF.

DPEP1 was originally characterized as a dipeptidase localized to the renal membrane (27). Ischemic or nephrotoxic acute kidney injury characteristically triggers an elevation in both the expression and enzymatic performance of DPEP1 (12). It was shown by mechanistic studies that whether through gene knockout or peptide-mediated blockade, the inhibition of DPEP1 can significantly reduce the recruitment of neutrophils in the lung, liver, and renal tubules, thereby alleviating inflammatory responses utilizing experimental paradigms of sepsis and renal ischemia-reperfusion injury (12,28). These findings align with our own observations, which revealed a pronounced infiltration of neutrophils within the lung tissue of IPF patients exhibiting high DPEP1 expression. Consequently, this underscores DPEP1 as a pivotal regulator of the inflammatory response in pulmonary endothelial cells during IPF. After the use of a DPEP1 inhibitor, the inflammatory response in mouse lung tissues can be significantly reduced, thereby mitigating the advancement of pulmonary fibrosis in the murine model. The great significance of deeply studying the pathogenic mechanism mediated by DPEP1 for developing novel therapeutic strategies targeting IPF is highlighted by this.

In addition to inflammatory injury, diverse roles are played by DPEP1 in cancer. In colon cancer, a poor clinical outcome is predictably linked to the downregulated expression of DPEP1 (29); conversely, in pancreatic cancer and lobular breast cancer, tumor progression seems to be inhibited by high expression of DPEP1 (30,31). Although we cannot directly translate its prognostic value in malignancies to inflammatory disorders, the molecular mechanisms by which DPEP1 impacts tumor biology offer critical insights into its pathogenic involvement in IPF. Specifically, DPEP1 has been shown to fuel tumor progression by triggering oncogenic cascades, including the Wnt/β-catenin and PI3K/AKT pathways (32), and a similar mechanism may function in pulmonary endothelial cell injury.

Although myofibroblasts are considered core participants in fibrosis, the role of pulmonary vascular endothelial cells in IPF has gradually gained attention in recent years; endothelial cells constitute approximately 30% of the total pulmonary cell population. Given that the depletion of these vascular endothelial cells and aberrant angiogenesis are established biological hallmarks of pulmonary fibrosis, they logically represent a pivotal element in IPF pathogenesis (33-35). Endothelial cells are the main participants and regulators of inflammatory responses; endothelial cells are quiescent under normal conditions, and they can be rapidly activated under injury or pathological conditions; activated endothelial cells play a pivotal role in the inflammatory response of tissue fibrosis, and existing inflammatory responses are usually directly initiated or exacerbated by them (35,36). An important role is played by endothelial cells in the inflammatory response of pulmonary fibrosis, and it is shown in this study that the DPEP1 gene is mainly localized within pulmonary vascular endothelial cells; immune cell recruitment can be promoted and the release of intercellular adhesion molecules, vascular cell adhesion molecules, monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, and IL-8 by endothelial cells can be promoted by DPEP1, therefore, the dynamic regulatory role of endothelial cells in the interplay of inflammation and fibrosis is explored deeply, which can provide a new direction for therapeutic strategies, particularly through regulating specific molecules and signaling pathways of endothelial cells.

Importantly, our bioinformatic analysis revealed that DPEP1 potentially modulates ARA metabolism. As a vital polyunsaturated fatty acid, ARA undergoes conversion into prostaglandins (PGs) or LTs via the cyclooxygenase (COX) or 5-lipoxygenase (5-LOX) pathways, respectively (37). The PG family is primarily comprised of PGD2, PGE2, PGF2, PGI2, and thromboxane A2 (TXA2). Concurrently, LTs encompass subclasses such as LTA4, LTB4, LTC4, LTD4, and LTE4; the latter three are collectively designated as cysteinyl leukotrienes (CysLTs) and function as potent pro-inflammatory mediators (38). It has been found in studies that the dysregulation of PGs and LTs contributes to the progression of pulmonary fibrosis (39). The suppression of AEC senescence by epoxyeicosatrienoic acids (EETs), the downstream CYP-catalyzed metabolites of ARA, was previously established by Zhang et al. (40), thereby alleviating the age-related pulmonary fibrosis degree in mice. The phenomenon of decreased ARA in IPF observed in this study is consistent with emerging literature viewpoints, indicating that the fibrosis process may be promoted by ARA metabolic disorders (41-43). Particular attention should be paid to the reduction of ARA levels in IPF patients (44-48). ARA is a recognized precursor of eicosanoids, and eicosanoids are key mediators of pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory responses (48). An increase in its conversion to downstream mediators during active fibrosis or inflammation may be indicated by the reduction of circulating ARA. Recent studies indicate that eicosanoid metabolic disorders characterized by excessive production of LTs and deficiency of prostaglandin E2 (PGE2) promote the occurrence and development of pulmonary fibrosis (39,49-51). Particularly, an imbalance in the production of ARA metabolites is observed in IPF patients, with increased levels of LTC4 and LTB4 in lung tissues (52-54). Although the PGE2 level in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) is decreased, the prostaglandin F2α (PGF2α) level in the serum of IPF patients is increased. Notably, a negative correlation is presented between the increased serum PGF2α level and forced vital capacity (FVC) and diffusing capacity of the lungs for carbon monoxide (DLCO) (55-57). Significant dysregulation of ARA in the serum fatty acid profile was observed by us in the IPF cohort, and a negative correlation was exhibited between serum DPEP1 and ARA expression levels. Stratification by GAP stage further revealed that although ARA levels were uniformly reduced across all IPF subgroups relative to controls, the differences among GAP stages I, II, and III did not reach statistical significance. Notably, DPEP1 levels in GAP stage III were significantly elevated compared with those in stages I and II, suggesting that DPEP1 upregulation may be more pronounced in advanced disease. The lack of significant inter-stage differences for ARA may reflect the relatively limited sample size within each GAP stratum, particularly the small number of GAP stage III patients (n=3). Future studies with larger, well-balanced cohorts across all GAP stages are needed to validate these findings and determine whether ARA and DPEP1 can serve as biomarkers reflecting IPF disease severity. It was shown in the study by Hoyt et al. (58) that DPEP1 is expressed in the intestinal epithelium and can catabolize leukotriene D4 (LTD4). Allergen transport in resistant mice could be enhanced by blocking DPEP1 using cilastatin, knocking out the Dpep1 gene, or oral administration of LTD4. DPEP1 hydrolyzes LTD4, which is a key inflammatory mediator produced from ARA via the 5-lipoxygenase pathway. Why a negative correlation is presented between DPEP1 and ARA is explained indirectly by their study. In addition, it was found by us that a negative correlation is also presented between the concentration of DPEP1 and DLCO; when a DPEP1 inhibitor was used, the expression level of ARA rose significantly, and PGE2, and TGF-β1 were also affected; it is evidenced by these proofs that the impaired lung function is related to lower concentrations of ARA, and the inflammatory response generated by DPEP1 regulating ARA metabolism is a crucial but not yet fully explored determining factor in the pathogenesis of IPF.

From a mechanistic standpoint, several aspects of our study warrant further discussion in the context of the current literature. First, although we did not perform functional experiments using primary human lung microvascular endothelial cells or direct in vitro enzymatic assays in the present study, converging lines of evidence support DPEP1’s role in endothelial-mediated ARA metabolism and pulmonary fibrosis. Our single-cell RNA sequencing analysis across two independent cohorts (GSE135893 and GSE233844) consistently identified endothelial cells as the primary cellular source of DPEP1 in IPF lungs. This finding was further corroborated by immunofluorescence co-localization of DPEP1 with the endothelial marker CD31 in BLM-treated mouse lung tissues, confirming that DPEP1 is specifically upregulated in pulmonary vascular endothelial cells within fibrotic areas. Furthermore, Hoyt et al. (58) demonstrated that DPEP1 possesses intrinsic enzymatic activity toward cysteinyl LTs, particularly LTD4—a key ARA-derived inflammatory mediator. DPEP1-mediated hydrolysis of LTD4 establishes a direct biochemical link between DPEP1 and the ARA metabolic pathway. In our study, pharmacological inhibition of DPEP1 with cilastatin in the BLM mouse model restored not only ARA levels but also its downstream metabolites PGE2 and cAMP, providing in vivo evidence that DPEP1 modulates ARA metabolism. This modulation likely involves both its direct enzymatic activity on LT substrates and indirect effects on the broader metabolic network. Nevertheless, we fully acknowledge that direct in vitro validation using primary human lung microvascular endothelial cells and purified enzyme activity assays would substantially strengthen the mechanistic evidence.

Second, regarding the depth of metabolite analysis, the present study measured total ARA levels in serum and assessed ARA, PGE2, and cAMP in mouse lung tissues. While these measurements provide important evidence for the involvement of the ARA metabolic pathway, comprehensive targeted metabolomic profiling—including individual PGs (PGD2, PGF2α, PGI2, thromboxane A2), LTs (LTB4, LTC4, LTD4, LTE4), EETs, and hydroxyeicosatetraenoic acids (HETEs)—would offer a more complete picture of how DPEP1 shapes the ARA metabolic network. The observed decrease in ARA levels, accompanied by alterations in PGE2 and cAMP, suggests a shift in the balance among competing metabolic branches, but the specific metabolic flux changes await confirmation through targeted lipidomics approaches. Third, with respect to the cilastatin sodium intervention, we administered the DPEP1 inhibitor throughout the entire 21-day BLM modeling period without intermediate time-point analysis. Although this experimental design follows the standard BLM-induced fibrosis protocol, it does not delineate the optimal therapeutic window. Future studies incorporating multiple cilastatin treatment regimens initiated at different time points (e.g., days 0, 7, 14, and 21 after BLM administration) would help define whether DPEP1 inhibition is most effective during the inflammatory phase, the fibrotic phase, or both, thereby clarifying the preventive versus therapeutic potential of cilastatin in the management of pulmonary fibrosis.

Fourth, our data demonstrate that DPEP1 inhibition restored PGE2 and cAMP levels while simultaneously suppressing TGF-β1/Smad3 pathway activation. Although formal pathway dissection experiments using specific antagonists (e.g., EP2/EP4 receptor antagonists, adenylyl cyclase inhibitors, or PKA inhibitors) were not performed in this study, the hierarchical relationship among PGE2, cAMP, and TGF-β1/Smad3 is well-established in the existing literature (51,59). PGE2 signals through its G protein-coupled receptors EP2 and EP4, which activate adenylyl cyclase, leading to increased intracellular cAMP. Elevated cAMP activates both protein kinase A (PKA) and the exchange protein directly activated by cAMP (Epac1/Epac2), both of which have been demonstrated to inhibit TGF-β1/Smad3-mediated fibrotic responses in lung fibroblasts and endothelial cells (51,59). Accordingly, we propose the following mechanistic cascade: DPEP1 upregulation leads to enhanced LT hydrolysis, which results in ARA depletion, leading to reduced PGE2 synthesis. This subsequently causes decreased intracellular cAMP levels, which in turn disinhibits TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling, thereby promoting fibrotic gene expression. Our observation that cilastatin treatment simultaneously reverses this entire cascade—restoring ARA, PGE2, and cAMP while suppressing Smad3 phosphorylation—is entirely consistent with this model. However, we acknowledge that direct pathway dissection using specific inhibitors or genetic approaches would be required to definitively establish the hierarchical relationships and causal connections among these signaling intermediates.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, our clinical sample size (30 IPF patients and 25 controls) is relatively modest, and the GAP stage III subgroup contained only 3 patients, which limits the statistical power for inter-stage comparisons and the generalizability of our findings. Larger multicenter cohorts are needed to validate the association between DPEP1 levels and IPF disease severity across all GAP stages. Second, despite the single-cell RNA sequencing evidence identifying endothelial cells as the primary DPEP1-expressing cell type in IPF lungs, we did not perform functional validation experiments using primary human lung microvascular endothelial cells (e.g., DPEP1 overexpression or knockdown, assessment of endothelial barrier function, inflammatory cytokine secretion, or pro-fibrotic mediator production). These experiments would provide direct cellular mechanistic evidence and represent an important direction for future work. Third, direct in vitro enzyme activity assays using purified DPEP1 protein with labeled substrates were not conducted. Although the work by Hoyt et al. (58) supports DPEP1’s intrinsic enzymatic activity toward LTD4 in the intestinal epithelium, formal enzymatic characterization within the specific context of pulmonary fibrosis warrants further investigation. Fourth, our metabolomic analysis was limited to total ARA, PGE2, and cAMP. Comprehensive targeted lipidomics covering the full spectrum of ARA metabolites—including PGs, LTs, and CYP450-derived eicosanoids—would better clarify which specific metabolic branches are affected by DPEP1 dysregulation in IPF. Fifth, the cilastatin sodium intervention employed a single dosing regimen throughout the 21-day BLM modeling period without exploring different time points of administration. Time-course studies incorporating multiple treatment windows are needed to define the optimal therapeutic strategy. Sixth, while the proposed signaling cascade, in which DPEP1 activation leads to ARA depletion, reduced PGE2 synthesis, decreased cAMP levels, and subsequent TGF-β1/Smad3 activation is supported by our data and consistent with the established literature, formal causal dissection using specific pharmacological inhibitors or genetic approaches (e.g., EP2/EP4 antagonists, adenylyl cyclase inhibitors, or PKA/Epac pathway modulators) has not yet been performed. Despite these limitations, our study provides the first evidence establishing DPEP1 as a key regulator of ARA metabolism and endothelial inflammation in IPF, and lays the groundwork for subsequent mechanistic investigations and therapeutic development targeting DPEP1.

Conclusions

In summary, this study provides preliminary insights into the pivotal role of DPEP1 in IPF pathogenesis and the potential mechanisms underlying its aberrant expression. We observed a marked upregulation of DPEP1 in the serum of IPF patients as well as in the fibrotic lung tissues of BLM-induced mice. Mechanistically, DPEP1 may propagate ARA metabolism, which accelerates the endothelial inflammatory cascade and consequently exacerbates pulmonary fibrosis. Taken together, these findings highlight DPEP1 inhibition as a highly promising therapeutic strategy for the management of IPF.
