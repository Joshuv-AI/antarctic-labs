A new tool requires a new approach: glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists and the scope of fertility care
Authors: Wendy S. Vitek, Kassie J. Bollig
Published: 2026

Reproductive endocrinologists and infertility specialists (REIs) have greatly improved the treatment of infertility by optimizing in vitro fertilization (IVF), from controlled ovarian stimulation to advances in the laboratory in fertilization, culture, preimplantation testing, and cryopreservation. IVF transformed challenging infertility cases from a condition we could do little to treat into one we can now manage effectively. Metabolic health is central to reproduction, yet treatment has lagged because lifestyle change, earlier antiobesity medications, and weight-loss surgery work inconsistently or slowly against the time pressures our patients face. Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists are the first medications to change that, and they appear to be for metabolic health what in vitro fertilization has been for infertility. The question facing the specialty is how far our role in managing it should now extend.

A cross-sectional survey by Wolfe et al (1) in this issue of F&S Reports suggests that many clinicians have already begun to tackle that question. Surveying fertility specialists about their use of weight-loss pharmacotherapy, principally GLP-1 and dual GLP-1/GIP receptor agonists, the investigators found that roughly half regarded prescribing as within the scope of fertility care, about a third were already prescribing, and nearly half expected to begin or continue over the next one to two years. Respondents most often prescribed to improve metabolic health before conception, and they identified the absence of clear guidelines and their own unfamiliarity as the main reasons they hesitated. The sample is small and self-selected, drawn from 35 clinicians engaged enough to attend a seminar on the subject, and it is not representative of a national sample. The survey is most notable for the gap it reveals: REIs are already prescribing these medications without guidelines to direct them.

The interest is well founded, since these medications produce approximately 15% weight loss, with benefits that extend to cardiovascular and metabolic health (2). The challenge is that this use has outpaced the evidence needed to manage them in the setting of infertility. A 2026 nationwide cohort study demonstrated that the prevalence of GLP-1 RA prescribing increased from 0.2 to 6.4 per 1,000 deliveries predelivery, from 2019 to 2024, equating to approximately 20,000 pregnancies in 2024 alone (3), a trend that has most assuredly continued. Best practices for management in the preconception window and early pregnancy are largely absent from the obesity-medicine literature: namely, when to stop relative to conception, how to manage the return of appetite and weight when the drug is stopped for fertility treatment, and how to blunt rebound weight gain in pregnancy. The early data give reason for caution. A 2025 cohort study, composed largely of women with obesity, found that discontinuation before or in early pregnancy was associated with greater gestational weight gain and with higher rates of preterm delivery, gestational diabetes, and hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (4). The findings are consistent with what is already understood about weight rebound, and it tempers any expectation that weight lost on a GLP-1 receptor agonist will be maintained once the drug is stopped for pregnancy.

A GLP-1 receptor agonist prescribed only to lower weight and then stopped at conception, with nothing else in the patient’s care changed, may achieve short-term weight loss while leaving her at risk for metabolic and pregnancy complications. Prescribing in a structured program with nutritional and behavioral support may better prepare the patient for weight maintenance after discontinuation, with a focused plan on approaching timing of conception from the outset. We do not yet have trials that define such a program for patients moving from a GLP-1 receptor agonist toward conception, and designing one is among the more useful contributions our specialty could make. The biology invites a further question. GLP-1 receptors are expressed in the pituitary, ovary, and endometrium, and preclinical work suggests reproductive effects that may be partly independent of weight loss (5). Whether these agents improve fertility directly, in addition to the benefits that follow from treating obesity, is unknown, but it is a question our specialty is well positioned to answer.

In practical terms, REIs who prescribe GLP-1 receptor agonists should do so within a program that provides the other elements of obesity care, rather than as an isolated service added to an in vitro fertilization plan. Those who are not positioned to build such a program should not simply decline to prescribe, which leaves patients to obtain these medications without support elsewhere; they should instead partner with obesity-medicine colleagues who can provide what we cannot. Obesity is a chronic disease, and our patients are best served when we treat it as one.

These medications are a genuine advance, and they deserve the structure that makes them effective while reducing inadvertent harm. Our specialty needs the prescribing guidance this survey shows is missing, along with the multidisciplinary programs and partnerships that allow a GLP-1 receptor agonist to serve as one component of comprehensive care. With that guidance in place, we can improve not only our patients’ chance of conception but also their metabolic health and the outcome of the pregnancy that follows.
