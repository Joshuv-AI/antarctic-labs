Explainable CNN framework for accurate crop disease detection using plant leaf images
Authors: Avik Ray, Tamilarasi Kathirvel Murugan, Logeswari Govindaraj, Iran Hussain
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Early and accurate disease detection is important for increasing the agricultural output, decreasing the financial costs, and ensuring food security. Traditional diagnostic procedures take much time and effort, involve the necessity of having deep expertise, and are not always suitable for large scale farming disease detection. For this purpose, the current research suggests developing an explainable lightweight CNN-based model for crop disease identification based on RGB leaf images. The model utilizes several innovative architectural solutions such as depth-wise separable convolution, SE blocks, skip connections, and guided attention-based feature learning that allow enhancing the effectiveness of features extraction and decreasing computation load. Moreover, Grad-CAM is used to visualize affected areas on a map and thus increase the interpretability of the model. The suggested solution was implemented and tested on the PlantVillage dataset containing 54,305 images for 38 crop diseases out of 14 crops. The results show that the training, validation, and testing accuracies equal 97.6%, 88.3%, and 97.63%, correspondingly, along with the Macro-F1 measure of 0.867 and Micro-ROC-AUC equal to 0.99. A comparative study reveals that the presented model performs comparably well in terms of classification with lightweight structure and built-in interpretability capabilities to be applied in the mobile and edge-enabled agriculture environment. The results show that the presented approach is capable of being used as an effective and interpretable tool for diagnosing plant diseases in real-time.

Introduction

Agriculture is essential for providing a livelihood and feed the world population. The production of crops helps maintain economic stability and improves the lives of rural communities in developing nations. A major challenge for farmers is plant diseases, which cause large amounts of yield losses and crop quality degradation worldwide every year1. Early detection and accurate diagnosis of plant diseases allow for timely intervention, reducing crop loss and minimizing unnecessary use of pesticides. Traditional disease diagnostics utilize visual inspection by agricultural specialists; however, they are time-consuming and subjective. This method may also not be practical for farmers in rural areas, where agricultural specialists may not be present2. In this context, integrating artificial intelligence (AI) and computer vision with agriculture is promising, especially when combined with Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) to classify plant images as diseased with a high degree of accuracy3. While many solutions have been developed using CNNs to detect plant disease recently, significant portions of the models are developed using small- or imbalanced-datasets. This limits their ability to be generalized to various crops and diseases4. Second, most models don’t make it clear how decisions are made. This is especially important in agriculture, where trust and understanding are key to getting people to use them. Explainable AI (XAI) methods like Grad-CAM are starting to fill this gap by giving us visual information about how models make decisions, but they haven’t been used much in agricultural AI systems yet5,6. Moreover, numerous advanced models employ intricate architectures such as hybrid transformers or rely on multi-modal data (e.g., integrating image data with sensor or satellite inputs)7,8.

In spite of the considerable advancements made in CNN-based disease identification models for plants, there are certain limitations which prevent these systems from being successfully implemented within real-time agricultural operations. Current deep learning models are mostly designed to deliver maximum efficiency within a laboratory setting using well-structured data sets with no disturbances in terms of lighting and backgrounds. But in practice, when applying the system to field data sets, the results might not be as promising due to numerous external influences that are present. Furthermore, many state-of-the-art solutions make use of advanced computing systems like Vision Transformers, CNN+transformer models, or multimodal sensor-based systems. All these technologies impose high demands for computational resources and stability of the network infrastructure, thus making them rather hard to implement in remote agricultural locations.

Despite the use of techniques like Grad-CAM and LIME in order to make models more transparent through explainable AI (XAI), such techniques are currently utilized mainly for post-hoc visualization in existing research papers without paying attention to efficiency, scalability, or usability in practical cases for farmers or agronomists. In addition to that, some existing approaches limit themselves to considering not more than a certain amount of plant species or different disease types which reduces the scope of their applicability in terms of large scale agricultural monitoring on diverse plant types. Thus, one needs to develop a lightweight, scalable and explainable framework for crop disease identification with high accuracy, yet keeping the computation cost relatively low and providing real-time implementation possibilities. To this end, the current paper proposes a novel XAI-based lightweight CNN architecture combining depth-wise separable convolution layers, squeeze-and-excitation blocks, residual networks and Grad-CAM-based interpretation mechanisms. Contrary to multimodal and transformer-based models that are computationally costly, the proposed approach considers only RGB leaf images and can thus be easily used on smartphones, drones and other edge devices in agriculture when classifying 38 types of diseases.

The innovation of the suggested approach can be attributed to the introduction of a lightweight and structurally improved CNN architecture coupled with the use of explainable AI techniques. The proposed model differs from the majority of existing works that deal with specific crops or only part of the PlantVillage dataset by its ability to scale up to 38 different plant diseases. Also, the architecture uses depthwise separable convolutions, skip connections, and feature refinement via attention mechanism to extract features while minimizing complexity. Finally, the use of Grad-CAM allows increasing the interpretability of the CNN and implementing it for agricultural purposes without access to cloud services or specialized software. The proposed framework is created to work as a one-modality system relying solely on RGB leaf images. All experiments presented in this paper were conducted only with RGB images as input. The system is designed to perform image-based plant disease classification using CNN architecture combined with explainability (Grad-CAM).

The rationale behind this research originates from an articulated need for an accessible and reliable plant disease detection framework. At the heart of this project lies the construction of a reliable yet interpretable machine learning algorithm for detecting disease in plants based solely on RGB imagery from the PlantVillage Dataset9. With cost-effective access through smartphones, the proposed model utilizes a minimal convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture that incorporates visual explanations using Grad-CAM to facilitate understanding of predictive decisions for users (farmers, agronomists, researchers) at a field level. As a result, this has led to the development of a high level of performance and interpretability (e.g., providing both explanations of individual predictions and minimally computationally intensive) which makes the model a particularly desirable candidate for deployment at the field level. The proposed framework is a new CNN architecture designed specifically for the automated detection of plant diseases. The model has been trained on the entire PlantVillage data set and covers 38 classes of crop diseases using only RGB image data. The integration of Grad-CAM provides an improved level of interpretability and transparency into the model’s prediction process creating more trustworthy and useful decision support tools for users. Additionally, the architecture being developed is lightweight and efficient by not relying on multi-modal or transformer based inputs, making it possible to deploy in resource-constrained environments. Finally, performance evaluations of the proposed framework are included through the use of various standard performance measures and a comparative analysis with other existing state-of-the-art models. In addition to being able to demonstrate its effectiveness on a variety of crop diseases, the framework has also been developed with a focus on scalability to provide a generalizable solution across many crops and diseases with a minimal computational burden.

The system’s performance is evaluated using multiple metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC, and PR curves. It is benchmarked against other top models from recent studies1,3,6,7,10. Experimental results show that the proposed system achieves a test accuracy of 97.63%. This figure surpasses many existing models while keeping interpretability and low computational costs. The rest of this paper is organized as follows. Section “Related works” provides a literature survey on deep learning-based plant disease detection and the role of explainable AI in improving model transparency. Section “Proposed methodology” describes the proposed CNN-based methodology, including preprocessing strategies, architectural design, and explainability methods. Section “Results and implementation” outlines the dataset details, visual analytics, evaluation metrics, and a performance comparison study. Finally, Section “Conclusion” concludes the paper and discusses future directions aimed at expanding the system’s capabilities for real-world agricultural deployment.

Related works

Deep learning has become one of the most widely studied applications of AI in agriculture over the last few years, including the detection of plant diseases. The primary motivation for using deep learning on agricultural disease diagnosis is to help automate the diagnosis process and improve diagnosis accuracy. For example, Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) have proven to be a very effective way to analyze leaf images in order to accurately identify disease with relatively little human involvement. Many researchers have investigated and built different models of CNNs, hybrid models and multimodal approaches that attempt to increase the accuracy, interpretability and deploy-ability of these models in dense and variable agricultural landscapes. Explainable AI (XAI) will help to improve the transparency of model predictions and, therefore, the likelihood of these deep learning methods to be adopted by farmers and agronomists. This section reviews some of the major advancements made in deep learning-based plant disease detection, categorizing the results of current research into five main categories: architectural innovation, explainability, multimodal integration, real world deploy-ability and transfer learning. This review will identify the limitations of current research, and will identify areas in which this research will contribute to addressing the gaps in the field.

CNN-based models for plant disease classification

Plant disease detection (or diagnosis) has seen some of its best solutions relying on CNNs. A CNN automatically identifies and extracts features from a leaf image spatially and texturally. Because of this automatic extraction of features, CNNs have been shown to effectively identify very slight visual cues of various diseases affecting plants. Hasan et al.1 described a hybrid CNN-SVM model called CSXAI that integrates visual explanations using Grad-CAM. The CSXAI model was designed to be a lightweight model and to provide high computational efficiency and an F1 score equal to 97.5% on the well-known PlantVillage dataset. Building on the strengths of both CNNs and SVMs, the model provided accurate results while optimising the decision boundary for classification needs, thus providing both accurate classification and an explanation to the end user. Likewise, Verma et al.2 proposed a unified lightweight CNN model developed for use in detecting diseases that affect major crops, such as corn, rice, and wheat. The accuracy achieved was 96.3 per cent across a range of public and custom datasets, showing that this lightweight model should be capable of being generalised across crops. The above studies collectively demonstrate the rapid growth of CNN types for scalable and robust land disease classification through CNN architectures.

Architectural enhancements to improve accuracy and generalization

While traditional CNNs have shown strong classification abilities, several researchers have proposed architectural enhancements to improve model robustness, generalization, and accuracy. Ashurov et al.3 proposed an innovative new architecture for a depth-wise CNN embedding with Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) blocks and skip residual connections. This new design improved the ability to recalibrate features while preserving the hierarchical organization of spatial features, ultimately leading to 98.1% classification accuracy on various datasets of plant leaves. The computational efficiency provided by the model’s depth-wise separable convolutions makes it ideal for real-time inference. Another advancement was made by Adegbola and collaborators’11 development of an ensemble learning model that utilizes ResNet, DenseNet and InceptionV3 as component networks working together to combine their strengths in order to provide maximum stability and accuracy in predicting plant species. The accuracy of the ensemble model was 98.6% on PlantVillage, indicating that this approach provided improved reliability, handling and robustness than any of the individual networks when processing complicated patterns of visual imagery during object recognition. Overall, these innovations in architecture demonstrate how fundamentally refining baseline CNN architectures can result in substantial improvements to both system performance and ease of deployment.

Explainable AI in agricultural diagnosis

One of the main weaknesses of deep learning models, especially in sensitive applications like agriculture, is that they are generally considered black boxes. In order for users, particularly farmers and agronomists, to be able to trust the outputs of the models, they must be able to understand why the model made each prediction. To give users better insight into how models are functioning, researchers have begun implementing XAI techniques into CNN-based models. For example, Natarajan et al.4 created an innovative deep neural architecture that was combined with meta-visualizations to provide interpretability in predicting plant diseases. This model achieved an accuracy above 98% while also generating easily interpretable heatmaps showing which parts of the plant were affected by disease. Similarly, Alhammad et al.6 utilized Grad-CAM and LIME to visualize the predictions of their CNN model for potato leaf images. The combined usage of saliency maps and local feature attribution allowed farmers and agronomists to follow how specific symptoms contributed to the overall classification of an object. An important aspect of using XAI in agriculture is that many times whether a proposed AI solution can actually be used in the real world is based upon the explainability of the AI solution. Thus, XAI technology is critical to enhancing the transparency, trust, and eventual adoption of AI solutions in agricultural applications.

Multimodal and sensor-based approaches

Multiple studies have investigated multimodal data solutions which combine visual data with environmental data and spectral data to improve plant disease detection beyond standard image-only methods. Aviles Toledo and his colleagues5 created a multimodal deep learning model which used remote sensing data together with attention mechanisms to forecast crop yields that plant breeding programs need. The model showed strong performance, achieving a high correlation with actual data (R² > 0.90). This proved that satellite imagery along with contextual information offered significant value. Kolluri et al.8 built on this idea by creating a CNN-RNN hybrid model that merged image data with environmental sensor information.The model demonstrated strong performance because it achieved high correlation with actual data (R² > 0.90) which proved that satellite imagery together with contextual information provided essential value. Kolluri et al.8 advanced this concept by developing a CNN-RNN hybrid model which combined image data with environmental sensor information. Their method produced an accuracy of 95.3% because it enabled better understanding of context and time-related changes. The models demonstrate their ability to diagnose diseases and estimate crop yields accurately, but they need advanced equipment and specialized systems which makes them unsuitable for use in remote areas that lack technical resources. The system requires access to sensor networks or satellites which creates deployment difficulties for regions that only support smartphone usage and basic internet connection.

Real-world deployment and model robustness

For plant disease detection models’ practical utility, a critical issue is the dropping performance level after practical applications are performed under uncontrolled conditions. Laboratory-based (or system-trained) models have poor generalization to field-captured (or real-world) data that is often subject to large amounts of illumination changes (poor lighting conditions) due to occlusions (objects obstructing a camera’s view) in addition to being disrupted by background noise. Adegbola et al. provide an example of this lack of generalization by creating a new dataset consisting of thousands of images of actual field captured images that have imperfections in them such as shadows, glare, inconsistent framing and have significantly different appearances than what would be expected from current laboratory-trained datasets.

Adegbola et al. successfully trained a convolutional neural network on this dataset producing a model with an accuracy of 93.8% which demonstrated that the CNN-based model could be very robust to the noise that is seen in the real-world images. Hamid et al. also built off of the work of Adegbola et al. by creating mobile-friendly, lightweight CNN architectures that could run on mobile devices. By optimizing their models for edge computing hardware, they were able to achieve greater than 94% real-time inference accuracy for potato and tomato diseases even under field conditions. These two studies provide evidence to support and encourage the movement away from laboratory-centric theories toward mobile devices providing direct support to farmers via diagnostic tools available as a mobile application or by utilizing handheld devices.

Reviews and taxonomies of deep learning techniques

To guide future developments, several comprehensive reviews have consolidated insights from existing plant disease detection systems. Shoaib et al.7 reviewed more than 100 studies that utilized deep learning and pointed to common obstacles, including dataset imbalance, a lack of generalizable cross-domain data, and overfitting small-scale datasets. Their literature review called for lightweight models and better curation of data. In addition, Tugrul et al.12 established a taxonomy classifying CNN architectures used in classifying plant diseases using the amount of convolution, feature aggregation methods, and training methodology as criteria for classification. Gülmez13,14 completed targeted reviews of rice and maize disease detection and described the nuances of disease phenotype collection for various cereal crops. Upadhyay et al.15 expanded their scope through their research by exploring precision agriculture systems employing CNNs and transformers and multimodal neural networks. Each of these reviews indicated researchers need to develop models that can scale along with their operations while offering results that are interpretable, and perform efficiently on diverse agricultural-based hardware systems.

Transfer learning and hybrid architectures

Transfer learning is one of the best ways to improve plant disease detection when there is not enough annotated data available to having to possibility of training your own models from scratch. By fine-tuning pretrained networks using agricultural datasets researchers have been able to tap into the knowledge gained from analyzing large amounts of data using datasets such as ImageNet. In a comparative analysis of different classifiers, Dolatabadian et al.16 demonstrated that CNNs outperformed traditional ML classifiers significantly (approximately 98%) in classification accuracy. Aboelenin et al.10 developed a hybrid architecture that combined CNN and ViT layers to allow the model to find both local and global features. The result was a classification accuracy of approximately 98.2% on mixed leaf datasets indicating that the hybrid architecture improved spatial generalization of the model predictions. Hukkeri et al.17 also demonstrated using pretrained networks (VGG16 and ResNet50) in combination with agricultural image datasets that transfer learning yields good quality models regardless of the size of the dataset used for training. These studies indicate that hybrid architectures and transfer learning continue to be important techniques in facilitating the expansion of AI-based solutions for agricultural applications with limited access to data.

On the other hand, however, there are certain limitations with current CNN-based and explainable AI-based plant disease detection models that should be addressed despite their impressive performance. First of all, many researchers focus mostly on model accuracy and overlook important considerations for deploying such systems in practice including efficiency of computation, scalability across various types of crops, inference in real time, and feasibility of using them in agriculture under resource-limited conditions. Lightweight CNN networks are known to offer faster inference but have lower abilities to represent features as well as generalize across various types of diseases. At the same time, transformer networks and hybrid multimodal systems provide more efficient learning of features but are associated with higher requirements for computation, data, and sensors infrastructure. Furthermore, although explainable AI algorithms like Grad-CAM and LIME are useful in terms of making predictions understandable, many existing works implement them mainly as visualization methods without taking into account model deployability and inference in light weight.Previous approaches have been found to lack robustness in practice when faced with environmental constraints such as background noise, illumination changes, and visual overlaps among different diseases.

Consequently, there is still a need for a model that will deliver high performance, be lightweight, interpretable, scalable, and usable in real-world applications for detecting various types of crop diseases using a smartphone or an edge-based agricultural device. The proposed framework seeks to solve these problems through the use of separable convolutions, squeeze and excitation operations, residual learning, attention-driven feature extraction, and Grad-CAM explainability in a lightweight CNN model trained on all the diseases present in the PlantVillage data set (38 diseases).

Observations and scope for improvement

Throughout the literature review, we have identified several commonalities among CNN-based models for detecting plant diseases. They provide good results across multiple crops, and there are strong examples to support the use of these models in agricultural applications. Unfortunately, some limitations exist that impede the practical use of CNN-based models for detecting plant diseases. For example, most models are trained on structured data, which do not conform well to what is usually found in agricultural systems, including varying lighting conditions, background noise, and lack of clarity in images. While implementing sensor-based and/or multimodal systems will provide additional reallife context, these types of systems do require additional hardware to operate and therefore cannot easily be implemented across many rural or low-resource areas where this type of infrastructure is unavailable. Finally, most models are computationally intensive, limiting their ability to run on portable or low-power devices. Another major challenge lies in the limited integration of explainable AI. Techniques such as Grad-CAM and LIME18,19 have been studied, but they are still mainly in an experimental stage and separate from the workflows of their end-users. Also, although Farmers are beginning to use more smartphones to do their agricultural work, there have only been limited numbers of models that have been developed for mobile or edge use. This makes it important to have easy-to-use, interpretable, and image-only methodologies that are both academically sound and usable. There appears to be a distinct gap between advances in technology related to machine learning (ML) and real-life use by the stakeholders in agriculture such as farmers and agronomists. The present research study aims to fill this void by developing a convolutional neural network (CNN)-based model that provides high accuracy and provides transparency by using Grad-CAM, with each being designed for low-cost, real-time use in diagnosing crop disease at the field level. The methodology was developed to use only RGB image inputs and to minimize the architectural complexity of the model, which matches the limitations of practical farming restrictions and digital capacity in most farming communities. Proposed methodology

This section outlines the proposed system for early crop disease detection. Section “System architecture” details the system architecture, Section “Workflow and algorithm” explains the workflow and core algorithm, and Section “Mathematical formulation” presents the mathematical formulation supporting the CNN-based diagnosis framework.

System architecture

The proposed system architecture for crop disease detection through image analysis uses explainable convolutional neural networks (CNNs) is shown in Fig. 1. The system operates exclusively with RGB image data that field personnel capture through drones and smartphones and stationary cameras used in agricultural settings. The system operates without requiring environmental sensor inputs which leads to simpler installation processes and enables its use in agricultural environments that lack these sensors because it requires no external technology.

The Data Input module initiates the pipeline by capturing RGB images of plant leaves through standard imaging equipment. The Preprocessing and Augmentation module processes these images through multiple essential changes. The system applies image rotation and scaling operations to create additional data samples which improve model performance, while color normalization ensures all image sources show consistent brightness and illumination levels. The CNN system needs this stage to provide it with uniform input data which supports its learning process.

Next, the images that have been processed are given to an image feature-extraction module that uses a lightweight visual convolutional neural network (CNN) for feature extraction. The backbone of the visual CNN consists of squeeze-and-excite (SE) blocks, depth-wise separable convolution (DWSC), and residual connections or skip connections. Each of these architectural enhancements improves the efficiency and representational power of the CNN to extract the complex disease patterns present in the images such as lesions, discolouration of the skin, and texture irregularities while using the least computational resources possible.

The proposed CNN architecture receives inputs in the form of RGB images of dimension 256 × 256 × 3. The network comprises three blocks containing 32, 64, and 128 filters each with 3 × 3 kernel size, batch normalization, ReLU activation function, and max-pooling layers. Depth-wise separable convolution is used in the deeper layers to decrease computational burden, and Squeeze-and-Excitation (SE) blocks refine channel-wise features. Residual skip connections have been employed to aid in backpropagation. Post feature extraction, Global Average Pooling and feature refinement with attention are performed before classifying the image. A fully connected layer with 256 units and subsequent dropout (0.5), as well as the Softmax layer with 38 units corresponding to the disease labels, completes the architecture. It has been trained using the Adam optimizer with learning rate of 0.001, batch size of 32, and loss as categorical cross-entropy. With 2.1 million parameters, it is lightweight enough for use on mobile devices. The uniqueness of the proposed CNN architecture resides in its use of depth-wise separable convolutions, SE blocks, residual learning, and Grad-CAM explainability in a computationally efficient neural network for multi-class crop disease detection.

The architecture of the proposed CNN model is built to ensure that it is both light-weighted and efficient enough to perform multi-class crop disease classification using deep learning. The network receives input images of size 256 × 256 × 3 in terms of the RGB image dimensions. The first convolution block is composed of Conv2D layers with a number of 32 filters with 3 × 3 kernels with batch normalization, followed by ReLU activation and a 2 × 2 max pooling layer. The second convolution block comprises of 64 filters of 3 × 3 kernels along with batch normalization and max pooling. In the third convolution block, depth-wise convolutional layers are used which comprise of 128 filters to increase efficiency.

Residual Skip connections are incorporated in between the layers to assist gradient backpropagation without the issue of vanishing gradients while training the model. After extracting features, the resulting feature maps are subjected to a Global Average Pooling (GAP) operation to avoid excessive complexity of parameters. Further, an attention-driven feature enhancement process is employed to focus on disease-specific spatial locations prior to classification. The fully connected classification phase involves a dense layer of 256 units with the help of ReLU activation function, after which there is a dropout layer with a dropout value of 0.5. The final output layer is the Softmax layer comprising 38 neurons.

The model was optimized using Adam optimizer with the initial learning rate set at 0.001 while using categorical cross-entropy loss function. The batch size was set to 32, and the network was trained for five epochs by incorporating early stopping to minimize chances of overfitting. For improved generalization and training capabilities, several regularization techniques were incorporated in the architecture, such as the use of batch normalization, drop out technique in fully connected layers, and L2 weight regularization. Image augmentation techniques were also performed extensively to enhance robustness towards different types of plant diseases. The proposed architecture involves about 2.1 million trainable parameters, which make it efficient and ideal for use in portable agricultural devices.

The proposed architecture is strictly an RGB-image based disease detection algorithm that uses only RGB images of leaf tissues taken from mobile, drone, or other imaging devices. Although the overall architecture was constructed in a modular fashion for future use of environmental sensing information like temperature, humidity, and soil moisture content among others, these multi-modalities are not implemented in the current experiment setup. In this study, image-based disease classification by means of CNN and Grad-CAM is considered, which ensures consistency and reproducibility within a single modality.

After the features have been extracted, they are to the feature fusion layer, which combines different features from the various prior layers into a single set of features using concatenation with an optional attention mechanism. The attention mechanism permits the feature fusion layer to focus on the most important spatial features associated with each disease and assist the feature classification process. The feature classification layer containing fully connected layers and concluding with a softmax activation function will receive the fused feature output. This softmax activation will yield both the predicted disease class together with its respective confidence level. The proposed system relies on its Explainable AI (XAI) module to assist users by improving their understanding of how the model generates predictions, allowing them to make better choices. Through the use of Grad-CAM (Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping) the model produces visual heat maps that highlight the pixels of the images that have contributed to the final classification output. The model can therefore provide interpretability to farmers or agronomists through visual evidence of how the model made a prediction. In addition, although the design system allows for future use of SHAP24, the existing design-against-image will not make use of it. Information will be presented in an easy to understand dashboard format. This dashboard will provide the user with the following summary of the model outputs:

• high level summary of what the prediction indicates (i.e. disease class) along with the confidence level.• a Grad-CAM based visual explanation of the infected areas on the leaf of the image.• actionable insight that provides direction for the user on time and how to intervene for effective disease management.

Workflow and algorithm

As depicted in Fig. 2., the workflow for the proposed system in identifying early signs of crop disease from RGB-Leaf images utilizing an Explainable Convolutional Neural Network Model (CCNNM) is comprised of three main stages: (1) Data Acquisition, (2) Data Preparation, and (3) Feature Engineering. The proposed system will Provide a scalable and interpretable approach for Agriculture or Farms environments that can be easily deployed using mobile or Edge Devices (Example Smart Phones, Drones, or Cameras). The first step within the overall Workflow consists of the acquisition of RGB images of plant leaves using field-deployable acquisition Devices (e.g., smartphones or drones). Therefore there is no need for additional sensor infrastructure. The second step, Data Preparation, begins with the preprocessing of all captured Images. This preprocessing stage involves cleaning and normalization of pixel intensities to ensure input quality is standardized across all collected inputs. Complementarily, Data Augmentation (i.e., rotating, scaling, and flipping) techniques will also be applied to increase training Data diversity, and enhance the generalization Capabilities of the CCNNM Model. Once preprocessed, the data are fed into the Feature Engineering Module where a CCNNM will Automatically extract Visual Features from RGB Images of Plant Leaves. The CCNNM will have been trained on the Recognition of the prevalent Spatial Patterns and Textures associated with Common Plant Diseases (such as Color Changes, Lesions and/or shape distortion). The extracted features go into an Attention and Prediction Unit. An attention mechanism is implemented to accentuate the most informative spatial feature map regions that the model will use to focus on disease-related areas. The key characteristics of the image will be generated and passed through a food pixel’s dense classification head with softmax activation, which will then generate an output with the predicted disease class and the associated confidence level. To support trust and transparency in AI-assisted decision-making, the framework features an Explainability and Output Module. This will utilise Grad-CAM to generate heatmaps of the individual leaf regions that materially influenced the model’s decision, and make this information available to users along with the prediction and confidence output. The final output will contain actionable recommendations, allowing the user (farmer or agriculturalist) to manage the identified disease appropriately. This modular framework guarantees that the overall system will not only be achieved with high diagnostic accuracy; but also be an interpretable as well as practically deployable solution in field environments, particularly where resources are limited.

Results and implementation

Dataset used

The research employs the PlantVillage Dataset which serves as a reference dataset for both plant disease classification and agricultural artificial intelligence research work and can be accessed through the Kaggle platform9. The dataset includes 54,305 RGB images which show both healthy and diseased plant leaves that researchers at Penn State University compiled and released under a public domain license. The images display 38 unique categories which include 14 different crop species such as tomato, potato, corn, apple, grape, and bell pepper and other crops. The image contains a clear label which shows the plant type and disease condition of the plant (e.g., Tomato - Late Blight, Potato - Early Blight, Corn - Healthy) which enables both binary and multi-class classification tasks. The PlantVillage dataset is particularly useful for deep learning research because it offers high-quality images at high resolution and contains a wide range of plant species. The research required artificial environmental sensor data because it wanted to create agricultural conditions which enable multiple learning methods to operate. The additional dataset provides important information about environmental conditions which includes air temperature (°C), relative humidity (%), soil moisture (%), and sunlight intensity (lux)—factors known to influence disease onset and spread. The agricultural climate patterns established the environmental variables which were generated to create validated simulation patterns through scientific research documented in literature.

The researchers introduced missing values into the data at random intervals and used mean substitution together with K-nearest neighbor (KNN) interpolation to fill in the gaps while all continuous variables received z-score normalization as their standardization method. The researchers used image augmentation techniques to create more diverse training samples which would help the model generalize better while reducing the effects of class imbalance. The first method enabled the system to perform random rotations within a range of plus and minus 15 degrees. The system used horizontal flips and zoom-in cropping and brightness/contrast adjustment and Gaussian noise injection as its second method. The system processed all images to a standard size of 256 × 256 pixels while it converted all pixel values into a range from 0 to 1. The researchers used stratified sampling to create three datasets which included training data with 70% and validation data with 15% and testing data with 15% while maintaining equal representation for all classes. The extended dataset allows for multi-modal CNN-based framework development which combines image data with sensor data to train models that analyze visual symptoms and environmental stress factors which match precision agriculture and real-field crop monitoring conditions.

Even though the PlantVillage dataset offers a standardized benchmark for measuring the performance of deep learning models, it mostly contains controlled laboratory images under well-lit conditions with no interference from environmental factors such as cluttered backgrounds, overlapping leaves, and shadows among others. As a result, the PlantVillage dataset does not provide a completely accurate representation of field conditions under which practical images may be captured, since practical images might be influenced by different types of interference. Data augmentation strategies involving techniques such as rotating, scaling, brightness and contrast change, and adding Gaussian noise were used extensively in training the proposed models, but the model needs to be validated using field datasets.

Comparative study

The researchers conducted a complete assessment of their CNN-based plant disease detection system by comparing it against multiple current leading methods. The comparison considers three primary factors: test accuracy, use of explainable AI (XAI) methods, and the dataset employed. The model demonstrated competitive results by achieving a test accuracy of 97.63% on the complete PlantVillage dataset9 which contains 38 classes while maintaining architectural simplicity and interpretability through Grad-CAM integration.

Hasan et al.‘s CSXAI model1 which integrates CNN with SVM and uses Grad-CAM showed a slightly better accuracy result of 99.09% yet the model tested only 10 classes from the PlantVillage dataset which does not reflect actual field conditions. Verma et al.‘s unified lightweight CNN2 demonstrated excellent generalization ability by achieving 95.32% accuracy in three different Corn Rice and Wheat disease classifications yet it did not include any XAI features. The SE-Res-CNN architecture developed by Ashurov et al.3 which combines depth-wise separable convolutions with squeeze-and-excitation blocks and residual connections achieved 98.00% accuracy on a custom multi-species dataset which included Grad-CAM visualizations. Alhammad et al.6 developed a DenseNet121-based model which reached 98.01% accuracy on the Potato Leaf dataset without using XAI while Aboelenin et al.10 created a hybrid EfficientNetB0 + Vision Transformer system which used SHAP explanations to achieve 97.68% accuracy on various leaf datasets. Table 3 shows that multiple models achieve excellent classification results but most of them depend on either specific class boundaries or need heavy computational power or they do not provide understandable results. The proposed model balances its operational performance with the ability to work in real time while providing users with understandable information which enables its effective use in agricultural environments that require quick explanations of results.

Conclusion

The research develops a deep learning system for plant disease identification through a CNN model which trained exclusively on the entire PlantVillage dataset that contains 38 different crop-disease categories. The proposed model achieved a robust test accuracy of 97.63%, demonstrating its capability to accurately classify a wide range of leaf diseases under varied conditions. The system proved effective through complete visualizations which included Grad-CAM heatmaps and prediction confidence distributions and per-class metrics because these elements showed how the system worked to create a usable solution for actual farming environments. The integration of explainable AI (XAI) tools ensures transparency in model decision-making, which is critical for user trust and diagnostic validation, particularly in precision farming environments where model recommendations directly impact crop health decisions. The system requires multiple enhancements which require testing for their potential to increase the system’s ability to function across various contexts. The system requires actual field images which need to be collected during natural light conditions and typical background situations to create a link between laboratory datasets and real-world operational environments. The model would benefit from multi-modal input extension which should include environmental sensor data that tracks temperature and humidity and soil moisture levels to create a complete disease risk assessment system. Incorporating transfer learning into the EfficientNet and Vision Transformer models using attention mechanism has the ability to improve the model’s performance for challenging instances. The model would bring about immense benefits to smallholder farmers by virtue of the model being designed for edge/mobile applications allowing for offline plant assessment to be conducted in field conditions. Although the classification performance is outstanding on the benchmark dataset PlantVillage, the dataset itself is constrained to the laboratory setting and thus does not fully capture the complexities of the agricultural environment. In field conditions, there could be challenges associated with inconsistent lighting, complex background environments, overlapping leaves, motion blurring, and various environmental conditions affecting model performance. As a result, efforts will be focused on validating the developed model framework on actual field data collected under real agricultural conditions.
