Beyond the usual suspects: An unusual cause of pulmonary aneurysm
Authors: Sabreena Moosa, Blair Warren, Fiona Anyumba, Laura L Donahoe, Micheal McInnis, Kong Teng Tan, Sebastian Mafeld
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBronchial artery to pulmonary artery fistula is a rare entity, and presence of an associated aneurysm in such cases is even more uncommon. This case report describes the case of a patient with an incidental diagnosis of a pulmonary aneurysm. The etiology was challenging to determine on noncontrast CT chest. The inflow bronchial artery and outflow pulmonary artery vasculature were delineated with Cone Beam CT, which helped guide management. The aneurysm was then treated with embolization of the inflow bronchial artery. This report presents a unique case utilizing Cone Beam CT in diagnosis and treatment planning, and highlights the diagnostic challenges of this rare entity, as well as utility of Cone Beam CT in further evaluation.Keywords: Bronchial artery, Pulmonary artery, Cone beam, Pulmonary aneurysm

Bronchial artery to pulmonary artery fistulae are an exceptionally rare entity. They may be congenital or acquired [1,2]. These unique fistulae in a high systemic pressure to low pulmonary arterial pressure setting present a diagnostic challenge when presenting with bronchial artery aneurysms, with only few cases reported in the literature [1,2].

Bronchial artery aneurysms can present as life-threatening emergencies if ruptured. Most patients are asymptomatic but may present with massive hemoptysis, hematoma or swallowing discomfort [1]. Typical diagnosis of pulmonary aneurysms is with CT prior to interventions, which include embolization and surgery [1,2]. In this report, we present the case of a pulmonary aneurysm of uncertain etiology confirmed with Cone Beam CT arising from a BA-PA fistula treated with coil embolization.

Case report

A 70-year-old patient initially presented with abdominal pain. Abdominal CT identified an incidental right lower lobe nodule followed by a thoracic CT for further characterization. The patient was asymptomatic from a respiratory perspective, with no hemoptysis and no pertinent respiratory physical exam findings. The patient had no known risk factors including congenital heart disease, infection, vasculitis, and on subsequent evaluation no clinical evidence of Hereditary Hemorrhagic Telangiectasia. Patient’s informed consent was obtained for publication of this case report.

Subsequent noncontrast thoracic CT demonstrated a right lower lobe mass with peripheral calcifications (Fig. 1A) and contrast enhanced CT (Fig. 1, Fig. 1) demonstrated homogenous contrast enhancement of the mass measuring up to 3.5 cm with adjacent tortuous and dilated bronchial artery arising from the descending thoracic aorta and which was in close proximity to the lower lobe pulmonary arterial vasculature. There was no evidence of thrombus formation, local compression on airways, or rupture at the time of study. These findings suggesting this to be an aneurysm but prompted diagnostic angiography for further clarification.

(A) Noncontrast and contrast-enhanced CT chest showing pulmonary aneurysm (red arrow) with wall calcifications, and prominent pulmonary artery (blue arrow). (B) Contrast enhanced CT chest showing a tortuous, prominent bronchial artery from the descending thoracic aorta (yellow arrows).

A diagnostic angiogram was conducted via femoral artery access; the right bronchial artery was initially subselected. This was identified as the inflow vessel to the pulmonary aneurysm and ultimately drained to the pulmonary artery (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Cone beam CT via the bronchial artery confirmed inflow from a hypertrophic right bronchial artery and outflow to a lower lobe pulmonary artery (Fig. 2A and B). This further delineated the aneurysm which was located proximal to the bronchial artery to pulmonary artery fistulous connection. The right lower lobe pulmonary artery was then subselected via femoral vein access, which showed no contrast flow into the aneurysm, likely secondary to high-pressure competitive flow from the bronchial artery (Supplementary Fig. 1B). No direct outflow to pulmonary veins seen. Of note, in this case, the main pulmonary artery pressure was normal (21 mmHg systolic/5 mmHg diastolic [mean pulmonary arterial pressure 10 mmHg]), the fistula was not causing pulmonary hypertension. Transthoracic Echocardiogram demonstrated normal right ventricular function, and a dilated right and left atrium.

(A) Cone beam CT and (B) 3D reconstructions via the bronchial artery confirmed inflow from a hypertrophic bronchial artery and outflow to a lower lobe pulmonary artery. (C) Postembolization angiogram of subselected bronchial artery with microcatheter (yellow arrow), Penumbra coils (blue arrow) deployed in the distal bronchial artery, just proximal to the aneurysm (red circle) and no contrast inflow into the aneurysm (red arrow).

The bronchial artery was embolized with the aim of flow cessation to cause thrombosis of the aneurysm. The right bronchial artery was selected with a C2 catheter and a microcatheter was used to advance distally (Fig. 2C). Penumbra (Penumbra Inc) coils were deployed in the distal bronchial artery, just proximal to the aneurysm. Contrast injection then showed no inflow into the aneurysm (Fig. 2C). The patient was discharged following overnight admission. Follow-up CT 2 months post-embolization with contrast in the pulmonary angiographic and delayed venous phase confirmed no contrast opacification of the aneurysm and unchanged positioning of embolization coils in the bronchial artery (Supplementary Fig. 2). The patient was asymptomatic at the last visit and CT was planned for follow-up in 1 year. Transthoracic Echocardiogram on 1.5 years follow-up demonstrated normal right ventricular function, and normal sized right and left atrium.

Discussion

This report presents a case of pulmonary aneurysm formation secondary to an underlying BA-PA fistula: a rare vascular malformation between the bronchial and pulmonary artery. The pathogenesis is suspected to be secondary to structural defects during fetal development which leads to this anomalous connection [2]. Increased pressure in blood flow may cause dilated bronchial arteries, pressure in the pulmonary vascular bed and aneurysm formation at the point of connection [1,2]. In this case, there was no pulmonary hypertension, possibly due to a narrow communication which may further explain the aneurysmal dilatation. Current recommendations are limited but case reports have recommended treatment at the time of identification due to the unknown nature and risk of rupture [2].

Past reported cases [3,4] have also highlighted the possibility of multiple inflow vessels and challenges in identifying these, with 3 of the previously reported cases requiring surgery [[3], [4], [5]]. In the present case, CT angiogram helped suggest the pulmonary mass was an aneurysm, however, was limited in assessment of inflow and outflow vessels. The use of a selective angiogram with cone beam CT was essential to clarify and confirm inflow and outflow vessels allowing for appropriate embolization planning, highlighting the utility of cone beam CT as a diagnostic tool to confirm and target the appropriate vessel. Past studies have utilized various techniques for diagnosis of inflow and outflow vessels including multiphasic CT chest and bronchial arteriography [5], and 3D CT reconstructions [3], which aided with surgical planning.

Little evidence exists regarding the risk of rupture and treatment options for a BA-PA fistula with bronchial artery aneurysm (Supplementary Table 1). In this case, the aneurysm was treated by deploying embolization coils in the bronchial artery just proximal to the aneurysm. The aneurysm itself was not coiled due to the possible risks of infection, migration and erosion within the lung given its location and large size. Follow-up CT demonstrated aneurysm thrombosis, demonstrating this approach as a treatment option for BA-PA fistula with bronchial artery aneurysm. Of note, patient’s cardiac biatrial dilatation also resolved on follow-up Echocardiogram post-treatment. Most past studies have reported treatment on diagnosis and have employed various treatment techniques including embolization and surgery (Supplementary Table 1). Another question is that of long-term outcomes, as seen in Shimokawaji et al. [4], whilst full case details are lacking, there is a theoretical risk of recanalization and some reports have included long-term follow up.

In conclusion, bronchial artery to pulmonary artery fistula with aneurysm formation is an extremely rare entity. This rare condition poses challenges for diagnosis and management. Interventional bronchial and pulmonary angiogram, with cone beam CT, may help delineate inflow and outflow vessels, aiding with diagnosis and planning for embolization. Given the unknown behavior of this finding and potential risk of rupture, treatment with embolization should be considered.
