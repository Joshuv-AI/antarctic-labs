 ABSTRACT

 This report presents the mechanical characterization of a specific Zry-4 cladding batch serving as
the foundation for a diverse range of fuel performance research at Oak Ridge National Laboratory (ORNL).
This effort supports research needs for the U.S. Department of Energy (DOE), particularly regarding
evaluating accident tolerant fuel (ATF) cladding coating concepts, expanding understanding of cladding
response to loss-of-coolant accidents (LOCA) transients, refining post-critical heat flux (CHF) limits
(t@T), and upcoming irradiation campaigns. The central objective was to define the baseline performance
of the substrate Zircaloy-4 (Zry-4) material leveraged across ORNL Advanced Fuel Campaign (AFC)
efforts through controlled high-temperature vacuum tensile testing. This work begins to address gaps in
existing models where implementation based on nominal heat-treatment labels, such as stress relief
annealed (SRA), often fail to capture the interplay of recovery, recrystallization, and grain growth. To
quantify this, data was benchmarked against the Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL) stress-
strain model to determine where this material falls in comparison to assumed values for materials in the
same heat treatment regime. Analysis of the tensile data revealed that this specific SRA batch exhibits a
transitional microstructural state best described by an effective cold-work (CW) parameter of 0.09,
diverging from the previous estimation of 0.5 for SRA materials. Additionally, comparative testing of Cr-
coated specimens demonstrated no distinct difference in axial strength relative to the bare substrate. This
suggests that the strengthening benefits of Cr coatings observed in burst scenarios are driven by residual
stress mechanisms acting solely in the hoop direction.

 1. Background

 ORNL maintains several ongoing research activities focused on the thermomechanical
performance of zirconium alloys. A specific batch of Zircaloy-4 cladding serves as the foundation for these
efforts, providing a common thread for evaluating Cr-coated Zr performance [1–3], refining the
understanding of zirconium thermomechanical response to LOCA transients [4–6], time-at-temperature
effects to support licensing post-CHF excursions [7,8], and serving as the basis for upcoming irradiation
campaigns. Because this material platform is used across such diverse and high-stakes research streams,
establishing a precise baseline of tensile data is essential to ensure the success of the broader effort.

 The requirement for a batch-specific baseline, despite zirconium being a mature and historic
material system, stems from the fact that the microstructural state in the literature is often obscured by broad
heat-treatment labels and a frequent lack of concurrently reported microstructural and mechanical data. In
industry and research practice, cladding states are typically categorized into discrete regimes based on final
treatment temperatures. SRA is generally defined between 450 and 500 °C while partially-recrystallized
annealed (pRXA) spans 500 to 530 °C [9]. Fully recrystallized annealed (RXA) treatments extend from
approximately 530 to 760 °C. Comstock et al. [9] also provide an alternate classification by defining them
via microstructural fraction rather than temperature alone. In this framework, SRA is defined as a
microstructure exhibiting less than 5% recrystallization, pRXA spans the transition domain of 5 to 95%
recrystallization, and RXA is defined as a fully converted structure containing greater than 95%
recrystallized grains. Whatever definition, these broad categories often overlook the nuanced interplay
between recovery, recrystallization, and grain growth, which are highly sensitive to time, temperature, and
prior cold-work history. Because these mechanisms can occur simultaneously and at varying rates, a
material labeled as "SRA" may possess significantly different dislocation densities and grain structures
depending on the specific path of its thermomechanical processing. This lack of precision introduces
uncertainty when leveraging external datasets or predictive models as a baseline for research efforts.
Additionally, without an accurate understanding of the substrate mechanical performance in relation to the
microstructure, the true efficacy of an ATF Cr coating cannot be isolated from the inherent properties of

the substrate for comparison against external coating processes and connections between LOCA
performance and tensile testing cannot be drawn fully.

 To support these diverse efforts across AFC and ensure the accuracy of upcoming testing planned
through FY26 and FY27, this work establishes a mechanical baseline for this Zry-4 batch using controlled
high-temperature vacuum tensile tests performed at 10⁻⁷ torr. The resulting data were compared against
literature models as a function of the cold work parameter in the PNNL composite model to understand
exactly where this material falls in comparison to assumed values for materials in the SRA treatment
regime. Additionally, testing was performed on Cr-coated specimens to add to the datasets evaluating the
mechanical differences between the coated and uncoated systems.

 2. Materials and Testing Methodology

 SRA Zircaloy-4 tubing was machined into axial tension test (ATT) geometries, a unique dual gauge
geometry (Figure 1(a)) developed at ORNL to derive mechanical properties [10] from cladding materials
in representative tube geometry. The final SRA treatment was performed in a vacuum furnace with a
minimum vacuum level of 10-4 torr and soaked at a temperature of 500 °C for a minimum of 6 hours and a
maximum of 10 hours, according to the manufacturer Cameco Co. For the Cr coated material, the coating
was deposited on the SRA tubing using high power impulse magnetron sputtering and again harvested in
the ATT geometry for comparison. Additionally, some testing was performed on Zry-4 specimens harvested
from a channel box in the RXA condition, representing the opposite end of the heat treatment spectrum
with higher ductility and lower strength. The RXA specimens were harvested into a modified dogbone
geometry, Figure 1(b). Testing was performed from RT up to 750 °C, below the dual phase region, in a
Varian vacuum tensile system [11] at a vacuum level of 10-7 torr. The specimens were tested at a strain rate
of 10-3 s-1 as determined by crosshead speed. Samples were held at temperature for ~30 min before pulling.
Note, ductility is presented throughout the work via stress-strain curves, but is not compared quantitatively,
given the difference in geometries between the ATT and modified dogbone, and the know effects of
geometry on total elongation [12], especially for sub-sized specimen.

Figure 1. (a) Cr coated SRA ATT specimen before and after testing at 750 °C. (b) RXA Zry-4 specimen in
the modified dogbone geometry as received and post testing at 750 °C.

 The ATT specimens consisted of dual gauges and were harvested from cladding with a wall-
thickness of 0.575 mm with an outer diameter of 9.5 mm. ATT specimens had a cord length of 2 mm, with
each gauge having a length of 4 mm. The modified dogbone geometry, Figure 1(b), had a gauge thickness,
width, and length of 0.7 mm, 2 mm, and 7.62 mm, respectively. Yield stress (YS) was determined using
the 0.2% offset method, and ultimate tensile strength (UTS) was defined as the maximum stress observed.
Digital image correlation (DIC) was performed on select specimens, for which the data will be briefly
presented here and expanded upon with future FY26 work. Images were collected using 50 mm lenses
acquired from Edmund Optics and FLIR Grasshopper3 cameras.

 The SRA Zry-4 tubing has been extensively investigated during ORNL activities, with the as-
manufactured microstructure well characterized by electron microscopy. Figure 2(a) presents an IPF map
collected by electron backscatter diffraction (EBSD), reproduced from Bell et al. [6]. To simply investigate
grain morphology of the RXA harvested from the channel box, optical imaging was performed after
polishing and etching the grain structure to leverage differential interference contrast techniques. The
differential interference contrast image for the RXA Zry-4 material is presented in Figure 2(b). The clear
discrepancy in grain morphology is evident, where the bimodal grain size in the SRA material is contrasted
with that of the uniform grain size and structure of the channel box. While not shown, the perpendicular
directions were also investigated for the RXA channel box material and of similar morphology, indicating
the structure as equiaxed. Figure 3 presents kernel average misorientation (KAM) maps acquired from the
SRA Zry-4 material [6], confirming the bimodal grain structure consists of high misorientation matrix
surrounding recrystallized grains.

 Further details around the form of the model and input parameters for the equations above can be
found in PNNL-17700 [13], and will not be discussed in detail here. The major variables of consequence
in the material models include neutron fluence, hydrogen pickup, oxygen pickup, and CW. Given the
material tested in this condition was as-received, the major variable of impact to this work is the level of
CW. Assigning precise CW value to SRA material for this model is inherently challenging. While the
fabrication process involves significant cold reduction, the final stress-relief anneal induces annealing
which fundamentally alters the effective mechanical and microstructural state. Recognizing this
uncertainty, PNNL-17700 acknowledges that CW levels are frequently not given and therefore estimated
based other factors in the reference database and in the example data, assumed to be 0.5 to represent the
standard SRA condition [13]. By benchmarking the currently tested material against this model, the
discrepancy between the ORNL Zry-4 SRA batch and the general behavior of Zircaloys can be established.

 The strength trends determined from the experimental stress-strain curves are presented in Figure
7(a) and Figure 7(b) for UTS and YS, respectively, alongside predictions of UTS and YS from the PNNL
model and UTS data from previous vacuum testing on RXA Zry-4 from Talia et al. [14]. Two PNNL curves
are presented, one for the previously attributed CW = 0.50 for SRA material previously and CW = 0.09,
the best fit to the experimental data in this work. This CW = 0.09 value showed well agreement for both
YS and UTS curves across all temperatures, although it was significantly lower than the previously assumed
CW level for SRA. The UTS values for the RXA Zry-4 specimens harvested from the channel box showed
well agreement with the work from Talia et al. [14]. The only discrepancy between the RXA trendlines was
at temperatures below ~150 °C.

3.3 Preliminary Comparison Against Burst Performance

 A key objective of this work is to enable future efforts to bridge the gap between simple mechanical
testing and LOCA burst performance. For LOCA fuel performance modeling, it remains an open question
how fundamental mechanical tests, such as creep or tensile testing, can reliably predict accident
performance of fuel cladding. Specifically, predicting deformation requires a clearer understanding of the
exact weighting between time-dependent creep and viscoplastic behavior. Currently, emphasis is only
placed on the creep performance due to entrenched historic models. Traditionally, LOCA performance is
assessed by internally pressurizing a cladding tube and heating it to simulate accident conditions. During
this transient, the cladding eventually deforms under load (colloquially known as “ballooning”) until
rupture. The engineering hoop stress and temperature at the moment of rupture define the cladding's "burst
strength," which serves as the industry's gold standard.

 UTS offers a natural point of comparison to connect applied burst testing to simple mechanical
testing, being the maximum stress that can be endured and the onset of plastic instability which leads to
failure. Figure 9 presents the UTS data collected in this work alongside the historic Chapman correlation
[15,16], an empirical model that is linked heating rate, fill pressure, and resulting burst temperature and has
been utilized for almost 50 years. Figure 9(a) compares these values from room temperature up to 900 °C,
while Figure 9(b) focuses on the region most relevant to burst testing.

 Examining the broader trends in Figure 9(a), the Chapman correlation and the 1 °C/s experimental
data from Bell et al. [5] generally align with the UTS values at higher temperatures but deviate at lower

 11

temperatures. At higher temperatures more relevant to burst testing, Figure 9(b), the curves are generally
in agreement, but discrepancies are present. This discrepancy found at higher temperatures more relevant
to LOCA performance is expected for a few reasons. First, comparing the maximum load in a tensile test
to the burst stress in a LOCA test is an inherent mismatch. This is because uniaxial tensile tests are
displacement-driven where load is a dynamic response to the material and UTS indicates the onset of plastic
instability, whereas burst tests are driven by constant internal pressure and increasing temperature where
burst is defined by material separation and decompression. Second, the transient heating in burst tests
produces microstructures that are not directly comparable to those in standard tensile tests, even at slower
heating rates. Finally, the ATT data probes longitudinal properties, while internally pressurized burst tests
probe hoop-direction performance, enabling discrepancies due to drawing and subsequently texture effects.

 Despite these fundamental differences, this data set establishes a foundation for ongoing FY26 work,
which will compare tensile properties to high-fidelity DIC burst data in a more rigorous manner. Recent
work by Bell et al. [5] demonstrated that for Fe-based alloys like C26M, DIC can identify the onset of non-
uniform strain during a burst test. Because this point corresponds to the classical definition of UTS and
plastic instability in mechanical testing, this technique will enable a much more direct comparison between
fundamental tensile properties and LOCA burst performance in future efforts. This will be valuable, as it
will illuminate the role of visco-plasticity more clearly in LOCA burst performance.

 4. Analysis and Discussion

4.1 Effective Cold Work and the Limitations of Nominal SRA Designations

 While the standard correlations often assume SRA material retains moderate residual cold work
(CW = 0.5) [13], the experimental data suggests a much softer and more evolved microstructural state (CW
= 0.09) for this Zry-4 batch. This indicates the parametric nature of CW in this model, leading to a pseudo
relation to microstructural state. Indeed, the developers of the PNNL model [13] explicitly noted the
difficulty in determining this CW parameter. They acknowledged that a single value related to processing
prior to heat treatment cannot easily capture the total microstructural state and should instead be treated as
an effective parameter.

 This discrepancy and ambiguity are a direct result of the nuanced interplay between recovery,
recrystallization, and grain growth during alpha annealing procedures. While SRA is nominally defined by
a temperature range of 450 to 500 °C, deemed below the recrystallization temperature, recrystallization is
actually a dynamic Arrhenius threshold governed by the confluence of time, temperature, and prior
deformation history [17]. In zirconium, this threshold is particularly sensitive to the strong plastic
anisotropy of the HCP lattice. The dominant prismatic slip system operates at a significantly lower critical
resolved shear stress than the twinning, basal, or pyramidal systems [18]. To maintain coherency during
forming, the material is forced to partition strain energy based on crystallographic alignment, effectively
fragmenting the microstructure into distinct "pockets" of varying stored distortion.

 Because the activation barrier for recrystallization scales inversely with stored energy, the 500 °C
treatment functions as a quasi-selective threshold. Structural heterogeneities act as preferential nucleation
sites [19], allowing the available thermal input to trigger recrystallization only in high-energy regions.
Meanwhile, lower-strain regions merely undergo static recovery within the 6 h window. This disparity
causes the microstructure to bifurcate, with new grain growth constrained by the local strain-energy field.

 13

 The dual morphology outlined is clearly observed in the grain structures shown in Figure 2 and
Figure 3. This microstructural observation aligns with using a lower CW = 0.09 in the tensile model
compared to more utilized CW = 0.5 for nominally SRA. This indicates the CW parameter functions as a
volume-averaged descriptor, integrating the mechanical contributions of the recovered matrix and the soft,
recrystallized grains.

 The discrepancy between the nominal CW = 0.5 SRA assumption and the fitted CW = 0.09
highlights an oversight risk in predictive modeling generally for zirconium cladding. Since models like the
PNNL stress-strain correlation serve as a standard benchmark for fuel performance codes like BISON,
blindly selecting default inputs based on broad heat-treatment categories and previous assumptions that
may have worked for a specific “SRA”, can lead to erroneous predictions. Even though the current batch
was denoted by the manufacturer as “SRA”, the 500 °C anneal places this specific batch at a transitional
onset of pRXA that a binary label fails to capture. Future efforts should transition models away from broad
labeling of material condition, and toward the use of internal state variables such as measured dislocation
densities, recrystallized volume fractions, and recrystallized grain size.

 One such avenue for improvement is to leverage an expanded version of the commonly used CAP
developed by Garzarolli et al. [20], which remains the industry standard for assessing alpha annealing heat
treatments based on recrystallized fraction [9]. While the CAP effectively identifies the kinetic window for
the onset of recrystallization, it lacks the explicit incorporation of grain growth kinetics for the newly
formed grains, possibly losing some nuance to assessing microstructural state. The overlap of these
mechanisms was recently demonstrated by Kim et al. [21] for Zr-Nb alloys, where given enough time
anneals even at 450 °C can lead to full depletion of the matrix and grains resembling more so that of a
“RXA” structure. Transitioning toward a model that accounts for the non-discrete nature of heat treatments
and simultaneous evolution of recrystallized volume fraction alongside grain size would allow for a more
accurate prediction of mechanical properties, particularly in the transitional pRXA regime where this batch
borders. These efforts would also be symbiotic with t@T research.

4.2 Cr Coated Cladding Mechanical Performance

 The lack of performance increase for Cr coated testing, at first glance, may be confusing given the
well documented mechanical performance improvements of Cr coated Zr alloys during accident conditions,
particularly during LOCA burst testing using the SATS, has demonstrated increases in burst temperatures
around ~60 to 80 °C [1]. In more impactful terms, this was equivalent to increasing the wall thickness of
the cladding by a factor of 1.77. Being internally loaded, burst testing more heavily probes strength in the
hoop direction. This work, in conjunction with previous works on burst testing, could suggest from a macro
engineering scale that the strengthening only occurs along the hoop direction. This is particularly supported
by the fact that from room temperature up to 750 °C, there was clearly no evidence of impact in axial
direction, also indicating that high temperature soaks did not remove any possible residual stresses in the
axial direction. These observations are in congruence with X-ray diffraction measurements performed by
Graening et al. [22] on various batches of HiPIMS Cr coatings, including batches made with the same -90
V processing parameters used to manufacture the current batch. Graening et al. showed that residual stress
in the axial direction was largely neutral for relevant batch comparisons, while residual stress in the
tangential direction was strongly compressive and therefore resistant to external tensile loading during burst
testing. As such the engineering scale observations in this work support prior observations that residual
stress components, and the effects on mechanical performance thereof, are largely in the hoop direction.

 5. Conclusions

 A representative mechanical baseline for this Zircaloy-4 batch provides a needed reference point
for ORNL’s broader fuel performance studies. By refining the CW parameter from the PNNL model from
the standard 0.5 assumption to a physically representative 0.09, these findings establish a relationship for
this material compared to other datasets, necessary for interpreting the batch's response across LOCA, t@T,
and irradiation campaigns. While a transition toward modeling based on internal microstructural state
variables should be pursued, the successful calibration of the substrate’s physical state provides a robust
platform for ongoing ORNL research leveraging this batch. The investigation into Cr-coated claddings
suggests decoupled axial behavior from the hoop-dominated strengthening observed in burst scenarios. This
isolation of directional properties alongside previous ORNL efforts further supports that the coating-
induced strengthening in the tangential direction is related to the residual stress state.
