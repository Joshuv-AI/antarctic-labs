Accelerating finite-element-based projector augmented-wave density functional theory calculations with scalable GPU-centric computational methods
Kartick Ramakrishnan, Phani Motamarri

Abstract
Accurate large-scale Kohn–Sham density functional theory (DFT) calculations are essential for modeling material systems, including interfaces, defects, nanoclusters, twisted two-dimensional heterostructures, and more. However, achieving desired accuracy at the scale of 10 4 10^{4} – 10 5 10^{5} electrons for these complex systems, with practical time-to-solution, remains a significant challenge for existing DFT implementations. We present GPU-centric computational methods and algorithmic innovations within a finite-element (FE) discretized projector augmented-wave (PAW) based density functional theory formulation ( PAW-FE ), for accurate, efficient, and scalable electronic-structure calculations on modern exascale computing systems. The FE discretization of the PAW governing equations, derived in this work within a collinear spin formalism, naturally accommodates generic boundary conditions and employs multi-resolution quadrature approach for accurate evaluation of atom-centered PAW integrals on coarse FE grids. The underlying FE-discretized PAW generalized Hermitian eigenproblem is solved using the Residual-based Chebyshev Filtered Subspace Iteration (R-ChFSI) procedure. In particular, exploiting R-ChFSI’s tolerance to inexact matrix–multivector products, we employ an approximate inverse PAW overlap matrix, reduced-precision arithmetic (FP32/TF32) and low-precision nearest-neighbour communication (BF16) during Chebyshev-filtered subspace construction, combined with a block-wise computation–communication overlap strategy to reduce the computational cost without sacrificing the numerical robustness. These strategies yield an overall CPU–GPU speedup of 8 × \times and 20 × \times on Intel and AMD GPU architectures, respectively. Relative to widely used plane-wave PAW implementations, PAW-FE achieves about 8 × \times reduction in minimum wall time to solution on system sizes of approximately 10,000 electrons using NVIDIA GPUs, with greater gains at larger system sizes, and around 6 × \times relative to norm-conserving pseudopotential-based FE implementations. We further demonstrate practical scalability on material systems comprising 130,000 electrons, establishing PAW-FE as an exascale-ready method for large-scale, chemically accurate first-principles simulations.

1  Introduction

Electronic-structure calculations have played a pivotal role in accurately predicting diverse material properties and today stand as the primary workhorse in modern materials discovery. Applications of electronic-structure calculations based on Kohn–Sham density functional theory (DFT)

This ubiquitous adoption is largely due to the computational tractability afforded by DFT, which enables simulations of complex material systems while retaining a close connection to the underlying quantum-mechanical description of electronic interactions. Widely used plane-wave implementations such as VASP,

Despite this success, many scientifically important problems now demand simulations at length scales that significantly exceed those accessible with conventional approaches. Computing spin textures such as magnetic skyrmions, modeling catalytic reactions on substrate-supported metal nanoparticles, studying defect formation and migration in crystalline solids, and describing electrode–electrolyte interfaces in solid-state batteries all require large simulation cells to eliminate finite-size artifacts, capture charge transfer and strain effects, avoid spurious defect–defect interactions, and represent chemically complex environments spanning several nanometers and involving thousands of atoms. In all these settings, an accurate electronic-structure description is essential, calling for simulations of
10000

100000
electrons at chemical accuracy. Such large-scale calculations are equally indispensable for validating machine-learned interatomic potentials (MLIPs) at length scales beyond those sampled during training.

Although the asymptotic cubic scaling of Kohn–Sham DFT is unavoidable for complex heterogeneous material systems, the practical barriers to routine large-scale calculations are of a different character: high time-to-solution, large memory footprints arising from the need for many basis functions per atom to achieve desired accuracy, poor parallel scalability on modern multi-node architectures, and eigensolver algorithms ill-suited to exploit the latest GPU hardware. The Projector Augmented-Wave (PAW) method introduced by Blöchl
7
addresses a few of these limitations by reformulating the all-electron problem, characterized by rapidly oscillatory valence wavefunctions near atomic cores, into an equivalent problem involving smooth pseudo-wavefunctions augmented by localized atom-centered corrections. Within the frozen-core approximation, this transformation allows valence states to be represented with significantly fewer basis functions while preserving all-electron accuracy, thereby substantially reducing both the dimensionality of the discretized Kohn–Sham problem and its associated memory footprint. Plane-wave implementations such as VASP
33
, Quantum ESPRESSO
18
, and ABINIT
20
have widely adopted the PAW method
7
;
34
, making them the predominant tools in the materials science community. However, plane-wave implementations inherently enforce periodic boundary conditions and rely on global fast Fourier transforms (FFTs), which incur substantial all-to-all communication. On modern GPU-accelerated supercomputers, where floating-point throughput has grown dramatically relative to memory bandwidth and inter-node communication capacity, FFT-based implementations are increasingly bandwidth-bound and communication-limited, posing a fundamental obstacle to scalable large-scale DFT on exascale systems.

Real-space discretizations provide an alternative route that is better suited to leverage multi-node computing architectures. In addition to improved parallel scalability, real-space methods allow the use of generic boundary conditions, which provide a more realistic computational setup for modelling slabs, surfaces, and nanoclusters
52
. Real-space methods include finite difference approaches such as Octopus

However, with the exception of GPAW, most existing real-space implementations rely on the norm-conserving pseudopotential (ONCV) approximation to solve the DFT problem. Although systematically transferable, ONCV pseudopotentials are hard, particularly for first-row and transition-metal elements, and therefore require a substantially larger number of basis functions to achieve chemical accuracy. This leads to a substantial increase in computational cost and memory footprint. In contrast, the projector augmented-wave (PAW) method reconstructs the true all-electron wavefunctions from smooth pseudo-wavefunctions through an explicit linear transformation. This formulation enables accurate recovery of the all-electron charge density and wavefunctions in the vicinity of atomic nuclei, allowing reliable evaluation of quantities sensitive to core regions such as forces, hyperfine interactions, and electrostatic potentials, while retaining the efficiency of a smooth basis representation. Furthermore, by relaxing the strict norm-conservation constraint, PAW typically achieves faster basis set convergence and improved transferability compared to norm-conserving pseudopotentials.

Real-space finite-element (FE) discretization for the projector augmented-wave method (PAW-FE
51
) has only recently been explored, where high-order spectral finite elements are employed to solve the PAW governing equations. This approach demonstrates a natural synergy between the smooth fields arising from the PAW formalism and the adaptive resolution and systematic improvability of finite-element discretizations. In PAW-FE, higher-order finite-element basis functions with compact support are used to discretize the governing equations, enabling systematic polynomial convergence while naturally accommodating periodic, semi-periodic, and non-periodic boundary conditions. The locality of these FE basis functions induces sparse operator structure requiring only nearest-neighbour communication
51
;
12
to evaluate its action, thereby enabling fine-grained parallelism well suited for large-scale simulations on exascale architectures. The FE-discretized PAW governing equations derived in our previous work

To efficiently solve this problem, this previous work extended the traditional Chebyshev filtered subspace iteration (ChFSI) eigensolver, originally employed for standard Hermitian eigenvalue problems, to GHEP. The resulting PAW-FE methodology implemented on distributed multinode CPU architectures demonstrated excellent agreement with plane-wave PAW implementations across representative benchmark systems with non-periodic, semi-periodic, and fully periodic boundary conditions, including comparisons of ground-state energies, energy variation against bond-length and lattice parameters, formation energies, surface energies, and band structures. In addition to accuracy, the proposed computational strategies enabled substantial performance gains relative to plane-wave implementations on multinode CPU architectures. Motivated by the promising results of
PAW-FE
on multi-node CPU architectures, this work develops new eigensolver algorithms and numerical implementation strategies for the PAW generalized Hermitian eigenproblem (GHEP) that are well-suited to modern multi-node GPU architectures. Contemporary GPU hardware, which is largely optimized for machine-learning workloads, delivers extremely high throughput through low-precision tensor cores (FP16/BF16/TF32), yet continues to face bottlenecks associated with memory bandwidth and data movement. Achieving optimal performance on such hardware, therefore, requires minimizing data movement, maximizing arithmetic intensity, overlapping communication with computation, and leveraging reduced-precision arithmetic without sacrificing numerical robustness. Finite-element (FE) discretizations are particularly well-positioned to meet these demands, as the cell-structured representation of both the Hamiltonian and the inverse PAW overlap operator enables their action to be reformulated as dense cell-level matrix–matrix operations, naturally reducing global synchronization and facilitating computation–communication overlap. Central to our approach is an extension of the recently proposed Residual-based Chebyshev Filtered Subspace Iteration (R-ChFSI) algorithm
30
to solve the PAW-GHEP efficiently on multi-node GPU architectures. Because R-ChFSI is tolerant to inexact matrix–vector products during Chebyshev filter subspace construction, it admits the use of reduced-precision arithmetic (FP32/TF32) and low-precision nearest-neighbour communication (BF16) in the Chebyshev filtering step that is used to construct the subspace rich in desired eigensubspace. Building on these foundations, the key contributions of this work are: (i) formulation of FE-discretized governing equations for the PAW method within a collinear spin formalism; (ii) a multi-resolution quadrature scheme for accurate evaluation of atom-centered integrals arising in the FE discretization of PAW matrices on coarse grids; (iii) an eigensolver strategy based on R-ChFSI that solves the nonlinear FE-discretized PAW generalized eigenproblem while avoiding expensive inversions of the PAW overlap operator; (iv) mixed-precision compute (FP32/TF32) and low-precision MPI communication (BF16) in the Chebyshev filtering step, leveraging R-ChFSI’s residual-based correction to recover double-precision accuracy while reducing time to solution; and (v) a computation–communication overlap strategy within the Chebyshev filtered blocked subspace construction for the PAW-GHEP. Collectively, these contributions enable
PAW-FE
to fully exploit modern exascale computing architectures to advance scalable, efficient electronic-structure calculations.

2  Mathematical formulation

We begin by presenting the governing equations of spin-polarised density functional theory (DFT) within the projector augmented-wave (PAW) formalism in a real-space setting. The formulation is first presented for an infinite periodic crystal, where the Kohn–Sham equations are solved within a periodic unit cell by invoking Bloch’s theorem. The corresponding formulations for fully non-periodic and semi-periodic systems are then obtained as special cases of the periodic framework through appropriate modifications to the boundary conditions and Brillouin-zone sampling.

3  Computational Methodology

This section presents the finite-element based computational methodology employed to solve the Kohn-Sham equations given by Eqn. (
17
) that arises in the PAW formalism. It begins with an overview of the finite-element discretisation, followed by a brief description of the self-consistent field (SCF) algorithm used to solve the resulting FE-discretised nonlinear generalised eigenproblem. Finally, a residual-based reformulation of the Chebyshev-filtered subspace iteration is introduced to address the central computational challenge of efficiently solving the underlying large, sparse PAW generalized eigenproblem at each SCF iteration.

3.1 Finite-element discretization

The finite-element method involves decomposing the spatial domain into non-overlapping subdomains called finite elements (or cells). The underlying finite-element basis is constructed over Gauss-Legendre-Lobatto nodal points as is usually done in spectral finite element methods
45
;
51
. FE basis functions chosen in this work are
\mathcal{C}^{0}
-continuous Lagrange polynomials that are compactly supported in cells sharing a nodal point. Discretizing the electronic fields involved in Eqn. (
17
) , we have

\displaystyle\widetilde{u}^{\sigma,h}_{i,\boldsymbol{\textbf{k}}}(\boldsymbol{\textbf{x}})=\sum_{J=1}^{M}{N^{h,p}_{J}(\boldsymbol{\textbf{x}})\widetilde{u}^{\sigma,J}_{i,\boldsymbol{\textbf{k}}}},\;V_{\text{el}}^{h,p_{\text{el}}}(\boldsymbol{\textbf{x}})=\sum_{J=1}^{M_{\text{el}}}{N^{h,p_{\text{el}}}_{J}(\boldsymbol{\textbf{x}})V_{\text{el}}^{J}}

where
\widetilde{u}^{\sigma,J}_{i,\boldsymbol{\textbf{k}}}

denote the nodal vectors representing the FE discretised wavefunctions and the electrostatic potential respectively, while

p
and
p_{\text{el}}

3.3 Residual-based Chebyshev filtered subspace iteration for PAW eigenproblem

As commonly employed in most real-space codes
17
;
36
;
12
, the Chebyshev filtered subspace iteration
63
;
54
is the method of choice to solve the nonlinear eigenvalue problem encountered in DFT. However, the method was initially employed to solve standard eigenvalue problem and subsequently was extended to solve the PAW generalised eigenvalue problem
42
;
51
. In this method, we first construct a Chebyshev-filtered (CF) subspace by applying a polynomial filter of
\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}
to an initial trial subspace, followed by Rayleigh-Ritz projection (RR-Projection) and subspace diagonalisation with subspace rotation (RR-GHEP and RR-SR). Specifically, the filtered subspace is obtained as
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{\text{f}}=T_{m}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{\text{in}}
where
T_{m}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}})
denotes the
m^{th}
degree Chebyshev polynomial of the matrix
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}}
and the columns of the
M\times N
matrix
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{\text{in}}
represent approximations to the desired eigenvectors of
{\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}
that has the same eigenspace of
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}}=\frac{2}{b_{U}-b_{W}}\left({\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}\right)-\frac{b_{U}+b_{W}}{b_{U}-b_{W}}\boldsymbol{I}
. The parameters
b_{U}
and
b_{W}
are chosen such that target eigenvalues of
\mathbf{S}^{-1}\mathbf{H}
are mapped to values strictly less than -1, while the unwanted eigenvalues are mapped into the interval
[-1,1]
. This spectral transformation enables the Chebyshev polynomial to selectively amplify components associated with the desired eigenspace while suppressing the rest. To ensure that the amplification of the desired eigenspace does not lead to floating-point overflow, we instead build the Chebyshev filtered space as
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{\text{f}}=\mathcal{C}_{m}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}},\widetilde{a_{0}}):={T_{m}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}})}/{T_{m}(\widetilde{a}_{0})}
, where

a_{0}
is an estimate of the lowest eigenvalue of
\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}
and
\widetilde{a}_{0}
is scaled and shifted
a_{0}
using the same affine transformation that maps
\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}
to
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}}
.
Consequently, the Chebyshev filtered space is constructed using the three-term recurrence relation of Chebyshev polynomials as given below:

\displaystyle\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{\text{f}}=\mathcal{C}_{m}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}},\widetilde{a}_{0})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{\text{in}}\;\text{where}\;\mathcal{C}_{m}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}},\widetilde{a}_{0})=T_{m}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\textbf{x}}})/T_{m}(\widetilde{a}_{0})

\displaystyle\text{and}\;T_{\theta+1}(\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}})=2\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}T_{\theta}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}})-T_{\theta-1}(\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}),\;\;\text{for}\;2\leq\theta\leq m

\displaystyle\text{with}\;\;T_{0}(\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}})=0,T_{1}(\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}})=\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}

(33)

Using Eqn. (
33
), the Chebyshev filtered space is constructed using the following recursive relation involving matrix-matrix products

\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}_{\theta+1}=\frac{2\zeta_{\theta+1}}{e}\left({\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}\right)\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}_{\theta}-\frac{2\zeta_{\theta+1}c}{e}\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}_{\theta}-\zeta_{\theta}\zeta_{\theta+1}\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}_{\theta-1},\;\;\text{for}\;2\leq\theta\leq m

(34)

where
\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}_{0}=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{\text{in}}
,
\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}_{1}=\frac{\zeta_{1}}{e}(\left({\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}\right)-c\boldsymbol{\textbf{I}})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{\text{in}}
and
\zeta_{\theta+1}=\frac{1}{\left(\zeta_{\theta}-\frac{1}{\zeta_{1}}\right)}
with
\zeta_{1}=\frac{e}{\left(a_{0}-c\right)}
,
e=\frac{\left(b_{U}-b_{W}\right)}{2}
and
c=\frac{\left(b_{W}+b_{U}\right)}{2}
.
The rapid growth property of Chebyshev polynomials outside
[-1,1]
amplifies the components in
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{\text{in}}
along the direction of the desired eigenvectors (occupied states) while dampening the components along the unwanted eigenvectors (unoccupied states), leading to the filtered subspace
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{\text{f}}
being a close approximation of the eigenspace of interest of the PAW eigenproblem
\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}=\varepsilon\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{u}}
in a given SCF iteration. The values of
b_{U}
,
a_{0}
correspond to the upper bound of the unwanted spectrum and the lower bound of the wanted spectrum, which are both estimated from a few iterations of the Generalised Lanczos iteration method as discussed in our previous work
51
. Furthermore, the value of
b_{W}
corresponding to the upper bound of the wanted spectrum is estimated from the eigenvalues of the previous SCF iteration or from
a_{0}
and
b_{U}
.

3.3.1 Proposed filtering approach leveraging approximate inverse of PAW overlap matrix:

In this work, instead of using the recurrence relation Eqn. (
34
) to construct the filtered space
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{\text{f}}
, we formulate this three-term recurrence relation in terms of the eigenproblem residuals [
\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{\text{in}}-\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{\text{in}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\text{in}}
] in the spirit of our recent work R-ChFSI
30
. Here, we note that the
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\text{in}}
is the diagonal matrix whose entries are an estimate for the eigenvalues of the wanted eigenspace. In contrast to the traditional Chebyshev filtered subspace iteration approach, we show that the residual-based reformulation is significantly more robust to approximations in matrix–multivector products during the construction of the filtered subspace for the PAW-GHEP. As demonstrated later, this robustness enables a more efficient evaluation of the action of
\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}^{-1}
on GPUs by permitting the use of inexpensive approximate inverses. Furthermore, it enables the efficient construction of the filtered subspace using low-precision compute and communication as discussed in the subsequent section.

To derive the recurrence relation in terms of residuals, we first introduce

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\textbf{Z}}_{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}\mathcal{C}_{\theta}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}},\widetilde{a}_{0})\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{\text{in}}=\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}_{\theta},\;\;\boldsymbol{\textbf{L}}_{\theta}=\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}_{\text{in}}\mathcal{C}_{\theta}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\text{in}},\widetilde{a}_{0})

(35)

where
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\text{in}}
is the affine transformation of
\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\text{in}}
using the same affine map that transforms
\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}^{-1}\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}
to
\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{\theta}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\text{in}},\widetilde{a}_{0})=T_{\theta}(\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Lambda}}_{\text{in}})/T_{\theta}(\widetilde{a}_{0})
which can be expressed using the recurrence relation mentioned in Eqn. (
33
). To this end, the recurrence relation for
\boldsymbol{\textbf{Z}}_{\theta},\boldsymbol{\textbf{L}}_{\theta}
similar to Eqn. (
34
) can be expressed as

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\textbf{Z}}_{\theta+1}=\frac{2\zeta_{\theta+1}}{e}\left(\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}{\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}}^{-1}\right)\boldsymbol{\textbf{Z}}_{\theta}-\frac{2\zeta_{\theta+1}c}{e}\boldsymbol{\textbf{Z}}_{\theta}-\zeta_{\theta}\zeta_{\theta+1}\boldsymbol{\textbf{Z}}_{\theta-1}

(36)

\displaystyle\boldsymbol{\textbf{L}}_{\theta+1}=\frac{2\zeta_{\theta+1}}{e}\boldsymbol{\textbf{L}}_{\theta}\left(\boldsymbol{\Lambda}_{\text{in}}\right)-\frac{2\zeta_{\theta+1}c}{e}\boldsymbol{\textbf{L}}_{\theta}-\zeta_{\theta}\zeta_{\theta+1}\boldsymbol{\textbf{L}}_{\theta-1}

with

4  Numerical Implementation

In this section, we propose various algorithmic strategies and implementation innovations to determine the ground-state DFT solution efficiently using the computational methods discussed in the previous section on modern GPU architectures. To this end, this section highlights the synergy between the projector-augmented-wave (PAW) formalism and the inherent advantages of the finite element method that leverage extreme levels of parallelism offered by evolving computing architectures. As discussed previously, the PAW generalised eigenproblem (PAW-GHEP)
\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}=\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}\widetilde{\boldsymbol{\Psi}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
is solved in each self-consistent field (SCF) iteration using the Chebyshev filtered subspace iteration approach (ChFSI). In particular, we resort to the residual-based reformulation of the Chebyshev filtered subspace iteration approach (R-ChFSI)

, tolerant to approximations in matrix-multivector multiplications as discussed previously. Recalling from section
3
, the FE discretised operators

and
S
are
M\times M
sparse matrices and

denotes the

multi-vector comprising FE-discretized pseudo-wavefunctions
where ’

’ denotes the total degrees-of-freedom(DoFs) to be solved and ’

Furthermore, as illustrated in Algo. (

\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}\widehat{\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}}^{-1}
on multivectors. Following this, we perform the Rayleigh-Ritz step, which scales cubically with system size and involves: (i) projecting the FE-discretised PAW GHEP onto space spanned by the columns of filtered space (

) (ii) solving the smaller projected generalized eigenvalue problem (iii) perform a subspace-rotation step to obtain the

At the end of each R-ChFSI iteration, the eigenproblem residual matrix

4.5 Computational Strategies for Rayleigh Ritz Procedure

The Rayleigh-Ritz (
RR
) procedure (steps 4-6 in algorithm (
2
)) starts becoming the computational bottleneck for large-scale systems comprising more than 10,000 electrons. From our benchmark calculations, we observe the decreasing cost of the various steps involved in
RR
as: (i)
RR-GHEP
, (ii)
RR-SR
, (iii)
RR-Projection
.

RR-Projection
:

We outline the computational strategy employed to construct the projected Hamiltonian (
\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}^{p}
) and projected PAW overlap (
\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}^{p}
) matrices, both of which are computed using the operation
\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\textbf{Y}}
, where
\boldsymbol{\textbf{Y}}=\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}
or
\boldsymbol{\textbf{Y}}=\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}
. To this end,
X
is partitioned across MPI tasks
t
according to the finite-element domain decomposition, yielding local blocks
\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}_{t}
. Similar in spirit of our previous works
51
;
12
, the processor level
\boldsymbol{\textbf{A}}^{p}_{t}=\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}_{t}^{\dagger}\boldsymbol{\textbf{Y}}_{t}
is computed locally in each processor after which the global matrix
\boldsymbol{\textbf{A}}^{p}
is assembled by summing these contributions using an
MPI_AllReduce
collective. Furthermore, to reduce the peak memory usage, only the lower triangle of
\boldsymbol{\textbf{A}}^{p}_{t}
is evaluated, and the computation is performed in blocks of size
N\times B_{v}
, where
B_{v}
refers to the choice of wavefunction blocksize. Furthermore, the local computation of
\boldsymbol{\textbf{A}}^{p}_{t}
is performed on the
compute
-stream while the communication of
\boldsymbol{\textbf{A}}^{p}_{t}
to build
\boldsymbol{\textbf{A}}^{p}
is done on the
communication
-stream, thereby achieving compute–communication overlap.

RR-GHEP
:

Towards scalable and efficient evaluation of the eigenvalue prblem
\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}^{p}\boldsymbol{\textbf{Q}}=\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}^{p}\boldsymbol{\textbf{Q}}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}
, the projected matrices
\boldsymbol{\textbf{H}}^{p},\boldsymbol{\textbf{S}}^{p}
are stored in a 2D-block-cyclic format. We employ the state-of-the-art
ELPA
library
43
;
61
to solve the projected GHEP on GPUs or CPUs, with
openMP
parallelisation based on system size. The use of GPU parallel
GEMM
in
ELPA
enables efficient scaling on multi-node GPUs. After computing the eigenpairs, we perform the subspace rotation step to recover the eigenvectors in the full finite-element space.

RR-SR
:

A mixed precision strategy, as shown in figure (
6
), is employed in
PAW-FE
without compromising on SCF convergence.
RR-SR
involves the computation of
\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}_{t}\boldsymbol{\textbf{Q}}
, where the entries
Q
are distributed over various MPI-tasks. The key steps in
RR-SR
involve the extraction and copying of the entries of
Q
and the subsequent
GEMM
operation to get the rotated space. As shown in figure (
6
), the two steps are overlapped over blocks of
Q
, with the extraction and copying occurring on a
communication
-stream (shown in orange) and the
GEMM
operation in the
compute
-stream. Additionally, these operations are performed in reduced precision (
FP32
) for the off-diagonal entries, reducing the overall cost. This is justified by the fact that
Q
approaches the identity matrix as the SCF procedure converges, so the off-diagonal entries correspond to small mixing corrections whose evaluation in reduced precision has minimal impact on the resulting eigenspace. Furthermore, the
GEMM
operation is done in batches of DoFs to reduce the peak memory requirement.

Twisted bilayer structures have emerged as a class of quantum materials of significant contemporary interest due to the rich variety of emergent electronic phenomena arising from moiré superlattices. 6  Perspectives and concluding remarks

In this work, we have presented the key computational methodologies underlying
PAW-FE
, a finite-element discretized projector augmented-wave DFT formulation designed for accurate, efficient, and scalable electronic-structure calculations on modern multi-node GPU architectures. The proposed approach
is built around a hardware-aware eigensolver strategy that leverages approximate inverse of PAW overlap matrix, mixed-precision arithmetic, low-precision communication, and computation–communication overlap. Relative to established plane-wave PAW based DFT codes,
PAW-FE
substantially reduces time-to-solution across a wide range of system sizes while naturally accommodating generic boundary conditions, and demonstrates scalability to the
10^{4}
–
10^{5}
electron regime otherwise inaccessible to existing systematically convergent DFT implementations.

A central design principle of
PAW-FE
is the synergy between the PAW formalism and high-order finite-element discretization. In particular, the multi-resolution quadrature rules employed in the finite-element framework enable accurate evaluation of atom-centered PAW integrals without increasing the number of degrees of freedom, preserving the efficiency of the discretized representation. Further, the locality of the FE basis induces a sparse operator structure requiring only nearest-neighbor communication, a property that maps favorably onto the distributed memory hierarchies of exascale architectures and naturally accommodates periodic, semi-periodic, and non-periodic boundary conditions within a single unified formulation. The latter capability enables direct, artifact-free modeling of slabs, surfaces, nanoclusters, and interfaces that would otherwise require artificial periodicity or large vacuum padding in widely used plane-wave approaches.

To achieve high computational efficiency on modern architectures, the operations within each SCF iteration are reformulated as high-arithmetic-intensity matrix–matrix multiplications well-suited to GPU execution. The dominant computational step, the solution of the PAW generalized Hermitian eigenproblem (PAW-GHEP), is addressed through the Residual-based Chebyshev Filtered Subspace Iteration (R-ChFSI) algorithm. R-ChFSI, being tolerant to approximations in matrix–multivector products arising in the construction of desired eigensubspace, admits several performance-oriented strategies without compromising the accuracy of the converged solution. Specifically, a computationally inexpensive FE-discretized PAW overlap matrix inverse is introduced; reduced-precision arithmetic (FP32/TF32) is employed during Chebyshev-filtered subspace construction; nearest-neighbour data transfers are performed in BF16 to reduce communication volume by a factor of 4
\times
during filtering; and a block-wise overlap strategy is developed for orchestrating computation and communication throughout. Together, these strategies yield a 3
\times
reduction in wall time for Chebyshev-filtered subspace construction and an overall CPU–GPU speedup of 8
\times
–
20
\times
across Intel and AMD GPU architectures.
PAW-FE
achieves a 8
\times
reduction in minimum wall time to solution relative to plane-wave PAW codes for systems of approximately 10,000 electrons, with greater gains expected for larger system sizes and a speedup of up to 6
\times
relative to the ONCV-based
DFT-FE
implementation. Time to solution per SCF iteration of approximately 2 minutes for systems comprising 130,000 electrons further demonstrates the practical scalability of
PAW-FE
to realistic large-scale simulations.

Looking ahead, the computational framework developed here opens several avenues for further development. A natural next step is the incorporation of kinetic-energy-dependent meta-GGA functionals such as
r2SCAN

15
and
LAK

38
, representing the next rung on Jacob’s ladder of exchange-correlation approximations. Incorporating solvent effects within the present framework is also of immediate interest for catalytic and electrochemical applications involving nanometer-scale systems. Extending the current collinear spin formalism to non-collinear magnetism and relativistic effects will be important for accurate modelling of magnetic materials and heavy elements and will form part of future investigations. Finally, systematic guidelines for the selection of finite-element discretization parameters
p
,
p_{\mathrm{el}}
, and
h
, together with appropriate quadrature rules, particularly for the
JTHv2.0

27
PAW datasets, will be provided in forthcoming work to facilitate broader adoption of
PAW-FE
across diverse materials systems.

\boldsymbol{\textbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}=\boldsymbol{\textbf{A}}_{\text{loc}}\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}+\boldsymbol{\textbf{A}}_{\text{nloc}}\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}

(iv)

Evaluate the local operator action together with the non-local contribution:

\boldsymbol{\textbf{Y}}^{(e,t)}=\boldsymbol{\textbf{A}}^{(e)}_{\mathrm{loc}}\,\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}^{(e,t)}+\sum_{a}\boldsymbol{\textbf{C}}^{(a,e,t)}\,\boldsymbol{\Delta}^{a}_{\boldsymbol{\textbf{A}}}\,\boldsymbol{\textbf{U}}^{a}.

(v)

Assemble the global vector
\boldsymbol{\textbf{Y}}=\boldsymbol{\textbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}

\boldsymbol{\textbf{Y}}=\sum_{t=1}^{n_{t}}{\boldsymbol{\textbf{B}}^{(t)}}^{T}{\boldsymbol{\textbf{Z}}^{(t)}}^{T}\sum_{e=1}^{E_{t}}{\boldsymbol{\textbf{E}}^{(e,t)}}^{T}\,\boldsymbol{\textbf{Y}}^{(e,t)}.

In algorithm (
4
), we make use of extensive dense linear algebra operations such as
GEMM
which is used to compute matrix-matrix products of the form
\boldsymbol{\textbf{C}}=\alpha\boldsymbol{\textbf{A}}\boldsymbol{\textbf{X}}+\beta\boldsymbol{\textbf{C}}
which is efficiently implemented in mathematical libraries such as
Intel MKL
for Intel CPU and GPUs,
cuBLAS
for Nvidia GPUs and
hipBLAS
for AMD GPUs. Similarly, the operation
GEMMBatched
performs a large number of small, independent
GEMM
operations in a single launch, enabling high-throughput when matrices share identical shapes but are stored as separate pointers. While the operation
GEMMStridedBatched
kernel is even more efficient when matrices are stored in memory with a fixed stride between consecutive operands, allowing the GPU to process a batch of small
GEMM
operations without pointers to address the input and output matrices.

In addition to these vendor-optimized primitives, we employ several custom kernels that are specifically designed to exploit the data layouts and sparsity patterns arising from finite-element discretization. In particular, the
stridedBlockAxpy
kernel performs a block-wise
axpby
operation of the form
\boldsymbol{\textbf{y}}_{i}=\alpha\boldsymbol{\textbf{s}}_{i}\boldsymbol{\textbf{x}}_{i}+\beta\boldsymbol{\textbf{y}}_{i}
, where
\boldsymbol{\textbf{x}}_{i}
and
\boldsymbol{\textbf{y}}_{i}
are contiguous blocks and
\boldsymbol{\textbf{s}}_{i}
is the DoF-dependent scaling factor. The
stridedCopyToBlock
kernel performs a gather-style operation that extracts DoFs from a processor-level block vector into a compact, contiguous block for each cell. This operation is primarily employed during cell-level extraction, where DoF associated with selected finite-element cells are copied into local buffers for subsequent evaluations. The copy pattern depends on the
processDoFToCellDoF
map (refers to
\boldsymbol{\textbf{E}}^{(e,t)}
), which encodes the correspondence between cell-level DoFs and their indices in the processor-level block vector.
To assemble contributions computed at the cell-level back into a processor-level block vector, we use the
axpyStridedBlockAtomicAdd
kernel, which performs a block-wise scaled addition into strided locations of the destination vector using the
processDoFToCellDoF
map. On GPUs, this kernel employs atomic updates, ensuring thread-safe updates when multiple cells contribute to the same DoF. Finally, the
accumulateAddLocallyOwned
kernel consolidates contributions associated with DoF defined on shared nodes into the locally owned DoF. Here, shared nodes refer to finite-element nodes that lie on subdomain interfaces and are therefore replicated across neighbouring MPI tasks in the domain decomposition. This operation involves nearest-neighbour MPI communication, during which values corresponding to these shared nodes are exchanged and accumulated to ensure consistency of the global vector across subdomains. By restricting communication to neighbouring subdomains and operating only on shared-node data, this kernel minimizes communication volume and latency, which is critical for achieving good strong-scaling performance on large-scale CPU–GPU systems.
