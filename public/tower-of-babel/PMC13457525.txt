Real-World Glucometric Outcomes of the MiniMed 780G Advanced Hybrid Closed-Loop System in Users with Type 1 Diabetes in Spain
Authors: Marga Giménez, Virginia Bellido, Carmen Quirós, Ignacio Conget, Noelia Gros Herguido, Daria Roca, Ana Pérez-Morales, Tim van den Heuvel, Maria Joanna Juachon, Goran Petrovski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAutomated insulin delivery systems are effective for the management of type 1 diabetes (T1D). However, analyses of how such effectiveness translates into real-world practice in Spain are lacking. The aim of this study was therefore to examine real-world data generated by users of the MiniMed 780G system with T1D in Spain.MethodsThe CareLink Personal software program was used to collect data from 16,392 users of the MiniMed 780G system. Outcomes assessed included measures of glycemic control. Comparisons were made between age groups and between pre- and post-advanced hybrid closed-loop (AHCL) system initiation. Additionally, longitudinal data from 12 months of continuous usage were analyzed.ResultsOverall, the mean time in range (TIR) was 75.9%, the time in tight range (TITR) was 51.0%, the time below 70 mg/dL (TB70) was 2.2%, and most users achieved international glycemic control targets with 77.4% having a TIR > 70%. Results were similar across age groups but tended to be better among users who utilized recommended optimal settings, with a TIR of 79.8%, TITR of 55.4%, and TB70 of 2.0%. Substantial improvements were observed between pre- and post-initiation of AHCL (n = 4294), with mean TIR increasing from 64.7% to 75.8% and the proportions of users achieving a TIR of > 70% more than doubling. In a longitudinal analysis of a cohort with 12 months of data (n = 6664), improvements in glycemic control were stable.ConclusionsThis analysis provides evidence of sustained improvements in glycemic control among users of the MiniMed 780G system in Spain, demonstrating how the use of such a system can result in large increases in the time individuals with T1D spend in range.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s13300-026-01878-0.Keywords: Diabetes mellitus, Type 1 diabetes, Insulin infusion systems, Automated insulin delivery systems, Advanced hybrid closed-loop

Key Summary Points

Why carry out this study?Automated insulin delivery systems have been shown to be effective in numerous studies but data on their real-world effectiveness in Spain are lacking.This study sought to analyze real-world data generated by users of an automated insulin delivery system, the MiniMed 780G system, in Spain.What was learned from this study?Automated insulin delivery system users in this study had a mean time in range of 75.9% and most users achieved international glycemic control targets.The glycemic control achieved with automated insulin delivery was stable over a 12-month period.This demonstrates how the use of advanced hybrid closed-loop systems can result in increases in the time individuals with type 1 diabetes spend in range.

Introduction

In Spain, the age-standardized prevalence rate of type 1 diabetes (T1D) is 726 per 100,000, the seventh highest among the EU15+ countries (i.e., the 15 European Union [EU] pre-2004 member countries, plus Australia, Canada, Norway, and the USA), and age-standardized incidence and mortality rates are 21 per 100,000 and 0.20 per 100,000, respectively [1]. In recent decades these rates have risen, with incidence rising by 118.3% and prevalence by 110.9% between 1990 and 2019 [1], although more recent studies have found evidence of stabilization [2–5]. Such large numbers of individuals with diabetes are likely associated with a large economic burden. Although data on the overall costs of managing T1D in Spain are generally lacking, an observational study found average annual costs of 27,274 euro (EUR) per patient among pediatric and adolescent persons with direct medical costs accounting for EUR 4070 (2014 EUR) and indirect costs accounting for EUR 23,204 [6]. Insulin-related hypoglycemia alone has been estimated to result in nationwide annual costs of EUR 377,516,935 in a 2016 study [7]. Supporting people with T1D in achieving glycemic control while avoiding hypoglycemia is thus critical to minimize risks of diabetes-related complications [8–12], in addition to reducing the economic and social burdens of hypoglycemia [7, 13–15].

A key innovation in helping people with diabetes achieve glycemic control targets has been combining continuous glucose monitoring with insulin pumps in closed-loop systems. Such systems result in improvements in a variety of glycemic control measures including glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and time in range (TIR) in addition to quality of life [16–22]. The use of the MiniMed 780G system in particular has demonstrated effective management of people with T1D in Spain in numerous prospective and retrospective studies [17, 23–31]. The effectiveness of this system in real-world populations has also been demonstrated through real-world evidence (RWE) generated by several studies in a variety of countries [32–38]. These include a study of over 100,000 users from Europe, the Middle East, and Africa that found that the majority of users achieve international glycemic control targets [32]. Such RWE is consistent with the results of randomized controlled trials, complementing the data provided by clinical studies [32], as has been seen for other interventions for diabetes [39]. However, such large-scale RWE is lacking for Spain, with the country having been excluded from previous analyses as a result of legal procedures, leaving open the question of how the extensive clinical data supporting the use of the MiniMed 780G system in Spain translates to real-world use.

The objectives of this study were therefore to provide an analysis of RWE generated by MiniMed 780G system users with T1D in Spain, with a particular focus on the impact of the use of this system on glycemic control. This included assessments of the time spent in various ranges, proportions of users achieving international glycemic control targets, and the stability of glycemic control over a 12-month period. These variables were further analyzed for subgroups of users divided based on age group and/or whether recommended optimal settings (ROS) were used.

Methods

Design

Data regarding glycemic control and advanced hybrid closed-loop (AHCL) system usage was collected from users of the MiniMed 780G system with T1D in Spain between 29 January 2024 and 29 January 2025. The data were collected through uploads to the CareLink Personal software program. In addition to analyzing outcomes for the overall cohort, further analyses were carried out to compare those using ROS (defined as glucose target of 100 mg/dL for ≥ 95% of the time and active insulin time of 2 h for ≥ 95% of the time [33, 36]) to the overall cohort, to compare different age groups (≤ 15 years, 16–55 years, and ≥ 56 years), to examine the stability of glycemic control over a 12-month period, and to compare glycemic control pre- and post-initiation of AHCL. Please note that the methodology described here has been used in related RWE studies conducted in other countries [32–38, 40], and will also be used in additional country-specific studies published in this series.

Data Source

The CareLink™ Personal software platform collects data from the users of MiniMed™ systems through either automatic nightly or manual uploads from the devices in addition to self-reporting of some parameters such as age group and gender. The platform is used by MiniMed users and healthcare professionals to track and report on glycemic control, as has been further described and validated elsewhere [41]. Data are collected from all users who have created a CareLink™ Personal account. However, only data from users who have provided consent were used, which is currently > 91% of the overall population (as of 2024). Ninety-nine percent of uploads are done automatically. The MiniMed system that is the focus of this analysis, the MiniMed 780G, has 3 months of data storage so only upload gaps of > 3 months will result in missing data.

Eligibility

Users residing in Spain who provided consent for their data to be aggregated were included in the analysis. Additionally, users needed ≥ 10 days of sensor glucose (SG) data in each evaluated period. For the overall cohort, the evaluated period was the post-AHCL initiation period. For those in the pre-post cohort with data from before and after AHCL initiation, the evaluated periods were the pre- and post-AHCL initiation periods. For those in the longitudinal cohort, ≥ 10 days of SG data were required for each month; this requirement was based on a previous publication validating a similar duration of continuous glucose monitoring metrics to estimate long-term glycemic control [42], in addition to the use of such a duration in numerous studies of closed-loop systems [32–35, 38, 43, 44]. All data were included regardless of whether the system was in AHCL or open-loop control.

Outcomes

A number of parameters relating to glycemic control were assessed in this analysis. These included TIR (70–180 mg/dL, 3.9–10.0 mmol/L), time in tight range (TITR, 70–140 mg/dL, 3.9–7.8 mmol/L), time below 70 mg/dL (TB70), time below 54 mg/dL (TB54), time above 180 mg/dL (TA180), and time above 250 mg/dL (TA250). International targets relating to these parameters were also assessed, including the proportions of users having TIR > 70%, TITR > 50%, TB70 < 4%, TB54 < 1%, TA180 < 25%, and TA250 < 5% [42, 45]. Other parameters reported in this publication include amounts of insulin delivered, glucose management indicator (GMI), and SG, coefficient of variation (CV), and composite targets. For reasons of General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) compliance, outcomes were only reported for subgroups consisting of > 100 users.

Ethics Compliance

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1964) and its subsequent amendments. Ethics committee approval was not required for this study as this study was a real-world analysis, and therefore deemed to not qualify as “human subjects research”. All CareLink™ Personal account holders are proactively asked to provide consent for the use of their de-identified data by Medtronic for research purposes. Users who did not provide consent were excluded from the analyses, and all data were analyzed only after de-identification.

In the overall cohort, the mean (SD) TIR among users was 75.9% (8.8%), with a mean TITR of 51.0% (9.5%) (Fig. 1). When comparing age ranges, the mean (SD) TIRs were 74.2% (8.5%) for those ≤ 15 years (n = 4039), 76.1% (8.8%) for those 16–55 years (n = 10,691), and 78.6% (8.4%) for those ≥ 56 years (n = 1539). For TITRs, the corresponding values were 50.5% (8.6%), 50.9% (9.7%), and 52.6% (10.0%). For users with ROS (glucose target of 100 mg/dL for ≥ 95% of the time and active insulin time of 2 h for ≥ 95% of the time [33, 36]), the mean (SD) TIR was 79.8% (7.3%) and the mean (SD) TITR was 55.4% (8.7%). These results were also broadly similar across age groups, with mean (SD) TIRs for users with ROS of 79.7% (7.0%) for those ≤ 15 years, 79.7% (7.4%) for those 16–55 years, and 81.3% (7.4%) for those ≥ 56 years.

Cohort 2: Changes in Glycemic Control Between Pre- and Post-AHCL Initiation

To assess the impact of AHCL, data from after its initiation were compared with data from ≥ 10 days prior to its initiation. In the overall cohort (n = 4294), mean TIR increased from 64.7% (13.5%) to 75.8% (9.0%) while TITR increased from 40.2% (12.6%) to 51.1% (9.7%) (Supplementary Fig. S1, see Supplementary Materials). This was accompanied by corresponding decreases in TA180, TA250, TB70, and TB54 (Supplementary Fig. S1). Similar results were observed across all age groups (Supplementary Fig. S1). Mean (SD) SG dropped from 159 (21.5) mg/dL to 147 (13.9) mg/dL, while GMI decreased from 7.12% (0.51%) to 6.83% (0.33%) (Table 2). Increases in the proportions of users reaching international glycemic targets were observed for all targets examined and across all age groups (Supplementary Fig. S2). The proportions of users achieving a TIR of > 70% or TITR of > 50% more than doubled, increasing from 36.0% to 76.3% and 20.5% to 54.5%, respectively. Similarly, the proportions of users achieving the combined target of a GMI < 7%, TIR > 70%, TB70 < 4%, and TB54 < 1% increased from 22.4% to 64.0% in the overall cohort, from 20.8% to 59.5% in those ≤ 15 years, from 23.0% to 65.1% in those aged 16–55 years, and from 26.0% to 77.7% in those ≥ 56 years.

Data from a 12-month period was examined to assess the stability of the glycemic control achieved by users (n = 6664), as summarized in Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. S3. Measures of glycemic control were found to be largely stable, with the TIR ranging from 77.4% to 78.3% and the TITR ranging from 52.7% to 53.9%. The TB70 was also stable over this time, not exceeding 2.43%. The mean SG increased slightly from 142 mg/dL to 144 mg/dL while GMI increased from 6.71% to 6.76% over the entire period The percentage of users reaching the combined target of a GMI < 7%, TIR > 70%, TB70 < 4%, and TB54 < 1% ranged from 62.9% to 65.3%.

Discussion

This analysis provides RWE showing that the MiniMed 780G system results in sustained improvements in glycemic control across users of different age ranges based in Spain. Larger benefits were observed in those who used ROS, as reported previously [32]. When comparing glycemic control before and after the initiation of AHCL, large improvements were found highlighting the impact of the system on diabetes management. Finally, the glycemic control achieved with the MiniMed 780G was found to be stable for at least 12 months, demonstrating the robustness of these outcomes.

The results described here are consistent with a relatively large body of clinical evidence generated in previous prospective and retrospective studies conducted in Spain that examined glycemic outcomes in users of AHCL systems and, in particular, the MiniMed 780G system [17, 23–31]. A Spanish multicenter prospective study of 150 people with T1D, of whom half initiated AHCL with MiniMed 780G systems, found that TIR increased from 62% to 77% over the first 3 months of use while the proportion of users with a TIR > 70% increased from 27% to 87% and HbA1c decreased from 7.43% to 6.88% over the same period [23]. Improvements in quality of life and sleep quality were also observed after 3 months of AHCL use [23]. Similarly, a prospective study of 135 users of the MiniMed 780G system in Spain found that improvements in TIR persisted for at least a year after the initiation of AHCL, improving from 67% to 77%, with broadly similar improvements observed regardless of age, previous treatment, or hypoglycemia risk [46]. A further previous prospective study of the MiniMed 780G system in the treatment of 117 adult and pediatric users with T1D in Spain found the initiation of AHCL resulted in improvements in HbA1c (6.9% to 6.6%), TIR (68.2% to 82.5%), TITR (43.7% to 57.3%), GMI (6.9% to 6.6%), time below range (4.3% to 2.0%), and time above range (36% to 15%) [27].

Comparable results were found among pediatric users. A retrospective analysis of 61 children (aged < 7 years) in Spain reported that use of MiniMed 780G was associated with improvements in TIR [29], while another study of 25 children with T1D found significant improvements in TIR and parental health-related quality of life [30]. Among older children and adolescents (aged 6–17 years), the use of MiniMed 780G increased TIR from 59% to 74% within the first 48 h of use with sustained benefits for at least 6 months [31].

Analyses of RWE generated from other countries and regions has also produced results that largely resemble those presented here [32–38]. Similar findings in terms of improvements in terms of TIR specifically have also been seen across several clinical trials [22, 25, 28, 47, 48]. In particular, the largest RWE study, a study of data collected from > 100,000 MiniMed 780G users over 3 years from 34 countries across Europe, the Middle East, and Africa, found that people using the system consistently achieve glycemic targets [32]. In that cohort, the mean TIR was 72.3%, the GMI was 7.0%, and the TB70 was 2.0%. When assessing the achievement of glycemic control targets by these users, 62.5% had a TIR > 70%, 88.4% had a TB70 < 4%, and 90.0% had a TB54 < 1%. Similarly to the current analysis, these results were stable over a 12-month period and consistent among age groups but were typically better among those that used ROS. In both these studies, only small proportions of users were found to be utilizing ROS. This could perhaps reflect barriers to the use of ROS such as a lack of clinician or patient education, clinical inertia, patient fears of hypoglycemia, lack of awareness, or possible worsening of retinopathy following a rapid improvement in blood glucose levels [49, 50]. However, it should be noted that the criteria used for identifying users of ROS were similarly stringent in both studies and that reasons for not using ROS were not investigated.

Such improvements in glycemic control are likely to result in economic benefits. Previous modelling from a Spanish healthcare system perspective using the IQVIA Core Diabetes Model found that hybrid closed-loop systems are likely to result in delays and reductions in complications, increased survival, improved quality of life, and reductions in costs compared with intermittent scanning continuous glucose monitoring and subcutaneous insulin infusion [51]. Over a time horizon of 50 years, survival improved, 1.15 quality-adjusted life years were gained, and cost savings of EUR 28,705 per person (2024 EUR) were found. These cost savings were primarily driven by reductions in ulcer, amputation, and nephrology treatment [51].

However, while this study demonstrates the effectiveness of the MiniMed 780G system, further research may be of benefit in further demonstrating the real-world outcomes of its use. A key limitation of this study was that the CareLink Personal program does not collect data on adverse events, including those that are of specific relevance to glycemic control such as severe hypoglycemia and diabetic ketoacidosis. In addition, as a result of the collection of data exclusively from AHCL systems, data on HbA1c were not available with GMI used as an alternative measure, potentially limiting some comparisons with other studies of glycemic control because of the potential physiological discordance between GMI and laboratory measures. A further limitation resulting from the study methodology was that variables other than those relating to glycemic control, such as age range and gender, were self-reported, with age being grouped to ensure privacy. Indeed, the use of AHCL systems in older adults in particular has been highlighted as an important area of emerging research given the additional complexities of disease management in older adults [52, 53]. Similarly, the reliance on collection of data from AHCL systems means that data on aspects such as prior treatments, reasons for any discontinuations, or failures to upload data (plus any link between any baseline characteristic and discontinuation or failure to upload data) were not available for analysis nor was it possible to include a control group for comparison. There was also no information on whether additional interventions such as patient education were delivered at the same time as AHCL initiation. Furthermore, the restriction of the longitudinal analysis to patients with 12 months of data may have resulted in a survivorship bias. Nevertheless, the real-world nature of the data used for the analysis presented here ensures that the insights found are of direct relevance to real-world T1D management in Spain. The use of the CareLink Personal system with its automatic upload features in addition to the 3-month data storage on the device reduces selection bias and facilitates a consistent data coverage thus ensuring the robustness of the results.

Conclusion

This RWE study shows that the initiation of AHCL control with MiniMed 780G devices results in large improvements in glycemic control, with substantial increases in the proportion of users achieving international glycemic control targets. These results from 16,390 users are aligned with those of clinical studies conducted in Spain [17, 23–31]. The use of such systems is therefore likely to be of great benefit to people with T1D in Spain, increasing the time they spend in target glucose ranges while reducing the time they spend both above and below those ranges. Importantly, improvements in TITR were observed without increases in time below range. These benefits can be realized across age groups and tend to be even larger among those who use ROS, suggesting that user and clinician education regarding the most optimal use of the system could maximize the improvements in glycemic control.
