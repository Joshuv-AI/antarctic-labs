Application of Quantitative Computed Tomography and Finite Element Analysis to the Lunate: An Exploratory Study of Bone Density and Strength in Young and Older Women
Authors: Masato Nakadai, Takuya Yoda, Nobuaki Suzuki, Naoto Tsubokawa, Koji Moriya, Hiroyuki Kawashima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeLunate collapse in older adult women has been sporadically reported as a condition distinct from classical Kienböck disease, which is typically observed in young men, and may represent fragility fractures associated with osteoporosis. Nonetheless, age-related differences in lunate bone density and mechanical properties have not been quantitatively assessed owing to the bone’s small and complex anatomy.MethodsWe conducted an exploratory cross-sectional study of 21 young women (20–29 years) and 57 older adult women (≥65 years). Using quantitative computed tomography with finite element analysis, we measured volumetric bone mineral density (vBMD) in the cortical, subchondral, and trabecular regions and estimated predicted bone strength. Between-group and within-group comparisons were performed, and correlations with age were assessed.ResultsOlder adult women demonstrated lower vBMD and predicted bone strength values than young women in both wrists, except for cortical vBMD. In young women, vBMD in all regions and predicted bone strength were higher in the dominant wrists. Age showed the strongest negative correlation with vBMD and predicted bone strength in the trabecular and subchondral regions.ConclusionsThis exploratory study demonstrates the feasibility of applying quantitative computed tomography with finite element analysis to the lunate and provides preliminary quantitative evidence of age-related bone deterioration. The pronounced decline in subchondral and trabecular bone suggests that lunate collapse in older women may share features with subchondral insufficiency fractures. Therefore, quantitative computed tomography with finite element analysis may be a promising tool for investigating small, anatomically complex bones and for establishing a methodological basis for future cadaveric validation and clinical studies.Type of study/level of evidencePrognostic III.Key words: Bone mineral density, Finite element analysis, Lunate, Osteoporosis, Quantitative computed tomography

Kienböck disease in older women has been occasionally documented. Unlike the classical form, which is typically observed in young men and primarily attributed to avascular necrosis associated with mechanical overuse, the condition in older women has been hypothesized to represent a fragility fracture associated with osteoporosis.1, 2, 3 However, quantitative evidence supporting this hypothesis remains limited, and age-associated differences in the lunate have not been adequately explored.

Dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry (DXA), widely used to assess bone mineral density, presents limitations for small, anatomically complex bones, including the lunate. Microcomputed tomography (micro-CT) enables high-resolution analysis of bone microarchitecture, and studies have applied it to investigate the microarchitecture of the lunate in both healthy individuals and patients with Kienböck disease.4 However, this approach is impractical for clinical applications. Conversely, quantitative computed tomography (QCT) enables volumetric bone mineral density (vBMD) measurements in Hounsfield units (HU) and, when combined with finite element analysis (FEA), provides a noninvasive estimate of bone strength, termed predicted bone strength (PBS). This methodology has been established in the vertebrae,5 proximal femur,6 and radius,7 and more accurately reflects biomechanical characteristics than DXA.8 FEA has been applied to the lunate in a few studies, primarily to investigate stress distribution and simulate osteotomy, suggesting its potential clinical utility in wrist biomechanics.9,10 Nevertheless, PBS-based approaches for bone strength measurement have not yet been applied to the lunate.

We hypothesized that the lunate, similar to major osteoporotic sites such as the femur, vertebrae, and radius, undergoes progressive bone fragility with age. This exploratory study aimed to establish the feasibility of applying QCT combined with FEA to the lunate and to quantitatively assess age-related differences in vBMD and PBS between young and older women.

Materials and Methods

Study design

This was an exploratory cross-sectional study designed to evaluate age-related differences in the lunate using QCT/FEA. The study protocol, including the use of CT data, was approved by the institutional review boards of the authors’ affiliated institutions.

Participants and eligibility criteria

This study included healthy female volunteers aged 20–29 years (young group) and women aged ≥65 years who underwent CT scanning for diagnostic evaluation of distal radius fractures (older group). Age categories were defined according to the criteria of the Japan Osteoporosis Society for calculating the young adult mean11 and the World Health Organization definition of older adults. The young group was recruited through posters and direct invitations, and informed consent was obtained. The older group provided informed consent during clinical evaluation. Participants with a history of wrist fracture, inflammatory joint disease, systemic bone disease, or prior osteoporosis treatment were excluded. Because this study was designed for methodological validation, no formal sample size calculation was performed.

CT acquisition and data processing

All scans were performed using clinical multislice CT (Aquilion Lightning/Aquilion Precision; Canon Medical Systems). Imaging parameters were as follows: 120 kV, auto mA, 512 × 512 matrix, slice thickness 0.5 mm, and pixel width 0.6 mm. A hydroxyapatite calibration phantom (B-MAS200; Kyoto Kagaku) was used for bone density calibration. Scans were obtained with the forearm in a neutral position and the wrist dorsiflexed at 30°, with the participant in the prone position and both upper extremities elevated to minimize trunk exposure. Images were saved in DICOM format.

Finite element model construction

Finite element (FE) models were generated using Mechanical Finder (version 12.0; Research Center of Computational Mechanics). Voxels with HU ≥ 300 were segmented as the lunate. This threshold was selected based on the default settings of the software, which provided reliable delineation of the lunate contours. The model was meshed with 1.0-mm tetrahedral elements, and triangular shell elements (1.0 mm side length, 0.4 mm thickness) were applied to the surface to construct the lunate FE model.5, 6, 7 Material properties were assigned based on ash density derived from HU values, and the Young’s modulus and yield stress of each element were calculated using equations reported by Keyak et al.12 Poisson’s ratio was set to 0.4.5, 6, 7

vBMD measurement

The lunate was divided into cortical, subchondral, and trabecular regions for vBMD measurement based on micro-CT morphological studies.4 The cortical, subchondral, and trabecular regions were defined as the outer 0.34 mm, 0.34–1.79 mm beneath the surface, and the area deeper than 1.79 mm, respectively. To reproduce this classification, the previously generated lunate mesh without shell elements was exported in STL (stereolithography) format, which represents the three-dimensional surface geometry of the model. The outer surface was isotropically reduced by 0.34 mm and 1.79 mm to create cortical and subchondral models, which were saved in IMP format, a proprietary intermediate file format used in Mechanical Finder for finite element model construction. These reduced models were superimposed onto the original lunate model to construct a three-layer FE model. The analysis priority was set in the following order: 1.79-mm reduction, 0.34-mm reduction, and the original model. These regions were defined using an automated isotropic surface reduction function in Mechanical Finder to ensure high reproducibility without operator-dependent variability. Regional vBMD values were calculated independently of the mean HU values of the corresponding elements (Fig. 1).

Boundary conditions

The distal articular surface of the lunate was completely restrained. A uniform displacement was applied to the proximal articular surface along the lunate axis in ramped 0.01-mm increments until the failure criteria were reached (Fig. 2). The yield of each element was determined at the point at which the Drucker–Prager equivalent stress exceeded the yield stress. The fracture load was identified by a rapid decline in load on the force–displacement curve7 (Fig. 3). Fracture load was defined as PBS.

Statistical analysis

Nonparametric tests were used because of the limited sample size. Group comparisons between young and older women were performed separately for dominant and nondominant wrists using the Mann–Whitney U test. Within-group comparisons between dominant and nondominant wrists were conducted using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test only for the young group, for which paired data from the same individuals were available. Correlations with age were assessed using Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient. Analyses were conducted using EZR software (Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University).13 No missing data were recorded.

Ethical approval and informed consent

This study was approved by the institutional review boards of the authors’ affiliated institutions. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrollment. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data statement

The datasets generated and analyzed during the current study are not publicly available due to institutional and ethical restrictions regarding patient privacy, but are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request and with approval of the institutional review board.

Results

Participants

The young group comprised 21 women with a mean age of 23.9 years (range, 22–29 years). The older group included 57 women with a mean age of 74.5 years (range, 65–94 years). Among the older group, 38 and 19 wrists were dominant (mean age, 75.2 years; range, 65–94 years) and nondominant (mean age, 73.2 years; range, 66–89 years), respectively (Table 1). vBMD and PBS of the lunate were successfully calculated for all participants.

Within-group comparisons (dominant vs nondominant wrists)

Analyses were performed to assess the effect of hand dominance on lunate bone properties in the young cohort. In the young group, vBMD was higher in the dominant wrists across the entire lunate, as well as in the cortical, subchondral, and trabecular regions; PBS was also higher (Table 2).

Correlation with age

Analyses were conducted to explore the association between bone properties and age within the study population. In dominant wrists, trabecular vBMD exhibited the strongest negative correlation with age (ρ = –0.704, 95% confidence interval [CI]: −0.81 to −0.55, P < .001), followed by subchondral vBMD (ρ = –0.658, 95% CI: −0.78 to −0.48, P < .001), entire lunate vBMD (ρ = –0.655, 95% CI: −0.79 to −0.49, P < .001), and PBS (ρ = –0.644, 95% CI: −0.79 to −0.45, P < .001). Cortical vBMD demonstrated only a weak negative correlation (ρ = –0.302, 95% CI: −0.52 to −0.05, P = .020) (Fig. 4).

In nondominant wrists, similar patterns were observed: trabecular vBMD (ρ = –0.701, 95% CI: −0.82 to −0.48, P < .001), subchondral vBMD (ρ = –0.685, 95% CI: −0.82 to −0.48, P < .001), entire lunate vBMD (ρ = –0.685, 95% CI: −0.82 to −0.48, P < .001), and PBS (ρ = –0.636, 95% CI: −0.82 to −0.37, P < .001) exhibited negative correlations with age, whereas cortical vBMD showed a weaker association (ρ = –0.335, 95% CI: −0.59 to −0.03, P = .035) (Fig. 4).

Discussion

This exploratory study applied QCT combined with FEA to the lunate to quantitatively evaluate age-related differences in healthy women. Older women exhibited significantly lower vBMD and PBS than younger women, with both parameters negatively correlated with age, aligning with previously reported findings in the proximal femur and vertebrae.14,15 In young women, dominant wrists showed higher vBMD and PBS, consistent with DXA-based reports on forearm bone density.16,17 These results support the validity of this approach and demonstrate a methodological advancement for the quantitative assessment of small, anatomically complex bones, such as the lunate, using clinical CT.

Region-specific analysis demonstrated that age-related decreases in vBMD were most pronounced in trabecular and subchondral bone, whereas cortical bone showed only minimal differences, consistent with the higher turnover rate and osteoporotic vulnerability of trabecular bone.18 Micro-CT studies of normal lunates have reported that the subchondral bone beneath the distal articular surface consists of short vertical trabeculae that absorb impact and provide support to the articular surface. Conversely, trabecular bone contains radially oriented trabeculae that connect the proximal and distal surfaces to enhance load-bearing capacity.4 Our findings may quantitatively represent the age-related degradation of these supportive structures. Nonetheless, in the cortical bone analysis, the cortical region was defined as 0.34 mm (thinner than the clinical CT slice thickness), which may have increased susceptibility to partial volume effects and limited its accuracy.19 Therefore, further methodological refinement may be required for accurate cortical assessment.

Finite element studies of bone strength using the same software have been reported for the vertebrae,5 proximal femur,6 and radius.7 Although fracture definitions and boundary conditions have not been standardized across studies, strong correlations between PBS and cadaveric fracture loads have been demonstrated,5, 6, 7 supporting the methodological validity of this analytical approach. We adopted the displacement-control approach described by Matsuura et al7 and identified fracture load by a rapid decline in load on the force–displacement curve. Thus, this study applied an established and validated methodology to the lunate, and the results can be considered methodologically justified.

Given these biomechanical findings, the observed deterioration of subchondral and trabecular bone may have clinical implications. Lunate collapse in older women is considered a fragility fracture associated with osteoporosis. Our results demonstrate pronounced age-related deterioration in these regions, which are critical for load-bearing and joint support. Thus, the underlying mechanism may resemble subchondral insufficiency fractures of the femoral head and condyle.20,21 Although pathological confirmation is required, this study provides preliminary evidence consistent with this interpretation.

This study has several limitations. First, it was designed as an exploratory investigation, and no formal sample size calculation for hypothesis testing was performed. The relatively small sample size may affect the stability of statistically significant results; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary and hypothesis-generating, with caution. Second, the study population was restricted to young and older women, excluding men and middle-aged individuals. Third, the correlation between PBS and actual fracture loads has not yet been validated in cadaveric experiments. Accordingly, definitive conclusions regarding absolute bone strength in the lunate cannot be drawn. Future studies should address these limitations by conducting larger studies based on appropriate sample size calculations, including broader age ranges and both sexes. In addition, the PBS definition should be validated using cadaveric lunate specimens to confirm whether it appropriately reflects the actual fracture mechanism.

In conclusion, this study demonstrated the feasibility of quantifying age-related differences in the lunate using clinical CT and showed that QCT/FEA can be applied to even small carpal bones with complex anatomy. Further research, including cadaveric validation, larger longitudinal investigations, and clinical correlations, may enhance understanding of lunate collapse in older women.
