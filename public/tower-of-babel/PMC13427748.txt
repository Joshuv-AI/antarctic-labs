Impact in engineering research: more than a number
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Of the many journal metrics available to consider in weighing up the performance of a research journal, probably the best known is the Impact Factor (IF). The IF provides a mean average of the citations accrued in one year by all the papers of a particular journal published in the previous two years, based on citation data from Web of Science. The release of a journal’s first IF will be of interest to the research community, and ours was published in June of this year.

An imperfect metric

The IF is widely used by researchers and others in the academic world as a tool to broadly understand the reach of a research journal’s publication output. But we must remember that the IF is an imperfect metric: it can be skewed upwards in several ways. For example, it can be inflated by publishing more reviews (which tend to accrue higher citations), it can be higher for journals in very active fields, and it can be elevated via the publication of just one or two very highly cited papers. It is also dependent on the coverage of journals in the database from which citations are counted. The IF of a journal certainly does not reflect the quality of any individual piece of research published within.

The IF should be considered alongside the variety of other available metrics available on the Journal Citations Reports from Clarivate, and from other bibliometric databases. Researchers should also bear in mind the more qualitative value that their community places on a journal as a venue for their work. Most journals will also provide important performance data on their publisher site, such as manuscript decision turnaround times and acceptance rates, which researchers should use to guide their decisions on where to submit based on what is important to them (our journal data is provided here1).

Impact case studies

Engineering research is one field which may not accrue as many citations as more fundamental research. Its impact may not always lie in creating new research directions (resulting in more publications and citations) but instead via implementation in the real world and/or solving practical problems. This sort of impact is hard to quantify. In the UK, universities submit Impact Case Studies to the Research Excellence Framework2, showcasing in a qualitative way, how their research benefits society. Inspired by this approach, we decided we would try to complement the news of our first IF, with some insight into the impact of our publications away from the creation of citations. So, we reached out to some of our authors who published with us in our first year of publication, 2022. We asked them to share how their papers have had impact four years on. Today, we publish a collection of short Impact Case Studies based on five of our publications from 2022, in a Viewpoint Collection3.

Our case studies show that a four-year time frame represents the point at which immediate community and media recognition have already been felt, and mid-term impacts are in train. These include further technology development, the receipt of venture capital or additional necessary grant funding, author career development and the building of teams and testbeds. The twists and turns of how research directions develop are also captured. Full real-world implementation and societal benefit, whilst still a clear goal for researchers, overall, remains some years away.

Responsible referencing

The origin of every IF lies in the citations found in the reference lists of the thousands of papers published every week, which determine the numerator in the IF metric. Due to the power and influence of the IF and another citation metric, the H-Index (a researcher’s H-index reflects a number H of papers that have been cited at least H times), it may be tempting to manipulate the reference list to inflate these metrics for attempted professional gain.

It is acceptable to self-cite and include reviewer references in a reference list, but with restraint and only if they directly improve the value of the paper for the reader. During peer review, authors, editors, and reviewers should feel confident to push back when they have concerns regarding excessive self-citation from authors or coercive citation requests from reviewers.

Status bias can also negatively impact reference lists. This can be seen in the preferential citation of highly cited papers, well-known authors, or journals, while less attention is paid to relevant work from less visible researchers, regions, journals or historically under-represented groups. This could be conscious (with the goal of signalling higher impact of a contribution) or unconscious.

These practices don’t just degrade the value of citation metrics. They slow down peer review and make the most relevant literature harder for readers to find. A good literature review will do the precise opposite, and the research community will appreciate that far more than a high H-index.

Citation metrics explore academic impact via the research literature. But for a broader view, Impact Case Studies recognise and celebrate the diversity with which research gives to society beyond the academic literature. At the four-year time point, our Case Studies also reveal insights into what our authors are experiencing on their journey from publication to practical utility. We hope that sharing these stories will be inspiring to other engineering researchers whose goal is for their work to make a difference in the real world.
