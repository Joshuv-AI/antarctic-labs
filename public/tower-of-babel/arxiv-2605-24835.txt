Poisson fields of two variables
Ken Goodearl and James J. Zhang

Abstract
. We study invariants and structures of Poisson fields of rational functions in two variables. For four particular families, we classify the members, establish criteria for isomorphisms and, with the exception of the Weyl Poisson field, describe the automorphism groups. Embeddings are also investigated, along with an analog of the Dixmier Conjecture: For which Poisson fields is every Poisson endomorphism an automorphism? The answer is negative for the first family, but positive answers are obtained for several subclasses of the other families. Finally, we exhibit a Poisson field which is not isomorphic to any Poisson field 𝕜 ⁡ ( x , y ) \Bbbk(x,y) for which { x , y } \{x,y\} is a polynomial in 𝕜 ⁡ [ x , y ] \Bbbk[x,y] .

Introduction

Poisson algebras have been used extensively
in many areas, and several important
questions, such as automorphism and
isomorphism problems, for Poisson algebras
or fields, have recently been studied by
several authors
[
1
,
10
,
7
,
8
]
.
The subject of Poisson algebras is closely
connected to Poisson geometry, deformation
quantization, and noncommutative algebraic
geometry. For example, Poisson fields are
viewed as an analogue of skew fields that
are essential in the study of noncommutative
birational geometry.

There are two important Poisson fields
that are commonly studied and have close
connections to other research topics. The
first one is the
Weyl Poisson field

K_{\Weyl}:=\Bbbk(x,y\mid\{x,y\}=1)

whose underlying commutative field is
the field of rational functions of two
variables
\Bbbk(x,y)
and whose Poisson
structure is determined by the equation

\{x,y\}=1
. Here we fix a base field

\Bbbk
that is of characteristic zero.
Throughout this introduction, we assume
that
\Bbbk
is algebraically closed.

The second well known instance is the

q
-skew Poisson field
, for a scalar

q\in\Bbbk
, namely

K_{q}:=\Bbbk(x,y\mid\{x,y\}=qxy)

whose underlying commutative field is

\Bbbk(x,y)
and whose Poisson structure
is determined by the equation
\{x,y\}=qxy
.
Note that the Weyl and
q
-skew Poisson
fields can be viewed as semiclassical
limits of the Weyl skew field

\Fract\Bbbk\langle x,y\mid xy-yx=1\rangle
and the skew field

\Fract\Bbbk\langle x,y\mid xy=[(p-1)q+1]yx\rangle
.

More generally, for every element

{\mathbf{f}}\in\Bbbk(x,y)
one can define a
Poisson algebra structure on
\Bbbk(x,y)

by using the equation

(E0.0.1)

\{x,y\}={\mathbf{f}}.

More precisely, there is a unique Poisson
structure on
\Bbbk(x,y)
such that
(
E0.0.1
) holds (see Corollary

9.4
). For simplicity,
we use
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
to denote this
Poisson field. In this case we say
{\mathbf{f}}

is the
flag
of
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
. Using
this convention,
K_{\Weyl}=K\{1\}
and

K_{q}=K\{qxy\}
. It is well-known that

K_{q}
is not isomorphic to
K_{\Weyl}

(e.g.,
[
5
, Corollary 5.3]
). In
fact,
K_{q}
cannot be embedded in

K_{\Weyl}

[
8
, Corollary 5.9(2)]
.
On the other hand, given two general
elements
{\mathbf{f}}_{1},{\mathbf{f}}_{2}
in
\Bbbk(x,y)
,
it is difficult to tell whether

K\{{\mathbf{f}}_{1}\}
is isomorphic to
K\{{\mathbf{f}}_{2}\}

or not. So we formally ask the following
questions:

Question 0.1

(Classification Problem)
.

Can we classify all Poisson fields of
the form
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
up to Poisson
isomorphism?

This problem is extremely difficult and
the moduli of Poisson fields
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}

is very complicated. We introduce some
invariants that can help or control the

Definition 0.2
.

Let
K
be a Poisson field of the form

K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
as defined above. The

of

is defined to be

(E0.2.1)

\fht(K):=\min\{\deg{\mathbf{g}}\;\mid\;K\cong K\{{\mathbf{g}}\},\;{\mathbf{g}}\in\Bbbk[x,y]\}

if there exist
{\mathbf{g}}\in\Bbbk[x,y]

. Otherwise, we
define
\fht(K):=\infty
.

For every non-negative integer

, let

denote the moduli space of
Poisson fields

with
\fht

h
.
Trivially,
Poi_{0}
consists of one
Poisson field, which is
K_{\Weyl}
.
(This assumes that we view the zero
polynomial as having degree less than
0
.)
It is easy to check that
Poi_{1}
is empty
(cf. Proposition
1.1
(1)(2)).
Dumas
[
3
, Proposition, p.12]

proved that every Poisson field

with
\fht
2 is isomorphic
to
K_{q}

(\cong K_{-q})
for some

q\in\Bbbk^{\times}
. This also gives
a classification of the Poisson fields
in

. In
[
6
]
we work out
a description of

(as well as
some partial classifications of

when
h=4,5
). Suppose
{\mathbf{f}}\in\Bbbk[x,y]

has degree 3. If
{\mathbf{f}}
is reducible (or
more generally if the curve
{\mathbf{f}}=0

is singular), then

is isomorphic
to either

or

. If the curve

]
. For

When

is irreducible of degree 4, we

6
]
. When

{\mathbf{f}}
is reducible of degree 4, it is
also very complicated to understand

K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
.

For higher

, it is impossible to

of

using currently available

consider several families of reducible

and the corresponding Poisson

.

Question 0.5

(Embedding Problem)
.

Let
{\mathbf{f}}_{1}
and
{\mathbf{f}}_{2}
be two elements
in
\Bbbk(x,y)
. Are there any effective

Motivated by the Embedding Problem, we

Definition 0.6
.

Let
K
and
K^{\prime}
be Poisson fields of
the form
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
.

(1)

We say
K
is
height cohereditary

if
\fht(K)\leq\fht(K^{\prime})
for arbitrary

.

(2)

We say

is

\fht(K^{\prime})\leq\fht(K)

.

Question 0.7
.

Is there a Poisson field
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
that
is not height (co)hereditary?

By definition,

is height cohereditary.
It follows from

that

is height cohereditary. In

and

. But we cannot verify

in particular, we do not know if

(resp.,

for

There is another notion of “height”,
called

-valuation height
and
denoted by
\vht_{1}
, see Definition

. The

-valuation height
has a few advantages:

(1)

is defined for all Poisson

(2)

(3)

is bounded above by

when applied to

[Lemma

In general,

,

and

The automorphism problem is always

.

Let

(solving a

]

does contain the

with

[

]
, but

In this paper we will compute

For which nonzero

Conventions

Throughout the paper,
\Bbbk
is a fixed
base field of characteristic zero,
all algebras and fields are commutative

\Bbbk
-algebras, and
K
is a field
extension of
\Bbbk
. We do not
assume
\Bbbk
algebraically closed
except where necessary. When
K
is
not denoted a general (Poisson) field,
we assume that
K=\Bbbk(x,y)
is a
rational function field in two
variables.

For any
{\mathbf{f}}\in K=\Bbbk(x,y)
,
there is a unique Poisson bracket on

K
such that
\{x,y\}={\mathbf{f}}

(Corollary
9.4
). The Poisson
field with this bracket will be denoted

K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
.

The symbol
\cong
between Poisson fields

means that
K
and

are

We denote the Poisson bracket on

by
\{-,-\}_{w}
, and we recall that this

\{f,g\}_{w}=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}\frac{\partial g}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial y}\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}\qquad\forall\;f,g\in K.

1.
Some initial results

We give some classification results and
some instances of Poisson subfields that
involve only direct bracket calculations
and changes of variables.

1.1.
Poisson fields isomorphic to
the Weyl or
q
-skew Poisson fields

Dumas showed in
[
3
]
that when

{\mathbf{f}}\in\Bbbk[x,y]
is a nonzero
polynomial with (total) degree at most

2
, the Poisson field
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
is
isomorphic to either
K_{\Weyl}
or
K_{q}

for some
q\in\Bbbk^{\times}
. We give some
extensions of Dumas’ arguments and
consequences.

Proposition 1.1
.

Let
K=K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
with
{\mathbf{f}}\in K^{\times}
.

(1)

If
{\mathbf{f}}\in\Bbbk(x)
or

{\mathbf{f}}\in\Bbbk(y)
, then
K\cong K_{\Weyl}
.

(2)

If
{\mathbf{f}}=f+g
with
f\in\Bbbk(x)
,

g\in\Bbbk[y]
, and
\deg g\leq 1
,
then
K\cong K_{\Weyl}
.

(3)

Suppose
{\mathbf{f}}=xf+g
with
f,g\in\Bbbk(y)
and
f\neq 0
. Then

K=\Bbbk(x^{\prime},y)
with
\{x^{\prime},y\}=x^{\prime}f
.

•

If
f\in\Bbbk^{\times}
, then
K\cong K_{\Weyl}
.

•

If
f=qy+r
with
q\in\Bbbk^{\times}
and

r\in\Bbbk
, then
K\cong K_{q}
.

(4)

Suppose
{\mathbf{f}}=x^{2}f+xg+h
with

f,g,h\in\Bbbk(y)
and
f\neq 0
.
Then
K=\Bbbk(x^{\prime\prime},y)
with

\{x^{\prime\prime},y\}=(x^{\prime\prime})^{2}+h^{\prime\prime}
and

h^{\prime\prime}:=fh-\frac{1}{4}g^{2}\in\Bbbk(y)
.

•

If
h^{\prime\prime}\in\Bbbk[y]
with

\deg h^{\prime\prime}\leq 1
, then
K\cong K_{\Weyl}
.

•

If
h^{\prime\prime}=\alpha y^{2}+\beta y+\gamma
with
\alpha\in\Bbbk^{\times}

and
\beta,\gamma\in\Bbbk
, and if

-\alpha
has square roots in
\Bbbk
,
then
K\cong K_{q}
with

q:=2\sqrt{-\alpha}\in\Bbbk^{\times}
.

Proof.

(1) If
\{x,y\}=f\in\Bbbk(x)
,
take
y^{\prime}:=f^{-1}y
. Then

K=\Bbbk(x,y^{\prime})
and
\{x,y^{\prime}\}=f^{-1}\{x,y\}=1
, so

K\cong K_{\Weyl}
. The case when

\{x,y\}\in\Bbbk(y)
is symmetric.

(2) If
\deg g\leq 0
, then

\{x,y\}\in\Bbbk(x)
, and so

K\cong K_{\Weyl}
by (1). Now suppose
that
g=\beta y+\gamma
with

\beta\in\Bbbk^{\times}
,
\gamma\in\Bbbk
.
If
y^{\prime}:=f+g
, then
K=\Bbbk(x,y^{\prime})

and

\{x,y^{\prime}\}=\beta\{x,y\}=\beta y^{\prime},

and again (1) implies that

K\cong K_{\Weyl}
.

(3) Set
x^{\prime}:=xf+g
. Then
K=\Bbbk(x^{\prime},y)
and
\{x^{\prime},y\}=\{x,y\}f=x^{\prime}f
.

When
f\in\Bbbk^{\times}
, we have
\{x^{\prime},y\}\in\Bbbk(x^{\prime})
, so
K\cong K_{\Weyl}

by (1).

Now suppose
f=qy+r
with
q\in\Bbbk^{\times}
and
r\in\Bbbk
. Then
K=\Bbbk(x^{\prime},f)
and
\{x^{\prime},f\}=q\{x^{\prime},y\}=qx^{\prime}f
, so
K\cong K_{q}
.

(4) First set
x^{\prime}:=xf
. Then

K=\Bbbk(x^{\prime},y)
and

\{x^{\prime},y\}=\{x,y\}f=x^{2}f^{2}+xgf+hf=(x^{\prime})^{2}+x^{\prime}g+hf.

Now set
x^{\prime\prime}:=x^{\prime}+\frac{1}{2}g
. Then

K=\Bbbk(x^{\prime\prime},y)
and

\{x^{\prime\prime},y\}=\{x^{\prime},y\}=(x^{\prime})^{2}+x^{\prime}g+hf=(x^{\prime\prime})^{2}-\tfrac{1}{4}g^{2}+hf,

so we have the first part of (4).
The first bullet then follows from (2).

Suppose
h^{\prime\prime}=\alpha y^{2}+\beta y+\gamma
with
\alpha\in\Bbbk^{\times}
,

\beta,\gamma\in\Bbbk
. Then

h^{\prime\prime}=\alpha(y+\frac{\beta}{2\alpha})^{2}+(\gamma-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\alpha})
.
If
y^{\prime}:=y+\frac{\beta}{2\alpha}
, then

K=\Bbbk(x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime})
and

\{x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime}\}=\{x^{\prime\prime},y\}=(x^{\prime\prime})^{2}+h^{\prime\prime}=(x^{\prime\prime})^{2}+\alpha(y^{\prime})^{2}+\gamma^{\prime}

where
\gamma^{\prime}:=\gamma-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\alpha}
.

By assumption, there exists

\zeta\in\Bbbk^{\times}
such that
\zeta^{2}=-\alpha
. If
x^{*}:=x^{\prime\prime}-\zeta y^{\prime}

and
y^{*}:=x^{\prime\prime}+\zeta y^{\prime}
, then

K=\Bbbk(x^{*},y^{*})
and

\{x^{*},y^{*}\}=2\zeta\{x^{\prime\prime},y^{\prime}\}=2\zeta\bigl((x^{\prime\prime})^{2}+\alpha(y^{\prime})^{2}+\gamma^{\prime}\bigr)=2\zeta x^{*}y^{*}+2\zeta\gamma^{\prime}\,.

Setting
q:=2\zeta
and

x^{**}:=x^{*}+\gamma^{\prime}(y^{*})^{-1}
, we
see that
K=\Bbbk(x^{**},y^{*})
and

\{x^{**},y^{*}\}=\{x^{*},y^{*}\}=qx^{*}y^{*}+q\gamma^{\prime}=q\bigl(x^{**}-\gamma^{\prime}(y^{*})^{-1}\bigr)y^{*}+q\gamma^{\prime}=qx^{**}y^{*}.

Thus,
K\cong K_{q}
in this case.
∎

K\cong K_{q}
.

(3)

Suppose
f=\alpha y^{2}+\beta y+\gamma

with
\alpha\in\Bbbk^{\times}
and
\beta,\gamma\in\Bbbk
.

•

If
\beta^{2}=4\alpha\gamma
, then

K\cong K_{\Weyl}
.

•

If
\beta^{2}\neq 4\alpha\gamma
and

\beta^{2}-4\alpha\gamma
has square
roots in
\Bbbk
, then
K\cong K_{q}

where
q=\sqrt{\beta^{2}-4\alpha\gamma}\in\Bbbk^{\times}
.

Proof.

(1) and (2) follow from Proposition

1.1
(1)(3).

(3) Write
f=\alpha(y^{\prime})^{2}+\gamma^{\prime}

where
y^{\prime}:=y+\frac{\beta}{2\alpha}

and
\gamma^{\prime}:=\gamma-\frac{\beta^{2}}{4\alpha}
. Then

K=\Bbbk(x,y^{\prime})
and

\{x,y^{\prime}\}=\{x,y\}=x\bigl(\alpha(y^{\prime})^{2}+\gamma^{\prime}\bigr).

\bullet
Suppose that
\beta^{2}=4\alpha\gamma
, so that
\gamma^{\prime}=0
.
Then
K=\Bbbk((y^{\prime})^{-1},x)
and

\{(y^{\prime})^{-1},x\}=-(y^{\prime})^{-2}\{y^{\prime},x\}=(y^{\prime})^{-2}\alpha x(y^{\prime})^{2}=\alpha x,

whence
K\cong K_{\Weyl}
by Proposition

1.1
(1).

\bullet
Suppose that
\beta^{2}\neq 4\alpha\gamma
, so that

\gamma^{\prime}\neq 0
. Then

K=\Bbbk(\gamma^{\prime}x,xy^{\prime})
and

\{\gamma^{\prime}x,xy^{\prime}\}=\gamma^{\prime}x\{x,y^{\prime}\}=\gamma^{\prime}x^{2}\bigl(\alpha(y^{\prime})^{2}+\gamma^{\prime}\bigr)=(\gamma^{\prime}x)^{2}+\gamma^{\prime}\alpha(xy^{\prime})^{2},

so Proposition
1.1
(4)
implies that
K\cong K_{q}
where

q:=2\sqrt{-\gamma^{\prime}\alpha}=\sqrt{\beta^{2}-4\alpha\gamma}
.
∎

What happens in Corollary
1.3

when
\deg f\geq 3
? As we show later, the
resulting Poisson fields need not be
isomorphic to
K_{\Weyl}
or to any

– see Proposition
4.6

and the remarks following Proposition

5.2
, Lemma

(after interchanging

and

). In
particular,
K\{xy^{3}\}
is not
isomorphic to
K_{\Weyl}
or to any
K_{q}
.

1.2.
Some Poisson subfields

Understanding subfields of
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
is helpful for
the Embedding Problem [Question
0.5
].
We exhibit a number of Poisson subfields of our standard
Poisson fields. Here and later, we use the following
observation without explicit mention:

Observation 1.4
.

[
7
, Lemma 1.6(1)]

Let
K
be a Poisson
field over
\Bbbk
and
f,g\in K
such that
\{f,g\}\neq 0
. Then
f

and
g
are algebraically independent over
\Bbbk
.

For any rational function field in one variable, we abbreviate the standard derivative by
′
. In particular,
f^{\prime}:=df/dx
and
g^{\prime}:=dg/dy
for
f\in\Bbbk(x)
and
g\in\Bbbk(y)
.

Examples 1.5
.

(1)

Let
K=K_{\Weyl}
. For any
f\in\Bbbk(x)\setminus\Bbbk
,
the subfield
\Bbbk(f,y/f^{\prime})
is a Poisson subfield of
K

isomorphic to
K
. If
f\in\Bbbk[x]
, then

[K:\Bbbk(f,y/f^{\prime})]=\deg f
.

Similarly, for any
g\in\Bbbk(y)\setminus\Bbbk
, the
subfield
\Bbbk(x/g^{\prime},g)
is a Poisson subfield of
K

isomorphic to
K
. If
g\in\Bbbk[y]
, then

[K:\Bbbk(x/g^{\prime},g)]=\deg g
.

In the first case, set
L:=\Bbbk(f,y/f^{\prime})
. Since

\{f,y/f^{\prime}\}=f^{\prime}\cdot(1/f^{\prime})-0\cdot(-y(f^{\prime})^{-2}f^{\prime\prime})=1,

L
is a Poisson subfield isomorphic to
K
.

Now suppose that
f\in\Bbbk[x]
, and note that
L(x)=K
.
If
\deg f=1
, then
x\in L
and so
[K:L]=1
. Next,
assume that
\deg f=n>1
. We have a polynomial

f(t)-f(x)\in L[t]
with
t
-degree
n
having
x
as a
root, so
[K:L]\leq n
. If this inequality is strict,

\sum_{i=0}^{m}b_{i}x^{i}=0
for some
b_{i}\in L
with
m<n

and
b_{m}\neq 0
. After multiplying this equation by a suitable
element of
\Bbbk[f,y/f^{\prime}]
, we may assume that all

b_{i}\in\Bbbk[f,y/f^{\prime}]
. However, this contradicts the fact
that
\Bbbk[x,y/f^{\prime}]
is a free module over
\Bbbk[f,y/f^{\prime}]

with basis
(1,x,\dots,x^{n-1})
. Therefore
[K:L]=n
, as
claimed.

The second case is proved in the same fashion.

(2)

Let
K=K_{\Weyl}
. For any nonzero integer
a
, the
subfield
\Bbbk(x^{a},xy)
is a Poisson subfield of
K

isomorphic to
K
, with
[K:\Bbbk(x^{a},xy)]=|a|
.
Similarly,
\Bbbk(xy,y^{a})
is a Poisson subfield of
K

isomorphic to
K
, with
[K:\Bbbk(xy,y^{a})]=|a|
.

In the first case, set
f(x)=x^{a}
and note that

\Bbbk(x^{a},xy)=\Bbbk(f(x),af(x)y/f^{\prime}(x))=\Bbbk(f(x),y/f^{\prime}(x)),

and so
\Bbbk(x^{a},xy)\cong K
by part (1). If
a>0
,
part (1) also shows that
[K:\Bbbk(x^{a},xy)]=a
. If

a<0
, we have
[K:\Bbbk(x^{a},xy)]=-a
because

\Bbbk(x^{a},xy)=\Bbbk(x^{-a},xy)
.

The second case is similar.

(3)

Let
K=K_{q}
for some
q\in\Bbbk^{\times}
. For any
integers
a,b,c,d
with
ad\neq bc
, the subfield

\Bbbk(x^{a}y^{b},x^{c}y^{d})
is a Poisson subfield of
K

isomorphic to
K_{(ad-bc)q}
, and

[K:\Bbbk(x^{a}y^{b},x^{c}y^{d})]=|ad-bc|
.

The Poisson statements are immediate from the fact that

(E1.5.1)

\displaystyle\{x^{a}y^{b},x^{c}y^{d}\}

\displaystyle=\bigl((ax^{a-1}y^{b})(dx^{c}y^{d-1})-(bx^{a}y^{b-1})(cx^{c-1}y^{d})\bigr)\{x,y\}

\displaystyle=(ad-bc)q(x^{a}y^{b})(x^{c}y^{d}).

For any integral matrix

A=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}r&s\\
t&u\end{smallmatrix}\right)

with
\det A\neq 0
, there is a
\Bbbk
-algebra endomorphism

\phi_{A}
of
K
sending
x\mapsto x^{r}y^{s}
and

y\mapsto x^{t}y^{u}
. Moreover, for any
B\in M_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
with

\det B\neq 0
, we have
\phi_{A}\circ\phi_{B}=\phi_{BA}
.
When
B\in GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, the map
\phi_{B}
is invertible,
whence
\phi_{A}(K)=\phi_{BA}(K)
.

Now set

A:=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)
,
so that
\phi_{A}(K)=\Bbbk(x^{a}y^{b},x^{c}y^{d})
. There is a matrix

B\in GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
, a composition of matrices corresponding to
suitable row operations, such that
BA
is upper triangular,
say

BA=\left(\begin{smallmatrix}r&s\\
0&u\end{smallmatrix}\right)
.
Then
\Bbbk(x^{a}y^{b},x^{c}y^{d})=\phi_{BA}(K)=\Bbbk(x^{r}y^{s},y^{u})

and
ru=\det(BA)=\pm(ad-bc)
. It follows that

[K:\Bbbk(x^{a}y^{b},x^{c}y^{d})]=|r|\cdot|u|=|ad-bc|
.

(4)

Let
K=K\{qx^{ac+1}y^{bd+1}\}
for some
q\in\Bbbk^{\times}
and
some integers
a,b,c,d
with
c,d\neq 0
. Then
\Bbbk(x^{c},y^{d})

is a Poisson subfield of
K
isomorphic to

K\{qcdx^{a+1}y^{b+1}\}
, and
[K:\Bbbk(x^{c},y^{d})]=|cd|
. If
there exist
\xi,\zeta\in\Bbbk^{\times}
such that
\xi^{a}\zeta^{b}=qcd
,
then
\Bbbk(x^{c},y^{d})\cong K\{x^{a+1}y^{b+1}\}
.

For the main case, we observe that

\{x^{c},y^{d}\}=cx^{c-1}dy^{d-1}\{x,y\}=cdqx^{ac+c}y^{bd+d}=qcd(x^{c})^{a+1}(y^{d})^{b+1}.

If there exist
\xi,\zeta\in\Bbbk^{\times}
with
\xi^{a}\zeta^{b}=qcd
, then we have

\displaystyle\{\xi x^{c},\zeta y^{d}\}

\displaystyle=\xi\zeta qcd(x^{c})^{a+1}(y^{d})^{b+1}=\xi^{-a}\zeta^{-b}qcd(\xi x^{c})^{a+1}(\zeta y^{d})^{b+1}

\displaystyle=(\xi x^{c})^{a+1}(\zeta y^{d})^{b+1},

and the subsidiary case follows. The degree equality is clear.

(5)

Let
K=K\{(qx^{a}-b)xy\}
for some
q,b\in\Bbbk^{\times}
and
some nonzero integer
a
. Then
\Bbbk(x^{a},y)
is a Poisson
subfield of
K
isomorphic to
K_{ab}
, and

[K:\Bbbk(x^{a},y)]=|a|
.

Observe that
\Bbbk(x^{a},y)=\Bbbk(bx^{-a}-q,y)
and

\{bx^{-a}-q,y\}=-bax^{-a-1}\{x,y\}=-bax^{-a-1}(qx^{a}-b)xy=ab(bx^{-a}-q)y.

The result follows.

Lemma 1.6
.

Let
K=K\{f(x)g(y)\}
for some nonzero
f\in\Bbbk(x)

and
g\in\Bbbk(y)
, and let
r(t)\in\Bbbk(t)^{\times}

and
\lambda\in\Bbbk^{\times}
. If there exists
p\in\Bbbk(x)^{\times}

with
r(p)\neq 0
and
\frac{p^{\prime}(x)}{r(p(x))}=\frac{\lambda}{f(x)}
, then

\Bbbk(p,y)
is a Poisson subfield of
K
isomorphic to

K\{\lambda r(x)g(y)\}
.

Proof.

If there exists
p\in\Bbbk(x)
with

\frac{p^{\prime}(x)}{r(p(x))}=\frac{\lambda}{f(x)}
, then

\{p,y\}=p^{\prime}(x)\{x,y\}=p^{\prime}(x)f(x)g(y)=\lambda r(p)g(y),

whence
\Bbbk(p,y)\cong K\{\lambda r(x)g(y)\}
.
∎

Corollary 1.7
.

Let
K=K\{f(x)g(y)\}
for some nonzero
f\in\Bbbk(x)

and
g\in\Bbbk(y)
, and let
\lambda,\mu\in\Bbbk^{\times}
.

(1)

Let
m\in\mathbb{Z}
. If there exists
p\in\Bbbk(x)^{\times}
with

\frac{p^{\prime}(x)}{p(x)^{m}}=\frac{\lambda}{f(x)}
, then
\Bbbk(p,y)

is a Poisson subfield of
K
isomorphic to

K\{\lambda x^{m}g(y)\}
.

(2)

If there exists
p\in\Bbbk(x)^{\times}
with

p^{\prime}(x)=\frac{\lambda}{f(x)}
, then
\Bbbk(p,y)
is a Poisson
subfield of
K
isomorphic to
K_{\Weyl}
.

(3)

If there exists
p\in\Bbbk(x)^{\times}
with

\frac{p^{\prime}(x)}{p(x)}=\frac{\lambda}{f(x)}
, then
\Bbbk(p,y)

is a Poisson subfield of
K
isomorphic to
K\{\lambda xg(y)\}
.
Similarly, if there exists
q\in\Bbbk(y)^{\times}
with

\frac{q^{\prime}(y)}{q(y)}=\frac{\mu}{g(y)}
, then
\Bbbk(x,q)
is a
Poisson subfield of
K
isomorphic to
K\{\mu f(x)y\}
. In case
such
p
and

both exist,
\Bbbk(p,q)
is a Poisson
subfield of

isomorphic to
K\{\lambda\mu xy\}=K_{\lambda\mu}
.

Proof.

(1) Apply Lemma
1.6
with
r(t)=t^{m}
.

(2) Take
m=0
and apply part (1) together with Proposition
1.1
(1).

(3) Take

and apply part (1).
∎

Remarks 1.8
.

In particular, Corollary

2.
Logarithmic derivatives

Various calculations with Poisson brackets
involve terms which are formal logarithmic
derivatives –
f^{\prime}(x)/f(x)
or
g^{\prime}(y)/g(y)

for
f\in\Bbbk(x)^{\times}
or
g\in\Bbbk(y)^{\times}
. To deal with these, we
develop some elementary calculus facts
about logarithmic derivatives.

Throughout this section,
F
stands for a
general field of characteristic zero. As
noted above, for
s(t)
in a rational
function field
F(t)
, we write
s^{\prime}(t)

for
ds/dt
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
F(t)\subseteq\overline{F}(t)
be
rational function fields over
F
and
over its algebraic closure
\overline{F}
.

(1)

If
s\in F(t)^{\times}
, then

(E2.1.1)

\dfrac{s^{\prime}}{s}=\frac{z_{1}}{t-a_{1}}+\cdots+\frac{z_{m}}{t-a_{m}}

in
\overline{F}(t)
, for some
z_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}

and some distinct
a_{i}\in\overline{F}
.

(2)

If
s\in F(t)^{\times}
and

(E2.1.2)

\dfrac{s^{\prime}}{s}=\frac{c_{1}}{(t-b_{1})^{r_{1}}}+\cdots+\frac{c_{n}}{(t-b_{n})^{r_{n}}}

in
F(t)
, for some
c_{j}\in F^{\times}
,
some distinct
b_{j}\in F
, and some

r_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, then
c_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}

and
r_{j}=1
for all
j=1,\dots,n
.
Moreover,

s=\alpha(t-b_{1})^{c_{1}}\cdots(t-b_{n})^{c_{n}}

for some
\alpha\in F^{\times}
.

Proof.

(1) Write
s=f/g
for some nonzero relatively
prime
f,g\in F[t]
. Without loss of
generality,
g
is monic. Let
a_{1},\dots,a_{n}

be the distinct roots of
f
in
\overline{F}
,
and let
a_{n+1},\dots,a_{m}
be the distinct
roots of
g
in
\overline{F}
. Since
f

and
g
are relatively prime,

a_{1},\dots,a_{n},\dots,a_{m}
are all
distinct. Write

f=\alpha(t-a_{1})^{z_{1}}\cdots(t-a_{n})^{z_{n}}

for some

\alpha\in F^{\times}
and
z_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
,
and write

g=(t-a_{n+1})^{-z_{n+1}}\cdots(t-a_{m})^{-z_{m}}
for some
z_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{<0}
.
Then

s=\alpha(t-a_{1})^{z_{1}}\cdots(t-a_{n})^{z_{n}}(t-a_{n+1})^{z_{n+1}}\cdots(t-a_{m})^{z_{m}}\,.

Since
(\alpha^{-1}s)^{\prime}/(\alpha^{-1}s)=s^{\prime}/s
,
(
E2.1.1
) follows by a direct computation.

(2) By comparing (
E2.1.1
) and
(
E2.1.2
)
in
\overline{F}(t)
, one sees that
r_{i}=1

for all
i
and that the set
\{a_{i}\}
is
equal to the set
\{b_{j}\}
. As a consequence,

m=n
and
c_{i}=z_{i}
after we set
a_{i}=b_{i}

for all
1\leq i\leq m=n
. Now let

u:=(t-b_{1})^{c_{1}}\cdots(t-b_{n})^{c_{n}}
and
observe that
u^{\prime}/u=s^{\prime}/s
. Then
(s/u)^{\prime}=0
,
and therefore
s/u\in F^{\times}
.
∎

Corollary 2.2
.

Let
\gamma\in F^{\times}
and

f(t)=\gamma(t-a_{1})\cdots(t-a_{n})\in F[t]

for some
a_{i}\in F
.
Then
\frac{1}{f}=\frac{s^{\prime}}{s}
for some
s\in F(t)^{\times}

if and only if
n>0
, the
a_{i}
are distinct, and

(E2.2.1)

\gamma^{-1}\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\\
j\neq i\end{subarray}}^{n}(a_{i}-a_{j})^{-1}\in\mathbb{Z}\qquad\forall\;i=1,\dots,n.

Proof.

Let
F(t)\subseteq\overline{F}(t)
as in Lemma

2.1
.

(\Longrightarrow)
: By Lemma
2.1
(1),

(E2.2.2)

\frac{1}{f}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}\frac{z_{j}}{t-b_{j}}

for some
z_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}
and some distinct
b_{j}\in\overline{F}
. We may assume that all
z_{j}\neq 0
.
Set
g(t):=\prod_{j=1}^{m}(t-b_{j})
and

g_{j}(t):=\frac{g(t)}{t-b_{j}}
for
j\in[1,m]
.
Multiply (
E2.2.2
) by
fg
to get

(E2.2.3)

g=f\sum_{j=1}^{m}z_{j}g_{j}\,.

Consequently,
f\mid g
in
\overline{F}[t]
. On the
other hand, if we substitute
t=b_{i}
in (
E2.2.3
)
for some
i\in[1,m]
, we find that

0=g(b_{i})=f(b_{i})z_{i}\prod_{j\neq i}(b_{i}-b_{j}),

whence
f(b_{i})=0
. Therefore
n=m
and, after
reindexing,
b_{i}=a_{i}
for all
i
, whence
f=\gamma g
.
In particular,
n>0
and the
a_{i}
are distinct. Now
divide (
E2.2.3
) by
f
, to get

\gamma^{-1}=\sum_{j=1}^{m}z_{j}g_{j}\,.

Substituting
t=a_{i}
, we get

\gamma^{-1}=z_{i}\prod_{j\neq i}(a_{i}-a_{j})
, establishing
(
E2.2.1
).

(\Longleftarrow)
: Set

z_{i}:=\gamma^{-1}\prod_{j\neq i}(a_{i}-a_{j})^{-1}\in\mathbb{Z}

for
i\in[1,n]
and

h(t):=\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}\frac{f}{t-a_{i}}\in F[t]
. Note
that
\deg h<n
. For
i\in[1,n]
, we have

h(a_{i})=z_{i}\gamma\prod_{j\neq i}(a_{i}-a_{j})=1.

Thus, the polynomial
h(t)-1
has
n
distinct roots, so it
must be zero. Therefore

\frac{1}{f}=\frac{h}{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{z_{i}}{t-a_{i}}=\frac{s^{\prime}}{s}\,,

where
s:=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(t-a_{i})^{z_{i}}
.
∎

Remark 2.3
.

In the linear case of Corollary
2.2
, that
is,
f(t)=\gamma(t-a_{1})
, the only requirement is that

\gamma^{-1}\in\mathbb{Z}
. In the quadratic case, the two
instances of (
E2.2.1
) are negatives of each other,
so the requirements are
a_{1}\neq a_{2}
and

\gamma^{-1}(a_{1}-a_{2})^{-1}\in\mathbb{Z}
. Given
a_{1}\neq a_{2}
,
one can always choose
\gamma
to satisfy the second condition.

In higher degrees, the
a_{i}
must be linearly dependent
over
\mathbb{Q}
, as follows. We claim that if the conditions
in the corollary hold and
n\geq 3
, then the nonzero
integers
z_{i}:=\gamma^{-1}\prod_{j\neq i}(a_{i}-a_{j})^{-1}

must satisfy

(E2.3.1)

z_{1}+\cdots+z_{n}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad z_{1}a_{1}+\cdots+z_{n}a_{n}=0.

As in the proof of Corollary
2.2
, we have

\frac{1}{f}=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{z_{i}}{t-a_{i}}\,,

and consequently

\sum_{i=1}^{n}z_{i}\prod_{j\neq i}(t-a_{j})=\gamma^{-1}.

Since the coefficients of
t^{n-1}
and
t^{n-2}
on the
left side of this equation must be zero, we find that

\sum_{i}z_{i}=0
and
\sum_{i}z_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}a_{j}=0
. But

\sum_{i}z_{i}\sum_{j\neq i}a_{j}=\sum_{j}\biggl(\sum_{i\neq j}z_{i}\biggr)a_{j}=-\sum_{j}z_{j}a_{j}\,,

and therefore
\sum_{j}z_{j}a_{j}=0
.

For an example, suppose
a_{1}=0,a_{2},a_{3}
are distinct elements
of
\Bbbk
with
a_{2}/a_{3}\in\mathbb{Q}
. There are nonzero integers

z_{2}
,
z_{3}
such that
z_{2}a_{2}+z_{3}a_{3}=0
. Set

z_{1}:=-(z_{2}+z_{3})
, so that (
E2.3.1
) holds, and set

s(t):=t^{z_{1}}(t-a_{2})^{z_{2}}(t-a_{3})^{z_{3}}
. In this case,

\frac{s^{\prime}}{s}=\frac{1}{f}
where
f(t)=(z_{1}a_{2}a_{3})^{-1}t(t-a_{2})(t-a_{3})
.
(Cf. Remarks
1.8
and
5.4

for an application.)

Definition 3.1

Recall that a

, then

\vht_{n}(K)\leq\vht_{1}(K)\leq\fht(K)

for every
n\geq 1
.

Proof.

(1) Suppose that
\vht_{n}(K)<2
. Then there is an integer
w<0

such that
{\mathcal{V}}_{nc,n,w}(K)\neq\emptyset
, namely,
there is some
\nu\in{\mathcal{V}}_{nc,w}(K)
such that

F^{\nu}_{0}(K)\cap{{}^{n}\Gamma}_{0}(K)=\Bbbk
. For every
positive integer
d
, we have
d\nu\in{\mathcal{V}}_{nc,dw}(K)
.
Note that
F^{d\nu}_{0}(K)=F^{\nu}_{0}(K)
by definition. Then

F^{d\nu}_{0}(K)\cap{{}^{n}\Gamma}_{0}(K)=\Bbbk
. This means
that
{\mathcal{V}}_{nc,n,dw}(K)\neq\emptyset
, and so
\vht_{n}(K)\leq 2+dw
. Consequently,
\vht_{n}(K)=-\infty
.

(2) If
\vht_{n}(K^{\prime})=+\infty
, nothing needs to be proved. Now assume
that
\vht_{n}(K^{\prime})<+\infty
, and let
w\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
{\mathcal{V}}_{nc,n,w}(K^{\prime})\neq\emptyset
. Then there is a non-classical

w
-valuation
\nu
of
K^{\prime}
such that

F^{\nu}_{0}(K^{\prime})\cap{{}^{n}\Gamma}_{0}(K^{\prime})=\Bbbk
. By restriction,

\mu:=\nu|_{K}
is a
w
-valuation
of
K
(possibly trivial), and there is some
l\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
such that
\mu
is a non-classical
(w-l)
-valuation. By Lemma
3.7
(1),
{{}^{n}\Gamma}_{0}(K)\subseteq{{}^{n}\Gamma}_{0}(K^{\prime})
, and by definition,
F^{\mu}_{0}(K)\subseteq F^{\nu}_{0}(K^{\prime})
. Consequently,
F^{\mu}_{0}(K)\cap{{}^{n}\Gamma}_{0}(K)=\Bbbk
. Therefore
{\mathcal{V}}_{nc,n,w-l}(K)\neq\emptyset
and
\vht_{n}(K)\leq 2+w-l\leq 2+w
.

(3) follows from the definition and Lemma
3.7
(2).

(4) If
\fht(K)=+\infty
, nothing needs to be proved.
Now assume that
d:=\fht(K)
is finite. We may thus assume that
\deg{\mathbf{f}}=d
. Let
\nu
be the discrete valuation on
K
from Lemma
3.4
with
\nu(x)=\nu(y)=-1
. We claim that
\nu
is a non-classical
(d-2)
-valuation.
This follows from the fact that

\nu(\{x,y\})=\nu({\mathbf{f}})=-d=-2-(d-2)=\nu(x)+\nu(y)-(d-2).

Moreover, Lemma
3.4
shows
that
\nu<0
on the set
\Bbbk[x,y]\setminus\Bbbk
, which implies that
F^{\nu}_{0}(K)\cap{{}^{1}\Gamma}_{0}(K)\subseteq F^{\nu}_{0}(K)\cap\Bbbk[x,y]=\Bbbk
. Therefore

\nu\in{\mathcal{V}}_{nc,1,d-2}(K)
.
Hence

d-2\geq\inf\{w\;\mid\;{\mathcal{V}}_{nc,1,w}(K)\neq\emptyset\}.

By definition,
d=2+(d-2)\geq\vht_{1}(K)
.
∎

Lemma 3.15
.

Let
K
be a Poisson field of the form
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
, and suppose that
\vht_{n}(K)=\fht(K)
for some positive integer
n
. Then
K
is height cohereditary.

Proof.

Let
K^{\prime}:=K\{{\mathbf{g}}\}
and
\phi:K\to K^{\prime}
an embedding.
By Lemma
3.14
(2)(4),
we have

\fht(K)=\vht_{n}(K)\leq\vht_{n}(K^{\prime})\leq\vht_{1}(K^{\prime})\leq\fht(K^{\prime}).

Therefore
K
is height cohereditary.
∎

Next we give a lower bound for valuation height.

Lemma 3.16
.

Let
K
be a Poisson field with
h\in K\setminus\Bbbk

and let
f(t)
be a Laurent polynomial

a_{l}t^{l}+a_{l+1}t^{l+1}+\cdots+a_{n}t^{n}
with

l\leq n
,
a_{i}\in\Bbbk
, and
a_{l},a_{n}\neq 0
.
Assume
n\geq 2
.
Suppose
u,v
are nonzero elements in
K
such that

\{u,v\}=uvf(h)
. Then
\vht_{n-1}(K)\geq 2+n
.

Proof.

By Corollary
3.11
,

h\in{{}^{n-1}\Gamma}_{0}(K)
. If
w
is an integer and

\nu\in{\mathcal{V}}_{nc,n-1,w}(K)
, then

\nu(h)<0
because
h\notin\Bbbk
. Then, using Lemma
3.10
(1),

\nu(u)+\nu(v)-w\leq\nu(uvf(h))=\nu(u)+\nu(v)+\nu(f(h))=\nu(u)+\nu(v)+n\nu(h)

which implies that
w\geq n(-\nu(h))\geq n
. Therefore

\vht_{n-1}(K)\geq 2+n
.
∎

In this paper, we will consider elements with the
following property.

Definition 3.17
.

Let
d
be a positive integer.
A polynomial
{\mathbf{g}}\in\Bbbk[x,y]
is called
d
-flabby
if

{{}^{d}\Gamma}_{0}(K\{{\mathbf{g}}\})=\Bbbk[x,y]
. If
{\mathbf{g}}
is
d
-flabby for some unspecified
d>0
, we just say that
{\mathbf{g}}
is
flabby
.

Proposition 3.18
.

Let
{\mathbf{f}}=\sum_{i=0}^{m}\sum_{j=0}^{n}c_{ij}x^{i}y^{j}
with
c_{ij}\in\Bbbk
and

c_{mn}\neq 0
, where
m\geq 1
,
n\geq 1
. Let
K
be

K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
. If
{\mathbf{f}}
is
d
-flabby for some
d\geq 1
, then

\vht_{d}(K)=\fht(K)=m+n
.
As a consequence,
K
is height cohereditary.

Proof.

By Lemma
3.14
(4),
\vht_{d}(K)\leq\fht(K)\leq m+n
. It remains to show
that
\vht_{d}(K)\geq m+n
. This is automatic if
\vht_{d}(K)=+\infty
, so assume
\vht_{d}(K)<+\infty
.

Let
w
be an integer such that
{\mathcal{V}}_{nc,d,w}(K)\neq\emptyset
. So there is a valuation

\nu\in{\mathcal{V}}_{nc,w}(K)
such that

F^{\nu}_{0}(K)\cap{{}^{d}\Gamma}_{0}(K)=\Bbbk
.
Then
\nu(x)<0
and
\nu(y)<0
, which implies
that
\nu({\mathbf{f}})=m\nu(x)+n\nu(y)
. Now the
equation
\{x,y\}={\mathbf{f}}
implies that

\nu(x)+\nu(y)-w\leq\nu(\{x,y\})=\nu({\mathbf{f}})=m\nu(x)+n\nu(y).

An easy computation shows that

2+w\geq 2+(m-1)(-\nu(x))+(n-1)(-\nu(y))\geq 2+m-1+n-1=m+n,

which implies that
\vht_{d}(K)\geq m+n
.

Therefore
\vht_{d}(K)=\fht(K)=m+n
. By Lemma
3.15
,
K
is height cohereditary.
∎

We now begin work on the families of Poisson
fields
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
described in the introduction, and study the Automorphism, Isomorphism, Embedding, and Dixmier Problems. One key tool is the
\gamma
-invariants
that were introduced in the last section.

Recall from Proposition
1.1
(1) that if
{\mathbf{f}}\in\Bbbk(x)^{\times}
or
{\mathbf{f}}\in\Bbbk(y)^{\times}
, then
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}\cong K_{\Weyl}
. So everything is
reduced to the Poisson Weyl field in those cases. The first larger family
we will consider is the following.

4.
Family (1):
K\{qx^{a}y^{b}\}

In this section, we consider the Poisson fields

K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
where
{\mathbf{f}}
is of the form
qx^{a}y^{b}
with
q\in\Bbbk^{\times}
and
a,b\in\mathbb{Z}
. It is convenient to
rewrite
{\mathbf{f}}
as
qx^{1+\kappa_{1}}y^{1+\kappa_{2}}

with
q\in\Bbbk^{\times}
and
\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}\in{\mathbb{Z}}
, and to relabel
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
as
K_{q,\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}}
or
K_{q,\kappa}
where
\kappa:=(\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2})
. Thus,
K_{q}=K_{q,0,0}
and
K_{\Weyl}=K_{1,-1,-1}
.

Note that
K_{q,\kappa}=K_{q,\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}}
is the quotient field of the
Poisson torus
T_{q,\kappa}=T_{q,\kappa_{1},\kappa_{2}}
, which is the Laurent polynomial ring

\Bbbk[x^{\pm 1},y^{\pm 1}]
with
Poisson structure determined by
\{x,y\}=qx^{1+\kappa_{1}}y^{1+\kappa_{2}}
.

Lemma 4.1
.

Retain the above notation, and let
q,q^{\prime}\in\Bbbk^{\times}
and
\kappa,\kappa^{\prime}\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}\setminus\{(0,0)\}
. For parts
(3)–(5)
, assume that
\Bbbk
is algebraically closed.

(1)

If there exist
\alpha,\beta\in\Bbbk^{\times}
such that
\alpha^{\kappa_{1}}\beta^{\kappa_{2}}=q
, then

T_{q,\kappa}\cong T_{1,\kappa}
and
K_{q,\kappa}\cong K_{1,\kappa}
.

(2)

T_{q,\kappa}\cong T_{q,\kappa_{0},0}
and
K_{q,\kappa}\cong K_{q,\kappa_{0},0}
where
\kappa_{0}:=\gcd(\kappa)
.
In particular,
T_{q,\kappa_{0},0}\cong T_{q,\kappa_{0},d\kappa_{0}}
and
K_{q,\kappa_{0},0}\cong K_{q,\kappa_{0},d\kappa_{0}}

for all
d\in{\mathbb{Z}}
.

(3)

Let
\kappa_{0}
and
\kappa^{\prime}_{0}
be nonzero integers. Then

T_{q,\kappa_{0},0}\cong T_{q^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime}_{0},0}

if and only if
\kappa_{0}=\pm\kappa^{\prime}_{0}
. In particular, if
\kappa_{0}=\pm\kappa^{\prime}_{0}
then
K_{q,\kappa_{0},0}\cong K_{q^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime}_{0},0}
.

(4)

T_{q,\kappa}\cong T_{q^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime}}
if and only if

\gcd(\kappa^{\prime})=\gcd(\kappa)
. In particular, if
\gcd(\kappa^{\prime})=\gcd(\kappa)
then
K_{q,\kappa}\cong K_{q^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime}}
.

(5)

There are Poisson algebra embeddings
T_{1,\kappa}\to T_{1,d\kappa}
and
K_{1,\kappa}\to K_{1,d\kappa}
for all nonzero integers
d
.

(6)

T_{q,\kappa}
is Poisson-simple, meaning that it has no proper nonzero Poisson ideals.

Proof.

Since the statements about Poisson fields follow immediately from those about Poisson tori, we just need to prove the latter.

(1) This is clear since
\Bbbk[x^{\pm 1},y^{\pm 1}]=\Bbbk[(\alpha x)^{\pm 1},(\beta y)^{\pm 1}]
and

(\alpha x)^{1+\kappa_{1}}(\beta y)^{1+\kappa_{2}}=\alpha\beta qx^{1+\kappa_{1}}y^{1+\kappa_{2}}=\{\alpha x,\beta y\}.

(2) Let
(a,b):=\kappa/\kappa_{0}
, so that
\gcd(a,b)=1
and there are
c,d\in\mathbb{Z}
such that
ad-bc=1
. Setting
\hat{x}:=x^{a}y^{b}
and
\hat{y}:=x^{c}y^{d}
, we see that
\hat{x}^{d}\hat{y}^{-b}=x
and
\hat{x}^{-c}\hat{y}^{a}=y
, so that
\Bbbk[x^{\pm 1},y^{\pm 1}]=\Bbbk[\hat{x}^{\pm 1},\hat{y}^{\pm 1}]
. Moreover, using Lemma
9.1
we have

\{\hat{x},\hat{y}\}=x^{a+c-1}y^{b+d-1}qx^{1+a\kappa_{0}}y^{1+b\kappa_{0}}=qx^{a(1+\kappa_{0})+c}y^{b(1+\kappa_{0})+d}=q\hat{x}^{1+\kappa_{0}}\hat{y}.

This establishes the first isomorphism, and then the second follows.

(3) If
\kappa_{0}=-\kappa^{\prime}_{0}
, we obtain
T_{1,\kappa_{0},0}\cong T_{-1,\kappa^{\prime}_{0},0}
from the observation that

\{x^{-1},y\}=-x^{-2}x^{1+\kappa_{0}}y=-x^{-1+\kappa_{0}}y=-(x^{-1})^{1+\kappa^{\prime}_{0}}y

in
T_{1,\kappa_{0},0}
. Consequently,
T_{q,\kappa_{0},0}\cong T_{q^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime}_{0},0}
via part (1).

Conversely, if
T_{q,\kappa_{0},0}\cong T_{q^{\prime},\kappa^{\prime}_{0},0}
, then
T_{1,\kappa_{0},0}\cong T_{1,\kappa^{\prime}_{0},0}
by (1), and so there exist
u,v\in T_{1,\kappa_{0},0}
such that
\Bbbk[x^{\pm 1},y^{\pm 1}]=\Bbbk[u^{\pm 1},v^{\pm 1}]
and
\{u,v\}=u^{1+\kappa^{\prime}_{0}}v
. We must have
u=\lambda x^{a}y^{b}
and
v=\mu x^{c}y^{d}
for some
\lambda,\mu\in\Bbbk^{\times}
and
\left(\begin{smallmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{smallmatrix}\right)\in GL_{2}(\mathbb{Z})
. Then

\lambda^{1+\kappa^{\prime}_{0}}\mu(x^{a}y^{b})^{1+\kappa^{\prime}_{0}}(x^{c}y^{d})=\{u,v\}=\lambda\mu(ad-bc)x^{a+c-1}y^{b+d-1}x^{1+\kappa_{0}}y,

whence
a(1+\kappa^{\prime}_{0})+c=a+c+\kappa_{0}
and
b(1+\kappa^{\prime}_{0})+d=b+d
. But then
b\kappa^{\prime}_{0}=0
and so
b=0
. Thus
a=\pm 1
and
\kappa_{0}=a\kappa^{\prime}_{0}=\pm\kappa^{\prime}_{0}
.

(4) follows from (2) and (3).

(5) In view of (2), we may assume that
\kappa=(\kappa_{0},0)
. View
T_{1,d\kappa}
as
\Bbbk[x^{\pm 1},y^{\pm 1}]
with
\{x,y\}=x^{1+d\kappa_{0}}y
. Set
u:=x^{d}
and observe that

\{u,y\}=dx^{d-1}x^{1+d\kappa_{0}}y=dx^{d(1+\kappa_{0})}y=du^{1+\kappa_{0}}y\,.

Thus
\Bbbk[u^{\pm 1},y^{\pm 1}]\cong T_{d,\kappa}\cong T_{1,\kappa}
.

(6) Suppose there is a proper nonzero Poisson ideal
I
in
T_{q,\kappa}
. Any prime ideal
P
minimal over
I
is a Poisson ideal (e.g.,
[
4
, Lemma 1.1(c)]
), so the quotient field of
T_{q,\kappa}/P
is a Poisson field. Since
P
is nonzero, the cosets of
x
and
y
in
T_{q,\kappa}/P
cannot be algebraically independent over
\Bbbk
, and hence the Poisson bracket of these cosets is zero (Observation
1.4
). But this means
\{x,y\}\in P
, which is impossible because
\{x,y\}
is invertible in
T_{q,\kappa}
. Therefore
T_{q,\kappa}
is Poisson-simple.
∎

Corollary 4.2
.

Assume that
\Bbbk
is algebraically closed,
q\in\Bbbk^{\times}
, and
\kappa\in\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

(1)

K_{q,\kappa}

is isomorphic to either
K_{q}
, or
K_{\Weyl}
, or
K_{1,n,0}

for some

.

(2)

K_{q,\kappa}
has a Poisson subfield isomorphic to

or
K_{\Weyl}
.

Proof.

(1) follows directly from Lemma
4.1
.

(2) By part (1), it is enough to deal with

. This has a Poisson subfield isomorphic to

.

Let

(1)

{{}^{w}\Gamma}_{0}(K)=\Bbbk

Proof.

(1)

. By Corollary

Case 2:

Let

(1)

(2)

Let

(3)

\vht_{1}(K)=\fht(K)=n+2
.

Proof.

(1) By definition,

is a Poisson subalgebra
of
K
such that

. By

{{}^{1}\Gamma_{0}}(K)\subseteq A

(with

(2) It suffices to show that

Proposition 4.8

(Automorphism Problem)

Let

\Bbbk=\overline{\Bbbk}

(1)

Then

(2)

If

(1) Let
G:=\langle\eta_{a,b},\,\tau_{c}\rangle

\Aut_{Poi}(K)
generated by the

and
\tau_{c}

Let
\phi

be an automorphism of
K

{{}^{1}\Gamma}_{0}(K)

(1)

for some

and

Then, with

\displaystyle=c\{x,f\}=cf_{y}\{x,y\}=cf_{y}x^{1+n}y\,,

2.1

\frac{1}{c}\left(\frac{cx+d}{x}\right)^{1+n}=:z\in{\mathbb{Z}},\quad{\text{and}}\quad f=g(x)y^{z}

\begin{aligned} \eta_{a_{1},b_{1}}\eta_{a_{2},b_{2}}&=\eta_{a_{1}a_{2},b_{2}(a_{1}x)b_{1}}\\

(2) If
n
is odd,
\tau_{-1}
is defined and

The Embedding and Dixmier Problems can also be solved using the

\gamma

Proposition 4.9
.

Let
q,r\in\Bbbk^{\times}
and consider
K_{r,m,0}
and
K_{q,n,0}
for
m,n\geq 2
.

(1)

(Embedding Problem)

There is an embedding from
K_{r,m,0}
into
K_{q,n,0}
if and only
if
m\mid n
and there exist
0\neq z\in\mathbb{Z}
,
\beta\in\Bbbk^{\times}
with
\beta^{m}=nqz/mr
.

(2)

The endomorphisms of
K_{q,n,0}
are exactly those

\Bbbk
-algebra endomorphisms
\phi
of
\Bbbk(x,y)
such that

\displaystyle\phi(x)

\displaystyle=cx,\quad c\in\Bbbk^{\times},\quad c^{n}=z\in\mathbb{Z}\setminus\{0\},

\displaystyle\phi(y)

\displaystyle=h(x)y^{z},\quad h(x)\in\Bbbk(x)^{\times}.

Consequently, every endomorphism of

has image
equal to
\Bbbk(x,y^{z})
for some
0\neq z\in{\mathbb{Z}}

(3)

(Dixmier Problem)

K_{q,n,0}
does not have

Proof.

(1) First assume that
m\mid n
and there exist
z
,
\beta
as stated. Set

, and note that

\displaystyle=\beta dx^{d-1}zy^{z-1}\{x,y\}=\beta dx^{d-1}zy^{z-1}qx^{1+n}y

\displaystyle=\frac{\beta nqz}{m}\,x^{d+n}y^{z}=\beta^{m+1}rx^{d(m+1)}y^{z}=r(\beta x^{d})^{m+1}(y^{z}),

and so
\Bbbk(x^{d},y^{z})
is a Poisson subfield isomorphic to
K_{r,m,0}
.

Conversely, assume that there is a Poisson algebra
morphism
\phi:K_{r,m,0}\to K_{q,n,0}
for some
m,n\geq 2
.
Let us use
y_{1},y_{2}
as the generators of
K_{q,m,0}

satisfying
\{y_{1},y_{2}\}=ry_{1}^{1+m}y_{2}

3.7
(1),
\phi

induces an injective map from

Thus
g(x):=\phi(y_{1})
is a non-constant polynomial.
Let

. Then we have

\displaystyle rg(x)^{1+m}f

\displaystyle=\phi(ry_{1}^{1+m}y_{2})=\phi(\{y_{1},y_{2}\})=\{\phi(y_{1}),\phi(y_{2})\}

\displaystyle=\{g(x),f\}=g^{\prime}(x)f_{y}\{x,y\}=g^{\prime}(x)f_{y}qx^{1+n}y\,.

Thus

\frac{f_{y}}{f}=\frac{rg(x)^{1+m}}{qg^{\prime}(x)x^{1+n}}\,\frac{1}{y}.

By Lemma
2.1
,

(E4.9.2)

\frac{rg(x)^{1+m}}{qg^{\prime}(x)x^{1+n}}=:z\in{\mathbb{Z}}\quad{\text{and}}\quad f=h(x)y^{z}

for some

we obtain that

.
Therefore

Write

with

\gamma_{i}\in\Bbbk

\gamma_{k}\neq 0

z\neq 0

(2) It is easy to check that if
c\in\Bbbk^{\times}
with

then the
\Bbbk

) is an

Conversely, let
\phi
be an arbitrary Poisson endomorphism
of

) and

(3) For instance, if

In
[
12
, §13.6]
, Stafford and van den Bergh showed that
D_{q}(\Bbbk)
, the division ring of the quantum plane
\Bbbk\langle x,y\mid xy=qyx\rangle
, is isomorphic to a proper sub-division algebra of itself. A Poisson version of this example shows that
K_{q}
fails to satisfy the Dixmier property, as follows.

Example 4.11
.

Let
K=K_{q}
for some
q\in\Bbbk
. We first construct special elements
f,g\in K
such that
\{g,f\}_{w}=gf(xy)^{-1}
. Namely, set
s:=(y-y^{-1})^{-1}
and take

\displaystyle f

\displaystyle:=s(xy-x^{-1}y^{-1})=(y-y^{-1})^{-1}(xy-x^{-1}y^{-1}),

\displaystyle g

\displaystyle:=s(x-x^{-1})=(y-y^{-1})^{-1}(x-x^{-1}).

It is clear that
s_{x}=0
and
s_{y}=-s^{2}(1+y^{-2})
. Then

\displaystyle g_{x}

\displaystyle=s(1+x^{-2}),

\displaystyle g_{y}

\displaystyle=-s^{2}(1+y^{-2})(x-x^{-1})=s^{2}(1+y^{-2})(x^{-1}-x).

Similarly, we have

\displaystyle f_{x}

\displaystyle=s(y+x^{-2}y^{-1}),

\displaystyle f_{y}

\displaystyle=s_{y}(xy-x^{-1}y^{-1})+s(x+x^{-1}y^{-2})

\displaystyle=-s^{2}(1+y^{-2})(xy-x^{-1}y^{-1})+s^{2}(y-y^{-1})(x+x^{-1}y^{-2})

\displaystyle=s^{2}[(-xy+x^{-1}y^{-1}-xy^{-1}+x^{-1}y^{-3})

\displaystyle+(xy+x^{-1}y^{-1}-xy^{-1}-x^{-1}y^{-3})]

\displaystyle=2s^{2}[x^{-1}y^{-1}-xy^{-1}]=2s^{2}(x^{-1}-x)y^{-1}.

Now we are ready to compute

\displaystyle\{f,g\}_{w}

\displaystyle=f_{x}g_{y}-f_{y}g_{x}

\displaystyle=s(y+x^{-2}y^{-1})s^{2}(1+y^{-2})(x^{-1}-x)-2s^{2}(x^{-1}-x)y^{-1}s(1+x^{-2})

\displaystyle=s^{3}(x^{-1}-x)[(y+x^{-2}y^{-1})(1+y^{-2})-2y^{-1}(1+x^{-2})]

\displaystyle=s^{3}(x^{-1}-x)[y+x^{-2}y^{-1}+y^{-1}+x^{-2}y^{-3}-2y^{-1}-2y^{-1}x^{-2}]

\displaystyle=s^{3}(x^{-1}-x)[y-x^{-2}y^{-1}-y^{-1}+x^{-2}y^{-3}]

\displaystyle=s^{3}(x^{-1}-x)(y-x^{-2}y^{-1})(1-y^{-2})

\displaystyle=s^{3}(x^{-1}-x)(xy-x^{-1}y^{-1})x^{-1}(y-y^{-1})y^{-1}

\displaystyle=s(xy-x^{-1}y^{-1})s(x-x^{-1})(-x^{-1}y^{-1})=fg(-x^{-1}y^{-1}).

Thus
\{g,f\}_{w}=gf(xy)^{-1}
, as announced. Consequently,

\{g,f\}=\{g,f\}_{w}\,qxy=qgf,

which implies that
\Bbbk(f,g)
is a Poisson subfield of
K
isomorphic to
K
.

Note that
K
has a (Poisson)
automorphism
\sigma
such that
x\mapsto x^{-1}
and
y\mapsto y^{-1}
. It is clear that
both
f
and
g
lie in the fixed field
\Bbbk(x,y)^{\sigma}
. Therefore
\Bbbk(f,g)\subseteq K^{\sigma}\subsetneq K
, showing that
K
fails to satisfy the Dixmier property.

. We say
{\mathbf{f}}

has distinct linear forms
if
l_{i}\not\in{\Bbbk^{\times}}l_{j}

for all
i\neq j
, which is equivalent to saying that
{\mathbf{f}}
does
not have a square divisor of positive degree.

By Lemma
6.1
, many homogeneous polynomials in
\Bbbk[x,y]
of degree 3 or
less are not
1
-flabby.
If
{\mathbf{f}}
is of the form
x^{2}\prod_{i=1}^{n}l_{i}
,
then one can also check that
{\mathbf{f}}
is not
1
-flabby
(by constructing a
1
-valuation
\nu
via Lemma
3.4

with
\nu(y)=-1
and
\nu(x)\gg 0
).
On the other hand, polynomials of degree 4 or more are likely to be
1
-flabby under
some generic conditions. Below is one case.

Lemma 6.2
.

Let
{\mathbf{f}}\in\Bbbk[x,y]
be a product

\prod_{i=1}^{n}l_{i}
of
distinct linear forms, where
l_{i}=a_{i}x+b_{i}y+c_{i}\not\in\Bbbk
.
Suppose

(E6.2.1)

for each
i
, there are at least three
j

such that
l_{i}
and
l_{j}
generate
\Bbbk[x,y]
.

Then
{{}^{1}\Gamma_{0}}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})=\Bbbk[x,y]
, i.e.,
{\mathbf{f}}
is
1
-flabby. This holds in particular if
{\mathbf{f}}
is a homogeneous polynomial of degree
n\geq 4
and
{\mathbf{f}}
has distinct linear forms.

As a consequence,
\vht_{1}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})=\fht(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})=\deg{\mathbf{f}}
and
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
is height
cohereditary.

Proof.

Note that (
E6.2.1
) implies
n\geq 4
.
By Corollary
3.9
,

{{}^{1}\Gamma}_{0}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})\subseteq\Bbbk[x,y]
. Since

\sum_{i}\Bbbk l_{i}
generates
\Bbbk[x,y]
, it suffices to show
that each
l_{i}\in{{}^{1}\Gamma}_{0}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})
. By definition,
it remains to show that
\nu(l_{i})\geq 0
for all
i
and
for all
1
-valuations
\nu
of
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
.

Let
\nu
be any
1
-valuation of
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
. Set

\alpha:=\min(\nu(l_{i})\mid i\in[1,n])\qquad\text{and}\qquad\beta:=\max(\nu(l_{i})\mid i\in[1,n]).

Then
both
\alpha
and
\beta
are finite. If
\alpha\geq 0
,
then
\nu(l_{i})\geq\alpha\geq 0
for all
i
, as required.
It remains to consider the situation when
\alpha<0
.

Case 1:
\alpha=\beta
. Then
\nu(l_{i})=\alpha
for all

i
. After possible renumbering,
l_{1}
and
l_{2}
generate
\Bbbk[x,y]
. By an affine change of basis

x\to l_{1}
,
y\to l_{2}
, we can assume that

l_{1}=x
and
l_{2}=y
. Then

n\alpha=\nu({\mathbf{f}})=\nu(\{x,y\})\geq\nu(x)+\nu(y)-1=2\alpha-1\,.

Thus,
(n-2)\alpha\geq-1
.
Since
n\geq 4
and
\alpha
is an integer, we have

\alpha\geq 0
, yielding a contradiction.

Case 2:
\alpha<\beta
.
Without loss of generality,
\alpha=\nu(l_{1})

and
\beta=\nu(l_{2})
. Then
l_{1}
is not of the form

al_{2}+b
for any
a,b\in\Bbbk
. So
l_{1}
and
l_{2}

generate
\Bbbk[x,y]
. After an affine change of generators, we may assume that
l_{1}=x
and
l_{2}=y
.

Subcase 2.1:
\beta>0
.
For each
i\geq 3
,
l_{i}=a_{i}x+b_{i}y+c_{i}
which
has
\nu
-value
\alpha
if
a_{i}\neq 0
and
0

if
a_{i}=0
(since
l_{i}\neq b_{i}y
when
i\geq 3
). By hypothesis (
E6.2.1
), there are
n^{\prime}\geq 3

factors
l_{j}
(including
l_{1}
) such that
l_{j}
and
l_{2}

generate
\Bbbk[x,y]
. Such
l_{j}=a_{j}x+b_{j}y+c_{j}
where
a_{j}\neq 0
, and thus
\nu(l_{j})=\alpha
. For all other
i\neq 2,j
, we have
l_{i}=b_{i}y+c_{i}

(with
b_{i},c_{i}\neq 0
) and consequently,
\nu(l_{i})=0
. Now

n^{\prime}\alpha+\beta=\nu({\mathbf{f}})=\nu(\{x,y\})\geq\alpha+\beta-1,

which implies that
(n^{\prime}-1)\alpha\geq-1
, yielding a contradiction.

Subcase 2.2:
\beta\leq 0
.
For each
i\geq 3
,
l_{i}=a_{i}x+b_{i}y+c_{i}
which
has
\nu
-value
\alpha
if
a_{i}\neq 0
and
\nu
-value
\geq\min(\beta,0)=\beta

if
a_{i}=0
. Thus
\nu(l_{i})=\beta
when
a_{i}=0
, by definition of
\beta
.

Suppose there are
n^{\prime}
-many

l_{i}
with
\nu(l_{i})=\alpha
and
m^{\prime}
-many
l_{i}
with
\nu(l_{i})=\beta
.
By hypothesis (
E6.2.1
),
n^{\prime}\geq 3
, and
m^{\prime}\geq 1
because
\nu(l_{2})=\beta
. Now

n^{\prime}\alpha+\beta\geq n^{\prime}\alpha+m^{\prime}\beta=\nu({\mathbf{f}})=\nu(\{x,y\})\geq\alpha+\beta-1,

which implies that
(n^{\prime}-1)\alpha\geq-1
, again yielding a contradiction.

Therefore

\alpha\geq 0
, which implies that
{\mathbf{f}}
is
1
-flabby.

The mentioned particular case is clear by verifying (
E6.2.1
).

The general consequences follow from Proposition
3.18
.
∎

For
{\mathbf{f}}
as in Lemma
6.2
, the
Poisson field
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
is not isomorphic
to
K_{\Weyl}
or any
K_{q}
or any
K_{1,n,0}

with
n\geq 2
, as we see from
Lemmas
4.3
–
4.5
. Also,

K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
is not isomorphic to

K\{p(x)xy\}
for any
p(x)\in\Bbbk[x]

with
\deg p(x)\geq 2
, in view of
Proposition
5.2
.

The last statement of Lemma
6.2

may fail if
{\mathbf{f}}
is not homogeneous.
For example, take

{\mathbf{f}}=(x+3)(x+4)\cdots(x+n)xy
for some

n\geq 4
. Then
{\mathbf{f}}
has distinct
linear forms, but

{{}^{1}\Gamma}_{0}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})=\Bbbk[x]

by Proposition
5.2
.

Proposition 6.3
.

Let
{\mathbf{f}}
and
{\mathbf{g}}
be two nonzero polynomials in
\Bbbk[x,y]
.
Suppose that

(a)

\phi
is a Poisson morphism from
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}\to K\{{\mathbf{g}}\}
, and

(b)

{\mathbf{f}}
is a product of distinct linear forms and satisfying
(
E6.2.1
).

Then the following hold.

(1)

\phi({\mathbf{f}})\in\Bbbk[x,y]{\mathbf{g}}
and
\deg{\mathbf{f}}\leq\deg{\mathbf{g}}
.

(2)

If
\deg{\mathbf{f}}=\deg{\mathbf{g}}
, then
\phi
preserves the degree.
Consequently,
\phi
is a
\Bbbk
-algebra isomorphism sending

x\mapsto c_{11}x+c_{12}y+c_{1}
and
y\mapsto c_{21}x+c_{22}y+c_{2}

for some
c_{1},c_{2}\in\Bbbk
and some
\begin{pmatrix}c_{11}&c_{12}\\
c_{21}&c_{22}\end{pmatrix}\in GL_{2}(\Bbbk)
. Further,
\phi({\mathbf{f}})=(c_{11}c_{22}-c_{12}c_{21}){\mathbf{g}}
.

(3)

If
{\mathbf{f}}
is homogeneous and
\deg{\mathbf{f}}=\deg{\mathbf{g}}
, then
\phi(x)
and
\phi(y)
are homogeneous of degree 1 up to an affine change of variables. In
this setting,
{\mathbf{g}}
is also homogeneous.

(4)

(Dixmier Problem)

K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
has the Dixmier property.

Proof.

(1) By Lemma
6.2
,
{{}^{1}\Gamma}_{0}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})=\Bbbk[x,y]
. By Lemma

3.7
(1),
\phi
maps
{{}^{1}\Gamma}_{0}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})
to

{{}^{1}\Gamma}_{0}(K\{{\mathbf{g}}\})
injectively. By Corollary
3.9
,

{{}^{1}\Gamma}_{0}(K\{{\mathbf{g}}\})\subseteq\Bbbk[x,y]
. So
\phi
restricts to an
injective algebra endomorphism of
\Bbbk[x,y]
.

Let
m=\deg{\mathbf{f}}
and write
{\mathbf{f}}=\prod_{i=1}^{m}l_{i}
where

l_{i}=a_{i}x+b_{i}y+c_{i}\not\in\Bbbk
.
Let
s=\min\{\deg\phi(l_{i})\mid i\in[1,m]\}
and

t=\max\{\deg\phi(l_{i})\mid i\in[1,m]\}
. Without
loss of generality, we may assume that
s=\deg\phi(l_{1})
and
t=\deg\phi(l_{2})
. If
s<t
, then

l_{1}
and
l_{2}
generate
\Bbbk[x,y]
. (For if not,
l_{2}=\alpha l_{1}+\beta
for some
\alpha,\beta\in\Bbbk
, which would imply
\deg\phi(l_{2})=\deg\phi(l_{1})
.) If
s=t
, we
can choose an
i\neq 1,2
such that
l_{1}
and
l_{i}

generate
\Bbbk[x,y]
. So we may also assume that

l_{1}
and
l_{2}
generate
\Bbbk[x,y]
. In both cases,
after an affine change of basis
x\to l_{1}
,
y\to l_{2}
,
we may assume that
l_{1}=x
and
l_{2}=y
. Or equivalently,

s=\deg\phi(x)
and
t=\deg\phi(y)
.

Let
\{-,-\}_{1}
(resp.,
\{-,-\}_{2}
) be the Poisson bracket on

K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
(resp.,
K\{{\mathbf{g}}\}
). Then

(E6.3.1)

\displaystyle\phi(x)\phi(y)\prod_{i=3}^{m}(a_{i}\phi(x)+b_{i}\phi(y)+c_{i})

\displaystyle=\phi({\mathbf{f}})=\phi(\{x,y\}_{1})=\{\phi(x),\phi(y)\}_{2}

\displaystyle=(\phi(x)_{x}\phi(y)_{y}-\phi(x)_{y}\phi(y)_{x})\{x,y\}_{2}

\displaystyle=(\phi(x)_{x}\phi(y)_{y}-\phi(x)_{y}\phi(y)_{x}){\mathbf{g}}\,,

which implies
\phi({\mathbf{f}})\in\Bbbk[x,y]{\mathbf{g}}
.

The last term of (
E6.3.1
) has degree at most

(s+t-2)+\deg{\mathbf{g}}.

The degree of the first term of (
E6.3.1
) is at least

(m-3)s+3t
since (
E6.2.1
) holds. Namely, since
l_{1}=x
there are at least three
l_{i}
with
b_{i}\neq 0
, and
\deg\phi(l_{i})=t
for these
l_{i}
(by definition if
s=t
,
and otherwise because

\deg\phi(x)<\deg\phi(y)=t
).
So we have

(s+t-2)+\deg{\mathbf{g}}\geq(m-3)s+3t
.
This implies that

(E6.3.2)

\deg{\mathbf{g}}-2\geq(m-4)s+2t\geq(m-2)s\geq m-2.

Therefore
\deg{\mathbf{g}}\geq m=\deg{\mathbf{f}}
.

(2) Since
m=\deg{\mathbf{f}}=\deg{\mathbf{g}}
, the inequalities (
E6.3.2
)
imply that
s=t=1
. Thus,
\phi
is a
\Bbbk
-algebra isomorphism
and both
\phi(x)
and
\phi(y)
have degree 1. The described forms of
\phi(x)
and
\phi(y)
follow. Further,

\displaystyle\phi({\mathbf{f}})

\displaystyle=\phi(\{x,y\}_{1})=\{c_{11}x+c_{12}y+c_{1},\,c_{21}x+c_{22}y+c_{2}\}_{2}

\displaystyle=(c_{11}c_{22}-c_{12}c_{21})\{x,y\}_{2}=(c_{11}c_{22}-c_{12}c_{21}){\mathbf{g}}\,.

(3) By part (2),
\deg\phi(x)=\deg\phi(y)=1
, consequently,

\phi(x)
and
\phi(y)
generate

\Bbbk[x,y]
. Up to an affine change of variables in
K\{{\mathbf{g}}\}
,
we may assume that
\phi(x)=x
and
\phi(y)=y
. The assertions
follow.

(4) This is an immediate consequence of part (2).
∎

Corollary 6.4

(Isomorphism Problem)
.

Let
{\mathbf{f}},{\mathbf{g}}\in\Bbbk[x,y]
be two nonzero polynomials.

(1)

Suppose there are some
c_{1},c_{2}\in\Bbbk
and some
\begin{pmatrix}c_{11}&c_{12}\\
c_{21}&c_{22}\end{pmatrix}\in GL_{2}(\Bbbk)
such that

(E6.4.1)

{\mathbf{g}}=\frac{{\mathbf{f}}(c_{11}x+c_{12}y+c_{1}\,,\,c_{21}x+c_{22}y+c_{2})}{c_{11}c_{22}-c_{12}c_{21}}\,.

Then
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}\cong K\{{\mathbf{g}}\}
.

(2)

Now assume that
{\mathbf{f}}
and
{\mathbf{g}}
are products of distinct linear forms and both satisfy (
E6.2.1
). If
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}\cong K\{{\mathbf{g}}\}
, then there exist
c_{1},c_{2}\in\Bbbk
and
\begin{pmatrix}c_{11}&c_{12}\\
c_{21}&c_{22}\end{pmatrix}\in GL_{2}(\Bbbk)
such that (
E6.4.1
) holds.

(3)

Assume that
{\mathbf{f}}
and
{\mathbf{g}}
are each products of
4
or more distinct homogeneous linear forms
(
up to scalars
)
. Then
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}\cong K\{{\mathbf{g}}\}
if and only if there is some
\begin{pmatrix}c_{11}&c_{12}\\
c_{21}&c_{22}\end{pmatrix}\in GL_{2}(\Bbbk)
such that

{\mathbf{g}}=\frac{{\mathbf{f}}(c_{11}x+c_{12}y\,,\,c_{21}x+c_{22}y)}{c_{11}c_{22}-c_{12}c_{21}}\,.

Proof.

(1) Under these conditions,
\hat{x}:=c_{11}x+c_{12}y+c_{1}
and
\hat{y}:=c_{21}x+c_{22}y+c_{2}
generate the field
\Bbbk(x,y)
over
\Bbbk
. Since

\{\hat{x},\hat{y}\}_{K\{{\mathbf{g}}\}}=(c_{11}c_{22}-c_{12}c_{21})\{x,y\}_{K\{{\mathbf{g}}\}}=(c_{11}c_{22}-c_{12}c_{21}){\mathbf{g}}={\mathbf{f}}(\hat{x},\hat{y}),

there is a Poisson isomorphism
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}\rightarrow K\{{\mathbf{g}}\}
sending
x\mapsto\hat{x}
and
y\mapsto\hat{y}
.

(2) This follows from part (1) and Proposition
6.3
(1)(2).

(3) Under the given conditions,
{\mathbf{f}}
and
{\mathbf{g}}
both satisfy (
E6.2.1
), and one implication is given by part (1). If
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}\cong K\{{\mathbf{g}}\}
, there exist
c_{i}
and
c_{ij}
as in (1) such that (
E6.4.1
) holds. Defining
\hat{x}
and
\hat{y}
as above, we have that
{\mathbf{f}}(\hat{x},\hat{y})
is homogeneous with respect to
x
,
y
. It follows that if
l_{1},\dots,l_{d}
are the linear factors of
{\mathbf{f}}
, then the
l_{i}(\hat{x},\hat{y})
are homogeneous with respect to
x
,
y
. Now
l_{k}=a_{k}x+b_{k}y
for some
a_{k},b_{k}\in\Bbbk
, and the homogeneity of
l_{i}(\hat{x},\hat{y})
implies that
a_{k}c_{1}+b_{k}c_{2}=0
. But
d\geq 4
and
l_{2}
is not a scalar multiple of
l_{1}
, so
c_{1}=c_{2}=0
. Therefore (
E6.4.1
) gives the required expression for
{\mathbf{g}}
.
∎

Remark 6.5
.

Corollary
6.4
(1) applies in particular when
{\mathbf{f}},{\mathbf{g}}\in\Bbbk[x,y]
are homogeneous polynomials with degree different from
2
such that
{\mathbf{g}}\in\Bbbk^{\times}{\mathbf{f}}
. Say
{\mathbf{f}}
and
{\mathbf{g}}
have degree
n\neq 2
and
{\mathbf{g}}=c{\mathbf{f}}
for some
c\in\Bbbk^{\times}
. Choose
a\in\Bbbk^{\times}
with
a^{n-2}=c
, and observe that
\frac{{\mathbf{f}}(ax,ay)}{a^{2}}=a^{n-2}{\mathbf{f}}(x,y)={\mathbf{g}}
.

On the other hand, there are many Poisson isomorphisms
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}\cong K\{{\mathbf{g}}\}
where
{\mathbf{f}}
and
{\mathbf{g}}
are homogeneous and products of distinct linear forms but
{\mathbf{g}}\notin\Bbbk^{\times}{\mathbf{f}}
. For instance, take

{\mathbf{f}}:=xy(x+y)(x+2y)\qquad\text{and}\qquad{\mathbf{g}}:=y(x+y)(x+2y)(x+3y).

Then (
E6.4.1
) holds with
c_{1}=c_{2}=0
and
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}c_{11}&c_{12}\\
c_{21}&c_{22}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\
0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)
.

Now we can compute the automorphism group of
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}

in the situation of Proposition
6.3
.

Theorem 6.6

(Automorphism Problem)
.

Let
{\mathbf{f}}\in\Bbbk[x,y]
be a polynomial
of degree
n\geq 4
with distinct linear forms and satisfying (
E6.2.1
).

(1)

There is an exact sequence of groups

1\to C\to\Aut_{Poi}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})\to{\mathbb{S}}_{n}

where
C
is a subgroup of
C_{n-2}
.
As a consequence,
|{\Aut_{Poi}}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})|\leq(n-2)n!
.

(2)

If
f
is homogeneous, then
C=C_{n-2}
and
n-2\leq|{\Aut_{Poi}}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})|
.

Proof.

Write
{\mathbf{f}}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}l_{i}
where
the
l_{i}
are the different linear forms
of
{\mathbf{f}}
.

(1) Let
\phi
be an automorphism of
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
. By Proposition

6.3
(2),
\phi(x)
and
\phi(y)
are linear forms, and
\phi({\mathbf{f}})=c{\mathbf{f}}
for some nonzero scalar

c
. The
\phi(l_{i})
are then also linear
forms of
{\mathbf{f}}
, so the
\phi(l_{i})=\gamma_{i}l_{\sigma(i)}
for some

\sigma\in{\mathbb{S}}_{n}
and some

\gamma_{i}\in\Bbbk^{\times}
. Then the map
\Phi:\phi\to\sigma
defines a group homomorphism

\Aut_{Poi}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})\to{\mathbb{S}}_{n}
. It remains to show that
the kernel of
\Phi
is (isomorphic to) a subgroup of
C^{\prime}:=\{a\in\Bbbk^{\times}\mid a^{n-2}=1\}\cong C_{n-2}
.

Let
\phi\in\ker\Phi
. Then
\phi(l_{i})=\gamma_{i}l_{i}

for all
i
. Up to a change of variables,
we may assume that
l_{1}=x
and
l_{2}=y
. Then

\phi(x)=\gamma_{1}x
and
\phi(y)=\gamma_{2}y
. By (
E6.2.1
), there is an
i\geq 3
such that
l_{i}=a_{i}x+b_{i}y+c_{i}
with
a_{i},b_{i}\in\Bbbk^{\times}
,
c_{i}\in\Bbbk
, and

\phi(l_{i})=\gamma_{i}l_{i}
implies that
\gamma_{i}=\gamma_{1}=\gamma_{2}
. Thus
\phi(x)=ax
and
\phi(y)=ay
for
some
a\in\Bbbk^{\times}
.
Then

a^{n}{\mathbf{f}}=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(al_{i})=\phi({\mathbf{f}})=\phi(\{x,y\})=\{ax,ay\}=a^{2}{\mathbf{f}},

so we obtain
that
a^{n-2}=1
and
a\in C^{\prime}
.

(2) For any
a\in C^{\prime}
, we have
{\mathbf{f}}(ax,ay)=a^{n}{\mathbf{f}}(x,y)=\{ax,ay\}
, so there is a Poisson
automorphism of
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
sending
x\mapsto ax
and
y\mapsto ay
. The assertion follows.
∎

Lemma 7.1
.

Let

(1)

If

The assertion follows by setting

Throughout the rest of

and

Lemma 7.2
.

Suppose
{\mathbf{f}}

Theorem 7.5

(Automorphism Problem)
.

Let
{\mathbf{f}}=c\prod_{i=1}^{m}(x+\xi_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{n}(y+\chi_{j})

where
c\in\Bbbk^{\times}
and
(\xi_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
,
(\chi_{j})_{j=1}^{n}

are families of distinct scalars in
\Bbbk
and
m,n\geq 3
. Define

•

G:=\{\phi\in\Aut_{Poi}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})\mid\phi(x)\in\Bbbk[x],\;\phi(y)\in\Bbbk[y]\}
;

•

m^{*}:=m-1
if there is some
s\in[1,m]
such that
m\xi_{s}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}
, and
m^{*}:=m
otherwise;

•

n^{*}:=n-1
if there is some
s\in[1,n]
such that
n\chi_{s}=\sum_{j=1}^{n}\chi_{j}
, and
n^{*}:=n
otherwise;

•

H:=\{(\gamma,\delta)\in(\Bbbk^{\times})^{2}\mid\gamma^{m^{*}}=\delta^{n^{*}}=\gamma^{m-1}\delta^{n-1}=1\}
.

Then the following hold.

(1)

G
is a normal subgroup of
\Aut_{Poi}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})
with index at most
2
.

(2)

G
is isomorphic to a subgroup of
H
.

(3)

\Aut_{Poi}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})=G
except possibly when
m=n
is even and
m^{*}=n^{*}=m
, in which case
G
is cyclic of order at most
m
.

(4)

|{\Aut_{Poi}}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})|\leq(m-1)(n-1)
.

Proof.

(1) We first claim that any
\phi\in\Aut_{Poi}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})
is either in
G
or satisfies
\phi(x)\in\Bbbk[y]
,
\phi(y)\in\Bbbk[x]
. By Proposition
7.3
(2), we have
\phi(x)=c_{11}x+c_{12}y+c_{1}
and
\phi(y)=c_{21}x+c_{22}y+c_{2}

for some
c_{1},c_{2}\in\Bbbk
and some
\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}c_{11}&c_{12}\\
c_{21}&c_{22}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)\in GL_{2}(\Bbbk)
, and
\phi({\mathbf{f}})\in\Bbbk^{\times}{\mathbf{f}}
. Thus,

(E7.5.1)

\phi({\mathbf{f}})=c\prod_{i=1}^{m}(c_{11}x+c_{12}y+c_{1}+\xi_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{n}(c_{21}x+c_{22}y+c_{2}+\chi_{j}).

Since
\phi({\mathbf{f}})\in\Bbbk^{\times}{\mathbf{f}}
, the factor
c_{11}x+c_{12}y+c_{1}+\xi_{1}
must be a scalar multiple of some
x+\xi_{i}
or of some
y+\chi_{j}
, and likewise for
c_{21}x+c_{22}y+c_{2}+\chi_{1}
. Note that if
\bigl(d_{ij}\bigr)=\bigl(c_{ij}\bigr)^{-1}
, then

\displaystyle d_{11}(c_{11}x+c_{12}y+c_{1}+\xi_{1})+d_{12}(c_{21}x+c_{22}y+c_{2}+\chi_{1})

\displaystyle=x+\text{a scalar}

\displaystyle d_{21}(c_{11}x+c_{12}y+c_{1}+\xi_{1})+d_{22}(c_{21}x+c_{22}y+c_{2}+\chi_{1})

\displaystyle=y+\text{a scalar},

so
c_{11}x+c_{12}y+c_{1}+\xi_{1}
and
c_{21}x+c_{22}y+c_{2}+\chi_{1}
cannot both be in
\Bbbk[x]
or both in
\Bbbk[y]
. Thus, either
c_{12}=c_{21}=0
, in which case
\phi\in G
, or else
c_{11}=c_{22}=0
, whence
\phi(x)\in\Bbbk[y]
,
\phi(y)\in\Bbbk[x]
. This verifies the claim.

Observe from (
E7.5.1
) that we can only have
c_{11}=c_{22}=0
when
m=n
. Thus,
\Aut_{Poi}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})=G
when
m\neq n
.

It is clear that
G
is a subgroup of
\Aut_{Poi}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})
, and it follows from the claim that
G
is normal. Suppose there exists
\psi\in\Aut_{Poi}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})\setminus G
. For any
\phi\in\Aut_{Poi}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})\setminus G
, we see from the claim that
\phi\psi^{-1}\in G
, whence
\phi G=\psi G
. Consequently,

|{\Aut_{Poi}}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})/G|\leq 2.

(2) For any
\phi\in G
, we have, by definition,

\phi(x)=c_{11}(\phi)x+c_{1}(\phi)\qquad\text{and}\qquad\phi(y)=c_{22}(\phi)y+c_{2}(\phi)

for some
c_{11}(\phi),c_{22}(\phi)\in\Bbbk^{\times}
and
c_{1}(\phi),c_{2}(\phi)\in\Bbbk
. The rule
\phi\mapsto(c_{11}(\phi),c_{22}(\phi))
defines a homomorphism
\Phi:G\rightarrow(\Bbbk^{\times})^{2}
. We need to show that
\Phi
is injective and
\Phi(G)\subseteq H
.

Fix
\phi\in G
, and abbreviate
c_{ii}:=c_{ii}(\phi)
,
c_{i}:=c_{i}(\phi)
. In view of Proposition
7.3
(2), (
E7.5.1
) reduces to

(E7.5.2)

c_{11}c_{22}{\mathbf{f}}=\phi({\mathbf{f}})=c\prod_{i=1}^{m}(c_{11}x+c_{1}+\xi_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{n}(c_{22}y+c_{2}+\chi_{j}).

Comparing leading coefficients in this equation, we obtain
c_{11}c_{22}c=cc_{11}^{m}c_{22}^{n}
, and so
c_{11}^{m-1}c_{22}^{n-1}=1
. We will be done on showing that

(a)

c_{11}^{m^{*}}=1
, and
c_{11}=1\implies c_{1}=0
;

(b)

c_{22}^{n^{*}}=1
, and
c_{22}=1\implies c_{2}=0
.

Since (a) and (b) are symmetric, we just prove (a).

Comparing factors from
\Bbbk[x]
in (
E7.5.2
), we see that there must be a permutation
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{m}
such that
c_{11}x+c_{1}+\xi_{i}=c_{11}(x+\xi_{\sigma(i)})
for all
i
, that is,

(E7.5.3)

c_{11}\xi_{\sigma(i)}=c_{1}+\xi_{i}\qquad\forall\;i\in[1,m].

Summing this equation over
i\in[1,m]
yields

(E7.5.4)

(c_{11}-1)\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}=mc_{1}\,.

In particular, the implication
c_{11}=1\implies c_{1}=0
follows.

Since
1^{m^{*}}=1
, we assume
c_{11}\neq 1
for the remainder of the proof of (a).

Claim 1:

s\in[1,m]
is a fixed point of
\sigma
if and only if
\xi_{s}=c_{1}/(c_{11}-1)
, if and only if
m\xi_{s}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}
.

The first forward implication follows from (
E7.5.3
), and the second equivalence is immediate from (
E7.5.4
). If
\xi_{s}=c_{1}/(c_{11}-1)
, then
c_{11}\xi_{s}=c_{1}+\xi_{s}=c_{11}\xi_{\sigma(s)}
, and so
\sigma(s)=s
because the
\xi_{i}
are distinct.

Claim 2:

\xi_{\sigma^{t}(i)}=(c_{11}^{-1}+\cdots+c_{11}^{-t})c_{1}+c_{11}^{-t}\xi_{i}
for all
i\in[1,m]
and
t\geq 0
.

This is an obvious induction on
t
.

Claim 3:
Let
{\mathcal{O}}
be a
\sigma
-orbit of cardinality
t>1
. Then
c_{11}
is a primitive
t
-th root of unity, i.e.,
t
is the order of
c_{11}
in the group
\Bbbk^{\times}
.

If
i\in{\mathcal{O}}
, then
\sigma^{t}(i)=i
, and by Claim 2 we see that
(c_{11}^{t}-1)\xi_{i}=\frac{c_{11}^{t}-1}{c_{11}-1}\,c_{1}
. Since there are at least
2
indices in
{\mathcal{O}}
, we must have
c_{11}^{t}=1
. Thus,
c_{11}
has order
d<\infty
in
\Bbbk^{\times}
, and
d\mid t
. Now
c_{11}^{d}\xi_{i}=(c_{11}^{d-1}+\cdots+1)c_{1}+\xi_{i}
for any
i\in{\mathcal{O}}
. Claim 2 shows that
\xi_{\sigma^{d}(i)}=\xi_{i}
and hence
\sigma^{d}(i)=i
. This forces
t=d
, proving Claim 3.

We can now complete the proof of (a) and thus of (2). If
\sigma
has a fixed point
s
, then by Claim 1,
s
is the only fixed point of
\sigma
, and
m^{*}=m-1
. By Claim 3, all the non-singleton orbits of
\sigma
have the same cardinality
t
, and
c_{11}^{t}=1
. Since
[1.m]\setminus\{s\}
is a disjoint union of these latter orbits,
t\mid m-1
, and thus
c_{11}^{m^{*}}=1
.

If
\sigma
has no fixed points, then
m^{*}=m
and
[1,m]
is a disjoint union of
\sigma
-orbits of cardinality
t
. In this case,
c_{11}^{t}=1
and
t\mid m
, so again
c_{11}^{m^{*}}=1
.

(3) That
\Aut_{Poi}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})=G
when
m\neq n
was already noted in the proof of part (1). Suppose there exists
\theta\in\Aut_{Poi}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})\setminus G
, so that
m=n
. Now
\theta(x)=c_{12}y+c_{1}
and
\theta(y)=c_{21}x+c_{2}
for some
c_{12},c_{21}\in\Bbbk^{\times}
and
c_{1},c_{2}\in\Bbbk
. Observe that
\theta^{2}\in G
and

\theta^{2}(x)=c_{12}c_{21}x+c_{12}c_{2}+c_{1}\,,\qquad\qquad\theta^{2}(y)=c_{12}c_{21}y+c_{21}c_{1}+c_{2}\,.

Then
(c_{12}c_{21},c_{12}c_{21})\in H
, whence

(c_{12}c_{21})^{m^{*}}=(c_{12}c_{21})^{n^{*}}=(c_{12}c_{21})^{2(m-1)}=1.

In the present case, (
E7.5.1
) reads

\displaystyle\theta({\mathbf{f}})

\displaystyle=c\prod_{i=1}^{m}(c_{12}y+c_{1}+\xi_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{m}(c_{21}x+c_{2}+\chi_{j})

\displaystyle=(c_{12}c_{21})^{m}c\prod_{j=1}^{m}(x+c_{21}^{-1}(c_{2}+\chi_{j}))\prod_{i=1}^{m}(y+c_{12}^{-1}(c_{1}+\xi_{i}))\,,

from which we conclude that
\theta({\mathbf{f}})=(c_{12}c_{21})^{m}{\mathbf{f}}
. On the other hand, Proposition
7.3
(2) says that
\theta({\mathbf{f}})=-c_{12}c_{21}{\mathbf{f}}
. Consequently,
(c_{12}c_{21})^{m-1}=-1
. In particular,
m^{*},n^{*}\neq m-1
, and so
m^{*}=n^{*}=m
. Thus
c_{12}c_{21}=-1
and
m
is even.

We now get
H=\{(\gamma,\gamma^{-1})\in(\Bbbk^{\times})^{2}\mid\gamma^{m}=1\}
, which is cyclic of order at most
m
, and then the same holds for
G
by part (2).

(4) We first show that
|H|\leq(m-1)(n-1)
, which is clear in case
m^{*}=m-1
and
n^{*}=n-1
.
If
m^{*}=m
, then all
(\gamma,\delta)\in H
satisfy
\gamma=\delta^{n-1}
, whence
|H|\leq n\leq(m-1)(n-1)
. Similarly,
|H|\leq m\leq(m-1)(n-1)
in case
n^{*}=n
. Therefore, we have
|{\Aut_{Poi}}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})|\leq(m-1)(n-1)
whenever
\Aut_{Poi}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})=G
.

Finally, suppose that
\Aut_{Poi}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})\neq G
, whence
G
has index
2
and
|{\Aut_{Poi}}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})|=2|G|
. By part (3),
m=n
is even and
|G|\leq m
. Since then
m\geq 4
, we conclude that
|{\Aut_{Poi}}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})|\leq 2m<(m-1)^{2}
in this case.
∎

Example 7.6
.

For some examples where
\Aut_{Poi}(K\{{\mathbf{f}}\})
is as large as possible, let
m,n\geq 3
and assume that
\Bbbk
contains a primitive
(m-1)
-st root of unity, say
\gamma
, and a primitive
(n-1)
-st root of unity, say
\delta
. Set
{\mathbf{f}}:=xy\prod_{i=1}^{m-1}(x-\gamma^{i})\prod_{j=1}^{n-1}(y-\delta^{j})
and
K:=K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
.

There is a field automorphism
\phi
of
K
sending
x\mapsto\gamma^{-1}x
and

. Since

\{\gamma^{-1}x,y\}=\gamma^{-1}{\mathbf{f}}=\gamma^{-m}{\mathbf{f}}={\mathbf{f}}(\gamma^{-1}x,y),

we see that
\phi\in\Aut_{Poi}(K)
. Similarly, there exists
\psi\in\Aut_{Poi}(K)
sending
x\mapsto x
and

. In the notation of Theorem
7.5
,
\phi,\psi\in G
. Note that
\phi^{s}\psi^{t}=\Id
if and only if
m-1\mid s
and

has order at least

(4) therefore implies that

has order

, the maximum possible.

One next goal of this section is to show that
the automorphism group of
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
is
trivial for some
{\mathbf{f}}
as in Theorem
7.5
. This of course holds if
\Bbbk
has no nontrivial
m^{*}
-th or
n^{*}
-th roots of unity, but it also holds for suitable
{\mathbf{f}}
when
\Bbbk
is algebraically closed. We go to a situation somewhat parallel to that of Corollary
5.7
(3).

Corollary 7.7
.

Let
K=K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
under the following hypotheses:

(a)

{\mathbf{f}}=c\prod_{i=1}^{m}(x+\xi_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{n}(y+\chi_{j})

where
c\in\Bbbk^{\times}
and
(\xi_{i})_{i=1}^{m}
,
(\chi_{j})_{j=1}^{n}

are families of distinct scalars in
\Bbbk
, with
m,n\geq 3
.

(b)

m\neq n
, or
m=n
is odd.

(c)

The families
(\xi_{i}-\xi_{1})_{i=2}^{m}
and
(\chi_{j}-\chi_{1})_{j=2}^{n}
are
\mathbb{Z}
-linearly independent.

(d)

(\xi_{i}-\xi_{1})^{m}\neq(\xi_{1}-\xi_{j})^{m}
for all
i\neq j
, and
(\chi_{i}-\chi_{1})^{n}\neq(\chi_{1}-\chi_{j})^{n}
for all
i\neq j
.

Then
\Aut_{Poi}(K)
is trivial.

Proof.

Set
x^{\prime}:=x+\xi_{1}
and
y^{\prime}:=y+\chi_{1}
. Then
K=\Bbbk(x^{\prime},y^{\prime})
with

\{x^{\prime},y^{\prime}\}=\{x,y\}={\mathbf{f}}=c\prod_{i=1}^{m}(x^{\prime}+\xi^{\prime}_{i})\prod_{j=1}^{n}(y^{\prime}+\chi^{\prime}_{j})

where
\xi^{\prime}_{i}:=\xi_{i}-\xi_{1}
for all
i
and
\chi^{\prime}_{j}:=\chi_{j}-\chi_{1}
for all
j
. Thus, there is no loss of generality in assuming that
\xi_{1}=\chi_{1}=0
.

Because of hypothesis (c), we cannot have
m\xi_{s}=\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}
for any
s\in[1,m]
, and so
m^{*}=m
in the notation of Theorem
7.5
. Similarly,
n^{*}=n
.

Towards a contradiction, suppose there is some nontrivial
\phi\in\Aut_{Poi}(K)
. Then
\phi(x)\neq x
or
\phi(y)\neq y
, so by symmetry we may assume that
\phi(x)\neq x
. By Theorem
7.5
(3),
\phi(x)=c_{11}x+c_{1}
and
\phi(y)=c_{22}y+c_{2}
for some
c_{11},c_{22}\in\Bbbk^{\times}
and
c_{1},c_{2}\in\Bbbk
. Then
c_{1}\neq 0
or
c_{11}\neq 1
.

By (
E7.5.4
),
(c_{11}-1)\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}=mc_{1}
. Since
\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}=\sum_{i=2}^{m}\xi_{i}\neq 0
by hypothesis (c),
c_{1}=0
would imply
c_{11}=1
, contradicting our current assumptions. Thus,
c_{1}\neq 0
, and then
c_{11}\neq 1
as well. From the proof of Theorem
7.5
(2), there is some
\sigma\in\mathbb{S}_{m}
such that (
E7.5.3
) holds. Due to the fact, noted above, that
m\xi_{s}\neq\sum_{i=1}^{m}\xi_{i}
for
s\in[1,m]
, Claim 1 in the previous proof shows that
\sigma
has no fixed points.

Setting
k:=\sigma(1)\neq 1
, we obtain
c_{11}\xi_{k}=c_{1}
from (
E7.5.3
). Setting
l:=\sigma^{-1}(1)
, we obtain
0=c_{1}+\xi_{l}
. From the proof of Theorem
7.5
(2),
c_{11}^{m}=1
, so

\xi_{k}^{m}=(c_{11}\xi_{k})^{m}=c_{1}^{m}=(-\xi_{l})^{m}\,.

Hypothesis (d) now implies that
k=l
, whence
-c_{11}c_{1}=c_{11}\xi_{l}=c_{11}\xi_{k}=c_{1}
, and thus
c_{11}=-1
.

Equation (
E7.5.3
) now reads
\xi_{\sigma(i)}=-c_{1}-\xi_{i}
for all
i
. It follows that
\xi_{\sigma^{2}(i)}=\xi_{i}
for all
i
, and consequently
\sigma^{2}=\Id
. Since
\sigma
has no fixed points,
[1,m]
is a disjoint union of
2
-point
\sigma
-orbits. The assumption that
m\geq 3
now yields
m\geq 4
, and so
\sigma
has an orbit
\{j,\sigma(j)\}
different from
\{1,k\}
. Since
\xi_{j}+\xi_{\sigma(j)}=-c_{1}=\xi_{k}
, we again contradict hypothesis (c).

Therefore
\Aut_{Poi}(K)
contains no nontrivial automorphisms.
∎

8.
The polynomial flag problem

In this section we will construct Poisson fields
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}

with infinite flag height, that is,
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}\not\cong K\{{\mathbf{g}}\}
for any polynomial
{\mathbf{g}}\in\Bbbk[x,y]
. Various bounds on rational functions are needed, which we define in general.

Let
F:=\Bbbk(x_{1},\cdots,x_{n})
be the field of rational
functions in
n
variables. If
n=2
, we also use
(x,y)
for

(x_{1},x_{2})
. For an
n
-tuple
\mathbf{s}=(s_{1},\cdots,s_{n})
of
elements of
F
, let
\Bbbk[\mathbf{s}]
denote the
\Bbbk
-subalgebra
of
F
generated by
s_{1},\cdots,s_{n}
. When this notation is used,
we do not assume that
s_{1},\cdots,s_{n}
are algebraically
independent. Let
Q(\Bbbk[\mathbf{s}])
denote the fraction field of

\Bbbk[\mathbf{s}]
, realized as a subfield of
F
. Let

\Phi=\Phi(F):=\{\mathbf{s}\in F^{n}\mid Q(\Bbbk[\mathbf{s}])=F\}.

The condition
Q(\Bbbk[\mathbf{s}])=F
forces that
s_{1},\cdots,s_{n}

are algebraically independent over
\Bbbk
and that
\Bbbk[\mathbf{s}]\cong\Bbbk[x_{1},\cdots,x_{n}]
. When
\mathbf{s}\in\Phi
,
we call
\mathbf{s}
a
fractional frame
of
F
. Let

\overline{\Phi}:=\{\Bbbk[\mathbf{s}]\mid\mathbf{s}\in\Phi\}.

By definition, the map
\phi:\Phi\to\overline{\Phi}
defined
by
\phi(\mathbf{s})=\Bbbk[\mathbf{s}]
is onto, but not one-to-one.

Next we will define some numerical invariants of an element

h\in F
. In the following discussion we fix
h\in F
. For each

\mathbf{s}\in\Phi
, write
h
as a rational function in

s_{1},\cdots,s_{n}
,

(E8.0.1)

h=\frac{f(s_{1},\cdots,s_{n})}{g(s_{1},\cdots,s_{n})}\,,

where
f:=f(s_{1},\cdots,s_{n})
and
g:=g(s_{1},\cdots,s_{n})
are in

\Bbbk[\mathbf{s}]
(namely, these are polynomials in
s_{1},\cdots,s_{n}
)
and where
\gcd(f,g)=1
in
\Bbbk[\mathbf{s}]
. (We may further assume that
g
is monic if necessary). Note that the pair

(f,g)
in (
E8.0.1
) is unique up to multiplication by scalars from

\Bbbk
. For a given
\mathbf{s}\in\Phi
and given (
E8.0.1
), we
consider a prime decomposition of the polynomial
g
in

\Bbbk[\mathbf{s}]
:

(E8.0.2)

g=p_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots p_{w}^{m_{w}},

where the
p_{j}
are pairwise non-associate primes in
\Bbbk[\mathbf{s}]
and
the
m_{j}\in\mathbb{Z}_{>0}
. (So
w=0
if
g\in\Bbbk^{\times}
.) The prime
decomposition is unique up to scalar multiples of the
p_{j}
and
a permutation of
p_{1},\cdots,p_{w}
. Let
\deg_{\mathbf{s}}
denote the
total degree of rational functions in
Q(\Bbbk[\mathbf{s}])
. Hence,

\deg_{\mathbf{s}}s_{i}=1
for all
i=1,\cdots,n
.

Definition 8.1
.

Let
h\in F
.

(1)

The
denominator degree bound
of
h
is defined to be

\ddb(h):=\min\{\deg_{\mathbf{s}}g\mid\mathbf{s}\in\Phi,\;{\text{$(f,g)$ as in \eqref{E8.0.1}}}\}.

(2)

The
denominator prime-divisor bound
of
h
is defined
to be

\dpb(h):=\min\{w\mid\mathbf{s}\in\Phi,\;g=p_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots p_{w}^{m_{w}}\;\text{as in \eqref{E8.0.2}}\}.

(3)

The
framed degree bound
of
h
is defined to be

\fdb(h):=\min\{\deg_{\mathbf{s}}h\mid\mathbf{s}\in\Phi,\;{\text{$(f,g)$
as in \eqref{E8.0.1}, $\deg_{\mathbf{s}}g=\ddb(h)$}}\}.

(4)

h
is called a
framed polynomial
if
h\in\Bbbk[\mathbf{s}]
for some
\mathbf{s}\in\Phi
.

By definition,
0\leq\dpb(h)\leq\ddb(h)<\infty
and the following
are equivalent:

(i)

\dpb(h)=0
,

(ii)

\ddb(h)=0
,

(iii)

h
is a framed polynomial.

We can calculate
\ddb
,
\dpb
, and
\fdb
for certain
elements in
F
.

Lemma 8.2
.

Let
u\in F\setminus\Bbbk
and let

\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{w^{\prime}}\sqcup\{b_{j}\}_{j=0}^{w}
be a set of distinct
scalars in
\Bbbk
, where
w
and
w^{\prime}
are non-negative
integers. Let

h=\frac{(u-a_{1})(u-a_{2})\cdots(u-a_{w^{\prime}})}{(u-b_{0})(u-b_{1})\cdots(u-b_{w})}
.

(1)

\ddb(h)\geq\dpb(h)\geq w
.

(2)

Let
\varphi:F\to F
be a
\Bbbk
-algebra map. Then

\ddb(\varphi(h))\geq\dpb(\varphi(h))\geq w
.

(3)

If
u=x_{1}+b_{0}
and
w+1\geq w^{\prime}
, then
\ddb(h)=\dpb(h)=w
.

(4)

If
u=x_{1}+b_{0}
and
w+1\geq w^{\prime}
, then
\fdb(h)=1
.

Proof.

Replacing
u
by
u-b_{0}
and
a_{i}
,
b_{j}
by
a_{i}-b_{0}
,
b_{j}-b_{0}
, we may assume that
b_{0}=0
. The first hypothesis in (3), (4) then becomes
u=x_{1}
.

(1) Pick any fractional frame
\mathbf{s}
and write
u
as a
rational function
u=\frac{f}{g}
where
f
and
g
are
co-prime polynomials in
\Bbbk[\mathbf{s}]
. Then

(E8.2.1)

\displaystyle h

\displaystyle=\frac{(u-a_{1})(u-a_{2})\cdots(u-a_{w^{\prime}})}{u(u-b_{1})\cdots(u-b_{w})}

\displaystyle=g^{w-w^{\prime}+1}\frac{(f-a_{1}g)(f-a_{2}g)\cdots(f-a_{w^{\prime}}g)}{f(f-b_{1}g)\cdots(f-b_{w}g)}

and the polynomials in
G:=\{f-a_{i}g\}_{i=1}^{w^{\prime}}\sqcup\{f-b_{j}g\}_{j=0}^{w}\sqcup\{g\}
are pairwise coprime.
Since
u
is not in
\Bbbk
, at most one of
f
,
g
is a scalar, so at most one element of
G

is a scalar. Thus the denominator of
h
, which is either

f(f-b_{1}g)\cdots(f-b_{w}g)\qquad\text{or}\qquad g^{w^{\prime}-w-1}f(f-b_{1}g)\cdots(f-b_{w}g)

depending on whether
w^{\prime}\leq w+1
or
w^{\prime}\geq w+1
, is a product
of at least
w
many non-scalar relatively coprime factors. So

\dpb(h)\geq w
. The inequality
\ddb(h)\geq\dpb(h)
has
already been noted.

(2) Note that
\varphi(h)
has the form

\frac{(\varphi(u)-a_{1})(\varphi(u)-a_{2})\cdots(\varphi(u)-a_{w^{\prime}})}{(\varphi(u)-b_{0})(\varphi(u)-b_{1})\cdots(\varphi(u)-b_{w})}

where
\varphi(u)\in F\setminus\Bbbk
. The assertion follows
from part (1).

(3) Let
\mathbf{s}=(x_{1}^{-1},x_{2},\cdots,x_{n})
. Then

h=\frac{s_{1}^{w-w^{\prime}+1}(1-a_{1}s_{1})(1-a_{2}s_{1})\cdots(1-a_{w^{\prime}}s_{1})}{(1-b_{1}s_{1})\cdots(1-b_{w}s_{1})}

which implies that
\ddb(h)\leq w
. The assertion follows from
part (1). In this case, one also sees that
\fdb(h)\leq 1
.

(4) We continue the proof of part (1) under the extra hypotheses
that
u=x_{1}
and
w+1\geq w^{\prime}
. Let
\mathbf{s}\in\Phi
such that the
denominator of
h
in (
E8.0.1
) has
\deg_{\mathbf{s}}=w
. But
in the form (
E8.2.1
), the denominator of
h
is

f(f-b_{1}g)\cdots(f-b_{w}g)
, due to
w-w^{\prime}+1\geq 0
. Thus, one
of the elements in
\{f-b_{j}g\}_{j=0}^{w}
must be a scalar. This
implies that none of
f-a_{i}g
or
g
is a scalar. So the
numerator of
h
has degree at least
w+1
, and hence

\deg_{\mathbf{s}}h\geq 1
. Thus
\fdb(h)\geq 1
. By the proof of
part (3),
\fdb(h)\leq 1
. The assertion follows.
∎

Definition 8.3
.

Let
V
be a nonempty subset of
F
.

(1)

The
denominator degree bound

of
V
is defined to be

\ddb(V):=\sup\{\ddb(h)\mid h\in V\}.

(2)

The
denominator prime-divisor bound

of
V
is defined to be

\dpb(V):=\sup\{\dpb(h)\mid h\in V\}.

Lemma 8.4
.

Let
K
be the Poisson field
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
for some
{\mathbf{f}}\in\Bbbk(x,y)
. Let
V
be the subalgebra
{{}^{1}\Gamma}_{0}(K)
.

(1)

If
{\mathbf{f}}\in\Bbbk[x,y]
, then
\ddb(V)=0
.

(2)

Write
{\mathbf{f}}=\frac{a}{b}
where
a,b
are in
\Bbbk[x,y]
, and
let
w
be the number of non-associate prime divisors of
b
.
Then
\dpb(V)\leq w\leq\deg b
.

Proof.

(1) By Corollary
3.9
,
V
is a subset of
\Bbbk[x,y]
.
Since every element in
\Bbbk[x,y]
has
\ddb
0, the assertion
follows.

(2) Let
A
be the localization
\Bbbk[x,y][b^{-1}]
. Then

A\subseteq K
is a noetherian normal domain such that
A
is a Poisson
subalgebra of
K
and
Q(A)=K
. By Theorem
3.8
,

V
is a subset of
A
. It remains to show that every
element in
A
has
\dpb
bounded by
w
.

Write
b=p_{1}^{m_{1}}\cdots p_{w}^{m_{w}}
as a prime decomposition.
Then
w\leq\deg b
. Since every element
h
in
A
is of the
form
cb^{-n}
with
c\in\Bbbk[x,y]
and
n\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
, one
can write
h
as
\frac{c}{p_{1}^{m_{1}n}\cdots p_{w}^{m_{w}n}}
.
Hence
\dpb(h)\leq w
. The assertion follows.
∎

Now we are ready to give an example where
K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
is not
isomorphic to
K\{{\mathbf{g}}\}
for any
{\mathbf{g}}\in\Bbbk[x,y]
.

Example 8.5
.

Let
h\in\Bbbk(x,y)
be an element with positive
\ddb
, see
Lemma
8.2
. Let
f(t)\in\Bbbk[t]
be a polynomial of degree

\geq 2
and
{\mathbf{f}}:=xyf(h)
. By Corollary
3.11
,

h\in{{}^{1}\Gamma}_{0}(K)
where
K:=K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
. Since

\ddb(h)\geq 1
, we have
\ddb(V)\geq 1
where

V={{}^{1}\Gamma}_{0}(K)
. If
K\cong K\{{\mathbf{g}}\}
for some

{\mathbf{g}}\in\Bbbk[x,y]
, then
\ddb(V)=0
by Lemma

8.4
(1), yielding a contradiction.

We can generalize the above example to the following theorem.
For every nonnegative integer
w
,
let
G_{w}
be the set of elements
{\mathbf{g}}\in\Bbbk(x,y)
such that

{\mathbf{g}}=a/b
for some coprime polynomials
a,b\in\Bbbk[x,y]
and that the number of non-associate prime divisors of
b
is no more than
w
.

Theorem 8.6
.

Let
w
be a nonnegative integer and let

h\in\Bbbk(x,y)
be a nonzero element such that

\dpb(\varphi(h))>w
for every
\Bbbk
-algebra map

\varphi:\Bbbk(x,y)\to\Bbbk(x,y)

(
see Lemma
8.2
(2)
)
.
Let
f(t)\in\Bbbk[t]
be a
polynomial of degree at least
2
. Then there is no Poisson
algebra morphism from
K\{xyf(h)\}
to
K\{{\mathbf{g}}\}
for any
{\mathbf{g}}\in G_{w}
.

Proof.

Let
K:=K\{xyf(h)\}
and
P:=K\{{\mathbf{g}}\}
where
{\mathbf{g}}\in G_{w}
. Suppose there is a
Poisson algebra morphism
\varphi:K\to P
. So we may
consider
K
as a Poisson subfield of
P
via the embedding

\varphi
. By Lemma
3.7
(1) and Corollary

3.11
,

h\in{{}^{1}\Gamma}_{0}(K)\subseteq{{}^{1}\Gamma}_{0}(P)
.
Since
\dpb(\varphi(h))>w
by assumption, we have
\dpb(V)>w

where
V={{}^{1}\Gamma}_{0}(P)
. By Lemma
8.4
(2),

\dpb(V)\leq w
, yielding a contradiction.
∎

Finally we list some open questions.

Questions 8.7
.

Let
K
be a Poisson field of transcendence degree at least 2.

(1)

Is there a connection between the following three properties
for Poisson fields
K
?

(a)

K
has the Dixmier property;

(b)

K
has only finitely many Poisson subfields with the same transcendence degree as
K
;

(c)

\Aut_{Poi}(K)
is finite.

(2)

What is the structure of the Poisson Cremona group
Cr_{2}(\Bbbk,1)=\Aut_{Poi}(K_{\Weyl})
?

(3)

Let
K=K\{{\mathbf{f}}\}
where
{\mathbf{f}}
is a nonzero polynomial in
\Bbbk[x,y]
. Dumas proved in
[
3
, Proposition, p.12]
that if
\deg{\mathbf{f}}\leq 2
, then
K
is isomorphic to either
K_{\Weyl}
or some
K_{q}
(Corollary
1.2
). This cannot hold when
\deg{\mathbf{f}}>2
, in view of Proposition
4.6
. Can such Poisson fields (with
{\mathbf{f}}\in\Bbbk[x,y]
) be classified?

(4)

Suppose
K
is a Poisson field (say with
\trdeg K=2
) such that
H=\Aut_{Poi}(K)
is finite. Then
K^{H}
is a Poisson field with the same transcendence degree as
K
. Does
K^{H}
have the Dixmier property?

(5)

Do there exist Poisson fields
K
(say with
\trdeg K=2
) which are
minimal
in the sense that

has no proper Poisson subfields with the same transcendence degree?

9.
Appendix: Some basic Poisson algebra items

Recall that, in this paper, a Poisson algebra is commutative by definition.

Lemma 9.1
.

Let
A
be a Poisson algebra,
x,y\in A
,
and
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Then

\{x^{a}y^{b},x^{c}y^{d}\}=(ad-bc)x^{a+c-1}y^{b+d-1}\{x,y\}.

If
x
and
y
are invertible in
A
, the
same formula holds for all
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}
.

Note: For
A=K_{q}
and the variables
x
,
y
, this is Equation (
E1.5.1
).

Proof.

This is a straight bracket calculation:

\displaystyle\{x^{a}y^{b},x^{c}y^{d}\}

\displaystyle=x^{a}\{y^{b},x^{c}y^{d}\}+y^{b}\{x^{a},x^{c}y^{d}\}

\displaystyle=bx^{a}y^{b-1}\{y,x^{c}y^{d}\}+ax^{a-1}y^{b}\{x,x^{c}y^{d}\}

\displaystyle=bx^{a}y^{b-1+d}\{y,x^{c}\}+ax^{a-1+c}y^{b}\{x,y^{d}\}

\displaystyle=bcx^{a+c-1}y^{b-1+d}\{y,x\}+adx^{a-1+c}y^{b+d-1}\{x,y\}

\displaystyle=(ad-bc)x^{a+c-1}y^{b+d-1}\{x,y\}

for allowable
a
,
b
,
c
,
d
.
∎

It is well known that a Poisson bracket on the polynomial algebra
\Bbbk[x,y]
is uniquely determined by
\{x,y\}
, and that every polynomial in
\Bbbk[x,y]
occurs as
\{x,y\}
for a (unique) Poisson bracket
\{-,-\}
(e.g.,
[
11
, p.235, last paragraph of §9.1.1]
). Here we note the corresponding facts for Poisson brackets on
\Bbbk(x,y)
.

Lemma 9.2
.

Let
K=\Bbbk(x,y)
with a Poisson bracket
\{-,-\}
, and set
f:=\{x,y\}
. Then
\{g,h\}=\{g,h\}_{w}f
for all
g,h\in K
.

Proof.

If
g=0
or
h=0
, the result is trivial, so assume
g,h\neq 0
.

First consider
g=x^{a}y^{b}
and
h=x^{c}y^{d}
for
a,b,c,d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}
. Applying Lemma
9.1
to both
\{-,-\}
and
\{-,-\}_{w}
, we obtain

\{x^{a}y^{b},x^{c}y^{d}\}=(ad-bc)x^{a+c-1}y^{b+d-1}f=\{x^{a}y^{b},x^{c}y^{d}\}_{w}f.

It immediately follows that
\{g,h\}=\{g,h\}_{w}f
for all
g,h\in\Bbbk[x,y]
.

In the general case,
g=st^{-1}
and
h=uv^{-1}
for some nonzero
s,t,u,v\in\Bbbk[x,y]
. Then

\displaystyle\{g,h\}

\displaystyle=\{st^{-1},uv^{-1}\}=\bigl(\{s,uv^{-1}\}t-s\{t,uv^{-1}\}\bigr)t^{-2}

\displaystyle=\bigl(\{s,u\}v-u\{s,v\}\bigr)v^{-2}t^{-1}-s\bigl(\{t,u\}v-u\{t,v\}\bigr)v^{-2}t^{-2}

\displaystyle=\{s,u\}v^{-1}t^{-1}-\{s,v\}uv^{-2}t^{-1}-\{t,u\}sv^{-1}t^{-2}+\{t,v\}suv^{-2}t^{-2}\,.

The same equation holds with
\{-,-\}
replaced by
\{-,-\}_{w}
, and thus, invoking
\{s,u\}=\{s,u\}_{w}f
etc., we conclude that
\{g,h\}=\{g,h\}_{w}f
.
∎

Lemma 9.3
.

Let
A
be a Poisson algebra and
f\in A
. Set
\{a,b\}^{\prime}:=\{a,b\}f
for
a,b\in A
. Then
\{-,-\}^{\prime}
is a Poisson bracket on
A
if and only if

(E9.3.1)

\bigl(\{a,b\}\{f,c\}+\{c,a\}\{f,b\}+\{b,c\}\{f,a\}\bigr)f=0\qquad\forall\;a,b,c\in A.

Proof.

It is clear that
\{-,-\}^{\prime}
is a bilinear, skew-symmetric biderivation on
A
, so it is a Poisson bracket if and only if it satisfies the Jacobi identity. Observe that

\{\{u,v\}^{\prime},w\}^{\prime}=\{\{u,v\}f,w\}f=\{\{u,v\},w\}f^{2}+\{u,v\}\{f,w\}f

for
u,v,w\in A
. Consequently,

\displaystyle\{\{a,b\}^{\prime},c\}^{\prime}+\{\{c,a\}^{\prime},b\}^{\prime}

\displaystyle+\{\{b,c\}^{\prime},a\}^{\prime}=\{\{a,b\},c\}f^{2}+\{a,b\}\{f,c\}f

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\{\{c,a\},b\}f^{2}+\{c,a\}\{f,b\}f

\displaystyle\qquad\qquad\qquad\qquad+\{\{b,c\},a\}f^{2}+\{b,c\}\{f,a\}f

\displaystyle=\{a,b\}\{f,c\}f+\{c,a\}\{f,b\}f+\{b,c\}\{f,a\}f

for
a,b,c\in A
.
Therefore
\{-,-\}^{\prime}
satisfies the Jacobi identity if and only if (
E9.3.1
) holds.
∎

Corollary 9.4
.

Let
K=\Bbbk(x,y)
and
{\mathbf{f}}\in K
. Then there is a unique Poisson bracket
\{-,-\}
on
K
such that
\{x,y\}={\mathbf{f}}
.

Proof.

Uniqueness is clear in case of existence. If such a bracket exists, it must equal
\{-,-\}_{w}{\mathbf{f}}
. We apply Lemma
9.3
with
\{-,-\}
and
\{-,-\}^{\prime}
replaced by
\{-,-\}_{w}
and
\{-,-\}_{w}{\mathbf{f}}
.

Let
a,b\in K
, and consider the derivation

\delta:=\{a,b\}_{w}\{{\mathbf{f}},-\}_{w}+\{-,a\}_{w}\{{\mathbf{f}},b\}_{w}+\{b,-\}_{w}\{{\mathbf{f}},a\}_{w}\,.

We compute that

\delta(x)=(a_{x}b_{y}-a_{y}b_{x})(-{\mathbf{f}}_{y})+a_{y}({\mathbf{f}}_{x}b_{y}-{\mathbf{f}}_{y}b_{x})+(-b_{y})({\mathbf{f}}_{x}a_{y}-{\mathbf{f}}_{y}a_{x})=0,

and similarly
\delta(y)=0
. Consequently,
\delta=0
, that is,

\{a,b\}_{w}\{{\mathbf{f}},c\}_{w}+\{c,a\}_{w}\{{\mathbf{f}},b\}_{w}+\{b,c\}_{w}\{{\mathbf{f}},a\}_{w}=0

for all
c\in K
. This verifies (
E9.3.1
) for
\{-,-\}_{w}
.
∎

Example 9.5
.

There exists a Poisson field
K
with an element
f\in K
such that the biderivation
\{-,-\}f
on
K
is not a Poisson bracket.

Take
K=\Bbbk(x_{1},x_{2},y_{1},y_{2})
to be the rank
2
Weyl Poisson field, where

\{x_{i},x_{j}\}=\{y_{i},y_{j}\}=0\qquad\text{and}\qquad\{x_{i},y_{j}\}=\delta_{ij}

for
i,j=1,2
. Observe that

\{x_{1},y_{1}\}\{x_{2},y_{2}\}+\{y_{2},x_{1}\}\{x_{2},y_{1}\}+\{y_{1},y_{2}\}\{x_{2},x_{1}\}=1,

whence (
E9.3.1
) fails with
a=x_{1}
,
b=y_{1}
,
c=y_{2}
and
f=x_{2}
. Therefore
\{-,-\}x_{2}
is not a Poisson bracket on
K
.
