The impact of systemic iron overload on retinal and choroidal microcirculation in transfusion-dependent beta-thalassemia patients
Authors: Figen Bezci Aygün, Merve Özge Algedik Tokyürek, Şule Unal, Tekin Aksu, Fatma Gümrük, Sibel Kadayıfcılar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThis study aimed to evaluate retinal and choroidal microcirculation in transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia (TDβT) patients and to explore associations between systemic iron overload and ocular structural and vascular changes.MethodsA total of 66 eyes from 33 TDβT patients and 66 eyes from 33 healthy controls were included in this case-control study. Retinal and choroidal structures were evaluated using Spectral-Domain OCT and OCT-Angiography (OCTA). Systemic iron overload was assessed using serum ferritin and MRI-T2* of the liver and heart. Liver iron load was classified by T2* values into normal, mild, and moderate. Key ocular parameters, including central macular thickness (CMT), subfoveal choroidal thickness (SCT), luminal area (LA), stromal area (SA), total choroidal area (TA), and vessel density (VD) of superficial (SCP), deep (DCP), and choriocapillaris (CC) plexi, were analyzed.ResultsChoriocapillaris VD was significantly lower in TDβT patients compared with controls (p < 0.001, p adjusted = 0.004), while ferritin levels showed positive correlations with CMT, SCP VD, and CC VD within the patient group. Patients with moderate liver iron overload showed increased trend toward LA, SA, TA, SCT, and inferior peripapillary VD, but this significance not existed after FDR correction. No significant relationship was found between iron overload and FAZ metrics.ConclusionLiver iron overload may be associated with changes in choroidal structure and perfusion in TDβT patients, although chronic anemia-related hypoxia and compensatory vascular responses may also contribute to these findings.Choroidal parameters may provide useful non-invasive indicators for monitoring ocular effects of systemic iron burden.

Introduction

Beta-thalassemia syndromes are autosomal recessive blood disorders characterized by defective beta-globin synthesis, resulting in low hemoglobin, anemia, and decreased red blood cell production. The condition is prevalent in Mediterranean countries, the Middle East, Central Asia, India, Southern China, the Far East, North Africa, and South America [1]. In Turkey, prevalenceof β-thalassemia carrier is approximately 2.1% [2], with higher rates in coastal regions such as the Marmara, Aegean, and Mediterranean areas [3–5]. Despite lower carrier rates in Southeastern Anatolia, homozygous cases are similarly frequent due to higher consanguinity and fertility rates in the region [5, 6].

Beta-thalassemia major, the most severe form, results in life-threatening anemia requiring regular blood transfusions and lifelong medical care. Ineffective erythropoiesis leads to chronic hemolytic anemia and increased intestinal iron absorption, resulting in iron overload (IOL), which is further exacerbated by regular transfusions in severe cases. The extent of iron accumulation in organs depends on individual iron loading rates and the efficacy of the employed iron chelator, which varies in its ability to target specific organs [7].

Untreated beta-thalassemia major can lead to complications such as growth retardation, hepatosplenomegaly, leg ulcers, and skeletal deformities from bone marrow expansion [1]. Regular blood transfusions can sustain normal growth until 12–13 years of age, but transfusion-related IOL poses significant risks, including liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, dilated myocardiopathy, cardiac siderosis, and left ventricular heart failure, contributing to considerable morbidity and mortality [1, 7]. While liver biopsy was historically the gold standard for detecting IOL, non-invasive MRI-based T2* techniques have become the preferred method due to their safety, reliability, and ability to assess both hepatic and cardiac iron concentrations. Current thalassemia management guidelines emphasize MRI monitoring to diagnose IOL and optimize iron chelation therapy (ICT) [7].

In beta-thalassemia patients, a wide range of ocular abnormalities have been documented such as short axial length, hyperopia, cataracts, and dry eye, as well as visual field defects and electroretinography (ERG) abnormalities. Additionally, pathophysiological changes associated with the disease can result in pseudoxanthoma elasticum (PXE)-like lesions, increased vascular tortuosity, retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) degeneration, and mottling in retina. Iron accumulation and the use of chelating agents may further contribute to RPE alterations, including butterfly-shaped or vitelliform maculopathy, RPE atrophy, and pattern dystrophy-like changes [8]. Advances in imaging modalities in ophthalmology, such as optical coherence tomography (OCT) and OCT-Angiography (OCTA), have enabled detailed, non-invasive evaluations of retinal and choroidal structure and microcirculation. OCTA, in particular, allows the assessment of superficial and deep capillary plexuses and the choriocapillaris without the need for contrast agents, providing valuable insights into microvascular alterations, offering a unique opportunityto investigate ocular alterations associated with anemia and systemic or retinal IOL in β-thalassemia patients [9–12].

In this study, we aimed to compare the retinal and choroidal microcirculation of transfusion-dependent beta-thalassemia (TDβT) patients with healthy controls and to investigate the relationship between systemic IOL parameters and retinal and choroidal structural and vascular changes. Furthermore, we sought to evaluate whether choroidal parameters could serve as non-invasive potential markers for ocular IOL in this patient population.

Methods

This case–control study included 66 eyes of 33 patients with β-thalassemia major and 66 eyes of 33 age- and sex-matched healthy controls. The study was conducted at a tertiary referral center between May 2023 and July 2024 and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Institutional Review Board approval was obtained (GO 22/338), and written informed consent was secured from all participants or legal guardians.

The β-thalassemia group consisted of transfusion-dependent patients who were followed at the pediatric hematology department since childhood, including adult patients who continued their follow-up in the same unit. Diagnosis was based on clinical history and hemoglobin electrophoresis. All patients received regular transfusions every 3–4 weeks to maintain hemoglobin levels between 9 and 12 g/dL and were treated exclusively with deferasirox (20–40 mg/kg/day) as an ICT. Laboratory parameters were recorded as the mean of the two most recent measurements obtained at least six months apart, including hemoglobin and ferritin levels. Patients were further classified according to ferritin levels (< 1000 µg/L and > 1000 µg/L). These ferritin thresholds were selected based on clinically relevant iron overload categories reported in previous thalassemia literature and management guidelines [7].

Exclusion criteria included a history of ocular surgery or trauma, uveitis, glaucoma, congenital ocular disorders, papilledema, retinal vascular disease or detachment, and systemic conditions affecting retinal or choroidal circulation. Individuals with high refractive errors (myopia ≤ − 4.0 D or hyperopia ≥ + 4.0 D) were excluded. The control group comprised healthy individuals without ocular or systemic disease and without medication use affecting retinal or choroidal measurements.

All participants underwent comprehensive ophthalmologic examination, including best-corrected visual acuity, intraocular pressure measurement, and slit-lamp biomicroscopy. Retinal and choroidal imaging was performed using spectral-domain optical coherence tomography (SD-OCT; Heidelberg Engineering) and optical coherence tomography angiography (OCTA; Zeiss Cirrus AngioPlex, version 10.0). Macular OCTA images were obtained using 6 × 6 mm scan protocols centered on the fovea. Images with significant motion artifacts or segmentation errors were excluded. Automated segmentation boundaries were reviewed and corrected manually when necessary. All measurements were performed under standardized imaging conditions by single experienced examiner between 08:00 and 12:00.

Central macular thickness was defined as the mean thickness within the central 1-mm area. Subfoveal choroidal thickness was manually measured on enhanced depth imaging OCT from the outer border of the retinal pigment epithelium to the choroid–scleral interface. Retinal nerve fiber layer thickness was obtained automatically.

Choroidal vascularity index (CVI) analysis was performed on a 1,500-µm-wide subfoveal region using Fiji software. Images were binarized using the Niblack auto-local thresholding method. Total choroidal area, luminal area, and stromal area were calculated, and CVI was defined as the ratio of luminal area to total choroidal areaOCTA was used to evaluate superficial and deep capillary plexi parameters, including perfusion density, vessel density, and foveal avascular zone metrics. Peripapillary OCTA scans were used to assess radial peripapillary capillary perfusion density and flux index within a predefined annular region. Superficial capillary and peripapillary plexi’s measurements were obtained directly from the automated OCTA device software output. In contrast, deep capillary plexus vessel density measurements were analyzed manually using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA). OCTA images were converted to 8-bit format and binarized using the Otsu thresholding method. Vessel density was then quantified from the binarized vascular signal area within the selected region of interest. All image acquisition, segmentation, image processing, and measurement procedures were performed identically in both patient and control groups under standardized conditions.Axial length related magnification was corrected using Littmann’s formula. MRI-T2* imaging (Siemens Medical Solutions, Erlangen, Germany) was employed to evaluate iron accumulation in the liver and heart prior to the onset of clinical symptoms. For liver assessments, a single breath-hold multiecho gradient echo sequence (8–16 echoes) was used, while heart imaging utilized a cardiac-gated, segmented multiecho gradient echo sequence (8 echoes) focusing on a single midpapillary ventricular short-axis slice.

Heart MRI-T2* results were categorized based on conduction times as follows: normal (> 20 ms), mild IOL (15–20 ms), moderate IOL (10–14 ms), and severe myocardial siderosis (< 10 ms). Liver MRI-T2* classifications were defined as normal (T2* >6.3 ms or < 2 mg Fe/g dry weight), mild (T2* 2.8–6.3 ms or 2–5 mg Fe/g dry weight), moderate (T2* 1.4–2.7 ms or 5–10 mg Fe/g dry weight), and severe (T2* <1.4 ms or > 10 mg Fe/g dry weight).

In this study, all patients heart MRI-T2* value classified as normal status. However, liver MRI-T2* results indicated varying levels of IOL, classified as normal, mild, or moderate.

Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS software (version 20.0, IBM). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were reported as percentages. Differences between groups based on liver MRI/T2* were analyzed using ANOVA, followed by a post-hoc Tukey test to identify significant pairwise differences between the groups. Correlations between clinical parameters (e.g., Hb, ferritin levels, heart and liver MRI-T2*) and ocular measurements were assessed using Spearman’s correlation coefficients. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Generalized estimating equations (GEE) with an exchangeable within-subject correlation structure and Gaussian family were used for all analyses to account for inter-eye correlation by Python version 3.11 (statsmodels version 0.14). Robust (sandwich) standard errors were applied. Each participant was treated as a cluster, and eyes were considered the units of observation. Multiple comparisons within each analysis family were controlled using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction at α = 0.05. Adjusted p-values are reported as p_adj. Because of the relatively limited sample size, extensive multivariable modeling was not performed to avoid model overfitting and unstable estimates.

Results

In this study, 132 eyes of 66 participants were included, comprising 66 eyes of 33 thalassemia patients and 66 eyes of 33 healthy controls. The mean age of the patients was 31.7 ± 10.0 years, while the control group had a mean age of 31.1 ± 9.2 years. There was no significant difference in age between the groups (p = 0.78). Gender distribution was the same, the female rate was 51.5% in both groups. In terms of retinal findings, all eyes in the thalassemia group exhibited normal retinal examination results, PXE-like lesions, RPE degeneration or vascular tortuosity were not detected. However, none of these associations survived after GEE analyses FDR correction (LA, SA and TA p_adj = 0.074, SCT p_adj = 0.281, inferior RPCPD p_adj = 0.277). Discussion

This study investigated retinal and choroidal microcirculation in TDβT patients and examined the relationship between systemic IOL parameters and ocular structural and vascular changes. The only significant difference between the patient and control groups was the reduced vessel density at the choriocapillaris level in the patient group. Additionally, in the patient group, choroidal thickness and specific choroidal parameters, including LA, SA, and TA, showed a negative correlation trend with liver MRI-T2*, while serum ferritin levels were positively and statistically correlated with SCP perfusion, VD, and CMT. These findings suggest a potential association between systemic IOL and retinal/choroidal vascular and structural parameters.

The reduced CC VD observed in thalassemia patients despite preserved choroidal thickness suggests that choroidal microvascular dysfunction plays a key role in ocular involvement. The positive correlations between CC VD and ferritin levels may suggest that choroidal circulation, particularly at the choriocapillaris level, participates in compensatory mechanisms. Additionally, elevated ferritin levels may be associated with activation of hypoxia-related pathways and compensatory retinal and choroidal vascular remodeling [13]. This hypothesis is further supported by the positive associations between ferritin levels, superficial capillary plexus perfusion, vessel density, and central macular thickness. Although CC VD was significantly lower in TDβT patients compared with controls, ferritin levels showed a positive correlation with CC VD within the patient group. This apparent discrepancy may reflect compensatory microvascular responses in earlier stages of iron overload, heterogeneity in disease severity, or the modulatory effects of iron chelation therapy. Furthermore, ferritin is an acute-phase reactant and may not reliably represent tissue-specific iron accumulation, particularly in the choroid. The absence of a significant correlation between liver T2* and CC VD further supports the possibility that systemic and ocular iron burden may not parallel each other directly. In addition, the relatively small sample size and the absence of severe cardiac siderosis in our cohort may have limited the detection of significant MRI-T2*–related associations. A potential non-linear or biphasic relationship between iron overload and choriocapillaris perfusion cannot be excluded, with compensatory vascular responses occurring in earlier stages and capillary loss predominating in more advanced stages. Since our cohort included relatively few patients with severe hepatic siderosis, further evaluation of this hypothesis was limited.

Consistent with our findings, Koçtekin et al. [10] reported positive correlations between ferritin levels and deep capillary plexus vessel density in parafoveal and perifoveal regions. In contrast, Cennamo et al. [12] and AttaAllah et al. [14] demonstrated negative associations between elevated ferritin levels and choriocapillaris or macular vessel density, particularly at higher ferritin thresholds. Other studies reported no significant relationship between ferritin levels and ocular vascular parameters [9, 11].

These discrepancies suggest that the impact of ferritin on retinal and choroidal vasculature may vary according to disease severity, systemic iron burden, and the balance between adaptive and pathological vascular responses. Moreover, ferritin may not always accurately reflect tissue iron accumulation, as direct measurement is limited to invasive methods such as biopsy [15]. Longitudinal ferritin trends rather than single measurements may better represent total body iron burden [16]. Accordingly, tissue-based assessments of iron overload, including MRI-T2* measurements, may provide a more reliable evaluation of chronic iron toxicity [17].

In TDβT, systemic iron overload predominantly affects the liver and typically precedes cardiac involvement. Initially, iron accumulates in Kupffer cells and portal macrophages and later redistributes to hepatocytes and mesenchymal cells, resulting in heterogeneous hepatic deposition [18]. Similarly, histochemical studies have shown non-uniform retinal iron distribution, with predominant localization in the choroid, RPE, and photoreceptors [19]. However, in vivo evidence regarding retinal iron toxicity remains limited.Menghini et al. [20] reported no structural retinal degeneration in patients with hemochromatosis using multimodal imaging, whereas animal studies have demonstrated iron accumulation mainly in the RPE and choroid, with histopathologic changes resembling age-related macular degeneration [21]. In the present study, heart MRI-T2* values were normal in all patients, precluding evaluation of cardiac iron–ocular relationships. In contrast, hepatic iron overload showed nonsignificant associations with choroidal parameters after statistical correction. Although patients with moderate hepatic iron overload tended to demonstrate larger luminal, stromal, and total choroidal areas, these associations did not remain statistically significant after false discovery rate correction. Similarly, subfoveal choroidal thickness appeared greater in the moderate overload group; however, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the exploratory nature of the analyses and the limited number of patients with moderate hepatic siderosis. These findings may reflect the combined effects of tissue iron accumulation, chronic anemia-related hypoxia, compensatory vascular responses, and iron chelation therapy on choroidal structure.Whether these changes reflect direct ocular iron deposition remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Iron overload is well known to impair vascular integrity and contribute to vasculopathy through structural and functional changes in the vascular wall, as demonstrated in cell culture and animal models. These changes include medial hypertrophy, calcification, fibrosis, endothelial dysfunction, and accelerated atherosclerosis [22]. In β-thalassemia major, chronic iron overload has been associated with impaired endothelium-dependent and independent vasodilation, indicating injury to both endothelial cells and vascular smooth muscle [23]. In contrast, our study demonstrated positive correlations between ferritin levels and superficial capillary plexus perfusion, vessel density, and choriocapillaris vessel density. This apparent discrepancy may reflect the complex and tissue-specific effects of iron metabolism in ocular tissues. Moreover, the use of consistent ICT in our cohort may have modulated these vascular effects. Previous studies have shown that ICT can improve nitric oxide–mediated endothelial vasodilation [24]. Accordingly, ICT may mitigate iron-induced vascular dysfunction, potentially explaining the preserved or enhanced ocular perfusion observed in our patients.

Cennamo et al. [12] reported significantly reduced vessel density in the superficial and deep capillary plexuses and the choriocapillaris in β-thalassemia major patients compared with healthy controls. Similarly, Koçtekin et al. [10] observed decreased SCP vessel density in foveal and radial peripapillary regions, along with reduced choriocapillaris flow area. In contrast, Kazancı et al. [11], studying a younger cohort, found no significant differences in SCP, DCP, or CC vessel densities.In the present study, only CC vessel density was significantly reduced among OCTA parameters. These may be related to differences in age, disease duration, severity of systemic iron overload, and chelation regimens. Although the DCP is often considered the most vulnerable plexus under hypoxic conditions, our findings suggest that the choriocapillaris may also be particularly susceptible.

Güvenç et al. [9] reported increased SCP, DCP, and peripapillary vessel densities in transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia patients, attributing these findings to endothelial dysfunction, angiogenesis, and chelation-related capillary expansion, whereas non–transfusion-dependent patients exhibited reduced foveal flow area and SCP vessel density due to chronic mild anemia. In line with these observations, our study found no significant differences in SCP, DCP, or peripapillary vessel densities between patients and controls. Moreover, systemic iron overload was in a nonsignificant relation with increased choroidal structural parameters (LA, SA, TA, and SCT) and inferior peripapillary RPCPD, while ferritin levels correlated positively with CMT and CC vessel density, supporting the contribution of these mechanisms.

Research on the effects of systemic iron overload on retinal and choroidal circulation remains limited. One study reported no association between MRI-T2* values and FAZ area [9], whereas another found a more circular and less complex deep capillary plexus FAZ in TDβT patients, potentially related to iron overload and desferrioxamine dosage [25]. In the present study, FAZ area, perimeter, and circularity showed no significant association with ferritin levels or systemic iron overload. Although FAZ enlargement is considered an early marker of retinal capillary dropout [26], findings in thalassemia patients are inconsistent [9–11]. To our knowledge, this is among the first studies to evaluate the relationship between systemic iron overload assessed by MRI-T2* and ocular structural and vascular parameters measured by OCT and OCTA in patients with transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia.

This study’s strengths include the combined use of OCT and OCTA to evaluate retinal and choroidal structure and microcirculation, together with systemic iron overload markers such as serum ferritin and MRI-T2*. A well-matched control group and a homogeneous cohort of transfusion-dependent β-thalassemia patients receiving standardized deferasirox therapy enhance data reliability. Correlations between ferritin and cardiac and hepatic MRI-T2* further support the validity of systemic iron assessment.

Limitations include the small sample size, cross-sectional design, single-center setting, and absence of patients with severe hepatic iron overload, which may limit further exploration of potential non-linear relationships between iron burden and choriocapillaris perfusion. Transfusion-related immune alterations [27], not assessed in this study, may also influence vascular responses and should be explored in future research. The homogeneous nature of the cohort, consisting exclusively of transfusion-dependent patients receiving deferasirox therapy and having normal heart T2* values, may limit the generalizability of the findings to other thalassemia populations. Several factors, including age, axial length, hemoglobin level, disease duration, transfusion burden may influence retinal and choroidal vascular parameters; therefore, residual confounding cannot be excluded. In addition, the wide age range of the participants may have introduced variability in OCTA measurements, and age or axial lengthbased subgroup analyses were not performed because of small sample size and concerns regarding further reduction of statistical power.

This study suggests that systemic IOL in TDβT patients may be associated with alterations in ocular vascular and structural parameters.The correlations between hepatic IOL and choroidal structural changes suggest that choroidal parameters may potentially serve as non-invasive markersfor monitoring iron-related complications.Additionally, positive associations between ferritin and retinal vascular parameters highlight the complex interplay between systemic iron metabolism and ocular health. Further research with larger cohorts and longitudinal designs is needed to better understand the mechanisms and long-term effects of systemic iron overload on ocular tissues.
