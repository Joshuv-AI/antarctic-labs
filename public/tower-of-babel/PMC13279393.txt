Clinical outcomes of internal iliosciatic fixation using a conventional reconstruction plate via an anterior approach for acetabular fractures involving the posterior column
Authors: Haolan Xiong, Hongfen Chen, Shenglu Cao, Xiaohua Chen, Fuming Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAcetabular fractures involving the posterior column remain surgically challenging because of the complex three-dimensional anatomy and restricted operative corridor. As an alternative to traditional posterior approaches, internal iliosciatic fixation through an anterior approach has gained increasing acceptance. Reconstruction plates, which offer the advantages of low cost and convenient intraoperative bending, are extensively used in clinical settings.MethodsA retrospective case series was conducted on 36 consecutive patients with acetabular fractures involving the posterior column who underwent internal iliosciatic fixation using an intraoperatively contoured reconstruction plate between July 2019 and November 2022. The modified Stoppa approach (n = 26) or the pararectus approach (n = 10) was employed. Fracture reduction was assessed using the modified Matta criteria, and hip function was evaluated using the modified Merle d’Aubigné-Postel score at one-year follow-up. Operative time, blood loss, fracture union, and complications were recorded.ResultsThe mean age of the patients was 42.6 ± 14.5 years (range: 21–71 years), with 25 males and 11 females. Mechanisms of injury included traffic accidents (n = 21), falls from height (n = 10), skiing injuries (n = 3), and others (n = 2). According to the Letournel-Judet classification, there were 13 both-column fractures, 11 anterior column posterior hemitransverse (ACPHT) fractures, 9 T-shaped fractures, and 3 transverse fractures. The mean operative time was 108 ± 31 min (range: 70–180 min), and mean intraoperative blood loss was 430 ± 120.9 mL (range: 200–1100 mL). Fracture reduction was rated as excellent in 27 patients (75%), good in 5 (14%), and fair in 4 (11%). At a mean follow-up of 14.4 ± 3.2 months (range: 12–24 months), the Merle d’Aubigné-Postel score was excellent in 26 patients (72%), good in 7 (19%), and fair in 3 (8%). All fractures achieved union, with a mean healing time of 3.7 months. Complications included transient obturator nerve injury in three patients (all resolved within three months) and incisional fat liquefaction in two obese patients. No deep infection, implant failure, or heterotopic ossification was observed.ConclusionInternal iliosciatic fixation using a conventional reconstruction plate via an anterior approach yields satisfactory fracture reduction and functional outcomes in patients with acetabular fractures involving the posterior column or quadrilateral plate. This technique is reliable and cost-effective, making it particularly suitable for resource-limited settings where pre-contoured anatomical plates or 3D-printed implants are not readily available.

Introduction

Acetabular fractures involving the posterior column present substantial technical challenges due to the complex three-dimensional anatomy, constrained surgical corridor, and proximity to critical neurovascular structures[1, 2]. Anatomical reduction with stable internal fixation remains essential for hip function recovery and prevention of post-traumatic arthritis. Conventional posterior approaches (e.g., the Kocher-Langenbeck approach) offer good fracture visualization but require extensive soft tissue dissection and carry risks of heterotopic ossification and nerve injury[3].

In recent decades, internal iliosciatic fixation—plate application along the medial cortex of the posterior column (iliosciatic buttress)—has emerged as an attractive anterior-based strategy for posterior column stabilization, avoiding the need for a separate posterior incision[4, 5]. This technique is particularly valuable when the quadrilateral plate is disrupted with medial femoral head displacement. The modified Stoppa or pararectus approach provides excellent exposure of the true pelvis, allowing direct manipulation and rigid fixation on the internal iliac fossa and quadrilateral surface[6].

Advances in digital orthopedics have spurred interest in patient-specific implants. Three-dimensional printed and anatomically pre-contoured plates theoretically offer optimal bone apposition, reduced operative time, and improved reduction accuracy[7–9]. However, customized implants require specialized facilities, incur high costs, and involve preoperative manufacturing delays—unsuitable for acute trauma. Anatomically pre-shaped plates, though commercially available, are often based on limited cadaveric data and may not accommodate wide anatomical variations; they are also expensive.

Conversely, conventional reconstruction plates provide a pragmatic, cost-effective alternative. They are universally available, inexpensive, immediately usable, and intraoperatively malleable. Stainless steel or titanium reconstruction plates can be readily contoured to the irregular iliosciatic pillar and quadrilateral surface, achieving individualized fit without the logistical and economic burdens of bespoke implants.

Given fiscal constraints and the need for accessible interventions, We routinely perform internal iliosciatic fixation with standard reconstruction plates for selected posterior column acetabular fractures. This approach, in our hands, yields satisfactory clinical and radiographic outcomes, offering superior cost-effectiveness and operative efficiency. This consecutive series reports on clinical efficacy, radiological parameters, and functional recovery.

Study population

Medical records of consecutive patients who underwent internal iliosciatic fixation using standard reconstruction plates for acetabular fractures, especially those involving the posterior column, between July 2019 and November 2022 were retrospectively reviewed. Inclusion criteria were: (1) fresh acetabular fractures involving the posterior column, defined as an injury-to-surgery interval of ≤ 3 weeks; and (2) presence of associated anterior column or quadrilateral plate involvement allowed but not required. Exclusion criteria included: (1) old fractures (injury-to-surgery interval > 3 weeks); (2) isolated posterior wall fractures; and (3) cases in which neither the modified Stoppa approach nor the pararectus (lateral rectus) approach was feasible.

Procedure

This study included 36 patients whose fractures were classified according to the Letournel-Judet system. Preoperative pelvic X-ray images, including the anteroposterior view, oblique view of the injured iliac bone, and obturator oblique view, were obtained for all patients. In addition, pelvic computed tomography (CT) images and three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction data were used to evaluate the fracture type and guide the selection of the surgical approach. However, for patients who underwent consultation surgery at outside hospitals, some did not have standard imaging data available due to limitations of local medical conditions.

A single modified Stoppa approach or the lateral rectus abdominis approach combined with an internal iliosciatic plate was used to fix acetabular fractures involving the posterior column. When the Stoppa approach was used, a combined iliac fossa approach was not required unless a high anterior column fracture was present. Based on preoperative CT images, the fracture lines were marked on a pelvic model, and the plate was pre-bent and sterilized beforehand to save operative time. Surgical method

All procedures were performed under general anesthesia. Following induction, the patient was placed in the supine position with a triangular pillow under the knees to maintain flexion of both the hips and knees. In the earlier period, 26 cases were treated via the Stoppa approach, while in the later period, 10 cases underwent the lateral rectus abdominis approach. For acetabular fractures involving the quadrilateral plate, reconstruction plates were consistently used for internal fixation in all cases.

A midline infraumbilical incision was made, and the skin and subcutaneous tissue were divided. The linea alba was incised longitudinally, and the rectus abdominis muscles were retracted bilaterally with preservation of their distal insertions. The lower abdominal wall muscles, external iliac vessels, femoral nerve, and iliopsoas muscle were retracted anterolaterally, while the extraperitoneal pelvic viscera were displaced posteromedially. During exposure, attention was paid to aberrant extraperitoneal communicating vascular branches along the true pelvic brim, extending from the pubic symphysis to the sacroiliac joints. In six patients in this series, arterial anastomotic branches between the inferior epigastric artery and the obturator artery, along with their accompanying veins, were identified. When such a vessel—commonly referred to as the “corona mortis”—was present, it was isolated and divided.

The iliopectineal fascia was incised, and subperiosteal dissection was performed to expose the fracture site. Fracture reduction was achieved using reduction forceps, bone levers, and lower limb traction. Provisional fixation was carried out with Kirschner wires or screws. A contoured reconstruction plate was then placed on the medial surface of the quadrilateral plate; when necessary, additional plating was applied along the arcuate line or within the iliac fossa after appropriate bending. Intraoperative fluoroscopy (C-arm) was used to confirm fracture reduction and to verify the position and length of the screws, ensuring that no screw entered the acetabulum. Associated sacroiliac joint injuries or iliac wing fractures were reduced and stabilized via the same exposure combined with an iliac fossa approach.

The integrity of the peritoneum was checked. A negative-pressure drain was placed in the retropubic space or on the medial or posterior aspect of the acetabulum. The rectus sheath was closed with interrupted sutures, followed by skin closure.

Postoperative treatment

After surgery, the affected limb was placed in a slightly elevated position with moderate flexion of the hip and knee to reduce soft tissue tension and alleviate pain. During incision closure, reconstruction of the rectus abdominis attachment at the pubic symphysis was performed. Thereafter, routine layer-by-layer closure was carried out. A negative-pressure drain was placed in the retropubic space and was removed when the daily drainage volume was less than 50 mL. Antibiotics were administered for 24 h. Pain was controlled with intravenous parecoxib and oral celecoxib. Low molecular weight heparin was used to prevent deep vein thrombosis. In addition, active and passive range-of-motion exercises of the lower extremities were performed postoperatively to provide mechanical venous thromboprophylaxis. At one week after surgery, the patient began walking with crutches without weight-bearing on the affected limb. Partial weight-bearing was initiated at 6 weeks, and full weight-bearing at 12 weeks.

Results

Among the 36 patients, 25 were male and 11 were female, with a mean age of 42.6 ± 14.5 years (range: 21–71 years). The causes of injury were traffic accidents in 21 cases, falls from height in 10 cases, skiing injuries in 3 cases, and other injuries in 2 cases. According to the Letournel-Judet classification, there were 13 both-column fractures, 11 anterior column posterior hemitransverse (ACPHT) fractures, 9 T-shaped fractures, and 3 transverse fractures. The left hip was involved in 14 cases and the right hip in 22 cases. The mean time from injury to surgery was 6.5 ± 2.6 days (range: 4–17 days) (Table 1). The mean operative time was 108 ± 31 min (range: 70–180 min), and the mean intraoperative blood loss was 430 ± 120.9 mL (range: 200–1100 mL). Discussion

This study evaluated the clinical outcomes of internal iliosciatic fixation using a conventional reconstruction plate for the treatment of acetabular fractures involving the posterior column. Acetabular fractures with posterior column involvement predominantly occur in middle-aged and young adults, often resulting from high-energy trauma such as motor vehicle accidents or falls from height, and they remain a challenging entity in orthopedic trauma surgery. Our results demonstrate that reconstruction plates offer significant advantages in restoring acetabular anatomy and improving patient function.

In complex acetabular fractures involving the posterior column, achieving anatomical reduction of the articular surface is crucial to minimize the risk of post-traumatic osteoarthritis and subsequent conversion to total hip arthroplasty (THA)[10]. In the present study, fixation with reconstruction plates resulted in satisfactory reduction in the majority of patients according to the modified Matta radiological criteria. This finding is consistent with the existing literature, which supports the concept of medial buttress plating of the pelvis as a means of providing adequate mechanical stability. Although biomechanical studies[11] have suggested that novel integrated anatomical plates may achieve less displacement and greater stiffness compared with conventional reconstruction plates, the reconstruction plate demonstrates high flexibility in adapting to variable fracture patterns.

In recent years, anatomical locking plates[12] and 3D-printed patient-specific implants[7, 8, 10] have gained increasing clinical application. Studies have reported that anatomical locking plates can shorten operative time, reduce intraoperative blood loss, and improve the excellent/good rate of Harris Hip Scores compared with conventional reconstruction plates. In addition, 3D-printed virtual surgical planning and patient-specific osteosynthesis plates enable more accurate fracture reduction and screw placement, particularly in complex fractures. However, the fit of pre-contoured anatomical plates varies with patient sex and height; specifically, all female pelvic shapes and shorter males (height < 175 cm) show only a moderate fit, often requiring substantial intraoperative bending[13]. In contrast, reconstruction plates can be pre-bent and sterilized before surgery, allowing immediate intraoperative use without reliance on expensive preoperative manufacturing facilities.

In resource-constrained healthcare environments, cost-effectiveness is a key determinant of treatment selection. Although 3D-printing technology can significantly shorten operative time and reduce complications, its high cost and dependence on advanced imaging equipment limit its widespread application[14]. The present study underscores the practical value of reconstruction plates: they are substantially less expensive than locking plates or custom-made implants and do not require complex preoperative planning. Studies[15] from low-income countries, such as Ethiopia, have shown that even without intraoperative fluoroscopy (C-arm), open reduction and internal fixation using conventional reconstruction plates can achieve favorable functional outcomes. This demonstrates that when technical or financial resources are limited, skilled use of standard reconstruction plates can still achieve ideal treatment goals.

This study employed the modified Matta criteria and the modified Merle d’Aubigné-Postel score for outcome assessment. Our results show that most patients achieve excellent or good functional recovery by one year of follow-up, and functional outcomes are clearly correlated with the quality of reduction. Notably, although anatomical radiographic reduction remains the gold standard, elderly patients may tolerate minor articular incongruity better, and the relationship between functional outcome and radiographic appearance is not strictly linear.

Conclusion

In summary, internal iliosciatic fixation using a conventional reconstruction plate is a reliable and cost-effective option for the treatment of acetabular fractures involving the posterior column. Although novel anatomical plates and 3D-printing technologies offer advantages in precision, the reconstruction plate—with its excellent versatility, reliable fixation, and superior cost-effectiveness—remains irreplaceable in clinical practice, particularly in resource-limited settings.
