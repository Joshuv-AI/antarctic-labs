Biochemical profiles and precipitating factors of diabetic ketoacidosis among type 1 and type 2 diabetes patients: a retrospective analysis from Syria
Authors: Mohammad Basheer Alameer, Anas Bitar, Ghaith Alhajjar, Mhd Wajih Kharbutly, Mhd Haitham Bastati, Zuhair Hamoush, Zaynab Alourfi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is a life-threatening metabolic emergency characterized by hyperglycemia, ketosis, and acidosis due to insulin deficiency and counterregulatory hormone imbalances. Its prevalence is notably higher in the Arab world than in Western regions. Understanding biochemical differences between type 1 and type 2 diabetes patients with DKA is essential for individualized treatment strategies. This study examines the biochemical profiles and precipitating factors of DKA in Syrian patients, assessing differences between initial and recurrent episodes amid local healthcare challenges.MethodsThis retrospective study has ethical approval from Damascus University and Damascus Hospital, allowing access to patient charts. The study included individuals diagnosed with diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and admitted to Damascus Hospital. Of 997 patients, 464 charts were excluded due to missing data or uncertain diagnoses. Patients were classified into first-time and recurrent DKA, with only initial episodes considered for analysis. Diagnosis criteria varied by age group, ensuring standardized classification. Insulin omission and unknown precipitating factors were common, especially in recurrent DKA cases.ConclusionsIn conclusion, DKA as the first presentation of diabetes is prevalent among Syrian patients. Background

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) is a hyperglycemic emergency, which usually presents as hyperglycemia, ketosis, and acidosis with an anion gap, driven by imbalance in the action of insulin and its counterregulatory hormones, mainly glucagon, cortisol, catecholamines, and growth hormone, which leads to the aforementioned presentation [1]. The prevalence of DKA among children and adolescents is 29.9% across Europe, the U.S., Australia, and New Zealand [2], with reports from the Center of Disease Control (CDC) indicating an increase in cases between 2000 and 2014 [3]. In contrast, the prevalence of DKA is much higher in the Arab world, with an overall prevalence of 46.7% of patients with type 1 diabetes and rates ranging from 17% to 100% [4]. Moreover, the prevalence of DKA among adult patients with type 1 diabetes ranges from 50 to 100 events per 1000 adults [5], while 28.6% of all DKA events can occur in patients with type 2 diabetes [6].

DKA causes water and electrolyte imbalance and hyperosmolarity, which in turn causes loss of consciousness and renal failure, making the condition life-threatening if not treated rapidly and effectively [1, 7]. Moreover, DKA patients’ management depends on assessing their biochemical profile with laboratory tests [1], which highlights the importance of understanding this profile and the factors that may affect it. Furthermore, there are many precipitating factors of DKA episodes, such as insulin insufficiency and infections, and these factors may influence clinical presentation and test results [8].

Type 1 and type 2 diabetes are distinct in their pathophysiology, which influences the biochemical profile of these patients [9, 10]. For instance, electrolyte levels such as potassium, sodium, and chloride may vary significantly between patients of Type 1 and Type 2 diabetes during DKA episodes [11]. Additionally, patients with Type 2 diabetes tend to have higher blood glucose levels and poorer kidney function [12], which can worsen electrolyte imbalances. Understanding these differences can help predict patient outcomes, identify complications, and improve treatment strategies, such as fluid therapy, insulin dosing, and electrolyte replacement [12–14], which could inform future therapeutic approaches.

A study conducted in Syria investigated the precipitating factors, outcomes, and recurrence of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) among Syrians [15]; however, it did not examine their biochemical profiles. Meanwhile, previous research outside Syria has analyzed differences in biochemical parameters between patients with type 1 and type 2 diabetes [11, 12] and compared laboratory values between initial and recurrent DKA episodes [16, 17]. These studies did not focus on the Syrian population, which faces unique healthcare challenges, highlighting the need for further exploration within this context.

This study aimed to examine the biochemical characteristics of Syrian patients with type 1 and type 2 diabetes during episodes of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), analyze differences between patients of type 1 and type 2 diabetes, assess variations between initial and recurrent DKA episodes, and examine precipitating factors of DKA episodes among Syrian patients in Damascus.

Methods

This is a retrospective study. Ethical approval was obtained from the ethical boards at Damascus University (ID number: MD-241023–135) and Syrian Private University after submitting a detailed research proposal. The research team was also granted permission by the ethical board at Damascus Hospital to access patients’ charts. We conducted this study in accordance with the ethical guidelines set out in the Declaration of Helsinki.

The inclusion criteria consisted of patients’ charts of those who were diagnosed with DKA and admitted to Damascus Hospital, which is a public sector hospital in Damascus, Syria, between 2016 and 2023. A total of 997 patients were admitted during that period. These patients’ charts were reviewed. However, 464 charts were excluded based on the following exclusion criteria:

Patients were categorized into two groups based on their medical history: those experiencing their first episode of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) and those with recurrent episodes. For individuals admitted multiple times within the timeframe of the study, only their initial DKA episode was included in the analysis to ensure consistency and eliminate duplication.

Precipitating factors were categorized according to the hospital department where patients were admitted, based on any preexisting conditions identified prior to their diagnosis of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). For instance, upper gastrointestinal (GI) bleeding was classified under gastrointestinal tract (GIT)-related factors, while strokes were grouped under neurological factors. Factors that could not be diagnosed clinically or with available tests were classified as unknown.

All tests were done on venous blood, except for arterial blood gases and pH, which were done on arterial blood. Samples were drawn from patients upon admission. The values analyzed were the patients’ first lab values after admission. Blood samples were tested in the hospital’s central laboratory.

The research team manually extracted the following data from patients’ paper charts: gender, age, hospital length of stay, type of diabetes, time of diabetes diagnosis, intrahospital mortality, precipitating factors of DKA episode, and laboratory values upon admission of the following: glucose, pH, bicarbonate, PCO2, Na, K, Cl, Cr, urea, white blood cell count (WBCs), and hemoglobin (Hb). The collected data were recorded using an online Google form and later transformed into an Excel sheet.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as frequencies and percentages for categorical variables and as medians with interquartile ranges (25th–75th percentiles) for continuous variables, as appropriate. Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with the aim of identifying differences in clinical characteristics and admission laboratory values between patients with type 1 and type 2 diabetes presenting with DKA, as well as between those experiencing a first DKA episode and those with recurrent episodes.

The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare continuous variables between independent groups, given the non-normal distribution of the data. The chi-squared test was employed to assess associations between categorical variables. Given 48 comparisons, we applied a Bonferroni correction by setting the statistical significance threshold at p < 0.00104 (0.05/48). Therefore, only p-values below 0.001 were considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 533 hospital admissions were analyzed, of which 263 (49.3%) represented a patient’s first episode of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). Among these, 126 patients (47.9%) had type 1 diabetes. Additionally, 71 patients (26.9%) received their initial diabetes diagnosis after their first DKA episode. Within this subgroup, 58 patients (81.7%) were diagnosed with type 1 diabetes after admission, representing 22% of all patients admitted with DKA for the first time, while 13 patients (18.3%) were diagnosed with type 2 diabetes. Among the study population, 58.3% were female, with 57.7% of Type 1 diabetes patients and 59.6% of Type 2 diabetes patients being female. Male patients accounted for 41.7%, distributed as 42.3% for Type 1 and 40.4% for Type 2. No statistically significant difference was observed in gender distribution (p = 0.67). The overall in-hospital mortality rate was 8.4%, with higher rates observed in Type 2 diabetes patients (16.0%) compared to Type 1 diabetes patients (4.3%) (p < 0.001). The median age for the entire sample was 27.0 years (IQR: 16.0–50.0), with Type 1 diabetes patients having a median age of 18.0 years (IQR: 14.0–25.0) and Type 2 diabetes patients a median age of 56.5 years (IQR: 48.0–67.0), showing a statistically significant difference (p < 0.001). Statistically significant differences were identified for pH, bicarbonate, creatinine, urea, and hemoglobin levels, while glucose, sodium, potassium, pCO₂, length of hospital stay, and chloride did not show significant differences. Further details are in Table 1.

The gender distribution did not differ significantly between first episodes (57.9% female, 42.1% male) and recurrent episodes (57.5% female, 42.5% male) (p = 0.94). Mortality rates were similar for first episodes (4.8%) and recurrent episodes (4.1%), showing no significant difference (p = 0.78). Statistically significant differences were observed in age and years since diagnosis of diabetes (p < 0.001). Further details are in Table 2.

Comparison of first and recurrent DKA episodes in patients with type 2 diabetes

No significant differences in gender distribution were noted between first episodes (59.9% female, 40.1% male) and recurrent episodes (58.8% female, 41.2% male) (p = 0.90). Mortality rates did not vary significantly between first episodes (14.6%) and recurrent episodes (19.6%) (p = 0.40). Significant difference was observed in years since diagnosis (p < 0.001), while other variables did not show significant changes. Further details are in Table 3.

Precipitating factors

Precipitating factors for DKA in type 1 and type 2 diabetes patients (Fig. 2)

The distribution of precipitating factors for diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) in Type 1 diabetes patients is as follows: The cause was unknown in 53.0% of cases. Gastrointestinal (GIT) issues accounted for 25.8%, followed by infections at 7.5% and respiratory conditions at 9.6%. Neurological and renal precipitating factors were less common, each contributing 0.9% of cases. while 2.3% of cases were attributed to other causes.

In Type 2 diabetes patients, the distribution of precipitating factors for DKA showed distinct patterns. Unknown causes were reported in 37.8% of cases. GIT issues contributed to 29.8%, and infections were observed in 14.4% of cases. Respiratory conditions accounted for 5.9%, while neurological, cardiological, and renal causes were identified in 2.7%, 4.3%, and 1.6% of cases, respectively. Malignancy-related causes were present in 2.1%, and other factors accounted for 1.6% of cases.

Precipitating factors for first and recurrent DKA episodes in type 1 diabetes patients (Fig. 3)

Among Type 1 diabetes patients with a first DKA episode, 38.9% of cases were of unknown etiology, and gastrointestinal issues also contributed 38.9%. Respiratory conditions accounted for 7.9%, while infections constituted 8.7%. Neurological and renal causes were identified in 1.6% of cases each. Other causes accounted for 2.4%.

For recurrent DKA episodes, unknown causes were more prevalent at 61.2%, while gastrointestinal issues accounted for 18.3%. Respiratory and infectious causes were identified in 10.5% and 6.8%, respectively. Neurological and renal issues were rare, contributing to 0.5% each, and other causes accounted for 2.3%.

Precipitating factors for first and recurrent DKA episodes in type 2 diabetes patients (Fig. 4)

In Type 2 diabetes patients with a first DKA episode, 40.1% of cases were due to unknown causes, and gastrointestinal issues contributed 24.8%. Infections accounted for 18.2%, respiratory issues for 5.1%, and cardiological conditions for 2.9%. Neurological and tumor-related causes were identified in 2.2% and 2.9% of cases, respectively, while renal issues contributed to 2.2%, and other causes contributed to 1.5%.

In recurrent DKA episodes among Type 2 diabetes patients, unknown causes decreased to 31.4%, while gastrointestinal causes rose to 43.1%. Infections constituted 3.9%, and respiratory and cardiological issues each accounted for 7.8%. Neurological and tumor-related causes were not observed, while other factors accounted for 1.9%.

Discussion

DKA episodes are a serious complication of diabetes that might be the first presentation of the disease, especially among type 1 patients [20]. In our sample, about 27% of all patients who were diagnosed with DKA for the first time had an episode before being diagnosed with diabetes; most of them were type 1 diabetics. A previous study from the U.S. estimated that 30–40% of all diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) cases occur in individuals who are later diagnosed with diabetes [21], while a study from Jordan reported 31.7% [22], and studies from Saudi Arabia, Kuwait, Taiwan, Romania, and Canada reported frequencies of 44.9%, 31.1%, 65%, 67%, and 18.6%, respectively [23]. There are many factors that contribute to this high prevalence, such as physicians’ low suspicion of diabetes among young children when they present with classical symptoms of diabetes, the increased susceptibility to dehydration and acidosis at this age, aggressive destruction of beta cells, and healthcare access barriers, which is the dominant factor in Syria. A part of the sample comprised patients who were diagnosed with type 2 diabetes following a DKA episode. Several studies have reported an increasing trend in type 2 diabetes diagnoses occurring after DKA over the past decade [24]. For instance, a study conducted in Qatar revealed that 40% of type 2 diabetes patients were diagnosed after experiencing a DKA episode [14]. This late diagnosis can largely be attributed to a lack of awareness regarding diabetes symptoms, which delays timely recognition and intervention. Implementing awareness campaigns—whether directed at the general public or targeted at individuals during clinical visits—represents a vital strategy to address this issue.

The age group of 10 to 20 years old was the most prevalent for first-time DKA patients. Similar results were reported in an Iranian study [25]. The average age for type 1 diabetes diagnosis is around this age, which explains this result [26]. There is another spike of cases between the ages of 45 and 60 among patients with type 2 diabetes, which is close to the results reported in a Chinese study [27]. There are several reasons for this spike, such as delayed diagnosis of diabetes, multiple co-morbidities, insulin production decline, and medication non-adherence [1, 24, 28, 29]. In contrast, patients aged 12–17 were more likely to have multiple episodes. Previous research reports that individuals aged between 10 and 30 have the highest rate of recurrent DKA, mainly due to poor glycemic control, insulin omission, and socioeconomic factors, which may have had a more significant influence on the patients in our sample [15, 30, 31].

Type 2 diabetes patients had higher mortality rates and longer hospital stays. Several comorbid conditions—such as anemia observed in some participants—as well as age, significantly contribute to explaining these findings. Moreover, type 2 diabetics may present with more severe cases of DKA and have renal dysfunction due to diabetic nephropathy, which is evident by higher values of urea and creatinine among them compared to type 1 patients. Lastly, limited access to healthcare, which may result in a delayed diagnosis, is an important aspect to examine in Syria, especially after over a decade of armed conflicts [15, 27, 32–34].

Patients with type 1 diabetes were more acidotic than type 2 patients, as they had lower pH and lower bicarbonate values. Type 1 diabetes patients are usually younger, and they exhibit a unique pathophysiology of insulin deficiency, increased ketone production, lack of metabolic compensation with limited ability to buffer acid loads effectively, and delayed diagnosis due to vague symptoms, all of which contribute to a more acidotic state [28, 35]. pCO2 values were also lower in type 1 patients, which indicates that more CO2 is being used to feed the buffer system, which supports a more acidotic state among them [36].

The precipitating factor for DKA was unknown for most patients of both types of diabetes. It is important to mention that insulin omission wasn’t clearly mentioned in patients’ charts, which means that most unknown factors could be insulin omission or inappropriate insulin dosage. In previous studies, insulin omission and treatment non-compliance were the most prevalent precipitating factors of DKA [37, 38]. In contrast, a previous study from Syria reported infection as the most precipitating factor, followed by insulin omission and unknown causes [15]. Insulin omission was also prevalent for patients with recurrent episodes of both types, but it is notable that it was the most prevalent among type 1 patients with recurrent episodes, which is probably due to difficulty in maintaining insulin dosage due to economic factors and healthcare unavailability. These factors are important to discuss in Syria, as difficulties have been reported in medication and testing supplies [39], along with low awareness among patients in the Arab world [40].

Limitations and recommendations

This study explores DKA among patients in a population that has faced the consequences of conflict and economic hardship. To give the best insight possible into this population, we included any biochemical tests that were available for most patients. However, due to economic difficulties, many tests, such as HbA1c, insulin levels, phosphate, and magnesium levels, to mention a few, weren’t available and couldn’t be studied. Moreover, because paper charts are still in use in Syria, collecting accurate data regarding DKA triggers and other potentially important variables has proven to be difficult. Collecting a relatively large sample was utilized to compensate for these limitations. We recommend the enhancement of quality control protocols and health information documentation. Previous papers can be useful in paving the way for this enhancement [41, 42].

Another limitation is that only 533 of the 997 admitted patients were included in the analysis. The remaining 464 charts were excluded because they were missing, incomplete, or lacked sufficient information to confirm a DKA diagnosis. This may affect the generalizability of the findings, as excluded cases could differ from those analyzed. Lastly, This study included DKA cases from two general hospitals in the city; however, the dedicated children’s hospital was not included, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to the entire population.

Conclusions

DKA as the first presentation of diabetes is prevalent among Syrian patients. Although type 1 patients were more acidotic, type 2 patients had longer admissions, likely due to comorbidities. Unknown factors and insulin omission were the most prevalent triggers of DKA, especially among patients with recurrent episodes.
