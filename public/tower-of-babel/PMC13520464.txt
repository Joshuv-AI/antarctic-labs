Environmental and occupational exposures for chronic kidney disease of unknown aetiology (CKDu): field investigations in rural Sri Lanka
Authors: Jennifer Pett, Mohammed Fahim, Richard Taylor, Nicholas J Osborne, Stephen Morrell, Sally Nathan, Nishantha Nanayakkara, Christine Linhart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic kidney disease of unknown aetiology (CKDu) is a public health concern, with a male predominance in rural agricultural communities in Sri Lanka. This cross-sectional field study investigated environmental and occupational exposures potentially contributing to CKDu prevalence in rural villages. Field observations and informal conversations with villagers were used to record potential exposures in situ and contextualise how they interacted in daily life.MethodsBased on CKDu occurrence in Kandy Teaching Hospital renal biopsy data, 12 villages in Matale and Badulla Districts with variable CKDu occurrence were included. Field observations documented housing conditions, drinking water sources, agricultural activities, and other potential exposures. Unstructured conversations with villagers provided information on less observable exposures, including temporal changes in drinking water supply. Local government offices provided information on village occupations and registered chronic kidney disease (CKD) patients.ResultsAgrochemical handling was performed almost exclusively by men and, particularly in paddy fields, often without personal protective equipment (PPE), despite reported use by farmers. Consistent use of improved water sources, such as reverse osmosis filters, was hindered by cost, limited availability and travel time, leading to continued use of well water. Villages with higher estimated CKD prevalence were characterised by greater dependence on agriculture, agrochemical use without adequate PPE and limited access to improved drinking water.ConclusionsThis study indicates that exposure measurement for CKDu research should better capture PPE use and drinking water-source use. Structured questionnaires on the primary drinking water source may not capture multiple water-source use. Similarly, closed-ended questions on PPE use may not reflect actual practice unless researchers and farmers share a clear understanding of what constitutes PPE. The finding that men almost exclusively applied agrochemicals may help explain the male predominance of CKDu and warrants further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-28172-9.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease of unknown aetiology, CKDu, Sri Lanka, Epidemiology, Field studies, Agrochemicals

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease of unknown aetiology (CKDu) is a public health problem, with population prevalence exceeding 2% reported in regions of Sri Lanka, Nicaragua, Guatemala and India [1]. A male predominance has been documented in CKDu-affected areas, with an estimated adult prevalence among people aged 18–60 years of 13.7% in men and 5.6% in women in Uddanam, India, 13.6% in men and 0.0% in women in northwest Nicaragua, and 7.7% in men and 2.6% in women in Anuradhapura, Sri Lanka [1].

Despite more than two decades of investigation into potential causes [2], no definitive causal pathway has been established. In Sri Lanka, proposed causes have included heat stress [3, 4], agrochemical exposure [5, 6], snake bite [6], drinking water quality [6–9], non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug use [3], betel nut chewing [10], infectious diseases such as leptospirosis and hantavirus [11], and heavy metal exposure [12, 13]. The absence of conclusive evidence for a singular causal exposure has led to hypotheses that CKDu has a multifactorial aetiology [14].

Agricultural work has consistently been identified as a distal risk factor, prompting the suggestion that CKDu be renamed “chronic interstitial nephritis in agricultural communities” (CINAC) [15]. However, the CINAC terminology may be overly restrictive if it implies that the disease is confined to agricultural workers or under-emphasises shared environmental or socioeconomic exposures. Several studies from Sri Lanka have reported a positive association between agricultural work and CKDu [3, 13, 16, 17]. However, the precise nature of the relationship remains poorly understood, and not all studies have demonstrated a consistent association. For example, a prevalence study conducted in the North Central Province of Sri Lanka using an internationally recognised CKDu research protocol [1] found non-significant associations for both short-term farming (< 10 years) and long-term farming (≥ 10 years) [6].

Treating agricultural workers as a homogeneous group may obscure occupation-specific proximate exposures and may partly explain discordant findings in previous studies. Heat stress from strenuous agricultural labour has been proposed as one such proximate exposure, but findings vary by region and industry. Research from Central America has indicated that sugarcane workers in physically demanding roles, such as cutters, have a higher risk of CKDu [18]. Conversely, a Sri Lankan study found that fishermen experienced greater heat stress than paddy farmers despite having a lower CKDu prevalence [19]. A clearer understanding of proximate occupational exposures is therefore essential to improve exposure measurement in future studies.

Contaminated drinking water, particularly with heavy metals and agrochemicals, has been extensively investigated as a potential cause of CKDu in Sri Lanka [2]. In response, the government has expanded access to improved water sources, including reverse osmosis (RO) filters [20]. Increased availability of RO filters has been suggested to correlate with a reported decline in hospital-reported chronic kidney disease (CKD) cases in the North Central Province [21]. However, that study reported only on RO filter availability, not daily use by individuals. Primary water source has previously been assessed in 1,497 Sri Lankan households in CKDu-endemic areas, with 49% reporting RO water as their primary source [22]. However, the definition of primary water source was not provided, and secondary or alternative water sources were not captured. Given that multiple water-source use is common in many low- and middle-income countries [23], its occurrence in CKDu-endemic areas requires further investigation. In addition, specific barriers to accessing improved water sources have not been adequately studied.

Most CKDu research conducted in Sri Lanka to date has been quantitative and has relied on questionnaires and surveys to capture proximate exposures [2]. However, because there is limited understanding of how potential exposures occur in daily life, observational methods offer a valuable opportunity to document exposures in context and to examine how they interact with everyday activities [24, 25]. Given the persistent uncertainty surrounding the aetiology of CKDu after two decades of investigation, a qualitative, field-based epidemiological study is well placed to address these methodological gaps.

This study aimed to explore environmental and occupational exposures that have been hypothesised to contribute to CKDu and to identify how these exposures occur in daily life, using a qualitative approach involving field observations and informal conversations with villagers. These data were supplemented by village-level comparisons of agricultural activities, recorded CKD cases, agrochemical use, access to improved drinking water sources, and other relevant exposures. This qualitative approach may provide a more nuanced understanding of how potential exposures occur in daily life and support more accurate exposure measurement in future quantitative studies.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional village-level field study, with repeated visits at different times of the year, was adapted from the World Health Organization (WHO) Healthy Villages survey model, which assesses village-level characteristics that influence community health, including water, sanitation, housing and hygiene [26]. To better capture potential exposures relevant to CKDu, the original WHO survey model was expanded to include domains that have been hypothesised to be associated with CKDu, including agrochemical use, agricultural activities and occupation [2].

Settings and sites

This study was conducted in villages in Matale District (Central Province) and Badulla District (Uva Province). Sri Lankan government administrative areas comprise 9 provinces, 25 districts, 331 divisional secretariats (DS) and 14,022 Grama Niladhari (GN) divisions. Each GN division consists of a small number of villages and has a local government office. Both Matale and northern parts of Badulla District are serviced by Kandy National Hospital, which provides the most comprehensive centralised source of CKD data for the study area. In addition to being a tertiary referral hospital, Kandy National Hospital conducts outreach nephrology clinics in surrounding rural areas.

Renal biopsies undertaken at Kandy National Hospital during 2011–2020 showed a higher proportion of tubulointerstitial nephropathy, the predominant histopathological finding in CKDu, among patients from Wilgamuwa DS (83%) and Mahiyanganaya DS (69%), than among patients from Galewela DS (26%). These findings were consistent with a 2017 government report identifying Wilgamuwa and Mahiyanganaya as high-prevalence CKDu areas [27]. This informed the selection of twelve villages for the study: two from Mahiyanganaya, six from Wilgamuwa, and four from Galewela. More villages were selected from Wilgamuwa because of its larger population and greater diversity of occupations within the selected GN areas. For confidentiality and in accordance with ethics approval, village names were anonymised and referred to by number.

Data collection

Village-level data were collected through field observations using a data collection sheet developed collaboratively by Australian and Sri Lankan researchers to ensure cultural and contextual relevance (Supplementary Table 1). Observational field notes were supplemented by informal conversations with residents, enabling methodological triangulation and documentation of less visible or changing exposures over time, such as access to drinking water sources [24]. Because the conversations were unstructured, no formal interview script was developed. Instead, conversations were guided by the field observations.

Fieldwork was conducted by two Sri Lankan research assistants who were native Sinhalese speakers, the predominant language in all villages. Both research assistants used the same data collection sheet and the same exposure domains for observation and informal conversations. Field notes were reviewed after each visit and compared across data sources to identify inconsistent or ambiguous observations.

Each research assistant spent one to three days in each village per visit, depending on village size. The number of informal conversations in each village was determined by the complexity of the local context, including the number of water sources and variation in agricultural activities. After each visit, observations and conversations were reviewed to determine whether new conceptual insights were continuing to emerge and to guide whether further conversations were needed [28].

On average, research assistants spoke with 10–15 villagers in each village, including farmers, people in non-agricultural occupations and the head of the GN office. Both men and women were included in conversations in each village. To maintain conversational flow and minimise participant anxiety, notes were not taken during the conversation. Instead, research assistants wrote detailed field notes immediately after each interaction. Field notes recorded in Sinhalese were translated into English by the research assistants. Photographs were taken to document field observations, including agrochemical labels, but no photographs were taken of villagers. Three field visits were conducted during July-August 2021, October-November 2021, and February-March 2022. The visits throughout the year were designed to measure seasonal variations in potential environmental and agricultural exposures. The first two visits occurred during the government ban on the importation of agricultural chemicals, introduced in April 2021 as part of an attempted transition to organic farming. This policy was largely reversed by November 2021. A planned fourth visit during May-June 2022 was cancelled for safety reasons because of continuing economic instability and political uncertainty in Sri Lanka at the time [29].

Despite their linguistic and cultural familiarity, some villagers expressed concern that the research assistants might report agrochemical use to authorities because of the contemporaneous ban on agricultural chemical imports. Repeated visits helped develop familiarity and build trust through prolonged engagement. The research assistants also explicitly clarified their independence from the government to mitigate concerns arising from the 2021 import ban.

Living conditions were assessed by research assistants walking through each village and documenting housing materials, structural conditions, and the proximity of residential dwellings to agricultural areas and livestock. In several instances, villagers invited research assistants into their homes, allowing internal living conditions to be recorded through direct observation and conversations about lived experiences.

Drinking water was assessed through systematic observation of available infrastructure, including private and communal wells, tank water and RO filtration units. To strengthen the accuracy of these assessments, observations were triangulated with information from the GN office and conversations with villagers. These conversations were essential for documenting how water sources were used in practice and for identifying barriers to accessing improved water sources.

Agricultural activities were assessed through a combination of field observation and conversations with villagers. Research assistants documented the types of crops cultivated and the labour demands associated with agricultural work. Agrochemical use was assessed by observing storage practices and application methods, and by photographing product labels on containers to identify active chemical ingredients. Farmers were also asked about the frequency of agrochemical use.

Community health concerns were assessed primarily through informal conversations. Research assistants asked villagers whether there were recurrent health issues or concerns in the village. Betel use, tobacco use, and alcohol consumption were also discussed.

Village population denominators could not be obtained directly from the Sri Lankan national census, as the lowest published level of disaggregation was the GN division [30]. Instead, village population estimates were obtained directly from GN offices, which also provided the number of registered CKD patients per village to enable estimation of village-level CKD prevalence. Patients with advanced CKD can register for government assistance through their local GN office if they provide a supporting letter from a nephrologist. The number of registered CKD patients therefore does not include residents with undiagnosed CKD or CKD that does not qualify for assistance, and it does not differentiate between CKD and CKDu. Predominant village occupations were also supplied by the GN office and checked during field visits through observation and conversation.

Secondary data on wet bulb globe temperature (WBGT) from climatechip.org, and altitude above sea level from Google Maps, were collected to inform potential village-level exposure to heat stress. Climatechip.org obtains WBGT data from gridded climate fields compiled by the Climatic Research Unit at the University of East Anglia, which combine, validate and interpolate weather-station observations onto a 0.5° × 0.5° global grid, from which heat indices including WBGT are derived [31].

Data analysis

Field notes of observations and conversations were reviewed after each visit to identify common observations and individual experiential statements applicable to each village [24]. Observations and conversations were coded by the first author (JP) into a priori exposure categories derived from two decades of CKDu research in Sri Lanka: drinking water sources, agricultural activities, heat stress, agrochemical use, village living conditions, and community health concerns [2]. Coding was reviewed by co-authors. Responses from villagers were triangulated with observations and other conversations to assess consistency in reported information and to identify and interpret inconsistencies between observed and reported information.

Drinking water sources were categorised by source type and village, and agrochemicals were categorised by chemical type and village. Agrochemicals were grouped as fertilisers, herbicides, or insecticides. It was not possible to measure the quantities of agrochemicals used. Similarities and differences across categories were tabulated in a spreadsheet program and compared between villages. As the analysis was primarily qualitative, inferential statistical methods, such as multiple regression, were not appropriate. Instead, thematic analysis was used to identify recurrent themes across field observations and conversations, with methodological triangulation used to compare observed and self-reported data [32–34]. Qualitative methods, including data-source triangulation, were used to address factors that could affect interpretation, including social desirability bias in self-reported practices, and to document less visible exposures, such as changes in drinking water sources over time. This approach also helped identify exposures that participants may not have recognised or understood, such as whether their clothing or masks met PPE specifications [32–34]. In accordance with qualitative principles, atypical observations were not excluded from the analysis [34], as they may reveal an important but uncommon potential causal factor, such as the observation of agrochemicals being applied by a woman.

Results

Using GN office registers of CKD patients, three villages had an estimated village population prevalence of CKD > 2%: Village 1 (9.8%) and Village 2 (4.2%) from Mahiyanganaya DS, and Village 3 (8.6%) from Wilgamuwa DS. The remaining villages had an estimated CKD prevalence of ≤ 2% (Table 1). It was not possible to calculate a separate prevalence for CKDu, and the registered CKD patients reflected people with diagnosed and often advanced CKD.

Living conditions

Houses across all villages were typically constructed of clay or cement bricks with asbestos or metal sheet roofing (Supplementary Material, Photo 1). Houses in Village 1 and 2 were generally less well maintained, with poor ventilation and some houses had no electricity. Paddy was often stored inside homes in recycled, washed fertiliser bags (Supplementary Material, Photo 2). Home gardens and small numbers of livestock near residences were common (Supplementary Material, Photo 3).

Drinking water

Water sources varied between villages and included well water (predominantly dug wells), natural sources (canals, lakes and streams), water tanks (filled by a truck or pumped from a nearby water source), piped water, RO filtration centres, and commercially bottled water (Table 2, Supplementary Material Photos 4–7). In the past, most villages relied on well water, but villagers reported that drinking water sources had changed over the past two decades because government and non-governmental organisations recommended avoiding well water due to concerns over potential contamination. This led to increased use of RO water and tank water, where available. However, access to improved water sources was inconsistent. Barriers included non-functional RO units; insufficient tank water supply; seasonal shortages of well water; affordability issues (RO water ranged from free to 500 Sri Lankan rupees per 20 L); logistical challenges, including transport costs and time required to access water sources; time restrictions on reticulated piped water; and poor maintenance of water pipes, resulting in mud infiltration. Villagers in Villages 1 and 2 explained “if they don’t have time” to collect RO water, they use their own well water even if it is not an approved source.

Concerns about drinking water quality and access to improved water sources were mentioned repeatedly, some villagers considered water to be the cause of CKDu: “Fertilisers do not cause kidney disease, water is the reason” (Villager, Village 3). In contrast, another villager remarked that “only I got kidney disease” despite 10 other family members using the same well. Concerns about water quality were most notable in Villages 1 and 2. A villager from Village 2 said that there had been “a remarkable decline of kidney diseases” after installation of the RO filter and asked why fixing the broken RO filter was not a priority.

Agricultural activities

Paddy cultivation was the predominant occupation over both Yala and Maha seasons in Villages 1–3, which also had the highest estimated CKD prevalence (10%, 4% and 9%, respectively). There were lower rates of CKD (≤ 2%) in Villages 9–12 where residents reported paddy farming was traditionally limited to the Maha season. Across many villages, CKD was considered linked with farming, “Farmers are the ones who mostly get kidney diseases” (Villager, Village 3).

Paddy fields were usually around 1 km from the village, except in Villages 4–7, where they were around 2–3 km away (Supplementary Material, Photo 8). Observed agricultural activities followed the Maha (September-March) and Yala (May-August) cultivation periods (Table 3).

Except for agrochemical spraying, which was undertaken almost exclusively by men, both women and men were observed to participate in agricultural activities. This was confirmed through conversation with villagers.

Heat stress

All villages were located between 100 and 200 m above sea level. Mean WBGT was not available for each village; however, the mean WBGT in each gridded climate area in which the villages were located indicated no significant differences, ranging from 23 °C in January to 26 °C in May, then decreasing to 24 °C by December [31].

Farmers were observed wearing head coverings when in the fields, and many had shelters nearby. Farmers reported taking drinking water from home to the field and often returning home for lunch. Workers reported being able to stop for breaks as needed. The labour intensity of agricultural practices, particularly paddy cultivation, was reported to have decreased substantially since the widespread introduction of machinery in the 1990s [35].

Agrochemical usage

All villages reported agrochemical use, primarily for farming, with occasional application in home gardens. Across the 12 villages, 34 different fertilisers, 13 insecticides and 22 herbicides were observed (Table 4). Fertilisers were the most commonly used class, with both organic (e.g. fish-based, plant-based and composted products) and inorganic types (e.g. urea, muriate of potash, and phosphate-based compounds) present in all villages (Supplementary Table 2). Insecticide use was more variable across villages, with carbamates and organophosphates being the most frequently observed class (Supplementary Table 3). Herbicides included a range of chemical classes such as phenoxy herbicides, anilides, and triazines (Supplementary Table 4). The greatest variety of agrochemicals was observed in Village 10 (n=19) and Village 1 (n=17). Villages 6 (n=3) and 8 (n=5) had the lowest numbers observed (Table 4).

In conversations with research assistants, some farmers expressed concern about the potential toxicity of agrochemicals: “within 3-5 days all the pests and even fish, crabs, frogs in the paddy fields died” (Farmer, Village 4). Other farmers questioned the link between agrochemicals and CKD, noting that rice was being imported due to a shortage and asking, if people in other countries use agrochemicals, “does that not cause kidney disease when our country people use that imported rice?” (Farmer, Village 3).

Across all villages, each step of agrochemical use, including dilution, spraying and cleaning equipment, was performed by men, with one observed exception. Villagers confirmed that agrochemical use was considered men’s work. Although farmers reported previous education on safe agrochemical handling and correct PPE use, several reported increasing the recommended dose or frequency beyond label instructions because of concerns about insufficient potency. Research assistants observed limited PPE use, with paddy farmers typically wearing long-sleeved shirts and trousers, but no shoes and only occasional cloth mask use during spraying (Supplementary Material, Photo 12). Cloth masks were more common when applying fertiliser, with one farmer saying he only uses it due to the “bad smell”. Farmers explained that they did not wear footwear because shoes could become stuck in paddy-field mud. One farmer remarked, “It is difficult to borrow PPE, and you cannot use shoes in mud” (Farmer, Village 7). Despite the observed absence of PPE, some farmers reported following PPE recommendations because they considered long clothing to be adequate PPE.

After applying agrochemicals, farmers bathed in the canals and waterways surrounding the paddy fields. Agrochemical containers were often washed in these same water sources before being buried or burnt next to fields. Containers were also seen discarded in forests, bushes and waterways around villages and fields (Supplementary Material, Photos 13-14).

Health

Western and Ayurverdic medication use was reported in all villages, although Ayurverdic medication was largely restricted to external application for body aches. All villages had access to medical clinics, although transportation costs and inability to take time away from work were cited as barriers to healthcare access, particularly in Villages 1-3. For people with kidney disease, there was an economic benefit from treatment adherence with one person explaining that they must provide an annual letter to the GN stating that they “continuously take medicine” to receive the government kidney disease allowance.

Tobacco smoking was common among men in all villages, and betel chewing (a mixture of areca nut, lime, betel leaf, and often tobacco) was common in both sexes. Informants in all villages expressed concerns about CKDu, particularly irregular village screening programs and uncertainty about CKDu prevention. Key concerns were limited access to affordable improved drinking water and occupational exposure to agrochemicals. Some villages had government education boards providing information on recommendations to avoid CKD (Supplementary Material, Photo 15).

Villagers frequently described the tension between economic necessity and health risks, which one farmer summed up: “Pesticides may kill us, but we still need to eat”. While many thought that contaminated drinking water and agrochemical exposure caused CKDu, they also emphasised that improved drinking water sources were unaffordable and agrochemicals were essential for sustaining agricultural livelihoods.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that within a small geographical area, neighbouring villages can differ substantially in agricultural practices, access to improved drinking water sources and other possible factors. These findings suggest that broad administrative categories may obscure relevant exposure differences and that future CKDu studies should measure exposures at village and individual levels. In particular, multiple water-source use and item-specific PPE practices require more detailed measurement than standard questionnaires may provide.

Housing conditions have not been extensively considered as potential causes of CKDu in Sri Lanka [2]. In this study, paddy was often stored in household living areas, potentially increasing exposure to fungal contaminants and mycotoxins. Although previous studies have considered mycotoxins as a risk factor [36, 37], none measured levels in house-stored paddy. Other potential health risks included particulate matter from cooking and zoonotic disease exposure from livestock near residences. Villages 1 and 2 had poorer housing quality than other villages and higher CKD prevalence. Living conditions are unlikely to directly explain higher CKDu prevalence, but may reflect other socioeconomic inequities, including access to improved drinking water sources or affordability of PPE.

Concerns about access to improved drinking water sources were common, particularly in Villages 1-2. However, the presence of an improved water source did not necessarily ensure its use, as villagers reported barriers including cost, travel time, limited supply, and poor maintenance. This variability in drinking water sources makes it difficult to draw firm conclusions about the relationship between water source and CKDu, and may partly explain conflicting findings in previous studies [6, 9, 38, 39]. Although the introduction of RO filters has been theorised to lead to a decline in CKDu cases [21], the current study shows that maintenance, access and affordability of RO filters remain concerns for villagers. A previous study reported that RO filter use has increased over the past two decades, however, it captured only primary water source [22]. The present study therefore highlights the importance of recording multiple water-source use. Future studies should use methods capable of capturing changes in drinking water patterns over time.

Overall, villages engaging in paddy farming had higher estimated CKD prevalence than villages with predominantly non-agricultural occupations or other forms of agriculture, such as tobacco farming or plantation crops including banana and mango. Villages 1-3, which almost exclusively cultivated paddy during both Maha and Yala seasons, had the highest estimated CKD prevalence (4.2-9.8%). In contrast, Villages 4-12, with limited or seasonal paddy farming, showed lower CKD prevalence (≤2%). This pattern suggests a potential relationship between CKD prevalence and intensity of paddy farming. Previous research from Sri Lanka has similarly found that paddy farmers may have a higher risk of CKDu than people in other agricultural occupations [19, 40], although a specific proximate exposure associated with paddy farming has not yet been identified.

The present study identified a potential exposure specific to paddy farming, where farmers were observed spraying agrochemicals while barefoot to prevent footwear from becoming stuck in mud. Although limited PPE use was observed across other farming activities, the absence of footwear among paddy farmers may increase dermal exposure to agrochemicals. Future studies should ensure that researchers and participants share a clear understanding of what constitutes PPE, as field observations revealed minimal PPE use despite farmers’ self-reported adherence to PPE recommendations. In future cross-sectional studies using closed-ended questionnaires, simple improvements could include a clear definition of PPE, assessment of PPE items individually, and distinction between consistent and intermittent use. For example, a study of farmers in Uva Province reported that 71% used “protective clothing” and 38% used a “mask”, yet neither the type was defined [41]. In the present study, the masks observed during field visits were cloth masks, which do not meet PPE specifications. Future studies should assess PPE use in line with international guidance, such as recommendations from the European Food Safety Authority [42].

While agrochemicals have been postulated as a cause of CKDu, no direct association has been established in Sri Lanka, unlike findings from studies based in the United States of America (USA) [43, 44]. In addition to occupational risk associated with agrochemical spraying, indirect non-occupational exposure to agrochemicals has been identified as a risk factor for CKD in USA studies. Indirect exposure has been theorised to occur through spray drift, water contamination and handling contaminated clothing [45]. The present study could not measure factors that may influence agrochemical toxicity, such as frequency of use or individual adherence to PPE requirements. However, occupational agrochemical exposure may offer a plausible explanation for the higher proportion of CKDu in men than women, due to the predominance of men spraying agrochemicals.

Heat stress was not mentioned as a concern by villagers performing agricultural work, due to the ability to take breaks and access shade in or near fields. Although the areas included in the study had similar WBGT, it was not directly measured in each village, limiting the ability to draw conclusions on heat exposure. All villages in this study had lower air temperature and relative humidity than the Northern Province [46], where CKDu has not been reported. Heat stress remains the leading causal hypothesis for CKDu among many researchers in Central America [47, 48], and acute kidney injury due to heat stress has been documented in both agricultural [49, 50] and non-agricultural sporting settings [51, 52]. Less research has been conducted on heat stress in Sri Lanka; future research could evaluate the role of heat in CKDu through longitudinal estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) monitoring in agricultural workers throughout the paddy-growing seasons.

This qualitative field study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. Village-level prevalence was based on GN office registers of CKD patients eligible for government assistance. The counts therefore do not include undiagnosed CKD, and likely under-ascertain early or less advanced CKD. They also do not distinguish CKDu from CKD due to known causes. Consequently, the high- and low-prevalence village categories should be interpreted as contextual indicators of registered CKD burden. The study’s village-level design also introduces potential misclassification bias, where individuals residing in a low-exposure villages may work in a higher-risk environment elsewhere, a phenomenon documented in previous research [53].

In addition, because the informal conversations were unstructured and conducted by two research assistants, inter-observer differences in probing, observation and note-taking may have occurred. The shared data collection sheet, repeated field-note review and triangulation with direct observations and GN office information reduced, but could not eliminate, this potential bias. Informal conversations with villagers were not audio-recorded, and the field notes may not have captured all details or the exact wording of participant statements. This was mitigated by writing field notes immediately after each conversation and triangulating this data with direct observation and GN office information. Data collection was also impacted by the political situation in Sri Lanka, particularly the immediate ban on the importation of agrochemicals (instigated April 2021 and rescinded November 2021), which may have influenced access to agrochemicals or disclosure of their use.

Conclusions

This study showed that registered CKD prevalence varied substantially between villages within small geographic areas. Drinking water sources changed over time, and improved sources such as RO filters were not used consistently because of cost, access difficulties, insufficient supply and poor maintenance. Direct dermal exposure to agrochemicals likely occurred during spraying due to inadequate PPE, with additional potential exposure from bathing in canals around paddy fields. These findings indicate that future exposure measurement should capture multiple drinking water sources and assess PPE use against clear, item-specific standards.

The finding that men overwhelmingly applied agrochemicals, despite both men and women participating in agricultural work, may help explain the reported predominance of CKDu in men. This study suggests that paddy farmers may have increased dermal exposure when spraying agrochemicals without footwear. These findings should be tested in carefully designed case-control studies examining agrochemical exposure, PPE use and CKDu risk. The study also identifies opportunities to improve exposure measurement, including measurement of multiple drinking water sources.
