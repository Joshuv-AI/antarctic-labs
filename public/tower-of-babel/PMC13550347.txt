Dynamic changes in oral microbiome composition during early childhood caries management
Authors: Yinbo Wang, Guiding Li, Yaopeng Liu, Juan Liu, Gang Li, Yanhong Li, Ni Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly childhood caries (ECC) is a prevalent dental disease with significant public health burdens. Oral microbiota plays a key role in caries development. Although several studies have compared the oral microbiota before and after caries treatment, few have investigated the dynamic changes in oral microbial communities beyond 3 months. This 6-month prospective study monitored dynamic changes in the dental plaque microbiome, salivary pH, and oral hygiene status among children with ECC undergoing caries management. The outcomes were assessed at baseline (T0), immediately post-treatment (T1), and at 1-month, 3-month, and 6-month follow-ups (T2–T4). Children’s oral hygiene status improved significantly after ECC management (p < 0.001). Their salivary pH levels increased at T1 but returned to the initial level during follow-up visits. Higher Chao1 scores were observed at T2, T3, and T4 when compared to T0 and T1 (p < 0.001). The principal coordinate analysis revealed distinct clustering patterns between baseline and post-treatment visits. Significant differences were observed in the relative abundance of Corynebacterium (p < 0.01), Corynebacterium matruchotii (p < 0.01), and Selenomonas noxia (p < 0.05) during the study period. The relative abundance of Corynebacterium and Corynebacterium matruchotii was significantly higher at T3 and T4 when compared to T1 (p < 0.01). Additionally, Saccharibacteria was positively correlated with children’s dmfs scores and negatively correlated with children’s salivary pH values. The above findings possibly indicated that comprehensive ECC management might be associated with dynamic changes in the oral microbiome. Further well-designed randomized controlled trials are warranted to determine the impact of dental treatment on oral microbiota dynamics.Key points• Dynamic changes observed in the oral microbiome following dental treatment• Abundance of Saccharibacteria associated with children’s caries status and salivary pH values• Children’s oral health–related behaviors improved after comprehensive dental managementSupplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s00253-026-13928-5.

Introduction

Early childhood caries (ECC), previously known as “nursing bottle caries,” is defined as “the presence of one or more decayed (non-cavitated or cavitated), missing (due to caries), or filled tooth surfaces in any primary tooth in a child less than 72 months of age” (American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry 2025; Zou et al. 2022). ECC is one of the most prevalent oral diseases among preschool children. Studies conducted in less-developed regions reported a high ECC prevalence of 83–94%, and most of the carious lesions remained unrestored (Feng et al. 2024; Zhou et al. 2021). Toothache, infection, and early tooth loss are common consequences of ECC, which can disrupt children’s daily routines by altering their eating or sleeping patterns, impact their academic performance by frequent absences from school, and lead to adverse nutritional status as well as reduced quality of life (Nadeeshani et al. 2023; Wong 2022). Given its high prevalence, low treatment rates, and significant negative consequences, ECC has emerged as a critical public health issue that requires greater awareness and more effective preventive measures.

Caries is a chronic disease influenced by multiple factors. The management of ECC involves not only restoring existing cavities but also emphasizing preventive measures to reduce caries risk in target patients. Preschool children could not maintain oral hygiene status independently due to their limited self-care abilities and dental care awareness. Collaborations between pediatric dental professionals and caregivers are crucial for comprehensive ECC management. Typical approaches for ECC prevention include professionally applied topical fluoride varnish, establishment of proper oral hygiene practices in early childhood, dietary modifications to control consumption of sugar-containing snacks or beverages, application of fissure sealants, oral health education (OHE), and regular dental check-ups (Wong 2022; Zou et al. 2022).

Specific bacteria, such as Streptococcus mutans, are well identified to be major contributors to the onset of dental caries, partly due to their abilities to possess high-affinity surface adhesins and an acid tolerance response, rapidly metabolize various carbohydrates, interact with commensal oral microbiota, and adapt to sudden and frequent environmental challenges (Lemos et al. 2019). However, a systematic review found that S. mutans counts had low accuracy for predicting caries risk (Ewoldsen et al. 2010). In a recent matched case–control study (Wu et al. 2024), in addition to S. mutans, Porphyromonas gingivalis and Bacteroides acidifaciens were identified as potential microbiome markers for differentiating preschoolers with rampant caries from those without caries. Moreover, that study also revealed that certain bacteria, for instance, Veillonella, which was enriched in caries-free preschool children, were positively correlated with children’s salivary pH values. Interventions, such as fluoride applications and health education, may alter the oral microbial communities among children (Yu et al. 2022; Zeng et al. 2025b). Skelly et al. (2020) provided three preventive measures to child patients, including 10% antiseptic povidone-iodine, fissure sealant, and fluoride varnish. Significant differences in taxonomic diversity were detected between children in the intervention group and those who did not receive the intervention.

However, limited studies have explored the dynamic changes in oral microbiota among preschool children undergoing comprehensive ECC management. Therefore, the oral hygiene status, salivary pH levels, and oral microbial composition of preschool children undergoing preventive and restorative treatments were analyzed in this prospective study, aiming to investigate the impact of ECC management on alterations in the oral microbiota among pediatric dental patients.

Materials and methods

Study design, study setting, and participant recruitment

This study was designed as a pre-post-intervention study. The Ethics Committee of the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Kunming Medical University reviewed and approved the study protocol (Ref No.: KYKQ2022MEC00109). All the procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Since the participants in this study were aged 3 to 5 years, informed written consent was obtained from all parents or legal guardians prior to recruitment.

Dental examinations were conducted at local kindergartens in Kunming. Children who met the following inclusion criteria were invited: (i) aged between 3 and 5 years old, (ii) diagnosed with ECC, (iii) without a previous dental treatment history, and (iv) without systemic diseases or developmental disabilities. The exclusion criteria were (i) children in mixed dentition, (ii) children who had used antibiotics within the past 3 months, (iii) children with enamel hypoplasia or dental erosion, (iv) children with carious lesions involving pulp, (v) children who were unable to cooperate with dental treatment even though non-pharmacological behavior management techniques were applied, (vi) children who were currently enrolled in other oral health promotion schemes, (vii) children with allergies to dental materials, and (viii) consent forms were not signed appropriately.

A single licensed pediatric dentist provided the following treatment to the recruited children in the clinical setting: (i) fluoride varnish (5% NaF, Duraphat®, Colgate-Palmolive Company, New York, NY, USA) applied to all children at 3-month intervals; (ii) sealant (Clinpro™, 3M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) for children whose molars were fully erupted, caries-free and exhibited deep pits and fissures; (iii) preventive resin restoration (PRR) for children with localized small carious lesions (Simonsen 2005) on posterior teeth, using composite (Filtek Z350XT Universal Restorative, 3M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) and flowable resin (Filtek Z350XT Flowable Restorative, 3M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) or sealant (Clinpro™, 3M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA); and (iv) composite restoration (Filtek Z350XT Universal Restorative, 3M ESPE, St. Paul, MN, USA) for children with cavities. Additionally, indirect pulp treatment was performed using Calcimol LC (VOCO GmbH, Cuxhaven, Germany) if carious lesions were close to the pulp. Additionally, at each follow-up visit, personalized OHE was delivered, including toothbrushing training tailored to each child’s toothbrushing practices, flossing guidance, and dietary instructions to reduce between-meal snacking. Children’s oral hygiene status, salivary pH values, and plaque composition were assessed at five time points: baseline (T0), immediate post-treatment (T1), 1 month (T2), 3 months (T3), and 6 months (T4).

Sample size calculation

According to a previous study (Tian et al. 2015), microbial diversity was assessed using the Shannon index. The standard deviation (SD) was estimated at 1.2, and the mean difference between pre- and post-dental treatments was assumed to be 0.8. The sample size was calculated using the formula n = [(tα + tβ) s/δ]2, where α = 0.05, β = 0.20, s = 1.2, δ = 0.8. As tα and tβ depend on degrees of freedom (df = n − 1), the iterative method was adopted (Luo et al. 2011), and a minimum of 16 children was required. Considering that 30% of the participants might drop out, at least 21 children should be recruited initially.

Outcome measurements

Dental examination

The dental examination was conducted by a single calibrated pediatric dentist, with intra-examiner reliability over 0.80. Dental caries status was assessed using the number of decayed, filled, and missing (due to caries) primary tooth surface (dmfs) index, following the criteria recommended by the World Health Organization (2013). Children’s oral hygiene status was assessed using the Simplified Debris Index (DI-S), with lower DI-S scores indicating better oral hygiene status (Greene and Vermillion 1964).

Salivary pH measurement

Children were instructed to refrain from eating or drinking for at least 90 min before the collection of salivary samples (Zhang et al. 2021). During sampling, children were seated with their heads tilted downward and leaning forward. Unstimulated saliva was collected from the floor of the mouth using a disposable pipette. A pen-type pH meter (PH5F, Sanxin, Shanghai, China) was employed to measure the salivary pH of the recruited preschool children (Wu et al. 2024).

Supragingival plaque collection

Following the dental examination and salivary collection, children were instructed to rinse their mouths with clean water to remove food debris. The supragingival dental plaque was collected to assess the composition of the microbiome following the method described in our prior publication (Wu et al. 2024).

DNA extraction, library preparation, and 16S rRNA sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from samples using the MagPure Stool DNA KF Kit B (MAGEN, Shenzhen, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The extracted DNA was transferred to a 1.5-ml centrifuge tube and stored at − 20 °C. The V3/V4 region of bacterial 16S rRNA was amplified using forward primer 338 F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3′) and reverse primer 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′) (Zeng et al. 2025a, b). A 50 µl PCR mixture containing 30 ng of template DNA was amplified under the following conditions: 95 °C for 3 min; 30 cycles of 95 °C for 15 s, 56 °C for 15 s, and 72 °C for 45 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Amplicons were then purified using DNA magnetic beads (BGI, LB00V60, Shenzhen, China). The products were denatured into single-stranded DNA, circularized, and digested to remove linear fragments. Rolling circle amplification using phi29 polymerase (BGI, Shenzhen, China) was used to generate DNA nanoballs (DNBs), which were loaded onto a patterned nanoarray and sequenced on the DNBSEQ-G400 platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China).

Questionnaire survey

A structured questionnaire was administered to parents to collect information related to their children’s dietary habits (including snacking frequency and consumption of sugary food or beverages after bedtime toothbrushing), toothbrushing habits (toothbrushing frequency and duration), and the use of dental floss, as well as parents’ education levels at baseline and the 6-month follow-up visit.

Bioinformatic and statistical analysis

Raw sequencing data were quality-filtered using iTools Fqtools fqcheck (v.0.25), cutadapt (v2.6), and readfq (v1.0) to obtain high-quality, clean reads (He et al. 2013). Tags were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) using a 97% threshold by USEARCH (v7.0.1090) (Edgar 2013), and UCHIME (v4.2.40) was used to remove chimeras (Edgar et al. 2011). α-diversity was assessed using the Chao1 (richness) and Shannon (diversity) indices, computed via Mothur (v1.31.2) (Schloss et al. 2009). β-diversity was estimated by principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and permutational multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) based on unweighted UniFrac distance (Lozupone and Knight 2005; Lozupone et al. 2007, 2011). Comparisons of children’s oral health status and microbial alterations across different time points were analyzed using repeated-measures ANOVA for normally distributed variables and the Friedman test for skewed variables, using IBM SPSS Statistics 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with significance levels adjusted using the Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. Changes in children’s oral health–related behaviors between T0 and T4 were analyzed using the related-samples McNemar test. The correlation between oral microbial structures and children’s dmfs scores, salivary pH levels, and oral hygiene status was analyzed using the Spearman rank correlation test.

Results

A total of 427 children were screened, and 30 children who met the inclusion criteria were invited to receive dental treatment. Among those, 22 children completed the dental treatment, and 17 children attended the 6-month follow-up visits (dropout rate, 22.7%). The average age of those children was 3.82 ± 0.80 years. Nine (52.9%) were boys, and eight (47.1%) were girls. Nearly half (47.1%) of parents held bachelor’s degrees, and 23.5% held postgraduate degrees. The average dmfs score of the participants was 11.24 ± 12.37. No new caries or secondary caries were detected during the follow-up visits.

Changes in children’s oral hygiene status and salivary pH levels

Significant differences were observed in children’s salivary pH levels (p < 0.001, repeated ANOVA) and the DI-S scores (p < 0.001, Friedman’s test) during the study period (Table 1). After Bonferroni adjustment, the pairwise comparison showed that salivary pH values were higher at T1 than at T0 (adjusted p = 0.003), T3 (adjusted p = 0.023), and T4 (adjusted p = 0.004). These findings indicated that children’s pH levels increased immediately after completion of all dental treatment procedures but returned to their initial levels during follow-up visits. Additionally, the decreasing trend in DI-S scores was observed during the follow-up period. The post hoc comparisons using Bonferroni correction revealed that, compared to T0, the DI-S scores were lower at T2 (adjusted p = 0.040), T3 (adjusted p = 0.040), and T4 (adjusted p < 0.001), indicating that children’s oral hygiene status improved after ECC management. Changes in oral health–related behaviors

The proportion of children who consumed snacks more than once daily decreased from 35.3% at baseline to 17.6% at the 6-month follow-up visit; however, this difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). Significant differences were observed in the proportion of children who consumed sugary foods or beverages after bedtime toothbrushing between baseline and 6 months (76.5% vs 29.4%, p = 0.008). Moreover, the proportion of children who brushed their teeth for over 2 min per session increased significantly at the 6-month follow-up when compared to baseline (64.7% vs 23.5%, p = 0.016). Additionally, all recruited children brushed their teeth twice daily and used dental floss to clean proximal tooth surfaces after ECC management (Table 2). Changes in the diversity of the oral microbial communities

Of the total 6,786,829 reads in 85 samples, 6,656,299 were retained. The rarefaction curve of the collected samples showed that the number of samples was sufficient to reach the plateau in terms of diversity (Supplementary Fig. S1). Taxonomic assignment classified all sequences into 26 phyla, 43 classes, 71 orders, 43 families, 254 genera, and 318 species.

Since the Chao1 index scores during the follow-up period were not all normally distributed, the Friedman test was used to analyze the changes in the Chao1 index. Significant differences were detected in the Chao1 index across the five time points (p < 0.001). Using Bonferroni corrections, the pairwise comparisons indicated that there was no significant difference in the Chao1 index between plaque samples collected at T0 and T1 (adjusted p = 1.000); however, when compared to T0 and T1, higher Chao1 scores were observed at T2 (adjusted p = 0.001), T3 (adjusted p = 0.001), and T4 (adjusted p < 0.001, Fig. 1a). Discussion

In the present study, ECC patients aged 3–5 years undergoing comprehensive caries management were followed for 6 months. After ECC management, no secondary caries or new caries were observed among the recruited children. The dynamic changes in these children’s salivary pH values, oral hygiene status, and the composition of supragingival plaque communities were assessed at five different time points, namely, baseline or pre-treatment (T0), immediately after treatment (T1), and at 1-month, 3-month, and 6-month post-treatment (T2 to T4). All treatments were provided by a single pediatric dentist, ensuring consistency in clinical practices and adherence to the original protocols. The ECC management included restorative procedures to address existing cavities and preventive measures to control caries development. Individually tailored OHE was also provided, emphasizing the importance of establishing proper oral hygiene practices and dietary modifications, aiming to reduce caries risk and promote long-term dental health in the recruited pediatric patients.

Favorable clinical outcomes were achieved with ECC management, as none of the patients developed new carious lesions during the follow-up period. Furthermore, a consistent improvement in oral hygiene status was observed throughout the whole study period, with the poorest hygiene status recorded at baseline. Turska-Szybka et al. (2014) also reported a reduction in the oral hygiene index from 1.19 ± 0.71 to 0.72 ± 0.48 following OHE. Similarly, Kokoceva-Ivanovska et al. (2018) observed significant improvements in oral health indices through regular dental check-ups and tooth cleanings. In this study, the DI-S scores decreased significantly after ECC management and reached the lowest level by the end of the 6-month visit. These findings suggest that our comprehensive caries management, which integrates OHE and behavioral guidance, is effective in improving children’s oral hygiene.

Previous studies reported that children with caries exhibited significantly lower saliva pH than caries-free children, with a negative correlation between saliva pH and caries incidence (Hegde et al. 2019; Singh et al. 2015). In this study, saliva pH values increased immediately after treatment, indicating a temporary reduction in caries risk. However, salivary pH levels returned to baseline at follow-up visits. Similar patterns in salivary pH alteration have been documented in prior studies (Kubala et al. 2018; Milleman et al. 2026). Chandel et al. (2017) reported that mouth rinse might increase salivary pH and reduce the bacterial load. Milleman et al. (2026) found that individuals’ salivary pH increased after applying oral hygiene practices, such as toothbrushing and/or mouth rinsing, and returned to near-baseline levels later. Controversially, Apriani et al. (2020) reported a sustained increase in salivary pH following fluoride application for 1 month. In our study, the salivary pH values at 1 month (7.29 ± 0.69) and 3 months (7.31 ± 0.42) were slightly higher than at baseline (7.14 ± 0.36). However, these differences were not statistically significant. It has been documented that patients’ dietary habits, oral hygiene practices, caries status, hormonal levels, and metabolic factors can also influence salivary pH values (AlSufyani 2023; Rovera et al. 2024; Santoso et al. 2022; Widowati et al. 2013; Wu et al. 2024). In the present study, children’s oral hygiene practices were significantly improved after 6 months of ECC management compared with baseline. All recruited children (100%) brushed their teeth twice daily and used dental floss. Toothbrushing duration increased significantly following ECC management. However, no significant changes were observed in children’s between-meal snacking frequency (p > 0.05). Likewise, a 24-month randomized controlled trial (Zhou et al. 2020) involving 306 children reported that after receiving social story-based OHE, the proportion of children with between-meal snacking habits decreased from 84.0 to 61.5% after 12 months, but increased to 71.2% at 24 months. In contrast, children’s toothbrushing performance showed consistent improvement throughout the study period, suggesting that dietary habits are more difficult to change than oral hygiene behaviors. As dietary patterns are closely associated with alterations in salivary pH (Santoso et al. 2022; Widowati et al. 2013), uncontrolled sugar consumption might contribute to a lower pH value. Further studies are encouraged to explore innovative and efficient strategies to improve children’s dietary patterns.

Additionally, we observed a negative correlation between children’s salivary pH levels and the abundance of Saccharibacteria (p < 0.01). Interestingly, a positive correlation between Saccharibacteria and children’s dmfs scores was also detected in our study. This finding indicated that Saccharibacteria might be associated with the occurrence of ECC among pediatric patients. A cross-sectional study conducted by You et al. (2023) revealed high abundances of Saccharibacteria in both the dental plaque samples (R2 = 0.026, p = 0.001) and the saliva samples (R2 = 0.013, p = 0.009) among children with dental caries, indicating that Saccharibacteria might serve as a bacterial marker for predicting caries.

In our study, both the Chao1 and Shannon indices were used to compare the α-diversity before and after ECC management. No significant differences were observed in the Shannon index, while higher Chao1 values were observed after ECC management (p < 0.001). These findings were partially consistent with another comparative study conducted by Ma et al. (2024). They also demonstrated a higher Chao1 index at the 1- and 3-month follow-ups, as well as a similar Shannon index throughout the study period. However, a higher Chao1 index was also observed between baseline and 0-day post-treatment by Ma and colleagues (2024). In our study, Chao1 values were slightly higher immediately after treatment than at baseline; however, this difference was not statistically significant. Furthermore, Tian et al. (2024) reported no significant change in α-diversity pre- and post-treatment. In terms of β-diversity, the microbial communities clustered closely together at baseline (T0) and immediately post-treatment (T1), suggesting similar microbial structures between these two visits. However, after ECC management, the pattern shifted toward more diverse directions. This finding aligns with another study conducted by Tian et al. (2024), indicating that dental treatment held the potential to alter the oral microbial structure. Additionally, there were no statistical differences in Chao1 index, nor the relative abundance of the top 10 most abundant species at 1-month, 3-month, and 6-month visits (p > 0.05), indicating that the oral microbial structure remained stable after comprehensive ECC management.

Previous studies suggest that early colonizers on tooth surfaces are typically Gram-positive aerobic and facultative anaerobic bacteria, while mature plaque is dominated by Gram-negative anaerobic bacteria (Nyvad et al. 1987). Additionally, improved oral hygiene can maintain plaque in an immature state (Grier et al. 2021). In this study, the relative abundance of Gram-positive bacteria, such as Corynebacterium, increased following comprehensive ECC management, suggesting a shift toward earlier colonization. This shift is likely associated with improved oral hygiene practices and plaque control. Corynebacterium has been implicated in efficient caries control, with its relative abundance increasing after caries treatment, particularly C. matruchotii and Corynebacterium durum (Tian et al. 2024). Mark Welch and colleagues (2016) proposed a hedgehog consortium to understand the community structure and metabolism of the dental plaque microbiome, in which Corynebacterium serves as the foundational taxon (Mark Welch et al. 2016). In this study, the increased abundance of C. matruchotii following comprehensive ECC management aligns with another pre- and post-intervention study conducted by Tian et al. (2024). There is evidence supporting that C. matruchotii is more likely to be enriched on non-carious tooth surfaces (Fakhruddin et al. 2019; Qudeimat et al. 2021; Tian et al. 2024).

Significant differences were observed in the distribution of S. noxia during the study period. A case–control study conducted by Yang et al. (2021) revealed a higher abundance of S. noxia in caries-free children when compared to children with caries. Similarly, Preza et al. (2008) found higher S. noxia levels in elderly patients without root caries than in those with caries. In a recent study (Wu et al. 2024), Selenomonas was positively correlated with toothbrushing duration and negatively correlated with sugary snack consumption frequency. Those findings indicated that S. noxia might be a protective factor against dental caries. However, S. noxia, first isolated from the human gingival crevice by Moore and colleagues in 1987, has been documented to be associated with periodontal diseases (Colombo et al. 2009; Faveri et al. 2008; Moore et al. 1987). Given that our target population is preschool children and periodontitis is not a common disease in this age group, few studies have investigated the association between S. noxia and periodontal diseases among preschool children. Future studies should focus on strain-level identification of S. noxia and explore the mechanisms through which it influences children’s oral health to gain a clearer understanding of its role and potential impact.

The major limitation of this study was the absence of a control group. Although the present study demonstrated dynamic changes over the course of ECC management, these changes could not be attributed solely to ECC management in the absence of a control group. Hendricks and colleagues (2024) reported a significantly higher detection rate of S. noxia in saliva samples from adolescents aged 11–17 years than in those from younger children, indicating an age-dependent association. Moreover, in this study, fluoride varnish, restoration, oral hygiene instruction, and dietary consultation were all included in the ECC management scheme. Regarding children’s oral health–related behaviors, there were no significant differences in the changes in snacking frequency before and after intervention, but children’s toothbrushing practices were significantly improved. A 24-month randomized controlled trial (Zeng et al. 2025b) demonstrated significant alterations in oral microbial profiles after OHE and fluoride varnish interventions. Since no control group was included in this study, it was unclear whether changes in S. noxia abundance were attributable to natural age-related microbial shifts, natural microbiome development, behavioral modification, or ECC management. Additionally, only children with ECC were involved in this study. It remains unclear whether similar alterations in oral microbiota occur in caries-free children. This limitation limits the feasibility of drawing causal conclusions about the impact of ECC management on oral microbial alterations. Given that randomized controlled trials provide higher-quality evidence than quasi-experimental studies, well-designed randomized controlled trials are recommended to explore the impact of dental treatment on dynamic changes in the oral microbiota in pediatric patients.

Another limitation of our study was the relatively short follow-up period, which only lasted for 6 months. Although this duration is longer than the 3-month follow-up periods reported in previous studies (Ma et al. 2024; Tian et al. 2024), it remains insufficient to assess microbiome stabilization or relapse risk, especially in the context of a chronic disease such as dental caries. Furthermore, secondary caries is considered the main cause of dental restoration failure, with an estimated prevalence of 20% at the patient level and 3.6% at the restoration level (Nedeljkovic et al. 2020). However, in our study, no secondary caries was detected among the participants over the 6-month follow-up period. Given that caries is a chronic disease, prospective studies with 12- to 24-month follow-up periods are recommended to better understand the dynamic changes in the oral microbiota among pediatric patients undergoing ECC management.

Moreover, although our study investigated the changes in children’s salivary pH levels, oral hygiene status, supragingival plaque composition, and oral health–related behaviors during ECC management, we did not explore functional predictions or metabolic pathways. Given that these analyses can provide deeper biological insights, integrating metagenomic or functional profiling into future studies is recommended. Additionally, the sample size of this study was relatively small, involving 17 children. The mean difference in the Shannon index between pre- and post-ECC management was 0.04, with an SD of differences of 0.36. The statistical power of the present study was 97%, corresponding to a Type II error rate of approximately 3%. This post hoc power analysis indicated that the current sample size was likely sufficient to detect the assumed effect size. However, the small sample size made it infeasible to incorporate multiple covariates, such as children’s demographics, socioeconomic status, changes in dietary patterns, oral hygiene practices, and salivary pH values, into regression analyses without risking model overfitting and reduced statistical power. Given the above limitations, future studies with larger sample sizes are needed to enable more comprehensive and reliable analyses.

In this study, comprehensive ECC management was delivered to preschool children, including topical fluoride varnish, fissure sealant, PRR, caries restoration, and OHE, based on individual oral health–related behaviors. The 6-month follow-up period revealed that children’s snacking behaviors and oral hygiene practices (toothbrushing frequency, toothbrushing duration, and use of dental floss) improved after ECC management. Dynamic variations in the oral microbiota were also observed at baseline, immediately after treatment, and at 1-month, 3-month, and 6-month post-treatment. These insights indicated that dental treatment, along with individually tailored OHE and preventive measures, could improve young children’s oral health–related behaviors. Additionally, comprehensive ECC management was associated with changes in children’s oral microbiota profile. However, the major limitations of this study were the lack of control groups, small sample size, and a relatively short-term follow-up period, which compromised generalizability and hindered causal interpretation. Long-term randomized controlled trials are encouraged to further investigate the effects of comprehensive ECC management on oral microbial changes and caries control in pediatric patients.
