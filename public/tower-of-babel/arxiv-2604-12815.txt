Abstract
The so-called SAGA-LD algorithm is used for efficient sampling from high-dimensional distributions in machine learning. Its intricate dynamics resists standard approaches of Markov chain theory. We prove, using a model-specific method, that SAGA-LD converges to a limiting distribution and a law of large numbers holds.



1
Introduction

Non-convex optimization procedures in machine learning are often based on the Langevin equation

dL_{t}=-F(L_{t})\,dt+\sqrt{2}dB_{t},

(1)

where
B_{t}
is some high-dimensional standard Brownian motion and
F=\nabla U
for some
function
U\geq 0
. Under mild conditions, the process
L_{t}
has an invariant density proportional
to
\exp(-U)
. A simple way of (approximate) sampling from this density would be Euler-Maruyama approximations
of (
1
). In practice, however,
F
is not available or prohibitively expensive to evaluate.

We consider the case where
F(x)
is of the form
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}F_{i}(x)
for some functions

F_{i}:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathbb{R}^{d}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
.
An often used sampling algorithm was first proposed in
[
11
]
: Stochastic Gradient Langevin Dynamics (SGLD),
initialized from some
\tilde{X}_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and given by the recursion

\tilde{X}_{n+1}=\tilde{X}_{n}-\eta F_{S_{n}}(\tilde{X}_{n})+\sqrt{2\eta}\xi_{n+1},

(2)

where
\xi_{n}
are an i.i.d. sequence of
d
-dimensional standard Gaussian variables,
\eta>0
is the step size, and

S_{n}
are a sequence of i.i.d. uniform draws from the set
\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
.
In practice, i.i.d.
minibatches
are drawn from the
F_{i}
and their average is used in (
2
) instead of just

F_{S_{n}}(X_{n})
. For the present, theoretical study we contend with this simplified version of SGLD.

Variance-reduction techniques produced several variants of SGLD, most notably SAGA-LD and SVRG-LD,
see e.g.
[
5
]
. They show superior performance at the price of a more involved
dynamics. In the present paper we concentrate on the SAGA-LD algorithm, to be defined in Section
2
below.

That algorithm is mathematically challenging. SAGA-LD, as a Markov chain, does not seem to satisfy
either of the two standard hypotheses of
stochastic stability theory for general state space Markov
chains: Foster-Lyapunov-type drift condtion
and minorization on a small set, see e.g.
[
10
]
.
As these two standard criteria fail,
we take an unusual route in this paper, originating
from
[
7
]
, that establishes stochastic stability and the convergence of
ergodic averages via showing a strong mixing property. In doing so we do not (and cannot)
rely on, say, Harris recurrence, but on the construction of specific couplings based on the
dynamics of SAGA-LD.

In Section
2
we formulate precisely our hypotheses and main result Theorem
2.4
:
ergodic averages of functionals of the SAGA-LD iterates converge in probability.
The latter result provides a reassuring theoretical guarantee
for numerical work with SAGA-LD.

Remark
2.5
explains its significance while Remark
2.6
discusses
possible future directions. Section
3

contains the proofs. Section
4
presents background material about strongly mixing processes.

2
Definitions and results

Fix our probability space
(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)
,
E[\cdot]
denotes the corresponding expectation.
Let an i.i.d. sequence
\xi_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
of
d
-dimensional standard Gaussian random variables be given.
Furthermore, let a sequence of i.i.d. random variables
S_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
be drawn uniformly
from the set
\mathcal{N}:=\{1,2,\ldots,N\}
. The initial state of the sampling algorithm is a random variable (or just
a constant)
X_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
.
We assume that

X_{0}
,
(\xi_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
,
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
are independent. Scalar product in
\mathbb{R}^{d}

is denoted
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
;
|\cdot|
is the Euclidean norm in
\mathbb{R}^{m}
, where
m
is always clear from
the context.
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m})
are the Borel sets of
\mathbb{R}^{m}
.

The “Stochastic Average Gradient Langevin
Dynamics” (SAGA-LD) is then defined as follows: there is an
N\times d
-dimensional process
G_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
which stores
the current estimates for the respective functions

F_{i}
,
i=1,\ldots,N
. Indeed, initialize this process by
G_{0}^{i}:=F_{i}(X_{0})
,
i=1,\ldots,N
and set

G^{i}_{n+1}=1_{\{S_{n}\neq i\}}G^{i}_{n}+1_{\{S_{n}=i\}}F_{i}(X_{n}),\ n\in\mathbb{N}.

(3)

The
d
-dimensional state variable for sampling is defined by the recursion

X_{n+1}=X_{n}-\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}G^{i}_{n}-\eta(F_{S_{n}}(X_{n})-G^{S_{n}}_{n})+\sqrt{2\eta}\xi_{n+1},\ n\in\mathbb{N}.

(4)

Here the updates

\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}G^{i}_{n}+\eta(F_{S_{n}}(X_{n})-G^{S_{n}}_{n})

(5)

replace the term
\eta F_{S_{n}}(\tilde{X}_{n})
appearing in the SGLD algorithm (
2
) above.

Our assumptions are listed below. They essentially coincide with those of
[
12
]

and are weaker than those of
[
5
]
(they do not imply
convexity of the function
U
in Section
1
).

Assumption 2.1
.

E[|X_{0}|^{2}]<\infty.

Assumption 2.2
.

There exist
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

\left\langle\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}F_{i}(x),x\right\rangle\geq c_{2}|x|^{2}-c_{1},\ x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}.

We may and will assume
c_{2}\leq 1
.

Assumption 2.3
.

For each
1\leq i\leq N
,
F_{i}
is Lipschitz continuous,
that is, there is
M>0
such that

|F_{i}(x)-F_{i}(y)|\leq M|x-y|

for each
i
, and for all
x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
. We may and will assume
M\geq 1
.

The total variation distance of two probabilities
\mu_{1},\mu_{2}
on
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{d})
is
defined as

d_{TV}(\mu_{1},\mu_{2}):=\inf_{X_{1}\sim\mu_{1},X_{2}\sim\mu_{2}}P(X_{1}\neq X_{2}),

here the infimum ranges over all random variables
X_{1}
(resp.
X_{2}
) with law
\mu_{1}
(resp.
\mu_{2}
).
The main achievement of the current paper is stated now.

Theorem 2.4
.

Let Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
and
2.3
hold. There is
\eta_{0}>0
such that,
for
0<\eta\leq\eta_{0}

there exists a probability
\mu_{*}=\mu_{*}(\eta)
on
\mathbb{R}^{d}\times\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
such that

d_{TV}(\mathrm{Law}(X_{n},G_{n}),\mu_{*})\to 0,\ n\to\infty.

For all measurable
functions
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)d}\to\mathbb{R}
such that

|\phi(u)|\leq C(1+|u|)

(6)

with some
C>0
,

E\left[\left|\frac{\phi(X_{1},G_{1})+\ldots+\phi(X_{n},G_{n})}{n}-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)d}}\phi(u)\mu_{*}(du)\right|\right]\to 0,\ n\to\infty.

(7)

A fortiori, the averages converge in probability.

Remark 2.5
.

It can be shown under suitable assumptions, see e.g.
[
5
]
, that
\mu_{*}(\eta)
is close to
the invariant law
\mu
of (
1
), for
\eta
small enough. Then, for large
n
, the law of
X_{n}

should approximate
\mu_{*}(\eta)
, hence also
\mu
.

In practice, however, what we need is approximating quantities like
\Phi:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\mu(dx)
for
some function
\phi
, using only
one generated realization
of
X_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
. In other words,
we need that averages like

\frac{\phi(X_{m+1})+\ldots+\phi(X_{m+n})}{n}

are close to
\Phi^{*}(\eta):=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{d}}\phi(x)\mu_{*}(\eta)(dx)
for
n
large. (
\Phi^{*}(\eta)
will
be close to
\Phi
for
\eta
small enough since
\mu_{*}(\eta)
is close to
\mu
.) To sum up, in order to use SAGA-LD in numerical work
with confidence, a law of large numbers should be established. This is precisely the content of (
7
) in
Theorem
2.4
.

Remark 2.6
.

Determining the convergence rate of
(X_{n},G_{n})
to
\mu_{*}
is an open problem of interest. It would also be desirable
to have almost sure convergence in (
7
). We expect that other variance-reduced schemes could be treated along the same lines.

\displaystyle E[|X_{n+1}|^{2}]=E\left[\left|X_{n}-\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}F_{i}(X_{n})\right|^{2}\right]+

(11)

\displaystyle+

\displaystyle E\left[\left\langle X_{n}-\frac{\eta}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}F_{i}(X_{n}),\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}F_{i}(X_{n})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}G^{i}_{n}-(F_{S_{n}}(X_{n})-G^{S_{n}}_{n})\right\rangle\right]

3.2
Representation by random maps

Let an
\eta
satisfying (
13
) be fixed for the rest of the paper.
We start by remarking that, although the process
X
badly fails the Markov property,
the pair
(X,G)

is
a general state space
Markov chain.

Representing Markov chains by random maps is a well-known approach to the analysis of
their ergodic properties, see e.g.
[
2
,
3
,
1
]
. The choice of these maps is crucial and allows for
the realization of various couplings.

Let us fix
\varepsilon>0
. We will define a specific representation for
(X_{n},G_{n})
with random mappings
such that, starting the chain from two different initializations
(X_{0},G_{0})
and

({X}_{0}^{\prime},{G}_{0}^{\prime})
, for
n
large enough,
(X_{n},G_{n})=({X}_{n}^{\prime},{G}_{n}^{\prime})
will
hold outside an event of probability at most
3\varepsilon
, see Proposition
3.7
below
for the exact formulation.

It will easily follow from this construction that
\mathrm{Law}(X_{n},G_{n})
converges to a limiting
law as
n\to\infty
and that the process
(X_{n},G_{n})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
is strongly mixing: see Section

4
for definitions and Subsection
3.3
for details.

We may and will assume that there is a sequence
(U_{n},U_{n}^{\prime})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
of
i.i.d. uniform random variables on
[0,1]^{2}
which is independent of
X_{0}
,
(S_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}}
.
We will construct a process using random function iterations driven by the noise
sequence
(U_{n},U_{n}^{\prime})
that has
the same law as
(X_{n},G_{n})
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
.

The following result is well-known, see e.g. page 228 of
[
3
]
(or Lemma A.5 of
[
4
]

We prove it here for

Lemma 3.3
.

Let
\kappa(x,A)
be a probabilistic kernel,
x\in\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}},A\in\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}})
. Let
U
be uniform on
[0,1]
. Then there is a function
\chi:\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}}\times[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}
such that

\chi(x,U)
has law
\kappa(x,\cdot)
, for all
x
.

Proof.

Fix a Borel isomorphism

\psi:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}^{m_{2}}
. Let
F(x,z):=\kappa\left(x,\psi\left((-\infty,z]\right)\right)

and define the “pseudoinverse”

F^{-}(x,u):=\inf\{q\in\mathbb{Q}:F(x,q)\geq u\},\ u\in(0,1),

this is easily seen to be
\mathcal{B}(\mathbb{R}^{m_{1}})\otimes\mathcal{B}([0,1])
-measurable.
Then, for every
x
,
F^{-}(x,U)
has distribution function
F(x,\cdot)
hence
its law is the pullback of
\kappa(x,\cdot)
by
\psi^{-1}
.
We may conclude by setting

\chi(x,u):=\psi(F^{-}(x,u))
: then
\chi(x,U)
will have law
\kappa(x,\cdot)
.
∎

4
On strongly mixing processes

For sigma-algebras
\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}\subset\mathcal{F}
, define

\alpha(\mathcal{G},\mathcal{H}):=\sup_{A\in\mathcal{G},B\in\mathcal{H}}|P(A\cap B)-P(A)P(B)|.

For a (say,
\mathbb{R}^{m}
-valued) stochastic process
W_{j}
,
j\in\mathbb{N}
we
define its
\alpha
-mixing coefficients as

\alpha_{W}(n):=\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}\alpha(\sigma(W_{l},0\leq l\leq j),\sigma(W_{l},l\geq j+n)),\ n\in\mathbb{N},

where
\sigma(\ldots)
refers to the sigma-algebra generated by the respective random variables.
We call the process
W

strongly mixing
if
\alpha_{W}(n)\to 0
,
n\to\infty
.

The following result appeared in
[
8
]
for the first time. It was restated
and reproved in Theorem A.3 of
[
9
]
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
W_{n}
,
n\in\mathbb{N}
be a real-valued process with
\alpha_{W}(n)\to 0
,

n\to\infty
such that

\lim_{V\to\infty}\sup_{n\geq 1}\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j=1}^{n}E[|W_{j}|1_{\{|W_{j}|\geq V\}}]\to 0,\ V\to\infty.

(18)

Then

E\left[\left|\frac{\sum_{j=1}^{n}[W_{j}-E[W_{j}]]}{n}\right|\right]\to 0,\ n\to\infty.

(19)

\Box

Lemma 4.2
.

If
C_{W}:=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}E[|W_{n}|^{2}]<\infty
then (
18
) is satisfied.

Proof.

Indeed, the Cauchy and Markov inequalities imply that

E[|W_{j}|1_{\{|W_{j}|\geq V\}}]\leq E[|W_{j}|^{2}]^{1/2}P(|W_{j}|\geq V)^{1/2}\leq C_{W}^{1/2}\left(\frac{C_{W}}{V^{2}}\right)^{1/2},

hence the supremum in (
18
) is dominated by
C_{W}/V
. ∎

[
9
]
. Remembering (
15

define the sigma-algebras

Lemma 4.3
.

Let
x_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{d},g_{0}\in\mathbb{R}^{Nd}
be arbitrary but fixed.
We have

\alpha_{(X,G)}(n)\leq 2\sup_{j\in\mathbb{N}}P(Z^{X_{j},G_{j}}_{j,j+n}\neq Z^{x_{0},g_{0}}_{j,j+n}),\ n\geq 1.

Proof.

Let
j\in\mathbb{N}
and
n\geq 1
be arbitrary. Let
A\in\mathcal{F}_{0,j}

and
B\in\mathcal{F}_{j+n,\infty}

Then there are

|P(A\cap B)-P(A)P(B)|=|\mathrm{cov}(1_{A},1_{B})|\leq|\mathrm{cov}(1_{A},1_{{B}^{\prime}})|+|\mathrm{cov}(1_{A},1_{B}-1_{{B}^{\prime}})|,

0

\displaystyle|\mathrm{cov}(1_{A},1_{B}-1_{{B}^{\prime}})|

\displaystyle 0+\mathrm{var}^{1/2}(1_{A})\mathrm{var}^{1/2}(1_{\bar{R}\cap B}-1_{\bar{R}\cap{B}^{\prime}}).
