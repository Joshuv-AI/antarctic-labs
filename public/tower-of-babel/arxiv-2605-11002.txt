MT-JailBench: A Modular Benchmark for Understanding Multi-Turn Jailbreak Attacks
Xinkai Zhang ∗, Zhipeng Wei ∗, Huanli Gong, Jing Ting Zheng, Yuchen Zhang, Yue Dong, N. Benjamin Erichson

Abstract
Multi-turn jailbreaks exploit the ability of large language models to accumulate and act on conversational context. Instead of stating a harmful request directly, an attacker can gradually steer the conversation toward an unsafe answer. Recent methods demonstrate this risk, but they are usually evaluated as black-box pipelines with different budgets, judges, retry rules, and strategy generation procedures. As a result, it is often unclear whether reported gains reflect stronger attack mechanisms or different experimental conditions. We introduce MT-JailBench, a modular evaluation framework for benchmarking multi-turn jailbreaks under fixed conditions. MT-JailBench implements each attack as five interacting modules: evaluation function, attack strategy, prompt generation, prompt refinement, and flow control. This design enables fair comparison across attack methods and component-wise analysis of what drives attack success. Using MT-JailBench, we find that resource budgets and evaluation functions are major confounders: controlling turns, retries, interactions, sampled strategies, and judges substantially change the ranking of attacks. At the component level, prompt generation accounts for most performance variation, while refinement and flow control provide moderate gains. We also find that explicit dynamic strategy generation is not always necessary; stochastic sampling from a fixed strategy can rival more elaborate diversification mechanisms. Finally, recomposing the best components yields a strong attack configuration that outperforms its source attacks and generalizes across diverse target LLMs. MT-JailBench therefore provides a modular framework for comparing multi-turn jailbreaks, understanding the impact of components, and guiding stronger red-teaming evaluations.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly used as conversational agents that remember prior context, adapt to user feedback, and help refine objectives over multiple turns
1
;
7
. This ability makes them useful for decomposing difficult problems, revising instructions, and supporting multi-step reasoning
50
or iterative planning
25
;
49
. However, it also expands the attack surface. Although harmful intent in a single-turn attack is usually localized within one prompt
40
;
36
, multi-turn jailbreaks exploit the conversational structure itself
21
;
43
. Rather than searching for a single prompt that elicits harmful content, the attacker can distribute the objective across the interaction: each prompt may appear benign, while the accumulated context steers the model toward an unsafe response. Early turns can establish a benign context, encourage the model to adopt a cooperative stance, and introduce assumptions that appear harmless before the harmful objective becomes explicit. By the time the request is sharpened, the model may have accepted parts of the framing or produced intermediate context that makes refusal less likely. This trajectory matters because many safety mechanisms are most reliable when harmful intent is explicit and localized to a single turn, and are less suited to objectives assembled gradually over a long context
10
.

Recent multi-turn attacks instantiate this pattern in different ways: Crescendo gradually steers benign conversations toward harmful outputs through context accumulation
29
; Chain of Attack dynamically refines the interaction as a semantic progression
39
; Foot-In-The-Door exploits incremental compliance over successive turns
37
; and X-Teaming uses multiple agents to plan, execute, and optimize attack trajectories
26
. These attacks matter not because they are merely longer versions of single-turn jailbreaks, but because they exploit the same mechanism that makes conversational LLMs useful: the ability to accumulate context, infer intent, and act on it.

Despite the growing safety concern posed by multi-turn attacks, there has been little systematic study of how existing methods compare when surrounding conditions are held fixed
22
. As a result, it often remains unclear what makes these attacks work and which failure modes defenses should target. Most attacks are introduced as complete pipelines, each with its own budget, judge, retry rule, and strategy generation procedure. One method may be allowed more turns, another may restart from many sampled strategies, and another may use a more permissive judge. Thus, the central issue is not only which method achieves the highest score, but why it does so
32
. We therefore need a benchmark that places existing methods in a common experimental setting and decomposes them into parts so that we can identify which components are responsible for improved performance. This motivates our research question:

When multi-turn jailbreaks are evaluated under fixed experimental conditions,

which components drive their success?

To study this question, we introduce MT-JailBench, an evaluation framework for benchmarking multi-turn jailbreaks under fixed experimental conditions. Rather than treating each attack as a black-box pipeline, MT-JailBench implements multi-turn jailbreaks as five interacting modules: evaluation function, attack strategy, prompt generation, prompt refinement, and flow control. The framework supports fixed external conditions, including the number of turns, retries, sampled strategies, and evaluation criteria, while its modular design enables controlled variation of individual modules to measure their contribution to attack success rate (ASR). Our study yields five main findings:

•

Resource-controlled comparison.

We compare five existing attacks after fixing turns, retries, target interactions, and sampled strategies.
We find that resource budgets substantially affect reported ASR. Some methods lose much of their advantage under constrained search budgets, showing that ASR partly measures how much search the attacker was allowed to perform.

•

Judge-controlled evaluation.

We evaluate the same attack outputs with different judges under a shared protocol.
We find that measured success changes substantially with the evaluation function because judges draw different boundaries between failure, partial progress, and success.

•

Component-wise analysis.

After controlling budgets and judges, we ablate prompt generation, refinement, and flow control one module at a time.
We find that prompt generation accounts for most performance variation, while refinement and flow control provide moderate gains.

•

Strategy diversification analysis.

We compare explicit strategy enumeration against repeated stochastic sampling from a fixed strategy, with refinement and flow control held fixed.
We find that stochastic sampling can rival more elaborate diversification mechanisms when paired with a strong generator and effective flow control.
This suggests that progress in multi-turn red teaming depends not only on expanding the attack space, but also on understanding how that space is searched.

•

Component recomposition.

We recombine strong components from Crescendo and XTeaming into
CrescendoX
.
We find that across open-weight and proprietary target models,
CrescendoX
outperforms its source attacks on 19 of 21 target LLMs; Figure
1
shows selected results.
This shows that MT-JailBench can be used not only to compare attacks, but also to identify reusable components and guide stronger multi-turn red-teaming methods.

Beyond these findings, MT-JailBench is designed as a reusable evaluation framework rather than a closed comparison among a fixed set of attacks. Its modular interface makes it easy to add new prompt generators, refinement methods, judges, control policies, target models, and datasets. Although our experiments focus on attacks, the framework also supports incorporating defenses such as model guardrails, input transformations, and output filters. MT-JailBench can therefore evaluate new multi-turn jailbreak methods and test whether defenses remain effective when the attacker adapts over several turns. More broadly, it provides a common experimental language for multi-turn red teaming, where progress can be measured not only by higher attack success rates, but also by a clearer account of which assumptions, budgets, and evaluation choices support those rates.

Contributions.

Our main contributions are as follows.

•

A modular evaluation framework for benchmarking multi-turn jailbreaks.
We introduce MT-JailBench, which implements multi-turn jailbreaks as five interacting modules: evaluation function, attack strategy, prompt generation, prompt refinement, and flow control. This design provides a reusable framework for evaluating and analyzing existing and future methods.

•

Problem Setup

We study multi-turn jailbreaks in a black-box setting
21
.
Let
\mathcal{M}
denote a target LLM, and let
x\in\mathcal{X}
denote a harmful behavior that the attacker wants the model to produce.
The attacker has no access to the model parameters, logits, safety classifiers, hidden states, or system prompt.
Instead, the attacker interacts with
\mathcal{M}
only through natural language queries and observes only the model’s textual responses.
A multi-turn jailbreak is a sequential interaction between the attacker and the target model.
At turn
t
, the attacker sends a prompt
q_{t}
, and the target model produces a response

r_{t}=\mathcal{M}(q_{t}\mid H_{t-1}),

(1)

where
H_{t-1}=\{(q_{i},r_{i})\}_{i=1}^{t-1}
denotes the conversation history before turn
t
.
After the response is generated, the history is updated as
H_{t}=H_{t-1}\cup\{(q_{t},r_{t})\}
.
An attack therefore produces a prompt sequence
Q=(q_{1},q_{2},\ldots,q_{T})
and a corresponding response sequence
R=(r_{1},r_{2},\ldots,r_{T})
.
The attacker’s goal is not necessarily to make every intermediate response harmful
26
.
In many multi-turn attacks, early turns are used to establish context, obtain related information, introduce a benign framing, or move the model into a cooperative conversational state
43
.
The harmful objective may only become clear after several turns, once the conversation has accumulated enough context.

We evaluate this outcome with an evaluation function
\mathcal{E}
, which maps either a response or a conversation history together with the harmful target to an attack-success signal:

e_{t}=\mathcal{E}(r_{t},x)\quad\text{or}\quad e_{t}=\mathcal{E}(H_{t},x).

Depending on the evaluator,
e_{t}
may be a binary label, a refusal indicator, a similarity score, or a graded harmfulness score.
In the simplest case,
\mathcal{E}
is applied only to the final response to determine whether the attack succeeded. In adaptive multi-turn attacks, however, evaluation signals can also be used to guide later interactions. This distinction matters because adaptive multi-turn jailbreaks differ not only in the prompts they generate, but also in how they use feedback from previous turns.

3
MT-JailBench

Multi-turn jailbreaks are harder to analyze than single-turn jailbreaks because the attack is no longer defined by one prompt and one response
22
. In the single-turn case, most of the attack logic is contained in the prompt itself
24
. In the multi-turn case, success depends on a sequence of decisions
21
: what prompt to send, how to use the model’s previous responses, when to retry, when to change direction, and when to stop. It also depends on external choices such as the number of allowed turns and sampled strategies, as well as the evaluator used to decide whether the attack succeeded.

Existing multi-turn jailbreak methods usually package all of these choices into complete attack pipelines. This makes them easy to execute but difficult to understand. When one method achieves a higher attack success rate than another, the improvement may come from better prompt generation, a more effective control policy, or simply a larger search budget. Without separating these factors, it is difficult to know which parts of the attack are actually responsible for the success.

3.1
Modular Evaluation Framework

MT-JailBench is designed to evaluate multi-turn jailbreaks under fixed experimental conditions while allowing the internal attack design to vary. A benchmark instance consists of a harmful behavior
x
, a target model
\mathcal{M}
, an attacker configuration, an evaluation function, and a resource budget. The resource budget specifies the maximum number of turns, retries, target-model interactions, and sampled attack strategies. By fixing these quantities, MT-JailBench separates the strength of an attack mechanism from the amount of search the attacker is allowed to perform.

:

Figure
1
provides a conceptual overview of MT-JailBench. Rather than treating an attack as a single end-to-end script, MT-JailBench represents each multi-turn jailbreak as five interacting components:

\mathcal{A}=(\mathcal{E},\pi,\mathcal{G},\mathcal{U},\mathcal{C}),

(2)

where
\mathcal{E}
is the evaluation function,
\pi
is the attack strategy,
\mathcal{G}
is the prompt generation mechanism,
\mathcal{U}
is the prompt refinement mechanism, and
\mathcal{C}
is the flow control policy.
\pi
and
\mathcal{G}
determine how the conversation is constructed,
\mathcal{E}
provides feedback on target-model responses,
\mathcal{U}
revises unsuccessful prompts, and
\mathcal{C}
decides whether to continue, retry, restart, or stop.

This decomposition is not only descriptive. In MT-JailBench, attack components are interchangeable modules, allowing attacks to run end to end or be modified one component at a time while the rest of the configuration remains fixed. As a result, the framework supports attack-level comparison, component-level analysis, and recomposition into new attack configurations.
In implementation, MT-JailBench operationalizes the five functional components through three standardized interfaces:
PromptGenerator
,
OutcomeEvaluator
, and
FlowController
. The
PromptGenerator
produces attack prompts and performs prompt refinement when requested by the control policy. The
OutcomeEvaluator
determines whether the current response satisfies the attack objective and may return auxiliary signals such as harmfulness scores, refusal indicators, or similarity scores. The
FlowController
governs the attack trajectory by deciding whether to advance, retry, restart, or terminate. The framework handles the remaining execution logic, including target-model interaction, conversation-state tracking, resource accounting, and coordination across modules. We provide the full system design, including concrete programming interfaces and workflow orchestration, in Appendix
C
.

3.2
Multi-Turn Attack Decomposition

We now describe how the five modules are executed during an attack. At turn
t
, the prompt generator produces a prompt, the target model responds conditioned on the conversation history, the evaluator scores the response with respect to the harmful target, and the flow controller selects the next action:

q_{t}\sim\mathcal{G}(\pi,x,H_{t-1},e_{<t};\gamma),\quad r_{t}=\mathcal{M}(q_{t}\mid H_{t-1}),\quad e_{t}=\mathcal{E}(r_{t},x),\quad a_{t}=\mathcal{C}(H_{t},e_{t}).

(3)

Here
H_{t}=\{(q_{i},r_{i})\}_{i=1}^{t}
is the conversation history,
e_{t}
the evaluation signal, and
a_{t}
the control action selected after turn
t
. Depending on
a_{t}
, MT-JailBench may advance, retry, restart, or terminate the attack. This loop defines the common execution interface used by all attacks; methods differ in how they instantiate these modules. We next describe the role of each component in the framework.

1.

Attack strategy.

The attack strategy
\pi
provides high-level guidance for the conversation before any particular prompt is sent. It may specify a benign framing, a persona, a sequence of topics, or a gradual path from background information to a more specific request. The prompt generation module
\mathcal{G}
turns this guidance into the concrete prompts. Separating strategy from generation lets MT-JailBench distinguish between selecting a better conversational path and writing better prompts along that path. The strategy may be fixed, for example by using few-shot examples, or generated dynamically using an LLM, for example by prompting for diversified personas.

2.

Prompt generation.

The prompt generation module
\mathcal{G}
produces the next prompt from the strategy, harmful target, conversation history, and previous evaluation signals. The generation mode
\gamma\in\{\text{offline},\text{online}\}
specifies whether prompts are planned before interaction or generated adaptively during interaction. In the offline setting, the attack first generates a prompt sequence
Q\sim\mathcal{G}(\pi,x)
and then follows this sequence during the conversation. In the online setting, each prompt is generated based on the current history and feedback. This distinction separates the quality of a planned trajectory from the ability to react to intermediate target-model responses.

3.

Evaluation function.

The evaluation function
\mathcal{E}
maps the target model’s response and harmful behavior to an evaluation signal
e_{t}
. This signal may be a binary success label, a refusal indicator, a similarity score, or a graded harmfulness score. Evaluation determines whether an attack is counted as successful, and may also provide feedback for prompt refinement and flow control.

4.

Flow control.

The flow control policy
\mathcal{C}
decides how the attack proceeds after observing the current history and evaluation signal. Its action
a_{t}
may advance the attack to the next turn, retry the current turn, restart by rolling back to the first or another earlier turn, or terminate the attack. Flow control determines how the attack spends its limited budget: whether to continue along the current trajectory, repair a local failure, or abandon an unpromising path.

5.

Prompt refinement.

When the flow controller requests retrying the current turn, the refinement module
\mathcal{U}
updates the current prompt using the evaluation signal,
q_{t}\leftarrow\mathcal{U}(q_{t},e_{t})
. Refinement differs from generation because it does not begin a new step in the planned conversation. Instead, it repairs the current prompt while preserving the local attack intent. Thus, refinement provides local adaptation, while flow control decides when such adaptation is worth spending budget on.

3.3
Expressing Existing Attacks in MT-JailBench

The component interfaces can express existing multi-turn jailbreak methods, as shown in Table
1
. This mapping is central to MT-JailBench because existing methods differ along several axes at once. One attack may use a binary judge with offline prompt generation, while another may use a score-based judge with online generation and dynamic strategies. Comparing these methods only as end-to-end pipelines does not reveal which design choices are responsible for performance differences.

MT-JailBench instead maps existing attacks into the common execution interface, turning indivisible pipelines into comparable components. The framework can therefore evaluate complete attacks under fixed budgets and evaluation protocols, replace individual modules for ablation, and recompose high-performing components into new configurations. The same interface also supports extension: new judges, prompt generators, refinement mechanisms, flow controllers, target models, datasets, and defenses can be added without changing the overall execution logic.

4
Experimental Results

We now use MT-JailBench to compare existing multi-turn jailbreak methods and study what drives their performance. We focus on five representative attacks: Crescendo
29
, CoA
39
, FITD
37
, ActorBreaker
27
, and XTeaming
26
. The preceding sections argue that attack success depends on several interacting factors: how much search budget the attacker has, how success is evaluated, how prompts are generated, how feedback is incorporated, and how the conversation trajectory is controlled. The experiments below isolate these factors one at a time.
We first compare representative attacks under fixed budgets, so that differences in performance are not simply explained by differences in search. We then examine how reported attack success changes across evaluation functions. After controlling for these external factors, we use the modular interface to isolate the roles of prompt generation, refinement, and flow control. Finally, we combine the strongest components and test the resulting configuration across a broader set of target LLMs.

Protocol.
We evaluate on HarmBench
24
, using the standard subset of 159 harmful behaviors adopted in prior multi-turn jailbreak work
26
. We report Attack Success Rate (ASR), defined as the percentage of harmful behaviors for which the target model produces a response judged to satisfy the target objective. For evaluation, we use three judges: two binary judges adapted from CoA and FITD, denoted
CoA-Binary
and
FITD-Binary
, and a 5-point harmfulness judge adapted from ActorBreaker and XTeaming, denoted
Score-Judge
. Full prompts, implementation details, and per-experiment configurations are provided in Appendix
F
and Appendix
G.1
–

4.1
How sensitive is attack success to resource budgets?

We first study the role of resource budgets. In multi-turn jailbreaks, a larger budget does more than make an experiment more expensive: it changes how extensively the attacker can search conversational trajectories. Additional turns, retries, target-model interactions, or sampled strategies give the attacker more opportunities to recover from refusals, abandon unpromising trajectories, and explore alternative paths. As a result, attack success reflects not only the quality of the attack mechanism, but also how much exploration the attacker performs. Thus, before comparing attack mechanisms, we need to ask how much of the performance gap remains when this budget is constrained.

We compare each method in two settings. The
abundant
setting configures each attack according to its original literature and allows up to 50 total interactions. The
limited
setting imposes the same constraints on all methods: at most three retries per turn, no restart upon failure, and at most 10 total interactions.
Table
2
shows that methods vary substantially in how strongly they depend on search budget. ActorBreaker, XTeaming, and CoA lose substantial ASR under limited resources, which is consistent with their control logic: ActorBreaker can switch across actors, XTeaming can restart with different strategies, and CoA can repeatedly backtrack when a trajectory has not succeeded. By contrast, Crescendo and FITD are comparatively stable. Thus, for resource-sensitive methods, ASR partly measures how much search the attack was allowed to perform. Multi-turn red-teaming evaluations should therefore report and control budgets explicitly; otherwise, differences in ASR can reflect different amounts of search rather than better attack mechanisms.

4.2
How sensitive is attack success to the evaluation function?

We next study the role of the evaluation function. Multi-turn jailbreaks often do not produce a clean binary outcome: a response may refuse the final request while still providing useful intermediate information, or satisfy part of the harmful objective without making the violation explicit. Since existing attacks use different judges to define success, reported ASR can change simply because the decision boundary between failure, partial progress, and success is drawn differently.

To measure this effect, we compare each method’s self-reported ASR in the abundant setting with a cross-validated ASR, where success requires agreement from all three judges:
CoA-Binary
,
FITD-Binary
, and
Score-Judge
. Table
3
shows that judge choice can substantially change measured success rates. The largest drops occur for methods evaluated with more permissive binary judges, namely FITD and CoA. In comparison,
Score-Judge
is more stable under cross-validation, especially for Crescendo and XTeaming. This suggests that part of the apparent gap between attacks reflects differences in evaluation criteria rather than attack behavior. Multi-turn red-teaming evaluations should therefore report the judge, control it in direct comparisons, and cross-validate new judges against existing ones.

Under this stricter protocol, Crescendo is the strongest method on three of the four target models, while XTeaming is consistently second-best and outperforms Crescendo on GPT-5. This result suggests that recent gains in multi-turn jailbreak performance are not always due to more effective conversational search. Some improvements come from simply expanding the search space or changing the success criterion. Crescendo, despite its simpler fixed-strategy design, searches the attack space efficiently; XTeaming remains competitive, especially on the more robust GPT-5 target. Thus, controlled evaluation changes the interpretation of performance gains: higher ASR under an unconstrained pipeline does not necessarily imply a better attack mechanism.

4.4
Which components drive attack performance under fixed conditions?

Having evaluated attack effectiveness under controlled conditions, we next use MT-JailBench to identify which components contribute most to performance. Starting from Crescendo, we replace one component at a time with the corresponding component from other methods, moving from prompt generation to refinement and then to flow control. This order follows the operational dependencies of a multi-turn attack: prompt generation determines the main conversational trajectory and sets the space of possible attack attempts; refinement provides local repair when an individual turn fails or receives a weak judge score; and flow control decides how to allocate the limited interaction budget across advancing, retrying, restarting, or stopping.
This procedure is not meant to exhaustively search the full space of attack compositions. Instead, it provides a controlled component-attribution test: by changing one module at a time under fixed budgets, targets, and judges, MT-JailBench can measure whether a component remains useful outside its original end-to-end pipeline. After each stage, we carry the best-performing configuration forward to form a simple recomposition path, illustrating how the benchmark can support systematic attack analysis and guide stronger multi-turn methods.

Figure 2

Component ablations using Crescendo as the starting configuration.

Figure

shows that components contribute unevenly. Crescendo prompt generation is strongest; replacing it with generators from other attacks reduces ASR substantially, indicating that prompt generation is a major source of performance variation. Notably, Crescendo and XTeaming use online prompt generation, which dynamically adjusts each prompt based on the conversation history; their higher ASRs may reflect better incorporation of the target model’s responses. Refinement and flow control can further improve ASR when strong modules are selected, but their effects are more moderate, suggesting that they introduce less performance variation than prompt generation.

4.5
Is explicit strategy diversification necessary?

The component ablation raises a further question. Crescendo uses a fixed high-level strategy, yet provides the strongest generator in our study. This makes it unclear whether multi-turn red teaming requires an explicit pool of diversified strategies, or whether comparable diversity can arise from stochastic generation within a fixed strategy. To test this distinction, we compare Crescendo and XTeaming generation while fixing refinement and flow control to XTeaming. For Crescendo, we sample with
temperature
=1.2 and
top_p

For XTeaming, strategies are generated in advance, and each run uses a distinct strategy from this explicit strategy pool. This setup isolates the source of diversity: stochastic trajectory variation for Crescendo versus explicit strategy enumeration for XTeaming.

4.6
CrescendoX
: Can strong components be recomposed into a stronger attack?

The preceding experiments identify prompt generation and flow control as the main sources of performance variation. We next test whether this component-level finding is predictive rather than merely descriptive. If the component analysis captures transferable mechanisms, then recombining the strongest components should yield an attack that outperforms its source attacks. We therefore combine Crescendo prompt generation with XTeaming refinement and flow control, yielding

CrescendoX

Its ASR exceeds Crescendo’s by more than 20 percentage points on several targets, including Llama-4-Scout, Gemma-4-E4B-IT, Qwen-3.5-Flash, Grok-4.1-Fast, and Nemotron-3-Nano. It also preserves strong performance where XTeaming is already effective, rather than trading off performance across model families. Absolute ASRs should be interpreted as system-level measurements: proprietary results may reflect deployment safeguards as well as base-model behavior, including GPT-5 safety classifiers and reasoning-based monitoring
31
, Gemini-3-Pro prompt filtering
12

2
.
The main conclusion is that component-level analysis can do more than explain existing attacks: it can predict useful recompositions that generalize across target models.

5
Conclusion

We introduced MT-JailBench, a modular evaluation framework for benchmarking multi-turn jailbreaks under fixed experimental conditions. Rather than only reporting which attack achieves the highest success rate, MT-JailBench addresses a more diagnostic question: when resource budgets and evaluation criteria are controlled, which components drive attack success? Specifically, MT-JailBench decomposes multi-turn jailbreaks into evaluation function, attack strategy, prompt generation, refinement, and flow control modules, and implements existing attacks through standardized interfaces.

Our experiments show that end-to-end attack success rates can conflate conditions with mechanisms responsible for performance. Resource budgets and evaluation functions substantially affect reported ASR, so direct comparisons require these factors to be fixed. Under controlled conditions, prompt generation explains most performance variation, while refinement and flow control can provide moderate gains. Recombining the strongest components yields
CrescendoX
, which improves over its source attacks across target models. This result validates the central premise of MT-JailBench: component-level analysis is not only explanatory but predictive. More broadly, progress in multi-turn red teaming should be measured through controlled comparisons that reveal which assumptions, budgets, judges, and mechanisms produce attack success, rather than aggregate ASR alone.

Scope of evaluation and limitations.

MT-JailBench should be interpreted as a framework for controlled comparison, not as an absolute measure of model safety. Our evaluation assumes a black-box, text-only threat model, harmful behaviors from HarmBench
24
, LLM-based judges, and finite resource budgets. Measured attack success may change under different behavior sets, judges, resource budgets, access policies, API safeguards, or system-level guardrails.

Appendix A
Broader Impact Statement

This work aims to improve the safety and reliability of large language models by studying vulnerabilities that arise in multi-turn interactions. As LLMs are increasingly used as conversational agents, safety failures may emerge not from a single explicit prompt, but from objectives accumulated across turns. MT-JailBench provides a controlled framework for evaluating these failures and identifying which experimental choices and attack components drive reported success.

As with prior work in security and red teaming, our results are dual-use. The same analyses that identify weaknesses in current systems could be misused to construct stronger attacks. However, systematic evaluation is necessary for building defenses that remain effective when attackers adapt over multiple turns. We therefore frame MT-JailBench as a tool for controlled comparison, component-level diagnosis, and future defense testing, rather than as an absolute measure of model safety.

Overall, we expect this work to have a positive impact by improving the rigor of multi-turn red-teaming evaluations and supporting the development of defenses that account for accumulated conversational context.

Appendix C
MT-JailBench System Design

This section describes the engineering design of MT-JailBench and explains how modularity is achieved through standardized programming interfaces.

C.1
Attack Interfaces

MT-JailBench operationalizes the five-module attack decomposition through three core interfaces:
PromptGenerator
,
OutcomeEvaluator
, and
FlowController
. Each attack implemented in the framework must instantiate all three interfaces, while the framework handles execution details such as target-model interaction, conversation-state tracking, and coordination across stages.

1.

The
PromptGenerator
interface unifies attack-prompt generation and refinement. It produces the next attack prompt conditioned on the harmful objective, the current conversation history, and any evaluation signals from prior turns. It also supports prompt refinement under non-linear control flows, such as retrying failed turns or revisiting earlier turns in the conversation.

2.

The
OutcomeEvaluator
interface determines whether the attack has satisfied its success criterion and may additionally return richer evaluation signals. In its simplest form, it outputs a binary success indicator. More advanced implementations may incorporate harmfulness scores, semantic similarity metrics, or auxiliary feedback derived from the target model’s responses.

3.

The
FlowController
interface governs the attack trajectory by selecting the next action given the current state and evaluation results. MT-JailBench supports a flexible set of control-flow actions, including advancing to the next turn, retrying the current turn, reverting to a previous turn, and terminating with success or failure. It also supports more sophisticated strategies, such as performing multiple retries and selecting the highest-scoring outcome before proceeding.

C.2
Defense Interfaces

Mainstream jailbreak defenses can be broadly categorized into two complementary paradigms: prompt pre-processing and response post-processing, where “pre” and “post” denote the defense’s position relative to the target-model interaction. Prompt pre-processing operates on the input side: it analyzes and, when appropriate, transforms user prompts before they are passed to the target model. Such defenses may block malicious prompts or expose malicious intent within the prompt, emphasizing prevention by neutralizing jailbreak attempts before they reach the model. Response post-processing, in contrast, operates on the output side. It evaluates responses generated by the target model and intervenes when unsafe or policy-violating content is detected. Such interventions may include rewriting the response, filtering specific content, or replacing the response entirely with a refusal message, such as “Sorry, I cannot assist with your request.” These defenses emphasize mitigation by addressing harmful outputs after they have been produced. In practice, defense systems may combine both paradigms.

MT-JailBench formalizes defenses through a single interface,
TextProcessor
. The name reflects a key commonality across defenses: regardless of their internal complexity, they ultimately transform text inputs or outputs. The
TextProcessor
interface defines two abstract methods corresponding to prompt pre-processing and response post-processing, respectively. Each defense in the framework must implement at least one of these methods. MT-JailBench also supports defense composition: users can configure a list of defenses to be executed sequentially at runtime.

C.3
Workflow Orchestration

Figure 5
:

MT-JailBench system design. Red and blue boxes denote attack and defense components, respectively, and the orange box represents the target LLM. Yellow rounded nodes indicate data flow through the pipeline.

MT-JailBench structures the jailbreak workflow into discrete
turns
, each corresponding to one round of interaction among the attack components, optional defense components, and the target model. In each turn, the framework executes the following six steps, as illustrated in Figure
5
.

1.

Prompt generation
:
PromptGenerator
produces the attack prompt.

2.

Prompt pre-processing
:
TextProcessor
may sanitize or transform the prompt before it reaches the target model.

3.

Response generation
: The target LLM generates a response to the processed prompt.

4.

Response post-processing
:
TextProcessor
may filter, rewrite, or replace the response if unsafe content is detected.

5.

Outcome evaluation
:
OutcomeEvaluator
determines whether the attack succeeded and may return auxiliary signals, such as harmfulness scores, to guide subsequent steps.

6.

Flow control
:
FlowController
selects the next action based on the evaluation results. Depending on this decision, the framework may advance to the next turn, retry the current turn, roll back to a previous turn, or terminate the workflow.

C.4
Resource Configuration

MT-JailBench allows users to set the maximum number of conversation turns through the
max_turns
parameter. Because some multi-turn attacks retry a turn when the target model’s response is unsatisfactory, we introduce an additional parameter,
max_interactions
, to bound the total number of target-model exchanges. An
interaction
denotes one such exchange and its surrounding steps: generating the attack prompt, invoking the target model, evaluating the response, and making a flow-control decision. MT-JailBench also supports fine-grained control over attack-specific behavior, such as the number of retries allowed within a turn and whether the attack may restart after failure.

Appendix E
Detailed Comparison of Multi-turn Jailbreak Components

In this section, we present a structured comparison of representative multi-turn jailbreak methods through the lens of the modular formulation introduced in Sec.
3.2
. We decompose each approach into five core components, including attack strategy, prompt generation, refinement, evaluation, and flow control. This perspective exposes key design differences that are often obscured in end-to-end implementations.

Crescendo:

•

Attack Strategy:
A fixed prompt template with few-shot examples for attack prompt generation.

•

Prompt Generation:
The first-turn prompt is generated using a fixed prompt template, and subsequent prompts are generated based on the conversation history.

•

Prompt Refinement:
The prompt is updated by the attacker LLM based on the target model’s response.

•

Evaluation Function:
In our implementation, Crescendo uses the same 5-score judge as in XTeaming and ActorBreaker.

•

Control Policy:
In our implementation, Cresceendo uses a refusal-guided policy that retries when the judge assigns a score of 1, and otherwise proceeds to the next turn.

Chain-of-Attack:

•

Attack Strategy:
A fixed prompt template with few-shot examples for attack prompt generation.

•

Prompt Generation:
CoA pre-generates multiple candidate chains (i.e., a set of attack sequences) and selects one based on the range of semantic similarity scores to the harmful target, preferring wider ranges. The selected chain is then used as the attack queries and executed sequentially.

•

Prompt Refinement:
The prompt is updated by the attacker LLM based on the target model’s response, while maintaining a similarity score close to the original.

•

Evaluation Function:
CoA uses a binary judge (0/1) to determine success.

•

Control Policy:
A similarity-guided policy that computes three semantic similarity scores after each turn:

–

sem
: similarity between the current response and the harmful target

–

posted_sem
: similarity from the previous turn

–

base_sem
: similarity computed using a response generated without prior conversation history, measuring the effect of accumulated context

The policy proceeds when
sem
exceeds both
base_sem
and
posted_sem
, backtracks when it is lower than both, and otherwise retries the current turn.

Foot-in-the-Door:

•

Attack Strategy:
Fixed prompt templates that start from benign queries via transformation and progressively generate intermediate queries with increasing semantic proximity to the harmful behavior.

•

Prompt Generation:
FITD pre-generates multiple trajectories (i.e., sets of attack sequences) and merges them into a single trajectory, which is then executed sequentially.

•

Prompt Refinement:
FITD applies two mechanisms:
Re-align
, which makes the target response more closely aligned to the prompt, and
Slippery Slope Paraphrase
, which inserts an intermediate step to smooth transitions between successive turns.

•

Evaluation Function:
FITD uses a binary judge (“safe” vs. “unsafe”) to determine success.

•

Control Policy:

A refusal-guided policy that retries when refusal is detected via string matching (e.g., “I can’t”, “I’m sorry”). Upon refusal, it invokes an LLM to determine alignment: if misaligned, apply
Re-align
; otherwise, apply
Slippery Slope Paraphrase
.

ActorBreaker:

•

Attack Strategy:
A fixed prompt template first extracts multiple “actors” from the harmful behavior, which are then used as anchors in a second template to dynamically condition attack generation.

•

Prompt Generation:
Conditioned on a selected “actor”, it generates an attack sequence, which are executed sequentially.

•

Prompt Refinement:
The prompt is updated by the attacker LLM based on the target model’s response and the initial attack queries.

•

Evaluation Function:
ActorBreaker uses a 5-score judge to determine success.

•

Control Policy:
A refusal-guided policy that retries when a binary judge indicates rejection, and otherwise proceeds to the next turn. If the attack still fails after the final turn, it restarts from turn 1 with a different actor.

XTeaming:

•

Attack Strategy:

Instead of the “actor” used in ActorBreaker, XTeaming employs an LLM to generate more structured conditioning signals, including persona, context, approach, and turn-level plan, which are collectively referred to as strategies. These strategies serve as anchors to dynamically condition attack generation.

•

Prompt Generation:

The first-turn prompt is generated using a turn-level plan, followed by dynamic generation of subsequent prompts conditioned on the conversation history and the plan.

•

Prompt Refinement:
XTeaming employs TextGrad, which treats natural language feedback as gradients. It consists of three LLM-backed steps: (1) forward pass to produce a textual loss, (2) backward pass to generate textual gradients (feedback), and (3) an update step that refines the prompt using this feedback.

•

Evaluation Function:
XTeaming uses a 5-score judge to determine success.

•

Control Policy:
A score-guided policy that advances only when the current score exceeds the best score observed so far. If the attack still fails after the final turn, it restarts from turn 1 with a different strategy.

Simplifications for prompt refinement.

1.

Crescendo prompt refinement:
Same as in Appendix
E

2.

CoA prompt refinement:
The original CoA uses the pre-generated attack sequence during refinement. Since not all attacks pre-generate such a sequence (e.g., Crescendo), we instead use the prompt from the conversation history.
The original CoA updater attempts to generate a new prompt whose semantic similarity to the harmful target exceeds a threshold. We retain this behavior and cap the number of attempts at 5, including the first attempt. This preserves fairness, as the retry process does not involve invoking the target model directly.

3.

FITD prompt refinement:
The original FITD uses two major refinement strategies:
Re-align
and
Slippery Slope Paraphrase
. We implement only
Slippery Slope Paraphrase
, where an intermediate turn is inserted to make the transition smoother upon refusal, as it better captures the core methodology of FITD and is more compatible with other attacks.
The original FITD also checks the target model for refusal and regenerates accordingly. To ensure fairness, we removed this early refusal check, since it bypasses the framework and interacts with the target model directly.

4.

Actor prompt refinement:

We discard the
output_type
parameter in its refinement template for compatibility, as it is specific to ActorBreaker. This parameter controls the format of the generated refinement output, but is not required for other attacks.

5.

XTeaming prompt refinement:
No changes. TextGrad is naturally compatible with other attacks.

2.

CoA flow control:

The original CoA flow control keeps retrying or backtracking until it either succeeds or exhausts available resources. For fairness, we removed this infinite loop. In addition, we replaced backtracking with retrying to maintain compatibility with other attacks. The final simplified logic is: continue if the current semantic similarity score is higher than both the previous turn’s score and the
base_score
(obtained by invoking the target model without conversation history); otherwise, retry.
