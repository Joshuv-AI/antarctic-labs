Generating the mapping class group of a nonorientable surface of genus g ≥ 13 g\geq 13 by two elements
Berkay Aybak, Hasan Ozden

Abstract
Let N g N_{g} be a closed, connected, nonorientable surface of genus g g . We prove that for g ≥ 13 g\geq 13 , the mapping class group Mod ⁡ ( N g ) \Mod(N_{g}) can be generated by exactly two elements. This improves the previously known bound of g ≥ 19 g\geq 19 .



2020 Mathematics Subject Classification.

Primary 57K20; Secondary 20F05, 57M07.

Keywords.

Mapping class group, nonorientable surface, generating set,
crosscap transposition, Dehn twist.

1
Introduction

Let
N_{g}
be a closed, connected, nonorientable surface of genus
g
.
The
mapping class group

\Mod(N_{g})
is the group of isotopy classes
of self-diffeomorphisms of
N_{g}
.
Finding minimal generating sets for mapping class groups is a long-standing
problem in geometric group theory and low-dimensional topology.

Because
\Mod(N_{g})
is non-abelian, every generating set must contain at
least two elements.
Szepietowski
[
Sze06
]
proved that
\Mod(N_{g})
is generated by
three elements for all
g\geq 3
.
Later, Altunöz, Pamuk, and Yıldız
[
APY21
]
answered a question
posed in
[
Far06
, Problem 3.1(a)]
by showing that
\Mod(N_{g})
can be
generated by exactly two elements for
g\geq 19
, one of which is a periodic
rotation of order
g
.

The genus bound
g\geq 19
in
[
APY21
]
is essentially a geometric
limitation: to isolate the necessary Dehn twists via the lantern relation,
the surface must be large enough so that the supports of the shifted
generators do not accidentally overlap.
In this note we lower the genus bound by choosing a different second
generator and employing a more efficient sequence of commutators.

Our main results are the following.

Theorem 1.1
.

For
g\geq 14
, the mapping class group
\Mod(N_{g})
is generated by two
elements,
T
and
u_{g-4}A_{2}C_{2}^{-1}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

The mapping class group
\Mod(N_{13})
is generated by two
elements,
T
and
u_{9}A_{2}B_{1}^{-1}
.

2
Preliminaries and the main results

Throughout this paper, curves on
N_{g}
and self-diffeomorphisms of
N_{g}

are considered up to isotopy.
We denote the right-handed Dehn twist about a two-sided simple closed curve

a
by the corresponding capital letter
A
.
We use the standard model for
N_{g}
as a sphere with
g
crosscaps

\mathcal{C}_{1},\dots,\mathcal{C}_{g}
arranged in a circular position,
and we let
T
denote the rotation by
2\pi/g
that maps
\mathcal{C}_{i}

to
\mathcal{C}_{i+1}
(indices taken modulo
g
).
For a model of the surface
N_{g}
and the classical two-sided curves used in
what follows, we refer to
Figure 2
.
The
geometric intersection number
of two isotopy classes of curves
a

and
b
is

i(a,b)=\min\bigl\{|\alpha\cap\beta|:\alpha\in a,\,\beta\in b\bigr\}.

For a family of two-sided simple closed curves, the union of their regular
neighborhoods is an orientable subsurface, which allows us to apply Dehn-twist
relations from the orientable setting; for these we always refer to

[
FM12
]
.

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
a
and
b
be isotopy classes of two-sided simple closed curves.
The following statements are equivalent
:

(i)

i(a,b)=0
,

(ii)

AB=BA
,

(iii)

A(b)=b
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
a
and
b
be isotopy classes of two-sided simple closed curves with

i(a,b)=1
.
Then

AB(a)=b.

For nonorientable surfaces, Dehn twists alone do not suffice to generate

\Mod(N_{g})
; additional elements are needed.
Let
K
be a subsurface of
N_{g}
(for
g\geq 2
) homeomorphic to a Klein
bottle with one boundary component;
K
contains exactly two crosscaps.
A
crosscap transposition
is a self-homeomorphism of
N_{g}
supported
on such a subsurface
K
that interchanges its two crosscaps.
The effect of a crosscap transposition on an arc
c
is illustrated in

Figure 1
.
We write
u_{\mu,\alpha}
or
u_{i}
for a crosscap transposition depending on
the context; the notation
u_{i}
stands for the transposition of the

i
-th and
(i{+}1)
-th crosscaps. By
\alpha_{i}
, we denote the boundary for the support of the crosscap transposition
u_{i}
.

Figure 1:
A crosscap transposition and the image of the arc
c
.

The following standard conjugation relations will be used throughout; see

[
Chi69
, Lemma 0]
and
[
LS17
, Relation 2.3]
.

•

If
f(a)=b
, then
fAf^{-1}=B^{s}
, where
s=\pm 1
according as

f
preserves or reverses the local orientation.

•

f\,u_{\mu,\alpha}\,f^{-1}=u_{f(\mu),f(\alpha)}
.

Since the rotation
T
maps
\mathcal{C}_{i}
to
\mathcal{C}_{i+1}
, we have
in particular
Tu_{i}T^{-1}=u_{i+1}
.
Also, note that
T^{k-1}(a_{2})=\gamma_{k}
, where
\gamma_{k}
is a two-sided curve passing through
k
,
(k+1)
,
(k+2)
and
(k+3)
indexed crosscaps (modulo
g
).

Figure 2:
A model of
N_{g}
for
g=2r+2
(or
g=2r+1
after removing
the last crosscap; in that case the curve
c_{r}
disappears) and some
classical two-sided curves.

Our proof reduces to establishing the following generating set.

Theorem 2.3

(
[
APY21
]
)
.

For
g\geq 7
, the mapping class group
\Mod(N_{g})
is generated by

T
,
A_{1}A_{2}^{-1}
,
B_{1}B_{2}^{-1}
, and
u_{g-1}
.

We can now prove
Theorem 1.1
.

Proof of
Theorem 1.1
.

Let
G
denote the subgroup of
\Mod(N_{g})
generated by
T
and

u_{g-4}A_{2}C_{2}^{-1}
.
Our goal is to show that
G
contains
A_{1}A_{2}^{-1}
,
B_{1}B_{2}^{-1}
,
and
u_{g-1}
from
Theorem 2.3
, and hence
G=\Mod(N_{g})
.

Set
G_{1}=u_{g-4}A_{2}C_{2}^{-1}
.
Conjugating by
T^{3}
gives

G_{2}=T^{3}G_{1}T^{-3}=u_{g-1}\Gamma_{4}B_{4}^{-1}\in G.

Using
G_{1}
and
G_{2}
we form the commutator

G_{3}=(G_{2}G_{1})\,G_{2}\,(G_{2}G_{1})^{-1}=u_{g-1}A_{2}B_{4}^{-1}\in G,

and then

G_{3}G_{2}^{-1}=A_{2}\Gamma_{4}^{-1}\in G.

Conjugating by
T
and then by
T^{3}
successively yields

\displaystyle T(A_{2}\Gamma_{4}^{-1})T^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{2}\Gamma_{5}^{-1}\in G,

\displaystyle T^{3}(\Gamma_{2}\Gamma_{5}^{-1})T^{-3}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{5}\Gamma_{8}^{-1}\in G.

Forming the product

G_{4}=(\Gamma_{2}\Gamma_{5}^{-1})(\Gamma_{5}\Gamma_{8}^{-1})=\Gamma_{2}\Gamma_{8}^{-1}\in G.

Since
\gamma_{2}
intersects
a_{2}
(with
i(\gamma_{2},a_{2})=1
), by

Lemma 2.2
and the conjugation formula,

G_{5}=(G_{4}G_{3})\,G_{4}\,(G_{4}G_{3})^{-1}=A_{2}\Gamma_{8}^{-1}\in G.

Hence
G_{4}G_{5}^{-1}=A_{2}\Gamma_{2}^{-1}\in G
.
Conjugating this element with the powers of
T
gives elements of
the form
\Gamma_{i}\Gamma_{i+1}^{-1}\in G
for
i=2,\ldots,g-4
.
Telescoping these yields

G_{6}=(A_{2}\Gamma_{2}^{-1})(\Gamma_{2}\Gamma_{3}^{-1})\cdots(\Gamma_{5}\Gamma_{6}^{-1})=A_{2}\Gamma_{6}^{-1}\in G.

Conjugating
G_{6}
by
G_{6}G_{1}
gives

G_{7}=(G_{6}G_{1})\,G_{6}\,(G_{6}G_{1})^{-1}=A_{2}C_{2}^{-1}\in G.

We can then isolate a single crosscap transposition:

G_{1}G_{7}^{-1}=(u_{g-4}A_{2}C_{2}^{-1})(C_{2}A_{2}^{-1})=u_{g-4}\in G.

Conjugating
u_{g-4}
with powers of
T
yields
u_{j}\in G
for all

j=1,\ldots,g-1
.
In particular
u_{g-1}\in G
, so from
G_{3}=u_{g-1}A_{2}B_{4}^{-1}\in G

we extract
A_{2}B_{4}^{-1}\in G
.

With
G_{7}=A_{2}C_{2}^{-1}\in G
and
A_{2}B_{4}^{-1}\in G
we form

G_{7}^{-1}(A_{2}B_{4}^{-1})=C_{2}B_{4}^{-1}\in G.

Conjugating by powers of
T
:

\displaystyle G_{8}

\displaystyle=T^{-3}(C_{2}B_{4}^{-1})T^{3}=B_{1}C_{2}^{-1}\in G,

\displaystyle G_{9}

\displaystyle=T^{2}(B_{1}C_{2}^{-1})T^{-2}=B_{2}C_{3}^{-1}\in G.

The commutator

(G_{8}G_{9}^{-1})\,G_{8}\,(G_{8}G_{9}^{-1})^{-1}=B_{1}B_{2}^{-1}\in G

gives the third generator of
Theorem 2.3
.

Finally, conjugating
B_{1}B_{2}^{-1}
with powers of
T
shows that

A_{1}C_{1}^{-1}
and
C_{1}C_{2}^{-1}
belong to
G
.
Their product gives
A_{1}C_{2}^{-1}\in G
, and then

(A_{1}C_{2}^{-1})(C_{2}A_{2}^{-1})=A_{1}A_{2}^{-1}\in G

supplies the last generator of
Theorem 2.3
, completing the proof.
∎

The ideas concerning the proof for
Theorem 1.2
are quite similar, with a subtle change; we use a different method to obtain the element
A_{2}\Gamma^{-1}_{2}
.

Sketch of the Proof of
Theorem 1.2
.

Let
H
denote the subgroup of
\Mod(N_{13})
generated by
T
and

u_{9}A_{2}B_{1}^{-1}
.
We aim to show that
H
contains
A_{1}A_{2}^{-1}
,
B_{1}B_{2}^{-1}
,
and
u_{12}
from
Theorem 2.3
, and hence
H=\Mod(N_{13})
. Following the ideas of the proof of
Theorem 1.1
closely, we obtain the elements:

•

H_{1}=u_{9}A_{2}B_{1}^{-1}
,

•

H_{2}=T^{3}H_{1}T^{-3}=u_{12}\Gamma_{4}C_{2}^{-1},

•

H_{3}=(H_{2}H_{1})H_{2}(H_{2}H_{1})^{-1}=u_{12}A_{2}C_{2}^{-1}
,

Using these, we also get
H_{3}H_{2}^{-1}=A_{2}\Gamma_{4}^{-1}\in H.
Conjugating this element by
T
and then by
T^{3}
successively yields

\displaystyle T(A_{2}\Gamma_{4}^{-1})T^{-1}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{2}\Gamma_{5}^{-1}\in H,

\displaystyle T^{3}(\Gamma_{2}\Gamma_{5}^{-1})T^{-3}

\displaystyle=\Gamma_{5}\Gamma_{8}^{-1}\in H.

These elements form the product

H_{4}=(\Gamma_{2}\Gamma_{5}^{-1})(\Gamma_{5}\Gamma_{8}^{-1})=\Gamma_{2}\Gamma_{8}^{-1}\in H.

By further conjugating
H_{4}
with
T^{3}
, we obtain

T^{3}H_{4}T^{-3}=\Gamma_{5}\Gamma_{11}^{-1}\in H.

By using this element, we also form the product

(\Gamma_{2}\Gamma_{5}^{-1})(\Gamma_{5}\Gamma_{11}^{-1})=\Gamma_{2}\Gamma_{11}^{-1}\in H.

On the other hand, conjugating
H_{3}H_{2}^{-1}=A_{2}\Gamma_{4}^{-1}
with
T^{-3}
gives

T^{-3}(A_{2}\Gamma_{4}^{-1})T^{3}=\Gamma_{11}A_{2}^{-1}\in H.

Also, product of gathered elements yields

H_{5}=(\Gamma_{2}\Gamma_{11}^{-1})(\Gamma_{11}A_{2}^{-1})=\Gamma_{2}A_{2}^{-1}\in H.

The rest of the proof follows as in
Theorem 1.1
, with slight adjustments; we gather the elements

•

H_{6}=(A_{2}\Gamma_{2}^{-1})(\Gamma_{2}\Gamma_{3}^{-1})\cdots(\Gamma_{5}\Gamma_{6}^{-1})=A_{2}\Gamma_{6}^{-1}
,

•

H_{7}=(H_{6}H_{3})H_{6}(H_{6}H_{3})^{-1}=A_{2}C_{2}^{-1}
,

•

H_{3}H^{-1}_{7}=u_{12},

•

u_{9}^{-1}H_{1}=A_{2}B_{1}^{-1},

•

H_{8}=(B_{1}A_{2}^{-1})H_{7}=B_{1}C_{2}^{-1}.

∎
