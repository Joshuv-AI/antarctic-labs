Latent-Conditioned Parameterized Quantum Circuits as Universal Approximators for Distributions over Quantum States
Quoc Hoan Tran, Koki Chinzei, Yasuhiro Endo, Hirotaka Oshima

Abstract
Many applications in quantum simulation, quantum chemistry, and quantum machine learning require not a single quantum state but an ensemble of states characterizing the heterogeneity of a target system. Preparing such ensembles state-by-state is prohibitive in both variational and fault-tolerant settings, motivating a generative-modeling approach. We introduce latent-conditioned parameterized quantum circuits (LPQCs), a hybrid quantum-classical framework in which classical neural networks map a latent variable sampled from a prior distribution to the parameters of a parameterized quantum circuit. We prove that LPQCs are universal approximators for probability measures over density operators in the 1 1 -Wasserstein distance, extending classical universal approximation theorems to the quantum-distribution setting. We additionally introduce a multimodal latent prior and a mixture-of-experts circuit architecture, and show that it empirically alleviates the barren plateau problem during optimization. Numerical experiments validate the framework on a synthetic multi-cluster ensemble of mixed quantum states and on a QM9-derived ensemble of 3-D molecular structures. In these tasks, LPQC outperforms recent quantum generative baselines while remaining competitive with typical classical baselines at substantially reduced output dimensionality. By leveraging classical expressivity in the latent space, LPQCs offer a tractable route to quantum generative modeling.



I
Introduction

Generative models for quantum data play a pivotal role in advancing quantum technologies. They approximate distributions over quantum states that are intractable classically. The central object is typically not a single state but an ensemble of
density operators, and such ensembles arise in several settings. In
quantum simulation, the finite-temperature equilibrium of a many-body
system is a Gibbs ensemble, whose preparation is a long-standing
challenge for quantum algorithms
32
;
24
.
In quantum chemistry, the many distinct molecular geometries in datasets
such as QM9
26
can each be represented as a
quantum state, so that the dataset induces an ensemble of quantum states.
Such a distribution, once learned, can support virtual screening, property
prediction, and data augmentation for hybrid quantum-classical
pipelines
27
;
35
.
In quantum machine learning (QML), state ensembles serve as training data for tasks such as anomaly
detection
20
and for warm-starting variational algorithms
40
.

Preparing such ensembles element by element is prohibitive in both near-term and fault-tolerant settings. Variational methods such as the Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE)
23
require a fresh optimization loop with enormous circuit evaluation times for each new target. Fault-tolerant routines based on quantum phase estimation (QPE)
1
, or block-encoded state preparation
13
instead need deep circuits and substantial ancilla overhead per state, placing them beyond near-term reach. The exponential dimensionality of quantum state spaces compounds this challenge, calling for universal generative frameworks that capture multimodal structure across these settings. A generative model that, once trained, produces samples from the entire ensemble in a single forward pass would dramatically reduce this cost.

Parameterized quantum circuits (PQCs) form a cornerstone of QML, serving as ansatze for optimization, classification, and generative modeling. Comprising tunable gates optimized classically, PQCs power hybrid variational quantum algorithms
4
such as the VQE and the Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)
9
. Recent results establish PQCs as universal approximators of continuous functions
14
and multivariate distributions
2
via expectation-value sampling. However, applying PQCs to distributions over quantum states, such as ensembles of density matrices, remains underexplored and inefficient, hindered by barren plateaus
21
where gradients vanish exponentially with system size.

We prove that latent-conditioned PQCs (LPQCs)—where classical neural networks (NNs) generate circuit parameters conditioned on latent variables—universally approximate any probability measure on the space of
n
-qubit density operators in the 1-Wasserstein distance. To enable practical training, we incorporate multimodal prior distributions in the latent space and soft attention mechanisms for multiple experts. We further show that the latent prior mitigates barren plateaus by biasing initializations toward gradient-rich regions. Numerical simulations of multi-cluster mixed states and quantum-chemical datasets demonstrate the model’s efficiency.

II
Preliminaries

Let
{\mathcal{H}}_{S}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2^{n}}
be the Hilbert space of an
n
-qubit system. Given the set
{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{S})
of bounded linear operators on
{\mathcal{H}}_{S}
, we define the convex set of density operators as

{\mathcal{D}}\!({\mathcal{H}}_{S}):=\bigl\{\rho\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}}_{S})\mid\rho\geq 0,\;\operatorname{Tr}(\rho)=1\bigr\}.

For two ensembles
P,Q
on
{\mathcal{D}}\!({\mathcal{H}}_{S})
we employ the 1-Wasserstein distance:

\mathcal{W}_{1}(P,Q):=\inf_{\pi\in\Pi(P,Q)}\,\mathbb{E}_{(\rho,\sigma)\sim\pi}\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{2}\,\left\lVert\rho-\sigma\right\rVert_{1}\Bigr],

(1)

where
\Pi(P,Q)
is the set of couplings having
P,Q
as marginals.

The learning task.—

Let
{\mathcal{E}}=\{(p_{j},\rho_{j})\}_{j}
denote an ensemble of
n
-qubit quantum states, where
\rho_{j}\in{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{H}}_{S})
and
\{p_{j}\}
are non-negative weights satisfying
\sum_{j}p_{j}=1
. The ensemble induces a probability measure
Q
on
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{H}}_{S})
, namely
Q=\sum_{j}p_{j}\,\delta_{\rho_{j}}
in the atomic case, or more generally any probability measure on
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{H}}_{S})
when the ensemble is continuous. Note that
Q
is not the same object as the averaged density matrix
\overline{\rho}=\sum_{j}p_{j}\rho_{j}\in{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{H}}_{S})
: averaging discards the spectral and multimodal structure.
Two ensembles with very different pure-state structures can share the same averaged density matrix.
Our goal is to construct from a finite sample of
{\mathcal{E}}
a generative model that approximates the underlying
Q
in the

1
-Wasserstein distance
\mathcal{W}_{1}
on
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{H}}_{S})
.

Latent-conditioned PQCs (LPQC).—

We fix a latent space
{\mathcal{Z}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
endowed with a prior density
r({\bm{z}})
that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure. To handle both pure states and mixed states, we introduce an ancillary Hilbert space
{\mathcal{H}}_{A}\cong\mathbb{C}^{2^{m}}
with
m
qubits. An LPQC is a family of unitaries acting on
{\mathcal{H}}_{S}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{A}
as

U({\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}))=U_{L}\bigl(\theta_{L}({\bm{z}})\bigr)\cdots U_{1}\bigl(\theta_{1}({\bm{z}})\bigr),\quad{\bm{z}}\in{\mathcal{Z}},

(2)

where the primitive gates
U_{\ell}(\cdot)
come from a fixed universal gate set.
Each angle vector
\theta_{\ell}({\bm{z}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K_{\ell}}
is produced by a classical neural network (NN)
f_{\ell}(\,\cdot\,;{\bm{w}}_{\ell}):{\mathcal{Z}}\to{\mathbb{R}}^{K_{l}}
with parameters
{\bm{w}}_{\ell}
(Fig.
1
).
Given
{\bm{w}}=({\bm{w}}_{1},\ldots,{\bm{w}}_{L})
, sampling
{\bm{z}}\sim r
and applying
U(\theta({\bm{z}}))
to
|0^{n+m}\rangle
followed by partial trace over
{\mathcal{H}}_{A}
yields the ensemble

\displaystyle(r,\rho_{\bm{w}})

\displaystyle:=\Bigl\{\,r({\bm{z}}),\;\rho_{\bm{w}}({\bm{z}}):=

\displaystyle\operatorname{Tr}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{A}}\bigl[U(\theta({\bm{z}}))|0^{n+m}\rangle\langle 0^{n+m}|U^{\dagger}(\theta({\bm{z}}))\bigr]\,\Bigr\}_{{\bm{z}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}}.

(3)

We want to show that
(r,\rho_{\bm{w}})
can approximate any target distribution
Q
on
{\mathcal{D}}\!({\mathcal{H}}_{S})
arbitrarily well in
\mathcal{W}_{1}
.

III
Universal Approximation Theorem (UAT) for Quantum States Distribution

We state and prove the main theoretical result.

Theorem 1

(Approximate universality of LPQCs)
.

Let
Q
be a probability measure on
{\mathcal{D}}\!({\mathcal{H}}_{S})
. Consider a compact latent space
{\mathcal{Z}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
, and a prior density
r
that is positive, continuous, and absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
{\mathcal{Z}}
. Then, there is an LPQC class
U({\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}))
such that for every
\varepsilon>0
the ensemble
(r,\rho_{\bm{w}})
defined in Eq. (
II
) satisfies

\mathcal{W}_{1}\bigl(Q,\,(r,\rho_{\bm{w}})\bigr)\leq\varepsilon\quad\textup{for some }{\bm{w}}.

(4)

Proof.

The proof proceeds in four steps that progressively bridge the gap between the arbitrary target distribution
Q
on
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{H}}_{S})
and the ensemble
(r,\rho_{\bm{w}})
produced by LPQC.

(i)

Finite catalogue approximation
(Sec.
III.1
): we exploit the compactness of
{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{H}}_{S})
in the trace-norm topology to replace
Q
with a finite atomic ensemble
P^{\ast}=\{(q_{i},\rho_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
that is
\varepsilon/3
-close to
Q
in
\mathcal{W}_{1}
.

(ii)

Tiling the latent space
: because the prior
r
is nonatomic, we partition
{\mathcal{Z}}
into disjoint measurable regions
\{R_{i}\}
whose probability masses match the catalogue weights
q_{i}
, and we assign to each region a branch unitary
V_{i}
that purifies the catalogue state
\rho_{i}
on
{\mathcal{H}}_{S}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{A}
. This defines a piecewise-constant target decoder
V({\bm{z}})
.

(iii)

Smoothing
: since
V({\bm{z}})
is discontinuous at region boundaries, we mollify the underlying Hamiltonian
H({\bm{z}})
with a narrow kernel to obtain a continuous approximation
V^{\prime}({\bm{z}})
that agrees with
V({\bm{z}})
.

(iv)

Uniform PQC approximation
(Sec.
III.2
): Lemma
3
combines the classical universal approximation theorem for NNs
f_{\ell}
with the density of the primitive gate set in
\mathrm{SU}(2^{n+m})
to produce a circuit
U({\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}))
that is close to
V^{\prime}({\bm{z}})
in operator norm under
r
. Translating this operator-norm bound into a trace-distance bound on the reduced states via the Fuchs–van de Graaf relation, and assembling a coupling that pairs each
\rho_{\bm{w}}({\bm{z}})
with the catalogue state of the cell containing
{\bm{z}}
, yields a Wasserstein bound of
2\varepsilon/3
between
(r,\rho_{\bm{w}})
and
P^{\ast}
. The triangle inequality then gives the desired bound
\mathcal{W}_{1}(Q,(r,\rho_{\bm{w}}))\leq\varepsilon
.

∎

The detailed steps of the proof are listed below.

III.1
Finite Catalogue Approximation

The first step replaces the arbitrary target
Q
by an atomic approximation.

Lemma 2

(Finite catalogue approximation)
.

For every probability measure
Q
on
{\mathcal{D}}\!({\mathcal{H}}_{S})
and every
\varepsilon>0
there exists a finite ensemble
P^{\ast}=\{(q_{i},\rho_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}\subset{\mathcal{D}}\!({\mathcal{H}}_{S})
such that
\mathcal{W}_{1}\bigl(Q,P^{\ast}\bigr)\leq\varepsilon
.

Proof.

Since
{\mathcal{D}}\!({\mathcal{H}}_{S})
can be equipped with the trace norm topology, it is compact and metrizable and then totally bounded.
Define the metric
d(\rho,\sigma)=\tfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert\rho-\sigma\right\rVert_{1}
, for any
\varepsilon>0
there exists a finite
\varepsilon
-net
\{\rho_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
such that the closed balls
B(\rho_{i};\varepsilon):=\{\sigma:d(\sigma,\rho_{i})\leq\varepsilon\}
cover the space. We define the Voronoi cells
C_{i}:=\bigl\{\sigma:d(\sigma,\rho_{i})\leq d(\sigma,\rho_{j})\text{ for all }j\bigr\}
and set
q_{i}:=Q(C_{i})
. If some
q_{i}=0
, we simply discard that index.
Now consider the coupling
\pi
obtained by sampling
\sigma\sim Q
and mapping it to
(\sigma,\rho_{i})
when
\sigma\in C_{i}
. Clearly,
\pi\in\Pi(Q,P^{\ast})
and for all pairs in the support we have
d(\sigma,\rho_{i})\leq\varepsilon
by construction. This deterministic coupling yields the bound for Eq. (
1
).
∎

III.2
Classical-Quantum Expressivity

To convert the finite catalogue into the output of a single latent-conditioned circuit, we need two ingredients: the ability to choose distinct latent regions that sample each catalogue element with the desired probability, and the ability to implement within each region a unitary that prepares the associated state. The following lemma settles the latter one.

Lemma 3

(Universal approximation of latent maps)
.

Let
{\mathcal{Z}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
be compact and let
V\colon{\mathcal{Z}}\to U(2^{n+m})
be measurable. Suppose

(i)

Each map
f_{\ell}(\,\cdot\,;{\bm{w}}_{\ell})
is a classical NN with activation functions satisfying the hypotheses of extensions of the classical universal approximation theorem
16
for measurable functions in
L^{1}
norm.

(ii)

Primitive gates
\{U_{\ell}(\cdot)\}
generate
\mathrm{SU}(2^{n+m})
densely.

Then for every
\delta>0
there exists a setting of the weights
{\bm{w}}:=\{{\bm{w}}_{\ell}\}_{\ell}
such that

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}\sim r}\,\left\lVert U({\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}))-V({\bm{z}})\right\rVert\leq\delta,

(5)

where
\theta_{\ell}({\bm{z}}):=f_{\ell}({\bm{z}};{\bm{w}}_{\ell})
and
U({\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}))
is the full circuit obtained by composing the primitive gates.

Proof.

For each layer
\ell
, we consider the target function
g_{\ell}:{\mathcal{Z}}\to\mathbb{R}
that returns the desired angle for the
\ell
-th primitive gate in some fixed decomposition of
V({\bm{z}})
(e.g. via cartesian coordinates in the Lie algebra). Extensions of the classical universal approximation theorem
16
imply that measurable real-valued functions on a compact domain can be approximated in
L^{1}
norm by NNs. Hence there exist weights
{\bm{w}}_{\ell}
such that

\displaystyle\int\lvert f_{\ell}({\bm{z}};{\bm{w}}_{\ell})-g_{\ell}({\bm{z}})\rvert r({\bm{z}})d{\bm{z}}\leq\delta/L.

(6)

We explicate why a perturbation on each gate angle translates into an operator-norm perturbation on the whole circuit.
For clarity, we write
U_{\ell}(\vartheta)=e^{-i\vartheta H_{\ell}}
with
\left\lVert H_{\ell}\right\rVert\leq 1
. Given two parameter vectors
\Theta=(\vartheta_{1},\dots,\vartheta_{L})
and
\Theta^{\prime}=(\vartheta_{1}^{\prime},\dots,\vartheta_{L}^{\prime})
, we define the corresponding circuits

U(\Theta)=U_{L}(\vartheta_{L})\cdots U_{1}(\vartheta_{1})
and
U(\Theta^{\prime})=U_{L}(\vartheta_{L}^{\prime})\cdots U_{1}(\vartheta_{1}^{\prime})
.
A telescoping expansion yields

\displaystyle U(\Theta)-U(\Theta^{\prime})=\sum_{k=1}^{L}U_{L}(\vartheta_{L})\cdots U_{k+1}(\vartheta_{k+1})

\displaystyle[U_{k}(\vartheta_{k})-U_{k}(\vartheta_{k}^{\prime})]U_{k-1}(\vartheta_{k-1}^{\prime})\cdots U_{1}(\vartheta_{1}^{\prime}).

(7)

Because every factor except the bracket is unitary (hence norm
1
), we can bound

\displaystyle\left\lVert U(\Theta)-U(\Theta^{\prime})\right\rVert\leq\sum_{k=1}^{L}\left\lVert U_{k}(\vartheta_{k})-U_{k}(\vartheta_{k}^{\prime})\right\rVert\leq\sum_{k=1}^{L}|\vartheta_{k}-\vartheta_{k}^{\prime}|.

(8)

The second inequality follows from Lemma
4
applied to the single-qubit exponentials, with the generator norm bound
\left\lVert H_{\ell}\right\rVert\leq 1
.

Taking
\Theta={\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}})=(f_{1}({\bm{z}};{\bm{w}}_{1}),\ldots,f_{L}({\bm{z}};{\bm{w}}_{L}))
and
\Theta^{\prime}=g({\bm{z}})=(g_{1}({\bm{z}}),\ldots,g_{L}({\bm{z}}))
, we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left\lVert U({\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}))-U(g({\bm{z}}))\right\rVert=\mathbb{E}\left\lVert U(\Theta)-U(\Theta^{\prime})\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{k=1}^{L}\mathbb{E}\left[|f_{k}({\bm{z}};{\bm{w}}_{k})-g_{k}({\bm{z}})|\right]\leq L\delta/L=\delta.

(9)

Since
U(g({\bm{z}}))
coincides with the target
V({\bm{z}})
by construction of the decomposition, the lemma follows.
∎

A useful corollary is the Lipschitz continuity of the matrix exponential, which we record for completeness.

Lemma 4

(Lipschitz continuity of
e^{iX}
)
.

For Hermitian
A,B\in{\mathcal{B}}({\mathcal{H}})
,

\left\lVert e^{iA}-e^{iB}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert A-B\right\rVert
.

Proof.

Write

e^{iA}-e^{iB}=\int_{0}^{1}\!\frac{d}{dt}\Bigl(e^{i\bigl((1-t)A+tB\bigr)}\Bigr)\,dt=i\int_{0}^{1}e^{i(1-t)A}\,(A-B)\,e^{itB}\,dt.

Taking the operator (spectral) norm and noting
\left\lVert e^{iX}\right\rVert=1
for Hermitian
X
yields the desired inequality.
∎

III.3
Proof for the UAT

We combine the results from subsections
III.1
and
III.2
to present the final proof of the UAT.

Proof.

(i) Catalogue approximation and tiling the latent space.

We invoke Lemma
2
to obtain a finite ensemble
P^{\ast}=\{(q_{i},\rho_{i})\}_{i=1}^{N}
satisfying the following bound:

\mathcal{W}_{1}(Q,P^{\ast})\leq\varepsilon/3.

(10)

Next, we prove that we can select pairwise disjoint measurable balls
R_{i}\subset{\mathcal{Z}}
whose volumes satisfy
\int_{R_{i}}r({\bm{z}})\,d{\bm{z}}=q_{i}
.

Since
r
is positive, continuous, and absolutely continuous with respect to Lebesgue measure on
{\mathcal{Z}}
, the induced measure
\mu(A)=\int_{A}r({\bm{z}})d{\bm{z}}
is a nonatomic probability measure. For nonatomic measures, it is always possible to partition
{\mathcal{Z}}
into disjoint measurable sets
\{R_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}
such that
\mu(R_{i})=q_{i}
and
\cup_{i=1}^{N}R_{i}={\mathcal{Z}}
(up to a set of
\mu
-measure zero). This follows from the flexibility of nonatomic spaces
11
.
Inductively, there exists a measurable
R_{i}\subset{\mathcal{Z}}
with
\mu(R_{1})=q_{1}
; then in the complement
{\mathcal{Z}}\backslash R_{1}
(which has measure
1-q_{1}
and remains nonatomic), there exists
R_{2}
with
\mu(R_{2})=q_{2}
, and so on. We define the bump functions
\chi_{i}({\bm{z}}):=\mathbbm{1}_{R_{i}}({\bm{z}})
so that
\sum_{i}\chi_{i}({\bm{z}})=1
almost everywhere.

(ii) Branch unitaries.
We choose for each
i
a purification
|\psi_{i}\rangle\in{\mathcal{H}}_{S}\otimes{\mathcal{H}}_{A}
such that
\operatorname{Tr}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{A}}|\psi_{i}\rangle\langle\psi_{i}|=\rho_{i}
. Consider a unitary
V_{i}\in U(2^{n+m})
such that
V_{i}|0^{n+m}\rangle=|\psi_{i}\rangle
. Let
H_{i}
be any Hermitian logarithm of
V_{i}
, i.e.
V_{i}=e^{iH_{i}}
with
\left\lVert H_{i}\right\rVert\leq\pi
. We define the target decoder map

V({\bm{z}}):=e^{iH({\bm{z}})},\qquad H({\bm{z}}):=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\chi_{i}({\bm{z}})\,H_{i}.

(11)

Because each
\chi_{i}
is bounded and
H_{i}
is Hermitian,
H(\cdot)
is measurable and
\left\lVert H({\bm{z}})\right\rVert\leq\pi
. Moreover, for
{\bm{z}}\in R_{i}
we have
\chi_{i}({\bm{z}})=1
and
\chi_{j\neq i}({\bm{z}})=0
, whence
V({\bm{z}})=V_{i}
.

(iii) Smoothing for continuity.

V({\bm{z}})
is piecewise constant, hence discontinuous at region boundaries. To enable approximation, we introduce a smoothed continuous approximation
V^{\prime}({\bm{z}})
by convolving
H({\bm{z}})
with a mollifier kernel
\phi_{\eta}
(e.g., Gaussian of width
\eta
), yielding
H^{\prime}({\bm{z}})=(H*\phi_{\eta})({\bm{z}})
, continuous, with
V^{\prime}({\bm{z}})=\exp(iH^{\prime}({\bm{z}}))
. We can choose
\eta
small so the boundary layers (where
\left\lVert H^{\prime}({\bm{z}})-H({\bm{z}})\right\rVert>0
) have total probability mass
\mu_{\eta}\leq\varepsilon^{2}/18
under
r
. Then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}\sim r}[\left\lVert V^{\prime}({\bm{z}})-V({\bm{z}})\right\rVert]\leq 0\cdot(1-\mu_{\eta})+2\cdot\mu_{\eta}\leq\varepsilon^{2}/9.

(12)

(iv) Uniform PQC approximation.
We apply Lemma
3
to the continuous
V^{\prime}({\bm{z}})
with
\delta=\varepsilon^{2}/9
to obtain circuit parameters
{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}})
satisfying

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}\sim r}\left\lVert U\bigl({\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}})\bigr)-V^{\prime}({\bm{z}})\right\rVert\leq\varepsilon^{2}/9.

We define
\rho_{V}({\bm{z}}):=\operatorname{Tr}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{A}}[|\psi({\bm{z}})\rangle\!\langle\psi({\bm{z}})|]
and note that
\rho_{\bm{w}}({\bm{z}})=\operatorname{Tr}_{{\mathcal{H}}_{A}}[|\phi({\bm{z}})\rangle\!\langle\phi({\bm{z}})|]
, where
|\phi({\bm{z}})\rangle=U({\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}))|0^{n+m}\rangle
and
|\psi({\bm{z}})\rangle=V({\bm{z}})|0^{n+m}\rangle
. We have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert\rho_{\bm{w}}({\bm{z}})-\rho_{V}({\bm{z}})\right\rVert_{1}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert|\phi({\bm{z}})\rangle\!\langle\phi({\bm{z}})|-|\psi({\bm{z}})\rangle\!\langle\psi({\bm{z}})|\right\rVert_{1}\Bigr]

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\mathbb{E}\left\lVert U({\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}))-V({\bm{z}})\right\rVert}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2\bigl(\mathbb{E}\left\lVert U({\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}))-V^{\prime}({\bm{z}})\right\rVert+\mathbb{E}\left\lVert V^{\prime}({\bm{z}})-V({\bm{z}})\right\rVert\bigr)}

\displaystyle\leq\sqrt{2(\varepsilon^{2}/9+\varepsilon^{2}/9)}=2\varepsilon/3.

(13)

The first inequality is from the contractivity of the trace norm under partial trace.
The second inequality is a direct application of the Fuchs–van de Graaf relation

\tfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert|\phi\rangle\!\langle\phi|-|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\right\rVert_{1}=\sqrt{1-|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^{2}}
and the bound
|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|\geq 1-\left\lVert U-V\right\rVert
when
|\psi\rangle=V|0\rangle
,
|\phi\rangle=U|0\rangle
, yielding
\tfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert|\phi\rangle\!\langle\phi|-|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\right\rVert_{1}\leq\sqrt{2\left\lVert U-V\right\rVert}
.
For a nonnegative random variable
X
, the Jensen’s inequality gives
{\mathbb{E}}[\sqrt{X}]\leq\sqrt{{\mathbb{E}}[X]}
.
Therefore,
\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert|\phi\rangle\!\langle\phi|-|\psi\rangle\!\langle\psi|\right\rVert_{1}\Bigr]\leq\mathbb{E}\Bigl[\sqrt{2\left\lVert U-V\right\rVert}\Bigr]\leq\sqrt{2\mathbb{E}\left\lVert U-V\right\rVert}
.

(v) Coupling and Wasserstein bound.
We construct a coupling
\pi
by sampling
{\bm{z}}\sim r
and pairing
\sigma({\bm{z}})=\rho_{\bm{w}}({\bm{z}})
with the fixed catalogue state
\tau_{i}=\rho_{V}({\bm{z}})
determined by the cell
R_{i}
containing
{\bm{z}}
. The measure form mixes differential
d{\bm{z}}
with discrete
di
as
\pi(d{\bm{z}},di)=r({\bm{z}})\,\mathbbm{1}_{R_{i}}({\bm{z}})\,d{\bm{z}}
.
Using Eq. (
III.3
), we evaluate the expected cost as

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert\sigma({\bm{z}})-\tau_{i}\right\rVert_{1}\Bigr]=\mathbb{E}_{\pi}\Bigl[\tfrac{1}{2}\left\lVert\sigma({\bm{z}})-\rho_{V}({\bm{z}})\right\rVert_{1}\Bigr]\leq\tfrac{2\varepsilon}{3}.

Because
\mathcal{W}_{1}
is the infimum over all couplings, we have

\mathcal{W}_{1}\bigl((r,\rho_{\bm{w}}),P^{*}\bigr)\leq 2\varepsilon/3.

(14)

Combining Eqs. (
10
) and (
14
) via the triangle inequality gives

\displaystyle\mathcal{W}_{1}\bigl(Q,\,(r,\rho_{\bm{w}})\bigr)\leq\mathcal{W}_{1}(Q,P^{\ast})+\mathcal{W}_{1}(P^{\ast},(r,\rho_{\bm{w}}))\leq\varepsilon.

This completes the proof.
∎

III.4
Smoothing via Soft Gating

The theoretical construction in Theorem
1
relies on a target decoder map
V({\bm{z}})
to prepare states from the finite catalogue. However, the hard transitions at region boundaries render
V({\bm{z}})
discontinuous and nondifferentiable, which blocks the differentiability arguments needed in step (iv) of the proof and would prevent gradient flow through
{\bm{z}}
in any practical realization. To address this within the proof, we replace the hard indicator functions
\chi_{i}({\bm{z}})
with smooth gating weights

\widetilde{\pi}_{i}({\bm{z}})\;=\;\operatorname{softmax}\bigl(f_{\mathrm{attn}}({\bm{z}})\bigr)_{i},

(15)

where
f_{\mathrm{attn}}
is a classical NN.
We then replace the hard
V({\bm{z}})=\exp\left(i\sum_{i}\chi_{i}({\bm{z}})H_{i}\right)
with a soft-attention decoder

\displaystyle V_{\mathrm{soft}}({\bm{z}})=\exp\bigl(i\,H_{\mathrm{soft}}({\bm{z}})\bigr),\quad H_{\mathrm{soft}}({\bm{z}})=\sum_{i}\widetilde{\pi}_{i}({\bm{z}})H_{i}.

(16)

This smoothing connects hard and soft branching: as the attention NN
f_{\mathrm{attn}}
becomes sufficiently expressive,
\widetilde{\pi}_{i}({\bm{z}})
can approximate the indicators
\chi_{i}({\bm{z}})
arbitrarily well by the classical universal approximation theorem
16
. Moreover,
\widetilde{\pi}_{i}
is
C^{\infty}
-smooth, allowing gradients
\nabla_{\bm{z}}V_{\mathrm{soft}}({\bm{z}})
to flow throughout the proof construction. The resulting operator-norm error satisfies

\displaystyle\|V_{\mathrm{soft}}({\bm{z}})-V({\bm{z}})\|\leq\|H_{\mathrm{soft}}({\bm{z}})-H({\bm{z}})\|

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{i}|\widetilde{\pi}_{i}({\bm{z}})-\chi_{i}({\bm{z}})|\,\|H_{i}\|\leq\pi\sum_{i}|\widetilde{\pi}_{i}({\bm{z}})-\chi_{i}({\bm{z}})|,

(17)

using
\|H_{i}\|\leq\pi
. Given
{\bm{z}}\in R_{j}
, then
\chi_{j}({\bm{z}})=1
and
\chi_{i\neq j}({\bm{z}})=0
, hence

\displaystyle\sum_{i}

\displaystyle|\widetilde{\pi}_{i}({\bm{z}})-\chi_{i}({\bm{z}})|

\displaystyle=(1-\widetilde{\pi}_{j}({\bm{z}}))+\sum_{i\neq j}\widetilde{\pi}_{i}({\bm{z}})

(18)

\displaystyle=1+\sum_{i}\widetilde{\pi}_{i}({\bm{z}})-2\widetilde{\pi}_{j}({\bm{z}})

(19)

\displaystyle=2(1-\widetilde{\pi}_{j}({\bm{z}}))\leq 2\max_{i}|\widetilde{\pi}_{i}({\bm{z}})-\chi_{i}({\bm{z}})|.

(20)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}\sim r}\|V_{\mathrm{soft}}({\bm{z}})

\displaystyle-V({\bm{z}})\|

\displaystyle\leq 2\pi\,\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}\sim r}[\max_{i}|\widetilde{\pi}_{i}({\bm{z}})-\chi_{i}({\bm{z}})|]\to 0,

(21)

so
V_{\mathrm{soft}}
inherits the universality of
V
within the proof of Theorem
1
while remaining fully differentiable.

IV
Practical Implementation

IV.1
Mixture of Experts

The mixture-of-experts (MoE) paradigm, originally introduced in classical machine learning
17
;
18
, has recently regained prominence as a scaling strategy for large NNs
31
;
10
.
Instead of a single monolithic network, the model maintains a collection of
E
specialized subnetworks (
experts
), and a learned gating function decides—for each input—which experts to consult and with what weights.
The motivation is twofold. First, when the target function or distribution has heterogeneous structures (e.g., distinct modes, clusters, or regimes), forcing one network to fit all of it leads to interference between regimes and slower training.
Routing each input to a specialized subset of experts mitigates this. Second, MoE provides a form of conditional computation: model capacity can be added by increasing
E
without proportionally increasing the cost per input, since only a few experts contribute meaningfully to any given output.

In our setting, both motivations apply. The target
Q
is a distribution over density matrices that is, in many cases of interest (e.g., the multi-cluster ensembles of Sec.
V
or chemically diverse molecular datasets), genuinely multimodal: different regions of the latent space
{\mathcal{Z}}
should map to qualitatively different families of quantum states. A single MLP
f({\bm{z}};{\bm{w}})
producing the full parameter vector
{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}})
must then encode all of this heterogeneity in one set of weights, which empirically slows convergence and limits final fidelity. The MoE architecture instead allocates
E
expert NNs
\{f^{(i)}({\bm{z}};{\bm{w}}^{(i)})\}_{i=1}^{E}
, each free to specialize on a portion of
{\mathcal{Z}}
, and uses a soft gating signal
\pi_{i}({\bm{z}})
to decide which experts contribute to producing the circuit parameters at any given
{\bm{z}}
. This is the structural counterpart of using a multimodal prior
r({\bm{z}})
: where the prior partitions the latent space into mode-aligned regions on the input side of the parameter-generation map, the MoE partitions capacity on the output side.

The soft gating mechanism
\widetilde{\pi}_{i}({\bm{z}})
introduced in Sec.
III.4
has a natural counterpart in the practical training architecture: we reuse the same form of softmax gating, but apply it to a different object. Whereas the soft-attention decoder
V_{\mathrm{soft}}
uses
\widetilde{\pi}_{i}({\bm{z}})
to mix branch Hamiltonians inside a single exponential [Eq. (
16
)], the MoE architecture uses gating weights to mix circuit parameters produced by separate expert networks. To keep this distinction explicit, we denote the MoE gating by

\pi_{i}({\bm{z}})=\frac{\exp\eta_{i}({\bm{z}})}{\sum_{j=1}^{E}\exp\eta_{j}({\bm{z}})},\quad\eta({\bm{z}})=f_{\mathrm{attn}}({\bm{z}};{\bm{w}}_{\mathrm{attn}})\in\mathbb{R}^{E}.

(22)

Given
E
expert NNs producing parameter vectors
{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}({\bm{z}})\in\mathbb{R}^{\sum_{\ell}K_{\ell}}
, the MoE circuit is

U_{\mathrm{soft}}({\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}))\;=\;U\!\left(\,\sum_{i=1}^{E}\pi_{i}({\bm{z}})\,{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}({\bm{z}})\,\right),

(23)

i.e., the experts produce parameter vectors, the gating weights
\pi_{i}({\bm{z}})
mix them, and the resulting vector
{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}})=\sum_{i}\pi_{i}({\bm{z}}){\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}({\bm{z}})
is fed into a single-layered ansatz
U(\cdot)
. The experts can be trained jointly or pretrained independently and softly combined when subsets of the data are available. The map
{\bm{z}}\mapsto{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}})\mapsto U_{\mathrm{soft}}({\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}))
is continuous and differentiable, so gradients flow through both the experts and the gating network.

We stress that Eq. (
23
) is not operator-equivalent to the soft-attention decoder in Eq. (
16
).
The two coincide only in degenerate cases (e.g., commuting generators or a single layer). The MoE architecture is therefore motivated on architectural grounds—smoothness, specialization across modes of
Q
, and gradient flow through
{\bm{z}}
—rather than as an operator-level realization of
V_{\mathrm{soft}}
.

Even though
U_{\mathrm{soft}}\neq V_{\mathrm{soft}}
, the MoE construction still inherits universality directly from Lemma
3
. The composite map

{\bm{z}}\;\longmapsto\;{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}})\;=\;\sum_{i=1}^{E}\pi_{i}({\bm{z}})\,{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}({\bm{z}})

(24)

is itself a classical neural network in
{\bm{z}}
: it is built from the expert NNs
f^{(i)}({\bm{z}};{\bm{w}}^{(i)})
, the gating network
f_{\mathrm{attn}}({\bm{z}};{\bm{w}}_{\mathrm{attn}})
, and smooth differentiable operations (softmax, multiplication, summation). It therefore satisfies the hypothesis of Lemma
3
, and the universal approximation guarantee carried by the general latent-conditioned parameterization applies to
U_{\mathrm{soft}}
without modification.

IV.2
Multimodal Mechanisms

The MoE structure is sufficient if
Q
is smooth, the latent map
{\bm{z}}\mapsto{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}})
does not need discrete branches, and the NNs
f_{\ell}
can directly learn smooth variations. However, when
Q
is multimodal, the basic ansatz could approximate this distribution via NNs learning jumps in
{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}})
, but discontinuities hinder gradient flow, leading to poor optimization.
Specifically, we can consider
r({\bm{z}})
as a multimodal prior (mixture with
M
modes)
r({\bm{z}})=\sum_{i=1}^{M}c_{i}p_{i}({\bm{z}})
, where
\{c_{i}\}
are non-negative mixing weights (
\sum c_{i}=1
), and each
p_{i}({\bm{z}})
is a component distribution.
For example, with Gaussian, Uniform, and Beta mixtures, we can assign
p_{i}({\bm{z}})=\mathcal{N}({\bm{z}}\mid\bm{\mu}_{i},\bm{\Sigma}_{i})
,
p_{i}({\bm{z}})=\mathcal{U}({\bm{z}}\mid{\bm{a}}_{i},{\bm{b}}_{i})
, and
p_{i}({\bm{z}})=\mathcal{B}({\bm{z}}\mid\bm{\alpha}_{i},\bm{\beta}_{i})
, respectively.
While we assign uniform
c_{i}
and fix each component
p_{i}({\bm{z}})
in our experiments, these factors can be trainable in location, scale, and shape, enabling the prior to adapt during optimization.

In our implementation, we use a basic ansatz for PQC
U({\bm{\theta}})=U_{L}(\theta_{L})\cdots U_{2}(\theta_{2})U_{1}(\theta_{1})U_{0}(\theta_{0})
as a hardware-efficient ansatz (HEA) for each layer:

\displaystyle U_{0}({\bm{\theta}}_{0})

\displaystyle=\prod_{p=1}^{n+m}R_{p}(\theta_{0,p}),

(25)

\displaystyle U_{\ell}({\bm{\theta}}_{\ell})

\displaystyle=\Biggl(\prod_{p=1}^{n+m}R_{p}(\theta_{\ell,p})\Biggr)\Biggl(\prod_{p=1}^{n+m-1}\mathrm{CNOT}_{p,p+1}\Biggr),

where
R_{p}(\theta_{\ell,p})=\exp(-iY\theta^{(1)}_{\ell,p}/2)\exp(-iZ\theta^{(2)}_{\ell,p}/2)
on physical qubit
p
and

\mathrm{CNOT}_{p,p+1}
forms a linear nearest-neighbor coupling.
Here,
Y
and
Z
are single-qubit Pauli
Y
and Pauli
Z
matrices, respectively.
The number of parameters per layer is
K_{\ell}=2(n+m)
and those parameters are produced by NNs
{\bm{\theta}}_{\ell}({\bm{z}})=f_{\ell}({\bm{z}};{\bm{w}}_{\ell})\in\mathbb{R}^{K_{\ell}}
.

) with

experts. We perform tomographies on each density matrix, convert them to vector form, and then perform dimension reduction with PCA and t-SNE.

IV.3
End-to-End Learning

Direct minimization of
\mathcal{W}_{1}(Q,(r,\rho_{\bm{w}}))
is intractable, so we use a loss
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{Wass}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})
approximating the 1-Wasserstein distance between density matrix ensembles
{\mathcal{X}}=\{\rho_{i}\}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}=\{\sigma_{j}\}
via
a symmetric, positive definite quadratic kernel
\kappa(\rho_{i},\sigma_{j})
. With normalized
\kappa
(i.e.,
\kappa(\rho,\rho)=1\,\forall\rho\in{\mathcal{D}}({\mathcal{H}}_{S})
), the cost matrix
{\bm{C}}=(C_{i,j})\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{X}}|\times|{\mathcal{Y}}|}
is
C_{i,j}=1-\kappa(\rho_{i},\sigma_{j})
. The distance solves an optimal transport linear program for plan
{\bm{P}}=(P_{i,j})\in\mathbb{R}^{|{\mathcal{X}}|\times|{\mathcal{Y}}|}
:

\displaystyle{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{Wass}}({\mathcal{X}},{\mathcal{Y}})

\displaystyle=\min_{{\bm{P}}}\sum_{i,j}P_{i,j}C_{i,j},

\displaystyle\text{s.t. }{\bm{P}}\mathbf{1}_{|{\mathcal{Y}}|}

\displaystyle={\bm{a}},\quad{\bm{P}}^{\top}\mathbf{1}_{|{\mathcal{X}}|}={\bm{b}},\quad{\bm{P}}\geq 0.

(26)

Here,
\mathbf{1}_{|{\mathcal{X}}|}
and
\mathbf{1}_{|{\mathcal{Y}}|}
are all-ones vectors with size
|{\mathcal{X}}|
and
|{\mathcal{Y}}|
, respectively, and
{\bm{a}}
and
{\bm{b}}
are histograms for
{\mathcal{X}}
and
{\mathcal{Y}}
(typically uniform:
{\bm{a}}=\tfrac{1}{|{\mathcal{X}}|}\mathbf{1}_{|{\mathcal{X}}|}
,
{\bm{b}}=\tfrac{1}{|{\mathcal{Y}}|}\mathbf{1}_{|{\mathcal{Y}}|}
).

Given target samples
{\mathcal{Y}}=\{\sigma_{j}\}\sim Q
, we sample
{\bm{z}}\sim r({\bm{z}})
and then compute
\pi({\bm{z}})
,
{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}})
, and
\rho_{\bm{w}}({\bm{z}})
to form an ensemble
{\mathcal{X}}=\{\rho_{i}\}
. We use the kernel based on the super-fidelity
22

\kappa(\rho,\sigma)=\tr(\rho\sigma)+\sqrt{[1-\tr(\rho^{2})][1-\tr(\sigma^{2})]}
, an upper-bound approximation of fidelity due to its efficiency for mixed states, needing fewer copies.
The test loss is the Wasserstein distance
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{Wass}}
, and the train loss combines
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{Wass}}
with an entropy regularization as

{\mathcal{L}}={\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{Wass}}+\lambda\sum_{i=1}^{E}\pi_{i}\log\pi_{i}
,
where
\lambda>0
.

V.1
Learning Multimodal Distributions

We employ a synthetic dataset of quantum density matrices designed to exhibit multimodal structure, facilitating the evaluation of LPQC as a generative model in capturing diverse quantum states.
The dataset is structured around four fixed pure states serving as cluster centers, chosen to represent a mix of product and highly entangled states:
|0\ldots 0\rangle

|1\ldots 1\rangle

\rho_{0}
,
\rho_{1}
,
\rho_{2}
, and
\rho_{3}
.
Around each center, we generate a cluster of
N_{s}/4
mixed states (
N_{s}=2048

25
) to the full system (
n+m
qubits) with rotation angles randomly generated from

We explain the structure used in LPQC.
For the PQC component, we employ the HEA defined in Eq. (
25
), where each parameter vector

{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}})\in{\mathbb{R}}^{K}
(

).
This MLP has an input dimension of
d
(corresponding to the latent dimension of
{\bm{z}}
),

D

K

(d,D,\ldots,D,K)

compactly as
\mathrm{MLP}(d,D^{(h)},K)

While the prior
r({\bm{z}})
can be trainable, in our implementation, we assume
r({\bm{z}})
is Gaussian or Uniform with fixed parameters.
For the single-mode case (
M=1
), the Gaussian is the multivariate normal distribution
\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{d})
, while the uniform is the flat distribution over the hypercube

For the multimodal case where

is a mixture with

\mathcal{N}(\bm{\mu}_{i},e^{-2}\mathbf{I}_{d})

\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}_{d})
.

In the MoEs scenario, instead of training
{\bm{\theta}}^{(i)}
(

\mathrm{MLP}(d,D^{(h)},K)

f^{(i)}({\bm{z}};{\bm{w}})
and the attention network
f_{\text{attn}}({\bm{z}};{\bm{w}}_{\text{attn}})
to produce the soft-attention weights
\eta({\bm{z}})
(obtained via softmax on the output logits).
Here,
f_{\text{attn}}({\bm{z}};{\bm{w}}_{\text{attn}})
is an MLP with architecture
\mathrm{MLP}(d,32^{(1)},E)

We split the train/test dataset 50-50, use the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of
0.001
and batch size of
128

Barren Plateau Analysis.

The observation in Fig.
4
ties into the barren plateau phenomenon
21
, where flat loss landscapes in PQCs lead to gradients that vanish exponentially, necessitating high measurement resources for accurate gradient estimation. Introducing a latent space to parameterize
{\bm{\theta}}

5

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left[\|\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\mathcal{L}}\|^{2}/\dim({\bm{\theta}})\right]
over 1024 independent trials in the PQC parameter space.
In the no-latent prior case, parameters are sampled uniformly, yielding Haar-random unitaries as the number of layers
L
increases, which causes
\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{\theta}}}\left[\|\nabla_{{\bm{\theta}}}{\mathcal{L}}\|^{2}/\dim({\bm{\theta}})\right]
to decay rapidly with
n,L
and saturate at extremely low values (e.g., around
10^{-10}
by
n=8,L=10
). By contrast, the latent space distorts the
{\bm{\theta}}
distribution away from uniformity, resulting in significantly higher gradient norms across all
L
. For instance, at
n=8,L=10
, LPQCs improve the gradient norm by up to two orders of magnitude over the no-latent baseline. Here, we display only the results from the Gaussian
r({\bm{z}})
, since the Uniform shows the same trend.

We investigate whether the LPQC offers any concrete advantage over a fully classical generative model on the same task. To assess this, we additionally implement a Latent-MLP (LMLP) baseline that mirrors the LPQC pipeline as closely as possible while removing the quantum component. A latent vector

used by LPQC and mapped through an MLP

m>0
); and (ii) when
m=0

For the synthetic multi-cluster task, we match the latent dimension

, hidden dimension
D=32
, and

hidden layers between LPQC and LMLP. Figure

). The structural advantage of LPQC lies in scaling: the LMLP generator produces an output of dimension

to span the vectorized density-matrix space, whereas LPQC requires only

L=10
,
n=8
,

reduction in output dimensionality, and the gap grows exponentially with

VI
Related Works and Discussion

UATs are originally established for classical NNs to demonstrate their capacity to approximate arbitrary continuous functions on compact subsets of Euclidean spaces
8
;
16
. In quantum settings, these theorems reveal that PQCs can serve as powerful approximators for a broad spectrum of functions over classical data, and even achieve the optimal resource compared with classical NNs
14
;
37
. This extends to generative modeling, where PQCs have been shown to approximate continuous multivariate probability distributions via expectation value sampling from measurements
2
, leveraging the Holevo bound to derive tight resource estimates. While these theorems affirm the roles of PQCs in classical data tasks such as regression and density estimation, they underscore the need for further generalization to purely quantum domains, such as distributions over density matrices.

Our paper introduces a generative framework where a low-dimensional latent variable
{\bm{z}}\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
(with
d
typically small) is sampled from a prior distribution
r({\bm{z}})
, mapped to PQC parameters
{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}})
via classical NNs
f({\bm{z}};{\bm{w}})
, and then used to produce quantum states
\rho_{{\bm{w}}}({\bm{z}})
via the PQC
U({\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}}))
. This pushforward approximates an arbitrary target distribution
Q
over density operators in the 1-Wasserstein distance. While PQCs with a universal gate set can trivially approximate any single quantum state, modeling distributions over states requires learning a parameter distribution
p({\bm{\theta}})
and converting its randomness into quantum outputs. Directly modeling a complex
p(\bm{\theta})
, especially with high-dimensional
\bm{\theta}\in\mathbb{R}^{K}
, is prone to the curse of dimensionality where sampling, density estimation, and optimization become inefficient.

In contrast, the latent
{\bm{z}}
is low-dimensional (
d\ll K
), making it easier to model sophisticated priors
r({\bm{z}})
. The deterministic map
{\bm{z}}\mapsto{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}})
(via NNs) then lifts the complexity to the high-dimensional
{\bm{\theta}}
, akin to classical normalizing flows: start with a simple low-dimensional base distribution and use a powerful mapping to generate complex outputs. Our framework leverages classical expressivity to mitigate barren plateaus by biasing initializations toward gradient-rich regions. The latent
{\bm{z}}
decouples distributional complexity (modeled in low dimensions) from the nonlinear mapping to states, making the model more tractable for quantum scales.

Another prominent idea considers the randomness of the measurement process, which can be controlled to model quantum distributions in the MPE framework
6
;
7
. Recent research uses this to demonstrate the universality of approximating pure-state distributions with a single many-body wave function, with practical layer-wise training to enhance optimization
34
. MPE provides a compact, projection-based method optimized for pure-state ensembles with efficient incremental training, while LPQCs offer a more versatile, parameter-generation paradigm for mixed state distributions, emphasizing classical expressivity for enhanced universality and mitigation of barren plateaus. MPE, while simpler in its core projection, may require more quantum resources for large ensembles due to repeated projections. Another key difference is verifiability in generative models for repeated sampling. In LPQCs, it is easy to control the random seed to generate the latent variable
{\bm{z}}
, while it requires overhead in quantum resources for post-selection to control random measurements in the MPE framework.

The LPQC construction used in our empirical experiments is straightforward, reflecting the procedure used in the UAT proof. However, it can be made more flexible. For instance, replacing the deterministic NN mapping
{\bm{z}}\mapsto{\bm{\theta}}({\bm{z}})
with normalizing flows
40
would allow learning more complex transformations from the latent space to the parameter space. It can potentially improve the approximation of
p({\bm{\theta}})
while preserving efficient sampling and density estimation, and also avoid the need to set the number
M
of prior modes heuristically. This could further mitigate barren plateaus by generating parameter distributions conditioned on quantum system contexts (e.g., molecular descriptors), enabling warm-starting and accelerating convergence.

Appendix A
Encoding Molecules to Quantum States

The QM9 dataset features small organic molecules, each incorporating up to
9 heavy atoms (C, N, O, F) supplemented by hydrogens, for a maximum of 29
atoms overall per molecule. For any given molecule (indexed as

j=0,1,\ldots,N-1
within the dataset), it is expressed as

\{({\bm{v}}^{j}_{i},{\bm{a}}^{j}_{i})\}_{i=0}^{m_{j}-1}
, with
m_{j}
denoting the heavy-atom
count,
{\bm{v}}^{j}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}
representing the 3-D positions, and

{\bm{a}}^{j}_{i}\in\{0,1\}^{k}
the one-hot encoded atomic species for the
j
th
molecule. We focus on heavy atoms only, giving
k=4
distinct atom types
(C, N, O, F).

The encoding must address arbitrary shifts, orientations, atom permutations,
and quantum normalization, while ensuring that the resulting amplitudes are
real and non-negative for ease of both encoding and decoding. We use the
strategy of Refs.
35
;
27
, with following
key stages.

(i)

Canonical atom ordering.
Atoms are reordered using canonical
SMILES strings generated via the RDKit toolkit
28
, producing
an ordering independent of the original input.

(ii)

Centering and rotation.
The molecule is translated so its
centroid coincides with the origin. It is then rotated such that the
first atom from the canonical SMILES ordering aligns with the
z
-axis.

(iii)

Global bounding-box normalization.
Across the entire training
subset, we compute the per-axis minimum

{\bm{v}}_{\min}=(v_{\min,x},v_{\min,y},v_{\min,z})
and the scalar
bounding-box side

\Delta=\max_{a\in\{x,y,z\}}(v_{\max,a}-v_{\min,a})
.
Each atom’s coordinates are then normalized as

\tilde{{\bm{v}}}_{i}=({\bm{v}}_{i}-{\bm{v}}_{\min})/\Delta
, placing all normalized
coordinates in
[0,1]
along the longest axis and within
[0,1]
along
the other two.

(iv)

Per-atom feature concatenation.
For atom
i
with normalized
position
\tilde{{\bm{v}}}_{i}=(\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{y}_{i},\tilde{z}_{i})
and
one-hot atom-type vector
{\bm{a}}_{i}=(p_{i}^{C},p_{i}^{N},p_{i}^{O},p_{i}^{F})
,
the per-atom feature vector is

(\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{y}_{i},\tilde{z}_{i},p_{i}^{C},p_{i}^{N},p_{i}^{O},p_{i}^{F})
,
of length
3+k=7
.

(v)

Auxiliary vector.
A length-
m
auxiliary vector
\bm{\alpha}

is appended, with
i
-th entry

\alpha_{i}=\sqrt{\max(0,\;3-\tilde{x}_{i}^{2}-\tilde{y}_{i}^{2}-\tilde{z}_{i}^{2})}
.
This auxiliary serves to make the encoded vector reach unit norm after
global rescaling and is not used in decoding.

(vi)

Global normalization and amplitude encoding.
The per-atom
feature vectors are concatenated, followed by the auxiliary vector and
zero-padding to length
2^{n}-1
, where
n=7
is the qubit count;
the final amplitude (index
2^{n}-1
) stores the atom count
m
as
bookkeeping. The resulting vector is divided by
2\sqrt{m}
to obtain
unit Euclidean norm, and the amplitudes
\{\alpha_{j}\}_{j=0}^{2^{n}-1}

define the encoded state
|\psi\rangle=\sum_{j}\alpha_{j}|j\rangle
on
n=7

qubits.

Appendix B
Decoding 3-D Molecules from Generated Quantum States and SMILES Generation

The encoding procedure in Appendix
A
maps each QM9
molecule to a 7-qubit pure quantum state whose amplitudes carry the
zero-padded, normalized concatenation of per-atom (position, type)
vectors. To evaluate generation quality, we apply the inverse procedure
to each quantum state produced by LPQC. The pipeline follows the
graph-theoretic protocol of
Refs.
35
;
27
, and proceeds
through five stages.

Stage 1: Amplitude readout.

LPQC generates density matrices on the 7-qubit Hilbert space; for the
QM9 task the generated states are pure
(
\rho_{\bm{w}}({\bm{z}})=|\psi({\bm{z}})\rangle\!\langle\psi({\bm{z}})|
, with
m_{\text{anc}}=0

ancilla qubits). The decoder operates on the amplitude vector

{\bm{x}}=|\psi({\bm{z}})|
obtained by taking the elementwise modulus of the
wavefunction amplitudes; since the encoder produces non-negative real
amplitudes by construction, modulus is the natural inverse on the
decoding side and discards only the global phase, which has no physical
meaning.

Stage 2: From amplitudes to atomic positions and types.

The amplitude vector
{\bm{x}}\in\mathbb{R}^{2^{7}}
is partitioned into a
sequence of
7
-entry per-atom blocks followed by an auxiliary block.
The
i
-th atom block occupies indices
[7i,7i+7)
in the layout

[\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{y}_{i},\tilde{z}_{i},p_{i}^{C},p_{i}^{N},p_{i}^{O},p_{i}^{F}]
. We
determine the number of occupied atom slots
m
as follows: starting
from
i=0
, we check whether
\sum_{t\in\{C,N,O,F\}}p_{i}^{t}>\tau

for an occupancy threshold
\tau
(we use
\tau=0.4
); the first
i

for which this sum falls below
\tau
is taken as
m
. Alternatively,
when the target atom count is known in advance,
m
may be set directly
without thresholding.

To invert the encoder’s global normalization, each amplitude is first
rescaled by a factor of
2\sqrt{m}
:
\tilde{{\bm{x}}}=2\sqrt{m}\,{\bm{x}}
. The
atom type of the
i
-th occupied slot is assigned by

t_{i}=\arg\max_{t\in\{C,N,O,F\}}\tilde{p}_{i}^{t}
,
corresponding to atomic numbers
\{6,7,8,9\}
respectively, and the
unnormalized coordinates are read out as

\tilde{{\bm{v}}}_{i}=(\tilde{x}_{i},\tilde{y}_{i},\tilde{z}_{i})
. Physical
coordinates are then recovered via the affine map

{\bm{v}}_{i}\;=\;2\Delta\,\tilde{{\bm{v}}}_{i}\;+\;{\bm{v}}_{\min},

where
\Delta
is the scalar bounding-box side and
{\bm{v}}_{\min}
is the
per-axis minimum, both saved from the training set. For our QM9 subset,

\Delta\approx 10.36
Å and

{\bm{v}}_{\min}\approx(-3.93,-4.54,-5.11)
Å. The factor of
2

relative to the strict inverse of the encoder’s normalization
(
{\bm{v}}_{i}=\Delta\tilde{{\bm{v}}}_{i}+{\bm{v}}_{\min}
) follows the convention of
Ref.
35
and effectively rescales the coordinate
spread relative to the fixed covalent-bond thresholds used in Stage 3.
Empirically, this choice improves the validity rate of decoded molecules
at the cost of a modest reduction in uniqueness, reflecting that the
stretched coordinates produce sparser, more chemically conservative
adjacency matrices.

Stage 3: Bond inference via covalent-radius distance thresholding.

Given the recovered Cartesian coordinates
\{{\bm{v}}_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
and atomic
numbers
\{a_{i}\}_{i=1}^{m}
(with
a_{i}\in\{6,7,8,9\}
for C, N, O, F), we
construct the adjacency-connectivity matrix

\mathrm{AC}\in\{0,1\}^{m\times m}
by thresholding pairwise interatomic
distances against tabulated covalent-bond cutoffs. Specifically, for each
atom pair
(i,j)
,

\mathrm{AC}_{ij}\;=\;\begin{cases}1&\text{if }d_{ij}\leq\tau_{a_{i}a_{j}}\text{ and }\deg(j)<\nu_{\max}(a_{j}),\\
0&\text{otherwise},\end{cases}

where
d_{ij}=\|{\bm{v}}_{i}-{\bm{v}}_{j}\|
and
\tau_{a_{i}a_{j}}
is a covalent-bond
threshold (computed as the sum of covalent radii of atoms
i
and
j

scaled by a factor of
2.2
to accommodate slightly extended generated
geometries), and
\nu_{\max}(a_{j})
is the maximum valence of atom
j

(
4
for C,
3
for N,
2
for O,
1
for F). The valence constraint during
adjacency construction prevents over-bonding in regions of unusually high
atomic density. The connectivity is then checked for graph connectedness;
a molecular graph that fragments into disconnected components is rejected
as invalid.

Stage 4: Bond-order assignment via valence completion.

The adjacency matrix
\mathrm{AC}
only encodes
whether
two atoms
are bonded, not the bond order (single, double, triple). To assign bond
orders, we solve the valence-completion problem: find a bond-order matrix

\mathrm{BO}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{m\times m}
such that

\mathrm{BO}_{ij}\geq\mathrm{AC}_{ij}
for all
(i,j)
and the row sums

\sum_{j}\mathrm{BO}_{ij}
match the standard valence of each atom type.
Algorithmically, we identify the set of unsaturated atoms

\mathrm{UA}=\{i:\deg_{\mathrm{AC}}(i)<\nu(a_{i})\}
and greedily
promote single bonds within
\mathrm{UA}
to double and triple bonds via
a maximum-weight matching on the unsaturated-atom subgraph, iterating
until all valences are satisfied or the procedure terminates without a
valid assignment. This step is implemented using the graph-theoretic
procedure of Refs.
35
;
27
.
If no valid bond-order assignment exists,
the molecule is flagged invalid.

Stage 5: Construction of RDKit molecule and SMILES generation.

With atomic positions, types, and bond orders in hand, we construct an
RDKit
RWMol
object by sequentially adding atoms with their atomic
numbers and bonds with their inferred orders. The resulting molecule is
sanitized using RDKit’s standard sanitization (
Chem.SanitizeMol
),
which checks aromaticity perception, valence consistency, kekulization,
and stereochemistry assignment; molecules that fail sanitization are
flagged invalid. The canonical SMILES string is then obtained via

Chem.MolToSmiles(mol, isomericSmiles=True)
. We additionally verify
chemical validity by round-tripping the SMILES (parse
\to
regenerate)
and re-sanitizing, which catches edge cases such as stereochemical
inconsistencies that pass the first sanitization but fail the round trip.

Generation-quality metrics.

The validity, uniqueness, and novelty metrics reported in
Table
1
are computed as follows.
Validity

is the fraction of generated quantum states whose decoded SMILES passes
both the sanitization and round-trip checks.
Uniqueness
is the
fraction of valid SMILES that are distinct as canonical strings.

Novelty
is the fraction of valid SMILES that do not appear in the
QM9 training set. The composite metrics

\mathrm{Unique}\times\mathrm{Valid}
and

\mathrm{Novel}\times\mathrm{Valid}
report the fraction of all generated
samples that are both valid and unique, or both valid and novel,
respectively, providing single measures that penalize models which produce
many invalid samples to inflate diversity. We use the implementation
provided by Ref.
35
for all metric computations to
ensure consistency with prior work.

Incremental Manybody Projected Ensemble (IMPE)

We follow the explanation and experimental settings in
34
to explain the IMPE method.
The goal is to approximate a target distribution
Q_{t}
over pure
n
-qubit states by constructing a parameterized ensemble
Q_{{\bm{\zeta}}}
through
T
iterative cycles of unitary transformations and measurements. First, sample a training dataset
\mathcal{S}=\{|\psi_{0}\rangle,\ldots,|\psi_{N_{s}-1}\rangle\}
of
N_{s}
states from
Q_{t}
. Start with an initial ensemble
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{0}=\{|\tilde{\psi}^{(0)}_{j}\rangle\}_{j}
sampled from a simple random distribution, such as Haar product states.
At each cycle
t=0,\ldots,T-1
, apply a PQC
V_{t}=V({\bm{\zeta}}_{t})
to the composite system comprising the primary data subsystem
{\mathcal{H}}_{S}
(
n_{d}
qubits) and auxiliary subsystem
{\mathcal{H}}_{A}
(
n_{a}
qubits), with states initialized as
|\tilde{\Psi}^{(t)}_{j}\rangle=|\tilde{\psi}^{(t)}_{j}\rangle_{P}\otimes|0\rangle_{A}
. Perform a projective measurement on
A
in the computational basis, yielding outcome
{\bm{z}}^{(t)}_{j}\in\{0,1\}^{n_{a}}
and updating the data state via:

\displaystyle\Phi^{(t)}_{j}(|\tilde{\psi}^{(t)}_{j}\rangle)

\displaystyle=\frac{(I_{S}\otimes\Pi_{A})V_{t}|\tilde{\Psi}^{(t)}_{j}\rangle}{\sqrt{\langle\tilde{\Psi}^{(t)}_{j}|V_{t}^{\dagger}(I_{S}\otimes\Pi_{A})V_{t}|\tilde{\Psi}^{(t)}_{j}\rangle}}

(27)

\displaystyle=|\tilde{\psi}^{(t+1)}_{j}\rangle_{S}\otimes|{\bm{z}}^{(t)}_{j}\rangle_{A},

(28)

where
\Pi_{A}=|{\bm{z}}^{(t)}_{j}\rangle\langle{\bm{z}}^{(t)}_{j}|_{A}
. The resulting ensemble
\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{t+1}=\{|\tilde{\psi}^{(t+1)}_{j}\rangle\}_{j}
iteratively refines the approximation, reducing resource demands compared to standard MPE.
The parameters
{\bm{\zeta}}_{t}
of each
V_{t}
are optimized layer-wise to minimize a distance metric
{\mathcal{D}}_{\mathrm{Wass}}(\mathcal{S},\tilde{\mathcal{S}}_{k+1})
between the training and generated ensembles; once optimized,
{\bm{\zeta}}_{t}
is fixed before proceeding to the next cycle. This decomposes the problem into
T
sub-tasks with fewer parameters each, aiding convergence.
In numerical experiments, each
V_{t}
employs a Hardware-Efficient Ansatz on
n_{q}=n_{d}+n_{a}
qubits with
L
layers:

V_{t}({\bm{\zeta}}_{t})=\prod_{l=1}^{L}\tilde{\Omega}_{t}\tilde{W}_{t}({\bm{\zeta}}_{t}),

where
\tilde{W}_{t}({\bm{\zeta}}_{t})=\prod_{j=1}^{n_{q}}e^{-i\zeta_{t,2j-1}\frac{X_{j}}{2}}e^{-i\zeta_{t,2j-2}\frac{Y_{j}}{2}}
implements single-qubit rotations (Pauli-
X
and -
Y
on qubit
j
, parameterized by
\zeta_{t,2j-1}
and
\zeta_{t,2j-2}
), and
\tilde{\Omega}_{t}=\prod_{j=1}^{n_{q}-1}\mathrm{CZ}_{j,j+1}
applies controlled-Z gates for nearest-neighbor entanglement.

In the experiments with the QM9-derived quantum dataset, we keep the same experimental setting of IMPE and the dataset mentioned in
34
. Here,
n_{d}=7
,
n_{a}=3
,
L
varies in
\{1,25,10,20,50,100\}
with
T=\frac{200}{L}
cycles. The mini-batch training is employed with a batch size
100
for each
V_{t}
, utilizing 200 training samples over
100\times L
epochs.
Then the total of training epochs over
T
cycles is
100\times L\times T=2\times 10^{4}
epochs.

Appendix D
Lie-algebraic perspective on why the latent prior alleviates barren plateaus

The empirical observation in Sec.
V
that the latent-conditioned parameterization maintains gradient norms one to two orders of magnitude larger than the no-latent and RD baselines (Fig.
6
) is consistent with a growing body of theoretical and empirical work on the origins of barren plateaus. We discuss the conjectural mechanism and leave a rigorous analysis as an open problem.

The recent unified analysis in Ref.
25
expresses the gradient variance of a parameterized unitary
U({\bm{\theta}})=\prod_{k}e^{-i\theta_{k}G_{k}}
as a sum of projections onto irreducible representations of the dynamical Lie algebra (DLA)
\mathfrak{g}
generated by
\{iG_{k}\}
. When
\dim\mathfrak{g}
scales as
\Theta(4^{n})
and the initial state and observable have nontrivial overlap with many irreducible blocks, the projections cancel via representation-theoretic orthogonality and the gradient variance decays as
O(e^{-cn})
. The standard no-latent baseline in which
{\bm{\theta}}
is drawn from a broad distribution (Haar-like or independent uniform) on
\mathbb{R}^{K}
is in precisely this regime. Here, a high-dimensional average over a large DLA produces the exponential suppression we observe in the leftmost column of Fig.
6
.

The LPQC parameterization replaces this broad average with the pushforward measure
p_{\bm{w}}=(f_{\bm{w}})_{\#}\,r
on
\mathbb{R}^{K}
, induced by the latent prior
r({\bm{z}})
on the low-dimensional space
{\mathcal{Z}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{d}
(
d\ll K
). The expected squared gradient norm becomes

\mathbb{E}_{{\bm{z}}\sim r}\bigl[\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{L}}(f_{\bm{w}}({\bm{z}}))\|^{2}\bigr]\;=\;\int_{\mathcal{Z}}\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{L}}(f_{\bm{w}}({\bm{z}}))\|^{2}\,r({\bm{z}})\,d{\bm{z}},

(29)

which is a
d
-dimensional integral over the image manifold
{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{w}}=\{f_{\bm{w}}({\bm{z}}):{\bm{z}}\in{\mathcal{Z}}\}
rather than a
K
-dimensional integral over the full parameter space. This is the same kind of dimensionality reduction at work in classical settings where overparameterized neural networks are effectively trained on a low-dimensional manifold of parameter space
19
. The hypothesis underlying the LPQC approach is that the high-dimensional averaging responsible for the exponential suppression analysis does not apply once the support of the parameter distribution is constrained to such a manifold. The cancellation across irreducible blocks of the DLA depends on the parameter distribution being spread sufficiently broadly to average over them.

This intuition is supported by several lines of work. It is shown empirically in Ref.
12
that NN-generated parameters maintain larger gradient norms than uniformly sampled ones across a range of ansatze and observables. In Ref.
36
, a complementary geometric perspective is provided. Parameters produced by smooth NN maps follow trajectories on a low-dimensional submanifold of the parameter space whose geometry is determined by the network’s Jacobian, and these trajectories tend to avoid the flat regions characterized by the DLA analysis. Prior work on initialization strategies
15
and classical-shadow-based warm-starting
30
demonstrates that careful choice of the initial parameter distribution can substantially reduce the severity of barren plateaus in practice.

The pushforward viewpoint above is a conjectural mechanism, not a proof. To upgrade it to a rigorous statement, one would need to (i) identify which low-dimensional submanifolds
{\mathcal{M}}_{\bm{w}}\subset\mathbb{R}^{K}
admit a uniform polynomial lower bound on
\|\nabla_{\bm{\theta}}{\mathcal{L}}\|^{2}
, in a manner that depends explicitly on the DLA structure and the choice of observable; (ii) show that such submanifolds are reachable by the image of a learned classical NN with polynomial parameter count; and (iii) verify that the meta-optimization over the generator weights
{\bm{w}}
itself does not introduce new barren plateaus. We are not aware of existing results that simultaneously address all three. The Lie-algebraic framework in Ref.
25
provides the natural language in which such a result would be stated, and recent work
36
suggests that the geometric tools required may be within reach. We leave a formal treatment of this problem to future work.
