Static features from mixing in short- and long-range Lindbladians: Markov property and correlations
Paul Rosa-Ruiz, Matteo Scandi, Ángela Capel, Álvaro M. Alhambra

Abstract
The classification of mixed-state phases requires criteria beyond two-point correlation functions, such as the decay of the mutual information (MI) and the conditional mutual information (CMI), with the latter encapsulated in the notion of Markov length . Here we show how such static properties of the fixed point of a Lindbladian follow from natural dynamical features of its generators: rapid mixing and frustration-freeness. We focus on systems with long-range interactions, and prove i) that local Lindbladians satisfying (global) rapid mixing and frustration-freeness have fixed-points whose CMI decays with the shielding distance, and ii) that (local) rapid mixing together with primitivity and regularity implies global decay of MI. For long-range interactions decaying with a power law with rate α \alpha , both quantities decay polynomially rather than exponentially, in contrast to the finite- and short-range regimes where exponential decay (a finite Markov length) is expected within a phase. We further show that Gibbs states of long-range, non-commuting Hamiltonians satisfy a local Markov property at any temperatures, extending the recent results (Chen–Rouzé, 2025) for short-range systems to the long-range regime relevant to a variety of experimental platforms. As a numerical example, we study the long-range Ising model both with and without a transverse field. We find regimes in which the polynomial decay of the CMI holds, in accordance with the bounds proven.

Introduction

Quantum many-body systems display a wealth of different phases, often going beyond the well-established paradigms of phase characterization such as the classic one based on symmetry breaking. Thus, the classification of quantum phases is still an ongoing effort, and constitutes one of the central areas of study at the intersection of condensed matter, quantum information and mathematical physics. For pure ground states of closed systems, a well-established definition of what it means to be within a phase is provided by adiabatic continuity of two points in the phase through Hamiltonians with a finite positive spectral gap

Since these are static properties of individual states, it is important to understand under what dynamical setting we expect them to arise.
Mixed states are generated through a dissipative process with an external, possibly uncontrolled, environment, so their open system dynamics is described by
Lindbladians

in a similar way as the gap (a property of the Hamiltonian) implies non-trivial features of its (static) ground states.

In this work we show how static properties of the fixed point (decay of CMI and MI) follow from natural dynamical features of their generating Lindbladians. Specifically, we consider the consequences of

(Definitions

We show that the MI decays in a global sense under the assumptions of locality and rapid mixing, and that the CMI also decays similarly under the additional assumption of frustration-freeness. While we show that our results apply to fixed-points of local Lindbladians, they do not directly apply to Gibbs states of non-commuting Hamiltonians since they are fixed points of a
quasi-local
(KMS) Lindbladian. However, we are able to prove a local Markov property on these Gibbs states in the long range regime extending the proof of

, trapped-ion quantum simulators

For short-range interacting systems, it is expected that both the MI and the CMI within a phase decay exponentially with the shielding distance. For the CMI, this is formalised through the notion of
Markov length

\xi

[
43
,
44
]
, which measures the decay rate of the exponential function

and plays the role of a correlation length for multipartite correlations; a finite Markov length has been established as a crucial stability condition for mixed-state phases
[
43
,
37
]
. Our results illustrate how the picture changes when interactions decay algebraically in long-range systems. For these systems, rapid thermalization implies that the fixed point exhibits a
polynomial
decay of CMI and MI, illustrating how a classification of mixed-state phases must accommodate this qualitatively different regime. Put together, we explore the conditions under which dissipative systems do not generate any long-range order in the sense that is relevant for the classification of mixed-state phases
[
43
,
37
,
27
]
, that is, with a fast decay of both the MI and the CMI.

These results provide static properties of fixed points of Lindbladians that are implied by dynamical properties of the Lindbladians. The converse direction has also been studied in the literature

The MCMI is an operator version of the CMI, which in particular implies decay of CMI and MI for specific subsystems. Correlations in long-range open systems.

In

77

CMI decay for short-range systems.
Significant advances have recently been made on the decay of CMI for quantum Gibbs states. In particular,
[
45
]
proves the pairwise Markov property for Gibbs states of finite-range Hamiltonians at all temperatures. Our results extend this to the long-range setting. Also for finite-range Hamiltonians,

Characterization of mixed phases.
Recent efforts have considered the role of both the CMI and the MI in the characterization of phases in the context of mixed states. In particular,
[
43
]
showed that a finite Markov length guarantees the existence of finite-depth channel circuits mapping between the states within a phase (a condition termed local reversibility
[

]
), then
[

]
proved that the decay of MI and CMI are robust properties of a phase, by showing their stability under shallow channel circuits. This equivalence was also recently refined by the notion of local stability (see also
[

d(x,y)=\sum_{j=1}^{D}\left|x_{j}-y_{j}\right|
. However, the precise structure of
\mathbb{Z}^{D}
is not necessary for most of the results discussed here.

If
\Lambda\subset\subset\Gamma
then
\Lambda
is a finite subset of
\Gamma
. Furthermore, we define
\mathcal{P}_{0}(\Gamma)
as the collection of all finite subsets of
\Gamma
.

Geometric constants.
We say
(\Gamma,d)
is

(\kappa,\nu)
-regular
if

\sup_{x\in\Gamma}\left|\{y\in\Gamma:d(x,y)\leq r\}\right|\leq\kappa\,r^{\nu}\qquad\text{for all }r\geq 1,

(2.1)

for constants
\kappa<\infty
and
\nu>0
.

In particular, for
\Gamma=\mathbb{Z}^{D}

with the
\ell^{1}
-metric, this holds with
\nu=D-1
and

\kappa=\kappa_{D}=\frac{D\pi^{D/2}}{\Gamma(D/2+1)}.

(2.2)

Inflation and distance.
For
X\subset\Gamma
and

r>0
, the
r
-inflation
of
X
is

X(r):=\{y\in\Gamma:d(X,y)\leq r\},\qquad d(X,y):=\inf_{x\in X}d(x,y).

(2.3)

We write
d_{y}(X):=\sup_{x\in X}d(y,x)
for the farthest distance
from
y
to
X
, and
\mathrm{diam}(X):=\sup_{x,y\in X}d(x,y)

for the diameter of
X
, i.e. the farthest distance between two sites in
X
.

2.1
Hilbert Space Structure

For every point
x\in\Gamma
we choose a finite-dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}_{x}\cong\mathbb{C}^{n_{x}}
of dimension
n_{x}>0
; from now on, we assume that every point has a Hilbert space of the same local dimension
n
, i.e.
\mathcal{H}_{x}\cong\mathbb{C}^{n},\quad\forall x\in\Gamma
.

The corresponding algebra of observables of each point
x
is the set of bounded linear operators
\mathcal{A}_{x}=\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{x})
from
\mathcal{H}_{x}
to itself. Then, for every subset
\Lambda\subset\Gamma
, the Hilbert space of the states on
\Lambda
is defined by taking the tensor product

\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}=\bigotimes_{x\in\Lambda}\mathcal{H}_{x},

with the corresponding algebra of observables

\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}=\bigotimes_{x\in\Lambda}\mathcal{A}_{x}\cong\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H}_{\Lambda}).

For every subset
\Lambda^{\prime}\subset\Lambda\subset\Gamma
, we get a partial order by identifying
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}
with
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}\otimes\mathbb{I}_{\Lambda\setminus\Lambda^{\prime}}
and write
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda^{\prime}}\subset\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
. The algebra of all strictly local observables
\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}^{\text{loc}}
is then defined as the inductive limit

\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}^{\text{loc}}=\bigcup_{\Lambda\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(\Gamma)}\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}.

(2.4)

The
C^{*}
-algebra of quasi-local observables
\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}
is taken to be the norm completion of
\mathcal{A}_{\Gamma}^{\text{loc}}
.

States.
A state on
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
is a positive
linear functional
\omega:\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}\to\mathbb{C}
with

\omega(\mathbb{I})=1
uniquely identified with a density matrix
\rho\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
,
\rho\geq 0
, via
\omega(O)=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho O)
(see Gleason’s Theorem
[
79
]
);
by a standard abuse of notation we use the term
state
for both the functional
\omega
and its associated density matrix
\rho
. We write
\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}):=\{\omega\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}^{*}:\omega\geq 0,\omega(\mathbb{I})=1\}
for the set of states on
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
. Note that if we are working in a finite-dimensional algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
the predual
\mathcal{A}_{*}=\mathcal{A}^{*}
is the same as the dual space, then, we will denote a state as an element of the dual
\rho\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}^{*}
, however, in case of considering infinite dimensional algebras, the state actually is part of the predual space.

Partial traces.
For
X\subset\Lambda
, the reduced state on
X
is
\rho_{X}:=\mathrm{Tr}_{\Lambda\setminus X}(\rho)
.
We write

\rho_{-X}:=\frac{\mathbb{I}_{X}}{n^{|X|}}\otimes\rho_{\Lambda\setminus X}

(2.5)

for the state obtained by replacing the
X
-marginal of
\rho
with the maximally mixed state on
\mathcal{H}_{X}
.

2.2
Interactions and
F
-Functions

The (local) interactions in open quantum systems will consist of a family
\mathcal{L}=\{L_{Z}:Z\subset\subset\Gamma\}
of bounded linear operators
L_{Z}:\mathcal{A}_{Z}\to\mathcal{A}_{Z}
in the so-called Lindblad form, which characterises generators of norm-continuous, one parameter semigroup of unital completely positive maps
[
62
,
63
]
.
Given a finite subset
\Lambda\subset\subset\Gamma
, we always use
∗
to denote the adjoint of a linear transformation on
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
with respect to the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle_{HS}
. Whenever we treat with finite dimensional algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
.

It is well-known
[
80
,
81
]
that for each finite
Z\subset\Lambda
, there exists a self-adjoint
H_{Z}=H_{Z}^{*}\in\mathcal{A}_{Z}
, a number
l_{Z}\in\mathbb{N}
, and jump operators
K_{1},\dots,K_{l_{Z}}\in\mathcal{A}_{Z}
such that, for all
O\in\mathcal{A}_{Z}
and
\rho\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda})
,

L_{Z}(O)=i[H_{Z},O]+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\end{subarray}}^{l_{Z}}K_{j}^{*}OK_{j}-\frac{1}{2}\{K_{j}^{*}K_{j},O\}

(2.6)

L_{Z}^{*}(\rho)=-i[H_{Z},\rho]+\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}j=1\end{subarray}}^{l_{Z}}K_{j}\rho K_{j}^{*}-\frac{1}{2}\{K_{j}^{*}K_{j},\rho\}.

(2.7)

Given a finite volume
\Lambda\subset\subset\Gamma
, we call a
local interaction
the operator

\mathcal{L}_{\Lambda}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\subset\Lambda\end{subarray}}L_{Z}.

(2.8)

As an abuse of notation, we sometimes write
\mathcal{L}_{\Lambda}=\{L_{Z}\}_{Z\subset\Lambda}
to denote the local interaction
\mathcal{L}_{\Lambda}=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\subset\Lambda\end{subarray}}L_{Z}
, and we call
\mathcal{L}_{\Lambda}
a dissipative process, as this can be thought as the generator of the time evolution of an open quantum system. We may remove the subscript of
\mathcal{L}_{\Lambda}
and simply write
\mathcal{L}
if the set
\Lambda
can be understood from the context.

The local operator
\mathcal{L}_{\Lambda}
, due to its Lindblad form, generates a norm-continous, one-parameter semigroup of operators denoted by

T^{\Lambda}_{t}(O_{A})=e^{t\mathcal{L}_{\Lambda}}(O_{A}),\quad O_{A}\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda},

(2.9)

which is well-defined since
\mathcal{L}_{\Lambda}
is bounded. We will simply denote by
T_{t}:=T^{\Lambda}_{t}

\forall t
, if
\Lambda
is clear from context.

Complete positivity is a general condition that some maps between
C^{*}
-algebras satisfy. For any finite dimensional Hilbert space
\mathcal{H}
, any linear map
\Phi:\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\to\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})
induces a linear map on
\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\otimes\mathbb{M}_{n}
, where
\mathbb{M}_{n}
denotes the set of
n\times n
matrices, by acting block-wise

\Phi\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n\times n}([[X_{i,j}]]_{i,j=1}^{n}):=[[\Phi(X_{i,j})]]_{i,j=1}^{n}.

(2.10)

We say that the linear map
\Phi
is completely positive if for every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, the matrix
\Phi\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n\times n}(X^{*}X)
is positive semidefinite for all
X\in\mathcal{B}(\mathcal{H})\otimes\mathbb{M}_{n}
. The completely positive maps are also completely bounded, i.e.

\left\lVert\Phi\right\rVert_{cb}:=\sup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}\left\lVert\Phi\otimes\mathbb{I}_{n\times n}\right\rVert<\infty.

(2.11)

We define a class of completely bounded maps as the following:

Definition 2.1
.

Given a finite volume
Y
, the set
\mathcal{CB}_{0}(Y)
is defined as

\mathcal{CB}_{0}(Y)=\{\Phi:\mathcal{A}_{Y}\to\mathcal{A}_{Y}:\left\lVert\Phi\right\rVert_{cb}<\infty,\,\Phi(\mathbb{I}_{Y})=0\}.

(2.12)

An example of an element in
\mathcal{CB}_{0}(Y)
is the commutator
\Phi(\cdot)=[\cdot,B]
for
B\in\mathcal{A}_{Y}
.

The decay of interactions with the distance is encoded in an
F
-function.

Definition 2.2

(
F
-function)
.

A non-increasing function
F:[0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)
is said to be a
F
-function on
(\Gamma,d)
if:

1.

F
is
uniformly summable
, in the sense that

\lvert\lvert\lvert F\rvert\rvert\rvert:=\sup_{x\in\Gamma}\sum_{y\in\Gamma}F(d(x,y))<\infty,

(2.13)

and

2.

F
satisfies a
convolution condition
:

C_{F}:=\sup_{x,y\in\Gamma}\sum_{z\in\Gamma}\dfrac{F(d(x,z))F(d(z,y))}{F(d(x,y))}<\infty

(2.14)

Note that an immediate consequence of (
2.14
) is that for any pair
x,y\in\Gamma
, we have the bound

\sum_{z\in\Gamma}F(d(x,z))F(d(z,y))\leq C_{F}F(d(x,y))

(2.15)

The two canonical families of
F
-functions used throughout
this paper are:

•

Short-range:

F_{a,b}(r)=e^{-ar^{b}}
for
a>0
,

b\in(0,1]
.

•

Long-range:

F_{\alpha}(r)=(1+r)^{-\alpha}
for

\alpha>D
, on a
(\kappa,D)
-regular space.

For
F_{\alpha}
on
\mathbb{Z}^{D}
, one has

\|F_{\alpha}\|\leq\frac{2\kappa_{D}}{\alpha-D},

where we use that the metric space
(\mathbb{Z}^{D},d)
is
(\kappa_{D},D-1)
-regular according to Equation (
2.1
):

\left|\{x:\ d(x,S)\in(r-1,r]\}\right|\ \leq\ \kappa_{D}\,|\partial S|\,(1+r)^{D-1}\qquad(\text{finite }S\subset\Lambda,\penalty\ r\in\mathbb{N}),

(2.16)

for
\kappa_{D}
in Equation (
2.2
) and

C_{F_{\alpha}}\leq c(\alpha,D)<\infty
for any
\alpha>D
.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
(\Gamma,d)
be a countable metric space equipped with an F-function
F
. Let
\mathcal{L}=\{L_{Z}\}_{Z\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(\Gamma)}
be a local interaction. We say that
\mathcal{L}
is a
doubly anchored
interaction governed by
F
if

\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}:=\sup_{x,y\in\Lambda}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\subset\Lambda\\
x,y\in Z\end{subarray}}\dfrac{\left\lVert L_{Z}\right\rVert_{cb}}{F(d(x,y))}<\infty

(2.17)

Except otherwise stated, we will assume that the dissipative system is doubly-anchored to a function
F
, unless it is not clear by the context. Note that a natural consequence of (
2.17
) is that

\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\subset\Lambda\\
x,y\in Z\end{subarray}}\left\lVert L_{Z}\right\rVert_{cb}\leq\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}F(d(x,y))

(2.18)

for every
x,y\in\Lambda
.

In a similar way, a closed quantum system with a local Hamiltonian
H=\sum_{Z\subset\Lambda}h_{Z}
, has long-range interactions if

\left\lVert H\right\rVert_{F}:=\sup_{x,y\in\Lambda}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\subset\Lambda\\
x,y\in Z\end{subarray}}\dfrac{\left\lVert h_{Z}\right\rVert_{\infty}}{F_{\alpha}(d(x,y))}<\infty

(2.19)

for the
F
-function

F_{\alpha}(r)=\dfrac{1}{(1+r)^{\alpha}};\quad r\geq 0,

for
\alpha>D
. In other words, there exists a
J>0
such that

\left\lVert h_{Z}\right\rVert\ \leq\ \frac{J}{(1+\mathrm{diam}(Z))^{\alpha}}

(2.20)

for every
Z\subset\Lambda
. Unless otherwise stated, all the Hamiltonians we consider will be
k
-local, i.e., for a
k\in\mathbb{N}
,

H=\sum_{Z\subset\Lambda,\ |Z|\leq k}h_{Z}.

(2.21)

2.3
Fixed Points and Mixing

Following the notation in
[
75
]
, we now define fixed points and mixing conditions. Let
(\Gamma,d)
be a countable metric space and consider a finite subset
\Lambda\subset\subset\Gamma
.

Definition 2.4
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=\{L_{Z}\}_{Z\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(\Gamma)}
be a dissipative interaction,
\pi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}^{*})
a state on the local algebra
\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
and denote by
T^{\Lambda}_{t}
the time evolution generated by
\mathcal{L}
. We say that
\pi
is a
dynamical fixed point
of
\mathcal{L}
if

\pi\circ T^{\Lambda}_{t}=\pi\quad\forall t\geq 0.

(2.22)

Remark 2.1
.

Note that, from the above definition,
\omega\circ T^{\Lambda}_{\infty}
is a dynamical fixed point for every
\omega\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}^{*})
, i.e.

\omega\circ T^{\Lambda}_{\infty}\circ T^{\Lambda}_{t}=\omega\circ T^{\Lambda}_{\infty}\,,

where
T^{\Lambda}_{\infty}:=\lim_{t\to\infty}T^{\Lambda}_{t}
.

This does not specify how fast an arbitrary state converges towards the fixed point. In order to get some information about the convergence speed, we define the following.

Definition 2.5
.

We say that
\pi\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}^{*})
is a
local dynamical fixed point governed by
g_{\Lambda}
with respect to
A\subset\Lambda
if it is a dynamical fixed point and there exists a non-increasing function
g_{\Lambda}:[0,\infty)\to[0,2]
for which one has

\left|(\pi-\omega)\circ T^{\Lambda}_{t}(O_{A})\right|\leq\left\lVert O_{A}\right\rVert\cdot p(\left|A\right|)\cdot g_{\Lambda}(t)

(2.23)

for all states
\omega\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}^{*})
,
p
a polynomial function,
O_{A}\in\mathcal{A}_{A}
,
t\geq 0
, and, from now on, we denote by
\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert:=\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{\infty}
.

However, in some cases, instead of treating with positive functionals
\omega\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda})
, we use the density matrix
\rho\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
corresponding to
\omega
by the correspondence (see Gleason’s Theorem
[
79
]
)

\omega(O)=\operatorname{tr}(\rho O),\quad\forall O\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}.

See Section
2.1
for more details.

When considering density matrices, the common definition of fixed-point is that it is contained in the kernel of the Lindbladian.

Definition 2.6

(Fixed-point)
.

A state
\rho\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
is a fixed-point of a Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
if

\mathcal{L}^{*}(\rho)=0.

(2.24)

Both Definitions
2.4
and
2.6
are equivalent:

Lemma 2.2
.

Let
\omega\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda})
be a state and let
\rho\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
be the density matrix corresponding to
\omega
. Then,
\omega
is a dynamical fixed-point if and only if
\rho
is a fixed-point.

Proof.

\Rightarrow
: Suppose
\omega
is a dynamical fixed-point (i.e.
\omega\,\circ\,T^{\Lambda}_{t}=\omega
). Then, for any
O\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
,

\operatorname{tr}((T^{\Lambda}_{t})^{*}(\rho)O)=\operatorname{tr}(\rho T^{\Lambda}_{t}(O))=\omega(T^{\Lambda}_{t}(O))=\omega(O)=\operatorname{tr}(\rho O).

(2.25)

By the non-degeneracy of the Hilbert-Schmidt inner product,
(T^{\Lambda}_{t})^{*}(\rho)=\rho
for all
t
. Differentiating at
t=0
yields

\mathcal{L}^{*}(\rho)=0.

(2.26)

Hence
\rho
is a fixed-point of the Lindbladian.

\Leftarrow
: Suppose
\rho
is a fixed-point of
\mathcal{L}
(
\mathcal{L}^{*}(\rho)=0
). Then, for any
O\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}
,

(\omega\,\circ\,T^{\Lambda}_{t})(O)=\omega(T^{\Lambda}_{t}(O))=\operatorname{tr}(\rho T^{\Lambda}_{t}(O))=\operatorname{tr}((T^{\Lambda}_{t})^{*}(\rho)O)=\operatorname{tr}(\rho O)=\omega(O).

(2.27)

Then,
\omega\,\circ\,T^{\Lambda}_{t}=\omega
.
∎

The Lemma above shows that we can indistinctively talk about fixed-points or dynamical fixed-points, only depending on whether our state is a functional or a density-matrix.

Even though in most of the cases we study local dynamical and static properties, there is a stronger notion of global speed of convergence called (global) rapid mixing. I is introduced and used more often in the literature (see
[
77
,
78
]
) and defined in terms of the contraction of the quantum Markov semigroup.

Definition 2.7

(Contraction)
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=\{L_{Z}\}_{Z\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(\Gamma)}
be a dissipative interaction. Define the
contraction of
T^{\Lambda}_{t}
as

\eta(T^{\Lambda}_{t}):=\sup_{O\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda},\left\lVert O\right\rVert=1}\left\lVert(T^{\Lambda}_{t}-T^{\Lambda}_{\infty})(O)\right\rVert.

(2.28)

Take
A\subset\Lambda
and define the
contraction of
T^{\Lambda}_{t}
relative to region
A
as

\eta^{A}(T^{\Lambda}_{t}):=\sup_{\omega\in\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda}^{*}}\sup_{O_{A}\in\mathcal{A}_{A},\left\lVert O_{A}\right\rVert=1}\left|\omega\circ(T^{\Lambda}_{t}-T^{\Lambda}_{\infty})(O_{A})\right|

(2.29)

Definition 2.8

(Global rapid mixing)
.

We say that
\mathcal{L}
satisfies
global rapid mixing
if

\eta(T^{\Lambda}_{t})\leq p(\left|\Lambda\right|)e^{-\lambda t},

(2.30)

where
\lambda>0
and
p
a
n
-degree polynomial. We denote it as
\text{RM}(n,\lambda)
, or by
\text{RM}(\lambda)
if the degree of the polynomial is not relevant as long as it is finite (
n<\infty
).

This definition considers any operator defined in
\Lambda
and as such it provides a trivial bound in the thermodynamical limit (
\left|\Lambda\right|\to\infty
).
However, a weaker condition than global rapid mixing can be required on
\mathcal{L}
if we only consider operators defined in a region
X\subset\Lambda
.

Definition 2.9

(Local rapid mixing)
.

We say that
\mathcal{L}
satisfies
local rapid mixing
if, for each
X\subset\Lambda
,

\eta^{X}(T^{\Lambda}_{t})\leq p(\left|X\right|)e^{-\lambda t}

(2.31)

where
\lambda>0
and
p
a
n
-degree polynomial. We denote it as
\text{LRM}(n,\lambda)
, or by
\text{LRM}(\lambda)
if the degree of the polynomial is not relevant as long as it is finite (
n<\infty
).

Remark 2.3
.

Note that for every two sets
A\subset B
, both subsets of
\Lambda
, then
\eta^{A}\leq\eta^{B}
. In particular,
\eta^{A}\leq\eta
for all
A\subset\Lambda
.

Remark 2.4
.

Note that if
\mathcal{L}=\{L_{Z}\}_{Z\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(\Gamma)}
is primitive (see Definition
2.11
) and satisfies local rapid mixing, then, its fixed point is a local dynamical fixed point governed by
e^{-\lambda t}
; this is the way we treat fixed points for locally rapidly mixing dissipative processes.

Local rapid mixing can be thought as the following: consider a state in a finite region
A\subset\Lambda
, then this state will converge into its local Gibbs distribution in a time that grows logarithmically with the size of the region
A
.

2.4
Frustration freeness and primitivity

Frustration freeness is a property of some dissipative processes analogous to the concept in ground states of closed systems. Some fixed points of local evolutions may not be locally steady; in other words, each local dissipative term may have a nonvanishing contribution to the fixed point, and it is the sum of all the local contributions which leaves the fixed point invariant. Frustration freeness excludes such cases.

Definition 2.10
.

Let
\mathcal{L}=\{L_{Z}\}_{Z\subset\Lambda}
be a local dissipative interaction. We say
\mathcal{L}
is frustration free if, for every fixed point
\sigma\in\mathcal{S}(\mathcal{A}_{\Lambda})
of
\mathcal{L}
and every
Z\subset\Lambda
, then

L_{Z}^{*}(\sigma)=0.

(2.32)

As pointed in
[
77
]
, while not in general, many of the most interesting cases of dissipative processes are frustration free:

•

many of the most common dissipative state engineering procedures
[
86
,
87
,
88
,
89
]
,

•

the parent Lindbladians of the RG fixed points of the set of matrix-product density operators
[
90
]

•

detailed balanced quantum Markov processes, in particular, the Glauber dynamics
[
76
,
69
]
and the KMS detailed balance Lindbladians such as
[
85
,
91
,
92
]
, which are Gibbs samplers for commuting and non-commuting Hamiltonians respectively (the latter Lindbladians are quasi-local, not strictly local),

•

typical noise channels such as depolarizing noise.

Another important property of dissipative processes is
primitivity
(see
[
93
]
).

Definition 2.11
.

A Lindbladian
\mathcal{L}
is
primitive
if it has a unique full-rank fixed-point.

When it comes to perturbing the Lindbladian, it is important to assure that after removing terms of a local Lindbladian, it will remain being primitive and have a unique fixed-point. That property is called regularity.

Definition 2.12

\mathcal{L}=\{L_{Z}\}_{Z\subset\Lambda}

Although this assumption might seem
odd, it turns out to be very natural when discussing scaling of a dissipative system with the
system size. In particular, primitive translationally invariant systems satisfy this property.

2.5
Information-Theoretic Quantities

Given two states

\displaystyle S(\rho)

\displaystyle:=-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\log\rho)

\displaystyle\text{(von Neumann entropy)},

(2.33)

\displaystyle S(\rho\|\sigma)

\displaystyle:=\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\log\rho)-\mathrm{Tr}(\rho\log\sigma)

\displaystyle\text{(quantum relative entropy)},

\displaystyle\text{Fid}(\rho,\sigma)

\displaystyle:=\|\sqrt{\rho}\sqrt{\sigma}\|_{1}

\displaystyle\text{(quantum fidelity)},

(2.34)

and the quantum Pinsker inequality gives

\|\rho-\sigma\|_{1}\leq\sqrt{2\,S(\rho\|\sigma)}
.

, for short times, is localized “around the bulk”; This holds due to frustration freeness and rapid mixing. For that, we use Lemma

.

\left\lVert\left((T^{\Lambda}_{t})^{*}-\left(T^{AB}_{t}\right)^{*}\right)(\sigma_{-A})\right\rVert_{1}\leq p(\left|A\right|,\left|C\right|)(e^{\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}C_{F}t}-1+t)R^{D}F(R/2)\,,

(3.28)

Putting both bounds together in Equation (

),

\left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{B\to AB}^{t}(\sigma_{-A})-\sigma\right\rVert_{1}\leq p(\left|A\right|,\left|C\right|)R^{nD}\left(e^{-\lambda t}+\bigl[e^{vt}-1+t\bigr]F(R)\right),

(3.29)

for
v=\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}C_{F}
. By choosing
t_{0}(R)=\dfrac{1}{\lambda+v}\log\left(\dfrac{F^{-1}(R)}{C_{0}}\right)

we obtain that the decay is given by

\left\lVert\mathcal{R}_{B\to AB}^{t_{0}}(\sigma_{-A})-\sigma\right\rVert_{1}\leq\,p(\left|A\right|,\left|C\right|)\cdot R^{nD}(F(R))^{\lambda/(\lambda+v)},

(3.30)

Define the final recovery map as

\mathcal{R}_{B\to AB}:=\mathcal{R}_{B\to AB}^{t_{0}}.

The existence of a local recovery map as
\mathcal{R}_{B\to AB}
implies that the conditional mutual information decays by Fawzi-Renner (see
[
42
]
). We can now apply Proposition
2.5
combined with the trace distance via Fid
\geq 1-\frac{1}{2}\left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert_{1}
, so that the CMI decays as

I(A:C\,|\,B)_{\sigma}\leq\,p(\left|A\right|,\left|C\right|)\,R^{nD/2}(F(R))^{\lambda/2(\lambda+v)},

(3.31)

so that
\sigma
satisfies a global Markov property. The additional factor of
1/2
in the rate comes from the square root when relating fidelity and
1
-norm.
∎

In particular, if the Lindbladian is long-range, then, the CMI decays polynomially with
R
:

Corollary 3.4
.

For local long-range Lindbladians with a decay rate

\alpha>\dfrac{n(\lambda+v)}{\lambda}D,

satisfying frustration freeness and RM(
n,\lambda
), the CMI decays as

I(A:C\,|\,B)_{\rho}\leq\,p(\left|A\right|,\left|C\right|)\,F_{\alpha^{\prime}}(R),

(3.32)

for
\alpha^{\prime}=\dfrac{\lambda\alpha}{2(\lambda+v)}-\dfrac{nD}{2}
.

3.2
Decay of Mutual Information

In order to prove decay of mutual information, we first study how perturbations on the Lindbladian affect the fixed point. In other words, we bound how close the fixed point of the perturbed Lindbladian is to the original one. Crucially, this argument only needs locality and rapid mixing, and does not rely on frustration freeness as Theorem
3.3
does.

Firstly, in Section
3.2.1
we study how far-enough perturbations in the Lindbladian are locally indetectable, using Lieb-Robinson bounds for long-range systems. Using this, in Section
3.2.2
we prove that removing the terms of the Lindbladian that cross the boundary between two far-enough regions does not affect the fixed-point locally. Finally, in Section
3.2.3
, we prove that the MI decays with the distance using the results above.

3.2.1
Perturbed dynamics

We first show that perturbations far away from a region cannot be distinguished locally because quantum systems have a finite (effective) speed of propagation given by the so-called Lieb-Robinson velocity, as defined in Theorem
2.7
.

Theorem 3.5
.

Let
(\Gamma,d)
be a countable metric space equipped with an F-function
F
. Let
\mathcal{L}=\{L_{Z}\}_{Z\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(\Gamma)}

\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}<\infty

with

\Lambda\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(\Gamma)

,

with convergence governed by

The left-hand side of Equation (

(resp.

(1):\quad\left|(\sigma-\pi)(T^{\Lambda}_{t}(O_{A}))\right|\leq p(\left|A\right|)\cdot\left\lVert O_{A}\right\rVert\cdot e^{-\lambda t}

\displaystyle(2):\;\lvert\lvert(\widetilde{T}^{\Lambda}_{t}

\displaystyle-T^{\Lambda}_{t})(O_{A})\rvert\rvert_{1}\leq\int_{0}^{t}\left\lVert Q_{C}(T^{\Lambda}_{s}(O_{A}))\right\rVert\,ds

\left|(\sigma-\pi)(O_{A})\right|\leq\left\lVert O_{A}\right\rVert\left(p(\left|A\right|)\cdot e^{-\lambda t}\right.\\
+\left.\dfrac{\left\lVert Q_{C}\right\rVert_{cb}}{C_{F}}\left(\dfrac{e^{\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}C_{F}\cdot t}-\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}C_{F}\cdot t-1}{\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}C_{F}}\right)\sum_{x\in A}\sum_{y\in C}F(d(x,y))\right).

(3.38)

Note that

where

.

\displaystyle\lvert(\sigma

\displaystyle-\pi)(O_{A})\rvert

(3.40)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert O_{A}\right\rVert\left(p(\left|A\right|)\,e^{-\lambda t}+\dfrac{\left\lVert Q_{C}\right\rVert_{cb}}{C_{F}}\left(\dfrac{e^{\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}C_{F}t}-\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}C_{F}t-1}{\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}C_{F}}\right)\left|A\right|\,\left|C\right|\,F(R)\right)

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert O_{A}\right\rVert\left(p(\left|A\right|)\,e^{-\lambda t}+\dfrac{\left\lVert Q_{C}\right\rVert_{cb}}{v\,C_{F}}e^{\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}C_{F}t}\,\left|A\right|\,\left|C\right|\,F(R)\right).

Defining

By specializing the previous result to algebraically decaying

If

4.3
Quasi-locality of the recovery-map

Although Equation (
4.22
) provides a recovery map for every temperature, it is not strictly localized in the region
A
, but it is a quasi-local recovery map. In order to be able to bound the CMI, we need a recovery map that is localized in
AB
. This section computes the error made by approximating
\mathcal{R}_{A}^{t}
by a strictly local map
\mathcal{R}_{A,l}^{t}
supported on
A(l)
; in particular, when considering
l=R=d(A,C)
, then note that
A(l)=A(R)=AB
, which is what is needed for bounding the CMI.

Firstly, we will relate the locality of the recovery map with the locality of the Lindbladian. For that purpose, we need to adapt the Lieb-Robinson bound for long-range Hamiltonians from a bound on the commutator to a bound on truncating the Hamiltonian.

Theorem 4.3

(Truncation bound under the Hastings–Koma Lieb–Robinson estimate)
.

Let
H=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\subset\Lambda\end{subarray}}h_{Z}
be a long-range Hamiltonian on
\Lambda\subset\subset\mathbb{Z}^{D}
such that Theorem
2.8
holds, and fix a region
X\subset\Lambda
.

Then, for every operator
O_{X}\in\mathcal{A}_{X}
supported on
X
, if the interaction rate satisfies
\alpha>\eta>d
,
then there exists a constant

\big\|T_{H,t}(O_{X})-T_{H_{R},t}(O_{X})\big\|\ \leq\ c\,\|O_{X}\|\,\left|X\right|\,(\mathrm{e}^{v|t|}-1-v|t|)\,F_{\alpha-D}(d(X,Y)).

(4.23)

Proof.

See Theorem
A.4
in the appendix.
∎

This directly yields the quasi-locality property of the CKG Lindbladian:

Lemma 4.4

(Quasi-locality)
.

For a
k
-local Hamiltonian
H
with long-range interactions and a region
A\subset\Lambda
, denote by

\mathcal{L}_{A}^{(R)}
the generator of the Gibbs sampler with
Metropolis weight
\gamma_{M}
(see Equation
4.2
), single-qubit Pauli jumps as the jump operators
\bm{A}_{\alpha}\in P_{A}^{1}
(see Equation (
4.4
)) and Hamiltonian

H_{R}:=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\subset B_{R}(A)\end{subarray}}h_{Z}.

(4.24)

Then,

\|\mathcal{L}_{A}-\mathcal{L}_{A}^{(R)}\|_{\infty\to\infty}=\hat{\mathcal{O}}(R^{-(\alpha-D)}).

(4.25)

Proof.

The proof is divided into two parts, each bounding the decay of one specific part of the Lindbladian (namely, the coherent and the incoherent part). Consider the CKG Lindbladian

\mathcal{L}_{A}[\rho]=\underbrace{-i[B_{A},\rho]}_{\text{coherent part}}+\underbrace{\sum_{\alpha\in P^{1}_{A}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\gamma_{\alpha}(\omega)\left(\bm{A}_{\alpha}(\omega)\,\rho\,\bm{A}_{\alpha}(\omega)^{\dagger}-\tfrac{1}{2}\!\left\{\bm{A}_{\alpha}(\omega)^{\dagger}\bm{A}_{\alpha}(\omega),\rho\right\}\right)\,d\omega}_{\text{incoherent part}},

(4.26)

where each Fourier component is given by

\bm{A}_{\alpha}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{+iHt}\bm{A}_{\alpha}e^{-iHt}\,e^{-i\omega t}\,f_{\alpha}(t)\,dt,\qquad\|\bm{A}_{\alpha}\|\leq 1,

(4.27)

for integrable kernels
13
13

13

Note that
e^{v\left|t\right|}-1-v\left|t\right|\leq(v\left|t\right|)^{2}e^{v\left|t\right|}/2
for every

by Taylor.

f_{\alpha}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R},(1+t^{2})e^{v|t|}dt)

and weights
\gamma_{\alpha}\in L^{1}(\mathbb{R})
.

From now on, for simplicity, we will assume that every
\alpha
is a jump operator in
P_{A}^{1}
.

We define the truncated operators

\bm{A}^{(R)}_{\alpha}(\omega)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{+iH_{R}t}\bm{A}_{\alpha}e^{-iH_{R}t}\,e^{-i\omega t}\,f_{\alpha}(t)\,dt,

\mathcal{L}_{A}^{(R)}[\rho]:=-i[B^{(R)},\rho]+\sum_{\alpha\in P_{A}^{1}}\!\int\!\gamma_{\alpha}(\omega)\mathcal{\,}D[\bm{A}^{(R)}_{\alpha}(\omega)](\rho)\,d\omega,

where
\mathcal{D}[L](X)=LXL^{\dagger}-\tfrac{1}{2}\{L^{\dagger}L,X\}
.

For later convenience, for any
O_{X}\in\mathcal{A}_{X}
supported in

, apply the Lieb-Robinson bound for long-range Hamiltonians given by Theorem

\|T_{H,t}(O_{X})-T_{H_{R},t}(O_{X})\|\leq\frac{C}{v}\big(\mathrm{e}^{v|t|}-1-v|t|\big)\,F_{\alpha-D}(R)\,\|O_{X}\|,

and insert the previous estimate to the dissipative part of the Lindbladian to obtain

\|\bm{A}_{\alpha}(\omega)-\bm{A}^{(R)}_{\alpha}(\omega)\|\leq\frac{C}{\sqrt{2\pi}v}\,F_{\alpha-D}(R)\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!|f_{\alpha}(t)|\,(\mathrm{e}^{v|t|}-1-v|t|)\,dt=:\Xi_{\alpha}(R)\sim R^{-(\alpha-D)}<\infty.

The quasi-locality of the jump operators is key to prove the quasi-locality of the incoherent part and the coherent part.

Incoherent part:
For the incoherent part, we have that for any operators
L,\widetilde{L}
and
X
, the map

\mathcal{D}[L]

satisfies
(\mathcal{D}L-\mathcal{D}\tilde{L})(X)=(L-\tilde{L})XL^{\dagger}+\tilde{L}X(L-\tilde{L})^{\dagger}-\frac{1}{2}\{L^{\dagger}L-\tilde{L}^{\dagger}\tilde{L},X\}
. Using
\|L^{\dagger}L-\tilde{L}^{\dagger}\tilde{L}\|\leq(\|L\|+\|\tilde{L}\|)\|L-\tilde{L}\|
. Then,

\|\mathcal{D}[L]-\mathcal{D}[\widetilde{L}]\|\leq 3(\|L\|+\|\widetilde{L}\|)\|L-\widetilde{L}\|.

Setting
L=\bm{A}_{\alpha}(\omega)
and
\widetilde{L}=\bm{A}^{(R)}_{\alpha}(\omega)
, then,

\Big\|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\gamma_{\alpha}(\omega)\big(\mathcal{D}[L]-\mathcal{D}[\widetilde{L}]\big)d\omega\Big\|\leq 3\,\Gamma_{\alpha}\,\left(\left\lVert\bm{A}_{\alpha}(\omega)\right\rVert+\left\lVert\bm{A}^{(R)}_{\alpha}(\omega)\right\rVert\right)\,\left\lVert\bm{A}_{\alpha}(\omega)-\bm{A}^{(R)}_{\alpha}(\omega)\right\rVert.

(4.28)

The norm of
\bm{A}_{\alpha}(\omega)
and
\bm{A}^{(R)}_{\alpha}(\omega)
can be bounded by a constant independent of
\omega
.

\left\lVert\bm{A}_{\alpha}(\omega)\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert\bm{A}_{\alpha}(t)\right\rVert\underbrace{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!|f_{\alpha}(t)|\,dt}_{=:M_{\alpha}}\leq\left\lVert\bm{A}_{\alpha}\right\rVert M_{\alpha}\leq M_{\alpha},

(4.29)

(same with
\left\lVert\bm{A}_{\alpha}^{(R)}(\omega)\right\rVert
) with the following constants:

\Gamma_{\alpha}:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!|\gamma_{\alpha}(\omega)|\,d\omega,\qquad M_{\alpha}:=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!|f_{\alpha}(t)|\,dt,

(4.30)

Going back to Equation (
4.28
) and integrating
against
|\gamma_{\alpha}(\omega)|
yields

\Big\|\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\!\gamma_{\alpha}(\omega)\big(\mathcal{D}[L]-\mathcal{D}[\widetilde{L}]\big)d\omega\Big\|\leq 3\,\Gamma_{\alpha}\,(M_{\alpha}+\Xi_{\alpha}(R))\,\Xi_{\alpha}(R).

(4.31)

Coherent part:
We will follow the proof of the Corollary A.
2
from
[

]
. We aim at bounding the coherent part of the CKG Lindbladian with the Metropolis weight (Equation (

)). The main differences with respect to finite or short-range comes from the Lieb-Robinson bounds used, while the part of the proof corresponding to the manipulations of
B_{a}
is unchanged with respect to

The coherent part is generated by the operator

B_{A}=\sum_{a\in A}B_{a},

where the operator
B_{a}
associated to the jump operator
\bm{A}_{a}
with Metropolis weight is

B_{a}:=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}b_{1}(t)\,e^{-i\beta Ht}\lim_{\eta\to 0^{+}}\left(\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}b_{2}^{\eta}(t^{\prime})\,\bm{A}_{a}(\beta t^{\prime})\,\bm{A}_{a}(-\beta t^{\prime})\,dt^{\prime}+\frac{1}{8\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\bm{A}_{a}^{\dagger}\bm{A}_{a}\right)e^{i\beta Ht}\,dt.

(4.32)

with the functions

\displaystyle b_{1}(t)

\displaystyle:=2\sqrt{\pi}\,e^{\frac{1}{8}}\left(\frac{1}{\cosh(2\pi t)}*\sin(-t)\,e^{-2t^{2}}\right),\qquad\|b_{1}\|_{1}<1,

(4.33)

\displaystyle b_{2}^{\eta}(t)

\displaystyle:=\mathbf{1}(|t|\geq\eta)\,\underbrace{\frac{1}{2\sqrt{2\pi}}\,\frac{\exp(-2t^{2}-it)}{t(2t+i)}}_{=b_{2}^{M}(t)}\equiv\mathbf{1}(|t|\geq\eta)\,b_{2}^{M}(t).

(4.34)

The goal is to show that
B_{a}
is quasi-local, meaning that replacing
H
by the Hamiltonian
H_{R}
(containing only terms inside
A(R)
) only introduces a small error.

The first step consists of truncating the double integral to
\left|t\right|,\left|t^{\prime}\right|\leq T
. Because
b_{1}\in L^{1}
(
\left\lVert b_{1}\right\rVert_{1}\leq 1
) with exponential decay and
b_{2}^{M}(t)\lesssim e^{-2t^{2}}/\left|t\right|
for large
\left|t\right|
, both integrals are absolutely convergent outside any finite window. In particular, truncating the integral to
\left|t\right|\leq T
introduces an error

\left\lVert b_{1}(t)\mathbb{I}(\left|t\right|\geq T)\right\rVert_{1}\lesssim e^{-cT},

(4.35)

and truncating the inner integral to
\left|t^{\prime}\right|\leq T
introduces an error controlled by

\left\lVert b_{2}^{M}(t)\mathbb{I}(\left|t^{\prime}\right|\geq T)\right\rVert_{1}\lesssim e^{-2cT^{2}}.

(4.36)

Both terms are exponentially small in
T
. Defining
B^{T}
as the double truncated version of
B
, we have that it can be locally approximated with an error

\left\lVert B_{A}-B_{A}^{(R)}\right\rVert\leq\left\lVert B_{A}-B_{A}^{T}\right\rVert-\left\lVert B_{A}^{T}-(B_{A}^{(R)})^{T}\right\rVert\lesssim e^{-cT}+e^{-2cT^{2}}+\left\lVert B_{A}^{T}-(B_{A}^{(R)})^{T}\right\rVert,

(4.37)

so the proof reduces to bounding the truncated difference
\left\lVert B_{A}^{T}-(B_{A}^{(R)})^{T}\right\rVert
. For simplicity we may denote
B\equiv B_{A}
.

The truncated
B^{T}
splits as

\displaystyle B^{T}:=

\displaystyle\int_{-T}^{T}b_{1}(t)\left(\lim_{\eta\to 0}\big(\underbrace{\int_{-T}^{T}\mathbb{I}(|t|\geq 1)b_{2}^{\eta}(t^{\prime})\bm{A}^{\dagger}(\beta(t^{\prime}-t))\bm{A}(-\beta(t^{\prime}+t))\,dt^{\prime}}_{(a)}\right.

(4.38)

\displaystyle+\underbrace{\int_{-T}^{T}\mathbb{I}(1\geq|t|\geq\eta)b_{2}^{\eta}(t^{\prime})\bm{A}^{\dagger}(\beta(t^{\prime}-t))\bm{A}(-\beta(t^{\prime}+t))\,dt^{\prime}}_{(b)}\big)\left.+\underbrace{\frac{(\bm{A}^{\dagger}\bm{A})(-\beta t)}{16\sqrt{2\pi}}}_{(c)}\right)dt.

Each of the terms requires a different treatment.

The term
(c)
involves the operator
\bm{A}^{\dagger}\bm{A}
evolved in time, and the difference

\left\lVert(\bm{A}^{\dagger}\bm{A})_{H}(t)-(\bm{A}^{\dagger}\bm{A})_{H_{R}}(t)\right\rVert,

is directly bounded by the Lieb-Robinson bound:

(c)\lesssim\left\lVert\bm{A}_{X}\right\rVert^{2}F_{\alpha-D}(R)(e^{vt}-1-vt).

(4.39)

The term
(a)
contributes the same order as term
(c)
. For
\left|t^{\prime}\right|\geq 1
the kernel

is bounded and decays as

, so there is no singularity. The difference between
H
and

enters only through the time evolution
\bm{A}_{H}(\beta t^{\prime})\mapsto\bm{A}_{H_{R}}(\beta t^{\prime})
. Applying Lieb-Robinson bound for long-range interactions and using
\left\lVert b_{1}\right\rVert_{1},\left\lVert b_{2}^{M}(\left|t^{\prime}\right|\geq 1)\right\rVert_{1}\lesssim 1
, one obtains

(a)\lesssim\left\lVert\bm{A}_{X}\right\rVert^{2}F_{\alpha-D}(R)(e^{2vt}-1-2vt).

(4.40)

The heart of the argument is controlling the term
(b)
. Due to the divergence of
b_{2}^{M}(t^{\prime})\sim 1/t^{\prime}
at
t^{\prime}=0
, one cannot simply bound
\left\lVert b_{2}^{M}(t^{\prime})\right\rVert\cdot\left\lVert\bm{A}_{H}(\beta t^{\prime})-\bm{A}_{H_{R}}(\beta t^{\prime})\right\rVert
and integrate.

The solution is the Corollary A.1 from
[
45
]
which bounds the coherent part by commutators of the jump operatos with the Hamiltonian:

\big(\mathbb{I}(1\geq|t|\geq\eta)\,\text{term}\big)\lesssim\beta\sup_{|t|\leq\beta(T+1)}\|[\bm{A},H]_{H}(t)-[\bm{A},H]_{H_{R}}(t)\|\\
+\sup_{|t|\leq\beta(T+1)}\|\bm{A}_{H}(t)-\bm{A}_{H_{R}}(t)\|.

(4.41)

Now the term
(b)
is controlled by the Lieb-Robinson bounds of the commutator
[\bm{A},H]
and the jump operator
\bm{A}
:

(c)\lesssim\beta\sup_{|t|\leq\beta(T+1)}\|[\bm{A},H]_{H}(t)-[\bm{A},H]_{H_{R}}(t)\|+\sup_{|t|\leq\beta(T+1)}\|\bm{A}_{H}(t)-\bm{A}_{H_{R}}(t)\|,

(4.42)

Using the Lieb-Robinson bounds for long-range interactions from
[
99
]
, and applying the same steps as in the proof of Theorem
4.3
, the following is satisfied for
\alpha>2D+1
:

\left\lVert\bm{A}_{H}(t)-\bm{A}_{H_{R}}(t)\right\rVert\lesssim\dfrac{t^{D+1}}{(l-vt)^{\alpha-D}}.

Finally, considering the exponentially small error made by truncating the integral of the coherent part to a time
T
(dependent on
R
) and using the quasi-locality of
B^{T}
, one ends up with the quasi-locality of the coherent part
B

\displaystyle\left\lVert B-B^{(R)}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\left\lVert B-B^{T}\right\rVert+\left\lVert B^{T}-B^{(R)}\right\rVert

(4.43)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left\lVert b_{1}(t)\mathbb{I}(\left|t\right|\geq T)\right\rVert_{1}+\left\lVert b_{2}^{M}(t)\mathbb{I}(\left|t\right|\geq T)\right\rVert_{1}+\left|A\right|\dfrac{(\beta T)^{D+1}}{(l-v(\beta T))^{\alpha-D}}

\displaystyle\lesssim e^{-cT}+e^{-2T^{2}}+\left|A\right|\dfrac{(\beta T)^{D+1}}{(l-v(\beta T))^{\alpha-D}}\lesssim\dfrac{\log(R)^{D+1)}}{R^{\alpha-D}}=\hat{\mathcal{O}}(R^{-(\alpha-D)}),

by choosing

T(R)=\dfrac{\alpha-D}{c}\log(R)-\dfrac{D+1}{c}\log\log(R)+\Theta(1).

Thus, we get a polynomial decay of the coherent part with a logarithmic correction.

\left\lVert B-B^{(R)}\right\rVert\leq\hat{\mathcal{O}}(R^{-(\alpha-D)})

(4.44)

for
\alpha>2D+1
.

Quasi-locality of the Lindbladian:
The original proof concludes by combining both the coherent and incoherent parts to give:

\left\lVert\mathcal{L}_{A,R}^{\dagger}-\mathcal{L}_{A}^{\dagger}\right\rVert_{\infty\to\infty}\leq\left\lVert\mathcal{D}^{\dagger}-\mathcal{D}_{R}^{\dagger}\right\rVert_{\infty\to\infty}+2\left\lVert B_{A}-B_{A}^{(R)}\right\rVert_{\infty\to\infty}\\
\lesssim\,\left|A\right|\left(\Gamma_{\alpha}\,(M_{\alpha}+\Xi_{\alpha}(R))\,\Xi_{\alpha}(R)+\dfrac{\log(R)^{D+1)}}{R^{\alpha-D}}\right)\\
\lesssim\left|A\right|\left(\dfrac{\log(R)^{D+1}}{R^{\alpha-D}}\right)=\hat{\mathcal{O}}(R^{-(\alpha-D)}).

(4.45)

This concludes the proof.

The quasi-locality of the recovery map follows from the quasi-locality of the CKG Lindbladian, which was proven in Lemma

. Indeed:

Lemma 4.5

(Lemma VII.

Consider the time-averaging map

\bigl\|(\mathcal{R}^{t}_{A,t,R})^{\dagger}-(\mathcal{R}^{t}_{A,t})^{\dagger}\bigr\|_{\infty\to\infty}\lesssim t\,\bigl\|\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}_{A,R}-\mathcal{L}^{\dagger}_{A}\bigr\|_{\infty\to\infty},

(4.46)

where
\mathcal{L}_{A}
(resp.
\mathcal{L}_{A,R}
) is the Lindbladian of the Gibbs sampler
with Hamiltonian
H
and jumps
A^{\alpha}\in P_{A}^{1}
(respectively the one associated to the
Hamiltonian
H_{R}
with jumps

Now that we have proven that the recovery map recovers
\rho_{\beta}
from
\rho_{\beta,-A}
(see Theorem

) and that it is quasi-local (see Lemma
4.5
), we have all the ingredients to prove the decay of the CMI:

Theorem 4.6
.

Let
H
be a
k
-local long-range Hamiltonian and a tripartition
A\sqcup B\sqcup C=\Lambda
as in Figure
1.1
. Then,
the CMI decays as

I(A:C\,|\,B)_{\rho}\leq\log(\dim(C))\,e^{\mu^{\prime\prime}\left|A\right|}\dfrac{(\log(R))^{\lambda^{\prime}(D+1)/2}}{R^{\lambda^{\prime}(\alpha-D)/2}}=\tilde{\mathcal{O}}(R^{-\alpha^{\prime}}),

(4.47)

for
\alpha^{\prime}:=\lambda^{\prime}(\alpha-D)/2
, where
\lambda^{\prime}:=\lambda/(1+\lambda)
and
\lambda
is defined in Equation (
4.51
) (note that
\lambda
depends on the inverse temperature
\beta
).

Proof.

The proof is straightforward once there is a quantum channel
\mathcal{R}_{A,t}
that recovers
\rho_{\beta}
from
\rho_{\beta,-A}
(see Theorem
4.2
). In order for
\mathcal{R}_{A,t}
to be a recovery map, it needs to be localized in a region
A(R)=A\cup B
. Lemma
4.5
proves that the quasi-locality of the recovery map is linearly related to the quasi-locality of the Lindbladian used in the definition of the recovery map. Then, using both Lemma
4.5
and
4.4
, the error made by localizing
\mathcal{R}_{A,t}
is

\bigl\|(\mathcal{R}^{t}_{A,R})^{*}-(\mathcal{R}^{t}_{A})^{*}\bigr\|_{\infty\to\infty}\lesssim t\,\left|A\right|\,\dfrac{(\log(R))^{D+1}}{R^{\alpha-D}}.

(4.48)

By choosing a time

t_{0}(R)=\dfrac{e^{\mu\left|A\right|/(1+\lambda)}R^{\beta/(1+\lambda)}}{(\log(R))^{(D+1)/(1+\lambda)}},

(4.49)

and using the triangular inequality, we get that the recovery map recovers the original state with a polynomial precision with logarithmic corrections, i.e.

\bigl\|\mathcal{R}^{t_{0}}_{A,R}[\rho_{\beta,-A}]-\rho_{\beta}\bigr\|_{1}\lesssim e^{\mu^{\prime}\left|A\right|}\dfrac{(\log(R))^{\lambda^{\prime}(D+1)}}{R^{\lambda^{\prime}(\alpha-D)}},

(4.50)

where
\lambda^{\prime}=\lambda/(1+\lambda)\in(0,1)

\lambda=\begin{cases}\frac{128\beta_{0}^{4}}{\beta^{3}(\beta+5\beta_{0})},&\text{if }\beta>4\beta_{0},\\[6.00006pt]
\frac{2\beta_{0}}{\beta+5\beta_{0}},&\text{if }\beta\leq 4\beta_{0},\end{cases}

(4.51)

I(A:C\,|\,B)_{\rho}\leq\Delta\log(\dim(C))+h_{2}(\Delta)\lesssim\log(\dim(C))\sqrt{\Delta},

(4.52)

where
h_{2}(x)=-x\log_{2}(x)-(1-x)\log_{2}(1-x)\lesssim\sqrt{x}\text{ for }x\in[0,1]
,
and

\quad\Delta:=\tfrac{1}{2}\bigl\|\mathcal{R}_{A,R}^{t}[\rho_{\beta,-A}]-\rho_{\beta}\bigr\|_{1}.

This proves the claim.
∎

, both at
T\to 0
and also at higher temperatures. We leave this question for future work.

5
Conclusions

We have established the decay of mutual information for fixed-point of long-range (local) Lindbladians under rapid mixing. If frustration-freeness is also satisfied by the Lindbladian, then the fixed point shows global Markov property, meaning that the conditional mutual information
I(A:C|B)
decays polynomially with distance and scales polynomially with the sizes
\left|A\right|,\,\left|C\right|
.

Additionally, we prove a local Markov property of the Gibbs state of a non-commuting long-range Hamiltonian. The proof is an extension of the proof from
[
45
]
to long-range Hamiltonians. We use the CKG Lindbladian
[
85
]
which is a quasi-local thermal Lindbladian whose fixed-point is a Gibbs state of a non-commuting Hamiltonian.

Tightness.

The decay exponent
\alpha^{\prime}=\dfrac{\lambda\alpha}{2(\lambda+v)}-\dfrac{nD}{2}
in Theorem
3.3
for the CMI
is not expected to be tight in general. A linear dependence on
\alpha
is expected from the
classical case (Section
4.4.1
),
which for short-range systems
(
\alpha\to\infty
) is consistent with
exponential decay.
The rate
observed numerically in Section
4.4.1
(namely,
q(\alpha)\approx 1.38\alpha
) is larger than the theoretical prediction
q(\alpha)\simeq\lambda\alpha/2(\lambda+v)
.
We leave the question of the optimal decay exponent depending on parameters such as
D,v
as an open problem.

Open problems.

Several natural directions remain open:

1.

Sharp threshold for rapid mixing.

The condition
\alpha>3D+1
for CKG rapid mixing is set by the method of

[
109
]
and is likely not tight, due to overheads related to the Lieb-Robinson bound and other proof steps. In 1D specifically, the classical long-range Ising
model thermalizes rapidly for all
\alpha>1
. Closing this gap
would immediately improve the thermal CMI result, both in the decay with distance and in the dependence with the size of
|A|,|C|
. A similar situation occurs for the MI in Theorem
3.8
, where we believe that a decay rate of just
\alpha
is possible
[
114
]
.

2.

Connection to classification of thermal phases.

The work of
[
44
]
shows that CMI decay implies the existence of
low-depth quantum circuits that prepare Gibbs states within the same fixed thermal phase.
Our results establish the prerequisites for extending this classification to
long-range systems, although the polynomial tails appear too large for a straightforward extension of that result. An explicit circuit construction remains to be done.

3.

Removing frustration-freeness.

Theorem
3.3
assumes frustration-freeness (FF).
Whether FF can be replaced by a weaker condition (e.g. detailed balance
or else) is an important open problem even in the short-range setting.

4.

Larger numerical simulations.
Numerically, we confirm the polynomial decay of the CMI for the classical long-range Ising chain, consistent with the above bounds, and find a similar polynomial regime in the long-range transverse field Ising model at a small transverse field
h
(Section
4.4.2
). As
h
or
\alpha
increase, the decay instead appears to cross over to an exponential one. Clarifying whether this is a genuine effect or a finite-size effect, is left for future work.

We now prove two technical lemmas that will allow us to prove some properties of open quantum systems with long-range interactions on
D
-regular graphs.

Lemma A.1
.

Let
\Gamma
be a
D
-regular graph and let
\mathcal{L}=\sum_{Z\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(\Gamma)}L_{Z}
be a local interaction governed by the
F
-function
F_{\alpha}(r):=1/(1+r)^{\alpha};\,r\geq 0
. Fix an
l>0
, then

\sup_{x\in\Gamma}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(\Gamma)\\
Z\ni x\\
{\rm diam}(Z)=l\end{subarray}}\left\lVert L_{Z}\right\rVert\leq\kappa\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}\,F_{\alpha-D}(l/2).

(A.1)

Proof.

For any
x\in\Gamma
,

\displaystyle\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(\Gamma),\,Z\ni x\\
\mathrm{diam}(Z)=l\end{subarray}}\left\lVert L_{Z}\right\rVert

\displaystyle\leq\sum_{n=0}^{l/2}\underbrace{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\Gamma\\
d(x,y)=l/2+n\end{subarray}}}_{=\kappa(1+l/2+n)^{D-1}}\underbrace{\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\in\mathcal{P}_{0}(\Gamma)\\
Z\ni x,y\end{subarray}}\left\lVert L_{Z}\right\rVert}_{\;\;\;\leq\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}F_{\alpha}(l/2+n)}

(A.2)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}\sum_{n=0}^{l/2}(1+l/2+n)^{D-1}F_{\alpha}(l/2+n)

\displaystyle=\kappa\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}\sum_{n=0}^{l/2}F_{\alpha-D+1}(l/2+n)

\displaystyle\leq\kappa\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}(1+l/2)F_{\alpha-D+1}(l/2)

\displaystyle=\kappa\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}F_{\alpha-D}(l/2),

where the last inequality follows from upper-bounding
F(l/2+n)\leq(l/2)
. Taking the superior gives the claim.
∎

.

for which we have used the bound obtained in

A.2
Lieb-Robinson bounds

Theorem A.4

(Truncation bound under the Hastings–Koma Lieb–Robinson estimate)
.

Let
H=\sum_{Z}h_{Z}
be a Hamiltonian on a lattice of bounded degree in dimension
d
such that Theorem
2.8
hold,
and fix a finite region
X
.

Then, for every operator
A_{X}
supported on
X
, if the interaction satisfies a power–law decay

\|h_{Z}\|\lesssim(1+\mathrm{diam}(Z))^{-\alpha}
with
\alpha>\eta>d
,
then there exists a constant
C_{d,\alpha,\eta}>0
such that

\big\|T_{H,t}(A_{X})-T_{H_{R},t}(A_{X})\big\|\ \leq\ \tilde{c}\|A_{X}\|\,\left|X\right|\,(\mathrm{e}^{v|t|}-1-v|t|)\,F_{\alpha-D}(d(X,Y)).

(A.12)

where
\tilde{c}
depends only on
c,v
in Equation (
2.38
).

Proof.

Since both
H
and
H_{R}
are self-adjoint (finite-volume lattice, bounded operators), the maps
t\mapsto T_{H,t}(A_{X})
and
t\mapsto T_{H_{R},t}(A_{X})
are differentiable in operator norm. Write

T_{H,t}(A_{X})-T_{H_{R},t}(A_{X})=\int_{0}^{t}\dfrac{d}{ds}\left[T_{H,t}\circ T_{H_{R},t-s}(A_{X})\right]ds.

Computing the derivative: since

\frac{d}{ds}T_{H,s}(B)=iT_{H,s}([H,B]),

and

\frac{d}{ds}T_{H_{R},t-s}(A_{X})=-iT_{H_{R},t-s}([H_{R},A_{X}]),

then,

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\left[T_{H,s}\!\left(T_{H_{R},t-s}(A_{X})\right)\right]=iT_{H,s}\!\left([H,T_{H_{R},t-s}(A_{X})]\right)-iT_{H,s}\!\left(T_{H_{R},t-s}([H_{R},A_{X}])\right).

(A.13)

Since
T_{H,s}
is a
*
-automorphism, this simplifies as

\displaystyle[H,T_{H_{R},t-s}(A_{X})]

\displaystyle-T_{H_{R},t-s}([H_{R},A_{X}])

\displaystyle=[H-H_{R},\,T_{H_{R},t-s}(A_{X})]+[H_{R},\,T_{H_{R},t-s}(A_{X})]-T_{H_{R},t-s}([H_{R},A_{X}]).

The last two terms cancel because
T_{H_{R},t-s}
is generated by
H_{R}
:

\displaystyle[H_{R},\,T_{H_{R},t-s}(A_{X})]=T_{H_{R},t-s}([H_{R},A_{X}]).

(A.14)

Hence:

\displaystyle\frac{d}{ds}\left[T_{H,s}\circ T_{H_{R},t-s}(A_{X})\right]=i\,T_{H,s}\!\left([H-H_{R},\,T_{H_{R},t-s}(A_{X})]\right).

(A.15)

The Duhamel formula gives

T_{H,t}(A_{X})-T_{H_{R},t}(A_{X})=i\int_{0}^{t}T_{t,s}^{H}\!\big([H-H_{R},\,T_{H_{R},s}(A_{X})]\big)\,ds.

(A.16)

Using that
T_{t,s}^{H}
contracts in operator norm,

\big\|T_{H,t}(A_{X})-T_{H_{R},t}(A_{X})\big\|\leq\int_{0}^{|t|}\!\big\|[H-H_{R},\,T_{H_{R},s}(A_{X})]\big\|\,ds.

(A.17)

Expanding
H-H_{R}=\sum_{Z\not\subset B_{R}(X)}h_{Z}
, we get

\big\|[H-H_{R},\,T_{H_{R},s}(A_{X})]\big\|\leq\underbrace{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\subset(B_{R}(X))^{C}\end{subarray}}\|h_{Z}\|\,\big\|[T_{H_{R},s}(A_{X}),O_{Z}]\big\|}_{(1)}\\
+\underbrace{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\not\subset B_{R}(X)\\
Z\cap X\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\|h_{Z}\|\,\big\|[T_{H_{R},s}(A_{X}),O_{Z}]\big\|}_{(2)}+\underbrace{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\not\subset B_{R}(X)\\
Z\cap X=\emptyset\end{subarray}}\|h_{Z}\|\,\big\|[T_{H_{R},s}(A_{X}),O_{Z}]\big\|}_{(3)},

(A.18)

where
\quad O_{Z}:=\frac{h_{Z}}{\|h_{Z}\|}.

For the first term, applying the bound in Theorem
2.8
with

O_{X}=A_{X}
,
O_{Z}=O_{Z}
,
r=0
, and noting that
d(X,Z)\geq R
, we have

\big\|[T_{H_{R},s}(A_{X}),O_{Z}]\big\|\leq\frac{c\,(e^{vs}-1)}{[1+d(X,Z)]^{\eta}}\,\|A_{X}\|.

Then,

(1)=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\subset(B_{R}(X))^{C}\end{subarray}}\|h_{Z}\|\,\big\|[T_{H_{R},s}(A_{X}),O_{Z}]\big\|=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=1\end{subarray}}^{\infty}\sum_{r=R+1}^{\infty}\underbrace{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}y\in\Lambda\\
d(X,y)=r\end{subarray}}}_{\leq\kappa r^{D-1}}\underbrace{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\ni y\\
d_{y}(Z)=l\end{subarray}}\left\lVert h_{Z}\right\rVert}_{\leq\kappa\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}F_{\alpha-D+1}(l)}F_{\alpha}(r)\\
\leq\kappa^{2}\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}\underbrace{\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}l=1\end{subarray}}^{\infty}F_{\alpha-D+1}(l)}_{\leq\dfrac{1}{\alpha-D}}\underbrace{\sum_{r=R+1}^{\infty}F_{\alpha-D+1}(r)}_{\leq\dfrac{F_{\alpha-D}(R)}{\alpha-D}}\leq\dfrac{\kappa^{2}\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}}{(\alpha-D)^{2}}F_{\alpha-D}(R).

(A.19)

For the second term
(2)
, note that every element
Z
in the sum has a diameter bigger or equal to
R
. However, Lieb-Robinson bounds cannot be used since
Z\cap X\neq\emptyset
, then, the trivial bound with the contractive property of
T^{H}
is applied in
\left\lVert[T_{H_{R},s}(A_{X}),O_{Z}]\right\rVert\leq 2\left\lVert A_{X}\right\rVert
. Using that fact, one can derive the following bound

(2)=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\not\subset B_{R}(X)\\
Z\cap X\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\|h_{Z}\|\,\big\|[T_{H_{R},s}(A_{X}),O_{Z}]\big\|\leq 2\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\not\subset B_{R}(X)\\
Z\cap X\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\|h_{Z}\|\,\big\|A_{X}\big\|\\
\leq 2\left\lVert A_{X}\right\rVert\sum_{r=R}^{\infty}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}x,y\\
x\in X\\
d(X,y)=r\end{subarray}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\ni x,y\end{subarray}}\left\lVert h_{Z}\right\rVert\leq 2\kappa\left|X\right|\left\lVert A_{X}\right\rVert\sum_{r=R}^{\infty}r^{D-1}F_{\alpha}(r)\\
\leq\dfrac{2\kappa\left|X\right|\left\lVert A_{X}\right\rVert}{\alpha-D}F_{\alpha-D}(R).

(A.20)

The last term,
(3)
, we can use the Lieb-Robinson bounds,

(3)=\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\not\subset B_{R}(X)\\
Z\cap X=\emptyset\end{subarray}}\|h_{Z}\|\,\big\|[T_{H_{R},s}(A_{X}),O_{Z}]\big\|\leq\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}x\in\mathsf{X}_{r}\end{subarray}}\sum\limits_{\begin{subarray}{c}Z\ni x,\\
d_{x}(Z)=R-r+l\end{subarray}}\left\lVert h_{Z}\right\rVert\underbrace{\big\|[T_{H_{R},s}(A_{X}),O_{Z}]\big\|}_{\leq\frac{c\,\big(e^{v(t-r)}-1\big)}{[1+r]^{\alpha}}\,\|A_{X}\|,}\\
\leq c\,\kappa\left\lVert A_{X}\right\rVert\left|X\right|\,\big(e^{v(t-r)}-1\big)\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}\underbrace{\sum_{r=1}^{R}F_{\alpha-D+1}(R-r+l)F_{\alpha}(r)}_{\leq C_{F}F_{\alpha-D+1}(R+l)}\leq\\
c\,\kappa^{2}C_{F}\left\lVert A_{X}\right\rVert\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}\left|X\right|\,\big(e^{v(t-r)}-1\big)\sum_{l=1}^{\infty}F_{\alpha-D+1}(R+l)\\
\leq\dfrac{c\,C_{F}\kappa^{2}\left\lVert A_{X}\right\rVert\left\lVert\mathcal{L}\right\rVert_{F}\left|X\right|\,\big(e^{v(t-r)}-1\big)}{\alpha-D}F_{\alpha-D}(R),

(A.21)

where we have used Lemma
A.2
.
Inserting into (
A.17
),

\displaystyle\big\|T_{H,t}(A_{X})-T_{H_{R},t}(A_{X})\big\|

\displaystyle\leq c\,F_{\alpha-D}(R)\int_{0}^{|t|}\!(e^{vs}-1)\,ds.

(A.22)

Evaluating the integral gives

\int_{0}^{|t|}(e^{vs}-1)\,ds=\frac{1}{v}(e^{v|t|}-1)-|t|\ =\ \frac{1}{v}\,(\mathrm{e}^{v|t|}-1-v|t|),

which proves (
A.12
) with
\tilde{c}=c/v
.
∎
