Validation of the Shoebox PureTest audiometry technology for remote data collection in clinical trials
Authors: Erin E. Robertson, Rolando J. Acosta, Gannie Tzoneva, Emily R. Redington, Matthew F. Wipperman, Pratyush Rai, Ramzi Majaj, Roland Barge, Meghan C. Drummond, Alan R. Shuldiner, Jacek K. Urbanek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
At present, traditional audiologic assessments are required to be performed in clinics and present a considerable burden to patients and sites (e.g., cost, time), particularly in clinical trials that require numerous longitudinal visits. This study evaluated an iPad-based remote audiometry technology, the Shoebox PureTest, for accuracy and usability, and aimed to determine whether it is fit for use in research and clinical studies. In participants with a wide range of hearing sensitivity, the Shoebox PureTest audiometer was determined to be sufficiently accurate, reliable, and usable, and was statistically equivalent to gold-standard conventional audiometry assessments at frequencies of 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz. The user experience survey demonstrated that > 80% of users rated the application and usability as ‘good’, and the overall satisfaction was rated as ‘excellent’ by 92% of participants. Whilst there are operational considerations for future use in clinical trials, the Shoebox PureTest audiometer has been shown to be a valid alternative to conventional hearing assessments which can be used for the optimization of clinical research studies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-49550-8.

Introduction

By 2050, it is projected that almost 2.5 billion people globally will be living with some form of hearing loss1, which can affect one or both ears and range from mild to profound in severity. A number of different forms of hearing loss have been identified, including age-related hearing loss, which can result from a combination of genetic predisposition and environmental noise exposure2–4. To date, treatment options have been limited to hearing aids and cochlear implants, neither of which achieves restoration of normal sound quality5. For some rare monogenic forms of hearing loss and deafness, preclinical research and a small number of clinical studies are ongoing to assess the potential utility of gene therapy5,6. To further progress research in this area, additional genetic targets need to be identified. The challenge is that there are limited data from population-level studies to inform drug development, and more large-scale population genetic studies are needed to identify new targets for precision therapy of common and rare forms of hearing loss and deafness.

There is a need for the identification of validated tools that can accurately and efficiently phenotype patients with hearing loss in large-scale population studies. At present, it is not feasible to conduct such large studies using traditional audiologic assessments as they are costly, time consuming, and require on-site administration by a licensed clinician. Portable or wearable devices may help solve some of these operational challenges and would allow for easier collection of more frequent longitudinal data in large samples of individuals. Further challenges with clinical trials in patients with hearing loss include the considerable burden on patients to have frequent visits to the clinic for auditory evaluations, and the need for long-term follow‑up to evaluate the longevity of therapy, especially in the case of novel gene therapies.

The Shoebox PureTest, a remote audiometry technology, uses an iPad and calibrated headphones to produce test signals at controlled levels to assist in the diagnosis of potential audiological conditions7. To mitigate variability in iPad or at-home testing, the Shoebox device implements device and transducer compensation, a standardized test methodology, and explicit instructions for administrators and patients to control environmental factors (e.g., ambient noise). It also performs room scans and device verification to confirm proper device and transducer function. Analytical and clinical validation has been demonstrated for the ShoeBox PureTest audiometer8–11, and the device has also been used in large population studies such as the National Health and Aging Trends Study12–14. Additionally, it exceeds the requirements for an audiometer to be used in an Occupational Health and Safety Administration (OSHA)-compliant hearing conservation program15, and hundreds of companies globally use it for occupational hearing testing of employees. The purpose of the current study was to evaluate the Shoebox PureTest audiometer for accuracy, test–retest reliability, and usability, and to determine whether it is a sufficiently valid alternative to conventional hearing assessments which can be used in clinical trials and other clinical research such as large genetic studies. This study includes a more robust assessment of test–retest reliability than has been seen in the literature previously, with Shoebox PureTest assessments performed multiple times both within the same day as well as across days. It also includes a more thorough usability assessment compared to what has been done previously. Clinical trials require the highest standards of validation for any device that is selected to collect outcome measure data; therefore, these additional validation steps are warranted to solidify the appropriateness of using Shoebox PureTest audiometry in clinical trials. Our hypothesis was that the Shoebox PureTest would prove to be equivalent to gold-standard audiometry within the equivalency bounds set a priori for this study.

Satisfaction with the Shoebox PureTest audiometer was rated as ‘excellent’ and usability was rated as ‘good’ based on the user experience survey

Sixty participants (out of 64 surveyed from Visit 1) self‑reported that they successfully completed the hearing test without any errors, retests, or assistance, resulting in a task success rate of 92% (i.e., the percentage of participants who successfully completed the task without any errors, retests, or assistance) and a self-reported error-based frequency retest rate of 22% (i.e., the percentage of participants who had to complete one or more frequency threshold retests during the hearing test). The remaining participants reported a partial success. This was due to either requiring administrator assistance; needing to pause the test due to external noise; or the application was unresponsive; or they misunderstood the instructions.

Over 60% of participants reported that they would be willing to complete the hearing test as often as required in the future. Participants were asked how the application made them feel, out of five negative (e.g., frustrated, anxious), one neutral, and five positive (e.g., confident, happy) potential responses, with 66% of participants having a positive emotional response to the Shoebox PureTest application (sum of positive responses). The tone of the text included with the application was also assessed, with five negative (e.g., lengthy, complicated), one neutral, and five positive (e.g., simple, friendly) potential responses, with 91% of participants reporting a positive response to the text included in the Shoebox PureTest application (sum of positive responses).

Most participants (80%) thought that the hearing test was not invasive. A few concerns about the test itself, the hardware, and the process were noted based on thematic analysis of the open-ended comments, including concern about the overall test sound volume (n = 5), a lack of more detailed guidance (n = 2), more practice tone(s) needed (n = 1), participants desiring better results feedback (n = 2), and a better iPad stand (n = 2).

In addition, observer-based metrics were collected during the study. The time taken to complete the Shoebox PureTest and the deviation or errors committed were collected for each participant across all 3 tests. Tests 1 and 2 were administered sequentially in Visit 1, and Test 3 was administered during Visit 2. The data collected on time taken to complete the Shoebox PureTest for the 1st, 2nd, and 3rd administrations showed an improvement in mean duration, decreasing from a median value of 15 min in the 1st test to 13 min in the 3rd test. Variability also decreased from Test 1 to Test 3. This indicated that the participants exhibited a learning curve for the self-administered tests. Data on the deviation and errors showed 5 instances (3.6%) in which the test was completed in the assisted mode, 2 instances (1.4%) of lack of attention leading to test disruption, and 4 abandoned tests (2.9%), out of a total of 138 pure tone tests.

Discussion

In this validation study, the Shoebox PureTest audiometer was shown to be sufficiently accurate, reliable, and usable in a sample of 64 adult participants with a broad range of hearing ability. These results demonstrate that, among the four formally assessed frequencies under evaluation, the Shoebox PureTest is statistically equivalent to gold-standard pure tone audiometry in participants with a wide range of hearing sensitivity. We further found excellent agreement of the Shoebox PureTest audiometer with gold-standard audiometry, with absolute agreement ICC values over 0.92 across all frequencies tested up to and including 12,500 Hz, and over 0.87 for 16,000 Hz. The test–retest reliability of the Shoebox PureTest audiometer was also found to be excellent and was nearly identical to that of the gold-standard audiometry, with absolute agreement ICC values of concordant measurements over 0.95 across all frequencies tested up to and including 12,500 Hz, and 0.88 for 16,000 Hz. Variations in age and biological sex had little effect on our conclusions. These findings expand upon the body of evidence describing the analytical and clinical validation of the Shoebox PureTest audiometer8–11,19. The current study provides a robust test–retest reliability assessment, demonstrating that the Shoebox PureTest audiometer is consistent in both intra-day and inter-day test–retests. Furthermore, extended high frequencies were measured in this study in an exploratory fashion, and the Shoebox PureTest audiometer was shown to maintain excellent agreement with gold-standard audiometry even at these exploratory frequencies. Given that extended high-frequencies may reveal early signs of hearing loss in certain clinical contexts, this was an important finding as it suggests that the Shoebox PureTest audiometer may also be useful for extended high-frequency measurement in clinical trials, although further validation with larger sample sizes is needed to definitively prove this.

Notably, most participants completed the Shoebox PureTest audiometer assessments on their own, without assistance or direct supervision from the study team, suggesting that this tool could be deployed in a remote test setting and would still yield highly accurate results. For the few participants who had trouble completing the assessment on their own, due to inconsistent responses leading the test to pause itself and request administrative assistance, the study team was able to complete the assessment in the assisted test mode successfully in all cases. Importantly, the assisted test mode does not require any clinical expertise on the part of the administrator, and it could therefore be performed successfully in a home or remote setting by a research assistant or a caregiver. Given these findings, it is reasonable to conclude that the Shoebox PureTest audiometer would be a suitable tool for at-home hearing testing in both observational research studies and clinical trial settings, where multiple follow-up assessments are needed depending on the exact requirements of the study protocol. It is important to note that screening assessments, including otoscopy and tympanometry, would still need to be completed by site staff minimally at the start of any study to rule out outer and/or middle ear conditions. Additionally, cases of significant asymmetric hearing would need to be flagged for further on-site assessment and referral for medical evaluation.

The user experience element of this study has suggested that the Shoebox PureTest audiometer assessment is easy to complete, with overall usability rated as good and satisfaction rated as excellent from a participant perspective, indicating that it could be successfully deployed in future research studies. A few considerations were raised by study participants which may need to be addressed should this technology be operationalized in future clinical trials. For example, better guidance and training through in-app tutorials or training modules with practice tones before testing, and provision of clear overall task progress feedback, are needed, particularly in the non-assisted mode. Poor quality supporting hardware (i.e., the provided iPad stand) can also negatively affect the user experience, as failure of these elements can disrupt testing. A quiet location is also likely to be needed, as one participant reported hearing background noise through the headphones and the application will interrupt the task flow if excessive background noise is detected. Finally, some participants may require live support throughout the process including required administrator support, desired results feedback, and assistance with touchscreen issues.

A limitation of this study was that all participants were adults; therefore, we cannot draw any conclusions about the use of the device in pediatric populations. In addition, as the study was conducted in a single geographic and socioeconomic setting, generalizability of the user experience findings may be limited. Participants in this region were generally well-educated and more familiar with technology and the use of tablets and apps than the general population, which may have influenced their responses. Furthermore, while the study included participants with a wide range of hearing abilities, from normal hearing to profound hearing loss, they were all from a single company, which may also bias the user experience survey results. Due to operational constraints randomization of the order of the assessments was not conducted, which may have had an effect on bias of the equivalence results. Furthermore, due to the nature of pure tone air conduction hearing thresholds in which measurements are taken in 5 dBHL increments, we found evidence that the difference in hearing threshold distributions deviated from normality via Shapiro–Wilk tests and Q-Qplots. However, the commensurate nonparametric equivalency tests yielded the same results as the parametric ones. Lastly, although a diverse range of participants in terms of hearing ability was included, a relatively small proportion of participants had severe‑to-profound hearing loss. The lack of a large enough number of participants with more profound forms of hearing loss limits this study’s ability to evaluate Shoebox PureTest performance at the extreme end of the hearing-loss spectrum. However, based on the data presented in individuals with less severe forms of hearing loss, Shoebox PureTest would be expected to phenotype individuals with more profound hearing loss.

Methods

Ethics statement

All study participants provided written and informed consent. This study was approved (approval date: 22 February 2024; 1370457) by the Western Institutional Review Board (WIRB®) now known as WIRB-Copernicus Group (WCG® IRB). The study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines and regulations of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design

The study comprised two visits (Fig. 4). Visit 1 included two Shoebox PureTest audiometry assessments, a gold-standard pure-tone audiometry assessment, and a user experience survey during a single site visit that took approximately 80 min to complete. Between 2 and 14 days later, a random subset of participants was asked to return for Visit 2 (approximately 40 min) during which they repeated the Shoebox PureTest audiometry assessment and the gold-standard pure-tone audiometry assessment. The purpose of the second visit was to collect additional data points to perform a robust assessment of test–retest reliability.Fig. 4Study design. UXR, user experience research.

Study design. UXR, user experience research.

Objectives

The primary objective was to assess the equivalence between the Shoebox PureTest and the gold-standard pure-tone audiometric assessments (using a Madsen Astera audiometer and Otosuite software)14 for the following formally assessed frequencies: 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz. Secondary objectives were to assess the test–retest reliability and the usability of the Shoebox PureTest audiometer.

Study participants

Participants were recruited internally via electronic announcements, email, and/or word of mouth at Regeneron Pharmaceuticals, Inc. Because hearing loss affects male and female individuals differently20, and because the prevalence of hearing loss tends to increase with age20, efforts were made to recruit an equal number of male and female participants and to involve ≥ 40 participants aged ≥ 40 years and ≥ 20 participants aged ≥ 50 years.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

To be eligible for inclusion, participants had to be full-time Regeneron employees aged ≥ 20 years within the New York tristate area. Participants using hearing aids or cochlear implants had to be willing to remove them or turn them off before testing. Participants were also required to have unremarkable otoscopy findings, ear canal volume within normal limits on immittance screening (0.6 to 1.5 cc), and absence of a Type B tympanogram on immittance screening, and to commit to abstaining from participating in any high-noise exposure activities without hearing protection (e.g., concerts, sporting events, or listening to personal headphones at high volume) for 2 weeks before Visit 1 and between Visits 1 and 2 for those who consented to participate in two site visits.

Participants were excluded if they had occluding ear wax in one or both ears; this may have been self-reported or visualized on otoscopy or detected on tympanometry. In the case of occluding ear wax, participants were permitted to enroll in the study only after visiting their primary care provider for ear wax removal or removing it themselves at home using over-the-counter ear wax removal kits. Participants were re-screened after ear cleaning. In addition, participants were excluded if they had eardrum perforation or active infection in one or both ears (self‑reported, visualized on otoscopy, or detected on tympanometry), Type B tympanogram on screening, or a history of Meniere’s disease with a recent history of fluctuating hearing in one or both ears (self-reported).

Participant confidentiality and data protection

Participants were de-identified by name or other direct identifiers. Participants’ age and sex were used as part of data analysis. The study coordinator assigned a unique study ID to each individual as a means of referencing participants so that during data analysis the study team members or study sponsor were not able to associate participants’ names or any other unique personal identifiers with the study data. The study coordinator also took reasonable and appropriate measures to protect the privacy of each study participant.

Sample size

Sample size calculations were based on per-frequency, paired-sample equivalence tests (i.e., two one-sided t-tests [TOST]) with a significance level of 5% and 80% power to detect a conservative ± 5 dBHL difference at four formally assessed frequencies: 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz. These four frequencies were selected because they comprise the four-frequency pure-tone average metric commonly used to summarize the audiogram and provide an estimate of speech reception ability. Per-frequency means and standard deviations were used for power calculations from previously published research11. For the primary objective, a Bonferroni correction was used to adjust for multiple comparisons across the four formally assessed frequencies, with the significance level for each per-frequency equivalence test set at α = 0.0125 (0.05/4) to maintain a family-wise error rate of 5%. For Visit 2, similar calculations were done. A 20% attrition rate was assumed from Visit 1 to Visit 2. Based on these data and assumptions, a sample size of 62 participants was targeted for enrollment for Visit 1, and 44 participants were targeted for Visit 2 to achieve a Visit 2 sample size of 35.

Data collection

The clinicodemographic variables collected included age, sex, history of hearing loss, use of hearing aid(s) or cochlear implant(s), history of noise exposure without hearing protection (e.g., loud music, machinery, ear buds), history of Meniere’s disease, otoscopy (i.e., subjective visualization of ear canal and eardrum), middle ear peak pressure (daPa), middle ear peak compliance (mL), ear canal volume (cm3), and tympanogram type (e.g., A, As, B). Otoscopy was performed using a Welch Allyn halogen pneumatic otoscope, and tympanometry was performed using a Madsen® Zodiac tympanometer. Clinicodemographic variables were used as stratifying factors in descriptive statistics and figures, and as covariates in regression analyses when applicable.

The gold-standard audiometric assessment was conducted on all participants by a trained Doctor of Audiology (the PI) in a certified sound booth with equipment that had been recently calibrated by the manufacturer. The Shoebox PureTest assessments were conducted in a nearby enclosed room in a quiet part of the building. For each audiometric assessment (gold standard and Shoebox PureTest), pure tone air conduction hearing thresholds were collected for the following frequencies: 250, 500, 1000, 2000, 4000, 8000, 10,000, 12,500, and 16,000 Hz. At each frequency, a hearing threshold for the right ear and left ear was obtained for each participant using the modified Hughson-Westlake procedure21 (note: this procedure is built in to the Shoebox PureTest proprietary algorithm). For the gold-standard assessment, if the participant reported having tinnitus, a pulsed tone was used as the test signal. For the Shoebox PureTest assessment, the algorithm is designed to switch automatically to a pulsed tone signal if it detects that the participant is responding inconsistently (potentially due to tinnitus). Bone conduction thresholds were obtained as part of the gold-standard assessment at 250, 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz in each ear for the purposes of characterizing the type of hearing loss if present (i.e., sensorineural, conductive, or mixed), using a bone transducer placed on the participant’s mastoid bone. Masking signals were presented to the non-test ear as clinically needed during the gold-standard air and bone conduction threshold assessments. Masking is not an included procedure in the Shoebox PureTest assessment.

The gold-standard assessment was performed using a computer-controlled Madsen® Astera2 audiometer with RadioEar DD450 high-frequency circumaural headphones and a standard bone conduction headband as the transducers22 The Shoebox PureTest assessment was performed using an iPad with the Shoebox software application installed on it, with RadioEar DD450 high-frequency circumaural headphones connected. Shoebox PureTest meets the following compliance standards: ANSI S3.6 2018, CSA Z107.6–16, FDA Class II, ISO 13485, and OSHA 29 CFR1910.95 audiometric requirementshttps://www.shoebox.md/content/uploads/OSHA-Hearing-Testing.pdf23.

Agreement of Shoebox PureTest audiometry and gold-standard audiometry

The agreement between the two measuring platforms was evaluated using a two-way, random effects, single measurement, absolute agreement ICC16,17 of concordant hearing thresholds across the two platforms for 500, 1000, 2000, and 4000 Hz16,17. The participant-, frequency-, and ear-specific hearing thresholds between Shoebox PureTest and the gold standard were compared.

For each frequency k, the icc function from the irr package in R was used to conduct the analysis26.

User experience research methodology

The user experience research element of the study consisted of a survey hosted on the SurveyMonkey platform, including qualitative and quantitative questions (with a mix of open and closed questions), which was designed following a systematic cross-functional review of the device (see Supplementary methods for a copy of the survey). The survey was designed to assess how participants interacted with the Shoebox PureTest tool by eliciting direct user feedback post-use, and aimed to provide usability insights to ensure the tool was easy to use, functional, and desirable.

Survey questions covered a range of user experience topics focused on task success, retest rate, error rate, net promoter score, overall satisfaction, usability, training and documentation, emotional response, perceived burden (NASA TLX)28, invasiveness, textual tone, issues experienced, and basic demographics.

Conclusions

The results of this study add to the current body of literature demonstrating that the Shoebox PureTest audiometer is a validated tool that is fit for use in clinical studies and has the potential to improve optimization of clinical trials within the auditory field. The Shoebox PureTest audiometer could also be used in the conduct of large-scale genetic studies to identify gene targets for common forms of deafness, which would inform drug development plans for gene therapies to treat hearing loss. Furthermore, the study design described here can be used as a model to guide the design and methodology of validation studies for other remote audiometry devices in the future.
