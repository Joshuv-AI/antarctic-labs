Blood-based kinase activity profiling to predict response to immune checkpoint inhibitors in patients with advanced stage NSCLC: the prospective IOpener study
Authors: Karlijn de Joode, Harry J Groen, Michel van den Heuvel, Jeroen S Kloover, Femke Van Der Meer, Cor H Van der Leest, Ben Van Den Borne, Rik De Wijn, Daan P Hurkmans, Dianne M A Van den Heuvel, Herbert M Pinedo, Ellen Kapiteijn, Reno Debets, Els M E Verdegaal, Sjoerd H Van der Burg, John P Groten, Joachim G J V Aerts, Ron H J Mathijssen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) tumor proportion score (TPS) is used as a biomarker to predict benefit from immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) in patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC). However, additional biomarkers are needed. The potential of kinase activity profiling of peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) for predicting response to ICB was previously shown in a discovery study. The goal of this prospective study is to evaluate the predictive value of blood-based kinase profiling for ICB response in patients with NSCLC and to compare the performance and added value to TPS.MethodsAdvanced stage patients with NSCLC, treated with anti-programmed cell death protein 1 ICB (±chemotherapy) were included in this multicenter study (N=210 patients). PBMCs were collected prior to ICB treatment and profiled using a peptide microarray with multiple kinase substrates (PamChip). Classification analysis was performed to discriminate between patients with or without progressive disease by Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) V.1.1 <24 weeks after treatment start. A predictive model was first established based on TPS and kinase activity profiles. Subsequently, the performance of the model was tested in a second (validation) cohort.ResultsIn the validation cohort, a significantly higher progression-free survival (PFS) rate was observed for patients with predicted benefit than for patients without predicted benefit according to the kinome-based prediction model (HR=0.56, p=0.01), which tended better compared with TPS alone (HR=0.66, p=0.07). When the kinome-based model was combined with TPS, the difference in PFS between patients with and without predicted benefit was further increased (HR=0.38, p<0.001). PFS rates were also higher for patients with predicted benefit in subgroups with decreased PD-L1 expression (TPS <1% (HR=0.46, p=0.027) and TPS 1–50% (HR=0.20, p=0.005)).ConclusionsThis study shows the predictive value of kinase activity profiling of PBMCs for ICB response in advanced NSCLC, which was superior compared with TPS. In particular, for patients with NSCLC with TPS <50%, combining TPS with the blood-based kinase test may identify patients with limited benefit from ICB.Trial registration numberNTR7015/NL6828.

Background

Immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) has become the standard first-line treatment for advanced patients with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) without any targetable mutations.1 However, only a subgroup responds to ICB, while patients are at risk of developing immune-related adverse events.13 To date, only programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression of the tumor is routinely used as a predictive biomarker for ICB response. PD-L1 expression is assessed by the PD-L1 tumor proportion score (TPS). This score mainly determines the treatment strategy in advanced patients with NSCLC without actionable targets. Patients with a TPS >50% typically receive anti-PD-(L)1 monotherapy, while those with a TPS <50% are usually treated with anti-PD-(L)1 therapy in combination with chemotherapy. For patients with a very low TPS (TPS <1%) chemotherapy alone may also be considered.1 2 4 Additional biomarkers are needed to more accurately predict benefit to ICB,57 as TPS does not always correlate with clinical outcome. Additional tumor tissue-based biomarkers such as tumor mutational burden and specific genomic alterations8 (eg, STK11/LKB1, KEAP1 and EGFR) have been correlated with ICB response but are not yet prospectively validated for guiding treatment decisions in advanced patients with NSCLC.9

Antitumor T-cell activity depends on kinase-mediated signaling pathways, which are essential for T-cell activation and effector function.1012 These pathways can be modulated and inhibited by immune checkpoint receptors. Binding of specific ligands to immune checkpoint receptors, such as ligation of programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) by PD-L1, affects several downstream protein kinase-mediated signaling pathways in parallel.13 14 Eventually, these signaling reactions decrease T-cell metabolism, cytokine production, and cytotoxic function, which will lead to an impaired inflammatory response and dampening (or exhaustion) of T-cell activity.1517 ICB blocks these inhibitory checkpoint receptors and thereby disrupts these inhibitory interactions, which may result in reactivation of different types of impaired or exhausted T cells. Hence, measuring the tyrosine kinase activity profiles of T cells reflects the activity in the accompanied downstream cellular processes, and may therefore predict ICB response.

A retrospective study was previously conducted to determine whether kinase activity profiling of peripheral mononuclear blood cells (PBMCs) accurately reflects T-cell response and could predict immune cell signaling before initiating ICB. In that study, we investigated the correlation between kinase activity profiles of PBMCs and ICB response in patients with NSCLC and melanoma.18 A multiplex peptide microarray platform consisting of 144 peptides derived from known substrates of tyrosine kinases was applied.18 This technology was previously used in oncological biomarker studies in different clinical settings.1921 The study showed distinct kinase activity profiles of PBMCs between patients with and without ICB response and initial predictive models could be developed.18

Here, we report a clinical study that was conducted with the aim to improve the predictive performance of kinase activity profiling of ICB treatment in advanced patients with NSCLC in a standardized and controlled setting. To this end, a first cohort of advanced patients with NSCLC treated with ICB was included to develop a model to predict ICB response. This model was subsequently evaluated in an independent second patient cohort. The objective was to generate a blood-based biomarker to predict ICB response in advanced stage patients with NSCLC and to guide patient stratification for optimal ICB treatment.

Methods

Inclusion

Patients with stage III irresectable or stage IV NSCLC could be included in the study if they were ≥18 years, had a WHO performance score ≤2 at study entrance and were planned to initiate ICB treatment for the first time. ICB could consist of first-line pembrolizumab (±chemotherapy) or second-line nivolumab monotherapy. Prior treatment for NSCLC was allowed if patients had not previously received ICB-containing treatment. Patients were treated in one of the participating centers in the Netherlands. The study was conducted in an observational setting and results for individual patients were not provided to the treating physician. The protocol was evaluated and approved by the Medical Ethical Committee of every participating site and all patients provided written informed consent before participating in the study.

Clinical data collection

Clinical data were collected in an electronic case record form by local data managers under the supervision of a Contract Research Organization (CRO, Avania B V, the Netherlands). Parameters collected at baseline included age, sex, WHO performance score, prior (systemic) treatment, PD-L1 TPS, presence of brain metastases and different blood parameters. Duration of treatment, follow-up therapy, radiological response and survival data were also collected. Response to treatment was evaluated according to Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) V.1.1 (ie, complete response, partial response, stable disease (SD) or progressive disease (PD))22 and determined between treatment initiation until 1-year clinical follow-up. SD had to be confirmed by at least one follow-up scan with an interval of ≥8 weeks. Patients were categorized according to having PD, defined as the occurrence of PD <12 weeks or occurrence of PD <24 weeks. In case the PD-L1 score was missing, patients were marked as non-evaluable for the analysis.

Preparation of PBMCs

Blood samples were collected in a sodium heparin tube before the first administration of ICB. Blood was sent by courier to Sanquin, Amsterdam, the Netherlands, under temperature-controlled conditions (15–25°C). At Sanquin, PBMC isolation was completed the same day as the blood was withdrawn, typically between 4 and 8 hours after blood collection. Isolated pelleted PBMCs were snap-frozen directly after isolation and cryopreserved at −80°C according to standard procedures (figure 1). Part of the samples of the model development cohort were processed at the laboratories of the University Medical Center Groningen and Erasmus Medical Center for logistic reasons.

Kinase activity profiling

Preparation of the samples and kinase activity profiling were performed by PamDx (‘s-Hertogenbosch, the Netherlands) according to standard procedures at centralized facilities as part of the design and development of an in-vitro diagnostic device under an ISO 13485 certified quality management system (details in online supplemental methods).

Study work flow

An initial cohort (model development cohort) was used to establish a predictive model and its performance was evaluated in a subsequent independent validation cohort. Both cohorts were planned to consist of 80 patients each to enable a determination of predictive accuracy with two-sided 90% CI of ±10%, at a predictive accuracy of 70%. In addition, this would allow for >95% power for detecting significant predictive performance (predictive accuracy >50%, α=0.05, one-sided binomial test). During the study, a protocol amendment was approved to expand the validation cohort to ensure that an additional number of patients with different PD-L1 scores were included. Cohorts were chronologically completed with patients from the different participating centers. Figure 1 shows an overview of the study design.

Statistical analysis

The final analysis was performed after 1-year clinical follow-up data were available for all patients. Data processing of the kinase activity profiles is described in detail in the online supplemental methods, and included normalization and a correction for systematic differences between measurement runs based on measurements on standard PBMC samples that were included in each run. Data analysis was performed in the statistical program R (V.4.3.3). Patient characteristics were analyzed for any significant differences between the cohorts. The Fisher’s exact test was used to test for the association of proportional data, while the Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test was applied for numerical data. Patients were divided into three subgroups according to TPS, being: TPS <1%, TPS 1–50% and TPS >50%. Progression-free survival (PFS) was set as the time between treatment initiation and occurrence of PD or death, whichever occurred first. Overall survival (OS) was determined as the time between start of ICB and death. Patients who did not have an event at time of analysis or were lost to follow-up were censored at the date of last contact. Analysis of survival data were performed using the R packages survival23 and survminer.24

Model development

The first (development) cohort was used to correlate kinase activity profiles of PBMCs with ICB response data and for training and cross-validation of predictive models. Analysis was started when 80 patients were included. 4 patients were found to be screen failures, resulting in 76 patients to be included in this cohort. The association of the multivariable kinase activity profiles with occurrence of PD was statistically tested using the global test approach of Goeman et al.25 26 Predictive models were trained with partial least squares discriminant analysis without prior feature selection using the kinase activity profiles as predictor variables and the occurrence of PD as the (binary) response variable. The optimal number of PLS components was determined by cross validation and a difference in the number of observations in both classes was accounted for by applying a random under-sampling scheme. Performance estimates for predicting PD based on the cohort used for model development were obtained by 20-fold cross validation, taking care to determine the optimal number of components on the training set in each fold only (double cross validation). Once the model was finalized, the model coefficients (used to convert a measured peptide profile in a predictive score) were fixed and documented before the model was applied to the validation cohort.

Initially, two models were defined during model development, that is, based on occurrence of PD at 12 weeks (PD12) and PD at 24 weeks (PD24), respectively, and both models were planned for evaluation with data from the validation cohort.

Model performance evaluation

Performance of both models was evaluated by application to patients in the independent validation cohort. First, predictive scores were generated by applying the models established in the model development cohort to kinase activity profiles measured in the validation cohort. These predictions were documented and submitted to the CRO supervising the clinical data collection (Avania B V, the Netherlands). Only after this, access to clinical follow-up data was made available to evaluate predictive performance of the models. Therefore, data analysis of the blood test in the validation study was carried out with blinded outcome of clinical response. The calculated predictive scores were rated on a scale from 0 to 100 and a predictive score ≥44 was taken as a positive test (predicted benefit), to allow optimal detection of patients with clinical benefit in the validation cohort. In addition, kinase activity profiles were identified, based on predetermined parameters, for which the trained model was not applicable because of a too large deviation from the kinase activity profiles in the training set. For this, the fully processed kinase activity profiles measured for the validation cohort were projected on the first two principal components derived from a principal component analysis (PCA) of the kinase activity profiles that were used to establish the model. The resulting PCA scores were used to calculate the squared Mahalanobis distance (d2) of the new profiles compared with the training set and a measurement was flagged as an outlier if d2 was greater than or equal to the 0.99 quantile point of the χ2 distribution with 2 df. These outlier scores were documented and submitted together with the predictive scores before unblinding clinical follow-up data, as described above. Patients were included in the analyses if PD-L1 expression was available and if they received at least one cycle of pembrolizumab-containing treatment and response evaluation occurred at least 6 weeks after treatment initiation while no other oncological treatment (for NSCLC) was started.

Upstream kinase analysis (post hoc analysis)

Following the performance evaluation, a post hoc analysis was performed on all patients with TPS <50% from both cohorts in order to predict which kinases are responsible for the observed changes in peptide phosphorylation. For this, upstream kinase analysis was performed by (non-exclusively) assigning groups of peptides to kinases based on information on kinase substrate relations in both experimental and in-silico databases for post-translational modifications. Subsequently, the global test26 was applied on these kinase-related groups of peptides to assess the association of the corresponding kinases with PFS. For the global test the directional parameter was set to true, that is, requiring that in a kinase-related group of peptides the direction of the association should be equal, and both the TPS group (<1% or >1%) and the provided treatment (pembrolizumab+chemotherapy or nivolumab) of the patients were taken into account. Results were visualized by annotating them on a kinase phylogenetic tree using the web-based Coral tool.27 The direction of the association of a kinase with PFS was determined based on the geometric mean of the HRs resulting from univariate Cox regression of peptides assigned to a kinase.

Results

Study cohorts and study design

An overview of the study workflow is summarized in figure 1. The final inclusion of patients in the cohorts was finalized in October 2021. Some patients had to be excluded from the analysis because blood samples showed technical outliers or patients were considered non-evaluable due to clinical conditions, for example, unknown PD-L1 expression or no follow-up data available (figure 1). For the first cohort (model development) patients with first and second-line ICB could be included, while for the validation cohort, only patients treated with first-line ICB were included. Consequently, differences in baseline characteristics were observed between the cohorts (table 1). The difference in treatment strategy most likely explains the PFS difference that is observed between the model development and validation cohort (online supplemental results, online supplemental figure 4). Notably, the proportion of patients with progressive disease at 12 weeks (PD12) was 49% in the model development cohort, but only 21% in the validation cohort (for the occurrence of progressive disease at 24 weeks (PD24) this was 62% and 38%). As a result, the proportion of patients with PD12 was too low to evaluate the predictive model in the validation cohort and therefore, we focused on evaluation of the model based on PD24. Results for the PD12 model are summarized in online supplemental results. A total of 61 patients could be included for the analysis of the first cohort based on PD24, that is, model development, and 136 patients were included for validation of the model (figure 1). Model development

Inclusion of the cohort for model development was finalized in November 2019 and clinical follow-up for PD24 was completed in November 2020. A first estimate of the predictive performance of the model was obtained by 20-fold cross-validation in the cohort used for model development and the performance was compared with TPS, as the latter is already used as a predictive biomarker in clinical practice. Overall, the kinome model resulted in an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.63, where the predictive performance was higher for patients with TPS <50% (AUC=0.67). Predictive performance of the model was further evaluated for PFS (p=0.02), for which PFS rates were also compared for TPS <50 versus >50% (p=0.051) and by combining both TPS and kinome score. For the latter, patients were assigned to the group with predicted benefit to ICB if they had a TPS >50% and/or were predicted as having no PD according to the kinome model. Patients with a TPS <50% and/or having PD according to the kinome model were assigned as having no predicted benefit (figure 1, cross table). This combined kinome/TPS score resulted in a significantly improved difference in PFS rates (p=0.004).

After establishing the prediction model for the development cohort, its performance was evaluated with data from the validation cohort. For this, predictive scores were generated by applying the established models to the kinase activity profiles measured in the validation cohort.

However, significantly different PFS rates were observed when using the (PD24) kinome model prediction (p=0.02, HR=0.57) (figure 2B). The prediction for PFS improved even further when TPS and kinome model predictions were combined as illustrated in figure 1 (p<0.001, HR=0.38) (figure 2C). No significant differences for PFS were found between the pathological tumor subtypes and the predictive performance for PFS was equal between the NSCLC subtypes (see online supplemental results).

Upstream kinase analysis

We performed a post hoc analysis after concluding the analysis for the predictive model, to obtain insight into the underlying biology of kinase activity profiling as a predictor of ICB response. All evaluable patients with TPS <50% from the combined model development and validation cohort (N=116) were selected to obtain a large and optimized set of kinase activity profiles for this purpose. These kinase activity profiles showed a significant association with both PD24 (p=0.002, global test) and PFS (p=0.01, global test), when both the TPS group (<1% or >1%) and the provided treatment (pembrolizumab+chemotherapy or nivolumab) were taken into account in the global test. In addition, this set of kinase activity profiles was used for an upstream kinase analysis (as described in Materials and Methods). Results are shown by means of a kinase phylogenetic tree (figure 5). High activity of VEGF receptor kinases (FLT1, FLT3, FLT4) and/or related kinases such as FGF receptor kinases that have been associated with immune suppression,2427 were most significantly related to an improved PFS. Activity of Src family kinases (eg, Lyn, HCK, Lck, and BLK), located downstream of the T-cell receptor,28 was correlated with decreased PFS.

Discussion

There is a clear clinical need for new biomarkers to predict response to ICB. Currently, PD-L1 expression is used as a diagnostic marker in advanced patients with NSCLC to determine the optimal treatment strategy. However, the predictive performance of PD-L1 score is poor, with an AUC of approximately 0.5–0.6.28 29 In the current study, the combination of the IOpener test with TPS reveals an improved predictive power for PFS, especially in patients with PD-L1 scores between 1% and 49%.

The IOpener blood test was first described in retrospective cohorts of patients with melanoma and NSCLC and is currently applied to several new ICB indications.18 Kinase activity profiling of PBMCs in peripheral blood offers a minimally invasive way to assess immune cell signaling prior to ICB initiation.18 In the present prospective multicenter study, we have demonstrated the predictive utility of this blood test for treatment response of patients with NSCLC. Compared with the previously reported retrospective study,18 all blood collection procedures were standardized across participating sites and study data were not disclosed and (blindly) collected by independent CRO.

In this study, we have focused on the evaluation of the kinome model for predicting PD24. The initial modeling included predicting PD within 12 weeks, which could not be confirmed in the validation cohort. We attribute this to a longer PFS in the validation cohort compared with the development cohort with a median PFS of 15 and 37 weeks, respectively. This resulted in a low proportion of patients with PD12 in the validation cohort (online supplemental results), which may be explained because patients who were treated with nivolumab as second-line treatment were included for model development but were not included in the validation cohort. Despite this, the prediction scores of the PD12 and PD24 models showed a strong correlation (online supplemental figures 4 and 5), indicating that the underlying differential kinase activity profiles are similar. Regardless of the observed differences, the performance evaluation of the PD24 model is based on the validation cohort in which the applied ICB (first-line pembrolizumab±chemotherapy) is most representative of the current clinical practice.

Both the model development and validation cohorts were planned to consist of 80 patients each to ensure a power of >95% for detecting significant predictive performance. Since the patient data collection was blinded and sample collection after the initial cohort continued for the validation phase, it was not possible to complement (or to correct for) patients missing in the development cohort. Although we did not reach a total of 80 patients included in the model development cohort, it is not expected that this has had a significant impact on the analysis.

Protein kinases play a key role in cell signaling, including pathways that lead to the activation and suppression of the antitumor activity of T cells, through T-cell receptor (TCR)-related signaling as well as by related co-stimulatory pathways.10 Here, we demonstrated the predictive value for ICB response of kinase activity measured in pretreatment PBMCs of patients with NSCLC. To obtain more insight into the biology that underlies the predictive value of kinase activity profiles for ICB response, a post hoc analysis was performed on the kinase activity profiles of 116 evaluable patients with TPS <50% included in this study. Results of this analysis indicate that high activity of VEGF receptor kinases (KDR, FLT1, FLT3, FLT4) and/or related FGF receptor kinases were significantly associated with an improved PFS. In contrast, activity of the Src family (Lyn, HCK, Lck, and BLK) was correlated with an increased risk for PD. Both VEGFR30 31 and FGFR pathways32 33 are known to be associated with stimulation of immune suppressive mechanisms as these pathways promote immune evasion.31 32 34 35 However, we hypothesize that elevated VEGFR/FGFR activity in PBMCs marks a suppressed but reversible immune state retaining reactivation capacity, which is supported by preclinical data demonstrating synergistic antitumor effects when combining VEGF/FGF inhibition with immune checkpoint blockade.3233 3639 In contrast, Lck and Fyn are essential for TCR transduction,101240 41 and lower activity of Src family kinases likely reflects lower activity in the TCR pathway. The upstream kinase analysis reflects the context-dependent roles of kinase activity in immune regulation. Together, these findings suggest that the kinase activity profile of peripheral immune cells reflects the systemic immune status and that the profile associated with a lower risk of progression is indicative of patients with a suppressed immune system that can indeed be reactivated by ICB. A comparison of the baseline samples to samples collected after initiation of ICB may potentially interest in this respect, but the results would likely depend on multiple factors, including the time point at which these samples are collected. An analysis of samples collected for part of the patients at an early time point during standard of care ICB treatment has been added in the online supplemental results. The observed performance of our model differs between patients with high and low TPS (eg, figure 4). While limitations of PD-L1 expression as a predictor of ICB response can be partially attributed to tumor heterogeneity and other practical challenges in PD-L1 determination,42 the variation in predictive performance based on peripheral blood profiling suggests a different underlying mechanism. It has been pointed out that the activity of anti-PD-1 treatment is not restricted to the tumor microenvironment; PD-1/PD-L1 interactions in lymph nodes also play a role in mobilizing T cells to the tumor following ICB.40 41 Possibly, different activation pathways are more prominent in tumors with high PD-L1 expression compared with those with low expression, with PMBCs in peripheral blood mostly reflecting the potential for activation of the peripheral immune system.

This study shows that tyrosine kinase activity profiling of immune cells can be used to predict response to ICB in patients with NSCLC and performs as good or even outperforms the TPS evaluation. Interestingly, the predictive performance of the kinome model was highest when applied in the subgroup of patients with TPS <50%, showing the potential of combining biomarkers based on tumor tissue and the patients’ immune system. TPS evaluation is part of the routine work-up before treatment for advanced NSCLC is initiated, that is, PD-L1 scores are investigated before treatment start. Adding a blood withdrawal for collection of PBMCs to this work-up routine, will allow for the possibility to collect additional information on prediction of ICB response (based on the kinome model) before treatment start. In addition, research has shown that for patients with low PD-L1 scores in particular, the combined positive score was more accurate in predicting response.43 Such combinations may be further extended by, for example, tumor mutational burden, specific mutations,8 and models based on clinical (laboratory) data.28 Other potential biomarkers include circulating tumor DNA, proteomics,44 hematological markers and even radiomics.45 Clinical decision-making for the optimal treatment regimen for patients with advanced NSCLC may be best supported by combinations of different biomarker modalities and this should be part of future research.

Hence, it is shown that reflection of the immune state as measured by kinase activity profiles provides the possibility to predict ICB response. The combination of blood-based tyrosine kinase activity profiling and the tumor-based TPS biomarker is of additional value compared with predictions based on TPS or tyrosine activity profiling alone and predictions lead to significant differences in both PFS and OS. Notably, patients with both TPS <50% and predicted PD based on the kinome model have only limited benefit from treatment with pembrolizumab±chemotherapy as the 1-year PFS in this group is only 16% (indicating a negative predictive value of the test of 84%) (figure 3C). Further research may focus on, for example, the group of patients with TPS 1–50%. For patients of this specific group that have a predicted PD based on the kinome model, chemotherapy alone might be a reasonable treatment option, or other treatment alternatives may also be considered. However, this should be further investigated in a prospective setting.

Conclusions

This study shows the predictive value of kinase-activity profiling of PBMCs for ICB response in advanced NSCLC, which was superior compared with TPS. The combination of the IOpener test with TPS reveals an improved predictive power for PFS, especially in patients with PD-L1 scores between 1% and 50%. In clinical practice, the IOpener test may serve as an aid for physicians to identify patients for whom alternatives to treatment with anti-PD-1 (±chemotherapy) would be more appropriate. Depending on the situation, alternatives for patients with TPS <50% and no predicted benefit could be treatment with chemotherapy alone, but also combination therapy with anti-cytotoxic T-lymphocyte associated protein 4+anti-PD-1 treatment may be considered.29 Currently, studies are initiated to implement kinase activity profiling combined with the TPS as a decision tool in this context.
