Effect of desflurane versus propofol on perioperative neurocognitive disorders in older adults undergoing major urological surgery: a randomized trial
Authors: Pawit Somnuke, Arunotai Siriussawakul, Orawan Pongraweewan, Aphichat Suphathamwit, Patumporn Suraarunsumrit, Patimaporn Wongprompitak, Peewara Treesuwan, Ornrawee Kampeeratammo, Sittiporn Srinualnad, Varat Woranisarakul, Chalairat Suk-ouichai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPerioperative neurocognitive disorders (PND), encompassing postoperative delirium (POD), delayed neurocognitive recovery (dNCR), and postoperative neurocognitive disorder (postoperative NCD), are commom complications of surgery in older adults. Urological surgery carries particularly high risk, with reported incidences reaching 30%. Elevated serum biomarkers in affected patients suggest diagnostic and prognostic utility. Although propofol may reduce postoperative neurocognitive disorder risk compared with other inhalational agents, data on desflurane remain limited despite its rapid pharmacokinetics. This study compared the incidence of POD, dNCR, postoperative NCD, perioperative complications, and biomarker profiles in patients receiving general anesthesia with propofol or desflurane.MethodsThis prospective randomized controlled trial enrolled patients aged 65 years or older undergoing major urological surgery, who were assigned to receive propofol (via target-controlled infusion) or desflurane. Anesthesia depth and cerebral oxygenation were maintained using bispectral index monitoring (40–60) and near-infrared spectroscopy (≥ 60%), respectively. Serum biomarkers were measured at 5 time points (T0–T4). POD was assessed on postoperative days 1–3 using the 3-Minute Diagnostic Interview for Confusion Assessment Method, whereas dNCR and postoperative NCD were assessed using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment between postoperative days 5–9 and at 3–6 months postoperatively, respectively.ResultsAmong 126 patients (propofol, 64; desflurane, 62), overall POD incidence was 14.1% versus 9.8% (P = 0.467), and dNCR incidence at days 5 through 9 was 4.7% versus 11.5% (P = 0.199). However, the number of delirium-present days was statistically significantly greater in the desflurane group (1 [1–1] vs. 1.5 [1–2]; P = 0.044). Interleukin-6 levels increased substantially in both groups, with higher levels on day 3 in patients with POD than in those without (63.8 [42.9–206.4] vs. 31.2 [20.9–69.8] pg/mL; P = 0.009).ConclusionsAlthough PND incidence did not differ between propofol and desflurane, delirium duration was longer with desflurane; however, this exploratory finding should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of delirium cases. Interleukin-6 may serve as a useful biomarker for POD monitoring. Bispectral index and near-infrared spectroscopy-guided anesthesia management may help reduce the incidence of PND.Trial registrationThis study was registered in the Thai Clinical Trials Registry on 24/09/2021 (registration number: TCTR20210924002).Keywords: Biomarkers, Delayed neurocognitive recovery (dNCR), Desflurane, Elderly, Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), Perioperative cognitive dysfunction (PND), Postoperative delirium (POD), Postoperative neurocognitive dysfunction (postoperative NCD), Propofol

Introduction

The global population is aging, with approximately 10% of people worldwide now older than 65 years; the highest proportions are found in Europe and East Asia [1]. Thailand has one of the highest proportions of older adults, with those aged 65 years or older accounting for 15.36% of the population [2]. This age group is particularly susceptible to cognitive impairment following surgical procedures [3, 4].

Frequent postoperative cognitive impairments include postoperative delirium (POD), which typically develops within days after surgery, and postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD), which may persist from 3 to 5 days to more than 1 year postoperatively [5–8]. According to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5)–based nomenclature, perioperative neurocognitive disorder (PND) is now the umbrella term encompassing POD, delayed neurocognitive recovery (dNCR; within 30 days postoperatively), and postoperative neurocognitive disorder (postoperative NCD; 30 days to 12 months postoperatively) [9].

The incidence of PND in noncardiac surgery ranges from 3% to more than 40% [10, 11], whereas in cardiac surgery, incidence may exceed 50% [12, 13]. dNCR and postoperative NCD incidence in older adults has been reported at 9% to 54% regardless of surgery type or anesthesia technique (general vs. regional) [14, 15]; however, incidence decreases to approximately 10% after 3 months [16].

Volatile inhalational anesthetic agents are thought to play a substantial role in PND development compared with intravenous agents such as propofol, possibly because of elevated serum biomarkers associated with systemic inflammation and subsequent cognitive impairment [17–22].

Desflurane, a volatile anesthetic, has been widely used for more than 3 decades because of its favorable pharmacokinetic profile, including rapid induction and recovery resulting from its low blood–gas partition coefficient [23]. Despite these advantages, the effect of desflurane on PND incidence remains controversial and understudied, particularly in major noncardiac procedures such as urological surgery, which is commonly performed in older patients. In contrast, studies of other inhalational agents, including isoflurane and sevoflurane, have reported detrimental effects on postoperative cognitive function compared with intravenous propofol [24, 25].

This study compared 2 anesthetic techniques—intravenous propofol and desflurane-based inhalational anesthesia—in older adults undergoing elective urological surgery. The primary aim was to evaluate differences in PND incidence, and the secondary aims were to assess perioperative complications and examine changes in serum biomarkers associated with neurocognitive dysfunction.

Materials and methods

Study design

This prospective, randomized controlled trial was conducted at an academic, university-affiliated tertiary care hospital in Bangkok, Thailand. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Faculty of Medicine Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, on August 11, 2021, prior to patient recruitment (Certificate of Approval [COA] No. Si 617/2021). Data were collected between June 2022 and March 2025. This study was registered in the Thai Clinical Trials Registry on September 24, 2021 (registration number: TCTR20210924002; https://trialsearch.who.int/Trial2.aspx?TrialID=TCTR20210924002).

Study population

The study enrolled patients aged 65 years or older with American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status 1 to 3 who were scheduled for elective urological surgery expected to last at least 2 h. All participants were required to be literate in Thai and capable of effective verbal communication. Exclusion criteria included allergy to anesthetic agents, preexisting psychological or cognitive disorders, history of drug abuse or psychostimulant use, and chronic alcohol consumption. Patients were withdrawn after randomization and receipt of either propofol or desflurane anesthesia if the surgery was canceled or modified, if major intraoperative complications or failed extubation occurred, if cognitive assessments could not be completed for any reason, or if consent was withdrawn.

Randomization and anesthesia protocol

Patients were randomly assigned to either the propofol group or the desflurane group, with anesthesia maintained using continuous intravenous propofol infusion or inhaled desflurane, respectively. Group assignments were sealed in opaque envelopes and revealed at anesthesia initiation. Both participants and outcome assessors were blinded to treatment allocation.

Anesthesia was induced using intravenous fentanyl (0.5–1 µg/kg), propofol (0.5–2 mg/kg), and either atracurium (0.5–0.6 mg/kg) or cisatracurium (0.1–0.15 mg/kg), followed by tracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation. Standard monitoring included noninvasive blood pressure measurement, electrocardiography, and pulse oximetry. In the propofol group, anesthesia was maintained using target-controlled infusion based on the Schneider model (Agilia; Fresenius Kabi, Fresenius Vial S.A.S., Brézins, France). Effect-site concentration was titrated to 3–5 µg/mL according to the bispectral index, which was monitored via sensors placed on the nasal dorsum—a method validated for accuracy compared with standard forehead placement [26, 27].

Cerebral oxygen saturation was monitored using near-infrared spectroscopy. Bispectral index values were maintained at 40 to 60 and near-infrared spectroscopy values at 60% or higher, with no more than a 20% reduction from baseline throughout the period from anesthetic induction to completion of surgery. Intraoperative analgesia and muscle relaxation were maintained through intermittent fentanyl and atracurium or cis-atracurium administration. The discontinuation of muscle relaxants and analgesics was performed 30–60 min before the completion of surgery at the discretion of the anesthesiologist. Hypotension was managed with intravenous inotropes or vasopressors to maintain mean arterial pressure at 65 mm Hg or higher and prevent systolic blood pressure decreases exceeding 20% from baseline. Peripheral oxygen saturation was maintained at 95% or higher by pulse oximetry. Agents known to affect postoperative neurocognitive outcomes—including ketamine, benzodiazepines, dexmedetomidine, pethidine, and antihistamines—were avoided [28–30]. Administration of propofol or desflurane was discontinued at the end of surgery.

Functional status and quality of life assessment tools

Barthel index for activities of daily living

The Thai version of the Barthel Index for Activities of Daily Living (BADL) was used to assess basic activities of daily living. This instrument comprises 10 items: feeding, transfers (between wheelchair and bed), personal toileting, mobility, bathing, walking on level surfaces, stair use, dressing, bowel control, and bladder control. Each item is scored in increments of 0, 5, 10, or 15, with a maximum total score of 100; higher scores reflect greater independence, and a score of 0 indicates complete dependency [31, 32].

Lawton–Brody instrumental activities of daily living scale

The Thai version of the Lawton–Brody Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (IADL) scale was used to evaluate more complex functional abilities; this version has demonstrated acceptable linguistic and content validity in Thai populations. The scale assesses 8 domains: telephone use, shopping, food preparation, housekeeping, laundry, transportation mode, medication management, and financial management. Each domain is scored as 0 or 1, yielding a total score of 0 to 8, with higher scores indicating greater functional independence [33, 34].

EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level questionnaire

Health-related quality of life was assessed using the Thai version of the EuroQol 5-Dimension 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L) questionnaire, which incorporates locally developed coefficient values for each dimension. Only the descriptive system was used, covering 5 dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities (work and daily tasks), pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each dimension is rated from 1 to 5, where 1 indicates no problems and 5 indicates extreme problems [35].

Neurocognitive assessment tools

Multiple neurocognitive assessment tools were administered before and after surgery. Evaluations were conducted by trained research assistants under the supervision of experienced geriatricians. All assessors were blinded to patients’ baseline cognitive status and group allocation.

Modified informant questionnaire on cognitive decline in the elderly

The Informant Questionnaire on Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE) was developed to assess cognitive decline, particularly in patients with Alzheimer disease [36]. The Modified IQCODE, derived from this instrument, evaluates changes in cognitive function over the preceding 10 years based on informant reports; a cutoff score of 3.42 has been identified as the optimal threshold for dementia screening [37].

3-Minute diagnostic interview for confusion assessment method

The 3-Minute Diagnostic Interview for Confusion Assessment Method (3D-CAM) is a brief tool that incorporates the Confusion Assessment Method (CAM) diagnostic algorithm for delirium [38]. The 3D-CAM has been validated against the DSM-5, demonstrating sensitivity and specificity exceeding 90%, supporting its use in routine clinical practice across various cultural settings [39, 40]. The Thai version, translated by Dr Titima Wongviriyawong and colleagues from the Division of Geriatric Medicine, Siriraj Hospital, Mahidol University, was used with permission from the original authors [38].

The 3D-CAM assesses 4 core features of delirium: (1) acute onset or fluctuating course, (2) inattention, (3) disorganized thinking, and (4) altered level of consciousness. Delirium is diagnosed when features 1 and 2 are both present along with either feature 3 or 4 [38].

Trained research assistants administered the Thai version of the 3D-CAM to assess baseline cognitive status on the day before surgery, upon ward admission. Postoperatively, the 3D-CAM was repeated at 24, 48, and 72 h (postoperative days 1 through 3). Delirium episodes identified during this period were recorded as POD incidence for each day; POD on any of the 3 postoperative days constituted a positive case for overall incidence.

Montreal cognitive assessment

The Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) is a neurocognitive screening tool comprising subtests that assess 8 cognitive domains: visuospatial/executive function (5 points), naming (3 points), memory (not scored initially), attention (6 points), language (3 points), abstraction (2 points), delayed recall (5 points), and orientation (6 points). Total scores range from 0 to 30 [41].

The Thai version of MoCA was used in this study. Based on established recommendations, dNCR and postoperative NCD (previously termed POCD) were defined as a decline of ≥ 1 standard deviation from the baseline MoCA score [9]. A Thai population study reported an SD of 2.14 for MoCA scores; therefore, a reduction of 2 points or more from baseline was considered indicative of dNCR and postoperative NCD [42]. Additionally, Hu et al. performed a sensitivity analysis comparing multiple definitions of dNCR and postoperative NCD (MoCA ≤ 26, ≥ 1 SD decline, ≥ 2-point decline, and Z-score ≥ 1.96), identifying a ≥ 2-point decline as the most accurate definition (sensitivity and specificity > 80%; AUROC 0.824) [43]. MoCA scores of 25 or higher indicate normal cognitive function, whereas scores of 18–24 suggest mild cognitive impairment and scores below 18 indicate dementia [40, 44, 45].

Trained research assistants administered the MoCA to assess baseline cognitive function on the day before surgery. Follow-up assessments were conducted once between postoperative days 5 and 9 before hospital discharge and once again at 3 to 6 months after surgery during scheduled postoperative follow-up visits at the hospital. All assessments were performed in person by trained research assistants who were blinded to group allocation.

Measurement of serum biomarker levels

Blood samples were collected from participants at 5 different time points: baseline on the day before surgery (T0), immediately after surgery (T1), and on postoperative days 1 (T2), 2 (T3), and 3 (T4). Plasma levels of interleukin (IL)-1β, IL-6, IL-10, tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), neuron-specific enolase (NSE), and S100 calcium-binding protein B (S100B) were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits from R&D Systems™ (MN, USA) for IL-1β, IL-10, TNF-α, NSE, and S100B, and from ImmunoTools (Germany) for IL-6.

Sample size calculation

Previous studies report PND incidence of approximately 29% following major urological surgery [46, 47]. Reported reductions in PND incidence with continuous intravenous propofol compared with inhalational anesthesia range from 40% to 80% [19, 48–50].

The sample size was calculated using the formula for comparing 2 independent population proportions. Parameters included estimated PND incidence of 0.14 (propofol, p₁) versus 0.29 (desflurane, p₂), α of 0.05, β of 0.20, power of 0.80, and 1:1 allocation. The required sample size was approximately 130 participants per group; allowing for 10% dropout, this was adjusted to 143 per group (286 total).

Interim analysis

An interim analysis was planned using the O’Brien–Fleming method within a 2-stage group sequential design [51, 52]. The fixed sample size formula was applied:

with p1 of 0.14, p2 of 0.29, α of 0.05, β of 0.20, and 2 planned analyses (K = 2). The adjusted critical value for the rejection boundary at interim analysis (RB(K, α, β) = RB(2, 0.05, 0.2)) was 1.008. Based on these parameters, required sample size was approximately 115 participants; after accounting for 10% dropout, this increased to 130 total (65 per group).

Statistical analysis

Perioperative demographic data, including baseline characteristics and clinical outcomes, were summarized using descriptive statistics. Categorical variables were compared using the χ² test or Fisher exact test and are presented as frequencies and percentages. Continuous data were assessed for normality using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov or Shapiro–Wilk test. Given the distribution, continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test and presented as median with interquartile range.

Due to repeated biomarker measurements obtained at multiple time points (T0 to T5) and the presence of missing data resulting from values below the ELISA detection limit, generalized estimating equations (GEE) with an exchangeable correlation structure were used for the analyses. The independent variables included treatment group, time, the treatment-by- time interaction, and baseline outcome values. When significant differences across time points within the same group were identified, pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction for multiple testing.

All tests were 2-tailed, and P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A preplanned interim futility analysis was incorporated into the study design because the rigorous anesthetic protocol included bispectral index and near-infrared spectroscopy monitoring to optimize anesthetic depth and cerebral oxygenation. After enrollment of the prespecified interim sample size, the observed incidences of PND were lower than anticipated in the original sample size calculation. Accordingly, participant recruitment was terminated according to the predefined futility criteria. A total of 130 patients were enrolled and randomly assigned to the propofol or desflurane group. Some patients were withdrawn from the final analysis because complete postoperative outcome assessment, including PND evaluation, could not be performed. The reasons for exclusion included conversion to less invasive procedures, postoperative COVID 19 infection, and early discharge. Consequently, 64 patients in the propofol group and 62 patients in the desflurane group were included in the final analysis, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

Baseline characteristics

Preoperative characteristics were similar between the propofol and desflurane groups. Median age was 70 years, with most participants being male and classified as American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status 2. Underlying disease profiles were comparable, except that chronic kidney disease was more prevalent in the propofol group (P = 0.019). Preoperative hemoglobin, hematocrit, and serum electrolyte levels did not differ between groups. Preoperative EQ-5D-5L, BADL, IADL, and Modified IQCODE scores were also comparable. No cases of preoperative delirium, as determined by 3D-CAM, were identified in either group. Baseline MoCA scores were similar, with most patients exhibiting cognitive impairment (MoCA score ≤ 24; Table 1).

Intraoperative parameters

Surgical procedures were similarly distributed between groups, with more than 90% performed laparoscopically. Operative and anesthesia times were comparable. Intraoperative parameters—including fluid intake, analgesic consumption, urine output, estimated blood loss, hypothermia and hypotension episodes, and blood transfusion requirements—did not differ between groups.

Cerebral oxygenation assessed by near-infrared spectroscopy was lower in the propofol group for both the left hemisphere (65.1% [57.8%–69.6%] vs. 69.8% [66.5%–75.4%]; P = 0.001) and right hemisphere (63.6% [57.6%–68.5%] vs. 70.9% [65.4%–75.6%]; P < 0.001). Total propofol consumption was greater in the propofol group, where it was used for maintenance, than in the desflurane group, where it was administered only during induction (1494 [1180–2057.5] vs. 135 [110–150] mg; P < 0.001). Recovery time from discontinuation of the anesthetic agent (propofol or desflurane) to extubation after recovery of consciousness and spontaneous ventilation was longer in the propofol group, although the difference was not statistically significant (15 [10–15] vs. 10 [10–15] min; P = 0.370; Table 2).

Postoperative clinical outcomes

Postoperative pain scores upon arrival at the postanesthesia care unit were higher in the desflurane group (numerical rating scale, 6 [2–7] vs. 7 [6–8]; P = 0.027); however, pain scores at ward discharge were similar. The incidence of POD on days 1, 2, and 3, as well as overall incidence across all 3 days, was comparable between groups. The duration of delirium episodes was shorter in the propofol group (1 [1–1] vs. 1.5 [1–2] days; P = 0.044).

EQ-5D-5L assessments at days 5 to 9 and 3 to 6 months did not differ between groups, nor did BADL and IADL scores at 3 to 6 months or MoCA scores at either time point. The incidences of dNCR and postoperative NCD, defined as a decline of ≥ 2 points from the baseline MoCA score, were similar at both postoperative assessments. The proportions of patients with MoCA score declines of less than 2 points, unchanged scores, or improved scores were also comparable. Hospital length of stay did not differ between groups (Table 3).

Serum biomarker level analysis

The longitudinal changes in serum biomarkers were analyzed using GEE. For IL-6, there was no significant group effect (P = 0.748), whereas a significant effect of time was observed (P < 0.001). In the desflurane group, IL-6 levels differed significantly between preoperative (T0) and postoperative days 1 (T2) and 2 (T3) (P = 0.001 and P = 0.002, respectively), while in the propofol group a significant difference was observed between T0 and T3 (P = 0.024).

No significant group or time effects were found for IL-1β (P = 0.515 and P = 0.129, respectively), IL-10 (P = 0.193 and P = 0.219, respectively), or TNF-α (P = 0.078 and P = 0.159, respectively). For NSE, there was no significant group effect (P = 0.997), although a significant effect of time was detected (P = 0.001). Pairwise comparisons showed a significant difference only in the desflurane group between T0 and T3 (P = 0.006). For S100B, neither group nor time effects reached statistical significance (P = 0.896 and P = 0.096, respectively) (Fig. 2).

When IL-6 levels were stratified by POD status, concentrations were higher in patients with POD than in those without on postoperative day 3 in the propofol group, but not at other time points. Discussion

PND has been associated with prolonged hospital stays, increased caregiver burden, and elevated mortality risk, particularly in older adults [53]. The proposed pathophysiological mechanism centers on neuroinflammation triggered by surgical trauma and anesthesia, which promotes systemic release of inflammatory mediators. These mediators may compromise blood–brain barrier integrity, activating astrocytes and microglia, triggering neuroinflammatory cascades, and ultimately causing cognitive dysfunction [29, 54].

The role of anesthesia in PND development remains controversial. Several meta-analyses have compared PND risk between total intravenous anesthesia with propofol and inhalational agents, particularly sevoflurane and isoflurane, with mixed findings. Nonetheless, some evidence suggests that propofol may confer protection against PND compared with these agents [24, 55]. Desflurane, a volatile anesthetic known for rapid induction and emergence because of its low blood–gas partition coefficient, has gained popularity in clinical practice [22]. Despite these pharmacokinetic advantages, the effect of desflurane on PND remains unclear, especially in major noncardiac surgery.

Previous studies comparing propofol and desflurane have yielded inconsistent results. A total knee arthroplasty study reported no difference in POD incidence [50], whereas a coronary artery bypass grafting study found a higher incidence of dNCR, previously termed POCD, on postoperative days 3 to 7 with propofol than with desflurane [56]. A more recent laparoscopic cholecystectomy study showed no difference in dNCR incidence between anesthetic groups [57]. Conversely, a study of cerebral aneurysm clipping reported higher dNCR incidence with desflurane, although this difference was not statistically significant [58]. Another study reported a 1.8-fold higher incidence of POD in patients receiving desflurane compared with propofol during general anesthesia [59]. In a Thai neurosurgical population, cognitive impairment occurred as early as 1 h postoperatively and was comparable between the propofol and desflurane groups [60].

Several studies have reported associations between anesthesia and inflammatory biomarker expression. Isoflurane induces IL-6 release [61], whereas sevoflurane reduces production of IL-6, IL-10, and TNF-α [62, 63]. Desflurane has been linked to increased plasma IL-10 concentrations [64]. A recent meta-analysis demonstrated correlations between perioperative inflammatory cytokine levels and PND, including IL-1β, IL-6, IL-10, TNF-α, NSE, and S100B [65, 66]. Our study therefore evaluated whether desflurane compared with propofol could reduce PND risk and examined the relationship between anesthetic type and perioperative serum biomarker expression. We focused on major urological surgery, a subset of noncardiac procedures with notably high PND incidence [46, 47, 67, 68].

Our findings showed comparable incidences of POD, dNCR, and postoperative NCD between the groups. Although delirium duration was longer in the desflurane group, this exploratory finding should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of delirium cases and the non-significant difference in POD incidence. Despite lower intraoperative NIRS values in the propofol group, neurocognitive outcomes remained similar, possibly reflecting the cerebral metabolic suppressive effects of propofol, which reduce cerebral metabolic rate and oxygen consumption and may preserve cognitive function despite lower cerebral oxygen saturation. Pain scores upon arrival at the postanesthesia care unit were also higher in the desflurane group, supporting evidence that continuous intravenous propofol may improve postoperative pain control [69, 70].

Contrary to previous reports [58], postoperative MoCA domain scores did not differ between the 2 anesthetic groups. Consistent with earlier studies, however, both groups demonstrated improved delayed recall performance postoperatively, possibly reflecting a practice effect rather than true cognitive improvement [71, 72]. The relatively low incidences of POD, dNCR, and postoperative NCD may be related to the use of intraoperative bispectral index and near-infrared spectroscopy monitoring, as well as strict protocols to avoid hypotension and oxygen desaturation. Although a high prevalence of baseline cognitive impairment (MoCA score ≤ 24) was observed, no patients had preoperative delirium, and baseline cognitive impairment was not associated with POD or dNCR assessed within 72 h and 5–9 days postoperatively, respectively [72].

Although various biomarkers have been proposed for monitoring PND trajectories, only IL-6 showed a statistically significant change in our study. This was observed on postoperative day 3 between patients with and without POD in the propofol group, suggesting potential utility of IL-6 in POD monitoring. However, despite serial measurements, IL-6 is a systemic inflammatory mediator influenced by surgical and physiological stress [73], and POD is a multifactorial condition not solely driven by inflammatory pathways. These factors may explain the lack of consistent association observed. Therefore, the prognostic and predictive value of IL-6 remains inconclusive and warrants further investigation with longer follow-up.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the final sample size was smaller than initially planned following the prespecified interim analysis using the O’Brien–Fleming approach. The observed incidence of PND was substantially lower than anticipated, suggesting a low likelihood that continued enrollment would demonstrate a statistically significant between group difference. Consequently, the study may have been underpowered, and the negative findings should be interpreted cautiously because of the potential for type II error. Although the group comparisons were prespecified, the findings should therefore be considered exploratory rather than definitive. In addition, the limited available data regarding PND after major urological surgery, together with the low incidence of PND observed in our cohort, potentially related to strict intraoperative control of anesthetic depth and hemodynamic parameters, supported the decision to perform an interim futility analysis to ensure efficient use of time and resources, resulting in early termination of recruitment. Second, the primary analysis followed a modified intention-to-treat approach. Several randomized patients were withdrawn after allocation because of procedural conversion to a less invasive procedure, early discharge, or incomplete cognitive assessment, which precluded reliable evaluation of the predefined endpoints. Consequently, post-randomization withdrawal may have introduced attrition bias and affected the internal validity of the study. In addition, because of the limited sample size and incomplete postoperative cognitive outcome data among withdrawn participants, formal sensitivity analyses were not feasible. Third, most participants were male. However, previous studies have generally reported no significant sex differences in postoperative cognitive outcomes [74], whereas findings regarding POD incidence remain inconsistent [75, 76]. Forth, the higher prevalence of chronic kidney disease in the propofol group resulted in baseline imbalance between groups. Nevertheless, previous evidence suggests that renal impairment does not substantially affect the pharmacokinetics or clearance of propofol or volatile anesthetics [77–80], making a clinically meaningful effect on PND through altered anesthetic metabolism unlikely. However, residual confounding related to this baseline imbalance cannot be entirely excluded. Given the modest sample size and limited number of outcome events, multivariable regression analysis was not performed, as the study may have been insufficiently powered for reliable adjusted modeling. Finally, additional limitations should be considered. POD was assessed only on postoperative days 1–3, later-onset cases may have been missed, potentially underestimating the overall incidence. This potential underestimation may have reduced the ability to detect differences between groups. Postoperative cognitive decline was assessed using the MoCA rather than a comprehensive neuropsychological battery. Therefore, subtle or domain specific cognitive changes may have been missed, limiting direct comparison with studies using more extensive neuropsychological assessments. Repeated MoCA assessments without adjustment for practice effects or inclusion of a non-surgical control group may have further limited the precision of postoperative cognitive outcome assessment and reduced the ability to distinguish true postoperative cognitive decline from normal test-retest variation and learning effects.

Based on our findings, no differences were observed between the propofol and desflurane groups regarding postoperative cognitive outcomes or other adverse events. Whether desflurane is optimal for anesthetic maintenance remains debatable despite its advantages of rapid induction and emergence. The American Society of Anesthesiologists has recommended against desflurane use because of its high global warming potential, which exceeds that of other commonly used volatile agents [81]. This recommendation remains controversial, however, as inhaled anesthetics are estimated to contribute less than 0.1% to total global greenhouse gas emissions.

Desflurane is present in the atmosphere at an extremely low concentration (approximately 0.37 parts per trillion), substantially lower than carbon dioxide. Desflurane also has a much shorter atmospheric half-life than carbon dioxide, suggesting that its overall contribution to global warming may be less substantial than commonly assumed [82]. In this study, low-flow anesthesia was used with total air and oxygen flow of 0.6 to 0.8 L/min to minimize anesthetic gas consumption and reduce environmental release.

Despite some limitations, our study has several important strengths. Although only approximately 44% of the initially calculated sample size was enrolled, the number of participants exceeded that of many previous studies comparing propofol and desflurane [50, 56–58]. The study was conducted by a multidisciplinary team comprising anesthesiologists, urological surgeons, geriatricians, nurses, and research staff. Use of a standardized protocol and systematic data collection contributed to the consistency and reliability of results.

Another key strength was the incorporation of bispectral index and near-infrared spectroscopy monitoring during anesthesia, which minimized potential confounding effects of excessive anesthetic depth and cerebral desaturation on postoperative cognitive impairment. These tools likely contributed to the relatively low PND incidence observed. This finding is consistent with previous work from our group, which reported higher PND rates in the absence of such monitoring [72, 83–85]. Future research should prioritize anesthetic depth and cerebral oxygenation monitoring, particularly in high-risk patients, to maintain hemodynamic stability and enhance safety. Long-term follow-up with clinical outcome assessment and serial serum biomarker measurement is also needed to clarify the role of inflammatory markers in PND development and progression.

Conclusions

PND is an adverse outcome predominantly affecting older adults following surgery. This study found no significant differences between desflurane and propofol in PND incidence or related outcomes. Although delirium duration was longer in the desflurane group, this exploratory finding should be interpreted cautiously given the small number of delirium cases. In addition, anesthesia guided by bispectral index and near-infrared spectroscopy monitoring may help reduce PND incidence. Further studies are needed to clarify the neurocognitive effects of different anesthetic agents in older surgical patients.
