Mesh-represented and learning-empowered hologram synthesis for full 3D holographic displays
Authors: Xiangyu Meng, Wenbin Zhou, Yifan Peng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Holographic displays are a transforming technology for immersive virtual and augmented reality systems. Exploring accurate yet efficient computer-generated holography (CGH) algorithms for three-dimensional (3D) content is a valuable research field. Recent advancements in layer-based CGH may exhibit limited capacity to convey comprehensive 3D information in accurately representing tilted angular spectrum and realizing realistic defocus blur. Alternative approaches based on point clouds and light fields may demand significant computational resources for preparing adequate target data for optimization. In addition, most existing CGH algorithms rely on heuristics to encode complex amplitudes into phase-only holograms for display, which can be highly ill-posed. Here we investigate an innovative CGH framework that overcomes these challenges using a unique combination of mesh-based representation, tilt-angle tailored wave propagation modeling, and complex-valued optimization, alongside a learning-empowered display calibration scheme using camera feedback. The resulting expanded hologram encoding capabilities enable the delivery of natural 3D depth cues, including smooth defocus blur and view-dependent effects. Experimental results conducted on our holographic near-eye display prototype demonstrate unprecedented full 3D visual quality, representing a significant advancement in creating immersive visualization experiences.

Introduction

Holography, offering immersive visual experience with rich depth cues, represents a transformative technology for near-eye displays (NEDs) toward a wide range of applications, spanning from entertainment and education to remote operation assistance and mixed reality telepresence1–3. One significant challenge associated with off-the-shelf NEDs is the essential issue of vergence-accommodation conflict (VAC)4, which can lead to visual fatigue and various side effects due to the restriction of having only one single focus depth available. In this context, holography stands out for its capability to encode and reconstruct the complete wave field information, aka., full 3D information. Advancements in digital computers and spatial light modulators (SLMs) over the past decade have facilitated the development of full-color computer-generated holographic (CGH) displays5–8. Various algorithms have been proposed to tackle the two primary challenges in CGH: numerical wave field diffraction representation and hologram encoding.

Classical algorithms for modeling numerical diffraction to simulate wavefront propagation within 3D space commonly rely on the Fresnel integral and Fourier transform9. These algorithms are adapted to different representations of 3D models (refer to Fig. 1b for visualization). Point cloud data, which is straightforward, simple, and widely favored in CGH, suffers from an inherent trade-off between computational efficiency and accuracy10,11. On the other hand, RGB-D representations using the angular spectrum method (ASM)12 as the propagation operator can be seamlessly extended from 2D images to 3D scenes13. However, they encounter challenges such as depth quantization and are limited to a single viewpoint. In contrast, light field supports multi-view hologram synthesis by employing the Short-Time Fourier Transform (STFT)14,15, albeit at the cost of a trade-off between spatial and angular resolutions. Besides these representations, meshes are extensively employed in various 3D applications due to their detailed geometric description of scenes crafted by artists. Wave fields rendered from meshes typically exhibit fewer speckle artifacts and discontinuities compared to point clouds or RGB-D models. Phase-only SLMs are the dominating wave modulation platform in holographic displays, delivering stable performance, improved light efficiency, and lightweight design towards dynamic wearable displays21,22. Within this context, the primary challenge of hologram encoding lies in solving the ill-posed inverse problem of generating a phase-only hologram (POH) that faithfully reconstructs the desired wave field. Double-phase amplitude coding (DPAC)23 represents a direct encoding method that is computationally efficient, but suffers from reduced light efficiency and high-order artifacts24. In contrast, iterative approaches commonly employ a variety of non-convex optimization strategies25, including phase retrieval techniques26,27 or gradient-based optimization methods 28,29. These iterative approaches typically yield superior phase-only solutions compared to direct methods but rely heavily on the fidelity of the wave propagation model. Besides the widely adopted ASM, advanced modeling techniques like high-order diffraction simulation24 and camera-calibrated propagation, either online or offline13,29,30, have been explored to faithfully represent the physical setup. However, most optimization methods focus solely on constraining the target amplitude11,29, as human vision primarily perceives intensity information. Notably, while this strategy enhances the reconstruction quality on supervised planes, it fundamentally compromises 3D visual cues such as defocus blur and continuous depth cues.

In this work, we investigate a set of innovative techniques aimed at tackling the aforementioned challenges, as shown in Fig. 1. Our methods encompass a designed perspective-to-orthogonal smooth wave field rendering pipeline (POS-WRP), which involves encoding mesh scenes into wave fields with fine-grained textures, photo-realistic shadings, and perspective depth cues. In addition, we introduce the phase-aware gradient descent (PAGD) algorithm, which is an iterative approach for computing physically accurate 3D holograms with complex-valued wave fields-aware optimization, supporting continuous depth perception. Moreover, we unveil an enhanced Flying Planes mesh-wave field-hologram dataset, tailored for true 3D holographic display calibration, effectively bridging the disparity in wavefront propagation between simulation and physical experiments. These elements are co-optimized via a learning-based calibration strategy with camera feedback. We demonstrate, via numerical simulations and physical holographic display prototypes, that the proposed mesh-represented, learning-empowered CGH framework delivers state-of-the-art performance in reconstructing holographic images with continuous depth cues and view-dependent effects.

Results

Rendering wave field of mesh-represented scenes

Our 3D mesh-based holographic display framework operates through a two-stage process. Initially, the input mesh scene undergoes rendering into a wave field on the hologram plane, which is subsequently followed by an optimization to convert the wave field into a phase-only hologram. The proposed perspective-to-orthogonal wave field rendering pipeline (POS-WRP) outlined in this subsection describes the methodology for encoding meshes with realistic shadings into the wave field, utilizing a robust rendering scheme, simultaneously conducting wave propagation of the facets in parallel.

With spectral resampling and ASM (refer to “Methods" for details), each tilted facet of a mesh scene can be propagated to the hologram plane, thereby creating a wave field representation of the entire scene. However, relying solely on the vanilla propagation presents challenges in maintaining consistent illumination, shading, occlusion, and, particularly, perspective depth cues. This issue arises because the wave field modulated by the SLM propagates solely within the paraxial region in an orthogonal manner (refer to Fig. 2). To overcome these limitations, we introduce an innovative wave field rendering pipeline that incorporates the aforementioned factors into a projection-corrected mesh and textures. Specifically, this approach involves applying a perspective-to-orthogonal transformation to both mesh coordinates and baked textures to retain the perspective cues. Texture baking31 involves encoding shading information from a scene into a texture for subsequent rendering. In this study, as shown in Fig. 2a, the input 3D scene comprises meshes and lighting data captured through a perspective camera. We employ physically based rendering (PBR) pipelines32 to bake the rendering outcomes onto a predefined output texture, yielding a texture containing the physical illumination of a scene (see Fig. 2e, left), which subsequently serves as the amplitude of the wave field across the mesh facets. Occlusion handling is naturally integrated during baking, where any obstructed portion of a facet is rendered black, indicating zero amplitude. It is worth noting that the occlusion modeling in PBR adheres to the principles of geometric optics32. While diffraction at occlusion edges is not explicitly accounted for, the facet size and spatial sampling rate render the visual loss negligible. This assumption enhances computational efficiency compared to a wave-based masking approach during wave propagation19,33.

For maintaining consistency in rendering results under orthogonal projection during wave field propagation from the SLM, where the projection space is orthogonal and differs from the perspective projection in the scene space, we transform both the original mesh and the baked texture into a projection-corrected form, as shown in Fig. 2b. This alignment is crucial for retaining the perspective depth cues in the final rendered output. With the projection-corrected meshes and textures, each visible facet can be represented as a plane wave field, where the position is determined by vertex coordinates and the amplitude by textures, accounting for occlusion effects. Assuming an initial phase of zero before tilted propagation, each plane wave is then rotated and propagated to the hologram plane, as described in Section “Methods". On the hologram plane, the phase values of the facet wave fields are aggregated into a histogram with a shift to center the distribution peak. This strategy facilitates the generation of a relatively smooth target phase (see Fig. 2d), which helps mitigate artifacts in neighboring regions of adjacent facets stemming from hardware constraints such as SLM crosstalk.

Phase-only hologram synthesis

The PAGD algorithm is proposed to encode the complex wave field obtained by POS-WRP into phase-only holograms for 3D holography with continuous depth cues. A camera-calibrated, learned model is employed to enhance the precision of PAGD, with the goal of reconstructing a 3D wave field with accurate phase information.

Amplitude-based optimization encounters three primary limitations. First, the number and positions of target planes are typically set manually, leading to a trade-off between computational complexity and the continuity of depth cues. For example, Fig. 3 demonstrates that phase information can help reduce the number of required supervised planes while maintaining equivalent imaging quality. Second, amplitude-based multi-layer representations are constrained to encoding scenes from a single viewpoint, whereas complex fields can capture directional light field information at each pixel in 3D space14. While phase carries important information for the target wave field, supervising it presents challenges due to the non-uniqueness of phase distribution for a given target. We note that, for any target wave field derived from meshes, there are two inherent shift invariances that must be considered. Firstly, any global shift in the phase of the entire field does not alter the wave field. Secondly, the phase value on each pixel can be shifted by 2π without changing the target field. Consequently, the target field derived from the mesh represents one of the feasible solutions subject to these two shift-invariances. The proposed solution to address this challenge involves introducing a complex-valued scale factor.

Box 1 outlines the proposed optimization pipeline that transforms the rendered wave field into a phase-only hologram. This process begins with the input mesh scene and the specified illumination and camera settings, which are then processed with the POS-WRP to generate the target wave field.

Subsequently, as illustrated in Fig. 5a, the rendered wave field is propagated to the near, medium, and far planes. During each iteration, the phase is randomly initialized and propagated to the same target planes using either the vanilla ASM or the camera-calibrated wave propagation model. We note that numerical models like ASM are proficient in simulations but struggle to replicate real-world optical systems due to imperfections such as aberrations, SLM non-linearities, stray light, and limited diffraction efficiency. To capture a more precise representation of the system’s behavior, we curate a Flying Planes hologram dataset to train the learned wavefront propagation model introduced by Choi et al.13, resulting in the PSNR improvement of 2 dB compared to the analytical model.

To facilitate training the camera-calibrated wave propagation model, we introduce a large-scale mesh-wave field-hologram dataset, the Flying Planes dataset, which contains 1000 randomly generated 3D scenes in OBJ format with corresponding complex-valued wave fields, RGB-Depth images, and phase-only holograms, as shown in Fig. 5b, c). The dataset is created by randomly sampling the position, rotation, and scale of rectangular primitives with random textures from the DIV2K dataset35. The complex-valued wave field is generated using the proposed POS-WRP, and the phases are synthesized with various phase encoders, including DPAC, SGD, and the proposed PAGD with ASM as the propagation operator. The experimental results on our benchtop holographic display prototype for four 3D scenes compared to baselines are shown in Fig. 6a, namely double-phase amplitude coding (DPAC), PAGD with ASM (ASM-PAGD), and RGB-D focal stacks with a camera-calibrated, learned model. Full-color images are post-processed after capturing. We observe that the DPAC provides poor image quality, as the resulting checkerboard pattern produces multiple replicas of the image. Although this aliasing caused by high-order diffractions can be mitigated by adding optical filters11, such an approach would increase the system’s complexity and introduce blurring to the reconstructed images. The ASM-PAGD utilizes the vanilla ASM as the propagation operator, resulting in aberrations and artifacts such as stripes, circles, and ringings in captured images. Inaccuracies within the propagation operator contribute to diminished image contrast. The RGB-D method with focal stacks achieves favorable image quality at supervised planes but exhibits distortion at unsupervised planes. The resulting defocus blur appears unnatural, accompanied by speckles in the out-of-focus regions. In contrast, our proposed method mitigates artifacts through a forward model, delivering crisp images with enhanced contrast. Herein, the camera focuses on three planes ranging from near to far. Although the RGB-D method excels in producing sharp images in in-focus regions, it unnaturally blurs out-of-focus regions. The focal stacks tend to blur out-of-focus regions with random noise, introducing artificial speckles. At the unsupervised plane, the out-of-focus regions of RGB-D exhibit unnatural blur, while our method offers smoothly varying blur. Abandoning the phase supervision introduces ringing artifacts; for instance, the basketball in the RGB-D method, displays edge ringing, a phenomenon mitigated by our method through employing a phase-aware optimizer. In addition, our approach prevents the unnatural spreading of out-of-focus blurring, as evidenced by the shaded areas (Fig. 6b). The RGB-D focal stacks disperse the energy from out-of-focus regions into shadow regions, thereby compromising image contrast.

View-dependent holography is a direct outcome of the proposed PAGD method. Leveraging the forward model’s capability to reconstruct the phase of the wave field, it effectively encodes the propagation characteristics of the target wave field into the phase via blazed gratings. Unlike existing 2.5D holography using RGB-D representations, our approach embeds depth information into the third “dimension”, phase. In contrast to the OLAS method15, our method excels in modeling aberrations through the forward model, resulting in higher image quality. Furthermore, in comparison to alternative end-to-end optimization strategies for view-dependent holography37, our proposed method offers distinct advantages. It requires training the forward model only once, eliminating the need for specific data per viewpoint during training. Additionally, our method does not necessitate precise modeling of the filter to replicate the physical characteristics of an iris.

By reconstructing the wave field’s phase, our method facilitates 3D imaging with continuous depth cues across multiple viewpoints. Specifically, the left side of Fig. 7c, a 3D scene is depicted featuring two objects situated at distinct depths. We observe that the object occlusion alters with varying viewpoints. Meanwhile, the right side of Fig. 7c exhibits a 3D outdoor scene encompassing a detailed background, illustrating our method’s ability to support depth cues and detailed texture variations across viewpoints.

Discussion

The presented POS-WRP method can facilitate rendering complex-valued wave fields from mesh-represented 3D scenes containing fine-grained textures. This scheme delivers high-fidelity wave field data with continuous depth cues and reduced speckle artifacts. Subsequently, the wave fields are encoded into phase-only holograms using the proposed PAGD optimizer for 3D holographic displays.

Recent studies on end-to-end holographic display frameworks29 have demonstrated enhanced display quality by integrating tailored wave propagation with physical display systems. However, this tightly coupled approach may result in reduced robustness, necessitating extensive manual fine-tuning for optimal holographic reconstruction. The interdependent nature of end-to-end frameworks could limit the model’s scalability and interpretability. To the best of our knowledge, although phases are intensively investigated to encode and represent 3D information in classic holography38, serving as a vital link between recording and reconstruction stages for depth information transmission, their significance appears somewhat overlooked in end-to-end designed holographic displays.

Unlike amplitude-only end-to-end optimization techniques, our approach adheres to the well-established physical two-step hologram process involving wave field recording and holographic reconstruction. These stages are interconnected through phase information, with the recording and reconstruction processes digitized using the suggested POS-WRP and PAGD methods, respectively. This decoupling could potentially streamline the CGH process by reducing ambiguities, as in many cases, end-to-end approaches may struggle to faithfully capture systematic errors of all kinds using implicit models. In addition, this method enhances the system’s extensibility and adaptability. For instance, the wave field of 3D scenes can be modeled using recently proposed implicit representations such as NeRF or 3D Gaussian splatting, while the diffraction of the reconstructed wave field from the SLM plane can be modeled using focal stacks or light fields.

The captured image quality is inherently limited by the capabilities of the hardware utilized. Primarily, the limited space-bandwidth product of state-of-the-art SLMs restricts the hologram encoding capacity, allowing for only very narrow tilted angles necessary for view-dependent display, as discussed in Section “Methods". Besides, the feature size of the sensor employed imposes limitations on resolving desired high-frequency information during image acquisition. Future endeavors could involve leveraging advanced industrial sensors and SLMs equipped with increased pixel counts and smaller feature sizes. These enhancements would facilitate precise high-frequency-supported wave propagation, allowing for improved calibration and enabling holographic reconstruction with larger tilted angles that are sensitive to high frequencies.

The proposed PAGD method can further be enhanced through the integration of time-multiplexed techniques39, primarily aimed at reducing sparkle artifacts and achieving natural defocus blur with reduced depth of field. Simulation results are provided in the Supplementary Section “Shallow Depth-of-Field Holography". In this investigation, we employed the commercially available HOLOEYE Pluto-2 SLM for wavefront modulation. Due to constraints in hardware synchronization and the restricted refresh rate (60 Hz) of the device, implementing a time-multiplexing display mode on our prototype is deemed impractical and falls beyond the main scope of our CGH framework. The full-color captured results presented were synthesized via post-processing, aligning with current practices in this research field. This limitation could potentially be overcome by utilizing the latest SLM models, such as HOLOEYE Leto-3, which offer enhanced hardware synchronization capabilities and a higher refresh rate (180 Hz), potentially facilitating real-time full-color holographic display at optimal frame rates.

The proposed mesh-based CGH approach leverages the widespread adoption of mesh-based representations in modern computer graphics32 and the availability of abundant 3D data. Furthermore, real-time rendering pipelines31 offer hardware and software acceleration for efficient mesh rendering. Although GPU parallelism has been utilized to enhance efficiency, achieving real-time hologram generation remains a goal for future exploration. Specifically, the time for generating one phase-only hologram from a mesh scene is on average 54.96 s (24.96 s for POS-WRP and 30 s for PAGD). The detailed quantitative results and comparisons are listed in Supplementary Section “Quantitative comparison between PAGD and Amplitude SGD" and Section “POS-WRP Runtime". Recent advancements in deep learning have shown promising results in accelerating CGH algorithms and enabling real-time hologram synthesis29. We envision the proposed PAGD method for phase-only hologram synthesis could be further expedited by applying convolutional neural networks (CNNs) similar to those presented in recent studies40–42.

While recent research on mesh-based holography has successfully integrated view-dependent lighting with fine specular and diffuse reflective details43,44, there is a lack of support for dynamic holographic displays utilizing SLMs. Other mesh-based holographic displays often encounter challenges of distorted image quality and restricted FoV20,45. Our CGH framework bridges the gap by connecting the graphics pipeline, mesh-based CGH, and holographic displays, effectively enabling the faithful representation of high-fidelity mesh content on holographic displays with enhanced depth perception.

Implementation details

Software implementation

The initial scene containing meshes and textures utilized in this study was created using Blender48. The POS-WRP method utilizes OpenGL in conjunction with customized vertex and fragment shaders to produce perspective-correct meshes and remap textures. Spectral remapping is accomplished through grid interpolation in PyTorch. To tackle anti-aliasing, the wave field is magnified by a factor of two in both dimensions, and a fill factor of 0.93 is applied.

To train the camera-calibrated wave propagation model, a total of 5860 phases are captured. These phases encompass 2D phases generated via stochastic gradient descent, higher-order gradient descent, and the double-phase amplitude encoding technique, as well as true 3D phases derived from the proposed Flying Planes mesh-wave field-hologram dataset. Each phase is recorded at eight target planes, positioned at distances of 10.00, 10.07, 10.14, 10.21, 10.28, 10.35, 10.42, and 10.49 cm from the SLM. The region of interest (ROI) is defined as 880 × 1600 due to the laser’s uniformity and the modulator’s efficiency. The model is trained on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU with a batch size of one for twenty epochs, utilizing the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.0005.

Hardware setup

A benchtop holographic display prototype is assembled to validate the proposed methodology, as depicted in Fig. 7b. Holograms are modulated using a HOLOEYE Pluto-2 phase-only SLM, with a resolution of 1920 × 1080 and a pixel pitch of 8μm. Illumination is provided by an RGB single-mode fiber-coupled laser (Fisba ReadyBeam) with principal wavelengths of 639.0, 524.9, and 445.8 nm, respectively. The laser beam traverses a collimating lens, a neutral density filter, a linear polarizer, and a beam splitter. The collimated, uniform beam, with the required polarization for the SLM, is incident perpendicularly onto the SLM. Subsequently, the modulated waves pass through an eyepiece and a camera lens before being recorded by a FLIR Grasshopper 3 sensor with a resolution of 1920 × 1200 in the RAW16 format. The eyepiece is a Nikon 50 mm SLR lens, while the camera lens is a Canon 35 mm SLR lens, with its focus distance adjusted using an Arduino microcontroller. In this setup, the wave propagation model is trained without the inclusion of an optical filter. However, to capture view-dependent holograms, an optical filter, specifically a pinhole, is positioned at the Fourier plane in front of the camera lens to mimic the pupil.

Prior to image acquisition, calibration procedures are executed for both the SLM and the camera. The SLM’s voltage response is calibrated using vortex patterns, with manual fine-tuning carried out through the calibration software supplied by the manufacturer. The camera is configured to focus on eight target planes, and depth calibration is conducted by displaying a set of ten holograms at the respective depths to determine the necessary offsets for precise focusing. To minimize jitter in the captured images, a homography-based affine transformation is applied49, ensuring dataset stability through image alignment.
