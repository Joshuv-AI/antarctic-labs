An order-reversing embedding of Turing degrees into Arthur–Nimue–Merlin degrees
Jean Abou Samra * & David Alexander Madore

Abstract
The Arthur–Nimue–Merlin degrees are a generalization of the Turing degrees introduced by Kihara as a tangible description of the partially ordered set of Lawvere–Tierney topologies on the effective topos (equivalently, subtoposes of the effective topos). They are defined in terms of a three-player game that introduces both angelic and demonic non-determinism into oracle queries. We construct an order embedding of the Turing degrees with their order reversed into the Arthur–Nimue–Merlin degrees, whose image we call the “co-Turing degrees”; we then study the order relationship of these co-Turing degrees with the (naturally embedded) Turing degrees within the Arthur–Nimue–Merlin degrees.

Introduction

¶
1.1.
We begin with a riddle. King Arthur is
desperate to know whether the Grail is in a particular French castle.
He can ask arbitrary questions to the all-knowing mage Merlin, who
promises to answer truthfully, but with a twist. Instead of merely
answering “yes” or “no” in plain English, the mischievous Merlin
provides a Turing machine, which halts if and only if the answer is
“yes”. Can Arthur, a mere mortal limited by the Church–Turing thesis,
find the Grail’s location before the end of days?

Indeed he can, with a simple trick: first ask Merlin whether the Grail
is in the castle, then ask whether it is
not
in the castle.
Exactly one of the two resulting Turing machines halts, and he can run
them in parallel to know which one, revealing the answer.

Let us spice up the rules. Each of Merlin’s replies now consists of

two
Turing machines; for a “yes” answer, both or none halt,
whereas for a “no” answer, exactly one halts.

Intuitively, the oracles should be understood in the following
way: querying a T0 oracle

“give me any one of the values in

adversarial nondeterminism). And for a T3 oracle

and now please give me any
one of the values in
G

adversarial forms of nondeterminism).

To make these intuitions precise, we introduce the Arthur–Nimue–Merlin game.

The game has three players named Arthur, Nimue and Merlin.
Arthur and Nimue are allied against Merlin (so the two possible
outcomes are that Arthur and Nimue win and Merlin loses,

•

3
Lattice structure on the degrees

In this section, we collect various results, some of which should be
considered as part of the folklore, pertaining to the lattice structure
of the T1–T3 degrees. We state and prove slightly more
than is strictly required for our main results, hoping that it will be
convenient for further study of Arthur–Nimue–Merlin degrees to have a
description of their lattice structure.

The binary join (i.e., sup, least upper bound) operation is
unproblematic: it is represented by the standard construction for the
join of two Turing degrees, which remains applicable at all other
levels: intuitively, having access to the join of two oracles means
that Arthur can freely query either one. This is made precise as
follows:

Proposition 3.1
.

The partially ordered sets
\mathscr{D}_{\mathrm{T0}},\mathscr{D}_{\mathrm{T1}},\mathscr{D}_{\mathrm{T2}},\mathscr{D}_{\mathrm{T3}}

preserve them. In each level, we have
[f]\vee[g]=[f\oplus g]

where
(f\oplus g)(2n):=f(n)
and
(f\oplus g)(2n+1):=g(n)
.

Proof.

We need simply observe that
f\oplus g\mathrel{\preceq_{\mathrm{T3}}}h
iff
f\mathrel{\preceq_{\mathrm{T3}}}h
and
g\mathrel{\preceq_{\mathrm{T3}}}h
(since the
\oplus
construction preserves each
T0–T3 level, if it computes the join at the T3 level, the same holds
for the lower levels). But this is now obvious by observing that the
initial configuration for the reduction game of
f\oplus g
to
h

tells Arthur+Nimue which reduction strategy (
f\mathrel{\preceq_{\mathrm{T3}}}h

or
g\mathrel{\preceq_{\mathrm{T3}}}h
) to apply.

It is also true that binary joins exist in basic T3 degrees and are

\mathbf{c}_{P,Q}={\boldsymbol{\omega}}
.
Now if such were the case, then by
4.17
we would
have
\mathbf{d}_{P\times Q,\,Q\times P}=\mathbf{0}
(in other
words, given four Turing machines of which an odd number halt, we can
compute the parity of how many halt among the first two); but this
allows us to (computably) decide the halting of an arbitrary
e
by decoding

((\mathtt{loop},e),(\mathtt{halt},e))
where
\mathtt{loop}
is a
fixed Turing machine that never halts and
\mathtt{halt}
is one that
halts immediately: indeed, if
e\in K
then this is in
Q\times P

whereas if
e\in\bar{K}
this is in
P\times Q
. But this is
impossible: so in fact
\mathbf{c}_{P,Q}<{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
, solving the riddle.

5
Co-Turing degrees and their order

In the previous section, we have defined “decoding” and “coding”
oracles for a promise problem
(P,Q)
. The relation between the two,
however, is not as straightforward as one might hope (we will remark
in
5.9
below that the
degree
\mathbf{c}_{P,Q}
is not a function of the degree

\mathbf{d}_{P,Q}
). We now turn to a particular case of the

\mathbf{d}_{P,Q}
and
\mathbf{c}_{P,Q}
, which we will denote by

\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{A}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{A}
respectively,
defined by a set
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, which will behave better in
this respect: the degree
\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{A}
will turn out to
be simply the Turing degree of
A
, while
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{A}
will
turn out to depend only on the Turing degree
[A]_{\mathrm{T0}}
of
A
and this will
provide our sought-after “co-Turing” degrees.

Definition 5.1
.

For
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
arbitrary and
i\in\{0,1\}
, we define

H_{i}^{A}:=\{e\mid\varphi_{e}^{A}(0){\downarrow}=i\}
to be the set of
oracle programs
e
which, when provided
A
(that is,
1_{A}
) as
oracle, terminate and return the value
i
. (Note that
H_{0}^{A},H_{1}^{A}

are inhabited and disjoint.)

We also define
\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{A}:=\mathbf{d}_{H_{0}^{A},\,H_{1}^{A}}

and
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{A}:=\mathbf{c}_{H_{0}^{A},\,H_{1}^{A}}
. We will
call
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{A}
the
co-Turing degree
associated
to
A
(we will see in
5.8
that it depends
only on the Turing degree of
A
).

¶
5.2.
Since it is the central contruction of this paper, we spell
out the following explicit description: the
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{A}
oracle is
one which can answer an arbitrary “yes”/“no” question, but, instead of
providing a direct answer
0
or
1
, it (adversarially) encodes its
answer in the form of a program
e
which, when run with
A
as
(ordinary Turing) oracle, terminates and computes the desired
answer
\varphi_{e}^{A}(0)
.

The deeper significance of the
H_{i}^{A}
construction will be
investigated around
7.26

below, but the immediate point is that is it turns a Turing-reduction on the
oracle
A
to a many-to-one reduction on the
H_{i}^{A}
. Indeed, the following
fact is standard (cf.
[
Soare 2016
, theorem 3.4.3(v)]
) and can also be
seen as a special case
of
7.5
below, but let us
spell it out:

Proposition 5.3
.

For
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, if
B
is Turing-reducible to
A
, then

(H_{0}^{B},H_{1}^{B})\mathrel{\preceq_{\mathrm{m}}}(H_{0}^{A},H_{1}^{A})
.

Proof.

Suppose
1_{B}=\varphi_{h}^{A}
witnesses that
B
is Turing-reducible
to
A
. Given
e\in H_{0}^{B}\cup H_{1}^{B}
, we computably convert it to a
program
e^{\prime}
by replacing every call to the oracle (supposed to yield
the characteristic function of
B
) by a call to
h
: then

\varphi_{e}^{B}=\varphi_{e^{\prime}}^{A}
as partial functions, in particular

e^{\prime}\in H_{i}^{A}
if
e\in H_{i}^{B}
, and
e\mapsto e^{\prime}
, which is computable
(even primitive recursive) witnesses the claimed many-to-one reduction.
∎

Proposition 5.4
.

If
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
are such that
B
is Turing-reducible
to
A
, then
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{A}\leq\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{B}
(as
T3 degrees).

Proof.

This follows trivially from

5.3
and
4.12
.
∎

Of course, under the same assumption, we also have

\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{B}\leq\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{A}
by

5.3
and
4.11
,
but in fact we can say more:

Proposition 5.5
.

For
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
arbitrary, the (a priori T1) degree

\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{A}
is the Turing degree
[A]_{\mathrm{T0}}

of
A
.

Proof.

To see that
\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{A}\leq[A]_{\mathrm{T0}}
, we note
that given
e\in H_{0}^{A}\cup H_{1}^{A}
, with the help of
A
(that is,

1_{A}
) as oracle, we can simply compute
\varphi_{e}^{A}
and return its
value, which is exactly that of the required decoding oracle.

Conversely, to see that
[A]_{\mathrm{T0}}\leq\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{A}
,
given
k\in\mathbb{N}
we can compute whether
k\in A
by first computing (by
means of the relative s-m-n theorem) the program
e
that queries
the oracle on the value
k
and then returns its answer, and then
asking the decoding oracle whether this
e
is in
H_{0}^{A}
or
H_{1}^{A}
.
∎

Recall that in the previous section we have seen the importance of
considering
\mathbf{d}_{P\times Q,\,Q\times P}
(and not just

\mathbf{d}_{P,Q}
) to understand
\mathbf{c}_{P,Q}
, and recall that
we have seen that
\mathbf{d}_{P\times Q,\,Q\times P}\leq\mathbf{d}_{P,Q}
holds in general
(
4.13
) but that this
inequality can be strict
(
4.14

or
4.15
). In the present situation, however,
this is an equality (it is also the Turing degree of
A
):

Proposition 5.6
.

For
A\subseteq\mathbb{N}
arbitrary, the degree
\mathbf{d}_{(H_{0}^{A}\times H_{1}^{A}),\;(H_{1}^{A}\times H_{0}^{A})}
equals
\mathbf{d}_{H_{0}^{A},\,H_{1}^{A}}=:\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{A}
.

Proof.

The inequality
\mathbf{d}_{(H_{0}^{A}\times H_{1}^{A}),\;(H_{1}^{A}\times H_{0}^{A})}\leq\mathbf{d}_{H_{0}^{A},\,H_{1}^{A}}
has already been seen in
full generality
in
4.13
. So we need
to see the converse.

But given
e\in H_{i}^{A}
we can computably produce
\mathtt{flip}\circ e\in H_{1-i}^{A}
by simply composing
e
with the function that
exchanges
0
and
1
. To decide by means of
\mathbf{d}_{(H_{0}^{A}\times H_{1}^{A}),\;(H_{1}^{A}\times H_{0}^{A})}
whether
e\in H_{0}^{A}\cup H_{1}^{A}
belongs to
H_{0}^{A}
or
H_{1}^{A}
, we ask whether
(e,\,\mathtt{flip}\circ e)
belongs in
H_{0}^{A}\times H_{1}^{A}
or
H_{1}^{A}\times H_{0}^{A}
. This proves
\mathbf{d}_{H_{0}^{A},\,H_{1}^{A}}\leq\mathbf{d}_{(H_{0}^{A}\times H_{1}^{A}),\;(H_{1}^{A}\times H_{0}^{A})}
(formally,
by applying

4.11
to
e\mapsto(e,\,\mathtt{flip}\circ e)
).
∎

This allows us to obtain the converse
of
5.4
from our main
technical result:

Proposition 5.7
.

If
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
are such that
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{A}\leq\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{B}
(as T3 degrees), then
B
is Turing-reducible
to
A
.

Proof.

By
4.5
, we have

\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{A}\vee\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{A}={\boldsymbol{\omega}}
, so our
assumption yields
\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{A}\vee\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{B}={\boldsymbol{\omega}}
.
Now by
5.5
,

\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{A}
is the Turing degree
\mathbf{a}
of
A
: thus,

\mathbf{a}\vee\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{B}={\boldsymbol{\omega}}
.
By
4.17
, this implies
\mathbf{d}_{(H_{0}^{B}\times H_{1}^{B}),\;(H_{1}^{B}\times H_{0}^{B})}\leq\mathbf{a}
. By

5.6
and

5.5
(again),

\mathbf{d}_{(H_{0}^{B}\times H_{1}^{B}),\;(H_{1}^{B}\times H_{0}^{B})}
is the
Turing degree
\mathbf{b}
of
B
. So we have proved
\mathbf{b}\leq\mathbf{a}
, as claimed.
∎

Putting all this together, we have shown:

Theorem 5.8
.

For
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, we have
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{A}\leq\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{B}
iff
[B]_{\mathrm{T0}}\leq[A]_{\mathrm{T0}}
(equivalently:
\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{B}\leq\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{A}
).

Thus, the mapping
\operatorname{coT}\colon[A]_{\mathrm{T0}}\mapsto\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{A}
is a well-defined injection from

\mathscr{D}_{\mathrm{T0}}^{\operatorname{op}}
(where

(\mathchar 124\relax)^{\operatorname{op}}
denotes the opposite order) to

\mathscr{D}_{\mathrm{T3}}
, which preserves and reflects the order.

Proof.

The first claim is the conjunction of propositions

5.4
and
5.7
;
the parenthetical remark
is
5.5
. The second
claim follows immediately from the first and from definitions.
∎

Remark 5.9
.

Returning to our example started in
4.15
, if

x\in\mathbb{R}
is uncomputable and
P
and
Q
denote its lower
\{r\in\mathbb{Q}\mid r<x\}
and upper
\{r\in\mathbb{Q}\mid r>x\}
Dedekind
cut respectively, then
\mathbf{d}_{P,Q}=[x]_{\mathrm{T0}}
is the
Turing degree of
x
, which is also (after identifying
x
with a
subset of
\mathbb{N}
, say the set of locations of
1
in its binary
expansion) equal to
\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{x}:=\mathbf{d}_{H_{0}^{x},\,H_{1}^{x}}
by
5.5
; but

\mathbf{c}_{P,Q}={\boldsymbol{\omega}}
follows from

4.16
(ii)
and the fact pointed out
in
4.15
that
\mathbf{d}_{P\times Q,\,Q\times P}=\mathbf{0}
, whereas

\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{x}:=\mathbf{c}_{H_{0}^{x},\,H_{1}^{x}}<{\boldsymbol{\omega}}

by
5.8
. This example shows that the
degree
\mathbf{c}_{P,Q}
is
not
a function of the degree

\mathbf{d}_{P,Q}
in general, even though the degree
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{A}

is
a function of
\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{A}
.

Remark 5.10
.

(Extension to T1 degrees.)

If
f\colon\mathbb{N}\dasharrow\mathbb{N}
is a partial function, we
can define
H_{0}^{f},H_{1}^{f}
in the obvious way (using, of course, the
convention that a program that calls the oracle at an undefined value
fails to terminate); then the analogue
of
5.3

still holds: namely,
[g]_{\mathrm{T1}}\leq[f]_{\mathrm{T1}}

implies
(H_{0}^{g},H_{1}^{g})\mathrel{\preceq_{\mathrm{m}}}(H_{0}^{f},H_{1}^{f})

(the only change to the proof is that
\varphi_{e^{\prime}}^{f}
might now
extend
\varphi_{e}^{g}
but this doesn’t affect anything), so the
analogue of
5.4
also
holds, and by defining
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{f}:=\mathbf{c}_{H_{0}^{f},\,H_{1}^{f}}
analogously to
5.1
we get an
order-reversing map
\operatorname{coT}_{1}\colon[f]_{\mathrm{T1}}\mapsto\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{f}
that extends
\operatorname{coT}

to
\mathscr{D}_{\mathrm{T1}}
.

We can also define
\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{f}:=\mathbf{d}_{H_{0}^{f},\,H_{1}^{f}}
in this context and we still have the analogues of

5.5
and
5.6

with the same proofs (so
\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{f}
is just the T1
degree
[f]_{\mathrm{T1}}
of
f
). We do not know, however, how to obtain the
analogue of
5.7
in this
context because it relies on the relative version of
4.17
, whose proof
depends on
\mathbf{a}
being a Turing degree. Thus we only get the
weaker conclusion that
\operatorname{coT}(\mathbf{a})\leq\operatorname{coT}_{1}(\mathbf{g})

implies
\mathbf{g}\leq\mathbf{a}
when
\mathbf{a}
is a T0 degree
while
\mathbf{g}
can be a T1 degree. (Still, this lets us conclude
that
\operatorname{coT}_{1}(\mathbf{g})={\boldsymbol{\omega}}
implies
\mathbf{g}=\mathbf{0}
.)
See also
8.2
below.

Remark 5.11
.

(Tentative extension to T2 degrees.)

Continuing the line of thoughts of the previous remark, one might even
attempt to do something with T2 degrees as follows: if

f\colon\mathbb{N}\dasharrow\mathcal{P}(\mathbb{N})
is a T2 oracle,
we can define
H_{0}^{f},H_{1}^{f}
as
J_{f}(\{0\}),J_{f}(\{1\})
respectively
(where
J_{f}
is defined
in
7.4
), in other words,
the set of Arthur strategies in querying the
f
oracle which,

whatever
the answers made by Merlin, terminate and return the
value
0
, resp.
1
. Again the analogue
of
5.3

still holds:
[g]_{\mathrm{T2}}\leq[f]_{\mathrm{T2}}
implies

(H_{0}^{g},H_{1}^{g})\mathrel{\preceq_{\mathrm{m}}}(H_{0}^{f},H_{1}^{f})
so the
analogue of
5.4
also
holds, and by defining
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{f}:=\mathbf{c}_{H_{0}^{f},\,H_{1}^{f}}
we get an order-reversing map
\operatorname{coT}_{2}\colon[f]_{\mathrm{T2}}\mapsto\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{f}
that extends
\operatorname{coT}

(and
\operatorname{coT}_{1}
) to
\mathscr{D}_{\mathrm{T2}}
. But now if we define

\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{f}:=\mathbf{d}_{H_{0}^{f},\,H_{1}^{f}}
then we only get

\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{f}\leq[f]_{\mathrm{T2}}
, and this inequality can
be strict since
\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{f}
is a T1 degree but there are

f
which are not T1, in other words the analogue
of
5.5
fails, making the

We will point out in
7.33
below
that if we are even bolder and extend
\operatorname{coT}
in the “obvious” way to a
map
\operatorname{coT}_{3}
on the T3 degrees, then we can have
\operatorname{coT}_{3}(\mathbf{g})={\boldsymbol{\omega}}
despite
\mathbf{g}>\mathbf{0}
.

6
Comparing co-Turing degrees with Turing degrees

In the previous section, we have defined for each
Turing degree
\mathbf{a}=[A]_{\mathrm{T0}}
a “co-Turing”
degree
\operatorname{coT}(\mathbf{a})=\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{A}
(in the T3 degrees),
and we have seen that
\operatorname{coT}(\mathbf{a})\leq\operatorname{coT}(\mathbf{b})
occurs
iff
\mathbf{a}\geq\mathbf{b}
. So we understand how the co-Turing
degrees are ordered. In this section, we would now like to compare a
co-Turing degree
\operatorname{coT}(\mathbf{a})
with a Turing degree
\mathbf{b}

(again, in the T3 degrees).

We know
a priori
from
[
Phoa 1989
, prop. 3]
that if

\operatorname{coT}(\mathbf{a})\geq\mathbf{b}
for
all
Turing
degrees
\mathbf{b}
then in fact
\operatorname{coT}(\mathbf{a})={\boldsymbol{\omega}}
(so that

\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{0}
by
5.8
); we will
proceed to show in this section that if
\operatorname{coT}(\mathbf{a})\geq\mathbf{b}
for

some
Turing degree
\mathbf{b}>\mathbf{0}
then in fact

\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{0}
. (To put it differently, not only will

\operatorname{coT}(\mathbf{a})
for
\mathbf{a}>0
not give Arthur omniscience,
it won’t even let him compute
any
deterministic function

\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{N}
that wasn’t already computable.)

Our strategy for this is to consider the promise problem
(P,Q):=(H_{0}^{A}\times H_{0}^{B},\,H_{1}^{A}\times H_{1}^{B})
, and to compare its
coding and decoding degrees with those of
(H_{0}^{A},\,H_{1}^{A})
(namely

\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{A}
and
\tilde{\mathbf{d}}_{A}
) and of
(H_{0}^{B},\,H_{1}^{B})
.

Proposition 6.1
.

For
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, if
P:=H_{0}^{A}\times H_{0}^{B}
and
Q:=H_{1}^{A}\times H_{1}^{B}
, then
\mathbf{d}_{P,Q}
equals the binary meet
(inf)
[A]_{\mathrm{T0}}\mathbin{\wedge_{\mathrm{T1}}}[B]_{\mathrm{T0}}
, taken inside the T1 degrees

\mathscr{D}_{\mathrm{T1}}
, of the Turing degrees of
A
and
B
.

(One may wish to compare with the constructions of the meet given in

3.3
and ¶
7
.)

Proof.

The inequality
\mathbf{d}_{P,Q}\leq[A]_{\mathrm{T0}}\mathbin{\wedge_{\mathrm{T1}}}[B]_{\mathrm{T0}}
follows from

\mathbf{d}_{P,Q}\leq\mathbf{d}_{H_{0}^{A},\,H_{1}^{A}}=[A]_{\mathrm{T0}}
and
\mathbf{d}_{P,Q}\leq\mathbf{d}_{H_{0}^{B},\,H_{1}^{B}}=[B]_{\mathrm{T0}}

(the inequalities follow from projection
and
4.11
,
and the equalities are given
in
5.5
).

The inequality
\mathbf{d}_{P,Q}\geq[A]_{\mathrm{T0}}\mathbin{\wedge_{\mathrm{T1}}}[B]_{\mathrm{T0}}
follows from the
explicit description of
\mathbin{\wedge_{\mathrm{T1}}}
in the
T1 degrees in
3.3
: given
(p,q,m,n)
such that

\varphi_{p}^{A}(m){\downarrow}=\varphi_{q}^{B}(n)
, we loop through
all
N=0,1,2,\ldots
and, for each one, computably produce an oracle
program
a_{N}
which runs
p
on
m
and checks whether the result
is
N
, returning
1
if true and
0
otherwise, and
b_{N}
analogously
for
q
on
N
, then we use
\mathbf{d}_{P,Q}
to test if
(a_{N},b_{N})

is in
P=H_{0}^{A}\times H_{0}^{B}
or in
Q=H_{1}^{A}\times H_{1}^{B}
(note
that it must be in one of them because
\varphi_{p}^{A}(m){\downarrow}=\varphi_{q}^{B}(n)
), and we continue the loop until it is found to be
in
Q
at which point we return the corresponding
N
.
∎

Recall that we have seen that
\mathbf{d}_{P\times Q,\,Q\times P}\leq\mathbf{d}_{P,Q}
holds in general
(
4.13
) but that this
inequality can be strict
(
4.14

or
4.15
). In the specific case we are
interested in this section, it is, in fact,
just like
in
5.6
, an
equality:

Proposition 6.2
.

For
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, if
P:=H_{0}^{A}\times H_{0}^{B}
and
Q:=H_{1}^{A}\times H_{1}^{B}
, then the degree
\mathbf{d}_{P\times Q,\;Q\times P}
equals
\mathbf{d}_{P,Q}
.

Proof.

The proof is essentially the same as
for
5.6
:

The inequality
\mathbf{d}_{P\times Q,\;Q\times P}\leq\mathbf{d}_{P,Q}
has already been seen in full generality
in
4.13
.

For the converse, recall that given
e\in H_{i}^{C}
(no matter what
C\subseteq\mathbb{N}
is) we can computably produce

\mathtt{flip}\circ e\in H_{1-i}^{C}
by simply composing
e
with the
function that exchanges
0
and
1
. To decide by means of

\mathbf{d}_{P\times Q,\;Q\times P}
whether
(a,b)\in P\cup Q
belongs to
P
or
Q
, we ask whether
((a,b),\,(\mathtt{flip}\circ a,\,\mathtt{flip}\circ b))
belongs in
P\times Q
or
Q\times P
.
∎

Lemma 6.3
.

For
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, if
P:=H_{0}^{A}\times H_{0}^{B}
and
Q:=H_{1}^{A}\times H_{1}^{B}
, then
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{A}\leq\mathbf{c}_{P,Q}

Proof.

By
4.12
, it is
enough to prove
(P,Q)\mathrel{\preceq_{\mathrm{m}}}(H_{0}^{A},\,H_{1}^{A})
. But the first projection witnesses this.
∎

Theorem 6.4
.

(i)
For
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, if
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{A}\geq[B]_{\mathrm{T0}}
then in fact one of
A,B
is computable.

In other words: for all Turing degrees
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}
, if

\operatorname{coT}(\mathbf{a})\geq\mathbf{b}
then
\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{0}
or

\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0}
(the converse being obvious).

(ii)
For
A,B\subseteq\mathbb{N}
, it is never true that

\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{A}\leq[B]_{\mathrm{T0}}
. In other words:
for all Turing degrees
\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}
, we have

\operatorname{coT}(\mathbf{a})\not\leq\mathbf{b}
.

Proof.

We start with (i), which is the harder claim (and which depends on our
previous results). Let
P:=H_{0}^{A}\times H_{0}^{B}
and
Q:=H_{1}^{A}\times H_{1}^{B}
. If
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{A}\geq[B]_{\mathrm{T0}}

then
\mathbf{c}_{P,Q}\geq[B]_{\mathrm{T0}}

by
6.3
; now
[B]_{\mathrm{T0}}\geq\mathbf{d}_{P,Q}
by
6.1
, so

\mathbf{c}_{P,Q}\geq\mathbf{d}_{P,Q}
.
By
4.5
, this implies

\mathbf{c}_{P,Q}={\boldsymbol{\omega}}
. By
4.17
, this implies

\mathbf{d}_{P\times Q,\,Q\times P}=\mathbf{0}
.
By
6.2
this is just
\mathbf{d}_{P,Q}=\mathbf{0}
. By
6.1
again, this
means
[A]_{\mathrm{T0}}\mathbin{\wedge_{\mathrm{T1}}}[B]_{\mathrm{T0}}=\mathbf{0}
.
By
3.6
, this means that
A
or
B
is
computable, as claimed.

Let us now prove (ii), which is easier. If
\tilde{\mathbf{c}}_{A}\leq[B]_{\mathrm{T0}}
, then
by propositions
4.5

and
5.5
we have

[A]_{\mathrm{T0}}\vee[B]_{\mathrm{T0}}={\boldsymbol{\omega}}
, which
by
3.1
means
[A\oplus B]_{\mathrm{T0}}={\boldsymbol{\omega}}
.
This is a contradiction as
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
is not a Turing degree (we remarked
in ¶
2
that
{\boldsymbol{\omega}}
is not T2).
∎

In light of the above, one might wonder if, perhaps,
\operatorname{coT}(\mathbf{a})\mathbin{\wedge_{\mathrm{T3}}}\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0}
always holds
(since we have just seen that no Turing degree
>\mathbf{0}
nor
co-Turing degree can be
\leq\operatorname{coT}(\mathbf{a})\mathbin{\wedge_{\mathrm{T3}}}\mathbf{b}
). In fact, this is
essentially
never
the case:

Proposition 6.5
.

If
\operatorname{coT}(\mathbf{a})\mathbin{\wedge_{\mathrm{T3}}}\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{0}
then
