High-value biochar from sunflower husk pyrolysis enhances growth and physiological performance of Spirodela polyrhiza
Authors: Julia Osmańska, Szymon Szufa, Zdzislawa Romanowska-Duda, Omar Mohammad, Hilal Unyay, Casey Wozniak, Piotr Piersa, Justyna Czerwińska, Paweł Kazimierski, Panagiotis Regkouzas, Alexandros Stefanakis, Bartosz Panek, Jude A. Onwudili
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Rising populations intensify food demand and place increasing pressure on already degraded soils, driving the need for sustainable, waste-derived fertilizers that can enhance plant growth. This research aimed to assess the potential use of biochar as a soil amendment to support plant growth. The biochar used in this study was produced from sunflower husk and examined in three variants obtained at pyrolysis temperatures of 300, 400, and 500 °C, applied at different dosage levels. Spirodela polyrhiza served as the model plant species. Assessment was based on changes in chlorophyll index, fluorescence, frond number, and overall plant condition. Temperature-dependent changes in biochar properties were further analysed using SEM, FTIR, BET, and GC-MS. The results indicate that the pyrolysis products used in this study did not exhibit toxic effects on Spirodela polyrhiza growth. Instead, all analysed variants showed a generally positive influence on plant development, reflected in a clear stimulatory response. The findings demonstrate that the impact of biochar on plant growth was strongly dependent on both the pyrolysis temperature and the applied dose, with the most favourable outcomes observed for biochar produced at 400–500 °C. Overall, the study identifies sunflower husk as a duckweed growth-stimulating material and supports its further consideration as an environmentally safe, waste-derived fertilizer component.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-58255-x.

Introduction

Global challenges such as climate change, rapid population growth, and continuous urbanization are putting pressure on agricultural systems, and recent projections suggest that total global food demand is likely to increase by approximately 50–60% by 20501. Fertilizers continue to play a central role in sustaining duckweed growth and crop productivity; however, ongoing limitations stemming from soil degradation and limited nutrient availability are prompting the search for complementary strategies to enhance their effectiveness2,3. Recently, interest has grown in incorporating amendments, such as biochar, alongside conventional fertilizers to further improve plant growth, while monitoring their effectiveness using bioindicators to ensure sustainable outcomes. Nowadays, chemical fertilizers are the most widely used type of fertilizers in agriculture. Supporters of these fertilizers claim they enhance crop yield and quality by providing nutrients in well-balanced, optimized proportions tailored to each crop’s specific requirements. Nevertheless, chemical fertilizers also have disadvantages, the most important of which is their negative impact on the natural environment4. Their overuse poses a significant threat to both current and future generations. The production, use, and disposal of these substances pollute water and soil. It can also contribute to greenhouse gas emissions and land degradation5.

Biochar can represent a promising option for enhancing plant growth6. It is a solid carbon product obtained by the thermal decomposition of biomass under conditions of limited oxygen access, a process called pyrolysis7. Pyrolysis is a thermal conversion method commonly used to transform organic-rich materials into more valuable products. This process initiates thermal decomposition of organic materials under oxygen-free or limited conditions. It can be conducted at various temperatures, ranging from 300 to 700 °C8. The most common feedstocks are agricultural and municipal waste, wood residues9. Thanks to its durable and highly porous structure, this material can contain extractable humic- and fulvic-like substances10. Furthermore, it has a high degree of microbial and chemical stability thanks to its molecular structure11.

The properties of biochar are diverse and influenced by various factors. The type of feedstock plays a crucial role, as well as process conditions—including maximum temperature and holding time12. Also, the type of technological procedures used before and after the pyrolysis process can influence the biochar properties. Due to these factors, achieving optimal end-product characteristics requires the selective choice of both feedstock and processing conditions13. For example, the lignocellulosic feedstock contributes to increased specific surface area in biochar. Plant-derived materials increase the cation exchange capacity, while animal waste feedstock enriches the product with biogenic elements9.

Biochar (often applied as a modified or enriched fertilizer) can significantly improve aqua and soil quality, support plant growth, and increase yields13. Biochar can also contain a variety of harmful substances, including heavy metals, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, polychlorinated dibenzodioxins, polychlorinated dibenzofurans, and volatile organic compounds. These toxicants can be present in the biomass used to produce biochar, or they can form during pyrolysis, although these were not analysed in the present study14,15. Due to the complex impact of biochar, which depends on its properties, as well as the diverse needs of soils in terms of nutrient availability, application methods, and crop types, it is crucial to continue research on different variants of this pyrolyzed biomass to identify the optimal conditions of biochar production16. In this study, the toxicity effects of biochar on Spirodela polyrhiza were analysed. The study was carried out using three fractions of biochar produced at 300 °C, 400 °C, and 500 °C from sunflower husks as the biomass feedstock.

The main objective was to determine whether sunflower husk biochar inhibits or promotes the growth and development of the macrophyte Spirodela polyrhiza. This study is highly novel, as it is the first to investigate the effects of this specific type of biochar on this species and, more broadly, among aquatic plants.

Our findings address a key research gap, demonstrating that even nitrogen-poor biochar can support plant growth through indirect mechanisms, such as modifying nutrient availability and environmental conditions. Previous research has primarily focused on S. polyrhiza in phytoremediation and nutrient recovery, with no reports on its response to biochar in aquatic systems. The Lemnaceae family comprises aquatic plants with simplified morphology, rapid vegetative reproduction (1–2 days), and high environmental sensitivity, which underpins their strong bioindicator potential17. In this study, Spirodela polyrhiza was selected due to its widespread use in toxicology, phytoremediation, and environmental monitoring18.

Beyond the primary focus of this study on the effects of biochar on aquatic plant growth, it is important to note that biochar has considerable potential as an adsorbent for contaminants in wastewater treatment, effectively removing both organic and inorganic pollutants from effluent streams. Moreover, surface modifications of biochar can significantly enhance its adsorption properties, making it a promising material in sustainable water purification technologies19,20.

These results are particularly relevant in light of Commission Regulation (EU) 2025/153, which recognizes Lemna minor and Lemna gibba as novel foods, and may contribute to the development of sustainable biomass production strategies for related Lemnaceae species with food potential, including Spirodela polyrhiza21.

Materials and methodology

Plant Spirodela polyrhiza

Spirodela polyrhiza is characterized by a rapid development cycle and intensive vegetative reproduction, which enable rapid biomass accumulation and support its industrial use. Thanks to its high growth efficiency, this plant can be used for biomass and biofuel production, making it an attractive component of the circular economy. This species can remove and accumulate pollutants from aquatic environments. Additionally, it’s highly effective in reducing the concentrations of heavy metals and nutrients22. It can adapt to a wide range of water types, further enhancing the ecological potential of this species17. S. polyrhiza has a high sensitivity to the presence of heavy metals such as silver, cadmium, copper, and chromium, responding with morphological changes, including reduced frond area and the length of regenerating roots23. The use of this aquatic plant enables the rapid acquisition of reliable results with minimal financial and technical effort, making this plant species particularly valuable as a bioindicator in toxicological studies and routine water quality monitoring24. Spirodela polyrhiza plants used in this study originated from a laboratory collection maintained at the University of Lodz, Poland, and were not collected from wild populations. The study complied with all applicable institutional regulations. No permits or licenses were required for the use of this plant material. Spirodela polyrhiza is not listed under CITES and is not considered a threatened species according to current conservation assessments.

Material: sunflower husk

Sunflower husk is an eco-friendly biomass by-product of sunflower seed processing that can be used as a biofuel. In this study, biochar particles used were sieved to a nominal size of 8 mm. Due to its high calorific value of 21.57 MJ/kg and low moisture content, it is commonly used as a substitute for pellet or coal burners. Additionally, because it originates from agricultural waste, it is a more cost-effective and sustainable alternative to wooden pellets. The sunflower husks used in this study were sourced from Ivano-Frankivsk, a region in Ukraine. The country accounts for approximately 25–30% of global sunflower production, with Ivano-Frankivsk contributing a smaller but still significant share of the national output25.

Growth analysis

The Spirodela polyrhiza used in this study was collected from aquatic plants grown in vitro at the Department of Plant Ecophysiology, University of Lodz. The experiment was conducted in a plate system, where 10 fronds were initially introduced into each sample, each in 10 mL of tap water. The study used tap water with a stable and controlled physicochemical composition, in accordance with ISO 2007926. This choice allows for a better representation of environmental conditions than synthetic media, which can introduce additional confounding factors (e.g., excess nutrients). This gives the obtained results greater ecological and interpretive significance. The pH measurements were conducted on day 0 and at the end of the experiment, showing a slight decrease from 7.4 at the beginning to 7.2 at the end, which is a natural phenomenon during plant cultivation. Chromium

The growth environment was at room temperature (24 ± 1 °C) and under a 16/8-hour light/dark cycle with a light intensity of 85 µmol photons m−2 s−1. Plants were cultivated in sterile Tissue Culture Plates (6-well format, Falcon), with each well measuring 3.5 cm in diameter and serving as an independent microreactor. This configuration minimized environmental variability and ensured uniform conditions across experimental replicates. The system was not sealed; slight evaporation could occur during the experiment. To maintain a constant medium volume and stable concentrations of the tested factors (including biochar), water was replenished daily to the initial volume of 10 mL. This procedure minimized artifacts associated with concentration changes due to evaporation.

Three types of biochars obtained from sunflower husks were used as test agents. The pyrolysis temperatures used for this experiment were 300, 400, and 500 °C. Each biochar was added to the substrate at four doses: 10, 50, 100, and 250 mg. For comparison, a control sample without pyrolyzed products was also prepared. All experiments were conducted in triplicate to ensure the reliability of the results. Plant cultivation took place under natural climatic conditions, i.e., those occurring between July 5, 2025, and July 14, 2025. During this period, the temperature was 24–25 °C, the photoperiod was 15 h of natural light and 9 h of darkness. To create growth response curves and analyse the relationship between biochar doses and average macrophyte growth, all visible plant fronts were recorded daily. The experiment lasted ten days. All visible fronds in each sample were recorded daily, and their number was systematically documented. This activity provided the basis for determining the growth dynamics of the plant under test. Thanks to that, it was possible to analyze the effect of the applied biochars on the plants. It also helps to establish which doses may be toxic or have a stimulating effect. Deviations from the expected exponential growth were interpreted as a sign of either a negative or positive impact of the tested substances. A reduction in the number of fronds relative to the control was considered an indicator of an inhibitory effect. In contrast, an increase in the number of fronds was considered a possible stimulatory effect on macrophyte development. This was the primary indicator of this study. The resulting data served as the basis for subsequent statistical analyses and enabled the determination of plant growth dynamics and the effects of different biochar doses on Spirodela polyrhiza development.

Physiological analysis methods

Determination of the chlorophyll index (a + b)

To accurately assess the potential toxicity of the biochars used, a key element of the analysis was to determine the chlorophyll content index-a physiological parameter reflecting the health of plants and their ability to conduct effective photosynthesis. The measurements were performed at three strategic time points: at the initial stage (before exposure), on the fourth day of the experiment, and at the end of the experiment. This three-phase measurement scheme enabled monitoring of chlorophyll content dynamics and assessing the impact of each biochar variant on photosynthetic processes. Chlorophyll content was measured using a handheld chlorophyll meter SPAD-502 (Konica Minolta, Japan). This device enables a rapid, non-invasive, and reproducible estimation of chlorophyll content based on leaf colour intensity27. This measurement enables accurate monitoring of plant physiological status, which is crucial for assessing the potential toxicity of the tested materials and their effects on basic metabolic processes in plants.

Chlorophyll fluorescence analysis

Chlorophyll fluorescence was measured using the Handy PEA (Hansatech Instruments Ltd., UK), an advanced fluorometer commonly used in plant physiology research. This instrument records fluorescence parameters with high temporal resolution, enabling accurate mapping of the dynamics of photochemical processes in chloroplasts. The Handy PEA can generate Rapid Light Curves (RLCs), which have been widely used in research on photosynthetic efficiency. For these measurements, individuals of Spirodela polyrhiza were collected from each experimental variant and grown under controlled conditions. But before the experiment began, each plant underwent a 20-minute dark adaptation. This allowed the complete relaxation of the photosystem II reaction centers and stabilization of their redox state. After that, the plant was exposed to a 670 nm light source, while chlorophyll fluorescence emission was recorded in the 680–760 nm range using a sensitive photodetector.

The basic parameter of this analysis was the maximum quantum efficiency of photosystem II (Fv/Fm), which can be calculated according to the equation:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${{\rm{F}}_{\rm{v}}}{\rm{/}}{{\rm{F}}_{\rm{m}}}{\rm{ = (}}{{\rm{F}}_{\rm{m}}} - {{\rm{F}}_{\rm{o}}}{\rm{)/}}{{\rm{F}}_{\rm{m}}}$$\end{document} where Fm is the maximum fluorescence obtained after the saturation pulse, F₀ is the initial fluorescence, recorded after dark adaptation.

To assess the changes in photosystem II efficiency during the exposure period, measurements were taken three times: on days zero, three, and six of the experiment.

The Fv/Fm typical reference value for plants in good health is approximately 0.83. A decrease in this parameter indicates environmental stress, PSII damage, or inhibition of photosynthetic processes28,29.

SEM analysis

Scanning electron microscopy was conducted on a Thermo Fisher APREO 2 S instrument fitted with an EDAX energy-dispersive X-ray detector. Specimens were mounted on carbon-taped stubs and examined under high vacuum. Imaging employed a 1 keV accelerating voltage, beam current of 0.2 nA and the T1 segmented lower in-lens detector to resolve surface morphology.

Shimadzu EDX-8100P XRF spectrometer was used for bulk quantitative analysis, the samples were homogenized and then weighed at approximately 3 g. The sunflower husk samples—including raw biomass and pyrolyzed biochars were placed in a measuring cup lined with Mylar foil. Each sample was analyzed under a nitrogen atmosphere. A 10 mm collimator directed the X-ray beam, and the SSD detector, cooled by a Peltier module, captured fluorescent X-rays. Three energy channels (Al–U, C–Sc, S–K) covered elements from carbon (Z = 6) through uranium (Z = 92). Specialist filters and thin-window technology enable enhanced detection of low-Z elements down to the ppm level without requiring extensive sample preparation. Data were processed using Shimadzu’s PCEDX-NAVI and PCEDX-PRO software to generate both qualitative spectra and quantitative concentration results.

Pyrolysis process

The biochar used in the study was obtained in a dedicated batch biomass pyrolysis installation. The biomass pyrolysis system consists of a batch reactor, a heating system with temperature control, and a condensation and combustion unit for the liquid pyrolysis products. The batch reactor used in the experiment is cylindrical, 350 mm in diameter, 500 mm high, and made of 3 mm steel, sealed with a DN350 flange and secured with 16 screws. It features thermocouple ports (Ø16 mm) and a gas outlet (Ø63 mm) for monitoring. During the experiment, the reactor is placed axially within a ceramic heating element with Kanthal coils, which ensure even heat distribution, and is surrounded by insulation. Temperature is controlled by a type K thermocouple and a PID controller. Biomass is loaded and, upon heating, undergoes pyrolysis, producing gaseous and liquid products. The gaseous and liquid pyrolysis products (in vapor form) are rapidly removed through a DN50 pipe to prevent condensation and secondary reactions. The resulting products are directed to a disposal chamber with a gas burner, ensuring safe handling.

Additionally, a CHNS-O elemental analysis is being conducted to determine the biomass’s elemental composition, a key factor influencing its thermochemical behaviour. This analysis also provides insight into the extent of pyrolysis, as the carbon content in the solid product typically increases with temperature, while the proportions of oxygen and hydrogen decrease. The elemental composition was measured using a CHNS-O Flash 2000 analyser (Thermo Scientific). The heating rate was approximately 5 °C/min. The exact heating rate may vary slightly across different grains (pellets) due to the large-scale reactor used for pyrolysis. In batch reactors, the heating rate cannot be estimated with the same accuracy as in, for example, TGA experiments. Therefore, the average heating rate for the entire reactor was determined by measuring the total process time and the temperature change within the biomass bed. The reactor was not purged with an inert gas due to the high bulk density of sunflower husk pellets and the resulting low air-to-biomass ratio. The reactor (50 dm3) contained approximately 32.5 kg of biomass and about 21 g of air, including ~ 4.8 g of oxygen. As the temperature increased, thermal expansion and evolving pyrolysis gases reduced the oxygen content to ~ 2.4 g, resulting in an oxygen-to-biomass ratio of approximately 1:1.3 × 104. Under these conditions, the effect of oxygen on the process is considered negligible, and the process can be regarded as true pyrolysis. When the temperature reached approximately 100 °C, residual moisture was released from the biomass, and the resulting water vapor further evacuated the remaining air. At around 200 °C, a temperature at which oxygen from air could potentially begin participating in surface reactions with the biomass, the oxygen concentration was already low enough that its effect would be negligible. It should also be noted that thermal decomposition of biomass begins around 200 °C. In the initial stage of decomposition, carbon monoxide is released, which is thermodynamically more prone to oxidation than the biomass itself. While inert gas purging is important in general, it is not critical for this type of batch reactor, especially when processing biomass with high bulk density. The residence time was 1 h, counted from the moment the target temperature was reached in the center of the biomass bed.

Attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR)

ATR-FTIR analysis was performed using a Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50 FT-IR spectrometer equipped with an iS50 ATR diamond crystal and an MCT-A detector. Spectra were recorded in the range of 400–4000 cm−1 at a resolution of 4 cm−1, averaging 64 scans per sample, and processed using OMNIC™ Spectra software. Both raw sunflower husk and pyrolyzed samples (300, 400, and 500 °C) were analysed to identify functional groups and monitor structural changes induced by thermal treatment.

Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface area analysis

The surface area, pore size distribution, and total pore volume of the raw sunflower husk and pyrolyzed samples (300, 400, and 500 °C) were determined by N₂ adsorption–desorption using a Micromeritics ASAP 2020 analyser equipped with NovaWin software. The procedure was adapted from methodologies reported by Onwudili and Scaldaferri (2023)30. Prior to analysis, all samples were degassed under vacuum at 120 °C for 12 h to remove residual moisture and volatiles. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms were collected at liquid nitrogen temperature (–196 °C). Specific surface areas were calculated using the BET method, while pore size distribution and total pore volume were obtained from the desorption branch of the isotherm using the Barrett–Joyner–Halenda model31.

Gas chromatograph

Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry was employed to identify organic compounds extractable from the produced biochars and to assess the presence of residual or newly formed organics following pyrolysis. Biochars obtained at 300, 400, and 500 °C were each subjected to solvent extraction. Approximately 1.0 g of biochar was transferred into a glass vial containing 2 mL of acetone, briefly vortex-mixed, and then sonicated for 10 min to maximise the desorption of loosely bound organic species. After extraction, the suspensions were passed through hydrophobic syringe filters (0.22 μm PTFE membrane) to remove particulate material prior to analysis.

Organic compounds in the filtrates were analysed following a previously established protocol by our research group32. The liquid sample was analysed using a Shimadzu GC-MS-QP2020 gas chromatograph equipped with a capillary column SH-I-5MS (5%diphenyl−95% dimethyl polysiloxane) (30 m long, 0.25 mm diameter,0.25 μm film thickness). The helium (purity > 99.9999) flow through the column was set to 1.0 mL/min. The temperatures of the ion source and the injector were set to 300 °C and 250 °C, respectively. For each analysis, 1 µL of sample was injected with a split ratio of 1:20. The oven temperature programme commenced at 40 °C, ramped at 10 °C min− 1 to 300 °C, and was held for 5 min.

Results

Pyrolysis results

In the conducted study, the pyrolysis temperatures were 300, 400, and 500 °C. After the pyrolysis product release has stopped, the reactor is switched off and subsequently cooled for approximately 10 h. Afterwards, the reactor is unloaded, and the carbonized material is subjected to further analysis. The greatest amount of biochar was obtained at 300 °C, accounting for 67% of the solid mass. At 400 and 500 °C, the yields of carbonized solid mass were 36.3% and 31.7%, respectively. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) has been conducted on raw sunflower husk. The study, carried out using a NETZSCH TG 209F3 apparatus, enabled evaluation of the sample mass changes as a function of temperature over 35 to 800 °C, under a nitrogen atmosphere and at a heating rate of 10 K/min. The initial mass of the sample was 10.65 mg, and the measurement was performed using an open ceramic crucible (Al2O3, 85 µl). The results are shown in Fig. A1, provided in the Supplementary Materials. During the first stage (up to 130 °C), a slight mass loss of approximately 2% occurred. This corresponds to the release of moisture and volatile compounds from the material, as indicated by the first peak on the DTG curve at 73.5 °C, with a maximum mass-loss rate of − 0.70%/min. The most significant mass changes occur between approximately 180 °C and 350 °C. Within this interval, a sharp decrease in the sample mass is observed - from about 95% to around 30%. The maximum mass loss rate is observed at 330.8 °C, at − 7.60%/min. This temperature range is typical for the thermal decomposition of organic biomass components such as cellulose, hemicellulose, and lignin. The point corresponding to 50% of the initial mass is reached at 335.9 °C. Biomass weight loss is significantly reduced once it reaches 400 °C. Moreover, no further changes are observed at 500 °C. At the final temperature of 798 °C, the residual mass of the sample is 26.1%. This shows that a raw sunflower husk has a relatively high content of inorganic substances and bound carbon. Additionally, an elemental analysis was conducted, and the results are listed in Table B1 provided in Supplementary Materials. In conclusion, the analysed biomass exhibits typical properties of lignocellulosic biomass: it contains a small amount of moisture, undergoes intensive decomposition in the range of 200–400 °C, and upon completion of the process, leaves a significant amount of inorganic residue.

SEM analysis of surface morphology changes showed that increasing the pyrolysis temperature leads to a more distinct surface voidage, as observed in Fig. 1. Sunflower husk pyrolysed at 500 °C has a higher surface voidage than the raw biomass and samples pyrolysed at lower temperatures. In general, hemicelluloses are a group of polysaccharides located in the plant cell wall, consisting of branched amorphous structures that link cellulose and lignin33. In the raw sunflower husk, this branched structure is clearly visible; however, after pyrolysis, it gradually disappears, indicating that hemicellulose decomposes during the process. In the raw sunflower husk, the surface is relatively smooth, dense, and organized. The fibrous structures are intact, and the cell walls are mostly continuous. The thick nature of the structure indicates the presence of hemicellulose and lignin as binding materials between cellulose34. The visible vessels and long fibers are typical of the lignocellulosic material with high hemicellulose content35. No structural damage has been observed yet, confirming that the biomass is in a natural, undegraded condition. At a pyrolysis temperature of 300 °C, the surface begins to exhibit noticeable morphological change. It forms tiny voids, pores, and fissures, indicating the beginning of hemicellulose decomposition. The fibrous network remains recognizable, but it is coarser and less dense. Partial volatilization of amorphous material results in local shrinkage and mild fragmentation of cell walls. This temperature range corresponds to the main decomposition stage of hemicellulose and is responsible for the apparent loss of structural integrity36. At 400 °C, the degradation is significantly more severe. The cell wall structure collapses, leaving a surface with irregular, interconnected voids. The breakdown of hemicellulose and partial depolymerization of cellulose result in extensive loss of structural integrity. The surface is brittle and porous, with numerous microcracks and extensive devolatilization. At 500 °C, the sunflower husk exhibits a highly distorted, porous morphology. The fibrous structure of the raw sample is almost destroyed and replaced by a porous, carbon-rich network. The surface is characterized by numerous large voids, channels, and cracks, revealing serious thermal degradation and carbonization. The development of such pores increases the specific surface area, indicating that the majority of hemicellulose and cellulose have degraded, leaving behind a char-like substance with high aromatic carbon structures. The SEM analysis illustrates the clear correlation between pyrolysis temperature and structural degradation. As the temperature increases, the dense surface of the fresh sunflower husk transforms into a porous, fragmented carbonaceous structure. The progressive formation of surface voidage and disintegration of the fibrous structure confirm the degradation of hemicellulose and cellulose, and the transformation of biomass into a thermally stable, carbon-rich product.

To complement the biological results with a rigorous assessment of statistical significance, an analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted using Statistica. The analysis evaluated the effect of biochar derived from sunflower husks on the growth of Spirodela polyrhiza. The results demonstrated that the model is statistically significant (F = 17.29; p < 0.000001), indicating significant differences among the experimental variants analyzed.

The analysis revealed numerous statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) among selected combinations of experimental factors. Attenuated total reflectance Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (ATR-FTIR) of raw material (Sunflower husk) and biochar

The ATR-FTIR spectra (Fig. 5) of raw sunflower husk and biochar produced at 300–500 °C show systematic chemical transformation consistent with progressive decomposition of polysaccharides and formation of an increasingly aromatic carbon matrix. The broad O–H stretching band (3400–3200 cm−1), associated with hydrogen-bonded hydroxyl groups in cellulose/hemicellulose and adsorbed moisture, decreases in intensity and narrows as temperature increases, indicating dehydration and the loss of oxygenated functionalities. Aliphatic C–H stretching bands at 2920 and 2850 cm−1 (–CH2/–CH3 groups in biopolymers and lignin side chains) diminish markedly above 300 °C, reflecting cleavage and volatilization of aliphatic structures. In addition, the carbonyl band in the 1730–1700 cm−1 region (C=O in ester/carboxylic groups, commonly associated with hemicellulose components) weakens with increasing thermal severity, supporting deacetylation/decarboxylation reactions. In the fingerprint region (1200–900 cm−1), the gradual loss of C–O–C/C–O stretching features characteristic of carbohydrates further confirms depolymerization of cellulose/hemicellulose. Conversely, bands assigned to aromatic structures (1600–1500 cm−1) become relatively more pronounced at higher temperatures, indicating aromatization and development of a condensed carbon framework; lignin’s higher thermal stability likely contributes to this aromatic backbone. Low-wavenumber features may also reflect increasing relative contribution of mineral/ash-related vibrations as organic matter is removed.

Discussion

Thermochemical valorization of biomass has become a widely studied and increasingly popular pathway for producing biochar. As a carbon-rich solid material, biochar offers remarkable versatility and can be used for a range of applications, including use as a renewable solid fuel and a plant growth stimulator. It was shown that applying biochar significantly improves organic duckweed growth. Abdul-Aziz et al. reported increased grain yield of 218.2% in 2022 and 106.3% in 2023 by adding 8 tons of biochar per hectare of soil37. Han et al. conducted even more advanced research by conducting a meta-analysis to provide a comprehensive evaluation and overall impact analysis of results from various biochar studies. By reviewing 381 datasets from 63 studies worldwide, they found that adding biochar to soil increased crop yield, nitrogen use efficiency, and water use efficiency by 14.45%, 13.97%, and 14.28%, respectively38. However, in some cases, adding biochar results in neutral or even negative plant responses39. Therefore, to achieve optimal plant growth with biochar as a fertilizer additive, it is crucial to study various biochar feedstocks and production methods.

Sunflower husk biochar is characterized by limited nutrient availability, particularly nitrogen, which is significantly lost during pyrolysis. Unlike animal manure, which contains easily digestible forms of nitrogen, phosphorus, and other macronutrients, biochar primarily serves an indirect function—it does not directly provide large amounts of nutrients but influences their retention and availability in the environment. While sunflower husk biochar may contain moderate amounts of minerals (especially potassium and ash), it remains relatively low in nitrogen compared to other plant biochars. Its advantage, however, is its relatively high stability and ability to adsorb and gradually release nutrients, which can indirectly support plant growth.

This study primarily addressed the phytotoxicity of sunflower husk and its impact on the growth of the plant Spirodela polyrhiza. The results of this study clearly indicate that biochar derived from sunflower husk, regardless of pyrolysis temperature, did not exhibit phytotoxic effects on Spirodela polyrhiza. All variants exhibited either a stimulating or a neutral effect on plant growth and physiological properties. The produced biochar enhances the availability of mineral nutrients and modifies the environment’s physicochemical properties, resulting in improved growth and development of the analysed plant. Those outcomes are consistent with findings reported by other researchers6,40.

During the experiment, the effects of different biochars on plant responses and on the physicochemical processes underlying them were analysed. The results show that the biochar obtained at a temperature of 400–500 °C has the strongest stimulating effect on the production of fronds. The increased production of fronds likely results from structural changes in the biomass during pyrolysis. As demonstrated by the SEM analysis, the material’s porosity increased with higher pyrolysis temperatures, potentially enhancing water retention and gas exchange. These observations are consistent with BET and BJH measurements, confirming that the morphological evolution observed under SEM is reflected in the overall textural development of the biomass. The measured specific surface area values (0.577–1.124 m2/g) are low, typical of sunflower husk, and consistent with previous studies (0.93, 1.3 m2/g)41,42. Although low, these values reflect the intrinsic nature of the biomass and do not compromise its potential functional properties. The combined evidence indicates that thermal treatment progressively transforms the material into a more porous, carbon-rich structure, linking physical changes to the chemical and structural modifications occurring during pyrolysis. Additionally, samples produced at elevated temperatures contained fewer volatile compounds, which often include phenolic substances and organic acids known to inhibit plant growth14,15. This relationship is further supported by the observation that the most efficient growth was observed with biochar produced at the highest pyrolysis temperature. The addition of 10 mg/10 ml of pyrolysed sunflower husk compared to the control sample enhances the number of fronds by over 50%. This proves that improvements in sorption properties, increased surface area, porosity, and pH stabilization in the aquatic environment stimulate plant growth. However, no linear relationship was observed between applied biochar doses and plant growth. It is possible that, with a higher addition of biochar to the environment, it may not be modified favourably. Increased alkalinity, micronutrient adsorption, or changes in dissolved substance concentrations do not support further intensification of macrophyte growth. The results obtained are consistent with those reported in the literature and highlight the importance of optimizing the dosage, especially in aquatic environments9,43.

The chlorophyll index analysis also confirms a positive impact of biochar obtained at higher temperatures. The highest chlorophyll content compared to control samples was observed in plants grown with biochars produced at 400–500 °C. This indicates that those variants improve nitrogen metabolism and intensify photosynthetic processes. The results from X-ray fluorescence spectrometer analysis revealed that biochar obtained from sunflower husk contains significant amounts of K, Ca, and Si. Those elements can directly improve the stability of photosynthetic pigments and chloroplast function44,45.

Fv/Fm values in the range of 0.759–0.799 may indicate the presence of mild stress; however, their relative stability suggests the absence of significant damage to the photosynthetic apparatus, particularly Photosystem II. In this context, the interpretation of results gains additional insight when considered alongside SPAD index values, which reflect the relative chlorophyll content in plant tissues. Observed changes in SPAD values, combined with stable Fv/Fm parameters, may indicate the activation of adaptive mechanisms, such as regulation of chlorophyll synthesis or reorganization of the photosynthetic apparatus in response to changing environmental conditions. This response likely reflects physiological acclimation rather than direct damage to photosynthetic structures, highlighting the plants’ ability to maintain photochemical efficiency even under stress46. The Fv/Fm ratio reflects stress differently in terrestrial and aquatic plants. In wheat, it remains stable throughout most of the drought period and only declines in the final phase, showing a strong correlation with yield loss, which makes it a good indicator of stress levels47. In barley, Fv/Fm decreases with increasing seeding rate, reflecting both environmental stress and competition among plants48. In contrast, in Lemnaceae (e.g., Spirodela polyrhiza), Fv/Fm remains at a high and stable level (approx. 0.75–0.80), and its decline occurs only under severe stress. This means that in aquatic plants, this index primarily reflects direct damage to the photosynthetic apparatus, whereas in terrestrial plants, it is more sensitive to various stress factors and competition.

Chlorophyll fluorescence analysis indicates that plants are not under physiological stress. Fv/Fm values in the range of 0.759–0.799 are typical for healthy plants and indicate proper functioning of the PS II. The lower values obtained in samples pyrolyzed at 300 °C do not necessarily reflect metabolic disorders, as they may be associated with residual organic compounds characteristic of lower pyrolysis temperatures46. The findings of this study indicate that biochar obtained from sunflower husk and pyrolysed at ≥ 400 °C can be used not only as a fertilizer additive for aquatic plants but also as a tool to support phytoremediation processes and long-term carbon sequestration.

This study focused on physiological indicators, including chlorophyll content (SPAD) and photosystem II efficiency (Fv/Fm), which are widely recognized as sensitive and reliable measures of plant condition and performance in aquatic systems. The observed stability of Fv/Fm, together with maintained or increased SPAD values, indicates preserved photosynthetic functionality and adequate pigment levels, both closely associated with sustained plant growth potential. Such an approach, based on non-destructive physiological parameters, is commonly applied in studies of aquatic macrophytes as a robust tool for assessing plant performance and productivity.

Studies on Vallisneria spiralis (a submerged macrophyte) have demonstrated that biochar does not disrupt plant growth or interfere with biogeochemical processes, including sediment oxidation driven by oxygen released from the roots49. Its influence is particularly significant in the case of cadmium (Cd(II)), whose bioavailability is reduced thanks to the material’s porous structure, high adsorption capacity, and alkaline pH. However, the literature also reports that biochar has limited short-term effectiveness for nutrient remediation, especially in organic-matter-rich sediments. This organic fraction occupies sorption sites (NH4+, PO43−) and reduces microbial activity within the first 30 days. The most significant potential of biochar lies in its pairing with biological agents. In combination with arbuscular mycorrhizal fungi (AMF) or plant growth-promoting bacteria (PGPB), its remediation efficiency increases, microbial stress is alleviated, and glomalin-like soil protein production is enhanced50.

Biochar not only enhances plant growth but also helps immobilize heavy metals and reduce their bioavailability, thereby enabling phytoremediation plants to function more efficiently6. When combined with fast-growing macrophytes such as Spirodela polyrhiza, integrated systems emerge in which biochar acts as a sorbent, and plants remove contaminants via bioaccumulation, enabling practical applications in water and soil treatment. Additionally, biochar stabilizes organic carbon, sequestering CO2 for centuries and enhancing plant photosynthesis, while macrophyte biomass can be reused for remediation, animal feed, energy, or further pyrolysis, creating a positive carbon balance14,15. Moreover, biochar can serve as a precursor for producing activated carbon used in the purification of flue gases and water51.

GC-MS results indicate a predominance of lipid-derived compounds (fatty acid methyl esters) at lower pyrolysis temperatures, suggesting that the resulting biochar retains a notable fraction of thermally unstable organic matter. This observation is consistent with previous studies demonstrating that biochars produced under mild-to-moderate pyrolysis conditions can contain a variety of adsorbed organic contaminants, including water-soluble organic acids, phenols, and other low-molecular-weight organics52. Such residual organics may influence the chemical behaviour, stability, and environmental safety of biochar, for instance, by affecting aqua/soil microbial communities, biochemical oxygen demand, or the mobility of nutrients and pollutants when applied to soil53.

The schematic in Fig. 6 summarizes the multifunctional role of sunflower husk biochar, with an emphasis on its use as a slow-release aquatic fertilizer that supports duckweed growth by improving nutrient retention and gradual release in water. In parallel, biochar contributes to phytoremediation by sorbing contaminants and helping immobilize metals, while also enabling reduced reliance on chemical fertilizers and synthetic biostimulants through its nutrient-holding and conditioning effects. By converting biomass residues into a stable, carbon-rich material, the pathway supports circular-economy waste valorization and climate change mitigation through long-term carbon storage in soils.

Given these considerations, it appears advisable to explore post‑treatment strategies (e.g., mild thermal treatment, controlled aeration, washing, or aging) to reduce or remove residual lipid‑derived or low‑stability organic matter before the practical application of biochar, especially in environmental or agricultural contexts. Further studies are required to evaluate the effects of these post‑treatment methods on plant growth and health, compared with untreated biochar, to confirm their practical benefits and safety. Many challenges remain despite its advantages, including the variability of biochar properties, which depend on pyrolysis conditions and feedstock; the potential reduction in the bioavailability of contaminants to microorganisms; and the short-lived effects reported in laboratory studies54. It is therefore essential that long-term investigation and optimisation of production parameters are conducted.

Conclusion

This study assessment indicates that biochar derived from sunflower husk, produced at pyrolysis temperatures between 300 °C and 500 °C, does not induce acute phytotoxic effects in Spirodela polyrhiza under the specific tap-water conditions tested. Within this experimental framework, the biochar variants analyzed demonstrated a stimulatory effect on plant development, characterized by a non-linear growth response. The most significant stimulation of frond production was observed with biochar produced at 500 °C applied at a 10 mg/10 mL dosage. These effects are attributed to enhanced porosity, increased surface area, and reduced volatile organic content at higher thermal treatment temperatures. While these results identify sunflower husk biochar as a potentially beneficial growth amendment for S. polyrhiza in a controlled aquatic environment, the findings are limited to the specific species. Consequently, long-term investigation and optimization of production parameters are essential to determine the sustained efficacy and environmental safety of this material across broader ecological and agricultural contexts.
