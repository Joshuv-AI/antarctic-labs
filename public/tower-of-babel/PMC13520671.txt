From notecards to know-how: scaling “Muddiest Point” feedback for high-enrollment microbiology
Authors: Jordan A. Smink, Robert W. Lamb
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMuddiest Point prompts can reveal what students do not yet understand, but in large microbiology courses, the volume of responses can make timely feedback difficult. This Tips and Tools article describes a scalable workflow for transforming end-of-unit notecard responses into a targeted review resource. Student submissions were grouped into recurring themes, prioritized by frequency and instructional importance, and used to create an instructor-generated, chaptered review video with accompanying worksheet prompts. The central innovation is the aggregation of many individual uncertainties into one reusable feedback artifact that preserves instructor voice while reducing the need for repeated individual explanations. Students could watch the full review or navigate directly to difficult concepts, and pause-and-predict prompts, live sketching, and worksheet fill-ins supported active use of the video. The article provides implementation guidance; accessibility considerations; adaptations for lecture, laboratory, hybrid, or online contexts; and options for low-overhead evaluation. The workflow offers a practical approach for sustaining formative feedback in high-enrollment courses using common instructional tools.

INTRODUCTION

Classroom assessment techniques (CATs) provide rapid, low-stakes ways to identify student understanding while a course is still in progress (1, 2). The Muddiest Point prompt asks students to name the concept, process, or step that remains most unclear. Because the prompt is brief and student-generated, it can reveal specific uncertainties that may not surface through exams or general review questions (3, 4). In large-enrollment microbiology courses, however, collecting student responses is often easier than acting on them; timely one-to-one feedback becomes impractical when hundreds of students submit individual points of confusion.

This article describes a scalable adaptation of the Muddiest Point technique for a high-enrollment general microbiology course. The novelty of this approach is the rapid conversion of individual muddiest-point responses into a shared, instructor-generated review artifact. At the end of each unit, students submitted notecards identifying one specific point of confusion. Responses were clustered into common themes, prioritized by frequency and instructional importance, and used to create a chaptered review video with aligned worksheet prompts. This approach preserves the diagnostic value of student-generated feedback while replacing hundreds of individual replies with a cohort-level response that students can watch in full or navigate by topic.

The video design drew on multimedia learning and feedback principles. Chaptering and segmentation organized content into short, navigable units (5). Signaling directed attention to relevant features (6, 7), while conversational narration, live sketching, pause prompts, and worksheet-based practice encouraged retrieval and self-checking (8–10). These features are especially useful in microbiology, where students must often coordinate sequence, mechanism, directionality, visual representations, and causal explanations.

This Tips and Tools article provides a reproducible workflow for converting muddiest-point responses into targeted review materials using common instructional tools.

PROCEDURE

Course context and teaching team

This workflow was implemented in a high-enrollment General Microbiology course at Mississippi State University with approximately 285 students. Lecture met twice weekly for 50 min. The learning management system was used to distribute review videos, worksheets, announcements, captions or transcripts, and related study materials.

The workflow used personnel and tools already available in the course. The lead instructor collected and sorted notecards, identified recurring points of confusion, selected themes, and framed each review segment. A collaborating instructor provided video recording, editing, and technical support. Peer-led Supplemental Instruction leaders received information about common areas of confusion so review sessions could reinforce the same concepts.

During assessment weeks, muddiest-point notecards were collected during Thursday lecture, clustered into themes, and compiled into a chaptered review video with an aligned worksheet by Saturday. Students were directed to use the video, worksheet, office hours, and Supplemental Instruction before the Tuesday unit exam. Video engagement was monitored through Canvas analytics, which allowed the instructor to view aggregate access patterns, and students also informally identified the review videos and worksheets as helpful study resources. After the exam, commonly missed concepts were briefly revisited through class discussion, office hours, or Supplemental Instruction.

Implementing the end-of-unit workflow

Each cycle followed five connected phases: collection, clustering, scripting, production, and release. As shown in Fig. 1, these phases formed an iterative loop rather than a one-time sequence. Student-identified confusion from one unit shaped the review artifact for that assessment, and remaining areas of difficulty informed subsequent class discussion, office hours, Supplemental Instruction, and future review materials. In this way, the workflow converted student uncertainty into a targeted resource before the next assessment while also building a reusable feedback cycle across units.

Iterative Muddiest Point response workflow. Student-submitted muddiest points move through five connected phases: collection, clustering, scripting, production, and release. The circular structure emphasizes that the process functions as an iterative feedback loop in which student confusion is rapidly aggregated, converted into a chaptered review video with aligned worksheet prompts, released through the learning management system, and used to guide subsequent review, office hours, and Supplemental Instruction.

During collection, students submitted one specific point of confusion from the unit (Fig. 2). The prompt emphasized specificity so that responses moved beyond broad topic labels and toward actionable questions. For example, instead of writing “DNA replication,” students were encouraged to identify the unclear step or relationship, such as leading-strand versus lagging-strand synthesis or the role of primers. Notecards were collected at the end of class, with an online version available for absent students.

After collection, the instructor sorted responses by conceptual similarity. Priority was given to themes that appeared frequently, represented foundational concepts, or were likely to affect upcoming assessments. Less common or highly individualized questions were redirected to office hours, Supplemental Instruction, discussion boards, or learning management system messaging. This kept the video broadly useful while preserving individual support pathways.

Across units, the approximately 285 student submissions typically produced 8–10 themes per unit after clustering. Although individual wording varied, most responses fell into a smaller number of repeated conceptual categories, such as distinguishing related processes, interpreting sequence or directionality, applying terminology, or connecting mechanisms to outcomes. In a typical cycle, 5–10 themes were selected for inclusion in the review video, while narrower or highly individualized questions were redirected to office hours, Supplemental Instruction, or learning management system messaging. This clustering step helped keep the review artifact focused on confusions that were both common and instructionally consequential.

Clustered themes became short review segments. Each segment used a consistent structure: a theme label, one representative student-facing question, a brief explanation or diagram, and one worksheet prompt students could complete while watching. Figure 2 demonstrates the student prompt, and the supplemental material provides an example of a transcription-focused worksheet aligned with a review video. Existing lecture slides, textbook figures, or instructor-generated diagrams were adapted whenever possible to avoid full re-teaching.

Videos were recorded with standard instructional tools, such as slide software, screen capture, and a document camera or tablet for drawing. Segments could be recorded in one continuous pass or stitched from shorter clips.

Loop closure occurred after posting. Announcements reminded students that the review was built from their submitted muddiest points and directed them to relevant chapters, worksheets, office hours, and Supplemental Instruction. After the exam, persistent areas of confusion were revisited briefly so student input continued to guide instruction.

Time, tools, and return on investment

The workflow was designed to fit alongside normal instructional preparation. Most steps used existing course materials, including lecture slides, textbook figures, worksheet prompts, the learning management system, and standard screen-recording tools. Sorting approximately 285 notecards typically took about 15 min once recurring patterns became familiar. Preparing and recording required additional time, but reusing existing materials and focusing only on frequent or consequential muddy points reduced workload.

The primary return on investment was that one targeted review artifact replaced or reduced less efficient repeated feedback. Instead of responding individually to many similar questions, the instructor addressed shared confusion once in a format available to all students. In subsequent offerings of the course, approximately 70% of the segment structure, diagrams, explanations, and worksheets could be reused with minor revision because many muddiest-point themes recurred across cohorts. These reusable components could also be revised, recombined, or adapted for later semesters, final exam reviews, or Supplemental Instruction sessions.

Although this Tips and Tools article was not designed as a formal outcomes study, several practical indicators suggested usefulness. Canvas analytics showed that students accessed the videos multiple times before unit exams. Students also anecdotally reported that they found the review videos helpful. In addition, the instructor observed that common questions could be addressed by directing students to specific video chapters and worksheet prompts, reducing the need to repeatedly explain the same concepts through individual emails or office-hour conversations.

Accessibility, adaptations, and evaluation

Accessibility was supported by providing multiple ways to engage with the same content. Videos were posted with captions or transcripts when available, and accompanying materials included downloadable slides, worksheet prompts, completed examples or answer keys, and low-bandwidth PDFs of key figures. The workflow can be adapted across instructional settings. In lecture courses, muddiest-point prompts can be collected before an exam and converted into a targeted unit review. Although not initially used in the laboratory courses, the workflow could be adapted into shorter “muddy mini” videos that address procedural or interpretive skills such as plate reading, aseptic technique, microscope use, dilution calculations, or biochemical test interpretation. The workflow also has a natural extension to hybrid or online courses. In this implementation, absent students were invited to submit muddiest points by email; at larger scale, the same function could be handled through a Canvas survey, discussion prompt, or anonymous free-response form.

Several pitfalls can be avoided by keeping the review focused: one question, one diagram or worked example, and one takeaway per segment. Dense text-heavy slides should be replaced when possible with labeled visuals, live sketching, or guided worksheet prompts. Niche questions should be acknowledged but redirected to office hours or individual messages.

Instructors may evaluate the workflow through low-overhead measures such as video view counts, average watch time, repeated views, drop-off points, pulse surveys, or mapping quiz and exam items to video themes. If student data will be disseminated beyond local course improvement, instructors should consult their institutional review board or human research protections office.

CONCLUSION

This workflow extends the Muddiest Point technique from a brief classroom assessment into a scalable feedback system for large-enrollment microbiology courses. Its central contribution is the rapid conversion of individual uncertainties into a shared, instructor-generated review artifact. By clustering notecard responses, scripting focused review segments, and pairing a chaptered video with guided worksheet prompts, instructors can preserve diagnostic value while making timely response feasible at scale.

The approach links formative assessment with multimedia learning in a practical teaching routine: student questions determine the review structure, while chaptering, signaling, live sketching, pause-and-predict prompts, and worksheet fill-ins support active engagement. Over time, the resulting segments can be revised, recombined, or adapted for later semesters, final exam review, laboratory preparation, or targeted remediation.

The model does not replace office hours, discussion, or individualized support; instead, it helps reserve those interactions for questions that remain unresolved after common conceptual barriers have been addressed for the full class. For instructors seeking a scalable alternative to individualized replies, the workflow can be adopted with minimal new tools while preserving immediacy, instructor presence, and conceptual precision.
