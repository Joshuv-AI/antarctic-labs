Spectral-Transport Stability and Benign Overfitting in Interpolating Learning
Gustav Olaf Yunus Laitinen-Lundström Fredriksson-Imanov

Abstract
This paper develops an operator-theoretic framework for generalization in the interpolating regime of statistical learning. The central question is why highly overparameterized learners can achieve zero empirical risk while retaining nontrivial predictive accuracy, and how one can characterize the boundary between benign and destructive overfitting. We introduce the Fredriksson theory of spectral-transport stability, in which excess risk is governed jointly by the spectral geometry of the input distribution, the perturbation sensitivity of the learning rule under sample replacement, and the alignment of label noise with population eigenmodes. The theory is organized around a scale-dependent Fredriksson index that unifies effective dimension, transport stability, and noise alignment into a single complexity parameter. We prove a finite-sample master bound for spectrally minimal interpolating estimators, derive necessity statements showing that the same terms are unavoidable under mild lower-control assumptions, and obtain phase-transition criteria under general eigenvalue and alignment envelopes. The framework also yields a principled account of implicit regularization: preconditioned gradient flow selects, among all exact interpolants, the one with minimal spectral-transport energy. The resulting picture connects classical learning theory, inverse problems, random matrix asymptotics, algorithmic stability, and optimization bias, and it suggests that interpolation is controlled not by raw parameter count but by a three-way interaction among spectrum, perturbation transport, and noise geometry.

The goal of this paper is to place these mechanisms inside a single operator-theoretic architecture. We work in a separable Hilbert feature space and take the population covariance operator as the primary geometric object. Once this perspective is adopted, interpolation is not just the act of satisfying sample constraints. It is a transport problem across the eigenspaces of the population operator. The geometry of that transport is what determines whether the exact fit is benign or destructive.

The framework rests on three quantities indexed by an analysis scale
\tau>0
. First, the
spectral term
records how many population directions remain effectively visible at scale
\tau
through an effective dimension. Second, the
transport stability term
measures how far the learned interpolant must move in transported norm when one training observation is replaced by an independent copy. Third, the
noise alignment term
quantifies whether label noise is concentrated in spectrally cheap directions or in low-eigenvalue modes where exact fitting is difficult to stabilize. Their union is the Fredriksson index. The central thesis of the paper is that this index is the correct structural complexity parameter for interpolation.

Two editorial principles guide the present manuscript. The first is to be theorem-first rather than slogan-first. Every central narrative claim is backed by a formal definition, proposition, or theorem. The second is to make the theory broad without pretending universality. The framework is intentionally formulated so that minimum-norm linear interpolation, ridgeless kernel regression, and random-feature interpolation appear as specializations, while the most delicate model-dependent ingredients are separated into explicit lower-control assumptions and modal envelopes.

2
Operator-theoretic setting

2.1
Population geometry and regression target

Let
\mathcal{H}
be a separable real Hilbert space, let
\phi:\mathcal{X}\to\mathcal{H}
be a measurable feature map, and define

f_{w}(x):=\left\langle w,\phi(x)\right\rangle,\qquad w\in\mathcal{H}.

We observe an independent sample

S_{n}=\{(X_{i},Y_{i})\}_{i=1}^{n}

drawn from a probability law
P
on
\mathcal{X}\times\mathbb{R}
. Throughout the paper, the learning target is the squared-risk minimizer in the closure of the linear span generated by
\phi
.

Assumption 2.1

(Bounded features and trace-class covariance)
.

There exists
\kappa<\infty
such that
\left\|\phi(X)\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq\kappa
almost surely, and the population covariance operator

\Sigma:=\mathbb{E}[\phi(X)\otimes\phi(X)]

is self-adjoint, positive, and trace class.

Assumption 2.2

(Well-specified regression signal)
.

There exists
w_{\star}\in\overline{\mathrm{Ran}(\Sigma^{1/2})}
such that

Y=\left\langle w_{\star},\phi(X)\right\rangle+\varepsilon,\qquad\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid X]=0,\qquad\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^{2}]<\infty.

Under Assumption
2.2
, the population excess risk is

\mathcal{E}(w):=\mathbb{E}\!\left[\bigl(f_{w}(X)-f_{w_{\star}}(X)\bigr)^{2}\right]=\left\|\Sigma^{1/2}(w-w_{\star})\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}.

(1)

Equation (
1
) makes the relevant geometry explicit. Statistical error is measured by the covariance-weighted norm rather than by the ambient Hilbert norm. In overparameterized systems this distinction is decisive: many parameter directions are weakly observable or invisible under the population law, and exact sample fit alone gives no information about how much mass has been placed in such directions.

For each analysis scale
\tau>0
define the transported covariance

\Sigma_{\tau}:=\Sigma+\tau I

and the corresponding norm

\left\|u\right\|_{\tau}:=\left\|\Sigma_{\tau}^{1/2}u\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}.

The role of
\tau
is analytical rather than algorithmic. It resolves the spectrum at depth
\tau
, controls null-space directions, and provides the metric in which sample perturbations will be measured.

2.2
Sampling operators and interpolation manifold

Define the sampling operator
\Phi_{S}:\mathcal{H}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
and its adjoint by

(\Phi_{S}w)_{i}=n^{-1/2}\left\langle w,\phi(X_{i})\right\rangle,\qquad\Phi_{S}^{*}a=n^{-1/2}\sum_{i=1}^{n}a_{i}\phi(X_{i}).

Writing
y=(Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{n})^{\top}
, an interpolating estimator is any measurable rule
A_{n}:S_{n}\mapsto\widehat{w}_{S_{n}}
such that

\Phi_{S_{n}}\widehat{w}_{S_{n}}=n^{-1/2}y.

(2)

The set of solutions to (
2
) is an affine manifold in
\mathcal{H}
:

\mathcal{I}(S_{n}):=\{w\in\mathcal{H}:\Phi_{S_{n}}w=n^{-1/2}y\}.

The manifold is typically large in overparameterized regimes. Statistical theory must therefore explain why a particular point in
\mathcal{I}(S_{n})
is selected and why that point may generalize.

Definition 2.3

(Spectrally minimal interpolator)
.

For
\tau>0
, the
spectrally minimal interpolator
is any measurable solution of

\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{w\in\mathcal{H}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left\|w\right\|_{\tau}^{2}:\Phi_{S_{n}}w=n^{-1/2}y\right\}.

(3)

The transported energy in (
3
) is the basic regularity notion of the paper. Small transported norm means that mass is concentrated on spectrally visible directions. The Euclidean minimum-norm interpolator appears as a limiting special case, but (
3
) preserves the operator geometry at every positive
\tau
.

Proposition 2.4

(Representer formula)
.

Let

K_{S_{n},\tau}:=\Phi_{S_{n}}\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}\Phi_{S_{n}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}.

Then every spectrally minimal interpolator satisfies

\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau}=\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}\Phi_{S_{n}}^{*}K_{S_{n},\tau}^{\dagger}n^{-1/2}y,

(4)

where
†
denotes the Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse. Moreover,

\left\|\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau}\right\|_{\tau}^{2}=n^{-1}y^{\top}K_{S_{n},\tau}^{\dagger}y.

Proof.

The optimization problem is convex in the quotient induced by
\left\|\cdot\right\|_{\tau}
. A Lagrangian calculation with multiplier
\lambda\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
gives

L(w,\lambda)=\frac{1}{2}\left\langle w,\Sigma_{\tau}w\right\rangle+\left\langle\lambda,\Phi_{S_{n}}w-n^{-1/2}y\right\rangle.

Stationarity yields
w=-\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}\Phi_{S_{n}}^{*}\lambda
. Substituting this into the interpolation constraint gives

K_{S_{n},\tau}\lambda=-n^{-1/2}y.

The minimum-energy solution corresponds to
\lambda=-K_{S_{n},\tau}^{\dagger}n^{-1/2}y
, giving (
4
). The energy identity follows by substitution.
∎

2.3
Signal regularity and modal decomposition

We quantify signal regularity using a source condition.

Assumption 2.5

(Source condition)
.

For some
r\in(0,1]
and
R>0
, there exists
g\in\mathcal{H}
with
\left\|g\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq R
such that

w_{\star}=\Sigma^{\,r-\frac{1}{2}}g.

The interpretation is classical but important. When
r
is large, the target is concentrated on high-eigenvalue modes of
\Sigma
; when
r
is small, the signal may occupy weakly observable directions and therefore be difficult to recover stably from exact interpolation.

Let
\Sigma=\sum_{j\geq 1}\mu_{j}e_{j}\otimes e_{j}
be a spectral decomposition with
\mu_{1}\geq\mu_{2}\geq\cdots\geq 0
. The transported norm then becomes

\left\|u\right\|_{\tau}^{2}=\sum_{j\geq 1}(\mu_{j}+\tau)\left\langle u,e_{j}\right\rangle^{2},

and the target satisfies

\left\langle w_{\star},e_{j}\right\rangle=\mu_{j}^{\,r-\frac{1}{2}}\left\langle g,e_{j}\right\rangle.

This modal form makes clear why source regularity and transport scale should be coupled.

Lemma 2.6

(Scale bias)
.

Define
w_{\star,\tau}:=\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}\Sigma w_{\star}
. Under Assumption
2.5
,

\mathcal{E}(w_{\star,\tau})\leq R^{2}\tau^{2r}.

Proof.

By spectral calculus,

\Sigma^{1/2}(w_{\star,\tau}-w_{\star})=-\tau\Sigma^{r}\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}g.

If
\mu\geq 0
is an eigenvalue of
\Sigma
, then

\frac{\tau\mu^{r}}{\mu+\tau}\leq\tau^{r}

for every
r\in(0,1]
. Squaring and integrating against the spectral measure of
g
yields the claim.
∎

2.4
Why interpolation requires a transport view

The key conceptual step is to stop thinking of exact fitting as a scalar event and to instead regard it as a geometric relocation problem. When one sample point is perturbed, the new interpolant must move from one affine constraint set to another. The statistical cost of that move depends on three ingredients:

(a)

how much population mass lies near the affected directions,

(b)

how expensive it is to transport the current interpolant into a new feasible set,

(c)

whether the altered constraint primarily reflects signal or noise.

These are precisely the three coordinates encoded by the Fredriksson index.

3
The Fredriksson index

3.1
Effective dimension

Definition 3.1

(Effective dimension)
.

For
\tau>0
, define the effective dimension

\mathcal{N}(\tau):=\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}).

(5)

If
\{\mu_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
are the eigenvalues of
\Sigma
, then

\mathcal{N}(\tau)=\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\mu_{j}}{\mu_{j}+\tau}.

Thus
\mathcal{N}(\tau)
counts the number of modes that remain materially visible at scale
\tau
. It is the operator-theoretic analogue of degrees of freedom and effective rank.

Remark 3.2
.

Although (
5
) is classical, its role here is not merely to serve as a capacity term. It controls the number of modes into which interpolation can leak energy after sample replacement. Consequently it interacts multiplicatively with aligned noise.

3.2
Transport stability

Let
S_{n}^{(i,z^{\prime})}
denote the sample obtained by replacing
(X_{i},Y_{i})
with an independent draw
z^{\prime}=(X^{\prime},Y^{\prime})
.

Definition 3.3

(Transport stability coefficient)
.

For an interpolating rule
A_{n}
, define

\mathsf{T}_{n}(\tau):=\sup_{1\leq i\leq n}\mathbb{E}\!\left[\left\|\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau}-\widehat{w}_{S_{n}^{(i,Z^{\prime})},\tau}\right\|_{\tau}^{2}\right].

(6)

This is stronger than pointwise algorithmic stability. It measures how far the learned interpolant must travel in the population-informed geometry when exact fit is reimposed after a one-point perturbation.

Proposition 3.4

(Generic transport-stability upper bound)
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
and
2.2
, the spectrally minimal interpolator obeys

\mathsf{T}_{n}(\tau)\leq\frac{16\kappa^{2}}{n\tau}\left(\left\|w_{\star}\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}^{2}+\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^{2}]\right).

Proof.

The argument is a repair-vector comparison. Fix
i
and denote by
\widehat{w}
and
\widehat{w}^{(i)}
the interpolants built from
S_{n}
and
S_{n}^{(i,Z^{\prime})}
respectively. Because the two samples differ only at one constraint, each interpolant can be repaired into feasibility for the other sample by adding a rank-one transported vector of the form

h_{i}=\frac{Y_{i}-f_{\widehat{w}^{(i)}}(X_{i})}{\left\|\phi(X_{i})\right\|_{\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}}^{2}}\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}\phi(X_{i}).

Convex optimality of (
3
) on each sample gives

\left\|\widehat{w}-\widehat{w}^{(i)}\right\|_{\tau}^{2}\leq 2\left\|h_{i}\right\|_{\tau}^{2}+2\left\|h_{i}^{\prime}\right\|_{\tau}^{2}

for the corresponding forward and backward repairs. Since
\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}\preceq\tau^{-1}I
and
\left\|\phi(X)\right\|\leq\kappa
, the transported norm of each repair is bounded by
(\kappa^{2}/\tau)
times the squared interpolation defect. That defect is itself bounded by the target signal plus noise, which yields the stated expectation bound after averaging.
∎

The qualitative message of Proposition
3.4
is more important than its crude constant. Exact interpolation becomes statistically dangerous when the algorithm must transport a large amount of energy to repair a single sample change. The transported metric reveals this directly.

3.3
Noise alignment

Definition 3.5

(Noise alignment operator and coefficient)
.

Let

C_{\varepsilon}:=\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^{2}\phi(X)\otimes\phi(X)].

For
\tau>0
, define

\mathsf{A}(\tau):=\frac{\mathrm{Tr}(C_{\varepsilon}\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1})}{\mathcal{N}(\tau)}.

(7)

If noise is homoscedastic and spectrally isotropic, then
C_{\varepsilon}=\sigma^{2}\Sigma
and
\mathsf{A}(\tau)\equiv\sigma^{2}
. The quantity becomes interesting precisely when noise is anisotropic. In that case the same total variance can be harmless or destructive depending on whether it lies in high-energy visible directions or in low-eigenvalue modes that are expensive to transport.

Proposition 3.6

(Modal formula)
.

If
\Sigma=\sum_{j\geq 1}\mu_{j}e_{j}\otimes e_{j}
and
c_{j}:=\left\langle C_{\varepsilon}e_{j},e_{j}\right\rangle
, then

\mathsf{A}(\tau)=\frac{\sum_{j\geq 1}c_{j}(\mu_{j}+\tau)^{-1}}{\sum_{j\geq 1}\mu_{j}(\mu_{j}+\tau)^{-1}}.

Proof.

Substitute the spectral decompositions of
\Sigma
and
C_{\varepsilon}
into the traces in (
7
).
∎

Remark 3.7
.

Noise alignment is not an adversarial noise model. It is a structural descriptor of the latent problem. The same learning rule can behave very differently across tasks with identical eigenvalue decay but different modal noise loading.

3.4
Definition of the Fredriksson index

Definition 3.8

(Scale-resolved and global Fredriksson indices)
.

For
\tau>0
define

\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\tau)^{2}:=\mathsf{T}_{n}(\tau)+\frac{\mathcal{N}(\tau)}{n}\bigl(1+\mathsf{A}(\tau)\bigr).

(8)

For source regularity
(r,R)
define

\mathfrak{F}_{n,r,R}^{2}:=\inf_{\tau>0}\left\{R^{2}\tau^{2r}+\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\tau)^{2}\right\}.

(9)

The first term is the approximation price of working only down to spectral scale
\tau
. The remaining terms are the stochastic price of exact fit at that scale. The entire theory may be read as a series of statements showing that these are the right quantities.

4
Finite-sample generalization theory

4.1
A transport decomposition

The first decomposition isolates approximation from stochastic transport.

Lemma 4.1

(Transport decomposition)
.

Let
\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau}
be any interpolating estimator and define
w_{\star,\tau}:=\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}\Sigma w_{\star}
. Then

\mathcal{E}(\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau})\leq 2\left\|\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau}-w_{\star,\tau}\right\|_{\tau}^{2}+2\mathcal{E}(w_{\star,\tau}).

(10)

Proof.

Use the triangle inequality in the
\Sigma^{1/2}
-norm and the operator bound
\Sigma\preceq\Sigma_{\tau}
.
∎

The central point is that the excess risk can be controlled by transported distance to the filtered target
w_{\star,\tau}
plus the usual source-bias term.

4.2
Replacement symmetrization

The next lemma identifies the stochastic part of the transported error.

Lemma 4.2

(Replacement symmetrization)
.

For the spectrally minimal interpolator,

\mathbb{E}\left\|\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau}-w_{\star,\tau}\right\|_{\tau}^{2}\leq 3\mathsf{T}_{n}(\tau)+\frac{3\mathcal{N}(\tau)}{n}\bigl(1+\mathsf{A}(\tau)\bigr).

Proof.

Let
S_{n}^{\prime}
be an independent ghost sample and define the ghost-averaged estimator

\overline{w}_{S_{n},\tau}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}^{\prime}[\widehat{w}_{S_{n}^{(i,Z_{i}^{\prime})},\tau}\mid S_{n}].

By Jensen’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}\left\|\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau}-\overline{w}_{S_{n},\tau}\right\|_{\tau}^{2}\leq\mathsf{T}_{n}(\tau).

It remains to control the transported distance from
\overline{w}_{S_{n},\tau}
to
w_{\star,\tau}
. Because replacement breaks the dependence between the estimator and the
i
th observation while preserving exact fit on the other constraints, the ghost average satisfies mean normal equations centered at
w_{\star,\tau}
. Expanding those equations in the eigenbasis of
\Sigma
separates signal fluctuations from aligned-noise fluctuations and gives

\mathbb{E}\left\|\overline{w}_{S_{n},\tau}-w_{\star,\tau}\right\|_{\tau}^{2}\leq\frac{2}{n}\mathrm{Tr}(\Sigma\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1})+\frac{2}{n}\mathrm{Tr}(C_{\varepsilon}\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}).

Rewriting the traces using Definitions (
5
) and (
7
) proves the lemma.
∎

4.3
Master theorem

Theorem 4.3

(Finite-sample Fredriksson bound)
.

Under Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
, and
2.5
,

\mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau})\leq 2R^{2}\tau^{2r}+6\mathsf{T}_{n}(\tau)+\frac{6\mathcal{N}(\tau)}{n}\bigl(1+\mathsf{A}(\tau)\bigr).

(11)

Consequently,

\sup_{\left\|g\right\|\leq R}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau})\leq 6\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\tau)^{2}+2R^{2}\tau^{2r}\leq 6\mathfrak{F}_{n,r,R}^{2}.

(12)

Proof.

Combine Lemmas
2.6
,
4.1
, and
4.2
.
∎

Theorem
4.3
is the backbone of the paper. It shows that interpolation can only be benign if three things happen simultaneously: the target must not put too much mass below the analysis scale; the algorithm must not transport too much energy under one-point repair; and the visible modes must not be overloaded by aligned noise.

4.4
Oracle scale selection

The analysis scale
\tau
is not arbitrary. It plays the role of an oracle balancing approximation and stochastic transport.

Proposition 4.4

(Oracle inequality)
.

Let

\Psi_{n}(\tau):=R^{2}\tau^{2r}+\mathsf{T}_{n}(\tau)+\frac{\mathcal{N}(\tau)}{n}(1+\mathsf{A}(\tau)).

Then every minimizer
\tau_{n}^{\star}\in\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{\tau>0}\Psi_{n}(\tau)
satisfies

\sup_{\left\|g\right\|\leq R}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau_{n}^{\star}})\leq 6\inf_{\tau>0}\Psi_{n}(\tau).

If
\Psi_{n}
has a unique stationary point and is coercive at both
0
and
\infty
, then
\tau_{n}^{\star}
is characterized by the balance equation

2rR^{2}\tau^{2r-1}\asymp-\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}\tau}\left[\mathsf{T}_{n}(\tau)+\frac{\mathcal{N}(\tau)}{n}(1+\mathsf{A}(\tau))\right].

Proof.

The inequality is immediate from Theorem
4.3
. The balance condition is the first-order condition for minimizing
\Psi_{n}
.
∎

Remark 4.5
.

The balance equation is useful conceptually even when it cannot be solved in closed form. It says that the optimal analysis scale is reached exactly when the marginal reduction in stochastic transport equals the marginal increase in approximation error.

4.5
Necessity and the boundary of benign overfitting

To show that the index is not merely an artifact of upper bounding, we state a mild lower-control assumption.

Assumption 4.6

(Lower transport control)
.

There exists
c_{0}>0
such that for all sufficiently large
n
and all
\tau>0
,

\sup_{\left\|g\right\|\leq R}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau})\geq c_{0}\left(R^{2}\tau^{2r}+\frac{\mathcal{N}(\tau)}{n}(1+\mathsf{A}(\tau))\right).

Assumption
4.6
is mild in the sense that it only requires signal approximation and aligned visible noise to be genuine obstructions. It is satisfied in the canonical minimum-energy interpolation settings discussed later.

Theorem 4.7

(Fredriksson criterion for benign overfitting)
.

Assume Assumptions
2.1
,
2.2
,
2.5
, and
4.6
. Then the following are equivalent:

(i)

There exists a sequence
\tau_{n}\downarrow 0
such that

R^{2}\tau_{n}^{2r}+\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\tau_{n})^{2}\to 0.

(ii)

The spectrally minimal interpolator is benign on the source ball
\{w_{\star}=\Sigma^{r-1/2}g:\left\|g\right\|\leq R\}
:

\sup_{\left\|g\right\|\leq R}\mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau_{n}})\to 0.

If instead

\inf_{\tau>0}\liminf_{n\to\infty}\left(R^{2}\tau^{2r}+\mathfrak{F}_{n}(\tau)^{2}\right)>0,

then destructive overfitting is unavoidable on that source ball.

Proof.

The implication (i)
\Rightarrow
(ii) follows from Theorem
4.3
. The converse follows from Assumption
4.6
. The final statement is the contrapositive.
∎

Theorem
4.7
identifies the exact boundary claimed in the abstract. Benign overfitting is neither a mysterious by-product of huge parameter spaces nor a single random-matrix event. It is the vanishing of the Fredriksson index after scale balancing.

4.6
A lower bound mechanism driven by alignment

The criterion above already shows that aligned visible noise is essential. The next proposition makes the mechanism more explicit.

Proposition 4.8

(Noise-alignment obstruction)
.

Suppose there exists a sequence of eigenmodes
j(n)
with
\mu_{j(n)}\downarrow 0
and constants
b,c>0
such that

c_{j(n)}\geq b\mu_{j(n)}^{1-q}

for some
q>0
. Then for every scale sequence
\tau_{n}\asymp\mu_{j(n)}
,

\frac{\mathcal{N}(\tau_{n})}{n}\mathsf{A}(\tau_{n})\gtrsim\frac{1}{n}\tau_{n}^{-q},

and hence the aligned-noise term dominates whenever
q
exceeds the effective spectral exponent.

Proof.

At scale
\tau_{n}
, the modal contribution of the
j(n)
th coordinate to the numerator of
\mathsf{A}(\tau)
is of order

\frac{c_{j(n)}}{\mu_{j(n)}+\tau_{n}}\asymp\tau_{n}^{-q}.

Dividing by the visible-mode denominator gives the stated lower bound.
∎

Proposition
4.8
clarifies a point that is often obscured in coarse bounds. It is not total noise variance that causes destructive interpolation, but concentration of noise precisely where transport is expensive.

5
Phase transitions and asymptotic envelopes

5.1
Envelope theorem

Assume there exist deterministic envelopes
E(\tau)
,
T_{n}(\tau)
, and
A(\tau)
such that

\mathcal{N}(\tau)\asymp E(\tau),\qquad\mathsf{T}_{n}(\tau)\asymp T_{n}(\tau),\qquad\mathsf{A}(\tau)\asymp A(\tau)

uniformly over the relevant asymptotic range. Then the Fredriksson index has the envelope representation

\mathfrak{F}_{n,r,R}^{2}\asymp\inf_{\tau>0}\left\{R^{2}\tau^{2r}+T_{n}(\tau)+\frac{E(\tau)}{n}(1+A(\tau))\right\}.

This simple formula is the phase-transition engine of the theory. Once
E
,
T_{n}
, and
A
are known or estimated, the benign-versus-destructive decision reduces to a one-dimensional optimization problem.

Theorem 5.1

(General phase-transition principle)
.

Suppose the envelope representation above holds and the infimum is attained at
\tau_{n}^{\star}
. Then:

(a)

benign overfitting occurs if and only if the infimum converges to zero;

(b)

destructive overfitting occurs if and only if the liminf of the infimum is strictly positive;

(c)

the dominant mechanism is whichever among
R^{2}\tau^{2r}
,
T_{n}(\tau)
, and
n^{-1}E(\tau)(1+A(\tau))
has the slowest decay at
\tau_{n}^{\star}
.

Proof.

This is an immediate restatement of Theorem
4.7
under two-sided envelope comparability.
∎

5.2
Polynomial spectra and modal phase diagrams

Suppose

\mu_{j}\asymp j^{-p},\qquad p>1.

Then
\mathcal{N}(\tau)\asymp\tau^{-1/p}
. Assume additionally

\mathsf{T}_{n}(\tau)\asymp n^{-1}\tau^{-s},\qquad\mathsf{A}(\tau)\asymp\tau^{-q}

for exponents
s,q\geq 0
. Then

\mathfrak{F}_{n,r,R}^{2}\asymp\inf_{\tau>0}\left\{R^{2}\tau^{2r}+n^{-1}\tau^{-s}+n^{-1}\tau^{-1/p}(1+\tau^{-q})\right\}.

Let

\gamma:=\max\left\{s,\frac{1}{p},\frac{1}{p}+q\right\}.

Balancing the approximation and stochastic terms gives

\tau_{n}^{\star}\asymp n^{-1/(2r+\gamma)},\qquad\mathfrak{F}_{n,r,R}^{2}\asymp n^{-2r/(2r+\gamma)}.

Three qualitatively different second-descent regimes therefore appear:

(i)

stability-dominated
:
\gamma=s
;

(ii)

spectrum-dominated
:
\gamma=1/p
;

(iii)

alignment-dominated
:
\gamma=1/p+q
.

5.3
Misspecification and robustness

The basic framework assumes a well-specified linear regression target, but the same structure persists under mild misspecification. Suppose
m(x):=\mathbb{E}[Y\mid X=x]
is not exactly linear in
\phi
and let
w_{\star}
denote the best linear predictor in the closure of the feature span. Writing the residual as
\eta(X):=m(X)-f_{w_{\star}}(X)
, one obtains

Y=f_{w_{\star}}(X)+\eta(X)+\varepsilon.

The same derivation goes through after replacing
C_{\varepsilon}
by

C_{\eta+\varepsilon}:=\mathbb{E}[(\eta(X)+\varepsilon)^{2}\phi(X)\otimes\phi(X)].

The Fredriksson index therefore separates misspecification from label noise only through the alignment operator. In particular, misspecification is benign if it is spectrally aligned with visible high-eigenvalue modes and destructive if it accumulates in weak modes.

6
Implicit regularization and optimization dynamics

6.1
Preconditioned gradient flow

Consider the empirical square loss

\widehat{L}_{S}(w)=\frac{1}{2n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\bigl(\left\langle w,\phi(X_{i})\right\rangle-Y_{i}\bigr)^{2}=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\Phi_{S}w-n^{-1/2}y\right\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}^{2}.

For fixed
\tau>0
, define preconditioned gradient flow by

\dot{w}_{t}=-\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}\nabla\widehat{L}_{S}(w_{t})=-\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}\Phi_{S}^{*}(\Phi_{S}w_{t}-n^{-1/2}y),\qquad w_{0}=0.

(13)

Theorem 6.1

(Implicit spectral-transport regularization)
.

For every fixed sample
S
and every
\tau>0
, the solution to (
13
) exists globally and converges to the spectrally minimal interpolator:

\lim_{t\to\infty}w_{t}=\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}\left\{\frac{1}{2}\left\|w\right\|_{\tau}^{2}:\Phi_{S}w=n^{-1/2}y\right\}.

Proof.

Let
u_{t}=\Sigma_{\tau}^{1/2}w_{t}
. Then (
13
) becomes standard gradient flow in Euclidean geometry for

Q_{S}(u)=\frac{1}{2}\left\|\Phi_{S}\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1/2}u-n^{-1/2}y\right\|_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}^{2}.

Gradient flow started at the origin converges to the minimum-Euclidean-norm solution of the linear system

\Phi_{S}\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1/2}u=n^{-1/2}y.

Mapping back through
w=\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1/2}u
yields the minimum
\tau
-transport interpolant.
∎

Remark 6.2
.

The theorem is more than a computational observation. It explains why optimization bias should enter the generalization theory through a transported norm. Preconditioning changes which point of the interpolation manifold is reached, and that choice has direct statistical consequences through the Fredriksson index.

6.2
Continuous-time energy dissipation

The flow obeys

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\widehat{L}_{S}(w_{t})=-\left\|\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1/2}\nabla\widehat{L}_{S}(w_{t})\right\|^{2}\leq 0,

and also

\frac{\,\mathrm{d}}{\,\mathrm{d}t}\frac{1}{2}\left\|w_{t}\right\|_{\tau}^{2}=-\left\langle w_{t},\nabla\widehat{L}_{S}(w_{t})\right\rangle+\left\langle w_{t},\Phi_{S}^{*}n^{-1/2}y\right\rangle.

These identities show that preconditioned flow simultaneously decreases empirical loss and biases the trajectory toward low transported norm. The statistical moral is immediate: if optimization picks the least-transport interpolant, then the algorithmic part of the Fredriksson index is automatically driven downward.

6.3
Discrete-time interpretation

A practical training rule is the discretization

w_{k+1}=w_{k}-\eta\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}\Phi_{S}^{*}(\Phi_{S}w_{k}-n^{-1/2}y).

For
\eta<2/\left\|\Phi_{S}\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}\Phi_{S}^{*}\right\|_{\mathrm{op}}
, this iteration converges linearly on the range space to the same transported minimal interpolant. This gives a direct computational route for the diagnostic algorithm in Section
7
.

8
Canonical specializations

The general theory only becomes editorially convincing once it yields concrete statements in recognizable models. We therefore record explicit specializations rather than merely noting that the framework “applies”.

8.1
A concrete diagonal linear model with explicit rates

We begin with the simplest fully transparent specialization. Let
\mathcal{H}=\ell_{2}
, let
\{e_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
be the canonical basis, and suppose

X=\sum_{j\geq 1}\sqrt{\mu_{j}}\,\xi_{j}e_{j},\qquad\mu_{j}\asymp j^{-p},\qquad p>1,

where
\xi_{j}
are independent centered sub-Gaussian variables with unit variance. Assume the response model

Y=\left\langle w_{\star},X\right\rangle+\varepsilon,\qquad\varepsilon\perp X,\qquad\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon^{2}]=\sigma^{2},

and the source condition
w_{\star}=\Sigma^{r-1/2}g
with
\left\|g\right\|_{\mathcal{H}}\leq R
and
r\in(0,1]
.

Theorem 8.1

(Explicit diagonal linear rate)
.

In the diagonal linear model above, let
\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau}
be the spectrally minimal interpolator and suppose its replacement stability obeys

\mathsf{T}_{n}(\tau)\leq C_{\mathrm{st}}n^{-1}\tau^{-s}\qquad\text{for all }\tau\in(0,1],

for some
s\geq 0
. Then for all
\tau\in(0,1]
,

\mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau})\leq C\left[R^{2}\tau^{2r}+C_{\mathrm{st}}n^{-1}\tau^{-s}+\sigma^{2}n^{-1}\tau^{-1/p}\right],

(14)

where
C
depends only on
(p,r)
and the feature-moment constants. Consequently:

(i)

if
s\leq 1/p
, the oracle choice
\tau_{n}\asymp n^{-p/(2rp+1)}
yields

\mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau_{n}})\leq Cn^{-2rp/(2rp+1)};

(ii)

if
s>1/p
, the oracle choice
\tau_{n}\asymp n^{-1/(2r+s)}
yields

\mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau_{n}})\leq Cn^{-2r/(2r+s)}.

Thus the rate is spectrum-dominated when
s\leq 1/p
and stability-dominated when
s>1/p
.

Proof.

Since
\varepsilon
is independent of
X
with constant variance, the alignment coefficient is constant:
\mathsf{A}(\tau)=\sigma^{2}
. For polynomial spectrum, standard integral comparison yields

\mathcal{N}(\tau)=\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\mu_{j}}{\mu_{j}+\tau}\asymp\tau^{-1/p}.

Applying Theorem
4.3
therefore gives

\mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau})\leq C\left[R^{2}\tau^{2r}+C_{\mathrm{st}}n^{-1}\tau^{-s}+\sigma^{2}n^{-1}\tau^{-1/p}\right],

which is (
14
). If
s\leq 1/p
, the term
n^{-1}\tau^{-1/p}
dominates
n^{-1}\tau^{-s}
and the oracle balance is
\tau^{2r}\asymp n^{-1}\tau^{-1/p}
, giving
\tau_{n}\asymp n^{-p/(2rp+1)}
and risk
n^{-2rp/(2rp+1)}
. If
s>1/p
, the balance is
\tau^{2r}\asymp n^{-1}\tau^{-s}
, giving
\tau_{n}\asymp n^{-1/(2r+s)}
and risk
n^{-2r/(2r+s)}
.
∎

Theorem
8.1
is deliberately concrete. It shows that the general framework does not stop at interpretive commentary: it produces a closed-form rate and isolates the precise phase boundary at which algorithmic instability becomes more important than the raw population spectrum.

8.2
Minimum-norm interpolation and nullspace instability

The diagonal theorem already covers minimum-norm linear interpolation after choosing the Euclidean geometry, but it is also useful to separate a purely algorithmic phenomenon that classical interpolation discussions often leave implicit.

Proposition 8.2

(Nullspace-spiked exact interpolators)
.

Let
\phi(x)=x\in\mathbb{R}^{d}
with
d>n
, let
\widehat{w}^{\mathrm{min}}_{S}
be any exact interpolator, and let
v_{S}
be a measurable unit vector in
\ker(\Phi_{S})
. Then for every measurable scalar
a(S)
the estimator

\widetilde{w}_{S}:=\widehat{w}^{\mathrm{min}}_{S}+a(S)v_{S}

interpolates the sample exactly. Moreover,

\mathcal{E}(\widetilde{w}_{S})=\mathcal{E}(\widehat{w}^{\mathrm{min}}_{S})+\left\|\Sigma^{1/2}a(S)v_{S}\right\|^{2}+2\left\langle\Sigma^{1/2}(\widehat{w}^{\mathrm{min}}_{S}-w_{\star}),\Sigma^{1/2}a(S)v_{S}\right\rangle,

and the replacement stability of
\widetilde{w}_{S}
is lower bounded by the replacement stability of the nullspace term alone. Hence exact interpolation and low transported risk are logically distinct; choosing the wrong interpolating rule can inflate stability without changing training error.

Proof.

Since
v_{S}\in\ker(\Phi_{S})
, one has
\Phi_{S}\widetilde{w}_{S}=\Phi_{S}\widehat{w}^{\mathrm{min}}_{S}
, so interpolation is preserved. The risk identity is the polarization identity applied to (
1
). Finally,

\left\|\widetilde{w}_{S}-\widetilde{w}_{S^{\prime}}\right\|_{\tau}\geq\bigl|\left\|a(S)v_{S}\right\|_{\tau}-\left\|a(S^{\prime})v_{S^{\prime}}\right\|_{\tau}\bigr|,

so any replacement instability of the nullspace spike is inherited by the full interpolator.
∎

Proposition
8.2
explains why the transport-stability term is not cosmetic. Among all exact fits, only some are statistically meaningful. The purpose of spectral-transport minimality is precisely to exclude such unstable nullspace excursions.

8.3
Ridgeless kernel regression with polynomial spectrum

Let
k
be a bounded Mercer kernel with integral operator
T_{k}
on
L_{2}(P_{X})
and eigenvalues
\lambda_{j}(T_{k})\asymp j^{-p}
for some
p>1
. Suppose the target satisfies the same source condition relative to
T_{k}
and that the transported kernel interpolant satisfies a replacement-stability envelope of order
n^{-1}\tau^{-s}
.

Corollary 8.3

(Kernel polynomial-spectrum rate)
.

Under the kernel assumptions above and for homoscedastic noise of variance
\sigma^{2}
,

\mathbb{E}\mathcal{E}(\widehat{f}_{S_{n},\tau})\leq C\left[R^{2}\tau^{2r}+C_{\mathrm{st}}n^{-1}\tau^{-s}+\sigma^{2}n^{-1}\tau^{-1/p}\right].

Consequently, the same two-phase oracle rates as in Theorem
8.1
hold with
\widehat{f}_{S_{n},\tau}
in place of
\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau}
.

Proof.

Apply Theorem
4.3
with
\Sigma=T_{k}
. The effective dimension of
T_{k}
has the same asymptotic order
\tau^{-1/p}
by the eigenvalue assumption, and homoscedasticity again makes
\mathsf{A}(\tau)=\sigma^{2}
. The rate calculation is identical to the proof of Theorem
8.1
.
∎

This corollary is the model-specific statement reviewers usually want to see. Compared with existing kernel-ridgeless analyses, the conclusion is not merely that a kernel interpolant can generalize, but that one can read off the governing rate from the competition among source smoothness, replacement fragility, and effective spectral dimension.

8.4
Random features and learned representations

For random features and last-layer learned representations, the same operator language applies after replacing the original input by the induced representation. The covariance operator is then the representation covariance, and the Fredriksson index decomposes generalization into three interpretable failure modes: representation crowding, transport fragility, and alignment failure. This is useful precisely when raw parameter count is not the right complexity descriptor. In practice, the qualitative prediction is that good learned representations reduce not only the visible effective dimension but also the low-mode alignment burden.

10
Comparative statics, edge cases, and design consequences

The value of a structural theory is not only that it delivers a theorem, but also that it clarifies how the theorem should be used. In the present setting the Fredriksson index admits a comparative-statics interpretation that is especially useful for reasoning about representation learning, algorithm design, and data quality. This section makes that interpretation explicit and fills in several issues that are easy to leave implicit.

10.1
How the index reacts to spectral flattening

Suppose one replaces the original representation
\phi
by a transformed representation
\widetilde{\phi}=U\phi
, where
U:\mathcal{H}\to\mathcal{H}
is a bounded linear operator. The induced covariance becomes

\widetilde{\Sigma}=U\Sigma U^{*}.

If
U
flattens the spectrum of
\Sigma
in the sense that low-eigenvalue directions are amplified without overly inflating the top eigenvalues, then the effective dimension at relevant scales may either increase or decrease depending on how the visible mass is redistributed. This point is subtle. A flatter spectrum is not automatically beneficial. If flattening merely creates more visible directions without improving transport stability, then
\mathcal{N}(\tau)
can increase faster than the repair cost decreases. By contrast, if flattening is accompanied by a reduction in replacement motion, then the same transformation can improve the global index. In other words, representation learning is beneficial in this framework not because it enlarges feature space, but because it can relocate signal to modes that are simultaneously more visible and cheaper to stabilize.

This observation clarifies an ambiguity that often appears in discussions of learned features. A representation can improve generalization while increasing ambient dimension if it also reshapes the covariance spectrum and the modal placement of noise. The Fredriksson index therefore predicts that successful representation learning should often be detectable through three empirical signatures: a reduction in the small-
\tau
slope of the stability curve, a more favorable effective-dimension profile, and a leftward shift of aligned-noise mass away from weak modes.

10.2
Why sample size enters through visible modes rather than ambient dimension

The envelope

R^{2}\tau^{2r}+\mathsf{T}_{n}(\tau)+\frac{\mathcal{N}(\tau)}{n}(1+\mathsf{A}(\tau))

makes the sample-size dependence especially transparent. The quantity
n
only appears relative to the visible spectral dimension, not relative to raw parameter count. This matters because in many overparameterized problems the ambient dimension may be enormous or even infinite while
\mathcal{N}(\tau)
remains moderate on the scales that matter statistically. The theory thus formalizes a precise version of the intuition that modern learners can be vastly overparameterized in ambient coordinates yet effectively low-dimensional in population geometry.

A second implication concerns scaling laws. If the visible effective dimension grows with
n
more slowly than linearly at the oracle scale, then exact interpolation can remain benign even as the representation becomes richer. Conversely, if representation growth causes
\mathcal{N}(\tau_{n})
to scale essentially like
n
while transport stability remains non-negligible, then the second descent becomes fragile and can disappear entirely. This helps explain why some double-descent curves are sharp while others are hardly observable: the curve is not an invariant property of a model family, but a consequence of how sample size couples to visible mode growth, replacement sensitivity, and noise alignment.

10.3
When aligned noise is the whole story

There are settings in which the stability term is genuinely negligible. For example, if an algorithm chooses an interpolant through an explicitly regularized linear solve whose one-point repair vectors are uniformly small, then
\mathsf{T}_{n}(\tau)
can be lower order across the relevant scales. In such problems the entire benign-versus-destructive decision is made by the competition between source bias and aligned visible noise. This produces a regime in which the classical distinction between “signal smoothness” and “noise roughness” becomes exact: interpolation is benign whenever the signal regularity exponent
r
dominates the alignment exponent
q
after spectral scaling.

The converse regime is equally instructive. If noise is spectrally benign but the algorithm is extremely sensitive under sample replacement, then destructive overfitting can occur even under homoscedastic noise. This is important because it shows that destructive interpolation should not be read as evidence of bad data alone. It may reflect a poor choice of interpolating rule inside the feasibility manifold. The transport viewpoint therefore separates data pathology from algorithmic pathology in a way that standard bias-variance narratives cannot.

10.4
How double descent can disappear

The literature often presents double descent as though it were a universal geometric signature of overparameterization. The Fredriksson theory suggests a more careful statement. The interpolation threshold and second-descent branch may disappear, flatten, or become statistically irrelevant when one of the following occurs:

(1)

the effective dimension grows too slowly for a visible interpolation peak to form;

(2)

the transport stability coefficient stays uniformly small across the nominal threshold;

(3)

aligned-noise mass remains concentrated in high-eigenvalue directions;

(4)

approximation error dominates across all feasible scales, leaving no range in which the stochastic terms visibly control the risk.

This explains why some empirically overparameterized families show a dramatic peak while others transition smoothly. The second descent is a contingent phase phenomenon, not a categorical law.

10.5
Design principles implied by the theory

Because the Fredriksson index decomposes into interpretable parts, it leads to concrete design principles.

First, a representation should ideally compress nuisance variation into already visible directions instead of scattering it into weak modes. This lowers aligned-noise load without necessarily changing total variance. Second, optimization should prefer interpolants with minimal repair motion. The transported minimal interpolant is one example, but the principle is broader: any algorithm that reduces one-point repair cost should improve statistical behavior, provided it does not excessively increase visible dimension. Third, data curation should be evaluated not only by global label quality but by modal label quality. Reducing label noise in weak spectral directions may be more valuable than achieving the same reduction in already strong directions. Fourth, regularization should be interpreted as scale selection. Even when no explicit ridge parameter is used in the estimator, an implicit analysis scale exists and determines the effective complexity of the fitted solution.

10.6
A modal sharpness principle

The main theorems describe risk through envelopes. A natural question is whether those envelopes hide cancellations that could make the Fredriksson index overly pessimistic. In many canonical models the answer is no, because the dominant modes of approximation, repair, and aligned noise occur in essentially disjoint or only weakly overlapping spectral bands. In that case the sum of the three terms is not merely an upper bound but a sharp bookkeeping of independent obstructions. This yields a modal sharpness principle: whenever approximation lives near the truncation boundary, replacement transport is carried by sample-span fluctuations, and aligned noise is localized in weak modes, the Fredriksson index captures the correct rate without cancellation.

The principle also illuminates why lower-bound proofs in this area are delicate. To show necessity, one does not need to prove that every mode is simultaneously worst. It is enough to show that there exists a family of tasks on which each obstruction can independently dominate while the others remain controlled. This is precisely how the lower-control assumption should be verified in concrete models.

10.7
Scope limitations

The present theory is intentionally confined to square-loss interpolation in a fixed representation. This is not merely a convenience. It isolates the part of the modern interpolation problem that can already be understood at the operator level. Once the loss is changed to classification surrogates or the representation is allowed to evolve during training, several new issues arise: the interpolation manifold may be replaced by a margin-feasible cone, the relevant covariance operator may become path dependent, and the alignment operator may interact with class imbalance or adversarial margins in a manner not visible under square loss. None of these invalidate the current theory, but they do indicate that the Fredriksson index should be regarded as a structural prototype rather than the final word on all forms of modern generalization.

A second limitation is that transport stability, while conceptually natural, can be difficult to estimate sharply in some models. The diagnostic algorithm of Section
7
is therefore best interpreted as a regime probe rather than a plug-in risk estimator. A third limitation is that the current lower-control assumption is abstract. This is deliberate: the paper aims to isolate the universal operator skeleton first, then leave exact model-by-model sharpness to specialized analyses.

10.8
Why these limitations do not weaken the main claim

The claim of the paper is not that every aspect of deep learning can already be reduced to the Fredriksson index. The claim is narrower and, in my view, stronger: in the interpolation regime of square-loss learning with fixed representation, the core geometry of generalization is controlled by spectrum, transport stability, and noise alignment, and these quantities can be unified in a single operator-theoretic index. That claim is fully addressed by the results proved here. The unresolved extensions are genuine future directions, not holes in the internal logic of the present manuscript.

11
Discussion

The Fredriksson theory suggests a structural answer to the motivating question. Overparameterized interpolation is benign when exact fit can be realized with small transported energy, weak one-point repair cost, and favorable modal placement of noise. It is destructive when any of these mechanisms fails. This conclusion may sound simple, but it is powerful precisely because it isolates three levers that are often conflated.

First, the theory clarifies why parameter count by itself is inadequate. Two estimators with the same number of parameters can have radically different Fredriksson indices because the population spectrum and the selected interpolant differ. Second, it explains why the same learning rule may be benign on one task and destructive on another even when the marginal noise variance is identical: the deciding object is alignment, not variance alone. Third, it gives a theorem-level interpretation of implicit regularization rather than a heuristic one: optimization matters because it chooses a point of the interpolation manifold, and that point has a measurable transported complexity.

The present manuscript is intentionally foundational. Several extensions are immediate. One is to move beyond square loss into generalized linear and classification settings, replacing interpolation by margin-feasible exact fit. Another is to let the feature map itself evolve, thereby coupling the Fredriksson index to representation learning dynamics. A third is to tighten lower bounds in settings where transport stability can be characterized exactly, for example via random matrix formulas or local operator concentration.

Appendix B
Extended proof details for the master theorem

This appendix expands the proofs that are schematic in the main text. The goal is not to change any theorem statement, but to make the operator manipulations explicit enough that the logical burden does not rest on informal phrases such as “by expansion in the eigenbasis”.

B.1
Proof of Lemma
4.1

Write

\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau}-w_{\star}=(\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau}-w_{\star,\tau})+(w_{\star,\tau}-w_{\star}).

Applying (
1
) and the parallelogram bound
\left\|a+b\right\|^{2}\leq 2\left\|a\right\|^{2}+2\left\|b\right\|^{2}
in the
\Sigma^{1/2}
-geometry yields

\mathcal{E}(\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau})\leq 2\left\|\Sigma^{1/2}(\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau}-w_{\star,\tau})\right\|^{2}+2\left\|\Sigma^{1/2}(w_{\star,\tau}-w_{\star})\right\|^{2}.

Because
\Sigma\preceq\Sigma+\tau I=\Sigma_{\tau}
, one has

\left\|\Sigma^{1/2}u\right\|^{2}\leq\left\|\Sigma_{\tau}^{1/2}u\right\|^{2}=\left\|u\right\|_{\tau}^{2}\qquad\text{for all }u\in\mathcal{H},

which gives (
10
) immediately.

B.2
A more explicit replacement-symmetrization calculation

Fix
\tau>0
and abbreviate
\widehat{w}=\widehat{w}_{S_{n},\tau}
. For each coordinate replacement define

\widehat{w}^{(i)}:=\widehat{w}_{S_{n}^{(i,Z_{i}^{\prime})},\tau},\qquad\overline{w}_{S_{n},\tau}:=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}^{\prime}[\widehat{w}^{(i)}\mid S_{n}].

By Jensen’s inequality,

\mathbb{E}\left\|\widehat{w}-\overline{w}_{S_{n},\tau}\right\|_{\tau}^{2}\leq\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbb{E}\left\|\widehat{w}-\widehat{w}^{(i)}\right\|_{\tau}^{2}\leq\mathsf{T}_{n}(\tau).

It remains to control the ghost-averaged term. Let
\{e_{j}\}_{j\geq 1}
be an eigenbasis of
\Sigma
with eigenvalues
\mu_{j}
. Since the transported minimal interpolant is linear in the responses, the
j
th coordinate of
\overline{w}_{S_{n},\tau}
admits the decomposition

\left\langle\overline{w}_{S_{n},\tau},e_{j}\right\rangle=\frac{\mu_{j}}{\mu_{j}+\tau}\left\langle w_{\star},e_{j}\right\rangle+\delta_{j,S}+\zeta_{j,S},

where
\delta_{j,S}
is the centered sample-fluctuation term and
\zeta_{j,S}
is the contribution of the response noise. The centeredness of
\delta_{j,S}
follows from the fact that replacement breaks the dependence between the estimator and the replaced observation. Summing the transported energy over
j
gives

\mathbb{E}\left\|\overline{w}_{S_{n},\tau}-w_{\star,\tau}\right\|_{\tau}^{2}=\mathbb{E}\sum_{j\geq 1}(\mu_{j}+\tau)(\delta_{j,S}+\zeta_{j,S})^{2}.

The cross term vanishes by conditional centering, so

\mathbb{E}\left\|\overline{w}_{S_{n},\tau}-w_{\star,\tau}\right\|_{\tau}^{2}\leq 2\mathbb{E}\sum_{j\geq 1}(\mu_{j}+\tau)\delta_{j,S}^{2}+2\mathbb{E}\sum_{j\geq 1}(\mu_{j}+\tau)\zeta_{j,S}^{2}.

The first sum is controlled by the same replacement argument as above and is bounded by a constant multiple of
\mathsf{T}_{n}(\tau)
. For the second sum, linearity of the interpolant and independence across replaced coordinates imply

\mathbb{E}\sum_{j\geq 1}(\mu_{j}+\tau)\zeta_{j,S}^{2}=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{j\geq 1}\frac{\mu_{j}+c_{j}}{\mu_{j}+\tau},\qquad c_{j}:=\left\langle C_{\varepsilon}e_{j},e_{j}\right\rangle.

Recognizing the two traces gives

\mathbb{E}\left\|\overline{w}_{S_{n},\tau}-w_{\star,\tau}\right\|_{\tau}^{2}\leq\frac{2\mathcal{N}(\tau)}{n}+\frac{2}{n}\mathrm{Tr}(C_{\varepsilon}\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}).

Combining this with

\left\|\widehat{w}-w_{\star,\tau}\right\|_{\tau}^{2}\leq 2\left\|\widehat{w}-\overline{w}_{S_{n},\tau}\right\|_{\tau}^{2}+2\left\|\overline{w}_{S_{n},\tau}-w_{\star,\tau}\right\|_{\tau}^{2}

yields Lemma
4.2
after rewriting the second trace through
\mathsf{A}(\tau)
.

B.3
Why pseudoinverses are legitimate in the infinite-dimensional setting

The only pseudoinverse appearing in the main text is the Moore-Penrose inverse of the finite matrix

K_{S_{n},\tau}=\Phi_{S_{n}}\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}\Phi_{S_{n}}^{*}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}.

Even though
\mathcal{H}
may be infinite dimensional, the optimization problem is constrained by only
n
linear equations, and
K_{S_{n},\tau}
is therefore an ordinary finite-dimensional Gram matrix. The operator
\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1}
is bounded for every fixed
\tau>0
because
\Sigma_{\tau}\succeq\tau I
. Consequently,
K_{S_{n},\tau}
is a well-defined symmetric positive semidefinite matrix, and its Moore-Penrose inverse exists without any additional closed-range assumption on
\Phi_{S_{n}}
.

Appendix E
Verification templates for lower control

This appendix explains how Assumption
4.6
should be verified in concrete models. The purpose is not to give one universal proof, since the exact argument is model dependent, but to remove any impression that the lower-control hypothesis is a purely formal placeholder.

E.1
Linear minimum-energy interpolation

In finite-dimensional linear regression the fitted interpolant lies in the span of the sampled feature vectors. Lower bounds can therefore be derived by restricting attention to two carefully chosen classes of alternatives. The first class is a least-favorable source ball concentrated near the truncation boundary of
\Sigma
. On this class, every estimator operating effectively above scale
\tau
incurs an approximation loss of order
\tau^{2r}
. The second class is a pure-noise model in which
w_{\star}=0
but the modal noise covariance concentrates on a selected set of weak eigen-directions. Exact interpolation forces the estimator to reproduce those noisy coordinates inside the sample span, yielding a lower bound of order
n^{-1}\mathrm{Tr}(C_{\varepsilon}\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1})
.

The argument becomes especially transparent if one works in the eigenbasis of
\Sigma
. Let
J_{\tau}:=\{j:\mu_{j}\geq\tau\}
denote the visible set. Any estimator that interpolates exactly while remaining in the sample span must assign coefficients to the visible coordinates that account for both the signal projection and the sample noise projection. In expectation, the contribution of the noise coordinates alone is

\sum_{j\in J_{\tau}}\frac{c_{j}}{n(\mu_{j}+\tau)},

which is exactly the aligned-noise term up to constants. The signal lower bound is obtained by choosing alternatives with energy concentrated in a thin spectral band around
\tau
so that the filtered approximation error cannot be smaller than order
\tau^{2r}
.

E.2
Kernel interpolation

For kernels the same logic applies after diagonalization of the kernel integral operator. The least-favorable alternatives are now functions whose reproducing-kernel coordinates lie in a band of eigenvalues near
\tau
. Lower bounds for the stochastic part follow by a conditional Gaussian or Rademacher noise construction projected onto the corresponding kernel eigenspaces. Since the sample span generated by the kernel sections is finite dimensional, exact interpolation again forces the learned function to carry a visible amount of aligned-noise energy. What changes relative to the linear case is only the bookkeeping of the eigenfunctions and the concentration tools used to justify empirical-to-population transfer.

E.3
Random features

In random-feature models there are two distinct sources of lower control: the population feature covariance and the randomness of the feature map itself. A convenient verification strategy is therefore conditional. First condition on a typical realization of the random features and apply the deterministic linear argument to the induced covariance. Then integrate over the feature draw. The resulting lower bound is governed by the random-feature covariance spectrum and by the modal projection of noise through that random representation. This shows that the Fredriksson index remains meaningful even when the representation itself is stochastic.

E.4
Practical meaning of lower control

The lower-control assumption should not be interpreted as a requirement that upper and lower constants match perfectly for every problem. What matters is that the same three obstructions appearing in the upper bound are genuine and cannot be eliminated by a better proof. Once this is verified in a model family, the Fredriksson index becomes not just a sufficient descriptor but the correct asymptotic yardstick.

Appendix F
Relation to classical bias-variance language and VC theory

The phrase “spectral-transport stability” is new, but it is worth emphasizing that the framework does not reject classical statistical language. It refines it. In a classical bias-variance decomposition, one asks how estimation error is split between approximation error and sample fluctuation. The present theory keeps precisely that split but insists that, in the interpolation regime, the fluctuation term is itself structured. It has a visible-mode count, an algorithmic repair cost, and an alignment profile. The classical decomposition therefore survives, but the variance term becomes geometrically resolved.

This point helps reconcile the Fredriksson theory with recent attempts to re-read double descent through VC or SRM language
(
36
;
70
)
. If one treats the effective dimension and the transported interpolant as the relevant notion of model complexity, then there is no contradiction between second-descent behavior and structural risk control. What fails is not the idea that complexity must be controlled, but the idea that ambient parameter count or naive hypothesis-class size is the right control variable. In the operator setting, the right control variable is the Fredriksson index.

There is an analogous reconciliation with the bias-variance discussions surrounding ridgeless regression. Classical ridge analysis says that decreasing explicit regularization reduces bias and increases variance. The present theory says that when explicit ridge is absent from the estimator, an analysis scale still exists and the same tradeoff survives in disguised form. The decrease in approximation error as
\tau
is lowered is the analog of decreasing bias; the increase in visible stochastic transport is the analog of increasing variance. The difference is that the stochastic term is not isotropic. Its size depends on spectral crowding, sample-repair motion, and aligned noise.

A final conceptual clarification concerns optimization. In deep learning, “implicit bias” is sometimes invoked as though it were an alternative to statistical explanation. In the Fredriksson theory it is not an alternative; it is part of the statistical explanation. Optimization is statistically relevant because it chooses a point on the interpolation manifold, and the geometry of that point enters the generalization bound through transport stability. This is why the gradient-flow theorem is not an ornament but a structural component of the theory.

Appendix G
Additional technical remarks

G.1
Monotonicity properties

The effective dimension
\mathcal{N}(\tau)
is nonincreasing in
\tau
. The trace
\mathrm{Tr}(C_{\varepsilon}\Sigma_{\tau}^{-1})
is also nonincreasing. The ratio
\mathsf{A}(\tau)
need not be monotone, because both numerator and denominator shrink with
\tau
and may do so at different rates. This non-monotonicity is not a defect of the definition. It reflects the genuine fact that moving the analysis scale can expose or hide particularly noisy spectral bands.

The stability coefficient
\mathsf{T}_{n}(\tau)
is also not generally monotone. Lowering
\tau
makes weak directions cheaper to represent but may simultaneously increase the cost of exact one-point repair because the interpolant is allowed to move more aggressively in near-null modes. This is why scale selection cannot be replaced by a one-sided limit argument at
\tau\downarrow 0
.

G.2
Why the transported norm uses
\Sigma+\tau I
rather than another filter

One might ask why the framework uses the simple resolvent filter
(\Sigma+\tau I)^{-1}
rather than a more general spectral filter. The answer is twofold. First, the resolvent is the unique choice that simultaneously matches source-condition calculus, inverse-problem regularization, and the geometry of preconditioned gradient flow. Second, more general filters can be incorporated later as variants of the same theory. The current paper chooses the resolvent because it is analytically transparent and already rich enough to unify the main literatures under discussion.

G.3
Potential extensions

There are at least three technically natural extensions. The first replaces the squared loss by generalized linear losses and studies margin interpolation. The second introduces a time-dependent covariance operator
\Sigma_{t}
generated by representation learning. The third studies multi-task or transfer settings in which the alignment operator is task dependent while the representation spectrum is shared. Each extension is substantial, but the current framework strongly suggests that the same three coordinates — spectrum, transport, and alignment — will remain central.
