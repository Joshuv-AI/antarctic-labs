Single-cell transcriptomic analysis identifies decreased CEBPB and PAX8 expression associated with immune landscape differences between medullary and papillary thyroid carcinoma
Authors: Xiu Chen, Linhong Jiang, Yan Si, Wei Chen, Shu Song, Zan Fu, Shanliang Zhong, Heda Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) is an aggressive neuroendocrine malignancy originating from thyroid C cells, characterized by higher metastatic potential and poorer prognosis compared with papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), the most common differentiated thyroid cancer subtype. However, the molecular mechanisms underlying the distinct malignant phenotypes between MTC and PTC remain incompletely elucidated. This study aimed to exploratorily characterize potential transcriptional and immunological differences between PTC and MTC at single-cell resolution.MethodsWe performed single-cell RNA-sequencing (scRNA-seq) analysis on tissues from one PTC and one MTC. Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) was performed to detect the expression of CEBPB and PAX8 in TPC-1 and TT cells; immunohistochemistry (IHC) was performed to detect the expression of CEBPB and PAX8 in PTC and MTC tissues.ResultsThe scRNA-seq analysis generated data on 19,334 genes and 0.11 billion unique molecular identifiers (UMIs), derived from 10,039 cells in MTC samples and 9,295 cells in PTC samples, classified into 22 clusters, each annotated to represent 10 cellular lineages. We observed an elevated quantity of epithelial and fibroblastic compartments in MTC, and a predominant population of T cells and natural killer (NK) cells were derived from PTC tissues. Our analysis integrated gene set variation analysis (GSVA), Single-Cell rEgulatory Network Inference and Clustering (SCENIC) analysis, and pseudotime trajectory analysis to present the upmost transcription factors and pathways in different cell clusters of MTC and PTC. We then proceeded to analyze subcluster 0 of epithelial cells, where MTC accounted for the major portion with a low degree of differentiation. Meanwhile, CEBPB, CEBPD and PAX8 exhibited the highest activity in cluster 0, which was further shown to display a downward trend through pseudotime trajectory analysis. RT-qPCR and IHC results revealed that the relative expression of CEBPB and PAX8 were downregulated in MTC compared with PTC.ConclusionsCollectively, our preliminary findings suggested that the downregulation of CEBPB with PAX8 may be associated with impaired immune surveillance in MTC, potentially contributing to its more aggressive clinical behavior compared with PTC. These exploratory observations provided a hypothesis-generating framework and candidate molecular targets for enhancing anti-tumor immunity in MTC.Keywords: Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC), medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), tumour microenvironment, single-cell sequencing, CEBPB

Introduction

Based on the cancer statistics reports, about 44,020 new cases were diagnosed with thyroid cancer in the United States in 2024 (1). And women have a higher incidence of thyroid cancer, ranking the eighth among female cancers. Papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) is the most prevalent pathological subtype of thyroid carcinoma (2), however medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC) is generally more aggressive and harbors a distinct genetic landscape compared with differentiated thyroid cancer. MTC is a malignancy of neuroendocrine disease originating from thyroid parafollicular cells, which accounts for about 3% of all thyroid cancers and has a relatively poor prognosis (3). Studies have reported the 10-year survival rate of approximately 45–95% (4), however, the survival rate of patients with locally advanced or metastatic disease is only 40% (5). MTC is classified as sporadic MTC(SMTC) and hereditary MTC. The majority of adult MTC is SMTC, accounting for 65–75% of all MTC (6), and the data show that the main pathogenesis of MTC is associated with mutations in the RET proto-oncogene (7). However, studies concerning the mechanisms of MTC progression are still inadequate.

Single-cell sequencing technology has emerged as a powerful approach to resolve tumor heterogeneity and characterize the cellular composition of the tumor microenvironment at unprecedented resolution (2). Therefore, we aimed to analyze the single-cell sequencing data of PTC and MTC to gain a comprehensive understanding of the difference between these two types of thyroid cancer. We further aimed to identify candidate genes influencing tumor development and provide potential basis for future therapeutic strategies for thyroid cancer.

CCAAT/enhancer-binding protein beta (CEBPB) is recognized as a critical regulator of the tumor immune microenvironment in clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC), presenting itself as a potential prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target (8). In gastric cancer, CEBPB has been demonstrated to transcriptionally upregulate SERPINE1, thereby activating the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade and promoting epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), which collectively enhance the metastatic capacity of cancer cells (9). Despite these insights, the functional significance and mechanistic role of CEBPB in thyroid carcinoma, particularly in MTC, remain entirely uncharacterized.

Therefore, this study employed single-cell RNA sequencing combined with experimental validation to identify CEBPB as exhibiting a more pronounced protective role in MTC compared to PTC. These findings provide a molecular rationale and propose novel biomarkers for the diagnosis and therapeutic stratification of MTC, as well as for its differential distinction from PTC.

Given the rarity of MTC and the single-patient design of the scRNA-seq component of this study, all findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Validation in larger, multi-center cohorts will be essential before any generalizable conclusions can be drawn. We present this article in accordance with the MDAR reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2026-1098/rc).

Methods

Patient samples

A total of two thyroid cancer samples were obtained, a case of PTC (a 17-year-old female patient; pT1bN1aM0, Stage I; classical PTC variant; BRAF V600E mutation positive; no prior treatment) was from the Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, another case of MTC (a 35-year-old female patient; pT1bN1bM0, Stage I; sporadic MTC; RET M918T mutation positive; no prior treatment) was collected at The First Afﬁliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University. Tissues were processed into single-cell suspensions for 10 × Genomics Chromium Single-Cell 3' kit (V3)]. Sequencing was outsourced to LC-Bio Technology (Hangzhou, China) and carried out on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument using 2×150 bp paired-end reads with multiplexing. Besides, we also collected 3 PTC tissues (a 40-year-old female, a 50-year-old male, a 78-year-old female patient) and 5 MTC tissues (a 31-year-old female, a 49-year-old female, a 52-year-old female, a 72-year-old female, a 79-year-old female patient) for immunochemistry (Table S1). The confirmed cohort tissues were collected from the Department of Pathology, Shanghai Public Health Clinical Center. All tissues were analyzed by two experienced doctors of the pathology. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Afﬁliated Hospital with Nanjing Medical University (Ethics code 2022-SR-473), and all patients have given written informed consent before the study.

Library construction and sequencing

To obtain a single-cell reaction system, the cell suspension concentration was adjusted to 1000 cells/ µL, then the cell suspension, 10X barcode gel magnetic beads and oil droplets were added to different lanes of Chromium Chip B to form Gel Bead-In-EMulsions (GEMs) via the microfluidic crossover system. Upon dissolution of the Single Cell 3’ Gel Bead in a GEM, R1 (read 1 primer sequence) are append to the molecules during GEM incubation. P5, P7, a sample index, and R2 (read 2 primer sequence) are append through End Repair, A-tailing, Adaptor Ligation, and PCR during library construction. The final libraries include the P5 and P7 primers adopted in Illumina bridge amplification. Sequencing libraries suitable for Illumina instruments were yielded through the Single Cell 3’ Protocol. Read 1 is used to distinguish different transcripts from each cell, and Read 2 is used to determine gene information. Finally, we submitted the gene expression matrices to the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (accession number GSE241184, GSE318583).

Gene set variation analysis

Gene sets corresponding to the 50 hallmark pathways were retrieved from the msigdbr package (version 7.5.1). Pathway-associated genes were subsequently excluded following the approach described by Lambrechts et al. (10). Applied Gene set variation analysis (GSVA) by conducting standard settings to distribute pathway activity estimates to individual cells through GSVA package (version 1.40.0).

Single-cell transcriptome analysis

The unprocessed gene expression profiles derived from the scRNA-seq dataset were generated and combined using R software (version 4.1.1) to convert them into a Seurat object using the Seurat R package (version 4.2) (11). Cells with poor quality or empty droplets were filtered out when nFeature_RNA fell below 200. Potential doublets and multiplets were discarded if nCount_RNA exceeded 30,000 or nFeature_RNA surpassed 8,000. Cells undergoing apoptosis were eliminated when mitochondrial-derived UMIs accounted for more than 10% of total counts. Ribosomal, mitochondrial and hemoglobin genes were excluded from the data sets. Finally, 19334cells and 60334 genes were retained for further analysis. The datasets were corrected for the batch correction vectors through using canonical correlation analysis (CCA), top 3000 variable genes were visualized through Seurat’s NormalizeData and FindVariableFeatures function in Seurat package with default parameters. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed for the highly variable genes after Z-score normalization. To decrease the dimensionality of this dataset, the first 30 principal components (PCs) further performed using t-distributed stochastic neighbour embedding (t-SNE) dimensionality. Clustering was performed with the FindClusters function at a resolution of 1. Differentially expressed genes characterizing each cluster were detected using the Seurat FindAllMarkersfunction. Cluster identities were subsequently assigned to specific cell types by cross-referencing these marker genes against PanglaoDB (12) and CellMarker 2.0 (13).

Cell culture

Human papillary thyroid carcinoma cell line TPC-1 was obtained from the FuHeng Biology (Shanghai, China, Cat No. FH1039). Human medullary thyroid carcinoma cell line TT was obtained from HyCyte (Shanghai, China, Cat No. TCH-C362). TPC-1 cells were maintained in RPMI-1640 (KEYGEN) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, life technologies). TT cells were cultured in Complete Medium for TT (HyCyte). All cells were incubated at 37℃ in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2.

Real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells by using RNA simple Total RNA kit (TIANGEN BIOTECH, Beijing, China) based on manufacturer’s instructions. We synthesized cDNA from 500 ng of total RNA with HiScript II Q RT SuperMix for qPCR, and then subjected to qPCR using ChamQ SYBR qPCR Master Mix (High ROX Premixed) (Vazyme) on the LightCycler® 480 System (Roche, Switzerland). The 2−ΔΔCt method was applied to assess the relative expression level of the target RNA, with β-actin as the internal reference gene. All RT-qPCR experiments were performed in three independent biological replicates. Primer sequences are provided in Table 1.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) analysis

The IHC staining was performed on formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections of thyroid carcinoma and adjacent normal tissues following standard protocols. Tissue sections were incubated with primary anti-PAX8 (Cat No. 10336-1-AP, RRID: AB_2236705) and anti-CEBPB antibody (Cat No. 23431-1-AP, RRID: AB_2879278) (both at 1:50 dilution; Proteintech, China). Immunoreactivity was assessed using the Immunoreactive Score (IRS) system by two independent pathologists who were blinded to the clinical information.

Single-Cell rEgulatory Network Inference and Clustering (SCENIC) analysis

SCENIC analysis was performed following the protocol established by Van de Sande et al. (14). Co-expression of transcription factors and their downstream genes were counted through GRNBoost2. Indirect targets elimination was achieved via RcisTarget (version 1.12.0). The activities of transcription factors and their target genes were scored by enrichment of target genes for each regulator through AUCell package (version 1.14.0).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the R software (version 4.1.1). The comparison of cell proportions among different tissue groups was performed using One-way Kruskal-Wallis rank sum test. The comparison of cell proportions of a certain cell type was performed using the two-sided Wilcoxon rank sum test. The correlation was assessed by calculating Spearman’s correlation coefficient. Two-sided Fisher’s exact test was used to identify the relationships between the two cellular ecotypes. P<0.05 was deemed as statistically significant.

Results

scRNA-seq of the tissues

To investigate the tumor microenvironment (TME) of medullary thyroid tumors versus papillary thyroid tumors, we procured tumor tissue samples from a female patient with MTC and a female patient with PTC for scRNA-seq. Post-exclusion of cells with substandard quality, a total of 10,039 cells from the MTC sample and 9,295 cells from the PTC sample were selected for further analysis. In these cells, 60,344 genes and 0.11 billion UMIs were found. These cells were subsequently stratified to 22 distinct clusters (table available at https://cdn.amegroups.cn/static/public/tcr-2026-1098-1.xlsx) and were functionally annotated to represent 10 cellular lineages, encompassing epithelial cells, macrophages, natural killer (NK) cells, monocytes, mast cells, progenitor cells, endothelial cells, B lymphocytes, fibroblasts, and T lymphocytes (Figure 1A, Figure S1). The top five marker genes were shown in Figure 1B. Additionally, our analysis revealed an elevated quantity of transcript counts within the epithelial and fibroblastic compartments of MTC when contrasted with other cells (Figure 1A,1C). We observed that a predominant population of T cells and NK cells were derived from PTC tissues (Figure 1C), potentially implicating a reduction of T cells and NK cells within the TME of MTC tissues. Further analysis revealed that MTC might be associated with less activation and cytotoxicity status of T cells (Figure S2). However, given the limitation of sample size in this study, these cell proportion differences cannot be attributed definitively to disease-specific biology and may reflect inter-individual variability.

Subclusters of endothelial cells

A sum of 2,253 endothelial cells were discerned and reclassified into 5 subclusters (Figure 2A). Cluster 0 of MTC exhibited more transcriptional activities than PTC in each cluster. Pathway enrichment via GSVA further uncovered that MTC endothelial subclusters were enriched in far more biological cascades than PTC endothelial cell populations. MTC endothelial cells displayed high expression of genes associated with TGF beta signaling, TNFa signaling via NFkB, Hypoxia, Unfolded protein response, Mitotic spindle, etc., while PTC endothelial cells had high inflammatory response and allograft rejection (Figure 2B). We implemented the SCENIC algorithm to uncover transcription factors driving divergent gene expression profiles between endothelial cells derived from MTC and PTC lesions. SCENIC examines genes that are differentially expressed to identify transcription factor binding sites that are significantly enriched, and analyses co-expression of transcription factors and their putative target genes. KLF10, HES1, IRF1, JUN, and SOX4 were the top five transcription factors responsible for the variances in gene expression observed within MTC endothelial cells, whereas JDP2, NPDC1, MXD4, KLF2 and JUND were the top five for PTC endothelial cell phenotypes (Figure 2C-2E). We showed the top transcription factors and pathways for MTC and PTC endothelial cell clusters in Figure 2F.

Subclusters of fibroblast cells

A total of 2,253 fibroblast cells were captured and partitioned into 5 clusters (Figure 3A). Cluster 0, 1, 2 of MTC each demonstrated heightened transcriptional activity (Figure 3A). GSVA pictured that MTC participated in more pathways than PTC fibroblast cells clusters. MTC fibroblast cells showed high expression of genes associated with TGF beta signaling, mitotic spindle, unfolded protein response, MYC targets v1, p53 pathway, et al., while PTC fibroblast cells had high activities in only five pathways, such as allograft rejection and inflammatory response (Figure 3B). SCENIC identified MEF2C, IRF1, EGR1, JUNB, and NR2F2 as the top five transcriptional regulators that may account for the observed disparities in gene expression patterns within fibroblast cells of MTC. In contrast, CEBPD, JUND, GATA6, FOS and JUN were the top five responsible for PTC fibroblast cell phenotypes (Figure 3C-3E). The inferred copy number variation (CNV) analysis showed that no significant difference was observed between the clusters. While in cluster 1, 2, 3, MTC epithelial cells had higher grade of differentiation (Figure 5A), which informed that PTC and/or MTC existed heterogeneity in epithelial cells aspect.

The IC50 AUC values for each corresponding drug were computed by calcPhenotype function from OncoPredic, and the selection of potential candidate drugs for MTC based on the following criteria: adjusted P value <0.05 (under t-test), log2FC value of MTC cells relative to PTC cells <−1, and correlation with tumor score <−0.5. A total of 7 predicted drugs were obtained, with 2 from the GDSC1 database database, 5 from the GDSC2 database (Figure 5B).

In the SCENIC analysis, genes regulated by BHLHE41, PAX8, SOX4, KLF6, CEBPD, CEBPB and MAFF were featured obvious upregulation in Cluster 0, while those modulated by FOXP1, UQCRB and NPDC1 underwent transcriptional inhibition. In regard to Cluster 3, the transcriptional targets of by FOXP1, UQCRB and NPDC1 were highly enriched, whereas genes regulated by BDP1 and HES1 were transcriptionally repressed. In Cluster 4, genes regulated by ZEB1, SPI1 and RUNX1 were mainly upregulated (Figure 5C). The GSVA analysis indicated that clusters 0, 3 and 5 participated in a greater number of biological pathways than clusters 1, 2 and 4, implying enhanced activity within these clusters (Figure 5D). Considering the results of cytoTRACE2 analysis, clusters 0 and 4 may be composed of malignant cells.

Then we display the top transcripts and core functional pathways for clusters 0 and 4 in Figure 5E. To ascertain the cellular states, a pseudotime trajectory analysis was executed encompassing all epithelial cell populations. Utilizing the sequential arrangement along the pseudotime axis, the epithelial cells were categorized into 5 stages (Figure 5F). The majority cells of Cluster 0 resided at the early pseudotime phase, whereas Clusters 2 and 3 predominantly occupied later stages, this distribution pattern was consistent across both PTC and MTC specimens (Figure 5F). We further analyzed the trend of the top five transcription factors (PAX8, CEBPB, CEBPD, TAGLN2 and JUN) for cluster 0, CEBPB, CEBPD and PAX8 showed a downward trend (Figure 5G).

PAX8 and CEBPB were decreased in MTC than PTC

To verify the accuracy of the identified differential expression genes, we examined PAX8 and CEBPB expression by RT-qPCR as well as IHC (CEBPD expression was not stable enough to undergo further study). Results revealed that the relative expression of CEBPB and PAX8 were downregulated in TT MTC cells, compared with TPC-1 PTC cells (Figure 6A, P<0.05). Meanwhile, CEBPB and PAX8 were significantly highly expressed in PTC than MTC, both in comparison with the negative control (Fig 6B-6H, P<0.05). These results were generally in consistent with our single-cell RNA sequencing analysis. Then the string database analysis presented that both PAX8 and CEBPB interacted with PPARG (Figure 6I,6J).

Discussion

Thyroid carcinomas are documented with the highest incidence among endocrine system, accounting for approximately one percent of all neoplasms initially diagnosed in cancer patients. Furthermore, the incidence of thyroid cancer has gradually increased in recent years. PTC accounts for 85–90% of all thyroid cancers with an over 90% 10-year survival rate (15,16), however, the 10-year survival rate of MTC patients is about 75% (17). This may correlate with both tumor specificity and heterogeneity. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) approach has equipped researchers with a high-resolution analytical paradigm to reveal the complex mechanistic environment of tumors and to explore tumor heterogeneity (18,19).

To characterize the transcriptional and immunological differences between MTC and PTC at single-cell resolution, we partitioned the cells into 22 groups, subsequently assigned to 10 discrete cell-type categories, according to the highly variable genes. We found an elevated quantity of transcript counts within the epithelial and fibroblastic cells of MTC in contrast with other cells, suggesting their high activity. We also found a reduction of T cells and NK cells within the TME of MTC tissues. Recently, researchers had compared the immune microenvironment between PTC and MTC, and found a relatively lower immune infiltration in MTC (20), which was partially in consistent with our study. Besides, previous literature had investigated that tumor mutational burden might be associated with abnormal immune cell infiltration such as increased resting NK cells and decreased CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells in PTC (21,22). Also, NK cells was decreased in ATC primary tumor than PTC (16). The results indicated that lower immune infiltration led to worse subgroup of thyroid cancer, and explained the speculation that MTC had a poor prognosis in immune perspective. However, we emphasize that this observation, derived from a single MTC patient, cannot be taken as definitive evidence for a generalized immune surveillance defect in MTC. Trajectory inference positioned cluster 0 at the least-differentiated apex of the pseudotime manifold, these cells were overwhelmingly MTC cells in origin, which is in consistent with the clinically recognized poorly differentiated nature of MTC malignancy.

Furthermore, our results also found that PAX8, CEBPB were downregulated in MTC tissues and cells, and PAX8 and CEBPB may interact with PPARG. These results indicate that PAX8, CEBPB are relevant to the molecular classification and potential targeted therapy of thyroid cancer.

In various other malignancies, including oral squamous-cell carcinoma, CEBPB has been shown to amplify tumor-cell chemotaxis toward macrophages and drive their M2 polarization, thereby fostering an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (23). Mechanistically, CEBPB fuels chronic inflammation via transcriptional up-regulation of VCAM1, and its sustained expression in macrophages perturbs both metabolic and immune homeostasis (24). These previous findings align well with our own observations. However, in prostate cancer (PCa) cells, CEBPB has been reported to play an indispensable role in PTE-evoked induction of SOD2 by enhancing its transcription. Furthermore, knockdown of CEBPB significantly attenuates PTE-triggered apoptosis in awakening quiescent PCa cells—a trend opposite to our results (25). This discrepancy may stem from the fact that the aforementioned study focused primarily on cell-autonomous gene functions, without accounting for the potential modulation by immune cells in the tumor microenvironment. In thyroid cancer, the functional relevance of CEBPB remains completely unexplored. Here we provide the first evidence that CEBPB might be involved in a tumor–immune axis in MTC rather than differentiated thyroid cancer, though functional experiments are required to confirm this.

Besides, thyroid tumorigenesis is driven by a complex landscape of genetic and epigenetic alterations. Sustained activation of the MAPK and PI3K-AKT cascades constitutes a central oncogenic axis (26,27). In addition, point mutations in genes such as BRAF, RAS, and RET also play important roles in thyroid cancer (27-29). Further research found that increased expression of METTL3 promotes the m6A modification level of PAX8, while YTHDC1 can recognize the m6A modification of PAX8, facilitate the translocation of PAX8 from the nucleus to the cytoplasm, and thereby potentiating the differentiated phenotype of PTC (30). In follicular neoplasia, chromosomal rearrangements generating PAX8-PPARγ fusion proteins disrupt terminal differentiation programmes while conferring a proliferative advantage (31,32). However, the contribution of PAX8 to MTC remains essentially uncharted, representing a critical knowledge gap that our ongoing investigations are designed to address.

However, our study is subject to several important limitations that must be explicitly acknowledged. Firstly, the single-cell transcriptomic analysis was derived from only one MTC patient and one PTC patient. The biological sample size is critically small and may not be representative of the full molecular and immunological heterogeneity of these two tumor types. The observed differences may reflect inter-individual variation rather than true disease-defining signatures. As such, this study must be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating in nature, and all findings require prospective validation in larger, multi-center cohorts with diverse genetic backgrounds and clinical stages. The rarity of MTC and the typically small tumor volume further constrain access to high-quality fresh tissue suitable for scRNA-seq, which poses ongoing challenges for the field. Second, the IHC validation cohort is too small to preclude any statistically robust diagnostic or translational conclusions. A large-scale, multi-center IHC studies with standardized scoring criteria and adequate statistical power will be required. Considering the IHC tissues contain a large number of normal tissues as a blank control, so we think it is enough to conclude that the downregulation of PAX8 and CEBPB is a specific feature of MTC tumors with a lack of normal thyroid cell lines. We also acknowledge that the current study lacks functional experiments, so future studies employing CRISPR-based loss-of-function approaches or organoid co-culture models will be essential to establish causality. Additionally, the absence of a robust, genetically faithful MTC animal model continues to impede rigorous in vivo functional validation. Besides, PAX8 positivity in IHC and scRNA-seq data may partly reflect technical limitations or microenvironmental contamination, and that definitive conclusions regarding PAX8’s role in MTC should await larger, more rigorously controlled studies.

Conclusions

In summary, this study represented the first single-cell comparative atlas of medullary versus PTC. We documented a pronounced depletion of cytotoxic T and NK lymphocytes within the MTC microenvironment, underscoring immune escape as a hallmark of this neuroendocrine subtype. Integrative genomics suggested that CEBPB and PAX8 might represent candidate molecules of interest for future mechanistic and therapeutic investigation. It is important to conduct in-depth pre-clinical researches on targeted therapy and combination therapy.
