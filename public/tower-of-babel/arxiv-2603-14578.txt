Power-Law Spectrum of the Random Feature Model
Elliot Paquette Ke Liang Xiao Yizhe Zhu

Abstract
Scaling laws for neural networks, in which the loss decays as a power-law in the number of parameters, data, and compute, depend fundamentally on the spectral structure of the data covariance, with power-law eigenvalue decay appearing ubiquitously in vision and language tasks. A central question is whether this spectral structure is preserved or destroyed when data passes through the basic building block of a neural network: a random linear projection followed by a nonlinear activation. We study this question for the random feature model: given data 𝐱 ∼ N ⁡ ( 0 , 𝐇 ) ∈ ℝ v \mathbf{x}\sim N(0,\mathbf{H})\in\mathbb{R}^{v} where 𝐇 \mathbf{H} has α \alpha -power-law spectrum ( λ j ​ ( 𝐇 ) ≍ j − α \lambda_{j}(\mathbf{H})\asymp j^{-\alpha} , α > 1 \alpha>1 ), a Gaussian sketch matrix 𝐖 ∈ ℝ v × d \mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{v\times d} , and an entrywise monomial f ⁡ ( y ) = y p f(y)=y^{\text{p}} , we characterize the eigenvalues of the population random-feature covariance 𝔼 𝐱 ​ [ 1 d ​ f ​ ( 𝐖 ⊤ ​ 𝐱 ) ⊗ 2 ] \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[\frac{1}{d}f(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 2}] . We prove matching upper and lower bounds: for all 1 ≤ j ≤ c 1 ​ d ​ log − ( p + 1 ) ⁡ ( d ) 1\leq j\leq c_{1}d\log^{-(\text{p}+1)}(d) , the j j -th eigenvalue is of order ( log p − 1 ⁡ ( j + 1 ) / j ) α \left(\log^{\text{p}-1}(j+1)/j\right)^{\alpha} . The proof combines a dyadic head-tail decomposition with Wick chaos expansions for higher-order monomials and random matrix concentration inequalities.

Introduction

When training large neural networks, the loss typically scales as a power-law in the number of parameters, amount of data, and total compute
[
22
,
18
]
.
The exponents of these scaling laws are of central importance: they determine how much additional data or compute is needed to achieve a given reduction in loss, and they govern compute-optimal training strategies.
A growing body of theoretical work has identified the spectral decay of the data covariance as
one
component controlling these exponents. Specifically, power-law eigenvalue decay of the form
\lambda_{j}\asymp j^{-\alpha}
appears as a key ingredient in scaling law models
[
4
,
31
,
39
,
42
]
.
Of course, the data covariance spectrum is not the only mechanism at play: other factors such as the structure of the learning algorithm, the target function complexity, and feature learning dynamics also shape scaling behavior
[
30
,
41
,
7
]
.

Nonetheless, power-law spectral structure is ubiquitous. It appears in the covariance of natural images (see Figure
1
), language model representations, and intermediate features of neural networks.
Understanding what controls these spectral exponents is therefore a path toward understanding, and eventually improving, scaling laws.
Indeed, recent work has shown that exploiting the power-law geometry of data enables accelerated optimization algorithms with provably better scaling law exponents
[
14
,
15
,
8
,
49
,
29
,
3
]
.

The most basic operation in a neural network is to take input data, multiply it by a weight matrix, and apply a nonlinear activation function. This is the fundamental multilayer perceptron (MLP) layer.
The
power-law random features
(PLRF) model formalizes this construction: data
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{v}
with power-law covariance
\mathbf{H}
is projected through a random sketch
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{v\times d}
and passed through a nonlinearity
f

[
31
,
39
,
40
]
.
In the linear case (
f
is the identity), the spectrum of the sketched covariance
\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}
is well understood: it inherits the power-law decay from
\mathbf{H}

[
39
,
28
]
.
This raises a fundamental question:
does the power-law spectral structure survive the nonlinearity?

If the exponent
\alpha
persists through each layer, the scaling law structure is maintained as data propagates through the network, which can be leveraged to understand and improve scaling laws.

We answer this question affirmatively for monomial activations.
Our main result (Theorem
1
) shows that for the monomial
f(y)=y^{\text{p}}
, the eigenvalues of the population random-feature covariance satisfy

\lambda_{j}\!\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\!\left[\tfrac{1}{d}f(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 2}\right]\right)\asymp\left(\frac{\log^{\text{p}-1}(j+1)}{j}\right)^{\!\alpha},

with matching upper and lower bounds for all
1\leq j\leq c_{1}d\log^{-(\text{p}+1)}(d)
, holding with probability
1-O(\log^{-4}(d))
.
The power-law exponent
\alpha
is inherited exactly from the input covariance.
The only modification is a logarithmic correction
\log^{\text{p}-1}(j+1)
that depends on the monomial degree: for
\text{p}=1
(the linear case) there is no correction, recovering the known result
[
28
,
5
,
39
]
, while for
\text{p}>1
the correction is mild and does not change the polynomial rate of decay. For
c_{1}d\log^{-(\text{p}+1)}(d)\leq j\leq d
, Theorem
1
also shows that the
j
-th eigenvalue is of order
j^{-\alpha}
up to a polylog factor.

Numerical experiments (Section
2.1
) confirm the predicted spectral slopes for monomials of degree
1
through
6
.
Moreover, the experiments suggest that the phenomenon extends well beyond the scope of the theorem: non-monomial activations such as ReLU and
\tanh
, as well as non-Gaussian data distributions including Rademacher, Student-
t
, and CIFAR-10, all exhibit similar power-law preservation.
This spectral characterization provides a building block for rigorous scaling law theory in nonlinear random feature models.

Related work

Power-law spectral decay has been used as a principled model for describing effective dimension in modern high-dimensional learning problems, and for predicting how risk scales with sample size, parameters, and compute.
In linear models, this perspective is closely tied to benign overfitting and scaling-law phenomena; see, e.g.,

[
5
,
28
,
48
]
.
Our results can be viewed as identifying how such a power-law structure propagates through
random sketching
and nonlinear feature maps, by characterizing the eigenvalue decay of the population random-feature covariance.

There is also a substantial literature on kernel methods and kernel random matrices beyond the isotropic setting.
Classical work analyzes the spectrum of kernel random matrices under fairly general distributions
[
13
]
, and more recent works study approximation and universality phenomena for empirical kernel matrices and kernel regression in high dimensions, including anisotropic regimes; see, e.g.,
[
12
,
6
,
48
,
27
,
52
,
38
,
23
,
19
]
.
Many kernel-regression analyses in power-law settings can be interpreted as assuming (explicitly or implicitly) a power-law spectrum for a feature representation
\varphi(\mathbf{x})
together with sufficient concentration of
\varphi(\mathbf{x})

[
10
,
43
,
44
,
9
]
.
In contrast, we start from an explicit random-feature construction and derive the resulting spectral decay from first principles.

Finally, there is a connection to asymptotic geometric analysis: random projections of ellipsoids (and the geometry induced by heavy spectral decay) have been studied from a geometric and information-based viewpoint, e.g.
[
17
]
. Our approach can be viewed as a random matrix counterpart in this direction: the power-law structure is encoded by an ellipsoidal covariance
\mathbf{H}
, and we track how random sketching and nonlinear feature maps reshape its spectrum, requiring a blend of random matrix concentration
[
21
,
25
]
and Wick chaos expansion techniques
[
20
]
adapted to the power-law setting.

Comparison with
[
53
]

The recent work
[
53
]
studies
kernel ridge regression
(KRR) under anisotropic Gaussian covariates whose covariance matrix exhibits a power-law spectrum.
A key object in their analysis is the associated
kernel integral (population) operator

T
,
defined for
\nu=N(0,\bm{\Sigma})
by

(Tf)(\mathbf{x})\;=\;\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{Y}\sim\nu}\!\bigl[k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{Y})\,f(\mathbf{Y})\bigr]\;=\;\int_{\mathbb{R}^{v}}k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})\,f(\mathbf{y})\,d\nu(\mathbf{y}),\qquad f\in L^{2}(\nu).

This operator depends only on the kernel
k
and the data distribution
\nu
(hence contains no finite-sample randomness),
and generalization bounds for KRR can be expressed in terms of the spectral decay of
T
.
When the kernel function is
k(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y})=\langle\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y}\rangle^{p}
, the spectrum of
T
is characterized in
[
53
, Corollary 2]
.

Our contribution is complementary: we analyze the
random feature model
obtained by applying a monomial nonlinearity to a random sketch,
and we characterize the eigenvalue decay of the resulting
finite-dimensional

d\times d
random feature matrix.
Compared to the operator-level viewpoint in
[
53
]
, our results directly quantify how a power-law spectrum in the population covariance
propagates through random sketching and the feature nonlinearity at finite width.
We expect this spectral characterization to be a useful input for studying the test error of random-feature ridge regression under power-law data.

Notation

Given two sequences
\{a_{n}\}
and
\{b_{n}\}
, we will write
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
if
a_{n}\leq C_{\alpha,\text{p}}b_{n}
for some constant
C_{\alpha,\text{p}}
depending only on
\alpha
and
p
.
We will write
a_{n}\asymp b_{n}
if
a_{n}\lesssim b_{n}
and
b_{n}\lesssim a_{n}
. For two vectors
\mathbf{u}
and
\mathbf{v}
, we will write either
\mathbf{u}\otimes\mathbf{v}
or
\mathbf{u}\mathbf{v}^{\top}
to be the outer-product of the
2
-tensor. We will write
\mathbf{u}\odot\mathbf{v}

\bm{\Sigma}\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n}

2
Main results

We start with a formal definition of the power-law spectral distribution of a Hermitian matrix with eigenvalues arranged in non-increasing order.

Definition 1
.

We say that a Hermitian matrix
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{v\times v}
has
\alpha
-power-law spectrum with coefficient
\alpha>1
if for all
1\leq j\leq v
,

\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{H})\asymp j^{-\alpha}.

(1)

The following theorem describes how the power-law coefficient
\alpha
propagates through random sketch and monomial activation.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\alpha>1
and
\mathbf{x}\sim N(0,\mathbf{H})
such that
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{v\times v}
has
\alpha
-power-law spectrum. If
v\geq d
and the sketch matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{v\times d}
has i.i.d. standard Gaussian entries, then given a monomial
f(y)=y^{\text{p}}
and
\text{p}\in\mathbb{N}
, there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2},c_{3}>0
depending only on
\alpha
and
p
so that with probability
1-O\left(\log^{-4}(d)\right)
, for all
1\leq j\leq c_{1}d\log^{-(\text{p}+1)}(d)
,

c_{2}\left(\frac{\log^{\text{p}-1}(j+1)}{j}\right)^{\alpha}\leq\lambda_{j}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\frac{1}{d}f(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 2}\right]\right)\leq c_{3}\left(\frac{\log^{\text{p}-1}(j+1)}{j}\right)^{\alpha}.

(2)

Moreover, if
c_{1}d\log^{-(\text{p}+1)}(d)<j\leq d
then

c_{2}\left(\frac{\log^{-2}(j+1)}{j}\right)^{\alpha}\leq\lambda_{j}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\frac{1}{d}f(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 2}\right]\right)\leq c_{3}\left(\frac{\log^{2\text{p}}(j+1)}{j}\right)^{\alpha},

(3)

and

\lambda_{d}\left(\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\frac{1}{d}f(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 2}\right]\right)\geq c_{2}\left(\frac{\log^{\text{p}-1}(d)}{d}\right)^{\alpha}.

(4)

Universality across data distributions.

Theorem
1
assumes Gaussian data, but the power-law preservation phenomenon appears to be universal across data distributions.
Figure
3
compares the eigenvalue spectra for four data sources (Gaussian, Rademacher, Student-
t(4)

\alpha=1.31
.
The fitted spectral slopes are remarkably consistent across all four distributions for each activation function.
This suggests a universality phenomenon: the power-law exponent of the random-feature covariance depends primarily on the power-law exponent of the input covariance, not on the specific distributional form of the data.
A related open question is universality in the sketch matrix
\mathbf{W}
: we expect the result to hold for random matrices
\mathbf{W}
beyond the Gaussian case, provided the entries have matching first and second moments and sufficiently light tails.
Indeed, our Theorem
2
for the linear case already establishes this under moment conditions on
\mathbf{W}
.

Beyond monomial activations.

The right panel of Figure
2
shows some non-monomial activations (ReLU,
\tanh
, Heaviside) also produce random-feature covariances with power-law spectral decay; however,
x^{2}e^{-x^{2}}
shows a strong deviation, and we remark that formally summing the logarithmic corrections suggests that for some classes of analytic activations, one should see non-power-law decay or altered exponents.
Theorem
1
currently covers only monomials; extending to general activations is an important open direction.
Since any sufficiently regular activation function can be expanded in a basis of Hermite polynomials (equivalently, Wick polynomials), such an extension would likely decompose the covariance into monomial-like contributions.
However, the logarithmic corrections in Theorem
1
make the convergence of such an expansion delicate.

Persistence across layers.

Theorem
1
characterizes the spectrum after a single random projection and activation, i.e., one layer of an MLP.
The result suggests that if one stacks multiple such layers (each applying a fresh random projection and monomial activation), the power-law exponent
\alpha
should persist through the network.
We test this experimentally in Appendix
E
for three architectures on CIFAR-10 data: a
4
-layer MLP (width
1024
), a VGG-style CNN, and a ResNet-20, each at both random initialization and after training.
The power-law exponent is preserved across all layers and architectures, with
R^{2}>0.87
for every layer in every model; however, the actual exponent drifts across layers, perhaps consistent with accumulating logarithmic corrections.
For trained networks, the final-layer spectral slope approximately recovers the input slope (
-1.35
vs.
-1.29
for ResNet-20;
-1.56
vs.
-1.29
for CNN), suggesting that training actively shapes the spectral structure.
Full experimental details, per-layer spectra, and a summary figure are given in Appendix
E
.

Open questions.

Several directions remain open:

1.

General activations.
Extending the result from monomials to polynomials and classical activations (ReLU,
\tanh
, sigmoid) is the most natural next step. The experiments strongly suggest that “tame” activations preserve the
j^{-\alpha}
behavior.

2.

Eigenvalue coverage.
The current two-sided sharp bound holds for the
j
-th eigenvalue up to
j=c_{1}d\log^{-(\text{p}+1)}(d)
, which is almost all
d
eigenvalues but with a polylogarithmic gap. This gap is related to the well-known missing logarithm in covariance estimation: the empirical covariance matrix requires
n\gtrsim d\log d
samples rather than
n\gtrsim d
in some heavy-tailed examples to achieve operator-norm concentration
[
50
, Remark 5.43]
, while for sub-Gaussian random vectors,
n\gtrsim d
suffices. Under only a finite moment assumption on the random vectors, the log factors also appear in
[
34
,
47
]
. For heavier-tailed data (such as the Gaussian chaos variables that arise here, which are not sub-Gaussian), this logarithmic factor can be removed by replacing the empirical covariance with a robust estimator
[
35
,
1
]
. We expect that getting sharp bounds for all eigenvalues is possible, but this would require different techniques.

3.

Universality in
\mathbf{W}
.
We expect the result to hold for sketch matrices with i.i.d. entries satisfying a two-moment matching condition, at least for light-tailed distributions. The reason is that the leading term in the expansion comes from the homogeneous chaos, which depends only on the first two moments of the sketch distribution. This is established for
\text{p}=1
in Theorem
2
.

4.

Test error under power-law data.
Combining this spectral characterization with the analysis of random-feature ridge regression
[
33
,
32
,
51
,
10
]
to obtain scaling laws for the test error is a natural goal.

Warm up: Linear Case

To illustrate our proof strategy for Theorem
1
, we first present the linear case

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[\frac{1}{d}f(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 2}\right]=\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}.

\mathbf{V}^{2}=c\mathbf{I}
with
c\asymp\frac{\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right|}{d}
, then
\mathbf{V}^{2}\succeq\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\frac{1}{d}\bm{\omega}^{(i)}\bm{\omega}^{(i)\top}\right)^{2}\right]
.

Step 1: Two-sided bound on
\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{0}\mathbf{W}_{0}
.

In the case that
k=0
, we have
\left|{\mathcal{K}_{0}}\right|=k_{*}
. So,
\left\|{\mathbf{V}^{2}}\right\|=O\left(\frac{k_{*}}{d}\right)
and
r(\mathbf{V}^{2})=k_{*}
. It is easy to check that for sufficiently large
d
,
\max\left\{\left\|{\mathbf{V}^{2}}\right\|,32\mathbb{E}[M]\right\}\leq\frac{1}{24}
. We now apply Lemma
4
with parameters
(t,q)=\left(\frac{1}{24},2\right)
as specified in the respective Lemma to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|{\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{0}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}}\right\|\geq\frac{1}{2}\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim k_{*}\exp\left(\frac{-1}{O\left(\left\|{\mathbf{V}^{2}}\right\|+\mathbb{E}[M]\right)}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(M\geq\frac{1}{24}\right)+\left(\left(\frac{8}{\log(2e)}\right)^{2}\mathbb{E}[M^{2}]\right)^{2}

(16)

\displaystyle\lesssim k_{*}\exp\left(\frac{-1}{O\left(\left\|{\mathbf{V}^{2}}\right\|+\mathbb{E}[M]\right)}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(M\geq\frac{k_{*}}{d}+\frac{1}{48}\right)+\mathbb{E}[M^{2}]^{2}

(17)

\displaystyle\lesssim k_{*}\exp\left(O\left(\frac{k_{*}}{d}+\frac{\sqrt{k_{*}}}{{d^{1-1/q}}}\right)^{-1}\right)+\frac{k_{*}^{q/2}}{d^{q-1}}+\left(\frac{k_{*}}{d}+\frac{\sqrt{k_{*}}}{d^{1-1/q}}\right)^{4}.

(18)

Choosing
q=4
, there exists
c_{1}>0
such that if
k_{*}=c_{1}d\log^{-1}(d)
then (
18
) is bounded by
O\left(\log^{-4}(d)\right)
. Which is to say that with probability at least
1-O\left(\log^{-4}(d)\right)
,

\left\|{\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{0}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}}\right\|<\frac{1}{2}.

(19)

Therefore,

\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}_{0}\preceq\frac{1}{d}\sqrt{\mathbf{H}_{0}}\mathbf{W}_{0}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\top}\sqrt{\mathbf{H}_{0}}\preceq\frac{3}{2}\mathbf{H}_{0}.

(20)

By the Loewner ordering of PSD matrices, we see that for all
1\leq j\leq c_{1}d\log^{-1}(d)
,

\lambda_{j}\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{0}\mathbf{W}_{0}\right)\asymp\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{H}_{0})\asymp j^{-\alpha}.

(21)

Step 2: Upper bound on
\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{k}\mathbf{W}_{k}
.

In the case that
k\geq 1
, we have

\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right|=k_{*}2^{k-1}
. Thus,
\left\|{\mathbf{V}^{2}}\right\|=O\left(\frac{k_{*}2^{k-1}}{d}\right)
and
r(\mathbf{V}^{2})=k_{*}2^{k-1}
. We see for sufficiently large
d
and any choice of
\beta\in(1,\alpha)
,
\max\left\{\left\|{\mathbf{V}^{2}}\right\|,32\mathbb{E}[M]\right\}\leq\frac{2^{\beta k}}{12}
. We apply Lemma
4
with parameters

(t,q)=\left(\frac{2^{\beta k}}{12},2\right)
to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|{\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{k}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}}\right\|\geq 2^{\beta k}\right)

(22)

\displaystyle\lesssim k_{*}2^{k}\exp\left(\frac{-2^{2\beta k}}{O\left(\left\|{\mathbf{V}^{2}}\right\|+2^{\beta k}\mathbb{E}[M]\right)}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(M\geq 2^{\beta k}\right)+\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[M^{2}\right]}{2^{2\beta k}}\right)^{2}

(23)

\displaystyle\lesssim k_{*}2^{k}\exp\left(\frac{-2^{2\beta k}}{O\left(\left\|{\mathbf{V}^{2}}\right\|+2^{\beta k}\mathbb{E}[M]\right)}\right)+\mathbb{P}\left(M\geq\frac{2k_{*}2^{k}}{d}+\frac{2^{\beta k}}{2}\right)+\left(\frac{\mathbb{E}\left[M^{2}\right]}{2^{2\beta k}}\right)^{2}

(24)

\displaystyle\lesssim k_{*}2^{k}\exp\left(\frac{-2^{\beta k}}{O\left(\frac{k_{*}2^{k}}{d}+\frac{\sqrt{k_{*}2^{k}}}{{d^{1-1/q}}}\right)}\right)+\frac{\left(k_{*}2^{k}\right)^{q/2}}{d^{q-1}2^{2\beta k}}+\left(\frac{k_{*}2^{k(1-\beta)}}{d}+\frac{\sqrt{k_{*}}2^{(1/2-\beta)k}}{d^{1-1/q}}\right)^{4}.

(25)

By choosing
q=4
, we get

\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|{\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{k}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}}\right\|\geq 2^{\beta k}\right)\lesssim\frac{2^{k}}{\log(d)}d^{1-2^{k(\beta-1)}}+\frac{2^{2k(1-\beta)}}{d\log^{2}(d)}+\left(\frac{2^{k(1-\beta)}}{\log(d)}\right)^{4}.

(26)

Since
\beta>1
, (
26
) is summable and moreover,

\mathbb{P}\left(\bigcup_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|{\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{k}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\top}-\mathbf{I}}\right\|\geq 2^{\beta k}\right)\leq O\left(\log^{-4}(d)\right).

(27)

It follows that with probability atleast
1-O\left(\log^{-4}(d)\right)
, for all
k\geq 1
,

\left\|{\frac{1}{d}\sqrt{\mathbf{H}_{k}}\mathbf{W}_{k}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\top}\sqrt{\mathbf{H}_{k}}}\right\|\leq(1+2^{\beta k})\left\|{\mathbf{H}_{k}}\right\|\leq\left(\frac{1}{k_{*}2^{k}}\right)^{\alpha}+\frac{2^{k(\beta-\alpha)}}{k_{*}^{\alpha}}.

(28)

Since
\beta<\alpha
, we see that

\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|{\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{k}\mathbf{W}_{k}}\right\|\leq O\left(k_{*}^{-\alpha}\right).

(29)

Finally, combining (
8
), (
21
) and (
29
), there exist positive constants
c_{1},c_{2}
and
c_{3}
depending only on
\alpha
such that for all
1\leq j\leq c_{1}d\log^{-1}(d)
,

c_{2}j^{-\alpha}\leq\lambda_{j}\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{H}\mathbf{W}\right)\leq\lambda_{j}\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{0}\mathbf{W}_{0}\right)+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\left\|{\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\top}\mathbf{H}_{k}\mathbf{W}_{k}}\right\|\leq c_{3}j^{-\alpha},

(30)

which concludes our proof.
∎

4
Higher order case

In this section, we will generalize Theorem
2
to higher order monomials
f(x)=x^{\text{p}}
. We carry over the assumption that the data
\mathbf{x}\sim N(0,\mathbf{H})
, but we
replace the moment assumption on the sketch matrix
\mathbf{W}\in\mathbb{R}^{v\times d}
for independent standard Gaussian entries. Since
\mathbf{W}
is left/right invariant under orthogonal transformations, we will assume without loss of generality that
\mathbf{H}
is diagonal. To obtain bounds on the eigenvalues of
\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}[f(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 2}]
, where it is understood that
f
is applied entry wise to
\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x}
, it will be convenient to write
\mathbf{x}=\sqrt{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{z}
for
\mathbf{z}\sim N(0,\mathbf{I})
and to denote
\mathbf{y}^{(i)}=\sqrt{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{W}_{\text{col}(i)}
. We shall begin by decomposing this kernel into leading and sub-leading terms.
For the
p
-th order monomials, the
ij
-th entry is given by

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[f(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 2}_{ij}\right]

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\left[f(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\sqrt{\mathbf{H}}\mathbf{z})_{ij}^{\otimes 2}\right]

(31)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\left[\left\langle\mathbf{y}^{(i)},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle^{\text{p}}\left\langle\mathbf{y}^{(j)},\mathbf{z}\right\rangle^{\text{p}}\right]

(32)

\displaystyle=\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{2\text{p}}}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{z}_{i_{1}}\dots\mathbf{z}_{i_{2\text{p}}}\right]\mathbf{y}^{(i)}_{i_{1}}\dots\mathbf{y}^{(i)}_{i_{\text{p}}}\mathbf{y}^{(j)}_{i_{\text{p}+1}}\dots\mathbf{i}^{(j)}_{i_{2\text{p}}}.

(33)

Using Isserlis’ theorem
[
20
]
, the expectation of a product of Gaussians can be expressed as a sum of
products of second moments. Visually,
this product may be interpreted as a graph made up of
2\text{p}
-vertices, where each vertex has exactly one edge. We will refer to an edge joining vertex
r
and vertex
s
as a pair
(r,s)
.
Let
\mathcal{P}_{2}(2\text{p})
be the set of all partitions of
\{1,\dots,2\text{p}\}
into
p
pairs. We may then write

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z}}\left[\mathbf{z}_{i_{1}}\dots\mathbf{z}_{i_{2\text{p}}}\right]=\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(2\text{p})}\prod_{(r,s)\in\xi}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{z}_{i_{r}}\mathbf{z}_{i_{s}}\right]=\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(2\text{p})}\prod_{(r,s)\in\xi}\delta_{i_{r},i_{s}}.

(34)

Thus,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[f(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 2}_{ij}\right]=\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(2\text{p})}\sum_{i_{1},\cdots,i_{2\text{p}}}\prod_{(r,s)\in\xi}\delta_{i_{r},i_{s}}\mathbf{y}_{i_{1}}^{(i)}\dots\mathbf{y}^{(i)}_{i_{\text{p}}}\mathbf{y}^{(j)}_{i_{\text{p}+1}}\dots\mathbf{y}^{(j)}_{i_{2\text{p}}}

(35)

However, given that sub-indices from
1
to
p
and sub-indices from
\text{p}+1
to
2\text{p}
correspond to entries of
\mathbf{y}^{(i)}
and
\mathbf{y}^{(j)}
respectively, we can interpret each pair
(r,s)\in\xi
to be the product

\mathbf{y}^{(i)}_{i_{r}}\mathbf{y}^{(j)}_{i_{s}}
. Combined with the fact that the direc-delta implies
i_{r}=i_{s}
, the sum of
\mathbf{y}^{(i)}_{i_{r}}\mathbf{y}^{(j)}_{i_{r}}
across the index
i_{r}
corresponds to the inner product
\left\langle\mathbf{y}^{(i)},\mathbf{y}^{(j)}\right\rangle
. Let
C_{\xi}
be the number of pairs formed between
\{1,\dots,\text{p}\}
and
\{\text{p}+1,\dots,2\text{p}\}
, or equivalently the number of inner products
\left\langle\mathbf{y}^{(i)},\mathbf{y}^{(j)}\right\rangle
formed. Then,

\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[f(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 2}_{ij}\right]=\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(2\text{p})}\left(\left\langle\mathbf{y}^{(i)},\mathbf{y}^{(i)}\right\rangle\left\langle\mathbf{y}^{(j)},\mathbf{y}^{(j)}\right\rangle\right)^{\frac{\text{p}-C_{\xi}}{2}}\left\langle\mathbf{y}^{(i)},\mathbf{y}^{(j)}\right\rangle^{C_{\xi}}.

(36)

We will write
A_{\xi}=\frac{\text{p}-C_{\xi}}{2}
and
q=C_{\xi}
for simplicity of notation. The cross term inner product can be further expanded into

\left\langle\mathbf{y}^{(i)},\mathbf{y}^{(j)}\right\rangle^{q}=\sum_{i_{1},\dots,i_{q}}\mathbf{y}_{i_{1}}^{(i)}\mathbf{y}_{i_{1}}^{(j)}\dots\mathbf{y}^{(i)}_{i_{q}}\mathbf{y}^{(j)}_{i_{q}}.

(37)

Since the summand is symmetric, we can rewrite this sum as an ordered sum. In particular, given an unordered multiset
(j_{1},\dots,j_{q})
, if
\ell
is the number of unique indices in
\{j_{1},\dots,j_{q}\}
and
(i_{1}<\cdots<i_{\ell})
is the ordered unique indices, we may define
\pi_{r}
to be the multiplicity of
i_{r}
in
(j_{1},\dots,j_{q})
. Then,
\pi=(\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{\ell})
is a composition of
q
, i.e.,
\sum_{r=1}^{\ell}\pi_{r}=q
.
There are exactly
\ell!
multisets that map to the pair
((i_{1}<\cdots<i_{\ell}),\pi)
. Thus, (
37
) becomes

\displaystyle\left\langle\mathbf{y}^{(i)},\mathbf{y}^{(j)}\right\rangle^{q}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{q}\sum_{\pi}\sum_{i_{1}<\dots<i_{\ell}}\ell!\left(\mathbf{y}^{(i)}_{i_{1}}\mathbf{y}^{(j)}_{i_{1}}\right)^{\pi_{1}}\dots\left(\mathbf{y}^{(i)}_{i_{\ell}}\mathbf{y}^{(j)}_{i_{\ell}}\right)^{\pi_{\ell}},

(38)

where the sum across
\pi
indicates the summation across all compositions of
q
of size
\ell
.
Using this new ordered tuple
(i_{1}<\cdots<i_{\ell})
, if we define
\mathbf{Y}^{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{{v\choose l}\times d}
and
\mathbf{D}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}
by

\mathbf{Y}_{(i_{1}<\dots<i_{\ell}),i}^{\pi}=\mathbf{y}_{i_{1}}^{(i),\pi_{1}}\dots\mathbf{y}_{i_{\ell}}^{(i),\pi_{\ell}}\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{D}=\operatorname{Diag}\left(\left\langle\mathbf{y}^{(i)},\mathbf{y}^{(i)}\right\rangle:1\leq i\leq d\right),

(39)

we see that each summand of (
36
) is just the
ij
-th entry of the sum of
Gram-matrices
\left(\mathbf{Y}^{\pi}\mathbf{D}^{A_{\xi}}\right)^{\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\pi}\mathbf{D}^{A_{\xi}}
. Therefore,

\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x}}\left[f(\mathbf{W}^{\top}\mathbf{x})^{\otimes 2}\right]=\sum_{\xi\in\mathcal{P}_{2}(2\text{p})}\sum_{\ell=1}^{C_{\xi}}\sum_{\pi}\ell!\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{D}^{A_{\xi}}\mathbf{Y}^{\pi\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\pi}\mathbf{D}^{A_{\xi}}\right).

(40)

Note that this representation gives a decomposition of the covariance kernel into a finite
p
-dependent collection of positive semidefinite terms. As such, by Loewner ordering, we see that it suffices to bound the eigenvalues of each positive semidefinite matrix.

4.1
Principal term

Following (
40
), we first consider pairings
\xi
that only matches elements of
\{1,\dots,\text{p}\}
to
\{\text{p}+1,\cdots,2\text{p}\}
. For such pairings, we have that
C_{\xi}=\text{p}
,
A_{\xi}=0
and we sum over all compositions
\pi
of
p
. The trivial composition is the all
1
-composition which we will denote by
\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}=(1,\dots,1)
. We will refer to
\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}
as the principal term, since we will derive both upper and lower bounds for its spectrum.

Before proceeding with the proof of the bounds for the principal term, it will be useful to untangle
\mathbf{H}
from
\mathbf{Y}^{\pi}
by recalling that
\mathbf{y}^{(i)}_{i_{k}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{H}_{i_{k}}}\mathbf{W}_{i_{k},i}
. So, if we define a new sketch matrix
\mathbf{W}^{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{{v\choose l}\times d}
, along with a covariance matrix
\mathbf{H}^{\pi}\in\mathbb{R}^{{v\choose l}\times{v\choose l}}
by

\begin{gathered}\mathbf{W}_{(i_{1}<\dots<i_{\ell}),i}^{\pi}=\mathbf{W}_{i_{1},i}^{\pi_{1}}\dots\mathbf{W}_{i_{\ell},i}^{\pi_{\ell}},\quad\text{and}\quad\mathbf{H}^{\pi}=\text{Diag}\left(\left\{\mathbf{H}_{i_{1}}^{\pi_{1}}\dots\mathbf{H}_{i_{\ell}}^{\pi_{\ell}}\right\}_{(i_{1}<\dots<i_{\ell})}\right),\end{gathered}

(41)

then we see that

\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{Y}^{\pi\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\pi}=\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}^{\pi\top}\mathbf{H}^{\pi}\mathbf{W}^{\pi}.

(42)

which is analogous to the linear case as seen in (
5
). As such, we may apply a similar proof strategy of block-wise decomposition of
\mathbf{Y}^{\pi\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\pi}
to isolate the leading eigenvalue terms.

Proposition 1

(Principal term)
.

Assume
\alpha>1
and
d\leq v
.
There are constants
c_{1},c_{2}
and
c_{3}
depending only on
\alpha
and
p
so that with probability
1-O(\log^{-4}(d))
, for all
1\leq j\leq c_{1}d\log^{-(\text{p}+1)}(d)
,

c_{2}\left(\frac{\log^{(\text{p}-1)}(j+1)}{j}\right)^{\alpha}\leq\lambda_{j}\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\right)\leq c_{3}\left(\frac{\log^{(\text{p}-1)}(j+1)}{j}\right)^{\alpha}.

(43)

And for all
c_{1}d\log^{-(\text{p}+1)}(d)<j\leq d
,

c_{2}\left(j^{-1}\log^{-2}(j)\right)^{\alpha}\leq\lambda_{j}\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\right)\leq c_{3}\left(\frac{\log^{2\text{p}}(j+1)}{j}\right)^{\alpha}

(44)

Moreover,

\lambda_{d}\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\right)\geq c_{2}\left(\frac{\log^{\text{p}-1}(d)}{d}\right)^{\alpha}.

(45)

Proof.

Let
k_{*}>0
and
\mathcal{K}_{0}
be the collection of ordered tuples
(i_{1}<\cdots<i_{\text{p}})
such that
\mathbf{H}_{(i_{1}<\cdots<i_{\text{p}})}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}
are among the first
k_{*}
largest eigenvalues of
\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}
. Let
\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{*}\times d}
and
\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\in\mathbb{R}^{k_{*}\times k_{*}}
be the respective row restriction of
\mathbf{W}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}
and
\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}
onto
\mathcal{K}_{0}
. Similarly, for all
k\geq 1
, let
\mathcal{K}_{k}
be the collection of ordered tuples
(i_{1}<\cdots<i_{\text{p}})
such that
\mathbf{H}_{(i_{1}<\cdots<i_{\text{p}})}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}
are among the
(k_{*}2^{k-1}+1)
to
(k_{*}2^{k})
largest eigenvalues of
\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}
. Let
\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}
and
\mathbf{H}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}
be the row restriction of
\mathbf{W}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}
and
\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}
onto
\mathcal{K}_{k}
. We set the rows of
\mathbf{W}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}_{k}
to be zero if the corresponding ordered tuple does not exist. We may then decompose
\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}
as

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}=\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{H^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}}\mathbf{W}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}=\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}+\frac{1}{d}\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{H}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}.

(46)

From Lemma
13
, we know for
j\leq d
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}})\asymp\left(\frac{\log^{(\text{p}-1)}(j+1)}{j}\right)^{\alpha},

(47)

and for
j>d
,

\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}})\lesssim\left(\frac{\log^{(\text{p}-1)}(j+1)}{j}\right)^{\alpha}.

(48)

As in the linear case, it suffices to show
\left\|{\frac{1}{d}{\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}_{\text{p}}}}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}_{\text{p}}\top}-\mathbf{I}}\right\|
is sufficiently bounded. Fix
k\geq 0
, by a rank-
1
decomposition we have

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\sqrt{\mathbf{H}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}{\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}}\sqrt{\mathbf{H}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}}-\mathbf{H}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{H}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}}\left(\frac{1}{d}\sum_{i=1}^{d}\bm{\omega}^{(i)}\bm{\omega}^{(i)\top}-\mathbf{I}\right)\sqrt{\mathbf{H}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}},

(49)

where
\bm{\omega}^{(i)}
are the column vectors of
\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}
. Let
M=\max_{i\in[d]}\frac{1}{d}\left\|\bm{\omega}^{(i)}\bm{\omega}^{(i)\top}-\mathbf{I}\right\|
. We first provide moments and tail estimates for
M
. Note that

\displaystyle M

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i}\frac{1}{d}\|\bm{\omega}^{(i)}\|^{2}+\frac{1}{d}

(50)

\displaystyle\leq\max_{i}\frac{1}{d}|\|\bm{\omega}^{(i)}\|^{2}-\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right||+\frac{\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right|+1}{d}

(51)

\displaystyle\leq\tilde{M}+\frac{2\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right|}{d},

(52)

where
\tilde{M}=\max_{i}\frac{1}{d}|\|\bm{\omega}^{(i)}\|^{2}-\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right||
. For simplicity of notation, we drop the superscription from
\bm{\omega}^{(i)}
and write just
\bm{\omega}
. Then each term

\|\bm{\omega}\|^{2}=\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{\text{p}})\in\mathcal{K}_{k}}\bm{\omega}_{i_{1}}^{2}\cdots\bm{\omega}_{i_{\text{p}}}^{2}

is a degree-
2\text{p}
polynomial in Gaussian random variables
\bm{\omega}
. Moreover,

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}(\|\bm{\omega}\|^{2})\leq\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\omega}\|^{4}-(\mathbb{E}\|\bm{\omega}\|^{2})^{2}\leq 3^{\text{p}}(\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right|)^{2}.

(53)

From the Gaussian hypercontractivity inequality in Lemma
5
,

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(|\|\bm{\omega}^{(i)}\|^{2}-\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right||\geq t\right)\leq e^{2}\exp\left(-C\left(\frac{t}{\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right|}\right)^{1/\text{p}}\right).

(54)

Taking union bound over
i\in[d]
, we obtain

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}(M\geq t)\lesssim d\exp\left(-C\left(\frac{td}{\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right|}-2\right)^{1/\text{p}}\right).

(55)

From (
54
), we also get the moment bound by taking
t_{0}=\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right|\log^{\text{p}}(d)
and

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\tilde{M}^{q}\right]

\displaystyle=d^{-q}q\int_{0}^{t_{0}}t^{q-1}\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{M}\geq t\right)dt+d^{-q}q\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}t^{q-1}\mathbb{P}\left(\tilde{M}\geq t\right)dt

(56)

\displaystyle\lesssim d^{-q}t_{0}^{q}+d^{-q}\int_{t_{0}}^{\infty}\exp(-C(t/k_{*})^{1/\text{p}})dt

(57)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\frac{\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right|}{d}\right)^{q}\log^{\text{p}q}(d),

(58)

where in the last inequality we use the gamma tail estimate for
x\geq 2(\text{p}-1)
and
\text{p}\geq 1
,

\int_{x}^{\infty}u^{\text{p}-1}e^{-u}du\leq 2x^{\text{p}-1}e^{-x}.

Therefore from (
52
),

\mathbb{E}\left[M^{q}\right]\lesssim\left(\frac{\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right|\log^{\text{p}}(d)}{d}\right)^{q}.

(59)

Let
\mathbf{A}_{i}=\frac{1}{d}\left(\bm{\omega}^{(i)}\bm{\omega}^{(i)\top}-\mathbf{I}\right).
It is easy to check via independence that
\mathbb{E}[\mathbf{A}_{i}^{2}]
is diagonal and the
j
-th diagonal term satisfies
\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\bm{\omega}^{(i)}\bm{\omega}^{(i)\top}\right)^{2}\right]_{jj}\leq 3^{\text{p}}\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right|.

By choosing
\mathbf{V}^{2}=\frac{3^{\text{p}}\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right|}{d}\mathbf{I}
, we see that

\sum_{i=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{A}_{i}^{2}\right]\succeq\mathbf{V}^{2}\quad\text{and}\quad\left\|{\mathbf{V}^{2}}\right\|=\frac{3^{\text{p}}\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right|}{d}.

(60)

Step 1: Two-sided bound on
\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}
.

If
k=0
, then
\left|{\mathcal{K}_{0}}\right|=k_{*}
. We now take
k_{*}=c_{1}d\log^{-(\text{p}+1)}(d)
for a sufficiently small constant
c_{1}>0
. Then,
\max\{4\sigma,32\mathbb{E}M\}\leq\frac{1}{24}
for sufficiently large
d
.
Using (
55
) and (
59
), we may apply Lemma
4
with
t=\frac{1}{24}
and
q=2
to get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|{\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}-\mathbf{I}}\right\|\geq\frac{1}{2}\right)\lesssim\frac{1}{\log^{4}(d)}.

(61)

Therefore, with probability at least
1-O(\log^{-4}(d))
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\preceq\frac{1}{d}\sqrt{\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}{\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}}^{\top}\sqrt{\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}}\preceq\frac{3}{2}\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}.

(62)

Hence, by the Loewner ordering of PSD matrices, for
1\leq j\leq c_{1}d\log^{-(\text{p}+1)}(d)
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\mathbf{W}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\right)\asymp\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{H}_{0}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}})\asymp\left(\frac{\log^{(\text{p}-1)}(j+1)}{j}\right)^{\alpha}.

(63)

Step 2: Upper bound on
\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{H}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}
.

If
k\geq 1
, then
\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right|=k_{*}2^{k-1}
. It is easy to check for any
\beta\in(1,\alpha)
that
\max\{4k_{*}2^{k-1},32\mathbb{E}[M]\}\leq 12\left(2^{\beta k}\right)
. Thus, using Lemma
4
with
q=2
and
t=12(2^{\beta k}-1)
, we get

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}-\mathbf{I}\right\|\geq 2^{\beta k}-1\right)

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{k-1}d^{1-\frac{1}{c_{3}}2^{(\beta-1)k}}+d\exp(-C2^{(\beta-1)k}\log^{(p+1)/\text{p}}(d))+\left(\frac{2^{(1-\beta)k}}{\log d}\right)^{4},

(64)

which is summable. Therefore, after taking the union bound, we see that with probability at least
1-O(\log^{-4}(d))
, for all
k\geq 1
,

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\preceq 2^{\beta k}\mathbf{I}.

(65)

In particular, this implies

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\|\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{H}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\|

\displaystyle\leq 2^{\beta k}\|\mathbf{H}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\|

(66)

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{\beta k}(2^{k-1}k_{*})^{-\alpha}\log(2^{k-1}k_{*})^{\alpha(p-1)}

(67)

\displaystyle\lesssim 2^{(\beta-\alpha)k}\left(\frac{\log(2^{k-1}k_{*})}{\log k_{*}}\right)^{\alpha(p-1)}\lambda_{k_{*}}(\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}).

(68)

Summing over
k\geq 1
, we obtain

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{d}\|\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{H}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\mathbf{W}_{k}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\|\lesssim\lambda_{k_{*}}(\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}).

(69)

Combining (
46
), (
63
) and (
69
), we see that for all
1\leq j\leq k_{*}
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\right)\asymp\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}})\asymp j^{-\alpha}(\log(1+j))^{\alpha(p-1)}.

(70)

This concludes the proof of (
43
).

Step 3: Upper bound on
\lambda_{j}\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\right)
for
j>c_{1}d\log^{-(\text{p}+1)}(d)
.

If
k_{*}<j\leq d
, we have
\log(j+1)\asymp\log d
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\lambda_{k_{*}}\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\right)

(71)

\displaystyle\lesssim\left(\frac{d}{\log^{\text{p}+1}(d)}\right)^{-\alpha}\log^{\alpha(\text{p}-1)}(d)

(72)

\displaystyle\lesssim j^{-\alpha}\log^{2\alpha\text{p}}(j+1).

(73)

Step 4: Lower bound on
\lambda_{j}\left(\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\right)
for
j>c_{1}d\log^{-(\text{p}+1)}(d)
.

We will first show the least singular value bound (
45
).
Let
\mathbf{r}_{j}^{\top}
be the
j
-th row of
\mathbf{W}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}
. Choose
n_{*}=cd
for some sufficiently small constant
c>0
, then

\displaystyle\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}=\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{H^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}}\mathbf{W}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{d}\sum_{j=1}^{\infty}\lambda_{j}(\mathbf{H^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}})\mathbf{r}_{j}\mathbf{r}_{j}^{\top}

(74)

\displaystyle\succeq\frac{1}{d}\lambda_{n_{*}}(\mathbf{H^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}})\sum_{j=1}^{n_{*}}\mathbf{r}_{j}\mathbf{r}_{j}^{\top}

(75)

\displaystyle=\lambda_{n_{*}}(\mathbf{H^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}})\cdot\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{n_{*}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{n_{*}},

(76)

where
\mathbf{W}_{n_{*}}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{*}\times d}
is the first
n_{*}
rows of
\mathbf{W}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}
. Now
\mathbf{W}_{n_{*}}^{\top}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times n_{*}}
is a random matrix with independent, isotropic rows
\bm{\omega}
such that for every
\mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{n_{*}}
and
\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}=1
,
\langle\bm{\omega},\mathbf{x}\rangle
is an order-
p
polynomial of independent Gaussian random variables with
\operatorname{Var}(\langle\bm{\omega},\mathbf{x}\rangle)=\|\mathbf{x}\|_{2}^{2}=1
. From Gaussian hypercontractivity in Lemma
5
,

\mathbb{P}(|\langle\bm{\omega},\mathbf{x}\rangle|\geq u)\leq 2\exp(-Cu^{2/\text{p}})\leq\frac{L}{u^{2+\eta}}.

for some
\eta>0
and
L>0
.
Then, from the least singular value bound of random matrices with independent rows in
[
25
, Theorem 1.3]
, we have with probability
1-e^{-Cd}
,
\lambda_{d}(\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{n_{*}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{n_{*}})=\Omega(1)
. This implies

\displaystyle\lambda_{d}\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\right)\geq\lambda_{n_{*}}(\mathbf{H^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}})\cdot\lambda_{d}\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{W}_{n_{*}}^{\top}\mathbf{W}_{n_{*}}\right)\gtrsim\lambda_{n_{*}}(\mathbf{H^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}})\geq c_{2}\left(\frac{\log^{\text{p}-1}(d)}{d}\right)^{\alpha}.

(77)

Since for
c_{1}d\log^{-(\text{p}+1)}(d)\leq j\leq d
,

\displaystyle\lambda_{j}\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\right)

\displaystyle\geq\lambda_{d}\left(\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}\right)\geq c_{2}d^{-\alpha}(\log(d))^{\alpha(\text{p}-1)}\geq c_{2}^{\prime}j^{-\alpha}(\log(1+j))^{-2\alpha}.

(78)

Combining this with (
73
) concludes (
44
).

4.2
Subleading terms and Wick chaoses

In this section, we focus on the remaining non-principal matrices of (
40
). We will refer to these matrices as lower order terms or subleading terms, and they consist of matrices of the form:

1.

\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{Y}^{\pi\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\pi}
, if

C_{\xi}=\text{p}
and
\pi\neq\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}
.

2.

\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{D}^{A_{\xi}}\mathbf{Y}^{\pi\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\pi}\mathbf{D}^{A_{\xi}}
, if
C_{\xi}\neq\text{p}
and
A_{\xi}\geq 1
.

Using (
41
), we can write the sample data matrix in both cases as
\mathbf{Y}^{\pi}\mathbf{D}^{A_{\xi}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{H}^{\pi}}\mathbf{W}^{\pi}\mathbf{D}^{A_{\xi}}
. Notice that in contrast to the principal term, when
\mathbf{Y}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}=\sqrt{\mathbf{H}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}}\mathbf{W}^{\mathbf{1}^{\text{p}}}
, the entries of
\mathbf{Y}^{\pi}\mathbf{D}^{A_{\xi}}
terms are not uncorrelated. Implying that even after applying our block-wise strategy,
\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{D}^{A_{\xi}}\mathbf{Y}^{\pi\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\pi}\mathbf{D}^{A_{\xi}}
would not concentrate to the diagonal covariance
\mathbf{H}^{\pi}
. Therefore, we will need to uncorrelate the lower order terms before applying our block-wise strategy. To do so, we will utilize the Wick product decomposition.

Definition 2
.

Let
\mathbf{P}_{n}
be the set of all Gaussian polynomials of degree less than or equal to
n
and
\mathbf{G}^{:n:}=\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{n}\cap\overline{\mathbf{P}}_{n-1}^{\perp}
, where the closure and orthogonal complement are taken with respect to the
L^{2}
inner-product. If
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
are centered joint Gaussians, then the Wick product denoted by
\left\langle g_{1}\cdots g_{n}\right\rangle
is defined as the orthogonal projection of
\prod_{i=1}^{n}g_{i}
onto
\mathbf{G}^{:n:}
, i.e.,

\left\langle g_{1}\cdots g_{n}\right\rangle\equiv\pi_{n}(g_{1}\cdots g_{n}).

(79)

For an overview on Wick products, see Appendix
B.1
or
[
20
]
. There are two main advantages of working with Wick products.
First, one can perform a change of basis to move between the original Gaussian product and the orthogonal projection. See Lemma
7
and
8
. This transformation can be systematically tracked by graphical representations known as Feynman diagrams:

Definition 3
.

A rank-
r
Feynman diagram of a collection of random variables
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
, is a graph
\gamma
consisting of
n
vertices labeled
g_{1},\dots,g_{n}
with
r
edges that are vertex disjoint. Let
\mathcal{P}(\gamma)
be the set of all paired vertices and
\mathcal{U}(\gamma)
the set of all unpaired vertices, we define the value of
\gamma
to be

v(\gamma)=\prod_{(r,s)\in\mathcal{P}(\gamma)}\mathbb{E}\left[g_{r}g_{s}\right]\prod_{i\in\mathcal{U}(\gamma)}g_{i}.

(80)

We say that a Feynman diagram is complete if the number of edges is
n/2
, i.e.,
\mathcal{U}(\gamma)=\varnothing
.

This implies that the machinery we used in the proof of the principal case should carry over nicely after projecting
\mathbf{Y}^{\pi}\mathbf{D}^{A_{\xi}}
. The second advantage is that the Wick matrices formed from the orthogonal projection of
\mathbf{Y}^{\pi}\mathbf{D}^{A_{\xi}}

inherently possess diagonal covariance, thus solving the correlation problem. See Lemma
9
. To illustrate the Wick decomposition method, we will start with the case that
A_{\xi}=0
before turning to the more general case
A_{\xi}\geq 0
in Section
4.2.2
.

4.2.1

We consider the lower order term
\frac{1}{d}\mathbf{Y}^{\pi\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\pi}
and a fixed composition

of

. Since the entries

\prod_{j=1}^{\ell}\mathbf{y}^{(a),\pi_{j}}_{i_{j}}=\sum_{\gamma}\prod_{(r,s)\in\mathcal{P}(\gamma)}\mathbb{E}\left[\mathbf{y}^{(a)}_{i_{r}}\mathbf{y}^{(a)}_{i_{s}}\right]\left\langle\prod_{j\in\mathcal{U}(\gamma)}\mathbf{y}^{(a)}_{i_{j}}\right\rangle.

4.2.2

A_{\xi}\geq 0

Lemma 1
.

There exists a finite collection of index sets
\mathcal{I}_{\theta}
and Wick matrices
\left\langle\mathbf{Y}^{\theta}\right\rangle\in\mathbb{R}^{|\mathcal{I}_{\theta}|\times d}
such that

\mathbf{D}^{A_{\xi}}\mathbf{Y}^{\pi\top}\mathbf{Y}^{\pi}\mathbf{D}^{A_{\xi}}\preceq\sum_{\theta}\left\langle\mathbf{Y}^{\theta}\right\rangle^{\top}\left\langle\mathbf{Y}^{\theta}\right\rangle.

(96)

Proof.

The general case follows a similar Wick decomposition as in the case of
A_{\xi}=0
, except now we have to also take into account extra terms gained from the diagonal matrix
\mathbf{D}^{A_{\xi}}
. Using (
38
), we may expand the entry of

. By Lemma
9
, we easily compute the first moment to be

\frac{1}{d}\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\omega}^{(a)}\bm{\omega}^{(a)\top}\right]=\frac{(\rho-2\eta)!}{d}\mathbf{I}.

(125)

For the second moment, we consider the quadratic form. Let
\mathbf{u}\in\mathbb{R}^{\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right|}
, then

\displaystyle\mathbf{u}^{\top}\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\bm{\omega}^{(a)}\bm{\omega}^{(a)\top}\right)^{2}\right]\mathbf{u}

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left[\bm{\omega}^{(a)\top}\bm{\omega}^{(a)}\mathbf{u}^{\top}\bm{\omega}^{(a)}\bm{\omega}^{(a)\top}\mathbf{u}\right]

(126)

\displaystyle\leq\left\|{\bm{\omega}^{(a)\top}\bm{\omega}^{(a)}}\right\|_{L^{2}}\left\|{\mathbf{u}^{\top}\bm{\omega}^{(a)}\bm{\omega}^{(a)\top}\mathbf{u}}\right\|_{L^{2}}.

(127)

It is easy to check via the triangle inequality that there exists
C_{\rho,\eta}>0
such that

\left\|{\bm{\omega}^{(a)\top}\bm{\omega}^{(a)}}\right\|_{L^{2}}\leq C_{\rho,\eta}\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right|.

(128)

For the latter term of (
127
), we observe from Lemma
7
that

(\bm{\omega}^{(a)})_{(\tilde{\mathbf{i}},\tilde{\mathbf{s}})}=\left\langle\prod_{j\in\mathcal{W}_{\rho,\eta}^{1}}\mathbf{W}^{\rho_{j}-2\eta_{j}}_{i_{j},a}\prod_{t\in\mathcal{W}_{\rho,\eta}^{2}}\mathbf{W}^{\rho_{\ell+t}-2\eta_{\ell+t}}_{s_{t},a}\right\rangle

\text{p}^{\prime}

2\text{p}^{\prime}

\displaystyle\operatorname{Var}\left[\mathbf{u}^{\top}\bm{\omega}^{(a)}\bm{\omega}^{(a)\top}\mathbf{u}\right]

(131)

So by Lemma
5

(132)

A straight forward computation then shows

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left[\left(\mathbf{u}^{\top}\bm{\omega}^{(a)}\bm{\omega}^{(a)\top}-(\rho-2\eta)!\right)^{2}\right]\leq 4V_{\rho,\eta}(2(\text{p}^{\prime}-1))^{\text{p}^{\prime}-1}.

and
r(\mathbf{V}^{2})=\left|{\mathcal{K}_{k}}\right|
. Before proceeding with the rest of the proof, we will obtain some bounds on

(136)

So, by Lemma

Lemma 3
.

Let
\{\bm{\omega}^{(i)}\}_{i=1}^{d}
be independent random vectors in
\mathbb{R}^{s}
such that for every
i\in[d]
and every
p\geq 1
, there exist a
C_{p}>0
with the property that for all
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}
,

\left\|{\left\langle\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\otimes 2},\mathbf{H}\right\rangle-\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{H})}\right\|_{L^{p}}\leq C_{p}\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{F}.

(172)

If
\mathbf{H}\in\mathbb{R}^{s\times s}
is PSD and
M=\max_{1\leq i\leq d}\left\|{\frac{\sqrt{\mathbf{H}}\left(\bm{\omega}^{(i)}\right)^{\otimes 2}\sqrt{\mathbf{H}}-\mathbf{H}}{d}}\right\|
then for all
q\geq 1
, there exists
C_{q}>0
such that for all
t>0
,

\mathbb{P}\left(M\geq\frac{2\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{H})}{d}+t\right)\leq\frac{C_{q}\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{F}^{q}}{d^{q-1}t^{q}}.

(173)

Moreover, for all
q>p
,

\mathbb{E}[M^{p}]\leq\left(\frac{2\operatorname{tr}{\mathbf{H}}}{d}+\frac{2pC_{q}\left\|{\mathbf{H}}\right\|_{F}}{d^{1-1/q}}\right)^{p}.

(174)

Proof.

(
173
) follows from the observation that
\sqrt{\mathbf{H}}\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\otimes 2}\sqrt{\mathbf{H}}
is rank-
1
so its operator norm is the quadratic
\left\langle\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\otimes 2},\mathbf{H}\right\rangle
. It follows that

\left\|{\sqrt{\mathbf{H}}\bm{\omega}_{i}^{\otimes 2}\sqrt{\mathbf{H}}-\mathbf{H}}\right\|\leq\left|{\left\langle\bm{\omega}^{\otimes 2},\mathbf{H}\right\rangle}\right|+\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{H}).

(175)

Plugging this into the tail bound gives

\displaystyle\mathbb{P}\left(M\geq\frac{2\operatorname{tr}{(\mathbf{H})}}{d}+t\right)

\displaystyle\leq d\,\mathbb{P}\left(\left\|{\frac{\sqrt{\mathbf{H}}\bm{\omega}_{1}^{\otimes 2}\sqrt{\mathbf{H}}-\mathbf{H}}{d}}\right\|\geq\frac{2\operatorname{tr}{(\mathbf{H})}}{d}+t\right)

(176)

\displaystyle\leq d\,\mathbb{P}\left(\left|\left\langle\bm{\omega}_{1}^{\otimes 2},\mathbf{H}\right\rangle-\operatorname{tr}{(\mathbf{H})}\right|>td\right).

(177)

Applying (
172
) with Markov’s inequality then gives the desired tail bound. For (
174
), using (
173
) we see that if
q>p
and
\epsilon>0
, then

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}[M^{p}]

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathbb{P}\left(M\geq t^{1/p}\right)\,\mathrm{d}t

(178)

Appendix C
Lattice point counting

In this section, we prove for completeness a lattice point counting problem, which gives the asymptotic behavior of the population covariance spectra, as well as an upper bound on Weyl Chamber version of the same counting problem (Theorem
4
). Problems like these have been studied extensively in number theory, see for example
[
2
,
36
,
46
]
.

Fix
k\geq 1
and positive real numbers
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k}
.
Define

\pi_{*}\coloneqq\min_{1\leq i\leq k}\pi_{i},\qquad m\coloneqq\#\{i:\ \pi_{i}=\pi_{*}\},\qquad\sigma_{0}:=\frac{1}{\pi_{*}}.

Define the unordered and ordered lattice point counts as

A_{k}(X)\coloneqq\#\Bigl\{(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}:\ s_{1}^{\pi_{1}}\cdots s_{k}^{\pi_{k}}\leq X\Bigr\},

and respectively

N_{\downarrow}(X)\coloneqq\#\Bigl\{(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}:\ 1\leq s_{1}<\cdots\leq s_{k},\ s_{1}^{\pi_{1}}\cdots s_{k}^{\pi_{k}}\leq X\Bigr\}.

Let
\zeta(s)
be the Riemann
\zeta
-function, which for
s>1
is given by the Dirichlet series

\zeta(s)\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n^{s}}.

We will use Tauberian theory to give the asymptotic behavior of the lattice point counting problems.

Theorem 3

(Unordered tuples)
.

As
X\to\infty
,

A_{k}(X)\sim\frac{\pi_{*}^{\,1-m}}{\Gamma(m)}\Biggl(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq k\\
\pi_{i}>\pi_{*}\end{subarray}}\zeta\!\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{*}}\Bigr)\Biggr)\,X^{1/\pi_{*}}\,(\log X)^{m-1}.

(202)

In particular, if all
\pi_{i}
are equal to a common value
\pi
(so
m=k
), then

A_{k}(X)\sim\frac{\pi^{\,1-k}}{\Gamma(k)}\,X^{1/\pi}(\log X)^{k-1}.

For the ordered case, the count differs from the unordered case, save for when all
\pi_{i}
are equal.

Theorem 4

(Strictly decreasing tuples)
.

Let
k\geq 1
and positive integers
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k}
. Define

\Pi_{r}\coloneqq\pi_{k}+\cdots+\pi_{k-r+1}
and
\theta_{r}\coloneqq r/\Pi_{r}
, and set

\theta^{\star}\coloneqq\max_{1\leq r\leq k}\theta_{r}.

For the ordered count

N_{\downarrow}(X)\coloneqq\#\{(1\leq s_{1}<\cdots<s_{k}):\ s_{1}^{\pi_{1}}\cdots s_{k}^{\pi_{k}}\leq X\},

let

\mu\coloneqq\#\{\,r\in\{1,\dots,k\}:\ \theta_{r}=\theta^{\star}\,\}.

Then, as
X\to\infty
,

N_{\downarrow}(X)\ \lesssim X^{\theta^{\star}}(\log X)^{\mu-1}.

In the case that all
\pi_{i}
are equal to a common value
\pi
,

N_{\downarrow}(X)\sim\frac{\pi^{\,1-k}}{\Gamma(k)\Gamma(k+1)}\,X^{1/\pi}(\log X)^{k-1}.

X\asymp\left(\frac{N}{\log(N)^{k-1}}\right)^{\pi}
as
X\to\infty

Proof.

, we know that

. So if we take the limit of the ratio between
\log(X)
and
\log(N)

\displaystyle\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac{\log(X)}{\log(N)}\asymp\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac{\log(X)}{\log(X^{1/\pi}(\log X)^{k-1}}=\lim_{X\to\infty}\frac{\log(X)^{k-1}}{\frac{1}{\pi}\log(X)^{k-1}+(k-1)\log(X)^{k-2}}=\pi.

(203)

Which is to say that for

and

depending only on

(204)

It follows that

\frac{N}{\log(N)^{k-1}}\asymp\frac{N}{\log(X)^{k-1}}\asymp X^{1/\pi},

(205)

as desired.

Theorem 5

(Delange’s Tauberian theorem)
.

Let
F(s)\coloneqq\sum_{n\geq 1}\theta(n)n^{-s}
be a Dirichlet series with nonnegative coefficients and convergent and analytic for
\mathrm{Re}\,(s)>\varrho>0
. Assume that for
\beta>0
, in a neighborhood of
\varrho
intersected with
(\varrho,\infty)

F(s)=\frac{H(s)}{(s-\varrho)^{\beta}}+G(s)

where
G
and
H
are analytic in a neighborhood of
s=\varrho
.
Then as
N\to\infty
,

\sum_{n\leq N}\theta(n)\sim\frac{H(\varrho)}{\varrho\Gamma(\beta)}N^{\varrho}(\log N)^{\beta-1}.

Proof of Theorem
3
.

Let
r(n)
be the number of
k
-tuples
(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})\in\mathbb{N}^{k}
with

s_{1}^{\pi_{1}}\cdots s_{k}^{\pi_{k}}=n
. Consider the Dirichlet series

F(s)\coloneqq\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{r(n)}{n^{s}}=\sum_{s_{1},\dots,s_{k}\geq 1}\frac{1}{(s_{1}^{\pi_{1}}\cdots s_{k}^{\pi_{k}})^{s}}=\prod_{i=1}^{k}\zeta(\pi_{i}s),\qquad\mathrm{Re}\,s>\sigma_{0}.

Each factor
\zeta(\pi_{i}s)
has a simple pole at
s=1/\pi_{i}
with principal part

\zeta(\pi_{i}s)=\frac{1}{\pi_{i}}\cdot\frac{1}{s-1/\pi_{i}}+O(1).

Therefore,
F
has its rightmost singularity at
s=\sigma_{0}=1/\pi_{*}
, and the pole at
s=\sigma_{0}
has order
m
equal to the multiplicity of the minimum exponent. Writing the principal part near
s=\sigma_{0}
,

F(s)=\frac{H(s)}{(s-\sigma_{0})^{m}}+G(s),

where
H
is holomorphic near
\sigma_{0}
with

H(\sigma_{0})=\Bigl(\prod_{\pi_{i}=\pi_{*}}\frac{1}{\pi_{*}}\Bigr)\prod_{\pi_{i}>\pi_{*}}\zeta\!\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{*}}\Bigr)>0,

and
G
is holomorphic on a half-strip
\{\mathrm{Re}\,s>\sigma_{0}-\delta\}
for some
\delta>0
(which follows as
\zeta
is meromorphic function with a simple pole at
s=1
).

Since
r(n)\geq 0
, we may apply Theorem
5
. It yields

\sum_{n\leq X}r(n)\sim\frac{H(\sigma_{0})}{\sigma_{0}\,\Gamma(m)}\,X^{\sigma_{0}}(\log X)^{m-1}\qquad(X\to\infty).

But
\sum_{n\leq X}r(n)=A_{k}(X)
by definition, and
\sigma_{0}=1/\pi_{*}
. Hence

A_{k}(X)\sim\frac{H(\sigma_{0})}{(1/\pi_{*})\,\Gamma(m)}\,X^{1/\pi_{*}}(\log X)^{m-1}=\frac{\pi_{*}^{\,1-m}}{\Gamma(m)}\Biggl(\prod_{\pi_{i}>\pi_{*}}\zeta\!\Bigl(\frac{\pi_{i}}{\pi_{*}}\Bigr)\Biggr)X^{1/\pi_{*}}(\log X)^{m-1},

which is exactly (
202
).
∎

Theorem 6
.

Let
k\geq 1
and positive integers
\pi_{1},\dots,\pi_{k}
. Define

\Pi_{r}\coloneqq\pi_{1}+\cdots+\pi_{r}
and
\theta_{r}\coloneqq r/\Pi_{r}
, and set

\theta^{\star}\coloneqq\max_{1\leq r\leq k}\theta_{r}.

For the ordered count

N_{\downarrow}(X)\coloneqq\#\{(s_{1}>\cdots>s_{k}\geq 1):\ s_{1}^{\pi_{1}}\cdots s_{k}^{\pi_{k}}\leq X\},

let

\mu\coloneqq\#\{\,r\in\{1,\dots,k\}:\ \theta_{r}=\theta^{\star}\,\}.

Then, as
X\to\infty
,

N_{\downarrow}(X)\ \lesssim\ X^{\theta^{\star}}(\log X)^{\mu-1}.

Proof.

Put
M:=\lfloor\log_{2}X\rfloor
. For each
i
, place
s_{i}
into a dyadic
interval,

s_{i}\in[2^{m_{i}},2^{m_{i}+1})\qquad(m_{i}\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}).

The constraint
s_{1}^{\pi_{1}}\cdots s_{k}^{\pi_{k}}\leq X
implies

\sum_{i=1}^{k}\pi_{i}m_{i}\ \leq\ M,

and the ordering
s_{1}>\cdots>s_{k}\geq 1
implies
m_{1}\geq\cdots\geq m_{k}\geq 0
.
For any fixed dyadic pattern
(m_{1},\dots,m_{k})
, the number of integer

k
-tuples
(s_{1},\dots,s_{k})
with
s_{i}\in[2^{m_{i}},2^{m_{i}+1})
and

s_{1}>\cdots>s_{k}
is at most

\prod_{i=1}^{k}\#\,[2^{m_{i}},2^{m_{i}+1})\ \lesssim\ \prod_{i=1}^{k}2^{m_{i}}\ =\ 2^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}}.

Therefore,

N_{\downarrow}(X)\ \lesssim\ \sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}m_{1}\geq\cdots\geq m_{k}\geq 0\\
\sum\pi_{i}m_{i}\leq M\end{subarray}}2^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}}.

(211)

Step 1: Forward differences and two linear forms.

Introduce forward differences

\Delta_{j}:=m_{j}-m_{j+1}\quad(j=1,\dots,k),\qquad m_{k+1}:=0.

Then
m_{j}=\sum_{\ell\geq j}\Delta_{\ell}
, and so by using summation by parts, we have the identities

\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}j\,\Delta_{j},\qquad\sum_{i=1}^{k}\pi_{i}m_{i}=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Pi_{j}\,\Delta_{j},

(212)

with
\Pi_{j}\coloneqq\sum_{i=1}^{j}\pi_{i}
. Since
m_{1}\geq\cdots\geq m_{k}\geq 0
, we have

\Delta_{j}\geq 0\quad(1\leq j\leq k).

Define
\theta_{r}\coloneqq r/\Pi_{r}
and
\theta^{\star}\coloneqq\max_{r}\theta_{r}
, and for each
j

set the nonnegative “deviation”

\eta_{j}\coloneqq\theta^{\star}\Pi_{j}-j\ \geq\ 0.

Using (
212
), we have the exact decomposition

\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}=\theta^{\star}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Pi_{j}\Delta_{j}-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}\Delta_{j}.

(213)

Step 2: The exponential penalty.

Let

J_{\mathrm{eq}}:=\{j:\eta_{j}=0\}=\{j:\theta_{j}=\theta^{\star}\},\qquad J_{\mathrm{str}}:=\{1,\dots,k\}\setminus J_{\mathrm{eq}},

and let
\mu
be the cardinality of
J_{\mathrm{eq}}
.
Define the feasible set of
\{\Delta_{j}\}
as

\mathcal{D}\coloneqq\{\Delta=(\Delta_{1},\dots,\Delta_{k})\in\mathbb{N}_{0}^{k}:\quad\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Pi_{j}\Delta_{j}\leq M\},

and the slice
\mathcal{D}_{A,B}
as the subset of
\mathcal{D}
for which

\mathcal{D}_{A,B}\coloneqq\{\Delta\in\mathcal{D}:\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Pi_{j}\Delta_{j}=A,\quad\sum_{j\in J_{\mathrm{str}}}\Delta_{j}=B\}.

On the feasible set
\mathcal{D}
, (
213
) yields

2^{\sum_{i=1}^{k}m_{i}}\ \leq\ 2^{\theta^{\star}\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Pi_{j}\Delta_{j}}\,2^{-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}\Delta_{j}}\leq X^{\theta^{\star}}\,2^{\theta^{\star}(\sum_{j=1}^{k}\Pi_{j}\Delta_{j}-M)-\sum_{j=1}^{k}\eta_{j}\Delta_{j}}.

(214)

Inserting (
214
) into (
211
) and passing to

\Delta
-variables gives

N_{\downarrow}(X)\ \lesssim\ X^{\theta^{\star}}\sum_{A,B=0}^{M}\sum_{\Delta\in\mathcal{D}_{A,B}}2^{\theta^{\star}(A-M)-\eta_{*}B},

(215)

where
\eta_{*}=\min_{j\in J_{\mathrm{str}}}\eta_{j}
.

Step 3: The cardinality of
\mathcal{D}_{A,B}
.

The cardinality of
\mathcal{D}_{A,B}
can be estimated above by specifying
(k-\mu)-1
non-negative integers less than
B
for the integers in
J_{\mathrm{str}}
and
\mu-1
non-negative integers less than
A
for remainder (noting the sum constraints determine the last integer). Hence,

|\mathcal{D}_{A,B}|\leq(1+B)^{k-\mu-1}(1+A)^{\mu-1}.

Hence using (
215
), we have

N_{\downarrow}(X)\ \lesssim\ X^{\theta^{\star}}\sum_{A,B=0}^{M}2^{\theta^{\star}(A-M)-\eta_{*}B}(1+A)^{\mu-1}(1+B)^{k-\mu-1}\lesssim X^{\theta^{\star}}M^{\mu-1}.

Since
M=\lfloor\log_{2}X\rfloor
, we have completed the proof.
∎

Proof of Theorem

.

If all
\pi_{i}
are equal to a common value
\pi

\theta^{\star}=1/\pi
and
\mu=k

Now we can connect the ordered and unordered cases, noting that the unordered count is the sum of the ordered counts for all permutations of the

Several patterns emerge from these experiments:

1.

Universal power-law preservation.

Every layer of every architecture exhibits power-law eigenvalue decay with high
R^{2}
, confirming that the phenomenon predicted by Theorem
1
for single-layer random features extends to multi-layer and non-MLP architectures. Albeit with drift in the exponents, although at finite size this is consistent with accumulating logarithmic corrections.

2.

Activation function controls the drift rate.

For randomly initialized MLPs, the rate at which the exponent drifts across layers depends strongly on the activation function (see Figure
9
).
With
\tanh
, the slope stays within
[-1.28,-1.27]
over four layers—near-perfect preservation.
With ReLU, it drifts to
-0.95
; with
\mathrm{He}_{3}(x)=x^{3}-3x
(the third Hermite polynomial), it drifts to
-0.83
.
This ordering is consistent with Theorem
1
: since
\tanh
is an odd function dominated by its linear (
\text{p}=1
) Hermite component, the logarithmic correction
\log^{\text{p}-1}(j+1)
is trivial (
\text{p}=1
gives no correction).
ReLU has significant even-degree components (
\text{p}=2
), and
\mathrm{He}_{3}
is a pure degree-
3
monomial in the Hermite basis, giving a correction of
\log^{2}(j+1)
per layer that accumulates visibly across depth.

3.

Normalization layers accelerate the drift.

Adding normalization after each activation worsens the spectral drift rather than mitigating it (see Figure
10
).
With ReLU alone, the slope reaches
-0.95
at L4; adding RMSNorm gives
-0.85
, and LayerNorm gives
-0.75
.

4.

Skip connections stabilize the exponent.

In the randomly initialized ResNet-20, the slope is remarkably stable across all layers, staying within
[-1.25,-1.17]
(e.g.,
-1.22
,
-1.25
,
-1.25
in Group 2).
This suggests that skip connections help prevent the per-layer drift seen in the MLP: by adding the block input to its output, the residual connection anchors the spectral structure.

5.

Training recovers the input exponent.

For the trained ResNet-20, the exponent flattens significantly at early layers (reaching
-0.52
at G1-B2), but recovers through the network and returns to
-1.35
at the final layer—close to the input exponent of
-1.29
.
The trained CNN shows a similar pattern: slopes flatten at middle layers (
-0.59
at B2), then steepen at the final layer (
-1.56
).
