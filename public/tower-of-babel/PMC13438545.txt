Continuous solar-driven recycling of lithium-ion battery cathode materials enabled by a stable benzobisthiazole-linked polymeric photocatalyst
Authors: Xiaohan Yu, Yuhua Fu, Jingfan Shao, Yuchen Yan, Jingya Xin, Jicheng Xu, Wei Huang, Yanguang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecovering valuable metals from spent lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) is essential but highly limited by high energy consumption and significant waste emissions. Herein, we propose a solar-driven recycling strategy applicable to a wide range of LIB cathode materials. The core of this approach lies in the efficient and stable hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) production, enabled by a rationally engineered benzobisthiazole-linked polymeric photocatalyst. The in-situ photogenerated H2O2 induces the redox reactions of metal species, promoting their dissolution and lithium-ion deintercalation. Under optimal conditions, this approach achieves Li+ leaching rates of up to 14 mmol h−1 g−1 and transition metal extraction efficiencies exceeding 90%. Outdoor trials under natural sunlight demonstrate continuous and stable recycling of LiCoO2 for over a 30-day period, achieving average daily leaching yields of 8.4 mmol Co2+ and 8.7 mmol Li+. A techno-economic analysis further supports the practical feasibility of our photocatalytic recycling strategy, underscoring its potential as a scalable and sustainable solution for recovering critical metals from LIB waste.Subject terms: Photocatalysis, Photocatalysis

Introduction

Lithium-ion batteries (LIBs) play a pivotal role in driving the global transition to electrified transportation and the large-scale deployment of renewable energy technologies1–3. However, their rapidly growing demand exerts unsustainable pressure on the planet’s limited mineral resources and vulnerable ecosystems4–6. Forecasts project a 10- to 40-fold increase in the global demand for critical cathode materials, namely lithium (Li), cobalt (Co), and nickel (Ni) by 20407. This dramatic rise is expected to exacerbate existing supply-demand imbalances and accelerate environmentally detrimental mining practices8. Compounding these concerns, the finite operation lifespan of LIBs results in substantial volumes of end-of-life battery waste, which, if improperly managed, poses serious environmental hazards due to the potential metal leaching into soil and groundwater9,10. Addressing these interconnected challenges necessitates the urgent development of efficient, scalable, and sustainable LIB recycling technologies11. Such innovations are essential for closing the materials loop and ensuring the long-term viability, resource efficiency, and environmental compatibility of the LIB industry.

Pyrometallurgy and hydrometallurgy are two conventional approaches for recovering lithium and transition metals from spent LIB cathodes12–15. Pyrometallurgy involves subjecting spent LIBs to high-temperature treatments to recover metal alloys or oxides (Fig. 1a)16. Although straightforward and industrially established, this method requires sustained temperatures that can exceed 1500 °C, leading to high energy consumption and significant carbon footprints. Hydrometallurgy, on the other hand, typically involves leaching metal elements from pretreated cathodes into solution using strong inorganic acids at moderate temperatures (Fig. 1b)17. Despite its lower thermal demands, the use of corrosive chemicals raises concerns about operational safety, equipment degradation, and the production of hazardous liquid waste, which compromise its environmental and economic sustainability at scale. To overcome these drawbacks, alternative recycling strategies have been actively pursued18–22. Among them, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2)-assisted processes have garnered particular attention. In these approaches, H2O2 serves as a reducing agent, converting high-valence metal species (e.g., Co3+ to Co2+ in LiCoO2), thereby facilitating their dissolution in the presence of mild organic acids under relatively benign conditions. Despite their promise, several challenges hinder practical implementation. For instance, H2O2 is predominantly synthesized via the energy-intensive anthraquinone process, and its rapid decomposition in the presence of transition metal ions necessitates excess dosing and continuous replenishment23. To improve the sustainability and efficiency of these processes, it would be highly desirable to integrate H2O2-based recycling with on-site, on-demand H2O2 generation, ideally powered by renewable energy.

H2O2 photosynthesis has recently emerged as a promising strategy for sustainable and decentralized H2O2 production, using abundant water and oxygen as feedstocks and solar energy as the sole energy input24,25. This green approach holds significant potential for diverse applications, including environmental remediation, medical disinfection, and organic transformations. Inspired by these advances in this field, we herein propose a solar-driven recycling strategy for spent LIB cathode materials (Fig. 1c). Specifically, a benzobisthiazole-linked polymeric photocatalyst was rationally designed and synthesized through the tandem dehydrogenative cyclization of initial imine linkages. Such structural evolution endows the catalyst with superior activity and exceptional structural robustness, ensuring efficient and stable H2O2 generation under prolonged irradiation—a key to maintaining long-term viability of the entire recycling system. The produced H2O2, in conjunction with acetic acid, is then supplied to leach metal ions from cathodes under mild conditions. Under optimized conditions, a two-phase system achieves leaching of Li at a rate of 3-14 mmol h−1 g−1 and over 90% extraction efficiencies of transition metals from representative cathode materials including LiCoO2, LiFePO4, and LiCo1/3Ni1/3Mn1/3O2. A 30-day outdoor evaluation demonstrates continuous recycling performance and excellent operational stability under natural sunlight. This green approach holds significant potential for diverse applications, including environmental remediation, medical disinfection, and organic transformations.

Results

Photocatalyst synthesis and characterizations

An efficient and durable photocatalyst is a prerequisite for ensuring the long-term operation of the proposed recycling system. Beyond efficient photon harvesting and charge separation, the candidate photocatalyst has to retain structural integrity under the acidic conditions characteristic of the leaching environment. Traditional inorganic semiconductors, such as TiO2, offer inherent structural stability; however, their photocatalytic performance is often constrained by a narrow light absorption range and a tendency to catalyze the decomposition of H2O226. In contrast, metal-free organic semiconductor photocatalysts, most notably covalent organic frameworks, have recently emerged as promising alternatives for H2O2 photosynthesis by virtue of their tunable structures and high photocatalytic activities27–29. Nevertheless, their long-term stability remains a critical challenge primarily due to the presence of reversible chemical linkages (commonly imine bonds) in constructing their crystalline polymer frameworks. These dynamic bonds are susceptible to hydrolytic cleavage under acidic or basic conditions and are vulnerable to oxidative degradation by reactive oxygen species generated during photocatalysis, ultimately leading to progressive structural collapse and performance decay over time30. To address these limitations, rational linkage engineering, particularly the replacement of labile linkages with more robust, irreversible covalent bonds, is essential for improving the structural robustness and operation lifespan of polymeric photocatalysts under harsh reaction environments31,32.

Guided by this design principle, a benzobisthiazole-linked porous organic polymer (TpTz) was synthesized via a stepwise polymerization of 2,3,6,7,10,11-hexakis(4-formylphenyl)triphenylene (HFPTP) and 2,5-diamino-1,4-benzenedithiol dihydrochloride (DABDT), which involves imine formation followed by dehydrogenative cyclization as illustrated in Fig. 2a. The second step irreversibly converts the initially formed imine linkages into aromatic thiazole rings. This structural evolution markedly enhances π-delocalization and planar rigidity across the polymer backbone, thereby improving the overall structural stability of the resulting photocatalyst. In addition, the incorporation of nitrogen/sulfur-rich benzobisthiazole units, which possess strong electron-withdrawing characteristics, provides abundant active sites and promotes efficient H2O2 production33–35.

The molecular structure of TpTz was first characterized using Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy and solid-state 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy. Compared to the spectra of the monomer precursors, the FT-IR spectrum of TpTz exhibits three new absorption bands at 1606, 1248, and 693 cm−1, corresponding to the characteristic stretching vibrations of C=N, C–N, and C–S bonds in thiazole units, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 1)34. The substantial attenuation of signals associated with terminal aldehyde (1693 cm−1) and amine (2789 cm−1) functionalities indicates complete polymerization. Moreover, the solid-state 13C NMR spectrum of TpTz displays a peak at 169 ppm, assignable to the S–C=N moiety in thiazole rings (Fig. 2b)36. The observed signals within the range of 119–142 ppm correspond to the phenyl carbons originating from the starting HFPTP37. No signals corresponding to unreacted terminal aldehyde groups are observed at 191 ppm, further confirming the high degree of polymerization. Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis reveals that TpTz is largely amorphous (Supplementary Fig. 2). It arises from the rapid kinetics of both the imine formation and subsequent cyclization steps, which limit the dynamic reversibility typically required for crystalline framework formation.

The microstructure of TpTz was investigated using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). As shown in Fig. 2c, d, TpTz exhibits a hollow nanotubular morphology with an average diameter of approximately 350 nm and lengths extending up to 50 μm. The estimated wall thickness of the nanotubes is around 30 nm. This open-channel architecture is advantageous for maximizing the exposure of catalytically active sites and facilitating mass transport. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) elemental mapping reveals the uniform distribution of C, N, and S throughout the structure. Nitrogen sorption analysis yields a Brunauer–Emmett–Teller specific surface area of 696.7 m2 g−1 (Fig. 2e). Thermal gravimetric analysis confirms the excellent thermal stability of TpTz with negligible mass loss up to 600 °C (Supplementary Fig. 3). To further assess structural robustness, TpTz was exposed to harsh chemical environments, including 2 M HCl and 2 M NaOH at 80 °C for 48 h. FT-IR spectra of the recovered samples remain nearly unchanged, indicating the preservation of molecular structure and overall structural integrity (Fig. 2f).

The optoelectronic properties of TpTz were further studied. Ultraviolet-visible (UV-vis) diffuse reflectance spectroscopy shows that TpTz exhibits broad visible-light absorption up to 600 nm (Fig. 2g). Tauc plot analysis based on this data indicates an optical bandgap of 2.69 eV (Supplementary Fig. 4a). In conjunction with the Mott-Schottky measurement, the conduction band and valence band edge positions of TpTz are determined to be −0.59 eV and 2.10 eV versus the normal hydrogen electrode, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4b, c). This band alignment meets the thermodynamic criterion for photocatalytic H2O2 production via the two-electron oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) (Supplementary Fig. 4d).

Visible-light-driven recycling of LIB cathode materials

Prior to applying TpTz in the proposed solar-driven recycling of cathode materials, its performance for H2O2 photosynthesis was evaluated in a biphasic solution reactor. This biphasic configuration not only enhances the activity and stability of the photocatalyst but also facilitates the spontaneous extraction of photogenerated H2O230,38. Specifically, the upper organic phase consisted of 15 mL of 1-octanol containing 30 mg of dispersed photocatalyst powder. In this setup, 1-octanol serves a dual role: as a sacrificial electron donor to enhance photocatalytic efficiency and stability, and as a high-boiling-point solvent (195 °C) to minimize evaporation during extended light irradiation39. The lower aqueous phase comprised 15 mL of 0.2 M acetic aqueous solution, simulating the acidic environment of the recycling conditions described later. Under visible-light irradiation, TpTz is photoexcited to generate electrons and holes, which drive the reduction of oxygen to H2O2 and the oxidation of 1-octanol to 1-octanal (Supplementary Fig. 5).

Fig 3a illustrates the time-dependent accumulation of H2O2 in the aqueous phase. A linear increase is observed, reaching a total yield of 5.01 ± 0.14 mmol g−1 when normalized to the catalyst weight, and a final H2O2 concentration of 10.03 ± 0.27 mM after 4 h. This translates to an average production rate of 1.25 ± 0.04 mmol h−1 g−1, which is comparable to or even exceeds those of many state-of-the-art photocatalysts reported in literature29. Similar activity was observed when using benzotrifluoride (with 10 vol% 1-octanol) as the oil phase, a solvent with higher O2 solubility than 1-octanol, demonstrating that O2 mass transport does not limit H2O2 production (Supplementary Fig. 6). Raising the reaction temperature to 80°C to match subsequent leaching conditions boosted H2O2 production 2.7-fold, achieving a total H2O2 yield of 13.1 mmol g−1 and a final H2O2 concentration of 27 mM after 4 h (Supplementary Fig. 7). This improvement was attributed to accelerated reaction kinetics and reduced activation barriers at elevated temperature40. Mechanistic investigation confirms that H2O2 is produced via the two-electron ORR driven by photogenerated electrons. The involvement of the superoxide radical intermediate (•O2−) is substantiated by electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy and in-situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 8)41,42.

The long-term operation stability of TpTz was evaluated through cycling experiments. As shown in Fig. 3b, TpTz exhibits negligible performance degradation over five consecutive cycles and a total duration of 20 h. Post-cycling structural characterizations reveal no discernible changes in composition or morphology (Supplementary Fig. 9). This exceptional durability is attributed to benzobisthiazole linkages that irreversibly lock the molecular frameworks and preserve the structural integrity of the polymer even under acidic conditions. To validate this conclusion, a control sample (TpIm) was synthesized with the same molecular backbone to TpTz, but featuring reversible imine linkages instead (Supplementary Fig. 10). In stark contrast, TpIm exhibits poor chemical stability in acidic solution, even under dark conditions, due to hydrolytic cleavage of the imine bonds (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Moreover, significant performance decay is observed in both acidic and neutral aqueous media (Supplementary Fig. 11b, c). This degradation is accompanied by noticeable catalyst mass loss and increased transparency of the catalyst dispersion (Supplementary Fig. 11d). Spectroscopic analyses of the recovered TpIm further confirm severe molecular and morphological degradation (Supplementary Fig. 11e, f). Collectively, the stark contrast in stability between TpTz and TpIm clearly underscores the critical importance of irreversible linkages in maintaining the structural robustness of polymeric photocatalysts.

Given the superior photocatalytic performance of TpTz, we subsequently explored its applicability in electrode material recycling. LiCoO2 powder was selected as a representative target and dispersed in the lower aqueous phase of the biphasic reactor. In this reaction configuration, the photogenerated H2O2 from the organic phase diffuses into the aqueous phase, where it reduces Co3+ in LiCoO2 to Co2+ (Fig. 3c). This reduction facilitates the acetic acid-assisted dissolution of the LiCoO2 lattice and promotes the simultaneous deintercalation of Li+ ions. Notably, the introduction of LiCoO2 does not interfere with the light absorption of TpTz dispersed in the upper organic phase. The leaching process was conducted at 80°C to expedite the leaching process. Upon top-down visible-light irradiation, the aqueous phase gradually changes color from colorless to pink, and a concurrent decrease in solid residue is observed (Fig. 3d). TpTz remains in the upper oil phase throughout the process (Supplementary Fig. 12). UV-vis spectroscopy of the aqueous phase shows a progressive increase in the characteristic Co2+ absorption peak at 512 nm (Fig. 3e). This observation confirms the successful extraction of Co2+. To quantify the leaching efficiency, the concentrations of Co2+ and Li+ in the aqueous phase were measured via inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis. Under excessive LiCoO2 conditions, the concentrations of both metal ions rise approximately linearly over time (Fig. 3f). The average leaching rates, normalized to the mass of TpTz, are derived to be 3.08 ± 0.08 mmol h−1 g−1 for Li+ and 2.55 ± 0.11 mmol h−1 g−1 for Co2+. This corresponds to an optimal efficiency obtained by directly adding equivalent or even more commercial H2O2 stock (Supplementary Fig. 13). The solar-driven recycling can also achieve a conversion efficiency of 90–100% when 20 mg of LiCoO2 is treated for 4 h (Supplementary Fig. 14).

To evaluate practical applicability, real cathode materials were recovered from spent commercial LIBs through discharging, disassembly, and stripping from the current collectors (Fig. 3g)22. The collected black mass was directly subjected to photocatalytic recycling under conditions identical to those used for pure LiCoO2. As shown in Fig. 3h, continuous metal leaching is sustained over 4 h, affording an average leaching rate of 2.96 mmol g−1 h−1 for Li+ and 2.49 mmol g−1 h−1 for Co2+, comparable to those obtained with commercial LiCoO2 powder.

To elucidate the role of individual components in the recycling system, a series of control experiments were conducted. As shown in Fig. 3i, the metal leaching rate decreases slightly when the overhead gas is switched from oxygen to air, but is significantly suppressed under argon. In the absence of light or the photocatalyst, only trace amounts of metal ions are detected in the leachate. Direct addition of an equivalent amount of commercial H2O2 (~30 mM) leads to a metal leaching efficiency similar to that achieved in the photocatalytic system (Supplementary Fig. 13). These observations confirm the indispensable role of photogenerated H2O2 in initiating the metal leaching reaction. Furthermore, removing acetic acid almost completely quenches metal leaching, underscoring its critical role in facilitating the dissolution of Co2+ and deintercalation of Li+ from LiCoO2. Although other acids can also promote metal dissolution, they exhibit high background contributions due to their strong acidity or intrinsic reducing capability12, which obscures the specific role of photogenerated H2O2 (Supplementary Fig. 15). The effects of various reaction parameters on the leaching rate were also systematically investigated and are summarized in Supplementary Fig. 16. Moreover, TpTz exhibits stable performance in the recycling system, showing no significant decline in metal leaching efficiency over consecutive cycles (Supplementary Fig. 17).

The solar-driven recycling strategy is also applicable to other cathode materials, including LiFePO4 and LiNi1/3Co1/3Mn1/3O2 (NCM), despite their distinct reaction mechanisms. For LiFePO4, the recycling process involves H2O2-mediated oxidation of Fe2+ to Fe3+, followed by selective Li+ leaching by acetic acid, leaving behind the residual FePO4, as indicated by XRD and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy measurements (Supplementary Fig. 18)43,44. Under excessive LiFePO4 conditions, a Li+ leaching rate of 13.23 ± 0.81 mmol h−1 g−1 is measured (Fig. 3j). When 50 mg LiFePO4 was used, a near-100% leaching efficiency can be achieved within 1 h (Supplementary Fig. 19). For multicomponent NCM cathodes, the recycling involves the reduction of high-valence metal ions by H2O2 to facilitate their dissolution—a process similar to that for LiCoO245,46. In the presence of excessive NCM, the optimal leaching rates are determined to be 4.03 ± 0.13, 1.18 ± 0.12, 1.22 ± 0.12, and 1.09 ± 0.11 mmol h−1 g−1 for Li+, Co2+, Ni2+, and Mn2+, respectively, with the metal ratios in the leachate closely reflecting the stoichiometry of the original NCM. For 20 mg of NCM cathode material, leaching efficiencies of 99.27 ± 0.45% (Li), 92.30 ± 0.87% (Co), 94.03 ± 1.30% (Ni), and 90.7 ± 1.78% (Mn) are achieved after 4 h (Supplementary Fig. 20).

Sunlight-driven recycling of LIB cathode materials

Building upon the above results, we further designed a continuous, closed-loop recycling system powered entirely by natural sunlight (Fig. 4a). In this scaled-up setup, a Fresnel lens was employed to concentrate solar flux, increasing the system temperature and accelerating metal leaching kinetics (Fig. 4b). This design reduces the demand for external energy inputs, significantly lowering operational costs. Moreover, the spatial decoupling of H2O2 photosynthesis from chemical leaching within the reactor allows for periodic leachate extraction and reactant replenishment without the need for catalyst separation, thus enabling continuous operation for extended periods, from days to months.

In a typical experiment, 600 mg of TpTz photocatalyst was dispersed in 300 mL of 1-octanol as the upper organic phase, while 1 g of LiCoO2 was suspended in 300 mL of 0.6 M acetic acid as the lower aqueous phase. The experiment was carried out outdoors on sunny days, typically from 9:00 am to 4:00 pm. By adjusting the distance and angle between the lens and the reactor, the incident light intensity was maintained within the range of 400–600 mW cm−2, while sunlight exposure elevated the system temperature to approximately 80 °C. The accumulation of Co2+ and Li+ ions in the aqueous leachate was monitored hourly. Using the data collected on January 13th, 2025 as an example, a continuous increase in both Co2+ and Li+ leaching amount was observed, reaching 9.5 mmol for Co2+ and 10.4 mmol for Li+, respectively, after 7 h (Fig. 4c). The metal concentrations then approached a plateau, which is attributed to accelerated H2O2 decomposition as Co2+ ions accumulate in the system (Supplementary Fig. 21)47.

We next conducted a 30-day outdoor recycling experiment from January to April 2025 in Suzhou, China. After each day’s reaction, the leachate was automatically extracted, and fresh acetic solution was replenished via a peristaltic pump. Using the same TpTz catalyst throughout, the system exhibits stable performance, achieving average daily leaching amounts of 8.4 mmol for Co2+ and 8.7 mmol for Li+ (Fig. 4d). Over the 30-day period, a total of 17.8 L leachate containing 14.2 mM Co2+ and 14.7 mM Li+ was collected (Fig. 4e). These results highlight the great potential of sunlight-powered recycling for sustained, practical implementation.

Furthermore, the metal ions in the leachate were selectively recovered via a stepwise precipitation method. Oxalic acid was first added to precipitate 45.7 g of pink cobalt oxalate dihydrate (CoC2O4∙2H2O). Following filtration, 9.1 g of lithium carbonate (Li2CO3) was precipitated by introducing sodium carbonate (Na2CO3) into the filtrate48. XRD patterns of the obtained precipitates match well with standard references for CoC2O4∙2H2O and Li2CO3, confirming satisfactory phase purity for potential reuse in future LIB manufacturing (Fig. 4f, g).

Technoeconomic analysis

To assess the economic viability of our solar-driven recycling system, a comprehensive technoeconomic analysis (TEA) was carried out based on a rationally scaled-up design. The proposed model employs a panel reactor with an irradiation area of 1 m2 and a solution volume of 0.2 m3, comprising equal volumes of organic and aqueous phases (Supplementary Fig. 22). The feasibility of such a configuration has been validated in prior photocatalytic studies49,50. With appropriate adaptation, we anticipate that a similar reactor setup can be applied to the solar-driven recycling of battery materials at a comparable scale. Taking real-world weather conditions into account, the average monthly effective irradiation period is assumed to be 15 days. Drawing from leaching efficiencies observed in our outdoor experiments, this system is projected to process 5 kg of spent LiCoO2 per month, yielding approximately 8.4 kg of CoC2O4∙2H2O and 1.7 kg of Li2CO3 monthly. The financial viability was evaluated over a projected 10-year operational lifetime using two key metrics: the levelized cost of product (LCP) and the end-of-life net present value (NPV)51,52. All cost and revenue estimations reflect current market prices for relevant raw materials and products involved in LiCoO2 recycling. Detailed calculations are provided in the Supplementary Information.

The recycling process comprises three main stages: acquisition and pretreatment of spent batteries, solar-driven leaching, and subsequent metal precipitation. Based on prior reports and the capacity of the proposed model system, the monthly cost for battery procurement and pretreatment is estimated at $118.553,54. The final metal precipitation step—including reagents and electricity—adds $33.8 per month. The cost of the solar-driven leaching process is primarily attributed to capital and operational expenditures. Capital costs, covering the facility (including photoreactor, Fresnel lens, and peristaltic pumps), photocatalyst, and 1-octanol, are projected at $480, calculated assuming a 3-month operational lifespan for the photocatalyst dispersion, amortized over a 10-year system duration. Operational costs, dominated by the continuous consumption of 0.6 M acetic acid solution (3000 L per month), are estimated at $37.2 per month. Consequently, the overall monthly cost of the leaching process amounts to $517.2. Combining all expenditures, the total LCP for the recycling process is calculated at $669.5 per month. As shown in Fig. 5a, the largest share of the LCP stems from the photocatalyst (65.8%), followed by battery acquisition (12.5%). On the revenue side, the monthly market value of the recovered products—CoC2O4∙2H2O (~$110/kg) and Li2CO3 (~$118/kg)—is estimated at $1124.655,56. This yields a substantial monthly profit of $455.1 and a net profit margin of 40.5% (Fig. 5b). Figure 5c illustrates the end-of-life NPV, showing that the system becomes profitable within the first year of operation. Over a 10-year operational period, the current system is projected to generate a total income of $134,952, resulting in a cumulative profit of $54,618.

The above analysis clearly highlights the strong economic potential of our solar-driven recycling system, along with significant environmental benefits, including low waste emissions and carbon footprints (Supplementary Table 1). This contrasts with traditional pyrometallurgy and hydrometallurgy, which are typically plagued by high energy consumption and toxic waste management issues. To further enhance its economic attractiveness, several improvements can be explored in the future. Since the photocatalyst represents the largest portion of the total cost, developing more cost-effective and efficient alternatives remains a top priority. Despite our efforts to explore low-cost candidates (Supplementary Fig. 23), TpTz currently offers the best balance of catalytic activity, stability, and economic viability. Furthermore, current material costs are expected to decrease with economies of scale. Additionally, improving photocatalyst stability via structural engineering offers a viable pathway to higher profitability; for instance, extending the photocatalyst replacement interval to 12 months could increase overall profit by ~50%. Further opportunities for cost optimization include the efficient recovery of acetic acid through membrane separation technology or the extraction of value-added byproducts, i.e., 1-octanal—which has a 5-fold value premium over 1-octanol—through fractional condensation systems57,58.

Discussion

We developed a solar-driven recycling strategy for spent LIB cathodes by integrating H2O2 photosynthesis with an acetic acid-assisted metal leaching process. To enable efficient and stable H2O2 production, a robust polymeric photocatalyst featuring irreversible benzobisthiazole linkages was specifically designed and synthesized. The in-situ generated H2O2 acted as a redox mediator, promoting transition metal dissolution and Li⁺ deintercalation under mild acidic conditions. In an optimized biphasic system, this integrated approach demonstrated broad applicability to various cathode materials, including LiCoO2, LiFePO4, and LiCo1/3Ni1/3Mn1/3O2, achieving metal leaching efficiencies exceeding 90% and Li extraction rates of 4-14 mmol h−1 g−1. Outdoor trials under natural sunlight enabled stable and continuous operation over 30 days, with average daily leaching yields of 8.4 mmol Co2+ and 8.7 mmol Li+ from LiCoO2. These were subsequently recovered as CoC2O4∙H2O and Li2CO3 with high phase purity, suitable for LIB remanufacturing. This strategy eliminated the need for external reducing agents or thermal inputs typical of conventional processes. TEA further highlighted its practical viability and cost-effectiveness. Our work here provides a sustainable and scalable blueprint for the closed-loop recovery of critical metals, addressing key energy and environmental challenges associated with LIB wastes.

Methods

Synthesis of TpTz

TpTz was synthesized via a stepwise polycondensation reaction between HFPTP and DABDT (HFPTP was separately prepared according to the literature with modifications59). The first stage involves dissolving DABDT (220 mg, 0.9 mmol) in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 50 mL) in a 250 mL round-bottom flask. The solution was cooled to 0 °C in an ice-water bath, followed by the dropwise addition of a solution of HFPTP (256 mg, 0.3 mmol) in DMF (30 mL) over 30 min. The resulting mixture was stirred at 0 °C for 2 h, then allowed to warm to room temperature and stirred for another 12 h. For the second stage, the reaction mixture was purged with O2 for 30 min and heated at 150 °C for 2 days. After cooling to room temperature, the precipitate was collected by filtration, washed thoroughly with DMF, H2O, and EtOH, and dried under vacuum at 80 °C to afford TpTz as a yellow powder (yield: 98%).

Photocatalytic H2O2 production in a biphasic system

In a typical experiment, TpTz (30 mg) was ultrasonically dispersed in 1-octanol (15 mL). The resulting dispersion was transferred into a top-irradiated Pyrex photoreactor (200 mL), followed by the addition of 15 mL of 0.2 M acetic acid, forming a biphasic mixture. The mixture was purged with O2 for 30 min and then the reactor was carefully sealed. Photocatalysis was conducted under illumination from a 300 W Xe lamp (China Education Au-light, CEL-HXF300) equipped with a 420 nm cutoff filter. The light intensity was calibrated to 300 mW cm−2 using a Newport light power meter. The reaction temperature was maintained at 25 °C using a water circulation system. To monitor the reaction, 0.2 mL aliquots of the lower aqueous phase were extracted every hour and filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter. The concentration of H2O2 was quantified using the xylenol orange/Fe2+ colorimetric assay. All experiments were performed in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. Data are reported as means ± standard deviation from three independent replicates.

For the cycling test, each photocatalytic cycle was conducted for 4 h. At the end of each cycle, the photocatalyst dispersion was recovered by centrifugation and washed twice with 1-octanol to remove residual reactants and byproducts. The photocatalyst was then redispersed in 15 mL of fresh 1-octanol and reintroduced into the photoreactor, which was subsequently charged with 15 mL of fresh 0.2 M acetic acid aqueous solution for the next reaction cycle.

Solar-driven recycling of LIB cathode materials in the laboratory

In a typical experiment, TpTz (30 mg) was ultrasonically dispersed in 1-octanol (15 mL). The resulting dispersion was transferred into a top-irradiated Pyrex photoreactor (200 mL) preloaded with 15 mL of 0.2 M acetic acid solution. A specific amount of cathode material—LiCoO2, LiFePO4, LiCo1/3Ni1/3Mn1/3O2, or real black mass—was then added, which immediately settled into the bottom of the reactor. The biphasic suspension was purged with O2 for 30 min before the reactor was carefully sealed. Photocatalytic reactions were carried out under irradiation from a 300 W Xe lamp (China Education Au-light, CEL-HXF300) equipped with a 420 nm cutoff filter. The light intensity was calibrated to be 300 mW cm−2. The reaction temperature was maintained at 80 °C using a water circulation system. To monitor the leaching process, 0.2 mL aliquots were periodically withdrawn from the lower aqueous phase, filtered through a 0.22 μm syringe filter, and analyzed by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometer (ICP-OES). Metal ion concentrations were quantified based on calibration curves prepared from standard solutions. All experiments were conducted in triplicate to ensure reproducibility. Data are reported as means ± standard deviation from three independent replicates.

Outdoor sunlight-driven recycling of LiCoO2

Sunlight-driven recycling experiments were conducted in a custom-designed photoreactor with a volume capacity of 1 L. Two reservoir bottles were connected to the reactor via peristaltic pumps: one for collecting the leachate and the other for replenishing fresh acetic acid solution. A uniform dispersion of TpTz (600 mg) in 1-octanol (300 mL) was gently added to the reactor containing 300 mL of 0.6 M acetic acid solution and 1 g of LiCoO2. A Fresnel lens was employed to concentrate incident sunlight on the top of the reactor, thereby enhancing light intensity and simultaneously raising the internal temperature. Experiments were carried out on clear sunny days, typically from 9:00 am to 4:00 pm. By adjusting the distance and angle between the Fresnel lens and the reactor, the light intensity was maintained in the range of 400–600 mW cm−2, while the internal temperature stabilized at around 80 °C. Throughout the reaction, 0.2 mL aliquots of the aqueous leachate were periodically withdrawn, filtered via a 0.22 μm syringe filter, and analyzed by ICP-OES. To maintain continuous operation, the aqueous leachate was withdrawn from the reactor every 3–4 h via a peristaltic pump and collected in a separate bottle, while fresh acetic solution was simultaneously replenished from a connected storage tank. After each full day of operation, the reactor was recharged with additional LiCoO2 to continue the recycling process.

Sequential precipitation for metal ion recovery

Metal ions were recovered from the aqueous leachate via a sequential precipitation process. Specifically, 650 mg of oxalic acid was added to 500 mL of leachate and stirred at room temperature for 30 min. The resulting pink precipitate was collected by filtration, thoroughly washed with deionized water, and identified as CoC2O4·2H2O. The remaining filtrate was concentrated by rotary evaporation to approximately half of its original volume. Subsequently, 100 mL of a saturated aqueous Na2CO3 solution was added, and the mixture was stirred and heated at 95 °C for 2 h. The resulting white precipitate Li2CO3, was recovered by filtration and washed with deionized water. Both CoC2O4·2H2O and Li2CO3 powders were dried in an oven at 80°C to remove residual moisture prior to XRD characterization.
