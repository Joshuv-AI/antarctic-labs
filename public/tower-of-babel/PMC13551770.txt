The relationship between spiritual well-being and moral resilience in nursing students: a mixed-methods study
Authors: Gamze Goke Arslan, Mehtap Genc, Muhammed Celik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study was conducted to investigate the relationship between spiritual well-being and moral resilience among nursing students via a convergent parallel mixed-methods design.MethodsThe quantitative phase included 236 nursing students purposively selected from a university in the Central Anatolia Region of Türkiye (response rate: 62.9%). The quantitative data were collected via the three-factor spiritual well-being scale and the Rushton Moral Resilience Scale. The qualitative phase consisted of semistructured in-depth interviews with 17 nursing students selected through purposive sampling.ResultsA weak positive correlation was found between the Harmony with Nature dimension and the overall Rushton Moral Resilience Scale score (r = 0.190, p = 0.003). A weak positive correlation was also identified between the overall Spiritual Well-Being Scale score and the overall Rushton Moral Resilience Scale score (r = 0.312, p < 0.001). In addition, the transcendence dimension was negatively correlated with moral adversity (r=-0.229, p < 0.001) and positively correlated with moral efficacy (r = 0.351, p < 0.001). The qualitative findings indicated that spiritual well-being was associated with moral resilience through empathy and compassion, a tendency toward right and good behaviors, motivation and satisfaction, coping with challenging situations, and professional responsibility. However, qualitative findings also revealed that spirituality-related values and beliefs may sometimes contribute to internal tensions in ethical decision-making processes.Conclusion and recommendationsSpiritual well-being was positively associated with moral resilience among nursing students. However, some aspects of spirituality may also create ethical tensions in clinical decision-making. Therefore, nursing education should strengthen content related to spirituality, ethics, cultural competence, and ethical decision-making while supporting students in managing potential value conflicts encountered in clinical practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04917-0.Keywords: Spiritual well-being, Moral resilience, Nursing students, Spirituality, Ethical decision-making, Mixed methods

Background

Spirituality, defined as a concept that gives meaning, purpose, and peace to a person’s existence [1], provides nursing with a broad scope, intense meaning, and a deep purpose by avoiding the risk of the profession becoming overly materialistic or becoming reduced to mere practice. It also directly affects nursing care and management in many ways, such as providing meaning and purpose to nursing care, encouraging harmony in healthcare communities, improving respect for ethical values, encouraging innovation in nursing, supporting nursing leaders, and illuminating the workplace [2]. Spiritual well-being, on the other hand, refers to a person devoting himself or herself to greater power; possessing a set of values, principles, ethics, and beliefs that provide a sense of purpose and meaning in life; and using these principles to guide his or her actions [1, 3].

Spirituality and morality are closely related concepts that affect each other mutually. Understanding humanity’s spiritual tendencies is addressed and evaluated within the framework of the humanities, where the ethical dimension is increasingly gaining more importance. Within this context, the relationship between an individual’s spiritual well-being and moral resilience provides a significant theoretical basis for understanding human values and behaviors [4, 5]. Moral resilience is defined as an individual’s ability to act honestly and maintain his or her integrity despite moral complexity, emotional turmoil, confusion, distress, or setbacks [6]. In other words, moral resilience is defined as “nurses’ ability to cope with stress, to make decisions, to solve problems, to establish a balance between their emotions, to show moral courage, to have a desire to solve the problem, to maintain their personal integrity, and to recover from physical and psychological distress to return to their former state when they are faced with moral/ethical challenges in their clinical settings” [7].

In clinical settings, nurses frequently encounter complex ethical dilemmas and crisis moments such as end-of-life care, sudden patient deterioration, or conflicting patient-family values where the meaning and value of life are deeply questioned. These high-stress environments directly trigger moral distress among nursing students, who often lack clinical coping mechanisms [8]. Moral resilience has been proposed as a strategy to mitigate moral distress in nurses. Moral resilience is not a detached concept here; it is the specific capacity to respond ethically competently to situations of moral adversity to protect a student’s moral integrity [9]. Crucially, this resilience does not operate in a vacuum. It is deeply anchored by the student’s spiritual well-being, which provides a foundational sense of purpose, connectedness, and ultimate meaning during times of existential crisis. Individuals with greater spiritual well-being tend to adopt a holistic perspective on life and demonstrate greater empathy, compassion, and respect for patient dignity. When faced with moral adversity, students with greater spiritual well-being can draw upon their internal belief systems to maintain moral resolve, thereby directly linking their spiritual health to their capacity for moral resilience [10–12].

The literature contains several studies in which the levels of spiritual well-being and moral resilience of nursing students are studied separately [3, 9–20]. In their quantitative study, Karimi et al. (2026) reported that spiritual well-being is a significant and independent component of moral sensitivity among nursing students. Furthermore, in their quantitative study, Cura et al. (2022) reported that senior nursing students had a high level of perception of spiritual care and moral sensitivity and that nursing education had a significant effect on students’ spiritual care and moral sensitivity. Another study revealed that nursing interns have low moral resilience and that moral sensitivity, moral identity, and moral resilience are positively correlated [18]. These quantitative findings can be used to assess students’ levels of spiritual well-being and moral resilience and their relationships with other concepts, but they cannot explain how nursing students use their spiritual values and internal processes to build moral resilience during specific clinical crises. Therefore, there is a critical need for mixed-methods studies that combine both quantitative and qualitative approaches to comprehensively investigate both the levels of and the deeper, lived experiences behind the relationship between spiritual well-being and moral resilience among nursing students. Taking this into consideration, the present study aimed to comprehensively investigate both the relationship between and the lived experiences of spiritual well-being and moral resilience among nursing students via a mixed-methods design.

Methods

Study design

In the present study, a convergent/parallel mixed methods research design was used to obtain diverse but complementary data. The aim of this method, in which qualitative and quantitative research methods are used simultaneously and the same research question is addressed via different research methods and techniques, is to facilitate a comprehensive understanding of the research problem. Qualitative and quantitative data are collected simultaneously but independently, with equal importance given to both data types. Each dataset is first analyzed separately and then integrated during the evaluation phase to increase the depth and scope of the findings [21, 22]. A convergent mixed-methods design was considered particularly appropriate for the present study because the relationship between spiritual well-being and moral resilience involves both measurable associations and subjective experiences. The quantitative component enabled the researchers to determine the direction and strength of the relationship between these constructs, whereas the qualitative component provided a deeper understanding of how nursing students perceive, experience, and interpret this relationship in clinical and educational contexts. By collecting and analyzing both forms of data concurrently and integrating the findings, the study aimed not only to identify whether a relationship exists but also to explain the underlying mechanisms, meanings, and contextual factors that may shape this relationship. In the quantitative part of the study, a descriptive and correlational design was used to determine the relationship between the students’ spiritual well-being and moral resilience levels. In the qualitative part of the study, an exploratory descriptive qualitative study design was used to investigate the relationships among the students’ opinions, experiences, and feelings regarding spiritual well-being and moral resilience. Throughout the research, the researchers strictly conformed to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist for cross-sectional studies and the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) guidelines for qualitative research.

Participants and sample size

The study population consisted of 375 2nd -, 3rd -, and 4th -year nursing students attending a university in the Central Anatolia Region of Türkiye from 2025 to 2026. A purposive criterion sampling method was utilized for the quantitative phase of the study.

Quantitative phase inclusion criteria:

Participants were eligible for the quantitative phase if they (1) were second-, third-, or fourth-year nursing students enrolled in the Faculty of Health Sciences, (2) had prior or active clinical practice experience, and (3) voluntarily agreed to participate in the research.

Quantitative phase exclusion criteria:

Students were excluded if they (1) did not have any clinical practice experience or (2) submitted incomplete data collection forms or scales.

In collecting quantitative data, the minimum sample size was calculated as 191 students via the following sampling formula for studies whose population is known: n = Nt²pq/d²(N-1) + t²pq) (N = 375; t = 1.96; p = 0.5; q = 0.5; d = 0.05) at a 95% confidence interval and 5% standard deviation [23]. The quantitative data of the study were obtained from 236 nursing students, resulting in a response rate of 62.9%.

For the collection of qualitative data, an in-depth interview technique was employed with 10 questions designed to determine nursing students’ perspectives on spiritual well-being and moral resilience. For the qualitative phase, purposive sampling was used to recruit participants who could provide rich information regarding the relationship between spiritual well-being and moral resilience. Students from different academic years (second-, third-, and fourth-year students) were invited to ensure a range of educational experiences. The qualitative phase included 17 students, as data saturation was reached when repetitive themes and statements were observed in the interviews.

Qualitative phase inclusion criteria:

Participants were eligible for the qualitative phase if they were (1) second-, third-, or fourth-year nursing students enrolled in the Faculty of Health Sciences; (2) participants in the quantitative phase of the study; (3) willing to participate in an in-depth interview; and (4) able to communicate their experiences and opinions regarding spiritual well-being and moral resilience.

Qualitative phase exclusion criteria:

Students were excluded if they (1) declined to participate in the interview process, (2) withdrew their consent at any stage of the study, or (3) were unable to complete the interview.

Data collection tools

The quantitative data were collected via the Personal Information Form, Three-Factor Spiritual Well-being Scale, and Rushton Moral Resilience Scale.

Personal information form

The form, prepared by the researchers on the basis of the relevant literature, consists of seven items, four of which question the sociodemographic characteristics of the students and three of which question their views on the nursing profession and their knowledge regarding spirituality and moral resilience [3, 10, 12, 13].

Three-factor spiritual well-being scale

The scale was developed by Ekşi and Kardaş (2017) to understand human life and to determine life processes in accordance with the values and ultimate meanings of human beings in terms of personal, social, environmental, and transcendental aspects [24]. The scale consists of 29 items and the following 3 dimensions: transcendence, harmony with nature, and anomie. The responses given to the items are rated on a 5-point Likert-type scale. There are 15 items related to the transcendence subdimension, 7 items related to the harmony with nature subdimension, and 7 items related to the anomie subdimension. The score range obtained from the scale varies between 29 and 145. As the scores increase, the level of spiritual well-being increases. While the score for the overall scale is calculated, items in the anomie dimension are reverse scored. A high score obtained from a dimension indicates that the individual possesses the characteristic questioned in that dimension. The scale also provides a total well-being score. The Cronbach’s alpha value for the original scale was calculated as 0.88 [24, 25]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale was calculated as 0.91.

Rushton moral resilience scale

The scale was developed by Heinze et al. (2021) to measure an individual’s capacity to maintain or restore their integrity when they are confronted with moral challenges [26]. A validity and reliability study of the Turkish version of the scale was conducted by Kovancı and Atlı Özbaş (2023) [27]. The Cronbach’s alpha value was calculated as 0.82 in Kovancı and Atlı Özbaş’s study [27]. The scale consists of 17 items and the following 4 dimensions: moral adversity, personal integrity, relational integrity and moral efficacy. Items 2, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 13, 14, 15, and 16 are reverse scored. The responses given to the items are rated on a four-point Likert-type scale ranging from 1 to 4. The minimum and maximum possible scores that can be obtained from the overall scale are 17 and 68, respectively. The scale has no cutoff point. The higher the score obtained from the scale is, the higher the respondent’s moral resilience level is [27]. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale was calculated as 0.63. Although this value is below the conventionally recommended threshold of 0.70, it may still be considered acceptable for exploratory research and when complex psychological constructs are measured. Nevertheless, the findings obtained from this scale should be interpreted with caution. Despite this limitation, the scale was retained because it is a previously validated instrument in the Turkish context and remains one of the few available measures specifically developed to assess moral resilience.

While the qualitative data were collected, a semistructured interview form prepared by the researchers on the basis of the relevant literature was used. The semistructured interview form consists of five warm-up questions concerning the participants’ age, sex, year at school, income level, and choice of nursing, as well as ten interview questions [9, 28]. The semistructured interview form used in the qualitative interviews was developed for this study, and its full English version is presented in Supplementary File 1.

Data collection

The data were collected from 2nd -, 3rd -, and 4th -year nursing students attending a university in the Central Anatolia Region of Türkiye during the 2025–2026 academic year between December 2025 and January 2026.

The quantitative data were collected via face-to-face, self-administered questionnaires. After the purpose of the research was explained to the students and their informed consent was obtained, the researchers distributed the paper-based forms, which were then completed independently by the students. Each student took approximately 10 min to complete the data collection forms.

Qualitative data were collected through in-depth interviews in which 10 questions designed to determine nursing students’ views on spiritual well-being and moral resilience were asked. After the purpose of the study was explained to the students and their written informed consent was obtained, contact information was obtained from those who volunteered to participate in the study. A schedule was then arranged with the participating students at their convenience when they were at school. To create a quiet and peaceful environment where privacy can be ensured, the interviews were conducted in the researchers’ office where a quiet and calm environment was ensured. A total of 17 nursing students were purposively selected and interviewed. Among them, 5 were second-year students, 6 were third-year students, and 6 were fourth-year students. Each participant was interviewed only once. The interviews conducted face-to-face were audio recorded after the participants’ consent was obtained. Qualitative data were collected through semistructured in-depth interviews guided by an interview form consisting of 10 predetermined open-ended questions. The same 10 core questions were asked all the participants to ensure consistency across the interviews. In addition, probing and follow-up questions were used when necessary to explore participants’ responses in greater depth and to clarify emerging issues. The interviews lasted between 45 and 90 min, depending on the richness of the participants’ responses.

Statistical analysis

For the statistical analysis of the quantitative data obtained in the study, SPSS (Statistical Package for Social Science) for Windows version 24.0 was used. The arithmetic means, standard deviations, numbers and percentages were calculated for the descriptive statistics. Whether the data were normally distributed was tested by evaluating whether the skewness and kurtosis values were in the range of -1–+1 [29]. The normality of the data was evaluated for the Three-Factor Spiritual Well-Being Scale and Rushton Moral Resilience Scale. The skewness values of the scales ranged between − 0.55 and 0.11, whereas the kurtosis values ranged between − 0.19 and − 0.14. Because these values were well within the acceptable range of ± 1, parametric tests were utilized for statistical analyses. Pearson correlation analysis was performed to reveal the relationships among the variables. As indicated in the literature, correlation coefficients between 0.00 and 0.25 indicate a very weak correlation, those between 0.26 and 0.49 indicate a weak correlation, those between 0.50 and 0.69 indicate a moderate correlation, those between 0.70 and 0.89 indicate a high correlation, and those between 0.90 and 1.00 indicate a very high correlation [30]. Throughout the study, the significance level was accepted as p < 0.05 at a 95% confidence interval. During the analysis of the quantitative data of the study, data belonging to eight students were missing; therefore, these data were not included in the analysis.

In the analysis of the qualitative data, theme analysis was used to interpret the data obtained after the researchers had transcribed the data. The data were analyzed via the MAXQDA (MAX Qualitative Data Analysis) 20.0 program. An inductive thematic analysis approach was adopted. Meaningful text segments were first assigned initial codes, which were then grouped into categories on the basis of conceptual similarities. These categories were subsequently refined into themes and subthemes through iterative discussion among the researchers. The theme analysis was carried out in the following four stages: coding the data, identifying relevant themes, organizing the identified codes and themes, and interpreting them. Appropriate theme and subtheme headings were determined for the interviewed texts, and the analysis was performed. To ensure methodological rigor and trustworthiness, the qualitative analysis was independently conducted by two researchers. Initially, the first four interview transcripts were coded separately by both researchers. Coding differences were compared and discussed until consensus was achieved, and a preliminary coding framework was developed. The remaining transcripts were subsequently independently coded via the agreed-upon framework, and any discrepancies were resolved through discussion and consensus. Credibility was further enhanced through participant validation (member checking), whereby participants were given the opportunity to confirm the accuracy of the interpretations. In addition, the themes, subthemes, and codes were reviewed by an experienced qualitative researcher who was not involved in the study, providing an external assessment of the analytical process. These procedures were undertaken to increase the credibility, dependability, and confirmability of the findings. These procedures were undertaken to increase the credibility, dependability, and confirmability of the findings. Transferability was supported by providing detailed descriptions of the study setting, participant characteristics, sampling procedures, and data collection process.

Moral empowerment stemming from spiritual well-being

This subtheme suggests that nursing students’ spiritual well-being levels play a supportive role in the moral resilience they demonstrate in clinical practice. Moral empowerment stemming from spiritual well-being occurred through the development of empathy and compassion, a tendency toward right and good behaviors, motivation and satisfaction, coping with challenging situations, and a professional approach and sense of responsibility.

Development of empathy and compassion

One who lacks empathy and compassion cannot be a nurse (P6).

In my opinion, I am overly compassionate, but people see me as a very despotic, rule-bound, perfectionist person, but in the hospital, I can’t behave like that, because compassion immediately becomes involved. `I feel as if the patient were my mother, my father, my child`. The old saying reads, ‘Where the pain is, there the life is. For the patient, his/her illness is very serious. I can’t belittle his/her illness. For someone with a headache, he/she feels that the bottom falls out of his/her world (P4).

I pay particular attention to the issue of privacy. For example, I paid even more attention to this while I worked in the intensive care unit during clinical practice. There, patients were not able to move at all. I felt very uncomfortable when any part of their body was exposed. I would immediately go and cover it up or draw the curtain. Because of what I learned in lessons in school and because it does not align with my values, I paid attention to those issues; I paid a lot of attention (P7).

I’m scared. I don’t know, how would I feel if that happened to me? I empathize a lot (P9).

Tendency toward right and good behaviors

No matter how wrong others are, I must always be good. I was raised that way from a young age. Because when you go against morality, you’re the one who gets hurt. I realized that. That is why I think moral principles are important in our lives. In my personal life, these are the things I pay the most attention to, even if the other person does not pay attention to them… (P10).

In this sense, I both evaluate the moral aspect of my actions and pay attention to all kinds of morality from a religious point of view in my life (P1).

For example, even if the other person is bad, I cannot do him/her any harm because of my compassion. I treat them very well. That is very important to me. When I behave this way, I find my work and life more meaningful (P13).

Motivation and satisfaction

It makes you feel satisfied. You provide care, and you see the reward. For example, I experienced that in the hospital. When I covered the patient with a blanket, she said, ‘May God bless you, my dear.’ And I said, ‘it’s not a thing, auntie.’ When I said, ‘You can call me if you have any problems,’ her eyes lit up. I even said to myself, ‘Oh my God, I’ve received a blessing from her’ (P7).

I think the joy in the hospital, everyone feels it differently. It makes me reach the peaks. I become very happy. In addition, when it is mutual, it truly gives incredible happiness. I leave the hospital happy that day. It is the happiness of having received the patient’s blessings. When I receive this kind of reward for the service I provide with both my words and my knowledge, I leave the hospital without feeling tired (P1).

As humans, we truly want to be appreciated; anyone who says ‘I don’t want to get appreciation’ is lying deep down. Even the smallest compliment makes me so happy. When I learned how to establish vascular access for the first time, I was so stressed with the first two patients who I was angry with myself for not being able to do it. Then, the third patient came, I established it, and the patient left happily. In addition, I said, ‘Okay, I’ve gained my self-confidence now.’ I thought, ‘This way, I can help every patient.’ It gives you happiness and excitement.

Coping with challenging situations

A spiritually advanced individual controls his/her negative emotions because he/she knows that the other person will feel bad emotionally, or he/she knows how the other person will feel. Thus, he/she can move on in a better way without reflecting his/her negative emotions onto the other person (P14).

Of course, coping with challenging situations is very good not only for me but also for the patient. Because when the patient is tense, I approach the patient calmly. I treat the patient calmly, with compassion, and try to calm him/her down and complete the moment in a way that will benefit both of us. At that moment, I think that God sees me and that this patient is someone left to my care. Therefore, I approach the patient with patience and compassion, not with anger (P11).

Professional approach and sense of responsibility

In my opinion, professionalism is very important in nursing, because you are caring for people and you have to give equal care to all of them. I believe that every person is of equal value. Therefore, I think I should give the same care to whomever it may be (P2).

When I treat a patient, I think the person is not just a patient but a human being. With this awareness, I act more carefully and responsibly (P12).

When spirituality is mentioned in nursing, patient privacy is the first thing that comes to my mind. Respecting human dignity is very important to me. That’s why I’m extremely sensitive about privacy (P13).

To tell the truth, I believe that no matter how bad or uneasy you feel, you should leave all that unease outside and enter the patient’s room with a smiling face because that patient’s situation has nothing to do with yours. The patient came there to get better. You cannot make him/her worse because of a bad situation you have experienced in your daily life. You cannot put him/her in a bad situation. I think it is necessary to act completely professionally. If I have had an argument with my fellow intern before entering the patient’s room, I try to leave that thought completely outside before going in (P17).

Tension created by spiritual sensitivity in the ethical decision-making process

This subtheme demonstrates that nursing students’ spiritual sensitivity can sometimes create internal conflict and tension in their ethical decision-making processes.

The tension created by spiritual sensitivity in the ethical decision-making process manifests itself through the dilemma between ethics and morality and the conflict between sentimentality and objectivity.

Dilemma between ethics and morality

For me, morality is what God commands. Ethical behavior, in fact, stems from my faith (P3).

When I speak to a patient, I think that God sees me. That’s why I have to behave ethically (P12).

I believe what you sow is what you reap. Therefore, in my opinion, behaving ethically is not only a professional responsibility but also a moral responsibility (P10).

I regard the patient as someone left to my care. Therefore, I cannot harm him/her (P11).

Effect of emotionality on objective decision-making

I value certain spiritual qualities of a person. I get this kind of thought: For example, if a patient who does not belong to my religion comes in, I might treat him/her differently. In fact, I should not do it. From a professional standpoint, it is a very bad thing. However, it affects me a lot. Spiritually, I cannot accept certain things. Sometimes people who do not belong to any religion come. Sometimes people who are atheists come. I cannot accept them at all. I take a dim view of them. For example, if those people came in as patients, I might take a dim view of them (P15).

In the unit, they told me that the patient had been arrested for a crime and what his crime was. The crime was slightly unusual. Therefore, I felt terrible, and I had bad thoughts about him. When I started the intervention with those bad thoughts, I couldn’t do much. I mean, while I was performing the intervention for that criminal, I was trying to do it quickly and leave. I was trying not to talk to or communicate with him at all (P5).

During my intensive care unit internship, there was a young female patient. She had stitches from her abdomen upward to her chest, and she needed a dressing change. There were a male nurse and a high school student nurse intern with me. Three of us went to the patient. The patient said to the male nurse, ‘Could you not do it?’ The male nurse said, ‘Other nurses are busy right now. I have to do this.’ We talked among ourselves, ‘At least let’s cover her with a sheet.’ We covered her with it, but maybe the patient wouldn’t even want that. In that sense, I might have hesitated a little. I felt bad. I imagined my own mother or sister in the patient’s place. Therefore, I’m not sure if what we did was right. In this situation, after applying the dressing, I didn’t truly want to talk to the patient or go near her. The patient is a good person, but when we went to take her vital signs the next day, I still felt a little bad. (P16).

I don’t consider myself very competent in ethical decision-making right now. Because when morality comes into play slightly more… For example, I didn’t want to make an eye contact while I took an ECG (Electrocardiogram) of a female patient. I realized that I looked at the ECG machine more to avoid making the patient uncomfortable. Because, according to societal norms and morality, a woman’s chest area should not be seen by a man (P17, a male nurse).

As presented in Table 4, the qualitative findings indicated that spiritual well-being was associated with moral resilience through empathy and compassion; professional responsibility; motivation and satisfaction; coping with challenging situations; and a tendency toward right and good behaviors. The qualitative findings also suggested that spiritual sensitivity may sometimes contribute to tensions in ethical decision-making processes.

Discussion

In the present study, a weak positive correlation was identified between the Harmony with Nature dimension of spiritual well-being and overall moral resilience, suggesting that spiritual tendencies related to harmony with nature may support students’ capacity to respond to ethical challenges. The qualitative findings of the present study also support these findings. In particular, the codes “development of empathy and compassion” and “professional approach and sense of responsibility” were designed to strengthen moral resilience in parallel with the Harmony with Nature dimension. This result is consistent with the results of similar studies in the literature. For example, in a study conducted with nurses in Saudi Arabia, spiritual well-being was demonstrated to play a mediating role in the relationship between moral resilience and compassion fatigue and to reduce compassion fatigue [5]. Similarly, it has been reported that spiritual intelligence is positively correlated with professional nursing outcomes such as quality of care, compassionate behavior, professional commitment, and clinical competence [31, 32]. These results support the qualitative findings of the present study, indicating that the code “development of empathy and compassion” strengthens moral resilience. Furthermore, in a study conducted with nursing students in Taiwan, students whose ethical decision-making competence levels were high had a lower risk of experiencing moral distress [17], which is consistent with the qualitative findings of the present study, which demonstrated that the “professional approach and sense of responsibility” code supports moral resilience. These qualitative findings do more than merely support the quantitative results. They help explain the mechanisms underlying the observed relationship by showing how empathy, compassion, respect for human dignity, and professional responsibility translate spiritual well-being into morally resilient behaviors during clinical practice. Thus, the weak but significant quantitative association appears to be driven by these underlying psychosocial processes.

In the present study, a weak positive relationship was identified between the anomie dimension and overall moral resilience. The positive association between the anomie dimension and moral resilience should be interpreted in light of the scale’s scoring procedure. Because the items comprising the Anomie dimension were reverse scored according to the original scale instructions, higher scores reflect lower levels of anomie and greater existential meaning, purpose, and coherence. Accordingly, the observed positive relationship suggests that nursing students experiencing lower levels of anomie demonstrate greater moral resilience. Qualitative findings help explain this relationship by highlighting the roles of meaning-making, motivation, and commitment to morally appropriate behavior. In particular, the codes “Tendency toward Correct and Good Behaviors” and “Motivation and Satisfaction” supported moral resilience through meaning, purpose, and internal integrity, which are conceptually consistent with higher scores on the reverse-scored Anomie dimension. This interpretation is also supported by the findings of a study conducted with nursing students in Iran via structural equation modeling [20]. In the same study, purposefulness and moral resilience were found to be protective factors against feelings of psychological emptiness; purposefulness was negatively correlated with psychological emptiness and positively correlated with moral resilience, and moral resilience played a mediating role in the relationship between purposefulness and psychological emptiness. These results support the potential protective role of moral resilience against emotionally challenging situations encountered by students in clinical settings [20], which is consistent with the qualitative findings obtained in the present study, indicating that codes such as “tendency toward right and good behavior” and “motivation and satisfaction” support moral resilience by strengthening individuals’ sense of meaning and purpose. The integration of both datasets suggests that meaning-making may be a key pathway linking spiritual well-being and moral resilience. While the quantitative findings identified a positive relationship, the qualitative narratives clarified that students derive motivation, satisfaction, and ethical commitment from their sense of meaning and purpose, thereby enhancing their capacity to cope with moral challenges.

On the other hand, qualitative findings have demonstrated that spiritual sensitivity can sometimes lead to internal tension in the ethical decision-making process. The dilemma between ethical principles and moral values is particularly related to the transcendence dimension, and in some cases, emotional sensitivity can make the objective decision-making process difficult, creating fragility in the harmony with nature dimension. A study conducted with nursing students in Australia revealed that spirituality has a multidimensional and multilevel structure and that it includes an individual component related to religiosity and personal beliefs [33]. This multidimensional structure of spirituality can sometimes lead to tension between an individual’s values and beliefs and ethical principles. However, in the literature, it has been reported that nursing students may experience similar ethical tensions in clinical settings. For example, a qualitative study conducted with nursing students in Iran demonstrated that the students experienced moral distress in clinical settings due to value conflicts, a lack of ethical knowledge, and unprofessional practices, which led to feelings such as shame, guilt, and a desire to withdraw from the environment [34]. These results suggest that while spiritual sensitivity increases ethical awareness, students may also experience internal tension because they become more sensitive to ethically challenging situations. The qualitative findings also revealed situations in which personal beliefs, religious values, or emotional reactions could influence students’ perceptions of patients and clinical decision-making processes. From a professional and ethical perspective, such situations may create a risk of bias and unequal care if not adequately addressed. Nursing practice requires respect for human dignity, justice, nondiscrimination, and the provision of equitable care regardless of patients’ religious beliefs, cultural background, or personal characteristics. Therefore, these findings highlight the importance of strengthening ethics education, cultural competence, and reflective practice within nursing curricula. Educational interventions that help students critically examine their personal values and distinguish between personal beliefs and professional responsibilities may contribute to more ethical, equitable, and patient-centered care. However, it can be argued that some psychosocial protective factors may balance this process. A multicenter study conducted with nursing interns in China revealed that moral injury was negatively correlated with moral resilience, moral courage, and positive coping [19]. In another study conducted with nursing students, factors that strengthen resilience, such as social support and goal self-efficacy, were inversely correlated with moral distress [14]. Taken together, these results suggest that the psychosocial resources that students have when they are faced with ethically challenging situations may play a protective role in reducing the experience of moral tension. An important meta-inference emerging from the integration of quantitative and qualitative findings is that spiritual well-being has a dual structure. The quantitative findings indicated that greater spiritual well-being was associated with greater moral resilience. However, qualitative findings revealed that the same spiritual sensitivity could also generate ethical dilemmas, emotional conflicts, and difficulties in maintaining objectivity in certain clinical situations. This complementary yet partially contradictory pattern suggests that spiritual well-being functions both as a protective resource and as a potential source of ethical tension. This complexity could not have been fully identified through either dataset alone.

Conclusion and recommendations

In the present study, a positive and significant relationship was revealed between the spiritual well-being and moral resilience levels of the participating nursing students, which suggests that spiritual tendencies, particularly those involving harmony with nature and transcendence, can contribute to the development of moral resilience by supporting the students’ ability to develop empathy and compassion, their inclination toward right and good behavior, their motivation and satisfaction, their ability to cope with difficult situations, their professional approaches, and their sense of responsibility. However, spiritual sensitivity can sometimes lead to internal tensions in ethical decision-making processes. In conclusion, the results indicate that spiritual well-being is a bidirectional construct because it not only supports moral resilience but also, in some cases, can complicate ethical decision-making. The mixed-methods design provided a deeper understanding of this relationship by demonstrating not only that spiritual well-being is associated with moral resilience but also how and under which circumstances this relationship operates. The qualitative findings expand the quantitative results by identifying the mechanisms that strengthen moral resilience as well as the ethical tensions that may arise from heightened spiritual sensitivity.

Therefore, incorporating more topics related to spirituality and ethics into nursing education curricula could contribute to the development of students’ moral resilience. However, it should also be considered that spiritual sensitivity can sometimes lead to internal tensions in ethical decision-making processes. Therefore, educational approaches that are more comprehensive should be developed to address spirituality and ethics issues in nursing education, taking into account the potential tensions that spiritual sensitivity may create in ethical decision-making processes. It is also believed that conducting studies at different universities, in different cultural contexts, and with larger samples will contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of the relationship between spiritual well-being and moral resilience.

Strengths and limitations

The study’s strongest point is that, in the present study, the relationship between spiritual well-being and moral resilience levels among nursing students was investigated via a mixed-methods research design. The inclusion of both quantitative and qualitative data made it possible to interpret the results in depth. Moreover, the collection of qualitative data through in-depth interviews contributed to a detailed understanding of the effect of the participants’ spiritual tendencies on ethical decision-making processes. However, since the study was conducted at a single center, the results are applicable only to the nursing students surveyed, and they cannot be generalized to all nursing students. Another limitation of the study is the relatively low internal consistency coefficient obtained for the Rushton Moral Resilience Scale (Cronbach’s α = 0.63). This value was lower than that reported in the Turkish validity and reliability studies of the scale. Possible explanations may include differences in sample characteristics, the relatively homogeneous structure of the study population, or contextual and cultural factors affecting item responses. Therefore, findings related to moral resilience should be interpreted with caution. In addition, the quantitative data were collected through self-reports, which may lead to the results being limited to the participants’ statements.
