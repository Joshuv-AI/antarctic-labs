On the Shirshov–Cohn theorem for JB-algebras
Mark Roelands, Samuel Tiersma

Abstract
It is shown that a JB-algebra which can be generated by the union of two of its associative Jordan subalgebras is a JC-algebra, hence special. A similar refinement of Macdonald’s principle for JB-algebras is obtained. Moreover, we prove that the free unital JB-algebra generated by n n projections is a JC-algebra if and only if n ∈ { 1 , 2 , 3 } n\in\{1,2,3\} . Finally, we give an explicit description of the free unital JB-algebra generated by two projections paralleling the Raeburn-Sinclair theorem for C*-algebras.



Keywords:
JB-algebras, Shirshov–Cohn theorem, Mcdonald’s principle, special Jordan algebras, exceptional JB-algebras, identities in Jordan algebras, Albert algebra, free JB-algebra generated by projections

Subject Classification: 17C05, 17C40, 17C10

For each associative real algebra

]
,

[

]

]

There are two classical results that address this theme: the Shirshov–Cohn theorem and Macdonald’s principle. The Shirshov–Cohn theorem asserts that every Jordan algebra which can be generated by two elements (and
1
) is special. According to Macdonald’s principle, if a Jordan identity involving three variables is linear in one of these and is satisfied by all special Jordan algebras, then this identity is satisfied by all Jordan algebras. Macdonald’s principle has been recognized as an indispensable tool
[
24
, p. 458]
in the development of the theory of Jordan algebras, allowing the efficient verification of many Jordan identities by reducing to the case of a special Jordan algebra and then calculating in the ambient associative structure.

In this paper we revisit these results in the setting of JB- and JBW-algebras. The prototypical example of a JB–algebra is the self-adjoint part
A_{sa}
of a C*-algebra
A
endowed with the Jordan product
x\circ y:=\frac{1}{2}(xy+yx)
. Note that while the C*-algebra product of two self-adjoint elements is generally not self-adjoint, the Jordan product does yield an intrinsic algebraic structure on the ordered vector space
A_{sa}

A JB-algebra is called a
JC-algebra
if it embeds in

for some complex Hilbert space
H
. The Shirshov–Cohn theorem for JB-algebras says that each JB-algebra which can be generated by two elements (and
1

Theorem 1.1
.

A JB-algebra is a JC-algebra if it can be generated by the union of two of its associative Jordan subalgebras.

This result is proved as
Theorem

3.1

A JB-algebra is a JC-algebra if it can be generated by three projections (possibly together with the unit element).

Let

Each of these results is proved by making a reduction using

Moreover, in

2.1
Jordan algebras

A real
Jordan algebra
is a real vector space
J
equipped with a commutative (but not necessarily associative) bilinear product
(x,y)\mapsto x\circ y
which satisfies the following
Jordan identity
for all
x,y\in J
:

x\circ(y\circ x^{2})=(x\circ y)\circ x^{2}.

(1)

A real linear subspace
E
of
J
is called a
Jordan subalgebra
of
J
if it is closed under the product
\circ
, and
E
is called a (Jordan)
ideal
of
J
if
x\circ y\in E
for all
x\in E
and
y\in J
.

We say
J
is
unital
if it has a
unit element

1_{J}
such that
1_{J}\circ x=x
for all
x\in J
. If
J
is not unital, then we may form its
unitization

\tilde{J}:=\mathbb{R}1_{J}\oplus J
with the product

(\lambda 1_{J}+x)\circ(\mu 1_{J}+y)=\lambda\mu 1_{J}+(\lambda y+\mu x+x\circ y)

for
\lambda,\mu\in\mathbb{R}
and
x,y\in A
; then
\tilde{J}
is a unital Jordan algebra containing
J
as an ideal.

Let
K
be a second Jordan algebra. A linear map
\phi\colon J\to K
is called a
Jordan homomorphism
if
\phi(x\circ y)=\phi(x)\circ\phi(y)
. If
J
and
K
are unital with unit elements
1_{J}
resp.
1_{K}
, then a Jordan homomorphism
\phi\colon J\to K
is called

if
\phi(1_{J})=1_{K}
.

.

Let
A
be an associative real algebra. Let
J\subset A
be a real linear subspace which is closed under taking squares. Then

is a real Jordan algebra under the

(2)

2.2
Identities and special Jordan algebras

A Jordan algebra
J
is called
special
if there exists an associative algebra
A
and an injective linear map
\alpha\colon J\to A
such that for all
x,y\in A
one has

\phi(x\circ y)=\tfrac{1}{2}(\alpha(x)\alpha(y)+\alpha(y)\alpha(x)).

A Jordan algebra which is not special is called
exceptional
. Albert proved in
[
2
]
that the Albert algebra
\mathbb{A}
in
Example

2.2
is exceptional. More examples are furnished by free Jordan algebras.

Definition 2.3
.

Let
S
be a set. The
free unital Jordan algebra
on the set
S
is a unital Jordan algebra
\FJ(S)
together with a map of sets
\iota_{S}\colon S\to J
satisfying the following universal property. For each unital Jordan algebra
J
and map of sets
\alpha\colon S\to J
there exists a unique unital Jordan homomorphism
\psi\colon\FJ(S)\to J
such that
\psi(\iota(s))=\alpha(s)
for all
s\in S
.

The free unital Jordan algebra exists and the pair
(\FJ(S),\iota_{S})
is unique up to a unique isomorphism
[
15
, Ch. 1, Sect. 6]
. A similar free object in the category of
special
unital Jordan algebras exists and is constructed explicitly as follows
[
22
, p. 684]
. Denote by
\mathbb{R}\{S\}
the free unital associative algebra on the set
S
, consisting of all real polynomials in the noncommuting variables
X_{s}
for
s\in S
. We endow
\mathbb{R}\{S\}
with the Jordan product (
2
). Let
\FSJ(S)
be the Jordan subalgebra of
\mathbb{R}\{S\}
generated by
\{X_{s}\colon s\in S\}
, and define
\iota_{S}\colon S\to\FSJ(S)
,
\iota_{S}(s):=X_{s}
. Then, given a unital special Jordan algebra
J
, say embedded in the unital associative algebra
A
, and a map of sets
\alpha\colon S\to J
, there exists a unique unital algebra homomorphism
\tilde{\psi}\colon\mathbb{R}\{S\}\to A
such that
\tilde{\psi}(X_{s})=\alpha(s)
for every
s\in S
, which restricts to a unique unital Jordan homomorphism
\psi\colon\FSJ(S)\to J
such that
\psi\circ\iota_{S}=\alpha
. Therefore, the pair
(\FSJ(S),\iota_{S})
is the
free unital special Jordan algebra
on the set
S
, in the sense that it satisfies the universal property in
Definition

2.3
but with
J
restricted to the class of unital
special
Jordan algebras.

For each positive integer
n
, denote
[n]:=\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
. A (real)
Jordan polynomial

F
is an element of
\FJ([n])
for some
n\geq 1
. Let
J
be a unital Jordan algebra. We say that
J

satisfies the Jordan polynomial

F\in\FJ([n])
if for all elements
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in J
the unique unital Jordan homomorphism
\psi\colon\FJ([n])\to J
such that
\psi(\iota_{[n]}(k))=x_{i}
for all
1\leq k\leq n
satisfies
\psi(F)=0
. It is easy to see that for the identity
F=0
to be satisfiable by some unital Jordan algebra, it is necessary that the constant term of
F
be zero. It follows that
J
satisfies
F
if and only if
\tilde{J}
satisfies
F
. Without creating ambiguity, we will say that a non-unital Jordan algebra
K

satisfies

F
if its unitization
\tilde{K}
satisfies
F
. If
G,H\in\FJ([m])
are Jordan polynomials, we will also say that a Jordan algebra
satisfies the identity

G=H
if it is satisfies the Jordan polynomial
F:=G-H
.

There exist identities satisfied by all special Jordan algebras, but not by all Jordan algebras. Two identities of total degree
8
resp.
9
were exhibited by Glennie in
[
11
, Thm 4(a)]
and read

\displaystyle G_{8}:H_{8}(X,Y,Z)

\displaystyle=H_{8}(Y,X,Z),

(7)

\displaystyle H_{8}(X,Y,Z)

\displaystyle:=\{U_{X}U_{Y}(Z),Z,X\circ Y\}-U_{X}U_{Y}U_{Z}(X\circ Y).

resp.

\displaystyle G_{9}:H_{9}(X,Y,Z)

\displaystyle=H_{9}(Y,X,Z),

(8)

\displaystyle H_{9}(X,Y,Z)

\displaystyle:=2(U_{X}Z)\circ U_{Y,X}U_{Z}(Y^{2})-U_{X}U_{Z}U_{X,Y}U_{Y}(Z).

Glennie’s identities are satisfied by
\FSJ(X,Y,Z)
but not by the Albert algebra
\mathbb{A}
.

A Jordan algebra
J
is called
identity-special
if it satisfies each identity satisfied by all special Jordan algebras. A unital Jordan algebra
J
is identity-special if and only if
J
is a homomorphic image of a (free) special Jordan algebra. (If
J
is identity-special then, with
S
equal to the set underlying

, the surjective unital Jordan homomorphism
\psi\colon\FJ(S)\to\tilde{J}
such that
\psi\circ\iota_{S}=\Id_{J}
factorizes through
\FSJ(S)

\mathbb{A}
is not identity-special, and thus gives another proof of the Albert algebra’s exceptionality.

P.M. Cohn showed that a quotient
\FSJ(S)/K
of the free special Jordan algebra
\FSJ(S)
by a Jordan ideal
K
is special if and only if
K=\langle K\rangle\cap\FSJ(S)
, where
\langle K\rangle\subset\mathbb{R}\{S\}
denotes the ideal of the associative algebra
\mathbb{R}\{S\}
generated by
K
. He used this speciality criterion to give the following example of an identity-special but not special Jordan algebra
[
7
, Scholium 6.1]
.

Example 2.4
.

Let
K
be the Jordan ideal of
\FSJ([3])
generated by
X_{1}^{2}-X_{2}^{2}
. Then the Jordan algebra
\FSJ([3])/K
is identity-special but not special.

We now come to two foundational results in the theory of Jordan algebras. A.I. Shirshov showed in
[

, Thm. 2]
that
\FJ([2])
is a special Jordan algebra. Cohn showed in
[

is again special. The Shirshov–Cohn theorem combines these results.

Theorem 2.5

(Shirshov–Cohn)
.

Each Jordan algebra which can be generated by two elements (possibly together with the unit element) is special.

Proof.

See
[

, Thm. 10]
.
∎

Shirshov’s theorem may be stated by saying that the canonical map
\FJ([2])\to\FSJ([2])
is an isomorphism. I.G. Macdonald proved in
[
23
]
that each non-zero element of the kernel of
\FJ([3])\to\FSJ([3])
has degree at least

in each of the generators. This result has come to be known as

.

Theorem 2.6

(Macdonald)
.

Let
F(X,Y,Z)
be a Jordan polynomial of degree at most
1
in
Z
. If
F
is satisfied by all special Jordan algebras, then
F
is satisfied by all Jordan algebras.

Proof.

We give an outline of the proof given by Jacobson in
[
19
]
. Given a subalgebra
B
of a Jordan algebra
J
, we denote by
M_{B}(J)
the
multiplication subalgebra
of
\End(J)
generated by the multiplication operators
L(b)
for
b\in B
. By Shirshov’s theorem,
\FJ([2])\cong\FSJ([2])
may be considered as a Jordan subalgebra of both
\FJ([3])
and
\FSJ([3])
.
The canonical surjective homomorphism
\FJ([3])\to\FSJ([3])
induces a surjective algebra homomorphism

\sigma\colon M_{\FJ([2])}(\FJ[3])\to M_{\FJ([2])}(\FSJ[3]).

(9)

Macdonald’s principle is equivalent to the assertion that
\sigma
is injective (confer
[
24

). Jacobson proved that
\sigma
is indeed an isomorphism by presenting both multiplication algebras in (
9
) using the same generators and relations.
∎

We conclude this section by an elementary lemma, which will prove useful later.

Lemma 2.7
.

Let
A
be a real Jordan algebra. Let
\tau
be a linear topology or linear convergence structure on
A
such that multiplication on
A
is separately
\tau
-continuous. Let
F
be a Jordan polynomial and let
D
be a Jordan subalgebra of
A
satisfying
F
. Then the
\tau
-closure
\overline{D}^{\tau}
of
D
is a real Jordan subalgebra of
A
satisfying
F
.

Proof.

First assume that
\tau
is a linear topology. Then
\overline{D}^{\tau}
is readily seen to be a Jordan subalgebra of
A
. The Jordan polynomial
F
is the sum of homogeneous Jordan polynomials

F(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})=\sum_{d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}}F_{d}(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n})

where for each
d=(d_{1},\ldots,d_{n})\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
and
1\leq i\leq n
, the Jordan polynomial
F_{d}
is homogeneous of degree
d_{i}
in
X_{i}
. Then for all
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}\in\mathbb{R}
and
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in D
we have

0=F(\lambda_{1}x_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{n}x_{n})=\sum_{d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}}\lambda_{1}^{d_{1}}\cdots\lambda_{n}^{d_{n}}F_{d}(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}).

Since
\mathbb{R}
is an infinite field, it follows that
D
satisfies each
F_{d}
(and that
F_{0}=0
if
D\neq 0
). Fix
d\in\mathbb{Z}_{\geq 0}^{n}
such that
m:=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}>0
. Let
\tilde{F}_{d}(W_{1},\ldots,W_{m})
be the complete linearization of
F_{d}
, so
\tilde{F}_{d}
is linear in each of its
m=\sum_{i=1}^{n}d_{i}
variables and for all
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\in A
we have

\tilde{F}_{d}(\underbrace{x_{1},x_{1},\ldots,x_{1}}_{d_{1}\text{ times}},\underbrace{x_{2},x_{2},\ldots,x_{2}}_{d_{2}\text{ times}},\ldots,\underbrace{x_{n},x_{n},\ldots,x_{n}}_{d_{n}\text{ times}})=F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}).

(10)

By induction on
0\leq k\leq m
we prove that for all
w_{1},\ldots,w_{k}\in\overline{D}^{\tau}
and
w_{k+1},\ldots,w_{m}\in D
we have
\tilde{F}_{d}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m})=0
. The induction base holds since
D
satisfies
\tilde{F}_{d}
. Let
0<l\leq m
and assume the statement is proved for
k=l-1
. Let
w_{1},\ldots,w_{l}\in\overline{D}^{\tau}
and
w_{l+1},\ldots,w_{m}\in D
be arbitrary. Choose a net
(a_{\lambda})_{\lambda}
in
D
such that
a_{\lambda}\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\tau}}{{\to}}w_{k}
. Since multiplication is separately
\tau
-continuous and
\tilde{F}_{d}
is linear in
W_{k}
, we have by the inductive hypothesis

\tilde{F}_{d}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{m})=\lim_{\lambda}\tilde{F}_{d}(w_{1},\ldots,w_{l-1},a_{\lambda},w_{l+1},\ldots,w_{m})=\lim_{\lambda}0=0.

This proves the statement for
k=l
and completes the inductive step. Now by induction the statement holds for
k=m
, whence
\tilde{F}_{d}
is satisfied by
\overline{D}^{\tau}
. But then
\overline{D}^{\tau}
also satisfies
F_{d}
in view of (
10
). We conclude that
F=\sum_{d}F_{d}
is satisfied by
\overline{D}^{\tau}
.

We now return to the general case that
\tau
is a linear convergence structure. Using the same argument, one shows by transfinite recursion on an ordinal
\alpha
that elements of the
\alpha
-adherence
\overline{D}^{\tau,\alpha}
of
D
satisfy each
\tilde{F}_{d}
and that
\overline{D}^{\tau,\alpha}
is a Jordan subalgebra of
A
if
\alpha
is a limit ordinal. Since the
\tau
-closure
\overline{D}^{\tau}
of
D
is equal to the
\alpha
-adherence
\overline{D}^{\tau,\alpha}
for a sufficiently large limit ordinal
\alpha
, the proof is complete.
∎

2.3
JB-algebras

A
JB-algebra
is a real Jordan algebra
J
which is complete in a norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
satisfying for all
x,y\in J
:

(1)

\lVert x\circ y\rVert\leq\lVert x\rVert\lVert y\rVert
;

(2)

\lVert x\rVert^{2}=\lVert x^{2}\rVert
;

(3)

\lVert x^{2}\rVert\leq\lVert x^{2}+y^{2}\rVert
.

The set of squares

J_{+}:=\{x^{2}:x\in J\}

is a closed convex cone in
J
, which determines an Archimedean partial order on
J
given for
x,y\in J
by

x\geq y:\iff x-y\in J_{+}.

Axiom (3) thus asserts that the norm
\lVert\cdot\rVert
is monotone on
J_{+}
. As is the case for C*-algebras, there is at most one norm on a real Jordan algebra making it a JB-algebra.

A
JB-subalgebra
of
J
is a closed linear subspace
E
of
J
which is closed under the product of
J
. The smallest JB-subalgebra of
J
containing a given set
S\subset J
is denoted
\JB(S)
and called the
JB-algebra generated by
S
. The JB-algebra generated by a single element
x
and possibly the unit element is an associative Banach algebra.

Let
J
be a JB-algebra with unit element
1_{J}
. An element
x\in J
is called
invertible
if
x
is invertible in the associative Banach algebra
\JB(1,x)
. The
spectrum
of
x
is the set

\sigma(x):=\{\lambda\in\mathbb{R}:\lambda\text{ is not Jordan invertible in }J\}.

(11)

Then according to the spectral theorem in JB-algebras, there is an isomorphism

\JB(1,x)\cong C(\sigma(x),\mathbb{R}),

sending
x
to the identical function
\id_{\sigma(x)}:t\mapsto t
. Hence, the norm on
J
coincides with the spectral radius norm:

\lVert x\rVert=\sup\{|\lambda|:\lambda\in\sigma(x)\}.

(12)

Moreover,
1_{J}
is an Archimedean order unit, i.e. for all

there exists
\lambda\geq 0
with
x\leq\lambda 1_{J}
, and the norm on
J
also coincides with the associated
order unit norm
(cf.
[

):

\lVert x\rVert=\sup\{\lambda\geq 0:-\lambda 1_{J}\leq x\leq\lambda 1_{J}\}.

(13)

If

is a non-unital JB-algebra, then it was shown by Behncke
[
6
]
that the unitization
\tilde{H}=\mathbb{R}1_{H}\oplus H
is a JB-algebra in the spectral radius norm (see also
[
13
, Thm. 3.3.9]
).

Examples 2.9
.

(1) The associative JB-algebras coincide, up to isomorphism, with spaces
C_{0}(X,\mathbb{R})
for some locally compact Hausdorff space

.

(2) Each C*-algebra

gives rise to a JB-algebra

, by endowing the real vector space

of self-adjoint elements with the

(14)

and the induced norm.

(3) The Albert algebra is a JB-algebra under the spectral norm.

(4) Let
(H,\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle)
be a real Hilbert space. We endow

with the product

(\lambda,\xi)\circ(\mu,\eta)=(\lambda\mu,\lambda\eta+\mu\xi+\langle\xi,\eta\rangle).

and the norm
\lVert(\lambda,\xi)\rVert=|\lambda|+\langle\xi,\xi\rangle^{1/2}
. Then

is a JB-algebra called a
spin-factor
.

Let
A
be a JB-algebra. We say
A
is a
JC-algebra
if

is isometrically isomorphic to a closed Jordan subalgebra of
B(H)_{sa}
for some complex Hilbert space

. We call

purely exceptional

admits no non-zero homomorphisms into a JC-algebra. Each JB-algebra

has a unique closed ideal

such that

is a JC-algebra and

is purely exceptional

, Thm. 4.19]
.

Alfsen–Shultz–Størmer established in
[
3
]
the following Gelfand–Naimark type theorem for JB-algebras. To state it, note that for an index set

and a JB-algebra

, the

direct sum

\ell^{\infty}(I,B):=\{f\colon I\to B\text{ with }\textstyle{\sup_{i\in I}\lVert f(i)\rVert<\infty}\}

with the supremum norm is again a JB-algebra under the pointwise Jordan product.

Theorem 2.10

(Gelfand–Naimark theorem for JB-algebras)
.

Each JB-algebra is isomorphic to a closed Jordan subalgebra of
B(H)_{sa}\oplus\ell^{\infty}(I,\mathbb{A})
for some complex Hilbert space
H
and index set
I
.

Theorem

2.10
readily yields that each purely exceptional JB-algebra is isomorphic to a JB-subalgebra of
\ell^{\infty}(I,\mathbb{A})
for a suitable index set
I
. Thus, the Albert algebra may be said to be the progenitor of all purely exceptional JB-algebras. The following theorem, which combines
[
3
, Lemma 9.4 and Theorem 9.5]
, is a consequence of this fact.

Theorem 2.11
.

Let
A
be a JB-algebra. Then the following are equivalent:

(1)

A
is a JC-algebra;

(2)

A
is a special Jordan algebra;

(3)

A
is an identity-special Jordan algebra;

(4)

A
satisfies Glennie’s identity
G_{8}
(or
G_{9}
);

(5)

there does not exists a surjective Jordan homomorphism
\pi\colon A\to\mathbb{A}
onto the Albert algebra;

Proof.

(1)
\implies
(2)
\implies
(3)
\implies
(4) is immediate, while (4)
\implies
(5) holds since
\mathbb{A}
satisfies neither
G_{8}
nor
G_{9}
. The remaining implication (5)

[

, Cor. 4.20]
.
∎

Notwithstanding the equivalence of (1) and (2) in the above theorem, the class of JC-algebras is strictly smaller than the class of special real Jordan algebras. Indeed, the universal property of the free special Jordan algebra on a nonempty
S
implies there does not exist a norm that makes
\FSJ(S)
into a JB-algebra, because Jordan homomorphism between JB-algebras are automatically contractive
[
4
, Prop. 1.35]
. However, it can be embedded as a non-closed Jordan subalgebra of a JC-algebra. This has as a consequence that the class of JC-algebras governs which identities hold in general special real Jordan algebras.

Proposition 2.12
.

Let
G
be a real Jordan polynomial which is satisfied by all JC-algebras. Then
G
is satisfied by each (identity-)special real Jordan algebra
J
.

Proof.

Let
J
be an identity-special real Jordan algebra. Then the unitization
\tilde{J}
is a homomorphic image of the free special Jordan algebra
\FSJ_{\mathbb{R}}(S)
on some set
S
. Hence we only need to show that
\FSJ_{\mathbb{R}}(S)
satisfies
G
. This will be proved by exhibiting an injective Jordan homomorphism of
\FSJ_{\mathbb{R}}(S)
into
B(H)_{sa}
for a suitable real Hilbert space
H
.

By construction,
\FSJ_{\mathbb{R}}(S)
is the real Jordan subalgebra of
\mathbb{R}\{S\}
generated by the set
S
. It suffices to find an injective
\mathbb{R}
-algebra homomorphism
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\{S\}\to B(H)
such that
\phi(s)\in B(H)_{sa}
for each
s\in S
, for then
\phi
restricts to an injective real Jordan homomorphism from
\FSJ_{\mathbb{R}}(S)
into
B(H)_{sa}
.

We endow
\mathbb{R}\{S\}
with the real inner product for which the set of mononomials in
S
is an orthonormal basis, and let
H
be the Hilbert space completion of
\mathbb{R}\{S\}
. For every
F\in\mathbb{R}\{S\}
, left multiplication by
F
on
\mathbb{R}\{S\}
extends to a bounded operator
\ell_{F}\in B(H)
. The universal property of the free associative
\mathbb{R}
-algebra gives a unique
\mathbb{R}
-algebra homomorphism
\phi\colon\mathbb{R}\{S\}\to B(H)
such that
\phi(s)=\ell_{s}+\ell_{s}^{*}
for every
s\in S
. The proof is finished by showing that
\phi
is injective.

Note that
H
inherits from
\mathbb{R}\{S\}
a grading given by the degree of words. For
s\in S
, the operator
\ell_{s}
has degree
1
, hence its adjoint
\ell_{s}^{*}
has degree
-1
. Let
G\in\mathbb{R}\{S\}
be non-zero, and let
n\geq 1
be its degree. Let
G_{n}
be the homogeneous component of
G
of degree
n
. The vector
\ell_{G}(1)
and
\phi(G)(1)
in
H
have the same homogeneous component of degree
n
, namely
G_{n}\neq 0
. Therefore
\phi(G)\neq 0
and
\phi
is injective.
∎

Remark 2.13
.

Each integral Jordan polynomial
F
which is satisfied by all JC-algebras, is satisfied by each special Jordan algebra
J
over every commutative ring

. (If
2
is not invertible in

, then

is understood to be a quadratic Jordan algebra over

in the sense of
[

, Ch. 1, Def. 3]
). Indeed, by restriction of scalars

becomes a special Jordan algebra over
\mathbb{Z}
. Therefore,

is a homomorphic image of
\FSJ_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
. Since
\FSJ_{\mathbb{Z}}(S)
is a Jordan subalgebra of
\FSJ_{\mathbb{R}}(S)
, it also embeds in

. Then

is fulfilled by

, hence by

.

Example 2.14
.

One may wonder whether it is possible to embed each special real Jordan algebra in a JC-algebra. The identity-special but not special real Jordan algebra

from

2.4
provides a counterexample. In fact, if

is a Jordan subalgebra of a JB-algebra

, then the norm closure
\overline{J}
of

in

. Since

2.4
JBW-algebras

In this section, we discuss the Jordan analogue of von Neumann algebras (or more accurately, W*-algebras), called JBW-algebras.

Let
M
be a unital JB-algebra. A linear functional
\phi\colon M\to\mathbb{R}
on
M
is called a
state
if
\phi
has norm
1
and is positive, i.e.
\phi(M_{+})\subset\mathbb{R}_{+}
, equivalently, if
\phi\in M^{*}
is bounded with
\lVert\phi\rVert=\phi(1)=1
. We say that
M
is
monotone complete
if each upper bounded increasing net
(x_{i})_{i}
in
M
has a supremum
x
in
M
. A state
\phi
on
M
is called
normal
if, for each increasing net
(x_{i})_{i}
in
M
with supremum
x
as above, one has
\phi(x)=\lim_{i}\phi(x_{i})
.

Definition 2.15
.

A unital JB-algebra
M
is called a
JBW
-algebra
if
M
is monotone complete and the normal states on
M
separate points.

Paralleling
2.9
, we have the following examples of JBW-algebras.

Examples 2.16
.

(1) An associative JB-algebra
C_{0}(X)
is a JBW-algebra if and only if
X
is a hyperstonean topological space.

(2) The self-adjoint part of a von Neumann algebra
A
is a JBW-algebra
A_{sa}
.

(3) Each finite-dimensional or reflexive JB-algebra is a JBW-algebra. In particular, the Albert algebra
\mathbb{A}
is a JBW-algebra.

A JB-algebra
M
is a JBW-algebra if and only if it is a Banach dual space. In this case, the linear span
M_{*}
of the normal states on
M
is the unique predual of
M
(up to isometric isomorphism). The weak topology
\sigma(M,M_{*})
on
M
determined by
M_{*}
is called the
\sigma
-weak topology
on
M
.

If
A
is a JB-algebra, then its bidual
A^{**}
is a JBW-algebra for a unique product extending the product on
A

[
4
, Prop. 2.4 and Cor. 2.50]
. Kaplansky’s density theorem for JB-algebras
[
4
, Prop. 2.69]
asserts that the unit ball of
A
is
\sigma
-strongly dense in the unit ball of
A^{**}
.

A
JW-algebra
is a JB-algebra which is isometrically isomorphic to a
\sigma
-strongly closed Jordan subalgebra of
B(H)_{sa}
. The bidual of a JC-algebra is a JW-algebra
[
4
, Prop. 2.77]
. If a JC-algebra is also a JBW-algebra, then it is in fact a JW-algebra
[
4
, Cor. 2.78]
. Alfsen–Shultz–Størmer have demonstrated in
[
3
]
that each JBW-algebra is isomorphic to
M\oplus C(X,\mathbb{A})
for some JW-algebra
M
and compact Hausdorff space
X
. Since each JB-algebra
A
is a JB-subalgebra of its bidual JBW-algebra
A^{**}
, this yields the Gelfand–Naimark theorem for JB-algebras which we stated in
Theorem

2.10
.

3
A generalization of the Shirshov–Cohn theorem

We will now prove the first main result of this article, which is the following generalization of the Shirshov–Cohn theorem for JB-algebras.

Theorem 3.1
.

Let
A
be a JB-algebra containing two associative Jordan subalgebras
B
and
C
such that
A
is generated as a JB-algebra by
B\cup C
. Then
A
is a JC-algebra.

Proof.

By assumption, the Jordan algebra
D
generated by
B
and
C
is dense in
A
. If
A
is not isomorphic to a JC-algebra, then there exists a surjective homomorphism
\pi\colon A\to\mathbb{A}
onto the Albert algebra
[
4
, Cor. 4.20]
. Since
\pi
is norm continuous
[
4
, Prop. 1.35]
, it follows that
\pi(D)
is dense in
\pi(A)
. Because
H_{3}(\mathbb{O})
is finite-dimensional, each of its subspaces is norm closed, so
\pi(D)=\pi(A)=\mathbb{A}
. Therefore
\mathbb{A}
is algebraically generated by
\pi(B)
and
\pi(C)
.

By the assumption that
B
and
C
are associative Jordan algebras, their homomorphic images
\pi(B)
and
\pi(C)
are also associative. Since a finite-dimensional associative JB-algebra is isomorphic to
\mathbb{R}^{k}
, we find that
\pi(B)\cong\mathbb{R}^{m}
and
\pi(C)\cong\mathbb{R}^{n}
for some integers
m,n\geq 1
(in fact, comparing ranks gives
m,n\leq 3
). Now
\mathbb{R}^{k}
can be generated as a Jordan algebra by a single element
(\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k})
by choosing
\lambda_{1},\ldots,\lambda_{k}
pairwise distinct. Hence each of
\pi(B)
and
\pi(C)
can be generated by a single element. This implies that the Albert
\mathbb{A}
is generated by two elements, which is absurd by the algebraic Shirshov–Cohn theorem. Therefore
A
is JC-algebra, proving the theorem.
∎

Corollary 3.2
.

Let
M
be a JBW-algebra containing two associative Jordan subalgebras
B
and
C
such that
M
is generated as a JBW-algebra by
B\cup C
. Then
M
is a JW-algebra.

Proof.

Let
D
be the JB-subalgebra of
M
generated by
B\cup C
, which is
\sigma
-weakly dense in
M
by assumption. Then
D
is a JC-algebra by
Theorem

3.1
, hence
D
satisfies Glennie’s identity
G_{8}
. Since the multiplication in
M
is separately
\sigma
-weakly continuous, by
Lemma

2.7
its
\sigma
-weak closure
M
satisfies Glennie’s identity
G_{8}
. But then
M
is a JC-algebra by
Theorem

2.11
, hence a JW-algebra.
∎

Remark 3.3
.

One can also deduce
Corollary

3.2
from
Theorem

3.1
in a similar way as to how in
[
12
, Lemma 2.3]
part (b) is deduced from part (a). The argument is as follows. If
D
is a
\sigma
-weakly dense JC-subalgebra of a JBW-algebra

, then by
[
4
, Thm. 2.65]
there is a normal homomorphism
\pi\colon D^{**}\to M
extending the inclusion map of
D
into

. Since

is
\sigma
-weakly dense in

, it follows that
\pi
is surjective. So there is a central idempotent

such that

restricts to an isomorphism of

4
Macdonald’s principle and operator commutativity

Let
J
be a JB-algebra and let
a,b\in J
. Recall that the multiplication operator defined by
a
is denoted
T_{a}\colon J\to J
,
T_{a}(x):=a\circ x
. One says that
a
and
b

operator commute
in
J
if
T_{a}\circ T_{b}=T_{b}\circ T_{a}
as linear endomorphism of
J
, equivalently, if
a\circ(x\circ b)=(a\circ x)\circ b
for all
x\in J
. Note that a priori, operator commutativity of
a
and
b
could depend on the ambient JB-algebra
J
. Van de Wetering proved that the formally weaker condition that
a
and
b
operator commute in
\JB(a,b)
implies that
a
and
b
operator commute in
J
. Using
Theorem

3.1
we shall prove a generalization of
[
34
, Theorem]
, in which we consider a general subset
S\subset J
rather than
\{a,b\}
.

Theorem 4.1
.

Let
J
be a JB-algebra and
S
a nonempty subset of
J
. Assume that all
s,s^{\prime}\in S
operator commute in
\JB(s,s^{\prime})
. Then
\JB(S)
is an associative JB-algebra whose elements pairwise operator commute in
J
. The analogous assertion holds for JBW-algebras.

We require the following lemma, asserting that in a JC-algebra
D\subset A_{sa}
, operator commutativity of two elements in
D
is equivalent to their commuting in the enveloping C*-algebra
A
.

Lemma 4.2

(Hanche–Olsen)
.

Let
A
be a C*-algebra and let
D
be a JC-subalgebra of
A_{sa}
. Let
x,y\in D
. Then
x
and
y
operator commute in
D
if and only if they commute in
A
, i.e.

T_{x}\circ T_{y}=T_{y}\circ T_{x}\iff xy=yx.

Proof.

See
[
14
, Lemma 5.1]
.
∎

We will prove
Theorem

4.1
by using
Theorem

3.1
to reduce to the case of a JC-algebra.

Proof of
Theorem

4.1
.

We prove that each nonempty finite subset
F
of
S
generates an associative JB-algebra by induction on the cardinality
|F|
of
F
. The base case
|F|=1
is clear. Let
n\geq 2
and assume that
\JB(F)
is associative whenever
|F|=n-1
. Now let
F\subset S
with
|F|=n
be given. Choose
y\in F
and let
F^{\prime}:=F\setminus\{y\}
. By the inductive hypothesis,
\JB(F^{\prime})
is associative. Since
F=F^{\prime}\cup\{y\}
, then
\JB(F)
is generated by the union of the associative JB-algebras
\JB(F^{\prime})
and
\JB(y)
. Now
Theorem

3.1
yields that
\JB(F)
is a JC-algebra, hence
\JB(F)\subset A_{sa}
for a C*-algebra
A
. We may assume that
\JB(F)
generates
A
as a C*-algebra. By assumption, all
a,b\in F
operator commute in
\JB(a,b)
, hence
a
and
b
commute in
A
by
Lemma

4.2
. Then
A
is generated by the set
F
of mutually commuting self-adjoint elements, hence
A
is an abelian C*-algebra. It follows that
\JB(F)\subset A_{sa}
is associative, completing the inductive step.

It follows that the filtered union
\bigcup_{F}\JB(F)
, where
F
ranges over all nonempty finite subsets
F\subset S
, is an associative Jordan algebra. Then the norm closure
\JB(S)
of
\bigcup_{F}\JB(F)
is also associative, by continuity of the product.

It remains to show that all
x,y\in\JB(S)
operator commute in
J
, not just in
\JB(S)
. To this end, let
z\in J
be arbitrary. We will show that
T_{x}(T_{y}(z))=T_{y}(T_{x}(z))
. By
Theorem

3.1
the JB-algebra generated by
S\cup\{z\}
is a JC-algebra, say
\JB(S\cup\{z\})\subset A_{sa}
for a C*-algebra
A
. Another two-step invocation of
Lemma

4.2
shows that since
x
and
y
operator commute in
\JB(S)
, these elements commute in
A
, hence
x
and
y
operator commute in
\JB(S\cup\{z\})
. We conclude that
T_{x}(T_{y}(z))=T_{y}(T_{x}(z))
. Since
z\in J
was arbitrary,
T_{x}\circ T_{y}=T_{y}\circ T_{x}
, that is,
x
and
y
operator commute in
J
.
∎

Using the same idea and method of proof as
Theorem

3.1
, we offer a generalization of Macdonald’s principle for JB-algebras, which includes as a special case Macdonald’s principle with inverses for a JB-algebra
[
22
, p. 695]
.

Theorem 4.3
.

Let
F(X_{1},\ldots,X_{n},Y_{1},\ldots,Y_{m},Z)
be a real Jordan polynomial of degree
1
in
Z
. Suppose that for each JC-algebra
J
, pairwise operating commuting
n
-tuple
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, pairwise operator commuting
m
-tuple
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
and element
z
in
J
one has
F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m},z)=0
.
Then for each JB-algebra
J
, pairwise operating commuting
n
-tuple
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
, pairwise operator commuting
m
-tuple
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
and element
z
in
J
one has
F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m},z)=0
.

Remarks 4.4
.

(1) In view of
Theorem

4.1
, the operator commutativity assumption on the tuple
(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})
is equivalent to
x_{i}\circ(w\circ x_{j})=(x_{i}\circ w)\circ x_{j}
for all
1\leq i<j\leq n
and
w\in\JB(x_{i},x_{j})
. A similar remark is in order for the tuple
(y_{1},\ldots,y_{m})
.

(2) The above formulation of Macdonald’s principle is purely JB-algebraic, since it asserts that the pertinent identities need only be verified in the restricted functional-analytic class of JC-algebras, in contrast with the larger algebraic class of all special real Jordan algebras.

Proof of
Theorem

4.3
.

By the Gelfand–Naimark theorem for JB-algebras,
Theorem

2.10
, we may assume that
J=\mathbb{A}
. As in the proof of
Theorem

3.1
, the associative Jordan algebra generated by
x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}
(resp. by
y_{1},\ldots,y_{m}
) can be generated by a single element
x\in\mathbb{A}
(resp.
y\in\mathbb{A}
). Let
G_{1},\ldots,G_{n},H_{1},\ldots,H_{m}
be real Jordan polynomials in one indeterminate such that
x_{i}=G_{i}(x)
and
y_{j}=H_{j}(y)
for all
1\leq i\leq n
,
1\leq j\leq m
. Consider the Jordan polynomial in three variables

K(X,Y,Z):=F(G_{1}(X),\ldots,G_{n}(X),H_{1}(Y),\ldots,H_{m}(Y),Z),

which is homogeneous of degree
1
in
Z
. By assumption, each JC-algebra fulfills
K
. By
Proposition

2.12
, each special real Jordan algebra fulfills
K
. By Macdonald’s principle,
K
is fulfilled by each real Jordan algebra. It follows that
F(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n},y_{1},\ldots,y_{m},z)=K(x,y,z)=0
.
∎

Example 4.5
.

We consider the linearization of Glennie’s identity
G_{8}
with respect to
Z
; let

H^{\prime}_{8}(X,Y,Z,W):=\{U_{X}U_{Y}(W),Z,X\circ Y\}+\{U_{X}U_{Y}(Z),W,X\circ Y\}-2U_{X}U_{Y}(\{Z,X\circ Y,W\}).

Let
J
be a JB-algebra and consider four elements
x,y,z,w\in J
. If one of
\{x,y\}
operator commutes with one of
\{z,w\}
, then the following identity is valid:

H^{\prime}_{8}(x,y,z,w)=H^{\prime}_{8}(y,x,z,w).

Indeed, we may assume without loss of generality that
x
and
w
operator commute. We apply
Theorem

4.3
to the Jordan polynomial

F(X_{1},X_{2},Y_{1},Z):=H_{8}^{\prime}(X_{1},Y_{1},Z,X_{2})-H_{8}^{\prime}(Y_{1},X_{1},Z,X_{2}),

which is satisfied by all JC-algebras because it is the linearization of Glennie’s identity
G_{8}
with respect to
Z
. Since
x
and
w
operator commute in
J
, we find that

H^{\prime}_{8}(x,y,z,w)-H_{8}^{\prime}(y,x,z,w)=F(x,w,y,z)=0.

Many more examples are produced via the same method of linearizing a Jordan polynomial satisfied by all JC-algebras with respect to one or more of its variables. For example, one could linearize
G_{8}
(or
G_{9}
) also with respect to
X
or
Y
.

5
The free JB-algebra generated by two projections

In this section we use the Shirshov–Cohn theorem to prove the existence and give an explicit description of the free unital JB-algebra generated by two projections, defined as follows.

Definition 5.1
.

Let
n
be a positive integer. A
free unital JB-algebra generated by
n
projections
is the data of a unital JB-algebra
J
and an
n
-tuple
(p_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
of projections in
J
which satisfies the following universal property. For every unital JB-algebra
T
and
n
-tuple
(q_{i})_{i=1}^{n}
of projections in
T
there exists a unique Jordan homomorphism
\phi\colon J\to T
such that
\phi(p_{i})=q_{i}
for every
i\in\{1,2,\ldots,n\}
.

The free unital JB-algebra generated by
n
projections is unique up to a unique isomorphism and will be denoted
\FJP(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
, if it exists. One can show its existence using methods from universal algebra, although this falls outside the scope of this article.

Raeburn and Sinclair have given an explicit description of the free unital C*-algebra generated by two projections. In conjuction with the Shirshov–Cohn theorem, their result yields the following explicit description of
\FJP(p_{1},p_{2})
.

Theorem 5.2
.

The free unital JB-algebra generated by two projections exists and is isomorphic to the JC-algebra

J:=\{f\in C([0,1],M_{2}(\mathbb{R})_{sa}):f(0),f(1)\text{ are diagonal}\},

(15)

via an isomorphism carrying the generating projections into the functions

p_{1}(t):=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad p_{2}(t):=\begin{pmatrix}t&\sqrt{t(1-t)}\\
\sqrt{t(1-t)}&1-t\end{pmatrix}.

(16)

Proof.

Raeburn and Sinclair show in
[
27
]
that the free unital C*-algebra
C^{*}(p_{1},p_{2})
generated
by two projections
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
may be identified with

A:=\{f\in C([0,1],M_{2}(\mathbb{C})):f(0),f(1)\text{ are diagonal}\}

such that the generating projections are given by (
16
). Let
J^{\prime}
be the JB-subalgebra of
A_{sa}
generated by
1
,
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
. Let us first show that the triple
(J^{\prime},p_{1},p_{2})
has the desired universal property. Let
T
be a unital JB-algebra with two projections
q_{1},q_{2}\in T
. It is to be shown that there exists a unique Jordan homomorphism
\phi\colon J^{\prime}\to T
such that
\phi(p_{i})=q_{i}
for
i=1,2
.

Denote by
T^{\prime}
the unital JB-subalgebra of
T
generated by
q_{1}
and
q_{2}
. By the Shirshov–Cohn theorem
T^{\prime}
is a JC-algebra, so
T^{\prime}
may be embedded into
B_{sa}
for a unital C*-algebra
B
. The universal property of
A=C^{*}(p_{1},p_{2})
yields a unital
*
-homomorphism
\tilde{\phi}\colon A\to B
such that
\tilde{\phi}(p_{1})=q_{1}
and
\tilde{\phi}(p_{2})=q_{2}
. Then the
*
-homomorphism
\tilde{\phi}
induces a Jordan homomorphism
\tilde{\phi}_{sa}\colon A_{sa}\to B_{sa}
. Now
\tilde{\phi}_{sa}^{-1}(T^{\prime})
is a JB-subalgebra of
A_{sa}
containing
1
,
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
, so that
J^{\prime}\subset\tilde{\phi}_{sa}^{-1}(T^{\prime})
and
\tilde{\phi}_{sa}(J^{\prime})\subset T^{\prime}\subset T
. By restriction we obtain a unital Jordan homomorphism

\phi=\tilde{\phi}|_{J^{\prime}}\colon J^{\prime}\to T
with
\phi(p_{i})=q_{i}
for
i=1,2
. It is unique since
J^{\prime}
is generated by
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
. Therefore,
J^{\prime}
has the required universal property.

We finish by showing that
J^{\prime}=J
, i.e.
J
equals the JB-subalgebra of
A_{sa}
generated by
1
,
p_{1}
and
p_{2}
. The containment
J^{\prime}\subset J
is clear. Towards showing
J\subset J^{\prime}
, note that

(U_{p_{1}}p_{2})(t)=\begin{pmatrix}t&0\\
0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad(U_{1-p_{1}}p_{2})(t)=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\
0&1-t\end{pmatrix}.

(17)

By the Stone–Weierstrass theorem the space of real polynomials in
1
and
t
is dense in
C([0,1],\mathbb{R})
. It follows that

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}f&0\\
0&g\end{pmatrix}:f,g\in C([0,1],\mathbb{R})\right\}\subset J^{\prime}.

This inclusion and
p_{2}\in J^{\prime}
yield that the set

L:=\left\{h\in C([0,1],\mathbb{R}):\begin{pmatrix}0&h\\
h&0\end{pmatrix}\in J^{\prime}\right\}

is a closed ideal of
C([0,1],\mathbb{R})
containing the function
h_{0}(t):=\sqrt{t(1-t)}
. It follows from the Stone–Weierstrass theorem that the inclusion
L\subset\{h\in C([0,1],\mathbb{R}):h(0)=h(1)=0\}
is an equality. This establishes that
J\subset J^{\prime}
, hence
J=J^{\prime}
as remained to be shown.
∎

6
JB-algebras generated by two projections and an element are special

The Shirshov–Cohn theorem asserts that a JB- or JBW-algebra is special if it can be generated by
two elements. Somewhat surprisingly, a generating set consisting of
three
projections also forces speciality. Similar to
Theorem

3.1
, we will prove this result as a consequence of the fact that the Albert algebra cannot be generated by three projections. We prove this fact using the Peirce decomposition discussed in
Section

2.1
and the following lemma.

Lemma 6.1
.

Let
e
and
f
be orthogonal projections in a JB-algebra
J
. Let
x\in J
be such that
e\circ x=f\circ x=\frac{1}{2}x
. Then
x^{2}=U_{e}(x^{2})+U_{f}(x^{2})
holds with
\lVert U_{e}(x^{2})\rVert=\lVert U_{f}(x^{2})\rVert=\lVert x\rVert^{2}
.

Proof.

Working in the JB-subalgebra
U_{e+f}(J)
, which contains
e
,
f
and
x
, we may assume that
e+f=1
. The Peirce multiplication rule (
4
) gives that
x^{2}=U_{e}(x^{2})+U_{f}(x^{2})\in U_{e}(J)+U_{f}(J)
. Using that
x^{2}
and
f=1-e
operator commute, we obtain

U_{x}(e)=2x\circ(x\circ e)-x^{2}\circ e=2x\circ\tfrac{1}{2}x-x^{2}\circ e=x^{2}\circ(1-e)=x^{2}\circ f=U_{f}(x^{2}).

According to
[
4
, Lemma 1.30]
for all
a,b\in J
it holds that
\lVert U_{a}(b^{2})\rVert=\lVert U_{b}(a^{2})\rVert
. Taking
a=x
and
b=e=e^{2}
yields

\lVert U_{e}(x^{2})\rVert=\lVert U_{x}(e)\rVert=\lVert U_{f}(x^{2})\rVert.

Because
U_{e}(J)+U_{f}(J)
is a direct sum of JB-algebras, we conclude that

\lVert x\rVert^{2}=\lVert x^{2}\rVert=\max\{\lVert U_{e}(x^{2})\rVert,\lVert U_{f}(x^{2})\rVert\}=\lVert U_{e}(x^{2})\rVert=\lVert U_{f}(x^{2})\rVert.

∎

Theorem 6.2
.

The Albert algebra
M_{3}(\mathbb{O})_{sa}
cannot be generated by two projections, a third element and the unit element.

Proof.

Let
a,p,q\in\mathbb{A}:=M_{3}(\mathbb{O})_{sa}
and suppose that
p
and
q
are projections. We shall prove that
\JB(1,a,p,q)\subsetneq\mathbb{A}
. By the spectral theorem
[
9
, Thm. III.1.1]
there exists a Jordan frame
\underline{e}=(e_{1},e_{2},e_{3})
and
\lambda_{1},\lambda_{2},\lambda_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
such that
a=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\lambda_{i}e_{i}
. Consider the Peirce decomposition (
3
) of
p
with respect to
\underline{e}
, say

p=\mu_{1}e_{1}+\mu_{2}e_{2}+\mu_{3}e_{3}+b_{12}+b_{23}+b_{13},

with
\mu_{1},\mu_{2},\mu_{3}\in\mathbb{R}
and
b_{rs}\in\mathbb{A}_{rs}(\underline{e}):=\{x\in\mathbb{A}:e_{r}\circ x=e_{s}\circ x=\frac{1}{2}x\}
for
1\leq r<s\leq 3.
After renumbering, we may assume that

b_{13}=0\text{ or }b_{12},b_{23}\neq 0.

We can find
s_{12}\in\mathbb{A}_{12}(\underline{e})
such that
\lVert s_{12}\rVert=1
and
b_{12}=\lVert b_{12}\rVert s_{12}
: if
b_{12}\neq 0
take
s_{12}=\lVert b_{12}\rVert^{-1}b_{12}
; if
b_{12}=0
choose an arbitrary
s_{12}\in\mathbb{A}_{12}(\underline{e})
of norm
1
. Then
s_{12}^{2}\in\mathbb{A}_{11}(\underline{e})+\mathbb{A}_{22}(\underline{e})=\mathbb{R}e_{1}+\mathbb{R}e_{2}
according to (
4
), that is,
s_{12}^{2}=\nu_{1}e_{1}+\nu_{2}e_{2}
for certain
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\in\mathbb{R}
. Since
s_{12}^{2}\geq 0
we have
\nu_{1},\nu_{2}\geq 0
. On the other hand,
Lemma

6.1
gives
|\nu_{1}|=|\nu_{2}|=\lVert s_{12}\rVert^{2}=1.
We arrive at
\nu_{1}=\nu_{2}=1
, i.e.
s_{12}^{2}=e_{1}+e_{2}
. Similarly, there exists
s_{23}\in\mathbb{A}_{23}(\underline{e})
such that
b_{23}=\lVert b_{23}\rVert s_{23}
and
s_{23}^{2}=e_{2}+e_{3}
.

Let
(E_{rs})_{r,s=1}^{3}
be the standard matrix units of
M_{3}(\mathbb{R})
. According to
[
24
, Prop. 17.1.1]
, there exists a Jordan automorphism
\phi\colon\mathbb{A}\to\mathbb{A}
such that
\phi(e_{h})=E_{hh}
for
1\leq h\leq 3
as well as
\phi(s_{12})=E_{12}+E_{21}
and
\phi(s_{23})=E_{23}+E_{32}
. We find that

\phi(a)=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_{1}&0&0\\
0&\lambda_{2}&0\\
0&0&\lambda_{3}\end{pmatrix},\quad\phi(p)=\begin{pmatrix}\mu_{1}&\beta_{12}&\beta_{13}\\
\beta_{12}&\mu_{2}&\beta_{23}\\
\beta_{13}^{*}&\beta_{23}&\mu_{3}\end{pmatrix},

with
\beta_{12}=\lVert b_{12}\rVert
,
\beta_{23}=\lVert b_{23}\rVert\in\mathbb{R}
and
\beta_{13}\in\mathbb{O}
. We claim that
\beta_{13}\in\mathbb{R}
. If
\beta_{13}=0
this is clear, and otherwise by assumption
\beta_{12},\beta_{23}\neq 0
. Comparing the
(1,3)
entries in
\phi(p)=\phi(p^{2})=\phi(p)^{2}
gives that

\beta_{13}=\mu_{1}\beta_{13}+\beta_{12}\beta_{23}+\beta_{13}\mu_{3}=\beta_{12}\beta_{23}+(\mu_{1}+\mu_{3})\beta_{13}.

From
(1-\mu_{1}-\mu_{3})\beta_{13}=\beta_{12}\beta_{23}\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
we infer that
\beta_{13}\in\mathbb{R}
. Therefore, we have

\JB(1,\phi(a),\phi(p))\subset M_{3}(\mathbb{R})_{sa}.

We now consider the projection
\phi(q)
. If
\rk(q)\geq 2
, then on replacing
q
by
1-q
, which has rank
\rk(1-q)=3-\rk(q)
, we may assume that
\rk(\phi(q))=\rk(q)\leq 1
. Then
\phi(q)
is an atom or zero. According to
[
35
, Prop. A.16]
there exists an associative subalgebra
\mathbb{K}\subsetneq\mathbb{O}
such that
\phi(q)\in M_{3}(\mathbb{K})_{sa}
. It follows that

\phi(\JB(1,a,p,q))\subset\JB(M_{3}(\mathbb{R})_{sa}\cup\{\phi(q)\})\subset M_{3}(\mathbb{K})_{sa}\subsetneq M_{3}(\mathbb{O})_{sa}=\mathbb{A}.

Applying
\phi^{-1}
, we conclude that
\JB(1,a,p,q)\subsetneq\mathbb{A}
, as desired.
∎

The algebraic statement about the Albert algebra in
Theorem

6.2
implies the following speciality result for JB-algebras. Note that Albert–Paige showed in
[
1
, Cor. 2]
that the exceptional Albert algebra
\mathbb{A}
can be generated by three elements.

Theorem 6.3
.

Let
A
be a JB-algebra which can be generated as a JB-algebra by two projections, a third element and possibly the unit element. Then
A
is a JC-algebra.

Proof.

Suppose first that
A
is unital. Let
a,p,q\in A
be elements with
p=p^{2}
and
q=q^{2}
such that
A=\JB(1,a,p,q)
. If
A
is not a JC-algebra, then by
Theorem

2.11
(4) there exists a surjective unital Jordan homomorphism
\phi\colon A\to\mathbb{A}
onto the Albert algebra. Note that
\phi(p)
and
\phi(q)
are projections in
\mathbb{A}
. Now
\mathbb{A}=\phi(A)
is generated by
\{1,\phi(a),\phi(p),\phi(q)\}
, which is absurd by
Theorem

6.2
. Therefore,
A
is a JC-algebra. The non-unital case is similar.
∎

Corollary 6.4
.

Let
A
be a JBW-algebra which can be generated as a JBW-algebra by two projections, a third element and possibly the unit element. Then
A
is a JW-algebra.

Proof.

The corollary is deduced from the preceding result as in
Corollary

3.2
.
∎

Lemma 6.5
.

The Albert algebra
M_{3}(\mathbb{O})_{sa}
can be generated by four atoms.

Proof.

According to
[
35
, Prop. A.16]
, the atoms in
M_{3}(\mathbb{O})_{sa}
are of the form

p:=\begin{pmatrix}\lVert x_{1}\rVert^{2}&x_{1}x_{2}^{*}&x_{1}x_{3}^{*}\\
x_{2}x_{1}^{*}&\lVert x_{2}\rVert^{2}&x_{2}x_{3}^{*}\\
x_{3}x_{1}^{*}&x_{3}x_{2}^{*}&\lVert x_{3}\rVert^{2}\end{pmatrix}

where
x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\in\mathbb{O}
associate, that is,
(x_{1}x_{2})x_{3}=x_{1}(x_{2}x_{3})
and
\lVert x_{1}\rVert^{2}+\lVert x_{2}\rVert^{2}+\lVert x_{3}\rVert^{2}=1
.
Set

q_{1}:=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\
0&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad q_{2}:=\frac{1}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1&0\\
1&1&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad q_{3}:=\frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix}1&-i&-i\\
i&1&1\\
i&1&1\end{pmatrix},\quad q_{4}:=\frac{1}{3}\begin{pmatrix}1&-j&-l\\
j&1&-jl\\
l&jl&1\end{pmatrix}.

We will prove that
\{q_{1},q_{2},q_{3},q_{4}\}
is a generating set for
M_{3}(\mathbb{O})_{sa}
. Let
J:=\JB(q_{1},q_{2},q_{3},q_{4})
.

First we show that
\{E_{11},E_{22},E_{33}\}\subset J
. Indeed,
E_{11}=q_{1}\in J
, hence
E_{22}=P_{0}(E_{11})(2q_{2})\in J
, which gives
E_{33}=P_{0}(E_{11}+E_{22})(3q_{3})\in J
.

Therefore,
J
contains each off-diagonal Peirce component of
q_{h}\in J
with respect to every pair of projections among
E_{11},E_{22},E_{33}
, which yields

\left\{\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&-i&0\\
i&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&-j&0\\
j&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-l\\
0&0&0\\
l&0&0\end{pmatrix}\right\}\subset J.

Then
J
contains the product of the last two matrices, so

\begin{pmatrix}0&-l&0\\
l&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}=2\begin{pmatrix}0&0&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&0\end{pmatrix}\circ\begin{pmatrix}0&0&-l\\
0&0&0\\
l&0&0\end{pmatrix}\in J.

We conclude that
J
contains the following generating set of the Albert algebra:

\left\{E_{11},E_{22},E_{33},\begin{pmatrix}0&1&0\\
1&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&-i&0\\
i&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&-j&0\\
j&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix},\begin{pmatrix}0&-l&0\\
l&0&0\\
0&0&0\end{pmatrix}\right\}\subset J,

whence
J=M_{3}(\mathbb{O})_{sa}
.
∎

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
n\geq 1
. The free unital JB-algebra generated by
n
-projections
\FJ(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
is a JC-algebra if and only if
n\leq 3
.

Proof.

If
n\leq 3
, then
\FJ(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
is generated as a unital JB-algebra by at most three projections
p_{1},\ldots,p_{n}
and
1
, hence is a JC-algebra by
Theorem

6.3
.

Now let
n\geq 4
. By
Lemma

6.5
, we can choose four atomic projections
q_{1},\ldots,q_{4}
which generate
\mathbb{A}
. By the universal property of
\FJ(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
there exists a unique unital Jordan homomorphism
\phi\colon\FJ(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})\to\mathbb{A}
such that
\phi(p_{h})=q_{h}
for
1\leq h\leq 4
and
\phi(p_{h})=0
for
h>4
. Then
\phi
is a surjective homomorphism onto the Albert algebra, hence
\FJ(p_{1},\ldots,p_{n})
is non-special by
Theorem

2.11
.
∎

Remark 6.7
.

Write
C^{*}(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})
for the free unital C*-algebra generated by three projections. As in the proof of
Theorem

5.2
,
Theorem

6.6
may be used to show that the free unital JB-algebra
\FJP(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})
generated by three projections can be constructed as the unital JB-subalgebra of
C^{*}(p_{1},p_{2},p_{3})_{sa}
generated by
p_{1}
,
p_{2}
and
p_{3}
.
