Perspectives and experiences of ICU healthcare professionals on conscious ICU patients’ involvement in shared decision-making: a qualitative study
Authors: Jiayu Zhang, Yuling Lei, Qi Zhou, Chuchu Zhang, Weiling Jiang, Zhenzhen Huang, Na Zhang, Xiaoyan Gong, Yiyu Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveTo explore intensive care unit (ICU) healthcare professionals’ perceptions and experiences of shared decision-making with conscious patients.DesignA qualitative descriptive study using semistructured interviews.SettingThree ICUs at a Joint Commission International-accredited hospital in Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, China.Participants16 ICU healthcare professionals (9 females, 7 males; aged 23–45 years) were interviewed between September and November 2024.ResultsWe identified 10 subthemes that were classified into three themes: (a) conscious but not ready: unstable decision-making ability, insufficient emotional readiness and limited cognitive evaluability; (b) the roles of doctors and nurses in shared decision-making (SDM): physicians as advisors, not decision-makers, nurses’ limited role in SDM and fragmented team communication hinders information flow and (c) the double-edged sword of patient participation: protection or blame, collaboration or burden, hope or harm and being caught between values.ConclusionsICU healthcare professionals acknowledge the value of involving conscious patients in SDM but also recognise its complexity. Challenges include assessing patients’ fluctuating decision-making capacity, poor team communication and the potential for both benefit and harm. These findings highlight the need for clearer professional roles and more structured support.Keywords: QUALITATIVE RESEARCH, Intensive Care Units, Decision Making

Introduction

In recent years, shared decision-making (SDM) has gained increasing attention in critical care. SDM is a collaborative process in which patients, surrogates and clinicians make healthcare decisions together, integrating medical evidence with individual values.1 2 In the intensive care unit (ICU), SDM is widely promoted as a standard of care, with benefits including improved family satisfaction, reduced psychological distress, shorter ICU stays and enhanced interprofessional collaboration.3 However, SDM remains challenging in critically ill patients, as decisions are often made by surrogates or through advance directives rather than by the patients themselves.4,7

As sedation strategies shift from deep to light levels, a growing number of ICU patients remain conscious and potentially capable of participating in clinical decisions.8 When patients express their preferences, resulting decisions are more likely aligned with their values, reducing the emotional burden on families.9 Conversely, patients excluded from medical decisions tend to experience greater regret and lower satisfaction.10 Although consciousness alone does not guarantee decision-making capacity, it allows direct engagement with patients about their values and preferences. Engaging conscious ICU patients in decision-making reinforces their dignity and respects patient autonomy.11

Despite the growing emphasis on SDM in ICU, existing research primarily focuses on processes involving clinicians and relatives or multidisciplinary teams, with limited attention to the direct involvement of conscious patients. Studies indicate that SDM between clinicians and relatives often reflects the values of relatives rather than patients and may lead to decision dilemmas and subsequent regret among relatives.12 One qualitative study13 revealed physicians’ dilemmas, including uncertainty about long-term prognosis, time constraints, liability pressure and fear of losing control. It also identified unmet needs among patients and families, such as being excluded from decision-making and lacking prognostic information, as well as the potential role of nurses in integrating non-clinical information and acting as communication bridges. A systematic review further shows that interprofessional SDM can improve processes and outcomes and advocates for structured, team-based approaches.14 However, these studies have paid insufficient attention to how conscious ICU patients participate in SDM and how healthcare professionals perceive this process.

Patient involvement also raises ethical complexities. Although autonomy is a fundamental principle in medical ethics,15 relational autonomy emphasises that decision-making is shaped not only by individual preferences but also by relationships with family members and clinicians, as well as by the broader care environment.16 Patients can express personal values that clinicians cannot substitute,17 yet emphasising autonomy without considering vulnerability may be ethically problematic—especially when poor outcomes are blamed solely on patient decisions.18 In critical situations, pressuring patients into decision-making can even cause psychological harm.19 20 Accurately assessing decision-making capacity remains challenging: underestimation risks paternalism, while overestimation may lead to inappropriate autonomy.20 Thus, clinicians bear an ethical responsibility to offer clear, transparent information and guidance.

Despite growing recognition of these ethical issues, most existing studies on conscious ICU patients have focused primarily on subjective experiences such as sleep or anxiety.21,23 The actual scope and modes of patient involvement in ICU decision-making, along with influencing factors, remain unclear.24 Furthermore, changes in sedation practices affect clinicians’ approaches to decision-making, highlighting the need to understand clinicians’ perceptions and responses. To address this gap, this study aimed to explore ICU healthcare professionals’ in-depth perceptions and experiences of involving conscious patients in SDM, using a qualitative descriptive approach.

Methods

Design

A qualitative descriptive study design was used. The qualitative descriptive methodology can comprehensively describe an unknown phenomenon and establish a structured understanding. This study adopts a qualitative descriptive methodology to explore and understand the perspectives of ICU healthcare workers regarding the involvement of conscious, decision-capable patients in SDM within the ICU. The aim is to gain a deeper understanding of the factors that influence ICU patients’ participation in SDM. Semistructured interviews were conducted with healthcare workers from three ICUs at a Joint Commission International (JCI)-accredited hospital in Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, China, between September and November. To ensure quality and transparency in reporting (online supplemental appendix 1), this study followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research (SRQR) Checklist.

Participants

Using purposive sampling, physicians and nurses from three adult ICUs were recruited as research participants. This sampling strategy was used to achieve maximum variation in profession, years of ICU experience, gender and ICU setting, thereby capturing diverse perspectives on conscious ICU patients’ involvement in SDM. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) holding a valid physician or nurse practice certificate; (2) being involved in bedside care and decision-making; (3) having at least 1 year of clinical work experience and (4) voluntarily agreeing to participate and providing written informed consent before the interview. The exclusion criteria included medical staff who had left frontline clinical positions, intern medical staff and staff from auxiliary departments, such as clinical laboratory and radiology departments.

Participants were recruited with the assistance of ICU head nurses. Recruitment information was shared in ICU communication groups, and interested staff contacted the researchers or were contacted by the researchers for further explanation of the study. After confirming voluntary participation, the interview time and location were arranged, and participants completed the research information form and informed consent form before the interview.

The research team determined the sample size based on the principle of code saturation. After interviewing and analysing data from 16 medical staff, they reached a consensus that the data were saturated, and subsequently ceased data collection.

Setting

This study was conducted in the adult general ICU of a JCI-accredited general hospital in eastern China, where the majority of the population is Han Chinese. The hospital has a total capacity of 6500 beds, including 185 ICU beds, and admits approximately 3000 ICU patients annually. It follows a patient-centred philosophy of care that emphasises sincerity, trust and compassion. The service model is guided by the principle of ‘treating the patient, not just the disease’. Healthcare staff routinely hold family meetings with patients and their relatives.

Data collection

The semistructured interview guide was developed based on a review of existing literature and was reviewed by experts in the field of intensive care. Initially, 20 medical staff were recruited. However, due to scheduling conflicts, only 16 medical staff (7 doctors and 9 nurses) ultimately participated in the interviews. The research team consisted of nine members, including a nursing professor, a nursing master’s supervisor, a nursing doctoral candidate and six nursing master’s students. The first author, a female student pursuing a nursing master’s degree, had 2 years of ICU work experience and previous experience conducting qualitative interviews. The first author conducted in-depth, face-to-face, semistructured interviews with each participant in a doctor’s office. Each interview was conducted one-on-one and involved only the first author and one participant, who was either a physician or a nurse. The semistructured interview guide was developed based on the study aim and discussions within the research team. The guide focused on healthcare professionals’ understanding and experiences of SDM involving conscious ICU patients. Main topics included perceptions of SDM in the ICU context, patient participation in treatment decisions, professional roles and responsibilities, family involvement, perceived benefits and challenges and communication within the ICU team. Follow-up questions were used to encourage participants to provide specific examples and elaborate on their experiences. Before the formal interviews, pilot interviews were conducted with one nurse and one physician to refine and finalise the interview guide (online supplemental appendix 2).

All participants provided written informed consent before the interviews. Immediately before each interview began, the researcher verbally reconfirmed participants’ willingness to participate and ensured that they felt comfortable proceeding. With the participants’ consent, each interview was audio-recorded. During the interviews, the interviewer asked questions in Mandarin and encouraged participants to express their thoughts. No participants withdrew from the interviews and none of the interviews were repetitive. Participants were numbered from 1 to 16 to maintain anonymity. The duration of the interviews ranged from 34 to 73 min. The interviewer took on-site notes and recorded participants’ facial expressions and body language. Within 24 hours after the interviews, the recordings were transcribed into Mandarin and reflective notes were written.

Data analysis

The first author transcribed the interview recordings into Chinese within 24 hours and the second author verified the accuracy of the transcripts against the audio recordings. MAXQDA 2020 was used by the first author to manage and analyse the data, following the thematic analysis approach outlined by Braun and Clarke. Thematic analysis is a flexible and widely used approach in qualitative data analysis, making it appropriate for identifying themes in this study. The first author carefully reviewed the Chinese interview transcripts and conducted coding in Chinese to preserve the original meanings expressed by participants. After coding, the identified themes were discussed with the research team until consensus was reached. Once Chinese coding and theme development had been finalised, selected quotations, codes and themes were translated into English during the manuscript-writing stage.

Rigour

This study follows established guidelines for qualitative research reporting, with rigour reflected in four key dimensions: credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability. The first author, a master’s student in nursing with 2 years of prior ICU clinical experience, conducted the interviews. This background helped her build rapport with participants and understand the clinical context, but it also carried the possibility of preconceptions about team communication, professional roles and patient participation. To support reflexivity, the first author kept a reflective diary throughout the research process to document her thoughts, assumptions and emotional responses to the data. There was no prior relationship between the first author and the participants. The second author verified the accuracy of the transcripts against the audio recordings, and the research team jointly reviewed the procedures for data collection, transcription, coding and theme development. Collaborative discussions and theme revisions helped critically examine the first author’s interpretations and reduce potential bias, thereby enhancing confirmability. Transferability was addressed by providing a detailed description of the research setting. Dependability was supported through thorough documentation of all stages of the research, including the interview guide, interview times and locations and the transcription process.

Theme 1: conscious but not ready

This study indicates that healthcare professionals generally value the involvement and perspectives of conscious patients in treatment decisions.

Because he is the one who is sick, he should have the right to know what treatment options are available and the pros and cons of each option. (Nurse 2)

The patient’s own opinion is very important. (Doctor 4)

However, this value is tempered by clinical realities, which suggest that consciousness alone is insufficient for meaningful participation.

In clinical practice, they often believe that being conscious does not necessarily mean that patients are fully prepared to engage in decision-making. Specifically, some patients are perceived to have unstable decision-making ability, along with limitations in psychological adjustment and cognitive comprehension.

Unstable decision-making ability

Both doctors and nurses expressed concern about the stability of decision-making abilities in conscious patients. What makes decision-making by conscious ICU patients unique is that their consciousness may be temporary, and no one can be certain how long they will remain conscious and capable of making decisions.

Many ICU patients may be conscious at the moment, but could lose consciousness in the very next second. (Doctor 1)

In the early stages, patients may be allowed to sign themselves, but in the later stages, if their condition changes, they will no longer be able to sign. (Doctor 3)

As advance directives are not yet widely implemented in China, healthcare professionals must rely on patients or their families to understand treatment preferences. Some express concern that if a patient loses decision-making capacity, delays may occur while awaiting temporary family authorisation.

When the patient’s condition changes, it is certainly too late for family members to rush over from far away and grant authorization. In fact, this also delays treatment. (Doctor 6)

Emotional readiness for decision-making

Healthcare professionals observed that while some conscious patients appeared alert, they lacked the emotional readiness to participate in medical decision-making. Faced with treatment choices, these patients often struggled to remain calm, showing signs of anxiety, fear or hesitation.

For most patients, they are not mentally prepared. (Doctor 2)

In an unfamiliar environment, the patient’s first reaction is often fear, especially when they are surrounded by numerous tubes and frequent alarm sounds from medical equipment. (Doctor 5)

This environmental stress, combined with the weight of medical decisions, often intensified patients’ emotional distress. Beyond fear of the unfamiliar setting, healthcare professionals also reported that some patients felt anxious about the consequences of their choices.

They fear a poor prognosis, yet the decision was made by themselves. (Nurse 3)

Limited cognitive evaluability

Healthcare professionals emphasised that patients’ ability to participate in SDM is closely tied to how well they understand their illness. In their experience, this understanding is strongly influenced by a patient’s level of education and access to general health information.

I believe the most important factor influencing a conscious patient’s decision-making is their own understanding of their illness. (Nurse 1)

They pointed out that explaining medical information to patients with little or no formal education can be particularly challenging in the ICU setting.

A patient’s level of education matters—if you try to explain treatment steps to someone who has never received formal education, it can be very difficult for them to understand. (Nurse 6)

Even for patients with some level of formal education, understanding medical issues may still be limited due to the specialised nature of medical knowledge.

After all, patients are not medical professionals, and even those with medical knowledge have a limited understanding of their own physical condition or illness. (Doctor 7)

In addition, professionals reflected that patients’ comprehension of illness and treatment is also shaped by broader societal exposure to health information, including media and physician-led education.

Although public awareness of medical issues has improved compared to the past, there is still a general lack of widespread understanding of medical knowledge in society. (Doctor 2)

Professionals further observed that being conscious does not necessarily mean that a patient is capable of making informed medical decisions. In their view, decision-making requires more than awareness—it involves calm thinking, rational judgement and cognitive coherence. However, in clinical practice, accurately assessing whether a patient truly possesses these abilities remains a challenging task.

Although patients may appear to be in a conscious state, their judgement or decision-making ability may still be limited to some extent. (Doctor 3)

A patient may appear conscious, but their thought processes may still differ significantly from those of a healthy individual. (Doctor 4)

Theme 2: the roles of doctors and nurses in SDM

Physicians as advisors, not decision-makers

In ICU SDM, physicians often position themselves as advisors rather than decision-makers. Many emphasise that their primary role is to present treatment options, explain potential risks and benefits and ultimately leave the decision to the patient. This reflects an intention to avoid imposing personal judgements on critically ill patients.

We do not guide decisions; we simply provide them with treatment options that are suitable for his condition based on his medical condition. (Doctor 5)

The final decision will definitely be made by them. We are only providing options. (Doctor 1)

In cases where disagreements arise between patients and their families, medical staff reported that they typically take a non-interventionist stance, leaving the final choice to those directly involved. This approach reflects respect for autonomy and clearly defines the boundaries of clinical responsibility.

However, when it comes to conflicts between patients and their families, we respect their choices. (Doctor 7)

Furthermore, some physicians expressed concern that fully distributing decision-making power among all stakeholders could compromise the efficiency of clinical care.

If everyone has the power to make decisions, the efficiency will be very low. (Doctor 2)

Nurses’ limited role in SDM

In the ICU, nurses are not merely nurses as the ones who interact most frequently with patients, they are also important observers and communicators. However, in practice, they are often marginalised as ‘silent executors’, merely carrying out medical orders with little opportunity to participate in medical decision-making.

In fact, doctors usually make the decisions on their own—they give the medical orders, and I just follow and carry them out. (Nurse 6)

We don’t have a voice in the decision-making process. (Nurse 3)

Nurses feel a lack of involvement in decision-making, partly because they often find it difficult to grasp the medical reasoning behind the complex treatment plans in the ICU.

The doctor makes the treatment plan—I don’t really understand it. (Nurse 2)

In addition, sociocultural biases lead patients and their families to place more trust in doctors while overlooking nurses.

When nurses try to explain the patient’s condition to the patient or their family, they often don’t listen or take it seriously. (Nurse 7)

Compared to nurses, patients tend to trust doctors more. (Nurse 8)

The lack of trust from patients can reduce nurses’ motivation to participate in SDM.

We feel psychological pressure—like the more we say, the more likely we are to make mistakes. (Nurse 3)

When it comes to the patient’s condition, we don’t explain much—it’s always the doctors who handle that part. (Nurse 2)

Fragmented team communication hinders information flow

SDM requires communication within the healthcare team, but in actual ICU practice, doctors and nurses often have different understandings of what constitutes ‘effective communication’.

Doctors may believe they have conveyed the necessary information, while nurses commonly feel that doctors are unwilling to share information proactively or that communication is lacking.

Because nurses are the ones who carry out the work, we definitely need to communicate with them. (Doctor 7)

During morning rounds, I ask the nurses to join us, and we usually communicate with them as well. (Doctor 1)

If we’re going to issue any medical orders, we usually inform the nurses. (Doctor 3)

Basically, it is mostly doctors. I think nurses are rarely involved in communication. For example, for some patients with severe conditions, there may be multidisciplinary team discussions, but nurses are not part of them. (Nurse 1)

Doctors generally don’t communicate much with nurses. (Nurse 7)

Doctors only hand over to other doctors; they don’t hand over to nurses. (Nurse 9)

Nurses expressed that they need communication with doctors to stay informed about changes in the patient’s treatment plan. This helps them feel less passive when communicating with patients.

Actually, I feel that we need doctors to communicate with us; otherwise, we’re left in a very passive position. (Nurse 6)

This mismatch becomes a barrier to information flow and ultimately affects collaboration in team-based decision-making.

If there’s a lack of communication between nurses and doctors, patients may feel that the medical team is not on the same page. For example, a nurse might say something is scheduled for today, but the doctor says it’s not—if this happens repeatedly, it undermines their trust in us. (Nurse 3)

Theme 3: the double-edged sword of patient participation

Protection or blame

Although patient participation is widely advocated as a means to protect patients’ rights and prevent disputes, some healthcare professionals recognise its potential risks: patients may retrospectively shift responsibility and blame the very decisions they were once encouraged to share.

To some extent, clinicians also view transparent communication as a strategy to reduce medicolegal risks by distributing responsibility more evenly.

I believe that ensuring patients have adequate access to information can help prevent doctor-patient conflicts. (Doctor 3)

However, participation does not necessarily translate into shared responsibility. Even when patients are involved in the decision-making process, healthcare professionals often remain the ones held accountable in the event of poor outcomes.

If the patient regrets it years later and says, “you asked me to be part of those decisions, but it should have been you doctors making them. You’re the ones who studied medicine, not me. I might not have made the right choice,” that’s completely possible. (Doctor 4)

If the patient’s expectations aren’t met, they might cause a scene or create trouble. (Nurse 3)

Collaboration or burden

Some healthcare professionals observed that patient participation in SDM is associated with noticeably improved cooperation and treatment adherence.

After the communication, they tend to be more cooperative. (Doctor 7)

Once you’ve explained things clearly and the patient is emotionally stable and cooperative, it takes a lot of pressure off the nurses. (Nurse 1)

In addition to enhancing patients’ cooperation, some healthcare professionals noted that patient participation also contributes to smoother coordination within the medical team, making the overall care process more efficient.

If everyone is well-informed, the subsequent treatment process tends to go more smoothly—including nurses carrying out medical orders and the cooperation of the patient’s family. (Doctor 2)

Although many healthcare professionals recognise that patient participation in SDM can enhance collaboration, some have voiced concerns about the potential increase in workload. Specifically, they noted that explaining complex medical information to patients is often time-consuming and challenging.

To be honest, I find that giving detailed explanations can be quite burdensome—unless the patient has a high level of understanding. (Nurse 2)

If we involve them in the process, I think it will only increase the workload for doctors and nurses. (Nurse 5)

Hope or harm

Some physicians noted that patient participation in the decision-making process helps enhance their understanding of their illness, which in turn helps build confidence in coping with their condition.

We aim to help the patient understand their illness, so they can be aware of their condition. This may give them more confidence. (Doctor 6)

Communicating more with the patient can help reduce their fear. (Doctor 5)

When patients are aware of their condition, it can create a kind of psychological suggestion—they may feel that their illness is so mild that they’ll likely be discharged in a few days. (Doctor 2)

However, many physicians emphasised that psychological resilience plays a vital role in the recovery of critically ill patients. Premature or overly direct disclosure of the diagnosis may weaken the patient’s confidence and will to survive, potentially hindering the effectiveness of treatment.

Many critically ill patients manage to survive wave after wave of setbacks because their mental strength and belief never collapsed. (Doctor 7)

I feel that for severe illnesses, knowing the full condition can be cruel—it may leave the patient counting the days in despair. (Doctor 1)

Therefore, when dealing with conscious but critically ill patients, physicians tend to prioritise offering comfort and encouragement rather than immediately involving them in complex medical decision-making.

When the patient is conscious, we mainly focus on providing comfort. If the condition is very serious, we usually avoid saying anything that might discourage them. (Doctor 7)

Caught between values

Healthcare professionals described decision-making with critically ill patients as a persistent tension between reason and emotion—one that often lacks a clear or satisfactory resolution. They are often caught between honouring the patient’s personal wishes and applying rational medical judgement.

This tension reflects a fundamental conflict between beneficence, understood as clinicians’ duty to preserve life, and respect for patient autonomy, understood as patients’ right to refuse treatment based on their personal values. In the complex ICU context, such conflicts often lack morally straightforward resolutions.

It ultimately depends on what is most important for the patient—whether it is respecting their current subjective wishes, or objectively aiming to extend their life or improve their quality of life. (Nurse 2)

In many cases, healthcare professionals recognise limited clinical benefit in continued treatment, yet patients and families insist on pursuing it aggressively.

We may assess that further treatment holds little value, but the patient—and often the family—insist on aggressive care. (Doctor 7)

Some patients expend significant energy, money, and endure considerable suffering, only for the outcome to remain unchanged in the end. (Doctor 1)

Conversely, there are times when clinicians consider the condition treatable, yet the patient chooses to forgo treatment—often due to financial or personal constraints.

In some cases, even when the prognosis is favorable, patients choose to leave the hospital—often due to financial constraints or other personal reasons. (Doctor 5)

Healthcare professionals also express concern that decisions made under intense pressure may not reflect what the patient truly wants in the long term.

Involving the patient in such extreme situations may not guarantee that they can make a decision they won’t regret later. (Nurse 1)

Discussion

This study aimed to address a critical gap in understanding healthcare professionals’ perceptions of conscious ICU patients’ involvement in SDM. Participants generally acknowledged the ethical importance and practical benefits of patient participation, such as promoting autonomy and enhancing treatment adherence. However, they also expressed several practical concerns related to patients’ physiological condition, cognitive capacity, emotional readiness, and uncertainty about professional boundaries in decision-making. These findings suggest that involving conscious ICU patients in decision-making is not only an ethical ideal but also a clinically and organisationally complex process. Unlike previous studies that have primarily focused on patient experiences or family–clinician SDM, this study offers deeper insight into healthcare professionals’ practical concerns and sheds light on the prevailing decision-making culture in ICU settings, particularly the perceptual gap between physicians and nurses.

Healthcare professionals reported that despite patients being conscious, their decision-making capacity often remained unstable, particularly during fluctuations in clinical condition or episodes of altered consciousness. This aligns with previous research highlighting the unpredictable nature of ICU conditions.25 One qualitative study similarly found that even patients assessed as conscious and capable of expressing preferences may still experience delirium, confusion, or impaired mental status.26 Beyond acute changes, the overall severity of illness also influenced patient involvement, consistent with studies suggesting that patient participation is more closely related to physical condition than personal willingness.27

Emotional readiness also appeared to be an important condition for meaningful participation. This finding is consistent with patient-perspective research, which reported that negative emotions contributed to conscious ICU patients’ reluctance to engage in decision-making.28 Negative emotional states may weaken patients’ or surrogate decision-makers’ ability to understand medical information, thereby reducing decision quality.29 Similarly, a Swedish study showed that patients felt more included when healthcare professionals explained the situation, guided the process, and involved them with trust and respect.30 Together, these findings suggest that supporting ICU patients’ participation requires more than assessing cognitive capacity; clinicians also need to provide emotional support and a sense of being safely guided.

In addition to emotional factors, patients’ cognitive understanding also affected their ability to participate meaningfully in SDM. The issue was not simply whether patients were awake, but whether they could understand illness-related information, weigh risks and benefits, and express relatively stable preferences under stress and uncertainty. Even when conscious, patients may have difficulty processing information or expressing consistent preferences because of cognitive fluctuations or the broader stress of critical illness.26 27 This finding suggests that consciousness alone does not necessarily indicate adequate decisional capacity.31 32 Similar concerns have been reported by nurses, who were sometimes unsure whether patients had truly understood the information provided.33 Because decisional capacity is still assessed mainly through physicians’ clinical judgement, practical assessment methods suited to the ICU context may be needed.34,36

Our study found that physicians preferred to act as advisors rather than decision-makers, typically providing patients with options from which they could choose. Previous research has indicated that physicians often assume a more dominant role, initiating and leading family meetings, delivering medical information, interpreting clinical conditions, and communicating potential risks. Although family members are encouraged to share their perspectives, the scope of available options and decision boundaries are usually predefined by physicians. 37In our study, ICU nurses perceived themselves mainly as implementers of medical orders and reported feeling marginalised during the decision-making process. This perception aligns with previous research where some nurses assert authority when faced with patient dissent,26 while others prefer negotiating roles emphasising active listening and humanistic care.25 38 Nurses’ perceived marginalisation may partly stem from traditional hierarchical dynamics and limited patient recognition and trust, reducing their engagement in SDM.39 40

An important finding of this study was the perceptual gap between physicians and nurses regarding communication effectiveness in the ICU. This gap may persist because physicians and nurses differ in their roles, clinical priorities, professional power positions, and understandings of where and how decisions are made. A study conducted in Australia similarly showed that nurses’ contributions to clinical decision-making may be constrained by professional hierarchies and role expectations, despite their continuous bedside knowledge of patients’ conditions.41 Therefore, improving SDM requires not only more frequent communication but also structured interdisciplinary mechanisms that formally include nurses’ bedside perspectives.41

Patient participation in SDM may have a dual effect on ICU workflow. On the one hand, adequate explanation and information sharing can improve patient cooperation, family understanding, and team coordination. On the other hand, meaningful participation requires repeated, individualised communication, which can be difficult to sustain in the fast-paced ICU environment. This finding aligns with previous research indicating that communication with patients is often complex and time-consuming, and that clinicians may sometimes reduce communication to manage workload pressures.42 Given that ICU clinicians and nurses already face heavy workloads,41 43 44 promoting patient participation should not simply mean increasing the frequency of communication. Future practice may therefore require more efficient decision-support strategies that support patient-centred care while remaining feasible within ICU workflows.

These tensions may be particularly shaped by the Chinese sociocultural context. In China, family-based decision-making often plays a central role in serious illness, and families may filter or withhold poor prognostic information to protect patients’ emotional well-being and preserve hope.45 As a result, clinicians must navigate not only the tension between medical judgement and patient autonomy, but also family expectations, culturally embedded norms of protective disclosure, and the risk of conflict.46 This differs somewhat from findings in a recent UK qualitative study on treatment escalation planning, where tensions were more often described as arising between professional medical judgement and patients’ or families’ culturally and religiously informed values.47 In the Chinese ICU context, by contrast, clinicians may be positioned within a more complex triadic relationship among patients, families, and healthcare professionals. This context may amplify the tensions observed in theme 3: ‘being caught between values’ reflects the need to balance patients’ potentially undisclosed preferences, families’ protective intentions, and clinicians’ professional judgement, while the ‘hope or harm’ dilemma becomes particularly salient when direct disclosure of a poor prognosis is perceived as potentially harmful rather than simply transparent.

ICU decision-making is complex because physicians, nurses, patients, and families may hold different priorities and interpretations of the same clinical situation. While clinicians often emphasise clinical evidence and prognosis, patients and families may place greater importance on clear explanations, relief of suffering, treatment effectiveness or quality of life.48,50 Disagreements may also arise from different understandings of prognosis or overly optimistic expectations about treatment outcomes.51 52 These situations rarely have a single correct answer and may place healthcare professionals in ethically and emotionally challenging positions.53 Therefore, these findings reinforce the need for value-sensitive approaches that support communication, clarify professional roles, and acknowledge the ethical complexity of ICU SDM.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size of healthcare professionals recruited from the ICUs was small. However, given the use of purposive sampling and the richness of the data collected, no new codes were generated during the coding process, suggesting that the sample size was sufficient for the purpose of this qualitative study. Second, this study was conducted in a single high-resource, JCI-accredited hospital in East China, which may limit the transferability of the findings to other settings, such as non-accredited, rural or lower-resource hospitals with different organisational cultures, team dynamics and patient populations. Third, this study explored only the perspectives of healthcare professionals; the voices of patients and family members were not included, which may have omitted important dimensions of SDM in ICU settings. Finally, as with qualitative interview research more broadly, the findings may be subject to social desirability bias, as participants may have provided responses they perceived as professionally or socially acceptable rather than fully disclosing challenging or controversial practices and beliefs.

Implications for future research and practice

This study has several implications for future research and practice. First, ICU clinicians need brief, context-sensitive tools to assess patients’ decision-making capacity, emotional readiness and understanding of key information. Second, structured interdisciplinary ICU rounds or brief daily huddles could be implemented to formally incorporate nurses’ bedside observations of patients’ comprehension and emotional state into decision-making. Third, to reduce clinician burden, future research could explore decision-support models, such as standardised prerecorded educational videos for common ICU procedures or diagnoses, which would allow patients and families to review information repeatedly without requiring repeated explanations from clinicians.

Conclusions

This study explored healthcare professionals’ views on SDM involving conscious patients in the ICU. While clinicians generally acknowledged the ethical value of patient involvement, they also expressed concerns about the instability of patients’ decision-making capacity and the difficulty of assessing their cognitive ability to make informed decisions. At the same time, patient involvement was seen as having both advantages and disadvantages. The findings underscore the importance of clearly defining the roles and responsibilities of healthcare professionals, improving team communication, and establishing effective support mechanisms to facilitate meaningful participation in SDM by conscious ICU patients.
