Online Steiner Forest with Recourse
Yaowei Long, Sepideh Mahabadi, Sherry Sarkar, Jakub Tarnawski

Abstract
In the online Steiner forest problem we are given a graph G G , and a sequence of terminal pairs ( u i , v i ) (u_{i},v_{i}) which arrive in an online fashion. We are asked to maintain a low-cost subgraph in which each u i u_{i} is connected to v i v_{i} for all the pairs that have arrived so far. If we are not allowed to delete edges from our solution, then the best possible competitive ratio is Θ ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) \Theta(\log n) . In this work, we initiate the study of low-recourse algorithms for online Steiner forest. We give an algorithm that maintains a constant-competitive solution and has an amortized recourse of O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\log n) , i.e., inserts and deletes O ⁡ ( log ⁡ n ) O(\log n) edges per demand on average.

Introduction

The
Steiner tree
problem is a classic question in network design.
Given a weighted graph and a subset of vertices called
terminals
, it asks to compute the cheapest tree that connects the terminals.
Since being included on Karp’s list of 21 NP-complete problems
[
61
]

it has been extensively studied.
The problem
is known to be APX-hard
[
37
]
;
however, getting a constant-factor approximation is straightforward, as computing a minimum spanning tree on the set of terminals already yields a 2-approximation
[
62
]
.
The current best approximation guarantee is
\ln{4}+\varepsilon<1.39

[
32
]
.

In the
online
version of the

problem,
introduced in a seminal work of Imase and Waxman
[
59
]
,
the terminals arrive online,
and after each arrival the algorithm must produce a solution that connects the current terminal set
–
without knowledge of the future arrivals
and without the possibility of deleting already added edges.
They
showed that the natural greedy algorithm is
O(\log n)
-competitive,
and that no algorithm can obtain a better competitive ratio.

Motivated by this impossibility result,
Imase and Waxman
[
59

also asked the question of whether deleting a small number of edges
would allow one to maintain a constant-competitive solution.
They showed how to maintain such a Steiner tree
while making

changes per
n
arrivals
(which improves upon the
O(n^{2})
changes needed if one naively recomputes the tree after each arrival).
In other words, they gave a constant-competitive algorithm with
O(\sqrt{n})

.
This was eventually improved over 20 years later by Megow, Skutella, Verschae, and Wiese
[
66
]

to constant amortized recourse.
Finally, Gu, Gupta, and Kumar
[
50
]
showed that it is in fact enough to make just a single edge swap per arrival.

In the more general
Steiner forest
problem,
instead of a set of terminals we are given a set of

,
and asked to compute the cheapest forest that connects the vertices of each terminal pair together.
The first (offline) approximation algorithm for the problem is a primal-dual approach by Agrawal, Klein, and Ravi
[
2

that yields a

this was further generalized by Goemans and Williamson
[
47
]

and the approximation ratio was improved only very recently to
2-2^{-11}

[
3
]

and subsequently to
1.994
[
58
]
.
As for algorithms based on more combinational techniques,
a natural greedy algorithm that repeatedly connects the closest yet-unconnected terminal pair
is no better than
\Omega(\log n)

[
36
]
,
but Gupta and Kumar
[
56

showed that the

Furthermore,
Groß, Gupta, Kumar, Matuschke,
Schmidt, Schmidt, and Verschae
[
49

gave a constant-factor approximation based on local search.

The focus of this work is the

introduced by Westbrook and Yan

who gave an

Next, Awerbuch, Azar, and Bartal

Finally, Berman and Coulston

In contrast to Steiner tree, for Steiner forest

1.3
Technical Overview

A natural attempt at maintaining an online
O(1)
-approximate Steiner forest is to, for each arrival in the input sequence, independently define the current snapshot of the online solution as the outcome of some offline
O(1)
-approximate algorithm for the current instance. This approach will automatically give an
O(1)
-approximate online solution. To control the recourse, the low-recourse online Steiner tree algorithm
[
50
]
exploits a well-behaved offline algorithm which constructs an offline solution under the guidance of a
clustering procedure
.

To design a low-recourse online Steiner forest algorithm, we also start with this framework, and fortunately, a generalized clustering procedure already exists for the Steiner forest problem
[
56
]
. However, the following fundamental difference between clustering procedures for Steiner tree and Steiner forest problems prevents us from continuing with the same approach as in
[
50
]
.

The Barrier.

On a high level, a clustering procedure will maintain a clustering
\mathscr{C}
(i.e., a partition) of the terminals and iteratively merge two
active
clusters in
\mathscr{C}
which are close enough in the contracted metric
\mathcal{M}/\mathscr{C}
(we can think of the input metric
\mathcal{M}
as a complete weighted graph on the terminals
1
1

1

In our model, we assume that the input metric
\mathcal{M}
has no Steiner node, i.e.,
\mathcal{M}
is a metric on terminals. See
Section

2
and particularly
Remark

2.1
for a further discussion.
and of
\mathcal{M}/\mathscr{C}
as the graph with each cluster contracted into a single vertex). In the clustering procedure for Steiner tree, clusters are always active, which means that the procedure is equivalent to merging clusters which are close in the original metric
\mathcal{M}
, and therefore, adding one edge to the solution suffices to simulate this merge. In contrast,
in the clustering procedure for Steiner forest,
some clusters will become inactive (i.e., they will stay in the clustering without participating in further merge operations). Hence, we need to measure the closeness in the contracted metric
\mathcal{M}/\mathscr{C}
, and thus simulating a merge operation between clusters
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathscr{C}
requires us to add into the solution a
C_{1}
-
C_{2}
shortest path in
\mathcal{M}/\mathscr{C}
, which potentially consists of many edges.

We note that the difference between the clustering procedures for these two problems is rooted in the inherent difference between Steiner forest and Steiner tree: a Steiner tree solution must be connected, while a Steiner forest solution may not. This is also the fundamental barrier to overcome when designing Steiner forest algorithms in other settings (e.g., the offline and classic online settings).

In other words, while applying the approach in
[
50
]
to Steiner forest may control recourse with respect to merges and their revocations (that is, when comparing the clustering procedures for two consecutive arrivals
t-1
and
t
, some merges done at
t-1
may be revoked, and new merges may be performed at
t
), it remains unclear how to bound the recourse in terms of edge changes.

Step 1: Pinning Edges.

We proceed completely differently from
[
50
]
and use the idea of
pinning edges
.
On a high level, when performing a merge by buying a shortest path, we
pin
the
cheapest

(1/\log n)
-fraction of edges on this path, where pinning an edge means that it will not be deleted in the future, even if the corresponding merge is later revoked. To bound the recourse
without harming the approximation, we will guarantee the following.

•

The number of pinned edges is linear in
n
at the end, which means that the total recourse will be bounded by
O(n\log n)
. This is relatively simple: intuitively, we will enforce the set of pinned edges to be acyclic.

•

The cost of the pinned edges should always be within a constant factor of the optimum, so that we can still guarantee a constant-factor approximation. To this end, an important step is to bound the total cost of all merges throughout the algorithm by
O(\log n\cdot\mathsf{OPT})
, including those that are later revoked (see the following Step 2 for a detailed discussion). This will bound the total cost of pinned edges by
O(\mathsf{OPT})
, since whenever we buy a path, the cheapest
(1/\log n)
-fraction of its edges, which we pin, have cost at most
(1/\log n)
-fraction of the path cost.

Step 2: Bounding the Total Merging Cost.

We employ a particular clustering procedure from the offline
timed gluttonous algorithm

[
56
]
. It proceeds by
levels
, from
0
to the top. At each level
i
, it iteratively merges two clusters if their distance is in
[2^{i},2^{i+1})
(the merging cost is at most their distance). Furthermore, the clustering procedure allows some flexibility in choosing the order of level-
i
merges; intuitively, we prioritize the level-
i
merges that already existed in the previous arrival.

For the above clustering procedure, we can show that for a fixed level
i
, the cost of all merging operations at this level throughout the algorithm is bounded by
O(\mathsf{OPT})
using a dual-fitting framework. At a high level, the dual-fitting argument is intuitive: we want to maintain a set of source vertices and obtain a feasible dual solution by growing balls around them. The subtlety in the formal analysis is that choosing (and maintaining) the right source vertices requires exploiting how the offline clustering procedures relate to one another across different arrivals.

Finally, the total merging cost is
O(\log n\cdot\mathsf{OPT})
, since the merges from levels beyond the top
O(\log n)
will have negligible costs. We emphasize that this total merging cost is not the cost of our online solution. The online solution consists of (i) the output of an offline
O(1)
-approximate algorithm for the current instance, and (ii) the edges pinned up to that point, which have cost
O(\mathsf{OPT})
as long as the total merging cost is
O(\log n\cdot\mathsf{OPT})
.

The graph representation of the metric

In the online setting, the instance

For the online instance

2.1
.

The way we define a Steiner forest instance

may seem unusual in the sense that
\mathcal{M}
is a metric only on the set of terminals (without Steiner vertices). However, we point out that this model is in fact common in the literature on online Steiner trees with low recourse
[
66
,
50
,
55
]
. The motivation for this definition is that maintaining a Steiner tree/forest in a general (non-metric) graph with low recourse is unrealistic, since even purchasing a simple path between two terminals can already incur large recourse.

3

3

In the seminal work
[
59
]
on online Steiner trees with recourse, the authors consider a different model in which recourse is measured by the number of
primitive path

Moreover, restricting to the

O(1)

2.2
.

We assume that, at the beginning, we know [a constant-estimation
\hat{n}
of] the length

of the online instance

. This assumption can be easily removed as follows. Initially, set the estimation
\hat{n}
to be a constant. Whenever the current number of arrivals exceeds

, we double

and restart the entire algorithm. Note that these restarts will only increase the final amortized recourse by a constant factor.

Clusterings.

Given a terminal set
V
, a

\mathscr{C}
is a partitioning of
V
into
clusters
. The

is the one in which each terminal forms its own singleton cluster. For two clusterings

, i.e.,

. Note that if

.

Contracted Metrics.

Given an original metric
\mathcal{M}=(V,{\sf dist}_{\mathcal{M}})
and a clustering
\mathscr{C}
of

, consider the graph obtained by contracting each cluster

in the graph representation of the original metric

. We refer to the shortest-path metric of this graph as

.

Sometimes we will further contract the graph

to denote its shortest-path metric. The vertex set of

(which is exactly

), so for

.

3
A Clustering Procedure

In this section, we describe a clustering procedure which is the core part of the offline constant-approximate Steiner forest algorithm (called the
timed gluttonous algorithm
) in
[
56
]
.

The input of the clustering procedure is a Steiner forest instance
(\mathcal{M},D)
, and it outputs a
clustering hierarchy

\mathscr{H}=(\mathscr{C}_{0},\mathscr{C}_{1},\mathscr{C}_{2},...,\mathscr{C}_{L+1})
of the terminal set
V
, where
L
denotes the maximum level (which will be defined shortly). The hierarchy
\mathscr{H}
satisfies that
\mathscr{C}_{0}
is the trivial clustering of
V
, and for each
0\leq i\leq L
,
\mathscr{C}_{i}\preceq\mathscr{C}_{i+1}
.

The Clustering Procedure.

Initially, for each terminal
v\in V
, define its
level
to be

{\sf level}(v)=\lceil\log_{2}{\sf dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(v,u)\rceil

where
u
is the mate of
v
. Note that
{\sf level}(v)\geq 0
since we have assumed
{\sf dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(\cdot,\cdot)
takes values at least
1
. The level of a cluster
C\subseteq V
is
{\sf level}(C)=\max_{v\in C}{\sf level}(v)
.
Let
L=\max_{v\in V}{\sf level}(v)
be the maximum level.

Then we iterate
i
from
0
to
L
. For each iteration
i
, we will construct
\mathscr{C}_{i+1}
based on
\mathscr{C}_{i}
as follows.

1.

First, we construct an auxiliary graph
H_{i}
, called the
virtual graph
, with vertices

V(H_{i})=\{C\in\mathscr{C}_{i}\mid{\sf level}(C)\geq i\}

corresponding to clusters
C\in\mathscr{C}_{i}
with
{\sf level}(C)\geq i
, called
i
-active
clusters. Naturally, each
C\in\mathscr{C}_{i}
with
{\sf level}(C)<i
is an
i
-inactive
cluster. For each
C_{1},C_{2}\in V(H_{i})
, there is an edge (called a
virtual edge
) in
H_{i}
connecting them iff

{\sf dist}_{\mathcal{M}/\mathscr{C}_{i}}(C_{1},C_{2})<2^{i+1}.

2.

We construct
\mathscr{C}_{i+1}
by
contracting
H_{i}
over
\mathscr{C}_{i}
. That is, we first copy all
i
-inactive clusters from
\mathscr{C}_{i}
into
\mathscr{C}_{i+1}
. Next,
for each connected component
Q
of
H_{i}
,
we add a cluster
C_{i+1}
into
\mathscr{C}_{i+1}
which is the union of all
\mathscr{C}_{i}
-clusters in this connected component, i.e.,
C_{i+1}=\bigcup_{C_{i}\in Q}C_{i}
(note that the
C_{i+1}
added in this way are
i
-active). Note that
\mathscr{C}_{i}\preceq\mathscr{C}_{i+1}
by the construction.

We note that the above clustering procedure is identical to that in Section 4.1 of
[
56
]
, so we list some observations below without formal proofs. These observations will be useful when analyzing our online algorithm in
Section

4
.

3.1
.

For each
i\geq 0
and any two different
i
-active clusters
C_{1},C_{2}\in\mathscr{C}_{i}
, we have
{\sf dist}_{\mathcal{M}/\mathscr{C}_{i}}(C_{1},C_{2})\geq 2^{i}
.

The above
3.1
follows
because in iteration
i-1
, we merge two
(i-1)
-active clusters in
\mathscr{C}_{i-1}
if they are close (i.e., they have distance less than
2^{i}
in
\mathcal{M}/\mathscr{C}_{i-1}
). A formal proof can be found in Section 4 in
[
56
]
.

3.2
.

For each demand pair
(u,v)\in D
, there is a cluster
C\in\mathscr{C}_{L+1}
such that
u,v\in C
.

This is basically because
{\sf dist}_{\mathcal{M}}(u,v)<2^{{\sf level}(u)+1}
by the definitions of
{\sf level}(\cdot)
and
L
. Then at the beginning of iteration
i={\sf level}(u)
, if
u
and
v
are still inside different clusters
C_{u},C_{v}\in\mathscr{C}_{i}
, then
C_{u}
and
C_{v}
are still

-active and they will be merged in this iteration.

The Forest-Forming Procedure.

For better understanding, we briefly describe a procedure forming a Steiner forest solution based on the clustering hierarchy (which is actually straightforward). Recourse

We are going to bound the total number of edge insertions by

, that is,

\sum_{t=1}^{n}|F^{(t)}\setminus F^{(t-1)}|\leq O(n\lambda),

which gives
O(\lambda)

Consider the changes from

to
F^{(t)}
. The new edges

come from either
A^{(t)}\setminus A^{(t-1)}
or

(since each inherited
\hat{e}^{(t)}\in\hat{F}^{(t)}
has

). In other words, we have

\sum_{t=1}^{n}|F^{(t)}\setminus F^{(t-1)}|\leq\sum_{t=1}^{n}\left(|A^{(t)}\setminus A^{(t-1)}|+\sum_{\text{non-inherited }\hat{e}^{(t)}\in\hat{F}^{(t)}}|E_{{\rm orig}}(\hat{e}^{(t)})|\right).

For the pinned edge set, we have
\sum_{t=1}^{n}|A^{(t)}\setminus A^{(t-1)}|\leq 2n-1

. Hence at the end

4.5
Approximation

To avoid clutter, we only show that after the last arrival
n
, the online solution

is an
O(1)
-approximation to the instance
(\mathcal{M}^{(n)},D^{(n)})
. The approximation in the other arrivals can be bounded in exactly the same way.

Let
\mathsf{OPT}
be the cost of the optimal solution to
(\mathcal{M}^{(n)},D^{(n)})
. Recall that

F^{(n)}=A^{(n)}\cup\bigcup_{\hat{e}^{(n)}\in\hat{F}^{(n)}}E_{{\rm orig}}(\hat{e}^{(n)})\,.

Hence, to bound the cost of

by
O(\log n\cdot\mathsf{OPT}/\lambda)
, it suffices to bound

•

{\sf cost}(A^{(n)})
(called the

) by
O(\log n\cdot\mathsf{OPT}/\lambda)
, and

•

\sum_{\hat{e}^{(n)}\in\hat{F}^{(n)}}{\sf cost}(E_{{\rm orig}}(\hat{e}^{(n)}))
(called the

) by
O(\mathsf{OPT})
.

The Forest-Forming Cost.

The forest-forming cost can be interpreted as the cost of the offline Steiner forest algorithm introduced in
Section

Lemma

3.3
.

Formally, for each level
i
and each virtual edge

{\sf cost}(E_{{\rm orig}}(\hat{e}^{(n)}))\leq 2^{i+1}.

(1)

To see this, we trace
\hat{e}^{(n)}
back through the inheritance relation until reaching a non-inherited virtual edge
\hat{e}^{(t)}=(C_{1},C_{2})\in\hat{F}^{(t)}_{i}
in some arrival

. At the moment when

is defined on
4

5

1

{\sf cost}(E_{{\rm orig}}(\hat{e}^{(t)}))\leq{\sf dist}_{(\mathcal{M}/\mathscr{C}^{(t)}_{i})\setminus A^{(t)}}(C_{1},C_{2})\leq{\sf dist}_{\mathcal{M}/\mathscr{C}^{(t)}_{i}}(C_{1},C_{2})\leq 2^{i+1},

(2)

where the last inequality is by

and the definition of
H^{(t)}_{i}
. The inheritance relation implies

, so we get the desired bound (

Given (

\displaystyle\sum_{\hat{e}^{(n)}\in\hat{F}^{(n)}}{\sf cost}(E_{{\rm orig}}(\hat{e}^{(n)}))\leq\sum_{i}|\hat{F}^{(n)}_{i}|\cdot 2^{i+1}=\sum_{i}(|\mathscr{C}^{(n)}_{i}|-|\mathscr{C}^{(n)}_{i+1}|)\cdot 2^{i+1}\leq O(\mathsf{OPT}),

where the equality is by

Conclusion

In this work we initiate the study of low-recourse algorithms for online Steiner forest.
We gave a constant-competitive algorithm with

We can further remove the assumption by
