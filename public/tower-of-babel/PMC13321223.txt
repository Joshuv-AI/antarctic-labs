A potential overestimation of CO2 physiological effects on evapotranspiration
Authors: Yi Hao, Xing Yuan, Xiazhen Xi, Zhenzhong Zeng, Giovanni Forzieri, Peili Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceRising CO2 levels reduce vegetation water depletion by decreasing stomatal conductance, which is expected to mitigate land surface drying. However, the interplay of these processes is more complex than previously understood. Our study uncovers a critical indirect mechanism by which vegetation changes trigger shifts in surface energy balance, leading to warmer and drier air, which in turn increases evapotranspiration loss. Most importantly, the enhanced indirect feedback offsets half of vegetation’s water-saving potential in northern mid-to-high latitudes, but it is not sufficiently considered in current assessments of vegetation’s hydrological effects. Omitting this feedback may distort vegetation’s role in mitigating future land surface drying and lead to an underestimation of drought risks in socioecological systems.

Evapotranspiration (ET) is a critical process governing water and energy exchanges at the land–atmosphere interface (1). Under CO2-driven warming, increased evaporative demand from rising radiative forcing reduces terrestrial water availability, exacerbates surface aridity (2), and triggers extreme droughts via rapid soil moisture depletion (3, 4), imposing abrupt water stress on both natural and managed ecosystems (5). However, vegetation physiological response to elevated CO2, termed CO2 physiological forcing (PHY), challenges this “warming leads to drying” paradigm (6, 7). Specifically, elevated CO2 reduces plant stomatal conductance since less stomatal opening is needed to maintain photosynthesis, thereby suppressing ET and increasing soil water retention (8, 9). Concurrently, vegetation changes affect atmospheric vapor pressure deficit (VPD) (10) by altering energy balance, which indirectly regulates ET through feedback processes but is often overlooked in previous studies.

Elevated CO2 concentrations reduce leaf-level transpiration by limiting stomatal conductance, while simultaneously promoting leaf area index (LAI) that expands evaporative surfaces, partially offsetting the water-saving effect from reduced stomatal conductance (10–12). These direct PHY impacts are often quantified using an improved Penman-Monteith equation (incorporating the linear response of stomatal resistance to CO2) (7, 13–15) and standalone land surface and hydrological models (16–19), demonstrating the capacity to mitigate or even outweigh the negative influences of radiative forcings on water availability in some regions (7, 13, 14). On the other hand, fully coupled Earth system modeling highlights that PHY can elevate atmospheric VPD through interconnected biophysical processes (10). Stomatal closure under elevated CO2 reduces transpiration and atmospheric humidity, shifts energy partitioning from latent to sensible heat flux, and drives temperature increases (20, 21). Concurrently, rising CO2-induced vegetation greening (CO2 fertilization effect) decreases surface albedo in mid-to-high latitudes, resulting in a greater absorption of solar radiation that further intensifies warming (22, 23). These changes amplify atmospheric VPD and indirectly regulate ET through feedback processes. However, the extent to which VPD feedback determines the direction and magnitude of PHY’s net impact on ET remains unclear. The underlying mechanisms of the indirect impact are still poorly understood due to two competing processes: ET enhancement from elevated vapor pressure gradients and ET reduction through high VPD-induced stomatal closure (24), modulating PHY’s indirect impact in opposing directions.

Understanding PHY’s indirect impact helps reconcile discrepancies among current mainstream approaches and optimize methodologies. If the indirect impact proves critical, incorporating vegetation-atmosphere feedback becomes essential when assessing vegetation physiological effects on ET (25, 26). Omitting this feedback may distort vegetation’s role in mitigating future land surface drying. This would affect the design of land-based adaptation strategies and drought/flood management in vulnerable regions (12). However, there remains a lack of comprehensive quantification and mechanistic understanding of how PHY indirectly affects ET through VPD feedback, especially as the CO2 rises.

Here, we used nine Earth system models (ESMs) participating in the Coupled Climate-Carbon Cycle Model Intercomparison Project (C4MIP) under Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6), which incorporated comprehensive land–atmosphere feedback processes to assess the indirect impact of PHY. The “indirect impact” specifically refers to CO2-driven vegetation changes altering atmospheric VPD, which then indirectly regulates ET—distinct from the commonly considered direct regulation of ET via LAI and stomatal conductance changes. The models conducted simulations with CO2 concentrations increasing annually by 1% from preindustrial levels until reaching 4 × CO2, including experiments with (1pctCO2) and without (1pctCO2-rad) PHY. We adopted 45°N to partition mid-to-high latitude and mid-to-low latitude zones, given their substantially divergent mechanisms according to our results. We first isolated the indirect impact of PHY under current climate conditions, elucidating how it differentially modulates ET across global regions. Furthermore, we examined how the indirect impact might change under future climate conditions as CO2 rises, and explored the associations with climatic and vegetation characteristics.

Results

Indirect Impact of CO2 Physiological Forcing on ET Under Current Climate.

All nine ESMs in the CMIP6-C4MIP ensemble reproduce the observed ET increase (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 and Table S1). Experiments excluding carbon cycle coupling (1pctCO2-rad experiment) show more pronounced changes, indicating that PHY attenuates ET enhancement by approximately 23% under current climate conditions (years 21 to 50 in simulations, mean CO2 ≈ 405 ppm) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2; from 11.3 ± 4.0 to 9.2 ± 3.0 mm y−1). This demonstrates that the water-saving effect from reduced stomatal conductance (which suppresses ET) outweighs the competing effect of increased LAI (which enhances ET), as supported by reduced canopy ET observed in free-air CO2 enrichment (FACE) experiments (27, 28). The suppression of ET favors water partitioning into runoff and soil moisture, but concurrently intensifies atmospheric aridity (Fig. 1A). Multimodel means show PHY-induced declines in atmospheric moisture content over 39% of global land areas, driven by ET reductions associated with stomatal closure. These changes are particularly evident in humid, densely vegetated regions, including the eastern United States, Europe, southern China, South American plains, and central Africa (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). This aligns with the finding that PHY exerts a stronger suppression on ET in humid regions than in arid regions (11). Concurrently, LAI-induced albedo reduction across mid-to-high latitudes (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B) amplifies surface heat absorption (20, 23). Combined with reduced ET cooling in humid zones and enhanced sensible heat flux, PHY drives temperature increases and thus elevates saturation vapor pressure (es) over 78% of land areas. About 45% of these regions show high intermodel consistency, which is consistent with observed increases in canopy temperature in FACE experiments worldwide (29). These changes further trigger significant VPD increases across 48% of land surfaces, with their distribution closely linked to PHY-induced warming, even if moisture content may increase. (Fig. 1A and SI Appendix, Fig. S3C).

The increasing VPD is expected to enhance ET, but a distinctive latitudinal pattern exists due to varying vegetation responses to VPD (Fig. 1B). We quantified the unidirectional forcing of VPD on ET using ∂ET/∂VPDs, which was achieved by holding actual vapor pressure (ea) constant in the partial derivative calculation, thereby eliminating the feedback of ET on VPD via changes in ea (Materials and Methods). The partial derivatives were calculated under soil moisture-controlled conditions (Materials and Methods) to eliminate land surface-induced spurious correlations from soil moisture-VPD covariation (30) (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). This spatial pattern was further verified through ridge regression analysis (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 and Materials and Methods). The results demonstrate that most mid-to-high latitude regions in the Northern Hemisphere show a strong positive ET response to VPD increases (Fig. 1B), with a 1-percentile rise in VPD corresponding to a 0.30 ± 0.05 percentile increase in ET. In contrast, arid and semiarid regions around 30°N exhibit negative ET responses to VPD. Here, soil moisture limitation constrains ET, and stomatal closure under high VPD further reduces ET (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). In addition, most low-latitude regions (except parts of South America, Central Africa, and Southeast Asian rainforests) show suppressed ET as stomatal sensitivity to VPD outweighs vapor gradient-driven ET enhancement. This is likely associated with high maximum stomatal conductance that confers greater sensitivity to high VPD, with a tendency toward isohydric behavior in humid tropical trees (31).

In regions where ET exhibits a negative response to VPD, PHY-induced VPD elevation further amplifies its water-saving effect, which can be viewed as vegetation self-regulation under drought conditions. However, in northern mid-to-high latitude regions, PHY indirectly promotes ET by intensifying atmospheric VPD, creating an antagonistic effect that counteracts the direct stomatal closure response. The indirect impact of PHY through VPD feedback was quantified by multiplying ∂ET/∂VPDs by the percentile change in VPD driven by PHY (∆VPDPHY), which originates from energy balance alterations (e.g., via radiation and temperature changes) that affect es (Materials and Methods). Under current climate conditions, the most pronounced indirect feedback occurs in North America, Europe, and eastern Russia (SI Appendix, Fig. S7). In mid-to-high latitude regions, the strength of the indirect feedback reduces PHY’s inherent water-saving effect by 54% (±26%) (Fig. 1C). In parts of North America and Russia, the indirect impact weakens PHY’s impact by more than 80%. Globally, the indirect impact diminishes PHY’s impact on ET suppression by 35% (±11%). In summary, although vegetation may suppress ET through stomatal regulation and retain water in the terrestrial system, its indirect impact via VPD feedback partially offsets this benefit by enhancing ET, thereby substantially limiting the water-saving potential, especially in northern mid-to-high latitudes. Although this effect is derived from model simulations, it is consistent with observational evidence from North American forests, where stomatal conductance shows no widespread decline, resulting in limited water-saving effects under elevated CO2 (32). Enhanced photosynthesis rather than stomatal closure is a more favorable strategy in these regions (33).

Intensification of VPD Feedback and Latitudinal Disparity Under Future CO2 Scenarios.

As CO2 increases from preindustrial levels to 4 × CO2 in the future, the indirect impact via VPD feedback intensifies predominantly in northern mid-to-high latitudes (Fig. 2A). Compared to current climate conditions, the indirect impact on ET is significantly enhanced in northern Eurasia, northern North America, and southern China under 4 × CO2 (Fig. 2B), reducing PHY’s ET-suppression capacity by 68% (±18%) in mid-to-high latitudes. Notably, the net PHY impact becomes statistically insignificant at high latitudes under 4 × CO2 (Fig. 2C). This suggests that the antagonism between PHY’s direct impact (ET reduced by stomatal closure) and indirect impact (ET promoted by increased VPD) is critical to determine the net PHY effect as CO2 increases. In mid-to-low latitudes, however, the indirect impact shows a reversed change. Fig. 2C further reveals that the net effect of PHY on ET is strongest in low latitudes and decreases poleward, while the indirect impact exhibits an opposite latitudinal gradient in the Northern Hemisphere. Therefore, we conclude that the spatial heterogeneity of PHY impacts on ET is determined not only by climate and vegetation conditions (1, 11) but also by the VPD feedback mechanism and its future changes. The latter explains why PHY strongly suppresses ET and enhances surface wetness in low latitudes, particularly in the Southern Hemisphere tropics where it offsets 49 to 87% of CO2 radiative effects on ET (Fig. 2C), while it provides limited benefits in northern mid-to-high latitudes (34). The latitudinal disparity in PHY impacts continues to intensify under rising CO2, exacerbating water resource challenges in northern mid-to-high latitudes.

To further elucidate the latitudinal patterns of future changes in the indirect impact of PHY, we decomposed it into the VPD forcing term (specifically referring to PHY-induced es change via altering energy balance) and the ET response term (∂ET/∂VPDs). While current climate shows substantial regional variability and intermodel uncertainty in PHY-induced VPD changes, when CO2 levels reach 2 × preindustrial and up to 4 × preindustrial levels, PHY drives spatially coherent VPD increases globally (Fig. 3A), with maximum enhancement in tropical regions and northern mid-to-high latitudes. This spatial pattern reflects different mechanisms across regions. In the tropics, strong ET suppression increases temperature through stomatal closure. In mid-to-high latitudes, vegetation greening reduces albedo, dominating local warming and intensifying VPD (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). However, south of 45°N, progressive decline in ∂ET/∂VPDs (Fig. 3B) leads to a widespread decrease or reversal of PHY indirect impact on ET. This is driven by intensified soil moisture constraints under future aridification (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A) and exceedance of optimal temperature thresholds (35, 36) that facilitates stomatal closure responses to VPD (SI Appendix, Fig. S9B). Conversely, north of 45°N (particularly in high-latitude regions), ET is unconstrained by soil moisture (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A), and vegetation prioritizes carbon gain through stomatal opening, as favorable temperature and radiation often co-occur with high VPD (37, 38). The absence of both soil moisture and stomatal limitations enables sustained ET enhancement as VPD increases. These explain why the indirect impact of PHY persistently intensifies in northern mid-to-high latitudes as CO2 increases, ultimately preventing PHY from effectively mitigating future surface drying and increasing the likelihood of decline in terrestrial water resources over these regions.

Discussion

Current understanding of the direct impact of PHY on ET has reached a consensus that stomatal closure reduces ET while LAI expansion enhances ET (1). Here, we move a step further and demonstrate how PHY-driven atmospheric VPD feedback indirectly regulates ET through spatially divergent feedbacks (Fig. 4). These indirect impacts will become increasingly important as CO2 rises, particularly in northern mid-to-high latitudes where they weaken PHY impact on ET suppression by half. Whether the indirect impact is incorporated offers further reconciliation for methodological discrepancies in quantifying the PHY effects, complementing the prevailing view that attributes the paradox of PHY-induced ET changes primarily to opposing stomatal conductance and LAI effects (10). Considering indirect VPD feedback explains: 1) why PHY induces strong ET reduction in low latitudes, while producing weaker and more uncertain effects in northern mid-to-high latitudes (34, 39), and 2) why widely used offline approaches for isolating PHY often estimate larger impacts than those derived from coupled simulations (25). Critically, omitting vegetation-VPD feedback may cause an overestimation of PHY’s benefits for increasing runoff and soil moisture, which could affect the design of vegetation-based adaptation strategies in water-stressed regions.

ET suppression induced by vegetation physiology is also crucial for alleviating drought stress. However, its mitigating role is limited in northern mid-to-high latitudes. Mounting evidence indicates these regions will experience pronounced drought amplification, with increasing risks transitioning to rapid-onset flash droughts (3). Even humid mid-to-high latitude regions are displaying declining vegetation resistance to flash droughts (5). In contrast, many low-latitude regions show effective drought mitigation by vegetation physiological responses to rising CO2. The mechanisms proposed in this study are applicable for clarifying these divergences: While ∂ET/∂VPDs weakens or even reverses to negative response in low latitudes as soil moisture constraints intensify and vegetation exceeds thermal optimum thresholds (35, 36), mid-to-high latitudes exhibit sustained ET enhancement with rising VPD due to the lack of these constraints. When high VPD coincides with optimal temperature and radiation, mid-to-high latitude vegetation may prioritize stomatal opening to maximize carbon uptake (37), thereby further exacerbating ET loss, especially during early drought stages when vegetation has not yet experienced wilting or severe water limitation (40). This accelerates soil moisture decline and triggers flash droughts (3). These results underscore the growing importance of VPD and ET dynamics, which not only drive gradual aridification but also become key to initiating extreme drought events. These changes intensify drought pressure on future crop production, which also faces a rising demand from a growing population (41). Although rising CO2 can increase crop yields and improve water-use efficiency, an associated rise in VPD may partially offset these benefits, limiting the effectiveness in alleviating drought stress, especially in major agricultural regions such as the US Corn Belt, China’s rice-growing areas, and the European plains (Fig. 2B). This is supported by studies on maize in the United States, which show that stomatal conductance-induced leaf warming and high VPD reduce gains in water-use efficiency by about 20% (42). Lower-than-expected crop drought resistance underscores the need for enhanced monitoring of rapid-onset droughts and implementing adaptive water allocation strategies in key agricultural zones.

Over mid-to-low latitudes, although PHY promotes surface humidification through both direct and indirect impacts, many ecosystems frequently face threats from high VPD stress. The PHY-driven VPD increase induces stomatal closure, indirectly suppresses ET, and establishes a positive feedback that further enhances VPD. This often leads to prolonged stomatal closure and subsequent tree mortality through “carbon starvation” (43), particularly in tropical regions where tree mortality is increasing due to warming and rising VPD (44). Concurrently, rising VPD may elevate wildfire risks, undermining biodiversity conservation and carbon sequestration (45). In other words, while PHY increases soil moisture and theoretically enhances vegetation growth (10), the adverse impacts of high VPD on vegetation often outweigh the benefits of soil water retention (46). These findings prompt us to reexamine whether PHY can serve as a panacea for mitigating surface drying and benefiting vegetation growth.

While the ESMs employed in this study integrate comprehensive vegetation physiological and feedback processes to isolate indirect PHY impact, there remain uncertainties in simulating vegetation dynamics and their climatic feedbacks (47). First, nutrient limitations are often insufficiently represented in models, which may lead to a failure to capture the saturation of CO2 fertilization effects on LAI (SI Appendix, Fig. S10) (48). Second, observed stomatal responses to CO2 vary with vegetation type, age, and climate, whereas most models rely on semiempirical parameterizations that cannot fully capture the variability (SI Appendix, Table S3), leading to overestimated sensitivity in some regions (47). In addition, plant hydraulic processes are simplified and tree mortality is often conservatively simulated (38). These biases also increase uncertainty in precipitation projections through land–atmosphere interactions (SI Appendix, Fig. S11), thereby affecting estimates of ET and indirect impacts. Moreover, vegetation’s adaptive responses to climate change may disrupt existing feedback balances (38). Addressing these issues requires capturing vegetation-climate coupled evolution using multisource observational data to improve the representation of vegetation responses in ESMs. It will also help reduce uncertainties in future ecohydrological projections and strengthen the scientific basis for water resource management and environmental sustainability.

In summary, this study reveals that the growing influence of VPD feedback is challenging the benefits of vegetation physiological effects. These findings not only advance our understanding of vegetation-climate feedback but also highlight the urgency for region-specific adaptation strategies. Recent observations suggest that many forests are shifting from carbon sinks to carbon sources (49). Concurrently, the CO2 fertilization effect is projected to weaken in the future (50), while vegetation water-saving benefits are partially offset by VPD feedbacks, leading to lower-than-expected drought resistance. These indicate that overreliance on PHY to mitigate droughts and enhance carbon sinks would be unsustainable, necessitating urgent implementation of emission reduction.

Materials and Methods

CMIP6 Model Simulations.

The Coupled Climate-Carbon Cycle Model Intercomparison Project (C4MIP) provides three experiments where CO2 concentrations increase annually by 1% from preindustrial levels (285 ppm) over 140 y until reaching 4 × CO2 (~1,134 ppm), including 1) the fully coupled control experiment (1pctCO2), 2) 1pctCO2-rad where CO2 increases only in the radiation module while the CO2 concentration in carbon cycle module remains fixed at preindustrial levels, and 3) 1pctCO2-bgc where CO2 increases only in the carbon cycle module with fixed concentrations in radiation-module. The dynamic changes in non-CO2 greenhouse gases, aerosols, and land-use change are not considered in these three sets of experiments. The hydrometeorological changes (e.g., VPD changes) induced by PHY can be isolated by differencing the 1pctCO2 and 1pctCO2-rad simulations (21).

The historical period focuses on simulation years of 21 to 50 (mean CO2 ≈ 405 ppm), representing current climate conditions equivalent to 2001–2030 (mean CO2 ≈ 408 ppm), while long-term changes are assessed using 30-y moving windows at 10-y intervals across the full 140-y simulation. Our analysis utilized nine CMIP6-C4MIP ESMs (SI Appendix, Table S2) selected based on their availability of three experiments and required output variables. Their performance in reproducing the observed trend in ET was further evaluated against five global ET products (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 and Table S1). These nine models adopt different stomatal conductance schemes, including three semimechanistic models and six semiempirical ones, with two incorporating a full plant hydraulics scheme (SI Appendix, Table S3). All model outputs were interpolated to a uniform 1° × 1° grid resolution for multimodel ensemble analysis.

Calculation of ∂ET/∂VPDs.

To investigate how atmospheric drying further regulates ET, we selected VPD as an indicator that comprehensively reflects atmospheric aridity conditions and quantified the relationship using ∂ET/∂VPDs. The saturation vapor pressure (es) was computed by the Tetens formula as follows:[1]esT=0.6108×exp17.27×TT+237.3,[2]VPD=esT-ea,

where T is the temperature in degrees Celsius, and ea is the actual vapor pressure calculated using specific humidity and surface pressure. A feedback loop couples VPD and ET: Rising temperature increases es, which elevates VPD and drives ET. This, in turn, increases ea and feeds back to VPD. To isolate the unidirectional forcing of VPD on ET, we held ea constant at its historical monthly mean in the ∂ET/∂VPDs calculation, thereby preventing the feedback. Since soil moisture typically covaries with VPD, and it acts both as a key limiting factor for ET and indirectly affects ET through its influence on LAI—these processes are not considered as “indirect impact” arising from atmospheric feedbacks. Therefore, ∂ET/∂VPDs was calculated under soil moisture–controlled conditions following ref. 30 to ensure the isolation of indirect impact driven by the atmosphere. Specifically, for each model’s 1pctCO2 outputs and for each grid cell, we stratified the monthly data in each 30-y moving window into 10 soil moisture percentile bins (0 to 10%, 10 to 20%, …, 90 to 100%) and performed linear regressions within each bin:[3]ETi=β0,i+β1,i∙VPDi+εi,i=1,2,⋯,10,

where ETi and VPDi denote the data points assigned to bin i for fitting. The regression coefficient β1,i represents ∂ET/∂VPDs for each grid in bin i. To ensure statistical robustness, we retained sensitivity estimates only for bins containing >10 data points and for those with statistically significant regression coefficients (P < 0.05). The sensitivity of ET to VPD for each grid was calculated as the mean of valid β1 values across all 10 bins, while non-significant grids were assigned zero. By calculating ∂ET/∂VPDs within narrow soil moisture percentile ranges, we decoupled the covariation between VPD and soil moisture (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Notably, this approach does not negate the fundamental physical constraints of soil moisture on ET, and the bin-derived ∂ET/∂VPDs still reflects the actual ET capacity of different aridity regimes.

Indirect Impact Via VPD Feedback.

The “indirect impact” refers to PHY inducing VPD changes by altering energy balance (thereby affecting es), which then indirectly regulates ET. The indirect impact is quantified using a percentile-based metric as follows:[4]PHYind=∂ET∂VPDs×ΔVPDPHY,

where the first term ∂ET/∂VPDs (percentile/percentile) represents the ET percentile change induced by a 1-percentile VPD increase (Calculation of ∂ET/∂VPDs). The prepartial-derivative percentile transformation for each calendar month (SI Appendix, Variable processing) removes seasonal covariation between VPD and ET, enabling combined binning and partial derivative calculation across all 360 mo in 30 y to increase the sample size for robust gradient estimation. The second term ∆VPDPHY (percentile) derived from the difference in monthly mean VPD percentiles between simulations with (1pctCO2) and without (1pctCO2-rad) PHY in each 30-y moving window, quantifies the VPD change driven by physical processes (e.g., radiation and temperature) that affect es. VPD change induced by ea is excluded, as it represents a consequence rather than a cause of ET variation. Both experiments share the same climatology for percentile conversion to ensure comparability. The product of these terms reveals how PHY-induced VPD changes indirectly alter ET, while enabling quantification of its proportion within PHY’s total impacts on ET percentiles.
