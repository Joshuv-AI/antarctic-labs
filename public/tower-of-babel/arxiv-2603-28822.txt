Triangles Circumscribed about Central Conics and Their Invariants
Mohammad Hassan Murad

Abstract
. We study families of triangles that are inscribed in a fixed circle and circumscribed about a central conic, extending the classical Chapple–Euler relation within the framework of Poncelet geometry. We establish several geometric invariants that arise when the circumcenter of the triangle coincides with either the center of the conic or one of its foci. These include invariance properties of orthic triangles, tangential triangles, polar circles, and trigonometric expressions such as sin 2 ⁡ A + sin 2 ⁡ B + sin 2 ⁡ C \sin^{2}A+\sin^{2}B+\sin^{2}C . We further derive explicit analytic constructions of central conics associated with a given triangle under special configurations, including cases where the foci are located at the circumcenter and orthocenter. In addition, we investigate extremal area problem within Poncelet families, and develop both homothetic and non-homothetic constructions of sequences of Poncelet pairs. These results provide a unified geometric framework linking classical triangle geometry, central conics, and Poncelet porism.

1.
Introduction

If a triangle is circumscribed about a conic, there exist infinitely many such triangles, all sharing the same circumcircle, that are also circumscribed about the same conic. In fact, if the conic is an ellipse lying entirely inside the circumcircle of the triangle, then every point on this circumcircle serves as a vertex of a triangle that is circumscribed about the conic. This phenomenon is a generalization of the classical
Chapple–Euler theorem
(porism) and, more broadly, a special case of the celebrated
Poncelet theorem
, established by
Jean-Victor Poncelet
(1788–1867) for pairs of smooth conics; see, for example,
[
4
]
.

In a previous paper
[
3
]
, we established the following result.

Theorem 1.1
.

A central conic with semi-minor axis
b
can be inscribed in a triangle if and only if

(1.1)

\left(R^{2}-d_{+}^{2}\right)\left(R^{2}-d_{-}^{2}\right)=4\varepsilon b^{2}R^{2},

where
R
is the circumradius of the triangle,
\varepsilon=1
(respectively,
-1
) if the conic is an ellipse (respectively, a hyperbola), and
d_{+}
and
d_{-}
denote the distances from the circumcenter of the triangle to the foci of the conic.

In the special case where the conic reduces to a circle, its foci coincide, and equation (
1.1
) reduces to the classical Chapple–Euler relation, also known as Euler’s formula for triangles:

(R^{2}-d^{2})^{2}=4R^{2}r^{2},

where
d
denotes the distance between the circumcenter and the incenter (or an excenter) of the triangle.

In this paper, we investigate families of triangles that share a fixed
circumcircle and are circumscribed about a central conic. Our main goal
is to identify geometric invariants and explicit constructions that arise
under special configurations of the conic relative to the triangle.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We establish new invariants for Poncelet families, including
trigonometric quantities and properties of associated triangles such as
orthic and tangential triangles.

•

We derive explicit analytic constructions of central conics
inscribed in a given triangle under prescribed geometric constraints.

•

We develop homothetic and non-homothetic constructions of sequences
of Poncelet pairs of circles and central conics.

•

We characterize triangles of extremal area within these families.

Motivated by the results obtained in the author’s earlier work, this paper presents several new developments. To keep the exposition largely self-contained and accessible independently of the previous paper, we restate certain results in a slightly reformulated but equivalent form; their proofs can be found in
[
3
]
.

Theorem 1.2
.

Let
\triangle ABC
be circumscribed about a central conic
\mathcal{D}
. Denote by
O
and
R
the circumcenter and the circumradius of
\triangle ABC
. Then the orthocenter of any triangle sharing the circumcircle of
\triangle ABC
and circumscribed about
\mathcal{D}
lies on a circle with center at a point
\hat{O}
and radius
\hat{R}
where
\hat{O}
is the reflection of
O
about the center of
\mathcal{D}
and

(1.2)

\hat{R}=\frac{d_{+}d_{-}}{R}

and
d_{\pm}
denote the distances from the circumcenter of
\triangle ABC
to the foci of
\mathcal{D}
.

Corollary 1.1
.

If a triangle is circumscribed about a central conic, one of whose foci coincides with the circumcenter of the triangle, then the orthocenter of the triangle coincides with the other focus of the conic distinct from the circumcenter of the triangle.

Proof.

Let
\triangle ABC
be circumscribed about a central conic
\mathcal{D}
whose foci be
F_{\pm}
. Denote by
O
and
H
the circumcenter and orthocenter of
\triangle ABC
, respectively.

Without loss of generality, we assume
O=F_{+}
. So,
d_{+}=0
. It now follows from Theorem
1.2
that
\hat{R}=0
and
H=\hat{O}=F_{-}
.
∎

Theorem 1.3
.

Suppose a triangle is circumscribed about a central conic. The following statements hold:

(a)

The conic is an ellipse if and only if both of its foci lie either inside or outside the circumcircle of the triangle.

(b)

The conic is a hyperbola if and only if one focus lies inside the circumcircle of the triangle while the other lies outside.

Without loss of generality, we choose a Cartesian coordinate system in which the central conic is in standard form, unless stated otherwise. In such a system, a central conic is given by the following equation:

(1.3)

\frac{x^{2}}{\alpha}+\frac{y^{2}}{\beta}=1

where
\alpha>0
and
\beta\in\mathbb{R}\setminus\{0\}
. So, the conic is an ellipse if
\beta>0
; a hyperbola if
\beta<0
.
It follows from Theorem
1.1
that a triangle of circumradius
R
may be circumscribed about the conic if and only if

(1.4)

\displaystyle\beta

\displaystyle=\frac{(R^{2}-d_{+}^{2})(R^{2}-d_{-}^{2})}{4R^{2}}

\displaystyle\alpha

\displaystyle=\beta+c^{2}

and
c
is the linear eccentricity of the conic.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
, we discuss several immediate consequences of Theorems
1.1
–
1.3
. Section
3
is devoted to deriving explicit formulas for the construction of inellipses associated with a given triangle, with particular emphasis on the cases where the center of the inellipse coincides with the circumcenter, and where the foci are located at the circumcenter and the orthocenter of the triangle.

In Section
4
, we present homothety-based constructions of sequences of triangles together with corresponding families of inscribed central conics. Section
5
is devoted to the study of invariants associated with Poncelet families of triangles that circumscribe a fixed central conic and share a common circumcircle. In Section
6
, we construct non-homothetic realizations of
3
-Poncelet pairs. Finally, in Section
7
, we identify triangles of extremal area within a Poncelet family in the cases where the circumcenter coincides with either the center of the conic or one of its foci.

Throughout this paper, all triangles are assumed to be nondegenerate, that is, to consist of three non-collinear points.

2.
Properties of Triangles Circumscribed about Central Conics

Proposition 2.1
.

Let
\mathcal{P}
be the family of triangles sharing a fixed circumcircle and circumscribed about a given central conic. Then
\mathcal{P}
contains at most two right triangles.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{D}
be the central conic. For any triangle
\triangle ABC\in\mathcal{P}
, let
O
and
R
denote its circumcenter and circumradius, respectively, and let
H
be its orthocenter.

By Theorem
1.2
, the orthocenter
H
lies on a circle
\mathcal{C}(\hat{O},\hat{R})
, where
\hat{O}
is the reflection of
O
with respect to the center of
\mathcal{D}
, and
\hat{R}=d_{+}d_{-}/R
, with
d_{\pm}
denoting the distances from
O
to the foci of
\mathcal{D}
.

We first consider the case
\hat{O}\neq O
. The triangle
\triangle ABC
is right-angled if and only if
H
lies on the circumcircle
\mathcal{C}(O,R)
. Thus,
H
must lie in the intersection

\mathcal{C}(O,R)\cap\mathcal{C}(\hat{O},\hat{R}).

Since two distinct circles intersect in at most two points, there are at most two such positions of
H
, and hence at most two right triangles in
\mathcal{P}
.

Next, consider the case
\hat{O}=O
. In this case, the circles
\mathcal{C}(O,R)
and
\mathcal{C}(\hat{O},\hat{R})
are concentric. Therefore, they intersect if and only if
\hat{R}=R
, which yields

R^{2}=d_{+}d_{-}.

Since
\hat{O}=O
, the foci of
\mathcal{D}
are equidistant from
O
, so
d_{+}=d_{-}=c
, where
c
is the focal distance. Hence
R=c
, and the circumcircle
\mathcal{C}(O,R)
passes through both foci of
\mathcal{D}
.

However, this contradicts (
1.2
), which precludes the existence of such a conic
\mathcal{D}
under these conditions. Therefore, this case cannot occur.

The proof is complete.
∎

Recall that a triangle is called
oblique
if it has no right angle.

Proposition 2.2
.

Let
\triangle ABC
be circumscribed about a central conic whose center or one of its foci coincides with the circumcenter of
\triangle ABC
. Then
\triangle ABC
is oblique.

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}(O,R)
denote the circumcircle of
\triangle ABC
, where
O
is the circumcenter and
R
is the circumradius, and let
H
be the orthocenter. It suffices to show that
H\notin\mathcal{C}(O,R)
, since a triangle is right-angled if and only if its orthocenter lies on its circumcircle.

By Theorem
1.2
, the orthocenter
H
lies on a circle
\mathcal{C}(\hat{O},\hat{R})
, where
\hat{O}
is the reflection of
O
in the center of the conic and
\hat{R}=\dfrac{d_{+}d_{-}}{R}
, with
d_{\pm}
denoting the distances from
O
to the foci of the conic. Thus,
H\in\mathcal{C}(O,R)\cap\mathcal{C}(\hat{O},\hat{R})
.

To determine whether such an intersection exists, consider the system

(2.1a)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(O,R)

\displaystyle:\ (x-x_{O})^{2}+(y-y_{O})^{2}=R^{2},

(2.1b)

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}(\hat{O},\hat{R})

\displaystyle:\ (x+x_{O})^{2}+(y+y_{O})^{2}=\frac{d_{+}^{2}d_{-}^{2}}{R^{2}}.

A direct computation shows that the existence of real intersection points is determined by the discriminant

(2.2)

\mathrm{disc}=-\bigl((R^{2}+d_{+}d_{-})^{2}-4R^{2}(x_{O}^{2}+y_{O}^{2})\bigr)\bigl((R^{2}-d_{+}d_{-})^{2}-4R^{2}(x_{O}^{2}+y_{O}^{2})\bigr).

In particular,
\mathcal{C}(O,R)
and
\mathcal{C}(\hat{O},\hat{R})
have a real intersection if and only if
\mathrm{disc}\geq 0
.

Case 1.
The center of the conic coincides with
O
. Then
d_{+}=d_{-}=c
, and (
2.2
) becomes

\mathrm{disc}=-(R^{2}+c^{2})^{2}(R^{2}-c^{2})^{2}<0,

since
R\neq c
(otherwise the conic degenerates by (
1.4
)). Hence, the two circles do not intersect, and therefore
H\notin\mathcal{C}(O,R)
.

Case 2.
One focus coincides with
O
. Without loss of generality, let
d_{+}=0
, so
d_{-}=2c
. Then (
2.2
) reduces to

\mathrm{disc}=-R^{4}(R^{2}-4c^{2})^{2}<0,

since
R\neq 2c
(again by (
1.4
)). Thus, no real intersection occurs, and
H\notin\mathcal{C}(O,R)
.

Alternatively, in this case
\hat{R}=0
and
\hat{O}=H
(see Corollary
1.1
). If
H\in\mathcal{C}(O,R)
, then
R=|OH|=d_{+}=0
, which contradicts the nondegeneracy of
\triangle ABC
.

In both cases,
H\notin\mathcal{C}(O,R)
, and hence
\triangle ABC
is not right-angled. Therefore,
\triangle ABC
is oblique.
∎

Lemma 2.1
.

Let
\triangle ABC
be circumscribed about a central conic with linear
eccentricity
c
and circumradius
R
.

(a)

If the circumcenter coincides with the center of the conic, then

3R^{2}>c^{2}.

(b)

If the circumcenter coincides with a focus, then

3R>2c.

Proof.

Let
O
and
H
be the circumcenter and orthocenter of
\triangle ABC
, respectively, circumscribed about a central conic
\mathcal{D}
.

The well-known identity

(2.3)

|AB|^{2}+|BC|^{2}+|CA|^{2}=9R^{2}-|OH|^{2}

implies that
3R>|OH|
.

It follows from the Theorem
1.2
and Corollary
1.1
:

(a)

If the circumcenter
O
coincides with the center of
\mathcal{D}
, then, by Theorem
1.2
, the distance between
O
and the orthocenter satisfies
|OH|=c^{2}/R
. Substituting this into the inequality
3R>|OH|
yields
3R^{2}-c^{2}>0
.

(b)

If the circumcenter
O
coincides with one of the foci of
\mathcal{D}
, then by Theorem
1.2
,
|OH|=2c
. Again, the inequality
3R>|OH|
implies
3R-2c>0
, completing the proof.

∎

Proposition 2.3
.

If
\triangle ABC
is circumscribed about a central conic whose center coincides with the circumcenter of
\triangle ABC
, then the conic is an ellipse. Let
R
be the circumradius of
\triangle ABC
and
c
be the linear eccentricity of the ellipse. Then the following statements hold:

(a)

If
R>c
, then every point on the circumcircle of
\triangle ABC
is a vertex of a triangle sharing the circumcircle of
\triangle ABC
and circumscribed about the ellipse. (Figure
1(a)
).

(b)

If
0<R<c
, then every point of the arc of the circumcircle lying in the exterior of the ellipse is a vertex of a triangle sharing the circumcircle of
\triangle ABC
and circumscribed about the ellipse (Figure
1(b)
).

Proof.

Without loss of generality, assume that the circumcircle
\mathcal{C}
of
\triangle ABC
is given by

(2.4)

\mathcal{C}:\;x^{2}+y^{2}=R^{2},

and let the central conic
\mathcal{D}
be given by (
1.3
), with its center at the origin. Since the center of
\mathcal{D}
coincides with the circumcenter of
\triangle ABC
, we have
d_{+}=d_{-}=c
, where
c
is the linear eccentricity of
\mathcal{D}
.

Substituting
d_{+}=d_{-}=c
into (
1.4
), we obtain

\beta=\frac{(R^{2}-c^{2})^{2}}{4R^{2}}.

Since
R\neq c
, it follows that
\beta>0
, and hence
\mathcal{D}
is an ellipse. Its semi-axes are given by

(2.5)

a=\frac{R^{2}+c^{2}}{2R},\qquad b=\frac{|R^{2}-c^{2}|}{2R}.

We now analyze the relative position of
\mathcal{C}
and
\mathcal{D}
.

Case (a):
R>c
.

In this case,

a=\frac{R^{2}+c^{2}}{2R}<R,

so the ellipse
\mathcal{D}
lies entirely inside the circle
\mathcal{C}

\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{D}=\emptyset

\mathcal{C}
lies in the exterior of
\mathcal{D}
(Figure

[
3

\mathcal{C}
is a vertex of a triangle inscribed in
\mathcal{C}
and circumscribed about
\mathcal{D}
. This proves part (a).

Case (b):
0<R<c
.

In this case, the ellipse
\mathcal{D}
intersects the circle
\mathcal{C}
. A direct computation shows that the intersection
\mathcal{C}\cap\mathcal{D}
consists of four real points

\displaystyle X_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\left(\pm\frac{(R^{2}+c^{2})\sqrt{(R^{2}+c^{2})(3R^{2}-c^{2})}}{4R^{2}c},\,-\frac{(R^{2}-c^{2})\sqrt{-(R^{2}-c^{2})(3R^{2}+c^{2})}}{4R^{2}c}\right)

\displaystyle Y_{\pm}

\displaystyle=\left(\pm\frac{(R^{2}+c^{2})\sqrt{(R^{2}+c^{2})(3R^{2}-c^{2})}}{4R^{2}c},\,\frac{(R^{2}-c^{2})\sqrt{-(R^{2}-c^{2})(3R^{2}+c^{2})}}{4R^{2}c}\right)

(cf. Lemma

Therefore, only those points of
\mathcal{C}
that lie in the exterior of
\mathcal{D}
can occur as vertices of triangles circumscribed about
\mathcal{D}

\mathcal{C}
and circumscribed about
\mathcal{D}

The proof is complete.

Corollary 2.1
.

Let a triangle be circumscribed about an ellipse whose center coincides with the circumcenter of the triangle. Then the triangle is acute (respectively, obtuse) if and only if its circumradius is greater (respectively, smaller) than the linear eccentricity of the conic.

Proof.

Recall that a triangle is acute if and only if its circumcenter lies strictly inside the triangle, and obtuse if and only if its circumcenter lies strictly outside the triangle.

First, suppose
R>c
. Since the center of
\mathcal{D}
coincides with the circumcenter
O
of
\triangle ABC
, and since
\mathcal{D}
is entirely contained in the closed disk of radius
R
centered at
O
(cf. Proposition
2.3
) and
\triangle ABC
circumscribes
\mathcal{D}
, it follows that the point
O
must lie in the interior of
\triangle ABC
. Hence,
\triangle ABC
is acute.

Next, suppose
R<c
. In this case, the ellipse
\mathcal{D}
extends beyond the circumcircle
\mathcal{C}
(cf. Proposition
2.3
). Since
\triangle ABC
circumscribes
\mathcal{D}
, the circumcenter
O
cannot lie inside
\triangle ABC
. Consequently,
O
lies outside
\triangle ABC
, and therefore
\triangle ABC
is obtuse.

The converse implications follow immediately by reversing the above arguments. See Figure
1
for an illustration.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\triangle ABC
be circumscribed about a central conic, one of whose foci coincides with the circumcenter of
\triangle ABC
. Then the following statements hold:

(a)

If the conic is an ellipse, then every point on the circumcircle of
\triangle ABC
is a vertex of a triangle sharing the circumcircle of
\triangle ABC
and circumscribed about the ellipse (Figure
2(a)
).

(b)

If the conic is a hyperbola, then every point of the arc of the circumcircle of
\triangle ABC

(2.6)

\mathcal{C}:\;(x-c)^{2}+y^{2}=R^{2}.

and

Corollary 2.2
.

Let a triangle be circumscribed about a central conic, one of whose foci coincides with the circumcenter of the triangle. Then the triangle is acute (respectively, obtuse) if and only if the conic is an ellipse (respectively, a hyperbola).

Proof.

Let
\mathcal{C}
be the circumcircle of
\triangle ABC
with center
O
and radius
R
, and let
H
be its orthocenter. Let
F_{\pm}
denote the foci of the central conic
\mathcal{D}
, and let
c
be its linear eccentricity. By assumption, one focus coincides with the circumcenter, say
F_{+}=O
, while the other focus is
F_{-}=H
.

Case 1:
R>2c
.

In this case,
\mathcal{D}
is an ellipse. Moreover, the ellipse lies entirely inside the circumcircle
\mathcal{C}
, so both foci
F_{+}=O
and
F_{-}=H
lie in the interior of
\mathcal{C}
. Hence, the orthocenter
H
lies inside
\triangle ABC
, which implies that
\triangle ABC
is acute.

Case 2:
R<2c
.

In this case,
\mathcal{D}
is a hyperbola. Then exactly one focus, namely
F_{+}=O
, lies inside
\mathcal{C}
, while the other focus
F_{-}=H
lies outside
\mathcal{C}
. Consequently, the orthocenter
H
lies outside
\triangle ABC
, which implies that
\triangle ABC
is obtuse.

Finally, if
R=2c
, then one focus of
\mathcal{D}
lies on the circumcircle
\mathcal{C}
; in particular,
H\in\mathcal{C}
. This would force
\triangle ABC
to be right-angled, but in this case
\mathcal{D}
degenerates and no triangle can be circumscribed about it, contradicting the hypothesis.

This completes the proof.
∎

Corollary 2.3
.

Let
\triangle ABC
be an oblique triangle circumscribed about a central conic whose center coincides with the circumcenter of
\triangle ABC
. If the semi-axes of the conic are
a
and
b

(a>b>0)
, then the circumradius of
\triangle ABC
is
a+b
(respectively,
a-b
) if it is an acute (respectively, obtuse) triangle.

Conversely,

(a)

If the circumradius of
\triangle ABC
is
a+b
, then every point on the circumcircle is a vertex of an acute triangle circumscribed about the conic.

(b)

If the circumradius of
\triangle ABC
is
a-b
, then there exist arcs of the circumcircle such that every point on either arc is a vertex of an obtuse triangle circumscribed about the conic.

Proof.

Since the center of the conic coincides with the circumcenter
O
of
\triangle ABC
, we have
d_{+}=d_{-}=c
. It follows from (
2.5
) that

(2.9)

R=a\pm b,\qquad|OH|=\frac{c^{2}}{R}=a\mp b,

where the upper (respectively, lower) sign corresponds to the case
R>c
(respectively,
R<c
).

By Corollary
2.1
, the triangle

3.3.
Explicit Formulas for the Steiner Inellipse and Circumellipse

In this subsection, we derive explicit analytic formulas for the
Steiner inellipse
and
Steiner circumellipse
by constructing an invertible affine map that sends an arbitrary nondegenerate triangle
\triangle ABC
to an equilateral triangle
\triangle A^{\prime}B^{\prime}C^{\prime}
.

Proposition 3.3
.

Given a nondegenerate triangle, there exists a unique ellipse inscribed in the triangle that is tangent to the midpoints of its sides (the Steiner inellipse), and a unique ellipse circumscribed about the triangle that passes through its vertices (the Steiner circumellipse). Moreover, the centers of both ellipses coincide with the centroid of the triangle.

Proof.

Let
\triangle ABC
be a nondegenerate triangle. We construct an affine map

T:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}^{2}
of the form

T\begin{pmatrix}x\\
y\end{pmatrix}=M\begin{pmatrix}x-x_{A}\\
y-y_{A}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det M\neq 0,

which sends
\triangle ABC
to an equilateral triangle
\triangle A^{\prime}B^{\prime}C^{\prime}
with

A^{\prime}=(0,0)
,
B^{\prime}=(1,0)
, and
C^{\prime}=\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)
.

Such a matrix
M
exists and is invertible since
A,B,C
are non-collinear.

Let
G=(x_{G},y_{G})
denote the centroid of
\triangle ABC
. The inverse map is given by

T^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
y^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}=M^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}x^{\prime}\\
y^{\prime}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}x_{A}\\
y_{A}\end{pmatrix}.

Then matrix
M
is given by

M=\begin{pmatrix}a_{11}&a_{12}\\
a_{21}&a_{22}\end{pmatrix}

where

\displaystyle a_{11}

\displaystyle=-\frac{3}{2}\frac{y_{G}-y_{C}}{D}\qquad a_{12}=\frac{3}{2}\frac{x_{G}-x_{C}}{D}

\displaystyle a_{21}

\displaystyle=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\frac{y_{A}-y_{B}}{D}\qquad a_{22}=-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\frac{x_{A}-x_{B}}{D}

and
D
is defined in (
3.1
). Therefore,
M
exists and invertible if and only if
\det D\neq 0
, that is,
A,B,C
are non-collinear.

The inverse of
M
is given by

M^{-1}=\begin{pmatrix}-x_{A}+x_{B}&-\sqrt{3}\,(x_{G}-x_{C})\\
-y_{A}+y_{B}&-\sqrt{3}\,(y_{G}-y_{C})\end{pmatrix}.

Let
\mathcal{I}^{\prime}
be the incircle of
\triangle A^{\prime}B^{\prime}C^{\prime}
, given by

\left(x^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(y^{\prime}-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}\right)^{2}=\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}\right)^{2},

and let
\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
be the circumcircle of
\triangle A^{\prime}B^{\prime}C^{\prime}
, given by

\left(x^{\prime}-\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2}+\left(y^{\prime}-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{6}\right)^{2}=\left(\frac{\sqrt{3}}{3}\right)^{2},

with center
I^{\prime}=\left(\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{6}\right)
.

A direct computation shows that

T^{-1}(I^{\prime})=M^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}\tfrac{1}{2}\\[2.0pt]
\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{6}\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}x_{A}\\
y_{A}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}x_{G}\\
y_{G}\end{pmatrix}=G.

Hence, the center of the image ellipses
T^{-1}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
,
T^{-1}(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})
coincide with the centroid of
\triangle ABC
.

Since affine transformations preserve tangency and ratios of segments, and since in an equilateral triangle the incircle is tangent at the midpoints of the sides, it follows that
T^{-1}(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})
is an ellipse inscribed in
\triangle ABC
and tangent to the midpoints of its three sides, and
T^{-1}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
is an ellipse circumscribed about
\triangle ABC
.

Uniqueness follows from the fact that an affine map is bijective and the circumcircle and incircle of
\triangle A^{\prime}B^{\prime}C^{\prime}
are unique. Therefore, the preimages
T^{-1}(\mathcal{I}^{\prime})
,
T^{-1}(\mathcal{C}^{\prime})
are the unique ellipses with the stated properties.

Theorem 5.1

(Angle-sum Invariant)
.

Let
\triangle ABC
be circumscribed about a central conic. Then the quantity

\sin^{2}A+\sin^{2}B+\sin^{2}C

is independent of the choice of the triangle in the family of triangles sharing the circumcircle of
\triangle ABC
and circumscribed about the conic if and only if the circumcenter of
\triangle ABC
coincides with either the center of the conic or one of its foci.

Proof.

Let
R
be the circumradius of
\triangle ABC
, and let
O
and
H
denote its circumcenter and orthocenter, respectively. By the law of sines,

\frac{|BC|}{\sin A}=\frac{|CA|}{\sin B}=\frac{|AB|}{\sin C}=2R,

which implies

\displaystyle\sin^{2}A+\sin^{2}B+\sin^{2}C

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{4R^{2}}\bigl(|AB|^{2}+|BC|^{2}+|CA|^{2}\bigr)

(5.1)

\displaystyle=\frac{9}{4}-\frac{|OH|^{2}}{4R^{2}},

where the last identity follows from (
2.3
).

We now analyze the expression in terms of the Poncelet family of triangles circumscribed about the given central conic and sharing the same circumcircle. Since
R
is fixed for the family, the quantity

\sin^{2}A+\sin^{2}B+\sin^{2}C

is constant if and only if
|OH|
is constant along the family.

By Theorem 3.4 in
[
3
]
, the distance
|OH|
remains invariant for all triangles in the Poncelet family if and only if the circumcenter
O
coincides with either the center of the conic or one of its foci.

The proof is complete.
∎

Theorem 5.2
.

Theorem 5.3

(Congruence of the Incircles of Orthic Triangles in a Poncelet Family)
.

Let
\triangle ABC
be an acute triangle circumscribed about an ellipse and let
\mathcal{P}
denote the family of triangles sharing the circumcircle of
\triangle ABC
and circumscribed about the ellipse. Then the incircles of the orthic triangles of the triangles in
\mathcal{P}
are congruent if and only if the circumcenter of
\triangle ABC
coincides with either the center of the ellipse or one of its foci .

Moreover, if the circumcenter of
\triangle ABC
coincides with one of the foci of the ellipse, then the incircle of the orthic triangle of
\triangle ABC
is independent of the choice of the triangle in
\mathcal{P}
.

Proof.

Let
\triangle H_{A}H_{B}H_{C}
denote the orthic triangle of
\triangle ABC
, and let
r_{H}
and
R_{H}
be its inradius and circumradius, respectively. It is well known that the circumcircle of the orthic triangle is the nine-point circle of
\triangle ABC
, and hence

R_{H}=\frac{R}{2}.

We apply the identity

(5.8)

\cos A+\cos B+\cos C=1+\frac{r}{R},

valid for any triangle, to the orthic triangle
\triangle H_{A}H_{B}H_{C}
. This yields

(5.9a)

\displaystyle r_{H}

\displaystyle=R_{H}(\cos\alpha_{H}+\cos\beta_{H}+\cos\gamma_{H}-1)

(5.9b)

\displaystyle=2R\cos A\cos B\cos C

(5.9c)

\displaystyle=\frac{R^{2}-|OH|^{2}}{4R},

where
O
and
H
denote the circumcenter and orthocenter of
\triangle ABC
, respectively.

Since
\triangle ABC
is acute, it follows from (
5.9c
) and Theorem 3.4 that
|OH|
is invariant within the Poncelet family
\mathcal{P}
if and only if the circumcenter coincides with either the center of the ellipse or one of its foci. Consequently,
r_{H}
is invariant in
\mathcal{P}
if and only if this condition holds, proving the first assertion.

For the second assertion, suppose that the circumcenter
O
coincides with one of the foci of the ellipse. Then, by Theorem
1.2
, the orthocenter
H
coincides with the other focus. Since
\triangle ABC
is acute,
H
lies in the interior of the orthic triangle and hence serves as its incenter. Therefore, the incircle of
\triangle H_{A}H_{B}H_{C}
is centered at a fixed point and has constant radius
r_{H}
, and is thus independent of the choice of
\triangle ABC\in\mathcal{P}
.

This completes the proof.
∎

\displaystyle T_{A}

6.2.
Dynamics of the Poncelet Iteration

The recurrence relations obtained in Corollary
6.1
naturally define a discrete dynamical system on the space of Poncelet pairs. To analyze this system, it is convenient to introduce the dimensionless parameter

x_{n}=\frac{c_{n}}{R_{n}},

which encodes the relative position of the foci of the conic with respect to the circumcircle.

In terms of
x_{n}
, the recurrence reduces to the one-dimensional iteration

(6.2)

x_{n+1}=\frac{x_{n}(5-4x_{n}^{2})}{2(1-4x_{n}^{2})}.

The map (
6.2
) has the following properties:

•

The only real fixed point is
x=0
, corresponding to the degenerate case when the conic becomes a circle.

•

The map has a singularity at
x=\tfrac{1}{2}
, which corresponds to the critical configuration
R=2c
.

In the elliptic regime
0<x<\tfrac{1}{2}
, the sequence
\{x_{n}\}
is increasing and approaches the critical value
\tfrac{1}{2}
. Thus, the iteration drives the system toward a degenerate configuration of the associated conic.

Remark 6.1
.

The above construction endows the family of Poncelet pairs with a natural discrete dynamical structure. In particular, the boundary between elliptic and hyperbolic configurations, given by
R=2c
, acts as a limiting state for the iteration. This provides a new perspective on the geometry of Poncelet families through the lens of dynamical systems.
