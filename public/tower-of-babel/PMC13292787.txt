Combined use of red ginger extract and a cooling perch improves thermoregulation and performance of broilers in tropical semi-open housing☆
Authors: Syafwan Syafwan, Agus Budiansyah, Ucop Haroen, Yatno Yatno, Sarwo Edy Wibowo, Kiki Kurniawan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study evaluated the combined effects of red ginger extract and a cooling perch system on thermoregulation and performance of broiler chickens under chronic heat stress in tropical semi-open housing. A total of 198 broiler chicks were allocated to 18 floor pens in a completely randomized design with three treatments: control, red ginger supplementation, and red ginger combined with a cooling perch. Birds were exposed to daily ambient temperatures exceeding the thermoneutral range. The combined treatment significantly reduced cloacal temperature, water intake, and the heterophil/lymphocyte ratio compared with the control group, indicating improved thermal status and reduced physiological stress. Growth performance did not differ significantly among treatments, while the feed conversion ratio was numerically lower, suggesting a trend toward improved feed utilization under heat stress conditions. These findings suggest that combining phytogenic antioxidant supplementation with conductive cooling may provide a practical strategy to improve thermoregulation and production efficiency in broilers raised under tropical conditions.

Introduction

Heat stress is a major constraint to broiler production, particularly in tropical regions where high ambient temperature and humidity impair thermoregulation and reduce growth performance (Brugaletta et al., 2022; Surai et al., 2019). Under these conditions, birds experience increased metabolic heat load, reduced feed intake, and compromised feed efficiency, leading to economic losses in commercial production systems (Oluwagbenga and Fraley, 2023). The situation is exacerbated in semi-open housing, where environmental control is limited, and birds must rely on physiological mechanisms that are often insufficient under high humidity conditions.

Oxidative stress is a key physiological consequence of heat exposure, contributing to mitochondrial dysfunction, increased reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and reduced cellular efficiency (Aryal et al., 2025; Oke et al., 2024). These disruptions negatively affect nutrient utilization and performance, highlighting the need for nutritional strategies that can improve physiological resilience under heat stress.

Phytogenic feed additives, such as red ginger (Zingiber officinale var. rubrum), are increasingly used as natural antioxidants in poultry production. Red ginger contains bioactive compounds, including gingerols, shogaols, flavonoids, and phenolic compounds that exhibit ROS-scavenging and anti-inflammatory properties (Akbarian et al., 2011; Haroen et al., 2024). These compounds have been associated with improved antioxidant status and performance in broilers exposed to environmental stressors (Al-Khalaifah et al., 2025; Kairalla et al., 2022; Wen et al., 2020).

In addition to nutritional strategies, improving heat dissipation is critical for maintaining thermal balance. Conductive cooling represents a practical approach to enhancing sensible heat loss in poultry. The avian foot is highly vascularized and can facilitate heat exchange when in contact with cooler surfaces (McQueen et al., 2023). Cooling perches have been shown to reduce core body temperature and thermal strain in poultry, supporting improved thermoregulation under heat stress conditions (Hu et al., 2016, 2021; Khalfi et al., 2024).

Despite these developments, most studies have evaluated nutritional and environmental strategies independently. Limited information is available on combined nutritional and environmental approaches that simultaneously target metabolic heat production and heat dissipation under practical production conditions. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the combined effects of red ginger supplementation and a cooling perch system on thermoregulation and performance of broiler chickens reared under tropical semi-open housing. It was hypothesized that the combination of phytogenic antioxidant supplementation and conductive cooling would improve thermoregulatory responses and production performance in heat-stressed broilers.

Materials and methods

All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Universitas Jambi and conducted in accordance with accepted standards for animal welfare.

Red ginger extraction

Fresh red ginger (Zingiber officinale var. rubrum) rhizomes were cleaned, sliced, and extracted in hot water at a 1:2 (w/v) ratio. The mixture was maintained at 60°C for 3 h to preserve heat-labile antioxidant compounds, in accordance with the principles of Mustafa and Chin (2023) for extraction. The aqueous extract exhibited high antioxidant capacity (95.64 mg/mL pre-study evaluation). During the experiment, the extract was supplied via drinking water at a concentration of 10 mL/L.

Birds and housing

A total of 198 unsexed Lohmann day-old broiler chicks were randomly assigned to 18 floor pens (11 birds/pen). Pens measured 2.0 m × 0.8 m × 1.5 m and were bedded with sand. Ambient temperature and relative humidity were monitored three times daily (07:00, 13:00, and 17:00) using a calibrated digital thermo-hygrometer, following microclimate monitoring procedures described by Hu et al. (2016). During the study, ambient temperature and relative humidity at 07:00, 13:00, and 17:00 averaged 27.3 ± 0.9°C and 82.14±4.1%, 31.3 ± 1.0°C and 63.5 ± 6.0%, and 30.8 ± 1.4°C and 65.5 ± 8.8%, respectively.

An artificial lighting program of 18L:6D was applied using an individual bulb in each pen. Birds were managed according to standard commercial broiler management practices.

Cooling perch system

The cooling perch system consisted of two galvanized metal pipes (5 m length, 5 cm above floor, 15 cm spacing). Pipes were connected to a RESUN CL-650 water chiller, which circulated chilled water at 20–25°C and ≈400 mL/min flow rate. The system was designed to enhance conductive heat dissipation through the plantar surface, following the engineering principles described by Hu et al. (2016). The system was checked daily to ensure continuous circulation and temperature stability.

Measured parameters

Performance and thermoregulatory responses

Performance variables (feed intake, water intake, body weight gain, FCR) were recorded weekly. Cloacal temperature, which is used as an indicator of core body temperature, was measured from four birds in each replicate using a digital veterinary thermometer (±0.1°C accuracy). Measurements were conducted at 13:00, corresponding to peak daily heat load, to capture maximal thermal strain, consistent with thermophysiology protocols used in heat-stress research.

Hematological indicators

From each replicate, four birds were sampled at day 14 (since the cool perches were applied after day 14) and day 35 for the heterophil/lymphocyte (H/L) ratio.

Blood samples were taken twice, at 14 and 35 days of age, from the wing veins of each pen's birds. Within two minutes of the chick being captured, blood was drawn from four birds that were randomly removed from each pen. The birds were not marked, and during the subsequent sampling, they were again selected at random. Syringe-needle assemblies that had been cleaned with an EDTA solution were used to collect blood samples of around 1 mL at day 14 and 3 mL at day 35 from each bird. For every broiler, a single blood smear was made and preserved with methanol. The smears were then instantly stained with 100% Wright's stain, washed with distilled water, and allowed to air dry. From 100 cells per slide, the heterophil/lymphocyte (H/L) ratio was measured and categorized using oil immersion microscopy (Syafwan et al., 2012).

Analyses followed standardized physiological procedures described by Brugaletta et al. (2022). Blood samples were collected between 09:00 and 11:00 h to minimize circadian variation in hormone levels.

Statistical analysis

Performance data were analyzed using the MIXED procedure of SAS (SAS Institute, Cary, NC), with treatment as the main effect and week as a repeated measure. Pen was considered the experimental unit. Covariance structures were selected based on Akaike’s Information Criterion.

The heterophil-to-lymphocyte ratio was analyzed using ANOVA with a completely randomized design. Differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Mean weekly feed intake in T1, T2, and T3 was 507.82, 536.43, and 503.20 g/bird/week, respectively, while average body weight gain was 330.79, 358.57, and 341.50 g/bird/week.

Discussion

The present study evaluated the combined effects of red ginger supplementation and a cooling perch system on thermoregulation and performance of broilers under tropical semi-open housing conditions. The results suggest that combining nutritional and environmental interventions improved thermal status, as evidenced by reduced core body temperature, water intake, and H/L ratio, while maintaining growth performance and a numerically lower feed conversion ratio. These findings indicate that the combined strategy was associated with reduced physiological stress under heat stress without compromising productivity.

Birds receiving red ginger extract alone showed higher body weight gain with similar feed intake compared with other treatments, suggesting that antioxidant supplementation supported growth under chronic heat stress without increasing feed consumption. This response is consistent with previous studies reporting that phytogenic additives can improve performance by enhancing metabolic efficiency rather than stimulating intake (Abdel-Moneim et al., 2021).

In contrast, the combination of red ginger extract and cooling perches resulted in comparable body weight gain despite numerically lower feed intake and a numerically lower feed conversion ratio. These findings suggest that the combined intervention supported productive performance while reducing the physiological burden associated with heat stress. This pattern suggests that conductive cooling reduced the need for excessive feed consumption associated with thermoregulatory demands. The notable reduction in feed intake, coupled with maintained body weight gain in the combined treatment, may reflect improved digestive and metabolic efficiency. Heat-stressed broilers commonly reduce feed intake as an adaptive response to minimize metabolic heat production (Nawab et al., 2018; Oluwagbenga and Fraley, 2023). Phytogenic additives—especially ginger—have been shown to stimulate digestive secretions, enhance gut motility, and improve nutrient digestibility, compensating for reduced intake during thermal challenge (Akbarian et al., 2011; Sohail et al., 2012; Utami et al., 2023). This mechanism may partially explain the numerically lower FCR observed despite lower feed intake. The observed treatment × week interaction further indicates that feed intake patterns changed with increasing thermal load as birds aged, supporting the importance of dynamic responses to heat stress in broilers (Abdel-Moneim et al., 2021; Hasheimi et al., 2013).

The reduction in core body temperature, water intake, and H/L ratio in birds receiving the combined treatment suggested improved thermal status and reduced physiological stress. These responses suggest that antioxidant supplementation and conductive cooling may have contributed through different physiological pathways, thereby improving thermal status under heat stress.

From a nutritional perspective, red ginger extract, rich in phenolic and pungent bioactives, likely improved antioxidant status and reduced oxidative stress, thereby enhancing metabolic efficiency and resilience in heat-stressed broilers (Kairalla et al., 2022; Oke et al., 2024; Wen et al., 2020). These bioactive compounds may further support nutrient utilization by enhancing antioxidant defences, reducing lipid peroxidation, and improving digestive function (Akbarian et al., 2011; Haroen et al., 2024; Shaukat et al., 2023; Wen et al., 2020). This metabolic stabilization may have contributed to more efficient utilization of available nutrients for productive processes.

From an environmental perspective, cooling perches provided an additional pathway for sensible heat loss, particularly during later growth stages when metabolic heat production increases. Conductive cooling via the perch may have contributed to external heat dissipation through the unfeathered feet, reducing reliance on evaporative cooling. Lower cloacal temperatures in cooled-perch birds are consistent with increased conductive heat dissipation. Prior studies in layers and broilers demonstrate that cooled perches reduce core temperature, panting, heat strain, and behavioral signs of discomfort (Hu et al., 2016, 2021; Khalfi et al., 2024). Thermal imaging studies have shown that legs and feet act as major heat-exchange sites during heat exposure (McQueen et al., 2023).

The reduction in water intake observed in birds provided with cooling perches suggests reduced thermoregulatory demand and potentially reduced reliance on evaporative cooling mechanisms such as panting, which are less efficient under humid tropical conditions (Giloh et al., 2012; Oluwagbenga and Fraley, 2023). This shift may reduce physiological strain and contribute to improved energy utilisation. Panting increases respiratory alkalosis risk, disturbs acid–base balance, and diverts energy toward respiratory effort (Lara and Rostagno, 2013; Oluwagbenga and Fraley, 2023; Sahin et al., 2002). Conductive cooling may reduce these drawbacks while contributing to improved thermoregulatory status (Hu et al., 2016; Khalfi et al., 2024). These findings reinforce recent recommendations advocating combined environmental and nutritional strategies that support both cooling and metabolic resilience (Mangan and Siwek, 2024; Oke et al., 2024).

The progressive increase in H/L ratio observed in control birds is consistent with the cumulative effects of chronic heat stress, whereas the lower values in treated groups indicate reduced stress load and reduced physiological stress responses (Brugaletta et al., 2022; Lara and Rostagno, 2013). The combined treatment was associated with the lowest H/L ratio, supporting the potential value of combining nutritional and environmental strategies.

Overall, the results suggest that combining phytogenic antioxidant supplementation with conductive cooling may improve thermoregulatory responses and contribute to a numerically lower feed conversion ratio in broilers under tropical conditions. This combined approach may offer a practical strategy for mitigating heat stress in semi-open housing systems where environmental control is limited.

Limitations and future directions

This study did not include direct measurements of endocrine or oxidative stress biomarkers, and therefore, mechanistic interpretations remain inferential.

The absence of a cooling-perch-only treatment prevented direct assessment of the individual contribution of conductive cooling and formal evaluation of synergistic interactions. Future studies employing factorial designs including a cooling-perch-only treatment are required to clarify these effects.

Future studies should incorporate behavioural indicators, blood gas analysis, mortality responses, and additional immune-related markers to provide a more comprehensive assessment of thermal status and physiological stress responses. Further studies should also include broader production parameters, including carcass traits, to further optimise practical heat-stress mitigation strategies.

Only one supplementation level and one cooling-perch setting were evaluated; therefore, optimal dosage, threshold effects, and economic efficiency could not be determined. Economic cost-benefit analysis was beyond the scope of the present study and should be evaluated in future studies to determine practical feasibility under commercial conditions.
