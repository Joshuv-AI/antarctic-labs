Cardiac organoids in toxicology: from construction strategies and research trends to precision screening applications
Authors: Liya Qiao, Yabing Xu, Xinyu Wang, Yue Zhu, Tianyi Zhang, Yiru Niu, Meng Chen, Sheng Yang, Geyu Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCardiovascular diseases (CVDs) are the leading cause of death worldwide, with significant attention focused on the development of therapeutic drugs and research into pathogenic mechanisms. In the real world, cardiotoxicity induced by multiple factors commonly exists and is closely associated with cardiovascular diseases. Traditional toxicity testing models (such as animal experiments and two-dimensional cell cultures) have limited clinical translation value due to species differences, inability to simulate the complex microenvironment of the human body, and cellular interactions. Cardiac organoids, as an emerging three-dimensional (3D) culture platform, possess 3D structure, multicellular composition, organ-specific functions, and self-organizing capabilities, which can highly mimic the physiological and pathological characteristics of the heart, providing a more precise humanized model for the study of cardiovascular disease mechanisms, drug development, and toxicity assessment. This paper systematically reviews the construction strategies of cardiac organoids and their application progress in toxicology: Firstly, it explores the construction standards and technical optimization of cardiac organoids, focusing on human-engineered heart tissue (hEHT) models and various cardiac organoid models that mimic the human heart, with particular attention to their unique characteristics, utility, and limitations. Secondly, through bibliometric analysis using CiteSpace, it reveals research hotspots and trends in cardiac organoid applications for toxicology, specifically by extracting collaboration networks, conducting co-citation analysis, and co-occurrence analysis. Finally, it summarizes specific applications of cardiac organoids in toxicology, including drug toxicity assessment, environmental pollutant toxicity evaluation, cardiac developmental toxicity studies, disease model construction, and multi-organ cascade studies for systemic toxicity assessment.Keywords: Cardiac organoids, Toxicology, Bibliometric analysis

Introduction

According to the 2024 Global Health Statistics released by the WHO, cardiovascular diseases remain the leading cause of death worldwide and have held the top position since 2000. Data from 2019 shows that 17.9 million people died from cardiovascular diseases globally, accounting for 32% of all disease-related deaths.This harsh reality has prompted an increasing focus on the development of therapeutic drugs for cardiovascular diseases and the research into their pathogenic mechanisms.

Given that cardiovascular drugs typically require long-term administration and may induce unexpected toxic effects under complex physiological environments and multifactorial interactions, it is crucial to recognize that cardiotoxicity is not solely a drug-induced phenomenon. A multitude of non-pharmacological factors prevalent in the real world can independently compromise cardiac integrity or significantly modulate drug effects. For instance, environmental pollutants [1], lifestyle [2], and pathological conditions of the body [3] may all potentially cause cardiotoxicity. A comprehensive evaluation of cardiac safety, environmental compatibility, and systemic risk profiles considering both the intrinsic drug properties and its interaction with external and internal stressors, is imperative during preclinical development. The primary reason for the failure of newly developed drugs in clinical trials is cardiotoxicity [4, 5]. According to historical data, approximately 30% of novel drugs are discontinued in early-stage development due to cardiovascular complications [6]. Therefore, we need more accurate toxicological models that can capture this multifactorial reality to facilitate clinical translation.

At present, the commonly used methods for cardiotoxicity detection include animal experiments, traditional 2D culture and 3D culture.However, people are increasingly aware of the limitations of animal research [7]. Although the cardiac structures of animals are similar to those of humans [8], such studies are not only costly but also limited in their applicability to humans due to species differences, which result in significant variations in cardiovascular physiology. Consequently, the experimental outcomes often have limited relevance to human conditions [9].Traditional 2D cell culture systems also have issues, as the cells they produce cannot accurately represent the functional, transcriptomic, and proteomic characteristics of primary human cells [10]. Moreover, they cannot replicate the internal environment of the human body or reproduce the complex interactions between cells that occur in vivo [11].

To address these problems, cardiac organoids have emerged as an innovative 3D culture platform. According to previous research and definitions, organoids should meet the following criteria: (1) possess a 3D structure; (2) contain multiple cell types; (3) be capable of demonstrating certain specific functions of the organ; and (4) have self-organizing capabilities [12]. As 3D in vitro cell constructs, cardiac organoids can better simulate the in vivo structure and functional specificity of the heart [9], thereby providing a more human-like experimental platform for the study of cardiovascular diseases and drug development.

Currently, cardiac organoids have been applied in multiple fields. They are used to study heart development processes, such as the organoids proposed by Li et al. [13], which can simulate the high proliferation rate and lack of fibrosis observed in neonatal heart regeneration, physiologically representing the native immature human heart. They are also employed in disease modeling, such as the hiPSC-based short QT syndrome (SQTS) model established by Shinnawi et al. [14], providing mechanistic insights into the treatment of SQTS. Additionally, cardiac organoids are utilized in drug testing, as demonstrated by Skardal et al. [15], who used 3D organoid technology to screen a group of FDA-recalled drugs, with the organoids revealing toxicity for some of these compounds. Furthermore, they hold potential in regenerative medicine and therapy, with attempts already being made to replace organs using organoid technology, such as Nie et al.’s [16] use of liver organoids to treat mice with acute liver failure (ALF).

This article mainly introduces the construction of cardiac organoids, the bibliometric analysis of cardiac organoids applied in toxicology, and the specific application of cardiac organoids in toxicology.

Methods for constructing human cardiac organoids

Tissue engineering methods

At present, there are various methods for constructing cardiac organoids. Here, we mainly discuss three of them (Fig. 1; Table 1). Tissue engineering approaches combine tissue engineering techniques and biomaterials to create 3D models of tissues and organs [17].This approach consists of three main components: (1) primary cells, such as implanted cells and iPSCs; (2) scaffolds that support the cells; (3) active factors that regulate cell activity [18]. Commonly used biomaterials include hydrogels [17, 19], gelatin, sodium hyaluronate, and polylactic acid, among which hydrogels are the most widely used [20, 21]. Due to their high biocompatibility, good permeability, and appropriate stiffness, hydrogels can better simulate the internal environment of tissues and organs [20]. A variety of different types of hEHT have been produced, including strip, ring, patch, heart-on-a-chip, chamber, and so on [22].

The strip-shaped hEHT is obtained by compacting cells and hydrogels around two parallel wires or columns [22], and is mainly used to analyze myocardial contractility [23, 24] and perform drug screening. For example, Huebsch et al. [25] see the potential for large-scale production of hEHT from a small number of cells, allowing hEHT to be used for high-throughput drug screening.

The ring-shaped hEHT was fabricated by condensing the hydrogel with cells in a circular casting mold and then transferring it onto a silicon passive stretcher [26]. Compared with the strip-shaped one, the ring-shape reduces mechanical stimulation, and the hole in the middle also reduces the risk of necrosis [18].

The patch-shaped hEHT is a flat tissue shape, mainly composed of cell layers accumulated on the coating plate, which can be generated with or without a scaffold [27]. Its flat structure, which allows it to be transferred to the area of myocardial infarction for local repair, as experimentally by Nakane et al. [28], the reticular ECT model showed the lowest rate of dead cells. However, the experimental results also show that the cells are easy to be unevenly distributed, which has certain limitations in application.

Heart-on-a-chip hEHT is to inoculate cells in a chip with a complete microfluidic system [29]. In detail, utilizing microfluidic chip technology as the core platform, cells are precisely integrated with physiological microenvironments to achieve high-fidelity mimicry of organ-specific functional units in vitro [30, 31]. This technology can not only precisely regulate the microenvironment, but also accurately measure the effect of drugs on myocardial potential, which is helpful for drug screening. However, complex equipment also limits the wide application of this technology [32].

Compared with the several types of hEHT introduced earlier, chamber hEHT is rather special. Researchers attempt to simulate the natural heart by mimicking its 3D structure [33]. Of course, due to the high degree of artificial intervention in the chamber structure, there are certain differences from the real heart structure. At the same time, due to the lack of a vascular system, the possibility of cell necrosis is relatively high, and its current application rate is not high.

Self-organization methods

The self-organization method typically relies on stem cells’ autonomous organization process, utilizing cells with high differentiation potential and specific developmental signaling molecules to generate cardiac organoids [18]. This self-organization of stem cells involves three critical processes: (1) spatiotemporal changes in cell positioning, (2) spatiotemporal changes in cell states, and (3) alterations in cell morphology [17].

Organoids formed through self-organization more faithfully recapitulate the natural embryonic development process. They exhibit enhanced intercellular physiological interactions and provide better insights into cardiac developmental mechanisms.Lee et al. [34] developed a unique biomimetic model for cultivating cardiac organoids, enabling pluripotent stem cells to undergo embryonic heart development in a self-organizing manner. This model serves as an effective platform for studying congenital heart defects with structural abnormalities and contributes to evaluating the cardiotoxicity of newly developed drugs. Hofbauer et al. [35] established self-organizing cardiac organoids using hiPSCs that intrinsically specify, pattern, and form chamber-like cardiac structures containing cavities. Furthermore, through signaling mechanisms, they control the segregation of myocardial and endothelial cell layers, and direct the expansion, inward migration, and differentiation of the epicardium. This model facilitates the mechanistic dissection of self-organization and congenital heart defects.

However, the intrinsic self-assembly of cardiac organoids relies on interactions with non-cardiac tissues and specific extracellular matrices, as the natural cardiac developmental process involves stimulation and intervention from other cell types. Furthermore, current technology recapitulates only specific stages of heart development. For instance, the organoids generated in Hofbauer et al.’s study more closely model the first heart field, primarily yielding precursors to the left ventricle and partial atrial structures [35]. The relevant technologies can still be further improved.

3D printing methods

3D bio-printing technology can produce three-dimensional biomimetic and biological functions of cells and biomaterials according to the specific location environment and biological structure morphology, reproduce the cellular microenvironment, and simulate the target organ structure in detail. At present, 3D printing technology has been used to create perfusion vascular networks, as well as various heart vessels and valves [36]. It can combine induced cardiomyocytes with biomaterials to make ring and patch tissues based on cardiomyocytes. Noor et al. [37], for example, 3D printed a vascularized heart patch that matched the tissue structure and physiological characteristics of the patient’s heart, indicating the potential of this method in engineering personalized tissues and organs or drug screening in the appropriate anatomical structure and patient-specific biochemical microenvironment. In addition, Lee et al. [38] 3D printed collagen and wrapped it in hydrogel to simulate the design of the components of the human heart, and realized the synchronous contraction of the ventricle.

Bioprinting enables the fabrication of complex tissue constructs and holds substantial promise for organ engineering applications. Nevertheless, widespread adoption faces challenges due to the requirement for specialized instrumentation and stringent biomaterial specifications.

Biliometric analysis

Data sources and analysis methods

We searched the Web of Science Core Collection (WOSCC) as the most comprehensive database for scientometric analyses. We used " TS= ((“cardiac organoid*” OR “heart organoid*” OR “cardiomyocyte organoid*” OR “stem cell-derived cardiac model*” OR “3D cardiac model*” OR “in vitro cardiac model*” OR “engineered heart tissue” OR “cardiac microtissue*” OR “heart-on-a-chip” OR “iPSC-derived cardiac model*”) AND (“toxicology” OR “drug toxicity” OR “cardiotoxicity” OR “chemical toxicity” OR “environmental toxicity” OR “toxicity screening” OR “high-throughput screening” OR “safety pharmacology” OR “adverse drug reaction*” OR “toxicological assessment” OR “hazard assessment” OR “risk assessment”))” as the search term, and no language and time restrictions were set. Bibliometric analysis was performed on the retrieved documents, and duplicate items were eliminated with Citespace. Results A total of 185 records were obtained, including 119 articles, 48 reviews and 3 highly cited articles.

This paper chooses the 6.3.1 version of CiteSpace software as the main tool to conduct a comprehensive analysis of the selected literature. The full records until the present search time (March 2025) with the abstract and complete cited references of each paper were extracted from the WOSCC database into tab-delimited plain text files for analysis. In terms of data processing, the selection time is from 2016 to 2025, the time slice is 1 year, and the source selects all items, including title (title), abstract (abstract), supplementary keywords (ID) and author keywords (DE), select Keyword, Author, Institution, Country for the node, all calculations use the g-index and scale factor k = 25 is used for all analyses, and set the others as default values. Citespace was used to extract the collaboration network and conduct co-citation analysis and co-occurrence analysis.

Analysis of co-authorship network

Through the analysis of the number of papers published by the authors and the cooperation network (Fig. 2A), we found 282 authors who study the field of applications in toxicology of cardiac organoids. According to the Price formula, the authors who published more than or equal to 3 articles were the core authors in this research field. According to statistics, there were a total of 8 core authors, of which only 2 had published 5 or more articles, indicating that there were few high-yield authors in this field, and there was still a large amount of research space. Eschenhagen Thomas and Hansen Arne are two of the most published or influential authors in the field between 2016 and 2024. Their research focuses on the use of human engineered heart tissue for drug screening and cardiac toxicology related studies.

Analysis of cooperation networks across countries and institutions

Based on the analysis of 169 cardiac organoid toxicology-related papers using CiteSpace software, it is shown that the international cooperation network (Fig. 2B) in this field exhibits significant regional characteristics and structural differences. At the national level, among the 35 participating countries, the United States (61 papers) and China (29 papers) are the core contributors. The United States, with a high centrality of 0.38, serves as a global cooperation hub, establishing extensive connections with countries such as England and Germany. Although China ranks second in terms of the number of published papers, its international cooperation network is relatively limited, possibly due to field preferences or a one-way citation pattern. Within Europe, a close cooperation cluster centered around Germany and the Netherlands has formed, which may benefit from the EU’s research alliance mechanism. At the institutional level (Fig. 2C), among the 216 participating institutions, the Berlin Institute of Health leads the domestic cooperation network in Germany, forming a regional collaborative alliance with the University of Hamburg and the German Center for Cardiovascular Research, etc. Meanwhile, Maastricht University has established cooperation with international top institutions such as Harvard University and the Chinese Academy of Sciences through interdisciplinary projects, highlighting a mixed model of “regional clusters + international scattered points”.

Overall, the current cooperation network presents a pattern of “domination by the United States, aggregation in Europe, and partial breakthroughs in China”. In the future, policy guidance is needed to strengthen cross-regional connections, especially the collaborative potential between China and the “Belt and Road” countries as well as emerging scientific research forces, in order to promote the balance and innovation efficiency of the global research network.

Keyword analysis

Keyword co-occurrence analysis (Fig. 3A) helps identify core themes and their relationships within a research field. High-frequency co-occurring keywords typically represent hotspots or important directions in the domain. The analysis revealed that the top ten keywords include“cardiomyocytes, maturation, in vitro, cell derived cardiomyocytes, engineered heart tissue, pluripotent stem cells, cardiotoxicity, differentiation, heart failure, heart”, with “cardiomyocytes” being the largest node exhibiting a complex connection network. Based on keyword frequency patterns, current research remains predominantly focused on methodological studies encompassing cardiomyocyte maturation and in vitro model construction. In terms of applications, the primary emphasis lies in drug toxicity screening and disease modeling.

Timeline analysis (Fig. 3C) reveals: While cardiomyocyte research (#0) remains foundational, the field’s focus is shifting toward complex model construction (organoids, 3D models) and application scenarios (drug screening, heart failure). Concurrently, the trend of technological convergence has become increasingly prominent, highlighting the need for interdisciplinary integration across biology, engineering, and clinical medicine. Future breakthroughs are likely to emerge in vascularization, functional maturation (e.g., electrophysiological-contractile coupling), and scalable production technologies.

Based on bibliometric analysis, cardiac organoid research has established a progressive developmental trajectory characterized by “fundamental technological breakthroughs → precision medicine applications → clinical translation exploration”. The current international collaboration network exhibits a heterogeneous pattern of “U.S. dominance, European consolidation, and Chinese breakthroughs”, while the limited cohort of core authors (8 high-yield researchers) and low institutional collaboration density (0.0141) collectively indicate that this field remains in its rapid growth phase. The evolution of research hotspots reveals distinct technology-driven characteristics: the early phase concentrated on engineered myocardial construction (2016–2020), the intermediate phase shifted toward pluripotent stem cell differentiation and disease modeling (2020–2022), and recent advancements have accelerated convergence on translational technologies such as microtissue engineering and organ-on-a-chip systems (2023–2025). Notably, keyword burst analysis identifies “cardiac fibrosis” (burst strength: 9.32) and “tissue engineering” (burst strength: 7.85) as emerging frontiers, suggesting that pathological mechanism deciphering and functional restoration are becoming new growth engines.

Application of cardiac organoids in toxicology studies

Assessment of drug cardiotoxicity

In the drug development process, drug candidates are often eliminated at an early stage due to inadequate efficacy or safety concerns, including cardiotoxicity [41–43]. Among them, drug-induced cardiotoxicity has become one of the key factors affecting the success rate of drug development and clinical conversion potential [44, 45].Therefore, the construction and application of cardiac organoid model to evaluate drug cardiotoxicity has become an important direction of current research.

A large number of articles have demonstrated the feasibility of cardiac tissue and organ technology in the assessment of drug-induced cardiac toxicity, evaluating aspects such as calcium transient monitoring [46], responses to major channel blockers and other drug interference [47, 48]. The effects produced by drugs through cellular changes, such as binding of cell surface receptors [49], mitochondrial damage [50, 51], oxidative stress [52], Ca2 + handling [48, 53], etc.

Moreover, chip-based cardiac models [54] and gold microfolded cantilever devices [55] provide a more physiologically relevant experimental platform for the assessment of drug-induced cardiac toxicity. At the application level, cardiac tissue and organ models have become an important platform for studying the mechanism of cardiac toxicity and drug safety assessment. Current research mainly focuses on the following aspects: Liang et al. [56] constructed vascularized cardiac organoids (Fig. 4A) with an endothelial-lined cavity structure through the Wnt signaling pathway. They also confirmed that this model could detect the cardiotoxicity induced by temozolomide, and that the vascularized organoids exhibited a more sensitive toxicity response and higher physiological relevance compared to the simple myocardial cell organoids (Fig. 4B). Archer et al. [57] constructed a three-cell 3D cardiac micro-tissue model (Fig. 4C) and systematically evaluated the cardiac toxicity of various FDA-approved drugs. They confirmed that this model could accurately detect myocardial damage induced by drugs such as doxorubicin and sunitinib, and identify the specific toxicity mechanism fingerprints of different drug categories, such as lapatinib preferentially damaging mitochondria and amphotericin B destroying the endoplasmic reticulum, etc. It has good sensitivity and specificity (Fig. 4D).Yang et al. [58] constructed vascularized chamber-like cardiac organoids (vcCOs) (Fig. 4E), and demonstrated that this model could replicate the cardiotoxicity induced by doxorubicin in a dose- and time-dependent manner, including decreased cell viability, reduced ATP production, and increased cell apoptosis. They also verified that captopril has a significant protective effect in alleviating cardiac injury-induced fibrosis and functional impairment (Fig. 4F). Mills et al. [59] established a high-throughput drug screening platform using self-assembled cardiac organoids (Fig. 4G). They discovered that inhibitors of the mevalonate pathway could promote the proliferation of cardiac muscle cells, and verified the activity of various known and new proliferative-promoting compounds. This provided a new strategy for cardiac regenerative medicine and drug screening (Fig. 4H). Beck et al. [60] utilized cardiac organoids combined with in vivo experiments (Fig. 4I) to elucidate that the cardiotoxicity induced by crizotinib has reversible characteristics, and revealed that it involves multiple molecular mechanisms such as immune toxicity, oxidative stress, downregulation of apelin receptor, and mislocalization of connexin-43. This demonstrated the significant value of cardiac organoids in evaluating the cardiotoxicity of targeted anti-tumor drugs and exploring the underlying toxicity mechanisms (Fig. 4J).

These studies have shown that cardiac organoid models possess higher physiological relevance, sensitivity and mechanism analysis capabilities in predicting the cardiac toxicity of chemotherapy drugs, targeted anti-tumor drugs and cardiovascular drugs. They provide a reliable in vitro evaluation platform for preclinical drug safety assessment and the formulation of individualized medication regimens.

Evaluation of cardiotoxicity of environmental pollutants

In addition to cardiotoxicity caused by drugs, environmental toxins, such as heavy metals, agricultural persistent organic pollutants, and other compounds such as nanoplastics, are also known as cardiovascular diseases. Next, this paper will introduce the application of cardiac organoids in the assessment of cardiac toxicity of environmental pollutants from the above aspects.

Heavy metal

Heavy metals have become one of the most important environmental pollutants because of their universality and persistence. In recent years, 3D cardiac organoid models have shown unique advantages in the toxicity assessment of environmental toxins, especially heavy metals. While traditional two-dimensional cell cultures and animal models have limitations in simulating human physiological responses, 3D cardiac organoids based on primary cells or induced pluripotent stem cells are able to more accurately reproduce the complex microenvironment of human heart tissue.

More recently, 3D bioengineered heart organoids have been used to assess the negative effects of lead, mercury, titanium, and glyphosate.As expected, these toxins had a negative effect on cardiac organoids. Interestingly, toxins also affect the beating rate of heart organoids. Exposure to TI led to the most significant reduction in heart rate in the heart organoids, followed by mercury, then glyphosate, and finally lead [61] (Fig. 5A).

It is worth noting that the cardiac organoid model has performed well in revealing the dose-effect relationship and mechanism of heavy metal toxicity. Compared with traditional models, 3D heart organoids can more accurately reflect human physiological responses to heavy metal exposure, and provide a reliable basis for formulating more accurate safe exposure limits.

Persistent organic pollutants

Persistent organic pollutants are toxic chemicals that can persist in the environment for years to decades. Exposure to persistent organic pollutants can have adverse effects on human health [62].

Studies using bisphenol A (BPA) and its analogues as examples have shown that cardiac organoids can effectively simulate the effects of low-dose environmentally relevant pollutants on the electrophysiological properties of the human heart [63] (Fig. 5B, C).The study found that acute exposure to 1 nM BPA significantly delayed the repolarization process of hiPSC-CMs by inhibiting hERG K+ channels, extending action potential duration (APD). In hiPSC-CMs with pacemaker function, BPA increases pacemaker rate by stimulating the If pacemaker channel. Notably, cardiac organoid models successfully revealed the pathophysiological dependence of BPA toxicity, causing only modest prolongation of APD in normal hiPSC-CMs, while rapidly inducing abnormal excitability and tachycardia like events in pathological models simulating Long QT syndrome.

Further studies have shown that BPA analogues, such as bisphenol AF, exhibit similar arrhythmic toxicity in cardiac organoids, which poses new challenges for the safety assessment of “BPA-free” products. These findings highlight the unique advantages of cardiac organoids in uncovering the mechanisms of pollutant toxicity, particularly in assessing the interaction of pollutants with cardiac pathologic states.

Nanomaterials

The human body is exposed to nanomaterials through a variety of ways, such as skin, gastrointestinal, respiratory and intravenous injection [64–68]. More and more data show that nanomaterials can be transported to organs such as the heart through the circulatory and lymphatic systems [69, 70]. Due to species-specific differences, it is difficult for traditional cell culture systems to accurately simulate the complex interactions between cells, and animal models cannot fully reproduce the physiological processes unique to humans. Human organoid technology breaks this limitation by bridging the gap between existing traditional cell culture and in vivo models.

A recent study systematically evaluated the effects of short - and long-term exposure to polystyrene nanoplastics (PS-NPs) on cardiac function using a cardiac organoid-on-a-chip model [71] (Fig. 5D). By integrating transcriptome sequencing technology, the mechanism of PS-NPS-induced cardiotoxicity was revealed, including key pathways such as oxidative stress, inflammatory response and apoptosis. In addition, the study also established a myocardial infarction model, combined with low-dose PS-NPs exposure, to explore the susceptibility of individuals to MNPs in pathological conditions, providing an important basis for understanding the toxic effects of MNPs in disease conditions.

Cardiac developmental toxicity study

Cardiac organoids serve as the core vehicle for constructing disease models of cardiac developmental toxicity, providing an exceptional technical tool for the assessment of cardiac developmental toxicity and the investigation of its underlying mechanisms in the field of toxicology. Currently, cardiac developmental toxicity studies rely on human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs)-induced cardiomyocyte cultures and animal testing [71, 72], but both have limitations due to structural [72, 73] or species differences [71, 73]. Cardiac organoids can mimic human fetal cardiac tissue in terms of gene expression, structure, cell populations, and early human cardiac developmental processes [74, 75]. They can mimic multiple critical stages of human cardiac development to more accurately assess cardiac developmental toxicity [75]. This model enables the direct quantification of the disruptive effects of exogenous compounds on cardiac developmental processes, as well as the elucidation of toxic targets and pathogenic mechanisms, thereby providing a human physiologically relevant core research system for developmental toxicity risk assessment, compound safety evaluation, and screening of targets for toxicity prevention and control in the field of toxicology. Ronaldson-Bouchard et al. [76] mimicked healthy mothers by modifying the culture conditions of cardiac organoids (normal glucose conditions: 3.5 mM glucose and 170 pM insulin) and mothers with pregestational type I/II diabetes (hyperglycemic conditions: 11.1 mM glucose and 1.14 nM insulin), in order to assess the effects of gestational diabetes mellitus (PGD) on cardiac development. Hoang et al. [77] utilized cardiac organoids to study the cardiac developmental toxicity risk categories of medications, such as thalidomide, vinpocetine, phenytoin, lithium, doxycycline and retinoids and compared with zebrafish whole embryo culture (zWEC), rifampicin, doxycycline and thalidomide were found to be significantly different in these two model systems (Fig. 6A–C). Wu et al. [78] evaluated the effects of cadmium on different stages of cardiac development (mesoderm, cardiac induction, and cardiac function) by using in vitro models of early embryonic development and cardiac organoids that mimic early cardiac development (Fig. 6D, E). Forsythe et al. [61] demonstrated that cardiac organoids can help to recognize the cardiotoxicity of environmental toxins (such as thallium and mercury) during postnatal developmental stages in humans. These findings suggest the utility of cardiac organoids in the study of cardiac developmental toxicity.

Disease model construction

Cardiac organoids provide a new platform for studying cardiac disease phenotypes. Traditionally, conventional cardiac disease phenotyping has relied on two-dimensional cultured human pluripotent stem cell-derived cardiomyocyte models [61] or cardiac tissue models composed of CMs (psc-cms) [22]. However, non-myocytes of cardiac tissue models are unable to accurately generalize cardiogenesis in vivo due to defects such as the lack of maturation of cardiac tissue models composed only of CMs compared to the natural heart [79] and the fact that cardiac tissue models usually lack the cellular heterogeneity present in the heart [80]. Cardiac organoids, on the other hand, are generated through a self-organizing developmental program that better preserves cellular biology and function in vivo to more accurately mimic cardiac-associated disease phenotypes [81–83]. Richards et al. [84] combined the major nongenetic factors of myocardial infarction with cardiac organoids, which were subjected to a hypoxic chamber incubator, with the addition of 10% O₂ and 1 µM norepinephrine, to form an apoptotic cell death gradient to simulate an in vitro myocardial infarction environment in order to mimic the organotypic myocardial response of the heart-like organ to infarction (Fig. 7A, B). Zhang et al. [71] constructed a myocardial infarction model on a cardiac organoid chip using the combined treatment of hypoxia/norepinephrine and treated it with low-dose PS-NPs. They found that the combined exposure of low-dose PS-NPs significantly aggravated cardiac injury. It is indicated that the sensitivity of the heart to PS-NPs exposure increases under the disease state. Song et al. [85] subjected heart-like organs to hypoxia-induced ischemia and ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury under controlled culture conditions to mimic phenotypes similar to acute myocardial infarction (AMI). Voges et al. [86] succeeded in simulating acute myocardial infarction by cryo-injuring localized tissues of the heart-like organ to mimic acute Myocardial infarction. Zhao et al. [23] designed electrophysiologically distinct atrial and ventricular tissues with lumen-specific drug responses and gene expression by combining directed cell differentiation with electric field modulation and demonstrated their spatially restricted responses to serotonin and ranolazine. Fan et al. [87] developed a 3D spherical model capable of effectively replicating key features of cardiac fibrosis, which can simulate hypertensive heart disease in vitro. And demonstrate the prospects of QSYQ in treating salt-sensitive hypertensive cardiac injury by targeting hypertrophic and fibrotic pathways (Fig. 7D).Yin et al. [88] established a liver organoid and cardiac organoid co‑culture system to model metabolic‑dependent myocardial injury, demonstrating that clomipramine metabolites generated by liver organoids induced cardiomyocyte dysfunction, including decreased beating rate and calcium handling abnormalities (Fig. 7C). These studies suggest that the use of cardiac organoids to mimic cardiac disease could offer promising prospects for preclinical therapy.

Multi-organ chips and systemic toxicity studies

The development of models of human relevance has been driven by the lack of predictive power of existing preclinical models [89–91]. Traditional research methods tend to study human tissues in isolation, however, in the real human environment, tissues do not exist independently but are in a highly integrated and dynamically interacting complex system. Therefore, it is difficult to fully reflect the true effects of a drug or treatment in the human body by studying a single organ (e.g., the heart) in isolation [31]. This limitation has prompted researchers to seek in vitro models that are closer to the human physiological environment. Conventional two-dimensional cell cultures are unable to reproduce the three-dimensional structure of tissues, while animal models have significant deviations in their predictions from human responses due to species differences [92, 93]. In this context, multi-organ-on-a-chip (MOC) technology is uniquely suited for its ability to simulate multi-organ interactions [93–95]. Such microfluidic platforms achieve a high degree of biomimetic simulation of tissue-tissue interfaces and inter-organ communication through precise control of fluid flow and mechanical forces [94, 95]. Yin et al. [92] developed a liver-heart organ-on-a-chip system based on human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs). The design of the system consists of functional culture chambers separated by porous membranes: the upper porous chamber is used to culture 3D human liver organoids, while the bottom is equipped with a micropillar array for cardiac organoids, which achieves dual-organ co-culture and allows researchers to assess the toxic effects of drug (e.g., antidepressant) metabolites on cardiac tissues directly after mimicking the metabolism process of the liver in vitro. Peng et al. [96] have developed a arrayed brain-heart microarray system. Each unit of the system contains an open organoid culture chamber surrounded by microfluidic channels that mimic vascular structures, enabling efficient transport of nutrients and metabolic wastes and providing an ideal platform for studying molecular communication between organoids. The feasibility of bi-directional communication between brain-like organs and heart-like organs via soluble factors was confirmed in vitro, which opens up new avenues for studying the mechanism of action of the neuro-cardiac axis under physiological and pathological conditions.

Outlooks

The paradigm of toxicological research is undergoing a revolutionary transition, shifting from traditional animal experiments toward predictive human organoid systems. While cardiac organoids have demonstrated unique advantages in drug risk assessment by circumventing interspecies biases and enabling high-throughput screening, their widespread adoption in industrial toxicology faces critical hurdles. As highlighted in previous sections, current models often suffer from limited cardiomyocyte maturity, lack of vascularization, and difficulties in long-term functional monitoring. However, beyond these technical limitations, the most pressing bottleneck lies in the lack of standardization, reproducibility, and robust quality control (QC) systems, which directly impacts the reliability and comparability of toxicity data.

Standardization as the foundation for toxicological screening

To truly translate cardiac organoid research models into industrial applications, establishing a comprehensive standardization system is an urgent priority. This system must focus on three core dimensions—cell source and differentiation, microenvironment and culture conditions, and evaluation metrics—to achieve uniformity in experimental operating protocols.

Regarding cell source and differentiation, research protocols must strictly define quality control criteria for human pluripotent stem cells (hPSCs) and unify cardiomyocyte differentiation strategies [99, 100]. Specifically, precise temporal regulation of key signaling pathways such as Wnt, BMP, and FGF, combined with standardized culture media formulations, is critical for minimizing experimental variability caused by fluctuations in exogenous chemical signals, thereby ensuring stable differentiation efficiency [101, 102].

Standardizing the microenvironment and culture conditions directly addresses challenges related to insufficient maturation and limited growth size in cardiac organoids. To this end, operational parameters for physical cues—such as electrical and mechanical stimulation—and protocols for dynamic culture systems like bioreactors must be harmonized [103]. Furthermore, core specifications for biomaterial scaffolds, including porosity and degradation rates, need to be established to prevent structural heterogeneity arising from material discrepancies.

Finally, the standardization of evaluation metrics centers on building a unified consensus for defining cardiac toxicity endpoints. For widely used detection technologies such as Micro-Electrode Arrays (MEA) and calcium imaging, standardized operating procedures must be formulated to assess core toxicity indicators, including hERG channel inhibition, calcium transient abnormalities, and apoptosis rates [104, 105]. This ensures data comparability across different laboratories, significantly enhancing the reference value of experimental results.

Enhancing reproducibility through precision control

Inter-batch variability remains a primary obstacle preventing cardiac organoids from transitioning from research to application. To address this, future research must prioritize reducing heterogeneity at the molecular, cellular, and tissue levels. By implementing multi-dimensional precision control, experimental reproducibility can be significantly improved.

Precise modulation of developmental signaling pathways is a critical strategy for enhancing reproducibility. Compared to traditional experience-based methods, more accurate regulatory strategies—such as the “three-step Wnt modulation protocol”—have been proven to successfully generate cardiac organoids with stable chamber structures and well-developed sarcomeric organization [106]. This approach substantially reduces experimental variations caused by deviations in signal regulation.

The stability of cellular composition within organoids also directly impacts their functional performance. The random distribution of different cell types, such as cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells, and fibroblasts, can lead to inconsistent functional outputs. Techniques like directed co-differentiation and fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) can effectively stabilize the proportions of various cell types within the organoids [107, 108].

Furthermore, advancements in manufacturing technologies provide essential technical support for improving reproducibility. Transitioning from traditional manual methods, such as the hanging drop technique, to automated, high-throughput manufacturing technologies like 3D bioprinting and microfluidics not only enables the scalable production of cardiac organoids but also ensures high uniformity in their size and morphology [109, 110]. This lays a solid technical foundation for industrial-scale applications of organoids, such as drug screening.

Establishing a multi-dimensional quality control (QC) framework

To achieve reliable toxicological assessment using cardiac organoids, it is essential to establish a rigorous quality control system that spans the entire lifecycle of organoid cultivation, ensuring quality and performance are managed across multiple dimensions.

At the morphological and cellular levels, quality control can leverage image analysis techniques to examine structural regularity and chamber formation. Simultaneously, flow cytometry should be employed to verify the expression of key cellular markers such as cTnT and CD31, thereby determining whether morphological construction and cell differentiation meet experimental requirements.

Quality control at the molecular and functional levels is equally critical. On one hand, technologies like single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) can be used to detect the expression levels of maturity markers, including NKX2-5 and various ion channels, to validate the molecular maturity of the organoids. On the other hand, functional assessment tools such as microelectrode arrays (MEA) and force sensors should be utilized to confirm that electrophysiological stability and contractile function meet established standards. Of particular importance is the monitoring of long-term stability indicators, such as cell viability and necrosis rates; this is a fundamental prerequisite for ensuring the validity and credibility of results in chronic toxicity studies.

Future perspectives: from single organs to multi-organ cascades

While current research has largely focused on isolated cardiac organoids, the ultimate goal is to reflect systemic drug effects. The establishment of the aforementioned standardization and QC frameworks is the prerequisite for developing multi-organ cascade systems based on organ-on-a-chip technologies. Only with standardized, high-quality single-organ modules can we effectively integrate heart, liver, and kidney organoids to simulate complex metabolic interactions and systemic toxicity. By addressing the challenges of maturity, vascularization, and, most critically, standardization, cardiac organoids will evolve from promising research tools into indispensable platforms for next-generation precision toxicology and preclinical drug safety evaluation.

Conclusion

Cardiac organoid research has garnered significant attention and witnessed rapid advancements in recent years, providing crucial tools and novel perspectives for disease modeling, drug screening, and regenerative medicine. By utilizing cardiomyocytes derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells (hiPSCs), researchers have successfully modeled the structural and functional characteristics of human cardiac tissues, establishing a highly valuable platform for cardiovascular disease investigations. Current research efforts predominantly focus on optimizing cardiac organoid generation protocols, while the expanding applications in drug screening and toxicity evaluation are increasingly being applied alongside technological advancements (Table 3).
