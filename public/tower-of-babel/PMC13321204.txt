Application of HRM analysis for specific DNA-based identification of the Hero green cultivar in Garcinia schomburgkiana
Authors: Worapan Sitthithaworn, Wullapa Jittachai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGarcinia schomburgkiana Pierre (“Madan”) is an economically important plant in Thailand widely used in food and cosmetic products. The Hero Green (HRG) cultivar has attracted commercial interest because of its large fruit size and suitability for industrial processing. However, morphological similarity among cultivars complicates accurate identification. This study aimed to develop a rapid DNA-based method for discrimination of the HRG cultivar using DNA sequencing and high-resolution melting (HRM) analysis. Chloroplast (matK and rbcL) and nuclear ITS regions were amplified and sequenced from HRG, Kiew Song La (KSL), and indigenous Madan varieties. Sequence analysis revealed limited variation in chloroplast markers, whereas the ITS1 region contained a unique single nucleotide polymorphism specific to HRG. Based on this variation, a novel primer pair targeting ITS1 was designed for HRM analysis. The HRM assay generated distinct and reproducible melting profiles that clearly differentiated HRG from KSL and indigenous varieties, with significant differences in melting temperature (p = 0.0095). Principal Component Analysis and Hierarchical Cluster Analysis consistently separated HRG from other cultivars. The limit of detection (LOD) was determined to be 0.01 ng/μL. This HRM-based method provides a rapid, sensitive, and cost-effective approach for authentication of the HRG cultivar and may support quality control of Madan raw materials in food, herbal, and cosmetic industries.Keywords: Garcinia schomburgkiana, HRM analysis, DNA marker, Internal transcribed spacer

Introduction

Garcinia schomburgkiana Pierre (Clusiaceae), referred to as “Ma-dan” in Thailand, is native to Indochina (Thailand, Vietnam, and Laos). The leaves and fruits are commonly consumed raw or cooked and are traditionally been used for constipation, cough relief, and as antidiabetic.1, 2 Phytochemical studies have identified xanthones and flavonoids from various plant parts, several of which exhibit cytotoxic activity against cancer cell lines.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 The leaves and fruits are rich in phenolics, flavonoids, and xanthones, supporting their antioxidant potential.12, 13, 14 In addition, the fruits show α-glucosidase inhibitory activity and contain hydroxycitric acid, suggesting potential applications in diabetes management and weight control.15, 16, 17

In Thailand, Madan fruits available on the market are either harvested from wild plants or cultivated on plantations. Nakonnayok Province serves as a central hub for Madan agriculture and has significant support from local authorities. Initiatives such as the establishment of the Madan Community Enterprise Group have encouraged the expansion of Madan plantations and product development, offering economic incentives to local farmers. The indigenous Madan variety has been improved through selective breeding, with a focus on key traits such as early fruiting, high yield, large fruit size, and a relatively straight fruit shape (Fig. 1). Propagation was carried out through grafting and budding techniques. This breeding process resulted in a Madan cultivar that differs from the typical indigenous plant, characterized by elongated fruits with rounded tips and bases and smooth green skin. The fruit size is notably larger than that of the commonly found indigenous Madan. The cultivar developer has named it “Hero Green (HRG)”, and it has been officially registered and certified by the Department of Agriculture, Ministry of Agriculture, Thailand. Additionally, another cultivar, known as Kiew Song La (KSL), has been developed and is distinguished by its relatively straight fruit shape and acute apex. Both cultivars represent significant advancements in Madan breeding aimed at improving fruit characteristics for commercial production.

Madan fruits, including the HRG cultivar, have attracted increasing interest because of their potential health-promoting properties. Their α-amylase and α-glucosidase inhibitory activities may delay carbohydrate digestion and reduce postprandial hyperglycemia.18, 19 In addition, inhibition of pancreatic lipase and cholesterol esterase may suppress lipid and cholesterol absorption, thereby contributing to reduced caloric intake and improved lipid profiles.17 Together with their antioxidant properties,18 these biological activities have supported the growing utilization of Madan fruits in dietary supplements and weight-management products. Among the available cultivars, HRG has gained particular commercial interest because its large fruit size is advantageous for industrial processing. Consequently, reliable methods for distinguishing HRG from KSL and indigenous Madan varieties are essential for quality control, cultivar authentication, and intellectual property protection.

Over the past decade, DNA-based authentication techniques have become increasingly important for verifying medicinal plant identity and preventing adulteration or misidentification that may compromise product quality and safety. Recent advances in DNA barcoding, particularly the use of markers such as rbcL, matK, ITS, and ITS2, together with multilocus approaches, have enabled robust species-level discrimination in herbal authentication studies.20 In parallel, developments in next-generation sequencing (NGS) technologiesand array-based molecular markers have improved analytical throughput and accuracy, allowing rapid screening of medicinal plants and complex herbal formulations.21

Among available molecular approaches, high-resolution melting (HRM) analysis has emerged as a powerful tool for cultivar discrimination.22 HRM detects subtle nucleotide variations in PCR amplicons based on differences in melting curve profiles, providing a closed-tube, post-PCR method that is rapid, cost-effective, sensitive, and highly reproducible. In medicinal plant research, HRM analysis has been successfully applied to identify soursop tea (Annona muricata),23 differentiate Andrographis paniculata from other members of the Acanthaceae family,24 and assess the purity of tea (Camellia sinensis).25 These characteristics make HRM particularly suitable for distinguishing closely related cultivars and detecting intraspecific variation in commercially important medicinal plants.

Given the increasing industrial and commercial value of Madan fruits, the development of accurate molecular methods for cultivar identification is crucial to ensure product authenticity, maintain quality standards, and protect cultivar intellectual property rights. Such approaches will also support the sustainable development of Madan cultivation in Thailand and provide valuable tools for future breeding programs and commercial exploitation. Therefore, this study aimed to differentiate the HRG cultivar from the KSL cultivar and indigenous Madan varieties using DNA-based analysis. To achieve this objective, novel primers targeting specific gene regions were designed for accurate amplification and subsequent HRM analysis. To the best of our knowledge, these primers have not been previously reported.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

The Hero Green (HRG) and Kiew Song La (KSL) cultivars of Garcinia schomburgkiana were collected from the Thasai Madan Community Enterprise Group in Nakhon Nayok Province, Thailand, where these cultivars were originally developed. Samples were obtained from three distinct plants of each cultivar. Identification of the HRG and KSL cultivars was confirmed by Mr. Supawich Punudom, who developed these varieties. Both cultivars are officially registered in the list of plant varieties that have received plant variety protection certificates from the Department of Agriculture, Thailand (Plant Variety Protection Division; https://www.doa.go.th/pvp/). Indigenous varieties were collected as detailed in Table 1 and identified by Dr. Pimpimon Tansakul (Department of Pharmacognosy and Pharmaceutical botany, Prince of Songkla University, Thailand). Herbarium specimens were deposited at the Faculty of Pharmacy, Srinakharinwirot University, with specimen codes provided in Table 1.

DNA isolation and nucleotide sequence analysis

Genomic DNA was isolated from 100 mg of fresh leaf or fruit tissue (as specified in Table 1) using the DNeasy Plant Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany) and subsequently used as a template for PCR amplification. Gene-specific primer pairs targeting the matK and rbcL genes, as well as the ITS region, were employed to amplify each locus. PCR amplification was carried out with an initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min, followed by 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 1 min, annealing for 1 min at the temperatures specified in Table 2, and extension at 72 °C for 2 min, with a final extension step at 72 °C for 10 min. The resulting PCR amplicons were purified using the Universal DNA Purification Kit (Tiangen, China) and submitted to Macrogen (Korea) for bidirectional sequencing. The obtained sequences were edited and aligned using BioEdit Sequence Alignment Editor26 and the Clustal Omega program (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/jdispatcher/msa/clustalo). Sequence identities were further confirmed through BLAST searches against the NCBI GenBank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/). A phylogenetic tree was subsequently constructed using Clustal Omega.

Primer design for HRM analysis

The ITS region was selected as the target for HRM analysis. Novel primers were designed by aligning the nucleotide sequences of the ITS region from Madan retrieved from the GenBank database and the sequences generated in this study. These primers were specifically designed to flank a DNA region containing a unique sequence specific to the HRG cultivar (Fig. 2). Amplification with this primer pair was expected to produce a 134 bp amplicon for each sample, containing a single variable site. The sequences of the designed primers have been submitted as part of a Thai patent application (Application Number: 2203003338).

Real-time PCR and HRM analysis

ITS amplicons from each sample were purified using the Universal DNA Purification Kit (Tiangen, China) and subsequently used for high-resolution melting (HRM) analysis. Real-time PCR reactions were carried out in a final volume of 20 μL containing 2 μL of genomic DNA (20 ng), 1× AccuMelt™ HRM Supermix (Quantabio, USA), and 200 nM of each primer (Table 1). Amplification was performed on a PCRmax Eco 48 real-time PCR system (Cole-Parmer, UK) with an initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 57 °C for 15 s, and extension at 70 °C for 15 s.

For HRM analysis, the amplified products were then denatured at 95 °C for 15 s and re-annealed at 55 °C for 15 s to allow random duplex formation. Melting curve analysis was conducted with a gradual temperature increase at 0.2 °C/s from 50 °C to 95 °C, interspersed with short pre- and post-melting steps. As the DNA denatured, the fluorescent dye was released, resulting in a measurable decrease in fluorescence intensity, which was continuously recorded by the instrument. Normalized melting curves and difference plots were generated using EcoStudy Software version 5.0, with melting temperatures (Tm) calculated from the negative derivative of fluorescence with respect to temperature (dF/dT). The ‘Hero Green’ cultivar was used as the reference sample for comparative analysis.

Limit of detection

The limit of detection (LOD) of the HRM assay was evaluated using ten-fold serial dilutions of the HRG ITS amplicon (0.1 to 0.00001 ng/μL). Five independent HRM assays were conducted at each concentration level. Successful detection was defined by the assignment of a melting temperature (Tm) within the established HRG reference range (80.13 ± 0.06 °C). The LOD was established as the lowest DNA concentration yielding correct species identification in ≥95% of replicate reactions.

Statistical and multivariate analyses

Significant differences between the mean Tm values of HRG and non-HRG cultivars were determined using Welch's t-test for unequal variances. Melting derivative profiles of samples S1–S10 were obtained from the negative first derivative of normalized fluorescence (-dF/dT) across the temperature range of 75–82.3 °C. The derivative values were used for multivariate analyses, including Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and Hierarchical clustering (HCA). PCA of the derivative melting curves was performed using a correlation matrix approach with GraphPad Prism version 11.0.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). HCA was conducted using OriginPro 2026 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).

Results

DNA sequences of matK, rbcL, and ITS

Chloroplast DNA regions, such as maturase K (matK) and the large subunit of ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase (rbcL), along with nuclear DNA regions, including the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) between ribosomal RNA genes, have been widely proposed as DNA markers for plant species identification.30 These DNA regions can be utilized either individually or in combination, depending on nucleotide variation.31 For G. schomburgkiana, the only available sequence in the GenBank database is the ITS sequence (GenBank no. AB110814.1). To explore potential DNA regions for the development of a DNA marker specific to the HRG cultivar, we sequenced the matK, rbcL and ITS regions from the collected samples. Universal primers amplified partial matK and rbcL sequences from multiple Madan sources, and sequence alignment revealed no variation among samples. BLAST analysis showed high similarity to other Garcinia species, and neighbour-joining trees based on pairwise alignments confirmed their phylogenetic relationships within the genus. ITS regions were also successfully amplified and exhibited high similarity to Garcinia sequences in NCBI Nucleotide BLAST searches, with phylogenetic relationships supported by neighbour-joining analysis. Phylogenetic trees showing relationships within the genus Garcinia, generated using the Tree View function of NCBI Nucleotide BLAST with the matK, rbcL, and ITS, sequences of the HRG sample as queries, are presented in Supplementary Fig. S1.

Notably, the HRG cultivar contained a unique nucleotide substitution in the ITS1 region (G instead of T) (Fig. 2), absent in other samples and distinct from the reference G. schomburgkiana ITS sequence (GenBank accession number AB110814.1). Phylogenetic analysis of the ITS region further demonstrated that the HRG samples formed a distinct branch in the tree topology, reflecting the presence of this single nucleotide variation, whereas the remaining samples exhibited highly similar genetic distances and clustered closely together (Fig. 3). This HRG-specific variation was subsequently exploited to develop an HRM assay for reliable cultivar discrimination.

HRM analysis for the specific identification of the HRG cultivar

The primers designed for HRM analysis were expected to generate a PCR amplicon of 134 base pairs, corresponding to the optimum length for HRM analysis.32, 33 Using primer prediction software (Sigma–Aldrich Oligo Evaluator; http://www.oligoevaluator.com/LoginServlet), the forward primer was determined to not form primer dimers or self-anneal, whereas the reverse primer exhibited the possibility for self-annealing. The predicted melting temperatures for the forward and reverse primers were 65.2 °C and 65.8 °C, respectively.

HRM analysis successfully amplified the amplicon in all the samples. Distinct melting profiles were observed, with the HRG cultivar exhibiting a unique melting curve compared with the KSL cultivar and indigenous Madan samples (Table 3). A significant difference in melting temperature (p value of 0.0095) was detected, indicating the presence of a specific sequence variation. Derivative, normalized, and difference melting curves (Fig. 4A, B and C) further confirmed the uniqueness of the HRG cultivar, which was consistent with the sequence variation identified through BLAST analysis. These findings validate the HRM method as a reliable tool for the specific identification of the HRG cultivar.

Limit of detection

The HRM assay reliably detected the HRG ITS amplicon down to a concentration of 0.01 ng/μL, with correct species identification obtained in ≥95% of replicate reactions. Principal component analysis and hierarchical clustering

The first two principal components accounted for 87.46% of the total variance, indicating that the PCA model provided a reliable representation of the dataset. PCA score plots revealed that samples S1–S3, corresponding to the HRG cultivar, clustered closely together, reflecting high similarity in their melting derivative profiles (Fig. 5A). In contrast, samples S9 and S10, classified as indigenous cultivars, were positioned within a similar PC1 region but were clearly separated from S1–S3 along PC2, indicating distinct variation and possible sub-group differentiation.

Principal component analysis (PCA) (A) and hierarchical clustering analysis (HCA) (B) based on melting derivative profiles for discrimination of the HRG cultivar (S1–S3) from other Madan cultivars. Analyses were performed using the negative first derivative of normalized fluorescence (−dF/dT) within the temperature range of 75–82.3 °C.

To further validate the grouping pattern observed in PCA, Hierarchical clustering was performed. The resulting dendrogram confirmed the close relationship among S1–S3 and demonstrated clear separation of these samples from the other cultivar groups (Fig. 5B). Moreover, the clustering pattern corresponded well with the melting temperature (Tm) profiles of the samples (Table 3). Samples S4–S8, which exhibited Tm values of approximately 80.0 °C, clustered within the same branch. Samples S9 and S10, with slightly lower Tm values of 79.8 and 79.9 °C, respectively, formed a separate branch, whereas samples S1–S3, corresponding to the HRG cultivar and exhibiting slightly higher Tm values (80.1–80.2 °C), clustered together in a distinct branch. These findings indicate that the HCA grouping pattern was consistent with the variation observed in the melting characteristics of the samples.

Discussion

The present study demonstrates the applicability of DNA-based markers for the discrimination of closely related Madan cultivars. DNA markers have long been recognized as reliable tools for plant identification and classification, particularly in taxonomically complex groups where morphological characteristics may be insufficient for accurate discrimination. Among the commonly used plant DNA barcodes, chloroplast regions such as matK and rbcL, together with the ITS region, have been widely applied in plant systematics and species authentication due to their universality and discriminatory capacity.34, 35 In the present study, sequencing analyses of the matK, rbcL, and ITS regions in Garcinia species, including the HRG cultivar, generated robust molecular data for species differentiation within the genus. The identical sequences observed in the matK and rbcL regions among different Madan sources, together with their high similarity to Garcinia sequences deposited in GenBank, support the usefulness of these chloroplast markers for genus-level identification. In addition, phylogenetic analyses based on BLAST pairwise alignments confirmed the close genetic relationships among Garcinia species.

Compared with the chloroplast markers, the nuclear ITS region exhibited substantially higher sequence variability and provided greater discriminatory power. A unique nucleotide substitution detected within the ITS1 region of the HRG cultivar, in which guanine (G) replaced thymine (T), served as a stable and reproducible genetic signature that was absent in the other Madan varieties examined in this study. This single nucleotide polymorphism enabled clear differentiation of the HRG cultivar and subsequently formed the basis for the development of a targeted HRM assay. Similar observations have been reported in previous studies, where ITS regions showed superior resolution for distinguishing closely related medicinal plant species and cultivars compared with chloroplast DNA barcodes, particularly at the intra-species or cultivar level.36

The novel primer pair developed in this study specifically amplified the ITS1 region containing the discriminatory nucleotide site, producing accurate and reproducible amplification profiles suitable for HRM analysis. The specificity of the primers was demonstrated by the consistent amplification behaviour and the clear separation of melting profiles between the HRG cultivar and the other Madan samples. These findings emphasize the critical importance of primer design in HRM-based genotyping, as successful discrimination depends on efficient amplification of sequence regions harbouring informative polymorphisms. Previous HRM studies on medicinal and herbal plants have similarly demonstrated that carefully designed primers targeting short polymorphic regions significantly improve assay sensitivity and genotype resolution.22

HRM analysis is recognized as a highly sensitive post-PCR technique capable of detecting minor sequence variations through differences in amplicon melting behaviour. In the present study, HRM analysis successfully differentiated the HRG cultivar based on its unique melting profile, which directly reflected the nucleotide substitution identified in the ITS1 sequence. The distinct melting curves and the statistically significant difference in melting temperature observed between the HRG cultivar and the other Madan varieties (p = 0.0095) further demonstrate the sensitivity and precision of HRM for single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) detection. The limit of detection (LOD) was determined to be 0.01 ng/μL.Comparable studies in medicinal plant authentication have also shown that even single-base substitutions can produce detectable shifts in melting behaviour, supporting the suitability of HRM as a rapid and cost-effective method for cultivar discrimination and quality control.22, 37

Multivariate analyses further strengthened the HRM findings. Both PCA and HCA consistently separated the HRG samples from the other cultivars based on derivative melting profiles. The close clustering of samples S1–S3 indicated high intra-group similarity and confirmed the reproducibility of the HRM assay. Moreover, the clustering patterns generated from PCA and HCA corresponded closely with the phylogenetic relationships inferred from ITS1 sequence analysis. The HRG cultivar formed a distinct branch in the neighbour-joining phylogenetic tree, whereas the remaining Madan samples clustered more closely together, consistent with their similar melting characteristics.

Interestingly, indigenous Madan samples S9 and S10 exhibited slight differences in melting temperature and melting curve behaviour compared with the other indigenous Madan samples, resulting in their separation into a distinct subcluster in both PCA and HCA analyses. These findings may suggest underlying genetic variation within the indigenous Madan group and raise the possibility that S9 and S10 represent a distinct cultivar or genotype. However, additional genetic and morphological investigations involving larger sample sizes would be required to confirm their taxonomic or cultivar status.

The agreement between phylogenetic analysis and HRM-based multivariate clustering suggests that even a single nucleotide variation can substantially influence melting behaviour and provide sufficient discriminatory power for cultivar identification. Similar correlations between phylogenetic relationships and HRM clustering patterns have been reported in previous DNA barcoding and HRM studies involving medicinal plants and agricultural cultivars.38

The sampling strategy employed in this study was designed to evaluate the distinctiveness of the HRG cultivar relative to other Madan plants collected from different locations within Nakhon Nayok Province. Although intra-individual variation was not assessed through sampling of multiple tissues from the same plant, three independent plants from each cultivar group were analysed to account for potential variation at the cultivar level. The highly consistent grouping patterns observed across sequencing, HRM, PCA, and HCA analyses support the robustness and reproducibility of the DNA-based identification approach developed in this study.

Despite the promising results obtained in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. The developed HRM assay was validated using high-quality DNA extracted from fresh leaf material under controlled laboratory conditions. The applicability of the assay to processed commercial products therefore remains to be further investigated. In processed herbal materials, including dried plant samples, powders, capsules, and finished supplements, DNA degradation, PCR inhibitors, and complex sample matrices may negatively affect amplification efficiency and melting profile resolution. Such factors could potentially reduce the accuracy and reproducibility of HRM-based identification. Similar challenges have been reported in previous studies involving DNA authentication of processed herbal products, where degraded DNA fragments and low template quality limited amplification success. Consequently, additional studies involving commercially processed Garcinia products are necessary to further evaluate the robustness, sensitivity, and practical applicability of the developed HRM assay under real-world conditions.

Conclusion

This study successfully differentiated the HRG cultivar from other Garcinia schomburgkiana varieties, including the KSL cultivar and indigenous Madan samples, using high-resolution melting (HRM) analysis. Sequence analysis identified a unique single nucleotide variation in the ITS1 region of the HRG cultivar, enabling the development of a specific primer pair targeting the discriminatory site. The developed HRM assay generated distinct and reproducible melting profiles, allowing rapid and reliable identification of the HRG cultivar. The consistency of the sequencing, phylogenetic, PCA, and HCA results further demonstrated the robustness and reliability of the developed method.

These findings highlight the potential of combining targeted primer design with HRM analysis as a rapid, sensitive, and cost-effective molecular tool for cultivar authentication and quality control of plant materials in the food, herbal, and cosmetic industries. In addition, the developed approach may contribute to intellectual property protection and industrial applications involving valuable plant cultivars. Furthermore, this strategy may serve as a framework for the authentication of other medicinal and economically important plant varieties, supporting advances in agricultural biotechnology and herbal product quality assurance.
