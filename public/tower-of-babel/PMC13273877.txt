Supranormal Ejection Fraction as a Clinical Red Flag: Differentiating Hemodynamic Stress From Intrinsic Cardiomyopathy in Heart Failure
Authors: Yanjia Chen, Xiuxiu Su, Jie Jiang, Jing Tang, Zeping Qiu, Wenbo Yang, Wei Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo show that heart failure with supranormal ejection fraction (HFsnEF, left ventricular ejection fraction [LVEF] ≥ 65%) is not a marker of cardiac health but a specific enrichment zone for malignant cardiomyopathies and to provide a phenotypic framework for differential diagnosis.Patients and MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 200 consecutive patients with HFsnEF admitted between December 1, 2017 and December 1, 2024. Patients were stratified into 2 phenotypes based on etiology: group A (hemodynamic loading, n=88), comprising valvular or hypertensive heart disease; and group B (intrinsic cardiomyopathy, n=112), comprising hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, cardiac amyloidosis, or Fabry disease. We compared their electromechanical profiles and event-free survival.ResultsDespite identical LVEF (median, 69%), the 2 groups exhibited distinct clinical signatures. Group A represented a classic secondary remodeling profile (older age and atrial fibrillation). In contrast, group B patients were younger (56.0 vs 68.8 years; P<.001) yet displayed severe diastolic stiffness (septal E/e' > 13) and a distinct electromechanical fingerprint (wide QRS or PR deviations). In a fully adjusted multivariable Cox model, the intrinsic cardiomyopathy phenotype was the dominant driver of the composite end point (adjusted hazards ratio, 3.91; 95% CI, 1.71-8.93; P = .001). Notably, N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide was not an independent predictor (P = .20) in this population, indicating that structural etiology overrides hemodynamic biomarkers in determining prognosis.ConclusionAn LVEF of 65% or more is not reassuring but signals underlying heterogeneity, often masking malignant intrinsic cardiomyopathies that masquerade as benign heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. A multimodal red flag approach—integrating clinical, imaging, and electrocardiographic data—is essential to trigger targeted evaluation, as standard heart failure with preserved ejection fraction management is insufficient for these high-risk phenotypes.

Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) has traditionally been defined by a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 50% or more.1, 2, 3 However, recent large-scale epidemiological analyses have revealed a clinical paradox: heart failure with supranormal ejection fraction (HFsnEF)—typically defined as LVEF of 65% or more—is associated with adverse outcomes comparable with, or even exceeding, those observed in typical HFpEF populations.4,5 This finding challenges the conventional dogma that higher left ventricular (LV) systolic function linearly correlates with a more favorable prognosis.

Crucially, HFsnEF is not a homogeneous entity. The supranormal LVEF often acts as a physiological confounder, masking significant etiological heterogeneity.6,7 We hypothesize 2 distinct phenotypes in HFsnEF: an acquired form driven by hemodynamic loading (eg, valvular disease, chronic hypertension, ischemia, or atrial fibrillation) and an intrinsic form driven by specific cardiomyopathies (eg, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy [HCM], cardiac amyloidosis, or Fabry disease).8,9 Current clinical guidelines, by treating all patients with HFsnEF uniformly, fail to account for the dramatically different natural histories conferred by these distinct etiologies.10,11

In patients with intrinsic cardiomyopathy, supranormal LVEF frequently represents a maladaptive geometric consequence of severe concentric hypertrophy and small cavity size, rather than true contractile reserve.12,13 This structural remodeling is often compounded by severe diastolic stiffness (eg, elevated E/e' ratio) and electromechanical desynchrony (eg, PR prolongation and QRS widening).14 Whether this synergistic failure of mechanical relaxation and electrical coordination drives the excess mortality risk in this specific subgroup remains to be elucidated.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to systematically validate this phenotypic dichotomy. We stratified a cohort of 200 patients with HFsnEF into intrinsic cardiomyopathy and hemodynamic loading groups to characterize their structural and electrophysiological disparities and to determine the differential prognostic significance of intrinsic myocardial disease in the setting of supranormal LVEF (Graphical Abstract).

Patients And Methods

Study Design and Population

We conducted a single-center, retrospective cohort study including consecutive patients hospitalized for heart failure at Ruijin Hospital between December 1, 2017, and December 1, 2024. Patients were eligible if they met the following criteria: (1) clinical diagnosis of heart failure according to current guidelines, characterized by typical symptoms and/or signs15; (2) LVEF of 65% or more on transthoracic echocardiography; and (3) elevated N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) levels (>125 pg/mL in sinus rhythm; >375 pg/mL in atrial fibrillation). To minimize confounding influences on natural history and baseline characterization, patients were excluded if they had the following: (1) prior history of major cardiac operation (eg, valve replacement/repair) or septal reduction therapy (alcohol septal ablation or surgical myectomy); (2) primary right ventricular cardiomyopathies (eg, arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy); (3) congenital heart disease, constrictive pericarditis, or acute coronary syndrome within 3 months before admission; (4) severe noncardiac comorbidities limiting life expectancy to less than 1 year (eg, active malignancy); and (5) missing key echocardiographic or follow-up data. A total of 200 patients formed the final analytic cohort. The study adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the institutional ethics committee.

Phenotypic Stratification

Patients were classified into 2 mutually exclusive phenotypic groups based on the primary etiology of heart failure at discharge: group A (hemodynamic loading phenotype): heart failure predominantly driven by external hemodynamic stress or acquired conditions, including hypertensive heart disease, valvular heart disease, and atrial fibrillation–related heart failure; group B (intrinsic cardiomyopathy phenotype): heart failure attributable to primary structural or genetic myocardial disorders, including HCM, Fabry disease, and cardiac amyloidosis. In cases with coexisting conditions, classification was based on the primary etiological driver; for example, patients with intrinsic cardiomyopathies complicated by secondary valvular dysfunction (eg, systolic anterior motion–related mitral regurgitation in HCM) were retained in group B, whereas those with primary severe valvular heart disease were assigned to group A.

Diagnoses were established using standardized multimodal criteria as follows:

Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy: unexplained LV hypertrophy (wall thickness ≥ 15 mm) on echocardiography or cardiac magnetic resonance imaging, after exclusion of loading conditions.16

Fabry disease: Reduced α-galactosidase A activity in males and/or pathogenic galactosidase α gene variants confirmed by genetic testing.17,18

Cardiac amyloidosis: Confirmed via endomyocardial biopsy (Congo red staining), grade 2/3 uptake on 99mTc-PYP scintigraphy in the absence of monoclonal gammopathy (for transthyretin amyloidosis), or laboratory evidence of light chain involvement for light chain amyloidosis.19

Multimodal Assessment

All echocardiographic studies were centrally analyzed offline by trained investigators. Key parameters including aortic root, left atrial (LA), interventricular septal thickness, LV posterior wall thickness, LV end-systolic diameter, and LV end-diastolic diameter—were measured. Left ventricular stroke volume (SV) was calculated as the product of the LV outflow tract (LVOT) area and the LV outflow tract velocity–time integral. To account for body size variability, the stroke volume index (SVI, mL/m2) was derived by dividing the SV by the body surface area. LVEF using the modified Simpson biplane method. LV mass index was calculated and indexed to body surface area. Diastolic function was evaluated with tissue Doppler imaging. Early diastolic mitral annular velocities (septal and lateral e') were obtained, and the E/e' ratio was used as an estimate of LV filling pressure. In atrial fibrillation, measurements were averaged over 5 to 10 consecutive beats to reduce variability.20

Electromechanical Profiling

Admission 12-lead electrocardiograms were analyzed. Electromechanical uncoupling was defined as ventricular dyssynchrony with a QRS duration of 120 milliseconds or more.

Laboratory Analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical data were extracted through structured review of electronic medical records, including age, sex, systolic blood pressure, renal function (estimated glomerular filtration rate [eGFR] using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula), cardiac troponin I, and NT-proBNP levels.

Follow-up and End Points

Follow-up was conducted through electronic medical records and structured telephone interviews from discharge to December 1, 2025. The primary outcome was a composite of cardiovascular mortality, nonfatal myocardial infarction, nonfatal stroke, or heart failure hospitalization. To preserve the natural trajectory of the underlying phenotype, patients undergoing valvular operation or septal reduction therapy during follow-up were censored at the date of intervention and counted as nonevents.

Statistical Analyses

Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) and compared using the independent Student t test. Nonnormally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. For clinical parameters traditionally recorded as discrete integer values (eg, interventricular septal thickness, posterior wall thickness, and chamber diameters), both mean ± SEM and median (interquartile range) are presented. This approach was chosen to provide a more comprehensive view of the data distribution and to clearly illustrate the magnitude of clinical disparity—captured by the mean—which may be obscured by identical medians resulting from the discrete nature of the measurements. Categorical variables are expressed as frequencies (percentages) and compared using the Pearson χ2 test or Fisher exact test. Event-free survival was estimated using Kaplan–Meier analysis with between-group comparisons using the log-rank test. Univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to identify independent predictors of the primary outcome. A parsimonious multivariable Cox proportional hazards model was constructed to strictly control for overfitting. The model was adjusted for biologically relevant covariates: phenotype (group B vs A), age, sex, eGFR, and Ln(NT-proBNP). Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM) and R software (version 4.2.0; R Foundation for Statistical Computing).

Results

Baseline Characteristics: The “Younger but Sicker” Paradox

The study cohort comprised 200 patients with HFsnEF, stratified into group A (hemodynamic loading, n = 88) and group B (intrinsic cardiomyopathy, n = 112) (Figure 1A). Group A consisted of atrial fibrillation (36.4%), hypertension (27.3%), and valvular disease (20.5%) (Figure 1B). As detailed in Table 1, the 2 phenotypes exhibited profoundly distinct clinical profiles despite comparable supranormal LVEF. Patients in group B were markedly younger than those in group A (56.0 ± 1.6 vs 68.8 ± 1.6 years; P < .001) and carried a substantially lower burden of age-related comorbidities, including atrial fibrillation (8.9% vs 38.6%; P < .001) and renal dysfunction (eGFR, 80.6 vs 70.4 mL/min/1.73 m2; P=.01). Contrary to physiological expectations, this younger and healthier demographic did not exhibit a more favorable biomarker profile. Instead, a striking biological discordance emerged: group B displayed NT-proBNP concentrations comparable with the older, multimorbid group A (1793 vs 2193 pg/mL, P = .46) and significantly higher troponin I levels (P = .01). This disproportionate biomarker release occurring alongside marked interventricular septal thickening and elevated septal E/e' (P < .001) suggests that the intrinsic myocardial disease in group B generates wall stress far exceeding that imposed by the hemodynamic loading factors predominant in group A. A critical physiological discordance was observed regarding forward hemodynamics. Although both phenotypes exhibited comparable supranormal LVEF, the SVI was significantly impaired in group B compared with that in group A (mean, 39.2 vs 45.9 mL/m2; P < .001). Diastolic Phenotypes: The Spectrum of Stiffness

Figure 2A shows the divergence in diastolic physiology between the 2 groups. Group A exhibited E/e' values clustering in the mild-to-moderate range (median, 13.0), consistent with secondary remodeling due to hypertension or volume/pressure overload. In contrast, group B showed a distinctly restrictive physiology, characterized by a median septal E/e' of 13.2 (P = .02) and a prominent long tail extending toward extreme stiffness values (E/e'>40). This tail was particularly enriched among patients with cardiac amyloidosis and Fabry disease (Figure 2B). Electromechanical Profiles: A Specific Fingerprint

We further characterized electromechanical coupling in sinus rhythm patients (Figure 2C). Group A maintained largely intact coupling, with 83.6% of patients exhibiting normal PR intervals and narrow QRS complexes. In contrast, group B was characterized by widespread electromechanical uncoupling, with only 70.6% maintaining normal parameters. Two distinct electromechanical fingerprints emerged within group B—ventricular desynchrony: QRS prolongation occurred nearly twice as frequently as in group A (16.7% vs 9.1%), reflecting diffuse myocardial infiltration or fibrosis that impairs conduction velocity; bidirectional PR deviation: PR disturbances were more common in group B (11.8% vs 5.5%). Notably, short PR intervals occurred exclusively in Fabry disease, whereas PR prolongation was characteristic of amyloidosis, highlighting etiology-specific conduction phenotypes.

Prognostic Trajectories and Independent Predictors of Outcome

Clinical outcomes diverged sharply over a median follow-up of 47 months. Kaplan–Meier curves (Figure 3A) revealed early and sustained separation, with significantly worse event-free survival in group B (log-rank P < .001). In univariable Cox analysis, the intrinsic cardiomyopathy phenotype (group B) was associated with a 2.2-fold increased risk of the composite end point (hazard ratio, 2.225; P = .04). To characterize the independent prognostic impact of the phenotype, a fully adjusted parsimonious multivariable Cox model was constructed (Table 2). Notably, even after adjustment for age, sex, renal function (eGFR), and hemodynamic stress (Ln[NT-proBNP]), the intrinsic cardiomyopathy phenotype remained the dominant driver of adverse outcomes. The adjusted hazard ratio for group B surged to 3.912 (95% CI, 1.714-8.931; P = .001). Intriguingly, although phenotype was highly significant, NT-proBNP levels were not an independent predictor in the multivariable model (P = .20). This suggests that in the HFsnEF population, the specific structural etiology (intrinsic cardiomyopathy) determines prognosis more than the hemodynamic severity reflected by natriuretic peptides. To identify the etiologies driving this excess risk, group B was further stratified by specific diagnosis (Figure 3B). A pronounced prognostic gradient emerged. Patients with cardiac amyloidosis and Fabry disease experienced the steepest decline in event-free survival, representing a hypermalignant subset within the HFsnEF spectrum (P < .001). Conversely, patients with HCM exhibited survival curves comparable with those of the hemodynamic loading cohort (group A; P = .36). Discussion

In the present study, we profiled the phenotypic heterogeneity of HFsnEF to unravel the clinical paradox of a younger but sicker population. Our findings highlight the following 3 critical insights: (1) supranormal LVEF is not a marker of health but often masks specific infiltrative cardiomyopathies; (2) the prognosis of this subset is driven by a mechanistic diastolic-electromechanical double hit; and (3) current 1-size-fits-all HFpEF management fails this high-risk group, necessitating a pivot to etiology-specific screening.

Reinterpreting Supranormal: The Maladaptive Physiology

Clinical dogma suggests that higher LVEF implies better contractile reserve.21 Our findings refute this. Group B patients, despite being 12 years younger than group A patients, faced a 4-fold higher risk of adverse outcomes. This suggests that in conditions such as amyloidosis or HCM, an LVEF of 65% or more represents maladaptive hypercontractility required to maintain SV from an obliterated cavity (the empty heart). Crucially, our sensitivity analysis reported that the survival difference disappeared when amyloidosis and Fabry disease were excluded. This confirms that the malignant trajectory of HFsnEF is not intrinsic to the LVEF value itself but is driven entirely by these specific, often-missed infiltrative etiologies hiding behind the normal EF label.

The Mechanism: Diastolic-Electromechanical Double Hit

Why does a seemingly perfect pump fail in HFsnEF? Our findings support a synergistic mechanism characterized by the interaction of impaired ventricular compliance and electromechanical uncoupling.

The Diastolic Hit

Although E/e' is widely used as a noninvasive surrogate of LV filling pressure, its limitations must be acknowledged. In patients with HCM, E/e' may not reliably reflect LA pressure and should not be interpreted in isolation. In the present study, although median E/e' values were only modestly different between groups, group B exhibited a broader distribution with a greater proportion of markedly elevated values, suggesting a higher burden of filling pressure elevation. Importantly, this signal was accompanied by increased wall thickness and a significantly reduced SVI, indicating that supranormal LVEF in these patients reflects a stiff, underfilled ventricle rather than enhanced contractile reserve. In this context, E/e' is best interpreted as a context-dependent marker of hemodynamic stress within a low-flow, high-stiffness physiology.

The Electrical Hit

In parallel, we identified a distinct electromechanical profile in group B, characterized by QRS prolongation and PR interval abnormalities. Although such findings are not specific, their coexistence with structural remodeling suggests impaired electrical conduction within a noncompliant myocardial substrate. In this setting, even subtle disturbances in atrioventricular timing or ventricular synchrony may contribute to impaired late diastolic filling and effective forward flow. This electromechanical uncoupling, when superimposed on a stiff ventricle, likely contributes to the observed discordance between preserved ejection fraction and reduced SV and may help explain the disproportionate elevation in cardiac biomarkers and adverse clinical outcomes.

Clinical Heuristic: A Red Flag Algorithm

On the basis of our findings, we propose a simplified 2-step clinical heuristic for patients presenting with HFsnEF (LVEF ≥ 65%), intended to facilitate early recognition of high-risk phenotypes.

Step 0: Exclude Clinical Mimics

Before phenotypic classification, clinicians should systematically exclude alternative causes of HFsnEF, including high-output states, constrictive pericarditis, and other non-LV causes of heart failure.

Step 1: Clinical Context

In older patients (eg, >65 years) with established hypertension or atrial fibrillation, HFsnEF is more likely to reflect a hemodynamic loading phenotype.

Step 2: Red Flag Features (Trigger for Further Workup)

In contrast, the presence of the following features should raise suspicion for intrinsic cardiomyopathy and prompt further evaluation as follows:•Disproportionately increased wall thickness or small LV cavity•Evidence suggestive of elevated filling pressures (interpreted in context)•Electrocardiographic abnormalities (eg, QRS widening and PR interval deviations)

Importantly, these features should be viewed as signals prompting comprehensive downstream testing, rather than diagnostic end points. Recognition of these red flag features is clinically relevant because patients with intrinsic cardiomyopathies—particularly infiltrative diseases such as amyloidosis and Fabry disease—show markedly worse outcomes and may benefit from etiology-specific diagnostic pathways, including strain imaging, cardiac magnetic resonance, radionuclide imaging, and laboratory evaluation for monoclonal proteins.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Its single-center, retrospective design raises the possibility of referral bias toward complex cardiomyopathies. Although tissue Doppler imaging and electrocardiography were available for all patients, advanced myocardial characterization—including cardiac magnetic resonance and global longitudinal strain—was not universally performed, limiting deeper exploration of fibrosis burden and conduction delay. Additionally, E/e' assessment in atrial fibrillation carries inherent variability, although averaging techniques were used to reduce measurement error.

Finally, although we used the septal E/e' ratio as a key marker, we acknowledge its inherent limitations. Specifically, in patients with HCM—who comprise the majority of our intrinsic cardiomyopathy cohort—an E/e' of more than 15 has been shown to correlate poorly with invasive LA pressure measurements.22 Therefore, our diagnostic heuristic relies on a double hit of combined echographic and electrocardiographic abnormalities rather than any single parameter.

Conclusion

Heart failure with supranormal ejection fraction is defined by profound etiological heterogeneity. Patients with intrinsic cardiomyopathy represent a distinct, malignant phenotype compared with those with hemodynamic loading etiologies. Hidden beneath the guise of a supranormal LVEF lies a lethal combination of severe diastolic stiffness and electromechanical uncoupling. This internal failure drives poor outcomes independent of age. Recognition of this phenotype via multimodal assessment is critical for initiating etiology-specific life-saving therapies.
