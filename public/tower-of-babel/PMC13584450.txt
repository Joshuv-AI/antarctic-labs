Comparative effectiveness of non-pharmacological interventions for older adults with oral frailty: a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Hao-qi Wu, Yuan-mei Lan, Tian-chao Chen, Yue-ying Feng, Xin-juan Wu, Xiao-ming Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral frailty is an emerging frailty phenotype that adversely affects older adults’ quality of life and overall health outcomes. Although non-pharmacological interventions have shown potential benefits in managing oral frailty, their comparative effectiveness remains unclear.ObjectiveTo evaluate and compare the effectiveness of different non-pharmacological interventions for the management of oral frailty in older adults.MethodsElectronic databases, including MEDLINE, EMBASE, CENTRAL, CNKI, and Wanfang, as well as the clinical trial registry ClinicalTrials.gov, were systematically searched from inception to October 2024 to identify eligible randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Study selection and data extraction were independently conducted by two reviewers. Methodological quality was assessed using the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for Randomized Trials (RoB 2). The certainty of the evidence was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach for network meta-analysis. Mean difference (MD) and confidence interval (CI) were estimated using a random-effects network meta-analysis within a frequentist framework.ResultsTwenty-six studies met the inclusion criteria, of which 18 provided sufficient data for inclusion in the network meta-analysis. Twelve RCTs involving 1,094 participants reported changes in tongue pressure from baseline to endpoint. Compared with control, e-health programmes [MD = 6.66, 95% CI (3.08, 10.24)], comprehensive training [MD = 5.57, 95% CI (2.62, 8.52)], and tongue resistance exercise [MD = 3.90, 95% CI (0.73, 7.07)] significantly improved tongue pressure. Ten RCTs involving 888 participants reported bite force outcomes, with chewing exercise demonstrating superior effectiveness compared with control [MD = 81.87, 95% CI (19.46, 144.29)]. Seven RCTs involving 666 participants reported salivary flow rate outcomes. E-health programmes [MD = 0.50, 95% CI (0.03, 0.97)], tongue resistance exercise [MD = 0.26, 95% CI (0.06, 0.46)], swallowing exercise [MD = 0.16, 95% CI (0.07, 0.25)], and comprehensive training [MD = 0.12, 95% CI (0.05, 0.18)] were associated with improvements in salivary flow rate.ConclusionsE-health programmes integrating remote supervision with oral health education and exercise training demonstrated the greatest improvements in tongue pressure and salivary flow rate, whereas chewing-based exercise produced the largest improvement in bite force. However, given that the current evidence is largely derived from studies with small sample sizes and short follow-up periods, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Using the GRADE approach, the certainty of evidence was rated as moderate only for comprehensive training with respect to tongue pressure and salivary flow rate, whereas the certainty of evidence for all other comparisons, including those involving e-health interventions, ranged from low to very low. Consequently, these findings should be interpreted with caution and require confirmation in larger, high-quality randomized controlled trials. Geriatric practice should prioritize symptom-targeted interventions to improve treatment effectiveness, rather than adopting a uniform approach.Trial registrationThe study protocol was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024593211).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07847-3.Keywords: Oral frailty, Non-pharmacological interventions, Network meta-analysis, Systematic review

Introduction

Population ageing is accelerating worldwide, with the proportion of adults aged 60 years and older projected to reach 22% by 2050 [1]. This shift has intensified interest in frailty, a geriatric syndrome defined by diminished physiological reserve and heightened vulnerability to adverse outcomes [2]. Within this context, early identification and intervention for frailty have become important strategies to maintain physical function and quality of life among older adults.

Beyond the well-established physical [3], cognitive [4], and social [5] dimensions of frailty, oral frailty has recently been proposed as a new phenotype. Oral frailty refers to the accumulation of age-related declines in oral functions, including reduced number of teeth, decreased tongue and lip motor function, impaired masticatory performance, and diminished salivary secretion [6]. Prior systematic review and meta-analysis showed that oral frailty is common among community-dwelling older adults, with pooled prevalence estimates ranging from 24% to 40% [7]. Moreover, oral frailty is not merely a localized condition. Studies have shown that it is closely associated with a range of adverse health outcomes, such as limited dietary diversity and malnutrition [8], increased risk of falls [9], higher rates of hospitalization, institutionalization [10] and mortality [6], underscoring its systemic health implications.

A variety of non-pharmacological strategies have been proposed to manage oral frailty, including tongue-strengthening exercises [11], chewing-based exercises [12], swallowing exercises [13], comprehensive training [14], and more recently, e-health programmes [15]. Previous systematic reviews have synthesized the effects of specific interventions, such as oropharyngeal exercises [16] or simple oral exercises [17], but each focused only on a single intervention type or a limited set of outcomes. Considerable heterogeneity exists in intervention protocols, outcome measures, and study quality, and direct head-to-head comparisons between different interventions are limited. As a result, it remains unclear which interventions are most effective for specific functional domains, such as tongue pressure, bite force, and salivary flow rate.

Network meta-analysis (NMA) provides a methodological approach to address these gaps. By combining direct and indirect evidence, NMA enables the simultaneous comparison of multiple interventions and provides probability-based rankings of their relative effectiveness [18]. For geriatric practitioners and allied health professionals in community and long-term care settings, such comparative evidence is critical to inform individualized, symptom-targeted interventions.

Therefore, we conducted a systematic review and network meta-analysis to compare the effectiveness of non-pharmacological interventions for managing oral frailty in older adults. Specifically, we aimed to (1) estimate the relative effects of different intervention categories on three key oral functional outcomes: tongue pressure, bite force, and salivary flow rate; (2) rank interventions according to their comparative effectiveness for each outcome; and (3) provide evidence-based guidance to inform symptom-targeted practice.

Materials and methods

This systematic review and network meta-analysis was designed and reported according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis guidelines (PRISMA) [19] and the extension statement for network meta-analysis (PRISMA-NMA) [20]. The study protocol was prospectively registered on PROSPERO (CRD42024593211).

Search strategy and study selection

The initial comprehensive search was conducted in the following electronic databases from inception to 16 October 2024: Medline (via PubMed), Embase (via Embase.com), CENTRAL (via Cochrane Library), Wanfang, and CNKI. In addition, we searched ClinicalTrials.gov for ongoing or unpublished studies focusing on non-pharmacological interventions for oral frailty. The full search strategies and results are presented in Supplement File 1.

Search results were imported into EndNote 21, and duplicates were removed. Two reviewers (H.Q.W. and Y.M.L.) independently screened titles and abstracts to identify potentially eligible studies. Full texts of potentially relevant articles were subsequently retrieved and assessed against predefined eligibility criteria. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (T.C.C.). Reference lists of included studies and relevant reviews were also manually screened to identify additional eligible studies.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion criteria were based on the PICOS framework. Population: older adults aged ≥ 60 years, recruited from community or long-term care settings. Intervention: any non-pharmacological intervention aimed at improving oral frailty or its functional components, including but not limited to tongue resistance training, chewing-based exercises, swallowing-based exercises, comprehensive programmes, e-health interventions, or other oral function-targeted training. Comparison: usual care, no intervention, placebo or sham intervention, or another eligible non-pharmacological intervention. Outcomes: at least one validated measure of oral health or oral function, such as tongue pressure, bite force, salivary flow rate, chewing or swallowing function, or composite oral function scores. Study design: only randomized controlled trials (RCTs) were eligible.

Studies were excluded if the enrolled participants had neurological diseases affecting oral function (e.g., stroke, Parkinson’s disease) or moderate-to-severe cognitive impairment (e.g., dementia). Oral dysfunction in these populations may reflect disease-specific pathology rather than age-related decline. We also excluded studies that evaluated oral hygiene products (e.g., toothpaste, toothbrushes, oral cleaning devices) or employed pharmacological and chemical agents (e.g., pilocarpine, capsaicin) as the primary intervention.

Data extraction process

Two reviewers (H.Q.W. and Y.M.L.) independently extracted data using a standardized Excel form. The extracted information included study characteristics (first author, year of publication, country), participant details (sample size, age, sex), intervention characteristics (type, duration, frequency), and outcome measurements. Outcome measurements should involve at least one validated assessment of oral health or oral function, including tongue pressure, bite force, salivary flow rate, chewing or swallowing function.

When studies reported means and standard deviations (SDs) of change values, these data were extracted directly. When only baseline and post-intervention values were available, mean change was calculated as the difference between post-intervention and baseline means. The SD of change was estimated using the formula based on recommendations for rehabilitation interventions, assuming a correlation coefficient (r) of 0.5 [21]. For studies reporting medians and interquartile ranges, data were converted to means and SDs using the method proposed by Luo [22].

Data analysis and synthesis

Given the heterogeneity in intervention content and delivery across included studies, we grouped conceptually similar interventions into common categories for synthesis. Operational definitions for each category are presented in Supplement File 2. All eligible studies were summarized in a descriptive table. Studies that could not be connected to other nodes in the network were excluded from the quantitative synthesis but remained in the qualitative summary.

To reduce baseline differences and adjust for variations in initial oral function across studies, we estimated treatment effects using changes from baseline to the final measurement. Due to variability in measurement instruments across studies, only outcome measures reported in the same units (e.g., Newton for bite force, kPa for tongue pressure, and mL/min for salivary flow rate) were synthesized. We calculated mean differences (MDs) rather than standardized mean differences. This improves the clinical interpretability of effect estimates and reduces heterogeneity caused by different measurements.

Network meta-analysis was performed using Stata 15.0 with the “network” package. We summarized pooled treatment effects as mean differences (MDs) with 95% confidence intervals, using random-effects models within a frequentist framework. Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using the tau-squared (τ²) statistic, and I² values were calculated for each pairwise comparison with available direct evidence, with I² values of 25%, 50%, and 75% considered indicative of low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. We assessed global inconsistency using the design-by-treatment interaction model, and evaluated local inconsistency via node-splitting analyses comparing direct and indirect evidence for each comparison. Loop-specific inconsistency factors were calculated for closed loops. Network plots were generated to visualize the geometry of the available evidence, with node size proportional to total sample size and line thickness representing the number of direct comparisons between interventions. League tables displaying all pairwise comparisons were constructed to present relative treatment effects. Relative rankings of interventions were estimated using the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA). SUCRA values range from 0% to 100%, with higher values indicating a greater probability of being the most effective intervention [18]. It should be noted that SUCRA reflects ranking probability based on point estimates and does not account for the precision of effect estimates [23]. Therefore, interventions may achieve high rankings even with wide confidence intervals, indicating substantial uncertainty around their true effects. When interpreting these rankings, we prioritized interventions that showed both high SUCRA values and statistically significant differences relative to controls. We also explicitly noted cases where highly ranked interventions lacked statistical significance due to imprecise effect estimates. Finally, we assessed publication bias visually using adjusted funnel plots, while acknowledging the small number of included studies.

Risk of bias

The revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for Randomized Trials (RoB 2) was used to evaluate each study across five key domains: the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting [24]. Each domain was rated as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk,” and an overall risk-of-bias judgment was derived in accordance with the RoB 2 algorithm. Two reviewers (H.Q.W. and Y.M.L.) independently assessed all included studies using the RoB 2 Excel tool provided on the official Cochrane website. Discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (T.C.C.) to finalize the quality assessment. Inter-reviewer agreement for study selection and risk-of-bias assessment was evaluated using Cohen’s kappa coefficient.

Certainty of evidence

The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) [25, 26] approach for network meta-analysis was used to assess the certainty of evidence for the three primary outcomes: tongue pressure, bite force, and salivary flow rate. For each network estimate, certainty was evaluated across six domains: within-study risk of bias, indirectness, inconsistency, imprecision, incoherence, and publication bias. The certainty of evidence for each comparison was subsequently rated as high, moderate, low, or very low. Two reviewers (H.Q.W. and Y.M.L.) independently conducted the assessments, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (T.C.C.).

Results

Study selection

A total of 6,200 potentially relevant records were identified from five electronic databases and one clinical trial registry. After 628 duplicates were automatically removed using EndNote, 5,572 records were screened by title and abstract, resulting in the exclusion of 5,326 irrelevant records. Of the remaining 246 full-text articles, 4 could not be accessed, 11 were duplicates identified through manual screening, 98 involved populations that did not meet the inclusion criteria, 72 had interventions that did not match the eligibility requirements, 22 were not RCTs, 5 were conducted in hospital settings, and 8 were study protocols. Ultimately, 26 studies met the inclusion criteria for the systematic review. Of these, 18 studies provided sufficient data for direct or indirect comparisons and were included in the network meta-analysis, while the remaining 8 studies were excluded from quantitative synthesis due to isolated interventions but were retained in the narrative synthesis. The study selection process is illustrated in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). Agreement between reviewers for full-text study selection was substantial (Cohen’s κ = 0.838).

Characteristics of the included studies

The 18 studies included in the network meta-analysis enrolled a total of 1,407 participants. These studies were published between 2008 and 2024 and were predominantly conducted in East Asia, including 9 studies from Japan, 7 from Korea, 1 from Taiwan (China), and 1 from the USA. Participants’ mean age ranged from 65.6 to 79.2 years, although 6 studies reported only age ranges and 2 studies did not report mean age. Risk of bias

Among the 18 trials included in the network meta-analysis, 1 study (5.6%) was judged as having low overall risk of bias, 12 studies (66.7%) were rated as having some concerns, and 5 studies (27.8%) were rated as high risk of bias. At the domain level, risk of bias was lowest for outcome measurement, with 17 studies (94.4%) assessed as low risk, reflecting the use of objective measurement instruments (e.g., tongue pressure meters, pressure-sensitive films). The most common source of bias was missing outcome data, with 3 studies (16.7%) rated as high risk due to substantial dropout rates (> 5%) or failure to apply intention-to-treat analysis. Detailed assessments of the included studies are presented in Supplement File 3. Agreement between the two reviewers regarding the overall risk-of-bias judgments was substantial (Cohen’s κ = 0.679).

Compared with the control group, only chewing-based exercise demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in bite force [MD = 81.87, 95% CI (19.46, 144.29)] (Table 4). Assessment of inconsistency and publication bias

Global inconsistency was evaluated using the design-by-treatment interaction model, and no significant inconsistency was detected for any outcome (all p > 0.05). Node-splitting analyses indicated no significant discrepancies between direct and indirect evidence (all p > 0.05), suggesting good local consistency. Additionally, loop-specific inconsistency tests for all closed loops revealed no evidence of inconsistency (all p > 0.05) (Supplement File 5). These findings should be interpreted with caution. For several closed loops, the indirect estimates were associated with substantial imprecision. For example, the indirect standard error was 493.34 for the comparison between comprehensive training and physical exercise for tongue pressure, and 32,724.95 for the comparison between comprehensive training and e-health interventions for bite force. These extremely large standard errors likely reflect sparse evidence, with indirect estimates informed by only a single trial in the corresponding network paths. Consequently, the node-splitting analyses had limited statistical power to detect inconsistency. Therefore, the non-significant results are more likely to reflect insufficient information rather than genuine agreement between direct and indirect evidence and should not be interpreted as evidence for the absence of inconsistency.

Visual inspection of adjusted funnel plots did not reveal obvious asymmetry for tongue pressure. For bite force and salivary flow rate, the included studies were approximately symmetrically distributed; however, the regression line showed a slight tilt, suggesting the potential for small-study effects or publication bias. Given the limited number of studies in several comparisons, the power to detect asymmetry was low, and these results should be interpreted with caution. The details are presented in Supplement File 6.

Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence, assessed using the GRADE approach for network meta-analysis, ranged from moderate to very low across the three outcomes (Supplementary File 7). For tongue pressure, the certainty of evidence was rated as moderate for comprehensive training, low for e-health programmes, tongue resistance training, and chewing-based exercise, and very low for the remaining five comparisons, each of which was informed by a single trial. For bite force, the certainty of evidence was low for chewing-based exercise and comprehensive training, and very low for electrical stimulation combined with chewing-based exercise, isometric jaw exercise, and e-health programmes. For salivary flow rate, the certainty of evidence was moderate for comprehensive training and low for e-health programmes, tongue resistance training, swallowing-based exercise, and chewing-based exercise. Across all outcomes, the most common reasons for downgrading were within-study risk of bias and imprecision, primarily due to wide confidence intervals and comparisons based on single small trials.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first network meta-analysis comparing the relative effectiveness of multiple non-pharmacological interventions for managing oral frailty in older adults. Our analysis of 18 randomized controlled trials encompassing 1,407 participants yielded several clinically meaningful findings. E-health programmes were associated with the greatest improvements in tongue pressure and salivary flow rate, whereas chewing-based exercise was associated with the greatest improvement in bite force. However, according to the GRADE assessment for network meta-analysis, the certainty of evidence for all three comparisons was low. Therefore, these apparent comparative advantages should be interpreted with caution and regarded as preliminary pending confirmation in larger, high-quality trials. These findings suggest that comprehensive multi-component programmes may not be universally superior and support the potential value of symptom-targeted interventions.

For tongue pressure, e-health programmes, comprehensive training, and tongue resistance exercises showed statistically significant improvements compared with control. E-health programmes ranked highest in terms of effectiveness. However, this ranking was based on only three small pilot trials and was therefore supported by low-certainty evidence. In contrast, comprehensive training also demonstrated a significant improvement in tongue pressure and was supported by moderate-certainty evidence. Although some previous studies have demonstrated the efficacy of tongue resistance training for improving tongue strength and swallowing function [48, 49], few have directly compared digitally delivered interventions with face-to-face approaches. The included e-health interventions employed diverse but similar strategies: Jung et al. [43] used a smartphone-based combined oral and whole-body exercise programme with video-guided instructions and real-time feedback; Ki et al. [15] developed the OHEMA mobile application for oral health education and exercise monitoring; and Tani et al. [31] compared tablet-based oral function exercise delivery with traditional paper-based instruction. These interventions included common features such as automated reminders, video demonstration, and remote supervision.

Several factors may contribute to the observed advantage of e-health programmes in improving tongue pressure. Home-based digital programmes may eliminate barriers faced by older adults with limited mobility or time, providing more opportunities for long-term participation [50]. On the other hand, step-by-step guidance and video demonstrations on online platforms help older adults master the skills of oral frailty exercises better, potentially making their training more efficient. In line with WHO, our findings suggest that digital health may help extend access to healthcare [51, 52]. Geriatric practitioners need to assist older adults in developing digital health literacy and improving their access to digital services. Ki et al. [15] attempted to address these issues by designing a user-friendly interface and involving family caregivers in the OHEMA application.

For bite force, chewing-based exercise was the only intervention that showed a statistically significant effect compared with the control group, providing the most robust evidence for this outcome among the interventions evaluated, although the certainty of evidence was rated as low because of concerns regarding risk of bias and imprecision. Previous meta-analysis has also reported that oral exercises, especially chewing-based protocols, are linked to improvements in bite force [17]. From a clinical perspective, improved bite force plays an important role in masticatory performance and dietary diversity, and may help reduce nutritional deficits which contribute to systemic frailty [53]. While isometric jaw exercise ranked highest in SUCRA probability (86.0%) with a numerically larger point estimate (MD = 270.90), the wide confidence interval [95% CI (–22.93, 564.73)] underscores potential uncertainty. This highlights a limitation of SUCRA rankings in network meta-analysis, and the results should therefore be interpreted with caution, as they reflect the relative ranking of point estimates without accounting for precision [23]. When both statistical significance and SUCRA rankings are considered, chewing-based exercise shows favourable evidence for improving bite force, although further high-quality studies are warranted.

Regarding salivary flow rate, four interventions: e-health programmes, tongue resistance exercises, swallowing exercises, and comprehensive training, have demonstrated significant improvements, with e-health programmes ranking highest. Among these interventions, comprehensive training was supported by moderate-certainty evidence, whereas the estimates for e-health programmes, tongue resistance training, and swallowing-based exercise were supported by low-certainty evidence, largely owing to the limited number and small sample sizes of the contributing trials. The observed effects were modest (0.12–0.50 mL/min), but may be clinically meaningful given that previous studies have reported age-related reductions in salivary flow of approximately 0.17 mL/min [54]. Even small increases in salivary secretion may improve oral comfort, reduce xerostomia, and improve oral function in older adults [55].

In our analysis, chewing-based exercise was not found to significantly improve salivary flow rate, while previous studies have indicated that masticatory stimulation may enhance salivation [15, 56]. One possible explanation for this difference is the temporary nature of chewing-induced salivary stimulation, which may not translate into sustained changes in unstimulated salivary flow rate. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Dodds et al. [57] reported that gum chewing can increase unstimulated salivary flow rate in older adults, but the improvement appears to depend on the number of consecutive intervention days. This finding underscores the importance of intervention duration and consistency, which may be insufficiently addressed in short-term studies. In our analysis, e-health programmes were associated with better salivary outcomes, which may be partly explained by their capacity to support longer-term and more consistent engagement with exercise protocols.

Overall, our findings support a symptom-targeted approach. Geriatric practitioners should undertake a comprehensive assessment of oral function and, when intervention is indicated, consider comprehensive multi-component oral training programmes, which were the only interventions supported by moderate-certainty evidence for improving tongue pressure and salivary flow rate. E-health programmes represent a promising approach, demonstrating the largest estimated improvements in these outcomes in our analysis. However, the supporting evidence was of low certainty and derived from only a small number of pilot trials with limited sample sizes. Consequently, these findings should be regarded as preliminary, and e-health programmes should be considered a potential strategy requiring further confirmation rather than an established evidence-based recommendation. Validated screening tools, such as the Oral Frailty Index-8 [58] and the Oral Frailty 5-item Checklist [59], may enable early identification of high-risk individuals and help practitioners in selecting appropriate interventions based on the pattern of functional decline.

Strengths & limitations

This study has several strengths. First, we strictly adhered to methodological guidelines for systematic reviews and network meta-analyses (PRISMA-NMA) and included only randomized controlled trials. Second, we applied strict eligibility criteria aligned with the concept of oral frailty, including only older adults without disease-specific oral dysfunction. This approach ensures that observed intervention effects reflect age-related oral functional decline rather than disease-related impairment.

Several limitations should also be acknowledged. Many included studies were small and had short follow-up periods, limiting the ability to assess long-term effectiveness. Additionally, most studies focused on physiological proxies rather than clinical endpoints such as oral health-related quality of life or nutritional status, constraining evaluation of broader intervention impact. Risk of bias was another concern: only one study was rated as low risk, largely due to challenges in blinding exercise interventions and incomplete outcome data from participant dropout. Evidence for e-health interventions remains preliminary, as most studies were pilot in scope; larger trials are needed to confirm effectiveness. Future studies should address these limitations by conducting RCTs with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up periods, incorporating patient-centered outcome measures to better assess clinical relevance.

Conclusion

This systematic review and network meta-analysis demonstrates that non-pharmacological interventions for oral frailty exhibit domain-specific effectiveness. E-health programmes integrating remote supervision with oral health education and exercise training were associated with the greatest improvements in tongue pressure and salivary flow rate, whereas chewing-based exercise was associated with the greatest improvement in bite force. However, these comparative findings were supported by low-certainty evidence, while comprehensive training was the only intervention supported by moderate-certainty evidence for tongue pressure and salivary flow rate. For geriatric practice, smartphone or tablet-based e-health platforms offer a promising delivery model that combines clinical effectiveness with practical advantages, including convenience and improved adherence, but their comparative clinical benefit remains uncertain and should be confirmed in higher-quality trials before firm recommendations are made. Future research should prioritize larger, adequately powered trials with longer follow-up periods and incorporate patient-centered outcomes to better assess clinical relevance and sustain intervention benefits.
