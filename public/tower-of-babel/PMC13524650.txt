Changes in postoperative symptom communities and network structure in patients with oral squamous cell carcinoma: A four-time-point study
Authors: Shumin Nie, Chunqin Liu, Yuqing Zhang, Ting Zhao, Xiaohua Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo describe changes in symptom communities and contemporaneous symptom network structure across four postoperative time points in patients with oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) undergoing flap reconstruction.MethodsThis prospective longitudinal study included 251 participants with complete data. Symptoms were assessed on postoperative days 1 (T1), 3 (T2), 7 (T3), and 30 (T4) using the Chinese version of the MD Anderson Symptom Inventory-Head and Neck Module. Gaussian graphical models with EBICglasso regularization were estimated separately at each time point. Symptom communities were identified using principal component analysis (PCA), with exploratory graph analysis (EGA) performed as a sensitivity analysis. Node strength and bridge strength were summarized. Paired Network Comparison Tests with 2000 permutations compared networks across time points; global-strength comparisons were Bonferroni-adjusted.ResultsIn the PCA solution, three, three, four, and five symptom communities were identified at T1, T2, T3, and T4, respectively; the sensitivity analysis yielded four, three, three, and six communities, indicating methodological sensitivity. Choking cough, somnolence, problems with mucus, and vomiting had the highest node strength at T1, T2, T3, and T4, respectively. Problems with mucus had the highest bridge strength at T1 and T2, lack of appetite at T3, and distress at T4. Global strength differed between T2 and T3 and between T2 and T4 after Bonferroni correction.ConclusionsThese descriptive, hypothesis-generating findings provide a complementary view of postoperative symptom interrelationships and may support stage-specific symptom assessment. They do not establish causal mechanisms or validated intervention targets.Keywords: Oral squamous cell carcinoma, Symptom clusters, Network analysis, Symptom management, Longitudinal study

Introduction

Oral cancer includes cancers of the lip, other parts of the mouth , and the oropharynx; collectively, these cancers rank as the 13th most common cancer worldwide.1 According to the Global Cancer Observatory (GLOBOCAN) estimates of the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC), approximately 389,846 new cases and 188,438 deaths from lip and oral cavity cancer occurred worldwide in 2022,2 highlighting its substantial global health burden. Surgical resection remains the primary treatment for patients with oral cancer.

However, surgical treatment of oral cancer is characterized by considerable complexity and invasiveness. The procedure often requires extensive tumor resection combined with neck lymph node dissection, which can easily result in defects of critical structures such as the tongue, buccal mucosa, and mandible. To further restore both function and appearance, patients with extensive surgical defects may require flap reconstruction.3 Such procedures frequently result in substantial anatomical and functional impairments, leading to chewing and swallowing difficulties, speech disorders, pain, and sleep disturbances.4,5 These concurrent and coexisting symptoms severely compromise patients’ functional status, quality of life, and survival.6,7 Importantly, the symptom burden is dynamic, varying in frequency and intensity over time, which complicates symptom management. Evidence indicates that patients undergoing surgery alone experience their heaviest symptom burden during the acute phase (peaking at 3–9 days post-surgery), whereas those receiving adjuvant radiotherapy or chemotherapy face prolonged distress during the treatment phase.8,9 Specifically, functional domains such as speech and swallowing show a significant decline in the early postoperative stage (1 month), with quality of life and functional status often being worse at this point than even before surgery or at later follow-ups (3 months).9,10 However, current understanding remains limited to linear descriptions of symptom severity over time. While it is established that the intensity of individual symptoms fluctuates with treatment stages, it remains unclear how the interrelationships among these symptoms evolve.

Patients with oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) after surgery often experience multiple co-occurring symptoms that evolve dynamically, yet current evidence largely fails to capture the temporal complexity of their interrelationships. Most existing studies rely on cross-sectional designs that obscure longitudinal patterns.11, 12, 13, 14 Even among longitudinal investigations, methodological limitations persist, some have mapped the trajectory of overall symptom burden—identifying peaks at specific intervals (e.g., 7 days post-surgery) and risk factors such as nutritional status,15,16 they predominantly treat symptoms as aggregated scores or independent checklists, neglecting the underlying organizational structure of symptom co-occurrence. Furthermore, Traditional cluster methods can identify groups of co-occurring symptoms but do not estimate conditional symptom-to-symptom associations within a network.17, 18, 19 Repeated contemporaneous network estimation may therefore provide a complementary description of how postoperative symptom interrelationships differ across recovery.20 By estimating partial correlations, this approach visualizes and quantifies symptom–symptom associations, providing a complementary perspective for understanding symptom interrelationships beyond symptom severity alone.

Consequently, this study utilized a longitudinal design to estimate and visualize contemporaneous symptom networks at four postoperative time points, facilitating the comparison of central nodes, bridge symptoms, and network stability across these stages. The study aimed to address the research questions regarding which symptoms demonstrate the strongest statistical associations and occupy central positions within the symptom network. Furthermore, how do these nodes with high centrality evolve across different postoperative stages? The findings are anticipated to provide novel insights into symptom management by shifting the focus from isolated symptom severity to symptom interconnectedness. By identifying central symptoms within the network, this study may highlight key areas for clinical assessment and support the development of more coordinated symptom management strategies, ultimately improving postoperative recovery and quality of life in patients with oral cancer.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study used a prospective, longitudinal design. A total of 270 patients with OSCC who underwent flap reconstruction surgery from June 2024 to June 2025 were selected in this study, at Shandong Provincial Hospital Affiliated to Shandong First Medical University in Jinan, Shandong Province, China. Symptom data were collected at four postoperative time points: Day 1 (T1), Day 3 (T2), Day 7 (T3), and Day 30 (T4). These time points were selected because they are associated with a higher likelihood of severe symptom manifestation.21 The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) aged 18 years or older; (2) histopathologically diagnosed OSCC and undergoing flap reconstruction surgery; (3) able to understand the study information and complete the questionnaire independently or with assistance; (4) informed and voluntary participation in this study. Patients were excluded if they received adjuvant oncological treatments (e.g., adjuvant chemotherapy or radiotherapy) during the 30-day postoperative symptom assessment period, had a history of other malignant tumors, presented with severe comorbid conditions such as cardiac, renal, or pulmonary failure, or declined to participate in the study.

Following the guidelines proposed by Epskamp and Fried,22 we used the heuristic criterion of P (P-1)/2 as a pragmatic reference for sample size. With P = 22 symptoms, this suggests a minimum of 231 participants. Initially, a total of 270 patients were recruited. During follow-up, 19 participants were excluded due to withdrawal (n = 4), transfer to other departments (n = 5), or loss to follow-up (n = 10). Consequently, we focused our longitudinal analyses on the 251 participants who provided complete symptom data at all four assessment points (complete-case retention rate = 92.96%). This sample size exceeds the pragmatic heuristic threshold, ensuring sufficient stability for network estimation across all time points. While a small number of participants (n = 9 for chemotherapy; n = 3 for radiotherapy) had received preoperative neoadjuvant therapy, these patients were retained to enhance the clinical generalizability of our findings.

Data collection procedure

Symptom assessments were conducted at four distinct postoperative time points: Day 1 (T1), Day 3 (T2), Day 7 (T3), and Day 30 (T4). To mitigate potential bias, data collection during hospitalization (T1–T3) was performed by research nurses who were not involved in the participants' clinical care and had undergone standardized training. Patients were encouraged to complete the questionnaires independently whenever possible. For participants with physical limitations but intact cognition, trained nurses read the items aloud verbatim in a neutral manner without providing interpretation or cueing, thereby preserving the integrity of self-reported data. The T4 evaluation was synchronized with the scheduled one-month postoperative clinic visit, during which outpatient nurses collected data following the same standardized procedure. All demographic and clinical covariates were collected through a combination of patients’ self-report (for demographic characteristics) and systematic abstraction from electronic health records (for clinical and disease-related variables).

Instruments

Demographic and clinical data

Demographic information included age, sex, marital status, residence, educational level, occupation, and monthly family income. Clinical data included tumor site, smoking history, reimbursement type, and preoperative chemotherapy and radiotherapy.

MD Anderson Symptom Inventory-Head and Neck Module (MDASI-H&N)

Patient symptoms were evaluated using the Chinese version of the MD Anderson Symptom Inventory-Head and Neck Module (MDASI-H&N), localized by Han23 Originally developed by Rosenthal et al., in 2007 based on the core MDASI,24 this instrument comprises two domains: symptom burden and the interference of symptoms with daily life. Items are rated on a 0–10 scale, where higher scores reflect greater symptom severity or impact on life. This study used only the first section to measure the patients' symptom burden. The MDASI-H&N has demonstrated high reliability and validity in Chinese populations. The Cronbach's α coefficients for the four time points were 0.969, 0.961, 0.970, and 0.937.

Statistical analysis

No item-level missing data were identified among the 251 participants included in the final analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0 and R 4.5.0. To ensure the comparability of symptom networks across all four time points, all 22 symptoms included in the MD Anderson Symptom Inventory-Head and Neck Module (MDASI-H&N) were retained, regardless of their prevalence at individual assessment stages. Symptom communities were identified using principal component analysis (PCA) with varimax rotation. The criteria for component extraction were as follows: eigenvalue ≥ 1, component loading ≥ 0.40, and each component containing at least two symptoms.25

For each period, all 22 symptoms were used to construct undirected partial correlation networks with the qgraph package (version 1.9.8) in R. In this study, the “bootnet” package (version 1.7.1) was used to estimate the symptom networks at four time points. The “EBICglasso” algorithm,26 combined with the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression, was applied to construct sparse networks.27 In the EBICglasso model, the optimal regularization parameter was selected using the Extended Bayesian Information Criterion (EBIC), and the EBIC hyperparameter was set to γ = 0.50, which is commonly recommended for psychological and symptom network analyses.

To facilitate intuitive comparison across different networks, the averageLayout function was used to generate a consistent layout, ensuring that identical nodes were fixed at the same positions in each network. Each node represents a symptom in the network, and the strength of associations between symptoms is indicated by the thickness and color intensity of the edges. Thicker and darker edges represent stronger associations. In addition, to enhance visualization and enable comparison of edge thickness between graphs, the maximum edge weight was set to 0.5 (no smaller than the strongest edge identified in any network), and the minimum edge weight was set to 0.05 (one tenth of the strongest edge). It is important to note that these parameters were applied solely for visual scaling to ensure consistency across plots and did not involve any data truncation or thresholding of the estimated network weights.

In addition, network centrality indices were calculated, namely strength, closeness, and betweenness. Strength refers to the sum of the weighted values of all edges connected to a node, reflecting its importance within the network. Closeness is defined as the reciprocal of the sum of the shortest path distances from all other nodes to the given node. Betweenness represents the frequency with which a node lies on the shortest paths between any two other nodes.28 Given that the interpretation of betweenness and closeness in networks is somewhat ambiguous and these indices have often been found unreliable in previous studies,29 we primarily reported node strength in this study. In addition, we estimated bridge centrality, which is defined as the sum of the absolute edge weights of all connections between a node and the nodes in other communities. By taking the absolute values of the edges, we ensured that both positive and negative associations contributed to the bridge strength, effectively quantifying the total intensity of inter-community communication while avoiding the potential cancellation effects of negative edges.30

The accuracy and stability of the network models were evaluated through several rigorous procedures. We estimated 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for edge weights and centrality indices, assessed the stability of strength centrality using case-dropping bootstrap procedures, and conducted bootstrapped difference tests to evaluate the significance of edge-weight and centrality variations. To further examine structural robustness, sensitivity analyses were performed by re-estimating networks with alternative EBIC hyperparameters (γ = 0.25 and γ = 0.75). Furthermore, exploratory graph analysis (EGA) was employed using the EGAnet package (version 2.4.0),31 where networks were estimated via the GLASSO algorithm and symptom communities were identified using the Walktrap algorithm to verify the stability of the PCA-derived community structure. All bootstrap-based procedures were performed using 1000 iterations to ensure model robustness and reproducibility. Correlation stability (CS) coefficients were calculated, with values > 0.25 and > 0.50 indicating acceptable and good stability, respectively.26

Finally, exploratory comparisons of temporal network differences were conducted using the paired-samples Network Comparison Test (version 2.2.3) (NCT; paired = TRUE, 2000 permutations).32,33 In these analyses, the difference in node strength (ΔStrength) was defined as the strength value at the earlier assessment minus that at the later assessment; thus, a positive value indicates a reduction in symptom centrality over time, while a negative value indicates an increase. For node-level comparisons, P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. Given that we conducted six pairwise comparisons of global strength for global strength across the time points, we applied a Bonferroni correction to these global strength comparisons to strictly control for Type I error inflation. However, to prevent severe power loss (Type II errors), edge-level P-values were not adjusted for multiple testing. Consequently, these analyses serve as an exploratory approximation of temporal network changes, and unadjusted edge-level results should be interpreted with caution.

Network accuracy and stability

Bootstrap analyses indicated moderate accuracy of edge-weight estimates across the four symptom networks (Supplementary Fig. S1). Although the bootstrap confidence intervals of several edge weights overlapped, some edge weights showed non-overlapping intervals, suggesting that selected associations could be distinguished from one another. Case-dropping bootstrap analyses were conducted to evaluate the stability of strength centrality. The CS coefficients for strength centrality were 0.44, 0.36, 0.52, and 0.67 for T1, T2, T3, and T4, respectively. These values indicated acceptable stability at T1 and T2 and good stability at T3 and T4 (Fig. 4). Bootstrapped difference tests for edge weights and node strength are presented in Supplementary Fig. S2 and S3. Sensitivity analyses using alternative EBIC hyperparameter values (γ = 0.25 and γ = 0.75) produced network structures that were largely consistent with those obtained in the primary analysis (Supplementary Fig. S4). In addition, exploratory graph analysis (EGA) was used to cross-validate the community detection results. EGA identified four, three, three, and six communities at T1, T2, T3, and T4, respectively (Supplementary Fig. S5). Although several central node groupings were retained, the number of communities and assignments of individual symptoms differed from those identified by PCA, indicating some methodological sensitivity in community detection.

Case-dropping bootstrap stability of strength centrality across the four symptom networks.

The stability coefficients at all four time points exceeded 0.25, indicating that the estimated network models demonstrated acceptable to good stability, with stronger stability observed at T3 and T4 (> 0.50).

Network comparison

Exploratory pairwise comparisons using the paired Network Comparison Test (NCT) indicated differences in global strength across postoperative time points (Supplementary Table S8). Before multiplicity adjustment, the global strength difference between T1 and T2 was nominally significant (ΔGS = 4.54, unadjusted P = 0.034); however, this difference was no longer significant after Bonferroni correction. In contrast, the differences between T2 and T3 (ΔGS = 8.57, Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.003) and between T2 and T4 (ΔGS = 8.02, Bonferroni-adjusted P = 0.003) remained statistically significant after correction. No significant differences in global strength were observed for the remaining comparisons after multiplicity adjustment (T1 vs. T3: ΔGS = 4.03; T1 vs. T4: ΔGS = 3.48; T3 vs. T4: ΔGS = 0.55).

At the edge level, exploratory pairwise comparisons identified differences in symptom-to-symptom connections across postoperative time points (Table S9). Without adjustment for multiple testing, the proportion of edges with unadjusted P values < 0.05 ranged from 35.9% for T1 vs. T4 to 68.4% for T2 vs. T3.

Discussion

The threat of cancer and the impact of surgery are often associated with a variety of physical and psychological symptoms experienced by patients with OSCC after surgery. To our knowledge, this is the first study to construct symptom networks at four postoperative time points in patients with OSCC using network analysis, and to systematically evaluate the temporal stability and structural characteristics of these networks. The main findings can be summarized as follows: First, although some symptoms were highly prevalent and severe across the postoperative period, their centrality within the networks was relatively low. Second, the symptom communities demonstrated dynamic evolution rather than structural stability. While core thematic domains persisted, the macroscopic structure differentiated from three to five distinct communities over time, suggesting complex and shifting interconnections among symptoms. Third, exploratory pairwise comparisons of contemporaneous networks across postoperative assessments suggested potential variations in symptom networks across different postoperative stages in terms of symptom centrality, global connectivity, and overall network configuration. Furthermore, sensitivity analyses using alternative EBIC hyperparameter values indicated that the estimated network structures were generally stable, though these findings should be interpreted with awareness of the methodological variability observed in community detection (EGA). These results indicate dynamic variations in postoperative symptom associations among patients with OSCC, providing exploratory insights into symptom interrelationships across different postoperative stages.

Symptom experiences among patients with OSCC during the postoperative period exhibit distinct characteristics. The pronounced symptom fluctuations may be related to the substantial surgical trauma associated with oral cancer surgery. Unlike previous reports indicating a peak in symptom burden at 7 days post-surgery,16 our study identified the most severe burden at approximately 3 days (T2), followed by a gradual decline by 1 month (T4). Symptom severity showed dynamic changes across time points rather than remaining static. Dysphonia peaked in the early postoperative stage (T1–T2), followed by dysphagia (T3) and gingival problems (T4). This predominance of somatic impairments aligns with Mathew et al.34 who identified speech difficulty, dysphagia, and gingival issues as top burdens, and corroborates Peng et al.,11 who highlighted swallowing, xerostomia, and mucus problems. Collectively, these findings suggest that physical functional deficits, rather than psychological distress, may represent major components of the postoperative symptom burden, contrasting with studies prioritizing psychological concerns.14 Notably, although distress ranked lower in severity, it emerged as a relatively stable bridge symptom by T4 and showed close associations with both physical and psychological symptom communities.

A critical insight from our network analysis is the dissociation between symptom severity and centrality. Although dysphonia was highly severe, it exhibited low network centrality, suggesting that high symptom severity does not necessarily correspond to a relatively central position within the symptom network. This finding aligns with Liu et al.,35 who identified vomiting as a central node at later stages, reinforcing the notion that the most severe symptoms are not necessarily the most strongly connected symptoms within the symptom network. Given that oral cancer treatment entails unique challenges such as facial disfigurement and functional loss,6 the interplay between physical discomfort and emotional distress is complex. These findings provide insights into the differences between symptom severity and symptom interconnectedness in patients with OSCC during postoperative recovery. They suggest that evaluating symptoms based solely on prevalence or severity may not fully capture the complexity of symptom interrelationships during recovery. Symptoms occupying relatively central positions in the network may represent key nodes within the symptom system and therefore warrant particular attention in clinical assessment and monitoring. Identifying such central symptoms illustrates the value of network-based analyses for understanding symptom dynamics and guiding focused observation throughout the postoperative period.

Our findings revealed a dynamically evolving community structure. While core thematic domains persisted, the macroscopic network progressively fragmented from three to five communities, indicating that despite thematic continuity, individual symptom memberships are highly dynamic. However, we also observed the emergence of transient communities, such as an airway–skin-related cluster, indicating potential changes in symptom associations during recovery. While this temporal variability differs from the relatively stable symptom cluster structures reported in many cross-sectional studies, it is consistent with the dynamic nature of postoperative recovery.36 These patterns may be related to the complex recovery process following oral cancer surgery. First, the extensive surgical trauma involves multiple functional systems (oropharynx, tongue, maxillofacial region), simultaneously impairing speech, swallowing, and respiration,37 which may contribute to the co-occurrence of multiple somatic symptoms. Second, psychological symptoms frequently co-occurred with physical symptoms; for instance, distress and sadness frequently exhibited relatively prominent bridge characteristics linking different symptom communities in the PCA-based networks. As noted in recent reviews,38 symptom clusters in patients with cancer are not static but fluctuate with disease progression and treatment phases. Therefore, the observed variations in cluster composition across time points may reflect temporal variations in symptom experiences during postoperative recovery rather than methodological inconsistency. Notably, our sensitivity analysis using EGA revealed variations in the number (i.e., 4, 3, 3, and 6 communities) and composition of symptom communities compared to PCA. Because bridge centrality is mathematically dependent on how these communities are defined, the main bridge-symptom findings (e.g., mucus problems, distress) must be interpreted with appropriate caution, as their exact bridge strength fluctuates under alternative community specifications.

Although symptom clusters with the same labels were identified at different time points, their internal symptom composition was not completely consistent. This phenomenon is common in longitudinal studies of symptom clusters and suggests that symptom aggregation is characterized by thematic stability but variable composition. For example, at T1, nausea and vomiting were included in the systemic somatic energy-consumption cluster, whereas at T3 they formed a distinct digestive-related cluster together with lack of appetite. This may be related to acute gastrointestinal discomfort caused by residual anesthesia and drug side effects in the early postoperative phase, which became the main source of energy consumption.39 As recovery progressed, however, digestive symptoms gradually co-occurred with dietary restrictions and insufficient nutritional intake, forming a new aggregation pattern. Similarly, at T1, the oropharyngeal functional-impairment cluster was mainly composed of difficulty with voice/speech and mucus problems, while at T2–T3, dysphagia and xerostomia were gradually added as new symptoms. These temporal changes may be associated with postoperative wound healing and the gradual recovery of eating functions, during which new functional impairments became apparent.39 Likewise, at T2, the oropharyngeal functional-impairment cluster was primarily characterized by dysphagia, difficulty with voice/speech, and mucus problems, whereas at T4, it further included xerostomia, gingival problems, and chronic oropharyngeal pain. Meng et al. have demonstrated that this evolutionary process may reflect a postoperative transition from acute tracheal intubation and incision-related injuries to functional compensation and chronic complications.40 Accordingly, the findings of the present study further suggest that symptom clusters with the same label are not fixed entities; rather, while the core functional domains remain relatively stable, the individual components within each cluster can dynamically adjust over time and throughout the course of the recovery process. These observations are consistent with the clinical logic of postoperative recovery and highlight the importance of considering temporal variations in symptom experiences during postoperative assessment and supportive care.

In this study, mucus problems emerged as an important node in the symptom networks, acting as a bridge symptom at T1 and T2 and as a central symptom at T3, suggesting that it may play a notable role in the postoperative symptom experience of patients with OSCC. This finding is generally consistent with previous research results. For example, studies have shown that mucus-related symptoms frequently occur in patients with head and neck cancer during concurrent chemoradiotherapy and significantly reduce their quality of life.41 In addition, perioperative endotracheal intubation and mechanical ventilation can irritate the airway mucosa, which may be associated with increased mucus production.42 Concurrently, mucus problems exhibited relatively prominent bridge strength within the PCA-based network structure, suggesting potential connections across the dynamically evolving symptom communities. This finding may indicate that mucus problems were closely associated with symptoms from multiple communities within the network structure. From a nursing practice perspective, recognizing bridge symptoms such as mucus problems may help nurses conduct comprehensive assessments and plan supportive care for postoperative patients with OSCC.

The study found that choking cough, somnolence, problems with mucus, and vomiting had the highest node strength on postoperative days 1, 3, 7, and 30, respectively. This finding suggests that symptoms occupying relatively central positions within the networks varied across postoperative stages. This finding is consistent with the study by Zhang et al.43 which identified different central nodes at two postoperative time points in patients with primary hepatic carcinoma. However, the high node strength of vomiting reflects its relative interconnectedness within the network rather than its prevalence, severity, or established priority for clinical intervention. We also found that lack of appetite and distress became bridge nodes in the late postoperative phase of OSCC. Badr et al.44 reported that psychological distress in patients with head and neck cancer steadily increased during radiotherapy and peaked in the fifth week. Given the important role of the oral cavity in speech, swallowing, respiration, and facial expression, treatment-related functional impairments may be associated with both physical and psychological symptom experiences. From a clinical perspective, these findings highlight the importance of considering temporal variations in symptom experiences across postoperative recovery stages.

Implications for nursing practice and research

The present findings highlight the importance of considering symptom interrelationships when assessing postoperative symptoms in patients with OSCC. Rather than focusing solely on symptom severity, nurses may benefit from paying attention to symptoms that occupy central or bridge positions within the symptom network, as these symptoms showed relatively stronger connections with other symptoms within the network structure. In addition, the observed temporal variations in core and bridge symptoms suggest that symptom experiences may vary across different postoperative stages. From a research perspective, the findings also demonstrate the potential value of network analysis for exploring complex symptom interactions and may inform future studies aimed at further examining symptom interrelationships and temporal network changes.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, convenience sampling may have introduced selection bias, limiting the generalizability of our findings; future multicenter studies with random sampling are needed. Second, reliance solely on subjective patient-reported outcomes (MDASI-H&N), without objective clinical indicators (e.g., nutritional or inflammatory markers), restricts our ability to validate symptom severity against biological correlates. Third, unmeasured confounding factors emerging post-discharge, particularly at T4 (such as variations in home care, diet, rehabilitation support, and wound recovery), were not fully controlled for, potentially influencing symptom trajectories. Finally, this observational study identifies statistical associations rather than causal mechanisms. Although the paired NCT procedure partially accounts for the paired design, it does not constitute a full longitudinal network model and cannot fully model within-subject temporal dependencies. Therefore, temporal network differences should be interpreted as exploratory. Future research should incorporate causal inference methods and advanced longitudinal models (e.g., mlVAR) to rigorously account for within-subject variance.

Conclusions

This study described the dynamic changes in symptom networks among patients with OSCC following flap reconstruction surgery. Although core thematic symptom domains remained identifiable across postoperative stages, community membership and network organization changed over time. Choking cough, somnolence, mucus problems, and vomiting occupied relatively central positions at different postoperative stages, whereas mucus problems, lack of appetite, and distress exhibited relatively prominent bridge characteristics within the PCA-based networks. In addition, symptom severity and network centrality were not always aligned. Given that these findings are based on associative network structures, they should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating. While these findings contribute to a better understanding of postoperative symptom interrelationships and highlight potential areas for comprehensive assessment, they do not establish causal mechanisms or validated intervention targets. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are warranted to further examine the clinical significance of these network characteristics and their implications for supportive care.
