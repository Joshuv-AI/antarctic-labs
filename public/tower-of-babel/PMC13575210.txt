3-D Numerical modeling and assessment of subsidence caused by underground copper ore mine
Authors: Avinash Singh, Mohammad Soyeb Alam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurface subsidence resulting from underground mining presents a significant challenge in mining engineering, potentially damaging surface structures, environmental concerns, the safety of personnel, and causing substantial economic losses for the mine. This study aims to investigate and predict ground subsidence resulting from underground mining activities, focusing on a copper ore mine in India, adopting the blast-hole stopping technique. In this study, various subsidence prediction modeling techniques (empirical, influence function, and theoretical/numerical/analytical) have been outlined for assessing mining-induced subsidence. A three-dimensional (3-D) numerical model was developed using the Finite Element Method (FEM) code Strand7, encompassing geologic complexities such as joints and faults, connections between different lithologies, and the stoping sequence selected from the mine plans to investigate the mechanism of surface subsidence induced by underground mining. The model simulates various mining phases, including the virgin state, ongoing operations, and predictions for the subsequent five and ten years, to assess the related strain and displacement in all directions. To improve model precision, rock mass characteristics and in-situ stress conditions were optimised via backward modeling. The novelty of the work lies in the coupling of backward modeling for geotechnical parameter improvement within a three-dimensional FEM framework, applied to a real-world mining sequence in a complex geological setting. This method enhances the accuracy of predictions and provides a practical approach to subsidence prediction in operational mines. This research helps to promote safer mine planning and enables the construction of dependable, site-specific methods.Keywords: Mining-induced subsidence, Three-dimensional numerical modeling, Surface strain and displacement prediction, Underground copper mineSubject terms: Natural hazards, Civil engineering

Introduction

Background on mining-induced subsidence

Land surface deformation refers to measurable changes in the Earth’s surface from its original position, expressed as either sideways shifting or vertical movement in the form of sinking (subsidence) or rising (uplift)1,2. It is a common consequence of underground mining, and it can vary in magnitude and extent and can occur immediately after mining activities or may continue over a prolonged period3,4. Although these processes generally proceed slowly, there is no doubt that they can reshape landscapes and disrupt mine environment systems5–7. Mining-induced subsidence happens when underground material is removed and the rocks above lose support, leading them to bend, crack, or collapse into empty spaces8. Although it is often considered an unavoidable result of underground mining, better prediction and monitoring can reduce its impacts9,10. In addition to computing the net present value (NPV) in forest areas, mining-induced subsidence needs to be modeled using three-dimensional numerical modeling in accordance with the regulatory criteria of the Ministry of Environment and Forests and Climate Change (MoEFCC). Furthermore, mine subsidence can be measured in two ways: 1) using various subsidence prediction models in prevalent geomining and geotechnical circumstances, and 2) using various mine subsidence monitoring techniques. However, the current study focuses on assessment using prediction models.

Mine subsidence prediction methods

Mine subsidence can be predicted using three main techniques: empirical method, Influence Function method and Theoretical method11. Traditionally, empirical methods have been employed in the field of rock engineering to characterise the tentative response trends associated with the studied phenomenon. These methods are based on a synthesis of past observations, typically in similar settings12. They can significantly affect ground deformation profiles, but they don’t account for the effects of geological heterogeneity and stress–strain interactions13. The influence function methods are among the most convenient approaches employed for estimating the shape and magnitude of subsidence14,15, but they may not fully account for the effects of stress–strain interactions and geological heterogeneity, which can have a significant impact on ground deformation. On the other hand theoretical methods like Numerical approaches provide a powerful, comprehensive, and adaptable framework for studying engineering systems, allowing for the consideration of complex geometries, variables, and processes that is limited to empirical and influence-function16. Among the mine subsidence prediction models, three-dimensional numerical modeling has an advantage over other models13,17 18. However, the accuracy of predicting mine subsidence with three-dimensional numerical models remains challenging due to the various inputs required, primarily geological and geotechnical aspects19,20. Therefore, to accurately predict using a three-dimensional numerical modeling technique, it is necessary to constrain rock mass properties and in-situ stress through backward modeling.

Before applying numerical modeling to solve rock mechanics problems, researchers and engineers must distinguish between different methodologies and codes21,22. Conventional numerical modeling tools for evaluating rock engineering issues consider the rock mass as either a continuum or a discontinuum23. The finite element methods (FEM) and finite difference methods (FDM) are based on the assumption that the rock mass behaves like a continuous medium24. Discrete element methods (DEM), on the other hand, assume that the rock mass is a discontinuum with a finite number of interacting singularities25,26. The boundary element method (BEM) focuses on the excavating surface rather than modeling the complex geology present as a result of the presence of various lithologies27. As a result, the ability of the boundary element method (BEM) to predict subsidence is not as powerful as that of the finite difference method (FDM) and the finite element method (FEM)28. The non-linear and non-homogeneous characteristics of the rock masses can be handled by both the finite element method (FEM) and the finite difference method (FDM). As a result, they have become critical tools for predicting mining-induced subsidence29. Combining FEM and DEM techniques is a more advanced approach to subsidence modeling30,31. This approach combines a continuum domain with discrete fractures, allowing subsequent fracturing to occur in response to changing stresses and strains. This technique, however, necessitates more computational effort32,33. Rock masses are difficult material to accurately represent via numerical modeling due to their complex geology and long history of formation34,35.

While three-dimensional numerical models offer a substantial breakthrough in mine subsidence prediction, their dependability is frequently hampered by uncertainty in key input parameters36. The classic forward modeling approach, which assumes or estimates parameters based on insufficient data, frequently results in gaps between projected and observed subsidence. This discrepancy is accentuated in complex geological settings and forested areas, where environmental sensitivity necessitates greater prediction precision. Furthermore, the inadequate integration of monitoring data for model calibration limits the models capacity to adapt to real-world conditions, thereby reducing their practical utility for decision-making and regulatory compliance. These difficulties underscore the critical need for new approaches that can limit input uncertainty. To address these constraints, the current study presents a comprehensive framework that utilizes backward modeling techniques to constrain rock mass characteristics and in-situ stress states by inferring these parameters from observed subsidence data37. The emphasis is on modeling mining-induced subsidence in wooded areas, where precise predictions are critical for ecological protection and compliance with MoEFCC requirements. This technique aims to enhance the predictive accuracy of mine subsidence models, enabling a more reliable assessment of environmental impacts and improved planning of mitigation strategies.

Knowledge gaps and objectives of the present study

Existing work on mining-induced subsidence prediction has established the general mechanisms and provided a wide range of empirical, influence-function and numerical approaches. However, most of the documented case studies are from coal mines or relatively simple layered deposits and often use two dimensional or three-dimensional formulation with idealised stratigraphy. In contrast, underground copper mines in hard-rock structurally complex terrains, like the Kolihan-Khetri region, are characterised by the strong lithological contrasts, irregular geometry of ore-bodies and anisotropic rock-mass fabrics for which systematically recorded three-dimensional subsidence investigations are limited in number.

From a methodological point of view, for many numerical applications, rock mass properties and in-situ stress fields are still directly taken from laboratory tests or generic empirical correlations without much emphasis on inverse or backward modeling to try to calibrate model parameters to observed or independent constrained deformation. This can result in non-unique parameter sets and low confidence in their quantitative predictions, particularly in those instances where regulatory decisions in environmentally sensitive and/or forested areas require robust estimates of subsidence magnitude, spatial extent and strain levels.

The Present study overcomes these knowledge and methodological shortcomings by creating a site specific three-dimensional finite element model for an underground copper ore mine in Kolihan region which explicitly captures ore body geometry, major lithological units and mining sequence while constraining rock mass and in situ stress parameters using a backward optimization procedure based on subsidence characteristics and quantifying the resulting displacement and strain fields to evaluate confinement of subsidence within existing troughs and mine planning and environmental assessment support. These enhancements improve the present model’s robustness, adaptability, and ability to capture both macroscopic subsidence trough formation and localized deformation features.

Geographical location of application area

The present field under study is located within one of India’s copper-rich belts, specifically the Khetri Copper Belt (KCB). KCB primarily consists of two large underground copper mines, namely Mine A and Mine-B. The Mining lease areas of Mine-A and Mine-B are situated at the northern tip of the KCB. The area falls within Survey of India Toposheet No. 44/P16. The geographical location of the study area is latitude N 28°00′46" to N 28°05′50" and longitude E 75°45′32" to E 75°49′53″38. The mine lease area is in sparsely populated hilly terrain and there are no towns or major roads within the lease boundary. Model inputs and geological conditions

Data sources and geotechnical inputs

For the present study, the subsidence model for Mine-B was developed using multiple sources of site-specific information, including current and future level plans and sections, the approved mining plan and method of working, and detailed geological and geotechnical reports describing the lithology and engineering properties of the ore body and host rocks (Young’s modulus, Poisson’s ratio, tensile and compressive strength, internal friction angle, etc.). All available rock-mechanics investigation reports for Mine-B were also consulted, together with in-situ stress values obtained from earlier measurements conducted by the National Institute of Rock Mechanics (NIRM) at the mine.

Geotechnical parameters for the main lithological units were mainly derived from a laboratory testing methods on intact core samples of ore, footwall and hangwall from 0, 64 and 124 mRL levels Mine-B. The geological model developed for this study serves as the foundation for the subsidence analysis and numerical simulation. The study area comprises a complex stratigraphic sequence of metamorphic rocks, including feldspar quartzite, amphibole quartzite, phyllite, and sericite quartzite, each with distinct mechanical properties that influence the ground response. Structural discontinuities such as joints, faults, and lithological contacts play a critical role in subsidence propagation and were explicitly incorporated into the model. These discontinuities not only affect stress redistribution but also act as potential planes of weakness, impacting the overall deformation mechanism. This comprehensive geological framework was then translated into a three-dimensional mesh using the Strand7 FEM platform, ensuring that the simulation accurately reflects actual field conditions and supports the accurate prediction of surface subsidence under ongoing and future mining operations.

Topographic, geological and mining characteristics

The western and eastern slopes of the mineralised hill have different strike directions and inclinations and in general, the western slope is rougher, steeper, and more challenging to traverse as compared to the eastern slope. From a modeling point of view, this system of ridges and valleys and the related slope geometry was adopted in defining the lateral limits and surface boundary conditions of three-dimensional numerical model. The asymmetric relief around the mineralised hill and the presence of the Kharkhara valley indicate the most possible zones of stress concentration and accumulation of vertical settlement and therefore served as the guide to the placement of profile lines and observation points in the simulations. These topographic controls are explicitly considered in the interpretation of the modelled subsidence troughs and strain distributions shown in the latter sections. Research methodology

The general methodology followed in this study is a structured numerical modelling workflow to predict subsidence at Mine-B. It starts from literature-based identification of modelling approaches and choice of a proper three-dimensional numerical framework. The model is then developed using systematic pre-processing (geometry construction, meshing and assignment of boundary conditions, in-situ stresses and rock-mass properties), solution and post-processing. A backward analysis is used to constrain key parameters using available geotechnical and stress information and the calibrated model is finally used for forward prediction under existing and future mining scenarios. Backward modeling optimisation

In order to reduce the uncertainty in the numerical model input and to get realistic prediction of mining induced subsidence, backward (inverse) modeling approach was adopted. In the first stage, initial values of rock mass elastic and strength parameters (e.g. E, ν, c, ϕ) for each lithological unit were obtained through laboratory testing and rock mass classification and the initial in-situ stress field was estimated from the previously measured stress data and stress information of the region. These parameters were implemented on the FEM-based software STRAND7 to build up a baseline three-dimensional model, and forward simulations were performed for the virgin, the current, the next five years and the next ten years mining stages.

The surface subsidence and strain patterns obtained from the baseline simulations were then compared with available information on the expected extent and magnitude of surface deformation over Mine-B including observed location of existing subsidence troughs and horizontal strain in the XX (E-W) and YY (N-S) directions (in mm/m). Where the predicted maximum vertical displacement, trough width or strain distribution was inconsistent with this information, selected rock-mass parameters and stress magnitudes were repeatedly adjusted within physically reasonable bounds and the simulations repeated. This backward optimisation procedure offers a systematic approach for improving the combination of rock-mass properties and in-situ stress to ensure that the response of the model is consistent with the field-based subsidence characteristics to improve confidence in the following subsidence assessment.

The virgin-state results indicate negligible surface strain in both horizontal directions, confirming that significant strain development is associated with the excavation-induced redistribution of stresses rather than with the natural pre-mining condition. In contrast, the post-mining scenarios show a clear increase in surface strain, with maximum XX strain increasing from 9 mm/m in the current mining stage to 10 mm/m in both the next 5 years and next 10 years scenarios. Similarly, the maximum YY strain increases from 19 mm/m in the current stage to 19.5 mm/m and 19.9 mm/m in the next 5 years and next 10 years scenarios, respectively. Thus, the difference among the three mined cases is relatively small, indicating that future extraction causes only marginal intensification of the existing deformation field rather than a major expansion of its spatial extent.

In addition, from Figs. 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20 and 21, it is also observed that developed surface strain (in XX and YY directions) is a part of pre-existing subsidence troughs confined within a common break line. However, on the footwall side, surface strain in the XX-direction in all three mining situations develops outside the lower trough (South side) to some extent only. However, its value ranges from 9.0 to 10 mm/m only. Observed maximum surface strain in YY-direction in all three mining situations, which varies from 19.0 to 19.90 mm/m, is also a part of the pre-existing lower subsidence trough (south side) and confined within a small area. Further, in general, outside the pre-existing subsidence troughs (which are confined within a common break line), no surface strain is observed in any of the directions (XX and YY directions) in all three mining situations.

The major observation is that a significant strain is mostly confined around some already existing subsidence troughs and a break line. Beyond these known domains of troughs, the model does not predict any significant strain formation in any of the three mining cases. The extension on the footwall side is only observed to be limited in the XX direction and even there the values are relatively modest. The behaviour implies that the redistribution of stress caused by mining in the future is being taken up primarily in a pre-existing disturbed geomechanical field as opposed to spreading the footprint of deformation over a large area. This matters as far as subsidence-management is concerned since it implies that further extraction would at most result in localized deformation within previously known impact regions rather than create truly new zones of intense surface strain.

The results of the strain analysis are significant in showing that the strain in YY direction is sufficiently higher than the strain in XX direction under all scenarios that occur after mining takes place. This shows that there is anisotropic deformation of the surface and not uniform radial response. Such anisotropy is probably controlled by the orientation of the orebody, the geometry and sequencing of stopes and the structural-geological framework represented at the calibrated model. In other words, the redistribution of stresses is determined by the mining layout and heterogeneity of the rock mass, which results in a stronger extension-compression effect in one of the principal horizontal directions than in the other. This is an important geomechanical insight as it indicates that the strain response is not simply governed by the volume of extraction, however, but the structure of the mine system in a directional manner.

The close similarity of the current, 5-year and 10-year strain fields is further evidence for this interpretation. The model shows that the changes in the future position of the mining are relatively small compared to the present mined-out geometry, and as a consequence, the resulting strain field is also broadly stable in extent. This is a good planning consideration, as in the simulated mine configuration, the controls on surface strain seem to be more a geometry of the established troughs and accumulated excavation history and less the creation of new deformation corridors at a rapid rate.

The displacement behaviour is consistent with the strain results in demonstrating that the principal subsidence response is confined in the areas that were already linked to the surface disturbance. The fact that there is greater downward movement along Sect. 3–3 indicates that this alignment is in a more responsive section of the subsidence system, which could be the cumulative influence of mine geometry, topographic location, and the relative location of the two troughs. The slight variation between the current and 5-year and 10-year scenarios once again shows that the extra intended extraction does not change the overall deformation basin essentially in the simulated conditions. Rather, it mainly changes the scale of response within a domain which is already determined.

The practical use of this finding is that the monitoring and mitigation activities need to be emphasized on the current trough system and its margins as opposed to the belief on equal expansion of the surface impact area in the future. Again, it supports the significance of sectional analysis, since even within the same mining phase, the magnitude of displacements can be extremely different in various localities due to the local geometries between the extraction areas and the underlying ground plane.

Angle of draw and its interpretation

Angle of draw is a significant parameter of subsidence which determines the lateral surface influence as compared to the depth of mining. The angle of draw is measured as the angle between the vertical drawn from the edge of the extracted zone and the line joining that point to the outer limit of significant surface deformation. In the present study, the modelled subsidence response shows that the angle of draw is relatively stable through the current, next 5 years and next 10 years mining stages. This is consistent with the results of the plan view strain and the sectional displacement showing that the significant deformation is still confined mostly within the existing subsidence troughs and a common break line with negligible outward expansion into previously unaffected ground.

Such behaviour indicates that the dominant subsidence geometry already exists through the current excavation plan and immediate response of the rock-masses. Thus, future mining is not expected to significantly widen the surface influence area, but rather to intensify deformation mainly within the already established disturbed zone. An engineering aspect of such interpretation is that it is useful in defining the boundaries of monitoring and prioritizing the management of subsidence along the current trough margins and break-line controlled areas.

Academic and industrial importance of the research

The calibrated three-dimensional numerical model in the current study presents a quantitative basis for evaluation of mining induced subsidence at Mine-B. The simulations show a maximum in downward vertical displacement of about 40 cm in the analysed sections in any of the three mining scenarios (current, next five years and next ten years) compared to the virgin state. Surface strains in the XX and YY directions range from about 0.1 mm/m in stable areas to peak values of about 10 mm/m (XX) and 19.90 mm/m (YY) in the subsidence troughs. These results indicate that significant deformation remains spatially confined to the existing troughs and the common break line, while vertical displacement and surface strain outside these zones are very low or essentially zero.

From an academic point of view, the work shows the potential of a site-specific three-dimensional FEM model coupled with backward optimisation of rock mass and in-situ stress parameters to reproduce both magnitude and spatial pattern of subsidence and strain in a structurally complex hard rock copper mine. This addresses the methodological gap to the previous studies in which uncalibrated parameter sets were used and only limited quantitative performance metrics were reported. The explicit study of strain and displacement ranges, along with their restriction to pre-existing troughs, provides a transparent benchmark for future numerical studies in similar geological environments.

From an industrial perspective, the findings of this study are helpful in directing mining operations towards safer and more efficient extraction processes. Mining-induced subsidence poses serious threats to infrastructure, environmental stability, and worker safety. Mining businesses can better predict and control surface impacts by precisely modeling projected ground deformations and strain distributions, lowering economic losses and liability. The computational approach used enables scenario planning across present and future mining stages, resulting in optimal mine design and extraction sequencing. Furthermore, the methodology helps regulators and stakeholders assess environmental compliance and disaster readiness. Overall, this study offers practical solutions to subsidence-related concerns while promoting sustainable and responsible mining practices.

Conclusions and future scope

This study presented the results of a detailed three-dimensional analysis of mining-induced subsidence at Mine-B in the Khetri Copper Belt. Geological data were used to develop a computational model that comprised the orebody and the surrounding rock, which were primarily constituted of feldspar quartzite, amphibole quartzite, phyllite, and sericite quartzite. Numerical results clearly demonstrate the capability of the FEM approach in reproducing the surface strain and displacement in X and Y direction and displacement in Z direction. The results show that the maximum downward vertical displacement along the analysed sections is of the order of a few decimetres (around 0.40 m), while surface strains in the XX and YY directions range from approximately 0.1 mm/m in stable areas to peak values of about 10 mm/m and 19.90 mm/m, respectively, within the subsidence troughs. It is also observed that developed surface strain (in XX and YY directions) is a part of pre-existing subsidence troughs confined within a common break line. It is further observed from Fig. 25 that, in and around the upper trough along Sect. 3–3, there is a slight downward displacement in all three mining situations with respect to the virgin state. Further, in general, outside the pre-existing subsidence troughs confined within a common break line, no significant displacement in the Z-direction is observed.

Predicted subsidence requires calibration using monitoring data to enhance its accuracy. Therefore, management has to develop a monitoring method to produce data for calibrating the predicted subsidence. Nonetheless, slope instability difficulties may occur due to the formation and collapse of troughs. Consequently, management must implement suitable steps to resolve this issue. Further, in the mining industry, the current way of preventing blast holes has an effect on the stability and safety of the rock structure and surface. In addition, the growing area of subsidence resulted in increased pressure on land acquisition, slope stability, soil conservation, and mine reclamation. The mine faced risks to its economic viability and safe operation, along with potential permanent environmental damage in the vicinity. It is recommended that surface subsidence management and ground control of the mine-out region be implemented in order to accomplish an all-win situation for the environment and the economy.
