A correlational study on the factors influencing burnout, job satisfaction, and turnover intention among emergency department nurses in the hail region of Saudi Arabia
Authors: Nojoud Abdullah Alrashidi, Chandrakala Sankarapandian, Abdulrahman Sulaim Almutairi, Allen Joshua Dominguez, Reynita Saguban, Sandro Villareal, Maysa M Mohsen, Sahar Hamdi El Sayed Abdelmaksoud, Sahar Mahmoud Abdulla Hashim, Analita Gonzales, Grace Ann Lagura, Jeneth Gutierrez, Nuha Ayad H Alatawi, Sameer A Alkubati, Evalynn Rondilla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEmergency department nurses play a critical role in providing quality care to patients, and gauging the degree of their burnout and its root causes holds significant importance. This study investigated the demographics and other factors associated with burnout, job satisfaction, and turnover among nurses in the Emergency Department (ED) in the Hail region of Saudi Arabia. A cross-sectional correlational method was employed. It included 370 nurses in the ED of seven hospitals in Hail region of the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia using self-selected volunteer sampling via WhatsApp professional groups. Data were collected using standard questionnaires to measure burnout, job satisfaction, and turnover intention, from December 2025- January 2026. A conventional response rate could not be calculated due to unknown group membership. Female nurses reported lower job satisfaction than male nurses (B = − 1.19, p = .037). Mid-career and experienced nurses reported lower satisfaction than new graduates (B = − 2.05, p < .001; B = − 2.38, p < .001), and higher burnout (B = 8.63, p = .009; B = 11.02, p = .001). Nurses with master’s degrees reported higher burnout than those with bachelor’s degrees (B = 7.84, p = .011). Nurses in large hospitals reported lower burnout (B = − 15.43, p = .013) yet also lower job satisfaction (B = − 3.38, p = .025) than those in small hospitals. Nurses in peer support and professional development reported lower turnover intention than those in mentorship programs (B = − 0.35, p = .038; B = − 0.42, p = .008), but also lower job satisfaction (B = − 2.85, p < .001; B = − 3.77, p < .001). Job satisfaction was negatively correlated with burnout (r = –.589, p < .001) and turnover intention (r = –.569, p < .001), while burnout was positively correlated with turnover intention (r = .683, p < .001). New graduate nurses reported higher satisfaction and lower burnout than experienced nurses. Master’s degree holders demonstrate higher burnout than their counterparts. Smaller hospitals have both better job satisfaction and higher burnout, indicating two separate JD-R paths. Both peer support and continuing education demonstrated a lower level of turnover intention compared to mentorship programs, but related to low job satisfaction. Compared to public hospital, private hospital nurses demonstrated higher turnover intentions in multi-variable analyses. Low job satisfaction was extremely related to high burnout and high turnover intentions and supports the importance of addressing these issues to help retain nurses. Caution should be applied when considering these results to a larger population due to the voluntary nature of the sample from the Hail Region.Keywords: Burnout, Job Satisfaction, Turnover Intention, Emergency Department, NursesSubject terms: Health care, Health occupations, Psychology, Psychology

Introduction

Emergency department (ED) nursing is generally viewed by all parties involved as a high-stress area of practice. As such, it contributes to higher rates of burnout among ED nurses and therefore, greater rates of nurse turnover. The RN turnover rate in the US currently stands at 17.6%1 in 2025. Similar findings are reported from studies conducted in other countries including Ethiopia2; sub-Saharan Africa3 and4 China5 also presents findings consistent with this trend. Similarly, Saudi Arabia has experienced similar difficulties within its emergency departments.

Conceptualized through the lens of the JD-R model (Job Demands Resources), burnout occurs when the job demands placed upon an employee exceeds the resources available to recover. Therefore, depleting the employees physical and psychological reserves6–8. Such depletions create negative attitudes towards both the work environment and job itself. There are empirical studies that have found a direct relationship between increases in burnout and decreases in job satisfaction9,10. Conversely, work environments which provide support for job satisfaction may be able to reduce burnout and subsequently reduce intent to turnover10.

Another key outcome dynamic related to burnout is nurse turnover. As is clearly documented in the literature, burnout is a major reason why many nurses plan to leave their positions. Specifically, researchers have identified that emotional exhaustion and depersonalization contribute significantly to workforce attrition9,11. Furthermore, high levels of turnover result in a variety of problems including decreased team cohesiveness; recruitment and training costs for new staff; and most importantly, compromise patient safety and quality of care12. Thus, in order to improve these clinical outcomes, retaining current staff members in emergency departments becomes strategically important since the degree of staffing stability will directly affect clinical outcomes13.

This study examines how professional experiences, demographic factors and hospital attributes associated with each other to relate the trajectory of job satisfaction, burnout and turnover intent. Early career-nurses transitioning from an academic environment to practicing clinically often experience a ‘transitional shock’14. This would likely be reflected in reduced job satisfaction scores. Moreover, as nurses become more experienced/senior, chronic stressors can accumulate causing a reduction in available coping skills and an increase in burnout15–17. Gender-related concerns due to work-place culture and work-life conflict also contribute additional layers of complexity to the picture18,19. Educational backgrounds of nurses and the access to continuing education and professional development opportunities also influence the trajectory of satisfaction20,21. Hospital size22, ownership structure23 and their relative availability of resources and general organizational climate, all can influence the relationship between these variables. For example, small hospitals tend to provide greater opportunity for a nurse to establish close bonds with patients and the community that enhances job satisfaction24. Meanwhile, differences in organizational culture based on hospital ownership type23, have also been found related to both job satisfaction and employee retention.

To date, little research has been conducted examining job satisfaction, burnout and turnover intent among ED registered nurses in Saudi Arabia. The physical and psychological demands associated with working in the ED are distinctly different than those associated with working in critical care settings. As such, it is imperative to conduct studies focusing on this specific group of professionals. In the local context, Hail city in northern region of Saudi Arabia currently experiencing significant economic and population growth. Hail city contains a mix of private/public health facilities servicing a primarily rural/urban patient base. Although the health care system in the region is growing at a very rapid rate, there is an almost complete lack of research concerning the nursing workforce within the Hail region. Therefore, this study will serve as a model for developing nursing retention strategies for ED within regions with similar emerging health care systems.

Methods

Study design, setting, and participants

This research employed a cross-sectional correlational design. Data were collected from seven Hospitals (Public and Private) within the Hail Region of Saudi Arabia during December 2025- January 2026.

Using the OpenEpi sample size calculator for population surveys (http://www.openepi.com/), with an expected frequency (prevalence) of 50%, 95% confidence level, and 5% margin of error, the minimum required sample was calculated as 248. We obtained 370 complete responses, exceeding both the OpenEpi estimate and the minimum threshold of 10–15 participants per predictor variable for multiple regression analysis25.

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were the following: Participants had to be working as an ED Nurse with at least six months of experience in an ED, and had to be practicing full-time bedside nursing. All supervisors, administrators, and part-time employees were excluded. The online Google Form did not include mandatory screening questions to automatically terminate ineligible respondents. Eligibility relied entirely on self-reported adherence to the criteria stated in the invitation message. This introduces risk of ineligible participation, which represents a limitation of the data collection method.

Data collection

An invitation to participate was distributed via several ED professional WhatsApp groups. These WhatsApp groups were used as primary means of communicating with nursing staff at each of the seven participating hospitals. A link to an electronic version of the eligibility criteria was contained within the invitation. Moreover, a QR code linking participants to a Google Forms survey was included. Once scanned, participants would view an informational page that described the study and sign their electronic informed consent prior to completing the questionnaire.

The exact number of ED nurses in Hail that belong to the same WhatsApp groups where they receive communications from other ED nurses is unknown. Since there is no formal registry of group membership, the exact coverage rate of this recruitment method cannot be determined relative to the total target population of approximately 700 ED nurses. As such, a traditional response rate cannot be calculated. Any ED nurse that does not use WhatsApp; not a member of those specific professional WhatsApp groups; or were on leave during the collection period (December 2025 to January 2026) would have been systematically excluded.

Measures

Sociodemographic and Occupational Variables. The researchers gathered sociodemographic and occupational variables including: age (less than 25, 26–30, greater than 30); gender; years of work experience as a registered nurse in the ED (new graduate (less than 1 year), mid-career (1–5 years), experienced (> 6 years)); degree level (bachelors, masters); support systems (mentorship programs (reference), peer support groups, professional development opportunities); size of the hospital (less than 100 beds (small reference), 100–300 beds (medium), greater than 300 beds (large)), location of the hospital (urban/city, rural/village), and type of hospital (public reference, private).

Job Satisfaction. Job satisfaction was measured using the 5-item scale by Judge et al.26. It uses a 7-point Likert scale rating (1 = strongly disagree to 7 = strongly agree). Example of items under the job satisfaction includes: “I feel fairly satisfied with my current job.“; “Most days I am enthusiastic about my work.“, “Each day at work seems like it will never end. (R)”. The researchers reversed negative wording, the average scores were determined thereafter. Average scores ranged from 5 to 35. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.83. This 5-item scale was selected over longer alternatives (e.g., the Minnesota Satisfaction Questionnaire) to minimize respondent burden in a busy ED setting while maintaining acceptable reliability and validity.

Turnover Intention. Turnover intentions were measured using the 3-item scale by Michaels and Spector27. Each item was scored on a 6-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 6 = strongly agree). Example of items include: “I often think about quitting my present job.“, “I will probably look for a new job in the next year”, and, “If I have my own way, I will be working for this organization one year from now. (R)”. The reverse-scored item was calculated as (7 minus the response value), and all three items were summed after reverse-scoring. Range: 3–18 with higher scores indicate greater intention to leave. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.79. This 3-item scale was chosen over the 6-item turnover intention scale28 due to its brevity and established validity in healthcare workforce research.

Burnout. Burnout was conceptualized as a multidimensional construct comprising emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and decreased personal accomplishment29, and was measured using the six-item short version of the Shirom-Melamed Burnout Measure (SMBM-6) as validated30. It has two scales with three-item sub-scales each: Emotional/Physical Exhaustion and Cognitive Weariness. Items were ranked on a seven-point Likert scale (1 = almost never to 7 = almost always). Example items under emotional/physical exhaustion include “I feel fed up at my work”, “I feel like my “batteries” are “dead.” and “my thinking process is slow”. For the subscale cognitive weariness, items include “I feel I am not thinking clearly’, “I have difficulty concentrating”, and; “I have difficulty thinking about complex things”. The sub-scale averages were calculated (range = 3–21 each), and a total burnout score was calculated as the sum of both sub-scale averages × 1.5 to be comparable with the original SMBM measure (possible range: 9–63). A higher total score represents more burnout. Cronbach’s alpha was 0.84 for emotional/physical exhaustion and 0.82 for cognitive weariness. The SMBM-6 was selected over the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI) because it measures burnout as a unified construct rather than three separate dimensions, aligning with recent theoretical developments and reducing respondent burden.

Data analysis

The data were evaluated using IBM SPSS Version 26. The descriptive statistics such as means, standard deviations, frequencies, and percentiles, were used to present an overview of the study population and study variables. Independent samples t-test was used to compare differences in continuous study variables across categorical variables. One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s HSD posthoc test analysis was utilized when comparing continuous study variables with three or more categories. Correlations between continuous and/or ordinal study variables were assessed utilizing Pearson correlations. To investigate the correlates of job satisfaction, burnout, and intentions to leave their jobs, three multiple linear regression analyses were performed. For variable selection into each model, the dual process framework of the JD-R Model31 was employed. This model suggests that when job demands are higher than available resources to handle these demands, burnout occurs due to the Health Impairment Pathway. In contrast, sufficient job resources can positively affect job satisfaction via the Motivational Pathway. Finally, turnover intent is viewed as a final result of both processes.

Variables were selected based on their theoretical alignment with these pathways: job resources and individual characteristics for the motivational pathway (job satisfaction), job demands and health impairment indicators for the burnout pathway, and both sets for turnover intention.

For Model 1 (job satisfaction), the independent variables entered were gender, years of experience, support system, hospital size, and hospital type. These represent job resources and individual characteristics within the motivational pathway of the JD-R model. For Model 2 (burnout), the independent variables entered were years of experience, education, hospital size, and hospital type, representing the health impairment pathway. Gender and support system were excluded from this model as they showed no bivariate correlation with burnout and lack theoretical grounding as job demands in the JD-R framework for this population. For Model 3 (turnover intention), variables from both pathways were included (experience, education, support system, hospital size, and hospital type), as turnover intention represents a distal outcome affected by both motivational and impairment processes. Age categories were included in each model due to the conceptual overlap between age and experience (r = .72, p < .01).

We acknowledge that theory-driven entry of all theoretically relevant variables regardless of bivariate significance would have been preferable to the approach used here. However, given the exploratory nature of this regional study and the risk of overfitting with a limited sample, we retained variables showing both theoretical relevance and empirical association. Future confirmatory research should employ full theory-driven specification.

Dummy coding was used for categorical variables as specified above. To assess multicollinearity, we used Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) for each variable. We considered all VIF values < 5 to indicate acceptable multicollinearity32 and found all of our final VIF values to be below this value. We treated missing data by deleting cases from listwise. Since the Likert scales were treated as continuous variables for regression and correlation analysis, we assumed equal interval spacing among categories and an approximately normal distribution of the data. For statistical analyses, we determined significance to be p < .05.

Ethics consideration

This research received approval from the Institutional Review Board of Hail Health Cluster (H-2025-10). Moreover, it adheres to the principles described in the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to participants completing surveys via Google Forms, informed consent was obtained electronically on a voluntary basis. Responses remained anonymous. No participant names or IP address information were recorded. Participants were assured their response(s) would remain anonymous/confidential; they could withdraw from participating at any time during the study without any penalty.

Individual personality factors, work load indicators, leadership quality and an organization’s culture all have substantial impact on job satisfaction and were therefore not included in this study.

Predictors of burnout

The regression model accounted for 10.5% of the variance (R² = 0.105; adjusted R² = 0.100; p < .001). A lot of the variability in burnout was attributed to unmeasurable variables, including job-specific demands, how individuals cope with their jobs, and an individual’s personal resilience.

Burnout among mid- career nurses (B = 8.63; p = .009) were greater than that of new graduates. Also, there was greater burn-out found among experienced nurse staff (B = 11.02; p = .001) than among graduate level nurses. Nurses who had earned Master’s degrees had more burnout than Bachelor’s degree (B = 7.84; p = .011). Furthermore, the nurses working in larger hospitals demonstrated less burnout than did those in smaller hospitals (B = -15.43; p = .013). The coefficients for large hospitals should be viewed with caution because the “reference group” (small hospitals) consisted of only 11 participants (3.0%); thus, this finding is likely spurious.

10% of the variance is explained by the model, which shows that hospital ownership and support systems are the two most important variables contributing to an employee’s job satisfaction. However, there is still considerable variance that can be attributed to unmeasured variables, including career opportunities, organizational commitment, and the employee’s personal circumstances.

Nurse participants who were part of peer support groups reported less intent to leave their jobs compared to nurse participants who were part of mentoring programs (B = -0.35, p = .038). Nurse participants who had access to professional development opportunities reported less intent to leave their jobs compared to nurse participants who were part of mentoring programs (B = -0.42, p = .008). Lastly, nurse participants working in private hospitals reported greater intent to leave their jobs compared to nurse participants working in public hospitals (B = 0.25, p = .010).

This multiple variable association controlled for all possible variables, presents a contradictory result to the non-significant bivariate comparison (see Table 1, p = .424), indicating that the relationship between hospital ownership and turnover intention does not exist until the potential confounders such as type of support system and hospital size have been accounted for.

Discussion

Synthesis through the job demands-resources framework

The findings of this research is most understandable using a dual process framework developed from the JD-R model7,8,31. As we synthesized the results by pathway (i.e., all associations were analyzed together), the modest explained variance (i.e., R² = 0.198 for job satisfaction; R² = 0.105 for burnout; R² = 0.100 for turnover intentions) in these outcome variables can be attributed to measured factors. Many other unmeasured factors may have contributed to the remaining variation. This includes but not limited to, specific aspects of their workload, leadership within their organization, organizational culture, personality traits, coping mechanisms, and individualized life circumstance. Therefore, the above-mentioned predictor variables should be viewed as partial associates and not complete explanations of each respective outcome variable.

New graduates were found to report greater job satisfaction than mid-career and experienced nurses. This could be due to the motivational effect of recent career entry combined with the availability of new training, excitement associated with the newness of their role, and high levels of optimism. Lower job satisfaction among female nurses and in larger hospital settings18 indicate that these two groups may be experiencing a relative deficit in available job resources. This includes autonomy, recognition, or support for balancing family and work responsibilities which were not adequately captured in our measurements. Smaller hospitals had the greatest job satisfaction scores and also had the greatest burnout scores. However, this subgroup comprised only 11 participants (3.0%), making these estimates potentially unstable. If replicated in larger samples, this pattern would be consistent with the JD-R model. Community ties may enhance satisfaction through the motivational pathway, while limited staffing may elevate burnout through the health impairment pathway7,8. Lower turnover intention among nurses in peer support and professional development (vs. mentorship) supports the JD-R resource-building hypothesis8. However, the low explained variance indicates that unmeasured distal factors career opportunities, organizational commitment, personal circumstances substantially influence turnover decisions33,34.

Factors affecting job satisfaction

Counterintuitive finding that peer support and professional development were associated with lower satisfaction than mentorship (B = − 2.85, p < .001 and B = − 3.77, p < .001) requires explanation. One possibility is variability in implementation quality across institutions rather than support type per se35,36. Alternatively, selection bias may be present if nurses experiencing higher baseline stress self-select into peer support and professional development. Such possibility cannot be evaluated with the current data.

The suppression effect for hospital ownership indicates that public hospital nurses’ lower turnover intention reflects unmeasured job security and pension benefits23. This becomes apparent only after controlling for confounders. Private healthcare organizations should therefore develop formalized peer support initiatives to address this retention gap. Alternative explanations should also be considered. First, the lower satisfaction among peer support and professional development participants may reflect self-selection. Nurses experiencing higher baseline stress or dissatisfaction may seek out these support types, creating a spurious association. Second, the reference category (mentorship programs) may have been implemented more effectively in this specific context, and the observed difference may not generalize to other settings. Third, the modest explained variance (R² = 0.198) indicates substantial unmeasured confounding, and the significant coefficients for support type may be unstable. These possibilities cannot be distinguished with the current cross-sectional data.

Factors affecting burnout

Mid-career professionals and experienced staff reported significantly higher levels of burnout compared to new graduate nurses. Further, master’s degree holders reported higher burnout than bachelor’s degree holders (B = 7.84, p = .011), likely due to enhanced responsibility and role expectations35,37. Nurses employed in larger hospitals had reduced burnout levels (B = − 15.43, p = .013), which likely results from access to better resource allocation and organizational support systems.

Factors affecting turnover intentions

The finding that peer support and professional development were associated with lower satisfaction than mentorship should be interpreted cautiously. This is because, given the modest explained variance and may reflect unmeasured confounding variables such as implementation quality or self-selection bias. One possible explanation, requiring direct testing, is that these support types address different JD-R pathways. They may provide sufficient resources to reduce turnover intent while being perceived as inadequate substitutes for personalized mentorship guidance.The general claim is supported by the literature that colleagues supporting each other through peer mentoring or peer-supported professional development is related to reduced turnover intent. This is compared with a traditionally mentor-centered approach, through improved psychosocial support, social capital, feeling of belonging, and career clarity37,39.

Correlation among study variables

The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The following interpretation describes theoretically expected sequential relationships, not empirically confirmed causal chains. The strong correlations between job satisfaction, burnout, and turnover intention are consistent with the JD-R Model’s theoretical pathways. Though directionality cannot be determined from cross-sectional data. Such a result is consistent with the JD-R model’s premise that prolonged high job demands coupled with insufficient resources are associated with increased job attrition7,8. In this case, causal direction cannot be established. Further, the results indicate that burnout and job satisfaction are distinct but strongly correlated aspects of well-being. This is consistent with theoretical feedback loop descriptions38,40. Given that the regression models explained only 10–20% of variance, these correlations should not be overinterpreted as comprehensive explanations of the relationships between these variables.

Current result found that there is a greater prevalence of burnout among mid-career and senior level nursing staff working at high intensity within emergency departments. This indicates that the sustained and intense clinical demands of working in ED settings may precipitate what has been termed a “resource depletion cycle.” That is, repeated exposure to high levels of stressful events is associated with depleted physical and psychological resources7,8.

Conversely, the inverse relationship between support systems (e.g., mentoring programs and opportunities for professional development), and turnover intentions supports the JD-R Models’ resource building hypotheses. In other words, institutional resources play a critical role in mitigating the effects of high levels of job demands on employees. As such, it provides support for the idea that organizations can protect their employees from the typical “demand-induced strain” associated with turnover by providing adequate resources to help alleviate such strains.

Considering the rapid expansion of the health care infrastructure within Hail, the importance of this research becomes even more pronounced. The findings clearly show that emergency room nurses are particularly susceptible to experiencing both “transition shock” and chronic stressors. This is due to the disparity between high-level clinical demands placed upon them and inadequate organizational resources to support their needs. As a result, our study affirms that the JD-R Model is an extremely viable theoretical framework for understanding nurse retention issues within the Saudi Health care system. Therefore, nurse retention appears to be more directly related to the availability of organizational resources rather than simply a matter of individual resilience.

Study implications

Hospital institution should reduce the number of hours nurses have to work per week and find a better balance in how many nurses are on each shift to prevent burnout. The findings that peer support and professional development were associated with less likely to leave suggests using these types of programs may also decrease turnover. It is important for hospitals to monitor how well they implement these because the studies found them to be associated with lower job satisfaction. Large hospitals offer structural resources (i.e. nurse-to-patient ratio) that help to mitigate burnout, while smaller hospitals may benefit from more staffing support to counteract potential isolation, as evidenced by the fact that small hospitals have stronger community ties.

Like all other professions, nurses continue to participate in continuing education; take special course/seminars/workshops; and pursue degrees of higher learning through organization sponsored/self-directed study that represents a type of self-development. Organizations may support self-development by offering financial resources for reimbursement of tuition costs and/or scholarships; supporting training sessions on managing stress; supporting seminar opportunities for developing leadership skills; and reimbursing/paying for travel/participation in national/international conferences of professional associations.

Limitations

The results of this study are subject to several limitations that must be taken into account when analyzing them. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference. The observed associations may be bidirectional or reversed. For example, nurses with higher turnover intention may report lower satisfaction as a consequence of their departure plans, rather than dissatisfaction causing turnover intent. This means that there could be reciprocal relationships between burnout, job satisfaction and intentions to leave a job.

One major limitation of this research was using an available sample from a convenience sample, through WhatsApp groups. As such, there will be some degree of selection bias present as well as limitations of how representative the results of the research are. The sampling procedure for this study most probably represents those nurses who are digitally connected; have large peer networks and work during their daytime hours (when they can monitor their messaging) versus those who experience social isolation and/or have high levels of burn-out and/or work at night. Thus, systematic differences in representing the relationships between support systems, burn-out and intention to leave employment would occur. Additionally, since there is no formal membership record of each of the approximately 700 emergency department nurses employed by Hail’s seven hospitals it is impossible to determine the extent to which this research sample covered all of them. Therefore, these results need to be viewed cautiously and may not generalize to emergency department nurses beyond the specific digital networking structure or location.

Additional potential biases exist due to reliance upon self-reporting instruments, which can elicit socially desirable responses; however, the anonymous web-based format likely mitigates, though does not eliminate, this bias. This study examined burnout, job satisfaction, and turnover intent specifically within one geographical area, Hail, and therefore does not allow us to make assumptions about how the findings may apply to other areas within Saudi Arabia. Further, the absence of electronic screening questions in the Google Form meant eligibility relied entirely on self-report. Ineligible respondents (e.g., part-time nurses, supervisors, or those with < 6 months experience) may have participated, potentially contaminating the dataset.

Likert scale data was analyzed as continuous variable data for purposes of both regression analysis and correlational analysis. Common in nursing research, treating Likert scales as continuous variable data assumes equal intervals between each category and that the distribution of data is normally distributed. These assumptions were not tested in this study. Future studies should examine whether ordinal regression methods may provide a better fit for examining relationships between these types of data. The same concern applies to the use of Pearson’s correlation coefficients. All three regression models explained less than 20% of the total variability in their respective dependent variables (R² = 0.198 for job satisfaction, R² = 0.105 for burnout, R² = 0.100 for turnover intention), suggesting that many factors influencing burnout, job satisfaction, and turnover intent were not measured.

Lastly, the use of p-values, confidence intervals, and regression on a non-probability convenience sample with self-selection bias does not support statistical generalization to the population of Hail ED nurses. These statistics describe internal patterns within our sample and should be interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Caution is warranted in extending conclusions beyond this specific volunteer sample.

Conclusion

The current study shows that emergency nurse retention in the Hail region has unique JD-R processes. The results show that recent graduate nurses have a significantly greater level of job satisfaction and less burn-out then do experience nurses; whereas small hospitals have an extremely high level of job satisfaction but also extremely high levels of burn-out. Peer support and professional development are the most effective forms of reducing turnover intentions compared to mentorship as a single form of professional development. Additionally, there was significant variability in how well peer support and professional development were implemented. This variability can influence whether or not they positively affect job satisfaction. After statistically adjusting for potential confounding variables (i.e., years working as an RN) private hospital nurses had significantly greater levels of turnover intentions than did public hospital nurses. It would be important to investigate further the nature of the job-security benefits provided to employees in public versus private settings. These findings illustrate the need to develop targeted interventions that address both JD-R motivational and impairment-related pathways to enhance retention rates in regional emergency departments. Due to the limitations of using a volunteer sample, caution should be exercised when making conclusions from these data. Further research with multiple sites over time will be required to establish if these relationships exist across different contexts.
