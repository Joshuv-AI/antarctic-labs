Feasibility Study of a Smartphone App and Web Portal for Remote Monitoring of Chronic Kidney Disease and Peritoneal Dialysis Populations: A Mixed-Method Study
Authors: Htay Htay, Arsh K. Jain, Sheena Gow, Priyanka Arya, Joel Fernandes, Mandar Gori, Ravichandran Kaliappan, Wei Wang, Marjorie Wai Yin Foo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRemote patient monitoring facilitates the management of chronic conditions like chronic kidney disease (CKD) and home-based peritoneal dialysis (PD). This study assessed the acceptance of a remote monitoring application in CKD and PD patients.MethodsIt was a single-center study at Singapore General Hospital from December 2021 to February 2023, recruiting CKD and PD patients. Patients were followed up for up to 6 months. The Viva Konnect patient app allowed users to record vitals with a Bluetooth-enabled device, log medication schedule, track symptoms, log diet, and monitor PD therapy.ResultsA total of 35 participants (13 CKD, 22 PD) enrolled; 8 (5 CKD, 3 PD) dropped out, leaving 27 (8 CKD, 19 PD) who completed the study. Among those who completed the study, 56% met the app acceptance criteria – defined as CKD patients logging data at least twice per week and PD patients at least once daily. Acceptance rates were 47.4% for PD and 75.0% for CKD. PD patients used the app a median of 6.7 (IQR: 1.8–16.5) times per week, spending 6.9 (IQR: 2.2–12.1) minutes per session. CKD patients used it a median of 6.2 (IQR: 2.6–6.7) times per week, with a median session time of 4.7 (IQR: 1.7–7.7) minutes. The median percentage of vitals recorded via smart companion devices was 52.9% (IQR: 26.7–82.2) for PD patients and 83.4% (IQR: 78.8–94.4) for CKD patients. Interviews identified three key themes that affected the usage: communication, convenience, and clarity.ConclusionOverall app usage was moderate, with the CKD patient population showing better app acceptance than the PD population. To improve the usage and overall engagement, future app development should incorporate two-way communication with clinicians, a reduced burden of data recording, and better integration into established clinical workflows.

Introduction

Remote patient monitoring plays a role in managing chronic medical conditions, including chronic kidney disease (CKD) and home-based dialysis therapies such as peritoneal dialysis (PD) [1–4]. The importance of remote health monitoring has increased substantially since the COVID-19 pandemic. Numerous studies have highlighted its benefits for patients with CKD, including those on dialysis, through monitoring and feedback [5–8]. These advantages include better blood pressure control, reduced reliance on antihypertensive medications, fewer emergency clinic or hospital visits, and improved patient quality of life [5, 8, 9].

A previous review identified approximately 13 mobile applications designed to aid CKD and kidney failure patients in tracking medication compliance and nutrition [2]. However, these apps lacked comprehensive features addressing health-related parameters or dialysis-specific information. Another review of apps for PD patients found that only a few out of 14 apps effectively catered to the unique needs of this population [1]. Most research has focused on PD patients, particularly those using automated peritoneal dialysis (APD) [5, 6].

Currently, no app provides comprehensive support for both CKD and PD patients, integrating features such as vital sign recording, medication tracking with reminders, nutritional logs, dialysis-specific parameters, and alerts for inactive usage. This highlights the need for a well-designed app that serves as a holistic tool for patient management. Thus, the main outcome of the study was to assess the acceptance of the Viva Konnect patient app [9].

Methods

The study was conducted at Singapore General Hospital (SGH) from December 2021 to February 2023. The study was registered on clinicaltrials.gov (NCT05012020).

Study Participants

Patients with CKD or those on PD treated at SGH were approached for recruitment. The inclusion criteria for the study were adult patients (≥21 years old) with CKD or on PD, patients or caregivers who could read and understand English, patients who were capable of providing informed consent, and patients who owned a digital device with internet access. Exclusion criteria included patients living in assisted facilities, those without the mental capacity to provide informed consent, and patients involved in another clinical trial that would interfere with the conduct of this study.

Viva Konnect

The Viva Konnect ecosystem consists of a patient-facing mobile application (app) and a web-based clinician interface (Clinic Portal). The app includes features such as recording vital signs, managing medications, tracking symptoms, maintaining dietary records, and monitoring PD therapy (for PD patients). The Clinic Portal allows the healthcare (HC) team to review the entries. Vital signs could be entered manually or transmitted via Bluetooth-enabled devices. The app included medication reminders and built-in usage analytics to track user behavior and trends. It also featured an inactivity prompt that alerted users if the app had not been used for ≥3 days.

Study Procedure

After providing consent, participants downloaded the app onto their phones and received guidance from a study team member on its features and use. Participants were provided with two Bluetooth-enabled smart devices to monitor vital signs and a smart weighing scale to record body weight. Training was also provided on how to pair the smart devices with the app. Participants were asked to use the devices to measure their vital signs daily. Participants also completed the Technology Readiness Index (TRI) at enrollment. The TRI 2.0 is a validated psychometric tool that evaluates an individual’s readiness to adopt and use new technologies [10]. Respondents are classified into five categories based on their level of adoption propensity: explorers, pioneers, skeptics, hesitators, and avoiders. To further understand the dynamics and experience of the patients, the HC professionals who used the Viva Konnect system also participated in a mixed-method analysis conducted after 3 months of use; this consisted of the mHealth App Usability Questionnaire (MAUQ) and a semi-structured interview. The MAUQ [11] is a validated questionnaire comprising three subscales: ease of use and satisfaction, interface, and usefulness. It utilizes a Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Participants attended appointments with their primary physicians as per the routine clinic schedule. Participants’ activity on the app was monitored for up to 7 months following recruitment.

Data Collection

The study collected the demographic and comorbidities data of all participants. The frequency and duration of app usage and health-related parameters were captured in the app, and participants’ responses to app reminders were also tracked.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was app acceptance, defined as the proportion of participants who used the app at least twice weekly for CKD patients and daily for PD patients. Average daily app usage time was also measured. Secondary outcomes included smart device usage, unplanned clinic visits, PD-related infections, and changes in eGFR, blood pressure, and antihypertensive use. User experience was assessed using MAUQ scores and qualitative feedback on app impact.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical data were presented as frequencies (percentage), and continuous data were reported as medians (interquartile ranges) where appropriate. Primary outcomes were summarized using descriptive statistics. Continuous variables were analyzed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test, while categorical variables were evaluated using Pearson’s chi-squared test. All tests were two-tailed, and p < 0.05 was considered significant.

Primary Outcomes

Among the participants who completed the study (n = 27), approximately 56% met the acceptance criteria. The app acceptance rates were 47.4% for PD patients and 75.0% for CKD patients. Overall, the median app usage for all PD patients was 6.7 (1.8–16.5) times per week, and the median time spent was 6.9 (2.2–12.1) minutes per session. The median app use for all CKD patients was 6.2 (2.6–6.7) times per week, and the median time spent was 4.7 (1.7–7.7) minutes per session. In an intention-to-treat analysis with all participants (n = 35) including dropouts, approximately 43% met the acceptance criteria.

Secondary Outcomes

The median percentage of vitals recorded using smart companion devices for PD patients was significantly lower than that of CKD patients, 52.9% (26.7–82.2%) versus 83.4% (78.8–94.4%, p = 0.04). During the study period, the use of smart companion devices to monitor health-related parameters (device compliance) was 59.3% among PD patients and 79.2% among CKD patients.

Among 19 PD patients, the number of exit-site infections, peritonitis episodes, and unplanned clinic visits remained similar before and during the study (online suppl. Table 1). Among eight CKD patients, blood pressure showed a downward trend with unchanged antihypertensive use. Kidney function remained stable, with no significant change before and during the study (online suppl. Table 2).

Among 27 participants who responded to the MAUQ, the median overall score was 5.1 (3.8–5.7; Table 3). In all three subscales: ease of use and satisfaction, interface, and usefulness, the scores were significantly lower in PD patients than in CKD patients (Table 3).

Qualitative Analysis

Nineteen patients (6 CKD and 13 PD) participated in one-on-one, audio-recorded, semi-structured interviews and provided feedback on their app experience. Interviews were also conducted with study’s ten HC professionals to provide insights into the Clinic Portal. They consist of doctors, nurses, and a dietician. Two independent reviewers analyzed the interview transcripts and performed thematic analysis. Three key themes emerged regarding app use: communication, convenience, and clarity. Discussion

The study revealed that app acceptance rate varied among PD and CKD patients. Approximately 50% of PD patients utilized the smart companion device to measure vitals, compared to 83% of CKD patients. For PD patients, there was no significant difference in PD-related infections, unplanned clinic visits, or hospitalizations before and during the study. Similarly, for CKD patients, there was no change in unplanned clinic visits, blood pressure, the number of antihypertensive medications, or eGFR levels before and during the study. Interviews with participants identified three themes regarding app utility: communication, convenience, and clarity.

Patients undergoing PD therapy are trained to manually record their PD-related parameters and vitals, such as blood pressure, pulse rate, and body weight, in a record book as part of their daily routine care. Recording these health-related parameters is an integral part of PD therapy. However, in the present study, the app’s acceptance for recording PD and health-related parameters was suboptimal, with only about 40% of participants using the app daily for this purpose. This could be attributed to the added burden of recording data both manually and digitally, as participants were instructed to maintain their usual manual records alongside app entries to ensure routine continuity after the study. Consequently, the study was unable to fully assess the app’s convenience or accurately gauge its true acceptance among PD patients.

Other factors contributing to the low uptake may include unfamiliarity with technology, resistance to adopting new tools, or the patient’s perception of the app’s usefulness. While some participants found manual recording in a book to be simpler and less technically challenging, others found it useful as they could easily assess their records on their phones. Unfamiliarity with the Bluetooth-enabled device and app hindered its adoption and consistent use. Additionally, some patients encountered technical difficulties in recording health-related parameters due to deficiencies in the smart companion devices, further impacting app usage.

The theme of communication was prevalent in many of the relationships found in this study; patient to HC team, HC team to patient, and patient to the digital ecosystem. The app provided notifications when a certain criterion is met (i.e., if a patient has not entered any information for 3 days, it reminds the patient with a personalized message), which some patients found helpful and encouraging (PD4, PD9) and noted that more can be done in providing feedback if the vitals are “good or bad” and for their HC team to “interfere when there is an irregularity in the therapy logs” (PD7, PD9). Therefore, notification triggers on abnormal recordings have to be flagged and communicated timely to create a sustainable patient-physician communication tool. Developing gamification elements to humanize the app to provide that feedback that app users seek can also improve the uptake and compliance [12]. The reminder function did not noticeably increase app usage. This could be due to the small sample size of the study or inconclusive benefits of the reminder function. However, this may have a role in seeing a steady use throughout the 6 months, rather than a decline as seen in other app studies [5].

Another factor for motivation could be the absence of feedback or response from both the app and their HC team that created a sense of one-way communication. Since HC professionals were not required to check the Clinic Portal or provide regular feedback, this may have further contributed to the low app usage. Perhaps, two-way communication could improve engagement and continuity of care in remote patient monitoring [13]. As the study sample was small, subgroup analyses were not performed, and potential differences in compliance by age, gender, or socioeconomic status could not be determined. Other chronic disease mHealth studies have also acknowledged that apps are limited at capturing the complex relationship of compliance and the social economical determinants, as well as intrinsic motivational factors contributing to uptake of health interventions [14]. It would be beneficial to perform a subgroup analysis to explore targeted populations that would benefit from additional interventions to improve compliance in future studies.

Going forward, incorporating a calibrated AI-generated auto-response could enhance the app’s usability and enable two-way communication. Additionally, the app could potentially be equipped with a facility to help patients troubleshoot errors during PD exchange procedures, which can provide greater support and improve engagement, while reducing the workload of the HC team.

On a deeper level, patients were also confused about how the data entries are helping with their health and asking questions like “does it improve the quality of our treatment” (PD1), “what is this information used for” (PD12). There appears to be a gap in understanding how these recordings lead to health benefits; fostering intrinsic motivation and providing feedback could enhance patient engagement and adherence.

This study is among the few that evaluated app acceptance in both PD and CKD populations, providing separate analyses for each group. It examined user engagement by reporting the average time spent on the app for CKD and PD patients separately. This information will help the app developers tailor the content to fit with this population and their health providers. This could encourage users to maintain consistent engagement with the app, in hopes of improving patient adherence to self-care behaviors and, in turn, clinical outcomes [14]. The study also reported a thematic analysis of patients’ feedback regarding their app usage experience. Despite its contributions, the study had limitations, including being single-center and involving a small sample. Technical challenges with accompanying devices also affected the study. Consequently, the findings may not be generalizable to other populations. Nonetheless, the study offers valuable insights into user behaviors and their readiness to adopt new technology.

In conclusion, this study supported the feasibility of remote monitoring through a mobile app for CKD and PD patients. Enhancing two-way communication with clinicians, reducing data entry burden, and integrating the app into existing workflows may improve engagement for both patients and clinicians. Future app development should address these areas to further enhance usage and compliance.
