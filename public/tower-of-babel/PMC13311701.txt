Building a gastroenterology fellowship programme in Rwanda: a model of international partnerships and sustainable capacity building
Authors: Hanna Aberra, Kulwinder S Dua, Dirk J Van Leeuwen, Florence Masaisa, Gamal Salah, Vincent Dusabejambo, Eric Rutaganda, Kara Neil, Janvier Murayire, Emile Sebera, Augustin Sendegeya, Innocent Dadamessi, Zerihun Abebe, Menelas Nkeshimana, Frederick Makrauer, Timothy Walker, Steve Bensen, Berhane Redae
Published: 2026

Gastrointestinal (GI) and liver diseases are a growing public health concern in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), including Rwanda.1 2 Digestive diseases have received limited attention and funding despite their significant contribution to morbidity and mortality. However, recent trends reveal a rising burden. Between 1990 and 2019, the prevalence of digestive diseases in SSA nearly doubled, increasing by 95.7% in males and 103.5% in females,3 highlighting the urgency for targeted interventions and specialist training.

In East Africa, the burden of digestive disease remains high, but clinical care capacity remains limited. A study across Ethiopia, Kenya, Malawi and Zambia highlighted major gaps in trained personnel, equipment and service availability.4 The estimated endoscopic procedure rates, 106 upper GI and 45 colonoscopies per 100 000 people annually, are <10% of those in high-income countries.5 This regional context underscores the critical need for specialist GI training programmes to provide the staff necessary to expand these services.

Rwanda has made remarkable strides in public health, supported by universal health coverage and strengthened primary care. However, access to GI care remains inadequate.

With a 2024 population nearing 14 million and an observed threshold in one observational study of ≥7.5 gastroenterologists per 100 000,6 Rwanda’s GI workforce shortage became a critical barrier to care. In response, Rwanda launched its first national gastroenterology and hepatology fellowship programme, marking a pivotal shift towards long-term self-sustaining specialist training. The programme aligns with the Government of Rwanda’s ‘4×4’ health workforce reform strategy, which seeks to quadruple the health workforce in 4 years.6 This article documents Rwanda’s journey in addressing GI health system gaps through the development of a local fellowship programme, offering lessons that may benefit other resource-limited settings facing similar workforce and infrastructure challenges.

Programme development and implementation

The Ministry of Health in Rwanda prioritises health workforce development across specialties and identified a critical shortage of gastroenterologists, including hepatologists. To address this gap, a GI fellowship programme was established in collaboration with the University of Rwanda and international partners. Trainees receive primarily local training supported by visiting faculty, with rotations at international centres. Graduates transition into mentorship roles for incoming fellows, creating a sustainable training model. Opportunities for advanced training in areas such as endoscopy and GI motility are supported through international collaborations and the World Endoscopy Organization’s ‘Train-the-Trainer’ programme. This approach aims to build long-term self-sufficiency while reducing reliance on external support.

In 2021, a faculty lead was appointed to establish Rwanda’s first GI and hepatology fellowship programme, supporting national goals to expand postgraduate training and specialist capacity. The competency-based programme is accredited by the University of Rwanda’s College of Medicine and Health Sciences and the Higher Education Council. Four fellows, 75% women, were selected as the inaugural cohort, and the programme has since expanded to additional cohorts.

During the first year, fellows train in Rwanda at three teaching hospitals: King Faisal Hospital Rwanda, the University Teaching Hospital of Kigali and Rwanda Military Referral and Teaching Hospital. Training includes outpatient and inpatient care, endoscopy and didactic sessions. Initially, visiting international faculty provided regular hands-on training and teaching. As local capacity increased, graduates assumed mentorship roles, reducing dependence on external faculty, who now focus on advanced skills training. Research is a core component of the programme. Each fellow completes a mentored research project, contributing to the regional evidence base. Participation in international conferences further supports research capacity and professional development.

To broaden clinical exposure, second-year fellows rotate at the Medical College of Wisconsin, gaining experience in subspecialty areas such as hepatology, inflammatory bowel disease, motility disorders and advanced endoscopy, including liver transplant care. Additional training at high-volume centres in India at Midas Institute of Gastroenterology through the World Endoscopy Organization provides intensive hands-on endoscopy experience.

Achievements

The first two cohorts of fellows graduated in November 2024 and October 2025 as fully certified subspecialists registered with the Rwanda Medical and Dental Council. In addition to clinical roles, they serve as faculty, contributing to training of subsequent cohorts and participating in undergraduate and postgraduate education, thereby strengthening overall educational quality. The inclusion of female instructors is expected to encourage more women to pursue subspecialty training in gastroenterology and hepatology.

For quality assurance, each graduate is evaluated annually using international performance standards. Caecal intubation and adenoma detection rates meet international benchmarks, averaging 97.5% and 32.5%, respectively. The new subspecialists also mentor incoming fellows, reducing reliance on short-term visiting faculty. Consequently, international faculty visits have decreased from monthly visits of one to two faculty for 1 week to less than one visit every 3 months.

The programme has significantly improved service delivery, with GI procedure access tripling over 2 years, reaching approximately 2000 procedures annually across major tertiary centres. Full-time GI and hepatology services were also established at the University Teaching Hospital of Butare, extending care beyond the capital. This supports decentralisation of specialised services and reduces travel burden for patients. With each graduating cohort, more specialists are deployed to peripheral centres, further increasing service availability nationwide.

Challenges

The programme initially faced a shortage of local gastroenterology specialists and trained faculty. This was addressed by recruiting expatriate specialists and establishing collaborations with international visiting faculty. Virtual teaching platforms were also adopted to maintain continuous academic support. As local capacity has grown, dependence on international faculty has declined. A remaining challenge is the development of advanced subspecialty training in interventional procedures, which is being pursued through structured local training complemented by international rotations.

Lessons learnt

Several key lessons emerged from implementing Rwanda’s GI fellowship programme. Strong collaboration among the Ministry of Health, University of Rwanda, hospitals and international partners was essential for aligning resources with national priorities. Partnerships with global academic institutions and professional societies provided critical technical expertise and training opportunities. Virtual learning platforms ensured continuity of education and engagement. The involvement of graduated fellows in teaching and mentorship is strengthening programme sustainability. Additionally, training extended beyond physicians to include nurses and technicians, who are integral to endoscopy services. Emphasis was placed on parallel capacity building for these cadres. Multidisciplinary collaboration with specialties such as surgery, radiology, oncology and pathology was also essential, as GI care cannot function in isolation.

Way forward

Future priorities include expanding the pool of local GI educators through targeted faculty development, with opportunities for advanced training in hepatology and interventional gastroenterology. Establishing continuous medical education programmes and structured academic career pathways will further strengthen mentorship capacity and leadership development. Positioning Rwanda as a regional training hub through strategic partnerships will also support cross-border capacity building and help address regional workforce shortages.

Conclusion

Rwanda’s gastroenterology and hepatology fellowship programme represents a significant milestone in strengthening specialised healthcare and medical education. Despite initial challenges, the programme has produced competent specialists who now contribute to clinical care, education and mentorship. This experience demonstrates the value of locally driven capacity-building supported by strong leadership and international collaboration. Continued investment in infrastructure, faculty development, research and regional partnerships will be essential to sustain progress and address the burden of digestive diseases in Rwanda and the broader SSA region.
