Case Report: Atypical magnetic resonance appearance of an organizing hematoma in the brain of a dog
Authors: Kathryn Dalzell, Rod Bagley, Cheng-Shun Hsueh, Jeremy Brozek, Amanda Jean Fales-Williams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An 8-year-old German Short-haired Pointer was admitted with acute onset of blindness, behavioral abnormalities, and hemiparesis. Neurologic findings localized to the left forebrain. A well-demarcated, circular lesion with mixed signal intensity and mixed contrast enhancement was demonstrated with magnetic resonance (MR) imaging. There was marked mass effect with an irregularly marginated peripheral area of hyperintensity on T2-weighted and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery sequence images and areas of signal-void on gradient-echo sequencing. The distinctly circular appearance of this abnormality is atypical for most disease processes including intracranial hematoma found with MR imaging of the intracranial structures in dogs. Differential diagnosis included primary hemorrhage, neoplasia, inflammatory disease, as well as uncommon diseases such as parasite migration. The dog was euthanized per the owner's request immediately following the MR. Pathologically, there was an organizing hematoma, characterized by centrally located fibrous tissue surrounded by an area of hyperacute hemorrhage extending into the adjacent neuroparenchyma in the thalamus. The imaging features of this intracerebral hemorrhage were similar to previous reports but also contained unique features including a geometrically circular border, a lack of peripheral hypointensity on T1-weighted images, and central contrast-enhancement. As this was a histologically confirmed hematoma, the imaging features in this case should be included within the spectrum of potential imaging abnormalities associated with intracranial hematomas in dogs.

Introduction

Magnetic resonance (MR) imaging is the primary tool used for characterizing many types of intracranial lesions in companion animals. Identifying distinctive imaging characteristics of various pathologies is essential for accurate ante-mortem diagnosis and subsequent decisions including treatment options and prognostication. There is a spectrum of MR imaging features associated with different intracranial diseases in dogs. Cerebrovascular abnormalities and hemorrhage are one category of diseases that can have diverse imaging characteristics (1–5). While cerebrovascular lesions and hemorrhage have some consistent imaging features, atypical appearances can

complicate the ability to make an accurate imaging diagnosis. Additionally, secondary pathophysiological consequences of intracranial disease processes, such as edema and secondary hemorrhage add further diversity to the lesion's MR appearance. In this report, we describe the appearance of an intra-axial hematoma with histopathologic confirmation in a dog with unique MR imaging features. This case adds to the expanding database of imaging features of intracranial hematomas in dogs and can help guide clinical decision-making for patients with similar appearing intracranial lesions.

Case presentation

An 8-year-old male castrated German Shorthaired Pointer was admitted to Iowa State University Lloyd Veterinary Medical Center for a 3-day history of suspected acute-onset blindness. The owners described the dog as anorexic, lethargic, and “bumping into things”. The dog had been otherwise healthy except for a historic pelvic fracture which healed with minimal long-term morbidity.

At presentation, the dog was able to stand and walk with support. The dog tended to propulsively circle to the right and would walk close to, and occasionally, impact the wall on its right side when moving around the examination space. There was an absent menace response with an intact palpebral reflex in the right eye and an intact menace response and palpebral reflex in the left eye. Ocular and fundic examinations were normal. On stimulation of the right nostril with a cotton tip applicator, the palpebral fissure would immediately close, but the dog's head did not move away from the stimulus. The dog exhibited a right sided hemiparesis, and when evaluated, postural reactions were absent on the right thoracic and pelvic limbs. All spinal reflexes were normal. Sinus bradycardia (between 58 and 82 beats/minute) was intermittent. The remaining neurological and physical examination was normal. These findings were consistent with a left-lateralized forebrain (rostrotentorial) abnormality. Differential diagnosis included acquired structural brain diseases such as cerebrovascular disease, neoplasia, inflammatory conditions, traumatic brain injury, acquired hydrocephalus, as well as other uncommon diseases such as intracranial parasite migration.

Complete blood cell count demonstrated mildly decreased hemoglobin, hematocrit, mean platelet volume, and lymphocytes. Serum chemistry showed mild decreases in calcium, phosphorus, magnesium, blood urea nitrogen, and alanine aminotransferase. Urinalysis was normal. The dog was placed under general anesthesia for MR imaging. Magnetic resonance imaging of the brain was performed using a 1.5 Tesla MR scanner (General Electric Signa Small Bore MRI with 25.1 Explorer Software, GE Healthcare, Boston, MA). Multiplanar (sagittal, transverse, and dorsal) images were obtained with the following sequences: T2-weighted (T2w), Fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR), gradient-echo (GRE) and T1-weighted (T1w) sequences before and following intravenous contrast administration with gadoteridol (Clarisca -GE Healthcare) as administered at a dose of 0.2 ml/kg.

Magnetic resonance imaging demonstrated an intra-axial focal abnormality within the left thalamus with resulting mass effect Figure 1. The thalamic lesion had a complex MR appearance of mixed signal intensity with a unique well-demarcated focal central circular abnormality surrounded by hyperintensity on T2w and FLAIR sequences. On the GRE sequence this focal abnormality was iso- to hyperintense in the center surrounded by a faintly hypointense border. On T1w imaging prior to intravenous contrast administration, the central focal abnormality was hypointense. Following intravenous contrast administration, this focal abnormality homogenously enhanced except for the immediate center of the lesion which lacked enhancement. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis collected in the lumbar cistern yielded acellular fluid with the presence of hemosiderin pigmentation.

Following discussion with the owner of the imaging findings and potential differential diagnoses, euthanasia was elected. Post-mortem evaluation of the brain was performed. On gross evaluation, there was mass effect of the left cerebral neuroparenchyma and an irregularly marginated focal region of red-brown discoloration in the region of the left thalamus Figure 2.

Transverse gross image from the left thalamic area demonstrating a hemorrhagic focus with an organizing hematoma. There is a centrally located organizing hematoma (asterisk) encompassed by an expansile region of peripheral hemorrhage (arrow).

The brain was fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 48 h prior to sectioning. The sections were routinely processed for histology and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), Masson's trichrome, and Congo red. The left thalamic parenchyma was replaced by an extensive area of hemorrhage mixed with fibrin and foamy macrophages surrounding a well-demarcated circular region of early fibrous tissue extending into the adjacent cerebral white matter Figure 3. The neuropil adjacent to the hematoma contained increased numbers of astrocytes, microglia, and oligodendrocytes and had markedly vacuolated myelin. The neurons were shrunken and hypereosinophilic with pyknosis and karyorrhexis. Under routine H&E staining, no morphological changes in the vascular wall were observed, including thickening of the tunica media, hyaline fibrosis, or lumen stenosis. Congo red staining was negative suggesting the absence of amyloid angiopathy. Notably, Masson's trichrome staining highlighted vessels associated with the central lesion, confirming the presence of fibrous tissue within the vascular walls. Immunohistochemistry against glial fibrillary acidic protein (GFAP) was performed with only 5% of cells staining positive. These histologic findings were consistent with organizing hematoma with granulation tissue within the left thalamus.

Discussion

Cerebrovascular disease (CVD) including intracranial hematoma formation in dogs was historically thought to be infrequent, as cerebrovascular-related lesions were uncommonly discovered on post-mortem and the paucity of population studies on the incidence of CVD in dogs (6, 7). With the ability to perform MR imaging ante-mortem, CVD is now a well-established cause of intracranial disease in dogs (8–15). The reported prevalence of CVD in dogs is 1.5%−2%, which may still be an underrepresentation since not all affected dogs undergo diagnostic imaging or necropsy (16).

Diagnostic criteria for suspecting an intracranial hematoma have been established previously relative to the differential diagnosis of intracranial hematoma compared to other space-occupying intracranial abnormalities such as neoplasia and inflammatory tissue (17). When evaluating the MR appearance of hemorrhagic lesions within the brain, it is essential to recognize the standard evolution of hemorrhage production and breakdown (18–33). The changes in observed appearance of an evolving hematoma are intrinsically related to four factors (I) pH, (II) osmolality, (III) oxygenation status of hemoglobin, and (IV) hemoglobin concentration. The effects of magnetic properties of hemoglobin help to define the 5 stages of hemorrhage into the hyperacute (< 24 h), acute (1–3 days), early subacute (3–7 days), late subacute (7–14 days), and chronic (>14 days) stages (16). The MR appearance of blood in various forms, however, is complex and is influenced by a number of factors including the magnet strength, imaging sequences used, characteristics of the surrounding tissue, and whether the blood is arterial or venous in origin. Given the diversity of appearance of intracranial parenchymal blood, the MR diagnostic appearance of organizing hematomas will likely expand as more confirmed cases are evaluated.

This patient had a histologically confirmed chronic organizing hematoma surrounded by hyperacute hemorrhage. The primary cause for this hematoma was not determined, however, speculation as to underlying hypercoagulability states, hypertension, local vascular factors, and pathophysiologic mechanisms resulting in intravascular coagulation disturbances should be considered in similar cases. The MR appearance of this abnormality can be separated into two pathologic components: a central organized hematoma and peripheral rapidly expansile hemorrhage extending into the adjacent neuroparenchyma. The central area was well-demarcated and symmetrically circular in appearance and was hyperintense on T2w/FLAIR sequences with a thin hypointense peripheral rim and hypointense on T1w sequences. There was ring enhancement extending from its margins toward, but not completely, to the center of the lesion. This imaging pattern correlates to the pathologic findings of an organized hematoma composed of a mature collagen matrix and neovascularization with scant evidence of hemorrhage or degraded clot products. Notably, this central region showed minimal to no signal-dropout on GRE, which is atypical for hemorrhagic lesions. This is speculated to be due to the absence of hemosiderin or ferritin within this area of the lesion, supporting the chronicity of the lesion and establishment of a primary fibrotic scar replacing the original lesion. Surrounding the chronic center of this lesion, there was an irregularly marginated expansile area of peripheral hemorrhage that is T2w/FLAIR hyperintense, isointense on T1w sequences, and contained multifocal signal-void regions on GRE. In addition, there was peri-lesional edema, and a mass effect associated with the rapid onset and expansile effect of the hemorrhage with subsequent compression of the adjacent neuroparenchyma (27).

One limitation of this case is the lack of diffusion-weighted imaging and apparent diffusion coefficient maps which may help in identifying cerebral vascular-based disease compared to other disease processes (27). In the future, similar cases may benefit from these additional imaging sequences to support an ante-mortem cerebrovascular diagnosis. The constellation of findings observed across multiple imaging sequences in this patient demonstrates some features previously described for spontaneous intracranial hematomas in dogs (17). There are differences, however, such as the imaging appearance following contrast enhancement (17). The most unique feature, however, is the almost symmetrical circular appearance of the periphery of the hematoma which differs from previously reported peripheral lesion geometry (17). Given these imaging features, this case adds to the expanding body of documented imaging features of canine intracranial hematoma and the spectrum of associated MR characteristics that support an ante-mortem diagnosis of this intracranial pathophysiologic process.
