TRIDENT-Risk: An AI-driven temporal ensemble framework for proactive high-severity accident risk forecasting in Intelligent Transportation Systems

Sanchit Vashisht, Shalli Rani, Fazlullah Khan

ABSTRACT
High-severity traffic accidents impose substantial human and economic burdens, necessitating proactive and data-driven risk forecasting mechanisms within intelligent transportation systems. This study proposed TRIDENT-Risk, a temporal risk-driven imbalance-optimized ensemble framework for high-severity accident prediction using the eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) model. The approach integrates spatial–temporal aggregation, multi-scale lag and rolling risk features, imbalance-aware gradient boosting, and threshold optimization to enhance minority-class detection. Experimental evaluation using publicly accessible US-Accidents dataset including about 7.7 million accident records from February 2016 to March 2023 demonstrated that the proposed TRIDENT-Risk achieved a ROC-AUC of 0.8917, PR-AUC of 0.7266, precision of 0.60, recall of 0.81, F1-score of 0.69 and accuracy of 0.82. Compared with the strongest baseline (MLP), the proposed framework improved high-risk recall from 0.70 to 0.81 (15.7% relative improvement), increased ROC-AUC from 0.8776 to 0.8917 and improved PR-AUC from 0.7016 to 0.7266, demonstrating superior minority-class detection and reliable proactive accident forecasting. Temporal grid-based aggregation was used to frame high-severity accidents as a binary prediction problem and an 80:20 chronological train-test split was used to eliminate temporal leaking. The proposed framework offers a scalable and interpretable solution for proactive traffic safety management and supports intelligent decision-making in next-generation transportation systems.

1. Introduction Road transportation systems form the backbone of modern economies, enabling mobility, trade, and social connectivity at an unprecedented scale [ 1 , 2 ]. However, the rapid expansion of vehicular networks has been accompanied by a persistent rise in traffic congestion, infrastructure strain, and, most critically, road traffic accidents [ 3 , 4 ]. High-severity accidents, in particular, impose substantial human, social, and economic costs, including loss of life, long-term injury, medical expenditure, traffic disruption, and infrastructure damage [ 5 , 6 ]. According to global transportation safety reports, road crashes remain one of the leading causes of death worldwide, highlighting the urgent need for intelligent and proactive safety management mechanisms within transportation ecosystems. The evolution of intelligent transportation systems (ITS) has introduced advanced sensing, data collection, and communication capabilities into traffic infrastructure [ 7 ]. Large-scale accident repositories, weather records, and temporal traffic logs now provide a rich foundation for predictive analytics [ 8 , 9 ]. These developments have enabled a transition from reactive accident response strategies toward predictive and preventive safety management [ 10 , 11 ]. Instead of merely responding to incidents after occurrence, modern transportation analytics aims to anticipate high-risk conditions and support early intervention through data-driven decision systems. In this context, forecasting high-severity accident risk at regional and temporal scales has emerged as a critical research challenge [ 12 , 13 ]. Accident risk prediction is inherently complex. Traffic incidents are influenced by dynamic interactions among environmental conditions, human behavior, infrastructure characteristics, and temporal patterns [ 14 , 15 ]. Weather fluctuations, visibility conditions, humidity levels, historical accident recurrence, and time-of-day variations collectively shape the probability of severe events. Furthermore, accident datasets typically exhibit strong class imbalance, as high-severity crashes occur less frequently than minor incidents, creating additional modeling challenges [ 16 ]. Conventional statistical approaches often struggle to capture nonlinear temporal dependencies and imbalanced distributions effectively, while purely deep learning (DL) based spatial architectures may introduce unnecessary complexity when the dominant predictive signals are temporal and contextual rather than strongly graph-structured [ 17 , 18 ]. Recent advancements in machine learning (ML), particularly ensemble-based gradient boosting techniques, have demonstrated strong performance in tabular and structured data environments [ 19 , 20 ]. These models effectively capture nonlinear interactions, adapt to heterogeneous feature distributions, and offer inherent robustness against overfitting [ 21 , 22 ]. Nevertheless, direct application of generic boosting models may not sufficiently address temporal dependencies across multiple horizons, risk accumulation effects, or imbalance-sensitive optimization objectives. In safety-critical domains, detection sensitivity for high-risk regions is often more important than maximizing overall accuracy, necessitating risk-aware threshold calibration and imbalance-adjusted training strategies [ 23 ]. To address these challenges, a temporal risk-driven ensemble modeling paradigm is proposed for high-severity traffic accident forecasting. The framework integrates multi-scale temporal feature engineering, imbalance-aware optimization, and decision-threshold calibration within a unified boosting architecture. Multi-horizon lag variables, rolling statistics, and risk acceleration indicators are incorporated to capture short-term and medium-term temporal dynamics. Class imbalance is explicitly handled through weighted optimization to enhance sensitivity toward high-risk events. Furthermore, threshold tuning is employed to balance precision–recall tradeoffs in accordance with safety-oriented operational priorities. This integrated design aims to maximize early detection capability while maintaining stable discriminative performance. Extensive experimentation is conducted using large-scale accident and contextual datasets aggregated at regional grid levels. The proposed framework is systematically benchmarked against widely adopted baseline models, including logistic regression, random forests, gradient boosting, and multilayer perceptrons. Comparative evaluation is performed using multiple metrics, including ROC-AUC, PR-AUC, recall for high-risk events, and F1-score, ensuring a comprehensive assessment under imbalanced conditions. Empirical results demonstrate that the proposed temporal risk-driven ensemble model achieves superior discriminative capability and significantly improved high-risk recall compared to baseline approaches, thereby reinforcing its suitability for proactive safety management within intelligent transportation environments. 1.1. Research gaps Existing studies have greatly improved the prediction of traffic accidents with ML and DL methods. However, there are still some research gaps. First, many approaches only optimize the total accuracy while ignoring the identification of the minority class, which is more important in safety-critical contexts. Second, modeling temporal dependencies is usually done using either short-term lag features or sequential DL architectures, but relatively few research combine multi-scale temporal accumulation with imbalance-aware optimization. Third, threshold calibration is rarely implemented while it is important to trade off false alarms and missed detections. Also, many existing methodologies are limited in their interpretability which hinders their practical applicability for transportation authorities. These research gaps motivate the development of the proposed TRIDENT-Risk framework. 3. Dataset description The proposed framework is developed and evaluated using the publicly available US-Accidents dataset((Accessed on: 14/02/2026) https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/sobhanmoosavi/us-accidents/data ) [ 35 , 36 ]. This countrywide dataset covers 49 states of the United States and includes accident records collected from February 2016 to March 2023. The data were gathered in real time using multiple traffic APIs that broadcast incident information captured by state departments of transportation, law enforcement agencies, traffic cameras, and road-embedded sensors. The dataset contains approximately 7.7 million accident records and provides rich contextual information including spatial coordinates, timestamps, severity levels, and environmental attributes such as temperature, humidity, and visibility. For computational feasibility and reproducibility, the dataset is spatially aggregated into regional grids and temporally discretized into hourly intervals. Each grid-time pair represents a spatial–temporal observation used for predictive modeling. High-severity events are defined based on severity levels exceeding a predefined threshold, thereby converting the problem into a binary classification task. The original dataset severity labels were used to define high-severity accidents, which were annotated using the same framework across all states and years in this study. The suggested approach uses standardized severity categories to assure model development and evaluation consistency. This uniform labeling technique reduces geographical or temporal inconsistencies and allows large-scale accident risk prediction. Although minor missing entries may exist due to network disruptions during data collection, appropriate preprocessing and imputation strategies are employed to ensure consistency. To balance spatial granularity, data density, and computational efficiency, the spatial grid resolution was chosen. A coarser grid can mask localized accident patterns due to excessive spatial aggregation, whereas a finer grid may increase spatial resolution but lead to sparse observations and unstable feature estimation. The grid resolution provides enough event density for reliable temporal feature extraction and enough spatial variability for large-scale accident risk prediction. 4.2. Experimental setup and implementation details This section describes the data preparation strategy, feature configuration, model implementation details, and evaluation protocol used to ensure reproducibility and fair comparison across baseline and proposed models. Particular attention is given to temporal consistency, imbalance handling, and computational stability, as these factors critically influence accident risk forecasting performance. 5.1. Data preprocessing and aggregation The raw US-Accidents dataset was first filtered to retain relevant attributes including spatial coordinates, timestamp information, severity level, and selected weather variables such as temperature, visibility, and humidity. Records with missing essential spatial or temporal information were removed to maintain structural consistency. Minor missing environmental values were imputed using median-based strategies within spatial–temporal neighborhoods to preserve statistical robustness. Spatial aggregation was performed by partitioning the study region into uniform geographic grids of fixed resolution. Each accident record was mapped to its corresponding grid cell based on latitude and longitude coordinates. Temporal discretizations was performed at hourly resolution, producing grid-hour observations. For each grid and hour, accident counts and contextual averages were computed. This transformation reduced sparsity and enabled structured supervised learning. To preserve the temporal causality, the dataset was ordered chronologically before model construction. The first 80% of observations were used as training data for the models, while the next validation subset was used just for hyperparameter modification and threshold optimization. The last 20% of the observations in chronological order remained back as an unseen test set. No random shuffling was undertaken to avoid predicting past data using future measurements at any time. This approach prevents temporal leakage and better reflects deployment scenarios where future risk must be predicted using historical data. All temporal features were constructed before the prediction horizon using just historical observations, ensuring no future accident information was used. The geographical aggregation resolution was fixed at Δ = 0.1 ∘ providing a compromise between spatial coverage and data sparsity. The prediction horizon was set to H = 1 hour, i.e., in the next hourly interval the framework predicts if at least one high-severity accident will occur or not. Feature configuration The final feature space included contextual, temporal, and engineered variables. Contextual variables consisted of accident counts and environmental attributes. Temporal encoding included hour-of-day and day-of-week indicators to capture cyclical mobility patterns. Multi-scale temporal intelligence was incorporated through lag variables at 1, 3, 6, and 12-hour intervals, rolling mean statistics over 6-hour and 12-hour windows, rolling standard deviation to capture volatility, and a risk acceleration term defined as the difference between short-term and medium-term lag values. These engineered features expanded the input dimensionality while preserving interpretability. All numerical features were retained in their natural scale for tree-based models. For neural network baselines, features were standardized using training-set statistics to ensure stable convergence. 5.3. Model configuration All baseline models were implemented using widely adopted ML libraries to ensure comparability. Logistic regression employed ℓ 2 regularization with default solver settings. Random Forest models were configured with 200 trees and balanced class weighting. To ensure fair comparison, hyperparameter optimization was also performed for the primary baseline models using controlled search spaces. The reported results correspond to the best-performing configurations identified on the validation set. The proposed TRIDENT-Risk model utilized an optimized gradient boosting configuration obtained via randomized search with three-fold cross-validation on the training set. The architectural design of proposed model was motivated by the characteristics of large-scale traffic accident data, which exhibit strong class imbalance, heterogeneous feature distributions, nonlinear interactions, and temporal persistence. Conventional deep learning models such as MLPs are highly dependent on balanced training data and often require extensive architectural tuning to effectively learn minority-class patterns. In contrast, gradient boosting ensembles inherently focus on difficult-to-classify samples through iterative residual learning and are particularly effective for structured tabular datasets. The integration of multi-scale temporal features allows the model to capture short-term fluctuations, medium-term accumulation effects, and longer-term risk persistence, while class-weighted optimization increases sensitivity toward rare high-severity events. Finally, threshold calibration aligns the decision boundary with safety-oriented objectives, resulting in improved minority-class recall and PR-AUC performance. The final hyperparameter configuration included a shallow tree depth, moderate subsampling, and controlled learning rate to balance bias and variance. Class imbalance was addressed using a positive class weight computed as the ratio of negative to positive samples in the training set. Validation probabilities were threshold optimized over candidate thresholds from 0.10 to 0.90 in increments of 0.01. The threshold that gave the best validation F1-score was chosen and then applied to the held-out test set. This decreases the possibility of overfitting to the test set, while ensuring that decision boundaries are consistent with operational safety goals. This strategy ensures that the final decision boundary aligns with safety-sensitive detection priorities rather than relying on a fixed probability cutoff. 5.4. Parameter interpretation. The regularization term in LR constrains the magnitude of coefficients, preventing instability in high-dimensional settings. RF parameters such as the number of trees and bootstrap sampling promote ensemble diversity and reduce variance. The depth parameter controls model expressiveness by limiting recursive partitioning. For gradient boosting models, the number of estimators determines ensemble capacity, while the learning rate governs incremental contribution strength. A smaller learning rate typically enhances stability at the cost of longer training time. Subsample and column sampling ratios introduce stochasticity, improving generalization performance. The γ parameter enforces minimum gain thresholds for tree splitting, reducing overfitting to noise. The imbalance parameter scale_pos_weight plays a crucial role in safety-critical contexts by amplifying the contribution of high-severity samples during loss minimization. This directly improves recall performance without drastically sacrificing discrimination capability. For the multilayer perceptron, the number of hidden units controls representational capacity, while the ReLU activation function ensures nonlinear modeling without vanishing gradients. The Adam optimizer adapts learning rates per parameter to accelerate convergence. To ensure a fair comparative evaluation, the baseline models were configured using validated parameter settings commonly adopted in prior literature and refined through validation-based performance assessment. For tree-based models and neural networks, hyperparameter values were selected after preliminary tuning experiments to obtain stable and competitive performance. The proposed TRIDENT-Risk framework subsequently employed randomized search within a constrained parameter space to further optimize its imbalance-aware forecasting objective. Therefore, performance differences cannot be attributed solely to hyperparameter selection but rather to the integration of temporal feature engineering, imbalance-aware optimization, and threshold calibration. The hyperparameter configuration of TRIDENT-Risk was selected through randomized search with cross-validation to ensure robust performance across temporal splits, thereby preventing overfitting and enhancing operational reliability. 5.5. Evaluation protocol Performance was evaluated using multiple complementary metrics. ROC-AUC was used to assess overall discriminative capability independent of threshold selection. PR-AUC was emphasized due to class imbalance and the importance of minority-class performance. Precision, recall, and F1-score for the high-severity class were reported to quantify operational effectiveness. Accuracy was included for completeness but was not treated as the primary optimization objective. All comparisons were conducted on the identical test set to ensure fairness. Probability-based metrics were computed directly from predicted scores, while classification metrics were derived using the optimized decision threshold. 5.6. The experimental results shows that the proposed TRIDENT-Risk framework regularly outperforms baseline models especially in detecting minority-class high-severity events. The proposed TRIDENT-Risk has the highest ROC-AUC of 0.8917 and PR-AUC of 0.7266 outperforming the best baseline MLP at 0.8776 ROC-AUC and 0.7016 PR-AUC. The proposed framework achieved an approximate 15.7% relative improvement in high-risk recall from 0.70 (MLP) to 0.81, while maintaining a competitive F1-score of 0.69 and accuracy of 0.82. The above improvements come from synergistically integrating multi-scale temporal feature engineering, imbalance-aware optimization and adaptive threshold calibration, which lead to more effective learning of temporal risk patterns and significant improvements of minority-class detection for proactive traffic accident forecasting. 6.8.2. Impact of temporal risk features. One of the main advantage of the proposed framework is that it enables explicit modeling of the temporal evolution of accident risk through multi-scale temporal feature engineering. Instead of depending exclusively on instantaneous observations, the framework employs lag variables, rolling mean statistics, rolling standard deviation and risk acceleration indicators to describe the temporal persistence, volatility and trend dynamics of traffic conditions. Rolling statistics and lag-based variables are the most important predictors which validates the efficacy of the engineering features as per the SHAP analysis. The findings suggest that the occurrence of accidents is substantially correlated with cumulative traffic conditions and not with isolated events. Temporal characteristics allow the model to capture repetitive congestion patterns, long-term bad weather conditions, and progressive risk accumulation that often precedes serious traffic incidents. The integration of temporal intelligence further enhances robustness of the model under highly dynamic traffic situations. The proposed framework can simultaneously capture both short-term fluctuations and medium-term historical dependencies which provides a more comprehensive representation of traffic safety conditions, resulting in better discrimination capability and substantially enhanced detection of high-severity accident events. 6.8.3. Practical implications for intelligent transportation systems. The proposed TRIDENT-Risk architecture has various practical benefits for intelligent transport systems and traffic safety management. Accurate identification of high-risk areas allows transportation authorities to allocate emergency response resources more efficiently, develop proactive traffic control strategies and provide timely warnings before an accident happens. This ability to foresee can assist in reducing emergency response times and increase overall road safety. The framework is particularly suited to application in modern intelligent transportation infrastructures, since it is based on structured accident and contextual data routinely acquired by traffic monitoring systems, meteorological stations and road-side sensing devices. Furthermore, its modular design allows its smooth integration with current traffic management centers, decision-support systems, as well as smart city infrastructure, without the need for specialized sensor equipment. Another advantage of the suggested framework is the interpretability. The SHAP-based methodology provides straightforward reasons for individual forecasts, which enables transportation engineers and policymakers to identify the primary risk variables affecting the occurrence of accidents. Such transparency boosts users’ faith in the predictive model and aids in evidence-based traffic safety planning, infrastructure upgrading and policy making. 7. Limitations and future scope The proposed TRIDENT-Risk architecture exhibits promising prediction performance, however several limitations still needs to discussed. The system depends on spatial grid aggregation which can smooth out localized road features like intersections and lane patterns. In addition, while a chronologically split train–test split was used to maintain temporal causality, no explicit spatially blocked validation on unseen geographic regions was conducted. Future research directions include extending the framework toward graph-enhanced or hybrid architectures that integrate road network structure with temporal boosting strategies. Incorporating adaptive online learning mechanisms could enable continuous model updating as new accident data become available. Additionally, coupling the predictive model with real-time traffic control systems or decision-support dashboards may facilitate proactive intervention strategies within intelligent transportation ecosystems. Exploring transfer learning across cities or states may further improve scalability and cross-regional adaptability of the framework. 8. Conclusion This study proposed a novel temporal risk-driven and imbalance-optimized ensemble architecture (TRIDENT-Risk) for high-severity traffic accident forecasting. In the proposed system, the spatial-temporal aggregation, multi-scale temporal feature engineering, imbalance-aware gradient boosting and risk-sensitive threshold calibration were combined into a unified predictive pipeline. The proposed model outperformed all the other models including LR, RF, XGBoost and MLP with the greatest ROC-AUC (0.8917) and PR-AUC (0.7266) and significant improvement in recall of minority-class (from 0.70 to 0.81), demonstrating its capability to identify high-risk traffic incidents under extremely imbalanced settings. The experimental results also showed the significant improvement in prediction performance by combining the elements of temporal risk accumulation and imbalance-aware optimization, without increasing the complexity of the model. Spatial validation demonstrated a high consistency between the anticipated risk hotspots and the actual accident distributions. SHAP-based interpretability analysis showed that lag characteristics and rolling risk statistics were the main contributors to the model predictions. The proposed framework offers a scalable, interpretable and practical method to proactively manage traffic safety by enabling early risk assessment, informed decision-making, and data-driven planning in intelligent transportation systems.
