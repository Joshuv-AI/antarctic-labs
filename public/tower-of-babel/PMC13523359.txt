Prevalence and associated factors of obesity-related hypertension among Malaysian adults: findings from the National Health and Morbidity Survey 2023
Authors: Nor Azian Mohd Zaki, Hamizatul Akmal Abd Hamid, Khairul Hasnan Amali, Syafinaz Sallehuddin, Lalitha Palaniveloo, Azli Baharudin, Kishwen Kanna Yoga Ratnam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObesity-related hypertension contributes significantly to cardiovascular risk and poses a major public health concern. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of obesity-related hypertension and its associated factors among Malaysian adults.MethodsData were derived from a nationwide health survey of adults aged ≥ 18 years (n = 10,071) conducted in 2023. Obesity-related hypertension was defined as the concurrent presence of general obesity, abdominal obesity, and hypertension (BMI ≥ 27.5 kg/m² with waist circumference ≥ 90 cm for men and ≥ 80 cm for women, and systolic BP ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg, or a prior physician diagnosis). Multinomial logistic regression accounting for the complex sampling design was used to identify associated sociodemographic, behavioural, and clinical factors.ResultsThe overall weighted prevalence of obesity-related hypertension among Malaysian adults was 10.0% (95% CI: 9.13, 10.86), while 10.8% (95% CI: 9.93, 11.80) presented with obesity only and 20.0% (95% CI: 18.78, 21.33) with hypertension only. Multinomial logistic regression analysis demonstrated that females (aOR: 1.53; 95% CI: 1.25, 1.86), adults aged 60 years and above (aOR: 2.80; 95% CI: 2.07, 3.77), and individuals with lower educational attainment (aOR: 1.71; 95% CI: 1.17, 2.49) had significantly higher odds of obesity-related hypertension compared with the normal reference group. Additionally, modifiable factors were independently associated with the condition, including physical inactivity (aOR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.03, 1.55), diabetes mellitus (aOR: 4.49; 95% CI: 3.53, 5.72), and hypercholesterolemia (aOR: 2.30; 95% CI: 1.88, 2.81).ConclusionsObesity-related hypertension affects approximately one in ten Malaysian adults and represents a distinct and significant cardiometabolic burden. The condition is strongly associated with demographic, behavioural, and metabolic factors. These findings highlight the need for integrated, targeted prevention strategies within primary care and national non-communicable disease frameworks to address this growing public health challenge.

Background

Obesity and hypertension are two of the leading public health challenges worldwide, significantly contributing to the growing burden of noncommunicable diseases such as cardiovascular disease, stroke, and premature death [1, 2]. Globally, about 1.4 billion people are diagnosed with hypertension, while the World Health Organization estimates that one in eight individuals is classified as obese [3, 4]. Forecasts from the World Obesity Federation predict that by 2030, the obesity epidemic will impact nearly one billion people, affecting approximately 14% of men and 20% of women [5]. Trends among children and adolescents are also concerning, with a pooled global prevalence of overweight and obesity of 22.2%, placing almost one in five youths at increased risk of cardio-metabolic complications [6].

The concurrent presentation of obesity and hypertension synergistically accelerates target organ damage, leading to a compounded burden of cardiovascular morbidities such as heart failure, stroke, and chronic kidney disease and a substantially elevated risk of premature all-cause mortality [7, 8]. In Southeast Asia, this cardiometabolic burden is particularly substantial due to rapid urbanisation and lifestyle transitions [9, 10]. The prevalence of overweight and obesity ranges from 11.6% to 62.8%, while the pooled hypertension prevalence among urban populations reached almost 35.0% [11, 12]. Malaysia reflects similar patterns, with obesity increasing steadily over the past decade and showing a consistently higher prevalence among women. The Report of the National Health and Morbidity Survey (NHMS) 2023 indicates that more than one-third of adults are currently classified as obese, representing a substantial increase over the past 12 years, while abdominal obesity now affects more than half of the population [13, 14]. Over the same period, approximately three in ten adults in Malaysia were affected by hypertension [13].

Crucially, hypertension in individuals with obesity is increasingly recognised as a distinct clinical and pathophysiological phenotype rather than merely essential hypertension occurring in the presence of adiposity [15]. Unlike essential hypertension in lean populations, obesity-related hypertension is driven by a unique cascade of biological mechanisms originating directly from excess adipose tissue [16]. Specifically, adipocytes act as an active endocrine organ, secreting factors that cause overactivation of the sympathetic nervous system and direct stimulation of the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) [17]. Furthermore, the accumulation of visceral fat induces chronic systemic inflammation that impairs endothelial vasodilation, while the physical compression of the kidneys by intra-abdominal adipose tissue directly alters renal hemodynamics and promotes sodium reabsorption [18]. Because obesity-driven metabolic and neurohumoral pathways synergistically increase cardiovascular morbidity, the identification and management of this combined phenotype should be prioritised through integrated clinical and public health approaches rather than siloed strategies [7].

To accurately capture this high-risk phenotype, particularly in Asian populations, it is necessary to consider both general and visceral adiposity. Asian populations are known to exhibit higher levels of visceral fat at lower BMIs compared to Western populations, meaning that a reliance on standard BMI alone often underestimates true cardiometabolic risk [19]. While BMI effectively measures general adiposity, waist circumference specifically captures visceral adiposity, making the two metrics complementary rather than redundant [20]. Epidemiological evidence from Asian cohorts confirms that utilising a composite definition incorporating both general and central obesity provides a significantly more precise prediction of incident hypertension and adverse cardiac events than either measurement alone [21–23].

While the independent epidemiological profiles of obesity and hypertension in Malaysia are well-documented, there remains a critical knowledge gap regarding their concurrent presentation. Using data from NHMS 2023, this study assessed the prevalence of obesity-related hypertension and its association with sociodemographic characteristics, health behaviours, and comorbidities among adults in Malaysia. By conceptualising obesity-related hypertension as a single, composite outcome, this study provides novel epidemiological insights necessary to shift public health strategies away from isolated disease management. The findings aim to guide policymakers and healthcare providers in developing targeted, integrated screening and prevention strategies aligned with Malaysia’s national Non-Communicable Disease (NCD) frameworks.

Methods

Study design and sampling

This study utilized data from a nationally representative, population-based cross-sectional survey. The sampling frame was based on the 2020 National Population and Housing Census, employing a two-stage stratified random sampling technique. The primary strata consisted of the 13 states and three federal territories of Malaysia, while the secondary stratum was defined by urban and rural localities within each state. In the first stage, the Department of Statistics Malaysia (DOSM) randomly selected 499 Enumeration Blocks (EBs), comprising 389 urban and 110 rural areas. In the second stage, 5,988 living quarters (LQs) were selected as the secondary sampling units. The target population included individuals aged five years and older residing in non-institutionalized households for at least two weeks prior to the survey and who were able to communicate independently or through a proxy. Detailed methodological procedures and the full survey design have been described previously [24].

For the present study, participants aged < 18 years were excluded (n = 2758), as the analysis focused on the adult population. Participants with missing or incomplete data on key variables required for the analysis, including sociodemographic characteristics, blood pressure measurement and anthropometric indicators, were also excluded (n = 787). After applying these exclusion criteria, a total of 10,071 participants were included in the final sample. This sample served as the denominator for all prevalence estimates and regression analyses.

Study instrument

Data were collected using validated questionnaires adopted from international standards. All questionnaires used in this study have been published in the National Health and Morbidity Survey (NHMS) 2023 report [9]. Physical activity and alcohol consumption were assessed using the WHO Global Physical Activity Questionnaire (GPAQ) and the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT), respectively, while non-communicable disease (NCD) associated factors were measured using the WHO STEPwise Approach to Risk Factor Surveillance (STEPS) [25–27].

Clinical and anthropometric measurements

Blood pressure was measured using a digital automatic monitor (Omron HEM-907). Three readings were taken at three-minute intervals; the average of the second and third readings was used for analysis. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure (BP) ≥ 140 mmHg, diastolic BP ≥ 90 mmHg, or a prior physician diagnosis [20]. Blood glucose and total cholesterol were measured via finger-prick capillary blood using validated portable devices (Accu-Chek Guide and CardioChek PA). Anthropometric measurements included body weight (SECA 813) and height (SECA 213) to calculate Body Mass Index (BMI). BMI was categorized according to the Malaysian Clinical Practice Guidelines, with obesity defined as BMI ≥ 27.5 kg/m² [28]. Waist circumference was measured twice using a SECA 201 measuring tape; abdominal obesity was defined as ≥ 90 cm for men and ≥ 80 cm for women [29].

Data collection

Fieldwork was carried out between July and September 2023. To ensure standardization, data collectors underwent comprehensive training on interview procedures and clinical assessment techniques. Face-to-face interviews were conducted using handheld mobile devices. All clinical instruments were calibrated and validated before the commencement of fieldwork.

Ethical approval was obtained from the Medical Research and Ethics Committee (MREC), Ministry of Health Malaysia (NMRR ID-22-00545-XAC). An information sheet was provided to all eligible participants and written informed consent was obtained before data collection.

Independent variables

The study examined sociodemographic factors, health behaviors, and comorbidities as independent variables. Sociodemographic factors included residential locality (urban vs. rural), sex (male vs. female), ethnicity (Malay, Chinese, Indian, and Others), marital status (single vs. married) and education level (no formal/primary, secondary, or tertiary). Age was stratified into three categories: younger adults (18–39 years), middle-aged adults (40–59 years) and older adults (≥ 60 years).

Health behaviors included physical activity and alcohol consumption. Physical activity was categorized as active if participants accumulated ≥ 600 MET-minutes per week, as per the GPAQ criteria. Alcohol consumption was defined as any intake of alcoholic beverages within the 12 months prior to the survey.

Comorbidities assessed were diabetes mellitus and hypercholesterolemia. Both conditions were defined by a combination of self-reported physician diagnosis and newly detected cases from clinical screening. Diabetes mellitus was determined by fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or random blood glucose ≥ 11.0 mmol/L. Hypercholesterolemia was defined by a total cholesterol level ≥ 5.2 mmol/L.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using the Complex Samples module in SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) to account for the multi-stage, stratified cluster sampling design of the NHMS 2023. The survey design was specified by incorporating primary sampling units (clusters), stratification (state and locality), and sampling weights. These weights adjusted for non-response and post-stratification to ensure the findings were nationally representative of the Malaysian adult population.

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participant characteristics and the prevalence of obesity-related hypertension. Participant characteristics were presented as frequencies and unweighted percentages, whereas prevalence estimates were reported as weighted percentages. Multinomial logistic regression adjusted for sampling design was used to determine the associations between sociodemographic factors, health behaviours and comorbidities with the four outcome groups (Normal, Obesity Only, Hypertension only, and Obesity-Related Hypertension), using the “Normal” group as the reference category. Variable selection followed a purposeful selection framework [30]. Initially, variables showing an association (p < 0.25) in the univariable analysis, along with those of known clinical relevance and biological plausibility (e.g., age and sex), were entered into a preliminary multivariable multinomial logistic regression model. A manual backward elimination procedure was then performed, where non-significant variables were removed based on the Wald statistic. The final model retained variables that were significantly associated at the level of p < 0.05.

Multicollinearity and interactions were assessed accordingly. Model fitness was evaluated via a weighted classification table and the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve for each outcome comparison. Findings are presented as adjusted odds ratios (aOR) with their 95% confidence intervals (CI).

Result

Characteristics of the study population

A total of 10,071 participants aged 18 years and above were included in the analysis. Most participants resided in urban areas (76.1%), were female (53.7%) and were Malay ethnicity (58.4%). Approximately one-third of participants were aged 18–39 years (38.2%), while 35.0% were aged 40–59 years and 26.9% were aged 60 years and above. The majority were married (66.8%) and had secondary education (64.9%) (Table 1).

Prevalence of obesity only, hypertension only and obesity-related hypertension

Overall, 59.2% (95% CI: 57.37, 60.98) of Malaysian adults were classified as normal, while 10.8% (95% CI: 9.93, 11.80) had obesity only, 20.0% (95% CI: 18.78, 21.33) had hypertension only and 10.0% (95% CI: 9.13, 10.86) had obesity-related hypertension. Obesity only was more prevalent among females (13.2%; 95% CI: 11.91, 14.53), adults aged 18–39 years (14.1%; 95% CI: 12.68, 15.73), those who were married (11.4%; 95% CI: 10.29, 12.51) and participants with secondary education (12.5%; 95% CI: 11.35, 13.69). Hypertension only was more common among males (21.5%; 95% CI: 19.60, 23.47), adults aged 60 years and above (54.6%; 95% CI: 51.77, 57.30), those who were married (23.2%; 95% CI: 21.70, 24.75), and participants with no formal or primary education (36.1%; 95% CI: 33.02, 39.21). The prevalence of obesity-related hypertension was higher among females (12.3%; 95% CI: 11.13, 13.57), adults aged 40–59 years (15.1%; 95% CI: 13.52, 16.87), those who were married (11.4%; 95% CI: 10.26, 12.60), and participants with no formal or primary education (11.7%; 95% CI: 9.63, 14.08). A higher prevalence of obesity-related hypertension was also observed among physically inactive individuals (11.3%; 95% CI: 9.94, 12.87), participants with diabetes mellitus (22.6%; 95% CI: 20.07, 25.26), and those with hypercholesterolemia (16.0%; 95% CI: 14.60, 17.59) (Table 2).

Prevalence of normal population, obesity only, hypertension only and obesity-related hypertension

Factors associated with obesity only, hypertension only and obesity-related hypertension

Multinomial logistic regression analysis was performed using the normal population as the reference group. After adjustment for potential confounders, females had significantly higher odds of obesity only (aOR: 1.65; 95% CI: 1.39, 1.96) and obesity-related hypertension (aOR: 1.53; 95% CI: 1.25, 1.86) compared with males. However, females had lower odds of hypertension only (aOR: 0.77; 95% CI: 0.65, 0.91). Older age was strongly associated with hypertension only and obesity-related hypertension. Participants aged 40–59 years had higher odds of hypertension only (aOR: 4.81; 95% CI: 3.83, 6.05) and obesity-related hypertension (aOR: 2.44; 95% CI: 1.92, 3.12), while those aged 60 years and above had markedly increased odds of hypertension only (aOR: 12.48; 95% CI: 9.52, 16.36) and obesity-related hypertension (aOR: 2.80; 95% CI: 2.07, 3.77), compared with participants aged 18–39 years. Compared with Chinese participants, Malays had significantly higher odds of obesity only (aOR: 2.80; 95% CI: 1.85, 4.22) and obesity-related hypertension (aOR: 1.85; 95% CI: 1.22, 2.81). Lower educational attainment was associated with increased odds of hypertension only and obesity-related hypertension only. Participants with secondary education had significantly higher odds of obesity-related hypertension (aOR: 1.55; 95% CI: 1.16, 2.08), while those with no formal or primary education had higher odds of obesity-related hypertension (aOR: 1.71; 95% CI: 1.17, 2.49), compared with tertiary-educated participants. Physically inactive individuals had significantly higher odds of hypertension only (aOR: 1.21; 95% CI: 1.02, 1.43) and obesity-related hypertension (aOR: 1.26; 95% CI: 1.03, 1.55). Participants with diabetes mellitus had substantially higher odds of obesity only (aOR: 1.62; 95% CI: 1.19, 2.19), hypertension only (aOR: 3.57; 95% CI: 2.91, 4.36), and obesity-related hypertension (aOR: 4.49; 95% CI: 3.53, 5.72). Similarly, hypercholesterolemia was significantly associated with hypertension only (aOR: 2.33; 95% CI: 1.97, 2.76) and obesity-related hypertension (aOR: 2.30; 95% CI: 1.88, 2.81) (Table 3).

Discussion

The coexistence of obesity and hypertension represents a critical public health challenge in middle-income countries; however, evidence specifically addressing the obesity-related hypertension phenotype remains limited. Using nationally representative data from the NHMS 2023, this study examines the prevalence and associated factors of obesity-related hypertension among Malaysian adults. By applying a multinomial analytical framework, it distinguishes this high-risk group from individuals with isolated obesity or hypertension, offering a more nuanced understanding of Malaysia’s cardiometabolic landscape. These findings provide important evidence to inform future prevention and management strategies.

We observed an obesity-related hypertension prevalence of 10.0%, which is comparatively lower than the 22.7% documented in Chinese adults aged 45 and older [31]. This discrepancy can be attributed to both demographic and methodological differences. Demographically, the older cohort evaluated by Zhang et al. inherently carries a higher baseline likelihood of vascular stiffening. Methodologically, our study applied a much stricter composite definition by requiring the simultaneous presence of general obesity, abdominal obesity, and clinical hypertension. Despite our lower overall prevalence, the necessity of examining these concurrent conditions is supported by a recent Malaysian national study, which demonstrated that overweight and obesity particularly central obesity are significantly associated with increased odds of having hypertension [32].

We observed that obesity-related hypertension was more prevalent in females than in males, consistent with prior studies [33]. Notably, a study conducted in Indonesia similarly reported higher rates of both obesity and hypertension among females compared with males [34]. The underlying mechanisms may involve sex-specific metabolic pathways, as overweight or obese hypertensive women tend to exhibit more atherogenic lipid profiles and significantly higher lipid accumulation product (LAP) than normotensive women, whereas such differences are not observed in men [35]. These findings may also reflect gender differences in adiposity distribution and cardiometabolic risk, as women generally exhibit higher total body fat and distinct regional fat accumulation patterns that are associated with adverse metabolic profiles and greater susceptibility to hypertension [36].

Adults aged 40 to 59 years and those aged 60 years and older had significantly higher odds of having obesity-related hypertension compared with younger adults. This finding is consistent with the well-documented increase in cardiometabolic burden associated with advancing age [37]. Ageing is accompanied by several physiological and metabolic changes, including increased arterial stiffness, endothelial dysfunction, chronic low-grade inflammation, and reduced insulin sensitivity, which collectively contribute to the development of both obesity and hypertension [38, 39].

Additionally, lower educational attainment was significantly associated with obesity-related hypertension. Participants with no formal or primary education, as well as those with secondary education, had markedly higher odds compared with those who had attained tertiary education. These findings are consistent with previous evidence showing that higher educational attainment is associated with a lower likelihood of obesity-related hypertension [40]. This association may be explained by poorer cardiovascular risk factor management among individuals with lower education, including less optimal blood pressure control compared with their more educated counterparts [41]. Furthermore, lower educational attainment is often associated with limited health literacy, which may reduce individuals’ ability to obtain, understand, and apply health information effectively, thereby contributing to poorer cardiovascular health management [42].

Prior research has suggested that place of residence and marital status may be associated with hypertension and its related conditions. Several studies report a higher prevalence of obesity-related hypertension and elevated blood pressure in urban populations compared with rural populations, largely due to greater exposure to sedentary lifestyles, unhealthy diets and other behavioural factors influencing cardiometabolic health [11, 31]. This reflects the ongoing nutrition transition in Malaysia, where rapid urbanisation has increased access to calorie-dense, ultra-processed foods [43]. However, the absence of an association between locality and obesity-related hypertension in the present study may suggest a convergence of lifestyle patterns, with obesogenic behaviours becoming increasingly prevalent across both urban and rural settings. Similarly, being single or widowed has been linked to a higher likelihood of hypertension due to reduced social support and poorer health behaviours [44, 45]. However, no significant association was observed in the present study. This may further reflect lifestyle convergence, with cardiometabolic burden becoming more uniformly distributed across population subgroups in Malaysia.

In terms of health behavioural factors, our study found that obesity-related hypertension was significantly associated with physical inactivity but not with alcohol consumption. Epidemiological evidence shows that higher levels of physical activity are generally associated with lower odds of obesity and hypertension, contributing to more favourable cardiometabolic profiles [46, 47]. In contrast, alcohol consumption has been consistently identified as a modifiable risk factor, with heavier and more frequent intake associated with increased likelihood of obesity and hypertension [48]. The lack of a significant association between alcohol consumption and obesity-related hypertension in this study may reflect the sociocultural context of the study population, characterised by generally low alcohol consumption, prevailing cultural norms, and the possibility of under-reporting of intake.

Our findings show that diabetes and hypercholesterolemia are significantly associated with obesity-related hypertension, consistent with evidence of a strong interrelationship between obesity, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidaemia [31]. This clustering reflects shared pathophysiological mechanisms, including insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation, adipokine dysregulation, oxidative stress, and neurohormonal activation, which collectively contribute to arterial stiffness and elevated blood pressure [16–18]. These interconnected pathways act synergistically, substantially amplifying cardiovascular risk beyond the effect of individual conditions [7, 49]. Similarly, hypercholesterolemia emerged as an associated factor of obesity-related hypertension, consistent with evidence showing that dyslipidaemia clusters with obesity and hypertension and, in combination with elevated BMI, contributes to increased hypertension risk through shared cardiometabolic and vascular mechanisms [50].

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. It used a large, nationally representative sample of Malaysian adults, enhancing the generalisability of the findings. Standardised protocols for anthropometric and blood pressure measurements ensured reliable data and allowed for a comprehensive assessment of sociodemographic, behavioural, and clinical correlates of obesity-related hypertension. In addition, the application of complex samples multinomial logistic regression accounted for the multi-stage sampling design and provided statistically robust estimates.

Despite these findings, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between the identified factors and obesity-related hypertension. Second, self-reported behavioural variables, including physical activity and alcohol consumption, may be subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. Furthermore, information on dietary intake, medication adherence, and the duration or severity of comorbidities was unavailable, which may have resulted in residual confounding. Finally, other unmeasured factors, such as genetic predisposition and psychosocial stress, may also have influenced the observed associations with obesity-related hypertension.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights that obesity-related hypertension affects one in ten adults in Malaysia, representing a substantial and distinct cardiometabolic burden. The concurrent presentation of these conditions is significantly driven by sociodemographic, behavioural, and clinical factors particularly older age, female, lower educational level, physical inactivity, diabetes mellitus, and hypercholesterolemia. Furthermore, the lack of a significant urban-rural disparity suggests a nationwide lifestyle convergence, indicating that obesogenic environments and their associated cardiovascular risks are now pervasive across all localities.

These findings highlight the importance of targeted, data-driven interventions focusing on high-risk groups. Integrating risk stratification, opportunistic screening, and tailored lifestyle interventions into primary care and community platforms such as PEKA B40 (Skim Peduli Kesihatan) healthcare scheme and KOSPEN (Komuniti Sihat Pembina Negara) may enhance early identification and management of this high-risk phenotype. Strengthening these approaches within existing national non-communicable disease strategies in Malaysia could improve prevention and control efforts. Overall, a combination of targeted interventions for high-risk groups and broader population-level strategies is essential to reduce the growing burden of obesity-related hypertension and its long-term cardiovascular consequences.
