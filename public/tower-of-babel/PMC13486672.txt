Driving mechanisms of forest ecological product value realization using fsQCA analysis of 43 cases in China
Authors: Xuerui Zhu, Wenlan Wang, Qi Shi, Fuying Zhao, Zushuai Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractForest ecological products (FEPs) play an increasingly important role in promoting low-carbon development, rural revitalization, and ecological sustainability. However, the mechanisms through which their ecological value is transformed into economic value remain insufficiently understood. Drawing on capital circulation theory, this study develops an analytical framework for the value realization of FEPs. Using 43 publicly available official cases in China, the study evaluates the effectiveness of value realization through a coupling coordination model and employs fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) to identify the configurational paths leading to high-level value realization. The results indicate that the sampled regions have achieved initial coordination between ecological protection and economic development, although significant regional disparities remain, particularly between eastern and western China. High-level value realization is driven by multiple configurational paths rather than a single dominant factor, highlighting the joint effects of capital investment, value enhancement, institutional arrangements, and market mechanisms. However, existing value realization models remain insufficiently diversified and generally lack effective benefit-regulation mechanisms to sustain long-term value creation. These findings extend capital circulation theory to the context of FEPs and provide practical implications for improving institutional arrangements, strengthening regional coordination, and developing diversified value realization paths.Keywords: Forest ecological products, Value realization, Capital circulation theory, Driving factors, Fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA)Subject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Environmental sciences, Environmental social sciences

Introduction

Ecological products are widely regarded as a localized conceptualization of ecosystem services in China1,2. They are generally defined as goods and services generated through interactions between natural ecosystems and human activities that contribute to human well-being, reflecting both ecological and socio-economic value3,4. FEPs refer specifically to material products, regulating and cultural services, and ecological attribute-embedded products derived from forest ecosystems through ecological processes and human management5. Their value realization is the process of transforming ecological value into economic value through the sustainable use of forest resources, supported by policy innovation, market mechanisms, and technological advancement.

With the advancement of China’s carbon neutrality and rural revitalization strategies, forests have become increasingly important for providing ecological products such as carbon sequestration, timber, and cultural services6. However, realizing their value remains challenging because of supply–demand mismatches, high transaction costs, and weak market competitiveness7. The mechanisms through which these values can be effectively realized remain insufficiently understood.

Existing studies have examined the value realization of FEPs from perspectives such as value accounting8, realization paths9, and efficiency evaluation10. However, most focus on the effects of individual factors, paying limited attention to the configurational effects arising from interactions among multiple conditions. Although some studies have adopted fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) to examine such interactions11, they generally apply a unified capital circulation framework across different ecosystem types without accounting for their distinct characteristics. Compared with wetlands and lakes, FEPs differ substantially in property rights structure, production cycles, value forms, and stakeholder composition. These differences reshape the assumptions and operational logic of capital circulation, limiting the framework’s explanatory power in forest contexts.

To address this gap, this study asks two research questions: (1) What key conditions influence the efficient value realization of FEPs? (2) What configurational paths of multiple conditions lead to high-efficiency value realization?

Focusing on forest ecosystems, this study analyzes 43 representative cases from different regions of China using fsQCA to identify the key driving conditions and their configurational paths. The findings contribute to refining the theoretical framework of value realization for FEPs and provide policy implications for improving realization efficiency and institutional design.

Theoretical analysis

The value realization of FEPs aims to establish paid-use rules through a combination of market and non-market mechanisms. This process promotes a positive interaction between forest ecosystems and socioeconomic development, ultimately achieving coordinated growth under the framework of the “Two Mountains” theory. Given that the government-led model for FEPs value realization suffers from homogeneous implementation methods, low standards, and insufficient operational efficiency, advancing this process via market-oriented mechanisms has become a core research focus. Grounded in multidisciplinary foundations, this paper takes Marxist political economy as its core theoretical support. Adopting the research framework and methods proposed by Gao et al. (2024)11, this study integrates ecological value theory, property rights theory, and externality theory to construct a comprehensive theoretical system for FEPs value realization. On this basis, it further demonstrates the scientific rationality of a proposed five-stage operational logic.

Marxist political economy constitutes the fundamental theoretical basis of this paper, within which the theories of capital circulation, ecological labor, and material transformation provide core logical support. In Capital, Marx posited that commodities can enter socioeconomic circulation on the premise of utility, scarcity, and clearly defined property rights, realizing their exchange value through the logical sequence of purchase-production-sales12. This defines the basic prerequisites and core path for commodity value realization, offering fundamental guidelines for FEPs. Meanwhile, Marx’s material transformation theory (also known as the metabolic rift theory) reveals that the capitalist mode of production disrupts the material exchange between humans and nature. Essentially, the value realization of FEPs is a process of repairing this metabolic rift and achieving the sustainable circulation of ecosystems13. Furthermore, as an extension of Marxist ecological economics, ecological labor theory clarifies that ecological labor refers to the productive efforts invested by humans to restore ecosystems and maintain ecological balance. The production of FEPs concretely embodies this labor, deriving its value from ecological labor inputs and the appreciation of natural capital14. This lays the theoretical foundation for the origin and formation of FEPs value.

Drawing on capital circulation theory, Gao et al. (2024) constructed the theoretical logic for ecological product value realization. Through an empirical analysis of 37 typical cases, they verified the driving effects of five core factors: property rights systems, capital investment, value enhancement, supply-demand matching, and benefit regulation11. Their research framework is highly consistent with the approach taken in this paper. They argue that ecological product value realization requires an operational logic of “element collaboration – process closed loop.” Building upon their findings, this paper refines the theoretical framework by proposing five specific stages: “preparation–investment–production–transaction–feedback regulation.” This adaptation accounts for the unique characteristics of FEPs, such as their long transformation cycles, highly latent value, and complex property rights. The core logic of the two frameworks resonates closely, further validating the stage division proposed in this study.

Beyond these core theories, extended frameworks—including ecological value, property rights, and externality theories—further optimize this paper’s theoretical system and provide multi-dimensional support for the five-stage model. Ecological value theory confirms that ecosystems possess direct use, indirect use, and non-use values. FEPs integrate both natural and social attributes, and their value realization is fundamentally a process of transforming potential ecological value into tangible economic and social value15. Property rights theory emphasizes that clearly defined rights are a prerequisite for the market-oriented allocation of resources, offering direct support for the property rights system constructed in the preparation stage16. Finally, externality theory highlights the prominent positive externalities of FEPs; their value can rarely be realized spontaneously by the market al.one, necessitating institutional design to internalize these externalities17. This concept permeates all five stages, particularly supporting value manifestation in the production stage and institutional oversight in the feedback regulation stage.

Synthesizing multidisciplinary theories, existing literature, and the inherent attributes of FEPs, it becomes evident that FEPs value cannot spontaneously circulate in capital markets like ordinary commodities. The process is constrained by five major bottlenecks, which form the core rationale for this paper’s five-stage division:

First, the complex and fragmented property-rights structure alters the institutional conditions for capital entry, corresponding to the preparation stage. Unlike wetland and lake resources, which are predominantly state-owned and managed by unified authorities, forest resources in China involve multiple rights holders, including state-owned forest farms, village collectives, and individual farmers. Moreover, forest land ownership, contractual management rights, and ownership of standing timber are institutionally separated18. Such fragmented and often ambiguous property-rights arrangements prevent capital from entering the circulation process directly. Consequently, a dedicated preparation stage for property-rights clarification and standardization is required as an institutional prerequisite, extending and reinforcing the starting point of the general circuit of capital.

Second, the ultra-long cycle of production and value accumulation reshapes the temporal dynamics of capital circulation, corresponding to the investment stage. Ecological products derived from wetlands and lakes, such as aquatic products and short-cycle tourism projects, generally exhibit relatively rapid capital turnover, which aligns with the profit-seeking logic of social capital. In contrast, FEPs—including carbon sequestration, timber production, and ecosystem restoration—often require decades to generate economic value, involving substantial upfront investment, delayed returns, and considerable uncertainty regarding future benefits19. This temporal mismatch raises the entry threshold for social capital and necessitates a distinct investment stage supported by diversified financing mechanisms that integrate fiscal, social, and intellectual capital. In doing so, it challenges the conventional assumption of one-time investment followed by periodic returns embedded in the general capital circulation framework.

Third, the diversified and highly latent forms of value expand the value content of the production process, corresponding to the production stage. The value of wetland and lake ecological products is typically embodied in tangible goods or readily observable cultural services, making value assessment relatively straightforward. FEPs, however, simultaneously encompass material products, regulating services, and cultural services. Among these, regulating services such as carbon sequestration and water conservation are highly intangible and latent, making them difficult to incorporate directly into market transactions as conventional commodities. As a result, a specialized production stage is required to facilitate value realization through innovative carriers and valuation mechanisms, thereby enriching and deepening the production dimension of the general circuit of capital.

Fourth, the highly dispersed supply structure and pronounced free-rider problems reduce transaction efficiency and increase coordination costs, corresponding to the transaction stage. Ecological products from wetlands and lakes are typically supplied by unified operating entities, resulting in relatively standardized products and lower transaction costs. By contrast, FEPs are provided by dispersed forest households and village collectives, leading to substantial heterogeneity in product types and quality. Furthermore, the non-excludable nature of many forest ecosystem services intensifies free-rider problems and weakens consumers’ willingness to pay20. These characteristics complicate supply–demand matching and increase transaction costs, thereby requiring a dedicated transaction stage centered on platform construction, quality standardization, and market coordination to improve circulation efficiency.

Fifth, extensive spatial spillover effects and the involvement of multiple stakeholder groups complicate the closure and redistribution mechanisms of capital circulation, corresponding to the feedback-regulation stage. The development of wetland and lake resources typically affects a relatively limited range of stakeholders, making benefit distribution comparatively straightforward. Forest ecosystems, however, generate broader positive externalities across larger spatial scales and involve numerous village collectives and forest-dependent households that bear the opportunity costs of ecological conservation. Without appropriate benefit-sharing arrangements, capital appreciation may create a paradox in which ecological value increases while local communities fail to benefit proportionally. Therefore, a dedicated feedback-regulation stage is needed to reconcile value enhancement with distributive equity, thereby strengthening the closed-loop operation of capital circulation.

In summary, the distinctive characteristics of FEPs challenge the core assumptions of the general circuit of capital with respect to institutional entry conditions, temporal turnover, value formation, transaction coordination, and distributive closure. The five-stage framework proposed in this study—preparation, investment, production, transaction, and feedback regulation—therefore represents a context-specific extension of capital circulation theory for FEPs. By adapting the general capital circulation framework to the institutional, ecological, and economic characteristics of forest resources, this framework constitutes the central theoretical contribution of the present study:

Preparation stage: Clarifying ownership is the absolute prerequisite for FEPs value realization21. This stage is supported by property rights theory and the Marxist premise that clear property rights enable commodity circulation. Gao et al. (2024) similarly identified property rights systems as a primary driver, noting that clear definitions reduce transaction costs and stimulate market vitality11. Furthermore, the Opinions on Establishing and Improving the Mechanism for Realizing the Value of Ecological Products explicitly mandates the unified confirmation and registration of natural resources to lay an institutional foundation for ecological markets22. In this stage, the core task is to dismantle institutional barriers by constructing a robust forest resource property rights system. Through tenure confirmation, circulation standardization, and property management, the boundaries between public welfare forests and commercial forests can be delineated. Empowering forest tenure subjects with comprehensive rights of possession, use, and benefit will resolve market obstacles caused by ambiguous ownership.

Research design

Case overview

A total of 43 forest-based practical cases were selected as the objects of analysis from the officially published or upon-request disclosed typical cases of ecological product value realization released by the Ministry of Natural Resources, the National Development and Reform Commission, and the provincial-level authorities of Hebei, Shandong, Guangxi, Ningxia, Xinjiang, Yunnan, Anhui, Chongqing, Guangdong, Fujian, and Zhejiang.

It should be noted that, due to nested administrative hierarchies, some cases in this study involve higher-level administrative units (e.g., prefecture-level cities) encompassing lower-level units (e.g., counties or townships). Such spatial overlap under administrative affiliation represents only a superficial geographic relationship. The analytical units, value-realization scenarios, and observational dimensions of each case are defined independently; therefore, this does not constitute sample duplication.

The cases cover 13 provinces and 2 municipalities across China. All cases were compiled and organized by central ministries or provincial government departments, spanning the eastern, central, and western regions of the country, thereby ensuring both institutional authority and regional representativeness (Table 1).

Evaluation on the effectiveness of value realization for forest ecological products

Deeply rooted in China’s paradigm of Ecological Civilization, the Two Mountains theory (i.e., “lucid waters and lush mountains are invaluable assets”) reflects the distinctive idea that ecological conservation and economic development can be mutually reinforcing. In the context of FEPs, this implies that ecological resources can be transformed into economic value through market-oriented and industrialized approaches, while maintaining the sustainable provision of ecosystem services.

The level of coordinated development under the Two Mountains framework reflects the interaction between ecological conservation performance and economic transformation. As a composite index integrating ecological and economic dimensions, the Two Mountains coupling coordination degree can effectively reflect the effectiveness of FEPs value realization through its dual subsystems. The ecological subsystem provides the material premise for value realization by measuring the quality and sustainability of forest ecological resources, while the economic subsystem reflects the direct effect of value realization by measuring the level of ecological resource transformation into economic benefits. The coupling coordination degree further measures the synergy between the two: a high degree indicates a positive cycle of ecological protection and value realization, while a low degree reflects an imbalance that hinders sustainable value realization. It should be noted that a moderate degree may occasionally result from low-level ecological and economic development with a small gap (i.e., low-level false coordination), but its core logic still effectively reflects the overall level of value realization.

Therefore, the coupling coordination degree of the Two Mountains is adopted as a key indicator to evaluate the effectiveness of FEPs value realization.

Indicator system for evaluating lucid waters and lush mountains

Forest resources constitute the fundamental basis of the Lucid Waters and Lush Mountains ecological system. In this study, multidimensional ecological indicators are employed to systematically characterize regional ecological endowments and ecosystem service functions. Forest coverage serves as the most direct and essential indicator, reflecting the scale, spatial distribution, and structural integrity of regional forest ecosystems. It determines the capacity to supply key ecosystem services, including carbon sequestration, oxygen release, soil and water conservation, and biodiversity maintenance. Beyond forest-specific attributes, forest ecological benefits exhibit strong interactions with atmospheric and aquatic environments. The proportion of days with good air quality is used to capture the air purification and dust retention functions of forest vegetation, thereby reflecting the indirect contribution of forest ecosystems to regional air quality and public health. Similarly, the compliance rate of surface water quality reflects the role of forests in water retention, runoff regulation, and non-point source pollution mitigation within watershed ecosystems, highlighting their importance in maintaining regional water security and ecosystem stability. Taken together, these three indicators capture the core ecological implications of Lucid Waters and Lush Mountains. They provide a systematic representation of the multifunctional ecosystem services of forest systems—spanning forest conservation, atmospheric regulation, and water protection—and establish a comprehensive and robust ecological evaluation framework for research on forestry–economic coordination25.

Indicator system for evaluating “Invaluable Assets”

In contrast to the ecological dimension of Lucid Waters and Lush Mountains, the Invaluable Assets dimension emphasizes the economic returns, industrial upgrading, and social equity derived from ecological resources. GDP per capita serves as a core macroeconomic indicator, reflecting regional economic development, living standards, and overall economic capacity. It provides a direct measure of the material benefits generated from ecological endowments. The share of the tertiary industry captures the upgrading of the regional industrial structure, indicating a transition from resource-intensive sectors toward service-oriented, low-carbon, and more sustainable development paths. It also reflects the transformation potential of forestry-related industries. The urban–rural disposable income ratio is used to assess income disparities between residents, providing an indicator of the inclusiveness and balance of regional economic development. Taken together, these three indicators encompass key dimensions of economic performance, industrial transformation, and social equity. They offer a systematic representation of the concept of Invaluable Assets and capture the broader benefits associated with the economic realization of ecological resources (Table 2).

Empirical analysis on driving factors of value realization of forest ecological products

In summary, the realization of FEPs value is jointly influenced by multiple factors, including property rights, capital investment, and the balance between conservation and development. At present, such value realization in China remains at an early stage, with relatively few representative cases, making it a typical small- to medium-sample research context. Moreover, the outcomes of FEPs value realization are continuous variables, which are not well suited to traditional binary coding approaches. The fsQCA method is well adapted to small- and medium-sample analyses, allows for precise calibration of continuous variables, and facilitates the exploration of configurational causal mechanisms driven by multiple factors. Therefore, this study adopts the fsQCA approach for empirical analysis.

Method description

Fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) is a set-theoretic method that examines how combinations of conditions jointly produce an outcome30. It is particularly suitable for studies with medium-to-small sample sizes and for analyzing complex causal relationships involving multiple interacting factors31.

Given the limited number of available cases and the exploratory nature of FEPs value realization13, fsQCA is well suited to identify configurational paths of influencing factors in this study.

Variable selection and calibration

This study focuses on the driving mechanisms underlying the realization of FEPs value. Drawing on existing research11 and practical logic, five dimensions are selected—namely, the improvement of property rights institutions, effective capital investment, value enhancement, promotion of supply–demand matching, and benefit regulation. A total of 15 specific measures are identified as conditional variables. The effectiveness of FEPs value realization is taken as the outcome variable for configurational analysis.

To ensure rigor and reproducibility in variable operationalization, all variables are explicitly defined and subjected to systematic text coding. First, regarding the conditional variables, given that the data are primarily derived from publicly available textual materials of typical cases on FEPs value realization, this study employs a structured text coding approach. For each conditional variable, three indicative measures are specified (e.g., “confirmation and registration of rights,” “promotion of transfer,” and “property rights management” under the dimension of property rights institutional improvement). Coding is conducted by identifying whether the implementation of each measure is mentioned in the case texts, thereby generating qualitative coding outcomes. Second, for the outcome variable, the coupling coordination degree of the Two Mountains concept (Ni) is used as a proxy for the effectiveness of FEPs value realization. This indicator comprehensively reflects the level of coordination between ecological conservation and economic development within a region, and thus objectively captures the overall performance of value realization.

Based on the set-theoretic nature of fuzzy-set Qualitative Comparative Analysis (fsQCA), all variables are calibrated into membership scores ranging from 0 to 1, indicating the degree to which each case belongs to a given set. To balance theoretical grounding, methodological suitability, and research reproducibility, this study adopts a four-value fuzzy-set calibration approach. The calibration rules are developed by integrating commonly used classification standards of coupling coordination degree, established research paradigms in the Two Mountains field, and the distributional characteristics of the case data. The specific procedures and criteria are as follows:

(1)The outcome variable in this study is the coupling coordination degree of the Two Mountains which reflects the level of synergy between regional ecological protection and economic development. Its calibration is based on two considerations.

First, from a theoretical and standard perspective, this study adopts a widely recognized classification framework of coupling coordination degree32,33(see Table 3). This framework divides the interval [0,1] into ten subcategories, which are further grouped into three overarching stages: the maladjustment–decline stage (0 ≤ Ni ≤ 0.4), the transitional–transformation stage (0.4 < Ni ≤ 0.6), and the coordinated development stage (0.6 < Ni ≤ 1). This classification is consistent with the stage-based understanding of synergy in the Two Mountains transformation34.

Second, to ensure methodological suitability and replicability, the ten subcategories are consolidated into four distinct membership levels to meet the requirements of fsQCA four-value fuzzy sets. This approach balances variable granularity with model parsimony and avoids fragmentation of configurational results. The thresholds, theoretical meanings, and assignment rules for each level are explicitly defined. Specifically, values of 0 ≤ Ni ≤ 0.40 include four subcategories (extreme, severe, moderate, and mild maladjustment), corresponding to the maladjustment–decline stage. Cases in this range have not formed an effective eco-economic synergy mechanism and are defined as fully out of the set of high coupling coordination of the Two Mountains, assigned a value of 0. Values of 0.40 < Ni ≤ 0.60 include two subcategories (on the verge of maladjustment and barely coordinated), corresponding to the transitional stage. These cases are in a critical phase of transformation from imbalance to coordination, but the synergy mechanism remains unstable; they are defined as weakly in the set and assigned 0.33. Values of 0.60 < Ni ≤ 0.80 include two subcategories (primary and intermediate coordination), corresponding to the mid-level coordinated development stage. These cases have established relatively stable eco-economic synergy but have not reached an optimal level; they are defined as strongly in the set and assigned 0.67. Values of 0.80 < Ni ≤ 1.00 include two subcategories (good and high-quality coordination), corresponding to the advanced coordinated development stage. These cases achieve a high level of synergy between ecological protection and economic development and meet the standards of high-quality Two Mountains transformation; they are defined as fully in the set and assigned 1.

(2)The calibration of condition variables follows a two-step approach combining qualitative text coding and quantitative fuzzy-set assignment. First, qualitative text coding is conducted by systematically reviewing policy documents, government work reports, and official implementation records related to Two Mountains initiatives for each case. Based on theoretical frameworks and disciplinary consensus, three observable and verifiable measures are predefined for each condition variable. Second, based on the coding results, a four-value fuzzy-set calibration widely used in fsQCA research is applied. Cases implementing no measures are assigned 0 (full non-membership), as no actions related to the condition variable are taken. Cases implementing one measure are assigned 0.33 (weak membership), reflecting limited or partial alignment. Cases implementing two measures are assigned 0.67 (strong membership), indicating relatively high alignment through multidimensional and substantive actions. Cases implementing all three measures are assigned 1 (full membership), indicating complete alignment with the target set.

All variable definitions, indicators, and calibration rules are systematically presented in Table 4, ensuring transparency and replicability in the coding and calibration process.

Data sources

Due to differences in implementation timing and data availability across regions, cross-sectional data for 2020 were used for all outcome variables. Environmental indicators, including forest coverage rate, the proportion of days with good air quality, and the share of surface water meeting quality standards, were obtained from official environmental reports and 2020 statistical bulletins. Socioeconomic indicators—such as GDP per capita, the share of the tertiary sector, and the urban–rural income ratio—were collected from national and regional statistical yearbooks.

Before conducting fsQCA‑based analysis, we set the raw consistency cutoff to 0.8 and the PRI consistency value to 0.75. Considering our limited sample size, we set the case frequency threshold to 1 following the existing study36. Afterwards, complex, intermediate and parsimonious solutions were generated accordingly. We select intermediate solutions for subsequent analysis because their derived conditional configurations fit better with existing theories and real‑world practices. Consistent with reporting guidelines proposed by FISS37, conditions appearing merely within intermediate solutions are classified as peripheral conditions, while variables existing in both intermediate and parsimonious solutions are defined as core conditions. Even in the absence of mature market mechanisms, government-led arrangements—such as ecological compensation and public welfare forest management—enable value realization through administrative redistribution (e.g., transfer payments). As shown in Table 7, this configuration takes effective capital investment and value enhancement as the two core conditions (★), while benefit regulation is absent as a core condition (⊗). This path reflects the synergistic effect of capital investment and market-oriented value realization. Investments in ecological industries, carbon sequestration projects, and ecotourism infrastructure facilitate the transformation of forest ecological resources into tradable assets and generate value-added returns, consistent with the logic of ecological capital theory. With a raw coverage of 0.51, this path explains the largest proportion of cases. However, the absence of benefit-regulation mechanisms may create distributive equity risks. Under a capital-led development model, value-added benefits tend to be concentrated among external investors, while local communities and forest-dependent households often receive limited returns despite bearing the opportunity costs of ecological conservation. To enhance the sustainability of this path, benefit-sharing mechanisms based on ecological compensation, collective asset ownership, and equity participation should be established to balance value creation with distributive equity.

This configuration shares the same core conditions as Configuration 2 (★), but differs in the absence of supply–demand matching (⊗), while property rights and benefit regulation remain non-significant. It is thus defined as a “capital–value basic driven” path. The results suggest an asymmetric relationship between capital–value dynamics and supply–demand matching. Even without well-developed market connections, strong capital investment and value enhancement can stimulate demand endogenously. This finding is consistent with Say’s Law, whereby supply can generate its own demand under certain conditions. In practice, some cases achieve this through vertical integration and direct-to-consumer (DTC) models. However, the lack of supporting conditions limits market expansion and scalability. Policy support—particularly in infrastructure and platform development—can help strengthen this path and improve its stability.

Configuration 4: supply‑demand‑value market‑driven path

As shown in Table 7, this configuration is characterized by the absence of property rights support and capital investment (⊗), with only the peripheral presence of value enhancement and supply–demand matching (●). It is labeled as a “supply–demand–value market-driven” path. This path reflects a market-led mechanism with low institutional dependence. Despite weak formal institutions, value realization is achieved through informal arrangements and grassroots innovation. This is consistent with the theory of informal institutional substitution, whereby informal mechanisms compensate for institutional deficiencies. Such configurations are typically observed in regions with active local markets but weak formal governance, including culturally distinctive areas where ecological products are integrated with tourism demand. However, sustainability challenges remain: unclear property rights may lead to resource overuse, while limited capital constrains upgrading. Incremental institutional reforms—such as property rights clarification and microfinance support—are needed to enhance long-term viability.

Robustness check

To assess the robustness of the fsQCA results, this study employs two robustness tests: adjusting the raw consistency threshold and examining the configurational paths associated with low levels of value realization38,39.

Adjustment of raw consistency threshold

As the raw consistency threshold directly shapes the identification of sufficient condition configurations, adjusting this parameter represents a standard robustness test for fsQCA finding.

Building on the baseline raw consistency threshold of 0.80, we adopt two alternative thresholds for comparative examination: 0.75 (a relaxed criterion) and 0.85 (a tightened criterion). All other analytical specifications — including variable calibration schemes, case frequency threshold, and PRI consistency threshold — remain fully consistent with the baseline model. We re-execute the truth table algorithm and standard fsQCA analysis under each threshold, and compare the output core configurations, core condition compositions, and overall solution metrics against the baseline results.

The comparative results demonstrate that the configurational paths obtained under the 0.75 and 0.85 thresholds are largely congruent with the baseline findings. The core conditions of each key configuration remain stable, and the underlying driving logic of each path stays fundamentally unchanged. Furthermore, both solution consistency and solution coverage under the two adjusted thresholds fall within acceptable ranges, with no order-of-magnitude discrepancy from the baseline results.

The above findings indicate that the core conclusions of this study do not depend on a specific consistency threshold setting, and the research results have good stability.

Causal asymmetry test with the opposite outcome variable

A core assumption of QCA is causal asymmetry, which holds that the configurations leading to a high-level outcome are not simply the inverse of those leading to a low-level outcome. To examine this assumption and assess the robustness of the results, we conduct an analysis of the opposite outcome, namely the low level of FEPs value realization (~ Y).

The complement set (~ Y) is constructed by calculating 1 minus the membership score of the original outcome set (Y). All antecedent conditions, calibration schemes, consistency thresholds, and case frequency thresholds are kept identical to those used in the baseline analysis. An fsQCA analysis is then performed for ~ Y, and the resulting configurations are compared with those associated with high value realization.

The results reveal clear causal asymmetry between the two outcomes. The low-outcome model exhibits lower solution consistency (0.83) and solution coverage (0.83) than the high-outcome model (0.86 and 0.94, respectively), suggesting that low value realization is driven by more fragmented and heterogeneous causal structures. Moreover, the paths leading to high value realization display a relatively clear pattern of dual-core synergy. In contrast, the configurations associated with low value realization lack a dominant core logic and are characterized by diverse combinations of present and absent conditions. This indicates that low value realization cannot be explained simply by the absence of the factors driving high value realization. Although some overlap exists in peripheral conditions, the core causal mechanisms differ substantially across the two outcomes.

Overall, these findings support the causal asymmetry assumption of QCA and provide additional evidence for the robustness of the baseline results.

Regional heterogeneity analysis

As shown in Table 5, the coupling coordination degree of the Two Mountains transformation exhibits clear regional heterogeneity between the eastern and western regions. All five cases with favorable coordination (coupling coordination degree > 0.80) are located in the eastern region, where most cases achieve intermediate or favorable coordination, indicating a relatively mature interaction between ecological protection and economic development. This regional disparity can be attributed to the eastern region’s more developed market system, stronger social capital participation, and more diversified ecological industries, which together facilitate the efficient transformation of ecological resources into economic and social value. By contrast, most western cases fall within the primary or reluctant coordination categories, with only a few reaching intermediate coordination. Constrained by fragile ecological conditions, lower levels of marketization, and a weaker industrial base, the western region generally exhibits a lower level of coupling coordination, reflecting comparatively weaker performance in the value realization of FEPs.

However, the sub-sample fsQCA does not reveal significant regional heterogeneity in the configurational paths leading to high-level value realization. One possible explanation is that, under relatively small sub-sample sizes, configurational analysis tends to identify common causal patterns rather than region-specific paths.

Discussion

The findings from the driving-factor analysis (Sect. 4.2) provide deeper insight into how the value of FEPs can be realized efficiently. The necessity analysis shows that none of the examined conditions—including property rights institutions, capital investment, value enhancement, supply–demand matching, and benefit regulation—independently satisfies the criterion for a necessary condition. In other words, high-efficiency value realization does not rely on the presence of any single determinant. Instead, it results from the interaction of multiple institutional, market, and resource-related factors operating in combination. This interpretation is consistent with Zhang et al. (2025), who argue that ecological resources can be effectively converted into economic value only through the coordinated influence of institutional arrangements, capital investment, and market mechanisms40. A similar perspective is presented in The State of the World’s Forests 2024, where the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) notes that the large-scale realization of forest ecosystem service values depends on the joint contributions of technological innovation, social participation, supportive policies, institutional arrangements, and financial investment, rather than on any single intervention41.

The configurational analysis further reveals four alternative paths leading to efficient FEPs value realization. Among these, the capital–value dual-core driving path and the capital–value foundational driving path account for a larger proportion of successful cases, whereas the property rights institutional driving path and the supply–demand–value market driving path are characterized by relatively lower coverage and the absence of strong core conditions. These results extend previous studies that mainly attribute ecological value realization to individual driving forces. For instance, XU et al. (2025) identify human capital as a primary contributor42, while LIN et al. (2024) emphasize the enabling role of digital technologies43. By comparison, our findings are more consistent with Gao et al. (2024), whose fsQCA analysis demonstrates that multiple complementary conditions, rather than isolated factors, jointly shape successful ecological product value realization11. The present study reinforces this configurational perspective by providing additional evidence from forest ecological products at the micro-case level. Moreover, the observed regional differences in coupling coordination are in line with Zhang et al. (2025), who report that variations in ecological resource endowments and economic development contribute to higher ecological value transformation efficiency in eastern China than in the central and western regions44. Building on these observations, this study further identifies distinct regional configurational patterns, illustrating that the mechanisms underlying FEPs value realization vary across different development contexts.

The coupling coordination analysis (Sect. 4.1) indicates that all typical case regions are in a state of coordinated development, with none classified as dysfunctional. This suggests that substantial progress has been made in exploring paths for FEPs value realization across regions, and that a mutually reinforcing relationship between economic development and forest ecological conservation has begun to emerge. This pattern is likely associated with China’s continuous promotion of ecological civilization and the implementation of policies aimed at realizing the value of ecological products. Nevertheless, only 11.63% of the regions achieve a high level of coordination (coupling coordination degree > 0.80), revealing pronounced east–west spatial disparities. Highly coordinated regions are concentrated primarily in eastern China, particularly in the southeastern coastal provinces and key ecological regions, whereas western China generally remains at lower coordination levels because of relatively weaker marketization and industrial development. Regions exhibiting primary or barely coordinated development are mainly distributed across ecologically sensitive areas in central and western China, semi-arid northern regions, and traditional industrial bases. These regional disparities may be attributed to differences in ecological endowments, economic development levels, and the efficiency of ecological value transformation. Regions with abundant ecological resources and well-developed market systems are generally more capable of converting FEPs into tangible economic benefits. In contrast, ecologically fragile and economically less-developed regions often face constraints arising from insufficient capital investment, underdeveloped markets, and limited institutional support. Interestingly, the sub-sample fsQCA reveals no pronounced regional differences in the core driving paths. This may be because analyses based on relatively small sub-samples are more likely to capture common causal configurations shared across regions rather than region-specific mechanisms.

From both theoretical and practical perspectives, this study makes several contributions to the existing literature. Theoretically, by considering the distinctive institutional, ecological, and economic characteristics of forest ecological products (FEPs), which differentiate them from other ecological products such as wetlands and lakes, this study develops a refined five-stage capital circulation framework for FEPs value realization. This framework extends the general capital circulation theory, enriches the quantitative analytical framework of the Two Mountains concept, and broadens the application of fuzzy-set qualitative comparative analysis (fsQCA) in ecological value realization research. Methodologically, this study strengthens the robustness of its findings through extensive robustness tests and regional heterogeneity analysis. The configurational results demonstrate that FEPs value realization depends on synergistic interactions among institutional, capital, market, and regulatory conditions rather than on any single factor in isolation, thereby providing further evidence of the complex causal mechanisms underlying ecological value transformation. Practically, this study identifies four core driving paths for FEPs value realization and further highlights the potential equity risk of “ecological gains, community losses” embedded in the capital–value dual-core path by incorporating benefit regulation and distributional equity analysis. Together with the pronounced east–west regional disparities in coupling coordination performance, these findings provide differentiated policy recommendations for promoting the high-quality and sustainable realization of FEPs value, rather than relying on uniform governance approaches.

Specifically, for regions prioritizing ecological protection but with weak market foundations, corresponding to the property rights system-driven configuration, improving property rights institutions should be the primary policy focus. Policymakers can advance this by deepening collective forest tenure reform, completing forest land ownership confirmation and registration, and establishing standardized platforms for forest land management rights transfer. Complementary policies such as ecological public welfare forest compensation and transfer payments for key ecological function zones should be improved to build a stable institutional and financial guarantee mechanism for value realization when market mechanisms are underdeveloped.

For economically developed regions with abundant ecological resources, corresponding to the capital-value dual-core driven configuration, policies should prioritize leveraging the synergy between multi-source capital investment and market-oriented value transformation. This includes increasing fiscal investment in forest carbon sink projects, forest wellness infrastructure and ecological product processing industries, while guiding social capital to participate through PPP models and green finance tools such as forestry loans. Crucially, policies must also address the equity gap highlighted in this path by strengthening benefit-sharing mechanisms: promoting “enterprise + village collective + farmer” dividend models, requiring ecological projects to sign interest linkage agreements with local communities, and ensuring ecological compensation funds directly benefit residents, thus avoiding the “ecological gains but community losses” paradox.

For regions with strong capital support but insufficient market matching, corresponding to the capital-value basic driven configuration, policies should focus on alleviating supply-demand bottlenecks through both market and public service interventions. On one hand, support should be provided for leading enterprises to build direct-to-consumer (DTC) sales channels through industrial chain integration. On the other hand, public policies should supplement market mechanisms by supporting the construction of regional public brands for FEPs, establishing standardized quality certification systems (e.g., organic forest product certification, geographic indication protection), and building cross-regional transaction platforms to expand market reach and achieve scale effects.

For regions with active private markets but lagging institutional reforms, corresponding to the supply-demand-value market-driven configuration, a gradual policy reform strategy is recommended. While preserving grassroots market vitality, policymakers can pilot forest tenure system reforms in these regions to clarify property rights boundaries, and introduce inclusive financial policies such as microcredit for ecological product operators and forestry right mortgage loans to alleviate capital constraints. These policies aim to upgrade informal market mechanisms into sustainable, regulated models while maintaining the region’s existing market advantages.

Moreover, the observed regional disparities in coupling coordination degree have clear public policy implications for reducing development inequality. Building on national policies such as the “Ecological Product Value Realization Pilot Program” and rural revitalization support policies, policymakers should increase targeted support for central and western regions: prioritizing value realization pilots in ecologically rich but economically underdeveloped areas, improving cross-regional ecological compensation and paired assistance mechanisms, and enhancing institutional innovation and capital investment in these regions to narrow regional gaps in value realization efficiency.

This study also has several limitations that warrant further investigation. First, the sample consists of officially published typical cases, which may introduce selection bias and limit the representativeness of the findings. Second, the study relies on cross-sectional data—a snapshot of the year 2020—and thus fails to capture the dynamic evolution of value realization mechanisms and coupling coordination over time. It is important to acknowledge that this cross-sectional nature of the 2020 data may affect the interpretation of the results: as a single-timepoint observation, it cannot reflect temporal changes in the relationship between ecological and economic dimensions, nor can it account for potential fluctuations caused by policy adjustments, external shocks, or long-term ecological restoration effects, which may lead to an incomplete understanding of the sustainability and stability of value realization mechanisms. Third, the study uses the “degree of coupling and coordination” as a proxy to reflect the realization of FEPs value, and while this indicator is useful in measuring the synergistic degree between ecological and economic dimensions (i.e., it reflects value realization by quantifying how well ecological protection and economic development are coordinated, a core requirement for sustainable value realization), it also has inherent limitations. As a composite indicator combining ecological and economic dimensions, it cannot distinguish the independent contributions of each dimension to value realization, nor can it fully capture the quality and depth of value realization. Fourth, the lack of micro-level data, such as household surveys, restricts the ability to analyze the role of individual actors in the value realization process.

Future research could address these limitations by expanding the sample to include non-typical regions, employing panel data to explore dynamic changes over time (thereby overcoming the constraints of the 2020 cross-sectional snapshot), optimizing the measurement of the coupling and coordination degree to better distinguish the contributions of ecological and economic dimensions, and incorporating field surveys to provide micro-level evidence. Such efforts would contribute to optimizing the paths for FEPs value realization and enhancing the stability and sustainability of the underlying mechanisms.
