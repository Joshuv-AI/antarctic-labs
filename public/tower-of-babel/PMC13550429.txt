Distinctive patterns of glucagon and incretin responses to oral and isoglycaemic intravenous glucose load in fibrocalculous pancreatic diabetes
Authors: Shivendra Verma, Riddhi Dasgupta, Shajith Anoop, Felix K Jebasingh, Sudipta Dhar Chowdury, Arun Jose, Gracy Varghese, Pamella Christudoss, Padmanaban Venkatesan, Grace Rebekah, Michael Horowitz, Nihal Thomas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractFibrocalculous pancreatic diabetes mellitus (FCPD) is characterised by pancreatic calcification, intraductal calculi and severe insulin deficiency, yet abnormalities in glucagon regulation and incretin hormone responses remain incompletely understood. We evaluated the glucagon, incretins and oxyntomodulin secretory responses to oral and intravenous glucose administration in participants with FCPD. Participants with FCPD (cases; n = 9; mean age 34 ± 7.2 years) and healthy individuals (controls; n = 6; mean age 29.8 ± 7.7 years) underwent an extended oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) and isoglycaemic intravenous glucose infusion (IIGI). Serial measurements of plasma glucose, insulin, C-peptide, glucagon, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1), glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP), oxyntomodulin (OXM), and pancreatic polypeptide (PP) were done. Serial hormone responses were analysed using linear mixed-effects models, with area-under-the-curve analyses using trapezoidal rule, performed as secondary summaries. On primary mixed-model analysis, the participants with FCPD exhibited significantly higher fasting and post-OGTT glucagon concentrations with glucagon exposure being greater following OGTT than IIGI in the group with FCPD, whereas no route-dependent difference was observed in control participants. GLP-1 and OXM responses were similarly increased in participants with FCPD, particularly following, oral glucose tolerance test, while GIP responses were attenuated. In contrast, C-peptide and pancreatic polypeptide responses were profoundly suppressed during both OGTT and IIGI in FCPD, consistent with severe pancreatic endocrine failure. Despite matched glycaemic exposure, the incretin effect and gastrointestinal glucose disposal were reduced in the FCPD group. These findings demonstrate persistent hyperglucagonemia accompanied by a distinctive pattern of incretin secretion in FCPD, characterised by exaggerated L-cell–derived hormone secretion with attenuated K-cell (GIP) response and impaired pancreatic peptide responses, highlighting altered entero-pancreatic hormonal regulation in this condition.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-55406-y.Keywords: Diabetes, Pancreatitis, FCPD, Glucagon, GLP-1, GIP, Oxyntomodulin, Pancreatic PolypeptideSubject terms: Diseases, Endocrinology, Gastroenterology, Medical research

Introduction

The American Diabetes Association (ADA) has classified diabetes mellitus (DM) caused by chronic pancreatitis or other pancreatic anomalies as pancreatogenic diabetes or Type 3 C diabetes mellitus1. Fibrocalculous Pancreatic Diabetes (FCPD) is a secondary form of DM that affects the exocrine and endocrine pancreatic functions, predominantly in populations from tropical countries2. The distinguishing features of FCPD include chronic abdominal pain on ingestion of a fat rich meal, large, intraductal calculi, with pancreatic fibrosis, steatorrhea, overt hyperglycaemia and frequent episodes of hypoglycaemia3 and an increased propensity to develop pancreatic adenocarcinoma4. The participants with FCPD may be resistant to ketosis and ketoacidosis on withdrawal of insulin5. The prevalence of FCPD has been reported in tropical countries. A previous study from Southern India reported a prevalence of 0.36% among diabetes and 0.019% among the general population6. In India, the prevalence of FCPD decreased from 1.6% during 1991–1995 to 0.2% during the years 2006–2010 whereas the age of diagnosis of FCPD had increased7. Clinical studies have reported the incidence of FCPD in Northern India8, Kerala9, Eastern India10 and Bangladesh11.

FCPD was traditionally considered a form of pancreatic diabetes with reduced to absent glucagon secretion. However, our previous study has elucidated significantly higher fasting and postprandial glucagon levels, which in turn, afford some protection from hypoglycaemia unawareness in patients with FCPD12. Likewise, studies from India by Yajnik et al.5, and Mohan et al.13., have suggested preserved, though not elevated, glucagon suppression. A study on pancreatectomized patients has reported hyperglucagonemia in response to the oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT), when compared to a corresponding Isoglycaemic intravenous glucose infusion (IIGI). Furthermore, the response of incretins namely the Glucagon like peptide-1 (GLP-1), Oxyntomodulin (OXM) and the Glucose-dependent Insulinotropic Polypeptide-(GIP) were higher in pancreatectomized patients when compared to healthy individuals. Notably, the GLP-1 level was threefold higher at 30 min of the OGTT in pancreatectomised patients when compared to healthy controls. Overall, the observations of this study proved the presence of glucagon secretory response to OGTT in pancreatectomised patients whereas, this response is suppressed during intravenous glucose administration suggesting that the glucagon secretion is of extra-pancreatic origin, possibly from the L-cells of the human gut with a potential glucoregulatory role14. While there are reports pertaining to the role of incretins in patients with Type 2 DM15,16, systematic studies on the incretin axis and glucagon secretion in patients with FCPD are sparse.

The islets of pancreas secrete insulin and amylin from beta cells, glucagon from alpha cells, somatostatin from delta cells and pancreatic polypeptide (PP) from F-cells as well as Ghrelin17. The key endogenous incretins are GLP-1 secreted from L-cells situated in ileum, and GIP secreted from K-cells in duodenum and jejunum18. All three hormones: glucagon, GLP-1 and OXM are derived from same glucagon gene with differential tissue specific post-translational modifications. Traditionally, GLP-1 was thought to be secreted primarily from the intestinal L-cells. However some studies have shown than pancreatic alpha cells may also secrete GLP-1, in tandem with endogenous insulin secretion, under high glucose concentrations19,20. Studies using flow cytometry, immunohistochemistry perfusions, and calcium imaging techniques in cadavers of humans with and without type 2 diabetes have demonstrated that approximately 40% of the subpopulation of the human α cells contain GLP-121. The action of the pancreas specific pro-convertase-2 (PC-2) enzyme leads to production of glucagon from pro-glucagon in the pancreas while proconvertase 1/3 (PC-1/3) in the entero-endocrine cells leads to production of GLP-1 and OXM22. However, other studies have challenged the concept of tissue specific expression of PC-1/3 and PC-223,24.

The GIP is also a glucagon gene product and the selective expression of PC-1/3 in K-cells produces a 42 amino-acid GIP (1–42). On the other hand, PC-2 expression in alpha cells forms a truncated GIP (1–30) along with glucagon. This truncated GIP (1–30) has equipotent insulinotropic property25. Given the complexity of glycaemic excursions, hypoglycaemic episodes and the significant risk of pancreatic adenocarcinoma in patients with FCPD, it is important to understand the glucoregulatory role of incretins, especially in young adults with FCPD.

Oxyntomodulin (OXM) is an endogenous 37–amino acid peptide hormone derived from the proglucagon precursor and secreted by the intestinal endocrine L-cells in response to food intake. It contains the full glucagon sequence extended by an eight–amino acid C-terminal tail. Following meal intake, OXM enters the circulation and acts as a dual agonist at the glucagon receptor (GCGR) and the glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor (GLP-1R), though it binds to each receptor with lower potency than their respective native hormones, glucagon and GLP-126.

We hypothesised that non-pancreatectomised participants with FCPD may exhibit relative hyperglucagonemia in association with altered incretin and other gut hormone responses, thereby contributing to the metabolic dysfunction. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to characterise glucagon, Oxyntomodulin, pancreatic polypeptide and incretin secretory responses to OGTT and IIGI administration in participants with FCPD and to compare it with the matched healthy participants. The secondary objective was to examine whether route-dependent differences (oral vs. intravenous) in glucagon secretion are observed in individuals with FCPD, which could suggest possible alterations in the gut–pancreas hormonal pathways in them.

Research design and methods

This case-control study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) for Ethics in Research on Humans (IRB #10709). The study protocol adhered to the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki as revised in 2008 and informed consent was obtained from the study participants. The outcome of this study was to evaluate the glucagon and incretin response to OGTT and IIGI procedures in participants with FCPD and compare it with normoglycemic healthy individuals. The sample size was calculated with reference to a previous study14, which reported a mean difference of 1220 pmol/l in glucagon levels in response to an extended OGTT (180 min duration) when compared to individuals without diabetes. The sample size for this study was determined as 12 participants (participants with FCPD n = 6 and controls n = 6) to achieve a power of 80% and level of significance for differences in mean values less than 5%.

Participants with FCPD were diagnosed as those with recurrent pancreatitis, abdominal pain, pancreatic ductal calculi or ductal dilatation on imaging (CT abdomen), pancreatic exocrine insufficiency and diabetes mellitus in the absence of alcoholism27. Male participants with FCPD (n = 09), aged between 18 and 45 years with an HbA1c ranging between 7% and 10% were recruited from the allied departments of the institution with an informed consent. All participants with FCPD were not pancreatomized and were treated using a basal-bolus insulin regimen which consisted of regular insulin thrice daily, prior to meals and neutral protamine Hagedorn (NPH) insulin at bedtime. Individuals with pancreatic carcinoma, Cushing’s syndrome, Hepatitis B and C, HIV infection, acquired lipodystrophy, chronic alcohol consumption or history of gastrointestinal surgery or pancreatic trauma were excluded from the study. BMI matched healthy, normoglycemic males (n = 06) were recruited as control participants. Whole body composition was assessed using a Dual-energy X-ray Absorptiometry (DXA) scanner (Hologic DEXA Discovery QDR 4500). Bilateral sections and whole-body composition data including total body fat percentage, fat mass, lean mass, and truncal fat were obtained by analysing the regions of interest (ROI) using APEX software (Version 4.0.2). The coefficient of variation of the Hologic scanner (CV) was 4% with high precision and can detect small differences in lean mass and fat mass of 1–2% between two measurements at 95% Confidence Interval28.

Biochemical and metabolic assessment

The study participants underwent biochemical assessment after 8–10 hours of overnight fast. The fasting and post prandial plasma glucose, lipid profile, glycosylated haemoglobin, and serum albumin were measured. Serum insulin was measured by a Chemiluminescent immunometric assay (Coefficient of Variation (CV): 7.3%) (Siemens Immulite 2000 XPI, Siemens healthcare, GmbH, Germany). C-peptide levels were measured by chemiluminescent assay using the Siemens Diagnostics kit with reportable range 0.1–20 ng/mL (Siemens healthcare GmbH, Germany (Intra assay CV: 1.7 to 2.3%) on the Siemens Immulite 2000 XPI system (Siemens healthcare GmbH, Germany). Serum total cholesterol (TC), Low density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), High density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and triglycerides were measured by colorimetric assay using the Roche Diagnostic kits on the automated Cobas 8000 analyser (Roche Diagnostics GmbH Manheim, Germany). HbA1c levels in blood was measured by high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using the Bio-Rad diagnostics kit on the BioRad D-100 analyser (Biorad Laboratories Inc, USA). The plasma glucagon levels were assayed by chemiluminescent ELISA (CLIA) using commercially available kits from Merck. The lowest detection limit for glucagon in plasma samples was 0.003 ng/mL (Intra assay CV: < 5%, Inter assay CV: < 10%). The plasma levels of incretins namely the Glucagon like Peptide − 1 (GLP-1), [Lowest detection limit: 2 Picomoles (Pm), Intra-assay CV: 6–8%, Inter-assay CV: 7–13%), Oxyntomodulin (OXM) (Detection limit: 200–3200 picograms (pg) /mL and CV less than 9%), and Gastric Inhibitory Peptide (GIP) Inter-assay: 1.8–6.1%, Intra-assay: 3.0–8.8%). The Pancreatic Polypeptide (PP) (Detection limit: 12.3 to 3000 pg/mL, Intra-assay CV: 5%, Inter-assay CV: 4.4%). Carbohydrate Antigen (CA) 19 − 9 was analysed by Chemiluminescent assay (CLIA) on an Automated analyser (Atellica® IM Solutions, Siemens Healthineers, Forchhem, Germany). The range of detection was 1.2 to 700 Units (U) per ml of serum. The detection value < 31 in a sample was considered as normal. The coefficient of variation was less than 6%.

Dynamic metabolic procedures

The participants with FCPD underwent a medical examination followed with a four-point capillary blood glucose monitoring daily for two weeks prior to the procedure. The glycaemic status was maintained on thrice daily dose of bolus (Regular) insulin regimen and basal insulin Neutral Protamine Hagedorn (NPH) at night. In participants with FCPD, optimal correction of glucotoxicity was achieved prior to the initiation of OGTT and IIGI procedures so as to avoid overt glycaemic excursions and aberrant incretin responses during the procedures. Participants with FCPD and with stable glucose levels were eligible for the extended OGTT procedure lasting for 180 minutes. To minimize the interference of exogenous insulin during the procedures, the time of insulin administration for the intermediate-acting insulin (NPH) was changed from bedtime to 4 pm, five days prior to the metabolic procedures.

Correction of glucotoxicity

Participants with FCPD who were eligible for the study procedures were provided with a glucose log book and a glucometer and instructed to monitor the fasting, pre- and post-prandial glycaemic profiles daily (fasting, premeal and 2 h post meal with meals including breakfast, lunch and dinner respectively) for one week preceding the OGTT and IIGI procedures. The subjects would follow-up with the self-monitored glucose readings daily and consult the Diabetes Nurse educator and the study investigators. Dietary counselling was provided to ensure all the subjects were consuming a standardized, balanced diet and taking precautions to avoid hypoglycaemia, especially nocturnal hypoglycaemia. The glycaemic status of participants with FCPD was maintained by a bolus insulin regimen thrice daily and basal insulin Neutral Protamine Hagedorn (NPH) at night. The insulin doses were titrated to achieve optimal glycaemic control as per the ADA targets (Fasting blood glucose or pre-meal: 80–130 mg/dl, Post prandial blood glucose < 180 mg/dl). Participants were also provided with phone numbers on which they could call for teleconsultation during any acute rise or fall in blood glucose levels during the two-week period for correction of glucotoxicity.

The participants with FCPD reported to the laboratory in a fasting state for at least eight hours overnight. Insulin and pancreatic supplements were withdrawn during this period. The venous access was checked, and an indwelling intravenous catheter was inserted contra-laterally in the veins of the antecubital fossa of the right arm and used for blood sampling. The blood samples were collected in fasting state. For the OGTT procedure, 75 grams of anhydrous glucose was dissolved in 200 ml of water. The subjects consumed the glucose mixture over two minutes, and blood samples were collected at 15, 30, 45, 60, 90, 120, 150 and 180 minutes after consumption of the glucose mixture. At the end of the OGTT procedure, participants with FCPD received half of the basal insulin dose from the previous regimen. Blood samples for glucagon and incretin assay were collected in sterile glass tubes containing Aprotinin solution (500 kIU/ml blood; Trasylol®, Bayer, Leverkusen, Germany), 0.3 ml of EDTA and protease inhibitors (Complete, Mini, EDTA-free”, Roche applied sciences, Germany). The tubes containing blood samples were stored on ice and were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 15 minutes in a refrigerated centrifuge to separate the plasma. All plasma samples were stored at minus 80˚C in a deep freezer and assayed within the three months from the procedure.

Intravenous isoglycaemic glucose infusion (IIGI)

To study the incretin effect in response to a glucose challenge, it was essential to perform the IIGI in tandem with the OGTT. The participants with FCPD who underwent the OGTT procedure were advised to monitor capillary blood glucose levels for the next three days and avoid a high calorie diet that may lead to glycaemic excursions. The insulin regimen was maintained in the same manner as that for the OGTT procedure. On the fourth day, participants reported to the laboratory in fasting state. An indwelling intravenous catheter was inserted contra-laterally in the veins of the antecubital fossa of the left arm and used for blood sampling. The second catheter was inserted in the veins of the antecubital fossa of the right arm. During the IIGI procedure, 20% (weight/volume) of dextrose was infused intravenously. Blood samples were drawn for Insulin, glucagon, and incretin assays at specific time points as that of the OGTT procedure. For the glucagon and incretin assays, venous blood samples were collected in glass test tubes containing EDTA and protease inhibitors (Aprotinin ®). Plasma glucose levels were checked every 5 min using a bedside glucose analyser (Analox GM-9D, UK) and documented for the entire procedure lasting 180 min. The dextrose infusion flow rate was meticulously adjusted using an infusion pump (Terumo®, TE-112) to match the plasma glucose levels as that of the OGTT procedure done previously. The total volume of dextrose infused was noted and calculated as the actual weight of dextrose in grams.

The mean concentration of each analyte on OGTT and IIGI procedure was estimated by calculating the Area Under Curve (AUC) using the trapezoidal rule and are presented as incremental value. The difference in Beta-cell secretory responses to both the stimuli represents the action of incretin factors and is expressed as the percentage of the physiological response to an oral glucose load, which is taken as the denominator (100%). The incretin effect was calculated by the formula: 100% x (AUC of OGTT – AUC IIGI)/ AUC of OGTT wherein AUC represents AUC of insulin or C-peptide29. The percentage of Gastrointestinal Glucose Disposal (GIGD) was calculated using the formula 100% x (OGTT glucose levels - IIGI glucose levels) / Glucose on OGTT30. Healthy control participants underwent OGTT and IIGI procedures in a similar manner.

Data analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 29.0). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or standard error of the mean (SEM), as appropriate. This approach was chosen to permit formal assessment of differences in incretin response to oral and intravenous glucose challenges in the FCPD group and is analogous to the analytical framework used by Lund et al.14. Serial glucose and hormone concentrations were analysed using generalised linear mixed-effects model to account for repeated measurements of C- peptide, glucagon, incretins, pancreatic polypeptide and oxyntomodulin within groups and between groups. The identity of the participants was included as a random effect, with fixed effects for group (FCPD group vs. control), the route of glucose administration (oral vs. Isoglycaemic intravenous), time points of the procedure, and their interaction terms, with the IIGI procedure considered as the reference category.

The Area-under-the-curve (AUC) and baseline-subtracted AUC (bsAUC) values were calculated using the trapezoidal method and the latter are presented as secondary summary measures to facilitate comparison with previous studies. The baseline subtracted AUC was calculated by subtracting the fasting value of the metabolite from the value of the metabolite at each time pint and then the sum of the AUC was calculated. Comparison between groups and comparisons of AUC-derived indices from OGTT and IIGI were performed using the independent-samples “t test or Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Between-group comparisons (FCPD vs controls) were performed using independent-samples t tests or Mann–Whitney U tests, as appropriate.

Results

The mean values of age and serum triglycerides were higher in the participants with FCPD when compared to the control group. The mean values of HDL cholesterol, LDL cholesterol and total cholesterol were higher in the FCPD group than control participants, though these findings were statistically not significant. There was no statistically significant difference between the two groups when compared for age, BMI and measures of body composition namely total fat mass, truncal fat mass and lean mass as shown in Table 1. The mean HbA1c level in the FCPD group was 8.4% (68 mmol/mol) and the median duration of diabetes was 9.2 years. None of the participants with FCPD had elevated CA 19.9 levels.

Plasma glucose excursions and C-peptide response

In the FCPD group, the mean plasma glucose levels were comparable during OGTT and IIGI [303 ± 15 mg/dL vs. 302 ± 15 mg/dL, p = NS], confirming effective replication of glycaemic exposure during intravenous glucose infusion. During IIGI, peak plasma glucose concentrations in the group with FCPD were attained later and at higher levels [371.7 ± 26.7 mg/dL at 120 min] as compared to the control participants [152.1 ± 12.1 mg/dL at 35 min], reflecting delayed glucose disposal in the FCPD group. To achieve glycaemic equivalence with OGTT, the total glucose infused during IIGI was higher in participants with FCPD when compared with the control participants [60.4 ± 26.4 vs. 38.0 ± 21.2, p = 0.11]. Despite comparable glucose exposure, C-peptide concentrations were consistently and profoundly suppressed at all time points in the group with FCPD group during both OGTT and IIGI (p < 0.01) when compared to the control participants (p < 0.01 for OGTT and p < 0.05 for IIGI comparisons) as presented in Fig. 1 and shown in Supplementary Table 1.

Moreover, a significantly negative incretin effect (− 8.25%) was observed in participants with FCPD when compared to control participants (44.8%). The total glucose infusion rate required to match glucose levels achieved during OGTT was much higher for all time points in the FCPD participants when compared to the control participants. Incretin secretory response

The Fasting GLP-1 concentrations were nearly two-fold higher in the participants with FCPD when compared with control participants during both OGTT and IIGI values (p = NS). GLP-1 levels during OGTT increased substantially in the participants with FCPD while GLP-1 response was blunted during IIGI. The stimulated GLP-1 concentrations during OGTT in participants with FCPD were more than four-fold higher than those observed in control participants (p < 0.01), indicating preserved and exaggerated enteroendocrine responsiveness despite severe pancreatic endocrine dysfunction as shown in Fig. 2. In contrast, GIP secretion was attenuated among participants with FCPD. Baseline GIP concentrations were significantly lower in the participants with FCPD than in control participants [p < 0.05]. Furthermore, unlike control participants, the participants with FCPD failed to demonstrate a robust post-OGTT rise in GIP levels as demonstrated in Fig. 2.

Oxyntomodulin and pancreatic polypeptide secretory response

The Oxyntomodulin (OXM) concentrations were similarly elevated by nearly two-fold in participants with FCPD at baseline compared to control participants. OXM concentrations demonstrated route-dependent variation, with attenuation of OXM responses during IIGI compared with OGTT in participants with FCPD. Mixed-model analysis suggested differences in serial OXM responses between the two groups, although overall AUC-derived summaries were comparable between groups. Pancreatic polypeptide (PP) concentrations were markedly blunted in the FCPD group at baseline as well as following OGTT and were numerically lower during IIGI than those observed in control participants across all conditions, as shown in Supplementary Tables 1 and illustrated in Fig. 3. consistent with severe pancreatic endocrine failure.

Discussion

In this study, we have demonstrated that participants with FCPD exhibit persistent fasting and postprandial hyperglucagonemia despite significant pancreatic endocrine failure and a distinctive pattern of incretin dysregulation. Fasting plasma glucagon concentrations were significantly higher in participants with FCPD when compared with the healthy control participants , and the glucagon levels remained quantitatively greater following oral glucose administration than during IIGI. These findings occurred alongside severely blunted C-peptide and pancreatic polypeptide responses, indicating near-complete loss of endogenous pancreatic β-cell and F-cell function.

Our observations are consistent with, and extend the findings in previous reports of preserved or enhanced glucagon secretion in the FCPD group despite extensive pancreatic fibrosis. We previously demonstrated elevated fasting and postprandial glucagon levels during a mixed-meal challenge in the FCPD group, with postprandial glucagon correlating positively with plasma glucose, suggesting that hyperglycaemia may contribute to glucagon excess in this condition (6). Mohan et al. similarly reported preserved glucagon secretion during an extended OGTT in insulin-naïve FCPD despite markedly reduced insulin secretion, while Yagnik et al. described comparable fasting glucagon levels between participants with FCPD and non-insulin-dependent diabetes despite profound β-cell dysfunction (5,7). Collectively, these studies indicate that glucagon secretion is relatively preserved in FCPD even in the face of severe pancreatic atrophy, challenging traditional expectations of parallel alpha- and beta-cell failure.

The present study adds a novel dimension by demonstrating that glucagon exposure is greater following oral glucose challenge than an isoglycemia intravenous glucose infusion in participants with FCPD, despite matched glycaemic excursions. This pattern resembles findings reported by Lund et al. in individuals with total pancreatectomy, in whom oral glucose paradoxically increased glucagon secretion14, whereas glucagon was suppressed in individuals with an intact pancreas (8). While the FCPD group in our study did not undergo pancreatectomy, the presence of gross pancreatic atrophy on imaging, together with markedly suppressed C-peptide and pancreatic polypeptide responses, suggests that classical pancreatic regulation of glucagon may be substantially impaired in theFCPD group.

Beyond glucagon, we observed a distinct incretin phenotype in the FCPD group characterised by exaggerated L-cell–derived hormone secretion and attenuated K-cell responses. GLP-1 responses to oral glucose were markedly exaggerated in participants with FCPD, with stimulated levels being several-fold higher than those observed during intravenous glucose and in healthy control group. Oxyntomodulin secretion response demonstrated a route-dependent variation on Generalised Linear mixed-model analysis, with relatively higher secretory responses observed during OGTT when compared to IIGI in participants with FCPD. In contrast, GIP secretion was significantly reduced at baseline and following oral glucose, with an absence of a robust postprandial rise. This divergent incretin response—enhanced L-cell activity with suppressed K-cell output differs from patterns typically described in type 1 or type 2 diabetes and appears to be a distinctive feature of FCPD.

Similar incretin secretory abnormalities have been reported previously in the FCPD group, with preserved or enhanced GLP-1 secretion but reduced GIP responses (16). Several mechanisms may underlie this dissociation. Malabsorption and altered gastric emptying, commonly seen in participants with FCPD may impair proximal nutrient delivery while disproportionately stimulating distal L-cells (17). In addition, GLP-1 secretion has been shown to remain preserved in chronic pancreatitis and pancreatectomised states and may improve with pancreatic enzyme supplementation (18,19). Other contributing factors may include altered prohormone processing and feedback inhibition of GIP secretion in states of hyperglucagonemia, given the presence of glucagon and GIP receptors on enteroendocrine and pancreatic cells (8,20).

Importantly, the quantitative elevation of glucagon in the FCPD group occurred alongside exaggerated secretion of L-cell–derived peptides and near-absence of pancreatic peptides, a combination that runs counter to traditional concepts of FCPD. The functional relevance of this glucagon excess is supported by our earlier observations in participants with FCPD demonstrating preserved hypoglycaemia awareness and greater glycaemic variability in a subset of patients with higher glucagon levels. Together, these findings suggest that hyperglucagonemia in the FCPD group is biologically meaningful and warrants further investigation.

Given that the pre-proglucagon gene is expressed in both pancreatic alpha-cells and intestinal L-cells, and that L-cells possess the enzymatic machinery to generate glucagon, GLP-1, and oxyntomodulin (23,24), the observed pattern of exaggerated L-cell hormone secretion coupled with elevated glucagon—particularly following oral glucose challenge raises the possibility of non-pancreatic contributions, potentially from the gut, to circulating glucagon in FCPD.

Some limitations in this study should be emphasized. The present study does not permit definitive gut localisation of glucagon origin, and pancreatic contributions cannot be entirely excluded despite extensive pancreatic atrophy. We have not performed intestinal biopsies and immunostaining of the intestinal tissue for Glucagon, GLP-1 and GIP receptors. It would possibly be required to go a step further and perform Liquid chromatography-high-resolution mass spectrometry (LCMS) and transcriptomics to study the capacity of the intestine to synthesize glucagon and other peptides in the FCPD group. Nevertheless, the study merits from the meticulous a design, systematiccontrol of glucotoxicity and meticulous dynamic procedures done in the FCPD group and the controls. The data obtained in this study will provide insights for further studies on the extra pancreatic source of glucagon in individuals with FCPD.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the group with FCPD is characterised by severe pancreatic endocrine failure accompanied by persistent hyperglucagonemia and a distinctive pattern of incretin dysregulation. Participants with FCPD exhibit altered L-cell–derived hormone responses, including augmented GLP-1 secretion, route-dependent variation in oxyntomodulin responses, alongside attenuated GIP responses and profoundly suppressed insulin, C-peptide, and pancreatic polypeptide secretion. The observation of greater glucagon exposure following an oral glucose challenge compared with an intravenous glucose challenge, in the group with FCPD, challenges the traditional paradigms of pancreatic diabetes and suggests that glucagon regulation in the group with FCPD may involve mechanisms beyond the pancreatic alpha-cell. However, although the results reported are worthy to support a hypothesis, they are not confirmatory. Furthermore, studies employing more specific analytical techniques such as histopathology and immunostaining of intestinal and pancreatic biopsy specimens and proteomics level assessments of pancreatic and intestinal homogenates are required to clarify the sources and regulatory pathways contributing to glucagon excess in this unique form of diabetes.
