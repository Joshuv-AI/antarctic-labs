METTL7A is a key regulator of hepatic lipid metabolism and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease progression
Authors: Fengsui Chen, Lijia Yao, Ling Yang, Xinbiao Li, Junwei Zhou, Dongliang Li, Zhixian Wu, Pin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Methyltransferase-like protein 7A (METTL7A), an m6A RNA methyltransferase and thiol methyltransferase enriched in hepatocytes, has been implicated in cancer biology, yet its role in hepatic lipid metabolism and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (MASLD/NAFLD) remains unknown. Hepatic steatosis was induced in HepG2 and Huh7 cells using oleic acid (OA). The role of METTL7A was examined through shRNA-mediated knockdown, RNA sequencing, lipid metabolic assays, and rescue experiments. Protein interactions were assessed via mass spectrometry and co-immunoprecipitation. An AAV-shMETTL7A mouse MASLD/NAFLD model was used to validate in vivo relevance. METTL7A expression was significantly upregulated in OA-induced steatotic hepatocytes. METTL7A silencing mitigated lipid accumulation, improved cell viability, reduced LDL-C, TC, and TG levels, and restored HDL-C. Transcriptomic profiling identified PLIN5 as a key downstream target reduced upon METTL7A depletion. Mechanistically, METTL7A knockdown inhibited PI3K/AKT activation and suppressed PLIN5 expression. Mass spectrometry revealed SAPCD2 as a METTL7A-interacting protein, and SAPCD2 overexpression restored lipid accumulation, PI3K/AKT activation, and PLIN5 levels in METTL7A-deficient cells. In vivo, AAV-mediated METTL7A knockdown alleviated hepatic steatosis, normalized serum lipid profiles and liver injury markers, and reduced SAPCD2, PLIN5, FAS, and ACC while increasing HSL expression. METTL7A is a previously unrecognized regulator of hepatic lipid homeostasis that promotes steatosis by sustaining SAPCD2 expression and activating the PI3K/AKT pathway and PLIN5 expression. Targeting METTL7A may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for MASLD/NAFLD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-56210-4.

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), formerly named nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) refers to a wide range of liver conditions marked by abnormal fat deposition in hepatocytes among individuals who drink little or no alcohol1. It has become one of the most prevalent chronic liver disorders worldwide, strongly associated with metabolic dysfunction and affecting roughly 30% of the global population2. MASLD/NAFLD is generally classified into two forms: metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver/nonalcoholic fatty liver (MAFL/NAFL) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis/nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (MASH/NASH)3. MAFL/NAFL involves simple steatosis without notable inflammation or fibrosis and is often viewed as relatively mild, though progression can occur. In contrast, MASH/NASH features steatosis accompanied by inflammation and hepatocellular damage, which may advance to fibrosis, cirrhosis, or even hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)3,4.

Key contributors to MASLD/NAFLD include obesity, type 2 diabetes, insulin resistance, hypertension, dyslipidemia, genetic susceptibility, and unhealthy lifestyle habits5,6. At present, few medications have been approved in the United States specifically for MASLD/NAFLD or MASH/NASH therapy5,7. Current management focuses on reducing excess weight through lifestyle modification, pharmacologic therapy, or bariatric and endoscopic procedures, and correcting metabolic abnormalities8,9. Therefore, a deeper understanding of the molecular pathways driving hepatic lipid accumulation is urgently needed for novel therapy development.

Hepatic steatosis develops when lipid acquisition exceeds lipid disposal—that is, when fatty acid uptake and de novo lipogenesis outpace fatty acid oxidation and lipid export. In MASLD/NAFLD, both hepatic fatty acid uptake and lipogenesis are elevated, whereas the compensatory increase in fatty acid oxidation is insufficient to normalize lipid levels10. Several molecular pathways have been implicated in regulating hepatic lipid deposition11. For instance, the PI3K/AKT pathway plays a vital role in regulating hepatic glucose and lipid metabolism12. Additionally, key enzymes such as acetyl-CoA carboxylase (ACC) and fatty acid synthase (FAS) drive de novo lipogenesis in hepatocytes11, while hormone-sensitive lipase (HSL) facilitates lipolysis and mobilizes triglycerides in the liver13. Despite these insights, the mechanisms governing hepatic lipid metabolism remain incompletely defined.

Methyltransferase-like protein 7A (METTL7A), also known as TMT1A, is an m6A RNA methyltransferase implicated in cancer prognosis and therapeutic resistance14–17. It is a member of the METTL7 family, which also includes METTL7B18. Although METTL7A is abundantly expressed in hepatocytes19, its function in hepatic lipid metabolism and its potential contribution to MASLD/NAFLD pathogenesis have not yet been investigated.

In this study, we first established an MASLD/NAFLD cellular model by treating HepG2 and Huh7 cells with oleic acid (OA), which led to increased METTL7A expression. We then depleted METTL7A in OA-treated HCC cells and evaluated their viability and lipid metabolism-related parameters. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of control and METTL7A-deficient HepG2 cells exposed to OA identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs), among which PLIN5, a key regulator of MASLD/NAFLD and lipid metabolism20, was significantly downregulated. Screening for PLIN5-interacting proteins revealed SAPCD2 as a potential mediator of PLIN5 function in OA-treated hepatocytes. Consistently, knockdown of SAPCD2 led to the downregulation of both PLIN5 and PI3K. Overexpression of SAPCD2 in METTL7A-deficient cells decreased cell viability and promoted lipid synthesis. Finally, using an MASLD/NAFLD mouse model, we confirmed the role of METTL7A in regulating hepatic lipid metabolism.

Materials and methods

Cell culture

HEK293T cells were cultured in 90% DMEM (IMC-305) with 10% FBS (Gibco, A5670801) and penicillin–streptomycin (IMMOCELL, 15,070,063). HepG2 cells (IM-H038) were maintained in the specified medium (IM-H038-1). Huh7 cells (IM-H040) were kept in specialized medium (IM-H040-1). All these cell lines and culture media were sourced from IMMOCELL. Cell culture was maintained in a designated culture incubator (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 3311E) provided with 5% CO₂ at 37 °C.

Cell viability assay

To assay cell viability, 0.2 mL full medium containing 1 × 104 HepG2 or Huh7 cells was inoculated into each well of 96-well plates. Following 24 h of culture, 20 µL of MTT solution (5 mg/mL, Beyotime, ST316) was added to each well. Four hours later, the liquid was discarded, and 0.15 mL DMSO was applied to each well. The crystal was then dissolved by continuous shaking for 10 min. OD490 values of the plate were evaluated using a Thermo Fisher Scientific microplate reader (A51119500C).

A non-specific shRNA (shNC) was constructed to function as a control. Western blot

Cells or liver tissues were washed with PBS and then lysed using RIPA buffer with vortexing and sonication on ice. The resulting lysates were then centrifuged, and the supernatant was transferred into fresh tubes. The protein concentration was measured with a BCA kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 23,227). A 20 μg aliquot of each denatured sample was separated by 10% SDS–PAGE and transferred onto activated PVDF membranes. Next, the membranes were incubated with 5% nonfat milk for 1 h to minimize background. After incubating with primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight, the membranes were washed and then kept in secondary antibody solution for 1 h. Following another round of washing, the membranes were incubated with enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) and then exposed to X-ray film to visualize the bands. ImageJ was used for band grayscale value quantification. The antibodies used for Western blotting are listed below: METTL7A (ImmunoWay, YN8429, 1:500), PI3K (Proteintech, 20,584–1-AP, 1:500), P-PI3K (Cell Signaling Technology, #17,366, 1:500), AKT (Proteintech, 60,203–2-Ig, 1:500), P-AKT (Proteintech, 80,455–1-RR, 1:500), PLIN5 (Proteintech, 26,951–1-AP, 1:500), SAPCD2 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, PA5-60,632, 1:500), HA-tag (Proteintech, 51,064–2-AP, 1:500), Flag-tag (Proteintech, 20,543–1-AP, 1:1000), β-actin (Proteintech, 20,536–1-AP, 1:500), HRP-Anti-Rabbit IgG (Proteintech, SA00001-2, 1:10,000), HRP-Anti-Mouse IgG (Proteintech, SA00001-1, 1:10,000).

RNA-seq and data analysis

Total RNA was extracted and purified from OA-treated HepG2 cells (shNC or shMETTL7A) using the method described above. For library preparation, 1 µg of RNA per sample was processed with the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (New England Biolabs) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA fragment selection and purification were performed using the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter), and library quality was evaluated with the Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. Clustering of index-coded libraries was carried out on the cBot Cluster Generation System using the TruSeq PE Cluster Kit v3-cBot-HS (Illumina). Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Three independent biological replicates were performed and analyzed for each experimental condition.

For data processing, raw reads containing adapter sequences or low-quality bases were removed to obtain high-quality clean reads. Filtering was conducted using fastp software (v0.23.4) with a Phred quality threshold of ≥ 20. Reads were first aligned to an rRNA database using Bowtie2 (version 2.2.8), and rRNA-aligned reads were excluded. The remaining clean reads were used for transcript assembly and gene expression quantification. A reference genome index was constructed, and paired-end clean reads were aligned to the reference genome (GRCh38) using HISAT2 (version 2.4) with default parameters. Mapped reads were assembled using StringTie (version 1.3.1) in a reference-guided mode, and raw expression counts were enumerated using featureCounts (v2.0.2). Transcript abundance was quantified as TPM using RSEM. Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed with the R package gmodels (version 2.19.1). Differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 R package (version 1.42.1), and genes or transcripts with an absolute log2 fold change > 1 ( |log2(FC)|> 1) and an adjusted P-value (padj < 0.05) were considered differentially expressed. Functional enrichment analyses were carried out using clusterProfiler (version 4.10.1).

Measurement of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), Total cholesterol (TC), Triglycerides (TG), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT)

The levels of HDL-C, LDL-C, TC, TG, AST, and ALT were measured using commercial assay kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions: HDL-C and LDL-C (Beyotime, S0213S), TC (Yeasen, 60723ES60), TG (Beyotime, S0219M), AST (Solarbio, BC1565), and ALT (Solarbio, BC1555). Absorbance was recorded using a Thermo Fisher Scientific microplate reader (A51119500C).

Oil Red O staining

Oil Red O staining was used to assess lipid droplet accumulation in liver cells. Cells or tissues were first fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and washed with PBS. Next, the fixed samples were treated with 0.5% Oil Red O solution prepared in isopropanol (Beyotime, C0157S) for 5 min at 37 °C in the dark. Finally, stained samples were then examined and imaged using a BX51 inverted OLYMPUS microscope equipped with a digital camera.

Mass spectrometry (MS)

Protein bands from control HepG2 cells and those overexpressing Flag-METTL7A were cut from Coomassie-stained gels, destained, and subjected to in-gel digestion. Proteins were reduced, alkylated, and digested with trypsin (enzyme-to-protein ratio 1:50) at 37 °C for approximately 20 h. The resulting peptides were desalted, dried, and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid (FA) before storage at − 20 °C. Peptide separation was carried out by nanoHPLC on a nanoElute system (Bruker) at 300 nL/min using a 25 cm × 75 μm, 1.9 μm C18 column (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mobile phase A contained 0.1% FA in water, and mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% FA in 99.9% acetonitrile. The gradient program was: 0–18 min, 5–35% B (linear); 18–20 min, 35–80% B (linear); 20–30 min, 80% B (isocratic). MS was performed on a timsTOF Pro in positive ion mode (1.5 kV spray voltage) with a scan range of 100–1700 m/z. Data were collected in PASEF mode (eight MS/MS scans per cycle) with a cycle time of 0.95 s, charge states 0–5, and a dynamic exclusion of 24 s. Raw files (.d format) were analyzed using MaxQuant (v1.6.14) for protein identification against the appropriate databases.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Cells were rinsed with ice-cold PBS and then lysed in pre-chilled RIPA buffer. The resulting lysates were scraped and transferred to centrifuge tubes. After continuous shaking on a platform at 4 °C for 20 min, the lysates were centrifuged. The supernatant was then collected as total cell lysate (TCL). For IP assays, the samples were mixed with Protein G Dynabeads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 10003D) pre-treated with either anti-HA (Proteintech, 66,006–2-Ig) or anti-FLAG (Proteintech, 66,008–4-Ig) antibody at 4 °C for 2 h. After three rounds of washing with lysis buffer, the beads were mixed with elution buffer for a few minutes, and the eluted proteins were collected, denatured, and analyzed via Western blotting.

Establishment of the MASLD/NAFLD mouse model

C57BL/6 J female mice21,22 (6 weeks old) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of the Basic Laboratory of the 900th Hospital. After one week of acclimation, the mice were randomly assigned to four groups using a computer-generated random number sequence, including Control, Model, Model + shNC, and Model + shMETTL7A, with six mice per group. Mice in the Control and Model groups received an intravenous injection of 0.1 mL sterile saline, while mice in the Model + shNC and Model + shMETTL7A groups were injected with 0.1 mL rAAV2/8 (5 × 1011 vg, OBiO) carrying shNC or shMETTL7A under the control of a U6 promoter via the tail vein. The rAAV2/8 vector was selected to ensure high-efficiency transduction specifically targeting liver tissues. One week later, mice in the Control group were fed a standard chow diet, whereas those in the remaining groups were given a high-fat diet (HFD, Research Diets, D12492) for two months. At the end of the experiments, the mice were fasted for 12 h before being anesthetized with 5% isoflurane for induction and 1% isoflurane for maintenance. Orbital blood was collected for serum biochemical analysis. The mice were then euthanized, and liver tissues were harvested for imaging. Subsequently, half of each liver sample was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological analysis, while the remaining tissue was snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen for RNA and protein extraction. To ensure unbiased evaluation, sample processing, histological examinations, and lipid quantification were performed by investigators blinded to the group allocations. All C57BL/6 J female mice were euthanized at approximately 4 months of age via cervical dislocation. At the time of sacrifice, the average body weight of the mice was approximately 25–28 g. The study adheres to the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, Refinement) principles to minimize animal suffering. All animal experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of 900th Hospital of PLA Joint Logistic Support Force (Code of Ethics: 2024–32).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism (version 9) was utilized for data analysis and plotting. Data are shown as mean ± standard deviation (SD). In the bar-dot plots, each data point indicates values from a biological replicate, and values for MTT and qPCR assays reflect the mean of technical triplicates. The specific statistical tests applied for each quantification are described in the corresponding figure legends. A P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

METTL7A expression is upregulated in an MASLD/NAFLD cellular model

To investigate the potential role of METTL7A in MASLD/NAFLD, we first optimized the in vitro steatosis model conditions using a concentration gradient of OA. HepG2 and Huh7 cells were treated with various concentrations of OA for 24 h, and cell viability was evaluated using MTT assays. Based on the cell viability results and the efficiency of intracellular lipid deposition assessed by Oil Red O staining, optimal and non-toxic concentrations of 1 mM for HepG2 cells and 200 μM for Huh7 cells were selected for subsequent experiments, which align with widely used protocols to induce lipid accumulation23,24. Huh7 cells exhibited cytotoxicity above 200 μM, whereas HepG2 cells maintained high viability up to 1 mM. Both cell lines displayed marked intracellular lipid deposition as detected by Oil Red O staining (Fig. 1A, B). qPCR and Western blotting revealed significant increases in METTL7A mRNA and protein levels in OA-treated HCC cells compared with controls (Fig. 1C, D). METTL7A knockdown mitigates lipid accumulation in OA-treated HCC cells

To define METTL7A’s function in hepatic lipid metabolism, we generated three METTL7A-targeting shRNAs. qPCR analysis in HEK293T cells showed effective knockdown by all constructs, with shMETTL7A-1 achieving the greatest suppression (Fig. 2A); thus, it was selected for further experiments. In both HepG2 and Huh7 cells, METTL7A depletion markedly reduced OA-induced lipid accumulation as shown by Oil Red O staining (Fig. 2B). MTT assays showed that METTL7A silencing partially rescued the OA-induced decrease in cell viability (Fig. 2C). Consistent with reduced lipid accumulation, METTL7A knockdown significantly lowered LDL-C, TG, and TC levels while increasing HDL-C in OA-treated cells (Fig. 2D). At the molecular scale, METTL7A depletion was confirmed by qPCR and Western blotting (Fig. 3A, B). Moreover, METTL7A deficiency downregulated ACC and FAS expression while upregulating HSL in OA-treated hepatocytes (Fig. 3B). Depletion of METTL7A causes reduced expression of PLIN5 and blocks the activation of the PI3K/AKT pathway

To delineate the mechanism by which METTL7A promotes lipid deposition in OA-treated HCC cells, we performed RNA-seq using control and METTL7A-deficient HepG2 cells exposed to OA for 24 h. Among the DEGs, PLIN5 attracted our attention due to its known function in hepatic lipid metabolism. qPCR and Western blotting validated the RNA-seq data, showing that PLIN5 expression was greatly decreased in OA-treated METTL7A-deficient HCC cells. Notably, PLIN5 expression was also strongly induced by OA treatment, similar to METTL7A (Fig. 4A and B). We also investigated the activation of PI3K/AKT pathway, a key player in hepatic lipid metabolism. Western blotting indicated that the phosphorylation of PI3K and AKT (P-PI3K and P-AKT), which represents activation of the pathway, was enhanced by OA in both cell lines; however, this effect was nullified by METTL7A depletion (Fig. 4C). Discussion

In this study, we identify METTL7A as a previously unrecognized regulator of hepatic lipid metabolism and MASLD/NAFLD progression. Although METTL7A is known as an m6A RNA methyltransferase implicated in cancer biology and also functions as a thiol methyltransferase in the liver, playing an essential role in drug metabolism, its metabolic functions in hepatocytes have remained unclear. Our findings demonstrate that METTL7A expression is markedly elevated during hepatocellular lipid loading and that METTL7A actively drives steatosis both in vitro and in vivo. These results broaden our understanding of METTL7A’s role in liver physiology.

The mechanism by which lipid accumulation induces METTL7A overexpression in hepatocytes remains unknown. Previous studies show that METTL7A mRNA expression is regulated by several noncoding RNAs, including miR-6807-5p, LINC01123, miR-654-5p, and miR-27a, across different biological contexts27–29. Notably, these noncoding RNAs also modulate hepatic lipid metabolism and MASLD/NAFLD30,31, raising the possibility that they may regulate steatosis by targeting METTL7A. Additionally, METTL7A transcription can be influenced by DNA methylation32, but whether this mechanism contributes to its increased expression under steatotic conditions remains to be clarified. Importantly, validation of METTL7A upregulation in liver tissues from MASLD/NAFLD patients will strengthen its clinical relevance.

To elucidate the mechanism underlying METTL7A’s lipogenic function in hepatocytes, we performed transcriptomic analyses and identified PLIN5 as a key candidate. PLIN5 is a central regulator of intracellular lipid metabolism, essential for lipid storage and oxidation33–35. Consistent with METTL7A’s pro-lipogenic role, PLIN5 knockout mice exhibit impaired lipid accumulation in the heart and skeletal muscle and show resistance to MASLD/NAFLD and HCC, whereas PLIN5 overexpression induces cardiac steatosis20,35. In our study, METTL7A silencing markedly reduced PLIN5 expression and intracellular lipid accumulation, suggesting that PLIN5 may serve as a critical mediator of METTL7A function. Nevertheless, functional rescue experiments are needed to confirm this hypothesis.

Given that PLIN5 transcription is sensitive to upstream signaling, we examined the PI3K/AKT pathway, a canonical regulator of hepatic lipogenesis that promotes PLIN5 expression in OA-treated HepG2 cells36. OA stimulation activated PI3K/AKT signaling, whereas METTL7A knockdown abolished this activation, indicating that METTL7A acts upstream of PI3K/AKT and PLIN5 to promote lipid deposition.

To further investigate how METTL7A influences PI3K/AKT signaling, we conducted MS-based interactome profiling and identified SAPCD2 as a METTL7A-interacting protein. SAPCD2 is known to enhance PI3K/AKT pathway activity and has been implicated in metabolic reprogramming in cancer37. In this study, we demonstrate that METTL7A not only preserves SAPCD2 expression but also physically interacts with it in hepatocytes. Crucially, SAPCD2 overexpression in METTL7A-deficient cells restored lipid accumulation, reversed changes in lipogenic and lipolytic gene expression, reactivated PI3K/AKT signaling, and increased PLIN5 levels. These findings highlight SAPCD2 as a central mediator of METTL7A-driven lipogenesis. However, the biological significance of the METTL7A–SAPCD2 interaction remains unclear. Since SAPCD2 is predicted to interact with PLIN5, it will be important to determine whether METTL7A, SAPCD2, and PLIN5 form a functional complex in hepatocytes. Additionally, although METTL7A is recognized as a dual methyltransferase, our current data primarily highlight its physical interaction with SAPCD2 and its necessity for maintaining the SAPCD2/PI3K/AKT/PLIN5 axis. Whether this regulation is strictly dependent on METTL7A’s enzymatic activity or involves a scaffolding function remains an open question. Future work involving the characterization of catalytic-dead METTL7A mutants or m6A-specific assays will be required to fully dissect these biochemical nuances. Furthermore, while our present findings establish METTL7A as a critical regulator of early-stage hepatic steatosis, its precise involvement in advanced MASH progression and immune cell infiltration warrants further systemic metabolic profiling in future studies.

Conclusion

Collectively, our findings identify METTL7A as a novel regulator of hepatic lipid homeostasis. Mechanistically, METTL7A interacts with SAPCD2 to sustain its expression, thereby activating the PI3K/AKT pathway and enhancing PLIN5-mediated lipid storage. These results suggest that METTL7A may represent an attractive therapeutic target for MASLD/NAFLD. Future studies are needed to determine whether targeting METTL7A confers therapeutic benefits in more advanced disease stages, including MASH/NASH and fibrosis.
