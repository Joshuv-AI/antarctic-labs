Parental speech and gesture scaffolding moderate the neural dynamics of parent-child synchrony
Authors: Ying Li, Ö Ece Demir-Lira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParent–child interactions foster children’s neurocognitive development through multiple pathways. A key pathway involves parental scaffolding, in which parents use speech, gesture, or their combination multimodally to guide children’s problem solving and learning. However, the neural processes underlying different scaffolding modalities remain largely unknown. In the present study, we examined the neural basis for the role of parental scaffolding during a parent–child cooperative spatial problem-solving task. We characterized three different types of scaffolds parents used, specifically speech, gesture, and multimodal (speech + gesture). Using an fNIRS-based hyperscanning, we simultaneously recorded neural activity from both members of the dyad. The results revealed parental scaffolding modalities were differentially related to the temporal and spatial structure of neural synchrony. Temporally, multimodal scaffolding (speech combined with gesture) was associated with the most sustained neural synchrony across the interaction, showing the flattest trajectory and the highest level of synchrony, particularly in the later stages. Speech-only scaffolding was associated with an attenuated decline , following a pronounced U-shape with strong late-interaction recovery from a lower baseline. Gesture alone was not reliably associated with attenuation of the decline observed under no scaffolding. Spatially, exploratory channel-based analyses indicated that channel pairs coupling child right dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and parental temporo-parietal areas showed greater synchronization under multimodal scaffolding than other areas. These findings characterize distinct temporal and spatial neural correlates of parental scaffolding modalities. Overall, this study advances our understanding of how parental scaffolding relates to the neural dynamics that support children’s engagement during naturalistic cooperative interactions.Keywords: Parent-child interactions: gesture, Scaffolding, Multimodal communication, FNIRS, Hyperscanning

Introduction

A rich body of work in developmental cognitive neuroscience yielded significant and fundamental insights into how environmental factors shape developmental trajectories (Farah, 2017, Hackman and Farah, 2009, Tooley et al., 2021). Grounded in Vygotsky’s socio-cultural theory (Vygotsky, 1978), parent-child interactions form the cornerstone of children’s development, contributing to multiple aspects of children’s development, including social-emotional, verbal, and spatial development (Feldman, 2007, Hirsh-Pasek et al., 2015, Huttenlocher et al., 2002). A growing body of work reveals how such proximal parent-child interactions accumulate to transform developmental trajectories, showing that specific features of parent–child interactions predict cognitive outcomes more than others, even after accounting for broader environmental factors, such as socioeconomic status (Demir-Lira et al., 2019a, Glenn et al., 2018, Huttenlocher et al., 2002, Uccelli et al., 2019). One central mechanism through which parents support development is scaffolding, defined as the process by which an expert supports a novice partner’s performance, specifically by increasing or decreasing the level of assistance (Wood et al., 1976). Prior work primarily focused on the unidirectional effects of the scaffolding cues and less is known about the role of scaffolding during dynamic interaction and the neural underpinnings of in situ processing of different scaffolding cues. Here, we ask how unimodal (speech) and multimodal (speech and gesture) parental scaffolding relate to the interpersonal neural dynamics of the parent-child interaction during a spatial problem-solving task.

Parental scaffolding through speech and gesture. Within parent-child interactions, parents play an active role in structuring tasks, directing attention, and providing support tailored to the child’s needs. Especially in learning contexts, parents scaffold their children’s learning through both verbal and nonverbal means, most notably via speech and hand gestures. Traditionally, research has largely emphasized the verbal channel, i.e., speech-based scaffolding. For example, both the quantity and quality of parental speech are strong predictors of children’s developmental outcomes (e.g., Rowe and Snow, 2020; Huttenlocher et al., 2002; Pruden et al., 2011). However, communication is inherently multimodal. Speech is frequently embedded in a broader multimodal context in communication more generally and in communication during learning more specifically (Goldin-Meadow, 2009, Holler and Levinson, 2019). Although research on parental scaffolding has primarily focused on unimodal speech, hand gestures represent another key modality through which parents communicate and support their children. Parents frequently convey information through hand gestures accompanying their speech, creating multimodal input that integrates both speech and gesture.

Prior literature suggests that gesture facilitates learning via several distinct mechanisms and it influences both the speakers and the listeners’ cognitive processes. Gestures can direct joint attention, anchoring shared reference between interlocutors. Gesture can also complement information provided in speech by reinforcing or disambiguating verbal content. Specifically, gestures help activate, maintain and package visuo-spatial motoric information (Kita et al., 2017). Thus, gesture might be particularly good at providing spatial information and highlighting spatial information encoded in speech. These functions are considered to lead to faster and more efficient processing, enhance semantic processing and promote integrated understanding contributing to common ground (Goldin-Meadow, 2009, Deák et al., 2000, Holler and Levinson, 2019, Wakefield et al., 2018).

Previous behavioral research has confirmed that multimodal input scaffolds children's learning and is positively associated with their development across multiple domains (Cartmill et al., 2010, Özçalışkan and Dimitrova, 2013, Rowe and Goldin-Meadow, 2009). In the context of language development, parents’ use of multimodal communication has been shown to predict children’s vocabulary growth (e.g., Rowe, Özçalışkan, and Goldin-Meadow, 2008; Iverson et al., 1999). In the spatial domain, the focus of the current study, emerging work focused on the role of domain-specific gestures. This work showed that parental gestures that communicate spatial information (e.g. gestures during puzzle-solving tasks) are positively associated with children’s own spatial gestures and language use during the interaction as well as better spatial performance on independent tasks (Clingan-Siverly et al., 2021, Cartmill et al., 2010). Notably, the predictive power of parental gesture remains significant even after accounting for parental speech (Rowe and Goldin-Meadow, 2009). Overall, multimodal parental input, comprising both speech and gesture, serves as a crucial scaffold for children’s learning and development in general and for their spatial learning and development in particular.

We situate the current study in the domain of spatial problem solving due to multiple reasons. Spatial thinking is defined as the mental processes involved in representing, analyzing and drawing inferences from spatial relations. Spatial thinking can involve thinking about relations within objects or between objects and based on perceived relations or imaged relations (Uttal et al., 2013). First, spatial tasks provide a particularly informative context for studying multimodal scaffolding. This is because gesture may be uniquely suited to convey spatial information that speech handles less efficiently. In such context, gesture better reveals continuous nature of spatial information, or shape, orientation and relative position of objects in a way speech cannot, for example a parent pointing the entirety of one side of puzzle piece to cue the meaning of the word “edge”. Second, a focus on spatial skills is also crucial because, early differences in spatial ability predict later academic and occupational outcomes, including mathematical achievement and entry into Science, Technology, Engineering, and Mathematics (STEM) fields (e.g., Casey et al., 1997; Benbow et al., 2000; Shea et al., 2001), even after controlling for verbal and mathematical skills (Wai et al., 2009, Uttal et al., 2013).

Neural mechanisms that underlie scaffolding. Despite this growing and converging behavioral evidence for scaffolding, some fundamental questions regarding the mechanisms by which each scaffolding modality plays a role in parent-child communication remain unanswered. Does the advantage of multimodal over unimodal scaffolding reflect differing neural processes (spatially and temporally), or does multimodal input simply provide more information through the similar neural pathway? Existing neuroimaging evidence offers relevant but incomplete information. Existing neuroimaging evidence of gesture comprehension in single participants suggests that multimodal (speech-gesture) input engages a broad neural network, including bilateral fronto-temporal regions, more robustly than unimodal (speech-only) input (Straube et al., 2012, Cacciante et al., 2024). However, most existing neuroimaging studies focuses on unidirectional effects of communicative cues, such as gestures, on the listener. Specifically, they focus on brain activation during prerecorded stimuli, rather than interacting partners engaged in dynamic communication (Holler and Levinson, 2019). For example, Demir-Lira et al., (2018) demonstrated that children exhibit a widespread pattern of neural activation, the inferior frontal gyrus area and the posterior middle temporal gyrus area, during gesture-speech integration tasks, similar but more extended than the patterns observed in adults. A shift in focus is needed from studying isolated individuals to investigating how multimodal input organizes bidirectional neural synchrony during live interactions, especially in parent-child dyads. This shift also necessitates focusing on neural systems that underlie dyadic communication and problem solving, such as dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and temporo-parietal junction (Czeszumski et al., 2022, Liu et al., 2024). This perspective aligns with long-standing socialization theories that conceptualize the dyad as a single, integrated system with emergent properties that cannot be reduced to either individual alone (Kochanska and Aksan, 2004).

Overall, the existing literature has multiple gaps. First, the bidirectional role of unimodal and multimodal scaffolding cues remains unclear. Specifically, how similar or different are in situ processing of different scaffolding modalities during dynamic interactions remains unknown. As a result, although prior work characterized areas involved in individual processing of multimodal scaffolding, little is known about how multimodal communication relates to systems that underlie dynamic communication, such as dorsolateral prefrontal cortex or temporo-parietal areas (Czeszumski et al., 2022, Liu et al., 2024). Second, it is not clear whether the roles of different scaffolding modalities in terms of their neural processes are stable across the course of the interaction or whether they fluctuate as interaction unfolds. Finally, the existing neuroimaging research is also primarily on adults, although evidence suggests that gestures might play a different and possibly more beneficial role for children, particularly because children are more likely to be in a transitional stage of learning (e.g. Church and Goldin-Meadow, 1986, Hostetter and Skirving, 2011).

To address these gaps, the literature could benefit from leveraging hyperscanning, a dual-brain neuroimaging technique that simultaneously records neural activity from both members of a dyad during live interaction. A growing body of research identifies neural synchrony, also referred to as the temporal coupling, alignment, coordination, or entrainment of neural activity between interacting partners, as a key neural signature of dyadic interaction (Nguyen et al., 2020, Nguyen et al., 2021, Speer et al., 2024). Neural synchrony is thought to reflect dynamic social processes such as engagement, attunement, joint attention, and communication (both verbal and nonverbal), all of which are fundamental to effective learning. According to the mutual prediction hypothesis, synchrony arises from each partner’s capacity for social predictive processing, whereby individuals continuously anticipate and adapt to one another’s behavior (Hamilton and Holler, 2023, De Felice et al., 2023, Piazza et al., 2020 & 2021). Although much of the existing literature has focused on socio-emotional contexts (Miller et al., 2019, Reindl et al., 2018, Deng et al., 2024), emerging evidence indicates that adult-child neural synchrony predicts children’s learning, even in cases where single-brain activity does not (Piazza et al., 2021, Hoehl et al., 2025, Schilbach and Redcay, 2025). For example, neural synchrony is more powerful in distinguishing between instructional strategies than using individual brain activity from the teacher or learner alone when using machine learning approaches (Pan et al., 2020); better predicts long-term memory retention than individual brain measures in adult learners (Davidesco et al., 2019); and better predicts learning in social referencing tasks in infants (Santamaria et al., 2020). These findings underscore the value of examining learning as a co-regulated neural process rather than a solely relying on individual partners.

Current study. Here, we aim to bridge two lines of research, behavioral work on parental scaffolding (unimodal and multimodal) and neuroimaging literature on neural synchrony. Our overarching goal is to situate the developing brain within its social and communicative context, revealing how everyday parent-child interactions scaffold the neural dynamics that support engagement in children’s proximal environments. To address the gaps in the literature, we first ask how in situ processing of different scaffolding modalities during dynamic interactions may be similar or different. The most straightforward prediction is that multimodal scaffolding simply leads to a higher level (i.e. quantity) of synchrony between interacting partners. Indeed, pointing leads to behavioral contingency between parents and children, such as gaze following (Butterworth & Jarrett, 1991). In addition, gesture has also been argued to serve a channeling function and better enable the brain to make top-down predictions about upcoming content, which is in line with the mutual prediction hypothesis (Holler and Levinson, 2019). However, it is also possible that multimodal scaffolding leads to a qualitatively different type of neural synchrony. The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and temporo-parietal junction are the most consistently identified regions of interest in dyadic neural synchrony literature, but they seem to play somewhat distinct roles. dlPFC is considered to reflect more cognitive control, problem solving and regulation, whereas TPJ is considered to reflect mentalizing and social referencing. Both the dlPFC and TPJ have been consistently implicated in hyperscanning studies of interactive learning in both teacher-student interactions as well as in more informal interactions between parents and children (Li et al., 2024, Tan et al., 2023). If the role of multimodal input is qualitative rather than merely quantitative, it is possible that multimodal input will differentially engage these systems. Multimodal communication is processed faster and more efficiently than speech alone and contributes to common ground and mentalizing (Holler, 2022), processes that are subserved by TPJ. In contrast, communication in the absence of visual signals has been associated with greater delta-band tracking association with attentional selection, which is subserved by dlPFC (Drijvers and Holler, 2022). Thus, multimodal input may shift the locus of synchrony from dlPFC-related coupling toward greater TPJ involvement. Distinct brain regions in parents and children could also contribute to synchrony, reflecting their complementary roles. Evidence from teacher-student studies supports this differentiation. For example, during various learning tasks, in typical teaching contexts, when teachers use scaffolding, teacher’s TPJ leads student PFC suggesting successful information transfer (Oku et al., 2023).

Second, it is not clear whether the roles of different scaffolding modalities in terms of their neural processes are stable across the course of the interaction or whether they fluctuate as interaction unfolds. Speech and gesture have been shown to play distinct roles in communication (Holler and Levinson, 2019). For example, speech is considered to be critical in the earlier stages of a conversation to establish a shared common ground, and multimodal communication might continue to play a role in later stages (Rasenberg et al., 2020). Similarly, neural synchrony also fluctuates dynamically across the course of interaction. For example, there might be an early peak in synchrony as the dyad adapts to the context and as the child gains more independence (Nguyen et al., 2023), although synchrony might also change over the course of the problem solving tasks as parent might scaffold the children by effectively modulates their behavior based on the child’s performance (Rosen et al., 2025). We argue that different scaffolding modalities might exert distinct temporal effects on synchrony.

To address these aims, we observed parents and children while they were engaged in a cooperative spatial problem-solving task and we simultaneously recorded neural activation using fNIRS hyperscanning. The parents were not provided with any specific instructions regarding a specific scaffold to use. Instead, we observed the naturalistic variability in the task-relevant parental scaffolding throughout the interaction. We then aligned behavioral coding of task-relevant parental scaffolding (speech and gesture) with fluctuations in parent-child neural synchrony. We hypothesize that multimodal parental input will be associated with greater overall neural synchrony, indicative of heightened engagement and interpersonal coordination, compared to unimodal input. Further, we anticipate that distinct scaffolding modalities will selectively modulate the temporal and spatial aspects of neural synchrony. Spatially, we expect the topography of neural synchrony to vary as a function of scaffolding modality. Prior research provides limited direct guidance regarding how spatial distribution might vary with scaffolding modality, but based on the limited information, we predict that multimodal scaffolding will be associated with greater TPJ involvement. Temporally, we anticipate that the influence of multimodal input on neural synchrony will fluctuate dynamically across the course of interaction and each modality might exert distinct temporal effects on synchrony. Again little is known about the role of scaffolding, but we predict the presence of scaffolding, and multimodal scaffolding in particular, to be associated with more sustained synchrony over the course of the interaction.

Method

Participants

A total of 45 families enrolled as participants in the study, and 42 parent-child dyads (84 participants) were included in the subsequent data analysis. Three dyads were excluded from the analysis due to either incomplete session participation or technical issues. The final sample size is similar to other parent-child fNIRS hyperscanning studies in the literature (Miller et al., 2019, Nguyen et al., 2020, Reindl et al., 2018; 2022). The children had an average age of 5.86 years old (range = 4.32 – 6.90 years, SD = 0.57, 19 females), and their parents had an average age of 38.67 years (range = 25.21 – 53.74 years, SD = 5.43, 4 males). All parents were primary caregivers based on parent report. All child participants were typically developing with no history of genetic syndromes, congenital malformations, or hearing, visual or physical impairment that may hinder their ability to complete the tasks. All the parents who participated in this study were white. 80.95% of the children who participated were white, and the remaining children were of mixed race. 90.48% of the families were not Hispanic/Latino. The mean family income was $113,900, but exhibited variability (range = $18,000–$235,000, SD = $48,087). The mean educational attainment of the parents was 16.39 years, corresponding to a college degree (range = 12 −18, SD = 1.80). Parent-child dyads were recruited using the university hospital’s electronic health records, university mass emailing, social media, and word of mouth. This study was approved by the university’s institutional review board (#201810826). All caregivers gave written informed consent for both themselves and their children. Children provided verbal assent before participating in the study. All families received a gift card for participating.

Procedures

The parent-child dyads participated in a 2-hour laboratory visit during which they engaged in a series of tasks while undergoing functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) imaging. fNIRS-based hyperscanning simultaneously measured neural activities from both members of each dyad during the task by capturing hemodynamic responses, quantified as changes in oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin concentrations detected by the fNIRS devices. Subsequently, the children underwent standardized tests while the parents completed multiple questionnaires about their child and family demographics.

fNIRS task design

During the fNIRS brain imaging session, the parent and the child sat on opposite sides of a round table, face-to-face. Given our specific interest in parental speech and gesture input during problem solving in the context of spatial development, in the current study we focused on the cooperative tangram task (Fig. 1). All dyads completed five structured tasks in a fixed order: 1) free conversation task (240 s), 2) an individual tangram puzzle task (120 s), 3) a cooperative tangram puzzle task (240 s), 4) a second individual tangram puzzle task (120 s), and 5) a tangram-related conversation task (240 s). The task sequence was identical across dyads. This protocol was adapted from prior parent-child interaction research (Nguyen et al., 2020). During the data collection, inter-task intervals were ensured at least 60 s to allow neural activity to return to baseline and to minimize any carryover effects from one task to the next. The present study focused on the cooperative tangram puzzle task. During this task, each dyad was provided with a single booklet consisting of eight templates and one set of tangram pieces and instructed to complete the puzzles together. Specifically, partners were asked to collaboratively manipulate seven geometric pieces to reconstruct target templates (e.g., abstract forms, objects, and animals) displayed in the booklet. Dyads were allowed to interact freely, were told to interact as they would at home, and were not given any additional instructions regarding how they should approach the task. All sessions were video recorded using a dual-angle camera system, that enabled recording from two different perspectives as well as a ceiling camera for a top view. More details about each task can be found in our previous publication (Li et al., 2024).

fNIRS data acquisition

Data was collected from both participants simultaneously using two NIRSport2 optical topography systems (manufactured by NIRx Medizintechnik GmbH, Germany) with one device allocated to the parent and one to the child. Eight light sources and eight detectors were grouped into four 2 × 2 probe sets which resulted in a total of 16 channels covering the dorsal lateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) and temporoparietal junction (TPJ) for both hemispheres. The selection of brain areas covered by the cap was informed by previous neuroimaging work on gesture processing and fNIRS hyperscanning research on interpersonal communication and problem solving (Li et al., 2024, Nguyen et al., 2020, Nguyen et al., 2021). The location of light sources and detectors were based on the international EEG 10–20 system for electrode placement. More information about the brain areas targeted can be found in supplementary materials (S1). The data was acquired using the Hyperscanning software (v2014 NIRx Medical Technologies LLC) at two near-infrared light wavelengths (760 and 850 nm) and at a sampling rate of 10.17 Hz. More details about configuration can be found in our previous publication (Li et al., 2024).

fNIRS data analysis

Preprocessing - Individual-level analyses. The collected fNIRS data were preprocessed using the AnalyzIR toolbox implemented in MATLAB (Santosa et al., 2018). First, a wavelet-based motion correction filter was applied to the raw optical density signals to attenuate motion-related spikes and other non-physiological artifacts while preserving the underlying hemodynamic fluctuations. Next, the modified Beer-Lambert law was used to convert the cleaned optical density signals into concentration changes of oxygenated (HbO) and deoxygenated hemoglobin (HbR), thereby transforming light density measurement into physiologically interpretable hemodynamic responses. Finally, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed to remove global and systemic noise components by regressing out dominant variance components. PCA was configured to remove only the first principal component. In summary, by applying wavelet filter, motion artifacts were first minimized to prevent their propagation into hemoglobin concentration estimates; the Beer-Lambert conversion was then performed; and PCA was applied last to further isolate neural-related hemodynamic activity by reducing structured noise in the concentration data. All preprocessing steps were implemented using functions provided in the AnalyzIR toolbox.

For subsequent analysis, we focused on HbO variations in the regional cerebral blood flow which are more frequently used in hyperscanning studies (Hoshi, 2016). After individual-level preprocessing, all channels from all participants were retained in their original form for further analysis, and each channel was treated equivalently in the statistical models.

Wavelet coherence transform (WTC) - Pair-level analysis. After individual preprocessing, two data series of the participants from each family were paired as a dyad to calculate interpersonal neural synchrony values. Wavelet Coherence Transform (WTC) algorithm was applied to quantify interpersonal neural synchrony (Cui et al., 2012) based on the “wcoherence” function implemented in MATLAB (Grinsted et al., 2004). The coherence values based on WTC include both spatial information (channel pair information) and temporal information (time course information). We excluded the first ten second and last ten second of the interaction to minimize potential confounds related to task onset and offset effects, as well as neural synchronization arising from the shared external stimuli, for example the noise of the door closing as the experimenter leaves the room. Based on earlier literature specifically focusing on conversation-based contexts (e.g. Nguyen et al., 2021), we chose 0.06–0.15 Hz (corresponding to ∼6–16 s) as our frequency band. To ensure better estimation of WTC, values located outside of the cone of interest (COI) were removed.

Behavioral coding

Parental speech coding. Using the video recording of tangram cooperative interactions, we transcribed all parental speech. The unit of transcription was the utterance, defined as any sequence of words preceded or followed by a change in conversational turn, intonation, or a pause. More specifically, utterance boundaries were determined using two primary criteria and one supplementary cue. First cue was grammatical completeness. Each complete sentence that could stand alone was coded as a separate utterance, including compound sentences with two independently standing clauses. Speech features such as false starts, self-corrections, stumbles, tag words, and conditional statements were treated as single utterances. Second, pause duration was considered. Any segment beginning after a pause of three or more seconds was coded as a new utterance.

We then identified all task-related spatial utterances by parents. Task-related spatial utterances were defined as those which included spatial words, i.e., words that provided spatial information about the tangram pieces they were working on or about spatial actions. Spatial words included dimensional adjectives such as “big, small”; shape terms such as “square, triangle”; descriptions of the relative position or location, such as “corner, edge”; and transformative terms describing the relative transformation or orientation of objects in space, such as “flip, turn” (see Clingan-Siverly et al., 2021 for further details on this coding). The reliability of two coders was 94%.

Parental gesture coding. Using the video recording, we coded parental gestures relevant to the task during tangram cooperative interactions. Task-related gestures were defined as gestures that offered spatial information. We categorized parental task-related gestures into three types: deictic gestures, iconic gestures, and trace gestures (Cartmill, Demir, and Goldin-Meadow, 2012). Deictic gestures involve pointing to a specific place or object. For instance, a parent might point to a tangram piece or a location in the booklet. Iconic gestures represent the attributes or actions associated with objects or events. For example, a parent might simulate holding a tangram piece and make a twisting motion with their fingers to indicate the action of "rotate." Trace gestures involve using a swiping motion to represent an attribute of an object or to provide spatial information about actions. For instance, a parent might swipe their index finger along the edge of a tangram piece or on the booklet to demonstrate the potential clue for solving the puzzle (see Clingan-Siverly et al., 2021 for further details on this coding). The reliability of two coders was 91%.

Combining fNIRS and behavioral data

To integrate the fNIRS data with behavioral data, we temporally aligned neural synchrony values and parental behavioral coding data by segmenting the 240-second cooperative interaction into smaller time windows. Specifically, the 240-second interaction was partitioned into eight consecutive 30-second epochs, resulting in eight distinct epochs per dyad. This segmentation allowed us to capture temporal fluctuations in both neural activation and scaffolding behaviors, and to examine their coupling at a finer-grained timescale.

Within each 30-second epoch, the frequency of speech and gesture scaffolding behaviors were coded separately. Preliminary inspection indicated that frequency of both speech and gesture scaffolding within a given epoch was relatively low and exhibited limited within-epoch variability. The distributions were positively skewed (skewness=2.33 for Speech, 4.38 for Gesture and 1.29 for Speech+Gesture), suggesting that scaffolding behaviors occurred infrequently and were not normally distributed across epochs. Given this distributional pattern and to avoid violating model assumptions and different units of coding, scaffolding was operationalized as a binary indicator (present vs. absent within each epoch) in subsequent analyses, rather than as a continuous frequency-based predictor. As a result, we categorized each epoch as belonging to one of the four following scaffolding modalities: 1) Speech: when parents only provided speech scaffolding during the epoch; 2) Gesture, when parents only provided gesture scaffolding during the epoch; 3) Speech + Gesture: when parents provided both speech and gesture scaffolding during the epoch; and 4) None: If parents did not provide any task-relevant speech or gesture scaffolding. Please see supplementary materials (S2) for a replication of the analysis using continuous frequency data.

Each epoch was then aligned with the corresponding WTC values, representing interpersonal neural synchrony for the same time window. We averaged WTC values for channels within a region of interest (ROI, left dlPFC, right dlPFC, left TPJ and right TPJ) pair from the parent and the child. Thus, each dyad had eight different WTC values per ROI pair. We used this approach of leveraging multiple epochs (time windows) to capture the dynamic changes in neural synchrony and to identify shifting relations with parental scaffolding modalities throughout the interaction, a method also applied in previous research (Nguyen et al., 2021).

Analysis plan

To address main questions about how the temporal structure of neural synchrony as a function of different scaffold modalities, different multi-level mixed models were fit using the lmer function in the lme4 package (Kuznetsova et al., 2017) in R (R Core Team, 2021). All variables were grand-mean centered. In all models, WTC values were the dependent variable. All multilevel models were within-dyad-level analyses, where dyad was the random factor and a random slope for epoch was included, which allowed for individual variability in both intercept and rate of change of WTC. We employed a two-step approach to build the lmer models. We had a priori top-down hypothesis that we aimed to test for each of our hypotheses. This guided the inclusion of specific fixed effects in the models. We utilized a chi-square test to identify the best-fitting model based on the goodness-of-fit statistics. This approach allowed us to select the model that provided the best overall fit to the data. Specifically, we first examined how neural synchrony evolved over the course of the interaction by modeling epoch as a fixed effect and testing whether including higher-order (e.g., quadratic) terms improved model fit. This approach allowed us to identify the functional form (linear vs. nonlinear) that best captured the temporal trajectory of synchrony. The models also included ROI pair as a predictor. Next, we examined the relation of scaffolding modality to neural synchrony by adding Speech, Gesture, and Speech + Gesture variables and interactions with epoch as fixed factors. All post-hoc comparison were conducted via emmeans function in R (Lenth and Piaskowski, 2025). To examine the spatial structure in more detail at the channel level, we identified the channel pair showing the highest WTC values within each scaffolding modality and compared its WTC value to the average of all other channel pairs across epochs.

Results

Descriptive statistics

Parental speech. To measure parental verbal scaffolding, we calculated the number of spatial utterances parents produced during the spatial problem-solving tangram task. On average, parents provided 12.24 spatial utterances (Range=2–34, SD=6.62) across the interaction. As mentioned above, we separated the interaction session into eight 30-second epochs. The average number of utterances that included task-relevant speech in one epoch was 1.53.

Parental gesture. To measure parental gesture scaffolding, we calculated the number of spatial gestures parents produced, including all categories of spatial gesture, during the cooperative interactions. On average, parents provided 14.86 gestures (Range= 2–57, SD=11.32) across whole interaction session. The average number of gestures in one epoch is 1.86.

Speech + Gesture - Overlap of Speech and Gesture. As discussed above, each epoch was classified into four mutually exclusive scaffolding modalities depending on whether parents used a speech and/or gesture scaffolding during that epoch: Speech, Gesture, Speech + Gesture, and None. After categorizing each epoch from each dyad, we had 336 epochs (42 dyads x 8 epochs) in total. Among all epochs across all dyads, Speech accounted for 18.45% of all epochs; Gesture accounted for 14.88% of all epochs; Speech + Gesture accounted for 55.65% of the epochs; and None accounted for 11.01% epochs. The frequency of each scaffolding modality varied across epochs as well. Supplementary materials Figure S1 reveals the number of dyads in each of the four scaffolding modalities per epoch. Thus, parents used a variety of scaffolds in their interactions, with multimodal (Speech+Gesture) scaffold being the most frequent.

Temporal distribution of neural synchrony

We first examined how neural synchrony, as measured by WTC, changed over the course of the interaction. Epoch was centered at the midpoint of the task and a quadratic term was included to capture nonlinear change. Model comparison confirmed that including the quadratic term significantly improved fit over a linear-only model, χ²(1) = 32.64, p < .001.

The linear epoch term was not significant. Role of parental scaffolding in neural synchrony

Temporal profile of neural synchrony as a function of parental scaffolding. To address our question about the dynamic relationship between parental scaffolding modalities and neural synchrony, Model 2 included scaffolding modality as a categorical fixed effect. Each epoch was classified into one of four mutually exclusive categories — Speech Only, Gesture Only, Speech + Gesture, or None. The resulting variable was coded with None (no scaffolding) as the reference level. Including scaffolding modality significantly improved model fit compared with Model 1, χ²(3) = 17.65, p < .001. To examine whether the temporal trajectory of synchrony differed across scaffolding modality, Model 3 added interaction terms between scaffolding modality and both the linear and quadratic epoch terms. Model 3 significantly improved fit over Model 2, χ²(6) = 94.84, p < .001. The model equations are as follows:

Model 2: WTC ∼ Epoch + Modality + Epoch² + ROI pair + (1 + Epoch | Dyad)

Model 3: WTC ∼ Epoch × Modality + Epoch² × Modality + ROI pair + (1 + Epoch | Dyad)

In Model 3 (see Table 1, Model 3), key fixed effects and interactions involving scaffolding were significant, indicating that the temporal trajectory of neural synchrony differed meaningfully across scaffolding modalities. When parents were not actively scaffolding (during no scaffolding epochs as reference), synchrony showed a steady decline across the task, with a significant negative linear slope and a marginally significant negative quadratic term. Relative to no scaffolding, all three active scaffolding modalities showed distinct trajectories (Table 1, Model 3). Speech + Gesture epochs did not significantly differ from no scaffolding in overall synchrony level but showed significantly more positive linear and quadratic terms, indicating that synchrony was sustained across the interaction during multimodal scaffolding rather than dipping and recovering. Speech Only epochs showed lower overall synchrony than no scaffolding but a pronounced U-shaped trajectory driven by the quadratic curvature, with a mid-interaction minimum and strong late recovery. Gesture Only epochs also showed lower overall synchrony, with a later, more modest upturn. To assess whether the reported effects were robust to demographic variation, we refit Model 3 with child sex, child age, and parental education as additional fixed effects. Adding these covariates did not significantly improve model fit, χ²(6) = 8.41, p = .210. Finally, to rule out possible carryover effects from the preceding epoch, we also refit Model 3 adding the prior epoch's modality (lag-1) as a covariate. No lagged term predicted synchrony and all concurrent interactions in Model 3 remained significant. Fig. 3 depicts trajectories of WTC as a function of scaffolding modality.

Neural synchrony (WTC) as a function of parental scaffolding modality across epochs. Curves show model-estimated quadratic trajectories of WTC across epochs for each parental scaffolding modality, derived from Model 3 fixed effects; shaded bands denote 95% confidence intervals. Follow-up Tukey-adjusted pairwise comparisons on overall synchrony level (estimated marginal means) indicated that Speech Only was associated with significantly higher synchrony than Gesture Only (estimate = 0.010, SE = 0.003, z = 3.17, p = .008). Similarly, Speech + Gesture produced significantly higher synchrony than Gesture Only (estimate = 0.009, SE = 0.003, z = 3.20, p = .008). No other pairwise comparisons on overall level reached significance (all ps >.10). Pairwise comparisons of condition-specific linear trends revealed significant differences in the rate of change across epochs. Speech Only showed a significantly more positive slope than None (estimate = 0.008, z = 5.05, p < .001) and Gesture Only (estimate = 0.004, z = 2.80, p = .026). Speech + Gesture also showed a significantly more positive slope than None (estimate = 0.009, z = 6.97, p < .001) and Gesture Only (estimate = 0.005, z = 4.21, p < .001). Gesture Only did not significantly differ from None (z = 2.13, p = .145), and Speech Only did not differ from Speech + Gesture (z = −1.12, p = .679). Finally, pairwise comparisons of condition-specific quadratic curvatures showed that Speech Only had significantly greater curvature than None (estimate = 0.004, z = 5.84, p < .001), Gesture Only (estimate = 0.002, z = 2.91, p = .019), and Speech + Gesture (estimate = 0.003, z = 5.30, p < .001). Gesture Only showed significantly greater curvature than None (estimate = 0.002, z = 3.36, p = .004). Speech + Gesture did not significantly differ from None (z = 2.51, p = .059) or Gesture Only (z = 1.74, p = .303).

We next wanted to better understand the differential relations of scaffolding modality to epoch slope and the quadratic term in Model 3. To examine whether scaffolding modalities contributed differently to synchrony across parts of the interaction, we fit separate linear mixed-effects models to the early (epochs 1–4) and late (epochs 5–8) windows, with epoch centered at the midpoint of each window. This also enabled us to better test the role of scaffolding in the change in WTC and pinpoint where the conditions diverged. The model equation for each window (early and late) was: WTC ∼ Epoch × Modality + ROI pair + (1 + Epoch | Dyad). In the early window, synchrony in None (reference) declined significantly across epochs (β = −0.015, SE = 0.005, p = .006). For the Epoch × Modality interactions, only Speech + Gesture reached significance (p = .049; Speech Only: p = .074; Gesture Only: p = .100), suggesting that only Speech + Gesture attenuated the decline. Pairwise comparisons with Tukey correction showed that the Modality did not differ from each other in overall synchrony level (all ps > .13) or slope (all ps > .21) during the first half of the interaction. In other words, scaffolding modalities were not distinguishable early on, with all showing an initial decline.

In the late window, synchrony in None (reference) declined significantly across epochs (β = −0.011, SE = 0.004, p = .003), but more importantly for our purposes this varied with scaffolding. The Epoch × Modality interaction was significant, F(3, 142.9) = 12.34, p < .001. Pairwise comparisons on overall synchrony level revealed that Speech + Gesture showed the highest sustained synchrony (EMM = 0.293), significantly higher than Speech Only (EMM = 0.283, p = .037), which in turn exceeded Gesture Only (EMM = 0.268, p = .012). Gesture Only did not differ from None (EMM = 0.265, p = .914). Pairwise comparisons of modality-specific slopes with Tukey correction confirmed the differentiation. Speech Only showed the steepest positive slope (+0.019), significantly exceeding all other modalities (vs. None: p < .001; vs. Gesture Only: p < .001; vs. Speech + Gesture: p = .001). Both Gesture Only (p = .025) and Speech + Gesture (p = .001) showed significantly more positive slopes than no scaffolding, but did not differ from each other (p = .874). Only no scaffolding presented a negative slope (−0.011), confirming continued decline through the end of the interaction. See Supplementary materials Table S2 for full model outputs of early and late window models.

Taken together, the contrast between early and late windows indicates that Model 3 interactions might come from the second half of the interaction, specifically it reveals that scaffolding modalities might differentiate to a greater extent in the second half of the interaction. Critically, although Speech Only and Speech + Gesture had statistically indistinguishable linear slopes in Model 3, the late-window analysis shows that they arrive at comparable positive trends through different dynamics. Speech + Gesture sustained the highest level of synchrony with a relatively flat late-window trajectory, whereas Speech Only showed the steepest positive slope, recovering strongly from a lower baseline. These two patterns are consistent with functionally distinct roles for multimodal and unimodal speech scaffolding: a maintenance dynamic in which synchrony is sustained and prevented from declining, versus a recovery dynamic in which synchrony drops early and rebounds in the second half.

Spatial distribution of neural synchrony as a function of parental scaffolding. Results from the three linear mixed-effects models reported above showed a significant main effect of ROI pair across all models (see Table 1). Specifically, in the final Model 3, the main effect of ROI pair was significant (F(15, 5266.7) = 1.761, p = .034), indicating that WTC values varied across spatial locations. However, none of the individual ROI pair coefficients reached conventional significance (all ps > .05), suggesting that this effect might not be driven by any single ROI pair but rather reflected a broadly distributed pattern across ROIs. See Supplementary Table S1 for full ROI pair coefficients from Model 3. To see if the patterns might vary with scaffolding, we tested whether adding interaction terms between ROI pair and Epoch and Modality (χ²(105) = 86.03, p = .912), or additionally with Epoch Squared and Modality (χ²(165) = 113.14, p = .999), would improve Model 3, but neither significantly did so.

Given the differential patterns identified above for the early and late portion of the interaction, we also examined ROI pair effect for early and late epochs separately using exploratory analyses. Follow-up analyses examining early and late epochs separately revealed a differential spatial pattern. The main effect of ROI pair was not significant during the early epoch (F(15, 2582.9) = 1.069, p = .380), but was significant during the late epoch (F(15, 2573.6) = 2.899, p < .001). Within the late epoch, three specific ROI pairs reached significance: child L-TPJ paired with parent L-TPJ (β = −0.010, SE = 0.004, p = .008), child right-dlPFC paired with parent L-TPJ (β = −0.008, SE = 0.004, p = .037), and child R-dlPFC paired with parent R-TPJ (β = −0.011, SE = 0.004, p = .003). However, no ROI pairs survived FDR correction for multiple comparisons (all ps with fdr >.05). See Supplementary Table S2 for full outputs of early and late epoch models. Together with the significant omnibus effect, this might suggest that WTC differences across spatial locations in the late stage also reflected a broadly distributed pattern rather than localized to specific ROI pairs. Finally, given that our primary research question concerned the spatial distribution of neural synchrony across different scaffolding modalities and given that no significant effect of or interaction of ROI pair with Modality, we conducted follow-up exploratory analyses examining WTC at the individual channel pair level separately for each modality. To identify standout channel pairs within each modality, we employed a combination of outlier detection and gap analysis which is appropriate for reducing the analytical space. WTC values were computed for all possible channel pair combination across 16 child channels and 16 parent channels, yielding 256 candidate channel pairs per modality. For each modality, channel pairs were ranked in descending order of their WTC values, and two complementary criteria were applied, 1) a statistical threshold of 3 standard deviations above the modality-specific mean WTC value, and 2) identification of the largest discontinuity in the ranked WTC distribution (known as gap analysis), which captures meaningful separation between the highest-ranking pair and the remainder of the distribution regardless of whether the absolute threshold is exceeded.

Across all four modalities, only two channel pairs exceeded the 3 SD threshold. In the Speech modality, child channel 2 (L-dlPFC) paired with parent channel 2 (L-dlPFC) emerged as a statistical outlier (WTC value = 0.346, z = 3.159). Corroborating this, the largest gap in the ranked distribution was observed between rank 1 (child l-dPFC and parent l-dlPFC) followed by rank 2 consisting of child channel 16 (R-TPJ) paired with parent channel 1 (L-dlPFC) (WTC values = 0.338, z = 2.707), indicating a clear separation between the top-ranked pair and the remainder of the distribution. In the Speech + Gesture modality, child channel 9 (R-dlPFC) paired with parent channel 7 (l-TPJ) was identified as the outlier channel pair (WTC value = 0.330, z = 3.583), with the largest gap located between rank 1 (child R-dlPFC and parent l-TPJ) followed by rank 2 consisting of child channel 9 (R-dlPFC) paired with parent channel 12 (R-dlPFC) (WTC value = 0.319, z = 2.739). No channel pairs exceeded the 3 SD threshold in the Gesture or None modalities. The full ranked WTC distribution for all four modalities are provided in the Supplementary Materials Figure S2.

Discussion

Growing research in developmental cognitive neuroscience acknowledges the importance of children’s proximal daily interactions as predicting variability in neurocognitive development, over and above broad factors such as socioeconomic status, especially in academically related domains (e.g., Girard et al., 2022; Romeo et al., 2018). Such interactions serve as learning contexts, and provide the opportunity for parents to scaffold their children through supporting their children’s problem solving. Previous research underscores the importance of examining how various modalities of parental scaffolding relate to children’s development outcomes and learning. Specifically, behavioral results highlighted the prevalence and importance of multimodal scaffolding (e.g., Goldin-Meadow, 2009; Özçalışkan and Dimitrova, 2013; Rowe and Goldin-Meadow, 2009; Wakefield et al., 2018 & Wakefield et al., 2019). A separate body of work examines the role of processing gestures by the listener at the neural level (e.g., Demir-Lira et al., 2018). Despite these advances, the neural basis of multimodal processing during natural, real-time interactions remains poorly understood. Specifically, the neural mechanisms that underlie the role and effect of different scaffolding modalities during dynamic interactions remain unclear. In this context, the present study explored how specific forms of scaffolding modality relate to neural synchrony in parent-child interactions. We applied fNIRS-based hyperscanning to measure the neural activity in both parents and children during a cooperative spatial problem-solving interaction. We specifically asked how the temporal and spatial nature of parent-child neural synchrony vary as a function of parental scaffolding modality. The findings revealed distinct temporal and spatial patterns of parent-child neural synchrony. Below, we discuss our findings.

Temporal structure of neural synchrony varies across scaffolding modalities. The pattern of neural synchrony varied by parental scaffolding modality, which differed on linear and quadratic components of the trajectory. Under no scaffolding, synchrony declined steadily across the interaction, consistent with prior hyperscanning work showing decreasing dyadic neural coupling during cooperative interactions (Mayseless et al., 2019). On the linear trajectory, the presence of speech (alone or combined with gesture) distinguished epochs in which synchrony was maintained from those in which it continued to decline, whereas gesture alone or no scaffolding did not reliably attenuate the decline. On the quadratic curvature, multimodal scaffolding produced the flattest, most sustained trajectory, whereas speech-only scaffolding produced a pronounced U-shape with strong late-interaction recovery. Yet, this recovery did not bring speech-only synchrony up to the level sustained by multimodal scaffolding. Our follow-up analyses of the early and late interaction windows offered convergent insight. Scaffolding modalities were largely indistinguishable in the first half of the interaction but differentiated substantially in the second half. In the late window, multimodal scaffolding sustained the highest level of synchrony with a relatively flat trajectory, whereas speech-only scaffolding produced the steepest positive slope, indicating recovery from a lower baseline. In other words, multimodal scaffolding maintained synchrony at a higher level, whereas speech-only scaffolding rebounded from an earlier decline. Similar to our results, Nguyen and colleagues (2021) found that the influence of communicative behavior on dyadic neural coupling intensified as the interaction progressed, suggesting that dyadic neural coupling reflects the gradual entrainment of neural rhythms through sustained interpersonal engagement rather than immediate moment-to-moment responses.

Importantly, the current study went beyond reporting neural synchrony as a single aggregated value by capturing dynamic changes in neural synchrony and in behavioral measures. In prior parent-child research, neural synchrony has typically been reported as a single aggregated measure linked to a concurrent also aggregated behavioral measure (e.g., Nguyen et al., 2020; Reindl et al., 2018; Piazza et al., 2021), without examining how synchrony itself evolves across the interaction and relates to changing behavior. A notable exception is Nguyen and colleagues (2020), who used epoch-level analysis to examine neural synchrony and turn-taking during parent-child conversation. However, the turn-taking measure was collapsed into a single value per dyad in this study as well. By characterizing the dynamic relations between neural and behavioral levels of analysis as the interaction unfolded, we add to the existing literature with a more temporally resolved view of dyadic coupling.

These results suggest that the neural mechanisms supporting multimodal and unimodal scaffolding may differ functionally. Multimodal scaffolding appeared to operate through sustained alignment from the outset, suggesting a maintenance pathway that prevents synchrony loss rather than repairing it after the fact. In contrast, unimodal speech scaffolding appeared to operate through a recovery or repair mechanism, producing the strongest trajectory increase later in the interaction but not the highest absolute level of synchrony. Multimodal input provides redundant and complementary channels of information that guide children's visual attention and shape what learners extract from the interaction (Wakefield et al., 2018). In our spatial problem-solving task, gesture combined with speech may have grounded children's comprehension by anchoring abstract verbal information in concrete, spatially relevant representations (Goldin-Meadow and Alibali, 2013). This combination may also better support children's active participation and alignment during interaction (Grebelsky-Lichtman, 2016) and is consistent with the broader literature on the role of gesture in maintaining common ground during conversation (Alibali et al., 2019, Holler and Bavelas, 2017). Together, these findings suggest that while speech is associated with neural synchrony, the addition of gesture might have sustained neural alignment by mitigating decline over the course of the interaction. To our knowledge, this distinction between multimodal scaffolding as a maintenance pathway and unimodal speech scaffolding as a repair pathway has not been previously characterized in the parent-child hyperscanning literature, and may have meaningful implications for understanding how scaffolding shapes the neural dynamics of joint problem-solving. Future work using more fine-grained experimental manipulations could systematically isolate the contributions of each scaffolding modality and provide a more precise characterization of these distinct neural pathways.

Our results also raise new questions about the nature of the relation between neural synchrony and dyadic coordination more broadly. Research on behavioral alignment, for example, lexical alignment and convergence on referential expressions during conversations, typically shows increasing similarity across an interaction, in contrast to the declining neural synchrony we observed under no scaffolding. One possibility is that behavioral alignment and neural synchrony index complementary but non-identical aspects of dyadic coordination. Behavioral alignment may reflect accumulation of shared representations, whereas neural synchrony in the frequency band examined here may reflect dynamic moment-to-moment coupling that can decline as dyads establish stable shared understanding and require less active mutual tracking. This interpretation is consistent with Mayseless and colleagues (Mayseless et al., 2019), who found that decreasing synchrony in adult problem-solving dyads was linked to increasing behavioral cooperation, suggesting that declining synchrony may reflect successful rather than failed coordination as the interaction progresses.

A related question concerns the directionality of the relation between parental scaffolding and neural synchrony. In the current study, parents were given no explicit instruction regarding which scaffolding modality to use; scaffolding decisions were made spontaneously in response to the natural demands of the cooperative task and varied throughout the interaction. This naturalistic design introduces an important consideration. Rather than scaffolding driving neural synchrony, an alternative interpretation is that parents initiated scaffolding precisely at moments when they sensed a decline in synchrony, engagement, or the cooperative process. That is, scaffolding may have been a response to, rather than a cause of, dynamic decoupling, possibly registered in neural signatures before becoming apparent in observable behavior. Whether changes in neural synchrony precede, follow, or co-occur with changes in scaffolding remains an open question. Because our results are correlational, examining the fine-grained temporal relationship between scaffolding onset and neural synchrony fluctuations would be an important next step in disentangling this directionality.

Spatial structure of neural synchrony varies across scaffolding modalities. We also examined the spatial structure of parent-child neural synchrony associated with each scaffolding modality. We did not observe differences as a function of scaffolding at the ROI pair level. However, our exploratory analyses at the channel level showed that scaffolding modality did make a difference at the specific channel pair level. During unimodal speech scaffolding, synchrony was most pronounced in a channel linking the left dlPFC of the child and parent compared to other channels. This is consistent with the view that listeners might engage in more effortful processing, relying more heavily on prefrontal regions, when the complementary information conveyed by visual signals is absent. Indeed, in an EEG hyperscanning study with adults, communication in the absence of visual signals was associated with greater delta-band tracking, which is linked to attentional selection (Drijvers and Holler, 2022). In contrast, when parents leveraged multimodal scaffolding, greater parent-child neural synchrony emerged in a channel coupling child right dlPFC and parental left TPJ. The involvement of parent TPJ in the coupling is consistent with our predictions and with the literature suggesting that multimodal input may shift the locus of synchrony from dlPFC-related coupling toward greater TPJ involvement. A similar complementary pattern has been observed in teacher-student interactions, where teachers recruit TPJ to mentalize about and predict the student, while students engage frontal regions to process and align with the teacher's input, with teacher TPJ leading student PFC in a way that suggests successful information transfer (Oku et al., 2023). Gesture has been identified as key to establishing and maintaining common ground (Holler, 2022). So, one possible explanation for this pattern is that parents draw on both speech and gesture cues when they perceive that their children require more extensive support during active problem solving. This is consistent with evidence that the TPJ plays a distinct role in predicting other agents and making adaptive decisions within shared social contexts (Carter et al., 2012).

Overall, the spatial findings converge with the temporal results reported above, in which multimodal scaffolding was associated with sustained levels of neural synchrony, particularly later in the interaction. The covariance of the parental TPJ under multimodal scaffolding suggests that parents might have been actively integrating multiple communicative cues — both speech and gesture — when supporting their child, which inherently demands broader social-cognitive engagement. This dual-channel input not only places greater integrative demands on the parent but may also offer the child richer and more redundant cues to process, jointly supporting sustained neural coupling throughout the interaction. Supporting this interpretation, prior behavioral work has shown that parents are more likely to use gestures when their children need greater support, specifically for example for younger children than older children in a similar puzzle-solving interaction (Vallotton et al., 2015). As our channel-level results were based on exploratory analyses, these should be considered as hypothesis generating for future work.

Limitations. Several limitations of the current study should be acknowledged. First, parents were not given explicit instructions to systematically vary their scaffolding. Thus, the frequency of each scaffolding modality naturally varied across dyads which led to scaffolding cues not being equally distributed. To pinpoint independent modality-specific mechanisms, future work should employ experimental manipulations. For example, parents can be told to use one cue over another to systematically isolate the contributions of different scaffolding modalities. This might enable better characterization of the neural pathways for how each modality relates to parent–child synchrony. Second, the temporal resolution of the epoch length used for scaffolding classification represents a methodological constraint. The current epoch duration was constrained by minimal time to estimate an appropriate coherence value for the indicated frequency range (Grinsted et al., 2004). This duration may not have been sufficiently fine-grained to capture moment-to-moment fluctuations in speech and gesture and in neural synchrony. Finer grained measures are needed to precisely track the overlap in speech and gesture or the sequential ordering of scaffolding behaviors within an epoch. Third, the scaffolding modalities examined in the current study were situated exclusively within task-oriented cooperative contexts. In everyday life, however, parents scaffold their children not only during structured problem-solving activities but also in unstructured, non-task contexts such as conversation, play and emotional exchanges (Salonen et al., 2007). Whether the patterns identified here would generalize to other interaction contexts, such as free play, book reading, or emotionally charged exchanges remains an open question. Our sample was relatively homogeneous in demographic background, which is a limitation that future studies should address by including families from more diverse socioeconomic contexts. For example, although our sample primarily represented higher-SES families, prior work suggests that gesture may play an especially important role in lower-SES contexts by supporting responsive and engaged parent–child communication (Vallotton et al., 2015). While our findings highlight the role of multimodal scaffolding even within a relatively homogenous group, future work should examine the role gesture in more diverse families. Finally, due to our task design, we do not have a distinct, subsequent measure of children’s learning. Future studies, primarily those using longitudinal designs, should examine how scaffolding use and synchrony predict children’s post-interaction neuroplasticity and later learning outcomes (Markus and Shamay-Tsoory, 2024).

Taken together, the findings of the current study suggest that different ways in which parents scaffold their children’s problem solving during dynamic parent–child interactions might have different neural signatures. Second-person neuroscience approaches are particularly well suited to capture these dynamics of parent-child interactions and characterize children’s proximal experiences. Overall, our findings underscore that parental scaffolding via speech and gesture may be closely related to the neural dynamics through which everyday parent-child interactions support children's engagement during naturalistic interactions, and may ultimately cascade to shape the developing brain.
