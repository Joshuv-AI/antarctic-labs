6
Conclusion and Outlook

In this work, we constructed the symmetry category for anomalous compact Lie group symmetries and calculated the Drinfeld center, enabling a convenient physical analysis of flat gauging using continuous SymTFT.

A promising application in condensed matter physics is to consider gapped and gapless boundary conditions of continuous SymTFT (“the categorical Landau paradigm”
22
;
24
) and classify phases with Lie group symmetries. Different from finite symmetries, here we will expect gaplessness resulted from Goldstone bosons when we break Lie group symmetries. We should also expect phases with continuous non-invertible symmetries as a result of flat gauging non-Abelian Lie groups.

This formulation of continuous symmetry categories invites a wide range of generalizations. For systems in higher dimensions, we expect to have an
n
-category
n{\rm Hilb}^{\kappa}(G)
. There has been various constructions for
n{\rm Hilb}
category
12
;
13
, but there is no existing literature describing the “
G
-graded” version. Even more generically, invertible symmetries forms a higher-group structure
62
;
41
;
98
, the higher representation theory of continuous higher-group symmetries will become handy when dealing with QFTs with Green-Schwarz mechanism.

For fermionic systems, it would be interesting to incorporate the fermionic parity symmetry and the related Gu-Wen type anomalies, in which case the symmetry category is expected to be
{\rm sHilb}^{\mu}(G)
, the SymTFT and corresponding boundary conditions could also be formulated
26
;
139
.

For open quantum systems where symmetries are decohered, there has been constructions of open SymTFT to classify mixed-state phases with strong and weak symmetries and symmetry breakings
100
;
122
;
114
, the same could be expected for continuous SymTFT.

We also put forward a series of open questions for mathematicians. During our quest for
\mathcal{Z}({\rm Hilb}^{k}(G))
, we did not recover the entire braided tensor category structure, thus

Question 6.1
.

What is the complete categorical structure of
\mathcal{Z}(\text{Hilb}^{k}(G))
within the framework of continuous categories? Furthermore, what is the rigorous analytic generalization of modularity that accommodates the continuous spectrum of simple objects in this center?

With answer to this question, we should consider taking the category to a
3
D TQFT, which physicists tend to formulate as a
BF+kCS
theory. The mathematical conundrum is the dualizability requirement in the target
3
-category when we build the fully-extended TQFT, so:

Question 6.2
.

Can we construct a
3
D (fully-)extended TQFT
Z
assigning
Z(S^{1})=\mathcal{Z}(\text{Hilb}^{k}(G))
by choosing the target 3-category to incorporate appropriate analytic data? If so, what replaces the standard finiteness obstructions of fully dualizable objects in this continuous regime?

With the
3
D TQFT
Z
well-defined, we intend to classify the topological boundary conditions (gapped boundaries) of
Z
as well as the conformal boundary conditions (gapless boundaries), leading to the question:

Question 6.3
.

What is the classification of possible topological and conformal boundary conditions for
Z
?

Ultimately, resolving these questions requires the mathematical formulation of TQFTs to transcend certain conditions enshrined in the standard dualizable framework, necessisating a analytic framework facilitated by functional analysis and operator algebras.

The involution on sections is defined by intertwining the bundle’s intrinsic involution with the groupoid’s inversion map

(125)

Appendix F
Injective Transgression

In this appendix we focus on one question: under what circumstances is the transgression

\tau:H^{4}(BG,\mathbb{Z})\to H^{3}_{G}(G,\mathbb{Z})=H^{3}(EG\times_{G}G,\mathbb{Z})=H^{3}(LBG,\mathbb{Z})

(133)

an injective homomorphism?

Suppose the Lie group
G
is compact connected, then its cohomology
H^{4}(BG,\mathbb{Z})
is torsion-free (see Theorem 6 of
72
). This means using
\mathbb{Q}
-valued cohomology does not loose any infomation. By taking tensor with
\mathbb{Q}
, we get the rational transgression

\tau\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\otimes\mathbb{Q}}}{{\longrightarrow}}\tau_{\mathbb{Q}}:H^{4}(BG,\mathbb{Q})\to H^{3}(LBG,\mathbb{Q})\ .

(134)

For
\mathbb{Q}
-valued cohomology, there is a convenient tool known as the Sullivan model
134
. The idea of Sullivan model is to take a commutative differential graded algebra (cdga) whose cohomology reproduces
H^{\bullet}(X,\mathbb{Q})
for some space
X
. In the following, we will assume
V
to be a graded vector space, and denote
V^{+}:=\bigoplus_{k\geq 1}V^{k}
. We also let
\Lambda^{k}V
denote the space spanned by
\{v_{1}\wedge\cdots\wedge v_{k}|v_{i}\in V\}
, and
\Lambda V=\bigoplus_{k}\Lambda^{k}V
.

Definition F.1

(Relative Sullivan model)
.

A relative Sullivan model is a morphism between commutative differential graded algebras (cdgas)

(B,d_{B})\hookrightarrow(B\otimes\Lambda V,d)\ ,\ b\mapsto b\otimes 1\ .

(135)

Definition F.2
.

A Sullivan model is a relative Sullivan model with

(B,d_{B})=(\mathbb{K},0)\hookrightarrow(\Lambda V,d)\ .

(136)

For connected compact Lie group
G
, the rational cohomology of
BG
admits a polynomial presentation

H^{\bullet}(BG,\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\cdots,x_{r}]\ ,\ {\rm deg}(x_{i})\in 2\mathbb{Z}

(137)

and the Sullivan model is

(\Lambda V,d)=(\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\cdots x_{r}],0)\ .

(138)

The Sullivan model of the free loop space
LBG
is given by (See Section 15 of
55
)

(\Lambda V\otimes\Lambda(sV),D)=(\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\cdots,x_{r}]\otimes\Lambda(sx_{1},\cdots sx_{r}),0)\ ,

(139)

where there is a map
S
relating
x_{i}
and
sx_{i}

\displaystyle S:x_{i}\mapsto sx_{i}\ ,\ {\rm deg}(sx_{i})={\rm deg}(s_{i})-1

(140)

\displaystyle S:sx_{i}\mapsto 0\ ,\ S(x_{i}x_{j})=S(x_{i})x_{j}+x_{i}S(x_{j})=(sx_{i})x_{j}+x_{i}(sx_{j})\ .

The free loop space cohomology is therefore

H^{3}(LBG,\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\cdots,x_{r}]\otimes\Lambda(sx_{1},\cdots sx_{r})\ .

(141)

Proposition F.3
.

Suppose
G
is a compact connected Lie group, the map
S:H^{4}(BG,\mathbb{Q})\to H^{3}(LBG,\mathbb{Q})
is injective.

Proof.

Suppose in
H^{4}(BG,\mathbb{Q})\cong\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\cdots,x_{r}]
, the degree
2
elements in
\{x_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[1,r]}
are denoted
\{y_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[1,m]}
and degree
4
elements are denoted
\{z_{i}\}_{i\in\mathbb{Z}\cap[1,n]}
.
A generic
\alpha\in H^{4}(BG,\mathbb{Q})
takes the form

\alpha=\sum_{a=1}^{n}A_{a}z_{a}+\sum_{1\leq j\leq k\leq m}B_{jk}y_{j}y_{k}.

(142)

Applying
S
map, we get

S(\alpha)=\sum_{a=1}^{n}A_{a}\,sz_{a}+\sum_{1\leq j<k\leq m}B_{jk}(y_{j}\,sy_{k}+y_{k}\,sy_{j})+\sum_{j=1}^{m}2B_{jj}y_{j}\,sy_{j}.

(143)

If
S(\alpha)=0
, then all
A_{a}=0
and for each
l
, we shall have

2B_{\ell\ell}y_{\ell}+\sum_{j<\ell}B_{j\ell}y_{j}+\sum_{k>\ell}B_{\ell k}y_{k}=0.

(144)

Knowing all
y_{i}
s are linearly independent, we proved
S(\alpha)=0\Rightarrow\alpha=0
. The map
S:H^{4}(BG,\mathbb{Q})\to H^{3}(LBG,\mathbb{Q})
is thus injective.
∎

We then demonstrate that the
S
map is actually the transgression. Consider the map

{\rm ev}:S^{1}\times LBG\ ,\ (t,\gamma)\mapsto\gamma(t)\ ,

(145)

where
t\in S^{1}
and
\gamma
is a loop on
BG
.The pullback of a cohomology class
\alpha\in H^{4}(BG,\mathbb{Q})
gives

{\rm ev}^{*}\alpha\in H^{4}(S^{1}\times LBG,\mathbb{Q})\ .

(146)

Integrating out along the
S^{1}
circle gives our desired rational transgression
137

\tau_{\mathbb{Q}}(\alpha)=\int_{S^{1}}{\rm ev}^{*}\alpha\in H^{3}(LBG,\mathbb{Q})\ .

(147)

How does this process look on the Sullivan model? A Sullivan model for
S^{1}
is just
(\Lambda(u),0)
, so the Sullivan model for
S^{1}\times LBG
is

(\Lambda(u),0)\otimes(\mathbb{Q}[x_{1},\cdots x_{r}]\otimes\Lambda[sx_{1},\cdots,sx_{r}],0)\ ,

(148)

giving the pullback of evaluation

{\rm ev}^{*}:v\mapsto v+u\,S(v)\ .

(149)

Evaluation around
S^{1}
is merely to take the coefficient of
u
, thus

\int_{S^{1}}{\rm ev}^{*}(\alpha)=S(\alpha)=\tau(\alpha)\ .

(150)

Thus we have:

Proposition F.4

Suppose
G

\tau_{\mathbb{Q}}:H^{4}(BG,\mathbb{Q})\to H^{3}(LBG,\mathbb{Q})

Suppose
