Long non-coding RNA SOCS2-AS1 inhibits gastric cancer progression via the miR-324-3p/GRIK3 axis: a mechanistic study
Authors: Houyun Zhang, Zhisheng Xia, Yuan Xu, Xiaolan Ouyang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGastric cancer (GC) remains a formidable threat to global health. Although SOCS2-AS1 is abnormally expressed in various cancers, its precise function and operational mechanisms in the context of GC have not been fully elucidated.MethodsThis study collected 102 pairs of GC and adjacent normal tissue samples and cultured multiple GC cell lines. SOCS2-AS1, miR-324-3p, and glutamate ionotropic receptor kainate type subunit 3 (GRIK3) expression were detected by qRT-PCR. Bioinformatics analysis was performed using databases such as GEPIA and Starbase. Functional experiments including CCK-8 assay and Transwell assay were conducted to evaluate cellular functions. Subcellular localization was determined using nuclear-cytoplasmic separation technology. Direct molecular interactions were validated via dual luciferase reporter assays.ResultsThe downregulation of SOCS2-AS1 in GC tissues and cell lines is significantly associated with an unfavorable patient prognosis. Overexpression of SOCS2-AS1 markedly suppressed GC cell proliferation, migration, invasion, and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) progression. Mechanistic studies revealed that SOCS2-AS1 targets miR-324-3p. and re-expression experiments confirmed its ability to reverse the anti-cancer effects of SOCS2-AS1. GRIK3 was identified as a direct downstream target of miR-324-3p. Ultimately, SOCS2-AS1 exerts its tumor-suppressive function through the miR-324-3p/GRIK3 axis.ConclusionThis research identifies SOCS2-AS1 as an anti-cancer lncRNA in GC and uncovers its mechanism of action via the SOCS2-AS1/miR-324-3p/GRIK3 axis in suppressing tumor progression. Thus, SOCS2-AS1 may serve as a potential diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target for GC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12957-026-04458-5.Keywords: Gastric cancer, SOCS2-AS1, miR-324-3p, GRIK3, ceRNA

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a malignant neoplasm that poses a major global health burden [1, 2]. Latest cancer statistics indicate that GC is classified among the most prevalent and deadly cancers worldwide, particularly imposing a high disease burden in East Asia [3, 4]. Despite significant advances in early diagnosis and comprehensive treatment over the past few years, GC’s subtle onset and non-specific early signs result in the vast majority of patients being identified at a late stage, leading to poor treatment outcomes and suboptimal five-year survival rates [5, 6]. The progression of GC is a sophisticated biological process that involves multiple stages and the involvement of multiple factors, encompassing various gene mutations, epigenetic alterations, and signaling pathway abnormalities [7]. Thus, discovering new diagnostic biomarkers as well as therapeutic targets is of great clinical significance for enhancing patient outcomes.

Long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have in recent years attracted considerable research interest for their function as fundamental regulators of gene expression in cancer [8]. They participate in tumorigenesis through multiple mechanisms, such as chromatin remodeling and post-transcriptional modifications [9]. In GC, multiple lncRNAs have been illustrated to serve as oncogenes or tumor suppressors [10]. For instance, by sequestering miR-671-5p, PVT1-214 upregulates the expression of SLC45A4, thereby promoting the proliferation of GC cells [11]. Conversely, SPINT1-AS1 is downregulated in GC; overexpression of SPINT1-AS1 inhibits tumor growth [12]. SOCS2-AS1 was selected as the focus of this study based on three lines of evidence. First, both the GEPIA and ENCORI databases consistently showed that SOCS2-AS1 expression is markedly reduced in GC tissues. Second, this downregulation was independently validated by a previous genome-wide lncRNA microarray study [13]. Third,, multiple studies indicate that SOCS2-AS1 exhibits abnormal expression patterns in various cancers, including thyroid and colorectal cancers [14, 15], suggesting it may be a tumor-associated lncRNA of significant research value. Despite these clues, the biological function and underlying mechanism of SOCS2-AS1 in GC have not been characterized. Therefore, we reasoned that SOCS2-AS1 may play an important role in GC progression, warranting detailed investigation.

Based on the above background, we hypothesize that SOCS2-AS1 exerts a tumor-suppressive role in gastric cancer. To test this hypothesis, we systematically investigated the expression of SOCS2-AS1 in clinical samples and cell lines, evaluated its clinical and prognostic significance, and explored its functional impact on malignant phenotypes and potential molecular mechanisms.

Materials and methods

Clinical tissue samples

From September 2021 to December 2022, a total of 102 pathologically confirmed GC specimens and their paired adjacent normal tissues (distant > 5 cm from the tumor) were collected from Shenzhen Baoan Shiyan People’s Hospital. None of the patients had undergone radiotherapy or chemotherapy before surgery, and all had signed informed consent forms. The study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Shenzhen Baoan Shiyan People’s Hospital and complies with the provisions of the Declaration of Helsinki. Postoperative tissue samples were stored at -80 °C pending RNA extraction. Overall survival data from the 5-year follow-up were recorded, and a survival analysis was conducted.

Cell culture

The normal human gastric epithelial cell line GES-1 (RRID: CVCL_EQ22) and GC cell lines, including AGS (RRID: CVCL_0139), HGC-27 (RRID: CVCL_1279), and MKN-45 (RRID: CVCL_0434), were all obtained from the Cell Bank of the Shanghai Institute for Biological Sciences. All cell lines were purchased between January and June 2021. Cell lines were identified via short tandem repeat (STR) analysis and tested to confirm they were free of mycoplasma. Mycoplasma testing was performed regularly (every three months) throughout the study. STR identification certificates and mycoplasma testing reports for all cell lines are provided in Supplementary Figure S1. All cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Invitrogen, USA) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ incubator.

Plasmid construction and viral transfection

An SOCS2-AS1 overexpression model was constructed by cloning its full-length cDNA into the pcDNA3.1 lentiviral vector. miR-324-3p mimic and inhibitor, as well as small interfering RNA targeting glutamate ionotropic receptor kainate type subunit 3 (GRIK3) (si-GRIK3) along with their corresponding controls, were obtained from RiboBio (China). Cells were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000.

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). Reverse transcription was performed using SuperScript II (Vazyme, China). Subsequently, qPCR was conducted using SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad, USA) on a CFX96 real-time PCR detection system. GAPDH and U6 were used as internal controls, and the 2–ΔΔCt method was applied to calculate relative gene levels. The primers used are listed in Supplementary Table 1.

Cellular function assays

Cell Proliferation Assay: Evaluated using the CCK-8 assay kit (Dojindo Molecular Technologies, Japan). Transfected cells were seeded in a 96-well plate. CCK-8 solution was added at days 0, 1, 2, and 3 post-transfections. After incubation for 2 h, absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

Cell Migration and Invasion Assay: Evaluated using Transwell chambers (Corning, USA). For migration assays, serum-free cell suspensions were added to the upper chamber. For invasion assays, the upper chamber basement membrane was pre-coated with Matrigel matrix gel. The lower chamber contained medium supplemented with 10% FBS as a chemotactic stimulus. After 24 h of culture, cells were fixed with methanol, stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and observed and counted under a microscope.

Caspase-3 activity analysis

Caspase-3 activity was measured using the Caspase-Glo 3 assay kit (Promega, USA). Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates and cultured in a 37 °C, 5% CO₂ incubator for 24 h. After designated treatments, the culture plates were equilibrated to room temperature. Subsequently, Caspase-Glo 3 reagent was added to each well containing of culture medium. The plates were gently mixed at 500 rpm for 30 s and incubated for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. Luminescence intensity was recorded using a microplate reader (BioTek, USA).

Bioinformatics analysis

Expression data for SOCS2-AS1, miR-324-3p, and GRIK3 in GC versus normal tissues were downloaded from the GEPIA and ENCORI databases. The lncRNASNP2 database predicted binding sites for SOCS2-AS1 and miR-324-3p, and those between miR-324-3p and GRIK3 were forecasted via the TargetScan database.

Nucleoplasm separation assay

Cells were fractionated using the PARIS nucleoplasm separation assay kit (Life Technologies, USA). U6 and GAPDH served as nuclear and cytoplasmic controls, respectively. The subcellular localization of lncRNA SOCS2-AS1 was determined by qRT-PCR analysis of nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay

Putative miR-324-3p binding sites in SOCS2-AS1 (or GRIK3 3’UTR) were cloned as wild-type (WT) or mutant (MUT) fragments into the pmirGLO vector. These reporter plasmids were co-transfected with miR-324-3p mimics/inhibitors or their controls into AGS and HGC-27 cells, followed by luciferase activity measurement after 48 h using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, USA).

RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay

To validate the interactions between SOCS2-AS1, miR-324-3p, and GRIK3, RIP experiments were performed using the EZ-Magna RIP Kit (Millipore, USA). Briefly, after cell lysis, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with magnetic beads labeled with anti-Ago2 antibody or control IgG. Subsequently, the precipitated complexes were treated with proteinase K to remove proteins, and the purified RNA was used for qRT-PCR analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 22.0 and GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student’ s t-test, while comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. All quantitative data were tested for normality using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Data that followed a normal distribution are presented as mean ± SD and analyzed using parametric tests. For data that did not follow a normal distribution, nonparametric tests were used. No additional data standardization was performed. The diagnostic value was evaluated by means of ROC curves. The optimal cutoff value is determined using the Joden index (J = sensitivity + specificity – 1) to maximize the sum of sensitivity and specificity. Survival curves were plotted using the Kaplan-Meier method. Independent prognostic factors were assessed using multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Decreased expression of SOCS2-AS1 in GC predicts unfavorable patient survival

Analyses of the GEPIA and Starbase databases indicated that SOCS2-AS1 expression was remarkably reduced in GC (Fig. 1A-B). This finding was corroborated by qRT-PCR results from our clinical cohort (102 matched pairs), which showed substantial downregulation of SOCS2-AS1 in GC tissues (0.44-fold, 95% CI: -0.64–-0.49, P < 0.001, Fig. 1C). Furthermore, SOCS2-AS1 expression levels were lower in multiple GC cells (MKN-45: 0.68-fold, 95% CI: 0.13–0.51, P < 0.001; HGC-27: 0.50-fold, 95% CI: 0.31–0.69, P < 0.001; AGS: 0.56-fold, 95% CI: 0.25–0.63, P < 0.001, Fig. 1D).

Subsequently, we evaluated the clinical significance of SOCS2-AS1. Low SOCS2-AS1 levels were notably correlated with lymph node metastasis (χ² = 4.71, P = 0.024) and TNM staging (χ² = 3.90, P = 0.038) (Table 1). ROC curve analysis demonstrated that SOCS2-AS1 expression levels exhibit excellent diagnostic discriminatory ability for GC (AUC = 0.9279, sensitivity = 86.27%, specificity = 88.24%, 95% CI: 0.89–0.97, Fig. 2A). Patients were divided into SOCS2-AS1 high-expression and low-expression groups based on the mean expression level as the cutoff value. Patients with low SOCS2-AS1 levels had a markedly worse overall survival, as determined by Kaplan-Meier analysis (χ² = 5.20, Log Rank P = 0.023, Fig. 2B). Multivariate Cox regression analysis confirmed SOCS2-AS1 as an independent prognostic indicator in GC patients (HR = 0.315, 95% CI: 0.14–0.73, P = 0.007, Fig. 2C).

Overexpression of SOCS2-AS1 suppresses the malignant phenotype of GC cells

For the purpose of elucidating the biological role of SOCS2-AS1, we successfully established SOCS2-AS1 overexpression models in AGS and HGC-27 cells via lentiviral transfection (AGS: 2.71-fold, 95% CI: -1.92–-1.53, P < 0.001; HGC-27: 2.56-fold, 95% CI: -1.78–-1.39, P < 0.001, Fig. 3A). Functional experiments revealed that SOCS2-AS1 overexpression substantially inhibited GC cell proliferation (AGS: 0.57-fold, 95% CI: 0.61–0.90, P < 0.001; HGC-27: 0.50-fold, 95% CI: 0.70–0.92, P < 0.001, Fig. 3B-C), and increased Caspase-3 activity (AGS: 3.55-fold, 95% CI: -2.85–-1.84, P < 0.001; HGC-27: 3.13-fold, 95% CI: -2.53–-1.53, P < 0.001, Fig. 3D). Moreover, SOCS2-AS1 overexpression suppressed cell migration ( AGS: 0.42-fold, 95% CI: 39.69–75.51, P < 0.001; HGC-27: 0.48-fold, 95% CI: 35.09–70.91, P < 0.001, Fig. 3E), and invasion capacity (AGS: 0.48-fold, 95% CI: 31.19–70.01, P < 0.001; HGC-27: 0.44-fold, 95% CI: 38.99–78.81, P < 0.001, Fig. 3F). Furthermore, overexpression of SOCS2-AS1 up-regulated E-cadherin (AGS: 3.08-fold, 95% CI: -2.31–-1.89; HGC-27: 2.74-fold, 95% CI: -2.03–-1.50) while down-regulating the homotypic markers N-cadherin (AGS: 0.42-fold, 95% CI: 0.36–0.78; HGC-27: 0.35-fold, 95% CI: 0.44–0.97) and MMP9 (AGS: 0.37-fold, 95% CI: 0.42–0.84 ; HGC-27: 0.43-fold, 95% CI: 0.32–0.85, all P < 0.001, Fig. 3G-H).

SOCS2-AS1 targeted to miR-324-3p

Cytoplasmic-nuclear fractionation experiments confirmed the predominant cytoplasmic localization of SOCS2-AS1 (Fig. 4A-B), while bioinformatics tools predicted the binding sites between SOCS2-AS1 and miR-324-3p (Fig. 4C). In DLR assays, miR-324-3p overexpression remarkably reduced luciferase activity in the wild-type SOCS2-AS1 reporter vector but exerted no impact on the mutant vector (P < 0.001, Fig. 4D-E). The RIP experiment also confirmed the interaction between the two (P < 0.001, Fig. 4F). The Starbase database and our clinical samples (2.52-fold, 95% CI: 1.17–1.48) both demonstrated a significant upregulation of miR-324-3p levels in GC (P < 0.001, Fig. 4G-H). Besides, an inverse correlation was observed between SOCS2-AS1 and miR-324-3p expression in GC tissues (r = -0.562, 95% CI: -0.69–-0.42, P < 0.001, Fig. 4I). Concurrently, ectopic expression of SOCS2-AS1 led to the suppression of miR-324-3p in GC cells (AGS: 0.43-fold, 95% CI: 0.45–0.75; HGC-27: 0.47-fold, 95% CI: 0.39–0.68, all P < 0.001, Fig. 4J).

SOCS2-AS1 exerts anticancer effects by targeting miR-324-3p

Co-transfection of miR-324-3p mimic in SOCS2-AS1-overexpressing cells partially reversed the downregulation of miR-324-3p caused by SOCS2-AS1 overexpression (AGS: 2.03-fold, 95% CI: -0.56–-0.29; HGC-27: 1.57-fold, 95% CI: -0.40–-0.13, all P < 0.001, Fig. 5A). More importantly, restoring miR-324-3p expression efficiently rescued the suppression of GC cell proliferation (AGS: 1.62-fold, 95% CI: -0.69–-0.42; HGC-27: 1.45-fold, 95% CI: -0.58–-0.30, all P < 0.001, Fig. 5B-C), and decreased Caspase-3 activity (AGS: 0.48-fold, 95% CI: 0.91–1.95; HGC-27: 0.59-fold, 95% CI: 0.52–1.55, all P < 0.001, Fig. 5D). Besides, restoration of miR-324-3p expression rescued migration (AGS: 1.59-fold, 95% CI: -45.89–-13.71; HGC-27: 1.73-fold, 95% CI: -45.69–-13.51, all P < 0.001, Fig. 5E), invasion (AGS: 1.91-fold, 95% CI: -50.94–-25.86; HGC-27: 1.69-fold, 95% CI: -43.54–-18.46, all P < 0.001, Fig. 5F), and epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT)-related protein expression, including E-cadherin (AGS: 0.81-fold, 95% CI: 0.22–0.79, P < 0.001; HGC-27: 0.62-fold, 95% CI: 0.82–1.44, P < 0.01), N-cadherin (AGS: 1.68-fold, 95% CI: -0.60–-0.03, P = 0.025; HGC-27: 1.82-fold, 95% CI: -0.62–-0.01, P = 0.044) and MMP9 (AGS: 2.63-fold, 95% CI: -0.75–-0.18, P < 0.001; HGC-27: 2.00-fold, 95% CI: -0.71–-0.09, P = 0.006). miR-324-3p directly targets GRIK3

We conducted further investigation into the downstream targets of miR-324-3p. TargetScan database predicted binding sites between miR-324-3p and GRIK3 (Fig. 6A). DLR assays and RIP experiment confirmed that miR-324-3p directly binds to GRIK3 (P < 0.001, Fig. 6B-D). Analysis of GEPIA and Starbase databases revealed that GRIK3 expression is downregulated in GC (Fig. 6E-F). GRIK3 was also underexpressed in GC tissues (0.44-fold, 95% CI: -0.67–-0.49, P < 0.001, Fig. 6G), and its expression was inversely associated with miR-324-3p levels (r=-0.623, 95% CI: -0.73–-0.49, P < 0.001, Fig. 6H). Furthermore, overexpression of miR-324-3p suppressed GRIK3 levels (AGS: 0.50-fold, 95% CI: 0.36–0.61; HGC-27: 0.47-fold, 95% CI: 0.40–0.65, all P < 0.001, Fig. 6I).

miR-324-3p drives gc progression via GRIK3 suppression

Finally, we validated the miR-324-3p/GRIK3 axis. Silencing GRIK3 rescued the elevated GRIK3 expression induced by the miR-324-3p inhibitor (AGS: 0.61-fold, 95% CI: 0.52–1.48; HGC-27: 0.57-fold, 95% CI: 0.59–1.55, all P < 0.001, Fig. 7A). Concurrently, GRIK3 inhibition significantly attenuated the inhibitory effects of miR-324-3p inhibitor on GC cell proliferation (AGS: 1.37-fold, 95% CI: -0.45–-0.19; HGC-27: 1.33-fold, 95% CI: -0.44–-0.19, all P < 0.001, Fig. 7B-C), and decreased Caspase-3 activity (AGS: 0.61-fold, 95% CI: 0.55–1.61; HGC-27: 0.45-fold, 95% CI: 1.22–2.29, all P < 0.001, Fig. 7D). Moreover, GRIK3 inhibition rescued migration (AGS: 1.55-fold, 95% CI: -40.30–-11.70; HGC-27: 1.44-fold, 95% CI: -37.50–-8.91, all P < 0.001, Fig. 7E), invasion (AGS: 1.89-fold, 95% CI: -55.64–-21.56; HGC-27: 1.66-fold, 95% CI: -43.44–-9.36, all P < 0.001, Fig. 7F), and EMT progression, including E-cadherin (AGS: 0.74-fold, 95% CI: 0.39–0.97, P < 0.001; HGC-27: 0.75-fold, 95% CI: 0.33–0.98, P < 0.001), N-cadherin (AGS: 1.96-fold, 95% CI: -0.65–-0.07, P = 0.007; HGC-27: 1.87-fold, 95% CI: -0.68–-0.03, P = 0.026) and MMP9 (AGS: 1.72-fold, 95% CI: -0.64–-0.06, P = 0.012; HGC-27: 1.82-fold, 95% CI: -0.68–-0.03, P = 0.028). Discussion

The development of GC is associated with many risk factors, including dietary factors and Helicobacter pylori infection, among others [16]. Currently, surgery combined with chemoradiotherapy remains the primary treatment strategy for GC. However, achieving curative surgery is challenging, and patients often face survival bottlenecks due to postoperative recurrence and metastasis [17]. Thus, in-depth investigation of the molecular mechanisms involved in GC to identify prognostic biomarkers and therapeutic targets has become crucial, thereby advancing clinical practice. Our research systematically investigated the expression and molecular mechanisms of the SOCS2-AS1 in GC. SOCS2-AS1 exhibited significantly reduced expression in GC, and its downregulation was closely associated with unfavorable prognosis in patients. In terms of function, SOCS2-AS1 directly binds miR-324-3p in vitro, relieving its suppression of the downstream target gene GRIK3 and effectively inhibiting multiple malignant phenotypes of GC cells.

Multiple studies indicate that lncRNAs are pivotal in cancer development and are considered highly promising therapeutic targets with excellent research prospects [18]. Numerous abnormally expressed lncRNAs contribute to disease mechanisms and prognostic disparities [19]. For instance, lncRNAs such as TRG-AS1 and SNHG4 serve as novel poor prognostic markers for GC and target miRNA regulation to drive malignant behavior [20, 21]. Although imaging techniques such as CT, MRI, and PET/CT are essential for disease assessment and treatment monitoring [22], they still have limitations. Combining imaging techniques with lncRNA detection may offer a more comprehensive strategy for the management of gastric cancer. Our study found that SOCS2-AS1 is significantly downregulated in GC and holds potential for diagnostic and prognostic purposes. Through in vitro overexpression models, we established that SOCS2-AS1 acts as a tumor suppressor by obviously inhibiting the malignant phenotype of GC cells. EMT is a critical step for cancer cells to acquire migratory and invasive capabilities [23, 24]. SOCS2-AS1 overexpression upregulates E-cadherin while downregulating N-cadherin and MMP9, clearly indicating its role in inhibiting tumor metastasis by reversing the EMT process. These results strongly demonstrate that SOCS2-AS1 has a tumor-suppressive role in GC.

At the molecular mechanism level, cytoplasmic lncRNAs often function via the ceRNA mechanism [25]. Our subcellular localization results indicated that SOCS2-AS1 is primarily localized in the cytoplasm, suggesting it may exert its function by sponge-like binding to miRNAs. Further screening for SOCS2-AS1-interacting miRNAs revealed and validated miR-324-3p as a direct binding partner. Previous studies indicate that miR-324-3p is involved in tumorigenic processes across multiple cancers, including ovarian and nasopharyngeal carcinomas [26, 27]. Crucially, miR-324-3p exerts a pro-tumorigenic effect in GC [28]. Reversion experiments validated that miR-324-3p overexpression rescues the tumor-suppressive phenotypes imposed by SOCS2-AS1 in GC cells, corroborating the initial finding of its elevated expression and inverse correlation with SOCS2-AS1 in clinical specimens. Although this study focuses on miR-324-3p as a direct target of SOCS2-AS1 in gastric cancer, SOCS2-AS1 may also bind to other miRNAs (such as miR-454-3p and miR-1264). Our preliminary screening indicates that miR-324-3p has a high predicted binding score among miRNAs associated with gastric cancer and has a established research background in this field. Future studies utilizing sequencing methods such as CLIP-seq will help comprehensively identify the miRNA profile bound by SOCS2-AS1 in gastric cancer cells.

To map the complete regulatory axis, we further explored downstream targets of miR-324-3p and identified GRIK3 as a direct target. GRIK3, a glutamate receptor, has demonstrated oncogenic roles in breast and colorectal cancers [29, 30]. Our findings identify GRIK3, frequently downregulated in GC, as the key downstream effector that mechanistically links miR-324-3p to enhanced tumor proliferation and metastasis. This finding is in line with the work of Gong et al., who also reported an association between GRIK3 and unfavorable prognosis in GC [31]. Mechanistically, in gastric cancer, GRIK3 may participate in malignant progression through the UBE2C/CDK1/Wnt signaling axis, as previous studies have shown that its downregulation releases Wnt/β-catenin activity to drive the progression of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [32]. Further research is needed to clarify the specific role of GRIK3 in downstream signaling pathways in gastric cancer cells. Qverall, we identified a novel regulatory pathway: SOCS2-AS1 binds to miR-324-3p, thereby releasing its inhibitory effect on GRIK3 and consequently suppressing the progression of gastric cancer. Although the role of the ceRNA mechanism in gastric cancer has been well documented, this study offers several unique insights. First, no previous study has demonstrated that SOCS2-AS1 is involved in the development and progression of gastric cancer. Second, previous studies have shown that SOCS2-AS1 promotes the progression of papillary thyroid carcinoma by destabilizing the p53 protein and inhibits the progression of colorectal cancer by stabilizing SOCS2 and trapping miR-1264. In contrast, our study reveals a distinct mechanism in gastric cancer: SOCS2-AS1 acts via the miR-324-3p/GRIK3 axis. These differing roles suggest that SOCS2-AS1 functions in a cancer-type-specific manner, which may depend on the expression patterns of its interacting partners. The SOCS2-AS1/miR-324-3p/GRIK3 axis offers potential opportunities for therapeutic intervention. For example, restoring SOCS2-AS1 expression via CRISPR activation (CRISPRa) or inhibiting miR-324-3p using antagomiRs may serve as new strategies to suppress GC progression, although these approaches require further validation in preclinical models.

In summary, our findings not only reveal the critical role of the SOCS2-AS1/miR-324-3p/GRIK3 regulatory axis in GC development but also provide theoretical support for utilizing SOCS2-AS1 as a potential diagnostic biomarker and prognostic indicator. These insights also pave the way for developing novel therapies targeting this pathway.
