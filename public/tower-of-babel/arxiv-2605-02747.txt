Functional perimeter and the dimensional Brunn-Minkowski inequality for log-concave measures

1
Introduction

The classical Brunn–Minkowski inequality asserts that for every pair of nonempty compact sets
K,L
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and every
\lambda\in(0,1)
, we have

(1.1)

\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\lambda K+(1-\lambda)L)^{\frac{1}{n}}\geqslant\lambda\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{\frac{1}{n}}+(1-\lambda)\mathrm{vol}_{n}(L)^{\frac{1}{n}},

where
\mathrm{vol}_{n}
denotes the
n
-dimensional Lebesgue measure. A central problem in modern convex geometry is to understand to what extent this dimensional concavity property extends beyond Lebesgue measure.

A natural framework for such extensions is provided by log-concave measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Recall that a Borel measure
\mu
with density
f=e^{-\psi}
is log-concave if
\psi
is convex. This class includes, on the one hand, uniform measures on convex bodies and, on the other hand, Gaussian-type distributions, and plays a fundamental role in convex geometry, analysis, and probability. The dimensional Brunn–Minkowski conjecture asks whether the concavity in (
1.1
) persists for all log-concave measures under symmetry assumptions. More precisely, if
\mu
is an even log-concave measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, does one have

(1.2)

\mu(\lambda K+(1-\lambda)L)^{\frac{1}{n}}\geqslant\lambda\mu(K)^{\frac{1}{n}}+(1-\lambda)\mu(L)^{\frac{1}{n}}

for every pair of symmetric convex sets
K,L
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and every
\lambda\in(0,1)
?

The conjecture was first formulated by Gardner and Zvavitch
[
27
]
in the Gaussian setting, where it was later confirmed by Eskenazis and Moschidis
[
23
]
. The general formulation mentioned here is due to Colesanti, Livshyts and Marsiglietti
[
18
]
. Further important progress was obtained by Cordero-Erausquin and Rotem
[
21
]
, who verified (
1.2
) for all rotationally invariant log-concave measures. It is also known that the conjecture follows from the logarithmic Brunn–Minkowski conjecture of Böröczky, Lutwak, Yang and Zhang
[
10
]
in every fixed dimension (see
[
47
]
), and is therefore valid in dimension
n=2
. More recently, a series of works
[
1
,
19
,
48
]
have also investigated functional forms of the conjecture.

In Section
3
we revisit an analytic mechanism due to Kolesnikov and E. Milman
[
38
,
39
]
, which has been the primary method towards weighted Brunn–Minkowski-type inequalities for measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
under central symmetry assumptions since the later work of Kolesnikov and Livshyts
[
37
]
. This method reduces (
1.2
) to coercivity estimates for the diffusion operator

Lu=\Delta u-\langle\nabla u,\nabla\psi\rangle

associated with
\mu
. The key point is that Brunn–Minkowski inequalities can be derived from their infinitesimal versions corresponding to perturbations of convex sets which are expressed via support functions and linearization along Minkowski combinations. This leads to differential inequalities involving curvature and weighted boundary integrals, which are ultimately controlled by spectral
\Gamma_{2}
-type quantities of the form

\int_{K}\big(\|\nabla^{2}u\|^{2}+\langle\nabla^{2}\psi\,\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle\big)\,d\mu

for solutions of appropriately chosen second-order elliptic equations.

Using this framework, Livshyts
[
45
]
proved a version of (
1.2
) for all even log-concave measures, with exponent
c_{n}=n^{-4-o_{n}(1)}
. Our first main result improves this bound.

Theorem 1.1
.

Let
\mu
be an even log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Then, for every pair of symmetric convex sets
K,L
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and every
\lambda\in(0,1)
,

(1.3)

\mu(\lambda K+(1-\lambda)L)^{c_{n}}\geqslant\lambda\mu(K)^{c_{n}}+(1-\lambda)\mu(L)^{c_{n}},

where

(1.4)

c_{n}\geqslant\frac{c}{n^{3}\ln n}.

Our approach is based on a refined analysis of the mechanism underlying the method of
[
38
,
39
,
45
]
. A central role is played by the control of the gradient of the potential
\psi
. More precisely, one seeks large subsets on which
|\nabla\psi|
is bounded, since such bounds translate into coercivity estimates for the operator
L
, as was already observed in
[
45
]
. This leads naturally to the study of the quantity

(1.5)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\psi(x)|\,d\mu(x),

for isotropic log-concave probability measures, which is interpreted as the
functional perimeter
of the log-concave function
e^{-\psi}
(see
[
17
,
20
,
54
,
55
]
). At the same time, we revisit the argument of Livshyts, clarifying some subtle points and providing the necessary justifications. This yields a complete and self-contained proof of Theorem
1.1
, together with an improved exponent.

To describe our second main result, for any convex body
A
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, consider the
\mu
-perimeter of
A
defined by

\mu^{+}(\partial A)=\liminf_{\varepsilon\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mu(A+\varepsilon B_{2}^{n})-\mu(A)}{\varepsilon}.

If
\mu
admits a density
f
with respect to Lebesgue measure, then

\mu^{+}(\partial A)=\int_{\partial A}f(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x).

The
maximal perimeter
of the measure
\mu
is defined as

\Gamma(\mu)=\sup\{\mu^{+}(\partial A):\ A\mbox{ is a convex body in }\mathbb{R}^{n}\}.

This quantity has been studied for the Gaussian measure by Ball and Nazarov
[
7
,
52
]
and for rotationally invariant log-concave measures by Livshyts
[
41
,
42
,
43
]
. In a more recent work
[
44
]
, Livshyts studied the maximal perimeter
\Gamma(\mu)
of isotropic measures
\mu
establishing a lower bound which, together with the resolution of the thin shell conjecture by Klartag and Lehec
[
36
]
and the result of Nazarov
[
52
]
, yields

\gamma_{n}=\inf\{\Gamma(\mu):\ \mu\ \mbox{is an isotropic log-concave probability measure on }\mathbb{R}^{n}\}\approx n^{1/4}.

For the corresponding supremal quantity

\Gamma_{n}=\sup\{\Gamma(\mu):\ \mu\ \mbox{is an isotropic log-concave probability measure on }\mathbb{R}^{n}\},

an upper bound
\Gamma_{n}\leqslant Cn^{2}
was established in
[
44
]
which was recently improved to
\Gamma_{n}\leqslant Cn^{3/2}
in
[
12
]
. We provide
an asymptotically sharp bound for
\Gamma_{n}
.

Theorem 1.2
.

For every
n\in\mathbb{N}
, we have
\Gamma_{n}\approx n
.

Both our main results rely upon the following asymptotically sharp upper bound for the
L_{1}
norm of the gradient of the logarithmic potential (
1.5
), which was first proven in
[
22
, Lemma 11]
.

Theorem 1.3

(Eldan and Klartag)
.

Let
\mu
be an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with density
f=e^{-\psi}
. Then,

(1.6)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\psi(x)|\,d\mu(x)\leqslant Cn,

where
C>0
is an absolute constant.

The fact that Theorem
1.3
was proved in
[
22
]
was brought to our attention by B. Klartag after the first online posting of the present paper. In Section
5
below, we present a new proof of this result which was obtained independently of the work of Eldan and Klartag. Our proof follows a functional and variational approach. The key idea is to study infinitesimal perturbations of
f
via the Asplund product and dilation, and to identify the first variation both at the pointwise level and after integration. This leads to inequalities connecting
\int|\nabla\psi|\,d\mu
with entropy-type expressions involving
f
. Combining these estimates with isotropic normalization and convexity arguments yields the desired linear bound for (
1.5
). We refer to Section
5
for further remarks related to Theorem
1.3
, including lower bounds and improved bounds under additional symmetry (e.g., in the radial case, see Proposition
5.5
).

It follows readily from Theorem
1.3
and Markov’s inequality that if
\mu
is an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then there exists a Borel set
A
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mu(A)\geqslant c>0
such that

|\nabla\psi(x)|\leqslant Cn\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for all }x\in A,

where
c,C>0
are absolute constants. A crucial difficulty, which is pertinent to Theorem
1.1
, is that such sets need not be convex, which prevents a direct application of the analytic machinery underlying the approach of
[
45
]
. This reveals a fundamental obstruction: further progress on the dimensional Brunn–Minkowski conjecture requires the construction of large symmetric
convex
subsets on which
|\nabla\psi|
is well controlled.

Our final contribution consists of a series of applications and reformulations of Theorem
1.3
, highlighting its geometric and functional consequences. A unifying principle is that the quantity
\int|\nabla\psi|\,d\mu
governs a range of geometric features associated with log-concave functions.

In §6.1, Theorem
1.3
is interpreted in the framework of moment measures. While it is known that the moment measure
\mu_{f}

of a log-concave function
f
with finite positive integral has finite first moment under mild assumptions, a fact that plays
a central role in the characterization of moment measures established by Cordero-Erausquin and Klartag
[
20
]
, the estimate (
1.6
) yields
the sharp quantitative bound

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|y|\,d\mu_{f}(y)\leqslant Cn,

for isotropic log-concave functions
f
.

In §6.2, the estimate of Theorem
1.3
is applied to the study of the pair of surface area measures
(\mu_{f},\nu_{f})
associated to a log-concave function,
in the sense of functional convexity (see
[
54
,
55
]
). Here
\mu_{f}
coincides with the moment measure if
f
is essentially continuous, while
\nu_{f}
captures boundary contributions through the Gauss map of the support of
f
and appears naturally in first variation formulas of Minkowski type. Building on the previous gradient estimates, we derive the following general theorem.

Theorem 1.4
.

Let
\mu_{f}
and
\nu_{f}
be the surface area measures of an isotropic log-concave function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|y|\,d\mu_{f}(y)+\nu_{f}(S^{n-1})\leqslant Cn

for some absolute constant
C>0
.

Theorem
1.4
contains and complements the bound for the moment measure
\mu_{f}
, providing uniform control on both components of the functional surface area.
This shows that, in the isotropic setting, the total mass of these measures is again of order
n
, reinforcing the idea that
Theorem
1.3
captures a fundamental dimensional constraint in the functional extension of classical convex geometric notions. Due to a generalized version of the co-area formula (see (
2.2
)), Theorem
1.4
equivalently asserts that

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{x:\ f(x)\geqslant t\})\,dt\leqslant Cn,

for every isotropic log-concave function
f
(see Corollary
6.4
). This is crucial for the proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Finally, in §6.3, the focus shifts to projections of log-concave functions. For an isotropic log-concave density
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we consider its projections onto
hyperplanes and prove that the average
L^{1}
-norm of these projections is of order
\sqrt{n}
, which is optimal with respect to the dimension.
This result can be viewed as a functional counterpart of classical estimates for volumes of projections of convex bodies, and is obtained by combining gradient bounds with Cauchy’s surface area formula and a geometric
interpretation of level sets.

The paper is organized as follows. In Section
2
we collect background material on convex bodies and isotropic log-concave measures. In Section
3
we develop the analytic approach to the dimensional Brunn–Minkowski problem and prove Theorem
1.1
. In Section
4
we prove Theorem
1.2
. In Section
5
we present a new proof of the gradient estimate of Theorem
1.3
and related remarks. Finally, in Section
6
we present applications and further geometric consequences of these main bounds.

2
Background information and auxiliary results

We work in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, equipped with the standard inner product
\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle
.
The associated Euclidean norm is denoted by
|\cdot|
, the Euclidean unit ball by
B_{2}^{n}
, and the Euclidean unit sphere by
S^{n-1}
.
Lebesgue measure in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is denoted by
\mathrm{vol}_{n}
, and we write
\omega_{n}=\mathrm{vol}_{n}(B_{2}^{n})
for the volume of the Euclidean unit ball.
We denote by
\sigma
the rotationally invariant probability measure on
S^{n-1}
. The Grassmann manifold
G_{n,k}
of
k
-dimensional subspaces
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is equipped with the Haar probability measure
\nu_{n,k}
.

Throughout the paper, the symbols
C,c,c^{\prime},c_{1},c_{2},\ldots
denote absolute positive constants whose values may change from line to line.
Whenever we write
a\approx b
, we mean that there exist absolute constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that
c_{1}a\leqslant b\leqslant c_{2}a
.

§2.1. Convex bodies.
A convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a compact convex set
K
with nonempty interior.
It is called symmetric if
K=-K
, and centered if its barycenter
\operatorname{bar}(K)=\frac{1}{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)}\int_{K}x\,dx
is at the origin.
For every convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
we denote by
\overline{K}
the homothetic copy of
K
scaled to have unit volume,
namely
\overline{K}:=\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{-1/n}K
.

Let
K
be a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
0\in{\rm int}(K)
.
The radial function of
K
is defined by
\varrho_{K}(x)=\max\{t>0:tx\in K\}
for all nonzero
x
, and the support function of
K
is given by

h_{K}(x)=\max\{\langle x,y\rangle:y\in K\}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. The Minkowski functional
of
K
is defined by
\|x\|_{K}=\inf\{t>0:\ x\in tK\}
for all
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. If
K
is symmetric then
\|\cdot\|_{K}
is a norm on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

The polar body
K^{\circ}
of a convex body
K
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with
0\in{\rm int}(K)
is the convex body

K^{\circ}:=\bigl\{y\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:\langle x,y\rangle\leqslant 1\;\hbox{for all}\;x\in K\bigr\}.

The surface area
S(K)
of
K
is defined by

S(K):=\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial K),

where
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
denotes the
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure on the boundary
\partial K
of
K
.

A convex body
K
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
is called isotropic if it has volume
1
, is centered, and its covariance matrix is a multiple of the identity.
Equivalently, there exists a constant
L_{K}>0
, called the isotropic constant of
K
, such that

\|\langle\cdot,\xi\rangle\|_{L_{2}(K)}^{2}:=\int_{K}\langle x,\xi\rangle^{2}dx=L_{K}^{2}\hskip 9.24994pt\text{for all}\;\xi\in S^{n-1}.

We shall use a number of geometric properties of isotropic convex bodies. For instance, it is known that
L_{K}\geqslant L_{B_{2}^{n}}\geqslant c
for an absolute constant
c>0
. A proof of this assertion may be found e.g. in
[
13
, Chapter 3]
.

Bourgain’s slicing problem
[
11
]
asks whether there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that

(2.1)

L_{n}:=\max\{L_{K}:K\ \hbox{is an isotropic convex body in}\ \mathbb{R}^{n}\}\leqslant C.

An affirmative solution was recently obtained by Klartag and Lehec
[
35
]
, following an important
contribution by Guan
[
32
]
(see also
[
8
]
for an alternative proof).
Consequently,
L_{K}\approx 1
, uniformly in
n
, for every isotropic convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For further background, we refer to the survey
[
30
]
.

§2.2. Log-concave functions.
A function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow[0,\infty)
is called log-concave if its support
K_{f}=\overline{\{f>0\}}

is a convex set in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and the restriction of
\ln{f}
to it is concave.
We denote by
{\rm LC}_{n}
the class of all upper semi-continuous log-concave functions. Note
that the class of convex bodies in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
embeds naturally into
{\rm LC}_{n}
using the map

K\mapsto\mathds{1}_{K}
.

A log-concave function
f\in{\rm LC}_{n}
is essentially continuous if the set of its discontinuity points has zero measure
for the
(n-1)
-dimensional Hausdorff measure. This is equivalent to the assumption that
f\in W^{1,1}(\mathbb{R}^{n})
(see
[
54
, Remark 3.3]
).
For every
f\in{\rm LC}_{n}
with
0<\int f<\infty
we have

(2.2)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{x:f(x)\geqslant t\})\,dt=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla f(x)|\,dx+\int_{\partial K_{f}}f(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)

(see
[
54
, Theorem 3.2]
). In particular,
f
is essentially continuous if and only if we have the co-area formula

(2.3)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla f(x)|\,dx=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{x:f(x)\geqslant t\})\,dt.

We emphasize that
[
54
, Theorem 3.2]
in fact contains a misprint, as the term
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{x:f(x)\geqslant t\})
in the co-area formula is replaced by
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\{x:f(x)=t\})
. We confirmed with the author of
[
54
]
that this was due to an erroneous reproduction of a formula from
[
24
, Section 5.5]
. It does not affect any further results from that paper and shall be used in this form in the sequel.

Let
f=e^{-\psi}\in{\rm LC}_{n}
. The Legendre transform of
\psi
is

\mathcal{L}\psi(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\{\langle x,y\rangle-\psi(y)\}.

It is always a convex, lower semi-continuous function, and satisfies the involution property

\mathcal{L}(\mathcal{L}\psi)=\psi
if
\psi
is lower semi-continuous, convex and proper (meaning
that
\mathrm{dom}(\psi):=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:\psi(x)<\infty\}\neq\varnothing
).

Given two log-concave functions
f=e^{-\psi}
and
g=e^{-\varphi}
in
{\rm LC}_{n}
, we define the sup-convolution or
Asplund product of
f
and
g
by

(f\star g)(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(y)\,g(x-y)=\exp\!\big(-(\psi\square\varphi)(x)\big),

where the inf-convolution of two convex functions
\psi
and
\varphi
is

(\psi\square\varphi)(x)=\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big\{\psi(y)+\varphi(x-y)\big\}.

For
t>0
and a log-concave function
f\in{\rm LC}_{n}
, the functional dilation is defined by

(t\cdot f)(x)=f\big(x/t\big)^{t}.

This transformation respects log-concavity and is a natural functional counterpart of geometric dilation.

§2.3. Isotropic log-concave probability measures.
We say that a Borel probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is log-concave
if
\mu(H)<1
for every hyperplane
H
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(we then say that
\mu
is full-dimensional) and
\mu(\lambda A+(1-\lambda)B)\geqslant\mu(A)^{\lambda}\mu(B)^{1-\lambda}
for any pair of compact sets
A,B
in
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
and any
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Borell
[
9
]
has proved that, under these assumptions,
\mu
has a log-concave density
f
. The Brunn-Minkowski inequality implies that if
K
is a convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
then the indicator function
\mathds{1}_{K}
of
K
is the density of a log-concave measure, the Lebesgue measure on
K
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
be a log-concave function with finite, positive integral. Its barycenter is defined by

\operatorname{bar}(f)=\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}x\,f(x)\,dx}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\,dx}.

We say that
f
is centered if
\operatorname{bar}(f)=0
.
We shall use the following result of Fradelizi
[
26
]
: if
f
is a centered log-concave density on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, then

(2.4)

\|f\|_{\infty}\leqslant e^{n}f(0).

The isotropic constant of a log-concave function
f
with finite positive integral is the affine-invariant quantity

(2.5)

L_{f}:=\left(\frac{\|f\|_{\infty}}{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\,dx}\right)^{1/n}\det(\operatorname{Cov}(f))^{1/(2n)},

where
\operatorname{Cov}(f)
denotes the covariance matrix of
f
. A log-concave function
f
is called isotropic if

\operatorname{bar}(f)=0,\hskip 9.24994pt\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\,dx=1,\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and}\hskip 9.24994pt\operatorname{Cov}(f)=I_{n}.

In this case,
L_{f}=\|f\|_{\infty}^{1/n}
. A full-dimensional log-concave probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is called isotropic if its
density
f
is isotropic. Then, we set
L_{\mu}:=L_{f}
.

Note that a centered convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)=1
is isotropic if and only if the log-concave function
L_{K}^{n}\mathds{1}_{K/L_{K}}
is isotropic.

Let
\mu
be a full-dimensional log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
For any
1\leqslant k\leqslant n-1
and any
k
-dimensional subspace
F
of
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, the marginal of
\mu
onto
F
is defined by

\pi_{F}(\mu)(B):=\mu(P_{F}^{-1}(B)),

for every Borel set
B\subseteq F
. The measure
\pi_{F}(\mu)
is log-concave and admits a density

(\pi_{F}f)(x)=\int_{F^{\perp}}f(y+x)\,dy.

If
f
is centered (respectively isotropic), then so is
\pi_{F}f

(see
[
13
, Proposition 5.1.11]
). In particular, if
\mu
is isotropic and
F_{\xi}=\{t\xi:t\in\mathbb{R}\}
for
\xi\in S^{n-1}
,
then the one-dimensional marginal

g_{\xi}(t)=(\pi_{F_{\xi}}f)(t)=\int_{F_{\xi}^{\perp}}f(y+t\xi)\,dy

is an isotropic log-concave density on
\mathbb{R}
. Consequently, as shown e.g. in
[
26
]
,

(2.6)

\|g_{\xi}\|_{\infty}=L_{g_{\xi}}\leqslant 1.

It is known that every centered log-concave density
f
admits an isotropic position: there exists
T\in GL_{n}
such that the push-forward density

f_{T}(x)=\frac{1}{|\det T|}\,f(T^{-1}x)

is isotropic (see
[
13
, Section 2.3]
).
Moreover,
f_{T}
is also log-concave, and
L_{f_{T}}=L_{f}
. It is also known (see
[
13
, Proposition 2.3.12]
) that
L_{f}\geqslant c
for every
isotropic log-concave function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
c>0
is an absolute constant. On the other hand, Ball

6
]
proved that for every
n

\tilde{L}_{n}:=\sup\big\{L_{f}:f\;\text{is a log-concave density on $\mathbb{R}^{n}$}\big\}\leqslant C_{1}L_{n},

and hence
\tilde{L}_{n}\leqslant C_{2}
by the affirmative solution of Bourgain’s slicing problem.

§2.4. Level sets of the density.
Let
\mu
be a centered log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with density

f=\exp(-\psi)
, where
\psi
is a convex function. For every
t\geqslant 0
we consider the convex set

R_{t}(\mu)=\{x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}:f(x)\geqslant e^{-t}f(0)\}.

Using the log-concavity of
f
we easily check that
R_{t}(\mu)
is convex. Note also that
0\in{\rm int}(R_{t}(\mu))
for every
t>0
.
To show that
R_{t}(\mu)
is bounded, we recall that since
f
is log-concave and has finite positive integral we
have that there exist constants
A,B>0
such that

(2.7)

f(x)\leqslant Ae^{-B|x|}

for all
x\in{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
(see
[
13
, Lemma 2.2.1]
). Therefore, if
x\in R_{t}(\mu)
we get
that
|x|\leqslant\frac{1}{B}\big(\ln(A/f(0))+t\big)
. Another consequence of (
2.7
) is that
f
has
finite moments of all orders.

The next proposition, which is essentially due to Klartag (see
[
33
, Lemma 5.2]
and
[
28
]

for the precise form below) shows that the measure of
R_{t}(\mu)
increases to
1

exponentially fast as
t\to\infty
.

Proposition 2.1
.

For every
t\geqslant 3n
we have that
\mu(R_{t}(\mu))\geqslant 1-e^{-t/4}
.

Proof.

Consider the convex function
\varphi
defined by
e^{-\varphi(x)}=f(x)/f(0)
. Note that
\varphi(0)=0
.
Then

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{\varphi(x)/2}d\mu(x)

\displaystyle=f(0)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\varphi(x)/2}dx=f(0)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-(\varphi(x)+\varphi(0))/2}dx

\displaystyle\leqslant f(0)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\varphi(x/2)}dx=2^{n}f(0)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\varphi(x)}dx=2^{n}.

For any
t>0
we have
R_{t}(\mu)=\{x:\varphi(x)\leqslant t\}
. From Markov’s inequality we get

1-\mu(R_{t}(\mu))=\mu(\{x:\varphi(x)>t\})\leqslant e^{-t/2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{\varphi(x)/2}d\mu(x)\leqslant 2^{n}e^{-t/2}.

If
t\geqslant(4\ln 2)n
then
2^{n}e^{-t/2}\leqslant e^{-t/4}
, and this implies that

\mu(R_{t}(\mu))\geqslant 1-e^{-t/4},\hskip 18.49988pt\text{for all}\;t\geqslant(4\ln 2)n.

The result follows. ∎

We shall also use the fact that if
\mu
is isotropic and
t
is large enough then
R_{t}(\mu)
contains a constant multiple of the Euclidean unit ball.
The proof of the next lemma is essentially contained in
[

, Lemma 5.4]
.

Lemma 2.2
.

Let

be an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
For any

and any

we have that

R_{t}(\mu)\supseteq\tfrac{1}{3}B_{2}^{n}.

and

.

§2.5. Stability of the Poincaré constant.
Let
\mu
be a Borel probability measure on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
.
Recall that the Cheeger constant
\chi_{\mu}
of
\mu
is the largest constant
c\geqslant 0
for which we have

(2.8)

\mu^{+}(\partial E)\geqslant c\,\min\{\mu(E),1-\mu(E)\}

for every Borel subset
E
of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
. The reciprocal Cheeger constant of
\mu
is
\psi_{\mu}:=1/\chi_{\mu}
.

We also say that
\mu
satisfies the Poincaré inequality with constant
\vartheta>0
if

(2.9)

{\rm Var}_{\mu}(f)\leqslant\vartheta^{2}\int|\nabla f|^{2}\,d\mu,

for all smooth functions
f
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, where
{\rm Var}_{\mu}(g)={\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}(g^{2})-({\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}(g))^{2}
is the
variance of
g
with respect to
\mu
. The Poincaré constant
\vartheta_{\mu}
of
\mu
is the smallest constant

\vartheta>0
for which (
2.9
) is satisfied.

It is known (see
[
5
, Theorem 2.3.1]
that if
\alpha(\mu)
is the smallest constant
\alpha>0
with the property
that every integrable, locally Lipschitz function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
satisfies

(2.10)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|f(x)-{\mathbb{E}}_{\mu}(f)|\,d\mu(x)\leqslant\alpha\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|\nabla f(x)|\,d\mu(x),

then,
\psi_{\mu}/2\leqslant\alpha(\mu)\leqslant 2\psi_{\mu}
. It is also known (see
[
15
]
) that

\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|f(x)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(f)|\,d\mu(x)\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|f(x)-m_{\mu}(f)|\,d\mu(x)\leqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|f(x)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(f)|\,d\mu(x)

for all
f
, where
m_{\mu}(f)
is the median of
f
with respect to
\mu
. It follows that if

\beta(\mu)
is the smallest constant
\beta>0
with the property
that for every integrable, locally Lipschitz function
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to{\mathbb{R}}
,

(2.11)

\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|f(x)-m_{\mu}(f)|\,d\mu(x)\leqslant\beta\int_{{\mathbb{R}}^{n}}|\nabla f(x)|\,d\mu(x),

then,
\alpha(\mu)/2\leqslant\beta(\mu)\leqslant\alpha(\mu)
. Combining the above, we see that

(2.12)

\psi_{\mu}/4\leqslant\beta(\mu)\leqslant 2\psi_{\mu}.

A theorem of Maz’ya
[
49
]
,
[
50
]
and Cheeger
[
16
]

shows that the Poincaré constant is bounded by the reciprocal Cheeger constant: If
\mu
is a Borel probability measure with
reciprocal Cheeger constant
\psi_{\mu}
then its Poincaré constant
\vartheta_{\mu}
satisfies

(2.13)

\vartheta_{\mu}\leqslant 2\psi_{\mu}.

On the other hand, the assumption that
\mu
is log-concave implies a reverse inequality with a constant that
does not depend on the dimension. Buser
[
14
]
(see also Ledoux
[
40
]
)
has shown that if
\mu
is a log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, then

(2.14)

\psi_{\mu}\leqslant c\,\vartheta_{\mu},

where
c>0
is an absolute constant.

E. Milman
[
51
, Theorem 5.5]
has shown that the ratio of the Cheeger constants of two log-concave probability measures
\mu
and
\nu

on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with densities
f
and
g
is controlled by their total variation distance

d_{{\rm TV}}(\mu,\nu)=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|f(x)-g(x)|\,dx.

More precisely, if
d_{{\rm TV}}(\mu,\nu)=1-\varepsilon
for some
\varepsilon\in(0,1)
then

(2.15)

\chi_{\mu}\leqslant\frac{c_{1}}{\varepsilon^{2}}\max\{1,\ln(1/\varepsilon)\}\,\chi_{\nu},

where
c_{1}>0
is an absolute constant. An alternative proof of (
2.15
) is given by Cattiaux and Guillin in

[
15
, Theorem 9.3.10]
. When
\nu
is itself a restriction
\mu_{A}
of
\mu
on a Borel set
A
, then we have

\displaystyle d_{{\rm TV}}(\mu,\mu_{A})

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\int_{A}\left|f(x)-\frac{1}{\mu(A)}f(x)\right|\,dx+\int_{A^{c}}f(x)\,dx\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left(\frac{1}{\mu(A)}-1\right)\mu(A)+\mu(A^{c})\right)=1-\mu(A).

In this case, the dependence on
\varepsilon=\mu(A)
in (
2.15
) can be improved to logarithmic as shown in
[
51
, Lemmas 5.1 and 5.4]
, though this improved dependence will be immaterial for us.

Proposition 2.3

(E. Milman)
.

Let
\mu
be a log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For every convex
set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mu(A)>0
we have that

(2.16)

\vartheta_{\mu_{A}}\leqslant C\max\left\{1,\ln\left(\frac{1}{\mu(A)}\right)\right\}\,\vartheta_{\mu},

where
\mu_{A}
is the restriction of
\mu
onto
A
and
C>0
is an absolute constant.

The Kannan–Lovász–Simonovits conjecture asks if there exists an absolute constant
C>0
such that

\psi_{n}:=\sup\{\psi_{\mu}:\mu\ \hbox{is an isotropic log-concave measure on}\ {\mathbb{R}}^{n}\}\leqslant C.

In view of the discussion above, an equivalent way to formulate the KLS conjecture is to ask that the Poincaré inequality
holds for every isotropic log-concave probability measure
\mu
on
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
with a constant
that does not depend on the measure or the dimension
n
. The best known result on this problem is due to Klartag
[
34
]
and
provides an (almost) affirmative answer. For any
n\geqslant 2
we have that

\psi_{n}\leqslant C\sqrt{\ln n},

where
C>0
is an absolute constant.

Combining Klartag’s estimate with Proposition
2.3
we immediately obtain the next fact that will be useful in the proof of the dimensional Brunn–Minkowski inequality.

Proposition 2.4
.

Let
\mu
be an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For every
\alpha\in(0,1)

and every convex set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mu(A)\geqslant\alpha
we have that

\vartheta_{\mu_{A}}\leqslant C(\alpha)\sqrt{\ln n},

where
\mu_{A}
is the restriction of
\mu
onto
A
and
C(\alpha)>0
is a constant that depends only on
\alpha
.

It should be noted that a reverse inequality to (
2.16
) holds for Borel sets
A
that have large measure without further convexity assumptions. It is proved in
[
15
, Proposition 9.2.5]
that if
\mu
is a log-concave probability measure such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|f(x)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(f)|\,d\mu(x)\leqslant c(t)\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla f(x)|\,d\mu(x)+t\,\mathrm{osc}(f)

for some
0<t<1/2
, some constant
c(t)>0
, and all Lipschitz functions
f
, where
\mathrm{osc}(f)=\sup f-\inf f

denotes the oscillation of
f
, then

\beta(\mu)\leqslant\frac{c(t)}{1-2t}.

Let
A
be a Borel set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
\mu_{A}
denote the restriction of
\mu
onto
A
as above. Then it is clear that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|f(x)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(f)|\,d\mu(x)\leqslant\int_{A}|f(x)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(f)|\,d\mu(x)+(1-\mu(A))\,\mathrm{osc}(f),

therefore

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|f(x)-\mathbb{E}_{\mu}(f)|\,d\mu(x)\leqslant\mu(A)\beta(\mu_{A})\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla f(x)|\,d\mu_{A}(x)+(1-\mu(A))\,\mathrm{osc}(f).

This implies the following result.

Proposition 2.5
.

Let
\mu
be a log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. For every Borel
set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
with
\mu(A)>\tfrac{1}{2}
we have that

\beta(\mu)\leqslant\frac{\mu(A)}{2\mu(A)-1}\beta(\mu_{A}),

and hence

(2.17)

\psi_{\mu}\leqslant\frac{8\mu(A)}{2\mu(A)-1}\,\psi_{\mu_{A}},

where
\mu_{A}
is the restriction of
\mu
onto
A
.

3
The dimensional Brunn–Minkowski inequality

In what follows,
\mu
is a probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with an even twice continuously differentiable density
f=\exp(-\psi)
.
We also consider the operator

Lu=\Delta u-\langle\nabla u,\nabla\psi\rangle

for twice continuously differentiable
u:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
.
We denote by
\mathcal{C}^{2}
the class of
C^{2}
-smooth symmetric convex bodies; these are the convex bodies whose support function is
twice continuously differentiable on the unit sphere. If
K
is a
C^{2}
-smooth symmetric convex body
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we write
n_{x}
for the normal vector at the point
x\in\partial K
.
We also denote by
\mathcal{C}^{2}_{+}
the class of convex bodies in
\mathcal{C}^{2}
with positive Gaussian curvature. We say that a function

h:S^{n-1}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}
is a function in
C^{2}_{+}(S^{n-1})
if it is the support function of a convex body in
\mathcal{C}^{2}_{+}
.

Following Livshyts
[
46
]
, we define the concavity power
p(\mu,K)
of a symmetric convex body
K\in\mathcal{C}_{+}^{2}
with respect to
\mu
to be the largest
p\geqslant 0
such that for every symmetric convex body
L\in\mathcal{C}_{+}^{2}
, we have

(3.1)

\frac{d^{2}}{d\lambda^{2}}\Bigg|_{\lambda=1}\mu(\lambda K+(1-\lambda)L)^{p}\leqslant 0.

We shall need the following straightforward fact.

Lemma 3.1
.

For every log-concave probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with an even twice continuously differentiable density, every symmetric convex body
K\in\mathcal{C}_{+}^{2}
and every invertible linear operator
T:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

p(\mu,K)=p(T_{\ast}\mu,TK),

where
T_{\ast}\mu
is the push-forward of
\mu
under
T
given by
(T_{\ast}\mu)(E)=\mu(T^{-1}E)
, where
E\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
is a Borel set.

Proof.

Let
p=p(T_{\ast}\mu,TK)
and consider a symmetric convex body
L\in\mathcal{C}_{+}^{2}
. Then,

\frac{d^{2}}{d\lambda^{2}}\Bigg|_{\lambda=1}\mu(\lambda K+(1-\lambda)L)^{p}=\frac{d^{2}}{d\lambda^{2}}\Bigg|_{\lambda=1}(T_{\ast}\mu)\big(\lambda TK+(1-\lambda)TL\big)^{p}\leqslant 0

by the definition of
p(T_{\ast}\mu,TK)
, so
p(\mu,K)\geqslant p(T_{\ast}\mu,TK)
. The converse inequality follows similarly.
∎

A standard local-to-global principle (see
[
39
, Section 3]
or
[
37
, Lemma 3.1]
) implies that for a given
\mu
and
p>0
,

(3.2)

\inf_{K\in\mathcal{C}_{+}^{2}}\ p(\mu,K)\geqslant p

if and only if for every pair of symmetric convex sets
K,L
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and every
\lambda\in[0,1]
, we have

(3.3)

\mu(\lambda K+(1-\lambda)L)^{p}\geqslant\lambda\mu(K)^{p}+(1-\lambda)\mu(L)^{p}.

Indeed, the derivation of (
3.2
) from (
3.3
) is immediate from the statement of
[
37
, Lemma 3.1]
. Conversely, to show that (
3.2
) implies (
3.3
), we apply
[
37
, Lemma 3.1]
to the class
\mathcal{F}
of all symmetric
\mathcal{C}_{+}^{2}
convex bodies. Note that equation (21) of
[
37
]
requires the validity of
\frac{d^{2}}{ds^{2}}\mu(K_{s})^{p}\leqslant 0
for any one-parameter family
\{K_{s}\}_{|s|<\varepsilon}
of symmetric convex sets that arise from Wulff shape perturbations of
K
but an inspection of the proof of this implication reveals that Minkowski convex combinations are sufficient to yield the conclusion (since the perturbation in the last paragraph of
[
37
, Proof of Lemma 3.1]
is chosen to be
\psi=h_{L}-h_{K}
). Then, having proven the Brunn–Minkowski inequality (
3.3
) for every pair of symmetric convex bodies
K,L\in\mathcal{C}_{+}^{2}
, we extend it to all symmetric convex sets by approximation.

In
[
38
]
, Kolesnikov and E. Milman developed a powerful method for proving lower bounds for concavity powers by appropriate uses of curvature and integration by parts along the lines of Hörmander’s
L_{2}
method (see also
[
19
, Section 2]
). Their main result, as presented in
[
37
, Proof of Lemma 2.3]
, reads as follows.

Theorem 3.2
.

Let
\mu
be an even measure with a twice continuously differentiable density
f=\exp(-\psi)
and
K
be a
\mathcal{C}_{+}^{2}
convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Suppose that every
u\in C^{2}(K)
with
Lu=1
on
K
satisfies

(3.4)

\frac{1}{\mu(K)}\int_{K}\big(\|\nabla^{2}u\|+\langle\nabla^{2}\psi\nabla u,\nabla u\rangle\big)\,d\mu\geqslant p,

where
\|\nabla^{2}u\|
is the Hilbert–Schmidt norm of the Hessian of
u
. Then, the concavity power of
K
with respect to
\mu
satisfies
p(\mu,K)\geqslant p
.

We emphasize that
[
37
, Lemma 2.3]
presents a similar characterization for the quantity
\inf_{K\in\mathcal{C}_{+}^{2}}p(\mu,K)
via the equivalence of (
3.2
) and (
3.3
) as the differential inequality (
3.4
) is required to hold
for every
symmetric convex set
K
. However, an inspection of the proof readily reveals that the criterion in fact holds for each individual
K
as well, thus offering a bound for each concavity power
p(\mu,K)
. To see this, observe that one can use
[
37
, Proposition 3.2]
to write the condition (
3.1
) defining the exponent
p(\mu,K)
as a weighted Poincaré-type inequality for functions
f
defined on the boundary of
K
. Then, one can use Kolesnikov and E. Milman’s
[
38
]
weighted version of the Reilly formula
[
37
, Proposition 3.4]
to rewrite this inequality in terms of the solution of the equation
Lu=1
with Neumann boundary data
f
. In the case of Gaussian measure, this was also explicitly exploited in
[
46
, Proof of Theorem A]
.

Livshyts
[
45
]
obtains the following estimate, which can be combined with Theorem
3.2
. The proof below clarifies a few
subtle points of the original argument.

Theorem 3.3

(Livshyts)
.

Let
\nu
be an even log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Let
K
be a
symmetric convex set in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and let
u:K\to\mathbb{R}
be an even function in
C^{2}(K)
. Then, for any symmetric
convex set
A\subseteq K
we have

\frac{1}{\nu(K)}\int_{K}\|\nabla^{2}u\|^{2}d\nu\geqslant\frac{\nu(A)}{\nu(K)}\cdot\frac{\left(\frac{1}{\nu(A)}\int_{A}Lu\,d\nu\right)^{2}}{n+\frac{1}{\nu(A)}\int_{A}(\vartheta_{\nu_{A}}^{2}|\nabla\psi|^{2}-2\langle\nabla\psi,x\rangle)\,d\nu}.

Proof.

We start with the inequality

(3.5)

\int_{K}\|\nabla^{2}u\|^{2}d\nu\geqslant\int_{A}\|\nabla^{2}u\|^{2}d\nu.

For every
t\in\mathbb{R}
we define
v_{t}(x)=u(x)-\frac{t}{2}|x|^{2}
. Direct computation shows that

(3.6)

\|\nabla^{2}u\|^{2}=\|\nabla^{2}v_{t}\|^{2}+2t\Delta v_{t}+t^{2}n

and

(3.7)

(Lu)(x)=(Lv_{t})(x)+tL(|x|^{2}/2)=(Lv_{t})(x)+tn-t\langle x,\nabla\psi(x)\rangle.

Therefore,

(3.8)

\Delta v_{t}=\langle\nabla\psi,\nabla v_{t}\rangle+Lu-tn+t\langle x,\nabla\psi\rangle.

Since
u
is even, we have that
v_{t}
is also even. Since
A
is symmetric, the restriction
\nu_{A}
of
\nu
onto
A
is even. It follows that
\int_{A}\frac{\partial v_{t}}{\partial x_{i}}d\nu_{A}=0
.
Using (
3.6
) and applying the Poincaré inequality to
\frac{\partial v_{t}}{\partial x_{i}}
with respect to
\nu_{A}
and summing over
i=1,\dots,n
, we get

(3.9)

\int_{A}\|\nabla^{2}u\|^{2}d\nu\geqslant\int_{A}\big(\vartheta_{\nu_{A}}^{-2}|\nabla v_{t}|^{2}+2t\Delta v_{t}+t^{2}n\big)\,d\nu.

Substituting (
3.8
) into (
3.9
) and completing the square we obtain

(3.10)

\displaystyle\int_{A}\|\nabla^{2}u\|^{2}d\nu

\displaystyle\geqslant\int_{A}\big(-t^{2}\vartheta_{\nu_{A}}^{2}|\nabla\psi|^{2}+2t(Lu-tn+t\langle x,\nabla\psi\rangle)+t^{2}n\big)\ d\nu

\displaystyle=2t\int_{A}Lu\,d\nu-t^{2}\left(n\,\nu(A)+\int_{A}\big(\vartheta_{\nu_{A}}^{2}|\nabla\psi|^{2}-2\langle x,\nabla\psi\rangle\big)\,d\nu\right).

The optimal value of
t
is

t=\frac{\int_{A}Lu\,d\nu}{n\,\nu(A)+\int_{A}\big(\vartheta_{\nu_{A}}^{2}|\nabla v_{t}|^{2}-2\langle x,\nabla\psi\rangle\big)\,d\nu},

which, combined with (
3.5
), gives

\int_{K}\|\nabla^{2}u\|^{2}d\nu\geqslant\int_{A}\|\nabla^{2}u\|^{2}d\nu\geqslant\nu(A)\,\frac{\left(\frac{1}{\nu(A)}\int_{A}Lu\,d\nu\right)^{2}}{n+\frac{1}{\nu(A)}\int_{A}(\vartheta_{\nu_{A}}^{2}|\nabla\psi|^{2}-2\langle\nabla\psi,x\rangle)\,d\nu}

and the theorem follows.
∎

Remark 3.4
.

In
[
45
, Proposition 4.2]
, Livshyts claims a similar bound to Theorem
3.3
with
\vartheta_{\nu_{A}}^{2}
replaced by
\vartheta_{\nu_{K}}^{2}
but the proof appears to have a gap. More specifically, rather than starting with inequality (
3.5
), Livshyts pursues all the steps until (
3.10
) on the set
K
instead of
A
and then bounds the integral on the right hand side of (
3.10
) from below by the corresponding integral on
A
. This step however appears to be problematic as the integrand of this expression may not be pointwise nonnegative.

The next proposition, which originates in
[
45
, Corollary 4.6]
, shows that for every even isotropic log-concave probability measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geqslant 10
, with
density
f=\exp(-\psi)
, we can find a symmetric convex body
A
of measure
\nu(A)\geqslant c
, on which
|\nabla\psi|\leqslant Cn^{2}
. We present here a different proof for completeness.

Proposition 3.5
.

Let
\nu
be an even isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
n\geqslant 10
, with
a
C^{1}
density
f=\exp(-\psi)
. There exists a symmetric convex set
A\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that
\nu(A)\geqslant c_{1}
and

|\nabla\psi(x)|\leqslant c_{2}n^{2}

for all
x\in A
, where
c_{1},c_{2}>0
are absolute constants.

Proof.

Consider the set
A=\frac{n-1}{n}R_{3n}(\nu)
. From Lemma
2.2
we know that
R_{3n}(\nu)\supseteq\frac{1}{3}B_{2}^{n}
. It follows that

A+\frac{1}{3n}B_{2}^{n}=\frac{n-1}{n}R_{3n}(\nu)+\frac{1}{3n}B_{2}^{n}\subseteq\frac{n-1}{n}R_{3n}(\nu)+\frac{1}{n}R_{3n}(\nu)=R_{3n}(\nu).

Now, let
x\in A
. There exists
v_{x}\in S^{n-1}
such that
|\nabla\psi(x)|=\langle\nabla\psi(x),v_{x}\rangle
. Consider the function

g(s)=\psi(x+sv_{x})
. This is a convex function with
g^{\prime}(0)=\langle\nabla\psi(x),v_{x}\rangle=|\nabla\psi(x)|
. It follows that

\displaystyle|\nabla\psi(x)|

\displaystyle=g^{\prime}(0)\leqslant 3n\big(g\left(\tfrac{1}{3n}\right)-g(0)\big)=3n\big(\psi\left(x+\tfrac{1}{3n}v_{x}\right)-\psi(x)\big)

\displaystyle\leqslant 3n\big(\psi\left(x+\tfrac{1}{3n}v_{x}\right)-\psi(0)\big)

because

. Since

, which gives
\psi\left(x+\tfrac{1}{3n}v_{x}\right)-\psi(0)\leqslant 3n
.
It follows that

|\nabla\psi(x)|=g^{\prime}(0)\leqslant 3n\cdot 3n=9n^{2}.

Finally, using Proposition
2.1
we see that

\nu(A)\geqslant\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n}\nu(R_{3n}(\mu))\geqslant\left(\frac{n-1}{n}\right)^{n}\big(1-e^{-3n/4}\big)\geqslant c

where

is an absolute constant.∎

Proposition 3.6

We are now in position to complete the proof of the main result of this paper.

Proof of Theorem
1.1

on

The discussion in this section leads to the following reduction of the problem to establish a dimensional Brunn-Minkowski inequality with exponent
c_{n}
.

Proposition 3.7
.

Let
n\geqslant 10
. Suppose that there exists a constant
d_{n}>0
such that for every even isotropic
log-concave probability measure
\nu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with a
C^{1}
density

there exists a symmetric convex set

such that
\nu(A)\geqslant c_{1}
and

|\nabla\psi(x)|\leqslant d_{n}

for all

, where
c_{1}>0
is an absolute constant. Then, for any pair of symmetric convex bodies

K
and
L
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and any

, one has

\mu(\lambda K+(1-\lambda)L)^{c_{n}}\geqslant\lambda\mu(K)^{c_{n}}+(1-\lambda)\mu(L)^{c_{n}},

where
c_{n}\geqslant c_{2}/(nd_{n}\ln n)
for an absolute constant

.

Remark 3.8
.

We know that the assumption of Proposition
3.7
is satisfied with
d_{n}\approx n^{2}
. A natural question raised by the proof of
Theorem
1.1
is whether one can obtain a stronger version of Proposition
3.5
.

Note that the proof of Theorem
1.1
uses the convexity of the set
A
only at the point where it is claimed that

\vartheta_{\nu_{A}}\leqslant C\vartheta_{\nu}
. For this assertion we employ Proposition
2.4
, which requires
that
\nu_{A}
is a log-concave probability measure, and this forces us to choose our set
A
to be convex. On the other hand, we know that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\psi(x)|\,d\nu(x)\leqslant Cn,

hence applying Markov’s inequality we see that the set

A_{0}=\{x:|\nabla\psi(x)|\leqslant 2Cn\}

has measure
\nu(A_{0})\geqslant\frac{1}{2}
, because

\nu(\{x:|\nabla\psi(x)|>2Cn\})\leqslant\frac{1}{2Cn}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\psi(x)|\,d\nu(x)\leqslant\frac{1}{2}.

Unfortunately,
A_{0}
need not be convex as can be seen by the example of the function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{2}\to\mathbb{R}
with

\psi(x,y)=\sqrt{x^{2}+1}+\sqrt{y^{2}+1}.

This is clearly an even convex function and

\nabla\psi(x,y)=\left(\frac{x}{\sqrt{x^{2}+1}},\frac{y}{\sqrt{y^{2}+1}}\right).

Therefore, the set

A_{0}=\left\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ |\nabla\psi(x,y)|\leqslant 1\right\}=\{(x,y)\in\mathbb{R}^{2}:\ |x||y|\leqslant 1\}

is evidently non-convex. It is an interesting question whether one can still compare
\vartheta_{\nu_{A_{0}}}
and
\vartheta_{\nu}
in this context.
Having an estimate
\vartheta_{\nu_{A_{0}}}\leqslant C\vartheta_{\nu}
(or some weaker but good enough estimate) would be enough for a
stronger estimate for
c_{n}
in Theorem
1.1
.

We conclude this section with a lower bound for the parameter
d_{n}
studied in Proposition
3.7
.

Proposition 3.9
.

There exists an even isotropic log-concave probability measure
\mu
with continuous density
f=e^{-\psi}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that for every symmetric convex body
B
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\big\||\nabla\psi|\big\|_{L_{\infty}(B)}\geqslant cn,

where
c>0
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Let
\nu_{K}
be an isotropic measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with density
f(x)=e^{-\psi(x)}=\frac{1}{n!\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)}e^{-\|x\|_{K}}
, where
K
is a
1
-symmetric convex body
and
\|\cdot\|_{K}
is the norm induced by
K
. Then, we know that

f(x_{1},\ldots,x_{n})=f(\varepsilon_{1}x_{\sigma(1)},\ldots,\varepsilon_{n}x_{\sigma(n)})

for all choices of signs
\varepsilon_{i}\in\{-1,1\}
and all permutations
\sigma
of
\{1,\ldots,n\}
.
Since
K
is
1
-symmetric, we easily check that
\overline{K}=\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{-1/n}K
is isotropic.
Note that

\frac{1}{\left(n!\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)\right)^{1/n}}=\|f\|_{\infty}^{1/n}=L_{\nu_{K}}\approx 1,

which implies that

\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{1/n}\approx\frac{1}{(n!)^{1/n}}\approx\frac{1}{n}.

It is straightforward to check that
R_{t}(\nu_{K})=tK
for every
t>0
. We shall show that

(3.11)

\frac{1}{\nu_{K}(tK)}\int_{tK}|\nabla\psi(x)|\,d\nu_{K}(x)=\frac{S(K)}{n\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)}

for every
t>0
.

To see this, consider the truncated function
f_{t}=f\cdot\mathds{1}_{tK}=f\cdot\mathds{1}_{R_{t}(\nu_{K})}
which is log-concave and upper semi-continuous. Therefore, the co-area formula (
2.2
) yields

(3.12)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{x:f_{t}(x)\geqslant u\})\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla f_{t}(x)|\,dx+\int_{t\partial K}f(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{tK}|\nabla\psi(x)|\,d\nu_{K}(x)+t^{n-1}e^{-t}f(0)S(K)

as
f|_{t\partial K}\equiv e^{-t}f(0)
. On the other hand,

(3.13)

\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{x:f_{t}(x)\geqslant u\})\,du

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}f(0)\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{x:\ f(x)\cdot\mathds{1}_{tK}(x)\geqslant e^{-s}f(0)\})\,ds

\displaystyle=f(0)\int_{0}^{t}e^{-s}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(s\partial K)\,ds+f(0)\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-s}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(t\partial K)\,ds

\displaystyle=\bigg(\int_{0}^{t}s^{n-1}e^{-s}\,ds+t^{n-1}e^{-t}\bigg)f(0)S(K).

Combining (
3.12
) and (
3.13
), we deduce that

(3.14)

\int_{tK}|\nabla\psi(x)|\,d\nu_{K}(x)=f(0)S(K)\int_{0}^{t}s^{n-1}e^{-s}\,ds.

On the other hand,

\displaystyle\nu_{K}(tK)

\displaystyle=f(0)\int_{tK}e^{-\|x\|_{K}}\,dx=t^{n}f(0)\int_{K}e^{-t\|y\|_{K}}dy=t^{n}f(0)\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\{y\in K:\|y\|_{K}\leqslant s/t\})\,ds

\displaystyle=t^{n}f(0)\left(\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)\int_{0}^{t}(s/t)^{n}e^{-s}ds+\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)\int_{t}^{\infty}e^{-s}ds\right)=f(0)\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)\left(\int_{0}^{t}s^{n}e^{-s}ds+t^{n}e^{-t}\right)

\displaystyle=n\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)f(0)\int_{0}^{t}s^{n-1}e^{-s}\,ds,

where the last equality is because of integration by parts. Formula (
3.11
) now follows.

Finally, we write

\frac{S(K)}{n\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)}=\frac{1}{n\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{\frac{1}{n}}}\,\frac{S(K)}{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{\frac{n-1}{n}}}\approx\frac{S(K)}{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{\frac{n-1}{n}}}

and choose
K
to be a multiple of
B_{\infty}^{n}
. For this choice, the last quantity is of order
n
. Thus,

\frac{1}{\nu_{K}(tK)}\int_{tK}|\nabla\psi(x)|\,d\nu_{K}(x)\geqslant cn,

which implies
\||\nabla\psi(x)|\|_{L_{\infty}(tK)}\geqslant cn
for every
t>0
. As for every symmetric convex body
B
there exists
t>0
for which
tK\subseteq B
, the conclusion follows.
∎

This shows that the best one can hope with the ideas that are exploited in the present work is a dimensional Brunn–Minkowski inequality with

4
Bounds for the weighted perimeter

As mentioned in the introduction, Theorem
1.4
(whose proof will appear in Section
6
) implies Corollary
6.4
, which asserts that for every
isotropic log-concave function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

(4.1)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{x:\ f(x)\geqslant t\})\,dt\leqslant Cn,

This will allow us to bound the maximal perimeter of the measure
\mu
with density
f
.

Proof of Theorem
1.2
.

Let
f
be the density of the isotropic log-concave probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, and let
A\subset\mathbb{R}^{n}
be an arbitrary convex body. Set

K_{t}=\{x:\ f(x)\geqslant t\},\hskip 18.49988ptt\geqslant 0.

We have

\mu^{+}(\partial A)=\int_{\partial A}f(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x).

Applying the layer-cake representation on
\partial A
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\partial A}f(x)\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)

\displaystyle=\int_{\partial A}\int_{0}^{f(x)}dt\,d\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(x)=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}\left(\partial A\cap\{f\geqslant t\}\right)\,dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial A\cap K_{t})\,dt.

Note that for a closed subset
C\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
and an arbitrary subset
M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^{n}
, we have

\partial C\cap M\subseteq\partial(C\cap M).

Therefore,

\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial A\cap K_{t})\leqslant\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial(A\cap K_{t}))\leqslant\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial K_{t}),

where the last inequality is the monotonicity of surface area of convex sets with respect to inclusion. Combining the above, we get by (
4.1
) that

\mu^{+}(\partial A)\leqslant\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial K_{t})\,dt\leqslant Cn.

This implies that
\Gamma(\mu)\leq Cn
, which in turn yields
\Gamma_{n}\leq Cn
. The asymptotically matching lower bound follows from considering
\mu
to be the isotropic probability measure on a cube (see also
[
12
, Section 5]
).
∎

5
Bounds for the functional perimeter

Let
\mu
be a log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with density
f=e^{-\psi}
, where
\psi
is a convex function.
In this section we give a new proof of the fact that if
f
is also assumed to be isotropic, then we have

(5.1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\psi(x)|\,d\mu(x)\leqslant Cn

for an absolute constant
C>0
. Note that in the
1
-dimensional case if
f
is symmetric, we get from
[
26
]
(see (
2.6
)),

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|\psi^{\prime}(x)|\,d\mu(x)=2\int_{0}^{\infty}\psi^{\prime}(x)e^{-\psi(x)}\,dx=2e^{-\psi(0)}=2f(0)\leqslant 2.

§5.1. Optimal upper bounds.
Our starting point is the fact (see
[
3
, Equation 4]
)
that if
f
is an integrable log-concave function such that

(5.2)

f\geqslant\alpha\mathds{1}_{B_{2}^{n}}

for some
\alpha>0
then

(5.3)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla f(x)|\,dx\leqslant n\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(y)\,dy+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(z)\ln\left(\frac{f(z)}{\alpha\|f\|_{\infty}}\right)\,dz.

This follows from
[
2
, Lemma 4.3]
.

Since we need a variant of this inequality, we present the full details. In what follows, the sum of two log-concave
functions is given by the sup-convolution (or Asplund product)

(f\star g)(x)=\sup_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big(f(y)g(x-y)\big).

The dilation operation is given by
(t\cdot f)(x)=f\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)^{t}
.

Lemma 5.1
.

Let
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
be a log-concave function. For any
r,a>0
we have

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}(f\star t\cdot(a\mathds{1}_{rB_{2}^{n}}))(z)=f(z)

at every point of continuity
z
of
f
, and

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{(f\star t\cdot(a\mathds{1}_{rB_{2}^{n}}))(z)-f(z)}{t}=r|\nabla f(z)|+f(z)\ln a

almost everywhere.

Proof.

By the definition of the Asplund product we have

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0}(f\star t\cdot(a\mathds{1}_{rB_{2}^{n}}))(z)=\lim_{t\to 0}\sup\left\{f(x)\cdot\mathds{1}_{rB_{2}^{n}}(y/t)a^{t}:z=x+y\right\}

\displaystyle\hskip 28.45274pt=\lim_{t\to 0}\sup\left\{f(z-rty)a^{t}:y\in B_{2}^{n}\right\}=f(z)

if we assume that
f
is continuous at
z
. For the second assertion of the lemma, we start by writing

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{(f\star t\cdot(a\mathds{1}_{rB_{2}^{n}}))(z)-f(z)}{t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\,\sup_{y\in B_{2}^{n}}\frac{f(z-rty)a^{t}-f(z)a^{t}+f(z)a^{t}-f(z)}{t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\,\sup_{y\in B_{2}^{n}}a^{t}\frac{f(z-rty)-f(z)}{t}+\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}f(z)\frac{a^{t}-1}{t}.

As the function
\psi=-\ln f
is convex, it has a first order Taylor expansion around almost every point
z\in\{f>0\}=\{\psi<\infty\}
(and even a second order Taylor expansion, by Alexandrov’s theorem) and thus the same holds for
f
. Moreover, for such points
z
the gradient
\nabla f(z)
is uniquely defined and we have

f(z+w)=f(z)+\langle\nabla f(z),w\rangle+c_{z}(|w|)

for some function
c_{z}:(0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
with
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}c_{z}(t)/t=0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\,\sup_{y\in B_{2}^{n}}a^{t}\frac{f(z-rty)-f(z)}{t}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\,a^{t}\,\sup_{y\in B_{2}^{n}}\left\{-r\langle\nabla f(z),y\rangle+\frac{c_{z}(rt)}{t}\right\}

\displaystyle=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}a^{t}\Big(r|\nabla f(z)|+\frac{c_{z}(rt)}{t}\Big)=r|\nabla f(z)|.

Evidently the same holds for every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}\setminus\overline{\{f>0\}}
and thus it holds for almost every
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
as the boundary of
\{f>0\}
always has Lebesgue measure 0. Finally,
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{a^{t}-1}{t}=\ln a
, and the lemma follows.
∎

The next lemma is a variant of
[
2
, Lemma 4.3]
. Similar computations can be found in
[
17
]
.

Lemma 5.2
.

Let
f=e^{-\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
be an integrable log-concave function. Then,

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(f\star(t\cdot f))(x)\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\,dx}{t}=n\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\ln f(x)\,dx.

Proof.

First we observe that

(f\star(t\cdot f))(z)=e^{-(1+t)\psi\left(\frac{z}{1+t}\right)}

for all
z\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. To see this, note that if
z=x+y
then

\displaystyle\psi(x)+t\psi(y/t)

\displaystyle=(1+t)\left(\frac{1}{1+t}\psi(x)+\frac{t}{1+t}\psi(y/t)\right)\geqslant(1+t)\psi\left(\frac{1}{1+t}x+\frac{t}{1+t}\frac{y}{t}\right)

\displaystyle=(1+t)\psi\left(\frac{z}{1+t}\right)

with equality if
x=y/t=z/(1+t)
. Now, we can write

\displaystyle\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(f\star(t\cdot f))(x)\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\,dx}{t}

\displaystyle=\frac{1}{t}\left((1+t)^{n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-(1+t)\psi(x)}dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\psi(x)}dx\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{(1+t)^{n}-1}{t}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-(1+t)\psi(x)}dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}e^{-\psi(x)}\,\frac{e^{-t\psi(x)}-1}{t}\,dx.

Since
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{1}{t}((1+t)^{n}-1)=n
and
\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{e^{-t\psi(x)}-1}{t}=-\psi(x)=\ln f(x)
, applying
the monotone convergence theorem we conclude the proof.
∎

We are now in position to prove Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
1.3
.

From Lemma

By the definition of

it follows that

and hence (

) is satisfied with
\alpha=e^{-3n}f(0)
but with radius
\tfrac{1}{3}
instead of 1. Then, Lemma
5.2
shows that

(5.5)

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}(f\star(t\cdot f))(x)\,dx-\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\,dx}{t}=n\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\ln f(x)\,dx.

On the other hand, Lemma
5.1
shows that

(5.6)

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{(f\star t\cdot(e^{-3n}f(0)\mathds{1}_{\tfrac{1}{3}B_{2}^{n}}))(z)-f(z)}{t}=\frac{1}{3}|\nabla f(z)|+f(z)\ln(e^{-3n}f(0))

almost everywhere. Note that

(f\star t\cdot(a\mathds{1}_{rB_{2}^{n}}))(z)=\sup\left\{f(x)\cdot\mathds{1}_{rB_{2}^{n}}(y/t)a^{t}:z=x+y\right\}=\sup\left\{f(z-rty)a^{t}:y\in B_{2}^{n}\right\}\geqslant a^{t}f(z)

for every
a,r>0
. Therefore,

\displaystyle h_{t}(z):=\frac{(f\star t\cdot(e^{-3n}f(0)\mathds{1}_{\tfrac{1}{3}B_{2}^{n}}))(z)-f(z)}{t}

\displaystyle\geqslant f(z)\frac{(e^{-3n}f(0))^{t}-1}{t}

\displaystyle\geqslant\ln(e^{-3n}f(0))\,f(z).

This last function is integrable, and hence applying Fatou’s lemma to the family of nonnegative functions
\{h_{t}(z)-\ln(e^{-3n}f(0))f(z)\}_{t>0}
and combining (
5.4
)–(
5.6
) we get

(5.7)

\frac{1}{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla f(z)|\,dz+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(z)\ln(e^{-3n}f(0))\,dz\leqslant n\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\,dx+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\ln f(x)\,dx.

Note that
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(z)\,dz=1
and
\nabla f(x)=-f(x)\nabla\psi(x)
. Moreover, since
f
is a centered log-concave function, from Jensen’s inequality
we have

(5.8)

\ln f(0)=\ln f\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}xf(x)dx\right)\geqslant\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\ln f(x)dx.

Therefore, (
5.7
) yields

(5.9)

\displaystyle\frac{1}{3}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\psi(x)|\,d\mu(x)

\displaystyle\leqslant\ln(e^{3n}f(0)^{-1})+n+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\ln f(x)\,dx

\displaystyle\leqslant 4n-\ln f(0)+\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\ln f(x)\,dx\leqslant 4n.

This completes the proof of the theorem.
∎

Remark 5.3
.

Theorem
1.3
contains a sharp upper bound for the first moment of
\nabla\psi
with respect to the isotropic log-concave probability measure
\mu
with density
e^{-\psi}
. This is the best integrability that one can hope for in this setting, even in the one-dimensional case. To see this, fix
p\geqslant 1
and consider the even log-concave function
f_{p}:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}
given by

f_{p}(x)=\frac{p}{2}\frac{\sqrt{\Gamma\!\left(3/p\right)}}{\Gamma\!\left(1/p\right)^{3/2}}\exp\!\left(-\left(\frac{\Gamma\!\left(3/p\right)}{\Gamma\!\left(1/p\right)}\right)^{p/2}|x|^{p}\right)

which satisfies
\int_{\mathbb{R}}f_{p}(x)\,dx=1=\int_{\mathbb{R}}x^{2}f_{p}(x)\ dx
and is thus an isotropic probability density. Moreover,

\int_{\mathbb{R}}|(-\log f_{p})^{\prime}(x)|^{1+\alpha}f_{p}(x)\,dx=\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|f_{p}^{\prime}(x)|^{1+\alpha}}{f_{p}(x)^{\alpha}}\,dx=p^{1+\alpha}\,\left(\frac{\Gamma(3/p)}{\Gamma(1/p)}\right)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\frac{\Gamma\left(\frac{(p-1)(1+\alpha)+1}{p}\right)}{\Gamma(1/p)}

for every
\alpha>0
. Using that
\Gamma(\varepsilon)\sim 1/\varepsilon
as
\varepsilon\to 0^{+}
, we obtain

\int_{\mathbb{R}}\frac{|f_{p}^{\prime}(x)|^{1+\alpha}}{f_{p}(x)^{\alpha}}\,dx\sim p^{1+\alpha}\,\left(\frac{p/3}{p}\right)^{\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\frac{\Gamma(1+\alpha)}{p}=3^{-\frac{1+\alpha}{2}}\Gamma(1+\alpha)p^{\alpha}

which is unbounded as
p\to\infty
.

Remark 5.4
.

A result similar to Theorem
1.3
for log-concave functions in John’s position, rather than in isotropic position,
appears in
[
31
, Remark 3.3]
.

Considering the special case where
\mu
has a radial density, we see that there exist isotropic log-concave probability measures for
which we have a
O(\sqrt{n})
bound.

Proposition 5.5
.

Let
\mu
be a radial isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, where
n\geqslant 2
. Assume that the density
f
of
\mu
is of the form

f=e^{-\psi}
, where
\psi(x)=g(|x|)
for a continuously differentiable function
g:[0,\infty)\to\mathbb{R}
.
Then,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\psi(x)|\,d\mu(x)\leqslant\sqrt{n+1}.

Proof.

We shall use the following result of Borell (see
[
13
, Theorem 2.2.5]
). If
G=e^{-g}:[0,\infty)\to[0,\infty)
is a log-concave function,
then the function

\Psi_{g}(p)=\frac{\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{p}e^{-g(r)}\,dr}{\Gamma(p+1)}

is log-concave on
[0,\infty)
.
Note that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x|^{q}d\mu(x)=n\omega_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{q+n-1}e^{-g(r)}dr=n\omega_{n}\Gamma(q+n)\Psi_{g}(q+n-1)

for every
q>-(n-1)
. Since
\mu
is a probability measure we have

n\omega_{n}\Gamma(n)\Psi_{g}(n-1)=n\omega_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{n-1}e^{-g(r)}dr=\mu(\mathbb{R}^{n})=1,

and since
\mu
is isotropic we also have

n\omega_{n}\Gamma(n+2)\Psi_{g}(n+1)=n\omega_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{n+1}e^{-g(r)}dr=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|x|^{2}d\mu(x)=n.

Recall that
f=e^{-\psi}
with
\psi(x)=g(|x|)
. Therefore, integration by parts shows that

\displaystyle I:=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\psi(x)|\,e^{-\psi(x)}dx

\displaystyle=n\omega_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{n-1}g^{\prime}(r)e^{-g(r)}dr

\displaystyle=(n-1)\,n\omega_{n}\int_{0}^{\infty}r^{n-2}e^{-g(r)}dr

\displaystyle=(n-1)n\omega_{n}\Gamma(n-1)\Psi_{g}(n-2).

Writing
n-1=\frac{2}{3}(n-2)+\frac{1}{3}(n+1)
and using the log-concavity of
\Psi_{g}
we get

\Psi_{g}(n-1)^{3}\geqslant\Psi_{g}(n-2)^{2}\Psi_{g}(n+1)

or equivalently,

\frac{1}{\Gamma(n)^{3}}\geqslant\frac{I^{2}}{(n-1)^{2}\Gamma(n-1)^{2}}\,\frac{n}{\Gamma(n+2)}.

It follows that

I^{2}\leqslant\left(\frac{(n-2)!}{(n-1)!}\right)^{2}\frac{(n+1)!}{(n-1)!}\,\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{n}=\frac{1}{(n-1)^{2}}\,n(n+1)\,\frac{(n-1)^{2}}{n}=n+1,

which proves the proposition. ∎

§5.2. Optimal lower bounds.
The next proposition shows that the quantity
\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\psi(x)|\,d\mu(x)
is always bounded from below by
c\sqrt{n}
.

Proposition 5.6
.

Let
\mu
be an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with essentially continuous density
f=e^{-\psi}
.
Then,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\psi(x)|\,d\mu(x)\geqslant c\sqrt{n},

where
c>0
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Since
f
is essentially continuous, it is also
W^{1,1}
(see
[
54
, Remark 3.3]
) and thus, the classical Sobolev inequality with a sharp constant yields

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\psi(x)|\,d\mu(x)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla f|\,dx\geq n\omega_{n}^{1/n}\bigg(\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f^{\frac{n}{n-1}}\,dx\bigg)^{\frac{n-1}{n}}\geq n\omega_{n}^{1/n}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\,dx\approx\sqrt{n},

where the second inequality follows from Jensen’s inequality.
∎

The next proposition shows the sharpness of Theorem
1.3
.

Proposition 5.7
.

There exists an even isotropic log-concave probability measure
\mu
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\psi(x)|\,d\mu(x)\geqslant cn,

where
c>0
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Let
\nu_{K}
be an isotropic measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with density
f(x)=e^{-\psi(x)}=\frac{1}{n!\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)}e^{-\|x\|_{K}}
, where
K
is a
1
-symmetric convex body
and
\|\cdot\|_{K}
is the norm induced by
K
. Since
K
is
1
-symmetric,
\overline{K}=\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{-1/n}K
is isotropic. Recall from the proof of Proposition
3.9
that
\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{1/n}\approx\frac{1}{n}
and that
R_{t}(\nu_{K})=tK
for every
t>0
. Applying (
3.11
) with
t\to\infty
, we get

\displaystyle\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\psi(x)|\,d\nu_{K}(x)=\frac{S(K)}{n\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)}=\frac{1}{n\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{\frac{1}{n}}}\,\frac{S(K)}{n\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{\frac{n-1}{n}}}\approx\frac{S(K)}{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{\frac{n-1}{n}}}.

It remains to observe that

\frac{S(K)}{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)^{\frac{n-1}{n}}}=\frac{S(\overline{K})}{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(\overline{K})^{\frac{n-1}{n}}}\approx n

if we choose
K
so that
\overline{K}=\frac{1}{2}B_{\infty}^{n}
.
∎

6
Reformulations and geometric applications

In this section we collect a number of reformulations of the gradient estimate from Theorem
1.3
. Some of the results are direct applications
of this estimate, once the relevant language has been established.

§6.1. Moment measure of a log-concave function.
Let
f=e^{-\psi}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
be
a pointwise finite log-concave function with finite positive integral. We define the moment measure
\mu_{f}
of
f
to be the Borel measure
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
which is the push-forward of
fdx
under
\nabla\psi
. Equivalently,

(6.1)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(y)\,d\mu_{f}(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}g(\nabla\psi(x))f(x)\,dx

for every Borel measurable function
g
such that
g\in L^{1}(\mu_{f})
or
g
is non-negative.

The question that was addressed by Cordero-Erausquin and Klartag in
[
20
]
is to characterize those measures
\nu
that
are moment measures of log-concave functions
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with finite positive integral. Under the assumption that
f
is essentially continuous
(or, equivalently, that
\psi
is essentially continuous) they showed that the moment measure
\mu_{f}
of a log-concave function
f
as above has the
following two properties: it is centered and it is not supported by a hyperplane. In particular, they showed
that the first moment of
\mu_{f}
is finite:

(6.2)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|y|\,d\mu_{f}(y)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|\nabla\psi(x)|\,f(x)\,dx<+\infty.

It turns out that these necessary conditions are also sufficient. The main result of
[
20
]
shows that there is a bijection
between essentially-continuous, convex functions
\psi
modulo translations, and finite measures on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
that are centered and not supported by a hyperplane.

Theorem 6.1

(Cordero-Erausquin–Klartag)
.

Let
\nu
be a Borel measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
such that

(i)

0<\nu(\mathbb{R}^{n})<+\infty
.

(ii)

The measure
\nu
is not supported by a lower-dimensional subspace.

(iii)

The barycenter of
\nu
lies at the origin; in particular,
\nu
has finite first moment.

Then, there exists an essentially continuous convex function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\rightarrow\mathbb{R}\cup\{+\infty\}

such that
\nu
is the moment measure of the log-concave function
f=e^{-\psi}
.
Moreover, this function
\psi
is uniquely determined up to translation.

Assuming that
f
is an isotropic log-concave function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, Theorem
1.3
immediately implies a quantitative version
of (
6.2
).

Theorem 6.2
.

Let
\mu_{f}
be the moment measure of an isotropic log-concave function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
Then,

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|y|\,d\mu_{f}(y)\leqslant Cn

for some absolute constant
C>0
.

§6.2. Surface area measures of a log-concave function.
Recall that for every convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
there exists
a Borel measure
S_{K}
on
S^{n-1}
, the surface area measure of
K
, such that

(6.3)

\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K+tL)-\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)}{t}=\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{L}(\xi)\,dS_{K}(\xi)

for every convex body
L
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Choosing
L=B_{2}^{n}
we see that
S_{K}(S^{n-1})
equals the surface area of
K
.

A natural question in “functional convexity” is to extend the notion of the surface area measure of a convex body to the setting
of functions in
{\rm LC}_{n}
. For any log-concave function
f:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
with
0<\int f<\infty
, Rotem considered
in
[
54
]
and
[
55
]
two surface area measures of
f
. Write
f=e^{-\psi}
, where
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]

is a convex function. Then, the first surface area measure
\mu_{f}
of
f
is a measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, defined as the push-forward

\mu_{f}=(\nabla\psi)_{\ast}(fdx).

Note that, in the terminology of §6.1,
\mu_{f}
is precisely the moment measure of
f
. The second surface area measure of
f
is a measure
\nu_{f}

on the sphere
S^{n-1}
, defined as the push-forward

\nu_{f}=(n_{K_{f}})_{\ast}(fd\mathcal{H}^{n-1}|_{\partial K_{f}}),

where
K_{f}=\overline{\{x:\ f(x)>0\}}
is the support of
f
, and
n_{K_{f}}
denotes the Gauss map
n_{K_{f}}:\partial K_{f}\to S^{n-1}
.

For example, if
f(x)=e^{-|x|^{2}/2}
then
\mu_{f}=e^{-|x|^{2}/2}dx
and
\nu_{f}\equiv 0
, because
\partial K_{f}=\partial\mathbb{R}^{n}=\varnothing
.
On the other hand, if
f=\mathds{1}_{K}
for some convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
then
\mu_{\mathds{1}_{K}}=\mathrm{vol}_{n}(K)\,\delta_{0}

and
\nu_{\mathds{1}_{K}}=S_{K}
, the usual surface area measure. The pair
(\mu_{f},\nu_{f})
is the pair of
surface area measures of
f
.

It is useful to observe that we do not assume any regularity for the definitions of
\mu_{f}
and
\nu_{f}
. Since
\psi=-\ln f

is a convex function, it is differentiable almost everywhere with respect to Lebesgue measure on the set
K_{f}=\overline{\{x:\ \psi(x)<\infty\}}
.
Therefore, the push-forward
(\nabla\psi)_{\ast}(fdx)
is well-defined. Similarly, since
K_{f}
is a closed convex set, its
boundary
\partial K_{f}
is a Lipschitz manifold, and hence the Gauss map
n_{K_{f}}
is defined
\mathcal{H}^{n-1}
-almost everywhere
and the push-forward is again well-defined.

Motivated by (
6.3
), for any
f,g\in{\rm LC}_{n}
one may consider the first variation of the integral of
f

in the direction of
g
, defined by

(6.4)

\delta(f,g)=\lim_{t\to 0^{+}}\frac{\int f\star\left(t\cdot g\right)-\int f}{t}.

Under some additional regularity assumptions,
\delta(f,g)
was studied by Colesanti and Fragalà in
[
17
]
.
The same first variation appears in the work of Cordero-Erausquin and Klartag
[
20
]
on moment measures that we
briefly discussed in §6.1, as a step in their proof of the Minkowski-type existence Theorem
6.1
. Rotem dropped
the additional hypotheses in
[
54
]
and proved that if
f=e^{-\psi},g=e^{-\varphi}\in{\rm LC}_{n}
,
0<\int f<\infty
and
\nu_{f}=0
then

(6.5)

\delta(f,g)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h_{g}d\mu_{f}

with no regularity assumptions, where

h_{g}=\mathcal{L}\varphi

is the Legendre transform of
\varphi
, which is the functional analogue of the support function of
g
. Note that since
f
is log-concave
and upper semi-continuous, it is only discontinuous at points
x\in\partial K_{f}

such that
f(x)\neq 0
. Therefore the condition
\nu_{f}=0
is equivalent
to the statement that
f
is essentially continuous. In his subsequent work
[
55
]
, Rotem obtained a very general version of (
6.5
).

Theorem 6.3

(Rotem)
.

Let
f,g\in{\rm LC}_{n}
such that
0<\int f<\infty
. Then,

(6.6)

\delta(f,g)=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}h_{g}d\mu_{f}+\int_{S^{n-1}}h_{K_{g}}d\nu_{f}.

Rotem proved in
[
55
, Proposition 1.6]
that
\nu_{f}(S^{n-1})<+\infty
. Assuming that
f
is an isotropic log-concave function on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,
we shall complement the estimate of Theorem
6.2
by providing a similar estimate for
\nu_{f}(S^{n-1})
. More precisely, we will show that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|y|\,d\mu_{f}(y)+\nu_{f}(S^{n-1})\leq Cn.

This is the content of Theorem
1.4
and it is again a consequence of Theorem
1.3
.

Proof of Theorem
1.4
.

Write
f=e^{-\psi}
for a proper lower semi-continuous convex function
\psi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
. For
\lambda>0
,
let
\psi_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
be the infimum convolution of
\psi
with the function
\frac{1}{2\lambda}|x|^{2}
, namely

\psi_{\lambda}(x)=\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big\{\psi(y)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}|x-y|^{2}\Big\}.

The function
\psi_{\lambda}
is also referred to as the Moreau envelope of
\psi
, see
[
53
, Definition 1.22]
. Notice that
\psi_{\lambda}
is always finite and
\psi_{\lambda}(x)\leqslant\psi(x)
at points
x
where
\psi(x)<\infty
. Observe further that the function

\Theta_{\lambda}(x,y)=\psi(y)+\frac{1}{2\lambda}|x-y|^{2}

is jointly convex in
(x,y)
and thus
\psi_{\lambda}=\inf_{y}\Theta_{\lambda}(\cdot,y)
is also convex. Moreover, it is a classical fact (see, e.g.,
[
53
, Theorem 2.26 (b)]
) that
\psi_{\lambda}
is of class
C^{1}
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.

Recall that a sequence of convex lower semi-continuous functions
\phi_{n}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]

epi-converges to
\phi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(-\infty,\infty]
if for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
the following conditions are met:

(i) for every sequence
x_{n}\to x
, we have

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\phi_{n}(x_{n})\geqslant\phi(x)

(ii) there exists a sequence
x_{n}\to x
for which

\limsup_{n\to\infty}\phi_{n}(x_{n})\leqslant\phi(x).

Given a sequence
\lambda_{n}\searrow 0
as
n\to\infty
we will show that the sequence of functions
\{\psi_{\lambda_{n}}\}_{n\geqslant 1}
epi-converges to
\psi
. It is well known that
\psi_{\lambda_{n}}
converges pointwise to
\psi
as
n\to\infty
(see, e.g.,
[
53
, Theorem 1.25]
) and thus property (ii) holds trivially for
x_{n}=x
. To verify property (i), fix some
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
and a sequence
x_{n}\to x
. By the Fenchel–Moreau theorem, since
\psi
is proper, lower semi-continuous and convex, we have

(6.7)

\psi(x)=\sup\big\{\ell(x):\ \ell\ \mbox{is an affine function with }\ell\leqslant\psi\big\}

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore, for such an affine function
\ell(y)=\langle y,a\rangle+b
, we also have

\begin{split}\psi_{\lambda_{n}}(x_{n})\geqslant\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big\{\ell(y)+\frac{1}{2\lambda_{n}}|x_{n}-y|^{2}\Big\}=\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big\{\langle y,a\rangle+b+\frac{1}{2\lambda_{n}}|x_{n}-y|^{2}\Big\}=\langle x_{n},a\rangle+b-\frac{\lambda_{n}}{2}|a|^{2}\end{split}

as the infimum is attained at
y=x_{n}-\lambda_{n}a
. Therefore,

\liminf_{n\to\infty}\psi_{\lambda_{n}}(x_{n})\geqslant\langle x,a\rangle+b=\ell(x)

and condition (i) follows from equation (
6.7
). This establishes that
\psi_{\lambda}
epi-converges to
\psi
as
\lambda\to 0^{+}
.

Now consider
f_{\lambda}=e^{-\psi_{\lambda}}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to(0,\infty)
and observe that
f_{\lambda}
is a
C^{1}
log-concave function. Since
f
is integrable, there exist constants
c_{1},c_{2}>0
such that

\psi(x)\geqslant c_{1}|x|-c_{1}

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. Therefore, we also have

\begin{split}\psi_{\lambda}(x)\geqslant\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big\{c_{1}|y|-c_{2}+\frac{1}{2\lambda}|x-y|^{2}\Big\}&\geqslant\inf_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}}\Big\{c_{1}|y|-c_{2}+\frac{1}{2\lambda}\big(|x|^{2}-2|x||y|+|y|^{2}\big)\Big\}\\
&=\inf_{t\geqslant 0}\Big\{\frac{1}{2\lambda}|x|^{2}-c_{2}+\Big(c_{1}-\frac{|x|}{\lambda}\Big)t+\frac{t^{2}}{2\lambda}\Big\}.\end{split}

The infimum of the latter expression is attained at
t_{\ast}=\lambda c_{1}-|x|
. If
t_{\ast}\geqslant 0
, this gives

\psi_{\lambda}(x)\geqslant c_{1}|x|-c_{2}-\frac{\lambda c_{1}^{2}}{2}

whereas if
t_{\ast}<0
then plugging
t=0
we get

\psi_{\lambda}(x)\geqslant\frac{1}{2\lambda}|x|^{2}-c_{2}\geqslant c_{1}|x|-c_{2}-\frac{\lambda c_{1}^{2}}{2},

since the second inequality holds for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. This way we conclude that

f_{\lambda}(x)\leqslant\exp\big(-c_{1}|x|+c_{2}+\tfrac{\lambda c_{1}^{2}}{2}\big)

for every
x\in\mathbb{R}^{n}
. In particular, the integrands of the parameters

Z_{\lambda}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f_{\lambda}(x)\,dx,\ \ \ m_{\lambda}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}x\,f_{\lambda}(x)\,dx\ \ \ \mbox{and}\ \ \ \Sigma_{\lambda}=\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\big(x-m_{\lambda})\otimes(x-m_{\lambda})\,f_{\lambda}(x)\,dx

are all bounded above by uniformly integrable functions when
\lambda\in(0,1)
. Since
f_{\lambda}
converges pointwise to
f
, the dominated convergence theorem yields

Z_{\lambda}\longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}f(x)\,dx=1,\ \ \ m_{\lambda}\longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}x\,f(x)\,dx=\operatorname{bar}(f)=0

and

\Sigma_{\lambda}\longrightarrow\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}x\otimes x\,f(x)\,dx=\operatorname{Cov}(f)=I_{n}

as
\lambda\to 0^{+}
. In other words, the renormalized
C^{1}
log-concave functions

F_{\lambda}(x)=\frac{|\det\Sigma_{\lambda}|^{1/2}}{Z_{\lambda}}f_{\lambda}\big(\Sigma_{\lambda}^{1/2}x+m_{\lambda}\big)

are isotropic and converge to
f
in the topology of epi-convergence of their convex potentials.

Write
F_{\lambda}=e^{-\Psi_{\lambda}}
for some
C^{1}
convex function
\Psi_{\lambda}:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
. Invoking a recent result of Falah and Rotem
[
25
, Theorem 1.9]
, we deduce that the pair of surface area measures
(\mu_{F_{\lambda}},\nu_{F_{\lambda}})
of
F_{\lambda}
converges cosmically to
(\mu_{f},\nu_{f})
as
\lambda\to 0^{+}
, that is, for every continuous function
\xi:\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}
for which the limit

\overline{\xi}(\theta)=\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\frac{\xi(\lambda\theta)}{\lambda}

exists (in the finite sense) uniformly in
\theta\in S^{n-1}
, we have

(6.8)

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\xi(y)\,d\mu_{f}(y)+\int_{S^{n-1}}\overline{\xi}(\theta)\,d\nu_{f}(\theta)=\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}\xi(y)\,d\mu_{F_{\lambda}}(y)+\int_{S^{n-1}}\overline{\xi}(\theta)\,d\nu_{F_{\lambda}}(\theta).

However, since
F_{\lambda}
are
C^{1}
and positive on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
, they are in particular essentially continuous. Thus
\nu_{F_{\lambda}}=0
and
\mu_{F_{\lambda}}
is the moment measure of
F_{\lambda}
. Plugging
\xi(y)=|y|
in (
6.8
), for which
\overline{\xi}(\theta)\equiv 1
, we finally conclude that

\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|y|\,d\mu_{f}(y)+\nu_{f}(S^{n-1})=\lim_{\lambda\to 0^{+}}\int_{\mathbb{R}^{n}}|y|\,d\mu_{F_{\lambda}}(y)\leqslant Cn

by Theorem
6.2
as the functions
F_{\lambda}
are isotropic log-concave.
∎

Combining Theorem
1.4
with the generalized co-area formula (
2.2
) of
[
54
, Theorem 3.2]
, we derive the following general statement about the perimeter of super-level sets of isotropic log-concave functions.

Corollary 6.4
.

There exists a universal constant
C>0
such that for every isotropic log-concave function
f
on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
,

(6.9)

\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{x:\ f(x)\geqslant t\})\,dt\leqslant Cn.

§6.3. Projections of a log-concave function.
Let
f:{\mathbb{R}}^{n}\to[0,\infty)
be a log-concave function.
Given
E\in G_{n,k}
, where
G_{n,k}
is the Grassmann manifold of
k
-dimensional subspaces of
{\mathbb{R}}^{n}
, the “section” of

f
with
E
is the restriction
f\big|_{E}
of
f
onto
E
and the “projection” or “shadow” of
f
onto
E
is the function

P_{E}f(x):=\max\{f(x+y):y\in E^{\perp}\},\hskip 9.24994ptx\in E

where
E^{\perp}
is the orthogonal subspace of
E
. For every log-concave function
g
and any
t>0
we define
\tilde{R}_{t}(g)=\{x:g(x)\geqslant t\}
,
t>0
. Note that
R_{s}(g)=\tilde{R}_{e^{-s}g(0)}(g)
for every
s>0
. It is not hard to check that

(6.10)

\tilde{R}_{t}(P_{E}g)=P_{E}(\tilde{R}_{t}(g))\hskip 9.24994pt\text{and hence}\hskip 9.24994ptR_{s}(P_{E}g)=P_{E}(R_{s}(g))

for every
t,s>0
. We denote by
\nu_{n,k}
the uniform probability measure on
G_{n,k}
.

Theorem 6.5
.

Let
\mu
be an isotropic log-concave probability measure on
\mathbb{R}^{n}
with density
f=e^{-\psi}

such that
f(0)=\|f\|_{\infty}
. Then,

\int_{G_{n,n-1}}\|P_{E}f\|_{1}d\nu_{n,n-1}(E)\leqslant C\sqrt{n},

where
C>0
is an absolute constant.

Proof.

Cauchy’s surface area formula asserts that

(6.11)

S(K)=\frac{n\omega_{n}}{\omega_{n-1}}\int_{G_{n,n-1}}\mathrm{vol}_{n-1}(P_{E}(K))\,d\nu_{n,n-1}(E)

for every convex body
K
in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
. On the other hand, for any
E\in G_{n,n-1}
we have

\displaystyle\|P_{E}f\|_{1}

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\|f\|_{\infty}}\mathrm{vol}_{n-1}(\tilde{R}_{t}(P_{E}f))\,dt=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}\|f\|_{\infty}\mathrm{vol}_{n-1}(R_{s}(P_{E}f))\,ds

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}\|f\|_{\infty}\mathrm{vol}_{n-1}(P_{E}(R_{s}(f)))\,ds,

taking into account (
6.10
). Combining the above, we write

\displaystyle\int_{G_{n,n-1}}\|P_{E}f\|_{1}d\nu_{n,n-1}(E)

\displaystyle=\int_{G_{n,n-1}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}\|f\|_{\infty}\mathrm{vol}_{n-1}(P_{E}(R_{s}(f)))\,ds\,d\nu_{n,n-1}(E)

\displaystyle=\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}\|f\|_{\infty}\int_{G_{n,n-1}}\mathrm{vol}_{n-1}(P_{E}(R_{s}(f)))\,d\nu_{n,n-1}(E)\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{n-1}}{n\omega_{n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}e^{-s}\|f\|_{\infty}S(R_{s}(f))\,ds

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{n-1}}{n\omega_{n}}\int_{0}^{\infty}\mathcal{H}^{n-1}(\partial\{x:\ f(x)\geq t\})\,dt\leqslant C\sqrt{n},

from Corollary
6.4
and the fact that
\frac{\omega_{n-1}}{n\omega_{n}}\approx\frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}
.
∎

Note that if
K
is an isotropic convex body in
\mathbb{R}^{n}
and
f=L_{K}^{n}\mathds{1}_{K/L_{K}}
then
P_{E}f=L_{K}^{n}\mathds{1}_{P_{E}(K/L_{K})}
. Therefore,

\displaystyle\int_{G_{n,n-1}}\|P_{E}f\|_{1}d\nu_{n,n-1}(E)

\displaystyle=L_{K}\int_{G_{n,n-1}}\mathrm{vol}_{n-1}(P_{E}(K))\,d\nu_{n,n-1}(E)

\displaystyle\approx\int_{G_{n,n-1}}\mathrm{vol}_{n-1}(P_{E}(K))\,d\nu_{n,n-1}(E)

in this case. Consider the example of the cube
Q_{n}=\left[-\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2}\right]^{n}
. Then,

\displaystyle\int_{G_{n,n-1}}\mathrm{vol}_{n-1}(P_{E}(Q_{n}))\,d\nu_{n,n-1}(E)

\displaystyle=\int_{S^{n-1}}\mathrm{vol}_{n-1}(P_{\xi^{\perp}}(Q_{n}))\,d\sigma(\xi)

\displaystyle=\frac{\omega_{n-1}}{n\omega_{n}}S(Q_{n})=\frac{2\omega_{n-1}}{\omega_{n}}\approx\sqrt{n}.

This shows that the upper bound that we obtained in Theorem
6.5
is optimal with respect to the dimension.
