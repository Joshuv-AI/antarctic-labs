Two is better than one: A Collapse-free Multi-Reward RLIF Training Framework
Shourov Joarder, Diganta Sikdar, Ahsan Habib Akash, Binod Bhattarai, Prashnna Gyawali, [4pt] Bangladesh University of Engineering, Technology, Bangladesh

Abstract
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has substantially improved the reasoning ability of LLMs, but often depends on external supervision from human annotations or gold-standard solutions. Reinforcement learning from internal feedback (RLIF) has recently emerged as a scalable unsupervised alternative, using signals extracted from the model itself. However, existing RLIF methods typically rely on a single internal reward, which can lead to reward hacking, entropy collapse, and degraded reasoning structure. We propose a multi-reward RLIF framework that decomposes the training signal into two complementary components: an answer-level reward based on cluster voting and a completion-level reward based on token-wise self-certainty. To combine these signals robustly, we apply GDPO-based normalization to reduce reward-scale imbalance. We further introduce KL-Cov regularization, which targets low-entropy token distributions responsible for disproportionate entropy reduction, preserving exploration and preventing late-stage collapse. Across mathematical reasoning and code-generation benchmarks, our method improves stability and robustness over prior unsupervised RL approaches, while achieving performance close to supervised RLVR methods. These results show that complementary internal rewards, combined with targeted regularization, can support stable long-horizon reasoning without relying on external ground-truth supervision. Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) have achieved striking success across domains, yet training them to reason through complex problems remains an open and central challenge in modern AI.
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has emerged as a powerful paradigm, enabling models such as DeepSeek-R1
(
5
)
and MiMo
(
21
)
to
attain
strong mathematical reasoning through reward signals derived from ground-truth answer verification.
However, RLVR critically depends on external supervision such as human-labeled reasoning traces, verified
reference
answers, or pre-trained reward models which limits scalability and practical applicability across domains where such supervision is unavailable, costly, or difficult to obtain.

Reinforcement Learning with Internal Feedback (RLIF) offers an unsupervised alternative that eliminates the need for external supervision entirely.
Instead of relying on ground-truth labels, RLIF methods
derive
reward signals
solely from intrinsic model signals:
either through consensus-based measures such as majority voting and semantic clustering
(
24
)
, or through entropy minimization measures such as the self-certainty score
(
28
)
.
These approaches have demonstrated that meaningful reasoning improvements are achievable without
labeled data, making them
a promising path toward
scaling LLM reasoning to arbitrary domains
(
30
;
27
;
16
)
.

Despite these promising results, existing RLIF methods exhibit recurring failure patterns, including reward hacking and model collapse
(
1
;
16
)
. Prior work has observed that models often achieve strong initial gains but gradually degrade after extended training. We argue that this decline stems from the structure of the reward signals themselves. For example, self-certainty rewards high-confidence token distributions, effectively using entropy minimization as a proxy for output quality. Majority voting, though consensus-based rather than explicitly entropy-driven, similarly favors outputs that the model already assigns high probability to, thereby indirectly reinforcing low-entropy behavior. Under both signals, the policy is incentivized to reduce token-level uncertainty, whether or not this reduction improves reasoning quality.

This incentive creates two compounding failure modes. First, high-entropy transitional tokens, such as
however
,
thus
,
because
, and
suppose
, which often serve as structural anchors in chain-of-thought reasoning, are disproportionately penalized
(
25
)
. This can weaken logical coherence even when final-answer accuracy temporarily improves. Second, because shorter responses contain fewer uncertain tokens, the reward implicitly favors brevity over depth
(
9
)
, encouraging terse, high-probability outputs rather than genuine improvements in reasoning quality.
This pattern of early improvement followed by degradation is an empirical instance of Goodhart’s Law
(
6
)
: when a single proxy becomes the sole optimization target, the policy learns to exploit the proxy rather than improve the underlying capability. As optimization proceeds, the reward signal and true task performance can decouple, leading to overoptimization.

This motivates a multi-reward RLIF objective that jointly optimizes structurally complementary signals rather than relying on a single proxy.
Specifically, we propose a multi-reward RLIF framework that combines answer agreement and model confidence as intrinsic rewards.
We then introduce a targeted regularization strategy to reduce collapse during optimization.
To prevent either reward from dominating optimization, we normalize the advantages induced by each reward independently.
We further introduce a targeted regularization strategy to reduce collapse during optimization. In particular, to mitigate entropy collapse while preserving exploration, we use KL-Cov regularization, inspired by
4
, which targets the small subset of tokens responsible for disproportionate entropy reduction while maintaining diversity in the remaining token distribution.
Across five diverse mathematical reasoning benchmarks and two coding benchmarks, we show that our method outperforms several baselines, including single-reward RLIF frameworks such as self-certainty and majority voting
28
;
30
while maintaining stable gains under continued training. This stability is crucial for RLIF: as models scale and reasoning trajectories grow more complex, training must preserve exploration beyond early high-confidence behaviors rather than collapse into narrow reward-seeking patterns.

Our contributions can be summarized as follows:

•

We propose a multi-reward RLIF framework with GDPO normalization that jointly optimizes answer-level consensus and completion-level self-certainty, mitigating the failure modes of single-proxy optimization.

•

We utilized KL-Cov regularization to the unsupervised multi-reward setting as a targeted policy constraint to high-covariance entropy-collapsing tokens while preserving exploration over the remaining token distribution.

•

Across five mathematical reasoning benchmarks and two coding benchmarks, we show that our method improves performance over single-reward RLIF baselines and remains stable under continued training.

Related Work

Reinforcement learning with internal feedback (RLIF).

To eliminate external supervision, recent work has explored RLIF, where the model’s own predictions serve as the reward signal.
INTUITOR
(
28
)
uses the average KL divergence between a uniform distribution and the model’s next-token distribution as a self-certainty score, rewarding more confident reasoning traces.
EMPO
(
24
)
takes a complementary approach, employing entropy minimization by semantically clustering rollout answers and rewarding rollouts proportionally to their cluster size.
TTRL
(
30
)
shows that majority voting yields surprisingly effective rewards for test-time RL without ground-truth labels.
Absolute Zero
(
27
)
and R-Zero
(
11
)
propose self-play frameworks where models generate their own training tasks, removing the need for curated datasets entirely.

29
extend RL-based reasoning to general domains without verifiers by maximizing the probability of generating reference answers.
Co-rewarding
(
26
)
seeks complementary supervision from multiple views to stabilize self-supervised RL, addressing the training collapse that single-view signals cause.
SSR-Zero
(
23
)
extends self-rewarding RL to machine translation, demonstrating that the paradigm generalizes beyond mathematical reasoning.
While these methods advance unsupervised RL in various directions, they each rely on a single reward signal or a single feedback mechanism, making them susceptible to the failure modes described in Section
1
.

Entropy minimization, entropy collapse, and model collapse.

Uncontrolled entropy minimization, rewarding more confident outputs to improve reasoning without any labels
(
1
;
16
)
, is also the primary driver of collapse.
2
formalize how clipping bias under spurious rewards reduces policy entropy, and
4
show that
{\sim}95\%
of entropy reduction happens in the earliest training steps, with a tiny
{\sim}0.02\%
fraction of high-covariance tokens with anomalously large centered cross-products between log-probability and advantage dominating the policy gradient and driving entropy to zero. The downstream pathology is well-documented:
17
report complete model collapse in self-training (identical outputs regardless of input),
25
show that all RLIF approaches exhibit a rise-then-fall trajectory attributable to over-optimization of a single internal signal, and
9
confirm this pattern across intrinsic-reward methods at scale, with collapse timing set by model priors.

Multi-objective RL and reward combination.

Combining multiple reward signals in RL is challenging because naive summation leads to scale dominance, where the reward with larger variance overwhelms the gradient signal.
GDPO
(
15
)
addresses this by normalizing each reward channel independently within groups before combination, ensuring that no single reward dominates purely due to scale differences.
Our work builds on the GDPO normalization principle by applying it in the context: combining answer-level and completion-level rewards for unsupervised LLM training, paired with covariance-aware regularization to prevent entropy collapse.

3
Method

We present a multi-reward RLIF framework.
The framework combines answer-level agreement, completion-level self-certainty, group-wise reward normalization, and covariance-aware regularization within a GRPO-style policy optimization objective.

At a high level, for each prompt
q
, our method samples
G
completions from the rollout policy
\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}
. From each completion, we extract the final answer and cluster the resulting answers by equivalence. Each completion is assigned two rewards: an answer-agreement reward determined by the size of its answer cluster, and a completion-level confidence reward computed using self-certainty. We normalize each reward channel within the
G
-sample group and form a rollout-level advantage from their weighted sum. The policy is then optimized with a GRPO-style objective, with additional log-ratio regularization applied to high-covariance tokens. Figure
1
illustrates the overall training pipeline.

Figure 1
:
Given a prompt, we sample G completions from the rollout policy and compute two intrinsic rewards: an answer-level cluster-voting reward over final-answer agreement, and a completion-level self-certainty reward over token-level predictive distributions. Each reward is normalized within the prompt group before being combined into rollout-level advantages. Optimization uses a GRPO-style objective, while KL-Cov penalizes only high-covariance tokens to regularize concentrated updates.

3.1
Preliminaries

Let
\pi_{\theta}
denote the current policy,
\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}
denote the rollout policy used to generate the current training batch, and
\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}
denote a frozen reference policy. Given a prompt
q
, we sample
G
completions from
\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}
. The
g
-th completion is denoted by
c_{g}=(c_{g,1},\ldots,c_{g,T_{g}})
, where
T_{g}
is its length, and its extracted final answer is denoted by
a_{g}
. During optimization, each token in completion
c_{g}
inherits the rollout-level advantage
A_{g}
computed from the reward signals.

We optimize the policy using a GRPO-style clipped surrogate objective
(
18
)
. For a generated token
y_{i}
with inherited advantage
A_{i}
, let

\rho_{i}=\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid q,y_{<i})/\pi_{\theta_{\mathrm{old}}}(y_{i}\mid q,y_{<i})

be the importance sampling ratio between the current and rollout policies. The per-token GRPO loss is

\mathcal{L}_{i}^{\text{GRPO}}(\theta)=-\min\!\Big[\rho_{i}A_{i},\;\text{clip}(\rho_{i},\,1{-}\epsilon,\,1{+}\epsilon)\,A_{i}\Big]

(1)

where
\epsilon>0
is the clipping parameter. The following sections describe how we combine multiple unsupervised reward signals and add targeted regularization for high-covariance tokens.

3.2
Answer-Level Reward via Cluster Voting

The first reward channel assigns higher reward to completions whose final answers agree with many other sampled completions for the prompt.
Given
G
rollouts for a prompt
q
,
we extract the final answers
a_{1},\ldots,a_{G}
and group them into
C
clusters by exact equivalence on the normalized answer string.
Rather than binary majority voting, which assigns reward only to the most popular answer, we assign each rollout
g
a reward proportional to the fraction with equivalent answers:

R_{\text{cluster}}(a_{g})=\begin{cases}\big|\{g^{\prime}\in\{1,\ldots,G\}:a_{g^{\prime}}\equiv a_{g}\}\big|\,/\,n_{\text{parseable}}&\text{if }a_{g}\text{ is
parseable},\\
0&\text{otherwise,}\end{cases}

(2)

where
n_{\text{parseable}}
is the number of rollouts (out of
G
) whose answer string parses successfully. For mathematical problems, the equivalence is measured with numerical consistency.
This provides a denser signal than binary majority voting: minority clusters receive small but nonzero rewards rather than zero, and this type of clustering is shown to be robust to surface-level variation in equivalent answers
(
24
;
13
)
.
In implementation, we additionally include a small auxiliary boxed-format reward (weight
0.1
) alongside the cluster reward to gate non-parseable completions (reported in in Appendix
F
).

3.3
Completion-Level Reward via Self-Certainty

Following
28
, we use self-certainty as a completion-level reward. Let
\mathcal{V}
denote the vocabulary and let
\mathcal{U}
denote the uniform distribution over
\mathcal{V}
. For a completion
c_{g}=(c_{g,1},\ldots,c_{g,T_{g}})
, we compute the self-certainty reward online from the current policy as

R_{\text{cert}}(c_{g})=\frac{1}{T_{g}}\sum_{t=1}^{T_{g}}D_{\text{KL}}\!\Big(\mathcal{U}\;\Big\|\;\pi_{\theta}(\cdot\mid q,c_{g,<t})\Big)

(3)

where
c_{g,<t}=(c_{g,1},\ldots,c_{g,t-1})
is the prefix before
token

t
of generation
g
.
Used in isolation, self-certainty
has been shown to
favor degenerate policies that produce overly deterministic or short templated completions regardless of the input
(
17
;
25
;
9
)
.
Our
qualitative examples (in Appendix
G
) shows similar reward-hacking,
with self-certainty completions reusing similar tokens across questions.
Consistently,
our own single-reward decomposition (Figure
3
)
demonstrate that self-certainty reward alone
collapses to
{\sim}100
-token templates with token-distribution entropy in
[0.03,0.17]
.

This motivates our multi-reward design, which combines self-certainty with cluster voting instead of optimizing either signal alone. These rewards provide complementary signals: self-certainty scores the policy’s confidence during completion generation, whereas cluster voting scores agreement among sampled final answers.

3.4
Group-Wise Multi-Reward Normalization

Since the cluster and self-certainty rewards have different numerical scales, cluster voting produces values in
[0,1]
while self-certainty values depend on vocabulary size, naive combination
can cause
one signal to dominate the advantage purely because of scale and the performance degrades. We confirm this empirically in Appendix
E
: replacing per-channel
z
-scoring with a single combine-then-normalize step degrades downstream accuracy across
different datasets
(Figure
6
), and a logprob-space gradient diagnostic (Figure
7
) shows that
the higher-variance self-certainty channel dominates the per-token gradient by roughly an order of magnitude whereas the cluster channel is effectively silenced.
To avoid this, we normalize each reward channel independently within the group of
G
completions for the same prompt, following the GDPO principle
(
15
)
.
For each reward channel
k\in\{\text{cluster},\text{cert}\}
, we compute the group mean and standard deviation as
\mu_{k}=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{g=1}^{G}R_{k}(g)
and
\sigma_{k}=\text{std}(\{R_{k}(g)\}_{g=1}^{G})
.
The normalized reward for rollout
g
under channel
k
is:

\hat{R}_{k}(g)=\frac{R_{k}(g)-\mu_{k}}{\sigma_{k}+\epsilon}

(4)

where
\epsilon>0
is a small constant for numerical stability.

We then combine the normalized rewards into a single rollout-level advantage:

A_{g}=\alpha\cdot\hat{R}_{\text{cluster}}(a_{g})+\beta\cdot\hat{R}_{\text{cert}}(c_{g})

(5)

where
\alpha,\beta

\geq

0
are reward weights.
The weights
\alpha
and
\beta
then control the relative importance of answer quality versus reasoning quality. For this study, we empirically set them as
\alpha
=
\beta
= 0.5.

3.5
KL-Cov: Covariance-Aware Policy Optimization

We observe that two rewards provide complementary supervision, but each also induces a different degeneration mode when optimized alone.
Cluster-only training drifts in answer space, with rising entropy and lengthening completions, while self-certainty collapses token-space entropy, producing peaked distributions and short
{\sim}100
-token templates (Figure
3
).
Although GDPO normalization mitigates these instabilities by balancing the two reward channels, the resulting policy remains vulnerable to collapse. In our ablation, the GDPO-normalized combination still degenerates around step 240 when trained without additional exploration regularization (Figure
4
).

We therefore require a regularizer that targets the tokens responsible for collapse without unnecessarily constraining the rest of the policy. We therefore adopt KL-Cov: each batch we identify the top-
k
(
k{=}0.02
) tokens by covariance and apply a targeted KL penalty only to them, leaving the remaining
{\sim}98\%
under the standard clipped GRPO surrogate.
Using the set of
N
batch tokens
\{y_{1},\ldots,y_{N}\}
defined in Section
3.1
, we compute
a covariance estimate between token log-probabilities and rollout advantages as the average centered cross-product across tokens:

\text{Cov}(y_{i})=\left(\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid y_{<i})-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{j}\mid y_{<j})\right)\cdot\left(A_{i}-\frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^{N}A_{j}\right)

(6)

A large positive
\text{Cov}(y_{i})
means that the token is more probable than average and has a higher than average advantage, or that the token is less probable than average and has a lower average advantage. The first scenario encourages entropy collapse and the second scenario basically has no effective gradient learning. We rank all
N
tokens by covariance and select the top-
k
fraction as the high-covariance set:

\mathcal{I}_{\text{KL}}=\big\{i\in\{1,\ldots,N\}\;\big|\;\text{Rank}\!\big(\text{Cov}(y_{i})\big)\leq k\cdot N\big\},\quad k\ll 1

(7)

where
\text{Rank}(\cdot)
orders tokens from highest to lowest covariance, so rank 1 is the most problematic token. The per-token loss depends on whether the token belongs to
\mathcal{I}_{\text{KL}}
:

\mathcal{L}(y_{i};\theta)=\begin{cases}-\min\!\Big[\rho_{i}A_{i},\;\text{clip}(\rho_{i},1{-}\epsilon,1{+}\epsilon)\,A_{i}\Big]&\text{if }i\notin\mathcal{I}_{\text{KL}}\\[6.0pt]
-\rho_{i}A_{i}+\beta_{\text{cov}}\big|\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid y_{<i})-\log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i}\mid y_{<i})\big|&\text{if }i\in\mathcal{I}_{\text{KL}}\end{cases}

(8)

The first case is the standard clipped GRPO surrogate (Eq.
1
).
The second case replaces clipping with a targeted penalty: the term
|\log\pi_{\theta}(y_{i}\mid y_{<i})-\log\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(y_{i}\mid y_{<i})|=|\log\rho_{i}|
is the absolute log-importance-ratio, an
\ell_{1}
surrogate for the token-level KL between
\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}
and
\pi_{\theta}
that mirrors the targeted KL penalty of
4
while remaining symmetric in the direction of the shift;
\beta_{\text{cov}}>0
controls the penalty strength.

Total Loss:
The total batch loss, with a global KL penalty against the frozen reference policy
\pi_{\text{ref}}
, is:

\mathcal{L}(\theta)=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathcal{L}(y_{i};\theta)\;+\;\gamma\,D_{\text{KL}}(\pi_{\theta}\|\pi_{\text{ref}})

(9)

where
\gamma\geq 0
is the reference KL coefficient of GRPO.
Following
(
4
)
, we use
k=0.02
, as the
{\sim}98\%
of tokens with normal covariance receive the standard clipped GRPO update, while the
{\sim}2\%
of outlier tokens, those responsible for gradient concentration, receive the additional KL penalty that prevents entropy collapse.
This targets only collapse-driving tokens, preserving exploration without degrading optimization for most tokens.

Single- & Multi-reward
analysis without KL-Cov.

Here, we analyze the effect of single- and multi-reward structures in the absence of KL-Cov regularization by comparing three reward settings based on cluster voting, self-certainty, and their combination.
All three settings are initialized from Qwen2.5-1.5B-Base and trained for 280 steps. Figure

5
Discussion

Figure
3
suggests that cluster voting and self-certainty expose complementary reward-hacking directions. The cluster-voting reward depends only on the extracted answer and is invariant to the token-level reasoning path. As a result, the policy can exploit this objective by drifting in reasoning space while concentrating final answers into a dominant cluster, producing long, high-entropy completions after collapse. Conversely, self-certainty acts directly on token probabilities but is invariant to answer correctness, allowing the policy to exploit the reward by becoming confidently deterministic, yielding short template-like outputs.
The multi-reward objective partially mitigates these failures because each reward constrains a direction left under specified by the other. Cluster voting discourages arbitrary confident templates that do not produce consistent answers, while self-certainty constrains unconstrained token-level drift under cluster voting. Empirically, this widens the stable training window and yields more controlled entropy and length dynamics, avoiding both the entropy rebound of cluster-only and the premature entropy collapse of self-certainty-only training.

Multi-objective reward design is motivated by the idea that individual signals capture distinct but incomplete aspects of desired behavior and can be additively combined
(
28
)
. Our work provides the first empirical demonstration of this effect in reasoning-training. However, though multi-reward training closes off single-reward loopholes ,complementarity alone is insufficient and KL-Cov remains necessary to stabilize the residual optimization dynamics.

Limitations.

Our framework introduces additional hyperparameters beyond standard GRPO, including the top-
k
covariance fraction
\beta_{\text{cov}}
and the channel weights
\alpha
and
\beta
. The KL-Cov sweep in Figure
4
suggests that performance is largely insensitive to
\beta_{\text{cov}}
across a
200{\times}
range. However, we do not conduct a systematic study of the relative weighting
\alpha/\beta
and instead adopt the symmetric default
\alpha=\beta=0.5
throughout.
In addition, the method relies on a sufficiently capable base model: the pretrained model’s initial reasoning ability plays an important role in its success. The proposed framework does not include safeguards for models with poor initial reasoning capability.

6
Conclusion

This paper presents a multi-reward framework with KL-Cov regularization for reinforcement learning from internal feedback (RLIF) mechanism in large language models. In response to the collapse observed under single-reward training, our approach combines two complementary intrinsic signals. Together, these rewards provide a richer training signal that mitigates the distinct degeneracies induced by either reward alone. KL-Cov further stabilizes optimization by targeting the small subset of tokens responsible for disproportionate entropy reduction, preventing late-stage collapse while preserving stable reasoning.

Across mathematical reasoning and coding benchmarks, our method achieves performance close to supervised RLVR approaches such as GRPO, without requiring gold-standard solutions. These results highlight the potential of multi-reward RLIF as a scalable alternative to externally supervised reinforcement learning. More broadly, they suggest that future work can explore additional internal signals, potentially stronger than those studied here, to support stable long-horizon reasoning. Such stability is central to RLIF: as models scale and reasoning trajectories become more complex, training must preserve exploration beyond early high-confidence behaviors rather than collapse into narrow reward-seeking patterns.

Per-channel GDPO normalization vs. naïve combination

This appendix isolates the contribution of per-channel GDPO normalization in the multi-reward advantage of Eq.
4
–
5
. We compare two policy-update variants on Qwen2.5-1.5B with the multi-reward + KL-Cov (
\beta_{\text{cov}}{=}0.05

Variants.

Let
R^{(\text{cf})}_{g}
denote the (cluster
+
format) channel and
R^{(\text{sc})}_{g}
the self-certainty channel for rollout
g
in a
G

\hat{z}(\cdot)
denote per-group
z
-score normalization.

•

Per-channel GDPO norm (ours).
Each channel is
z
-scored
within its own group
before being combined:
A_{g}=w_{\text{cf}}\,\hat{z}(R^{(\text{cf})})_{g}+w_{\text{sc}}\,\hat{z}(R^{(\text{sc})})_{g}

•

Combine-then-normalize.
Raw weighted rewards are summed at their native scales
first

To confirm that the trajectory gap in Figure

through its per-token logprobs
\ell

\nabla_{\theta}\mathcal{L}=J^{\top}\nabla_{\ell}\mathcal{L}

\mathcal{L}

gap at parameter space (up to anisotropy of

J^{\top}
in the specific directions

\nabla_{\ell}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cf}}^{\mathrm{lin}}
and

\nabla_{\ell}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sc}}^{\mathrm{lin}}
point, which is shared
across channels and therefore symmetric in the cf/sc ratio). The logprob-space
norm ratio is consequently a faithful proxy for the relative magnitude with

without any traversal of the model and trivially safe
under FSDP.

At every training step we linearize the clipped surrogate around its actual
per-token coefficient
c^{\mathrm{eff}}_{g,t}\in\{\rho_{g,t},\bar{\rho}_{g,t}\}

which sum to
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{policy}}^{\mathrm{clip}}=w_{\mathrm{cf}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cf}}^{\mathrm{lin}}+w_{\mathrm{sc}}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sc}}^{\mathrm{lin}}
exactly the
operating point (the global KL term and the KL-Cov
|\log\rho|
penalty do not
split by channel and are excluded). We back-propagate each piece only as far as

\ell
and report the masked, completion-token L2 norms

\|\nabla_{\ell}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{cf}}^{\mathrm{lin}}\|_{2}
and

\|\nabla_{\ell}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{sc}}^{\mathrm{lin}}\|_{2}
at every step.
