Prevalence and risk factor analysis of early-onset dyslipidemia-a cross-sectional study of 3.7 million population
Authors: Fen Liu, Liting Cai, Qian Zhao, Jing Tao, Yaoguo Wang, Juan Wang, Deyang Li, Junqing Xie, Xiaomei Li, Yining Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChina has a large population, and the prevalence of dyslipidemia varies across regions. The prevalence of dyslipidemia is closely associated with the increasing burden of cardiovascular disease. Currently, there is a lack of high-quality data on early-onset dyslipidemia in Northwest China. This study aims to comprehensively assess the prevalence and risk factors of early-onset dyslipidemia among adults in this region, providing epidemiological evidence for disease prevention and health promotion.MethodsThis is a cross-sectional study based on the universal health check-up program conducted in northwestern China from January to December 2019. Adults (aged 18 to 65 years old) living in both rural and urban areas were included. Participants received a questionnaire survey, physical examination, and laboratory tests, including liver and kidney function, complete blood count, fasting blood glucose, and lipid profile.ResultThis study included a total of 3,559,141 participants (43.2% male and 56.8% female). The prevalence of early-onset dyslipidemia was 25.7%, with hypercholesterolemia accounting for 3.6%, hypertriglyceridemia for 10.6%, elevated low-density lipoprotein cholesterol for 2.7%, and reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol for 14.5%. Abnormalities in a single lipid parameter were observed in 20.5% of participants, while abnormalities in two, three, and four lipid parameters were observed in 4.6%, 0.6%, and 0.1% of participants, respectively. Male sex, age, diabetes, hypertension, smoking, alcohol consumption, and overweight/obesity were all significant risk factors for early-onset dyslipidemia.ConclusionThe age-standardized prevalence of early-onset dyslipidemia and its subtypes in Northwest China remains lower than the national level. Existing evidence consistently indicates that males, advancing age, diabetes, hypertension, smoking, alcohol consumption, and overweight/obesity are independent risk factors for early-onset dyslipidemia. Therefore, systematic screening should be initiated for this population (< 40 years old), with stratified management involving enhanced lifestyle interventions combined with evidence-based lipid-lowering medications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12944-026-03002-4.Keywords: Early-onset dyslipidemia, Prevalence, Risk factors

Introduction

The China Cardiovascular Disease Report (CCDR) indicates that plasma lipid levels are generally elevated in the Chinese population, and the prevalence of dyslipidemia remains high. This condition can lead to atherosclerotic lesions, significantly increasing the incidence and mortality rates of coronary heart disease and ischemic stroke [1]. Against the backdrop of rapid economic development, the detection rate of dyslipidemia among adults in China has continued to rise, particularly in urban areas and among adults with obesity or central obesity. Dyslipidemia is a pathological state caused by disorders in lipid metabolism, manifested by various changes in lipid parameters, including elevated levels of total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) and reduced level of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), which may occur singly or in combination [2].

According to estimates by the World Health Organization, the prevalence of elevated plasma TC levels among adults aged 25 years and older worldwide reached as high as 39% in 2008. Additionally, over one-third of deaths from ischemic heart disease and ischemic stroke attributed to elevated LDL-C levels [3]. Surveys conducted in East Asian and Southeast Asian countries indicate that the patterns of dyslipidemia vary by gender, urban-rural areas, and geographic regions, with urban residents having higher plasma LDL-C levels than rural residents [4]. In 2020, the number of deaths attributable to high LDL-C was approximately 4.51 million, an increase of 19% from 2010 [5]. Although it has been confirmed that dyslipidemia are closely associated with the occurrence of cardiovascular disease (CVD) and are one of the important risk factors [3], the pathological mechanisms are complex. The etiological classification of this disorder is primarily divided into two categories: primary and secondary. Primary (hereditary) dyslipidemia is primarily caused by genetic factors, while secondary dyslipidemia is associated with specific organ dysfunction, such as abnormalities in the kidneys, thyroid, or liver abnormalities [6]. A sedentary lifestyle and unhealthy dietary habits are also associated with the development of hyperlipidemia. The incidence of this condition is increasing among young people [7].

Studies have shown that the early onset of dyslipidemia is associated with the early development of coronary and peripheral arterial atherosclerotic diseases and an increased incidence of CVD in adulthood [8]. Dyslipidemia can asymptomatically accelerate atherosclerosis, promote lipid deposition and plaque formation, leading to vascular stenosis and sclerosis. This significantly increases the incidence and mortality rates of myocardial infarction, cerebral infarction, and peripheral vascular disease [9]. In the United States, there are differences in the prevalence of hypercholesterolemia and hypertriglyceridemia among different racial groups. The prevalence of hypercholesterolemia and hypertriglyceridemia among whites was 12.2% and 10.9%, respectively, while in Black individuals, it was 9.4% and 4.4%, respectively. The prevalence of hypercholesterolemia in Mexican Americans was 10.1%, and that of hypertriglyceridemia was 13.2% [10]. In adult Uyghur Chinese, the major residents of northwestern China, the prevalence of hypercholesterolemia among has risen from 0.8% to 3.8%, with a more significant increase among women since 2021. A retrospective cohort study on dyslipidemia prevalence among Uyghur adults in China revealed that the primary characteristic of dyslipidemia in this population is low HDL-C. The prevalence of LDL-C, hypercholesterolemia, and low HDL-C showed a declining trend, particularly pronounced among males. while the increase in hypercholesterolemia prevalence among women was greater than that among men [11]. A cross-sectional study involving 178,558 participants revealed that the crude prevalence of dyslipidemia among Chinese adults was 35.6%. Among these, the prevalence rates for high TC and high LDL-C ranged from 2.5% to 16.7% and 2.1% to 19.7%, respectively. High TG was more common in southwestern and northeastern China, with prevalence rates ranging from 20.4% to 35.9%. Additionally, the prevalence of dyslipidemia in the Yangtze River basin ranged from 27.8% to 37.2% [12].

The geographical location, living environment, and dietary habits of the northwestern region differ from those of other regions in China. The local diet consists mostly of foods high in carbohydrates and fat, with relatively less vegetables [13]. However, there is little epidemiological evidence on dyslipidemia in northwestern China. We conducted a cross-sectional study, using multistage stratified population-based sample to analyze data, aims to address three scientific questions: [1] Describe the epidemiological characteristics of early-onset dyslipidemia in northwestern China; [2] Identify environmental and behavioral risk factors for early-onset dyslipidemia; [3] Provide effective references for managing the prevention of early-onset dyslipidemia in this region. The study results will provide evidence-based guidance for developing precise lipid management strategies.

Methods

Data source

All data was sourced from the Northwestern China Health Administration (NWCHA) under the authorization of the National Health Commission (NHC) of the People’s Republic of China.

Study population

This study was designed as a cross-sectional retrospective study. Researchers utilized electronic medical record systems to collect data (questionnaires, physical examination, laboratory tests, including liver function, kidney function, blood routine, and blood glucose and lipid tests after one night of fasting) from the national health check-up program in northwestern China from January to December 2019. This study only utilized anonymous data collected as part of routine health check-ups. According to relevant ethical guidelines, the use of such data does not require informed consent from the participants.

Inclusion criteria: [1] The study population consisted of urban and rural residents aged ≥ 18 years, with women < 65 years and men < 55 years [14, 2]. With complete lipid records. Peoples volunteer to participate the health check program with questionnaires, physical examination etc [3]. In this study, patients diagnosed with atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) were found to have dyslipidemia following standard lipid-lowering therapy.

Exclusion criteria: [1] No health record number; [2] Missing or inconsistent gender and age; [3] Heart failure or renal failure; [4] Nausea tumors or sarcomas; [5] Hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism; [6] nephrotic syndrome; [7] Missing data on lipid indices; [8] Missing or inconsistent hypertension, diabetes mellitus, and body mass index. The flowchart for screening the study population is shown in Fig. 1. The final number of valid cases was 3,559,141.

Risk classification and diagnostic criteria

In this study, early-onset dyslipidemia is defined as dyslipidemia diagnosed in men aged ≤ 55 years or women aged ≤ 65 years; these age cutoffs are based on the diagnostic criteria for early-onset ASCVD [15]. The Third Report of the National Cholesterol Education Program (NCEP) Adult Treatment Panel on Detection, Evaluation, and Treatment of High Blood Lipids (NCEP-ATP III) established standardized classification levels for TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, and TG [16]. Specifically, high TC was defined as TC ≥ 6.22 mmol/L; high LDL-C as LDL-C ≥ 4.14 mmol/L; Low HDL-C as HDL-C ≤ 1.00 mmol/L, and high TG as TG ≥ 2.26 mmol/L. Individuals who have been regularly taking lipid-lowering medications for the past six months but still have abnormal lipid levels are diagnosed with dyslipidemia. Hypertension was defined as an systolic blood pressure greater than or equal to 140 mmHg, diastolic blood pressure greater than or equal to 90 mmHg, or self-reported use of antihypertensive medication [17, 18]. Diabetes mellitus was defined as a history of diabetes mellitus or a fasting blood glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, a random blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L, or an OGTT two-hour blood glucose ≥ 11.1 mmol/L with signs or symptoms of diabetes mellitus [19]. According to the National Clinical Practice Guidelines for Obesity Management (2024 Edition), the classification of BMI for Chinese adults is as follows: BMI below 18.5 kg/m² is considered underweight, 18.5 to 24 kg/m² is considered normal weight, 24 to 28 kg/m² is considered overweight, 28.0 to 32.5 kg/m² is mild obesity, 32.5 to 37.5 kg/m² is moderate obesity, 37.5 to 50 kg/m² is severe obesity, and 50 kg/m² or above is extremely severe obesity [20].

Statistical analysis

Missing data account for less than 5% of all variables and were handled using complete case analysis (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics of the 2019 cohort are stratified by sex. All statistical analyses are performed using R software (version 4.1.0) to analyze factors such as age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, smoking, alcohol consumption, occupation, education level, and marital status. Categorical variables in descriptive data are expressed as frequencies (percentages), and categorical variables are compared using the chi-square test. Multivariate logistic regression models assess the risk of early-onset dyslipidemia, and forest plots were visualized using Graph Pad Prism 10.1.2. All tests were two-sided, and p < 0.050 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

This study included a total of 3,559,141 participants, comprising 43.2% males (1,538,600 individuals) and 56.8% females (2,020,541 individuals). Participants ranged in age from 18 to 65 years. The vast majority (62.3%) were farmers. Educational attainment was predominantly at the junior high school level or above (including junior high, senior high, and technical schools), accounting for 58.3%. 81.0% were married. The prevalence rates of hypertension and diabetes were 12.6% and 3.6%, respectively. Smokers constituted 14.3%, occasional drinkers 11.1%, and frequent drinkers 1.1%. Male participants exhibited significantly higher smoking and drinking rates than females. The proportions of overweight and obese individuals are as follows: overweight (31.6%), mildly obese (14.7%), moderately obese (3.4%), and severely obese (0.8%). Distribution of the prevalence of dyslipidemia

Distributions of the prevalence of dyslipidemia stratified by various factors are presented in Fig. 2. Results indicate that among the 3,559,141 participants, those aged 40 years and older exhibited higher prevalence of dyslipidemia (28.2%), among those, high TG (13.3%), high LDL-C (3.7%), and high TC (5.5%). By gender (men vs. women), dyslipidemia (32.4% vs. 20.6%), hypertriglyceridemia (14.8% vs. 7.5%), low HDL-C (19.3% vs. 10.9%), and high LDL-C (3.0% vs. 2.5%) were higher among men than women. Prevalence were higher among individuals with diabetes, hypertension, smoking, and higher alcohol consumption compared to those without these conditions. Occupationally, civil servants had the highest prevalence rates. Educationally, participants with primary school education or below showed the highest prevalence of dyslipidemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and both low HDL-C and high LDL-C, while illiterate participants had the highest prevalence of hypercholesterolemia. Married individuals exhibited the highest prevalence rates for dyslipidemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and high LDL-C. Among overweight and obese participants, higher BMI correlated with increased prevalence rates across all dyslipidemia categories, though prevalence rates showed a decreasing trend among severely obese individuals. Analysis of factors associated with dyslipidemia

Compared to participants aged 40 years or older, among those younger than 40 years, 47.6% had dyslipidemia, 40.6% had high TG, 27.7% had high TC, 34.7% had high LDL-C, and 51.5% had low HDL-C. The prevalence rates were significantly higher in men than in women (dyslipidemia: 54.5% vs. 45.5%; hypertriglyceridemia: 59.8% vs. 40.2%). while women exhibit significantly higher rates of hypercholesterolemia (58.1% vs. 41.9%), high LDL-C (51.7% vs. 48.3%), and low HDL-C (57.4% vs. 42.6%). High-risk populations were predominantly concentrated among rural residents with the prevalence of dyslipidemia 62.8%, hypertriglyceridemia 57.4%, hypercholesterolemia 57.8%, high LDL-C 63.2% and low HDL-C 66.4%. Married individuals exhibited the highest prevalence including 83.0% dyslipidemia, 85.4% hypertriglyceridemia, 86.9% hypercholesterolemia, 86.4% low HDL-C, and 81.1% low HDL-C. Educational attainment was predominantly at the secondary level (including junior high, senior high, and vocational secondary schools), with specific proportions of dyslipidemia at 58.6%, hypertriglyceridemia at 57.9%, hypercholesterolemia at 54.7%, LDL-C at 54.6%, and low HDL-C at 60.4%. Among diabetic patients, the overall prevalence of dyslipidemia was 6.8%, comprising 10.8% hypertriglyceridemia, 11.1% hypercholesterolemia, 8.7% low HDL- C and 4.6% low HDL- C. Among hypertensive patients, 17.2% had dyslipidemia, including 22.0% hypertriglyceridemia, 25.6% hypercholesterolemia, 23.1% low HDL-C and 13.1% low HDL- C. The BMI values of patients with various dyslipidemia types were predominantly concentrated within the overweight and obese ranges. The baseline composition of participants is shown in Table 3.

Analysis of risk factors for dyslipidemia

Age, male gender, smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, occupation as a civil servant, and single status each independently increased the risk of dyslipidemia (including elevated TC, TG and LDL-C). Higher educational attainment was associated with a higher risk of dyslipidemia. Increased BMI significantly impacts dyslipidemia and various lipid markers. Individuals with normal or underweight status had lower prevalence of dyslipidemia, while overweight and obesity including mild, moderate and severe obesity, all constituted risk factors for dyslipidemia (Table 4; Fig. 4).

Discussion

Based on 3,559,141 individuals aged 18–65 years (43.2% male, 56.8% female; predominantly farmers and those with secondary education) undergoing health examinations in Northwest China, this study systematically characterized the prevalence patterns of early-onset dyslipidemia in this region for the first time: [1] The prevalence of early-onset dyslipidemia was 25.7%, comprising 10.7% high TG, 3.6% high TC, 2.7% high LDL-C, and 14.5% low HDL-C; [2] Prevalence of early-onset dyslipidemia and its subtypes were higher in males than females, with BMI positively correlated with prevalence; [3] Phenotype overlap analysis revealed single, double, triple, and quadruple dyslipidemia subtypes at 20.5%, 4.6%, 0.6%, and 0.1%, respectively; [4] Multivariate logistic regression confirmed that age, male sex, smoking, alcohol consumption, hypertension, diabetes, overweight/obesity, educational attainment, civil servant occupation, and single marital status were all independent risk factors for early-onset dyslipidemia and its subtypes.

A study reported that the prevalence of dyslipidemia among Chinese adults is 34%. The lipid standards used in this study were defined as follows: hypercholesterolemia ≥ 6.22 mmol/L, HDL-C ≥ 4.14 mmol/L, LDL-C ≤ 1.04 mmol/L, and hypertriglyceridemia ≥ 2.26 mmol/L, which are consistent with the standards used in our study [21]。Additionally, a cross-sectional survey revealed that the combined prevalence of dyslipidemia among multi-ethnic populations (Han, Uyghur, Kazakh) in Xinjiang reached as high as 52.72% [22]. This study reports for the first time that the prevalence of early-onset dyslipidemia in Northwest China is 25.7%, significantly lower than previously published national and multi-ethnic combined rates in Xinjiang. Furthermore, the prevalence of early-onset dyslipidemia and its subtypes is higher in males than in females. This gender disparity aligns with the epidemiological pattern of dyslipidemia in the general adult population of Southwest China, where males exhibit higher prevalence than females [23]. Before age 60, several factors explain why the weighted prevalence of dyslipidemia is higher in men than in women: First, behavioral exposure differences—men smoke and drink significantly more than women, directly high TG and low LDL-C levels [22]; Second, loss of hormonal protection—estrogen lowers serum lipid levels and mitigates atherosclerosis progression. Premenopausal women benefit from estrogen protection, whereas men of the same age lack this safeguard [24]; Third, differences in fat distribution—men are more prone to visceral fat accumulation. Visceral obesity drives insulin resistance, leading to high TG, increased free fatty acid release, reduced lipoprotein clearance, and high LDL-C. This accumulation of multiple cardiovascular metabolic risk factors amplifies the risk of dyslipidemia occurrence [25]。.

The prevalence rates of hypertriglyceridemia, hypercholesterolemia, low HDL-C, and high LDL-C among Chinese adults were 12.2%, 7.5%, 15.3%, and 8.0%, respectively [21]. In contrast, this study found 10.7% had high triglycerides, 3.6% had high total cholesterol, 14.5% had low HDL-C, and 2.7% had high LDL-C. The high prevalence of both elevated TG and low HDL-C is not coincidental; rather, it represents a core manifestation of metabolic syndrome. According to trends identified in a global meta-analysis of metabolic syndrome, the global prevalence of low HDL-C is approximately 40.2%, while that of elevated TG is approximately 28.9%, and the two often occur together [26]. Residents in China’s northwestern regions have a relatively high intake of beef and mutton, resulting in a diet characterized by high fat content and elevated intake of saturated fatty acids and dietary cholesterol. Maintaining this dietary pattern over the long term can increase the risk of obesity, cardiovascular disease, and dyslipidemia by promoting energy excess and disrupting lipid metabolism [27], and this association is particularly pronounced among younger populations. High TG are one of the key components of dyslipidemia that contributes to atherosclerosis; they are often accompanied by low HDL-C levels and an increased proportion of small and dense LDL particles, the latter of which have significant atherogenic potential [28]. The inverse relationship between low HDL-C levels and an increased risk of ASCVD is well established; both low and very high plasma HDL-C levels are associated with ASCVD and non-ASCVD mortality [29].

The impact of hypercholesterolemia on risk assessment varies across different cardiovascular risk calculators. Elevated lipid levels in early life contribute to the development of atherosclerotic plaques and influence disease severity [30]. Cumulative cholesterol burden refers to the cumulative integral of LDL-C levels over time, reflecting the total load of atherogenic lipoproteins to which the vascular endothelium is continuously exposed from early life; it represents the cumulative LDL-C exposure of the arterial wall over a specific time period [31]. If an individual has persistently elevated plasma low-density lipoprotein cholesterol levels beginning in childhood, the risk of cardiovascular damage increases significantly. Familial hypercholesterolemia is a classic example of a hereditary dyslipidemia in this context; it not only predisposes individuals to early-onset dyslipidemia but is also characterized by clinical manifestations such as xanthelasma and nodular xanthomas [32]. Because some patients exhibit atypical clinical signs and have no obvious subjective symptoms in the early stages, and because no targeted screening programs are in place, familial hypercholesterolemia carries a high risk of being missed among individuals with early-onset dyslipidemia. It may be mistakenly classified as simple dyslipidemia, thereby delaying early intervention. This suggests that early-onset atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease is not limited to middle-aged and older adults but can also occur in younger individuals. In individuals who have been exposed to high LDL-C levels since childhood, the prolonged duration of lipid-induced endothelial damage, oxidative stress, and foam cell formation accelerates the initiation and progression of atherosclerotic lesions, which may occur even before dyslipidemia or ASCVD manifests with obvious clinical symptoms [33]. This cumulative effect indicates that early-onset dyslipidemia is not merely a transient lipid abnormality but rather the result of long-term cholesterol overload, which significantly increases the risk of developing early-onset ASCVD.

Although the prevalence of early-onset dyslipidemia and its subtypes among 18-65-year-olds in Northwest China currently falls below the national adult average, these findings underscore the urgent need to strengthen dyslipidemia prevention and treatment efforts in this region. The prevalence of single-type early dyslipidemia was 20.5%, two types 4.6%, three types 0.6%, and four types 0.1%. This indicates a relatively low prevalence of all four lipid abnormalities in the Northwest region. However, with lifestyle changes and population aging, this prevalence may increase in the future. It is recommended to strengthen health education, promote healthy lifestyles, implement regular lipid testing, and conduct early interventions for high-risk populations to control the progression of dyslipidemia and reduce the risk of cardiovascular disease. Young adults with dyslipidemia are less likely to receive treatment, possibly due to a lack of appropriate risk stratification. It is worth noting that while the CHINA-PAR model has demonstrated good predictive ability for 10-year ASCVD risk in the Chinese adult population (C-statistic of 0.794 for men and 0.811 for women), the average age of the cohort was close to 50 years, and the validation data were primarily concentrated in the 40–79 age group [34]. Therefore, the applicability of this model in young adults (especially those under 40 years of age) requires further validation, which underscores the current shortage of risk stratification tools for the management of dyslipidemia in this population.

Gender, age, smoking, alcohol consumption, BMI, marital status, occupation, educational attainment, hypertension, and diabetes have all been identified as independent risk factors for early-onset dyslipidemia and its subtypes. Research indicated [35] that age is an independent risk factor for dyslipidemia and its subtypes. As individuals age, metabolic function gradually declines and fat-breaking capabilities diminish, thereby increasing the risk of dyslipidemia. Men are more susceptible to dyslipidemia than women, primarily attributed to lifestyle factors (such as smoking and alcohol consumption) and physiological characteristics (such as estrogen levels and visceral fat accumulation). Compared to non-smokers and non-drinkers, smokers and drinkers exhibit higher prevalence and risk of early-onset dyslipidemia and its subtypes. This finding aligns with a prevalence risk survey of dyslipidemia among adults in Northwest China [22]. Another study in Chinese adults reported a significant association between smoking and increased dyslipidemia risk, with smokers exhibiting high TC, low HDL-C, and high TG [36]. Smoking and alcohol consumption often co-occur, and their combined effect substantially high TG while reducing HDL-C [37]. Furthermore, alcohol consumption is a risk factor for dyslipidemia [38]. While moderate drinking may offer some cardiovascular protection, excessive alcohol intake elevates triglyceride levels, thereby increasing hepatic burden and disrupting lipid metabolism [37]. Current evidence regarding the combined effects of smoking and drinking on lipid profiles remains inconclusive, and the specific mechanisms warrant further investigation.

Hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia frequently coexist in clinical practice. Approximately 9.2% of hypertensive patients also have diabetes. Hypertension and diabetes are often accompanied by dyslipidemia, with hypertension closely associated with high TC and low LDL-C levels [39, 40]. Hypertension-related dyslipidemia is primarily linked to endothelial dysfunction, altered vascular reactivity, and membrane receptor abnormalities [41]. Diabetes-related dyslipidemia, however, is primarily attributed to insulin resistance [42].

A prospective cohort study conducted in southwest China [43] indicated that overweight and obesity are significant risk factors for dyslipidemia in the Chinese population, consistent with the findings of this study regarding BMI’s impact on early-onset dyslipidemia and its subtypes. Dyslipidemia impairs arterial structure and function, accelerating atherosclerosis [44]. Higher BMI correlates with increased prevalence of early dyslipidemia and its subtypes, while underweight status exerts a protective effect against dyslipidemia, potentially due to differences in fat storage and metabolism. These findings align with results from the Persian Gulf Cohort Study (PGCS) [45]. Multiple studies consistently confirm a positive correlation between BMI and central obesity with dyslipidemia, with the risk of dyslipidemia increasing as BMI rises [35, 46]. However, the observed decline in severe obesity prevalence in this study may stem from improvements achieved through pharmacological treatment in patients with early-onset dyslipidemia.

Previous studies have identified higher educational attainment as an independent risk factor for dyslipidemia [47, 48]. However, in this study, the prevalence of early-onset dyslipidemia was higher among individuals with lower educational levels, and the specific mechanisms by which educational attainment influences early-onset dyslipidemia remain unclear. One possible explanation is that participants diagnosed with early-onset dyslipidemia consciously altered their lifestyles to increase physical activity levels. Compared to workers and farmers, civil servants exhibited higher prevalence rates of early-onset dyslipidemia and its subtypes. Occupational type showed a positive correlation with the prevalence of early-onset dyslipidemia and its subtypes, consistent with findings from a prospective cohort study of Chinese government employees [49]. It is important to note that as this study focused on populations in the Northwest region, its findings may differ when applied to other areas. Beyond demographic and geographic variations, local climatic conditions, dietary habits, and lifestyles may also contribute to regional differences in the types of early-onset dyslipidemia observed across different regions.

This study has several limitations. First, as a cross-sectional study, it can only reflect the prevalence of dyslipidemia in the population at a specific point in time; it cannot establish causal relationships between variables, nor can it analyze long-term trends in these indicators. Second, due to limitations in the scope of the physical examination, screening for characteristic physical signs such as xanthelasma and xanthomas was not conducted. Additionally, this study did not collect data on participants’ use of natural lipid-lowering dietary supplements.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provides data on the prevalence of early-onset dyslipidemia and its subtypes (hypertriglyceridemia, hypercholesterolemia, high LDL-C, and low HDL-C) in Northwest China. Results indicate that the overall prevalence of early-onset dyslipidemia in this region is lower than the national average, with low HDL-cholesterol and hypertriglyceridemia being the predominant subtypes. Preventing and controlling premature dyslipidemia presents multiple challenges, as its occurrence is closely associated with various factors including gender, age, smoking, alcohol consumption, body mass index, educational attainment, marital status, hypertension, and diabetes. Therefore, strengthening public health education and implementing proactive interventions targeting these risk factors are essential to effectively address these challenges and advance the scientific prevention, control, and health management of dyslipidemia.
