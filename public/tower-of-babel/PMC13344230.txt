Severe maternal morbidity according to sickle cell disease genotype
Authors: Tetsuya Kawakita, Natsume Furukawa, Sora A. Jones, Salimah Navaz Gangji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSickle cell disease (SCD) is a genetic hemoglobinopathy that disproportionately affects individuals of African descent and is associated with elevated risks of maternal complications. However, the contribution of specific SCD genotypes to severe maternal morbidity (SMM) and postpartum readmission remains poorly characterized at the population level.ObjectiveWe sought to examine the association between SCD genotypes—hemoglobin SS (HbSS) disease, hemoglobin SC (HbSC) disease, sickle thalassemia, and sickle cell trait—and SMM, hospital readmission, and related adverse maternal outcomes.Study designThis was a retrospective cohort study using the Nationwide Readmissions Database from 2016 through 2022, representing over 20 million weighted delivery hospitalizations. SCD genotypes were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification codes. The primary outcome was SMM (non‐transfusion, no sickle crisis), identified using the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention algorithm, excluding transfusion and sickle cell crisis. Secondary outcomes included transfusion, hospital readmission within 42 days, and sickle cell crisis. Adjusted relative risks (aRRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using survey‐weighted modified Poisson regression, adjusting for demographic, clinical, and hospital‐level characteristics.ResultsAmong 20,928,182 delivery hospitalizations, 13,501 had HbSS disease, 1526 had HbSC disease, 2063 had sickle thalassemia, 149,219 had sickle cell trait, and 20,761,873 had no SCD or trait. Compared with individuals without SCD or trait, SMM risk was substantially elevated among those with HbSS disease (6.0%; aRR, 5.81; 95% CI, 5.25–6.43), HbSC disease (7.9%; aRR, 6.06; 95% CI, 4.66–7.87), sickle thalassemia (3.5%; aRR, 3.61; 95% CI, 2.57–5.06), and sickle cell trait (1.4%; aRR, 1.41, 95% CI, 1.32–1.50). A similar graded pattern was observed for hospital readmission and transfusion. When compared with HbSS disease directly, no statistically significant difference in SMM risk was detected for HbSC disease (aRR, 1.21; 95% CI, 0.92–1.59), whereas sickle thalassemia was associated with a lower risk (aRR, 0.63; 95% CI, 0.44–0.89).ConclusionSCD is associated with markedly elevated risks of SMM, transfusion, and hospital readmission, with the magnitude varying by genotype. HbSS and HbSC disease confer markedly elevated risks, with no statistically significant difference detected between the two genotypes. Sickle thalassemia is associated with comparatively lower but still clinically significant risks. These findings underscore the need for genotype‐informed, multidisciplinary peripartum management.

INTRODUCTION

Severe maternal morbidity (SMM), defined as unexpected, life‐threatening complications arising during labor and delivery, has emerged as a critical indicator of maternal health care quality in the United States [1, 2]. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) defines SMM using 21 clinical indicators, including conditions such as eclampsia, acute renal failure, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), and disseminated intravascular coagulation [3]. Despite improvements in obstetric care, the rate of SMM continues to rise [4], disproportionately affecting individuals with underlying chronic conditions [5].

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is the most common inherited hemoglobinopathy in the United States, affecting approximately 100,000 individuals and occurring in roughly one in 500 births among individuals of African descent [6, 7]. Among SCD genotypes, hemoglobin SS (HbSS) disease is typically the most severe, characterized by frequent vaso‐occlusive crises and end‐organ damage [8, 9]. Hemoglobin SC (HbSC) disease is generally considered clinically milder; however, pregnancy may precipitate sickle cell complications for the first time in individuals with HbSC disease [10]. Sickle thalassemia encompasses a heterogeneous group with variable clinical severity.

Although a prior systematic review demonstrated that SCD genotype modifies the risk of adverse perinatal outcomes such as maternal mortality and preeclampsia [11], and other studies have shown a three‐ to sixfold increased risk of SMM associated with SCD [12, 13, 14], several important gaps remain. First, no study has distinguished genotype‐specific risks for SMM as defined by the CDC algorithm. Second, prior nationally representative analyses spanned the transition from established International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐9‐CM) to ICD‐10‐CM coding systems [12, 13], introducing potential misclassification, and were limited by single‐center designs with small sample sizes [14]. Third, the contribution of SCD genotypes to postpartum hospital readmission has not been systematically examined.

We sought to examine the association between SCD genotypes and SMM, hospital readmission, and related adverse maternal outcomes using a large, nationally representative database.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This was a retrospective cohort study using data from the Healthcare Cost and Utilization Project (HCUP) Nationwide Readmissions Database (NRD) from 2016 through 2022 [15]. The NRD is an all‐payer, discharge‐level database developed by the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) to support analyses of national hospital readmissions. It includes verified patient linkage variables that allow tracking of individuals across hospitalizations within the same calendar year. The NRD contains data from nonfederal, short‐term, acute‐care hospitals across participating states and is designed to produce nationally representative estimates when weighted appropriately. This study was determined to be exempt from human subjects review by the Institutional Review Board, as only deidentified data were used. This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline [16].

Study population

The study population included individuals aged 11–55 years admitted for delivery, identified using established International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM) codes [17]. We excluded hospitals with fewer than 100 annual deliveries to improve the stability of estimates for rare maternal outcomes, delivery hospitalizations involving transfer from another facility to minimize duplicate counting, and index admissions occurring in November or December to ensure complete ascertainment of 42‐day readmissions, given that the NRD does not allow linkage across calendar years [18].

Exposure

The primary exposure was sickle cell status, classified into five mutually exclusive groups using ICD‐10‐CM diagnosis codes: (1) HbSS disease (D57.0), (2) HbSC disease (D57.2), (3) sickle thalassemia (D57.4), (4) sickle cell trait (D57.3), and (5) no sickle cell condition (reference group). Individuals without any sickle cell–related diagnosis code served as the reference.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was SMM (non‐transfusion, no sickle crisis) during the delivery hospitalization, identified using the CDC algorithm comprising life‐threatening obstetric complications, excluding transfusion and sickle cell crisis [3]. First, sickle cell crisis is intrinsic to the pathophysiology of SCD and its inclusion as an outcome indicator would create exposure‐outcome overlap [14]. Second, transfusion in individuals with SCD frequently reflects prophylactic or disease‐directed management rather than an acute complication of delivery, and its inclusion would conflate treatment intensity with morbidity. Secondary outcomes included in‐hospital transfusion, hospital readmission within 42 days of discharge, and sickle cell crisis. We additionally examined individual components of SMM (excluding sickle cell crisis and transfusion) and primary indications for readmission by sickle cell status.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the characteristics of readmitted patients. Continuous variables were compared using two‐sided t‐tests, and categorical variables using chi‐square tests. Modified Poisson regression models with robust variance were used to estimate adjusted relative risks (aRRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for each outcome. Models were adjusted for maternal age, primary expected payer (private, government, self‐pay, or other), median household income quartile by patient ZIP code, obesity, pregestational diabetes, chronic hypertension, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, multiple gestation, prior cesarean delivery, malpresentation, hospital characteristics (bed size and teaching status), and calendar year. These covariates were selected a priori based on established associations with SMM in the literature [2, 4, 19]. Survey weights provided by HCUP were applied to generate nationally representative estimates and to account for the complex survey design of the NRD. E‐values were calculated for statistically significant associations to estimate the minimum strength of association that an unmeasured confounder would need to have with both the exposure and the outcome to explain away the observed findings [20].

In addition to the primary analyses comparing each sickle cell group with the reference (no sickle cell condition), we performed head‐to‐head comparisons of HbSC disease and sickle thalassemia against HbSS disease to characterize genotype‐specific risk differences. Cumulative readmission rates within 42 days of delivery were estimated for each SCD category using survey‐weighted counts and plotted to visualize the temporal pattern of postpartum readmissions by genotype. Effects were considered significant if the 95% CI did not include the null or if the p value was less than 0.05. All analyses were performed using Stata version 18.5 (StataCorp).

Figure 2 illustrates adverse pregnancy outcomes according to sickle cell genotype. Compared with individuals without SCD or trait, progressively higher risks were observed across all outcomes among those with sickle cell trait and SCD phenotypes. SMM occurred in 0.8% of individuals without SCD or trait and was modestly increased among those with sickle cell trait (1.4%; aRR, 1.41; 95% CI, 1.32–1.50), but substantially higher among those with HbSS disease (6.0%; aRR, 5.81; 95% CI, 5.25–6.43), HbSC disease (7.9%; aRR, 6.06; 95% CI, 4.66–7.87), and sickle thalassemia (3.5%; aRR, 3.61; 95% CI, 2.57–5.06). A similar graded pattern was observed for hospital readmission, which occurred in 1.6% of individuals without SCD or trait and was increasingly common among those with sickle cell trait (2.9%; aRR, 1.43; 95% CI, 1.37–1.50), HbSS disease (7.9%; aRR, 4.05; 95% CI, 3.71–4.43), HbSC disease (9.9%; aRR, 4.32; 95% CI, 3.49–5.34), and sickle thalassemia (5.1%; aRR, 2.67; 95% CI, 2.01–3.54). Transfusion demonstrated the largest relative differences, with rates of 1.2% in the reference group compared with 2.4% among individuals with sickle cell trait (aRR, 1.66; 95% CI, 1.57–1.75) and markedly elevated risks among those with HbSS disease (14.1%; aRR, 8.91; 95% CI, 8.28–9.59), HbSC disease (13.9%; aRR, 8.03; 95% CI, 6.56–9.83), and sickle thalassemia (10.2%; aRR, 7.05; 95% CI, 5.77–8.60).

Proportions of individual components of SMM (excluding sickle cell crisis and transfusion) are presented in Table S1. Overall, acute renal failure, ARDS, and disseminated intravascular coagulation were the most common SMM components across sickle cell phenotypes. Among individuals with HbSS disease and HbSC disease, ARDS and acute renal failure were the two most frequent components, followed by sepsis. In contrast, among individuals with sickle thalassemia, ARDS remained the most common component, while pulmonary edema or acute heart failure was the second most frequent SMM indicator, exceeding the proportions observed for sepsis and disseminated intravascular coagulation in this subgroup.

Primary indications for hospital readmission by sickle cell status are presented in Table S2. Among individuals without SCD or trait and those with sickle cell trait, hypertension was the most common indication for readmission, accounting for 36.7% and 46.2% of readmissions, respectively. In contrast, among individuals with HbSS disease, HbSC disease, and sickle thalassemia, hemorrhage and anemia were the dominant causes of readmission. Sickle cell crisis was a common indication for readmission among individuals with SCD, occurring in 18.3% of those with HbSS disease, 16.6% of those with HbSC disease, and 6.7% of those with sickle thalassemia, and was absent among individuals without SCD or trait.

Figure 4 illustrates the cumulative readmission rate within 42 days of delivery by SCD type. Individuals with HbSC disease had the highest cumulative readmission rate (9.9%), followed by HbSS disease (7.9%) and sickle thalassemia (5.1%). Those with sickle cell trait had a lower cumulative readmission rate (approximately 3%), though still higher than those without SCD or trait (1.6%). Across all SCD subtypes, the steepest increase in readmissions occurred within the first 7 days after delivery, with a more gradual rise thereafter.

Comment

Principal findings

In this retrospective cohort study using the NRD from 2016 to 2022, we found that SCD was associated with markedly elevated risks of SMM, transfusion, and hospital readmission, with the magnitude of risk varying by genotype. Individuals with HbSS and HbSC disease experienced approximately sixfold elevated risks of SMM compared with individuals without SCD or trait, whereas sickle thalassemia showed a lower but still clinically significant increase. Notably, there was no significant difference in SMM between HbSC disease (7.9%) and HbSS disease (6.0%), indicating that HbSC disease confers a peripartum SMM risk comparable to HbSS disease in this cohort. When compared directly with HbSS disease, no statistically significant differences were detected for HbSC disease across most outcomes, although these comparisons were limited by smaller sample sizes. In contrast, sickle thalassemia was associated with consistently lower risks. These findings demonstrate heterogeneity in maternal risk profiles across SCD subtypes and underscore the importance of genotype‐specific considerations in peripartum risk assessment.

Results in the context of the literature

Our findings are consistent with and extend prior studies demonstrating a strong association between SCD and SMM. Ha et al., in a single‐center cohort of non‐Hispanic Black individuals (2011–2020), reported a fivefold increased risk of SMM (aRR, 5.4; 95% CI, 4.6–6.3); however, that study was limited to 132 deliveries with SCD and did not stratify by genotype [14]. Early et al., using the National Inpatient Sample (2012–2018), found comparable associations (aRR, 5.51; 95% CI, 4.71–6.45) [13]. Our study captured a more recent nationally representative sample of over 20 million delivery hospitalizations and, importantly, distinguished among HbSS, HbSC, sickle thalassemia, and sickle cell trait.

A notable finding is that no statistically significant difference in SMM risk was detected between HbSS and HbSC disease. Although HbSC disease is generally considered milder outside of pregnancy [10], our data suggest that individuals with HbSC disease face substantially elevated peripartum risks that are statistically indistinguishable from those observed in HbSS disease in this cohort. Healthcare providers should not underestimate the risk of HbSC disease as a milder disease. Our data argue against this practice and support managing pregnant individuals with HbSC disease with the same intensity of peripartum surveillance and multidisciplinary planning as those with HbSS disease. The higher risk of sickle cell crisis among individuals with HbSC disease compared with HbSS disease may reflect underrecognition and undertreatment, given the perception of milder severity. In contrast, sickle thalassemia was associated with lower risks relative to HbSS disease, though still more than threefold higher than the non‐SCD population.

Among individual SMM components, ARDS and acute renal failure were the most prevalent indicators in HbSS and HbSC disease, consistent with chronic vaso‐occlusion, hemolysis, and endothelial dysfunction [8]. The prominence of air and thrombotic embolism aligns with the hypercoagulable state in SCD [14, 21]. Readmission patterns also differed: hypertension dominated readmissions in the non‐SCD population, whereas hemorrhage, anemia, and sickle cell crisis were the leading indications among individuals with SCD.

Clinical implications

These findings carry several clinical implications. First, our data reinforce the SMFM recommendation for multidisciplinary management involving maternal‐fetal medicine specialists and hematologists [22], and suggest that such coordination should extend to all SCD genotypes, including HbSC disease. Second, the high prevalence of transfusion (14.1% in HbSS; 13.9% in HbSC) underscores the importance of proactive blood bank coordination and consideration of prophylactic transfusion strategies [23]. Third, the elevated thromboembolic SMM components (air and thrombotic embolism occurring in 19.5% of individuals with HbSC disease who experienced SMM compared with 12.0% of those with HbSS disease; Table S1) support consideration of venous thromboembolism prophylaxis, particularly for individuals with HbSC disease [21]. Current SMFM and ASH guidelines do not yet formally stratify venous thromboembolism (VTE) prophylaxis by SCD genotype; our findings may support consideration of genotype‐specific VTE risk in future guideline development. Fourth, the distinct readmission patterns suggest that postpartum discharge planning for individuals with SCD should incorporate hematology‐focused follow‐up rather than standard surveillance protocols. Because the cumulative readmission curve rose most steeply within the first 7 days after delivery across all SCD genotypes, we suggest that hematology‐focused follow‐up occur within 7 days of hospital discharge. This timing is intended to capture the acute complications (hemorrhage, anemia, sickle cell crisis, and VTE) that drive early readmission in patients with SCD.

Research implications

Future prospective studies incorporating detailed clinical data, including hydroxyurea use, baseline hemoglobin levels, transfusion history, and disease severity markers, are needed to validate genotype‐specific risk estimates. Administrative databases cannot capture these variables, limiting the ability to distinguish between the effects of SCD genotype, disease severity, and access to care. Interventional studies evaluating transition‐of‐care protocols and hematology‐obstetrics co‐management models are also warranted. Additionally, future studies should explore whether the elevated risk observed among individuals with sickle cell trait represents a true biological association or residual confounding.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has several strengths. First, we utilized a large, nationally representative database capturing over 20 million delivery hospitalizations from 2016 to 2022. Second, to our knowledge, this is the largest study to distinguish among HbSS, HbSC, sickle thalassemia, and sickle cell trait in examining maternal outcomes. Third, we examined multiple clinically relevant outcomes using a consistent analytic approach. Fourth, E‐values provide additional context regarding the potential influence of unmeasured confounding.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the NRD is based on administrative claims and is subject to misclassification of diagnoses, including SCD genotype; the accuracy of ICD‐10‐CM codes for distinguishing SCD subtypes has not been extensively validated. Second, the NRD lacks clinical detail such as laboratory values, medication use, and disease severity markers. Third, the NRD does not include race, precluding stratification by or adjustment for race, which is relevant given that SCD disproportionately affects individuals of African descent and that race is a social construct associated with structural determinants influencing maternal outcomes [5, 24]. Consequently, the observed associations may reflect both disease pathophysiology and race‐associated structural factors that cannot be disentangled in these data, and the modestly elevated risks among individuals with sickle cell trait may be attributable to residual confounding rather than a direct biological effect. Fourth, the NRD does not permit linkage across calendar years, potentially underestimating readmission burden. Fifth, transfusion and readmission may reflect institutional practice variation. Finally, causal inferences cannot be drawn given the observational design.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, SCD is associated with markedly elevated risks of SMM, transfusion, and hospital readmission, with the magnitude varying by genotype. Both HbSS and HbSC disease are associated with markedly elevated risks relative to the non‐SCD population, and no statistically significant difference between the two genotypes was detected. These findings underscore the need for genotype‐informed, multidisciplinary peripartum care. Future prospective studies are needed to inform genotype‐specific management strategies.
