Composite probiotics improve growth performance and enhance immune and antioxidant capacity in weaned yaks by modulating rumen microbiota and their metabolites
Authors: Zhilong Wang, Shuxiang Wang, Long Ye, Jiyuan Zhang, Shengchun Xu, Yuxia Ding, Jiaming Wang, Yingkui Yang, Honglu Wang, Haisheng Shi, Chunye Li, Shatuo Chai, Dongwen Dai, Jiaying Lv, Yijuan Ma, Xun Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAfter weaning, calves undergo a critical transition from liquid milk to solid feed. During this period, the digestive system is not yet fully mature and the rumen microbial community is still being established, which can easily lead to stress responses, growth retardation and other problems. Nutritional management and microecological regulation at this stage are crucial for rumen development, maturation of immune function and subsequent growth performance. Probiotics are regarded as an important nutritional and microecological strategy to alleviate weaning stress and promote the maturation of rumen and intestinal function. Therefore, in the present study, sixteen 8-month-old weaned yaks were randomly allocated to two groups (n = 8 per group): a composite probiotic group (CP) and a control group (CK). In the CP group, a composite probiotic preparation (Bacillus subtilis, Lactobacillus acidophilus and Bacillus licheniformis; viable count ≥ 1 × 1010 CFU/g) was supplemented at 0.2% of dietary dry matter from the beginning of the formal trial, whereas the CK group received only the basal diet. This study investigated the effects of dietary supplementation with this composite probiotic on the growth performance, serum biochemical indices and rumen microbial community structure and metabolic function of weaned yaks.ResultsThe results showed that dietary supplementation with 0.2% compound probiotics significantly improved growth performance in weaned yaks, reduced serum urea nitrogen, increased serum catalase (CAT) activity, and decreased the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β, thereby providing immune and oxidative-stress protection during the weaning-stress period. Rumen fermentation parameters indicated that the CP group had significantly lower NH3-N and significantly higher microbial crude protein (MCP), accompanied by significantly increased acetate, butyrate, and total volatile fatty acids (TVFA), suggesting enhanced ruminal energy yield and nitrogen assimilation. Compound probiotics also improved rumen microbial community structure by increasing the abundances of fiber degradation- and fermentation-related taxa, including Prevotellaceae_UCG_001, Ruminococcaceae_UCG_001, and Papillibacter, while decreasing Veillonellaceae_UCG_001, which may contribute to improved fermentation capacity and microecological balance. In addition, compound probiotics enhanced linoleic acid and unsaturated fatty acid–related metabolic pathways, increased the levels of multiple nutritional short peptides and beneficial lipid intermediates, and reduced lipid peroxidation and potentially pro-inflammatory metabolites. Collectively, these findings suggest that compound probiotics may remodel rumen microecology, promote fermentative energy production and efficient conversion of nitrogen into microbial protein, and coordinately regulate lipid metabolism and redox homeostasis, thereby providing a metabolic basis for greater weight gain and improved feed efficiency in weaned yaks under reduced feed intake.ConclusionCompound probiotics may promote the enrichment of fiber-degrading bacteria such as Prevotellaceae_UCG_001, thereby simultaneously enhancing short-peptide supply and VFA production, which in turn increases microbial protein synthesis and reduces losses of NH3-N and urea nitrogen. Meanwhile, lipid metabolism appears to be reshaped toward a low-inflammation and low–lipid peroxidation profile, accompanied by enhanced antioxidant capacity and attenuated inflammatory responses. This coordinated optimization of ruminal carbon–nitrogen metabolism and host anti-inflammatory and antioxidant defenses may collectively contribute to higher average daily gain and improved feed conversion efficiency in weaned yaks, representing a key pathway through which compound probiotics exert beneficial effects. In addition, the robustness and reproducibility of these findings should be further validated in larger cohorts, through independent replication trials, or using in vitro fermentation systems. Background

Yaks are one of the most important domestic animals on the Qinghai–Tibetan Plateau, mainly distributed in alpine grassland regions at altitudes of 3,000–5,000 m. They not only provide meat, milk, fiber and other products, but also serve as a major source of income for local herders [1].Weaning is a critical turning point in the growth and development of yak calves, marking the transition from complete dependence on maternal milk to reliance on solid feed. During this period, the digestive system is still immature and the rumen microecology is underdeveloped, so calves have weak disease resistance and limited digestive and absorptive capacities, which often leads to diarrhea, growth retardation and even death [2]. Probiotics, as safe and effective microecological preparations, have great potential for improving gastrointestinal health and production performance in ruminants. They exert their effects through multiple mechanisms, including competing with pathogenic bacteria for nutrients and adhesion sites in the gut to inhibit the overgrowth of harmful bacteria; secreting organic acids, hydrogen peroxide and bacteriocins to directly antagonize pathogens; and strengthening intestinal mucosal barrier function [3].Composite probiotics consist of multiple strains with complementary functions that can act synergistically to achieve functional and ecological niche complementarity [4]. Previous studies have shown that supplementation with lactic acid bacterial preparations can reduce the incidence of diarrhea and increase average daily gain in weaned yak calves [5]. Similarly, feeding calves mixed preparations composed of multiple probiotic strains has been reported to effectively reduce diarrhea during the weaning period and enhance immune function and growth performance [6]. Against this background, the present study used weaned yaks as experimental animals to investigate the effects of dietary supplementation with composite probiotics on growth performance, serum biochemical indices and rumen microbiota structure and metabolic function.

Methods

Experimental site, animals and treatments

The experiment was conducted in January 2025 at Qinghai Zhonghui Animal Husbandry Technology Co., Ltd. in Huangyuan County, Xining City, Qinghai Province, China. Sixteen healthy 8-month-old weaned yaks were selected from a pasture at an altitude of approximately 3,200 m and transferred to the experimental farm. A completely randomized design was adopted. The yaks were randomly assigned to two treatments, with 8 animals per group: a composite probiotic group (CP) and a control group (CK). In the CP group, a composite probiotic preparation was added to the basal diet at 0.2% of dietary dry matter from the beginning of the formal experimental period. The composite probiotic product was purchased from Shandong Bioengineering Co., Ltd. (Shandong Province, China) and consisted of Bacillus subtilis D165, Lactobacillus acidophilus HC1356, and Bacillus licheniformis D132, with viable counts ≥ 1 × 1010 CFU/g. The three strains were mixed at a 1:1:1 ratio. These strains are deposited at the China General Microbiological Culture Collection Center (CGMCC, Beijing, China): Bacillus subtilis D165 (CGMCC No.26389), Lactobacillus acidophilus HC1356 (CGMCC No.26888), and Bacillus licheniformis D132 (CGMCC No.26397). The CK group received only the basal diet. The ingredient composition and nutrient levels of the basal diet are shown in Table 1. The diet was provided as a total mixed ration (TMR). Yaks in both groups were group-housed in the same pen and fed once daily at 08:00, with free access to drinking water. The barn was routinely cleaned and disinfected according to standard farm practices, and identical rearing conditions were maintained for both groups. During heavy snowfall and strong winds, unified cold-protection and ventilation management measures were implemented. The formal experimental period lasted for 60 days.

Sample collection

During the experiment, the daily amounts of feed offered and refusals were recorded for each group to calculate dry matter intake (DMI). All yaks were weighed after a 12-h fast at the beginning and end of the trial to determine initial body weight (IBW), final body weight (FBW), total weight gain (TWG), average daily gain (ADG) and feed-to-gain ratio (F/G). On the last day of the experiment, blood samples were collected from all yaks via jugular venipuncturein the morning before feeding after an overnight fast. The samples were allowed to stand for 2 h and then centrifuged at 1,095 × g for 10 min at 4 °C. The serum supernatant was aliquoted into 2-mL cryovials and stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analyses of serum biochemical parameters.On the same day, five yaks were randomly selected from each group for collection of rumen fluid. Approximately 50 mL of rumen fluid was obtained using a rumen sampling tube and a sterile syringe. The first 20 mL of rumen fluid was discarded to minimize oral contamination. The remaining rumen contents were immediately filtered through four layers of sterile gauze and aliquoted into 2-mL cryovials, which were snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at − 80 °C for later determination of rumen bacterial community composition and metabolomic profiles.

The sample size in this study (n = 8 per group) was determined with reference to previous studies on nutritional regulation in yaks [7]. For the microbiome and metabolomics analyses, following conventional high-throughput sequencing experimental designs, five representative animals were randomly selected from each group for sampling; this sample size has been demonstrated to be sufficient for comparisons of community structure [8]. We performed a post hoc evaluation of statistical power for the primary growth indicator (using average daily gain, ADG, as an example) by calculating effect sizes and confidence intervals (CIs). The 95% CI for the between-group difference was estimated using Welch’s t-test [9], and the small-sample bias–corrected standardized effect size (Hedges’ g) was calculated [10]. The mean difference in ADG between the CP and CK groups was 0.105 kg/d (+ 104.92 g/d), with a 95% CI of 0.009–0.201 kg/d; the corresponding Hedges’ g was 1.11, which is considered a large effect according to Cohen [11]. These results indicate that the current sample size is sufficient to provide a robust effect-size estimate for the main differences in growth performance. Nevertheless, given the complexity of high-throughput sequencing data, future studies will increase the sample size to further validate the stability and reproducibility of the microecological findings.

Serum biochemical parameters

Serum concentrations of calcium (Ca), phosphorus (P), albumin (ALB), glucose (GLU), total protein (TP), cholesterol (CHOL), urea nitrogen (UN), triglycerides (TG), lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST) were determined using commercial kits (Jiangsu Lianbiao Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Jiangsu, China) and an automatic biochemical analyzer, according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Serum antioxidant and inflammatory indices

Serum inflammatory cytokines were determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) using a microplate reader. The measured cytokines included TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6. Serum antioxidant indices, including malondialdehyde (MDA), catalase (CAT), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), superoxide dismutase (SOD) and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC), were determined using commercial kits (Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Determination of rumen fermentation parameters

Ruminal pH was measured using a high-precision pH meter (REX PHS-3 C; Shanghai Leici Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The concentration of NH3-N was determined with a UV–visible spectrophotometer (TU-1810; Beijing Purkinje General Instrument Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) using the phenol–hypochlorite colorimetric method [12]. Microbial crude protein (MCP) was measured according to the Bradford method using the Coomassie Brilliant Blue assay [13]. Total volatile fatty acids (TVFA) were quantified following the method of Erwin using a gas chromatograph (GC-2014; Shimadzu, Japan) [14].

Rumen microbiota analysis

Microbial DNA was extracted from rumen fluid samples using the CTAB method. DNA concentration and purity were assessed by 1.0% agarose gel electrophoresis, and DNA was diluted with sterile water to 1 ng/µL. The V3–V4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene was amplified using barcoded primers 341 F (5′-CCTAYGGGRBGCASCAG-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACNNGGGTATCTAAT-3′). PCR amplification was carried out in a 25-µL reaction system containing 5 µmol/L of each primer and approximately 5 ng of template DNA. The PCR program was as follows: initial denaturation at 94 °C for 5 min; 30 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 50 °C for 30 s and extension at 72 °C for 60 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 7 min. PCR products were checked on 1.0% agarose gel, purified using a MinElute Gel Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Germany), and pooled in equimolar amounts for paired-end library construction. PCR amplification, pooling, purification, library construction and sequencing were performed by Novogene Bioinformatics Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform according to standard protocols.

Microbial co-occurrence network construction

Spearman’s correlation analysis of relative abundances at the phylum level was performed using the R package psych (version 4.5.1). Correlations with coefficients ≥ 0.5 and P ≤ 0.05 were used to construct the co-occurrence networks. Gephi (version 0.10.1; https://gephi.org) was used to visualize the co-occurrence networks of the CP and CK groups.

Rumen metabolomics

Samples were slowly thawed at 4 °C. An aliquot of 100 µL rumen fluid was mixed with 400 µL of pre-cooled methanol/acetonitrile solution (1:1, v/v), vortexed thoroughly and placed at − 20 °C for 30 min. After centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C, the supernatant was collected and dried under vacuum. For LC–MS analysis, 100 µL of acetonitrile–water solution (1:1, v/v) was added to re-dissolve the residue, followed by vortexing and centrifugation at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was then injected for analysis.Chromatographic separation was performed on an Agilent 1290 Infinity LC ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system using an ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (1.7 μm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm). The column temperature was maintained at 25 °C, the flow rate at 0.5 mL/min and the injection volume at 2 µL. The mobile phases were as follows: phase A, 100% water with 25 mM ammonium acetate and 25 mM ammonium hydroxide; phase B, 100% acetonitrile. The gradient program was: 0–0.5 min, 5% A and 95% B; 7 min, 35% A and 65% B; 8–9 min, 60% A and 40% B; 9.1–12 min, 5% A and 95% B. Samples were maintained at 4 °C in the autosampler throughout the analysis.Following UHPLC separation, mass spectrometric detection was performed with an AB TripleTOF 6600 mass spectrometer using electrospray ionization (ESI) in both positive and negative ion modes. Source parameters were set as follows: Gas 1 (nebulizer gas) 60, Gas 2 (heater gas) 60, curtain gas (CUR) 30 psi, ion source temperature 600 °C and spray voltage ± 5,500 V for positive and negative modes, respectively. The m/z scan range for MS¹ was 60–1,000 Da and 25–1,000 Da for MS².

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS (version 27.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Data are expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Normality was tested prior to analysis. Variables that were normally distributed with homogeneous variances were analyzed using independent-samples t-tests to compare the two groups. Data that did not meet these assumptions were analyzed using the Mann–Whitney U test. with 0.05 < P < 0.10 indicating a trend of change, P < 0.05 indicating a significant difference, and P < 0.01 indicating a highly significant difference.

Effects of composite probiotics on rumen bacterial communities

To evaluate the effect of composite probiotics on rumen bacterial communities, high-throughput sequencing of the 16 S rRNA gene was performed on rumen fluid samples from the CP and CK groups. A total of 1,796 ASVs were shared by all samples, with additional unique ASVs in each group, as shown in the UpSet plot (Fig. 2A). Alpha diversity indices (Chao1、Shannon and Simpson index) did not differ significantly between the CP and CK groups (P > 0.05; Fig. 2B), indicating that composite probiotics did not markedly alter the richness and diversity of rumen bacteria. However, principal coordinates analysis (PCoA) based on Bray–Curtis distances combined with Adonis tests revealed clear separation between the two groups (Fig. 2C), suggesting a significant effect of composite probiotics on rumen community structure.Rumen bacterial composition analysis showed that, at the phylum level, Bacteroidota and Firmicutes dominated the rumen bacterial communities in both groups (Fig. 2D). At the genus level, Prevotella, unidentified_F082 and Rikenellaceae_RC9_gut_group were predominant (Fig. 2E).At the phylum level, no significant differences were observed between the CP and CK groups in the relative abundances of the top 10 bacterial phyla (P > 0.05; Fig. 2F). LEfSe analysis indicated that composite probiotics significantly increased the relative abundances of Prevotellaceae_UCG_001, Ruminococcaceae_UCG_001 and Papillibacter, while decreasing Veillonellaceae_UCG_001 (P < 0.05; Fig. 2G).

Rumen microbial co-occurrence network analysis

At the ASV level, Spearman’s correlation coefficients were calculated to infer associations among microbial taxa, and co-occurrence networks were constructed, followed by an evaluation of network topological features. After annotating nodes by phylum, the CP network comprised 152 nodes and 207 edges, with positive and negative correlations accounting for 74.40% and 25.60%, respectively (Fig. 3A). The CP network showed an average degree of 2.724 and a modularity of 0.929. The CK network also contained 152 nodes but had 195 edges, of which 78.97% were positive and 21.03% were negative (Fig. 3B); its average degree and modularity were 2.566 and 0.925, respectively.In terms of node taxonomic composition, nodes in the CP network were mainly assigned to Bacteroidota (61.18%) and Firmicutes (34.21%), whereas the CK network, in addition to Bacteroidota (48.68%) and Firmicutes (38.82%), also included a proportion of Proteobacteria (5.26%) and other phyla. Overall, the CP network exhibited a slightly higher number of edges and average degree and a more clearly modular structure, suggesting more complex potential microbial interactions and a more tightly organized network.

Effects of composite probiotics on rumen metabolomics

Untargeted metabolomics was used to explore the effects of composite probiotics on rumen metabolites. A total of 2,698 metabolites were detected in all samples, including 1,646 in positive ion mode and 1,052 in negative ion mode. Partial least-squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) revealed clear separation between the CP and CK groups in both ion modes (Fig. 4A), indicating distinct metabolic profiles. Compared with the CK group, 69 metabolites were significantly upregulated and 46 were significantly downregulated in the CP group (Fig. 4B).

Correlation analysis

To explore the potential associations among rumen microbiota, fermentation parameters, immune–antioxidant indices, and differential metabolites following dietary supplementation with the compound probiotic, Spearman correlation analyses were performed. First, correlations between differential taxa and immune–antioxidant indices showed that Prevotellaceae_UCG_001 was positively correlated with CAT, whereas Ruminococcaceae_UCG_001 and Papillibacter were negatively correlated with IL-1β (Fig. 5A). Second, correlation analysis between differential taxa and rumen fermentation parameters indicated that Prevotellaceae_UCG_001 was positively correlated with acetate, microbial crude protein (MCP), total volatile fatty acids (TVFA), and the acetate-to-propionate ratio (A: P), and negatively correlated with ammonia nitrogen (NH3-N); Veillonellaceae_UCG_001 was negatively correlated with acetate and TVFA (Fig. 5B). Finally, correlations between differential taxa and differential metabolites revealed that Veillonellaceae_UCG_001 was positively correlated with deoxy phytoprostane J1, whereas Ruminococcaceae_UCG_001 was negatively correlated with deoxy phytoprostane J1. Prevotellaceae_UCG_001 was positively correlated with tyrosyl-proline, prolyl-tyrosine, serylproline, and H-Pro-Trp-OH. Papillibacter was negatively correlated with 4-hydroxyestradiol, and positively correlated with H-Pro-Trp-OH, linoleic acid, and PA(18:1(12Z)-2OH(9,10)/2:0) (Fig. 5C).

Discussion

Yaks are an endemic livestock species on the Qinghai–Tibet Plateau. During the weaning period, they experience multiple stressors, including weaning stress and hypoxia at high altitude, which can predispose them to oxidative damage and disturbances in immune homeostasis [15]. With the ban on in-feed antibiotics, functional additives such as probiotics have attracted increasing attention as microbiota-targeted interventions. Therefore, in the present study, we combined 16 S rRNA sequencing and untargeted metabolomics to investigate the effects of dietary supplementation with a compound probiotic on growth performance, rumen microbiota, and rumen metabolites in weaned yaks.The 16 S rRNA sequencing results showed no significant differences between groups in the Chao1, Shannon, or Simpson indices, indicating that compound probiotic supplementation did not markedly alter rumen microbial richness, evenness, or dominance. However, beta-diversity analysis revealed a significant separation of overall community composition between groups, suggesting that the global structure of the rumen microbiota was reshaped. Compound probiotics may, under the premise of maintaining relatively stable community species diversity, exert regulatory effects by reshaping the community structure [16, 17]. Further co-occurrence network analysis provided higher-dimensional evidence for this trend of structural reorganization. Compared with the CK group, the microbial network in the CP group exhibited a higher average degree and modularity index. A higher average degree indicates denser inter-node connectivity, which typically reflects stronger functional coordination among microbial taxa and more stable ecological relationships. Meanwhile, increased modularity suggests the emergence of well-defined functional modules characterized by strong within-module connections; such modules often correspond to microbial functional guilds that carry out specific metabolic pathways [18]. Collectively, these network topological features indicate that the microbial community formed more complex and stable ecological modules, suggesting that the compound probiotic may help maintain the stability of the post-weaning rumen microecosystem by enhancing synergistic interactions among microorganisms. In this study, the relative abundances of Prevotellaceae_UCG_001, Ruminococcaceae_UCG_001, and Papillibacter were significantly higher in the CP group than in the CK group, whereas the relative abundance of Veillonellaceae_UCG_001 was reduced. As a dominant fermentative taxon commonly detected in the rumen, Prevotellaceae_UCG_001 can generate fermentation products such as acetate and succinate via proteolysis, starch hydrolysis, and polysaccharide degradation pathways [19]. Ruminococcaceae_UCG_001 harbors a diverse repertoire of polysaccharide-degrading enzymes, enabling the conversion of plant cell-wall polysaccharides into fermentable substrates. It produces acetate and butyrate as major end products, thereby serving as an important energy source for ruminants [20]. Papillibacter is often categorized as a butyrate-associated taxon that can convert fermentation intermediates into butyrate, participating in carbon-flow allocation and redox balance [21]. The coordinated enrichment of these fiber-degrading and acid-producing taxa is consistent with the significant increases observed in acetate, butyrate, and total volatile fatty acid (TVFA) concentrations in this study. Under forage-based diets, the enrichment of fiber-degrading microbial communities in yaks can promote the degradation and fermentative conversion of structural carbohydrates, allowing more substrates to enter fermentation and be converted into volatile fatty acids [22]. VFAs are the primary end products of rumen fermentation: acetate is a key precursor for lipogenesis in ruminants, whereas butyrate is a preferred energy substrate for ruminal epithelial cells and is closely linked to epithelial development and maintenance of absorptive function [23]. Thus, elevated acetate, butyrate, and TVFA may indicate enhanced fermentative energy output and, potentially, improved substrate utilization efficiency via increased metabolizable energy supply and support of ruminal epithelial function, thereby contributing to improved feed conversion and higher average daily gain in the CP group. Notably, the relative abundance of Veillonellaceae_UCG_001 decreased. Members of the Veillonellaceae are capable of lactate assimilation and conversion, fermenting lactate into VFAs and contributing to lactate turnover. Under highly fermentable substrate conditions, they may reduce the risk of lactate accumulation and help maintain rumen homeostasis [24]. In this study, ruminal pH did not change significantly, whereas fermentation end products increased overall, suggesting that the ruminal fermentation environment remained generally stable. On this basis, we speculate that this shift more likely reflects a reduced intensity of lactate production and turnover following adjustments in substrate utilization and fermentation patterns, thereby maintaining lactate-utilizing taxa at a more appropriate abundance within the community. This hypothesis still requires further validation in subsequent studies incorporating lactate and related indicators. Therefore, dietary supplementation with the compound probiotic may improve ruminal fermentation function and feed conversion efficiency by promoting the enrichment of dominant fermentative taxa such as Prevotellaceae_UCG_001, enhancing volatile fatty acid production, and optimizing community organizational structure, ultimately contributing to the greater weight gain observed in yaks in the CP group.

We also conducted metabolomic analyses. Alterations in lipid metabolism–related pathways further suggest that the compound probiotic may modulate the metabolic status of weaned yaks. Within lipid metabolism, the linoleic acid metabolism pathway and the biosynthesis of unsaturated fatty acids were significantly enriched. Among the differential metabolites, linoleic acid and PA(18:1(12Z)-2OH(9,10)/2:0) were increased, whereas arachidonic acid, 4-hydroxyestradiol, and deoxy-phytoprostane J1 were significantly decreased. Previous studies have indicated that linoleic acid is a key substrate detectable after dietary unsaturated fatty acids enter the rumen; in the rumen, it typically undergoes microbially mediated biohydrogenation and is progressively converted into more saturated fatty acids [25]. Huws et al. further reported that uncultured lineages phylogenetically affiliated with Prevotella, Clostridiales, and Ruminococcaceae constitute core microbial groups responsible for ruminal biohydrogenation of unsaturated fatty acids [26]. Therefore, in conjunction with the shifts observed in Ruminococcaceae taxa in the present study, we speculate that the compound probiotic may modulate the transformation of unsaturated fatty acids by remodeling the rumen microbiota, thereby influencing ruminal lipid metabolism.In addition, PA(18:1(12Z)-2OH(9,10)/2:0), a key intermediate involved in membrane lipid remodeling and signal transduction, was significantly upregulated, suggesting that the rumen microbial community may be undergoing active proliferation and membrane turnover. This may enhance microbial metabolic activity and favor more efficient fermentation and energy conversion in the rumen [27]. At the host level, serum triglycerides (TG) showed a near-significant trend (P = 0.085), implying that, against the backdrop of pronounced responses in ruminal lipid metabolic pathways, compound probiotic intervention may be accompanied by adaptive adjustments in lipid absorption, transport, or deposition. However, since this evidence remains at the trend level, further validation is warranted in future studies by increasing the sample size, extending the trial duration, and integrating high-resolution lipidomics.

Meanwhile, arachidonic acid is a key precursor for pro-inflammatory mediators such as prostaglandins and leukotrienes; thus, its decreased level suggests a reduced potential for the downstream production of pro-inflammatory lipid mediators [28]. Consistently, the serum level of the pro-inflammatory cytokine IL-1β was significantly decreased in this study, whereas TNF-α and IL-6 did not differ between groups. The maturation and release of IL-1β typically require inflammasome-mediated processing of pro-IL-1β and cleavage by caspase-1. Under weaning stress, the compound probiotic may preferentially attenuate the inflammasome-related IL-1β axis, resulting in a selective reduction of IL-1β, which is particularly sensitive to stress-associated danger signals and changes in the redox milieu [29]. In addition, weaning stress can induce fluctuations in host immune responses, and the induction thresholds and temporal dynamics of different cytokines are not necessarily synchronized [30, 31]. Kim et al. [32].reported that, following weaning in calves, cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6 often exhibit distinct temporal dynamics, with fluctuations occurring in the early post-weaning period followed by a return toward baseline. Under the endpoint sampling design used here, only IL-1β showed a significant change, which may indicate that the compound probiotic exerts a more sensitive regulatory effect on inflammasome-associated inflammatory axes, while its influence on other pathways represented by TNF-α and IL-6 is weaker or occurs within a different time window. Further validation using multi-time-point monitoring is warranted to delineate the peak responses and trajectories of these cytokines. Moreover, we observed significant downregulation of both deoxy-phytoprostane J1 and 4-hydroxyestradiol. Notably, deoxy-phytoprostane J1 is considered an oxidative lipid product generated via free radical–mediated, non-enzymatic lipid peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids [33]. In contrast, 4-hydroxyestradiol is a catechol estrogen metabolite that can be further converted into reactive quinones and form adducts with DNA, thereby being associated with an increased risk of oxidative damage [34]. The concurrent decreases in these two metabolites suggest that compound probiotic intervention may reduce the activity of reactions related to free radical–driven lipid peroxidation in the rumen, thereby limiting the generation and accumulation of oxidized lipids and ultimately alleviating the oxidative stress burden in the host. Consistent with this interpretation, serum catalase (CAT) activity was significantly increased in the present study, whereas superoxide dismutase (SOD), glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px), and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC) did not differ significantly. This asynchronous antioxidant response may be attributable to strain-specific and dose-dependent effects of probiotics, and it also reflects marker-specific differences in the responsiveness of antioxidant indices. Mechanistically, these indicators represent distinct substrates and reaction steps: CAT primarily decomposes and removes hydrogen peroxide; SOD catalyzes the dismutation of superoxide anions to hydrogen peroxide; GSH-Px further reduces hydrogen peroxide and lipid hydroperoxides; and T-AOC reflects the overall antioxidant capacity of the organism [35, 36]. Therefore, an increase in a single enzyme activity does not necessarily coincide with synchronous changes in all antioxidant indices. Similar phenomena have been reported previously. For example, in dairy calves, supplementation with compound probiotics significantly increased serum CAT, whereas some composite indices (e.g., T-AOC) did not necessarily change in parallel [37]; Another study showed that supplementation with Bacillus megaterium in suckling calves did not significantly affect serum CAT, T-SOD, or T-AOC [38]. Together, these findings suggest that the antioxidant system’s response to probiotic intervention may vary with probiotic strains and experimental conditions [39]. Notably, this study did not include single-strain treatments or dose-gradient groups, and the serum parameters were obtained from endpoint sampling; thus, the underlying mechanisms warrant further investigation through dose–response experiments, single-strain controls, and multi-time-point sampling .

In addition, several short peptide metabolites (Serylproline, Asp Thr Leu, Tyrosyl-Proline, Prolyl-tyrosine, and H-Pro-Trp-OH) were significantly upregulated. After being taken up by microorganisms via specific transport systems, short peptides are mainly hydrolyzed intracellularly into amino acids, thereby efficiently directing nitrogen toward microbial assimilation pathways for microbial crude protein (MCP) synthesis. In contrast, if short peptides are hydrolyzed into free amino acids in the rumen fluid and subsequently deaminated, NH₃-N production would increase, thereby elevating the risk of nitrogen loss [40–42]. In the present study, the increase in short peptide levels was accompanied by a significant increase in MCP and a concomitant decrease in NH₃-N, suggesting that the additional peptide-bound nitrogen was preferentially routed toward microbial assimilation. This shift may be associated with the significant enrichment of Prevotellaceae-related taxa. Previous studies have shown that Prevotellaceae lineages generally harbor diverse peptidase families and exhibit strong potential for peptide degradation and utilization, which may help reduce excessive protein degradation in the rumen and improve dietary nitrogen utilization in ruminants [43]. Therefore, we speculate that the compound probiotic may optimize ruminal protein turnover by enriching such functional taxa, thereby channeling more nitrogen into microbial assimilation for MCP synthesis and reducing the likelihood of nitrogen accumulation or loss in the form of NH₃-N. Furthermore, reduced NH₃-N implies a lower nitrogen burden entering the portal circulation and being converted to urea in the liver, which is consistent with the observed decrease in serum UN, and may ultimately improve dietary nitrogen utilization efficiency in weaned yaks.

To further explore the potential associations among the rumen microbiota, metabolite profiles, and host phenotypes following dietary supplementation with the compound probiotic, Spearman correlation analyses were performed. The results showed that Prevotellaceae_UCG_001 was significantly positively correlated with acetate, total volatile fatty acids (TVFA), and microbial crude protein (MCP), but negatively correlated with ammonia nitrogen (NH₃-N). This pattern may highlight the dual central roles of Prevotellaceae-related taxa in ruminal carbon and nitrogen metabolism. As dominant rumen bacteria, Prevotellaceae possess extensive polysaccharide-degrading systems and can efficiently ferment carbohydrates to produce acetate and other VFAs, thereby providing carbon skeletons and ATP to support microbial growth [44, 45]. In parallel, the positive correlations between Prevotellaceae_UCG_001 and multiple short peptides may be consistent with the peptidase-enriched genomic features of this lineage, suggesting active participation in protein turnover and the generation of peptide-bound nitrogen [46]. The concurrent supply of fermentative energy and high-quality nitrogen substrates may facilitate microbial anabolic conversion of NH₃-N into MCP, thereby contributing to improved nitrogen utilization efficiency. In contrast, Veillonellaceae_UCG_001 was negatively correlated with acetate and TVFA. Previous studies have shown that some Veillonellaceae members utilize intermediates such as lactate or succinate and participate in propionate formation; therefore, its negative association with acetate accumulation or TVFA may reflect shifts in fermentation pathway partitioning and substrate utilization [47]. In addition, Prevotellaceae_UCG_001 was positively correlated with serum catalase (CAT), suggesting a potential link between microbial shifts and the host antioxidant phenotype. This relationship may be mediated by changes in fermentation products and the decreased abundance of oxidized lipid metabolites, but functional validation is still required [48]. Meanwhile, Ruminococcaceae_UCG_001 and Papillibacter were significantly negatively correlated with IL-1β. Members of Ruminococcaceae include fibrolytic and butyrate-producing taxa, which have been suggested to modulate inflammatory responses—potentially via mechanisms such as histone deacetylase (HDAC) inhibition—and to support barrier function [49]. Regarding lipid metabolism, Papillibacter was positively correlated with linoleic acid and PA, which may reflect active lipolytic activity and adaptive membrane lipid biosynthesis. Its negative correlation with 4-hydroxyestradiol and positive correlation with H-Pro-Trp-OH further suggest that its variation may be associated with shifts in the redox-related metabolic milieu, although this requires verification using targeted assays and functional experiments.

Therefore, we speculate that dietary supplementation with the compound probiotic may promote the enrichment of fiber-degrading taxa such as Prevotellaceae_UCG_001, thereby increasing the production of beneficial short peptides and reducing the levels of lipid peroxides. This improvement in the ruminal microecology and metabolic milieu may enhance rumen fermentation capacity and nitrogen utilization efficiency, ultimately promoting the growth and development of weaned yaks and effectively alleviating weaning stress–induced oxidative damage and inflammatory responses. It should be emphasized that the correlation analyses in this study only suggest potential interactions. Future work will improve statistical robustness by increasing the sample size and conducting independent replication, and will further validate the direct causal links between key microbial taxa and host phenotypes by integrating in vitro rumen fermentation and functional intervention experiments (e.g., single or combined supplementation of core differential genera) with targeted metabolite quantification.

Conclusions

Composite probiotics improved growth performance and feed conversion efficiency in weaned yaks, which was accompanied by enhanced immune and antioxidant capacity and reduced inflammatory and lipid peroxidation responses. These beneficial effects may be related to the enrichment of fiber-degrading bacteria, especially Prevotellaceae_UCG_001, and to changes in rumen metabolites involved in carbon–nitrogen metabolism and lipid metabolism. By promoting VFA production, short-peptide supply and microbial protein synthesis, while reducing NH3-N and urea nitrogen losses, composite probiotics may improve rumen nutrient utilization and host metabolic status. Overall, these findings suggest that composite probiotics can regulate rumen microbiota and metabolic functions to support post-weaning growth and health in yaks. Further studies with larger sample sizes and independent validation are needed to confirm these mechanisms.
