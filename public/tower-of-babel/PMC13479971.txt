Gaps and priority areas in psychosocial support for emergencies and disasters: a qualitative study in Iran
Authors: Samira Sadat Pourhosseini, Fatemeh Molaahmadi-Hassanabadi, Mahla Iranmanesh, Vahid Yazdi-Feyzabadi, Zahra Khajeh, Nouzar Nakhaee, Ali Bahramnejad, Amirhossein Alikhani, Reza Fallah, Aliakbar Haghdoost
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundInefficiencies within the structures, processes, and resources associated with mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) can lead to adverse individual and collective health outcomes among disaster survivors. Therefore, accurately identifying the barriers to implementing these interventions is essential for strengthening the disaster response system. This study aimed to explore and extract the key challenges and recommendations related to MHPSS in disasters within the national health system in Iran.MethodsThis study was conducted in December 2024 using a qualitative research design and a directed content analysis approach. Participants consisted of 20 experts in mental health, disaster management, and health policy, selected through purposive sampling. Data were collected via semi-structured, in-depth interviews, and the trustworthiness of the findings was ensured using Lincoln and Guba’s criteria.ResultsNine major challenge domains were identified. At the macro governance level, a limited recognition of the importance of MHPSS and insufficient attention to the long-term psychosocial consequences of disasters were reported. Within the domains of organization and coordination, the absence of a unified command and the presence of parallel activities across agencies were identified. Structural constraints included the lack of formally approved organizational structures, inadequate infrastructure, and the absence of a clear framework for the management and integration of volunteers. In the area of human resources, key challenges comprised a shortage of psychiatrists, misalignment between workforce capacity and assigned responsibilities, and insufficient attention to the motivation and welfare of responders. Training and research gaps were noted, including insufficient pre-disasters training and limited context-specific applied research. In addition, ineffective risk communication and limited consideration of cultural and social dimensions were identified as prominent obstacles to delivering effective MHPSS interventions.ConclusionsMHPSS in disasters is confronted with complex and intersectoral challenges, the resolution of which requires a coordinated and evidence-informed approach. Enhancing policymakers’ awareness, ensuring sustainable financial resources, developing needs based training programs, leveraging local human resources, and incorporating cultural considerations can substantially improve the effectiveness of MHPSS services during crises and provide a foundation for future policymaking and planning in the health system.

Introduction

Over recent decades, emergencies and disasters have emerged as major threats to human societies [1]. An emergency is generally understood as a sudden situation that calls for immediate response and can usually be managed within existing local capacities. A disaster, by contrast, involves far greater scale and severity surpassing a community’s routine coping mechanisms and causing serious disruption to its core functions [2]. (In this study, both terms are intentionally employed to capture the entire continuum of such events, from locally manageable incidents to large scale occurrences that overwhelm conventional systems and resources). Owing to its geography and climatic conditions, Iran experiences recurrent emergencies, making disaster risk management an undeniable national priority [3]. Beyond damage to infrastructure and economic assets, such events profoundly affect individual and social well being [4]. Among the various harms, psychological sequelae are both highly consequential and frequently under-recognized, with lasting effects on survivors’ quality of life [1].

Exposure to emergencies and disasters is associated with multiple mental health conditions, including anxiety, depression, and post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), that may persist for years after the precipitating event [5]. Survivors often exhibit higher levels of depressive and anxiety symptoms than the general population even decades later [6] and post-disaster depression can elevate suicide risk, particularly among individuals with limited social support [7]. Disaster-related mental disorders also impair social relationships and occupational functioning, potentially leading to social isolation and reduced quality of life [5].

There is a substantial disparity between high-income countries (HICs) and low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) in mental health financing and workforce capacity. Per capita mental health expenditure in HICs is approximately US$ 65 and low-income countries spend as little as US$ 0.04. Similarly, the density of specialized mental health workers is dramatically higher in HICs (67.2 per 100,000 population) than in low- and middle-income settings (1.1–2.4 per 100,000) [8]. In a recent multinational study by Stein et al. (2023), effective treatment coverage for PTSD was substantially higher in HICs than in LMICs. Specifically, effective treatment coverage was estimated at 17.8% in HICs, compared with only 4.1% in LMICs [9]. These pronounced inequalities highlight the far greater unmet need for mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) in low and middle income countries [10, 11].

The prevalence of PTSD in Iran has been estimated to be substantially higher than the global average. Findings from a national meta-analysis conducted by Hosseinnejad et al. (2022) indicated that the pooled prevalence of PTSD among Iranian disaster survivors reached 60.2% up to 2019 [12]. In another recent study, the overall prevalence of PTSD in the Iranian general population was estimated at 31.87% for the period 2019–2024 [13]. Despite this considerable burden, mental health and psychosocial support (MHPSS) interventions following emergencies and disasters have not consistently been prioritized in many developing countries and, in some contexts, have been largely neglected [14, 15]. Evidence from a systematic review conducted in low and middle income countries indicates that the most significant barriers to implementing post-disaster MHPSS programs include structural constraints, cultural challenges, and mental health related stigma [11]. Given the high prevalence of PTSD in Iran and the complex psychological and social consequences of emergencies and emergencies and disasters, rigorous scientific evaluation of implemented MHPSS programs is not only warranted but essential [16].

Existing studies in Iran have largely focused on estimating the prevalence of psychological disorders following emergencies and disasters, with limited attention given to the structural, organizational, and operational challenges associated with the implementation of MHPSS interventions [12, 16, 17]. This research gap in the literature has constrained the translation of epidemiological findings into actionable strategies for strengthening emergency mental health response systems. By qualitatively examining the key challenges surrounding MHPSS implementation and proposing contextually grounded operational recommendations, the present study seeks to address existing gaps in evidence and practice.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a directed content analysis approach, in which data analysis begins with reference to existing theoretical frameworks while allowing for the generation of new codes to capture data that fall outside predefined categories [18]. This approach was chosen to better understand the challenges and priority areas of MHPSS in disaster settings in Iran, where international guidelines and evidence exist but have not been sufficiently examined in relation to local implementation. Directed content analysis allowed the research team to use established disaster mental health frameworks as a starting point, while remaining open to new themes that emerged from experts’ real-world experiences. In this way, the method supported both the application of existing knowledge and the identification of context-specific issues relevant to the Iranian setting.

In Iran, the Iranian Red Crescent Society (IRCS) plays the primary operational role in disaster response and emergency service delivery. As the first responder in affected areas, it is responsible for providing immediate relief as well as initial MHPSS services. In addition, the Ministry of Health and Medical Education (MoHME) and the State Welfare Organization of Iran play key roles in ensuring mental health care and social support for disaster survivors. These institutions contribute to the MHPSS system through the deployment of psychological and social work teams, the provision of counseling and psychological treatment services, and the coordination of supportive interventions across affected communities.

Participants

Participants comprised twenty key informants who were involved in the design, policymaking, management, or implementation of MHPSS programs in disaster and emergency contexts. Inclusion criteria were: relevant educational background or professional experience in MHPSS, disaster management, or health policy; prior involvement in disaster management programs; and provision of informed consent. Exclusion criteria included unwillingness to continue participation or inability to engage effectively in the interview process. Participants’ demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Purposive sampling was employed, a widely used strategy in qualitative research to recruit individuals with rich and relevant experience related to the phenomenon under study [19, 20]. An initial list of potential participants was developed based on professional networks, organizational records, and referrals from key informants, and was subsequently expanded through snowball sampling. Sample size was determined based on data saturation, which was achieved after 20 interviews.

Data collection

Data were collected through in-depth, semi-structured interviews. The interview guide was developed by the research team following a comprehensive review of the literature and clarification of the study objectives. To assess and refine the interview guide, three pilot interviews were conducted with experts in disaster mental health and disaster management, leading to further enrichment of the interview questions. The core interview questions included:

Data were collected between June and December 2024. Participants were initially contacted via email or telephone and provided with information regarding the study’s purpose, objectives, and rationale. Upon obtaining consent, interviews were conducted either face-to-face at the participant’s workplace or preferred location, or remotely via telephone or secure online platforms. Interviews continued until data saturation was reached. Saturation was defined as the point at which three consecutive interviews yielded no new themes or codes. This was assessed through ongoing team review following each interview. In total, 20 interviews were conducted, each lasting between forty-five and sixty minutes. All interviews were conducted in Persian and translated into English by one of the researchers proficient in the language. Due to the geographical dispersion of participants, ten interviews were conducted by telephone, eight via Google Meet, and two in person. All interviews were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Data analysis

Using qualitative content analysis approach, analysis began with an initial set of themes derived from existing theories and prior research, which guided the development of preliminary coding categories [17]. The analytic process was conducted collaboratively by three researchers (NN, AH, SSP). Initially, predefined coding categories were developed based on the study framework. Interview transcripts were read multiple times to achieve immersion and an overall understanding of the data. Subsequently, transcripts were reviewed line-by-line to identify key statements and core concepts, which were assigned initial codes. After completing primary coding, codes were grouped into predetermined categories according to conceptual similarity. Through iterative discussion among the three researchers, similar codes were further organized into subcategories with clearly defined conceptual labels. Through this process, directed content analysis allows for both the examination of established theoretical assumptions and their refinement or expansion based on empirical findings. This analysis was mainly informed by the conceptual framework proposed by Al-Dahash and colleagues [2]. All coding and data management procedures were conducted using MAXQDA version 2020. Discrepancies in coding and interpretation were resolved through team discussion and consensus throughout the analytic process.

Trustworthiness

To enhance the trustworthiness of the findings, the criteria proposed by Yvonna Lincoln and Egon Guba were applied, including credibility, confirmability, dependability, and transferability [21]. Credibility was strengthened through member checking; whereby preliminary findings were shared with participants for validation. Prolonged engagement with the data over a six-month period and maximum variation sampling in terms of educational background and organizational affiliation across different levels further enhanced credibility. To ensure confirmability, two researchers independently coded the data and subsequently reviewed their coding with a third researcher. An external auditor who was not involved in data collection also reviewed the analytic process. The multidisciplinary composition of the research team further reduced potential bias. Dependability was supported by providing a detailed account of the data collection procedures, study objectives, methodological limitations, analytic strategies, and steps in theme development, thereby enabling external auditing. Transferability was facilitated by offering comprehensive descriptions of the study context, core concepts, participant selection process, interview guide development, coding procedures, and analytic process. This level of detail allows readers to assess the applicability of the findings to other settings.

Ethical considerations

Prior to each interview, participants were informed about the study’s purpose and provided verbal informed consent. To maintain confidentiality, each participant was assigned a unique code, and no identifying information was reported. Audio recordings and transcripts were stored in secure systems. Participants were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any stage without any negative consequences. Interviews were conducted in locations or secure online platforms agreed upon by the participants. To acknowledge participants’ contribution, a summary of the study results was provided to them after the completion of the interviews.

Macro-level governance

Participants reported that senior leaders largely prioritize immediate physical and infrastructural needs, treating MHPSS as peripheral. Participants emphasized that empowering managers and policymakers in the field of MHPSS, along with establishing performance indicators to evaluate their effectiveness, could play a pivotal role in enhancing the impact and institutionalization of these interventions.Officials do not realize that PTSD can unravel the entire fabric of a family—leading to divorce, violence, and road accidents in the future. A student with PTSD loses concentration; their life course shifts toward psychosocial harms. The consequences surface ten or twenty years later, yet no interventions are made because the depth of the problem is not understood. For this reason, training and performance evaluation indicators should be established for them, so that they gain a deeper understanding and remain accountable (P1)

Several participants emphasized that the social dimension of MHPSS is underweighted relative to the psychological dimension, despite social support being a prerequisite for effective mental-health care. These social dimensions, and the investments they require, are neglected (MP2)

Others highlighted insufficient attention to pre-disaster preparedness and the absence of systematic quality assessment of disaster-management activities. Some participants highlighted the necessity of establishing MHPSS as a preventive and foundational function within the disaster management cycle. They also identified the lack of qualitative evaluation of crisis management activities as a critical challenge, noting that this contributes to suboptimal quality and limited effectiveness of certain MHPSS interventions. In this context, participants emphasized the importance of defining clear monitoring indicators and transparent reporting mechanisms to track the performance of organizations and teams engaged in MHPSS activities.Anyone can form a MHPSS team, deliver some trainings, spend public funds—and on the day of the incident, if they fail to act, no one asks, ‘You received money and time to build a team; why were you absent, and if present, what did you actually deliver? Organization and coordination

Although the Ministry of Health is recognized as the steward for mental health, participants described the practical absence of a unified command, with dispersed capacities operating outside a single umbrella. This fragmentation weakens intersectoral and interorganizational communication and fosters duplication. Participants recommended that all MPHSS services be supervised by a centralized unit within the MoHME. They stressed that each organization should appoint a representative with adequate authority, and clear operational guidelines, joint drills, and regular evaluations be conducted to prevent duplication of efforts. Many participants noted significant differences in perspectives and approaches between organizations responsible for disaster management, resulting in poor collaboration and lack of unified procedures.There is as much chaos and lack of planning as you can imagine. Good work happens, but each organization does its own thing. Everyone brings food parcels—some households receive ten, others none. You see 15,000 homes destroyed and 150,000 tents distributed. It’s disastrous. All of this has a psychosocial dimension; when morale is harmed, physical health is harmed as well (P12)

Structural factors

The multiplicity of actors, Ministry of Health, medical universities, the State Welfare Organization, the Red Crescent, and crisis headquarters, compounds coordination challenges. This fragmentation also affected resource and aid distribution, as the absence of a shared structural framework often led to competition, duplication, and overlap of responsibilities. Developing a joint MHPSS operational plan, alongside standardized communication and reporting protocols, could clarify roles, responsibilities, and inter-organizational interactions throughout the disaster lifecycle. Participants also pointed to unprepared implementation infrastructure in some organizations, including the lack of an approved organizational chart for volunteers, unclear security and protection arrangements, and insufficient logistics and support. One interviewee explained.The absence of an approved structure is a major challenge. If a volunteer is injured while traveling to a disaster, how are they supported? Insurance does not apply. They came to help, yet their own family is left unprotected (P9)

Workforce

The most prominent workforce gap was the shortage of psychiatrists during emergencies and disasters; some responder organizations reportedly lack psychiatric coverage altogether. While psychologist availability is relatively better, many responses face a shortage of locally based psychologists. Acknowledging that addressing the shortage of mental health specialists in the short term is challenging, participants proposed a stepped-care model. In this approach, trained alternative personnel, including healthcare staff, teachers, religious leaders, volunteers, and students in relevant fields, provide primary interventions, with referrals to specialists as needed. Participants described idiosyncratic personnel management, with non-specialists sometimes appointed to MHPSS leadership or qualified staff reassigned due to personal preferences or conflicts. To support this, participants recommended the creation of a comprehensive and categorized human resources database and systematic monitoring of workforce deployment based on competency standards. Limited attention to responder motivation and well-being, stemming from low pay and benefits, inadequate welfare provisions, and lack of recognition, was another recurrent concern. They also emphasized the need to support responders both systemically and individually.These are hard, exhausting, sometimes dangerous roles. Responders witness traumatic events, live on perpetual standby, and workplace welfare is ignored; their efforts are neither acknowledged nor appreciated. It is essential for responders to learn stress management and self-care, while the organization should also implement supportive policies for them (P5)

Financing and economics

Participants emphasized the absence of a dedicated budget line for MHPSS within disaster management, despite MHPSS activities requiring specific personnel and materials. They described substandard equipment and logistics that sap morale and contribute to burnout.We were once deployed abroad on a 65-year-old aircraft. Everyone was so stressed they kept smoking. Then we saw the Indonesian team, supposedly poorer than us, far better equipped. That kills motivation and breeds burnout (P19)

We were once deployed abroad on a 65-year-old aircraft. Everyone was so stressed they kept smoking. Then we saw the Indonesian team, supposedly poorer than us, far better equipped. That kills motivation and breeds burnout (P19)

Several respondents underscored the importance of enhancing financial support for MHPSS services through dedicated and sustainable budget lines within responsible organizations, as well as diversifying funding sources via private sector, charitable, and international contributions. Economic conditions were said to drive volunteer attrition. In this regard, attention to volunteers’ motivation, including indirect financial support, moral incentives, relative job security, and organizational recognition, was highlighted as a priority for sustaining and strengthening human capital in MHPSS teams.In Iran, the economic situation discourages volunteering. Our volunteer numbers decline each year in line with inflation and inequality, the higher the Gini coefficient, the fewer volunteers we have. It’s true that they volunteer, but we also need to motivate them in various ways (P18)

Education and training

Participants reported limited preparedness training for responsible organizations and senior officials.Suppose I become a provincial governor today, where have I been trained for disasters leadership? From governors and mayors to ministers and directors-general, where is the training? (P6)

They also criticized the lack of skills-based training for health professionals. University curricula and in-service programs, they argued, do not adequately prepare psychologists, psychiatrists, physicians, nurses, and disaster-health graduates to deliver MHPSS in real-world crises. As one respondent reflected.Many theoretical lessons collapse in practice. During wartime, we had expert surgeons, yet operating with embedded weapons is different. Our training does not translate into skills; in the field we make impaired decisions and create new challenges (P8)

Many theoretical lessons collapse in practice. During wartime, we had expert surgeons, yet operating with embedded weapons is different. Our training does not translate into skills; in the field we make impaired decisions and create new challenges (P8)

Participants also recommended adopting effective, evidence-based educational methods, producing training content tailored to specific target groups, and periodically evaluating training programs using standardized assessments to improve the quality and preparedness of the MHPSS system.

Discussion

The findings of this study show that MHPSS in emergencies and disasters faces a set of interconnected, multilayered challenges that manifest at managerial, organizational and coordination, structural, workforce, economic, educational, research, information, and cultural levels. These challenges operate not only individually but also in interaction with one another, generating a cycle of weakness that seriously affects the effectiveness, sustainability, and coherence of supportive services.

At the macro management level, insufficient understanding among senior managers of the importance of MHPSS underlies many challenges. This managerial neglect leads to poor planning in the preparedness phase, lack of clear structures, reactive and scattered actions during crises, and disregard for long term psychological consequences. This problem is rooted in the long standing prioritization of physical and infrastructural relief over MHPSS in Iran [22]. A study in Bangladesh also showed that disaster management programs lacked any operational plan for mental health support [14]. This pattern indicates that in many developing countries, neglect of MHPSS is the norm rather than the exception. In contrast, Japan’s experience with the establishment of the Disaster Psychiatric Assistance Team (DPAT) (2013) demonstrates that the institutionalization of MHPSS is a gradual yet feasible process, requiring high level managerial insight and formal structures with defined responsibilities [23].

To operationalize MHPSS, a redefinition of these supports from a reactive measure to an integral part of the preparedness and prevention phase is proposed. This redefinition may include providing mandatory training to managers and responders, conducting periodic retraining based on lessons learned from previous disasters, and embedding these objectives in national high-level policy documents with an independent budget. Other studies have also emphasized strengthening pre-disaster infrastructure and ensuring long term funding [11].

The results of this study showed that even when psychological support is provided, the social support dimension is frequently neglected, with interventions primarily focused on individual level psychological care, rather than addressing the social needs of affected populations. This limited approach contrasts with international MHPSS frameworks and Inter Agency Standing Committee (IASC) guidelines, which emphasize a multi-layered and integrated MHPSS approach [24]. A study in Zimbabwe demonstrated that social support in emergencies and disasters is largely reactive, with limited preventive or comprehensive roles [25]. This comparison highlights that weak integration of social support and community empowerment is a common challenge in developing countries. In Iran, part of this gap stems from the historical and institutional focus of the health system on medical treatment, with social aspects often falling outside its core mandate, resulting in inadequate responses to the real needs of affected populations, particularly vulnerable groups. The results of the study by Addi et al. (2026) also state that there is no institutional structure for integrating social and psychological support in Iran, and that the health system, with its prevailing clinical approach, has practically created no place for social workers to play a role in disaster management [26]. Comparing the findings of this study with those of the study on the lived experiences of social workers during the earthquake in Turkey shows that, despite triple challenges individual (lack of specialized training), systemic (lack of coordination, focus on quantity over quality, absence of feedback), and structural (bureaucracy and insufficient authority) Turkish social workers were able to play active roles in advocacy, networking, and coordination [27]. Furthermore, a study in Japan demonstrated that for the integration of psychological and social support, the multidisciplinary DPAT team including psychiatrists, nurses, social workers, psychologists, pharmacists, and administrative staff is designed to provide psychiatric care and responds to mental health needs in disaster-stricken areas within 48 h of a natural disaster [28].

The neglect of social support in Iranian disasters is rooted in the clinically oriented definition of disaster management. some researchers have identified the presence of trained social workers at the incident scene and hospitals during the early hours of disasters as essential for rapid assessment, effective coordination, and practical response to the urgent and multifaceted needs of victims in modern disaster management [29]. Without structural redesign, it will remain merely a symbolic addition. Community based capacities (skill databases, religious leaders, women) are hidden assets that have spontaneously activated themselves in past disasters, but the lack of a formal structure has rendered them temporary and scattered. An effective solution is the systematic linkage of these capacities with social workers through a national action plan that both defines the institutional role of social support and prevents their co-option by the clinical approach.

Another critical finding is the absence of a coherent system for monitoring and evaluating MHPSS quality. The lack of performance indicators, transparent reporting, and systematic documentation of experiences leads to program continuation without evidence of effectiveness, limiting organizational learning. This finding has also been corroborated by other studies, both Iranian [22] and international [30]. This deficiency at the policy level further reduces investment and reinforces the marginal status of MHPSS within disaster management, creating a cycle of inefficiency.

According to the study participants, defining measurable performance indicators for managers and MHPSS teams including the availability of trained human resources, the existence of psychosocial preparedness programs, and regular reporting on intervention implementation can facilitate organizational learning, improve intervention quality, and promote sustainable investment. Moreover, Augustinavicius et al. (2018) suggest collaboration between researchers and practitioners to adapt validated tools to practical needs, alongside concurrent attention to quantitative and qualitative process evaluation [30].

During emergencies and disasters, numerous actors, including governmental and nongovernmental organizations and volunteer groups, become involved. The lack of a single commander or unified decision making body in disaster management especially regarding MHPSS can lead to conflicting decisions, resulting in duplication of efforts and inter organizational misalignment. Weak communication among organizations involved in MHPSS is a principal barrier to effective service provision. One reason is the failure to share information about survivors’ psychological needs, which can lead to duplicate or incomplete services. Such challenges cause confusion and increase stress among survivors. They can also erode survivors’ trust in responsible institutions, making psychological recovery more difficult. The study by Maghsoudi and Moshtari (2021) on the Kermanshah earthquake in Iran shows that the presence of multiple actors without a strong coordinating authority leads to challenges in needs assessment, distribution, and coordination. They identified lack of a single commander and poor inter organizational communication as major disruptors of operations, which underscores the findings of the present paper [31]. In this regard, the weakness of inter organizational coordination in Iran has also been confirmed in another study [32]. Poor inter-organizational coordination and communication is not a challenge unique to Iran; numerous international studies have also addressed it. Abbas and Miller (2025) in New Zealand found that weak interpersonal relationships and a lack of prior communication are major barriers to information sharing [33]. In Japan, Ueno et al. (2025) identified ambiguity in coordination roles and inadequate information sharing as key challenges [34]. Furthermore, the systematic review by Abudu et al. (2025) identified coordination and governance as one of seven main challenge categories globally [35].

In Iran, given the Ministry of Health’s legal mandate in public health, the findings underscore the need to redefine its role as the primary leader and coordinator of MHPSS during emergencies and disasters. This role requires structural reforms, the development of intersectoral protocols, and strengthened accountability mechanisms. This finding is supported by another study conducted in Iran [36]. All involved organizations should appoint representatives with sufficient authority, and clear roles and responsibilities for each institution should be defined through operational guidelines, standardized protocols, and joint drills. To ensure effective resource management and reduce duplication of efforts, developing a formal plan for resource storage and distribution, along with an integrated information system to record needs and allocate aid, is essential to prevent the provision of duplicate or incomplete services.

The findings indicate that workforce challenges are intertwined with structural and economic constraints. The shortage of trained specialists in disasters such as psychiatrists, clinical psychologists, and social workers becomes exacerbated. The entry of volunteers without a clear organizational framework reduces efficiency and may cause harm. The lack of job and welfare support, along with a non-specialized approach to hiring, leads to burnout, reduced motivation, and decreased quality of care. Furthermore, the non-specialized approach to hiring, in which individuals are recruited without proper assessment of their psychosocial competencies, severely undermines the quality of services. Supporting these findings, Alghamdi’s study (2022), emphasizes that emergency service providers in disasters, without sufficient psychological and financial support, suffer from depression, anxiety, and PTSD [37].

This study found that despite humanitarian, national, and international support, challenges such as limited financial resources during crises and the lack of a dedicated budget line for MHPSS in emergencies and disasters persist. Limited financial resources means that during disasters, available funds are typically directed toward immediate tangible needs food, water, shelter, and physical medical services while mental health is relegated to lower priorities. The absence of a separate budget allocation for MHPSS often results in these programs either being completely omitted or implemented with minimal, symbolic funding that fails to achieve meaningful outcomes. These findings are consistent with the review by Rowe and Nadkarni (2024), which included 37 studies from developing systematic countries. They identified limited resources for mental health as a major barrier and demonstrated that in low and middle-income countries, budget shortages directly lead to a scarcity of specialized human resources. Their systematic review further confirms that the pattern of challenges identified in the present study including workforce problems and inadequate financial structures is not confined to a specific geographical context but rather recurs across many developing countries affected by natural disasters [11]. A study conducted in Lebanon also showed that dependence on external humanitarian funding and the short-term nature of financing are among the main barriers to the sustainability of mental health services in disasters affected countries [38].

To address human resource challenges, creating a comprehensive database of specialized and substitute personnel is essential. Training local forces including healthcare workers, teachers, religious leaders, volunteers, and students and shifting from an expert centered model to a stepped, community-based approach compensates for the shortage of specialists. In this regard, Rowe and Nadkarni (2024) have introduced the task-sharing approach as one of the most effective strategies in developing countries [11]. In this model, trained local responders serve as the first line, providing initial interventions and identifying at-risk individuals. A smart referral system and remote support including telephone counseling and tele-mental health services facilitate timely access to specialist guidance, while complex and referred cases are managed by psychologists and psychiatrists. Maximizing the use of technology, establishing national mental health emergency hotlines, and implementing online counseling platforms are key operational strategies within this model. Furthermore, the systematic deployment of personnel based on competence, along with supportive and incentive policies to reduce burnout and the allocation of a dedicated budget, ensures the sustainability of services. This issue has also been confirmed by other researchers [39].

Among other domains that influence the effectiveness of MHPSS in emergencies and disasters are the education and research systems. The results of this study show a deep gap between education, research, and practice. This gap stems from structural deficiencies, including the lack of systematic attention to documenting practical experiences, the misalignment of training with real emergencies and disasters conditions and the absence of links to the findings of applied research, the production of descriptive and low-impact studies, and a disconnect between research findings and the policymaking process. A study conducted in Turkey found that the lack of training in the necessary skills for MHPSS in disaster settings was identified as a barrier to effective interventions by social workers [27]. As Kamali et al. (2020), while acknowledging the gap between education, research, and practice, argue that these shortcomings disrupt the organizational learning cycle and hinder the transformation of experience into actionable knowledge [40].

According to the study participants, documenting field experiences and transforming them into practical knowledge through a database and a knowledge translation platform facilitates access to evidence for practitioners and policymakers and enhances training and learning. This finding is in line with other studies [11]. Establishing a competency‑based educational system requires linking performance evaluation indicators to final outcomes.

Poor media management during disasters and the lack of clear guidelines lead to the spread of rumors and misinformation. This problem is exacerbated by the official information vacuum and journalists’ unfamiliarity with psychological literacy, which increases social anxiety, disrupts MHPSS, and undermines public trust. As long as the principles of disaster communication are not taught and clear guidelines are not established, these challenges will persist. Consistent with these findings, another study confirms that poor information and media management in Iranian disasters stems from structural deficiencies such as inadequate training, weak organizational communication, and a lack of clear guidelines [22].

To reduce the psychological impact of disasters, the development of a national media management guideline (including scientific messages, ethical standards, and news dissemination protocols) is recommended. In this regard, designing a national risk communication program based on MHPSS principles can help deliver targeted information, protect the psychological well-being of the audience, and rebuild public trust through training media personnel and social media actors, establishing official communication channels, and continuously monitoring media performance. At the international level, Rozanov and Rutz (2021) have emphasized the necessity of a policy framework for disaster information management. While advocating for information transparency, they concurrently stress the imperative of balancing such transparency with freedom of expression and averting censorship. This observation underscores the significance of formulating national guidelines that simultaneously respond to the MHPSS needs of the population and uphold democratic values without transgressing them [41].

The findings of this study indicate that cultural factors act as barriers to the effective implementation of MHPSS programs in disasters. Lack of familiarity of relief workers with local customs can have serious consequences, even posing a physical threat (e.g., physical altercations with local people). Furthermore, in the early days of a disasters, affected communities prioritize tangible needs such as food and shelter over mental health. A key reason for this low priority is the fear of social stigma; in many cultures, mental health problems are associated with shame and social rejection, and seeking help is perceived as admitting weakness and jeopardizing one’s social standing. The study by Ocampo et al. (2021) demonstrates that cultural bias can reduce the effectiveness of MHPSS programs and cause harm to affected communities. They provide examples from disasters affected regions, including the use of trauma screening tools without local validation, and the tendency of mental health providers in some host countries to perceive refugees culture as a barrier to detecting mental disorders. These examples reflect the same limited familiarity with local customs identified in the present study. That study further warn that “one-size-fits-all” approaches based on the western-centric assumption that disorders such as PTSD are uniform across all cultures not only limit effectiveness but also lead to the cultural alienation of survivors [42]. Furthermore, The study by Slewa-Younan et al. (2022) found that social stigma is the most significant barrier to help-seeking among Congolese, Arabic-speaking, and Chinese communities, and that these communities have a greater preference for informal support (family, friends, social networks). Accordingly, strategies such as collaborating with cultural gatekeepers like priests, storytelling by individuals with lived experience, and a long-term approach to reducing stigma have been recommended [43]. To increase the acceptance of MHPSS in disasters, it is essential to identify local cultural beliefs and adapt Western approaches to each context [11]. Raising public awareness and correcting misconceptions, including fatalism and the neglect of psychological needs, strengthens community participation. Furthermore, training responders in local culture and involving religious leaders and trusted community figures reduce cultural tensions and ensure effective engagement.

The findings of this study indicate that strengthening MHPSS in emergencies and disasters in Iran requires systematic reforms at the levels of policy, organizational structure, funding, training, and cultural awareness. While some challenges are specific to the Iranian context, many, particularly regarding leadership, inter organizational coordination, shortage of specialized personnel, and the training practice gap, are comparable to experiences in other LMICs.

Therefore, the results of this study may be transferable and informative for countries with similar systems, provided they are reinterpreted within each country’s cultural and institutional framework. Although the findings are based on the Iranian context, identified challenges, such as limited integration of social support, human resource gaps, and the importance of cultural sensitivity, may be relevant to other countries with comparable disaster management structures. Nevertheless, localization and adaptation of interventions to local policies, culture, and resources are essential for their effectiveness.

Study limitations and strength

This study was subject to the inherent limitations of qualitative research, including potential recall bias in participants’ accounts of past experiences and limited transferability of findings to other contexts due to the selection of specific experts. Additionally, purposive sampling may have reduced the diversity of perspectives, and the researcher’s positionality may have influenced data collection and interpretation. Furthermore, the lack of micro-level analysis of program implementation in the field limited the operational insight of the findings. Nevertheless, this study represents the first multi-stakeholder qualitative investigation of MHPSS in emergencies and disasters in Iran, sampling diverse sectors including disaster management, policymaking, mental health, and relief organizations. The use of directed content analysis enabled the precise identification of challenges and operational priorities, and the findings can inform the improvement of MHPSS policies and planning at the national level.

Future research could examine mixed-methods (quantitative and qualitative) approaches in the field of MHPSS in emergencies and disasters in Iran. Additionally, conducting longitudinal studies to understand the process of changes and the sustainability of interventions is recommended. Furthermore, research in more diverse groups in terms of geography, organization, and culture, as well as deeper analysis of program implementation at the field level through observation and interviews with frontline implementers, is advised. Furthermore, it is essential to disaggregate data based on different population groups in future research to identify which groups are most left behind in receiving MHPSS services and to develop targeted strategies to improve their access.

Conclusion

The study revealed multi-layered challenges in the provision of effective MHPSS services in Iranian disaster settings, primarily stemming from weaknesses in integrated governance, cross sectoral coordination, sustainable human resources, and cultural adaptation. Stakeholder-proposed solutions, centered on systematization, formalization, and long-term capacity building, indicate that addressing these challenges requires a shift from short-term, reactive measures toward a comprehensive, nationally integrated program embedded within the country’s health and disaster management structures. The success of such transformation necessitates political commitment and full engagement of key institutions to translate these solutions into actionable and monitorable interventions. Furthermore, in addition to macro level interventions, actively engaging community-based groups such as local networks, indigenous groups, and religious leaders are essential. In fact, without harnessing intra community capacities, addressing the longstanding neglect of the social support dimension will not be feasible.

The results of this research can be used at various levels of policymaking, planning, and implementation and can help policymakers, operational managers, service providers, and researchers to revisit national disaster-management policies with an emphasis on the MHPSS dimension.
