Food-derived peptide RDP3 mitigates pyroptosis to enhance oral mucosal repair via the IL-2Rβ/PI3K axis
Authors: Junyuan Wang, Yuliu Yang, Zeqiong Ru, Yingyu Zhang, Xueying Xiong, Yujing Ding, Xin Yu, Mengfan Ma, Wenrou Su, Yihang Huang, Xinwang Yang, Naixin Liu, Ying Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOral ulcers represent a prevalent mucosal disease with incompletely elucidated pathogenesis and a clinical deficiency in multifunctional therapeutics. This study investigates a food-derived peptide, RDP3, with tissue-penetrating capability, which significantly promotes repair of oral ulcer mucosal in vivo and in vitro in a low concentration (1 nM). This is the first time to report a food-origin peptide capable of accelerating oral ulcer mucosal repair. RDP3 can not only accelerate wound healing but also restore microbiome homeostasis. Mechanistically, RDP3 functions as a novel peptide-antagonist of the interleukin-2 receptor β subunit (IL-2Rβ; binding affinity KD = 0.99 μM). This interaction suppresses pathological PI3K signaling, thereby inhibiting NLRP3/GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis, reducing inflammation, and promoting mucosal regeneration. These findings position RDP3 as a promising multifunctional therapeutic candidate for oral ulcer treatment. Subject terms: Cell biology, Diseases, Drug discovery

Introduction

Oral ulcers are a prevalent global mucosal disorder, affecting 20%-30% of the population1. Recurrent mucosal disruption, intense pain, and persistent inflammation characterize these lesions, severely impairing patients’ quality of life2. Although most ulcers are self-limiting, the clinical management of acute-stage pain and chronic non-resolving conditions remains less than ideal3. Currently, mainstream therapeutic agents (e.g., Bingpeng San, Kangfuxin solution) mainly relieve symptoms through local analgesia and facilitating wound healing. However, they are typically constrained by poor targeting, a singular therapeutic effect, and potential risks of mucosal irritation4. Consequently, an unmet clinical need persists for agents capable of concurrently modulating inflammation, pain and tissue regeneration.

The pathogenesis of oral ulcers involves a complex interplay of immune, genetic, traumatic, and microbial factors, though core mechanisms remain elusive5. The healing process mirrors skin wound repair, progressing through hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling6,7. Moderate inflammation protects wounds, but its dysregulation, particularly through inflammasome-mediated pyroptosis, impedes healing8,9. Pyroptosis propagates tissue damage via gasdermin pore formation and pro-inflammatory cytokine release (e.g., IL-1β, IL-18), potentially impeding re-epithelialization and fostering chronicity10. Whether pyroptosis is a key driver of the prolonged healing and recurrent nature of oral ulcers requires urgent investigation to inform targeted therapies.

Interleukin-2 receptor β (IL-2Rβ) is a critical immunomodulatory hub. IL-2 binding induces heterodimer formation, activating both the PI3K/AKT and NF-κB pathways to create a pro-inflammatory cytokine feedback loop11. While clinical studies have associated IL-2Rβ polymorphisms with inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)12, its role in oral ulcers remains poorly defined. Given the shared immune microenvironment of oral and gastrointestinal mucosa13, IL-2Rβ dysregulation may similarly underpin oral ulcer pathogenesis, calling for a mechanistic investigation. Therapeutic strategies targeting IL-2Rβ highlight its dual function: Activating agents (e.g., IL-2/anti-IL-2 antibody complexes and engineered IL-2 variants) enhance CD8⁺ T and NK cell cytotoxicity, while inhibitory agents (e.g., miniprotein conjugates, IL-2Rβ-blocking antibodies, and inhibitor like rapamycin) suppress IL-2/IL-15 signaling to ameliorate inflammation14,15. Peptide antagonists targeting the IL-2Rβ extracellular domain (e.g., 107M-E118 and 178Y-Q199) have been developed to competitively inhibit IL-2 binding and suppress T-cell proliferation16. However, their clinical translation is hindered by poor protease stability, weak in vivo efficacy, inadequate tissue penetration, and insufficient safety evaluation17–19. Thus, developing safe and potent IL-2Rβ antagonists is urgently needed.

Recently, food-derived bioactive peptides have emerged as promising next-generation candidates for immunotherapeutic applications due to their high safety and diverse bioactivities. Peptide-based approaches offer a compelling paradigm for overcoming these limitations. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) like LL-37 derivatives combat pathogens (e.g., P. gingivalis) while reducing inflammation20. Defensins (hBD-2/3) strengthen mucosal barriers but face enzymatic degradation and poor bioavailability21. Marine collagen peptides (MCPs) dampen inflammation but lack antimicrobial effects, requiring combination therapies22. The clinical translation of peptide drugs remains challenging due to their frequent need for combination therapy, potential safety risks, and limited functionality23. Future efforts should prioritize developing self-sufficient, multifunctional peptides with high potency and improved safety profiles to accelerate their adoption into clinical use.

Our previous work identified RDP3, a rice-derived peptide that potently inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome-driven inflammation24. In the oral ulcer model of rats, RDP3 demonstrated triple therapeutic efficacy: accelerating ulcer healing, suppressing inflammatory responses, and exerting antibacterial effects. In this study, RDP3 inhibits NLRP3 inflammasome activation by targeting the IL-2Rβ/PI3K axis, providing mechanistic elucidation into oral ulcer pathogenesis. This identifies IL-2Rβ as a key therapeutic target and positions RDP3 as a, multifunctional peptide antagonist with significant clinical potential.

Results

RDP3 promotes oral ulcer healing in vitro and in vivo

To investigate the cellular functions of RDP3 in oral ulcer healing, we quantitatively assessed its effects on the proliferative and migratory capacities of OECs using MTS and Transwell assays, respectively. As shown in Fig. 1A, RDP3 promoted OEC migration and increased the proliferation rate of OECs (Fig. 1B). Collectively, these results demonstrate that RDP3 facilitates cellular proliferation and migration, suggesting its therapeutic potential for accelerating oral ulcer healing.

A RDP3 enhances the migration of OECs (10× magnification, n = 12); B RDP3 promotes OEC proliferation (n = 12); C RDP3 accelerates oral ulcer musosal healing in rats (n = 9, showing significant efficacy from day 5 post-injury (RDP3 vs. PBS); D RDP3 demonstrates significant antibacterial effects in vivo (n = 9);E. RDP3 promotes tissue regeneration at ulcer margin (scale bar: 100 μm). **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ****P < 0.0001, ##P > 0.05.

An oral ulcer model was established in rats via chemical induction to evaluate wound-healing potential of RDP3. Ulcer areas were measured daily to systematically therapeutic assessment. The results (Fig. 1C) demonstrated a 93.28% ± 7.71% healing rate in RDP3-treated group on day 7, significantly exceeding (P < 0.0001) the 57.26% ± 10.89% observed in PBS control, indicating enhanced ulcer healing with RDP3 intervention. Notably, on day 7 of modeling, the positive group (Kangfuxin, 10 mg/mL) achieved a healing rate of 66.2% ± 7.6%, while the RDP3 group demonstrated a better superior efficacy (P < 0.0001), highlighting its prominent therapeutic advantages over existing treatments.

Oral microenvironment homeostasis critically determines mucosal regeneration. RDP3 significantly suppressed the OD600-measured bacterial density during ulcer healing (RDP3 vs. PBS, P < 0.0001; Fig. 1D). Histological evaluation using H&E staining confirmed healing progression. On day 7 post-injury, PBS-treated animals exhibited disrupted epithelial integrity, epithelial hyperplasia, and severe inflammatory infiltration compared to normal tissue (Fig. 1E). Conversely, both RDP3 and Kangfuxin-treated ulcers displayed complete re-epithelialization with normalized epithelial thickness and minimal inflammatory infiltration. And no statistically significant difference (P > 0.05) was observed between RDP3 and Kangfuxin in mucosal epithelial thickness. These findings suggest RDP3 accelerates ulcer reduction while promoting tissue regeneration at ulcer margins.

RDP3 exerts anti-inflammatory activity in vivo and in vitro

Oral ulcers involve complex inflammatory responses, where effective inflammation suppression accelerates healing25,26. In the OEC, RDP3 treatment significantly reduced the concentrations of pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-1β, IL-6, TNF-α, and IL-18) compared to PBS controls (Fig. 2A), confirming its anti-inflammatory potential.

In vivo, cytokine expression (IL-1β, TNF-α and IL-18) was assessed in ulcer tissues post-RDP3 intervention. On day 7 post-injury, RDP3-treated tissues shown significantly lower cytokine levels than PBS group (Fig. 2B). qRT-PCR analysis further revealed markedly reduced mRNA expression of IL-1β, TNF-α, IL-2, IL-6, and IL-18 in RDP3-treated ulcer tissues (Fig. 2C), corroborating the protein-level findings. These results indicate that RDP3 establish RDP3’s anti-inflammatory function during ulcer healing.

RDP3 alleviates pyroptosis via the IL-2Rβ/PI3K axis

Transcriptome sequencing elucidate RDP3-mediated molecular mechanisms in oral ulcer mucosal healing. A comparison between the PBS group and the RDP3 group revealed 286 upregulated genes and 342 downregulated genes. KEGG pathway analysis demonstrated significant enrichment in PI3K signaling and IL-2Rβ (both inflammation-associated pathways), suggesting RDP3 targets IL-2Rβ to suppress downstream PI3K signaling (Fig. 3A, B).

A Volcano plot of differentially expressed genes (n = 6), showing 286 upregulated genes (red) and 342 downregulated genes (blue); B KEGG pathway analysis with the PI3K signaling pathway highlighted in red box; C RDP3 suppresses PI3K signaling pathway components (n = 3); D RDP3 reduces NLRP3/GSDMD-related proteins (n = 3). *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001, ##P > 0.05.

Validation in rat ulcer models confirmed RDP3 significantly inhibited PI3K pathway activation (Fig. 3C). Given established crosstalk between PI3K and NLRP3 inflammasomes-key regulators of pyroptotic cell death-protein expression of pyroptosis mediators (NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1, cleaved caspase-1, GSDMD, and GSDMD-N) was assessed27. RDP3 markedly reduced expression of NLRP3/GSDMD-related proteins (Fig. 3D), demonstrating suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome activation and pyroptosis-mediated tissue damage.

In OECs, RDP3 treatment suppressed LPS-induced PI3K pathway activation (Fig. 3C) and inhibited NLRP3 inflammasome assembly (NLRP3, ASC), caspase-1 activation (caspase-1, cleaved caspase-1), and GSDMD cleavage (GSDMD, GSDMD-N)(Fig. 3D). These results demonstrated that RDP3 exerts therapeutic effects by alleviating pyroptosis through PI3K pathway inhibition during inflammatory injury.

RDP3 exhibits permeability in vivo and in vitro

Preliminary evaluation of RDP3’s permeability was performed using FITC-labeled RDP3. As shown in Fig. 4A, FITC-RDP3 adhered to the oral mucosal epithelium within 0.5 h. By 4 h, FITC-RDP3 penetration to the basal layer was observed, demonstrating transmucosal permeability. In OECs, the FITC-RDP3 signal predominantly localized to the cell membrane region with tight perinuclear accumulation, indicating the cell membrane-penetrating capability of RDP3 (Fig. 4B).

RDP3 exhibits high affinity of binding to IL-2Rβ

Molecular docking simulations demonstrated a high-affinity binding of RDP3 to the IL-2Rβ N-terminal domain (ΔG = -38 kcal/mol), with pronounced structural complementarity (Fig. 4C). Structural analysis revealed specific polar interactions, denoted by yellow dashed lines: (i) a hydrogen bond between IL-2Rβ Cys31 and RDP3 Pro8, and (ii) dual hydrogen bonds connecting RDP3 Lys9 with both IL-2Rβ Asn29 and Thr124, collectively stabilizing the molecular interface. Complementarily, microscale thermophoresis (MST) analysis demonstrated concentration-dependent migration shifts of IL-2Rβ with increasing RDP3 concentrations. The resulting sigmoidal binding curve yielded a dissociation constant of 0.99 μM (Fig. 4D), confirming high-affinity binding.

In rat oral ulcer models, RDP3 treatment significantly suppressed IL-2Rβ mRNA expression compared to the PBS-treated control group (Fig. 4E). Immunofluorescence co-localization showed that the fluorescence intensity changes of FITC-labeled RDP3 and IL-2Rβ exhibited highly similar trends (Fig. 4F), with intensity correlation analysis confirming robust interaction (Pearson’s r = 0.96, Fig. 4G).

Inhibition of IL-2Rβ promotes oral ulcer healing in vivo and in vitro

To investigate the role of IL-2Rβ in oral mucosal repair, rapamycin (the specific inhibitor of, IL-2Rβ),was used in this study for functional validation. Migration model of OECs showed that the rapamycin-treated significantly enhanced cell migration compared to the PBS control group, suggesting the role of IL-2Rβ in oral mucosal repairation (Fig. 5B). However, the RDP3-treated group exhibited a better pro-migratory effect than that of the rapamycin group, the rat oral ulcer model was then established to further validate this observation (Fig. 5A). Observation of the ulcer healing process across groups revealed that the healing trends in the PBS, RDP3, and rapamycin groups were consistent with results of in vitro. Among them, the RDP3-treated group demonstrated the most pronounced ulcer healing. These results indicated that inhibition of IL-2Rβ do promote oral ulcer healing, suggesting IL-2Rβ as a regulatory target in oral mucosal repairation. Nevertheless, the rapamycin group also exhibited a pro-healing effect with a slightly weaker than that of the RDP3 group, indicating other targets for RDP3 in wound-healing process.

Pyroptosis injury in oral ulcer was also examined to assess the effects of rapamycin-treated. As shown in Fig. 5C, the expression of IL-2Rβ was significantly reduced in the rapamycin group, confirming the inhibitory effect of rapamycin on IL-2Rβ. The expression of GSDMD and GSDMD-N were inhibited in the RDP3 and rapamycin groups, suggesting that inhibition of IL-2Rβ can modulate pyroptotic damage (Fig. 5D–E).

Construct the mutant peptide RDP3-M based on virtual mutation

To investigate whether IL-2Rβ is the key functional receptor for RDP3. To establish IL-2Rβ as the principal molecular target of RDP3, we employed structure-guided virtual saturation mutagenesis targeting three residues (Ala1, Ala4, Ala6) with the lowest calculated binding energy contributions (Tab. S2). Computational predictions suggested that substituting Ala1 with Tyr, Ala4 with Phe, and Ala6 with Asp would enhance binding affinity through favorable energy gains (Tab. S3), leading to the successful construction of a binding-saturated variant RDP3-M (LAAFMDGPK-NH₂). Molecular docking results revealed that RDP3-M exhibited a binding free energy of −42 kcal/mol with IL-2Rβ and formed significantly more hydrogen bonds compared to RDP3, as indicated by the yellow dashed lines in Fig. S1A. However, affinity measurements (Fig. S1B) revealed that RDP3-M exhibited significantly reduced binding capacity (measured affinity = 241 μM), contrary to the molecular docking predictions, resulting in a binding-impaired variant RDP3-M.

Further structural analysis using circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy revealed that both RDP3 and RDP3-M predominantly adopt beta-sheet and random coil conformations, with notable differences in their secondary structure composition (Fig. S1C). Specifically, RDP3-M exhibited an increased proportion of beta-sheet and a decreased random coil content compared to RDP3 (Fig. S1D). These findings suggest that the introduced mutations may have perturbed the peptide’s structural stability, potentially contributing to its impaired binding affinity.

RDP3-M- impairs oral ulcer mucosal healing capacity

Migration assays revealed RDP3-M failed to reduce cell migration compared to RDP3 (Fig. S2A). In rat ulcer models on day 7 post-induction, healing rates were 63.11% ± 17.15% (RDP3) versus 48.70% ± 11.52% (RDP3-M) and 29.51% ± 14.43% (controls). The therapeutic efficacy of RDP3-M was markedly diminished relative to RDP3 (Fig. S2B).

Structural modifications attenuate RDP3-mediated pyroptosis alleviation

The impact of RDP3-M on pyroptosis inhibition was investigated at cellular and animal levels. RDP3-M showed significantly reduced suppression of the PI3K pathway both in vivo and in vitro (Fig. S3A-B). Under inflammatory conditions, RDP3-M failed to inhibit GSDMD expression in OECs (Fig. S3C, E). Similarly, in vivo, RDP3-M exhibited diminished inhibition of GSDMD compared to RDP3 (Fig. S3D, F), indicating that RDP3 alleviates pyroptosis primarily through IL-2Rβ interaction. These results suggest that Ala1, Ala4, and Ala6 serve as critical residues mediating RDP3’s interaction with the IL-2Rβ receptor.

Discussion

Oral ulcer, the most prevalent oral mucosal disorder, severely impacts patients’ quality of life28. Current therapies often exhibit limited efficacy due to incomplete symptom resolution, necessitating the development of novel treatments29. This study investigates RDP3 (AAAAMAGPK-NH₂, 786 Da), a bioactive peptide derived from unique Yunnan rice, which demonstrates high safety and efficacy in promoting oral ulcer mucosal repair. RDP3 demonstrates multifunctionality, integrating anti-inflammatory action, tissue regeneration promotion, and antibacterial effects. Mechanistically, RDP3 targets IL-2Rβ-mediated PI3K signaling, inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome and pyroptosis. This dual-action mechanism attenuates inflammation while reducing cellular damage and enhancing mucosal repair.

The existing treatments for oral ulcers are still limited. For example, phthalide dimers from Angelica roots exhibit narrow functionality30, Kangfuxin liquid requires high concentrations (10 mg/mL) for efficacy, and traditional mouthwashes like chlorhexidine carry risks such as Candida infections31,32. In contrast, RDP3 combines safety with multifunctionality, integrating anti-inflammatory, analgesic, antibacterial, and pro-healing properties to target core ulcer symptoms. Unlike high-molecular-weight natural compounds (e.g., Lycium barbarum polysaccharides, 3-15 kDa), which suffer from poor permeability33, RDP3 demonstrates potent bioactivity at low concentrations (10⁷-fold lower than Kangfuxin liquid) while retaining molecular probe utility.

Inflammation regulation typically plays a dominant role in tissue regeneration34. Previous studies have shown that RDP3 can alleviate inflammatory kidney injury by inhibiting the NLRP3 inflammasome24. As a key molecular sensor in the innate immune system, NLRP3 is activated upon recognizing pathogen- or damage-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs/DAMPs), This activation triggers caspase-1, which promotes the maturation of IL-1β and IL-18 and induces pyroptosis35,36. This form of programmed cell death is characterized by the formation of membrane pores, cellular swelling and rupture, and the release of large amounts of inflammatory factors, directly leading to the disruption of mucosal barrier integrity and amplification of the inflammatory cascade37. In the oral microenvironment, factors such as microbial infection, physical injury, or autoimmune dysregulation can activate NLRP3, inducing pyroptotic death in gingival epithelial cells. This process significantly exacerbates the pathological progression of oral diseases such as periodontitis by impairing mucosal barrier function and intensifying local inflammatory responses38. Experimental mucosal inflammation can be effectively alleviated by the targeted inhibition of key molecules in the pyroptosis pathway (e.g., NLRP3 or GSDMD), as shown in recent studies, offering novel therapeutic strategies for related diseases39. Notably, this study demonstrates a significant reduction in the release of inflammatory factors and mucosal tissue damage by inhibiting NLRP3-driven pyroptosis, further elucidating the impact of pyroptotic cell damage on oral ulcers.

The oral mucosa and intestinal mucosa both belong to the mucosal barrier system and are highly correlated in terms of pathological mechanisms40. Recent studies have shown that IL-2Rβ is significantly upregulated during the progression of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)41, suggesting its potential key role in the regulation of mucosal immunity. Therefore, we hypothesize that IL-2Rβ may also be involved in the development of oral ulcers. As a member of the type I cytokine receptor family, IL-2Rβ (CD122) can recruit the PI3K p85 subunit to activate the AKT signaling pathway, thereby regulating the survival, proliferation, migration, and inflammatory cytokine expression of immune cells42. Notably, the PI3K-AKT pathway is central to regulating inflammatory responses and pyroptosis; its aberrant activation is closely linked to NLRP3 inflammasome assembly and GSDMD-mediated pyroptosis43. Dysregulation of this pathway is not only widely implicated in tumorigenesis and metabolic diseases but also promotes the development of various inflammatory disorders, making it a highly promising therapeutic target44. However, the role of the IL-2Rβ/PI3K axis in oral ulcers remains unexplored. This study utilized transcriptome analysis to investigate the mechanism of action of RDP3 in oral ulcer healing. The results demonstrate that RDP3 promotes mucosal tissue repair by inhibiting the IL-2Rβ/PI3K axis and mitigating pyroptosis-associated injury, providing the evidence for the important role of this signaling pathway in oral ulcer pathogenesis. Furthermore, rapamycin an indirect inhibitor of the IL-2Rβ signaling pathway, was used in subsequent experiments. This small-molecule compound inhibits downstream IL-2R signaling, blocks cell proliferation, and exhibits broad cross-species applicability. The experimental data indicate that IL-2Rβ inhibition effectively promotes oral ulcer healing, revealing its key role in ulcer pathology. It is noteworthy that the development of IL-2Rβ antagonists remains nascent, with no commercial products currently available. Existing monoclonal antibodies and direct inhibitors are primarily designed for mice and humans, lacking specialized preparations for rats. In this study, the potential of RDP3 as a peptide antagonist of IL-2Rβ was confirmed. Moreover, experiments with the mutant peptide RDP3-M confirmed that the main therapeutic effect of RDP3 depends on its specific binding to IL-2Rβ. These findings not only elucidate the critical role of the IL-2Rβ/PI3K-AKT axis in oral ulcer development but also provide important strategic insights for optimizing the targeted binding capacity and therapeutic potential of RDP3.

Compared to existing peptide-based therapies, RDP3 demonstrates unique and comprehensive advantages. It integrates antibacterial, antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, pro-healing, and analgesic functions, offering significant translational potential. Additionally, its well-defined key target, IL-2Rβ, reduces potential risks associated with non-specific effects. Moreover, its origin from rice makes it highly safe profile. Notably, RDP3 demonstrates high potency at low doses in animal models, aligning with the current pursuit of high efficacy in peptide drug development. Its multifunctionality avoids the complexity of combination therapies, making it promising as a standalone treatment and overcoming the limitations of many peptide drugs that require combinatorial regimens.

Nevertheless, RDP3 still faces certain gaps before becoming a viable drug. The oral environment is complex, with peptides susceptible to enzymatic degradation, pH changes, and salivary clearance. Formulation strategies (e.g., mucoadhesive systems, nanocarriers) are needed to improve stability and retention. Furthermore, in investigating the role of potential targets IL-2Rβ, it was found that RDP3 may still have other pathways of action. However, this study primarily relied on pharmacological inhibition, which may lack sufficient target specificity and thus affect the accuracy of functional interpretation. Future studies may focus not only on structural optimization (e.g., cyclization, D-amino acid substitution) to enhance enzymatic stability or targeted delivery systems to improve enrichment, but also on employing more precise genetic intervention strategies, such as constructing conditional knockout animal models, utilizing CRISPR-Cas9 for cell-specific knockdown, or developing highly selective small-molecule inhibitors, to achieve more accurate regulation and analysis of IL-2Rβ function.

This study introduces RDP3, a food-derived peptide with demonstrated efficacy in oral ulcer treatment. In a rat oral ulcer model, RDP3 demonstrated triple therapeutic efficacy: accelerating ulcer healing, suppressing inflammatory responses, and exerting broad-spectrum antibacterial effects. Mechanistic studies revealed that RDP3 specifically targets IL-2Rβ to inhibit the PI3K pathway, thereby modulating pyroptosis, reducing wound inflammation, and promoting mucosal repair. As a potent peptide antagonist of IL-2Rβ—a previously unexplored therapeutic target for oral ulcers—RDP3 highlights IL-2Rβ as a promising target. These findings provide both mechanistic insight into oral ulcer pathology and a promising basis for novel peptide-based therapeutics.

Methods

Peptide synthesis

The peptides RDP3 and RDP3-M, synthesized by Wuhan Tiande Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (purity ≥ 95%), were used in this study. Their structures were confirmed by mass spectrometry analysis.

Establishment of rat oral ulcer model

Male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (180-200 g, 6-8 weeks old) were sourced from Beijing Spelford Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Certification NO.: SCXK(Xiang)2021-0002). After acclimation (12-h light/dark cycle), animals were used for experiments. All procedures were approved by Kunming Medical University’s IACUC (kmmu20241522) following international animal welfare standards.

The oral ulcer model was established in accordance with published protocols45. Briefly, rats were anesthetized with isoflurane (RWD, Shenzhen, China) until loss of righting reflex. A 3-mm diameter filter paper saturated with 33% acetic acid (≥30 s saturation) was applied to the mandibular gingiva for 60 s.The study adopted a randomized four-group design (n = 6 per group), including: 1) an RDP3-treated group receiving 1 nM RDP3, 2) a PBS-treated model control group, 3) a Kangfuxin-treated positive control group, 4) an untreated normal control group, 5) an RDP3-M-treated group receiving 1 nM RDP3-M, 6) and rapamycin (MCE, New Jersey, USA) group receiving 1 mg/kg/day46. For the rapamycin group, rats received intraperitoneal injections of rapamycin at a dose of 1 mg/kg daily at 9:00 a.m., starting three days before the chemical induction of oral ulcers and continuing until the end of the regular treatment period. Ulcer sites received 50 μL topical treatment twice daily. Following each treatment, animals were maintained under fasting conditions with water restriction for 30 minutes. Ulcer dimensions were measured on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 post-inductions.

Hematoxylin-Eosin (H&E) staining

On day 7 post-modeling, rats were euthanized under isoflurane anesthetized. Ulcer site tissues were collected and immediately embedded in OCT cryomatrix (Optimal Cutting Temperature compound ; Sakura Finetek, Tokyo, Japan), and sectioned at 8 μm thickness.Tissue sections were subjected to H&E staining using established histological methods47. Stained sections were examined using an upright microscope (Primovert, Oberkochen, Germany) and imaged for pathological assessment.

Cell culture

Primary oral epithelial cell (OEC) isolation was modified from established protocols48. Gingival tissue from male SD rats were promptly dissected under sterile conditions and processed with Dispase II (2.5 mg/mL, Solarbio, Beijing, China) at 4 °C for 16 h. Following epithelial layer separation, tissues were digested in collagenase I (2 mg/mL)/hyaluronidase (1 mg/mL, both Solarbio, Beijing, China) at 37 °C for 4 h. Enzymatic activity was terminated with primary oral epithelial cell-specific culture medium (Pricella, Wuhan, China). Cell suspensions were subjected to centrifugation (1200 rpm, 5 min), after which the resulting sediments were resuspended in complete culture medium: DMEM/F12 (Vivacell, Shanghai, China) with 15% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (Vivacell, Shanghai, China), 1% antibiotics (penicillin-streptomycin-amphotericin B solution)(KeyGEN BioTECH, Jiangsu, China), 5 mM Y27632-Rho inhibitor (MCE, New Jersey, USA), 2% B27 supplement (BioSharp, Beijing, China), and 20 ng/mL EGF (Proteintech, Wuhan, China) at 37 °C in a 5% CO₂ environment.

Cell proliferation and migration assays

Primary OEC proliferation (MTS assay) and migration (transwell assay) in response to RDP3 were evaluated49,50,51. Based on dose-response results, cells were treated with 1 nM RDP3, 50 nM rapamycin52, and 1 nM RDP3-M for comparative analysis alongside PBS vehicle control transwell migration assays were also performed following rapamycin treatment.

LPS-induced inflammatory model of cells

The OEC were plated in 24-well plates (1 × 10⁵ cells/well)53. At logarithmic growth phase, cells received a 24-hour pretreatment with lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 2 μg/mL, Sigma, Missouri, USA) prior to experimental interventions.The groups were divided into the normal group (Untreated), the PBS model group (LPS + PBS), the RDP3 group (LPS + 1 nM RDP3), and the RDP3-M group (LPS + 1 nM RDP3-M).

Immunofluorescence

OEC were seeded on coverslips (5 × 10⁴ cells/well) in 24-well plates54. Adherent cells underwent 24-h exposure to 10 nM FITC-labeled RDP3 prior to fixation with 4% paraformaldehyde (BioSharp, Beijing, China). After blocking (1 h), samples were incubated with antibody IL-2Rβ (Affinity, China, 1:200) at 4 °C overnight. Following PBST washes, cells were incubated with Cy3-conjugated secondary antibody (Proteintech, China, 1:200) and counterstained with DAPI (Sigma, Missouri, USA).

To detect the effect of GSDMD on inflammatory cells, inflammatory OECs were divided into four groups: the normal group (untreated), the PBS model group (PBS), the RDP3 group (1 nM), and the RDP3-M group (1 nM). Furthermore, to examine the impact of IL-2Rβ inhibition, additional experiments were conducted by treating OECs with the IL-2Rβ inhibitor rapamycin. In these assays, OECs were divided into five groups: the normal group, the PBS group, the RDP3 group (1 nM), the rapamycin group (10 nM), and the RDP3 + rapamycin group. The expression levels of IL-2Rβ and GSDMD were then assessed in these groups.

For the transmucosal experiment, FITC-RDP3 (1 nM in PBS) was topically applied to rat mandibular gingiva. Tissues were obtained at 0.5 h, 1 h, 2 h, and 4 h post-application (n = 4/timepoint), embedded in OCT, and cryosectioned (8 μm). The sections were DAPI-counterstained.

For the transcellular membrane experiment, OECs were plated on coverslips (5 × 10⁴ cells/well) and incubated with FITC-RDP3 (1 nM, 24 h). Cell samples were subjected to 15-min staining at 37 °C using 5 μM Dil (1,1’-dioctadecyl-3,3,3’,3’-tetramethylindocarbocyanine perchlorate; Beyotime), followed by nuclear counterstaining with DAPI. All samples were imaged using a confocal microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

ELISA

The effect RDP3 (1 nM) on inflammatory cytokine secretion (TNF-α, IL-18, IL-1β, NeoBioscience, Shenzhen, China) in tissue homogenates during ulcer healing were quantified by ELISA on day 755. To process tissue from the wound site on day 7 post-administration in rats with oral ulcers, physiological saline was added at a ratio of 100 mg/mL based on tissue weight. Steel beads were used to facilitate homogenization. The tissue homogenate was then centrifuged at 14,000 rpm for 25 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected, diluted with an equal volume of physiological saline, and subsequently used for ELISA analysis. All tests were performed per manufacturer protocols.

Quantitative RT-PCR

Tissue/cellular total RNA was obtained following the Omega RNA extraction kit (Omega, Georgia, USA), with concentration measured by spectrophotometer (Thermo, Massachusetts, USA). cDNA synthesis was performed with a FulenGen reverse transcription kit. mRNA expression of IL-6, TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-18, IL-2, and IL-2Rβ was quantified by qPCR (FulenGen, Guangzhou, China) with GAPDH normalization. Quantitative analysis of relative expression was conducted by applying the 2⁻ΔΔCt algorithm. The primers are listed in Tab. S156.

Transcriptome sequencing

Ulcers tissues from model group (PBS-treated) and RDP3 (n = 6) were harvested on day 7 for RNA-seq (Majorbio Bio - Pharm Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Functional annotation of differentially expressed genes was conducted through GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses using clusterProfiler57,58.

Molecular docking

The structure of RDP3 was predicted using Robetta (robetta.bakerlab.org). IL-2Rβ coordinates (PDB: 5M2F) were prepared in PyMOL59. Docking simulations used AutoDockTools for preparation and ZDock for rigid-receptor/flexible-ligand conformational sampling. Top-scoring complexes were analyzed for binding interactions (Chengdu Fendong Biotechnology Co., Ltd).

Molecular interaction

Peptide-IL-2Rβ binding affinity was quantified by MicroScale Thermophoresis (MST)60. RDP3 (serially diluted) and Cy5-labeled IL-2Rβ (MCE, New Jersey, USA) were incubated in binding buffer (20 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, pH 7.4) before MST measurement at 25 °C (Monolith NT.115) (Wuhan Baileibo Biotechnology Co., Ltd).

Western blotting

Cell lysis was carried out on ice for 30 min using RIPA buffer (BioSharp, China) containing 1% PMSF (BioSharp) and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Beyotime, China) at a 100:1:1 ratio61. Tissues were homogenized in similar buffer (1 mL/g) at −20 °C and 65 Hz for 20 min. After centrifugation at 14000 rpm and 4 °C for 25 min, the supernatants were collected and protein concentrations were measured using the BCA assay (Beyotime, Jiangsu, China). Proteins were denatured in 5×loading buffer (EpiZyme, Shanghai, China) at 95 °C for 10 min.

Samples (30 μg) underwent 10% SDS-PAGE gels and electrotransferred to PVDF membranes62. After blocking (5% BSA/TBST, 1 h), membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: NLRP3 (Affinity, China, 1:1000), GSDMD (Affinity, China, 1:1000), ASC (Affinity, China, 1:1000), Cleaved Caspase-1 (Affinity, China, 1:1000), Caspase-1 (CST, USA, 1:1000), PI3K (Affinity, China, 1:1000), P-PI3K (Abmart, China, 1:500), AKT (Affinity, China, 1:2000), P-AKT (Affinity, China, 1:1000), IL-1β (Servicebio, China, 1:1000), Cleaved-IL-1β (Wanleibio, China, 1:1000), IL-18 (Wanleibio, China, 1:1000), TNF-α (Wanleibio, China, 1:1000), IL-6 (Proteintech, China, 1:1000), GAPDH (Proteintech, China, 1:8000), and Tubulin (Proteintech, China, 1:2000). Membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (Proteintech, China, 1:5000) at room temperature for 1 h and developed with ECL substrate (BioSharp, Beijing, China). Signal detection used X-ray film (Bio-Rad, California, USA).

Bacterial quantification

Ulcer tissues collected on day 7 were homogenized in sterile saline (1 mL/g)63,64. Following centrifugation of the homogenates at 14,000 rpm for 25 min at 4 °C, and supernatants were collected. Serial dilutions (100 μL) were inoculated into Brain Heart Infusion (BHI, CHINOOK, China, CN230226) broth (20 mL) and incubated aerobically (37 °C, 100 rpm). After 7 days, bacterial density was measured at OD600.

Circular dichroism spectrum

Circular dichroism (CD) spectra were acquired using a JASCO spectropolarimeter(JASCO, Tokyo, Japan). A baseline measurement was first performed using ultrapure water (ddH₂O) as the solvent. Subsequently, RDP3 and RDP3-M peptide samples, diluted to 1 mg/mL with ddH₂O, were loaded into a quartz cuvette, and CD data were collected under identical experimental conditions. Secondary structure analysis was conducted based on the obtained spectra65.

Statistical analysis

Data represent ≥3 independent biological replicates. Comparisons used: Student’s t-test (two groups), One-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post-hoc (multiple groups). Analyses performed in GraphPad Prism v10. Data presented as mean ± SEM and P ≤ 0.05 as significance.
