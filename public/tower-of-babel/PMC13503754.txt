Tribological performance of Al5052/B₄C/Granite hybrid composites: machine learning prediction and multi-response optimization
Authors: Movva Naga swapna sri, S. Revathi, Chitrada Prasad, Akanksha Mishra, Ataklti Hagos Hailu, Deepak Gupta, Aashim Dhawan, Shahbaz Juneja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study investigates the sustainable wear performance of Al5052/B₄C/granite hybrid composites produced by the stir casting process. Wear tests were executed on a pin on disc by varying load from 20 N to 50 N and sliding speed from 2 m/s to 3 m/s to evaluate the specific wear rate (SWR) and coefficient of friction (COF). From the results, the lowest specific wear rate of 0.201 × 10⁻³ mm³/Nm was obtained at 20 N load, 2 m/s sliding speed, and 4 wt% granite reinforcement. SEM analysis of worn surfaces revealed small cracks and minimal wear debris, indicating low fatigue wear and better resistance to surface degradation. ANN, ANFIS, and LightGBM models were also used to predict wear performance. LightGBM showed the highest accuracy, with R² values of approximately 0.9992 for SWR and 0.9987 for COF. ANOVA confirmed that sliding speed was the most significant factor affecting both responses. The Composite Desirability Function (CDF) was employed to identify the best parameter combination for reducing both SWR and COF. According to CDF results, the optimum conditions were 32.12 N load, 2.0 m/s sliding speed, and 6 wt% granite reinforcement. Under these settings, the predicted minimum COF was 0.4129 with desirability of 0.92346, and the predicted minimum wear rate was 0.3229 with desirability of 0.93323. Overall, this study demonstrates that granite powder can be effectively used as a sustainable reinforcement to improve the wear resistance of Al5052-based hybrid composites for industrial tribological applications.

Introduction

Metal matrix composites (MMCs) have proved to be highly fit for a wide range of engineering applications because they exhibit higher specific strength than conventional metals and alloys due to the presence of a secondary reinforcing phase1,2. Moreover, they have been used in structures and automobiles and aircraft parts because of their excellent wear resistance and fatigue resistance properties. Since aluminum alloys possess natural properties such as low weight, good corrosion resistance, and ease of formability, aluminum composites have become increasingly popular among different kinds of metal matrix composites. But their application in areas with heavy loads and high temperatures has been restricted because of their relatively low wear resistance and hardness properties. To address these limitations, reinforcements are incorporated into aluminum alloys to develop AMCs, thereby enhancing their overall performance. The inclusion of reinforcements significantly improves mechanical and tribological properties. Various reinforcements have been employed for aluminum alloys, based on the desired enhancement of property values. Some ceramic reinforcements used to improve hardness, stiffness, and abrasion resistance include SiC, Al2O3, B4C, ZrO2, TiC, Si3N4, and TiB2. In addition, solid lubricants like MoS2 and GR are added to enhance their self-lubricating capability and reduce friction. More recently, CNTs and graphene have attracted considerable interest due to their exceptional strength-to-weight ratio and ability to further enhance the mechanical performance of AMCs3–5. Some of the manufacturing methods used for producing MMCs include spray deposition, in situ manufacturing, stir casting and powder metallurgy. Among them, Stir casting is selected due to its low cost, simple setup for large-scale production. It allows easy incorporation of reinforcements with reasonable mechanical properties6,7. Many researchers have done work on different reinforcements. For example, Murlidhar Patel et al.8 used B4C as reinforcement to produce Al 5052 composites through the stir casting technique. These results show that the spreading of the B4C particles in the AA5052 matrix is homogeneous. In comparison with the AA5052, the fabricated MMC exhibits lower density but higher compressive strength and hardness. In study9, the mechanical characteristics of Al 5052 metal matrix composite containing graphite and boron carbide were studied. The stir casting technique was adopted in manufacturing an aluminum matrix composite test piece containing different percentages of boron carbide and graphite (2–8%) and the use of B4C and graphite in AMCs demonstrated improved mechanical properties. In another study10, Samples were produced by reinforcing aluminum alloy 5052 with graphite (0.5%) and alumina Al2O3 (2–6%). The samples were subjected to a melting process at 700 °C and reinforcements were pre-heated at 350 °C. The results indicated that the MMC made from Al2O3 reinforced with 0.5%, graphite of 6% which possesses good wear resistance. Researchers have used B4C powder as reinforcement for many alloys to improve mechanical and Tribological properties11, and the fabrication of B4C reinforced aluminium composites is achieved through the process of stir casting followed by hot rolling, as described by Donghyun Lee et al.12. The volume fractions of B4C/Al6061 composites of various fractions (5–30%) were found to be consistent with the targeted volume fractions of B4C, as observed from the results of microstructure characterization. A high level of bonding strength at the interface can be achieved, evidenced by the formation of an interfacial layer in the stir casting that preserved its novel structure after hot rolling. In another work13, AA5083 and B4C particles (5–20%) were used and according to the results, the addition of B4C particles improves the BHN and UTS of the composite material, and there was also a decrease in density due to the increased amount of B4C particles in the aluminium matrix. The B4C/Al interface created by the reaction between B4C and Al6061 resulted in the formation of a multiphase layer of Al4C3, chromium diboride CrB2, MgO, and titanium boride TiB214. The impact of hybridization on the mechanical and wear characteristics of AMMCs containing sillimanite and B4C has been studied by M. Pethuraj et al.15. With an increase in reinforcing material quantity, the rate of wear decreased. This was observed during the analysis of the results of dry sliding wear tests performed at different loading conditions (10–50 N) and sliding speed (0.75–2.25 m/s). In contrast to 5 wt% Al2SiO5-based composites, the 10 wt% Al2SiO5 composite had a 44% wear reduction rate. An increase in reinforcing caused a drop in the COF, and oxidative, adhesive, and abrasive wear were involved in the formation of protective tribofilms. The Al-6061 T6 alloy has been used as a reinforcement material for Al2O3, SiC, and B4C, according to a study conducted by Sri Ram Murthy Paladugu et al.16. High tensile strength and wear strength exist in the hybrid composites with 2wt% of SiC, Al2O3, and B4C. The researchers employed POD equipment to examine the wear features of AA6351/B4C/SiC composite under the loading range of 20 to 100 N and sliding speed range of 1 to 5 m/s. AA-5 wt% SiC composite exhibits its highest wear volume of 21.91 × 10− 3 mm3/Nm under a load of 100 N and sliding speed of 2 m/s as per the experimental results. But there has been a significant reduction in the wear volume after adding the B4C particles into the composite17. In another research18, A cost-effective stir casting process was adopted in the making of Al6061 alloy composites, incorporating 10 wt% of B4C. Taguchi L9 Orthogonal Array method and GRG algorithms were employed for multi-objective optimization purposes. The optimal levels of experimental parameters to minimize wear and friction properties were achieved through hyperparameter tuning and automated GRG calculations with Python programming.

Materials and methods

In the present study, the powders of granite and B4C are used as the reinforcing elements, and Al5052 serves as the base metal. The chemical compositions of Al5052 alloy and granite powder are presented in Table 119,20 and Table 221,22 respectively. A consistent 5 wt% B4C reinforcement with an average particle size of 30 μm, along with varying percentages of granite powder (0, 2, 4, and 6 wt%), each with particle size of 50 μm, were used to produce the hybrid composites of Al5052 using the stir-casting method and Al5052 was heated to a temperature of 750 °C inside a stir-casting furnace and B₄C and granite powder reinforcements were preheated to 500 °C before their addition to the molten alloy to improve wettability and reduce moisture-related defects. In this process, mixing of the reinforcement particles was carried out using a four-bladed stainless-steel impeller at 500 rpm for ten to fifteen minutes to ensure proper dispersion of the particles. Subsequently, the mixture of molten composite slurries was poured into moulds, which were maintained at 150 to 200 °C. The steps involved in the manufacturing of composites are presented in Fig. 1 below. Wear tests were conducted as per ASTM G9923,24 using POD equipment. The wear test samples were cut and prepared with dimensions 30 mm long and 10 mm in diameter. The test sample was placed into contact with the EN31 steel disk once secured in the pin holder25,26. With loads varying between 20 and 50 N at sliding velocities between 2 and 3 m/s, the tests were performed using a constant radius of curvature of 80 mm. Each set of wear test parameters was replicated thrice to ensure the reliability of the results, while the average values obtained were utilized for further calculations. Results and discussion

Impact of input parameters on wear perfromance

The incorporation of B4C and granite powder particles, which strengthened the composite specimens, contributed largely to the reduction in weight loss. As per Archard’s law, SWR is increased by increasing the load on the samples31. This shows that an increase in the wear rate corresponds to an increase in the weight loss of hybrid composites and metal samples. Figures 2 and 3 show the impact of load on the SWR and COF. The inclusion of granite powder particles effectively reduces the SWR, especially with a decrease in sliding speed. At a sliding speed of 3 m/s, the wear rate varies between 0.69 and 2.04 mm³/Nm (Fig. 2(a)), while at 2 m/s, it ranges from 0.28 to 0.68 mm³/Nm (Fig. 2(b)). As the granite content increases (0 to 6 wt%), which significantly reduces the wear rate. Lower speeds lead to reduced wear rates due to several factors. Higher speeds produce higher amounts of heat energy, which makes abrasive wear occur. Slower speeds will also reduce the rate at which the surfaces come into contact, resulting in plastic deformation rather than abrasive wear32. Slower speeds also lead to reduced frictional forces and more effective lubrication, further lowering wear rates by reducing direct contact between the surfaces. In essence, decreasing the sliding speed minimizes the factors contributing to aggressive wear, leading to a lower wear rate. Figures 3(a) and 3(b) present the COF results at sliding speeds of 2 m/s and 3 m/s. Comparing the two figures, the lowest COF of 0.38 was observed for the Al 5052 + 5% B4C + 4% granite composite at a load of 50 N and a sliding speed of 2 m/s (Fig. 3(a)). Increasing the granite content enhances the reinforcement effect, leading to improved stability and reduced variation in the COF. Since both the Al matrix and hybrid reinforcements form an excellent interfacial bond, they keep the COF relatively constant as well as minimize variations in wear. Moreover, with an increase in load, COF decreases for its composites. This decrease could be ascribed to the deformation of surface irregularities due to higher stress levels, leading to increased contact surface area and less resistance to shearing force at the interface. From Fig. 4, SEM analysis of the Al5052 + 5% B4C + 4% Granite composite under a load of 50 N and a sliding speed of 2 m/s, reveals surface features that demonstrate the positive impact of adding 4 wt% granite on wear performance. The low wear rate might be due to the effective anti-adhesive wear capability of the composite, since there is minimal peeling off of particles. The presence of small cracks on the surface indicates low fatigue wear, which means the composite material has better wear resistance. Also, there is minimal wear debris around the edges, thus indicating the wear resistance ability of the composite to maintain the surface integrity under operation. These results indicate that adding 4% granite enhances the wear resistance and reduces the COF of the composite.

From the comparative analysis of past studies on the aluminium matrix composites reinforced with granite powder33–41, it becomes highly evident that the COF varies between 0.389 and 0.538, and SWR mainly lies between 0.229 and 0.351 × 10⁻³ mm³/Nm under dry sliding conditions (Table 4). This is because granite powder particles are inherently abrasive in nature, causing micro-cutting, ploughing, and three-body abrasion, which leads to wear and friction. In contrast, the present investigation of the Al5052/B₄C/granite hybrid composite exhibits significantly low values of SWR (0.201 × 10⁻³ mm³/Nm) and COF (0.378), which are the lowest in the analyzed research. This development can be attributed to the synergistic effect of hybrid reinforcement, where B₄C and granite powder contribute to enhanced hardness and load-bearing capacity, while also improving interfacial bonding and stabilizing the tribolayer formation during sliding. Overall, the results confirm that the reduction in SWR (approximately 10–40%) and friction compared to conventional granite-reinforced aluminum composites.

Analysis of machine learning model

The proposed ANFIS model’s structure comprises five layers, namely the input layer, which calculates the degree of membership for each input variables (load, sliding speed and reinforcement weight%) using membership functions; the rule layer, where fuzzy rules are applied; the normalisation layer, which performs firing strength normalisation; the defuzzification layer, which determines the weighted output; and finally, the output layer (SWR and COF), which aggregates all these values. In this way, ANFIS will be able to adaptively tune its fuzzy rules and membership functions while being capable of modelling complex nonlinearities42,43. An ANFIS model is constructed based on the data obtained from the wear test, and ANFIS model is tuned to predict two outputs (wear rate and COF), then the data is employed to train the ANFIS model (Fig. 5). It is noted that ANFIS model structure is very useful when it comes to predicting the wear behaviour that is affected by many interconnected process variables. Statistical validity of fit for the models have been tested through ANOVA analysis. The relationship between regression and error variances is measured through the F statistic48–50. In the ANOVA test, if the p-value is below 0.05, statistical significance is achieved. Table 6 presents the ANOVA results for SWR. It shows that sliding speed is the most significant factor (73.7%) affecting the SWR, followed by load (23.8%). and reinforcement (1.94%). The low contribution of reinforcement percentage (1.94%) indicates that, within the investigated range, its effect on SWR was less significant than that of sliding speed and load. This may be attributed to the dominant influence of sliding conditions and the already enhanced wear resistance provided by the two reinforced composite matrix. Table 7 presents the ANOVA results for COF. It shows that sliding speed is the most significant factor (61.7%) affecting the SWR, followed by load (21.6%). and reinforcement (7.6%). Sliding speed is the most significant factor affecting both SWR and COF because it directly controls the intensity of interaction between two sliding surfaces. As sliding speed increases, frictional heat rises sharply, which can soften the material and make it more prone to material removal, thereby increasing the wear rate. At the same time, this heat and continuous motion alter the surface conditions such as forming or breaking oxide layers and transfer films, which directly influence the coefficient of friction. Higher speed also means more frequent surface contact, leading to increased adhesion, ploughing, and deformation. Because sliding speed simultaneously affects temperature, surface chemistry, and contact mechanics, it has a stronger overall impact on both wear rate and COF.

Optimization Study by CDF Method

The Composite Desirability Function (CDF) is used in this study to find the best combination of process parameters that simultaneously optimize multiple performance characteristics. This helps identify the optimal set of conditions that provides the best overall performance instead of optimizing each response separately.

Fig. 19Multi Response optimization results for Composite Desirability Function.

Multi Response optimization results for Composite Desirability Function.

Figure 19 shows the Composite Desirability Function optimization results, which help identify the best combination of input parameters (load, sliding speed, and reinforcement) to minimize both wear rate and COF. The overall desirability value is D = 0.9283, which is very close to 1, indicating an excellent set of optimal conditions. The red vertical lines represent the selected optimal values of the parameters: load of 32.12 N, sliding speed of 2.0 m/s, and reinforcement of 6wt %. The predicted minimum COF is 0.4129 with a desirability of 0.92346, and the predicted minimum wear rate is 0.3229 with a desirability of 0.93323, which were achieved at the optimal settings. Since both desirability values are high and close to 1, it means both responses are well optimized at the same time. The graphs also show how each parameter affects the responses. Sliding speed has a strongly increasing effect on both SWR and COF, confirming it is the most influential factor. Load has a moderate effect, while reinforcement slightly reduces wear and COF, improving performance.

The optimized parameter combination obtained from CDF represents the maximum overall desirability for simultaneous minimization of wear rate and COF and differs from the experimental condition yielding the minimum value of a single response. Overall, CDF demonstrates that the chosen parameter combination provides the best compromise to simultaneously minimize wear rate and COF with high reliability.

Conclusion

In this study, Al5052 hybrid composites were fabricated with the change in percentage of granite powder at 0%, 2%, 4%, and 6% and keeping B4C constant. Granite and B4C powder were used for reinforcement to make an analysis of the sustainable wear properties of hybrid composites of Al5052/B₄C/granite. The following is the conclusion drawn from the analysis.

The results showed that the addition of granite powder improved the wear resistance of the composites. An increase in granite content generally reduced the wear rate. The lowest specific wear rate of 0.201 × 10⁻³ mm³/Nm was obtained at 20 N load, 2 m/s sliding speed, and 4 wt% granite powder. For wear rate prediction, LightGBM achieved an RMSE of 0.0139, MAE of 0.0108, MSE of 0.00019, and R² of 0.9992. These results confirm that LightGBM provides excellent agreement between experimental and predicted values, making it a reliable model for predicting tribological behaviour.The Composite Desirability Function was used to find the best combination of input parameters for optimizing multiple performance characteristics. According to that results, the optimum parameters were 32.12 N load, 2.0 m/s sliding speed, and 6 wt% granite reinforcement. Under these optimized conditions, the predicted minimum COF of 0.4129 and SWR of 0.3229 were obtained.
