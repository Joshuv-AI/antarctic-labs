Nanotechnology-based detoxification of profenofos using red blood cell membrane-coated polymeric nanoparticles
Authors: Sidra Altaf, Faqir Muhammad, Rao Zahid Abbas, Mostafa A Abdel-Maksoud, Salman Alrokayan, Ahmed Othman Alsabih
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOrganophosphate (OP) poisoning poses a critical global health challenge due to the absence of specific antidotes. Profenofos (PRF), an organophosphate pesticide, is associated with severe toxicity and high risk of mortality. In this study, a novel biomimetic nanoformulation was synthesized and evaluated for its detoxification effectiveness and safety against profenofos.MethodsThe nanoformulation consisted of red blood cell (RBC) membrane-coated poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) nanoparticles (RBC-PLGA NPs). RBC membranes were used to cloak PLGA nanoparticles, and the formulation was characterized using a zeta sizer, transmission electron microscopy, and Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy. In vitro assays were performed to evaluate the adsorption of profenofos, while in vivo studies were conducted to assess survival rate, erythrocyte acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity, and hepatotoxic and renal toxicity markers in a rabbit model.ResultsRBC-PLGA NPs effectively adsorbed profenofos, with removal efficiencies reaching up to 89.92% at a concentration of 4 mg/mL. In vivo experiments showed a significant increase in survival rates following treatment with RBC-PLGA NPs. Moreover, acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity in erythrocytes was restored by the fourth day post-treatment, and no substantial hepatotoxicity or renal toxicity was observed.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that RBC membrane-coated PLGA nanoparticles effectively detoxify profenofos, significantly enhancing survival rate and restoring acetylcholinesterase activity without inducing notable toxicity. This work highlights a biomimetic nanoplatform that mimics erythrocyte functionality for toxin detoxification, offering a potential approach for the management of organophosphate poisoning.Graphical Abstract Keywords: Toxicity, Poison, Acetylcholinesterase activity, Profenofos, PLGA nanoparticle, Red blood cell membrane

Introduction

Profenofos belongs to a class of strong organophosphate insecticides that are widely used in modern agriculture to enhance agricultural productivity [1]. Despite its effectiveness, it poses serious toxicological hazards to both humans and animals, either because it causes irreversible inhibition of acetylcholinesterase (AChE) [2, 3].

Acute organophosphate poisoning remains a big public health problem because it mostly occurs as ingestion through food products, accidents at the workplace, or by self-administration [4, 5]. The treatment protocols primarily include the use of drugs like atropine and oximes, which are clinically acceptable. In regard to effectiveness and side effects, they have been quite disappointing [6]. Moreover, the organophosphates that resemble profenofos, lipophilic in nature, are rapidly absorbed and distributed in deep tissues, complicating detoxification efforts and enhancing toxic outcomes [7].

A recent development in this context is the use of nanotechnology as an approach to developing new detoxification strategies [8]. Nanomaterials have unique physicochemical properties [9] that could be engineered for targeted neutralization or removal of the toxic compounds from biological systems [10, 11]. Some of the developments already showing potential include methods of degradation of profenofos residues by polymer-stabilized Fe₂O₃-ZnO nanocomposites [12]; the infusion of IV lipid emulsions combined with charcoal hemoperfusion for the treatment of lipophilic toxin exposures [13]. Such methods are not yet used routinely in clinical toxicology practice due to associated risks, technical limitations in terms of delivery, or lack of scalability.

Safety is raised as a concern when working with metal-based nanomaterials, despite their effectiveness. A special handling protocol has to be used with the nanoparticles that contain metals such as magnesium, nickel, Fe₂O₃, and ZnO [14]. These agents hold potential cytotoxic effects in the event of improper stabilization or to initiate pro-inflammatory reactions [15]. Additionally, conventional detoxification agents, such as activated charcoal, may interfere with therapeutic regimens due to concerns about gastrointestinal consequences, highlighting the need for advanced biocompatible alternatives.

One such option is the poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) nanoparticle. PLGA is an FDA-approved and EMA-listed biodegradable and biocompatible polymer with a vast range of therapeutic uses [16]. Its potential application in cancer, sepsis, and infectious disease treatment has been studied through research on PLGA nanoparticles due to their safety profile with controlled release [17, 18].

A significant advancement in this aspect is the synthesis of biomimetic PLGA nanoparticles coated with red blood cell (RBC) membranes. The nanoparticles are stealthed with the RBC membrane disguise from immune surveillance, hence acquiring a prolonged systemic circulation lifetime and has the ability to scavenge organophosphate chemicals. Furthermore, the use of red blood cell (RBC) membranes as coatings on nanoparticles offers not only biocompatible camouflage but also serves as an external source of acetylcholinesterase (AChE) [19]. This provides a functional enzymatic surface capable of actively hydrolyzing accumulated acetylcholine, thereby mitigating the neurotoxic effects associated with organophosphate poisoning.

In nanomedicine, the concept of bionic detoxification via cell-mimicry nanostructures suggests a significant leap forward. The RBC-membrane-coated PLGA nanoparticles are further proof of the new generation of detoxification tools. The current research explores the potential of RBC-membrane-coated PLGA nanoparticles to alleviate toxicity caused by profenofos, utilizing a rabbit model. This research study seeks to demonstrate the viability of using nanotechnology for detoxifying organophosphate poisoning and aims to support its eventual application in clinical settings.

Materials and methods

Animals and ethical statement

Adult local breed rabbits weighing 1.0–1.8 kg were obtained from a reputable source in the Faisalabad livestock market and housed in the Animal House Facility, University of Agriculture, Faisalabad, Pakistan. Permission to procure and use the animals for research purposes was obtained from the respective vendor prior to the study. All experimental procedures were conducted in accordance with internationally accepted guidelines for the care and use of laboratory animals. The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Biosafety and Bioethics Committee, following the National Biosafety Guidelines 2005, Punjab Biosafety Rules, and Bioethics Protocol of the University of Agriculture, Faisalabad (Approval No. 8817/ORIC).

Animal housing and acclimatization

Animals were acclimatized for seven days prior to the experiment and maintained under controlled laboratory conditions (temperature 22 ± 2 °C, 12 h light/dark cycle), with free access to standard pellet diet and clean drinking water ad libitum. Rabbits were housed in standard laboratory cages and maintained under hygienic conditions. Animals were observed daily to monitor their general health status and well-being.

Anesthesia and euthanasia

At the end of the study, animals were first rendered rapidly and deeply anesthetized using a single intravenous injection of overdose of pentobarbital sodium (100 mg/kg). Adequate anesthetic depth was confirmed by the absence of righting reflex and lack of corneal and pedal withdrawal reflexes. This step ensured that the animals were fully unconscious and free from pain or distress prior to proceeding with euthanasia. Following confirmation of deep anesthesia, euthanasia was achieved through the same intravenous administration of pentobarbital, which resulted in respiratory and cardiac arrest and humane death. Death was confirmed by the absence of heartbeat, respiration, and reflex responses before any tissue collection or disposal. All steps were carried out in strict compliance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee and internationally recognized standards for the humane treatment of laboratory animals.

Derivation of RBC membranes

RBC membranes are obtained by exposing RBCs to a hypotonic environment of distilled water. Briefly, 0.5 mL of blood was collected from rabbits into an Eppendorf tube, and 0.5 mL of distilled water was added. The resulting solution was sonicated for 5 min and then centrifuged with microcentrifuge (HERMLE–Z 216 MK) for 3 min at 13,000 rpm. This hypotonic procedure was repeated 5–6 times until a clear light pink-colored layer of RBC membranes was obtained at the bottom as a pellet. The resulting pellet of RBC membranes was then mixed with 1 mL of distilled water to prepare a homogeneous suspension of RBC membranes.

Preparation of cell membrane-cloaked PLGA nanoparticles

RBC-camouflaged nanoparticles were synthesized using a previously described protocol [18]. Briefly, polymeric cores of poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) (PLGA) were prepared by the nano-precipitation method. A solution of carboxy-terminated (50:50) PLGA (Sigma-Aldrich®: Molecular weight: 7000–17,000) was prepared. Then, a magnetic stirrer (Velp Scientifica AREC) was used to stir 1 mL of the PLGA solution into 2 mL of water, and the mixture was kept under vacuum to promote the evaporation of the volatile component, acetone. The resulting polymeric NP solution was mixed with equal volume of the rabbit-RBC membrane suspension and sonicated for 2 min using an Elmasonic sonicator (E 30 H) with 100 W power. After sonication, the samples were stored for further analysis.

Characterization of cell membrane-cloaked PLGA nanoparticles

The developed RBC membrane coated poly(lactic-co-glycolic acid) nanoparticles were characterized to assess their physicochemical properties and structural features.

Particle size and surface charge (zeta potential) were measured using dynamic light scattering on a Zetasizer (ver. 7.11; Serial No. MAL1127001, Malvern ZEN 3600). The instrument operates with a single-frequency laser diode (λ = 658 nm) providing a 40mW output, with a laser warm-up time of 6 min and the measurements were performed at a detection angle of 173°. Zeta potential was determined by electrophoretic light scattering using the same instrument.

Morphological analysis was carried out using transmission electron microscopy with a JEOL JEM-1010 Transmission Electron Microscope (JEOL Ltd., Japan). A drop of the nanoparticle suspension was placed on a carbon-coated copper grid, followed by negative staining with 1% uranylacetate stain. After air drying, imaging was performed at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was conducted using a PerkinElmer Spectrum Two FT-IR Spectrometer equipped with an ATR accessory over the range of 4000–400 cm⁻¹ to examine functional groups and potential interactions among formulation components.

Determination of AChE activity

Measurements of AChE activity were carried out based on an improved methodology from a previously reported protocol [20]. The analysis was conducted by using the acetylcholinesterase Elabscience assay kit with Catalogue No: E-BC-K174. The thiocholine produced by the enzyme AChE from acetylcholine reacted with 5,5’-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) to form a yellow-colored product called 5-mercapto-nitrobenzoic acid (TNB). The intensity of the color of TNB was read at a wavelength of 412 nm, correlating to the activity of the AChE enzyme. The enzyme activity was determined using the formulae and protocol provided in the assay kit and measured using a 96-well plate reader (Tecan Infinite M200) at an absorbance of 412 nm. The activity of acetylcholinesterase enzyme was assessed by monitoring the change in absorbance at 412 nm over a duration of 20-minute.

In-vitro assessment of profenofos removal capacity of RBC-PLGANP

The adsorptive potential and detoxification capacity of red blood cell membrane coated PLGA nanoparticles (RBC-PLGANP) against lethal profenofos (PRF) were evaluated using an in vitro incubation assay under controlled conditions. PRF adsorption was examined under two conditions, at a fixed RBC-PLGA nanoparticle concentration with increasing PRF levels, and at a constant PRF concentration with varying RBC-PLGA nanoparticle concentrations.

In each case, RBC-PLGANP-PRF mixtures were prepared and vortexed for 30 min at room temperature to facilitate adsorption. Following incubation, the mixtures were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min to separate nanoparticle-bound PRF from the unbound fraction.

After centrifugation, the RBC-PLGANP pellet containing adsorbed PRF was separated, and the supernatant containing unbound organophosphate (PRF) was carefully collected. The residual PRF concentration in the supernatant was quantified by measuring absorbance at 250 nm. The percentage of PRF removal was calculated by using the residual PRF concentration in treated samples.

In-vivo assessment of survival rate following RBC-PLGANP treatment

Rabbits were randomly allocated into three experimental groups (n = 8 per group). Group I served as the control group and received an equivalent volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) under the same experimental conditions as the treated groups. Group II (OP-intoxicated group) received profenofos alone, administered either orally (300 mg/kg) or intravenously (700 mg/kg). The selected doses were based on reported toxicological evaluations of profenofos, including WHO guidelines and published literature [21, 22], which indicate moderate acute toxicity and dose-dependent inhibition of acetylcholinesterase. The doses were further optimized to induce consistent and measurable toxicity required for evaluating the detoxification efficacy of RBC-PLGA nanoparticles across different administration routes. Group III (treatment group) received the prepared RBC-PLGA nanoparticle (RBC-PLGA-NP) formulation at a dose of 200 mg/kg immediately following profenofos exposure.

Two independent experiments were conducted to assess the effect of the administration route of the RBC-PLGANP formulation. In the first experiment, both profenofos (300 mg/kg) and RBC-PLGANP were administered orally. In the second experiment, profenofos was administered intravenously (700 mg/kg), followed immediately by intravenous administration of RBC-PLGANP. Animal survival was continuously monitored, and survival rates were recorded for each group.

Assessment of AChE activity recovery following RBC-PLGANP treatment

To evaluate the recovery of acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity following RBC-PLGANP administration, blood samples were collected from treated rabbits challenged with organophosphate (OP) exposure via either oral or intravenous routes. Sampling was performed on days 0, 2, and 4 post-treatment. Red blood cell (RBC) ghosts were prepared from the collected blood samples, and AChE activity was quantified using a previously established protocol. The extent of enzymatic activity recovery following RBC-PLGANP treatment was assessed by comparing AChE levels across time points and treatment groups.

Assessment of modulation of profenofos intoxication by RBC-PLGA nanoparticles

a) Determination of Profenofos lethal dose (LD₁₀₀)

The lethal dose of PRF was evaluated by administering a subcutaneous injection of single dose of PRF ranging from 1000 to 1500 mg/kg and rabbit survival was examined. Following administration, animals were closely monitored, and survival was recorded. The minimum dose that resulted in 100% mortality within 1 h post-administration (LD₁₀₀) was defined and used for subsequent intoxication modulation experiments.

b) Determination of RBC-PLGANP mediated modulation of PRF lethal dose intoxication

To assess the protective effect of RBC-PLGA nanoparticles (RBC-PLGANP) against PRF-induced intoxication, the LD₁₀₀ dose of PRF was administered via subcutaneous injection. Immediately following PRF subcutaneous exposure, varying doses of RBC-PLGANP at 0,50,100,150,200 mg/kg were administered intravenously (injection volume: 0.2mL) in respective groups. The severity of intoxication was evaluated using an established intoxication scoring system.

The intoxication score was based on the presence of characteristic organophosphate toxicity symptoms, including muscle cramping, excessive salivation, generalized tremors, and hypoventilation. Each observed symptom was assigned a score of one point, yielding a maximum possible intoxication score of four, as previously described in the literature [23].

Hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity assessment of RBC-PLGANP

The potential hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity of RBC-PLGA nanoparticles were assessed in two groups of rabbits. Each group received a single administration of the nanoparticle formulation. Rabbits in the first group were euthanized on day 3, and those in the second group on day 7 post-administration. Blood samples were collected from all animals prior to euthanasia to evaluate liver and kidney function.

Serum levels of total bilirubin, creatinine, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), albumin, fibrinogen, and total protein were measured using commercial kits (Quimica Clinica Aplicada S.A.). These markers were chosen to monitor liver and kidney function. ALT and AST for hepatocellular injury, bilirubin for hepatic excretory function, albumin and total protein for liver synthetic capacity, creatinine for renal function, and fibrinogen as an indicator of systemic inflammatory response.

Liver and kidney tissues were collected, fixed, and processed for histopathological analysis. Sections were prepared, stained, and examined under a light microscope to identify any structural changes associated with RBC-PLGA nanoparticles administration [24].

Statistical analysis

Data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0.1). One-way or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) were applied to compare differences among groups, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test for comparisons. The data were analyzed without formal assessment of normality prior to selecting statistical tests.

Results

Characterization of RBC-PLGANP and RBC-PLGANP/PRF complex

The particle size of the PLGA NP, RBC-PLGANP, and RBC-PLGANP/PRF was determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS) and the average hydrodynamic diameter was found to be 101.1 nm, 121.1 nm, and 142.5 nm, respectively, as shown in Fig. 1.

The surface charge of the PLGA NP, RBC-PLGANP and RBC-PLGANP/PRF was measured by evaluating zeta potential. The values we got were found to be -24.7mV, -32.4mV and − 29.8mV respectively as shown in Fig. 2.

Zeta potential of PLGA nanoparticles, RBC-PLGA nanoparticles, and RBC-PLGA NP/PRF complex. Zeta potential values are expressed in millivolts (mV) and were measured to assess surface charge characteristics

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging

TEM was utilized to investigate the structural characteristics of the complex formed between RBC-loaded poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) nanoparticles and PRF. As shown in Fig. 3, the nanoparticles were spherical with smooth surfaces and a clear core-shell structure. The particle size measured 50 nm, and the particles were well-dispersed with minimal aggregation, confirming the successful formation of the RBC-PLGA nanostructures.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image showing the morphological structure of the RBC-PLGA NP/PRF complex. The image illustrates particle shape and membrane coating. Scale bar is indicated on the micrograph

FTIR spectroscopy (surface analysis)

Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was carried out on the RBC-PLGA nanoparticles (RBC-PLGANP) and the RBC-PLGANP/profenofos (PRF) complex. As shown in Fig. 4A and B, the main transmittance peaks of the nanoparticles remained unchanged after loading with PRF. This indicates that profenofos was successfully incorporated into the nanoparticles rather than simply adhering to their surface, confirming the formation of a stable RBC-PLGANP/PRF complex.

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectra of (A) RBC-PLGA nanoparticles and (B) RBC-PLGA NP/PRF complex, illustrating characteristic functional group transmittance patterns in the range of 4000–600 cm⁻¹

AChE activity analysis of RBC-PLGANP

The activity of acetylcholinesterase (AChE) was assessed in red blood cell (RBC) membranes and in RBC-polymeric nanoparticles to determine if the enzyme’s activity was retained during the coating process of the membranes onto the nanoparticles. Results showed that the AChE activity was comparable between both formulations, indicating that there was minimal loss of the membrane-bound AChE enzyme during the preparation of the RBC-PLGANP, as demonstrated in the accompanying Fig. 5.

Acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity associated with hollow red blood cell membranes and RBC-PLGA nanoparticles. Enzyme activity is expressed as nmol/min/ml. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3)

In-vivo analysis

Detoxification potential of RBC-PLGANP after oral PRF administration

a) Survival rate

Oral administration of profenofos (PRF) alone resulted in rapid mortality, with all rabbits in Group II (PRF-intoxicated group) succumbing within 2 h. In contrast, rabbits in Group III (PRF + RBC-PLGANP) that received RBC-PLGA nanoparticles (200 mg/kg) immediately after PRF exposure showed a marked improvement in survival. Group I (control) showed no mortality throughout the observation period. The protective effect of RBC-PLGA-NP treatment was evident from the delayed onset of mortality and increased overall survival, as illustrated in Fig. 8. This indicates that RBC-PLGA nanoparticles can sequester profenofos in vivo, reducing systemic toxicity and improving survival outcomes.

Survival curve of rabbits following oral administration of profenofos (PRF) alone and PRF combined with RBC-PLGA nanoparticles. Survival was monitored over time (hours). Data were analyzed using Kaplan–Meier survival analysis (n = 8 per group). Survival differed significantly between groups as assessed by the log-rank (Mantel–Cox) test (p < 0.0001). Median survival was 1.415 in the PRF group, while median survival was not reached in the RBC-PLGA nanoparticles co-treated group

b) Restoration of AChE activity in red blood cells

The study also investigated the relationship between the ability of RBC-PLGANP to capture organophosphate and their effectiveness in preserving and enhancing AChE enzyme activity in RBCs. Thus, the restoration of AChE enzyme activity by RBC-PLGANP after exposure to PRF was assessed using the activity of RBC-AChE as an indicator. The research revealed that the acetylcholinesterase enzyme activity of RBC returned to normal value by the 4th day following the administration of RBC-PLGANP subsequent to PRF exposure, as depicted in Fig. 9. This recovery demonstrates that RBC-PLGA nanoparticles not only capture the toxin but also help preserve and restore enzymatic function, highlighting the mechanism by which they mitigate organophosphate-induced toxicity.

Blood samples and vital organs, including the liver and kidneys, were collected from both groups for analysis. Serum levels of bilirubin, creatinine, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), albumin, fibrinogen, and total protein were measured to assess organ function, as shown in Fig. 14. Liver and kidney tissues were processed, sectioned, stained, and examined histologically (Fig. 15).

The evaluation at two different time points was carried out to monitor any time-dependent changes following nanoparticle administration. Any variations observed between day 3 and day 7 may be related to the gradual distribution, accumulation, and clearance of nanoparticles within the body. In particular, the liver and kidneys, being primary sites for metabolism and excretion, may exhibit mild changes over time due to increased exposure. However, comparison between day 3 and day 7 indicated no significant alterations in biochemical parameters or tissue architecture, suggesting that RBC-PLGANP did not induce any noticeable hepatic or renal toxicity over the study period. Thus supporting the overall biocompatibility of the formulation.

Discussion

This research demonstrates the efficacy of RBC-membrane-coated PLGA nanoparticles to detoxify a widely used organophosphate pesticide, Profenofos in experimental rabbit model. The utilization of nanoparticle technology in conjunction with the natural properties of red blood cell membranes provides a potential strategy for mitigating the toxicity associated with profenofos exposure [25]. The application of nanoparticles for agricultural purposes, including the remediation of pesticide contamination, has garnered considerable attention in recent years [25, 26]. The unique physicochemical properties of nanoparticles, including their extensive surface area and capacity to interact with and encapsulate various compounds, make them suitable for targeted delivery and controlled release of active ingredients [27].

The observed increase in nanoparticle size from bare PLGA (101.1 nm) to RBC-PLGANP (121.1 nm) and further to RBC-PLGANP/PRF complex (142.5 nm) aligns with prior reports of successful erythrocyte membrane cloaking and progressive surface functionalization of nanoparticles [19]. The more negative zeta potential after coating (− 32.4mV) confirms membrane presence, improving colloidal stability and mimicking native RBC surface charge, as demonstrated by Gao et al. 2016 [26].

TEM imaging corroborated the intact membrane shell, consistent with the preservation of membrane architecture critical for biological function. FTIR analysis showed no chemical alteration upon PRF binding, indicating physical adsorption, in line with prior studies on organophosphate interactions with biomimetic surfaces [27].

Preservation of membrane-bound AChE activity after coating confirmed that RBC-PLGANP maintained functional protein integrity, a key factor for organophosphate binding and detoxification [28]. This retention of enzymatic activity is crucial for the “biomimetic sink” effect and was also observed in similar RBC-membrane coated nanocarriers [29].

In-vitro studies revealed dose-dependent PRF adsorption by RBC-PLGANP, with saturation observed at higher toxin concentrations. These findings echo previous works demonstrating limited binding capacity governed by membrane protein availability [29, 30]. Increased nanoparticle doses enhanced detoxification, suggesting tunable therapeutic potential.

In vivo, oral PRF administration caused rapid mortality in untreated animals, while RBC-PLGANP treatment significantly improved survival and facilitated recovery of RBC-AChE activity by day four. This parallels results from biomimetic detoxification systems reported by Zhang [30], underscoring effective systemic toxin sequestration and enzyme function restoration. The improved survival and restoration of RBC acetylcholinesterase activity observed in rabbits treated with RBC-PLGA nanoparticles can be attributed to their biomimetic design. By coating PLGA nanoparticles with RBC membranes, the nanoparticles effectively sequester circulating profenofos, reducing its availability to inhibit AChE. This toxin adsorption allows enzymatic function to recover, mitigating neuromuscular and systemic toxicity. Immediate administration post-exposure was critical, as early sequestration prevented irreversible AChE inhibition, highlighting the importance of timely intervention to achieve detoxification without inducing hepatic or renal toxicity.

Similar protective effects were observed following intravenous PRF challenge, highlighting the formulation’s efficacy across exposure routes, as previously reported for nanoparticle-based antidotes [31]. Dose-dependent reduction in intoxication scores further confirmed RBC-PLGANP’s therapeutic value.

Biochemical and histopathological analyses showed no hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity at early and later time points, supporting the formulation’s safety profile consistent with prior biocompatibility studies on RBC membrane-coated nanomaterials [32].

Collectively, these results affirm RBC-PLGANP as a potential biomimetic nanoplatform for organophosphate detoxification, combining preserved membrane protein function, effective toxin adsorption, in vivo efficacy, and favorable safety. Importantly, this study provides novel evidence for the possible application of RBC membrane-coated nanoparticles in mitigating profenofos toxicity, demonstrating a biomimetic strategy that integrates toxin sequestration and functional AChE restoration.

Study limitations

This study has certain limitations. It was conducted in an acute rabbit model, which may not fully represent chronic or repeated exposure scenarios of organophosphate poisoning. Long-term toxicity, immunogenicity, and pharmacokinetic profiling of RBC-PLGA nanoparticles were not evaluated, and dose–response optimization was limited to the tested range. Although the safety profile of RBC-PLGA nanoparticles was assessed separately, the absence of a nanoparticle-only control group in the main experimental design may have limited a more comprehensive evaluation of the baseline biological effects of the formulation.

Conclusion

This study developed a biomimetic nanoformulation using red blood cell membranes coated on PLGA nanoparticles for detoxification of profenofos. The RBC-PLGA nanoparticles mitigated AChE inhibition and significantly improved survival outcomes in treated rabbits. Restoration of AChE activity was achieved and hepatic or renal toxicity was not observed under the experimental conditions, indicating the biocompatibility of the formulation. This study provides novel preliminary evidence for the potential application of RBC membrane-coated nanoparticles in profenofos detoxification, demonstrating a biomimetic strategy that combines toxin sequestration with functional enzyme restoration. These findings support the potential of RBC-PLGA nanoparticles as a promising therapeutic platform for managing organophosphate poisoning. However, further studies are required to evaluate long-term safety, pharmacokinetics, dose optimization, and clinical applicability.
