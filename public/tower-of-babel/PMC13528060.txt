Molecular characterisation of telitacicept response phenotypes in systemic lupus erythematosus: an exploratory integrated transcriptome and B-cell receptor repertoire profiling study
Authors: Mengjie Chen, Wenlong Wang, Yongjun Cheng, Yuanjia Tang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTelitacicept, a dual BAFF/APRIL inhibitor, has shown clinical efficacy in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), but the underlying molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood.MethodsWe performed an integrative longitudinal study in ten patients with SLE before and after telitacicept treatment, alongside ten healthy controls. Paired transcriptome RNA sequencing and dedicated B-cell receptor (BCR) sequencing and repertoire analysis were integrated with serial clinical phenotyping including SLEDAI-2 K, anti-dsDNA antibodies, and complement C3/C4 levels over 12 months of follow-up. Background

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a complex and potentially life-threatening systemic autoimmune disease characterized by dysregulated immune responses and multi-organ involvement [1]. Despite advances in standard immunosuppressive therapies [2], a significant proportion of patients remain refractory or experience significant drug-related toxicity [1], underscoring the need for more targeted and effective treatments. The pathogenesis of SLE involves a critical role for B cells, including autoantibody production, cytokine secretion, and antigen presentation [3]. This central role has made B-cell targeting a rational therapeutic strategy [3].

Telitacicept is a novel, recombinant fusion protein that simultaneously binds to and neutralizes two key B-cell survival factors: B‑cell activating factor (BAFF) and a proliferation-inducing ligand (APRIL) [4]. By inhibiting the BAFF/APRIL signaling axis, telitacicept promotes apoptosis of autoreactive B cells and plasma cells, positioning it as a promising biologic therapy for SLE [4]. Its clinical efficacy and favorable safety profile have been established in randomized controlled trials [5, 6] and subsequently corroborated in real-world studies [7, 8]. However, the current body of evidence remains predominantly anchored in clinical outcome metrics, such as disease activity scores and serological markers [5–8]. A significant gap exists in our understanding of the comprehensive molecular and transcriptomic landscape that underlies the clinical response to telitacicept. Questions regarding its precise effects on global gene expression programs, immune cell repertoire remodeling, and the molecular basis of inter-patient variability in treatment response remain largely unexplored.

Therefore, while confirming the clinical effectiveness of telitacicept, this study aims to delve deeper into the underlying biological mechanisms driving its therapeutic impact. We employed an integrative approach, combining longitudinal clinical data with paired transcriptomic sequencing and B-cell receptor (BCR) repertoire analysis in patients with SLE before and after telitacicept treatment. Our objectives are threefold: (1) to define the transcriptome-wide changes induced by telitacicept and identify unique responsive gene signature; (2) to characterize the consequent remodeling of the B-cell immunophenotype and immunoglobulin repertoire; and (3) to evaluate post-treatment molecular profiles for evidence of compensatory or counter-regulatory inflammatory pathway activation. By integrating molecular profiling with clinical phenotyping, this study suggests potential molecular underpinnings of telitacicept’s efficacy, which may inform future treatment strategies for SLE.

Methods

Study population

Patients with SLE who fulfilled the 2019 European League Against Rheumatism/American College of Rheumatology classification criteria [9] were recruited from the First People’s Hospital of Wenling. All patients received standard therapy in combination with telitacicept based on their clinical condition. Standard therapy consisted of glucocorticoids (GC), hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), and mycophenolate mofetil (MMF). Clinical data was abstracted from electronic medical records and disease activity was.

quantified using the systemic lupus erythematosus disease activity index 2000 (SLEDAI-2 K) [10]. Healthy controls (HC) were matched to patients in a 1:1 ratio based on age and sex. Peripheral whole blood specimens were obtained at baseline and 3 months following telitacicept treatment and stored in PAXgene tubes (QUIAGEN) at − 80 °C until processing (Fig. 1 Data collection). The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of First People’s Hospital of Wenling (Ethics approval number: KY-2024-1112-01).

Workflow of the study

RNA sequencing and data processing

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol™ Reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018). The concentration of total RNA was measured by Qubit RNA HS assay kit (Invitrogen, Q32852). VAHTS Stranded mRNA-seq Library Prep Kit for Illumina V2 (Vazyme Biotech, NR612-02) was used for library preparation according to the instructions. Trim_galore (v0.6.10) was used to remove adapters. Clean FASTQ files were mapped against the hg38 genome (GRCh38) reference using STAR (v2.7.10b_alpha_23-06-09). We employed featureCounts (v2.0.6) for calculating read counts. Finally, all raw counts were normalized using fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM) normalization.

B cell receptor repertoire sequencing and analysis

Targeted BCR sequencing was performed using the Epi™ BCR‑seq kit (Epibiotek), which employs BCR‑specific primers to amplify the full‑length V(D)J region of B cell receptors. Total RNA (200 ng–1 µg) from peripheral whole blood was used for mRNA capture, followed by reverse transcription and PCR amplification (12–18 cycles). Paired‑end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) was conducted on a next‑generation sequencing platform. Raw sequencing reads were processed using MiXCR (v3.0.13). Paired-end reads were aligned against Homo sapiens hg38 reference genomes in RNA-seq mode, allowing partial alignments to maximize recovery of immunoglobulin transcripts. To improve clonotype assembly, we performed two rounds of partial assembly, followed by CDR3 extension, and finally assembled clonotypes with relaxed quality thresholds to maximize sensitivity. The resulting clonotype tables contained V(D)J gene assignments, CDR3 nucleotide and amino acid sequences, and clonotype abundances. Clonotype tables were further analyzed with VDJtools (v1.2.1) to characterize repertoire features.

Rarefaction analysis was performed to account for differences in sequencing depth across samples. All samples were downsampled to a common read depth of 1,053,397 reads, which corresponded to the minimum sequencing depth across all samples. Rarefaction curves were generated to verify that the chosen depth captured the majority of clonal diversity, as evidenced by the curves reaching a plateau. Diversity metrics, including number of unique clonotypes, D50 index, and Shannon-Wiener index, were then calculated on the rarefied data.

To address the compositional nature of immunoglobulin heavy chain variable (IGHV) usage data, we applied a subcompositional normalization approach [11]. For each sample s and each component i within a predefined set S, the normalized frequency was calculated as:

where f i, s is the raw frequency of component i in sample s, and the denominator is the sum of raw frequencies of all components in set S for the same sample. This transformation ensures that the frequencies of all components in S sum to one within each sample, allowing valid comparison of their relative abundances independent of the absolute abundance of the set. This principle was applied to two analyses: (1) Top 10 IGHV genes, where S represents the set of the 10 most frequently used IGHV genes; and (2) IGHV4-34 V-J pairing, where S represents the set of J genes paired with IGHV4-34. Normalized frequencies of the top 10 genes were compared between SLE2 (post-treatment) and SLE1 (pre-treatment) using Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank tests. The Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied, with q < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

To track the fate of individual B cell clones following telitacicept treatment, unique CDR3 amino acid sequences were used as clonal identifiers. Clones with extremely low abundance (total frequency < 1 × 10⁻⁶ across pre- and post-treatment samples) were excluded to minimize noise from sequencing. Clones were then categorized into five mutually exclusive fates based on their frequency dynamics: (1)Lost: clones that were present before treatment (pre-treatment frequency > 0) but absent after treatment (post-treatment frequency = 0); (2) New: clones that were absent before treatment (pre-treatment frequency = 0) but detected after treatment (post-treatment frequency > 0); (3) Contracted: clones present at both time points that showed a substantial decrease in frequency, defined as a log2 fold change < -1 ; (4) Expanded: clones present at both time points that showed a substantial increase in frequency, defined as a log2 fold change > 1; (5) Stable: clones present at both time points that remained relatively stable, defined as an absolute log2 fold change ≤ 1. The log2 fold change was calculated as log2 [(post-treatment frequency + 10− 8) / (pre-treatment frequency + 10− 8)], where a pseudocount of 10− 8 was added to avoid division by zero.

Analysis of differential gene expression and functional analysis

A paired comparison of gene expression (post-treatment vs. pre-treatment) for each patient was performed using the R package edgeR (v3.36.0) to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs), with the thresholds set at P value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 2. We used raw counts as input data for edgeR. A union set of all patient-specific DEGs was created after removing duplicates, constituting the final total DEG list. A comprehensive analysis was conducted on the final total DEG list. First, the Kruskal Wallis test was employed to assess overall differences in gene expression across the three groups: pre-treatment SLE patients (SLE1), post-treatment SLE patients (SLE2), and healthy controls (HC). For genes demonstrating significant overall differences (P < 0.05), pairwise post-hoc comparisons were conducted using Wilcoxon test with Bonferroni correction. Following the statistical analysis, genes with total FPKM > 30 (summed across all samples) were selected for subsequent classification. The pairwise comparisons were evaluated in a specific sequence: SLE1 vs HC, SLE2 vs HC, and SLE2 vs SLE1. For each comparison, a significant result was denoted as ‘Y’ (adjusted P < 0.05) and a non-significant result as ‘N’ (adjusted P > 0.1). Genes were then categorized into distinct sets based on their significance patterns. Gene set 1(patterns ‘YNN’ and ‘YNY’): Genes that were differentially expressed between pre-treatment SLE and HC but showed no significant difference between post-treatment SLE and HC. Gene set 2 (patterns ‘YYN’ and ‘YYY’): Genes that were differentially expressed between pre-treatment SLE and HC and remained differentially expressed post-treatment, or showed persistent differences that may reflect ongoing dysregulation. The analytical approach described above was designed to address the heterogeneity inherent in SLE and to specifically distinguish molecular abnormalities reversed by therapy from those that persist. A schematic diagram of the analytical workflow is provided in supplemental figure S1. We acknowledge the limitations of FPKM for cross-sample comparisons; therefore, the pattern-based findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating. Enrichment analysis was performed using the Gene Ontology (GO) and Reactome databases.

Cell deconvolution analysis

We utilized the R package CIBERSORT (v0.1.0) for cell deconvolution analysis, with FPKM-normalized expression data as input, to infer the relative abundance of immune cells from the gene expression profiles. The analysis was based on the previously established leukocyte signature matrix (LM22), which comprises 547 genes that distinguish 22 human hematopoietic cell phenotypes [12]. Subsequently, we analyzed the dynamics of immune cell infiltration by comparing its patterns before and after treatment in patients relative to healthy controls.

Analysis of BAFF/APRIL signaling and its downstream pathways

Given that telitacicept binds to both BAFF and APRIL, thereby inhibiting their interactions with various B-cell receptors [5], we further analyzed the BAFF/APRIL signaling axis and its downstream pathways, specifically the PI3K-Akt and NF-κB pathways [13, 14], to elucidate the underlying mechanisms of the drug. The pathway identifiers (hsa04151 for PI3K-Akt and hsa04064 for NF-κB) were obtained from the KEGG PATHWAY Database (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html). Using the R package KEGGREST (v1.34.0), we retrieved all genes annotated to these pathways and examined their expression levels in healthy controls as well as in patients before and after treatment.

Statistical analysis

The Wilcoxon test was applied to analyze quantitative variables between two independent groups. For the analysis of overall IGHV total usage between pre- and post-treatment samples, the Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed rank test was employed. Differences across three independent groups were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test. Multiple comparisons were conducted using the Bonferroni correction. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to analyze IGHV gene usage and clinical parameters in patients of SLE, followed by Benjamini‑Hochberg FDR correction for multiple comparisons, and an FDR-adjusted q < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Unless stated otherwise, statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.1.1 and R 4.1.0.

Results

Clinical characteristics

Figure 1 illustrates the study design. Ten patients diagnosed with SLE and ten healthy controls were recruited. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics, including pre-treatment medications, are summarised in supplemental table S1. Post-treatment concomitant therapies during the 12-month follow-up period are detailed in supplemental table S2. At baseline, the cohort presented with a mean SLEDAI-2 K score of 9.3 ± 6.9, and a median disease duration of 4.5 years. Half of the patients (n = 5, 50.0%) had lupus nephritis, and most were positive for anti-dsDNA antibodies (n = 9, 90.0%). The majority of patients were not treatment-naïve (n = 8, 80.0%) and were receiving standard-of-care therapy, including hydroxychloroquine (n = 6, 60.0%) and prednisolone at a median dose of 7.5 mg/day.

Suppression of humoral immunity and B cell clonal diversity

To characterize the transcriptional effects of telitacicept in SLE, we analyzed differentially expressed genes post-treatment. Analysis of downregulated genes (Gene set 1 mentioned in Methods) post-treatment identified a signature potentially linked to B cell function (Fig. 2A). Pathway enrichment confirmed significant suppression of humoral immunity, most notably the “B Cell Receptor Signaling Pathway” and “Complement Activation” (Fig. 2B). Consistent with this molecular suppression, B cell receptor repertoire analysis suggested a contraction in clonal diversity following therapy. After depth normalization(Fig. S2), a consistent decreasing trend with telitacicept treatment was observed across the diversity metrics, including unique clonotype count, D50 index, and Shannon-Wiener index (Fig. 2C).

Telitacicept may modulate B Cells and inhibit proinflammatory signaling

Immunophenotyping via deconvolution analysis suggested estimated changes in immune cell composition following telitacicept treatment (Fig. 2D). This was characterized by an estimated reduction in plasma cell abundance and a concomitant partial reconstitution of the naïve B-cell pool (Fig. 2E), consistent with the expected pharmacologic effect of dual BAFF/APRIL inhibition (Fig. 2F). Further investigation into downstream signaling cascades demonstrated significant downregulation of genes within the PI3K-Akt (Fig. 2G) and NF-κB (Fig. 2H) pathways post-treatment. Key downregulated genes included critical signaling nodes and transcriptional targets, suggesting a potential attenuation of these pathways, although direct evidence is lacking.

Observed IGHV repertoire dynamics associated with clinical parameters after treatment

We first examined whether telitacicept treatment affects the overall abundance of IGHV genes within the BCR repertoire. The total frequency of all IGHV genes significantly decreased after treatment (P = 0.0273; Fig. 3A). To investigate whether the relative composition of the most frequently used IGHV genes was altered, we selected the top 10 IGHV genes in pre-treatment samples (Fig. 3B). No significant changes in the relative usage of top 10 IGHV genes were observed following telitacicept treatment (FDR q > 0.05 for all comparisons). Nominal trends included decreased usage of IGHV3-30 and IGHV4-34, and IGHV1-46 (Fig. 3C).

To explore whether telitacicept treatment may reshape the BCR repertoire at the clonal level, we tracked the fate of 4,529 individual clones expressing three IGHV genes (IGHV1-46, IGHV3-30, and IGHV4-34) across paired pre- and post-treatment samples. In this exploratory analysis, the majority of clones (58.7%, n = 2,660) were lost after treatment, while 16.2% (n = 732) were newly detected after treatment. Among clones that persisted at both time points, 12.5% (n = 568) showed contraction, 11.4% (n = 514) showed expansion, and only 1.2% (n = 55) remained stable. For IGHV4-34-expressing clones, 58.5% were lost, 17.0% were newly detected, 12.1% contracted, 11.3% expanded, and 1.0% remained stable(Table S3; Fig. 3D).

Spearman correlation analysis revealed significant clinical associations for these IGHV genes at baseline after FDR correction for multiple comparisons (q < 0.05) (Fig. 3E). Specifically, higher usage of IGHV4-34 correlated positively with SLEDAI-2 K scores and IgM levels, but negatively with complement C3 levels. Similarly, IGHV3-30 usage showed positive correlations with SLEDAI-2 K scores and IgM levels, alongside negative correlations with C3 levels. Usage of IGHV1-46 was positively associated with SLEDAI-2 K scores, and IgM levels, and negatively associated with C3 and C4 levels. Furthermore, analysis of VJ gene pair usage for IGHV4-34 showed that IGHJ4 was consistently the dominant partner at both time points. A decreasing trend of IGHJ5 usage was observed after treatment (Fig. 3F).

Telitacicept treatment may upregulate interferon-stimulated genes

A distinct cluster of upregulated genes (Gene set 2 mentioned in Methods) was identified (Fig. 4A). Reactome pathway enrichment analysis revealed significant activation of pathways associated with the innate immune system and interferon signaling (Fig. 4B), including “Interferon Alpha/Beta Signaling,” “Neutrophil Degranulation,” and “Cytokine Signaling in the Immune System.”

To assess the specificity of this response, we evaluated independent cohorts from the GSE224705 dataset comprising SLE patients treated with mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) or glucocorticoids plus hydroxychloroquine (GC + HCQ). The key characteristics of our discovery cohort and the GSE224705 comparator cohorts are summarised in supplementary table S4. The data suggest that post-treatment timepoints and glucocorticoid doses were reasonably comparable across the cohorts. The telitacicept-responsive gene signature was not recapitulated in either cohort (Fig. 4C, D), suggesting that this signature may be associated with telitacicept treatment, although cross-study differences limit definitive conclusions.

Interferon-based stratification showed differences in disease activity scores

Based on the longitudinal expression patterns of interferon-stimulated genes (ISGs) before and after treatment, we exploratorily stratified patients into ISG-upregulated (n = 7) and non-upregulated (n = 3) subgroups via hierarchical clustering (Euclidean distance, complete linkage; Fig. 5A). To assess the robustness of the subgroup assignment, we performed two sensitivity analyses using Ward’s linkage method and k-means clustering (k = 2), both of which yielded the same subgroup assignments as the original hierarchical clustering (Fig. S3A, B). Baseline ISG expression did not differ significantly between subgroups (Fig. 5B), indicating that the observed interferon signature was a treatment-induced response rather than a pre-existing difference.

Comparative transcriptomic analysis at baseline was performed using raw counts with DESeq2 (v1.34.0), identifying a set of DEGs that distinguished the two subgroups (threshold: P value < 0.05 and |log2 fold change| > 0.6; Fig. 5C, D). Pathway analysis of these baseline DEGs indicated that the ISG-up subgroup was characterized by pre-activation of immune pathways, including “Interferon Gamma Signaling”, while metabolic and matrix-related pathways were suppressed (Fig. 5E).

Clinically, all patients exhibited improvement in key disease activity parameters at the 3-month assessment, including reductions in SLEDAI-2 K scores and anti-dsDNA antibody levels, alongside increases in complement C3 and C4 levels (Fig. 5F). However, longitudinal follow-up over 12 months suggested possible differences in disease course between subgroups. The ISG-up subgroup achieved a more pronounced initial reduction in SLEDAI-2 K scores. Notably, some patients within this subgroup experienced a subsequent increase in SLEDAI-2 K scores after the 3-month timepoint, though this may be partially influenced by their higher baseline scores. In contrast, the ISG-not-up subgroup, while showing a more modest initial response, maintained a relatively stable disease activity score throughout the follow-up period (Fig. 5G). Given the limited sample size in this study, these observations are preliminary and highlight the need for validation in larger cohorts.

Illustration of druggable reactome pathways

As the upregulation of interferon-stimulated gene pathways was associated with disease activity scores in our cohort, we explored known drug-target interactions within these pathways (https://idg.reactome.org/PathwayBrowser/#/R-HSA-909733.6) [15]. The type I interferon (IFN α/β) signaling pathway, a key upstream regulator of ISG induction, presents multiple actionable nodes for pharmacological intervention such as janus kinase (JAK)1,2 inhibitors and tyrosine kinase (TYK)2 inhibitors (Fig. 5H). However, these represent hypothesis-generating observations that warrant further investigation rather than established therapeutic strategies.

Discussion

This study moves beyond confirming the clinical efficacy of telitacicept in SLE to suggest potential impacts on the transcriptomic landscape and B-cell immunobiology. By integrating longitudinal clinical data with paired RNA sequencing and BCR repertoire analysis, we provide a preliminary view of the drug’s mechanism of action, suggesting immune dynamics and possible molecular features of differential treatment response.

Firstly, our results are broadly consistent with telitacicept’s intended core mechanism, pointing to remodeling of the B-cell compartment. We observed what appeared to be a reduction in plasma cells, partial recovery of naive B cells, and evidence of downregulation of the PI3K-Akt and NF-κB pathways. Together with the present prospective study [16], these observations raise the possibility that telitacicept may affect the dynamics and composition of B-cell subsets in SLE, though further validation by flow cytometry or single-cell technologies is needed. BCR repertoire analysis revealed alterations in specific B-cell clones. Approximately 60% of clones expressing IGHV4-34, IGHV3-30, and IGHV1-46 were lost after treatment, while 16% were newly detected. These hypothesis-generating observations suggest that telitacicept may remodel the BCR repertoire through clonal loss and replacement, rather than simply modulating the relative abundance of persistent clones. Studies indicate skewed V(D)J gene usage in SLE compared to healthy controls [17–19]; this is evidenced by aberrant antibody repertoires, suggesting clonal expansion due to antigen selection or impaired B-cell maturation [20]. An elevated frequency of the IGHV4 gene family has been consistently reported in SLE [21]. Notably, the IGHV4-34 gene is prominently associated with autoreactivity and disease activity [20, 22]. While confirming the association of IGHV4-34 with disease activity, our research identified significant correlations involving the IGHV3-30 and IGHV1-46 genes. Furthermore, telitacicept not only reduced the overall usage of IGHV genes but also may have differentially affected specific VJ pairs derived from IGHV4‑34: IGHJ4‑paired clones remained consistently dominant, whereas IGHJ5 usage showed a trend toward contraction. Given the baseline correlation between the usage of these specific IGHV genes and higher disease activity scores, this finding suggests that telitacicept may preferentially target expanded B-cell clones expressing these genes. However, we acknowledge that these correlations are preliminary and require validation in larger cohorts with direct experimental evidence.

Another notable finding was the induction of ISG signature following telitacicept treatment, a response that was not evident in patients receiving standard-of-care therapies. Type I interferons (IFNs) act as an immunomodulatory bridge linking the innate and adaptive immune systems [23]. Dysregulation of the type I interferon pathway is a well-established hallmark of systemic lupus erythematosus [3]. An upregulation of ISGs is observed in the peripheral blood cells of individuals with SLE [24]. The ISG-high status is strongly associated with elevated disease activity and is molecularly intertwined with increased BAFF expression from myeloid cells [25]. Thus, the BAFF/APRIL axis functions not in isolation but as an integral component within a broader, interferon-driven pathogenic network [14]. There may be biological explanations for our seemingly paradoxical findings. Telitacicept exerts its therapeutic effect by disrupting the BAFF/APRIL axis [26, 27], a central signaling hub, which consequently perturbs the closely integrated immune network. While the immune system exists in a pathological homeostasis dominated by the “Interferon-BAFF-Plasma Cell” axis [14], disrupting this axis by abruptly removing BAFF could trigger a rebound of innate immunity. The subsequent ISG upregulation may reflect a complex immune system reconfiguration involving several mechanisms: (1) Release of negative feedback: Highly active B cells may exert tonic inhibition on innate immune cells like plasmacytoid dendritic cells; their abrupt removal could trigger a rebound interferon production. (2) Altered antigen clearance: Depletion of B cells, key antigen-presenting and clearing cells, may shunt autoantigens towards phagocytes, activating cGAS-STING [28] or TLR pathways [29] that directly induce ISGs. (3) Baseline hypersensitivity: In our study, patients in ISG-upregulated subgroup presented in a state of “double-high” immune activation, characterized by concurrent pre-activation of interferon-gamma signaling and suppression of metabolic pathways. Consequently, even minor fluctuations or transient rebounds in interferon levels following treatment can be amplified by the hypersensitive JAK-STAT pathway [30], potentially triggering a robust ISG transcriptional response. However, the ISG-based subgrouping was derived empirically from this single cohort and should be considered exploratory; confirmation in independent predefined cohorts is required.

The clinical implications are noteworthy and warrant further investigation. While both subgroups showed initial improvement, the ISG-up subgroup exhibited greater initial SLEDAI-2 K reduction but showed an increase in disease activity scores during long-term follow-up. We acknowledge that the ISG-up subgroup had higher baseline disease activity scores, which themselves predict subsequent increases in disease activity scores. Therefore, the observed trajectory differences may be partially explained by regression toward the mean. This preliminary observation suggests that the ISG signature may not merely be an epiphenomenon but rather may tentatively identify patients with a distinct, potentially more volatile, pathobiology, although this requires confirmation in larger cohorts. For these patients, the persistent or rebounding interferon signal might sustain inflammation and limit sustained remission. This observation generates the hypothesis that combining a JAK inhibitor might have additive or synergistic effects, potentially leading to deeper and more durable responses. We acknowledge that this is an untested, hypothesis-generating speculation; direct evidence is lacking, and future preclinical and clinical studies are required to evaluate this possibility. Given the small sample size and data-driven nature of the subgroup definition, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary. Validation in larger, independent cohorts with predefined subgroup criteria is necessary before any clinical application can be considered.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. The primary constraint is the small sample size, which limits the statistical power of our subgroup analyses and precludes formal adjustment for potential confounding effects such as changes in cell composition following treatment. Patients in this study received concomitant background therapies, which may have contributed to the observed clinical and molecular changes. Although the telitacicept-responsive gene signature was not observed in independent cohorts receiving standard-of-care therapies, we cannot exclude potential interactions between telitacicept and concomitant medications. Beyond these concerns, the cross-cohort comparison with the GSE224705 dataset is limited by differences in platform (RNA-seq vs. microarray) and experimental protocols. Standardised normalisation was therefore not feasible, and the comparison should be interpreted as qualitative and supportive rather than confirmatory. Independent validation in a prospectively collected RNA-seq cohort with standardised protocols is required. The mechanistic hypotheses regarding the ISG response, while grounded in our differential expression data, require functional validation in vitro and in vivo. Furthermore, the pattern-based classification using FPKM-normalised values is subject to the compositional limitations of FPKM. These findings should be considered exploratory and await validation in independent cohorts using standardised normalisation methods. Nevertheless, this work represents the first investigation into the mechanistic basis of telitacicept treatment for SLE at the transcriptomic and molecular levels, providing a valuable foundation for future research and therapeutic development.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this integrated analysis suggests a dual-faceted model for telitacicept’s action in SLE: it appears to influence the composition of the B-cell repertoire while simultaneously affecting the interferon-stimulated genes. These preliminary findings highlight the potential of deep molecular phenotyping to reveal the complex biology underlying treatment response and to generate hypotheses for future therapeutic strategies. However, these observations require validation in larger, independent clinical cohorts and further experimental studies to confirm their generalizability and elucidate the precise mechanistic links between B-cell targeting and interferon pathway dynamics.
