Clinical features and imaging diagnostic challenges of fetus-in-fetu associated with abdominal cryptorchidism: a case report

Background

Fetus-in-fetu (FIF) is an uncommon developmental anomaly first described more than two centuries ago. The most widely accepted embryological explanation is abnormal monozygotic diamniotic twinning, in which a malformed parasitic twin is incorporated into the body of the host twin during early embryogenesis. An alternative hypothesis regards FIF as an extremely well-differentiated teratoma; this controversy explains why careful radiological and pathological differentiation remains clinically important [1–4].

FIF is estimated to occur in approximately 1 in 500,000 live births and most commonly arises in the retroperitoneum. Other reported locations include the cranial cavity, mediastinum, sacrococcygeal region, oral cavity, scrotum and pelvis [3–6]. Genitourinary-associated FIF is particularly rare. A recent review of testicular-origin FIF reported only 9 previously published cases; older reports include FIF within or arising from an undescended testis, intraperitoneal testicular FIF, scrotal FIF, and FIF associated with ipsilateral testicular atrophy or absent testis [7–12].

The present case is clinically important for two reasons. First, the lesion was associated with an intra-abdominal undescended testis, creating a preoperative impression of cryptorchidism with a possible testicular tumor. Second, prenatal and postnatal imaging showed features overlapping with mature teratoma. We report this case according to the CARE guidelines [13] to emphasize the imaging clues, operative findings, pathological criteria and follow-up considerations for this rare condition.

Case presentation

Patient information

A male neonate aged 3 days was admitted to Quanzhou Maternity and Children’s Hospital in December 2020 because of jaundice for half a day. He was delivered after a singleton pregnancy. There was no known family history of twins, congenital anomalies or similar disease.

Clinical findings

At admission, the body temperature was 36.6 °C, pulse rate was 135 beats/min, respiratory rate was 50 breaths/min, and body weight was 3.31 kg. The infant was alert and had visible jaundice. The abdomen was mildly distended without dilated abdominal wall veins. A firm, mobile, non-tender mass of approximately 5.0 cm x 4.0 cm was palpable in the right abdomen.

The right hemiscrotum was underdeveloped and no right testis was palpable in the scrotum or inguinal region. The left testis was palpable. A cystic, transilluminating, non-tender mass was present in the left scrotum, consistent with hydrocele.

Timeline

Prenatal period: routine fetal ultrasonography detected a mixed echogenic mass in the fetal right lower abdomen, and FIF was considered. Fetal abdominal MRI suggested a cystic-solid mass in the lower right abdomen, with teratoma included in the differential diagnosis.

Day 3 after birth: the neonate was admitted for jaundice and evaluation of the abdominal mass and right nonpalpable testis.

Hospital course: postnatal abdominal CT and MRI were performed, followed by exploratory laparotomy and excision of the mass arising from the right intra-abdominal undescended testis.

Postoperative day 5: the infant was discharged without complications.

Follow-up: abdominal and scrotal ultrasonography was performed at 1 month, 6 months, 1 year and 2 years after surgery, with no recurrence or complication detected.

Diagnostic assessment

Prenatal ultrasound revealed a mixed echogenic mass in the right lower abdominal cavity and suggested FIF. Fetal abdominal MRI demonstrated a well-defined mass in the lower right abdomen measuring approximately 37.8 mm x 27.6 mm x 30.1 mm. The lesion showed heterogeneous high signal on T2-weighted imaging with internal mixed components, and teratoma was considered in the differential diagnosis.

Postnatal contrast-enhanced CT showed an oval, well-circumscribed mixed-density mass in the right middle and lower abdomen, measuring approximately 32.5 mm x 37.4 mm x 44.8 mm. The lesion contained low-density and soft-tissue-density components, with multiple patchy and stripe-like high-density calcified areas. Retrospective review of the images showed fat-containing components and linear calcified elements suggestive of an axial skeletal structure. MRI also demonstrated a cystic-solid lesion with heterogeneous internal signal. These findings raised the main differential diagnosis of FIF versus mature teratoma (Fig. 1).

Chest radiography and cardiac ultrasonography revealed no significant abnormalities. Serum AFP and HCG showed no abnormal elevation when interpreted according to neonatal age-adjusted reference ranges. The final preoperative diagnosis remained uncertain because both FIF and teratoma can present as a cystic-solid mass with fat and calcified tissue.

Therapeutic intervention

Exploratory laparotomy was performed under general anesthesia with endotracheal intubation. A 5.0 cm x 4.0 cm x 4.0 cm encapsulated cystic-solid mass was identified in the right abdominal cavity. The mass was located along the course of the right undescended testis and was continuous with atrophic right testicular and epididymal tissue. No separately viable right testicular parenchyma could be identified. The mass had no significant adhesion to surrounding organs. Because the lesion originated from the right intra-abdominal undescended testis and normal testicular tissue was not preserved, excision of the mass with the atrophic right testicular/epididymal tissue was performed rather than orchiopexy.

The vascular pedicle and spermatic cord-related structures were controlled during excision. There was no injury to adjacent bowel, bladder or major vessels. The contralateral left testis was preserved.

Pathological findings

Gross examination showed an intact capsule. After the specimen was opened, limb-like, organoid and fatty tissue structures were visible (Fig. 2). Histopathological examination demonstrated multiple differentiated tissues, including cranial/brain tissue, spinal/axial skeletal structures, intestine, esophagus, liver, bone, bone marrow, skeletal muscle, skin and skin appendages, fibrous connective tissue, ganglion tissue, and epididymal duct tissue (Fig. 3). The attached tissue was consistent with atrophic testicular and epididymal tissue.

The presence of an organized spinal/axial skeletal structure fulfilled the Willis criterion for FIF. Based on the operative and pathological findings, the final diagnosis was FIF associated with right abdominal cryptorchidism.

Follow-up and outcomes

The postoperative course was uneventful. The infant resumed feeding on postoperative day 2 and was discharged on postoperative day 5. Abdominal ultrasonography at 1 month, 6 months, 1 year and 2 years after surgery showed no residual or recurrent mass. Scrotal ultrasonography confirmed normal appearance of the left testis. No postoperative complications were observed during the 2-year follow-up period.

Patient perspective

Because the patient was a neonate, a direct patient perspective could not be obtained. The parents were satisfied with the surgical outcome and agreed to publication of this case and the accompanying anonymized images.

Discussion

Embryology and rarity

FIF is generally regarded as a malformed monozygotic twin incorporated within the host. The competing hypothesis is that FIF represents an extreme form of mature teratoma. The distinction is not merely semantic: FIF usually has a more organized body plan, while teratoma is a neoplasm with malignant potential. The present case supports the monozygotic twin hypothesis because the specimen contained a recognizable axial skeletal structure and multiple organoid tissues arranged around it.

Association with genitourinary structures is very unusual. Published reports include FIF located in a cryptorchid testis, intraperitoneal testicular FIF, FIF presenting as an abdominal mass with cryptorchidism, scrotal FIF, and cases associated with absent or atrophic ipsilateral testis [7–12]. The small number of reported cases explains why preoperative recognition remains challenging for pediatric surgeons and radiologists.

Imaging findings and diagnostic uncertainty

Imaging is essential for defining the anatomy of FIF and planning surgery. In this case, prenatal ultrasound first detected a mixed echogenic abdominal mass and suggested FIF. MRI and CT subsequently showed a cystic-solid mass containing fat-like components and multiple calcified stripe-like structures. These findings were highly relevant because the combination of fat, calcification and an organized axial skeletal component favors FIF. However, the initial diagnosis remained uncertain because mature teratoma can also contain fat and calcification.

A vertebral column or axial skeleton is regarded as a key diagnostic clue and was described in the Willis criterion. Radiologically, a vertebral axis is not always easy to identify, particularly when the mass is small, when skeletal elements are fragmented, or when the lesion is located close to bowel gas and pelvic structures. Therefore, the radiological report should specifically assess for a vertebral axis, limb buds, long bones, organoid structures, a capsule, feeding vessels and the relationship with the spermatic cord/testicular vessels.

Differential diagnosis with mature teratoma

The main differential diagnosis is mature teratoma. Both FIF and teratoma may be cystic-solid and may contain fat, calcifications and tissues from multiple germ layers. The features favoring FIF are an encapsulated mass, partial or complete skin covering, a pedicle with vascular supply, recognizable limb or organ structures, and especially a vertebral column or axial skeleton with other organs arranged around it [2, 6, 14]. Mature teratoma usually lacks a mature axial skeletal organization and is more disordered histologically.

Pathological confirmation is therefore necessary when imaging is equivocal. In the present case, the gross appearance of limb-like and internal organ-like tissues and the microscopic demonstration of spinal/axial skeletal tissue, brain, intestine, liver, bone marrow, skin appendages and other differentiated tissues confirmed FIF rather than mature teratoma.

Surgical management and prognosis

Complete excision is the treatment of choice for FIF. The operation should be planned as for a potentially complex abdominal mass: the surgeon should identify the capsule, vascular pedicle, relation to bowel and urinary tract, and relation to the spermatic cord/testicular vessels. In our patient, the lesion originated from the right intra-abdominal undescended testis and no viable right testicular parenchyma was visible; therefore, orchiectomy of the abnormal right undescended testicular tissue with complete mass excision was performed. Orchiopexy would be appropriate only if a viable separable testis could be preserved.

Although FIF is generally benign, rare malignant recurrence has been reported. Follow-up should include physical examination, abdominal ultrasonography and tumor markers when clinically indicated. Our patient remained well without recurrence during 2 years of follow-up.

Clinical significance

This case emphasizes that FIF should be considered when an infant with a nonpalpable abdominal testis has an abdominal mass containing fat and calcified skeletal-like structures. Awareness of this rare association may help avoid unexpected intraoperative findings, facilitate counseling of the family, and guide complete excision with preservation of contralateral gonadal function.

Limitations

This report describes a single case, and genetic testing to confirm monozygosity between host and FIF tissue was not performed. In addition, some imaging features suggesting a vertebral axis were recognized more clearly on retrospective review than at the time of initial diagnosis. Larger compilations of genitourinary-associated FIF are needed to define optimal imaging criteria and follow-up protocols.

Conclusions

FIF associated with abdominal cryptorchidism is exceptionally rare and can be misdiagnosed as cryptorchidism with testicular or abdominal teratoma. In a neonate with a nonpalpable testis and an abdominal cystic-solid mass, the presence of fat, calcified skeletal-like structures, a possible vertebral axis and relation to the spermatic cord/testicular vessels should prompt consideration of FIF. Complete surgical excision with pathological confirmation and long-term follow-up is recommended.
