Associations among authenticity-related strain, self-concept clarity, emotional suppression, and psychological distress in Chinese students
Authors: Jieying Sui, Ma Yue, Fang Liu, Adnan Jahangir, Abdulelah Ahmed Alghamdi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Authenticity-related strain has been increasingly recognized as a factor associated with psychological distress; however, the processes underlying this association remain insufficiently understood among university students. This study examined the direct and indirect associations between authenticity-related strain and psychological distress among Chinese university students, with self-concept clarity and emotional suppression evaluated as potential indirect pathways. A descriptive cross-sectional design was employed to collect self-reported data from full-time undergraduate and postgraduate students (N = 5,202) enrolled in accredited public and private universities across Henan Province, China. A stratified recruitment approach was used to enhance sample diversity. Data were collected through a secure online survey administered between March and April 2025. Validated scales assessed authenticity-related strain (self-alienation subscale), self-concept clarity, emotional suppression, and psychological distress (DASS-21). Structural equation modeling with bootstrapping (5,000 resamples) was used to estimate direct and indirect associations, adjusting for age and gender. Authenticity-related strain was positively associated with psychological distress (β = 0.213, p < 0.001). Both self-concept clarity (β = 0.142, 95% CI [0.091, 0.231]) and emotional suppression (β = 0.152, 95% CI [0.102, 0.243]) demonstrated significant indirect associations. A sequential pattern of indirect associations was also observed, such that authenticity-related strain was associated with lower self-concept clarity, which was associated with higher emotional suppression and greater psychological distress (β = 0.058, 95% CI [0.009, 0.111]). These indirect associations remained robust in sensitivity analyses, requiring substantial unmeasured confounding to attenuate the observed associations. The final model explained 41% of the variance in psychological distress (R2 = 0.41). This study extends current understanding of authenticity-related challenges by identifying self-concept clarity and emotional suppression as key variables associated with psychological distress among university students. The findings highlight the potential relevance of identity-related and emotion-regulation processes to student mental health and may inform future research and context-sensitive support strategies in higher education settings.

Introduction

Authenticity strain as a psychological risk factor

Authenticity—the ability to act in accordance with one’s true self—is widely recognized as a core component of psychological well-being. Theoretical perspectives such as Self-Determination Theory (SDT)1 emphasize the centrality of autonomy, asserting that when individuals cannot align their behaviors with their intrinsic values and beliefs, psychological functioning is compromised. Within this framework, authenticity strain reflects an internal psychological tension that arises when individuals feel compelled to suppress or conceal their genuine identity in response to external expectations or perceived social consequences. This strain can manifest as self-alienation—a sense of disconnection from one’s inner self—and is considered a meaningful indicator of diminished autonomy and internal coherence.

Empirical research consistently supports an association between inauthenticity and psychological distress. Lower dispositional authenticity has been associated with higher depressive symptoms and lower self-esteem among university students, particularly among those reporting limited relational support2. Similarly, authenticity-related difficulties, including self-alienation and susceptibility to external influence, have been positively associated with anxiety symptoms in cross-cultural student samples3. Evidence from medical student populations further suggests that self-alienation is associated with elevated levels of depression and stress, highlighting the psychological burden of experiencing a disconnection from one’s authentic self4. Collectively, these findings suggest that authenticity-related difficulties are consistently linked with poorer psychological well-being across diverse educational and cultural contexts. These findings converge with theoretical propositions from SDT, suggesting that when individuals lack the freedom to express their true identity, the resulting psychological friction undermines emotional well-being. In high-pressure academic environments such as Chinese universities, where conformity, performance, and social harmony are emphasized, the experience of authenticity strain may be especially pronounced. Students may feel compelled to adopt externally driven roles or suppress personal values to meet institutional or familial expectations, thereby generating internal dissonance. This conflict can accumulate over time and manifest as psychological distress, particularly when individuals perceive limited opportunities for genuine self-expression.

These theoretical and empirical perspectives suggest that authenticity-related difficulties may be closely linked with adverse psychological outcomes. Among university students, who are simultaneously navigating identity development and substantial academic and social demands, authenticity-related strain may be an important factor in emotional well-being. The relevance of this possibility is particularly evident in the context of psychological distress, a growing concern within higher education settings.

Psychological distress in University populations

The growing prevalence of psychological distress among university students has emerged as a global public health concern5, with elevated rates of anxiety, depression, and stress being consistently documented across academic environments6,7. University life presents a unique constellation of stressors, including academic demands, financial strain, interpersonal challenges, and identity development pressures, that render students particularly vulnerable to emotional dysregulation and mental health difficulties8,9. In China, the competitive nature of higher education, combined with societal expectations around academic success and filial duty, has further intensified psychological risk among students10. Recent surveys conducted across Chinese universities have reported alarming levels of psychological strain, with significant proportions of students exhibiting clinically relevant symptoms of distress11,12. While many studies have focused on external stressors, there is a growing recognition that internal psychological factors, particularly those related to identity formation and emotional authenticity, play a critical role in shaping students’ emotional well-being. This shift in focus reflects a broader movement in psychological research toward understanding how personal experiences of self-incongruence may give rise to emotional vulnerabilities, especially during the formative years of higher education.

Growing evidence suggests that internal psychological processes related to identity and self-experience may contribute to variations in student mental health. Given the theoretical relevance of authenticity-related strain and the empirical associations reported in prior research, the present study hypothesizes that authenticity-related strain is positively associated with psychological distress among university students (Hypothesis 1).

Self-concept clarity as a potential indirect pathway

A central cognitive factor through which authenticity strain may exert its psychological effects is self-concept clarity—the extent to which an individual’s beliefs about themselves are clearly defined, internally consistent, and stable over time13. When individuals experience internal conflict about expressing their true identity, this incongruence may disrupt the consolidation of a coherent self-concept. Theoretically, Identity Theory14 posits that stable self-definitions are critical for adaptive functioning; when individuals are unable to consistently enact identity-relevant roles, they are more likely to experience psychological strain. In parallel, the Self-Concept Clarity Framework suggests that identity confusion weakens one’s ability to regulate emotions and to cope effectively with stressors15.

Empirical evidence supports the role of self-concept clarity in psychological adjustment and suggests that it may represent an important mechanism linking authenticity-related processes with psychological distress. Lower self-concept clarity has been associated with elevated depressive symptoms, reduced emotional competence, and poorer psychological functioning among student populations16. This further indicates that the association between self-concept clarity and depressive symptoms may operate through broader psychological processes, including meaning in life and emotion regulation16. Conversely, higher self-concept clarity has been linked to greater subjective well-being and lower levels of emotional distress among Chinese adolescents and young adults, highlighting its potential protective role in psychological adjustment17. Additional evidence suggests that self-concept is an important predictor of mental health among Chinese college students, with social adaptation representing one potential explanatory pathway18. Collectively, these findings suggest that diminished self-concept clarity may undermine emotional resilience and increase vulnerability to psychological distress. These findings reinforce the interpretation that diminished self-concept clarity undermines emotional resilience and increases vulnerability to psychological strain, particularly in academic contexts characterized by high social and identity demands. In educational and sociocultural environments characterized by high expectations and normative conformity, such as those in Chinese universities, students may find it especially difficult to develop a stable sense of self. The pressure to meet external academic or familial standards may require them to prioritize socially sanctioned behaviors over personal authenticity, contributing to an unstable or conflicted self-view. This fragmentation of self-concept may impair their ability to interpret emotions, assert personal needs, or maintain psychological boundaries, thereby elevating distress levels. Given these theoretical and empirical foundations, the current study hypothesizes that self-concept clarity mediates the relationship between authenticity-related strain and psychological distress (Hypothesis 2).

Emotional suppression as a potential indirect pathway

Beyond cognitive disruption, authenticity strain may also activate maladaptive emotional regulation processes—most notably, emotional suppression, or the deliberate inhibition of emotional expression in social contexts. According to Emotion Regulation Theory19, suppression is a response-focused strategy that operates after an emotion has been activated, aiming to inhibit its external display. While such regulation may offer short-term social benefits, it often increases physiological arousal, reduces positive emotional experience, and long-term psychological costs. When individuals experience authenticity strain, feeling that their true thoughts, feelings, or identities are unacceptable, they may increasingly rely on suppression to conform to perceived social expectations, particularly in environments that discourage emotional expressiveness.

Empirical studies provide compelling support for the role of suppression in psychological distress, particularly within collectivist cultural settings such as China, where interpersonal harmony is often prioritized over individual emotional expression. Greater reliance on expressive suppression has been associated with heightened negative affect and lower perceived social support among Chinese young adults20. Similarly, suppression has been linked directly to depressive symptoms and indirectly to psychological distress through increased social anxiety and loneliness among Chinese adolescents21. These findings suggest that habitual suppression may contribute to poorer psychological functioning by limiting adaptive emotional processing and weakening interpersonal resources. These findings are consistent with prior research showing that habitual suppression weakens emotional clarity and contributes to emotional exhaustion, ultimately increasing risk for depression, anxiety, and stress22,23. The cultural context further compounds these effects. In many East Asian societies, including China, the suppression of negative emotion is not only tolerated but socially reinforced, particularly among students navigating hierarchical academic environments24. This normative endorsement of emotional restraint can make suppression appear adaptive on the surface, yet it may mask chronic emotional disengagement and intensify internal conflict when enacted habitually under authenticity strain25. Students who feel disconnected from their true selves may thus adopt suppression as a compensatory mechanism, which, over time, undermines psychological well-being. Based on this theoretical and empirical grounding, the present study hypothesizes that emotional suppression mediates the relationship between authenticity strain and psychological distress (Hypothesis 3).

A proposed cognitive–emotional indirect association framework

While prior research has independently linked authenticity strain to self-concept disturbance and maladaptive emotion regulation, the present study advances existing models by testing a sequential mediation framework in which authenticity strain first disrupts self-concept clarity, thereby fostering greater emotional suppression and ultimately leading to psychological distress. This hypothesized pathway is theoretically consistent with dual-process perspectives in psychology, which suggest that cognitive structures (e.g., identity clarity) provide a foundation for emotion regulation processes26. Specifically, when individuals lack a coherent self-concept, they may be less able to interpret their emotional experiences, articulate their personal needs, or select context-appropriate emotion regulation strategies, thereby increasing their reliance on suppression. Empirical evidence supports this proposed ordering. Lower self-concept clarity has been associated with heightened psychological distress and a greater tendency to rely on suppression-based coping strategies, suggesting that impaired identity structure may have downstream consequences for emotional regulation27. Similarly, fragmented self-concept has been linked to lower emotional awareness and a stronger preference for inhibitory emotional regulation strategies among Chinese college students28. Collectively, these findings suggest that self-concept clarity may play an important role in shaping how individuals regulate emotional experiences, providing empirical support for a sequential pathway linking authenticity-related difficulties, emotional suppression, and psychological distress.

These findings indicate that clarity of self-knowledge is a prerequisite for effective emotional self-management, a relationship particularly salient in cultural contexts that discourage overt emotional expression. Furthermore, self-concept clarity and emotional suppression are functionally distinct but theoretically aligned constructs. Self-concept clarity operates at the intrapersonal level—governing how coherently one understands the self—while suppression reflects an interpersonal regulation strategy shaped by social context. Authenticity strain, by introducing cognitive dissonance and internal self-conflict, can compromise the coherence of the self-schema. This cognitive fragmentation, in turn, limits the individual’s capacity to engage in emotionally congruent expression, prompting habitual suppression. Over time, this dual disruption of identity and emotional communication can heighten psychological distress, especially in high-pressure educational systems where emotional needs are often subordinated to performance norms. Thus, the proposed sequential pathway is not merely statistical but conceptually grounded: self-concept clarity functions as a structural prerequisite for adaptive emotion regulation. Based on this theoretical rationale and supporting empirical evidence, the current study hypothesizes that self-concept clarity and emotional suppression sequentially mediate the relationship between authenticity strain and psychological distress (Hypothesis 4).

This study

Despite growing interest in the psychological implications of authenticity, the literature remains limited in several important respects. First, prior research has often examined authenticity, self-concept clarity, and emotion regulation as separate constructs, thereby limiting understanding of how these variables may relate jointly to psychological distress. While authenticity has been consistently associated with well-being and distress outcomes, fewer studies have examined the processes by which authenticity strain is linked to these outcomes, particularly via integrated cognitive and emotional pathways. Second, although self-concept clarity and emotional suppression have each been associated with mental health, their potential interrelationship as part of a sequential pattern has received relatively limited empirical attention. This is notable given theoretical perspectives from identity and self-regulation frameworks, which suggest that challenges in maintaining a coherent sense of self may be related to the ways individuals regulate their emotional experiences. Third, existing research has often been constrained by methodological limitations, including modest sample sizes, limited sampling diversity, and insufficient attention to construct validity within culturally specific student populations. These limitations highlight the value of large-scale, theory-informed studies that examine how identity-related and emotion-regulation processes jointly associate with psychological distress in academic contexts. In addition, authenticity strain remains relatively underexplored within higher education research, despite its conceptual relevance during emerging adulthood, a developmental period characterized by identity formation and increasing social complexity. The present study addresses these gaps by examining a hypothesized indirect association model in which self-concept clarity and emotional suppression mediate the relationship between authenticity strain and psychological distress. Drawing on self-determination theory, identity theory, and emotion regulation theory, the study offers a theoretically integrated perspective on how authenticity-related challenges may be linked with student mental health through identifiable psychological processes. By using a stratified recruitment approach and a large, demographically diverse student sample, the study enhances the contextual relevance of its findings. Overall, this research contributes to a more nuanced understanding of student well-being by highlighting the interplay between identity-related and emotion-regulation processes and may inform future research and context-sensitive support strategies in higher education settings.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This study employed a descriptive cross-sectional design to collect self-reported data from university students using an online survey administered between March and April 2025. The design was selected for its suitability in capturing a large and diverse sample within a limited time frame, enabling the efficient assessment of psychological constructs at a single point in time.

The study targeted undergraduate and postgraduate students enrolled in accredited public and private universities across Henan Province, China. Formal invitations were extended to recognized universities through coordinated engagement with institutional authorities to facilitate broad institutional participation. Participant recruitment was conducted through multiple channels, including student affairs offices, official university mailing lists, academic departments, and verified institutional social media platforms (e.g., WeChat official accounts). This multi-channel recruitment strategy enabled broad coverage across institutions, academic disciplines, and geographic regions, resulting in a diverse, heterogeneous student sample within the Chinese higher education system.

Sampling approach

A stratified recruitment approach was used to enhance diversity across key demographic and academic characteristics, including institution type, academic discipline, and gender. Rather than employing a probability-based stratified sampling framework, the study utilized broad recruitment across participating universities and student groups to encourage participation from diverse segments of the student population. Institutions varied in geographic location, administrative structure, and academic orientation, while academic disciplines were categorized into five broad domains (STEM and Health Sciences, Social Sciences and Education, Humanities and Arts, Business and Law, and Other disciplines) to capture variation in student experiences. Gender distribution was also considered during recruitment to reflect known differences in psychological outcomes. This approach was intended to enhance sample heterogeneity and improve coverage across the higher education context of Henan Province; however, because participation was voluntary and recruitment was not based on random selection, the sample should not be considered statistically representative of all university students in China. Accordingly, the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution regarding generalizability beyond the study population.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected using a secure web-based survey platform. Participants accessed the questionnaire through a shared institutional link and were first presented with an informed consent form. Upon providing electronic consent, participants completed demographic questions followed by standardized psychological scales measuring the constructs of interest. All survey instruments were administered in Simplified Chinese using previously validated Chinese versions of each scale, as reported in prior peer-reviewed studies. No additional modifications or translations were made, ensuring linguistic and cultural appropriateness while maintaining measurement validity. To ensure data quality, responses were monitored for completion time and response patterns. Submissions with excessively short completion times or uniform response patterns (e.g., identical responses across items) were flagged as potentially inattentive and excluded from analysis. Additionally, the survey platform was configured to limit multiple submissions from the same device where feasible.

Eligibility criteria

Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were enrolled as full-time undergraduate or postgraduate students at an accredited public or private university in Henan Province, China. They demonstrated sufficient proficiency in Simplified Chinese to comprehend the survey content. No minimum age restriction was applied, as it is not uncommon for first-year undergraduate students in the Chinese education system to be under 18. Participants who self-reported a clinically diagnosed mental health disorder or current use of psychiatric medication were excluded to reduce potential confounding effects on psychological distress outcomes. Although this information was self-reported and could not be independently verified, it was included as a screening criterion to improve the sample’s internal consistency. The potential for reporting bias is acknowledged as a limitation. Additionally, international or exchange students were excluded to ensure cultural and educational homogeneity within the sample. Finally, responses were excluded from analysis if they contained more than 10% missing data on any of the core study variables to preserve data quality and the validity of the statistical analyses.

Sample size adequacy

Sample size adequacy was evaluated according to established recommendations for structural equation modeling (SEM) and mediation analysis, in conjunction with an a priori power analysis conducted with G*Power 3.129,30. Assuming a significance level of α = 0.05, statistical power of 0.95, and a model with three predictors, a minimum sample size of approximately 600 participants was required to detect small-to-medium effects. The final analytic sample consisted of 5,202 participants, substantially exceeding these requirements and ensuring sufficient statistical power for stable parameter estimation, path analysis, and mediation testing.

Data screening and missing data handling

A total of 5,659 responses were initially collected. Following data screening, 457 responses were excluded due to one or more of the following criteria: (a) exceeding 10% missing data on key study variables, (b) failure to meet eligibility criteria, or (c) response patterns indicative of inattentive or disengaged participation (e.g., straight lining or implausibly rapid completion). The final analytic sample comprised 5,202 participants. Item-level missingness was subsequently assessed. Item-level missingness was low across all study variables (less than 5%). Given the very small proportion of missing responses and the large sample size, mean imputation was applied at the item level within each scale to retain valid cases and preserve statistical power. Although more advanced approaches, such as Full Information Maximum Likelihood (FIML) or Multiple Imputation, may offer methodological advantages under conditions of greater missingness, simulation studies suggest that simple imputation procedures are unlikely to meaningfully influence parameter estimates when the proportion of missing data is minimal. Nevertheless, the use of mean imputation represents a methodological limitation and should be considered when interpreting the findings. The overall study procedure is summarized in Fig. 1, which outlines the recruitment strategy, participant screening process, survey administration, data cleaning procedures, and derivation of the final analytic sample.

Ethical protocols

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Xinxiang Institute of Engineering, China (Approval No. RS-9047). All participants provided informed electronic consent prior to participation. For participants under 18, written permission was obtained from a parent or legal guardian in accordance with national research ethics guidelines. Participants were assured of the anonymity and confidentiality of their responses and were informed of their right to withdraw from the study at any time without penalty.

Measures

Sociodemographic variables

Participants’ sociodemographic information, including their age, sex, educational level, academic discipline, primary source of financial support, and current living status, was obtained. A total of 5,202 students participated in the study. Of these, 54.67% were female, and 45.33% were male. The majority of participants were undergraduate students (68.32%), while 31.68% were enrolled in postgraduate programs. Participants represented diverse academic disciplines, including 32.67%, 35.55%, 19.67%, 6.71%, and 5.4%. Most students were enrolled in public universities (62.33%), whereas 37.67%% attended private institutions. Regarding financial support, 65.29% reported family support as their primary source of funding, followed by scholarships (11.32%) and self-support or other sources (23.39%). In terms of living arrangements, 73.21%% resided in university dormitories, 20.13% lived with family members, and 6.66% reported other living situations.

Authenticity strain

Authenticity strain was assessed using the self-alienation subscale of the Authenticity Scale developed by Wood et al. (2008)31, which was validated for use with Chinese populations by Xia, Lv, and Xu (2022)32. The self-alienation subscale comprises four items that assess the extent to which individuals experience disconnection from their true selves. Participants responded using a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (does not describe me at all) to 7 (describes me very well), with higher scores indicating greater self-alienation. The self-alienation dimension was selected because it captures the internal self-disconnection and psychological incongruence that are conceptually central to authenticity-related strain. Previous research has similarly examined self-alienation as a distinct authenticity-related construct associated with maladaptive psychological functioning and emotional well-being33,34. The Chinese version of the Authenticity Scale, including the self-alienation subscale, has demonstrated satisfactory psychometric properties among college and community samples in China3,32.

Self-concept clarity

Self-concept clarity was assessed using the Self-Concept Clarity Scale (SCCS) developed by Campbell et al. (1996)13, which has been adopted and validated across various Chinese student populations35–37. This scale comprises 12 items that evaluate the extent to which individuals possess a clearly defined, internally consistent, and stable sense of self (e.g., “In general, I have a clear sense of who I am and what I am”). Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). After reverse-scoring these items, a composite score was calculated by averaging the 12 items, with higher scores indicating greater self-concept clarity. The SCCS has demonstrated strong construct validity and internal consistency in studies involving university students35,38,39.

Emotional suppression

Emotional suppression was assessed using the Expressive Suppression subscale of the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ), initially developed by Gross and John (2003)40 and subsequently translated and validated for use in Chinese populations by Wang et al. (2007)41. This subscale includes four items that evaluate an individual’s habitual tendency to inhibit the outward expression of their emotions. An example item is: “I control my emotions by not expressing them.” Participants rated their agreement with each item using a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). Higher scores indicate greater reliance on emotional suppression as an emotion regulation strategy. The Chinese version of the ERQ has demonstrated good reliability and validity across various student populations42–44, making it suitable for use in the present study context. Previous studies have shown that expressive suppression, as measured by the ERQ, is a reliable predictor of psychological functioning across cultural settings22,45.

Psychological distress

Psychological distress was measured using the Depression Anxiety Stress Scales – 21 items (DASS-21) developed by Lovibond and Lovibond (1995)46 and validated by Gong et al. (2010)47. This self-reported scale assesses the frequency and severity of three core emotional states over the past week: depression, anxiety, and stress. Each subscale contains seven items, rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (did not apply to me at all) to 3 (applied to me very much or most of the time). In this study, all 21 items were summed to yield an overall measure of psychological distress, with higher scores indicating greater emotional burden. The DASS-21 has been widely validated and adopted across various populations, including Chinese48–51.

Confounding variables

Age and gender were included as covariates in the analysis due to their potential influence on emotional regulation and psychological distress. These variables were statistically controlled to reduce confounding effects in the mediation model.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 28.0 and AMOS 24.0. A significance level of p < 0.05 was set for all inferential tests. Prior to testing the hypothesized model, several preparatory analyses were conducted to ensure the dataset’s suitability and integrity. To evaluate potential Common Method Bias (CMB) due to the self-report nature of the data, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted as an initial diagnostic. In addition, a common latent factor approach was applied during confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) to assess whether a latent method factor accounted for substantial shared variance across indicators. This dual strategy was employed to increase confidence in the distinctiveness of the measured constructs. The assumptions underlying multivariate analysis were assessed using diagnostic checks. The normality of the variable distributions was assessed using skewness and kurtosis. Homoscedasticity was evaluated by inspecting residual scatterplots for randomness and equal variance. Multicollinearity among predictor variables was assessed using Variance Inflation Factors (VIFs) to ensure that no predictors exhibited excessive interdependence. To establish the measurement model’s psychometric quality, reliability, and validity analyses were conducted. Internal consistency reliability was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha and composite reliability. Convergent validity was assessed through average variance extracted (AVE), while discriminant validity was examined using the Fornell–Larcker criterion. A confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) was conducted to assess the adequacy of the measurement model before testing the structural model. Model fit was evaluated using multiple fit indices, including the chi-square to degrees of freedom ratio, Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker–Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), and Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). Further, to test the hypothesized sequential mediation model, structural equation modeling (SEM) was employed. Confidence intervals were generated to evaluate hypothesized indirect associations involving self-concept clarity and emotional suppression. Confidence intervals were generated to determine the significance of mediation pathways involving self-concept clarity and emotional suppression.

Results

Common method bias

Two diagnostic procedures were employed to assess the potential impact of CMB, given the exclusive use of self-report measures. First, Harman’s single-factor test was conducted through exploratory factor analysis. The unrotated factor solution indicated that the first factor accounted for 30.19% of the total variance, which is below the commonly accepted threshold of 40%. This suggested that no single factor dominated the data and that common method variance was unlikely to be a substantial concern. Second, a common latent factor (CLF) approach was applied within the CFA framework. A latent method factor was introduced, and all observed items were allowed to load onto both their theoretical constructs and the method factor. The standardized regression weights with and without the CLF were compared, and the differences were negligible. The results of both Harman’s single-factor test and the CLF procedure suggested that common method variance was unlikely to fully account for the observed relationships among the study variables. Nevertheless, because all constructs were assessed using self-report measures collected at a single time point, the possibility of residual CMB cannot be entirely ruled out. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Preliminary diagnostic analysis

Prior to hypothesis testing, the dataset was examined for compliance with key statistical assumptions (Table 1). Normality was assessed by examining the skewness and kurtosis values for all continuous variables. The values fell within the acceptable range of ± 2, indicating that the data were approximately normally distributed and suitable for multivariate analysis. Homoscedasticity was evaluated through visual inspection of residual scatterplots generated from regression analyses. The plots showed a random, evenly dispersed pattern, suggesting that the assumption of constant variance was not violated. To assess multicollinearity, VIFs were computed for all predictor variables. All VIF values were well below the commonly accepted threshold of 5, indicating no problematic multicollinearity among the independent variables.

Reliability and validity

Table 2 presents the results of the reliability and convergent validity analyses for the study constructs. All constructs demonstrated high internal consistency, with Cronbach’s alpha (α) values ranging from 0.87 to 0.90, exceeding the commonly accepted threshold of 0.70. Composite reliability values also ranged from 0.89 to 0.91, further confirming that each construct exhibited strong internal consistency. In terms of convergent validity, all constructs had AVE values above the recommended threshold of 0.50. Specifically, AVE values ranged from 0.598 to 0.675, indicating that the respective latent constructs accounted for a substantial proportion of the variance in the observed indicators. These results collectively confirm that all four constructs demonstrated acceptable reliability and convergent validity, supporting their adequacy for inclusion in the measurement and structural models.

Inter-construct correlations and discriminant validity

The bivariate correlations and discriminant validity among the four core constructs were assessed using the Fornell–Larcker criterion. As shown in Table 3, authenticity strain demonstrated a moderate negative correlation with self-concept clarity (r = − 0.501, p < 0.001), indicating that higher levels of authenticity strain were associated with lower self-concept clarity. It was also moderately and positively correlated with emotional suppression (r = 0.432, p < 0.001) and psychological distress (r = 0.452, p < 0.001), suggesting that higher authenticity strain was associated with greater emotional suppression and psychological distress. Similarly, self-concept clarity showed a moderate negative correlation with emotional suppression (r = − 0.434, p < 0.001) and a moderate-to-strong negative correlation with psychological distress (r = − 0.471, p < 0.001), indicating that lower self-concept clarity was associated with higher emotional suppression and psychological distress. The association between emotional suppression and psychological distress was moderate and positive (r = 0.492, p < 0.001), suggesting that higher levels of emotional suppression were associated with higher levels of psychological distress. In addition, discriminant validity was supported for all constructs, as the square root of the AVE for each variable exceeded the absolute value of its correlations with the other constructs. These results confirm that each construct was empirically distinct, supporting the validity of the measurement model.

The measurement model with four latent constructs was assessed using CFA (Table 4). Model fit was evaluated using multiple indices, and the overall results indicated an acceptable to good fit. The χ²/df was 1.96, which fell below the commonly accepted threshold of 3.00, suggesting adequate fit. The CFI was 0.94, and the TLI was 0.93, indicating a good fit for the model. Direct and indirect path analysis

The structural model examined the hypothesized indirect associations involving self-concept clarity and emotional suppression in the association between authenticity strain and psychological distress (Table 5). The model demonstrated substantial explanatory power, accounting for 41% of the variance in psychological distress (R2 = 0.41, F = 1123.73, p < 0.001). All hypothesized direct paths were statistically significant. Authenticity strain was positively associated with psychological distress (β = 0.213, p < 0.001), indicating that higher levels of authenticity strain were associated with greater psychological distress. It was also negatively associated with self-concept clarity (β = −0.401, p < 0.001), suggesting that higher authenticity strain was linked to lower self-concept clarity, and positively associated with emotional suppression (β = 0.385, p < 0.001), indicating a relationship with higher levels of emotional suppression. Furthermore, self-concept clarity was negatively associated with psychological distress (β = −0.353, p < 0.001), whereas emotional suppression was positively associated with psychological distress (β = 0.396, p < 0.001). Bootstrapping analysis (5,000 resamples) indicated statistically significant indirect effects. The indirect path from authenticity strain to psychological distress via self-concept clarity was significant (β = 0.142, 95% CI [0.091, 0.231]), supporting an indirect association through self-concept clarity. The indirect effect via emotional suppression was also significant (β = 0.152, 95% CI [0.102, 0.243]), indicating that emotional suppression represents an additional indirect pathway linking authenticity strain and psychological distress.

The sequential indirect pathway through both self-concept clarity and emotional suppression was also significant (β = 0.058, 95% CI [0.009, 0.111]), suggesting a pattern of associations in which authenticity strain was related to lower self-concept clarity, which was associated with higher emotional suppression, with both variables being associated with higher psychological distress. Overall, these findings are consistent with the hypothesized pattern of indirect associations involving self-concept clarity and emotional suppression (Fig. 2).

Sensitivity analysis

To assess the robustness of the observed mediation effects to potential unmeasured confounding, a post hoc sensitivity analysis was conducted using the approach proposed by Imai, Keele, and Yamamoto (2010)52. This method evaluates the degree to which an unmeasured confounder must influence the mediator and the outcome to nullify the estimated Average Causal Mediation Effects (ACMEs). The analysis was performed using the mediation package in R with 5,000 nonparametric bootstrap resamples. Results indicated that the ACME for both mediators—self-concept clarity and emotional suppression- remained statistically significant unless a hypothetical unmeasured confounder explained more than 29% (R² = 0.29) of the residual variance in both the mediator and the outcome. This threshold suggested that the mediation effects were reasonably robust to omitted variable bias. The sensitivity plots further illustrated that a relatively strong unmeasured confounder would be required to reduce the indirect effects to non-significance, supporting the stability of the mediation findings (Fig. 3).

Discussion

The present study aimed to examine the psychological correlates of authenticity strain among university students in Henan Province, with a particular focus on the sequential roles of self-concept clarity and emotional suppression in relation to psychological distress. Grounded in contemporary identity and emotion regulation theories, the study proposed a dual-path indirect-association framework that captures both cognitive and emotional self-regulatory processes. The research contributes to the literature by examining a hypothesized sequential indirect association model within a student population drawn from a broad range of universities across Henan, thereby strengthening the contextual relevance of the findings within educational psychology. The results indicated a significant direct association between authenticity strain and psychological distress, such that higher levels of authenticity strain were associated with higher levels of distress. In addition, two distinct indirect pathways were observed: one involving self-concept clarity and another involving emotional suppression. Specifically, a higher authenticity strain was associated with lower self-concept clarity and with higher emotional suppression. Both variables, in turn, were associated with psychological distress. Furthermore, the findings were consistent with a hypothesized sequential pattern of indirect associations, in which authenticity strain was associated with lower self-concept clarity, which was subsequently associated with higher emotional suppression and, in turn, with higher psychological distress. Overall, these findings are consistent with the proposed pattern of indirect associations and highlight potential cognitive and emotional pathways through which authenticity strain may be related to student well-being.

The present study identified a significant positive association between authenticity strain and psychological distress, supporting the first hypothesis. This finding suggests that authenticity-related internal conflict is closely linked with elevated psychological distress among university students. More broadly, this pattern aligns with a substantial body of literature indicating that disruptions in authentic self-expression are associated with poorer psychological outcomes in academic settings. Prior research has consistently shown that lower levels of authenticity, as well as heightened self-alienation and susceptibility to external influence, are associated with increased symptoms of depression, anxiety, and stress among student populations2,4. Importantly, these associations appear to extend across cultural contexts. Evidence from both collectivist and individualist settings suggests that authenticity is positively linked with well-being indicators such as life satisfaction and negatively associated with emotional exhaustion and distress3,33. Taken together, the current findings reinforce the view that authenticity-related processes represent a robust psychological correlate of distress across diverse student populations. From a theoretical perspective, these results are consistent with SDT53, which emphasizes the importance of autonomy for psychological well-being. Authenticity strain may reflect challenges in maintaining a sense of autonomy when individuals perceive pressure to conform to external expectations. In cultural contexts such as China, where social harmony and relational obligations are highly valued, students may experience heightened tension between external expectations and internal self-congruence. Such tensions may, in turn, be associated with increased psychological distress.

Consistent with Hypothesis 2, the analysis indicated that authenticity strain was associated with lower self-concept clarity, which was, in turn, associated with higher psychological distress. This pattern suggests that difficulties in maintaining an authentic self may be linked with a less coherent and stable self-concept, which is further associated with elevated emotional distress among students. This finding aligns with a growing body of literature indicating that self-concept clarity is closely associated with psychological well-being. Prior research has consistently shown that lower self-concept clarity is linked with higher levels of perceived stress, depressive symptoms, and anxiety across student and emerging adult populations54. Evidence from Chinese samples similarly demonstrates that higher self-concept clarity is associated with more favorable mental health outcomes, including lower levels of depression and anxiety, suggesting that this relationship is observable across cultural contexts55. From a theoretical perspective, identity theory and self-concept clarity frameworks emphasize the importance of a coherent and stable self-view for psychological functioning. Within this context, authenticity strain may reflect challenges in maintaining self-consistency when individuals perceive pressure to conform to external expectations. In collectivist cultural settings such as China, where identity development is often shaped by relational and social norms, such challenges may be more pronounced, potentially corresponding with lower self-concept clarity and higher psychological distress. These findings may have implications for student well-being initiatives. Approaches designed to promote self-reflection and self-understanding, such as values clarification or reflective writing exercises, may warrant further investigation as strategies to support students experiencing authenticity-related challenges. However, longitudinal and experimental research is needed to clarify their effectiveness and the directionality of the observed associations.

Consistent with Hypothesis 3, the results indicated that authenticity strain was positively associated with emotional suppression, which was, in turn, associated with higher psychological distress. This pattern suggests that authenticity-related challenges may be linked with greater reliance on suppression-based emotion regulation, which is further associated with elevated distress. This finding aligns with a growing body of empirical literature demonstrating that expressive suppression is consistently associated with poorer psychological outcomes across student populations. Evidence from Chinese samples indicates that higher levels of suppression are linked with increased negative affect, depressive symptoms, and reduced perceived social support, even after accounting for culturally relevant cognitive styles such as Zhong Yong thinking20,21. Cross-cultural research further supports these associations, showing that greater reliance on suppression is positively related to depression, although individual differences in suppression capacity may moderate these relationships56. Additional research suggests that suppression is closely linked with other maladaptive cognitive processes, such as rumination, particularly brooding tendencies, which are themselves associated with psychological distress57. Taken together, these findings indicate that suppression may co-occur with other regulatory difficulties, forming part of a broader pattern of emotional and cognitive processes associated with distress. From a theoretical perspective, these results are consistent with emotion regulation theory, which conceptualizes expressive suppression as a response-focused strategy that alters outward emotional expression19. While suppression may serve short-term social or interpersonal functions, frequent reliance on this strategy has been associated with less adaptive emotional processing and poorer psychological outcomes. In collectivist cultural contexts such as China, suppression may be socially normative; however, sustained reliance on suppression may be associated with increased emotional strain and reduced well-being. Approaches that encourage emotional awareness and adaptive emotional expression, such as expressive writing or guided emotional reflection, may warrant further investigation as potentially relevant strategies for students experiencing authenticity-related challenges. However, longitudinal and experimental research is needed to clarify their effectiveness and the directionality of the observed associations.

In line with Hypothesis 4, the results were consistent with a hypothesized sequential pattern of associations in which authenticity strain was associated with lower self-concept clarity, which was subsequently associated with higher emotional suppression and, in turn, with higher psychological distress. This pattern suggests a chain of associations linking authenticity-related challenges with both cognitive and emotional regulatory processes. This interpretation is supported by prior research indicating that lower self-concept clarity is associated with less adaptive emotion regulation and poorer psychological adjustment. For example, studies among Chinese adolescents have shown that reduced self-concept clarity is linked with greater emotional dysregulation and higher levels of depressive symptoms and stress58. Self-concept clarity has also been negatively associated with depressive symptoms among nursing students, with emotion regulation capacity influencing the strength of this relationship16. Taken together, these findings suggest that self-concept clarity and emotion regulation are closely interconnected and jointly associated with psychological well-being. From a theoretical perspective, these findings are consistent with the self-concept clarity framework13 and identity theory14, which emphasize the importance of a stable and coherent sense of self for effective psychological functioning. Within this context, authenticity strain may reflect challenges in maintaining self-consistency, which may be associated with greater reliance on certain emotion regulation strategies, such as suppression. Emotion regulation theory further conceptualizes suppression as a response-focused strategy that modifies emotional expression, which, when used frequently, has been associated with less adaptive psychological outcomes19. In the Chinese cultural context, individuals may be more inclined to regulate emotional expression in socially appropriate ways. While such strategies may serve interpersonal functions, sustained reliance on suppression may be associated with increased psychological strain. Approaches that support self-reflection and adaptive emotion regulation may warrant further investigation as potentially relevant strategies for students experiencing authenticity-related challenges. However, longitudinal and experimental research is needed to clarify the directionality of the observed associations and to further examine these relationships.

Although the large sample size increased statistical power and facilitated the detection of statistically significant associations, the observed effects were not only statistically significant but also of meaningful magnitude. The standardized path coefficients linking authenticity strain with self-concept clarity, emotional suppression, and psychological distress, as well as the associations of self-concept clarity and emotional suppression with psychological distress, generally fell within the small-to-moderate or moderate effect-size range. Furthermore, the model accounted for 41% of the variance in psychological distress, indicating substantial explanatory value. These findings suggest that the observed associations are not only statistically reliable but also potentially meaningful from psychological and practical perspectives among university student populations.

Study implications

The findings of this study offer context-specific insights into psychological support practices for university students in China, particularly those experiencing tensions related to authenticity within academically and socially structured environments. By demonstrating that authenticity strain is associated with psychological distress alongside lower self-concept clarity and higher emotional suppression, the study highlights specific psychological dimensions that may be relevant targets for student support initiatives. Rather than addressing distress broadly, interventions may benefit from focusing on identity-related processes, such as helping students clarify personal values, goals, and self-perceptions, while also promoting more adaptive forms of emotional expression. For example, structured reflection-based programs that encourage students to articulate personal beliefs and reconcile them with social expectations may be particularly relevant in collectivist contexts where identity negotiation is shaped by relational norms. Similarly, interventions that move beyond suppression-based regulation—such as cognitive reappraisal or guided emotional processing—may be worth considering in student support services. Importantly, these implications should be interpreted cautiously, given the cross-sectional nature of the data. However, the observed pattern of associations suggests that addressing both cognitive (self-concept clarity) and emotional (suppression) processes may be relevant when designing contextually sensitive mental health support strategies for university populations. Future research using longitudinal or experimental designs is needed to further clarify the directionality and potential causal pathways among these variables.

Study limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the cross-sectional design precludes conclusions regarding temporal ordering, causality, and the direction of the observed associations among authenticity-related strain, self-concept clarity, emotional suppression, and psychological distress. Although the proposed framework was theoretically informed by self-determination theory, identity theory, and emotion regulation theory, the findings should be interpreted as patterns of association rather than evidence of causal processes. Moreover, the study evaluated a hypothesized indirect association model without formally comparing alternative structural configurations. Other theoretically plausible explanations may account for the observed relationships. For example, lower self-concept clarity may be associated with greater authenticity-related difficulties, or psychological distress may itself influence perceptions of authenticity and emotion regulation processes. Future longitudinal, experimental, and model-comparison studies are needed to clarify temporal ordering, examine potential bidirectional relationships, and evaluate competing theoretical explanations. Second, all variables were assessed using self-report instruments administered during a single survey session. Although procedural safeguards, including anonymous participation, were implemented to reduce potential bias, self-report measures remain vulnerable to response-related influences such as social desirability and common method variance. Furthermore, while statistical analyses suggested that common method variance was unlikely to fully account for the observed associations, residual method-related bias cannot be entirely excluded. Future research would benefit from incorporating multi-method approaches, including behavioral, observational, or informant-based measures, to strengthen methodological rigor. Third, although item-level missing data were minimal, mean imputation was used to retain valid cases and preserve statistical power. While this approach is unlikely to have materially influenced the findings, given the low proportion of missingness, future studies may benefit from employing more advanced missing-data techniques, such as multiple imputation or full information maximum likelihood estimation. Fourth, the sample was drawn exclusively from university students in Henan Province, China, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions, cultural contexts, and non-student populations. Differences in educational environments, sociocultural norms, and demographic characteristics may influence the observed associations. In addition, authenticity-related strain was operationalized using the Authenticity Scale’s self-alienation dimension rather than a multidimensional assessment of authenticity. Consequently, the findings primarily reflect the role of self-alienation as a specific aspect of authenticity-related difficulties and may not generalize to other dimensions of authenticity, such as authentic living or accepting external influence. Finally, although the study focused on theoretically relevant cognitive and emotional processes, other factors—including social support, personality characteristics, and coping styles—were not examined and may contribute to a more comprehensive understanding of authenticity-related strain and psychological distress.

Conclusion

This study examined the associations between authenticity strain and psychological distress among university students in Henan Province, with particular attention to the roles of self-concept clarity and emotional suppression. The findings indicated that authenticity strain was associated with lower self-concept clarity and higher emotional suppression, both of which were, in turn, associated with greater psychological distress. Although the cross-sectional design precludes causal inferences, the results contribute to a growing body of literature by clarifying potential cognitive and emotional processes through which authenticity-related challenges are linked with student well-being. These findings also highlight the relevance of culturally sensitive approaches to mental health support within higher education contexts.
