The association of oral health with anxiety symptoms among older adults in China: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Minghui Sun, Zhiyun Wu, Xinyan Ma, Hanqing Zhao, Hao Ai, Chao Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundOral health status has been shown to be associated with mental health. However, its association with anxiety remains insufficiently understood. The present study is the first to investigate the association between oral health and anxiety among elderly Chinese individuals.MethodsThis study included 11,034 participants aged ≥ 65 years from the 2018 wave of the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey. Oral health habits were assessed based on the number of natural teeth, frequency of tooth brushing, and denture wearing status. The 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder scale was used to screen for anxiety symptoms, and logistic regression analysis was performed to calculate the odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsThe prevalence of anxiety symptoms was 12.04% among Chinese older adults. In the fully adjusted model, participants with ≥ 20 teeth exhibited significantly lower odds of anxiety symptoms compared to those with fewer than 20 teeth (OR = 0.84; 95% CI = 0.72–0.98). Compared with never brushing, brushing once daily (OR = 0.83; 95% CI = 0.70–0.98), twice daily (OR = 0.73; 95% CI = 0.60–0.90), and three or more times daily (OR = 0.68; 95% CI = 0.49–0.92) each showed an inverse association with anxiety symptoms. Similarly, denture use was also associated with lower odds of anxiety symptoms (OR = 0.80; 95% CI = 0.71–0.91). Notably, a significant interaction was observed between tooth brushing frequency and smoking status.ConclusionsThis study indicates that retaining ≥ 20 teeth, brushing at least once daily, and using dentures are associated with a lower likelihood of anxiety symptoms among the elderly population. Further prospective studies are warranted to confirm these associations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07853-5.

Introduction

As global life expectancy continues to rise, the unprecedented acceleration of population aging poses a critical public health challenge for both China and the world. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), the proportion of adults aged 65 years and older is projected to account for 16.7% of the global population by the mid-21st century [1]. Older adults are more susceptible to disease and other adverse life events. Owing to this greater vulnerability, they experience more severe psychological distress, of which anxiety is the most common [2]. Anxiety is the sixth leading cause of disability worldwide and represents a negative emotional state characterised by pain and psychological discomfort [3, 4]. Previous research has estimated the prevalence of depression and anxiety among Chinese older adults at approximately 13.61% and 11.79%, respectively [5]. Compared with other populations, older adults face more serious mental health challenges [6, 7]. Anxiety, as one of the major mental disorders, has attracted increasingly widespread attention. Given the limited effectiveness of pharmacotherapy in treating mental disorders, a growing body of research has now shifted focus onto modifiable risk factors associated with psychiatric symptoms.

Oral diseases are attracting increasing global attention, affecting over 3.5 billion people worldwide [8]. As individuals age, their oral structures and functions progressively deteriorate [9, 10]. Oral dysfunction in the elderly resulting from oral diseases may further contribute to psychological distress, nutritional deficiencies, and reduced social interaction, thereby influencing the development of mental health symptoms [11, 12]. Previous studies have indicated that higher tooth brushing frequency and denture use are correlated with a reduced risk of depressive symptoms in older adults [13]. Beyond brushing and denture use, the number of natural teeth also plays a crucial role in oral health, with tooth loss affecting 23.7% of older adults globally [14]. However, the association between these oral health indicators and anxiety remains unclear.

Based on participants from the Chinese Longitudinal Healthy Longevity Survey (CLHLS), we simultaneously examined the associations between multiple dimensions of oral health (tooth count, brushing frequency, and denture use) and anxiety, rather than focusing on a single indicator as in previous studies, providing empirical evidence to support mental health prevention strategies.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study used data from the CLHLS, a nationally representative, community-based longitudinal study primarily investigating the lifestyle, genetic factors, and disease status among adults aged 65 years and older. The survey employed a multistage, stratified, cluster sampling design. Participants were initially selected from 23 provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions across China, representing approximately 85% of the national population. All participants completed face-to-face interviews performed by trained survey personnel. Details regarding the study design have been reported in a prior publication [15, 16]. We utilized cross-sectional data from the 2018 wave (eighth wave) of the CLHLS. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study protocol was approved by the Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University (Beijing, China) (IRB00001052-13074).

Based on the objectives of our study, we initially included 15,874 participants. After excluding individuals based on the following criteria; (1) incomplete anxiety data (N = 1,461); (2) incomplete oral health data (N = 447); (3) baseline age under 65 years (N = 87); and (4) missing relevant covariates (N = 2,845), a final sample of 11,034 participants was included in the present study (Fig. 1).

Assessment of oral health

Oral health habits in this study were determined based on responses to three questions regarding tooth count, denture use, and tooth brushing frequency. Specifically, participants were asked to report their current number of teeth, whether they wore dentures (yes/no), and their tooth brushing frequency, with the following options: “never,” “occasionally,” “once a day,” “twice a day,” or “three or more times a day.” Previous studies have indicated that having fewer than 20 teeth was correlated with various diseases and compromised dental aesthetics [17, 18]. Accordingly, participants were categorized into two groups according to tooth count: those with 20 or more teeth and those with fewer than 20 teeth.

Assessment of anxiety symptoms

We used the validated Chinese version of the 7-item Generalized Anxiety Disorder Scale (GAD-7) to assess anxiety symptoms. Each item is rated on a 0–3 scale, and responses are summed to generate a total score. A total score of ≥ 5 was considered indicative of anxiety symptoms [5, 19].

Assessment of covariates

The analysis included a range of covariates that could potentially confound the association between oral health and anxiety. These included demographic characteristics (age, sex, race, marital status), socioeconomic factors (residential status, annual income, employment status), lifestyle behaviors (physical activity, alcohol consumption, smoking status), and clinical measures (BMI, hypertension status). At the baseline assessment, trained investigators conducted standardized measurements of height, weight, waist circumference, and hip circumference following established protocols. The presence of hypertension was determined based on the average of two consecutive blood pressure measurements, with a cutoff of ≥ 140 mmHg for systolic or ≥ 90 mmHg for diastolic pressure [20].

Statistical analysis

In this study, continuous variables were presented as median (interquartile range), and categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages). Differences between the two groups were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test and the chi-square test, respectively. Multivariable logistic regression was employed to examine the associations between oral health indicators and anxiety. We constructed three hierarchical models: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, and BMI; Model 3 was further adjusted for race, annual income, residential status, marital status, smoking status, alcohol consumption, employment status, physical activity, and hypertension.

We conducted stratified analyses by age, sex, BMI, residential status, smoking status, and drinking status. Multiplicative interaction was assessed by including cross-product terms in the regression models.

Furthermore, we conducted a series of sensitivity analyses. First, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using the more stringent cutoff of GAD-7 ≥ 10 (indicating clinically significant anxiety), in order to test the robustness of the results under different cutoff values. Second, we additionally adjusted for depression symptom as a confounding factor to avoid bias. Third, E-values were calculated to quantify the robustness of the findings to potential unmeasured confounding [21].

A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4.

Results

Characteristics of the study participants

A total of 11,034 participants were included in the study, comprising 4,941 (44.80%) men and 6,093 (55.20%) women. The median age was 84.00 years (interquartile range 75.00–94.00). Statistically significant differences in anxiety symptoms were observed based on several sociodemographic and lifestyle factors, including sex, BMI, age, place of residence, annual household income, smoking status, exercise status, alcohol consumption, occupational physical activity, and use of dentures. Relative to those who never brushed their teeth, the ORs and 95% CIs for anxiety symptoms were 0.85 (95% CI = 0.69–1.05) for occasional brushing, 0.83 (95% CI = 0.70–0.98) for brushing once daily, 0.73 (95% CI = 0.60–0.90) for brushing twice daily, and 0.68 (95% CI = 0.49–0.92) for brushing three times or more daily.

Furthermore, denture use was associated with lower odds of anxiety symptoms compared with non-use (OR = 0.80, 95% CI = 0.71–0.91).

Subgroup and sensitivity analysis

Subgroup analyses revealed that the inverse association between retaining at least 20 teeth and anxiety was particularly pronounced among participants aged 65–79 years, women, and urban residents (Supplementary Table S1). Brushing teeth twice daily was consistently associated with lower odds of anxiety symptoms across multiple subgroups. These included individuals aged 80–99 years, women, both urban and rural residents, those with normal weight, as well as non-smokers and non-drinkers (Supplementary Table S2). Furthermore, denture use was linked to lower anxiety symptoms among participants aged over 80 years, rural residents, underweight and obese individuals, and those who did not smoke or consume alcohol (Supplementary Table S3). In the interaction analysis, a significant multiplicative interaction was observed between tooth brushing frequency and smoking status in relation to anxiety symptoms (P for interaction < 0.05) (Supplementary Table S2).

The sensitivity analysis results showed that the findings from the reanalysis using a GAD-7 cutoff of ≥ 10 were directionally consistent with the main analysis results (Supplementary Table S4). The results of the re-analysis with additional adjustment for depression symptoms showed that the direction remained consistent with the main analysis (Supplementary Table S5). The E-value analysis showed that the E-values (with the corresponding E-values for the confidence interval bound in parentheses) for tooth count ≥ 20, tooth brushing once per day, twice per day, at least three times per day, and denture use were 1.67 (1.16), 1.69 (1.16), 2.06 (1.45), 2.30 (1.37), and 1.80 (1.40), respectively (Supplementary Table S6).

Discussion

In this sample of older Chinese adults, a greater number of teeth, daily tooth brushing, and denture use were each independently associated with lower odds of anxiety symptoms. Notably, we observed an interaction between tooth brushing frequency and smoking status in relation to anxiety. These findings underscore the importance of integrating oral health promotion into mental health interventions targeting the elderly, given its potential to mitigate anxiety.

Evidence regarding the association between tooth count and anxiety symptoms is scarce. To our knowledge, only one study has explored this relationship, among 4,746 Korean adults, suggesting that having fewer than 20 remaining teeth was associated with greater severity of anxiety [22]. Our findings were consistent with this previous study and further indicate that a higher number of teeth was correlated with lower anxiety symptoms among Chinese older adults. A possible explanation was the difficulty in dietary intake resulting from tooth loss in the elderly population, which may contribute to symptoms of depression and anxiety [23]. This was because food serves as a medium for maintaining social connections and expressing friendship [24, 25]. Moreover, tooth loss may require older adults to wear dentures, and the process of wearing dentures can lead to oral health issues, such as periodontal disease [26]. Studies have shown that periodontal disease-related neurological inflammation or oral pain may further exacerbate anxiety symptoms [27].

Regarding the association between tooth brushing frequency and anxiety, our study found that among older adults, compared with not brushing, brushing once, twice, or three or more times per day was associated with a 17%, 27%, and 32% lower odds of anxiety, respectively. Our findings provide new epidemiological evidence for the role of oral hygiene in mental health and suggest that, among older adults, a minimum brushing frequency of once daily may be associated with lower odds of anxiety. These results are consistent with previous research. For instance, a study involving 1,604 students showed a negative correlation between anxiety and tooth brushing frequency [28]. A study from the COVID-19 pandemic period revealed that increased generalized anxiety symptoms were associated with a reduced likelihood of brushing teeth [29]. Furthermore, evidence suggests that changes in the oral microbiota due to tooth brushing frequency may influence anxiety through the modulation of metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids and tryptophan [30–32]. However, since these mechanisms were not directly assessed in the present study, further mechanistic research is needed to substantiate our findings. Importantly, the observed association between tooth brushing and lower anxiety has important implications for health management in the older population. Maintaining a tooth brushing frequency of at least once daily was a fundamental aspect of oral hygiene and may also serve as a proactive strategy for anxiety reduction. Therefore, integrating regular tooth brushing into broader mental health promotion efforts could represent a simple, low-cost, and scalable public health intervention to support mental well-being among older adults.

Our study also found an association between denture use and a lower likelihood of anxiety. Although direct evidence on this relationship remains limited, several research have explored the correlation between denture use and other health outcomes. For instance, a cross-sectional study involving 8442 older adults suggested that denture use might reduce the risk of depressive symptoms in the elderly [13]. A longitudinal study indicated that, compared with denture wearers, those without dentures might have a 15% higher risk of all-cause mortality [33]. Additionally, a cohort study showed that denture use might be associated with a lower risk of malnutrition [34]. These findings may, to some extent, provide indirect evidence supporting the potential impact of denture use on the mental health of older adults. However, other research has suggested that denture use might instead lead to poor oral health, which could contribute to anxiety symptoms [35]. This discrepancy may be due to the high prevalence of conventional dentures among the elderly population studied, which are prone to movement [36, 37]. The resulting oral inflammation or pain could, in turn, negatively affect their mental well-being [38].

Interestingly, we observed an interaction between tooth brushing frequency and smoking status among the older adults in this study, suggesting that smoking status may modify the relationship between tooth brushing frequency and anxiety symptoms. Based on these subgroup findings, the following public health recommendation can be made for high-risk groups of older adults with anxiety in China: brushing teeth twice a day may be particularly beneficial, especially among non-smoking older adults. These findings highlight the importance for older adults in China at high risk for anxiety to prioritize daily lifestyle habits, including smoking cessation. This antagonistic interaction may be explained by potential biological mechanisms. Regular brushing maintains oral flora homeostasis and relieves inflammation, while insufficient brushing worsens anxiety [39, 40]. Smoking may impair the beneficial bacteria in the oral cavity. Meanwhile, nicotine in cigarette smoke can temporarily alleviate anxiety, but it disrupts the body’s neurotransmitters and activates the HPA axis over the long term [41–43]. The two factors aggravate each other, triggering systemic inflammation via the oral-brain axis and disrupting emotional regulation. Rigorous, high-quality research is warranted to validate our findings and to clarify the precise biological pathways involved. Such insights are essential for developing targeted strategies that integrate oral health into broader mental health promotion initiatives.

This study possesses several notable strengths. This was the first study to examine the relationship between oral health habits (including tooth brushing frequency, tooth count, and denture use) and anxiety symptoms in older adults. The robustness of these associations was corroborated through subgroup analyses and E-value calculations.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design precludes any causal inference. Moreover, reverse causality cannot be ruled out: given that key oral health indicators were self-reported, we cannot determine whether anxiety influences oral health behaviors or vice versa. Future longitudinal studies are needed to clarify temporal dynamics. Second, the oral health indicators were all self-reported, which was susceptible to recall bias, misclassification bias, and social desirability bias. Furthermore, given the advanced age of the participants and the potential for cognitive impairment to affect the reliability of self-reported measures, this limitation is of particular concern in the present study. In future studies, it is recommended to use clinically assessed oral health indicators or validated objective measures, such as standardized dental examinations or caregiver-verified reports, which can help reduce reporting bias and improve the accuracy of estimates in older adults. Third, because the study population only included Chinese older adults, and given the differences in healthcare systems across countries, cultural practices related to oral hygiene, and the accessibility of dental care, the generalizability of our findings to other populations may be limited. Future studies that consider race, culture, and healthcare policies are necessary to validate our findings. Fourth, this study only measured whether participants wore dentures; therefore, the type of dentures, such as complete dentures, partial dentures, or implant-supported dentures, could not be distinguished. Given that different types of dentures may have varying effects on oral function and quality of life, further investigations taking these factors into account are necessary. Fifth, using a GAD-7 cutoff score of ≥ 5 to define anxiety symptoms will include mild anxiety. This may overestimate patients’ anxiety status and inflate the prevalence estimate. However, in our study, we aimed to capture the presence of any anxiety symptoms, not only clinically significant anxiety, because even mild anxiety symptoms may be associated with oral health behaviors in older adults. Furthermore, the sensitivity analysis using a GAD-7 cutoff of ≥ 10 to define anxiety (clinically significant anxiety) yielded results consistent with the main analysis. Finally, although the E-value analysis indicates that unmeasured confounders would need to be substantially stronger than the measured covariates to fully explain the observed association. Nevertheless, it is still worth noting the potential influence of important unmeasured confounders, such as education level, cognitive status, chronic pain, nutritional status, and dental care, which warrant further investigation in future studies.

Conclusions

This study suggests that among Chinese older adults, a higher number of teeth, brushing teeth at least once daily, and denture use are correlated with lower odds of anxiety symptoms. Notably, an interaction was observed between tooth brushing frequency and smoking status in relation to anxiety. Large-scale prospective studies in other ethnic populations are warranted to confirm the generalizability of our findings.
