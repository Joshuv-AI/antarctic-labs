Zinc oxide nanoparticles mitigate drought stress and enhance secondary metabolite production in Hypericum perforatum
Authors: Siba Mouid Al-Haliem, Muthanna Jasim Mohammed, Amera Mahmood Al-Rawi, Heidar Meftahizade
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Drought stress represents a major abiotic factor responsible for reducing the growth, yield and quality of important plants. This research was investigated the potential of ZnO Nano particles (ZnO NPs) to alleviate drought stress in Hypericum perforatum (common St. John’s Wort). A factorial experiment using randomized complete block design (RCBD) was performed with 3 replications and 4 different irrigation treatments (100%, 70%, 40%, and 25% of field capacity) combined with 4 different concentrations of ZnO NPs (0, 10, 50, and 100 mg L− 1). Drought stress had a significant negative impact on different morphophysiological characteristics, including plant height, root length, leaf area, chlorophyll concentration, relative water content, and photosynthetic efficiency (Fv/Fm). Additionally, drought stressed plants exhibited significantly higher activity of catalase (CAT) and peroxidase (POD), proline content, total phenolic content, content of essential oils, and accumulation of hypericin than non-stressed plants. These biochemical adjustments indicate the activation of osmotic regulation and antioxidant defense mechanisms that contribute to drought stress tolerance. The application of ZnO NPs via foliar application reduced the adverse effects of drought and significantly improved growth characteristics, levels of photosynthetic pigments, and plant water status under drought conditions. Moreover, in combination with the application of ZnO NPs (especially at concentrations of 50 and 100 mg L⁻¹), moderate drought stress (40–70% FC) resulted in significantly higher essential oils and hypericin accumulation compared to all other treatments. Principal component and correlation analysis confirmed the relationships between physiological performance and stress-related metabolites. Overall, application of ZnO NPs represent an innovative opportunity for mitigating drought related stress in H. perforatum.

Introduction

Hypericum perforatum L. (St. John’s wort) is a medicinal plant known for its diverse secondary metabolites and pharmacological potential [1]. St. John’s wort is a perennial herb, belonging to the Hypericaceae family, is widely distributed across Europe, Asia, and parts of North Africa and the America [2]. It has been traditionally used for wounds therapy, burns, diseases of the digestive system [3–5]. The medicinal properties of Hypericum perforatum is attributed to rich phytochemical including hypericin, pseudohypericin, hyperforin, quercetin, and hyperoside [6, 7]. The phenolic constituents of Hypericum perforatum have shown effective antimicrobial, antiviral, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, antidepressant, and anticancer properties [7–9].

Drought stress can lead to abiotic stresses, which intensively disrupts biochemical, physiological, and molecular functions [10, 11], leading to diminished growth, yield, and quality of medicinal plants. Furthermore, drought impairs the water and nutrients uptake and photosynthetic processes, resulting in a cascade of physiological disruptions, eventually dropping crop yields [12–14]. Besides, drought lessens the cell osmotic stability in leaves through the accumulation of several compatible and non-compatible biological compounds [15, 16].

Chemical fertilization significantly improves plant performance, however chemical fertilizers pollute the environment and endanger human health. On the other hand, extreme usage of chemical fertilizers causes detrimental effects on the soil structure, microorganisms, water holding capacity, imbalances in soil nutrients, and soil fertility [17, 18]. Therefore, it is urgent need for sustainable agricultural and environmentally friendly agricultural systems to provide optimal nutrition for plant production without imperiling the environment [19–21].

Zinc (Zn) is an essential micronutrient of plant nutrition programs in farms for securing the crop quality and yield [22]. This micronutrient required in trace amounts for optimal plant growth and development, playing a vital role in numerous physiological processes, including protein and carbohydrate metabolism, growth regulation, and photosynthesis [23–25]. Although the plant demand for Zinc is minimal, inadequate availability causes physiological stress due to malfunctioning of Zinc-dependent enzymatic and metabolic systems [24, 26]. Zn involves in plant defense and increased antioxidant enzyme activities under stress, also encourages N uptake and plant growth by regulating the antioxidant system and C–N metabolism [27].

Nanotechnology is a novel capacity of scientific research applied in agriculture and biotechnology, which are promising tools for alleviating plant stress [28–30]. NPs scavenges the ROSs -mediated salinity, and decrease the Na+/K+ ratio in plant roots [31, 32]. Many studies documented the transformative tool NPs in plant production and sustainable agriculture under stress conditions [22, 33–35].

The nutrients nano form, potentially can improve medicinal plants quality and yield by encouraging certain phytobiochemical processes; water and nutrient uptake, antioxidant activities, photosynthesis, and strengthening plant structure [36–38]. Furthermore, application of these biostimulants gained attention to improve plant tolerance to environmental stress [39, 40]. Nutrient–NPs are compatible and non-toxic form of nutrients, have developed as a capable practical tool to enhance the tolerance of plants against stressful toxicity [41].

Although several molecular mechanisms associated with ZnO nanoparticle-mediated stress responses have been proposed in previous studies, the present study mainly focuses on the physiological, biochemical, and phytochemical responses of Hypericum perforatum under drought stress conditions. We hypothesized that supplementing of Zinc nano material would help plant resistance to drought stress and increase the growth and quality of Hypericum perforatum, resulting lead to sustainable and eco-friendly agriculture. Thus, we exposed this plant to the different concentrations of ZnO NPs under different irrigation levels.

Despite the increasing interest in the application of nanomaterials in plant stress physiology, limited information is available regarding the integrative effects of zinc oxide nanoparticles on both physiological performance and secondary metabolite biosynthesis in medicinal plants under varying drought regimes. In particular, the interactive influence of ZnO nanoparticles and graded water deficit on Hypericum perforatum, remains insufficiently explored. Therefore, the present study aims to provide a comprehensive evaluation of the combined effects of ZnO nanoparticles and different irrigation levels on growth, water status, photosynthetic efficiency, antioxidant responses, and phytochemical traits. This work not only elucidates the mechanistic basis of nanoparticle-mediated drought tolerance but also highlights a novel and practical strategy for enhancing both yield and phytochemical quality of medicinal plants under water-limited conditions.

Materials and methods

A factorial experiment was carried out with a randomized complete block design (RCBD) and three replications during 2023–2024 growing season. Each experimental plot was 2.5 × 5.5 m, and plants were grown in 5 rows, with a spacing of 30 cm in rows 40 cm apart per replication. Various drought stress at 4 levels ((control = 100% field capacities (FCs)), 70% FC, 40% FC, and 25% FC) and foliar spraying of ZnO NPs with foliar-applied at 0, 10, 50, and 100 mg L− 1 were done in the during experiments.

Seed cultivation and fertilizer application

Each experimental unit measured 2.5 × 5.5 m, and treatments were arranged in a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replications. Number of plants per plot was 60 plants and for physiological and biochemical analysis, ten representative plants were randomly selected from the central rows of each plot to avoid border effect. The plant material used in this study was obtained from cultivated sources. Hypericum perforatum is routinely cultivated in the region, and several commercial farms are available. Therefore, no specific permission was required for plant collection. The taxonomic identification of the plant material was confirmed by Dr. Heidar Meftahizade.

Water stress was applied during experiment (4 months) using three gradient levels. soil moisture content in each plot was daily measured by means of a time-domain reflectometry sensor from a 20 cm soil layer. Cultivation

Prior to sowing, a basal fertilization was applied at rates of 40 kg ha⁻¹ nitrogen and 50 kg ha⁻¹ phosphorus (P₂O₅). Irrigation was supplied using a drip (trickle) irrigation system to ensure precise water application. Weed management was carried out manually throughout the growing season using hand hoeing. Foliar spraying of zinc oxide nanoparticles was performed when the plants reached the 6 to 8-leaf stage [43].

US1019F; CAS no. 13463-67-7) was performed using various analytical techniques described in the literature [44]. The morphology of the ZnO NPs were obtained with the Thermo Scientific™ Q250 Scanning Electron Microscope SEM. The size distribution of the ZnO NPs will be determined using the Malvern ZetaSizer Nano Particle Size Analyzer and the zeta-potential (HPG) will be measured using a Malvern ZetaSizer Nano Particle Size Analyzer. Traits measurement

To count the number of dark glands, three leaves were selected from the upper, middle, and lower parts of each plant separately and the number of dark glands was counted under a light microscope. A leaf area meter (USA-1300, Cor-Li) was used to measure leaf area.

The samples for physiological and biochemical assays were collected during the flowering phase (late June, 2024) [9].

The maximum quantum yield of photosystem II (FV/FM)

The Fv/Fm values were estimated using the OJIP protocol [45]. Young mature leaves of H. perforatum from the middle part of saplings were carefully selected for each experimental treatment. After 20 min in the dark-adapted condition, chlorophyll fluorescence parameters were recorded using a portable fluorometer (Fluorpen FP 100-MAX, Photon Systems Instruments, Drasov, Czech Republic).

Total phenolic content

The protocol was employed to assess the total phenolic content. Hypericum perforatum leaves (500 mg) were extracted with 20 ml of methanol (with a distilled water ratio of 8:2 v/v) using 10 min of room temperature ultrasonic [47]. The extraction process continued for another 20 h at darkness and room temperature. The methanol extracts were centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min. Folin Ciocalteu (Merck, Germany) was used as reactive agent. An extract aliquot (100 µl) was applied to and blended with 2 ml Folin Ciocalteu reagent (1:10 diluted with distilled water) and 2.5 ml Na2CO3 aqueous 7.5 (w/v). The total phenolic content of the resulting solutions was calorimetrically assessed at room temperature at a wavelength of 765 nm (UV 2600, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan) after 80 min of incubation in which gallic acid was added as normal. Total phenolic content was measured as equivalent (mg GAE/gDW) to gallic acid.

Catalase (CAT) activity

CAT was measured based on a method with minor modifications, which relies on monitoring the decrease in hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) concentration [48]. An increase in CAT activity corresponds to a decrease in absorbance. For the assay, 3 mL of phosphate buffer, 4.51 µL of H₂O₂, and 50 µL of the enzyme extract were added to a spectrophotometer cuvette and mixed. Absorbance was recorded at 240 nm immediately and monitored for 2 min. A blank solution containing 3 mL phosphate buffer and 4.51 µL H₂O₂ was used for calibration. Enzyme activity was expressed as ΔA₂₄₀ min⁻¹·mg⁻¹ protein.Catalase (CAT) activity was determined by monitoring the decomposition of H₂O₂ at 240 nm in a reaction mixture containing 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 15 mM H₂O₂, and enzyme extract.

Peroxidase (POD) activity

POD activity was measured using guaiacol as a substrate following [49] with slight modifications. An increase in enzyme activity leads to an increase in absorbance. For the assay, 3 mL of phosphate buffer, 4.51 µL of H₂O₂, 3.35 µL of guaiacol, and 50 µL of enzyme extract were added to a cuvette and mixed. Absorbance was recorded at 470 nm immediately and monitored for 2 min. A blank solution containing 3 mL phosphate buffer, 4.51 µL H₂O₂, and 3.35 µL guaiacol was used. Peroxidase (POD) activity was assayed using guaiacol as substrate in a reaction mixture containing 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 6.0), 20 mM guaiacol, 10 mM H₂O₂, and enzyme extract. The increase in absorbance was monitored at 470 nm.

Proline quantification

To assess proline levels, the protocol described by [50] was adapted. Initially, 0.05 g of finely ground and nitrogen-dried plant tissue was mixed with 5 mL of a 3% sulfosalicylic acid solution and thoroughly homogenized using a porcelain mortar. The resulting homogenate was left to stand for 48 h before filtration. Then, a 1 mL aliquot of the filtrate was combined with 1 mL of ninhydrin reagent and 1 mL of glacial acetic acid. The mixture was incubated in a boiling water bath for one hour. After allowing the samples to cool on ice, 2 mL of toluene was added, and the solution was vigorously shaken for 5 to 20 s. The upper toluene layer was separated and its absorbance was read at 520 nm to determine proline concentration.

Essential oil content

After harvest, aerial plant parts were shade-dried at room temperature (25 ± 2 °C) under natural ventilation until constant weight was achieved. Dried samples were stored in sealed dark glass containers at 4 °C and protected from light and moisture until essential oil extraction. The essential oil was extracted from dried aerial plant parts using hydro distillation with a Clevenger-type apparatus for 4 h according to standard essential oil extraction procedures.30 g of the completely dry shoot tops of the plant were weighed and placed in the relevant balloons with a ratio of 1:20 (w/v) and heated under controlled boiling conditions. The obtained essential oil layer was separated from the aqueous phase, dried over anhydrous sodium sulfate to remove residual moisture, and stored in sealed dark glass vials at 4 °C until GC-MS analysis. These methodological details were added to improve reproducibility and analytical transparency.

GC-MS profiling

GC-MS analysis was performed using hydrodistilled essential oil extracted from aerial plant parts by a Clevenger-type apparatus. The analysis was carried out using an Agilent 7890B GC system coupled with a 5977 A mass selective detector equipped with an HP-5MS capillary column (30 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 μm). Helium was used as carrier gas at a flow rate of 1 mL min − 1. The oven temperature was programmed from 60 °C to 280 °C at 10 °C min − 1. Compound identification was performed by comparison of mass spectra with NIST and Wiley spectral libraries and confirmed using retention indices when available.

Extraction and quantification of hypericin content

Hypericin extraction and quantification was performed according to previously published analytical methods with minor modifications. Briefly, dried powdered aerial plant material (100 mg) was extracted with methanol under dark conditions to minimize photodegradation of hypericin. The extracts were centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min and the supernatant was used for spectrophotometric analysis. Hypericin quantification was performed at 592 nm using a UV–VIS spectrophotometer. HPLC analysis of phenolic compounds

The shoot extracts were extracted using a continuous Soxhlet extractor, where the samples were soaked in absolute ethanol (99.8%) for 48 h after oil extraction using petroleum ether (60–80 °C) to remove fats (defatting). The obtained solution was then concentrated using a rotary evaporator (RVE) to approximately 20 mL for all samples. An acid hydrolysis process was then carried out by taking 5 mL of the crude ethanolic extract and adding 20 mL of HCl (1 N), followed by heating in a water bath for 30 min. After cooling, 30 mL of ethyl acetate was added in two stages and separated using a separating funnel. The upper ethyl acetate layer was collected twice and stored in a refrigerator until chromatographic analyses were performed [52]. Four different extraction fractions were assayed and the acid-hydrolyzed samples were analyzed using an HPLC device of the type Shimadzu LC-2010HT, after purification through membrane filters with a pore size of 0.1 μm. The analysis was performed at a wavelength of 320 nm, with a flow rate of 1 mL min− 1, using a mobile phase consisting of 85% acetonitrile and 15% water, and a C18 column with dimensions of 240 × 4.60 mm. The retention time (Rt) for each standard compound is shown in Table 5. Phenolic compounds were identified by comparing retention times with authentic external standards under identical chromatographic conditions. Calibration curves were prepared for each standard compound using serial dilutions, and quantification was based on peak area measurements. The injection volume was 20 µL. Detection at 320 nm was selected because the majority of phenolic acids and flavonoids present in Hypericum perforatum exhibit strong UV absorption in this spectral region.The standards were supplied by Fluka (Switzerland) and BDH companies. The measurements were carried out at the University of Mosul, College of Agriculture and Forestry.

Statistical analysis

The two-way factorial ANOVA analysis was done and the means were compared (Tukey’s HSD Test: P < 0.05) using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 Software’s. The bar charts were prepared with Excel Software’s (ver. 2010). The means were used for Principal Component Analysis and Pearson’s Correlation Estimation and data analyzed using Minitab Software’s Ver. 20. For multivariate analysis, mean values of all biological replicates were used. Prior to PCA, all variables were standardized (z-score normalization) to avoid scale effects. Pearson correlation analysis was performed using treatment means to evaluate relationships among physiological, biochemical, and phytochemical parameters.

Drought stress generally reduced morpho–physiological, physiological, and photosynthetic attributes, however it improved antioxidant activity, phenolic compounds, proline, EO, and hypericin contents. Plant height significantly was reduced under drought levels. Nano Oxide Zinc significantly improved plant height under 25 and 100% FC, and 50 mg L− 1 ZnO NPs increased plant height in well irrigated plants (100% FC). The highest plant height (71.0 cm) was measured in plants treated with 50 mg L− 1 ZnO NPs under full irrigation condition and the shortest plants was observed in response to 10 mg L− 1 ZnO NPs under 25% FC irrigation (Fig. 3a). The root growth significantly was limited under drought stress, especially at 25% FC irrigation. Application of ZnO NPs tended to increase in root growth under all irrigation levels compared to controls. 100 mg L− 1 ZnO NPs showed the highest effect on root growth; the shortest roots (16.7 cm) were recorded under 25% FC irrigation, while the longest roots (38.3 cm) were measured at full irrigation condition in response to 100 mg L− 1 ZnO NPs (Fig. 3b). Leaf area meaningfully was reduced under drought stress compared to full irrigation condition, especially at 40 and 25% FC irrigation; application of ZnO NPs significantly extended the leaf growth; The highest leaf area (3.5 cm2) was measured under full irrigation condition with application of 100 mg L− 1 ZnO NPs, and the lowest under 25% FC (1.8 cm2) (Fig. 3c). With irrigation decreases, the number of dark glands per leaf significantly were declined; application of ZnO NPs meaningfully improved the numbers of nodules, especially under full irrigation and 70% FC conditions. The highest number of nodules (17.3 number) was observed under full irrigation + 50 mg L− 1 ZnO NPs; no differs from its values under 10 and 100 mg L− 1 ZnO NPs. The lowest nodules (7.0 number) were observed at 40 and 25% FC irrigation (Fig. 3d).

The minimum EO content (0.057%) was gained at 25% FC irrigation and application of 10 mg L− 1 ZnO NPs and the least hypericin content (0.06%) at 25% FC irrigation in response to 50 mg L− 1 ZnO NPs. Overall, our findings confirmed that slight drought stress and 50 and 100 mg L− 1 ZnO NPs significantly can increase EO and hypericin contents of hypericum perforatum.

Discussion

Water deficiency under severe drought stress notably reduced plant growth and photosynthetic attributes, while it improved antioxidant activity, phenolic, proline, essential oil, and hypericin content. Drought stress markedly reduces plant height, shoot dry weight, leaf area index, relative water content, and oil yield in medicinal plants [53].

Application of ZnO NPs significantly and progressively ameliorated the adverse effects of drought and improved growth photosynthetic characteristics, as well as enhanced EO and hypericin content. Foliar application of 100 ppm ZnO-NPs significantly has improved the growth and physiological traits of tomato and aid to achieve the highest nutrient uptake [3]. Application of 50 and 100 mg L− 1 ZnO NPs enhanced growth and fruit yield of eggplant by 12.2% and 22.6%, respectively, compared with control [8].

The limitation of vegetative growth under water deficit conditions can be mechanistically attributed to reduced cell turgor. In addition, drought stress alters hormonal balance, particularly by reducing auxin transport, which further constrains plant development. Under drought condition, the closure of stomata declines the availability of CO₂ leading to over reduction of the electron transport chain of photophosphorylation resulting in production of reactive oxygen species that can affect the integrity of cellular structures which significantly abridge plant growth and development [15, 54].

The observed improvement in growth-related traits following ZnO NPs application may be associated with the role of zinc in auxin biosynthesis and meristematic activity, as previously suggested in the literature [1]. Enhanced root development under nanoparticle treatment may have facilitated more efficient water and nutrient acquisition. thereby partially offsetting the inhibitory effects of drought stress, as also reported by [6]. The decline in leaf relative water content reflects a disruption in plant water balance and membrane integrity under drought conditions. Such reductions are typically associated with leading to cellular dehydration [2]. The improvement of RWC in ZnO-treated plants may be explained by enhanced osmotic adjustment and stabilization of cellular membranes. Zinc is known to play a role in maintaining membrane structure and function, which could contribute to improved water retention capacity under stress conditions [1]. Zinc is an essential micronutrient involved in the plant physiological processes, including the activation of more than 300 enzymes, the synthesis of chlorophyll, auxin metabolism (via tryptophan), carbohydrate metabolism and maintenance of membrane integrity [7]. In Zn-treated plants photosynthetic rate was 60.30% higher than control, due to improving share of photosynthetic nitrogen [27]. Previous studies have suggested that zinc uptake and transport may involve ZIP transporters and their associated regulatory networks [52]. However, these molecular mechanisms were not investigated in the present study and therefore remain speculative. Zinc finger proteins have been reported to participate in plant growth regulation and stress responses; however, their involvement was not examined in the present study [4]. Zinc has been reported to influence carbon allocation and assimilate transport in some plant species, which may partially explain the improved growth performance observed in the present study [27].

Drought stress impairs photosynthetic performance through oxidative damage to chloroplast structures and inhibition of photosynthetic enzymes. The reduction in PSII efficiency indicates photoinhibition and disruption of electron transport processes [9]. The ameliorative effects of ZnO nanoparticles on chlorophyll content and Fv/Fm suggest enhanced protection of the photosynthetic apparatus. This improvement may be linked to the role of zinc as a cofactor in enzymes involved in chlorophyll biosynthesis and antioxidant defense systems [6].

The activation of antioxidant enzymes such as catalase and peroxidase under drought conditions represents a key adaptive response to elevated reactive oxygen species (ROS). These enzymes function to detoxify hydrogen peroxide and maintain cellular redox balance [43]. The reduction in CAT and POD activities in ZnO nanoparticle-treated plants suggests a decrease in oxidative stress intensity, thereby reducing the requirement for elevated enzymatic defense. This observation is consistent with previous studies indicating that nanoparticles can enhance ROS homeostasis and reduce oxidative damage [5]. Phenolic compounds play a central role in the non-enzymatic antioxidant defense system and are typically upregulated under stress conditions to counteract oxidative damage. The observed modulation of TPC under ZnO nanoparticle treatment indicates a close relationship between phenolic metabolism and ROS dynamics. A reduction in phenolic accumulation under nanoparticle application suggests decreased oxidative pressure and a shift toward a more balanced physiological state.

The stimulation of essential oil biosynthesis under moderate stress conditions can be explained by the activation of secondary metabolic pathways as part of an adaptive response. Mild stress conditions often act as elicitors, enhancing the production of valuable secondary metabolites. The further enhancement observed under ZnO nanoparticle application suggests a synergistic interaction, potentially mediated through improved nutrient availability, enhanced enzymatic activity, and increased metabolic flux toward secondary biosynthesis pathways. Hypericin accumulation appears to be regulated by the interaction between stress signaling and metabolic activity. The combined effect of moderate drought stress and ZnO nanoparticles may be associated with enhanced metabolic activity related to hypericin biosynthesis. However, the enzymatic pathways involved were not directly investigated in the present study. Zinc may function as a catalytic or structural component in these enzymatic pathways, thereby promoting the accumulation of this important bioactive compound [4, 7].

Although several molecular and physiological mechanisms have been proposed in the literature to explain zinc-mediated stress tolerance, including auxin regulation, zinc transport systems, transcriptional regulation, and aquaporin-associated responses, these mechanisms were not directly investigated in the present study. Therefore, the proposed explanations should be considered as potential interpretations based on previous reports rather than direct evidence generated from the current experiment. Further molecular studies are required to verify the underlying mechanisms in Hypericum perforatum.

Conclusion

The present study revealed that drought stress exerts a profound influence on the morpho-physiological, biochemical, and phytochemical attributes of Hypericum perforatum. Water deficit markedly impaired growth, photosynthetic performance, and water status, while simultaneously stimulating antioxidant defense mechanisms and the accumulation of stress-related metabolites, including proline and phenolic compounds. In conclusion, ZnO nanoparticle application under drought stress improved physiological performance, enhanced antioxidant enzyme activities, and increased the accumulation of valuable secondary metabolites in Hypericum perforatum. These findings suggest that ZnO nanoparticles may play a beneficial role in improving plant performance under water-deficit conditions. However, further studies at molecular and field levels are required to fully elucidate the underlying mechanisms and long-term implications. Importantly, the combined application of ZnO NPs with moderate drought stress resulted in the highest accumulation of essential oil and hypericin, highlighting a synergistic interaction between Nano–nutrition and controlled stress conditions. These findings underscore the potential of ZnO nanoparticles not only as stress-alleviating agents but also as effective elicitors of secondary metabolite production in medicinal plants. In conclusion, ZnO nanoparticles represent a promising practical strategy for enhancing drought tolerance and improving both the quantitative and qualitative traits of Hypericum perforatum. Overall, the exogenous application of 100 mg L-1 ZnO NPs promote the growth and performance of this plant in drought condition.
