Explainability guided feature selection for speech emotion recognition using SHAP and permutation importance
Authors: S I M M Raton Mondol, Sangmin Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSpeech Emotion Recognition (SER) systems commonly rely on high-dimensional handcrafted acoustic feature sets, which increase computational complexity, risk overfitting, and reduce interpretability. To address these challenges, we propose an explainability-driven, redundancy-aware feature selection framework that integrates SHAP-based attribution with permutation-based performance importance through a unified rank-sum criterion. A correlation-controlled redundancy penalty is further introduced to mitigate multicollinearity while preserving acoustically meaningful descriptors. The framework operates entirely in the original feature space, enabling transparent and interpretable model refinement. Experiments were conducted on four widely used emotional speech corpora (CREMA-D, RAVDESS, SAVEE, and TESS) under within-corpus evaluation settings. To assess the generalizability of the proposed framework, experiments were performed using multiple classifiers, including XGBoost, Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Random Forest (RF). Using the XGBoost classifier, the proposed framework achieved accuracies of 0.600 on CREMA-D, 0.752 on RAVDESS, 0.726 on SAVEE, and 0.998 on TESS, while reducing the feature space by up to 40–70%. Macro-F1 and UAR metrics exhibit similar trends, confirming stable performance gains. Additional experiments using SVM and RF further indicate that the proposed feature selection framework generalizes across different classifiers while maintaining competitive performance. The findings indicate that explainability can serve not only as a post-hoc interpretation tool but as an active optimization mechanism for compact and interpretable SER models.Keywords: Speech emotion recognition, Explainable AI, SHAP-based interpretability, Permutation importance, Handcrafted acoustic features

Introduction

Speech Emotion Recognition (SER) has emerged as a fundamental component of affective computing, enabling machines to infer emotional states from spoken language [1]. By analyzing acoustic characteristics such as prosodic, spectral patterns, and voice quality, SER systems aim to bridge the gap between human affective expression and machine understanding. These systems play a crucial role in a wide range of applications, including healthcare monitoring [2], intelligent virtual assistants, customer service analytics, human–computer interaction [3], or education [4].

Despite its wide applications, SER remains challenging due to variability across speakers and contexts, overlapping acoustic patterns, recording inconsistencies, class imbalance, and the subjective nature of emotion labeling [5]. Traditional SER pipelines often extract hundreds of handcrafted features, encompassing prosodic, spectral, temporal, and voice-quality descriptors. While such rich feature sets can improve representational capacity, they also introduce the curse of dimensionality, increasing the risk of overfitting, inflating computational costs, and reducing model interpretability. As SER systems are increasingly deployed in sensitive domains such as healthcare, interpretability and transparency are becoming as important as predictive accuracy.

Over the past decade, numerous techniques have been developed for SER. These approaches can be broadly grouped into three categories. First, methods relied on hand-crafted acoustic features, including prosodic features (e.g., fundamental frequency, energy, and speech rate), spectral features (e.g., MFCCs and LPCs), and tonal descriptors. These features were typically combined with conventional machine learning classifiers such as GMMs, SVMs, and HMMs [6–8]. Second, with the rise of deep learning, end-to-end frameworks were introduced to automatically learn feature representations. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs), recurrent neural networks (RNNs), and BiLSTM architectures became widely adopted [9]. More recently, Transformer-based models have gained prominence [10]. For example, Chen et al. [11] proposed a hierarchical Transformer with a token-semantic module to reduce training time and model size, while Lu et al. [12] introduced a frame-to-segment Transformer aggregation strategy for utterance-level representation learning. Third, recent research has increasingly leveraged pre-trained models (PTMs) to extract emotion-relevant representations. Early PTMs were CNN-based, such as AlexNet [13] and ResNet [14], whereas more recent models adopt Transformer architectures, including wav2vec 2.0 [15] and HuBERT [16]. Trained in large-scale data using supervised or self-supervised learning (SSL), these models produce embeddings that achieve state-of-the-art performance across various speech tasks. In SER, approaches such as the upstream–downstream framework by Morais et al. [17] and emotion2vec proposed by Ma et al. [18] demonstrate the effectiveness of self-supervised representations for emotion-related tasks.

A major challenge in SER lies in training models on large, high-dimensional feature spaces where the relevance of individual features is not always clearly understood. This issue is further exacerbated by the limited size and diversity of available datasets, which restrict effective model training and generalization [19]. As a result, predefined feature sets often produce high-dimensional representations that hinder the model’s ability to capture meaningful emotion-related patterns.

In addition, transparency and explainability are critical in SER, particularly for safety-sensitive applications. Interpretable systems enable error analysis, bias detection, and more reliable decision-making [20]. Explainable AI (XAI) [21], aims to enhance the transparency and accountability of AI models, addressing concerns related to trust and fairness [22, 23]. In the context of SER, integrating XAI techniques supports the development of more interpretable and trustworthy emotion recognition systems. Among various XAI techniques, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) [24], offers a theoretically grounded framework based on cooperative game theory to quantify the contribution of each feature to a model’s prediction. SHAP has been successfully applied in domains such as finance, medicine, and computer vision, but its potential for systematic feature selection in SER remains underexplored.

To address the challenges of high-dimensional feature spaces and unclear feature relevance, we propose a supervised machine learning framework that integrates explainability-driven feature selection with performance-based validation. Specifically, we combine SHAP importance [24] with permutation-based feature importance [25] and redundancy control to iteratively refine the feature set in a model-dependent manner. This formulation ensures the retention of features that are both decision-relevant and performance-critical, while progressively eliminating redundant or less informative features.

The proposed architecture consists of three main components: (1) a feature refinement module that performs iterative importance scoring and redundancy-aware selection, (2) a supervised classification module for emotion recognition, and (3) an explainability module that quantifies feature contributions and provides feedback to guide subsequent optimization steps. Unlike conventional approaches where explainability is applied post hoc, our framework integrates explainability as a core iterative mechanism for feature optimization within the SER pipeline.

Materials and methods

Dataset

Numerous public datasets are available for speech emotion recognition (SER), generally categorized into actor-based, induced, and natural emotional corpora. Model performance is strongly influenced by dataset authenticity, with natural datasets offering more realistic emotional expressions; however, they often present challenges related to accessibility and legal constraints [26]. Emotional speech datasets differ in language, number of emotions, speaker diversity, and recording conditions [27]. Among them, actor-based datasets are the most widely used, particularly in English, and typically include core emotions such as neutral, happy, sad, and angry.

In this study, we utilize four widely adopted English actor-based datasets: CREMA-D [28], RAVDESS [29], SAVEE [30], and TESS [31].

Experimental protocol

To ensure reproducibility, all experiments were conducted using identical train-test splits across all classifiers. For within-corpus evaluation, each dataset was divided into training and testing subsets using stratified sampling with a 70:30 ratio. Experiments were repeated using two random seeds to assess result stability.

Feature selection was performed exclusively on the training partition to prevent information leakage. SHAP importance and permutation importance were computed independently and combined using the proposed rank-sum criterion. Top-K feature subsets were then evaluated to analyze the trade-off between feature compactness and recognition performance.

Three classifiers were employed for evaluation: XGBoost (XGB) [32], Support Vector Machine (SVM) [33], and Random Forest (RF) [34]. All models were trained and evaluated under identical experimental settings for fair comparison.

Performance was evaluated using Accuracy (ACC), Macro-F1 score, and Unweighted Average Recall (UAR).

Feature extraction

From each utterance, we extract a total of 296 handcrafted acoustic features using standard speech analysis techniques. The feature set is organized into four major categories. Prosodic features capture fundamental frequency (pitch), intensity (energy), and their statistical descriptors, reflecting variations in speech melody and loudness. Spectral features characterize the frequency-domain properties of speech and include Mel-frequency cepstral coefficients (MFCCs) with per-coefficient statistical functionals, as well as spectral centroid, bandwidth, contrast, and roll-off. Voice quality features describe micro-variations in phonation and include jitter, shimmer, harmonic-to-noise ratio (HNR), and glottal noise measures. Temporal dynamics features model changes over time in acoustic contours, including pitch slopes, energy dynamics, and related statistical measures. Prior to model training, all features are standardized to zero mean and unit variance.

Performance was measured using Accuracy (ACC), Macro-averaged F1-score (Macro-F1), and Unweighted Average Recall (UAR) to account for class imbalance and per-class robustness (Table 1).

Across four speech emotion recognition corpora (CREMA, RAVDESS, SAVEE, and TESS), we evaluated the proposed SHAP- and permutation-guided feature selection framework using all classifiers. On CREMA-D (6 classes, 7,441 samples), XGBoost showed consistent improvement after feature selection, with accuracy increasing from 0.593 to 0.600 and macro-F1 from 0.591 to 0.598. Random Forest also exhibited modest gains, improving accuracy from 0.534 to 0.540 and macro-F1 from 0.522 to 0.530. In contrast, SVM performance remained nearly unchanged, indicating that feature reduction had limited impact on this corpus for kernel-based classification.

On TESS (7 classes, 2,800 balanced samples), all classifiers achieved near-ceiling performance. XGBoost improved slightly from 0.995 to 0.998 accuracy, while SVM and RF maintained approximately 0.999 and 0.998 accuracy, respectively. These results suggest that the TESS corpus is highly separable even without aggressive feature reduction, leaving limited room for measurable improvement.

Larger improvements were observed on more challenging or smaller datasets. On SAVEE (7 classes, 480 samples), XGBoost improved from 0.708 to 0.726 accuracy, with macro-F1 increasing from 0.680 to 0.696. SVM also benefited slightly, improving from 0.761 to 0.764 accuracy and from 0.744 to 0.747 macro-F1. Random Forest demonstrated moderate gains, with accuracy increasing from 0.708 to 0.715 and macro-F1 from 0.683 to 0.692.

On RAVDESS (7 classes, 1,440 samples), XGBoost again showed consistent improvement, increasing accuracy from 0.744 to 0.752 and macro-F1 from 0.728 to 0.737. However, SVM experienced a slight performance decrease after feature selection, with accuracy dropping from 0.834 to 0.821 and macro-F1 from 0.826 to 0.813, suggesting that SVM may rely more heavily on the complete feature representation for this corpus. Random Forest showed marginal reductions as well, although overall trends remained relatively stable.

Overall, the results indicate that the proposed feature selection framework generalizes across multiple classifiers while providing the most consistent benefits when combined with XGBoost. The improvements are particularly noticeable in datasets with limited sample sizes or higher class confusability, whereas gains remain minimal in highly separable datasets where baseline performance is already saturated.

The class-wise evaluation results in Table 3 demonstrate the effectiveness of the proposed feature selection framework when combined with the XGBoost classifier across four benchmark speech emotion recognition datasets. On the CREMA-D dataset, the model shows relatively balanced performance, with the highest F1-score observed for angry (0.72), followed by sad (0.61) and neutral (0.59). However, the model exhibits comparatively lower performance for disgust (0.53) and fear (0.54), indicating that these emotions remain more challenging to distinguish due to their acoustic similarity with other emotional states. Among the evaluated classifiers, XGBoost provided the most consistent overall improvements after feature selection and therefore serves as the primary model for detailed class-wise analysis. For the RAVDESS dataset, the model achieves improved overall performance, with strong results for fear (F1 = 0.79) and surprise (F1 = 0.79), while neutral achieves the highest F1-score of 0.82 due to its very high recall (0.95). In contrast, sad shows relatively lower recall (0.44), resulting in a lower F1-score (0.54).

On the SAVEE dataset, the model demonstrates strong recognition capability, particularly for neutral (F1 = 0.86) and sad (F1 = 0.77), while disgust remains the most difficult class with the lowest F1-score (0.47). The higher recall for happy (0.78) also contributes to a competitive F1-score of 0.68. Finally, the TESS dataset shows near-perfect classification performance across all emotion categories, with F1-scores reaching 1.00 for most classes and 0.99 for surprise. This indicates that the proposed model generalizes extremely well on datasets with clearer emotional articulation and less acoustic variability. Overall, the results highlight the robustness of the proposed approach while also revealing that certain emotions, particularly disgust and fear, remain comparatively harder to recognize in more complex datasets.

The feature ranking analysis demonstrates that the proposed framework consistently prioritizes acoustically meaningful descriptors across datasets and selected feature subset sizes (K). Spectral and MFCC-based features were frequently selected in all corpora, while prosodic and temporal-dynamics descriptors became more prominent in RAVDESS and TESS. Notably, even in TESS, where baseline performance is near ceiling, the method still achieves marginal but consistent improvements, indicating that the selection process does not harm performance even in highly controlled settings.

Discussion

Effectiveness of explainability-guided feature selection

The experimental results demonstrate that explainability signals derived from SHAP values, when combined with permutation-based importance, can serve as an effective model-driven criterion for feature selection rather than merely a post-hoc interpretation mechanism. Across the four evaluated datasets, the proposed method consistently maintained or slightly improved classification performance while operating on substantially reduced feature subsets.

In the within-corpus setting, modest but consistent improvements were observed on CREMA-D and SAVEE, while improvements on RAVDESS remained relatively modest. On the TESS dataset, which already exhibits near-perfect separability, the proposed feature selection framework still achieved marginal improvements despite the baseline performance being close to the theoretical ceiling. These results indicate that the explainability-guided ranking successfully identifies acoustically informative features while removing redundant or weakly contributing descriptors.

Importantly, the selected features show strong alignment with well-known emotion-relevant acoustic characteristics. Frequently selected descriptors include pitch statistics, MFCC-based spectral features, intensity measures, and voice-quality indicators such as harmonic-to-noise ratio. This confirms that the proposed framework preserves interpretable acoustic cues rather than selecting features arbitrarily.

These observations indicate that the contribution extends beyond simple dimensionality reduction. Instead, the proposed framework reframes feature selection as an explainability-guided optimization process embedded directly within the SER pipeline.

Multi-model evaluation and feature generalization

While the within-corpus experiments demonstrate consistent performance improvements for XGBoost, the additional experiments using Support Vector Machine (SVM) and Random Forest (RF) provide further insight into the generalizability of the proposed feature selection framework across different classifiers.

The results indicate that the proposed feature selection consistently improves XGBoost performance across all evaluated datasets. In particular, noticeable gains are observed on smaller or more challenging datasets such as SAVEE and RAVDESS, suggesting that removing redundant and less informative acoustic features can improve discriminative capability and reduce overfitting.

For SVM, the impact of feature selection is more dataset-dependent. Slight improvements are observed in SAVEE, while performance on RAVDESS decreases marginally after feature reduction. This behavior suggests that SVM may rely more strongly on the complete high-dimensional feature representation in certain datasets. Random Forest demonstrates modest but generally stable improvements following feature selection, although its overall performance remains lower than XGBoost and SVM.

The consistently high performance observed on TESS across all classifiers indicates that the dataset is highly separable and contains relatively clear emotional distinctions, leaving limited room for measurable improvement through feature reduction. In contrast, larger improvements on CREMA-D and SAVEE suggest that the proposed framework is particularly beneficial in datasets with higher acoustic variability or limited sample sizes.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that the proposed explainability-driven feature selection framework generalizes across multiple learning paradigms while providing the most stable and consistent benefits when combined with XGBoost. These results further support the effectiveness of combining SHAP attribution, permutation importance, and redundancy-aware ranking for compact and interpretable SER systems.

Conclusion

This study proposed an explainability-guided feature selection framework for speech emotion recognition that integrates SHAP-based feature attribution with permutation importance to identify informative acoustic descriptors. Unlike conventional dimensionality reduction methods such as principal component analysis, the proposed approach retains the original feature space and therefore preserves interpretability while enabling model-driven feature selection.

Experimental evaluation was conducted on four widely used emotional speech datasets (CREMA-D, RAVDESS, SAVEE, and TESS) under within-corpus evaluation settings. The results show that the proposed framework can maintain or improve classification performance while reducing the number of handcrafted acoustic features. Across multiple classifiers, including XGBoost, SVM, and RF, the selected-feature models achieved comparable or improved Accuracy, Macro-F1, and UAR relative to full-feature baselines, demonstrating classifier-independent effectiveness of the proposed framework. The most consistent improvements were observed with XGBoost, particularly on CREMA-D, SAVEE, and TESS. The selected feature subsets consistently retained acoustically meaningful descriptors, including MFCC-based spectral features, pitch statistics, intensity measures, and voice-quality indicators.

Overall, the findings demonstrate that explainability-guided feature selection offers a transparent and effective alternative to traditional dimensionality reduction approaches, enabling compact and interpretable SER models without sacrificing predictive performance.

Future work will investigate integrating the proposed framework with deep neural speech representations and adaptive model selection strategies to further improve robustness and applicability in modern SER systems.
