1.
Introduction

The main motivation of this paper (as well as
[
BR
]
) is to understand
quantum group symmetries
, a generalization of the classical work on group actions on polynomial rings. In other words, we are interested in Hopf algebras which (co)act on Artin–Schelter (AS) regular algebras. The most fundamental examples of Hopf algebras are group algebras (which act by automorphisms) and universal enveloping algebras of Lie algebras (which act by derivations). Together, these two classes of Hopf algebras essentially encompass all actions by cocommutative Hopf algebras, by the Cartier–Kostant–Gabriel theorem. Much work has been done on actions by finite-dimensional cocommutative (or more generally, semisimple) Hopf algebras, although many open questions remain. See, for example,
[
CW93
,
KKZ10
,
EW14
,
CWZ14
,
CWWZ14
,
CKWZ16
]
and references therein.

In this paper, we study actions by dual group algebras, which are commutative Hopf algebras. If
G
is a finite group, an action by the dual group algebra
(\Bbbk G)^{*}
on a
\Bbbk
-algebra
A
is equivalent to a
G
-grading on
A

[
Mon93
, Example 1.6.7]
, where
\Bbbk
is a field. The invariant ring under the
(\Bbbk G)^{*}
-action is then easily seen to be
A_{e}
, the identity component of
A
under the
G
-grading. We study dual group actions by viewing a
(\Bbbk G)^{*}
-action as a
coaction
by
\Bbbk G
(see Definition
2.3
).

Of main interest are coactions by noncommutative Hopf algebras, called
genuine
coactions in
[
EGMW17
]
; see also
[
KO24
]
. Indeed, a Hopf algebra is said to admit
no quantum symmetry
when it does not admit inner-faithful coactions by noncommutative Hopf algebras. Coactions on two-dimensional AS regular algebras are reasonably well understood – see, for example,
[
WW16
,
RVdB17b
]
. Indeed, the classification of
\Bbbk G
-coactions (or
G
-gradings) in this case is complete: the only two-dimensional AS regular algebra which admits an inner-faithful coaction by the group algebra of a nonabelian group is the quantum plane
\Bbbk\left\langle x,y\right\rangle/(xy+yx)
, graded by the nonabelian group
\Gamma\coloneqq\left\langle f,g\mid f^{2}=g^{2}\right\rangle

[
Cra24
, Theorem 1.1]
. Some work has been done on coactions on higher-dimensional AS regular algebras
[
KKZ17
,
GKMV24
]
, but no such classification exists in dimensions three and higher.

In this paper, we focus on three-dimensional AS regular algebras, with the goal of generalizing Crawford’s result classifying group gradings on two-dimensional AS regular algebras. One advantage of our approach through coactions it that we can use Manin’s
universal coacting Hopf algebra
construction
[
Man88
]
: given a quadratic algebra
A
, there is a Hopf algebra
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}(A)
which (right) coacts on it in a universal way, preserving the
\mathbb{N}
-grading of
A
(see Definition
2.5
). If we assume that
A
has a
faithful

G
-grading (see Definition
2.1
) which refines its
\mathbb{N}
-grading, then
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}(A)
surjects onto
\Bbbk G
. Therefore, we can classify all the group gradings of
A
by classifying the group algebras which arise as quotients of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}(A)
.

Notice that a Hopf algebra is a group algebra if and only if it is cocommutative and is spanned by its group-like elements. Given this observation, our strategy is as follows.

(1)

Classify the cocommutative Hopf quotients of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}(A)
.

(2)

Given a cocommutative Hopf quotient of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}(A)
, determine when it is spanned by its group-like elements.

Step (
2
) is straightforward, so the main difficulty lies in step (
1
). To find cocommutative quotients of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}(A)
, we use the well-known fact that a cocommutative Hopf algebra is involutive, meaning its antipode has order two. Therefore, step (
1
) is split into two parts: first, we
involutize

\underline{\operatorname{aut}}(A)
by taking the quotient by the Hopf ideal
(S^{2}(x)-x\mid x\in\underline{\operatorname{aut}}(A))
(see Definition
5.1
), and then we find cocommutative quotients of the involutization of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}(A)
.

For simplicity, we restrict our attention to the
(one-parameter) skew polynomial rings
over an algebraically closed field
\Bbbk
of characteristic zero:

A_{q}=A_{q}(n)\coloneqq\frac{\Bbbk\left\langle x_{1},\dots,x_{n}\right\rangle}{(x_{j}x_{i}-qx_{i}x_{j}\mid i<j)},

where
q\in\Bbbk^{*}
. First, we describe the universal coacting Hopf algebra
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}(A_{q})
. Although this Hopf algebra has been studied before, it is difficult to find the explicit presentation of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}(A_{q})
that we need in the literature for arbitrary
q\in\Bbbk^{*}
and an arbitrary number of variables. See, for example,
[
Man88
, Example 9.6]
,
[
CWZ14
, Lemma 5.1]
,
[
RVdB17a
, Appendix A]
,
[
RVdB17b
]
, and
[
CWW19
]
. Therefore, our first main result is a description of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}(A_{q})
. In the theorem below, the
quantum determinant
of a

matrix

is defined by

\operatorname{det}_{q}(P)\coloneqq\sum_{\sigma\in S_{k}}(-q)^{-I(\sigma)}p_{1\sigma(1)}\dots p_{k\sigma(k)},

where

(Theorem

We then proceed to classify the cocommutative Hopf algebras which right coact on

Theorem 1.2

(Theorem

)
.

Let
q\in\Bbbk^{*}
. If
q\neq-1
, then the only cocommutative Hopf algebras which right coact on
A_{q}(2)

\Bbbk\mathbb{Z}^{2}
and

(see Definition

). If

, we get an additional coaction by (Hopf quotients of)

As a consequence of Theorem

, we recover Crawford’s classification of group gradings on two-variable skew polynomial rings
[

, Theorem 1.1]
. The formulas for the coactions are omitted here, as they are clear from the proof, which constructs each of these Hopf algebras coacting on

as an explicit quotient of the universal involutive coacting Hopf algebra
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}(A_{q}(2))
.

We then prove a similar result for three-variable skew polynomial rings. Note that we have to exclude the case
q=\pm 1
due to the computational difficulty of this situation.

Theorem 1.3

(Theorem
7.2
)
.

Let
q\in\Bbbk^{*}\setminus\{\pm 1\}
. Then the only cocommutative Hopf algebras which right coact on

inner-faithfully are the Hopf quotients of
\Bbbk\mathbb{Z}^{3},\mathcal{B}_{q^{\pm 1}}
, and
\mathcal{C}_{q^{\pm 1}}
(see Definition

). Consequently,

does not have a faithful grading by a nonabelian group.

It is also clear that any grading of the commutative polynomial ring

must be by a factor group of
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
, so the preceding result implies that only when

can

possibly be graded by a nonabelian group, similarly as for

. Conversely, gradings by nonabelian exist for

, because writing

as an Ore extension, one easily sees that

is graded by

.

In the upcoming paper
[

]
, we will study group gradings of more general regular algebras of dimension

by different methods, involving an explicit analysis of superpotentials. This will allow us to show that the only gradings of

by nonabelian groups

. The methods of

Preliminaries

We start by outlining our goals and recalling the required definitions and algebras of interest for this paper.

2.1.
Group gradings and coactions of group algebras

First, we recall the definition of a group grading.

Definition 2.1
.

We say that a
\Bbbk
-algebra

is

by a group

A
can be decomposed into abelian groups as

A=\bigoplus_{g\in G}A_{g},\quad\text{where }A_{g}A_{h}\subseteq A_{gh},

for all

.

If
A
is
G
-graded, we say that the
G
-grading is
faithful
if
\{g\in G\mid A_{g}\neq 0\}
generates the group
G
.

We will always assume that group gradings are faithful, since this means that the algebra cannot be graded by a proper subgroup of the grading group.

Remark 2.2

The notion of a faithful grading from Definition

matches the terminology used in
[
Dăs08
]
. However, other authors prefer to call such a grading
connected

[

, Proposition 2.4]

It is well-known that a grading of a

Since we always assume that group gradings are faithful, this should correspond to some property of the right coaction of the group algebra defined by the grading. This property is

Definition 2.4

Let

\rho(M)\not\subseteq M\otimes H^{\prime}

In other words, we are interested in inner-faithful coactions of group algebras.

2.2.
Grading skew polynomial rings using Manin’s universal coacting Hopf algebra

Fix
n\geq 2
and
q\in\Bbbk^{*}
. Our main goal is to classify (faithful) group gradings of the skew polynomial ring

A_{q}(n)\coloneqq\frac{\Bbbk\left\langle x_{1},\ldots,x_{n}\right\rangle}{(x_{j}x_{i}-qx_{i}x_{j}\mid j>i)}.

We usually drop the
(n)
if the value of
n
is clear and simply write
A_{q}
instead of
A_{q}(n)
.

We will classify the gradings by classifying the possible (inner-faithful) coactions of group algebras on
A_{q}
. We only consider gradings of
A_{q}
which are compatible with its natural
\mathbb{N}
-grading, where each
x_{i}
is homogeneous of degree 1. In terms of coactions, this means that we require the map
A_{q}\to A_{q}\otimes\Bbbk G
to respect the
\mathbb{N}
-grading of
A_{q}
. In other words,
x_{i}
maps to
\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}\otimes g_{ji}
for some
g_{ji}\in\Bbbk G
.

To achieve our classification, we will use the following construction: there is a universal right coacting Hopf algebra for
A_{q}
, denoted
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})

[
Man88
]
. We define this Hopf algebra now, but we postpone its explicit construction to Sections
3
and
4
.

Definition 2.5
.

A
\Bbbk
-algebra

is

A=TV/(R)
, where

is a finite-dimensional vector space and

. A quadratic algebra

is automatically

-graded as
A=\bigoplus_{k\in\mathbb{N}}A_{k}
, where
A_{k}
is the image of

.

Given a quadratic algebra

, the
universal right coacting Hopf algebra
of

, denoted
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A)
, is the Hopf algebra which right coacts on
A
via a map
\rho\colon A\to A\otimes\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A)
, compatible with its natural

-grading, satisfying the following universal property: if

via a map

, also compatible with its natural

-grading, then there is a unique Hopf algebra homomorphism

2.3.
Observations about group algebras

A Hopf algebra
H
is group algebra if and only if
H
is cocommutative and is spanned by its group-like elements. Given this observation, our strategy to achieve the classification of group gradings of
A_{q}
is the following:

(1)

Classify Hopf ideals
I
of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
such that
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})/I
is cocommutative.

(2)

For
I
as above, determine when
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})/I
is spanned by its group-like elements.

To aid with (
1
), we use the well-known property that cocommutative Hopf algebras are involutive (defined below).

Definition 2.6
.

A Hopf algebra
H
is
involutive
if
S_{H}^{2}=\id_{H}
.

Proposition 2.7

(
[
Mon93
, Corollary 1.5.12]
)
.

Cocommutative Hopf algebras are involutive.

Therefore, we make the following observation: let
I
be a Hopf ideal of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
such that
H\coloneqq\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})/I
is cocommutative. Then it must be the case that
S_{H}^{2}=\id_{H}
. Hence, to find cocommutative quotients of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
, we first “involutize”
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
(see Definition
5.1
), and then we further specialize to cocommutative quotients. Once we have found a cocommutative quotient
H
of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
, step (
2
) is straightforward.

2.4.
Maximal cocommutative quotients

A Hopf algebra has both a universal commutative quotient (its
abelianization
) and a universal involutive quotient (its
involutization
). However, Hopf algebras do not have universal cocommutative quotients, making the study of cocommutative quotients of Hopf algebras more difficult, but also richer, than studying commutative or involutive quotients. Although universal cocommutative quotients do not exist, we now prove that Hopf algebras still have
maximal
cocommutative quotients.

Proposition 2.8
.

Let
H
be a Hopf algebra and let
\varphi\colon H\to C
be a Hopf algebra homomorphism, where
C
is cocommutative. Then there exists a cocommutative quotient
K=H/I
of
H
(where
I
is a Hopf ideal of
H
) and a Hopf algebra homomorphism
\psi\colon K\to C
such that the diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces K}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces C}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\psi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\pi}
\scriptstyle{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces\varphi}

commutes, and the cocommutative quotient

is

is another Hopf ideal of

In other words, Proposition

Therefore, one can study cocommutative quotients of a Hopf algebra by classifying the maximal ones. At first, this seems like a potentially impossible task, but we will see later in this paper that we can fully classify the maximal cocommutative quotients of

Let

Proof.

Let

\mathcal{B}_{2}

\mathcal{B}_{3}
be bases for

,
\varepsilon(I)=0
, and
S(I)\subseteq I
by construction, so it remains to check that
\Delta(I)\subseteq H\otimes I+I\otimes H
.

Fix
x\in I
, and let
V
be a finite-dimensional subspace of
H
such that
\Delta(x)\in V\otimes V
. Letting
J\in X
, we have
x\in J
by definition, and thus

\Delta(x)\in(H\otimes J+J\otimes H)\cap(V\otimes V).

We claim that
(H\otimes J+J\otimes H)\cap(V\otimes V)=V\otimes(J\cap V)+(J\cap V)\otimes V
, which we will prove by using Lemma
2.9
. To that end, we now construct a basis for
H\otimes H
which satisfies the hypotheses of Lemma
2.9
. Let
\mathcal{B}_{0}
be a basis for
J\cap V
, and let
\mathcal{B}_{1}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}
be bases of
V
and
J
, respectively, which contain
\mathcal{B}_{0}
. Finally, we let
\mathcal{B}
be a basis of
H
containing
\mathcal{B}_{1}\cup\mathcal{B}_{2}
.

Note that
\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}=\{u\otimes v\mid u,v\in\mathcal{B}\}
is a basis of
H\otimes H
. Furthermore,
\mathcal{B}_{1}\otimes\mathcal{B}_{1}
is a basis of
V\otimes V
, while
\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}_{2}
and
\mathcal{B}_{2}\otimes\mathcal{B}
are bases of
H\otimes J
and
J\otimes H
, respectively. Since all of these are subsets of
\mathcal{B}\otimes\mathcal{B}
, it follows by Lemma
2.9
that
(H\otimes J+J\otimes H)\cap(V\otimes V)=V\otimes(J\cap V)+(J\cap V)\otimes V
, as claimed.

Since
V
is finite-dimensional, there exists
\mathcal{J}\in X
such that
J\cap V=\mathcal{J}\cap V
for all
J\in X
with
J\subseteq\mathcal{J}
. Note that we then have
I\cap V=\bigcap_{J\in X}J\cap V=\mathcal{J}\cap V
. It follows that

\Delta(x)\in\bigcap_{J\in X}\Big(V\otimes(J\cap V)+(J\cap V)\otimes V\Big)=V\otimes(\mathcal{J}\cap V)+(\mathcal{J}\cap V)\otimes V\\
=V\otimes(I\cap V)+(I\cap V)\otimes V\subseteq H\otimes I+I\otimes H.

In other words, we have
\Delta(x)\in H\otimes I+I\otimes H
, and thus
\Delta(I)\subseteq H\otimes I+I\otimes H
. Therefore,
I
is a Hopf ideal, as claimed.

To conclude the proof, we must show that
H/I
is cocommutative. Write
\Delta^{\mathrm{op}}\coloneqq\tau\circ\Delta
for the opposite comultiplication on
H
, where
\tau\colon H\otimes H\to H\otimes H
is the swap map
\tau(u\otimes v)=v\otimes u
. Since
H/J
is cocommutative for all
J\in X
, we have

\Delta(h)-\Delta^{\mathrm{op}}(h)\in\bigcap_{J\in X}\big(H\otimes J+J\otimes H\Big)

for all
h\in H
. Proceeding as above, we can show that
\Delta(h)-\Delta^{\mathrm{op}}(h)\in H\otimes I+I\otimes H
, which proves that
H/I
is cocommutative. The result follows.
∎

3.
The universal Hopf algebra of a Frobenius algebra

Instead of directly describing the construction of the universal coacting Hopf algebra
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
, it is easier to construct
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A_{q}^{!})
, where
A_{q}^{!}
is the
quadratic dual
of
A_{q}
. It is well-known that
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A_{q}^{!})
and
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
are isomorphic
[
Man88
, Theorem 6.10]
.

Definition 3.1
.

The
quadratic dual
of a quadratic algebra
A
, denoted
A^{!}
, is defined by

A^{!}\coloneqq TV^{*}/(R^{\perp}),

where
R^{\perp}\subseteq V^{*}\otimes V^{*}
consists of the elements of
V^{*}\otimes V^{*}
vanishing on
R
, in other words,

R^{\perp}\coloneqq\{\alpha\in V^{*}\otimes V^{*}\mid\alpha(R)=0\}.

The quadratic dual of
A_{q}
is

A^{!}_{q}\coloneqq\frac{\Bbbk\left\langle t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\right\rangle}{(t_{i}^{2},t_{j}t_{i}+q^{-1}t_{i}t_{j}\mid j>i)},

which is a
quantum Grassmann algebra
of dimension
n
, a special type of
Frobenius algebra
. We define these notions next.

Definition 3.2
.

A quadratic algebra
A
is said to be a
Frobenius algebra
of dimension
n
if

(1)

\dim(A_{n})=1
.

(2)

A_{k}=0
for
k>n
.

(3)

For all
k
, the multiplication map
A_{k}\otimes A_{n-k}\to A_{n}
is a perfect duality.

The algebra
A
is called a
quantum Grassmann algebra
if, in addition,

(4)

\dim A_{k}=\binom{n}{k}
.

Let
A=TV/(R)
be a Frobenius algebra of dimension
n
. We now describe the construction of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)
, which will later allow us to construct
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
. The content of this section is well-known (see, for example,
[
Man88
, Chapter 9]
and
[
CWZ14
, Section 3]
), but we still opt to present this construction in detail to fix our notation and to maximize clarity.

Before we begin describing
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)
, we mention the properties that
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)
must satisfy: since
A
is a graded left
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)
-comodule algebra via a map
\lambda\colon A\to\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)\otimes A
, we have:

(1)

\lambda
is co-associative and co-unital.

(2)

\lambda
is a
\Bbbk
-algebra homomorphism.

(3)

\lambda
respects the natural
\mathbb{N}
-grading of
A
, meaning
\lambda(A_{1})\subseteq\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)\otimes A_{1}
.

Furthermore,
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)
is the universal Hopf algebra satisfying the above properties. This means that we should only impose relations on
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)
which are forced by the above conditions. Doing so will completely describe
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)
.

We now begin the construction of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)
. First, since
\dim(A_{n})=1
, there exists
D\in\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)
such that
\lambda(w)=D\otimes w
for all
w\in A_{n}
. The element
D\in\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)
is called the
quantum determinant
of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)
. It is easy to see that
D
is group-like.

Lemma 3.3
.

The quantum determinant
D
is group-like. Consequently,
D
has an inverse
D^{-1}\in\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)
.

Proof.

By the co-associativity of
\lambda
, we have
\Delta(D)\otimes w=D\otimes\lambda(w)
for all
w\in A_{n}
. Therefore,

\Delta(D)\otimes w=D\otimes D\otimes w,

so
\Delta(D)=D\otimes D
. Similarly, the co-unitality of
\lambda
implies that
\varepsilon(D)w=w
, so
\varepsilon(D)=1
. This concludes the proof.
∎

Using the perfect duality between
A_{k}
and
A_{n-k}
, we introduce the following notation.

Notation 3.4
.

Fix
w\in A_{n}\setminus\{0\}
. For each
k,\ell\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, choose bases
\{t_{i}^{(k)}\}
of
A_{k}
and
\{s_{i}^{(\ell)}\}
of
A_{\ell}
such that

(3.1)

t_{i}^{(k)}s_{j}^{(n-k)}=\delta_{ij}w.

Letting
d_{k}\coloneqq\dim(A_{k})
, we define elements
x_{ij}^{(k)},y_{ij}^{(k)}\in\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)
such that

(3.2)

\lambda(t_{i}^{(k)})=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}}x_{ij}^{(k)}\otimes t_{j}^{(k)},\quad\lambda(s_{i}^{(k)})=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}}y_{ij}^{(k)}\otimes s_{j}^{(k)},

and let
X^{(k)}\coloneqq(x_{ij}^{(k)})
and
Y^{(k)}\coloneqq(y_{ij}^{(k)})
in
M_{d_{k}}(A)
.

We now describe how the comultiplication and counit of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)
interact with the elements
x_{ij}^{(k)}
and
y_{ij}^{(k)}
defined in Notation
3.4
.

Lemma 3.5
.

For all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
i,j\in\{1,\dots,d_{k}\}
, we have

\Delta(x_{ij}^{(k)})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d_{k}}x_{i\ell}^{(k)}\otimes x_{\ell j}^{(k)},\quad\Delta(y_{ij}^{(k)})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d_{k}}y_{i\ell}^{(k)}\otimes y_{\ell j}^{(k)},

and
\varepsilon(x_{ij}^{(k)})=\varepsilon(y_{ij}^{(k)})=\delta_{ij}
.

Proof.

The co-associativity of
\lambda
implies that

\sum_{j,\ell=1}^{d_{k}}x_{ij}^{(k)}\otimes x_{j\ell}^{(k)}\otimes t_{\ell}^{(k)}=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}}x_{ij}^{(k)}\otimes\lambda(t_{j}^{(k)})=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}}\Delta(x_{ij}^{(k)})\otimes t_{j}^{(k)}.

Swapping
j
and
\ell
in the first summation, we get

\sum_{j,\ell=1}^{d_{k}}x_{i\ell}^{(k)}\otimes x_{\ell j}^{(k)}\otimes t_{j}^{(k)}=\sum_{j=1}^{d_{k}}\Delta(x_{ij}^{(k)})\otimes t_{j}^{(k)},

and thus

\Delta(x_{ij}^{(k)})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d_{k}}x_{i\ell}^{(k)}\otimes x_{\ell j}^{(k)}

for all
i,j
. By the same argument, we also have
\Delta(y_{ij}^{(k)})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d_{k}}y_{i\ell}^{(k)}\otimes y_{\ell j}^{(k)}
for all
i,j
. The counitality of
\lambda
implies that

\sum_{j}\varepsilon(x_{ij}^{(k)})t_{j}^{(k)}=t_{i}^{(k)},\quad\sum_{j}\varepsilon(y_{ij}^{(k)})s_{j}^{(k)}=s_{i}^{(\ell)},

by (
3.2
), from which it follows that
\varepsilon(x_{ij}^{(k)})=\varepsilon(y_{ij}^{(k)})=\delta_{ij}
.
∎

Having analyzed the coalgebra structure of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)
, we now move on to the antipode. For a matrix
M=(m_{ij})\in M_{d}(\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A))
, define
S(M)
to be the matrix whose
(i,j)
-entry is
S(m_{ij})
.

Lemma 3.6
.

For all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, the matrices
X^{(k)}
and
Y^{(k)}
are invertible, and we have
S(X^{(k)})=(X^{(k)})^{-1}
and
S(Y^{(k)})=(Y^{(k)})^{-1}
.

Proof.

We have

(3.3)

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell}S(x_{i\ell}^{(k)})x_{\ell j}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}x_{i\ell}^{(k)}S(x_{\ell j}^{(k)})=\varepsilon(x_{ij}^{(k)})=\delta_{ij},

\displaystyle\sum_{\ell}S(y_{i\ell}^{(k)})y_{\ell j}^{(k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell}y_{i\ell}^{(k)}S(y_{\ell j}^{(k)})=\varepsilon(y_{ij}^{(k)})=\delta_{ij}.

Then (
3.3
) gives

S(X^{(k)})X^{(k)}=X^{(k)}S(X^{(k)})=I_{d_{k}},\quad S(Y^{(k)})Y^{(k)}=Y^{(k)}S(Y^{(k)})=I_{d_{k}},

in other words,
X^{(k)}
and
Y^{(k)}
are invertible, and we have
S(X^{(k)})=(X^{(k)})^{-1}
and
S(Y^{(k)})=(Y^{(k)})^{-1}
.
∎

As we show next, the matrices
X^{(k)}
and
Y^{(n-k)}
are inverse to each other, up to multiplication by
D^{-1}
and transposition.

Proposition 3.7
.

For all
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, we have
X^{(k)}(Y^{(n-k)})^{\mathsf{T}}=DI_{n}
. Consequently,
(X^{(k)})^{-1}=(Y^{(n-k)})^{\mathsf{T}}D^{-1}
, and

S(x_{ij}^{(k)})=y_{ji}^{(n-k)}D^{-1}

for all
i,j\in\{1,\dots,d_{k}\}
.

Proof.

It follows from (
3.1
) that

(3.4)

\lambda(t_{i}^{(k)}s_{j}^{(n-k)})=\delta_{ij}\lambda(w)=\delta_{ij}D\otimes w.

On the other hand,
\lambda
is an algebra homomorphism, so we get

(3.5)

\displaystyle\lambda(t_{i}^{(k)}s_{j}^{(n-k)})

\displaystyle=\lambda(t_{i}^{(k)})\lambda(s_{j}^{(n-k)})=\sum_{a,b=1}^{d_{k}}x_{ia}^{(k)}y_{jb}^{(n-k)}\otimes t_{a}^{(k)}s_{b}^{(n-k)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{a,b=1}^{d_{k}}\delta_{ab}x_{ia}^{(k)}y_{jb}^{(n-k)}\otimes w\quad(\text{by \eqref{eq:duality of a with b}})

\displaystyle=\sum_{\ell=1}^{d_{k}}x_{i\ell}^{(k)}y_{j\ell}^{(n-k)}\otimes w.

Combining (
3.4
) and (
3.5
), we deduce that
\sum_{\ell=1}^{d_{k}}x_{i\ell}^{(k)}y_{j\ell}^{(n-k)}=\delta_{ij}D
. Therefore,

X^{(k)}(Y^{(n-k)})^{\mathsf{T}}=DI_{d_{k}}

for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
. By Lemma
3.6
, it follows that
S(X^{(k)})=(X^{(k)})^{-1}=(Y^{(n-k)})^{\mathsf{T}}D^{-1}
. We conclude that
S(x_{ij}^{(k)})=y_{ji}^{(n-k)}D^{-1}
, which finishes the proof.
∎

We summarize our findings in the next result.

Proposition 3.8
.

The Hopf algebra
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)
contains elements
x_{ij}=x_{ij}^{(1)}
for
i,j\in\{1,\dots,n\}
and
D^{\pm 1}
, subject to the relations which make
\lambda
into an algebra homomorphism, and the relations

X^{(k)}(Y^{(n-k)})^{\mathsf{T}}D^{-1}=(Y^{(n-k)})^{\mathsf{T}}D^{-1}X^{(k)}=I_{d_{k}},

for all
k\in\{1,\ldots,n\}
, where we use the notation from Notation
3.4
.

The comultiplication and counit on the elements
x_{ij}
is given by

\Delta(x_{ij})=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{ik}\otimes x_{kj},\quad\varepsilon(x_{ij})=\delta_{ij}.

The antipode on the elements
x_{ij}
is given by
S(x_{ij})=y_{ji}^{(n-1)}D^{-1}
.

Proof.

By the discussion above, the relations and the formulas for the comultiplication, counit, and antipode given in the statement are all necessary to have a well-defined left coaction on
A
.
∎

Note that we do not claim that the relations in Proposition
3.8
are sufficient to define a Hopf algebra, but they are certainly necessary. In general, more generators and relations may be required, which can be obtained via Manin’s
Hopf envelope
construction
[
Man88
, Chapter 8]
.
We work this out in detail in the next section, but only for the special case of the algebras
A_{q}^{!}
.

4.
Construction of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})

As mentioned before, we can construct
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
by describing
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A_{q}^{!})
using the discussion from Section
3
. This is because
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})\cong\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A_{q}^{!})
, by the following well-known result.

Proposition 4.1

(
[
Man88
, Theorem 6.10]
,
[
HNU+24
, Lemma 2.1.5(3)]
)
.

Let
A
be a quadratic algebra. Then
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A)\cong\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A^{!})
.

We now proceed to construct the Hopf algebra
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A_{q}^{!})\cong\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
. This Hopf algebra has been studied before, but it is difficult to find the explicit presentation of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q}(n))
we want in the literature, in particular for arbitrary
q\in\Bbbk^{*}
and arbitrary number of variables
n
. For example,
[
RVdB17a
, Appendix A]
describes the Hopf algebra
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{1}(n))
for arbitrary
n
, while
[
CWZ14
, Lemma 5.1]
describes
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q}(2))
for arbitrary
q\in\Bbbk^{*}
. See also
[
Man88
, Example 9.6]
,
[
RVdB17b
]
, and
[
CWW19
]
.

We keep the notation from Notation
3.4
with

A=A_{q}^{!}=\frac{\Bbbk\left\langle t_{1},\ldots,t_{n}\right\rangle}{(t_{i}^{2},t_{j}t_{i}+q^{-1}t_{i}t_{j}\mid j>i)},

where we make the following choices.

Notation 4.2
.

Define
w\coloneqq t_{1}\dots t_{n}
. For
k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, define

\mathbb{I}_{k}\coloneqq\{\mathbf{r}=(r_{1},\dots,r_{k})\in\mathbb{Z}^{k}\mid 1\leq r_{1}<r_{2}<\dots<r_{k}\leq n\}.

Note that
\mathbb{I}_{1}=\{1,\dots,n\}
.
Given
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{I}_{k}
, we define
t_{\mathbf{r}}\coloneqq t_{r_{1}}t_{r_{2}}\dots t_{r_{k}}
, so that
\{t_{\mathbf{r}}\}
is a
\Bbbk
-basis of
(A^{!}_{q})_{k}
indexed by
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{I}_{k}
.

For
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{I}_{k}
, let
\widehat{\mathbf{r}}
be the element of
\mathbb{I}_{n-k}
obtained by removing
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}
from
(1,\dots,n)
, and let
\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}\in S_{n}
be the permutation

\sigma_{\mathbf{r}}\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}1&\dots&k&k+1&\dots&n\\
r_{1}&\dots&r_{k}&\widehat{r}_{1}&\dots&\widehat{r}_{n-k}\end{pmatrix}.

For all
\mathbf{r}\in\mathbb{I}_{k}
, we have

\lambda(t_{\mathbf{r}})=\sum_{\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{I}_{k}}x_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{c}}\otimes t_{\mathbf{c}}.

for some elements
x_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{c}}\in\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{\ell}(A)
. Note that we have dropped the superscript
(k)
from
t_{\mathbf{c}}
and
x_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{c}}
in this equation, since it is implied by the fact that
\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{I}_{k}
. We will continue to drop the superscript below for variables indexed by
\mathbb{I}_{k}
, unless it is needed for clarity.

We introduce quantum determinants and quantum minors, which will be important for our presentation of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
.

Definition 4.3
.

Let
S
and
T
be subsets of
\{1,\dots,n\}
for some
n
. Given a function
f\colon S\to T
, we define its
inversion set
as

\Inv(f)\coloneqq\{(i,j)\in S\times S\mid i<j\text{ and }f(i)>f(j)\}.

The
inversion number
of
f
is
I(f)\coloneqq|\Inv(f)|
. Given a
k\times k
matrix
P=(p_{ij})\in M_{n}(R)
over some
\Bbbk
-algebra
R
, we define the
quantum determinant
of
P
as follows:

\operatorname{det}_{q}(P)\coloneqq\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}(-q)^{-I(\sigma)}p_{1\sigma(1)}\dots p_{n\sigma(n)}.

Define
X\coloneqq(x_{ij})\in M_{n}(\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q}))
. Of course,
X=X^{(1)}
from Notation
3.4
. Given
\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{I}_{k}
, we define
X_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{c}}
to be the
k\times k
sub-matrix of
X
using rows
r_{1},\dots,r_{k}
and columns
c_{1},\dots,c_{k}
. The quantum determinant
\operatorname{det}_{q}(X_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{c}})
is known as a
quantum minor
of
X
.

For example,

\operatorname{det}_{q}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}=ad-q^{-1}bc.

We also called the element
D
from the previous section a quantum determinant. The next result shows that there is no conflict of terminology.

Lemma 4.4
.

We have
D=\operatorname{det}_{q}(X)
, where
X=(x_{ij})\in M_{n}(\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q}))
.

Proof.

Recall that
D
is defined by the property that
\lambda(w)=D\otimes w
, where
w=t_{1}\dots t_{n}
. We have

\displaystyle\lambda(w)

\displaystyle=\lambda(t_{1}\dots t_{n})=\lambda(t_{1})\dots\lambda(t_{n})

\displaystyle=\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{1j}\otimes t_{j}\right)\dots\left(\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{nj}\otimes t_{j}\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}x_{1\sigma(1)}\dots x_{n\sigma(n)}\otimes t_{\sigma(1)}\dots t_{\sigma(n)}

\displaystyle=\sum_{\sigma\in S_{n}}(-q)^{-I(\sigma)}x_{1\sigma(1)}\dots x_{n\sigma(n)}\otimes t_{1}\dots t_{n}

\displaystyle=\operatorname{det}_{q}(X)\otimes w,

and therefore
D=\operatorname{det}_{q}(X)
.
∎

Next, we state the main result of this section, which fully describes the universal right coacting Hopf algebra
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
.

Theorem 4.5
.

The Hopf algebra
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
is generated by
x_{ij}
for
1\leq i,j\leq n
and
D^{-1}
, subject to the following relations:

(4.1a)

\displaystyle x_{ik}x_{ij}=qx_{ij}x_{ik}\quad(k>j),

(4.1b)

\displaystyle ad-q^{-1}bc=\operatorname{det}_{q}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}=da-qcb,

(4.1c)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-q)^{k-i}\operatorname{det}_{q}(X_{\hat{k}\hat{\imath}})D^{-1}x_{kj}=\delta_{ij},

(4.1d)

\displaystyle D^{-1}D=DD^{-1}=1,

for all
i,j,k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
, and all
2\times 2
sub-matrices
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}
of
X
, where
D\coloneqq\operatorname{det}_{q}(X)
is the quantum determinant of the matrix
X=(x_{ij})
. The Hopf structure of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
is given by

\displaystyle\Delta(x_{ij})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}x_{ik}\otimes x_{kj},

(4.2)

\displaystyle\varepsilon(x_{ij})

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij},

\displaystyle S(x_{ij})

\displaystyle=(-q)^{j-i}\operatorname{det}_{q}(X_{\hat{\jmath}\hat{\imath}})D^{-1}.

Remark 4.6
.

One way to prove Theorem
4.5
would be to take the presentation of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{1})
from
[
RVdB17a
, Appendix A]
and twist it by an appropriate
2
-cocycle
, as in
[
HNU+24
, Theorem 2.2.3]
. We opt for a more direct approach, as the presentation given in
[
RVdB17a
]
relies on a lot of technical machinery. Furthermore, our approach has the advantage that (
4.1
) excludes some unnecessary relations given in
[
RVdB17a
, Appendix A]
. In particular, Raedschelders and Van den Bergh give relations of the form

\sum_{\mathbf{m}\in\mathbb{I}_{k}}(-q)^{I(\sigma_{\mathbf{m}})-I(\sigma_{\mathbf{r}})}\operatorname{det}_{q}(X_{\widehat{\mathbf{m}}\widehat{\mathbf{r}}})D^{-1}\operatorname{det}_{q}(X_{\mathbf{m}\mathbf{c}})=\delta_{\mathbf{r}\mathbf{c}},

which are in fact consequences of (
4.1
). This is explained in more detail in Remark
4.11
.

We will use results from
[
CFRS14
]
to help prove Theorem
4.5
, so we recall some of their terminology next.

Definition 4.7
.

Let
A
be a
\Bbbk
-algebra, and let
q\in\Bbbk^{*}
. A
q
-Manin matrix in
A
is a matrix
P=(p_{ij})\in M_{n}(A)
whose entries satisfy
p_{ik}p_{ij}=qp_{ij}p_{ik}
for all
i,j,k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
with
j<k
, and

ad-q^{-1}bc=da-qcb

for all
2\times 2
sub-matrices
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\

Let

Throughout the rest of the proof, if

5.
Involutization of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})

Having described the Hopf algebra
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
, we now move on to step (
1
) of the method outlined in Subsection
2.3
. In other words, we begin our classification of cocommutative quotients of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
. As mentioned in Subsection
2.3
, we start by “involutizing” the Hopf algebra
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
. It is easy to see that every Hopf algebra has a maximal involutive quotient, which we call its
involutization
. In other words, the ideal
(S_{H}^{2}(x)-x\mid x\in H)
of a Hopf algebra

is always a Hopf ideal.

Definition 5.1
.

If
H
is a Hopf algebra, we define its
involutization
as

H_{\mathrm{inv}}\coloneqq\frac{H}{(S_{H}^{2}(x)-x\mid x\in H)},

where
S_{H}^{2}=S_{H}\circ S_{H}
.

The main goal of this section is to explicitly compute the involutization of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
. To that end, we establish the following notation.

Notation 5.2
.

Let
H_{q}(n)\coloneqq\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q}(n))_{\mathrm{inv}}
. We often omit the
(n)
if the value of
n
is clear, and simply write
H_{q}
instead of
H_{q}(n)
. We write
h_{ij}
for the image of
x_{ij}
in
H_{q}
, and we abuse notation by also using
D
to denote the image of the quantum determinant of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
in
H_{q}
. Furthermore, we define
\mathcal{H}\coloneqq(h_{ij})\in M_{n}(H_{q})
.

Remark 5.3
.

The universal involutive coacting Hopf algebra of an Artin–Schelter regular algebra has been studied before – see, for example,
[
CWZ14
, Definition 2.9(b)]
and
[
WW16
, Definition 2.7(b)]
. The Hopf algebra
H_{q}
satisfies the following universal property: if
K
is an involutive Hopf algebra which right coacts on
A_{q}
, then there is a unique homomorphism of Hopf algebras
H_{q}\to K
such that the diagram
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A\otimes H_{q}}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces A\otimes K}

commutes.

The following result gives an explicit description of
H_{q}
by generators and relations.

Proposition 5.4
.

Let

. As an algebra,

is generated by

for

and

, subject to the following relations:

(5.1a)

\displaystyle h_{ik}h_{ij}=qh_{ij}h_{ik}\quad(k>j),

(5.1b)

\displaystyle ad-q^{-1}bc=\operatorname{det}_{q}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}=da-qcb,

(5.1c)

\displaystyle h_{ij}D=q^{2(j-i)}Dh_{ij},

(5.1d)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{n}(-q)^{i-k}\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{H}_{\hat{k}\hat{\imath}})h_{kj}=\delta_{ij}D,

(5.1e)

\displaystyle D^{-1}D=DD^{-1}=1,

for all
i,j,k\in\{1,\dots,n\}
,
\mathbf{r},\mathbf{c}\in\mathbb{I}_{k}
, and all
2\times 2
sub-matrices
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}
of
\mathcal{H}
, where
D\coloneqq\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{H})
is the quantum determinant of the matrix
\mathcal{H}=(h_{ij})
. The Hopf structure of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
is given by

\displaystyle\Delta(h_{ij})

\displaystyle=\sum_{k=1}^{n}h_{ik}\otimes h_{kj},

\displaystyle\varepsilon(h_{ij})

\displaystyle=\delta_{ij},

\displaystyle S(h_{ij})

\displaystyle=(-q)^{j-i}\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{H}_{\hat{\jmath}\hat{\imath}})D^{-1}.

To prove Proposition
5.4
, we simply have to compute
S^{2}(x_{ij})
for all
i,j
.

Lemma 5.5
.

We have
S^{2}(x_{ij})=q^{2(j-i)}Dx_{ij}D^{-1}
.

Proof.

We have
S(x_{ij})=y_{ji}D^{-1}
, by Proposition
3.7
. Since
S
is an anti-homomorphism and
S(D^{-1})=D
, we therefore have
S^{2}(x_{ij})=DS(y_{ji})
. It follows from Lemma
3.6
and the proof of Theorem
4.5
that
S(y_{ji})=q^{2(j-i)}x_{ij}D^{-1}
, which concludes the proof.
∎

Equipped with Lemma
5.5
, the proof of Proposition
5.4
follows easily.

Proof of Proposition
5.4
.

By Lemma
5.5
, we have

H_{q}=\frac{\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})}{(x_{ij}D-q^{2(j-i)}Dx_{ij})}.

The result now follows immediately from Theorem
4.5
.
∎

6.
The two-variable case

In this section, we consider the case where
n=2
: we classify group gradings of the quantum plane

A_{q}(2)=\frac{\Bbbk\left\langle x,y\right\rangle}{(yx-qxy)}.

Although this case has already been studied
[
Cra24
]
, it is still useful to work out the details of our approach using Manin’s universal quantum group, since this will lead to insights in the higher-dimensional cases. Furthermore, our method yields stronger results: on top of classifying group gradings of
A_{q}(2)
, we also classify all possible inner-faithful right coactions of cocommutative Hopf algebras on
A_{q}(2)
by classifying the maximal cocommutative quotients of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q}(2))
.

6.1.
Crawford’s result

Crawford proved that
A_{q}(2)
can only be graded by abelian groups unless
q=-1
.

Theorem 6.1

(
[
Cra24
, Theorem 1.1]
)
.

Suppose
A_{q}(2)
has a faithful
G
-grading which refines its natural
\mathbb{N}
-grading, where
G
is a nonabelian group. Then
q=-1
and
G
is a quotient of the group
\left\langle f,g\mid f^{2}=g^{2}\right\rangle
.

Given Theorem
6.1
, we make the following definition.

Notation 6.2
.

We let
\Gamma\coloneqq\left\langle f,g\mid f^{2}=g^{2}\right\rangle
.

6.2.
Cocommutative quotients of
H_{q}(2)

We now proceed to classify all cocommutative quotients of
H_{q}(2)
. First, we apply Proposition
5.4
to give an explicit presentation of
H_{q}(2)
. This presentation was also given in
[
CWZ14
, Lemma 5.3]
.

Corollary 6.3
.

Let
q\in\Bbbk^{*}
. The Hopf algebra
H_{q}(2)
has generators
h_{11},h_{12},h_{21},h_{22}
, and
D^{-1}
, subject to the relations

(6.1)

\begin{gathered}\begin{aligned} h_{22}h_{11}&=h_{11}h_{22},&h_{21}h_{12}&=q^{-2}h_{12}h_{21},\\
h_{12}h_{11}&=qh_{11}h_{12},&h_{22}h_{21}&=qh_{21}h_{22},\\
h_{21}h_{11}&=q^{-1}h_{11}h_{21},&h_{22}h_{12}&=q^{-1}h_{12}h_{22},\end{aligned}\\
DD^{-1}=1=D^{-1}D,\end{gathered}

where
D=h_{11}h_{22}-q^{-1}h_{12}h_{21}
is the quantum determinant of
H_{q}(2)
.

Proof.

The relations
h_{12}h_{11}=qh_{11}h_{12}
and
h_{22}h_{21}=qh_{21}h_{22}
come from (
5.1a
).

By (
5.1d
) with
i=1
and
j=2
, we have

h_{22}h_{12}-q^{-1}h_{12}h_{22}=0,

so we get the relation
h_{22}h_{12}=q^{-1}h_{12}h_{22}
. The relation
h_{21}h_{11}=q^{-1}h_{11}h_{21}
follows similarly.

Finally, by (
5.1d
) with
i=j=1
, we get

h_{22}h_{11}-q^{-1}h_{12}h_{21}=D.

Recalling that
D=h_{11}h_{22}-q^{-1}h_{12}h_{21}=h_{22}h_{11}-qh_{21}h_{12}
, it follows that
h_{22}h_{11}=h_{11}h_{22}
and that
h_{21}h_{12}=q^{-2}h_{12}h_{21}
.

It is easy to check that the relations (
6.1
) imply all the relations in (
5.1
).
∎

Remark 6.4
.

The algebra
H_{q}(2)
is also isomorphic to a deformation of
GL_{2}
denoted by
GL_{q,q^{-1}}(2)
which is given by Takeuchi in
[
Tak90
]
(see also
[
CWZ14
, Proposition 5.4]
).

To state the classification of cocommutative Hopf coactions on
A_{q}(2)
, we must introduce the following family of Hopf algebras.

Definition 6.5

(
[
GZ10
, Construction 1.1]
)
.

For
n\in\mathbb{Z}
and
q\in\Bbbk^{*}
, define the Hopf algebra

\mathcal{A}(n,q)\coloneqq\frac{\Bbbk\left\langle x^{\pm 1},y\right\rangle}{(yx-qxy)}

with Hopf structure given by

\displaystyle\Delta(x)

\displaystyle=x\otimes x,

\displaystyle\Delta(y)

\displaystyle=y\otimes 1+x^{n}\otimes y,

\displaystyle\varepsilon(x)

\displaystyle=1,

\displaystyle\varepsilon(y)

\displaystyle=0,

\displaystyle S(x)

\displaystyle=x^{-1},

\displaystyle S(y)

\displaystyle=-x^{-n}y.

In other words,
x
is group-like and
y
is
x^{n}
-skew primitive.

Certainly,
\mathcal{A}(n,q)
is cocommutative if and only if
n=0
. As we will show in this section, the Hopf algebras
\mathcal{A}(0,q^{\pm 1})
(and their quotients) are the only cocommutative Hopf algebras which coact on
A_{q}(2)
inner-faithfully but are not group algebras.

Theorem 6.6
.

Let
q\in\Bbbk^{*}
. Then, up to isomorphism, the maximal cocommutative quotients of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
are the following.

(1)

The group algebra of
\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

(2)

The Hopf algebra
\mathcal{A}(0,q^{\pm 1})
defined in Definition
6.5
.

(3)

The group algebra of
\Gamma
(only if
q=-1
).

Consequently, if
C
is a cocommutative Hopf algebra which right coacts on
A_{q}(2)
inner-faithfully, then
C
is a quotient of one of the Hopf algebras on the list above.

Note that when we say “maximal cocommutative quotient”, this is meant in the sense of Proposition
2.8
. As an immediate consequence of Theorem
6.6
, we recover Crawford’s result (Theorem
6.1
).

For the rest of this section, we fix a Hopf ideal
I
of
H_{q}(2)
such that
\overline{H}\coloneqq H_{q}(2)/I
is cocommutative. We write
g_{ij}\coloneqq h_{ij}+I
for the images of the generators
h_{ij}
in
\overline{H}
, and
\overline{D}\coloneqq D+I
for the image of the quantum determinant in
\overline{H}
. The following lemma analyzes the consequences of requiring
\overline{H}
to be cocommutative.

Lemma 6.7
.

We have the following conditions:

(1)

g_{12}
and
g_{21}
are linearly dependent.

(2)

g_{12}
and
g_{11}-g_{22}
are linearly dependent.

(3)

g_{21}
and
g_{11}-g_{22}
are linearly dependent.

Proof.

In this proof, we use the standard fact that if
V
is a vector space and
u,v\in V
, then
u\otimes v=v\otimes u
in
V\otimes V
if and only if
u
and
v
are linearly dependent.

The coproduct on
g_{11}
is

\Delta(g_{11})=g_{11}\otimes g_{11}+g_{12}\otimes g_{21}.

By the cocommutativity of
\overline{H}
, it follows that

g_{11}\otimes g_{11}+g_{12}\otimes g_{21}=g_{11}\otimes g_{11}+g_{21}\otimes g_{12},

and therefore

g_{12}\otimes g_{21}=g_{21}\otimes g_{12}.

Condition (
1
) follows.

For (
2
), consider

\Delta(g_{12})=g_{11}\otimes g_{12}+g_{12}\otimes g_{22}.

Since
\overline{H}
is cocommutative, we have

g_{11}\otimes g_{12}+g_{12}\otimes g_{22}=g_{12}\otimes g_{11}+g_{22}\otimes g_{12},

and therefore,

(g_{11}-g_{22})\otimes g_{12}=g_{12}\otimes(g_{11}-g_{22}).

Condition (
2
) follows. Condition (
3
) is obtained similarly by considering
\Delta(g_{21})
.
∎

First, we consider what happens when
g_{12}=g_{21}=0
.

Lemma 6.8
.

The ideal
I=(h_{12},h_{21})
of
H_{q}(2)
is a Hopf ideal, and
H_{q}(2)/I\cong\Bbbk\mathbb{Z}^{2}
as Hopf algebras.

Proof.

We omit proving that
I
is a Hopf ideal, since this is straightforward. Adopting the notation
\overline{H}
,
g_{ij}
, and
\overline{D}
from above, we know that
g_{11}
and
g_{22}
commute by Corollary
6.3
. Furthermore, we can easily see that
g_{11}
and
g_{22}
are group-like, and therefore are invertible in
\overline{H}
. It is now clear that
\overline{H}\cong\Bbbk\mathbb{Z}^{2}
by
g_{11}\mapsto(1,0)
and
g_{22}\mapsto(0,1)
.
∎

Therefore, it remains to consider the cases where at least one of
g_{12}
or
g_{21}
is nonzero.

Lemma 6.9
.

Assume that
q\neq 1
. Suppose that
g_{12}\neq 0
or
g_{21}\neq 0
. Then
g_{11}=g_{22}
.

Proof.

First assume that
g_{12}\neq 0
. By Lemma
6.7
, we must have
g_{11}-g_{22}=\lambda g_{12}
for some
\lambda\in\Bbbk
. To prove the result, it suffices to show that
\lambda=0
. Considering the antipode on
\overline{H}
, we have

S(g_{11}-g_{22})=(g_{22}-g_{11})\overline{D}^{-1}=-\lambda g_{12}\overline{D}^{-1},

where we used that
g_{11}-g_{22}=\lambda g_{12}
in the last equality. On the other hand,

S(g_{11}-g_{22})=\lambda S(g_{12})=-\lambda qg_{12}\overline{D}^{-1},

and therefore
(1-q)\lambda g_{12}=0
, which implies that
\lambda=0
(since
q\neq 1
), and therefore
g_{11}=g_{22}
. The case
g_{21}\neq 0
follows similarly.
∎

We now consider the case where exactly one of
g_{12}
or
g_{21}
is zero.

Proposition 6.10
.

The ideals
I_{1}=(h_{11}-h_{22},h_{21})
and
I_{2}=(h_{11}-h_{22},h_{12})
are Hopf ideals of
H_{q}(2)
, and
H_{q}(2)/I_{1}\cong\mathcal{A}(0,q)
and
H_{q}(2)/I_{2}\cong\mathcal{A}(0,q^{-1})
as Hopf algebras.

Proof.

We only prove the result for
I_{1}
, since the proof for
I_{2}
is similar. To that end, we let
I\coloneqq I_{1}
, and adopt the notation from above: we let
\overline{H}=H_{q}(2)/I
,
g_{ij}=h_{ij}+I
, and
\overline{D}=D+I
.

First, we prove that
I
is a Hopf ideal of
H_{q}(2)
. It is clear that
\varepsilon(I)=0
. Let
\Phi
be the composition

\Phi\colon H_{q}(2)\xrightarrow{\Delta}H_{q}(2)\otimes H_{q}(2)\twoheadrightarrow\overline{H}\otimes\overline{H}.

To prove that
\Delta(I)\subseteq H_{q}(2)\otimes I+I\otimes H_{q}(2)
, it suffices to show that
\Phi(I)=0
. We have

\Phi(h_{21})=g_{21}\otimes g_{11}+g_{22}\otimes g_{21}=0,

since
g_{21}=0
in
\overline{H}
. Furthermore,

\Phi(h_{11}-h_{22})=g_{11}\otimes g_{11}+g_{12}\otimes g_{21}-(g_{21}\otimes g_{12}+g_{22}\otimes g_{22})=g_{11}\otimes g_{11}-g_{11}\otimes g_{11}=0,

where we used that
g_{21}=0
and
g_{11}=g_{22}
. It follows that
\Delta(I)\subseteq H_{q}(2)\otimes I+I\otimes H_{q}(2)
, as required.

Finally, we check the antipode:

S(h_{21})=-q^{-1}h_{21}D^{-1}\in I,\quad S(h_{11}-h_{22})=(h_{22}-h_{11})D^{-1}\in I,

and thus
S(I)\subseteq I
. We conclude that
I=I_{1}=(h_{11}-h_{22},h_{21})
is a Hopf ideal of
H_{q}(2)
.

We now prove the isomorphism in the statement of the proposition. The Hopf algebra
\overline{H}
is generated by
g_{11}
,
g_{12}
, and
\overline{D}^{-1}
, subject to the relation
g_{12}g_{11}=qg_{11}g_{12}
, by Corollary
6.3
. By definition of the quantum determinant
D
, we have
\overline{D}=g_{11}^{2}
, so
g_{11}
is invertible in
\overline{H}
. Note that

\Delta(g_{11}^{-1}g_{12})=1\otimes(g_{11}^{-1}g_{12})+(g_{11}^{-1}g_{12})\otimes 1,

and thus
g_{11}^{-1}g_{12}
is primitive. We therefore get an isomorphism of Hopf algebras

\displaystyle\mathcal{A}(0,q)

\displaystyle\xrightarrow{\sim}\overline{H}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto g_{11},

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle\mapsto g_{11}^{-1}g_{12}.\qed

The last case to consider is when
g_{12}
and
g_{21}
are both nonzero in
\overline{H}
. As we prove next, this can only happen if
q^{2}=1
.

Lemma 6.11
.

Suppose
g_{12},g_{21}\neq 0
. Then
q^{2}=1
.

Proof.

Assume, for a contradiction, that
q^{2}\neq 1
. Lemma
6.7
implies that
g_{21}=\lambda g_{12}
for some
\lambda\in\Bbbk^{*}
. By (
6.1
), we have
g_{12}g_{21}=q^{2}g_{21}g_{12}
. Since
g_{21}=\lambda g_{12}
and
\lambda\neq 0
, it follows that
g_{12}^{2}=q^{2}g_{12}^{2}
. But
q^{2}\neq 1
by assumption, so
g_{12}^{2}=0
.

Similarly, we have
qg_{21}g_{11}=g_{11}g_{21}
. On the other hand, using that
g_{21}=\lambda g_{12}
, we get

qg_{21}g_{11}=\lambda qg_{12}g_{11}=\lambda q^{2}g_{11}g_{12}=q^{2}g_{11}g_{21},

and thus
g_{11}g_{21}=q^{2}g_{11}g_{21}
. But
q^{2}\neq 1
, so
g_{11}g_{21}=0
.

Note that by Lemma
6.9
,
g_{11}=g_{22}
. Thus by definition of
D
, we have

\overline{D}=g_{11}g_{22}-q^{-1}g_{12}g_{21}=g_{11}^{2}-\lambda q^{-1}g_{12}^{2}=g_{11}^{2}.

Therefore,
g_{11}
is invertible in
\overline{H}
. But then
g_{11}g_{21}=0
implies that
g_{21}=0
in
\overline{H}
, a contradiction.
∎

As the next result shows, if
q=-1
and
g_{12},g_{21}\neq 0
, then the only possibility is that
\overline{H}
is a quotient of
\Bbbk\Gamma
.

Proposition 6.12
.

Let
\lambda\in\Bbbk^{*}
. Then the ideal
I=(h_{11}-h_{22},h_{21}-\lambda h_{12})
of
H_{-1}(2)
is a Hopf ideal and
H_{-1}(2)/I\cong\Bbbk\Gamma
as Hopf algebras.

Proof.

We preserve the notation
\overline{H}=H_{-1}(2)/I
,
g_{ij}=h_{ij}+I
, and
\overline{D}=D+I
from above. First, it is clear that
\varepsilon(I)=0
. As before, we let
\Phi
be the composition

\Phi\colon H_{-1}(2)\xrightarrow{\Delta}H_{-1}(2)\otimes H_{-1}(2)\twoheadrightarrow\overline{H}\otimes\overline{H}.

We have

\displaystyle\Phi(h_{11}-h_{22})

\displaystyle=g_{11}\otimes g_{11}+g_{12}\otimes g_{21}-(g_{21}\otimes g_{12}+g_{22}\otimes g_{22})

\displaystyle=g_{11}\otimes g_{11}+g_{12}\otimes(\lambda g_{12})-(\lambda g_{12})\otimes g_{12}-g_{11}\otimes g_{11}=0,

where we used that
g_{22}=g_{11}
and
g_{21}=\lambda g_{12}
. Similarly,

\displaystyle\Phi(h_{21}-\lambda h_{12})

\displaystyle=g_{21}\otimes g_{11}+g_{22}\otimes g_{21}-\lambda(g_{11}\otimes g_{12}+g_{12}\otimes g_{22})

\displaystyle=(\lambda g_{12})\otimes g_{11}+g_{11}\otimes(\lambda g_{12})-\lambda g_{11}\otimes g_{12}-\lambda g_{12}\otimes g_{11}=0.

Checking the antipode, we get

S(h_{11}-h_{22})=(h_{22}-h_{11})D^{-1}\in I,\quad S(h_{21}-\lambda h_{12})=(h_{21}-\lambda h_{12})D^{-1}\in I,

and therefore
S(I)\subseteq I
, so
I
is a Hopf ideal of
H_{-1}(2)
. It follows from Corollary
6.3
that
\overline{H}
is generated by
g_{11}
,
g_{12}
, and
\overline{D}^{-1}
subject to the relation
g_{12}g_{11}=-g_{11}g_{12}
. In this case, we have
\overline{D}=g_{11}^{2}+\lambda g_{12}^{2}
. Letting
\mu\coloneqq\sqrt{\lambda}
, we see that

(g_{11}+\mu g_{12})^{2}=(g_{11}-\mu g_{12})^{2}=g_{11}^{2}+\lambda g_{12}^{2}=\overline{D}.

Therefore, letting
u\coloneqq g_{11}+\mu g_{12}
and
v\coloneqq g_{11}-\mu g_{12}
, it follows that
\overline{D}=u^{2}=v^{2}
, and thus
u
and
v
are both invertible in
\overline{H}
. Hence,

\overline{H}=\frac{\Bbbk\left\langle u^{\pm 1},v^{\pm 1}\right\rangle}{(u^{2}-v^{2})}.

It is straightforward to check that
u
and
v
are group-like, so
G(\overline{H})=\left\langle u,v\mid u^{2}=v^{2}\right\rangle\cong\Gamma
, and thus
\overline{H}\cong\Bbbk\Gamma
.
∎

We are now ready to prove Theorem
6.6
.

Proof of Theorem
6.6
.

Let
C
be a cocommutative Hopf algebra and let
\varphi\colon H_{q}(2)\to C
be a Hopf algebra homomorphism. Write
g_{ij}\coloneqq\varphi(h_{ij})
. If
g_{12}=g_{21}=0
, then Lemma
6.8
implies that
\varphi
factors through
\Bbbk\mathbb{Z}^{2}
. So, suppose at least one of
g_{12}
or
g_{21}
is nonzero.

First, we assume that
q\neq 1
. Then
g_{11}=g_{22}
, by Lemma
6.9
. Proposition
6.10
implies that if
g_{21}=0
then
\varphi
factors through
\mathcal{A}(0,q)
, while if
g_{12}=0
then
\varphi
factors through
\mathcal{A}(0,q^{-1})
.

Next, if
g_{12},g_{21}\neq 0
, then Lemma
6.11
implies that
q=-1
. By Lemma
6.7
(
1
),
g_{21}=\lambda g_{12}
for some
\lambda\in\Bbbk^{*}
. Now it follows by Proposition
6.12
that
\varphi
factors through
\Bbbk\Gamma
.

Finally, we now consider the case
q=1
. Since
H_{1}(2)
is commutative, it follows that
\overline{H}\coloneqq\im(\varphi)
is commutative. By Lemma
6.7
, every pair of
g_{12},g_{21}
, and
g_{11}-g_{22}
is linearly dependent. Assume that
g_{12}\neq 0
, since the other case is similar. Now we can get
g_{21}=\lambda g_{12}
and
g_{22}=g_{11}+\mu g_{12}
for some
\lambda,\mu\in\Bbbk
. In other words,

\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}&g_{12}\\
g_{21}&g_{22}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}&g_{12}\\
\lambda g_{12}&g_{11}+\mu g_{12}\end{pmatrix}.

For any matrix
M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in GL_{2}(\Bbbk)
, we have an automorphism of
\Bbbk[x_{1},x_{2}]
with

\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}x_{1}\\
x_{2}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}ax_{1}+bx_{2}\\
cx_{1}+dx_{2}\end{pmatrix}.

This induces an automorphism of the universal coacting Hopf algebra via

\begin{pmatrix}h_{11}&h_{12}\\
h_{21}&h_{22}\end{pmatrix}\mapsto\begin{pmatrix}h^{\prime}_{11}&h^{\prime}_{12}\\
h^{\prime}_{21}&h^{\prime}_{22}\end{pmatrix}\coloneqq M^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}h_{11}&h_{12}\\
h_{21}&h_{22}\end{pmatrix}M

We claim that we can choose
M
so that
\varphi(h_{21}^{\prime})=0
. Define
\alpha\coloneqq\frac{\mu+\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4\lambda}}{2}
and
\beta\coloneqq\frac{\mu-\sqrt{\mu^{2}+4\lambda}}{2}
, and let
M\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\
\alpha&1\end{pmatrix}
. Then

M^{-1}\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}&g_{12}\\
g_{21}&g_{22}\end{pmatrix}M=\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}+\alpha g_{12}&g_{12}\\
0&g_{11}+\beta g_{12}\end{pmatrix}.

Let
a\coloneqq g_{11}+\alpha g_{12}
,
b\coloneqq g_{12}
, and
c\coloneqq g_{11}+\beta g_{12}
. Using the fact that
\alpha^{2}=\alpha\mu+\lambda
and
\beta^{2}=\beta\mu+\lambda
, we can easily see that

\Delta(a)=a\otimes a,\qquad\Delta(c)=c\otimes c.

In other words, both
a
and
c
are group-like. Note that, if
\alpha\neq\beta
(equivalently,
\mu^{2}+4\lambda\neq 0
), then
a
and
c
are sufficient to generate
\overline{H}
. Otherwise,
\overline{H}
is generated by
a
and
b
(since
a=c
in this case). As a result, the rest of the proof is split into two cases.

Case 1
.

\mu^{2}+4\lambda\neq 0
.

As mentioned above, in this case the elements
a
and
c
generate the Hopf algebra
\overline{H}
, so
\overline{H}
is generated by group-like elements. Therefore,
\overline{H}
is an abelian group algebra, and thus is a quotient of
\Bbbk\mathbb{Z}^{2}
.

Case 2
.

\mu^{2}+4\lambda=0
.

In this case, we have
\mu=2\alpha
, so it is straightforward to check that
\Delta(b)=a\otimes b+b\otimes a
. It follows that
a^{-1}b
is primitive, and therefore
\overline{H}
is a quotient of
\mathcal{A}(0,1)
via the map
x\mapsto a
and
y\mapsto a^{-1}b
.
∎

7.
The three-variable case

We now move on to the case where
n=3
: we classify group gradings of the three-variable skew polynomial ring

A_{q}(3)=\frac{\Bbbk\left\langle x_{1},x_{2},x_{3}\right\rangle}{(x_{j}x_{i}-qx_{i}x_{j}\mid j>i)}.

Just like we did in the two-variable case, we also classify all possible inner-faithful coactions of Hopf algebras on
A_{q}(3)
(but only for
q^{2}\neq 1
).

7.1.
The universal involutive Hopf algebra

We now explicitly describe the Hopf algebra
H_{q}(3)
in the three-variable case. By (
5.1
),
H_{q}(3)
has the following relations:

(7.1a)

\displaystyle h_{ik}h_{ij}=qh_{ij}h_{ik}\quad(k>j),

(7.1b)

\displaystyle ad-q^{-1}bc=\operatorname{det}_{q}\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}=da-qcb,

(7.1c)

\displaystyle h_{ij}D=q^{2(j-i)}Dh_{ij},

(7.1d)

\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^{3}(-q)^{i-k}\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{H}_{\hat{k}\hat{\imath}})h_{kj}=\delta_{ij}D,

(7.1e)

\displaystyle D^{-1}D=DD^{-1}=1,

for all
i,j,k\in\{1,2,3\}
and all
2\times 2
sub-matrices
\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}
of
\mathcal{H}
, where the quantum determinant of
H_{q}(3)
is

D=h_{11}h_{22}h_{33}-q^{-1}h_{11}h_{23}h_{32}-q^{-1}h_{12}h_{21}h_{33}+q^{-2}h_{12}h_{23}h_{31}+q^{-2}h_{13}h_{21}h_{32}-q^{-3}h_{13}h_{22}h_{31}.

7.2.
Cocommutative quotients of
H_{q}(3)

Fix a Hopf ideal
I
of
H_{q}(3)
such that
\overline{H}\coloneqq H_{q}(3)/I
is cocommutative. We write
g_{ij}\coloneqq h_{ij}+I
for the images of the generators
h_{ij}
in
\overline{H}
, and
\overline{D}\coloneqq D+I
for the image of the quantum determinant in
\overline{H}
.

To state the classification result, we introduce two cocommutative Hopf algebras which are not group algebras. Both of these are extensions of the Hopf algebra
\mathcal{A}(0,q)
from Definition
6.5
.

Definition 7.1
.

For
q\in\Bbbk^{*}
, we define Hopf algebras

\mathcal{B}_{q}\coloneqq\frac{\Bbbk\left\langle x^{\pm 1},y^{\pm 1},z\right\rangle}{(yx-xy,zx-qxz,zy-yz)},\quad\mathcal{C}_{q}\coloneqq\frac{\Bbbk\left\langle x^{\pm 1},y^{\pm 1},z\right\rangle}{(yx-xy,zx-qxz,zy-q^{2}yz)},

with Hopf structures given by
x
and
y
being group-like and
z
being primitive.

It is straightforward to check directly that
\mathcal{B}_{q}
and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
are Hopf algebras, but in any case this will follow from Proposition
7.3
below.

The following is the main result of this section, which classifies all inner-faithful cocommutative coactions on
A_{q}(3)
for
q\neq\pm 1
. Just like the two-variable case, we only get coactions by abelian groups in this case, and also by
\mathcal{B}_{q^{\pm 1}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{q^{\pm 1}}
, analogously to the coaction by
\mathcal{A}(0,q^{\pm 1})
in the two-variable case.

Theorem 7.2
.

Let
q\in\Bbbk^{*}\setminus\{\pm 1\}
. Then, up to isomorphism, the maximal cocommutative quotients of
\underline{\operatorname{aut}}^{r}(A_{q})
are the following.

(1)

The group algebra of
\mathbb{Z}^{3}
.

(2)

The Hopf algebra
\mathcal{B}_{q^{\pm 1}}
.

(3)

The Hopf algebra
\mathcal{C}_{q^{\pm 1}}
.

Consequently, if
C
is a cocommutative Hopf algebra which right coacts on
A_{q}(3)
inner-faithfully, then
C
is a quotient of one of the Hopf algebras on the list above. In particular,
A_{q}(3)
does not have a faithful grading by a nonabelian group.

First, we show how the Hopf algebras
\mathcal{B}_{q^{\pm 1}}
and
\mathcal{C}_{q^{\pm 1}}
arise as quotients of
H_{q}(3)
.

Proposition 7.3
.

Let
i,j\in\{1,2,3\}
with
i\neq j
, and define

I_{ij}\coloneqq(h_{ii}-h_{jj},h_{k\ell}\mid k\neq\ell,(k,\ell)\neq(i,j))

as an ideal of
H_{q}(3)
. Then
I_{ij}
is a Hopf ideal of
H_{q}(3)
, and

H_{q}(3)/I_{ij}\cong\begin{cases}\mathcal{B}_{q},&\text{if }j=i+1,\\
\mathcal{B}_{q^{-1}},&\text{if }i=j+1,\\
\mathcal{C}_{q},&\text{if }(i,j)=(1,3),\\
\mathcal{C}_{q^{-1}},&\text{if }(i,j)=(3,1),\end{cases}

where
\mathcal{B}_{q}
and
\mathcal{C}_{q}
are defined in Definition
7.1
.

Proof.

It is straightforward to prove that
I_{ij}
is a Hopf ideal of
H_{q}(3)
(the proof is similar to that of Proposition
6.10
). We now let
I=I_{ij}
and adopt the notation from above: we have
\overline{H}=H_{q}(3)/I
,
g_{ij}=h_{ij}+I
, and
\overline{D}=D+I
. We will only prove the result in the cases
(i,j)=(1,2)
and
(i,j)=(1,3)
, since the other cases are similar.

Case 1
.

(i,j)=(1,2)
.

The Hopf algebra
\overline{H}
is generated by
g_{11},g_{33},g_{12}
, and
\overline{D}^{-1}
, subject to the relations

\displaystyle g_{12}g_{11}

\displaystyle=qg_{11}g_{12}\quad

\displaystyle\text{by }\eqref{eq:involutive q commute}\text{ with }i=j=1,k=2,

\displaystyle g_{33}g_{11}

\displaystyle=g_{11}g_{33}\quad

\displaystyle\text{by }\eqref{eq:involutive 2x2 minor}\text{ with }\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}h_{11}&h_{13}\\
h_{31}&h_{33}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle g_{12}g_{33}

\displaystyle=g_{33}g_{12}\quad

\displaystyle\text{by }\eqref{eq:involutive 2x2 minor}\text{ with }\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}h_{12}&h_{13}\\
h_{32}&h_{33}\end{pmatrix}.

The other relations from (
7.1
) do not result in any additional relations in
\overline{H}
. We also have
\overline{D}=g_{11}^{2}g_{33}
, and thus
g_{11}
and
g_{33}
are invertible in
\overline{H}
(in fact, it is easy to check that
g_{11}
and
g_{33}
are group-like). Furthermore,

\Delta(g_{11}^{-1}g_{12})=1\otimes(g_{11}^{-1}g_{12})+(g_{11}^{-1}g_{12})\otimes 1,

so we see that
g_{11}^{-1}g_{12}
is primitive. It follows that

\displaystyle\mathcal{B}_{q}

\displaystyle\to\overline{H}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto g_{11},

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle\mapsto g_{33},

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\mapsto g_{11}^{-1}g_{12}

is an isomorphism of Hopf algebras.

Case 2
.

(i,j)=(1,3)
.

This is similar to Case
1
, except
\overline{H}
is generated by the elements
g_{11},g_{22},g_{13}
, and
\overline{D}^{-1}
, and the relations are slightly different:

\displaystyle g_{13}g_{11}

\displaystyle=qg_{11}g_{13}\quad

\displaystyle\text{by }\eqref{eq:involutive q commute}\text{ with }i=j=1,k=3,

\displaystyle g_{22}g_{11}

\displaystyle=g_{11}g_{22}\quad

\displaystyle\text{by }\eqref{eq:involutive 2x2 minor}\text{ with }\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}h_{11}&h_{12}\\
h_{21}&h_{22}\end{pmatrix},

\displaystyle g_{13}g_{22}

\displaystyle=q^{2}g_{22}g_{13}\quad

\displaystyle\text{by }\eqref{eq:involutive 2x2 minor}\text{ with }\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}h_{12}&h_{13}\\
h_{22}&h_{23}\end{pmatrix}.

Similarly to Case
1
, we now see that the map

\displaystyle\mathcal{C}_{q}

\displaystyle\to\overline{H}

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto g_{11},

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle\mapsto g_{22},

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\mapsto g_{11}^{-1}g_{13}

is an isomorphism of Hopf algebras.

The other cases follow similarly with very minor changes.
∎

7.2.1.
Consequences of
\overline{H}
being a Hopf algebra

As a first step in the proof of Theorem
7.2
, we analyze some consequences of the requirement that
\overline{H}
is a Hopf algebra. We start with an observation which follows easily by the structure of the comultiplication on
\overline{H}
.

Lemma 7.4
.

Choose
i,j,k
such that
\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}
. If
g_{ij}=0
, then either
g_{ik}=0
or
g_{kj}=0
.

Proof.

We have

0=\Delta(g_{ij})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}g_{i\ell}\otimes g_{\ell j}=g_{ik}\otimes g_{kj}.

The result follows.
∎

The second observation is that when
g_{ij}=0
, we can get some further restrictions by referring to the
n=2
case. For example, if
g_{12}=0
, then Lemma
7.4
implies that
g_{13}=0
or
g_{32}=0
. In the first case, the first row of the matrix
\mathcal{G}=(g_{ij})
has two zeros and in the second case, the second column of
\mathcal{G}
has two zeros. In other words,

\textbf{Case 1: }\mathcal{G}=\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}&0&0\\
g_{21}&g_{22}&g_{23}\\
g_{31}&g_{32}&g_{33}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\textbf{Case 2: }\mathcal{G}=\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}&0&g_{13}\\
g_{21}&g_{22}&g_{23}\\
g_{31}&0&g_{33}\end{pmatrix}.

As we show in the next proposition, the subalgebra of
\overline{H}
generated by the entries of
\mathcal{G}_{\hat{1}\hat{1}}
(in Case 1) or
\mathcal{G}_{\hat{2}\hat{2}}
(in Case 2) is a Hopf quotient of
H_{q}(2)
, allowing us to use Theorem
6.6
. Before proceeding, it will be useful to have the following calculation at hand.

Lemma 7.5
.

Choose
i,j,k
such that
\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}
. If
g_{ik}=0
or
g_{ji}=0
, then

g_{ii}g_{jk}=q^{\varepsilon}g_{jk}g_{ii},

where
\varepsilon=\begin{cases}0,&i\in\{1,3\},\\
-2,&(i,j,k)=(2,1,3),\\
2,&(i,j,k)=(2,3,1).\end{cases}

Proof.

The relation (
7.1b
) applied to the
2\times 2
minor
\mathcal{G}_{\hat{k},\hat{j}}
has the form

g_{ii}g_{jk}-q^{\pm 1}g_{ik}g_{ji}=g_{jk}g_{ii}-q^{\mp 1}g_{ji}g_{ik}

when
i\in\{1,3\}
, the form

g_{ji}g_{ik}-q^{-1}g_{jk}g_{ii}=g_{ik}g_{ji}-qg_{ii}g_{jk}

when
(i,j,k)=(2,1,3)
, and the form

g_{ik}g_{ji}-q^{-1}g_{ii}g_{jk}=g_{ji}g_{ik}-qg_{jk}g_{ii}

when
(i,j,k)=(2,3,1)
.
The result follows immediately since
g_{ik}g_{ji}=g_{ji}g_{ik}=0
.
∎

Proposition 7.6
.

Choose
i,j,k
such that
\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}
and suppose
g_{ij}=0
, so that
g_{ik}=0
or
g_{kj}=0
by Lemma
7.4
. Let
\mathcal{G}\coloneqq(g_{ij})\in M_{3}(\overline{H})
. Then the following hold.

(1)

Suppose
g_{ik}=0
and let

D^{\prime}\coloneqq\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{\imath}\hat{\imath}})=g_{jj}g_{kk}-q^{\varepsilon}g_{jk}g_{kj},

where
\varepsilon=-1
when
k>j
and
\varepsilon=1
when
j>k
. Then
D^{\prime}
is invertible in
\overline{H}
and the subalgebra
H^{\prime}\coloneqq\Bbbk\left\langle g_{jj},g_{kk},g_{jk},g_{kj},(D^{\prime})^{-1}\right\rangle
is a Hopf subalgebra of
\overline{H}
which is a Hopf quotient of
H_{q}(2)
.

(2)

Suppose
g_{kj}=0
and let

D^{\prime}\coloneqq\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{\jmath}\hat{\jmath}})=g_{ii}g_{kk}-q^{\varepsilon}g_{ik}g_{ki},

where
\varepsilon=-1
when
k>i
and
\varepsilon=-1
when
i>k
. Then
D^{\prime}
is invertible in
\overline{H}
and the subalgebra
H^{\prime}\coloneqq\Bbbk\left\langle g_{ii},g_{kk},g_{ik},g_{ki},(D^{\prime})^{-1}\right\rangle
is a Hopf subalgebra of
\overline{H}
which is a Hopf quotient of
H_{q}(2)
.

Proof.

For (
1
), we set
r\coloneqq i
, and for (
2
), we set
r\coloneqq j
.
Let
n\coloneq\min(\{1,2,3\}\setminus\{r\})
and
m\coloneqq\max(\{1,2,3\}\setminus\{r\})
. We either have
g_{rn}=g_{rm}=0
or
g_{nr}=g_{mr}=0
. Thus, using the relations (
7.1b
) is it easy to check that
g_{rr}
commutes with both
g_{mm}
and
g_{nn}
. Moreover, by Lemma
7.5
we see that
g_{rr}
commutes with
g_{mn}g_{nm}
and
g_{nm}g_{mn}
, and thus commutes with
D^{\prime}
. Now a direct calculation using the formula for
D
shows that
\overline{D}=g_{rr}D^{\prime}=D^{\prime}g_{rr}
. Hence,
g_{rr}
and
D^{\prime}
are units in
\overline{H}
.

We now check that
H^{\prime}
satisfies all the relations of
H_{q}(2)
from (
6.1
). We already know that the generators
g_{nn},g_{mm},g_{nm},g_{mn}
of
H^{\prime}
satisfy

g_{nm}g_{nn}=qg_{nn}g_{nm},\qquad g_{mm}g_{mn}=qg_{mn}g_{mm},

as these are already relations of
H_{q}(3)
. Furthermore, it follows from (
7.1d
) that

\overline{D}=\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}(-q)^{n-\ell}\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{\ell}\hat{n}})g_{\ell n}=\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{n}\hat{n}})g_{nn}+(-q)^{n-m}\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{m}\hat{n}})g_{mn},

since
\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{r}\hat{n}})g_{rn}=0
(in case (
1
) we have
g_{rn}=0
, and in case (
2
) we have
\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{r}\hat{n}})=0
). Using that
g_{rn}g_{nr}=g_{rm}g_{mr}=0
, we get

\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{n}\hat{n}})=g_{rr}g_{mm},\qquad\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{m}\hat{n}})=(-q)^{\delta_{r,2}}g_{rr}g_{nm}=\begin{cases}g_{rr}g_{nm},&\text{if }r\in\{1,3\},\\
-qg_{22}g_{13},&\text{if }r=2,\\
\end{cases}

and thus

\overline{D}=g_{rr}g_{mm}g_{nn}-q^{-1}g_{rr}g_{nm}g_{mn}=g_{rr}(g_{mm}g_{nn}-q^{-1}g_{nm}g_{mn})=g_{rr}D^{\prime},

since
n-m=-1
when
i\in\{1,3\}
and
n-m=-2
when
i=2
. Now, the relation (
7.1b
) gives

\overline{D}=g_{rr}D^{\prime}=g_{rr}(g_{nn}g_{mm}-q^{-1}g_{nm}g_{mn})=g_{rr}(g_{mm}g_{nn}-qg_{mn}g_{nm}).

Combining the above equations and using the fact that
g_{rr}
is invertible, we deduce that

g_{nn}g_{mm}=g_{mm}g_{nn},\qquad g_{nm}g_{mn}=q^{2}g_{mn}g_{nm}.

Once again using (
7.1d
), we get

0=\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}(-q)^{n-\ell}\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{\ell}\hat{n}})g_{\ell m}=\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{n}\hat{n}})g_{nm}+(-q)^{n-m}\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{m}\hat{n}})g_{mm},

since
\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{r}\hat{n}})g_{rm}=0
. Therefore,

g_{rr}g_{mm}g_{nm}-q^{-1}g_{rr}g_{nm}g_{mm}=0.

Since
g_{rr}
is invertible, we conclude that
g_{mm}g_{nm}=q^{-1}g_{nm}g_{mm}
. We can similarly prove that
g_{mn}g_{nn}=q^{-1}g_{nn}g_{mn}
using the relation

\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}(-q)^{m-\ell}\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{\ell}\hat{m}})g_{\ell n}=0

from (
7.1d
). Therefore,
H^{\prime}
satisfies all the relations from (
6.1
).

It remains to check that
H^{\prime}
is a Hopf algebra with the correct Hopf structure. There is nothing to check for
\varepsilon
. For the comultiplication, we have

\Delta(g_{nn})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}g_{n\ell}\otimes g_{\ell n}=g_{nn}\otimes g_{nn}+g_{nm}\otimes g_{mn}\in H^{\prime}\otimes H^{\prime},

since
g_{nr}\otimes g_{rn}=0
. Similarly, we also have

\Delta(g_{mm})=g_{mm}\otimes g_{mm}+g_{mn}\otimes g_{nm}\in H^{\prime}\otimes H^{\prime}.

Next, we have

\Delta(g_{nm})=\sum_{\ell=1}^{3}g_{n\ell}\otimes g_{\ell m}=g_{nn}\otimes g_{nm}+g_{nm}\otimes g_{mm}\in H^{\prime}\otimes H^{\prime},

since
g_{nr}\otimes g_{rm}=0
, and similarly,

\Delta(g_{mn})=g_{mn}\otimes g_{nn}+g_{mm}\otimes g_{mn}\in H^{\prime}\otimes H^{\prime}.

Therefore,
\Delta
restricts to a well-defined comultiplication on
H^{\prime}
which matches up with the comultiplication of
H_{2}(2)
. It remains to check the antipode: we have

S(g_{nn})=\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{n}\hat{n}})\overline{D}^{-1}=(g_{rr}g_{mm}-q^{\pm 1}g_{rm}g_{mr})\overline{D}^{-1}=g_{rr}g_{mm}\overline{D}^{-1},

since
g_{rm}g_{mr}=0
. Using that
\overline{D}=g_{rr}D^{\prime}
, and that
g_{rr}
commutes with
D^{\prime}
and
g_{mm}
, we conclude that

S(g_{nn})=g_{mm}(D^{\prime})^{-1}\in H^{\prime}.

Proving that
S(g_{mm})=g_{nn}(D^{\prime})^{-1}\in H^{\prime}
is identical to the above. If
m-n=1
, then

S(g_{nm})=-q\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{m}\hat{n}})\overline{D}^{-1}=-q(g_{rr}g_{nm}-q^{\pm 1}g_{rm}g_{nr})\overline{D}^{-1}=-qg_{rr}g_{nm}\overline{D}^{-1},

since
g_{rm}g_{nr}=0
. If
m-n=2
(in which case
n=1
,
m=3
, and
r=2
), then

S(g_{13})=q^{2}\operatorname{det}_{q}(\mathcal{G}_{\hat{3}\hat{1}})\overline{D}^{-1}=q^{2}(g_{12}g_{23}-q^{-1}g_{13}g_{22})\overline{D}^{-1}=-qg_{13}g_{22}\overline{D}^{-1},

since
g_{nr}g_{rm}=g_{12}g_{23}=0
in this case. In either case, using that
\overline{D}=g_{rr}D^{\prime}
, we conclude that

S(g_{nm})=-qg_{nm}(D^{\prime})^{-1}\in H^{\prime}.

Checking that
S(g_{mn})=-q^{-1}g_{mn}(D^{\prime})^{-1}
is similar. Therefore,
S
restricts to an antipode on
H^{\prime}
, and thus
H^{\prime}
is a Hopf subalgebra of
\overline{H}
.

We can now see that the map
H_{q}(2)\to H^{\prime}
defined by

g_{11}\mapsto g_{nn},\qquad g_{12}\mapsto g_{nm},\qquad g_{21}\mapsto g_{mn},\qquad g_{22}\mapsto g_{mm}

is a surjective homomorphism of Hopf algebras.
∎

Proposition
7.6
allows us to reduce to
H_{q}(2)
, so we can use Theorem
6.6
to get the following consequence.

Corollary 7.7
.

Suppose
q\neq\pm 1
. Choose
i,j,k
such that
\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}
and suppose
g_{ij}=0
, so that
g_{ik}=0
or
g_{kj}=0
by Lemma
7.4
. Then the following hold.

(1)

If
g_{ik}=0
, then either
g_{jk}=g_{kj}=0
, or else only one of
g_{jk}
and
g_{kj}
is zero and
g_{jj}=g_{kk}
.

(2)

If
g_{kj}=0
, then either
g_{ik}=g_{ki}=0
, or else only one of
g_{ik}
and
g_{ki}
is zero and
g_{ii}=g_{kk}
.

In either case, the diagonal entries
g_{11},g_{22},g_{33}
pairwise commute, and
\overline{D}=g_{11}g_{22}g_{33}
, so
g_{11},g_{22},g_{33}
are units in
\overline{H}
.

Proof.

Let
H^{\prime}
,
m
and
n
be as in Proposition
7.6
and its proof, so that
H^{\prime}
is generated by
g_{nn},g_{mm},g_{nm},g_{mn}
and
(D^{\prime})^{-1}
, where
D^{\prime}=g_{nn}g_{mm}-q^{-1}g_{nm}g_{mn}
. Since
\overline{H}
is cocommutative, so is
H^{\prime}
. Therefore,
H^{\prime}
is a cocommutative quotient of
H_{q}(2)
. By the proof of Theorem
6.6
and the assumption that
q\neq\pm 1
, it follows that either
H^{\prime}
is a quotient of
\Bbbk\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, in which case we have
g_{nm}=g_{mn}=0
, or
H^{\prime}
is a quotient of
A(0,q^{\pm 1})
, in which case one of
g_{nm}
and
g_{mn}
is zero and
g_{nn}=g_{mm}
.

In either case, since
g_{nm}g_{mn}=0
, we must have
\overline{D}=g_{11}g_{22}g_{33}
and
g_{11},g_{22},g_{33}
pairwise commute by the relation (
7.1b
).
∎

7.2.2.
Consequences of cocommutativity

We now proceed as we did in the two-variable case: we analyze the consequences of requiring
\overline{H}
to be cocommutative. As one would expect, the conditions we get are more complicated than the ones from Lemma
6.7
.

Lemma 7.8
.

Choose
i,j,k
such that
\{i,j,k\}=\{1,2,3\}
. Then the following hold in
\mathop{\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!2}}\overline{H}
.

(1)

g_{12}\wedge g_{21}=g_{31}\wedge g_{13}=g_{23}\wedge g_{32}
.

(2)

(g_{ii}-g_{jj})\wedge g_{ij}=g_{kj}\wedge g_{ik}
.

Proof.

In this proof, we make the identification
a\wedge b=\frac{1}{2}(a\otimes b-b\otimes a)
.

We have

\Delta(g_{11})=g_{11}\otimes g_{11}+g_{12}\otimes g_{21}+g_{13}\otimes g_{31}=g_{11}\otimes g_{11}+g_{21}\otimes g_{12}+g_{31}\otimes g_{13},

since
\overline{H}
is cocommutative. Rearranging, we see that

g_{12}\otimes g_{21}-g_{21}\otimes g_{12}=g_{31}\otimes g_{13}-g_{13}\otimes g_{31}.

It follows that
g_{12}\wedge g_{21}=g_{31}\wedge g_{13}
. We can proceed similarly by considering
\Delta(g_{22})
to conclude that
g_{12}\wedge g_{21}=g_{23}\wedge g_{32}
, which proves (
1
).

For (
2
), consider

\Delta(g_{12})=g_{11}\otimes g_{12}+g_{12}\otimes g_{22}+g_{13}\otimes g_{32}=g_{12}\otimes g_{11}+g_{22}\otimes g_{12}+g_{32}\otimes g_{13}.

Therefore, we get

(g_{11}-g_{22})\otimes g_{12}-g_{12}\otimes(g_{11}-g_{22})=g_{32}\otimes g_{13}-g_{13}\otimes g_{32}.

It follows that
(g_{11}-g_{22})\wedge g_{12}=g_{32}\wedge g_{13}
, which proves (
2
) in the case
(i,j,k)=(1,2,3)
. The other cases of (
2
) follow similarly.
∎

The next result is a well-known lemma which characterizes when
a\wedge b=c\wedge d
, where
a,b,c,d
are elements of a vector space.

Lemma 7.9
.

Let
V
be a vector space and suppose
a\wedge b=c\wedge d
in
\mathop{\bigwedge\nolimits^{\!2}}V
, where
a,b,c,d\in V
. Then either
a\wedge b=c\wedge d=0
, or there exists a matrix
M\in SL_{2}(\Bbbk)
such that

\begin{pmatrix}c\\
d\end{pmatrix}=M\begin{pmatrix}a\\
b\end{pmatrix}.

Proof.

Suppose
a\wedge b=c\wedge d\neq 0
. Then it must be the case that
\spn\{a,b,c,d\}
is two-dimensional, with basis given by
\{a,b\}
or
\{c,d\}
. In particular, there exist
\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta\in\Bbbk
such that

c=\alpha a+\beta b,\quad d=\gamma a+\delta b.

It follows that

\displaystyle a\wedge b

\displaystyle=c\wedge d=(\alpha a+\beta b)\wedge(\gamma a+\delta b)

\displaystyle=\alpha\delta(a\wedge b)+\beta\gamma(b\wedge a)

\displaystyle=(\alpha\delta-\beta\gamma)a\wedge b.

Therefore,
\alpha\delta-\beta\gamma=1
, and thus the result follows by setting
M\coloneqq\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}
.
∎

7.2.3.
The case
q\neq\pm 1

We now start working toward a proof of Theorem
7.2
. Our first aim is to show that at least one of the generators
g_{ij}
is zero, which will allow us to use Corollary
7.7
.

In the following result, we show that all the wedge products from Lemma
7.8
(
1
) are zero using Lemma
7.9
. The proof is immediate from the relations (
7.1
), unless
q^{6}=1
.

Lemma 7.10
.

Suppose
q\neq\pm 1
. Then

g_{12}\wedge g_{21}=g_{31}\wedge g_{13}=g_{23}\wedge g_{32}=0.

Proof.

Assume, for a contradiction, that
g_{12}\wedge g_{21}=g_{31}\wedge g_{13}=g_{23}\wedge g_{32}\neq 0
(these are all equal by Lemma
7.8
(
1
)). Then Lemma
7.9
implies that there exists
M=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}\in SL_{2}(\Bbbk)
such that
\begin{pmatrix}g_{31}\\
g_{13}\end{pmatrix}=M\begin{pmatrix}g_{12}\\
g_{21}\end{pmatrix}
. In other words,

g_{31}=\alpha g_{12}+\beta g_{21},\quad g_{13}=\gamma g_{12}+\delta g_{21}.

Right-multiplying the second equation by
\overline{D}
, we get
g_{13}\overline{D}=\gamma g_{12}\overline{D}+\delta g_{21}\overline{D}
, and therefore

q^{4}\overline{D}g_{13}=\gamma q^{2}\overline{D}g_{12}+\delta q^{-2}\overline{D}g_{21},

by (
7.1c
). Left-multiplying by
q^{-4}\overline{D}^{-1}
, it follows that
g_{13}=\gamma q^{-2}g_{12}+\delta q^{-6}g_{21}
. Thus,

\gamma g_{12}+\delta g_{21}=g_{13}=\gamma q^{-2}g_{12}+\delta q^{-6}g_{21}.

Now,
g_{12}
and
g_{21}
are linearly independent by assumption, and thus
\gamma=\gamma q^{-2}
and
\delta=\delta q^{-6}
. Since we are assuming that
q\neq\pm 1
, it follows that
\gamma=0
. Furthermore, if
q^{6}\neq 1
, then
\delta=0
, which would contradict
M\in SL_{2}(\Bbbk)
. Therefore, it must be the case that
q^{6}=1
. Since
M\in SL_{2}(\Bbbk)
, we have
\alpha\neq 0
and
\delta=\alpha^{-1}
, so we get
g_{13}=\alpha^{-1}g_{21}
. By right multiplying the other equation
g_{31}=\alpha g_{12}+\beta g_{21}
by
\overline{D}
and arguing similarly, we can that
\beta=0
, and thus
g_{31}=\alpha g_{12}
.

We also have
g_{23}\wedge g_{32}=g_{31}\wedge g_{13}\neq 0
, so proceeding as above, there exists another matrix
M^{\prime}=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&\mu\\
\nu&\eta\end{pmatrix}\in SL_{2}(\Bbbk)
such that
\begin{pmatrix}g_{31}\\
g_{13}\end{pmatrix}=M^{\prime}\begin{pmatrix}g_{23}\\
g_{32}\end{pmatrix}
. In other words,

g_{31}=\lambda g_{23}+\mu g_{32},\quad g_{13}=\nu g_{23}+\eta g_{32}.

Similarly as in the previous paragraph, we deduce that
\mu=\nu=0
and that
\eta=\lambda^{-1}
, and thus
g_{31}=\lambda g_{23}
and
g_{13}=\lambda^{-1}g_{32}
. Combining the above, we have

(7.2)

g_{31}=\alpha g_{12}=\lambda g_{23},\quad g_{13}=\alpha^{-1}g_{21}=\lambda^{-1}g_{32}.

Now, Lemma
7.8
(
2
) implies that
(g_{11}-g_{22})\wedge g_{12}=g_{32}\wedge g_{13}
. Combining this with (
7.2
), we get

(g_{11}-g_{22})\wedge g_{12}=g_{32}\wedge g_{13}=g_{32}\wedge(\lambda^{-1}g_{32})=0,

so
g_{12}
and
g_{11}-g_{22}
are linearly dependent, say
g_{11}-g_{22}=\xi g_{12}
for some
\xi\in\Bbbk
. Note that (
7.1c
) implies that

(g_{11}-g_{22})\overline{D}=\xi g_{12}\overline{D}=q^{2}\xi\overline{D}g_{12}=q^{2}\overline{D}(g_{11}-g_{22})=q^{2}(g_{11}-g_{22})\overline{D}.

Right-multiplying by
\overline{D}^{-1}
, it follows that
g_{11}-g_{22}=q^{2}(g_{11}-g_{22})
. Since
q^{2}\neq 1
by assumption, we conclude that
g_{11}=g_{22}
.

We can proceed similarly using Lemma
7.8
to further deduce that
g_{11}=g_{22}=g_{33}
. Therefore, the above shows that
\overline{H}
is generated by the three elements
g_{11}
,
g_{12}
, and
g_{21}
.

By (
7.1a
), we have
g_{12}g_{11}=qg_{11}g_{12}
. Similarly, (
7.1a
) also gives
g_{33}g_{31}=qg_{31}g_{33}
, which implies that

g_{11}g_{12}=qg_{12}g_{11},

where we used that
g_{33}=g_{11}
and that
g_{31}=\alpha g_{12}
from (
7.2
). But then

g_{12}g_{11}=qg_{11}g_{12}=q^{2}g_{12}g_{11},

so
g_{12}g_{11}=0
, since
q^{2}\neq 1
. Similarly, we can also deduce that

(7.3)

g_{11}g_{12}=g_{12}g_{11}=g_{11}g_{21}=g_{21}g_{11}=g_{12}g_{21}=g_{21}g_{12}=0.

Now, we can use (
7.1b
) to give

g_{11}g_{32}-qg_{12}g_{31}=g_{32}g_{11}-q^{-1}g_{31}g_{12}.

Using that
g_{32}=\lambda\alpha^{-1}g_{21}
and that
g_{31}=\alpha g_{12}
from (
7.2
), we deduce that

\lambda\alpha^{-1}g_{11}g_{21}-q\alpha g_{12}^{2}=\lambda\alpha^{-1}g_{21}g_{11}-q^{-1}\alpha g_{12}^{2}.

But we know from (
7.3
) that
g_{11}g_{21}=g_{21}g_{11}=0
, so we conclude that
qg_{12}^{2}=q^{-1}g_{12}^{2}
. Since
q^{2}\neq 1
, it follows that
g_{12}^{2}=0
. By a completely symmetric argument, we can also deduce that
g_{21}^{2}=0
.

Recall that

\overline{D}=g_{11}g_{22}g_{33}-q^{-1}g_{11}g_{23}g_{32}-q^{-1}g_{12}g_{21}g_{33}+q^{-2}g_{12}g_{23}g_{31}+q^{-2}g_{13}g_{21}g_{32}-q^{-3}g_{13}g_{22}g_{31}.

Using equations (
7.2
) and (
7.3
), as well as the fact that
g_{12}^{2}=g_{21}^{2}=0
, we see that
\overline{D}=g_{11}^{3}
. But
\overline{D}
is invertible in
\overline{H}
, and thus
g_{11}
must also be invertible. Now, (
7.3
) implies that
g_{12}=g_{21}=0
, a contradiction.
∎

Next, we show that some more of the wedge products from Lemma
7.8
are zero.

Lemma 7.11
.

Suppose
q\neq\pm 1
. Then

g_{21}\wedge g_{32}=g_{23}\wedge g_{12}=(g_{11}-g_{33})\wedge g_{13}=(g_{11}-g_{33})\wedge g_{31}=0.

Proof.

Fix
i,j\in\{1,3\}
with
i\neq j
. By Lemma
7.8
, we have

(g_{ii}-g_{jj})\wedge g_{ij}=g_{2,j}\wedge g_{i,2}.

Assume, for a contradiction, that
(g_{ii}-g_{jj})\wedge g_{ij}=g_{2,j}\wedge g_{i,2}\neq 0
. In particular, this means that
g_{ii}\neq g_{jj}
and that
g_{ij}
,
g_{2,j}
, and
g_{i,2}
are all nonzero. By Lemma
7.9
, there exists
M=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha&\beta\\
\gamma&\delta\end{pmatrix}\in SL_{2}(\Bbbk)
such that

(7.4)

g_{ii}-g_{jj}=\alpha g_{2,j}+\beta g_{i,2},\quad g_{ij}=\gamma g_{2,j}+\delta g_{i,2}.

Right-multiplying the first of equations in (
7.4
) by
\overline{D}
, we get

(g_{ii}-g_{jj})\overline{D}=\alpha g_{2,j}\overline{D}+\beta g_{i,2}\overline{D}.

By (
7.1c
), it follows that

\overline{D}(g_{ii}-g_{jj})=\alpha q^{2(j-2)}\overline{D}g_{2,j}+\beta q^{2(2-i)}\overline{D}g_{i,2}.

Left-multiplying by
\overline{D}^{-1}
, we deduce that

g_{ii}-g_{jj}=\alpha q^{2(j-2)}g_{2,j}+\beta q^{2(2-i)}g_{i,2}.

Using that
g_{ii}-g_{jj}=\alpha g_{2,j}+\beta g_{i,2}
, we get

\alpha g_{2,j}+\beta g_{i,2}=\alpha q^{2(j-2)}g_{2,j}+\beta q^{2(2-i)}g_{i,2}.

Since
g_{2,j}
and
g_{i,2}
are linearly independent, we conclude that
\alpha=\alpha q^{2(j-2)}
and
\beta=\beta q^{2(2-i)}
. But
i,j\in\{1,3\}
, so it follows that
2-i=j-2=\pm 1
. In other words, we have
\alpha=q^{\pm 2}\alpha
and
\beta=q^{\pm 2}\beta
. By the assumption that
q^{2}\neq 1
, we must have
\alpha=\beta=0
. This contradicts
M\in SL_{2}(\Bbbk)
.
∎

We are now able to show that at least one of the generators
g_{ij}
is zero in
\overline{H}
.

Lemma 7.12
.

Suppose
q\neq\pm 1
. Then
g_{ij}=0
for some
i\neq j
.

Proof.

Assume, for a contradiction, that
g_{ij}\neq 0
for all
i,j
. By Lemma
7.10
, we have

g_{21}=\lambda g_{12},\qquad g_{31}=\mu g_{13},

for some
\lambda,\mu\in\Bbbk^{*}
. Furthermore, Lemma
7.11
implies that

g_{23}=\alpha g_{12},\qquad g_{32}=\beta g_{12},\qquad g_{33}=g_{11}+\gamma g_{13},

for some
\alpha,\beta,\gamma\in\Bbbk
with
\alpha,\beta\neq 0
.

Now, it follows by Lemma
7.8
that

(g_{11}-g_{22})\wedge g_{12}=g_{32}\wedge g_{13}=\beta g_{12}\wedge g_{13}.

Assume, for a contradiction, that
g_{12}\wedge g_{13}=0
, meaning
g_{13}=\xi g_{12}
for some
\xi\in\Bbbk^{*}
. Then

g_{13}\overline{D}=\xi g_{12}\overline{D}=\xi q^{2}\overline{D}g_{12}=q^{2}\overline{D}g_{13}=q^{-2}g_{13}\overline{D},

where we used (
7.1c
). This implies that
g_{13}=0
, a contradiction. Therefore,
g_{12}\wedge g_{13}\neq 0
.

Since we have

(g_{11}-g_{22})\wedge g_{12}=\beta g_{12}\wedge g_{13}\neq 0,

Lemma
7.9
implies that there exists a matrix
M=\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\
c&d\end{pmatrix}\in SL_{2}(\Bbbk)
such that

g_{11}-g_{22}=ag_{12}+b\beta g_{13},\qquad g_{12}=cg_{12}+d\beta g_{13}.

The linear independence of
g_{12}
and
g_{13}
implies that
c=1
and
d=0
. Since
M\in SL_{2}(\Bbbk)
, it follows that
b=-1
. Therefore,

g_{11}-g_{22}=ag_{12}-\beta g_{13}.

Multiplying both sides by
\overline{D}
. we have

(g_{11}-g_{22})\overline{D}=(ag_{12}-\beta g_{13})\overline{D}=\overline{D}(aq^{2}g_{12}-\beta q^{4}g_{13}).

Since
\overline{D}
commutes with
g_{11}
and
g_{22}
, we conclude that

g_{11}-g_{22}=aq^{2}g_{12}-\beta q^{4}g_{13}

upon multiplying by
\overline{D}^{-1}
. Using that
g_{11}-g_{22}=ag_{12}-\beta g_{13}
, we get

ag_{12}-\beta g_{13}=aq^{2}g_{12}-\beta q^{4}g_{13}.

It follows that
a=aq^{2}
and that
\beta=\beta q^{4}
. However, since
q^{2}\neq 1
by assumption, we conclude that

. Therefore, we have
g_{22}=g_{11}+\beta g_{13}
.

Summarizing, we have

\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}&g_{12}&g_{13}\\
g_{21}&g_{22}&g_{23}\\
g_{31}&g_{32}&g_{33}\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}g_{11}&g_{12}&g_{13}\\
\lambda g_{12}&g_{11}+\beta g_{13}&\alpha g_{12}\\
\mu g_{13}&\beta g_{12}&g_{11}+\gamma g_{13}\end{pmatrix}.

We now analyze what the relations (
7.1
) become under the above conditions:

•

g_{23}g_{21}=qg_{21}g_{23}
implies that
g_{12}^{2}=0
, where we used that

.

•

g_{11}g_{22}-q^{-1}g_{12}g_{21}=g_{22}g_{11}-qg_{21}g_{12}

•

, where we used that

.

•

g_{11}g_{33}-q^{-1}g_{13}g_{31}=g_{33}g_{11}-qg_{31}g_{13}

g_{13}^{2}=0
, where we used that
g_{11}g_{13}=g_{13}g_{11}=0

.

It now follows that
\overline{D}=g_{11}^{3}

Now that we have shown that some

must be
0
. Next, we show that at least one of the opposite generators

Lemma 7.13
.

Suppose
q\neq\pm 1
and let
i,j\in\{1,2,3\}
with
i\neq j
. Then
g_{ij}=0
or
g_{ji}=0
.

Proof.

Assume, for a contradiction, that
g_{ij}
and
g_{ji}
are both nonzero, for some
i\neq j
. By Lemma
7.12
, it follows that
g_{k\ell}=0
for some

. Then Lemma
7.4
implies that
g_{km}=0
or
g_{m\ell}=0
, where

. It must be the case that either
k
or
\ell
(but not both) is equal to one of
i
or
j
, since there are only three possibilities for each of these elements. Without loss of generality, we may assume that
k
or
\ell
is equal to
i
(and thus

).

Case 1
.

k=i
.

In this case, the above gives that
g_{j\ell}=0
, so it follows from Lemma
7.4
that
g_{i\ell}=0
. But now Corollary
7.7
implies that
g_{ij}
or

Case 2
.

\ell=i
.

In this case, we have
g_{kj}=0
, and thus
g_{ki}=0
, by Lemma
7.4
. But now Corollary
7.7
implies that
g_{ij}
or
g_{ji}
is zero, a contradiction.
∎

In the following result, we show that if
g_{ij}\neq 0
, then the diagonal generators
g_{ii}
and
g_{jj}
are equal.

Lemma 7.14
.

Suppose
q\neq\pm 1
and let
i,j\in\{1,2,3\}
with

. If
g_{ij}\neq 0
, then
g_{ii}=g_{jj}
.

Suppose
g_{ij}\neq 0
, so

. Let
k
be the unique element of
\{1,2,3\}\setminus\{i,j\}

Case 1
.

g_{ki}=g_{kj}=0
.

In this case we can apply Corollary
7.7
(
1
) to conclude that

, since
g_{ij}\neq 0
.

Case 2
.

g_{ik}=g_{jk}=0
.

Similar to the previous case, except we apply Corollary
7.7
(
2
).

Case 3
.

Neither of the previous two cases holds.

Note that since
g_{ji}=0
, it follows from Lemma

g_{ki}=0

7.7
(

(

When some of the diagonal elements

’s are zero.

Lemma 7.15
.

Suppose
q\neq\pm 1
and choose

such that

. If

, we have

Now if

,

,

.

.

The next result is the last ingredient we need to prove Theorem

.

Proposition 7.18
.

Suppose
q\neq\pm 1
. If
g_{ij}\neq 0

, then

, and

and

We are now ready to prove Theorem

.

Proof of Theorem
7.2
.

Let
C
be a cocommutative Hopf algebra, and let
\varphi\colon H_{q}(3)\to C
be a Hopf algebra homomorphism. Write
g_{ij}\coloneqq\varphi(h_{ij})
. If
g_{ij}=0
for all
i\neq j
, then it is easy to see that
\overline{H}\coloneqq\im(\varphi)
is a quotient of
\Bbbk\mathbb{Z}^{3}
. So, assume that
g_{ij}\neq 0
for some
i\neq j
. By Proposition
7.18
, it must be the case that
g_{ii}=g_{jj}
and
g_{k\ell}=0
for all
k\neq\ell
such that
(k,\ell)\neq(i,j)
. It is now clear from Proposition
7.3
that any Hopf map
H_{q}(3)\to C
, where
C
is cocommutative, factors through one of the algebras on our list. From the universal property of
H_{q}(3)
, it is clear that any coaction on
A_{q}(3)
by a cocommutative Hopf algebra factors through one of the given ones. Finally, it is straightforward to check that the largest group algebra occurring as a Hopf algebra quotient of
\mathcal{B}_{q^{\pm 1}}
or
\mathcal{C}_{q^{\pm 1}}
is
\Bbbk\mathbb{Z}^{2}
, so every coacting group algebra is abelian.
∎

8.
Applications and questions

We now spend some time presenting some applications of the main results of the paper, as well as some open questions.

8.1.
GK-dimension 1 Hopf coactions on
A_{q}(2)

First, we partially answer the following question of Chan–Walton–Zhang.

Question 8.1

(
[
CWZ14
, Question 5.11]
)
.

Let
q\in\Bbbk
such that
q
is not a root of unity. Is there a noncommutative Hopf algebra of GK-dimension 1 which coacts on
A_{q}(2)
inner-faithfully?

In
[
CWZ14
, Theorem 0.4]
, it is shown that if a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra
H
acts inner-faithfully on
A_{q}(2)
, where
q
is not a root of unity, then
H
is a group algebra. In fact, by examining the proof of
[
CWZ14
, Theorem 0.4]
, it is easy to see that
H
must be the group algebra of a finite abelian group, and that the same result also holds for coactions by finite-dimensional Hopf algebras. Therefore, Chan–Walton–Zhang proved that all finite-dimensional (in other words, GK-dimension 0) inner-faithful coactions on
A_{q}(2)
for
q
not a root of unity are by group algebras of finite abelian groups.

Using Theorem
6.6
, we know that there are inner-faithful coactions on
A_{q}(2)
by noncommutative Hopf algebras of GK-dimension
2
– these are the Hopf algebras
\mathcal{A}(0,q^{\pm 1})
from Definition
6.5
. If
q
is a root of unity, then it is easy to show that
\mathcal{A}(0,q^{\pm 1})
has a noncommutative quotient of GK-dimension
1
.

Corollary 8.2
.

Suppose
q\neq 1
and
q^{n}=1
for some
n\geq 2
. Then the noncommutative Hopf algebra

\frac{\mathcal{A}(0,q^{\pm 1})}{(x^{n}-1)}\cong\frac{\Bbbk\left\langle x,y\right\rangle}{(x^{n}-1,yx-q^{\pm 1}xy)}

has GK-dimension 1 and coacts on
A_{q}(2)
inner-faithfully. ∎

However, if
q
is not a root of unity, then there are no noncommutative, cocommutative Hopf algebras of GK-dimension
1
which coact on
A_{q}(2)
inner-faithfully, as we show next.

Proposition 8.3
.

Suppose
q
is not a root of unity. Then there are no noncommutative, cocommutative Hopf algebras of GK-dimension
1
which coact on
A_{q}(2)
inner-faithfully.

Proof.

Assume, for a contradiction, that
H
is a noncommutative, cocommutative Hopf algebra of GK-dimension
1
which coacts on
A_{q}(2)
inner-faithfully. By Theorem
6.6
, it must be the case that
H\cong\mathcal{A}(0,q^{\pm 1})/I
for some Hopf ideal
I
of
\mathcal{A}(0,q^{\pm 1})
. Let
\overline{x}
and
\overline{y}
be the images of
x
and
y
in
H
.

Since
H
is cocommutative, it follows by the Cartier–Kostant–Gabriel theorem
[
EGNO15
, Theorem 5.10.2]
that
H\cong U(P(H))\#\Bbbk G(H)
, where
P(H)=\Bbbk\overline{y}
is the Lie algebra of primitive elements of
H
and
G(H)=\left\langle\overline{x}\right\rangle
is the group of group-like elements of
H
. It is easy to see that
\overline{y}\neq 0
, as otherwise
H\cong\mathcal{A}(0,q^{\pm 1})/I
would be commutative. Therefore,
P(H)
is a one-dimensional Lie algbera, so
\GKdim(U(P(H)))=\GKdim(\Bbbk[\overline{y}])=1
.

By
[
AAH21
, Lemma 2.2]
, we see that

1=\GKdim(H)=\GKdim(U(P(H)))+\GKdim(\Bbbk G(H))=1+\GKdim(G(H)).

Therefore,
\GKdim(\Bbbk G(H))=0
, in other words,
G(H)
is a finite group. It follows that
G(H)=\left\langle\overline{x}\right\rangle
is a finite cyclic group, so
\overline{x}^{n}=1
for some
n\in\mathbb{N}
. Hence, we have

\overline{y}=\overline{y}\,\overline{x}^{n}=q^{\pm n}\overline{x}^{n}\overline{y}=q^{\pm n}\overline{y}.

However,
q
is not a root of unity, so
q^{n}\neq 1
, and thus
\overline{y}=0
, a contradiction.
∎

Proposition
8.3
suggests that the answer to Question
8.1
may be “no”, but our methods do not yield any information about coactions by non-cocommutative Hopf algebras which may coact on
A_{q}(2)
.

8.2.
Cosemisimple Hopf coactions on commutative domains

Next, we consider the following question of Julien Bichon.

Question 8.4

(Julien Bichon, cf.
[
WW16
, Question 2.21]
)
.

Is there an infinite-dimensional, noncommutative, cosemisimple, involutive Hopf algebra which coacts on a commutative domain inner-faithfully?

Question
8.4
is related to
[
EW14
, Theorem 4.1]
, which shows that if
H
is a finite-dimensional cosemisimple Hopf algebra which coacts on a commutative domain inner-faithfully, then
H
is the group algebra of a finite abelian group. Note that, by the Radford–Larson theorem
[
Rad12
, Theorem 16.1.2]
, if
H
is a finite-dimensional Hopf algebra then the following conditions are equivalent:

•

H
is semisimple.

•

H
is cosemisimple.

•

H
is involutive.

However, when
H
is infinite-dimensional, the above are no longer equivalent. Therefore, an analog of
[
EW14
, Theorem 4.1]
for infinite-dimensional Hopf algebras would necessarily involve choosing some subset of the three conditions above. One such possibility is Question
8.4
, which to our knowledge is still an open question.

From Corollary
6.3
, we see that the universal involutive Hopf algebra
H_{1}(2)
right coacting on
\Bbbk[x,y]
is commutative (in fact
H_{1}(2)\cong\mathcal{O}_{GL_{2}}
, the coordinate ring of the general linear group). Thus any involutive Hopf algebra which coacts on
\Bbbk[x,y]
inner-faithfully must be commutative, as was also noted in
[
CWZ14
, Proposition 5.4]
.

Therefore, one natural starting point for Question
8.4
is to consider the following question: are there noncommutative involutive Hopf algebras which coact on
\Bbbk[x,y,z]
inner-faithfully? To answer this, we can study the Hopf algebra
H_{1}(3)
. However, it is not clear from the presentation given in (
6.1
) whether
H_{1}(3)
is commutative, just like
H_{1}(2)
turned out to be commutative. As we show next, the Hopf algebra
H_{1}(3)
is noncommutative and has significantly worse properties than
H_{1}(2)
.

Proposition 8.5
.

The Hopf algebra
H_{1}(3)
is noncommutative, non-noetherian, and has infinite GK-dimension. In particular,
H_{1}(3)\not\cong\mathcal{O}_{GL_{3}}
.

Proof.

Let
I\coloneqq(h_{11}-1,h_{22}-1,h_{33}-1,h_{12},h_{21},h_{31},h_{32})
as an ideal of
H_{1}(3)
. It is straightforward to check that
I
is a Hopf ideal. By (
7.1
), we see that
H_{1}(3)/I
is generated by
a\coloneqq h_{13}+I
and
b\coloneqq h_{23}+I
with no relations, and that
a
and
b
are primitive elements of
H/I
. It follows that
H/I\cong U(\mathfrak{f}_{2})
as Hopf algebras, where
\mathfrak{f}_{2}
is the free Lie algebra on two generators. The result follows.
∎

In the proof of Proposition
8.5
, an inner-faithful coaction of
U(\mathfrak{f}_{2})
is constructed. We mention this explicitly in the following corollary.

Corollary 8.6
.

The Hopf algebra
U(\mathfrak{f}_{2})
coacts on
\Bbbk[x,y,z]
inner-faithfully via

\displaystyle\rho\colon\Bbbk[x,y,z]

\displaystyle\to\Bbbk[x,y,z]\otimes U(\mathfrak{f}_{2})

\displaystyle x

\displaystyle\mapsto x\otimes 1

\displaystyle y

\displaystyle\mapsto y\otimes 1

\displaystyle z

\displaystyle\mapsto x\otimes a+y\otimes b+z\otimes 1,

where
\mathfrak{f}_{2}
is the free Lie algebra on two generators
a
and
b
. ∎

In fact, the result from Proposition
8.5
applies to
H_{1}(n)
for arbitrary
n\geq 3
.

Corollary 8.7
.

If
n\geq 3
, then
H_{1}(n)
is noncommutative, non-noetherian, and has infinite GK-dimension.

Proof.

This is easily seen by noting that the map

\displaystyle\pi\colon H_{1}(n)

\displaystyle\to H_{1}(3)

\displaystyle h_{ij}

\displaystyle\mapsto\begin{cases}h_{ij},&\text{if }i,j\leq 3,\\
1,&\text{if }i=j\geq 4,\\
0,&\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

is a surjective homomorphism and using Proposition
8.5
.
∎

Remark 8.8
.

Corollary
8.7
is reminiscent of
[
Bic08
, Proposition 3.4]
, which studies Hopf coactions on the algebra
\Bbbk^{n}
. In particular,
[
Bic08
, Proposition 3.4]
proves that the universal Hopf algebra which coacts on
\Bbbk^{n}
is finite-dimensional and commutative if
n\leq 3
, but is infinite-dimensional and noncommutative if
n\geq 4
. It is interesting that a similar phenomenon occurs for involutive Hopf coactions on polynomial rings.

Although
H_{1}(n)
is noncommutative and involutive for
n\geq 3
, it is not clear whether
H_{1}(n)
or any its of its noncommutative Hopf quotients are cosemisimple. We state this as a question.

Question 8.9
.

Let
n\geq 3
. Is
H_{1}(n)
cosemisimple? If not, are there any noncommutative cosemisimple Hopf quotients of
H_{1}(n)
?
