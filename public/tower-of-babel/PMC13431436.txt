Predictive Risk Models for Frailty Onset in Older Adults: A Scoping Review of Methodological Trends, Model Performance, and Clinical Translation Gap
Authors: Jiechenming Xiao, Weihong Zhang, Dan Xu, Huiping Mao, Heng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIdentifying older adults at risk of frailty is crucial for early intervention. Although numerous prediction models have emerged, no scoping review has systematically mapped their methodological trends and barriers to clinical implementation. This scoping review aimed to examine methodological characteristics, model performance, and translational gaps in frailty onset prediction models for older adults.MethodsA systematic search of six Chinese and English databases was conducted from inception to February 2026following Arksey and O’Malley’s framework and the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines.ResultsThirty studies, reporting 31 frailty prediction models published between 2018 and 2026, were included. Six overarching trends emerged: Most studies originated from China (73.33%), and community-dwelling older adults were the predominant study population (73.33%). After 2024, longitudinal designs using larger public databases became more common; Machine learning was increasingly adopted (38.7%) but showed no clear advantage over logistic regression (median AUC 0.813 vs 0.860); Conventional predictors, including age, multimorbidity, and depression, remained dominant; Calibration was under-reported, particularly in machine learning models (50%); Clinical translation was limited, with external validation and Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis plus Artificial Intelligence (TRIPOD+AI) adherence each reported in only 22.6% of models, static presentation formats predominating (83.9%), and no studies evaluating health economic outcomes or prospective clinical impact.ConclusionFrailty prediction models are constrained by insufficient calibration reporting, limited external validation, and substantial translation barriers. Future research should prioritize rigorous external validation, TRIPOD+AI adherence, and clinically integrated digital tools supported by implementation science.Keywords: aging, older adults, frail elderly, machine learning, risk assessment, clinical translation

Introduction

Frailty is a dynamic syndrome associated with aging, with a reported global prevalence ranging from 5.8% to 33.3% among older adults, and is strongly associated with adverse outcomes such as falls, disability, and mortality.1–3 Unlike the irreversible nature of the aging process, frailty is considered reversible, and early identification and targeted interventions can delay or even reverse its progression.4,5 Therefore, the development of effective early risk stratification tools is of paramount importance for mitigating the disease burden in geriatric populations.

Frailty is broadly conceptualized as a state of increased vulnerability to stressors, resulting from age-related declines in physiological reserve across multiple organ systems.6 The most widely adopted operational definitions include the Fried Frailty Phenotype, which identifies frailty based on five physical criteria, and the Frailty Index, which operationalizes frailty as the accumulation of health deficits.7 Beyond its physical domain, frailty also encompasses psychological (eg, depression and cognitive decline), social (eg, social isolation), and nutritional dimensions, reflecting its multifaceted nature.5,7 Importantly, frailty is a dynamic state: individuals can transition between robust, prefrail, and frail statuses over time.8 Within this dynamic framework, frailty onset, defined as the transition from a non-frail to a frail state during follow-up, represents the critical target for prospective prediction.

Risk prediction models integrate multiple predictors to quantitatively estimate the probability of an individual developing frailty in the future, thereby supporting clinical decision making.9 In the context of frailty, such models are designed to estimate the probability of incident frailty over a specified period based on baseline characteristics, enabling earlier management and mitigation of adverse health outcomes. A critical distinction exists between prediction modelling and traditional epidemiological association studies: whereas the latter primarily identifies risk factors, the former emphasizes the quantitative assessment of predictive performance and clinical utility.10

Over the past decade, research on frailty risk prediction models has increased substantially, encompassing a diverse range of modeling approaches, including traditional regression methods and machine learning algorithms. Recent systematic reviews have provided initial syntheses of this literature. For instance, Kong et al11 systematically appraised the quality of frailty prediction models, and Ou et al12 conducted a meta-analysis specifically focusing on machine learning approaches for community-dwelling older adults. However, the exact nature and extent of this heterogeneity across specific methodological dimensions remain poorly characterized. Furthermore, a persistent lack of adherence to established reporting standards, such as the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis (TRIPOD) Statement, the TRIPOD + Artificial Intelligence (AI) extension, and the Prediction model Risk of Bias Assessment Tool (PROBAST), complicates the critical appraisal and comparison of existing models.9,13

Three critical knowledge gaps currently constrain the advancement of this field: (1) methodological trends remain unclear, as systematic reviews of missing data handling, validation strategies, and the evolution of modeling techniques are lacking; (2) a comprehensive landscape of model performance is absent, with a paucity of head-to-head comparisons of discrimination and calibration across different modeling methods; and (3) a pronounced clinical translational gap exists, given that model calibration, clinical utility, and deployable tools are seldom systematically evaluated, resulting in minimal tangible impact of published models in routine clinical practice.7,11,12

Given the considerable fragmentation of the existing evidence base and these unresolved knowledge gaps, a scoping review approach, which maps the research landscape and identifies overarching patterns rather than providing a narrow evidence synthesis, is particularly suited to this field. Accordingly, this review was designed to address three specific objectives are: first, to characterize the methodological features of frailty prediction studies, encompassing study design, data sources, modeling algorithms, predictor selection, and missing data handling, with particular attention to temporal trends; second, to synthesize and compare reported model performance metrics, including discrimination and calibration, across different modeling approaches; third, to critically appraise the clinical translational gap by examining external validation practices, adherence to reporting guidelines, and the availability of clinically deployable digital tools. Through this systematic mapping, we aim to provide a critical reality check on prevailing deficiencies, clarify the field’s evolution, and identify priority actions for future research—ranging from rigorous calibration and external validation to the development of deployable digital tools and implementation science studies. By delineating the current state and charting a path forward, this review seeks to establish a foundational framework for clinically meaningful and actionable frailty prediction models that ultimately support early, personalized prevention in older adults.

Methods

The decision to conduct a scoping review rather than a systematic review was based on differences in research objectives. Systematic reviews aim to synthesize evidence to answer specific clinical questions and to assess study quality, whereas scoping reviews are designed to map the research landscape, identify key characteristics, and delineate knowledge gaps.14,15 According to the typology proposed by Munn et al14 the present review addresses three of the six primary purposes of a scoping review: examining how research is conducted, identifying key characteristics of a concept, and analyzing knowledge gaps. This review was conducted in accordance with the framework established by Arksey and O’Malley14 and incorporated methodological refinements recommended by Levac et al16 and Peters et al17 to enhance methodological rigor. Stakeholder consultations were conducted to validate the review findings and enhance the study robustness. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines were followed to ensure transparent reporting.18 The review protocol was prospectively registered on the Open Science Framework (OSF) platform (https://osf.io/34px8) to enhance transparency and prevent duplication.

Search Methodology

PubMed, Web of Science Core Collection, Embase, Wanfang Database, and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) were systematically searched using a combination of subject terms and keywords to identify relevant studies from the inception of each database to February 28, 2026. The reference lists of the included articles were reviewed using the snowball method. Gray literature (eg, conference abstracts) was not searched because it often lacks comprehensive details on model development and validation, which could introduce bias. An experienced librarian assisted with database searching, validated the study methodology, and peer-reviewed the search strategy. A detailed example of the search strategy for a Chinese database (CNKI) and an English database (PubMed) is provided in Table S1.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: study design: cohort, case-control, or cross-sectional studies; population: adults aged ≥60 years, reflecting the geriatric population most at risk for frailty; outcome: risk prediction models for physical frailty, defined using validated tools such as Fried Frailty Phenotype or the Frailty Index; and model type: multivariate models (≥2 predictors) with clear descriptions of model development and validation methods, Models were required to incorporate at least two predictors, as frailty is a multidimensional syndrome influenced by multiple factors. Explicit development and validation reporting: models were required to clearly describe the methodology used for development and validation (eg, regression and machine learning) to ensure transparency and reproducibility. Studies were excluded if they were not published in Chinese or English; were conference proceedings, abstracts, reviews, commentaries, animal studies, or duplicate reports; had unavailable full texts; or contained incomplete data. Studies focusing on mental or cognitive frailty, or those describing only model construction methods, were excluded.

The selection of predictors and inclusion of prediction models in this review were guided by theoretical relevance and methodological consistency with the frailty construct. These factors are recognized as integral to frailty pathophysiology and progression, as supported by existing literature.11,19,20

Study Selection

The retrieved English-language studies were imported into EndNote X9.3.3 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA) and the Chinese studies were imported into NoteExpress (V3.7) (Beijing Aegean Software Co., Ltd., China). Duplicate records were first identified using the built-in deduplication functions of EndNote X9.3.3 and NoteExpress V3.7, followed by a manual review to remove any remaining duplicates. Study selection was conducted in two stages. Initial screening, involving independent assessment of titles and abstracts, was conducted by two pairs of reviewers (Pair 1: YH; Pair 2: XJ). Records that met the initial screening criteria were then subjected to full-text review, which was also performed independently within each pair. Disagreements were resolved through discussion within each pair, with a third reviewer (MH for Pair 1; XD for Pair 2) serving as a tie-breaker when consensus could not be reached. An additional search of reference lists was performed by one investigator (XJ) to identify any articles not captured through the electronic search. The final set of included studies was agreed upon by all authors. In alignment with the scoping review paradigm, no formal assessment of methodological quality or risk of bias was conducted.

Data Charting

Data extraction and assessment were guided by the Checklist for Critical Appraisal and Data Extraction for Systematic Reviews of Prediction Modeling Studies (CHARMS), which was applied in its original format to evaluate methodological transparency and reporting completeness of model development, validation, and deployment.21 A prepiloted, standardized data collection form was used to capture the following domains: study-level characteristics (eg, study design and sample size), model specifications (eg, analytical approach, number and categories of predictors), performance indices (discrimination assessed using the area under the curve [AUC] or C-index; calibration evaluated using the Hosmer-Lemeshow test p-value), and validation strategies (internal and external). Two reviewers (YH and XJ) independently performed data abstraction, and any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion; unresolved discrepancies were resolved by a third reviewer (MH).

Collating, Summarizing, and Reporting the Results

The synthesized findings are presented in four summary tables, supplemented by six additional tables (Tables S2–S7) that provide model-specific details. Within these tables, individual frailty prediction models are ordered by ascending publication date. Quantitative summarization of study characteristics relied on standard descriptive indices—namely frequencies, measures of central tendency (mean), and relative frequencies (percentages). All descriptive analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel 365. To summarize the characteristics of the frailty risk prediction models, basic qualitative content analysis was used, which is a widely recommended for synthesizing information in scoping reviews.

Consultation with Stakeholders

During the study, we sought input from various healthcare professionals, including geriatricians, community physicians, geriatric nurses, and community nurses. These professionals were consulted at the beginning of the project to ensure that the research topic was relevant to and applicable within their respective areas of practice. They provided insights that informed refinement of the study. After obtaining the study results, the preliminary findings were discussed with these professionals to evaluate the limitations and clinical applicability of existing frailty prediction models.

A total of 16,587 citations were identified through the systematic database search. After screening, 30 studies, reporting 31 prediction models published between 2018 and 2026, were included, with a notable increase in publication volume from 2024 onward. Trend 1: Geographic Concentration and Setting Predominance

Geographically, research activity was heavily concentrated in China, accounting for 73.33% (n=22) of the included studies, far exceeding contributions from Japan, South Korea, Thailand, Brazil, the Netherlands, and Spain. Regarding study settings, community-dwelling older adults were the predominant target population (73.33%, n=22), whereas hospitalized (16.67%) and long-term care populations (3.33%) were markedly underrepresented. This geographic and setting skewness limits the generalizability of existing models to institutionalized or acutely ill geriatric populations. Trend 2: The Longitudinal Turn and Database-Driven Scale Expansion

A clear temporal shift in study design was observed. Although cross-sectional designs still accounted for 66.67% (n=20) of all studies, a marked shift toward longitudinal designs emerged from 2024 onward, including six prospective cohorts (20%), two retrospective cohorts, and two other longitudinal studies. This transition was closely linked to the increasing use of large, publicly accessible longitudinal databases. Ten studies developed their models based on publicly available databases. Among the 30 studies with considerable variation in sample size, the median sample size was 1,183 participants (range: 214–60,930), and those with larger sample sizes (≥5,000 participants) were all derived from public longitudinal databases. Studies utilizing these databases achieved substantially larger sample sizes (median = 4,878 participants) compared with those using primary data collection (median < 500). Table 1 presents the characteristics of the included studies, including the comparative patterns outlined above.

Trend 3: The Algorithmic Diversification and Performance Paradox

A total of 31 predictive models for frailty risk assessment in older adults were reported across 30 studies (Abe et al23 constructed two models stratified by gender). Modelling approaches demonstrated a pronounced diversification. Logistic regression remained the dominant method (61.29%, 19/31 models) due to its interpretability, while machine learning adoption increased after 2024, encompassing random forest, XGBoost, and Bayesian networks (38.71%, 12/31 models). Figure 1 illustrates the temporal trends of the two modeling approaches: regression models and machine learning. Despite this algorithmic shift, no systematic performance advantage was observed for machine learning. The median AUC for machine learning models was 0.813, marginally lower than the 0.860 for traditional regression models. Trend 4: Persistent Reliance on Traditional Predictors and Nascent Novel Incorporation

Conventional clinical and sociodemographic predictors remained dominant: age (n=20), multimorbidity (n=11), depression (n=10), and activities of daily living (n=9) were the most frequently included. However, a nascent trend toward novel, digitally derived predictors emerged in a minority of recent studies, including wearable-sensor gait parameters, physical performance tests, and administrative claims data. Trend 5: Pervasive Methodological Under-Reporting

Methodological rigor was inconsistent across studies. Only 33.33% (n=10) explicitly reported their handling of missing data; among these, multiple imputation was the most common (n=8). Five studies reported excluding cases with missing data. More than half of the included studies did not report methods for handling missing data. Calibration reporting was markedly insufficient: although 74.19% (23/31) of models reported some calibration metric, this proportion dropped sharply among machine learning models, of which only half (6/12) provided calibration assessments. Figure 3b presents a comparison of calibration performance between regression models and machine learning. This underreporting undermines the clinical trustworthiness of predicted probability estimates. The detailed performance characteristics of the individual models are listed in Table 2.

Trend 6: The Research-to-Practice Translational Gap

A profound research-to-practice gap persisted across all translational stages. Adherence to reporting guidelines was notably low, with only 22.58% (7/31) of the models explicitly reporting adherence to the TRIPOD or TRIPOD + AI statements. External validation was performed for only 22.58% (7/31) of models and was mostly confined to the same country or database, without independent cross-population testing. Model presentation formats were largely static, with 83.87% (26/31) of the models presented as nomograms or regression equations; only five models provided interactive digital tools, such as web-based calculators or online tools. Only one study integrated the developed model into an electronic health record system. Evaluation of clinical utility was extremely limited. Decision curve analysis was performed in only 32.26% (10/31) of the models, and no studies conducted health economic evaluations or prospective validation of clinical impact. Clinical impact was assessed in only three studies that employed machine learning approaches. The clinical translation gap is visualized in Figure 5.

Discussion

This scoping review systematically mapped 31 frailty prediction models from 30 studies, revealing a paradoxical landscape: the field has undergone rapid methodological diversification, most notably the post-2024 surge in machine learning adoption and database-driven scale expansion, yet fundamental deficits in calibration reporting (particularly among machine learning models), external validation, and clinical deployment persist. These findings extend prior systematic reviews by providing a comprehensive, trend-oriented assessment of both methodological evolution and translational barriers, rather than focusing narrowly on quality appraisal or algorithm-specific performance. The discussion that follows interprets these six overarching trends to clarify their implications for model generalizability, methodological rigor, and clinical utility, ultimately outlining a roadmap to bridge the gap between prediction research and routine frailty prevention in older adults.

The Geographic and Study Settings Were Largely Concentrated, Which Limited the Generalizability of the Frailty Risk Prediction Models

The substantial concentration of frailty prediction research in China likely reflects the interplay of rapid population ageing, national policy emphasis on proactive health strategies,52 and favorable data accessibility through publicly available longitudinal databases such as CHARLS and CLHLS. While this concentration has enabled large-scale studies with substantial sample sizes, it raises important questions about generalizability. Frailty prevalence, phenotype expression, and associated risk factors may vary across populations due to genetic, environmental, lifestyle, and healthcare system differences.53 Models developed predominantly in Chinese community-dwelling populations may not perform equivalently in other ethnic groups or healthcare contexts without external validation and recalibration.

The underrepresentation of hospitalized and institutionalized older adults in the current evidence base represents a particularly critical gap. Frailty prevalence is substantially higher in these populations, and they frequently present with more complex multimorbidity, functional impairments, and acute medical conditions.19 However, only a minority of studies targeted these settings. This gap is especially concerning given that hospitalized older adults are at heightened risk of adverse outcomes where frailty screening could directly inform clinical management. Future research should prioritize model development and transportability testing in these under-represented settings and investigate whether predictors and model performance differ meaningfully across care settings.

Methodological Transformation and Database-Driven Scale Expansion of Frailty Risk Prediction Models

The observed transition from cross-sectional predominance toward longitudinal designs represents a substantive methodological advance. Cross-sectional studies, while useful for identifying associated factors, cannot establish temporal precedence between predictors and frailty onset, inherently conflating association with prediction.54 Longitudinal designs, by measuring predictors at baseline and assessing frailty outcomes during follow-up, align with the fundamental requirement of prediction models, which forecasts future events using baseline characteristics.55 This shift indicates increasing methodological maturity in the field and should be encouraged. However, it is also important to acknowledge that the majority of included models were derived from cross-sectional data, which predict prevalent frailty rather than incident frailty. This distinction limits the causal interpretability and temporal validity of these models in clinical prognostication, reinforcing our call for greater adoption of longitudinal designs in future research.

The concurrent rise in public database utilization has dramatically increased sample sizes, with database-derived studies achieving median sample sizes approximately tenfold larger than primary data collections. According to Riley et al56 such sample sizes mitigate overfitting risks, enhance parameter estimation stability, and improve the reliability of statistical power. However, this data-source shift introduces a trade-off: predictor selection becomes constrained by predefined database variables, potentially excluding novel biomarkers, environmental exposures, or fine-grained functional measures not captured in routine surveys. This constraint may explain, in part, the persistent reliance on traditional predictors observed in Trend 4. Furthermore, the geographical concentration of these databases (primarily CHARLS and CLHLS in China) reinforces the geographic skewness of the evidence base. Researchers should consider supplementing database-derived analyses with targeted primary data collection to capture novel predictor domains.

While the Algorithms Used in Frailty Risk Prediction Models are Growing Increasingly Diverse, the Model Performance Remains in Need of Further Enhancement

The pronounced diversification of modelling approaches reflects a broader trend toward data-driven methodologies in clinical prediction research, as shown by machine learning adoption increasing after 2024. Logistic regression remains the most widely used method, predominantly because of its favorable interpretability, which enables clinicians to understand the direction and clinical relevance of individual predictors.57 However, the absence of systematic performance advantages for machine learning over logistic regression warrants careful interpretation. This parity may reflect several factors. First, frailty risk is largely driven by well-established clinical and sociodemographic factors that linear models capture effectively; the relationship between these factors and frailty may be predominantly additive rather than highly interactive or non-linear.58 Second, many machine learning studies in this review used sample sizes insufficient for realizing their theoretical advantages; complex algorithms typically require substantially larger datasets to achieve optimal performance and avoid overfitting.59 Third, machine learning models often require more extensive hyperparameter tuning and rigorous internal validation to achieve generalizable results, including procedures that may not have been consistently implemented across the included studies.

Nevertheless, machine learning offers distinct advantages for handling high-dimensional heterogeneous data, as exemplified by Kawaguchi et al50 who used 697 candidate predictors from claims data in an XGBoost model and achieved favorable internal validation performance. The integration of explainable AI methods, such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) values, represents a promising direction for enhancing clinical acceptability by helping clinicians identify key drivers of individual risk estimates.47,50 Future research should integrate XAI methodologies from the early stages of model development and validate generalizability using independent, multicenter datasets.

Frailty Risk Prediction Models Continue to Be Dominated by Traditional Predictors, with Emerging Predictors Being Underrepresented

The predominance of conventional predictors, including age, multimorbidity, depression, and activities of daily living, reflects their robust and replicable epidemiological evidence base as risk factors for frailty.60–62 These factors are clinically accessible, well-understood by practitioners, and easily collected in routine assessments, making them practical choices for model development. However, this persistent reliance also reveals a conservative tendency in predictor selection that may constrain the predictive ceiling of current models.

Encouragingly, a nascent trend toward novel, digitally derived predictors has emerged in a minority of recent studies, including wearable-sensor gait parameters,30 physical performance measures,49 and administrative claims data.50 These efforts reflect a growing recognition that frailty is a multidimensional syndrome influenced by physiological, functional, psychological, social, and environmental domains. However, such explorations remain at an early stage and face multiple challenges: standardization of measurements across studies, comparability of findings, and clinical accessibility of novel assessment tools.

Notably, biomarkers (eg, interleukin-6, C-reactive protein) and environmental factors (eg, air pollution) have been associated with frailty progression in epidemiological studies,6,63–65 yet none of the models in this review incorporated such variables. This gap represents a missed opportunity to enhance model performance and clinical interpretability. Studies relying on public databases are constrained by predefined variable sets, reflecting a fundamental trade-off between data depth (rich, novel predictors) and sample breadth (large, generalizable populations). Future research should explore strategies to integrate novel predictor domains, including biomarkers, monomolecular data, and environmental exposures, while maintaining adequate sample sizes and methodological rigor.

The Methodological Approaches Used in Frailty Risk Prediction Models are Critically Inadequate and Warrant Substantial Enhancement

The inadequate handling and reporting of missing data represents a critical methodological concern. Only one-third of studies explicitly reported their approach to missing data. Multiple imputation, the preferred approach for preserving uncertainty and yielding unbiased estimates, was used in only eight studies, while five relied on complete-case analysis, a method that can introduce substantial selection bias when data are not missing completely at random.57 The robustness and reproducibility of prediction models depend not only on modelling algorithms but also on transparent data preparation procedures; without such transparency, readers cannot adequately evaluate potential biases.66 This deficit is particularly concerning given that missing data are ubiquitous in both clinical and community-based datasets.

The marked underreporting of calibration, particularly among machine learning models, of which only half provided calibration assessments, represents another major methodological shortcoming. Calibration assesses the agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes; even a well-discriminating model with poor calibration can systematically overestimate or underestimate risk, potentially misleading clinical decisions.67 Underreporting of calibration may be attributable to machine learning’s historical emphasis on predictive accuracy over probability reliability, compounded by the absence of embedded calibration frameworks in many algorithmic workflows. This gap directly contravenes both the TRIPOD statement and the TRIPOD+AI extension, which mandate transparent reporting of calibration metrics.13,68 Notably, while explainable AI methods such as SHAP partially alleviate concerns about model interpretability, they do not substitute for rigorous calibration assessment. Future studies should consider calibration assessment an indispensable component of model development and validation, ensuring accurate probability estimates alongside predictive precision.

Translational Barriers to Clinical Implementation of Frailty Risk Prediction Models

The findings reveal a profound and multifaceted gap between model development and clinical deployment, manifesting across four interrelated domains. First, adherence to reporting guidelines is critically low, with only 22.58% of models explicitly following TRIPOD or TRIPOD+AI statements. The TRIPOD statement mandates detailed reporting of model development processes, predictor selection, missing data handling, and internal validation methods, while TRIPOD+AI extends these requirements to encompass explainability and calibration reporting for machine learning models.13,68 This low adherence limits readers’ ability to evaluate methodological quality and impedes model replication across different datasets, which is a fundamental prerequisite for establishing generalizability.

Second, external validation remains the most formidable barrier. Over three-quarters of models did not undergo external validation, and among the few that did, validation was predominantly confined to the same country or database as the development sample, lacking independent cross-population testing. Without validation in independent populations that differ geographically, temporally, or clinically from derivation cohorts, models remain essentially untested hypotheses rather than clinically actionable tools.69 This limitation directly impedes healthcare policy formulation and clinical decision making, as practitioners cannot confidently apply models to their local populations.

Third, presentation formats are overwhelmingly static, with 83.87% of models published as nomograms or regression equations. While nomograms are visually accessible, their manual application is time-consuming and error-prone in busy clinical settings. The scarcity of interactive digital tools, including web-based calculators, electronic health record plugins, or mobile applications, reflects a disconnect between prediction modelling and implementation science, constituting a substantial barrier to clinical adoption.

Fourth, evidence for clinical utility remains fragmented. Decision curve analysis was performed in only one-third of models, and no studies provided health economic evaluations or prospective validation of clinical impact. In the absence of such evidence, clinicians and policymakers cannot determine whether implementing a model improves patient outcomes or represents a cost-effective use of resources.70 The evidence gap implies that even a model achieving excellent discrimination remains clinically non-actionable. According to implementation science frameworks, the clinical impact of prediction models must be substantiated through prospective studies comparing model-guided interventions with usual care.71 Given these multifaceted barriers, future research should adhere rigorously to TRIPOD+AI reporting guidelines, prioritize independent external validation across diverse populations and settings, develop user-friendly digital tools integrated into clinical workflows, and design implementation science studies to evaluate clinical impact and health economic value. Only by systematically addressing these translational gaps can frailty prediction models be integrated into routine clinical practice.

Future Directions

Addressing these interconnected gaps requires a coordinated, multi-stakeholder effort. First, researchers should adhere rigorously to the TRIPOD+AI reporting guidelines throughout all stages of model development and validation, with particular attention to calibration reporting and missing data handling. Second, independent external validation across diverse geographical, ethnic, and clinical settings must become a mandatory prerequisite for clinical implementation, rather than an optional post-hoc exercise. Third, model development should incorporate implementation science principles from the outset, including stakeholder engagement, usability testing, and the development of deployable digital tools integrated into clinical workflows. Fourth, researchers should explore novel predictor domains, including biomarkers, immunomolecular data, and environmental factors, beyond conventional clinical indicators, while balancing the trade-off between data depth and sample breadth. Fifth, the generation of health economic and clinical impact evidence through prospective implementation studies is essential for translating predictive accuracy into tangible patient benefit. Only by systematically addressing these translational barriers can frailty prediction models fulfil their potential to support early, precise, and individualized frailty screening and intervention.

Limitations

This review has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its conclusions. First, the search was restricted to Chinese and English databases; studies published in other languages and grey literature (eg, conference abstracts and dissertations) were not included, which may have introduced selection bias. Second, consistent with scoping review methodology, formal assessment of methodological quality and risk of bias was not conducted for the included studies. Therefore, this review aimed to map the research landscape rather than provide graded evidence-based recommendations. Readers should critically appraise the methodological rigor of individual models when citing them. Third, heterogeneity was evident in the definitions of frailty outcomes across the included studies (encompassing nine distinct assessment instruments), which may have compromised the comparability of model performance. No subgroup analyses stratified by frailty instruments were conducted, and future studies may further elucidate the impact of this heterogeneity on model performance. Fourth, this review focused exclusively on prediction models for physical frailty and did not include studies addressing cognitive or psychological frailty. Future updates should broaden the search scope and consider conducting a systematic review and meta-analysis of high-quality models to further advance our understanding of frailty prediction.

Conclusion

This scoping review systematically mapped the research landscape of prediction models for incident frailty in older adults. By synthesizing the evidence into six overarching methodological and translational trends, this review provides the comprehensive, trend-oriented framework that identifies not only the rapid algorithmic diversification and database-driven scale expansion but also the paradoxical absence of discriminative benefit from machine learning and the persistent gaps in calibration reporting, external validation, and clinical deployment, thereby offering a clear roadmap for future research and implementation efforts.

The findings indicate that research output in this field has grown steadily, with methodological approaches transitioning from traditional regression toward machine learning approaches and from cross-sectional designs toward longitudinal cohort studies. The use of public databases has improved both sample size and standardization. Nevertheless, notable deficiencies in methodological rigor persist, most prominently reflected in inadequate reporting of calibration, inconsistent handling of missing data, and a marked lack of external validation. Predictors are still dominated by conventional clinical indicators, while novel dimensions, such as biomarkers and environmental factors, have not yet been effectively integrated. Most critically, the translation of models into clinical practice faces multifaceted barriers, including low adherence to reporting guidelines, insufficient external validation, predominantly static presentation formats, and a lack of clinical utility evidence, which represent the main barriers. Future research should prioritize the following: rigorous adherence to the TRIPOD+AI reporting guidelines, independent external validation across diverse settings, the development of digital tools integrated into clinical workflows, and the generation of clinical utility and health economic evidence through implementation science studies. Only by bridging the translational gap between research and practice can frailty prediction models support early and precise screening and individualized interventions for frailty in older adults.
