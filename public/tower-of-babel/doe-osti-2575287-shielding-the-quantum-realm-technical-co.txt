 ABSTRACT

Advancements in quantum technology, which include quantum computing, quantum sensing, quantum
communications, and associated research, continue to revolutionize various fields of study by solving
challenging problems deemed too complex for classical computer infrastructure [1][2]. However, as
quantum systems also become increasingly complex, it is critical to secure their electromagnetic
operational environments and assess the associated impacts on quantum systems and infrastructure [3].
The goal of this synopsis is to bring attention to the susceptibility of quantum technology facilities to
potential issues related to the radio frequency (RF) environment within the broader electromagnetic
context [4]. A framework is proposed for identifying, mitigating, monitoring, and auditing these often
complex operational environments. This framework also emphasizes the importance of collaboration
among researchers, engineers, and security specialists to address the evolving challenges of quantum
technologies and RF environments. By establishing best practices for the consideration and evaluation of
these criteria, the operational integrity, resilience, and security of quantum technology systems and
facilities can be enhanced to support continued development and application in research and industry.

 1. INTRODUCTION

1.1 OVERVIEW

Quantum computing as described herein is a type of computing that uses principles of quantum
mechanics to process data and communicate with classical computing systems [4]. Differing from
classical binary bits which are either 0 or 1, quantum bits (i.e., qubits) can be both 0 and 1 simultaneously
owing to the superposition principle [3]. Qubits are the basic building blocks of quantum information and
allow quantum computers to perform complex calculations in parallel.

The current advancements present promising potential to research areas such as cryptography, materials
science, and artificial intelligence (AI). As quantum technologies continue to advance, different types of
qubits (e.g., superconducting qubits, trapped ions, topological qubits) require highly secure
electromagnetic environments to preserve their fragile quantum states. RF signals are integral to quantum
computing—they precisely control and manipulate qubit states at specific frequencies to maintain
coherence and signal integrity in systems operating at cryogenic temperatures [4].

This report will highlight the basic principles of RF sensitivities inherent in quantum technology and
research facilities and relevant strategies that may be employed to minimize, to the extent possible,
accidental or intentional interference with quantum infrastructures.

1.1.1 About Quantum Computers and Technologies

Quantum technology takes advantage of quantum mechanics to process information in novel ways. The
ability to solve complicated problems exponentially faster, such as factoring large numbers (important to
cryptography) or simulating complex systems, is a significant advancement [4].

Qubits as the basic unit of information in quantum computers are central to their operations. Unlike
traditional bits, which can only be in one state at a time, qubits can be in many states simultaneously,
hence enabling parallel processing and exponential computational capacity [5]. However, the very nature
of qubits also makes them sensitive to external interference, particularly from RF spectrum noise [6].
Furthermore, quantum computer infrastructure generates significant RF emanations [6][7].

 1

1.1.2 Diversity of Qubits

The diversity of qubits is a key aspect to understand. They require differing support infrastructures in
order to be utilized. Each type of qubit has unique operational principles, advantages, and challenges:

  Superconducting qubits are based on superconducting circuits and operate at cryogenic
 temperatures, thereby enabling fast gate operations and integration with classical electronics.
 However, they are highly susceptible to environmental noise and decoherence [4].
  Trapped ion qubits utilize lasers to manipulate their states, which offers higher fidelity but
 requires complex setups [4].
  Topological qubits, although still experimental, encode quantum information using principles of
 topology and particles called “anyons,” which offers theoretical robustness against errors [4].
  Photonic qubits encode information in the quantum states of photons, thereby allowing room-
 temperature operation, although some components such as detectors still require cryogenic
 cooling, and long-distance transmission [4].
  Semiconductor qubits use quantum dots or impurities in semiconductors to form qubits, thereby
 taking advantage of electron spin states for quantum operations [4][8].
  Nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) qubits manipulate nuclear spins using magnetic fields and
         RF pulses [6][8].
  Atomic qubits rely on individual atoms or molecules, such as Rydberg atoms, thereby offering
 longer coherence times and higher fidelity [4].
  Hybrid qubits combine different qubit types to leverage their strengths [9].

Qubits are very sensitive to the electromagnetic interference (EMI)/RF environment and need to be
isolated as much as possible to maintain their quantum states [4][10]. Factors such as thermal
fluctuations, EMI, cosmic rays, mechanical vibrations, and magnetic field fluctuations can cause qubits to
lose coherence [4][11]. For example, superconducting qubit temperatures must be maintained at near
absolute zero (−459.67℉) to function optimally [12][13]. The sensitivity of various qubit generation
schemes to EMI varies extensively and is a relevant consideration to sustain when designing quantum
computing facilities [4]. This report explores these sensitivities and strategies to shield quantum systems
from interference, alteration, or misappropriation to protect the integrity of quantum operations and
information.

1.2 QUANTUM COMPUTING AND RESEARCH INFRASTRUCTURE

1.2.1 Facility Infrastructure Overview

Quantum technology and research facilities integrate specialized designs to support the specific
operational needs of quantum systems. The infrastructure accommodates the extreme sensitivity of qubits
to environmental factors, such as temperature fluctuations, EMI, and mechanical vibrations [10][14].
Some common facility requirements are listed below:

 1. Thermal Requirements

  Cryogenic Cooling Systems—Essential for maintaining temperatures near absolute zero, which
 are needed for superconducting qubits [13][15].
  Temperature Stability—Systems need to minimize thermal changes to prevent decoherence in
           qubits [15].
  Humidity Control—Precise humidity control is necessary to prevent condensation and maintain
 the integrity of systems [15][16].

 2. Electromagnetic and Radiation Requirements

  Electromagnetic Shielding—Facilities must isolate quantum systems from external EMI using
 special shielding materials [11].
  RF-Isolated Environments—Attenuation levels should be verified to prevent RF noise from
 disrupting operations [11].
  Radiation Shielding—Facilities may require shielding against high-energy particles that can
 cause interference with quantum systems [17].
  Low-Radiation Materials—Construction materials with minimal radioactive content should be
 used to reduce background radiation [17].

 3. Mechanical Requirements

  Vibration Isolation Platforms—Mechanical vibrations and shocks should be mitigated using
 isolation systems to maintain qubit coherence [18].
  Structural Stability—Facilities should be designed to minimize mechanical disruptions from
 external sources.

 4. Power Supply Requirements

  Clean Power Sources—Facilities need a filtered, continuous, and stable power supply to prevent
 electrical noise [19].
  Redundancy—Facilities need backup power systems to ensure uninterrupted operation during
 outages or service fluctuations [19].

 5. Security Requirements

  Physical Access Controls—Consideration must be given to using multi-layered security
 measures to prevent unauthorized access to sensitive equipment and data [20].
  Cybersecurity Measures—Facilities should implement NIST-recommended encryption protocols
 and cybersecurity controls. Quantum-resistant encryption is recommended.

 3

 6. Specialized Laboratory Spaces

  Cleanrooms—Some lab spaces may include cleanroom environments for assembling and
 maintaining components [10].
  Shielded Test Chambers—Some facilities need dedicated spaces for testing quantum systems in
 EMI-safe environments [11].

 7. Advanced Environmental Monitoring Systems

  Continuous Monitoring—Real-time monitoring of temperature, humidity, EMI, RF, and
 vibration levels is needed to ensure the best operating conditions for quantum systems [16][18].
  Automated Alerts—Many systems have automated alerts to advise of deviations from baseline
 norms, thereby enabling response to potential disruptions.

 8. Quantum Network Infrastructure

  Quantum Communication Channels—Facilities may require infrastructure to support emerging
 quantum communication, such as fiber-optic networks for transmitting quantum information
 [22].
  Quantum Key Distribution (QKD) Systems—Facilities are integrating QKD systems for secure
 communication between quantum facilities [22]. These systems are useful in both quantum and
 classical networks.

 9. Safety Systems

  Cryogen Safety Measures—These facilities may have specific safeguards for cryogenic system
 leaks or malfunctions, including sensors that detect low oxygen levels and emergency ventilation
 systems that activate automatically [23].
  Fire Suppression Systems—To prevent chemical fire extinguishing agents from damaging
 sensitive equipment, fire suppression systems in these spaces often rely on inert gases like argon
 or nitrogen instead of water or conductive materials [24].

 10.Redundancy and Fault-Tolerant Systems

  Redundant Systems—Power backup systems are used for critical infrastructure components,
 such as cooling and environmental controls, to ensure uninterrupted operation [19].
  Fault-Tolerant Design—Overall infrastructure is designed to minimize the impact of component
 failures on quantum operations [19].

Quantum computing facilities need carefully coordinated infrastructure systems to meet the specific
demands of quantum technology. These systems are critical for preserving qubit stability, ensuring
consistent operation, preventing disruptions, and protecting sensitive data.

Notably, data centers are evolving into hybrid facilities that incorporate quantum computing [25][26].
These hybrid facilities will be confronted with the considerations outlined herein to realize optimal
operational capability.

1.2.2 SCADA and IoT

Supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA) systems and internet of things (IoT) devices form the
backbone of industrial controls for infrastructure around the world. Many of these devices utilize RF for

 4

communication links that provide command and control functions. To compound the complications in
providing adequate security for these devices, along with their critical operational roles, these devices
frequently remain inadequately protected against RF-based threats because legacy control systems often
lack modern security features [27].

The wireless nature of these devices extends the attack surface beyond the physical security boundaries,
thereby enabling remote exploitation without the need for facility access. Cybersecurity experts have
recognized this discrepancy and continue to warn critical infrastructure service providers and offer best
practices for securing facilities [28]. However, cybersecurity usually begins when the RF has landed on
the controlling device or when examining protocol RF data traffic and triggering a security event and
incident management device. This area is further discussed in subsequent sections utilizing the MITRE
ATT&CK framework as a means to extend RF, cyber, and physical security to the quantum realm [27].

1.3 QUANTUM NETWORKING

Quantum networking relies on quantum phenomena such as entanglement and superposition to provide
secure communication, distributed computing, and enhanced sensing [29]. Unlike classical networks, it
faces unique challenges tied to the fragility of quantum states and the principles of quantum mechanics
[4][21]. RF sensitivities remain in quantum networks, including combined quantum/classical network
interfaces.

Quantum networking presents several challenges in maintaining quantum states during information
transmission, such as preserving entanglement across distances, protecting against decoherence,
developing quantum repeaters for long-distance communication, creating interfaces between quantum and
classical systems, standardizing quantum network protocols, and ensuring the security of RF in the
network infrastructure needs further examination [22].

 2. RF SENSITIVITY IN QUANTUM COMPUTING AND RESEARCH

2.1 AWARENESS OF COMMON RF FREQUENCIES POSING CHALLENGES AROUND
 QUANTUM COMPUTING AND RESEARCH FACILITIES

Quantum computing systems operate within specific RF ranges, depending on the type of qubit, and must
be protected from interference [13][15]. These RF-based systems support control signals, readout
frequencies, qubit coupling, and clock systems [4]. Because of how commonplace RF signals are due to
cellular phone calls, music, emergency services radios, SCADA, various IoT devices, and more, it is
essential to have an awareness of common RF frequencies that could introduce challenges for efficient
operations at quantum computing and research facilities.

2.2 RF SENSITIVITY IN QUANTUM HARDWARE

RF interference creates a challenging environment for quantum technologies, thus threatening the
viability of complex quantum operations. Decoherence, the process wherein qubits prematurely lose their
quantum state while interacting with the surrounding environment, is induced by external RF signals and
is a primary cause of RF interference. This process effectively turns quantum information into classical
information and erodes the fragile superposition states essential for quantum computing [4].
Simultaneously, crosstalk errors emerge when unwanted resonances occur during the application of RF
pulses meant to implement quantum gates on neighboring qubits. This allows unintended interactions
between quantum elements [30]. These proximity-based interactions can degrade computational accuracy
in qubit architectures. In cryogenic quantum systems, RF energy absorption can cause thermal

 5

fluctuations that raise operating temperatures and, as a result, destabilize qubits that require near-absolute
zero conditions to function properly [13][15].

 3. PHYSICAL SECURITY

Quantum technology facilities require strong physical security measures to protect advanced systems and
critical research data. The International Organization for Standardization (ISO) provides an
internationally recognized standards framework for protecting facilities and data. Specifically, ISO 27001
Annex A11 discusses the need for physical security [31]. This framework is useful for establishing a
baseline security effort to ensure a safe and accountable operational environment.

Modern access systems verify identities and manage entry permissions, thus restricting access to only
approved staff in high-security zones. Advanced camera networks continuously monitor entry points,
hallways, and equipment zones, which helps prevent unauthorized access and aids post-incident reviews.
Combining elements such as personnel screening, barriers, access controls, visitor logs, and live
surveillance creates a multi-layered approach to strengthen defenses against physical risks [19]. These
protocols help maintain the security and integrity of quantum research operations. Security measures must
also align with safety standards, regulatory requirements, and audit procedures [19].

 4. INTERSECTION OF CYBERSECURITY AND RF

4.1 RELATIONSHIP OF RF TO CYBERSECURITY IN QUANTUM COMPUTING

RF risks—whether from natural sources or human activity—are a serious challenge for quantum systems
because qubits are fragile and easily disrupted by perturbations in their surrounding environment. Even
minor RF interference can crash operations or enable attacks that exploit both digital and physical
weaknesses [28].

To protect quantum systems, evaluation teams should evaluate how RF threats intersect with
cybersecurity. Combining wireless signal defenses, physical safeguards (e.g., access controls), and
cybersecurity protocols into a unified plan could be an effective approach. Addressing these overlapping
risks allows for effective risk management for an entire system.

4.2 MITRE ATT&CK FRAMEWORK

The MITRE ATT&CK framework is a comprehensive matrix of tactics and techniques used by
adversaries to compromise and exploit systems. Although the framework primarily focuses on cyber
threats, some techniques can be related to RF-based attack vectors and common RF frequencies in
quantum computing facilities [28]. The framework presents an opportunity to translate a commonly
accepted format to identify mechanisms to shield the quantum realm and interconnect cyber-secure
operations.

When assessing RF vulnerabilities within the lexicon of the MITRE ATT&CK framework, specific
tactics, techniques, and procedures employed by an adversary may include the following:

  Initial Access (T1071)—RF systems may be susceptible to unauthorized access through the
 exploitation of RF communication protocols or weak encryption methodologies. These loopholes
 could allow adversaries to gain an initial foothold in the facility’s network or act as a launch
 point for disruption or alteration [28].

 6

  Execution (T1203)—This could manifest as an adversary executing malicious RF signals or
 commands to control quantum systems adversely or alter intended qubit functions [28].

  Collection - Network Sniffing (T1040)—Eavesdropping on RF signals can be likened to network
 sniffing, whereby adversaries intercept and analyze communications to extract information. RF
 emanations could facilitate the collection of sensitive data, with adversaries utilizing software-
 defined radios or spectrum analyzers to capture signals and reconstruct sensitive data being
 communicated [28].

  Impact - Network Denial of Service (T1498)—RF jamming can prevent communication by
 overwhelming the RF environment with noise, effectively causing a denial of service. In
 addition, electromagnetic pulse attacks can cause a denial of service by disrupting electronic
 systems and communications within a facility [28].

4.3 PAYMENT CARD INDUSTRY DATA SECURITY STANDARDS

The Payment Card Industry Data Security Standards (PCI DSS) established cybersecurity standards for
the payment card industry demanding strong safeguards and auditing practices [32]. PCI DSS specifically
identifies the need in Requirement 11.2.1 to conduct routine scans for rogue wireless devices [32]. The
need to identify and detect all wireless access points, both authorized and unauthorized, is highlighted to
reduce the paths for unauthorized access to critical systems. Although PCI DSS is directly applicable to
the payment card industry, the principles from Requirement 11.2.1 are relevant for establishing a secure
working environment for quantum or other RF-sensitive environments.

Like the MITRE ATT&CK framework, PCI DSS provides a useful tool to reference and use as a guide to
take advantage of established and effective working models to advance the success of secure operations in
the quantum technology environment.

 5. MITIGATION AND RESILIENCE STRATEGIES

5.1 EMI/RF SHIELDING AND MITIGATION TECHNIQUES

Shielding quantum systems facilities from EMI requires practical, layered defenses. Some common
defense techniques include the following:

  Faraday Cages—Enclosures made of conductive metals (e.g., copper or aluminum) block outside
 radio signals. These cages protect delicate hardware, such as quantum processors, from stray
 signals emitted by nearby electronics or cell towers [33][34].

  RF-Absorbing Materials—Foam panels or wall liners are made from carbon-loaded polymers.
 These are installed in walls, floors, or ceilings to prevent interference from seeping into lab
           spaces [19].

  Shielded Cabling—Cables wrapped in conductive braiding or foil reduce interference during
 data or power transfer. These are used for connections between lab equipment, especially near
 cryogenic systems or qubit arrays [15].

  RF Filters—Adds signal filters to power lines or control systems to block disruptive frequencies
 (e.g., Wi-Fi bands) but allow essential signals, like qubit control pulses, to pass through
 unimpeded [35].

 7

  Grounding and Bonding—Properly ground all metal surfaces, from equipment racks to door
 frames, to redirect stray signals safely into the earth and prevent static buildup that could
 destabilize experiments [19].

  RF-Optimized Enclosures—Build equipment housing using seamless, conductive materials (e.g.,
 welded aluminum). Ensure doors and vents have conductive gaskets to prevent signals from
 leaking through gaps [15].

  Isolation Chambers—Use dedicated shielded rooms with thick metal walls and filtered utilities
 to provide ultra-low-noise environments. These are critical for testing prototype systems or
 running long-duration experiments [11][19].

  Distance—Place electronics or items that emit EMI further away from quantum systems to
 reduce interference by attenuating the emission [17].

By implementing these RF shielding techniques, quantum systems facilities can significantly reduce the
risk of RF interference, thereby ensuring the reliable and secure operation of quantum systems. Regular
testing and maintenance of shielding measures are also important to ensure continued effectiveness.

5.2 INCIDENT RESPONSE STRATEGIES

The development and maintenance of a comprehensive incident response plan for detecting breaches or
anomalies—including reporting procedures, communications, and recovery strategies—should be
developed for each quantum computing or research facility based on the specific quantum infrastructure
needed [19]. However, this is merely a framework for facilities to adapt from for best practices as
quantum fields continue to rapidly develop.

An incident response plan for quantum computing facilities should address the unique challenges posed
by quantum infrastructure and potentially sensitive data. Key elements include the following:

  Risk Assessment and Threat Identification—Identify potential vulnerabilities specific to
 quantum systems, such as quantum hardware, algorithms, and cryptographic protocols [36].

  Detection Mechanisms—Implement advanced monitoring tools to detect anomalies, breaches, or
 system failures in real-time, including quantum-specific threats like side-channel attacks or
 quantum algorithm exploitation [36]

  Incident Classification—Define categories and severity levels for incidents to prioritize response
 efforts based on impact [36].

  Reporting Procedures—Establish clear guidelines for reporting incidents internally and
 externally, including timelines, responsible parties, and escalation protocols [36].

  Containment Strategies—Develop methods to isolate affected systems or components to prevent
 further damage or data compromise [36].

  Damage Assessment—Evaluate the scope and impact of the incident, including potential data
 loss, system downtime, or compromised quantum computations [36].

  Recovery Plans—Outline steps to restore quantum systems, including hardware recalibration,
 algorithm revalidation, and data integrity checks [36].

 8

  Communication—Define roles and responsibilities for internal and external communication
 during incidents to ensure transparency and maintain coordination among stakeholders [36].

  Legal and Regulatory Compliance—Address requirements for reporting incidents to regulatory
 bodies and ensure adherence to data protection laws [36].

  Post-Incident Analysis—Conduct thorough reviews to identify root causes, improve response
 strategies, and implement preventive measures [36].

  Training and Awareness—Regularly train staff on incident response procedures; update them on
 emerging quantum-specific threats [36].

  Testing and Drills—Periodically test the incident response plan through simulations to ensure
 readiness and identify gaps [36].

By incorporating these elements, incidents at quantum technology facilities can be effectively managed
and advanced systems and sensitive data will be safeguarded.

5.3 NEED FOR RECOVERY STRATEGIES

Although recovery strategies are not the focus of this paper, it is important to have a plan to reduce the
effects of an incident on the overall safe recovery and continued operations of the quantum computing
and research facilities [36].

 6. OPERATIONAL BEST PRACTICES

6.1 PROACTIVE RF ENVIRONMENTAL ASSESSMENT

6.1.1 Inventory of RF-Emitting Devices

Quantum computing and research facilities demand a stable electromagnetic environment. RF-emitting
devices can generate noise that degrades qubit coherence and disrupts system operations [4]. Maintaining
an inventory of these devices verifies each unit’s emission profile and operational status. Detailed records
support timely inspections, calibration, and targeted interference mitigation.

Comprehensive documentation underpins safety protocols. An accurate inventory enables rapid
identification of anomalous emissions and faulty equipment. Systematic monitoring guides remediation
measures and adherence to electromagnetic safety standards, thus reducing risks and preserving quantum
system fidelity.

6.1.2 Identification of RF Emission

Quantum computing facilities, like other advanced facilities or laboratories, likely consist of many
sources of EMI and spurious emissions throughout the RF spectrum. Identifying the source and severity
of these emissions is imperative for reducing the effects to nearby quantum systems. These sources fall
into three groups: purpose-built emitters (e.g., wireless networking equipment), accidental/incidental
emitters from commonly used electronics, and natural sources [8][37].

Intentional radiators are emitters that are built specifically to transmit radio waves (e.g., cell phones, lab-
grade signal transmitters, or wireless sensors used in experiments) [37].

 9

Incidental radiators are emitters which include devices leaking RF signals despite not being designed to
do so. Computers, power adapters, and common lighting fixtures, especially those with components like
electronic ballasts, can create stray signals. For instance, aging fluorescent or certain LED light fixtures
could interfere with experiments if internal electronics lack proper shielding [37].

Although generally not a direct concern in most laboratory settings, natural sources of EMI, such as solar
flares, radiation from cement, and items with natural magnetic properties, can be problematic for quantum
systems [11].

6.1.3 Advantages of Inventory

Maintaining a complete inventory of every identifiable RF emitting device near quantum systems helps
promote awareness of risks to equipment. This awareness enables personnel to prevent problems and
reduces the complications from reacting to problems.

The primary advantage of maintaining an inventory is to spot all possible interference sources and log
every item emitting RF energy, such as outdated computer monitors, lab tools with aging components,
and everyday appliances like microwaves and badge scanners.

With a complete inventory, evaluating teams can rank devices by how much risk each device may impose
upon the system. Factors such as overlapping device frequencies, signal strength, and proximity to
quantum hardware determine which items need immediate attention. A wireless router in an adjacent
room, for instance, might be marked for closer inspection.

Knowing which devices are problematic may provide mitigation solutions commensurate with the
severity of identified risk. High-risk items might be moved to a separate area, wrapped in shielding
material, or replaced with low-emission models [38]. In some cases, adjusting how and when high-risk
items are used, such as restricting operation to off-hours, can reduce interference.

An inventory also functions as a reference for normal RF levels in a facility. Regular scans of the
environment help to catch new or unusual signals. If a mysterious spike appears, teams can cross-
reference it with the inventory to quickly locate the source (e.g., a newly installed smart thermostat or a
malfunctioning power supply).

Maintaining documentation of interference issues could help identify concerning patterns, such as if
certain equipment fails more often under specific conditions or shielding degrades faster than expected.
These insights could guide future procurements and laboratory layouts, thereby implementing lessons
learned.

Fewer RF disruptions mean more uptime for quantum systems to produce reliable data for experiments.
Increased efficiency mitigates unnecessary system performance concerns, thus allowing researchers to
focus on conducting worthwhile experiments and less time troubleshooting potential operational issues.

6.2 SPECTRUM MANAGEMENT

Custodians of quantum facilities should frequently evaluate the electromagnetic environment to ensure
systems operate as intended. In the United States, the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) sets
the guidelines for RF emissions standards regarding the acceptable emanations within a given frequency
range. These facilities must comply with FCC guidelines to prevent undesirable interference with other
networks [39].

 10

Conducting regular RF spectrum checks in quantum facilities and laboratories can assist in the
identification of and planning for addressing stray signals that could throw off experiments or damage
equipment. Ongoing monitoring systems provide real-time alerts for unusual activity, thereby allowing
personnel to act before small issues become major problems [40].

6.3 IN-PLACE MONITORING SYSTEM (IPMS) ENSURING ELECTROMAGNETIC
 CLEANLINESS

In-place monitoring systems (IPMSs) installed directly in a quantum facility are essential for maintaining
extremely quiet electromagnetic conditions. The systems constantly track, analyze, and pinpoint
interference sources, thereby helping personnel stay ahead of any problems while maintaining the system.

An IPMS can catch issues early, whether it’s a faulty lab instrument emitting stray signals or an
unauthorized device nearby. This protects both the integrity and accuracy of experiments on a given
system in addition to the physical security of the hardware. Such systems provide indications as to
whether existing shielding, like specialized wall coatings or Faraday cages, is working within
specifications. This provides ongoing feedback regarding maintenance efforts that may need to be
undertaken [4][6].

Because quantum systems seem to react to even the faintest EMI, IPMS becomes a core tool for
maintaining quantum system viability. As quantum systems become more prevalent and complex,
monitoring tools will be expected to evolve. Future work should explore expanding IPMS beyond
traditional RF monitoring practices because current detectors could miss interference from emerging
devices or unconventional signal types.

6.4 MEDICAL DEVICE EFFECT ON RF ENVIRONMENT

Personal medical devices can potentially be affected by the RF environment and interfere with quantum
technology due to their electromagnetic emissions. Common examples are listed below:

  Pacemakers—Pacemakers emit RF signals to communicate with external programming devices.
 These signals can contribute to RF noise in sensitive environments.

  Implantable Cardioverter Defibrillators (ICDs)—ICDs use RF communication for monitoring
 and programming, which can introduce RF interference. ICDs can be negatively affected by RF
 radiation [41].

  Hearing Aids with Wireless Connectivity—Modern hearing aids often use Bluetooth or other
 wireless technologies for connectivity, operating in the 2.4 GHz band, which can contribute to
 RF noise.

  Continuous Glucose Monitors (CGMs)—CGMs transmit data wirelessly to receivers or
 smartphones, typically using Bluetooth, which can introduce RF signals into the environment.

  Insulin Pumps—Insulin pumps, which often use Bluetooth, may use RF communication to
 connect with monitoring devices or smartphones, contributing to RF emissions.

  Wearable Health Monitors—Devices like fitness trackers and smartwatches often use Bluetooth
 and Wi-Fi for data transmission, operating in frequency bands that can interfere with quantum
 operations.

 11

  Wireless Electrocardiogram (ECG) Monitors—Wireless ECG monitors transmit data using RF
 signals, which can contribute to RF noise.

  Bone Conduction Hearing Devices—These devices use Bluetooth for connectivity.

The awareness of medical devices entering sensitive areas that contain quantum systems can help ensure
safety and minimize the risk of RF interference.

6.5 PERSONAL SECURITY AWARENESS TRAINING

Training staff on RF safety and the potential impacts of RF to quantum experiments is critical to the
safety and integrity of research environments. This training function protects both the technology and
integrity of qubit functions [4][35].

Training also ensures everyone recognizes health risks tied to RF exposure, such as prolonged contact
with high-energy signals, and follows protocols to stay within safe limits. Addressing these risks upfront
reduces potential workplace injuries [37].

Building a workplace focused on safety starts with clear, ongoing education. Staff who know how to spot
RF hazards can take ownership of preventing problems before escalation [42]. For example, lab
technicians will be conditioned to ensure that all electronics meet shielding standards before beginning
work. This mindset reduces downtime caused by interference incidents [43].

6.6 DEVELOPMENT OF SPECIFIC RF SPECTRUM SECURITY GUIDELINES FOR
 QUANTUM COMPUTING AND RESEARCH FACILITIES

Quantum computing facilities should establish RF spectrum guidelines to address the following key areas:
auditing RF environments, identifying interference sources, creating response plans for interference
incidents, assessing how RF disruptions affect qubit types, designing effective shielding, ensuring
electromagnetic compatibility in controlled spaces, and setting safety standards for RF exposure in
workspaces.

Maintaining stable quantum systems requires actively controlling RF interference. The extreme sensitivity
of qubits to tiny RF fluctuations demands layered protections—from shielding that blocks external
electromagnetic noise to tightly regulating internal RF-generating equipment. Operational requirements
should be implemented to prevent accidental signal contamination during experiments or maintenance
[4].

Specific operational needs, such as cryogenic temperatures and high-strength magnetic fields, add
complexity. These environments require specialized RF-resistant hardware and custom solutions to
prevent the interferences discussed herein [4][6]. Adopting the strategies outlined in this report as
baseline practices helps facilities minimize RF-related risks, preserve qubit stability, and support the
growth of quantum research.

 7. AUDITS

Audits and inspections of a facility’s RF environment should be conducted regularly to help identify
possible sources of interference, such as new equipment installations or changes in the surrounding
environment. Initial audit considerations should include the following reviews and products:

 12

 1. Review of Inventory of RF Emitting Devices—Maintain and review a list of approved devices
 and locations; update any changes.

 2. Review of Anomalous RF Signals Found in Spectrum Monitoring—Implement ongoing
 monitoring of RF spectrum while examining RF anomalies, documenting, locating, and resolving.

 3. Review of Physical Security Apparatus—Verify protection levels of the physical security
 apparatus around facilities, support infrastructure, and personnel access.

 4. Review of RF Shielding Performance—Verify the effectiveness of RF shielding (e.g., Faraday
 cages), physical barriers, and monitoring systems to prevent EMI and unauthorized access.

 5. Quantum Systems Risk Assessment (QSRA)—Conduct regular QSRAs to identify new system
 vulnerabilities and prioritize mitigation strategies.

 6. Governance and Policy Frameworks—Ensure governance structures and policies are in place for
 quantum risk management and compliance with emerging standards.

 7. Incident Response and Recovery Plans—Review and test incident response plans for RF-specific
 issues, ensuring rapid containment and recovery from breaches or RF disruptions.

 8. Training and Awareness Programs—Assess the effectiveness of training for staff on quantum
 risks, RF safety, and secure operational practices [36][37].

 9. Regulatory and Standards Compliance—Audit compliance with relevant standards (e.g., FCC)
 and monitor the evolving regulatory landscape for RF safety and security requirements
       [35][37][40].

These audit items require more exploration and testing to collectively address the specific technical and
operational challenges of securing the RF environment of quantum computing facilities in the near and
long term.

 8. CONCLUSION

Protecting the electromagnetic environment in quantum research and technology centers is critical
because qubits and relevant supporting systems are highly vulnerable to disruptions. A comprehensive
strategy combining physical, RF, and environmental safeguards helps strengthen these systems against
threats.

Minor RF disturbances can lead to corrupted data, operational failures, or permanent qubit damage.
Establishing strong protective measures for RF operations is a nonnegotiable for these facilities.

Solutions involve standard security practices such as controlled access and monitoring systems, as well as
targeted methods to reduce signal interference and maintain stable RF conditions. Shielding sensitive
equipment and continuously monitoring ambient RF levels can prevent external disruptions.

As quantum capabilities grow, ongoing innovation in security measures will need to keep up. New
quantum applications will inevitably introduce risks which will require experts to develop adaptive
strategies and foster continuous innovation. By focusing on maintaining stable RF conditions, quantum
facilities can protect infrastructure investments and continue progressing in quantum research.

Success is dependent on teamwork between quantum researchers, engineers, and security specialists. This
collaborative effort ensures quantum systems remain secure and provide for breakthroughs that push
