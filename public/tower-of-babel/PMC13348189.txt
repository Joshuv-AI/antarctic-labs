Metacycloprodigiosin produced by a high-yield Streptomyces spectabilis restores colistin susceptibility in resistant Gram-negative pathogens
Authors: Yaoyao Shen, Ansley M. Nemeth, Roberta Melander, Christian Melander, Wei-chen Chang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The prodiginine family constitutes a distinctive class of tripyrrolic natural products notable for their red pigmentation and diverse bioactivities. While the biological activities of the linear prodiginine prodigiosin have been extensively studied, cyclic prodiginines remain comparatively underexplored despite structural features that may confer distinct membrane-perturbing properties and enhanced antibacterial activity. Here, a high-producing colony variant of Streptomyces spectabilis (ISP-5512) is described that yields the cyclic prodiginine metacycloprodigiosin at up to 300 mg L−1 isolated yield, representing the highest reported production to date. This high-yield production enabled a comprehensive biological evaluation of metacycloprodigiosin against a diverse panel of colistin-resistant Gram-negative (CRGN) pathogens as a colistin adjuvant, and demonstrates that 5 µM metacycloprodigiosin reduces the colistin minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) up to 32 768-fold across A. baumannii, K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, and E. coli isolates harboring both chromosomal and plasmid-mediated (mcr-1) resistance. Initial investigation into the adjuvant action of metacycloprodigiosin reveals that an increase in reactive oxygen species (ROS) could underpin evident colistin potentiation activity in CRGN A. baumannii. These findings establish metacycloprodigiosin as a potent colistin adjuvant, highlight the potential of cyclic prodiginines for combating antimicrobial resistance, and provide insight into structure–activity relationships governing membrane-active antibiotic adjuvants.

Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) poses a global threat to human health and was associated with an estimated 5 million deaths in 2019.1,2 A significant number of AMR infections, particularly those acquired in hospital settings, are associated with a group of six key bacterial pathogens, four of which are Gram-negative bacteria including Acinetobacter baumannii, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Enterobacter species.3,4 Colistin (polymyxin E), a member of the polymyxin family of natural polypeptides originally derived from Paenibacillus polymyxa5 is currently used as an antibiotic of last resort for the treatment of extensively drug-resistant (XDR) Gram-negative bacteria.6 Colistin targets Gram-negative bacteria by interacting with the negatively charged phosphate groups on the lipid A anchor of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) in the bacterial outer membrane (OM). This interaction leads to competitive displacement of divalent cations Mg2+ and Ca2+, in turn disrupting the overall integrity of the OM and allowing the uptake of colistin.7,8 Colistin then disrupts the inner membrane to ultimately cause death. Unfortunately, the increased use of colistin has resulted in the global emergence of colistin resistance, driven either by chromosomal point mutations or plasmid-mediated colistin-resistance genes,9–11 thereby compromising this critical last line of defense against MDR Gram-negative infection.12 In light of this, the growing crisis of colistin resistance needs urgent attention.

Prodiginines are a class of tripyrrole alkaloids characterized by a conserved three pyrrole ring system (A, B, and C rings) and are divided into two major structural classes: linear or cyclic prodiginines (Fig. 1A). The primary differences among linear prodiginines arise from variations in the alkyl chain length on the C-ring, whereas cyclic prodiginines contain an additional ring formed on the outside of the C-ring (cycloprodigiosin and streptorubin B) or due to an alkyl linker between A- and C-rings (cyclononylprodigiosin).13 Prodigiosin, a prototypical prodiginine, was first isolated from the ubiquitous opportunistic pathogen Serratia marcescens in 1929,14 and its chemical structure was subsequently elucidated in 1962.15 Notably, its striking red pigmentation is believed to underlie historical reports of “bleeding” bread and unexplained red stains on food, phenomena now attributed to the growth of S. marcescens and related species, whose colonies strikingly mimic drops of fresh blood.16,17 Prodigiosin has attracted widespread attention because of its promising biological activities, including potent anti-cancer properties through induction of apoptosis in cancer cells,18–20 immunosuppressive activities via distinct mechanisms from those of cyclosporine A or FK506, and notable anti-infective properties.21–26

To date, investigations into the antibacterial properties of the prodiginine family have largely focused on linear prodiginines. Prodigiosin has been reported to exhibit antibacterial activity against a variety of Gram-positive pathogens, including Staphylococcus aureus, Streptococcus pyogenes, Enterococcus avium, Bacillus subtilis, as well as select Gram-negative organisms, such as E. coli, and Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi.27–29 Additionally, undecylprodigiosin is reported to demonstrate significant inhibition of growth of oxacillin-resistant S. aureus isolates with MICs of 1–8 µg mL−1.30 Studies have demonstrated that co-administration of prodigiosin with established antibiotics, such as ampicillin, results in a significant reduction in the minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of ampicillin against methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) decreasing from 512 to 0.5 µg mL−1.31 Moreover, we recently disclosed that 1.25 µM prodigiosin effectively restores colistin susceptibility to the highly colistin-resistant K. pneumoniae strain B9, lowering the MIC of colistin from 512 to 2 µg mL−1.32 Multiple mechanisms have been suggested to underlie the antibacterial properties of prodiginines, including pH modulation, reactive oxygen species (ROS) induction, and DNA/RNA cleavage; however, their effects appear most often associated with their role as a hydrophobic stressor of bacterial membranes.27,33–36

Despite their antimicrobial activity, the impact of structural differences between linear and cyclic prodiginines remains understudied. While prodigiosin and undecylprodigiosin represent well-studied linear members, cyclic prodiginines such as cycloprodigiosin, streptorubin B, and metacycloprodigiosin (1) feature additional ring systems that rigidify the tripyrrole scaffold and alter C-ring geometry. Prior work suggests cyclic prodiginines exhibit better antibacterial activity than linear prodiginines against select bacterial strains. Notably, cycloprodigiosin is reported to display better activity against E. coli, Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium, B. subtilis, and S. aureus than prodigiosin.37 Streptorubin B, another cyclic prodiginine, significantly impairs the growth of MRSA N315 at 4 μg mL−1 and strongly inhibits biofilm formation in the same strain, with an IC50 of 0.22 µg mL−1.38 However, systematic evaluation of cyclic prodiginines has lagged behind that of the linear class. In particular, the potential of cyclic prodiginines to modulate the activity of last-resort antibiotics such as colistin against Gram-negative pathogens remains unexplored. Given the membrane-disrupting activity of prodiginines and the established role of prodigiosin as a colistin adjuvant,32,34 it was hypothesized that cyclic prodiginines such as metacycloprodiogin might similarly potentiate colistin activity against colistin-resistant Gram-negative bacteria via destabilization of the bacterial OM.

Metacycloprodigiosin, first isolated from Streptomyces longisporus ruber, is a cyclic prodiginine featuring a unique 12-membered carbocyclic ring.39,40 Biosynthetic investigations reveal that metacycloprodigiosin is generated from linear undecylprodigiosin via oxidative carbocyclization catalyzed by McpG, a Rieske non-heme iron-dependent oxygenase-like enzyme.41 Despite extensive bioactivity studies of the linear prodiginine prodigiosin, the biological properties of metacycloprodigiosin remain comparatively underexplored. Building on this limited characterization and the established colistin-potentiating activity of prodigiosin, we sought to discover and isolate similar natural products effective in augmenting the activity of colistin against colistin-resistant Gram-negative bacteria. In this study, we describe screening Streptomyces spectabilis ISP-5512 and identifying a high-yield colony that, to the best of our knowledge, affords the highest isolated yield of metacycloprodigiosin reported to date (up to 300 mg L−1) (Table S1). This high production enabled a detailed antibacterial evaluation of metacycloprodigiosin in combination with colistin against a panel of colistin-resistant Gram-negative isolates. These studies establish that metacycloprodigiosin significantly enhances colistin activity across several Gram-negative species and resistance backgrounds, indicating that cyclic prodiginines can potentially be leveraged as antibiotic adjuvants against the urgent threat posed by XDR Gram-negative bacteria.

Experimental

Microorganisms and culture conditions

Streptomyces (S. albus B-12378, S. albus subspecies albus B-1811, S. albus subspecies chlorinus B-24108, S. spectabilis ISP-5512) used in this work were provided by the USDA-ARS Culture Collection (NRRL). Seeds of Streptomyces were activated on SFM agar (2% soybean flour, 2% mannitol, 2% agar) plates and incubated at 28 °C for 5 days. After seed activation, Streptomyces spectabilis ISP-5512 formed two distinct morphological colonies, designated S. spectabilis ISP-5512-red and S. spectabilis ISP-5512-white according to the colony color. Acinetobacter baumannii strains 3941, 3942, 4106, 4112 and 4119 were obtained from the Walter Reed Army Institute for Research (WRAIR). A. baumannii strains 17978mcr-1 and SM1536mcr-1, Klebsiella pneumoniae strains B9, C3, C5 and F2210291mcr-1, Pseudomonas aeruginosa strains BE-292, BE-293, BE-294, BE-295, BE-297, BE-298 and 47085mcr-1, and Escherichia coli strains 25922mcr-1 and YD626mcr-1 were all obtained from Professor Robert Ernst at the University of Maryland, Baltimore. A. baumannii strains 1A3, 1A7, 1E4, 1E6, 1G2, 1H7, 2B6 and 3A4, K. pneumoniae strains E2, E6, F1, F3, F9, H5, I2, I4 and I6, and P. aeruginosa strains TRPA161 and TRPA162 were all obtained from Professor Yohei Doi at the University of Pittsburgh. A. baumannii strain BEI55202 was obtained from the Biological and Emerging Infections (BEI) Resources Repository. Stocks of each Gram-negative bacterial strain were stored in 25% glycerol and maintained at −80 °C. Bacterial colonies were grown on Lennox LB prior to use in biological assays. Strains harboring mcr-1 were plated on Lennox LB supplemented with 30 µg mL−1 gentamicin for plasmid retention. Cation-adjusted Mueller Hinton broth (CAMHB) was used as the liquid medium for all antibacterial assays unless otherwise stated. Unless otherwise stated, all biological assays were completed in duplicate at least two separate times. All isolated fractions tested biologically were dissolved in molecular biology grade DMSO and stored at −20 °C until further use.

Production and extraction of secondary products from Streptomyces

To obtain crude extracts from the five Streptomyces strains for initial testing, spores were inoculated into 100 mL conical flasks containing 15 mL of seed TSBY medium (3% tryptone soya broth, 0.5% yeast extract, 10.5% sucrose, 0.1% antifoam) and glass beads, and cultivated at 30 °C and 220 rpm for 2 days. SFM liquid medium (2% soybean powder, 2% mannitol and 0.1% antifoam) was used as fermentation medium. A 2% (v/v) aliquot of the 2-day seed culture was inoculated into a 500 mL conical flask containing 150 mL TSBY, and cultures were grown under the same condition for 5 days. A total of 450 mL fermentation broth for each strain was harvested and extracted five times with an equal volume of ethyl acetate (EtOAc) after adjusting the pH to 3 with formic acid. The organic phases were combined and concentrated under reduced pressure to afford the syrup extracts with masses of 702, 238, 239, 657, and 412 mg from strains B-12378, B-1811, B-24108, ISP-5512-red, and ISP-5512-white, respectively.

To purify metacycloprodigiosin from strain S. spectabilis ISP-5512-red, seed culture and fermentation were performed with the same procedures as above. Colistin potentiation assays indicated that the active components were mainly distributed in the mycelia of the fermentation broth. Therefore, the cells from 2 L fermentation broth of S. spectabilis ISP-5512-red were harvested by centrifugation at 6000 rpm for 20 min and extracted five times with an equal volume of MeOH. After the MeOH fractions were combined and evaporated, 30 g crude extract was obtained.

Activity-guided purification of metacycloprodigiosin

To determine the antimicrobial fractions in the fermentation extract of S. spectabilis ISP-5512-red, the EtOAc extract was subjected to a silica gel column (3 × 50 cm, 200–300 mesh). Elution was carried out stepwise, starting with a gradient n-hexane/EtOAc (from 50 : 1 to 1 : 1, v/v), followed by a gradient EtOAc/methanol (MeOH) (from 10 : 1 to 1 : 1, v/v). Fractions were collected based on thin-layer chromatography (TLC) monitoring. A total of ten fractions, labeled Fac. A–J, were collected. These fractions were subjected to colistin potentiation assays. Evaluation of the colistin minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) against two colistin-resistant bacterial strains, K. pneumoniae B9 and A. baumannii 4106, revealed that fraction C exhibited the most potent adjuvant activity (Table 2).

To obtain pure material and determine the structure, two rounds of silica column separations were performed. Compound purification was guided by TLC and the characteristic red color (Fig. 2A). First, 30 g crude MeOH extract from 2 L fermentation broth of S. spectabilis ISP-5512-red was passed through a 5 × 40 cm column loaded with silica gel using a stepwise eluent of n-hexane/EtOAc (from 100 : 1 to 1 : 1, v/v), followed by a gradient EtOAc/MeOH (from 10 : 1 to 1 : 1, v/v) to afford four fractions, Fac. A–D. Fac. B (1.2 g), which contained the red compound, was then passed through another 3 × 40 cm silica column, first with a stepwise n-hexane/EtOAc (from 50 : 1 to 0 : 1, v/v), then EtOAc/MeOH (from 10 : 1 to 1 : 1), yielding nine fractions (Fac. BA–BI). Fac. BD–BG (709 mg in total) were identified as metacycloprodigiosin by both HR-ESI-MS and NMR analysis.

Antimicrobial assays

Broth microdilution for minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) determination

Clinical Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) microdilution MIC protocols were used to determine the MIC for colistin and metacycloprodigiosin (1).42 Bacterial strains were cultured in duplicate for 4 to 6 hours at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm in CAMHB and subcultured to approximately 5 × 105 colony forming units (CFU) per mL in fresh CAMHB. Bacterial strains harboring mcr-1 were cultured in CAMHB supplemented with 30 µg mL−1 gentamicin for plasmid retention. Aliquots (1 mL) were placed in culture tubes and dosed with colistin from a stock solution, such that the concentration equaled the highest concentration tested (up to 2048 µg mL−1 for colistin or 200 µM for 1). Samples were then aliquoted (200 µL) into the first row of a 96-well microtiter plate (Greiner Bio-One, 650161), with the remaining wells filled with 100 µL per well of the initial bacterial subculture. Row one wells were mixed at minimum five times, and 100 µL was transferred to row two. Row two was then mixed at minimum five times, and 100 µL was transferred to row three. The serial dilution process was repeated until the second to last row had been mixed, leaving the final row as a blank of fresh CAMHB. Plates were then covered with GLAD Press n’ Seal and incubated under stationary conditions at 37 °C for 16–20 hours. A BioTek Synergy HTX Multimode plate reader was used to determine the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of each well. The lowest dilution with <10% growth as assessed by the plate reader, which corresponds to no visible growth by visible inspection, was recorded as the MIC.

Broth microdilution for measurement of colistin potentiation

Bacterial strains were cultured in duplicate for 4 to 6 hours in CAMHB at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm and then subcultured to approximately 5 × 105 CFU mL−1 in fresh CAMHB. Bacterial strains harboring mcr-1 were cultured in CAMHB supplemented with 30 µg mL−1 gentamicin for plasmid retention. Aliquots (4 mL) were plated into culture tubes and corresponding isolated fractions were dosed from a stock sample in DMSO. Aliquots (1 mL) of the solution dosed with compound were placed into separate culture tubes and then further dosed with colistin so the resulting concentration was the highest concentration to be tested (64 µg mL−1). Bacteria treated with antibiotic alone were used as a control. Samples containing isolated fraction/colistin combination were aliquoted (200 µL) into the first row of a 96-well microtiter plate, with the remaining wells filled with 100 µL per well of the remaining 4 mL bacterial subculture containing the respective isolated fraction. Each treatment condition was serially diluted by mixing row one wells a minimum of five times and transferring 100 µL to the following row. The serial dilution process was repeated until the second to last row had been mixed, leaving the final row as a blank of fresh CAMHB. Plates were then covered with GLAD Press n’ Seal and incubated under stationary conditions at 37 °C for 16–20 hours. A BioTek Synergy HTX Multimode plate reader was used to determine the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of each well. The lowest dilution associated with <10% growth as assessed by the plate reader, which corresponds to no visible growth by visible inspection, was recorded as the MIC.

1-N-Phenylnaphthylamine (NPN) uptake assay for evaluation of outer membrane integrity

The NPN uptake assay was completed as previously described43,44 to measure changes in the permeability of the outer membrane of AB4106 and KPB9 upon treatment with colistin, metacycloprodigiosin (1), and a combination of the two. Stocks of NPN (20 mM) prepared in acetone were stored and protected from light at −20 °C. Bacterial strains were cultured in duplicate in fresh CAMHB and incubated at 37 °C until an OD600 of 0.5 ± 0.02 was reached. Cultures were centrifuged for 10 minutes at 4000g and room temperature. After centrifugation, cells were resuspended in half the volume of HEPES buffer (5 mM, pH 7.2). This bacterial suspension is used directly in the assay. The wells of a 96-well plate (four per sample) were supplemented with 100 µL bacterial suspension in HEPES buffer (5 mM, pH 7.2), 50 µL of 40 µM NPN in buffer (to give a final concentration of 10 µM NPN), and 50 µL of the test substance in buffer. The later 50 µL portion contained the test substance at a concentration of four-fold higher than the desired final concentration. Control wells were prepared as follows: (a) 200 µL buffer, (b) 150 µL buffer and 50 µL NPN, (c) 100 µL bacterial suspension and 100 µL buffer, (d) 100 µL bacterial suspension, 50 µL NPN, and 50 µL buffer. All non-bacterial components were added to the 96-well plate in advance and the bacterial suspension in buffer was added immediately before the measurement of fluorescence; values were recorded within 3 minutes. Fluorescence was measured on a BioTek HTX Synergy Multimode plate reader with excitation at 340/30 nm and emission at 460/40 nm. Separate treatments were measured at minimum two times across four separate biological samples. For each test substance, it was ascertained that alone or with 10 µM NPN there was no fluorescence increase compared to only NPN in buffer. Results are expressed as relative NPN uptake factors. Statistical analysis was completed using one-way ANOVA in GraphPad Prism 11.0.2.

2′,7′-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCF-DA) assay to probe the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS)

ROS production was evaluated as previously described with minor changes.32 To determine change in the general intracellular ROS upon treatment, 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate fluorescent dye (DCFH-DA) was used. Stocks of DCFH-DA (20 mM) prepared in DMSO were stored and protected from light at −20 °C. Bacterial cultures were incubated overnight in CAMHB at 37 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. Cultures were diluted 1 : 2 in fresh CAMHB for each condition. Subcultures were either left untreated or dosed with one of the following conditions: 0.075% tert-butyl hydroperoxide (positive control), metacycloprodigiosin (1), colistin, or 1 and colistin. Cultures were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C and 240 rpm. The treated culture (2 mL) was centrifuged for 6 minutes at 15 000 rcf and washed with 1× PBS. Pellets were resuspended in 1 mL of 100 μM DCFH-DA (20 mM stock in DMSO diluted in 1× PBS) and incubated in the dark for 1.5 h at 37 °C with shaking at 240 rpm. After 1.5 hours, cells were pelleted for six minutes at 15 000 rcf and then washed with 1× PBS and finally resuspended in fresh CAMHB. Cells were able to recover for 1 h at 37 °C. The cell suspension (100 µL) was aliquoted into black-walled, clear-bottomed 96-well plates. Fluorescence was measured on a BioTek HTX Synergy Multimode plate reader with excitation at 485/20 nm and emission at 528/20 nm. Separate treatments were measured at minimum two times across four separate biological samples. CAMHB containing no bacterial cells was measured for background fluorescence, and 100 μL DCFH-DA in 1× PBS was measured to track spontaneous fluorescence. Results are expressed as OD600-normalized fluorescence intensity. Statistical analysis was completed using one-way ANOVA in GraphPad Prism 11.0.2.

Results

Preliminary bioactive evaluation of crude fermentation extracts from five Streptomyces strains

To screen natural products against colistin-resistant Gram-negative pathogens (CRGN), four Streptomyces strains, including S. albus B-12378, S. albus subspecies albus B-1811, S. albus subspecies chlorinus B-24108, S. spectabilis ISP-5512 were obtained from the NRRL Culture Collection and cultivated on SFM solid medium for spore production. After five days of incubation, two distinct colony morphologies of S. spectabilis ISP-5512 were observed. One morphology includes white mycelia, while the other exhibits red-pigmented mycelia. The distinct colony morphologies were maintained over several generations of propagation, suggesting the occurrence of stable genetic alterations, as previously reported in S. avermitilis and S. rimosus.45,46 Accordingly, the two variants were designated as S. spectabilis ISP-5512-white and S. spectabilis ISP-5512-red, respectively (Fig. 1B). In total, five Streptomyces strains were subsequently examined for the ability of their secondary metabolites to potentiate colistin activity. Crude ethyl acetate (EtOAc) extracts of the five Streptomyces cultures were tested in combination with colistin against two representative CRGN strains, A. baumannii 4106 (AB4106) and K. pneumoniae (KPB9), at 50 µg mL−1 and 20 µg mL−1 to assess potential enhancement of colistin efficacy (Table 1). Both AB4106 and KPB9 were highly resistant to colistin, with baseline colistin MICs of 1024 µg mL−1 and 512 µg mL−1, respectively. Additionally, relevant activity was defined as reduction to or below the clinical breakpoint for colistin susceptibility of 2 µg mL−1.42 The extract from S. spectabilis ISP-5512-red exhibited pronounced colistin potentiation against both CRGN strains when dosed at 20 µg mL−1, lowering the MIC of colistin 64-fold (16 µg mL−1) and 1024-fold (0.5 µg mL−1), respectively.

Purification and characterization of metacycloprodigiosin

To further identify and characterize the bioactive metabolite produced by S. spectabilis ISP-5512-red, 30 g crude methanol (MeOH) extract from 2 L of fermentation broth underwent purification. Guided by TLC profiling, a major red compound was isolated utilizing two successive silica gel columns, yielding 709 mg of the red pigmented compound (approximately 350 mg L−1 from 2 L fermentation) predominantly distributed in fractions BD–BG (Fig. 2A). The purified compound, designated as 1, was obtained as a red solid with a molecular formula of C25H33N3O and m/z 392.2713 [M + H]+ (calc.: 392.2696, ESI-positive), established by high-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (HR-ESI-MS), which is overall consistent with 11 degrees of unsaturation (Fig. 2C). Comparison of 1H- and 13C-NMR spectra in CDCl3 (0.1% trifluoroacetic acid, (TFA)) with those reported for metacycloprodigiosin revealed highly similar resonance signals (Table 3).47 To confirm the chemical structure and distinguish 1 from other cyclic prodiginines, such as streptorubin B, 2D-NMR spectra were collected. Sequential 1H–1H COSY correlations from H14 to H24 delineate an undecylalkyl spin system; while long-range 1H–13C HMBC correlations from H21/H23 to C11, and from H22 to C10/C12 confirm the connectivity between C11 and C22. These data support the presence of a 12-membered macrocyclic ring characteristic of metacycloprodigiosin (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, the optical rotation of 1 exhibits a large negative value ([α]23D −2613° (c 0.0025, CHCl3)) close to the reported (R)-metacycloprodigiosin isolated from Streptomyces longisporus ruber ([α]20D −2370°) and Saccharopolyspora sp. nov. ([α]25D −2170° (c 0.001, CHCl3)).40,48–50 Collectively, these analyses support the assignment of compound 1 as (R)-metacycloprodigiosin, a cyclic prodiginine pigment produced by actinomycete.

Evaluation of metacycloprodigiosin against colistin-resistant Gram-negative isolates

Metacycloprodigiosin (1) was initially evaluated alone and in combination with colistin against the two previously tested CRGN strains to confirm activity. Against both strains, 1 has a stand-alone MIC of >200 µM (Table S2). At 5 µM, the highest concentration of 1 tested, the MIC of colistin is lowered to 1 µg mL−1 in AB4106 and 0.25 µg mL−1 in KPB9, indicating significant enhancement of colistin activity in the presence of 1 (Table 4). When 1 is dosed at 2.5 µM, the colistin MIC is lowered to 4 µg mL−1 and 0.5 µg mL−1 against AB4106 and KPB9, respectively. At 1 µM, colistin adjuvant activity is further reduced in both strains, suggesting a concentration-dependent effect of 1 on colistin activity (Table 4).

Colistin potentiation activity of 1 against colistin-resistant A. baumannii and K. pneumoniae

To evaluate the scope of 1 as a colistin adjuvant, subsequent screening of 1 at a standardized concentration of 5 µM in combination with colistin was completed against a diverse panel of CRGN isolates, including additional A. baumannii and K. pneumoniae strains, as well as P. aeruginosa, and Escherichia coli strains. Specifically, we were looking for the ability of 1 to suppress the colistin MIC of each strain to the clinical susceptibility breakpoint of resistance (≤2 mg mL−1). The panel encompasses both chromosomally-encoded resistant strains, as well as those harboring the plasmid-borne mobile colistin resistance gene (mcr-1) (Tables 5 and S2–5). While certain mcr-1-positive isolates are not classified as phenotypically resistant (colistin MIC = 2 μg mL−1), their original characterization confirmed the presence of phosphoethanolamine (PEtN) modified lipid A, known to impact colistin activity.51 Given the rapid dissemination of mcr-1, inclusion of these isolates is important to assess adjuvant activity against strains with latent or emerging resistance.

Among all additional isolates screened, 1 displays an MIC of ≥200 µM, indicating it is not intrinsically microbicidal (Table S2). Against the A. baumannii isolates screened, compound 1 restores colistin activity when dosed at 5 µM in most strains, lowering the colistin MIC to the target of ≤2 µg mL−1, apart from 1A3, 1H7, 3A4, and BEI55202 (Tables 5 and S3). Similarly, colistin activity is significantly enhanced in the presence of 5 µM 1 against all but two K. pneumoniae isolates, C5 and E2 (Tables 5 and S4). Evaluation of compound 1 in combination with colistin against several colistin-resistant P. aeruginosa isolates demonstrates restoration of colistin in all strains, except for PA BE-292, and BE-293 (Tables 5 and S5). Notably, when dosed at 5 µM, 1 reduces the MIC of colistin ≥32 768-fold from >2048 µg mL−1 to 0.125 µg mL−1 against strain TRPA162. Additionally, against the P. aeruginosa strain harboring the mcr-1 gene, which displays a baseline colistin MIC of 2 µg mL−1, addition of 1 reduces the MIC 8-fold to 0.25 µg mL−1. Likewise, colistin activity was enhanced in the presence of 5 µM compound 1 against mcr-1 positive E. coli isolates, with both strains displaying an 8-fold reduction in the MIC of colistin (Table 5). For instance, in strain EC YD626mcr-1, the colistin MIC decreases from 2 µg mL−1 to 0.25 μg mL−1 upon treatment with 1. Overall, these results demonstrate that 1 serves as a potent colistin potentiator across multiple CRGN species. However, the degree of activity varies by bacterial genus and strain background.

Assessment of the effects of metacycloprodigiosin on outer membrane integrity and reactive oxygen species production

To probe how 1 acts on the Gram-negative cell, we first sought to understand the impact of 1 against the OM. To address this, we utilized 1-N-phenylnapthylamine (NPN), a fluorescent probe commonly used to gauge membrane permeability (Fig. 3A and B). We compared colistin at its stand-alone MIC against both AB4106 (1024 µg mL−1) and KPB9 (512 µg mL−1) to cells treated with 1 in the presence or absence of colistin (Fig. 3A and B). In both strains, cells treated with 1, either in the presence or absence of colistin, exhibit little to no change in fluorescence relative to untreated bacterial controls (Fig. 3A and B). To directly compare to linear prodigiosin, we investigated the activity of prodigiosin and colistin as previously disclosed32 using the NPN uptake assay (Fig. S6) and KP9, which confirmed the impact of prodigiosin and colistin on OM permeability. Although prodiginines are largely understood to impact the membrane, our results indicate that at low concentrations (5 µM and 2.5 µM) 1 does not impact membrane integrity and exerts its colistin potentiation activity via a mechanism independent of membrane disruption.

By using another fluorescent probe, 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA), we quantified reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in AB4106 or KPB9 cells treated with 1 alone or in the presence of colistin (Fig. 3C and D). To confirm assay responsiveness, we included the effect of the oxidizer tert-butyl hydroperoxide. In both AB4106 and KPB9, 0.0075% tert-butyl hydroperoxide induces a significant increase in ROS (ca. 25-fold increase vs. untreated control). The active combination of 5 µM 1 and 1 µg mL−1 colistin elicits a 593% increase in ROS production in AB4106 compared to the untreated bacterial control (Fig. 3C). In contrast, the active combination of 1 and colistin against KPB9 only induces a 42% increase in ROS (Fig. 3D). Previously, we disclosed that the combination of colistin and linear prodigiosin induces a 1656% increase in ROS compared to the untreated control.32 Overall, the mechanism of adjuvant activity for 1 in KPB9 is likely not underpinned by ROS; however, in AB4106 the substantial increase in ROS production could imply that elevated ROS could be driving antibacterial activity when colistin and 1 are dosed together.

Discussion

Antimicrobial resistance continues to represent an urgent global threat, with CRGN bacteria of particular concern given limited therapeutic alternatives and the spread of plasmid-borne mcr genes into the human pathogen pool.52 Considering these challenges, our efforts focused on uncovering compound(s) capable of augmenting colistin activity. We investigated products isolated from Streptomyces as a source for such compounds, given its well established capacity to produce a range of bioactive secondary metabolites.53

Crude extracts from five Streptomyces cultures were screened in combination with colistin against two highly resistant strains to identify compounds capable of potentiating colistin activity. One extract, derived from a distinct colony variant of S. spectabilis (ISP-5512-red), demonstrated potent colistin adjuvant activity against KPB9. Utilizing bioassay-guided fractionation, we identified and characterized the active compound as metacycloprodigiosin (1), a cyclic prodiginine, afforded at levels up to 300 mg L−1 by the high-producing ISP-5512-red strain, allowing for comprehensive evaluation as a colistin potentiator. Prodiginines are a family of compounds that exhibit a variety of biological activities, making them valuable in drug discovery.26,27,33 Many studies on prodiginines have focused on the anti-infective properties of linear prodiginine, prodigiosin;28,54 however, our results suggest cyclic metacycloprodigiosin can be isolated in quantities sufficient to support robust biological investigation.

The antibacterial activity of metacycloprodigiosin has been previously investigated against clinically common pathogens,55 and its considerable activity has been established against both Gram-positive and select Gram-negative species. However, a comprehensive investigation of its biological activity has been limited by its availability. By discovering a new high-producing system, we have established the potential of metacycloprodigiosin to potentiate colistin activity against MDR Gram-negative bacteria. Metacycloprodigiosin restores colistin susceptibility against a broad panel of CRGN isolates, including A. baumannii, K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, and E. coli. These studies have established that when metacycloprodigiosin was dosed 5 µM, the MIC of colistin is reduced up to 8192-fold in A. baumannii, 256-fold in K. pneumoniae, 32 768-fold in P. aeruginosa, and 8-fold in E. coli. The most notable effects are observed in highly colistin-resistant strains with high standalone colistin MICs, in which metacycloprodigiosin restores colistin activity to levels at or below the clinical breakpoint (2 µg mL−1).42

Compared to linear prodigiosin, metacycloprodigiosin possesses a 12-membered carbocyclic ring that rigidifies the C-ring side chain and distinguishes it from other prodiginines. Prior studies on cycloprodigiosin and streptorubin B suggest that cyclic prodiginines exhibit greater antibacterial potency than their linear counterparts.33 In comparison to our previous report showing that 5 µM prodigiosin reduces the colistin MIC to 4 µg mL−1 and 1 µg mL−1 against colistin-resistant strains AB4106 and KPB9;32 respectively, our findings with metacycloprodigiosin, in which the colistin MIC is lowered to 1 µg mL−1 and 0.25 µg mL−1 against the same two strains, further support the idea that macrocyclization within the prodiginine family can enhance the overall bioactivity.

Despite established membrane-associated activities of prodiginines,26,32,34 NPN uptake experiments do not reveal significant changes in OM permeabilization by 1 at the active concentration alone, or in combination with colistin. To that end, the lack in increased NPN uptake observed with 1 indicates it is unlikely to impact OM membrane integrity at concentrations that potentiate colistin activity. In contrast to previously described activity with prodigiosin in KPB9, in which 1.25 µM prodigiosin and 2 µg mL−1 colistin induces a 1656% increase in ROS,32 the active combination of 1 and colistin does not induce a comparable increase in ROS. In AB4106, a significant increase in ROS is observed in the presence of 5 µM 1 and 1 µg mL−1 colistin, indicating the possible contribution of increased ROS to increased colistin activity in the presence of 1. Collectively, these results indicate colistin adjuvant activity of 1 is unlikely due to direct membrane disruption, and that oxidative stress could contribute to augmented colistin activity in AB4106 but is unlikely to be a dominant mechanism for colistin potentiation in KPB9. Future studies that directly compare linear and cyclic prodiginines, such as examining their interactions with model membranes or lipopolysaccharides and determining the full extent by which ROS mediates cell death, will be crucial for clarifying how macrocyclic constraint influences membrane engagement, oxidative stress, and antibiotic potentiation.

From a biosynthetic perspective, our notable high yield of metacycloprodigiosin in S. spectabilis ISP-5512 suggests that this strain may be a useful platform for preparative production, or generating analogs through mutasynthesis or pathway engineering. This high expression system should allow rapid isolation of metacycloprodigiosin in sufficient quantities for in depth evaluation of its biological activity across multiple disease indications. Our observation of distinct red and white colony morphologies hints at underlying regulatory differences in prodiginine pathway expression, which could potentially be exploited to further increase titers or to control production in heterologous hosts. The combination of robust biosynthesis and pronounced antibacterial synergy of metacycloprodigiosin highlights its potential as a valuable platform for developing cyclic prodiginines as antibacterial adjuvants.

Conclusion

This study identified a high-yield Streptomyces spectabilis producer of the cyclic prodiginine metacycloprodigiosin and demonstrated that metacycloprodigiosin can restore colistin susceptibility across a broad range of colistin-resistant Gram-negative strains.

Among isolates evaluated, the strongest colistin potentiation activity was observed in P. aeruginosa TRPA162, in which 5 µM metacycloprodigiosin lowers the colistin MIC by at least 32 768-fold, from >2048 µg mL−1 to 0.125 µg mL−1. Additionally, 5 µM 1 and 1 µg mL−1 colistin treated AB4106 elicits a 593% increase in ROS production, implying oxidative stress could contribute to cell death when colistin and 1 are co-administered. These findings expand the known biological roles of cyclic prodiginines and suggest that this scaffold may be developed further as an adjuvant to extend the useful lifetime of colistin and potentially other antibiotics.
