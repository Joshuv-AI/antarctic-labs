Quantum cellular automata are a coarse homology theory
Matthias Ludewig

Abstract
We show that quantum cellular automata naturally form the degree-zero part of a coarse homology theory. The recent result of Ji and Yang that the space of QCA forms an Ω \Omega -spectrum in the sense of algebraic topology is a direct consequence of the formal properties of coarse homology theories.



1
Introduction

Quantum cellular automata (QCA) are certain locality preserving automorphisms of
C^{*}
-algebras that arise as tensor products over matrix algebras attached to points in space.
In a very recent paper
[
4
]
, Ji–Yang prove strong structural results on the space of QCA, which they call the
QCA conjecture
: The space of QCA on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is homotopy equivalent to the loop space of the space of QCA on
\mathbb{Z}^{n+1}
.
In this paper, we give a somewhat simplified proof, as well as a conceptual reason for this equivalence: QCA form a coarse homology theory.

To define QCA on non-compact spaces, one typically starts with a discrete metric space
X
and attaches matrix algebras
\mathscr{A}_{x}
to each point in space.
Then, an automorphism
\alpha
of the infinite tensor product

\mathscr{A}_{X}:=\bigotimes_{x\in X}\mathscr{A}_{x}

is a QCA if there exists
R>0
such that
\alpha
sends each of the subalgebras
\mathscr{A}_{x}
to the tensor product of
\mathscr{A}_{y}
over those
y
in an
R
-neighborhood around
x
.
One of the main points I would like to make in this paper is that the “correct” type of space to consider for studying QCA is
not
a metric space but a
bornological coarse space
:
The observation is that only the large scale structure of space matters for QCAs;
however, next to the large scale (coarse) structure, metric spaces also carry an unnecessary small scale structure (a topology, even a uniform structure).

A bornological coarse space is a set
X
together with the information on which sets are to be considered
bounded
, as well as a collection of subsets of
X\times X
which are called
entourages
or
controlled subsets
and which encode uniform bounds from above on distances (see §
2.1
for precise definitions).
The collection
\mathcal{B}
of bounded subsets of a bornological coarse space
X
is partially ordered by inclusion, and a
net
on
X
is just a functor from the poset
\mathcal{B}
to the category of finite-dimensional
C^{*}
-algebras and injective
*
-homomorphisms, which satisfies a certain cocontinuity hypothesis.
Particularly nice examples are the
local matrix nets
or
spin systems
, which are given by a tensor product of matrix algebras indexed by a locally finite subset of sites.
The crucial observation is now that the notion of QCA can be formulated using only the coarse structure: An automorphism
\alpha
of a local matrix net is a QCA if there exists an entourage
E\subset X\times X
such that
\alpha(\mathscr{A}_{x})
commutes with
\mathscr{A}_{y}
unless
(x\mathchar 59\relax y)\in E
; in other words, only pairs of points from
E
interact via
\alpha
.

The reason for working with the larger class of coarse spaces instead of the more rigid framework of metric spaces is not the desire to study QCA on strange coarse spaces.
Instead, the reason is that—in contrast to the category of metric spaces—the category of bornological coarse spaces is well-behaved enough to do homotopy theory with it
[
1
]
.
This leads to the notion of a
coarse homology theory
, which is a functor from bornological coarse spaces to spectra that sends close maps to homotopic maps and satisfies a Mayer–Vietoris axiom for decompositions of spaces.
Important examples include coarse
K
-homology and coarse ordinary homology.
Using the Mayer–Vietoris property of coarse homology theories, the QCA hypothesis
Q(X\otimes\mathbb{Z})\cong\Omega Q(X)
from
[
4
]
is a direct consequence of the following theorem, which is the main result of this paper and identifies the QCA group as a generalized homology invariant of large-scale geometry.

Theorem
.

There is a coarse homology theory
Q
such that the degree zero group
Q_{0}(X)
is the group
\mathrm{QCA}(X)
of quantum cellular automata on
X
.

The category of local matrix nets is too rigid for the homotopical constructions required for the construction of
Q
.
To remedy this, inspired by
[
4
]
, we introduce in this paper the new notion of an
Azumaya net
, which plays a role analogous to Azumaya algebras in algebra.
These are nets
\mathscr{A}
such that there exists another net
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
on the same space such that we have a (controlled) isomorphism

\mathscr{A}\otimes\mathscr{A}^{\prime}\cong\mathscr{B}

of nets, where
\mathscr{B}
is a local matrix net.
This is a straightforward generalization of the notion of an Azumaya algebra over a commutative ring
R
, which is an
R
-algebra
A
such that there exists an
R
-algebra
A^{\prime}
with
A\otimes_{R}A^{\prime}\cong M_{n}(R)
.
Denoting by
\textsc{Az}(X)
the symmetric monoidal category of Azumaya nets over
X
, there are natural maps
K(\textsc{Az}(X))\to\Omega K(\textsc{Az}(X\otimes\mathbb{R}))
, where
K
denotes the algebraic
K
-theory spectrum for symmetric monoidal categories.
The spectrum
Q(X)
is then defined as

Q(X)=\colim_{n\to\infty}\Omega^{n+1}K(\textsc{Az}(X\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n}))\mathchar 59\relax

where the colimit is taken with respect to the maps above.
One further new result that follows directly from this construction is that we have a canonical isomorphism

\mathrm{QCA}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})\cong K_{0}(\textsc{Az}(\mathbb{Z}^{n-1})).

In other words, the group of QCA on
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
is identified with the Grothendieck group of Azumaya nets in one dimension lower.
For
n=1
, this isomorphism is precisely the GNVW index
[
3
]
, but in higher dimensions, this seems to be new.

2
Nets and QCA on coarse spaces

In this section, we give a brief recollection of the basic notions of coarse geometry, before developing the theory of nets on bornological coarse spaces and giving the definition of the group of quantum cellular automata.

2.1
Preliminaries on coarse spaces

Definition 2.1
.

Let
X
be a set.

(1)

A
bornology
on a set
X
is a collection
\mathcal{B}
of subsets of
X
(called
bounded
subsets) which is closed under taking finite unions and subsets and with the property that
\bigcup\mathcal{B}=X
.
A
bornological space
is a set together with a bornology.

(2)

A
coarse structure
on
X
is a collection
\mathcal{C}
of subsets of
X\times X
(called
entourages
), which contains the diagonal

\Delta=\{(x\mathchar 59\relax x)\mid x\in X\}

and which is closed under taking subsets, finite unions, compositions

E\circ F=\{(x\mathchar 59\relax z)\in X\times X\mid\exists y\in X:(x\mathchar 59\relax y)\in F\mathchar 59\relax(y\mathchar 59\relax z)\in E\}

and inverses

E^{-1}=\{(y\mathchar 59\relax x)\in X\times X\mid(x\mathchar 59\relax y)\in E\}.

A
coarse space
is a set together with a coarse structure.

For a subset
Y\subseteq X
of a coarse space
X
and an entourage
E
, we denote the
E
-fattening of
Y
by

Y_{E}=\{x\in X\mid\exists y\in Y:(x\mathchar 59\relax y)\in E\}.

Definition 2.2
(bornological coarse spaces
[
1
]
).

A
bornological coarse space
is a set
X
together with a bornology
\mathcal{B}
and a coarse structure
\mathcal{C}
, which are compatible in the sense that for any
B\subseteq X
and any entourage
E\in\mathcal{C}
, the fattening
B_{E}
is bounded again.

We usually just write
X
instead of
(X\mathchar 59\relax\mathcal{B}\mathchar 59\relax\mathcal{C})
for a bornological coarse space, suppressing the bornology and coarse structure in notation.

Remark 2.3
.

If
\mathcal{C}
is a coarse structure on a set
X
, then there is a canonical bornology determined by the coarse structure, consisting of those subsets
B\subseteq X
such that
B\times B
is an entourage of
\mathcal{C}
.
Hence any coarse space is canonically a bornological coarse space.
However, bornological coarse spaces allow for more flexibility with respect to the bornology, which is sometimes useful.

Example 2.4
.

If
\mathcal{E}
is any collection of subsets of
X\times X
with
\Delta\subseteq\bigcup\mathcal{E}
, then one can consider the smallest coarse structure
\mathcal{C}=\langle\mathcal{E}\rangle
containing
\mathcal{E}
, the
coarse structure generated by
\mathcal{E}
.

Example 2.5
.

If
d
is a (pseudo-) metric on
X
, then the
metric coarse structure
is the coarse structure generated by the entourages
\{(x\mathchar 59\relax y)\in X\times X\mid d(x\mathchar 59\relax y)\leq R\}
for
R\geq 0
.
The bornology induced by this coarse structure is the collection of subsets of
X
with finite
d
-diameter.
Whenever we view metric spaces as coarse spaces (such as
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
or
\mathbb{R}^{n}
), we always understand them to be equipped with the metric coarse structure and the induced bornology, unless otherwise specified.

Example 2.6
.

Given two bornological coarse spaces
X
and
X^{\prime}
, there is a bornological coarse space
X\otimes X^{\prime}
which has underlying set
X\times X^{\prime}
, bornology
\mathcal{B}\times\mathcal{B}^{\prime}
and coarse structure
\mathcal{C}\times\mathcal{C}^{\prime}
, the elementwise cartesian product.
As noted in
[
1
, Example 2.32.]
, the bornological coarse space
X\otimes X^{\prime}
is
not
the categorical product of
X
and
X^{\prime}
; the latter does exist and has the same underlying set and coarse structure, but a different bornology.
The product
\otimes
yields an auxiliary symmetric monoidal structure on
BornCoarse
.
In particular, we have canonical identifications

\mathbb{R}^{n-1}\otimes\mathbb{R}=\mathbb{R}^{n}\mathchar 59\relax\qquad\mathbb{Z}^{n-1}\otimes\mathbb{Z}\cong\mathbb{Z}^{n}.

Definition 2.7
(coarse maps).

Let
X
and
X^{\prime}
be bornological coarse spaces and let
f:X\to X^{\prime}
be a map.

(1)

f
is called
proper
if for every
B^{\prime}\subseteq X^{\prime}
bounded,
f^{-1}(B^{\prime})\subseteq X
is bounded.

(2)

f
is
controlled
if for every entourage
E
of
X
,
(f\times f)(E)
is an entourage of
X^{\prime}
.

(3)

f
is called
coarse
if it is proper and controlled.

We denote by
BornCoarse
the category of bornological coarse spaces and coarse maps.

What allows us to do homotopy theory with bornological coarse spaces is the following notion of equivalence for coarse maps.

Definition 2.8
(closeness).

Let
X
and
X^{\prime}
be coarse spaces.

(1)

f\mathchar 59\relax g:X\to X^{\prime}
are
close
if the set
(f\times g)(\Delta)=\{(f(x)\mathchar 59\relax g(x))\mid x\in X\}
is an entourage of
X^{\prime}
.

(2)

A coarse map
f:X\to X^{\prime}
is a
coarse equivalence
if there exists a coarse map
g:X^{\prime}\to X
such that the compositions
f\circ g
and
g\circ f
are each close to the identity.

Example 2.9
.

For any dimension
n
, the inclusion maps
\mathbb{Z}^{n}\hookrightarrow\mathbb{R}^{n}
are coarse equivalences.
However,
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is not coarsely equivalent to
\mathbb{R}^{m}
for
n\neq m
.

To formulate support conditions in coarse geometry that are invariant under coarse fattenings, we use the notion of a big family, defined as follows.

Definition 2.10
.

A
big family on

X
is a collection
\mathcal{Y}
of subsets of
X
that is closed under taking subsets, finite unions and fattenings.

Any subset
Y\subseteq X
generates a big family of
X
that we denote by
\{Y\}
, given by

\{Y\}=\{Z\subseteq X\mid\exists E\in\mathcal{C}:Z\subseteq Y_{E}\}.

If
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Z}
are big families, then their elementwise union and intersection

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}\Cup\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle=\{Y\cup Z\mid Y\in\mathcal{Y}\mathchar 59\relax Z\in\mathcal{Z}\}

\displaystyle\mathcal{Y}\Cap\mathcal{Z}

\displaystyle=\{Y\cap Z\mid Y\in\mathcal{Y}\mathchar 59\relax Z\in\mathcal{Z}\}

are again big families.

In the ordinary homotopy theory of topological spaces, contractible spaces are
motivically trivial
, in the sense that they evaluate trivially on any homology theory.
In the theory of coarse spaces, this role is played by so-called flasque spaces:

Definition 2.11
(flasqueness).

A bornological coarse space
X
is called
flasque
if there exists a coarse map
f:X\to X
with the following properties:

(1)

f
is close to the identity map of
X
;

(2)

For every bounded set
B\subseteq X
, there exists
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that
f^{n}(X)\cap B=\emptyset
, where
f^{n}
is the
n
-th iterate of
f
;

(3)

For every entourage
E
, the set

\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}(f^{n}\times f^{n})(E)

is an entourage.

Such a map
f
is called a
map witnessing the flasqueness
.

2.2
Nets on coarse spaces

Let
X
be a bornological coarse space.
The bornology
\mathcal{B}
of
X
is a partially ordered set and hence may be viewed as a category with exactly one morphism for each inclusion
B\subseteq B^{\prime}
.

Definition 2.12
(nets).

A
net
on
X
is a functor

\mathscr{A}:\mathcal{B}\longrightarrow\left\{\begin{subarray}{c}\text{category of}\\
\text{finite-dimensional}\\
\text{$C^{*}$-algebras}\\
\text{and injective}\\
\text{ $*$-homomorphisms}\end{subarray}\right\}\mathchar 59\relax\quad~~B\longmapsto\mathscr{A}_{B}.

with the following property:
Whenever
B\subseteq X
is bounded and
\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
is a directed subset such that
\bigcup\mathcal{B}^{\prime}=B
, then canonical map

\colim_{B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}\mathscr{A}_{B^{\prime}}\longrightarrow\mathscr{A}_{B}

(2.1)

is an isomorphism.

The property (
2.1
) of the functor
\mathscr{A}
is there to avoid pathological phenomena, see Remark
2.20
below.
Given a net
\mathscr{A}
, we define for any subset
Y\subseteq X

\mathscr{A}_{Y}:=\colim_{\begin{subarray}{c}B\in\mathcal{B}\\
B\subseteq Y\end{subarray}}\mathscr{A}_{B}\mathchar 59\relax

where the colimit is taken in the category of
*
-algebras.
We could also take the limit in the category of
C^{*}
-algebras (which would amount to taking the completion of
\mathscr{A}_{Y}
), but for the current paper it is more convenient to work with uncompleted algebras.
By (
2.1
), we can as well take the colimit over an arbitrary directed system of bounded subsets covering
B
instead.

Since the structure maps of
\mathscr{A}
are required to be injective, all the algebras
\mathscr{A}_{Y}
for
Y\subseteq X
may be viewed as subalgebras of
\mathscr{A}_{X}
, and making these identifications,
\mathscr{A}_{X}
is just the union of all
\mathscr{A}_{B}
.

Definition 2.13
.

If
\mathscr{A}
and
\mathscr{B}
are nets on
X
, their
tensor product

\mathscr{A}\otimes\mathscr{B}
is defined by

(\mathscr{A}\otimes\mathscr{B})_{B}:=\mathscr{A}_{B}\otimes\mathscr{B}_{B}.

Definition 2.14
(homomorphisms).

Let
\mathscr{A}
,
\mathscr{B}
be two nets.
A
homomorphism
of nets
\varphi:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{B}
is a
*
-homomorphism
\varphi:\mathscr{A}_{X}\to\mathscr{B}_{X}
which is controlled in the sense that there exists an entourage
E
of
X
such that for all bounded sets
B\subseteq X
, we have

\varphi(\mathscr{A}_{B})\subseteq\mathscr{B}_{B_{E}}.

In this case, we say more precisely, that
\varphi
is
E
-controlled
.
We call
\varphi

local
if it is
\Delta
-controlled.

Let
\varphi:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{B}
be a homomorphism of nets such that its controlled
*
-homomorphism
\mathscr{A}_{X}\to\mathscr{B}_{X}
is an isomorphism.
If the inverse
*
-homomorphism is again controlled, then we say that
\varphi
is an
isomorphism
of nets.
We denote by
\textsc{Net}(X)
the groupoid of nets on
X
whose morphisms are (controlled) isomorphisms of nets.

Definition 2.15
(types of nets).

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a net on
X
.

(1)

\mathscr{A}
is
semilocal
if whenever
Y\mathchar 59\relax Y^{\prime}\subseteq X
satisfy
Y\cap Y^{\prime}=\emptyset
, then
\mathscr{A}_{Y}
and
\mathscr{A}_{Y^{\prime}}
commute inside
\mathscr{A}_{Y\sqcup Y^{\prime}}
.

(2)

\mathscr{A}
is
local
if it is semilocal and whenever
Y\mathchar 59\relax Y^{\prime}\subseteq X
satisfy
Y\cap Y^{\prime}=\emptyset
, then the inclusion maps induce an isomorphism

\mathscr{A}_{Y}\otimes\mathscr{A}_{Y^{\prime}}\cong\mathscr{A}_{Y\sqcup Y^{\prime}}.

(3)

\mathscr{A}
is called a
matrix net
if
\mathscr{A}_{B}
is a full matrix algebra for each
B\subseteq X
bounded.

(4)

\mathscr{A}
is called
Azumaya
if there exists a net
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
on
X
and a local matrix net
\mathscr{B}
on
X
such that

\mathscr{A}\otimes\mathscr{A}^{\prime}\cong\mathscr{B}.

We write

\textsc{Loc}(X)\subseteq\textsc{Az}(X)\subseteq\textsc{Net}(X)

for the full subgroupoids of local matrix nets, respectively Azumaya nets.

Example 2.16
.

Whenever
q:X\to\mathbb{N}
is a function with locally finite support (meaning that for each bounded set
B\subseteq X
, we have
q(x)=1
for all but finitely many
x\in B
), we obtain a local matrix net by setting

\mathscr{A}_{B}:=\bigotimes_{x\in B}M_{q(x)}(\mathbb{C})\mathchar 59\relax

where
M_{q}(\mathbb{C})
denotes the algebra of complex
q\times q
matrices.
This is the class of nets considered in
[
4
]
.
Any local matrix net is isomorphic in
\textsc{Net}(X)
to one of this form.
The more general class of local matrix nets may be viewed as a “coordinate free version” of these nets.

Remark 2.17
.

While the terminology “local matrix net” seems more appropriate in our context, we remark that these are often called
spin system
in the mathematical physics literature.

Remark 2.18
.

It follows from Corollary
2.29
that any Azumaya net on
X
is isomorphic to a semilocal net
\mathscr{A}
, because subnets of local nets are necessarily semilocal.
Throughout, we may therefore replace
\textsc{Az}(X)
by the full subcategory of semilocal Azumaya nets.

Definition 2.19
(support).

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a net on
X
.
A subset
Y\subseteq X
is a
support
for
\mathscr{A}
if
for every
B\in\mathcal{B}
, we have
\mathscr{A}_{B}=\mathscr{A}_{Y\cap B}
.
If
\mathcal{Y}
is a big family in
X
, we write

\textsc{Loc}(\mathcal{Y})\subseteq\textsc{Loc}(X)\mathchar 59\relax\qquad\textsc{Az}(\mathcal{Y})\subseteq\textsc{Az}(X)\mathchar 59\relax\qquad\textsc{Net}(\mathcal{Y})\subseteq\textsc{Net}(X)

for the full subcategories of those nets that are supported on some member
Y
of
\mathcal{Y}
.

Remark 2.20
.

Any local net has a locally finite support
Y
, meaning that
Y\cap B
is finite for each
B\subseteq X
bounded.
Indeed, by locality, we have

\mathscr{A}_{F}=\bigotimes_{x\in F}\mathscr{A}_{x}

for each finite subset
F\subseteq X
.
Now by (
2.1
), we have

\mathscr{A}_{B}=\colim_{F\subseteq B~\text{finite}}\mathscr{A}_{F}=\bigotimes_{x\in B}\mathscr{A}_{x}.

Since
\mathscr{A}_{B}
is finite-dimensional, we have
\mathscr{A}_{x}\neq\mathbb{C}
for only finitely many
x\in B
.
Without (
2.1
) on the functor
\mathscr{A}
, there are pathological examples of local nets without locally finite support using the axiom of choice.
Namely, if
\mathcal{U}
is a free ultrafilter on
X
(which always exists as soon as
X
is infinite), we may define a function
\mu:\mathcal{B}\to\{0\mathchar 59\relax 1\}
by
\mu(B)=0
if
B\notin\mathcal{U}
and
\mu(B)=1
if
B\in\mathcal{U}
.
This function is additive because filters cannot contain two disjoint sets.
Therefore, setting

\mathscr{A}_{B}:=M_{2}(\mathbb{C})^{\otimes\mu(B)}

defines a local net on
X
.

\mathscr{A}
does not have locally finite support because
\mathcal{U}
does not contain any finite sets.

If
\mathscr{A}
is a net on
Y\subseteq X
, we obtain a net
\tilde{\mathscr{A}}
on
X
given by
\tilde{\mathscr{A}}_{B}:=\mathscr{A}_{B\cap Y}
.
This gives identifications of nets on
Y
with nets on
X
.
Using these identifications, we have canonical isomorphisms

\displaystyle\textsc{Az}(\mathcal{Y})

\displaystyle\cong\colim_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}\textsc{Az}(Y)\mathchar 59\relax

(2.2)

\displaystyle\textsc{Loc}(\mathcal{Y})

\displaystyle\cong\colim_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}\textsc{Loc}(Y)\mathchar 59\relax

\displaystyle\textsc{Net}(\mathcal{Y})

\displaystyle\cong\colim_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}\textsc{Net}(Y).

2.3
Nets and coarse maps

If
f:X\to X^{\prime}
is a coarse map, then we may form the pushforward net
f_{*}\mathscr{A}
, given by
(f_{*}\mathscr{A})_{B^{\prime}}=\mathscr{A}_{f^{-1}(B^{\prime})}
for
B^{\prime}\subseteq X^{\prime}
bounded, which is a net on
X^{\prime}
.
This is well defined because
f
is proper.

We have

(f_{*}\mathscr{A})_{X^{\prime}}=\colim_{B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}(f_{*}\mathscr{A})_{B^{\prime}}=\colim_{B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}}\mathscr{A}_{f^{-1}(B^{\prime})}=\mathscr{A}_{X}\mathchar 59\relax

since
\{f^{-1}(B^{\prime})\mid B^{\prime}\in\mathcal{B}^{\prime}\}\subseteq\mathcal{B}
is cofinal.
Hence if
\varphi:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{B}
is a morphism of nets, then we obtain a
*
-homomorphism

f_{*}\varphi:(f_{*}\mathscr{A})_{X}=\mathscr{A}_{X}\xrightarrow{~~\varphi_{X}~~}\mathscr{B}_{X}=(f_{*}\mathscr{B})_{X}.

If
\varphi
is
E
-controlled, then for each
B^{\prime}\subseteq X^{\prime}
bounded, we have

\varphi((f_{*}\mathscr{A})_{B^{\prime}})=\varphi(\mathscr{A}_{f^{-1}(B^{\prime})})\subseteq\mathscr{B}_{f^{-1}(B^{\prime})_{E}}\subseteq\mathscr{B}_{f^{-1}(B^{\prime}_{(f\times f)(E)})}=(f_{*}\mathscr{B})_{B^{\prime}_{(f\times f)(E)}}\mathchar 59\relax

hence
f_{*}\varphi
is
(f\times f)(E)
-controlled.

The pushforward functor
f_{*}
respects tensor products, in the sense that there are canonical isomorphisms

f_{*}(\mathscr{A}\otimes\mathscr{B})\cong f_{*}\mathscr{A}\otimes f_{*}\mathscr{B}.

Combining the above observations, we obtain that for each coarse map
f:X\to X^{\prime}
, we get a symmetric monoidal functor

\displaystyle f_{*}:\textsc{Net}(X)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\textsc{Net}(X^{\prime}).

One easily checks that the pushforward of a local net is again local. It follows that
f_{*}
also sends Azumaya nets to Azumaya nets, so
f_{*}
restricts to functors

\displaystyle f_{*}:\textsc{Az}(X)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\textsc{Az}(X^{\prime})\mathchar 59\relax

\displaystyle f_{*}:\textsc{Loc}(X)

\displaystyle\longrightarrow\textsc{Loc}(X^{\prime}).

If
f:X\to X^{\prime}
and
g:X^{\prime}\to X^{\prime\prime}
are two coarse maps, then we have obvious natural isomorphisms of functors

(g\circ f)_{*}\cong g_{*}f_{*}.

These structures assemble to (pseudo-)functors

\textsc{Net}\mathchar 59\relax\textsc{Az}\mathchar 59\relax\textsc{Loc}:\textsc{BornCoarse}\longrightarrow\texttt{CMon}(\textsc{Cat})

from bornological coarse spaces to the bicategory of symmetric monoidal categories (i.e., commutative monoid objects in
Cat
).

Proposition 2.21
.

Suppose that
f\mathchar 59\relax g:X\to X^{\prime}
are close.
Then we have natural isomorphisms of functors

f_{*}\cong g_{*}:\textsc{Net}(X)\to\textsc{Net}(X^{\prime}).

Since they are full subcategories, the same is true for the pushforward functors on Azumaya and local nets.

Proof
.

For any net
\mathscr{A}
on
X
, we have the identification
(f_{*}\mathscr{A})_{X}=\mathscr{A}_{X}=(g_{*}\mathscr{A})_{X}
of the global algebras.
This is clearly natural in
\mathscr{A}
, but we need to show that this isomorphism is controlled.
To this end, let
E=(g\times f)(\Delta)
, which is an entourage of
X^{\prime}
as
f
and
g

Proposition 2.23
.

If

2.4
Tensor factors and homomorphisms

Let
\mathscr{B}
be a net on
X
.
A
subnet
of
\mathscr{B}
is a net
\mathscr{A}
on
X
such that
\mathscr{A}_{B}\subseteq\mathscr{B}_{B}
for each
B\subseteq X
bounded.
We write
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathscr{B}
if
\mathscr{A}
is a subnet of
\mathscr{B}
.

Definition 2.24
.

Let
\mathscr{B}
be a net and let
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathscr{B}
be a subnet.
The
commutant
of
\mathscr{A}
in
\mathscr{B}
is the net
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}
given by

\mathscr{A}^{\prime}_{B}=(\mathscr{A}_{X})^{\prime}\cap\mathscr{B}_{B}\mathchar 59\relax

where
(\mathscr{A}_{X})^{\prime}=\{b\in\mathscr{B}_{X}\mid\forall a\in\mathscr{A}_{X}:ab=ba\}
is the commutant of
\mathscr{A}_{X}
in
\mathscr{B}_{X}
.

\mathscr{A}
is a
tensor factor
of
\mathscr{B}
if the canonical homomorphism
\mathscr{A}\otimes\mathscr{A}^{\prime}\to\mathscr{B}
is an isomorphism of nets.

Notice that
\mathscr{A}^{\prime}_{X}=(\mathscr{A}_{X})^{\prime}
and that multiplication induces a canonical algebra homomorphism
\mu:\mathscr{A}_{X}\otimes(\mathscr{A}_{X})^{\prime}\to\mathscr{B}_{X}
which restricts to
*
-homomorphisms

(\mathscr{A}\otimes\mathscr{A}^{\prime})_{B}=\mathscr{A}_{B}\otimes(\mathscr{A}_{X})^{\prime}\cap\mathscr{B}_{B}\longrightarrow\mathscr{B}_{B}

(2.3)

for every
B\subseteq X
.
However, it is generally not clear that these maps are isomorphisms, even if
\mu
is an isomorphism globally.
We have the following criterion to have an isomorphism of nets, compare
[
4
, Lemma 63]
.

Lemma 2.25
.

A subnet
\mathscr{A}\subseteq\mathscr{B}
of a net
\mathscr{B}
is a tensor factor if and only if the multiplication map
\mu:\mathscr{A}_{X}\otimes(\mathscr{A}_{X})^{\prime}\to\mathscr{B}_{X}
is injective and there exists an entourage
E
such that

\mathscr{B}_{B}\subseteq\mathscr{A}_{B_{E}}\cdot\mathscr{A}^{\prime}_{B_{E}}.

(2.4)

for all bounded sets
B\subseteq X
.

Proof
.

(
\Longrightarrow
) Suppose that
\mathscr{A}
is a tensor factor of
\mathscr{B}
.
Then multiplication yields a controlled
*
-homomorphism
\mu:(\mathscr{A}\otimes\mathscr{A}^{\prime})_{X}\cong\mathscr{A}_{X}\otimes(\mathscr{A}_{X})^{\prime}\to\mathscr{B}_{X}
whose inverse is controlled as well.
If
E
is a control for the inverse, then we have

\mathscr{B}_{B}\subseteq\mu\bigl((\mathscr{A}\otimes\mathscr{A}^{\prime})_{B_{E}}\bigr)=\mathscr{A}_{B_{E}}\cdot\mathscr{A}_{B_{E}}^{\prime}.

(
\Longleftarrow
)
Suppose that
\mu
is injective and that (
2.4
) holds.
By (
2.4
), we have

\mathscr{B}_{X}=\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\mathscr{B}_{B}\subseteq\bigcup_{B\in\mathcal{B}}\mathscr{A}_{B_{E}}\cdot\mathscr{A}_{B_{E}}^{\prime}=\mathscr{A}_{X}\otimes(\mathscr{A}_{X})^{\prime}.

This shows that
\mu
is also surjective, hence an isomorphism.

\mu
is always controlled (in fact, local) and (
2.4
) states precisely that the inverse is
E
-controlled.

Proposition 2.28

2.5
QCA and coarsely local automorphisms

Let
X
be a bornological coarse space.
A
quantum cellular automaton
, or
QCA
for short, is just another name for a (controlled) automorphism of a local matrix net on
X
.
QCA are usually considered modulo a special class of automorphisms, called
finite depth quantum circuits
.
However, this notion is not coarsely invariant; instead, we introduce the new notion of a
coarsely local
automorphism.
In §
2.6
below, we show that the two notions agree on spaces of bounded geometry.

We need the following notions:
A collection
(B_{i})_{i\in I}
of subsets of
X
is called
uniformly bounded
if
\bigcup_{i\in I}B_{i}\times B_{i}
is an entourage.
In particular, each
B_{i}
must be bounded.
It is
locally finite
if each
B\subseteq X
bounded has non-trivial intersection with at most finitely many
B_{i}
.

Definition 2.31
(coarsely local automorphisms).

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a local matrix net on
X
.
An automorphism
\alpha
of
\mathscr{A}
is
coarsely local
if
there exists a uniformly bounded and locally finite collection
(B_{i})_{i\in I}
of subsets of
X
, together with a decomposition

\mathscr{A}\cong\bigotimes_{i\in I}\mathscr{A}^{(i)}

into pairwise commuting tensor factors
\mathscr{A}^{(i)}
of
\mathscr{A}
supported on
B_{i}
, such that
\alpha
restricts to an automorphism of
\mathscr{A}^{(i)}
for each
i\in I
.
We denote by
\LAut(\mathscr{A})\subseteq\Aut(\mathscr{A})
the subgroup consisting of finite products of coarsely local automorphisms.

The infinite tensor product in the above definition is to be understood as

\mathscr{A}_{B}\cong\bigotimes_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\\
B_{i}\cap B\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}\mathscr{A}^{(i)}_{B}\mathchar 59\relax

which is a finite tensor product because the family
(B_{i})_{i\in I}
is locally finite.
We require that the isomorphism above is given by multiplication, which makes sense because the subalgebras
\mathscr{A}^{(i)}_{B}\subseteq\mathscr{A}_{B}
all pairwise commute.
Note that
\alpha
is controlled by
\bigcup_{i\in I}B_{i}\times B_{i}
.
Any local automorphism is coarsely local; here one may take the tensor decomposition
\mathscr{A}\cong\bigotimes_{x\in X}\mathscr{A}|_{x}
, which is subordinate to the decomposition of
X
into singleton subsets.

Lemma 2.32
.

\LAut(\mathscr{A})
is a normal subgroup of
\Aut(\mathscr{A})
.

Proof
.

Let
\alpha
be a coarsely local automorphism subordinate to the tensor factorization
\mathscr{A}\cong\bigotimes_{i\in I}\mathscr{A}^{(i)}
.
Then if
\beta\in\Aut(\mathscr{A})
is arbitrary,
\mathscr{A}\cong\bigotimes_{i\in I}\beta_{*}\mathscr{A}^{(i)}
is another tensor factorization, and
\beta\circ\alpha\circ\beta^{-1}
restricts to an automorphism of
\beta_{*}\mathscr{A}^{(i)}
for each
i\in I
.
If
\mathscr{A}^{(i)}
is supported on
B_{i}
and
\beta
is
E
-controlled, then
\beta_{*}\mathscr{A}^{(i)}
is supported on
(B_{i})_{E}
.
Clearly, the collection
((B_{i})_{E})_{i\in I}
is again uniformly bounded.
Since
B\cap(B_{i})_{E}\neq\emptyset
if and only if
B_{E^{-1}}\cap B_{i}\neq\emptyset
and
(B_{i})_{i\in I}
is locally finite by assumption,
((B_{i})_{E})_{i\in I}
is also locally finite.
Hence
\beta\circ\alpha\circ\beta^{-1}
is again coarsely local.
This implies the lemma.

\Box

By the above lemma, we can make the following definition:

Definition 2.33
(unstable QCA group).

For a local net
\mathscr{A}
on
X
, the group of
quantum cellular automata on
\mathscr{A}
is defined by

\mathrm{QCA}(\mathscr{A}):=\Aut(\mathscr{A})/\LAut(\mathscr{A}).

We now proceed to define the stable QCA group, where one is allowed to tensor with additional degrees of freedom.

Lemma 2.34
.

If
\varphi:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{B}
is a

\varphi_{*}:\mathrm{QCA}(\mathscr{A})\longrightarrow\mathrm{QCA}(\mathscr{B})\mathchar 59\relax\qquad[\alpha]\longmapsto[\varphi\circ\alpha\circ\varphi^{-1}].

, we have
\varphi_{*}=\psi_{*}

Proof
.

The group homomorphism is well-defined because the conjugation of a coarsely local automorphism on
\mathscr{A}
by an isomorphism
\varphi:\mathscr{A}\to\mathscr{B}
is a coarsely local automorphism on
\mathscr{B}

Let

be a support for
\mathscr{A}

\mathscr{A}_{B}=\bigotimes_{x\in B\cap Y}\mathscr{A}_{x}

Since
\varphi

\displaystyle(\psi_{*}\alpha)(\psi(a))

\displaystyle=\psi(\alpha(a))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Ad}_{u}(\varphi(\alpha(a)))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Ad}_{u}(\varphi_{*}\alpha(\varphi(a)))

\displaystyle=\mathrm{Ad}_{u}(\varphi_{*}\alpha(\mathrm{Ad}_{u}^{-1}(\psi(a)))).

\psi_{*}\alpha=\mathrm{Ad}_{u}\circ(\varphi_{*}\alpha)\circ\mathrm{Ad}_{u}^{-1}.

Therefore
\varphi_{*}\alpha

\displaystyle=[\mathscr{A}\otimes\mathscr{B}\mathchar 59\relax(\alpha\otimes\beta)\circ(\alpha^{\prime}\otimes\beta^{\prime})]

\displaystyle=[\mathscr{A}\otimes\mathscr{B}\mathchar 59\relax\alpha\otimes\beta]\cdot[\mathscr{A}\otimes\mathscr{B}\mathchar 59\relax\alpha^{\prime}\otimes\beta^{\prime}]

\displaystyle=\bigl([\mathscr{A}\mathchar 59\relax\alpha]*[\mathscr{B}\mathchar 59\relax\beta]\bigr)\cdot\bigl([\mathscr{A}\mathchar 59\relax\alpha^{\prime}]*[\mathscr{B}\mathchar 59\relax\beta^{\prime}]\bigr)\mathchar 59\relax

Hence this monoid operation commutes with the group structure.

2.6
Quantum circuits

Let

be a bornological coarse space.
The following is a straightforward adaptation to general coarse spaces of the usual notion of quantum circuits in QCA theory:

Definition 2.39
(quantum circuits).

Let
\mathscr{A}
be a local matrix net on
X
.
An automorphism
\alpha
of
\mathscr{A}
is a
depth one quantum circuit
if there exists a uniformly bounded, locally finite and pairwise disjoint collection
(B_{i})_{i\in I}
of subsets such that
\alpha
is controlled by the entourage
\bigcup_{i\in I}B_{i}\times B_{i}
.
We say that
(B_{i})_{i\in I}
is the

of the quantum circuit
\alpha
.
An automorphism
\alpha

\mathscr{A}
is a
depth
n

if it is a composition of at most
n
depth one quantum circuits and a
finite depth quantum circuit
if it is a quantum circuit of some finite depth
n
.
By
\mathrm{Cir}(\mathscr{A})\subseteq\Aut(\mathscr{A})
, we denote the subgroup of finite depth quantum circuits.

If
\alpha
is a depth one quantum circuit with support
(B_{i})_{i\in I}
, then
\alpha
restricts to an automorphism of
\mathscr{A}_{B_{i}}
for each
i\in I
and acts as the identity on
\mathscr{A}_{X\setminus\bigcup_{i\in I}B_{i}}
.
Since any automorphism of a matrix algebra is inner, there exist unitaries
u_{i}\in\mathscr{A}_{B_{i}}
such that

\alpha(a)=\Bigg(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\\
B_{i}\cap B\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}u_{i}\Bigg)\cdot a\cdot\Bigg(\prod_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\\
B_{i}\cap B\neq\emptyset\end{subarray}}u_{i}^{*}\Bigg)

for each
a\in\mathscr{A}_{B}
.
Here each product is finite since the collection
(B_{i})_{i\in I}
is locally finite.

Remark 2.40
.

Any depth one quantum circuit is a coarsely local automorphism.

Remark 2.41
.

There are two interrelated problems with the notion of quantum circuits:

(1)

It is claimed in
[
4
, Prop. 2.16]
that
\mathrm{Cir}(\mathscr{A})
is a normal subgroup of
\Aut(\mathscr{A})
for general discrete metric spaces.
However, the proof contains a gap that we do not know how to fix without additional hypothesis; in fact, it seems that
\mathrm{Cir}(\mathscr{A})
is not a normal subgroup in general.
To illustrate the problem, suppose that
\alpha
is a depth one quantum circuit with support
(B_{i})_{i\in I}
.
Then if
\beta\in\Aut(\mathscr{A})
is
E
-controlled,
\beta\circ\alpha\circ\beta^{-1}
has support
((B_{i})_{E})_{i\in I}
.
Now, since the fattened collection
((B_{i})_{E})_{i\in I}
is no longer pairwise disjoint, one must try to regroup the sets
(B_{i})_{E}
into finitely many pairwise disjoint subcollections (“layers”), showing that
\beta\circ\alpha\circ\beta^{-1}
is a finite depth quantum circuit.

However, this “distribution into layers” argument may fail in general.
For example, consider a graph where every vertex has finite degree, but which is not
n
-colorable for any
n
(for example, the disjoint union
\bigcup_{n\in\mathbb{N}}K_{n}
, where
K_{n}
is the complete graph with
n
vertices).
Equip the vertex set
X
with the pseudometric where two vertices have distance
k
if one needs at least
k
edges to connect them with a path.
Then the collection
(B_{x})_{x\in X}
of singleton subsets of
X
is uniformly bounded, locally finite and pairwise disjoint, but its
1
-fattening may not be regrouped into finitely many layers with the same properties.

(2)

The notion of quantum circuits is
not
a coarsely invariant notion.
The problem is that images of pairwise disjoint collections of subsets under coarse maps need not be pairwise disjoint again, and it may not even be possible to divide them into finitely many pairwise disjoint families.

There are conditions on the underlying space which ensure that the notion of a quantum circuit is nevertheless well-behaved.

Definition 2.42
.

Let
X
be a coarse space.

(1)

A subset
Y\subseteq X
is called
uniformly locally finite
if for each entourage
E
, there exists a number
n\in\mathbb{N}
such that for all
B\subseteq X
with
B\times B\subseteq E
, we have

\#(B\cap Y)\leq n.

(2)

X
is said to have
bounded geometry
if it admits a coarsely dense and uniformly locally finite subset
Y\subseteq X
.
(Here
Y
is called
coarsely dense
if there exists an entourage such that
Y_{E}=X
.)

Of course, basic examples for spaces with bounded geometry are
\mathbb{Z}^{n}
or
\mathbb{R}^{n}
.
However,
\mathbb{R}^{n}
is of course not uniformly locally finite and also contains locally finite subsets which are not uniformly locally finite.

Proposition 2.43
.

If
\mathscr{A}
admits a uniformly locally finite support, then we have

\LAut(\mathscr{A})=\mathrm{Cir}(\mathscr{A}).

In particular, it follows from Lemma

above that under the above support assumption on
\mathscr{A}
,
\mathrm{Cir}(\mathscr{A})
is a normal subgroup of
\Aut(\mathscr{A})
.

Proof
.

We always have the inclusion
\mathrm{Cir}(\mathscr{A})\subseteq\LAut(\mathscr{A})
, so it remains to show the converse inclusion.
We start with some preliminary observations.
For an entourage
E
of
X
, write

E[x]:=\{y\in X\mid(y\mathchar 59\relax x)\in E\}.

Observe that
E[x]\times E[x]\subseteq E^{-1}\circ E
, hence the collection
(E[x])_{x\in X}
is uniformly bounded.
Morever, if

is a uniformly locally finite support for
\mathscr{A}
, the collection
(E[y]\cap Y)_{y\in X}
is uniformly bounded and locally finite.

Let

be such that for any

with
B\times B\subseteq E\circ E^{-1}
, we have
\#(B\cap Y)\leq n
.
Then each

is contained in

for at most

points

:
Indeed, we have

if and only if

, and

, hence

by choice of

Therefore, after well-ordering

, we obtain a decomposition

such that the collections

are each pairwise disjoint.

Let now
\alpha
be a coarsely local automorphism of
\mathscr{A}
and let
\mathscr{A}\cong\bigotimes_{i\in I}\mathscr{A}^{(i)}
be a tensor decomposition as in the definition of coarse locality, where
\mathscr{A}^{(i)}
is supported on the member

of a uniformly bounded and locally finite collection
(B_{i})_{i\in I}
.
We assume that
\mathscr{A}^{(i)}
is non-trivial, so that

for each

.
We now use the considerations above for the entourage

Here we have

E[y]=\{x\in X\mid\exists i\in I:x\mathchar 59\relax y\in B_{i}\}=\bigcup_{\begin{subarray}{c}i\in I\\

Hence each

is contained in some

.
We may therefore choose for each
B_{i}
an element

such that
B_{i}\subseteq E[y_{i}]
.
This gives a decomposition

, where
I_{k}=\{i\in I\mid y_{i}\in Y_{k}\}
.
Moreover, this results in a decomposition

, where
\alpha_{k}
acts as

and trivial on

3
A coarse homology theory

In this section, we construct a coarse homology theory whose degree zero groups are given by quantum cellular automata.

Let us fix set-theoretic size issues for the constructions in this section: We choose two Grothendieck universes, whose elements are called
small sets
and
large sets
.
All bornological coarse spaces and nets on these are assumed to have underlying sets coming from the first Grothendieck universe.
The set of all nets on a bornological coarse space will then form a large set.

3.1
QCA and
K
-theory

If
\mathfrak{C}
is a symmetric monoidal category, its
K
-theory group
K_{0}(\mathfrak{C})
is the abelian group obtained by group completing the commutative monoid of isomorphism classes of objects of
\mathfrak{C}
, with monoid operation given by the tensor product of
\mathfrak{C}

[x]+[y]:=[x\otimes y].

To define the higher
K
-theory groups of

, one uses the classifying space functor

, the latter denoting the
\infty
-category of (large) simplicial sets.
This functor preserves commutative monoid objects: If
\mathfrak{C}
is a symmetric monoidal category, then

is a commutative monoid in
Spaces
.
Here one may form the
\infty
-categorical group completion functor

to obtain a grouplike commutative monoid in spaces, which by May’s recognition principle is the same information as an infinite loop space, i.e., a connective spectrum.
In total, we get a

-theory functor

K:\texttt{CMon}(\textsc{Cat})\xrightarrow{~~B~~}\texttt{CMon}(\textsc{Spaces})\xrightarrow{~~\begin{subarray}{c}\text{group}\\
\text{completion}\end{subarray}~~}\texttt{CMon}^{\mathrm{grp}}(\textsc{Spaces})\simeq\textsc{Spectra}_{\geq 0}

from symmetric monoidal categories to connective spectra.
The functor

commutes with colimits, and group completion is a left adjoint, so
K
also preserves colimits.

-theory functor has the property that

3.3
Definition of the coarse homology theory

The notion of group-valued coarse homology theories was first axiomatically introduced by Mitchener
[
5
]
.
The modern point of view is that a group-valued homology theory should be the “shadow” of a functor valued in spectra (or some other stable
\infty
-category), which yields a group-valued functor by taking homotopy groups.
The following definition is equivalent to that of Bunke–Engel
[
1
, Definition 4.22]
.

Definition 3.11
.

A functor

F:\textsc{BornCoarse}\longrightarrow\textsc{Spectra}

is a
coarse homology theory
if it satisfies the following axioms:

(1)

F
is
coarsely invariant
, i.e., whenever
f\mathchar 59\relax g:X\to X^{\prime}
are close, then
F(f)\simeq F(g)
.

(2)

F

vanishes on flasques
, i.e., whenever
X
is flasque, then
F(X)\simeq 0
.

(3)

F
satisfies the
Mayer-Vietoris axiom
:
Whenever
\mathcal{Y}
and
\mathcal{Z}
are two big families in a coarse space
X
, then we have a pushout square

{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F(\mathcal{Y}\Cap\mathcal{Z})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F(\mathcal{Y})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F(\mathcal{Z})}
{\lx@inpgf@ignorespaces F(\mathcal{Y}\Cup\mathcal{Z}).}

Here for any big family in
X
, we define
F(\mathcal{Y}):=\colim_{Y\in\mathcal{Y}}F(Y)
.

(4)

F
is
u
-continuous
.
This means that if for an entourage
E
on a bornological coarse space
X
,
X_{E}
denotes the space
X
with the same bornology but equipped with the smallest coarse structure containing
E
, then

F(X)=\colim_{E\in\mathcal{C}}F(X_{E}).

Taking
F
to be one of the functors
K\circ\textsc{Az}
of
K\circ\textsc{Loc}
, we have already checked axioms (1) and (2) and axiom (4) is also easy to check.
However, neither functor satisfies the Mayer-Vietoris axiom.
The problem is that all the
K
-theory spectra are connective, which generates a problem at the degree zero level.
Fortunately, non-connective deloopings are provided by Thm.
3.4
.

Definition 3.12
.

For a bornological coarse space
X
, we define

Q(X):=\colim_{n\to\infty}\Omega^{n+1}K\bigl(\textsc{Az}(X\otimes\mathbb{R}^{n})\bigr)\mathchar 59\relax

where the colimit is taken in the
\infty
-category of spectra along the maps provided by Thm.
3.4
.
Varying
X
, this defines a functor

Q:\textsc{BornCoarse}\longrightarrow\textsc{Spectra}.

Since all the spectrum maps from Thm.
3.4
induce homotopy equivalences in positive degree, they particularly induce homotopy equivalences on the corresponding infinite loop spaces, hence the spectrum
Q(X)
may equivalently be described as the
\Omega
-spectrum
E_{0}\mathchar 59\relax E_{1}\mathchar 59\relax\dots
, where the
n
-th space

is given by

Its homotopy groups are given by the formula

Q_{n}(X):=\pi_{n}(Q(X))=K_{n+m+1}\bigl(\textsc{Az}(X\otimes\mathbb{R}^{m})\bigr)\mathchar 59\relax\qquad\text{for}~~m\geq-n-1.

The definition is made so that for any bornological coarse space

Our main result is now the following.

Proof

Coarse invariance follows from Prop.

It was shown in

that one has a canonical isomorphism

K_{0}(\textsc{Loc}(X))\cong H\mathcal{X}_{0}(X\mathchar 59\relax\mathbb{Q}_{>0})\mathchar 59\relax

where
H\mathcal{X}_{n}(X)
denotes the coarse homology groups valued in the abelian group
\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
(see

1

) and
\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{P}}
denotes the set of maps from the set
\mathbb{P}
of all prime numbers to
\mathbb{Z}

The group
H\mathcal{X}_{0}(X\mathchar 59\relax\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{P}})
is just a quotient of the group of functions
X\to\mathbb{Q}_{>0}
of locally finitely support, and the isomorphism above is given by sending a local matrix
\mathscr{A}
net to its dimension function
q(x)=\dim(\mathscr{A}_{x})^{1/2}

2.16
.

Since
Q_{-1}(X)=K_{0}(\textsc{Az}(X))
, we get a canonical map

H\mathcal{X}_{0}(X\mathchar 59\relax\mathbb{Q}_{>0})\longrightarrow Q_{-1}(X).

Since
H\mathcal{X}_{k}(\mathbb{Z}^{n})\cong H\mathcal{X}_{k-n}(*)
(as is the case for all coarse homology theories), we see that for
X=\mathbb{Z}^{n}
, the left group is zero and so does not provide any non-trivial elements.
