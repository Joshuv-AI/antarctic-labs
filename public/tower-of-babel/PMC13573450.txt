Making themselves scarce: immune cells in the ovary
Authors: Bettina Toth, Elisabeth Reiser, Samuel M Vorbach, Monika Frank, Lea Huber, Thomas Seppi, Elisabeth Pechriggl, Bettina Böttcher, Katharina Feil, Marko Konschake, Ute Ganswindt, Michael Niederwanger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractResearch questionIn human and animal reproduction, the majority of the ovary’s reproductive potential is lost before puberty. In order to survive, oocytes must interact with stromal cells. However, the specific distribution and role of ovarian immune cells compared to lymphatic organs is still indistinct.DesignImmune cell composition in the ovary, spleen, and lymph nodes was analyzed in six-week-old female C57BL/6 mice using flow cytometry. The following populations were examined: macrophages, dendritic cells, natural killer cells, NKT cells, B cells, T cells, CD4 + T cells and CD8 + T cells.ResultsOnly 3% of the cells that make up the ovary are immune cells. Ovarian immune cells consisted of natural killer cells (24%), T cells including CD4+- and CD8 + T cells (21%), macrophages (17%), dendritic cells (7%), NKT- and B cells (each 11%). In contrast, spleen and lymph nodes were dominated by B and T cells (spleen: 56% and 32%; lymph nodes: 33% and 62%).ConclusionsOvarian immune cells are scarce in healthy young mice. Apart from a relatively large T-cell population, the ovarian immune compartment is dominated by innate immune cells, particularly macrophages and natural killer cells, which is in line with the multifarious role of macrophages in multiple aspects of ovarian physiology. In contrast, adaptive immune cells such as B and T lymphocytes are primarily enriched in secondary lymphoid organs, including the spleen and lymph nodes.Keywords: Immune cells, Ovary, Aging

Introduction

The ovary is composed of extracellular matrix components, a heterogeneous population of fibroblasts, smooth muscle cells, endothelial cells, stem cells, theca–interstitial cells, and immune cells [1]. Moreover, blood vessels, lymphatic vessels, and nerves permeate the ovary. Increasing evidence suggests that the ovarian microenvironment significantly influences reproductive processes, including follicle and oocyte development [2].

Both humans and mice possess a finite pool of primordial follicles that constitute the ovarian reserve. In humans, most of this reproductive potential is lost by puberty [3]. Apoptosis, cell death, and additional yet-uncharacterized mechanisms contribute to this decline. Close interaction between stromal cells and oocytes are essential for oocyte survival [4].

Despite the recognized importance of the ovarian microenvironment, the role of immune cells in ovarian physiology remains insufficiently understood, particularly in comparison to other organs. This study investigates the composition of immune cells in the ovaries of young female mice and compares it to the composition in spleen and lymph nodes. Given the low abundance of immune cells in the ovary as a protective adaptation to create a privileged microenvironment that allows for the safe development of oocytes which are vulnerable to immune-mediated destruction, we hypothesized that innate immune cells would predominate over adaptive immune cells.

Methods

Tissue harvesting

Ten six-week-old female C57BL/6 mice (Charles River, Germany) were sacrificed via cervical dislocation and placed in dorsal recumbency. Prior to scarification, mice received an intraperitoneal injection for anesthesia and analgesia. The anesthetic mixture consisted of 7.5 mL NaCl, 0.5 mL Domitor (0.5 ampoule), 1.0 mL Dormicum (1 ampoule), and 1.0 mL Fentanyl (0.5 ampoule). Mice were weighed prior to administration, and the injected volume was adjusted to body weight at a dose of 0.1 mg/kg. A 27 G needle (EN ISO 6009: medium grey; EN ISO 9626: 0.04 mm) was used for injection. A U-shaped incision was made at the hind limbs, and the skin was separated from the abdominal wall and pinned laterally to minimize contamination. After opening the abdominal cavity, inguinal lymph nodes, spleen, and ovaries were identified and removed. Tissues were placed in MACS Tissue Storage Solution (Miltenyi Biotec, Germany) and transported on ice. Estrous cycle was determined via vaginal swob: eight and two mice were in estrus and proestrus stage, respectively.

FACS analysis of immune cells

Whole organs (ovaries and spleen) and lymph nodes were placed in HBSS (H6648, Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA) supplemented with 2% FBS (10082147, Gibco life technologies, Carlsbad, California, USA), cleaned of residual fat and connective tissue and transferred to RPMI (R8758, Sigma-Aldrich, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA). Lymph nodes were opened and incubated with 250 µg/ml Collagenase D (11088858001, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) and 240 µg/ml DNase I (1010459001, Roche, Basel, Switzerland) at 37 °C for 25 min. Enzymatic activity was stopped using EDTA (AM92060G, Invitrogen, Carlsbad, California, USA). After filtration through a 100 μm strainer, cells were centrifuged and resuspended in PBS-FVS700. Non-specific antibody binding was blocked using 1% Fc Blocking Reagent (130-059-901, Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Nordrhein-Westfalen, Germany). Cells were stained with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies for 15 min in the dark and analyzed on a Beckman Coulter DX Flex cytometer. Data were processed using FlowJo v10.8.0 (Becton Dickinson, NJ, USA).

FACS gating strategy

Cell viability was assessed using BD Horizon Fixable Viability Stain 700, with viable cells defined as FVS700-negative/low events. Cell debris and doublets were excluded by sequential gating on FSC-A versus SSC-A and FSC-A versus FSC-H, respectively. Leukocytes were then selected as CD45 + cells using BV421-conjugated anti-mouse CD45. Within the CD45 + population, B cells, NK cells, and T cells were identified using CD19, NK1.1, and TCRβ, respectively; NKT cells were defined as CD45+ TCRβ + NK1.1 + events. Myeloid cells were analyzed by hierarchical gating and resolved into dendritic cells and macrophages on the basis of CD11b and F4/80 expression. T cells and NK cells were excluded prior to Cd11b analysis to avoid double counting in the CD11b+ population. The gating strategy of the three organs is depicted in Fig. 1. Statistics

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.5.0 (774) (GraphPad Software, San Diego, California, USA). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

For normally distributed data, ordinary ANOVA or Brown–Forsythe/Welch ANOVA was applied, followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. For non-normal data, the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn’s post-hoc correction was used. Significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Immune cells constituted approximately 3% of all ovarian cells and were predominantly macrophages, NK cells, and T cells. Composition of the ovarian immune cell population was as follows: macrophages 17.1% ± 5.0, dendritic cells 6.8% ± 2.4, NK cells 23.9% ± 7.0, NKT cells 11.1% ± 4.8, B cells 11.0% ± 2.2, and total T cells 21.5% ± 10.0 (of which CD4⁺ 9.8% ± 3.8 and CD8⁺ 4.5% ± 1.5).

In the spleen, immune cell populations were dominated by B cells (55.8% ± 3.1) and T cells (total 32.0% ± 2.1; of which CD4 + 18.4% ± 1.5 and CD8 + 11.2% ± 0.9). Macrophages, DCs, NK and NKT cells contributed to a minor extent to the entire immune cell population in the spleen (< 10% in total).

Lymph nodes similarly contained high proportions of B cells (33.4% ± 4.3) and T cells (total 62.1% ± 4.4; of which CD4 + 34.1% ± 2.0 and CD8 + 25.1% ± 2.6). Contribution of macrophages, DCs, NK and NKT cells to the entire immune cell population in the lymph nodes was negligible (2.5% in total).

Discussion

This study provides the first direct comparison of ovarian, splenic, and lymph node immune cell composition within a single experimental framework. Our findings confirm previous reports regarding immune cell distributions in spleen and lymph nodes [5] and demonstrate that immune cells constitute only a small fraction (3%) of ovarian cells in young mice. These ovarian immune cells are predominantly innate immune cells—macrophages, NK cells, and T cells—consistent with recent studies highlighting the unique immunological environment of the ovary [4, 6, 7].

This difference in immune cell composition may suggest that immune surveillance in the ovary relies less on classical antigen-driven responses and more on rapid, locally regulated innate mechanisms. Innate immune cells play essential roles in ovarian physiology, including follicle development, atresia, ovulation, and post-ovulatory tissue remodeling [8]. NK cells, accounting for 23.9% of ovarian immune cells in our study, exhibit cytotoxic, pro-angiogenic, and regulatory functions and can promote the activation of dendritic cells, macrophages, and T cells [5]. In humans, NK cell populations in follicular fluid shift during follicle maturation, with a decline in cytotoxic NK cells [9]. Macrophages, accounting for 17.1% of ovarian immune cells in our study, are key regulators of folliculogenesis, tissue restructuring at ovulation as well as corpus luteum formation and regression [10]. Also, they are increasingly recognized as central players in ovarian aging where inflammation plays a critical role and the interaction of the immune cells most likely contributes to the maintenance of reproductive function in the ovary [11].

The immune landscape of the ovary differs markedly from that of secondary lymphoid organs, which are specialized for antigen presentation and lymphocyte activation and thereafter strongly harbor T and B cells. The low numbers of these cell subsets in the ovary may reflect the need to limit cytotoxic immune activity in the ovary in order to preserve tissue integrity and reproductive function. The ovary seems to store immune-privileged or tightly regulated microenvironment.

This study is limited by its modest sample size; however, animal studies are delicate and our data provide important foundation for future work. Since immune cells in the ovary account for less than 3% of the total cell population in the tissue, FACS analysis of mice tissues reached its reliable detection count-limit when quantifying very small relative immune cell subpopulations. Also, with other methods such as single-cell RNA sequencing, the type and total number of detected immune cells remains low [12].

This study expands the current state of knowledge regarding immune cell populations in the healthy ovary. Building on this foundation, changes in the ovarian immune cell composition, e.g. related to aging can be further investigated. The impact of gonadotoxic therapy on ovarian immune cells will also be studied in the future. In particular, understanding the role of immune alterations in treatment-induced ovarian aging may help further clarify the process of age-related oocyte loss.
