EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
 This report provides an overview of engineering and experimental efforts to establish a
new capability for measuring H2 uptake rates for Tritium Producing Abosrber Rod (TPBAR)
getter materials. This new system was used to gain a better understanding of the impact of
contaminant species (CO2, CO, CH4, and H2O) on getter performance under relevant hydrogen
uptake conditions (350 °C, 20 torr).

 Herein, we successfully established a high-resolution gravimetric sorption (HRGS)
system in a flow through configuration that can measure sample mass changes with high
resolution (1 µg or 10 µg) over long experiment times (days), high temperature, and varying
pressures. We coupled this capability with an in-line universal gas analyzer (UGA) system to
characterize potential surface chemistry side reactions occurring after contaminant gas exposure.

 The major findings of this study suggest that methane and CO have the most detrimental
effects on getter H2 uptake performance showing large changes in rate after exposure compared
to CO2 and water. The effect of these contaminant species did not influence getter sample
capacity (~ 1.8 wt% H2). After CO and methane exposure, changes in getter rate were
accompanied by observable Ni-surface defects and pinholes via SEM imaging suggesting
possible Ni- layer corrosion. Additionally, the increase in C and O signal in the zircaloy layer,
observed via EDX, point to C and O species transport through the getter sample resulting in the
formation of carbides and oxide species. Thus, co-exposure studies where mixed gases or water
vapor are introduced together will be important to further understand what mechanisms are
contributing to Ni-layer corrosion and changes in getter performance.

 Abstract
 A Tritium Producing Burnable Absorber Rod (TPBAR) is a specialized component used
in commercial nuclear reactors to produce tritium (3H ) for the U.S. Department of Energy’s
Tritium Modernization Program. TPBARs include a zirconium alloy (Zircaloy-4) getter tube to
bind tritium as a metal hydride with a nickel (Ni) barrier coating. Since Zircaloy is susceptible to
surface reactions with contaminant gases and surface impurities (e.g. CO, CO2, CH4 and H2O)
that can form unwanted surface oxide and carbon (coke), the Ni coating acts as a protective
barrier to prevent getter poisoning effects. Herein, we used a custom high resolution gravimetric
sorption (HRGS) system developed at Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) to benchmark
getter rate changes resulting from contaminant species (e.g. CO, CO2, CH4, and H2O) exposure
at 20 torr total pressure using hydrogen (H2) as a tritium surrogate at 350 ⸰C. The HRGS system
can operate under high pressure, vacuum, and corrosive gas conditions within a wide
temperature range (-196 ⸰C to 400 ⸰C). Using an in-situ exposure approach and in-line gas
analysis, we tracked changes in hydrogen uptake performance in real-time at 20 torr pressure and
350 ⸰C gettering conditions. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with energy dispersive X-ray
Spectroscopy (EDX) was used for post-sample imaging and elemental composition analysis.

 Hydrogen uptake measurements reveal that the custom HRGS system is good for TPBAR
getter performance evaluation. Herein, we demonstrate sample reproducibility and detail a
workflow for contaminant gas exposure studies that can be improved upon and modified for
future studies. Contaminant species studies with methane and CO resulted in a decrease in getter
rate when exposed for 1 hour. The getter rate decreased by ~44% when exposed to CO.
Generally, the ultimate hydrogen uptake was unaffected by exposure to different gases, with all
rods reaching approximately 1.8% hydrogen despite slower rates for rods exposed to methane
and CO. SEM/EDX reveal larger surface defects on the Ni-layer after exposure to methane and
CO, suggesting that more complex surface chemistry is present and warrant further investigation.
Future studies will focus on longer exposure times and mixed gas studies to evaluate effect on
performance and surface morphology.

 Introduction
 A Tritium Producing Burnable Absorber Rod (TPBAR) is a specialized component used
in commercial nuclear reactors to produce tritium (3H) for the U.S. Department of Energy’s
Tritium Modernization Program. TPBARs include a zirconium alloy (Zircaloy-4) getter tube to
bind tritium as a metal hydride with a nickel (Ni) protective barrier coating. Since Zircaloy is
susceptible to surface reactions with contaminant gases and surface impurities (e.g. CO, CO2,
CH4 and H2O) that form unwanted surface oxide and carbon (Coke), the Ni coating acts as a
protective barrier to prevent getter poisoning effects.1

 From previous post-irradiation examination (PIE) campaigns, surface carbon deposits
have been observed on multiple TPBAR components with unknown effects on performance.2, 3
Thus, fundamental studies to understand carbon transport and deposition mechanisms are
needed. While the carbon source and transfer mechanism during TPBAR operation are under
investigation, gaseous sources such as CO2, CO, CH4 have been previously observed to react at
metallic surfaces under relevant temperature conditions 350 ⸰C.4-6 Thus, gaseous and surface
contaminant species are likely a contributing source to the observed carbon deposits that must be
better understood to accurately predict TPBAR performance and reliability.

 For Nickel-plated getters (NPG), the role of Ni is of particular interest due to known
competitive catalysis mechanisms at Ni surfaces. Accessible catalytic reactions for Ni at
relatively low temperature (350 ⸰C) include reverse water-gas shift or water-gas shift, CO2 or CO
methanation, and methane reforming reactions which can result in surface C(coke) deposition
under high carbon, hydrogen lean, and dry conditions.7-9 Surface C or coke formation occurs
when carbon surface species (CO2, CO, CH4) cannot be removed by hydrogenation or
gasification reactions on a metal surface. Instead surface bound carbon species can follow
disproportionation pathways that result in C(coke) deposition.10 Surface absorbed species can
also lead to changes in Ni surface oxidation state and surface morphology resulting in defects
that can reduce the fidelity of the Ni-barrier protection of the zircalloy getter.

 Herein, we used a custom high resolution gravimetric sorption (HRGS) system developed
at Los Alamos National Laboratory (LANL) to benchmark getter rate changes resulting from
contaminant species (CO, CO2, CH4, and H2O) exposure at 20 torr total pressure using hydrogen
(H2) as a tritium surrogate at 350 ⸰C. The HRGS system can operate under high pressure,
vacuum, and corrosive gas conditions with a wide temperature range (-196 ⸰C to 400 ⸰C). In our
preliminary benchmarking experiments, we aimed to (1) stand-up the HRGS capability for getter
testing, (2) develop a suitable testing workflow for contaminant exposure, (3) benchmark
changes in getter performance after contaminant exposure, and (4) better understand the effect of
individual contaminant species on getter rate and uptake.

 Methods & Experimental Approach
 In this section, we describe the components of LANL developed high resolution
gravimetric sorption (HRGS) capability that uses an in-line universal gas analyzer (UGS) mass
spectrometer to benchmark getter rate and uptake performance. Contaminant exposure was
introduced by charging the HRGS cell with 20 torr of contaminant gas (CO2, CO, CH4) for 1
hour or a 100 µL volume injection of water. SEM-EDX was used to characterize getters after
exposure studies.

 In-line Analysis with Universal Gas Analyzer (UGA) Mass Spectrometer

 A universal gas analyzer (UGA) is an instrument designed to measure and identify the
composition of gases in a sample using mass spectrometry. A small volume of sample gas is
drawn into the analyzer via vacuum pump. At the UGA, the gas is ionized and passes through a
quadrupole mass spectrometer to separate ions based on their mass-to-charge ratio (m/z). Using
the resulting mass fingerprints, gas composition can be determined. In the LANL system, the in-
line UGA allows for real-time gas composition analysis that can be correlated to gravimetric
changes in the HRGS system.

 The UGA mass spectrometer was attached to one HRGS system for in-line analysis. The
UGA was run in two modes: full spectra scanning and select-ion detection. For each experiment
(except for H2O), the UGA was initialized in full spectra scanning mode to collect baseline UGA
spectra over the full range of the UGA mass spectrometry detector.

 Getter Rate Sample Preparation

 Getter samples were provided by Pacific Northwest National Laboratory (PNNL)
(LOT#431147-210802A). The material was prepared, cleaned, and activated by PNNL. LANL
cut as received getter samples into 0.5-inch sample lengths for loading into alumina sample
holder basket. We incorporated a thermal treatment step in air (400 ⸰C) to remove additional
organic contaminants that may have been introduced during the second cuts made to the samples.

 Experimental Approach

 Getter Gas Exposure Workflow

 Initial and final mass measurements under He were necessary to verify the
experimentally measured gas uptake through the Δmass observed in He and H2. The buoyancy
factor, determined by the specific gas and sampling pressure, affects the measured sample weight
in the HRGS system and must be accounted for. The use of these distinct measurement
conditions ultimately enables a double check of the final Δmass and, correspondingly, the extent
of uptake observed.

Baseline H2 uptake experiment with no contaminant gas exposure

 Getter samples (0.5 inch in length) were thermally pre-treated under flowing air at 10
sccm and 20 Torr (21% O2 bal N2) at 400°C for 1 h for removal of water and organic
contaminants. Next, the sample chamber was cooled to 350 ⸰C under He for 2 h for initial mass
sampling and ensure balance stabilization. Subsequently, the system was switched over to H2, the
outlet gas valve was opened to allow UGA sampling. The sample was allowed to react to
completion (i.e. no measurable uptake observed over a 24-h period). After completion, the
system was switched back to He to obtain the Δmass measured in He.

Contaminant gas (CO2, CO, CH4) exposure and getter performance experiment

 Getter samples (0.5 inch in length) were thermally pre-treated under flowing air at 10
sccm and 20 Torr (21% O2 bal N2) at 400°C for 1 h for removal of water and organic
contaminants. Next, the sample chamber was heated to 350 ⸰C under He for 2 h for initial mass
sampling and ensure balance stabilization. Subsequently, the system was switched over to H2, the
outlet gas valve was opened to allow UGA sampling. The sample was allowed to react for up to
2 days or approximately half H2 wt % loading. Experiment run times varied due to changes in

uptake rates. At this point, the contaminant gas was charged into the system at high temperature
for 1 h. After inoculation with the contaminant gas the system was switched back to H2 at 20

back to He to obtain the Δmass measured in He.

Contaminant water (H2O) exposure and getter performance experiment

 Getter samples (0.5 inch in length) were thermally pre-treated under flowing air at 10
sccm and 20 Torr (21% O2 bal N2) at 400°C for 1 h for removal of water and organic
contaminants. Next, the sample chamber was heated to 350 ⸰C under He for 2 h for initial mass
sampling and ensure balance stabilization. Subsequently, the system was switched over to H2.
The sample was allowed to react for up to 2 days or approximately half H2 wt % loading. At this
point, the sample chamber on the HRGS system was opened and 100 µL of water was injected
into the sample basket. The sample chamber was closed and held at 350 C for 1 h to allow for
vaporization of the water. The pump was slow to reach target vacuum, suggesting that the water
vapor was present in the chamber. Next, He was flowed at 10 sccm for 1 hour, before switching
back to H2. The sample was allowed to run to completion. After completion, the system was
switched back to He to obtain the Δmass measured in He. UGA sampling was not utilized during
the water exposure experiment.

 Getter Performance Calculation & Normalization

 Getter performance rates and final uptake values were calculated using the mass changes
over time recorded by the HRGS system. During the experiment, the automatic sample decoupler
(ASD) was initiated periodically during the experiment to tare the balance during the experiment
and record the weight of the permanent magnet in the free-floating condition or a “zero-point”
(ZP). After ZP is recorded, the measuring point (MP), where the weight of the sample holder and
the permanent magnet are recoupled, is recorded. The ZP was measured every 5 minutes during
the uptake experiments and used as background subtraction point for data post-processing,
demonstrated in Figure 3.

 Experimental Results
 Contaminant Gas Effect on Hydrogen Getter Rate & Uptake

 First, baseline measurements of the as received TPBAR getter were conducted in the
HRGS with in-line UGA sampling to understand and document baseline getter performance. To
remove adventitious carbon or organic contaminants, the as received TPBAR getter pieces were
first exposed to an elevated thermal treatment at 400 ⸰C in air for 1 hour. From earlier studies,
our team learned that additional thermal treatments lead to further getter activation; please see
Appendix A for further discussion on these findings.

 Figure 5. Replicate H2 runs demonstrating reproducibility.

 Getter uptake rates for two pieces of the same length (0.5-inch) were measured to be ~3
wt% H2/day with a total uptake of ~1.75 wt % H2, shown in Figure 5. The total gettering time to
reach full uptake was 36 hours at 350 ⸰C and 20 torr of H2. The mass uptake curves (dotted lines)
show similar uptake behavior, with both samples reaching nearly the same plateau.

 A helium exposure run was used to verify that changes in the mass uptake determined by
the HRGS system were due to differences in getter uptake rate, not an artifact of the sampling
method. TPBAR getter rod was exposed to the same contaminant gas exposure protocol where
the sample environment switches from hydrogen to He for 1 h at 350 ⸰C and then switched back
to hydrogen to measure changes in H2 uptake rate (Figure 6). The sample showed an expected
buoyancy related variation in mass due to chamber environmental changes, but the getter rate
was unchanged after He exposure. The corresponding UGA data showed a strong He peak that
dissipated overtime, indicating no sign of He sorption or adverse surface chemistry hindering
mass uptake of hydrogen.

 Scanning electron microscopy is a powerful imaging technique that was used here for

samples used for baseline H2 uptake experiments shown in Figure 15, show a smooth surface

 Conclusions
 This study set out to establish a new capability for measuring H2 uptake rates for TPBAR
getter materials and gain understanding of the impact of contaminant species (CO2, CO, CH4,
and H2O) on getter performance under relevant conditions (350 °C, 20 torr). Herein, we
successfully established a high-resolution gravimetric sorption (HRGS) system in a flow through
configuration that can measure sample mass changes with high resolution (1 µg or 10 µg) over
long experiment times (days), high temperature, and varying pressures. We coupled this
capability with in-ling universal gas analyzer (UGA) system to characterize potential surface
chemistry side reactions occurring after contaminant gas exposure. The results reported here are
preliminary and are limited by a small sampling size per contaminant species exposure.

 The findings of our study suggest that methane and CO have the most detrimental effects
on getter H2 uptake performance showing large changes in rate after exposure. On the other
hand, CO2 and water exposure showed minimal getter rate changes suggesting that on their own,
these gases do not hinder getter performance. However, the current limitation of our HRGS
system complicates the water exposure measurements and will be addressed in future studies.

 It is clear from this study that the catalytic properties of Ni are playing a role slowing
getter uptake rates in CO and methane environments. The getter sample capacity was unaffected,
and every sample measured reached a similar H2 saturation level regardless of gas exposure (~
1.8 wt% H2). Changes in getter rate were accompanied by observable Ni-surface defects and
pinholes after CO and methane exposure via SEM imaging. These dramatic morphology changes
suggest that adverse surface chemistry is occurring leading to the deterioration or corrosion of
the Ni-layer resulting in a high concentration of defects that likely compromise its effectiveness
in protecting the zircaloy layer. Additionally, the increase in C and O signal in the zircaloy layer,
observed via EDX, point to C and O transport through the getter sample that may point changes
in zircaloy and Ni phases or composition in the form of carbides and oxide species. Thus, co-
exposure studies where mixed gases or water vapor are introduced together will be important to
further understand what mechanisms are contributing to Ni-layer corrosion.
