Branching plasticity and candidate gene–hormone networks associated with shade responses in soybean under relay strip intercropping
Authors: Lingxiao He, Tao Gong, Fenda Tang, Panxia Shao, Hao Xu, Jian Li, Ming Guo, Zhetao Zhang, Mei Xu, Xiaopei Tang, Xiaoling Wu, Wenyu Yang, Yao Zhao, Weiguo Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundBranching is a key determinant of high-yield plant architecture in soybean, particularly in maize- soybean relay strip intercropping where plants experience an “initially shaded–then fully illuminated” light regime. However, the genetic regulation of branching responses to shading remains poorly understood.MethodsWe evaluated 11 branching-related traits across 202 soybean accessions grown under monoculture (SS) and relay strip intercropping (RI). Branch number (BN), branching incidence (BI), and total branch length (TBL) were assessed together with stress tolerance indices (STI) and relative distance plasticity index (RDPI). Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) using mixed linear model (MLM) and three-variance-component MLM (3VmrMLM) were combined with haplotype and protein structural analyses to refine candidate genes.ResultsBased on Pearson correlation analysis of all 11 traits, BN, BI, and TBL measured before maize harvest showed the strongest and most consistent associations with branch seed weight within the corresponding cropping system (BSW_SS under SS and BSW_RI under RI), whereas other traits showed weaker or environment-dependent associations. Higher STI values calculated from these traits during the co-growth phase were negatively associated with BSW_RI, suggesting weaker compensatory recovery after light restoration in genotypes with more stable early branching patterns between SS and RI. In contrast, mediation analysis indicated that RDPI was positively associated with BSW_RI mainly through improved mature branching architecture (MB_index), which accounted for approximately 70% of the total positive effect. GWAS identified 57 and 74 significant QTNs using MLM and 3VmrMLM, respectively, and LD-window genes were filtered for exonic nonsynonymous or premature stop-codon variants, yielding 883 genes with putative functional variants. Two high-confidence genes emerged: Glyma.02G058600 (PP2C55), exhibiting shading-specific haplotype effects likely linked to GA-mediated branch–stem balance, and Glyma.02G059900 (DA1-related protein), showing stable effects across environments and implicated in ABA-mediated suppression of axillary meristems.ConclusionsThese results provide insight into the genetic and physiological basis of soybean branching responses under relay strip intercropping, clarify that branching plasticity and relative shade tolerance represent distinct response dimensions in this system, and identify putative loci that may be useful for breeding soybean cultivars with improved shade adaptation and yield stability.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09314-y.

Introduction

Soybean (Glycine max (L.) Merr.) is a major source of both oil and protein for human consumption and livestock feed [1]. However, with the continuous decline of arable land in China, increasing soybean yield can no longer rely solely on expanding monoculture acreage [2]. Maize–soybean relay strip intercropping, a representative intercropping system, enhances resource-use efficiency through interspecific complementarity [3]. Nevertheless, soybean seedlings in this system are frequently subjected to shading stress from the maize canopy, leading to excessive stem elongation and reduced branch number and length—typical shade-avoidance responses—that ultimately impair yield formation [4].

Branching plays a central role in shaping the ideal plant architecture of soybean [5]. Branches not only determine canopy light interception and ventilation but also directly influence pod distribution and yield stability [6, 7]. Previous studies have shown that increasing branch number can markedly improve per-plant yield, as demonstrated by miR156b-overexpressing lines with increased branching and seed production [8]. Importantly, branch development under relay strip intercropping is closely linked to shade-induced hormonal regulation. Under low red: far-red (R: FR) light conditions, PHYTOCHROME-INTERACTING FACTORS (PIF4 and PIF5) regulate abscisic acid (ABA) biosynthesis and signaling, contributing to bud inhibition and shade responses [9, 10]. Consistently, ABA-deficient or ABA-insensitive mutants often show enhanced branching and reduced shade sensitivity, while exogenous ABA suppresses axillary bud outgrowth [9–11]. In addition, a BRC1/HD-ZIP I cascade further connects bud dormancy with ABA signaling in axillary buds [12].

In addition to ABA, GA signaling is also involved in branching and tillering control. Although ABA and gibberellins (GAs) are generally regarded as antagonistic hormones in many developmental processes, this antagonism may not fully apply to branching or tillering regulation. GA₃ has been reported to exert a stronger inhibitory effect on bud and tiller outgrowth in wheat than ABA [13], and in rice, GA-mediated degradation of DELLA/SLR1 destabilizes MOC1, thereby reducing tiller number while promoting stem elongation [14]. These findings suggest that ABA- and GA-related pathways jointly influence the balance between bud outgrowth and stem elongation under shading, highlighting the regulatory complexity of shade-responsive branching. However, the genetic basis and natural variation underlying shade-responsive branching in soybean remain largely unknown. Although several genes regulating soybean plant architecture and branching have been identified, including GmSPL9 [15], Dt2 [16], GmIAA27 [17], BRC1 [18], RAV [19], MAX [20], and MYB181 [21], most were characterized under normal light conditions. This limitation is particularly relevant in maize–soybean relay strip intercropping systems, where shading substantially alters plant architecture and yield formation. Integrating phenotypic evaluation with GWAS is therefore necessary to dissect the genetic architecture underlying soybean branching responses to shading in this system.

To address this gap, we systematically evaluated 11 branching-related traits across 202 soybean germplasm accessions from Southwest China during the 2024 growing season at two experimental sites using a randomized complete block design with two replicates. Stress tolerance index (STI) and relative distance plasticity index (RDPI) were calculated using trait values under relay strip intercropping (RI) and sole cropping (SS) to quantify each accession’s response to shading stress. In this framework, STI and RDPI were used as complementary indices, with STI describing relative stability of branching traits under RI and RDPI describing the magnitude of branching plasticity between SS and RI. We conducted genome-wide association studies (GWAS) using both the mixed linear model (MLM) and the three-variance-component mixed linear model (3VmrMLM), which enabled the simultaneous detection of quantitative trait nucleotides (QTNs) and QTN × environment interactions (QEIs) associated with branching shade responses. By integrating haplotype effect analysis and in silico protein structure prediction, we refined putative candidate genes and identified loci potentially involved in branching plasticity under shading. Collectively, this study provides novel insights into the genetic basis of soybean branching responses to shading stress and identifies putative candidate loci for breeding soybean ideotypes with enhanced shade adaptation and yield stability in maize–soybean relay strip intercropping systems.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

A total of 202 soybean accessions were used in this study. These accessions were collected from major soybean-producing regions in Southwest China, including Chongqing (CQ), Sichuan (SC), Guizhou (GZ), and Yunnan (YN), and included both released cultivars and local landraces. The panel was selected based on the availability of accession type, geographic origin, and matched whole-genome resequencing data for GWAS; accession details are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The maize cultivar used was Zhongyu 3 (developed by Nanchong Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Zoeve Seed Co., Ltd., and the Crop Research Institute of Sichuan Academy of Agricultural Sciences).

Field experiments

Field trials were conducted in 2024 at two locations: the Modern Agricultural Research and Development Base of Sichuan Agricultural University in Chongzhou (CZ) and the Modern Agricultural Industrial Park in Renshou (RS), Sichuan Province, China. The two sites differed in topography, soil fertility, and climatic conditions. The CZ site is located in a plain area, with topsoil pH of 7.27, organic matter content of 14.61 g/kg, total nitrogen of 1.86 g/kg, total phosphorus of 1.94 g/kg, and total potassium of 22.54 g/kg. In contrast, the RS site is located in a hilly area, with topsoil pH of 6.80, organic matter content of 13.12 g/kg, total nitrogen of 1.05 g/kg, total phosphorus of 1.52 g/kg, and total potassium of 23.96 g/kg. During the soybean growing season from June to November 2024, CZ had an approximate mean temperature of 22.9 °C and cumulative precipitation of 431.4 mm, whereas RS had an approximate mean temperature of 24.2 °C and cumulative precipitation of 252.6 mm. Field management practices were as follows. Maize received a one-time compound fertilizer application of 1,110 kg/ha (27% N, 8% P, and 5% K), incorporated at a depth of 5–10 cm before sowing. No fertilizer was applied to soybean. Supplemental drip irrigation was provided to both maize and soybean only when no effective rainfall occurred for several consecutive weeks after sowing. No artificial maize canopy management, such as leaf removal, topping, thinning, or early harvest, was conducted during the co-growth period; therefore, shading under RI resulted from the natural canopy structure of the relay strip intercropping system.

At both sites, two cropping systems—soybean monoculture (SS) and maize–soybean relay strip intercropping (RI)—were established, forming four environmental conditions that combined locations and cropping systems. In each environment, the 202 soybean accessions were planted in a randomized complete block design with two field replicates. For each accession, five representative plants were sampled and measured within each field replicate, resulting in ten plant-level observations per accession in each light-environment. Within each field replicate, five soybean plants with uniform growth were selected for trait measurement from the central soybean rows of each plot. Border rows were excluded to minimize edge effects, and obviously damaged or abnormal individuals were not sampled. Thus, five plants were measured per field replicate for each accession, resulting in ten plant-level observations per accession in each light-environment. Two-meter-wide strip plots were established, each consisting of two rows of maize and two rows of soybean. The width of maize and soybean rows was 0.4 m, with 0.6 m between the maize and soybean rows. Maize plants were spaced at 0.20 m within rows, and soybean plants at 0.08 m. In the monoculture system, soybean row spacing was 0.5 m and plant spacing was 0.16 m. Shading in the RI treatment was defined by the maize–soybean relay strip intercropping configuration and the co-growth period before maize harvest, during which soybean plants were shaded by the maize canopy.

Branch-related agronomic traits, including plant height (PH), branch number (BN), branching incidence (BI), total branch length (TBL), branch length (BL), and branch angle (BA), were measured before maize harvest, approximately 50–55 days after soybean sowing. In addition, composite indices such as mean stem node length (MSNL), reproductive branch node length (RBNL), the ratio of total branch length to plant height (RTBHL), and vertical projected area (VPA) were calculated. Shade-tolerance indices, including the STI and the RDPI, were also computed for branching traits using data from SS and RI conditions. All trait measurements followed the methods described by Qiu et al. [22], and the detailed calculation formulas for derived indices (e.g., RTBHL, STI, RDPI) are provided in the Supplementary Table S2. At physiological maturity, yield-related traits were further evaluated. Specifically, branch seed weight per plant (BSW, representing yield contribution from branches) and stem seed weight per plant (SSW, representing yield contribution from the main stem) were measured. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate relationships among branching traits, shade-response indices, and yield-related traits.

Resequencing data and SNP genotyping

Whole-genome resequencing data for the 202 accessions were obtained from our previous study (BioProject PRJNA1294229). In brief, sequencing libraries (~ 350 bp insert size) were prepared, and paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was conducted on the Illumina NovaSeq platform with an average depth of 10.16×.

Clean reads were aligned to the Williams 82 (Wm82.a4.v1, GCF_000004515.6) reference genome using BWA-MEM v0.7.17 with parameters mem -t 10 -k 32 -M. SNP calling was performed using SAMtools v1.3.1 and BCFtools v1.4 (samtools-1.3.1 mpileup -q 1 -C 50 -t SP -t DP -m 2 -F 0.002; bcftools-1.4 call -mv -f GQ). After quality control (dp4-miss0.2-maf0.05), a set of high-quality SNPs was retained for genome-wide association analysis.

Specifically, plant-level observations were first averaged for each accession, and these accession means were then used to calculate the population mean, variance, coefficient of variation (CV), Shannon diversity index, skewness, and kurtosis. Comprehensive evaluation of branching shade tolerance

To comprehensively evaluate the shade-response performance of branching traits in soybean, a multi-index analysis integrating principal component analysis (PCA), the affiliation function method (ASF), and grey relational analysis (GRA) was performed. The stress tolerance index (STI) and relative distance plasticity index (RDPI) of three representative early-stage branching traits—branch number (BN), branching incidence (BI), and total branch length (TBL)—were used as evaluation indicators. STI describes the relative stability of these branching traits under RI compared with SS during the maize–soybean co-growth phase. Higher STI values indicate greater branching stability, reflecting smaller changes in branching traits between SS and RI. In contrast, RDPI quantifies the magnitude of phenotypic plasticity of branching traits between the two cropping systems during the co-growth phase. Higher RDPI values indicate greater branching plasticity, reflecting larger changes in branching traits between SS and RI. However, higher RDPI values were not interpreted as universally adaptive; in this study, they were considered favorable only in the specific context of branching responses under RI-induced shading. Instead, RDPI values of BN, BI, and TBL were integrated with STI values using PCA, ASF, and GRA, followed by hierarchical clustering to define relative plasticity groups.

(1) PCA.

PCA was conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics v26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) using the standardized Z-scores of the three traits as input variables to reduce dimensionality and assign weights objectively. Two principal components with eigenvalues greater than 1 were extracted, together explaining 87.98% of the total variance.

Comprehensive evaluation

A hierarchical clustering was performed using the PCA score (F), the ASF score (D), and the grey relational degree (GRD). Hierarchical clustering was performed to summarize variation in shade-response patterns among accessions. Cluster assignment was determined with reference to the dendrogram structure and cluster size. After Z-score standardization, Euclidean distance and the Ward.D2 linkage were applied to compute the dissimilarity matrix. The clustering and visualization were implemented in R (v4.3.2) using the packages stats v4.5.2(for hclust and dist), data.table v1.17.8 (for data processing), ggtree v 3.16.3 (for dendrogram visualization), and showtext v0.9.7 (for font rendering). Materials were partitioned into k clusters (here k = 5), and the dendrogram was visualized in a circular layout with clade highlighting to distinguish groups of similar shade-tolerance profiles.

Linear regression-based mediation analysis

MB_index was calculated as a PCA-derived composite index of mature branching architecture under RI and was interpreted as an integrated descriptor of mature branch-system size and development. Mature-stage BN, BI, and TBL at R6 were first averaged across field replicates and locations, standardized to Z-scores, and subjected to PCA. The first principal component score was extracted as MB_index for each accession, explaining 66.38% of the total variation. Higher MB_index values indicate a larger and more developed mature branch system under RI conditions, reflecting the integrated contribution of branch number, branching incidence, and total branch length at the R6 stage. To evaluate whether early shade-response indices were associated with branch yield through mature branching architecture, a linear regression-based mediation analysis was performed following a standard a–b–c framework. In this analysis, X represented the early shade-response index (RDPI_index or STI_index), M represented mature branching architecture (MB_index), and Y represented branch seed weight under RI (BSW_RI). This directional mediation framework was biologically motivated by the temporal sequence of trait development under relay strip intercropping: early branching response was measured before light restoration during the maize–soybean co-growth phase, mature branching architecture was assessed at the R6 stage after canopy light was restored, and branch yield was evaluated at maturity under RI conditions. Genome-wide association study (GWAS)

GWAS was performed using two complementary methods: a single locus mixed linear model (MLM) [23] implemented in TASSEL v5.2, and a multi-locus multi-environment model (3VmrMLM) implemented in the R v4.3.2 package “IIIVmrMLM” (Windows version, IIIVmrMLM v1.0_R.zip) [24]. These two approaches were combined because they capture different but complementary aspects of the genetic architecture underlying branching responses under shading.

For the MLM analysis, the STI of branching-related traits was used as the input phenotype to identify loci associated with relative shade-response performance. Specifically, STI values for BN, BI, and TBL were derived from genotype-level adjusted phenotypic estimates under SS and RI conditions. To assess the potential influence of BLUP shrinkage on GWAS phenotypes, genotype BLUEs were additionally estimated for the component traits by treating genotype as a fixed effect and compared with adjusted BLUPs. Pearson correlations between BLUEs and adjusted BLUPs were 0.9995, 0.9983, and 0.9996 for BN, BI, and TBL under SS, respectively, and 0.9993, 0.9995, and 0.9982 under RI, respectively, indicating that genotype ranking and major phenotypic variation were largely preserved. Therefore, adjusted BLUP-derived STI phenotypes were retained for the MLM-GWAS, and the possible influence of shrinkage was considered when interpreting GWAS results. The kinship matrix (K) and population structure (PCA) were included as covariates to control false positives. Because MLM evaluates one marker at a time while accounting for population structure and kinship, it provides relatively conservative single-locus association signals, but may have limited sensitivity for loci with weaker or environment-specific effects.

In contrast, the 3VmrMLM approach directly incorporated multi-environment phenotypic values of BN, BI, and TBL under SS and RI, enabling the simultaneous detection of main-effect QTNs and QTN × environment interactions (QEIs). Thus, the two GWAS approaches were complementary: MLM focused on STI-based shade-response loci, whereas 3VmrMLM captured trait-specific genetic effects and environment-dependent associations across SS and RI conditions. Because 3VmrMLM jointly models multiple loci and multiple environments, it is particularly informative for identifying environment-responsive loci, although its higher sensitivity may also produce a larger number of association peaks that require cautious interpretation and additional biological filtering. After quality control, 2,681,756 high-quality SNPs were retained for GWAS. The genome-wide significance threshold for MLM was determined using a Bonferroni correction, corresponding to P = 1/2,681,756 = \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:3.7\times\:{10}^{-7}\left(-{\mathrm{log}}_{10}\left(P\right)\ge\:6.43\right)$$\end{document}. Because Bonferroni correction can be overly conservative for correlated SNP markers, an additional suggestive threshold of −log₁₀(P) ≥ 5 was applied only to BN_STI to retain putative loci for downstream comparative analysis. These loci were treated as exploratory signals rather than high-confidence associations and were interpreted with caution because of the increased risk of false positives. According to the recommendation of Li et al. [24], a LOD threshold of 3 was used in 3VmrMLM to identify significant QTNs and QEIs. To identify candidate genes, a linkage disequilibrium (LD) window of ± 100 kb around each significant QTNs or QEIs was applied based on the LD decay distance estimated from the population.

Haplotype analysis and protein structure prediction

Functional annotation and conserved-domain prediction of candidate genes were performed using InterPro, Pfam, SMART, and PANTHER online databases/resources. These databases were used to identify conserved domains, protein family classification, and putative functional annotations of proteins encoded by candidate genes. All databases were accessed in [accessed in August 2025].

For haplotype analysis, SNPs within the candidate gene region were extracted from the genome-wide dataset using PLINK v1.9 with chromosome and physical position parameters (--chr, --from-bp, --to-bp) and a minor allele frequency (MAF) threshold of ≥ 0.05. The extracted SNP data were converted into binary and VCF formats, and genotype phasing was conducted using Beagle v5.5 (17Dec24.224.jar). The phased VCF files were then converted into PLINK binary format using PLINK v2.0, where variant IDs were automatically assigned in the “chromosome: position” format. Subsequently, the dataset was transformed into PED and INFO files using PLINK for visualization in Haploview v4.2, which was employed to construct haplotype blocks and generate linkage disequilibrium (LD) maps [25]. Phenotypic differences among haplotypes were evaluated using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s HSD test, and haplotype effects were visualized as boxplots in OriginPro 2022 (OriginLab Corporation, Northampton, MA, USA).

Protein sequences corresponding to representative haplotypes were reconstructed from CDS sequences edited according to the identified SNPs and translated in silico using Biostrings v 2.76.0 (R v4.3.2). Structural models were predicted with the SWISS-MODEL server (accessed on September, 2025), generating PDB-format homology models for each haplotype (i.e., computational models rather than experimentally determined structures). Structural visualization and mutation site highlighting were performed in UCSF ChimeraX (v1.10.1) [26]. Attention was given to nonsynonymous substitutions located within conserved domains or secondary structural elements (e.g., α-helices, β-sheets).

Phenotypic traits including plant height (PH), stem node number (SNN), branch number (BN), branch length (BL), branch angle (BA), and several composite branch-related indices were measured before maize harvest (approximately 55 days after soybean sowing) (Table 1).

Statistical analysis for phenotypic traits variation of soybean

Substantial phenotypic variation was observed across both locations and environments. BN ranged from 0 to 13 and exhibited moderate heritability (H2 = 0.43 under SS and H2 = 0.30 under RI). Traits such as PH (H² = 0.74 and 0.55), MSNL (H² = 0.64 and 0.47), RTBHL (H² = 0.38 and 0.33), and BI (H² = 0.50 and 0.30) showed moderate to high heritability, indicating stable genetic control across environments. In contrast, SNN (H² = 0.06 under RI), BA (H² = 0.08 and 0.18), and VPA (H² = 0.09 and 0.19) displayed low heritability, suggesting a stronger influence of environmental variation. Collectively, these results demonstrate broad phenotypic and genetic diversity among branch-related traits, providing a solid foundation for subsequent association analyses.

Across the four environments, the distributions of most traits were approximately symmetric. BN and BI exhibited low to moderate skewness (|0.03|–|1.75|) and kurtosis (|0.01|–|4.43|), whereas PH, SNN, BA, MSNL, RBNL, and RTBHL showed mild right-skewness and moderate kurtosis. Trait distribution patterns were assessed using skewness and kurtosis. For the core branching traits used in GWAS, residual diagnostics based on Q–Q plots and residual-versus-fitted plots did not indicate severe violations of linear-model assumptions, although some deviations were observed as expected for count- or zero-inflated branching traits.

TBL in SS_CZ (skewness = 5.11, kurtosis = 34.06), BL in RI_RS (skewness = 2.56, kurtosis = 21.82), and VPA in RI_RS (skewness = 3.58, kurtosis = 15.35) showed strong deviations from normality, indicating the presence of extreme individuals. These deviations likely reflect genotype-by-environment interactions and heterogeneous branching responses under shading, underscoring the importance of incorporating environmental effects in subsequent statistical analyses to improve GWAS inference and the detection of environmentally responsive loci.

Early branching indices identify BN, BI, and TBL as primary contributors to yield variation under shading

Based on the best linear unbiased prediction (BLUP) values calculated across locations, we examined the relationships between branching traits and yield under different light environments. Compared with SS, RI significantly reduced total seed weight per plant by 21.90% and total seed number per plant by 20.37% (paired t-test, p = 1.95 × 10⁻²⁸ and p = 5.51 × 10⁻²¹, respectively), providing context for the plant-level yield penalty under relay strip intercropping. As shown in Fig. 1A and B, at approximately 55 days after soybean sowing, before maize harvest, branching traits exhibited significant stage-specific negative correlations with yield-related traits under both cropping systems (p ≤ 0.01), suggesting that assimilate allocation at this early co-growth stage may have been directed more toward vegetative growth than reproductive output. Further analysis revealed that branching traits were significantly and negatively correlated with seed weight per stem (SSW_SS, p ≤ 0.01) under monoculture and with branch seed weight (BSW_RI, p ≤ 0.01) under relay strip intercropping. These findings suggest that yield-related variation under monoculture was more closely associated with main stem productivity, whereas under relay strip intercropping, branch yield showed a stronger association with total yield performance.

To further clarify the roles of branching traits under shading stress, we analyzed the correlations between BSW_RI and STI, RDPI of individual branch traits (Fig. 1C and D). The STI values of BN, BI, and TBL were highly and negatively correlated with BSW_RI (p ≤ 0.01), whereas their RDPI values exhibited strong positive correlations (p ≤ 0.01). In contrast, both the STI and RDPI values of BA were significantly and negatively correlated with BSW_RI (p ≤ 0.01). Moreover, the STI values of PH, BL, MSNL, and RTBHL were also negatively correlated with BSW_RI (p ≤ 0.01), while their RDPI values showed no significant association. These results suggest that different branch-related traits were associated with branch yield to different degrees under shading stress, with BN, BI, and TBL showing the strongest and most consistent associations with BSW_RI. Therefore, the STI and RDPI values of these three traits were selected for the subsequent experiments and analyses.

Mediation analysis links early branching plasticity with mature architecture and branch yield under shading

To examine how early shading responses were associated with final branch yield, we first analyzed the relationship between mature branching structure and branch seed weight under RI conditions (Fig. 2A). The composite mature branching index (MB_index), derived from BN, BI, and TBL at the R6 stage, showed a strong positive correlation with BSW_RI (r = 0.67, p = 3.80 × 10⁻²⁷). Genotypes with higher MB_index tended to accumulate more seeds on branches, suggesting that mature branching architecture was strongly associated with reproductive performance under relay strip intercropping-induced shading. Thus, MB_index reflects not only a PCA-derived statistical score, but also the overall size and development of the mature branch system under RI conditions.

We first evaluated whether early branching plasticity (RDPI_index) during the maize–soybean co-growth phase was associated with final branch yield under shading (Fig. 2B). In the mediation framework, RDPI_index was specified as the predictor, MB_index as the mediator, and BSW_RI as the response. The a-path from RDPI_index to MB_index was significantly positive (a = 0.19 ± 0.06, p = 0.002), and the b-path from MB_index to BSW_RI was also strongly positive (b = 2.88 ± 0.24, p < 10⁻²⁴). The direct effect of RDPI_index on BSW_RI was positive but nonsignificant (c′ = 0.21 ± 0.20, p = 0.30), whereas the indirect effect through MB_index was large and statistically robust (a×b = 0.54 ± 0.15; 95% CI [0.25, 0.83]). The total effect remained significantly positive (c = 0.75 ± 0.26, p = 0.005), with approximately 70% of this effect statistically mediated through MB_index. These results suggest that the positive association between early branching plasticity and branch yield was statistically mediated largely through mature branch architecture.

Then, early shade tolerance (STI_index) was evaluated as an alternative predictor (Fig. 2B). In sharp contrast to RDPI_index, STI_index exhibited a significant negative phenotypic correlation with branch seed weight (Fig. 1C). Mediation analysis further clarified this relationship. The a-path from STI_index to MB_index was significantly negative (a = − 0.15 ± 0.06, p = 0.013). The b-path from MB_index to BSW_RI remained strongly positive (b = 2.81 ± 0.23, p < 10⁻²⁴), consistent with the RDPI_index model. Accordingly, STI_index showed a significantly negative indirect effect through MB_index (− 0.43 ± 0.16; 95% CI [− 0.75, − 0.11]). In addition, STI_index exerted a significant negative direct effect on branch yield (c′ = −0.63 ± 0.20, p = 0.002), producing a strongly negative total effect (c = − 1.06 ± 0.26, p < 10⁻⁴). Approximately 40% of this negative association was statistically mediated through MB_index, whereas the remaining 60% was not explained by mature branching architecture alone. Together, these two mediation patterns support a common associative framework: genotypes with stronger early plastic responses, reflected by higher RDPI_index and lower STI_index, tended to develop a larger mature branching system after canopy light was restored. In contrast, genotypes maintaining more stable early branching patterns, reflected by higher STI_index, tended to show more limited mature branching and lower branch seed yield under relay strip intercropping.

Therefore, the STI and RDPI values of these three traits were further integrated for subsequent PCA, ASF, and GRD analyses to assess overall shade-response performance across genotypes. Based on the combined evaluation of PCA, ASF, and GRD indices, the 202 soybean accessions were categorized into five classes: Highly Plastic (Group Ⅰ, n = 64), Plastic (Group Ⅱ, n = 39), Moderate (Group Ⅲ, n = 75), Stable (Group Ⅳ, n = 22), and Highly Stable (Group Ⅴ, n = 2) (Fig. 2C). Although average silhouette width suggested that a finer partition could also be considered, the six-cluster solution produced two singleton clusters, each containing only one accession. Therefore, the five-group classification was retained because it preserved the major clustering structure while providing a more stable and interpretable summary of plasticity patterns across accessions. Representative accessions from each group were used to visualize phenotypic variation (Figs. 2D–H). Accession C013 (Group Ⅰ) exhibited the largest variation in branch-related traits across environments but maintained the highest branch yield at maturity, showing minimal yield reduction under shading stress. In contrast, accession G011 (Group Ⅴ) displayed the opposite pattern, with both branching and branch yield severely suppressed by shading. The remaining groups showed intermediate performance, supporting the notion that accessions exhibiting greater early-stage branching plasticity between monoculture and intercropping conditions tended to show higher branch yields at maturity. Within the overall analytical framework, RDPI and STI were jointly used to characterize complementary dimensions of shade response at the phenotypic level. Notably, compared with RDPI_index, STI_index showed a larger absolute total effect and stronger statistical support in the mediation framework. Correspondingly, the GWAS analyses used STI-derived phenotypes in MLM and multi-environment raw trait values in 3VmrMLM to identify genetic loci associated with branching responses under RI conditions.

Multi-environment GWAS identifies stable and shade-responsive branching loci enriched on chromosomes 2 and 11

Before genome-wide association studies (GWAS), the SNP marker dataset and population structure of the 202-accession panel were summarized to support association analysis. After quality control using the dp4-miss0.2-maf0.05 filtering criteria, 2,681,756 high-quality SNPs were retained for GWAS. Among them, 2,633,211 SNPs were located on the 20 assembled soybean chromosomes and were used for chromosomal distribution and density visualization (Figure S1A). PCA and kinship analyses revealed population structure and genetic relatedness among the 202 accessions, supporting the inclusion of both population structure and kinship covariates in the MLM model (Figures S1B, C). When PCA was colored by accession type, local landraces and released cultivars showed some degree of differentiation but no complete subgroup separation, indicating that accession-type-related population structure was detectable but not sufficiently strong to form fully distinct clusters in this panel (Figure S1B). Therefore, both PCA covariates and kinship correction were included in the MLM framework to reduce the potential influence of population structure on association signals. To identify putative candidate genes, a ± 100 kb genomic window around each significant QTN or QEIs was used, because LD decay analysis in the 202-accession panel showed that genome-wide LD approached the r² inflection point at approximately 100 kb (Figure S1D). Q–Q plots were further generated to compare Q-only, K-only, and Q + K correction models for BN-, BI-, and TBL-based STI phenotypes (Figure S2). The Q-only model, implemented as GLM with PCA covariates, showed stronger upward deviation from the expected distribution, whereas models including the kinship matrix reduced p-value inflation. Therefore, the Q + K model was used for the final MLM-GWAS.

Building on the phenotypic characterization and shade-tolerance classification of branch-related traits, GWAS were conducted to identify genomic loci underlying branching variation under different light environments. The analysis was based on whole-genome resequencing data from 202 soybean accessions and multi-environment phenotypic datasets collected under SS and RI conditions in Chongzhou and Renshou. Two complementary statistical approaches were employed: the mixed linear model (MLM) and the three-variance-component mixed linear model (3VmrMLM). For the MLM analysis, TBL- and BI-based STI loci were identified using the Bonferroni-corrected threshold of –log₁₀(p) ≥ 6.43, yielding 33 and 3 significant QTNs, respectively (Figs. 3 and 4A and B; Table S3). For BN_STI, a suggestive threshold of –log₁₀(p) ≥ 5 was used to retain 21 putative QTNs for downstream comparison (Figs. 5A, B; Table S3). These loci were treated as putative signals for subsequent comparative analysis rather than as Bonferroni-level significant associations. In contrast, the 3VmrMLM method, which integrates phenotypic data across SS and RI environments, detected 23, 27, and 24 QTNs for TBL, BI, and BN, respectively, with a logarithm of odds (LOD) ≥ 3, and also identified 23, 18, and 19 significant QEI effects for these traits (Figs. 3, 4 and 5 C–D; Tables S4–S5). Because 3VmrMLM jointly models multi-environment phenotypes and detects both main-effect QTNs and QTN-by-environment interaction effects, it yielded more association peaks than the single-locus MLM. These peaks should not be interpreted as evidence that the entire soybean genome is broadly associated with the target traits; rather, they reflect a complex polygenic architecture together with local LD and environment-dependent genetic effects. Thus, 3VmrMLM is particularly informative for identifying loci with environmental responsiveness, whereas MLM provides more conservative single-locus evidence based on STI-derived phenotypes.

Under the MLM model, 28, 38, and 348 genes were located within LD regions associated with BN-, BI-, and TBL-based STI values, respectively, while the 3VmrMLM model identified 357, 299, and 251 genes linked to QTNs for these traits, together with an additional 179, 231, and 256 genes associated with QEI effects (Tables S6–S8). These represent genes located near significant QTNs rather than confirmed functional candidates. The relatively large numbers—particularly for TBL—likely reflect the combined effects of a 100 kb LD window and locally dense QTN distribution, and the greater sensitivity of the multi-environment multi-locus framework. To refine this gene set, LD-window genes were further filtered based on putative functional coding variants. Only genes containing exonic nonsynonymous substitutions or premature stop-codon variants were retained, yielding 883 putative candidate genes. Among these, 242, 243, and 398 genes corresponded to BI, BN, and TBL, respectively, with only 5, 10, and 9 genes shared between the MLM and 3VmrMLM models (Tables S9–S11). The limited overlap (< 5%) likely reflects a combination of methodological sensitivity, phenotype definition, and statistical power. Specifically, the two approaches differed in significance thresholds (− log₁₀(p) vs. LOD), phenotype sources (STI-derived traits vs. multi-environment trait values), and model structures (single-locus vs. multi-locus), all of which may affect detection sensitivity. In addition, loci with weaker or environment-specific effects may not be consistently detected across models, and the limited number of field replicates may also have reduced overlap between the two approaches. After integrating association consistency, predicted coding-variant effects, conserved-domain annotation, and biological relevance to branching, hormone signaling, meristem regulation, or plant architecture, three putative candidate genes were selected for subsequent haplotype analysis: LOC100802359 (Glyma.02G058600), encoding a probable protein phosphatase 2 C; LOC100806605 (Glyma.02G059900), encoding a DA1-like ubiquitin-interacting protein; and LOC100807143 (Glyma.02G060002), encoding a BEL1-like homeobox transcription factor. These genes were retained as putative candidates, but their biological functions in branching regulation and shade adaptation remain to be experimentally validated.

Glyma.02G058600 contained four major haplotypes (Hap1–Hap4) (Fig. 6C). Under shading stress, Hap4 exhibited significantly lower branch number (BN), branch incidence (BI), and total branch length (TBL) than Hap1 (p ≤ 0.001; Fig. 6D), whereas no significant differences were detected among haplotypes under monoculture conditions. This pattern indicates that the haplotype effects of Glyma.02G058600 are predominantly expressed under shading stress. The in silico predicted protein model suggested that residue 287 is located near the interface between a β-sheet and an α-helix (Fig. 6E). The E287D substitution between Hap1 and Hap4 may potentially affect local folding or hydrogen-bonding interactions due to the shorter side chain of aspartic acid. However, this inference is based on an in silico structural prediction, and its biological significance remains speculative pending experimental validation.

Glyma.02G059900 exhibited two predominant haplotypes (Hap1 and Hap2) (Fig. 7C). Significant differences in branching traits were observed between the two haplotypes under both monoculture and intercropping conditions (p ≤ 0.01; Fig. 7D). Among these traits, BN and BI showed stable haplotype-dependent effects, whereas TBL variation was more environment dependent, suggesting that Glyma.02G059900 may contribute to both shade adaptation and branching regulation. For Glyma.02G059900, the in silico predicted protein model identified two haplotype-specific substitutions, D27G and S159A (Fig. 7E). Because the structural differences were inferred from in silico prediction, their functional relevance remains uncertain and will require further experimental validation.

In contrast, although Glyma.02G060002 was located within a GWAS-associated region, it possessed three haplotypes (Hap1–Hap3) that showed no significant differences in BN, BI, or TBL under either monoculture or shading conditions (p > 0.05; Figure S3C, D). Thus, no haplotype-based phenotypic association was detected for this locus in the present analysis, and its role in branching regulation requires further validation. Collectively, the haplotype and structural analyses of Glyma.02G058600 and Glyma.02G059900 revealed environment-dependent allelic effects and potential functional variations that may influence protein stability and branching phenotypes under shading stress.

Discussion

Soybean is a vital oil, food, and feed crop that plays a central role in global food security and industrial production [27]. Maintaining its productivity is particularly important under the current pressures of declining arable land and environmental degradation. Maize–soybean relay strip intercropping has proven to be an efficient system, often achieving a land equivalent ratio (LER) greater than 1.4, thereby representing a sustainable approach to enhance soybean self-sufficiency [28]. Previous studies have demonstrated that branch number is positively correlated with yield; however, shading stress in relay strip intercropping systems can markedly suppress branch formation, with reductions of up to 26% reported during the seedling stage [29]. Our findings were consistent with this trend, though the reduction observed in this study was even greater, likely due to higher shading intensity in the present environments.

Correlation analyses further revealed distinct branching–yield associations under different cropping systems. Under monoculture (SS), branching traits were significantly associated with main stem yield (p ≤ 0.01), whereas under relay strip intercropping (RI), they were significantly associated with branch yield (p ≤ 0.01). This pattern is consistent with the well-established principle that “yield depends on the main stem under SS but on branches under RI” [30]. At approximately 55 days after sowing, before maize harvest, branching traits showed stage-specific negative correlations with yield-related traits (p ≤ 0.01), suggesting a transient allocation trade-off during the early co-growth stage. At this stage, branching traits mainly reflected vegetative architectural development rather than final reproductive capacity; therefore, this negative association should not be interpreted as a general negative effect of branching on final yield. Wu et al. [31] reported that maintaining soybeans in a vegetative state before maize harvest ultimately enhances per-plant yield because the extended growth period compensates for the early reduction in photosynthate accumulation caused by shading. Taken together, the reduction in branch-associated yield under RI likely reflects the combined effects of early assimilate limitation during the maize–soybean co-growth phase, developmental adjustment of branching under shading, and the extent of architectural compensation after light restoration, rather than a single causal mechanism [31, 32].

Importantly, STI and RDPI should not be interpreted as interchangeable indices in this study. Under RI-induced shading, STI primarily reflected the relative stability of early branching architecture between SS and RI, whereas RDPI reflected the magnitude of branching responsiveness to the altered light environment. In contrast to classic abiotic stresses such as drought [33], heat [34], or salinity [35]—where higher STI usually correlates positively with yield—we observed an opposite pattern under relay strip intercropping. In this system, higher STI did not necessarily indicate stronger recovery capacity after shading; instead, it reflected that early branching traits under RI remained more similar to those under SS during the maize–soybean co-growth phase. Accordingly, genotypes with higher STI may have maintained more stable early branching patterns but exhibited weaker post-shading architectural compensation, resulting in lower branch yield under RI. By comparison, BN-, BI-, and TBL-based RDPI values showed significant positive correlations with branch yield under RI, although the correlation coefficients were modest. This is consistent with the role of RDPI as an indicator of early-stage architectural responsiveness: genotypes with greater early plasticity during maize–soybean co-growth tended to develop more favorable mature branching architecture after canopy light was restored. Such post-shading architectural adjustment may be related to dry matter accumulation and yield compensation under relay intercropping systems [32]. Therefore, our interpretation of high RDPI as favorable and high STI as unfavorable should be considered specific to branching responses under RI-induced shading, rather than generalized to all stress conditions.

In the mediation analysis, approximately 70% of the positive association between RDPI and BSW_RI was statistically mediated by MB_index, whereas the remaining 30% was not explained by mature BN, BI, and TBL. This non-mediated component may be associated with additional branch-level or physiological factors not captured by MB_index, such as branch pod number, seed size, seed filling, photosynthetic recovery after maize harvest, or assimilate remobilization. These possibilities remain speculative and require further physiological validation. Therefore, the mediation framework used in this study should be understood as a biologically informed, temporally ordered association model rather than as direct evidence of definitive causal relationships among early branching response, mature branching architecture, and branch yield. From a breeding perspective, the lower broad-sense heritability of BN under RI compared with SS (H² = 0.30 vs. 0.43) further indicates that branching expression is more environmentally sensitive under shading conditions. This has important implications for breeding: selection based solely on monoculture performance may not reliably identify genotypes with favorable branching architecture under relay strip intercropping. Therefore, breeding programs targeting shade-adaptive soybean ideotypes should include direct selection under RI or simulated shading conditions and integrate early plasticity indices, mature branching traits, and yield-related performance to improve selection accuracy [30, 31]. Although the present design allowed comparative evaluation across locations and cropping systems, the limited number of field replicates may have reduced the precision of genotype × environment estimation and the sensitivity of GWAS for detecting weaker genetic effects. The relatively low overlap between loci identified by MLM and 3VmrMLM likely reflects methodological complementarity, environmental specificity, and limited statistical power for weaker loci, rather than inconsistency between the two GWAS frameworks. The relatively large number of association peaks detected by 3VmrMLM likely reflects the higher sensitivity of this multi-environment, multi-locus framework to loci with environment-dependent effects [24], together with the contribution of local LD among adjacent markers. Therefore, these peaks should not be interpreted as evidence that the entire soybean genome is broadly associated with branching traits under relay strip intercropping. Instead, they support a complex polygenic architecture, and downstream biological interpretation should focus on loci retained after overlap analysis and multi-step candidate-gene filtering.

Although several genes regulating soybean branching or plant architecture have been reported previously, including GmSPL9 [15], Dt2 [16], GmIAA27 [17], BRC1 [18], RAV [19], MAX [20], and MYB181 [21], the two prioritized chromosome 2 candidates identified here were not previously established as core branching regulators in soybean. Instead, Glyma.02G058600 and Glyma.02G059900 were prioritized as putative candidates based on overlapping GWAS support, haplotype effects, coding-variant annotation, and their possible functional links to hormone- and development-related processes relevant to shade-responsive branching. Glyma.02G058600, encoding protein phosphatase 2C55, belongs to the PP2C family, the largest class of protein phosphatases in plants [36]. Extensive studies have shown that PP2Cs interact with PYR/PYL/RCAR receptors and SnRK2 kinases to form a complex signaling network [37–39], functioning as key negative regulators of the abscisic acid (ABA) pathway in responses to drought [40, 41], saline–alkali [41, 42], and cold stresses [43]. In general, members of the PP2C-A subfamily primarily mediate ABA signal transduction [44, 45]. However, phylogenetic analysis by Fan et al. classified GmPP2C55 into the PP2C-D subfamily, and its promoter region contains a GA-responsive element but lacks ABA-responsive motifs [46]. These annotations suggest that its potential regulatory role may be more closely associated with GA-related responses than with canonical ABA signaling. In our study, the haplotype effects of Glyma.02G058600 were significant only under shading stress. Together with the observed phenotypic trends of reduced branching and increased plant height, this pattern is consistent with a possible association between this gene and GA-related elongation responses under shading [47]. GA is a well-established growth-promoting hormone that stimulates stem elongation and internode extension but is often associated with reduced branching [48]. Therefore, Glyma.02G058600 may represent a putative candidate linking shade-responsive branching variation with GA-related growth regulation; however, this proposed role is inferred from annotation, haplotype association, and previous literature and requires functional validation.

In addition, Glyma.02G059900 encodes a DA1-related protein. In Arabidopsis, the DA1 gene family encodes putative ubiquitin receptors that regulate final seed and organ size by restricting the duration of cell proliferation [49]. Li et al. further demonstrated that the CUC2/CUC3–DA1–UBP15 regulatory module suppresses axillary meristem initiation in both rosette and cauline leaves, thereby reducing branch formation [50]. Moreover, several studies have shown that DA1 expression is inducible by ABA [49, 51, 52], which is widely recognized as an inhibitor of axillary bud outgrowth [13]. In this study, Glyma.02G059900 exhibited significant haplotype effects under both monoculture and relay strip intercropping conditions, maintaining stable associations with BN and BI. This cross-environment consistency suggests that Glyma.02G059900 may be associated with basal branching variation across environments rather than being limited to shading-specific responses. Shading stress has been reported to alter endogenous ABA levels in a context-dependent manner [9, 53, 54], potentially modifying ABA-associated developmental responses. Collectively, these observations suggest that Glyma.02G059900 is a putative candidate that may connect developmental regulation of organ/meristem growth with ABA-associated signaling; however, this interpretation is based on gene annotation, haplotype association, and literature evidence and remains to be tested experimentally. Because transcriptomic or direct expression evidence was not available for these prioritized chromosome 2 candidates, their proposed roles should be regarded as inference-based and require further validation at both the expression and functional levels.

Together, these physiological, architectural, and genetic observations support a hypothetical conceptual framework for branching responses under relay strip intercropping (Fig. 8). During the maize–soybean co-growth phase, reduced light quantity and altered light quality may promote shade-avoidance responses, shifting the balance between branch initiation and internode elongation. Genotypes with higher branching plasticity, reflected by higher RDPI, tended to develop more favorable mature branching architecture after canopy light was restored, whereas genotypes with higher STI maintained more similar early architectures between SS and RI and tended to show lower branch yield under RI. From a hormone-related perspective, previous studies have reported that shading can affect GA signaling and ABA-related pathways, both of which are associated with stem elongation, bud outgrowth, and axillary meristem activity. In this context, Glyma.02G058600, encoding a PP2C protein, and Glyma.02G059900, encoding a DA1-like ubiquitin-interacting protein, were prioritized as putative candidate genes potentially relevant to branching responses under RI. Because direct hormonal measurements were not conducted in this study, the proposed links between the prioritized candidate genes and ABA- or GA-related pathways should be interpreted cautiously as inference-based associations rather than definitive mechanistic evidence. We propose a PP2C55–DA1-like–GA–ABA gene–hormone model as a hypothetical framework linking light environment, early branching plasticity, mature branching architecture, and branch yield. However, the functional roles of these candidate genes and their potential links to GA–ABA signaling require further experimental validation.

From a breeding perspective, the QTNs identified in this study provide a useful starting point for developing soybean ideotypes adapted to relay strip intercropping, particularly for integrating branching plasticity with yield stability. Loci detected by both MLM and 3VmrMLM should be prioritized for marker validation because they were supported across analytical frameworks, whereas 3VmrMLM-specific QTNs and QEIs may be valuable for identifying alleles with environment-responsive effects under RI. For practical selection, these markers should be combined with direct phenotypic evaluation under RI or simulated shading conditions, focusing on early plasticity indices, mature branching architecture, and branch yield to improve both adaptive plasticity and yield stability.

Conclusions

This study systematically evaluated branching-related traits across 202 soybean germplasm accessions grown under maize–soybean relay strip intercropping and identified distinct branching–yield associations between monoculture and relay strip intercropping systems. Shading stress reduced branch number more strongly than previously reported, underscoring the strong environmental influence on early soybean development. Correlation analyses showed that yield-related variation was more closely associated with main stem productivity under monoculture, whereas branch yield showed a stronger association with total yield performance under relay intercropping. Through genome-wide association and haplotype analyses, two high-confidence putative candidate genes—Glyma.02G058600, encoding PP2C55, and Glyma.02G059900, encoding a DA1-like protein—were prioritized as candidate genes associated with branching shade responses. Functional annotation and in silico prediction suggest that Glyma.02G058600 may be associated with GA-related stem elongation responses, whereas Glyma.02G059900 may be associated with ABA-related regulation of axillary meristem activity. Collectively, these findings provide new insights into the possible genetic and gene–hormone associations underlying soybean branching responses under shading stress. From a breeding perspective, the QTNs and putative candidate genes identified here provide a useful starting point for developing soybean ideotypes with improved shade adaptation and yield stability in maize–soybean relay strip intercropping systems, although further validation will be required before practical deployment.
