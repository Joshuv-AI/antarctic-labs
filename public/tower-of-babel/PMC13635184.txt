A web-based semi-supervised deep learning platform for automated AS-OCT assessment and monitoring of infectious keratitis
Authors: Yiming Sun, Peng Jin, Jingxuan Cai, Pengjie Chen, Nuliqiman Maimaiti, Yaqi Wang, Xianwen Liu, Zhichao Hu, Guiping Qian, Gangyong Jia, Peifang Xu, Juan Ye
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Infectious keratitis (IK) is a leading cause of corneal blindness globally. Accurate severity assessment is critical but remains limited by the subjective and limited nature of slit-lamp and anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) assessments. This study presents the ‘Intelligent Quantitative Keratitis Image Analysis Platform’, a web-based deep learning system for automated IK assessment through lesion segmentation, parameterization, stratification, and three-dimensional (3D) visualization based on AS-OCT data. A semi-supervised framework combining U-Net and Swin Transformer architectures was trained on 3130 AS-OCT images to address imaging artifacts, low contrast, scale variation, and irregular lesion boundaries. The model achieved Dice scores of 0.922 for corneal tissue and 0.834 for infiltrative lesions. Lesion characteristics were quantified within a polar coordinate system for radial stratification, yielding classification accuracies of 0.802 for infiltration thickness (IT) and 0.905 for infiltration width (IW). Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) between AI and ground truth were 0.961 (IT) and 0.960 (IW), numerically exceeding the inter-expert agreement (ICCs: 0.786–0.955). Multimodel visualization via 3D corneal reconstructions aligned with slit-lamp images enabled anatomically coherent monitoring of disease progression. Overall, the platform provides a non-invasive, objective, and efficient approach for IK evaluation and monitoring.

Introduction

Infectious keratitis (IK) is one of the leading causes of corneal blindness worldwide, accounting for ~5 million cases of bilateral blindness and 2 million cases of monocular vision loss each year, particularly in low- and middle-income countries1–4. Timely and accurate assessment of IK is essential for selecting appropriate treatment and improving clinical outcomes. However, current clinical evaluations face several limitations: Slit-lamp imaging provides only a two-dimensional, front-facing view of lesions, while anterior segment optical coherence tomography (AS-OCT) is capable of capturing cross-sectional corneal structure, is often underutilized, typically relying on only a few slices. This leads to incomplete and subjective assessments, difficulty in longitudinal comparison, and heavily dependent on clinician expertise5,6.

These challenges underscore the urgent need for more comprehensive, objective, and standardized tools to support IK management. Deep learning (DL), a branch of artificial intelligence (AI), has shown considerable promise in enhancing diagnostic accuracy, clinical decision-making, and workflow efficiency across various medical domains7–10. In IK, prior DL studies have primarily focused on improving diagnostic performance, reporting mean sensitivities and specificities of 86.2% and 96.3%, respectively, on external datasets2. However, automated tools for lesion assessment, monitoring, and stratification remain underdeveloped6.

AS-OCT provides high-resolution volumetric imaging of the anterior segment, including detailed corneal structures11,12. Its key advantage lies in the precise delineation of corneal ulceration, infiltrates and stromal haze, enabling objective quantification of lesion depth, extent and progression. This makes AS-OCT particularly valuable for characterizing and monitoring both superficial and deep-seated forms of IK13–15. When combined with DL algorithms, this modality has the potential to enable accurate, reproducible, and longitudinal evaluation of IK progression.

Segmentation serves as the foundation for downstream algorithmic analysis. U-Net remains the most widely adopted architecture for medical image segmentation, effectively combining multi-scale features from the encoder and decoder via skip connections16. Although several approaches have been proposed to address poor object-background contrast17, they often fail to achieve satisfactory segmentation performance for low-contrast or ill-defined targets. In AS-OCT images, noise artifacts and indistinct corneal structures further complicate accurate segmentation of the cornea and lesions. Moreover, the need for large-scale manual annotations presents a significant barrier to scalability. Semi-supervised segmentation offers a promising solution by leveraging self-training and consistency regularization, which have shown success in natural image tasks and are increasingly being adapted to medical imaging18,19. Therefore, a semi-supervised framework for lesion segmentation is well-suited to address these challenges and reduce annotation burdens.

In this study, we developed and validated a web-based platform, ‘Intelligent Quantitative Keratitis Image Analysis Platform’, which integrates deep learning-based lesion detection, quantitative parameter extraction, three-dimensional (3D) visualization, and automated stratification. By combining established approaches for the automated diagnosis of anterior segment diseases and etiological differentiation, this platform aims to support timely and objective evaluation of corneal pathology.

Results

Patient demographics and dataset construction

A total of 16,000 AS-OCT images were initially collected from 125 patients diagnosed with infectious keratitis between September 2018 and March 2024. After excluding 12,663 low-quality or irrelevant scans, 3130 images were included for model training and internal validation. The mean age was 58.4 ± 15.6 years (range: 3–92 years), and 69.4% of patients were male. An independent hold-out cohort comprising 207 AS-OCT images from 61 patients, distinct from the training cohort, was used to evaluate model generalizability. For the left edge, errors were −0.005 ±± 0.342 mm and −0.009 ±± 0.178 mm; for the right edge, −0.147 ±± 0.281 mm and −0.007 ±± 0.193 mm, demonstrating accurate lesion localization.

Linear correlation analysis (Fig. 2c) showed strong agreement between the AI system and ground truth (R2 = 0.874 for IT and 0.905 for IW). In comparison, manual measurements by three ophthalmologists (Groups A-C) yielded lower agreement for IT (R2 = 0.437–0.705) and comparable but slightly lower agreement for IW (R2 = 0.877–0.882), indicating reduced interobserver variability with AI-based quantification of lesion parameters.

Reproducibility analysis using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) on 207 test images demonstrated high consistency. For IT, the ICC between AI (Group D) and ground truth (Group G) was 0.961, numerically exceeding the ICC of ophthalmologists (0.868, 0.786, and 0.837). For IW, ICCs were 0.960 (AI), and 0.955, 0.947, and 0.929 for ophthalmologists, indicating comparable or superior consistency of the AI system.

Stratification performance based on IT and IW is shown in Fig. 2d. Agreement between the AI system was higher than that of ophthalmologists for both IT-based stratification (Degree of infiltration thickness, DIT; 0.802 vs. 0.623, 0.429, and 0.444) and IW-based stratification (Degree of infiltration width, DIW; 0.905 vs. 0.873, 0.687, and 0.802), highlighting reduced observer variability and improved robustness of AI-based stratification.

Clinical web-based deep learning platform deployment

We developed an integrated ‘Intelligent Quantitative Keratitis Image Analysis Platform’ (Supplementary Fig. 3) incorporating AI-based modules. The platform enables access to patient records, including visit history, clinical findings, treatments, and multimodal imaging (e.g., AS-OCT, slit-lamp, confocal microscopy; Supplementary Fig. 3a, b), supporting longitudinal follow-up.

Uploaded AS-OCT images are automatically processed by deep learning modules (Supplementary Fig. 3c), generating quantitative metrics such as infiltration thickness, corneal thickness, and lesion area, and providing stratification based on infiltration depth. This enables standardized and efficient assessment of IK.

In addition, previously developed modules20,21 were integrated to enhance clinical utility including models for anterior segment diseases diagnosis (Supplementary Fig. 4a) and etiological classification of IK using multimodal data (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Together, these components support AI-assisted evaluation of IK in real-world clinical settings.

Discussion

We developed the ‘Intelligent Quantitative Keratitis Image Analysis Platform’, an AI-powered system for comprehensive and quantitative assessment of corneal infiltration using AS-OCT. The platform demonstrated robust performance in lesion detecting and severity stratification (mild, moderate, severe), and enables 3D reconstruction of affected eyes. When integrated with slit-lamp images, this provides a multimodal and anatomically aligned view of disease extent, which may facilitate clinical interpretation and monitoring.

Accurate assessment of IK severity is essential for clinical decision-making, including treatment intensity and use of systemic therapy22–24. Traditionally, severity evaluation relies on slit-lamp biomicroscopy. However, AS-OCT is increasingly favored for its high-resolution cross-sectional imaging, allowing more precise assessment of stromal infiltration, ulcer depth and corneal structure. Prior studies have demonstrated its utility in differentiating inflammatory and infectious conditions and in identifying morphology suggestive of specific pathogens, particularly when slit-lamp visualization is limited25–28. Compared with previous approaches that rely on selected AS-OCT slices or focus primarily on classification, our platform analyzes volumetric AS-OCT data and aligns it with co-registered slit-lamp images, enabling more comprehensive and spatially consistent evaluation.

Reliable segmentation and quantification are fundamental to intelligent IK assessment. In routine practice, measurements are often based on a limited number of manually selected AS-OCT slices, which is subjective and may not fully capture the lesion and spatial heterogeneity. In contrast, our approach enables automated, whole-volume analysis, reducing observer variability and providing more comprehensive characterization of lesion geometry and distribution. To address known challenges in AS-OCT lesion segmentation, such as imaging artifacts, low contrast, irregular boundaries, and class imbalance29–31, we developed a semi-supervised framework that allows effective training with limited manual annotation. This design achieved strong segmentation performance and supports scalable application in clinical settings.

To improve clinical applicability, we further developed an automated stratification module grounded in quantitative parameters and existing clinical evidence. The strategy reflects clinical understanding: superficial corneal involvement (upper one-third) typically corresponds to milder inflammation and potentially reversible disease, whereas deeper stromal infiltration (lower two-thirds) is associated with a higher risk of scarring and visual loss. Larger lesions (exceeding 6 mm) and those involving the pupillary zone are also indicative of more severe disease. However, the lack of universally accepted objective grading standards remains a limitation, and further large-scale studies are needed to establish standardized criteria.

Existing clinical evidence supports the relevance of this stratification approach to disease severity and outcomes. Anatomic features measurable by AS-OCT have been shown to correlate strongly with presenting visual acuity (VA), with lesion size, depth, and location consistently associated with worse VA in a cohort of 667 patients32. Among these, stromal infiltrate depth appears particularly informative. Posterior stromal involvement was independently associated with poorer VA (PR 1.22, 95%% CI 1.09–1.36, p << 0.001)32, consistent with evidence that deeper infections reflect greater tissue disruption. In the Steroids for Corneal Ulcers Trial (SCUT), deeper infiltrates were associated with greater visual improvement following adjunctive corticosteroid therapy, whereas infiltrate diameter was not predictive of treatment response22. Long-term studies further suggest that initial infection depth may influence irregular astigmatism and subsequent visual impairment33. These findings indicate that stromal depth may reflect both baseline severity and aspects of healing trajectory, supporting its use in risk stratification.

Central infiltrate location was also independently associated with poor presenting VA (PR 1.26, 95%% CI 1.14–1.38, p << 0.001)32, likely due to involvement of the visual axis. This is consistent with SCUT findings demonstrating greater benefit of adjunctive corticosteroids in centrally located ulcers, supporting the inclusion of lesion location in severity stratification frameworks22.

In contrast, the role of infiltrate diameter appears less consistent. Although larger epithelial defects were associated with worse VA, stromal infiltrate diameter showed weaker associations after adjustment32. While larger lesions have been linked to more severe disease and higher rates of surgical intervention, only some studies have demonstrated associations with clinical endpoints22,34. AS-OCT studies suggest that reductions during treatment are more pronounced in stromal thickness than in en face diameter35, indicating that infiltrate depth may better capture dynamic changes in disease activity and response than surface dimensions alone.

Emerging evidence also suggests that AS-OCT parameters may capture pathogen-related characteristics and early treatment response. For example, significant reductions in infiltrate thickness and corneal edema, as well as decreases in central corneal thickness, have been observed during healing of viral keratitis (p << 0.001)36. Notably, reductions in infiltrate thickness and back-shadow intensity may precede overt clinical improvement, suggesting a potential role for AS-OCT in early detection of treatment response.

Taken together, these findings support a stratification approach that prioritizes stromal depth, central involvement, and selected size parameters. The consistent associations between AS-OCT–derived features and presenting VA, along with their ability to reflect dynamic changes, provide a quantitative basis for integration into clinical workflows. While further validation is required, this approach may support more standardized assessment and individualized management.

This study has several strengths. It leverages deep learning for automated lesion detection, quantification, and stratification, enabling early detection of subtle features and facilitating longitudinal comparisons, also enabling ophthalmologists to directly assess the lesion’s position relative to the pupil and determine the extent of visual axis involvement. Second, the incorporation of 3D reconstruction allows cross-modal visualization, aiding clinicians in correlating slit-lamp and OCT findings. Finally, the web-based platform supports long-term data management and follow-up, streamlining the clinical workflow.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this is a single-center study, and model performance may be affected by variability across AS-OCT devices, acquisition protocols, and patient populations. Differences in image characteristics and operator technique may introduce variability. External validation in multicenter cohorts is therefore necessary. Second, the dataset excludes post-surgical cases and lacks longitudinal follow-up, limiting evaluation of long-term outcomes. Accordingly, the system should not yet be used for treatment decision-making or prognostic prediction. Future work will focus on larger, multicenter, and longitudinal datasets to further validate and refine the model.

In summary, this study demonstrates that AI-based analysis of AS-OCT can provide accurate, objective, and reproducible quantification of corneal lesions in IK. The proposed platform integrates lesion segmentation, stratification, 3D visualization, and multimodal alignment, offering a clinically interpretable tool for IK assessment and monitoring.

Methods

Patient selection and image acquisition

This study included patients clinically diagnosed with infectious keratitis between September 2018 and March 2024 at the Eye Center of the Second Affiliated Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University. Diagnosis was confirmed by ocular surface specialists based on clinical evaluation. Patients with a history of corneal surgery or other ocular conditions affecting corneal lesion were excluded. Only one eye per patient was included.

AS-OCT scans were acquired using a swept-source CASIA SS-1000 (Tomey Corporation, Nagoya, Japan). Each scan was centered on the corneal apex and consisted of 128 radial cross-sectional images acquired over a 360-degree sweep and exported in BMP format. Images included in model development and validation were required to have minimal artifacts and clearly visible corneal lesions.

For the independent hold-out cohort, when multiple slices were obtained from the same eye, images were selected by at least 10°. Representative eyes used for validating intelligent stratification and 3D visualization were not included in the training or testing datasets and consisted of the full set of 128 slices per eye, including slices without visible lesions.

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital, School of Medicine, Zhejiang University (approval ID: IR2021001176), and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All data were collected during routine clinical care and fully de-identified prior to analysis. As the dataset contained no direct patient identifiers, the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, granted a waiver of informed consent.

Image quality evaluation and ground truth annotation

Based on our prior work37, three common imaging artifacts were assessed: (1) Central artifact: Hyper or hypo-reflective regions caused by the central beam; (2) Eyelid occlusion: Coverage or distortion of the anterior chamber angle by the eyelid; (3) Blur: Motion-induced artifacts.

Ground truth annotations were performed as previously described37. Corneal and lesion boundaries were manually delineated on each image using Labelme38 by three general ophthalmologists, requiring approximately five minutes per slice. All annotations were subsequently reviewed and refined by an ocular surface expert to ensure accuracy and consistency. Prior to annotations, all annotators were trained and standardized according to predefined criteria: (1) Lesion: Hyper-reflective regions corresponding to clinical infiltration. (2) Cornea: Cornea and anterior sclera visible on AS-OCT images without deformation caused by imaging artifacts.

Development and validation of the deep learning detection module

To enable automatic detection and segmentation of corneal lesions in AS-OCT images, we developed a deep learning detection module (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Overview of the study workflow.a Detection module: The development dataset was randomly divided into training and internal validation sets at a 7:3 ratio. The training set included 20% labeled and 80% unlabeled images for model development. b Parametric analysis, stratification, and 3D visualization modules.

a Detection module: The development dataset was randomly divided into training and internal validation sets at a 7:3 ratio. The training set included 20% labeled and 80% unlabeled images for model development. b Parametric analysis, stratification, and 3D visualization modules.

The module employed a semi-supervised learning framework combining U-Net39 and Swin Transformer40 architecture with a cross-teaching paradigm, enabling extraction of both local and global features. The dataset was split into training and internal validation sets at a 7:3 ratio. Among 2191 training images, 438 (20%%) were labeled and 1753 (80%%) were unlabeled. All images were preprocessed by cropping, resizing into 448 ×× 448 pixels, and normalization. Data augmentation, including random flipping and rotation, was applied to enhance variability.

For labeled data, both networks generated segmentation outputs that were supervised using the Dice loss function. For unlabeled data, probability maps from both networks were binarized at a threshold of 0.5. Dropout was applied to perform multiple stochastic forward passes, enabling uncertainty estimation via information entropy. These uncertainty maps were used to refine pseudo-labels, which were then used to supervise the counterpart network with uncertainty-weighted loss. This cross-teaching strategy provided implicit consistency regularization and leveraged complementary strengths of convolutional (local detail) and transformer-based (global context) representations.

Model performance was evaluated after each iteration on the validation set using the Dice coefficient, and model weights were saved upon improvement. Training was conducted for 5200 epochs with a batch size of 8, using stochastic gradient descent (SGD) with a weight decay of 0.0001 and momentum of 0.9. All experiments were implemented in Python 3.8 with PyTorch 1.9.0.

Segmentation performance was assessed using: (1) Direct metrics: Dice coefficient, accuracy, precision and sensitivity; (2) Indirect metrics: Relative absolute error (RAE), and intersection-over-union for angle-based evaluations (IoU-angles). A sensitivity analysis restricted to 10 representative images with clear and typical infiltrates was conducted to confirm that results were not driven by slice selection.

Accuracy was evaluated using 168 test images with Dice >> 0.8 through: (1) Bland–Altman analysis of IT and IW (slice- and patient-level); (2) X/Y coordinate error of key points; (3) linear correlation (R2) with ground truth.

Patient-level Mann–Whitney U tests were used to assess the effects of sex and eye laterality, while Spearman correlation analysis evaluated the effect of age on measurement accuracy.

Reproducibility was assessed using intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) across multiple observers: Group G (ground truth), Group D (AI system), and three ophthalmologists with varying levels of experience (Groups A–C: 5, 1, 3 years of experience respectively).

Intelligent stratification and 3D visualization

Based on segmentation and parametric analysis, the system performed automated lesion stratification and 3D visualization.

Stratification criteria included5,41–43: (1) Mild: IT << 1/3; (2) Moderate: 1/3 ≤≤ IT << 2/3; (3) Severe: IT >> 2/3 or IW >> 6 mm.
