Petale® behavioral auditory test for infants aged nine months to three years: A diagnostic accuracy pilot study
Authors: Bruno Maetz, Pierre Fayoux, Christophe Vincent, Philippine Toulemonde, Alexandre Caze
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesScreening for hearing impairments is part of the mandatory medical visits for infants at nine months and two years in France, however adherence to guidelines is low, diagnostic methods are varied and accuracy is undocumented. The aim of this research was to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of the Petale® behavioral auditory test for infants aged nine months to three years who were screened for hearing impairments by a general practitioner (GP) or pediatrician.MethodsIn this prospective diagnostic accuracy study, infants in France aged nine months to three years were tested with Petale® (index test) during a GP or pediatrician consultation and a specialist hearing assessment with a conditioned orientation reflex (COR) test carried out by an Ear-Nose-Throat specialist (reference test).ResultsOverall, 157 infants were included in the analysis population and 119 had both an index test and a reference test. Median age was 1.5 years (IQR 1.0;2.0). A hearing impairment was identified with Petale® for 58 (36.9%) infants. Sensitivity was 66.67% (95%CI 44.89–88.44), specificity was 71.29% (62.46–80.11), PPV was 29.27% (15.34–43.20), NPV 92.31% (86.39–98.22) and accuracy 70.59% (62.40-78.77). The Kappa index for concordance was 0.249 (95%CI 0.079–0.418) indicating a weak concordance between the two diagnostic tests. Overall, 98.7% of practitioners and 99.1% of parents were satisfied or very satisfied with Petale®.ConclusionsThe diagnostic accuracy of Petale® is unsatisfactory. Nevertheless, parents’ and practitioners’ satisfaction with the test is high and the test was shown to be an easy, quick-to-use, cost-effective solution for this population where data on proven audiometry testing protocols are lacking.

Introduction

The number of children with permanent hearing loss in France is estimated at 1/1000 at birth and 3/1000 at three years [1]. A large number of hearing impairments in children are not detected during newborn audiometry screening as they don’t develop until later [2, 3], or they are not identified [4]. Estimated rates of children with permanent hearing loss not picked up by newborn screening range from 0.32 to 77.87 per 1000 children [5]. Relatedly, less than 60% of children with moderate or worse hearing loss at kindergarten age had been identified during newborn hearing screening [6]. Early detection of auditory function is essential as hearing impairments can result in language delays and academic difficulties when the child starts school [7], as well as impact quality of life [8].

In France, auditory screening is part of the mandatory medical certificates delivered during the infant’s ninth and 24th months, required according to French laws. However, a study in the North of France showed that only 15.9% of general practitioners systematically use auditory screening and 50% never carry out screening [9]. In addition, the type of test utilized is left to the practitioner’s discretion and the methods used are often subjective, such as clicking fingers or crinkling paper [10]. One study reports the most common test to be the whisper test, used by 68.3% of GPs, and the Sensory Baby Test, used by 26.8% [9]. While the whisper test is simple and cost-effective to implement, as a subjective behavioral test requiring a certain level of language comprehension, it is not adapted for use with young infants and there are concerns over the lower sensitivity in children and the overall reproducibility of the test, particularly in primary care settings [11]. Specialist testing generally utilizes Conditioned Orientation Reflex (COR) Audiometry, similar to Visual reinforcement audiometry (VRA) but using multiple sound sources and requiring specific equipment and specialist training to undertake.

In this context, the Petale® (Petit Équipement de Test Auditif de l’Enfant) equipment and test protocol were developed specifically for infants and very young children, focusing on the use of orientation reflexes to detect auditory responses to familiar sounds and aiming to be easy to use. The Petale® test relies on non-conditioned orientation reflexes where the practitioner observes natural orientation behaviors, such as head turn or eye movement, in response to auditory stimuli. It is thus suitable for younger children, from nine months to four years old, bridging a critical gap where standard behavioral tests may be less reliable and physiological tests less informative about functional hearing. However, as yet, no robust empirical diagnostic accuracy data has been published for the Petale® test. The aim of this research is to evaluate the diagnostic accuracy of the Petale® auditory test for infants aged 9 months to 3 years who received auditory screening from a general practitioner (GP) or pediatrician. The reference standard test utilized was the COR test carried out by an Ear-Nose-Throat (ENT) specialist. We also evaluate satisfaction from pediatricians and parents with the Petale® test.

Methods

Study design and setting/participants

In this prospective diagnostic accuracy study, we included infants who received auditory testing during a GP or pediatrician visit at any of the ten participating centers between January 2020 and December 2022. Inclusion criteria were: age at testing between 9 months and 3 years, receiving audiometry testing with the Petale® system during a GP or pediatrician consultation (irrespective of the reason for consultation), and receiving a COR hearing test with an ENT specialist. Exclusion criteria were known hearing loss, or presence of a condition that prevented the child from participating in the test (ear infection, disability).

Auditory testing

Index test: Petale®

GPs and pediatricians carried out the Petale® test following standardized protocols. The infant was seated on the lap of their parent or guardian facing the GP and speakers were placed at approximately 40 cm on either side of the infant. Four noises were presented in random order (dog barking, baby crying, music and bird chirping) at four different volumes (30, 40, 50 and 60dB SPL). The remote-controlled sounds were played once for each ear and the GP or pediatrician observed the infant’s response categorizing it as ‘no opinion, unsatisfactory, uncertain, or satisfactory’. Both ears were tested. The diagnosis of a hearing impairment was based on the results at 30 dB SPL, per ear, using the following primary decision rules: For each ear the most negative test result was retained. If the test result was different between the two ears, the most negative result was retained at the patient level. A hearing impairment was defined as at least one “Doubtful” or “Unsatisfactory” result. Tests with “No opinion” were considered as missing.

Two secondary exploratory diagnostic thresholds were tested. Exploratory threshold for hearing impairment 1: the test was considered positive (hearing impairment) if, for at least one of the sounds, for at least one of the ears, the infant’s reaction was “unsatisfactory”. Exploratory threshold for hearing impairment 2: test was considered positive (hearing impairment) if, for at least two of the sounds, for at least one of the ears, the infant’s reaction was “doubtful” or “unsatisfactory”.

Reference standard: Conditioned orientation reflex (COR)

Using a COR test (also known as visual reinforcement audiometry) carried out within one month of the Petale® test, a hearing impairment was diagnosed by ENT specialists when a positive result in at least one of the ears was present at ≥ 30dB. The average tonal threshold was calculated as the average of the thresholds obtained at 250 Hz, 500 Hz, 1000 Hz et 4000 Hz. ENT specialists were blinded to the Petale® results.

Statistical methods

Quantitative variables were described by frequencies, mean, standard deviation, median, extreme values (minimum and maximum) and the number of missing data. Diagnostic accuracy was assessed by sensitivity (% of positive tests among infants with hearing impairments), specificity (% of negative tests among infants without hearing impairments), positive predictive value (PPV, probability of correctly classifying when the test is positive), negative predictive value (NPV, probability of correctly classifying when the test is negative) and accuracy. Agreement between the two tests (Petale® and COR) was estimated using the Kappa coefficient and its 95% confidence interval (CI). The Kappa coefficient can be interpreted as follows: ≤0: No agreement; 0.01–0.20: No or poor agreement; 0.21–0.4: Poor agreement; 0.41–0.6: Moderate agreement; 0.61–0.80: Strong agreement and 0.81–1.00: Almost perfect agreement. All comparisons were made at the statistical significance level set at p < 0.05. All calculations were made with SAS for Windows (v 9.4; SAS Institute Inc).

Satisfaction

To evaluate the acceptability of the Petale® test, we asked practitioners to rate on a 4-point Likert scale (from very difficult to very easy): material set-up, software use (tablet), results production, overall use, test duration (very long to very quick), integration of the test in the consultation, satisfaction with Petale® (not at all satisfied to very satisfied) and whether they would continue to use the test (Yes/No). We also asked parents for their level of satisfaction with the Petale® test (not at all satisfied to very satisfied). The results were described by frequencies.

Ethics statement

This study received approval Comité de Protection des Personnes Ile de France XI (CPP) 25/02/2020 Reference: CPP : 20,013–59,216. Informed consent for minors/parental. Parents were present with the infants for all aspects of testing. The STARD guidelines [12] for reporting on diagnostic accuracy tests were followed for the drafting of this manuscript.

Results

Inclusions

In total, 165 infants were included in the study. Eight exclusions were made for lack of parental consent, ineligibility based on age and one infant with no recorded data (Fig. 1). From the analysis population of 157 infants, 38 did not receive the COR reference test, leaving 119 infants with both an index test and a reference test.

Baseline participant characteristics

In the analysis population, the median age of the infants was 1.5 years (IQR 1.0;2.0); 27 (17.2%) were under one year old, 60 (38.2%) were between 1 and 2 years old and 70 (44.6%) were two or over. Seventy-eight (49.7%) were female.

Index test and clinical examination

In the analysis population, most Petale® assessments were undertaken during consultation with a GP (90, 57.3%) and the remainder were taken by a pediatrician (67, 42.7%). The tests took on average (SD 1.8) minutes to set up and the average overall test duration was 8.3 (SD 6.4) minutes.

Overall, using the Petale® test, 16 (10.2%) had a non-satisfactory result, 42 (26.8%) had a doubtful result and 99 (63.1%) had a satisfactory result. Using the primary pre-established threshold for hearing impairment, a hearing impairment was identified for 58 (36.9%) infants (Table 2). Using the exploratory threshold 1, where an impairment was declared if the result was non satisfactory only, a hearing impairment was identified for 32 (20.4%) infants. Finally, using the exploratory hypothesis 2, where a hearing impairment was declared if the result was non satisfactory or doubtful for at least two sounds sound, 27 (17.2%) had a hearing impairment.

Hearing impairment as identified on the reference standard

Of the 119 infants who received a COR reference test, 18 had a hearing impairment and 101 did not have a hearing impairment. The median time between the two tests was 19.0 (IQR 10–28) days.

Comparison of the index/reference results (Table 1)

Crosstabulations of the results of the Petale® test and the COR reference standard are presented in Table 1 for the population with both index and reference tests. There were 12 (10.1%) true positives, 72 (60.5%) true negatives, 29 false positives (24.4%) and 6 (5.0%) false negatives. Sensitivity was 66.67% (95%CI 44.89–88.44), specificity was 71.29% (62.46–80.11), PPV was 29.27% (15.34–43.20), NPV 92.31% (86.39–98.22) and accuracy 70.59% (62.40–78.77). The Kappa index for concordance was 0.249 (95%CI 0.079–0.418) indicating a weak concordance between the two diagnostic tests.

Satisfaction

Overall, 153 (98.7%) of practitioners were satisfied or very satisfied with Petale®. The majority (154, 99.4%) said they would continue to use the test after the study (Table 3). Most parents (107, 88.4%) were very satisfied with the Petale® test for auditory screening of their infant, 13 (10.7%) were satisfied, and one parent was not satisfied at all.

Discussion

This is the first study to assess the diagnostic accuracy of Petale®, and indeed, to investigate the diagnostic accuracy of a non-conditioned subjective hearing test for use in children of this age group. We compared the outcomes of this behavioral orientation test carried out by GPs and pediatricians, with a conditioned orientation reflex test carried out by ENT specialist for infants aged 9 months to 4 years in France. We observed only modest diagnostic accuracy of the Petale® auditory test. With the original threshold for hearing impairment (one doubtful or unsatisfactory response in at least one ear, i.e. unilateral or bilateral), sensitivity was low at 66.67% and specificity was 71.29%. Utilizing Petale® with the same testing procedure and applying the same diagnostic criteria, a previous study found much higher sensitivity at 94.1% (95%CI 82.9–100) with low specificity at 38.8% (95%CI 25.1%−52.4%) [13]. In addition, when the diagnostic criteria were set to ‘at least two’ doubtful or unsatisfactory responses, sensitivity was still high at 94.1% (82.9%−100%), and specificity increased to 57.1% (95%CI 43.3% − 71%), however in our study, using the same thresholds did not produce the same level of diagnostic accuracy and our sensitivity and specificity values were much lower at 50.0% (95%CI 26.9–73.1) and 88.12% (81.81–94.43), respectively. Petale® has been widely used in France over several decades, although this is the first diagnostic evaluation. The Local Actions for Prevention and Health Education (APESAL) carried out auditory testing of 27,367 children over the 2016/17 school year. They identified 1,689 children with a negative Petale® test, of whom 1,440 carried out secondary confirmatory testing with an ENT specialist. In this large population based study, the sensitivity of Petale is reported as 879/1440, 61.4% however specificity was not calculated [14].

This difference may be explained by the difference in practitioners. In the present study, Petale® was administered by GPs or pediatricians, whereas in the previous study, Petale® was carried out by ENT specialists who are formally trained to detect hearing anomalies. This may indicate that GPs and pediatricians require more specific training to correctly use the material to screen for hearing impairments. The low sensitivity of Petale compared to COR results may also be explained by the high incidence of transient hearing loss in this age group. Potentially, the month between the two tests could lead to the patient being assessed in different clinical situations.

The development of Petale® was based on the principles of Ewing and Ewing [15]’s distraction test, which was the first to propose the presentation of frequency-specific sounds and observation of the infant’s natural orienting responses (e.g., head turns) to assess hearing sensitivity rather than absolute thresholds. The sounds selected for Petale® were based on the late Professor Vaneecloo’s (unpublished) work to identify the most ‘reactionary’ sounds from forty familiar sounds and the Petale® test has now been in use for over a decade in France.

Intended as an auditory screening test, the primary goal of Petale® in this context would be for high sensitivity (that is, a low number of false negatives) so the likelihood of missing a child with a potential hearing impairment is minimized. Minimum acceptable sensitivity values are generally from 80%, and the rate observed here thus falls short. Pre-planned exploratory analyses using two different thresholds to evaluate whether a stricter criterion of an unsatisfactory of doubtful response on at least two sounds, or only unsatisfactory results proffered higher diagnostic accuracy gave negative results with even lower sensitivity at 38.89% and 50.0% respectively.

However, satisfaction results were high and feedback from practitioners shows that they would continue to use the Petale® test; finding it easy to install, quick to use and considering it to be reliable. Currently, there are no alternative tests available with better proven efficacy. The most commonly used tests, such as the whisper test, do not have proven efficacy. An alternative test, the Moatti box test where the operator presents four sounds to the infant and observes their response provides a subjective decision and has not published diagnostic accuracy. As such, although the diagnostic accuracy of the Petale screening test is low, the alternatives are not proven to offer a better result. In addition, many children in France are not receiving the recommended auditory screening at 9 months and two years [9, 16].

Potential explanations for the lack of sensitivity in Petale® may be the choice of the threshold. In Petale®, we considered as abnormal, situations where the child did not react to 30dB sounds emitted. This choice was based on a WHO definition, which considers a loss of ≥ 30dB in the better of the two ears to be a ‘disabling hearing impairment’. Further, the target age group in this study, from 9 months to 3 years covers a wide developmental range; conditioned orientation reflex may be better for older children where conditioning is possible; but for younger infants conditioning is less reliable and other methods should be chosen. Alternatively, the choice of the reference test may be criticized for infants of this age group as behavioral tests such as the COR are limited to testing an appropriate behavioral reaction to a stimulus and not an auditory response [17] introducing variability due to non-auditory influences, such as attention or motor ability. The presence of the operator for the whisper test, Moatti box and Sensory Baby Test may influence the results. This may result in false positives, especially in cases of neurological diversity [18]. Finally, comparing our results to previously recorded results demonstrating high accuracy when Petale was administered by ENT specialists [13], the lower sensitivity in our study may be due to lack of specific training for GPs and pediatricians.

All efforts were made in this study to apply a standardized protocol to the audiometry testing protocol, data collection and analysis and this methodological rigor may be one of the reasons why our results differ so much from earlier thesis-based research. Despite these efforts, some limits apply in that we included tests carried out by both GPs and pediatricians and descriptive data shows that there were differences, notably in the time taken to install the material and to complete the test. This may reflect procedural differences and likely affected diagnostic performance and should be further investigated in future work. In addition, we do not have detailed information on the application of the COR reference test, and these types of tests are influenced by attention, developmental stage and operator. We cannot be sure that all ENT specialists were using the same methods and thresholds. Finally, the 38 infants who did not attend the specialist ENT appointment and do not have reference standard results should be acknowledged as potentially resulting in selection bias.

Conclusions

In this study, the levels of sensitivity and specificity observed were insufficient and below the levels expected based on previous work. This requires further investigation, for example, working with a wider panel of reference standards and analysing differences between general practitioners and paediatricians. Nevertheless, our results show the high practitioner satisfaction with this test which is easy and quick to install and carry out, and almost all practitioners said they would continue to use it. In the absence of proven alternatives with similar easy-to-use and cost-effective criteria, the practical advantages of the Petale® test as screening for hearing deficits in infants aged 1–4 years, may be considered as we await further investigation of diagnostic performance.
