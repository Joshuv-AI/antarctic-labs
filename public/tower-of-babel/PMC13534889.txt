Evaluation of bone preparation approaches using length‐based analysis and targeted sequencing for forensic human identification of historic skeletal remains
Authors: Ciara Rhodes, Jacqueline Tyler Thomas, Kimberly Sturk‐Andreaggi, Tal Simmons, Sarah Seashols‐Williams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvances in DNA technology have significantly enhanced the forensic community's ability to develop genetic profiles from unidentified human skeletal remains. However, sampling requires mechanical grinding of hard tissues before DNA isolation. This processing can compromise genetic profiles, particularly in aged bones. We compared the industry‐standard pulverization method with an alternative powder‐free preparation involving prolonged demineralization and subsequent slicing of 19th‐century cortical bone. Data from DNA quantification, STR genotyping, and targeted SNP sequencing were used to evaluate powdered samples versus demineralized slices from paired human bones. Average human DNA yields for pulverized samples and demineralized slices were 0.032 ng and 0.692 ng, respectively. Demineralized slices recovered more amplifiable DNA than traditional homogenization methods (p < 0.05). No pulverized samples produced STR profiles, whereas demineralized slices from the same bone samples yielded partial profiles. Samples underwent DNA repair, library preparation, and hybridization capture using the FORensic Capture Enrichment (FORCE) panel. Applying low‐coverage (1X) analysis of high‐throughput sequencing (HTS) data, demineralized slices outperformed those prepared by traditional pulverization methods (p < 0.05) and substantially increased the information recovered compared with conventional STR analysis methods. Based on HTS data from pulverized samples, DNA fragment length ranged from 27 to 95 bp, and FORCE SNP recovery was 33.23%. In contrast, for demineralized slices, DNA fragment length ranged from 85 to 114 bp, and FORCE SNP recovery was 83.24%. The required reagents and equipment are typically available in forensic labs, and the workflow outlined herein significantly increases the success of DNA recovery from challenging skeletal samples.Keywords: bone slices, degradation, demineralization, DNA analysis, hybridization capture, pulverization, unidentified human remains

INTRODUCTION

Skeletal remains represent one of the most challenging biological samples for forensic laboratories. The workflow from hard tissue to data analysis for human identification can be complicated, especially when working with older or degraded modern bone fragments. The processes used to powder skeletal samples can significantly affect DNA quality and quantity, a concern that is heightened when working with problematic skeletal elements [1, 2]. While numerous extraction chemistries and enhanced detection techniques for obtaining DNA from skeletal samples are outlined in the scientific literature, the absence of consensus on the most optimal method adds to the complexity, given the variety of options available [3].

Several advances in forensic and ancient DNA technologies have led to successful efforts in identifying human skeletal remains. However, the customary sampling processes for obtaining genetic information from hard tissues require mechanical force to grind the elements into a fine powder for DNA isolation. Such preparation approaches destroy and potentially consume limited starting material. Furthermore, they often result in method‐induced degradation within genetic profiles developed using length‐based STR analyses [1, 2].

These issues are compounded when working with aged and degraded bone, as postmortem factors can influence the preservation and survivability of the biomolecules within skeletal elements [2, 3]. Bones subjected to harsh environmental conditions may not yield DNA amenable to traditional autosomal STR genotyping applications. An additional problem of concern is microbial contamination of skeletal elements. Endogenous DNA in aged skeletal remains may often be overwhelmed with microbial DNA, and such bacterial competition interferes with subsequent molecular assays used for human identification [4, 5, 6].

Targeted sequencing provides a potential solution for forensic human identification when traditional length‐based genotyping methods for autosomal DNA from degraded skeletal samples are not possible. When DNA is highly degraded (e.g., < 100 bp in size), the success rate of obtaining complete genetic profiles through length‐based STR analysis significantly decreases. The requisite length of DNA fragments necessary for successful amplification‐based enrichment of SNPs is between 60 and 80 bp [7], while hybridization capture enrichment is amenable to even smaller DNA fragments, with lengths as short as 30–35 bp [8]. High‐throughput sequencing (HTS) can simultaneously generate tremendous amounts of forensically relevant genetic information from short fragments of template DNA, making it ideal for challenging samples such as bone.

Previous research optimized a powder‐free processing method through prolonged demineralization and slicing of weathered bovine cortical bone [1]. Bovine STR profiles generated from demineralized slices yielded a higher average percentage of detected alleles and higher molecular weight loci retention than pulverized samples [1].

The primary goal of this study was to assess whether alternative, powder‐free processing of bone samples may alleviate induced damage often observed in STR profiles or HTS data developed from problematic skeletal samples. This work investigated the recovery of STRs and SNPs within DNA extracts of pulverized samples and demineralized slices prepared from 19th‐century human cortical bone. In collaboration with the Armed Forces Medical Examiner System's Armed Forces DNA Identification Laboratory (AFMES‐AFDIL), hybridization capture enrichment with the FORensic Capture Enrichment (FORCE) panel [9] and HTS were performed.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sample details

A declaration of exemption was granted by Virginia Commonwealth University's Institutional Review Board, as research involving deceased individuals is not considered human subjects research under the Common Rule (45 CFR 46.102(e)(1)). Human samples were collected from aged and weathered skeletonized remains submitted by law enforcement agencies and police departments across Virginia to the Office of the Chief Medical Examiner (OCME) between the 1970s and the 1990s. Once deemed historical and of no use for forensic DNA testing, custody of samples was returned to the submitting law enforcement agencies before being transferred to Virginia Commonwealth University's Department of Forensic Science for teaching purposes. The skeletal remains included in this study are dated to the 19th century (1801–1900) based on the recovery location and forensic anthropological analysis of their taphonomic condition. Known details regarding the samples are displayed in Table S1. The skeletal remains were exposed to uncontrolled environmental conditions and/or scavenging before collection and stored in cardboard boxes at room temperature. Before and after cleaning and sampling, all skeletal elements were photographed (Figure S1).

Sample preparation

Several preventive measures were implemented to minimize exogenous DNA contamination as this project worked with low‐template DNA samples. Experiments were performed in dedicated pre‐PCR and post‐PCR laboratory spaces. Controls (DNA reagent blanks and negative amplification reactions) were evaluated with every batch of samples. Known handler profiles were compared to profiles generated and eliminated as the source.

Before sampling, all bones were cleaned individually, with shafts rinsed using distilled water to loosen dirt and debris in sterile plastic bins. Femoral shafts were washed with a 10% bleach solution, followed by a rinse with distilled water. After cleaning, samples were placed in sterile plastic bins and dried overnight in a sterile chemical fume hood.

In a dedicated chemical fume hood for bone processing, the exterior of each bone element was sanded using a Dremel® rotary tool (Dremel, Racine, WI, USA) and a grinding cone before a small window (~2 × 2 cm) from the midshaft diaphyseal region was cut with a 545‐diamond cutting wheel (Dremel). Small pieces of bone were soaked in a 50% bleach solution for 5 min, rinsed with distilled water, and finally rinsed using 100% ethanol before drying them in a sterile laminar flow hood overnight.

This work focused on comparison analysis, so obtaining the proper mass of starting material for each skeletal element (n = 12) was critical. For each donor, the mass of bone needed was ~1.2 g (0.6 g for pulverization in triplicate and 0.6 g for demineralization and slicing in triplicate). After cleaning the bone pieces, each donor bone element was portioned into two sets of 0.6 g, placed into labeled sterile collection tubes, and stored at −20°C until needed. If excess skeletal material was available, it was put into additional labeled sterile collection tubes and stored at −20°C.

Total demineralization and slice preparation

Following the procedure previously described [1], a demineralization buffer was prepared using 1% sodium lauroyl sarcosine (SLS) in 0.5 M EDTA solution. Bone cuttings were placed in 5‐mL tubes with 4.5 mL of buffer. Samples were loaded on a Thermo Scientific™ Tube Revolver (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and incubated at 56°C with gentle agitation (40 rpm) for 3–5 days, with the buffer changed every 24 h. The samples were not fixed or embedded before slice preparation. Following demineralization, the small bone fragments were sliced manually using a sterile razor blade, with an approximate thickness of 0.25–0.50 mm. Each bone element was weighed, prepared (0.2 g) in triplicate (n = 34), and stored in sterile 5 mL tubes at −20°C until DNA isolation.

Pulverization comparison study

A comparative analysis was performed using the traditional processing method of pulverization. Bone samples were crushed into a fine powder using a sterile stainless‐steel mortar and pestle with liquid nitrogen. Each bone element was weighed, prepared in triplicate (0.2 g), and stored in sterile 5‐mL tubes at −20°C until DNA isolation (n = 39).

DNA isolation

Large‐volume lysis using 3 mL of extraction buffer (1% SLS, 0.5 M EDTA) and 100 μL of protease was performed on the powdered samples, and demineralized slices, and treatment groups were processed separately. The samples were incubated overnight at 56°C with gentle agitation (40 rpm). Following incubation, samples were centrifuged at 4000 × g for 3 min to pellet any undigested material from the lysate.

Isolation of DNA was performed using the QIAamp® DNA Blood Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) following the manufacturer's protocol for “Crude Cell Lysates and Other Samples” in conjunction with “DNA Purification from Blood or Body Fluids (Spin Protocol)” from the QIAamp® DNA Mini and Blood Mini Handbook [10] with the following modifications: 6 mL of Buffer AL (Qiagen) and 6 mL of 100% ethanol were added to achieve optimal pH (≤ 7.0) of lysates, and before elution, samples were incubated at room temperature for 5 min to maximize DNA yield and increase DNA concentration. All samples were eluted in 50 μL of Buffer AE (Qiagen; 10 mM Tris‐Cl, 0.5 mM EDTA, pH 9.0) and stored at −20°C until DNA analysis.

Assessment of DNA quality and quantity

The Quantifiler™ Trio Quantification Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) assesses total human and male DNA within samples and allows for the highly sensitive quantification of large autosomal (214 bp), small autosomal (80 bp), and Y (75 bp) targets with a detection limit of 0.001 ng/μL [11]. Conversely, the microbial 16S rDNA qPCR assay used in this work amplifies the V4 variable region (~250 bp) of bacteria and archaea through the 16S rRNA gene‐specific 515F‐806R primer pair with a detection limit of 0.001 ng/μL [12].

Quantification of total human and human male DNA

All samples were quantified using the Quantifiler™ Trio DNA Kit following the manufacturer's protocol, modified for half‐volume reactions [11]. Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis was performed using the QuantStudio™ Real‐Time PCR Software v1.3 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and exported into Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, WA, USA). Total DNA yield was calculated by multiplying a sample's final DNA elution volume by the concentration. The degradation index (DI) of samples was calculated by dividing the concentration value of the small autosomal target by the large autosomal target.

Quantification of microbial 16S rDNA

To assess bacterial DNA quantity in the samples, the ZymoBIOMICS™ microbial community DNA standard D6306 (Zymo Research, Irvine, CA, USA) was serially diluted from 10 ng/μL to 0.001 ng/μL. Samples and controls were quantified using the bacterial DNA quantification assay described by Seashols‐Williams et al. [12]. Bacterial qPCR analysis was performed using the QuantStudio™ Real‐Time PCR Software v1.3 and exported into Microsoft Excel. Raw data were analyzed with automatic threshold and baseline parameters [12]. Total DNA yield was calculated by multiplying a sample's final DNA elution volume by the concentration.

STR amplification AND purification

Samples, corresponding RBs, and controls were amplified using the PowerPlex® Fusion 6C System (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA) following the manufacturer's protocol [13] except prepared in half‐volume reactions. All samples were prepared to a target input concentration of 0.50 ng. Thermal cycling was performed using the Applied Biosystems™ Proflex™ PCR System (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using the 9600 Emulation mode with the following PCR parameters: 96°C for 1 min, 30 cycles of 96°C for 5 s, 60°C for 1 min, and 60°C hold for 10 min.

All PowerPlex® Fusion 6C PCR products were purified using the QIAquick® PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's recommendations [14], with the following modification: samples were eluted in 10 μL of Hi‐Di™ formamide (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Capillary electrophoresis and STR analysis

Capillary electrophoresis (CE) was performed on the amplified and purified PowerPlex® Fusion 6C PCR products and controls using an Applied Biosystems™ 3500 Genetic Analyzer and Data Collection Software v4.0.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For fragment separation, 1 μL of the amplified sample was added to 11 μL of Hi‐Di™ formamide and 0.5 μL WEN Internal Lane Standard 500 (Promega Corporation). CE run conditions followed the manufacturer's recommendations [13] and included the use of POP‐4® polymer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), a 36 cm capillary array, with an injection voltage of 1.2 kV. Injection time was increased from 15 to 25 s. The generated electropherograms were analyzed using GeneMapper™ ID‐X v1.6 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with an analytical threshold (AT) of 150 RFU and a stochastic threshold of 300 RFU.

Sample processing at the AFMES‐AFDIL

DNA extracts of sample replicates (pulverized samples: n = 11, demineralized slices: n = 10), and associated RBs (n = 7) were shipped on ice overnight to the AFMES‐AFDIL in Dover, DE. Upon arrival, the samples were stored at −20°C until processing.

USER treatment and end repair

To reduce the effects of cytosine deamination, all sample extracts and corresponding RBs underwent DNA repair treatment using the Uracil‐Specific Excision Repair (USER™) treatment (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, Massachusetts, USA) using 3 μL of USER™ and a 1‐h incubation at 37°C [15]. The repaired samples were then purified with the MinElute PCR Purification Kit (Qiagen), using a 10X Buffer PB (Qiagen) to sample volume ratio, and eluted in 55 μL of Tris‐EDTA. The repaired extracts and RBs were then quantified using the dsDNA HS (High Sensitivity) Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific) on the Qubit™ 2.0 Fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) to determine the input for library preparation.

Library preparation and hybridization capture

Samples were divided into three sets, each with a combination of pulverized and demineralized slice samples. For laboratory processing and sequencing, each set included library negative and positive controls (NC and PC, respectively) to assess library and capture success. An NC and PC were introduced during library preparation [15]. The positive control was 1 ng of enzymatically fragmented K562 cell line DNA (Promega Corporation), as previously described [9, 15, 16].

Sample libraries were prepared using the KAPA HyperPrep Kit (Roche Sequencing, Indianapolis, IN, USA) with KAPA 8 nt unique dual‐indexed (UDI) adapters (Roche Sequencing) according to the manufacturer's recommendations [17] unless noted. The UDI adapter stock concentration (15 μM) was used for all samples and RBs at adapter ligation. For the NC and PC, a 1:10 adapter dilution was used based on a DNA input of ≤1 ng [18]. Post‐ligation cleanup used a 1.3x AMPure XP (Beckman Coulter, Brea, CA, USA) purification for small DNA fragment retention with elution in 20 μL Tris‐EDTA [15, 18]. A 12‐cycle library PCR was performed using KAPA HiFi HotStart Uracil+ ReadyMix and 20 μM Illumina Primer Mix (Roche Sequencing) according to manufacturer recommendations [18]. The library PCR products were purified using the MinElute kit and eluted in 25 μL Tris‐EDTA [18]. Library quality was assessed using the Agilent DNA 7500 Kit on the 2100 Bioanalyzer instrument (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA) for all samples [18].

Purified libraries were enriched through hybridization capture using the myBaits (Arbor Biosciences, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) FORCE V2 (Design ID: D10223FRCv2) custom community panel for forensics and ancient DNA [19]. The myBaits FORCE V2 capture assay tested in this study includes 5402 nuclear SNPs (4342 autosomal SNPs) targeted by ~20,000 unique baits [18]. Hybridization conditions followed the manufacturer's v5.03 recommendations [20] with samples incubated overnight (approximately 24 h) at 62°C with a heated lid (72°C) on a Veriti thermal cycler (Thermo Fisher Scientific) [9, 18]. The captured product was amplified in duplicate for 19 cycles and purified as described by Tillmar et al. [9] with the following modifications: 20 μM of Illumina Primer Mix was used, and elution in 20 μL of Tris‐EDTA [18]. Purified captured libraries were quantified using the Agilent DNA 7500 Kit on the 2100 Bioanalyzer.

Pooling and high‐throughput sequencing

Separate pools were created for each sample set (n = 3), including the purified capture products corresponding to RBs and library NC. The library PCs were omitted from sequencing to avoid crosstalk and were used solely to evaluate the success of library preparation and hybridization capture [9, 15, 16, 18]. Sets included a total of 10–11 libraries (6–8 samples, 2–3 RBs, and an NC).

The samples were combined in equal volumes for pooling and quantified using the Agilent DNA 7500 Kit and 2100 Bioanalyzer to determine DNA molarity [9, 16, 18, 21]. Based on the molarity detected in the 100–7500 bp region, each library pool was diluted to 4 nM [15]. The 4 nM pool was denatured, diluted to a final loading concentration of 8 pM or 12 pM with 2.5% PhiX Control v3 (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) to assess sequencing success [15, 21]. Paired‐end sequencing (76 × 76 cycles) was completed on the MiSeq FGx Forensic Genomic System (Illumina) in Research Use Only mode using a MiSeq Reagent Kit v3 (150 cycles) [9].

Sequence data analysis

Demultiplexed FASTQ files were generated from the raw data of the MiSeq Reporter software (Illumina) and imported into the Parabon Fx Forensic Analysis Platform (Parabon NanoLabs, Inc., Reston, VA, USA) and analyzed according to Gorden et al. [16] after ingest with the GammaFORCE‐Kinship panel. In this workflow, overlapping paired reads were merged; merged read pairs were aligned to the Genome Reference Consortium Human Reference 38 (GRCh38) [22] using BWA‐MEM [23] and duplicate mapped reads were removed [16]. The Parabon Fx software employs a genotype likelihood approach to evaluate low‐coverage (≥ 1X) SNPs, thereby maximizing the use of available information [16]. This approach enhances the probative value of the analysis and mitigates potential inaccuracies in the genotype calls produced from a fixed threshold [16]. To account for the effects of DNA damage within the 19th‐century skeletal samples, cytosine deamination was assessed at the time of alignment and incorporated into the genotype probabilities [16]. This process facilitates the estimation of mismatch rates at the fragment ends by integrating the probability of a deamination event for each position in the sequencing read into a genotype likelihood calculation [16].

Statistical analysis

JMP® Pro 17.2.0 (JMP Statistical Discovery LLC, Cary, NC, USA) was used for statistical analysis. Student's t‐tests were employed to compare total human DNA and 16S rDNA yields, STR profile metrics (average peak heights and percentage of alleles detected), and SNP profile metrics (number of reads covering target SNPs, mean read length, mean insert size, and proportional recovery of SNPs (%) at 1X and 5X coverage) of pulverized samples and demineralized slices. A level of ⍺ = 0.05 was used to determine which tests were statistically significant (Figure 1).

Box and whisker plots displaying total human DNA yields as measured from the small autosomal target concentrations (A) and microbial 16S rDNA yields (B) within all replicates of pulverized samples (left box, blue; n = 39) and demineralized slices (right box, yellow; n = 34). Outliers are shown as circles. Asterisks indicate statistical significance (p < 0.05).

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Evaluation of processing methods on recovered DNA

The average human DNA yields for the pulverized samples and demineralized slices were 0.032 ng and 0.692 ng, respectively. The average 16S rDNA yields for the pulverized samples and demineralized slices were 0.039 ng and 0.712 ng, respectively. Demineralized slices ultimately recovered more amplifiable DNA than traditional homogenization methods for DNA analysis of skeletal remains (p < 0.05).

Figure 2 displays the observed Quantifiler™ Trio DNA quantities (ng/μL) across the targets for all samples. Results from the Quantifiler™ Trio qPCR data suggest high levels of degradation within the targeted autosomal DNA. The likelihood of autosomal DNA degradation within these historic skeletal samples is especially evident when evaluating the human quantification data for the pulverized samples (Table S2).

Box and whisker plots displaying target DNA concentrations (ng/μL) within all sample replicates. (A) large autosomal (L.A.) target, (B) small autosomal (S.A.) target, and (C) Y target for the pulverized samples (left box, blue; n = 39) and demineralized slices (right box, yellow; n = 34). Outliers are shown as circles.

The presence of amplification inhibition was determined by assessing IPC cycle threshold (Ct) values. The IPC Ct values were evaluated for each processing method, and 59% of pulverized samples and 71% of demineralized slice samples had IPC Ct values within the validated and acceptable range (27–31). Table S2 displays the sample IPC Ct and DI values assessment for the pulverized samples and demineralized slices. Due to failed large autosomal amplification, DI values for pulverized samples could not be calculated. Therefore, degradation was assumed to be very high. For the demineralized slices obtained from the same donors, 68% yielded large autosomal DNA quantities, and therefore DI values could be calculated, with averages ranging from 3.855 to 247.081. Generally, DI values less than 1 indicate no DNA degradation for the evaluation of degraded DNA, and values between 1 and 10 indicate slight to moderate DNA degradation. DI values above 10 typically indicate significant DNA degradation and possible PCR inhibition.

STR analysis

Because no reference samples were available for profile comparison, concordance across replicates was relied upon, when possible, to establish genotypes for samples and was used to determine STR artifacts when enhanced detection methods were utilized. No pulverized samples yielded an STR profile, whereas demineralized slices of the same two donors yielded partial profiles, ranging from 6 to 34 alleles across replicates.

Figure 3 overlays all amplicon dye channels (excluding ILS) within STR electropherograms for pulverized replicates P_82871 and P_1971 and demineralized slice replicates D_82871 and D_1971. Larger loci were less retained; however, this is a known limitation with STRs and length‐based analyses when working with highly degraded historical samples.

SNP analysis

Figure 4 displays the mapping metrics for the pulverized samples and demineralized slices. Significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed between treatment groups for merged pairs, mapped merged pairs, mapped unique merged pairs, and merged pairs covering targets (Figure 4).

(A) Merged pairs. (B) Mapped merged pairs. (C) Mapped unique merged pairs. (D) Merged pairs covering targets. Outliers are shown as circles. Asterisks indicate statistical significance (p < 0.05).

Table S3 displays detailed sequencing and SNP coverage metrics for all samples. Significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed between the treatment groups for total sequencing reads (p = 0.0084), reads mapped to GRCh38 (p = 0.0085), unique reads (p = 0.0127), and reads covering target SNPs (p = 0.0262).

In a study by Thomas et al. [18], the prediction of DNA profiling success in historical bone samples was evaluated through human quantification data in conjunction with sequencing, using samples ranging from 80 to 800 years postmortem. Before DNA extraction, the samples were pulverized into a powder using a Waring blender cup (Waring, Torrington, CT, USA) [18]. Within their qPCR results, all samples yielded small autosomal target amplification, indicative of DNA fragments ~80 bp in length (< 0.1 ng/μL, with some as low as 0.0001 ng/μL). While this study did not perform DNA repair prior to library preparation and hybridization capture with the FORCE panel, the average DNA fragment length ranged from 55 to 125 bp, and the average SNP recovery was 44.40% for the historical bone samples [18].

Within the observed human qPCR data from the historical samples sequenced in this study, 9.09% (1 out of 11) of the pulverized samples and 90.00% (9 out of 10) of the demineralized slices provided quantification data for the small autosomal target (Table S4). Based on the mapped reads from FORCE data, the DNA fragment length (average insert size) for pulverized samples ranged from 27 to 95 bp, and the average SNP recovery across samples was 33.23% (Table S3). In contrast, the DNA fragment length for demineralized slices ranged from 85 to 114 bp, and average SNP recovery was 83.24% (Table S3).

Figure 5 displays the insert size (DNA fragment length) values observed within the treatment groups. The mean insert size values observed were 59.58 bp and 98.45 bp for pulverized samples and demineralized slices, respectively (p < 0.05).

Box and whisker plot displaying the mean insert size for pulverized samples (left box, blue) and demineralized slices (right box, yellow) at a 1X coverage threshold. Asterisks indicate statistical significance (p < 0.05).

When evaluating proportional SNP recovery ≥ 1X and 5X coverage, significant differences (p < 0.05) were observed in mean recovery for pulverized samples and demineralized slices (Figure 6). At the 1X coverage threshold, the proportional SNP recovery for pulverized samples and demineralized slices was 33.23% and 83.24%, respectively (p < 0.05). When samples were evaluated at the 5X coverage threshold, the proportional SNP recovery for the pulverized samples and demineralized slices decreased to 13.70% and 54.77%, respectively (p < 0.05) (Table S3). Given that the FORCE panel includes Y‐SNPs, it is possible that SNP recovery data could be disproportionate in male samples over female samples. However, the same donors were evaluated for pulverized and sliced comparisons; therefore, the sex balance was consistent between datasets.

Box and whisker plot displaying the SNPs recovered for pulverized samples (left box, blue) and demineralized slices (right box, yellow) at 1X (A) and 5X (B) coverage thresholds. Outliers are shown as circles. Asterisks indicate statistical significance (p < 0.05).

Sequence data observed suggest that alternative sample preparation approaches (i.e., prolonged demineralization and subsequent slicing) of challenging bone samples may improve the recovery of DNA and provide more information than traditional processing methods utilizing pulverization. When assessing the reads covering target SNPs, significant differences were observed between demineralized slices and pulverized samples (p < 0.05).

CONCLUSIONS

Skeletal remains are among the most challenging biological sample types encountered in forensic laboratories. The process from hard tissue to data interpretation for human identification is often labor‐intensive and time‐consuming, especially when working with aged and degraded contemporary bone elements. Due to the nonstandard nature of DNA isolation and purification of skeletal samples within crime laboratories, procedures may vary by jurisdiction.

This work provides empirical data that supports the use of this alternative technique to maximize recovery efforts when working with century‐old human remains for forensic identification. During large‐volume lysis, equal masses (0.2 g) of the same paired donors were incubated overnight for the powdered and demineralized slice samples before DNA isolation. While the demineralized slice samples dissolved, undigested material remained in the powdered samples after overnight incubation. It is plausible that less bone mass was fully digested in the powdered samples, resulting in less DNA available for extraction. However, using highly degraded historical remains, this work demonstrates that the prolonged demineralization step before slicing and subsequent lysis yields higher DNA recovery and greater downstream success than pulverization.

Using the powder‐free approach, partial STR profiles for 2 of the 7 unidentified individuals estimated to be from the 19th century were successfully obtained. Through targeted sequencing of forensically relevant SNPs, demineralized slices outperformed pulverized samples, with 100% of the demineralized slices and 45% of the pulverized samples of the same donors resulting in at least 4000 FORCE SNPs (Table S3) and providing additional genetic information about the historic samples.

Techniques such as DNA repair before library preparation, combined with hybridization capture enrichment methods with HTS, have succeeded in several ancient DNA studies [24, 25, 26, 27, 28]. Both treatment groups likely benefited from the DNA repair before the HTS workflow. Incorporating DNA repair of extracts from poor‐quality forensic skeletal samples before length‐based STR analyses and other PCR‐based workflows may also improve profile development, as primer binding would not be reduced due to uracils in the target region [21].

Although pulverizing the sample may take less time than prolonged demineralization and slicing, this study's results show that the additional time spent on problematic skeletal elements through alternative front‐end processing may offer significant advantages. Homogenizing skeletal elements into a fine powder dramatically impacts the recoverable template DNA, as shown in this work's length‐based and targeted HTS data from the historical skeletal remains. Extended demineralization followed by slicing presents a viable option for problematic skeletal samples at the start of the forensic process. The necessary reagents and equipment are likely readily available within forensic laboratories, and the optimized workflow presented herein significantly increases the success of DNA recovery from highly degraded historical samples.

Continued communication and knowledge‐sharing between the forensic and ancient DNA communities can improve results in both research areas [29]. As presented in this work, prolonged demineralization and slicing may minimize the implications surrounding the destructive nature of traditionally utilized sampling processes for isolating genomic material from contemporary or historic bone samples.
