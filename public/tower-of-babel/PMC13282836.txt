Letter to the Editor: “Efficacy of Acupuncture Combined With Robotic Therapy in the Treatment of Limb Dysfunction After Stroke: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis”
Authors: Xue Fu, Juanjuan Li
Published: 2026

Dear Editor

Zhu et al1 presented a systematic review and meta-analysis on acupuncture combined with robotic therapy for poststroke limb dysfunction. While the topic is important, several issues warrant caution.

First, the authors appropriately introduce the concept of minimal clinically important difference (MCID) but then largely underplay its implications. Across key comparisons, the pooled mean differences for Fugl-Meyer assessment scores are consistently below the accepted MCID of 6 points. This scenario applies to acupuncture + robot versus robot alone (standard mean difference [MD], 4.89), versus acupuncture alone (MD, 4.68), and versus conventional therapy (MD, 5.98). Statistically significant results that do not meet MCID thresholds should not be described as demonstrating “significant clinical implications,” especially in stroke rehabilitation, where small numerical gains often fail to translate into meaningful functional change.2

Second, there is substantial clinical heterogeneity that is not adequately addressed. The included trials mix upper- and lower-limb robots, exoskeleton versus end-effector devices, and different training paradigms, despite evidence that dose, device type, and training characteristics markedly influence outcomes and cost-effectiveness of robotic therapy.3,4 On the acupuncture side, the protocols vary widely (body, scalp, and electroacupuncture), with different acupoints, stimulation parameters, treatment frequencies, and treatment durations. Such variability makes the underlying construct of “acupuncture combined with robotic therapy” poorly defined and weakens the justification for pooled effect estimates.

Third, the authors’ assessment of publication bias is likely over-optimistic. Most included trials are small, single-center Chinese studies reporting positive results—exactly the scenario in which nonpublication of negative trials is most probable. Although Begg and Egger tests for Fugl-Meyer assessment scores did not show statistical evidence of bias, these tests are underpowered with few and heterogeneous studies and can yield false reassurance.5 For modified Barthel index and other outcomes, the number of studies is too small for reliable quantitative assessment, yet the narrative conclusion remains strongly positive. A more cautious stance acknowledging the high a priori risk of publication bias in this literature would be appropriate.

Fourth, the internal validity of the included randomized controlled trials (RCTs) is likely overstated. The authors emphasized that all included studies are RCTs and described this as providing a “strong scientific basis.” However, the grading of recommendations assessment, development and evaluation summary in their own Table 2 classifies the evidence as low to very low for all critical outcomes, driven by serious inconsistency, imprecision, and risk of bias. Several trials use vague or unconventional randomization descriptions (eg, “the order of treatment is random”), and there is no evidence of preregistered protocols, raising concerns about selective outcome reporting despite the risk of bias 2 assessments. In such a context, the mere label “RCT” should not be equated with robust evidence.

In summary, the available evidence suggests that acupuncture combined with robotic therapy may offer modest additional improvements, particularly in activities of daily living, compared with monotherapy or conventional care. However, effect sizes on motor impairment are mostly sub-MCID, the interventions are highly heterogeneous, and the body of evidence is low-certainty with plausible publication bias.
