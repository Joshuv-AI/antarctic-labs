Optical creation of dark–bright soliton lattices in multicomponent Bose–Einstein condensates
Y. Braver, D. Burba, Th. Busch, G. Juzeliūnas, P. G. Kevrekidis

Abstract
We present a widely accessible and experimentally realizable technique for the controlled creation of dark–bright solitons and soliton lattices in atomic Bose–Einstein condensates. The method is based on preparing the condensate in a dark state of a Λ \Lambda -coupled three-level system. Numerical simulations reveal that individual dark–bright solitons created through this scheme can survive over experimentally accessible timescales, even when the coupling laser fields are switched off. Meanwhile, the fate of soliton lattices upon the quench of the fields depends on the scattering lengths. When they are all equal, the lattice is found to persist on timescales comparable to the condensate lifetime, even though the analysis of dynamical stability reveals that they possess unstable modes. In this case the resulting destabilization is not found to be detrimental, as it leads to recurrent dynamics. On the other hand, for unequal scattering lengths the lattice structure gets destroyed once the instability sets in.



I
Introduction

Atomic Bose–Einstein condensates (BECs) constitute a canonical platform for the study of nonlinear coherent structures due to the BECs’ high degree of controllability, long coherence times, and potential multicomponent and multidimensional nature
Pitaevskii and Stringari 2016
. In single-component settings, they support dark
Frantzeskakis 2010
and bright
Abdullaev et al. 2005
matter-wave solitons in the presence of repulsive and attractive interactions, respectively. These excitations have been experimentally realized in elongated condensates
Kevrekidis et al. 2008
, starting with the early observations of dark solitons
Burger et al. 1999
;
Denschlag et al. 2000
and bright solitary waves
Khaykovich et al. 2002
;
Strecker et al. 2002
.

Multicomponent condensates enable a richer class of nonlinear states. In particular, two-component BECs support dark–bright (DB) solitons
Busch and Anglin 2001a
, where a bright component is localized within the density dip of a dark soliton
Becker et al. 2008
;
Middelkamp et al. 2011
;
Hamner et al. 2011
;
Rabec et al. 2025
. Despite their featuring bright solitonic structures, these can arise even in repulsive systems through an effective potential induced by the dark soliton, a mechanism with direct analogies in nonlinear optics
Trillo et al. 1988
;
Christodoulides 1988
;
Ostrovskaya and Kivshar 1998
and connections to integrable vector nonlinear Schrödinger models
Sheppard and Kivshar 1997
. Extensions to spinor condensates further broaden the spectrum of possible soliton complexes
Nistazakis et al. 2008
;
Xiong and Gong 2010
;
Bersano et al. 2018
;
Chang et al. 2004
;
Chang et al. 2005
;
Kawaguchi and Ueda 2012
;
Stamper-Kurn and Ueda 2013
;
Ieda et al. 2004
;
Romero-Ros et al. 2019
and enable fundamental insights
into their interactions
Lannig et al. 2020
.

A key property of solitons is their particle-like character, enabling effective descriptions of their motion in external potentials and of their interactions
Frantzeskakis 2010
;
Kevrekidis and Frantzeskakis 2016
;
Katsimiga et al. 2017
;
Yan et al. 2011
;
Meng et al. 2024
. These features have been extensively explored experimentally, including collisions of dark and bright solitons
Weller et al. 2008
;
Stellmer et al. 2008
;
Nguyen et al. 2014
and the controlled realization of different
multicomponent (including three-component ones) and magnetic solitonic excitations
Becker et al. 2008
;
Farolfi et al. 2020
;
Bersano et al. 2018
. Recent experiments have further advanced control over such nonlinear structures, including the interferometric manipulation of bright solitons
Wales et al. 2020
.
Current efforts increasingly focus on multisoliton configurations, where collective effects emerge. In this direction, dense ensembles of DB solitons, which are sometimes described as soliton gases, have been recently observed
Mossman et al. 2024
, opening new avenues for studying nonequilibrium dynamics and emergent behavior in multicomponent nonlinear quantum systems.

The aim of the present work is to describe and characterize a method to generate multicomponent solitary waves, notably
dark–bright solitons and lattices thereof, via the preparation of a BEC
in a dark state of a
\Lambda
-coupled three-level system
(
Juzeliūnas et al. 2005a
;
Braver et al. 2025
)
. We note that such
a technique is in line with the significant recent interest in the
experimental production of such configurations. Among recent examples,
we note that our technique is more controllable than the one recently experimentally demonstrated based on dynamical instabilities in counterflow dynamics, which is less predictable in its coherent
structure generation
Hamner et al. 2011
. It is arguably more comparable to and similarly versatile
as the recent winding technique of
Mossman et al. 2024
. Another related proposal of creation of dark–bright solitons was considered in Ref.
Juzeliūnas et al. 2007
using the tripod scheme without, however, direct extensions to soliton lattices. This motivates
our exploration of creating DB solitons and their lattices both within
the integrable Manakov limit
Manakov 1974
, where such multisoliton solutions are known to exist
Sheppard and Kivshar 1997
;
Yan et al. 2015
, as well as for the situation where scattering lengths are non-equal, as is the case for
87
Rb. In what follows, these two cases are investigated in the
absence and in the presence of an optical-box confinement.

II
The model

We consider a three-level atomic system coupled in a
\Lambda
configuration, as shown in Fig.
1
(a). The single-particle Hamiltonian is given by

\hat{H}(x)=-\Delta+\hat{H}_{0}(x).

(1)

The second term describes the internal degrees of freedom and can be written in the rotating frame as

\hat{H}_{0}(x)=-\mathrm{i}\tfrac{\Gamma}{2}\left|3\right\rangle\!\left\langle 3\right|+[\Omega_{1}(x)\left|3\right\rangle\!\left\langle 1\right|+\Omega_{2}(x)\left|3\right\rangle\!\left\langle 2\right|+{\rm H.c.}].

(2)

For simplicity, we consider the case where the single- and two-photon detunings are zero. However, the dark-state analysis remains valid over some finite interval of detunings
(
Braver et al. 2025
)
. Furthermore, we have introduced the spontaneous decay rate

\Gamma
for the excited state
|3\rangle
represented through the
imaginary part of the excited-state energy. Following Refs.
(
Łącki et al. 2016
;
Jendrzejewski et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2018
)
, to create the subwavelength barriers we choose the Rabi frequencies of the coupling beams to be

\begin{split}\Omega_{1}(x)&=\Omega_{10}\sin x,\\
\Omega_{2}(x)&=\Omega_{20}.\end{split}

(3)

with
\Omega_{10}\gg\Omega_{20}
, where the amplitudes
\Omega_{10}
and
\Omega_{20}
are taken to be real and positive. Throughout this work, we are using recoil units, where the length is measured in units of
1/k=\lambda_{\rm L}/2\pi
,
with
\lambda_{\rm L}
being the wavelength of the laser field characterized by
\Omega_{1}
. The energy is measured in recoil energies
E_{{\rm R}}=\hbar^{2}k^{2}/2m
, where
m
is the mass of the atom.

The Hamiltonian in Eq. (
2
) possesses a dark eigenstate
given by

|{\rm D}(x)\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\zeta|^{2}}}(|1\rangle-\zeta|2\rangle),

(4)

where

\zeta(x)=\frac{\Omega_{1}(x)}{\Omega_{2}(x)}.

(5)

The dark state has no contribution from the lossy excited state
|3\rangle
,
ensuring an infinite lifetime.
The remaining two eigenstates of this Hamiltonian are given (for the simpler case of
\Gamma=0
) by

|\pm\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(|{\rm B}\rangle\pm|3\rangle\right),\quad\mathrm{where}\quad|{\rm B}\rangle=\frac{\zeta^{*}|1\rangle+|2\rangle}{\sqrt{1+|\zeta|^{2}}},

(6)

with

\hat{H}_{0}|\pm\rangle=\pm\Omega|\pm\rangle\quad\mathrm{and}\quad\Omega=\sqrt{\left|\Omega_{1}^{2}\right|+\left|\Omega_{2}^{2}\right|}.

(7)

In Eq. (
6
),
|{\rm B}\rangle
is the so-called bright
state, representing a superposition of atomic ground states orthogonal
to the dark state
|{\rm D}\rangle
. Later we will consider the adiabatic
motion of atoms in the dark-state manifold, which is justified when the
total Rabi frequency
\Omega
is much larger than the characteristic
kinetic energy of the atomic center of mass motion.

Figure 1:
\Lambda
atom-light coupling scheme. (a) Bare-state basis
\{|1\rangle,|2\rangle,|3\rangle\}
: states
|1\rangle
and
|2\rangle
of the ground state multiplet are coupled to an excited state
|3\rangle
via position-dependent Rabi frequencies
\Omega_{1}(x)
and
\Omega_{2}(x)
, with single-photon detuning
\tilde{\Delta}
and spontaneous emission rate
\Gamma
. (b) Dark–bright basis
\{|\mathrm{B}(x)\rangle,|\mathrm{D}(x)\rangle,|3\rangle\}
: the bright state
|\mathrm{B}(x)\rangle
couples to
|3\rangle
with total Rabi frequency
\Omega(x)=\sqrt{|\Omega_{1}(x)|^{2}+|\Omega_{2}(x)|^{2}}
, while the dark state
|\mathrm{D}(x)\rangle
is decoupled from the light field.

Let us next consider the equations for an atomic BEC interacting with
the laser fields. We start with the Schrödinger equation for the full
state vector
|\Phi(x)\rangle
of a single atom

\mathrm{i}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\Phi(x)\rangle=\hat{H}(x)|\Phi(x)\rangle

(8)

and use the expansion

|\Phi(x)\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{3}\Phi_{i}(x)|i\rangle.

(9)

Here
|i\rangle
are the bare atomic states
of
\hat{H}_{0}
appearing in Eq. (
2
). To account for the interaction
between the atoms, we use the Gross–Pitaevskii approach for the
multicomponent BECs
(
Pitaevskii and Stringari 2016
)
. In practice, this
amounts to supplementing the Schrödinger equations for
\Phi_{i}(x)

with the nonlinear terms, thereby promoting the single-particle
wave functions to the wave functions (order parameters) of the components
of the condensate
(
Juzeliūnas et al. 2005a
)
. This way we arrive at

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\dot{\Phi}_{1}

\displaystyle=\left(-\Delta+g_{11}|\Phi_{1}|^{2}+g_{12}|\Phi_{2}|^{2}\right)\Phi_{1}+\Omega_{1}^{*}\Phi_{3},

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\dot{\Phi}_{2}

\displaystyle=\left(-\Delta+g_{12}|\Phi_{1}|^{2}+g_{22}|\Phi_{2}|^{2}\right)\Phi_{2}+\Omega_{2}^{*}\Phi_{3},

\displaystyle\mathrm{i}\dot{\Phi}_{3}

\displaystyle=\left(-\Delta-\mathrm{i}\frac{\Gamma}{2}\right)\Phi_{3}+\Omega_{1}\Phi_{1}+\Omega_{2}\Phi_{2}.

(10)

For atoms adiabatically following the dark state, the population of
the excited state
|3\rangle
described by the wavefunction
\Phi_{3}

is small at all times, allowing us to neglect collisions
in the equation for
\Phi_{3}
. The coefficients
g_{ij}
describing
interaction between the atoms in the corresponding internal states
are related to the
s
-wave scattering lengths
a_{ij}
as
g_{ij}=8a_{ij}N/kR^{2}

where
R
is the radius of the cross-section of the cloud
Juzeliūnas et al. 2007
. We therefore absorb
the total number of particles,
N
, in the coupling strengths and
normalize the wave function to unity:
\sum_{i=1}^{3}\int\mathrm{d}x\,|\Phi_{i}|^{2}=1
.
Under the constraint of a fixed total number of particles, Eqs. (
10
)
then possess a stationary state of the form
\Phi_{i}(x,t)=\varphi_{i}(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mu t}
, where
\mu
is the chemical potential
Leggett 2001
.

To obtain an equation for the evolution of atoms adiabatically following
the dark state, we perform the following change of variables:

\begin{split}\Phi_{{\rm D}}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\zeta|^{2}}}(\Phi_{1}-\zeta^{*}\Phi_{2}),\\
\Phi_{{\rm B}}&=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\zeta|^{2}}}(\zeta\Phi_{1}+\Phi_{2}).\end{split}

(11)

Inserting these definitions into Eqs. (
10
), we obtain
a set of equations for
\Phi_{{\rm D}}
,
\Phi_{{\rm B}}
and
\Phi_{3}
,
in which the wave functions
\Phi_{{\rm D}}

and
\Phi_{{\rm B}}
are coupled via non-adiabatic terms. Under the
adiabatic assumption of slow center-of-mass motion for atoms in the
dark internal states, justified for the systems to be considered,
and the assumption of equal nonlinear couplings (
g_{11}=g_{12}=g_{22}=g
),
we reach the dark-state GPE
(
Juzeliūnas et al. 2005a
)

\mathrm{i}\dot{\Phi}_{{\rm D}}=-\Delta\Phi_{{\rm D}}+U\Phi_{{\rm D}}+g|\Phi_{{\rm D}}|^{2}\Phi_{{\rm D}}.

(12)

This equation is fully decoupled from those for
\Phi_{{\rm B}}
and
\Phi_{3}
, mirroring (in the mean-field context) the decoupling of single-particle dark state
|{\rm D}\rangle
from states
|{\rm B}\rangle
and
|3\rangle
[cf.
Fig.
1
(b)].
In contrast, the equation for the “bright-state BEC”
\Phi_{{\rm B}}

remains coupled to
\Phi_{3}
, allowing atoms in
\Phi_{{\rm B}}
to
transition into
|3\rangle
and decay, which is also displayed in Fig.
1
(b).

It is apparent from Eq. (
12
) that reduction to the
dark-state manifold results in the appearance of a geometric scalar potential

U(x)
, which emerges from the position-dependence of the atom–light
coupling
(
Dalibard et al. 2011
;
Goldman and Dalibard 2014
)
and for the present scheme is given by
(
Juzeliūnas et al. 2005b
;
Juzeliūnas et al. 2005a
;
Łącki et al. 2016
)

U=\frac{\nabla\zeta^{*}\cdot\nabla\zeta}{(1+|\zeta|^{2})^{2}}=\frac{\epsilon^{2}\cos^{2}x}{(\epsilon^{2}+\sin^{2}x)^{2}},

(13)

where
\epsilon=\Omega_{20}/\Omega_{10}
. The subwavelentgh barriers are created for
\epsilon\ll 1

(
Łącki et al. 2016
;
Jendrzejewski et al. 2016
;
Wang et al. 2018
)
.

Once the solution to Eq. (
12
) is found, one can return
to the wave functions
\Phi_{1}
and
\Phi_{2}
of the internal-state
basis according to

\Phi_{1}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{1+|\zeta|^{2}}}\Phi_{{\rm D}},\qquad\Phi_{2}=-\frac{\zeta}{\sqrt{1+|\zeta|^{2}}}\Phi_{{\rm D}}.

(14)

This follows from Eqs. (
11
) with
\Phi_{{\rm B}}=0
,
since for adiabatic atomic motion in the dark state, described by Eq. (
12
)
for the wavefunction
\Phi_{{\rm D}}
, the system is almost completely
in the dark state with a negligible population of the bright and excited
states.

In our simulations, the states
|1\rangle
and
|2\rangle
are taken to
be two hyperfine levels of
{}^{87}{\rm Rb}
:
|1\rangle=|F=1,m_{F}=-1\rangle

and
|2\rangle=|F=2,m_{F}=1\rangle
, while state
|3\rangle
is the
5\,{}^{2}{\rm P}_{\frac{3}{2}}

state, whose decay rate is
\Gamma=38.1\,\mathrm{MHz}

(
Gutterres et al. 2002
)
. Due to the decay, the energies and chemical potentials of the stationary states acquire an imaginary part. However, for the dark stationary states considered in this work, these imaginary parts turn out to be
\sim 10^{6}
times smaller than the real parts of the respective quantities and lead to a loss of only
\sim 10^{-5}N
atoms in 1 second. Similarly, the stationary wave functions can be chosen to be essentially real (i.e., having a negligible imaginary part). Thus, having estimated the effect of the decay rate, we set
\Gamma=0
for practical computations.

The scattering lengths of the considered states are
(
Egorov et al. 2013
)
:
a_{11}=100a_{0}
,

a_{12}=98.0a_{0}
,
a_{22}=95.4a_{0}
, where
a_{0}
is the Bohr
radius. In dimensionless units, we set
g_{11}=100
(with
g_{12}
and
g_{22}
following accordingly) corresponding to a radius of cross-section
of the order of
R\sim 2\,\mathrm{\mu m}
and the number of atoms being
of the order of
N\sim 10^{4}
. We assume a laser wavelength of

\lambda_{\rm L}=780\,\mathrm{nm}
, the amplitude of the Rabi frequency
\Omega_{2}
is chosen to be
\Omega_{20}=2000
, and we set
\epsilon=0.1
.

III
A single dark–bright soliton

To get a basic understanding of the ground state of the system coupled
to the light fields, we start by considering the ground state of Eq. (
12
)
for
x\in[-\pi/2,\pi/2]
, thereby taking into account only a single
peak of the scalar potential. The spatial part of the ground state

\Phi_{{\rm D}}(x,t)=\varphi_{{\rm D}}(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mu t}
, calculated
under Neumann boundary conditions and setting
g=g_{11}
,
is depicted in Fig.
2
(a). The wavefunction
has a dip of the density at the position of the peak, but, being the
ground state, features no nodes. In terms of components, it follows
from Eq. (
14
) and
\zeta=\epsilon^{-1}\sin x
that

\varphi_{2}
mostly inherits the shape of
\varphi_{{\rm D}}
,
but gets zeroed-out at the position of the peak of
U
, and changes
sign there. As a result,
\varphi_{2}
acquires the shape of a dark
soliton. Meanwhile,
\varphi_{1}
has a maximum at the position of
the peak of
U
and decays away from it.
This results in
\varphi_{1}
having the shape of a bright soliton,
although its tails do not decay exponentially.
These findings are shown in Fig.
2
(b),
with the curves
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
obtained by numerically
calculating the stationary state of Eqs. (
10
). The
calculation has been performed respecting the fact that
g_{ij}
are
different, although in that case the resulting wavefunctions differ marginally
from the case of equal
g_{ij}
.

We are especially interested in the stability of the free solitons
when there are no light fields. To this end we consider quenching
the fields off and tracking the subsequent evolution governed by the
free

equations

\begin{split}\mathrm{i}\dot{\Psi}_{1}&=\left(-\Delta+g_{11}|\Psi_{1}|^{2}+g_{12}|\Psi_{2}|^{2}\right)\Psi_{1},\\
\mathrm{i}\dot{\Psi}_{2}&=\left(-\Delta+g_{12}|\Psi_{1}|^{2}+g_{22}|\Psi_{2}|^{2}\right)\Psi_{2}.\end{split}

(15)

It is well-known that in the case of equal nonlinear couplings such
a system of equations possesses stationary solutions
\Psi_{i}(x,t)=\psi_{i}(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mu_{i}t}

of the form of dark–bright solitons
(
Sheppard and Kivshar 1997
;
Busch and Anglin 2001b
)
.
Such solutions can be found numerically even away from the case of
equal
g_{ij}
. It is instructive to compare the stationary
states
\varphi
of the system with the light fields on with the
stationary states
\psi
with the fields off. These are obtained using the Newton–Raphson iteration using the states
\varphi_{1}
and
\varphi_{2}
as the
initial guess and under the constraint that the number of particles
in each component be preserved

\int\mathrm{d}x\,|\varphi_{i}|^{2}=\int\mathrm{d}x\,|\psi_{i}|^{2}.

(16)

The chemical potentials of the two components are therefore not fixed
but rather obtained during the iteration procedure. The comparison
is shown in Fig.
2
(b). It is apparent that
the shape of the dark soliton
\varphi_{2}
closely matches that
of the stationary one (
\psi_{2}
). On the other hand, the match
between
\varphi_{1}
and
\psi_{1}
is less accurate due to the trigonometric
function-dictated decay of
\varphi_{1}
, reflected in the factor
1/\sqrt{1+|\zeta|^{2}}
.
To quantify the match of the wave functions, we calculate the overlap,
obtaining the value
\left\langle\varphi|\psi\right\rangle=\sum_{i=1}^{2}\int\mathrm{d}x\,\varphi^{*}_{i}\psi_{i}=0.9964
.
Such a high value of the overlap is guaranteed by the fact that most
of the atoms (95%) occupy state
\varphi_{2}
, which indeed matches

\psi_{2}
with high accuracy.

In the case of equal nonlinear couplings, the dark–bright soliton
is dynamically stable
Yang 2010
;
Carretero-González et al. 2024
. To investigate the stability of the stationary state

\psi
obtained for unequal
g_{ij}
, we perform the Bogoliubov–de
Gennes (BdG) stability analysis
Kevrekidis and Frantzeskakis 2016
by considering the evolution of state

\Psi_{i}(x,t)=\psi_{i}(x)\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mu_{i}t}+[a_{i}(x)\mathrm{e}^{\lambda t}+b_{i}^{*}(x)\mathrm{e}^{\lambda^{*}t}]\mathrm{e}^{-\mathrm{i}\mu_{i}t},

(17)

where
a_{i}
and
b_{i}
represent the perturbation. Inserting
this expression into the system (
15
), keeping only the linear
terms, and requiring the existence of a nontrivial solution leads
to the eigenvalue equation

\hat{G}(x)B(x)=\mathrm{i}\lambda B(x).

(18)

Here
B(x)=\begin{pmatrix}a_{1}(x)&b_{1}(x)&a_{2}(x)&b_{2}(x)\end{pmatrix}^{\mathsf{T}}

while
\hat{G}(x)
is a 4-by-4 operator matrix containing the Laplace term and depending
on
\psi_{i}(x)
. It follows from Eq. (
17
) that eigenvalues

\lambda
having positive real part indicate an exponential growth
of the corresponding perturbation.

For the state
\psi
shown in Fig.
2
(b), numerical diagonalization of
\hat{G}
yields a spectrum of eigenvalues
\lambda
that have no appreciable real parts, allowing us to conclude that the state
\psi
is dynamically stable.
The state
\varphi
, having large overlap with
\psi
, may be expected likewise not to possess any exponentially growing modes in its dynamical evolution. To check if this is the case, we directly propagate Eqs. (
15
) starting from the state
\varphi
resulting from the optical approach. Calculations of dynamics for an interval of one second, shown in Fig.
3
, reveal that the DB soliton stays in place, corroborating its dynamical robustness after being created from our experimentally tractable optical pulse-based procedure. On a finer scale, both dark and bright
solitons undergo breathing dynamics, which is the result of the state
\varphi
not being identical to the stationary configuration
\psi
initially, but they nevertheless remain localized at their initial positions.

Next we extend our considerations to dark–bright soliton lattices by enlarging the
extent of the
x
-axis. The interval
x\in[-\pi,\pi)
allows us
to study a single cell of a lattice, as shown in Fig.
4
(a).
Here we display a comparison of
\varphi
, representing the stationary
state of Eqs. (
10
), with the stationary state
\psi

of the free system, obtained under the constraint (
16
).
As in the case of a single dark–bright soliton, the overlap between
the dark solitons is strong, yielding a total overlap
\left\langle\varphi|\psi\right\rangle=0.9975
.
Calculating the BdG spectrum we again find that the system is dynamically
stable. This is in line with previous studies that have demonstrated
the existence of an exact stationary solution of Eq. (
15
)
in the form of a pair of DB solitons. It has been also
shown that these states are stable in certain
regimes of nonlinear couplings
(
Katsimiga et al. 2018
)
. Having obtained the solution
\psi

for a single cell, we can study the stability of an infinite periodic
lattice using the Floquet–Hill approach
Deconinck and Nathan Kutz 2006
.
For a periodic state
\psi
,
the
\hat{G}
operator in Eq. (
18
) becomes periodic, allowing
us to focus on a single cell of a lattice and introduce a quasimomentum-like
parameter
q
. The linearization equation then transforms into
\hat{G}_{q}(x)B_{q}(x)=\mathrm{i}\lambda_{q}B_{q}(x)

upon introducing eigenvectors of the form
B_{q}(x)=\mathrm{e}^{\mathrm{i}qx}C_{q}(x)
.
Constructing
\hat{G}_{q}
and diagonalizing for a range of values
q\in[-\pi/L,\pi/L)

provides us with stability signatures for an infinite lattice. This
time, apart from a range of purely imaginary eigenvalues
\lambda_{q}

we obtain a host of eigenvalues possessing non-zero real parts, as
shown in Fig.
4
(b). The values enable us
to estimate the time before the instability has grown appreciably.
The largest eigenvalue of
0.0087
leads to the time interval of

4.8\,\mathrm{ms}
. In practice, however, the instability develops an order of magnitude later, as we discuss next.

To study the effect of this instability, we calculate the dynamics
governed by Eqs. (
15
) for a lattice on the interval
x\in[-3\pi,3\pi)
,
starting from state
\varphi
, i.e., with the initial condition
being
\Psi_{i}(x,t=0)=\varphi_{i}(x)
. We note that while the stability
analysis is only applicable to the stationary state
\psi
, the large
overlap between
\varphi
and
\psi
leads to a similar evolution
of these states. The evolution of
|\Psi_{1}|^{2}
and
|\Psi_{2}|^{2}

Let us now assess the stability of the lattice in the presence of a trapping potential. As is well-known
Busch and Anglin 2001b
, in the presence of a harmonic trap, a single soliton will oscillate harmonically around the center of the trap. Therefore, the lattice can be sustained only under very specific conditions, where the trap frequency and the distance between the solitons are fine-tuned such that the interaction between the solitons cancels the effect of the trap. Clearly, an optical-box trap
Gaunt et al. 2013
, allowing for a homogeneous condensate, is more appropriate for the lattice considered herein, especially if its length is chosen in integer multiples of the spatial period of the dark-state potential. The simulations of dynamics of a system subject to hard-wall boundary conditions (and equal nonlinear couplings) are shown in Fig.
5
(c). Contrary to the case of periodic boundary conditions, the solitons never return to a configuration similar to the initial one. Nevertheless, the amplitudes of oscillations of the solitons’ positions do not exceed the distance between them, so that the lattice structure is retained. Once again, however, this no longer remains true for the case of unequal nonlinear couplings of
{}^{87}{\rm Rb}
. In that case, the solitons move far away from their initial positions, and in our simulations we typically observe that the regular lattice structure gets destroyed on a timescale of
\sim 10\,\mathrm{ms}
.

V
Conclusions and Future Challenges

In summary, we have shown that a 3-component BEC interacting with
the laser fields in the
\Lambda
configuration can be used to create a reproducible dark–bright soliton lattice, which is the lowest-energy
state of the dark-state manifold. The resulting state
\psi
has
a large overlap with the stationary state
\varphi
of the fields-free
system. The BdG analysis of
\varphi
reveals that, while a single DB soliton or a pair thereof is dynamically stable, a DB soliton lattice becomes unstable for sufficiently long evolution times, possessing a number of modes that cause deviations of the solitons from their initial positions. However, if the nonlinear couplings are tuned to an identical value, then after a period
of growth of the amplitude of these solitonic motions, recurrence of the original
configuration can be observed. When the laser
fields are quenched, the state
\psi
prepared using optical means
evolves in a similar fashion, with the solitons additionally experiencing
breathing.

It would be particularly interesting to explore similar phenomena in
systems involving a larger number of components to explore whether more
exotic soliton configurations can be accordingly prepared. Moreover,
it would be natural to extend relevant considerations to higher dimensional
settings and the highly active investigation of vortex-bright solitary
waves
Richaud et al. 2023
;
Richaud et al. 2022
;
Ohsawa et al. 2026
.
