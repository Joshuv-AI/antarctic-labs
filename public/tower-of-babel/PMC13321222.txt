Three centuries of technological innovation: Opportunity is the mother of invention
Authors: Jordan G. Okie, James H. Brown, Astrid Kodric-Brown, Joseph R. Burger, Tatiana P. Flanagan, Trevor S. Fristoe, Sean T. Hammond, Norman Mercado-Silva, Jeffrey C. Nekola, Jennifer Richter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceOur research provides a broad, inductive, evidence-based perspective on technological innovation between 1690 to 1990. Distributions of breakthroughs across inventor attributes, environments, categories, and periods reveal how opportunity, ingenuity, collaborations, and environment played fundamental roles in inventiveness feeding invention and innovation. While most inventions were made by one or a few creative individuals in conducive environments, collaborative networks and institutions have been expanding their role in fostering innovation. Prominent innovation hubs emerged in rural areas for household and agricultural technologies, cities for transportation and information/communication, and institutional environments for armaments and medical technology. Our macroecological approach sheds light on how inventor attributes, teams, and environments fostered innovations underlying the spectacular evolution of the Technosphere.

How do technological breakthroughs occur? What characteristics of inventors, teams, and their socioecological environments fostered major innovations that have transformed our lives? These questions have been addressed by multiple authors from different disciplines, from cognitive science to psychology, sociology, economics, engineering, and the sciences of complexity and technology (e.g., 1, 2). Anthropologists and biologists have addressed similar questions in primates and other animals, aiming to identify the evolutionary origins, ecological conditions, and social behaviors that underlie both the common themes and wide variations (3).

Several recent studies have addressed the roles of intrinsic traits and extrinsic environments in technological innovation. Some of them have supported a paradigm of “collaborative interaction”: innovation, problem solving, and creativity that arises from special, emergent properties of networks among individuals with diverse interests and experiences (e.g., 4–7). This paradigm points to the importance of cities, hospitals, academic institutions, and research parks in attracting groups of creative individuals with different backgrounds and skills who organize into collaborative teams to tackle challenging problems. This paradigm may apply to the industrial-technological-informational revolution that resulted in the spectacular advances in science and technology during the last century (8). This is in contrast to other studies that have supported a paradigm of “genius” and ingenuity: the idea that innovations tend to be made by exceptionally creative people who take on a personal challenge to solve particular theoretical and practical problems (9–13). These two paradigms have often been advanced as alternative hypotheses or explanations. They have given rise to colloquial expressions, such as whether opportunity or necessity is “the mother of invention.” Each extreme has attracted its supporters. But these categories might be more realistically viewed as extremes of multiple spectra that extend from solitary individuals to large groups, working independently or collaboratively, in isolated or interconnected environmental settings, with similar or divergent backgrounds and skills, working on a narrowly specialized or broadly interdisciplinary problem (14).

Here we address some of these questions with a macroecological analysis of a sample of more than 400 major technological inventions in material culture made between 1690 and 1990. Rather than taking a deductive approach and trying to distinguish between alternative hypotheses, separate causation from correlation, and dependent and independent variables to draw definitive conclusions, we have taken an inductive approach—gathering and analyzing data to reveal patterns and subsequently formulate tentative theory, concepts, and principles to explain these patterns and guide future research (15). Our approach draws inspiration from macroecology (e.g., 16, 17), which pioneered informatics, statistical, and theoretical approaches involving the compilation and analysis of large- and multiscale datasets from dispersed literature and other sources to discover general patterns and elucidate the structure and dynamics of complex systems, including human systems (18–27).

We assembled a large quantitative dataset of major inventions and identified general patterns in how the pace of invention and characteristics of inventors and inventor teams differed based on environment, time periods, and technology categories. Each invention included in the dataset gave rise to transformative technological innovation, in the sense that the invention was widely adopted, changing the way people lived their lives or conducted their affairs, or was an important step that led to the deployment of subsequent technologies (e.g., as in 28).This dataset allowed us to reveal how the basic attributes of inventors and their socioecological and geographic environments promoted transformative innovations. Perhaps surprisingly, this question has received relatively little scientific scrutiny. There are many anecdotal accounts of individual inventions and claims for the roles of individuals and their social environments. But there is a paucity of strong inference from evidence-based science (29).

Approach

Several recent studies have used the extensive data on patents to address questions about technological innovations (30–34). However, patent data do not distinguish between inventions that were slight variations on preexisting designs or novel ideas that had no practical impact versus true breakthroughs that fundamentally changed lives. For example, the U.S. Patent Office lists millions of patent applications since 1790, including almost one million (955,024) in 2015 alone, but it provides no basis for determining which patents were transformative. We avoided such problems by assembling our own new database containing a large sample of more than 400 inventions that represent major technological breakthroughs (Fig. 1 and Dataset S1; see Materials and Methods for details). Each breakthrough involved a phase of ideation and material creation (i.e. manifestation) of an invention followed by a process of refinement, development, and adoption, culminating in a major technological innovation (35–37). While many minor inventions never became innovations [e.g., sensu (36)], we targeted breakthroughs that represent both major inventions and innovations in technology.

The database’s technological inventions, ordered temporally, color-coded according to the environment of origin, with numbers indicating team size.

To this aim, we searched the print and online literature for inventions from 1690–1990 that reporters, historians, and technologists considered to be highly significant innovations in seven categories of technology—agriculture, armaments, information and communication, household, industry, medical, and transportation. We restricted our study to 1690–1990, because it was difficult to identify the creator and location of inventions before 1690, and it seemed premature to assess the full impact of inventions made after 1990. For each invention, we recorded the year in which a working prototype was first created. Our data focus on players involved in the initial “origin” phase of technological innovation (in particular, the physical manifestation of the invention) and their environmental context. Without this phase, each innovation could never have arisen, though we acknowledge that subsequent steps [e.g., scaling up manufacturing, marketing, and adoption (35)] are also important in the innovation process (35, 36). We recorded the attributed inventors and classified the environmental setting of each as 1) rural, including villages and small towns of less than ~3 thousand inhabitants; 2) city, urban centers of more than ~3 thousand inhabitants; or 3) institutional, including hospitals, military bases, academic institutions, and research laboratories. For the inventor and team characteristics, we focused on inventor age, gender, team size, and cultural diversity, due to their various potential roles in mediating the ingredients of innovation, such as team function (38–42), access to opportunities (43), the development of knowledge and skill (44), and the facilitation of creative thinking (45).

Our macroecological approach revealed robust, general patterns of variation across time periods, people, and socioenvironmental contexts associated with the technological innovations that materially changed human lives and the evolution of the Technosphere (46).

Results

Pace of Invention and Innovation.

For most categories (agriculture, armaments, household, industry, and transportation) and environments (rural and cities), invention rate—quantified as number of inventions per 50-y time interval—peaked between 1840–1940 (Fig. 2). A hump-shaped invention rate curve might suggest a bias whereby later inventions have been considered less transformative by historians and technologists. This is unlikely, however, because rates of invention increased over time for medical and information/communication categories and within institutional environments.

Invention rate (in number of inventions per 50-y period) differs between rural, city, and institutional environments and technology categories. Rural environments were hubs in agriculture, household technology, and early transportation and industrial technology crucial to the expansion of the Technosphere. Up until ~1840, rural environments supported similar overall invention rates as cities, from 1840 to 1939 cities had the highest rates, driven largely by pronounced peaks in transportation and industrial innovation, and from 1940–1989 institutions had the greatest invention rate. Institutions exhibit a monotonic increase in invention rate over the timeline, driven by increasing medical, armament, and information/communication invention rates, whereas cities and rural environment exhibit a hump-shaped pattern with a peak in invention rate around 1840–1939. Note the log-scale on the vertical axes.

Changing demography complicates interpreting the influence of the environment. Over the 300-y time span, there was an increase in the global population and progressive urbanization. Since the proportion of people living in cities increased over time, by chance alone we would expect invention rates in cities to increase relative to rural areas. This null hypothesis that the number of inventors and inventions is simply a constant proportion of the population is rejected by the patterns in Fig. 3, which plots the per capita frequency of inventions in 50-y bins for each environment. For none of the categories nor environments are the per capita rates of invention constant over time. The curves for five categories (agriculture, household, industry, military, and transportation) and two environments (rural and city) are conspicuously hump-shaped, increasing until ~1840 and subsequently decreasing (category average R2 = 67%; cities R2 = 72%; rural R2 = 59%; Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S2). In contrast, per capita invention rate in medicine and information/communication technology increased over time (R2 = 37 and 70%, respectively), as did per capita invention rate from institutions (R2 = 82%; Fig. 3 and SI Appendix, Fig. S2).

Patterns in per capita rates of invention (for 50-y time bins). Whereas per capita invention rates in institutional environments increased over time, at least until 1950, per capita invention rates declined since ~1870 in cities and in rural areas since ~1910. Although cities were hubs of innovation in all categories, rural environments were also innovation hubs in certain time periods in agricultural and industrial technologies. The rural and urban per capita invention rates were calculated as the number of inventions per 50-y time interval divided by the estimated global-scale number of people in that environment. As the historical number of people and households associated with institutions is poorly known, the per capita invention rate for institutions was calculated by dividing the number of inventions by global total population size. As such, this rate can be thought of as a minimum expectation, as a much fewer number of people are associated with institutions than the global population size, and only temporal trends in the institutional per capita invention rate should be compared to city and rural per capita invention rates, not the absolute values. Note the log-scale on the vertical axes.

Characteristics of the Environment.

Invention rate varied among environments, but the patterns differed between technology categories and time periods (Figs. 2 and 3, SI Appendix, Fig. S1). For example, most agricultural inventions occurred in rural areas (67%), while most transportation inventions occurred in cities (71%). Per capita innovation rates were usually higher in cities than rural environments, yet rural environments were also important innovation hubs, surpassing cities on a per capita basis in four of the twelve 50-y time periods for agricultural and industrial technologies and surpassing cities in overall invention rate in nine of the 24 time bins across agricultural, household, industrial, and transportation technologies. Between 1690 and 1740 rural environments were the sites of transformative industrial and transportation inventions that kick-started the industrial revolution and economic globalization (e.g., the steam engine, internal combustion engine, factory system, and railway line). They were also hubs of innovation in household technologies (e.g., washing machine, sewing machine, and gas stove).

In medicine, 60% of innovations arose from institutions, highlighting the increasing role of universities, hospitals, and military bases in basic and applied scientific research. During the most recent period (1940–1990), most of the innovation in industry, armaments, and information and communications technology also arose from institutions. Examples include the Transmission Control Protocol, Internet Protocol, World Wide Web, satellite tracking and GPS, DNA fingerprinting, magnetic resonance scanning machine, oral contraceptive pill for birth control, defibrillator, and AK 47 assault rifle.

The high development areas and nation-states of western Europe and North America were major centers of innovation, producing 415 of the 417 inventions. This presumably reflects the concomitant rising living standards in these nations, which undoubtedly fostered innovation (47) but may also reflect our Western bias in the historical accounts (see Discussion).

Characteristics of the Inventors and Teams.

Team size.

In the vast majority of cases, inventions were credited to just one individual (82% before 1830 and 72% thereafter); 13% were credited to collaborations of two individuals, 3% to teams of three, <1% to teams of four or more, leading to a mean team size of 1.6 (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). In urban and rural environments the per capita invention rate, whether calculated for 50-y or 20-y time periods, was not significantly correlated with the mean team size of those periods (urban: 50-y bins: r = 0.088, P = 0.879; 20-y bins: r = 0.011, P = 0.727; rural: 50-y bins: r = –0.654, P = 0.159; 20-y bins: –0.154, P = 0.583; SI Appendix, Fig. S5). In contrast, there was an indication that invention rate was positively associated with team size in institutions, as it exhibited statistical significance when per capita invention rate was calculated for 20-year time periods (50-y bins: r = 0.724, P = 0.167; 20-y bins: r = 0.794, P = 0.004).

Several additional patterns suggest the importance of collaborations in some innovations. First, large teams were involved in the development of two large, complicated military systems: the atomic bomb and nuclear submarine. Second, the percentage of inventions by teams increased substantially from 8.9% for 1690–1889 to 18.1% for 1890 to1989 (Fig. 4A; and χ2 = 7.38, P = 0.007). The percentage of inventions by large teams (≥ 6) increased markedly during the last 100 y, from 0% for 1690–1889 to 1.6% for 1890–1939 and 4.0% for 1940–1989 (Fig. 4, χ2 = 5.145, P = 0.023). Third, the increasing role of institutions (Fig. 2) and increasing frequency of teams at institutions (Fig. 4A) makes for an increase in mean team size at institutions (50 y-bins: r = 0.851, P = 0.068; 20-y bins: r = 0.725, P = 0.012) and over all environments (50-y bins: r = 0.847, P = 0.033; 20-y bins: r = 0.581, P = 0.023). Fourth, cities and institutions had twofold higher percentages of inventions by teams of two or more individuals (19.4% and 24.6%, respectively) than rural areas (10.4%; χ2 = 7.42, P = 0.024; Fig. 4A).

Patterns of variation across environments and time periods in inventor team size and diversity. (A) Most inventions were attributed to solo inventors and small teams, and all environments fostered solo inventors and teams of varying sizes. Teams have been increasing in relative frequency since ~1790. Institutions and cities harbor a greater percentage of teams of two and three inventors than rural environments, but cities and rural environments had similarly low percentages of teams of six or more individuals. (B) The percentage of teams with cultural diversity (comprised of individuals born in more than one country) has increased over the timeline and was greater in institutions and rural environments than cities. Similarly, the percent of inventions involving immigrants and expatriates increased over the timeline, and institutions harbored an overall greater percentage of inventions involving these individuals. (C) The percentage of teams with gender diversity (having men and women) was higher in rural environments, although the difference was not statistically significant. The percentage of inventions involving women increased over the timeline, and for the period of 1940–1989, cities harbored a greater percentage of inventions involving women than other environments. Note that greater statistical fluctuations are expected <1740 due to the small number of innovations during this early period.

Geographic and cultural diversity.

The percentage of inventions by immigrants and expatriates (individuals whose birthplace was not the country identified with the invention) increased substantially over the timeline (r = 0.934, P = 0.006), driven largely by greater increases in cities and institutions than in rural environments (Fig. 4B; cities: r = 0.882, P = 0.020; institutions: r = 0.832, P = 0.081; rural: r = 0.649, P = 0.163).The percentage of an environment’s teams that were “culturally diverse”–teams composed of a mix of birthplace nationalities–was greater in institutions (41.1%) and rural areas (27.2%) than cities (10.3%; χ2 = 8.386, P = 0.015). The percentage of teams with cultural diversity showed a pattern of increase during 1790 to 1989, going from 0% in 1790–1839 to 37% in 1940–1989 and was more than 2-times higher for 1840–1989 (25.4%) compared to 1690–1839 (10.0%; Two-sample Poisson-rate test: Z-score: –2.332, P = 0.020). This temporal trend presumably reflects, at least in part, global patterns of migration, such as the increasing movement of people between nation-states. Increasing opportunities for immigrants in cosmopolitan cities, such as London, Paris, and New York, the locations of 19% of the inventions, may also have played a role.

Gender.

Only a very small percentage of the inventions (4.3% of our sample) were credited to women. Although the percentage involving at least one woman increased significantly over time (Fig. 4A; two-sample Poisson rate test: P = 0.020, z-score = –2.331), the magnitude of change was small: from a minuscule 1.1% from 1690 to 1839 to a still scant 5.1% from 1840 to 1990 (Fig. 4C; two-sample Poisson rate test: P = 0.020, z-score = –2.331). Most of the documented inventions by solitary women and mixed-gender teams were in household technologies (Pearson chi-sq = 16.068, P = 0.013). Over the entire timeline, there were few significant differences between environments in the percentage of teams with mixed-gender (χ2 = 0.976, P = 0.614) and in the percentage of inventions by women (two-sample Poisson rate tests: city versus rural: z-score = 0.463, P = 0.643; city versus institutional: P = 0.584, z-score = 0.548; rural vs institutional: z-score = 0.066, P = 0.948). However, over the whole timeline, in cities, there was a more distinct pattern of increase in the percentage of inventions involving women (Fig. 4C; cities: r = 0.888, P = 0.018; rural: r = 0.317, P = 0.541; institutions: r = 0.487, P = 0.406), and between 1940 and 1990, inventions by women were more common in cities and institutions than in rural environments (two-sample Poisson rate test for city vs rural: P = 0.046, z-score = 2.00; institutional vs rural: P = 0.083, z-score =–1.732).

Age.

The age distribution of inventors is remarkable for its breadth (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). Individuals made their seminal contributions at ages ranging from 15 to 75, an average of 39 y, with negligible differences between innovation types (GLM: R2 = 2.07%, F-value = 1.37, P = 0.226, SI Appendix, Table S1) or environments (GLM: R2 = 0.98%, F-value = 1.82, P = 0.144, SI Appendix, Table S2). We detected no trend of increasing age of the inventors over the 300 y (GLM: R2 < 0.01%, F-value = 0.24, P = 0.63, SI Appendix, Tables S3 and S4), even though some might have been expected due not only to increased life expectancies but also to inventors with more education and skill development being better able to utilize advancing technologies of increasing complexity and scientific sophistication (33, 44), as seen with Nobel laureates in science (44).

Discussion

Analysis of our newly compiled dataset of over 400 inventions reveals how attributes of inventors, teams, and their environments contributed to the development of new technologies from 1690 to 1990. Most inventions were attributed to single inventors of a variety of ages and emerged from settings ranging from remote rural areas and small settlements to the world’s largest urban centers, as well as military, medical, and research institutions. The patterns suggest that the “collaborative interaction” and “ingenuity” paradigms both play important roles in technological evolution. They elucidate the role of socioenvironmental conditions in fostering acts of ingenuity while also indicating an expanding role for collaborative interactions and larger, more diverse teams in the 20th Century.

The Role of Opportunity.

Many of the patterns we observed in the distributions of inventions among environments, ages, genders, teams, and technology categories can be interpreted as emerging from talented individuals, whether in isolation or in groups, exhibiting their creativity or “genius” in favorable environments by coming up with novel solutions to practical problems. This phenomenon can be summarized as “opportunity is the mother of invention and innovation.”

While this phrase deliberately contrasts with the old proverb “necessity is the mother of invention,” we do not consider opportunity and necessity as mutually exclusive (e.g., 48). Nevertheless, in studies of technological evolution, “necessity” is difficult to assess without relying on highly subjective rationales, and we find no evidence that it was a general driving force across the wide spectrum of categories in our database–there were many examples of innovations in which the inventor(s) or society did not “need” the new technology (e.g., the Post-it note, coffee filter, and video game console), and there were many periods and environments in which an inventor, institution, or society could have greatly benefitted from an invention yet failed to come up with it (e.g., to avoid defeat in war, famine, or a medical crisis). In contrast, it is reasonable to assume that in nearly all 417 cases, the inventors: 1) had substantial prior opportunities to engage with technological components related to the invention, 2) were sufficiently healthy, secure, and nourished to have the time and energy for concentrated thinking and tinkering, and 3) benefited from opportunities conferred by the invention, which presumably motivated their inventiveness (e.g., resulting from monetary benefits or increased efficiencies in everyday activities).

Overall, our inductive findings highlight the interacting influences of “user-driven innovation” (e.g., sensu 13), behavioral ecology, and psychological ingredients in cultivating the creativity and problem-solving essential to major technological innovations.

Considering our results within the broader contexts of behavior, ecology, and evolution is important for developing a more general and unified understanding of novel traits in humans and other organisms (50, 51). Among the most prominent technologies of nonhumans are the tools made by several nonhuman primates to perform foraging tasks, such as probes to extract honey and insects, hammers to smash open bones, nuts, and fruit, and cups, spoons, straws, and sponges to collect and drink water (50, 52–57). Many published studies of innovation and tool use by wild primates and prehistoric and modern humans address how genetics, environment, cognition, and sociality influence invention, transmission, and retention of material culture (1, 52, 53, 58, 59). But the different disciplinary backgrounds of the investigators seem to have largely precluded cross-taxa comparisons to identify themes and suggest unifying explanations.

Studies of human inventions have largely focused on the inventor and aimed to distinguish between ingenuity and “collaborative interaction” hypotheses (see above references). Studies of tool use by nonhuman primates have focused on the ecological and social environment (e.g., 3, 60), and aimed to distinguish between opportunity and necessity hypotheses (e.g., 50, 52, 61)—e.g., on whether the tools were invented or transmitted during periods of food scarcity and environmental stress, or during times when resources are abundant and individuals have time to experiment [i.e., “greater opportunities for invention likely contributed to insect-extraction tool use” (62)]. They have also examined the role of cognitive capacity (51). While some studies have identified cases where both necessity and opportunity were identified as important drivers of tool-use in nonhuman primates (e.g, 63–65), comparative analyses suggest a more pervasive and crucial role of ecological opportunity (52). The nonhuman studies mirror our findings and arguments that the unprecedented affluence of 18th century England was responsible for nurturing psychological and behavioral traits that drove the inventiveness and innovation responsible for the Industrial Revolution (47). Thus the interacting roles of ingenuity and opportunity apparently shape geographic and temporal patterns of tool use and innovation across many organisms.

Rises, Peaks, and Plateaus in Innovation.

Differences between environments and technology categories in the pace of invention over time offer insight into the drivers of innovation. The peaks in cities and rural areas from the mid-19th Century to the early-20th Century support arguments that innovations in household, electricity, agriculture, and urban sanitation were part of a pulse that transformed living conditions in the United States, replacing the hard manual labor of earlier generations with machines powered initially by hand and then increasingly by electricity and fossil fuels (66).However, the observed increase over time of per capita invention rate in institutional environments, medicine, and information and communication technology offers a contrasting perspective to findings based on economic data, patent data, and citation networks that suggest a 20th Century decline in research productivity and disruptive science and technology across a variety of fields (67, 68).

Intriguingly, even though cities fostered a greater total number of inventions, per capita invention rates in cities and rural areas had converged to roughly similar rates (3.6 to 3.8 per million population per decade across technology types) by 1940–1990 (SI Appendix, Fig. S2) and appear to have plateaued in institutional environments. This convergence may indicate that any relative advantage of cities in nurturing genius or supporting unique networks of interactions may be diminishing over time. This reduction may be partly attributable to the reduced geographic barriers to collaborative interaction, information exchange, and access to raw materials and manufactured products provided by the dramatic expansion in communication and transportation technologies and infrastructure (e.g., libraries, the telephone, television, train, fossil-fuel powered ship, and mass-produced automobile) (1). Finally, the convergence also reflects the role of institutions in the increasingly complicated innovations emerging in communications, electronics, and medical technology as economies transition into the Information Age (69, 70).

Urban Versus Rural Innovation Hubs.

Whereas several well-cited publications focus on cities, especially large ones, as centers of interaction and resulting innovation (4, 71, 72), our analyses offer a different and more nuanced perspective. Although cities have indeed been locations of major inventions, some of which were products of teams which are more likely to form in larger population centers, rural environments were hubs for many of the most transformative inventions of the Anthropocene. Additionally, the interpretation of the pattern is complicated by rural-to-city and city-to-rural “brain drains” of inventors moving to and from cities for reasons not directly relevant to their creative prospects.

It is also noteworthy that only just a few metropolitan areas have exceptionally high rates of invention—namely, New York (19 inventions), Paris (29 inventions), and London (30 inventions). Otherwise, urban environments had similar numbers of inventions as rural areas and institutions. This highlights the recognized wide-ranging variability in the degree to which cities “achieved either cultural or technological creativity,” with many cities failing to do so (73, 74). For example, many cosmopolitan cities–including in Western nations with developed infrastructures and economies–had relatively low invention rates. This suggests that many other factors, in addition to interactions among a large population of diverse individuals, influenced the opportunities available in different cities.

The Role of Teams in the Ingenuity and Interactions Paradigms.

While the prevalence of solo inventors and small teams reveals a strong role of unique and creative individuals, it does not rule out an important role of interaction and collaboration. Indeed, the observed increases in team size and diversity through time likely reflect the increased variety and specialization of skills and education required to make increasingly complicated technological advances (33, 44). So, rather than attributing the pattern by a single, simplistic paradigm, it seems more realistic to acknowledge the complexity of the phenomenon, and of the many and diverse characteristics of humans and environmental features that contribute to problem solving, creativity, and scientific innovation (e.g., 9, 41, 42, 75, 76).

Strengths and Limitations of the Macroecological Approach.

A special feature of our study is its macroecological approach. It complements, but does not substitute for, more detailed perspectives that can be obtained from patent data and biographical accounts. Its strength comes from its reliance on broadscale statistical patterns in a large database of many inventions, spanning a wide area of geographic space and historic time and including the multidimensional context surrounding each invention. It incorporates a wide variety of inventions and emphasizes patterns of variation across inventors and their environments as well as emergent central tendencies. This approach inherently avoids the potential biases of drawing misleading inferences from isolated and anecdotal examples. Thus, it can provide broad, yet nuanced, insights into longstanding questions about the creative process. Nevertheless, we caution at extrapolating beyond 1990, as it is unclear whether our results and conclusions apply to the last few decades of rapid growth and development of the modern industrial, technological, and informational economy, especially for technologies such as artificial intelligence (AI).

The implications and limitations of the macroecological approach should also argue for caution. Potentially misleading conclusions can be inferred from such coarse analyses of mostly categorical data. Perhaps most important are biases in data and analyses due to social norms in the contemporary society of the inventors and subsequent accounts of their contributions, especially uneven attention to gender, social class, ethnicity, and language. There can be no doubt that credit for the transformative inventions in our database has been unequally and unfairly attributed to men over women, group leaders over other contributors, Western Europe and North America as opposed to other geographic regions, and English-language versus other sources of data. These biases are revealed in detailed accounts of individual inventions and biographies of the credited inventors: contributions of women and junior collaborators have frequently and systematically been ignored (26, 27).

While these limitations suggest that our results be interpreted with qualification and caution, they do not invalidate them. Rather they highlight the importance of maintaining open scholarly dialogue on the role of biases—and other factors that we have not addressed—in accounting for the often strong empirical patterns. For example, we are not aware of any obvious biases that could explain some of the apparent differences in team size or diversity between rural and urban environments, or the differences between environments and technology categories in temporal patterns of invention rate. But we view this study as an initial and necessarily limited effort. We encourage further research, including extensions, additions and modifications to the database, alternative categorizations of attributes of inventors and environments, and applications of new statistical analyses. There are multiple rich opportunities for future work.

Materials and Methods

Database.

All coauthors contributed to the database. We conducted an extensive and intensive search to generate initial lists of possible inventions. We started from encyclopedic accounts such as (77). We also searched “invention” and “inventor” in Web of Science, Google Scholar, Wikipedia, and Google Search, and benefited from the extensive research undertaken by authors such as (78). We then filtered the initial lists and added information from different sources to come up with final entries in each of the seven technology categories by rigorously enforcing our own criteria–that the innovation resulting from the invention was transformative, meaning it was widely adopted and substantially changed the way people lived their lives and conducted their affairs or it was a pivotal step in the evolution, development, and deployment of subsequent technologies (e.g., as in 28). We tried to recognize the first practical design that became commercially successful, rather than subsequent minor modifications. A few of the inventions might be considered by others as not transformative, contributing to noise in statistical patterns. We emphasize that the database is not intended to be an exhaustive or comprehensive list of all inventions that experts might judge to be important.

For each invention and its resulting innovation, we determined the inventor(s) from published accounts and filled in the database with information on the inventor’s name(s), age(s), gender(s), and place of birth, and the invention’s date, location, and environment (rural, urban, or institutional). In the few cases of competing claims of independent discovery, we consulted additional sources for evidence to assign priority. In doing so we tried to determine whether the inventor worked primarily alone, in close association with other identifiable individuals, or as part of a large team of unidentified collaborators. We made use of ref. 79, but additional research was often required.

The resulting standardized, quantitative database has 417 entries representing a sampling of major inventions/innovations designed to be suitable for statistical analysis, namely differences between environments, time periods, and technology categories in invention rates and inventor and team characteristics.

Analysis.

The advantage of our macroecological approach is that collection and analysis of data for many equivalent “particles” can reveal very general, robust empirical patterns (16). Small differences in which data to include may add statistical noise but will not significantly alter the outcome of the analysis and the conclusions drawn. For clarity of presentation, we used simple, robust statistical and graphical methods: Pearson’s chi-squared tests, two-sample Poisson rate tests, Pearson correlation coefficients, and general linear models (GLMs) with time as a covariate, innovation category and environment as fixed factors, and each invention’s average inventor age as the response variable. For example, temporal trends are clearly seen by plotting data within 20-y or 50-y intervals or as cumulative values over the entire 300-y period. We aimed to avoid overreliance on significance levels and P-values in judging scientific results (80–82), so we report P-values and effect sizes and let readers judge for themselves the significance of the results. All data analysis was performed using Matlab R2021a, with the exception of GLMs, which were performed using Minitab version 20.2. To investigate differences between environments in per capita invention rates, we used published estimates of global rural and urban population sizes over time (83 and Dataset S2).
