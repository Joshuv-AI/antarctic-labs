Abstract
. Let 𝒯 \mathcal{T} denote a Galton–Watson tree with offspring distribution ξ \xi satisfying 𝔼 ⁡ ( ξ ) = 1 \mathbb{E}(\xi)=1 , and let 𝒯 n \mathcal{T}_{n} be the Galton–Watson tree conditioned to have exactly n n nodes. We show that, under a mild moment condition on ξ \xi , the number of occurrences of a fixed rooted plane tree 𝐭 \mathbf{t} as a general subtree in 𝒯 n \mathcal{T}_{n} is asymptotically normal as n → ∞ n\to\infty , with both mean and variance linear in n n . In addition, we prove that this limiting distribution is nondegenerate except for some special cases where the variance remains bounded. These results confirm a conjecture of Janson in recent work on the same topic. Finally, we present examples showing that if the proposed moment condition on ξ \xi is violated, the conclusion may fail.



1.
Introduction

Throughout this paper, all trees are rooted and ordered (or plane), i.e., the order of the root branches matters. Let
t
be a fixed tree, and for any other tree
T
, let
N_{\textbf{t}}(T)
denote the number of occurrences of
t
as a general subtree of
T
, and let
n_{\textbf{t}}(T)
denote the number of occurrences of
t
attached to the root of
T
. We use the same notation as in Janson
[
9
]
, where these parameters are rigorously defined; see
[
9
, Section 2]
.

Informally, a rooted tree
T
is drawn upside down, with the root as the highest node. An occurrence of a rooted tree
t
as a general subtree of
T
is an embedding of
t
into
T
(as rooted ordered trees) such that the image of the root of
t
is the highest node in its image in
T
, and for each node
v
of
t
, the degree of its image in
T
is greater than or equal to the degree of
v
in
t
.

The tree functional
N_{\textbf{t}}
satisfies the so-called
additive property
, with
n_{\textbf{t}}
as its associated
toll function
. That is, if a tree
T
has root branches
T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{d}
, then

N_{\textbf{t}}(T)=\sum_{k=1}^{d}N_{\textbf{t}}(T_{k})+n_{\textbf{t}}(T).

Subtree pattern counts are natural parameters to study in random trees, and they have been investigated in various forms throughout the literature. The most common and extensively studied pattern is the so-called
fringe-subtree pattern
. A fringe subtree of a tree
T
is the subtree induced by a node together with all its descendants. Results on fringe-subtree counts can be found, for example, in
[
1
,
8
]
and the references therein. Another type of subtree pattern—defined via tree embeddings in which the degrees of the internal nodes are preserved—was studied in
[
3
]
.

We only consider the Galton–Watson tree
\mathcal{T}
with a critical offspring distribution
\xi
satisfying the following standard conditions (which we assume to hold from now on without further mention):

•

\mathbb{P}(\xi=0)>1
,

•

\mathbb{E}(\xi)=1
, and

•

\gcd\{k\ :\ \mathbb{P}(\xi=k)>0\}=1
.

The first condition ensures that the Galton–Watson process can die out; the second is the criticality condition; and the third is required only to guarantee that
\mathbb{P}(|\mathcal{T}|=n)>0
for all sufficiently large
n
(where,
|T|
denotes the number of nodes in tree
T
). If the third condition is omitted and, for example,
\gcd\{k:\mathbb{P}(\xi=k)>0\}=d>1
, then one must restrict to
n\equiv 1\pmod{d}
. We shall always assume that
\mathrm{Var}(\xi)<\infty
, although this will follow from the assumptions imposed throughout the paper.

Under this setting, Janson
[
9
]
proved a law of large numbers for
N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, where
\mathcal{T}_{n}
is the Galton–Watson tree conditioned on having
n
nodes, with offspring distribution
\xi
satisfying
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{\Delta})<\infty
, and where
\Delta:=\Delta(\textbf{t})
denotes the maximum outdegree of the nodes in
t
. In the same paper (see
[
9
, Section 5]
), it was conjectured that, under an additional suitable moment condition on
\xi
, the random variable
N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
should satisfy a nondegenerate central limit theorem, except for a few exceptional cases. This has already been verified in the case where
\xi
is bounded; see
[
9
, Proposition 5.1]
. Here, we extend this result further.

Theorem 1
.

Let
\mathcal{T}_{n}
be the conditioned Galton–Watson tree with offspring distribution
\xi
, satisfying
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{2\Delta(\textbf{t})+1})<\infty
. Then, as
n\to\infty
, the mean and variance of
N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
satisfy the asymptotic estimates

\mathbb{E}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=\mu n+o(\sqrt{n})\quad\text{and}\quad\mathrm{Var}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=\gamma^{2}n+o(n),

where
\mu=\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}))
, and
\gamma\geq 0
is a constant. Moreover, if

\limsup_{n}\mathrm{Var}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=\infty,

then
\gamma\neq 0
, and

\frac{N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})-\mu n}{\gamma\sqrt{n}}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1).

An explicit expression for
\mu
, in terms of the degree sequence of
t
, can be found in
[
9
, Equation (3.2)]
. However, our method does not provide an explicit expression for the constant
\gamma
.

The proof of this theorem uses a truncation argument together with variance estimates. This approach was suggested by Janson in
[
9
]
, and it has already been applied to prove a general central limit theorem in
[
12
, Theorem 1]
. Although the toll function
n_{\textbf{t}}(\cdot)
is local for a fixed
t
, the result in
[
12
, Theorem 1]
does not apply in our setting because
n_{\textbf{t}}(\cdot)
fails to satisfy the condition stated in Equation (2) of that theorem when
t
has height at least
2
. Therefore, a different approach is required.

We remark that the condition
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{2\Delta+1})<\infty
is already strong enough to cover most classical families of random trees, including rooted labelled trees and plane trees; see
[
4
]
for further examples. In Section
5
, we show that this moment assumption is not far from optimal in the sense that if
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{2\Delta})=\infty
, then there exists a tree
t
with maximum outdegree
\Delta
for which the variance
\mathrm{Var}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))
grows superlinearly in
n
.

Throughout, asymptotic statements are taken as
n\to\infty
unless explicitly stated otherwise. We write
f(n)=O(g(n))
to mean that there exist constants
C>0
and
n_{0}
such that
|f(n)|\leq C\,|g(n)|
for all
n\geq n_{0}
. We write
f(n)=o(g(n))
if
f(n)/g(n)\to 0
, and
f(n)\sim g(n)
if
f(n)/g(n)\to 1
. We also use Vinogradov notation:
f(n)\ll g(n)
means
f(n)=O(g(n))
, and
f(n)\gg g(n)
means
g(n)\ll f(n)
.

2.
Preliminaries

In this section, we establish several auxiliary lemmas; the first is the most important. We also prove the mean estimate in Theorem
1
. Before stating our key lemma, let us first fix notation. For a tree
T
and a positive integer
M
, the
cut-off tree

T^{(M)}
is the subtree induced by the nodes at distance at most
M
from the root. The number of nodes at distance exactly
M
from the root in
T
is denoted by
w_{M}(T)
.

The infinite size-biased tree, also known as the Kesten tree
[
11
]
, is denoted by
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
. Its distribution can be defined locally by

\forall M,\ \forall T,\ \mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M)}=T\right)=w_{M}(T)\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{T}^{(M)}=T\right).

In particular, its root-degree distribution, denoted by
\hat{\xi}
, is defined by
\mathbb{P}(\hat{\xi}=k)=k\,\mathbb{P}(\xi=k)
. For a comprehensive background on Galton–Watson trees and many useful results, see
[
6
,
7
]
. Throughout, we follow the notation of
[
12
]
.

Lemma 2
.

Let
t
and
\textbf{t}^{\prime}
be fixed trees with maximum outdegree at most
\Delta
and height at most
M
. Then the following statements hold:

(a)

If
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{\Delta+2})<\infty
, then
\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T})\,|\mathcal{T}^{(M)}|)<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\,|\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M)}|)<\infty
.

(b)

If
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{\Delta+2})<\infty
, then
\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\,|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{(M)}|)=O(1)
as
n\to\infty
.

(c)

If
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{2\Delta+1})<\infty
, then
\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\,n_{\textbf{t}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=O(1)
as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

We prove these statements by induction on the height of
t
.

For the first statement of Part (a), assume
M=1
, i.e.,
t
is a star; suppose that
t
has
d\leq\Delta
leaves. Then

n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T})=\binom{\deg(\mathcal{T}^{(M)})}{d}\quad\text{and}\quad|\mathcal{T}^{(M)}|=1+\deg(\mathcal{T}^{(M)}),

where
\deg(\cdot)
denotes the root degree. Since
M=1
, the root degree
\deg(\mathcal{T}^{(M)})
has the same distribution as
\xi
. Taking expectations yields

\mathbb{E}\bigl(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T})\,|\mathcal{T}^{(M)}|\bigr)=\mathbb{E}\!\left((1+\xi)\binom{\xi}{d}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left((1+\xi)^{\Delta+1}\right),

which is finite because
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{\Delta+2})<\infty
implies
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{\Delta+1})<\infty
.

Similarly,

\mathbb{E}\bigl(n_{\textbf{t}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\,|\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M)}|\bigr)=\mathbb{E}\!\left((1+\hat{\xi})\binom{\hat{\xi}}{d}\right)\leq\mathbb{E}\!\left((1+\hat{\xi})^{\Delta+1}\right).

This expectation is finite provided
\mathbb{E}(\hat{\xi}^{\Delta+1})<\infty
. Since
\hat{\xi}
is the size-biased version of
\xi
(and
\mathbb{E}\xi=1
), we have

\mathbb{E}(\hat{\xi}^{\Delta+1})=\mathbb{E}(\xi^{\Delta+2})<\infty.

This establishes the base case
M=1
.

Now assume that
M>1
, and let
t_{1},t_{2},\dots,t_{d}
be the branches of
t
, ordered from left to right. Clearly, the maximum outdegrees and heights of these branches are bounded above by
\Delta
and
M-1
, respectively. Condition on the event
\deg(\mathcal{T})=k
, and let
T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{k}
be the branches of
\mathcal{T}
, also ordered from left to right. Then

n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T})\,|\mathcal{T}^{(M)}|=\left(1+\sum_{i=1}^{k}\bigl|T_{i}^{(M-1)}\bigr|\right)\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})}\prod_{j=1}^{d}n_{t_{j}}(T_{i_{j}}),

where the sum on the right-hand side is over all increasing
d
-tuples
(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})
with

1\leq i_{1}<\cdots<i_{d}\leq k
. Taking the conditional expectation yields

(1)

\mathbb{E}\!\left(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T})\,|\mathcal{T}^{(M)}|\,\middle|\,\deg(\mathcal{T})=k\right)=\mathbb{E}\!\left(\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})}\prod_{j=1}^{d}n_{t_{j}}(T_{i_{j}})\right)+\mathbb{E}\!\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq k\\
(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})\end{subarray}}\bigl|T_{i}^{(M-1)}\bigr|\prod_{j=1}^{d}n_{t_{j}}(T_{i_{j}})\right).

Since the
T_{i}
are independent copies of
\mathcal{T}
, the first term equals

\sum_{(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})}\prod_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\bigl(n_{t_{j}}(\mathcal{T})\bigr).

By the induction hypothesis,
\mathbb{E}\left(n_{t_{j}}(\mathcal{T})\right)
is finite for every
j
. So the above term is also finite. For the second term on the right-hand side of (
1
), two cases can occur:

•

If
T_{i}=T_{i_{j}}
for some
j
, say
j_{0}
, then

\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{d})\end{subarray}}\left|T_{i}^{(M-1)}\right|\prod_{j=1}^{d}n_{t_{j}}(T_{i_{j}})\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{d})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left(\left|T_{i}^{(M-1)}\right|n_{t_{j_{0}}}(T_{i}^{(M-1)})\right)\prod_{j\neq j_{0}}\mathbb{E}\left(n_{t_{j}}(T_{i_{j}})\right).

Since the
T_{j}
’s are independent copies of
\mathcal{T}
, the above is equal to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{d})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\mathcal{T}^{(M-1)}\right|n_{t_{j_{0}}}(\mathcal{T})\right)\prod_{j\neq j_{0}}\mathbb{E}\left(n_{t_{j}}(\mathcal{T})\right).

By the induction hypothesis, each factor is finite and independent of
k
, so this term is finite.

•

If
T_{i}\neq T_{i_{j}}
for any
j
, then the
T_{i_{j}}
’s, together with
T_{i}
, are independent of each other, and we have

\mathbb{E}\left(\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{d})\end{subarray}}\left|T_{i}^{(M-1)}\right|\prod_{j=1}^{d}n_{t_{j}}(T_{i_{j}})\right)=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{d})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left(\left|T_{i}^{(M-1)}\right|\right)\prod_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left(n_{t_{j}}(T_{i_{j}})\right),

which is equal to

\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{d})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left(\left|\mathcal{T}^{(M-1)}\right|\right)\prod_{j=1}^{d}\mathbb{E}\left(n_{t_{j}}(\mathcal{T})\right).

Once again, by the induction hypothesis, this is finite (note that
\mathbb{E}(|T_{i}^{(M-1)}|)
is finite by
[
8
, Lemma 3]
, and the other factors are finite by the induction hypothesis).

Combining the two cases, we have some constant terms summed over
i\in\{1,2,\dots,k\}
and
(i_{1},\dots,i_{d})
with the above properties. Thus, there exists a constant
C\geq 0
, independent of
k
, such that

\mathbb{E}\left(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T})|\mathcal{T}^{(M)}|\mid\deg(\mathcal{T})=k\right)\leq C(k+1)\binom{k}{d}\leq C(k+1)^{\Delta+1}.

Taking the expectation again, we get

\mathbb{E}\left(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T})|\mathcal{T}^{(M)}|\right)\leq C\mathbb{E}\left((\xi+1)^{\Delta+1}\right),

which we have already established to be finite. Hence, we are done for the first part of Statement (a).

The second part of Statement (a) can be handled in the same way. Indeed, conditioning on the event
\deg(\hat{\mathcal{T}})=k
, the tree
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
consists of a root attached to a copy of
\hat{\mathcal{T}}
and
k-1
copies of
\mathcal{T}
, and all these branches are independent of one another. The only difference is that, in this case, we may encounter terms such as
\mathbb{E}(n_{t_{j}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})|\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M-1)}|)
,
\mathbb{E}(|\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M-1)}|)
, and
\mathbb{E}(n_{t_{j}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}}))
. However, these terms are all finite by the induction hypothesis. Thus, we deduce that there exists a constant
C^{\prime}\geq 0
such that

\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})|\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M)}|)\leq C^{\prime}\mathbb{E}\left((\hat{\xi}+1)^{\Delta+1}\right),

which is finite, as we have seen before. Therefore, the proof of Part (a) is complete.

For Part (b), we also proceed by induction in a similar fashion. So, for
M=1
, (assuming that
t
has
d\leq\Delta
leaves) we have

n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})=\binom{\deg(\mathcal{T}_{n})}{d}
and
|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{(M)}|=1+\deg(\mathcal{T}_{n})
. Hence,

\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{(M)}|)\leq\mathbb{E}\left((1+\deg(\mathcal{T}_{n}))^{\Delta+1}\right)=O(\mathbb{E}\,w_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{n})^{\Delta+1}).

Applying
[
6
, Lemma 2.2]
, we deduce that the above is a bounded term.

Now assume that
M>1
, and condition on the event that
\deg(\mathcal{T}_{n})=k
and that the branches
T_{1},T_{2},\dots,T_{k}
(ordered from left to right) of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
have sizes
n_{1},n_{2},\dots,n_{k}
, respectively. Then, these branches of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
are
k
independent copies of
\mathcal{T}_{n_{1}},\mathcal{T}_{n_{2}},\dots,\mathcal{T}_{n_{k}}
. Using the same argument as in the proof above, we have

\mathbb{E}(n_{t_{j}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{(M-1)}|)=O(1),

for any branch
t_{j}
of
t
, by the induction hypothesis. Hence, we deduce that

\mathbb{E}\left(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{(M)}|\,\Big|\,\deg(\mathcal{T}_{n})=k\;\wedge\;(|T_{1}|=n_{1},|T_{2}|=n_{2},\dots,|T_{k}|=n_{k})\right)=O((1+k)^{\Delta+1}).

Taking the expectation again, we obtain

\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{(M)}|)=O\left(\mathbb{E}(1+\deg(\mathcal{T}_{n}))^{\Delta+1}\right)=O(1).

The proof of Part (b) is complete.

For Part (c), we also proceed by induction. If
M=1
, i.e.,
t
and
\textbf{t}^{\prime}
are both stars (with
d
and
d^{\prime}
leaves respectively), then we have

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})n_{\textbf{t}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(\binom{\deg(\mathcal{T}_{n})}{d}\binom{\deg(\mathcal{T}_{n})}{d^{\prime}}\right)

\displaystyle\leq\mathbb{E}\left(\deg(\mathcal{T}_{n})^{2\Delta}\right)

\displaystyle=\mathbb{E}\left(w_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{n})^{2\Delta}\right),

which is bounded above by a constant, see
[
6
, Lemma 2.2]
, under the assumption that
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{2\Delta+1})<\infty
.
For
M>1
, let
t_{1},\,t_{2},\dots,\,t_{d}
be the branches of
t
and
t^{\prime}_{1},\,t^{\prime}_{2},\,\dots,\,t^{\prime}_{q}
the branches of
\textbf{t}^{\prime}
. As before, we condition on the event that
\deg(\mathcal{T}_{n})=k
, with
T_{1},\,T_{2},\,\dots,\,T_{k}
denoting the branches of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
, and let
n_{i}=|T_{i}|
for
1\leq i\leq k
. Then, we have

n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})=\sum_{(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{d})}\prod_{j=1}^{d}n_{t_{j}}(T_{i_{j}})

n_{\textbf{t}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})=\sum_{(i^{\prime}_{1},i^{\prime}_{2},\dots,i^{\prime}_{q})}\prod_{j=1}^{q}n_{t^{\prime}_{j}}(T_{i^{\prime}_{j}})

where the summations are over
(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{d})
and
(i^{\prime}_{1},i^{\prime}_{2},\dots,i^{\prime}_{q})
(arranged in ascending order), defined similarly as before. Thus,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})n_{\textbf{t}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\,\middle|\,\deg(\mathcal{T}_{n})=k\wedge(|T_{1}|=n_{1},|T_{2}|=n_{2},\dots,|T_{k}|=n_{k})\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}(i_{1},i_{2},\dots,i_{d})\\
(i^{\prime}_{1},i^{\prime}_{2},\dots,i^{\prime}_{q})\end{subarray}}\mathbb{E}\left(\prod_{j=1}^{d}n_{t_{j}}(T_{i_{j}})\prod_{\ell=1}^{q}n_{t^{\prime}_{\ell}}(T_{i^{\prime}_{\ell}})\right)

Since the branches are independent, the expectations that appear in the product above are of the form
\mathbb{E}\,n_{t_{j}}(\mathcal{T}_{n_{i_{j}}})
,
\mathbb{E}\,n_{t^{\prime}_{\ell}}(\mathcal{T}_{n_{i^{\prime}_{\ell}}})
, or
\mathbb{E}\left(n_{t_{j}}(\mathcal{T}_{n_{i_{j}}})n_{t^{\prime}_{\ell}}(\mathcal{T}_{n_{i_{j}}})\right)
, each of which is bounded (by the induction hypothesis). Hence,

\mathbb{E}\left(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})n_{\textbf{t}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\,\middle|\,\deg(\mathcal{T}_{n})=k\wedge(|T_{1}|=n_{1},|T_{2}|=n_{2},\dots,|T_{k}|=n_{k})\right)=O\left(\binom{k}{d}\binom{k}{q}\right)

Therefore,

\displaystyle\mathbb{E}\left(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})n_{\textbf{t}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\mathbb{E}\left(\binom{\deg(\mathcal{T}_{n})}{d}\binom{\deg(\mathcal{T}_{n})}{q}\right)\right)

\displaystyle=O\left(\mathbb{E}\,\deg(\mathcal{T}_{n})^{2\Delta}\right),

which is already known to be bounded under our assumption, so the proof of the lemma is complete.
∎

We now present the first application of Lemma
2
, which is a version
[
9
, Lemma 3.5]
with an explicit bound on the error term but under a slightly stronger condition on
\xi
.

Lemma 3
.

Assume that
\xi
satisfies
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{\Delta+2})<\infty
. Then, we have

(2)

|\mathbb{E}n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})-\mathbb{E}n_{\textbf{t}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})|=O(n^{-1/2}).

Proof.

First, by definition, we have

\displaystyle\left|\mathbb{E}n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})-\mathbb{E}n_{\textbf{t}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\right|

\displaystyle=\left|\sum_{T}n_{\textbf{t}}(T)\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{(M)}=T\right)-\sum_{T}n_{\textbf{t}}(T)\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M)}=T\right)\right|

\displaystyle\leq S_{1}+S_{2},

where

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{|T|\leq n/2}n_{\textbf{t}}(T)\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{(M)}=T\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M)}=T\right)\right|,

\displaystyle S_{2}

\displaystyle:=\sum_{|T|>n/2}n_{\textbf{t}}(T)\left|\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{(M)}=T\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M)}=T\right)\right|.

To estimate
S_{1}
, we make use of Equation (5.42) in the proof of
[
8
, Lemma 5.9]
. For
|T|\leq n/2
, the result states that

(3)

\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{(M)}=T\right)-\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M)}=T\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M)}=T\right)\cdot O\left(\frac{|T|}{n^{1/2}}\right).

Hence,

\displaystyle S_{1}

\displaystyle=\sum_{|T|\leq n/2}n_{\textbf{t}}(T)\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M)}=T\right)\cdot O\left(\frac{|T|}{n^{1/2}}\right)

\displaystyle\ll n^{-1/2}\sum_{T}n_{\textbf{t}}(T)|T|\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M)}=T\right)

\displaystyle\ll n^{-1/2}\mathbb{E}\left(n_{\textbf{t}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})|\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M)}|\right).

The latter is bounded by
O(n^{-1/2})
by Part (a) of Lemma
2
.

Next, we estimate
S_{2}
. We trivially have

S_{2}\leq\sum_{|T|>n/2}n_{\textbf{t}}(T)\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{(M)}=T\right)+\sum_{|T|>n/2}n_{\textbf{t}}(T)\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M)}=T\right).

We rewrite the first summation on the right-hand side as follows:

\displaystyle\sum_{|T|>n/2}n_{\textbf{t}}(T)\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{T}^{(M)}=T\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}k\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{T}^{(M)}|>\tfrac{n}{2}\wedge n_{\textbf{t}}(T)=k\right)

\displaystyle=\sum_{k\geq 0}k\mathbb{P}\left(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}^{(M)})=k\right)\cdot\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{T}^{(M)}|>\tfrac{n}{2}\mid n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}^{(M)})=k\right).

By Markov’s inequality, we obtain

\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{T}^{(M)}|>\tfrac{n}{2}\mid n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}^{(M)})=k\right)\leq\frac{2}{n}\mathbb{E}\left(|\mathcal{T}^{(M)}|\mid n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}^{(M)})=k\right).

Therefore,

\displaystyle\sum_{|T|>n/2}n_{\textbf{t}}(T)\mathbb{P}\left(\mathcal{T}^{(M)}=T\right)

\displaystyle\leq\frac{2}{n}\sum_{k\geq 0}k\mathbb{P}\left(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}^{(M)})=k\right)\mathbb{E}\left(|\mathcal{T}^{(M)}|\mid n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}^{(M)})=k\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\mathbb{E}\left(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}^{(M)})\cdot\mathbb{E}\left(|\mathcal{T}^{(M)}|\mid n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}^{(M)})\right)\right)

\displaystyle=\frac{2}{n}\mathbb{E}\left(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}^{(M)})|\mathcal{T}^{(M)}|\right)

\displaystyle=O(n^{-1}),

where we used Lemma
2
in the final step.

Similarly, by the same argument, we obtain

\sum_{|T|>n/2}n_{\textbf{t}}(T)\mathbb{P}\left(\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M)}=T\right)\leq\frac{2}{n}\mathbb{E}\left(n_{\textbf{t}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M)})|\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M)}|\right)=O(n^{-1}).

Thus, we conclude that
S_{1}+S_{2}=O(n^{-1/2})
, completing the proof of the lemma.
∎

The next lemma gives an asymptotic estimate of the expectation
\mathbb{E}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))
.

Lemma 4
.

Assume that
\xi
satisfies
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{\Delta+2})<\infty
. Then, we have

\mathbb{E}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=\mu n+o(\sqrt{n})

as
n\to\infty
, where
\mu=\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}))
.

Proof.

Consider the centered toll function
f(T)=n_{\textbf{t}}(T)-\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}}))
. The associated additive functional is then given by

F(T)=N_{\textbf{t}}(T)-|T|\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})).

We trivially have

n_{\textbf{t}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\leq n_{\textbf{t}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\,|\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M)}|\quad\text{and}\quad n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T})\leq n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T})\,|\mathcal{T}^{(M)}|.

On the other hand, by Lemma
2
, both
\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T})\,|\mathcal{T}^{(M)}|)
and
\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})\,|\hat{\mathcal{T}}^{(M)}|)
are finite. Therefore, we conclude that
\mathbb{E}|f(\mathcal{T})|
is also finite.

Moreover, by Lemma
3
, we have

(4)

\mathbb{E}\left|f(\mathcal{T}_{n})\right|=\mathbb{E}\left|n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})-\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}}))\right|=O(n^{-1/2}).

In particular, the first part of
[
8
, Theorem 1.5]
applies, and as a consequence we obtain

\mathbb{E}\left(F(\mathcal{T}_{n})\right)=n\,\mathbb{E}f(\mathcal{T})+o(\sqrt{n}).

Writing
F(\mathcal{T}_{n})
in terms of
N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
, we deduce that

\mathbb{E}N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})-n\,\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}}))=n\,\mathbb{E}f(\mathcal{T})+o(\sqrt{n}),

which is equivalent to the equation stated in the lemma.
∎

Next, we shift our attention to the variance. As we will see in the next section, the proof of the central limit theorem relies on a truncation lemma, which is mainly based on convergence in
L^{2}
. Therefore, estimating the variance plays a crucial role in the rest of the proof. We will need to consider the truncated version of our toll function. To that end, we define for every
p,q\in\mathbb{N}\cup\{\infty\}
the additive functional
F_{p,q}
associated with the following toll function:

f_{p,q}(T)=\begin{cases}n_{\textbf{t}}(T)\ &\text{ if }\ p\leq|T|<q\\
0\ &\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

Observe that, in particular, we can write our original functional
N_{\textbf{t}}
as
F_{1,\infty}
. Furthermore, for each positive integer
k
, let

\mu_{k}:=\mathbb{E}n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{k}).

Then, we define
\tilde{F}_{p,q}
to be the “centered” additive functional with the toll function

\tilde{f}_{p,q}(T)=\begin{cases}n_{\textbf{t}}(T)-\mu_{|T|}\ &\text{ if }\ p\leq|T|<q\\
0\ &\text{otherwise}.\end{cases}

We have the following lemma.

Lemma 5
.

Assume that
\xi
satisfies
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{2\Delta+1})<\infty
and let
\tilde{F}_{p,q}
and
\tilde{f}_{p,q}
be as defined above. Then, we have

(5)

\mathbb{E}\big(\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\tilde{F}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\big)=O(1),

as
n\to\infty
, where the implied constant is independent of
p
and
q
.

Proof.

We can assume that
p\leq n<q
; otherwise, the expression inside the expectation equals zero, and there is nothing to prove. By definition

\tilde{F}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n})=\sum_{v\in\mathcal{T}_{n}}\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n,v}),

where
\mathcal{T}_{n,v}
denotes the fringe subtree of
\mathcal{T}_{n}
rooted at
v
. The strategy is to split the sum according to the distance
d(v)
from
v
to the root. So, we define

\Sigma_{1}:=\sum_{d(v)<M}\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n,v}),\ \text{and }\ \Sigma_{2}:=\sum_{d(v)\geq M}\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n,v}).

Let us look at the contribution from
\Sigma_{1}
first. Observe that

\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\Sigma_{1}\right)=\sum_{\ell=0}^{M-1}\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\sum_{d(v)=\ell}\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n,v})\right).

For each
\ell
, let
t_{1},\ t_{2},\cdots,\ t_{d}
be the branches of
t
rooted at nodes at level
\ell
. Conditioning on
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{(\ell)}
, let
T_{1},\ T_{2},\cdots,T_{k}
be the branches
\mathcal{T}_{n}
rooted at the nodes at level
\ell
. Further, we also condition on the sizes of
T_{1},\ T_{2},\cdots T_{k}
, say
n_{1},\ n_{2},\cdots,n_{k}
. Thus, we have

\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\sum_{d(v)=\ell}\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n,v})\Big|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{(\ell)}=T\wedge(\forall i\ (|T_{i}|=n_{i}))\right)=

(6)

\mathbb{E}\left(\left(\sum_{\pi}\prod_{j=1}^{d}n_{t_{j}}(T_{\pi_{j}})-\mu_{n}\right)\sum_{\begin{subarray}{c}1\leq i\leq k\\
p\leq n_{i}<q\end{subarray}}\left(n_{\textbf{t}}(T_{i})-\mu_{n_{i}}\right)\right),

where the first summation inside the expectation is over possible embeddings
\pi
of
\textbf{t}^{(\ell)}
into
T^{(\ell)}
, and for each embedding
\pi
, the node ranked
j
-th at level
\ell
in
\textbf{t}^{(\ell)}
is mapped to the node ranked
\pi_{j}
in
T^{(\ell)}
.

We know from Lemma
2
that
(\mu_{k})_{k\geq 1}
is a bounded sequence. Expanding the term inside the expectation (
6
) and using the fact the
T_{i}
’s are independent copies of conditioned Galton–Watson trees, the expectations of the non-constant terms are of the form
\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n_{i}}))
,
\mathbb{E}(n_{t_{j}}(\mathcal{T}_{n_{i}}))
or
\mathbb{E}(n_{t_{j}}(\mathcal{T}_{n_{i}})n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n_{i}}))
; these are all bounded terms according to Lemma
2
. Hence

\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\sum_{d(v)=\ell}\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n,v})\Big|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{(\ell)}=T\wedge(\forall i\ (|T_{i}|=n_{i}))\right)=O\left(w_{\ell}(T)n_{\textbf{t}^{(\ell)}}(T^{(\ell)})\right)

Taking the expectation again and summing over
\ell\in\{0,1,2,\cdots,M-1\}
, (noting that
w_{(\ell)}(T)\leq|T^{(\ell)}|
) we deduce from Lemma
2
that

\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\Sigma_{1}\right)=O\left(\sum_{\ell=0}^{M-1}\mathbb{E}\left(n_{\textbf{t}^{(\ell)}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{(\ell)}|\right)\right)=O(1).

We take the same approach for the contribution from
\Sigma_{2}
, and we have

\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\Sigma_{2}\right)=\sum_{\ell\geq M}\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\sum_{d(v)=\ell}\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n,v})\right).

However, we know that the height of
t
is
M
. Hence, for
\ell\geq M
,
\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
is completely determined by
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{(\ell)}
. Thus,

\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\sum_{d(v)=\ell}\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n,v})\Big|\mathcal{T}_{n}^{(\ell)}=T\wedge(\forall i\ (|T_{i}|=n_{i}))\right)=\tilde{f}_{p,q}(T)\sum_{i=1}^{k}\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n_{i}}))=0.

Therefore, we deduce using the law of total expectation that
\mathbb{E}\left(\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\Sigma_{2}\right)=0
. Putting all contributions together proves the lemma.
∎

We are now ready to prove the linear bound on the variance. Let us state the result.

Lemma 6
.

Assume that
\xi
satisfies
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{2\Delta+1})<\infty
and let
F_{p,q}
be as defined previously. Then, there exists a function
\varepsilon(p)
that is independent of
q
and
n
, such that

\mathrm{Var}(F_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))\leq\varepsilon(p)n.

Moreover,
\varepsilon(p)\to 0
as
p\to\infty
.

Proof.

We begin by decomposing the toll function
f_{p,q}
into two parts

f_{p,q}(T)=\tilde{f}_{p,q}(T)+f_{p,q}^{(1)}(T),
where
f_{p,q}^{(1)}(T)=\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{|T|}))=\mu_{|T|}
. What is important to notice in this decomposition is that
\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{k}))=0
for every
k
, and
f_{p,q}^{(1)}(T)
depends only on the size of
T
.

The above decomposition of the toll function leads to a decomposition of the corresponding additive function
F_{p,q}
as follows

F_{p,q}(T)=\tilde{F}_{p,q}{(T)}+F_{p,q}^{(1)}(T).

Let us first deal with the functional
F_{p,q}^{(1)}(T)
whose toll function
f_{p,q}^{(1)}(T)
depends only on the size of
T
. We trivially have

\mathbb{E}f_{p,q}^{(1)}(\mathcal{T}_{n})=\begin{cases}\mu_{n}\ &\text{if }\ p\leq n<q\\
0\ &\text{otherwise }.\end{cases}

We have already established from Lemma
3
that
\mu_{n}=\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}}))+O(n^{-1/2})
. The term
\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\hat{\mathcal{T}}))
is a constant which we shall denote by
\theta
. For an additive functional whose toll function depends only on the size of the tree, one can make use of
[
8
, Theorem 6.7]
. When applied to
F_{p,q}^{(1)}
, we obtain

(7)

\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Var}(F_{p,q}^{(1)}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Var}\left(F_{p,q}^{(1)}(\mathcal{T}_{n})-\theta n\right)\ll\left(\sup_{p\leq k<q}|\mu_{k}-\theta|+\sum_{p\leq k<q}\frac{|\mu_{k}-\theta|}{k}\right)^{2}.

By the asymptotic estimate of
\mu_{k}
, we have

(8)

\sup_{p\leq k<q}|\mu_{k}-\theta|+\sum_{p\leq k<q}\frac{|\mu_{k}-\theta|}{k}\ll\sup_{k\geq p}k^{-1/2}+\sum_{k\geq p}k^{-3/2}.

For
\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{F}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))
, as in
[
12
, Equation (38)]
which makes use of
[
8
, Equation (6.28)]
, we obtain

\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{F}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))\ll\sum_{p\leq k\leq n}\frac{n^{1/2}}{(n-k+1)^{1/2}k^{3/2}}\left|\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{k})\tilde{F}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{k}))\right|.

We have already established in Lemma
5
that
|\mathbb{E}(\tilde{f}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{k})\tilde{F}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{k}))|=O(1)
where the right-hand side is independent of
p,q
and
k
. Moreover, we have

\displaystyle\sum_{p\leq k\leq n}\frac{n^{1/2}}{(n-k+1)^{1/2}k^{3/2}}

\displaystyle=\sum_{p\leq k\leq n/2}\frac{n^{1/2}}{(n-k+1)^{1/2}k^{3/2}}+\sum_{n/2<k\leq n}\frac{n^{1/2}}{(n-k+1)^{1/2}k^{3/2}}

\displaystyle<2^{1/2}\sum_{p\leq k\leq n/2}\frac{1}{k^{3/2}}+\frac{2^{3/2}}{n}\sum_{1\leq k<1+n/2}\frac{1}{k^{1/2}}

\displaystyle\ll\sum_{k\geq p}\frac{1}{k^{3/2}}+\frac{1}{p^{1/2}},

provided that
p\leq n
, otherwise, the sum would be zero. Therefore, we deduce that

(9)

\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{F}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=O\left(\sum_{k\geq p}\frac{1}{k^{3/2}}+\frac{1}{p^{1/2}}\right),

where the right side is a function of
p
only that tends zero as
p\to\infty
. To complete the proof of the lemma, we use the Minkowski inequality, which gives

\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(F_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))}\leq\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{F}_{p,q}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))}+\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(F_{p,q}^{(1)}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))}.

The lemma follows easily by combining Equations (
7
)–(
9
).
∎

Observe that if we set
p=1
and
q=\infty
, then Lemma
6
shows that
\mathrm{Var}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=O(n)
. We prove the central limit theorem in the next section.

3.
Proof of the central limit theorem

The key ingredient in the proof of the central limit theorem is the use of the so-called truncation argument, which is based on a classical result in probability theory; see, for example,
[
2
, Theorem 4.2]
and
[
10
, Theorem 4.28]
. This technique is quite effective in proving central limit theorems for parameters of random trees; it has been used several times in the past, see for example
[
5
,
13
,
12
]
. Since the argument is based on
L^{2}
-convergence, we also obtain a convergence in variance as a bonus. The idea is summarised in the next lemma whose statement was taken from
[
12
]
.

Lemma 7
.

Let
(X_{n})_{n\geq 1}
and
(W_{p,n})_{p,n\geq 1}
be sequences of centred random variables. If for some random variables
W_{p}
,
p=1,2,\dots
, and
W
we have

•

W_{p,n}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\to}_{n}W_{p}
for every
p\geq 1
, and
W_{p}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\to}_{p}W,

•

\mathrm{Var}(X_{n}-W_{p,n})=O(\sigma^{2}_{p})
uniformly in
n
, and
\sigma^{2}_{p}\to_{p}0
,

then

X_{n}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\to}_{n}W.

If we assume further that
X_{n}
,
W_{p,n}
,
W_{p}
, and
W
have finite second moments, for every
n,p\geq 1
, and

•

\mathrm{Var}W_{p,n}{\to}_{n}\mathrm{Var}W_{p}
, and
\mathrm{Var}W_{p}{\to}_{p}\mathrm{Var}W,
,

then we also have
\mathrm{Var}X_{n}{\to}_{n}\mathrm{Var}W.

We are now ready to prove the central limit theorem in our main theorem.

Proof of the CLT in Theorem
1
.

We apply the above lemma with

\displaystyle X_{n}=n^{-1/2}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})-\mathbb{E}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})),\ \text{and}\ W_{p,n}=n^{-1/2}(F_{1,p}(\mathcal{T}_{n})-\mathbb{E}(F_{1,p}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))).

It is clear that

X_{n}-W_{p,n}=n^{-1/2}(F_{p,\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{n})-\mathbb{E}(F_{p,\infty}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))).

Hence, by Lemma
6
,
\mathrm{Var}(X_{n}-W_{p,n})\leq\varepsilon(p)
which tends to 0 as
p\to\infty
. Thus, the second condition in Lemma
7
is satisfied.

On the other hand, for every fixed
p
the toll function
f_{1,p}
associated with
F_{1,p}
is bounded and local, so by
[
8
, Theorem 1.13]
, there exists
\gamma^{2}_{p}\geq 0
such that
W_{p,n}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\to}_{n}\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma^{2}_{p})
and
\mathrm{Var}W_{p,n}\to_{n}\gamma^{2}_{p}
.

To verify that the first condition in Lemma
7
is satisfied, we need to show that the sequence
(\gamma^{2}_{p})_{p}
is convergent. To achieve this, notice that for
p\leq q
, the additive functional
F_{1,q}-F_{1,p}=F_{p,q}
. Therefore, by Lemma
6
, we obtain

|\gamma_{q}-\gamma_{p}|^{2}\leq\lim_{n\to\infty}\mathrm{Var}(W_{q,n}-W_{p,n})\leq\varepsilon(p).

This implies that
(\gamma^{2}_{p})_{p}
is a Cauchy sequence, hence it is convergent. Thus, there exists
\gamma\geq 0
such that
\gamma_{p}^{2}\to\gamma^{2}
as
p\to\infty
and the corresponding
W
in this case satisfies

W\sim\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma^{2}).

Furthermore, we also deduce that
\mathrm{Var}(X_{n})\to\gamma^{2}
as
n\to\infty
.
∎

4.
Non-degeneracy

In the previous section, we proved that, under the conditions of Theorem
1
on
t
and
\xi
,

\frac{N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})-\mu n}{\sqrt{n}}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,\gamma^{2})\ \text{ and }\ \mathrm{Var}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=\gamma^{2}n+o(n),

as
n\to\infty
. These results are of limited significance if
\gamma
vanishes. So, in this section, we look at the cases in which this can happen. It is clear that
\gamma
is a function of
t
and
\xi
, and we want to provide a set of conditions on
t
and
\xi
which guarantee that
\gamma
does not vanish. Note that it is not required in this problem that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}=\textbf{t})\neq 0
.

Assumption 1
.

The pair
(\textbf{t},\xi)
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
1
and there exist trees
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
such that

(A1)

\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}=\tau_{i})>0
for
i\in\{1,2\}
,

(A2)

|\tau_{1}|=|\tau_{2}|
,

(A3)

\tau_{1}^{(M-1)}=\tau_{2}^{(M-1)}
(as rooted plane trees)

(A4)

N_{\textbf{t}}(\tau_{1})\neq N_{\textbf{t}}(\tau_{2})
,

where
M
is the height of the tree
t
.

Proposition 8
.

Assume that the pair
(\textbf{t},\xi)
satisfies Assumption
1
, then
\gamma>0
, i.e,

\frac{N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})-\mu n}{\gamma\sqrt{n}}\overset{\mathrm{d}}{\to}\mathcal{N}(0,1)\ \text{ and }\ \mathrm{Var}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))\sim\gamma^{2}n,

as
n\to\infty
.

Proof.

The proof is based on a method first applied in
[
12
, Subsection 6.1]
. Consider the structure
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{*}
obtained from
\mathcal{T}_{n}
after deleting every occurrence of
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
as fringe subtrees, while marking the nodes to which these copies were attached. Let
n^{*}
denote the number of these marked nodes. By the law of total variance

\mathrm{Var}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))\geq\mathbb{E}\left(\mathrm{Var}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\ |\ \mathcal{T}_{n}^{*})\right).

Let us estimate the right-hand side. Under Assumption
1
, define

\delta:=N_{\textbf{t}}(\tau_{1})-N_{\textbf{t}}(\tau_{2})\quad\text{and}\quad p_{i}:=\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}=\tau_{i})\quad\text{for each }i\in\{1,2\}.

Note that
\delta\neq 0
, and
p_{1},p_{2}>0
. To construct
\mathcal{T}_{n}
from
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{*}
, we reattach either
\tau_{1}
or
\tau_{2}
to each marked node of
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{*}
. At each marked node, the probability of attaching
\tau_{i}
is given by

\frac{p_{i}}{p_{1}+p_{2}},\quad\text{for }i\in\{1,2\}.

Furthermore, the choices of attaching
\tau_{1}
or
\tau_{2}
at different marked nodes are independent. Observe also that, given
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{*}
, the only variation in
N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
comes from the occurrences of
t
in those copies of
\tau_{i}
; this is because of Statement (A3) in Assumption
1
. Thus, conditioning on
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{*}
, the variable
N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
can be decomposed as follows:

N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})=h(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{*})+\delta\cdot Y_{n},

where the variable
h(\mathcal{T}_{n}^{*})
is
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{*}
–measurable, and the conditional distribution of
Y_{n}
given
\mathcal{T}_{n}^{*}
is the binomial
B(n^{*},p)
, where
p=p_{1}/(p_{1}+p_{2})
. Hence, we deduce that

\mathrm{Var}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\ |\ \mathcal{T}_{n}^{*})=\frac{\delta^{2}p_{1}p_{2}}{(p_{1}+p_{2})^{2}}n^{*}.

On the other hand,
n^{*}
is the number of occurrences of
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
as a fringe subtree in
\mathcal{T}_{n}
. Hence, we have
\mathbb{E}(n^{*})\sim(p_{1}+p_{2})n
, see for example
[
8
, Theorem 1.3]
. Therefore,

\mathbb{E}\left(\mathrm{Var}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\ |\ \mathcal{T}_{n}^{*})\right)\sim\frac{\delta^{2}p_{1}p_{2}}{p_{1}+p_{2}}n.

Finally, we obtain
\mathrm{Var}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))\gg n
, which proves that
\gamma>0
.
∎

Proposition 9
.

Assume that the pair
(\textbf{t},\xi)
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
1
and such that
\gamma=0
, then there exist a function
g:\mathbb{N}\to\mathbb{R}
and constants
\alpha_{1},\alpha_{2},\ \ldots
, such that

N_{\textbf{t}}(T)=g(|T|)+\sum_{i}\alpha_{i}n_{\textbf{t}_{i}}(T)

for all
T
with
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}=T)>0
, where
\textbf{t}_{1},\ \textbf{t}_{2},\ \ldots
are the fringe subtrees of
t
.

Proof.

We choose a fixed tree
\tau
with height
M-1
, such that
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}=\tau)>0
. For each tree
T
with
\mathbb{P}(\mathcal{T}=T)>0
, let
\tau(T)
be the tree obtained from attaching
T
to the left-most leaf of
\tau
at level
M-1
. Since, we are assuming that
\gamma=0
, by Proposition
8
,
N_{\textbf{t}}(\tau(T))
depends only on the size of
T
otherwise it would be possible to construct the trees
\tau_{1}
and
\tau_{2}
in Assumption
1
, and contradicting the assumption that
\gamma=0
.

On the other hand, by construction
N_{\textbf{t}}(\tau(T))
equals
N_{\textbf{t}}(T)
plus a linear combination of
n_{\textbf{t}_{i}}(T)
’s where the coefficients depends on
t
and
\tau
only.
∎

Note that, in Proposition
9
, most of the coefficients
\alpha_{i}
are in fact zero. For example, given a fringe subtree
\textbf{t}_{i}
of
t
with height at least
1
, the coefficient
\alpha_{i}
vanishes unless the root of
\textbf{t}_{i}
is the only node at that level in
t
that is not a leaf.

Corollary 10
.

Assume that the pair
(\textbf{t},\xi)
satisfies the conditions of Theorem
1
and such that
\gamma=0
, then

\mathrm{Var}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=O(1)\quad\text{ as }\quad n\to\infty.

Proof.

This follows easily from Proposition
9
and Part (c) of Lemma
2
.
∎

There are nontrivial degenerate cases. For example, taking
t
to be a path of fixed length already yields degeneracies. Additional examples arise if the
\gcd
-condition on
\xi
is relaxed; see
[
9
]
for a detailed discussion. Further special cases also occur if one counts occurrences of subtrees from a finite collection of trees, rather than a single tree
t
. We do not pursue this direction in this work.

5.
The moment assumption in Theorem
1

The central limit theorem stated in Theorem
1
is guaranteed by the assumption that
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{2\Delta+1})
is finite. This assumption is natural, as such a moment condition is necessary to make sure that
\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\,n_{\textbf{t}^{\prime}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))
remains bounded for any two trees
t
and
\textbf{t}^{\prime}
, each having maximum degree at most
\Delta
, as shown in Part (c) of Lemma
7
. However, it is not clear whether Theorem
1
would still hold if this condition were not satisfied. While it is difficult to answer this completely, we provide two examples which indicate that this moment condition is not too far from the optimal condition that guarantees the full statement of Theorem
1
.

Example 11

(
t
is a path)
.

Let us consider the case where
t
is a path of length
2
, i.e., we can take
\Delta=1
. Then, as shown in Janson
[
9
]
,

N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})=n-w_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{n})-1\quad\text{ and }\quad\mathrm{Var}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=\mathrm{Var}(w_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{n})).

Now, if we assume that
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{3})=\infty
but
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{3-\epsilon})<\infty
for some
\epsilon\in(0,\tfrac{1}{2})
, then
\mathbb{E}(w_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))\to\mathbb{E}(w_{1}(\hat{\mathcal{T}}))=\mathbb{E}(\xi^{2})<\infty
. However, using (
3
), we have

\mathbb{E}(w_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{n})^{2})\gg\sum_{k\leq\sqrt[3]{n}}k^{2}\mathbb{P}(w_{1}(\hat{\mathcal{T}})=k)=\sum_{k\leq\sqrt[3]{n}}k^{3}\mathbb{P}(\xi=k)\to\infty.

Similarly, using (
3
) again, we obtain the upper bound

\mathbb{E}(w_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{n})^{2})\ll\sqrt{n}\sum_{k\leq\sqrt{n}}k^{2}\mathbb{P}(\xi=k)+n^{(2\epsilon+1)/2}\sum_{\sqrt{n}<k<n/2}k^{3-\epsilon}\mathbb{P}(\xi=k)+n^{2}\mathbb{P}\left(w_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq n/2\right).

The first two terms on the right-hand side are both
o(n)
. For the third term, observe that

\mathbb{P}\left(w_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq n/2\right)\leq\frac{\mathbb{P}\left(w_{1}(\mathcal{T})\geq n/2\right)}{\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{T}|=n\right)}\ll n^{3/2}\mathbb{P}\left(w_{1}(\mathcal{T})\geq n/2\right),

and by Markov’s inequality, we have

\mathbb{P}\left(w_{1}(\mathcal{T})\geq n/2\right)=\mathbb{P}\left(w_{1}(\mathcal{T})^{3-\epsilon}\geq(n/2)^{3-\epsilon}\right)\ll n^{-3+\epsilon}.

Hence, we also have

n^{2}\mathbb{P}\left(w_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq n/2\right)\ll n^{(2\epsilon+1)/2}=o(n).

Therefore, we deduce that the variance
\mathrm{Var}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))
is not linear in
n
, but it tends to infinity. Together with the fact that
w_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{n})\geq 0
and
\mathbb{E}(w_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=O(1)
, this implies that the fluctuations of
N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n})
cannot be Gaussian.

Example 12

(
t
is a star)
.

Here, we let
t
be a star with
\Delta
leaves where
\Delta\geq 3
. Now assume that
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{\Delta+2})<\infty
and
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{2\Delta})=\infty
. As in the proof of Lemma
6
, we split the toll function as

n_{\textbf{t}}=\tilde{f}+f^{(1)},

where
f^{(1)}(T)=\mu_{|T|}
and
\tilde{f}(T)=n_{\textbf{t}}(T)-\mu_{|T|}
. Let
\tilde{F}
and
F^{(1)}
be the additive functionals associated with
\tilde{f}
and
f^{(1)}
, respectively. From the Minkowski inequality, we have

(10)

\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))}\geq\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{F}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))}-\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(F^{(1)}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))}.

Using the same argument as in the derivation of Equation (
7
), we obtain from
[
8
, Theorem 6.7]
that

(11)

\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(F^{(1)}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))}\ll\sqrt{n}\left(\sup_{k}|\mu_{k}-\theta|+\sum_{k=1}^{\infty}\frac{|\mu_{k}-\theta|}{k}\right)=O(\sqrt{n}),

where the last estimate follows from Lemma
3
. Next we estimate
\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{F}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))
. From
[
8
, Equation (6.27)]
, we infer

\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{F}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\frac{\mathbb{P}(S_{n-k}=n-k)}{\mathbb{P}(S_{n}=n-1)}\,\pi_{k}\,\mathbb{E}\!\left(\tilde{f}(\mathcal{T}_{k})\bigl(2\tilde{F}(\mathcal{T}_{k})-\tilde{f}(\mathcal{T}_{k})\bigr)\right).

where
S_{m}
is the sum of
m
independent copies of
\xi
, and
\pi_{k}=\mathbb{P}\left(|\mathcal{T}|=k\right)
. We write

\tilde{F}(\mathcal{T}_{k})=\sum_{\ell\geq 0}\sum_{d(v)=\ell}\tilde{f}(\mathcal{T}_{k,v}),

and obtain

\tilde{f}(\mathcal{T}_{k})\bigl(2\tilde{F}(\mathcal{T}_{k})-\tilde{f}(\mathcal{T}_{k})\bigr)=\tilde{f}(\mathcal{T}_{k})^{2}+2\sum_{\ell\geq 1}\sum_{d(v)=\ell}\tilde{f}(\mathcal{T}_{k})\tilde{f}(\mathcal{T}_{k,v}).

Since
t
is a star, the expectation of the second term on the right-hand side is zero (using the same argument as in the proof of Lemma
5
). Hence,

\mathbb{E}\!\left(\tilde{f}(\mathcal{T}_{k})\bigl(2\tilde{F}(\mathcal{T}_{k})-\tilde{f}(\mathcal{T}_{k})\bigr)\right)=\mathbb{E}(f(\mathcal{T}_{k})^{2})=\mathbb{E}(n_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{k})^{2})-\mu_{k}^{2}.

Since
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{\Delta+2})<\infty
,
\mu_{k}=O(1)
. Therefore, using (
3
), there exists a constant
c_{1}>0
such that

\mathbb{E}\!\left(\tilde{f}(\mathcal{T}_{k})\bigl(2\tilde{F}(\mathcal{T}_{k})-\tilde{f}(\mathcal{T}_{k})\bigr)\right)\gg\mathbb{E}(w_{1}(\mathcal{T}_{k})^{2\Delta})\gg\sum_{j\leq c_{1}\sqrt{k}}j^{2\Delta+1}\mathbb{P}\left(\xi=j\right).

From the above, together with
[
8
, Lemma 5.2, Part (i)]
, we deduce that there exist constants
c_{2},c_{3}>0
such that

\displaystyle\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{F}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))\gg\sum_{k\leq c_{2}n}\frac{1}{k^{3/2}}\sum_{j\leq c_{1}\sqrt{k}}j^{2\Delta+1}\mathbb{P}\left(\xi=j\right)\gg\sum_{j\leq c_{3}\sqrt{n}}j^{2\Delta}\mathbb{P}\left(\xi=j\right).

The latter clearly tends to
\infty
as
n\to\infty
because of the assumption
\mathbb{E}(\xi^{2\Delta})=\infty
. Therefore, combining this with Equations (
10
) and (
11
), the variance
\mathrm{Var}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))
in this case grows superlinearly in
n
, that is

\frac{1}{n}\mathrm{Var}(N_{\textbf{t}}(\mathcal{T}_{n}))\to\infty\quad\text{ as }\quad n\to\infty.
