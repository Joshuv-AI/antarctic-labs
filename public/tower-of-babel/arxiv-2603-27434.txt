Bounds on median eigenvalues of graphs of bounded degree
Hricha Acharya, Zilin Jiang Shengtong Zhang

Abstract
We prove that for every integer d ≥ 3 d\geq 3 , the median eigenvalues of any graph of maximum degree d d are bounded above by d − 1 \sqrt{d-1} . We also prove that, in three separate cases, the median eigenvalues of a graph of maximum degree d d are bounded below by − d − 1 -\sqrt{d-1} : when the graph is triangle-free, when d − 1 d-1 is a perfect square, or when d ≥ 75 d\geq 75 . These results resolve, for all but finitely many values of d d , an open problem of Mohar on median eigenvalues of graphs of maximum degree d d . As a byproduct, we establish an upper bound on the average energy of graphs of maximum degree at most d d , generalizing a previous result of van Dam, Haemers, and Koolen for d d -regular graphs.

Introduction

Spectral graph theory has traditionally focused on extremal portions of the spectrum, particularly the largest eigenvalue and, more generally, the leading eigenvalues. In contrast, eigenvalues near the center of the spectrum have received much less attention. Among these, the
median eigenvalues
form a natural and important object of study, motivated both by intrinsic spectral questions and by applications arising from Hückel molecular orbital theory in chemistry
[
5
]
.
Let
G
be a simple graph of order
n
, and let
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}
denote the eigenvalues of its adjacency matrix
A_{G}
. The
median eigenvalues
of
G
are the eigenvalues
\lambda_{h}
and
\lambda_{\ell}
, where

h=\lfloor(n+1)/2\rfloor\quad\text{and}\quad\ell=\lceil(n+1)/2\rceil.

The systematic study of median eigenvalues was initiated by Fowler and Pisanski
[
2
,
3
]
, who conducted computational experiments on graphs of maximum degree at most three, also known as
subcubic graphs
. They conjectured that, apart from finitely many exceptions, every subcubic graph has its median eigenvalues in the interval
[-1,1]
.

Subsequent work of Mohar
[
7
,
9
]
confirmed the conjecture of Fowler and Pisanski for
bipartite
subcubic graphs, and identified the Heawood graph, namely the incidence graph of the Fano plane, as the unique exception, with median eigenvalues
\pm\sqrt{2}
. Recently Acharya, Jeter, and Jiang
[
1
]
completely resolved the conjecture, proving that every subcubic graph, except for the Heawood graph, has its median eigenvalues in
[-1,1]
.

With the subcubic case settled, we turn to graphs of maximum degree
d
for a general integer
d
. Mohar
[
8
]
proved that the median eigenvalues of any graph of maximum degree
d
are at most
\sqrt{d}
in absolute value, and conjectured that
\sqrt{d}
could be improved to
\sqrt{d-1}
. Mohar also pointed out that the median eigenvalues of the incidence graph of a projective plane of order
d-1
are equal to
\pm\sqrt{d-1}
; hence the bound
\sqrt{d-1}
would be optimal whenever
d-1
is a prime power.

In this paper, we confirm Mohar’s conjecture for all but finitely many values of
d
. We begin with the upper bound on the median eigenvalues.

Theorem 1.1
.

For every integer
d\geq 3
, the median eigenvalues of any graph of maximum degree
d
are at most
\sqrt{d-1}
.

We then prove the lower bound on the median eigenvalues in three separate cases.

Theorem 1.2
.

For every integer
d\geq 3
, the median eigenvalues of any triangle-free graph of maximum degree
d
are at least
-\sqrt{d-1}
.

Theorem 1.3
.

For every integer
d\geq 2
such that
d-1
is a perfect square, the median eigenvalues of any graph of average degree at most
d
are at least
-\sqrt{d-1}
.

Theorem 1.4
.

For every integer
d\geq 75
, the median eigenvalues of any graph of maximum degree
d
are at least
-\sqrt{d-1}
.

Remark
.

Theorem

1.3
replaces the maximum-degree hypothesis with an average-degree hypothesis.

A key ingredient in the proof of

Bounds on median eigenvalues

Lemma 2.1
.

For every
n
-vertex graph
G
of average degree
\overline{d}
, its eigenvalues
\lambda_{1},\dots,\lambda_{n}
satisfy

\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=0,\quad\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}=\overline{d}n,\quad\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{3}\geq 0,\quad\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{4}\geq\left(2\overline{d}^{2}-\overline{d}\right)n,

and moreover
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{3}=0
if and only if
G
is triangle-free.

Proof.

Notice that
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{k}=\tr(A_{G}^{k})
counts the number of closed walks of length
k
in
G
. Clearly,
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=0
, and
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}=\overline{d}n
, since this quantity counts each edge of
G
twice. The sum
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{3}
counts six times the number of triangles in
G
, and is therefore non-negative; moreover, it is equal to
0
if and only if
G
is triangle-free. Finally, by considering only the closed walks of the form
ababa
,
abaca
, or
abcba
, we obtain the lower bound via the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality:

\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{4}\geq\sum_{i}d_{i}^{2}+d_{i}(d_{i}-1)=\sum_{i}2d_{i}^{2}-d_{i}\geq\left(2\overline{d}^{2}-\overline{d}\right)n,

where
(d_{1},\dots,d_{n})
denotes the degree sequence of
G
.
∎

To prove

Theorem 3.1

(McClelland
[
6
]
)
.

For every graph
G
with average degree at most

, its average energy satisfies

\varepsilon(G)\leq\sqrt{d}

with equality if and only if

is an empty graph or a matching, and

Proof.

Since
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}\leq dn

2.1

\varepsilon(G)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert\leq\sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}}\leq\sqrt{d}.

Equality holds if and only if
\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert=\sqrt{d}
for all
i
, and
d
is equal to the average degree of
G
.

Suppose that all eigenvalues of
G
have the same absolute value. Let
C
be an arbitrary connected component of
G
. By the Perron–Frobenius theorem, the largest eigenvalue of
C
has multiplicity
1
. If all eigenvalues of
C
have the same absolute value and still sum to
0
, then
C
must have order at most
2
; that is,
C
is either a single vertex or a single edge. Since this holds for every connected component of
G
, the graph
G
is either an empty graph or a matching.
∎

Upper bound on average energy

To prove
Theorem

1.5
, the key analytic ingredient is a carefully chosen polynomial with no

Lemma 4.1
.

Let
d
be an integer at least
2
, and define

f(x)=\left(x-d\right)\left(x-\sqrt{d-1}\right)^{2}\left(x+d+2\sqrt{d-1}\right).

Then, for every graph
G
of maximum degree
d
, the spectrum
\{\lambda_{i}\}
and the average energy
\varepsilon(G)
of
G
satisfy

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}f(\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert)\geq 2\sqrt{d-1}\left(d+\sqrt{d-1}\right)^{2}\left(\varepsilon(G)-\sqrt{d-1}-\frac{1}{d+\sqrt{d-1}}\right).

Proof.

Expanding
f(x)
yields
x^{4}-\alpha x^{2}+\beta x-\gamma
, where the coefficients are defined by

\alpha=d^{2}+3d-3+2d\sqrt{d-1},\quad\beta=2\sqrt{d-1}\left(d+\sqrt{d-1}\right)^{2},\quad\gamma=d(d-1)(d+2\sqrt{d-1}).

The left hand side of

can be rewritten as

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{4}-\alpha\cdot\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}+\beta\varepsilon(G)-\gamma,

is at least

2\overline{d}^{2}-\overline{d}-\alpha\overline{d}+\beta\varepsilon(G)-\gamma,

where
\overline{d}
is the average degree of
G
.

Notice that the quadratic function
x\mapsto 2x^{2}-(1+\alpha)x
is decreasing on the interval
(-\infty,(1+\alpha)/4)
. One checks that
(1+\alpha)/4>d
for

. Since
\overline{d}\leq d<(1+\alpha)/4
, (

) is at least

, which is equal to the right-hand side of (
12
).

G
, where
h=\lfloor(n+1)/2\rfloor
and
\ell=\lceil(n+1)/2\rceil
. We first prove that
\lambda_{h}\leq\varepsilon(G)
. If
\lambda_{h}\geq 0
, then
h\geq n/2
, and hence

\varepsilon(G)=\frac{2}{n}\sum_{i\colon\lambda_{i}\geq 0}\lambda_{i}\geq\frac{2h\lambda_{h}}{n}\geq\lambda_{h}.

Otherwise, if
\lambda_{h}<0
, then
\lambda_{h}\leq\varepsilon(G)
holds trivially. The proof of
\lambda_{\ell}\geq-\varepsilon(G)
is analogous.
∎

5
Lower bound when
d
is at least
75

Finally, we present the proof of
Theorem

1.4
, which reuses a couple of ideas from the proof of
Theorem

1.3
. The proof of the following optimization problem is deferred to
Appendix

A
.

Proposition 5.1
.

Fix an integer
d\geq 75
. Set

\varepsilon_{0}=\sqrt{d-1}\quad\text{and}\quad\varepsilon_{1}=\sqrt{d-1}+\frac{1}{d+\sqrt{d-1}}.

Then there exists
\delta\in(\varepsilon_{0},d)
such that the maximum value of

(2\varepsilon_{0})^{x+y+z}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{1}}{2\varepsilon_{0}}\right)(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0})^{x}\left(\frac{\alpha(\delta)\cdot(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon)}{y}\right)^{y/2}(d-\varepsilon_{0})^{z}

under the constraint that

\varepsilon_{0}\leq\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{1},\quad x\geq\frac{1}{2},\quad 0\leq y\leq\frac{1}{2}-z,\quad 0\leq z\leq\frac{1}{(\delta-\varepsilon_{0})^{2}},

is strictly less than
1
, where
\alpha(\delta)
is defined by

\alpha(\delta)=\frac{2\varepsilon_{0}(d+\varepsilon_{0})^{2}}{(d-\delta)(d+2\varepsilon_{0}+\delta)}.

Proof of
Theorem

1.4
.

Fix an integer
d\geq 75
. Let
G
be a graph of maximum degree
d
, let
\lambda_{1}\geq\lambda_{2}\geq\cdots\geq\lambda_{n}
be the eigenvalues of its adjacency matrix
A_{G}
, let
\lambda_{h}
and
\lambda_{\ell}
be the median eigenvalues of
G
, and let
\varepsilon
be the average energy of
G
. We define the constants
\varepsilon_{0}
and
\varepsilon_{1}
as in
Proposition

5.1
.
Assume for the sake of contradiction that the lower median eigenvalue
\lambda_{\ell}
is less than
-\varepsilon_{0}
. By
Theorem

4.2
, we are done as soon as
\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{0}
. Hereafter, in view of
Theorem

1.5
, we assume that

\varepsilon_{0}\leq\varepsilon\leq\varepsilon_{1}.

(14)

Let
I
denote the set of indices
i
such that
\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert\neq\varepsilon_{0}
. Since
\prod_{i\in I}(x-\lambda_{i}^{2})\in\mathbb{Z}[x]
, we deduce that
\prod_{i\in I}\left(\varepsilon_{0}^{2}-\lambda_{i}^{2}\right)
is a nonzero integer.
Consequently,

\prod_{i\in I}\left\lvert\varepsilon_{0}^{2}-\lambda_{i}^{2}\right\rvert\geq 1.

(15)

We shall derive a contradiction by showing that the last inequality fails whenever
d\geq 75
. We factor the above product as

A:=\prod_{i\in I}\left\lvert\varepsilon_{0}+\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert\right\rvert\quad\text{and}\quad B:=\prod_{i\in I}\left\lvert\varepsilon_{0}-\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert\right\rvert.

We first estimate
A
. Applying the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means, we obtain

A\leq\left(\varepsilon_{0}+\frac{1}{\lvert I\rvert}\sum_{i\in I}\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert\right)^{\lvert I\rvert}.

(16)

Since
\lambda_{\ell}<-\varepsilon_{0}
, we have
\{\ell,\dots,n\}\subseteq I
, and so
\lvert I\rvert\geq n/2
. Since

\varepsilon=\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{i\in I}\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert+\sum_{i\notin I}\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert\right)=\frac{1}{n}\left(\sum_{i\in I}\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert+(n-\lvert I\rvert)\varepsilon_{0}\right),

we obtain

\varepsilon_{0}+\frac{1}{\lvert I\rvert}\sum_{i\in I}\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert=\varepsilon_{0}+\frac{1}{\lvert I\rvert}\left(n\varepsilon-\left(n-\lvert I\rvert\right)\varepsilon_{0}\right)=2\varepsilon_{0}+\frac{n}{\lvert I\rvert}(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0}).

One checks that the function
x\mapsto(1+1/x)^{x}
is increasing on
[0,\infty)
. Since
\lvert I\rvert\leq n
, we have the estimate

A\leq\left(2\varepsilon_{0}+\frac{n}{\lvert I\rvert}(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0})\right)^{\lvert I\rvert}=(2\varepsilon_{0})^{\lvert I\rvert}\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0}}{2\varepsilon_{0}}\cdot\frac{n}{\lvert I\rvert}\right)^{\lvert I\rvert}\leq(2\varepsilon_{0})^{\lvert I\rvert}\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0}}{2\varepsilon_{0}}\right)^{n}\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:average_energy inequality}}}{{\leq}}(2\varepsilon_{0})^{\lvert I\rvert}\left(1+\frac{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{0}}{2\varepsilon_{0}}\right)^{n}=(2\varepsilon_{0})^{\lvert I\rvert}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{1}}{2\varepsilon_{0}}\right)^{n}.

(17)

We now turn to
B
. Let
J=\{i\colon\lambda_{i}<-\varepsilon_{0}\}
. Notice that
J\subseteq\{\ell,\dots,n\}
, and in particular,

J\subseteq I\quad\text{and}\quad\lvert J\rvert\geq n/2.

(18)

We estimate part of
B
corresponding to
J
using the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means:

B_{J}:=\prod_{i\in J}\left\lvert\varepsilon_{0}-\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert\right\rvert=\prod_{i\in J}\left(\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert-\varepsilon_{0}\right)\leq\left(\frac{1}{\lvert J\rvert}\sum_{i\in J}\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert-\varepsilon_{0}\right)^{\lvert J\rvert}.

Since
\lambda_{i}
with
i\in J
have the same sign and
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}=0
by
Lemma

2.1
, we have

\sum_{i\in J}\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert=\left\lvert\sum_{i\in J}\lambda_{i}\right\rvert=\frac{1}{2}\left(\left\lvert\sum_{i\in J}\lambda_{i}\right\rvert+\left\lvert\sum_{i\notin J}\lambda_{i}\right\rvert\right)\leq\frac{1}{2}\sum_{i}\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert=\frac{1}{2}n\varepsilon\leq\lvert J\rvert\varepsilon,

which implies that

B_{J}\leq\left(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0}\right)^{\lvert J\rvert}.

(19)

We shall further divide
I\setminus J
into two subsets
K
and
L
as follows:

K=\{i\in I\setminus J:\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert\leq\delta\}\quad\text{and}\quad L=\{i\in I\setminus J:\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert>\delta\},

where
\delta\in(\varepsilon_{0},d)
is a parameter to be determined later, and it depends only on
d
.

To estimate the part of
B
corresponding to
K
, we apply the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means:

B_{K}:=\prod_{i\in K}\left\lvert\varepsilon_{0}-\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert\right\rvert=\left(\prod_{i\in K}\left(\varepsilon_{0}-\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert\right)^{2}\right)^{1/2}\leq\left(\frac{1}{\lvert K\rvert}\left(\sum_{i\in K}\left(\varepsilon_{0}-\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert\right)^{2}\right)\right)^{\lvert K\rvert/2}.

(20)

Define
g(x)
as follows:

g(x)=(d-x)\left(\varepsilon_{0}-x\right)^{2}\left(x+d+2\varepsilon_{0}\right).

Clearly
g(x)=-f(x)
, where
f(x)
is defined in
Lemma

4.1
, and
g(x)\geq 0
for all
x\in[0,d]
. By
Lemma

4.1
, we have

\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i}g(\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert)\leq 2\varepsilon_{0}\left(d+\varepsilon_{0}\right)^{2}\left(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon\right).

(21)

Observe that the quadratic function
x\mapsto(d-x)(x+d+2\varepsilon_{0})
is decreasing on
(0,d)
. For each
i\in K
, since
0\leq\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert\leq\delta<d
, we have

\left(\varepsilon_{0}-\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert\right)^{2}=\frac{g(\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert)}{(d-\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert)(\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert+d+2\varepsilon_{0})}\leq\frac{g(\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert)}{(d-\delta)(\delta+d+2\varepsilon_{0})}.

Summing over
i\in K
, we obtain

\sum_{i\in K}\left(\varepsilon_{0}-\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert\right)^{2}\leq\frac{\sum_{i\in K}g(\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert)}{(d-\delta)(\delta+d+2\varepsilon_{0})}\leq\frac{\sum_{i}g(\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert)}{(d-\delta)(\delta+d+2\varepsilon_{0})}\\
\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:g_estimate}}}{{\leq}}\frac{2\varepsilon_{0}\left(d+\varepsilon_{0}\right)^{2}}{(d-\delta)(\delta+d+2\varepsilon_{0})}\left(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon\right)n=\alpha(\delta)\cdot(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon)\cdot n,

where
\alpha(\delta)
is defined in
Proposition

5.1
.
Substituting the last estimate into (
20
), we obtain that

B_{K}\leq\left(\frac{\alpha(\delta)\cdot(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon)n}{\lvert K\rvert}\right)^{\lvert K\rvert/2}.

(22)

To estimate part of
B
corresponding to
L
, we simply use
\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert\leq d
to get that

B_{L}\leq(d-\varepsilon_{0})^{\lvert L\rvert}.

(23)

We also estimate
\lvert L\rvert
here. Using the fact that
\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}
equals the degree sum of
G
, which is bounded by
nd
, we obtain that

\sum_{i\in L}(\varepsilon_{0}-\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert)^{2}\leq\sum_{i}(\varepsilon_{0}-\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert)^{2}\leq n\varepsilon_{0}^{2}-2\varepsilon_{0}\sum_{i}\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert+\sum_{i}\lambda_{i}^{2}\leq n(2d-1-2\varepsilon_{0}\varepsilon)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:average_energy inequality}}}{{\leq}}n(2d-1-2\varepsilon_{0}^{2})=n.

Since
\lvert\lambda_{i}\rvert>\delta>\varepsilon_{0}
for
i\in L
, the above inequality implies that

\lvert L\rvert\leq\frac{n}{(\delta-\varepsilon_{0})^{2}}.

(24)

Set
x=\lvert J\rvert/n
,
y=\lvert K\rvert/n
, and
z=\lvert L\rvert/n
. Then
\lvert I\rvert/n=x+y+z\leq 1
. In view of (
14
), (
18
), and (
24
), the parameters
\varepsilon,x,y,z
satisfy the constraints in
Proposition

5.1
. Combining the bounds (
17
), (
19
), (
22
), and (
23
), we obtain that

\displaystyle\left(\prod_{i\in I}\left\lvert d-1-\lambda_{i}^{2}\right\rvert\right)^{1/n}

\displaystyle=\left(AB_{J}B_{K}B_{L}\right)^{1/n}

\displaystyle\leq(2\varepsilon_{0})^{x+y+z}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{1}}{2\varepsilon_{0}}\right)\left(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0}\right)^{x}\left(\frac{\alpha(\delta)\cdot(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon)}{y}\right)^{y/2}\left(d-\varepsilon_{0}\right)^{z}.

According to
Proposition

5.1
, there exists
\delta\in(\varepsilon_{0},d)
such that the right hand side of the last inequality is strictly less than
1
, which contradicts (
15
).
∎

6
Concluding remarks

Our results confirm Mohar’s conjecture for all but
64
values of
d
— the exceptions are
d\in\{4,\dots,74\}\setminus\{5,10,17,26,37,50,65\}
. In particular, it would be interesting to settle his conjecture for
d=4
.

Conjecture 6.1
.

The median eigenvalues of any graph of maximum degree
4
are at least
-\sqrt{3}
.

Inspired by the result of
[
1
]
on subcubic graphs, it is natural to conjecture that the bound can be strengthened by excluding the incidence graph of the projective plane of order
3
.

Conjecture 6.2
.

The median eigenvalues of any graph of maximum degree
4
, except for a vertex-disjoint union of incidence graphs of projective planes of order
3
, are at most
\sqrt{2}
in absolute value.

For graphs of maximum degree
5
,
Theorems

1.1
and
1.3
already imply that the median eigenvalues are bounded in absolute value by
2
. We note that the incidence graph of the projective plane of order
4
is not the only connected
5
-regular graph attaining this bound. Indeed, the Cayley graph of the group
\mathbb{Z}/12\mathbb{Z}
generated by
\{\pm 3,\pm 4,6\}
is a connected
5
-regular graph whose median eigenvalues are
-2
and
+1
.

A significant related result is due to Mohar and Tayfeh-Rezaie
[
10
]
, who proved that for every integer
d\geq 3
, the median eigenvalues of any
bipartite
graph
G
of maximum degree
d
are at most
\sqrt{d-2}
in absolute value, unless
G
is the vertex-disjoint union of incidence graphs of projective planes of order
d-1
, in which case the median eigenvalues are
\pm\sqrt{d-1}
.

It is natural to relax the condition on maximum degree to one on average degree. From
Theorems

3.1
and
4.2
, we obtain the following bound on the median eigenvalues.

Corollary 6.3
.

The median eigenvalues of any graph of average degree
d
are at most
\sqrt{d}
in absolute value. ∎

Theorem

1.2
suggests a stronger conjecture that improves the bound from
\sqrt{d}
to
\sqrt{d-1}
.

Conjecture 6.4
.

For every real number
d\geq 2
, the median eigenvalues of any graph of average degree at most
d
are at most
\sqrt{d-1}
in absolute value.

We point out that the conjecture fails for
d\in[1,2)
. For example, the vertex-disjoint union of
a
triangles and
b
single edges has average degree
(6a+2b)/(3a+2b)
, but the median eigenvalues are
1
in absolute value.

Proof of
Proposition

5.1
.

Fix an integer
d\geq 75
. We shall choose
\delta\in(\varepsilon_{0},d)
later so that

\alpha(\delta)\cdot(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{0})\geq 1/5\quad\text{and}\quad\delta-\varepsilon_{0}\geq\sqrt{2}.

(25)

First, when
\varepsilon,y,z
are fixed, the function

(2\varepsilon_{0})^{x+y+z}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{1}}{2\varepsilon_{0}}\right)\left(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0}\right)^{x}\left(\frac{\alpha(\delta)\cdot(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon)}{y}\right)^{y/2}(d-\varepsilon_{0})^{z}

(26)

is proportional to
(2\varepsilon_{0})^{x}(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0})^{x}
, which is a decreasing function of
x
because

2\varepsilon_{0}(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0})\leq 2\varepsilon_{0}(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{0})=\frac{2\sqrt{d-1}}{d+\sqrt{d-1}}<1.

Therefore, the maximum value of (
26
) is attained when
x=1/2
. Under this assumption, the function becomes

(2\varepsilon_{0})^{1/2+y+z}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{1}}{2\varepsilon_{0}}\right)\left(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{\alpha(\delta)\cdot(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon)}{y}\right)^{y/2}(d-\varepsilon_{0})^{z}.

Second, when
y
and
z
are fixed, the above function is proportional to
(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{0})^{1/2}(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon)^{y/2}
, which attains its maximum when
\varepsilon=(y\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{1})/(y+1)
. Under this assumption, the function becomes

(2\varepsilon_{0})^{1/2+y+z}\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{1}}{2\varepsilon_{0}}\right)\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{0}}{y+1}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{\alpha(\delta)\cdot(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{0})}{y+1}\right)^{y/2}(d-\varepsilon_{0})^{z}.

(27)

Third, when
z
is fixed, the above function is proportional to

(2\varepsilon_{0})^{y}\left(\frac{\alpha(\delta)\cdot(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{0})}{y+1}\right)^{(y+1)/2}

One checks that if
a^{2}b>3e/2
, then the function
y\mapsto a^{y}(b/(y+1))^{(y+1)/2}
is increasing on
[0,1/2]
. Since
(2\varepsilon_{0})^{2}\cdot\alpha(\delta)\cdot(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{0})\geq 4(d-1)/5\geq 57
by (
25
), the maximum value of (
27
) is attained when
y=1/2-z
. Under this assumption, the function becomes

(\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{1})\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{0}}{3/2-z}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{\alpha(\delta)\cdot(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{0})}{3/2-z}\right)^{1/4-z/2}(d-\varepsilon_{0})^{z}.

(28)

Fourth, the above function is proportional to

\frac{(d-\varepsilon_{0})^{z}}{(3/2-z)^{3/4-z/2}}.

One checks that if
a>e^{-1/2}
, then the function
z\mapsto a^{z}/(3/2-z)^{3/4-z/2}
is increasing on
[0,1/2]
. Since
d-\varepsilon_{0}\geq 60
for
d\geq 75
and
1/(\delta-\varepsilon_{0})^{2}\leq 1/2
by (
25
), the maximum value of (
28
) is attained when
z=1/(\delta-\varepsilon_{0})^{2}=:z^{*}
. Under this assumption, the function becomes

(\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{1})\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{0}}{3/2-z^{*}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{\alpha(\delta)\cdot(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{0})}{3/2-z^{*}}\right)^{1/4-z^{*}/2}(d-\varepsilon_{0})^{z^{*}}.

(29)

Suppose that
75\leq d\leq 139
. We choose
\delta\in(\varepsilon_{0},d)
so that
\alpha(\delta)\cdot(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{0})=1/4
, which is a quadratic equation in
\delta
. Solving this equation gives
\delta=\sqrt{(d+\varepsilon_{0})(d-7\varepsilon_{0})}-\varepsilon_{0}
. One checks numerically for every
d\in\{75,\dots,139\}
that (
25
) is satisfied and (
29
) is strictly less than
1
.

Suppose that
d\geq 140
. We choose
\delta\in(\varepsilon_{0},d)
so that
\alpha(\delta)\cdot(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{0})=1/5
, which is a quadratic equation in
\delta
. Solving this equation gives
\delta=\sqrt{(d+\varepsilon_{0})(d-9\varepsilon_{0})}-\varepsilon_{0}
. One checks that

\delta-\varepsilon_{0}\geq d/3\text{ for }d\geq 140,

(30)

and so (
25
) is satisfied. Since
\alpha(\delta)\cdot(\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{0})=1/5
, the maximum value of (
29
) simplifies to

(\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{1})\left(\frac{\varepsilon_{1}-\varepsilon_{0}}{3/2-z^{*}}\right)^{1/2}\left(\frac{1}{15/2-5z^{*}}\right)^{1/4-z^{*}/2}(d-\varepsilon_{0})^{z^{*}}\\
=\frac{\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{1}}{\sqrt{d+\varepsilon_{0}}}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{5}}{(15/2-5z^{*})^{3/4-z^{*}/2}}\cdot\exp\left(\frac{\ln(d-\varepsilon_{0})}{(\delta-\varepsilon_{0})^{2}}\right).

We estimate the three factors in the last expression separately. First, we have

\frac{\varepsilon_{0}+\varepsilon_{1}}{\sqrt{d+\varepsilon_{0}}}\leq\frac{2\sqrt{d}+1/d}{\sqrt{d}}=2+1/\left(d\sqrt{d}\right)\leq 2+1/444.

Second, since
z^{*}=1/(\delta-\varepsilon_{0})^{2}\leq 9/d^{2}\leq 1/2000
via (
30
), we have

\frac{\sqrt{5}}{(15/2-5z^{*})^{3/4-z^{*}/2}}\leq\frac{\sqrt{5}}{(15/2-1/400)^{3/4-1/4000}}\leq\frac{1}{2+1/40}.

Third, since
d\mapsto\ln d/d^{2}
is a decreasing function on
(\sqrt{e},\infty)
, we have that

\exp\left(\frac{\ln(d-\varepsilon_{0})}{(\delta-\varepsilon_{0})^{2}}\right)\stackrel{{\scriptstyle\eqref{eq:delta_inequality}}}{{\leq}}\exp\left(\frac{9\ln d}{d^{2}}\right)\leq\exp\left(\frac{9\ln 140}{140^{2}}\right)\leq\frac{111}{110}.

Therefore, the product of the three factors is at most

\frac{2+1/444}{2+1/40}\cdot\frac{111}{110}=\frac{889}{891}

which is strictly less than
1
.
∎
