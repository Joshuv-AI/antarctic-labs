cSTMM: A Unified Complex Spherical Student’s t t Mixture Model for Directional Statistics in Mask-Based Blind Speech Separation


Abstract
Mask-based blind speech separation (BSS) estimates source-wise time–frequency (TF) masks by clustering multichannel observations using spatial information. The directional statistical approach clusters normalized multichannel observations on the complex unit sphere, without explicitly extracting phase and level difference features based on the plane-wave or spherical-wave assumptions. However, prior studies have mostly compared a small number of separately defined directional statistical mixture models, whereas a broader distribution family would enable a more systematic study of how density profiles affect separation performance. We propose the complex spherical Student’s t t mixture model (cSTMM), a directional mixture model that connects the complex angular central Gaussian mixture model (cACGMM), complex Bingham mixture model (cBMM), and complex Watson mixture model (cWMM) through the degrees-of-freedom parameter ν \nu . We also derive a generalized minorization-maximization (MM) based procedure for parameter estimation. A no-restart evaluation on noise-free LibriSpeech mixtures reverberated with measured room impulse responses shows that a single development-selected value ν ∗ = 1 \nu^{\ast}=1 achieved higher test-set mean signal-to-distortion ratio improvements (SDRi) than the cACGMM-equivalent setting ν = M \nu=M in all acoustic conditions, with an average condition-wise gain of 0.25 dB. The experiments also numerically verify that the proposed formulation numerically recovers the cACGMM, cBMM, and cWMM cases.

\nu\to\infty

\nu\to\infty

The proposed framework enables the density profile of normalized complex observations to be varied within a single maximum-likelihood mask estimation framework, rather than choosing among separately defined directional mixture models.

2
Preliminaries

2.1
Mask estimation with directional statistics

Let
\bm{y}_{tf}\in\mathbb{C}^{M}
be the short-time Fourier transform (STFT) coefficients of multichannel observation at frame
t
and frequency bin
f
, where
M
is the number of microphones. The cSTMM in this paper uses the normalized observation

\bm{z}_{tf}=\frac{\bm{y}_{tf}}{\|\bm{y}_{tf}\|_{2}},\hskip 9.24994pt\mathcal{T}_{f}=\{t\mid\|\bm{y}_{tf}\|_{2}\geq\varepsilon\},

(1)

where
\mathcal{T}_{f}
is the set of valid TF bins at frequency bin
f
. Let
s^{(n)}_{tf}
be the
n
th source signal in the STFT domain and
\bm{h}^{(n)}_{f}
the transfer function from the
n
th source to the microphones.
Under the usual dominant source approximation,

\bm{y}_{tf}\approx\bm{h}_{f}^{(n)}s_{tf}^{(n)}

(2)

when source
n
dominates the TF bin, so
\bm{z}_{tf}
is mainly determined by the transfer function vector of the dominant source. Let
d_{tf}
be the index of the dominant source at frame
t
and frequency bin
f
. A frequency-wise mixture model is

p(\bm{z}_{tf};\Theta_{f})=\sum_{n=1}^{N}w_{f}^{(n)}p(\bm{z}_{tf}\mid d_{tf}=n;\Theta_{f}),

(3)

where mixture weights
w_{f}^{(n)}
satisfy
w_{f}^{(n)}\geq 0
and
\sum_{n}w_{f}^{(n)}=1
, and
\Theta_{f}
is the set of parameters at frequency bin
f
. The posterior probability

\gamma_{tf}^{(n)}=\frac{w_{f}^{(n)}p(\bm{z}_{tf}\mid d_{tf}=n;\Theta_{f})}{\sum_{n^{\prime}}w_{f}^{(n^{\prime})}p(\bm{z}_{tf}\mid d_{tf}=n^{\prime};\Theta_{f})}

(4)

is used as the soft mask. Since the model is fitted independently at each frequency, permutation alignment is applied afterward
[
4
]
.

2.2
Established probability models

The cWMM uses the complex Watson density
[
13
]

p(\bm{z})\propto\exp(\kappa|\bm{a}^{\mathrm{H}}\bm{z}|^{2})

(5)

with
\|\bm{a}\|_{2}=1
and
\kappa\geq 0
. The cBMM uses the complex Bingham density
[
14
]

p(\bm{z})\propto\exp(\bm{z}^{\mathrm{H}}\bm{A}\bm{z}),

(6)

where
\bm{A}
is Hermitian. The cACGMM uses the complex angular central Gaussian density
[
15
]

p(\bm{z})\propto(\bm{z}^{\mathrm{H}}\bm{P}\bm{z})^{-M},

(7)

where
\bm{P}\succ\bm{O}
is scale-invariant. These models differ not only in isotropy but also in how their densities decay away from high-probability directions. The role of the cSTMM is to place these choices in one distribution family and to allow
\nu
to vary the shape of that family.

3
Proposed method

3.1
Full cSTMM and relation to the cACGMM and cBMM

For each frequency and source, the cSTMM uses a mixture weight
w_{f}^{(n)}
and a Hermitian matrix
\bm{A}_{f}^{(n)}
. On the complex unit sphere, the component density is given by
[
16
]

p(\bm{z}\mid\bm{A},\nu)=C(\bm{A},\nu)\left(1-\frac{2}{\nu}\bm{z}^{\mathrm{H}}\bm{A}\bm{z}\right)^{-(\nu+M)/2},

(8)

with

\lambda_{\max}(\bm{A})<\frac{\nu}{2}.

(9)

Because
\|\bm{z}\|_{2}=1
,
\bm{A}
and
(\bm{A}-a\bm{I})/(1-2a/\nu)
define the same normalized density for any
a<\nu/2
. We therefore use the representation in which the largest eigenvalue of
\bm{A}
is zero. The exact normalizer
C(\bm{A},\nu)
is expressed by a Gauss hypergeometric function of matrix argument
[
17
]
. In implementation, we evaluate the analytically equivalent simplex integral, with a hypergeometric series fallback.

The family contains the established models. As
\nu\to\infty
, (
8
) tends to the complex Bingham density (
6
).
If
\nu=M
and
\bm{P}=\bm{I}-2\bm{A}/M\succ\bm{O}
, (
8
) becomes the cACG density (
7
) up to scale invariance.

3.2
Degenerate eigenvalue case and relation to the cWMM

The Watson-type case is obtained by starting from a positive semidefinite matrix of rank at most one before the above reparameterization. Let
\bm{B}=\rho\bm{a}\bm{a}^{\mathrm{H}}
,
0\leq\rho<\nu/2
and
\|\bm{a}\|_{2}=1
. The equivalent canonical representation satisfying
\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\bm{A})=0
is

\bm{A}=\frac{\bm{B}-\rho\bm{I}}{1-2\rho/\nu}=-\kappa(\bm{I}-\bm{a}\bm{a}^{\mathrm{H}})

(10)

with

\kappa=\frac{\rho}{1-2\rho/\nu}.

(11)

Thus the model has one zero eigenvalue in the preferred direction and
M-1
tied nonpositive nonprincipal eigenvalues. Its density is

p(\bm{z}\mid\bm{a},\kappa,\nu)\propto\left[1+\frac{2\kappa}{\nu}(1-|\bm{a}^{\mathrm{H}}\bm{z}|^{2})\right]^{-(\nu+M)/2}.

(12)

As
\nu\to\infty
, (
12
) tends to the complex Watson density (
5
).

The Watson-constrained case also has a simple exact normalizing integral. With respect to the normalized uniform measure on the complex unit sphere,
R=|\bm{a}^{\mathrm{H}}\bm{z}|^{2}
follows
\mathrm{Beta}(1,M-1)
, so
U=1-R
follows
\mathrm{Beta}(M-1,1)
. Therefore

Z(\kappa,\nu)=(M-1)\int_{0}^{1}u^{M-2}\left(1+\frac{2\kappa}{\nu}u\right)^{-(\nu+M)/2}du,

(13)

with
C(\kappa,\nu)=Z(\kappa,\nu)^{-1}
. This expression is useful for checking the HCA behavior below in the Watson-constrained case. For large
\kappa
, the tangent space scaling gives
Z(\kappa,\nu)\asymp\kappa^{-(M-1)}
when the tail integral is finite, formally
\nu>M-2
.

3.3
Generalized MM-like updates

For each frequency bin
f
, we maximize the log-likelihood
L_{f}(\Theta_{f})
. Jensen’s inequality gives the responsibility update (
4
). A tangent lower bound for
-\log(1-x)
gives

\displaystyle\phi_{tf}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\frac{2}{\nu}\bm{z}_{tf}^{\mathrm{H}}\bm{A}_{f}^{(n)}\bm{z}_{tf},

(14)

\displaystyle w_{f}^{(n)}

\displaystyle\leftarrow\frac{1}{|\mathcal{T}_{f}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{f}}\gamma_{tf}^{(n)}.

(15)

For each component, the terms depending on
\bm{A}_{f}^{(n)}
reduce to the minorizer

\mathcal{M}(\bm{A})=G\log C(\bm{A},\nu)+\mathrm{tr}(\bm{A}\bm{S})

(16)

with

G=\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{f}}\gamma_{t},

(17)

and
\bm{S}
defined as above. If

\displaystyle\bm{A}

\displaystyle=\bm{U}\mathrm{diag}(0,\lambda_{2},\ldots,\lambda_{M})\bm{U}^{\mathrm{H}},

\displaystyle\bm{S}

\displaystyle=\bm{V}\mathrm{diag}(\sigma_{1},\ldots,\sigma_{M})\bm{V}^{\mathrm{H}},

(18)

von Neumann’s trace inequality aligns
\bm{U}\leftarrow\bm{V}
, leaving the eigenvalue objective

\displaystyle G\log C(\bm{A},\nu)+\sum_{j=2}^{M}\lambda_{j}\sigma_{j}.

(19)

The HCA comes from a local tangent-space normalizer approximation. Around the principal eigendirection, write

\displaystyle\bm{z}=\frac{e^{i\theta}(1,\bm{\xi}^{\top})^{\top}}{(1+\|\bm{\xi}\|^{2})^{1/2}}.

(20)

For concentrated components,

\bm{z}^{\mathrm{H}}\bm{A}\bm{z}\approx\sum_{j=2}^{M}\lambda_{j}|\xi_{j}|^{2}.

(21)

With
b_{j}=-2\lambda_{j}/\nu>0
,

C(\bm{A},\nu)^{-1}\approx K\int_{\mathbb{C}^{M-1}}\left(1+\sum_{j=2}^{M}b_{j}|\xi_{j}|^{2}\right)^{-(\nu+M)/2}d\bm{\xi}.

(22)

The change of variables
\eta_{j}=b_{j}^{1/2}\xi_{j}
gives

\displaystyle\log C(\bm{A},\nu)=\mathrm{const}+\sum_{j=2}^{M}\log(-\lambda_{j})

(23)

up to terms independent of
\lambda_{j}
. The HCA stationary point is therefore

\lambda_{j}^{\rm HCA}=-\frac{G}{\sigma_{j}},\hskip 9.24994ptj=2,\ldots,M.

(24)

Here, high concentration does not simply mean large
\nu
, but rather localization around the principal eigendirection.

The updates for the rank-one case can also be derived using HCA. The updates for
\gamma^{(n)}_{tf}
and
\phi^{(n)}_{tf}
are the same as in the full case, so we focus on
\bm{a}^{(n)}_{f}
and
\kappa^{(n)}_{f}
updates below.
Suppressing
(f,n)
, let

\bm{S}=\frac{\nu+M}{\nu}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{f}}\frac{\gamma_{t}}{1-\phi_{t}}\bm{z}_{t}\bm{z}_{t}^{\mathrm{H}}.

(25)

The HCA surrogate is

\widetilde{Q}(\bm{a},\kappa)=G(M-1)\log\kappa-\kappa\{\mathrm{tr}(\bm{S})-\bm{a}^{\mathrm{H}}\bm{S}\bm{a}\}.

(26)

Hence
\bm{a}
is updated as a principal eigenvector of
\bm{S}
and the concentration by

\kappa^{\rm HCA}=\frac{G(M-1)}{\mathrm{tr}(\bm{S})-\sigma_{1}}=\frac{G(M-1)}{\sum_{j=2}^{M}\sigma_{j}},

(27)

where
\sigma_{1}
is the largest eigenvalue of
\bm{S}
.

Note that the HCA eigenvalue update does not by itself ensure monotonicity of the likelihood.
To enforce monotonicity, one could use the HCA eigenvalue update as a proposal, which is accepted/rejected in a finite backtracking procedure. However, we leave this variant for future work and use the HCA update as an approximate update in the experiments.

Experimental conditions

]

(2,3)

4.2
Main separation result

The main comparison tests whether
\nu
gives a useful degree of freedom beyond the cACGMM-equivalent setting. We compare the cSTMM with a single common value
\nu^{\ast}=1
against cSTMM with
\nu=M
, selected on development mixtures and then fixed for all test conditions. This avoids condition-wise test-set hyperparameter tuning. Table
1
shows the test-set SDRi results, where the same no-restart evaluation setup was used for all conditions. Under this setup, the mean SDRi gain is positive in every condition, and all gains remain statistically significant after Holm correction at the 0.05 level based on two-sided Wilcoxon signed rank tests. Across the 18 conditions, the paired mean SDRi gain averages 0.250 dB and ranges from 0.025 to 0.533 dB. Thus the effect of changing the density profile is systematic in this evaluation, although the gains are small for
(M,N)=(2,3)
and should not be overstated.

4.3
Included model recovery

Figure
1
verifies the model inclusion relationships numerically using 64 development mixtures per condition at
\mathrm{RT}_{60}=610
ms. The included models use matched initialization to isolate distributional equivalence from local-optimum effects. Across the six conditions, the mean absolute SDRi differences are
1.7\times 10^{-13}
dB for cACGMM recovery at
\nu=M
,
2.8\times 10^{-3}
dB for cBMM recovery at
\nu=10^{4}
, and
7.6\times 10^{-4}
dB for cWMM recovery by the rank-one variant at
\nu=10^{4}
. These results numerically verify the inclusion and limiting-case relationships.

5
Conclusion

We introduced the cSTMM, a directional mixture model for mask-based BSS that connects the cACGMM, cBMM, and cWMM through
\nu
and eigenvalue constraints. Experiments show that
\nu^{\ast}=1
gives systematic but modest SDRi gains over
\nu=M
in a fixed no-restart clean benchmark, while controlled recovery tests verify the expected included-model regimes. Future work will evaluate source-free tuning of
\nu
and robustness in noisy, real environments, and downstream transcription evaluation.
